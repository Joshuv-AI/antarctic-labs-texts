Mechanism of lipid-dependent cold sensitivity in a model ion channel
Authors: Chieh-Chin Li, Crina M Nimigean
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTemperature sensing enables organisms to detect and respond to environmental changes. While temperature-responsive ion channels are key to this process, the physico-chemical mechanisms by which they sense temperature remain poorly understood. Here, we investigate the molecular details of temperature sensing in the model bacterial channel, SthK from Spirochaeta thermophila. We show that SthK is cold sensitive, displaying higher activity below 30 °C. Remarkably, SthK cold sensitivity depends strongly on membrane lipids, being sensitive in amine-containing lipids but insensitive in anionic lipids. Combining cryo-EM structural analysis, mutagenesis, and functional assays, we identify an intersubunit salt bridge that acts as temperature sensor. This salt bridge forms only in closed states, and determines channel opening by controlling closed-state stability. Lower temperatures weaken salt-bridge interactions, favoring channel opening, and lipid headgroups tune temperature sensitivity by modulating salt-bridge strength. These findings highlight how thermosensitivity can emerge from cooperative interactions between protein and the surrounding membrane.Subject terms: Ion transport, Ligand-gated ion channels, Cryoelectron microscopy

Introduction

Temperature sensing is essential for organismal survival, enabling the detection of thermal fluctuations and the maintenance of physiological homeostasis1. In mammals, thermal stimuli are primarily detected by somatosensory neurons that express temperature-gated ion channels. Among these, the transient receptor potential (TRP) channel family, especially the subset known as thermo-TRPs, has been the dominant focus of thermosensation research due to their striking temperature-dependent gating properties and clear physiological relevance in heat and cold sensing2. These channels include both heat- and cold-sensitive members; for example, TRPV1 responds to noxious heat, TRPV3 and TRPV4 to innocuous warmth, and TRPM8 and TRPA1 to cold, among others, forming a modular system for detecting a wide range of thermal cues.

Despite their shared ability to detect thermal stimuli, the molecular basis of temperature gating in TRP channels remains elusive. Multiple regions have been implicated in thermosensitivity, such as the pore domain, pore turret, N-terminal ankyrin repeats, and C-terminal tail, although experimental findings are often conflicting. For example, the pore turret has been proposed to contribute to TRPV1 heat activation, yet other studies suggest it is dispensable3,4. Similarly, the C-terminal domain appears to influence temperature tuning in TRPV1–TRPM8 chimeras, but heat sensitivity persists in vampire bat TRPV1 variants lacking most of this region5,6. These inconsistencies raised the possibility that no single conserved domain underlies thermosensation. In that vein, Clapham and Miller proposed that temperature gating could arise from a large difference in heat capacity (ΔCp) between channel states, contributed by multiple elements within the channels rather than from a discrete temperature sensor7. This model is supported by a rationally-designed Shaker channel study, which demonstrated that temperature sensitivity can arise from changes in heat capacity from strategically-introduced residues rather than from a specialized domain8.

Structurally, some of the thermosensitive channels have been shown to respond to temperature by undergoing conformational rearrangements9,10 and others by employing temperature-dependent ligand binding11, although the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Beyond thermo-TRPs, temperature-dependent reversible unfolding of a cytoplasmic domain has also been shown to control voltage-dependent gating in a bacterial voltage-gated sodium channel12, highlighting an alternative structural mechanism by which temperature sensitivity can be encoded.

We proceeded to investigate the molecular basis of thermosensation from a different perspective, by employing SthK, an ion channel from Spirochaeta thermophila, as a model protein. This extreme thermophile survives within a temperature range of approximately 40–73 °C, with an optimal growth temperature around 65 °C13, which led us to hypothesize that SthK activity may be sensitive to temperature. SthK can be expressed in E. coli at high levels, producing large quantities of ion channel sample, lending itself to biophysical and biochemical investigations prohibitive to mammalian ion channels that express only in low amounts14. The possibility to isolate the pure ion channel from other cellular components also allows for the manipulation of the environmental lipid composition.

SthK is a bacterial homolog of mammalian cyclic nucleotide-gated (CNG) and hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-modulated (HCN) channels, sharing a canonical architecture that includes a K⁺-selective pore, a voltage-sensing domain (VSD), and a cyclic nucleotide-binding domain (CNBD) that responds to cAMP as a partial agonist14,15. SthK displays robust, ligand- and voltage-dependent gating and retains many mechanistic features of its eukaryotic counterparts. Notably, its activity is strongly modulated by lipid composition: anionic phospholipids such as phosphatidic acid (PA) and phosphatidylglycerol (PG) enhance activation, whereas phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP₂) inhibits gating by stabilizing the resting conformation16,17. These properties make SthK a tractable and informative model for dissecting the biophysical basis of thermosensitivity under chemically defined conditions. Motivated by this pronounced lipid sensitivity, we investigated whether the membrane also influences temperature-dependent channel behavior. Although thermosensation has been extensively studied in various ion channels, the role of the lipid environment in shaping thermal responses remains poorly understood. This is particularly relevant given that most thermoreceptors are membrane proteins and thus are embedded in dynamic lipid membranes that may modulate their gating energetics and sensitivity.

Here, we report that the SthK channel is indeed robustly sensitive to temperature, with higher activity at lower temperatures. Unexpectedly, its cold sensitivity is lipid-dependent: amine-containing lipids support activation at low temperatures, whereas anionic lipids abolish it. Given the sharp lipid headgroup-dependence, we investigated the possibility that temperature sensing involves an intersubunit, state-dependent salt bridge—previously shown to be important for gating energetics and sensitive to nearby anionic lipids16—as electrostatic interactions such as salt bridges in solution can weaken at low temperatures18–20. We indeed find that removal through mutagenesis, or weakening of this salt bridge led to elimination or pronounced shifts of temperature sensing, respectively, similar to the effects generated by incorporating different lipids. Lower temperatures, combined with amine-containing lipids, work together to weaken the salt bridge, thereby making SthK open more readily at lower temperatures. These findings reveal the physico-chemical mechanism by which this temperature-sensitive channel senses environmental temperature changes.

Results

SthK channels are temperature sensitive in specific lipids

To examine the temperature sensitivity of SthK, we purified SthK channels from E. coli, reconstituted them in liposomes of different lipid compositions, and measured channel activity using a single-mixing stopped-flow assay over a range of temperatures from 10 to 45 °C (Fig. 1a). In this assay, the rate of intraliposomal fluorescence quenching via Tl+ influx through open channels is an indicator of SthK activity21,22. Both the protein reconstitution efficiency and the size of proteoliposomes with different lipid compositions were similar across the experimental conditions tested (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b).

The activity of SthK depends on cAMP binding and lipid composition, with higher activity in the presence of anionic lipids16. We first tested whether SthK, incorporated into liposomes composed of 3:1 DOPC:POPE lipids and in the presence of cAMP, was temperature sensitive over a temperature range of 20–45 °C. Surprisingly, instead of increasing with temperature, channel activity rose as the sample was cooled to 20 °C, as indicated by higher influx rates (Fig. 1b, c). We thus extended our investigations to lower temperatures and found that channel activity continued to increase with decreasing temperatures, plateauing below 16 °C (Fig. 1b, c). These results indicate that SthK is a cold-sensitive ion channel, with a notable temperature coefficient (Q₁₀; see “Methods” for definition) of 6.8 in DOPC:POPE lipids (Fig. 1c). Although this is below the extremely large Q10 values observed for thermosensors such as TRPV123, it is nevertheless comparable to that reported for several well-established thermosensitive TRP channels. For example, among reported Q10 values are 9.9 for TRPV424, 8.8 for rat snake TRPA125, and 6.6 for TRPV326.

The above experiments were performed in the presence of cAMP. To investigate whether cooling alone can activate the channel, we tested channel activity in the absence of cAMP after prolonged exposures to low temperatures and found no increase in activity even with extended incubation (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Moreover, in liposomes reconstituted with SthK but without cAMP, we consistently observed negligible activity across all tested temperatures and lipid compositions (Supplementary Fig. 2), suggesting that vesicles did not become leaky at low temperatures. When cAMP was present, prolonged exposure to low temperatures did not result in either activity loss or desensitization (Supplementary Fig. 1c). To assess whether high temperatures caused irreversible damage to the protein, we re-cooled samples previously heated to 42 °C and remeasured activity (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Channel activity remained unchanged, indicating that the decrease in activity at higher temperatures was not due to protein degradation.

We next asked whether this cold sensitivity depended on the lipid environment. In both DOPC-only liposomes and DOPC liposomes supplemented with POPS (DOPC:POPS 3:1), SthK similarly exhibited pronounced cold sensitivity between 13 and 28 °C (Fig. 1d), with a midpoint (Tmid) around 20 °C across the tested lipid compositions (see “Methods” and Supplementary Table 1). Conversely, when DOPC liposomes were supplemented with POPA, POPG, or 18:1 cardiolipin (CL) at a 3:1 ratio, SthK activity displayed little temperature sensitivity over the temperature range investigated (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 2). Reconstitution of SthK into a more complex lipid composition (PC:PE:PG)—used here as a bacterial membrane proxy given that the native lipid composition of S. thermophila remains unknown and that PE and PG are the predominant phospholipids in E. coli membranes27—shifted the temperature-sensitive window further (Tmid = 28.8 °C, Supplementary Fig. 3), compared with PC:PE (Tmid = 20.6 °C) and PC:PG, for which a midpoint temperature could not be defined (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1), and was accompanied by increased channel activity at lower temperatures.

The above results support the conclusion that SthK is temperature sensitive and that its temperature sensitivity depends on lipid composition. We next investigated the molecular mechanism for thermosensitivity in these channels.

Lipids with amine-containing headgroups render SthK cold sensitive

We first investigated whether the SthK sensitivity to cAMP changes with temperature by measuring the cAMP EC₅₀ at 13, 22, and 28 °C. Although a modest increase in EC₅₀ was observed at 28 °C, the values at 13 and 22 °C were statistically indistinguishable (Supplementary Fig. 4), despite a threefold difference in channel activity between these temperatures (Fig. 1c). Thus, temperature-dependent changes in SthK sensitivity to cAMP are not a primary driver of the observed thermosensitivity in SthK.

POPE has a melting temperature (Tm) of 25 °C, close to the temperature range where SthK becomes cold sensitive. Although POPE constituted only 25% of the total lipid composition, with the majority being DOPC (Tm = −17 °C), we considered the possibility that lipid phase transition might contribute to temperature sensitivity. To test this, we replaced POPE with DOPE (Tm = −16 °C, well below the cold-sensitive range) and found that SthK remained cold sensitive, with a Q₁₀ of 8.1 (Fig. 2a), effectively ruling out lipid phase transition as the underlying cause.

We next asked whether specific chemical features of the lipid headgroups contribute to SthK cold sensitivity. PC, PS, and PE all have amine-containing headgroups (Supplementary Fig. 5). To investigate whether the amine group is essential for SthK cold sensitivity, we reconstituted SthK in DOPC supplemented with 18:1 phosphatidylpropanol (PPro, Fig. 2b). DOPE and PPro have similar structures that differ by only one atom; PPro has a methyl group in place of the amine in PE. In PPro-containing liposomes, the cold sensitivity of SthK is completely abolished (Fig. 2c), indicating that the amine group is indeed essential for SthK cold sensitivity. To enable direct comparison of temperature sensitivity across lipid environments, Q₁₀ values were statistically compared (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Structure of SthK in PE-containing lipid nanodiscs

To explore the interaction between the lipid amine and SthK at lower temperatures, we reconstituted SthK in the presence of cAMP in lipid nanodiscs containing 1:1 DOPC:DOPE lipids and plunge-froze the grids at 6 °C for single-particle cryo-EM analysis. Two distinct conformations were identified by the data processing algorithms (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 2), both very similar to previously reported closed SthK structures15,16, as well as to each other, with a Cα RMSD of 0.41 Å for the transmembrane domain (TMD) and 0.42 Å for the cyclic nucleotide-binding domain (CNBD) when aligned separately. The difference between them lies in a slight relative rotation between the CNBD and the TMD (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Because the rotation is in the same direction as that observed in the previously described pre-open state (Supplementary Fig. 8b)28, we designated the two conformations as closed and intermediate-closed. Both structures exhibit a closed pore consistent with a non-conducting state (Supplementary Fig. 8c), despite conditions intended to open the channel. This was likely due to the fact that despite the several-fold increase in activity in PE-containing lipids upon lowering the temperature, the overall open population remains still too low (~ 1%) for structural identification (previous single-channel recordings at room temperature yielded Po ~ 0.001 at 0 mV in 25% PE16). Due to their similarity, for the analysis that follows, we will only consider one of the structures, the closed conformation.

An examination of the lipid densities bound near gating-sensitive locations revealed a lipid bound near an intersubunit salt bridge formed by residues Arg136 and Asp226 (Fig. 3a, b), previously reported to modulate the stability of the SthK channel closed states16. The lipid density is distinct, with two carbon tails and a continuous lipid head group, and we modeled it as a PE molecule (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 8d). The salt bridge is state-dependent: the R136–D226 distance is short in the closed conformation and large in the open conformation (Fig. 3c)16. Since the salt bridge forms in the closed state and needs to break for the channel to open, its strength is thus expected to modulate the gating equilibrium, with the rate of channel opening (kopen in Fig. 3c) being dependent on the salt bridge strength. Salt bridge strength can be modulated by many factors, such as distance between the charges, other charged residues or lipids nearby, ionic strength, and dielectric constant, but also, importantly, temperature. Temperature has been shown to modulate salt bridge strength via changes in the solvent dielectric constant and desolvation penalty (Fig. 3d). Both factors contribute to weakening salt bridges at lower temperature, although the desolvation penalty was proposed to be the major contributor18–20,29.

Given the dependence of the temperature sensitivity on the lipid headgroup, we also determined a cryo-EM structure of SthK reconstituted in PC-only nanodiscs under otherwise comparable conditions (Supplementary Fig. 9). A clear lipid density bound near the salt bridge, larger than that seen in PE conditions, is observed in the PC-only nanodiscs structure, to accommodate the bulkier choline headgroup (Supplementary Fig. 8e). In addition, in contrast to PE-containing nanodiscs, in which both closed and intermediate-closed conformations were observed, SthK adopts exclusively a closed conformation in the PC-only environment (Cα RMSD = 0.344 Å to the PE closed conformation and 0.580 Å to the PE intermediate-closed conformation). This observation indicates that, in the presence of PE, SthK is biased toward populating the intermediate-closed conformation at lower temperatures and is more prone to opening. This structural trend is consistent with our functional measurements showing robust channel activity and pronounced cold sensitivity of SthK in PE-containing membranes.

In summary, lower temperatures can weaken the salt bridge, destabilize the closed state to open the channel, thus increasing channel activity. Based on this, we hypothesized that the R136–D226 is the temperature sensor of SthK. This hypothesis is consistent with our finding that amine-containing headgroup lipids support temperature-dependent channel activity; the amine group (positively charged) of the lipid is within interaction distance of the side chain of D226 (negatively charged), in a position to electrostatically weaken the salt bridge. This is reminiscent of the previously reported interaction between the negatively charged phosphate of a PA lipid binding at the same location and the positively charged R136, which was shown to effectively eliminate the R136–D226 salt bridge interaction16. We therefore proceeded to test our hypothesis that this intersubunit salt bridge serves as the temperature sensor of SthK, and that its weakening by amine-containing lipids underlies its cold sensitivity.

The intersubunit salt bridge as the cold sensor of SthK

If the intersubunit salt bridge serves as the cold sensor of SthK, disrupting it by mutating either Arg136 or Asp226 should abolish temperature sensitivity. Attempts to express Arg136 mutation failed, even in an adenylate cyclase-knockout E. coli strain (C43 cyaA−)30. In contrast, the Asp226-to-Asn substitution (D226N) yielded reasonable expression levels, as previously reported16. We indeed found that cold sensitivity was completely abolished in SthK D226N in PE-containing liposomes, and the mutant channel activity in the presence of cAMP was high, similar to that observed in SthK WT in PE-containing liposomes at low temperatures (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 10). However, we cannot exclude the possibility that SthK D226N is maximally active due to the loss of the salt bridge, thereby masking any temperature-elicited activity increases. Hence, we next sought a milder disruption of the salt bridge as opposed to breaking it completely.

Lys229 (K229) is one helical turn away from D226 (Fig. 4b). In SthK WT, the side chain of K229 is relatively far from the side chain of D226 (4.9 Å). We hypothesized that a longer arginine side chain at the same position (K229R) may get within interaction distance of D226, and thereby weaken the R136–D226 salt bridge. Such focused manipulation of the temperature sensor is predicted to perturb rather than eliminate the temperature sensitivity of SthK.

We examined the activity of SthK K229R in liposomes containing only PC or supplemented with PS or PE (Fig. 4c, left). The activity of SthK K229R was overall higher than WT in the presence of saturating cAMP in these lipids (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 10), with no activity in the absence of cAMP, indicating that the mutant channel functions normally, as a cAMP-gated channel. However, the midpoint temperature (Tmid) in each of these lipid compositions was shifted by approximately 10 °C towards an increased sensitivity to cold temperatures (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Table 1). This observed shift in activation threshold is consistent with our hypothesis that the K229R salt bridge is weakened, reflecting the inherently temperature-dependent energetic contribution of this interaction. Consequently, the destabilization of the closed state in the mutant enables channel opening at less cool temperatures than WT. Interestingly, in the presence of anionic lipids such as PG or CL, where SthK WT showed no temperature sensitivity, SthK K229R displays a large increase in activity at cold temperature (Fig. 4c, right). We attribute this to a weakening of the intersubunit salt bridge, jointly caused by the arginine substitution (K229R) and the presence of anionic lipids16. Together, these factors destabilize the closed state and enhance channel activity at lower temperatures. In the presence of PA, no observable change in activity was noted, likely because the disruption of the intersubunit salt bridge by the small head group of PA is already maximal even in SthK WT16. Taken together, our results showed that the intersubunit salt bridge R136–D226 serves as the cold sensor of SthK, and perturbation of this salt bridge with either mutations or via the type of lipid bound near the salt bridge alters the temperature sensitivity of SthK.

Discussion

In this study, we report the molecular mechanism underlying temperature sensing by the SthK ion channel and its physical basis (Fig. 5a). We found that SthK is a cold-sensing ion channel, exhibiting higher activity at lower temperatures in the presence of cAMP. The cold sensitivity of SthK is lipid-dependent, showing particular sensitivity in the presence of amine-containing lipids such as PC, PS, or PE. Through structural and functional studies, we demonstrated that the intersubunit salt bridge R136–D226 acts as the temperature sensor of SthK. Our results show that the intersubunit salt bridge is weakened by lipid amines and further compromised by low temperatures, resulting in channel activity increasing at temperatures below 30 °C. Among amine-containing lipids, SthK shows particularly strong cold activation in the presence of PE compared to PC or PS. This may reflect the smaller and less bulky geometry of the PE head group (Supplementary Fig. 5), which could fit more snugly in the binding site and thus more effectively destabilize the salt bridge and enhance cold sensitivity.

This highlights the ease of tuning temperature sensitivity of channels via this mechanism by either lipids with different headgroups bound near the salt bridge or by having select residues located within interaction distance of the salt bridge.

The R136–D226 salt bridge is located at the water–lipid interface, rendering it sensitive not only to the chemical nature of the lipid headgroups but also to the surrounding solvent environment (Supplementary Fig. 8f). Prior studies have suggested that temperature-dependent changes in solvent dielectric constant may weaken salt bridges at lower temperatures, as electrostatic attraction decreases with increasing dielectric constant29. However, such effects are modest within the temperature range of our system (e.g., the dielectric constant for water changes from 76.5 at 30 °C to 83.8 at 10 °C31) and likely contribute only marginally to the cold sensitivity. In contrast, the desolvation penalty—the energetic cost of dehydrating charged side chains to form a salt bridge—was proposed to be more strongly temperature-dependent18. Previous studies have shown that hydration is stronger at lower temperatures, leading to a higher desolvation penalty and greater energetic cost for solvent removal, which in turn disfavors salt bridge formation18–20. While it can be debated whether a single salt bridge per subunit (four in total) can account for the full energetics of temperature gating, its weakening facilitates channel opening at low temperatures, although only in the presence of cAMP, indicating that it modulates rather than drives gating.

The mechanism proposed here may provide a template for future investigations into other cold thermoreceptors that feature state-dependent salt bridges. In TRPM8, a previously-studied cold thermoreceptor, an intersubunit salt bridge between Arg851 and Asp866 in mouse TRPM8 was observed in the closed state structure but not in the open-state structure, located near the membrane interface, raising the possibility that it too may be modulated by lipid and solvent effects32. Indeed, not only is this salt bridge involved in PIP2 binding, but altering its strength by mutating R851 was reported to reduce cold sensitivity32,33. The convergence of state dependence and lipid sensitivity at this site raises the possibility that TRPM8 may share a similar lipid-modulated cold-sensing mechanism with SthK. Although cold and heat thermoreceptors exhibit opposite trends in response to temperature changes—cold-sensing thermoreceptors become more active as temperature decreases, while heat-sensing thermoreceptors become less active—the underlying principles governing salt bridge dynamics may be shared. For instance, heat-activation in thermoreceptors might involve the strengthening of a strategically positioned salt bridge, stabilizing the open state and promoting channel activity at elevated temperatures.

While the process of salt bridge weakening at lower temperatures has been previously reported, most of these reports are based on in silico studies18–20,34–36. Experimental evidence is limited. To the best of our knowledge, only one other study has demonstrated this phenomenon: the dissociation of L-xylulose reductase (XR) from tetramer to dimer at low temperatures, attributed to weakening of an intersubunit salt bridge at the subunit interface.37. The tendency of salt bridges to weaken at lower temperatures is also reflected in nature. Proteins from thermophilic organisms typically contain more salt bridges than those from mesophilic species, which enhances thermal stability by reinforcing intramolecular interactions at high temperatures38–42. However, this same feature often makes thermophilic proteins less stable at ambient temperatures, as the higher number of salt bridges, while stabilizing at elevated temperatures, can become marginal, or even destabilizing, when electrostatic interactions weaken at lower temperatures41,42. Although the temperature dependence of salt bridge strength has long been reported and illustrated in nature, this concept has not been applied to thermosensation. Here, we take advantage of a model ion channel to provide a framework for understanding how salt bridge energetics can underlie temperature sensing.

The intersubunit salt bridge between R136 and D226 was previously shown to also be susceptible to disruption by anionic lipids16. PA robustly activates SthK across a broad temperature range, rendering the channel highly active, which could mask any further temperature-induced increases. PG and CL also show no temperature dependence, but they increase channel activity to a much lesser extent than PA16, which would argue against high channel activity masking the temperature effect. In addition, these lipids were proposed to destabilize the salt bridge via their charged headgroup, similar to the amine-containing lipids, so why don’t they also confer temperature sensitivity to SthK? A previous computational study reported that PG-containing membranes not only promote stronger arginine–lipid headgroup interactions than PC-only bilayers but also exhibit a localized enrichment of cationic counter-ions at the membrane interface, enhancing local electrolyte screening43. We speculate that anionic lipids such as PG or CL give rise to a more electrostatically shielded interfacial environment, where the charged residues experience reduced sensitivity to solvent fluctuations. As a result, the stability of the salt bridge becomes less influenced by temperature-dependent changes in hydration, diminishing its role in mediating temperature sensitivity.

The salt bridge–weakening mutation K229R results in a higher overall Q₁₀ in the presence of amine-containing lipids (PC, PS, or PE), and markedly increased channel activity at lower temperatures in the presence of anionic lipids such as PG and CL, although the Q₁₀ values remain relatively low (Q₁₀ = 1.8 and 1.5, respectively). Notably, SthK exhibits an inverse thermal response—higher activity at lower temperatures—which contrasts with the temperature dependence of ion diffusion, where mobility increases with temperature (Q₁₀ = 1.3–1.5 for diffusion of ions alone)44. As a result, the observed Q₁₀ for SthK may underestimate the intrinsic temperature sensitivity of its gating, since the reduced rate of ion diffusion at lower temperatures partially offsets the increase in channel activity under those conditions.

In addition to lipid headgroup chemistry, acyl-chain properties also influence the temperature sensitivity of SthK. We observed modest differences in channel activity and apparent Q₁₀ values between POPE- and DOPE-containing membranes, where the thinner DOPE bilayers support higher channel activity (Fig. 2a). Differences in bilayer thickness between POPE and DOPE membranes may contribute to these effects, because a thinner bilayer may increase water exposure of the interfacial salt bridge at the lipid–water interface, weakening the interaction through electrostatic screening and thereby increasing channel activity, potentially further supporting our hypothesis. Nevertheless, in addition to altering bilayer thickness, changes in lipid tail length may also alter other membrane physical properties, including membrane fluidity, hydrophobic mismatch between the transmembrane helices and the bilayer, and the lateral pressure profile. All these effects have been reported to modulate ion channel function45. Together, these observations suggest that the temperature-dependent behavior of SthK is governed by the combined physical properties of the surrounding membrane.

The temperature sensor identified here for SthK—a state-dependent intersubunit salt bridge—is highly conserved in HCN channels at both the sequence and structural levels16,46. For example, Decher et al. reported an analogous intersubunit salt bridge in the HCN2 channel that couples the S4–S5 linker to the C-linker and plays a critical role in stabilizing the closed state and regulating channel gating46; this suggests that HCN channels may share a related structural mechanism with SthK. However, HCN channels have not been identified as cold thermoreceptors: whole-cell recordings of human HCN2 expressed in HEK293 cells showed reduced activity at lower temperatures47, which is inconsistent with cold-sensing properties. However, our findings with SthK suggest that thermosensitivity is highly contingent on the surrounding lipid environment, and the complex and heterogeneous membrane composition of HEK293 cells may mask a putative thermosensitive behavior of HCN channels. This raises the possibility that, in select lipid environments, HCN channels may indeed respond to temperature. Future studies may offer broader insights into how membrane composition shapes thermal responsiveness in ion channels and open avenues for targeting temperature-sensitive channels in physiological and pathological contexts.

The larger increase in activity at lower temperatures and the considerably higher temperature midpoint displayed in SthK K229R, compared to the extent to which lipid composition modulates WT SthK, may reflect the inherent flexibility of bound lipids and their limited ability to destabilize the salt bridge, relative to the consistently positioned K229R side chain. This way, the presence of the K229R side chain with its more persistent destabilizing effect on the salt bridge promotes channel activation. Such a specific shift in temperature threshold indicates that the energetic contribution of this interaction is intrinsically temperature-dependent, underscoring its potential role in thermosensation. While these observations support a model in which salt bridge destabilization contributes to temperature sensitivity, the mechanism proposed here may be oversimplified. Mutations and lipids likely modulate salt bridge stability through distinct physical principles, including differences in spatial persistence, interaction dynamics, and the local hydration environment associated with salt bridge formation. Further studies will be required to disentangle these potentially divergent effects.

The temperature sensitivity of SthK is lipid-dependent, and its range can be further shifted by mutations. In wild-type SthK, the temperature-sensitive window is 10–30 °C, whereas in K229R it expands to 20–40 °C (Fig. 5b), further approaching the survival range of Spirochaeta thermophila (40–73 °C)13, the bacterium from which SthK was identified. These findings suggest that SthK’s thermal sensitivity is influenced not only by residues near the salt bridge but also by its surrounding chemical and physical environment, which may differ substantially under physiological conditions. Consistent with this view, reconstitution of SthK into a more complex lipid composition (PC:PE:PG)—used here as a bacterial membrane proxy given that the native lipid composition of S. thermophila remains unknown and that PE and PG are the predominant phospholipids in E. coli membranes27—shifted the temperature-sensitive window further toward the physiological range (Tmid = 28.8 °C, Supplementary Fig. 3), compared with PC:PE (Tmid = 20.6 °C). Taken together, these observations suggest that temperature-dependent salt bridge formation may be strongly influenced by both sequence variation and the surrounding lipid environment.

The ability of SthK and, presumably, other temperature-sensitive ion channels to respond to temperature may therefore arise from a finely tuned balance between structural elements and the membrane environment. Not only must the salt bridge possess an intermediate strength—neither too strong to resist thermal modulation nor too weak to contribute meaningfully to gating—but its sensitivity can also be easily masked by the surrounding lipid composition. For example, lipid environments lacking destabilizing headgroups, such as the absence of amine-containing lipids examined here, may stabilize the salt bridge and obscure any thermally induced gating effects. Conversely, excessive destabilization could prevent the salt bridge from forming altogether, eliminating its contribution to channel function. Thus, as with the Goldilocks principle, only through the combined interplay of salt bridge properties and membrane context can the system reach the just-right conditions needed for temperature sensitivity.

Here, we show that SthK ion channel senses temperature through a lipid-modulated intersubunit salt bridge (R136–D226) located at the membrane interface. We demonstrate that weakening of this salt bridge at lower temperatures, particularly in the presence of amine-containing lipids, promotes channel opening and cold activation. The temperature-sensing gating via salt bridge interaction strength modulation may only emerge under finely tuned structural and environmental conditions—an energetic Goldilocks zone where the interaction is neither too strong nor too weak. Identifying such a mechanism in SthK not only uncovers a form of lipid-dependent thermosensitivity but also provides a framework for exploring similarly constrained temperature-sensing strategies in other ion channels.

Methods

Expression and purification of SthK

Protein expression and purification was performed as previously described14. E. coli cells were grown in LB media at 37 °C, supplemented with ampicillin. Upon reaching OD600 of 0.4, the temperature was lowered to 20 °C for further growth until OD600 reached 0.8. Protein expression was initiated by adding 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactoside (IPTG). Cells were harvested after 12–14 h through centrifugation at 6000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was resuspended in breaking buffer (50 mM Tris, 100 mM KCl, pH 8) with 1 mg of lysozyme (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mg of DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich), 85 μg ml−1 PMSF (Roche), Leupeptin (0.95 μg ml−1, Roche), Pepstatin (1.4 μg ml−1, Roche), and ultra cOmplete protease inhibitor (Roche), and further disrupted by sonication. To extract SthK from cell membrane, lysate was supplemented with 30 mM DDM (n-dodecyl-β-d-maltopyranoside, Anatrace) and incubated for 1.5 h at 4 °C with constant agitation. The insoluble fraction was removed by centrifugation at 37,500 × g for 50 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and loaded onto a 5 ml Hitrap column (Cytiva) charged with Co2+. The column was washed with 75 ml of wash buffer (20 mM HEPES, 100 mM KCl, 100 µM cAMP, 30 mM imidazole, 0.5 mM DDM, pH 7.8) and SthK was eluted with 10 ml of elution buffer (20 mM HEPES, 100 mM KCl, 100 µM cAMP, 300 mM imidazole, 0.5 mM DDM, pH 7.8). The protein was concentrated to about 15 mg ml−1 and further purified by gel filtration on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) in 20 mM HEPES, 100 mM KCl, 0.5 mM DDM, pH 7.4. The SthK fraction was pooled, concentrated to about 10 mg ml−1, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for future use. All purification steps were performed at 4 °C.

Mutations in SthK were introduced by site-directed mutagenesis using Q5 polymerase (New England Biolabs) and the following primers: D226N-forward: CTC GTC TCG AAA CTG AAT GCC GCA AAG CTC CTC, D226N-reverse: GAG GAG CTT TGC GGC ATT CAG TTT CGA GAC GAG, K229R-forward: AAA CTG GAC GCC GCA AGG CTC CTC CAC, and K229R-reverse: CTC CCT GTG GAG GAG CCT TGC GGC GTC. Due to lower expression levels observed in SthK mutants, E. coli C43 cyaA− cells were used for protein expression30,48. Cells were grown in terrific broth media at 37 °C until reaching an OD600 of 1, followed by induction with 0.5 mM IPTG at 20 °C for 12–14 h. The subsequent protein purification steps were carried out as for SthK WT.

Preparation of proteoliposomes for stopped-flow assay

For single-mixing stopped-flow Tl+ influx assay, purified SthK was reconstituted into large unilamellar vesicles encapsulating the fluorophore ANTS (8-aminonaphthalene-1,3,6-trisulfonic, Life Technologies) as previously described21,22,49. Initially, 10 mg of lipids in chloroform were dried to a thin film in a glass tube under a gentle N2 gas stream, followed by overnight drying under vacuum. The dried lipids were rehydrated in 743 μl reconstitution buffer (10 mM HEPES, 140 mM KNO3, pH 7.4) supplemented with 50 mM CHAPS detergent (3-((3-cholamidopropyl) dimethylammonio)-1-propanesulfonate). The resulting solution was sonicated until transparent. Subsequently, 300 μg of purified SthK and 371 μl of ANTS solution (reconstitution buffer supplemented with 75 mM ANTS, pH 7.4) were added and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. An additional 667 μl ANTS solution was added to reach a final concentration of 25 mM. Detergent was removed by addition of 667 mg of BioBeads (SM-2, BioRad) as a 50% slurry in reconstitution buffer, followed by a 3-h incubation under constant agitation at 21 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a new glass tube and stored at 13 °C overnight. The next day, liposomes were briefly sonicated in a water bath sonicator, extruded through a 100 nm filter (mini extruder, Avanti Polar Lipids), and external ANTS was removed passing through a PD-10 desalting column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with reconstitution buffer. To eliminate any trace amount of external ANTS, only the initial 3 ml of the eluted fraction from desalting column was collected. The eluted fraction was further diluted with reconstitution buffer to a final lipid concentration of 0.227 mg ml−1 for the following single-mixing stopped-flow experiments.

Nanodisc reconstitution for cryo-EM structural determination

To eliminate endogenous lipids that may tightly bind to SthK, the buffers used for immobilized metal ion affinity chromatography and size-exclusion chromatography were supplemented with 0.02 mg ml−1 DOPE, facilitating efficient lipid exchange. The membrane scaffold protein MSP1E3 was prepared according to established methods50 and concentrated to 15 mg ml−1 in reconstitution buffer (10 mM HEPES, 100 mM KCl, pH 7.4). A lipid stock solution of DOPC:DOPE at a 1:1 ratio was prepared at 100 mM, solubilized in reconstitution buffer supplemented with 200 mM sodium cholate (Anatrace). The nanodisc reconstitution was conducted in a 250 μl volume, aiming for a final SthK concentration of 100 μM. SthK, MSP1E3, and lipids were mixed at a molar ratio of 1:1.1:70 in reconstitution buffer supplemented with 3 mM cAMP, followed by a 20-min incubation at room temperature. Detergent removal commenced with the addition of 100 mg BioBeads (BioRad) equilibrated in reconstitution buffer. Samples were gently agitated and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C, with subsequent transfer of the supernatant to a new tube containing 100 mg BioBeads. Further incubation at 4 °C overnight under gentle agitation was performed. The pooled supernatant was combined with a wash of the BioBeads using a total of 250 μl reconstitution buffer. The 500 μl sample was filtered through a Spin-X column (Costar) and loaded onto a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL gel filtration column (Cytiva) equilibrated in reconstitution buffer supplemented with 200 μM cAMP. The peak corresponding to nanodiscs containing SthK was collected and concentrated to approximately 8 mg ml−1 (1 absorbance (A280) = 1 mg). The final sample was supplemented with cAMP to a final concentration of 3 mM and fluorinated Fos-choline-8 (Anatrace) to a final concentration of 3 mM in reconstitution buffer. For PC-only nanodiscs, an identical procedure was used, except that DOPE was omitted from the purification buffers and DOPC was used as the sole lipid during nanodisc reconstitution.

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data collection

UltrAu-Foil R1.2/1.3 300-mesh gold grids (Quantifoil) were glow-discharged for 80 s. A volume of 3.5 μl of ice-cold sample was applied to the grid, followed by a 30 s incubation at 6 °C and 100% humidity. Subsequently, the grid was blotted for 2.5 s with 0 blot force and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI, Thermo Fisher). Identical grid preparation and vitrification conditions were used for both PC-only and PE-containing nanodisc samples.

Data acquisition was carried out using a Titan Krios microscope (FEI, Thermo Fisher) operated at 300 kV, equipped with a Gatan K3 imaging system at ×105,000 nominal magnification. The calibrated pixel size was set at 0.4125 Å (super resolution). For PE-containing nanodiscs, a 20 eV energy filter slit width was used, and videos were recorded at a dose rate of 26.89 e−/Å2 per s with a total exposure of 1.80 s, resulting in accumulative dose of 48.40 e−/Å2 using Leginon51. For PC-only nanodiscs, data were collected using a 15 eV energy filter slit width, with a dose rate of 26.83 e−/Å² per s and the same total exposure time of 1.80 s, resulting in a cumulative dose of 48.29 e−/Å². In both cases, frames were recorded every 0.05 s for a total of 40 frames per micrograph, with data collected over a nominal defocus range of 0.4–2.5 µm for PE-containing nanodiscs and 0.6–2.5 µm for PC-only nanodiscs.

Cryo-EM data processing

Cryo-EM data processing was carried out using Relion-4.052. A total of 7118 movies were processed for PE-containing nanodiscs, which were motion-corrected and binned three times. For PC-only nanodiscs, data were processed using the same workflow, with an initial dataset comprising 7458 movies. CTF estimation without dose-weighting of the micrographs was performed with CTFFIND 4.153. Subsequently, 1,383,889 particles were picked for PE-containing nanodiscs using crYOLO54 after fine-tuning the general model for SthK and extracting them with a 256-pixel box size. For PC-only nanodiscs, 1,378,978 particles were picked using the same procedure. Junk particles were removed through 2D classification, followed by 3D refinement using an ab-initio SthK model as a reference in C1 before further sorting particles by 3D classification without alignment. The selected particles underwent multiple rounds of CTF refinement, Bayesian polishing, and 3D refinement in C4, as no asymmetry was observed. For PE-containing nanodiscs, both closed and intermediate-closed conformations were resolved and selected after an additional round of 3D classification in C4 without alignment, whereas only a single closed state population was observed and selected for PC-only nanodiscs.

Iterative CTF refinement and Bayesian polishing were performed for each state until the resolution converged. To prevent overfitting, SIDESPLITTER55 was integrated into all subsequent 3D refinements for the closed and intermediate-closed states. For PC-only nanodiscs, SIDESPLITTER was applied during refinement of the closed state following the same strategy. Particles were unbinned during Bayesian polishing to a pixel size of 0.9 Å/px. This resulted in a final resolution of 2.6 Å for the closed state from 79,604 particles and 2.8 Å for the intermediate-closed from 113,615 particles for PE-containing nanodiscs, and 2.5 Å for the closed state from 377,840 particles for PC-only nanodiscs, based on the gold standard Fourier shell correlation cut-off at 0.143. Local resolution was calculated in Relion.

Model building and validation

For model building of both the closed and intermediate-closed states, a previously published SthK structure (PDB: 7TJ5) was docked into the final cryo-EM density maps using ChimeraX56 and manually adjusted in COOT57. Residues that could not be resolved in previous structures were added, and the resulting coordinates were real-space refined in PHENIX58,59. cAMP and lipids were manually added to each model in COOT, and outliers were fixed. The final models were validated using MolProbity60. Pore diagrams were made using HOLE and Cα RMSD calculations were performed using ChimeraX56,61. All structural figures were prepared using ChimeraX.
