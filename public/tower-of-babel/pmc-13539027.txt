Context-dependent roles of osteopontin in aging-related neurological disorders
Authors: Dong Huang, Zhen Zhang, Xili Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteopontin (encoded by secreted phosphoprotein 1) is a multifunctional matricellular phosphoglycoprotein that has emerged as an important mediator in the aging nervous system. During aging, osteopontin interacts with microglial priming, vascular remodeling, myelin repair, and innate immune responses and is consistently implicated in major late-life neurological disorders. However, its biological effects are highly context dependent. Depending on its cellular source, proteolytic processing, receptor interactions, anatomical distribution, and disease stage, osteopontin may either exacerbate chronic neuroinflammation and tissue injury or promote phagocytic clearance, neuronal survival, remyelination, neuroplasticity, and tissue repair. This review critically synthesizes studies indexed in PubMed and Google Scholar through 3 April 2026 on the role of osteopontin in brain aging, Alzheimer's disease and related dementias, Parkinson's disease and Lewy body disorders, cerebrovascular disease, vascular cognitive impairment, cerebral small vessel disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. In Alzheimer's disease, cerebrospinal fluid and plasma osteopontin concentrations increase from the prodromal to symptomatic stages, whereas microglial or perivascular secreted phosphoprotein 1 expression correlates with amyloid pathology, synaptic remodeling, and cognitive decline. However, under specific conditions, osteopontin also enhances macrophage-mediated amyloid-beta clearance. In stroke, elevated circulating osteopontin predicts poor clinical outcomes, whereas experimental studies demonstrate that appropriately timed exogenous osteopontin, regulatory T cell-derived osteopontin, and osteopontin-mediated autophagic and reparative pathways promote white-matter repair, peri-infarct plasticity, and blood–brain barrier integrity. Evidence from Parkinson's disease, Lewy body disease, frontotemporal dementia, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis further supports the role of osteopontin as both a candidate biomarker and a regulator of selective neuronal vulnerability. Rather than being uniformly detrimental or protective, osteopontin should be regarded as a context-dependent regulator of age-related neuroimmune remodeling. This perspective reconciles seemingly conflicting findings and supports the development of therapeutic strategies that are tailored to disease stage, protein fragment, and cell type rather than broadly targeting osteopontin.Keywords: Osteopontin, aging, neuroinflammation, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, stroke, cerebral small vessel disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

Introduction

Population aging is reshaping the landscape of neurological disease. Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), cerebrovascular cognitive disorders, and many forms of motor system degeneration become substantially more prevalent with advancing age. However, these disorders are linked not only by chronology but also by a shared tissue environment characterized by inflammaging, dysregulated innate immunity, impaired proteostasis, vascular stiffening, myelin vulnerability, and a diminished capacity for coordinated repair. Within this context, osteopontin (OPN), also known as secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1), has emerged as a key molecular mediator linking immune activation to tissue remodeling.1–3

OPN has long been recognized as a cytokine-like matricellular protein with pleiotropic functions in cell adhesion, chemotaxis, survival signaling, and extracellular matrix organization. In the nervous system, it is produced by multiple cell types, including neurons, microglia, infiltrating macrophages, and perivascular cells, and its expression is markedly upregulated following injury and during chronic neurodegeneration.2,4 This broad inducibility initially led to its characterization as a nonspecific inflammatory marker. However, accumulating evidence supports a more nuanced role. Rather than serving solely as a byproduct of tissue injury, OPN actively regulates microglial state transitions, influences phagocytic activity, modulates cytokine production, governs synaptic engulfment, and participates in axon–glia–vascular crosstalk.5–9

This complexity is particularly relevant in the aging brain. Aging promotes the emergence of SPP1-expressing myeloid cell states, and accumulating evidence suggests that OPN may either exacerbate or mitigate age-related cognitive decline depending on the cellular context.10–13 In AD, elevated OPN levels in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), plasma, neurons, and disease-associated microglia and macrophages have been consistently reported. However, experimental studies have alternatively characterized OPN as a driver of plaque-associated neuroinflammation and as a facilitator of amyloid-beta (Abeta) clearance.14–24 Similarly, in stroke, elevated circulating OPN is frequently associated with greater injury severity and poorer clinical outcomes, whereas local OPN signaling promotes neuroblast migration, oligodendrocyte lineage repair, autophagy, and stabilization of the blood–brain barrier (BBB).25–33 Comparable context-dependent effects have also been described in PD, Lewy body disease, frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).34–41

A meaningful review of OPN in neurological aging therefore cannot be based on a simple “beneficial versus detrimental” dichotomy. Rather, the key unresolved questions concern when, where, in which molecular form, and through which receptor systems OPN exerts its effects. This review synthesizes the current evidence from that perspective. We first summarize the aspects of OPN biology most relevant to the aging nervous system, then critically evaluate its role across age-associated neurological disorders, and finally discuss future directions for biomarker standardization and the development of context-specific therapeutic strategies. This review was conducted in accordance with the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA). 42

OPN biology

OPN (Figures 1 and 2), encoded by SPP1 in humans and Spp1 in mice, is not merely an extracellular matrix constituent but a pleiotropic, proteoform-dependent matricellular cytokine that integrates mineral metabolism, wound repair, leukocyte positioning, and fibrosis.43–45 Human SPP1 is located on chromosome 4q22.1, whereas murine Spp1 is located on chromosome 5. The canonical human secreted protein comprises approximately 314 amino acids and belongs to the small integrin-binding ligand, N-linked glycoprotein (SIBLING) family of highly acidic, intrinsically disordered phosphoglycoproteins.43,46–48 Its lack of a stable tertiary structure permits extensive post-translational modification and proteolytic diversification. OPN contains a canonical arginine-glycine-aspartate (RGD) motif that engages αv-class integrins, a thrombin-exposed SVVYGLR/SLAYGLR motif that binds α4β1 and α9β1 integrins, and domains that interact with CD44 variants, calcium, and hydroxyapatite.49–55 Phosphorylation, largely mediated by secretory pathway kinases such as FAM20C, together with glycosylation, tyrosine sulfation, and transglutaminase-dependent cross-linking, modulates its affinity for minerals and receptors.56–61 OPN biology is therefore best understood at the level of its isoforms and proteolytic fragments rather than total SPP1 expression. At least five human splice variants have been identified: OPNa, the full-length transcript; OPNb, lacking exon 5; OPNc, lacking exon 4; OPN4, lacking exons 4 and 5; and OPN5, which contains an additional exon derived from intron 3 and exhibits cancer cell- and tissue-specific expression patterns.62–64 A second layer of diversification arises from alternative translation of the same Opn mRNA. Initiation at the upstream start codon produces secreted OPN, whereas initiation at a downstream start codon removes the signal peptide and generates intracellular OPN, which regulates dendritic cell and macrophage signaling programs rather than functioning as a secreted ligand.65,66 A third, pathobiologically important layer of diversification is proteolytic processing. Thrombin cleaves human OPN at Arg168↓Ser169 (the homologous cleavage site in mice is Arg153↓Ser154), thereby separating the N-terminal OPN-R fragment from the C-terminal fragment and exposing the cryptic SVVYGLR/SLAYGLR integrin-binding sequence. Subsequent removal of the C-terminal arginine by carboxypeptidase B2 or carboxypeptidase N generates OPN-L and attenuates α4β1/α9β1 integrin binding.67–70 Matrix metalloproteinases further remodel OPN. Matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP-3) and MMP-7 cleave OPN near Gly166↓Leu167 and Asp210↓Leu211, whereas MMP-3 also cleaves at Ala201↓Tyr202. Plasmin, cathepsin D, caspase-8, and other proteases generate additional fragments, making the local protease milieu a key determinant of OPN function.69–73 Mechanistic studies further support this proteoform-centered model. In cancer, tumor- and macrophage-derived SPP1 functions through autocrine and paracrine signaling loops. In breast cancer recurrence, it promotes tumor cell proliferation, macrophage recruitment, and interleukin-4 (IL-4)-dependent protumor macrophage polarization. In prostate cancer, SPP1-high tumor-associated macrophages suppress T-cell function through adenosine signaling and contribute to resistance to immune checkpoint blockade. In ovarian cancer and triple-negative breast cancer, SPP1–CD44- and integrin-mediated signaling pathways drive CD8-positive T-cell exhaustion, chemoresistance, and tumor growth.74–77 Importantly, proteolytic cleavage is not merely an incidental event. Thrombin-resistant OPN knock-in models and pharmacological inhibition of thrombin phenocopy OPN deficiency in melanoma, indicating that thrombin-cleaved OPN functions as a causal tumor-promoting ligand rather than a passive biomarker.67,70 In fibrotic disease, SPP1 marks and regulates pathogenic stromal–myeloid niches. Renal Spp1 promotes fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation through transforming growth factor-beta/small mothers against decapentaplegic (TGF-β/SMAD) signaling, whereas in liver fibrosis, thrombin-cleaved OPN activates hepatic stellate cells through α4/α9 integrins, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling. In addition, fibroblast growth factor 18 (FGF18)–SPP1 intercellular signaling links myofibroblasts to stellate cell activation. In pulmonary fibrosis, macrophage-derived SPP1–CD44 and integrin signaling converge to promote fibroblast activation, profibrotic macrophage polarization, and extracellular matrix deposition.78–83 In neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration, SPP1 acts as an instructive signal for microglial state transitions. Perivascular SPP1 promotes microglial C1qa/Ctsb expression, synapse engulfment, and Abeta-associated synapse loss in Alzheimer models, whereas thrombin-cleaved OPN regulates inflammatory activity and relapse in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, supporting a fragment-specific inflammatory mechanism.24,70,84,85 In autoimmune arthritis, thrombin cleavage exposes the SLAYGLR epitope, enabling α9/α4-dependent monocyte migration and synovial inflammation. Antibody-mediated blockade of this cryptic epitope suppresses synovial proliferation, bone erosion, and inflammatory-cell infiltration in experimental rheumatoid arthritis.68,86 OPN also exerts context-dependent effects in cardiovascular, skeletal, and metabolic diseases. In the vasculature, phosphorylated OPN inhibits vascular smooth muscle cell calcification and mineral deposition, whereas chronic plaque- and adipose tissue-derived SPP1 promotes macrophage accumulation and retention, inflammatory activation, and insulin resistance. In bone, OPN–αvβ3 signaling regulates osteoclast calcium signaling, adhesion, survival, and bone resorption.87–95 Collectively, OPN should be regarded as a dynamically processed signaling platform whose biological effects are determined by its cellular source, splice and translation isoforms, phosphorylation state, and protease-dependent receptor specificity. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting disease-relevant OPN proteoforms or specific proteolytic cleavage events are likely to offer greater precision than approaches based solely on total SPP1 expression.

MMP-mediated cleavage of OPN and its impact on integrin-recognition motifs.

OPN contains multiple protease-sensitive regions and integrin-binding motifs that are differentially exposed or disrupted after proteolytic processing. The classical RGD motif at residues 159–161 mediates binding to several RGD-dependent integrins, including αvβ3, αvβ5, αvβ1, α5β1, α8β1, and αvβ6. Adjacent to this region, the SVVYGLR sequence at residues 162–168 can interact with non-RGD integrins such as α4β1 and α9β1; however, cleavage by MMP-3 or MMP-7 between Gly166 and Leu167 disrupts the complete SVVYGLR motif, potentially reducing SVVYGLR-dependent integrin recognition while leaving RGD-dependent adhesion or migration intact or enhanced. Additional MMP cleavage events have been reported at Ala201/Tyr202 and Asp210/Leu211, with MMP-3 and MMP-7 cleaving at the latter site. Other proteases, including MMP-2, MMP-9, plasmin, and cathepsin D, may also generate OPN fragments, although the receptor-binding properties of many of these fragments remain incompletely defined. Thrombin-dependent generation and further processing of OPN fragments regulate integrin binding and inflammatory activity.

OPN-FL (amino acids 17–314) contains an α4β1-binding region, ELVTDFPTDLPAT at residues 131–143, an RGD motif at residues 159–161, and a cryptic or poorly accessible SVVYGLR motif at residues 162–168. Thrombin cleavage at Arg168–Ser169 generates the N-terminal OPN fragment, commonly referred to as OPN-R or OPN-N, in which SVVYGLR becomes exposed as a new C-terminal motif. This cleavage expands the integrin-binding repertoire of OPN: the RGD motif supports interactions with RGD-dependent integrins, whereas the exposed SVVYGLR motif promotes binding to α4β1, α9β1, and α4β7, thereby enhancing adhesion, migration or chemotaxis, inflammatory-cell recruitment, and cell survival. In contrast, the C-terminal thrombin fragment, OPN-C or OPN-CTF, lacks the ELVTDFPTDLPAT, RGD, and SVVYGLR motifs, resulting in loss of classical integrin-binding modes; its receptor interactions may be context dependent and remain incompletely resolved. Further processing of OPN-R by carboxypeptidase B2/activated thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor removes the C-terminal Arg168 residue to generate OPN-L, markedly attenuating SVVYGLR-dependent binding and reducing thrombin-enhanced inflammatory activity. Sources and selection criteria of articles

We searched PubMed and Google Scholar from database inception to 3 April 2026 using combinations of the following terms: “osteopontin,” “OPN,” “SPP1,” “aging,” “brain aging,” “microglia,” “Alzheimer disease,” “Parkinson disease,” “Lewy body disease,” “frontotemporal dementia,” “stroke,” “ischemic stroke,” “intracerebral hemorrhage,” “subarachnoid hemorrhage,” “cerebral small vessel disease,” “vascular cognitive impairment,” and “amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.” Priority was given to primary mechanistic studies, human biomarker studies, omics analyses, and recent systematic reviews. The reference lists of relevant articles were also screened to identify additional eligible studies. To minimize factual drift, we included only studies that were retrievable through PubMed or Google Scholar and provided sufficient methodological detail to support disease-specific interpretation. Given the heterogeneity of the available evidence with respect to species, biospecimens, analytical platforms, and disease stages, this article is presented as a critical narrative review rather than a formal meta-analysis (Table 1).

Biology of OPN in the aging nervous system

Molecular forms and receptor systems

OPN is a highly modified phosphoglycoprotein that functions as both an extracellular matrix-associated protein and a soluble immunomodulator. Although research in the nervous system has focused primarily on secreted OPN, intracellular OPN has also been identified and is thought to contribute to cell-intrinsic signaling pathways beyond the canonical secretory pathway.2,4 The protein contains RGD motif that mediates integrin binding and, following proteolytic cleavage, can expose additional cryptic receptor-binding sequences that expand its its repertoire of integrin interactions.50,104 Consequently, OPN signals through multiple integrins, including α4β1 and α9β1, as well as members of the CD44 receptor family, thereby linking extracellular danger signals to cellular adhesion, migration, survival, and inflammatory transcriptional programs.2,4,50,104

For neurobiology, this molecular diversity is highly relevant. Full-length OPN, thrombin-cleaved OPN, and smaller peptide fragments are not biologically equivalent. Proteolytic processing can alter receptor preference, tissue distribution, and downstream signaling strength, which likely explains why some studies identify OPN as a potent mediator of neuroinflammation, whereas others demonstrate neuroprotective or reparative effects.4,50,104 This aspect has received relatively little attention in clinical biomarker research, where most assays measure total circulating or CSF OPN without distinguishing its proteolytic state, tissue or cellular origin, or post-translational modifications.

Cellular sources in the aging CNS

In the healthy adult brain, OPN expression is generally low and restricted to specific regions, but it increases markedly in response to ischemia, hemorrhage, neurodegeneration, and chronic inflammation.2,5,6 Depending on the disease context, OPN has been localized to neurons, activated microglia, infiltrating macrophages, and perivascular cells.2,14,24,35,36 These cellular sources are unlikely to be functionally equivalent. Neuronal OPN may contribute to cellular stress responses and neuronal survival, whereas myeloid-derived OPN is better positioned to regulate chemotaxis, phagocytosis, cytokine production, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Perivascular OPN appears to play a particularly important role at the interface between vascular pathology and parenchymal immune activation. 24

Microglia play a central role in this process. Several experimental studies have shown that OPN directly modulates microglial function. In primary cell cultures, OPN enhances microglial survival under stress while attenuating lipopolysaccharide-induced production of nitric oxide, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL-6, and inducible nitric oxide synthase, suggesting that it can suppress specific inflammatory responses. 7 Other studies have demonstrated that OPN-derived peptide fragments enhance microglial motility, migration, and phagocytic activity through signaling pathways involving focal adhesion kinase, extracellular signal-regulated kinase, and protein kinase B (Akt).8,9 These findings help explain why OPN has been consistently associated with both detrimental and reparative microglial phenotypes. Rather than serving solely as a marker of microglial activation, OPN actively shapes microglial functional states.

OPN as a determinant of aging-associated myeloid states

Aging profoundly alters microglial identity, and OPN appears to play a central role in this transition. Single-cell analyses of amyloid mouse models have shown that aging and Abeta deposition converge to generate activated response microglia enriched in repair-associated genes, including Spp1, Gpnmb, and Dkk2. 10 More recent lineage-tracing studies following brain injury have demonstrated that Spp1-positive disease-associated microglia-like cells exhibit age-dependent plasticity. In neonatal brains, many of these cells reacquire a homeostatic phenotype, whereas in older brains injury leaves a more persistent cellular imprint.11, 13 These findings are conceptually important because they suggest that SPP1 expression may represent not only a marker of microglial activation but also a branching point between transient adaptive responses and chronic maladaptive remodeling.

The strongest evidence that OPN participates in physiological brain aging comes from a 2026 study demonstrating that Spp1-positive microglia accumulate in the brains of aged mice and humans and that microglia-specific Spp1 deficiency exacerbates age-related memory decline by impairing phagocytosis, Akt signaling, mitochondrial complex I function, and oxidative phosphorylation. 12 At the human tissue level, increased cortical SPP1 and ITGAX expression in older adults is associated with faster cognitive decline and a greater likelihood of common neuropathological changes. 13 Collectively, these findings suggest that the emergence of SPP1-expressing myeloid cell states during aging should not be interpreted as inherently pathogenic. In certain contexts, particularly in the absence of overt neurodegenerative lesions, OPN may represent a compensatory homeostatic or bioenergetic response. This dual role provides an important conceptual framework for interpreting disease-specific findings.

Osteopontin in AD and related dementias

Human biomarker studies

Among age-associated neurological disorders, AD has the most extensive body of literature on OPN. Early neuropathological studies demonstrated increased OPN immunoreactivity in hippocampal pyramidal neurons from patients with AD and reported positive associations with both Abeta burden and aging. 14 Subsequent CSF studies showed that OPN concentrations are elevated in AD and that higher CSF levels are associated with greater cognitive impairment, particularly during the early symptomatic stages of the disease. 15 A later longitudinal study extended these findings to the prodromal stage, demonstrating increased CSF OPN levels in individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) who subsequently progressed to AD, together with dynamic increases in both plasma and CSF OPN around the time of clinical conversion. 16 These early studies established two important observations that remain relevant: OPN can be measured in clinically accessible biofluids, and its elevation precedes the onset of end-stage dementia.

More recent biomarker studies have further strengthened the evidence supporting OPN as a marker of preclinical and mixed-pathology cognitive aging. Plasma OPN levels are elevated not only in AD but also in vascular cognitive impairment (VCI), vascular dementia, and AD with concomitant cerebrovascular disease. Moreover, plasma OPN concentrations are associated with cortical infarcts, lacunes, white-matter hyperintensity burden, brain atrophy, and circulating inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-8, and TNF. 17 A 2024 study involving the PREVENT-AD and Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohorts demonstrated that CSF OPN levels correlated with synaptic biomarkers in cognitively unimpaired individuals with a family history of AD and were higher in participants with amyloid-positive and tau-positive biomarker profiles, including those identified by positron emission tomography. Importantly, higher OPN levels were associated with more rapid progression to clinical AD, supporting its potential utility as a biomarker of presymptomatic sporadic AD rather than merely a byproduct of established dementia. 18

These clinical observations are increasingly supported by systems-level analyses of human brain tissue. In older adults enrolled in the Religious Orders Study and the Rush Memory and Aging Project, cortical SPP1 and ITGAX expression was associated with accelerated cognitive decline and multiple common neuropathological changes, suggesting that the OPN axis reflects cumulative pathological burden rather than a single lesion type. 13 These findings underscore the translational potential of OPN, which lies at the intersection of neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and vascular injury, three interconnected processes that frequently coexist in the aging brain. However, its principal limitation is its lack of disease specificity. Because OPN is elevated across a range of inflammatory and vascular disorders, it is unlikely to serve as a standalone diagnostic biomarker for AD. Instead, its greatest potential may be as a disease-modifying or stage-reflective biomarker incorporated into multimodal diagnostic panels.

Studies in models and human tissue

The mechanistic literature helps explain why OPN is closely associated with AD progression, while also illustrating the complexity of its biological role. In APP/PS1KI mice, inflammatory changes closely accompany neurodegeneration, with OPN among the molecules upregulated alongside other inflammatory mediators. 20 Subsequent single-cell analyses demonstrated that amyloid accumulation drives the emergence of activated response microglia enriched in Spp1 and other repair-associated genes and that this response is further accelerated by aging and influenced by sex and Apoe status. 10 Thus, Spp1 functions as part of the broader disease-associated microglial program rather than acting in isolation.

A major advance came with the demonstration that not all CD11c-positive microglia are functionally equivalent. In 5XFAD mice, Qiu and colleagues identified CD11c-positive microglia as the exclusive source of OPN and showed that OPN expression distinguishes disease-associated microglia into two functionally distinct subsets: a protective OPN-negative population that more efficiently clears Abeta and a pathogenic OPN-positive population that promotes neuroinflammation, exhibits impaired Abeta clearance, and correlates with cognitive impairment and neuropathological severity in human AD tissue. 23 Genetic deletion of OPN or treatment with an anti-OPN antibody reduced pro-inflammatory microglia, amyloid plaque burden, dystrophic neurites, and cognitive deficits.2325 These findings provide some of the strongest evidence that OPN can function as a direct pathogenic mediator rather than merely a secondary correlate in AD.

Independent studies have reached similar conclusions from a vascular perspective. De Schepper and colleagues demonstrated that, in mouse models of AD, perivascular macrophages and fibroblasts are major sources of SPP1 and that perivascular OPN signaling drives microglial phagocytic states and synaptic engulfment in response to Abeta oligomers. 24 Notably, these effects are not uniformly beneficial. Although phagocytic microglial states are often considered protective, SPP1-dependent microglial activation in this context contributed to synaptic loss, whereas Spp1 deficiency prevented this effect. 24 These findings place OPN at a critical interface linking vascular amyloid deposition, perivascular niche remodeling, microglial activation, and synaptic injury, an axis that is particularly relevant in older adults with mixed vascular and neurodegenerative pathology.

Additional mechanistic insights come from interventional studies targeting related myeloid pathways. Agonistic activation of human TREM2 in an AD model reduced Spp1 expression in Iba1-positive cells while promoting microglial proliferation and attenuating neuropathology, suggesting that the OPN-associated program is not merely a downstream marker but part of a therapeutically modifiable inflammatory state. 22 Exercise-induced improvement in APP/PS1 mice has likewise been associated with enhanced microglial phagocytosis accompanied by SPP1-related programs, further supporting the view that the biological significance of OPN depends on the broader phenotypic context in which it is expressed. 19

Taken together, these studies provide complementary phenotypic, spatial, transcriptomic, and interventional evidence linking OPN/SPP1 to AD-associated myeloid responses. However, they do not yet establish a comprehensive mechanistic framework. Rigorous mechanistic studies of OPN/SPP1 in AD remain limited, particularly regarding its upstream regulators, receptor interactions, downstream signaling pathways, cell type-specific functions, temporal dynamics, and causal contribution to human disease progression. Accordingly, the available evidence supports a context-dependent role for OPN/SPP1 in AD-related neuroinflammation, amyloid pathology, vascular–microglial crosstalk, and synaptic injury. Nevertheless, additional studies are required to determine when OPN/SPP1 functions as a protective mediator, a maladaptive effector, or simply a marker of broader disease-associated myeloid states.

Why OPN may appear protective in some ADstudies: a context-dependent interpretation

Despite compelling evidence supporting a pathogenic role for OPN, several studies have also reported reparative effects in AD. One of the most frequently cited examples is the study by Rentsendorj and colleagues, which demonstrated that glatiramer acetate-induced upregulation of OPN in recruited macrophages enhances macrophage-mediated Abeta fibril uptake and is associated with reduced plaque burden in AD models. 21 In that study, OPN knockdown impaired Abeta phagocytosis, whereas treatment with recombinant OPN restored phagocytic function, supporting a direct role for OPN in macrophage-mediated clearance rather than a coincidental association. 21 These findings are consistent with a broader body of evidence showing that exogenous or contextually induced OPN can promote phagocytosis and tissue repair.

The apparent discrepancy between these findings and those reported by Qiu and colleagues or De Schepper and colleagues arises only if all OPN-expressing cells are assumed to be functionally equivalent. Current evidence does not support this assumption. Macrophage-derived OPN in a therapeutic recruitment setting is likely to operate within a distinct receptor environment, at a different stage of disease, and potentially in a different proteolytic state than OPN produced by chronically plaque-associated CD11c-positive microglia or perivascular stromal cells.4,21,23,24 Thus, OPN may facilitate beneficial debris clearance when associated with an effective phagocytic program yet promote maladaptive inflammation and synaptic injury when linked to chronically activated, dysfunctional myeloid states. Consistent with this interpretation, a 2025 systematic review of OPN in AD concluded that substantially more studies support inflammatory and neurodegenerative roles than overt neuroprotective functions, indicating that OPN is best regarded as a context-dependent double-edged mediator whose overall effects are determined by the disease setting. 19

Related dementias and broader cognitive aging

OPN dysregulation is not limited to classic AD. As early as 2010, CSF OPN measurements were reported to distinguish patients with AD from healthy controls more effectively than those with FTD, while also suggesting a broader role for OPN in the early stages of neurodegenerative disease. 15 More recently, patient-derived neuronal and xenograft models of MAPT-N279K-associated FTD demonstrated robust OPN upregulation as part of a molecular program characterized by oxidative stress, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, and neuroinflammation. Genetic or experimental reduction of OPN improved graft survival and reduced microglial infiltration, providing compelling evidence that neuron-derived OPN can actively shape the inflammatory microenvironment in tauopathy-associated dementia.27,34 These findings extend the role of OPN beyond AD and suggest that SPP1 may represent a convergent mediator across multiple proteopathy-driven dementias.

Taken together, the literature on AD and related dementias supports three key conclusions. First, OPN is among the most consistently elevated inflammatory mediators detected in AD-relevant tissues and biofluids across the continuum from presymptomatic biomarker positivity to symptomatic dementia.15–19 Second, OPN occupies a sufficiently upstream position within disease-associated pathways to influence amyloid plaque compaction, phagocytic competence, synaptic engulfment, and cognitive decline.21–24 Third, the biological effects of OPN are highly dependent on its cellular source and disease context. This final point has important translational implications: systemic or nonselective OPN-targeted therapies may suppress pathogenic microglial populations while simultaneously impairing compensatory phagocytic and reparative responses. Consequently, the successful therapeutic targeting of OPN will require a much more detailed understanding of compartment-specific biology than is currently available.

OPNin PD and Lewy body disorders

Compared with AD, the literature on OPN in PD and Lewy body disorders is less extensive and largely based on earlier studies. Nevertheless, it remains conceptually informative because it also demonstrates context-dependent effects. Human studies have consistently reported elevated OPN levels in PD-related tissues and biofluids. In the substantia nigra of healthy human brains, OPN is localized primarily to dopaminergic neurons, whereas in PD it is also detected in activated microglia and Lewy bodies.35,36 Serum and CSF OPN concentrations are increased in patients with PD. Higher serum OPN levels have been associated with greater motor impairment, whereas elevated CSF OPN levels are associated with dementia and lower exposure to dopaminergic therapy. 36 More broadly, in Lewy body disorders, serum and CSF OPN levels are likewise elevated, and a polymorphism in the SPP1 gene has been associated with increased disease susceptibility, supporting the hypothesis that OPN is not merely a reactive marker but may also contribute to disease vulnerability. 38

The functional evidence is likewise context dependent. In mice exposed to MPTP, genetic deletion of OPN attenuated dopaminergic neurodegeneration in the substantia nigra and striatum, suggesting that endogenous OPN contributes to toxin-induced neuronal injury. 36 This finding is consistent with the biomarker literature and supports a pathogenic role for sustained OPN signaling within the inflammatory milieu of PD. However, an apparently contrasting result was reported using an RGD-containing OPN-derived peptide, which protected tyrosine hydroxylase-positive neurons from toxic injury in primary ventral mesencephalic cultures and in the rat substantia nigra. 37 These findings are not necessarily contradictory. Full-length endogenous OPN in a chronic inflammatory environment may promote microglial activation and leukocyte recruitment, whereas specific OPN-derived peptide fragments may activate survival-promoting integrin signaling pathways in vulnerable dopaminergic neurons.

Another informative observation comes from primate and human tissue studies showing that OPN expression decreases in surviving dopaminergic neurons following MPTP exposure and in PD, even as its expression increases in activated microglia. 35 This shift suggests that the same molecule may serve distinct functions in neuronal stress responses and non-neuronal inflammatory remodeling. From a translational perspective, these findings reinforce a central concept in OPN biology: tissue-level elevation alone does not identify the biologically dominant cellular source or determine whether the relevant effects are cell-autonomous, paracrine, or proteoform dependent.

What conclusions can be drawn for aging-associated synucleinopathies? First, OPN is a reproducible biomarker candidate in PD and Lewy body disorders, although its disease specificity remains limited because OPN is elevated across multiple inflammatory conditions.36,38 Second, both clinical and experimental evidence supports a role for OPN in selective nigrostriatal vulnerability.35–37 Third, therapeutic strategies should distinguish between chronic endogenous OPN signaling and fragment-based or receptor-selective interventions. Overall, PD provides one of the clearest examples of why measurement of total OPN alone is insufficient to capture its biological complexity.

OPN in cerebrovascular disease

Ischemic stroke

Stroke is the neurological disorder in which the dual role of OPN as both an injury marker and a repair mediator is most clearly illustrated. Clinical studies have consistently shown that higher circulating OPN levels are associated with more severe disease and poorer clinical outcomes. In patients receiving reperfusion therapies, admission OPN levels predict poorer functional outcomes at 3 months. 26 In a large prospective cohort of patients with ischemic stroke, elevated baseline plasma OPN levels were independently associated with death, major disability, and recurrent vascular events at 1 year. 27 Moreover, a thrombin-cleaved N-terminal OPN fragment appears to provide subtype-specific diagnostic information during the hyperacute phase of ischemic stroke, particularly in atherothrombotic stroke.28,35 Collectively, these findings support OPN as a clinically relevant biomarker of acute cerebrovascular injury and future vascular risk.

However, experimental studies indicate that local OPN signaling can exert profound reparative effects after ischemic injury. In rodent models of ischemic stroke, recombinant OPN reduces infarct volume and improves neurological outcomes, at least in part by suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation in microglia.29, 36 OPN–integrin signaling also promotes peri-infarct autophagy and neuroplasticity, suggesting that OPN contributes to the transition from acute injury to tissue repair and structural remodeling. 30 Particularly compelling is evidence that brain-infiltrating regulatory T cells facilitate long-term white-matter repair after ischemic stroke through OPN-dependent signaling to microglia, thereby promoting oligodendrogenesis and functional recovery. 31 These findings position OPN at the center of an immunoreparative pathway rather than as solely a mediator of inflammation.

The recent literature on pontine infarction further refines this concept. A 2026 multi-omics and experimental study identified OPN as a central regulator linking microglial activation to BBB disruption and neuroinflammation following pontine infarction, while also demonstrating that exogenous OPN administration attenuated microglia-mediated inflammatory injury and improved vascular integrity. 32 These findings cannot be readily reconciled with the negative prognostic significance of elevated plasma OPN in human stroke unless the assumption that biomarker elevation necessarily indicates therapeutic inhibition is reconsidered. Instead, circulating OPN likely reflects the magnitude of tissue injury and the systemic inflammatory response, whereas appropriately timed local OPN signaling may facilitate repair within the injured brain.

Older experimental studies are fully consistent with this stage-dependent interpretation. OPN mediates the lateral migration of neuroblasts from the subventricular zone following focal cerebral ischemia, indicating that it contributes to the orchestration of endogenous regenerative responses after injury. 33 Rather than functioning as a static marker of inflammation, OPN acts as a dynamic regulator of tissue remodeling whose biological role evolves over time. During the acute phase after ischemia, OPN may promote leukocyte recruitment, protease activation, and vascular dysfunction. In later stages, however, it may facilitate debris clearance, white-matter regeneration, and neural network reorganization. This temporal duality is likely to be even more relevant in the aging brain, where regenerative capacity is diminished and microglial states are already primed by aging.11,12,25

Intracerebral hemorrhage and subarachnoid hemorrhage

The literature on hemorrhagic stroke supports a similar conclusion. Following intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), OPN expression remains elevated for several weeks in perihematomal tissue and promotes sustained migration of neuroblasts from the subventricular zone toward the lesion. 105 In experimental ICH, recombinant OPN improves functional recovery while reducing apoptosis through the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt/glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta (GSK-3β) pathway. 99 In hyperglycemic ICH, OPN attenuates inflammation through integrin-β1-dependent inhibition of Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) signaling. 106 Collectively, these findings suggest that local OPN functions as an endogenous reparative signal in a pathological environment otherwise characterized by marked oxidative stress and inflammation.

Human studies, however, again highlight the value of OPN primarily as a biomarker of disease severity rather than recovery. Plasma OPN levels are elevated during acute ICH and are associated with hematoma volume, neurological deficit, and poor short-term outcomes. 107 These findings parallel those reported in ischemic stroke: elevated circulating OPN identifies patients with more severe disease, even though experimental enhancement of local OPN signaling within the injured brain may confer therapeutic benefit. The most parsimonious interpretation is that circulating OPN reflects the systemic response to the magnitude of brain injury, whereas tissue-level OPN signaling remains pleiotropic and may contribute to endogenous repair.

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) provides some of the strongest evidence that OPN influences both vascular and immune responses after hemorrhage. In rodent models, intranasal recombinant OPN attenuates early brain injury following SAH, while recombinant OPN also reduces experimental cerebral vasospasm through anti-apoptotic mechanisms.108,109 In patients with severe aneurysmal SAH, CSF OPN levels are markedly elevated, and acute-phase plasma OPN independently predicts poor clinical outcomes, indicating that OPN also has prognostic value in the clinical setting.101,102 Recent review articles have synthesized these findings by proposing OPN as a key mediator of multiple post-SAH complications, including early brain injury, delayed cerebral ischemia, and hydrocephalus. 110

Notably, more recent studies of SAH continue to demonstrate the context-dependent nature of OPN biology. A 2024 rat study reported that OPN modulates microglial activation states and attenuates inflammatory responses after SAH, supporting a protective role. 111 In contrast, a 2025 study found that pharmacological inhibition of OPN reduced neuroinflammation after SAH through the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/NF-kB pathway. 112 Rather than being mutually exclusive, these findings likely reflect differences in experimental timing, dosing, the source of OPN, and the biological endpoints examined. It is plausible that excessive early OPN signaling within one tissue compartment amplifies innate immune activation, whereas later or exogenously administered OPN in another compartment promotes BBB stabilization and limits apoptosis. To resolve these apparent discrepancies, future studies should systematically characterize the phase-specific effects of OPN in aged animal models, as SAH in older adults occurs within a markedly different vascular and immunological environment than that of young animals.

Cerebral small vessel disease and vascular cognitive impairment

The interface between vascular aging and cognitive decline is particularly relevant to OPN biology. Plasma OPN levels are elevated in VCI and vascular dementia, and in mixed AD–cerebrovascular phenotypes, they are associated with neuroimaging markers, including lacunes, cortical infarcts, white-matter hyperintensities, and cerebral atrophy. 17 More directly, a 2024 pilot study reported that plasma OPN levels are associated with dementia specifically in individuals with cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD), with strong associations with white-matter lesion burden. 113 This finding is a conceptually important because it positions OPN within the clinically common setting of mixed vascular and neurodegenerative cognitive impairment that characterizes much of geriatric practice.

In older adults, CSVD is characterized by endothelial dysfunction, impaired BBB integrity, disrupted perivascular clearance, and chronic glial activation. OPN is biologically well positioned to integrate these processes through its established roles in endothelial activation, extracellular matrix remodeling, leukocyte trafficking, and myeloid cell-state transitions, all of which may contribute to progression from covert vascular brain injury to overt cognitive impairment.2,17,24,113 Although the CSVD-specific literature remains limited, the available evidence suggests that OPN may be particularly informative in mixed pathological conditions, where vascular and neurodegenerative mechanisms overlap. This context may ultimately prove more clinically relevant than the search for disease-specific OPN signatures in highly selected patient cohorts.

Overall, cerebrovascular disorders provide some of the strongest evidence that OPN should be interpreted both as a biomarker of injury severity and as an active participant in tissue repair. Human biomarker studies consistently associate higher circulating OPN levels with poorer clinical outcomes, whereas experimental and tissue-based studies repeatedly demonstrate roles for OPN in BBB maintenance, white-matter repair, autophagy, neuroblast migration, and vascular stabilization.26–33,99,101,102,105–113 These observations are not contradictory. Rather, they indicate that successful translation will require distinguishing systemic biomarker measurements from local tissue biology and acute injury responses from subacute reparative processes.

OPN in ALS and other late-life neurological conditions

Although ALS is not always classified among the canonical dementias of aging, its incidence increases with advancing age, and its pathobiology overlaps with age-related inflammatory remodeling. OPN is of particular interest in ALS because it is expressed by selectively vulnerable motor neuron populations. In the mouse spinal cord, OPN serves as a marker of α-motor neurons, the subtype primarily responsible for force-generating motor function. 39 Human anatomical studies subsequently demonstrated that SPP1 is expressed in the sensorimotor cortex and spinal cord and that its expression is altered in ALS, with evidence suggesting reduced expression in affected motor systems. 40 These observations suggest that OPN is linked not only to inflammatory processes but also to intrinsic motor neuron identity and selective neuronal vulnerability.

Clinical biomarker studies have now begun to complement the anatomical literature. A 2025 study reported that serum OPN levels are elevated in ALS compared with healthy controls and increase with more anatomically advanced disease stages. Higher serum OPN concentrations were associated with shorter survival and more rapid progression to advanced anatomical stages, although they were not associated with all conventional measures of clinical progression. 41 These findings suggest that OPN more closely reflects disease dissemination than day-to-day functional disability. Whether OPN also plays a mechanistic role in ALS progression remains uncertain; however, the convergence of neuronal expression data and serum biomarker findings argues against OPN being merely an epiphenomenon.

Beyond ALS, emerging evidence in FTD suggests that neuron-derived OPN can directly influence neuroinflammation and neuronal loss. 34 Collectively, these findings raise an underexplored possibility: in some neurodegenerative disorders, OPN may be produced not primarily by myeloid cells but by stressed neurons that actively shape the surrounding immune microenviroment. If confirmed, this concept would indicate that describing OPN simply as a “microglial inflammatory marker” is insufficient to capture its diverse biological roles across a substantial subset of age-associated neurodegenerative diseases.

Future directions and translational priorities

Why the literature appears contradictory

The most striking feature of the OPN literature is not inconsistency itself, but its systematic context dependence. Multiple biological variables consistently influence the direction and magnitude of OPN-mediated effects.

First, the cellular source of OPN is critical. OPN produced by plaque-associated CD11c-positive microglia in AD may amplify inflammatory injury and impair efficient Abeta clearance, whereas OPN produced by recruited macrophages, regulatory T cell (Treg)-conditioned microglia, or stressed neurons may serve fundamentally different functions.21,23,24,31,34 This distinction is mechanistic rather than merely descriptive. The cellular source determines the spatial compartment in which OPN is released, the accompanying inflammatory or reparative mediators, the receptor repertoire of neighboring responder cells, and the local enzymatic environment that processes OPN after secretion. In AD models, OPN expression by plaque-associated CD11c-positive microglia is embedded within a chronically activated disease-associated microglial program, whereas OPN induced in recruited macrophages following immunomodulatory treatment is associated with enhanced Abeta uptake and clearance. Perivascular macrophage- and fibroblast-derived SPP1 represents yet another biological context, acting within the vascular-parenchymal niche to regulate microglial phagocytic and complement-associated states, as well as synaptic engulfment. In stroke, Treg-derived OPN engages microglial integrin signaling within a reparative white-matter niche rather than a plaque-associated inflammatory niche. Cellular source may also influence the molecular form of OPN. OPN undergoes extensive post-translational modification, including phosphorylation and O-glycosylation, and is proteolytically processed by thrombin, matrix metalloproteinases, plasmin, cathepsin D, and other proteases into fragments with distinct receptor-binding properties. These include exposure of the SVVYGLR/SLAYGLR motif, which enhances binding to α4β1 and α9β1 integrins. Thus, the term “cellular source” should be viewed as a shorthand for a broader biological context encompassing the producing cell type, anatomical niche, disease stage, post-translational and proteolytic processing environment, receptor availability, and downstream cellular responses. However, direct proteomic evidence comparing post-translationally modified OPN derived from specific cellular sources—including microglia, infiltrating macrophages, neurons, Tregs, and perivascular cells—in human AD remains limited. Accordingly, source-dependent OPN proteoform diversity should be regarded as a plausible and testable mechanism rather than an established conclusion. Second, anatomical compartment also matters. Plasma OPN is strongly influenced by systemic inflammatory and vascular responses and therefore may behave differently from CSF OPN or parenchymal OPN. This distinction is particularly evident in stroke and dementia, where elevated circulating OPN levels predict poorer clinical outcomes even when exogenous tissue-level OPN exerts beneficial effects.15–18,26–28,101,102,107 Third, molecular form matters. Full-length OPN, thrombin-cleaved OPN, and defined peptide fragments exhibit distinct receptor interactions and therefore should not be regarded as interchangeable analytes or therapeutic interventions.4,28,37,50,104 Fourth, timing matters. During acute injury, the same signaling pathway may contribute to inflammatory injury during the early expansion phase yet support debris clearance and tissue remodeling during subsequent stages of recovery. Finally, age itself is an important determinant. The aging brain exhibits altered microglial plasticity, reduced mitochondrial reserve, impaired vascular resilience, and changes in protease activity, all of which are likely to influence the biological effects of OPN.10–13 Experimental studies support this concept. In adult models of ischemic stroke, thrombin-cleaved OPN was reported to provide greater neuroprotection than intact full-length OPN, and OPN peptide mimetics derived from sequences adjacent to the thrombin-cleavage site likewise conferred neuroprotection, indicating that proteolytic processing can alter biological activity rather than merely degrade the protein. In contrast, in a preterm hypoxic–ischemic brain injury model, neither full-length OPN nor thrombin-cleaved OPN conferred neuroprotection, whereas selected OPN-derived peptides exacerbated injury, emphasizing that the biological effects of OPN fragments are both age and context dependent. In Parkinsonian models, an RGD-containing OPN-derived peptide protected tyrosine hydroxylase-positive dopaminergic neurons, whereas genetic deletion of endogenous OPN attenuated MPTP-induced nigrostriatal degeneration, indicating that the effects of receptor-selective peptide fragments cannot be assumed to replicate those of the endogenous full-length OPN protein. Microglial studies further demonstrate that OPN-derived peptides containing the RGD motif, with or without SLAYGLR-related sequences, enhance microglial motility and phagocytosis through focal adhesion kinase (FAK)-, extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)-, and Akt-dependent signaling pathways. Conversely, inflammatory models outside the CNS demonstrate that thrombin cleavage exposes the SVVYGLR/SLAYGLR motifs, which preferentially promote α4β1- and α9β1-integrin-dependent leukocyte or monocyte migration, as shown in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis and rheumatoid arthritis models. Clinically, thrombin-cleaved N-terminal OPN rather than total OPN alone, has demonstrated early subtype-specific diagnostic value in acute atherothrombotic ischemic stroke. These observations support treating total OPN, thrombin-cleaved OPN, and synthetic OPN-derived peptides as related but biologically distinct molecular entities. Accordingly, future biomarker and interventional studies should, whenever feasible, report the specific epitope or peptide sequence assayed, cleavage state, species, biological compartment, and timing of sample collection or intervention.

Biomarker opportunities and limitations

From a translational perspective, OPN is an attractive biomarker because it can be measured in plasma and CSF, appears early in disease trajectories, and reflects clinically relevant pathological processes that are often not captured by proteinopathy-centered biomarkers alone.15–18,26–28,41,101,102,107,113 In AD, OPN may help characterize the inflammatory-synaptic dimension of preclinical disease. In stroke, it may improve early risk stratification. In CSVD and mixed dementia, it may provide insight into the otherwise difficult-to-quantify interface between vascular injury and neuroinflammation. In ALS, it may reflect the extent of anatomical disease dissemination. Nevertheless, OPN is unlikely to become a clinically useful standalone biomarker unless three major challenges are addressed.

The first challenge is analytical heterogeneity. Published studies employ different antibody pairs, sample matrices, normalization methods, and reporting units, making direct comparisons across studies difficult. The second is molecular resolution. Very few clinical studies distinguish full-length OPN from proteolytically cleaved fragments, despite evidence that these proteoforms can have distinct biological activities.4,28,50,104 The third is biological specificity. Because OPN is elevated across a wide range of inflammatory and vascular conditions, its interpretation will likely require integration with disease-specific biomarkers, such as Abeta/tau panels in AD, neurofilament light chain in ALS, or neuroimaging and hemostatic markers in stroke. Accordingly, the most promising translational strategy is likely to involve multimodal biomarker integration rather than reliance on a single biomarker.

Therapeutic implications

The therapeutic potential of OPN is substantial but equally complex. In AD, the strongest interventional evidence currently supports reducing pathogenic OPN signaling in selected microglial populations. 23 In stroke and hemorrhagic brain injury, experimental studies more frequently support enhancing or mimicking reparative OPN signaling, at least within appropriately defined therapeutic windows.29–33,99,105–109,111 These disease-specific differences argue against the development of a universal anti-OPN or pro-OPN therapeutic strategy.

A more realistic approach is precision modulation of OPN biology. One strategy would be to target specific receptor pathways—for example, inhibiting integrin interactions that mediate pathogenic plaque-associated inflammation while preserving signaling pathways involved in phagocytosis or tissue repair. Another approach would be to modulate OPN fragment generation rather than total protein abundance, particularly in diseases characterized by altered thrombin or matrix metalloproteinase activity. Cell-type-specific delivery systems, including myeloid-targeted biologics or nanoparticle-based platforms, may also be necessary to suppress detrimental microglial or perivascular OPN signaling without interfering with beneficial neuronal or reparative immune functions. Finally, age should be explicitly incorporated into preclinical study design. An intervention that is beneficial in a young adult stroke model may prove ineffective or even harmful in an aging brain, where Spp1-positive microglia may contribute to bioenergetic resilience. 12

Key priorities for the next phase of the field

Several priorities emerge from the current evidence. First, future studies should routinely distinguish among gene expression, tissue immunoreactivity, CSF levels, plasma levels, and fragment-specific assays rather than treating these measurements as equivalent indicators of a single biological process. Second, cellular-source mapping should be integrated with functional perturbation studies in aged models and, whenever feasible, human tissue. Third, longitudinal clinical studies should determine whether OPN improves prognostic performance when incorporated into established biomarker panels rather than evaluated as an isolated marker. Fourth, mechanistic investigations should explicitly compare early and late interventions, as temporal factors likely account for a substantial proportion of the apparent contradictions in the literature. Fifth, the field would benefit from a consensus framework that categorizes OPN-related findings into pathogenic, reparative, compensatory, or severity-marker domains rather than applying the broad term “activation” across biologically distinct contexts.

Taken together, the current evidence suggests that OPN represents one of the clearest examples of a molecule whose translational value derives from its pleiotropic biology. OPN is informative because it reflects, and in some contexts regulates, the transition of the aging nervous system among injury, adaptation, and failed repair. The central challenge is to translate this complexity into actionable biological insights without oversimplifying the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

OPN has moved from the periphery of neuroinflammation research to the center of several fundamental questions in neurological aging. It is consistently elevated in AD, PD, stroke, CSVD/VCI, and ALS-related contexts, marks aging-associated myeloid remodeling; and influences processes including phagocytosis, synaptic engulfment, mitochondrial function, BBB integrity, white-matter repair, and neuronal survival.10–13,15–18,23,26–33,39–41,99,101,102,105–113 The central conclusion from the current literature is that OPN is neither inherently harmful nor beneficial. Rather, its biological effects depend on the cellular source, anatomical compartment, molecular form, and stage of disease progression.

This framework helps reconcile why elevated circulating OPN often predicts poor prognosis, why specific microglial OPN programs may contribute to pathology in AD, and why exogenous or contextually induced OPN can nevertheless enhance repair after cerebrovascular injury. For the field of aging neuroscience, this distinction has important clinical implications. Older brains have reduced biological reserve and greater pathological complexity, making nonspecific immune modulation particularly challenging. Future studies should therefore prioritize stage-specific and cell-specific interventions alongside biomarker platforms capable of distinguishing among OPN compartments and proteoforms. If this precision can be achieved, OPN may transition from a broad inflammatory correlate to a clinically useful tool for mechanism-based stratification and therapeutic decision-making in late-life neurological disease.
