Autoinducer-2 relieves soil stress-induced dormancy of Bacillus velezensis by modulating sporulation signaling
Authors: Qin Xiong, Huihui Zhang, Xia Shu, Xiting Sun, Haichao Feng, Zhihui Xu, Ákos T Kovács, Ruifu Zhang, Yunpeng Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe collective behavior of bacteria is regulated by quorum sensing (QS). Autoinducer-2 (AI-2) is a common QS signal that regulates the behavior of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Despite the plethora of processes described to be influenced by AI-2 in diverse Gram-negative bacteria, the AI-2-regulated processes in Bacilli are relatively unexplored. Here, we describe a novel function for AI-2 in Bacillus velezensis SQR9 related to the sporulation. AI-2 inhibited the initiation of sporulation through the phosphatase RapC and the DNA binding regulator ComA. Using biochemistry experiments, we demonstrated that AI-2 interacts with RapC to stimulate its binding to ComA, which leads to an inactive ComA and subsequently a sporulation inhibition. The AI-2 molecule could be shared across species for inhibiting Bacillus sporulation and it also plays the same role in different soil conditions. Our study revealed a novel function and regulatory mechanism of AI-2 in inhibiting sporulation in Bacilli.Subject terms: Environmental microbiology, Applied microbiology

Introduction

Microbes live in various habitats and are widely used in medicine, industry, and agriculture. However, to survive and form stable communities with other microbes in the given niches is dependent on the abilities of the microbes to exert certain functions, such as antibiotic production, waste degradation, pathogenicity, or host beneficial functions. The behavior of bacteria in a population is under regulation by quorum sensing (QS)1, a strategy during which bacterial cells sense the population density and express certain traits or induce differentiation processes. The study of QS has drawn much attention because it provides targets for the manipulation of disease2, industrial fermentation, and agricultural production3,4.

Quorum sensing is achieved through bacterial perception of signal molecules produced by individual cells that gradually increase along with cell density, and are either sensed by receptors displayed on the cell membrane or reimported into cells where they bind directly to regulatory proteins. QS has been reported to regulate many bacterial social behaviors, such as biofilm formation, virulence, and symbiosis5. Bacterial QS signal includes N-acyl-homoserine lactones (AHLs), cholera autoinducer 1 (CAI-1), autoinducer-2 (AI-2), and some specific oligopeptides6. The AHLs controlled by LuxI/LuxR-like system are the most studied QS signals, however, these molecules have only been described in Gram-negative bacteria7. In Gram-positive bacteria, unique oligopeptide QS signals are detected by receptors at the cell surface or located within the cytoplasm8. For example, the ComP protein senses ComX in Bacillus subtilis9, and AgrC senses autoinducing peptide (AIP) in Staphylococcus aureus10. In addition, extracellular signal peptides can also be detected by receptors of the RRNPP family within the cytoplasm. The RRNPP family is widespread among Firmicutes, and it is named for the prototypical members, Rap, Rgg, NprR, PlcR, and PrgX11. Among them, the Rap system is best illustrated in B. subtilis, which encodes 11 Rap paralogs (RapA-K and Rap60). The core mechanics of this system is: that the extracellular peptides are encoded by phr genes and transported intracellular by the Opp system, where it eventually binds to Rap protein to form the Rap-Phr complex that can influence the activity of their target regulator (Spo0F, DegU, or ComA) via blocking dephosphorylation, or by directly DNA binding12. Besides these unique molecules specific to their respective bacterial group, AI-2 is a general QS signal that plays important roles in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria13. However, it is less explored whether and how the general AI-2 signal functions in nature and cross-species.

AI-2 regulates biofilm formation in Bacillus velezensis, Staphylococcus suis, Bacillus cereus, and Bacillus subtilis14–17, and is essential for the virulence of Lactobacillus rhamnosus and enterohemorrhagic Escherichia coli18. AI-2-mediated processes generally involve the production, release, reimportation, and sensing of the signal19. AI-2 is synthesized from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) by the LuxS enzyme20 and subsequently released from the cell by free diffusion. In models of Gram-negative bacteria, the reimportation of AI-2 into bacterial cells is generally controlled by the LuxP/LuxQ two-component and lsr operon-like transport apparatus in a density-dependent manner7,21. After being imported back into the cell, the intracellular AI-2 functions as a signal to regulate various bacterial behaviors. In the Gram-negative bacterium, Vibrio harveyi, AI-2-regulated luminescence relies on phosphorylated LuxO, which in turn controls the expression of sRNA (Qrr) and LuxR, a transcription regulator of luminescence genes22. In contrast to the well-explored regulatory pathways, the mechanism of how AI-2 influences gene regulation in Gram-positive bacteria remains unclear.

Bacterial cells grow and interact with each other, which leads to various types of interactions. At high cell densities where bacteria secrete common goods that are used by any member of the whole population, bacteria engage in a social lifestyle (i.e. traits influence the whole population and not just single cells). QS systems have an important influence on these pathways. Sporulation has been mostly studied in the Bacillus genus, a group of Gram-positive bacteria. The initiation of sporulation in Bacilli is mainly controlled by phosphorylation of the global regulator, Spo0A23. The phosphorylation and therefore activation of Spo0A are controlled in two ways, the phosphorylation cascade activates Spo0A by histidine kinases through Spo0F and Spo0B, while Spo0F is directly dephosphorylated by Rap phosphatases reducing the phosphate flow in the pathway. The activities of Rap phosphatases are repressed by corresponding signaling Phr peptides. Sporulation of Bacilli has been studied for decades and the regulatory pathway has been dissected to understand the influence of environmental stress conditions, including nutritional deficiency24. The progression of sporulation means that the Bacillus cells become dormant cells. Dormant spores can help Bacilli survive harsh environments, but for probiotic Bacilli, dormant cells are not conducive to their plant growth-promoting functions. Therefore, inhibiting the sporulation of Bacilli to maintain further vegetative growth might promote Bacilli to play a prolonged plant growth-promoting function.

Bacillus velezensis SQR9 is a plant beneficial rhizobacterium, isolated from the rhizosphere of cucumber with the capability to promote plant growth25. Our previous study demonstrated that a defect in the luxS gene, which codes for the synthetase of AI-2, caused impaired biofilm formation and root colonization by B. velezensis SQR914. In this study, we discovered a novel regulation function of AI-2 for inhibiting sporulation of B. velezensis SQR9. Elucidating the molecular mechanism revealed that AI-2 directly binds to RapC and stimulates its protein-protein interaction with ComA. The interaction of RapC and ComA inhibits the activity of the latter regulator, resulting in the inhibition of the sporulation process. Moreover, sporulation of B. velezensis SQR9 was inhibited by AI-2 which originated from another bacterial species, E. coli. This study revealed a novel regulatory mechanism by AI-2 on the sporulation of B. velezensis. Additional soil experiments further confirm the potential of AI-2 as an additive to microbial fertilizers.

Methods

Strains and cultural conditions

The strains and plasmids used in this study are shown in Supplementary Table 1. B. velezensis strain SQR9 (CGMCC accession no.5808, China General Microbiology Culture Collection Center, NCBI accession NO. CP006890) and its derivative strains were routinely grown at 37 °C in lysogeny broth (LB) medium (1% (w/v) tryptone, 0.5% (w/v) yeast extract, and 0.5% (w/v) NaCl). For spore formation experiments, SQR9 and its derivative strains were cultivated 12 h in MSgg medium (100 mM 3-(N-morpholino) propane sulfonic acid (MOPS), 5 mM potassium phosphate, 2 mM MgCl2, 700 μM CaCl2, 50 μM MnCl2, 50 μM FeCl3, 1 μM ZnCl2, 2 μM thiamine, 0.5% (w/v) glycerol, 0.5% (w/v) glutamate, 50 μg/mL tryptophan, 50 μg/mL phenylalanine, and 50 μg/mL threonine, pH 7.0)26. E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔluxS, which was used to express ComA and RapC proteins, was cultured in LB at 37 °C and induced for protein expression at 16 °C with 0.5 mM IPTG. E. coli Top10 was used for plasmid constructions and propagation. Antibiotics were used at the following concentrations: erythromycin, 1 μg/mL; zeocin, 20 μg/mL and spectinomycin, 100 μg/mL for B. velezensis strains, ampicillin 100 μg/mL and kanamycin 30 μg/mL for E. coli. AI-2 was purchased from Omm Scientific Inc (Texas, USA), and all AI-2 solutions were diluted with ddH2O to the desired concentration for the experiment.

Mutant and complementation strain construction

The rapA and rapC deficient marker-free mutants of B. velezensis were constructed using the Pbc-pheS*-cat (PC) cassette and overlap-PCR-based strategy as described by Xu et al. 27. The kinA and kinB, kinC, and kinD double mutants were constructed using the same method. All the mutants were verified by DNA sequencing. For the complementation of comA, we replaced the amyE gene in the genome of the comA mutant with the comA gene and the spectinomycin resistance gene, using the promoter of amyE to drive the expression of both the comA gene and the spectinomycin resistance gene. We initially screened transformants that grew on spectinomycin plates and subsequently sequenced comA with validation primers to confirm successful complementation.

Protein expression and purification

The plasmid pCold TF DNA was used for expressing RapC. The recombined plasmid carrying the RapC coding sequence was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3)ΔluxS for expression. The transformed E. coli BL21 (DE3)ΔluxS was cultured at 37 °C to OD600 of 0.5. Isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopryanoside (IPTG) was added with a final concentration of 0.5 mM to induce protein expression at 16 °C overnight. The protein was purified from the lysate of the bacterial cells by NGC Chromatography system (BioRad, CA, USA) with nickel column. Thrombin was used to remove the tag, and a nickel column was used to purify the tag-free RapC protein.

The plasmid pET29a(+) was used for the expression of ComA following the method described by Liu et al. 28. The recombinant plasmid was transformed into E. coli Top10 and E. coli BL21 (DE3)ΔluxS, the transformants were selected on LB agar medium with Kanamycin and verified by sequencing. The His-tagged protein was purified by His-affinity resin chromatography. The purified proteins were collected and stored in a PBS buffer at −80 °C. All proteins were evaluated by Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA).

Quantitative and global transcription analysis

B. velezensis SQR9 was inoculated to MSgg medium. AI-2 (purchased by Omm Scientific Inc, Texas, USA) was added to the medium to a final concentration of 4 μM. After 12 h, bacterial samples were collected for RNA extraction. Total RNA extraction was performed using a Bacterial RNA kit (OMEGA, Biotek, USA) following the instructions. The extracted RNA was checked using Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA).

For qRT-PCR, extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScriptTM RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). The gene quantitative PCR was performed using TB green Premix EX Taq (Takara) with a QuantStudio 6 Flex (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). The following PCR program was used: cDNA was denatured for 30 s at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles consisting of 5 s at 95 °C and 34 s at 60 °C. Primers were provided in Supplementary Table 2. The 2-∆∆CT method was used to analyze the real-time PCR data. The recA gene was included as an internal control. Each treatment included three independent replicates.

For RNA-seq, the library was prepared and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000, and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated at the Beijing Allwegene Technology Company, Beijing, China. Clean reads were obtained after quality control and mapped to the reference genome using Tophat2. RNA-Seq data was normalized to FPKM (fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped). P value was calculated using a negative binomial distribution-based test and false discovery rate (FDR) was calculated using BH (Benjamini/ Hochberg). Sequence data was deposited on the Sequence Read Archive (SRA). The SRA accession number is PRJNA673673. Three replicates were included for each treatment.

Determination of spore formation of Bacillus

The sporulation frequency of SQR9 and the derivated strains were determined at 12 h post-inoculation (the time point shown differentially expressing profile of sporulation genes). Strain SQR9 was inoculated into the flask with 100 mL MSgg medium to a final concentration of 1% inoculum volume and cultured for 12 h at 37 °C. AI-2 was added to the medium to a final concentration of 2 μM, 4 μM, and 10 μM, respectively, at the beginning of cultivation. Afterthen, 1 mL of bacterial culture was collected, one half was diluted and spread on LB agar for counting all living cells, another half was treated with an 80 °C water bath for 20 min to kill the non-sporulating cells29, diluted, and spread on LB agar for counting the spore number. The ratio of spores to the total cells was recorded as sporulation frequency. Three independent replicates were included for each treatment.

Bio-Layer Interferometry (BLI) assays

BLI experiments were performed using an Octet RED96 instrument (Pall ForteBio, CA, USA) at 25 °C in Modified Kinetics buffer (1× PBS, 0.05% Tween-20). Super Streptavidin (SSA) biosensors (Pall ForteBio, CA, USA) were pre-equilibrated in the buffer for 10 min at room temperature. The sensor loading by RapC was performed for 5 min and the RapC concentration was 50 μg/mL. In this experiment, two associations were performed. The first association was solution I, which was a different quorum sensing signal or buffer (CSF; AI-2; (CSF + AI-2); Buffer); the second association was solution II, which was a mixture of the corresponding solution I and protein ComA ((CSF+ComA); (AI-2+ComA); (CSF + AI-2+ComA); ComA); and the final dissociation was also carried out in the same solution I as the first association. The concentration of CSF and AI-2 was the same in solution I and II, and the concentration of ComA was the same in solution II. The direct binding of RapC protein to AI-2 is similar to the above. The RapC protein was loaded with SSA biosensors, and then the protein was associated and dissociated from different concentrations of AI-2. The association and dissociation time were 90 s. The AI-2 concentrations range from 200 µM to 12.5 µM with a two-fold dilution. The data were analyzed using the global fitting algorithm included in the Octet Data Analysis Software 9.0 (Pall ForteBio, CA, USA). Three replicates were included for each treatment.

Native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

The HEPES native gel electrophoresis was performed using the Precast-GL gel with 12% polyacrylamide (Art. No. C601102, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The electrophoresis conditions were carried out according to the product instructions. AI-2, CSF, purified RapC, and ComA, if needed, were added to PBS to a final concentration of 100 μM,10 μM,10 μM or 15 μM, and 10 μM, respectively. The mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 20 min. After the end of incubation, loading buffer (12 mM Tris-HCl, 0.02% bromophenol blue, 5% glycerol, and 2.88 mM β-mercaptoethanol) was added and mixed, and the sample loading volume was 20 μL. Three replicates were included for each treatment. The observation was performed with Gel Doc XR (Bio-Rad, CA, USA).

Treatment of SQR9 with E. coli culture supernatant

The cultures of E. coli BL21 and luxS gene mutant (BL21(DE3)ΔluxS) were inoculated into 100 mL MSgg medium with 1% inoculum. After 18 h at 37 °C, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered by a 0.22 μm filter and freeze-dried into powder. The powder generated from 100 mL fermentation was dissolved with 2 mL PBS, and the solution was filtered with a 0.22 μm filter. Finally, 2 mL of the filtrate was added to 100 mL MSgg medium, and 2 mL PBS was added as a control in parallel. Meanwhile, the medium was inoculated with SQR9. After culturing at 37 °C for 12 h, the sporulation frequency was measured. Three replicates were included for each treatment.

Co-culture experiment of B. velezensis and E. coli

First, culture B. velezensis and E. coli separately in LB medium. When the cell density reached OD600 of 1.0, inoculate the strain into MSgg medium at a ratio of 1:10 (v/v). The bacteria in MSgg was cultured at 37 °C, 170 rpm for 18 h, after that, the cultures were used to assess the spore formation of SQR9. Three replicates were included for each treatment.

Sporulation of SQR9 in soil

The soil is collected from Nanjing (32°03’ N, 118°37'12” E) of Jiangsu Province, China. The soil used in this study is Yellow-brown soil. The soil was air-dried, ground, and screened through a 2 mm sieve, sterilized in transparent bottles, and cooled and dried at room temperature for 3 days. Afterward, soil pH, moisture, and water holding capacity were determined according to the standard methods30. The soil pH is 6.74 and the maximum water-holding capacity is 40.1% (Mw/Ms).

For sporulation of SQR9, 30 g of sterilized soil was transferred into a 100 mL sterile flask, and bacterial cells of SQR9 grown to log stage were centrifuged and resuspended in sterile 10 mM MgCl2 solution to a final OD600 of 0.6. Then, the corresponding volume of the bacterial suspension with different concentrations of AI-2 solution was inoculated into the flask. Then, the flasks were placed in a plant incubator at 30 °C, with 16 h light, and 8 h night. Briefly, SQR9 was inoculated into sterile soil with a final concentration of 107 CFUs/g, and AI-2 was added to the soil with a final concentration of 50 μM, 100 μM, and 200 μM, respectively, and an equal volume of sterile water as a control. The sporulation frequency of SQR9 was measured at 2-day post-inoculation. AI-2 was applied with a much higher concentration than that used in the liquid medium experiment because the soil particles are expected to withhold part or the majority of the exogenously added compound. We found that 200 μM AI-2 could inhibit the sporulation of SQR9 in the soil under normal water content. According to the drought grade standard of the ‘People’s Republic of China Water Conservancy Industry Standard (SL424-2008)’, the relative humidity of the soil between 50–60% is mild drought, 40–50% is moderate drought, 30–40% is severe drought, and less than 30% is extremely drought. Similarly, water was added to the soil to create a relative soil moisture content of 60% (regular moisture) and 40% (moderate drought), respectively. That is, the soil with 40% soil relative humidity is 16.04 g of water in 100 g of soil, and the soil with 60% soil relative humidity is 100 g of soil containing 24.06 g of water. Three replicates were included for each treatment.

Statistical methods

Results are expressed as Mean ± SD (standard deviations). Statistical significance of differences between the two experimental groups was carried out using Student’s T test in GraphPad Prism 9.0 software and a two-tailed was taken to indicate statistical significance.

Results

AI-2 down-regulates spore formation of B. velezensis SQR9

Our previous study demonstrated that the AI-2 mutant of B. velezensis SQR9 (SQR9/ΔluxS) was impaired in biofilm formation and root colonization14. To explore how AI-2 influences gene expression in SQR9, RNA-seq analysis revealed 1319 genes to be up-regulated and 1459 genes to be down-regulated in the SQR9 strain 12 h after treatment with AI-2. AI-2 treatment enriched various differentially expressed genes (DEGs) related to cell wall biogenesis and lipid glycosylation, which are major processes contributing to sporulation (Fig. 1a)31. Moreover, genes related to sporulation in B. velezensis SQR9 were significantly down-regulated at the late growth stage after AI-2 addition, including expression of spore coat protein-coding genes (Supplementary Fig. 1). qRT-PCR was used to verify these findings, demonstrating that the transcription of sporulation genes has indeed been sharply reduced by AI-2 (Fig. 1b). Subsequently, evaluation of the sporulation frequency in response to AI-2 revealed that addition of 4 μM exogenous AI-2 decreased the spore level to 0.1–1% in comparison with the wild-type SQR9 frequency of about 8% in the absence of AI-2, while the AI-2 synthesis deficiency mutant SQR9/ΔluxS displayed 11% frequency (Fig. 1c). Importantly, titration of AI-2 showed that the sporulation frequency was decreased along with the increase of AI-2 (Fig. 1c). These results indicated that both self-produced or exogenous AI-2 contributed to the inhibition of B. velezensis SQR9 spore formation. The result of spore formation curves of SQR9 also indicated that AI-2 significantly inhibited spore formation at 12 h and 24 h after inoculation. Meanwhile, there was no significant difference observed in spore formation between AI-2 treatment and untreated control at 36 h and 60 h after inoculation (Supplementary Fig. 2).

RapC, a regulator of Spo0A phosphatase is required for AI-2-dependent regulation of sporulation

The initiation of spore development is regulated by the global transcription factor, Spo0A in Bacilli. To explore whether AI-2 targets the Spo0A pathway to inhibit spore formation, the expressions of the Spo0A-P regulated genes such as spoIIAA, spoIIAB, and spoIIGA, were investigated using qRT-PCR. The results revealed that expression of spoIIAA, spoIIAB, and spoIIGA were indeed down-regulated upon AI-2 addition (Fig. 2a), while expression of abrB, a regulator gene that transcriptionally is repressed by Spo0A32, was increased by AI-2 addition, suggesting that AI-2 might affect sporulation through the Spo0A pathway.

The error bars indicate the standard deviations based on three independently replicated experimental values. “*” represents a significant difference (p < 0 .05), “ns” indicates no significant difference.

High levels of Spo0A-P initiate spore formation, and this condition is derived from the phosphorylation of the kinase KinA, KinB, KinC and KinD on the phosphorylation transport chains of Spo0F and Spo0B33, of which KinA and KinB are mainly responsible for the activation of spore formation behavior via the phosphorelay, while KinC and KinD mainly play an important regulatory role during initiation of biofilm development34,35. In addition, the phosphorylation of Spo0A is also negatively regulated by RapA and RapB phosphatase, and the RapC protein of the same family can inhibit the transcription of RapA and RapB by binding ComA and influencing its activity to bind the promoter of the target genes36. Moreover, phosphatases YnzD, YisI, and Spo0E can directly dephosphorylate Spo0A-P24. Quantitative analysis of the expression of these kinase-coding genes (KinA, KinB, KinC, and KinD) showed no significant changes upon AI-2 addition, but the genes coding for phosphatase RapA and RapC were up- and down-regulated, respectively (Fig. 2b). To further confirm whether the phosphatases or histidine kinases are involved in AI-2-dependent regulation of sporulation in B. velezensis SQR9, the genes coding for the respective kinases (SQR9/ΔkinAΔkinB, SQR9/ΔkinCΔkinD) or phosphatases (SQR9/ΔrapA, SQR9/ΔrapB and SQR9/ΔrapC) were deleted, and their sporulation efficiency was assayed in response to AI-2. As expected from the observations in B. subtilis, spore formation of kinase mutant SQR9/ΔkinAΔkinB was significantly reduced, while the mutant SQR9/ΔkinCΔkinD was similar to that of wild-type in B. velezensis (Supplementary Fig. 3)37. However, exogenous AI-2 could still reduce the initiation of sporulation in SQR9/ΔkinCΔkinD strains (Supplementary Fig. 3), suggesting that the kinases are not involved in AI-2-dependent regulation of spore development in B. velezensis SQR9. The SQR9/ΔrapA, SQR9/ΔrapB, and SQR9/ΔrapC mutants all displayed comparable sporulation frequency to the wild-type SQR9. While exogenous AI-2 could reduce sporulation in SQR9/ΔrapA and SQR9/ΔrapB mutants, deletion of rapC gene has completely prevented the sporulation inhibition by AI-2 (Fig. 2c), indicating that RapC is necessary for AI-2-mediated inhibition of spore development in B. velezensis SQR9.

ComA, a regulator of rapC gene expression is involved in AI-2-mediated sporulation inhibition

ComA is a global regulator identified in most Bacilli and activates gene expression of rapC, phrC, srfA, and rapA38. RapC additionally functions as a suppressor of ComA by direct binding, during which it suppresses the binding of ComA to its target promoters, including that of rapC39. This feedback mechanism leads to a balance between RapC expression and ComA activity. Interestingly, the deletion of comA dramatically reduced the sporulation and changed the colony morphology of B. velezensis SQR9, and the complemented strain was restored to wild-type levels (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Figure 4), indicating that ComA plays an important role in the initiation of sporulation. AI-2 had no influence on sporulation in the absence of comA, i.e. in the absence of strong sporulation (Fig. 3a). To figure out whether AI-2 acts upstream of ComA, a known ComA target gene, srfA involved in the synthesis of surfactin was evaluated for its transcription in the presence or absence of AI-2 using different mutant strains. The results showed that the regulation of srfA by AI-2 is lost in strains lacking RapC or ComA (Fig. 3b). The activity of RapC to suppress ComA has been previously described to be inhibited by the PhrC pentapeptide (ERGMT), also known as the competence and sporulation-stimulating factor (CSF), which interacts with RapC to suppress its binding of ComA38. Unexpectedly, the deletion of phrC did not influence the AI-2-dependent inhibition of sporulation (Fig. 3a). Since ComA plays a critical role in sporulation (Fig. 3a), we speculated that AI-2 might influence the interaction between RapC and ComA to influence the sporulation, and additionally also the transcription of rapC.

AI-2 interacts with RapC to stimulate its binding to ComA

RapC is an inhibitor of ComA, we wondered how AI-2 influences the interaction between RapC and ComA. Bio-Layer Interferometry (BLI) assay was performed to measure the binding activity between RapC and AI-2. The results indicated that AI-2 could directly bind to RapC, with a KD value of 566 µM (Fig. 4a). The interaction between ComA and RapC was also assessed in vitro using a BLI assay, which revealed a KD value of 13.5 nM between RapC and ComA (Fig. 4b). When the suppressor peptide, CSF was added, the KD value for RapC-ComA binding has increased to 26.8 nM (Fig. 4b), indicating a weaker binding activity in the presence of CSF. In contrast, when AI-2 was supplemented in the assay, the KD value for RapC-ComA binding decreased to 5.49 nM, and even in the presence of CSF, AI-2 reduced the KD value to 8.69 nM (Fig. 4b). These results indicate that AI-2 stimulates binding of RapC to ComA even when the repressor CSF was present. Native gel binding assays were additionally performed to evaluate the direct binding of RapC to ComA that was slightly influenced by the presence of CSF (Fig. 4c). When AI-2 was added, the fraction of free RapC (i.e. unbound by ComA) was reduced, indicating that AI-2 has stimulated the binding between ComA and RapC (Fig. 4d). Collectively, AI-2 possibly inhibits sporulation in B. velezensis SQR9 by stimulating the binding of RapC to ComA, resulting the suppression of ComA activity.

The AI-2 molecule is a cross-species signal influencing Bacillus sporulation and maintenance under moderate drought stress

Since the structure of AI-2 is conserved among different species, we wondered whether the AI-2 produced by a Gram-negative species has an impact on the sporulation of a Gram-positive bacterium. Therefore, Escherichia coli BL21 was investigated for its effect on sporulation of B. velezensis SQR9. The addition of E. coli BL21 supernatant significantly reduced the sporulation frequency of B. velezensis SQR9, while the supernatant from the AI-2-deficient mutant of E. coli BL21 did not inhibit the sporulation of B. velezensis SQR9 (Fig. 5a). When SQR9 was co-cultured with E. coli BL21, the sporulation of SQR9 was inhibited, but when co-cultured with E. coli BL21 luxS mutant, the SQR9 sporulation inhibition was weakened (Fig. 5b). These results indicate that AI-2 is not only a molecule responsible for self-density sensing to inhibit the dormancy, but also is a cross-species signal for Bacillus to sense the density of non-self bacteria.

Bacillus spp. are native soil bacteria and drought is a serious stress in soil that drives Bacilli to form spores. We tested whether AI-2 can prevent Bacillus from falling into inactive dormancy under drought stress in soil (Supplementary Fig. 5). The effect of AI-2 on SQR9 sporulation was assessed under relative soil water content of 40% (moderate drought stress) and 60% (no drought stress). Indeed, AI-2 decreased the sporulation frequency of SQR9 under moderate drought stress as well as under normal conditions (Fig. 5c).

Discussion

AI-2 is an important QS signal regulating various bacterial behaviors in both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, but the regulation mechanism in Gram-positive bacteria remains unclear. In this study, we demonstrated that AI-2 inhibits sporulation of B. velezensis, revealing a novel function of AI-2. AI-2 directly interacts with RapC by stimulating its repression of ComA activity, a positive regulator of sporulation, and therefore inhibiting sporulation. This novel regulatory mechanism by AI-2 on reducing the initiation of sporulation is summarized in Fig. 6. At low cell density, there is a lack of CSF and AI-2 within cells, RapC binds to ComA and inhibits its activity, so spore formation is inhibited at a low level of Spo0A phosphorylation in SQR9. At high cell density, but with low AI-2 levels, CSF is transported into cells, CSF inhibits the binding of RapC to ComA, and ComA has normal activity to promote phosphorylation of Spo0A to further promote spore formation. In the case of the wild-type cells, at relatively high cell density, both CSF and AI-2 are imported into cells, and AI-2 promotes the binding of RapC to ComA to inactivate ComA even in the presence of CSF, which eventually leads to inhibition of spore formation in SQR9. Our study additionally revealed a cross-species regulatory function of AI-2 from the Gram-negative E. coli toward the Gram-positive B. velezensis, and an ecologically-relevant function of AI-2 in relieving the dormancy of Bacillus in the soil.

Schematic representation of AI-2 influences on sporulation.

Initiation of B. velezensis sporulation depends on the level of Spo0A phosphorylation, which is regulated by ComA via an unknown mechanism. RapC represses the DNA-binding ability of ComA by direct protein-protein interaction. In the presence of CSF, the binding of RapC with ComA is suppressed, while in the presence of AI-2, the binding of RapC with ComA is enhanced, leading to the reduced phosphorylation of Spo0A and diminished initiation of sporulation.

AI-2 is known to regulate numerous bacterial behaviors, including motility, virulence, biofilm formation, bioluminescence, colony morphology, and type III secretion20. For example, AI-2 regulates the motility of Helicobacter pylori and Edwardsiella piscicida40,41, the production of virulence factors in Edwardsiella piscicida, Streptococcus agalactiae, and Vibrio cholerae41–43, and also the expression of bioluminescence, biofilm formation, type III secretion, and protease production in V. harveyi22. Cell-cell communication signals (e.g. Phr peptides) have so far been suggested to positively influence initiation of sporulation. Four Phr peptides have been identified that regulate sporulation, CSF, the PhrA peptide, the PhrE peptide, and the PhrH peptide44,45. These Phr peptides consist of five or six amino acids. CSF and the PhrA peptide are derived from precursors encoded by the genes, phrC and phrA, respectively46,47. They inhibited the dephosphorylation of spo0F-P by binding to RapB and RapA, respectively. The other pentapeptide PhrE is encoded by phrE, and it can inhibit the phosphatase activity of RapE, which dephosphorylating spo0F-P. The hexapeptide PhrH is encoded by phrH and is most likely dependent on sigma A factor48. PhrH is the antagonist of RapH, which promotes the dephosphorylation of Spo0F-P45. The four Phr molecules can inhibit the dephosphorylation of spo0F-P by suppressing Rap proteins phosphatase activity. Then, the phosphate group of spo0F-P can eventually be passed to spo0A, and then initiate spore formation49. In contrast, AI-2 inhibits the initiation of sporulation of SQR9. There is no doubt that spore formation was induced at high cell densities in the medium (Supplementary Fig. 2). In the natural environment, because AI-2 can also be produced by other bacteria, a high concentration of AI-2 does not reflect a high cell density of the strain itself, so a higher AI-2 concentration may be an indicator of high cell density of the whole bacterial community, which also indicate a nutrient-rich environment for growth. We propose that SQR9 may sense the AI-2 concentration as an indicator of a nutrient-rich environment, and therefore delay sporulation for better growth, but a real starvation signal could cover the AI-2 effect, as this study and other publications reported a high sporulation frequency at late growth stages in the medium. It is speculated that AI-2 might enhance the plant-promoting effects of a rhizosphere-beneficial Bacillus as previously demonstrated14. In soil with suitable water conditions, most of the Bacilli inoculated into the soil are vegetative cells, which can swim freely to colonize the rhizosphere. In soil under moderate drought stress, spore formation of soil-inoculated Bacilli increases; therefore, the number of vegetative cells decreases, and fewer bacterial cells swim and colonize the rhizosphere. When AI-2 is added to moderate drought-stressed soil, the stress-induced dormancy is alleviated and rhizosphere colonization of Bacilli can increase (Fig. 7). Indeed, supplementation of AI-2 reduced the sporulation frequency of Bacilli under drought stress as well as under normal condition (Fig. 5c), suggesting its potential role in promoting activity of this plant-beneficial bacterium.

Schematic representation of how AI-2 may influence bacteria in soil.

In soil without drought stress, Bacilli colonize plant rhizosphere in large quantities. In soils with moderate drought stress, the dormancy of Bacilli increases, and rhizosphere colonization is reduced. When AI-2 is added to the soil with moderate drought stress, the stress-induced dormancy is alleviated and rhizosphere colonization by Bacilli is increased.

Bacilli are well-known model microbes for sporulation, a well-studied dormancy process initiated by sensing disadvantageous environmental cues, such as nutrient exhaustion33. QS is a typically social behavior during which bacteria detect the cell density and exert function as a population. Our results in this study indicated that sporulation is not only influenced by individual bacterial behavior but also by the population-level QS process. The observation that E. coli was capable of inhibiting sporulation of B. velezensis via AI-2 highlights that initiation of sporulation might be influenced at the bacterial community level, not simply within the species or genus. At the bacterial community level, delaying sporulation in the presence of high AI-2 concentration might create a critical “decision” by the Bacillus cells to avoid falling into dormancy even at low nutrient availability or under drought-stress conditions. This decision may benefit the population in a moderately stressed environment.

Most QS studies were aimed at Gram-negative bacteria and their major QS molecule, AHLs5. We previously found that AI-2 regulates the biofilm formation of B. velezensis SQR914, while AI-2 has also been reported to regulate many other physiological processes of bacteria20, however, the molecular mechanisms remain mostly unknown in Bacilli. More and more cell-cell signaling systems are known in the B. subtilis group, and the two of the more clearly studied are regulated by oligopeptide pheromones, ComX and CSF50, while AI-2 is a general signaling molecule possibly recognized by most bacteria producing this substance20. Interestingly, all of these three molecules exert direct or indirect influence as cell-cell signaling molecules by modulating the activity of ComA50. CSF enhances ComA activity and thereby stimulates downstream gene expression by binding to RapC, the repressor of ComA38. While the transcription-activated function of ComA also relies on its phosphorylation, which is regulated by the signaling peptide ComX via the membrane-bound histidine kinase ComP44, the repression of ComA by RapC is independent of the phosphorylation of ComA51. In this study, we revealed that the sporulation inhibitory effect of AI-2 depends on RapC and ComA, but not on CSF. Although both CSF and AI-2 influence RapC, their effects are opposite. Our proposed model in B. velezensis depicts that inhibition of ComA activity by RapC and induction of rapC expression by ComA create a negative feedback loop, in which interaction of RapC with ComA inhibits the activity of ComA to bind rapC promoter, therefore suppresses rapC expression and reduces the inhibition of ComA activity. Moreover, the suppression of ComA activity by RapC can be inhibited by CSF. When CSF is present, the transcriptional activation activity of ComA is restored, when both CSF and AI-2 are present, AI-2 could bind RapC to relieve the inhibition of RapC activity by CSF, therefore promoting RapC to bind ComA to suppress the activity of ComA (Fig. 6). This model could also explain the reduced expression of rapC when AI-2 is added into the SQR9 cultivation system (Fig. 2b). However, the model in B. subtilis is different, in which ComA affects the expression of RapA. The RapA4-PhrA4 system has been found in Bacillus velezensis NAU-B3, in which RapA4 can inhibit spore formation by binding to ComA to make it lose transcriptional activating activity, and PhrA4 can relieve the inhibitory function of RapA452. However, RapA cannot bind to ComA, it is targeted to spo0F in B. subtilis53. The AI-2 mode of action in the Gram-positive B. velezensis is very different from that of the Gram-negative Vibrio species. In Vibrio, AI-2 binds to LuxPQ, a membrane-bound histidine kinase acting as a receptor, to regulate the phosphorylation of LuxO, the response regulator that acts as a repressor of downstream gene expression when its phosphorylation level is high54. Here, we revealed for the first time how AI-2 overrides the influence of another QS signal, CSF, and therefore influences ComA-regulated processes, including activation of sporulation. These results largely expand the knowledge of QS signaling in bacteria, especially in Bacilli.

Surprisingly, we noticed that AI-2 induced srfA expression (Fig. 3b) and reduced sporulation (Fig. 3a) in a ComA-dependent manner, but phosphorylated ComA induced both syntheses of surfactin and sporulation55,56. Moreover, in the presence of AI-2, the swarming of SQR9 which depends on ComA, was also increased14. The opposite influence of AI-2 on the synthesis of surfactin and initiation of sporulation in Bacilli is unexpected and future research is needed to describe the underlying mechanism. We hypothesized that the ComA regulon is differentially affected depending on its interaction partner. This hypothesized model has been observed for SinR, which represses biofilm genes alone and represses motility genes when bound with SlrR57.

The structure of AHLs is variable among species even from one strain to another, however, the structure of AI-2 is conserved in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria58. It suggests that AI-2 might circulate as a shared signaling molecule in microbial communities, and therefore can be produced and sensed by distinct bacteria in a certain local environment. Here, we demonstrated that E. coli BL21 influences the sporulation of B. velezensis and confirmed the contribution of AI-2 in this process. AI-2 can be produced by various bacteria in a community and sensed as a public signal molecule by different bacterial members. Due to the function of AI-2 affecting chemotaxis, biofilm formation, and virulence of bacteria59, the property as an inter-species signal indicates that it might act during microbiome assembly and might contribute to the regulation of functional traits within diverse bacteria in a certain environmental niche.
