Sequential Fair Allocation With Replenishments: A Little Envy Goes An Exponentially Long Way
Chido Onyeze, Sean R. Sinclair, Chamsi Hssaine, Siddhartha Banerjee

Abstract
We study the trade-off between envy and inefficiency in repeated resource allocation settings with stochastic replenishments, motivated by real-world systems such as food banks and medical supply chains. Specifically, we consider a model in which a decision-maker faced with stochastic demand and resource donations must trade off between an equitable and efficient allocation of resources over an infinite horizon. The decision-maker has access to storage with fixed capacity M M , and incurs efficiency losses when storage is empty (stockouts) or full (overflows). We provide a nearly tight (up to constant factors) characterization of achievable envy-inefficiency pairs. Namely, we introduce a class of Bang-Bang control policies whose inefficiency exhibits a sharp phase transition, dropping from Θ ⁡ ( 1 / M ) \Theta(1/M) when Δ = 0 \Delta=0 to e − Ω ⁡ ( Δ ​ M ) e^{-\Omega(\Delta M)} when Δ > 0 \Delta>0 , where Δ \Delta is used to denote the target envy of the policy. We complement this with matching lower bounds, demonstrating that the trade-off is driven by supply , as opposed to demand uncertainty. Our results demonstrate that envy-inefficiency trade-offs not only persist in settings with dynamic replenishment, but are shaped by the decision-maker’s available capacity, and are therefore qualitatively different compared to previously studied settings with fixed supply.

Introduction

In many shared resource allocation settings — food banks, power systems, medical supply chains, donation centers, etc. — a decision-maker faced with stochastic supply and demand seeks to achieve two objectives: allocating as much supply as she has on-hand, while also serving the agents sharing the resource as equitably as possible (equivalently, minimizing the
envy
agents incur as a result of inequitable allocations). In attempting to achieve these objectives, the decision-maker is faced with two sources of inefficiency: stockouts in periods where demand exceeds supply, and overflow in periods where supply exceeds demand. For example, soon after the start of COVID lockdowns, Feeding America reported that
98\%
of food banks experienced an increase in demand for food assistance, with

59\%
of these having low inventory levels
(
FeedingAmerica 2020
)
.
More recently, in the aftermath of the January 2025 wildfires in Los Angeles,
the overwhelming influx of clothing donations impeded relief efforts due to inventory overflow
(
The Guardian 2025
)
.
These high-profile examples reflect the persistent challenges faced in shared resource environments, and underscore the need for equitable allocation policies that are robust to these two sources of inefficiency.

The trade-off between envy and inefficiency has been extensively studied of late, with many works characterizing the Pareto frontier between these objectives under various models
(
Bertsimas et al. 2011
,
Lien et al. 2014
,
Donahue and Kleinberg 2020
,
Manshadi et al. 2023
,
Sinclair et al. 2023
,
Banerjee et al. 2023
,
Liao et al. 2022
,
Yin and Kroer 2022
)
.
Building on this line of work, this paper examines how stochasticity in
supply
(e.g., incoming donations in the social-good applications described above) shapes a capacitated decision-maker’s allocation decisions in dynamic settings. In particular, stochastic incoming supply (also referred to as
inventory replenishments
) introduces an important new consideration to existing studies of envy-inefficiency trade-offs: while the decision-maker must allocate conservatively enough to prevent stock-outs, she must also allocate aggressively enough to avoid inventory overflow due to the accumulation of incoming inventory. As a result, perfectly equitable solutions that are not adaptive to current inventory levels are bound to be inefficient, either via frequent stockouts or overflows.

Against this backdrop, this work seeks to answer the following research questions:

Do established envy-inefficiency trade-offs in dynamic environments persist in the presence of stochastic supply with capacity constraints? Can we design policies that achieve the optimal trade-off?

1.1
Main Contributions

We consider a model in which a decision-maker allocates divisible resources between agents arriving online over an infinite horizon. The decision-maker has access to a storage of size
M
. We first consider a setting with a single resource and homogeneous agents. At the beginning of period
t
, the decision-maker receives a stochastic resource donation. She then observes a random number of new agents, each requesting a share of the resource, and chooses how much to allocate to each agent. If the total amount allocated to agents exceeds the amount of available inventory (an event we refer to as a
stockout
), we assume the decision-maker covers the additional amount required using an outside option (at a cost). On the other hand, if the amount of remaining inventory exceeds the available storage capacity
M
, any overflow must also be thrown out, similarly at a cost.

The decision-maker evaluates the quality of her policy according to the following three metrics: (i) ex-post envy
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}
, which represents the difference between the maximum and minimum allocations across all rounds, (ii) the overflow (or waste)
\overline{W}
, which represents the long-run average amount of resource thrown out at the end of each period due to overflow, and (iii) the stockout rate
\overline{V}
, representing the long-run average amount of resource that needs to be acquired from an outside option due to stockouts. Given
\overline{W}
and
\overline{V}
, we define a combined inefficiency metric
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}=h\overline{W}+b\overline{V}
, where
h,b
respectively denote the per-unit overflow/stockout costs.

Figure 1
:
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}
vs
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}
for various values of
M
. Each curve illustrates the theoretical lower bound on the achievable inefficiency as a function of
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}
. Even modest increases in
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}
lead to exponential reductions in inefficiency. Moreover, for fixed
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}
, increasing capacity (darker lines) improves efficiency.

Our main contribution is to demonstrate that the trade-off between envy and inefficiency exhibits a
sharp phase transition
in capacitated settings with stochastic replenishments. In particular:

1.

We first demonstrate the inefficiency of static allocation policies by showing that the natural
proportional allocation rule
that allocates the expected donation per agent in each period (thereby incurring
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}=0
) leads to
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}
scaling as
\Theta(M^{-1})
(
Theorem

4.5
). This is furthermore the best scaling for
any
static allocation, with all other static allocation policies incurring
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}=\Theta(1)
(
Theorem

4.8
).

2.

We subsequently show that introducing a limited amount of envy generates
exponential
gains in efficiency. Namely, we propose a simple Bang-Bang policy parameterized by
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}>0
, which over-
(resp., under-) allocates by
\frac{\Delta_{\text{Fair}}}{2}
, depending on the current inventory level
. We prove that this algorithm achieves

\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}=e^{-\Omega(\Delta_{\text{Fair}}M)}
(
Theorem

4.9
).

3.

We prove that the above trade-off is optimal in terms of scaling
(
Theorem

5.1
):
even under deterministic demand, any policy that guarantees
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}>0
must incur
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}=e^{-O(\Delta_{\text{Fair}}M)}
.

Finally, we show that our results naturally extend to settings with multiple resources and heterogeneous agents, thereby demonstrating the universality of our scaling laws.

From a managerial perspective, our results (illustrated in
Figure

1
) underscore the central role of capacity constraints in shaping fairness-efficiency trade-offs in real-world settings. In particular, not only do larger capacities directly mitigate inefficiency in perfectly fair settings (since it naturally takes longer for inventory levels to deplete, under a fixed allocation), but they also magnify the efficiency gains achievable from modest relaxations in fairness. These findings highlight the importance of explicitly incorporating capacity considerations into the design of fair allocation policies.

1.2
Related Work

The study of fair resource allocation has deep roots in economics and operations research
(
Varian 1974
,
Rawls 2017
,
Scarf et al. 1957
)
. We focus here on fair allocation in dynamic settings; see
Aleksandrov and Walsh 2019
for a comprehensive survey of other settings.

Fair resource allocation.

Dynamic fair allocation typically falls into two categories: (i) a fixed set of agents with online resource arrivals
(
Aleksandrov et al. 2015
,
Mattei et al. 2018
,
Banerjee et al. 2022
,
Bansal et al. 2020
,
Bogomolnaia et al. 2022
,
He et al. 2019
,
Aziz et al. 2016
,
Zeng and Psomas 2020
,
Friedman et al. 2017
)
, and (ii) a fixed resource budget with agents arriving online. Our work fits into the latter stream, where past works have considered alternative fairness objectives, such as Nash Social Welfare
(
Azar et al. 2010
,
Banerjee et al. 2022
)
, max-min fairness
(
Lien et al. 2014
,
Manshadi et al. 2023
)
, and variants of envy-freeness
(
Friedman et al. 2017
,
Cole et al. 2013
,
Friedman et al. 2015
,
Freund and Hssaine 2025
)
. Closest to our work are
Sinclair et al. 2023
, which studied envy-inefficiency trade-offs under a fixed budget, and
Banerjee et al. 2023
, which extended this setting to perishable resources.
Similar to these works, our goal is to characterize the trade-off between envy and inefficiency. However, our setting differs fundamentally due to stochastic supply, which causes the decision-maker’s capacity constraints to become central to the envy-inefficiency trade-off. Moreover, stochastic replenishments align our model with classical inventory frameworks, naturally leading to an infinite-horizon formulation. As a result, our algorithmic and analytical tools diverge significantly from prior work.

Trade-offs in queueing control.

Our model builds on the design of fair control policies in queuing systems, beginning with the foundational work of
Kelly et al. 1998
. Much of this literature focuses on
proportional fairness
and, more generally,
\alpha
-fair objectives that balance equity and efficiency
(
Abbas et al. 2015
,
Raz et al. 2004
)
. Our emphasis on explicit trade-offs is also related to staffing and capacity-sizing results in the Halfin–Whitt regime
(
Halfin and Whitt 1981
)
. There, many-server scaling balances two opposing performance metrics: server idleness (inefficiency) and customer delay (a fairness-related service disparity). In contrast, we study a fixed-capacity (finite-buffer) setting where inefficiency is determined when the inventory level hits the boundaries
\{0,M\}
. This also has connections to large-buffer asymptotics and large deviations
(
Ganesh 2004
)
. In our analysis, we show that the Bang-Bang policy induces a “rate-function” for the stationary distribution at
\{0,M\}
that decays as
e^{-\Theta(\Delta M)}
.

A related thread is the quality-of-service (QoS) control literature, which designs scheduling and routing policies to meet deadlines, while ensuring throughput and stability. Foundational work integrates delay, throughput, and reliability constraints
(
Hou et al. 2009
)
, followed by extensions to multi-hop networks with end-to-end deadlines, and predictive scheduling
(
Singh and Kumar 2018
,
Chen and Huang 2018
)
. We view this line of work as complementary: whereas QoS models trade deadline/latency against throughput and reliability, our model trades ex-post envy against boundary inefficiency under fixed capacity and no waiting.

Preliminaries

In this section we present our basic model, focusing on the setting with a single resource and homogeneous agents, deferring the extension to multiple resources and heterogeneous agents in
Section

6
.

Model Primitives.

We consider a decision-maker who manages a warehouse (also referred to as a
store
) of a single divisible resource over an infinite horizon of discrete time periods. The store has a capacity
M>0
and initially contains
S_{0}\in[0,M]
units of the resource. At the start of period
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the decision-maker receives an additional budget of
B_{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
resources (also referred to as a
donation
); moreover,
N_{t}\in\mathbb{N}
homogeneous agents arrive, each requesting a share of the resource.
The decision-maker must then decide on a per-agent allocation
A_{t}\in[0,A_{\max}]
, where
A_{\max}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a finite upper bound on the maximum possible allocation in each period.
1
1

1

The assumption that each individual receives the same allocation
A_{t}
follows from the fact that agents are homogeneous
(
Varian 1974
)
.
We let
S_{t-1}\in[0,M]
be the inventory level at the start of round
t
. When the total allocation exceeds the post-donation inventory level (i.e.,
S_{t-1}+B_{t}<N_{t}A_{t}
), the decision-maker supplements the allocation via an outside option (at a cost). On the other hand, if the post-allocation inventory level exceeds the store’s capacity
M
, any excess is thrown away. The store’s inventory level is updated at the end of round
t
via the following inductive relation:

S_{t}=(S_{t-1}+B_{t}-N_{t}A_{t})\Big|_{0}^{M},

(1)

where we use the notation
x|_{0}^{M}=\max\{0,\min\{x,M\}\}
to denote the projection of
x
in
[0,M]
. For ease of notation, we define the
inventory drift
in round
t
to be
Z_{t}=B_{t}-N_{t}A_{t}
, noting that
S_{t}=\left.(S_{t-1}+Z_{t})\right|_{0}^{M}
, by
Equation

1
. For ease of notation, we let
Z_{\min}=A_{\max}N_{\max}
and
Z_{\max}=B_{\max}
, noting that
Z_{t}\in[-Z_{\min},Z_{\max}]
for all
t
.

We assume
B_{t}
is drawn i.i.d. from a distribution
\mathcal{B}
supported on a subset of
[0,B_{\max}]
, and let
\mu_{\mathcal{B}}{>0}
denote the mean of
\mathcal{B}
, known to the decision-maker. Similarly,
N_{t}
is drawn i.i.d. from a distribution
\mathcal{N}
supported on a subset of
\{0,\ldots,N_{\max}\}
, and let
\mu_{\mathcal{N}}{>0}
denote its known mean.
2
2

2

The assumption that
N_{t}
is discrete and both
N_{t}
and
B_{t}
are bounded is for technical simplicity. Our results extend naturally for any
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{B}
which are sub-Gaussian.
Finally, we assume
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{N}
are independent of
M
, and that
\mathcal{B}
has strictly positive variance
\sigma_{\mathcal{B}}^{2}
.

Remark 2.1

The assumption that the supply and demand processes are i.i.d. across time is for analytical tractability. In Section
7.3
we numerically demonstrate that our envy-inefficiency laws continue to hold under seasonal supply and demand patterns that one expects to observe in practice.

Policies.

We define an allocation policy
\mathcal{A}
to be a mapping from the current round
t
, the realized supply and demand
B_{t}
and
N_{t}
, and the current inventory level
S_{t-1}
, to a per-individual allocation quantity
A_{t}
.
Policy
\mathcal{A}
is said to be
time-homogeneous
if
it only depends on the current round
t
through the inventory level
S_{t-1}
and the realized demand and supply.
Our achievability results all involve time-homogeneous policies, while our impossibilities hold under arbitrary policies.

Measures of Envy and Inefficiency.

Our aim is to understand the trade-off between
envy
(unfairness) and
inefficiency
, as described in the introduction.
To formalize this trade-off, we measure the
unfairness
of any policy
\mathcal{A}
via its
ex-post
— or
hindsight
—
envy
, in line with prior literature
(
Varian 1974
,
Sinclair et al. 2023
,
Banerjee et al. 2023
)
.

Definition 2.2

(Ex-post envy)

The
ex-post envy
of a sequence of allocations
(A_{t},t\in\mathbb{N})
is given by:

\Delta_{\text{Fair}}\;=\;\sup_{t,t^{\prime}:N_{t},N_{t^{\prime}}>0}\,|A_{t}-A_{t’}|.

(2)

This metric is deliberately stringent: it bounds the disparity between any two agents
anywhere
along the sample path, rather than on average, or with time-discounting. We choose this worst-case metric since average-case metrics can obscure persistent disparities or biases in allocations. For instance, a food bank that distributes equal amounts on most days but shuts down entirely on specific days may exhibit low average envy, but still leaves certain populations (such as those only available on closure days) underserved.

This standard metric of unfairness also helps to motivate the necessity of supplementing (at cost) the allocation in periods of stockout. It is easy to see that if the algorithm is restricted to not allocate anything in periods where the inventory level is zero, it necessarily has
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}=\Omega(1)
; such events are likely to occur over sample paths with prolonged spells of zero donations or surge demand.
This helps motivate our inefficiency metric, which is in line with standard models of inventory control
(
Scarf et al. 1957
)
.
In particular, we define the
overflow
(or
waste
)
W_{t}
incurred in round
t
as the amount of resources lost due to the inability to store more resources than capacity
M
. Formally:

W_{t}=(S_{t-1}+B_{t}-N_{t}A_{t}-M)^{+},

(3)

where
(\cdot)^{+}=\max\{\cdot,0\}
is used to define the positive part. We moreover define the
stockout quantity

V_{t}
in round
t
to be the additional resource the decision-maker needs to acquire in order to allocate
A_{t}
. Formally:

V_{t}=(S_{t-1}+B_{t}-N_{t}A_{t})^{-},

(4)

where
(\cdot)^{-}=-\min\{\cdot,0\}
.
With these definitions in hand, we can define our measure of inefficiency.

Definition 2.3

(Inefficiency)

Given per-unit overflow cost
h\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and per-unit stockout cost
b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, the
inefficiency
of a sequence of allocation decisions
(A_{t},t\in\mathbb{N})
is given by:

\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}=\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}hW_{t}+bV_{t}\right)=h\overline{W}+b\overline{V},

(5)

where
\overline{W}
and
\overline{V}
respectively denote the long-run average overflow and stockouts under
(A_{t},t\in\mathbb{N})
, i.e.:

\overline{W}=\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t},\quad\overline{V}=\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V_{t}.

Remark 2.4

While our measure of inefficiency is natural for the applications motivating our work, our analysis provides explicit bounds on the inventory level’s hitting times of states
\{0,M\}
, as well as the stationary probabilities of these states. Thus, a corollary of our analysis is a scaling law for any inefficiency metric that is a function of these states.

Envy-Inefficiency Trade-off.

We say that a policy achieves the
optimal envy-inefficiency trade-off
if it lies on the Pareto frontier of the two metrics
(\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}},\Delta_{\text{Fair}})
,
defined as all pairs
(\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}},\Delta_{\text{Fair}})
that are the solution to the following optimization problem:

\min_{A_{t},t\in\mathbb{N}}\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}\quad\text{s.t. }\quad\Delta_{\text{Fair}}\leq\Delta\,\text{ almost surely},

(6)

where
\Delta\geq 0
can be thought of as an “unfairness budget” given to the allocation policy. It is easy to see that, for any
\Delta\geq 0
, there exists an optimal solution to
Equation

6
such that
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}=\Delta
. As a result, in the remainder of this work we use
\Delta
and
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}
interchangeably.

Note that a trivial part of the Pareto frontier is obtained by considering
\Delta\rightarrow+\infty
, in which case one optimal policy is to simply set
A_{t}=(S_{t-1}+B_{t})/N_{t}
(i.e., to allocate the entirety of the available inventory) whenever
N_{t}>0
.
This results in the minimum possible
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}\approx 0
, where the inefficiency corresponds to the average amount by which the total incoming budget exceeds
M
over a stretch of periods with zero demand. Our focus is thus on the scaling behavior of
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}
as a function of
M
and
\Delta
.

Envy-Inefficiency Scaling Laws: Intuition and Technical Challenges

Our key observation in the derivation of our envy-inefficiency scaling laws is that the long-run average overflow (respectively, stockout quantity) is proportional to the long-run average fraction of time for which
S_{t}=M
(resp.,
S_{t}=0
). Therefore, it suffices to characterize the steady-state probability that
S_{t}\in\{0,M\}
. In general, however,
S_{t}
is a complex Markov chain over a continuous state space; consequently, there are few standard tools for characterizing its steady-state measure. In this section, we build some intuition into the form of our final envy-inefficiency scaling laws by considering more tractable processes that approximate the inventory dynamics of our real system at a high level. We then preview the technical challenges in translating these insights to the real dynamics.

be this stylized birth-death process, with discrete state space
\{0,1,\ldots,M\}
. Moreover,

A_{t}
that perturbs
\mu_{\mathcal{B}}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}
by
\pm\Delta_{\text{Fair}}/2
, as our Bang-Bang policy will do in
Section

4.3
.

Solving for the stationary distribution
\pi
in this case,
we obtain
\pi[0]=\pi[M]=\left(2\cdot\frac{\rho^{M/2}-1}{\rho-1}+\rho^{M/2}\right)^{-1}
, where
\rho=\frac{2p+\Delta_{\text{Fair}}}{2p-\Delta_{\text{Fair}}}
. (We refer the reader to Appendix
A
for a derivation of this fact.) Noting that, for constant
p\in(0,1/2)
and small
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}>0
,
\rho=e^{\Theta(\Delta_{\text{Fair}})}
, we obtain
\pi[0]=\pi[M]=e^{-\Theta(\Delta_{\text{Fair}}M)}
. As a result,
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}=e^{-\Theta(\Delta_{\text{Fair}}M)}
.

Overview of technical challenges.

While the above heuristic arguments provide some intuition into the form of our envy-inefficiency trade-off, the technical challenge lies in formally establishing these results for the actual inventory dynamics, and under a wide range of demand and supply distributions. We briefly highlight some of the main technical ideas.

The main difficulty in bounding the overflow
\overline{W}
and stockout quantity
\overline{V}
under the true inventory dynamics stems from the structure of the process
S_{t}
(as defined in
Equation

1
).
This process forms a Markov chain that evolves continuously within the interior
(0,M)
but has absorbing states
\{0,M\}
, making it challenging to analyze or characterize the steady-state probabilities at these boundaries using standard techniques.
We eschew this difficulty by using a novel renewal-reward argument to express the steady-state mass of states
0
and
M
in terms of their hitting times and hitting probabilities. We then derive bounds for these quantities under the static and Bang-Bang policies we consider via a careful martingale analysis.

4
Envy-Inefficiency Trade-offs under Time-Homogeneous Allocation Policies

As described in
Section

2
, the overflow and stockout quantities
\overline{W}
and
\overline{V}
are the driving measures of inefficiency in our setting. In this section, we develop a general framework for analyzing these for arbitrary time-homogeneous allocation policies, leveraging tools from renewal theory. Building on this, we characterize the extreme point of the envy-inefficiency trade-off when
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}=0
. We then introduce a simple class of adaptive control policies parameterized by a target fairness bound
\Delta
, and use our framework to demonstrate that a limited amount of adaptivity generates exponential gains in efficiency.

4.1
Bounds on Overflow and Stockout

We first establish the intuitive fact that the long-run average waste and stockout quantity respectively grow with the fraction of time the warehouse is full (i.e.,
S_{t}=M
) and empty (i.e.,
S_{t}=0
). For ease of notation, we let
H_{M}=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}\left\{S_{t}=M\right\}
and
H_{0}=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}\left\{S_{t}=0\right\}
under any policy
\mathcal{A}
. Additionally, for any
\alpha\in[0,A_{\max}]
, we let
Z_{t}(\alpha)=B_{t}-\alpha N_{t}
be the drift in round
t
for any policy that allocates
\alpha
in that round.

Proposition 4.1

Consider any allocation policy such that
A_{t}=\alpha_{M}
if
S_{t-1}=M
, and
A_{t}=\alpha_{0}
if
S_{t-1}=0
, for some
\alpha_{M},\alpha_{0}>0
. If
H_{M}
and
H_{0}
exist, then:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{1}(\alpha_{M})^{+}\right]H_{M}\leq\overline{W}\leq Z_{\max}\cdot H_{M}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{1}(\alpha_{0})^{-}\right]H_{0}\leq\overline{V}\leq Z_{\min}\cdot H_{0}.}

We provide some intuition for
Proposition

4.1
, deferring a formal proof to Appendix
B.1
. The upper bound on
\overline{W}
follows from the fact that waste is only incurred in periods where
S_{t}=M
; in these periods, the amount thrown away is the excess over
M
, i.e.,
S_{t-1}+B_{t}-N_{t}A_{t}-M\leq Z_{\max}
. The lower bound, on the other hand, follows from the fact that, in periods where
S_{t-1}=M
, the waste incurred is given by
(B_{t}-N_{t}A_{t})^{+}=Z_{1}(\alpha_{M})^{+}
, by definition. The bounds on
\overline{V}
follow similar lines. Namely, a stockout only occurs in periods
t
such that
S_{t}=0
; in these periods, the maximum amount by which demand can exceed the available budget is
Z_{\min}
. Moreover, whenever
S_{t-1}=0
, the policy allocates
\alpha_{0}
by assumption, incurring an underage of
Z_{1}(\alpha_{0})^{-}
.

Both the static and Bang-Bang policies we analyze in the remainder of this section satisfy the condition that a fixed amount is allocated at the boundaries. Hence, it suffices to characterize
H_{M}
and
H_{0}
in order to obtain tight bounds on the inefficiency of these policies.
Toward this goal, we introduce the following notation:

•

For any initial state
S\in[0,M]
, we let
\tau(S)=\inf\left\{t>0:S_{t}\in\{0,M\}\mid S_{0}=S\right\}
be the first time
S_{t}
hits
0
or
M
, and define
E(S)=\mathbb{E}[\tau(S)]
. Additionally, let
\tau_{M}(S)=\inf\{t>0:S_{t}=M\mid S_{0}=S\}
and
\tau_{0}(S)=\inf\{t>0:S_{t}=0\mid S_{0}=S\}
respectively denote the first time the process reaches
M
or
0
, starting from state
S
.

•

We let
p_{M}=\mathbb{P}(S_{\tau(M)}=M\mid S_{0}=M)
be the probability that, given initial state
M
,
S_{t}
reaches
M
before 0. Similarly
p_{0}=\mathbb{P}(S_{\tau(0)}=0\mid S_{0}=0)
is used to denote the probability that
S_{t}
reaches 0 before
M
, given that initial state 0.

The following theorem, one of the main drivers of our results, relates
H_{M}
and
H_{0}
to the hitting times introduced above.

Theorem 4.2

Fix a time-homogeneous policy
\mathcal{A}
such that
E(M)<\infty
,
E(0)<\infty
,
p_{M}\in(0,1)

p_{0}\in(0,1)
, and
\tau_{M}(S_{0})
and
\tau_{0}(S_{0})
are finite almost surely. Then
H_{M}
and
H_{0}
exist almost surely, and satisfy:

H_{M}=\frac{1}{E(M)+E(0)\cdot\frac{1-p_{M}}{1-p_{0}}},\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;H_{0}=\frac{1}{E(M)\cdot\frac{1-p_{0}}{1-p_{M}}+E(0)}.

(7)

Theorem

4.2
highlights the driving forces behind
H_{M}
and
H_{0}
. Namely, it describes the intuitive phenomenon that policies which take longer to reach either boundary (via an increase in either
E(M)
or
E(0)
) will spend less time at these boundaries. Additionally, it highlights the saliency of the quantity
\frac{1-p_{M}}{1-p_{0}}
. In words,
1-p_{M}
corresponds to the probability that
S_{t}
reaches 0 before
M
given initial state
M
;
1-p_{0}
corresponds to the probability that
S_{t}
reaches
M
before 0 given initial state 0. For higher values of
\frac{1-p_{M}}{1-p_{0}}
, the inventory level drifts away from
M
and toward 0, resulting in a smaller (resp., larger) fraction of time spent at
M
(resp., 0). This relationship also underpins our envy-inefficiency scaling laws: policies that slow the drift toward
0
or
M
(lengthening
E(0)
and
E(M)
) and that avoid heavily favoring one boundary over the other (letting
p_{0}\approx p_{M}
) reduce both stockouts and overflows, at the cost of occasionally larger disparities in per-period allocations.

Proof 4.3

(Proof sketch)

The result follows from a careful application of the Renewal Reward theorem

4.2
Inefficiency of Static Allocation Policies (
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}=0
)

We now characterize the inefficiency of
static
policies, wherein
A_{t}=\alpha
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, for some
\alpha\geq 0
. Note that by definition,
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}=0
for this class of policies.

We first turn our attention to the natural static policy that sets
\alpha^{*}:=\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}
in each period. In the remainder of this work, we refer to this policy as the
proportional

Theorem

4.5
recovers the scaling behavior of the stylized birth-death process analyzed in

Theorem 4.5

The static policy that allocates
\alpha^{*}=\mu_{\mathcal{B}}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}
in each round achieves:

\overline{W}=\Theta(M^{-1})\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\overline{V}=\Theta(M^{-1}).

Therefore,
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}=\Theta(M^{-1})
.

Note that under this policy, the inventory drift
Z_{t}(\alpha^{*})
satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{t}(\alpha^{*})\right]=0
. We will later see that this choice is uniquely optimal across the space of all static policies. Furthermore,
Theorem

4.5
illustrates the poor performance of static policies; in particular, we will later see that extremely simple adaptive policies achieve an inefficiency scaling as

, an exponential improvement over the proportional allocation policy.

We provide a sketch of the proof of

, deferring the complete proof to Appendix

Proof 4.6

(Proof sketch)

For ease of notation, we let
Z_{t}=Z_{t}(\alpha^{*})
for all
t
, and let
\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}^{2}]
. We first argue that the stopping times
E(M),E(0)
and hitting probabilities
p_{M},p_{0}
are well defined and finite, since
\text{Var}[B_{t}]>0
. Thus, by
Proposition

4.1
and

4.2
, it suffices to deduce expressions for

, and

.

We then prove that
p_{M}=1-\Theta(M^{-1})
. We approach this by conditioning on the first step of our random walk. Namely, starting from
S_{0}=M
, if
Z_{1}\geq 0
, then
Q_{t}
necessarily hits
M
before 0. Therefore, it suffices to analyze the likelihood that
Q_{t}
hits
M
before 0 if

. We formalize this via the law of total probability, which gives us:

p_{M}=1-\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}<0)\mathbb{P}(Q_{\tau}\leq 0\mid Z_{1}<0,S_{0}=M).

We provide a closed-form expression for
\mathbb{P}(Q_{\tau}\leq 0\mid Z_{1}<0,S_{0}=M)
by applying the optional stopping theorem to the martingale
(Q_{t},t\geq 1)
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[Q_{\tau}\mid Z_{1}<0,S_{0}=M]=\mathbb{E}[Q_{1}\mid Z_{1}<0,S_{0}=M]
.
Conditioning the left-hand side of this equation on the event
\{Q_{\tau}\leq 0\}
and solving for
\mathbb{P}(Q_{\tau}\leq 0\mid Z_{1}<0,S_{0}=M)
, we establish that

\mathbb{P}(Q_{\tau}\leq 0\mid Z_{1}<0,S_{0}=M)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Q_{\tau}\mid S_{0}=M,Z_{1}<0,Q_{\tau}\geq M]-M-\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}\mid Z_{1}<0]}{\mathbb{E}[Q_{\tau}\mid S_{0}=M,Z_{1}<0,Q_{\tau}\geq M]-\mathbb{E}[Q_{\tau}\mid S_{0}=M,Z_{1}<0,Q_{\tau}\leq 0]},

which can be seen to be
\Theta(M^{-1})
by inspection. Therefore,
p_{M}=1-\Theta(M^{-1})
. Symmetric arguments establish that
p_{0}=1-\Theta(M^{-1})
. Plugging these bounds into (
7
) in
Theorem

4.2
, it remains to argue that
E(M)
and
E(0)
scale as
\Theta(M)
.

These bounds similarly follow from applications of the optional stopping theorem on appropriately defined martingales. For the upper bound on
E(M)
, we consider the martingale
Q_{t}^{2}-\sigma^{2}t
, where
\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}^{2}]
. Then, by the optional stopping theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Q_{\tau}^{2}\mid S_{0}=M]-\sigma^{2}E(M)=M^{2}.

(9)

Solving for
E(M)
, and using the fact that
Q_{\tau}^{2}\leq(M+Z_{\max})^{2}
almost surely, we obtain
E(M)=O(M)
.

Unfortunately, (
9

. We overcome this difficulty by conditioning on the first step of the process, as we had done before. In particular, suppose
Z_{1}\geq 0
. Then, by definition,
\tau=1
. If
Z_{1}<0
, on the other hand,
\tau=1+\tau(M+Z_{1})
, where, recall,
\tau(M+Z_{1})
denotes the first time the process hits

or

, starting from state

. Therefore, it suffices to lower bound
\mathbb{E}[\tau(M+Z_{1})\mathds{1}\left\{Z_{1}<0\right\}]

yields the following key inequality, for any
S\in[0,M]

\mathbb{E}[\tau(S)\mid S]\geq\frac{S(M-S)}{\sigma^{2}}.

Plugging
S=M+Z_{1}{<M}
into the above, and integrating over all

\mathbb{E}[\tau(M+Z_{1})\mathds{1}\left\{Z_{1}<0\right\}]=\Omega(M)
, thereby establishing that

. The lower bound on

follows similar lines; we omit it as such.

Remark 4.7

An immediate corollary of the Central Limit Theorem is that

Theorem 4.8

Any static policy that allocates
\alpha\neq\alpha^{*}
in each round incurs

\overline{V}=\Theta(1)
when

\alpha>\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}
, and
\overline{W}=\Theta(1)
when
\alpha<\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}
. Therefore, for any

,

.

We provide some intuition behind the result, deferring a formal proof to Appendix

. Fix
\alpha\neq\alpha^{*}
. If
\alpha>\alpha^{*}
,

has negative drift; as a result, one would expect it to spend a constant fraction of time in state 0 in steady state. For
\alpha<\alpha^{*}
, on the other hand,
S_{t}
has positive drift, resulting in a constant fraction of time spent in state

. In the former case, the long-run average stockout rate will be large; in the latter, the long-run average overflow dominates.

4.3
Envy-Inefficiency Trade-off Under a Bang-Bang Policy

In this section we demonstrate that simple adaptive policies — namely, the popular class of Bang-Bang policies from the stochastic control literature
(
Scokaert and Rawlings 2002
,
Cerf and Mariconda 1995
)
— can yield an exponential improvement in efficiency, at the price of limited envy. At a high level, our algorithm proceeds as follows: when the store is more than half empty, our algorithm allocates slightly less than
\mu_{\mathcal{B}}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}
in order to avoid the risk of a stockout; when it is more than half full, it allocates slightly more than
\mu_{\mathcal{B}}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}
in order to avoid the risk of overflow.

Formally, our algorithm takes as input a target envy level
\Delta>0
. Then, for each round
t\in\mathbb{N}
it sets:

A_{t}=\begin{cases}\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}-\frac{\Delta}{2}&\text{if }S_{t-1}<\frac{M}{2}\\
\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}+\frac{\Delta}{2}&\text{if }S_{t-1}\geq\frac{M}{2}.\end{cases}

(10)

Note that under this algorithm, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}\mid S_{t-1}\geq M/2]

\displaystyle=\mu_{\mathcal{B}}-\mu_{\mathcal{N}}\!\left(\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}+\frac{\Delta}{2}\right)=-\,\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}\Delta}{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}\mid S_{t-1}<M/2]

\displaystyle=\mu_{\mathcal{B}}-\mu_{\mathcal{N}}\!\left(\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}-\frac{\Delta}{2}\right)=\;\;\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}\Delta}{2}.

Thus, the expected inventory drift is negative when the inventory is above
M/2
, and positive when it is below
M/2
. In other words, Bang-Bang (
\Delta
) uses a slack of
\Delta
in fairness to induce an
M/2
-reverting drift of magnitude
\mu_{\mathcal{N}}\Delta/2
. This steers the process away from the boundary states
\{0,M\}
, reducing the stationary boundary mass (and hence overflow/stockout) relative to static policies for which
\Delta=0
.

Theorem

4.9
below formalizes this intuition, establishing that such a simple
M/2
-reverting policy yields exponential gains in efficiency.

Theorem 4.9

Fix
\Delta\in(0,2\mu_{\mathcal{B}}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}})
, and suppose there exist strictly positive
\epsilon
and
\delta
such that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(B_{t}-N_{t}(\mu_{\mathcal{B}}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}+\Delta/2)\geq\epsilon)\geq\delta\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{P}(B_{t}-N_{t}(\mu_{\mathcal{B}}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}-\Delta/2)\leq-\epsilon)\geq\delta.

(11)

Then, the Bang-Bang (
\Delta
) policy achieves

\overline{W}=e^{-\Omega(\Delta M)}\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\overline{V}=e^{-\Omega(\Delta M)}.

Therefore,
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}=e^{-\Omega(\Delta M)}
.

Before proving our main result, we comment on the conditions on
\Delta
required for
Theorem

4.9
to hold. The upper bound
\Delta<2\mu_{\mathcal{B}}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}
simply enforces that
A_{t}>0
for all
t
.
Equation

11
on the other hand guarantees that the inventory drift
Z_{t}
is lower (resp., upper) bounded by a positive (resp., negative) constant with constant probability. These are mild regularity conditions that ensure that the hitting times are well-defined, thereby allowing us to apply
Theorem

4.2
. They effectively impose an upper bound on
\Delta
with respect to the distributional primitives
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{N}
. We provide a sufficient condition on
\Delta
for these to hold in Appendix
D.1
, noting that the range of
\Delta
for which this condition holds increases with the variance of
\mathcal{B}
.

Theorem

4.9
implies the optimality of the popular class of Bang-Bang policies which, in contrast to the optimal policies derived in existing work on fair allocation
(
Sinclair et al. 2023
,
Banerjee et al. 2023
)
, are not forward-looking. While the optimality of these policies is well-established for
linear
stochastic control problems
(
Scokaert and Rawlings 2002
,
Cerf and Mariconda 1995
)
, our inefficiency metric and dynamics are non-linear in the allocations; it is therefore not a priori obvious that this simple policy class is scaling-optimal in our setting. We prove the theorem below.

Proof 4.10

(Proof)

For notational convenience, we analyze Bang-Bang (
2\Delta
) instead of Bang-Bang (
\Delta
) throughout.

Note that our algorithm only ever allocates
A_{t}\in\{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}-\Delta,\mu_{\mathcal{B}}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}+\Delta\}
. By our assumptions on
\Delta
, there exist
\epsilon,\delta>0
such that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(B_{t}-N_{t}\left(\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}+\Delta\right)\geq\epsilon\right)\geq\delta\implies\mathbb{P}\left(B_{t}-N_{t}\left(\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}-\Delta\right)\geq\epsilon\right)\geq\delta,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(B_{t}-N_{t}\left(\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}-\Delta\right)\leq-\epsilon\right)\geq\delta\implies\mathbb{P}\left(B_{t}-N_{t}\left(\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B}}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}+\Delta\right)\leq-\epsilon\right)\geq\delta.

Hence, the conditions of
Lemma

B.3
(see Appendix
B.1
) hold, and
H_{M}
and
H_{0}
exist. By
Proposition

4.1
, it suffices to upper bound these two terms.

Recall, by

,
H_{M}\leq\frac{1}{E(M)}
and
H_{0}\leq\frac{1}{E(0)}
. We show that

E(M)=e^{\Omega(\Delta M)}
, and therefore
H_{M}=e^{-\Omega(\Delta M)}
. The proof that
E(0)=e^{\Omega(\Delta M)}
(and therefore
H_{0}=e^{-{\Omega}(\Delta M)}

In order to bound
E(M)
, we define the following sequence of stopping times (see

\tau_{0}=0
. For all

5
Impossibility Result

We now provide a lower bound on the inefficiency of
any
policy that achieves an ex-post envy of
\Delta
. Our results will then imply that the Bang-Bang policy analyzed in
Section

4.3
lies on the envy-inefficiency Pareto frontier in a small-
\Delta
regime, and is therefore order-wise optimal.

Theorem 5.1

Suppose
B_{t}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1/2)
and
N_{t}=1
almost surely, for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Fix
0<\Delta\leq 1/9
. Then, for any policy such that
\max\limits_{t,t^{\prime}}|A_{t}-A_{t^{\prime}}|=\Delta
,
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}=e^{-O(\Delta M)}
.

We highlight two key features of
Theorem

5.1
. First, the instance we construct for our lower bound highlights that the Pareto frontier is driven by
supply
, rather than demand, uncertainty. Second, the proof of our results considers the wider class of
anticipatory
policies wherein the decision-maker has full knowledge of future randomness. This shows that the impossibility is due to inevitable stochastic fluctuations in the environment, rather than an information-theoretic loss due to the fact that the policy is non-anticipatory.

Proof 5.2

Fix
a>0
, and consider any allocation policy such that
A_{t}\in[a,a+\Delta]
. We partition the time horizon into epochs of length
L\in\mathbb{N}
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
B^{(k)}
be the total budget arrival in epoch
k
; note that
B^{(k)}\sim\text{Binomial}(L,1/2)
. Moreover, let
Y_{+}^{(k)}\coloneqq B^{(k)}-(a+\Delta)L
and
Y_{-}^{(k)}\coloneqq B^{(k)}-aL
.

Notice that the policy necessarily incurs an overflow of at least 1 whenever
Y_{+}^{(k)}\geq M+1
. This follows from the fact that the unreflected inventory level
Q_{t}\geq S_{0}+Y_{+}^{(k)}
at the end of epoch
k
(since
a+\Delta
is the most it can allocate in each period).
Therefore, on this event
Q_{t}\geq M+1
. We use this to lower bound
\overline{W}
as follows:

\displaystyle\overline{W}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{t}\geq\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor T/L\rfloor}\frac{\mathds{1}\left\{Y_{+}^{(k)}\geq M+1\right\}}{\lfloor T/L\rfloor}=\mathbb{P}(Y_{+}^{(k)}\geq M+1),

where the final equality follows from the Strong Law of Large Numbers.

In a similar vein, the policy incurs an underage of at least one whenever
Y_{-}^{(k)}\leq-1
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\overline{V}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V_{t}\geq\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor T/L\rfloor}\frac{\mathds{1}\left\{Y_{-}^{(k)}\leq-1\right\}}{\lfloor T/L\rfloor}=\mathbb{P}(Y_{-}^{(k)}\leq-1).

Hence, it suffices to lower bound
\mathbb{P}(Y_{+}^{(k)}\geq M+1)
or
\mathbb{P}(Y_{-}^{(k)}\leq-1)
to obtain a lower bound on
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}
. We partition our analysis into three cases.

Case 1:

M+1+(a+\Delta)L\leq L/2
. Note that we can always choose
L
to satisfy this inequality as long as
a<\frac{1}{2}-\Delta
. In this case:

\mathbb{P}(Y_{+}^{(k)}\geq M{+}1)=\mathbb{P}(B^{(k)}\geq M+1+(a+\Delta)L)\geq\mathbb{P}(B^{(k)}\geq L/2)\geq\frac{1}{2},

since
B^{(k)}\sim\text{Binomial}(L,\tfrac{1}{2})
is symmetric around its mean
L/2
. Therefore, in this case we have
\overline{W}\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

Case 2:

-1+aL\geq L/2
. Note that we can always choose
L
to satisfy this inequality as long as
a>\frac{1}{2}
. In this case, we have:

\mathbb{P}(Y_{-}^{(k)}\leq-1)=\mathbb{P}(B^{(k)}\leq aL-1)\geq\mathbb{P}(B^{(k)}\leq L/2)\geq\frac{1}{2},

again, by symmetry of
B^{(k)}
. Therefore,
\overline{V}\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

Cases 1 and 2 imply that, if
a>\frac{1}{2}
or
a<\frac{1}{2}-\Delta
, we can always choose
L
such that the policy incurs constant inefficiency. It remains to prove the claim for
a\in\left[\frac{1}{2}-\Delta,\frac{1}{2}+\Delta\right]
.

Case 3:

a\in\left[\frac{1}{2}-\Delta,\frac{1}{2}+\Delta\right]
. To obtain positive lower bounds on
\mathbb{P}(Y_{-}^{(k)}\leq-1)
and
\mathbb{P}(Y_{+}^{(k)}\geq M+1)
, we require
aL-1\geq 0
and
M+1+(a+\Delta)L\leq L
, which hold for
L\geq\max\left\{\frac{1}{a},\frac{M+1}{1-a-\Delta}\right\}
, which is satisfied for any
L\geq\frac{M+1}{\frac{1}{2}-2\Delta}
.

We bound
\mathbb{P}(Y_{-}^{(k)}\leq-1)
. We have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{-}^{(k)}\leq-1)=\mathbb{P}(B^{(k)}\leq aL-1)=\mathbb{P}(L-B^{(k)}\geq L(1-a)+1)=\mathbb{P}(L-B^{(k)}\geq L/2+L(1/2-a)+1)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(L-B^{(k)}\geq L/2+L\Delta+1),

where the inequality follows from
a\geq\frac{1}{2}-\Delta
. By symmetry,
L-B^{(k)}\sim\text{Binomial}(L,1/2)
.

We use the following bound on the Binomial tail.

Proposition 5.3

(Adapted from Proposition 7.3.2 in
Alon and Spencer 2016
)

Suppose
L
is even. Then, for any integer
t\in(0,L/8]
:

\mathbb{P}\left(B^{(k)}\,\geq\,\tfrac{L}{2}+t\right)\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{15}\,e^{-16t^{2}/L}.

Letting
t=L\Delta+1
, it suffices to show that
\frac{(L\Delta+1)^{2}}{L}=O(M\Delta)
, which holds for
L=cM/\Delta
, for some
c>0
. (Without loss of generality, we assume
L
is even.)

We conclude by showing that there exists
c>0
such that (i)
L\Delta+1\leq L/8
, and (ii)
L\geq\frac{M+1}{\frac{1}{2}-2\Delta}
. For the first property, we have:

\displaystyle L\left(\frac{1}{8}-\Delta\right)\geq 1\iff c\geq\frac{\Delta}{M\left(\frac{1}{8}-\Delta\right)}.

The above holds for any
c\geq 8
, as long as
\Delta\leq\frac{1}{9}
. For the second property, we require
c
such that:

\displaystyle c\geq\frac{(M+1)\Delta}{M\left(\frac{1}{2}-2\Delta\right)},

which holds for any
c\geq 0.8
, for
\Delta\leq\frac{1}{9}
.

6
Extension to Multiple Resources, Multiple Agent Types

Up to this point, our analysis has considered a single-resource, homogeneous-agent setting. In this section, we outline how our results extend naturally to more complex models involving multiple resources and agent types. While the proofs require technical modifications of the arguments already presented, the core insights remain unchanged. Since the single-resource setting is a special case of this more general model, the lower bound from
Theorem

5.1
still applies.

Generalized Model.

The decision-maker manages a warehouse with total capacity
M>0
, and
K
different types of resources. Initially there are
S_{0,k}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
units of each resource
k\in[K]
(with
\left\lVert S_{0}\right\rVert_{1}\leq M
). At the beginning of each period, the decision-maker receives an additional budget of
B_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{\geq 0}
resources, drawn i.i.d. from a distribution
\mathcal{B}
supported on a subset of
[0,B_{\max}]^{K}
, with mean
\mu_{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{\geq 0}
and strictly positive variance for each resource. She then observes a random number
N_{t,\theta}\in\mathbb{N}
new agents of type
\theta
. Agents of type
\theta
have utility

u(x,\theta)=w_{\theta}^{\top}x
for an allocation
x\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{\geq 0}
, where
w_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{>0}
corresponds to the preference weights that type
\theta
assigns to each resource.
We assume
N_{t,\theta}
is drawn i.i.d. from a distribution
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
supported on a subset of
[0,N_{\max}]
. We use
\Theta
to denote the set of all possible types, and let
\mu_{\mathcal{N},\theta}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
denote the respective means of
(\mathcal{N}_{\theta},\theta\in\Theta)
, with
\mu_{\mathcal{N}}=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_{\mathcal{N},\theta}
. The decision-maker must then decide on an allocation
A_{t,\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}_{\geq 0}
of the resources to allocate to each of the
N_{t,\theta}
agents. If the on-hand inventory exceeds
M
at the end of the period, the decision-maker decides which of the resources to throw out in order to satisfy the capacity constraint. Let
W_{t,k}
and
V_{t,k}
respectively denote the overflow and stockout quantities of resource
k
at the end of period
t
. As before, these quantities respectively represent the amount of resource
k
wasted or purchased via an outside option at the end of the period. Formally:

W_{t,k}=(S_{t-1,k}+B_{t,k}-\sum_{\theta}N_{t,\theta}A_{t,\theta,k}-S_{t,k})^{+}\quad,\quad V_{t,k}=(S_{t,k}-(S_{t-1,k}+B_{t,k}-\sum_{\theta}N_{t,\theta}A_{t,\theta,k}))^{+}.

We have the following metrics of inefficiency and envy in the multi-resource setting.

Definition 6.1

(Inefficiency)

Given per-unit overflow cost
h\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and per-unit stockout cost
b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, the
inefficiency
of a sequence of allocation decisions
(A_{t,\theta},t\in\mathbb{N},\theta\in\Theta)
is given by:
3
3

3

It is a straightforward extension to have different overflow and stockout costs
h_{k}
and
b_{k}
per resource type.

\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}=\lim\sup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{K}hW_{t,k}+bV_{t,k}\right)=h\overline{W}+b\overline{V},

where

\overline{W}=\lim\sup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{K}W_{t,k}\quad,\quad\overline{V}=\lim\sup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{K}V_{t,k}.

Definition 6.2

(Ex-post envy)

The
ex-post envy
of allocation decisions
(A_{t,\theta},t\in\mathbb{N},\theta\in\Theta)
is given by:

\Delta_{\text{Fair}}\;=\;\sup_{t,\theta,t^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}:N_{t,\theta}>0,N_{t^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}}>0}\,|u(A_{t^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}},\theta)-u(A_{t,\theta},\theta)|.

The Bang-Bang policy.

We now describe the modification to the Bang-Bang policy for the multi-resource, multi-type setting. At a high level, we split the capacity
M
into
K
“virtual stores”, each with capacity
M/K
. We then run Bang-Bang independently on every resource
k\in[K]
using the same fairness tolerance
\Delta
. Formally, for fixed
\Delta>0
, the Bang-Bang (
\Delta
) policy allocates:

A_{t,\theta,k}=\begin{cases}\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B},k}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}-\frac{\Delta}{2}&S_{t-1,k}<\frac{M}{2K}\\
\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B},k}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}+\frac{\Delta}{2}&S_{t-1,k}\geq\frac{M}{2K}.\end{cases}

(16)

At the end of the period, the policy chooses any
S_{t,k}
that minimizes the instantaneous overflow and stockout costs, such that
\left\lVert S_{t}\right\rVert_{1}\leq M
. Note that for
K=1
, we recover our original Bang-Bang (
\Delta
) policy. Moreover, it is easy to see that this policy yields an envy bound of
O(\Delta)
since:

\Delta_{\text{Fair}}=\max_{t,\theta,t’,\theta^{\prime}:N_{t,\theta}>0,N_{t^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}}>0}|u(A_{t^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}},\theta)-u(A_{t,\theta},\theta)|\leq\max_{\theta}\sum_{k}w_{\theta,k}\Delta=O(\Delta).

The following theorem establishes that the fundamental envy-inefficiency trade-off from the single-resource setting is unchanged.

Theorem 6.3

Fix
\Delta\in(0,2\min_{k}\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B},k}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}})
, and suppose there exist strictly positive
\epsilon
and
\delta
such that, for all
k\in[K]
:

\mathbb{P}\left(B_{t,k}-\left(\sum_{\theta}N_{t,\theta}\right)(\mu_{\mathcal{B},k}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}+\Delta/2)\geq\epsilon\right)\geq\delta\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{P}\left(B_{t,k}-\left(\sum_{\theta}N_{t,\theta}\right)(\mu_{\mathcal{B},k}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}-\Delta/2)\leq-\epsilon\right)\geq\delta.

Then the Bang-Bang (
\Delta
) policy achieves

\overline{W}=e^{-\Omega(\Delta M)}\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\overline{V}=e^{-\Omega(\Delta M)}.

As a result,
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}=e^{-\Omega(\Delta M)}
. Moreover, the static policy that allocates
\frac{\mu_{\mathcal{B},k}}{\mu_{\mathcal{N}}}
of resource
k
to each type
\theta
individual in each period achieves

\overline{W}=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{M}\right)\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\overline{V}=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{M}\right).

As a result,
\Delta_{\text{Efficiency}}=\Theta(1/M)
.

We prove the result in Appendix
E.1
. In Appendix
E.2
we discuss how to incorporate type-dependent preferences in the allocation decisions.

Conclusion

In this work, we studied fundamental trade-offs between fairness and efficiency in repeated resource allocation problems under stochastic replenishments with capacity constraints. Our results demonstrate that while strict fairness (
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}=0
) leads to significant inefficiency, even a small relaxation of fairness (
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}>0
) can dramatically improve performance, with inefficiency decreasing exponentially as both ex-post envy and capacity increase. We provided both theoretical guarantees and empirical validation, showing that our proposed allocation policy, Bang-Bang, effectively balances fairness and efficiency.

Our work suggests several open avenues, including extending the insights to non-i.i.d. donation and demand models, and exploring fairness notions beyond envy-freeness. From a technical perspective, while our results establish exponentially decaying inefficiency of our Bang-Bang policy, they do not guarantee the best constant in the exponent. Establishing this (at least in the large-deviations sense), or finding better policies under other natural scalings, are important and promising open questions.

E.2
Extending Bang-Bang to Type-Dependent Allocations

We close out with showing how to incorporate type-dependent preferences in the baseline allocations.

We define the
fluid Eisenberg-Gale
(EG) allocation
\{a_{\theta}^{\textsf{EG}}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
as the solution to

\displaystyle\max_{\{a_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_{\mathcal{N},\theta}\,\log\bigl(w_{\theta}^{\top}a_{\theta}\bigr)

(31)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_{\mathcal{N},\theta}\,a_{\theta}\;\leq\;\mu_{\mathcal{B}},

\displaystyle a_{\theta}\;\geq\;0,\qquad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

Eq.

31
represents a fluid-limit version of the classical Eisenberg-Gale program, which is known to produce allocations that are Pareto-efficient, envy-free, and proportional in offline resource allocation settings
(
Eisenberg 1961
,
Varian 1974
,
Jain and Vazirani 2010
)
. In our context, we use
Eq.

31
to define
type-specific
allocations
a_{\theta,k}^{\textsf{EG}}
, in contrast to the earlier baseline of
\mu_{\mathcal{B},k}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}
, which does not account for differences in type-specific weights.

Modified Bang-Bang policy over virtual stores.

Partition the warehouse into
K
virtual stores, each with capacity
M/K
. Fix a fairness budget
\Delta>0
.
Let
S_{t-1,k}
denote the inventory in virtual store
k
at the start of period
t
. For every arriving type
\theta
,

A_{t,\theta,k}\;=\;\begin{cases}a_{\theta,k}^{\textsf{EG}}-\frac{\Delta}{2}&\text{if }S_{t-1,k}<\dfrac{M}{2K},\\[5.69054pt]
a_{\theta,k}^{\textsf{EG}}+\frac{\Delta}{2}&\text{if }S_{t-1,k}\geq\dfrac{M}{2K}.\end{cases}

(32)

It is again easy to see that the resulting algorithm satisfies
\Delta_{\text{Fair}}=\Theta(\Delta)
. Additionally, one can also show it achieves
counterfactual envy
(again first considered in
Sinclair et al. 2023
as a “regret” benchmark against the fluid EG solution) as:

\max_{t,\theta}|u(A_{t,\theta},\theta)-u(a_{\theta}^{\textsf{EG}},\theta)|=\Theta(\Delta).

We conjecture that a straightforward (albeit technical) modification to our analysis (by adjusting the ‘center’ points in the technical results around
a_{\theta,k}^{\textsf{EG}}
vs
\mu_{\mathcal{B}}/\mu_{\mathcal{N}}
) shows the envy-inefficiency bounds for this modified algorithm.
