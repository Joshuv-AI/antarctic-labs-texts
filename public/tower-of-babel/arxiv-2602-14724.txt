Cheeger’s isoperimetric problem for Gaussian mixtures
Lukas Liehr

Abstract
. In any dimension n n , we determine the Cheeger constant and the Cheeger sets of the Gaussian mixture μ ⁡ ( x ) = p ​ γ ​ ( x − a ) + ( 1 − p ) ​ γ ​ ( x − b ) , \mu(x)=p\gamma(x-a)+(1-p)\gamma(x-b), where p ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] p\in[0,1] , a , b ∈ ℝ n a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n} , and γ : ℝ n → ( 0 , ∞ ) \gamma:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(0,\infty) denotes a Gaussian. In particular, we characterize the Cheeger sets for μ \mu in terms of specific half-spaces perpendicular to a − b a-b , thereby confirming the conjectured solution to the Cheeger problem for Gaussian mixtures. Finally, we study the regime of parameters p , a , b p,a,b in which μ \mu admits a unique Cheeger set.

1.
Introduction and results

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Given
A\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, we denote by
\mu_{+}(A)
its lower Minkowski content,

\mu_{+}(A)=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mu(A^{\varepsilon}\setminus A)}{\varepsilon},

where
A^{\varepsilon}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\mathrm{dist}(A,x)<\varepsilon\}
denotes the open
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
A
.
When
\mu
has a smooth density
w
with respect to Lebesgue measure, then
\mu_{+}(A)
is a natural notion of perimeter with density, and for sets
A
with sufficiently regular boundary
\partial A
, the quantity
\mu_{+}(A)
coincides with the weighted integral

\int_{\partial A}w\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
where
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
denotes the
n-1
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
[
30
,
2
]
.
We say that
\mu
satisfies Cheeger’s inequality if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every
A\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
one has

\min\{\mu(A),1-\mu(A)\}\leq C\mu_{+}(A).

The optimal constant in the previous inequality is called Cheeger’s constant for
\mu
(originally introduced in
[
16
]
) and is given by the infimum

h_{\mu}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\\
0<\mu(A)<1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu_{+}(A)}{\min\{\mu(A),1-\mu(A)\}}.

If this infimum is attained at some set
S
then we call
S
a Cheeger set. The reciprocal of
h_{\mu}
yields the optimal constant for the
L^{1}
-Poincaré inequality on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
: for every Lipschitz function
f
one has

\min_{c\in{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|f-c|\,d\mu\leq C_{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|\nabla f|\,d\mu,

with optimal constant
C_{1}=\frac{1}{h_{\mu}}
. The optimal constant
C_{2}
for the corresponding
L^{2}
-Poincaré inequality satisfies the inequality
C_{2}\leq 4/h_{\mu}^{2}

[
24
]
.

The problem of determining the Cheeger constant and the Cheeger sets of a measure
\mu
is closely related to the isoperimetric problem. The latter consists in identifying, for each prescribed volume
v\in[0,1]
, those sets
A
with
\mu(A)=v
that minimize
\mu_{+}(A)
. This gives rise to the isoperimetric function of
\mu
, which is defined by

I_{\mu}:[0,1]\to[0,\infty),\quad I_{\mu}(v)=\inf\{\mu_{+}(A):A\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),\,\mu(A)=v\}.

In terms of the isoperimetric function one has the equivalent representation of the Cheeger constant via

h_{\mu}=\inf_{v\in(0,1)}\frac{I_{\mu}(v)}{\min\{v,1-v\}}.

Solutions to the Cheeger and isoperimetric problem are only known for special types of measures, including log-concave measures. In this case, half-spaces typically turn out to be solutions to the isoperimetric problem, see
[
24
,
8
]
and the references therein. A classical example of a log-concave probability measure is the Gaussian measure, which has density with respect to the Lebesgue measure given by

\gamma:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to(0,\infty),\quad\gamma(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}}.

Denoting the Borel measure corresponding to the latter density also by
\gamma
, one has
I_{\gamma}=\varphi\circ\Phi^{-1}
where

\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}\,dt,\quad\varphi=\Phi^{\prime}.

Together with the fact that
\Phi
is log-concave on the whole real line, one obtains that the Cheeger constant for
\gamma
is given by
h_{\gamma}=\sqrt{2/\pi}
and the Cheeger sets for
\gamma
are half-spaces whose boundary intersect the origin
[
8
]
. We also refer to
[
21
,
11
]
for further results related to the isoperimetric problem of Gaussian-type measures, and to
[
6
,
12
,
33
,
15
,
31
,
5
]
for studies on isoperimetric problems for measures with density.

If
\mu
is not log-concave then the problem of determining the Cheeger constant and the Cheeger sets for
\mu
becomes mostly intractable. In the present paper we investigate the Cheeger problem for non-log-concave probability measures that are Gaussian mixtures of the form

\mu(x)=p\gamma(x-a)+(1-p)\gamma(x-b)

(1.1)

where
p\in[0,1]
and
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The study of the Cheeger problem for measures of the form (
1.1
) is motivated by a series of papers in the literature on isoperimetric problems, PDEs, and inverse problems: in
[
34
,
14
]
the authors establish (non-sharp) Poincaré inequalities with respect to measures of the form (
1.1
). In
[
12
, Section 5, Question 6]
it is conjectured that half-spaces are solutions to the isoperimetric problem for Gaussian mixtures of type (
1.1
). In
[
4
, Theorem 3.16]
, the latter conjecture was proved affirmatively in the one-dimensional symmetric case, i.e.,
n=1
and
p=\frac{1}{2}
. Moreover,
[
4
]
provides further evidence of the conjecture in the planar case
n=2
. In
[
22
,
23
]
the Cheeger constant was used to establish stability estimates for certain non-linear inverse problems (related developments and extensions appear in the recent works
[
1
,
20
]
). Here, the value
h_{\mu}
serves as a quantification of the disconnectedness of
\mu
. Based on heuristics and numerical observations it is suggested in
[
22
]
that certain sets whose boundary represent an optimal Cheeger cut positioned in between the two means
a
and
b
(such as a straight cut which corresponds to sets that are half-spaces) are Cheeger sets for the measure in (
1.1
).

1.1.
Main results

In order to state the main results of the paper, we introduce for
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
\beta\geq 0
the function
Q_{\alpha,\beta}:{\mathbb{R}}\to(0,\infty)
via

Q_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=\alpha\Phi(x)+(1-\alpha)\Phi(x-\beta).

Moreover, we define for
c\in\mathbb{R}
and a unit vector
\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
the half-spaces

H_{c,\nu}^{-}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ x\cdot\nu<c\},\quad H_{c,\nu}^{+}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ x\cdot\nu>c\}.

Notice that if
a=b
or
p\in\{0,1\}
in (
1.1
) then
\mu
coincides up to translation with
\gamma
, for which the solution of the Cheeger problem is known. We therefore exclude these values in the following statement. We also remark that whenever we say two Borel sets
A,B
satisfy
A=B
then equality is meant up to
\mu
-null sets, i.e., the symmetric difference of
A
and
B
satisfies
\mu(A\Delta B)=0
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
a\neq b
, let
p\in(0,1)
, and let
\mu
be the measure

\mu(x)=p\,\gamma(x-a)+(1-p)\,\gamma(x-b).

Define
d:=|a-b|
,
m:=\min\{p,1-p\}
, and
r^{*}>0
via the equality
Q_{m,d}(r^{*})=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Then the Cheeger constant of
\mu
is given by the minimum

h_{\mu}=\min_{r\in[0,r^{*}]}\bigl(\log Q_{m,d}\bigr)^{\prime}(r).

If
\mathcal{O}\subset[0,r^{*}]
is the set of minimizers and if
\nu:=\frac{b-a}{|b-a|}
, then the following holds:

(1)

If
p\leq\frac{1}{2}
, then a Borel set
E
is a Cheeger set for
\mu
if and only if
E=H_{a\cdot\nu+t,\nu}^{\pm}
for some
t\in\mathcal{O}
.

(2)

If
p>\frac{1}{2}
, then a Borel set
E
is a Cheeger set for
\mu
if and only if
E=H_{-b\cdot\nu+t,-\nu}^{\pm}
for some
t\in\mathcal{O}
.

The above theorem states that, in any dimension
n
, the Cheeger constant of
\mu
is given by the minimum of the
C^{\infty}
function
(\log Q_{m,d}\bigr)^{\prime}
on the compact interval
[0,r^{*}]
. In particular,
h_{\mu}
is dimension-free and depends only on the distance
d=|a-b|
and the minimum
m=\min\{p,1-p\}
. Moreover, uniqueness of the Cheeger set up to complement corresponds to
\mathcal{O}
being a singleton. In the symmetric case
p=\frac{1}{2}
this uniqueness property is achieved and an explicit formula for the Cheeger constant and the Cheeger sets is proved.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the Cheeger constant of the symmetric Gaussian mixture

\mu(x)=\frac{1}{2}\,\gamma(x-a)+\frac{1}{2}\,\gamma(x-b)

is given by

h_{\mu}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{8}|a-b|^{2}}.

Moreover,
E
is a Cheeger set for
\mu
if and only if
E=H_{a\cdot\nu+t,\nu}^{-}
or
E=H_{a\cdot\nu+t,\nu}^{+}
, where
\nu=\frac{b-a}{|b-a|}
and
t=\frac{1}{2}|b-a|
.

Finally, we investigate uniqueness of the Cheeger set of
\mu
. The next theorem says that the Cheeger sets
H_{a\cdot\nu+t,\nu}^{\pm}
(resp.
H_{-b\cdot\nu+t,-\nu}^{\pm}
) derived in Theorem
1.1
correspond up to complements to at most two distinct half-spaces. Furthermore, for every
p
, if the distance
|a-b|
between the two Gaussian centers is sufficiently large, then
\mu
admits a unique Cheeger set.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
a\neq b
, let
p\in(0,1)
, and let
\mu=\mu_{p,a,b}
be the measure in (
1.1
).
Up to complements and sets of measure zero the following holds:

(1)

The measure
\mu
has at most two Cheeger sets.

(2)

There exists
c(p)>0
such that for every
a,b
with
|a-b|\geq c(p)
, the measure
\mu
has a unique Cheeger set.

Given the previous statement, one may wonder if it can happen that
\mu
has (up to complements) two distinct Cheeger sets. This situation can indeed occur (e.g. take
d=3
and
p\approx 0.075
) and one can show that the set of parameters
(p,d)\in(0,1)\times(0,\infty)
for which
\mu=\mu_{p,a,b}
with
|a-b|=d
admits two distinct Cheeger sets is meager in
(0,1)\times(0,\infty)
. Results in this direction will be the subject of future research.

1.2.
Outline and proof approach

After reducing to the canonical mixture
\mu=p\gamma+(1-p)\gamma(\cdot-\mathbf{d})
with
\mathbf{d}=de_{1}
, the proof combines the following key ingredients: sharp isoperimetric inequalities for
\mu
restricted to specific subclasses
\mathcal{A}
of the Borel sets. We show that, within
\mathcal{A}
, sets with fixed
\mu
-volume that minimize
\mu_{+}
are half-spaces orthogonal to
e_{1}
. This is the content of Section
2
. Next, we establish a local log-concavity argument in Section
3
yielding a monotonicity principle for the corresponding half-space Cheeger ratios with respect to
\mu
. These tools allow a global comparison across a partition of all Borel sets, which will be used to prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
in Section
4
. Finally, Section
5
contains the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

2.
Isoperimetric inequalities

2.1.
Preliminaries

In what follows, we work with the abbreviations
H_{t}^{-}\coloneqq H_{t,e_{1}}^{-}
and
H_{t}^{+}\coloneqq H_{t,e_{1}}^{+}
where
e_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
denotes the first unit vector. With this abbreviation it follows that
\gamma(H_{t}^{-})=\Phi(t)
. Moreover, we have the following identities for the
\mu
-measure and Minkowski content of half-spaces.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mu
be the measure
\mu=p\gamma+(1-p)\gamma(\cdot-\mathbf{d})
where
\mathbf{d}=de_{1}
,
d>0
and
p\in[0,1]
. Then we have the two identities

\begin{split}\mu(H_{r}^{-})&=p\Phi(r)+(1-p)\Phi(r-d),\\
\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{-})&=\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{+})=p\varphi(r)+(1-p)\varphi(r-d).\end{split}

(2.1)

Proof.

The first identity follows directly from integration. To obtain the second identity we use that
H_{r}^{-}
and
H_{r}^{+}
have smooth boundary and therefore

\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{-})=\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{+})=\int_{B}\left(p\gamma+(1-p)\gamma(\cdot-\mathbf{d})\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},

where
B=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:x_{1}=r\}
. The second identity then follows again from a direct integration.
∎

Recall that the isoperimetric function for the Gaussian measure
\gamma
is given by
I_{\gamma}=\varphi\circ\Phi^{-1}
. In particular, for every
E\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
one has

\gamma_{+}(E)\geq I_{\gamma}(\gamma(E)),

(2.2)

which is known as the Gaussian isoperimetric inequality
[
26
]
. This inequality was established by Borell
[
9
]
and independently by Sudakov and Tsirel’son
[
35
]
, and admits several later proofs and strengthenings, including Bobkov’s functional inequality
[
7
]
and semigroup approaches (see
[
28
,
32
,
7
,
13
,
3
]
and the references therein). Moreover, it was shown in
[
13
]
that equality in (
2.2
) is achieved only by half-spaces
H_{t,\nu}^{\pm}
satisfying
\gamma(E)=\gamma(H_{t,\nu})
, and stability issues are discussed in
[
17
]
. Notice that the Gaussian isoperimetric inequality implies that if
E
satisfies
\mu(E)\leq\frac{1}{2}
then the map sending
E
to a half-space
H_{t,\nu}
satisfying
\gamma(E)=\gamma(H_{t,\nu})
is perimeter-decreasing and gives rise to the notion of Ehrhard symmetrization
[
10
,
19
]
.
In the remainder of this section, we make use of the following elementary property of the Gaussian isoperimetric function
[
8
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The Gaussian isoperimetric function
I_{\gamma}:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
is symmetric around
\frac{1}{2}
and strictly increasing on
[0,\frac{1}{2}]
.

An important ingredient in the preceding proofs is the following theorem, which is known as the shift-inequality for Gaussian measure
[
29
]
(see also
[
25
,
27
,
18
]
for further studies on the Gaussian measure of shifts and dilates).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
E\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and let
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\gamma(E)=\Phi(t)
. Then for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and every
\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
one has

\Phi(t-|\lambda|)\leq\gamma(E+\lambda\nu)\leq\Phi(t+|\lambda|),

with equality if and only if
E
coincides up to null sets with a half-space perpendicular to
\nu
(one for the lower bound and one for the upper bound).

A consequence of the latter Theorem is the following observation.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
E\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
such that
\gamma(E)=\Phi(t)=\gamma(H_{t}^{-})
. Further, let
\mathbf{d}=(d,0)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1}
with
d>0
. Then it holds that

\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})\geq\Phi(t-d)

with equality if and only if
E=H_{t}^{-}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda=-d
and let
\nu=e_{1}
. Then
\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})=\gamma(E+\lambda\nu)
. Since
\gamma(E)=\Phi(t)
, an application of Theorem
2.3
, yields the bound

\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})\geq\Phi(t-d).

Assume that equality holds in the latter inequality. Then equality holds in the lower bound of Theorem
2.3
, so
E
is up to null sets a half-space perpendicular to the vector
\nu
. Hence,
E=H_{a}^{-}
or
E=H_{b}^{+}
for some
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

If
E=H_{a}^{-}
then
\gamma(E)=\Phi(a)=\Phi(t)
so
a=t
and
E=H_{t}^{-}
.

If
E=H_{b}^{+}
then
\gamma(E)=1-\Phi(b)=\Phi(t)
, hence
b=-t
. But then

\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})=\gamma(H_{-t-d}^{+})=\Phi(t+d)>\Phi(t-d),

where we used that
d>0
. This contradicts equality. It therefore follows that
E=H_{a}^{-}
up to null sets.
∎

2.2.
Isoperimetric inequalities for
\mu

Given
\mathbf{d}=de_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
d>0
, we define two classes of sets
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\subset\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
via

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}&\coloneqq\Bigl\{E\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\ \gamma(E)\geq\tfrac{1}{2},\ \gamma(E-\mathbf{d})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigr\},\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}&\coloneqq\Bigl\{E\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\ \gamma(E)<\tfrac{1}{2},\ \gamma(E-\mathbf{d})>\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigr\}.\end{split}

(2.3)

The next statement can be regarded as a solution to the isoperimetric problem of the measure
\mu
when restricted to sets in
\mathcal{A}
resp.
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\mu
be the measure
\mu=p\gamma+(1-p)\gamma(\cdot-\mathbf{d})
where
\mathbf{d}=de_{1}
,
d>0
, and
p\in(0,1)
.

(1)

Let
E\in\mathcal{A}
and let
r\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
\mu(E)=\mu(H_{r}^{-})
.
Then
r\in[0,d]
and

\mu_{+}(E)\geq\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{-}),

with equality if and only if
E=H_{r}^{-}
.

(2)

Let
E\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
and let
s\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
\mu(E)=\mu(H_{s}^{+})
.
Then
s\in[0,d]
and

\mu_{+}(E)\geq\mu_{+}(H_{s}^{+}),

with equality if and only if
E=H_{s}^{+}
.

Proof.

Claim (i).
If
\mu_{+}(E)=+\infty
, the claim is trivial. Hence, we assume that
\mu_{+}(E)<\infty
.
By Lemma
2.1
it holds that

\mu(E)=\mu(H_{r}^{-})=p\Phi(r)+(1-p)\Phi(r-d).

(2.4)

Since
\gamma(E)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\Phi
is strictly increasing, there exists a unique
t\geq 0
such that

\gamma(E)=\Phi(t).

Applying Theorem
2.3
with
\lambda=-d
, and
\nu=e_{1}
gives

\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})=\gamma(E-de_{1})\geq\Phi(t-d).

By assumption, we have that
\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})\leq\frac{1}{2}
and since
\Phi(0)=\frac{1}{2}
and
\Phi
is increasing we obtain
t-d\leq 0
. Hence,

\gamma(E)=\Phi(t)\leq\Phi(d)

which implies that

\mu(H_{r}^{-})=\mu(E)\leq p\Phi(d)+(1-p)\Phi(0)=\mu(H_{d}^{-}).

The strict monotonicity of
r\mapsto\mu(H_{r}^{-})
implies
r\leq d
.

Using
\mu(E)=p\gamma(E)+(1-p)\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})
, we further obtain the inequality

\mu(E)\geq p\Phi(t)+(1-p)\Phi(t-d).

(2.5)

Now define
F(u):=p\Phi(u)+(1-p)\Phi(u-d)
. Then
F
is strictly increasing on
\mathbb{R}
. Combining (
2.4
), (
2.5
) and the assumption that
\mu(E)=\mu(H_{r}^{-})
yields
F(r)=\mu(E)\geq F(t)
, which implies

r\geq t\geq 0.

(2.6)

Moreover, from
\mu(E)=p\Phi(t)+(1-p)\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})
and (
2.4
) we obtain

\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})=\Phi(r-d)+\frac{p}{1-p}\bigl(\Phi(r)-\Phi(t)\bigr)\geq\Phi(r-d),

(2.7)

where the last inequality follows from
r\geq t
.

Since
E\in\mathcal{A}
, we have
\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
and therefore

\Phi(r-d)\leq\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(2.8)

According to Lemma
2.2
we have that
I_{\gamma}
is strictly increasing on
[0,\frac{1}{2}]
which gives

I_{\gamma}(\gamma(E-\mathbf{d}))\geq I_{\gamma}(\Phi(r-d))=\varphi(r-d).

Combining the latter bound with the Gaussian isoperimetric inequality yields the inequalities

\displaystyle\mu_{+}(E)

\displaystyle\geq p\gamma_{+}(E)+(1-p)\gamma_{+}(E-\mathbf{d})

\displaystyle\geq pI_{\gamma}(\gamma(E))+(1-p)I_{\gamma}(\gamma(E-\mathbf{d}))

\displaystyle=pI_{\gamma}(\Phi(t))+(1-p)I_{\gamma}(\gamma(E-\mathbf{d}))

\displaystyle\geq p\varphi(t)+(1-p)\varphi(r-d).

Finally, (
2.6
) and the fact that
\varphi
is strictly decreasing on
[0,\infty)
imply

\varphi(t)\geq\varphi(r),

so

\mu_{+}(E)\geq p\varphi(r)+(1-p)\varphi(r-d)=\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{-}),

where the last identity is Lemma
2.1
. This proves the inequality.

Now suppose that
E\in\mathcal{A}
satisfies
\mu_{+}(E)=\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{-})
. If
t
is defined as above then the inequalities derived beforehand show that

\mu_{+}(E)\geq p\varphi(t)+(1-p)\varphi(r-d)\geq p\varphi(r)+(1-p)\varphi(r-d)=\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{-}).

Hence, we obtain that
t=r
. Moreover,
r
satisfies
\mu(E)=\mu(H_{r}^{-})
. Consequently,

\begin{split}\mu(E)&=p\Phi(t)+(1-p)\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})=p\Phi(r)+(1-p)\gamma(E-\mathbf{d}),\\
\mu(E)&=\mu(H_{r}^{-})=p\Phi(r)+(1-p)\Phi(r-d).\end{split}

(2.9)

This implies that
\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})=\Phi(r-d)
. Hence,
E=H_{r}^{-}
by Corollary
2.4
.

Claim (ii):
The second claim is analogous to the first claim by considering the measure
\tilde{\mu}=(1-p)\gamma+p\gamma(\cdot-\mathbf{d})
which arises from the measure
\mu
by applying a reflection along the hyperplane
\{x_{1}=\frac{d}{2}\}
.
∎

Define the isoperimetric function of
\mu
restricted to the sets
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
via

I_{\mu,\mathcal{S}}(v)=\inf\{\mu_{+}(A):A\in\mathcal{S},\,\mu(A)=v\}.

For instance, we have seen that
I_{\gamma,\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=\Phi^{\prime}\circ\Phi^{-1}
. Theorem
2.5
shows that if
\mu
is the measure
\mu=p\gamma+(1-p)\gamma(\cdot-\mathbf{d})
where
\mathbf{d}=de_{1}
,
d>0
,
p\in(0,1)
, and if
Q=p\Phi+(1-p)\Phi(\cdot-d)
, then
I_{\mu,\mathcal{A}}
is given by

I_{\mu,\mathcal{A}}:[Q(0),Q(d)]\to(0,\infty),\quad I_{\mu,\mathcal{A}}=Q^{\prime}\circ Q^{-1}.

A similar equality holds for the class
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
. Hence, we can regard Theorem
2.5
as a solution to the isoperimetric problem restricted to
\mathcal{A}
resp.
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
.

3.
Local log-concavity

Recall that the Gaussian measure is log-concave, implying that
\Phi
is log-concave on
{\mathbb{R}}
. This global log-concavity property yields the Cheeger constant of the Gaussian
\gamma
via the formula
h_{\gamma}=\sqrt{2/\pi}
. Clearly, the mixture measure
\mu
given in (
1.1
) is not globally log-concave. However, the next Proposition identifies a compact interval where the distribution function of the measure
\mu
in one dimension is strictly log-concave. It turns out that this local log-concavity property, when combined with the results in Section
2
, is sufficient for deriving the Cheeger constant of
\mu
for all parameters
p\in[0,1]
,
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and in arbitrary dimensions
n
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For every
a\geq 1
and every
d\geq 0
it holds that the function

J(x)\coloneqq a\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}\,dt+\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(t-d)^{2}}\,dt

is strictly log-concave on the interval
[0,\frac{d}{2}]
.

For the proof of Proposition
3.1
we require the following Lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f(t)=e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}
and
F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt
. Then for every
x\in[0,1]
we have the strict inequality

\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}-1}{2}\right)<x+\frac{f(x)}{F(x)}.

Proof.

Define
r(x)=\frac{f(x)}{F(x)}
and
g(x)=\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}-1}{2}\right)-x
. We want to show that the difference function

h(x)\coloneqq r(x)-g(x)

satisfies
h(x)>0
for all
x\in[0,1]
. To do so, we make the following two observations: first, a direct calculation shows that the function
r
satisfies the differential equation

y^{\prime}(x)=-xy(x)-y(x)^{2}.

Second, the function
g
satisfies the identity

g^{\prime}(x)+xg(x)+g(x)^{2}=e^{x^{2}-1}-1

Since
e^{x^{2}-1}-1\leq 0
for every
x\in[0,1]
, we obtain

g^{\prime}(x)\leq-xg(x)-g(x)^{2}.

Hence,
r
satisfies the differential equation
y^{\prime}=-xy-y^{2}
while
g
satisfies the corresponding differential inequality
y^{\prime}\leq-xy-y^{2}
.

Using the latter properties of
r
and
g
, it follows that on the interval
[0,1]
the derivative of
h
satisfies

\begin{split}h^{\prime}&\geq r^{\prime}+xg+g^{2}\\
&=-xr-r^{2}+xg+g^{2}\\
&=-x(r-g)-(r^{2}-g^{2})\\
&=-xh-(r+g)h\end{split}

(3.1)

Thus, for every
x\in[0,1]
we have

h^{\prime}(x)+(x+r(x)+g(x))h(x)\geq 0.

(3.2)

Define

k(x)=\int_{0}^{x}(t+r(t)+g(t))\,dt,\quad I(x)=e^{k(x)}.

Then

(Ih)^{\prime}=I(h^{\prime}+k^{\prime}h),

which shows in combination with inequality (
3.2
) that
Ih
is non-decreasing on
[0,1]
. Observe further that

h(0)=r(0)-g(0)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}-e^{-\frac{1}{2}}>0.

Since
I
is strictly positive, this implies
I(0)h(0)>0
. The property that
Ih
is non-decreasing implies that
Ih>0
on
[0,1]
. Since
I>0
it follows that
h>0
which yields the claim.
∎

We are prepared to prove Proposition
3.1
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Fix
a\geq 1
and
d\geq 0
. It suffices to show that for every
x\in[0,\frac{d}{2}]
it holds that
(\log J)^{\prime\prime}(x)<0
. To do so, we define

f(t)=e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}},\quad F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt,\quad T(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt.

With the latter definitions it holds that

J(x)=aF(x)+F(x-d)

Moreover, define
N=JJ^{\prime\prime}-(J^{\prime})^{2}
. Then
(\log J)^{\prime\prime}(x)<0
is equivalent to the condition that
N(x)<0
.

It will be convenient to introduce the linear function

z(x)=d-x.

With this function, the condition that
x\in[0,\frac{d}{2}]
can be expressed via the condition that
z(x)\geq x\geq 0
. Moreover, it is easy to see that the function
J
can be expressed via

J(x)=aF(x)+T(z(x)).

The first and second derivatives of the function
x\mapsto T(z(x))
satisfies

\frac{d}{dx}T(z(x))=f(z(x)),\quad\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}T(z(x))=z(x)f(z(x)).

Therefore,

J^{\prime}(x)=af(x)+f(z(x)),\quad J^{\prime\prime}(x)=-axf(x)+z(x)f(z(x)).

We now write
N(x)
as a quadratic in
a
: using the definition of
J
and the expression derived for
J^{\prime}
and
J^{\prime\prime}
we obtain that

N(x)=a^{2}A(x)+aB(x)+C(x)

where the functions
A,B,C
are defined via

\begin{split}A(x)&=-xF(x)f(x)-f(x)^{2},\\
B(x)&=F(x)z(x)f(z(x))-2f(x)f(z(x))-xf(x)T(z(x)),\\
C(x)&=T(z(x))z(x)f(z(x))-f(z(x))^{2}.\end{split}

(3.3)

Consider the following three inequalities between the functions
A,B
and
C
:

(i)

A(x)\leq 0
for every
x\geq 0
,

(ii)

C(x)\leq 0
for every
x
so that
z(x)\geq 0
,

(iii)

B(x)<-A(x)
for every
x
satisfying
0\leq x\leq z(x)
.

If (i), (ii) and (iii) hold and
0\leq x\leq z(x)
, then using
a\geq 1
we obtain

N(x)=a^{2}A(x)+aB(x)+C(x)<a(a-1)A(x)\leq 0,

showing that
J
is strictly log-concave on the interval
[0,\frac{d}{2}]
. Thus, it suffices to show the validity of the inequalities (i), (ii) and (iii).

Proof of (i).
Since
xF(x)f(x)\geq 0
and
f(x)>0
for every
x\geq 0
, the inequality stated in (i) follows immediately.

Proof of (ii).
We have that

C(x)=f(z(x))(T(z(x))z(x)-f(z(x))).

Hence, it suffices to show that

f(z(x))-T(z(x))z(x)\geq 0.

To do so, we observe that

\int_{z}^{\infty}tf(t)\,dt=f(z).

Therefore,

f(z(x))-T(z(x))z(x)=\int_{z(x)}^{\infty}(t-z(x))f(t)\,dt\geq 0.

Proof of (iii).
By definition of the function
B
and the positivity of
T
and
f
, we have that

B(x)\leq F(x)z(x)f(z(x)).

It therefore suffices to prove that

F(x)z(x)f(z(x))\leq xF(x)f(x)+f(x)^{2}.

Dividing both sides by
F(x)f(x)>0
we obtain

\frac{z(x)f(z(x))}{f(x)}\leq x+r(x),

where
r
denotes the ratio

r(x)=\frac{f(x)}{F(x)}.

Consider
h(u)=uf(u)
. Then
h^{\prime}(u)=f(u)(1-u^{2})
which implies that
h
increases on
[0,1]
and decreases on
[1,\infty)
and it attains its maximum at
t=1
. Now fix
x\geq 0
and
z(x)\geq x
. If
x\geq 1
then
z(x)\geq x\geq 1
which yields

z(x)f(z(x))=h(z(x))\leq h(x)=xf(x),

where we used that
h
is decreasing on
[1,\infty)
. This gives

\frac{z(x)f(z(x))}{f(x)}\leq x\leq x+r(x).

It remains to consider the case when
z(x)\geq x\geq 0
and
x\in[0,1]
. Using that
x=1
is the global maximum of
h
on
[0,\infty)
we obtain that

\frac{z(x)f(z(x))}{f(x)}=\frac{h(z(x))}{f(x)}\leq\frac{h(1)}{\varphi(x)}=\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}-1}{2}\right).

Finally, using Lemma
3.2
, we have

\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}-1}{2}\right)\leq x+r(x)

and this completes the proof of inequality (iii).
∎

As an application of Proposition
3.1
, we obtain the following Corollary, which will be frequently applied in the upcoming section.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
p\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and let
\mathbf{d}=(d,0)
with
d>0
. Further, let
\mu
be the measure
\mu=p\gamma+(1-p)\gamma(\cdot-\mathbf{d})
. Then the function

t\mapsto\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{d-t}^{+})}{\mu(H_{d-t}^{+})}

is strictly decreasing on
[0,\frac{d}{2}]
.

Proof.

Define the measure
\tilde{\mu}=(1-p)\gamma+p\gamma(\cdot-\mathbf{d})
. Then we have

\mu(H_{d-t}^{+})=\tilde{\mu}(H_{t}^{-}),\quad\mu_{+}(H_{d-t}^{+})=\tilde{\mu}_{+}(H_{t}^{-}).

Therefore,

\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{d-t}^{+})}{\mu(H_{d-t}^{+})}=\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{+}(H_{t}^{-})}{\tilde{\mu}(H_{t}^{-})}=(\log J)^{\prime}(t)

where
J
is given as in Proposition
3.1
with
a=\frac{1-p}{p}\geq 1
. Since
J
is strictly log-concave on
[0,\frac{d}{2}]
it follows that
(\log J)^{\prime}
is strictly decreasing on
[0,\frac{d}{2}]
.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2

We start with a technical observation that concerns the location of the median
r^{*}
, defined via the equality
Q_{p,d}(r^{*})=\frac{1}{2}
, relative to the parameter
d
. Recall that for
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
\beta\geq 0
, the function
Q_{\alpha,\beta}
is defined by
Q_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=\alpha\Phi(x)+(1-\alpha)\Phi(x-\beta)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
p\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}]
, let
d>0
, and let
r^{*}
be the unique value such that
Q_{p,d}(r^{*})=\frac{1}{2}
. Then
r^{*}\in[\frac{d}{2},d)
.

Proof.

Using that
\Phi(-\frac{d}{2})=1-\Phi(\frac{d}{2})
as well as
0<p\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
\Phi(x)>\frac{1}{2}
for every
x>0
it follows that

Q(\tfrac{d}{2})-\tfrac{1}{2}=(\Phi(\tfrac{d}{2})-\tfrac{1}{2})(2p-1)\leq 0

and hence
Q(\frac{d}{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Moreover, we have

Q(d)=p(\Phi(d)-\tfrac{1}{2})+\tfrac{1}{2}>\tfrac{1}{2}.

Since
Q
is continuous and strictly increasing, we obtain
r^{*}\in[\frac{d}{2},d)
.
∎

We recall that the Cheeger constant for the Gaussian
\gamma
satisfies
h_{\gamma}=\sqrt{2/\pi}
. Therefore for every
E\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
it holds that

\gamma_{+}(E)\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\min\{\gamma(E),1-\gamma(E)\}.

Now introduce for
\mathbf{d}=de_{1}
with
d>0
the classes of sets
\mathcal{X}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
via

\begin{split}\mathcal{X}&=\{E\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}):\gamma(E)<\tfrac{1}{2},\,\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\},\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}&=\{E\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}):\gamma(E)\geq\tfrac{1}{2},\,\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})>\tfrac{1}{2}\}.\end{split}

(4.1)

Together with
\mathcal{A}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
defined in (
2.3
), it follows that the Borel sets
\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
split into a union of the form

\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})=\mathcal{A}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\cup\mathcal{X}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}.

For
E\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with
0<\mu(E)<1
we call the quantity
\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\min\{\mu(E),1-\mu(E)\}}
the Cheeger ratio of
E
with respect to the measure
\mu
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
p\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}]
,
d>0
, and let
r^{*}>0
be the unique value such that
Q(r^{*})=Q_{p,d}(r^{*})=\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Then the Cheeger constant of
\mu=p\gamma+(1-p)\gamma(\cdot-\mathbf{d})
satisfies

h_{\mu}=\min_{r\in[0,r^{*}]}\bigl(\log Q\bigr)^{\prime}(r).

If
\mathcal{O}\subset[0,r^{*}]
denotes the set of minimizers, then
E
is a Cheeger set for
\mu
if and only if
E=H_{t}^{\pm}
for some
t\in\mathcal{O}
.

Proof.

Define the minimum
\sigma>0
by
\sigma=\min_{r\in[0,r^{*}]}\bigl(\log Q\bigr)^{\prime}(r)
. We start by showing that
h_{\mu}\geq\sigma
. To do so, we apply a comparison argument which is split into Case 1 to Case 4.2.

Case 1.
Let
E\in\mathcal{X}
. Since
\gamma(E)<\frac{1}{2}
and
\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})\leq\frac{1}{2}
it follows that
\mu(E)<\frac{1}{2}
. Hence,

\min\{\mu(E),1-\mu(E)\}=\mu(E)=p\gamma(E)+(1-p)\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})

Moreover, the Gaussian isoperimetric inequality implies that

\gamma_{+}(E)\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\gamma(E),\quad\gamma_{+}(E-\mathbf{d})\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\gamma(E-\mathbf{d}).

Hence, we have

\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\min\{\mu(E),1-\mu(E)\}}=\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\mu(E)}\geq\frac{p\gamma_{+}(E)+(1-p)\gamma_{+}(E-\mathbf{d})}{p\gamma(E)+(1-p)\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})}\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}.

Consider the half-space
H_{r^{*}}^{-}\in\mathcal{A}
. Then

\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{r^{*}}^{-})}{\min\{\mu(H_{r^{*}}^{-}),1-\mu(H_{r^{*}}^{-})\}}=2(p\varphi(r^{*})+(1-p)\varphi(r^{*}-d)).

Since
r^{*}>0
by Lemma
4.1
, it follows that

\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{r^{*}}^{-})}{\min\{\mu(H_{r^{*}}^{-}),1-\mu(H_{r^{*}}^{-})\}}<2\varphi(0)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}.

(4.2)

Thus every Cheeger ratio of
E\in\mathcal{X}
is strictly larger than the Cheeger ratio of the set
H_{r^{*}}^{-}
which belongs to
\mathcal{A}
, implying that no set in
\mathcal{X}
is a Cheeger set.

Case 2.
Let
E\in\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
. Similar to Case 1, we first observe that
\mu(E)>\frac{1}{2}
and apply Cheeger’s inequality for the Gaussian measure to obtain

\gamma_{+}(E)\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}(1-\gamma(E)),\quad\gamma_{+}(E-\mathbf{d})\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}(1-\gamma(E-\mathbf{d})).

An analogous argument as in Case 1 yields

\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\min\{\mu(E),1-\mu(E)\}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}.

Together with the strict inequality (
4.2
), it follows that the Cheeger ratio of every
E\in\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
is strictly larger than the Cheeger ratio of
H_{r^{*}}^{-}\in\mathcal{A}
and no set in
\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
is a Cheeger set for
\mu
.

Case 3.1.
Suppose that
E\in\mathcal{A}
satisfies
\mu(E)\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Let
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
be the unique value such that
\mu(E)=\mu(H_{r}^{-})=Q(r)
. From Theorem
2.5
it follows that
r\geq 0
and since
\mu(E)\leq\frac{1}{2}
we have
r\leq r^{*}
. Applying Theorem
2.5
implies

\mu_{+}(E)\geq\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{-})=Q^{\prime}(r).

Therefore, we obtain

\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\min\{\mu(E),1-\mu(E)\}}=\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\mu(E)}\geq\frac{Q^{\prime}(r)}{Q(r)}=(\log Q)^{\prime}(r)\geq\sigma.

Case 3.2.
Suppose that
E\in\mathcal{A}
satisfies
\mu(E)>\frac{1}{2}
. Again, let
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\mu(E)=\mu(H_{r}^{-})=Q(r)
. Since
\mu(E)>\frac{1}{2}
it follows that
r^{*}<r
and from Theorem
2.5
we obtain
r\leq d
, hence
r\in(r^{*},d]
. Moreover, Theorem
2.5
shows that
\mu_{+}(E)\geq\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{-})=Q^{\prime}(r)
. Hence,

\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\min\{\mu(E),1-\mu(E)\}}=\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{1-\mu(E)}=\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\mu(H_{r}^{+})}\geq\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{-})}{\mu(H_{r}^{+})}=\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{+})}{\mu(H_{r}^{+})}.

By Lemma
4.1
we have
(r^{*},d]\subset[\frac{d}{2},d]
. Applying Corollary
3.3
shows

\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{r}^{+})}{\mu(H_{r}^{+})}>\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{r^{*}}^{+})}{\mu(H_{r^{*}}^{+})}=\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{r^{*}}^{-})}{\min\{\mu(H_{r^{*}}^{-}),1-\mu(H_{r^{*}}^{-})\}}=(\log Q)^{\prime}(r^{*}).

Similarly as in Case 1 and Case 2, this shows that the Cheeger ratio of
H_{r^{*}}^{-}
is strictly smaller than the Cheeger ratio of every set
E\in\mathcal{A}
satisfying
\mu(E)>\frac{1}{2}
.

Case 4.1.
Let
E\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
satisfy
\mu(E)<\frac{1}{2}
. Let
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
be the unique value such that
\mu(E)=\mu(H_{s}^{+})
. Then Theorem
2.5
yields

\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\min\{\mu(E),1-\mu(E)\}}=\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\mu(E)}=\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\mu(H_{s}^{+})}\geq\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{s}^{+})}{\mu(H_{s}^{+})}.

Using the assumption
\mu(E)<\frac{1}{2}
together with Theorem
2.5
and Lemma
4.1
it follows that
s\in(r^{*},d]\subseteq[\frac{d}{2},d]
. Therefore, Corollary
3.3
implies

\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{s}^{+})}{\mu(H_{s}^{+})}>\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{r^{*}}^{+})}{\mu(H_{r^{*}}^{+})}=\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{r^{*}}^{-})}{\mu(H_{r^{*}}^{-})}=(\log Q)^{\prime}(r^{*})\geq\sigma.

Case 4.2.
Finally, let
E\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\mu(E)\geq\frac{1}{2}
. If
s
is the unique value such that
\mu(E)=\mu(H_{s}^{+})
then the condition
\mu(E)>\frac{1}{2}
together with Theorem
2.5
implies
s\in[0,r^{*}]
. Thus,

\begin{split}\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\min\{\mu(E),1-\mu(E)\}}&=\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{1-\mu(H_{s}^{+})}=\frac{\mu_{+}(E)}{\mu(H_{s}^{-})}\geq\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{s}^{+})}{\mu(H_{s}^{-})}=\frac{\mu_{+}(H_{s}^{-})}{\mu(H_{s}^{-})}\\
&=(\log Q)^{\prime}(s)\geq\sigma.\end{split}

(4.3)

Combining the above cases, it follows that
h_{\mu}\geq\sigma
. Moreover, if
t\in\mathcal{O}
is a minimizer then the set
H_{t}^{-}
belongs to
\mathcal{A}
and both
H_{t}^{-}
and
H_{t}^{+}
are Cheeger sets for
\mu
. This shows that
h_{\mu}=\sigma
.

It remains to show that if
E
is a Cheeger set for
\mu
then there exists
t\in\mathcal{O}
such that
E=H_{t}^{-}
or
E=H_{t}^{+}
. To show this, observe that if
E
is a Cheeger set then it follows from the above that the relevant cases are Case 3.1 and Case 4.2, i.e.,
E\in\mathcal{A}
with
\mu(E)\leq\frac{1}{2}
or
E\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
with
\mu(E)\geq\frac{1}{2}
. In the first case, it follows from the inequalities discussed in Case 3.1 that
\mu_{+}(E)=\mu_{+}(H_{t}^{-})
for some
t\in\mathcal{O}
. Theorem
2.5
therefore shows that
E=H_{t}^{-}
. Using an analogous argument for the second case implies that if
E\in\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
with
\mu(E)\geq\frac{1}{2}
then
\mu_{+}(E)=\mu_{+}(H_{s}^{+})
for some
s\in\mathcal{O}
and Theorem
2.5
yields
E=H_{s}^{+}
. Consequently, we have

E\in\{H_{t}^{\pm}:t\in\mathcal{O}\}.

On the other hand, if
t\in\mathcal{O}
then by the discussion above,
H_{t}^{-}\in\mathcal{A}
is a Cheeger set and also
H_{t}^{+}
is a Cheeger set. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By applying a reflection along the hyperplane
\{x_{1}=\tfrac{d}{2}\}
, we obtain an analogous statement for Theorem

in the case where

.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

In the case where

, we have that the value

for the measure

. It follows from an equivalent formulation of Corollary

, that

. Hence,

.

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
p\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, let
d>0
, and let
f\coloneqq(\log Q_{p,d})^{\prime}
. Then
f^{\prime}
has at most two zeros on
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

Let
Q=Q_{p,d}
and let
q=1-p
. Consider the function
h
defined by
h=\frac{Q^{\prime\prime}}{Q^{\prime}}
. A direct computation shows that
f^{\prime}=f\big(h-f\big)
. Since
f(x)>0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the zeros of
f^{\prime}
coincide with the zeros of
f-h
. Further, define

F\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}Q\big(f-h\big).

Since
Q(x)>0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the functions
F
and
f-h
have the same zeros, and
f^{\prime}
and
f-h
have the same zeros as well.
Thus it suffices to show that
F
has at most two zeros on
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Using that
Q\cdot f=Q^{\prime}
, we can write

F(x)=Q^{\prime}(x)-Q(x)\,h(x).

Differentiating and using
h=Q^{\prime\prime}/Q^{\prime}
gives

F^{\prime}(x)=-Q(x)\,h^{\prime}(x).

(5.1)

Next, we now introduce the ratio

k(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{\varphi(x-d)}{\varphi(x)}=\exp\Big(dx-\frac{d^{2}}{2}\Big).

It follows from a calculation that the derivative of
h
can be expressed in terms of
p,q,d
and
k(x)
via

h^{\prime}(x)=-1+\frac{pq\,d^{2}\,k(x)}{(p+q\,k(x))^{2}}.

(5.2)

Consider the function
S:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
and its derivative
S^{\prime}
given by

S(u)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{u}{(p+qu)^{2}},\quad S^{\prime}(u)=\frac{p-qu}{(p+qu)^{3}}.

Then
S
is strictly increasing on
(0,p/q)
and strictly decreasing on
(p/q,\infty)
,
with a unique maximum at
u=p/q
, satisfying

\max_{u>0}S(u)=S(p/q)=\frac{1}{4pq}.

Since
k:{\mathbb{R}}\to(0,\infty)
is a strictly increasing bijection, the map

x\mapsto S(k(x))
is strictly increasing up to the unique point
x_{0}

where
k(x_{0})=p/q
and strictly decreasing thereafter. Hence the second term
in (
5.2
) satisfies

\max_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{pq\,d^{2}\,k(x)}{(p+q\,k(x))^{2}}=pq\,d^{2}\max_{u>0}S(u)=\frac{d^{2}}{4}.

Consequently we have the following:

•

If
d<2
, then
h^{\prime}(x)<0
for all
x
.

•

If
d=2
, then
h^{\prime}(x)\leq 0
for all
x
, and
h^{\prime}(x)=0
iff
x=x_{0}
.

•

If
d>2
, then
h^{\prime}
has exactly two distinct zeros
\alpha<\beta
and

h^{\prime}(x)<0\ (x<\alpha),\qquad h^{\prime}(x)>0\ (\alpha<x<\beta),\qquad h^{\prime}(x)<0\ (x>\beta),

(5.3)

where we used that
h^{\prime}(x)\to-1
as
x\to\pm\infty
.

Next we observe that

\lim_{x\to-\infty}F(x)=0

(5.4)

Moreover, from (
5.1
),
F^{\prime}(x)=-Q(x)h^{\prime}(x)
and
Q(x)>0
, so
F^{\prime}
has the opposite sign of
h^{\prime}
. According to the above discussion on the sign-behavior of
h^{\prime}
, there exists
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
F
is strictly increasing on
(-\infty,\alpha)
(for every
d
). From this and the limit in
(
5.4
)
, it follows that
F>0
on
(-\infty,\alpha)
.

Combining the latter with the sign information above we obtain:

•

If
d<2
, then
h^{\prime}<0
everywhere, hence
F^{\prime}>0
everywhere and
F
is strictly increasing. Thus,
F(x)>0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, hence
F
has no zeros.

•

If
d=2
, then
h^{\prime}\leq 0
everywhere, so
F^{\prime}\geq 0
and
F
is nondecreasing. Moreover,

F^{\prime}(x)>0
except at at most one point, hence
F
is strictly increasing. Thus,
F(x)>0
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

•

If
d>2
, let
\alpha<\beta
be as in (
5.3
). Then
F^{\prime}
is positive on

(-\infty,\alpha)
, negative on
(\alpha,\beta)
and positive on
(\beta,\infty)
, so
F
has at most two zeros on
{\mathbb{R}}
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
(1).

Let
f\coloneqq(\log Q)^{\prime}
and let
r^{*}>0
be the unique value such that
Q(r^{*})=\frac{1}{2}
. Suppose
f
had three distinct minimizers in
[0,r^{*}]
,
say
0\leq a<b<c\leq r^{*}
. Then
b\in(0,r^{*})
is an interior minimizer,
hence
f^{\prime}(b)=0
. Also, since
f(a)=f(b)
and
f
is continuous on
[a,b]

and differentiable on
(a,b)
, Rolle’s theorem yields

\xi_{1}\in(a,b)
such that
f^{\prime}(\xi_{1})=0
. Similarly, from
f(b)=f(c)

we get
\xi_{2}\in(b,c)
with
f^{\prime}(\xi_{2})=0
. Thus
f^{\prime}
would have three
distinct zeros
\xi_{1}<b<\xi_{2}
, a contradiction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
(2).

The uniqueness of the Cheeger set for
p=\frac{1}{2}
was discussed in Theorem
1.2
. It suffices to consider the case when
p\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.

Let
q=1-p
,
Q=Q_{p,d}
,
f=f_{p,d}=(\log Q)^{\prime}
and let
r^{*}=r^{*}(p,d)
be defined via the equation
Q(r^{*})=\frac{1}{2}
. Since
p<\frac{1}{2}
, it follows from the identity

Q(\tfrac{d}{2})-\tfrac{1}{2}=(\Phi(\tfrac{d}{2})-\frac{1}{2})(2p-1)<0

(5.5)

that
r^{*}>\tfrac{d}{2}
. We have that
Q^{\prime}_{p,d}(\tfrac{d}{2})=\varphi(\tfrac{d}{2})
and from (
5.5
), we obtain

Q_{p,d}(\tfrac{d}{2})=q+(2p-1)\Phi(\tfrac{d}{2})>q+(2p-1)\cdot 1=q+2p-1=p,

where we used that
2p-1<0
.
Therefore,

f_{p,d}(d/2)=\frac{Q^{\prime}_{p,d}(d/2)}{Q_{p,d}(d/2)}\leq\frac{\varphi(d/2)}{p}=\frac{1}{p\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-d^{2}/8}.

(5.6)

Moreover, since
Q(0)\leq\frac{1}{2}
we have that

f_{p,d}(0)\geq 2p\varphi(0).

Since the right-hand side in (
5.6
) tends to zero as
d\to\infty
and since
d/2\in[0,r^{*}]
it follows that
0
is not a minimizer.

We proceed by showing that neither
r^{*}
is a minimizer for sufficiently large
d
.
To do so, define the right–tail

\varepsilon_{d}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}1-\Phi(r^{*})\in(0,1).

Using
Q_{p,d}(r^{*})=1/2
we obtain

q\Phi(r^{*}-d)=\frac{1}{2}-p\Phi(r^{*})=\frac{1}{2}-p(1-\varepsilon_{d})=\Big(\frac{1}{2}-p\Big)+p\varepsilon_{d}.

Hence,

\Phi(r^{*}-d)=\frac{\tfrac{1}{2}-p}{q}+\frac{p}{q}\varepsilon_{d}=\alpha_{p}+\frac{p}{q}\varepsilon_{d},

(5.7)

where
\alpha_{p}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}-p}{q}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Let
t_{p}=-\Phi^{-1}(\alpha_{p})>0
. Then

\Phi(r^{*}-d)\geq\alpha_{p}=\Phi(-t_{p})

and hence
r^{*}-d\geq-t_{p}
. Since
r^{*}\leq d
by Lemma
4.1
, it follows that
\varphi(r^{*}-d)\geq\varphi(t_{p})
. From that we obtain

f(r^{*})\geq 2q\varphi(t_{p}).

The right hand-side of the previous inequality is independent of
d
, thus
(
5.6
)
implies that
r^{*}
is not a minimizer for sufficiently large
d
.

Since both
0
and
r^{*}
are not minimizer, it follows that every minimizer lies in the open interval
(0,r^{*})
. If there would be two minimizers, then
f^{\prime}
would have two distinct zeros in
(0,r^{*})
and by Rolle’s theorem also a third zero, contradicting Lemma
5.1
.
∎
