In Vivo Ultrasound Study of the Angular Artery Anatomy: Practical Indications for the Treatment of the Deep Pyriform Space
Authors: Antonella Franceschelli, Maria Cazzulani, Simone Ugo Urso, Paola Molinari, Michela Piludu, Martina Zuccaro, Luca Bastiani, Giovanni Mosti
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe increase in adverse events parallels the increase in aesthetic procedures. Injecting fillers at bone level in the pyriform space has been considered safe, as the angular artery (AA) has been described as crossing this space in a plane closer to the skin than to the bone. However, severe adverse events can occur with this injection modality, even in the pyriform space.ObjectivesOur study aimed to assess the course and depth of AA in the pyriform space using Doppler ultrasound (US).MethodsThe pyriform space was investigated by a US device provided with a 20 MHz probe in healthy volunteers. They were studied in a semi-seated position with the probe positioned vertically with its central pointer at the angle between the lateral nose nostril and the cheek skin. The distances between the AA-skin and AA-bone planes were measured in millimeters.ResultsThe AA distance from the bone was <1 mm in 3% of cases and >3 mm in 57% of cases. The AA runs outside the pyriform space in 5% of cases.ConclusionsThe course of AA in pyriform space is highly variable, and sometimes, it can be close to the bone. A US examination of the AA in the pyriform space can identify the AA course, aiding in avoiding complications such as injecting or compressing the AA.Level of Evidence: 4 (Diagnostic)

An increase in adverse events has been reported, along with an increase in the number of aesthetic procedures. The deep pyriform space is a midface cavity cradled by the pyriform aperture and deep medial cheek compartment. The pyriform fossa increases in size with age because of maxillary bony resorption and recession. Fillers in the deep pyriform space give support to the overlying cheek fat and contrast premaxillary depression. The course of the angular artery (AA), often described in the roof of the pyriform space, would allow safe injection on the bone without concern for vascular injury.1

The AA variable course is why the deep injection of fillers on the supraperiosteal plane of the pyriform space is considered safe in clinical practice (ie, without concern for vascular injury). According to previous literature, the deep injection is recommended to avoid/minimize the risk of intra-arterial or near-the-AA injection, potentially creating a vascular complication. Although the knowledge of the face's vascular anatomy is widely recognized as essential to prevent complications, the extensive arterial variations mean that practitioners cannot ensure a safe procedure.

This study aims to investigate the depth of the AA in the deep pyriform space through a Doppler ultrasound (US) study.

METHODS

Case Series

Healthy adult volunteers were enrolled in this study in 5 different centers in Italy.

Inclusion criteria: healthy volunteers, aged 18 to 75, of both sexes who never received any treatment in the pyriform space. Exclusion criteria: patients <18 or >75 years, with systemic skin diseases (such as scleroderma, dermatomyositis, and skin cancer of the face), pregnancy or breastfeeding, and patients who received previous medical aesthetic treatments or had adverse events following treatment in the studied area. The volunteers were recruited by 5 operating doctors with a master’s in aesthetic medicine from 5 different centers in Italy and with great experience in the US examination of the face vessels. All the volunteers accepted to be included in the study and signed the informed consent. The study was performed in adherence with the Declaration of Helsinki for studies on humans. This was an observational, completely noninvasive study; it was performed by 5 doctors under their strict control and did not involve drug administration. For this kind of study, ethical approval is not requested by Italian authorities.

Ultrasound Imaging

A Clarius Scanner HD3 (Clarius Mobile Health, Vancouver, Canada) with a linear 20 MHz US transducer (L20 HD3 Ultra-High-Frequency Linear Scanner; Clarius) was used for all the measurements. The patients were examined in a semi-seated position, with the head slightly tilted to the right to investigate the left pyriform space and slightly tilted to the left to investigate the right pyriform space. The probe was positioned vertically with its central pointer at the angle between the lateral nose nostril and the cheek skin (Figure 1). Both pyriform spaces were studied. The examination was conducted with the “no touch” technique by placing a thick layer of gel between the probe and the skin (US transmission gel G009; FIAB, Florence, Italy); in this way, the probe did not directly touch the skin surface and did not cause any compression of the examined area as well (Video).

All the measurements were taken as millimeters. The value of 0.1 mm was attributed to the parameter “AA-bone plane” when the AA was in the immediate proximity of the periosteal plane.

When the AA was not visible in the pyriform space because it ran lateral to it, a value of 0 was given at both parameters “AA-skin-plane” and “AA-bone plane.”

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS Version 24 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY), with significance set at P < .05. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages, while continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation.

Monovariate analysis was performed to explore the relationship between age, gender, and all parameters studied: the t test for gender and Spearman's rho for age. Moreover, multivariate analysis by linear regression analyses was performed to explore the relationship between age (adjusted for age) and all the studied parameters.

RESULTS

One hundred and sixty-six healthy adult volunteers (age: 18-75 years, mean 49.8 ± 13.1; 149 females, 17 males) were enrolled in this study. As both pyriform spaces were investigated, 332 measurements of the AA location were recorded.

DISCUSSION

With the rising popularity of fillers for facial rejuvenation and the lack of regulations on credentialing qualified injectors, the number of filler-related complications is increasing.

Nonpermanent facial fillers are associated with rare but potentially severe complications. The severity and impact of complications depend on the anatomical region of the face and eventually require a profound knowledge of facial anatomy.2 Although the knowledge of the face vascular anatomy is widely recognized as essential to prevent complications, the extensive arterial variations mean that practitioners cannot ensure a safe procedure. Regarding the pyriform space, our data, in agreement with another recently published paper,3 show that it is possible to find a variable course of the AA that can be close to the cutaneous plane (56% of cases) or even laterally to the pyriform space (5.1% of cases). In all these cases, a safe injection at the bone level is possible without concern for vascular injury. However, the AA may also be very close to the periosteal plane. Actually, in 38.6% of cases, the distance of the AA from the bone is <3 mm, and in 3% of cases, the course of this artery is on the bone or at a 1 mm distance without any significant difference regarding age and gender. Absolutely excluding the possibility that an arterial branch may be confused with the AA is not possible. However, even injecting an arterial branch is not complication risk free. It means that physicians cannot be sure that it is always safe to inject at the supraperiosteal level. In these cases, a bolus injection and every injection at the supraperiosteal plane must be strictly avoided because of the risk of injecting into the AA or next to the AA with the risk of extrinsically compressing this artery (Figure 3). Although complications from filler injections are frequently mild, vascular occlusion is indeed one of the most severe concerns. Prompt recognition and effective management of complications are vital skills for any healthcare provider involved in cosmetic procedures like filler injections, which ensure patient safety and optimal outcomes.4 In the midface, there is no guaranteed safe location for minimally invasive procedures because of high variability in the course of the angular segment of the facial artery.5-7 In many previous studies, the location and the depth of the midfacial segment of the facial artery were studied in cadaveric dissections or by using complicated and very expensive multiplanar computed tomography (CT) or angiography.4-10

The studies on cadaveric dissections provided excellent contributions to our understanding of the arterial blood supply of the midface.7,9,10 However, they are limited by the dissection process itself, which often removes surrounding landmarks, and they do not represent the real-life situation in which doctors are during their patients' injections. Investigation of face arteries by CT or angiography is extremely useful for anatomic studies. However, these studies are invasive, expensive, and not extensively feasible in the clinical routine when patients come for aesthetic treatment.

In our in vivo anatomical study of the AA at the pyriform space, we showed that a study of AA is possible by using US. The US examination is noninvasive, simple, rapid, and easily accessible to all aesthetic doctors with short training and at low cost, especially if compared with other techniques. In addition, it is easily feasible in every doctor's office when the patients come for consultation or immediately before the requested treatment. In particular, the AA course and depth represent vital anatomical information in the pyriform space. Their assessment by the US makes a safe injection possible to improve this space and nasolabial folds as well. Without the US investigation, practitioners could only aspirate with the needle. However, the available studies demonstrate that aspiration cannot be relied on and should not be employed as a safety measure. Adopting injection techniques that avoid injecting an intravascular volume with embolic potential is safer than utilizing an unreliable test to risk a potentially disastrous injection.11 The use of US examination to study the facial anatomy and, in particular, the facial vessels could be questioned. However, the US examination is an excellent diagnostic tool extensively used in all body compartments, including the vessel system. It has been used for many years, as the sole preoperative imaging, even to plan leg artery surgery.12 There is no reason why the US examination cannot be considered accurate and trustworthy. Actually, some papers on the use of US and facial vessels13 and even on the facial artery in the nasolabial fold,3 without correlation with other diagnostic examinations, have already been published.

A weak point of this study is the lack of correlation with cadaver or surgical or angiographic data. Nevertheless, this observational study aimed only to highlight the possibility that AA can have a variable course and run deeply in the pyriform space. This pathway can be easily, quickly, noninvasively and at a low cost, assessed by US examination. This chance represents, in our opinion, a valuable strength of this study, provided the examination technique is correct: proper probe position, a large amount of gel, and no-touch technique. Another significant strength of this study is the large sample size of 166 patients with a total of 332 measurements of the depth of the AA. All 5 doctors (experienced in the US examination) involved in this study carried out the measurements in the same way to ensure consistent data: same US equipment, same probe positioned in the same way, same patient position, and no-touch technique.

Unfortunately, the US study is not widely used. The vast majority of clinicians do not have US equipment or the necessary expertise to obtain valuable information. Nevertheless, the US study of the pyriform area for the AA course and depth detection is very easy. It provides the clinician with extremely important information on how to avoid risky injections. This is why we suggest always introducing the US examination at this level before injecting any filler.

CONCLUSIONS

The course of AA in the pyriform space is highly variable. In 3% of cases, it can be within 1 mm of the bone, raising the risk of intravascular injection or AA compression when injecting in the pyriform space. The US study of this area may easily identify the course of AA in the pyriform space, aiding to avoid potential serious complications.
