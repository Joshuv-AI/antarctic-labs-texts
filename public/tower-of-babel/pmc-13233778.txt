Conserved RNA helicase Vasa regulates ribonucleoprotein condensate dynamics and mRNA localization
Authors: Siran Tian, Hyosik Kim, Harrison A. Curnutte, Tatjana Trcek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGerm granules are germline ribonucleoprotein condensates that concentrate proteins and mRNAs essential for animal development. Although the DEAD-box RNA helicase Vasa is a conserved core germ granule component, its role within these condensates remains poorly understood. Using Drosophila and human cell systems, we showed that condensation of Oskar (Osk), the fly germ granule nucleator, occurred independently of Vasa. However, in late oocytes lacking Vasa, Osk-eGFP formed aggregates and exhibited markedly reduced exchange with the aggregate surroundings. Consistently, Vasa increased Osk-eGFP condensate recovery in cells. Additionally, mRNA localization to germ granules was not persistent in oocytes lacking Vasa, while in cells, co-expression of Vasa and Osk was necessary and sufficient for mRNA localization to condensates. Notably, localization of nanos messenger ribonucleoprotein (mRNP) to condensates reduced Vasa-dependent enhancement of Osk-eGFP exchange. Together, our study reveals the DEAD-box RNA helicase Vasa as a central regulator of condensate dynamics and mRNP localization in vivo and in cellulo.

Introduction

Vasa is a conserved DEAD-box RNA helicase and serves as a canonical germline marker across species.1 It localizes to ribonucleoprotein (RNP) condensates known as germ granules, which have been described across metazoans, including human,2 mouse,3 zebrafish,4 C. elegans,5 and Drosophila.6 Despite its evolutionary conservation and ubiquitous presence in the germline, Vasa’s function within germ granules remains unclear.

To investigate this, we used Drosophila germ granules, also called polar granules, as a model system. These RNP condensates form at the posterior of oocytes and embryos where they enrich specific proteins and mRNAs essential for development.7,8 In Drosophila, Vasa is a core component of germ granules, whose formation relies on Oskar (Osk) protein.9,10 As the principal germ granule organizer, Osk is enriched at the posterior through localization and local translation of osk mRNA.10,11,12,13

osk mRNA encodes two Osk protein isoforms, Short Osk and Long Osk, which are generated by alternative start codon usage.14 Although these isoforms share extensive sequence similarity,15,16 they perform distinct functions at the posterior pole. Specifically, Short Osk is a germ granule nucleator and an mRNA-binding protein that recruits additional granule components.14,17,18,19 In contrast, Long Osk is excluded from germ granules and instead localizes to endocytic membranes, where it helps anchor germ granules and maternally deposited mitochondria at the posterior.19,20,21 However, unlike Short Osk, the long isoform neither binds mRNAs in vivo nor forms condensates in cells.15,16,19

Another key difference between the two isoforms is their ability to interact with Vasa. Previous yeast two-hybrid assays showed that Vasa interacts more efficiently with the Short isoform than the long isoform.17 The Short Osk-Vasa interaction has been reproduced across biochemical assays, cell-based interaction reporters, and fly genetic analyses.17,18,19,22,23 Importantly, this interaction contributes to Vasa posterior localization and occurs independently of Vasa RNA-binding and helicase activity.17,19,24

Although the interaction between Short Osk and Vasa is well established, the function of Vasa within germ granules remains poorly understood, particularly in the context of Short Osk’s role as the germ granule nucleator. Maternal loss of vasa expression prevents germ cell formation and leads to embryonic lethality.6,25,26 However, examining Vasa’s specific function in germ granules is challenging. Vasa performs several essential functions throughout oogenesis, including maintaining germline stem cells,27,28 stabilizing the PIWI-interacting RNA (piRNA) processing complex,29 and regulating the Short Osk protein abundance.14 Therefore, perturbations of vasa function could affect multiple developmental processes, obscuring its role in germ granules, which form at last stages of oogenesis.30

To circumvent this issue, in this study, we used a Drosophila oocyte system in combination with human U2OS cells to examine Vasa’s effect on Osk condensates. In both systems, we showed that Osk condensation occurred independently of Vasa. In late fly oocytes lacking Vasa, Osk-eGFP formed aggregates. Furthermore, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) revealed minimal recovery of Osk-eGFP, indicating that its exchange between condensates and their surroundings was greatly reduced in the absence of Vasa. Similar aggregation occurred in embryos and was independent of the eGFP tag.

Short Osk is the germ granule nucleator, condenses in Drosophila S2 cells, and recruits Vasa in both S2 cells and flies.14,16,17,22 Therefore, we focused on Vasa’s effect on Short Osk condensates in human U2OS cells. Consistently with our in vivo observations, Vasa increased FRAP recovery of Short Osk-eGFP condensates and reduced Short Osk-eGFP partitioning into condensates in U2OS cells.

We further found that Vasa was required for germ granule mRNA localization to condensates in both flies and U2OS cells. Moreover, in U2OS cells, co-expression of Vasa and Short Osk was necessary and sufficient for germ granule mRNA localization to condensates. Notably, localization of a conserved germ granule messenger ribonucleoprotein (mRNP) nanos (nos) reduced FRAP recovery of Short Osk-eGFP condensates in cells even in the presence of Vasa. This indicates that nos mRNP limits Vasa-mediated enhancement of Short Osk-eGFP exchange between condensates and their surroundings. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the DEAD-box RNA helicase Vasa serves as a central regulator of condensate dynamics and mRNA localization in vivo and in cellulo.

Results

Drosophila Vasa is dispensable for Osk condensation in vivo

DEAD-box RNA helicases, such as Vasa,31,32 have emerged as central regulators of RNP condensates, by modulating formation, material properties, and intermolecular interactions among condensate components.33,34,35,36,37 Given the established roles of Short Osk in germ granule formation and the physical interaction between Short Osk and Vasa,14,17,18,19,20,21,22 we speculated that Vasa may regulate germ granule assembly by modulating Short Osk condensation. To test this hypothesis, we crossed flies expressing endogenous osk gene tagged with eGFP (osk-eGFP) by CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing with those lacking Vasa protein expression (vasaD1/vasa1) (Figure S1A).29,37,38 Although Osk-eGFP marks both Osk isoforms, Short Osk is the predominant isoform that localizes to and nucleates germ granules in flies and forms condensates in S2 cells.10,14,16,19,20 In addition, we previously used Osk-eGFP as a marker for germ granules in flies.37 Thus, Osk-eGFP condensates at the posterior primarily reflect condensation of Short Osk. We analyzed oocytes expressing one copy of osk-eGFP and one copy of untagged osk (Figure S1A) following our previous quantitative analysis.37

Using anti-Oskar antibody staining to detect Osk in stage 10 oocytes, as well as in embryos before and after pole-cell formation (nuclear cycle [NC] 1–8 and 14, respectively), we confirmed that Osk-eGFP recapitulated the spatiotemporal localization of untagged endogenous Osk in w1118 flies, a commonly used control line (Figures 1A and S1B).37,39 Immunostaining could not be performed on stage 14 oocytes because the chorion blocks antibody access at this stage. Additionally, we and others have shown that the number and morphology of Osk-eGFP condensates closely resembled those of untagged Osk visualized by electron microscopy.16,20,30,37 Moreover, embryos expressing one copy of osk-eGFP and one untagged osk allele showed hatching rates comparable to those of w1118.37 Together, these results indicate that eGFP tagging does not markedly affect Osk localization, condensation, or germ granule function.

Next, we examined the effect of Vasa expression on Osk condensation in vivo. Fluorescence microscopy showed that in ovaries derived from vasaD1/vasa1 females, Osk-eGFP formed condensates independently of Vasa, from stage 10 oocytes through NC 14 embryos (Figure 1A), consistent with previous observations in Drosophila S2 cells.16,22 Western blot analysis confirmed that Vasa protein expression was not detectable in vasaD1/vasa1 ovaries (Figures S1C and S1D), consistent with previous reports.24,26 Notably, Osk condensates also formed in oocytes and embryos from vasaD1/vasa1 flies carrying only untagged osk alleles (Figure S1E), indicating that condensation of Osk-eGFP was not driven by the eGFP itself. Osk-eGFP forms aggregates and shows minimal exchange in late oocytes lacking Vasa

Next, we focused our analysis on stage 14 oocytes, the final stage of oogenesis. At this stage, cytoplasmic streaming has ceased,40 and the germ plasm is functionally mature, with components such as Osk and Vasa stably associated with granules.37,41,42 This steady-state condition makes stage 14 suitable for quantitative analysis.

Using structured illumination microscopy (SIM), a super-resolution technique (see STAR Methods), coupled with a spot detection algorithm,43,44,45 we found that the number and intensity of Osk-eGFP condensates decreased by 26% and 19%, respectively, in stage 14 oocytes derived from vasaD1/vasa1 compared to wild-type (WT) controls (Figures 1B and 1C). This reduction agrees with the previous finding that loss of Vasa expression correlates with a reduced Short Osk abundance in ovaries (Figures S1C and S1D).14 Given that Osk condensate size approaches the resolution limit of our microscope (see STAR Methods),46,47 the observed 19% decrease in fluorescence intensity may reflect a reduction in condensate volume, protein concentration, or both. As these cannot be distinguished, we collectively refer to this as “protein partitioning into condensates” throughout the study.

Notably, in vasaD1/vasa1 oocytes, Osk-eGFP condensates no longer presented as distinct puncta but instead appeared aggregated, often connecting with each other into clusters of Osk condensates (Figure 1D). Using SIM coupled with the spot detection algorithm, we measured an average distribution of 1.4 ± 0.1 condensates per μm2 in WT stage 14 oocytes, comparable to our previous measurements and to the 1.7 germ granules per μm2 reported by electron microscopy.20,37 In contrast, condensate distribution was significantly reduced in vasaD1/vasa1 oocytes (0.6 ± 0.1 condensates per μm2) (Figure 1E). The aggregated condensate morphology and reduced condensate distribution persisted in NC 1–8 embryos, even when Osk was untagged (Figures S1F–S1I). Together, these results demonstrate that Osk forms aggregates in vivo in the absence of Vasa.

Because changes in condensate morphology could reflect changes in material properties,48 we performed FRAP on stage 14 oocytes to measure Osk-eGFP exchange between condensates and their surroundings (Figure S2A), as described previously.47 We observed that in the presence of Vasa, 21.0% ± 1.8% of Osk-eGFP recovered by FRAP, with a half-time (t½) of 134.2 ± 33.4 s (Figures 1F and 1G; see STAR Methods). In the absence of Vasa, however, fluorescence recovery was nearly abolished, with only 0.9% ± 0.6% of Osk-eGFP exchanging with a t½ exceeding the duration of the experiment (240 s) (Figure 1G). Notably, this loss of recovery was not observed in embryos laid by vasaD1/vasa1 females (Figure S2B; Table S1), suggesting that in later developmental stages, embryo-specific factors regulate Osk-eGFP material properties independently of Vasa. Thus, in stage 14 oocytes lacking Vasa, Osk-eGFP condensates were more aggregated and showed minimal exchange with the surroundings, whereas in the presence of Vasa, they were more punctate and exhibited greater exchange. These results indicate that Vasa may limit the aggregation of Osk condensates in vivo.

Establishing a human cell line system to study the effect of Vasa on Osk condensates

In flies, Vasa performs several essential developmental functions, including maintaining germline stem cells,27,28 stabilizing the piRNA processing complex,29 and regulating the Short Osk protein abundance.14 These functions may confound the interpretation of Vasa’s regulation of Osk condensates, which assemble at later stages of oogenesis.30 To circumvent this issue, we turned to human osteosarcoma (U2OS) cells, which lack the expression of Drosophila genes and DDX4, the human homolog of Vasa.49

We focused on Short Osk because it nucleates germ granules in flies, condenses in Drosophila S2 cells, and recruits Vasa in both flies and S2 cells.14,16,17,22 For expression, we used Short Osk that lacks the first nuclear localization signal (Short Osk ΔNLS1). In flies, this mutation disrupts nuclear localization of Short Osk while minimally affecting germ granule assembly and egg hatching rate,16 indicating that Short Osk ΔNLS1 remains functional. Using transient transfection, we co-expressed 3×HA-Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP and FLAG-Vasa-IRFP in U2OS cells, hereafter referred to as Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP and Vasa-IRFP, respectively (Figures 2Ai and 2Aii; see legends for details). Fluorescent tagging does not alter Vasa localization to nuage and pole plasm during oogenesis or embryogenesis,37,39,50,51 and flies expressing vasa tagged at its endogenous locus are homozygous viable,51 indicating that Vasa’s function was largely uninterrupted by tagging.

We observed that, as in vivo (Figure 1A) and in S2 cells,16 the formation of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates occurred independently of Vasa in U2OS cells (Figure 2B). This condensation was not driven by the eGFP tag, as FLAG-tagged Short Osk ΔNLS1 also formed condensates without Vasa (Figure S3A). Unlike ovaries derived from vasaD1/vasa1 females, which showed reduced Short Osk expression (Figures S1C and S1D), Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP levels were comparable in cells expressing Vasa-IRFP and IRFP alone (negative control) (Figures S3C–S3E). This suggests that the Vasa-dependent increase in Short Osk abundance in ovaries may require ovary-specific cofactors absent in U2OS cells or that ΔNLS1 stabilizes Short Osk. Additionally, Vasa-IRFP colocalized with Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP in cells, whereas IRFP did not (PCC(Costes): 0.79 ± 0.03 and 0.08 ± 0.01, respectively) (Figures 2B and 2C). FLAG-Vasa showed a similar colocalization coefficient with Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP as Vasa tagged with FLAG and IRFP (PCC(Costes): 0.74 ± 0.02). These data suggest that Vasa localizes to Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates in U2OS cells independently of the IRFP tag (Figures S3F and S3G).

Therefore, co-expression of Vasa-IRFP and Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP in U2OS cells reconstituted Osk condensates and recapitulated the spatial colocalization of Vasa and Short Osk observed in vivo and in S2 cells.16,22,39

Vasa promotes Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP exchange between condensates and their surroundings in U2OS cells

Next, we applied FRAP analysis to examine Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates in U2OS cells. We observed that in the presence of Vasa-IRFP, Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP exhibited a mobile fraction of 72.5% ± 9.0% and a t½ of 16.1 ± 2.8 s (Figures 2E and 2F; Table S2). Notably, the increase in condensate mobile fraction was elicited by Vasa rather than the IRFP tag (Figures S3H and S3I; Table S3). In contrast, Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP co-expressed with IRFP exhibited a much lower mobile fraction (8.4% ± 1.8%) and a shorter t½ (3.3 ± 0.9 s) (Figures 2E and 2F; Table S2).

Although Vasa enhanced condensate recovery in both stage 14 oocytes and U2OS cells (Figures 1F and 2E), the magnitude of FRAP recovery and condensate morphology differed between the two systems. Specifically, upon expression of Vasa-IRFP, both mobile fraction and t½ in U2OS cells were higher and faster, respectively, than those recorded in stage 14 oocytes (Figures 1F, 1G, 2E, and 2F). Moreover, when co-expressed with IRFP, Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates appeared round and non-aggregated, unlike the irregular ones formed by Osk-eGFP in vasaD1/vasa1 oocytes (Figures 1D and 2B). Furthermore, their fluorescence intensity was approximately 2.5-fold higher than in cells co-expressing Vasa-IRFP (Figure 2G), indicating a reduced partitioning of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP into condensates upon Vasa-IRFP co-expression.

These differences likely reflect the distinct physiological contexts of oocytes and U2OS cells. While Osk condensates may recruit additional components to increase stability and preserve their morphology in late oocytes, U2OS cells may lack these factors. Alternatively, the NLS itself may help modulate the material properties of Osk condensates. Regardless of these differences, collectively, our data suggest that Vasa-IRFP modulates the material properties of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates by promoting molecular exchange with the surrounding environment while limiting protein accumulation in the condensate phase.

Mutations disrupting the LOTUS-Vasa interaction in vitro abolish Vasa localization to Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates in cells

Vasa interacts with Short Osk in vitro, in cellulo and in vivo.15,16,17,18,19,22,23,52 Although Vasa may interact with multiple regions of Short Osk,17,52 the LOTUS domain itself is sufficient for Vasa binding and stimulation of its helicase activity in vitro.15,23 It is also required for Vasa recruitment to Osk condensates in S2 cells.16 Given this physical and functional association, we tested whether residues essential for the LOTUS-Vasa interaction in vitro could influence Vasa’s ability to modulate the material properties of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates in cells.

To do this, we used previously characterized point mutations in the LOTUS domain of Short Osk (A162E and L228E) and at the C terminus of Vasa (F504E and F508E).22,23 Based on the published co-crystal structure,23 these residues lie within the predicted LOTUS-Vasa contact region and were previously shown to abolish the LOTUS-Vasa interaction in vitro and in Drosophila S2 cells.22,23

Our fluorescence microscopy showed that these mutations strongly reduced Vasa-IRFP localization to Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates (Figures 3A, 3B, S4A, and S4B). Therefore, residues essential for the LOTUS-Vasa interaction in vitro are also required for Vasa localization to short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates in cells.

Mutations disrupting the LOTUS-Vasa interaction in vitro impair Vasa’s ability to enhance Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP exchange in cells

FRAP analysis further revealed that co-expression of Vasa-IRFP with Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP carrying the A162E, L228E, or A162/L228E mutations reduced the mobile fraction from 72.5% ± 9.0% to 21.0% ± 2.8%, 22.0% ± 5.3%, and 13.3% ± 2.4%, respectively (Figures 3C–3E; Table S2), with the corresponding t½ values of 16.1 ± 2.8, 6.0 ± 3.0, 9.4 ± 2.6, and 16.4 ± 4.8 s, respectively (Figure 3E; Table S2). In these Vasa and Osk mutants, Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates also exhibited higher intensity than those formed by co-expression of WT Osk and Vasa proteins, suggesting increased Short Osk partitioning into condensates (Figures 3F and S4E). These findings demonstrated that residues essential for the LOTUS-Vasa interaction in vitro were also important for Vasa’s ability to enhance Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP exchange in U2OS cells.

Finally, we examined Vasa point mutations known to impair its RNA binding in vitro. These included O11 (I256N) and O14 (I271M), which impair RNA binding while retaining ATPase activity,24 and GNT (K295N), which abolishes both ATP and RNA binding.29,38 Consistent with observations in vivo,24,38 these mutations did not affect Vasa-IRFP localization to Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates in U2OS cells (Figures S4F and S4G). FRAP recovery curves were also comparable between these Vasa mutants and WT Vasa-IRFP (Figures 2E, S4H, and S4I; Table S2). Moreover, condensate fluorescence intensities were similar to or lower than those with WT Vasa-IRFP (Figure S4J). Thus, mutations that disrupt Vasa RNA and ATP binding in vitro do not substantially alter Vasa’s ability to modulate the material properties of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates in cells.

Vasa is critical for germ granule mRNA localization to Osk condensates in vivo

In Drosophila, mRNAs such as nos, polar granule component (pgc), and germ cell-less (gcl) localize to germ granules formed by Osk and are required for embryonic patterning and germ cell formation.39,53,54,55,56,57,58 Since maternal loss of vasa expression disrupts embryonic patterning and abolishes germ cell formation,6,25,26 we speculated that these defects could be due to impaired mRNA localization.

Using single-molecule fluorescent in situ hybridization (smFISH), we examined nos, pgc, and gcl mRNA localization to Osk condensates in WT and vasaD1/vasa1 stage 10 to stage 14 oocytes (Figure S1A). In vasaD1/vasa1, only 60% of stage 10 oocytes exhibited nos localization, and all lost detectable posterior localization by stage 14 oocytes (Figures 4A, S5Ai, and S5Aii). In contrast, WT oocytes robustly localized nos to germ granules beginning at stage 10 of oogenesis (Figure 4A), consistent with previous observations.37,56 Similar trends were also observed for pgc and gcl (Figures S5Ai and S5Aii). Importantly, Osk-eGFP condensates persisted through oogenesis (Figure 1A), indicating that loss of mRNA localization was not due to lack of Osk condensation. Co-expression of Vasa-IRFP and Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP is necessary and sufficient for localization of germ granule mRNAs to condensates in cells

We next asked whether Vasa and Short Osk are sufficient to localize germ granule mRNAs. To test this, we co-expressed Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP with either IRFP or Vasa-IRFP together with nos, pgc, or gcl in U2OS cells. Using smFISH coupled with SIM, we quantified colocalization between these mRNAs and Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP. In IRFP-expressing cells, colocalization coefficients for nos, pgc, and gcl with Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates were 0.15 ± 0.03, 0.07 ± 0.02, and 0.18 ± 0.03, respectively (Figures 4B, 4C, S5B, and S5C). Importantly, co-expression with Vasa-IRFP markedly increased these values to 0.65 ± 0.03, 0.50 ± 0.03, and 0.46 ± 0.04, respectively (Figures 4B, 4C, S5D, and S5E), independent of mRNA expression levels (Figure S5F). Notably, the colocalization pattern mirrored the pattern observed for the three mRNAs in WT embryos, with nos showing the highest colocalization, followed by pgc and gcl.39 Therefore, Vasa and Short Osk are necessary and sufficient not only for mRNA recruitment to condensates but also to recreate the spatial patterning hierarchy observed in embryos within U2OS cells.

Finally, because the nos 3′ UTR is required for nos localization in embryos,59 we replaced it with the 3′ UTR of bicoid (bcd) or tubulin (tub), both of which abolish posterior nos localization in embryos.53,59 As expected, nos-bcd 3′ UTR and nos-tub 3′ UTR mRNAs greatly reduced colocalization with Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP when co-expressed with Vasa-IRFP (PCC(Costes): 0.13 ± 0.03 and 0.15 ± 0.03, respectively) (Figure S5E), revealing that co-expression of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP and Vasa-IRFP in U2OS cells recapitulated the mRNA localization specificity observed in embryos.60 Together, these results show that co-expression of Vasa-IRFP and Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP is sufficient for germ granule mRNA localization to condensates in U2OS cells.

Mutations disrupting the LOTUS-Vasa interaction in vitro impair Vasa-dependent nos localization to condensates

To investigate the Vasa-dependent mRNA localization to Osk condensates further, we focused on nos mRNA, which showed the strongest colocalization with Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP (Figure S5E). This provided a sensitive readout for detecting localization changes caused by mutations in either Short Osk or Vasa.

We observed that point mutations in the LOTUS-Vasa binding interface (Short Osk: A162E, L228E, and A162 E/L228E; Vasa: F504E, F508E, and F504E/F508E)23 significantly reduced nos mRNA localization to levels comparable to that observed in the IRFP control (dashed purple line) (Figures S5G–S5I). Together, these results indicate that nos localization is sensitive to the mutations that disrupt the LOTUS-Vasa interaction in vitro.

In contrast, co-expression of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP with Vasa-IRFP carrying the O11, O14, or O11/O14 mutations decreased nos colocalization to intermediate levels between IRFP and Vasa-IRFP (Figures S5J and S5K). Notably, nos localization was severely impaired in cells expressing VasaGNT-IRFP (PCC(Costes): 0.24 ± 0.03), reaching a level comparable to the nos-tub 3′ UTR control (PCC(Costes): 0.25 ± 0.03) (Figures 4D and 4E). Although O11, O14, and GNT mutations did not affect Vasa-IRFP recruitment to Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates (Figures S4F and S4G), GNT caused the most severe nos localization defect. These results suggest that additional Vasa activities, which may include Vasa’s helicase activity, act synergistically to generate a robust nos mRNA localization to Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates in cells.

Localization of nos mRNP reduces Vasa-enhanced Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP exchange

So far, we have shown that Vasa increases the mobile fraction of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates and promotes mRNA localization to these condensates in U2OS cells (Figures 2E and 4B). Next, we asked whether localization of the nos mRNA together with its proteins (mRNP) influences FRAP recovery. To do this, we generated a construct that co-expressed nos and Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP.

FRAP analysis showed that, when co-expressed with Vasa-IRFP, Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates had a mobile fraction of 72.5% ± 9.0% and a t½ of 16.1 ± 2.8 s (Figure 2F). However, upon nos expression, the mobile fraction dropped to 29.4% ± 3.6% while t½ decreased to 8.7 ± 2.6 s (Figures 4F and 4G; Table S2). Nevertheless, the condensates still recovered more than in cells co-expressing only IRFP (Figures 4F and 4G). For comparison, co-expression with nos-tub 3′ UTR, which poorly localized to Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates (Figures 4B and 4C), exhibited a mobile fraction of 59.9% ± 2.5% and a t½ of 17.3 ± 2.5 s, similar to those recorded in the absence of nos mRNP expression (Figures 4F and 4G; Table S2).

To determine whether the FRAP changes were due to localized nos mRNP or its cytoplasmic presence, we examined condensates in cells expressing nos and VasaGNT-IRFP. The GNT mutation retained Vasa’s ability to modulate condensates (Figures S4H–S4J) but disrupted nos localization (Figures 4D and 4E). These condensates exhibited a mobile fraction of 56.7% ± 6.3% and a t½ of 11.4 ± 1.8 s, comparable to those recorded in cells that did not express nos mRNA (Figures 4H and 4I; Table S2).

Together, these data revealed that localized nos mRNP reduces the exchange of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP between condensates and their surroundings in the presence of Vasa, compared to those that do not localize nos. However, in the presence of both nos mRNP and Vasa-IRFP, Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates still remained relatively dynamic, in contrast to the rigid, unrecoverable condensates observed in cells co-expressing IRFP (Figure 4J). Therefore, although localization of nos mRNP requires Vasa (Figures S5B–S5E), nos mRNP and Vasa may have opposing effects on the condensate dynamics in U2OS cells. This observation suggests a potential negative feedback mechanism that limits Vasa’s enhancement of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP exchange between the condensates and surroundings.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated how the conserved RNA helicase Vasa regulates Drosophila Osk condensates. We observed that in late oocytes, Vasa enhanced Osk-eGFP exchange between condensates and their surroundings and prevented their aggregation (Figures 1D and 1F). We found that Vasa enhanced Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP exchange independently of its IRFP tag and reduced Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP partitioning into the condensate phase in U2OS cells (Figures 2E and 2G). Mutations that disrupt Vasa interactions with the LOTUS domain of Short Osk impaired both Vasa-IRFP localization to condensates (Figures 3A, 3B, S4A, and S4B) and its ability to enhance Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP exchange (Figures 3D and S4C). Furthermore, Vasa was required for sustained localization of germ granule mRNAs to condensates in vivo (Figures 4A and S5Ai–S5Aii), while co-expression of Vasa-IRFP with Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP was sufficient to drive this localization in cells (Figures 4B and 4C). Notably, localization of nos mRNP had an opposing effect on condensate recovery compared to Vasa (Figure 4F), consistent with previous observations that RNAs and mRNPs modulate condensate material properties.61,62,63,64 Together, our findings demonstrate that the DEAD-box RNA helicase Vasa functions as a central regulator of condensate dynamics and mRNA localization in vivo and in cellulo.

Furthermore, mutations in Vasa that disrupt RNA binding in vitro (O11, O14, and GNT) did not significantly alter Vasa-IRFP recruitment to Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates in cells (Figures S4F and S4G), consistent with previous in vivo observations.24,38 We also observed that nos mRNA localization was only moderately affected by the O11 and O14 mutants (Figures S5J and S5K), while the GNT mutant caused a stronger defect (Figures 4D and 4E). These results indicate that while the interaction between Vasa and Short Osk could be sufficient to localize Vasa-IRFP to Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates, additional Vasa activities are required for mRNA localization, consistent with a parallel study.65

The mild nos mRNA localization defect observed with the O11 and O14 mutants in human cells does not imply that mRNA localization is independent of Vasa’s RNA-binding activity. The difference between in vitro and in cellulo results likely arose from cofactors present in cells and absent in vitro, such as Short Osk, which can interact with Vasa. Such interactions could rescue the loss of Vasa’s RNA-binding activity caused by point mutations, as proposed previously.66

Notably, while Vasa and Short Osk can each bind RNA in vitro,15,24,67 both proteins are required for germ granule mRNA localization to condensates in vivo and in cells (Figures 4A–4E and S5D–S5E). However, the mechanistic basis of how these proteins function to recruit mRNAs remains unclear.

Biochemical assays showed that Vasa binds the LOTUS domain of Short Osk, which stimulates its helicase activity.23 Vasa may also interact with other regions of Short Osk, including the SGNH RNA-binding domain.17,52 These observations suggest several non-mutually exclusive possibilities for the role of Vasa in mRNA localization in vivo. For example, Vasa may act as a protein chaperone that modulates the conformation of short Osk, increasing Short Osk’s affinity and specificity for germ granule mRNAs. In the absence of Vasa, other domains of Short Osk could inhibit the SGNH RNA-binding domain. Similar intramolecular inhibition has been observed in other phase-separated proteins such as G3BP1 and TDP-43.68,69 Alternatively, Vasa and Short Osk may function as a complex in which Vasa acts as an RNA clamp to stabilize interactions among Vasa, Short Osk, and bound mRNA, as observed in the piRNA processing complex in nuage.29 In another possibility, Vasa may bind mRNAs and deliver them to Short Osk, while short Osk selectively retains specific transcripts, potentially through the SGNH domain.65 Finally, Vasa may remodel RNA structures into conformations that are preferentially recognized by Short Osk.

In conclusion, DEAD-box RNA helicases maintain condensate integrity by remodeling intramolecular and intermolecular RNA-RNA interactions.33,34,35,36 Beyond this canonical role, our study reveals that Vasa directly shapes condensate properties through its influence exerted on both proteins and RNAs. By orchestrating intermolecular protein-protein, protein-RNA, and RNA-RNA interactions, Vasa serves as a multifaceted regulator that controls condensate biophysical properties and composition, thereby safeguarding against aberrant or pathological condensate formation. Given the evolutionary conservation of Vasa, this work offers broader insights into the functions of DEAD-box RNA helicases across germ granules.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we showed that Vasa enhances the recovery of Osk-eGFP condensates in Drosophila and promotes the recovery of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates in human cells. Using previously characterized mutations that disrupt the LOTUS-Vasa interaction in vitro (Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP: A162E and L228E; Vasa-IRFP: F504E and F508E), we found that these mutations impair Vasa-IRFP localization to condensates and abolish its ability to enhance Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP exchange in U2OS cells. However, the mechanism by which Vasa modulates condensate dynamics remains unclear. One possibility is that Short Osk-Vasa heterodimers form assemblies that are more fluid than Short Osk alone. Alternatively, Vasa may function as a protein chaperone, remodeling the conformation of Short Osk within condensates. Distinguishing between these possibilities will require more extensive structural analysis.

Additionally, we showed that Vasa is required for mRNA localization to condensates in both flies and U2OS cells. However, the mechanism by which Vasa mediates this process remains unresolved. Although both Vasa and Short Osk are RNA-binding proteins that can individually bind RNA in vitro,15,24,67 how they coordinate to recruit mRNAs into condensates in vivo remains unclear. Whether they function as a complex or whether one protein modulates the activity of the other prior to RNA binding remains to be determined. Addressing this question will require future structural and biochemical studies.

In addition, our cell-based experiments relied on Vasa tagged with IRFP at its C terminus (Vasa-IRFP). While we demonstrated that the IRFP tag does not account for Vasa’s ability to modulate condensates, the tagged protein may not fully recapitulate the function of endogenous Vasa. Vasa requires an intact C terminus for proper function in flies and mice,70,71,72,73 and tagging DEAD-box helicases potentially introduces functional artifacts.74

Vasa has been shown to promote Short Osk phosphorylation in flies.75 Post-translational modifications of Short Osk may differ in U2OS cells compared with endogenous Short Osk. Furthermore, the Short Osk construct used in cells lacks NLS1 and is tagged with eGFP. Together, deletion of NLS1, fluorescent tagging, and altered post-translational modifications could influence the behavior of Short Osk phase.

Finally, we show that localization of the nos mRNP reduces condensate recovery in the presence of Vasa. However, we did not test other germ granule mRNAs to determine whether these mRNAs may also regulate condensation of Osk.

Experimental model and study participant details

Fly stocks

All fly stocks and crosses were raised on standard cornmeal agar media at 25°C. To quantify the number and fluorescence intensity of Osk-eGFP condensates, we used eggs laid by females expressing Osk genetically modified with CRISPR/Cas9 at the endogenous locus with eGFP (osk-eGFP; gift from Graydon Gonsalvez Lab). We used heterozygous osk-eGFP for our experiments following our previous quantitative analysis.37 To generate flies lacking Vasa proteins, we used two vasa alleles vasaD1 and vasa1. While vasa1 produces Vasa protein detectable in the germarium of adult ovaries, vasaD1 has no detectable Vasa protein in ovaries.26 Both alleles encode identical amino acid sequences to WT Vasa.24 Females carrying vasa1/cyo; osk-eGFP/TM3sb (vasa1/cyo; BDSC: 1574) were crossed with males expressing vasaD1/cyo; osk-eGFP/TM3sb (vasaD1/cyo; gift from Ruth Lehmann lab). The resulting vasa1/vasaD1; osk-eGFP/TM3sb progeny were used for experiments. Sex was not considered in the study design because all ovaries and embryos were derived from females aged 5–10 days. In addition, the embryos analyzed were at early developmental stages, at which determining the sex of individual embryos is not feasible.

Cell culture

U2OS cells (ATCC: HTB-96) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Corning: 10-013-CV) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Corning: 35-011-CV) and 1× penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher: 15070063). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The cell line used in this study was not authenticated or tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Method details

Microscopes

Both fixed fly samples and cells were imaged in 3D with a z-step size of 150 nm for colocalization analysis and quantification of condensate number or intensity. Imaging was performed using an HC PL APO 100×/1.47 OIL CORR TIRF oil objective on a vt-instant Structured Illumination Microscope (vt-iSIM; BioVision Technologies). 405 nm, 488 nm, 561 nm and 642 nm lasers were used. SIM is a super-resolution imaging technique that provides approximately 2-fold higher resolution than confocal microscopy, and its fast-imaging speed minimizes photobleaching.43 We previously used this approach to obtain high-resolution views of mRNA-bound germ granules and stress granules, enabling us to examine the spatial relationship between mRNAs and granule proteins.37,39,47,79

For FRAP assays, live-cell imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM980 laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a temperature-controlled chamber. Oocyte and embryo FRAP experiments were done at room temperature, whereas FRAP in cultured cells was performed at 37°C. Images were acquired with a PL APO 63×/1.4 DIC oil objective using a 488 nm laser.

Immunostaining of fly ovaries

Females were fed with yeast powder the day before the dissection. Ovaries were dissected at room temperature (RT) in Schneider’s insect medium (Sigma: S0146) supplied with 10% FBS, 1× penicillin-streptomycin and 200 μg/mL insulin (Sigma: I5500-500 MG).37 Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences: 15713) in 0.1% PBTx (1× PBS (Thermo Fisher: 70011044), 0.1% Triton X-100 (Millipore: TX1568-1) for 20 min (mins) at RT, followed by two 10-min washes in 0.1% PBTx.79

Afterward tissues were blocked for 30 min at RT in blocking buffer (0.1% PBTx, 0.5% BSA (Millipore: A3294-50G), 2% normal goat serum (Abcam: ab7481)). Primary antibody incubation was carried out overnight at 4°C using rabbit anti-Osk antibody (gift from Anne Ephrussi Lab; RRID:AB_3740176) diluted 1:500 in the blocking buffer. Afterward, samples were washed five times in 0.1% PBTx for 10 min each at RT and re-blocked in the blocking buffer for 30 min at RT.

Incubation with the secondary antibody lasted 4 h (hrs) at RT using goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa 488 antibody (Thermo Fisher: A11070) diluted 1:1000 in the blocking buffer. Samples were washed five times in 0.1% PBTx for 10 min each. DNA was stained with 1 μg/mL DAPI (Sigma: 10236276001) diluted in 0.1% PBTx for 10 min at RT. Finally, samples were mounted on slides using ProLong glass antifade mountant (Thermo Fisher: P36980) and cured overnight at RT before imaging.

Immunostaining of fly embryos

Embryos were collected as described before44 and stored in 100% methanol (Thermo Fisher: A412-4) at 4°C. To rehydrate embryos, they were washed twice in 50% methanol diluted in 0.3% PBTx (1× PBS, 0.3% Triton X-100) for 5 min at RT. Then embryos were washed and permeabilized in 0.3% PBTx for 15 min, repeated three times at RT.

The subsequent immunostaining procedure followed the same steps as described for ovaries (see above), except that the blocking buffer contained 0.3% PBTx, 1% BSA and 2% normal goat serum. All washes were performed using 0.3% PBTx.

Western blotting in fly ovaries

Twenty-five to thirty well-fed females aged three to seven days were anesthetized with CO2 and dissected in Schneider’s insect medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1× penicillin-streptomycin and 200 μg/mL insulin.37 Ovaries were rinsed quickly in 1× PBS, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored in −80°C until use. For lysis, ovaries were homogenized in 100 μL of cold lysis buffer (150 mM sodium chloride (Thermo Fisher: S271-500), 50 mM Tris-HCl (Promega: H5121), 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate (Sigma: 30970-25G), and 0.1% SDS (Thermo Fisher: 15553027)). Lysates were then incubated on ice for 20 min. Downstream procedures for western blotting were performed as described previously.79 For fly experiments, the following antibodies were used: primary antibodies rabbit anti-Vasa (gift from Ruth Lehmann Lab; 1:4000; RRID:AB_2940894), rabbit anti-Osk (gift from Anne Ephrussi Lab; 1:5000; RRID:AB_3740176), mouse anti-α-tubulin (DSHB: 12G10; 1:1000), and secondary antibodies goat anti-rabbit IgG (HRP) (Abcam: ab6721; 1:10000) and goat anti-mouse IgG (HRP) (Abcam: ab6789; 1:10000).

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) of live oocytes and embryos

For live imaging of oocytes, about 10 well-fed females aged three to seven days were anesthetized with CO2 and dissected in Schneider’s insect medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1× penicillin-streptomycin. Dissection forceps were used to tease apart the connective tissue holding the ovarioles together. Ovaries were glued to a coverslip and mounted in a Lumox imaging chamber (Sarstedt: 94.6150.101) and embedded with Halocarbon 200 oil (Halocarbon: 200). For embryo imaging, embryos were collected on apple juice-agar plates after 1 h of egg laying at 25°C. They were dechorionated in 50% bleach for 1–2 min, rinsed through a Nitex nylon mesh, and washed twice with water. Embryos were then glued to a coverslip using the same procedure as for ovaries.

Because individual germ granules were smaller than the resolution limit of the microscope and therefore difficult to examine with FRAP individually, a 3 μm × 3 μm region of interest (ROI) containing germ plasm and germ granules was studied instead, as done previously.16,47 To establish the pre-bleach state, samples were imaged for 5 s at 1-s intervals and then bleached with a strong 488-nm laser pulse. Recovery was recorded by acquiring images every second thereafter as described previously.47

Transfection of cells

Xfect transfection reagent (Takara: 631318) was used following the manufacturer’s instruction. Specifically, one day before transfection, 1.2×105 cells were seeded either into a well of a 6-well plate (Corning: 351146) or into a 30 mm glass bottom dish (CELLTREAT: 229632). If the cells were used for smFISH experiments, each well also contained a collagen-coated coverslip (Neuvitro: GG-18-1.5-COLLAGEN). On the day of transfection, a total of 5 μg DNA (containing one or two plasmids) or 7.5 μg DNA (for co-transfection with three plasmids) was mixed with Xfect reagent and added to the cells. After 4–5 h, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1× penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were allowed to recover for 24 h before fixation, sample collection for western blotting or live-cell imaging.

Immunostaining of cells

Cells were seeded on collagen-coated coverslips as described above and washed three times with 1× PBS at RT. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at RT followed by three washes in 1× PBS for 5 min each. Cells were then permeabilized in 0.1% PBTx for 5 min at RT and blocked for 30 min in blocking buffer containing 1% BSA diluted in 0.1% PBTx.

Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4°C using rabbit anti-FLAG (Abcam: ab205606) diluted 1:500 in the blocking buffer. Samples were washed three times with 1× PBS for 10 min each, followed by secondary antibody incubation for 2 h at RT using goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa 647 (Thermo Fisher: A32733) diluted 1:1000 in the blocking buffer. Samples were washed three times with 1× PBS for 10 min each. DNA was stained with 1 μg/mL DAPI diluted in 1× PBS for 10 min at RT. Finally, samples were mounted on slides using ProLong glass antifade mountant and cured overnight at RT before imaging.

Western blotting in cells

Cells growing in a 6-well plate were quickly washed with 1 mL of 1× PBS. Afterward, 1 mL 1× PBS was added to each well. Cells were then scraped with a cell scraper and transferred to 1.5 mL tubes. Samples were centrifuged at 15,000 g for 3 min to remove PBS, and the resulting pellets were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until use. Pellets were resuspended in 50 μL of lysis buffer (150 mM sodium chloride, 50 mM Tris (Millipore: 648314-100 ML), 1% Triton X-100) and incubated on a rotator for 20 min at 4°C. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 g for 10 min at 4°C to remove the cellular debris. Downstream procedures for western blotting were performed as described previously.79 For cell experiments, the following antibodies were used: primary antibodies rabbit anti-FLAG (1:5000), mouse anti-HA (Thermo Fisher: 26183; 1:5000) and mouse anti-β-actin (Abcam: ab8224; 1:5000), and secondary antibodies goat anti-rabbit IgG (HRP) (1:10000) and goat anti-mouse IgG (HRP) (1:10000).

FRAP of live cells

As described in STAR Methods - transfection of cells, after 24 h of transfection on a 30 mm glass-bottom dish, cells were washed twice with 1× PBS. 1 mL of Live Cell Imaging Solution (Thermo Fisher: A59688DJ) supplemented with 10 μL of Prolong Live Antifade Reagent (Thermo Fisher: P36975) was added, and cells were incubated at 37°C for 30 min.

For FRAP of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP granules in cells, an ROI was drawn as tightly as possible around the granule of interest. Imaging was then performed as described above.

smFISH in fly oocytes

Fifteen to twenty-five well-fed females aged three to seven days were anesthetized with CO2 and dissected in Schneider’s insect medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1× penicillin-streptomycin. Ovaries were washed three times in 1× PBS and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at RT. They were then washed three times in 1× PBS and stored in 100% methanol at 4°C overnight or longer before smFISH experiments. Probe sequences for labeling nos, pgc and gcl mRNAs were described previously.79

smFISH in cells

After 24 h of transfection, cells on collagen-coated coverslips were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at RT and washed three times in 1× PBS. Coverslips were then stored in 70% ethanol (Thermo Fisher: BP2818500) at 4°C overnight. The next day, samples were rehydrated in 1× PBS and washed three times in 1× PBS at RT. Cells were then permeabilized with 0.25% PBTx (1× PBS, 0.25% Triton X-100) for 3 min at RT, followed by three washes in 1× PBS. Finally, samples were stained with smFISH probes with a modified protocol. Specifically, the probe hybridization was performed for 4–6 h at 37°C instead of overnight before the washing step.

qRT-PCR for cells

After 24 h of transfection, cells were washed three times in chilled 1× PBS. Afterward, 1 mL Trizol (Thermo Fisher: 15596026) was added directly to each well. The samples were then transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and stored at −20°C until use. For RNA extraction, 200 μL of chloroform (Thermo Fisher: C298-500) was added to each tube and the RNA was purified using RNA Clean & Concentrator kit (Zymo: R1014) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Approximately 1 μg of total RNA was treated with RQ1 RNase-Free DNase (Promega: M6101) and cDNA was synthesized using SuperScript III reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher: 18080093) following the manufacturer’s protocols. For each 10 μL qRT-PCR reaction, approximately 100 ng of cDNA, 1.5 μL of 1 μM forward and reverse primers, and 5 μL iTaq universal SYBR Green supermix (Bio-Rad: 1725122) were combined. Quantitative PCR analysis was performed on a CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantifying the number and intensity of condensates

Non-deconvolved images from vt-iSIM were used for condensate intensity analysis. For other experiments, images were deconvolved using Huygens Essential (Scientific Volume Imaging). The number and fluorescence intensity of condensates were quantified using Airlocalize spot detection algorithm as described previously.37

Western blot analysis

The analysis was performed according to the ImageJ User Guide (Section 30.13, Gels). For Drosophila samples and U2OS cells, protein levels were normalized to α-tubulin and β-actin loading controls, respectively.

Quantifying condensate distribution

We quantified Osk-eGFP or Osk condensate distribution as described in our previous publication.37 Here, “distribution” refers to the number of condensates per μm2, reflecting their spread over a defined area. A two-dimensional ROI of known micrometer dimensions was cropped from the germ plasm. For each embryo, three ROIs from distinct z-slices were averaged, as different regions of the germ plasm were in focus across the z stack.

FRAP analysis on Osk-eGFP condensates in oocytes and embryos

The analysis was performed as described previously.47 Briefly, fluorescence recovery of ROIs was quantified in ImageJ.76 Recovery curves were generated using easyFRAP,78 with full-scale normalization selected to account for differences in bleaching depth across experiments. Consequently, recovery curves were scaled from 0 to 1, with 1 corresponding to the pre-bleached ROI and 0 to the ROI immediately after photobleaching. As described previously,47 for live oocytes and embryos, the bleached ROI with the dimensions of 3 × 3 μm (please see above) was modeled as containing three populations: P1, a diffusive population residing in the intergranular space with fast mobility (M1); P2, a population located within granules that exchanges with the surrounding cytoplasm at a slower mobility (M2); and P3, the immobile fraction within granules that does not exchange. To quantify these populations, average recovery curves of normalized FRAP data were fitted to a two-term exponential equation f(t)=a(1−e−bt)+c(1−e−dt), using Sigma plot (Grafiti). In this model, a and c represent the mobile fractions of P1 and P2, respectively, while b and d correspond to their recovery rate constants. From these constants, half-time recovery (t1/2) values for P1 and P2, denoted t½ (P1) and t½ (P2), were calculated using the equations t1/2(P1)=ln⁡(2)b and t1/2(P2)=ln⁡(2)d, respectively. M2 and t½ (P2) were used to generate Figure 1G. FRAP data are included in Table S1.

FRAP analysis on Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates in cells

The analysis was performed as described previously16 and the normalization was carried out as described above. Average recovery curves of normalized FRAP data were fitted to a single-term exponential equation f(t)=a(1−e−bt) using Sigma plot, where a represents the mobile fraction and b represents the recovery rate constant. t1/2 was calculated using the equation t1/2=ln⁡(2)b. FRAP data are included in Tables S2 and S3.

Co-localization analysis of germ granule mRNAs with Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP granules

Analyses were performed using PCC(Costes) using ImageJ plugin JACoP as described before.39,47,77

qRT- PCR analysis of germ granule mRNA levels in cells

Human GAPDH was used as a control to calculate the relative transcript levels, which was 2−(Cq(geneofinterest)−AverageofCq(GAPDH)). Primers are listed in Table S4.

Statistical analysis

For fly experiments, two-tailed Mann-Whitney test was used to analyze the average number of Osk-eGFP condensates per oocyte, FRAP, western blot, and condensate distribution data. Two-tailed t test was used to analyze the intensity of Osk-eGFP condensates.

For U2OS cell experiments, FRAP, western blot, and qRT-PCR data were analyzed using two-tailed Mann-Whitney test when comparing two groups or Kruskal-Wallis test when comparing more than two groups. Colocalization and intensity of Short Osk ΔNLS1-eGFP condensates were analyzed using two-tailed t test for comparisons between two groups or ANOVA for comparisons among more than two groups.

Asterisks (∗, ∗∗, ∗∗∗, and ∗∗∗∗) indicate p < 0.05, 0.01, 0.001, and 0.0001, respectively. “Not significant” indicates p > 0.05.
