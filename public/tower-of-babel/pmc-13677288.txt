Validation and modification of a Chinese smell identification test (CSIT-12) in patients with Parkinson's disease and rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder
Authors: Xiao-Niu Liang, Xiao-Yue Wang, Jian-Cai Zhu, Yun-Wei Niu, Cong Fu, Hua-Lan Yang, Jian Wang, Huan Yu, Zuo-Bing Xiao, Yi-Min Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo enhance olfactory screening for Chinese patients with Parkinson's disease (PD) or rapid-eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD) by developing and modifying a Chinese Smell Identification Test 12 (CSIT-12).MethodsIn a discovery cohort, 69 PD patients and 47 healthy controls (HCs) were tested on 24 distinct odors using the Sniffin’ Sticks Screening Test 12 (SSST-12) and CSIT-12. The 12 odors with the highest area under the curve (AUC) were selected to construct the Chinese version of the Sniffin’ Sticks Screening Test 12 (C-SSST-12). This newly developed test was then validated in an independent cohort comprising 48 PD patients, 48 RBD patients, and 48 HCs. The reliability and validity of SSST-12, CSIT-12, C-SSST-12, and further abbreviated odor subsets were compared.ResultsScores on SSST-12, CSIT-12, and C-SSST-12 were significantly lower in both PD and RBD compared to HCs (all P < 0.001). Both the CSIT-12 and SSST-12 demonstrated good discriminative capacity for PD versus HCs (CSIT-12: AUC = 0.832, sensitivity = 77.1%, specificity = 83.3%; SSST-12: AUC = 0.913, sensitivity = 85.4%, specificity = 81.3%) and RBD versus HCs (CSIT-12: AUC = 0.881, sensitivity = 85.4%, specificity = 83.3%; SSST-12: AUC = 0.893, sensitivity = 79.2%, specificity = 81.3%). Notably, the C-SSST-12 achieved the highest discriminative performance for PD versus HCs (AUC = 0.930, sensitivity = 81.3%, specificity = 85.4%) and RBD versus HCs (AUC = 0.949, sensitivity = 89.6%, specificity = 85.4%). A 5-odor CSIT-12 subset and a 3-odor C-SSST-12 subset retained high discriminative validity.ConclusionsThis study highlights the discriminative value of utilizing culturally familiar odors for the screening of PD and RBD in Chinese populations. Furthermore, the abbreviated odor subsets offer highly efficient, rapid screening options suitable for community-based testing and fast-paced clinical environments.

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder globally after Alzheimer's disease, affecting an estimated 1.7 million individuals over the age of 55 in China [1]. Olfactory impairment, with a prevalence ranging from 50% to 90% [2], is one of the most critical non-motor symptoms of PD and frequently precedes the onset of motor symptoms by several years [3], [4], [5]. In contrast, other atypical parkinsonian syndromes, such as multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, and corticobasal degeneration, are less frequently associated with olfactory deficits. Consequently, olfactory dysfunction represents a promising biomarker for both the early screening and differential diagnosis of PD.

Rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD) is a parasomnia characterized by intermittent rapid eye movement sleep atonia accompanied by dream-enactment behaviors. Approximately 50% to 90% of patients diagnosed with RBD eventually develop PD, dementia with Lewy bodies, multiple system atrophy, or other α-synucleinopathies, as well as Alzheimer's disease [6]. The estimated 5-year risk of RBD converting to a neurodegenerative disease is 17.7%, which escalates to 40.6% at 10 years and 52.4% at 12 years [7]. Furthermore, long-term follow-up studies of 10 to 14 years indicate that 73.5% to 92.5% of RBD patients ultimately convert to PD or parkinsonism [8]. Accordingly, RBD is widely recognized as a prodromal stage of PD, making this cohort a high-priority target population for neuroprotective clinical trials. Olfactory impairment serves as a sensitive, stable biomarker in RBD and appears uniquely useful for identifying patients at high risk for early phenoconversion to PD [9].

Olfactory testing is an ideal screening modality due to its non-invasiveness, low cost, and ease of administration. Internationally, the most widely utilized tools include the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) [10] and the Sniffin’ Sticks Screening Test 16 (SSST-16), originating from the United States and Germany, respectively. The SSST-16 was subsequently modified and shortened to the Sniffin’ Sticks Screening Test 12 (SSST-12), which has been adopted in clinical practice across multiple countries, including the United States, Germany, the United Kingdom, and Greece [11], [12], [13], [14]. To optimize diagnostic performance across different ethnic groups, culturally adapted versions of the SSST-12 have been developed in Sri Lanka [15], Estonia [16], Greece [17], and India [18]. Notably, all reported superior diagnostic accuracy compared to the original German version.

In China, although the SSST-12 has been applied to patients with PD or RBD [19], [20], [21], [22], low correct identification rates among HCs for specific odors, such as lemon (54.8%), cloves (52.1%), and cinnamon (45.1%), highlight a clear lack of cultural adaptation. To address this, two recent studies validated the Brief Smell Identification Test (B-SIT) and a modified Brief Smell Identification Test for Chinese (B-SITC) in Chinese patients with PD [23], [24]. Another study demonstrated that culturally adapting the SSST-12 for Chinese population significantly improves odor identification accuracy [19]. However, these existing tests still retain unfamiliar Western odors, have not been validated in RBD cohorts, or lack a direct comparison with the SSST-12. Furthermore, while several studies have ranked odorants within the UPSIT [25], [26] and Sniffin’ Sticks Identification Test [11], [25], [27] by diagnostic performances to establish abbreviated subsets with accuracy comparable to full-length tests, most of these subsets were developed in Western populations and lack cross-cultural validity.

To overcome these limitations, our team previously developed the Chinese Smell Identification Test 12 (CSIT-12), which incorporates 12 culturally familiar odors (such as prickly ash and red bean) distinct to Chinese cuisine. In the present study, we combined the SSST-12 and CSIT-12 to comprehensively evaluate olfactory identification performance across HCs, patients with PD, and patients with RBD. This study aimed to: (1) develop and validate a Chinese version of the Sniffin’ Sticks Screening Test 12 (C-SSST-12) tailored specifically to the Chinese population for the early screening of PD and RBD; (2) identify and validate highly time-efficient abbreviated odor subsets (consisting of 3, 4, 5, or 6 odors) suitable for large-scale community screening and fast-paced clinical environments. By integrating culturally familiar odors with statistically optimized selection, the C-SSST-12 and its abbreviated subsets are expected to enhance early screening accuracy for both manifest PD and prodromal RBD in Chinese populations.

Methods

Study participants

All clinical assessments and follow-up visits were conducted between August 2022 and November 2024 at the Department of Neurology and the Sleep Center of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University. A total of 117 patients with PD, 48 patients with RBD, and 95 HCs were enrolled. The diagnosis of PD was established by two movement disorder specialists in accordance with the 2015 International Parkinson's and Movement Disorder Society Clinical Diagnostic Criteria [28]. The diagnosis of RBD was based on the 2023 International Classification of Sleep Disorders, Third Edition-Text Revision (ICSD-3-TR) criteria [29], and all patients in the RBD group were confirmed via polysomnography. HCs were recruited from the staff of Huashan Hospital and from healthy family members of patients with PD or RBD; eligible controls were over 50 years of age and had no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. Among the 95 HCs, 18 were hospital staff and 77 were healthy family members.

Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) a history of maxillofacial surgery; (2) a history of nasal or paranasal sinus diseases (e.g., rhinosinusitis, nasal polyposis, or allergic rhinitis); (3) chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (e.g., asthma), chronic sinusitis, or an acute upper respiratory tract infection within 1 week prior to testing; (4) excessive alcohol or drug use that could impair olfactory function; (5) a history of stroke, epilepsy, encephalitis, traumatic brain injury, malignancies, major cardiac events, or severe psychiatric illness; (6) inability to complete the clinical assessments. Furthermore, patients with PD or RBD were excluded if they exhibited potential cognitive impairment, defined as a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) ≤ 18 [30].

Clinical assessments

Demographic characteristics and medical history were obtained through structured, face-to-face interviews. Global cognition function was evaluated using the MMSE [31] in all patients with RBD and patients with PD during their med-on state. Motor symptoms in patients with PD were assessed during the Med-off state using the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale part III (MDS UPDRS-III) [32]. The study was approved by the Human Studies Institutional Review Board of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Olfactory identification testing

The “Sniffin’ Sticks” test, originally developed by G. Kobal in Erlangen, Germany, evaluates nasal chemosensory function using specialized felt-tip pens. The complete version comprises three subtests (odor threshold, discrimination, and identification) and takes approximately 20 to 30 min to administer [33]. The odor identification subtest requires participants to identify olfactory stimuli using a multiple-choice format. In this study, standard odor identification was evaluated using the SSST-12 [12], which consists of 12 common odorants (orange, leather, cinnamon, peppermint, banana, lemon, liquorice, coffee, cloves, pineapple, rose, and fish). A detailed protocol for SSST-12 administration has been described previously [34].

The CSIT-12 was developed by the School of Perfume and Aroma Technology at the Shanghai Institute of Technology. It was specifically designed for the Chinese culture context and features 12 familiar household odors (prickly ash, watermelon, ginger, hawthorn, cumin, red bean, aniseed, soy sauce, red dates, garlic, sesame, and onion).

Prior to testing, participants were instructed to refrain from smoking, chewing gum, or consuming sweets. Assessments were conducted in a quiet, air-conditioned room. A brief medical history regarding olfactory experiences, current medications, comorbidities, smoking habits, and occupation was recorded. Examiners wore cotton gloves throughout the procedure. The cap of each odor stick was removed, and the tip was positioned approximately 2 cm in front of both nostrils for no longer than 3–4 s. Participants were then required to select the correct odorant from a four-alternative forced-choice list. A 30-s interval was maintained between consecutive odor presentations.

Study design and workflow

Patients with PD were enrolled consecutively. The discovery cohort comprised the first 69 consecutively recruited patients with PD and 47 HCs. These participants completed both the CSIT-12 and the SSST-12, yielding a total of 24 candidate odors. For each of the 24 odorants, we performed univariate logistic regression with PD status as the dependent variable in the discovery cohort. The area under the curve (AUC) was derived from the predicted probabilities, and the 12 odorants with the highest AUC values were selected. All AUC calculations were performed with 10-fold cross-validation to mitigate optimism. Additionally, time-efficient subsets consisting of the top 3, 4, 5, or 6 odors with the highest AUC values were derived and analyzed separately from the CSIT-12, SSST-12, and C-SSST-12 frameworks.

The validation cohort consisted of the remaining 48 patients with PD and 48 HCs enrolled after the discovery phase, alongside 48 patients with RBD. Olfactory performances on the CSIT-12, SSST-12, C-SSST-12, and their respective abbreviated subsets were evaluated to determine their discriminative validity in differentiating patients with PD and RBD from HCs. Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages (%), whereas continuous variables are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD). The Pearson Chi-squared test was used to compare categorical variables. P-values for the 24 individual odors and comparisons across the three groups were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). For continuous variables, normality and homogeneity of variance were verified. The Student's t-test was applied for normally distributed data, while the Wilcoxon signed rank test was used for non-normally distributed data where appropriate. Comparisons across three groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons using the Dwass-Steel-Critchlow-Fligner (DSCF) method.

The discriminate capacity of each odorant to distinguish between the 47 HCs and 69 patients with PD in the discovery cohort was evaluated using logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation. Diagnostic performance was quantified using the AUC, sensitivity, overall accuracy, Youden's index, positive likelihood ratio (PLR), and negative likelihood ratio (NLR). The 12 odors with the highest AUC values were selected to construct the C-SSST-12. The diagnostic metrics (AUC, sensitivity, and specificity) of the C-SSST-12 and its abbreviated subsets (3-, 4-, 5-, 6-odor subtests) were further validated in the independent validation cohort (48 HCs, 48 patients with PD, and 48 patients with RBD). Test-retest reliability over the 6-month interval was evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient and Kendall's tau-b.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P-value <0.050 was considered statistically significant. 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated where applicable. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.3, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R software (version 4.1.2, the ggplot2 package and the pROC package).

Discriminative validity of the full length tests and abbreviated subsets

In the discovery cohort, the newly established C-SSST-12 achieved the highest diagnostic accuracy for discriminating patients with PD from HCs, yielding an AUC of 0.954 (sensitivity: 89.9%, specificity: 95.7%), which outperformed the CSIT-12 (AUC = 0.912) and the SSST-12 (AUC = 0.936).

This superior performance was confirmed in the independent validation cohort. The C-SSST-12 maintained the highest discriminative capability for differentiating both PD and RBD from HCs (PD: AUC = 0.930; RBD: AUC = 0.949) compared to the CSIT-12 (PD: AUC = 0.832; RBD: AUC = 0.881) and the SSST-12 (PD: AUC = 0.913; RBD: AUC = 0.893) (Table 3, Fig. 3). In general, diagnostic performance scaled positively with the number of odors included in the subsets. Using an AUC > 0.8 threshold as the baseline benchmark for acceptable diagnostic accuracy [35], ultra-short versions were successfully derived. For differentiating PD from HCs, a 5-odor subset of the CSIT-12 (comprising cumin, red bean, prickly ash, red dates, and ginger) and a 3-odor subset of the C-SSST-12 (comprising cumin, red bean, and pineapple) demonstrated sufficient discriminative validity. For differentiating RBD from HCs, a 4-odor CSIT-12 subset and a 3-odor C-SSST-12 subset achieved acceptable accuracy to serve as rapid screening versions (Table 3, Fig. 3).

Test-retest reliability of olfactory tests and subsets in HCs

At the 6-month follow-up, test-retest reliability assessments in HCs revealed modest reproducibility for the standard tests. The CSIT-12 exhibited fair reliability, with a Spearman's rank correlation of 0.364 (P = 0.137) and a Kendall's tau-b 0.315 (P = 0.124). Similarly, the SSST-12 demonstrated low-to-moderate reliability, with Spearman's correlation coefficient 0.328 (P = 0.184) and Kendall's tau-b 0.270 (P = 0.172) (Table 4). Notably, the combined C-SSST-12 displayed the highest longitudinal stability among all evaluated batteries, yielding a Spearman's correlation of 0.437 (P = 0.070) and a Kendall's tau-b of 0.382 (P = 0.066) (Table 4). Discussion

This study developed, internally derived, and externally validated a culturally adapted olfactory screening tool, the C-SSST-12, for Chinese patients with PD and RBD. The validation cohort findings confirmed that the C-SSST-12 is a reliable screening tool. By merging the widely recognized SSST-12 framework with the culturally relevant CSIT-12, we also established and verified highly time-efficient odor subsets optimized for rapid clinical application. Our findings demonstrate that the C-SSST-12 significantly enhanced discriminative performance, and that statistically optimized abbreviated subsets can preserve robust discriminative accuracy while substantially minimizing testing time.

Consistent with a large body of literature, our results confirm that olfactory identification function is profoundly impaired in patients with both PD and RBD compared to HCs. Olfactory dysfunction is a well-established prodromal feature of PD, frequently preceding the presentation of classical motor symptoms by several years [3], [4], [5]. Furthermore, hyposmia is highly prevalent in RBD [9]. Our findings align with prior studies in Chinese cohorts reporting severe hyposmia in both PD and RBD [20], [22], thereby reinforcing the immense clinical value of olfactory testing as a non-invasive biomarker for early detection of PD or other α-synucleinopathies.

Crucially, our study demonstrated that the C-SSST-12 significantly optimizes discriminative performance for both PD (AUC = 0.930) and RBD (AUC = 0.949), outperforming both the SSST-12 and CSIT-12. These results strongly validate the necessity of cultural adaptation in olfactory testing. This performance matches the landmark adaptation study in Sri Lanka [15] and exceeds the diagnostic metrics reported in similar studies from Estonia, Greece, and India [16], [17], [18]. The original German version of the SSST-12 contains several odorants, such as lemon, cloves, and cinnamon, that are culturally unfamiliar to many Chinese individuals, which typically leads to low correct baseline identification rates and compromised diagnostic sensitivity [19], [20], [22].

This phenomenon is mirrored globally, for instance, several poorly recognized SSST-16 items were removed to create the Colombo SSST-12 in Sri Lanka, which subsequently yielded an improved sensitivity of 91.0% and a specificity of 93.0% [15]. Similarly, researchers in Estonia modified specific odor items to reflect local familiarity, outperforming the original version [16]. In Greece, replacing six problematic odorants (including anis, turpentine, and liquorice, apple, lemon, and cinnamon) significantly improved identification rates and yielded reliable normative data for the Greek population [17]. In China, a previous study demonstrated that replacing poorly recognized items (cinnamon, liquorice, colophony, and cloves) with familiar local alternatives (sesame oil, soy sauce, chocolate, and garlic) significantly enhanced the diagnostic accuracy of the SSST-12 when differentiating PD from multiple system atrophy [19]. Collectively, these cross-cultural adaptations underscore the discriminative superiority of culturally tailored olfactory instruments.

Beyond the full-length test, our study provides compelling evidence supporting the utility of optimized odor subsets for rapid clinical screening. In our cohort, the odor with the highest correct identification rate among patients with PD was peppermint, whereas orange and peppermint were the top-performing odors in the RBD group, matching the outcomes of a prior machine-learning analysis [27]. Remarkably, the 3-odor subset of the C-SSST-12 (cumin, red bean, and pineapple) and the 5-odor subset of the CSIT-12 (cumin, red bean, prickly ash, red dates, and ginger) both achieved an AUC > 0.80 for both PD and RBD, demonstrating a discriminative capacity comparable to full-length batteries.

While our previous work identified a highly accurate 5-odor subset (coffee, orange, mint, pineapple, and fish) for discriminating PD from HCs, that subset was selected strictly based on mathematical diagnostic performance rather than cultural familiarity [22]. Several external studies have similarly verified that abbreviated odor panels can match the diagnostic utility of full-length testing [11], [25], [26], [27]. For example, an 8-odor subset performed equivalently to the full SSST-16 [11], and a machine learning study in England and Germany showed that a 3-odor subset of the SSST-16 (anise, licorice, and banana) could accurately classify PD and RBD [27]. Furthermore, a 7-odor subset shared between the SSST-12 and UPSIT (licorice, banana, clove, rose, mint, pineapple, and cinnamon) proved highly effective in distinguishing PD and dementia with Lewy bodies from HC [25], while a separate 12-item subset performed comparably to the full 40-item UPSIT [26]. Other investigations have similarly confirmed that simplified 3- to 5-odor configurations provide acceptable sensitivity and specificity [36], [37]. Crucially, by drastically shortening administration time, these abbreviated subsets enhance the feasibility of large-scale community screening and routine assessments in busy clinical settings.

Strengths of this study included: (1) Diagnoses of PD and RBD were established through specialist evaluation using standardized clinical criteria, ensuring high diagnostic accuracy; (2) The CSIT-12 was jointly developed with professional fragrance institutes, ensuring its cultural relevance and ecological validity within the target population; (3) We directly compared multiple olfactory tests and their subsets within the same cohort, enabling a robust evaluation of relative discriminative performance; (4) The inclusion of an independent validation cohort enhances the reliability and generalizability of our findings. Notably, while previous studies have proposed optimized odor subsets, many lacked external validation or yielded inconsistent results across cohorts. Our study addresses this gap by demonstrating reproducible performance. Limitations of this study included: (1) Test-retest reliability was assessed exclusively in HCs, and future studies should evaluate longitudinal stability in PD and RBD cohorts with larger sample size; (2) Although our cohort was thoroughly characterized, large-scale, multicenter studies across China are required to confirm generalizability and further refine odor selection; (3) Our cohort had relatively preserved cognition, which is typical for early-stage PD. However, cognitive impairment is common in advanced PD and may affect olfactory test performance. Future studies should validate the C-SSST-12 and its abbreviated subsets in patients with varying cognitive status, including those with mild cognitive impairment and dementia.

In conclusion, the culturally adapted C-SSST-12 demonstrates superior discriminative performance for PD and RBD in Chinese populations compared with existing olfactory assessments. Furthermore, both the simplified 3-odor subset of the C-SSST-12 and the 5-odor subset of the CSIT-12 offer time-efficient, cost-effective, and non-invasive alternatives well-suited for community-based screening. Future research should focus on validating the C-SSST-12 and its abbreviated subsets in large scale cohorts to further optimize prediction accuracy. Additionally, longitudinal studies are necessary to determine whether the C-SSST-12 can serve as a viable biomarker for monitoring disease progression in PD.
