Intelligent Field‐Adaptive Electrohydrodynamic Printing for Curved Conformal Electronics
Authors: Chang Liu, Yiwen Feng, Xu Chen, Kaiwen Chen, Ran Zhang, Xiaopeng Zhang, Liujia Suo, Xiangji Chen, Zefei Li, Fengshu Wang, Ruixuan Xu, Shuaiwu Liu, Shiwen Liang, Lingjie Kong, Dazhi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCurved conformal electronics are increasingly important for wearable devices, biosensing, and advanced communication systems, requiring high‐resolution and high‐precision manufacturing on complex surfaces. Electrohydrodynamic printing is a promising method for fabricating conformal electronics, but its application on curved insulating substrates is hindered by local electric‐field disturbances arising from residual charge accumulation and spatial variations, leading to poor dimensional control. To address this challenge, an intelligent field‐adaptive electrohydrodynamic printing framework is established, in which alternating‐electric‐field modulation reduces the effects of residual charge, the equivalent electrical gap quantitatively characterizes spatially varying electric‐field conditions, and machine learning‐based parameter regulation compensates for local electric‐field variations. Based on this framework, a machine‐learning and alternating‐electric‐field synergistic modulated electrohydrodynamic printing approach is developed to achieve stable printing and precise line‐width control on curved substrates. The approach rapidly optimizes parameters for target line widths, achieving average relative errors around 10% or lower at the initial printing position, and further reduces average relative line‐width errors by up to 7.24 percentage points through adaptive regulation during curved‐substrate printing. Diverse high‐precision patterns are printed, and the conformal frequency‐selective surface and antenna with excellent electromagnetic performance are fabricated, demonstrating the potential of this framework for advancing curved conformal electronics manufacturing.Keywords: alternating electric field, artificial neural network, curved conformal electronics, electrohydrodynamic printing, insulating substrates

Introduction

Curved conformal electronics refers to electronic systems that can geometrically conform to non‐planar substrates, enabling functional integration on 3D surfaces [1]. With the escalating demand for 3D electronic systems and structure‐function integration, curved conformal electronics has garnered increasing attention and demonstrated extensive application potential in aeronautics and aerospace, biomaterials and medical technology, wearable devices, and advanced equipment [2, 3, 4]. Compared to the manufacturing of conventional planar electronics, the construction of curved conformal electronics is significantly restricted by complex spatial geometries. Currently, the main methods for manufacturing curved conformal electronics encompass curved surface lithography [5], transfer printing [6], inkjet printing [7], aerosol printing [8], and electrohydrodynamic (EHD) printing [9, 10]. Although curved surface lithography provides high resolution, it is restricted by the complexity of mask design and cost‐related factors. Transfer printing methods have limited applicability to non‐developable surfaces and those with complex convex‐concave features. Inkjet printing and aerosol jet printing offer advantages in process simplicity, fabrication flexibility, and damage‐free manufacturing. However, their dependence on droplet formation, atomization conditions, and ink physicochemical properties limits printing resolution and material adaptability, thereby restricting the dimensional accuracy and functional performance of fabricated structures. In contrast, EHD printing uses an electric field to generate fine liquid jets that are much smaller than the nozzle diameter, thereby enabling the deposition of functional microstructures with line widths smaller than the nozzle size [11]. As a result, EHD printing offers clear advantages in printing resolution, pattern precision, and material adaptability [12, 13]. Benefiting from these advantages, EHD printing has been increasingly explored for the fabrication of conformal electronics on curved surfaces [14, 15, 16].

However, the performance advancement of curved conformal electronics places increasingly stringent demands on fabrication accuracy and process stability. Since these devices are commonly constructed on curved insulating substrates, conventional EHD printing is constrained by local electric‐field fluctuations induced by residual charge accumulation and spatial variations during curved‐surface deposition, which greatly limits its further development for high‐precision conformal electronics manufacturing [17]. More specifically, the deposited liquid carries like charges during EHD printing, which are difficult to dissipate on insulating substrates [18]. As a result, residual charges accumulate on the substrate surface and disturb the local electric‐field distribution [19]. Meanwhile, during curved‐surface printing, the spatial relationship between the nozzle and the grounded electrode changes continuously along the printing path, further causing position‐dependent fluctuations in the driving electric field [20]. As the electric field governs both the printing state and structural dimensions, the combined effects of residual charge accumulation and position‐dependent electric field fluctuations directly lead to unstable printing and deposition‐size variations. This significantly increases the difficulty of dimensional control in conformal surface patterning, thereby hindering the reliable fabrication of delicate functional structures on complex curved surfaces [21, 22, 23]. Various strategies have been explored to mitigate local electric‐field variations arising from residual charge accumulation and spatial changes in nozzle position during EHD printing on curved insulating substrates. These include modifying the local electric field environment, such as introducing dielectric coatings [17] or removing ground electrodes while relying on self‐induced electric fields [24], as well as adjusting process parameters, including the applied voltage [20] or nozzle‐substrate distance [25]. However, these approaches exhibit significant limitations. The introduction of dielectric coating requires pre‐deposition of nanoparticle layers on the substrate surface, resulting in increased process complexity and limited adaptability to complex curved geometries. Methods relying on self‐induced electric fields can regulate the local electric‐field distribution to a certain extent and partially alleviate field fluctuations induced by variations in nozzle position. However, owing to the inherently insulating nature of the substrate, they have limited ability to suppress residual charge accumulation and the resulting electric‐field distortion. Moreover, they cannot fully compensate for the position‐dependent field fluctuations caused by continuous changes in the relative position between the nozzle and the grounded electrode. Parameter adjustment strategies can reduce the charge carried by the liquid but cannot fundamentally suppress the adverse effects of residual charges. Moreover, compensating for position‐induced electric field variations through empirical tuning of process parameters relies heavily on trial‐and‐error, leading to limited regulation accuracy, poor adaptability to complex curved surfaces, and substantial consumption of time and experimental resources. Overall, existing methods can only partially alleviate local electric‐field disturbances caused by residual charge accumulation and position‐dependent nozzle‐electrode variations during EHD printing on curved insulating substrates. These methods still exhibit clear limitations and cannot fundamentally resolve the field fluctuations that undermine printing stability and dimensional accuracy. More fundamentally, a unified field‐adaptive theoretical and technical system for this process has not yet been established. Such a system is required to mitigate the adverse effects associated with residual charge accumulation through an appropriate physical mechanism, quantitatively describe the influence of nozzle‐electrode positional variations on the local electric field, and compensate for position‐dependent field variations to maintain stable and high‐precision deposition.

To address local electric‐field disturbances arising from residual charge accumulation and position‐dependent effects during EHD printing, this work establishes a unified intelligent field‐adaptive theoretical and technical framework for high‐precision fabrication of conformal structures on curved insulating substrates. In this framework, alternating‐electric‐field modulation serves as the physical mechanism for mitigating residual‐charge effects, an equivalent electrical gap is introduced as a quantitative descriptor of position‐dependent electric field variations, and machine‐learning‐guided multi‐parameter regulation is used to compensate for these field variations and maintain stable, consistent line‐width formation.

In this paper, based on the intelligent field‐adaptive EHD printing theoretical and technical framework, a machine‐learning and alternating‐electric‐field synergistic modulated electrohydrodynamic printing approach, abbreviated as ML‐AE‐EHD printing, is developed. Within this approach, an alternating electric field is introduced to establish stable printing conditions through dynamic charge neutralization [26, 27], thereby enhancing electric field stability, and experimental data are collected to develop a predictive model that characterizes the mapping from process parameters and nozzle position to the resulting printing state and line‐width dimensions. An equivalent electrical gap is further introduced to represent position‐dependent electric field conditions as a unified model input. Optimal process parameters required to achieve target line widths under stable printing conditions are then determined through inverse optimization based on the intelligent optimization algorithm, enabling rapid and reliable identification of suitable parameter combinations without reliance on empirical trial‐and‐error, while establishing a robust parameter baseline for subsequent regulation. Intelligent and coordinated regulation of multiple process parameters is further implemented along the printing path on curved insulating substrates in response to variations in the equivalent electrical gap, enabling effective compensation for electric field fluctuations induced by spatial variations in nozzle position, thereby ensuring stable printing and consistent, high‐precision control of line‐width dimensions across complex curved surfaces. The proposed ML‐AE‐EHD printing approach achieves high‐precision patterning with controllable line widths on diverse insulating curved substrates and enables the fabrication of a curved conformal frequency‐selective surface (FSS) [28, 29, 30] and a curved conformal antenna [31, 32, 33] with excellent electromagnetic performance. Successful high‐precision and stable manufacturing on curved insulating substrates not only verifies the effectiveness of the ML‐AE‐EHD printing approach but also confirms the validity and practicality of the proposed intelligent field‐adaptive theoretical and technical framework. These results demonstrate that this work advances the manufacturing level of conformal electronics and provides technical support for their further development.

Materials

In this work, a signal generator (Keysight 33521B) provides electrical signals with defined voltage amplitude, frequency, and duty cycle. The signals are amplified by a high‐voltage amplifier (Trek 10/10B‐HS) and applied between the nozzle and the curved insulating substrate to generate a controllable electric field. A five‐axis motion platform governs the relative motion between the nozzle and the substrate, enabling precise control of the velocity and the nozzle‐substrate distance. The nozzle orientation is adjusted in real time so that its axis remains aligned with the normal local surface of the curved substrate. A dispensing machine (MUSASHI V2) supplies back pressure to ensure continuous and stable delivery of the paste during printing. The output of the high‐voltage amplifier is connected to the needle, which functions as the upper electrode, while a metal plate placed beneath the curved insulating substrate is grounded and serves as the bottom electrode. In this work, a nano‐silver paste (Shenzhen Sryeo Electronic Paste Co., Ltd.) with a solids content of 85% and silver particle sizes ranging from 300 to 500 nm is used as the printing material. A stainless‐steel nozzle with an outer diameter of 100 µm is used for the printing experiments. Alumina ceramic is selected as the printing substrate. Prior to printing, the substrate is ultrasonically cleaned in absolute ethanol for 10 min and subsequently dried under a nitrogen stream.

Methodology

Figure 1 shows the proposed intelligent field‐adaptive EHD printing theoretical and technical framework and its validation in stable, high‐precision printing on curved insulating substrates. Figure 1a illustrates how alternating‐electric‐field modulation suppresses residual charge accumulation and mitigates the associated electric‐field distortion within the intelligent field‐adaptive EHD printing theoretical and technical framework. Neutralization between positive and negative surface charges changes the surface‐charge distribution and consequently reconfigures the local electric field, thereby mitigating residual‐charge effect [34]. The printing process on curved insulating substrates involves dynamic charge compensation through the directional migration and redistribution of electrons and cations within the silver paste under the applied alternating electric field, thereby enhancing printing stability. The liquid bridge [35] is primarily governed by two driving forces: the electric field force induced by the alternating electric field and the mechanical pulling force exerted by the substrate [36]. The magnitude of the electric field force depends on the local electric field strength, which is governed by multiple coupled factors. These factors include the voltage applied between the nozzle and the ground electrode, the spatial distance between the nozzle and the ground electrode, the distance between the nozzle and the substrate, and the dielectric properties of the substrate [37, 38, 39]. Under the conditions of a curved insulating substrate, as the nozzle moves along the printing path on the curved surface, both the geometric distance between the nozzle and the ground electrode and the substrate structure vary with the spatial position [40]. This causes the electric field between the nozzle and the substrate to change with the spatial position, thereby affecting the stability of the liquid bridge and the line‐width dimensions of the deposition.

As shown in Figure 1c, the ML‐AE‐EHD printing approach serves as the execution layer of the intelligent field adaptive EHD printing framework. It consists of three integrated stages: process modeling based on machine learning, determination of optimal process parameters for target line widths, and intelligent regulation of process parameters along printing paths on curved substrates. In Stage I, an alternating electric field is introduced to alleviate residual charge accumulation and establish stable printing conditions. Moreover, experimental data are collected to construct a predictive model based on an artificial neural network(ANN) [42]. To enhance model performance, the artificial lemming algorithm (ALA) [43, 44, 45] is employed to optimize network weights and biases, improving convergence behavior and generalization capability. An equivalent electrical gap is further introduced as a unified model input to represent the position‐dependent electric field conditions. This enables the model to capture the nonlinear coupling among process parameters, nozzle position, and the resulting printing state and line‐width dimensions. In Stage II, intelligent inverse optimization is performed to determine the process parameters that achieve the target line width at the initial position under stable printing conditions, where the discrepancy between the model‐predicted output and the target printing state and line‐width dimensions is defined as the fitness function of ALA (The fitness function is shown in Equation S2). Through iterative updates of individual positions, optimal parameter combinations are efficiently identified without reliance on empirical trial‐and‐error, establishing a robust parameter baseline for subsequent path‐dependent regulation. In Stage III, building upon this baseline, intelligent regulation of process parameters is implemented along the printing path on curved substrates. The equivalent electrical gap is first calculated at each discrete position along the printing path to characterize position‐dependent electric field conditions, and process parameters corresponding to each position are then determined through ALA‐based inverse optimization. The fitness function incorporates both the deviation between predicted and target printing outcomes and a smoothness constraint on parameter variation between adjacent positions, suppressing abrupt parameter changes and ensuring parameter continuity (The fitness function is shown in Equation S4). These position‐specific parameters are subsequently applied to regulate the printing process along the printing path, enabling coordinated adaptation to position‐dependent electric field conditions. As a result, stable and continuous printing is achieved while maintaining high‐precision control of line‐width dimensions across complex curved surfaces. These stages collectively constitute the ML‐AE‐EHD printing approach, enabling charge neutralization and intelligent parameter regulation for stable and precise electrohydrodynamic printing on curved insulating substrates.

Figure 1d illustrates the liquid bridge states under different printing approaches on curved insulating substrate. Conventional EHD printing uses a unidirectional direct‐current electric field to drive material deposition. AE‐EHD printing introduces alternating‐electric‐field modulation to mitigate residual‐charge‐related disturbances and improve liquid‐bridge stability. Building on AE‐EHD printing, ML‐AE‐EHD printing further incorporates the equivalent electrical gap and machine‐learning‐assisted coordinated regulation of multiple process parameters to compensate for position‐dependent electric‐field variations on curved substrates. In conventional EHD printing, residual charges have a tendency to accumulate on the curved insulating surface. This accumulation causes the liquid bridge to be disrupted by Coulomb forces [46], resulting in significant deflection and whipping, and ultimately reducing the printing stability.

By contrast, in AE‐EHD printing, the alternating electric field enables periodic charge neutralization, thereby effectively weakening the influence of residual charges on the liquid bridge. As a result, the whipping behavior of the liquid bridge is significantly suppressed, and the printing stability is improved. However, during printing on curved insulating substrates, as the nozzle moves along the printing path, the spatial relationship between the nozzle and the grounded electrode continuously changes. Accordingly, the equivalent electrical gap varies, which further causes changes in the electric field strength between the nozzle and the substrate. Without process parameter regulation, the liquid bridge tends to deflect in response to these local electric field variations, and this deflection becomes more pronounced with increasing equivalent electrical gap, thereby weakening the printing stability under curved‐surface conditions. In contrast, ML‐AE‐EHD printing compensates for the electric field fluctuations induced by changes in the equivalent electrical gap through the intelligent coordinated regulation of multiple process parameters, enabling the liquid bridge to maintain deposition along the local normal direction of the curved surface throughout the printing process. The results indicate that ML‐AE‐EHD printing not only reduces the influence of residual charges on liquid bridge stability by means of the alternating electric field, but also further suppresses the adverse effects of electric field disturbances caused by spatial position changes on liquid bridge behavior through intelligent process parameter regulation along the printing path, thereby effectively improving the stability, consistency, and controllability of deposition behavior on curved surfaces.

During the printing process, the printing state demonstrates multiple modes. A stable liquid bridge can only be formed when the printing state is favorable, which allows for the deposition of line widths with high consistency. Therefore, to precisely achieve the formation of the target line width on curved substrates, the constructed model must simultaneously predict both the printing state and line width dimensions, thus ensuring that the optimized parameters have practical feasibility [47, 48]. This work employs an ANN model to predict printing state and line width dimensions. To construct the ANN model within the ML‐AE‐EHD printing approach for curved insulating surfaces, seven key process variables that have a significant influence on the printing state and line‐width dimensions are carefully selected. These variables encompass: voltage amplitude (ranging from 1.0 to 3.1 kV), frequency (ranging from 10 to 800 Hz), duty cycle (ranging from 4 to 97%), equivalent electrical gap (ranging from 0.65 to 10.45 mm), nozzle‐substrate distance (ranging from 150 to 450 µm), velocity (ranging from 1 to 8 mm/s), and back pressure (ranging from 15 to 100 kPa). The dataset used in this work is collected from printing experiments conducted on semicylindrical substrates with curvature radii ranging from 5 to 100 mm. To ensure adequate coverage and sampling resolution within the 7D process‐parameter space while considering the experimental workload, Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) [49, 50, 51] is used to sample 1200 process‐parameter combinations. After normalization of each process parameter, the centered L 2‐discrepancy of the 1200 sample points is 0.0081, while the mean and standard deviation of the nearest‐neighbor distances are 0.3184 and 0.0650, respectively. Moreover, 95.42% of the sample points have nearest‐neighbor distances within the range of the mean plus or minus two standard deviations (shown in Figure S1). These quantitative results indicate that the samples provide uniform and adequate coverage of the process‐parameter space, with good overall space‐filling characteristics, balanced local spacing, and no obvious clustering or spatial isolation. For each process‐parameter combination, the printing experiment is independently repeated three times to ensure the reliability of the experimental results. The resulting dataset is subsequently divided into training and test sets at a ratio of 75:25 for model training and generalization performance evaluation. All printing experiments used for dataset construction were conducted on substrates with semicylindrical curved surfaces. Accordingly, the training set consists entirely of curved‐substrate samples covering curvature radii ranging from 5 to 100 mm.

Although ANN can learn non‐linear mapping relationships between input features and outputs by simulating biological neural systems, their performance in practical applications is highly susceptible to the initial settings of weights and biases [52, 53, 54]. This susceptibility may cause the networks to converge at local optima, thus restricting their learning accuracy and generalization capabilities [55, 56]. The ALA effectively identifies optimal solutions to complex problems by mimicking the behavior of lemmings in nature. Therefore, in Stage I of the ML‐AE‐EHD printing approach for curved insulating surfaces, the ALA is introduced to optimize the weights and biases of the ANN. This action enhances the network's learning ability and generalization performance. As a result, an ALA‐ANN model is constructed to predict the printing states and line width dimensions. Results and Discussion

To more comprehensively assess the ALA‐ANN model's capability to identify printing states and predict line width dimensions during Stage I of the ML‐AE‐EHD printing approach for curved surfaces, this work additionally introduces the traditional ANN, the genetic algorithm‐optimized artificial neural network (GA‐ANN) [57], and the particle swarm optimization‐optimized artificial neural network (PSO‐ANN) [58] as comparison models.

Figure 4 shows the confusion matrices [59] for the different models. As depicted in Figure 4a, the baseline ANN model can already differentiate between the two printing states to a certain degree. However, there are still obvious cases of misclassification. In the all dataset, the ANN model achieves recognition accuracies of 87.57% and 90.19% for favorable and poor printing states, respectively. This indicates that there is still room for improvement in the model's ability to characterize the boundaries of printing states under complex process parameter conditions. After optimization using swarm intelligence algorithms, the model's ability to discriminate between the two types of printing states is enhanced. As shown in Figure 4b, the GA‐ANN model attains recognition accuracies of 95.48% and 93.26% for favorable and poor printing states, respectively, on the all dataset. As shown in Figure 4c, the PSO‐ANN model achieves recognition accuracies of 93.79% and 92.55% in the all dataset, respectively, which also outperform the baseline ANN model. This suggests that optimizing the network's initial weights and biases can boost model performance and improve the ability to distinguish between printing states. In contrast, Figure 4d shows that the ALA‐ANN model exhibits superior classification results across diverse datasets. On the training set, the ALA‐ANN attains recognition accuracies of 98.13% and 95.42% for favorable and poor printing states, respectively. On the test set, the recognition accuracy for these two states is 95.40% and 93.90%, respectively, and the overall recognition accuracy is 94.33%. On the all dataset, they reach 97.46% and 95.04%, respectively. All these accuracies are the highest or near‐highest among the four models.

Meanwhile, as shown in Figure 5c, the R2 values of the PSO‐ANN model increased by 0.0643 and 0.0478, respectively. Moreover, the predicted points are more concentrated around the ideal fit line. In contrast, as shown in Figure 5d, the ALA‐ANN model exhibits optimal fitting and generalization performance, attaining R2 values of 0.9723 and 0.9452 on the training and test datasets, respectively. Moreover, most of the predicted points are within a ±20% error margin, which suggests that the ALA‐ANN model can precisely map the intricate nonlinear relationship between printing process parameters and line width.

After acquiring the printing process parameters that satisfy the target line width requirements at the initial position, the equivalent electrical gap at each discrete position point along the printing path on the curved substrate is further computed. At each position point, the corresponding process parameters for the target line width are ascertained based on the ALA. Conclusions

To achieve high‐precision fabrication of conformal functional structures on curved insulating substrates for curved conformal electronics, this work presents an intelligent field‐adaptive EHD printing theoretical and technical framework to address local electric‐field fluctuations during curved‐surface printing. By integrating alternating‐electric‐field modulation for reducing residual‐charge effects, an equivalent electrical gap for characterizing path‐dependent electric‐field conditions, and machine learning based parameter regulation for compensating position‐dependent field variations, this framework gives rise to the ML‐AE‐EHD printing approach, thereby enabling stable and controllable deposition and the high‐precision fabrication of conformal functional structures on curved insulating substrates.

In Stage I of the ML‐AE‐EHD printing approach, the alternating electric field enables periodic charge neutralization, thereby alleviating the influence of residual charges on the driving electric field, suppressing the whipping behavior of the liquid bridge, and improving the printing stability. On this basis, a high‐precision prediction model for the printing state and line width dimensions is established. The results indicate that the model shows outstanding performance across diverse datasets. On the test set, the recognition accuracy for the printing state attains 94.33%, and the R2 between the predicted line width and the actual results reaches 0.9452, with a mean absolute relative error of 8.41%. These results verify the predictive accuracy and robustness of the model for both the printing state and line width dimensions.

In Stage II of the ML‐AE‐EHD printing approach, the printing process parameters corresponding to the target line width at the initial position on the curved surface are optimized through ALA‐based inverse search. The experimental results show that the average relative errors for target line widths of 30, 60, and 90 µm are 10.24%, 6.77%, and 5.21%, respectively. This confirms the effectiveness of the proposed approach in inversely optimizing process parameters for target‐width fabrication at the initial position on curved substrates.

In Stage III of the ML‐AE‐EHD printing approach, the process parameters are intelligently regulated along the curved‐surface printing path according to variations in the equivalent electrical gap. By comparing the actual line widths on curved substrates before and after regulation, the average relative errors for target line widths of 30, 60, and 90 µm are found to decrease by 7.24 percentage points, 6.70 percentage points, and 2.41 percentage points, respectively. These results confirm the effectiveness of the proposed approach in achieving line width control during printing on curved insulating substrates. Based on this approach, delicate patterns with varying line widths are successfully printed on diverse curved insulating substrates, and the curved conformal FSS and CPW antenna with excellent electromagnetic performance are successfully fabricated. These results demonstrate that the ML‐AE‐EHD printing approach enables stable and precise fabrication of conformal structures on curved insulating substrates, while further validating the effectiveness of the proposed intelligent field‐adaptive EHD printing framework. This work provides a systematic theoretical and technical foundation for improving the manufacturing capability of conformal electronics and promoting their further development.
