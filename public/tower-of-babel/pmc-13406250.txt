Physeal Growth May Resume Following Removal of Transphyseal Epiphysiodesis Screws
Authors: Evan L. Honig, Erikson Nichols, Pari L. Palandjian, Sarah Pilcher, Jeremi M. Carswell, Roger Widmann, Emily R. Dodwell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 14 +6 year-old (bone age 13 +0 years) genetically 46, XX, phenotypic female patient underwent distal femur and proximal tibia screw epiphysiodesis for a leg length discrepancy related to overgrowth following juvenile ACL reconstruction. The patient was considering, but had not committed to, gender affirmation treatment (internally the patient felt male). The patient later started testosterone, which altered predictions of height and leg length discrepancy at maturity. Screws were removed to minimize overshortening of the previously long leg. Upon screw removal, modest growth of the operative limb resumed. Following removal of transphyseal screws in skeletally immature patients, growth may resume.

Epiphysiodesis has historically been favored over limb lengthening for the treatment of leg length discrepancy when the difference at maturity is predicted to be 2 to 5 cm. Percutaneous screw epiphysiodesis is a minimally invasive technique with few complications that inhibits physeal growth of the longer limb. Many surgeons believe that percutaneous epiphysiodesis with transphyseal screws (PETS), placement of two cannulated fully threaded screws across the physis, may lead to irreversible growth arrest,1,2 although original reports of this technique indicated potential reversibility.3

This case involves a 46, XX 14 +6 year-old (bone age 13 +0 years) patient who underwent screw epiphysiodesis of the left distal femur and proximal tibia for a predicted 2.0 cm leg length discrepancy (LLD) at maturity. It is standard of care at our institution to obtain standing films preoperative to anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) surgery, to document alignment and length, as both growth inhibition and overgrowth are possible following pediatric ACL reconstruction due to proximity to the open physes. In this case, 1 month preoperative to the ACL index surgery, the patient's LLD was initially 8-mm long on the left side. A LLD developed and was attributed to overgrowth following ACL reconstruction, performed at age 11 years. Percutaneous epiphysiodesis with transphyseal screws was performed approximately 3 years post ACL surgery, when the LLD was 1.9 mm and anticipated to be 2.0 cm at maturity. Upon PETS removal 22 months following index epiphysiodesis surgery, growth of the epiphysiodesed limb resumed.

Case Report

A 14 +6 years-old female (46, XX, female bone age 13 +0 years) patient was indicated for epiphysiodesis due to a predicted LLD of 2.0 cm at skeletal maturity. The etiology of the leg length discrepancy was considered developmental: overgrowth secondary to Modified Macintosh procedure for ACL instability4 at age 11 +7 years.

At the time of epiphysiodesis, height was 160 cm. Based on bone age of 13 years, according to the female standards of Greulich and Pyle,5 and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) growth charts,6 assuming continuation of estrogen-driven puberty, height at maturity was predicted at 166.4 cm. The patient underwent screw epiphysiodesis of the left distal femur and proximal tibia with tantalum bead insertion for optimal physeal monitoring per institutional standard care. Percutaneous epiphysiodesis with transphyseal screws is frequently the preferred technique employed at our institution due to minimal postoperative pain, rapid return to sports, and immediate cessation of growth compared with drill/curette epiphysiodesis. The Halifax (Halifax Biomedical) 1-mm tantalum bead and its insertion device are U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved (Regulation Number 21CFR§878.4300, 501(k) Number: 103417) and also approved in Canada (CMDCAS Certificate #US09-5583; Health Canada's Medical Device License #82181) and Europe (EC Certificate Full Quality Assurance System: US09/5582; SGS System Certification, ISO 13485:2003; SGS System Certification, ISO 9001:2008; Certificate US09/5585). The Tantalum Bead set is indicated for use to measure the movement of implants after surgery with the aid of an x-ray system (radiostereometric analysis).

Two fully threaded cannulated 7.3-mm Synthes screws were placed in the distal femur and proximal tibia, effectively tethering the physis at each site and resulting in complete growth inhibition.2 The technique involved making medial and lateral skin incisions proximal to the femoral physis and distal to the tibial physis. Guide wires were placed at both medial and lateral cortices and directed across the physes in an oblique fashion, crossed on the anterior-posterior view. The guide wires were overdrilled, and screws were inserted with a goal of at least four threads across the tibial physis and 5 to 6 threads across the femoral physes. Beads were placed in the epiphysis with a separate percutaneous incision and passed through cortex using the trochar function of the inserter2 (Figure 1). Metaphyseal beads were placed through the same incision as screw insertion. A 2-mm separate drill hole was required at the metaphyseal bone due to stronger cortex at this location. Progression of Chronologic Age, Bone Age, Height, and Bone Length Over the Course of Treatment

The patient desired to start testosterone therapy to align physical appearance with gender identity, including height optimization. The patient was initiated on testosterone cypionate 25 mg subcutaneously monthly at the chronologic age of 16 +1 years, 19 months postepiphysiodesis. The dosage was gradually increased over 1 +8 years to reach a weekly maintenance dose of 50 mg weekly. Bone age before starting testosterone was estimated at 13 +6 years, according to the female standards of Greulich and Pyle.5 At the time of testosterone initiation, height was 164.6 cm and predicted adult female height was 168.9 cm through the Bayley-Pinneau method, a method commonly used in pediatric endocrinology8 and 169.7 cm through the Multiplier7 Mobile App (International Center for Limb Lengthening, Sinai Hospital of Baltimore) and bone age.

The screws had completely inhibited growth from the time of their insertion, based on no change in interbead distance. Figure 2 demonstrates the interbead distance measurement perpendicular to the physis and the threads counted across the physis. Imaging was completed per physician preference and availability of imaging, including standing hip to ankle imaging via EOS and standard X-rays calibrated to a mag ball, ruler, or other object of known length. Measurements on EOS/radiograph have been noted to have excellent agreement with interrater reliability at 0.96 and intrarater reliability at 0.95.9 The accuracy and reliability of measurements made with EOS and tantalum beads has been reported at standard error <1 mm between beads. Physes remained open at the time of limb equalization (Figure 3). It was recognized that if left in situ, with additional testosterone-driven growth now anticipated, the once shorter leg would likely overcorrect and an opposite leg length discrepancy would develop. Screw removal was recommended to avoid overshortening of the previously long limb.

A, Anterior-posterior and (B) lateral radiographs obtained approximately 2 years postoperatively, before screw removal, demonstrating physes not yet fused.

At 22 months following index epiphysiodesis, the patient underwent removal of hardware (ROH). The patient was 16 +5 years old at the time of removal. At the time of hardware removal, leg lengths were equal, with individual bone segment lengths as recorded (Table 1).

The alternative to screw removal on the surgical leg would have been to perform epiphysiodesis on the contralateral side, although this would have caused equivalent shortening on that side, thus defeating one of the goals of testosterone treatment, height optimization.

At 8 months following removal of implants, the patient was 17 +1 years old and had grown 2.5 cm. There was a 1-mm LLD, long on the right side. There was evidence of growth resumption at the femur with an interbead distance of +1.5 mm at the distal femur and +0.0 mm at the proximal tibia. At 12 months post implant removal, the patient was 17 +5 years and had grown an additional 3.4 cm; the change in interbead distance was an additional +7 mm at the distal femur and +0.5 mm at the proximal tibia. At 27 months post implant removal, the patient was 18 +8 years and had grown an additional 0.7 cm. The physes were radiographically closed (Figure 4). Since the time of screw removal, the distal femur had grown a total of +9.0 mm, and there was +1.0 mm growth at the proximal tibia.

At skeletal maturity, the PETS treated left femur measured 46.3 cm, whereas the untreated right femur measured 46.7 cm. At the tibia, the PETS treated left tibia measured 37.0 cm, whereas the untreated right tibia measured 37.2 cm. At skeletal maturity, there was a leg length difference of 5 mm, long on the right side. The left (treated) leg measured 83.6 cm, and the untreated right leg measured 84.1 cm (Figure 4). Discussion

This report documents a case of PETS reversibility when screws were removed at a time when the physes remained open and future growth was still expected. Reinitiation of growth was most notable at the distal femur, although there was also evidence of slight growth resumption at the proximal tibia. The differential in growth resumption between the tibia and femur may or may not be related to the natural closure of the proximal tibia approximately one year before the distal femur (e.g. by the time PETS screws were removed, the tibia was naturally closer to complete physeal closure).10,11

If PETS were permanent, left distal femoral and proximal tibial growth would have been inhibited even with implant removal, and there would have been no change in inter-bead distance following implant removal. Instead, we observed that the leg treated with PETS, and subsequent implant removal, grew 15 mm (11 mm at the femur of which 9 mm was at the distal femur, and 4 mm at the tibia, of which 1 mm was at the proximal tibia) resulting in final discrepancy of −5 mm (5 mm short on the surgical side). Although growth resumed, velocity differed, was slower, than on the untreated right side.

Removal of PETS screws should be considered in cases of overcorrection or alterations in estimated growth and subsequent discrepancy predictions. Although screw epiphysiodesis results in immediate cessation of physeal growth,2 the physis may or may not fuse prematurely with screws in situ. The potential for growth resumption following screw removal may exist, provided the physis remains open on radiographic examination. The use of tantalum beads as fixed bony landmarks permits early and accurate measurement of physeal growth. A limitation of our study is that bone length and interbead distance measurements were obtained across different imaging modalities. Variations in positioning as well as subtle differences in calibration, may have lead to a small degree of error related to imaging technique (0-3mm error estimated).

This is a single case definitively demonstrating growth resumption post screw removal. If growth resumption is consistently demonstrated in future studies of screw removal, surgeons may more confidently select PETS as an option for young patients requiring temporary epiphysiodesis or in those with unpredictable growth. However, until further evidence is available, we do not recommend the placement of screws or other larger diameter threaded implants across a physis unless complete physeal closure would be an acceptable outcome. Screw removal should be used in cases of overcorrection, with growth resumption possible but not guaranteed.
