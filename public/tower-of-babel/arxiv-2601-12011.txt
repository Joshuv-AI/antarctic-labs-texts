Why Loss Re-weighting Works If You Stop Early: Training Dynamics of Unconstrained Features
Yize Zhao, Christos Thrampoulidis

Abstract
The application of loss reweighting in modern deep learning presents a nuanced picture. While it fails to alter the terminal learning phase in overparameterized deep neural networks (DNNs) trained on high-dimensional datasets, empirical evidence consistently shows it offers significant benefits early in training. To transparently demonstrate and analyze this phenomenon, we introduce a small-scale model (SSM). This model is specifically designed to abstract the inherent complexities of both the DNN architecture and the input data, while maintaining key information about the structure of imbalance within its spectral components. On the one hand, the SSM reveals how vanilla empirical risk minimization preferentially learns to distinguish majority classes over minorities early in training, consequently delaying minority learning. In stark contrast, reweighting restores balanced learning dynamics, enabling the simultaneous learning of features associated with both majorities and minorities.



I
Introduction

Classification of imbalanced datasets is a pervasive challenge in practical machine learning. Most real-world datasets exhibit classes with varying numbers of examples, where classes with significantly fewer training examples are commonly referred to as minorities. A classical and widely adopted approach to learning from imbalanced data is
reweighting
, which modifies the loss function to effectively increase the contribution of minority class examples during training. Concretely, instead of a learning model
f

\mathcal{L}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\ell\left(f(\bm{x}_{i}),y_{i}\right)

(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm{RW}}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i\in[n]}\omega_{y_{i}}\cdot\ell(f(\bm{x}_{i}),y_{i}),

(1)

where
\omega_{y_{i}}\propto 1/\hat{\pi}_{y_{i}}
is typically set proportional to the inverse empirical frequency

However, modern machine learning practice, particularly with large-scale deep neural networks (DNNs) and complex high-dimensional datasets, presents a more nuanced picture regarding the statistical optimality and practical efficacy of

A clear understanding of these early-training benefits of

Why does reweighting help early in training?

How does it modify the learning dynamics to favor minorities?

The inherent complexity of DNNs and of real-world datasets makes it challenging to directly answer these questions.

Our contribution is to identify and analyze a small-scale model (SSM) that transparently demonstrates the exact impact of reweighting on training dynamics, specifically favoring minority classes.

In Section

In Section

With necessary abstractions of the data, the DNN, and the loss function, and appropriate interpretations of its spectral components, our SSM effectively replicates the empirically observed DNN behaviors related to reweighting.
Finally, Section

II
Motivating Experiment

We empirically illustrate the different learning dynamics induced by vanilla empirical risk minimization (ERM) and loss reweighting in an imbalanced classification setting. We construct a 4-class subset of the MNIST dataset with synthetic imbalance: two classes are designated as majorities with 100 training samples each, and the remaining two as minorities with 10 samples each (imbalance ratio

d=32
under both standard cross-entropy (CE) and reweighted CE, using the Adam optimizer with learning rate

III
Small-Scale Model

III-A

Model Description

Our model abstracts the inherent complexities of both the DNN architecture and the input data by using a model of unconstrained features (UFM).
It further combines this with a simplification on the loss from cross-entropy to squared-loss. For concreteness, we focus on a STEP-imbalanced setting.

Architecture/data abstraction.
The UFM substitutes the training embeddings
\bm{h}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}_{i})
, which are typically high-dimensional representations generated by the last activation layer of a DNN parameterized by
\bm{\theta}
, with directly trainable vectors
\bm{h}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This abstraction simultaneously bypasses the network architecture (
\bm{\theta}
) and the input data (
\bm{x}_{i}
). The underlying rationale is that overparameterized DNNs are sufficiently expressive to learn data embeddings that are primarily driven by minimizing the training loss, rather than being strictly constrained by the specific architecture. The UFM has been widely adopted and validated, e.g.,
[
38
,
23
,
6
,
42
,
41
,
7
]
.

Formally, we abstract the DNN as a
bilinear model
where the logits
\bm{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
are defined as
\bm{L}:={\bm{W}}{\bm{H}}
. Here,
{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
represents the network’s classifiers (analogous to the last-layer weights), and
{\bm{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
is a matrix comprising the trainable embeddings
\bm{h}_{i},i\in[n]
for each example. Both
{\bm{W}}
and
{\bm{H}}
are parameters to be optimized during training.

Loss simplification.
Instead of the cross-entropy loss, we employ the squared-loss. This simplification is a common practice in theoretical analyses, as squared-loss generally leads to more interpretable and analytically tractable learning dynamics. Specifically, we use squared-loss with a
simplex-encoded label (SEL) matrix

\bm{Z}:=(\mathds{I}_{k}-\frac{1}{k}\mathds{1}_{k}\mathds{1}_{k}^{\top})\bm{Y}
.
This matrix is a centered version of the one-hot encoding matrix
\bm{Y}\in\{0,1\}^{k\times n}
of the data. The centering accounts for the property of cross-entropy to yield centered logits, i.e., such that
\mathbf{1}_{k}^{\top}\bm{L}=\mathbf{0}

[
32
]
. Viewed this way, the simplification to squared-loss can alternatively be considered a first-order approximation of the cross-entropy loss.

Overparameterization.
To model overparameterization, within our bilinear model, we set hidden dimension
d\geq k
. This ensures model parameters exist that can perfectly interpolate the labels, meaning
\bm{L}=\bm{Z}
.

STEP-Imbalance.
For concreteness, we assume
k
classes, where the first
k/2
are
majorities
and the remaining
k/2
are
minorities
. Within each group, all classes have an equal number of examples. The number of samples in majorities is
R
times the number of samples in minorities, where
R
is the
imbalance ratio
. For simplicity and without compromising the generality of our findings, we set
k=4
for our detailed discussion. See Fig.
2
(a) the 1-hot label matrix
\bm{Y}
.

Remark III.1
.

We view this model as the minimal canonical model capable of transparently justifying how reweighting balances feature learning rates during training. It is minimal because it essentially describes the dynamics of a two-layer linear neural network with standard basis inputs. The model is canonical in that it avoids imposing explicit assumptions on the geometry of the abstracted input data—unlike a simpler linear model would necessitate, e.g., as in
[
26
,
17
,
30
,
18
,
25
,
2
]
. Instead, its abstraction is based on assuming an overparameterized and sufficiently expressive architecture.

A key Spectral Interpretation

The SSM isolates the encoding of the imbalanced data structure within the spectral components of the SEL matrix,
\bm{Z}
, in a way that directly reveals how the model prioritizes learning about majorities over minorities.
Let
\bm{Z}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}{\bm{V}}^{\top}
be the SVD of the SEL matrix. It can be formally shown that
\bm{\Sigma}
has
k-1
non-zero singular values, which are categorized into
three distinct levels
such that the largest and smallest values each have a multiplicity of
k/2-1
, while the middle value has a multiplicity of one. These three distinct levels correspond to three groups of principal components each associated with features that the model must separately learn to distinguish: (i) majority classes from each other (
maj-maj
feature), (ii) majority classes from minority classes (
maj-min
feature), and (iii) minority classes from each other (
min-min
feature). These features are directly encoded as singular vectors of
\bm{Z}
.

For illustration, consider the case
k=4
. Observe in Fig.
2
(b) the left singular matrix
\bm{U}
, where each column represents one of these features. We can interpret the sign of each of the
k
entries in a singular vector
\bm{u}_{j}
as an indicator of how strongly the respective class is associated with that principal component: if
\bm{u}_{j}[c]>0
or
\bm{u}_{j}[c]<0
, it signifies that class c aligns with or opposes this component, respectively; if
\bm{u}_{j}[c]=0
, the class is neutral to that component.
We thus find
three principal components, corresponding to distinct features, ordered by the magnitude of their respective singular values:

1.

maj-maj
: The first principal component (
j=1
) is neutral to minorities and distinguishes between the two majorities (indicated by the opposing signs of
\bm{u}_{1}[1]
and
\bm{u}_{1}[2]
).

2.

maj-min
: The second principal component (
j=2
) distinguishes between majorities and the minorities (indicated by the opposing signs of
\bm{u}_{2}[1],\bm{u}_{2}[2]
and
\bm{u}_{2}[3],\bm{u}_{2}[4]
).

3.

min-min
: The last principal component (
j=3
) is neutral to majorities and distinguishes between the two minorities (indicated by the opposing signs of
\bm{u}_{3}[3]
and
\bm{u}_{3}[4]
).

III-C

Reweighting Changes the Learning Dynamics Eliminating Preferential Learning Against Minorities

We now leverage our SSM to illuminate how reweighting fundamentally alters the learning dynamics. Specifically, we compare the training dynamics under gradient descent for vanilla ERM versus loss reweighting:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\bm{W}},{\bm{H}})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i\in[n]}\|\bm{z}_{i}-{\bm{W}}\bm{h}_{i}\|^{2},\textit{versus}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm{RW}}({\bm{W}},{\bm{H}})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i\in[n]}\omega_{y_{i}}\,\|\bm{z}_{i}-{\bm{W}}\bm{h}_{i}\|^{2}.

\omega_{y_{i}}
are set inversely proportional to the square root of the frequency of the respective class
y_{i}\in[k]
. Concretely, if the class
y_{i}
of the
i
-th example is a majority, then
\omega_{y_{i}}\propto\sqrt{(R+1)k/(2R)}
, otherwise, for a minority class,
\omega_{y_{i}}\propto\sqrt{(R+1)k/2}
.

2.

Preferential learning in vanilla ERM hurts minorities:
With vanilla ERM (

3.

Reweighting restores balanced Learning:
In contrast, loss reweighting (

Recalling that singular values directly correspond to distinct features, our SSM thus captures the

While vanilla ERM

learns all features at the same rate, thus enabling accurate classification of minorities much earlier.
Note the direct one-to-one correspondence of these behaviors to what we observed empirically in the DNN experiment presented in Section

IV
Analysis

We now derive the gradient flow dynamics of the Small-Scale Model (SSM) to theoretically ground the observations in Section
III
.
Our analysis proceeds in three steps: (A) We identify a connection between the square-loss UFM and the framework of
27
, establishing closed-form dynamics for vanilla ERM (
\mathcal{L}
) under spectral initialization; (B) Leveraging closed-form expressions for the spectral factors of the SEL matrix, we derive analogous closed-form dynamics for reweighted ERM (
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{RW}}
) in Theorem
IV.1
, proving that reweighting effectively flattens the spectrum of the label matrix; (C) Finally, we contrast the relative rates of learning of features, demonstrating that reweighting compresses the effective learning window independent of the imbalance ratio.

IV-A

Dynamics of Vanilla ERM

Consider the minimization of the UFM with squared loss
\mathcal{L}({\bm{W}},{\bm{H}})=\frac{1}{2}\|(\bm{Z}-{\bm{W}}{\bm{H}})\|^{2}

This objective fits logits
\bm{L}={\bm{W}}{\bm{H}}
to the centered sparsity matrix
\bm{Z}
and is mathematically equivalent to training a two-layer linear network with orthogonal inputs. We can therefore adapt the solutions provided by
27
and
8
.

Recall
\bm{Z}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}{\bm{V}}^{\top}
is the SVD of the SEL matrix. Assume spectral initialization where
{\bm{W}}(0)=e^{-\delta}\bm{U}\mathbf{R}^{\top}
and
{\bm{H}}(0)=e^{-\delta}\mathbf{R}{\bm{V}}^{\top}
for a small scale
e^{-\delta}
and arbitrary rotation
\mathbf{R}
. As shown in Proposition (
IX.1
) (see Appendix
IX
), the gradient flow dynamics decouple along the singular directions of
\bm{Z}
. Specifically, the parameters evolve as:

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}(t)=\bm{U}\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\sqrt{{\bm{A}}(t)}\mathbf{R}^{\top},\quad{\bm{H}}(t)=\mathbf{R}\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\sqrt{{\bm{A}}(t)}{\bm{V}}^{\top},

(2)

where
{\bm{A}}(t)=\text{diag}(a_{1}(t),\dots,a_{r}(t))
describes the scalar evolution of each mode. The factor
a_{i}(t)
follows a sigmoid trajectory depending on the singular value
\sigma_{i}
:

\displaystyle a_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{1+(\sigma_{i}e^{2\delta}-1)e^{-2\sigma_{i}t}}.

(3)

Eq. (
3
) implies that the time
T_{i}
required to learn the
i
-th feature (i.e., for
a_{i}(t)\to 1
) is inversely proportional to its singular value:
T_{i}\propto{1}/{\sigma_{i}}
.
In the STEP-imbalanced setting, the spectrum
\bm{\Sigma}
is skewed. Majority-associated singular values are roughly
\sqrt{R}
times larger than minority ones. Consequently, vanilla ERM learns majority features at time
T_{\text{maj}}\propto 1/\sqrt{R}
and minority features at
T_{\text{min}}\propto 1
. The gap between these times increases with the imbalance ratio
R
, explaining the staged learning observed in Fig.
1
(bottom).

IV-B

Dynamics of Reweighted ERM

We now analyze the dynamics under the reweighted objective. Consider the weighted square-loss function
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{RW}}({\bm{W}},{\bm{H}})
with class-dependent weights
\omega_{i}
. In matrix form,
\mathcal{L}_{\rm{RW}}({\bm{W}},{\bm{H}})=\frac{1}{2}\|(\bm{Z}-{\bm{W}}{\bm{H}})\bm{\Omega}^{1/2}\|_{F}^{2}

\bm{\Omega}=\text{diag}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})
.

In the STEP-imbalanced setting with
R
imbalance ratio, without loss of generality, we assume the first
k/2

n=Rk/2+k/2=(R+1)k/2
. the right singular vectors
{\bm{V}}

\displaystyle{\bm{V}}^{\top}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\sqrt{\frac{1}{R}}\mathbb{F}^{\top}\otimes\mathds{1}_{R}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\\

\mathbf{0}&\mathbb{F}^{\top}\end{array}\right],

where
\mathbb{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2\times(k/2-1)}
is an orthonormal basis for the subspace orthogonal to
\mathds{1}_{k/2}

[
32
]

We set the weights inversely proportional to the square root of the respective class frequency. That is, the weight for majority samples is
\sqrt{n/R}=\sqrt{(R+1)k/(2R)}

\sqrt{R}
times larger. Dropping the constant factor

for simplicity, we choose:

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}:=\sqrt{R+1}\cdot\operatorname{diag}(\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\frac{1}{R}}\,\mathds{1}_{Rk/2}^{\top}&\mathds{1}_{k/2}^{\top}\end{bmatrix})\,.

IV-C

Implications for Learning Times

The key implication of Theorem
IV.1
is that reweighting modifies the learning rate for each feature
i
by a factor of
\lambda_{i}
. A critical metric for comparison is the
effective learning window

\Delta T
, defined as the relative time difference between learning the first and last principal components:

\displaystyle\Delta T:=\frac{T_{\max}-T_{\min}}{T_{\min}}.

(15)

For vanishing initialization, we find that under vanilla ERM, the window scales with the imbalance ratio
R
(see Cor. (
IX.3
)):

\displaystyle\Delta T_{\rm{vanilla}}=\sqrt{R}-1.

(16)

This value grows unboundedly with
R
, explaining the severe delay in minority learning. In contrast, for reweighted ERM, the effective weights
\lambda_{i}
counteract the spectral decay. Specifically, the singular values of the effective sparsity matrix become equal. The window becomes:

\displaystyle\Delta T_{\rm{RW}}=\sqrt{2}\frac{\sqrt{R+1}}{\sqrt{R}+1}-1.

(17)

This value is upper bounded by
\sqrt{2}-1
irrespective of the imbalance
R
, confirming that reweighting effectively compresses the learning timeline and enables simultaneous feature acquisition. See Appendix
IX
for full analysis and further experimental validation.

Our model already rather effectively captures the early-training impacts of reweighting under overparameterization. In future work, we aim to address limitations by extending the analysis beyond squared loss and also to test-time dynamics.

VII
Background on Loss Reweighting

VII-A

Statistical Optimality in the Classical Regime

Loss reweighting is theoretically grounded, being statistically optimal in the population
limit for optimizing balanced accuracy, which weighs class-conditional
accuracies equally rather than by class frequencies.
Concretely, let
\pi_{c}
be the prior probability of class

c\in[k]
in a
k
-class classification dataset. The standard
error of a hypothesis
h
is

\mathcal{R}(h):=\sum_{c\in[k]}\pi_{c}\mathcal{R}_{c}(h)=\mathbb{E}_{c\sim P(c),\bm{x}\sim P(\bm{x}|c)}[\mathbf{1}[h(\bm{x})\neq c]],

where
\mathcal{R}_{c}(h)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim P(\bm{x}|c)}[\mathbf{1}[h(\bm{x})\neq c]]
is the class-conditional error.
In practice, given
n
samples
(\bm{x}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}

with labels
y_{i}\in[k]
, we minimize the empirical risk

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell\left(h(\bm{x}_{i}),y_{i}\right),

(18)

where
\ell
denotes a differentiable proxy to the zero-one loss.
The balanced error is
\mathcal{R}_{\text{bal}}(h):=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{c\in[k]}\mathcal{R}_{c}(h)
, with empirical counterpart

\mathcal{L}_{\text{bal}}(h)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{c\in[k]}\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i:y_{i}=c}\ell\left(h(\bm{x}_{i}),c\right)
,
where
n_{c}=|\{i:y_{i}=c\}|
. This can be rewritten as a reweighted loss:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{reweight}}(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\cdot\ell(h(\bm{x}_{i}),y_{i}),

(19)

where
\omega_{i}=1/(n_{y_{i}}/n)
is the inverse empirical frequency of each class.
Under mild assumptions, this re-weighted loss is statistically optimal when

n\rightarrow\infty
and the hypothesis space is bounded.

VII-B

Nuanced Picture in the Overparameterization Regime

However, modern machine learning challenges the (classical) population analysis:
neural networks operate in high-dimensional regimes where the hypothesis-space
dimension exceeds the training sample count. In such overparameterized settings,
classical methods often lose their optimality. Indeed, several works

[
3
,
15
]
have identified weighted cross-entropy’s
failure to substantially improve balanced accuracy of well-trained overparameterized
deep neural networks on imbalanced datasets.

Investigating this behavior theoretically,
26
and
16
attribute this failure to the implicit optimization bias of gradient-based optimizers used to minimize the loss during training. A long line of research
[
9
,
10
,
29
,
14
,
21
]
has supported the idea that empirical risk minimization of overparameterized networks—where many minimizers of the loss can exist—is empirically successful precisely because first-order gradient optimizers are biased toward solutions that maximize the margin over the training data among all train loss minimizers. Applied to weighted cross-entropy,
26
and
16
leverage this type of analysis to demonstrate that loss reweighting has no effect on the implicit optimization bias: irrespective of the choice of weights, when trained long enough, the configuration chosen by gradient-based optimization applied to weighted cross-entropy is the same as vanilla cross-entropy.

16
identified that in this overparameterized regime reweighting still works, but should be done differently than suggested by Eq. (
19
) and should instead be applied to the logits. See also related
[
4
,
22
,
37
,
36
]
and follow-up works e.g.,
[
2
,
1
,
34
,
18
,
25
,
35
,
40
]
.

Despite being very insightful and leading to practical modifications, there are two limitations in the above implicit bias analysis: First, the implicit optimization bias analysis provides explicit characterizations of the max-margin classifiers to which optimizers converge only in limited settings (even for two-layer linear networks, it has not been rigorously shown whether gradient descent converges to the global minimizer of the non-convex max-margin optimization). Second, and perhaps more importantly, the implicit bias toward a max-margin classifier manifests very late in training: even in linear models, convergence to the max-margin solution with gradient descent is exponentially slow (convergence can be accelerated with normalization, but still requires many iterations).

On the other hand, it has been empirically observed that loss reweighting can actually be beneficial when either used with early stopping or as a complementary technique to modern loss variants optimized for the overparameterized regime, leading to empirical speed-ups and optimization boosts in early training iterations
[
4
,
37
,
19
]
.

IX-A

Background on Vanilla ERM

Consider the square-loss UFM:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{W}},{\bm{H}}}~~\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\bm{z}_{i}-{\bm{W}}\bm{h}_{i}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{Z}-{\bm{W}}{\bm{H}}\|^{2}\right\}\,.

(20)

which fits logits
{\bm{W}}{\bm{H}}
to the centered sparsity matrix
\bm{Z}
. Although CE loss is more common in practice, we note that square-loss has shown competitive performance to CE minimization in various settings
[
13
,
5
]
.
The neural-collapse literature has extensively studied Eq. (
20
) primarily in the balanced case (e.g.,
[
23
,
11
,
31
,
33
]
) but recently also for imbalanced data (e.g.,
[
20
,
12
]
). Most works focus on global minima of regularized UFM, with less attention to unregularized cases or training dynamics. While some landscape analyses provide partial answers about global convergence
24
,
11
, they are limited to regularized cases and do
not
characterize dynamics. For example,
[
11
]
’s analysis of the ’central path’ in balanced one-hot cases—these results relies on approximations. Thus, a significant gap remains in understanding UFM training dynamics, even for simple balanced one-hot data with square loss.

By interpreting the UFM with square loss in Eq. (
20
) as a two-layer linear network with orthogonal inputs, we identify a connection to
27
,
8
’s analysis, that to the best of our knowledge has thus far remained unexplored in the neural-collapse literature.
27
provide explicit characterization of gradient descent dynamics (with small initialization) for square-loss UFM. The key insight in adopting their results, is rewriting (
20
) as
\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\bm{z}_{i}-{\bm{W}}{\bm{H}}\bm{e}_{i}\|^{2}
with
orthogonal
inputs
\bm{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. This enables direct application of their result, originally stated in
[
27
]
and formalized in
[
8
]
. For completeness, we state this here in our setting and terminology as a proposition below.

Proposition IX.1

(
[
27
,
8
]
)
.

Consider gradient flow (GF) dynamics for minimizing the square-loss UFM (
20
). Recall the SVD
\bm{Z}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}{\bm{V}}^{\top}
. Assume weight initialization

{\bm{W}}(0)=e^{-\delta}\bm{U}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\quad\text{and}\quad{\bm{H}}(0)=e^{-\delta}\mathbf{R}{\bm{V}}^{\top}

for some partial orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
(
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathds{I}_{r}
) and initialization scale
e^{-\delta}
. Then the iterates
{\bm{W}}(t),{\bm{H}}(t)
of GF are as follows:

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}(t)=\bm{U}\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\sqrt{{\bm{A}}(t)}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\bm{H}}(t)=\mathbf{R}\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\sqrt{{\bm{A}}(t)}{\bm{V}}^{\top}

(21)

for
{\bm{A}}(t)=\operatorname{diag}(a_{1}(t),\ldots,a_{r}(t))
with

\displaystyle a_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{1+(\sigma_{i}e^{2\delta}-1)e^{-2\sigma_{i}t}},~i\in[r].

(22)

Moreover, the time-rescaled factors
a_{i}(\delta t)
converge to a step function as
\delta\rightarrow\infty
(limit of vanishing initialization):

\displaystyle a_{i}(\delta t)\rightarrow\frac{1}{1+\sigma_{i}}\mathds{1}[{t=T_{i}}]+\mathds{1}[{t>T_{i}}],

(23)

where
T_{i}=1/\sigma_{i}
and
\mathds{1}[{A}]
is the indicator function for event
A
. Thus, the
i
-th component is learned at time
T_{i}
inversely proportional to
\sigma_{i}
.

Proof.

After having set up the analogy of our setting to that of
[
27
,
8
]
, this is a direct application of
[
8
, Thm. 1]
. Specifically, this is made possible in our setting by: (i) interpreting the UFM with square-loss in (
20
) as a two-layer linear network (ii) recognizing that the covariance of the inputs (which here are standard basis vectors
\bm{e}_{j},j\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
) is the identity matrix, hence the (almost) orthogonality assumption (see
[
27
]
and
[
8
, Sec. 4.1]
) holds.
∎

This result requires initializing
{\bm{W}},{\bm{H}}
in a way that makes them aligned with the SVD factors of the SEL matrix. While this might appear as a strong assumption,
[
27
,
28
]
conjectured and verified experimentally that the characterization remains qualitatively accurate under small random initialization.
Our experiments with the SEL matrix confirm this - Fig.
5
(middle row) shows the singular values of the logit matrix during training closely follow the predicted exponential trend in Eq. (
37
). This reveals that dominant singular factors, corresponding to primary semantic concepts, are learned first. In the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
, the theorem shows convergence to:

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}(t)\rightarrow{\bm{W}}_{\infty}:=\bm{U}\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\bm{H}}(t)\rightarrow{\bm{H}}_{\infty}:=\mathbf{R}\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{V}}^{\top},.

(24)

This aligns with
[
32
]
’s regularization-path analysis of UFM with CE loss, where normalized quantities converge as regularization goes to zero.

IX-B

Controlling the rate of learning via Reweighting

Consider minimizing the following weighted version of (
20
):

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{W}},{\bm{H}}}~~\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[n]}\omega_{i}\|\bm{z}_{i}-{\bm{W}}\bm{h}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\left(\bm{Z}-{\bm{W}}{\bm{H}}\right)\bm{\Omega}^{1/2}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right\}\,,

(25)

where
\bm{\Omega}=\operatorname{diag}([\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m}])
is a diagonal matrix of weights, one for each context. We consider the STEP-imbalanced one-hot classification setting described. Concretely, let the one-hot label matrix
be

\displaystyle\bm{Y}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathds{I}_{k/2}\otimes\mathds{1}_{R}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}_{k/2\times k/2}\\
\mathbf{0}_{k/2\times Rk/2}&\mathds{I}_{k/2}\end{bmatrix}

(26)

where
R
is the imbalance ratio and without loss of generality we assumed that the first
k/2
classes are majorities and that minorities have
1
example each. Here, the choice of
1
sample per minority is done without loss of generality and just to maintain simplicity in the formulas. Thus, the total number of examples is
n=Rk/2+k/2=(R+1)k/2
. Recall that
\bm{Z}=(\mathds{I}_{k}-\frac{1}{k}\mathds{1}_{k}\mathds{1}_{k}^{\top})\bm{Y}
.

Recall that
\bm{Z}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}{\bm{V}}^{\top}
.
32
derives the spectral components in closed form as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}=\operatorname{diag}(\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{R}\mathds{1}_{k/2-1}&\sqrt{(R+1)/2}&\mathds{1}_{k/2-1}\end{bmatrix})\,

(27)

\displaystyle\bm{U}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{F}&-\sqrt{\frac{1}{k}}\mathds{1}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\sqrt{\frac{1}{k}}\mathds{1}&\mathbb{F}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times(k-1)}\,,

and

{\bm{V}}^{\top}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\sqrt{\frac{1}{R}}\mathbb{F}^{\top}\otimes\mathds{1}_{R}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\\
-\sqrt{\frac{2}{(R+1)k}}\mathds{1}_{Rk/2}^{\top}&\sqrt{\frac{2}{(R+1)k}}\mathds{1}_{k/2}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbb{F}^{\top}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{(k-1)\times m}\,.

Above,
\mathbb{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2\times(k/2-1)}
is an orthonormal basis of the subspace orthogonal to
\mathds{1}_{k/2}
.

We now set the weights inversely proportional to the square-root of the respective class frequency, i.e. the weight for the first
Rk/2
majority samples is
\sqrt{n/R}=\sqrt{(R+1)k/(2R)}
, while for the minorities is
\sqrt{R}
times larger, i.e.,
\sqrt{n}=\sqrt{(R+1)k/2}
. The relative rates of learning of majority/minority features do not change if we uniformly scale all weights by a constant. Thus, for simplicity and without affecting our analysis, we drop the factor
\sqrt{k/2}
, and, in matrix form, choose

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}:=\sqrt{R+1}\cdot\operatorname{diag}(\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\frac{1}{R}}\,\mathds{1}_{Rk/2}^{\top}&\mathds{1}_{k/2}^{\top}\end{bmatrix})\,.

(30)

Direct calculation yields the following:

\displaystyle{\bm{V}}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}{\bm{V}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{R+1}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\frac{1}{R}}\mathbb{F}^{\top}\otimes\mathds{1}_{R}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\\
-\sqrt{\frac{2}{R(R+1)k}}\mathds{1}_{Rk/2}^{\top}&\sqrt{\frac{2}{(R+1)k}}\mathds{1}_{k/2}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbb{F}^{\top}\end{array}\right]\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\frac{1}{R}}\mathbb{F}\otimes\mathds{1}_{R}&-\sqrt{\frac{2}{(R+1)k}}\mathds{1}_{Rk/2}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\sqrt{\frac{2}{(R+1)k}}\mathds{1}_{k/2}&\mathbb{F}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\frac{R+1}{R}}\,\mathds{I}_{k/2-1}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\frac{\sqrt{R}+1}{\sqrt{R+1}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\sqrt{R+1}\,\mathds{I}_{k/2-1}\end{bmatrix}=:\bm{\Lambda}.

(34)

where in the last equation, we defined the diagonal matrix
\bm{\Lambda}
with entries:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}:=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{R+1}{R}}&i\in[k/2-1]\\
\frac{\sqrt{R}+1}{\sqrt{R+1}}&i=k/2\\
\sqrt{R+1}&i=k/2+1,\ldots,k-1\end{cases}

(35)

This calculation is handy in the proof of the following theorem which is our main theoretical result. The proof shows that
\bm{\Lambda}
is the effective weight matrix applied to the spectrum of the label matrix.

Theorem IX.2
.

Consider gradient flow (GF) dynamics for minimizing the
weighted
square-loss UFM (
25
) with weight matrix as shown in Eq. (
30
) in an
R
-STEP-imbalanced setting. Assume same parameter initialization as in Proposition
IX.1
, i.e.,

{\bm{W}}(0)=e^{-\delta}\bm{U}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\,,\,{\bm{H}}(0)=e^{-\delta}\mathbf{R}{\bm{V}}^{\top}
, for partial orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(k-1)}
, initialization scale
e^{-\delta}
and SVD
\bm{Z}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}{\bm{V}}^{\top}
. Finally, consider the
effective weights

\lambda_{i},i\in[k-1]
defined in Eq. (
35
). Then the iterates
{\bm{W}}(t),{\bm{H}}(t)
of GF evolve as follows:

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}(t)=\bm{U}\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\sqrt{\bm{B}(t)}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\bm{H}}(t)=\mathbf{R}\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}}\sqrt{\bm{B}(t)}{\bm{V}}^{\top}

(36)

for
\bm{B}(t)=\operatorname{diag}(\beta_{1}(t),\ldots,\beta_{k-1}(t))
with

\displaystyle\beta_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{1+(\sigma_{i}e^{2\delta}-1)e^{-2\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}t}},~i\in[k-1].

(37)

Moreover, the time-rescaled factors
\beta_{i}(\delta t)
converge to a step function as
\delta\rightarrow\infty
:

\displaystyle\beta_{i}(\delta t)\rightarrow\frac{1}{1+\sigma_{i}}\mathds{1}[{t=T_{i}}]+\mathds{1}[{t>T_{i}}],

(38)

where
T_{i}=1/(\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i})
. Thus, the
i
-th component is learned at time inversely proportional to
\lambda_{i}\cdot\sigma_{i}
.

Proof.

The GF updates are given by:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{{\bm{W}}(t)}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\left(\bm{Z}\bm{\Omega}^{1/2}-{\bm{W}}(t){\bm{H}}(t)\bm{\Omega}^{1/2}\right)\bm{\Omega}^{1/2}{\bm{H}}(t)^{\top}=-\left(\bm{Z}-{\bm{W}}(t){\bm{H}}(t)\right)\bm{\Omega}{\bm{H}}(t)^{\top}

(39a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{{\bm{H}}(t)}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-{\bm{W}}(t)^{\top}\left(\bm{Z}\bm{\Omega}^{1/2}-{\bm{W}}(t){\bm{H}}(t)\bm{\Omega}^{1/2}\right)\bm{\Omega}^{1/2}=-{\bm{W}}(t)^{\top}\left(\bm{Z}-{\bm{W}}(t){\bm{H}}(t)\right)\bm{\Omega}

(39b)

where
{\bm{W}}(0)=e^{-\delta}\bm{U}\mathbf{R}^{\top}
and
{\bm{H}}(0)=e^{-\delta}\mathbf{R}{\bm{V}}^{\top}
. As in
[
27
,
28
]
change variables to
\overline{{\bm{W}}}(t),\overline{{\bm{H}}}(t)
defined as

{\bm{W}}(t)=\bm{U}\overline{{\bm{W}}}(t)\mathbf{R}^{\top},\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\bm{H}}(t)=\mathbf{R}\overline{{\bm{H}}}(t){\bm{V}}^{\top}\,.

At
t=0
, these matrices are diagonal and equal to
e^{-\delta}\mathds{I}
by initialization assumption. Substituting these in the update equations (
39
) and also using
\bm{Z}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}{\bm{V}}^{\top}
, we get that

\displaystyle\bm{U}\frac{\mathrm{d}{\overline{{\bm{W}}}(t)}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{R}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\bm{U}\left(\bm{\Sigma}-\overline{{\bm{W}}}(t)\overline{{\bm{H}}}(t)\right){\bm{V}}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}{\bm{V}}\overline{{\bm{H}}}(t)^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}

(40a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\frac{\mathrm{d}{\overline{{\bm{H}}}(t)}}{\mathrm{d}t}{\bm{V}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}\overline{{\bm{W}}}(t)^{\top}\left(\bm{\Sigma}-\overline{{\bm{W}}}(t)\overline{{\bm{H}}}(t)\right){\bm{V}}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}\,.

(40b)

Left and Right multiplying these with the partial unitary matrices
\bm{U},\mathbf{R},{\bm{V}}
and recalling from (
34
) that
{\bm{V}}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}{\bm{V}}=\bm{\Lambda}
(given in (
35
)), we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{\overline{{\bm{W}}}(t)}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\left(\bm{\Sigma}-\overline{{\bm{W}}}(t)\overline{{\bm{H}}}(t)\right)\bm{\Lambda}\overline{{\bm{H}}}(t)^{\top}

(41a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{\overline{{\bm{H}}}(t)}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\overline{{\bm{W}}}(t)^{\top}\left(\bm{\Sigma}-\overline{{\bm{W}}}(t)\overline{{\bm{H}}}(t)\right)\bm{\Lambda}\,.

(41b)

At this point, recall that
\bm{\Lambda}
is diagonal, and of course so is
\bm{\Sigma}
. Since
\overline{{\bm{W}}},\overline{{\bm{H}}}
are initialized to be diagonal matrices, they will remain diagonal throughout training. We thus arrive at a decoupled system of differential equations as in the unweighted case treated by
[
27
,
8
]
.

In fact, the update equations are same as in the unweighted case (compare to
[
8
, Eq. 39]
)
with the only difference being the presence of the
effective weight matrix

\bm{\Lambda}
. Having reached this point, we can follow almost the exact same steps as in the proof of
[
8
, Thm. 1]
. For brevity and because the adjustments are easy to check we directly present the formula for the diagonal entries
\overline{w}_{i}(t),\overline{h}_{i}(t)
of
\overline{{\bm{W}}}(t),\overline{{\bm{H}}}(t)
that solve (
41
) (see
[
8
, Eq. 48]
). For
i\in[k-1]
:

\displaystyle\overline{w}_{i}(t)=\overline{h}_{i}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{i}e^{2\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}t}}{\sigma_{i}e^{2\delta}-1+e^{2\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}t}}}=\sqrt{\sigma_{i}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{1+(\sigma_{i}e^{2\delta}-1)e^{-2\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}t}}}=:\sqrt{\sigma_{i}}\sqrt{\beta_{i}(t)}\,,

(42)

where we defined

\beta_{i}(t):=\frac{1}{1+(\sigma_{i}e^{2\delta}-1)e^{-2\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}t}}\,,i\in[k-1]

Note the similarity to the unweighted case (Eq. (

\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}

alone.

From this, we can also easily determine the limit of

as

(see

\displaystyle\beta_{i}(\delta t):=\frac{e^{2\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}\delta t}}{e^{2\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}\delta t}+(\sigma_{i}e^{2\delta}-1)}=\frac{e^{2\delta(\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}t-1)}}{e^{2\delta(\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}t-1)}+(\sigma_{i}-e^{-2\delta})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{1}{1+\sigma_{i}}\mathds{1}[{t=\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}}}]+\mathds{1}[{t>\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}\lambda_{i}}}]\,.

(43)

∎
