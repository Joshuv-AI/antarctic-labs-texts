Correlating ultrasonic communication with behavior in a small-scale rat colony living in a naturalistic habitat
Authors: Jordan E. Saltzman, Kate Lawson, Stephen V. Mahler, Ron D. Frostig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTraditional studies of rat communication have focused on a few individuals in simplified laboratory settings, leaving colony-level vocal-behavioral dynamics largely unexplored. Here, we examined the nocturnal lives of a rat colony housed in an enhanced naturalistic habitat (ENH). Using infrared cameras and ultrasound microphones, we recorded 18,412 ultrasonic vocalizations (USVs) alongside social behaviors. Analyses revealed structurally distinct USVs not previously characterized statistically or behaviorally. Notably, 22 kHz calls—typically associated with aversion—occurred across multiple social behaviors lacking obvious aversive context. Brief exposure to cat hair elicited uniquely modified 22 kHz calls as rats fled to their burrows, remaining hidden for two days. The introduction of an unfamiliar caged rat, and the disruption of the colony’s social structure then showed how social stressors alter vocal-behavioral dynamics. These findings reveal the structured and context-sensitive nature of rat vocal communication, establishing the ENH as a powerful model for studying social-vocal behavior.

Introduction

Social neuroscience is a growing field built on the recognition that social interactions profoundly shape brain function and behavior.1,2,3,4,5 Rats are well-suited for studies in this domain, given their rich social repertoire6: within colonies, individuals groom one another, share food, huddle for warmth, warn others of predators, and cooperatively excavate burrows.7,8 Communication is central to coordinating these complex behaviors.

Rats communicate through visual, somatosensory, olfactory, and acoustic cues. However, their poor eyesight and nocturnal habits make olfactory, somatosensory, and vocal signaling their primary communication modes. Possessing a rich vocal repertoire, rats combine and modify their calls to convey context-relevant information.9,10,11,12,13 Given the complexity and social relevance of their communication system, studying rat behavior and vocalizations at the colony level under naturalistic conditions is critical for advancing the aims of social neuroscience.

Adult rats communicate through sonic and ultrasonic vocalizations (USVs). USVs are above 20 kHz and are broadly categorized into 50 kHz and 22 kHz calls.14 50 kHz USVs, spanning 32–96 kHz, are short (10–180 ms), broadband (∼10 kHz), and linked to affiliative states such as play, exploration, and reward anticipation.15,16,17,18 In contrast, 22 kHz calls are longer (300–4000 ms), lower in frequency (20–30 kHz), and narrowband (<4 kHz),14,19,20 typically associated with aversive contexts like predator threat,21,22,23 aggression, and pain, as well as the male post-ejaculatory period.11,20,24,25,26 These patterns underscore the behavioral relevance of USV communication.

Most research on rat vocalizations has been conducted in highly controlled laboratory settings, often using dyadic interactions. While such studies offer fine-grained insights, they likely provide only a partial view of rats’ social and vocal behaviors, thus reducing their ecological validity.27,28,29,30 To address this gap, we employed an enhanced naturalistic habitat (ENH; Figure 1) to investigate USVs (see Figure S1 for example spectrograms) and their associated behaviors at the colony level.Figure 1The enhanced naturalistic habitat (ENH)(A) Rats in the enhanced naturalistic habitat.(B) Ultrasound microphones are suspended from the ceiling in a triangular configuration to record rat vocalizations. Prior work in our lab has shown that both brief31 and prolonged32 exposure to naturalistic environments led to functional and anatomical plasticity that contrast sharply with effects observed in standard laboratory housing. Building on this, we developed the ENH to study social-communicative behaviors under more ethologically relevant conditions. The ENH bridges field and laboratory approaches: rats dig burrows, explore freely, socialize, huddle, and self-regulate sleep and feeding, while their behavior and USVs are recorded via infrared cameras and ultrasound microphones. Additionally, the ENH supports controlled experimental manipulations to examine how vocal communication adapts to changes in the physical and social environment (see Figure S2 for an overview of the experimental timeline). By modeling key aspects of wild rat ecology, it reveals communicative behaviors not readily observed in conventional lab settings.

To understand these communicative behaviors, we adopt a “colony-level” analytical approach: correlating the colony’s vocal output with the concurrent behaviors captured through video recording. The ENH is well-suited to this group-level analysis because multiple rats often engaged in overlapping activities—for example, several rats simultaneously exploring and interacting. Such concurrent and partially shared behavioral states are common in animal groups more broadly.33,34 A group-level framework leverages this natural overlap in behavioral states and yields meaningful insights without requiring individual tracking or call localization—methods that remain technically challenging in the ENH.

Our findings demonstrate that behaviorally anchored, colony-level studies of USV communication provide powerful tools for social neuroscience and open new avenues for understanding the behavioral and neural foundations of social life.

Results

Behavior and vocalization trends

What vocal patterns emerge across behaviors in a naturalistic rat colony? To address this question, we compiled vocal-behavioral data across all sessions and adopted a colony-level framework. Analyses were restricted to periods when vocalizations were present and most rats (≥60%) were visible, as well as during periods when the colony retreated into burrows and was not visible (Table S2). Because multiple behaviors often occurred concurrently—for example, some rats exploring while others interacting—vocalizations were assigned to all overlapping behaviors. Our initial analysis examined the relative usage of call types across behaviors, expressed as percent call emission. To capture the full behavioral repertoire, we included both baseline sessions and experimental manipulations, noting which behaviors were absent during baseline but emerged following manipulation. Across four baseline sessions, the eight rats vocalized at a rate of 9.9 calls/min (SEM = 0.10). Additionally, we used kernel density estimates (KDEs) to characterize the distribution of USV principal frequencies (PFs) across behaviors. While several acoustic features varied across behaviors (Figures S5-S14), PF was the most discriminative parameter. KDEs showed that behaviors divide into two clusters: social and exploratory behaviors had bimodal PF distributions (20–30 kHz and 50–60 kHz peaks; “bimodal cluster”, Figures 2A–2C). By contrast, non-social, rearing, stationary, and threat-related behaviors showed unimodal PF distributions with a strong peak below 30 kHz and comparatively few calls above this range. Because most of the probability density—as estimated by the KDE—lies under 30 kHz, we refer to this group as the “sub-30 kHz cluster” (Figures 2D–2F). Thus, ENH rats emitted 22 kHz USVs across all behaviors, but 50–60 kHz USVs were restricted to specific states. In the following sections, we treat these clusters separately to examine within-group variation.

PF distributions: Bimodal cluster

We aimed to assess vocal patterns during social and exploratory states. Behaviors in this cluster exhibited bimodal PF distributions with peaks at 20–30 kHz and 50–60 kHz, separated by a trough near 40 kHz (Figure 2B). This pattern indicates that rats consistently produce both low- and high-frequency calls across varied states. We then quantified differences in these vocal patterns by comparing PF distributions using Wasserstein distances (WD) with 95% confidence intervals, and associated p values derived from permutation testing with WD as the test statistic.

During exploration, rats produced a high proportion of 22 kHz calls (41%) alongside complex calls (23%; Figure 2A). By contrast, social behaviors (e.g., social investigation, following, boxing/wrestling, chasing, and swarming) showed lower 22 kHz usage (<30%) and were dominated by complex calls. WD analyses reflected this divergence: exploration was clearly separated from social behaviors (5–8 kHz, p ≤ 0.001 for all comparisons), whereas varied social behaviors were more similar (2–4 kHz difference) with few statistically significant comparisons (Figure 2C).

Within social states, swarming and chasing were particularly alike (WD = 1.4 kHz, p > 0.05), both characterized by low 22 kHz production (15%) and mean PFs around 49–50 kHz. The shared vocal patterns suggest a shared quality to these behavioral states, which are both characterized by rapid locomotion.

Across the bimodal cluster, other call subtypes (e.g., upward ramp, flat, and short calls) occurred at relatively stable rates (5%–8%), but certain behaviors showed context-dependent increases. For example, swarming showed more short calls (12%) and trills (8.8%), while following and boxing/wrestling also exhibited elevated trill rates (7.2%).

Finally, frequency modulated (FM)/constant frequency (CF) variants—call types newly described in this paper—occurred at low but consistent rates. As described in “Call variants and evaluation of structural distinctness”, these calls contain an FM segment that either monotonically increases (FM-U) or decreases (FM-D) in frequency content, and a “CF” segment that has flat frequency content. FM-U_CF was most common (percent call emission = 1.2%–3.2%), while FM-D_CF, CF_FM-U, and CF_FM-D calls each accounted for <1% percent call emission. Although our analyses show these USVs are structurally distinct, their behavioral significance requires further investigation.

In summary, social and exploratory behaviors were characterized by a bimodal PF frequency distribution and prominent emission of both complex and 22 kHz USVs. 22 kHz signaling was notably higher during exploration, while varied social behaviors were characterized by broadly similar vocalization patterns.

PF distributions: Sub-30 kHz cluster

We aimed to assess vocal patterns during stationary, non-social, and fear-related states. Behaviors in the sub-30 kHz cluster were dominated by 22 kHz calls, with variation in the use of higher-frequency USVs (Figures 2D and 2E). When at least one rat was stationary—lying down or standing still—emitted calls were almost exclusively 22 kHz (percent call emission = 94.8%). Stationary behavior was common throughout recordings and typically occurred as a gradual transition from active exploration or interaction.

Rearing and non-social behaviors overlapped with stationary periods but showed greater vocal diversity, with increased production of complex (8%–10%) and flat calls (3%–4%), reflected in subtle bumps in the 50–60 kHz range (Figure 2E). During these periods, rats stood on their hind legs, sniffing, and whisking the air, with minimal social interaction and movement through the ENH (see Supplementary Videos “rearing” and “non-social”). Microphones and cameras did not detect external disturbances preceding stationary, rearing, and non-social behaviors, suggesting that they may arise from unmeasured environmental cues or internal social dynamics.

Fear-related behaviors revealed additional context-dependent complexity. After exposure to cat hair (see “cat hair experiment”), rats retreated into burrows and engaged in risk-assessment behaviors (peeking, pioneering). During these states, rats emitted distinctive “accented 22 kHz” calls, characterized by a flat 22 kHz fundamental followed by a brief, narrow vertical accent near the end of the vocalization (∼1 kHz above the fundamental; Figure 3B). These calls were largely exclusive to burrow hiding (19.7%), peeking (19.4%), and pioneering (13.8%) following initial cat hair exposure. In summary, 22 kHz and complex calls formed the largest components of the colony’s vocal profile, though their prevalence varied by behavior. The production rates of other call types (Figures 2A and 2D) were also relatively stable across behaviors-hinting at an underlying hierarchical structure in call usage, where a small number of call types dominate overall production while others are used more sparingly but consistently.

Taken together: within the bimodal cluster, exploration was associated with elevated 22 kHz call rates, whereas social behaviors were dominated by complex calls. In the sub-30 kHz cluster, 22 kHz signaling predominated but was not exclusive. Although fear- and threat-related behaviors align with the traditional view of 22 kHz calls as aversive signals, their high prevalence during exploratory, rearing, non-social, and stationary states points to broader communicative roles. Additional analyses using other acoustic features are reported in the supplementary material (Figures S5–S14).

Call variants and evaluation of structural distinctness

Evaluating call type distinctness

During manual review of 18,412 USV spectrograms, we identified five recurring call structures that were not readily accommodated by commonly used categorical schemes.9,11,15,16,17 While similar spectrographic features have appeared in prior reports, these variants have not, to our knowledge, been systematically delineated or quantitatively evaluated.

One variant, which we term the Accented 22 kHz call, was observed following initial exposure to cat hair (see cat hair experiments) and featured a vertical “accent” at the tail-end of the vocalization, ∼1 kHz above the fundamental frequency (Figure 3B). The other four call types featured a combination of frequency-modulated (FM) and constant-frequency (CF) components: the FM segment describes a monotonic increase (FM-U) or decrease (FM-D) in frequency content, while “CF” refers to the call’s flat component.

We observed four distinct variants of this FM/CF structure across colony recordings: FM-U_CF, CF_FM-U, FM-D_CF, and CF_FM-D (Figure 3A). To evaluate whether these calls represent structurally consistent acoustic motifs rather than arbitrary gradations along a continuum, we applied dynamic time warping (DTW) and K-Means clustering with “DTW Barycenter averaging” (DBA) to the extracted call contours (see STAR Methods and Figure S16).

DTW was performed under varying constraint conditions, with each comparison yielding an alignment cost—a similarity measure representing the amount of temporal warping required to match two call shapes. We hypothesized that alignment costs would be significantly lower, indicating greater similarity, for self-comparisons (e.g., FM-U_CF vs. FM-U_CF) than for across-type comparisons (e.g., FM-U_CF vs. FM-D_CF).

Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn’s post hoc (Bonferroni-corrected) revealed significant differences (p < 0.001) across all DTW constraint settings, so we report results from the unconstrained DTW implementation (Figure 3C). For each variant, self-comparisons yielded significantly lower alignment costs than comparisons with other variants (p < 0.001 for all), confirming their structural distinctiveness. Notably, self-comparisons yielded non-zero DTW alignment costs, reflecting within-type variability that DTW is designed to detect. Mean rank differences (MRDs) quantify the rank gap between groups: negative MRDs indicate lower costs for self-comparisons relative to across-type comparisons, and larger absolute values reflect greater structural dissimilarity between variants.

FM-U_CF self-comparisons had significantly lower alignment costs than comparisons with FM-D_CF (MRD = −2294.26), CF_FM-D (−1893.16), and CF_FM-U (−1121.13).

CF_FM-U self-comparisons had significantly lower costs than comparisons with FM-D_CF (−831.89), CF_FM-D (−807.43), and FM-U_CF (−264.15).

CF_FM-D self-comparisons had significantly lower costs than comparisons with CF_FM-U (−873.71), FM-U_CF (−776.63), and FM-D_CF (−383.42).

FM-D_CF self-comparisons had significantly lower costs than comparisons with FM-U_CF (−734.84), CF_FM-U (−594.82), and CF_FM-D (−223.06).

Call type clustering

To further assess distinctiveness of FM/CF variants, we applied DBA K-Means clustering to the extracted call contours. This approach (1) validates manual classifications against unsupervised clustering and (2) generates a barycenter for each cluster—a consensus sequence capturing the average signal in the cluster. We hypothesized that clustering would yield stable groups corresponding to the four FM/CF variants.

Clustering was performed with cluster counts (N) initialized from 2 to 5. At N = 4, barycenters aligned with the four known FM/CF structures, and the silhouette score—a measure of clustering quality—indicated moderate separation (0.52; Figure 3D). Each cluster was predominantly composed of a single variant (Figure 3E). However, while FM-D_CF calls form their own cluster, this cluster is not as strongly differentiated, and the barycenter does not cleanly reproduce the call. This may be attributed to the rarity of these calls—with more data, we’d likely observe a more distinct cluster with a “cleaner” barycenter.

Varying cluster number (N = 2–5) showed expected tradeoffs between granularity and cluster compactness, with highest silhouette values at lower N (e.g., N = 2, score = 0.65) reflecting broader grouping of modulation patterns. Across clustering conditions, core modulation motifs remained identifiable.

Taken together, DTW and clustering analyses indicate that these FM/CF variants form statistically distinguishable and internally consistent acoustic motifs within this colony. Rather than introducing entirely new call categories, these results demonstrate that call variants can be quantitatively delineated and validated within a naturalistic social environment. The resulting barycenters capture reproducible contour shapes across recordings, supporting the presence of stable and recurring modulation patterns embedded within the broader diversity of rat ultrasonic communication.

Cat hair experiments

To evaluate colony behavioral and vocal responses to predator threats, we placed a tuft of cat hair in the ENH for a 45-min period. Despite the short exposure and the lack of an actual predator, we observed dramatic changes in behavior and vocalizations, persisting for several days. Supplementary Videos (titled “cat hair then burrows”) provide an example of the rats’ response.

Experiment 1: Cat hair exposure

Cat hair exposure induces rapid shifts in colony behavior and vocalizations. During baseline (BL) recordings, rats vocalized predominantly in the 40–65 kHz range (Figure 4A, Baseline) with diverse calls, including a high proportion of complex calls (percent call emission = 51.6%; Figure 4F, left). Upon cat hair exposure, two rats briefly (<30 s) sniffed the hair. Vocalizations shifted to 22 kHz calls (Figures 4A and 4F, post exposure day 0), followed by coordinated retreat into burrows. The PF distribution shifted substantially (WD = 29.52 kHz, CI [28.06, 30.93], p = 0.002), with call frequency spiking from 51 to 185 calls/min, remaining elevated for 15 min (Figure 4B). Rats produced overlapping 22 kHz calls with prominent harmonics (Figure 3B, top panel). The overlapping vocalizations may represent a state of acute urgency and threat.

For the rest of the night, rats remained in the burrows, occasionally peeking out. There were two recorded instances of pioneering behavior, with a rat first emerging approximately 300 s after exposure to briefly investigate (<60 s) the cat hair before retreating to the burrows (Supplementary Video “pioneering”).

Vocalizations predominantly consisted of 22 kHz calls, including short (<300 ms) vocalizations. Notably, 22.3% of 22 kHz USVs featured a unique vertical “accent”, consistently occurring at the tail-end of the vocalization, ∼1 kHz above the fundamental frequency (Figure 3B). These “accented 22 kHz” calls first occurred about 3 min post-exposure and were unique to the initial exposure, not recurring in subsequent sessions (Figure 4C). The highly consistent timing and frequency offset of the accent across hundreds of calls, together with prior evidence that individual rats produce similarly complex call structures (e.g., split and multi-step calls), suggests that each accented 22-kHz vocalization originates from a single rat rather than from synchronized calling by multiple individuals.

24 h following exposure (day 1), rats remained mostly in the burrows, producing only 22 kHz USVs (Figure 4A, day 1). By day 2, 22 kHz calls continued to dominate (percent call emission = 62.9%), with a gradual return of other call types (percent call emission: 10.8% flat, 10.8% complex, and 4.8% upward ramp). Although video data for this day was lost, behavioral observations indicated that rats had begun emerging from their burrows. Call frequency remained low, peaking at 23 calls/min. Day 2’s partial recovery in call diversity is reflected in the “spreading out” of the KDE: probability density is still highest around 22 kHz, but now extends toward higher frequencies, with a small bump in the 50–60 kHz range. Still, WD measurements indicate that day 1 (WD = 29.48 kHz, CI [28.16 and 30.78], p = 0.0002) and day 2 (WD = 25.49 kHz, CI [23.4 and 27.58], p = 0.0002) have similar and significant shifts in their PF distributions relative to baseline.

Non-predator stimulus

One month after the initial cat hair experiment, the experimenter’s hair was introduced as a potentially non-predatory/non-aversive stimulus. Rats briefly sniffed the hair and carried it into their burrows. Following exposure, the PF shifted toward higher frequencies relative to baseline (WD = 6.38 kHz, CI [3.98 and 9.20], p = 0.0342; Figure 4D). Both the behavioral response and vocalization shift indicate that the stimulus was non-aversive. The significant shift in vocal output may reflect effects of the novel stimulus itself or natural variability in vocal behavior. Notably, the WD was markedly lower than that observed following cat hair exposure, indicating that the non-predator stimulus had a substantially smaller effect on vocal signaling.

Experiment 2: Cat hair re-exposure

Three months after the initial exposure, rats were re-exposed to the same cat hair for 45 min. Their response mirrored the first exposure: sniffing the hair, emitting 22 kHz calls (shift from BL: WD = 17.9 kHz, CI [14.78, 20.94], p = 0.0003), and retreating to the burrows (Figure 4E, Baseline and post exposure day 0). Call frequency was significantly lower in the second exposure (p ≤ 0.0001), peaking at 36 calls/min compared to 185 calls/min in the first exposure, with total calls dropping from 1,454 to 414. The rats also did not produce overlapping 22 kHz vocalizations, in contrast to the initial exposure.

In the following days, rats returned to baseline behavior and vocalization patterns more quickly than after the first exposure (day 1: WD = 11.58 kHz, CI [8.25 and 15.14], p = 0.0036; day 2: WD = 11 kHz, CI [7.63 and 15.62], p = 0.00048). On post exposure day 1, 22 kHz calls made up 80% of vocalizations, but by day 2, no 22 kHz calls were emitted (Figure 4F, left). Rats emerged earlier, exhibiting exploratory and eating/drinking behaviors (Figure 4F, right).

In summary, cat hair exposure triggered rapid behavioral and vocal changes, including a surge in 22 kHz calls, immediate retreat to the burrows, and a cessation of all non-defensive behaviors for up to two days. Vocal dynamics following initial exposure were distinct in several ways: the rats vocalized at a significantly elevated rate, producing overlapping 22 kHz calls and unique accented 22 kHz calls. While a second exposure also elicited 22 kHz signaling and burrow retreat, the response was attenuated, indicating possible partial habituation. Non-predator stimuli did not produce similar effects, highlighting the specificity of the cat hair’s impact.

After transferring four rats to a second habitat, the remaining rats in the ENH exhibited increased exploratory behavior, while stationary and rearing behaviors were absent. PD day 1

24 h post-disruption, rats in the ENH emitted primarily 22 kHz calls (97.2%) and a few upward ramps (2.8%), coinciding with rearing, stationary, and non-social behaviors. The PF distribution shifted toward lower frequencies compared to baseline (WD = 18.67 kHz, CI [16.6 and 20.81], p = 0.0003).

PD day 4

Vocal and behavioral patterns on day 4 resembled day 1, with 22 kHz calls dominant (82.4%) and upward ramps stable (3.7%). Complex calls increased (5.6%) alongside exploratory behavior. Aggressive interactions (boxing, wrestling) were more common and were frequently paired with bursts of complex calls and occasional 22 kHz USVs. The PF distribution remained largely different from baseline (WD = 13.33 kHz, CI [11.16 and 15.55], p = 0.0003).

PD day 5–day 7

By day 5, call types and PF distribution shifted back toward baseline (WD = 5.18 kHz, CI [2.35 and 8.16], p = 0.166). 22 kHz calls declined (day 5: 47.3%, day 6: 52.7%, day 7: 35.2%) as complex calls increased (34.3% on day 7). Day 6 (WD = 5.99 kHz, CI [3.1 and 9.01], p = 0.0492) shows a sharp uptick in passage-guarding behaviors—associated with aggressive encounters—which were rarely observed during baseline sessions.

By day 7, passage-guarding, boxing, and wrestling declined, while exploratory, rearing, non-social, and social-investigative behaviors dominated. Importantly, vocal output on day 7 was comparable to the original baseline (WD = 2.2, CI [1.73 and 5.91], p = 0.733). We therefore used day 7 as the new baseline for comparisons in subsequent reintroduction sessions.

Rats reintroduced

Reintroducing one rat on day 0 and three rats on day 1 increased 22 kHz call production (day 0: 66.1%, day 1: 82.4%), with complex USVs remaining second most frequent (day 0: 12.6%, day 1: 8.1%). Rearing, exploratory, non-social, and social investigative behaviors predominated. PF distributions shifted toward lower frequencies relative to baseline (day 0: WD = 11.02 kHz, CI [7.16 and 14.66], p = 0.0007; day 1: WD = 15.24 kHz, CI [11.83 and 18.60], p = 0.0006).

Post-reintroduction (PR) days 0 and 1

Following reintroduction, the vocal profile began to normalize. 22 kHz calls declined to 50.1% on PR day 0 and 43.8% on day 1, with complex and flat USVs remaining common. Rearing and exploratory behaviors persisted, and PR day 1 saw a marked increase in social-investigative behaviors. PF distributions shifted closer to baseline (day 0: WD = 7.5, CI [4.17 and 11.28], p = 0.159; day 1: WD = 6.98, CI [3.86 and 10.15], p = 0.159).

In summary, colony disruption elicited a delayed vocal and behavioral response. Immediately following removal (PD day 0), rats predominantly emitted 50 kHz-range calls, and no defensive behaviors were observed. By PD day 1, 22 kHz calls became dominant and social behavior declined. In the subsequent days, 22 kHz signaling remained elevated alongside increased aggression, possibly reflecting a renegotiation of social hierarchies. By PD day 7, vocal signaling returned to baseline levels, and aggressive interactions subsided. Reintroduction briefly elevated 22 kHz call rates, but both vocal and behavioral patterns quickly normalized, with decreased 22 kHz signaling and a rise in social investigative behaviors. Taken together, these results indicate that disruptions to colony social structure have significant and lasting impacts on a colony’s behavioral and vocal dynamics.

Experiment 2: New caged rat introduced

Forty-one days after the initial caged-rat exposure, a novel caged rat was introduced to the ENH following a 15-min baseline recording. Control: Empty cage with food

During baseline recordings, rats primarily emitted both 22 kHz (28.1%) and 50 kHz-range calls (37.5% complex, 8.3% short, 7.3% flat) while engaging in a mixture of social, non-social, and exploratory behaviors. Following introduction of an empty cage containing food, 22 kHz calling increased to 40.7%, while complex call emissions fell to 21.6%; however, unlike caged-rat conditions, no significant shift in the principal frequency distribution was observed (WD = 5.08 kHz, 95% CI [2.39 and 9.14], p = 0.299; Figure 6C).

Behaviorally, rats spent more time exploring and engaging in boxing and wrestling, and less time stationary or peeking from tunnels. Rats rapidly retrieved and cached food throughout the habitat despite continuous free access to identical pellets via feeders. Notably, swarming behavior was not observed during this condition, indicating that swarming was specific to the presence of an unfamiliar conspecific rather than to the introduction of a novel object or food alone.

In summary, introduction of an unfamiliar caged conspecific elicited robust change in the colony’s vocal-behavioral dynamics, including increased call rates, shifts in principal frequency distributions, and the emergence of swarming behavior, which was not observed under other experimental conditions. Notably, 22 kHz calling was prominent during initial exposure but was reduced during subsequent encounters with a caged rat. In contrast, introduction of an empty cage containing food did not elicit swarming behavior or comparable shifts in vocal frequency distributions; instead, rats engaged in food retrieval and caching behavior. Together, these results indicate that the observed vocal and behavioral changes were specific to the presence of an unfamiliar caged rat rather than to a novel object or food alone.

Discussion

This study presents, for the first time, a longitudinal survey of a rat colony’s vocal and behavioral repertoire in a naturalistic setting. Eight adolescent Sprague-Dawley (SD) male rats (1.5 months old) were introduced to the enhanced naturalistic habitat (ENH), where within the first week they exhibited behaviors characteristic of their wild kin: immediately adopting nocturnality, excavating burrow systems, exploring their environment, forming social bonds, and displaying defensive behaviors. This demonstrates that SD rats have retained a rich repertoire of “wild-like” behaviors despite many generations of artificial selection.

Although individual behavior tracking and call localization remain technically challenging in such a complex, three-dimensional environment35,36,37—particularly due to the presence of artificial and natural burrow systems—we show that a colony-level analytical framework yields meaningful insights into collective social behavior and vocal communication. By enabling the formation and maintenance of social bonds and dominance hierarchies over time, the ENH offers an ethologically rich context for future research into the neural basis of naturalistic social behavior.38

Behavioral and vocal trends

The diversity of rat vocalizations raises a fundamental question: what function does this diversity serve? Increasing evidence shows that rat vocal signaling extends beyond the classic affiliative-50 kHz versus aversive-22 kHz framework. For instance, rats emit ∼44 kHz calls during fear conditioning,39 ∼31 kHz calls under social stress,40 and modulate 50 kHz calls based on reward type.16 Even earlier studies show 22 kHz calls during the post-ejaculatory period,41,42,43 challenging their exclusive association with aversion.44,45 More recently, a study on the urban ecology of New York city rats observed that 22 kHz USVs are observed in varied, non-aversive contexts.46 This provides convergent evidence across independent paradigms that, under ecologically relevant conditions, these calls are produced in non-distress situations.

Consistent with this broader view, analysis of 27 h of naturalistic colony recordings revealed that 22-kHz vocalizations occurred across a wide range of behaviors that are not inherently aversive.47,48,49 Exploration and social activity featured both 22 kHz and high-frequency calls, while rearing, stationary, and non-social states were dominated by sustained 22 kHz vocalizations. These shifts reflected recurring colony-wide dynamics: high locomotor and social activity periods (associated with diverse calls) alternated with low-activity periods dominated by 22 kHz signals. Transitions across these states appeared spontaneous,50 without obvious external triggers, suggesting that 22 kHz calls may serve broader communicative functions beyond distress signaling.

Call variants and evaluation of structural distinctness

Decades of research have categorized ultrasonic vocalizations (USVs) by acoustic features and behavioral contexts, leading to the broad distinction between 50 kHz and 22 kHz calls—generally associated with affiliative/reward expectancy and aversive states, respectively. While this binary framework captures general trends, it belies the full diversity of rat vocalizations. Within the 50 kHz range alone, USVs vary markedly in principle frequency, duration, and modulation pattern. Recognizing this heterogeneity, several groups have proposed finer-grained classification schemes.15,16,51

Building on prior classifications9,11,15,16,17 describing up to 14 USV subtypes, we manually reviewed 18,412 USVs and consistently identified five recurring call structures—Accented 22 kHz, FM-U_CF, FM-D_CF, CF_FM-U, and CF_FM-D—that, to our knowledge, do not fit existing categories. Differences in rat strain used across studies (e.g., Long-Evans vs. SD) may account for discrepancies between previously described USV types and those observed here. We refer to these as call variants that do not readily fit existing classification schemes, rather than proposing a comprehensive taxonomic redefinition of the repertoire. Indeed, the rat USV field lacks a standardized taxonomy, and categories are often defined differently across laboratories. Our intent here is to document and quantitatively evaluate recurring spectrotemporal patterns observed in a naturalistic colony context.

To assess the distinctness of the FM/CF variants, we applied dynamic time warping (DTW) similarity and DBA K-means clustering on call contours. Unlike conventional acoustic features (e.g., principal frequency, duration) that capture only isolated parameters, DTW directly compares contour trajectories, making it sensitive to subtle time-frequency modulations. This approach provides a temporally sensitive and interpretable framework for evaluating categorical structure. By contrast, dimensionality reduction methods such as UMAP and t-SNE can be powerful for visualizing latent organization,52,53 but their implementation on time-series data poses challenges: they treat individual time-frequency bins as independent and do not natively model sequential dependencies. Several groups have proposed modifications to address this limitation,54,55,56,57 but such temporal-aware approaches remain an active area of research and are beyond the scope of the present study. Accordingly, DTW offered a robust method for evaluating whether these calls form reproducible categories.

Our DTW-based clustering confirmed four stable and well-defined clusters—FM-U_CF, FM-D_CF, CF_FM-U, and CF_FM-D—observed across colony recordings. FM-U_CF calls were the most frequent, but all four exhibited distinct spectrotemporal structures. These clusters may represent recurring acoustic motifs within a continuous vocal landscape, reflecting stable spectrotemporal patterns that emerge under naturalistic conditions. We interpret these findings as evidence of underappreciated categorical structure in the rat USV repertoire. At the same time, we acknowledge the ongoing discussion of whether rodent repertoires are best conceptualized as continuous or categorical and note that our clustering results represent an initial step toward establishing these categories. Further work, including expanded quantitative comparisons and behavioral validation, will be required to situate these calls more fully within the framework of rat communication.

Cat hair experiments

22 kHz calls were not only linked to diverse behaviors but also exhibited varied spectrographic features. Our cat hair experiments demonstrate context-dependent modulation of these USVs. While baseline calling averaged 9.9 calls/min (SEM = 0.10), exposure to cat hair drove a dramatic increase, with over 1,000 USVs produced within 45 min. Of these, 22.7% were accented 22 kHz calls, coinciding with burrow retreat and hiding. Although prior studies have presented spectrograms containing similar accent features,58 those calls were not explicitly described as a distinct structural variant nor analyzed for behavioral specificity. Here, accented 22 kHz calls were tightly linked to initial predator-odor exposure, emerging specifically during acute retreat and burrow hiding. These context-specific accented 22 kHz calls were absent in the days following cat hair exposure and during a second exposure.

Furthermore, rats emitted overlapping 22 kHz USVs only following initial exposure. These deviations suggest that alarm-induced 22 kHz USVs are distinct from 22 kHz vocalizations associated with other behaviors. The distinctive accent and overlapping delivery may convey information about threat urgency, consistent with broader evidence that mammals modulate alarm calls based on predator characteristics.59,60 This disruption of vocal turn-taking under threat also points to a breakdown in the structured, reciprocal nature of colony communication—an effect that merits further study.61,62

Despite the absence of a live predator, cat hair triggered immediate retreat, suppression of non-defensive behaviors, and prolonged burrow hiding for up to two days—behaviors consistent with classic descriptions of predator fear responses.23,63 A second exposure elicited rapid 22 kHz production and burrow retreat, but non-defensive behaviors recovered more quickly, possibly reflecting habituation, or scent degradation from long-term storage.

Colony disruption experiment

Following predator threat responses, we examined how disrupting group social life affected vocal and social behavior. Physically breaking up the colony altered vocalization patterns, but the shift was delayed. Immediately after removing half the group, the remaining four rats continued to socialize and vocalize in the 50 kHz range. By the next day, however, 22 kHz calls dominated. This increase in 22 kHz vocalizations coincided with heightened fighting, potentially suggesting a renegotiation of social dominance relationships. In this context, 22 kHz calls seem to signal aversion or social conflict. As fighting subsided, 22 kHz calls diminished, indicating a return to stable social dynamics. This return to baseline took seven days, demonstrating the significant and long-lasting impacts of social disruption on colony life. The initial delay, however, raises the question: why did the remaining rats initially emit only 50 kHz calls following the disruption?

Caged rat experiments

Introduction of an unfamiliar caged conspecific elicited rapid swarming behavior and a pronounced shift toward 22 kHz vocalizations during initial exposure. As swarming subsided, rats engaged primarily in rearing and exploration, consistent with broad environmental assessment rather than direct social interaction. When the same caged rat was reintroduced 24 h later, swarming and exploration persisted, indicating sustained interest; however, 22 kHz calling was markedly reduced, the principal frequency distribution shifted toward the 50 kHz range, and social-investigative behavior became prominent. Introduction of a novel caged rat weeks later produced a similar vocal-behavioral profile, with renewed swarming, exploration, and social investigation accompanied by predominantly 50 kHz vocalizations.

These patterns suggest that habituation alone cannot fully explain the reduction in 22 kHz signaling across repeated encounters. Although 22 kHz calls diminished with repeated exposure, persistent swarming indicates that the caged rat remained behaviorally salient to the colony. In contrast, introduction of an empty cage containing food elicited exploration and food caching—despite continuous access to identical pellets via feeders—but did not induce swarming or comparable shifts in vocal frequency distributions. This difference indicates that the vocal and behavioral responses observed during caged rat experiments were specific to the presence of an unfamiliar conspecific rather than to a novel object or food alone. Together, these findings further support the context-dependent use of 22 kHz vocalizations, which do not appear to signal a fixed internal state but instead vary with social and environmental circumstances.

Limitations of the study

While the ENH captures key aspects of a rat’s natural environment, several limitations remain. Our setup housed eight same-age male rats, whereas wild colonies are typically mixed in age and sex and face ecological pressures such as predation and resource scarcity. Recordings were also restricted to a 1-h window (20:00–21:00), and vocal patterns may vary across other periods of the circadian cycle. Further, we did not analyze sonic (“squeak”) vocalizations, which should be incorporated in future studies to provide a more complete picture of rat communication.

Behavioral annotations were performed by a single researcher to ensure consistency across the large dataset. Given the novelty of the ENH and the complexity of behaviors expressed, we prioritized building a consistent labeling framework rather than dividing annotation across multiple raters at this early stage. Although the absence of formal inter-rater validation is a limitation, labels were based on clear operational criteria, applied systematically, and cross-checked against the literature. A further challenge was the prevalence of concurrent behaviors (e.g., rearing overlapping with stationary), which required assigning vocalizations to multiple categories. This approach captured overlapping activity but introduced dependence between categories, potentially reducing contrasts between distributions.

In addition, behavioral analyses were aligned to periods containing USVs, allowing us to characterize the behavioral contexts in which specific call types occurred. We did not directly compare behavioral repertoires during vocal versus non-vocal intervals; such contrasts would provide additional insight into how vocal activity relates to ongoing behavior but introduce substantial analytical challenges in a naturalistic, multi-animal setting with continuous activity and overlapping behaviors. Developing frameworks capable of addressing this question is an important direction for future work.

The colony also experienced major social events (e.g., predator exposure, removal, or introduction of individuals), which may have had lasting effects on social and vocal dynamics beyond the few days directly assessed. Future technical developments—such as individual rat tracking and more precise measures of aggression or cooperation—will provide deeper insights. Implementing such methods in the ENH remains challenging due to occlusions, burrow systems, and close interactions, but advances in tracking and pose estimation are likely to mitigate these barriers. Although this study did not focus on individual variation, such differences are critical for shaping group dynamics and warrant further investigation.

Further, we note that this study focuses on a single social group (N = 1 at the colony level), with eight individuals contributing to within-colony variation. We provide a high-resolution characterization of the vocal-behavioral dynamics within this single colony, comparable to foundational work in behavioral ecology in which a single animal group is deeply characterized to establish methodological and conceptual frameworks for future replication. However, as this work represents a single ecological replicate, colony-level generalization must be interpreted with caution. Replication across multiple independently housed colonies will be essential to determine which vocal-behavioral patterns are colony-specific and which reflect stable features of rat social systems. Although colony-level replication is limited, the dataset contains extensive within-colony behavioral and vocal variation across contexts, allowing analysis of structured dynamics within the group even as broader generalization awaits multi-colony replication.

Finally, a continuous 32 kHz ultrasonic background noise, likely generated by electronic equipment, was present throughout recordings. This noise is not unique to our setup but pervades our vivarium environment more broadly, making its specific contribution difficult to isolate. Although we cannot entirely exclude the possibility of an effect on vocal output, it represents a constant and unvarying stimulus. From a basic neuroscience perspective, animals adapt to steady sensory input, making it unlikely that this background noise selectively drove the 22 kHz production observed here.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates how a colony’s social life mediates vocalization and supports the behaviors necessary for survival: rats use it to negotiate their social relationships, fight and cooperate, and warn conspecifics of predator threats. Listening in on the world of ultrasonic communication highlights the inseparable nature of social behavior and vocal signaling in rats. When we ignore vocal communication, we miss a central aspect of how rats navigate their social world.

Experimental model and study participant details

Subjects

Eight male Sprague Dawley rats (Charles River Laboratories, United States), 1.5 months old at the start of the experiment, were housed in the enhanced naturalistic habitat (ENH) for 7 months. To habituate the rats to human contact (as they would have contact with the primary investigator and vivarium technicians throughout the study’s duration), the primary investigator handled the rats daily for approximately 5 minutes over 4 days before their introduction to the ENH. The rats were housed in the same holding room prior to introduction to the ENH but did not have direct physical contact with one another. It is not known whether any of the rats were littermates.

In the ENH, the rats had ad libitum access to food pellets and water and were maintained on a 12:12 light/dark cycle with lights off at 18:30 h. Temperature was maintained at 72 °F/ 22°C. All procedures were approved by the UC Irvine Animal Care and Use Committee.

The enhanced naturalistic habitat

The ENH is a 3m x 3.6m x 2.4m (width x height x length) room in the vivarium, equipped with autoclaved peat moss bedding, wooden play structures, and a large stock tank. The stock tank contained both a man-made tunnel system near the surface and rat-made burrow systems deeper within the tank (Figure 1). A 0.91m tall plexiglass barrier encloses the habitat to prevent escape. At the center of the ENH is a galvanized round stock tank, 2m in diameter and 1m deep, containing interconnected PVC pipes arranged radially to form the artificial basis rats' tunnel structure. The stock tank and surrounding areas are filled with packed peat moss bedding, which the rats use for constructing their natural burrows. The topsoil was cleaned of rat droppings on a biweekly basis. This ENH expands on the naturalistic habitat described in Polley et al. (2004)32 by adding external space, structures, and bedding.

Method details

USV and behavior recording

Colony experiments

The experiments assessed the natural social and vocal behavior of a colony of eight male rats under ethologically relevant baseline conditions and following experimental manipulations. Three Avisoft CM16/CMPA (Avisoft Bioacoustics, Berlin, Germany) ultrasound microphones, positioned in a triangular configuration 172.7 cm (68 in) above the ground and 101.6 cm (40 in) above the stock tank, recorded USVs with a 250 kHz sampling rate and 16-bit resolution. The microphones, which have a flat frequency response between 25 kHz – 140 kHz, were connected to Avisoft’s ultrasound recording interface, UltraSoundGate 416H. This interface contains four microphone channels (each channel has a separate A/D converter), a Delta-Sigma ADC with an integrated adaptive anti-aliasing filter, and a relatively flat frequency response between 20 Hz – 370 kHz.

Two 4K ANNKE (ANNKE INNOVATION (HK) CO., LIMITED, Hong Kong) infrared cameras (840-860 nm peak wavelength, Figure S15) were mounted on opposing walls to capture behavior from two directions. Recordings took place between 20:00 and 21:00 h, when the ENH was completely dark, across 27 sessions. The experimenter briefly (≤ 5 min) entered the ENH prior to each session to activate recording equipment and to introduce experimental manipulations following a 15-minute baseline recording. The same female investigator conducted all sessions and was not present during recordings. To assess whether investigator presence was associated with 22kHz vocalizations, potentially indicating stress responses, we examined the distribution of principal frequencies of USVs emitted during and immediately following investigator entry into the habitat (Figure S3). No relationship was observed between investigator presence and the principal frequency of emitted USVs.

A visual overview of the experimental protocol is provided in the supplementary materials (Figure S2).

Baseline experiments

Rats behaved freely without experimental manipulation. Four hour-long baseline recordings were collected during the first eight weeks following colony establishment, between 20:00 and 21:00 h. Additional baseline sessions were conducted prior to each major manipulation (cat hair, colony disruption, and caged rat experiments), as detailed in their respective STAR Methods subsections. These data were included in the Behavior and Vocal Trends analysis.

Cat hair experiments

The cat hair experiments assess how ethologically relevant predator stimuli affect colony vocal-behavioral dynamics. Two cat hair experiments were conducted three months apart, following the same protocol with the same tuft of cat hair, which had been preserved in a plastic bag. The cat hair had been collected ∼24 hours prior to the first session, from a 5-year-old domestic male cat. After a 15-minute baseline, the experimenter placed cat hair in the stock tank, removing it after 45 minutes. Recordings continued for 20 more minutes, with additional hour-long sessions on the two following nights (no cat hair reintroduced). Between the two cat hair experiments, the female experimenter’s hair was introduced to the ENH as a potentially non-predatory/non-aversive stimulus. Following the cat hair protocol, the experimenter’s hair was placed in the stock tank for a 45-minute period following the 15-minute baseline.

Colony disruption experiments

The colony disruption experiments assess how ethologically relevant social disruptions affect colony vocal-behavioral dynamics. After a 45-minute baseline, four rats were transferred to a second habitat over the course of 20 minutes. The remaining four rats in the ENH were recorded for 45 more minutes. Over the next nine days, ENH was recorded in five hour-long sessions. One rat was reintroduced to ENH on Day 10, with the remaining three returning on Day 11. The staggered reintroduction is due to the experimenter initially managing to catch only one rat. The reunited colony was recorded during hour-long sessions on Days 12 and 13.

Caged rat experiments

Over the course of two successive recording sessions (Experiment 1), an experimentally naive male Sprague Dawley rat solo-housed inside its cage was placed at the center of the habitat. A 15-minute baseline recording was taken at the beginning of each session, after which the experimenter briefly entered the room to place a caged rat in the center of habitat. When the hour-long recording concluded, the experimenter removed the caged rat and returned him to a holding room. The same caged rat was used for the first two recording days. In Experiment 2, conducted 41 days after the conclusion of Experiment 1, another experimentally naïve male Sprague–Dawley rat was introduced to the habitat in a cage. Because food was placed on top of the caged rat’s enclosure, an empty cage containing food was introduced as a control 43 days after the conclusion of Experiment 2.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Noise and interference

A continuous 32kHz noise, likely from electronic equipment in the vivarium, was present throughout the sessions. Though digitally removed before analysis, its potential effect on rat behavior was not assessed. Because this noise occurred in all vivarium rooms, including both holding and experimental spaces, it could not be experimentally controlled. Additionally, 70 kHz+ USVs were strongly attenuated (Methods S1: Figures S17–S21). The CM16/CMPA microphones were mounted 172.7 cm (68 in) above the ground to prevent access by the rats, which are sufficiently athletic to jump onto equipment and potentially damage it. As a result of this placement, the microphones faintly detected 70–80 kHz calls but not those above 80 kHz. These higher-frequency calls were likely missed due to atmospheric attenuation, the large size of the ENH, and microphone height.

Preliminary analysis

Audio files (.wav) were processed in DeepSqueak51 using the Rat Detector YOLO R1 for short-duration calls in the 30–90 kHz range, and the Long Rat Detector for long-duration 22kHz calls (15–29 kHz). Call spectrograms were generated using a 0.00313s window size and NFFT (Non-Uniform Fast Fourier Transform) with 50% overlap. Because DeepSqueak’s automated detections and classifications performed insufficiently for reliable use (see Methods S1: Table S3 for evaluation), all audio files were manually reviewed. To minimize false negatives and ensure complete coverage of each recording, recordings were systematically examined in consecutive 5s windows across the full duration of each session, including segments without detector-identified events.

Ultrasonic vocalizations were defined as discrete, temporally continuous signals with a coherent spectrotemporal structure characteristic of rat USVs. Movement-related noise in the habitat was readily identifiable by its diffuse spectral energy, broadband frequency content extending from audible ranges into ultrasound, and lack of a stable frequency trajectory, in contrast to the narrowband, structured contours of USVs. All detected calls were manually verified, labeled by type, and exported to a call statistics file containing timestamps and acoustic parameters for all validated calls.

Video recordings were cross-referenced to annotate the behaviors occurring at each call timestamp. Behavioral annotations were therefore applied continuously and event-aligned, rather than at fixed temporal intervals. Behaviors were labeled according to the ethogram (Table 1), which is based on Schweinfurth’s (2020)8 review of rat social behavior. The only newly introduced category is swarming behavior, observed specifically in the context of caged-rat experiment (see Table 1 for behavior descriptions). Annotations were performed by a single researcher to ensure consistency across the large video dataset.

Defining and labeling call types

Call types were manually labeled in DeepSqueak, according to a categorization scheme15,16,17 consisting of 14 50 kHz subtypes and 22 kHz calls. Of the 14 subtypes originally described by Wright et al. (2010)15 11 were observed in this study and are depicted in Figure S1. Differences in rat strain used across studies (Long-Evans vs. Sprague Dawley) may contribute to discrepancies between previously described USV types and those observed here. Visual analysis of 18,412 call spectrograms suggested four additional 50kHz subcategories, as the spectrographic structure of these calls did not fit cleanly into the previously described USV subtypes. The four potential subcategories are referred to as FM-D/CF, FM-U/CF, CF/FM-U, and CF/FM-D (Figure 2A). ‘CF’ stands for constant frequency, ‘FM’ stands for frequency modulation. ‘U’ represents Up, and ‘D’ represents Down, indicating the direction of frequency modulation.

To validate the distinct call structures of the four FM/CF variety calls, we used dynamic time warping (DTW) and DTW Barycenter Averaging (DBA) K-Means clustering. These approaches and their implementation are detailed below.

Dynamic time warping and barycenter averaging

Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) and Dynamic Barycenter Averaging (DBA) K-Means were used to evaluate whether the four FM/CF variants have distinct call contours. DTW measures the similarity between time-series signals by finding the temporal alignment that minimizes the Euclidean distance between them. Signals with similar shapes (similar time-varying patterns) will have lower alignment costs compared to signals with dissimilar shapes.67,68,69

DBA K-Means clustering

Given DTW as a similarity measure, many distance-based clustering techniques, including K-Means, can be adapted for time-series clustering. However, standard K-Means uses Euclidean averaging to compute cluster centroids, which is suboptimal for DTW-based clustering. DTW barycenter averaging (DBA) provides a strong alternative: introduced by Petitjean et al. (2010),70 DBA computes cluster centroids under DTW by iteratively refining an average sequence to minimize its DTW-based distance to a given set of sequences. This process produces a consensus sequence, representing the most typical signal in the data.

DTW and DBA K-Means implementation

DTW and DBA K-Means were used to evaluate whether the four FM/CF variants exhibit distinct spectral structures. Because DTW measures similarity by allowing for non-linear alignments, it is well-suited for comparing time-series representations of calls that may vary in duration and frequency. Additionally, DBA K-Means provides a centroid-based clustering method that accommodates DTW-aligned sequences, allowing us to assess whether same-type calls naturally group together. If call types exhibit large across-type differences and minimal within-type differences, we expect same-type calls to form coherent clusters under DBA K-Means and to exhibit lower DTW alignment costs relative to different-type calls.

The analyses described here focus on spectral shape, consistent with common approaches that classify call subtypes based on spectral contour and relate subtypes to behavior.9,11,14,15,16,17While this analysis does not account for other acoustic features, these features may contribute to behavioral significance. However, their role in call type distinctiveness is beyond the scope of this study.

Pre-processing

First, all original call classifications were reviewed to identify and remove noisy signals, ensuring that only high-quality vocalizations were retained. In RavenPro,64 non-signal regions of the spectrogram were filtered out through a bandpass filter, eliminating background noise that could interfere with subsequent signal extraction.

Analysis

The following analyses were implemented using a custom Python script. Spectrograms of pre-processed signals were generated via Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) with the following parameters: sampling rate – 250 kHz, NFFT – 2048, hop length – 256, window length – 409.6 samples, window type – Hann.

Applying DTW and DBA K-Means directly to spectrograms is computationally expensive and unreliable due to their high-dimensional nature. To obtain a lower-dimensional representation of spectral shape, we extracted a 1D time-series representation from each spectrogram (Figure S16). This process involved: 1) converting amplitude values to a decibel scale; 2) extracting the peak decibel value from each time bin; 3) mapping each peak value to its corresponding frequency bin, forming a 1D representation of frequency fluctuations over time.

This method preserves the essential spectral shape of each call. To ensure accurate signal extraction, all spectrograms were manually reviewed.

Using the tslearn library,65 DTW was performed on the spectral shape data with a Euclidean distance metric. The behavior of DTW may be affected by the selected warping constraints, which place global bounds on the set of admissible warping paths between two time-series signals. Sakoe-Chiba bands and Itakura parallelograms are widely used constraints, and they can greatly affect the accuracy of DTW-based clustering and classification.69 To explore the effects of different warping constraints, we tested DTW under the following conditions: no constraints, and with Sakoe-Chiba radii and Itakura slopes set to 1, 5, 10, 15, and 20.

For each DTW comparison, signals were normalized (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1) and re-interpolated to match the length of the longer signal. While DTW inherently accommodates length differences by stretching or compressing sequences, extreme length mismatches tend to inflate DTW distances. Since this analysis focuses on spectral shape rather than duration, re-interpolation minimizes the potential confound of length-driven distance inflation. Prior research suggests that re-interpolating signals to a common length does not negatively impact DTW accuracy.67

DTW outputs alignment costs for every pairwise comparison. To determine whether same-type calls exhibit significantly lower DTW costs than different-type calls, we performed a Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni’s correction. For each test, comparisons were structured such that one call type served as the reference (‘focal’) group while being compared against other call types (e.g., FM-U_CF vs. all others). Mean rank differences were then assessed across groups.

DBA K-Means clustering was then applied to spectral shape data using tslearn. Since K-Means require input sequences of uniform length, all signals were first resampled to the same length and normalized (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1). The following parameters were used: number of clusters: 2–5, maximum iterations: 50, initialization number: 10, distance metric: DTW, maximum barycenter iterations: 100.

To assess clustering quality, silhouette scores were then calculated in the custom Python program. Silhouette scores range from –1 to 1, with higher values indicating better-defined clustering: a score near 1 suggests that the point is well matched to its own cluster and poorly matched to neighboring clusters, while values near 0 indicate overlapping clusters, and negative values suggest possible misclassification.

Acoustic feature distributions

To assess behavioral differences in vocalization tendencies, we compared acoustic feature distributions across behavioral contexts (see colony experiments). The evaluated acoustic features are summarized in Table S2. All analyses were conducted using a custom Python program.

Kernel density estimation

To visualize and estimate the probability distribution functions (PDFs) of acoustic features, we applied kernel density estimation (KDE) with a Gaussian kernel. Using the SciPy library,66 the kernel bandwidth was selected using Scott’s Rule – which sets bandwidth proportional to the standard deviation and inverse cube root of the sample size. KDE then provides a smooth, continuous representation of data distributions, enabling comparison of behavioral differences in vocalization tendencies.

Wasserstein distance

To quantify differences between acoustic feature distributions, we computed pairwise Wasserstein Distances (WD). WD measures the amount of ‘work’ required to transform one probability distribution into another. As it quantifies this in terms of our unit of measurement, WD serves as an interpretable effect size metric.71 For instance, a WD of 30kHz reflects a substantially larger shift than a WD of 5kHz—even though both might yield statistically significant p-values. In systems with natural variability, such as vocal behavior, small shifts can be statistically significant but biologically negligible. Reporting effect sizes and CIs helps avoid over-interpreting such results and provides a clearer understanding of both the magnitude and uncertainty of observed effects.72,73

To account for differences in sample sizes and ensure the robustness of WD estimates, we used a bootstrapping approach with 10,000 resamples to compute 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Statistical significance of observed WDs was assessed via permutation testing, using WD as the test statistic. For each pairwise comparison, observations were pooled and randomly reassigned to groups to generate a null distribution of WD values. Permutations were performed at the bout level, where a bout was defined as a temporally contiguous grouping of vocalizations, with each vocalization separated by ≤ 1 s of silence (a gap of > 1s defines a new bout). Bouts thus represent natural groupings of calls likely corresponding to a single behavioral episode or interaction. The p-value was calculated as the proportion of permuted distances greater than or equal to the observed WD and corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.
