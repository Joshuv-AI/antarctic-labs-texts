Introduction

Intracorneal ring segments (ICRS) were first proposed in the 1940s in order to treat myopia. In 2000, they were suggested as a way to decrease corneal steepening in keratoconus affected eyes.1 Corneal allogenic intrastromal ring segments (CAIRS) were first used in 2015 as a novel treatment of corneal ectasia and keratoconus. The technique involves an intrastromal implantation of ring segments of allogenic tissue from any source, including freshly cut, preserved, unprocessed, processed or packaged segments.2,3 CAIRS were developed as a natural substitute for synthetic intrastromal ring segments, aiming to decrease astigmatism and increase corneal stability, while avoiding the risks of synthetic materials, such as infection, inflammation, extrusion, and melting. In addition, a more individualized approach was made possible, by the ability to modify the size and shape of CAIRS to better fit each patient's anatomy and disease progression.3 Placing the segments along the steep meridian helps flatten the corneal curvature within the ring's arc, recenters the cone, and decreases irregular astigmatism. This leads to a hyperopic shift and overall improvement in refractive and visual outcomes.4

Astigmatism is a common refractive error resulting from the cornea's inability to bring parallel light rays to a single focal point on the retina. It may be regular or irregular, and can arise from corneal, lenticular, or retinal causes. Regular astigmatism is typically classified into four types: with-the-rule, against-the-rule, oblique, and bi-oblique. Management options range from spectacles and contact lenses to surgical interventions such as refractive procedures, astigmatic keratotomy, and toric intraocular lens implantation.5

This case report highlights the potential role of CAIRS as an effective management option for high astigmatism.

Case report

A 54-year-old male patient, presented with decreased vision in both eyes. There was no history of ocular trauma, refractive surgery, or any prior corneal interventions in both eyes.

The left eye had a preoperative uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) of 0.1 and corrected visual acuity (CVA) of 0.8 (decimal scale), caused by high corneal astigmatism (refraction: +3.5/9.25 × 109), and the patient was seeking visual improvement.

The right eye was amblyopic, with keratoconus, and no improvement with hard contact lens (BCVA was 0.1 (decimal scale), refraction: +8.5/−9.5 × 88), and the patient doesn't want to do any interventions to his right eye.

Pre-operative investigations

Preoperative tomography (Pentacam) of the left eye confirmed high, regular corneal astigmatism (Simulated keratometry (SimK) 48.30/39.94 D, cylinder 8.36 D @ 18°, Maximum keratometry (Kmax) 49.11 D) with a normal pachymetric profile (thinnest point thickness 545 μm) as shown in (Fig. 1A).

(B) Axial curvature, anterior and posterior elevation, and pachymetry maps of the left eye.

Comprehensive preoperative Scheimpflug tomography confirmed high astigmatism, characterized by a bow-tie pattern on axial curvature map. Pachymetry demonstrated a normal corneal thickness (Fig. 1B), while tomography indices were abnormal, consistent with keratoconus suspect, without features of advanced ectasia. Overall, these findings support the diagnosis of keratoconus suspect with high astigmatism.

Pentacam tomography of the right eye showed irregular corneal steepening with an asymmetric bow-tie pattern and inferior steepening, with corresponding anterior and posterior elevation changes, consistent with keratoconus (Fig. 1C).

Pre-operative anterior segment optical coherence tomography (OCT) (Anterion, Heidelberg Engineering) of the left eye demonstrated preserved corneal stromal architecture without structural abnormalities.

Management \ surgical details

CAIRS implantation was performed in the left eye to manage visually significant high corneal astigmatism. The plan was made based on the surgeon experience from previous CAIRS and ICRS cases, no specific nomogram was followed. According to preoperative corneal tomography, creation of intrastromal channel was planned. A tunnel with an inner diameter of 4.4 mm and an outer diameter of 7.0 mm was created, with a depth of 270 μm. Two entry incisions were positioned at 287° and 107°.

Femtosecond cut CAIRS parameters

Two symmetric arcuate allogenic stromal ring segments were cut by Ziemer Z8 femtosecond laser device, with a segment width of 1400 μm, arc length of 50°, and segment depth of 400 μm.

They were aligned along the axis of 17° and 197° guided by the CALLISTO eye system (ZEISS) (Fig. 2). The procedure was done successfully without any intraoperative complications.

Preoperative surgical planning for CAIRS implantation in the left eye, showing a schematic representation of tunnel creation, incision placement, and segment positioning.

Post-operative investigations

At 1-year follow-up, corneal tomography demonstrated a sustained improvement in regularity. Anterior axial curvature maps showed a marked decrease in astigmatism, from approximately 8 diopters preoperatively to 3 diopters. Posterior corneal curvature remained stable and pachymetric analysis confirmed corneal thickness preservation (Fig. 3A and B).

Difference maps

Anterior axial curvature difference mapping demonstrated a redistribution of corneal curvature at 1 year after CAIRS implantation. It showed central and paracentral flattening, mainly along the preoperatively steep meridian, compatible with a significant reduction in corneal astigmatism from 8.4D preoperatively to 1.0D at 1 year. These changes were broad and symmetric, indicating improved corneal regularity. (Fig. 4A).

(A, B) One-year postoperative anterior axial curvature and anterior tangential curvature difference maps of the left eye.

(A) Anterior axial curvature difference map.

(B) Anterior tangential curvature difference map.

Anterior tangential curvature difference map demonstrated localized reshaping of the cornea, with zones of flattening along the preoperatively steep meridian. Compared with axial mapping, the tangential difference map showed more focal curvature changes, consistent with the segment-specific effect of CAIRS implantation. These findings further support a targeted regularization of corneal astigmatism. (Fig. 4B).

Epithelial thickness difference mapping demonstrated mild, organized epithelial remodeling at 1 year following CAIRS (Fig. 5A).

(A, B) One-year postoperative epithelial thickness and pachymetry difference maps of the left eye.

(A) Epithelial thickness difference map.

(B) Pachymetry difference map.

Pachymetry difference mapping showed minimal, diffuse changes in corneal thickness at 1 year following CAIRS. The difference map showed preservation of central and paracentral corneal thickness. Central corneal thickness decreased by 6 μm, which is clinically insignificant. These results are supportive of long-term stability of the cornea after CAIRS (Fig. 5B).

Anterior segment OCT (Anterion, Heidelberg Engineering)

Anterior segment OCT performed at 1 year postoperatively, confirmed appropriate intrastromal position of the ring segments, which were well integrated with the corneal stroma, and within the planned implantation depth of 270μm. The surrounding stroma appeared well preserved, with an intact epithelium overlying and no signs of segment migration, extrusion, or any interface-related complications, supporting a stable segment positioning at one year (Fig. 6A).

Slit lamp examination

Slit-lamp photo at 1 year postoperatively, showed well-positioned intrastromal segments, clear overlying cornea and absence of haze, neovascularization, extrusion, or any inflammatory reaction (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

CAIRS is a surgical procedure that involves inserting ring segments from donor corneal stromal tissue into the patient's stromal channels. It was developed as a natural substitute for synthetic intrastromal ring segments, aiming to decrease astigmatism and improve corneal stability while lowering the synthetic ring risks. CAIRS was primarily introduced as an alternative of synthetic ring segments for keratoconus and corneal ectasia. Its allogenic nature provides improved biocompatibility and incorporation with corneal stroma, resulting in stable long-term results.3

The main clinical evidence supporting CAIRS comes from a study by Jacob et el. In which 24 eyes of 20 patients were included. The authors reported significant improvement in the corrected (1.29 ± 1.33 lines; range: 0 to 5 lines), and uncorrected (2.79 ± 2.65 lines; range: 0 to 8 lines) distance visual acuity, along with keratometric readings flattening, and reduction in topographic astigmatism within 3- and 5-mm optical zones.6 In another study by Jacob et al. Including 32 eyes of 29 patients with at least one year of follow-up, significant improvements were demonstrated in flat keratometry (K1), steep keratometry (K2), mean keratometry (Km), maximum keratometry (Kmax), and topographic astigmatism.2

Management of astigmatism ranges from spectacles and contact lenses to refractive surgery, keratotomy, and toric intraocular lens implantation, depending on the degree of astigmatism.5 In addition, to prevent inadequate outcomes, accurate axis alignment is crucial. As cyclotorsion occurs in the supine position, it might make it challenging to place the patient's eye correctly during corneal or intraocular lens implant procedures. As a result, toric lens may become misaligned, losing approximately 3.3% of its effectiveness for every degree of error.7

In the present case, the patient had almost more than 8 diopters of astigmatism, whereas most available procedures are able to correct up to 6D. Accordingly, CAIRS was considered a reasonable and tissue-preserving option in this setting.5

In our case, multimodal imaging showed objective evidence of structural change and stability following CAIRS implantation. With flattening along the formerly steep meridians, difference maps showed a notable decrease in corneal astigmatism, indicating successful corneal reshaping corresponding with the biomechanical action of intrastromal segments. This flattening and redistribution pattern is comparable to the corneal regularization documented in CAIRS studies for ectatic corneas, where improvements in both topographic and refractive parameters were noted following implantation.3

Crucially, at the one-year follow-up, pachymetry difference mapping showed diffuse minimal change, confirming the cornea's general safety. Preservation of both total corneal thickness and posterior corneal shape, along with the stable segment placement demonstrated on anterior OCT and slit-lamp photos, support that CAIRS can regularize the corneal shape successfully.3 These results are in keeping with the safety profile reported in larger CAIRS series involving ectatic cornea, which showed favorable anatomical and refractive outcomes with low complication rates.8

Although CAIRS implantation was well tolerated in this case, potential risks should be considered, especially in corneas without established ectasia. The procedure alters corneal structure and biomechanics, and its effect in otherwise normal corneas could be less predictable. Complications such as infection, inflammation, or segment extrusion can occur; therefore, careful selection and long-term follow-up are essential.3 The primary benefit of using CAIRS to treat keratoconus and ectasia is its reversibility and the low risk of impairing visual acuity, as refractive and visual outcomes can return to baseline in case of segment explantation.9

Although limited by single-case design and a follow-up duration of one year, this report shows objective clinical and tomographic evidence supporting the achievability of CAIRS in high corneal astigmatism.

This case highlights the potential role of CAIRS as an adjunctive option in the management of high corneal astigmatism, particularly in situations where the magnitude of astigmatism limits the effectiveness of other refractive procedures. In our case, the level of astigmatism overrides the corrective range of most available other options, making CAIRS a practical and more conservative approach. This was reflected in a meaningful reduction in corneal astigmatism, with improved corneal regularity and stable tomographic findings over one year follow up. These findings support the role of CAIRS as a step that may facilitate the use of additional refractive modalities in selected cases.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the feasibility of CAIRS implantation in high corneal astigmatism, especially when other options are limited. With careful patient selection, CAIRS could represent a tissue-preserving option in selected cases and future studies are needed in this matter.
