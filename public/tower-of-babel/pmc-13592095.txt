Enhancing surveillance of foodborne bacteria and antimicrobial resistance using a large centralized laboratory database: Case study of Campylobacter and nontyphoidal Salmonella in Peru
Authors: Juan Carlos Gómez de la Torre, Luis Alvarado, Maritza Quiroz Reyna, Ines Cajamarca, Silvia Tipe, Belqui Vargas, Josh M Colston, Lucero Romaina Cachique, Maribel Riveros Ramirez, Pablo P Peñataro Yori, Craig T Parker, Maribel Paredes Olortegui, Theresa Ochoa, Francesca Schiaffino, Margaret N Kosek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives:Rising antimicrobial resistance of foodborne pathogens threatens effective treatment. In LMICs, surveillance systems fail to capture diagnostic data generated by private laboratories. We assessed the value of private laboratory data to characterize the epidemiology, seasonality, and AMR profiles of Campylobacter and non-typhoidal Salmonella in Peru.Methods:A retrospective analysis was performed on stool culture data between 2020 and 2024. Positivity and resistance proportions were summarized by age group, sex, and hospitalization status. Seasonal trends were evaluated using generalized additive models, with interaction terms to assess age-specific seasonal effects.Results:Campylobacter and NTS were isolated in 4.1% and 2.2% of samples (N = 47,724). Infants had over 20-fold higher odds of Campylobacter positivity compared to individuals ≥5 years. Ciprofloxacin resistance exceeded 95% in both Campylobacter jejuni and Campylobacter coli , while azithromycin resistance reached 8.2% and 38.9%, respectively. NTS positivity was highest in children under 5 years, remaining stable across older age groups. Resistance was observed to ceftriaxone (40.6%), ciprofloxacin (32.6%), and azithromycin (11.3%). Campylobacter exhibited significant, age-dependent seasonal patterns, with peak positivity between July and October.Conclusion:Despite limitations in the availability of clinical data and harmonization of methods, private laboratories can provide timely information on pathogen burden, AMR, and seasonality that supports national surveillance. The use of this system may contribute to health security as reporting is significantly accelerated using automated laboratory data systems relative to passive centralized reporting systems.Keywords: Foodborne pathogens, Campylobacteriosis, Salmonellosis, Antimicrobial resistance, Trends

Introduction

Foodborne pathogens cause an estimated 2.2 billion cases a year, and over 880,000 deaths in 2021 [1]. Additionally, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in foodborne pathogens increases the risk of adverse outcomes in human infections [2]. However, the majority of foodborne illnesses are associated with acute, self-limiting, gastrointestinal episodes that go largely unreported and are not captured by centralized surveillance systems and thus are not able to be disseminated to inform clinicians or public health practitioners. In this sense, large-scale private laboratories serve as an underutilized big-data resource to report and survey trends in pathogen and AMR positivity over time [3].

To evaluate the utility of such an approach, we focused on two major foodborne pathogens with increasing AMR profiles: Campylobacter spp. and non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) [4,5]. Both are bacteria associated with acute and prolonged episodes of diarrhea, or excretion in children and adults, in both high and low- and middle-income countries [6–9]. Campylobacter, which causes an estimated 291.4 million cases and 71,200 deaths per year [1], is more frequently associated with gastrointestinal illness in children in resource constrained settings, and has sequelae that include weight loss, reduced linear growth [10,11], as well as post infectious neuropathies such as Guillain Barre Syndrome (GBS). NTS causes approximately 35 million cases of gastroenteritis, and 35,800 deaths per year, worldwide [4], and infections are also attributed to contaminated animal products, including poultry meat, pork and beef [12–16]. However, human contact with live mammals and poultry is also recognized as an important source NTS outbreaks [17].

Both Campylobacter and NTS are recognized as major causes of gastrointestinal illness in Peru. Robust epidemiologic studies related to Campylobacter in the Peruvian Amazon show high incidence rates of disease in children under the age of two (incidence of up to 0.7 cases per child-year), and increasing rates of AMR and multi-drug resistance [10,11,18–20]. Epidemiologic data from the rest of the country is nearly non-existent, despite recurrent outbreaks of GBS in the country linked with C. jejuni, and a recent health directive indicating continuous epidemiologic surveillance of Campylobacter associated diarrhea in sentinel hospitals [21–23]. NTS is a notifiable disease for the poultry industry in Peru, yet as a human disease is not subject to epidemiologic surveillance (acute diarrheal diseases are reported as a single diagnostic category). That said, NTS has been identified as responsible for multiple food-borne outbreaks [14,24], yet large-scale data on the overall burden of NTS is absent.

Therefore, this study aimed to leverage over 4 years of continuous data collection related to foodborne pathogens from one of the largest private laboratories in Peru. Here we report the overall isolation rates of Campylobacter and NTS in human stool samples, and their phenotypic patterns of antimicrobial resistance.

Methods

Data source

Data was sourced from the largest private laboratory network in Peru providing services to patients seeking private care. The network includes 34 laboratories in the capital of Lima, and three additional ones in Arequipa, Cajamarca and Piura. It also serves two tertiary level private hospitals in Lima. De-identified data from all stool cultures processed by this network between January 2020 and December 2024 were included in the analysis. Metadata associated with de-identified samples includes date of sample reception, birthdate, sex, outpatient/ inpatient status and district.

Ethics statement

All participants provided consent for the use of their biological samples in future research. Written informed consent was obtained from all adults, and from the legal guardians of individuals under 18 years of age. This study was approved as a secondary data analysis by the Ethics Review Committee of Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia (SIDISI 220207).

Data description and analysis

Laboratory methods

Samples were identified as positive or negative for Campylobacter by culture on non-selective Tryptone Soya Agar (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK), supplemented with 5% sheep (AREZ, Lima, Peru). Briefly, a 0.45 μm nitrocellulose filter (Millipore, MA, USA) was placed on top of the specified agar and 100uL of fecal suspension were placed on to the filter [25,26]. After passive filtration for 30 minutes at 37oC, filters were removed and places were incubated at at 42 °C for 24-48 hours under a microaerophilic atmosphere (5% O2 and 10%CO2; Campygen, Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) [25].

Campylobacter species ( C. jejuni and C. coli) were identified by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry (MS) (VITEK MS v3.2, bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France). Samples were also tested for Salmonella positivity by enrichment in Selenite Cystine Broth (Condalab, Madrid, Spain) for 18 to 24 hours at 35°C, and subsequent subculture on MacConkey (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) agar and xylose lysine deoxycholate agar (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) at 35 °C in ambient atmosphere for 24 to 48 hours. Species were identified using the Vitek 2 system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France), VITEK MS, and group O somatic serotyping (Somatic Group Salmonella, Probac, Brazil).

Antimicrobial resistance phenotypes (susceptible, intermediate, or resistant) were determined using standard disk diffusion methods Mueller-Hinton agar (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK); the medium was supplemented with 5% sheep blood for Campylobacter isolates [27]. Zone diameter breakpoints (mm) from the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI M45 & M100) were applied for Campylobacter and Salmonella. The CLSI zone diameter breakpoints for Enterobacteriaceae were used for antimicrobials for which there are no established breakpoints for Campylobacter spp. For reporting purposes, isolates classified as intermediate or resistant were combined into a single “non-susceptible’’ category.

Statistical methods

Patient’s age was grouped as follows: 0-11 months, 12-23 months, 24-59 months, 5-11 years, 12-19 years, 20-64 years, over 65 years, and undetermined if a date of birth was missing. Additional variables included sex of the patient and hospitalization status (inpatient vs outpatient, as an approximation of symptom severity). Positivity rates for Campylobacter and Salmonella were calculated and stratified by sex, age group, and hospitalization status. Differences in positivity between sex and hospitalization status were assessed using chi-square tests with statistical significance defined as P-value less than 0.05.

To explore how information from private laboratories can inform national level epidemiologic trends of major pathogens, we set to evaluate seasonal trends in Campylobacter and NTS isolates from patients samples whose declared district of residence was the department of Lima, the capital of Peru. Specifically, generalized additive models (GAM) were fitted to individual level data [28,29]. Pathogen positivity was modeled using a binomial distribution with a link logit function. To evaluate the seasonal component, cyclic cubic regression splines were applied to the day of the year. The models were adjusted for age, which was evaluated as a categorial fixed effect (≥60 months vs <11 months, 12-23 months, and 24-59 months), sex (male vs female) and year of sampling (2020 vs 2021, 2022, 2023 and 2024) (see equation A):

Additionally, to further explore if observed seasonal patterns varied by age, a model was constructed that included unique splines for each age group. Models were optimized using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) and performed using the MGCV package in R [28]. To visualize the overall seasonal burden of both pathogens, population-averaged predicted probabilities were calculated using frequency-weighted marginal effects [30], accounting for age group, sex, and year of isolation. All visualizations were done using ggplot2.

Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed in RStudio v.2024.04.2. and STATA v.18.

Results

A total of 47,724 stool samples were processed (average number of monthly stool samples processed = 795; median = 819, interquartile range = 724-913), of which 21,000 were from children under 5 years of age. Fewer than half of the samples (44.3%) came from male patients, 89.4% were associated with non-hospitalized patients and 85.4% of samples were from patients who declared their district of residence to be within the department of Lima.

Campylobacter was isolated from 4.1% (1954/47,724) of fecal samples, of which 90.8% (1775/1954) were classified as C. jejuni, 7.9% (156/1954) as C. coli, and the rest as Campylobacter spp. The proportion of Campylobacter positive samples by month and calendar year can be observed in Figure 1. With regards to Salmonella, 2.2% (1065/47,724) of stool samples tested positive. A single isolate was identified as S. enterica serovar Typhi and the rest as NTS. There was no difference in the proportion of positive samples for Salmonella by sex. There was an overall decreasing trend in Campylobacter positivity with increasing age (Cochran–Armitage test for trend, z = − 45.703, exact P-value < 0.001). Campylobacter spp. was isolated in 11.2% (906/8067) of stool samples from 0 to 11-month-olds, followed by 9.3% (566/6086) of stool samples from 12 to 23 months old, 4.5% (309/6847) of stool samples from 24 to 59 months old, and less than 2.5% in the remaining age groups, with patients between 12 and over 65 years with less than 1% positivity. NTS follows a similar, yet less pronounced age structure (Cochran–Armitage test for trend, z = −10.75, exact P-value < 0.001) given that among all age groups, NTS was isolated in between 3.1 and 1.0% of stool samples evaluated. Resistance to azithromycin, the main antibiotic used to treat complicated cases of campylobacteriosis, was found in 8.2% (124/1505) of isolates identified as C. jejuni and 38.9% (49/126) of isolates identified as C. coli, with available phenotypic data. In addition, 95.9% (1702/1774) of C. jejuni isolates and 94.1% (144/153) of C. coli isolates showed resistance to ciprofloxacin. Among NTS isolates with antibiotic resistance phenotypes, 40.6% (432/1065) were resistant to ceftriaxone, 32.6% (347/1064) to ciprofloxacin, and 11.3% (110/1052) to azithromycin. Seasonality of Campylobacter and NTS

A total of 40,776 observations from samples from participants from Lima were included in the analysis. In the final adjusted GAM model, an overall significant, seasonal pattern was observed for Campylobacter (edf = 5.64; P-value < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 2, Figure 2). Additionally, age continued to be strongly associated with Campylobacter positivity, with infants aged 0-11 months having the highest probability of being positive for Campylobacter (aOR: 21.12; 95% CI: 17.54-25.43) compared to individuals who were older than 5 years of age, after adjusting for sex, year of isolation and time of the year. Increased probability of positivity was also seen in 12-23 months of age category (aOR: 17.32; 95% CI: 14.28-20.99), and in the 24 to 59 months age category, compared to individuals older than 5 years of age (aOR: 8.11; 95% CI: 6.57-10.00). Additionally, males had 16% higher odds of testing positive for Campylobacter compared to females (aOR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.04-1.28) (Supplementary Table 2). Unadjusted GAM models that evaluated Campylobacter positivity, accounting for seasonal trends, are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Given that a significant seasonal trend was observed in the adjusted GAM model for Campylobacter, the variation of seasonality by age group was explored through a final model that included an interaction term between day of the year and age group. The final model revealed that infants 0-11 months had a complex seasonal pattern (edf = 5.62; P-value = 0.002), with multiple peaks and fluctuations throughout the calendar year, yet with over 15% probability of positivity between July and October. Children 12-23 months of age exhibited a single annual wave (edf = 2.43; P-value < 0.001) that peaked in the same months as the younger age group. Similarly, Campylobacter positivity in children 24-59 months exhibited a single, yet weaker annual wave (edf = 1.56; P-value = 0.027) (Supplementary Table 2). With regards to NTS, in the final adjusted GAM model, a seasonal effect was not evidenced meaning that the overall probability of positivity for NTS remains flat throughout the year in Lima (Supplementary Table 2, Figure 2). Age continued to represent a risk factor for positivity, given that children in the younger age groups had an increased probability of being positive for NTS in comparison to individuals 5 years or older. When the effect of age on seasonality was evaluated, a seasonal pattern was not observed in any of the established age groups (Supplementary Table 2, Figure 3). Discussion

Positivity rates for Campylobacter and NTS are presented for more than 47,000 samples collected over a 4-year period, as well as phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility data from almost 2000 Campylobacter isolates and over 1000 NTS isolates. This represents the first large-scale report from a private diagnostic laboratory both in Peru and the region of Andean South America. Given the general absence of reporting from private laboratories, this dataset holds considerable value and serves as an example of how large-scale, continuously collected data can contribute to the surveillance of pathogens of public health concern and can help model epidemiologic trends over time that can contribute to disease control strategies at a national level.

Global and regional efforts to perform AMR surveillance, such as the World Health Organization’s Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS), do not yet report AMR rates on a timely and actionable manner [31] that allows for near real time public health responses. In fact, GLASS reports that less than 50% of countries, territories or areas (CTAs) are enrolled in the GLASS-AMR program and that less than 50% of CTAs report antimicrobial sensitivity testing for gastrointestinal pathogens. This is expected as public health systems in low- and middle-income countries traditionally rely on manual reporting of culture data and phenotypic antimicrobial resistance information, do not centralize pathogen and AMR surveillance information, and are continuously threatened by lack of availability of reagents and consumables to adequately test and report on both community and hospital acquired infections [32]. Therefore, private laboratories using laboratory information management systems (LIMS) have the opportunity to contribute by reporting real-time large-scale information that can serve as a monitoring tool across time and across large catchment areas [33]. This is not to say that such data does not have frequently cited limitations, such as a lack of rigorous case definitions (reason for sending the sample for processing) and does not censor multiple samples from a single individual or have easily linked clinical outcomes with the data. While these limitations mean data must be interpreted with caution and cannot substitute harmonized, validated official surveillance programs, the scale, continuity, and accessibility of private laboratory data make it a valuable complement to high-quality geographically limited surveillance studies in this context, especially if these systems continue to release data with a delay greater than 1-2 years from clinical isolation as is currently the lag time acknowledged by the World Health Organization (although the data dashboard from the GLASS currently lags 3 years from the time of sample collection) [34].

Over a four-year period, Campylobacter positivity was observed in 4.1% of stool samples, and NTS in 2.2% using culture-based diagnostics. Campylobacter positivity was highly correlated with age with 11.2% positive stool samples from 0 to 11 months, and a considerable decrease in positivity up to adulthood. This pattern has been noted in other large-scale epidemiologic studies, although Campylobacter has been noted to peak among 12-23-month-olds compared to <1 year old children [10,11]. A high proportion of positivity in early childhood and subsequent decline could be explained by overall intense Campylobacter exposure and subsequent acquired immunity. A higher proportion of Campylobacter positivity was observed among outpatient samples compared to inpatient samples, a finding consistent with global surveillance trends an regardless of diagnostic methodology [1]. This likely reflects the typically self-limiting clinical course of campylobacteriosis and lack of more severe clinical manifestations that would warrant hospitalization, as is more frequently seen with Salmonella infections or other enteric pathogens such as Shigella. It is worth noting that the 4.1% positivity rate reported in this study likely underestimates the true burden of Campylobacter in this population, as culture-based methods have lower sensitivity than molecular diagnostic approaches [35].

High rates of antimicrobial resistance were observed for both Campylobacter and NTS. Specifically, over 95% of C. jejuni samples were resistant to ciprofloxacin in this large multiyear dataset with data predominantly from Lima, the capital of Peru. Reports from high income countries such as the US report quinolone resistance levels in C. jejuni of only 38.2% and C. coli of 37.0% [36], while evidence from Latin America indicates increased quinolone resistance levels [37–39]. Rates as high as this have been noted in the Peruvian Amazon though the external validity of this data has been doubted by clinicians in other areas of Peru and South America. This data provides key missing information that Campylobacter resistance to fluoroquinolones is dominant throughout Peru, and not just in the region of the Amazon. Rates in the United Stated of C. jejuni and C. coli resistant to Azithromycin reported by NARMS in 2023 are only 0.9% and 4.3% compared with the 8.2% and 38.9% observed in this study, respectively [36]. Increase in Campylobacter resistance to quinolones and macrolides in Peru could be attributed to the poorly regulated use of antibiotics in the poultry industry and likely contamination of the environment with Campylobacter that has experienced selective pressure with these classes of antibiotics. Moreover, rates of resistance to NTS are also cause for alarm. Third generation cephalosporins are the current first line of treatment of complicated cases of gastrointestinal salmonellosis. However, the current data set shows over 40.6% resistance to ceftriaxone, cefotaxime and cefixime, an alarming proportion given reports in other areas of the world, such as only 2.4% ceftriaxone resistance across NARMS data in 2023 [36].

Finally, a seasonal pattern was only evidenced for Campylobacter, which in turn was greatly influenced by age group. Campylobacter positivity peaked during the months of July through October, months that in Lima are generally characterized by lower ambient temperature and high humidity compared to the rest of the year. Studies exploring climate variability and Campylobacter incidence have also found seasonal patterns, yet with heterogeneous peaks in positivity, with most European countries peaking during spring and summer months [40–43].

NTS did not exhibit a statistically significant seasonal pattern, although slightly higher proportion of samples were positive between November and January, months in which ambient temperatures in Lima begin to increase. Seasonality of NTS has been demonstrated in high income countries such as Denmark and other European countries where increased positivity was observed during the warmer months of the year [44,45].

It is important to note that the absence of clinical metadata, including information on prior antibiotic use, presence or absence of gastrointestinal symptoms, as well as the severity of symptoms, limits our ability to assess whether isolates subjected to phenotypic resistance testing are clinically representative, and if prior antibiotic exposure could have influenced the observed resistance profiles. Additionally, this data set comes from private laboratory network, making the study population inherently limited to individuals seeking and affording private health care. This represents an important source of selection bias that should be considered when extrapolating these findings to the general Peruvian population.

In conclusion, these findings highlight the critical value of private laboratory data for strengthening national and regional surveillance of foodborne pathogens. Large laboratories with LIMS are common in Latin America and Asia and thoughtful integration of such data, alongside validated official surveillance systems, could improve global monitoring of antimicrobial resistance in key pathogens. The high rates of antimicrobial resistance observed in Campylobacter and NTS show that there is an urgent need for coordinated actions to monitor rates of food-borne related illnesses and its associated rates of antimicrobial resistance. Reporting large scale private-sector data could enhance the timeliness, representativeness, and overall impact of AMR monitoring in Peru and similar settings.
