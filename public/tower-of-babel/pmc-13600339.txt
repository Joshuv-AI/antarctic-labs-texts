Hybrid deep learning time-to-event modeling of major adverse cardiovascular events using coronary artery calcium score scans
Authors: Justin N. Kim, Juhwan Lee, Ammar Hoori, Hao Wu, Tao Hu, Sadeer Al-Kindi, Mohamed Makhlouf, Robert Gilkeson, Sanjay Rajagopalan, David L. Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The Agatston score from non-contrast coronary calcium scoring scan (CACS) is a robust predictor of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) but summarizes total calcium burden and may miss other risk-related imaging information in CACS. We hypothesized that a hybrid deep-learning (DL) model integrating image features with clinical, Agatston, and calcium-omics data could improve MACE risk prediction.Methods:In this retrospective cohort of 1950 patients, a DenseNet-121 encoder extracted DL-derived features from CACS volume, which were fused with tabular data (clinical features, Agatston, and 15 calcium-omics features) within a deep survival model. Performance was assessed via five-fold cross-validation using Harrell’s concordance-index (C-index) and time-dependent area under the curve (AUC), and net reclassification index (NRI), relative to tabular-only baseline.Results:Expanding the tabular feature set from clinical variables alone to include Agatston and calcium-omics improved discrimination for both tabular-only baseline and hybrid models. Incorporating DL-derived imaging representations consistently outperformed tabular baselines in C-indices (0.802 vs. 0.789 with the full feature set) and yielded higher time-dependent AUCs during first three years of follow-up. The hybrid model also achieved an NRI of 0.119 (p = 0.037). Patients reclassified from low to high risk had a significantly higher event rate (37.5%) compared to those remaining low risk (1.1%, p < 0.001).Conclusion:These preliminary findings suggest that integration of DL-derived CACS representations with clinical and hand-crafted features can provide complementary prognostic information and modestly improve MACE risk stratification compared to tabular data alone.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease remains the leading global cause of mortality, demanding the need for accurate individualized risk stratification [1]. Predicting risk to major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) – including myocardial infarction, stroke, and coronary revascularization – is central to timely intervention and improved outcomes. In current practice, non-contrast coronary artery calcium score (CACS) scanning provides the whole-heart Agatston score, a guideline-endorsed, well-validated risk marker [2-4]. Despite its utility, the Agatston score offers a single summary of calcium burden and may not capture the heterogenous morphology, distribution, and context of coronary calcifications that relate to risk [5].

To address limitations of Agatston scoring, our prior work has introduced “calcium-omics”, a set of hand-crafted imaging features, quantifying density, morphology, and spatial distribution of calcifications across major coronary arteries and have demonstrated incremental prognostic value over Agatston alone [6]. Nevertheless, such predefined hand-crafted features may not capture the full spectrum of risk-related information within CACS imaging data [7,8]. Moreover, calcification-centric features do not capture other relevant anatomy that are relevant to cardiovascular risk [9-15].

Deep learning (DL) offers an alternative by learning hierarchical representations directly from images [16-18] and has demonstrated strong performance in cardiovascular risk prediction [19-21]. Hybrid DL models integrating imaging and with tabular data have further improved risk predictions and discrimination [22,23]. Yet, hybrid time-to-event modeling that learns directly from non-contrast CACS scans remains unexplored. Prior hybrid survival work has focused on contrast-enhanced coronary CT angiography, limiting scalability due to contrast use and additional segmentation requirements [23]. Evidence from low-dose chest CT suggests that DL can extract prognostic signal from routine non-contrast imaging [24]. Collectively, these findings motivate an evaluation of DL-based hybrid survival modeling directly from CACS.

We hypothesized that DL-derived representations from non-contrast CACS images provide prognostic information beyond tabular data, and that fusing imaging with clinical features, Agatston score, and calcium-omics descriptors would further improve MACE risk discrimination. In this proof-of-concept study, we develop and internally validate a hybrid DL survival model to assess the feasibility and incremental value of fusing latent imaging representations with established MACE risk factors.

Methods

Study design and patient population

We performed a retrospective analysis of MACE-enriched subset from the CLARIFY registry at University Hospitals of Cleveland (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04075162). University Hospitals of Cleveland offers no-cost CACS exams for patients referred by cardiology department. The analytic cohort was not intended to represent an unselected asymptomatic screening population or to estimate population-level MACE incidence. Instead, the cohort was sampled to provide sufficient event numbers for model development and internal time-to-event evaluation. The registry is de-identified and managed under IRB approval in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent. Eligible participants underwent non-contrast CACS between 2014 and 2020 and had no known coronary disease at baseline, defined as no prior myocardial infarction, stable or unstable angina, significant coronary stenosis, or coronary revascularization. The time origin of survival analysis was the date of CACS acquisition. MACE status was determined over each patient’s total available follow-up rather than at a fixed follow-up horizon, with follow-up duration measured from the index CACS examination to the first qualifying MACE event or censoring. The primary endpoint was MACE, defined as myocardial infarction, stroke, coronary revascularization, or heart failure, identified through electronic medical record review. The enriched sampling strategy contributed to the higher observed event proportion. From the eligible registry population, patients were sampled with stratification based on sex, diabetes mellitus, smoking status, and Agatston score category.

CT calcium score image acquisition and pre-processing

All CACS images were acquired with electrocardiogram-gated non-contrast protocols (120 kVp, 30 mAs, in-plane 0.5 ×0.5 mm, 2.5-mm slice thickness) using Brilliance iCT Philips scanner with soft-kernel filtered back projection reconstruction. Expert cardiologists annotated coronary calcifications for Agatston and calcium-omics and were reviewed by a third reader. Original CT volumes (~55 axial slices at 512 × 512) were resampled using trilinear interpolation and resized to 256 × 256 × 64 voxels for computational efficiency. Image orientation was standardized to the Right-Anterior-Superior coordinate system, and intensities were clipped to [−1024, 2000] HU and scaled to [0, 1].

Feature sets and extraction

As summarized in Fig. 1, we derived three feature categories from CACS images and electronic health record. For model comparisons, tabular inputs were evaluated using progressively inclusive feature sets: Clinical, Clinical + Agatston, and Full tabular features (Clinical+Agatston+calcium-omics). The Full tabular model served as the primary tabular comparator because it combined reliably available clinical variables with directly observed CACS-derived predictors, rather than relying on clinical variables alone.

Clinical features. Clinical covariates used for model training were sex, age, diabetes mellitus, and smoking status, which were reliably available in the analytic cohort. Other metabolic markers (e.g., cholesterol, blood pressure, and body mass index) are summarized in baseline comparisons but were excluded in model training due to high rate of missingness. To avoid the bias from multiple imputation of primary risk factors, we focused on evaluating the incremental value of DL-derived representations over a core set of reliably documented clinical features.

Tabular survival baseline: DeepSurv

We used DeepSurv as the tabular reference model because it preserves the Cox PH framework while allowing nonlinear modeling amongst features [25]. However, it is to be noted that DeepSurv, by its design, is limited to processing tabular inputs only, while our hybrid DL approach (Section 2.5) incorporates DL-derived imaging features. Hybrid deep learning and architecture

Architecturally, the proposed hybrid model is distinguished from DeepSurv by its input representation. In hybrid DL approach, DL-imaging embeddings are concatenated with the tabular embeddings and passed through the same DeepSurv head Cox partial log-likelihood loss. Removing the imaging branch reduces the hybrid to DeepSurv on tabular data, so any performance gain over the baseline isolates the incremental prognostic value of DL-derived imaging features.

The image branch encoded each CACS volume with DenseNet–121, followed by adaptive average pooling and flattening to form 516 or 1024-dimensional vector (base vs. large configuration). This vector was linearly projected to 128-dimensional image embeddings to regularize capacity and align scales with tabular branch. Tabular inputs (clinical features, Agatston scores, and calcium-omics) underwent min-max scaling for continuous variables and one-hot encoding for categorical variables, then passed through multi-layer perceptron (MLP) to produce a 128-dimensional tabular embedding. The two 128-dimensional embeddings – imaging and tabular – were concatenated into 256-dimensional representations and fed to DeepSurv head that outputs the log-risks of MACE. Training uses the same negative partial log-likelihood as Section 2.4, ensuring same methodology with the tabular-only DeepSurv baseline and isolating the contribution of DL-derived imaging features from CACS. Model training, cross-validation, and temporal validation

We performed five-fold, event-stratified cross-validation as the primary internal validation strategy. To mitigate overfitting and prevent information leakage, model selection and all preprocessing steps were performed independently within each training fold, and validation folds held strictly separate throughout training and evaluation. Models were trained for up to 200 epochs with a batch size of 4 using Adam optimizer (learning rate: 0.0001). No data augmentation was applied. The model state with the highest validation C-index was retained for each fold (early stopping by model selection). As an additional robustness analysis, we performed a temporally separated hold-out validation experiment. Patients scanned during 2014–2018 (n = 1127, events=175) were used for model development, and patients scanned during 2019–2020 (n = 823, events=64) were reserved as a distinct test cohort. Because follow-up duration was shorter in the 2019–2020 cohort, temporal validation emphasized C-index and 1-year time-dependent AUCs. Longer-horizon 2- and 3-year AUCs were not estimated as insufficient follow-up precluded valid calculation. Results from temporal validation are reported in the Supplementary Materials. All experiments were conducted on two NVIDIA A40 GPUs on Linux HPC system using Python 3.10.8, PyTorch 2.5.0, CUDA 12.1, MONAI 1.4.0. Preprocessing, feature selection, and scalar fitting were performed within each training fold only to prevent information leakage.

Performance metrics and statistical analysis

Discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s C-index and time-dependent area under the curve (AUC). Given the median follow-up of 569 days, time-dependent AUCs were evaluated at 1, 2, and 3-years, corresponding to 1347, 773, and 363 patients at risk, respectively. Time horizons beyond were not analyzed due to limited numbers at risk. The C-index measures concordant ranking of event times, whereas time-dependent AUC captures sensitivity and specificity at clinically relevant horizons. For both C-index and mean AUCs, fold-wise means and 95% confidence interval (CI) were computed from patient-level bootstrapping with 1000 iterations. All models were trained and evaluated using identical cross-validation splits for fair comparisons.

For clinical interpretability, patients were stratified at the median predicted risk and plotted Kaplan-Meier curves with 95% CI and hazard ratio (HR) were computed. Baseline clinical characteristics were compared between MACE and non-MACE groups, with continuous variables reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group differences were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U-test for continuous variables and Chi-square test for categorical variables. Clinical variables with substantial missingness were included in baseline comparisons but excluded from model training to avoid bias from imputation. Between-model differences in C-indices were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to account for the non-normal distribution of fold-wise estimates. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted in the identical Python environment described above.

Results

Among 2199 patients from the registry, 1950 were included in this retrospective MACE-enriched analytic cohort (Table 1). The median follow-up was 569 days (interquartile range, [IQR]: 287–971 days). During follow-up, 239 patients (12.3%) experienced MACE and 1711 (87.7%) remained MACE-free. Baseline clinical characteristics of patients with and without MACE are summarized in Table 1. Among 239 patients with MACE, first qualifying events included myocardial infarction (n = 57), stroke (n = 79), coronary revascularization (n = 69), and heart failure (n = 34). The MACE group (n = 239) and non-MACE group (n = 1711) differed significantly across several variables. Patients with MACE were older (65.7 vs. 59.9 years), had higher Agatston scores (411.7 vs. 193.2), and had a higher prevalence of diabetes (26.8% vs. 13.0%) and smoking (57.3% vs. 26.9%). Statistically significant differences were also observed for sex, systolic blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, and total cholesterol. No significant differences were observed in BMI or diastolic blood pressure.

Across both tabular-only and hybrid models, discrimination increased with progressive inclusion of features from (Clinical) to (Clinical+Ag) and (Full) sets (Fig. 3). For DeepSurv (tabular) baseline, mean C-indices improved from 0.736 for (Clinical) to 0.759 for (Clinical+Ag), and to 0.789 for (Full). Incorporation of DL-derived imaging features increased C-indices across all feature sets, with the hybrid model achieving 0.743 for (Clinical), 0.763 for (Clinical+Ag), and 0.802 for (Full). For the Full feature set, the hybrid model achieved a statistically significantly but numerically modest improvement in C-index compared with the tabular-only model (0.802, CI: (0.735–0.870) vs. 0.789, CI: 0.729–0.850; p < 0.05). Across folds, the hybrid model achieved higher C-indices than tabular baselines in 4 of 5 folds for (Clinical+Ag) and (Full) feature sets. Fold-wise C-indices are reported in Supplementary Table S2.

Time-dependent discrimination assessed by AUCs at 1–3 year horizons showed similar trends across feature sets (Fig. 4). For the DeepSurv (tabular) baseline, mean AUCs averaged over the three time points increased from 0.740 (95% CI: 0.659–0.820) with (Clinical) features to 0.748 (0.692–0.804) for (Clinical+Ag), and 0.762 (0.701–0.822) for the (Full) feature set. The hybrid model incorporating DL-derived imaging features yielded higher mean AUCs for all configurations, with values of 0.756 (0.670–0.842) for (Clinical), 0.765 (0.687–0.843) for (Clinical+Ag), and 0.802 (0.735–0.869) for (Full). Across all three horizons, the hybrid model maintained higher AUCs than the tabular-only baseline for the (Clinical+Ag) and (Full) feature sets (Fig. 4B-C).

Temporal held-out validation was performed using scans from 2014 to 2018 for model development and scans from 2019 to 2020 exclusively for testing (Supplementary Table S3). On the temporal test set, tabular-only performance improved with progressive feature inclusion, reaching a C-index of 0.7785 and 1-year AUC of 0.7845. The image-only deep learning model showed lower discrimination (C-index: 0.6439; 1-year AUC: 0.6625) as compared to tabular, whereas the Full hybrid model achieved the highest performance (C-index: 0.7829; 1-year AUC: 0.7919). All performance results were approximately within ranges of our previous 5-fold cross-validation, suggesting minimal impact of any instrumentation or population changes over time.

Kaplan–Meier analysis survival curves for the representative best-performing fold is stratified at median predicted risk and shown in Fig. 5. In this fold, the hybrid model (HR: 18.12; 95% CI: 5.63–29.38) demonstrated a wider separation between risk strata compared to the tabular baseline (HR: 5.96; 95% CI: 2.79–12.76). Log-rank test showed significant differences between high and low-risk groups for both models (p < 0.0001). Across all five-folds, the median HR was 12.5 with a range of 8.7–18.1. Divergence between hybrid risk groups was observed within the first 12 months of follow-up.

Reclassification analysis showed that the modest C-index improvement corresponded to clinically relevant patient-level risk reassignment. In this representative fold, 32 patients (8.2%) were reclassified between risk categories after incorporation of DL-derived imaging features. Among 8 patients with subsequent MACE who were initially categorized as low-risk by the tabular baseline, the hybrid model correctly reclassified 6 (75%) individuals to the high-risk category. The hybrid model correctly down-classified non-MACE patients, improving specificity. Overall, the hybrid model achieved a net reclassification index of 0.119 (95% CI: 0.007–0.231, p = 0.037), and patients reclassified to high-risk had a significantly higher event rate than those remaining at low-risk (37.5% vs. 1.1%, p < 0.001).

Image encoder ablation results comparing base and large DenseNet–121 configurations are shown in Table 2. Across all tabular feature sets, the base encoder achieved higher mean C-indices with narrower 95% confidence intervals than the large variant. Using (Clinical) features, the base encoder achieved a mean C-index of 0.743 (95% CI: 0.657–0.828), compared with 0.662 (0.592–0.731) for the large encoder. Similar differences were observed for (Clinical+Ag) and the (Full) feature set, where the base encoder achieved the highest discrimination (0.802 (0.735–0.870) vs. 0.759 (0.694–0.825)). In contrast, in image-only settings without tabular inputs, the large encoder showed slightly higher performance (0.577 (0.497–0.657)) than the base encoder (0.549 (0.449–0.649)).Fold-wise C-indices are reported in Supplementary Table S4.

Grad-CAM visualizations were generated from multiple layers of the image encoder to examine regions contributing to MACE risk estimates (Fig. 6). Attention frequently localized to coronary calcifications (arrows in Rows 1 and 3) and adjacent cardiomediastinal region (e.g., myocardium and proximal great vessels). In several cases (Row 2), activations extended along the pericardial sac, overlapping regions anatomically consistent with epicardial adipose tissue. Earlier layers produced more diffuse activations, whereas deeper layers produced more spatially compact patterns. Rows 1–2 exhibited more concentrated activations near the heart and proximal aorta, while Rows 3 exhibited more broader, lower-intensity patterns.

Discussion

Extending our previous work on CACS imaging [6,26-29], this proof-of-concept study suggests that incorporating DL representations from CACS along with clinical features, Agatston score, and hand-crafted calcium-omics descriptors can provide improvements in time-to-event modeling of MACE relative to a tabular-only baseline. We also observed that progressively expanding the tabular feature set improved discrimination in both the hybrid and tabular models, corroborating that detailed descriptors of calcification burden improve risk stratifications [29-31]. Across all feature sets, hybrid model consistently outperformed tabular-only models, suggesting that learned imaging representations complement, rather than replace, established hand-crafted features [32-34]. Because the hybrid and tabular models differed only in the inclusion of the image branch, the performance gains can be attributed to the incremental prognostic information in CACS representations. Nevertheless, the absolute improvement is modest and the representational overlap between learned and pre-defined features cannot be fully disentangled. Thus, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary.

Time-dependent analysis further provided insight into the clinical relevance of this incremental information. The hybrid model showed consistently higher AUCs during the first three years of follow-up, a window that aligns with the cohort’s median follow-up. Kaplan-Meier analyses demonstrated clear separation between predicted risk strata within this window, suggesting that hybrid approach may help identify patients at higher risk in near term. Beyond three years, heavy censoring reduces certainty, and apparent widening of risk curves at later time points should therefore be interpreted conservatively.

Encoder ablation experiments illustrated that a compact DenseNet outperformed a higher-capacity variant when fused with tabular inputs. This finding is consistent with previous multimodal survival studies [35] and suggest that, in presence of strong structured covariates, larger image encoders may introduce redundancy or overfitting rather than complementary prognostic information.

Visualizations of Grad-CAM were used as exploratory tools to examine regions contributing to model predictions. Activations often extended beyond coronary calcifications to include myocardium and proximal great vessels, and regions consistent with epicardial or pericoronary adipose tissue. These observations are concordant with growing evidence that epicardial adipose tissue and broader cardiac morphology contribute to cardiovascular risk [9,12,14,36,37]. However, because the model was trained on whole CACS volumes without anatomical constraints, the learned representations may reflect a mixture of coronary and extra-coronary information, which complicates mechanistic interpretation. Activations involving extra-cardiac osseous structures, such as vertebral bodies and the sternum, were also observed in some cases. Although prior studies have reported association between skeletal health, bone mineral density, vascular calcification, and cardiovascular risk [38-41], these activations may reflect confounding from age-related anatomy, bone-density differences, field-of-view effects, or other correlated imaging features rather than an independent biological pathway linking skeletal and vascular disease. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory, and anatomically constrained or prospective studies are needed to determine whether such regions contain independent prognostic information.

From a clinical perspective, the hybrid model may be best viewed as a complementary risk-refinement tool rather than a replacement for established clinical, Agatston, and calcium-omics features. Although the C-index gain was modest, the reclassification findings suggest that DL-derived CACS representations may help identify selected patients whose risk to MACE is underestimated by tabular features alone. In practice, such upward reclassification could support closer follow-up, more intensive preventive therapy discussions, or additional risk assessment in selected patients.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective, single-center design and dependence on internal cross-validation limits generalizability and may introduce optimistic bias. Although the primary tabular comparator included Agatston score and calcium-omics features, the clinical covariate set was limited by missing data and excluded several guideline-relevant risk factors, including lipid measures and blood pressure. The benefit over contemporary clinical risk factors remains uncertain. The number of observed MACE events, while sufficient for proof-of-concept evaluation, highlights the need for validation in larger and more diverse populations. Future work could explore more advanced fusion strategies and investigation of generative approaches to further amplify prognostic imaging signal without additional acquisition [42].

In conclusion, we established the feasibility of integrating DL-derived representations from non-contrast CACS with hand-crafted imaging features and clinical data for survival modeling of MACE. The hybrid approach demonstrates improved discrimination and reclassification, supporting its potential clinical utility. These findings suggest that advanced deep learning applied to low-cost, opportunistic CACS imaging may improve cardiovascular risk assessment and inform clinical decision-making.

Summary

In this study, using non-contrast CTCS images, we developed a hybrid deep-learning survival model to predict major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). DenseNet–121 features from CTCS were integrated with tabular features (e.g., clinical, Agatston, and calcium-omics descriptors) within a DeepSurv framework. The hybrid model improved discrimination versus a tabular DeepSurv baseline (C-index 0.802 vs. 0.789), with higher, more stable 1–3-year time-dependent AUCs. The hybrid model correctly reclassified 6 of 8 MACE cases otherwise assigned to lower risk by DeepSurv (net reclassification index 11.9%). Our study highlights the potential of DL-derived features from CTCS to improve MACE prediction beyond established metrics.
