Two Variations on the XTrace Algorithm
Eric Hallman

Abstract




Abstract
: This paper studies two potential modifications of
XTrace
(Epperly et al., SIMAX 45(1):1-23, 2024), a randomized algorithm for estimating the trace of a matrix. The first is a variance reduction step that averages the output of
XTrace
over right-multiplications of the test vectors by random orthogonal matrices. The second is to form a low-rank approximation to the matrix using the whole Krylov space produced by the test vectors, rather than the output of a single power iteration as is used by
XTrace
. Experiments on synthetic data show that the first modification offers only slight benefits in practice, while the second can lead to significant improvements depending on the spectrum of the matrix.

1
Introduction

We’d like to estimate the trace of a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
which can be accessed only via matrix-vector products (matvecs)
\bm{\omega}\mapsto\mathbf{A}\bm{\omega}
. One simple method is to use the Girard-Hutchinson estimator
[
5
]
: if
\bm{\omega}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\omega}\bm{\omega}^{T}]=\mathbf{I}_{N}
, then

\mathbb{E}[\bm{\omega}^{T}\mathbf{A}\bm{\omega}]=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\bm{\omega}\bm{\omega}^{T})]=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\bm{\omega}\bm{\omega}^{T}])=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}).

More sophisticated methods reduce the variance of the estimator by constructing a low-rank approximation
\mathbf{A}\approx\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}
and using the identity

\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A})=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q})+\mathbb{E}[\bm{\omega}^{T}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{T})\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{T})\bm{\omega}].

For a little extra variance reduction, Epperly et al.
[
2
]
recommend a normalization step: first, draw the vectors from a spherically symmetric distribution such as
\bm{\omega}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{N})
. Then, rescale the vectors
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{T})\bm{\omega}
to have norm
\sqrt{N-\rank(\mathbf{Q})}
.

Meyer et al.
[
8
]
showed that when
\mathbf{A}
is positive definite, it suffices to compute
\mathbf{Q}
from a single power iteration
\mathbf{Q}=\orth(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega})
. However, to naïvely use the same set of test vectors
\mathbf{\Omega}
for both the low-rank approximation
\mathbf{Q}
and the Girard-Hutchinson estimator would produce a biased estimate. One solution is to use different sets of test vectors
\mathbf{\Omega}_{1}
and
\mathbf{\Omega}_{2}
for each step: in
[
8
, Alg. 1]

\mathbf{\Omega}_{1}
and
\mathbf{\Omega}_{2}
are set to have the same number of columns, and Persson et al.
[
10
]
propose an adaptive method to more efficiently allocate the test vectors.

Epperly et al.
[
2
]
made the key insight that no such compromise is needed. Their
XTrace
estimator uses all of the test vectors for both low-rank approximation and the Girard-Hutchinson estimator, using a leave-one-out technique to ensure that the estimator remains unbiased. Algorithm
1
provides a simple implementation; the authors also show how to compute each of the bases
\mathbf{Q}_{(i)}
efficiently so that, for
m
test vectors, the overall method requires only
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}N)
operations besides the matvecs
\mathbf{\Omega}\mapsto\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega}
.

1.1
Contributions

This paper proposes two modifications to the
XTrace
estimator that will further reduce its variance without adding bias.

1.

Average the output of
XTrace
over multiple random rotations
\mathbf{\Omega}\mapsto\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{U}
.

2.

Orthogonalize each
\bm{\omega}_{i}
against
[(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega})_{-i},\,\mathbf{\Omega}_{-i}]
, not just
(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega})_{-i}
.

Neither of these proposals requires any additional matvecs with
\mathbf{A}
.

We also consider the more general question: what invariance properties must an optimal trace estimator have? For the purposes of this paper, a trace estimator
\est_{\mathbf{A}}(\cdot)
could be called “optimal” if it is admissible within the class

\mathcal{F}:=\{g(\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{A}^{2}\mathbf{\Omega})\ :\ \mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{A}^{2}\mathbf{\Omega})]=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A})\}.

(1)

That is,
\est_{\mathbf{A}}(\cdot)\in\mathcal{F}
, and there is no other estimator
\est_{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}(\cdot)
satisfying
\var(\est_{\mathbf{A}}^{\prime}(\mathbf{\Omega}))\leq\var(\est_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega}))
for all
\mathbf{A}
, with strict inequality for at least one
\mathbf{A}
.

We observe that such an estimator must be
basis-invariant
in that it can be expressed as a function of
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})
, and we show in
theorem

5
that our modified algorithm has certain invariance properties that
XTrace
does not.

1.2
Related work and commentary on optimality conditions

It is worth mentioning that a variety of trace estimators exist that are not in the class of functions defined in
eq.

1
.

First, we could permit biased estimators, comparing them by mean-squared error instead of variance. One such estimator follows from the Variance-reduced Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature proposed by Bhattacharjee et al.
[
1
, Thm. 5]
, which constructs a Krylov space and deflates sufficiently-converged eigenvalues, resulting in a very small but nonzero bias. More generally, when computing the trace of some matrix function such as
f(\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{A}^{-1}
or
\exp(\mathbf{A})
, it is common to approximate the function using Chebyshev polynomials or quadrature. Truly unbiased estimators exist
[
11
]
, but have not gotten much attention in the literature on trace estimation.

Second, the requirement that
\mathbf{A}
be accessible strictly through matvecs precludes probing methods, which in addition to applying matvecs will partition sparse matrices using graph-coloring algorithms (see
[
4
]
and the references therein). It also precludes multilevel methods such as in Frommer et al.
[
3
]
, which are effective when
f(\mathbf{A})
can be approximated using a multigrid hierarchy.

Third, we could consider adaptive methods for choosing how to apply the matvecs: given a budget on the number of matvecs, matrix loads, and memory, one could define

\est_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega})=g(\mathbf{Y}_{0},\mathbf{Y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{Y}_{q-1}),

where
\mathbf{Y}_{0}=\mathbf{\Omega}
,
\mathbf{Y}_{1}=\mathbf{A}\cdot g_{1}(\mathbf{Y}_{0})
,
\mathbf{Y}_{2}=\mathbf{A}\cdot g_{2}(\mathbf{Y}_{0},\mathbf{Y}_{1})
, and so on. This function class would be broad enough to accommodate the adaptive method proposed in
[
10
]
, or the multi-polynomial method in
[
6
]
which approximates
f(\mathbf{A})
using polynomials of varying degree.

1.3
XTrace is not rotation-invariant

The
XTrace
estimator is symmetric with respect to the test vectors
\bm{\omega}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\omega}_{m}
. Epperly et al., citing Halmos
[
7
]
, note that any minimum-variance unbiased trace estimator must have this property. However,
XTrace
is not invariant under arbitrary rotations
\mathbf{\Omega}\mapsto\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{U}
. For a reproducible example, consider the inputs

\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}5&0&0&0&0\\
0&4&0&0&0\\
0&0&3&0&0\\
0&0&0&2&0\\
0&0&0&0&1\\
\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{\Omega}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1/2\\
0&1/2\\
0&1/2\\
0&1/2\\
\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{U}_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}.

fig.

1
shows the output of
algorithm

1
when the random samples are replaced by
\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{U}_{\theta}
. As
\theta
ranges over
[0,\pi/2]
, the trace estimate oscillates. Thus by taking the average output over
\theta
, we could obtain an estimator with lower variance.

Notation

For a matrix
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{X}_{-i}
denotes
\mathbf{X}
but with the
i
th column removed and
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{X})
denotes the column space of
\mathbf{X}
. The vector
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the
i
th standard basis vector.
\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}
is the Moore-Penrose inverse, equal to
(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}
when
\mathbf{X}
has full column rank.
\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{Y}
is the Kronecker product of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
, having
(i,j)
block entry equal to
\mathbf{X}_{ij}\mathbf{Y}
.

A “random orthogonal” matrix always means one sampled from the Haar distribution.
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{N})
is the standard multivariate Gaussian distribution, and
\chi_{N}^{2}
is the chi-squared distribution with
N
degrees of freedom.

3
Basis-invariant estimators

Let
\est_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega})
be a trace estimator with finite variance. The law of total variance states that

\var(\est_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega}))=\mathbb{E}[\var(\est_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega})|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega}))]+\var\left(\mathbb{E}[\est_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega})|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})]\right),

and the law of total expectation states that

\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\est_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega})|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})]]=\mathbb{E}[\est_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega})].

Therefore, by conditioning on
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})
and taking expectations, we can obtain an estimator with the same expectation and lesser-or-equal variance. Such an estimator is also
basis-invariant
: it depends only on
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})
, and transformations
\mathbf{\Omega}\mapsto\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{B}
will not affect the output for any invertible matrix
\mathbf{B}
.

When
\mathbf{\Omega}
has i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries, we can use a standard result about the distributions of such matrices
[
9
]
to express the conditional expectation in a cleaner form.

Lemma 1
.

If
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m}
has i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries, then it has distribution equal to a product of three independent terms
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R}
, where

•

\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m}
has orthonormal columns and is Haar-distributed on the Stiefel manifold
V_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
;

•

\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is orthogonal and Haar-distributed;

•

\mathbf{R}
is upper triangular with independent entries
\mathbf{R}_{ii}\sim\chi_{N-i+1}^{2}
,
\mathbf{R}_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and consequently
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R}]=N\cdot\mathbf{I}_{m}
.

Furthermore, conditioning on
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})
is equivalent to conditioning on
\mathbf{Q}
.

Thus in principle, we could always estimate
\mathbb{E}[\est_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega})|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})]
by fixing an orthonormal basis
\mathbf{Q}
for
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})
and repeatedly sampling
\est_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R})
for random
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{R}
. Depending on the estimator, it may be possible to simplify the expression further. In the case of the plain Girard-Hutchinson estimator, for example, we can compute the conditional expectation exactly.

Theorem 2
.

If
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m}
has i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries and
\mathbf{Q}
is any orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{1}{m}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{\Omega}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega})|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{Q})\right]=\tfrac{N}{m}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}).

Proof.

By
lemma

1
and the law of total expectation, we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{1}{m}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{\Omega}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega})|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})\right]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{U}^{T}\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R})|\mathbf{Q}]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{m}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{U}^{T}])

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{m}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{U}^{T}|\mathbf{R}]])

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{m}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{1}{m}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{T})\mathbf{I}_{m}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{m^{2}}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{R})\right]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{m^{2}}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{\Omega}\|_{F}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{m^{2}}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\right)\cdot mN

\displaystyle=\tfrac{N}{m}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}).

In the above, we used the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{U}^{T}]=\tfrac{1}{m}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{M})\mathbf{I}_{m}
for symmetric
\mathbf{M}
, and the linear and cyclic properties of the trace operator.

It is already generally known that first orthogonalizing
\mathbf{\Omega}
slightly reduces the variance of the Girard-Hutchinson estimator; this proof frames that result in terms of conditional expectations.

3.1
Application to XTrace

For
XTrace
, on the other hand, some numerical experiments suggest that

\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{XTrace}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R})|\mathbf{Q})]\neq\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{XTrace}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})|\mathbf{Q})]

(2)

in general. If so, then there is no result for
XTrace
exactly analogous to
theorem

2
which by taking expectations removes the dependence on
\mathbf{R}
entirely.

There is, however, an analogous result for
XTraceFull
. To derive it, we introduce
algorithm

4
as a computational primitive called
LeaveOneOutFull
. It uses the first
m-1
columns of
\mathbf{\Omega}
for deflation, and reserves the final column for the Girard-Hutchinson estimator.
XTraceFull
is then the average of
LeaveOneOutFull
over all column permutations
\mathbf{\Omega}\mapsto\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{\Pi}
. Similarly,
algorithm

3
describes a computational primitive
LeaveOneOut
for
XTrace
.

Lemma 3
.

For orthogonal
\mathbf{U}
and fixed
\mathbf{\Omega}
, the value of the functions

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle\mapsto\texttt{{LeaveOneOutFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{U}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}

\displaystyle\mapsto\texttt{{LeaveOneOut}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{U})

depend only on the final column of
\mathbf{U}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{u}
be the final column of
\mathbf{U}
. Since

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\left((\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{U})_{-m}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{e}_{m}\mathbf{e}_{m}^{T})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{T})\mathbf{U}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{T})\right),

and similarly for
\mathcal{R}\left((\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{U})_{-m}\right)
, the range of the orthonormal basis
\mathbf{Q}
computed by
algorithm

4
or
algorithm

3
is determined entirely by
\mathbf{u}
for any given
\mathbf{\Omega}
. After that, the algorithms compute

\bm{\mu}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{T})\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{e}_{m}=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{T})\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{u},

which again depends only on
\mathbf{u}
.

Lemma 4
.

For any invertible block upper triangular matrix
\mathbf{R}
with block sizes
(m-1,1)
,

\texttt{{LeaveOneOutFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega})=\texttt{{LeaveOneOutFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{R}).

This result does not hold for
LeaveOneOut
.

Proof.

algorithm

4
effectively computes the unpivoted QR factorization

[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega}_{-m},\,\mathbf{\Omega}]=[\mathbf{Q},\,\bm{\mu}]\widehat{\mathbf{R}}.

The transformation
\mathbf{\Omega}\mapsto\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{R}
does not alter either
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{Q})
or
\mathcal{R}(\bm{\mu})
, and so the output of
algorithm

4
is unchanged. The same cannot be said of
algorithm

3
because

\mathcal{R}([\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega}_{-m},\,\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{e}_{n}])\neq\mathcal{R}([\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega}_{-m},\,\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{r}])

for general nonzero vectors
\mathbf{r}
.

We are now set up to provide our main result about
XTraceFull
.

Theorem 5
.

If
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m}
has i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries and
\mathbf{Q}
is any orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{XTraceFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega})|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{LeaveOneOutFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})|\mathbf{Q}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{XTraceFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})|\mathbf{Q}],

for random orthogonal
\mathbf{U}
. The value of
\texttt{{LeaveOneOutFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})
depends only on
\mathbf{Q}
and the last column of
\mathbf{U}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{\Pi}
be a random permutation. Use, in order, the fact that
\mathbf{\Omega}
and
\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{\Pi}
have the same distribution conditional on
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})
,
lemma

1
, and
lemma

4
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{XTraceFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega})|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{LeaveOneOutFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{\Pi})|\mathbf{\Omega}]|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{LeaveOneOutFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{\Omega})|\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{LeaveOneOutFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R})|\mathbf{Q}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{LeaveOneOutFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})|\mathbf{Q}].

The second equality follows from the fact that
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Pi}
have the same distribution:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{XTraceFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})|\mathbf{Q}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{LeaveOneOutFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Pi})|\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{U}]|\mathbf{Q}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{LeaveOneOutFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})|\mathbf{Q}].

The final claim is just restating the result of
lemma

3
.

Thus for
LeaveOneOutFull
, as for the plain Girard-Hutchinson estimator in
theorem

2
, the expected output conditional on
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Omega})
can be expressed in a form that makes no reference to the triangular factor
\mathbf{R}
.

3.2
Sampling strategies

To compute
\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{XTraceFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})|\mathbf{Q}]
or estimate it, there are a few options worth considering:

•

Express
\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{LeaveOneOut}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})|\mathbf{Q}]
as an integral over the sphere
\mathcal{S}^{m-1}
and compute it via quadrature. This is feasible if the number of test vectors
m
is very small (say,
2\leq m\leq 4
).

•

Approximate
\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{LeaveOneOut}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})|\mathbf{Q}]
by sampling random unit vectors
\mathbf{u}
. This will come at a marginal cost of
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
operations per sample.

•

Approximate
\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{XTraceFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})|\mathbf{Q}]
by sampling random orthogonal matrices
\mathbf{U}
. It is plausible that orthonormal vectors will yield negatively correlated estimates, which could make up for the added orthogonalization costs compared to sampling vectors independently.

•

Approximate
\mathbb{E}[\texttt{{XTraceFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})|\mathbf{Q}]
by starting with
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{m}
and transforming it using a sequence of random Givens rotations (Kac’s random walk) or Householder reflections.

Implemented properly, the computational cost of any of these methods will be independent of the dimension
N
of
\mathbf{A}
.

4
Practical implementation

This section will cover how to compute the value of
\texttt{{LeaveOneOut}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})
given the last column
\mathbf{u}
of
\mathbf{U}
. The extension to evaluating
\texttt{{XTraceFull}}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{U})
given a whole matrix
\mathbf{U}
is straightforward.

First, compute the QR factorization

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Omega}&\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{0}&\mathbf{Q}_{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{0}&\mathbf{M}\\
&\mathbf{R}_{1}\end{bmatrix},

and right-multiply both block columns by
\mathbf{R}_{0}^{-1}
to get the same factorization over a different set of starting vectors,

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{0}&\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{0}\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{0}&\mathbf{Q}_{1}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m}&\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}_{0}^{-1}\\
&\mathbf{R}_{1}\mathbf{R}_{0}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{R}}.

(3)

Given a vector
\mathbf{u}
, we’d like to cheaply find an orthonormal basis for the reduced matrix

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{0}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\dagger})&\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{0}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\dagger})\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}\left(\mathbf{I}_{2m}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}&\\
&\mathbf{u}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}&\\
&\mathbf{u}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}\right)

The two vectors removed from the basis can be expressed in the form
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{S}
for some
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2}
, and must satisfy

\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{S}\,\bot\,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}\left(\mathbf{I}_{2m}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}&\\
&\mathbf{u}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}&\\
&\mathbf{u}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}\right)

One solution is given by

\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{R}^{-T}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{u}&\\
&\mathbf{u}\end{bmatrix}.

Specifically, the first and second columns are the vectors that would be left out by removing
\mathbf{Q}_{0}\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{0}\mathbf{u}
, respectively, from
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{0}&\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{0}\end{bmatrix}
.

The result
\mathbf{S}
does not in general have orthogonal columns, so find the factorization
\mathbf{S}=\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{L}
, where
\mathbf{L}
is
lower
triangular. Why lower triangular? Because in order to find
\bm{\mu}
in
algorithm

4
, we effectively want the unpivoted QR factorization

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{\bot}&\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{0}\mathbf{U}_{\bot}&\mathbf{Q}_{0}\mathbf{u}&\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{0}\mathbf{u}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}_{(\mathbf{u})}&\bm{\mu}&\mathbf{Q}\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{2}\end{bmatrix},

where
[\mathbf{U}_{\bot},\,\mathbf{u}]
is orthogonal. The final column
\mathbf{Q}\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{2}
is the result of orthogonalizing
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{0}\mathbf{u}
against all the other columns, and so will be parallel to
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{s}_{2}
. But we don’t want to orthogonalize
\mathbf{Q}_{0}\mathbf{u}
against
all
other columns, just the ones that form
\mathbf{Q}_{(\mathbf{u})}
. So to find
\bm{\mu}
(parallel to
\mathbf{Q}\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1}
), we have to orthogonalize
\mathbf{s}_{1}
against
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{2}
.

Now to compute the trace estimate. As a preprocessing step, define

\mathbf{H}:=\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q},

(4)

noting that the first
m
columns of
\mathbf{H}
were already computed in
eq.

3
. We then find that

\displaystyle\widehat{\operatorname{tr}}_{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{Q}_{(\mathbf{u})}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{(\mathbf{u})})+\bm{\nu}^{T}\mathbf{A}\bm{\nu}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{I}-\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}^{T})\mathbf{Q}^{T}\right)+(N-\rank(\mathbf{Q}))\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{Q}^{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H})-\operatorname{tr}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}^{T}\mathbf{H}\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}\right)+(N-\rank(\mathbf{Q}))\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{H}\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H})-\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{2}^{T}\mathbf{H}\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{2}+(N-\rank(\mathbf{Q})-1)\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{H}\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{1}.

The end result is presented as
algorithm

5
, where the outer loop averages the result over multiple random rotations
\mathbf{\Omega}\mapsto\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{U}
. As with
XTrace
, the loops can be eliminated through vectorization, unless the samples are being computed iteratively via Givens rotations or Householder reflections as mentioned in
section

3.2
. In the latter case, only the inner loop can be eliminated.

5
Numerical experiments

This section presents a comparison of the estimators
XTrace
and
XTraceFull
(both with and without resampling) on a variety of synthetic examples. In all cases we fix the matrix dimension
N=1000
, sample
\mathbf{\Omega}
with i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
entries, and report the root-mean-squared relative error over 1000 trials. For each trial, each estimator will use the same set of test vectors
\mathbf{\Omega}
. The variant of
XTraceFull
with resampling will right-multiply
\mathbf{\Omega}
by
25
randomly sampled orthogonal matrices, the first of which will always be the identity matrix. Code for
XTraceFull
is given in
, and the experiments were run using Octave 6.2.0.

We use diagonal matrices with six choices for the eigenvalues
\bm{\lambda}
:

•

flat
:
\bm{\lambda}=(3-2(i-1)/(N-1):i=1,2,\ldots,N)
;

•

poly
:
\bm{\lambda}=(i^{-2}:i=1,2,\ldots,N)
;

•

inv-poly
:
\bm{\lambda}=(2-i^{-2}:i=1,2,\ldots,N)
;

•

exp
:
\bm{\lambda}=(0.7^{i}:i=0,1,\ldots,N-1)
;

•

step
:
\bm{\lambda}=(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{\text{50 times}},\underbrace{10^{-3},\ldots,10^{-3}}_{N-50\text{ times}})
;

•

step-decay
:
\bm{\lambda}=(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{\text{50 times}},51^{-2},\ldots,N^{-2})
.

Results are presented in
fig.

2
. For four of the six examples, the results are largely the same for all three methods. For the other two,
inv-poly
and
step
,
XTraceFull
performs significantly better than
XTrace
. In the first case, it is because the outlying eigenvalues are the ones
closer
to zero, which the Krylov space
[\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega}]
can detect significantly better than the space
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega}
can. The second case is due to the lagging eigenvalues being precisely equal to one another, in which case a sufficiently large Krylov space captures the leading eigenvalues perfectly. When the lagging values are modified to decay slowly (example
step-decay
), the difference between the methods vanishes.

6
Conclusion and future work

We have proposed two changes to
XTrace
that will reduce its variance without adding bias or requiring any additional matrix-vector products. In practice, the benefits are minor except for matrices with certain distributions of eigenvalues.

The techniques outlined in
section

4
could potentially be extended to cases where we deflate vectors over a larger Krylov space
[\mathbf{\Omega},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{\Omega},\ldots,\mathbf{A}^{q-1}\mathbf{\Omega}]
. This can be useful in cases where we want to estimate the trace of
f(\mathbf{A})
and matvecs with
f(\mathbf{A})
are approximated by matrix polynomials, e.g., representing the number of triangles in a graph as
\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}^{3})
.
