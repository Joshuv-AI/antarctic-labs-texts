Implementation of antimicrobial stewardship interventions on prescribing rates for urinary tract infections in residential aged care facilities: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Antonina Maria Lin, Nadine Hillock, John Turnidge, Jaklin Eliott, Nicola Spurrier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and rationaleCurrent research identifies a large proportion of urinary antimicrobial prescribing in aged care as being non-concordant, however, the efficacy of interventions targeted at reducing these prescriptions in aged care remains unclear. This review summarises effective interventions targeting non-concordant urinary antimicrobial prescriptions in aged care.MethodsThis review follows PRISMA reporting guidelines and includes studies published up to June 2024. The search aimed to capture interventional studies and included studies that examined urinary tract infections in the aged care setting. All hospital-based studies and non-interventional studies were excluded. The search was conducted in 6 databases: PubMed, Scopus, Embase, PsychInfo, CINAHL Ultimate, and AgeLine. All studies were assessed against the ROBINS-I risk of bias tool. Meta-analyses for effects on urinary prescribing and hospitalisations were conducted on eligible studies and pooled risk ratios were calculated using DerSimonian and Laird random effects modelling.ResultsTwenty studies were included and interventions from four eligible randomised controlled trials were selected for the meta-analysis. The meta-analyses indicate that educational interventions coupled with routine audits were effective at reducing urinary prescribing (RR = 0.64, 95% CI = 0.49 – 0.83) and does not increase the risk of hospitalisations (RR = 1.02, 95% CI = 0.70 – 1.49). The review found that while effective interventions were heterogeneous in design, the majority included a combination of educational material(s) with routine audits and feedback for clinicians.DiscussionEducational interventions coupled with routine audits and feedback are effective at reducing inappropriate urinary prescribing in aged care and do not increase risk of hospitalisation. The synthesis of evidence in this review is limited by the heterogeneity of these tailored interventions and the lack of consistent reporting of changes in prescribing. However, the studies pooled in the meta-analysis can give some confidence in the efficacy and safety of some of these interventions.Systematic review registrationThis review is registered with the International Prospective Register for Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42024569226).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-026-03177-y.

Introduction

Background and rationale

Antimicrobial resistance is an emerging and growing health concern that threatens healthcare systems and economies globally [1]. Poor guideline concordance leading to the overprescription and inappropriate prescribing of antimicrobials plays an important role in driving antimicrobial resistance [2]. Residents of aged care facilities have several risk factors that increase the likelihood of receiving antimicrobial treatment, including cognitive impairment, comorbidities, and increased frequency in hospital admissions [3].

Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs) is a common infection observed in aged care settings but are also frequently over-diagnosed or mis-diagnosed; studies suggest UTIs account for 30%-50% of antimicrobial prescriptions in the elderly population but 40% are misdiagnoses [4, 5]. Moreover, routine urine dipstick tests in aged care perpetuates the overdiagnosis of UTIs which has led to many international initiatives to reduce routine dipstick tests, an example of which is ‘To Dip of Not to Dip’ in Australia [6]. Irrespective of the appropriateness of a prescription, the use of antimicrobials entails risks to the patient beyond the exacerbation of antimicrobial resistance, such as alterations to the patient’s own microflora and a subsequent misbalance of the immune system [7]. Despite the risks overprescribing poses to health at both a patient and population level, misconceptions still exist among older adults and their caregivers regarding antimicrobial use of UTIs and asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB), with many believing the benefits outweigh the harms [8]. Of concern is the development and spread of multi-drug-resistant organisms amongst residents and staff which could lead to significant challenges in the care of residents in aged care facilities [9].

Antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) refers to a group of actions or interventions used to promote the responsible management and use of antimicrobials, preventing inappropriate prescribing behaviours, reducing adverse effects and minimising antimicrobial resistance and healthcare costs [10]. AMS includes but is not limited to changes to organisational policies, the provision of clinical aids, guidelines and decision support tools, education to staff, family and/or patients, and the implementation of regular audits and feedback [11]. Facilities can establish a stewardship team which oversees the actions or appoint AMS champions on-site to further enhance program uptake [12].

A large proportion of the current literature investigating the effectiveness of AMS interventions were implemented in ambulatory or hospital settings, with most systematic reviews focusing on hospital-based AMS interventions [13, 14]. Moreover, of all systematic reviews summarising the effects of AMS interventions in an aged care setting, none have focussed on the effects AMS has on urinary-specific prescribing in aged care facilities. One review of interventions aimed at reducing antimicrobial prescribing in aged care suggests educational interventions in combination with on-site experts for audits seemed most effective and was supported in a similar review published two years later [15, 16]. However, these reviews examined the effect AMS interventions had on all antimicrobial prescriptions rather than focussing on urinary-specific prescribing or ASB. Generalisations of these findings into practice for other contexts cannot be assumed due to the complex nature of social norms and healthcare practices across countries and different settings and even in the treatment of other infections [17]. A summary of the impact AMS can have on all urinary-specific prescribing within the aged care setting has yet to be explored and published. This gap warrants a closer examination and summary of literature pertaining to AMS interventions and its effects on urinary-specific prescribing within the context of residential aged care facilities.

The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to identify and summarise types of AMS programs or interventions most effective at reducing inappropriate urinary prescribing for asymptomatic bacteriuria in residential aged care facilities. It aims to answer the following questions:

Are AMS interventions effective at reducing inappropriate urinary prescribing in aged care?

Are AMS interventions effective at reducing inappropriate urinary prescribing in aged care?

What types of interventions are effective at reducing inappropriate urinary prescribing in aged care?

What types of interventions are effective at reducing inappropriate urinary prescribing in aged care?

Methods

Design and registration

The systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted based on guidelines published in the Preferred Reporting for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guideline [18]. Guidelines and recommendations published under the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Review and Interventions were consulted as a guide for conducting this study [19]. The study was registered with the International Prospective Register for Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD42024569226.

Search strategy and sources

The following databases were searched: PubMed (1998–2024), Scopus (1991–2024), Embase – Ovid (1974–2024), PsychInfo – Ovid (1967–2024), CINAHL Ultimate – EBSCOhost (1998–2024), and AgeLine – EBSCOhost (2013–2024). The search included studies written in any language and published up to 30 June 2024. The full search strategy is included in Supplementary material 1.

Keywords, MeSH terms and Emtree entries along with their Boolean operatories (OR, AND) were used to capture the following key concepts of interest and their associated synonyms:

i.Residential Aged Care Facilities/Nursing Homes

Residential Aged Care Facilities/Nursing Homes

ii.Urinary Tract Infections/Asymptomatic Bacteriuria

Urinary Tract Infections/Asymptomatic Bacteriuria

iii.Antimicrobial Stewardship/Intervention

Antimicrobial Stewardship/Intervention

Additionally, a citation search based on references from literature identified following the initial screen was done to ensure all relevant studies were captured.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome, Timeframe (PICOT) framework was used to design an inclusion and exclusion criteria for this systematic review. The following study designs were eligible for inclusion:

i.Randomised Controlled Trials

ii.Quasi-experimental (controlled pre/post)

iii.Interrupted time series (before/after studies)

The initial search conducted in March 2024 identified a considerable heterogeneity in terms that could describe antimicrobial stewardship. Moreover, the recency of the term ‘Antimicrobial Stewardship’ had implications on the scope of literature that was captured initially; the MeSH and Emtree terms for antimicrobial stewardship were registered in 2013 which would limit the search to only capture studies that were published after the registration of the term in 2013. To avoid this limitation, the more generic term ‘intervention’ was included to broaden the search and capture relevant studies published prior to 2013.

There is literature suggesting that the focus of an antimicrobial stewardship intervention may be dependent on the specific conditions or definitions of UTI and asymptomatic bacteriuria being targeted [20]. This indicated the possibility of a large heterogeneity in terms used to describe asymptomatic bacteriuria in literature. To avoid any limitations based on definitions in the search, terms such as cystitis, asymptomatic bacteriuria, and abbreviations (ASB, ABU, UTI) were used.

Studies that examined UTIs amongst other infections (such as respiratory tract infections) were included if data pertaining to urinary prescribing could be extracted independently from other infections. Other systematic reviews, pharmacological studies, editorials and letters were excluded.

Screening and eligibility

The selection and inclusion of the studies were guided by PRISMA reporting guidelines [18]. Following the search for relevant literature, captured citations were deduplicated in Endnote and uploaded into Covidence, a web-based collaboration software platform for the management of systematic reviews [21]. Two independent reviewers screened title and abstracts separately prior to a full-text review for final inclusion. Disagreements were resolved through the involvement of a third reviewer.

Data extraction

The data collection form for interventional reviews (RCTs and non-RCTs) from The Cochrane Collaboration was adapted to include all relevant variables required for the aims of the systematic review. The extracted variable and associated headers were used on all included studies and were organised into RCTs and non-RCT study designs. The modified data extraction form was initially tested by two independent reviewers using two selected articles prior to the formal data extraction process, done independent by two reviewers. Authors of papers with missing or insufficient information were emailed to request additional data.

Risk of bias (quality) assessment

The Cochrane risk of bias tool, ROBINS-I tool, for interventional studies, was adapted to grade the quality of studies and evaluate the strength of evidence [22]. The risk of bias ratings for each signalling question (low, moderate, serious, critical) was converted into numerical values (1, 2, 3, 4) within a spreadsheet for ease of assessing overall study risk by calculating an average numerical value of risk. The completed ROBINS-I evaluation tool is included in the Supplementary material 2. The following definitions were used to define low, moder, serious, and critical risk of bias:

i.Low risk (1): The study is comparable to a well-performed randomised trial with regards to this signalling question.

Low risk (1): The study is comparable to a well-performed randomised trial with regards to this signalling question.

ii.Moderate risk (2): The study is sound for a non-randomised study but cannot be considered comparable to a well-performed randomised trial with regards to this signalling question.

Moderate risk (2): The study is sound for a non-randomised study but cannot be considered comparable to a well-performed randomised trial with regards to this signalling question.

iii.Serious risk (3): The study has some important problems with regards to this signalling question.

iv.Critical risk (4): The study is too problematic with regards to this signalling question to provide any useful evidence on the effects of this intervention.

Critical risk (4): The study is too problematic with regards to this signalling question to provide any useful evidence on the effects of this intervention.

v.N/A: Study did not provide evidence pertaining to this signalling question.

N/A: Study did not provide evidence pertaining to this signalling question.

Once an overall numerical rating was provided for the study, the studies were then recorded as being low, moderate, or high risk. The signalling questions and risk of bias assessment used was the same across all included studies regardless of study design and methodology.

Outcome effect measures

This systematic review considered the following outcomes:

i.Changes in total prescribed/administered antimicrobials for suspected UTIs.

Changes in total prescribed/administered antimicrobials for suspected UTIs.

ii.Changes in the proportion of prescriptions that are guideline concordant. These guidelines can be country-specific based on the location at which the study was conducted.

Changes in the proportion of prescriptions that are guideline concordant. These guidelines can be country-specific based on the location at which the study was conducted.

Data synthesis

Key information and outcomes from selected studies were summarised in Microsoft Excel. Data in the meta-analysis was pooled based on outcome measurements, including antimicrobial prescribing rates and hospitalisations. Due to inter-study heterogeneity, the meta-analysis was conducted using the DerSimonian and Laird random effects modelling. For both antimicrobial prescribing rates and hospitalisations, we calculated relative risks with 95% confidence intervals. We use the I [2] test to quantify the degree of statistical heterogeneity. Numerical calculations and analysis were performed on IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0 and JBI SUMARI [23]. Data that could not be analysed quantitatively was presented as a descriptive synthesis.

Results

2108 studies were captured in the search and following deduplication and screening by title/abstract and full-text, 20 studies were identified and included in the review for data extraction (Fig. 1). There were disagreements between the two independent reviewers for 3 studies, and a third reviewer was invited to assist in reaching a collective consensus where the 3 studies were subsequently excluded.

All studies included in this systematic review were conducted between 2005 and 2024 with the majority having been conducted in North America, and the remainder in various countries across Europe. A few studies (8/20) had reported on other secondary outcomes, most commonly reporting on rates of urinalysis/urine culturing [25, 27, 32, 34, 37, 38, 40, 42]. Other common secondary outcomes included mortality, hospitalisations, and adverse events. Only four studies reported on the rates of C. difficile as part of their secondary outcomes, with one of these studies also reporting on MRSA rates pre- and post-intervention [26, 37, 40, 41].

Intervention types

The type and number of AMS interventions implemented by the studies were varied and heterogeneous; most studies (16/20) used a form of educational intervention in conjunction with clinical tools or audits. All RCTs implemented and examined the effects of multifaceted interventions which employed the use of numerous activities promoting and reminding carers and staff members about the responsible use of antimicrobials.

Educational interventions

All educational studies targeted nursing home staff and physicians, and some (7/20) also provided educational materials to residents and family members. Thirteen studies provided educational training orally through a seminar or webinar [24, 25, 28, 29, 31, 32, 36–40, 42, 43]. Three studies included interactive activities as part of their educational seminars which involved the discussion of example case studies [24, 29, 39]. One study provided the option for individual detailing sessions administered by an AMS pharmacist [38]. Nine studies provided either a clinical decision-making tool or communication tool in conjunction with the educational materials provided and all nine studies reported significant reductions in urinary prescribing for presumed UTIs [24, 25, 28, 30, 37–41]. Some of the studies (5/20) included routine audits and feedback after the distribution of educational material with audits being focused on identifying non-concordant prescribing and providing individualised education and feedback to clinicians [24, 30, 31, 34, 37]. All educational interventions provided written material to staff and physicians in various forms including handouts, posters, and pocket cards. All but one study reported reductions in antimicrobial prescribing for UTIs, however, study authors also reported suboptimal intervention implementation fidelity with only 63.8% of physicians and 65% of nurses completing all educational programs provided as part of the intervention [36].

Urine culturing

Eight of the studies examined the effect of AMS intervention(s) on urine culturing as one of their secondary outcomes [25, 27, 32, 34, 37, 38, 40, 42]. All studies reported a reduction in urine culturing post-intervention; however, not all were statistically significant. Two of the studies reported a nonsignificant reduction in urine culturing and the reductions reported were relatively small between study groups (0.45/1000RD reduction and 0.13/1000RD reduction respectively) [34, 37]. The studies reporting on urine culturing had specifically included urine culturing specific information and interventions as part of their AMS programs.

Adverse events

Ten studies examined hospitalisations as one of the many adverse events that could occur to a resident [24, 25, 30, 32, 34, 36–39, 41]. Of these ten studies, three found that the intervention led to a decrease in hospitalisations, one found no difference between groups and six studies found a slight but nonsignificant increase in hospitalisations following the intervention that was not associated with UTIs. Seven studies also examined mortality as an adverse event. Two studies reported no differences in mortality between control and interventional arms, even when examining changes in rate across time. The remaining five studies reported a slight but nonsignificant increase in resident mortality in the intervention group. Overall, these studies indicate no association between AMS interventions and risk of hospitalisations or adverse events.

C. difficile rates

There is some evidence to show that antibiotic treatment for a UTI can increase the risk of developing a C. difficile infection (CDI) making CDI a potential adverse event of antibiotic treatment [44]. Four of these studies reported on numbers or rates of CDI as part of the adverse patient events examined. However, all studies did not find any significant changes in CDI rates before, during and after the intervention.

Effect of AMS interventions on rate of prescribing (prescriptions/resident days)

RCTs were grouped by PICOT for the meta-analysis, resulting in four grouped RCTs being selected. Other PICOT groupings based primarily on outcome measures (either reporting outcomes as an overall number of prescriptions or as a measure of appropriateness) resulted in two RCTs in each group and were not considered for meta-analysis due to the unreliability of heterogeneity statistics and pooled estimates with just two studies. These four RCTs reported on the effect if educational interventions in conjunction with clinical tools and audits and had similar outcome measures (number of prescriptions/resident days) [24, 30, 34, 37]. Random-effects modelling was chosen after considerable statistical heterogeneity across studies was detected (X2 = 33.69). The relative risk for antimicrobial prescribing post-intervention was 0.64 (CI: 0.49 – 0.83) indicating a decrease in urinary prescribing attributable to the intervention (Fig. 2). The calculated I2 indicated significant heterogeneity attributable to true study heterogeneity (I2 = 91%). Two studies were assigned larger study weight due to their considerably larger sample sizes and relative risks were similar across three of the four studies. A limitation of the analysis is the moderately small number of studies (four RCTs) amenable to meta-analysis. A random-effects model was chosen to account for the anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity between the included studies. The high I2 indicates considerable heterogeneity, however the limited number of studies may have led to a lower power to detect between-study variability, producing an I2 value that is less reliable. This limits the certainty of the pooled intervention effect which may not necessarily be representative of the evidence for AMS interventions on urinary prescribing and therefore should be interpreted with caution.

Effect of AMS interventions on rate of hospitalisations

Additional analysis on hospitalisation rates was conducted to determine the effect of interventions on resident safety (Fig. 3). As done previously, random-effects modelling was chosen due to statistical heterogeneity across studies (X2 = 19.1). The relative risk for hospitalisation post-intervention was 1.02 (CI: 0.70 – 1.49) indicating no significant change in the risk of hospitalisation due to interventions. However, relative risks varied widely across studies. Again, there was significant heterogeneity attributable to true study heterogeneity (I2 = 84%) and two studies were assigned larger study weights due to their larger sample sizes.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis found that educational AMS interventions were effective at reducing either overall or inappropriate urinary prescriptions in an aged care setting. This is consistent with the results of other systematic reviews that advise AMS interventions are effective at promoting responsible use of antimicrobials for any infection in numerous other settings [14, 45]. In addition, the pooled relative risk calculated as part of the meta-analysis suggests no substantial difference in hospitalisations due to interventions or the reduction in urinary prescribing, dispelling the long-held belief that antimicrobial prescribing actively averts hospitalisations. However, there was notable heterogeneity between studies in design and types of interventions which affected the synthesis of evidence.

Meta-analyses

Inter-study heterogeneity was expected to occur as studies were conducted with tailored interventions, different sample sizes and in different countries. The large variance in relative risks was due to one study having higher relative risk than the other study (RR = 0.91) [37]. However, this study did not stratify facility randomisation by baseline antimicrobial rates, leading to a less pronounced measured change in prescribing rates before and after the intervention. When study authors adjusted the reported incidence rate ratio to account for these differences in baseline rates across facilities in control and interventional groups, a more significant reduction attributable to the intervention was noted (RR = 0.73).

The design of the interventions across the studies varied greatly due to the interventions having been tailored to the recruited facilities and the policies of the countries they were conducted in; however, they shared notable similarities. All interventions included educational seminars/webinars addressing guideline-concordance and misconceptions regarding the care of ASBs and UTIs. All four interventions provided some form of clinical decision-making tool in the form of pre-determined order forms or clinical algorithms. All interventions included regular audits and feedback, iteratively tailoring the intervention based on prescribing behaviours and providing clinicians with timely feedback on areas for improvement.

Another meta-analysis was conducted to analyse hospitalisation rates. There is literature suggesting prescriptions for non-specific symptoms, unlikely infections and ASB could be driven by the fear that withholding treatment would lead to an increased possibility of patient hospitalisations [46, 47]. Relative risks of hospitalisations varied across the studies (0.21 – 1.47) and the pooled relative risk was 1.02 (CI: 0.70 – 1.49) (Fig. 3) which indicated no substantial risk of hospitalisations attributable to interventions. The variation in relative risks in hospitalisations is expected due to all studies having been conducted in different countries. Differences in policies regarding hospital transfers and frequency of hospitalisations between aged care facilities across countries has been demonstrated in other studies and would have been a factor in large differences in relative risk across studies [48, 49]. Moreover, elderly in are more likely to unexpectedly be hospitalised due to accidents, especially due to falls [50].

Descriptive synthesis of all studies

Almost all studies (19/20) included at least one educational component to their intervention either in writing (posters/pocket cards) or orally (seminars/webinars). Educational tools in these studies addressed guideline-concordant prescribing and common misconceptions in the diagnosis and care of ASB. All but one of these studies reported reductions in overall or inappropriate urinary prescriptions indicating that targeted educational materials to promote guideline-concordant prescribing are effective.

The study that did not report reductions in overall or inappropriate urinary prescriptions stated that antimicrobial overuse for presumed UTIs had been recognised previously, with numerous policy changes and research having taken place in the past to promote responsible prescribing for UTIs [36]. Study authors believe this resulted in less opportunity or agency to promote practice changes at the recruited nursing homes. This was further supported by the suboptimal intervention implementation fidelity reported. Studies that promoted active monitoring of the implementation of their interventions through audits and feedback experienced more pronounced reductions in urinary prescribing in general. One of these studies suggested that habitual feedback and management incorporated into the intervention promoted the uptake of interventions by nursing home staff during the study [24]. Moreover, routine personalised audit and feedback was effective at improving clinician prescribing behaviour, as noted in the RCTs included in the meta-analysis [24, 30, 34, 37]. Therefore, it sees likely that interventions designed with a component for active monitoring and reiterative improvements can improve intervention fidelity, clinician prescribing behaviours and reduce overall or inappropriate urinary prescriptions.

Studies conducted within the last decade (from 2016) incorporated their educational materials electronically which included including recommendations within the facility’s electronic medical record system, providing online webinars or recordings, and e-learning modules. These materials were accessible at a clinician’s time of choosing rather than being scheduled, which circumnavigated time-based restrictions and barriers to participating in educational interventions. Educational sessions specific to urinary prescribing tended to have an emphasis on the identification and management of bacteriuria and highlighted its key differences differentiating it from an active urinary infection. This aligns many of the educational interventions with addressing common misconceptions regarding the management of UTIs and ASBs in the elderly, with many interventions having been effective at improving clinician awareness regarding this issue. Very few studies included a dedicated stewardship team; without a consistent team that could manage the AMS interventions, some studies cited uncertainty with intervention fidelity and noted difficulties in sustaining these interventions within facilities where staff turnover rates were high. This was the case with the facility recruited by Mitchell et al. [36], which could not sustain any meaningful improvement in urinary prescribing. This highlights the need to establish a dedicated stewardship team and/or leadership within aged care facilities.

Study limitations

Although several studies were included in this review, the vast diversity in the types of interventions and lack of consistency in the reporting of outcomes limited the synthesis of evidence in this review. Due to the heterogeneity of both study methodology and reporting, only four studies could be pooled for a meta-analysis. It was anticipated that intervention designs would differ between studies due to the tailored nature of these AMS programs but reported outcomes and units also different significantly across the studies which limited the synthesis of evidence.

Although almost all studies demonstrated reductions in prescribing, a few were analysed under low power leading due to their small sample sizes which led to low statistical significance in reported results. However, this is a limitation of the individual studies included in the review and not a limitation of the review itself.

Conclusion

While this systematic review and meta-analysis identified educational interventions as being the most implemented type of AMS intervention in the aged care setting, there is evidence suggesting that interventions are most effective at reducing inappropriate urinary prescribing when coupled with routine audits and feedback. The past decade has seen a shift in physical educational resources to electronic modules and webinars which improves clinician access by mitigating schedule-based barriers in accessing the intervention. Routine audits and feedback conducted provided timely correspondence and advice to clinicians and staff on areas for improvement, serving to assist in improving guideline-concordant prescribing. In summary, electronically providing access to educational materials and regular feedback are most effective at improving concordance with guidelines and reduces inappropriate urinary prescribing.

Residents of aged care facilities were also found to experience minimal risk for further health-related complications and adverse events, disproving the longstanding myth that withholding antimicrobials could lead to further resident risk or harm. Considering the positive improvements and minimal patient risk, stakeholders and clinicians are encouraged to invest in the provision of electronic resources and feedback for staff as an essential part of their care to aged care residents.
