Initiatives to enhance referral patterns from primary care to specialist kidney care:a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Anukul Ghimire, Vinash Kumar Hariramani, Abdullah Abdulrahman, Feng Ye, Janice Y Kung, Somkanya Tungsanga, Alan Grill, Alexander Singer, Ayub Akbari, Branko Braam, David Collister, Elena Qirjazi, Kailash Jindal, Mark Courtney, Matthew Cooper, Matthew James, Marcello Tonelli, Nikhil Shah, Paul E Ronksley, Sabin Shurraw, K Scott Brimble, Scott Klarenbach, Sophia Chou, Soroush Shojai, Vinay Deved, Andrew Wong, Deenaz Zaidi, Ikechi Okpechi, Aminu K Bello
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo highlight data on interventions aimed at enhancing referral patterns from primary care to nephrology.DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis.Data sourcesMEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Web of Science and PsycINFO.Eligibility criteriaStudies reporting interventions aimed at decreasing referral volume, reducing wait times and/or increasing guideline-concordant referrals in adult patients with chronic kidney disease from primary care to specialist kidney care, from inception to 15 April 2024.Data extraction and synthesisStudies were subcategorised based on taxonomy of initiatives, type of intervention and single versus multi-faceted interventions. Our primary outcomes were changes to referral volume, wait times and proportion of guideline-concordant referrals. Data were pooled using a random effects model and a meta-analysis was conducted using pooled mean difference (MD) and OR.Results20 studies used single and 13 used more than one intervention. Overall, interventions resulted in an increase in total referrals (MD: 19 referrals per month, 95% CI 7 to 30; I2=98%; p<0.001) and a non-significant decrease in the proportion of guideline-concordant referrals (OR: 0.32, 95% CI 0.09 to 1.16; I2=100%; p=0.08). These effects were consistent when stratified by single versus multi-faceted interventions, and by taxonomy of interventions. Interventions categorised as financial, regulatory or legislative incentives (n=2) led to an increase in guideline-concordant referrals (OR: 1.15, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.29; I2: 0%; p=0.02). Organisational changes showed a non-significant reduction in wait time of 24 days (95% CI −64 to 15, I2=99%; p=0.23) but tended to decrease guideline concordant referrals (OR: 0.17, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.86; I2: 100%; p=0.03).ConclusionOrganisational changes decreased wait times. Overall, interventions increased referral volume and decreased guideline-concordant referrals. The data have implications for designing programmes to improve primary care-nephrology interface in kidney disease management.

Introduction

The number of patients with kidney failure worldwide is expected to reach 5.4 million by 2030.1 This is due to an increase in the prevalence of patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), which is disproportionally increasing strain on health systems across all world regions and in particular lower middle-income countries (LMICs).24 Effective and efficient referral mechanisms will support high quality care for the right person in the right place and time, mitigating risk of major adverse health consequences associated with CKD. Providing timely care to prevent kidney failure and improve outcomes for patients with CKD requires effective collaboration between primary care practitioners (PCPs) and nephrologists. It has been estimated that 72–95% of Canadian patients with CKD are managed in primary care,5 6 where PCPs have an important role in reducing the risk of adverse outcomes of CKD including progression to kidney failure and death from cardiovascular disease.7 8 Strategies towards reducing these adverse clinical outcomes would include a timely access to specialist care which in turn requires a system that supports effective prioritisation and comprehensive patient health information and enables efficient communication between patients, PCPs and specialists. However, many specialists see referrals that do not require specialist care (if managed appropriately with backup through the PCPs), whereas high-risk patients may not receive adequate care or may experience adverse and costly consequences of waiting too long for specialist evaluation. This is more germane in LMICs where there is already a strained nephrology workforce working in under-resourced health systems.4

The existing system for arranging specialist care relies on physician or patient initiatives and does not necessarily make use of established risk stratification approaches. The current system has been deemed inefficient as it provides limited interactions between specialists and PCPs, does not help PCPs work to their full scope of practice and results in many unnecessary referrals while perpetuating late referrals of patients with severe forms of chronic diseases.

As a result, the international kidney care community, via initiatives such as the c (KDIGO),6 has developed a set of criteria to guide timely specialist referral for management of CKD and plan for kidney replacement therapy (KRT). Many studies have shown that late referral to nephrology is associated with increased hospitalisations, higher costs and increased mortality,912 and the benefits of timely referrals have been well documented.13 14 This resulted in interest on the implications of premature, guideline-discordant referrals, which account for a large proportion of referrals to nephrology.13 15 16 Our group recently showed that in Alberta, Canada, only 41% of nephrology referrals were guideline concordant, and between 2006 and 2019, the volume of guideline discordant referrals increased more rapidly than that of guideline concordant referrals.17 These referrals may further prolong wait times and increase costs to the healthcare system.18

Previous systematic reviews that analysed ways to change referral patterns from PCPs to specialists (in general) have found that organisational interventions (defined as primary care and specialist provider schemes, general practitioner fundholding schemes and open access referral schemes),19 and educational activities (such as the dissemination of guidelines with structured referral sheets)20 were linked to lower unnecessary referral rates. When these interventions were applied to improve referral patterns in nephrology, they have not had a similar impact.21

Uncertainties remain on the best strategies to enhance guideline-concordant referrals to specialist kidney care from primary care. We therefore conducted a systematic review of studies that investigated interventions for reducing wait times, decreasing the volume of referrals and/or increasing the proportion of guideline-concordant referrals from PCP to specialised kidney care services across all world regions. A key objective was to assess which interventions are the most effective in enhancing referral patterns from primary care to nephrology.

Methods

The study protocol has been previously published and is registered with PROSPERO (CRD42021247756).22 We used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis for Protocols 2015 (PRISMA)23 in the conduct and report of this systematic review.

Search strategy

Key databases such as Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Web of Science and Ovid PsycINFO were searched. All relevant studies published from inception to 15 April 2024 (date ranges of all data sources shown in online supplemental Figure 1) with no restriction on language were included. We developed and executed the search strategy (online supplemental Figure 1). The protocol22 was updated to include studies published until 15 April 2024 to ensure comprehensiveness of our search. We used both controlled vocabularies (eg, medical subject headings) and keywords. We also manually searched references of publications that met our criteria. The search strategy is provided as online supplemental Figure 1.

Eligibility criteria and intervention classification

Studies were included if designed to increase guideline-concordant referrals or to reduce wait times and/or volume of referrals of patients with CKD who were ≥18 years of age and had not initiated KRT, from primary care to specialist kidney care. We defined PCP as family physicians, family doctors and general practitioners (including nurse practitioners). Studies that explicitly mentioned general internists, paediatricians or other specialists (eg, urologists) being included in the referral sources were excluded. Since the focus was on how interventions may affect the likelihood of guideline concordant versus discordant referrals (as opposed to the absolute frequency of one vs the other), we accepted the definition of guideline concordance as provided by each primary study, rather than using an external definition such as the KDIGO guideline.2428 Studies selected for inclusion include randomised clinical trials, controlled before-after studies, interrupted time series studies, observational studies, quality improvement reports and descriptive/qualitative studies. Studies were excluded if referrals were not from PCP to a specialist kidney care service, did not clearly report on any of the outcomes of interest (guideline concordant referral rate, referral numbers or wait times) or if we could not obtain an English version of the full text of study. We also excluded review articles, editorials, letters to the editor, commentaries, case studies, case reports and images.

Intervention and outcomes

Studies analysing interventions of any type that focused on reducing wait times, decreasing referral numbers and/or increasing guideline-concordant referrals were included. Modelling studies that projected possible change in referral trends from a hypothetical intervention, but that did not implement an intervention in a real-life practice setting, were excluded. Studies categorised based on the taxonomy of the intervention (as defined by others)29 into categories of (1) provider education, (2) provider reminder systems, (3) audit and feedback, (4) organisational change, (5) financial, regulatory or legislative incentives, (6) promotion of self-management, (7) patient reminder systems, (8) facilitated relay of clinical data to providers or (9) patient education. Specific definitions for these terms are provided in our systematic review protocol22 and listed in online supplemental Table 1. This taxonomy is based on previous work that modifies several well-established classification systems and has been used widely.29 30

Studies were classified as using single versus multiple types of intervention, and each type of intervention was recorded. The primary outcomes of interest were changes in wait times, changes in the total number of referrals and changes in the proportion of guideline-concordant referrals.

Study selection

Studies identified from the search strategy were uploaded into Covidence, a web-based screening tool (https://www.covidence.org/)AG, ST and DZ independently screened titles and abstracts of the studies identified from the search to assess eligibility. Next, we obtained and screened the full text of the articles identified according to our inclusion and exclusion criteria.22 IGO resolved any disagreements between AG, ST and DZ.

Data extraction

FY, VKH and AA extracted data into a study sheet that was developed before the extraction process. AG resolved any disagreements in extracted data. For each study, the following data elements were collected: publication year, country of study, study design, intervention type and taxonomy, comparator or control population, study duration, inclusion and exclusion criteria reported, population studied, assessment of renal function, guideline used to define guideline concordant referrals. For both the intervention and control group, we collected data on sample size, CKD stages24 included in the study population, and mean age, reported estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and albuminuria. Information on whether a study included patients receiving KRT (ie, kidney transplant, haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis) was also recorded. We extracted data on changes to mean waiting time, total number of referrals and proportion of guideline concordant referrals as reported in the studies.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias of each study was assessed using the National Institute of Health (NIH) study quality assessment tools for before-and-after studies.31 This tool contains 12 questions to address various domains of bias. For each question, a score of ‘0’ was given if the answer to the question was ‘No’ (translating to that specific element being absent from the study) and a score of ‘1’ was given if the answer to the question was ‘Yes’. We summed the individual scores for each question to calculate the total score which was then divided by 12 (maximum possible score). We categorised studies as low risk of bias if the quality assessment score was >0.75, moderate risk of bias if the quality assessment score was 0.25–0.75 and high risk of bias if the quality assessment score was <0.25. We judged each domain as having a low, high or unclear risk of bias.

Data analysis

Results were pooled overall and within specific categories based on the taxonomy and the type of the intervention.29 For continuous data, such as the average of monthly referrals and average wait times, the mean difference (MD) was calculated. For dichotomous data, such as whether referrals were guideline-concordant, the OR was computed. When available, we imputed missing SDs using the IQR.32 33 If spread data were not reported, we imputed missing SDs using the same study designs’ arithmetic mean of the SDs.32 33 We extracted data from graphs if required.

MD and ORs were pooled using random effects models.34 The between-study variance (τ2 statistic) was estimated using Restricted Maximum Likelihood method.35 Statistical heterogeneity was tested using the χ2 test and quantified using the I2 statistic (I2 values of <25%, 25%–50% and>50% indicated low, medium and high heterogeneity, respectively).36 When the I2 statistic suggested medium heterogeneity or greater, additional emphasis was given to subgroup analysis by category to explore the reasons for the heterogeneity. We assessed publication bias by visual inspection of the contour-enhanced funnel plot37 and used variance-inverse weighted regression (also known as Egger’s test) if more than ten studies reported the data for each outcome.38 The outcomes reporting bias39 was assessed by comparing the demographic features of studies that reported a certain outcome of interest (total referrals, proportion of guideline concordant referrals and wait times) to those that do not report a specific outcome.39 To further explore sources of heterogeneity, we conducted meta-regression analyses using other commonly reported covariates from the included studies, including mean age, proportion of males, baseline eGFR, study quality, study duration, region/country, follow-up time and year of publication.

Two studies evaluated two distinct interventions at separate time points for different populations. People who live in the same area are likely to receive similar healthcare services and have comparable patterns of referrals to specialist kidney care services. In sensitivity analysis, we used a multilevel mixed-effect regression to account for nested random effects from the multiple interventions within studies. All analyses were completed in STATA 18 (STATA Corporation, College Station, TX). We performed meta-analyses using meta commands in STATA. A p value of <0.05 considered as statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or public members were not involved in any aspect of our study.

Results

Baseline characteristics of studies

Our PRISMA flow chart (figure 1) summarises the study retrieval process. After 6171 articles were retrieved from the search, 3429 were removed as duplicates. Subsequently, 2742 studies underwent abstract screening (‘Studies screened’ on figure 1) and 1883 of these studies were excluded. 73 articles underwent full-text review (‘Studies assessed for eligibility’ on figure 1) from which 33 studies were included based on the defined inclusion criteria (figure 1).1621 4070 Eight of these articles were conference abstracts and 25 were full manuscripts. The studies took place in Canada (n=9), the UK (n=9), the USA (n=7), Chile (n=2), England (n=1), Australia (n=1), Spain (n=2), Malaysia (n=1) and Japan (n=1). The majority of the articles were identified as being pre-post designs (n=19) with the other studies being interrupted time series (n=2), observational studies (n=6), randomised control trial (RCT) (n=3), qualitative study (n=1), cross-sectional study (n=1) and prospective cohort study (n=1) as shown in table 1. 20 studies used single interventions and 13 studies used multiple interventions. Most studies used organisational interventions (n=26) either as a single intervention or as part of multiple interventions. Demographics of the studies are highlighted in online supplemental Tables 2-4.

Effect of interventions on total number of referrals

11 studies reported the effects of interventions on total number of referrals per month (figure 2). The pooled effect indicated an increase in total referrals associated with interventions, and this effect was statistically significant (MD): 19 referrals per month, 95% CI 7 to 30; p<0.001); however, there was a high level of statistical heterogeneity (I2=98%). These findings remained consistent when using multilevel mixed-effect regression. After analysing the interventions based on their taxonomy, differentiating between multifaceted and single interventions, and analysing based on types of interventions, consistent results were observed (online supplemental Figures 2-4). The two studies with intervention taxonomy of financial, regulatory or legislative incentives had a pooled MD of 48 (95% CI 0 to 97; I2=99%; p=0.049). Two trials and two pre-post design studies involving 8646 participants reported findings as the proportion of the total number of referrals (online supplemental Figure 5), and the pooled effect from these interventions was not statistically significant (OR: 0.95, 95% CI 0.49 to 1.83; I2=90%; p=0.9).

Effect of interventions on referral wait times

Overall, pooled results from the five studies (10 287 participants) that reported the effects of interventions on wait times did not demonstrate a significant effect: −16 days (95% CI −50 to 18; I2=99%; p=0.4) (figure 3). Results remained non-significant after excluding one study that used multifaceted intervention (MD: −24, 95% CI −64 to 15; I2=99%; p=0.2) (online supplemental Figure 6). Two of the studies reporting wait times used eGFR reporting interventions; pooling results from these two studies showed a significant increase in wait times by 21 days (95% CI 13 to 29; I2=80%; p<0.001) (online supplemental Figure 7) while the three other interventions all decreased waiting times. The three interventions that decreased waiting times (Ong et al,55 Poyah et al70 and Schachter et al52) all fell into the taxonomy of organisational change and represented different types of interventions (e-consult, modification of triage process and waiting time benchmarks) (table 1).

Effect of interventions on guideline-concordant referrals

Overall, 14 studies with 53 226 participants reported guideline-concordant referrals (figure 4) and demonstrated that the interventions were not significantly associated with the proportion of guideline-concordant referrals (OR 0.32; 95% CI 0.09 to 1.16; I2=100%; p=0.08). Similar results were observed when the studies with intervention taxonomy provider education and provider reminder system were considered (online supplemental Figure 8). The intervention taxonomy of organisational change decreased the proportion of guideline concordant referrals (OR 0.17; 95% CI 0.03 to 0.86; I2=100%; p=0.03). Interventions categorised as financial, regulatory or legislative incentives taxonomy resulted in a higher percentage of guideline concordant referrals (OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.29; I2=0.0%; p=0.02). When dividing the intervention taxonomy into single and multiple faceted categories, we observed non-significant decreases in guideline concordant referrals (online supplemental Figure 9). For interventions involving e-consultations and electronic communication, the OR of referrals being guideline concordant (defined as concordant with the advice provided and/or needing a referral as defined by the study criteria) was quite low at 0.004 (95% CI 0.0004 to 0.04; I2=87%; p<0.001) (online supplemental Figure 10).

Publication bias

The contour-enhanced funnel plots for the average monthly referrals and guideline-concordant referrals (online supplemental Figure 11 and 12) were asymmetric and had missing studies in the significant region, indicating that the asymmetry was more likely due to other factors rather than publication bias. Publication bias was not assessed for wait times due to the low number of studies (<10) reporting the outcome.

Outcome reporting bias and demographics of studies

The demographics of studies reporting total referrals (online supplemental Table 1), wait times (online supplemental Table 2) and guideline concordant referrals (online supplemental Table 3) were similar to those not reporting these respective outcomes. Studies reporting total referrals were more often interrupted time series and randomised control trials compared with those that did not report total referrals (online supplemental Table 1).

Sources of heterogeneity

Meta-regression analyses using covariates of mean age, proportion of males, study duration, region/country, follow-up time and year of publication are shown in online supplemental Tables 5 and 6. For the outcome of volume of monthly referrals, none of the selected covariates significantly affected the MD in monthly referrals (online supplemental Table 5). The measures of heterogeneity, including both I2 and between-study variance T2, remained similar after adjusting for these variables (online supplemental Table 5). For the outcome of guideline-concordant referrals, adjusting for age, sex and baseline eGFR reduced the measures of heterogeneity, including both I2 and between-study variance T2 (online supplemental Table 6). Specifically, I2 decreased from 100% to 72%, 68% and 88%, while T2 dropped from 5.37 to 0.03, 0.03 and 0.12 for age, proportion of males and baseline eGFR, respectively. However, because only 5 to 8 studies were included, statistical power was limited in discerning whether these variables significantly modified the effect sizes.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review of initiatives to enhance referral patterns from PCP to specialist kidney care programmes. We highlight four key findings in this review of 33 published studies. First, interventions had a statistically significant effect on increasing the total number of referrals. These effects were also noted for subcategories of studies based on intervention taxonomies, types of interventions and single versus multi-faceted interventions. Second, interventions overall did not have statistically significant changes on impacting waiting times or guideline-concordant referrals. Third, we found that most organisational changes tended to decrease wait times, except for eGFR reporting, which had the opposite effect. Finally, interventions that were categorised as financial, regulatory or legislative incentives increased the proportion of guideline concordant referrals. It is worth noting that there was variability in the definitions of guideline-concordant between studies and most interventions were studied in high-income countries (vs in resource-limited regions), as shown in online supplemental Table 1.

Our review follows on the work done by Blank et al,71 Akbari et al20 and Faulkner et al,19 that also examined interventions to impact referral patterns from primary care to specialists. These reviews had mixed results. Akbari et al found that referral practices were most enhanced by provider education interventions that (1) disseminated guidelines with referral sheets (but not by passive dissemination of guidelines) and (2) by involving consultants to lead educational workshops to PCP about referring (but not by simply providing feedback to PCP about their referral practices).20 Financial and organisational interventions were not effective overall. The review by Faulkner et al linked provider education to influencing clinical behaviour, which led to more appropriate referrals, but did not impact total referral rates. They did, however, notice an impact on referral rates from 22 studies identified as being organisational interventions.19 The review from Blank et al found that among educational interventions, peer review and feedback had more favourable effects on changing referral practice.71

In contrast to the previously published reviews,20 71 our study found that interventions that were focused on provider education did not enhance referral patterns; these interventions tended to increase total referrals (MD 12 referrals per month, 95% CI 6 to 19) and had a non-statistically significant impact on decreasing the proportion of guideline concordant referrals (OR 0.34, 95% CI 0.06 to 1.94). There may be various reasons for the differences with the outcomes of our study. First, in contrast to these previous reviews, our analysis excluded referrals from internists and paediatricians (who would be classified as primary care in certain health systems, particularly in the USA), making the target audience of these education-based interventions different. Second, our work only focused on the referral of nephrology patients needing kidney care only, a group of patients who are often complex and needing intensity ambulatory care. Recent work in our field did highlight that patients managed by nephrologists tend to have the greatest degree of medical complexity when compared with patients managed by other specialists.72 These patient-specific factors might influence physician behaviour when it comes to referring, and it is not clear whether the studies included in our review considered patient complexity in the design of interventions. Third, the classification scheme for interventions differs between our work and the previous work, which might limit comparison between the reviews. For example, the review by Blank et al grouped each study into one intervention type, rather than separating studies with multi-faceted interventions into their different components.71 One challenge for interpretation is that PCP education was often delivered in parallel with other interventions. For example, three of the four studies (Akbari et al,48 Hingwala et al16 and Noble et al56) that reported total referral rates using provider education interventions also included eGFR reporting as an intervention, as shown in online supplemental Figure 4. In our study, eGFR reporting increased monthly referrals16 48 50 53 54 56 61 (MD 28 referrals per month, 95% CI 11 to 44), increased wait times16 53 (MD, 21 days, 95% CI 13 to 29), and had minimal impact on guideline concordant referrals48 56 58 61 (OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.76 to 1.20). Previous work has shown that eGFR reporting leads to an increase in total referrals, with a trend towards an increase in guideline discordant referrals.73 Thus, since these studies reported the net effect on referral patterns and wait times after both PCP education and eGFR reporting were applied, the individual effects of each could not be fully determined. However, provider education significantly decreased the rate of guideline-concordant referrals overall—even in studies that did not use eGFR reporting.49 62 This suggests that provider education does not enhance referral patterns from PCP to specialist kidney care.

In contrast to these negative findings, our results showed that certain financial, regulatory or legislative interventions have the potential to increase the rate of guideline-concordant referrals, as shown in online supplemental Figure 8.58 61 These interventions that showed a positive impact on the outcomes of interest included the Quality and Outcomes Framework in the UK; a pay-for-performance model that provides incentives for PCPs to enhance quality of care in their practices. Further, although the majority of interventions in the taxonomy of organisational changes were those that used eGFR reporting (which tended to increase both wait times16 53 and total referrals),16 48 50 53 54 56 61 we did identify a small number of organisational changes that decreased wait times, as shown in online supplemental Figure 6.52 55 70 These organisational changes were all single interventions, focused on modification of the triage and referral process and did not directly use eGFR reporting as an intervention. Thus, although there were positive results from these interventions, the pooled results in the meta-analysis for organisational changes may have been skewed by the impact of the large number of interventions using eGFR reporting.

In interpreting our study findings, it is critical to recognise that the studied interventions are complex with heterogenous defining parameters, scope and implementation route. For example, some interventions are targeted to individual care providers, and others to the health system leading to varying results on changes to the facilitators and barriers involved in promoting physician behaviour change.74 A number of factors such as system and organisational barriers, nature of interventions (mismatched approaches, lack or limited trust in the evidence) and individual level factors (skills and attitudes, awareness and agreement) as well as contextual influences affect implementation and resulting impact.75

This might explain the lack of improvement in referral quality noted from some of these interventions. For example, it has been postulated that unfamiliarity among PCPs with understanding eGFR reporting increased the total number and early guideline discordant referrals.48 56 Thus, a single-faceted pre-post intervention that only looks at whether eGFR reporting changes referral patterns might demonstrate a lack of efficacy with practice change. The implication of this finding is that more effective behaviour change (and consequently higher referral quality) might result from (1) first assessing knowledge gaps among PCPs in understanding how to interpret eGFR, (2) understanding the barriers and facilitators in promoting high-quality referrals using eGFR and (3) designing interventions that target these specific facilitators and barriers.76 Although our review did not specifically assess whether these studies used effective implementation science-based tools (such as the T-CaST framework),76 the majority of reported interventions were single—rather than multi-faceted—suggesting they were not targeting the various facilitators and barriers involved in driving behaviour change.

Our study used the NIH assessment tools for before-and-after studies.31 Although there are various risks of bias assessment tools to evaluate the methodological quality of included studies, including ROBIS (for RCTs),77 ROBINS-I (for non-randomised studies),78 AMSTAR-2 (for the quality of systematic reviews themselves)79 and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (for non-randomised studies),80 our included studies were largely observational and used a pre-post design (online supplemental Table 2). Thus, we leveraged the NIH assessment tools for before-and-after studies tool for the risk assessment, recognising the limitation of using this tool for other types of studies. Another important limitation in our analysis comes from the variations in the definitions of guideline-concordance. Our analysis did not specify which guidelines could be used to define guideline-concordance and so this may limit the interpretation of an intervention to certain guideline criteria only. As an example, the Canadian Society of Nephrology (CSN) recommends referral once urine albumin to creatinine ratio exceeds 60 mg/mmol whereas KDIGO recommends referral at ACR 30 mg/mmol.24 25 Thus, an intervention that reduced referral for patients with ACR values between 30 and 60 mg/mmol would have enhanced the proportion of guideline concordant referrals by KDIGO criteria but not the CSN criteria. A previous systematic review has found a notable degree of heterogeneity when it comes to referral recommendations across guidelines.81 Therefore, the possibility that interventions may be more effective in improving concordance with specific criteria than with others should be considered.

Further, we have conducted additional analyses using meta-regression to identify sources of heterogeneity in our findings. The variations in the definition of appropriate referrals might have been an important factor in heterogeneity observed in the various intervention approaches. These variations in the definition of guideline-concordance also further divided the studies into small, inconsistent subgroups and limited the interpretation of the pooled results. Additional heterogeneity may have arisen for unobserved factors. For example, we could not assess if PCPs were more aware of one guideline over others and if there were temporal changes in the recognition and awareness of CKD among PCPs. These factors may have influenced referral patterns and may represent undetected sources of heterogeneity. Thus, given (1) the variability in the definitions of ‘guideline-concordant’ between studies, (2) lack of interventions from low-middle-income countries, (3) variations in definition of appropriate referrals and (4) potential undetected sources of heterogeneity, our results will need to be interpreted with caution (especially for healthcare systems not represented in our review, where the application of guideline recommendations may be impaired due to resource limitations and shortages in the nephrology workforce).

There are other limitations worth highlighting as well. First, we restricted our studies to only those from PCP to specialist kidney care and did not include general internists in our definition of PCP. In certain healthcare systems, general internists are considered primary care20 and thus interventions targeting referral patterns in these systems may have been excluded. We decided to exclude this as general internists are considered specialists in our country (Canada); however, we acknowledge this is not the case everywhere. Second, since some studies used multifaceted interventions, meta-regression could have been used to explore the effects of the various components. This would have been especially valuable for studies reporting the effects of eGFR reporting, which we could anticipate would increase total referrals and possibly decrease guideline-concordant referrals.73 Unfortunately, this was not possible due to the relatively small number of studies examining each outcome. Third, our study only included information on the referrals that were made but was not able to assess the population who were not referred but may still have had a guideline-concordant reason to be referred. Fourth, our review did not include studies where there was not an English version present, which may add language bias. Fifth, our analysis excluded studies reporting interventions that did not clearly report data on the outcomes of interest, which may subject our analysis to outcome reporting bias. Further, we did find that there were differences in study design between studies that reported total referrals to those where these data were not reported, suggesting that the lack of reporting on total referrals may not be completely random.39 Sixth, the lack of a control group in the pre-post studies limits the ability to interpret the outcomes from these interventions. Finally, our systematic review did not have any patient or public involvement. Having perspectives from these groups would allow us to better understand if certain reported outcomes (eg, waiting times) are more relevant to stakeholders such as patients and members of society.

Our findings have implications for practice and for future research. The relatively small number of interventions in the field, and limited effectiveness in positively impacting referral patterns, highlights the need for novel strategies to enhance referral quality. This is important as unnecessary referrals for specialist care can increase wait times and delay care for patients who warrant more urgent specialist evaluation. Further, unnecessary referrals may lead to decentralised and uncoordinated care between providers and may lead to specialists unnecessarily interfering with the role of PCP to manage common medical problems such as uncomplicated mild to moderate CKD. This concept, recently termed as ‘scope inversion”, has been shown to be prevalent in up to 65% of specialist visits82 and could lead to patients getting less comprehensive care than if they were managed by PCP.83 There are limited data on how to reduce scope inversion. A recent study has highlighted the potential of a ‘back-referral criteria’, where patients who may potentially be referred to primary care from specialty care are identified.84 This study, however, was a retrospective analysis highlighting the potential number of patients meeting ‘back-referral’ criteria, and the outcomes of applying such criteria are not known.

Thus, future interventions should focus on reducing the burden of guideline discordant referrals for primary care as well as specialists and reducing scope inversion. In primary care, innovations are needed to address the (1) barriers that impede optimal management of CKD, such as lack of education and training among physicians and nurses and the heavy workload of PCP,85 (2) barriers that impede co-management of patients with nephrologists such as poor information exchange and unclear division of responsibilities with nephrologists86 and (3) barriers to dissemination of knowledge between nephrology and PCP. In addition, future guidelines should more clearly delineate the roles of PCP and specialists, as few available guidelines specify when patients should be co-managed by PCP and nephrologists as opposed to managed primarily by nephrologists, or explicitly discuss the role of PCP in CKD management.81

Our data also point to the limitations of using guidelines alone to drive practice change in kidney care. Despite the availability of various guidelines providing recommendations for referrals,24 25 our review showed only a few interventions that positively affected the outcomes of interest. Guidelines alone are unlikely to lead to physician behaviour change,87 and effective knowledge translation and implementation science-based interventions are needed to close quality indicator gaps.88 This will be of the utmost importance in the area of referral practices in nephrology, as the rising prevalence of CKD1 2 coupled with the highly complex profiles of patients with kidney diseases72 is expected to lead to significant strain on the global nephrology workforce.4 89 Our work signals the urgent need to couple quality improvement initiatives with effective knowledge translation strategies to reduce unnecessary referrals to nephrology.

In conclusion, we found that interventions classified as organisational changes were effective in decreasing wait times. Most interventions led to an overall increase in the volume of referrals across taxonomies and types of interventions. The data have a strong implication for designing programmes aimed at enhancing primary care to specialist interface in chronic disease management. Even though this work focused on CKD, the principles and concepts may be applicable to the organisation of referral services from PCP to specialists in other chronic disease domains such as diabetes, hypertension, chronic heart and lung diseases and cancer care.
