Detecting high-dimensional entanglement by randomized product projections
Jin-Min Liang, Shuheng Liu, Shao-Ming Fei, Qiongyi He

Abstract
The characterization of high-dimensional entanglement plays a crucial role in the field of quantum information science. Conventional entanglement criteria measuring coherent superpositions of multiple basis states face experimental bottlenecks on most physical platforms due to limited multi-channel control. Here, we introduce a practically efficient detection strategy based on randomized product projections. We show that the first-order moments of such projections can be used to estimate entanglement fidelity, thereby enabling practical and efficient certification of the Schmidt number in high-dimensional bipartite systems. By constructing optimal observables, it is sufficient to merely measure a single basis state, substantially reducing experimental overhead. Moreover, we present an algorithm to obtain a lower bound of the Schmidt number with a high confidence level from a limited number of experimental data. Our results open up resource-efficient experimental avenues to detect high-dimensional entanglement and test its implementations in modern information technologies.

Schmidt number and fidelity-based witness
—The Schmidt number (
\mathrm{SN}
) of a pure quantum state
|\psi\rangle
is defined by Schmidt rank (
\mathrm{SR}
), i.e., the number of nonzero Schmidt coefficients in their Schmidt decomposition
[
62
]
,
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{l=1}^{\mu}\sqrt{s_{l}}|a_{l}b_{l}\rangle
, where
s_{l}>0
,
\sum_{l}s_{l}=1
,
\mu
denotes the
\mathrm{SR}
, and
\{a_{l}\}
,
\{b_{l}\}
are respectively orthonormal states. For mixed state
\varrho
, its
\mathrm{SN}
is defined by
[
62
]
,
\mathrm{SN}(\varrho)=\min_{\mathcal{D}(\varrho)}\{\mu_{\max}:\varrho=\sum_{l}p_{l}|\psi_{l}\rangle\langle\psi_{l}|,\mu_{\max}=\max_{l}\mu_{l}\}
, where
\mu_{l}
is the
\mathrm{SR}
of pure state
|\psi_{l}\rangle
and
\mathcal{D}(\varrho)
denotes the set of all pure-state decomposition of the state
\varrho
. A larger
\mathrm{SN}
in a bipartite state implies a higher degree of entanglement.

A typical way to determine the
\mathrm{SN}
of a state
\varrho
is to estimate the fidelity
F(\varrho)={\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|)
between
\varrho
and a maximally entangled state
|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}|jj\rangle/\sqrt{d}
. For any
\varrho
with
\mathrm{SN}(\varrho)=\mu
,
F(\varrho)\leq\mu/d

[
62
]
. This inequality can be transformed into an
\mathrm{SN}=\mu+1
witness,
\mathcal{W}_{\mu}=\mu\mathbbm{1}_{d}^{\otimes 2}/d-|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|

[
62
,
53
,
28
,
75
]
such that
{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathcal{W}_{\mu})\geq 0
for all
\varrho
with
\mathrm{SN}(\varrho)\leq\mu
, where
\mathbbm{1}_{d}
denotes the identity. Thus, if
{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathcal{W}_{\mu})<0
,
\varrho
has at least
\mathrm{SN}(\varrho)=\mu+1
.

Schmidt number criterion via randomized product projections
—Consider a two-qudit state
\varrho
shared by Alice and Bob. To detect the
\mathrm{SN}
of
\varrho
, we measure
\varrho
with randomized product projections
[
65
,
32
]
. The measurement process includes two stages. The first stage allows Alice and Bob to perform measurements with any product observables
\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}
after applying the same random unitary
U^{\otimes 2}
on the state
\varrho
. The expectation values for a fixed unitary
U
is,
E_{U}(\varrho)={\mathrm{tr}}[U^{\otimes 2}\varrho U^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}]
. This randomized measurement strategy has been used to classify topological order
[
65
]
and analyze multipartite entanglement
[
32
]
. When Alice and Bob are on the same platform, the local operation
U^{\otimes 2}
can be implemented by passing both parties through a single
U
setup at different times, thereby reducing the operational costs while simultaneously mitigating imperfections of the unitary.

Then, we use a random orthogonal matrix
O
satisfying
O^{\top}O=OO^{\top}=\mathbbm{1}_{d}
, where
\top
denotes the transpose operation. In this scenario, Alice and Bob implement the same orthogonal matrix
O^{\otimes 2}
on the state
\varrho
and perform measurements with product observables
\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}
, similar to the unitary process. The expectation value for a fixed orthogonal matrix
O
is,
E_{O}(\varrho)={\mathrm{tr}}[O^{\otimes 2}\varrho O^{\top\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}]
. The implementation of
O^{\otimes 2}
can also be simplified using a strategy similar to that of
U^{\otimes 2}
.

After sampling random unitary
U
and orthogonal matrix
O
respectively according to the Haar measure on unitary and orthogonal groups
[
25
,
10
,
27
,
12
]
, we consider two first-order moments,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\varrho)=\int dUE_{U}(\varrho),\penalty\ \mathcal{Q}(\varrho)=\int dOE_{O}(\varrho).

(1)

The experimental feasibility of our protocol critically depends on the rank
r
(i.e. the number of nonzero eigenvalues) of the observable
\mathcal{M}
. When the observable has rank
r
, estimating the expectation of
E_{U}(\varrho)
and
E_{O}(\varrho)
requires measurements across
r
basis states, simultaneously controlling
r
experimental channels in physical platforms. To minimize this technical complexity, we construct an optimal observable with
r=1
,
\mathcal{M}=|j\rangle\langle j|
, where
\{|j\rangle\}_{j=0}^{d-1}
denotes the computational basis in a
d
-dimension Hilbert space. This construction reduces the number of channel controls to one, independent of local dimensions
d
. Such low-rank implementations circumvent multi-channel challenges, making our protocol more easily implementable in practical physical platforms.

Using the optimal observable
\mathcal{M}=|j\rangle\langle j|
, we then have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\varrho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S})}{d(d+1)},\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)=\frac{1+{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\varrho\mathbb{S}\right)+{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\varrho\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}}\right)}{d(d+2)},

(2)

by using the Schur–Weyl duality in the unitary and orthogonal group
[
25
,
10
,
27
,
12
]
. Here,
\mathbb{S}=\sum_{j,k=0}^{d-1}|jk\rangle\langle kj|
is the
\mathrm{SWAP}
operator and
\top_{B}
denotes the partial transpose to the subsystem B. See detailed proof in Appendix
A
.

Now, we present our result showing that the moments
\mathcal{R}(\varrho)
and
\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)
in Eq. (
2
) can be used to calculate the fidelity
F(\varrho)={\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+})
and characterize high-dimensional entanglement.

Observation 1
.

For any bipartite state
\varrho
of equal dimension
d
, the fidelity
F(\varrho)
is given by

\displaystyle F(\varrho)=(d+2)\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)-(d+1)\mathcal{R}(\varrho).

(3)

The Schmidt number of
\varrho
is lower bounded by

\displaystyle\mu=\begin{cases}1,&F(\varrho)=0,\\
\lceil dF(\varrho)\rceil,&F(\varrho)\in(0,1],\\
\end{cases}

(4)

where the
\lceil\cdot\rceil
is ceil function.

Equation (
3
) provides a formula to estimate the fidelity from the first-order moments
\mathcal{R}(\varrho)
and
\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)
obtained by randomized product projection. Then, using obtained fidelity we obtain a
\mathrm{SN}
estimation of
\varrho
based on Eq. (
4
). Detailed proof is presented in Appendix
A
, which utilizes the Eq. (
2
), the relation
d|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|=\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}}
, as well as the property of the fidelity-based witness
\mathcal{W}_{\mu}
. We remark that Eq. (
4
) is equivalent to the fidelity-based witness
\mathcal{W}_{\mu}
. This equivalence implies that our criterion and
\mathcal{W}_{\mu}
possess the same capability to detect
\mathrm{SN}
. Moreover, Eq. (
3
) is invariant under the transformation
O\otimes O
. This indicates that our approach is robust against orthogonal
O\otimes O
noise in the measurement stage. As a comparison, the previous methods, such as MUBs and direct fidelity estimation, require well-controlled measurements and thus are fragile to the measurement noise model.

Note that randomized measurements have been extensively investigated for entanglement characterization
[
33
,
77
,
39
,
80
,
54
]
in a reference-frame-independent manner. However, the randomized product projection in observation
1
requires both parties to synchronise their measurement bases. Consequently, our protocol is less suited for scenarios involving distant laboratories.

Observation
1
provides a theoretical criterion and naturally requires us to estimate the moments
\mathcal{R}(\varrho)
and
\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)
exactly with infinite samples of random operations, which is impossible in a practical experimental scenario. Therefore, we next introduce an algorithm to obtain an
\mathrm{SN}
estimation from the measured experimental data with limited projections.

Experimental data acquisition and the number of randomized product projections
—For the estimation of the integral over unitary and orthogonal groups, we independently sample
N
unitaries
\{U_{l}\}_{l=1}^{N}
and
N
orthogonal matrices
\{O_{l}\}_{l=1}^{N}
from a unitary
2
-design and orthogonal
2
-design
[
25
,
10
,
27
,
12
]
. After performing measurements on a rank-one observable
\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}=|j\rangle\langle j|^{\otimes 2}
, we obtain a set of the expectation values
\mathcal{S}_{U}^{(N)}=\{E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\}_{l=1}^{N}
and
\mathcal{S}_{O}^{(N)}=\{E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)\}_{l=1}^{N}
, where the expectations values are

\displaystyle E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}[U_{l}^{\otimes 2}\varrho U_{l}^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}],

(5)

\displaystyle E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}[O_{l}^{\otimes 2}\varrho O_{l}^{\top\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}].

(6)

The data set
\mathcal{S}_{U}^{(N)}
and
\mathcal{S}_{O}^{(N)}
can then be used to obtain an estimator of the fidelity
F(\varrho)
.

The number of randomized product projections depends on the number of random operations,
N
. Note that measuring the observable
\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}
on states
U_{l}^{\otimes 2}\varrho U_{l}^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}
and
O_{l}^{\otimes 2}\varrho O_{l}^{\top\otimes 2}
equivalent to perform the global product projection

and
|u^{l}\rangle\langle u^{l}|^{\otimes 2}
on

\{|e^{l}\rangle=U_{l}^{{\dagger}}|j\rangle\}_{l=1}^{N}
and
\{|u^{l}\rangle=O_{l}^{\top}|j\rangle\}_{l=1}^{N}
. As a result, the number of projections for estimating the fidelity
F(\varrho)
is
2N
.

In Appendix
B
, we show that in the worst case the number of random operations scales as
N=\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
. For maximally entangled states under a depolarizing, dephasing, or random noise channel,
N=\mathcal{O}(1)
is a constant independent of local dimension
d
. This scaling matches the numerical results in the next section. As a comparison, the scaling of the MUBs method
[
46
]
is
\mathcal{O}(md)
, where
m\in[2,d+1]
is the number of MUBs. Thus, our randomized projection is significantly efficient for large local dimensions.

Classical post-processing algorithm for the Schmidt number estimation
—With the data set
\mathcal{S}_{U}^{(N)}
and
\mathcal{S}_{O}^{(N)}
in hand, the natural estimator of the fidelity is inferred via Eq. (
3
),

\displaystyle F_{e}(\varrho)=(d+2)\mathcal{Q}_{e}(\varrho)-(d+1)\mathcal{R}_{e}(\varrho),

(7)

where the estimated moments
\mathcal{R}_{e}(\varrho)=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{U}]
and
\mathcal{Q}_{e}(\varrho)=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{O}]
are defined as the mean values of
\mathcal{S}_{U}
and
\mathcal{S}_{O}
, respectively.

To estimate the statistical distribution of
F_{e}(\varrho)
, a direct approach is to calculate many
F_{e}(\varrho)
from several data sets. However, obtaining more data sets is extremely challenging since additional experimental runs are required to estimate the expectation values of observables. In the following, we present an algorithm to estimate an
\mathrm{SN}
lower bound by only using a single data set
\mathcal{S}_{U}
and
\mathcal{S}_{O}
.

For each expectation values
E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)
and
E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)
defined in Eqs. (
5
,
6
), we begin by estimating the quantity
F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)=(d+2)E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)-(d+1)E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)
via Eq. (
3
). Then, we collect the data set
\tilde{F}_{e}(\varrho)=\{F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)\}_{l=1}^{N}
and construct the confidence interval of
F(\varrho)
,
[F_{\mathrm{lb}},F_{\mathrm{ub}}]
, where the lower bound and upper bound are

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{lb}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{F}_{e}(\varrho)\right]-\frac{st_{\alpha,N-1}}{\sqrt{N}},

(8)

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{ub}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{F}_{e}(\varrho)\right]+\frac{st_{\alpha,N-1}}{\sqrt{N}}.

(9)

Here
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{F}_{e}(\varrho)]
and
s
are the sample mean and sample standard deviation of the data set
\tilde{F}_{e}(\varrho)
, respectively. The positive factor
t_{\alpha,N-1}
is the upper percentage point of the
t
-distribution with
N-1
degrees of freedom
[
58
]
and the confidence level (CL)
\alpha\in(0,1)
. See details in Appendix
C
. Note that
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{F}_{e}(\varrho)]
is an unbiased estimator of
F(\varrho)
shown in Appendix
B
. Finally, we use the lower bound
F_{\mathrm{lb}}
as an estimator of
F(\varrho)
and thus obtain a
\mathrm{SN}
estimator
\mu_{\mathrm{est}}
based on Eq. (
4
) by replacing
F(\varrho)
with
F_{\mathrm{lb}}
.

The basic idea of our algorithm is that we treat the fidelity
F(\varrho)
as an unknown mean of a population. After sampling random unitaries and orthogonal matrices, we estimate the confidence interval of the unknown mean by using statistical methods
[
45
]
. We have several remarks on the algorithm. First, confidence intervals are random quantities varying from sample to sample. Whether the constructed confidence interval covers the true fidelity
F(\varrho)
or not depends on the CL. A CL
\alpha
implies that
\alpha\times 100\%
confidence intervals contain
F(\varrho)
by repeated sampling. On the other hand, a trade-off exists between the CL and the estimated precision of the
\mathrm{SN}
. A higher CL induces a wider confidence interval of the fidelity
F(\varrho)
, thus corresponding to a smaller lower bound of the
\mathrm{SN}
for a fixed
N
. For estimating the
\mathrm{SN}
, a higher CL is desired since we need to ensure that the obtained confidence interval covers the fidelity
F_{\mathrm{lb}}
with greater certainty.

Moreover, when the number of samples is sufficiently large (often empirically taken as
N\geq 30
), the algorithm is valid based on the central limit theorem
[
45
]
. For the small sample size, using the
t
-distribution to construct a confidence interval assumes that the unknown population is normal. However, the
t
distribution-based confidence interval is relatively robust to this assumption
[
45
]
. See examples in Appendix
D
.

|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|
under a random noise channel, and cases for a finite number of measurements per random operation, are provided in Appendix
D
.

Conclusion and outlooks
—We have introduced a criterion and a classical post-processing algorithm to estimate the Schmidt number of bipartite high-dimensional states by considering the practical experimental challenges. The proposed method offers several significant advantages. First, various experimental platforms can randomly select projections that align with their specific device, as long as the random operations satisfy the properties of a unitary or orthogonal
2
-design. In contrast, the MUBs criterion requires experimental platforms to measure the state on MUBs, which is challenging for practical physical platforms such as cold-atom systems
[
21
]
.

Second, our results rely on the first-order measurement information of observables and require a
2
-design of unitary and orthogonal matrices. Existing high-order randomized measurements
[
77
,
39
]
involve unitary
4
-design, which construction is a challenging task
[
2
]
. Finally, our criterion only requires measuring a single measurement basis, reducing the required experimental controls to a single channel independent of the local dimension
d
. Thus, our approach applies to many platforms, including integrated-optics platforms
[
56
]
, and is also practical for detecting time-bin entangled states
[
43
]
.

There are several directions for future research. First, it would be interesting to generalize our work to other entanglement witnesses beyond the maximally entangled state. Next, one may study the performance of the proposed classical estimation method for high-order moments in randomized measurements
[
41
,
76
,
8
,
6
,
18
,
81
]
, see generalizations in Appendix
E
and
F
. Finally, one may study the potential of our approach to the multipartite case
[
9
]
. Our results would also make it possible to experimentally characterize the entanglement of high-dimensional quantum channels
[
19
]
.

Proof of the observation

Before proving the observation in the main text, we present a useful corollary showing how to calculate Haar integrals over the unitary and orthogonal groups. The proof of Corollary

Corollary 1
.

Given an bipartite operator
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathds{C}^{d}\otimes\mathds{C}^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle\int dU\,U^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{A}U^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}

\displaystyle=a_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}+a_{2}\mathbb{S},

(12)

\displaystyle\int dO\,O^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{A}O^{\top\otimes 2}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}+c_{2}\mathbb{S}+c_{3}\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}},

(13)

where the coefficients are

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a_{1}=\frac{{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A})-d^{-1}{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{A})}{d^{2}-1}\\
a_{2}=\frac{{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{A})-d^{-1}{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A})}{d^{2}-1}\end{cases}\penalty\ \mathrm{and}\penalty\ \begin{cases}c_{1}=\frac{(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{A})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}}\mathcal{A})}{d(d-1)(d+2)}\\
c_{2}=\frac{-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A})+(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{A})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}}\mathcal{A})}{d(d-1)(d+2)}\\
c_{3}=\frac{-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{A})-{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{A})+(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}}\mathcal{A})}{d(d-1)(d+2)}\end{cases}

(14)

Now, we prove Observation 1 in the main text.

Observation 1
.

For any bipartite state
\varrho
of equal dimension
d
, the fidelity
F(\varrho)
is given by

\displaystyle F(\varrho)=(d+2)\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)-(d+1)\mathcal{R}(\varrho).

(15)

The Schmidt number of
\varrho
is lower bounded by

\displaystyle\mu=\begin{cases}1,&F(\varrho)=0,\\
\lceil dF(\varrho)\rceil,&F(\varrho)\in(0,1],\\
\end{cases}

(16)

where the
\lceil\cdot\rceil
is ceil function.

Proof.

Given rank-1 observables
\mathcal{M}=|j\rangle\langle j|
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{M})={\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2})={\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2})={\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2})={\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{M}^{2})=1.

(17)

where
|j\rangle
are computational basis of a
d
dimension system. The moment
\mathcal{R}(\varrho)
with observable
\mathcal{M}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\varrho)

\displaystyle=\int dU{\mathrm{tr}}[(U\otimes U)\varrho(U^{{\dagger}}\otimes U^{{\dagger}})(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M})]=\int dU{\mathrm{tr}}[\varrho(U^{{\dagger}}\otimes U^{{\dagger}})(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M})(U\otimes U)]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho\int dU(U\otimes U)(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M})(U^{{\dagger}}\otimes U^{{\dagger}})\right]=\frac{{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho(\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}+\mathbb{S})\right]}{d(d+1)}=\frac{1+{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S})}{d(d+1)},

(18)

where the fourth equation holds since Eq. (
12
) in corollary
1
.

The moment
\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)
with observable
\mathcal{M}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}

\displaystyle=\int dO{\mathrm{tr}}[(O\otimes O)\varrho(O^{\top}\otimes O^{\top})(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M})]={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho\int dU(O^{\top}\otimes O^{\top})(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M})(O\otimes O)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho\int dU(O\otimes O)(\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M})(O^{\top}\otimes O^{\top})\right]=\frac{{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho\left(\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}+\mathbb{S}+\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}}\right)\right]}{d(d+2)}=\frac{1+{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\varrho\mathbb{S}\right)+{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\varrho\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}}\right)}{d(d+2)},

(19)

where the last equation is true by using Eq. (
13
) of corollary
1
.

Based on Eqs. (
18
,
19
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}})

\displaystyle=d(d+2)\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)-d(d+1)\mathcal{R}(\varrho).

(20)

Notice that the fidelity-based witness is
\mathcal{W}_{\mu}=\mu\mathbbm{1}_{d}^{\otimes 2}/d-|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|
, where
|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}|jj\rangle/\sqrt{d}
. For any state
\varrho
with Schmidt number
\leq\mu
, we have
{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{W}_{\mu}\varrho)\geq 0
. Recall that
|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle
can be expressed as

\displaystyle|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j,k=0}^{d-1}|j\rangle\langle k|\otimes|j\rangle\langle k|=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j,k=0}^{d-1}|j\rangle\langle k|\otimes(|k\rangle\langle j|)^{\top}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}}.

(21)

As a result, the fidelity for any state
\varrho
is

\displaystyle F(\varrho)={\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|)=\frac{1}{d}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}})=(d+2)\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)-(d+1)\mathcal{R}(\varrho),

(22)

which is the first result of observation 1.

For any state
\varrho
with Schmidt number
\mu
,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{W}_{\mu}\varrho)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu-{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}})}{d}=\frac{\mu}{d}-F(\varrho)\geq 0.

(23)

The above inequality also implies that for any state
\varrho
with Schmidt number
\mu
, we have

\displaystyle dF(\varrho)\leq\mu.

(24)

Thus, we finish the proof.
∎

From the proof, the choice of observable depends on the moments
\mathcal{R}(\varrho)
and
\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)
which are used to estimate
{\mathrm{tr}}[\varrho\mathbb{S}]
and
{\mathrm{tr}}[\varrho\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}}]
, respectively. Hence, the observable must be selected satisfying
a_{2}\neq 0
and
c_{3}\neq 0
in Eqs.(
12
,
13
). Without loss of generality, we assume a diagonal observable of the form
\mathcal{M}=\mathrm{diag}(m_{1},m_{2},\cdots,m_{r},0,\cdots,0)
with rank
r
and nonzero real numbers
m_{1},\cdots,m_{r}
. These parameters can be chosen such that the conditions
a_{2}\neq 0
and
c_{3}\neq 0
hold. In practice, however, we prefer the simplest structure that satisfies the requirements, thereby easing experimental implementation. It turns out that a rank-one projection
\mathcal{M}=|j\rangle\langle j|
suffices, where
\{|j\rangle\}_{j=0}^{d-1}
is the computational basis in a
d

\displaystyle a_{1}=a_{2}=\frac{1}{d(d+1)},c_{1}=c_{2}=c_{3}=\frac{1}{d(d+2)},

since
{\mathrm{tr}}(\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2})={\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2})={\mathrm{tr}}(\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2})=1

In summary, fidelity estimation can be achieved using only a rank-one projection. This choice not only fulfills the theoretical requirements but also offers a more experimentally friendly scheme.

Appendix B
Variance estimation

The fidelity is estimated via

\displaystyle F_{e}(\varrho)=(d+2)\mathcal{Q}_{e}(\varrho)-(d+1)\mathcal{R}_{e}(\varrho),

(26)

where the estimated moments
\mathcal{R}_{e}(\varrho)=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{U}]
and
\mathcal{Q}_{e}(\varrho)=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}_{O}]
are defined as the mean values of
\mathcal{S}_{U}
and
\mathcal{S}_{O}
, respectively. Equivalently, we obtain an estimator
F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)=(d+2)E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)-(d+1)E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)
, where

\displaystyle E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)={\mathrm{tr}}[\varrho U_{l}^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}U_{l}^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}],E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)={\mathrm{tr}}[\varrho O_{l}^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}O_{l}^{\top\otimes 2}].

(27)

So, the variance of
F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)
depends on the variance of
E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)
and
E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)
, such as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]

\displaystyle=(d+2)^{2}\mathrm{Var}\left[E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]+(d+1)^{2}\mathrm{Var}\left[E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right].

(28)

The following two subsections demonstrate that the variance of the estimator
F_{e}(\varrho)
is
\mathrm{Var}\left[F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]=\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
scaling as
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
in the worst case. This result relies on the variance bound
\mathrm{Var}\left[E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]=\mathrm{Var}\left[E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]=\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})
which is established in next two subsections. In contrast, for two specific states
\varrho_{\mathrm{iso}}^{v},\varrho_{\mathrm{deph}}^{v}
, we find that the variance
\mathrm{Var}\left[F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]=\mathcal{O}(1)
, and thus becomes independent of the local dimension
d
. This result is proved in the final subsection.

If
N
experimental data are available,
F_{e}^{(1)}(\varrho),F_{e}^{(2)}(\varrho),\cdots,F_{e}^{(N)}(\varrho)
, the unbiased estimator can be constructed from the empirical mean
\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)
. The estimator
\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)
is an unbiased estimator since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]=F(\varrho),

(29)

where the last equation holds due to the following equation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]

\displaystyle=(d+2)\mathbb{E}\left[E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]-(d+1)\mathbb{E}\left[E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]

\displaystyle=(d+2)\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{tr}}[\varrho O_{l}^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}O_{l}^{\top\otimes 2}]\right]-(d+1)\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{tr}}[\varrho U_{l}^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}U_{l}^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}]\right]

\displaystyle=(d+2)\int dO{\mathrm{tr}}[\varrho O_{l}^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}O_{l}^{\top\otimes 2}]-(d+1)\int dU{\mathrm{tr}}[\varrho U_{l}^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}U^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}]

\displaystyle=(d+2)\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)-(d+1)\mathcal{R}(\varrho)

\displaystyle=F(\varrho).

(30)

The mean square error of the estimator
\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)
depends on its variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)\right]=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\mathrm{Var}\left[F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right].

(31)

Based on Chebyshev’s inequality, the failure probability of
|\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)-\mathbb{E}[\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)]|\geq\epsilon
for a precision
\epsilon
has an upper bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left(|\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)-\mathbb{E}[\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)]|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)\right]}{\epsilon^{2}}.

(32)

To make sure that the failure probability is at most
\delta
, let
\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)\right]}{\epsilon^{2}}\leq\delta
. For the general case, the variance
\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)\right]=\frac{\mathcal{O}(d^{2}){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})}{N}
and thus the number of samples
N=\frac{\mathcal{O}(d^{2}){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})}{\delta\epsilon^{2}}
, which depends on local dimension
d
. However, for two special cases
\varrho_{\mathrm{iso}}^{v},\varrho_{\mathrm{deph}}^{v}
, we have
\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{F}_{e}(\varrho)\right]=\frac{\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})}{N}
and thus the number of samples
N=\frac{\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})}{\delta\epsilon^{2}}
, which is independent of on local dimension
d
.

B.1
Variance of unitary item

To estimate the variance of the unitary item
E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)
, we first decompose the state
\varrho
in the generalized Gell-Mann matrices basis
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}

[
35
,
5
]
such as
\varrho=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j,k=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jk}\lambda_{j}\otimes\lambda_{k}
. Here, the operators
\lambda_{j}
satisfying
{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j})=0
for

and
{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k})=d\delta_{jk}
. Then, the variance of

can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\varrho^{\otimes 2}U_{l}^{\otimes 4}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 4}U_{l}^{{\dagger}\otimes 4}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho^{\otimes 2}\int dUU^{\otimes 4}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 4}U^{{\dagger}\otimes 4}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{4}}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\sum_{j_{2},k_{2}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{j_{2}k_{2}}\lambda_{j_{1}}\otimes\lambda_{k_{1}}\otimes\lambda_{j_{2}}\otimes\lambda_{k_{2}}\int dUU^{\otimes 4}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 4}U^{{\dagger}\otimes 4}\right].

(33)

Based on the Schur-Weyl duality of the unitary group
[
25
,
10
,
27
,
12
,
84
]
, the operator

\displaystyle\int dUU^{\otimes 4}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 4}U^{{\dagger}\otimes 4}

\displaystyle=\frac{24}{\Delta}C_{(4)},\Delta=d(d+1)(d+2)(d+3),

(34)

where the operator
[
84
]

\displaystyle C_{(4)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{24}P_{(1)}+\frac{1}{24}\left(P_{(12)}+P_{(13)}+P_{(14)}+P_{(23)}+P_{(24)}+P_{(34)}\right)+\frac{1}{24}\left(P_{(12)(34)}+P_{(13)(24)}+P_{(14)(23)}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{24}\left(P_{(123)}+P_{(132)}+P_{(124)}+P_{(142)}+P_{(134)}+P_{(143)}+P_{(243)}+P_{(234)}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{24}\left(P_{(1234)}+P_{(1243)}+P_{(1324)}+P_{(1342)}+P_{(1423)}+P_{(1432)}\right),

(35)

and

denotes the permutation operator of the permutation
\pi
. See Example 3.36. in
[
84
]
. Thus, the variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{4}\Delta}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\sum_{j_{2},k_{2}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{j_{2}k_{2}}\lambda_{j_{1}}\otimes\lambda_{k_{1}}\otimes\lambda_{j_{2}}\otimes\lambda_{k_{2}}\int dUU^{\otimes 4}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 4}U^{{\dagger}\otimes 4}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{4}\Delta}\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\sum_{j_{2},k_{2}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{j_{2}k_{2}}\left[d^{2}\delta_{j_{1}k_{1}}\delta_{j_{2}k_{2}}+d^{2}\delta_{j_{1}j_{2}}\delta_{k_{1}k_{2}}+d^{2}\delta_{j_{1}k_{2}}\delta_{k_{1}j_{2}}+6{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j_{1}}\lambda_{k_{1}}\lambda_{j_{2}}\lambda_{k_{2}})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{4}\Delta}\left[d^{2}\left(\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}j_{1}}\right)^{2}+d^{2}\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}^{2}+d^{2}\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{k_{1}j_{1}}+6\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},k_{1}\\
j_{2},k_{2}\end{subarray}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{j_{2}k_{2}}{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j_{1}}\lambda_{k_{1}}\lambda_{j_{2}}\lambda_{k_{2}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{4}\Delta}\left[d^{6}\left[{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{1}\varrho)\right]^{2}+d^{4}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})+d^{4}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})+6\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},k_{1}\\
j_{2},k_{2}\end{subarray}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{j_{2}k_{2}}{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j_{1}}\lambda_{k_{1}}\lambda_{j_{2}}\lambda_{k_{2}})\right],

(36)

where the second inequality holds since
\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}^{2}=d^{2}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})
and

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{k_{1}j_{1}}\leq\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}|T_{j_{1}k_{1}}||T_{k_{1}j_{1}}|\leq\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\frac{|T_{j_{1}k_{1}}|^{2}+|T_{k_{1}j_{1}}|^{2}}{2}\leq\sum_{j,k=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jk}^{2}=d^{2}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2}).

(37)

\Phi_{1}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}\lambda_{j}\otimes\lambda_{j}

and the overlap

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{1}\varrho)=\frac{1}{d^{4}}\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{l_{1}l_{2}}d\delta_{jl_{1}}d\delta_{jl_{2}}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jj},

(38)

which implies
\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jj}=d^{2}{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{1}\varrho)
. Now, we consider the item

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},k_{1}\\
j_{2},k_{2}\end{subarray}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{j_{2}k_{2}}{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j_{1}}\lambda_{k_{1}}\lambda_{j_{2}}\lambda_{k_{2}})

j_{2},k_{2}\end{subarray}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\left|T_{j_{1}k_{1}}\right|\left|T_{j_{2}k_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=d^{2}\left(\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\left|T_{j_{1}k_{1}}\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}\left(\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}1^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=d^{2}\times d^{4}\times d^{2}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})

\displaystyle=d^{8}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2}),

(39)

where the first inequality holds since
|{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j_{1}}\lambda_{k_{1}}\lambda_{j_{2}}\lambda_{k_{2}})|\leq\sqrt{{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j_{1}}^{2}){\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{k_{1}}^{2}){\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j_{2}}^{2}){\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{k_{2}}^{2})}=d^{2}
and the second inequality uses the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Thus, the variance has an upper bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{4}\Delta}\left[d^{6}\left[{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{1}\varrho)\right]^{2}+d^{4}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})+d^{4}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})+6\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},k_{1}\\
j_{2},k_{2}\end{subarray}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{j_{2}k_{2}}{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j_{1}}\lambda_{k_{1}}\lambda_{j_{2}}\lambda_{k_{2}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{4}\Delta}\left[d^{6}\left[{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{1}\varrho)\right]^{2}+d^{4}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})+d^{4}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})+6d^{8}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{6}\left[{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{1}\varrho)\right]^{2}+d^{4}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})+d^{4}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})+6d^{8}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})}{d^{5}(d+1)(d+2)(d+3)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2}).

(40)

By examining the scaling of each term in the numerator relative to the denominator
d^{5}(d+1)(d+2)(d+3)\sim d^{8}
, the dominant contribution for large

is identified as the term proportional to

. All other terms (

and

and
1/d^{4}
and vanish as

\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})
, which implies that the variance of the estimator
E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)
does not grow with the local dimension
d
; it is ultimately controlled only by the purity of the bipartite state.

B.2
Variance of orthogonal item

Similarly, the variance of
E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho^{\otimes 2}O_{l}^{\otimes 4}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 4}O_{l}^{{\dagger}\otimes 4}\right]\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho^{\otimes 2}\int dOO^{\otimes 4}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 4}O^{\top\otimes 4}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{4}}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\sum_{j_{2},k_{2}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{j_{2}k_{2}}\lambda_{j_{1}}\otimes\lambda_{k_{1}}\otimes\lambda_{j_{2}}\otimes\lambda_{k_{2}}\int dOO^{\otimes 4}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 4}O^{{\dagger}\otimes 4}\right].

(41)

Based on the Schur-Weyl duality for the orthogonal group

,

\displaystyle\int dOO^{\otimes 4}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 4}O^{\top\otimes 4}

(42)

F_{d}

\mathcal{B}_{4}

{\mathrm{tr}}(M),{\mathrm{tr}}(M^{2}),{\mathrm{tr}}(M^{3}),{\mathrm{tr}}(M^{4})

since

[

has an upper bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{4}}\sum_{x}a_{x}\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\sum_{j_{2},k_{2}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{j_{2}k_{2}}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\lambda_{j_{1}}\otimes\lambda_{k_{1}}\otimes\lambda_{j_{2}}\otimes\lambda_{k_{2}}C_{x}\right],

(43)

based on the Supplemental Theorem 6 in Ref.
[

. The variance has an upper bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{4}}\sum_{x}a_{x}\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\sum_{j_{2},k_{2}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}k_{1}}T_{j_{2}k_{2}}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\lambda_{j_{1}}\otimes\lambda_{k_{1}}\otimes\lambda_{j_{2}}\otimes\lambda_{k_{2}}C_{x}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{4}}\sum_{x}a_{x}\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\sum_{j_{2},k_{2}=0}^{d^{2}-1}|T_{j_{1}k_{1}}||T_{j_{2}k_{2}}|d^{2}

B.3
Special cases for different states

The above bound is the worst case for general states. Here, we consider two important cases:

\displaystyle\varrho_{\mathrm{iso}}^{v}

\displaystyle=v|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|+[(1-v)/d^{2}]\mathbbm{1}_{d}^{\otimes 2},

\displaystyle\varrho_{\mathrm{deph}}^{v}

\displaystyle=v|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|+[(1-v)/d]\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}|jj\rangle\langle jj|,

(47)

where the parameter
v\in[0,1]
.

In particular, the correlation matrices of the states
\varrho_{\mathrm{iso}}^{v}
and
\varrho_{\mathrm{deph}}^{v}
are diagonal matrices. Thus, the following inequality is true,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},k_{1}\\
j_{2},k_{2}\end{subarray}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\left|T_{j_{1}k_{1}}\right|\left|T_{j_{2}k_{2}}\right|\left|{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j_{1}}\lambda_{k_{1}}\lambda_{j_{2}}\lambda_{k_{2}})\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\left|T_{j_{1}j_{1}}\right|\left|T_{j_{2}j_{2}}\right|\left|{\mathrm{tr}}(\lambda_{j_{1}}\lambda_{j_{1}}\lambda_{j_{2}}\lambda_{j_{2}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\left|T_{j_{1}j_{1}}\right|\left|T_{j_{2}j_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=d^{2}\left(\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\left|T_{j_{1}j_{1}}\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}\left(\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}1^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{j_{1}j_{1}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=d^{2}\times d^{2}\times d^{2}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})

\displaystyle=d^{6}{\mathrm{tr}}\left(\varrho^{2}\right).

(48)

The above inequality implies that the variance
\mathrm{Var}\left[E_{O}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]=\mathrm{Var}\left[E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]=\mathcal{O}(1/d^{2}){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})
. Then, the variance of the estimator
F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)
reduces to
\mathrm{Var}\left[F_{e}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]=\mathcal{O}(1)
, which is independent of local dimension
d
.

More generally, we consider the random noise state

\displaystyle\varrho_{\mathrm{rand}}^{v}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=v|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|+(1-v)\sigma,

(49)

where
v\in[0,1]
and the random states
\sigma
is sampled according to the Hilbert-Schmidt metric
[
87
]
. From the derivation, we note that the dominate item in the variance estimation for
\varrho_{\mathrm{rand}}^{v}(\sigma)
is the quantity
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\left|\hat{T}_{j_{1}k_{1}}\right|
, where
\hat{T}_{j_{1}k_{1}}
is the correlation matrix of
\sigma
. This claim is true since

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\left|vT_{j_{1}k_{1}}+(1-v)\hat{T}_{j_{1}k_{1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}v\left|T_{j_{1}k_{1}}\right|+(1-v)\left|\hat{T}_{j_{1}k_{1}}\right|

\displaystyle=v\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\left|T_{j_{1}k_{1}}\right|+(1-v)\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\left|\hat{T}_{j_{1}k_{1}}\right|

\displaystyle=vd^{2}+(1-v)\sum_{j_{1},k_{1}=0}^{d^{2}-1}\left|\hat{T}_{j_{1}k_{1}}\right|,

(50)

where
T_{j_{1}k_{1}}=\{1,-1\}
is the correlation matrix of the state
|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|
.

To see the scaling of
\mathcal{L}
with local dimension
d
, we numerically simulate
10^{3}
random states
\sigma,
and we find that
\mathcal{L}\leq d^{2}
. This bound implies the variance of the state
\varrho_{\mathrm{rand}}^{v}(\sigma)
is also
\mathcal{O}(1)
independent of local dimension
d
.

B.4
Variance estimation with finite samples

In the previous section, we assumed the number of samples for estimating the expectation value of observables is infinite. However, this assumption is impossible because practical cases always obtain a finite number of samples to estimate the expectation values. In a practical situation, a finite number of samples is available.

Now, to estimate the fidelity
F(\varrho)
, the total number of measurements is denoted as
N_{\mathrm{tot}}=N\times K
. Here,
N
is the number of random unitaries or orthogonal matrices, and
K
is the number of measurements for a fixed unitary or orthogonal matrix. For the
l
-th unitary/orthogonal, the expectation value
E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho U^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}U^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}\right]
is estimated on a quantum device. To do this, we measure the expectation value
\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}
on
K
copies of
\varrho
and record all
K
measurement outcome
X_{1}^{(l)},\cdots,X_{K}^{(l)}
. We construct an unbiased estimation of the expectation value
E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)
as
\hat{X}^{(l)}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}X_{k}^{(l)}
since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\hat{X}^{(l)}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}X_{k}^{(l)}\right]=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[X_{k}^{(l)}\right]=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)=E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho).

(51)

The variance of the estimator
\hat{X}^{(l)}
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{X}^{(l)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{Var}\left[X_{k}^{(l)}\right]=\frac{KE_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\left[1-E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]}{K^{2}}=\frac{E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\left[1-E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]}{K}.

(52)

The unbiased estimator for the moment
\mathcal{R}(\varrho)
can be given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{R}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{X}^{(l)}=\frac{1}{NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}X_{k}^{(l)}.

(53)

The unbiasedness of this estimator can be verified since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\hat{\mathcal{R}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\frac{1}{NK}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}X_{k}^{(l)}\right]=\mathcal{R}(\varrho).

(54)

Now, we derive the variance of the estimator
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
. By the definition of variance, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{\mathcal{R}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\hat{\mathcal{R}}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\frac{1}{N^{2}K^{2}}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}=1}^{N}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{K}X_{k_{1}}^{(l_{1})}X_{k_{2}}^{(l_{2})}\right]-\left[\mathcal{R}(\varrho)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}K^{2}}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}=1}^{N}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[X_{k_{1}}^{(l_{1})}X_{k_{2}}^{(l_{2})}\right]-\left[\mathcal{R}(\varrho)\right]^{2}.

(55)

For
l_{1}=l_{2}=l
, the first term is

\displaystyle\mathrm{first\penalty\ term}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}K^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[X_{k_{1}}^{(l)}X_{k_{2}}^{(l)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{K}X_{k_{1}}^{(l_{1})}\right)^{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\frac{E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)}{K}+\frac{K-1}{K}\left[E_{U}^{(l)}(\varrho)\right]^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{NK}\mathcal{R}(\varrho)+\frac{K-1}{NK}\int dU\left[E_{U}(\varrho)\right]^{2}

(56)

The item

\displaystyle\int dU\left[E_{U}(\varrho)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\int{\mathrm{tr}}\left[U^{\otimes 2}\varrho U^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}\otimes U^{\otimes 2}\varrho U^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho^{\otimes 2}U^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}U^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}\right]=\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2}),

(57)

where the last equation has been proved in the last subsection.

For
l_{1}\neq l_{2}
, the first term is

\displaystyle\mathrm{first\penalty\ term}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}K^{2}}\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}=1}^{N}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[X_{k_{1}}^{(l_{1})}X_{k_{2}}^{(l_{2})}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{K}\frac{1}{K}X_{k_{1}}^{(l_{1})}\right]\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\sum_{k_{2}=1}^{K}\frac{1}{K}X_{k_{2}}^{(l_{2})}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{l_{1}\neq l_{2}=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[E_{U}^{(l_{1})}(\varrho)E_{U}^{(l_{2})}(\varrho)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{N-1}{N}\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[E_{U}^{(l_{1})}(\varrho)E_{U}^{(l_{2})}(\varrho)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{N-1}{N}\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[E_{U}^{(l_{1})}(\varrho)\right]\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[E_{U}^{(l_{2})}(\varrho)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{N-1}{N}\left[\mathcal{R}(\varrho)\right]^{2}.

(58)

Hence, the variance of
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{\mathcal{R}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{NK}\mathcal{R}(\varrho)+\frac{K-1}{NK}\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})+\frac{N-1}{N}\left[\mathcal{R}(\varrho)\right]^{2}-\left[\mathcal{R}(\varrho)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{NK}\mathcal{R}(\varrho)+\frac{K-1}{NK}\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2}).

(59)

Similarly, we can calculate the variance of the orthogonal part associated with the moment
\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)
. Based on the analysis of the last subsection, we find that the variance
\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{NK}\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)+\frac{K-1}{NK}\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})
, where
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}
is an unbiased estimation of the moment
\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)
. In summary, the unbiased estimation of the fidelity can be constructed by
\hat{F}=(d+2)\hat{\mathcal{Q}}-(d+1)\hat{\mathcal{R}}
. We find the variance of the estimator
\hat{F}
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{F}\right]

\displaystyle=(d+2)^{2}\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{\mathcal{Q}}\right]+(d+1)^{2}\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{\mathcal{R}}\right]

\displaystyle<\frac{(d+2)^{2}}{NK}\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)+\frac{(d+2)^{2}(K-1)}{NK}\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})+\frac{(d+1)^{2}}{NK}\mathcal{R}(\varrho)+\frac{(d+1)^{2}(K-1)}{NK}\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{(d+2)^{2}}{NK}\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)+\frac{(d+1)^{2}}{NK}\mathcal{R}(\varrho)+2\frac{(d+2)^{2}(K-1)}{NK}\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2d+3+(2d+3){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S})+(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}})}{dNK}+2\frac{(d+2)^{2}(K-1)}{NK}\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2}).

(60)

Here, we introduce a pure state
\Phi_{1}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}\lambda_{j}\otimes\lambda_{j}
since
{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{1}^{2})=1
and the overlap
{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{1}\varrho)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}T_{jj}=d^{-1}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S})
, which implies
{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S})=d{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{1}\varrho)
. The trace
{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}})=d{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|)
. Thus, the variance turns to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{F}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2d+3+(2d+3){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S})+(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}})}{dNK}+2\frac{(d+2)^{2}(K-1)}{NK}\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2d+3+(2d+3)d{\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{1}\varrho)+(d+1)d{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|)}{dNK}+2\frac{(d+2)^{2}(K-1)}{NK}\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2+3/d+(2d+3){\mathrm{tr}}(\Phi_{1}\varrho)+(d+1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|)}{NK}+2\frac{(d+2)^{2}(K-1)}{NK}\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})

\displaystyle<\frac{6+3/d+3d}{NK}+2\frac{(d+2)^{2}(K-1)}{NK}\mathcal{O}(1){\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{O}(3d)}{NK}+\frac{\mathcal{O}(d^{2})(K-1)}{NK}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2}).

(61)

In the limit of an infinite number of measurements per random operation, we have
\lim_{K\to\infty}\left[\frac{\mathcal{O}(3d)}{NK}+\frac{\mathcal{O}(d^{2})(K-1)}{NK}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})\right]=\frac{\mathcal{O}(d^{2})}{N}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})
which matches the result in the previous subsections. The total number of measurements required is
N_{\mathrm{tot}}=NK=\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/\delta\epsilon^{2})
. Since
K
is associated with the precision of expectation values of the observable, we care about the number of random operations. As a result,
N=\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
for general states, which has the same scaling as the direct fidelity estimation.

Now, consider the special case where the state is a maximally entangled state under different noise channels. In this case, the variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\hat{F}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{O}(3d)}{NK}+\frac{K-1}{NK}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2}).

(62)

Again, in the limit of an infinite number of measurements per random operation, we have
\lim_{K\to\infty}\left[\frac{\mathcal{O}(3d)}{NK}+\frac{K-1}{NK}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})\right]=\frac{1}{N}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho^{2})
, which also matches the result in the previous subsection. Since
K
is associated with the precision of expectation values of the observable, we care about the number of random operations. As a result,
N=\mathcal{O}(1)
for these special states by letting
K=\mathcal{O}(d)
. Compared with direct fidelity estimation
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
and the MUBs method
\mathcal{O}(md)
, the randomized projection method in this work achieves
\mathcal{O}(1)
for maximally entangled states under different noises, making it more efficient for high-dimensional quantum states.

Note that in the proof of the variance we assume sampled random unitaries are unitary
4
-design and random orthogonal matrices are orthogonal
4
-design.

Appendix C
Confidence interval for the mean and the bootstrapping
resample technique

Suppose we are interested in estimating a population parameter, such as the mean
g
of a population, from a random sample. Let
X=\{X_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
be a sample of
N
independent and identically distributed observations drawn from the population. The population distribution is unknown, and we do not impose any prior assumption about its form.

A common point estimator for the population mean
g
is the sample mean
\bar{X}=\mathbb{E}[x]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}
, which is an unbiased estimation of the mean
g
. When the population variance is unknown, the confidence interval for
g
is constructed by

\displaystyle\bar{X}\pm t_{\alpha,N-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}.

(63)

Here,
s=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(X_{j}-\bar{X}\right)^{2}}{N-1}}
is the sample standard deviation, and the critical factor
t_{\alpha,N-1}
comes from the
t
-distribution. Note that
t_{\alpha,N-1}
depends on the confidence level
\alpha
and sample size
N
. For example,
t_{95\%,11}\neq t_{95\%,20}
.

The central limit Theorem
[
52
,
4
]
states that for larger
N
(often empirically taken as
N\geq 30
), the sample mean
\mathbb{E}[x]
is approximately normally distributed, with mean
g
and standard deviation
\sigma(\bar{X})=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}
. Regardless of the distribution of the population, as the sample size is increased, the sampling distribution of the sample mean becomes a normal distribution. That is, we can always obtain a confidence interval with sample size
N\geq 30
, and the population distribution is not important.

For a small sample size
N<30
, the confidence interval is only efficient when the population distribution is normal. If the assumption is violated, the constructed confidence interval may be inaccurate. However, the
t
distribution-based confidence interval is relatively robust to this assumption
[
45
]
.

Two of the traditional methods for obtaining a confidence interval for the mean of a non-normal distribution are the central limit theorem
[
52
,
4
]
, reported above, and the bootstrap resampling technique
[
17
,
16
,
15
]
. The bootstrap resampling technique is a popular non-parametric method and is useful for small sample sizes. The detailed process is the following
[
17
,
16
,
15
,
68
,
50
]
.

Input:
Given a data set
X=\{X_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
with
N
samples randomly generated from the population. Generally,
N<30
.

Step 1.
Resample the observed sample with replacement and calculate the
sample mean for this bootstrap sample.

Step 2.
Repeat
Step 1.

B
times.

Output:
Construct the confidence interval for the data set with size
B
by using the Eq. (
63
).

Using the above knowledge, we can estimate the fidelity in the main text. In particular, we express the fidelity as

\displaystyle F(\varrho)

\displaystyle=(d+2)\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)-(d+1)\mathcal{R}(\varrho)=(d+2)\int dOE_{O}(\varrho)-(d+1)\int dUE_{U}(\varrho)=\int dWF_{W}(\varrho),

(64)

where the quantity
F_{W}(\varrho)=(d+2)E_{O}(\varrho)-(d+1)E_{U}(\varrho)
. We can treat the fidelity
F(\varrho)
as a mean of an unknown population. Thus, we can estimate the fidelity
F(\varrho)
from a statistical view. After sampling
N
random unitaries and random orthogonal matrices, we obtain
N
fidelities estimated by a single unitary and a single orthogonal matrix. Except for obtaining a point estimation from finite samples, we also try to construct a confidence interval of the unknown mean,
F(\varrho)
. The construction approach follows Eq. (
63
).

Note that the population distribution underlying the mean
F(\varrho)
is unknown. Hence, it is unclear whether it follows a normal distribution. Empirically, the direct use of Eq. (
63
) for constructing a confidence interval typically requires a sample size
N\geq 30
. In our setting, however, obtaining a large number of samples is challenging because each measurement involves implementing a local high-dimensional unitary or orthogonal operation, which is experimentally demanding. We therefore aim to extract the maximum information from a limited set of experimental observations. Consequently, we prefer to work with a small number of random operations, typically
N<30
. When the population distribution is unknown, confidence intervals built from such small samples may, in principle, be inaccurate. Nevertheless, as noted earlier, the
t
-distribution-based interval is relatively robust to departures from normality
[
45
]
. As supported by SFig.
1
, this direct construction performs satisfactorily even for
N<30
.

d=20
and (b)
d=30

N=15

99.9\%
. We set the number of resampling

Appendix E
The entanglement quantification

Here, we show that the moments
\mathcal{R}(\varrho)
and
\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)
induce lower bounds of entanglement measures in quantum information.

An important entanglement measure is based on the trace distance,
E_{T}(\varrho)=\min_{\sigma\in\mathrm{SEP}}D_{T}(\varrho,\sigma)
, given by the smallest trace distance between
\varrho
and separable state set
\mathrm{SEP}

[
48
]
, where the trace distance is defined as half of the trace norm,
D_{T}(\varrho,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\|\varrho-\sigma\|_{{\mathrm{tr}}}
, and
\|C\|_{{\mathrm{tr}}}
denotes the trace norm of
C
, i.e., the sum of the absolute values of eigenvalues of
C
. We present our results as follows.

Observation 2
.

For any bipartite state
\varrho
of equal dimension
d
, the entanglement measure based on the trace distance
E_{T}(\varrho)
has a lower bound,

\displaystyle E_{T}(\varrho)\geq\frac{1}{d}-(d+2)\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)+(d+1)\mathcal{R}(\varrho).

(77)

Proof.

Let
\sigma_{\mathrm{opt}}
be the closest separable state. We have the inequality,
E_{T}(\varrho)=\frac{1}{2}\|\varrho-\sigma_{\mathrm{opt}}\|_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}|{\mathrm{tr}}[(\varrho-\sigma_{\mathrm{opt}})]|\geq\frac{1}{2}|{\mathrm{tr}}[(\varrho-\sigma_{\mathrm{opt}})\mathcal{W}_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}]|\geq-{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathcal{W}_{1})
, where
\mathcal{W}_{1}=\mathbbm{1}_{d}^{\otimes 2}/d-|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|
is the fidelity-based entanglement witness
[
28
]
and
\mathcal{W}_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}=2\mathcal{W}_{1}-(\frac{2}{d}-1)\mathbbm{1}_{d}^{\otimes^{2}}
. The first inequality holds due to the absolute value inequality. The second inequality is true since the absolute values of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{W}_{1}^{{}^{\prime}}
are equal to one. The third inequality is based on the property of the entanglement witness
\mathcal{W}_{1}
. By expressing
{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathcal{W}_{1})
in terms of
\mathcal{R}(\varrho)
and
\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)
based on observation 1,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathcal{W}_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}-{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|)=\frac{1}{d}-F(\varrho)=\frac{1}{d}-(d+2)\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)+(d+1)\mathcal{R}(\varrho),

(78)

we complete the proof.
∎

Following the Refs.
[
83
,
59
]
, other entanglement measures such as the concurrence
[
73
]
, the G-concurrence
[
26
]
, the entanglement of formation
[
74
]
, the geometric measure of entanglement
[
70
]
, and the robustness of entanglement
[
67
]
have a connection with the entanglement witness
\mathcal{W}_{1}
. Thus, these entanglement measures can also be bounded by a function of
\mathcal{R}(\varrho)
and
\mathcal{Q}(\varrho)
.

Appendix F
Randomized symmetric projections

In the main text, we propose a method to estimate the fidelity with the maximally entangled state using random unitaries and orthogonal matrices. Here, we note that the estimation of fidelity can be achieved by using only random unitaries.

Given an observable
\mathcal{M}=|j\rangle\langle j|
and random unitary
U
, the
integral

\displaystyle\int dUU^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}U^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}

\displaystyle=a_{1}\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}+a_{2}\mathbb{S}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}+\mathbb{S}}{d(d+1)},

(79)

where the last equation uses the Eq.(
13
). Thus,
\mathrm{SWAP}
operator can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle=d(d+1)\int dUU^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{M}^{\otimes 2}U^{{\dagger}\otimes 2}-\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d}=d(d+1)\int dU|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{j}|\otimes|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{j}|-\mathbbm{1}_{d}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d},

(80)

where the pure state
|e_{j}\rangle=U|j\rangle
. For a bipartite state
\varrho
, the fidelity is

\displaystyle F(\varrho)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho|\phi_{d}^{+}\rangle\langle\phi_{d}^{+}|)=\frac{1}{d}{\mathrm{tr}}(\varrho\mathbb{S}^{\top_{B}})

\displaystyle=(d+1)\int dU{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho U\mathcal{M}U^{{\dagger}}\otimes\left(U\mathcal{M}U^{{\dagger}}\right)^{\top}\right]-\frac{1}{d}

\displaystyle=(d+1)\int dU{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho U\mathcal{M}U^{{\dagger}}\otimes U^{*}\mathcal{M}^{\top}U^{T}\right]-\frac{1}{d}

\displaystyle=(d+1)\int dU{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho U\mathcal{M}U^{{\dagger}}\otimes U^{*}\mathcal{M}U^{T}\right]-\frac{1}{d}

\displaystyle=(d+1)\int dU{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho\left(|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{j}|\otimes|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{j}|\right)^{\top_{B}}\right]-\frac{1}{d}

\displaystyle=(d+1)\int dU{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho\left(|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{j}|\otimes|e_{j}^{*}\rangle\langle e_{j}^{*}|\right)\right]-\frac{1}{d},

(81)

where
|e_{j}^{*}\rangle
denotes the complex conjugate of
|e_{j}\rangle
. Hence, we require to perform
U\otimes U^{*}
on
\varrho
and then measure the product observable
|j\rangle\langle j|^{\otimes 2}
. This process equivalents to perform random symmetric projections
|e_{j}\rangle\langle e_{j}|\otimes|e_{j}^{*}\rangle\langle e_{j}^{*}|
on
\varrho
. The measured results can be used to estimate the fidelity by Eq.(
81
) and then infer the Schmidt number. In practice, the fidelity can be estimated by a finite number of random samples

\displaystyle F(\varrho)=(d+1)\sum_{l=1}^{N}{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\varrho\left(|e_{j}^{l}\rangle\langle e_{j}^{l}|\otimes|e_{j}^{l*}\rangle\langle e_{j}^{l*}|\right)\right]-\frac{1}{d}.

(82)

Remark that this method has also been proposed in Ref.
[
41
]
. Compared with our strategy in the main text, we use the same random operations, which can be achieved by using a fixed measurement channel. Moreover, we use random orthogonal matrices, which are only real-value matrices and thus are more easily performed experimentally. Interestingly, Eq.(
81
) introduces a random symmetric projection to detect the Schmidt number. In this view, the MUB criterion
[
46
]
is a special case of symmetric projections. Thus, it would be interesting to investigate the performance of randomized symmetric projections.
