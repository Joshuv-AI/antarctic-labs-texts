Fast convergence of Majorana Propagation for weakly interacting fermions
Giorgio Facelli, Hamza Fawzi, Omar Fawzi

Abstract
Simulating the time dynamics of an observable under Hamiltonian evolution is one of the most promising candidates for quantum advantage as we do not expect efficient classical algorithms for this problem except in restricted settings. Here, we introduce such a setting by showing that Majorana Propagation, a simple algorithm combining Trotter steps and truncations, efficiently finds a low-degree approximation of the time-evolved observable as soon as such an approximation exists. This provides the first provable guarantee about Majorana Propagation for Hamiltonian evolution. As an application of this result, we prove that Majorana Propagation can efficiently simulate the time dynamics of any sparse quartic Hamiltonian up to time t max ​ ( u ) t_{\textup{max}}(u) depending on the interaction strength u u . For a time horizon t ≤ t max ​ ( u ) t\leq t_{\textup{max}}(u) , the runtime of the algorithm is N O ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( t / ε ) ) N^{O(\log(t/\varepsilon))} where N N is the number of Majorana modes and ε \varepsilon is the error measured in the normalized Frobenius norm. Importantly, in the limit of small u u , t max ​ ( u ) t_{\textup{max}}(u) goes to + ∞ +\infty , formalizing the intuition that the algorithm is accurate at all times when the Hamiltonian is quadratic.

•

The only assumption on the Hamiltonian, beyond the fact that it is quartic, is that it is

Application to weakly interacting Hamiltonians

As an application of Theorem
1.1
, we consider the case of weakly interacting Hamiltonians of the form

H=H_{0}+uV

(7)

where
H_{0}
is quadratic,
V
is quartic, and

u=0
, then
\deg(A(t))=\deg(A)
for all
t\geq 0
, or equivalently that

\eta^{\star}(H,A,t,\ell)=0\quad\forall t\geq 0\text{ and }\ell\geq\deg(A).

(8)

Using perturbation-theory techniques, we show that for any observable
A
, there is a
t_{\textup{max}}(u)>0
such that
A(t)=e^{\textup{i}(H_{0}+uV)t}Ae^{-\textup{i}(H_{0}+uV)t}
can be very well approximated by a low-degree Majorana polynomial up to time
t_{\textup{max}}(u)
. More precisely, we prove the following.

Theorem 1.2

(Low-degree approximability of
A(t)
for weakly interacting interacting Hamiltonians)
.

Let
H=H(u)=H_{0}+uV
be a
\Delta
-sparse fermionic Hamiltonian where
H_{0}
and
V
are quadratic and quartic interactions, respectively. Let
A
be an arbitrary observable. Then, for all
t<t_{\textup{max}}(u):=\log(e/u)/(8e^{2}\Delta(\deg A+2))
,

\eta^{\star}_{\textup{F}}(H(u),A,t,\ell)\leq\dfrac{\left(t/t_{\textup{max}}(u)\right)^{(\ell-\deg(A))/2}}{1-t/t_{\textup{max}}(u)}\|A\|_{\textup{F}}.

(9)

An important feature of the theorem above is that
t_{\textup{max}}(u)\to+\infty
as

, and thus the Theorem recovers the well-known fact (
8
) for quadratic Hamiltonians. Also observe that if, e.g.,

then

\varepsilon

with a polynomial of degree

The proof of Theorem

Corollary 1.3

(Efficient simulation of weakly interacting Hamiltonians with Majorana Propagation)

Overview of techniques

The main technical ingredients in this paper are new Frobenius norm inequalities which depend on
degree
rather than system size. We highlight here two such inequalities.

•

Norm of commutator:
Observe that if
H
is a Hamiltonian on
N
modes, then
3
3

3

Indeed, note that the linear map
[H,\cdot]:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
can be written as
H\otimes 1-1\otimes H
and so has eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(H)-\lambda_{j}(H)
. We are using the fact that if
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
is a linear operator which is self-adjoint with respect to the trace inner product, then
\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|\mathcal{L}(A)\|_{\textup{F}}/\|A\|_{\textup{F}}
is equal to the spectral radius of
\mathcal{L}
.

\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{\|[H,A]\|_{\textup{F}}}{\|A\|_{\textup{F}}}=\lambda_{\max}(H)-\lambda_{\min}(H)\gtrsim\Omega(N).

(10)

We prove in Theorem
3.4
that if we restrict the maximization above to operators
A
which are of degree at most

, then the value is upper bounded by a quantity that only depends on
d
, and not on
N
. More precisely we show that if
H
is a quartic Hamiltonian which is

-sparse, then

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{A}\\
\deg(A)\leq d\end{subarray}}\frac{\|[H,A]\|_{\textup{F}}}{\|A\|_{\textup{F}}}\leq 2\Delta\sqrt{d(d+2)}.

(11)

•

Trotter error:
Assume now that
H
is a quartic Hamiltonian which is split into
G
groups, namely

H=H_{1}+\dots+H_{G},

and define, respectively

\displaystyle\tau_{t}(A)

\displaystyle=e^{\textup{i}Ht}Ae^{-\textup{i}Ht}

(12)

\displaystyle\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{t}(A)

\displaystyle=e^{\textup{i}H_{G}t}\dots e^{\textup{i}H_{1}t}Ae^{-\textup{i}H_{1}t}\dots e^{-\textup{i}H_{G}t}.

An important question in the simulation of quantum dynamics is to bound the error
\|\tau_{t}(A)-\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{t}(A)\|/\|A\|
, see e.g.,
[
10
]
. It is easy to verify that
\tau_{t}(A)-\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{t}(A)\approx C(H_{1},\ldots,H_{G})t^{2}+O(t^{3})
as
t\to 0
, more precisely

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\tau_{t}(A)-\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{t}(A)}{t^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq G}[[H_{i},H_{j}],A].

(13)

As such, we have,

\max_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\frac{\|\tau_{t}(A)-\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{t}(A)\|_{\textup{F}}}{\|A\|_{\textup{F}}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq G}\lambda_{\max}([H_{i},H_{j}])-\lambda_{\min}([H_{i},H_{j}]).

For standard examples of Hamiltonians, it is easy to see that the right-hand side scales like
\Omega(N)
. In this paper we show that we can obtain a system-size independent bound on
\|\tau_{t}(A)-\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{t}(A)\|_{\textup{F}}
provided that
A
is restricted to be of low degree. Namely if
H
is quartic and
\Delta
-sparse, then we prove in Theorem
3.5
that

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\in\mathcal{A}\\
\deg(A)\leq d\end{subarray}}\frac{\|\tau_{t}(A)-\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{t}(A)\|_{\textup{F}}}{\|A\|_{\textup{F}}}\leq C\cdot(d\Delta G)^{2}\cdot t^{2}

(14)

for some absolute constant
C>0
.

Preliminaries

Majorana algebra

For even
N
, a system with
N/2
fermionic modes is described by a Fock space
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes N/2}
with an occupation number basis
\{|n_{1},\dots,n_{N/2}\rangle\}_{n_{1},\dots,n_{N/2}\in\{0,1\}}
. The algebra
\mathcal{A}
of operators acting on this Fock space is generated by the creation and annihilation operator
c_{j}^{*},c_{j}
for
j\in[N/2]
, where
|n_{1},\dots,n_{N/2}\rangle=(c_{1}^{*})^{n_{1}}\cdots(c^{*}_{N/2})^{n_{N/2}}|0,\dots,0\rangle
. A state is a positive linear functional
\rho:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying
\rho(1)=1
. A pure state takes the form
\rho(A)=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle
for some unit vector
|\psi\rangle\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes N/2}
, which we also call a state. In this paper, we describe
\mathcal{A}
with Majorana operators, defined as

\begin{cases}\upgamma_{2j}&=c_{j}^{*}+c_{j}\\
\upgamma_{2j+1}&=\textup{i}(c_{j}^{*}-c_{j}).\end{cases}

(15)

The Majorana operators satisfy the canonical anticommutation relations (CAR)

\{\upgamma_{i},\upgamma_{j}\}=2\delta_{ij}\,,\quad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\,.

Any observable
A\in\mathcal{A}
has a unique expansion as

A=\sum_{X\subset[N]}a_{X}\upgamma_{X}

where, for each
X\subset[N]
,

\upgamma_{X}:=\textup{i}^{r_{X}}\prod_{x\in X}\upgamma_{x}\,,

(16)

where the product is ordered such that
x_{i}<x_{j}
for
i<j
, and where we set
r_{X}=0
for
\left\lvert X\right\rvert=0,1\;\textup{mod }4
and
r_{X}=1
otherwise (in particular
r_{X}=|X|(|X|-1)/2\;\textup{mod }2
). As such,
\upgamma_{X}
is Hermitian for all
X
. We use the convention that
\upgamma_{\emptyset}=1
, and we let
\mathcal{M}
be the collection of all
2^{N}
Majorana monomials. The
degree
of an observable
A
is defined as

\deg(A)=\max_{X\subset[N]}\{|X|:a_{X}\neq 0\}.

We recall the following facts about Majorana monomials.

Proposition 2.1
.

For all
S,T\subset[N]
, we have the following properties

1.

Multiplication of two Majorana monomials

\upgamma_{S}\upgamma_{T}=\textup{i}^{(\left\lvert S\right\rvert\left\lvert T\right\rvert+\left\lvert S\cup T\right\rvert)\textup{mod }2}(-1)^{|\{(t,s)\in T\times S:t<s\}|}\upgamma_{S\triangle T}.

2.

\{\upgamma_{S},\upgamma_{T}\}=0\iff[\upgamma_{S},\upgamma_{T}]=2\upgamma_{S}\upgamma_{T}\iff\left\lvert S\right\rvert\left\lvert T\right\rvert-\left\lvert S\cap T\right\rvert\text{ is odd}

3.

[\upgamma_{S},\upgamma_{T}]=0\iff\{\upgamma_{S},\upgamma_{T}\}=2\upgamma_{S}\upgamma_{T}\iff\left\lvert S\right\rvert\left\lvert T\right\rvert-\left\lvert S\cap T\right\rvert\text{ is even}

4.

For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}

e^{\textup{i}\frac{\theta}{2}\upgamma_{T}}\upgamma_{S}e^{-\textup{i}\frac{\theta}{2}\upgamma_{T}}=\begin{cases}\upgamma_{S}&\text{ if }[\upgamma_{S},\upgamma_{T}]=0\\
\cos(\theta)\upgamma_{S}+\textup{i}\sin(\theta)\upgamma_{T}\upgamma_{S}&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

(17)

We will also need the following simple corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

For any
S\subset[N]
,
S\neq\emptyset
such that
|S|
is even, and
A\in\mathcal{A}
,

\deg([\upgamma_{S},A])\leq\deg(A)+|S|-2.

(18)

Proof.

It suffices to show that for any
T
,
\deg([\upgamma_{S},\upgamma_{T}])\leq|T|+|S|-2
. If
|S\cap T|
is even then
[\upgamma_{S},\upgamma_{T}]=0
and there is nothing to do. If
|S\cap T|
is odd then
\deg([\upgamma_{S},\upgamma_{T}])=|S\triangle T|=|S|+|T|-2|S\cap T|\leq|S|+|T|-2
as desired.
∎

2.1
Norms on
\mathcal{A}

To quantify the approximation of observables, one can use several norms on
\mathcal{A}
. For an observable
A
and an estimator
\widehat{A}
(assumed to be Hermitian), the operator norm
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{op}}
can be interpreted as the difference of expectation values on the worst-case state

\|A-\widehat{A}\|_{\textup{op}}=\sup\{|\rho(A)-\rho(\widehat{A})|:\rho\text{ state}\}=\sup\{|\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle-\langle\psi|\widehat{A}|\psi\rangle|:|\psi\rangle\text{ state}\}.

In this paper, we work with the normalized Frobenius norm defined, for
A\in\mathcal{A}
, as

\|A\|_{\textup{F}}=\sqrt{\tr(A^{*}A)},

where
\tr
is the normalized trace, i.e.,
\tr(1)=1
. Note that

\|\upgamma_{X}\|_{\textup{F}}=1\text{ and }\tr(\upgamma_{X})=0\;\;\forall X\neq\emptyset.

(19)

If the observable
A
has an expansion

A=\sum_{X\subset[N]}a_{X}\upgamma_{X}

then

\|A\|_{\textup{F}}=\left(\sum_{X\subset[N]}|a_{X}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(20)

The Frobenius norm between observables
A
and
\widehat{A}
can be interpreted in terms of the squared difference of expectation values on an average-case state. In fact, taking a random pure state
|\psi\rangle\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes N/2}
and Hermitian operators
A
and
\widehat{A}
, a standard calculation gives

\displaystyle\int_{\psi}\left(\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle-\langle\psi|\widehat{A}|\psi\rangle\right)^{2}d\psi

\displaystyle=\int_{\psi}2^{N}\tr\left((A-\widehat{A})^{\otimes 2}|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|^{\otimes 2}\right)d\psi

(21)

\displaystyle=2^{N}\tr\left((A-\widehat{A})^{\otimes 2}\frac{I+F}{2^{N/2}(2^{N/2}+1)}\right)

(22)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+2^{-N/2}}\left(\left(\tr(A-\widehat{A})\right)^{2}+\tr(A-\widehat{A})^{2}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+2^{-N/2}}\left(\left(\tr(A-\widehat{A})\right)^{2}+\|A-\widehat{A}\|^{2}_{\textup{F}}\right),

(24)

where
F
denotes the swap operator. Assuming
\tr(A)=\tr(\widehat{A})
, which is the case for the estimators we consider in this paper (e.g., Majorana propagation), we obtain that the average squared difference is proportional to the squared Frobenius norm.
Interpretations of the Frobenius norm of operators are discussed in
[
8
]
.

2.2
Hamiltonian

We define a Hamiltonian
H\in\mathcal{A}
with quartic interactions as a Hermitian operator of the form:

H:=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}}h_{X}\quad\text{where}\quad h_{X}=\mathcal{h}_{X}\upgamma_{X},\;\;\mathcal{h}_{X}\in\mathbb{C},|\mathcal{h}_{X}|\leq 1\,.

(25)

The Hamiltonian is assumed to only have terms of degree two or four, as this is the case for the majority of physical fermionic Hamiltonians. For this reason, we will write

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{2}\cup\mathcal{X}_{4}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{2}:=\{X\subset[N]:\left\lvert X\right\rvert=2\,\text{and}\,h_{X}\neq 0\}\,,

(26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{4}:=\{X\subset[N]:\left\lvert X\right\rvert=4\,\text{and}\,h_{X}\neq 0\}\,.

Throughout this work, we will assume that the Hamiltonian is
\Delta
-sparse, i.e.,

\max_{i\in[N]}\left\lvert\{X\in\mathcal{X}:i\in X\text{ and }h_{X}\neq 0\}\right\rvert\leq\Delta\,.

(27)

The sparsity assumption implies a number of useful and easy-to-prove consequences that we list here.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any Majorana monomial
A\in\mathcal{M}
, the number of terms
h_{X}
of the Hamiltonian such that
[h_{X},A]\neq 0
is bounded above by
\Delta\deg A
, i.e.,

|\{X\in\mathcal{X}:[h_{X},A]\neq 0\}|\leq\Delta\deg A\,.

(28)

Proof.

First observe that given
p\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, there are at most
\Delta
terms of the Hamiltonian that have a non-zero commutator with the degree-one monomial
\upgamma_{p}
. This follows directly from the assumption (
27
), and proves (
28
) when
A
has degree 1.
For an arbitrary monomial
A=\upgamma_{p_{1}}\dots\upgamma_{p_{d}}
where
d=\deg A
, observe that for any
h\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

[h,A]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\upgamma_{p_{1}}\dots\upgamma_{p_{j-1}}[h,\upgamma_{p_{j}}]\upgamma_{p_{j+1}}\dots\upgamma_{p_{d}}.

Hence

|\{X:[h_{X},A]\neq 0\}|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\{X:[h_{X},\upgamma_{p_{j}}]\neq 0\}|\leq\Delta d

as desired.
∎

When analyzing the complexity of MP, it will be important to control the growth of the degree of the observable after each Trotter step. To achieve this, we next show how one can color the terms in the Hamitonian such that any two terms with the same color have disjoint support. With this decomposition, given any observable
A
it will then be easier to control by how much the degree of
A
grows after having applied one Trotter step.

Proposition 2.4
.

There is an efficient algorithm that can compute a partition of the terms
\mathcal{X}
of
H
in
G\leq 4\Delta
groups

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}^{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{X}^{G}

(29)

such that for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,G\}
and any
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}^{i}
,
X\cap Y=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Consider the graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{X},E)
where each node corresponds to a term of the Hamiltonian, and where an edge exists between two terms
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}
iff

X\cap Y\neq\emptyset
.
Finding a partition such as the one in Eq. (
29
) amounts to finding a coloring of
\mathcal{G}
. Denoting by
\deg_{\mathcal{G}}(X)
the number of edges at node
X\in\mathcal{X}
, we have that

\deg_{\mathcal{G}}(X)\leq\sum_{x\in X}\left\lvert\{Y\in\mathcal{X}:x\in Y\text{ and }Y\neq X\}\right\rvert\leq\sum_{x\in X}(\Delta-1)\leq 4(\Delta-1)\,,

since by assumption on the set
\mathcal{X}
,
\left\lvert X\right\rvert\leq 4
. A standard result
[
11
]
is that a simple greedy algorithm can color the graph using
\deg_{\mathcal{G}}(X)+1\leq 4\Delta
colors.
∎

3
Majorana Propagation algorithm

We first describe in Section
3.1
precisely the MP algorithm that we consider in this work, and state the main result in Theorem
3.3
concerning its convergence. Before delving in the proof, in Section
3.2
we discuss two important technical results needed to analyze the convergence and complexity of the algorithm. The proof of Theorem
3.3
is then delayed to Section
3.3
.

3.1
Description of the algorithm

Trotter dynamics

The MP algorithm we consider in this section starts by decomposing the Hamiltonian
H
into
G
terms

H=\sum_{g=1}^{G}H^{g}\,,\quad H^{g}=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}^{g}}h_{X}

such that for each
g\in\{1,\ldots,G\}
, all the monomial terms in
H^{g}
have disjoint support. Proposition
2.4
shows that one can efficiently find such a decomposition with
G\leq 4\Delta
. For any
A\in\mathcal{A}
,
g\in[G]
and
\delta t
, let

\tau^{g}_{\delta t}(A)=e^{\textup{i}\delta tH^{g}}Ae^{-\textup{i}\delta tH^{g}}\,,

(30)

and let
\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}
be a Trotter approximation of the unitary evolution operator
\tau_{\delta t}
obtained as the composition of
\{\tau^{g}_{\delta t}\}_{g=1}^{G}

\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}(A)=(\tau_{\delta t}^{G}\circ\dots\circ\tau_{\delta t}^{1})(A)\,.

(31)

Note that since the individual terms inside each
H^{g}
pairwise commute, we have

\tau^{g}_{\delta t}(A)=\prod_{X\in\mathcal{X}^{g}}e^{\textup{i}\delta th_{X}}A\prod_{X\in\mathcal{X}^{g}}e^{-\textup{i}\delta th_{X}}

and therefore Eq. (
31
) is equivalent to first-order Trotter, where each single Hamiltonian term gives rise to the adjoint action of a single unitary, and the ordering of the unitaries is dictated by the decomposition. This particular ordering allows us to control the growth of the degree during a Trotter step. Indeed, the following short Proposition shows that for each
g\in[G]
,
\deg\tau^{g}_{\delta t}(A)\leq 3\deg(A)
, which implies that
\deg(\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}(A))\leq 3^{G}\deg(A)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
S\subset[N]
and let
\upgamma_{X_{1}},\ldots,\upgamma_{X_{k}}
be a set of degree-two and degree-four Majorana monomials whose supports are pairwise disjoint (hence they pairwise commute). Define, for any choice of real numbers
\{\theta_{i}\}
, the following operator

O=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}e^{\textup{i}\theta_{i}\upgamma_{X_{i}}}\right)\upgamma_{S}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}e^{-\textup{i}\theta_{i}\upgamma_{X_{i}}}\right).

Then
O
is a Majorana polynomial that is a sum of at most
2^{|S|}
monomials and satisfies
\deg(O)\leq 3|S|
. Moreover, it can be computed in time
O(k+2^{|S|})
.

Proof.

We can assume without loss of generality that
X_{i}\cap S\neq\emptyset
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
(indeed, if
X_{i}\cap S=\emptyset
then
[\upgamma_{X_{i}},\upgamma_{S}]=0
and the corresponding Majorana rotation has no effect). Since the
X_{i}
are pairwise disjoint, this implies that
k\leq|S|
. Furthermore, we know from Proposition
2.1
that for any
X
with
|X|\leq 4

\deg\left(e^{\textup{i}\theta\upgamma_{X}}\upgamma_{S}e^{-\textup{i}\theta\upgamma_{X}}\right)\leq\max(|S|,|S\triangle X|)\leq|S|+2.

Hence since
O
is the result of applying
k
operations of this form to
\upgamma_{S}
, we get

\deg(O)\leq|S|+2k\leq|S|+2|S|=3|S|

as desired. To compute
O
, we first loop through the sets
X_{i}
and only keep the ones for which
X_{i}\cap S\neq\emptyset
, the corresponding runtime is
O(k)
. To compute the product, we use the formula in (
17
) sequentially and each time the total number of Majorana monomials gets multiplied by at most
2
. The total number of times we use Eq. (
17
) is at most
1+2+2^{2}+\dots+2^{|S|}=O(2^{|S|})
.
∎

Degree truncation

In the MP algorithm we truncate the observable to degree at most
\ell
after each Trotter evolution. More precisely, consider the truncation map
\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
defined on any observable
A=\sum_{Y\subset[N]}a_{X}\upgamma_{X}
as

\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}(A)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subset[N]\\
\ |X|\leq\ell\end{subarray}}a_{X}\upgamma_{X}.

The truncation map satisfies the following important properties.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any observable
A\in\mathcal{A}
, and any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, the following holds:

(i)

\|\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}(A)\|_{\textup{F}}\leq\|A\|_{\textup{F}}

(ii)

\min_{P\in\mathcal{A},\deg P\leq\ell}\|P-A\|_{\textup{F}}=\|\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}(A)-A\|_{\textup{F}}=(\sum_{X\subset[N],|X|>\ell}|a_{X}|^{2})^{1/2}
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of (
20
).
∎

Using this notation, we can express the MP algorithm in simple terms. Choose a time step

and then let, assuming

is an integer,

A^{\delta t,\ell}_{\textup{MP}}(t)=\left(\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}\circ\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}\right)^{t/\delta t}(A)\,.

(32)

Remark
.

If
t/\delta t
is not an integer, we adapt the definition (

A^{\delta t,\ell}_{\textup{MP}}(t)=\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}\circ\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{t-\lfloor t/\delta t\rfloor\delta t}\circ\left(\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}\circ\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}\right)^{\lfloor t/\delta t\rfloor}(A).

(33)

H
with sparsity
\Delta
, Observable
A
, Horizon

Parameters

\delta t>0
,

1. Decompose the Hamiltonian

into

such that for any

have disjoint supports (see Proposition
2.4
)

2. Define
\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}
according to Eq. (

)

3. Return
A^{\delta t,\ell}_{\textup{MP}}(t)
as defined in (

)

Theorem 3.3

(Analysis of Majorana propagation)
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{A}
be an arbitrary observable, and let
A^{\delta t,\ell}_{\textup{MP}}(t)
be the result of applying the MP algorithm with parameters
\delta t
and
\ell\geq\deg(A)
for a time horizon
t\geq 0
(see Algorithm
1
). Then:

(i)

The operator
A^{\delta t,\ell}_{\textup{MP}}(t)
can be computed in time complexity at most
\frac{t}{\delta t}\cdot N^{O(\ell)}
.

(ii)

We have the bound

\|A^{\delta t,\ell}_{\textup{MP}}(t)-A(t)\|_{\textup{F}}\leq 34t\cdot\delta t\cdot\Delta^{2}(\ell+2)^{2}\|A\|_{\textup{F}}+\left\lceil\dfrac{t}{\delta t}\right\rceil\eta^{\star}_{\textup{F}}(H,A,t,\ell)\,,

(34)

where
A(t)=e^{\textup{i}Ht}Ae^{-\textup{i}Ht}
and
\eta^{\star}_{\textup{F}}(H,A,t,\ell)

) with
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{\textup{F}}
.

3.2
Ingredients

We start by proving two results, introduced in Section
1.2
, that will play an important role in the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

3.2.1
Bound on the commutator’s Frobenius norm

Theorem 3.4
.

For any observable
A\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\|[H,A]\|_{\textup{F}}\leq 2\Delta\sqrt{\deg A(\deg A+2)}\|A\|_{\textup{F}}.

Proof.

Write
A=\sum_{Y\subset[N]}a_{Y}\upgamma_{Y}
, and
H=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{h}_{X}\upgamma_{X}
, where
|\mathcal{h}_{X}|\leq 1
for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
. From Proposition
2.1
we know that for
X\in\mathcal{X}
and arbitrary
Y\subset[N]
,
[\upgamma_{X},\upgamma_{Y}]=0\text{ if }|X\cap Y|
even, and otherwise
[\upgamma_{X},\upgamma_{Y}]=2d_{X,Y}\upgamma_{X\Delta Y}
for some unit complex number
d_{X,Y}\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus we have

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in\mathcal{X},Y\subset[N]\\
|X\cap Y|\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{h}_{X}a_{Y}d_{X,Y}\upgamma_{X\triangle Y}=2\sum_{Z\subset[N]}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in\mathcal{X},Y\subset[N]\\
|X\cap Y|\text{ odd},\,X\triangle Y=Z\end{subarray}}\mathcal{h}_{X}a_{Y}d_{X,Y}\right)\upgamma_{Z}.

We can then write

\displaystyle\|[H,A]\|^{2}_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle=4\sum_{Z\subset[N]}\left|\sum_{(X,Y)\in\mathcal{P}(Z)}\mathcal{h}_{X}a_{Y}d_{X,Y}\right|^{2}

(35)

where, for a fixed
Z\subset[N]
, we let

\mathcal{P}(Z)=\{(X,Y):X\in\mathcal{X},Y\subset[N]\text{ s.t. }X\triangle Y=Z\text{ and }|X\cap Y|\text{ odd}\}.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have for any
Z
, and using the fact that
|\mathcal{h}_{X}d_{X,Y}|\leq 1

\left|\sum_{(X,Y)\in\mathcal{P}(Z)}\mathcal{h}_{X}a_{Y}d_{X,Y}\right|^{2}\leq|\mathcal{P}(Z)|\sum_{(X,Y)\in\mathcal{P}(Z)}|a_{Y}|^{2}.

(36)

Note that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{P}(Z)|

\displaystyle=|\{X\in\mathcal{X}:|X\cap(X\triangle Z)|\text{ odd}\}|

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}|\{X\in\mathcal{X}:|X\cap Z|\text{ odd}\}|

\displaystyle=|\{X\in\mathcal{X}:[\upgamma_{X},\upgamma_{Z}]\neq 0\}|

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\Delta|Z|

where in (a) we used the fact that
|X\cap(X\triangle Z)|=|X|-|X\cap Z|
and that
|X|
is even, and in (b) we used Proposition
2.3
.
Plugging (
36
) in (
35
) we get

\displaystyle\|[H,A]\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{Z\subset[N]}\Delta|Z|\sum_{(X,Y)\in\mathcal{P}(Z)}|a_{Y}|^{2}

\displaystyle=4\Delta\sum_{Y\subset[N]}|a_{Y}|^{2}\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X},|X\cap Y|\text{ odd}}|X\triangle Y|

\displaystyle\leq 4\Delta^{2}\sum_{Y\subset[N]}|a_{Y}|^{2}|Y|(|Y|+2)

\displaystyle\leq 4\Delta^{2}(\deg A)(\deg A+2)\|A\|^{2}_{\textup{F}}

where we used the fact that for
X\in\mathcal{X}
, and
a_{Y}\neq 0
,
|X\triangle Y|\leq|Y|+2\leq\deg A+2
, and that
|\{X\in\mathcal{X}:|X\cap Y|\text{ odd}\}|\leq\Delta|Y|\leq\Delta\deg A
using Proposition
2.3
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

3.2.2
Trotter error bound

The second result deals with the Trotter error bound for low-degree observables.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\tau_{t}(A)=e^{\textup{i}Ht}Ae^{-\textup{i}Ht}
, and let
\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{t}(A)
be the Trotter approximation of
\tau_{t}
as defined in (
31
), for a given decomposition
H=H^{1}+\dots+H^{G}
of the Hamiltonian. Then for any
A\in\mathcal{A}
with
\deg A=d
, we have

\|\tau_{t}(A)-\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{t}(A)\|_{\textup{F}}\leq 2(\Delta\cdot t\cdot(d+2))^{2}\left(G^{2}+1\right).

(37)

Remark
.

The bound above holds for an arbitrary decomposition
H=H^{1}+\dots+H^{G}
, i.e., it does not use the fact that the monomial terms inside each
H^{i}
have disjoint supports. If the latter holds, then one can prove the following improved bound:

\|\tau_{t}(A)-\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{t}(A)\|_{\textup{F}}\leq 2(t\cdot(d+2))^{2}\left(G^{2}+\Delta^{2}\right).

(38)

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

We first prove the following general lemma, an application of Taylor’s theorem with integral remainder.

Lemma 3.6
.

For any finite-dimensional super-operators
\mathcal{L}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{G}
,

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G}}\cdots e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\id+t(\mathcal{L}_{G}+\cdots+\mathcal{L}_{1})

(39)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{G}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G}}\cdots e^{t\mathcal{L}_{i+1}}e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{i}\left((t-\tau)\mathcal{L}_{i}+t\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathcal{L}_{j}\right).

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
G
. The identity is clearly true for
G=1
using the Taylor’s theorem with integral remainder. Assuming the identity holds for some
G
, we can write:

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G+1}}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G}}\cdots e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}}

\displaystyle=e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G+1}}+te^{t\mathcal{L}_{G+1}}(\mathcal{L}_{G}+\cdots+\mathcal{L}_{1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{G}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G+1}}\cdots e^{t\mathcal{L}_{i+1}}e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{i}((t-\tau)\mathcal{L}_{i}+t\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\dots+t\mathcal{L}_{1}).

We expand
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G+1}}
with Taylor’s theorem to
p
-th order; in the first term for
p=1
, and
p=0
for the second one to get:

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G+1}}\cdots e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\id+t\mathcal{L}_{G+1}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau(t-\tau)e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{G+1}}\mathcal{L}_{G+1}^{2}

\displaystyle+t\left(\id+\int_{0}^{t}d\tau e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{G+1}}\mathcal{L}_{G+1}\right)(\mathcal{L}_{G}+\cdots+\mathcal{L}_{1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{G}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G+1}}\cdots e^{t\mathcal{L}_{i+1}}e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{i}((t-\tau)\mathcal{L}_{i}+t\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\dots+t\mathcal{L}_{1})

which, after rearranging, gives the formula of Eq. (
39
) for
G+1
. Hence this holds for general
G
.
∎

For any
i=1,\ldots,G
, let
\mathcal{L}_{i}=\textup{i}[H^{i},\cdot]
, and let
\mathcal{L}=\textup{i}[H,\cdot]
so that
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{1}+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{G}
. Note that
e^{t\mathcal{L}}(A)=e^{\textup{i}Ht}Ae^{-\textup{i}Ht}=\tau_{t}(A)
and
(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G}}\cdots e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}})(A)=\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{t}(A)
. Using the expansion (
39
) of
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G}}\cdots e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}}
, and using

e^{t\mathcal{L}}=\id+t\mathcal{L}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau(t-\tau)e^{\tau\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}^{2}

we have, by the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\left\lVert(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G}}\cdots e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}}-e^{t\mathcal{L}})(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{G}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\left\lVert e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G}}\cdots e^{t\mathcal{L}_{i+1}}e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{i}\left((t-\tau)\mathcal{L}_{i}+t\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\dots+t\mathcal{L}_{1}\right)(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}

(40)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau(t-\tau)\left\lVert e^{\tau\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}^{2}(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}.

Since
e^{s\mathcal{L}_{i}}
is a conjugation by a unitary for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we have,
\left\lVert e^{s\mathcal{L}_{i}}(B)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}=\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}
for any operator
B\in\mathcal{A}
. Hence the integrand of the first term in (
40
) can be bounded above by

\displaystyle\left\lVert(t-\tau)\mathcal{L}_{i}^{2}(A)+t\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathcal{L}_{i-1}+\dots+\mathcal{L}_{1})(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle\leq(t-\tau)\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{i}^{2}(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}+t\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{i}\mathcal{L}_{j}(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}},

and the integrand of the second term in (
40
) is bounded by
(t-\tau)\left\lVert\mathcal{L}^{2}(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}
. Putting things together, we get

\left\lVert(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G}}\cdots e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}}-e^{t\mathcal{L}})(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left(\left\lVert\mathcal{L}^{2}(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}+\sum_{i=1}^{G}\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{i}^{2}(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}+2\sum_{1\leq j<i\leq G}\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{i}\mathcal{L}_{j}(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\right).

(41)

Using Theorem
3.4
, we know that

\|\mathcal{L}(A)\|_{\textup{F}}\leq 2\Delta\sqrt{\deg A(\deg A+2)}\|A\|_{\textup{F}}.

By applying the inequality again, and using the fact that
\deg\mathcal{L}(A)\leq\deg A+2
, we get

\|\mathcal{L}^{2}(A)\|_{\textup{F}}\leq 4\Delta^{2}(\deg A+2)\sqrt{\deg A(\deg A+4)}\|A\|_{\textup{F}}\leq 4\Delta^{2}(\deg A+2)^{2}\|A\|_{\textup{F}}.

The same bound applies to the quantities
\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{i}^{2}(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}
and
\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{i}\mathcal{L}_{j}(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}
. Hence we get at the end

\left\lVert(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{G}}\cdots e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}}-e^{t\mathcal{L}})(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\leq 2t^{2}\Delta^{2}(\deg A+2)^{2}(1+G^{2}).

This proves (
37
).

To prove (
38
), it suffices to note that if for a given
g\in[G]
,
X\cap Y=\emptyset
for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}^{g}
then
H^{g}
is
1
-sparse, i.e.,

\max_{x\in[N]}|\{X\in\mathcal{X}^{g}:x\in X\text{ and }h_{X}\neq 0\}|=1.

Hence, it follows from Theorem
3.4
that

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{i}(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\leq 2\sqrt{\deg A(\deg A+2)}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}},

and so
\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{i}\mathcal{L}_{j}(A)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\leq 4(\deg A+2)^{2}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}
for all
i,j
.
Using this fact in (
41
) gives (
38
).
∎

3.3
Proof of Theorem
3.3

We are now ready to prove the main theorem on the analysis of Majorana Propagation.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Let
t_{0}=0<t_{1}<\dots<t_{K}=t
be the time steps of the algorithm, i.e.,
t_{k}=k\cdot\delta t
for
k=0,\ldots,\lfloor t/\delta t\rfloor
and
K=\lceil t/\delta t\rceil
. For convenience, we omit the superscript
\delta t
and write
A^{\ell}_{\textup{MP}}(t)
instead of
A^{\ell,\delta t}_{\textup{MP}}(t)
.

(i)

We first analyze the runtime for computing
A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t)
.
At each time step
t_{k}
, we have a Majorana polynomial
A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t_{k})
of degree at most
\ell
and thus is a sum of at most
N^{\ell}
monomials. Using Proposition
3.1
, computing
\tau^{1}_{\delta t}(A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t_{k}))
can be done in time
O(N^{\ell}(N+2^{\ell}))=O(N^{2\ell})
and the resulting polynomial has degree at most
3\ell
. For the second step, applying Proposition
3.1
, we see that computing
\tau^{2}_{\delta t}\circ\tau^{1}_{\delta t}(A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t_{k}))
can be done in time
O(N^{3\ell}(N+2^{3\ell}))=O(N^{6\ell})
. As a result, computing
\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}(A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t_{k}))
can be done with a total runtime of
O(N^{2\ell}+N^{6\ell}+\dots+N^{2\cdot 3^{G}\ell})=N^{O(\ell)}
as
G\leq 4\Delta
for quartic
\Delta
-sparse Hamiltonians.

Even though we do not pursue this further, we remark that
it is possible to analyze more closely the evolution of the number of monomials through the algorithm. Using Proposition
3.1
, we have that
\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}(A)
has degree at most
3^{G}\deg(A)
and if
A
has at most
s
terms, then
\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}(A)
can be written as a sum of at most
s\cdot 2^{\deg(A)}\cdot 2^{3\deg(A)}\cdots 2^{3^{G}\deg(A)}\leq s\cdot 2^{3^{G+1}\deg(A)}
monomials.
In addition, the truncation step cannot increase the number of monomials. As such, each step of the algorithm multiplies that number of terms by at most
2^{3^{G+1}\ell}
.

(ii)

We now analyze the error. Let us first consider the case where
t/\delta t
is an integer. By the definition of
A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t)
and the triangle inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t)-A(t)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}\circ\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}(A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t-\delta t))-A(t)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}

(42)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}\left(\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}(A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t-\delta t))-A(t)\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}+\left\lVert\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}(A(t))-A(t)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}.

Using the fact that
\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}
is nonexpansive for the Frobenius norm, and
letting

\tau_{\delta t}(\cdot)=e^{\textup{i}H\delta t}(\cdot)e^{-\textup{i}H\delta t}

(43)

we have, continuing from (
42
),

\displaystyle\|A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t)-A(t)\|_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}(A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t-\delta t))-A(t)\|_{\textup{F}}+\|\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}(A(t))-A(t)\|_{\textup{F}}

(44)

\displaystyle\leq\|\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}(A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t-\delta t))-\tau_{\delta t}(A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t-\delta t))\|_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle+\|\tau_{\delta t}(A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t-\delta t))-\tau_{\delta t}(A(t-\delta t))\|_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle+\|\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}(A(t))-A(t)\|_{\textup{F}}.

We can use (
38
) from Theorem
3.5
to bound the first term as

\|\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}(A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t-\delta t))-\tau_{\delta t}(A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t-\delta t))\|_{\textup{F}}\leq\kappa\cdot(\delta t)^{2}\|A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t-\delta t)\|_{\textup{F}}\overset{(a)}{\leq}\kappa\cdot(\delta t)^{2}\|A\|_{\textup{F}}

where we let

\kappa:=2(\ell+2)^{2}(G^{2}+\Delta^{2})\overset{(b)}{\leq}34\Delta^{2}(\ell+2)^{2}

(45)

and where we used in
(a)
that
\|A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t-\delta t)\|_{\textup{F}}\leq\|A\|_{\textup{F}}
(a consequence of the nonexpansiveness of
\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}
for the Frobenius norm, and the unitarity of
\tau^{\textup{trot}}_{\delta t}
), and in
(b)
we used the fact that
G\leq 4\Delta
(Proposition
2.4
).

Continuing from (
44
), we get

\|A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t)-A(t)\|_{\textup{F}}\leq\kappa\cdot(\delta t)^{2}\|A\|_{\textup{F}}+\|A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell}(t-\delta t)-A(t-\delta t)\|_{\textup{F}}+\|\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}(A(t))-A(t)\|_{\textup{F}}\,.

(46)

Remark that the second term in Eq. (
46
) is identical to the LHS but at time
t-\delta t
. We can then iterate the inequalities to get

\displaystyle\|A^{\ell}_{\textup{MP}}(t)-A(t)\|_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t}{\delta t}\kappa\cdot(\delta t)^{2}\|A\|_{\textup{F}}+\sum_{k=1}^{t/\delta t}\|\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}(A(k\cdot\delta t))-A(k\cdot\delta t)\|_{\textup{F}}

(47)

\displaystyle\leq t\cdot\kappa\cdot\delta t\|A\|_{\textup{F}}+\frac{t}{\delta t}\eta^{\star}_{\textup{F}}(t,\ell)\,,

where we used the fact that
\|\textup{Trunc}_{\ell}(A(k\cdot\delta t))-A(k\cdot\delta t)\|_{\textup{F}}\leq\eta^{\star}_{\textup{F}}(t,\ell)
. Plugging the bound (
45
) for
\kappa
completes the proof of Theorem
3.3
in the case where
t/\delta t
is an integer.

The case where
t/\delta t
is not an integer is identical, except that the first term in (
47
) will be of the form
\kappa\cdot(s^{2}+\frac{t_{K-1}}{\delta t}(\delta t)^{2})\|A\|_{\textup{F}}
where
t_{K-1}=\lfloor t/\delta t\rfloor\delta t
and
s=t-t_{K-1}\in(0,\delta t)
. Since
s\leq\delta t
and
t=t_{K-1}+s
then we still have
\kappa\cdot(s^{2}+t_{K-1}\delta t)\leq\kappa\cdot t\cdot\delta t
. In addition, the second term in (
47
) will be of the form
\left\lceil\frac{t}{\delta t}\right\rceil\eta^{\star}_{\textup{F}}(t,\ell)
.

∎

4
Weakly interacting fermions

In this section, we assume our Hamiltonian
H
has the form

H:=H_{0}+uV=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{2}}h_{X}+u\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{4}}h_{X}.

(48)

where we recall that
\mathcal{X}_{2},\mathcal{X}_{4}
are degree-two and degree-four sets of interactions and
u\in[0,1]
.
Our goal is to prove Theorem
1.2
, which we recall here for convenience:

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume
H
is a
\Delta
-sparse Hamiltonian of the form (
48
). Let
A
be an arbitrary observable. Then for all
t<t_{\textup{max}}(u):=\log(e/u)/(8e^{2}\Delta(\deg A+2))
, there exists a degree-
\ell
observable
P
such that

\|A(t)-P\|_{\textup{F}}\leq\frac{\left(t/t_{\textup{max}}(u)\right)^{(\ell-\deg(A))/2}}{1-t/t_{\textup{max}}(u)}\|A\|_{\textup{F}}

(49)

where
A(t)=e^{\textup{i}tH}Ae^{-\textup{i}tH}
.

The proof involves two natural steps: First, in Section
4.1
we derive a series expansion of
A(t)
in powers of
u
that is reminiscent of Dyson series, where the
k
’th term of the series has degree at most
\deg(A)+2k
. Secondly, we show in Section
4.2
that truncating the series derived will incur the error stated in Equation (
49
).

4.1
Expressing
A(t)
as a series in
u

It is well-known that
A(t)=e^{\textup{i}tH}Ae^{-\textup{i}tH}
has the following multi-commutator series expansion

A(t)=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\textup{i}t)^{p}}{p!}[H,[H,\dots[H,A]]]\,.

(50)

By using the fact that
H=H_{0}+uV
, and rearranging the terms according to the power of
u
, one can show the following result.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given a Hamiltonian
H=H_{0}+uV
and an arbitrary observable
A\in\mathcal{A}
, the time-evolved observable
A(t)
has the following series expansion

A(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}u^{n}\sum_{m_{0},\dots,m_{n}=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{t^{n+\sum_{i}m_{i}}}{(n+\sum_{i}m_{i})!}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n-1}}\cdots\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{0}}A

(51)

where
\mathcal{F}(\cdot):=\textup{i}[H_{0},\cdot]
and
\mathcal{G}(\cdot):=\textup{i}[V,\cdot]
.

Proof.

For any integer
p\geq 0
, we can expand the
p
-th order nested commutators in (
50
) and rearrange the terms according to the power of
u
as follows:

\displaystyle\textup{i}^{p}[H,[H,\dots[H,A]]]

\displaystyle=\textup{i}^{p}[H_{0}+uV,[H_{0}+uV,\dots[H_{0}+uV,A]]]

(52)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{p}u^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0},\dots,m_{n}\\
m_{0}+m_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}+n=p\end{subarray}}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n-1}}\cdots\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{0}}A.

To obtain the desired equality (
51
), we simply need to plug (
52
) into (
50
) and swap the summations over
p
and
n
. To justify this last step, define the sequence
\alpha_{p,n}
as

\alpha_{p,n}=\frac{(\textup{i}t)^{p}}{p!}u^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0},\dots,m_{n}\\
m_{0}+m_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}+n=p\end{subarray}}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n-1}}\cdots\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{0}}A

for
p\geq n
and
\alpha_{p,n}=0
for
p<n
.
It satisfies

\|\alpha_{p,n}\|_{\textup{op}}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\dfrac{(2tC)^{p}u^{n}}{p!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0},\dots,m_{n}\\
m_{0}+m_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}+n=p\end{subarray}}1=\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\dfrac{(2tC)^{p}u^{n}}{p!}\binom{p}{n}=\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\dfrac{(2tCu)^{n}(2tC)^{p-n}}{n!(p-n)!}

where
C=\max(\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}},\left\lVert H_{0}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}})
. Then, the double sum satisfies

\sum_{n,p}\left\lVert\alpha_{p,n}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(2tCu)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{p=n}^{\infty}\dfrac{(2tC)^{p-n}}{(p-n)!}=\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}e^{2tCu}e^{2tC}.

It is thus an absolutely convergent double series and the sums can be inverted to obtain

\displaystyle A(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}u^{n}\sum_{p\geq n}\frac{t^{p}}{p!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0},\dots,m_{n}\\
m_{0}+m_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}+n=p\end{subarray}}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n-1}}\cdots\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{0}}A,

which leads to the desired expression.
∎

4.2
Series truncation

Given an integer
k
, define
A^{k}(t)
as the truncation of Eq. (
51
) up to the
k
’th power of
u

A^{k}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{k}u^{n}\sum_{m_{0},\dots,m_{n}=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{t^{n+\sum_{i}m_{i}}}{(n+\sum_{i}m_{i})!}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n-1}}\cdots\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{0}}A\,.

(53)

Note that, by Eq. (
18
),
\deg(A^{k}(t))\leq\deg(A)+2k
.
The main goal of this section is to bound the error
\|A^{k}(t)-A(t)\|_{\textup{F}}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider an arbitrary operator
A\in\mathcal{A}
. Then, for any
t<t_{\textup{max}}(u):=\frac{\log(e/u)}{8e^{2}\Delta(\deg A+2)}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that

\|A^{k}(t)-A(t)\|_{\textup{F}}\leq\frac{(t/t_{\textup{max}}(u))^{k+1}}{1-t/t_{\textup{max}}(u)}\|A\|_{\textup{F}}.

(54)

Before proving this lemma, we first prove a bound on the norm of nested commutators contained in Eq. (
53
). Recall that
\mathcal{F}(\cdot):=\textup{i}[H_{0},\cdot]
and
\mathcal{G}(\cdot)=\textup{i}[V,\cdot]
, where
H_{0}
is the quadratic part of
H
and
V
is the quartic one.

Proposition 4.4

(Frobenius norm of nested commutators)
.

For any tuple
(m_{0},\dots,m_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n+1}
and
m=m_{0}+\dots+m_{n}
, and
A\in\mathcal{A}
,

\left\lVert\mathcal{F}^{m_{n}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n-1}}\cdots\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{0}}A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\leq\left(4\Delta(\deg A+2n)\right)^{n+m}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}.

(55)

Proof.

This is a straightforward application of Theorem
3.4
.
Let
B=\mathcal{F}^{m_{n}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n-1}}\cdots\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{0}}A
. Note that by Eq. (
18
),
\deg(B)\leq d+2n
where
d=\deg(A)
. Denoting
C(\Delta,d):=2\Delta\sqrt{d(d+2)}
and applying Proposition
3.4
to the
m_{n}
leftmost
\mathcal{F}
’s, we then have

\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\leq C(\Delta,d+2n)^{m_{n}}\left\lVert\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n-1}}\cdots\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{0}}A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\,.

We apply the same Proposition to
\mathcal{G}
, though now the superoperator is acting on a operator of degree
d+2(n-1)
. We then have

\displaystyle\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\Delta,d+2n)^{m_{n}}C(\Delta,d+2n-2)\left\lVert\mathcal{F}^{m_{n-1}}\mathcal{G}\cdots\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{0}}A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\Delta,d+2n)^{m_{n}+1}\left\lVert\mathcal{F}^{m_{n-1}}\mathcal{G}\cdots\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{0}}A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\,.

Repeating the same steps prescribed above, we arrive at

\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\leq C(\Delta,d+2n)^{n+m}\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\,.

Finally note that
C(\Delta,d+2n)\leq 4\Delta(d+2n)
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Lemma
4.3
.

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

We have

\left\lVert A^{k}(t)-A(t)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\leq\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}u^{n}\sum_{m_{0},\dots,m_{n}=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{t^{n+\sum_{i}m_{i}}}{(n+\sum_{i}m_{i})!}\left\lVert\mathcal{F}^{m_{n}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n-1}}\cdots\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{0}}A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}.

(56)

Using the bound (
55
),

\left\lVert\mathcal{F}^{m_{n}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{n-1}}\cdots\mathcal{F}^{m_{1}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{F}^{m_{0}}A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}(4\Delta(\deg(A)+2n))^{n+\sum_{i}m_{i}},

(57)

and letting
\deg A=d
, we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert A^{k}(t)-A(t)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}u^{n}\sum_{m_{0},\dots,m_{n}=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(4t\Delta(d+2n))^{n+\sum_{i}m_{i}}}{(n+\sum_{i}m_{i})!}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}u^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0},\dots,m_{n}=0\,:\\
\sum_{i}m_{i}=m\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\dfrac{(4t\Delta(d+2n))^{n+m}}{(n+m)!}

where in the last line we partitioned the sum over
(m_{0},\dots,m_{n})
into sums over
(m_{0},\dots,m_{n})
such that
\sum_{i}m_{i}=m
, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We then have

\displaystyle\left\lVert A^{k}(t)-A(t)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}u^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(4t\Delta(d+2n))^{n+m}}{(n+m)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{0},\dots,m_{n}=0\,:\\
\sum_{i}m_{i}=m\end{subarray}}^{\infty}1

\displaystyle=\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}(4tu\Delta(d+2n))^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(4t\Delta(d+2n))^{m}}{(n+m)!}\binom{n+m}{n}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(4tu\Delta(d+2n))^{n}}{n!}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(4t\Delta(d+2n))^{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(4tu\Delta(d+2n))^{n}}{n!}e^{4t\Delta(d+2n)}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\dfrac{n^{n}\left(4tu\Delta(d+2)e^{4t\Delta(d+2)}\right)^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\left(4ue4t\Delta(d+2)e^{4t\Delta(d+2)}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\left((u/e)\cdot 4e^{2}t\Delta(d+2)e^{4e^{2}t\Delta(d+2)}\right)^{n}\,,

where the second inequality is achieved by noting that
(d+2n)\leq n(d+2)
and the third one via the inequality
n^{n}/n!\leq e^{n}
.
Letting
t^{*}=\frac{1}{4e^{2}\Delta(d+2)}
, the series converges when

\frac{u}{e}\cdot\dfrac{t}{t^{*}}e^{t/t^{*}}<1.

Now we use the following simple proposition

Proposition 4.5
.

If
0<t<\frac{1}{2}t^{*}\log(e/u)=:t_{\textup{max}}
then
\frac{u}{e}\frac{t}{t^{*}}e^{t/t^{*}}<\frac{t}{t_{\textup{max}}}<1
.

Proof.

If
t<t_{\textup{max}}
then
\frac{u}{e}\frac{t}{t^{*}}e^{t/t^{*}}<\frac{u}{e}\frac{t}{t^{*}}\sqrt{\frac{e}{u}}=\frac{t}{t^{*}\cdot\sqrt{e/u}}\leq\frac{t}{t^{*}\cdot\log(\sqrt{e/u})}=\frac{t}{t_{\textup{max}}}<1
.
∎

Hence if
t<\frac{\log(e/u)}{8e^{2}\Delta(d+2)}=:t_{\textup{max}}(u)
we have

\left\lVert A^{k}(t)-A(t)\right\rVert_{\textup{F}}\leq\|A\|_{\textup{F}}\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{t}{t_{\textup{max}}(u)}\right)^{n}=\|A\|_{\textup{F}}\dfrac{\left(\frac{t}{t_{\textup{max}}(u)}\right)^{k+1}}{1-\frac{t}{t_{\textup{max}}(u)}}\,

as desired.
∎

Remark
.

It is possible to prove a bound of the form (
54
) for the operator norm instead of the Frobenius norm, using essentially the same proof, but with a different argument for the inequality (
55
). We omit this here as we focus on the Frobenius norm in this paper.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Using Lemma
4.3
and the observation that
\deg A^{k}(t)\leq\deg A+2k
, we get with
k=\lfloor(\ell-\deg A)/2\rfloor
,

\eta^{\star}_{\textup{F}}(H(u),A;t,\ell)\leq\frac{(t/t_{\textup{max}}(u))^{(\ell-\deg A)/2}}{1-t/t_{\textup{max}}(u)}\|A\|_{\textup{F}}.

∎

4.3
Majorana Propagation for weakly interacting Hamiltonians

So far we have only shown the
existence
of an operator
P
satisfying
\deg(P)\leq\deg A+2k
which converges to
A(t)
exponentially fast in
k
. Note that the operator
P=A^{k}(t)
used in the proof (see Equation (
53
)) involves an infinite series and it is not clear if it can be computed efficiently.

In the following, we prove Corollary
1.3
, which states that the MP algorithm is able to find such a low-degree approximation of
A(t)
with complexity
N^{O(\log(t/\varepsilon))}
. To show this, we use the results of Theorem
4.1
in Theorem
3.3
and then specify the scalings of
\delta t,\ell
with respect to
\varepsilon,t
for the error of MP to be less than
\varepsilon
.

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

We assume that
\|A\|_{\textup{F}}=1
.
From Theorem
1.2
we know that for
t\leq t_{\textup{max}}(u)/2
,

\eta^{\star}_{\textup{F}}(H(u),A,t,\ell)\leq 2\cdot 2^{-(\ell-\deg A)/2}.

Plugging this in the error bound (
34
) of the output of MP, we get that the MP algorithm with time step
\delta t
and truncation parameter
\ell
has error at most

\displaystyle\|A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell,\delta t}(t)-A(t)\|_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle\leq 34t\cdot\delta t\cdot\Delta^{2}(\ell+2)^{2}+2\lceil t/\delta t\rceil 2^{-(\ell-\deg A)/2}

\displaystyle\leq 34t\cdot\delta t\cdot\Delta^{2}(\ell+2)^{2}+2(t/\delta t+1)2^{-(\ell-\deg A)/2}.

We need to choose parameters
\delta t
and
\ell
such that the right-hand side is at most
\varepsilon
. For a given
\ell
, the optimal
\delta t
, which minimizes the right-hand side is

\delta t=\sqrt{\frac{2}{34}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2^{-(\ell-\deg A)/2}}}{\Delta(\ell+2)}

(58)

for which the error bound becomes

\displaystyle\|A_{\textup{MP}}^{\ell,\delta t}(t)-A(t)\|_{\textup{F}}\leq C_{1}t\Delta(\ell+2)2^{-(\ell-\deg A)/4}+2\cdot 2^{-(\ell-\deg A)/2}

where
C_{1}=2\sqrt{34\cdot 2}
. We find
\ell
such that both terms are less than
\varepsilon/2
, i.e., we want, with
L=\ell/4

\displaystyle L2^{-L}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{16C_{1}t\Delta}2^{-\deg A/4}

(59)

\displaystyle 2^{-(4L-\deg A)/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

(60)

We use the next simple proposition to solve the first inequality.

Proposition 4.6
.

If
\rho>0
and
a>0
, then for any
v\geq\max(0,a+\log_{2}(4a/\rho+4/\rho^{2}))
, we have
v2^{-v}\leq\rho 2^{-a}
.

Proof.

The function
v\mapsto v2^{-v}
is increasing on
[0,1/\log 2]
and decreasing on
[1/\log 2,\infty)
and takes the (maximal) value
1/(e\log 2)\approx 0.53
at
v=1/\log 2
. For
y>0
, define

v_{0}(y):=\begin{cases}0\quad\text{ if }y\geq 1/(e\log 2)\\
\text{the unique solution in $[1/\log 2,+\infty)$ to $v_{0}2^{-v_{0}}=y$ if $y<1/(e\log 2)$}.\end{cases}

Note that in the second case, this unique solution can be expressed in terms of the Lambert function as
v_{0}(y)=W_{-1}(-y\log 2)/(-\log 2)
.
By definition, for any
y>0
, we have
v2^{-v}\leq y
for all
v\geq v_{0}(y)
. It is not hard to check that for all
y>0

v_{0}(y)\leq\begin{cases}\max(0,2+\log_{2}(1/y)+\log_{2}\log_{2}(1/y))&\text{ if }y\in[0,1)\\
0&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

With
y=\rho 2^{-a}
, and assuming that
y\in[0,1)
we have

\displaystyle 2+\log_{2}(1/y)+\log_{2}\log_{2}(1/y)

\displaystyle=2+a+\log_{2}(a\rho^{-1}+\rho^{-1}\log_{2}(\rho^{-1}))

\displaystyle\leq 2+a+\log_{2}(a\rho^{-1}+\rho^{-2})

\displaystyle=a+\log_{2}(4a\rho^{-1}+4\rho^{-2})

Hence with this we have that for any
\rho,a>0

v_{0}(\rho 2^{-a})\leq\max(0,a+\log_{2}(4a\rho^{-1}+4\rho^{-2})).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Hence if we take
L\geq\max\left(0,\deg A/4+\log_{2}\left(4\frac{16C_{1}t\Delta\deg A}{\varepsilon}+4\left(\frac{16C_{1}t\Delta}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}\right)\right)
the first inequality (
59
) is satisfied. To satisfy the second inequality (
60
), we can take
L\geq\deg A/4+\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(\frac{4}{\varepsilon})
. Finally, since
\ell=4L
, we have the desired error bound with

\ell=\deg A+4\left\lceil\log_{2}\left(4\frac{\max\left(1,16C_{1}t\Delta\deg A+(16C_{1}t\Delta)^{2}/\varepsilon\right)}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\rceil.

(We used the assumption that
\varepsilon<4
which implies that the argument of the logarithm is
\geq 1
, and thus that the whole quantity is necessarily
\geq 0
.)
With such a choice of
\ell
, note that, from (
58
),
\delta t=\varepsilon^{O(1)}
, and so the total complexity of the algorithm is
t/\delta t\cdot N^{O(\ell)}=N^{O(\log((1+t)/\varepsilon))}
.
∎

As a well-known benchmark for simulation of fermionic systems, we focus on the Fermi-Hubbard model, described by the following Hamiltonian

H=-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle,\sigma}c^{*}_{i,\sigma}c_{j,\sigma}+c^{*}_{j,\sigma}c_{i,\sigma}+U\sum_{i}n_{i,\uparrow}n_{i,\downarrow}\,,

(61)

where
c^{*}_{i,\sigma},c_{i,\sigma}
are the fermionic creation and annihilation operators. Here
\langle i,j\rangle
runs over all neighbouring sites and
\sigma\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
specifies the spin degree-of-freedom. The first sum in the Hamiltonian represents hopping between sites, while the second one represents the Coulomb repulsion between two electrons on the same site. Even though this is a simplified model of electrons in materials, it already contains complex underlying physics and has also been a central object of study for advancing many classical algorithms for many-body systems
[
16
]
, and more recently quantum algorithms
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.

We consider an initial state where all the sites are filled in an antiferromagnetic pattern except for the central one which is empty. In other words, letting
N_{\text{centre}}=(L^{2}+1)/2
, we consider

|\psi\rangle=\prod_{i\neq N_{\text{centre}}}c_{i,\sigma(i)}^{*}|\text{0}\rangle\qquad\sigma(i)=\begin{cases}\uparrow&\text{ if ($i<N_{\text{centre}}$ and $i$ odd) or ($i>N_{\text{centre}}$ and $i$ even)}\\
\downarrow&\text{ if ($i<N_{\text{centre}}$ and $i$ even) or (if $i>N_{\text{centre}}$ and $i$ odd)}.\end{cases}

(62)

For each system size, we plot in Fig.
2
the probability of the central site being empty as a function of time, i.e. we plot the time evolution of
\langle h_{N_{\text{centre}},\uparrow}h_{N_{\text{centre}},\downarrow}\rangle
where
h_{j,\sigma}=1-c^{*}_{j,\sigma}c_{j,\sigma}
. We do this for different values of interaction strength
U\in[0,4]
. Additionally, for
L=3
we also plot the exact solution as this is readily available at this system size. Across all system sizes, at fixed
U

\ell
generally leads to higher accuracy. At the same time, as we increase
U
it can be noticed that the plot for a given maximal degree tends to diverge at increasingly earlier times. This is testament of the behaviour of the characteristic maximal time
t_{\textup{max}}(u)
with respect to the interaction strength
U
.
It is interesting to note that for all values of
L
and for
U\leq 2
, the
\ell=8,10
curves are roughly overlapping, which may be caused by the fact that the truncation parameter is already well converged across all these cases (with the deviation from the exact case for
L=3
being caused by the Trotter approximation).

As a final remark, let us note that during simulation, each newly generated Majorana string
\upgamma_{S}
that satisfies
|S|>\ell
is removed after every single Majorana rotation, as opposed to after having gone through the full Trotter sweep. This can only worsen the accuracy as it might be possible that, within a Trotter step, strings surpassing the threshold might recombine to produce lower-degree strings. However, as seen from the numerical results, the overall results remain highly accurate and reflect the analytical results concerning the MP algorithm.
