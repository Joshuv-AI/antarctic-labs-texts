A reverse entropy power inequality for i.i.d. log-concave random variables
Zhen Fu, and Jiange Li

Abstract
. We show that h ∞ ​ ( X + Y ) ≤ h ∞ ​ ( Z + W ) h_{\infty}(X+Y)\leq h_{\infty}(Z+W) , where X , Y X,Y are independent log-concave random variables, and Z , W Z,W are exponential random variables having the same respective ∞ \infty -Rényi entropies. 1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a random vector taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that it has density
f
with respect to the Lebesgue measure. The classical
differential entropy
(also called
Boltzmann-Shannon entropy
) of
X
is defined as

h(X)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\log f(x)dx.

The celebrated
entropy power inequality
(or EPI for short) of Shannon
[
18
]
(and Stam
[
19
]
) states that for independent random vectors
X
and
Y
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the entropies of
X,Y
, and
X+Y
exist, it holds that

e^{\frac{2}{d}h(X+Y)}\geq e^{\frac{2}{d}h(X)}+e^{\frac{2}{d}h(Y)},

(1.1)

where equality holds if and only if
X
and
Y
are Gaussian random vectors with proportional covariance matrices. The EPI found its original applications in the study of channel capacity in information theory. It has been known very well that EPI is closely related to geometric and functional inequalities in a variety of mathematical fields, maybe most notably the Brunn-Minkowski inequality (or BMI for short) in convex geometry. We refer the interested reader to
[
7
,
12
]
for profound connections among EPI, BMI, and many other geometric and functional inequalities.

Convexity (or concavity) underpins the validity of the reversal of many geometric and functional inequalities. A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is called
log-concave
if it can be written as
f(x)=e^{-V(x)}
, where
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is a convex function. We say that a random vector
X
taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is log-concave provided that its density is a log-concave function. (We also say that
X
has log-concave distribution). The class of log-concave functions can be thought of as functional analogs of convex bodies. They are prominent objects studied in convex geometry, high dimensional probability and statistics, as well as theoretical computer science. The class of log-concave distributions include a wide range of important probability distributions, such as the Gaussian distributions, uniform distributions on convex bodies, and exponential distributions.

The reversal of EPI was first addressed by Bobkov and Madiman
[
3
]
. They showed that for independent log-concave random vectors, under linear volume preserving maps, inequality (
1.1
) can be reversed at the cost of a constant factor on the r.h.s of the inequality. This can be thought of as a functional analog of Milman’s reverse BMI for convex bodies
[
13
]
. It is necessary to place log-concave random vectors “at right positions” via linear volume preserving maps for the reversed EPI to hold. We are interested in the sharp reverse EPI for
independent and identically distributed
(or i.i.d. for short) log-concave random vectors. In this case, linear volume preserving maps are not needed and the question can be phrased as follows.

Conjecture 1.1

(Folklore)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be i.i.d. log-concave random vectors taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The entropy increment
h(X+Y)-h(X)
is maximized when
X
and
Y
have exponential distributions.

Clearly, the entropy increment is affine invariant. An earlier result of Cover and Zhang
[
6
]
shows that log-concavity and identical distribution (without independence between
X
and
Y
) yield the upper bound
d\log 2
of the entropy increment. Huang, Slomka, Tkocz, and Vritsiou (
[
10
]
, Section 6) observed that the improvement of Cover and Zhang’s bound for Conjecture
1.1
will lead to important progress on Hadwiger’s covering problem. Ball, Nayar, and Tkocz
[
2
]
studied the entropy increment for a two dimensional log-concave random vector
(X,Y)
, whose marginals have identical differential entropy (but not necessarily independent). This variant of reverse EPI and its generalizations are entropic analogs of Busemann’s convexity theorem of intersection bodies
[
4
]
and the convexity of
p
-cross-section bodies conjectured by Gardner and Giannopoulos
[
8
]
(the end of Section 5, and the discussion in
[
11
]
).

Aforementioned forward and reverse EPIs have been studied for general Rényi entropies. For
p\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
, the order
p
Rényi entropy of
X
is defined as

h_{p}(X)=\frac{1}{1-p}\log\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)^{p}dx.

(1.2)

For
p\in\{0,1,\infty\}
, by taking limits, we have

h_{0}(X)=\log|\mathrm{supp}\{f\}|
,
h_{\infty}(X)=-\log\|f\|_{\infty}
, and
h_{1}(X)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\log f(x)dx
. Here, we denote by
|\mathrm{supp}\{f\}|
the Lebesgue measure of the support of
f
, and
\|f\|_{\infty}
is the essential supremum of
f
, and
h_{1}(X)
is the classical differential entropy of
X
.

It is suspected that a transition will occur in the increment of Rényi entropy. More precisely,
there exists
p_{0}\in(0,1)
such that
h_{p}(X+Y)-h_{p}(X)
is maximized by exponential random vectors for
p\geq p_{0}
, and is maximized by uniform random vectors taking values in the cube
[0,1]^{d}
for
0<p\leq p_{0}
. It is perhaps worth to point out that the increment of Rényi entropy of order
p>1
(respectively,
0<p<1
) boils down to the minimization (respectively, maximization) of
\|f\ast f\|_{p}/\|f\|_{p}
over the class of integrable log-concave functions. This can be thought of as a special type of reverse Young’s convolution inequality. Our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be independent real-valued log-concave random variables. Let
Z
and
W
be independent exponential random variables such that
h_{\infty}(Z)=h_{\infty}(X)
and
h_{\infty}(W)=h_{\infty}(Y)
. We have

\displaystyle h_{\infty}(X+Y)\leq h_{\infty}(Z+W).

A discrete analog holds for integer-valued monotone log-concave random variables.

Remark 1.3
.

If
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
are independent real-valued random variables, and
U_{1},\cdots,U_{n}
are independent uniform random variables such that
h_{\infty}(U_{i})=h_{\infty}(X_{i})
for
i=1,\cdots,n
, Rogozin
[
17
]
proved that

h_{\infty}(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n})\geq h_{\infty}(U_{1}+\cdots+U_{n}).

Hence, our result can also be seen as a reverse Rogozin type result for two log-concave random variables. However, we are not able to establish analogs of Theorem
1.2
for three or more log-concave random variables or in multi-dimensions.

Another related problem is the characterization of log-concave distributions that maximize the Rényi entropic increment
h_{p}(X-Y)-h_{p}(X)
. In this case, it is suspected that exponential distributions are always maximizers. This resembles the Rogers-Shephard inequality in convex geometry
[
16
]
. In a certain sense, the symmetry of
X-Y
(implicitly) makes this problem more tractable than its counterpart of the
X+Y
case. Melbourne and Tkocz (
[
15
]
, Theorem VI.1) showed that the increment of Rényi entropy of order
p\geq 2
is indeed maximized by exponential random vectors. A discrete analog was obtained by Melbourne and Palafox-Castillo (
[
14
]
, Theorem 2.11) for integer-valued monotone log-concave random variables.

2.
Rearrangement and majorization

Rearrangement and majorization are classical tools for proving (sharp) geometric and functional inequalities. This section contains preliminary backgrounds of decreasing rearrangement and majorization theory (in one dimension). Hence, readers familiar with rearrangement theory are recommended to skip this section.

2.1.
Decreasing rearrangement

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a Borel measurable set with Lebesgue measure
|A|
. The
decreasing rearrangement
of
A
is defined as
A^{\downarrow}=[0,|A|)
. The following properties of decreasing rearrangement may be scattered in the literature. We provide proof of them for the convenience of the reader.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be Borel measurable sets.

(1)

For all
\lambda\geq 0
, it holds that
(\lambda A)^{\downarrow}=\lambda A^{\downarrow}
.

(2)

It holds that
A^{\downarrow}+B^{\downarrow}\subseteq(A+B)^{\downarrow}
.

(3)

Let
\{A_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of measurable sets such that
A_{n}\subseteq A_{n+1}
for all
n=1,2\cdots
. It holds that

(\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n})^{\downarrow}=\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}^{\downarrow}
.

Proof.

Statement (1) readily follows from the definition. By definition,
A^{\downarrow}=[0,|A|)
,
B^{\downarrow}=[0,|B|)
, and
(A+B)^{\downarrow}=[0,|A+B|)
. The Brunn-Minkowski inequality in one dimension yields
|A+B|\geq|A|+|B|
. Hence,
A^{\downarrow}+B^{\downarrow}=[0,|A|+|B|)\subseteq(A+B)^{\downarrow}
. This proves statement (2).

By definition,
A_{n}^{\downarrow}=[0,|A_{n}|)
. Since
A_{n}\subseteq A_{n+1}
, we have

\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}^{\downarrow}=\big[0,\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|A_{n}|\big)=\left[0,|\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}|\right)=(\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n})^{\downarrow}.

This proves the last statement.
∎

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
be a Borel measurable function such that all super level sets
\{f>\lambda\}
have finite measures (which is called
vanishing at infinity
). We define the
decreasing rearrangement

f^{\downarrow}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
as

f^{\downarrow}(x)=\sup\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:x\in\{f>\lambda\}^{\downarrow}\right\}.

(2.1)

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
be a Borel measurable function.

(1)

For all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, it holds that
\{f^{\downarrow}>\lambda\}=\{f>\lambda\}^{\downarrow}
. (Hence, this gives an equivalent definition of the decreasing rearrangement
f^{\downarrow}
).

(2)

If
f
is concave, then
f^{\downarrow}
is also concave. Consequently, if
f
is log-concave, then
f^{\downarrow}
is also log-concave.

Proof.

For each
x\in\{f^{\downarrow}>\lambda\}
, by the definition of
f^{\downarrow}
in (
2.1
), there exists
\lambda_{x}>\lambda
such that
x\in\{f>\lambda_{x}\}^{\downarrow}\subseteq\{f>\lambda\}^{\downarrow}
. This shows that
\{f^{\downarrow}>\lambda\}\subseteq\{f>\lambda\}^{\downarrow}
. To see the other direction, we apply Proposition
2.1
(statement (3)) to write

\{f>\lambda\}^{\downarrow}=\left(\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{f>\lambda+1/n\}\right)^{\downarrow}=\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{f>\lambda+1/n\}^{\downarrow}.

Hence, for each
x\in\{f>\lambda\}^{\downarrow}
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x\in\{f>\lambda+1/n\}^{\downarrow}
. By the definition of
f^{\downarrow}
in (
2.1
), this yields
f^{\downarrow}(x)\geq\lambda+1/n>\lambda
, and hence,
x\in\{f^{\downarrow}>\lambda\}
. This completes the proof of the first statement.

Suppose
f
is concave. This is equivalent to the following set-theoretic inequality. For any
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in[0,1]
, it holds that

(1-t)\{f>\lambda_{0}\}+t\{f>\lambda_{1}\}\subseteq\{f>(1-t)\lambda_{0}+t\lambda_{1}\}.

(2.2)

Next we prove the inclusion inequality (
2.2
) for
f^{\downarrow}
. This will yield the concavity of
f^{\downarrow}
. We apply statement (1) of the current proposition and Proposition
2.1
(statements (1) and (2)) to obtain

\displaystyle(1-t)\{f^{\downarrow}>\lambda_{0}\}+t\{f^{\downarrow}>\lambda_{1}\}

\displaystyle=(1-t)\{f>\lambda_{0}\}^{\downarrow}+t\{f>\lambda_{1}\}^{\downarrow}

\displaystyle=((1-t)\{f>\lambda_{0}\})^{\downarrow}+(t\{f>\lambda_{1}\})^{\downarrow}

\displaystyle\subseteq((1-t)\{f>\lambda_{0}\}+t\{f>\lambda_{1}\})^{\downarrow}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{f>(1-t)\lambda_{0}+t\lambda_{1}\}^{\downarrow}

\displaystyle=\{f^{\downarrow}>(1-t)\lambda_{0}+t\lambda_{1}\}.

Suppose that
f(x)=e^{V(x)}
is log-concave, i.e.,
V(x)
is concave. Write
f^{\downarrow}(x)=e^{\tilde{V}(x)}
. For all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\{\tilde{V}>\lambda\}=\{f^{\downarrow}>e^{\lambda}\}=\{f>e^{\lambda}\}^{\downarrow}=\{V>\lambda\}^{\downarrow}.

Using the first statement, we conclude that
\tilde{V}=V^{\downarrow}
. Since
V
is concave,
V^{\downarrow}
, and hence,
\tilde{V}
are concave. (One can show the concavity of
\tilde{V}
by establishing the corresponding set-theoretic inequality). This yields the log-concavity of
f^{\downarrow}
.
∎

The following is a Hardy-Littlewood type inequality.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f,g
be non-negative functions supported on
[a,b]
. Then it holds

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)g(x)dx\geq\int_{0}^{b-a}f^{\downarrow}(x)g^{\downarrow}(b-a-x)dx.

Proof.

Write
f(x)=\int_{0}^{f(x)}d\lambda
,
g(x)=\int_{0}^{g(x)}d\sigma
and apply Fubini-Tonelli’s theorem to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)g(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{a}^{b}\mathbbm{1}_{\{f>\lambda\}}(x)\mathbbm{1}_{\{g>\sigma\}}(x)dxd\lambda d\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\{f>\lambda\}\cap\{g>\sigma\}|d\lambda d\sigma.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{b-a}f^{\downarrow}(x)g^{\downarrow}(b-a-x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\{f^{\downarrow}>\lambda\}\cap(b-a-\{g^{\downarrow}>\sigma)\}|d\lambda d\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\{f>\lambda\}^{\downarrow}\cap(b-a-\{g>\sigma\}^{\downarrow})|d\lambda d\sigma,

where the second equality follows from Proposition
2.2
(statement (1)). Then the statement can be proved by applying the following inequality to
A=\{f>\lambda\},B=\{g>\sigma\}
. For any measurable sets
A,B\subseteq[a,b]
, it holds that

|A^{\downarrow}\cap(b-a-B^{\downarrow})|\leq|A\cap B|.

We assume that
A^{\downarrow}\cap(b-a-B^{\downarrow})\neq\emptyset
. Note
A^{\downarrow}=[0,|A|)
and
b-a-B^{\downarrow}=[b-a-|B|,b-a)
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle|A^{\downarrow}\cap(b-a-B^{\downarrow})|

\displaystyle=|A|-(b-a-|B|)

\displaystyle=|A|+|B|-(b-a)

\displaystyle\leq|A|+|B|-|A\cup B|

\displaystyle=|A\cap B|.

This completes the proof.
∎

2.2.
Majorization

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be two integrable functions such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)dx

We say that
f
is
majorized
by
g
if it holds for all
t\geq 0
that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(f(x)-t)_{+}dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}(g(x)-t)_{+}dx.

(2.3)

For
t\geq 0
, we write
m_{f}(t)=|\{x\in\mathbb{R}:f(x)>t\}|
and define
m_{g}(t)
in a similar manner. Then we can rewrite (
2.3
) as

\int_{t}^{\infty}m_{f}(s)ds\leq\int_{t}^{\infty}m_{g}(s)dx.

A real-valued function
f
on
\mathbb{R}
is called
unimodal
if there exists a point
x_{0}
such that
f
is non-decreasing for
x<x_{0}
and non-increasing for
x>x_{0}
. Clearly, log-concave functions are unimodal. The following result provides a sufficient condition for the majorization of
g
over
f
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose that for any Borel measurable set
A
of finite measure, there exists a Borel measurable set
B
with equal measure as
A
such that

\int_{A}f(x)dx\leq\int_{B}g(x)dx.

(2.4)

Then
f
is majorized by
g
. If
f
and
g
are unimodal, it suffices to verify the above inequality for intervals.

Proof.

For any given
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we write
A=\{f>t\}
. Then there exists
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
of the same measure as
A
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(f(x)-t)_{+}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{A}(f(x)-t)dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B}(g(x)-t)dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B}(g(x)-t)_{+}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}(g(x)-t)_{+}dx.

This shows the majorization of
g
over
f
.

If
f
and
g
are unimodal, both sides of inequality (
2.4
) are maximized by intervals of the same measure as
A
and
B
. Hence, it suffices to verify inequality (
2.4
) for intervals.
∎

The following property is a classical result in the majorization theory. The preliminary version was first proved by Hardy, Littlewood, and Pólya
[
9
]
and various extensions were discussed in Chong
[
5
]
. We include a proof for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
f
and
g
be non-negative functions on
\mathbb{R}
such that
f
is majorized by
g
. Suppose
\phi
is a convex function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\phi(0)=0
and it continuous at 0. Then it holds that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(f(x))dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(g(x))dx.

(2.5)

Proof.

Write
M_{f}(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}m_{f}(s)ds
and
M_{g}(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}m_{g}(s)ds
. One can check that
M_{f}(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)dx=M_{g}(0)
.
Since
f
is majorized by
g
, it holds for all
t\geq 0
that
M_{f}(t)\leq M_{g}(t)
. Since
m_{f}(t)
is non-increasing,
m_{f}^{\prime}(t)
exists almost everywhere. Since
\phi
is convex, both
\phi^{\prime}(t)
and
\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)
exist almost everywhere. Then we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(f(x))dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{f(x)}\phi^{\prime}(t)dtdx=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime}(t)m_{f}(t)dt

\displaystyle=-\phi^{\prime}(t)M_{f}(t)\big|_{0}^{\infty}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)M_{f}(t)dt

\displaystyle=-\phi^{\prime}(t)M_{g}(t)\big|_{0}^{\infty}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)M_{f}(t)dt

\displaystyle\leq-\phi^{\prime}(t)M_{g}(t)\big|_{0}^{\infty}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)M_{g}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(g(x))dx.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Main Results

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be i.i.d. log-concave random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We have

h_{2}(X+Y)\leq h_{2}(X)+d\log 2.

(3.1)

Moreover, the inequality can not be improved.

Remark 3.2
.

One can check that equality of (
3.1
) can be achieved by exponential and Laplace distributions with densities
\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}e^{-\lambda_{i}x_{i}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(x_{i})
, and
\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{2}e^{-\lambda_{i}|x_{i}|}
, respectively, where
\lambda_{i}>0
for
i=1,\cdots,d
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{Y}
be independent copies of
X
and
Y
, respectively. Let
f
be the density of
X-\tilde{X}
. Since
f
is symmetric and log-concave, we have

\|f\ast f\|_{\infty}=(f\ast f)(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)^{2}dx\geq f(0)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(2x)dx=\frac{f(0)}{2^{d}}.

This can be rewritten as

h_{\infty}((X-\tilde{X})+(Y-\tilde{Y}))\leq h_{\infty}(X-\tilde{X})+d\log 2.

For any i.i.d. log-concave random vectors
U
and
V
, it holds that
h_{\infty}(U-V)=h_{2}(U)
. This observation, together with the inequality above, implies that

\displaystyle h_{2}(X+Y)

\displaystyle=h_{\infty}((X+Y)-(\tilde{X}+\tilde{Y}))

\displaystyle=h_{\infty}((X-\tilde{X})+(Y-\tilde{Y}))

(3.2)

\displaystyle\leq h_{\infty}(X-\tilde{X})+d\log 2

\displaystyle=h_{2}(X)+d\log 2.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Note that
X-\tilde{X}
and
Y-\tilde{Y}
are symmetric and independent. Then, we can proceed with (
3
) to obtain

\displaystyle h_{2}(X+Y)

\displaystyle=h_{\infty}((X-\tilde{X})+(Y-\tilde{Y}))

\displaystyle=h_{\infty}((X-\tilde{X})-(Y-\tilde{Y}))

\displaystyle=h_{\infty}((X-Y)-(\tilde{X}-\tilde{Y}))

\displaystyle=h_{2}(X-Y).

Hence, ineuality (
3.1
) is the same as the
p=2
case of Theorem VI.1 of Melbourne and Tkocz
[
15
]
. For any log-concave random vectors
(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, it was proved in
[
11
]
that

e^{h_{2}(X+Y)}\leq e^{h_{2}(X)}+e^{h_{2}(Y)},

which implies the one-dimensional case of (
3.1
) as long as
X,Y
are jointly log-concave (even unnecessary to be independent).

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
X
and
Y
be independent real-valued log-concave random variables. Let
Z
and
W
be independent exponential random variables such that
h_{\infty}(Z)=h_{\infty}(X)
and
h_{\infty}(W)=h_{\infty}(Y)
. We have

\displaystyle h_{\infty}(X+Y)\leq h_{\infty}(Z+W).

(3.3)

Consequently, for i.i.d. real-valued log-concave random variables
X
and
Y
, we have

\displaystyle h_{\infty}(X+Y)\leq h_{\infty}(X)+1.

(3.4)

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be integrable unimodal functions. Then it holds that

\|f\ast g\|_{\infty}\geq\|f^{\downarrow}\ast g^{\downarrow}\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the statement for functions supported on finite intervals since the general case will follow from the standard approximation argument. Since the desired inequality is translation and scaling invariant, we can assume that
f
and
g
achieve their maximums at the same point (if the maximums are achieved in two intervals, we assume the middle points of the two intervals coincide) and that
|\mathrm{supp}\{f\}|=1\leq a=|\mathrm{supp}\{g\}|
. By definition, we have

(f^{\downarrow}\ast g^{\downarrow})(x)=\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{x}f^{\downarrow}(y)g^{\downarrow}(x-y)dy,&x\in[0,1]\\
\int_{0}^{1}f^{\downarrow}(y)g^{\downarrow}(x-y)dy,&x\in[1,a]\\
\int_{x-a}^{1}f^{\downarrow}(y)g^{\downarrow}(x-y)dy,&x\in[a,a+1).\\
\end{cases}

For
x\geq y
, note that
g^{\downarrow}(x-y)
is non-increasing w.r.t.
x
. This implies that
f^{\downarrow}\ast g^{\downarrow}
is non-increasing in
[1,1+a]
, and hence the maximum of
f^{\downarrow}\ast g^{\downarrow}
is achieved at some
x\in[0,1]
. For such a
x
, there exists
I_{x}=(b,b+x)
such that
\{f>\lambda\}\subseteq I_{x}\subseteq\{f\geq\lambda\}
and
\{g>\sigma\}\subseteq I_{x}\subseteq\{g\geq\sigma\}
for some
\lambda,\sigma\geq 0
.
(The existence of
I_{x}
follows from the unimodality of
f,g
and that
f,g
achieve their maximums at the same point, but such an interval might not be unique.) Define
f_{x}(y)=f(y)1_{I_{x}}(y)
and
g_{x}(y)=g(2b+x-y)1_{I_{x}}(2b+x-y)
. For any
y\in[0,x]
, we have

f^{\downarrow}(y)=f_{x}^{\downarrow}(y)
and
g^{\downarrow}(x-y)=g_{x}^{\downarrow}(x-y)
. Then we obtain

\displaystyle(f^{\downarrow}\ast g^{\downarrow})(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}f_{x}^{\downarrow}(y)g_{x}^{\downarrow}(x-y)dy\leq\int_{b}^{b+x}f_{x}(y)g_{x}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{b}^{b+x}f(y)g(2b+x-y)dy

\displaystyle\leq(f\ast g)(2b+x),

where the first inequality follows from Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
X
and
Y
be independent monotone log-concave random variables. Let
Z
and
W
be independent exponential random variables such that
h_{\infty}(Z)=h_{\infty}(X)
and
h_{\infty}(W)=h_{\infty}(Y)
. For all
0<p\leq\infty
, we have

\displaystyle h_{p}(X+Y)\leq h_{p}(Z+W).

(3.5)

Proof.

We will show the majorization of
X+Y
over
X+W
(i.e., the density of
X+Y
majorizes that of
X+W
) and, in the same vein, the majorization of
X+W
over
Z+W
. Hence,
Z+W
is majorized by
X+Y
. This, together with inequality (
2.5
), yields the statement (
3.5
).

Now, we show the majorization of
X+Y
over
X+W
. Note that
X+Y
and
X+W
are log-concave. By Proposition
2.4
, it suffice to show that for any
[a,a+\delta]\subseteq[0,\infty)
, there exists
[b,b+\delta]\subseteq[0,\infty)
such that

{\mathbb{P}}(X+W\in[a,a+\delta])\leq{\mathbb{P}}(X+Y\in[b,b+\delta]).

(3.6)

Let
f_{X}
and
f_{Y}
be the densities of
X
and
Y
, respectively. We can perturbate
f_{X}
and
f_{Y}
if necessary such that both of them have infinite support. We can also make shifts if necessary so that
\|f_{X}\|_{\infty}=f_{X}(0)
and
\|f_{Y}\|_{\infty}=f_{Y}(0)
. Write
f_{Y}(x)=f_{Y}(0)e^{-V(x)}
for
x\geq 0
, where
V(x)
is an non-decreasing convex function with
V(0)=0
. Then
W
has density
f_{W}(x)=f_{Y}(0)e^{-f_{Y}(0)x}
for
x\geq 0
. Consider the map
\phi=F_{W}^{-1}\circ F_{Y}
, where
F_{Y}
and
F_{W}
are the cumulative distribution functions of
Y
and
W
, respectively. One can check that
W\overset{d}{=}\phi(Y)
and
\phi(y)=-f_{Y}(0)^{-1}\log(1-F_{Y}(y))
. Then we can rewrite inequality (
3.6
) as

{\mathbb{P}}(X+\phi(Y)\in[a,a+\delta])\leq{\mathbb{P}}(X+Y\in[b,b+\delta]).

By conditioning on
Y
, it suffices to show for all
y\geq 0
that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(X\in[a,a+\delta]-\phi(y))\leq{\mathbb{P}}(X\in[b,b+\delta]-y).

(3.7)

Next, we demonstrate that this inequality holds for
b=\phi^{-1}(a)
.
Since
Y
is log-concave, the tail probability
1-F_{Y}(y)
is also log-concave. Hence,
\phi
is a convex map. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(y)=\frac{e^{-V(y)}}{1-F_{Y}(y)}\geq 1.

(3.8)

Case 1
:
y\geq\phi^{-1}(a+\delta)
. Since
X
is non-negative, inequality (
3.7
) trivially holds.

Case 2
:
\phi^{-1}(a)<y<\phi^{-1}(a+\delta)
. Inequality (
3.8
) implies that

y-\phi^{-1}(a)\leq\phi(y)-a
, which is equivalent to
a+\delta-\phi(y)\leq\phi^{-1}(a)+\delta-y
. Since
X
is non-negative, we obtain

{\mathbb{P}}(X\in[0,a+\delta-\phi(y)]\leq{\mathbb{P}}(X\in[0,\phi^{-1}(a)+\delta-y]),

which is inequality (
3.7
).

Case 3
:
y\leq\phi^{-1}(a)
. Similar to Case 2, we can apply inequality (
3.8
) to obtain

a-\phi(y)\geq\phi^{-1}(a)-y\geq 0
. This, together with the monotonicity of the density of
X
, yields inequality (
3.7
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

As shown in Proposition
2.2
, log-concavity is preserved by decreasing rearrangement. Then inequality (
3.3
) follows from Lemmas
3.5
and
3.6
, and inequality (
3.4
) follows from simple calculations.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

We give a short proof of the
p=\infty
case of inequality (
3.5
) via measure transportation (in fact the change of variable). (This, together with Lemma
3.5
, yields a short proof of Theorem
3.4
). Since the map
\phi
transports
Y
to
W
, it holds for any function
\varphi
integrable with respect to
F_{W}
that

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varphi(\phi(x))f_{Y}(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\varphi(x)f_{W}(x)dx.

For any
y\geq 0
, we apply this identity to
\varphi(x)=f_{X}(y-x)
for
0\leq x\leq y
to obtain

\displaystyle(f_{X}\ast f_{W})(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{y}f_{X}(y-x)f_{W}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\phi^{-1}(y)}f_{X}(y-\phi(x))f_{Y}(x)dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\phi^{-1}(y)}f_{X}(\phi^{-1}(y)-x)f_{Y}(x)dx

\displaystyle=(f_{X}\ast f_{Y})(\phi^{-1}(y)),

where the inequality follows from the observation that
y-\phi(x)\geq\phi^{-1}(y)-x
, which is implied by (
3.8
), and the assumption that
f_{X}
is non-increasing. This yields

h_{\infty}(X+Y)\leq h_{\infty}(X+W)

and similarly we can obtain

h_{\infty}(X+W)\leq h_{\infty}(Z+W).

These two inequalities together yield the
p=\infty
case of inequality (
3.5
). One can see that the monotonicity of
f_{X}
and the expansion property of the transportation map
\phi
play the key role.

Remark 3.8
.

Let
(X,Y)
be an
origin symmetric
log-concave random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. (The marginals are not necessarily independent). A result of Ball (
[
1
]
, Theorem 5) imply that

e^{h_{\infty}(X+Y)}\leq e^{h_{\infty}(X)}+e^{h_{\infty}(Y)}.

This inequality can be seen as the entropic analog of Busemann’s convexity theorem of intersection bodies
[
4
]
, which also motivates the study of reverse EPIs in
[
2
,
11
]
.

4.
Analogs for Interger-valued Log-concave Random Variables

We first recall the majorization of two sequences. Let
\{f(k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
\{g(k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be two summable non-negative sequences such that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f(k)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}g(k)
. We say that
\{f(k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
majorizes
\{g(k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
if the decreasing rearrangements
\{f^{\downarrow}(k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
\{g^{\downarrow}(k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
satisfy that
\sum_{k=1}^{n}f^{\downarrow}(k)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{k}g^{\downarrow}(k)
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Karamata’s inequality (or majorization inequality) says that if
\{f(k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
majorizes
\{g(k)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
then it holds for any convex function
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}
that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\varphi\circ f)(k)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\varphi\circ g)(k).

(4.1)

An integer-valued random variable
X
is called
log-concave
if its probability mass function
\{f(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a log-concave sequence; that is,
f(k+1)^{2}\geq f(k)f(k+2)
holds for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Similar to (
1.2
), the order
p
Rényi entropy of
X
is defined as

H_{p}(X)=\frac{1}{1-p}\log\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)^{p}.

Particularly, we have

H_{\infty}(X)=-\log\|f\|_{\infty}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be i.i.d log-concave random variables on
\mathbb{Z}
. It holds that

H_{2}(X+Y)<H_{2}(X)+\log 2.

(4.2)

Moreover, the inequality cannot be improved.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{X}
and
\tilde{Y}
be independent copies of
X
and
Y
, respectively. We denote by
\{f(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
the probability mass function of
X-\tilde{X}
. We define the geometric sequence
\{g(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
as
g(k)=f(0)\left(\frac{1-f(0)}{1+f(0)}\right)^{k}
. One can check that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f(k)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g(k)
. Note that
f(0)=g(0)
and
\{g(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
is a log-affine sequence. We have the majorization of
\{f(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
over
\{g(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
. We apply Karamata’s inequality (
4.1
) to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}f(k)^{2}-f(0)^{2}\geq 2\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}g(k)^{2}-f(0)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+f(0)^{2}}{2}\cdot f(0)>\frac{f(0)}{2}.

The above inequality, together with the symmetry of
X-\tilde{X}
, implies that

\displaystyle H_{\infty}((X-\tilde{X})+(Y-\tilde{Y}))

\displaystyle=-\log(f\ast f)(0)=-\log\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)^{2}

\displaystyle<-\log f(0)+\log 2=H_{\infty}(X-\tilde{X})+\log 2.

(4.3)

Observe that for any i.i.d. discrete log-concave random variables
U
and
V
, it holds that
H_{2}(U)=H_{\infty}(U-V)
. We have

\displaystyle H_{2}(X+Y)

\displaystyle=H_{\infty}((X+Y)-(\tilde{X}+\tilde{Y}))

\displaystyle=H_{\infty}((X-\tilde{X})+(Y-\tilde{Y}))

\displaystyle<H_{\infty}(X-\tilde{X})+\log 2

\displaystyle=H_{2}(X)+\log 2.

The inequality follows from (
4
).
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Similar to the continuous case, the identity
H_{2}(X+Y)=H_{2}(X-Y)
holds for any
\mathbb{Z}
-valued i.i.d log-concave random variables
X
and
Y
. Hence, Proposition
4.1
is the same as the
p=2
case of Theorem 2.11 of Melbourne and Palafox-Castillo
[
14
]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be i.i.d. log-concave random variables on
\mathbb{Z}
. Assume that their probability mass function
f
is monotone. It holds that

\|f\ast f\|_{\infty}>e^{-1}\|f\|_{\infty}.

Moreover, the inequality can not be improved. In other words, we have

H_{\infty}(X+Y)<H_{\infty}(X)+1.

Proof.

We assume that
f
is non-increasing and supported on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Write
f(k)=e^{-V(k)}
, where
V
is non-decreasing and convex. Hence,
\|f\|_{\infty}=e^{-V(0)}
. We apply the convexity of
V
to obtain

\displaystyle(f\ast f)(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}e^{-[V(i)+V(k-i)]}\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k}e^{-[V(0)+V(k)]}=(k+1)e^{-V(k)}\cdot\|f\|_{\infty}.

Write
\phi(k):=(k+1)e^{-V(k)}=e^{\log(k+1)-V(k)}
. It suffices to show that

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\phi(k)>1/e.

(4.4)

Since
\phi
is log-concave, the supremum in (
4.4
) can be achieved, say at
k=k^{*}
. Consider the following scenarios.

Case 1
:
k^{*}=0
. Then it is necessary to have
\phi(0)\geq\phi(1)
, which can be rewritten as

V(1)-V(0)\geq\log 2.

Since
V
is convex, it holds that

V(k)\geq V(0)+k(V(1)-V(0))\geq V(0)+k\log 2.

As a consequence, we obtain

1=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f(k)=e^{-V(0)}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}e^{-(V(k)-V(0))}\leq\phi(0)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k}=2\phi(0).

Hence, it is necessary to have
\phi(0)\geq 1/2
and inequality (
4.4
) holds.

Case 2
:
k^{*}\geq 1
. Then it is necessary to have

\phi(k^{*}-1)\leq\phi(k^{*})\leq\phi(k^{*}+1)
, which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle V(k^{*})-V(k^{*}-1)

\displaystyle\leq\log(1+1/k^{*})

\displaystyle V(k^{*}+1)-V(k^{*})

\displaystyle\geq\log(1+1/(k^{*}+1)).

Owing to the convexity of
V
, we obtain for
k\leq k^{*}
that

\displaystyle V(k)

\displaystyle\geq V(k^{*})-(k^{*}-k)[V(k^{*})-V(k^{*}-1)]

\displaystyle\geq V(k^{*})+(k-k^{*})\log(1+1/k^{*})

(4.5)

and for
k\geq k^{*}
that

\displaystyle V(k)

\displaystyle\geq V(k^{*})+(k-k^{*})[V(k^{*}+1)-V(k^{*})]

\displaystyle\geq V(k^{*})+(k-k^{*})\log(1+1/(k^{*}+1)).

(4.6)

Apply the estimates (
4
) and (
4
) to obtain

\displaystyle 1=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f(k)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-V(k^{*})}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{k^{*}}\left(1+\frac{1}{k^{*}}\right)^{k^{*}-k}+\sum_{k=k^{*}+1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{1}{k^{*}+1}\right)^{-(k-k^{*})}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-V(k^{*})}\left(1+k^{*}\left(1+\frac{1}{k^{*}}\right)^{k^{*}+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\phi(k^{*})\left(\frac{1}{k^{*}+1}+\left(1+\frac{1}{k^{*}}\right)^{k^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle<e\phi(k^{*}).

In the last inequality, we use the fact that
(x+1)^{-1}+(1+1/x)^{x}\nearrow e
as
x\rightarrow\infty
. Hence, it is necessary to have
\phi(k^{*})>1/e
, i.e., inequality (
4.4
) holds.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Proposition
4.1
and Theorem
4.3
are tight for geometric distributions
f(k)=(1-\lambda)\lambda^{k}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. One can check that
(f\ast f)(k)=(k+1)(1-\lambda)^{2}\lambda^{k}
and that

e^{H_{2}(f\ast f)-H_{2}(f)}=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f(k)^{2}}{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(f\ast f)(k)^{2}}=\frac{(1+\lambda)^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\rightarrow 2,~~\text{as}~\lambda\rightarrow 1.

It is clear that
\|f\|_{\infty}=1-\lambda
. Set
\lambda=e^{-1/(k+1)}
. As
k\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\frac{\|f\ast f\|_{\infty}}{\|f\|_{\infty}}\geq(k+1)(1-\lambda)\lambda^{k}=(k+1)(1-e^{-1/(k+1)})e^{-k/(k+1)}\rightarrow\frac{1}{e}.

If the probability mass function of
X
is symmetric about an integer or a half-integer, we can apply the argument in Proposition
4.1
to establish
H_{\infty}(X+Y)<H_{\infty}(X)+1/2
.
