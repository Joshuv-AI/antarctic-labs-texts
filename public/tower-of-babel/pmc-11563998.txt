Intrapartum antibiotic exposure and infectious diseases in childhood – a population-based cohort study
Authors: Mikael Hakkola, Sofia Ainonen, Eveliina Ronkainen, Minna Honkila, Marika Paalanne, Tytti Pokka, Eero Kajantie, Niko Paalanne, Terhi Ruuska-Loewald
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Added value of this study

The present results show that the use of intrapartum antibiotics, mostly penicillin, for preventing early-onset group B streptococcal disease in newborn infants is associated with an overall increased long-term risk of infectious diseases in childhood. The risk of later infections appears to be dependent on the bacterial pathogens and their antimicrobial susceptibility to penicillin. Exposure to intrapartum antibiotics was associated with an increased risk of urinary tract infections mostly caused by penicillin resistant pathogens, but also with long-term protection from severe infections caused by pathogens susceptible to penicillin.

Implications of all the available evidence

Exposure to maternal intrapartum antibiotics, used for preventing early-onset group B streptococcal disease, has been shown to change the composition of the child's developing gut microbiome. This study shows the use of maternal intrapartum antibiotics to be associated with a small overall increase in the risk of later infectious diseases in the offspring. In addition, there may be a long-term protective effect against severe infectious diseases caused by penicillin-susceptible microbes.

Introduction

Group B streptococcus (GBS) is recognised as one of the leading causes of severe neonatal infections with global estimates of 230,000 invasive infections and 90,000 deaths annually.1 Use of intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) to prevent neonatal early-onset GBS disease has significantly reduced the incidence of early-onset GBS disease.2,3 Universal antenatal screening for maternal GBS colonisation, currently the recommended prevention strategy in many high-income countries, has been shown to reduce risk of the early-onset disease by 40–65% compared to a risk-based approach.4,5 Universal antenatal screening began in Finland during the year 2012 and was fully implemented by January 1, 2013. Universal screening of pregnant women and subsequent IAP, however, exposes up to 30% of infants to antibiotics at birth.6

Intrapartum antibiotics influence the early gut colonisation patterns leading to changes in the composition of the infant gut microbiome.7, 8, 9, 10 These changes are most evident during the first few months after birth.11 In our previous study, we showed that the effect of perinatal antibiotics on the gut microbiome of infants is greater than that of subsequent courses of oral antibiotics at the age of one year.12 The composition of a child's gut microbiome has been associated with the risk of obesity and autoimmune diseases, and also with the risk of bronchiolitis and urinary tract infections, both common childhood infections.13, 14, 15 The clinical long-term effects of intrapartum antibiotic exposure are not yet fully understood.

Our overarching hypothesis was that intrapartum antibiotic exposure is associated with increased risk of childhood infections because exposure to intrapartum antibiotic has earlier been shown to influence the development of gut microbiome in children. We set out to investigate the long-term clinical effects of intrapartum antibiotic exposure on infectious diseases using structured electronic medical records linked to comprehensive national registers.

Methods

Study design and setting

The cohort for this register-based study was composed of 49,299 vaginally delivered children born in Oulu University Hospital and Oulaskangas Hospital in Northern Finland. The data were collected from structured electronic medical records and comprehensive national registers, which were linked through the unique personal identity codes recorded in Finland's Population Information System since 1971. The study was conducted with permission from the Review Board of the National Institute for Health and Welfare, Helsinki, Finland (decision number THL/1522/5.05.00/2019). All analyses and reporting took place according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study cohort

Children born to mothers living in Northern Finland deduced from the mother's place of residence recorded in the Medical Birth Register during the period from January 1, 2007, to December 31, 2018, were included in the cohort. Children with major congenital anomalies defined according to EUROCAT's classification and those delivered by Caesarean section (CS) were excluded.16 All the children included were followed up from birth until the completion of the study on December 31, 2019, or death, whichever occurred first.

Exposures

The main exposure was administration of intrapartum antibiotics to the mother 24 h or less before the birth of the child and data on this were retrieved from the digital delivery records maintained at Oulu University Hospital and Oulaskangas Hospital, Finland. Data on the use of postnatal antibiotics, defined as antibiotic exposure in the first 0–6 days of life, were retrieved from the intensive care information system of Oulu University Hospital and from individual electronic medical records.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the annual number of infectious disease episodes leading to an emergency room visit or outpatient visit in hospital or hospitalisation from birth until five years of age. The first year of life was additionally divided into three periods: 0–6 days, 7–28 days, and 29−365 days. Infections were defined as separate episodes if the diagnosis differed from that at the previous visit or if it took place more than two days after discharge from hospital with the same diagnosis. Data on infectious disease episodes were retrieved from the National Care Register for Health Care using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes. The secondary outcomes were the number of severe infection episodes in the cohort, defined as a positive blood culture or cerebrospinal fluid sample, reported to the Finnish National Infectious Diseases Register, the number of antibiotic courses administered, the numbers of tympanostomies, tonsillectomies, adenotonsillectomies, and adenoidectomies performed, the cumulative incidence of at least one infectious disease episode leading to an emergency room visit or outpatient visit in hospital or a period of hospitalisation, and the cumulative incidence of at least one severe infection during the first five years of life. The severe infection outcomes were additionally divided into two groups based on the known usual penicillin susceptibility of the causative pathogens found in Finland. Data on the antibiotic courses administered were retrieved from Dispensations Reimbursable Register maintained under the National Health Insurance (NHI) Scheme using ATC code J01. Data on surgical procedures were retrieved from the Care Register for Health Care using the Nordic Classification of Surgical Procedure codes, and data on the cumulative incidence of infectious disease episodes were retrieved from the Care Register for Health Care using ICD-10 codes.

Covariates

Possible confounding covariates related to pregnancy, maternal factors, birth, and perinatal factors were identified prior to analysing the data. Covariates were selected based on authors’ discussions about possible confounders and causal relations between variables. Directed acyclic graph drawn in DAGgity.net was also used to help the process (Supplementary Figure S1). Maternal and pregnancy related factors included age, parity, smoking during pregnancy, prepregnancy body mass index, gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders during pregnancy, asthma diagnosis, autoimmune disease diagnosis, neurodevelopmental disease diagnosis, educational level, and occupational status. Perinatal and birth-related factors included prolonged rupture of the membranes, birth year, sex, 5-min Apgar score, birth weight and gestational age. Data on maternal and pregnancy related covariates were retrieved from the Medical Birth Register, digital delivery records of the hospitals, the Care Register for Health Care, and Statistics Finland. Data on perinatal and birth related factors were retrieved from the Medical Birth Register and digital delivery record. Missing values were processed according to the statistical analysis plan (Statistical Analysis Plan).

Data sources

Data used here were retrieved from the Medical Birth Register, the Finnish National Infectious Diseases Register, digital delivery records of the hospitals, the Care Register for Health Care, the register of dispensations reimbursable under the National Health Insurance Scheme, and Statistics Finland. The registers and electronic medical records used are described in detail in the statistical analysis plan (Statistical Analysis Plan).

Bias

Possible sources of bias were addressed when considering the methods to be used in this research. The possibility of selection bias was low as the method was population-based, and the likelihood of both information and observer bias was reduced by the high quality of the data sources, including national registers and the electronic medical record system. To reduce any confounding bias, we adjusted the analysis by means of many covariates.

Sample size

The minimum number of patients needed was calculated separately for the outcomes of any infection episode, respiratory infection episode, and urinary tract infection episode. Estimated incidences of at least one infection episode during the first five years of life used in calculations were 30%, 15%, and 3%, respectively.17,18 To obtain a clinically significant odds ratio of 1.3 with a power set to 80%, a statistical significance threshold of 0.05 (two-sided alpha level) and a ratio of exposed to unexposed children of 1:3, it was calculated that sample sizes of 303 exposed children and 909 unexposed children were needed to analyse any infection episodes. Correspondingly, the sample size needed for respiratory infections was 751 exposed children and 2253 unexposed children and that for urinary tract infections 3566 exposed and 10,698 unexposed children.

Statistical methods

Incidence rate ratio (IRR) of infection episodes leading to hospital visits, antibiotic courses, and surgical operations was analysed using the count data. For count data we first used one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov to check if the distribution of response variable followed the Poisson distribution. If Poisson distribution was valid, log-Poisson regression was used to estimate IRR, if not the IRR was estimated using negative binomial regression with a logarithmic link. Logarithm transformed follow-up time of the children at risk was included to model as offset variable in the Poisson regression model and negative binomial regression model. The analyses were first adjusted for birth year and sex (crude analysis) and then for maternal and pregnancy related covariates (age, parity, smoking during pregnancy, prepregnancy body mass index, gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders during pregnancy, asthma diagnosis, autoimmune disease diagnosis, neurodevelopmental disease diagnosis, educational level and occupational status) and also for perinatal and birth-related covariates (prolonged rupture of the membranes, birth year, sex, 5-min Apgar score, birth weight, and gestational age) (analysis adjusted for all covariates). If model included category of the dependent variable for which one of the covariates was constant that covariate was removed from the model as stated in the result tables. We performed a sensitivity analysis in which children who received postnatal antibiotics were excluded. We also performed a secondary sensitivity analysis dividing the cohort based on the GBS screening strategy in use at the time of the birth for the primary outcome any infectious disease episode. The Cox proportional hazards model with adjustment for both maternal and pregnancy-related and perinatal and birth-related covariates was used to test differences in the cumulative incidences of infection episodes. The linearity of outcome and continuous predictors were assessed visually by plotting the Martingale residuals vs. each continuous predictor (Supplementary Figure S2). Because of violation in linearity assumption for covariate birth year a categorial covariate “GBS screening method in use at the time of birth (birth before January 1, 2013 during the risk-based GBS screening era, or birth after December 31, 2012 during the universal GBS screening era)” was used in this analysis instead. Maximum likelihood method was used to evaluate significance of parameters. We tested the proportional hazards assumption (Supplementary Figure S3). After Cox model analysis, cumulative incidence curves were produced using survival function that based on the fitted model hazard. The survival analysis began at birth of each child and ended when the child reached the age of five, the follow-up period of the study was completed, or child had an infection episode. The origin and start time of the survival analysis were the same for each child, i.e. the birth of each child The data were analysed using SPSS statistics, version 29.0 (IBM Corp, New York, USA) and Stata Statistical Software (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA).

Role of the funding source

Funders did not have a role in study design; in the collection, analysis, and interpretation of data; in the writing of the report; and in the decision to submit the paper for publication.

Results

Study population

A total of 49,299 children were delivered vaginally in Oulu University Hospital and Oulaskangas Hospital, Finland during the study period (Fig. 1). Mother's place of residence registered to Medical Birth Register was outside the geographic cohort for 889 of these children and they were excluded from the cohort. Additionally, 2832 children were excluded because of a major congenital anomaly diagnosis, and data on the timing of intrapartum antibiotic exposure were missing for three children. Analyses were performed for 9733 children exposed to intrapartum antibiotics and 35,842 children not exposed to intrapartum antibiotics, in total 45,575 children. Baseline characteristics were mainly similar in these two groups (Table 1). Annual proportion of children exposed to intrapartum antibiotics increased after the universal screening for GBS colonisation was in full use by the January 1, 2013 (Supplementary Figure S4). There were 77 (<0.1%) deaths in the cohort during the follow-up period. There were no other reasons for loss to follow-up. The number of missing covariate values were low (Table 1): there were 299 (3.1%) children exposed to intrapartum antibiotics and 1177 (3.3%) unexposed children with at least one missing value.Fig. All infections

Intrapartum antibiotic exposure was associated with an increased risk of any infection episode leading to hospitalisation or an emergency department visit (Table 2). The crude analysis pointed to a statistically significant increased risk at age 7–28 days (crude incidence rate ratio [IRR] 1.27, 95% CI (1.08−1.49), 29−365 days (IRR 1.07, 95% CI 1.00−1.14), 1−2 (IRR 1.12, 95% CI 1.05–1.20), and 2−3 years (IRR 1.14, 95% CI 1.04–1.25). The analysis adjusted for all covariates of the study, showed the increase in risk to be statistically significant at ages of 7–28 days (adjusted incidence rate ratio [aIRR] 1.30, 95% CI 1.10−1.54) and 1−2 years (aIRR 1.10, 95% CI 1.02–1.18). The cumulative incidence of at least one infection episode was significantly higher (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 1.06, 95% CI 1.01−1.10) in the children exposed to intrapartum antibiotics (Fig. 2). In sensitivity analysis excluding children who received postnatal antibiotics, a similar association remained (Supplementary Table S1). In a secondary sensitivity analysis, separating children born before and after universal GBS screening, intrapartum antibiotic exposure was statistically significantly associated with increased risk of any infectious disease in children born during the risk-based screening era at age 1–2 years (aIRR 1.13, 95% CI, 1.01–1.27) and in children born during the universal GBS screening era, at age 7–28 days (aIRR 1.38, 95% CI 1.13–1.70) (Supplementary Table S2). Respiratory infections

Intrapartum antibiotic exposure was associated with an increased risk of upper and lower respiratory infections in crude analysis adjusted for birth years and sex at ages of 7–28 days (IRR 1.25, 95% CI 1.02–1.52), 29−365 days (IRR 1.09, 95% CI 1.01–1.18), and 2−3 years (IRR 1.14, 95% CI 1.04–1.25, but only at 7−28 days (aIRR 1.26, 95% CI 1.03−1.56) when adjusting for all the covariates of the study (Table 2). Sensitivity analysis, excluding children who received postnatal antibiotics did not alter the results as statistical significance was achieved in the same age group in the adjusted analysis (Supplementary Table S1). The cumulative incidence of at least one respiratory infection episode was higher in children exposed to intrapartum antibiotics (aHR 1.05, 95% CI 0.996–1.12), but this result was not statistically significant (Fig. 2).

Urinary tract infections

Intrapartum antibiotic exposure was associated with a risk of urinary tract infections (Table 2). Both the crude analysis and the adjusted analysis pointed to a statistically significant increase in risk at ages of 7–28 days (IRR 2.23, 95% CI 1.20–4.14; aIRR 2.72, 95% CI 1.41−5.25) and 1−2 years (IRR 1.64, 95% CI 1.22–2.20; aIRR 1.70, 95% CI 1.24−2.33). The sensitivity analysis excluding children who received postnatal antibiotics produced similar results (Supplementary Table S1). The cumulative incidence of at least one urinary tract infection episode was higher in children exposed to intrapartum antibiotics (aHR 1.14, 95% CI 0.96–1.35), but statistical significance was not achieved (Fig. 2).

Gastroenteritis

Intrapartum antibiotic exposure was associated with a statistically significant increase in the risk of gastroenteritis at an age of 7–28 days (IRR 3.20, 95% CI 1.41–7.27; aIRR 4.16, 95% CI 1.73–10.03) in both the crude analysis and the adjusted analysis (Table 2). The sensitivity analysis excluding children who received postnatal antibiotics did not affect these results (Supplementary Table S1). The cumulative incidence of at least one gastroenteritis episode was higher in children exposed to intrapartum antibiotics (aHR 1.046, 95% CI 0.90–1.21), but this result was not statistically significant (Fig. 2).

Severe infections

No association was found between intrapartum antibiotic exposure and risk of severe infection during the first five years of life (aIRR 0.77, 95% CI 0.52−1.15) (Table 3) in this study. A decreased risk of severe infections caused by pathogens susceptible to penicillin was observed (aIRR 0.41, 95% CI 0.21−0.80). No association was found between intrapartum antibiotic exposure and severe infections caused by pathogens not susceptible to penicillin (aIRR 1.23, 95% CI, 0.75–2.02). Other outcomes

No association was found between intrapartum antibiotic exposure and the number of antibiotic courses received during the first five years of life (aIRR 1.01, 95% CI 0.98−1.04). No statistically significant association was found between intrapartum antibiotic exposure and the number of tympanostomies (aIRR 1.04, 95% CI 0.96−1.12) or tonsillectomies, adenotonsillectomies and adenoidectomies (aIRR 0.95, 95% CI 0.82−1.09) performed on children during the first five years of life (Table 3). Results in the sensitivity analysis excluding children exposed to postnatal antibiotics were similar (Supplementary Table S4).

Theoretical estimation of number needed to harm

We calculated theoretical estimation of number need to harm from the cumulative incidence of at least one infection episode during the first five years of life. The difference of cumulative incidence of at least one any infectious disease episode was 1.7% (38.1% in the exposed children and 36.4% in the unexposed children). This was estimated to correspond 17 additional children suffering an infectious disease episode leading to emergency room visit, outpatient hospital visit, or hospitalisation during the first five years of life per 1000 children exposed to intrapartum antibiotics.

Discussion

This register-based cohort study shows that the use of maternal intrapartum antibiotics, mostly penicillin, for preventing early-onset GBS disease, was associated with an increased long-term overall risk of infectious diseases and increased risk of urinary tract infections in the offspring. Intrapartum antibiotic exposure was not associated with an increased risk of severe infections. Furthermore, the occurrence of severe infectious diseases caused by penicillin-susceptible organisms was decreased in the children exposed to antibiotics at birth.

Our literature review revealed two previous studies to the effect of intrapartum antibiotic exposure on the long-term risk of infectious diseases in children.19,20 In a cohort of 1052 children, there was no association observed between intrapartum antibiotic exposure and the risk of childhood infectious diseases during the first year of life.19 This study was conducted in Finland, in a population similar to one used in this study, but the sample size was smaller than in this study which may explain the negative finding. In a large cohort of 173,757 Israeli children up to 18 years of age, there was no long-term association observed between intrapartum antibiotic exposure and the risk of childhood respiratory infections.20 This is in line with the results of the present study as intrapartum antibiotics had significant association to respiratory infections only during the first month of life. There are some previous studies that have found that intrapartum antibiotics change the composition of the infant gut microbiome, and we also know that the composition of the microbiome may be a risk factor for infectious diseases.7, 8, 9, 10,13, 14, 15 Such may partly explain the altered long-term risk of infectious diseases observed here in children exposed to intrapartum antibiotics. While our literature review found very limited research into the long-term effects of intrapartum antibiotics on infectious diseases, some previous studies have observed their effect on other non-communicable diseases.21, 22, 23, 24 Current evidence suggests that intrapartum antibiotics are associated with increased weight gain in childhood.21,22 On the other hand, two recent studies found no association between intrapartum antibiotics and childhood asthma or allergic disorders.23,24

The observed increase in the overall risk of infectious diseases after intrapartum antibiotic exposure appeared to be small in the present study. For instance, other individual risk factors such as maternal smoking have been found to increase the risk of infectious diseases during childhood more than intrapartum antibiotic exposure in this study.25 Because intrapartum antibiotic exposure is common in children, the observed association warrants further study into long-term risk of childhood infectious diseases in the future. For instance, the higher incidence rate ratio of urinary tract infections in children exposed to intrapartum antibiotics might be of clinical significance because early childhood febrile urinary tract infections often require hospitalisation.26 In the present study, however, it was reassuring that there was no association found between later severe infections and exposure to antibiotics at birth. Furthermore, intrapartum antibiotics appeared to be associated with a long-term protection against severe infections caused by pathogens susceptible to penicillin. Protection from penicillin susceptible pathogens, other than GBS, caused by intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis has previously been suggested as the incidence of neonatal listeriosis was found to have decreased in the era of widespread penicillin prophylaxis.27 We were not able to investigate separately short-term, during the first three months of life, and long-term effects of intrapartum antibiotic exposure in this study because, the number of severe infections in this cohort was too low for this.

Children delivered by CS were excluded from this cohort. CS itself has been found to be associated with an increased risk of childhood hospitalisation for infection, so that CS could have acted as confounding factor distorting the results.28 International guidelines also currently recommend the routine administration of antibiotics to every woman undergoing CS regardless of the GBS status.29 Because of this it would have been difficult to form a large enough control group of children whose mothers had not received antibiotics during CS to enable us to perform a separate analysis for those delivered by CS.

The main strengths of this study are the large population-based cohort and the good quality of the national registers as sources of data, both of which factors increase the reliability of the results. We were able to link accurate data on the use of antibiotics during birth, as obtained from the digital delivery records, with the national register data. This was a hypothesis driven study, as we knew from our previous studies of children living in the same area that intrapartum antibiotics change the composition of the microbiome, and that the microbiome composition is associated with a risk of urinary tract infections.12,14,30, 31, 32 We report here separately on the association of intrapartum antibiotics with severe infections in order to facilitate the interpretation of the clinical significance of this phenomenon.

The main limitation is the observational study design, which does not allow us to draw conclusions regarding causality in the associations that were found. The population-based cohort increases the generalisability of the present study. The cohort was, however, born in a high-income country and represents mostly children of Finnish ethnicity. We report adjusted hazard ratios when comparing the cumulative incidence of infectious diseases during the first five years of life, which can be misleading because the hazard ratios can be different at different points of follow-up.33 Hazard ratios also have a built-in selection bias as they only compare the children who survived without an infection episode during the later stages of follow-up.33 Unmeasured confounding remains as a methodological limitation as we might not have recognised all confounding variables and covariate data in the registers may have errors. For example, it is possible that pregnant women lie about smoking during pregnancy. It is possible that healthcare seeking behaviour affects the probability of pregnant women accepting intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis, however, this anthropological bias is likely low in this study as over 99.5% of pregnant women in Finland take part in the free maternity health clinic services and almost all women with GBS colonisation receive intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (Statistical analysis plan).34 In our hypothesis we assumed that the exposure to the intrapartum antibiotics would be the factor causing changes to the risk of infectious diseases in children, however, we cannot exclude the possibility of maternal GBS carriage having an impact on the risk of infectious diseases in the offspring regardless of intrapartum antibiotic exposure. We did not add GBS colonisation as a covariate because of the collinearity of these variables. Furthermore, there may be complex associations between the maternal vaginal microbiome, maternal immunity, and the risk of GBS carriage which were not in the scope of this epidemiological study.35

In conclusion, we found that exposure to maternal intrapartum antibiotics was associated with an overall increase in the occurrence of infectious diseases in the offspring. This increase appeared to be small, however, and there was no increase in severe infections. Changes in early colonisation of the microbiome or modifications of immune system could be possible explanations for the found associations and should be further studied.
