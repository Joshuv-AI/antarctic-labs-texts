The CLT for lamplighter groups with an acylindrically hyperbolic base
Maksym Chaudkhari, Christian Gorski, Eduardo Silva

Abstract
. We prove a Central Limit Theorem for the drift of a non-elementary random walk with a finite exponential moment on a wreath product A ≀ H ≔ ⨁ H A ⋊ H A\wr H\coloneqq\bigoplus_{H}A\rtimes H with A A a non-trivial finite group and H H a finitely generated acylindrically hyperbolic group. We also provide the upper bounds on the central moments of the drift. Furthermore, our results extend to the case where A A is an arbitrary (possibly infinite) finitely generated group.



1.
Introduction

The classical Central Limit Theorem (CLT) states that if
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a collection of i.i.d. random variables with
\ell\coloneqq\mathbb{E}(X_{1})<\infty
and
\sigma^{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{V}(X_{1})<\infty
, then the sequence

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\ell n}{\sigma\sqrt{n}},n\geq 1,

converges in distribution to a standard Gaussian
[
Lin22
]
(see also
[
Fel68
, Chapter X.1]
).
Extensions of this result have been studied intensely in the context of random walks on groups, a class of countable state Markov chains satisfying space-homogeneity.

More precisely, let
G
be a finitely generated group endowed with a left-invariant metric
d
. Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
G
that is
non-degenerate
: the support of
\mu
,
\text{supp}(\mu)
, generates
G
as a semigroup. The
\mu
-random walk on
G
is the sequence of random variables
Z_{n}=g_{1}\cdots g_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, where
(g_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. distributed according to
\mu
. If
\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left[d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{1})\right]<\infty
, then by Kingman’s subadditive ergodic theorem,
[
Kin68
]
, we have a well-defined
asymptotic drift

\ell\geq 0
, satisfying

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})}{n}=\ell,

almost surely and in
L^{1}
. One should compare this to the law of large numbers.

The next natural line of investigation is with respect to the fluctuations of
d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})
around its mean. In the case
G=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, this is addressed by the classical CLT. For other groups, however, one can observe a wide array of exotic behaviors for these fluctuations, which in general will strongly depend on the geometric properties of the group
G
.

There are finitely generated groups for which the normalized distances
d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})/\sqrt{n}
converge to a non-degenerate, non-Gaussian distribution (
[
EZ22
, Proposition 6.2]
), or for which it is not possible to normalize the differences
d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})-\ell n
,
n\geq 1
, to obtain a convergence in law to a non-trivial distribution (see the remark after Theorem 8 in
[
Bjö10
]
).

Currently, all non-trivial examples of finitely generated groups known to satisfy a CLT for the distance to the identity with a Gaussian (or a combination of folded Gaussian) limiting distribution are either finite extensions of abelian groups, which is close to the classical setting, or groups admitting sufficiently strong negative curvature properties, such as a proper action on a hyperbolic space.

By non-trivial we mean the groups that satisfy the CLT for a sufficiently large class of random walks and the CLT for them is not an immediate corollary of the CLT for other known examples.
Notably, this class of groups includes non-abelian free groups
[
SS87
,
Led01
]
, hyperbolic groups
[
Bjö10
,
BQ16a
]
, mapping class groups and
\mathrm{Out}(F_{N})

[
Hor18
]
. A more comprehensive list of groups that satisfy a CLT is given in Subsection
1.1
.

The main goal of the present paper is to establish the CLT for the distance to the identity in a new class of groups, namely wreath products of the form
A\wr H\coloneqq\bigoplus_{H}A\rtimes H
, where
\bigoplus_{H}A
denotes the direct sum of isomorphic copies of a given finitely generated group
A
indexed by a finitely generated acylindrically hyperbolic group
H
. The definition of the class of acylindrically hyperbolic groups is recalled in Definition
2.5
, and it contains, in particular, free groups, discrete subgroups of
\text{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
, and more generally all hyperbolic groups. The action of
H
on
\bigoplus_{H}A
by conjugation within
A\wr H
corresponds to translation of the indices of the direct sum (see Subsection
2.4
). In the crucially important special case of a finite group
A
, the wreath product is also called a
lamplighter group
. This name is related to a classical interpretation, where the elements of
\bigoplus_{H}A
are viewed as lamp configurations on
H
, while the
H
-component of each element of
A\wr H
describes the position of a lamplighter who is changing states of individual lamps.
To simplify the exposition, we will work only with the lamplighter groups for the rest of the article until the last Subsection
5.1
.
We call a metric on a lamplighter group
A\wr H

standard
if it is the word metric associated with the generating set composed of a disjoint union of
A
and a finite generating set of
H
(see Subsection
2.4.1
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
A
be a non-trivial finite group and let
H
be a finitely generated acylindrically hyperbolic group. Let
d
be a standard metric on
A\wr H
and let
\mu
be a symmetric probability measure on
A\wr H
with a finite exponential moment. Suppose that the projection of
\mu
to
H
is a generating measure, or more generally, a non-elementary measure on
H
. Denote by
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 0}
the
\mu
-random walk on
A\wr H
and by
\ell
its asymptotic drift. Then there exists
\sigma>0
such that the sequence of random variables

\left\{\frac{d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})-\ell n}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\right\}_{n\geq 1}

converges in distribution to a standard Gaussian. In addition, for any
p>1
there exists a constant
C=C(p,d,\mu)>0
such that

\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left|d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})-\mathbb{E}^{\mu}d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})\right|^{p}\leq Cn^{\frac{p}{2}}(\log n)^{5p}\text{ for all }n\geq 1.

Although
H
is acylindrically hyperbolic and hence can be studied via its actions on hyperbolic spaces, the standard metric on
A\wr H
behaves very differently from any hyperbolic metric. This can be seen from various geometric and combinatorial properties of the standard word metric on wreath products; we now describe an illustrative (though by no means exhaustive) list of examples.

(1)

The
divergence function
of a one-ended group is a quasi-isometry invariant that measures the minimal length of paths between two points avoiding a given ball in the Cayley graph. One-ended acylindrically hyperbolic groups always have superlinear divergence
[
Sis16
, Theorem 2 and Proposition 4.1]
(see also the discussion on p. 2503 of
[
GS18
]
), whereas wreath products always have linear divergence
[
Iss23
, Theorem 1]
.

(2)

In a wreath product, for every arbitrarily large radius, there exists an element that maximizes the distance to the identity within the ball of that radius around it (such elements are called
dead ends of arbitrarily large depth
)
[
Sil23
, Theorem A]
. These elements can be interpreted as regions of the group that exhibit positive curvature (see, e.g.,
[
KM20
, Theorem 2.7]
and
[
BNDK22
]
). This property is not satisfied by any word metric on a hyperbolic group
[
Bog97
]
.

(3)

In
[
Par92
]
it is shown that the growth series of balls in groups of the form
A\wr F_{k}
, where
A
is finite and non-trivial, and
F_{k}
is a free group of rank
k\geq 2
is not a rational function. This contrasts with the case of hyperbolic groups, where the growth series is always rational
[
ECH+92
, Theorem 3.4.5]
(see also
[
Can84
]
).

Point (
1
) is key here. In proofs of the CLT for random walks on groups with hyperbolic properties, one uses divergence of the group to argue that the geodesic connecting
\mathsf{id}_{G}
to
Z_{2n}
must pass through a controlled neighbourhood of
Z_{n}
with good probability, from which one can approximate
d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{2n})
by a sum of independent random variables. This is the perspective taken in
[
CCHR25
]
. As wreath products have linear divergence, this ingredient is missing, so one must use deeper properties of the word metric.

As we explain in Subsection
2.4.1
, the values of the standard word metric on
A\wr H
can be expressed in terms of solutions of the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) on the Cayley graph of
H
. Namely, the word length of an element can be expressed as the minimal length of a path on the Cayley graph of
H
that starts at the identity
\mathsf{id}_{H}
, visits all positions where there are lamps lit, and which finishes its journey in some determined position of
H
.

In the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we leverage the geodesic tracking results of Mathieu and Sisto
[
MS20
]
to bound the defect of the
\mathrm{TSP}
along a trajectory of a random walk in
H
. In our setting, this defect roughly quantifies the discrepancy between the sum of optimal solutions to two TSP problems versus the optimal solution to their concatenation. The importance of this quantity is explained by the connection between the standard metric on the Cayley graph of
A\wr H
with the
\mathrm{TSP}
in the Cayley graph of
H
, outlined in Subsection
2.4.1
. Here, the locations which must be visited in the TSPs are (roughly) the lamps which have been lit during the course of the random walk over different time intervals—times in
[0,m]
,
[m,n+m]
, and
[0,n+m]
respectively. Since random walks on acylindrically hyperbolic groups track geodesics, all lamps lit during a given time interval lie, with high probability, close to a geodesic from the origin to the lamplighter’s final position in
H
. Since the random walk has linear progress, the lamps lit from times
[0,m]
and from times
[m,n+m]
do not have too much spatial overlap with high probability. A deterministic argument then allows us to quantitatively bound the defect in terms of the distance to the geodesic and the overlap between segments. The defect bounds we obtain are not uniform in
m,n
as they are in
[
MS20
]
, but they grow slowly in
m,n
, and a slight modification of the argument in
[
MS20
]
gives the same conclusion. The results of Mathieu and Sisto, along with the variants relevant to our setting, are recalled in Section
3
. These are then combined, in Section
5
, with deterministic combinatorial estimates for the TSP established in Section
4
, to prove Theorem
1.1
. Moreover, with some additional technical modifications, our argument allows us to obtain the CLT for the random walks on the more general wreath products
A\wr H
where
A
is an arbitrary finitely generated group. We explain these modifications in the Subsection
5.1
, see Corollary
5.6
.

1.1.
Background

The CLT for the distance to the identity on non-abelian free groups is due to Sawyer and Steger
[
SS87
]
and Ledrappier
[
Led01
]
for finitely supported step distributions. This was later extended to non-elementary hyperbolic groups by Björklund
[
Bjö10
]
for the Green metric under a finite exponential moment assumption, and subsequently by Benoist and Quint
[
BQ16a
]
to general word metrics under a finite second moment assumption. Mathieu and Sisto
[
MS20
]
further extended these results to a larger class of groups, including all acylindrically hyperbolic groups. Additional classes of groups that satisfy a version of the CLT are linear groups by Benoist and Quint
[
BQ16b
]
, mapping class groups and
\mathrm{Out}(F_{n})
by Horbez
[
Hor18
]
, groups that act on
\mathrm{CAT}(0)
-spaces with contracting isometries by Le Bars
[
Bar22
]
, groups with contracting elements by Choi
[
Cho23
]
, groups that contain superlinear divergent geodesics by Chawla, Choi, He and Rafi
[
CCHR25
]
, and groups of affine transformations of a horospherical product of hyperbolic spaces by Bahmanian, Forghani, Gekhtman and Mallahi-Karai
[
BFGMK25
]
.

Wreath products are a classical construction in group theory that have provided many examples of interesting probabilistic phenomena in random walks on groups. One of the first such examples is due to Kaimanovich and Vershik
[
KV83
]
, who showed that the lamplighter group
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
has exponential volume growth but that every random walk on it with a symmetric, finitely supported step distribution has zero asymptotic drift. Before their work, it was known that positive asymptotic drift implies exponential growth, but the converse was open. In contrast, they proved that any non-degenerate symmetric random walk with finitely supported steps on
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for
d\geq 3
has a non-trivial Poisson boundary, thus providing the first examples of an amenable group with this property. Varopoulos used the wreath product
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
to provide the first example of a group for which the return probability to the origin of a simple random walk decays asymptotically as
\mathrm{exp}(-n^{1/3})

[
Var83
,
Var84
]
. This property was further studied by Pittet and Saloff-Coste
[
PSC02
]
, who showed that for any fixed
0<\alpha<1
there are wreath products of the form
G\wr\mathbb{Z}
for which the return probability to the origin of a simple random walk decays asymptotically faster than
\mathrm{exp}(-n^{\alpha})
. Erschler studied the asymptotic growth of the expected distance to the identity on such wreath products
G\wr\mathbb{Z}
, showing in particular, that the drift of a random walk can grow at an intermediate rate between
\sqrt{n}
and
n

[
Èrs01b
,
Èrs01a
]
, answering a question of Vershik.

More recently, Erschler and Zheng studied random walks on
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with a finite
(2+\varepsilon)
-moment for some
\varepsilon>0
, and proved that
d(\mathsf{id},Z_{n})
, normalized by its expectation, converges almost surely to a constant
[
EZ22
, Theorem 1.1]
. They also proved in
[
EZ22
, Proposition 6.2]
that for any non-trivial finite group
A
, the drift of a simple random walk on
A\wr\mathbb{Z}
satisfies the following version of a central limit theorem: there are constants
\sigma,c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that the sequence of random variables
d(\mathsf{id},Z_{n})/(\sigma\sqrt{n})
converges in law to the distribution
c_{1}(\mathcal{R}_{1}-|B_{1}|)+c_{2}|B_{1}|
, where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion and
\mathcal{R}_{t}
denotes the size of its range at time
t
. Regarding other limit laws, Revelle established a law of the iterated logarithm on certain wreath products
G\wr\mathbb{Z}

[
Rev03
]
. Our main result (Theorem
1.1
) is the first to establish a CLT for random walks on wreath products whose base group is not
\mathbb{Z}
.

One cannot expect a version of the CLT to hold for random walks on every finitely generated group. Indeed, as Björklund remarked in the paragraph following Theorem 8 in
[
Bjö10
]
, the distance to the identity in a simple random walk on
(\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z})\times F_{2}
exhibits fluctuations of order
n^{1/2}
in directions governed by the
F_{2}
factor and of order
n^{3/4}
in those governed by the
\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
factor. Moreover, Erschler and Zheng constructed finitely generated groups
G
for which
d(\mathsf{id},Z_{n})
, normalized by its expectation, converges in law to different limit distributions along distinct subsequences
[
EZ22
, Proposition 6.6]
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Metric spaces and paths

In this article, we will always assume that we work with a proper geodesic metric space
(X,d)
.
A path
\gamma\colon[a,b]\rightarrow X
is a continuous map from the segment
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
to
X
. The path
\gamma
is endowed with the natural orientation and we refer to
\gamma(a)
as a starting point and
\gamma(b)
as the ending point of
\gamma
.
If
\gamma
consists of a single point, this point is considered to be both the ending point and the starting point of
\gamma
.

Definition 2.1
.

A path
\gamma\colon[a,b]\rightarrow X
is called a geodesic segment connecting
\gamma(a)
and
\gamma(b)
if it is an isometric embedding of
[a,b]
into
X
. The length of a geodesic segment will be denoted by
|\gamma|=d(\gamma(a),\gamma(b))=|b-a|
.

Let us also recall the notion of piecewise geodesic and its length.

Definition 2.2
.

We call a path
\alpha\colon[a,b]\rightarrow X
a piecewise geodesic if it can be represented as the concatenation of finitely many geodesic segments. If
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k}
is the list of these segments, then the length of
\alpha
is defined as
|\alpha|=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\gamma_{i}|
.

It is easy to see that the length of the piecewise geodesic does not depend on the choice of representation.

To simplify the notation, we will extend the length notation to lists of geodesic segments.
For any two points
P,Q\in X
we will denote by
PQ
a geodesic segment joining them, and the length of this segment will be denoted by
|PQ|=d(P,Q)
. Moreover, for any finite list of geodesic segments
\theta
, we will denote by
|\theta|
its total length computed as the sum of the lengths of its entries. Notice that if the list has repetitions, we also repeat the corresponding lengths in the sum.

Finally, we will also use the the following definition of a projection.

Definition 2.3
.

For a point
P\in X
and geodesic segment
\gamma
we will denote by
\pi_{\gamma}(P)
the set of all points on
\gamma
that minimize the distance to
P
.

2.2.
The Traveling Salesman Problem.

We will work with a version of the Traveling Salesman Problem with fixed starting and ending points.

Definition 2.4
.

For the points
A,B\in X
and a finite set of points
L\subset X
, the
\mathrm{TSP}(A,L,B)
denotes the length of the shortest piecewise geodesic path in
X
which starts at
A
, ends at
B
, and visits every point in
L
. We will call a solution of
\mathrm{TSP}(A,L,B)
any path
\alpha
of minimal length that satisfies these conditions.

It is easy to see, that to find a solution of the
\mathrm{TSP}(A,L,B)
we only need to consider piecewise geodesic paths
\alpha
constructed by connecting the points from
L\cup\{A,B\}
by geodesic segments.

Whenever
\alpha
is a path of this form, we can list the points of
L=\{l_{1},\ldots,l_{k}\}
in the order of their first appearance along
\alpha
,
L=(l_{\pi(1)},\ldots,l_{\pi(k)})
. Then the length of
\alpha
, denoted by
|\alpha|
, is computed as

|\alpha|=d(A,l_{\pi(1)})+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}d(l_{\pi(i)},l_{\pi(i+1)})+d(l_{\pi(k)},B).

Notice that
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}(k)
determines
\alpha
uniquely up to the choice of the geodesic segments connecting
A
with
l_{\pi(1)}
,
B
with
l_{\pi(k)}
, and
l_{\pi(i)}
with
l_{\pi(i+1)}
for
i=1,\ldots,k-1
. Therefore, solving the TSP is equivalent to finding a permutation that minimizes the sum above. In particular, the shortest path always exists in this setting.

For any solution
\alpha
of
\mathrm{TSP}(A,L,B)
, we will refer to the points in
\{A,B\}\cup L
as the
nodes
of
\alpha
.

2.3.
Acylindrically hyperbolic groups

We recall the definition and notable examples of the acylindrically hyperbolic groups, and we refer the reader to
[
Osi16
]
for a detailed treatment of the subject.

Recall that a geodesic metric space
(X,d)
is called
hyperbolic
, if there exists
\delta>0
such that for every geodesic triangle
T
in
X
,
every side of
T
is contained in the
\delta
-neighborhood of the union of the other two sides.

Let
G
be a group that acts on a metric space
(X,d)
by isometries. This action is called
acylindrical
if for every
r\geq 0
, there exist
N,\,R>0
such that whenever
x,y\in X
satisfy
d(x,y)>R
, there are at most
N
elements
g\in G
that satisfy both inequalities
d(x,gx)<r
and
d(y,gy)<r
. The action is called
non-elementary
if
G
is not virtually cyclic and the
G
-orbits are unbounded.

Definition 2.5
.

A group
G
is called acylindrically hyperbolic if it admits a non-elementary acylindrical action on a hyperbolic geodesic metric space
(X,d)
by isometries.

Examples of acylindrically hyperbolic groups include all non-elementary hyperbolic groups, all non-elementary relatively hyperbolic groups,
Out(F_{n}),n\geq 2
, the mapping class groups
MCG(\Sigma_{g,p})
of connected oriented surfaces of genus
g\geq 0
with
p\geq 0
punctures, except for the case when
g=0
and
p\leq 3
, and many fundamental groups of compact
3
-manifolds (see
[
MO15
]
).

The main reason why we consider the base group
H
to be acylindrically hyperbolic in the statement of Theorem
1.1
is to be able to use Proposition
5.2
in Section
5
, which gives quantitative estimates on how a random walk on an acylindrically hyperbolic group tracks geodesic segments.

2.4.
Wreath products

We consider the restricted wreath product
G=A\wr H
, where
A
is a non-trivial finite group and
H
is a finitely generated group. More explicitly,
G=\bigoplus_{H}A\rtimes H
, where the direct sum
\bigoplus_{H}A
is viewed as the group of functions
f:H\rightarrow A
with finite support, and
H
acts on
\bigoplus_{H}A
by left translation, so for all
h,x\in H
and all
f\in\bigoplus_{H}A
, we have
h.f(x)=f(h^{-1}x).

If
g_{1}=(f_{1},h_{1})
and
g_{2}=(f_{2},h_{2})
are two elements of
G=A\wr H
, their product
g_{1}g_{2}
can written explicitly as
g_{1}g_{2}=(f_{1}\cdot(h_{1}.f_{2}),h_{1}\cdot h_{2})
.

2.4.1.
The standard word metric on wreath products

Let
S_{H}
be a finite symmetric generating set of
H
.
The standard generating set
S
of
G=A\wr H
associated with
S_{H}
is given by

S\coloneqq\Big\{(\delta_{a},\mathsf{id}_{H}),\,(\mathbf{0},s)\Big|a\in A,\;\text{and}\;s\in S_{H}\Big\},

where
\delta_{a}(x)=a
iff
x=\mathsf{id}_{H}
, and otherwise
\delta_{a}(x)=\mathsf{id}_{A}
, and
\mathbf{0}(x)=\mathsf{id}_{A}
for any
x\in H
.

It is well-known that the word length of any
g=(f,h)\in G
with respect to
S
can be computed as follows:

|g|_{\mathrm{S}}=\mathrm{TSP}(\mathsf{id}_{H},\mathrm{supp}(f),h)+|\mathrm{supp}(f)|,

where the
\mathrm{TSP}
is solved with respect to the distance
d_{H}
on the Cayley graph of
H
induced by the word metric corresponding to
S_{H}
, see, e.g.,
[
Par92
, Theorem 1.2]
.

2.5.
Random walks on groups

Let
G
be a countable group and consider a probability measure
\mu
on
G
. Consider the product space
\Omega\coloneqq G^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
endowed with the product
\sigma
-field. For each
n\geq 1
we denote by

\displaystyle X_{n}:\Omega

\displaystyle\to G

\displaystyle w\coloneqq(w_{1},w_{2},\cdots)

\displaystyle\mapsto X_{n}(w)\coloneqq w_{n}

the
n
-th coordinate map. We endow
\Omega
with the product probability measure
\mu^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}.

We denote by

\displaystyle\theta:\Omega

\displaystyle\to\Omega

\displaystyle w\coloneqq(w_{1},w_{2},\cdots)

\displaystyle\mapsto\theta(w)\coloneqq(w_{2},w_{3},\ldots)

the shift map in the space of increments.

Now we define the
\mu
-random walk
\{Z_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
on
G
as follows. We define
Z_{0}(w)=\mathsf{id}_{G}
for each
w\in\Omega
, and for each
n\geq 1
we set

Z_{n}(w)\coloneqq Z_{n-1}(w)\cdot X_{n}(w).

We remark that

Of course, the inequalities in Claim

5.
Bounds on the moments of the defect and the CLT

In this section we apply Lemma
4.1
to obtain upper bounds on the moments of the defective adapted cocycles when the base group
H
is acylindrically hyperbolic.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
G=A\wr H
be a lamplighter group with a finitely generated acylindrically hyperbolic base group
H
. Let
d
be a standard metric on
G
. Assume that the probability measure
\mu
on
G
has finite exponential moment, and its projection onto
H
,
\mu_{H}
is a non-elementary measure. Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the corresponding length defective adapted cocycle and
\Psi_{m,n},\,m,n\geq 1,
be its defects. Then, for every
p\geq 1
, there exists a constant
K_{p}(\mu,d)
such that for any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
the following inequality holds:

\mathbb{E}^{\mu}|\Psi_{m,n}|^{p}\leq K_{p}\log(n+m)^{5p}.

Moreover, if we assume that
\mu
has finite support, then the right-hand side of the inequality could be strengthened to

\mathbb{E}^{\mu}|\Psi_{m,n}|^{p}\leq K_{p}\log(n+m)^{2p}.

We will use the following two propositions in our proof. These results will guarantee logarithmic growth of the deviation from the geodesic and linear progress of the trajectory of the random walk projected to
H
. We note that these propositions are the only parts of our arguments that require
H
to be an acylindrical hyperbolic group.
The first proposition is a straightforward corollary of Theorem 9.1 and Theorem 10.7 in
[
MS20
]
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
H
be a finitely generated acylindrically hyperbolic group and let
\mu_{H}
be a symmetric non-elementary probability measure on
H
with finite exponential moment. Choose an arbitrary finite symmetric generating set of
H
and let
d_{H}
be the corresponding word metric on
H
. Finally, we denote by
\overline{Z}_{n}
the position of the random walk driven by
\mu_{H}
at time

. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

There exists a constant
K
such that for any
n\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mu_{H}}\left(d_{H}(\overline{Z}_{n},e_{H})\leq n/K\right)\leq Ke^{-n/K}

(2)

For any
p\geq 1
, there is a constant
C_{1}
such that for any
n\geq 1
and any geodesic segment
\alpha
joining
\mathsf{id}
and
Z_{n}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mu_{H}}\left(\max_{0\leq k\leq n}d_{H}(\overline{Z}_{k},\alpha)\geq C_{1}\log n\right)\leq C_{1}/n^{2p}

Proof.

The first statement immediately follows from Theorem 9.1, Remark 10.2 and statement 1 in Proposition 10.3 in
[
MS20
]
. The second statement follows from Theorem 10.7 and Remark 10.2 in
[
MS20
]
.
∎

We note that if
\mu_{H}
-random walk

on

in Proposition

is a projection of a
\mu

, the inequalities in Proposition

hold as inequalities with respect to

. We will keep this convention for the remainder of the article.

Proposition 5.3
.

Under the same hypotheses as Proposition

,
for any
p\geq 1
, there is a constant

such that with for
any

, with probability at least

the following holds. For all

∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

We will divide the proof into three steps. In Step
1
we will establish some a priori bounds on the moments of the defect
\Psi_{m,n}
and deal with the case where either
m
or
n
is sufficiently small relative to their sum. In the second step, we will prove the desired upper bound for the general case of the measure with finite exponential moment under the assumption that
m
and
n
are sufficiently large. Finally, in Step
3
we will briefly explain how to modify the argument from Step
2
to decrease the right hand side if we assume that
\mu
has finite support.

For
p\geq 1
denote by
\kappa_{p}(\mu,d)
the
p
-th moment of measure
\mu
with respect to distance
d
:

\kappa_{p}(\mu,d)=\mathbb{E}^{\mu}d(\mathsf{id},Z_{1})^{p}.

Step 1

Notice that we have the following elementary upper bounds:

\displaystyle|\Psi_{m,n}|=|d(\mathsf{id},Z_{m})+d(Z_{m},Z_{m+n})-d(\mathsf{id},Z_{m+n})|

\displaystyle\leq d(\mathsf{id},Z_{m})+|d(Z_{m},Z_{m+n})-d(\mathsf{id},Z_{m+n})|\leq 2d(\mathsf{id},Z_{m})

and

\displaystyle|\Psi_{m,n}|=|d(\mathsf{id},Z_{m})+d(Z_{m},Z_{m+n})-d(\mathsf{id},Z_{m+n})|

\displaystyle\leq d(Z_{m},Z_{m+n})+|d(\mathsf{id},Z_{m})-d(\mathsf{id},Z_{m+n})|\leq 2d(Z_{m},Z_{m+n})

Therefore, we can derive the following upper bound for any
p\geq 1
:

\displaystyle|\Psi_{m,n}|^{p}\leq 2^{p}d(\mathsf{id},Z_{m})^{p}\leq 2^{p}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}d(Z_{k},Z_{k+1})\Big)^{p}\leq 2^{p}m^{p-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}d(Z_{k},Z_{k+1})^{p}.

Since
d(Z_{k},Z_{k+1})
are i.i.d. with the same distribution as
d(Z_{0},Z_{1})=d(\mathsf{id},Z_{1})
, taking the expectation, we get

\mathbb{E}^{\mu}|\Psi_{m,n}|^{p}\leq 2^{p}m^{p}\kappa_{p}(\mu,d).

The inequality
\mathbb{E}^{\mu}|\Psi_{m,n}|^{p}\leq 2^{p}n^{p}\kappa_{p}(\mu,d)
can be proved in the same way.

Therefore, if
\min{(m,n)}\leq M\log(m+n)^{2}
for some fixed
M>0
, we get the inequality

\mathbb{E}^{\mu}|\Psi_{m,n}|^{p}\leq 2^{p}M^{p}\log{(m+n)}^{2p}\kappa_{p}(\mu,d),

which shows that under the assumption
\min{(m,n)}\leq M\log(m+n)^{2}
the moment
\mathbb{E}^{\mu}|\Psi_{m,n}|^{p}
grows no faster than a polynomial of degree
2p
in
\log(m+n)
.

Step 2

From now on, we will assume that
\min{(m,n)}\geq M\log(m+n)^{2}
for some large constant
M
that depends only on
\mu
and
d
and will be defined precisely later.

Since
|\Psi_{m,n}|
has all moments, we can limit the expectation of
|\Psi_{m,n}|^{p}
over the event
\{|\Psi_{m,n}|\geq(n+m)^{2}\}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\big[|\Psi_{m,n}|^{p}:|\Psi_{m,n}|\geq(n+m)^{2}\big]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\mu}|\Psi_{m,n}|^{2p}}{(n+m)^{2p}}\leq\frac{2^{2p}(m+n)^{2p}\kappa_{2p}(\mu,d)}{(n+m)^{2p}}=2^{2p}\kappa_{2p}(\mu,d).

Hence, the expectation over this set has a constant upper bound that does not depend on
m,n
.

Therefore, in our subsequent estimates it suffices to bound the expectation of
|\Psi_{m,n}|
on the event
S_{m,n}=\{|\Psi_{m,n}|\leq(n+m)^{2}\}
.

Let us fix some
p\geq 1
. Notice that for any event
A
with
\mathbb{P}^{\mu}(A)\leq\frac{M_{1}}{(m+n)^{2p}}
for some constant
M_{1}>0
, we have
\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\big[|\Psi_{m,n}|^{p}:A\cap S_{m,n}\big]\leq M_{1}
, so it suffices to bound the expectation
\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\big[|\Psi_{m,n}|^{2}:A^{C}\cap S_{m,n}\big]
taken over the intersection fo
S_{m,n}
with the complement
A^{c}
.

Our goal is to show that the trajectory of the random walk on
G
up to time

projected onto

will satisfy the conditions of Lemma
4.1
with
N
and
D
bounded above by polynomials of
\log(m+n)
of low degree with probability at least
1-\frac{M_{2}}{(m+n)^{2p}}
for some constant
M_{2}>0
. To simplify the notation, we will say that an event
A\subseteq\Omega
that depends on
m
and

and is measurable with respect to
\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m+n})
holds with
overwhelming probability
if
\mathbb{P}^{\mu}(A)\geq 1-\frac{M_{3}}{(m+n)^{2p}}

.

We will show that, with overwhelming probability, the lamps switched on at any given time

in the Cayley graph of

.
Since measure

e(\mu,d)=\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\exp(\alpha d(\mathsf{id},X_{1}))<\infty

Therefore, by the Chebyshev inequality, there exists a constant

\mathbb{P}^{\mu}\left(d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{1})>C_{0}\log(m+n)\right)\leq\frac{1}{(m+n)^{2p+1}}.

|\Psi_{m,n}|\leq 24(N+1)D+2N.

Next, we will establish upper bounds on

\overline{Z}_{k}

C_{0}\log(m+n)
-neighborhood of
\mathcal{M}
is bounded above by
K_{0}(12D+2C_{0}\log(m+n))+1
. Since every lamp in
\mathcal{M}
is within
C_{0}\log(m+n)
distance from the corresponding position
\overline{Z}_{k}
, and each position corresponds to at most
C_{0}\log(m+n)
lamps, we conclude that

N\leq(K_{0}(12D+2C_{0}\log(m+n))+1)C_{0}\log(m+n).

Therefore, we see that
N
is bounded above by a polynomial of
\log(m+n)
of the degree at most
3
, and as a result,
24(N+1)D+2N
may be bounded above by
C_{2}\log(m+n)^{5}
for some constant
C_{2}\geq 1
that may be explicitly expressed as a polynomial depending only on the constants
K_{0},C_{0},C_{1}
defined above.

As a result, in the case when
\min(m,n)\geq M\log(m+n)^{2}
, we obtain the inequality

\mathbb{E}|\Psi_{m,n}|^{p}\leq C_{2}^{p}\log(n+m)^{5p}+M_{2},

for some constant
M_{2}>0
which accounts for the expectation over the events of small probability described above (these are the events where the trajectory
\overline{Z}_{0},\ldots,\overline{Z}_{m+n}
behaves irregularly).

Combining this with the results obtained in Step
1
, we see that there exists a constant
K_{p}>0
such that for all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\mathbb{E}|\Psi_{m,n}|^{p}\leq K_{p}\log(n+m)^{5p}.

Step 3.

If we assume that
\mu
has finite support, then we can run the same argument as in Step
2
, but in this case we may assume that for some constant
C_{0}\geq 1
, for every
k=1,\ldots,m+n,
the lamps switched at time
k
are within the distance
C_{0}
from the position
\overline{Z}_{k-1}
in the Cayley graph of
H
and there are at most
C_{0}
such lamps. Moreover, we may also assume that
d_{H}(\overline{Z}_{k-1},\overline{Z}_{k})\leq C_{0}
for every
k=1,\ldots,m+n
. Therefore, in our estimate of
D
, the upper bound on the distance between consecutive positions
\overline{Z}_{k}
and
\overline{Z}_{k+1}
will be constant, and the upper bound on
D
will be linear in
\log(m+n)
. Moreover, the upper bound on
N
will be the product of a linear function of
\log(m+n)
with the largest possible number of active lamps in the element from
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
, so this time it will also be linear in
\log(m+n).

As a result, the upper bounds obtained in both previous steps would not exceed
K_{2}\log(m+n)^{2p}
for a suitable constant
K_{2}\geq 1
, which completes the proof of the inequality.
∎

Now we are ready to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 5.4

(see Theorem 1.1)
.

Let
H
be a finitely generated acylindrically hyperbolic group and let
A
be any nontrivial finite group. Assume that
G=A\wr H
is equipped with a standard metric
d
, and
\mu
is a symmetric probability measure on
G
with finite exponential moment such that its projection onto
H
is a non-elementary measure. Then there exists
\sigma>0
such that

\frac{d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})-\ell(\mu,d)n}{\sqrt{n}}

converges to
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
in distribution.

Moreover, for each
p\geq 1
, there exists a constant
C
that depends only on
p
and
\mu
, such that for all
n>1
,

\mathbb{E}^{\mu}|d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})-\mathbb{E}^{\mu}d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})|^{p}\leq Cn^{\frac{p}{2}}(\log n)^{5p}.

Proof.

The CLT for the drift follows from Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
5.1
applied to
p=2
. The fact that
\sigma>0
follows from
[
MS20
, Theorem 4.12]
. We note that this the only part of the proof that uses symmetry of
\mu
, and it can be replaced by weaker assumptions, see the statement of
[
MS20
, Theorem 4.12]
.

Finally, the upper bounds on the moments of
|d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})-\mathbb{E}^{\mu}d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})|
follow from Theorem
5.1
and Theorem
3.5
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

The last claim in Theorem

implies that in the case when

can be improved to

5.1.
The case of a finitely generated group of lamps

In this subsection we describe the modifications to the proof of Theorem
5.1
that lead to the proof of the CLT in the case where
A
is an arbitrary finitely generated group.

Let
S_{A}
be a finite symmetric generating set of
A
, and
S_{H}
be a finite generating set of
H
.
In this case, the standard generating set of
G=A\wr H
is given by

S\coloneqq\Big\{(\delta_{a},\mathsf{id}_{H}),\,(\mathbf{0},s)\Big|\ a\in S_{A},\;\text{and}\;s\in S_{H}\Big\}.

Then for any element
g=(f,h)\in G
the corresponding word length
with respect to
S
can be computed as

|g|_{\mathrm{S}}=\mathrm{TSP}(\mathsf{id}_{H},\mathrm{supp}(f),h)+\sum_{x\in\mathrm{supp}(f)}|f(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}

We will call the corresponding metric on
G=A\wr H
the standard metric.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
H
be a finitely generated acylindrically hyperbolic group and let
A
be any nontrivial finitely generated group. Assume that
G=A\wr H
is equipped with a standard metric
d
, and
\mu
is a symmetric probability measure on
G
with finite exponential moment such that its projection onto
H
is a non-elementary measure. Then there exists
\sigma>0
such that

\frac{d(\mathsf{id}_{G},Z_{n})-\ell(\mu,d)n}{\sqrt{n}}

converges to
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
in distribution as
n\xrightarrow{}\infty
.

Proof.

The proof follows exactly the same outline as the proof of Theorem
1.1
. The only modifications are performed in Step 2 in the proof of Theorem
5.1
. These modifications do not change the fact that the upper bounds on the moments of the defect are polynomial in
\log(m+n)
.

4

In fact, a polynomial of degree 5 would still work.
Since a polynomial in
\log(m+n)
satisfies the slow growth assumption, the results of Section
3
remain applicable in this case as well.

As in Step 2 of Theorem
5.1
, let us fix
p>1
.
For
x\in H
, denote by
f_{1}(x)\in A
the state of the lamp at
x
after the first
m
steps of the random walk. Similarly, let
f_{2}(x)
denote the change in the state of the lamp at
x
under the trajectory of the random walk between the times
m+1
and
m+n
, and let
f_{3}(x)
be the final state of the lamp at
x
at time
m+n
. It is easy to see that
f_{3}(x)=f_{1}(x)f_{2}(x)
. In the notation from the proof of Theorem
5.1
,
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
, and
L_{3}
are the supports of
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
and
f_{3}
respectively. The arguments related to the defect of
\mathrm{TSP}
and the upper bounds on
N
and
D
remain essentially unchanged.
However, instead of proving an upper bound on the difference
|L_{1}|+|L_{2}|-|L_{3}|
, we now need to obtain the upper bound on

\sum_{x\in H}|f_{1}(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}+|f_{2}(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}-|f_{3}(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}.

The triangle inequality for the word length implies that
|f_{1}(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}+|f_{2}(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}-|f_{3}(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}
is always non-negative, and it is easy to see that it can take positive value only if
x\in L_{1}\cap L_{2}
.

Since we have the inequality
|L_{1}\cap L_{2}|\leq N
, and Step 2 in Theorem
5.1

\log(m+n)
that does not depend on
x
. Moreover, it suffices to find an upper bound on
|f_{1}(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}+|f_{2}(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}
. Notice that in Step 2 we established that with overwhelming probability, for every
k=1,\ldots,m+n
, the lamp at
x
may be modified at time
k
only if the position of the projected random walk
\overline{Z}_{k-1}
satisfies the inequality
d_{H}(x,\overline{Z}_{k-1})\leq C_{0}\log(m+n)

x
on the right by an element
a\in A
such that
|a|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}\leq C_{0}\log(m+n)
. It remains to notice that by Proposition
5.3

|f_{1}(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}\leq C_{0}\log(m+n)(2K_{0}C_{0}\log(m+n)+1)

|f_{2}(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}\leq C_{0}\log(m+n)(2K_{0}C_{0}\log(m+n)+1).

As a result, we have established the desired upper bound

|f_{1}(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}+|f_{2}(x)|_{\mathrm{S}_{A}}\leq 2C_{0}\log(m+n)(2K_{0}C_{0}\log(m+n)+1),

that does not depend on
x\in L_{1}\cap L_{2}

The rest of the proof follows the same steps as those in the proof of Theorem
