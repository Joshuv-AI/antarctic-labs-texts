A Thiosemicarbazone Schiff Base and Its Titanium(IV) Complexes: Spectral Analysis, DFT Studies, and Biological Evaluation
Authors: Priyanka Ghanghas, Anshul Yadav, Astha Kamboj, Anita Choudhary, Manjinder Kour, Dinesh Kumar, Kavita Poonia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA new Schiff base (SB) ligand 2‐(1‐(4‐((4‐methyl‐3H‐benzo[b][1,4]diazepin‐2‐yl)oxy)phenyl)ethylidene)hydrazine‐1‐carbothioamide (L) was prepared through a condensation reaction of 4‐(4‐methylbenzo[b][1,4]diazepin‐2‐yloxy)acetophenone with thiosemicarbazide. The nature of bonding and the stereochemistry of the Ti metal complexes have been elucidated by elemental analysis, IR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and mass spectrometry. From IR spectra, it has been observed that the ligand is a neutral bidentate that coordinates to the metal ions through azomethine nitrogen, and the sulfur atom of the thiocarbonyl group. The synthesized ligand and its complexes were screened for antibacterial activities against Escherichia coli (E. coli), Staphylococcus aureus, Extended Spectrum β‐Lactamase (ESBL), Methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and Mycobacterium tuberculosis bacteria (M. tuberculosis). Antifungal activities were performed against Aspergillus fumigatus (A. fumigatus) and Candida albicans (C. albicans). The Ti metal complexes exhibit markedly good biological activities compared to the free ligand, with the highest inhibition against Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Density functional theory (DFT) computations were performed to analyze the electronic structure, stability, and to support the interpretation of the experimental spectroscopic data. These comprehensive findings suggest that the synthesized Schiff base metal complexes are promising candidate for antimicrobial therapeutic applications.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains one of the most serious infectious diseases worldwide and continues to pose a major public health challenge despite significant advances in diagnosis and treatment. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 10.7 million people fell ill with TB worldwide in 2024, including 5.8 million men, 3.7 million women, and 1.2 million children and young adolescents. Despite a slowdown in the annual increase in TB cases and a decline in TB‐related deaths, the disease remains a significant public health threat, with 8.3 million new cases reported in 2024 [1]. The emergence of multidrug‐resistant and extensively drug‐resistant TB has further limited the effectiveness of conventional chemotherapeutic agents, emphasizing the urgent need for the development of novel antimicrobial compounds with improved efficacy, lower toxicity, and reduced susceptibility to resistance mechanisms. In this regard, metal‐based therapeutics have emerged as promising alternatives owing to their unique modes of action and the possibility of fine‐tuning their physicochemical and biological properties through ligand design [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Among various classes of bioactive organic molecules, Schiff bases (SBs) have garnered considerable attention due to their versatile pharmacophore, the azomethine (─C═N─) group, which not only confers a broad spectrum of biological activities but also facilitates the formation of stable metal complexes through chelation with various transition metals [8, 9, 10]. SB metal complexes remain a central focus in coordination and inorganic chemistry owing to their structural adaptability, tunable electronic properties, and wide‐ranging practical applications [11, 12].

These compounds are readily synthesized via the condensation of a carbonyl group with a primary amine. First reported in 1864 by the German chemist, Hugo Schiff [13], SB have numerous applications, including chemical sensors [14], catalysts [15], luminescence [16], dyes and pigments [17], polymer stabilizers [18], and intermediates in organic synthesis [19]. Beyond their industrial relevance, SB exhibit significant biological potential. The azomethine group can form hydrogen bonds with cellular targets, modulating biochemical pathways, and thereby highlighting their promise as antimicrobial and therapeutic agents [20, 21].

Thiosemicarbazones represent an important subclass of SBs owing to the presence of azomethine nitrogen and thioamide sulfur atoms, which enable the formation of stable coordination complexes with transition metals. Their structural versatility and ease of functionalization have attracted considerable interest in medicinal chemistry, where they exhibit diverse biological activities, including antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, antitubercular, and anticancer properties. Metal coordination often enhances their pharmacological potential by improving stability, lipophilicity, cellular uptake, and interactions with biological targets [22]. Among transition metals, Ti(IV)has emerged as a promising metal center because of its low toxicity, excellent biocompatibility, and strong affinity toward N, O, S‐donor ligands. Coordination of Ti(IV)with thiosemicarbazone ligands can significantly modify the electronic and physicochemical properties of the resulting complexes, leading to improved biological performance and making these compounds attractive candidates for the development of novel antimicrobial agents and other therapeutic applications [23, 24, 25].

The SB ligand 2‐(1‐(4‐((4‐methyl‐3H‐benzo[b][1,4]diazepin‐2‐yl)oxy)phenyl)ethylidene)hydrazine‐1‐carbothioamide, with its unique donor atom configuration, provides a robust scaffold for designing SB with enhanced chelation capabilities. Coordination of a SB ligand to transition metals, such as Ti(IV), often yields complexes with improved stability, reactivity, and biological activity. The interaction between the metal and the SB ligand often boosts their antimicrobial activity, making these complexes strong candidates against drug‐resistant bacteria.

This study focuses on the synthesis of SBs derived from 4‐(4‐methylbenzo[b][1,4]diazepin‐2‐yloxy)acetophenone and their subsequent complexation with Ti(IV) ions. The antibacterial potential of synthesized compounds was assessed against Escherichia coli (E. coli), Staphylococcus aureus, Extended Spectrum β‐Lactamase (ESBL), Methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and Mycobacterium tuberculosis bacteria (M. tuberculosis). Antifungal activity was performed with Aspergillus fumigatus (A. fumigatus) and Candida albicans (C. albicans).

Recent advances illustrate how density functional theory (DFT), in concert with spectroscopy, can establish bond character and electronic configuration in chemical structures [26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Guided by the existing literature, DFT calculations were conducted to examine the electronic structure, characteristics, stability, and reactivity of the generated complexes [32, 33]. Frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs) analysis and HOMO–LUMO energy and gap of these compounds provided molecular‐level insight into donor–acceptor interactions and helped rationalize the observed spectroscopic trends [34, 35].

Considering the remarkable biological potential of thiosemicarbazone SBs and the growing interest in Ti(IV) coordination compounds, the present study was undertaken to synthesize a novel thiosemicarbazone SB ligand derived from 4‐(4‐methylbenzo[b][1,4]diazepin‐2‐yloxy)acetophenone and its corresponding Ti(IV)chloride and bromide complexes. The synthesized compounds were characterized using elemental analysis, FT‐IR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, mass spectrometry, and DFT calculations to establish their structural and electronic properties. Furthermore, their antibacterial, antifungal, and antitubercular activities were evaluated to investigate the influence of Ti(IV)coordination on the biological behavior of the ligands and to assess their potential as promising metal‐based antimicrobial agents.

Experimental Section

Materials and Methods

All chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and procured from Sigma–Aldrich, India, including the metal salts (TiCl4 and TiBr4), thiosemicarbazide, and sodium acetate. None of the compounds was further purified before usage. The solvents used in the synthesis and purification processes were methanol and ethanol, which served as reaction media and for recrystallization. The synthesized compounds were characterized using elemental analysis, FT‐IR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, LC–MS, and powder PXRD techniques. Elemental (C, H, N, and S) analyses were performed using a CHNS elemental analyzer. FT‐IR spectra were recorded in the range of 4000–400 cm−1. 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded in DMSO‐d6 using tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal reference, and chemical shifts are reported in ppm (δ). Mass spectra were recorded using an LC–MS system operating in positive ion mode. X‐ray diffraction patterns were recorded using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) and the diffraction data were indexed using X'Pert HighScore Plus software.

Synthesis of SB Ligand

The ligand 2‐(1‐(4‐((4‐methyl‐3H‐benzo[b][1,4]diazepin‐2‐yl)oxy)phenyl)ethylidene)hydrazine‐1‐carbothioamide (L) was synthesized by gradually mixing hot methanolic solution of 4‐(4‐methylbenzo[b][1,4]diazepin‐2‐yloxy)acetophenone (0.01 mol) with an equal molar hot methanolic solution of thiosemicarbazide (0.01 mol) in the presence of sodium acetate (0.01 mol), maintaining an equimolar ratio. To this mixture, 2–3 drops of hydrochloric acid were added, and the reaction was stirred continuously with a magnetic stirrer (Scheme 1). The addition of acid is necessary because thiosemicarbazide polymerizes at pH values above 6. The reaction mixture was irradiated in a microwave (M.W.) at 90°C for 7 min. The mixture was then brought to room temperature and left undisturbed overnight to facilitate crystallization. A milky white solid precipitate was collected by filtration, rinsed sequentially with distilled water and cold methanol, and then dried in a desiccator. Antimicrobial Assessment

Method for Antibacterial Study

The antibacterial activity of the ligand and its metal complexes was evaluated against E. coli, S. aureus, ESBL, and MRSA using the agar well diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton Agar (MHA) [36]. The test compounds were dissolved in DMSO to prepare stock solutions (100 mg/mL), followed by serial dilutions (100, 50, 5, and 1 mg/mL). The bacterial inoculum was standardized to 0.5 McFarland turbidity and uniformly swabbed onto the agar surface. Wells were created using sterile tips, and 70 µL of each test solution was loaded into the respective walls. Streptomycin was used as the positive control and DMSO as the negative control. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h, and the zones of inhibition were measured in millimeters. All experiments were performed in replicates (n = 3) Table S1a–d, and results are expressed accordingly.

Method for Antifungal Study

The antifungal activity of the synthesized compounds was evaluated using the agar well diffusion method on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA). Test solutions were prepared in DMSO at different concentrations (100, 50, 5, 1 mg/mL). The fungal cultures were inoculated on the agar surface, and 70 µL of each test solution was introduced into the wells. The reference drug, itraconazole (0.5 mg/mL) was used as the positive control, while DMSO/distilled water served as the negative control. The plates were incubated at 28°C for 24–48 h, and the zones of inhibition were measured in millimeters. All experiments were carried out in replicates (n = 3) Table S1e,f [37].

Method for Tuberculosis Activity

For inoculum preparation, cultures on solid medium older than 2 weeks were used. A suspension was prepared in MGIT broth containing glass beads, adjusted to greater than 1 McFarland turbidity standard, followed by adjustment to 0.5 McFarland. The suspension was vortexed for 3–4 min, then allowed to stand undisturbed for 20 min to facilitate sedimentation of larger clumps. The resulting supernatant was transferred to a sterile tube and left to settle for an additional 15 min. The supernatant obtained was then adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard. A 1:5 dilutions were subsequently prepared by mixing 1 mL of this suspension with 4 mL sterile saline under gentle agitation. The test compounds were dissolved to the required concentrations, and MGIT tubes were labelled accordingly. To each MGIT tube, 0.8 mL of supplement was added. Test compounds were introduced into tubes 3–6, while tubes 1 and 2 served as controls. Specifically, 100 µL of DMSO were added as negative control. All tubes were gently mixed to ensure uniform distribution. Drug‐susceptibility test (DST) inoculum were prepared at 1:5 and 1:100 dilutions. For the growth control tube (tube 1), 0.5 mL of the undiluted DST inoculum was added. For test tubes (tubes 3–6), 0.5 mL of the 1:5 diluted inoculums were inoculated. Finally, all tubes were incubated at 37°C in the MGIT 320 system for 2 weeks. Growth was recorded qualitatively based on instrument detection.

DFT Calculations

All quantum–chemical calculations were carried out with Gaussian 16 [38]. Geometry optimizations and vibrational analyses were performed using DFT with the dispersion‐corrected B3LYP‐D3(BJ) functional and def2‐SVP basis set for all atoms. Solvent effects were included using SMD implicit solvation model with methanol as the solvent. Self‐consistent field (SCF) energies and geometry steps were converged using the program defaults, and all stationary points were verified as minima by harmonic frequency calculations (no imaginary modes). Gaussian's analytical second derivatives were used throughout. The ligand was treated as a closed‐shell singlet with charge 0. For the metal complex, we optimized the isolated cationic coordination unit [Ti(L)2Br2]2+, rather than an explicit salt pair with outer‐sphere bromides. Calculations on the bromide complex were performed on the cationic coordination unit [Ti(L)2Br2]2+ in order to describe the inner coordination sphere around Ti(IV) and its spectroscopic signature. Similar cation‐focused treatments, with omission of outer‐sphere/non‐coordinating counter‐anions, have been reported in previous transition‐metal DFT studies [39, 40]. This is standard practice for gas‐phase DFT models of ionic coordination compounds when non‐coordinating counter‐anions do not participate directly in bonding; closely related organometallic studies adopt the same approach for electronic‐structure analysis [41]. Both cis and trans configurations of the complex were optimized at the same computational level and the lower energy trans configuration was selected for the subsequent electronic structure and Time‐dependent DFT (TD‐DFT) calculations. Computed IR fundamentals were then compared with the observed modes [42, 43, 44, 45]. Frontier molecular orbitals (HOMO/LUMO) were visualized in Chemcraft [46] from the Gaussian checkpoint files using an iso‐value of 0.02.

IR Spectra of Complexes

In the IR spectra of the metal complexes, the absorption bands corresponding to the carbonyl and azomethine groups were observed to shift to lower frequencies compared to those in the free ligand, appearing at ∼1220–1212 cm−1 and 1612–1640 cm−1, respectively. This downward shift indicates that the metal ion coordinates via the nitrogen atom of the azomethine (>C═N) group and the sulfur atom of the thiocarbonyl (>C═S) group. A similar shift was also noted in the band associated with the ─C─O─C─ group [48, 49]. The IR spectra of the ligand and metal complexes are revealed in Figures S1–S3, and the significant IR peaks are outlined in Table 1.

The optimized geometry of the cationic coordination unit [Ti(L)2Br2]2+ provides a structural model for interpreting the spectroscopic results. Each ligand coordinated to Ti(IV) in a κ2‐(N,S) mode through the azomethine nitrogen and thiocarbonyl sulfur atom. The two calculated chelate bite angle (∠N─Ti─S) are 73.11° and 79.54° These small angles show that two five‐membered rings are not geometrically equivalent. These restricted bite angles contribute to the noticeable distortions from an ideal octahedral coordination. These findings are consistent with the literature findings on the complexes of a similar category [50, 51, 52]. The Br─Ti─N angle is approximately 80°–115°, while the Br─Ti─S angles range from 83°–100°. Two azomethine nitrogen show a pseudo‐trans separation of (∠N─Ti─N) 158°, substantially deviating from the ideal 180°.

The optimized geometries of the ligand and complex are presented in Figure 2. Coordinates of the optimized geometries are given in the Tables S2 and S3. Relative to the free SB ligand for which C═N and C═S bond lengths are 1.29 and 1.68 Å, the complex shows elongation of these bonds. The two coordinated C═N bonds are 1.30 and 1.31 Å, while C═S bonds are 1.73 and 1.74 Å. It also shows the new metal–donor interactions (Ti─N = 2.23, 2.26 Å; Ti─S = 2.41, 2.46 Å). The calculated Ti‐N and Ti─S bond lengths fall within the range reported for crystallographically characterized six‐coordinated Ti metal complexes, where Ti─N distances were reported to be around 2.25–2.27 Å [52, 53], and Ti─S distances are typically reported around 2.44–2.46 Å [54, 55, 56].

Optimized structures of the free ligand (left) and [Ti(L)2Br2]2+ (right).

The terminal bromides complete the six‐coordinate environment, with Ti─Br bond lengths of 2.43 Å. As discussed above, the coordination angles involving the bromide ligands also demonstrate the distortion of the coordination sphere. Accordingly, the calculated structure is more accurately described as a distorted octahedral, trans‐like configuration rather than a regular trans‐octahedral complex. The slight inequality within the Ti─N/Ti─S pairs is readily attributed to unsymmetrical trans environments (Br vs. S) and chelate‐ring constraints which is a known sources of ground‐state distortion in octahedral complexes [57, 58, 59]. Both cis and trans like configurations were investigated, but the trans structure was found to be 8.9 kJ mol− 1 lower in energy than the cis structure, indicating that the trans‐like configuration is the energetically preferred isomer. These structural features are consistent with the observed downshifts of ν(C═N) and ν(C═S) and with the appearance of ν(Ti─N), ν(Ti─S), and ν(Ti─Br) in the far‐IR, providing a coherent, molecular‐level picture of κ2‐(N,S) coordination to Ti(IV). The DFT predicted IR spectrum of the cation [Ti(L)2Br2]2+ reproduced the principal experimental vibrational bands. The azomethine νC═N stretching vibrations are predicted at 1626.17 and 1626.44 cm−1. The νC═S stretching vibration is observed at 1223.92 cm−1. These observed modes are in close agreement with the experimentally observed ν(C═N) and ν(C═S) bands at 1612 and 1220 cm−1, respectively. Several normal modes involving Ti─Br stretching are predicted in the 300–340 cm−1 region, whereas mixed Ti–S and Ti–N stretching vibrations are distributed over the 368–437 and 475–547 cm−1 regions, respectively.

For [Ti(L)2Br2]2+, the HOMO and LUMO energies are −0.2303 and −0.1545 a.u., respectively. As shown in Figure 3, the HOMO is largely localized over the π‐conjugated framework of the coordinated SB ligand. In contrast, the LUMO is primarily localized on the Ti(IV) coordination sphere, with electron density distributed over the Ti atom and the coordinated donor atoms (Figure 3).

HOMO (left) and LUMO (right) iso‐surfaces for [Ti(L)2Br2]2 +.

The HOMO–LUMO gap narrows to 0.0759a.u. compared with the free ligand (0.1569a.u.). This gap reduction, dominated by stabilization of a Ti‐centered LUMO, indicated increased electronic softness [60]. These results provide a qualitative description of the electronic structure of the complex. TD‐DFT calculations were performed to investigate the low‐lying singlet excited states of the optimized structure. A total of 40 singlet excitations were calculated, with the lowest‐energy states (S1 and S2) exhibiting negligible oscillator strengths, while several higher‐energy states showed comparatively larger oscillator strengths and are therefore expected to contribute more significantly to the calculated absorption spectrum. 1H NMR Spectrum of Ligand and Metal Complexes

The 1H NMR spectra of the ligand (L) (Figure S4) and its corresponding Ti metal complexes (Figures 4 and 5) indicate downfield shifts in certain resonance signals upon complexation. The aromatic protons of the ligand appear as multiplets in the range of δ 7.83–6.75 ppm. A peak corresponding to the methyl proton adjacent to the imine group is observed at δ 2.27 ppm. In the Ti metal complexes, this signal shifts downfield to δ 2.54 ppm, confirming the involvement of the imine group in complex formation [61, 62]. The δ 2–4 ppm region also contains the residual DMSO‐d6 solvent resonance (δ ≈ 2.50 ppm) and weak residual water signal (δ ≈ 3.3 ppm), which partially overlap with the methyl proton resonance of the complexes. Consequently, slight variations in the apparent integration and peak shape are observed in this region and are attributed to solvent overlap. The observed deshielding is attributed to coordination of the azomethine nitrogen to the metal center, which decreases the electron density around the imine group and consequently affects the adjacent methyl protons.

The superior antimicrobial activity of the complexes compared to the ligand can be explained by Tweedy's chelation theory, which proposes that upon complexation, the polarity of the metal ion decreases due to partial sharing of its positive charge with the donor atoms of the ligand. This enhances the lipophilic character of the complex, facilitating its penetration into microbial cell membranes and thereby increasing biological activity. Nonetheless, chelation is not the sole factor governing antimicrobial efficacy. Other parameters, including the nature of the ligand and metal ion, the geometry of the complex, the type of coordinating atoms, concentration, as well as the balance between lipophilicity and hydrophilicity, also play crucial roles in determining activity. In addition to the chelation effect, the nature of the coordinated halide ion may also contribute to the observed differences in the biological activities of the Ti(IV) complexes. Variations in the electronic properties, size, and polarizability of chloride and bromide ligands can influence the coordination environment, stability, and overall physico‐chemical properties of transition‐metal complexes, which may consequently affect their interactions with biological systems. In the present study, the Ti(IV) bromide complex exhibited comparatively higher antimicrobial activity than the corresponding chloride analogue, suggesting that the coordinated halide may influence the biological behavior of the complexes.

In Vitro Activity of Tuberculosis

The reduction in the HOMO–LUMO gap upon coordination reflects changes in electronic distribution and frontier orbital characteristics, including increased softness/polarizability [59, 67, 68, 69]. However, these electronic features are interpreted only in terms of molecular structure and orbital properties and are not used to directly account for the observed biological activity, which is based on experimental results. While mechanisms such as redox processes or membrane interactions have been discussed for related SB metal complexes in the literature, no specific mechanistic interpretation is proposed in the present study [70]. The anti‐tubercular activity of ligand (L) and its Ti metal complexes was evaluated using the broth microdilution method in Middlebrook 7H9 medium, with 1% DMSO as the control. Microbroth cultures were employed to monitor M. tuberculosis (H37Ra) inhibition, and growth was confirmed by optical density measurements. As expected, uninhibited cultures in 1% DMSO showed rapid growth, with visible colony formation on 7H10 agar within two weeks of incubation. p‐Aminosalicylic acid, a standard anti‐TB drug, produced a more than 2‐log reduction (≈100‐fold decrease) in colony counts compared to the control (Table 5).

Limitations of the Study and Future Perspectives

The continued development of Ti(IV) thiosemicarbazone complexes presents several opportunities for further investigation. Although the present study provides comprehensive structural, computational, and biological insights, additional studies could further strengthen the understanding of this class of compounds. Single‐crystal x‐ray diffraction analysis would provide definitive structural elucidation, while solvent‐phase computational investigations could offer closer correlation with experimental observations. Furthermore, detailed mechanistic studies, molecular docking, toxicity profiling, pharmacokinetic evaluation, and in vivo biological investigations would contribute to a deeper understanding of the structure–activity relationships and therapeutic potential of Ti(IV) thiosemicarbazone complexes. Such studies may facilitate the rational design and development of more effective metal‐based antimicrobial and antitubercular agents.

Conclusion

In the present study, a novel thiosemicarbazone SB ligand and its Ti(IV) chloride and bromide complexes were successfully synthesized and characterized using elemental analysis, FTIR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, mass spectrometry, XRD and DFT calculations. The spectroscopic and computational studies confirmed the proposed coordination of the ligand to the Ti(IV) center through the azomethine nitrogen and thiocarbonyl sulfur atoms. Structural studies confirmed the bidentate coordination mode of the ligand. Biological evaluation demonstrated that Ti(IV) complexation enhanced the antimicrobial activity of the ligand against the tested bacterial, fungal, and M. tuberculosis strains, confirming the beneficial effect of metal coordination on biological performance. These findings underscore the importance of metal–ligand interactions in designing effective antimicrobial agents. Among the synthesized compounds, the Ti(IV) bromide complex exhibited the most promising antitubercular activity, indicating its potential as a lead candidate for further investigation. The DFT calculations provide additional insights into the optimized geometries and electronic properties of the complex. Combined experimental and computational results demonstrate that Ti(IV) coordination significantly influences the structural and biological properties of the ligand, highlighting these complexes as promising candidates for the development of new metal‐based antimicrobial agents.
