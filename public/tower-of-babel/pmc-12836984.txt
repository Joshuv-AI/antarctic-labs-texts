In vivo dynamic monitoring of self-assembled melatonin nanodrug combined with photothermal effects to alleviate hypoxia to attenuate tumor aggressiveness
Authors: Yun Zeng, Dan Chen, Ke Li, Xueqing Kang, Peng Chang, Yijun Lu, Jingwen Ma, Wei Wang, Qinglai Yang, Li Tang, Wenhua Zhan, Qi Pan, Yonghua Zhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTumor biophysics is characterized by a fundamental aspect known as hypoxia. Melatonin (MLT) can decrease the activity of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), thereby preventing tumor cell survival, proliferation, invasion, metastasis, angiogenesis, and energy metabolism.ResultsTo monitor bioluminescence and assess the ability of combined MLT and photothermal therapy (PTT) to alleviate hypoxia, we constructed a modified A549-HRE-Luc cell line that responded to hypoxia. A versatile nanocarrier was developed to encapsulate MLT and combine near-infrared (NIR)-II fluorescence imaging, magnetic resonance (MR) imaging, and PTT, to effectively suppress tumor HIFs. The therapeutic effects of MLT on HIF-1α and PTT-mediated alleviation of tumor hypoxia were assessed via bioluminescence, which was contributed by the luciferase reporter gene in vivo. Additionally, it demonstrated the capability of NIR-II fluorescence imaging and T1-weighted MR imaging by including Mn2+, and the AS1411 aptamers, which were coordinated with metal ions, was used to specifically target the modified A549-HRE-Luc cells.ConclusionsUltimately, the findings from both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that the simultaneous use of MLT and PTT could effectively suppress hypoxia in tumors and trigger programmed apoptosis. As a result, this approach holds promise as a potential combined therapeutic option with enhanced therapeutic effects.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03960-4.

Background

Melatonin (MLT), also known as N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine, is a versatile chemical that is naturally created and released in the pineal gland of the brain [1]. Additionally, it can be synthesized within the mitochondria of all cells [2]. MLT has various functions, such as controlling circadian rhythms and annual reproductive cycles, exerting antioxidant effects, and regulating the immune system [3]. Nevertheless, under normal physiological conditions, MLT is exclusively produced within the pineal gland and cannot be released into the bloodstream. Thus, to treat diseases, it is necessary to administer the treatment externally [4]. The complex mechanism of MLT, a drug with multiple targets and pathways, is incompletely understood. However, in recent years, its therapeutic potential in treating cancer has garnered significant interest [5–6]. MLT has been shown to inhibit tumor proliferation and angiogenesis, regulate the immune system and apoptotic activity, making it a promising candidate for cancer treatment [7]. It states that mM-level doses of MLT, which are mostly concentrated in the region of 1–5 mM, are widely used in tumor therapy. High concentrations of MLT are very uncommon. However, lower amounts (0.1 mM) of MLT can also affect the pathways of tumor cells [7].

Hypoxia is a fundamental characteristic of tumor biology, and the availability and utilization of oxygen are intricately linked to tumor angiogenesis. Hypoxia stimulates the upregulation of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) expression in cells. These factors function as transcription factors, triggering the activation of numerous downstream genes. This process facilitates cell survival, proliferation, invasion, metastasis, angiogenesis, and energy metabolism. Additionally, it enables malignancies to evade therapeutic interventions [8, 9]. MLT can control the onset and progression of cancers caused by hypoxia in tumors. Current research indicates that MLT plays a role in suppressing the activity of HIFs [10, 11]. MLT can suppress the activity of HIFs, resulting in the inhibition of angiogenesis. Consequently, tumor cells are deprived of nutrients and eventually die. Hypoxia can promote the survival of cancer cells by downregulating apoptotic elements such as caspase-3, -8, and -9, cytochrome complex (Cyt c), Fas/FasL, and Bax. Conversely, MLT up-regulates and activates apoptotic elements, which prevents cancer cells from surviving. Additionally, in hypoxic cancer cells, MLT inactivates or down-regulates Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, inhibits the proliferation of hypoxic tumor cells by blocking the cell cycle and up-regulating p21/WAF1 and p53, and regulates the cell cycle by reducing the expression of cyclin A and cyclin D in hypoxic cells [10]. Nevertheless, this solitary antiangiogenic “starvation therapy” is typically insufficient to fully eradicate the tumor. Hence, the integration of MLT with additional medications or therapies to alleviate tumor hypoxia will contribute to augmenting its efficacy and show a synergistic effect in inhibiting cancers to a certain degree [12–14].

Therefore, the combination of MLT with noninvasive tumor treatment techniques such as chemodynamic therapy [15], photodynamic therapy [16], and photothermal therapy (PTT) is currently a highly investigated and widely utilized area of study. PTT is a form of physical therapy specifically designed for treating tumors. Furthermore, in addition to its direct cytotoxic effects on tumor cells through tissue heating, it can also stimulate the upregulation of heat shock protein levels, thereby enhancing the uptake and presentation of antigens by antigen-presenting cells [17]. Previous research has demonstrated that this approach can enhance the oxygen partial pressure of tumor cells by accelerating blood flow to facilitate oxygen delivery, eliminate some tumor cells, decrease oxygen consumption, and control HIFs [18, 19]. Thus, the concurrent use of PTT and MLT at identical targets such as HIF-1 can effectively mitigate tumor hypoxia and augment therapeutic effectiveness. Nevertheless, MLT is usually administered orally [20], and the extent to which the bioavailability of MLT can change depending on the manner of administration. Furthermore, MLT exhibits inadequate stability and solubility in water. Nanocarriers containing MLT exhibit greater efficacy than conventionally delivered MLT. This is primarily attributed to the ability of nanocarriers to avoid early oxidation of MLT and prolong the exposure time, hence enhancing drug penetration [21]. PTT also necessitates the utilization of nanocarriers [22]. However, the majority of existing MLT nanocarriers were developed based on the intrinsic physiological properties of MLT and were frequently employed in the central nervous system [23], such as in the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease [24]. Furthermore, a few studies have demonstrated the efficacy of nanocarriers loaded with MLT in the treatment of triple-negative breast cancer [25, 26] and in the prevention of bone tumor recurrence [27]. An early study revealed that the controlled release of MLT significantly increased when magnetothermal nanoparticles (NPs) carrying MLT were exposed to magnetic heating. This synergistically enhanced the cytotoxicity of nanothermia [28]. Although there are some reports on this topic, there is currently a lack of relevant research investigating the potential of combining MLT and PTT to alleviate tumor hypoxia and prevent tumor proliferation. Hence, it is essential to develop MLT nanomedicine for the treatment of tumor hypoxia.

Nevertheless, to achieve the combined therapeutic benefit of MLT and PTT for alleviating hypoxia, it is crucial to effectively suppress and monitor tumor HIFs. However, there remains a deficiency in noninvasive techniques for detecting tumor hypoxia, and there is an absence of technologies capable of directly observing the in vivo expression level of HIFs. Bioluminescence is a handful technique that can detect HIFs in real time in vivo, providing an indirect indication of tumor hypoxia. Harada et al. developed a novel reporter gene that could detect the hypoxic state of tumor cells. This gene consisted of five copies of the hypoxia response element, which enhanced the expression of the oxygen-dependent degradation domain and luciferase fusion when the cells were under hypoxic conditions. The luminescence produced by this gene could provide real-time information about the activity of HIF-1 [29]. Researchers subsequently detected HIF activation by directly observing the combination of HIFα and HIFβ subunits, which are functional HIF transcription factors. They also used split nanoluciferase as a tool to measure the interactions between HIFα and HIFβ proteins, which are caused by the stabilization of HIF-1α or HIF-2β [30]. Roennfeldt et al. created a novel and adaptable reporter system called NanoFIRE. This system incorporated nanoluciferase and fluorescent integrated reporter elements to investigate cellular signaling pathways regulated by HIFs and other transcription factors [31]. Currently, there is a lack of research on the use of bioluminescence to monitor the inhibition of HIFs by MLT, and there is also a shortage of designs for nanocarriers that can effectively distribute MLT and achieve PTT. Characterizing the real-time expression of HIFs in tumor cells is challenging, and providing feedback on the effects of MLT and PTT on the biological impacts of tumor hypoxia is even more difficult.

To achieve real-time monitoring and evaluation of the ability of combined MLT and PTT to relieve hypoxia, inspired by a series of predominant near-infrared (NIR)-II probes [32–35], we developed a multifunctional IRFE-PEG-DOTA (IPD) [36] self-assembled nanoplatform called MLT@IPD-Apt/Mn2+ (MIAM). This nanocarrier integrated NIR-II fluorescence imaging, magnetic resonance (MR) imaging, and PTT. By encapsulating MLT, the nanocarriers inhibited tumor HIFs and allowed direct observation of the level of tumor hypoxia and the therapeutic effect on a bioluminescent A549-HRE-Luc model. The nanocarrier possessed NIR-II fluorescence imaging capabilities and PTT effects. Additionally, the inclusion of Mn2+ endowed the nanocarrier with the ability to perform T1-weighted MR imaging. In this study, the AS1411 aptamer, in coordination with metal ions, was used to specifically target modified A549-HRE-Luc cells. The luciferase reporter gene was used to continuously monitor [37] and assess the ability of the nanodrug to alleviate tumor hypoxia in vivo. Ultimately, the results from experiments conducted in vitro and in vivo demonstrated that the combination of MLT and PTT effectively suppressed hypoxia in tumors and triggered programmed cell apoptosis. This finding suggests that this approach has the potential to be an effective and beneficial treatment option.

Methods

Synthesis and characterization of the MIAM NPs

The synthesis of IPD was conducted using a procedure identical to that previously reported [32, 36]. The steps leading up to the synthesis of compound 8 are the same as those reported in previous literature [36], but the subsequent steps are different to conjugate alkynyl-PEG600 to synthesize IPD (shown in Fig. S1). A solution containing 1.0 mg of IPD was prepared by dissolving it in 1.0 mL of deionized water. The solution was then stirred at 700 rpm for 3 h to facilitate self-assembly. Subsequently, the solution was dialyzed for 12 h using a membrane filter (MWCO = 1 000 Da) to eliminate free IPD. To obtain MLT@IPD, 80 µL of an MLT solution with a concentration of 5 mg mL− 1 was added to 1.0 mL of the previously prepared IPD solution. The mixture was then stirred for 2 h at 350 rpm. MLT@IPD/Mn2+ (MIM) NPs were obtained by adding and stirring 100 µL of Mn(II) acetate solution (5 mg mL− 1) for 3 h at 350 rpm. Afterwards, 100 µL of MgSO4 (5 mg mL− 1) was introduced into the MLT@IPD solution, whose pH was adjusted to 7.0. Subsequently, 5 µL of AS1411 aptamer (100 µmol L− 1) was added, and the mixture was stirred at 350 rpm for 30 min. Ultimately, the solution was purified using a PD-10 column to obtain the MIAM NPs.

The morphology and size distribution of the MIAM NPs were examined using a transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The MIAM NP solution was applied to the copper grid three times, allowed to dry, and then examined. A dynamic light scattering (DLS) particle size analyzer was used to measure the size distribution and zeta potential of the MIAM NPs. To demonstrate the presence of MLT encapsulated in MIAM NPs and AS1411 coordinated on the surface of the NPs, a UV-vis spectrophotometer was used to measure the UV-vis absorbance of the MLT, IPD, MIAM NPs, MIM NPs, AS1411, and AS1411 + IPD mixed solutions within the wavelength range of 240 to 450 nm. The fluorescence spectra of the MIM NPs, IPD, and MIAM NPs were analyzed using a fluorescence spectrophotometer in the NIR-II region from 850 to 1500 nm.

MLT inhibits HIFs in A549-HRE-Luc cells

Human non-small cell lung cancer A549-HRE-Luc cell lines were constructed through both transient and stable transfection methods [37]. These cell lines were then cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The cells were incubated at 37 °C under either 5% CO2 or 1% O2/5% CO2/94% N2. The MLT concentration gradient was established as follows: 0, 250, 500, 750, and 1 000 µM. Subsequently, changes in the hypoxic response of the A549-HRE-Luc transient cell line were observed and recorded after 12 h of MLT treatment. The stably transfected cell lines were distributed into 96-well plates, with cell quantities ranging from 1 × 104 to 1 × 106 per well in five gradients. Once the cells had attached to the wall, the cell well plates were transferred to a hypoxic incubator for 12 h. Subsequently, they were removed from the incubator to observe the cell bioluminescence. To confirm the hypoxic response of the A549-HRE-Luc stably transfected cell line, the cells were placed in 96-well plates and exposed to hypoxia for 0, 6, 12, and 24 h. The bioluminescence intensities of the cells were then measured to determine the effects of different durations of hypoxic culture. A subcutaneous tumor nude mouse model was established by introducing A549-HRE-Luc stably transfected cells. Tumor bioluminescence imaging was performed at 3-day intervals for 24 days. The experimental group received MLT one week after the introduction of the tumor. The dose was 50 mg kg− 1, with a 2-day interval between each administration. Throughout the treatment procedure, the body weights and tumor volumes of the nude mice were measured at regular intervals every 3 days and then compared.

In vitro cell culture studies of the MIAM NPs

To demonstrate the internalization of MIAM NPs within cells in vitro, A549-HRE-Luc cells were placed in confocal culture dishes and allowed to grow until they reached 50% confluence. MIAM NPs were then introduced and incubated for various durations (1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 h). To demonstrate the targeting efficacy of AS1411, three internalization groups, named MIAM NPs, MIM NPs, and blocking groups, were observed and compared. The MIAM NPs and MIM NPs were added separately and incubated for 8 h. AS1411 was initially introduced to A549-HRE-Luc cells in the blocking group, followed by a 2-h incubation period. Subsequently, the MIAM NPs were added and incubated for 8 h. Next, a 4% solution of paraformaldehyde was used to fix the cells. The uptake of MIAM NPs or MIM NPs (λex/λem: 405/435–475 nm) into cells was visualized using propidium iodide (PI) fluorescence (λex/λem: 561/575–625 nm) to identify the location of the nucleus via a confocal laser scanning microscope.

A total of 100 µL of the A549-HRE-Luc cell suspension was added to each well of a 96-well plate at a concentration of 1 × 104 cells/mL. MIAM NPs, MLT, MIM NPs, IPD, and IPD-Apt/Mn2+ (IAM) NPs at various concentrations (0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, and 0.30 mg mL− 1) were introduced into a 96-well plate (n = 3). PBS was used as the control. Following a 12-h incubation period, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for an additional 2 h. A microplate reader was used to quantify the absorbance at 450 nm and determine the rate of cell survival. To confirm the effectiveness of the MIAM NPs in killing cancer cells through chemo/PTT, five groups were established. Additionally, laser irradiation was applied to each group using an 808 nm laser with a power intensity of 2.0 W cm− 2 for 5 min. The same method was used to calculate the rates of cell survival. Then, the combination index (CI) value was calculated according to a reference [38] to prove the synergistic effect.

The killing efficacy of the MIAM NPs against tumor cells was assessed using flow cytometry. The cells were transferred to a 12-well plate and placed in a 37 °C incubator for a 12-h period of incubation. Subsequently, 2 mL of culture medium containing MLT, IPD, MIM NPs, IAM NPs, MIAM NPs, or PBS were added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 8 h. Subsequently, the digested cells were collected, resuspended in PBS, and quantified. The binding buffer in the apoptosis detection kit was diluted with PBS at a ratio of 1 : 5. Then, 100 µL of the diluted solution was used to resuspend the cells. After that, 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC was added, and the solution was gently mixed. The mixture was then incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. Subsequently, 10 µL of PI was added, followed by the addition of 400 µL of PBS after a 5-min interval. The resulting mixture was then analyzed using a flow cytometry apparatus for detection.

To assess the ability of the MIAM NPs to alleviate hypoxia, six groups were established: the Saline, Laser (L), MIAM, MIAM + L, IAM, and MLT groups. A549-HRE-Luc cells were cultured under both hypoxic and normoxic conditions. The cells, at a concentration of 1 × 104/mL, were added to a 96-well plate with a total of 3 replicates. Following the addition of the samples, an 808 nm laser was used to irradiate the cells at a power intensity of 1.0 W cm− 2 for 5 min. The cells were cultured for 8 h in either a 5% CO2/air or 1% O2/5% CO2/94% N2 environment. Following the incubation period, the luciferase detection reagent and A549-HRE-Luc cells were combined in equal amounts and incubated for 10 min. An animal imaging system was utilized to assess the efficacy of the MIAM NPs in mitigating tumor hypoxia.

In vivo MR and NIR-II fluorescence imaging

Female BALB/c nude mice (13 ~ 20 g, approximately 4 weeks old) were injected with 100 µL of A549-HRE-Luc cells at a concentration of 1 × 106/mL on their backs. Following the introduction of the tumor, the mice were nurtured until the tumors reached a specific size (200 mm3) in preparation for further experiments in vivo. Mice bearing subcutaneous tumors were divided into two groups (n = 3): the MIAM and the MIM groups. NPs from each group (200 µL, 0.5 mg mL− 1) were administered to the mice via tail vein injection. Following the administration of anesthesia to the mice, MR images of the tumor sites were obtained before and after injection at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 h, with MR imaging parameters of T1 WI (TR = 250 ms, TE = 10.618 ms, and FOV = 12 cm × 12 cm). The signal intensities of the images were then quantitatively analyzed. Meanwhile, a live animal imaging system that detects NIR-II fluorescence (λex/λem: 980 nm/1100 – 1700 nm) was utilized to record the signals before and after injection at specific time intervals of 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h. The analysis of the fluorescence signal at the tumor sites was conducted using ImageJ. Following the collection of images, the mice were euthanized, and their hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, kidneys, and tumors were removed for NIR-II fluorescence imaging under identical conditions.

In vivo synergistic chemo/PTT

Mice with subcutaneous tumors were separated into three groups (n = 3): the MIAM, the IAM, and the Saline groups. Each mouse was injected with 200 µL (0.5 mg mL− 1) of either MIAM, IAM, or saline through the tail vein. The mice were administered D-luciferin via intraperitoneal injection at specific time intervals (0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h) prior to bioluminescence imaging. Imaging was carried out using a small animal living imaging system to assess the levels of hypoxia in the tumors.

Mice with subcutaneous tumors were randomly divided into 5 groups (n = 3 for bioluminescence imaging and n = 5 for therapy). The groups were named Saline, Laser (L), IAM + L, MIAM, and MIAM + L. Intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin was administered on the morning of the initial day for the purpose of conducting bioluminescence imaging. On the second day, in the morning, 200 µL (0.5 mg mL− 1) of each sample was injected via the tail vein. After 24 h, the laser was applied to the tumor site of the mice using an 808 nm laser with a power intensity of 2.0 W cm− 2 for 5 min, if deemed necessary. The experiment was conducted once every three days. Throughout the treatment, the bioluminescence imaging intensity, body weight, tumor volume, and mortality of each mouse were continuously monitored. Following the completion of the treatment, the mice were euthanized, and the organs and tumors were surgically excised.

Statistical analysis

The data were expressed as the mean ± SD. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare several groups, and the Newman-Keuls test was used for a post-hoc analysis with R software, version 4.1.2. Significant differences were denoted as (n. s.) for no significant differences, (*) for P < 0.05, (**) for P < 0.01, and (***) for P < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the MIAM NPs

Figure 1 shows the procedure of self-assembly by the driving force of hydrophobic interaction between MLT and hydrophobic ends of IPD [39], where the IPD enclosed MLT and coordinated with Mn2+ and AS1411. The chemical structure of IPD is shown in Fig. 2a, and its NMR spectra in each synthesis step are shown in Figs. S2-S12. By utilizing divalent metal ions to overcome intermolecular repulsion and facilitate the self-assembly of DNA strands [40], the addition of Mg2+ was found to be beneficial for the formation of MIAM NPs [41], but it has no therapeutic function. To self-assemble into the core of NPs, the chemical structure of IPD is composed of a hydrophilic PEG structure at two ends and a hydrophobic structure in the center. Then, to overcome the negative charge under the action of Mg2+ and create the shell, DNA aptamers, as well as PEG, directly assembled, inspired by the self-assembly of DNA nanostructures with the assistance of Mg2+ [42]. Mg2+ can help DNA form NPs by neutralizing the negative charges on the DNA backbone, which reduces electrostatic repulsion and allows the DNA strands to condense and self-assemble into compact structures [43]. TEM was used to examine the structure and determine the size distribution of the MIAM NPs. Fig. 2b and c demonstrate that the spherical MIAM NPs had a diameter of approximately 150 nm. The size of the hydrated particles and the zeta potential of the MIAM NPs were measured to be 175.2 nm and + 6.44 ± 0.19 mV, respectively. On the other hand, the zeta potentials of MIM NPs and IPD were found to be + 3.68 ± 0.42 mV and + 20.63 ± 0.8 mV, respectively (Fig. 2d and e). These results suggest that AS1411 was present on the surface of the MIAM NPs. The UV absorbance peaks of IPD, MLT, and AS1411 appeared at 356 nm, 280 nm, and 260 nm, respectively, as shown in Fig. 2f. On the other hand, MIM NPs exhibited two peaks at 356 nm and 280 nm, showing the effective encapsulation of MLT. The MIAM NPs exhibited two distinct peaks at wavelengths of 260 nm and 356 nm, suggesting effective coordination of AS1411 and complete absorption of MLT. Fig. 2g displays the absorbance and fluorescence spectra of the MIAM NPs, which exhibited peaks at 771 nm and 1010 nm, respectively. These peaks were similar to those of the IPD and MIM NPs, enabling the use of NIR-II fluorescence imaging.

Stability, drug release profiles, and Fenton-like reactions of the MIAM NPs

After being left at room temperature for 48 h, the concentration of Mn2+ in the MIAM NPs remained at approximately 65%, as shown in Fig. 2h. The hydrated particle sizes of the MIAM NPs shown in Fig. 2i and j did not substantially vary among the various solution conditions. Additionally, the solution remained clear and transparent, suggesting a high level of stability.

The standard curve for MLT may be represented by the equation y = 2.5837x + 0.0147, where y represents the UV absorbance at 280 nm and x represents the concentration of MLT. The coefficient of determination (R2) for this curve is 0.9964 (shown in Fig. S13). The drug encapsulation efficiency and drug loading content were determined to be approximately 80% and 13.8% (w/w), respectively. Fig. 2k shows that the release rate of the free MLT solution was comparable to that of the MIAM NPs. In the control group, which received the free MLT solution, more than 80% of the MLT was released in 4 h. In contrast, the MIAM NPs released almost 80% of the MLT in 48 h at pH 5.0. Additionally, the MIAM NPs released only 50% of the MLT in 48 h at pH 7.0. This suggests that the MIAM NPs are more stable in a neutral environment. When administered in vivo, the acidic environment of the tumor may facilitate the release of MLT from the MIAM NPs.

Mn2+ may undergo a Fenton-like reaction with H2O2, which is overexpressed in tumor cells. This reaction generates hydroxyl radicals (·OH), which can destroy tumor cells [44]. Figure 2l and m demonstrate that in the absence of MIAM NPs, the methylene blue (MB) solution remained blue, with the most intense UV-vis absorbance peak occurring at 665 nm. As the concentration of the MIAM NPs increased, the solution became progressively colorless, and the UV absorbance decreased. This suggests that the Mn2+ in the MIAM NPs could react with H2O2 to produce ·OH, which oxidized MB and caused it to lose its color. Similarly, as the quantity of H2O2 increased, the solution progressively lost its color, and the UV-vis absorbance decreased. The findings demonstrate that the MIAM NPs had the ability to initiate the Fenton-like reaction.

Photothermal properties of the MIAM NPs

Figure 3a shows that the MIAM NPs achieved a peak temperature of 63.6 °C. After undergoing five cycles of turning on and off, the highest temperature consistently remained above 60 °C, which suggested that the MIAM NPs had excellent photothermal stability. The photothermal characteristics of the MIAM NPs were assessed upon exposure to laser light at various intensities. Fig. 3b shows that the temperature of the MIAM NPs increased proportionally with increasing laser power. When exposed to a power irradiation of 1.0 W cm− 2, the highest temperature reached 45.9 °C. However, when exposed to power irradiation at 1.5 W cm− 2 and 2.0 W cm− 2, the maximum temperatures of the MIAM NPs reached 53.7 °C and 65.1 °C, respectively. The photothermal characteristics of MIAM NPs at various concentrations were assessed. Fig. 3c shows that the greatest temperature recorded was 46.7 °C when the concentration was 0.12 mg mL− 1, but the highest temperature recorded was 65.3 °C when the concentration was 1.00 mg mL− 1. Fig. 3d shows that the temperature of the saline control was consistently maintained at approximately 29 °C. The highest temperature recorded for IPD was 59.6 °C, but the maximum temperature for MIAM NPs exceeded 65 °C. Fig. 3e displays photographs of the MIAM NPs during laser irradiation. The findings demonstrated that the MIAM NPs exhibited favorable photothermal characteristics and maintained their photothermal stability. According to Fig. 3f and g, the tumor locations in the Saline, MIM, and MIAM groups reached maximum temperatures of 33.3 °C, 32.9 °C, and 32.3 °C, respectively, when laser irradiation was not applied. There was no significant difference between these values. Following laser irradiation, the temperature of all three groups increased. The Laser (L) group experienced a temperature increase of only 36.3 °C, the IAM + L group experienced a temperature increase of 40.3 °C, and the MIAM + L group experienced a temperature increase of 43.1 °C. The temperature increase in the last two groups was a result of injecting nanoprobes into mice. The MIAM group had a higher temperature because AS1411 was added, which has targeting capabilities. This finding is consistent with a prior publication [18].

MLT inhibits HIFs in A549-HRE-Luc cells

Figure 4a and b demonstrate the cell viability when cells treated with different concentrations of MLT in a normoxic or hypoxic environment, respectively, indicating that tumor cells were more tolerant to MLT toxicity. Fig. 4c shows a significant increase in the expression of HIF-1α in hypoxic cells compared to normoxic cells when the MLT concentration was 0 mg mL− 1. This indicates the activation of the HIF-1α signaling pathway in A549 cells. When varying quantities of MLT were added to hypoxic cells, even though the MLT concentration was substantially lower than the IC50, the expression of HIF-1α in the hypoxia-treated group of cells notably decreased. This finding suggested that the activation of HIF-1α in A549 cells under hypoxic conditions was effectively suppressed. Fig. 4d shows that the lowest quantity of cells capable of detecting in vitro bioluminescence was 5 × 104. Fig. 4e shows expressed HIF-1α was more when tumor cells were treated in a hypoxic environment than in a normoxic environment but inhibited by MLT with increased concentrations. Fig. 4f shows that after the cultivated cells were subjected to hypoxic conditions for 12 h and 24 h, the bioluminescence intensities of the cells were approximately 6.56 and 10.87 times greater, respectively, than those of the cells under normoxic conditions. These differences were statistically significant (***P < 0.001). Fig. 4g shows that bioluminescence was observable at the tumor site one week after tumor injection in the control group. Over time, the bioluminescence of the tumor site exhibited a consistent and gradual increase in intensity, corresponding to an increase in tumor volume. Notably, bioluminescence continued to increase even after 23 days of continuous observation. Throughout the treatment procedure, the medicine did not have a noteworthy effect on the body weights of the nude mice, as shown in Fig. 4h. However, the tumor volumes increased, as depicted in Fig. 4i. The velocity was inferior to that of the control group, and the sizes of the tumors progressively increased as the duration of therapy increased. Following the completion of therapy, the tumor volumes in the administration group decreased compared to those in the control group, accompanied by reduced bioluminescence (as shown in Fig. 4j).

In vitro cell culture studies

Figure 4k shows that the fluorescence of the MIAM NPs was mostly in the cytoplasm. The integrated images of the MIAM NPs and PI provide further clarification. Over time, the MIAM NPs progressively accumulated in the cytoplasm, creating a noticeable distinction between the cytoplasm containing the MIAM NPs and the cell nucleus labeled with PI. This finding suggested that the MIAM NPs could successfully penetrate cells and accumulate in the cytoplasm, which was also shown by the fluorescence quantitative statistics chart.

Figure 4l shows that following incubation, the intensity of blue fluorescence in the cytoplasm was greatest for the MIAM NPs. The brightness of the stained cytoplasm in the blocking group and the MIM group was lower than that in the MIAM group. However, the red fluorescence intensity of the PI-stained nuclei remained consistent across all groups. This is because AS1411 can target A549 cell lines by transfecting nucleolin on the cell membrane. On the other hand, the MIM NPs lack the ability to specifically target tumor cells, resulting in fewer MIM NPs entering the cytoplasm. The presence of preadded AS1411, which binds to nucleolin, blocked the targeting sites of nucleolin. As a result, the subsequently added MIAM NPs were unable to bind to nucleolin. Thus, the fluorescence intensities of the MIM group and the blocking group were lower than that of the MIAM group, which exhibited active targeting capacity. Fig. 4m and n display the quantitative statistical data illustrating the distribution of the NPs and their affinity. These graphs indicated that AS1411 exhibited a favorable targeting effect on tumor cells, whereas the MIAM NPs were able to efficiently and actively penetrate the cytoplasm of the tumors.

Figure 5a and b display the cell viability of several samples, revealing that the IC50 of MLT was 0.4343 and 1.481 mg mL− 1 in a normoxic or hypoxic environment, respectively. According to the IC50 of MLT, neither the MIAM NPs nor the MIM NPs exhibited significant cytotoxicity. This is because MLT, when encapsulated in IPD to produce MIAM NPs, prevented free MLT from exerting its cytotoxicity to some degree. At a dose of 0.1 mg mL− 1, the cell survival rate of the IPD without MLT group remained greater than 50%, suggesting that the MIAM NPs exhibited favorable biocompatibility and biosafety. Fig. 5c shows that when an 808 nm laser was used for photothermal irradiation, the MIAM NPs had the greatest ability to induce cell death. At low concentrations, the cell killing ability was insignificant. However, when the concentration reached 0.1 mg mL− 1, laser irradiation significantly increased the synergistic killing capacity, resulting in an approximately 50% reduction in the cell survival rate of the MIAM NPs. This investigation conclusively showed that the MIAM NPs had a strong photothermal effect and could efficiently eradicate tumor cells. The CI of PTT and MLT treatment was 0.53 < 1, which indicated a synergistic effect occurred rather than the sum of individual treatments. These findings suggest that MIAM NPs have promise for use in future research in vivo.

As shown in Fig. 5d, the apoptosis rates were measured for different NPs and a control group treated with PBS. The control group treated with PBS had the lowest percentage of apoptotic cells (1.33%). The IPD, MLT, and IAM NPs had apoptosis rates of 3.31%, 5.30%, and 7.11%, respectively. The MIM and MIAM NPs had the greatest killing ability, with apoptosis rates of 9.05% and 8.99%, respectively. This is likely because MLT can generate ·OH through the production of Mn2+, resulting in a certain degree of cell killing ability. The synergistic effect of the two components resulted in potent cytotoxicity and efficient elimination of tumor cells by the MIAM NPs. This was further confirmed by Calcein-AM/PI co-staining of living and dead cells, as shown in Fig. S14.

To assess the ability of MIAM NPs to alleviate hypoxia, six groups were established: PBS, Laser (L), MLT, IAM, MIAM, and MIAM + L. The bioluminescence intensities of cells grown in a hypoxic environment were much greater than those of cells cultured in a normoxic environment, as shown in Fig. 5e and f. When comparing the effects of PBS, Laser (L), and MLT-related samples, it was shown that MLT-related NPs were able to suppress HIF-1α. Consequently, the bioluminescence intensities of the cells were weaker than those of the controls, which is consistent with earlier results [10, 11]. While laser irradiation has the potential to reduce tumor hypoxia in vivo, this effect was not clearly shown in the groups treated with PBS and Laser (L) or MIAM and MIAM + L in cell culture studies. As a result, the bioluminescence intensities of the IAM and MIAM groups were lower than those of the other three groups. This is because the presence of Mn2+ in these two groups caused a Fenton-like reaction that produced ·OH, which killed some tumor cells. MLT significantly inhibited the expression of HIF-1α (*** P < 0.001).

In vivo MR and NIR-II fluorescence imaging

Figs. S15, S16, and S17 display the outcomes of hemolysis, acute toxicity testing, and blood biochemistry analysis before conducting animal imaging and therapy trials. Fig. S18 displays the MR and NIR-II fluorescence images in vitro. As shown in Fig. 5g, there was no MR imaging signal at the tumor location before the injection (Pre). Following the injection, the nanoprobes were progressively gathered at the tumor locations. After 3 h, the most potent signals from the MIAM NPs appeared in the tumors. Subsequently, the signals progressively diminished over time. First, Mn2+ plays a crucial role in MR imaging. While the exact in vivo half-life of Mn-DOTA as an MR imaging contrast agent is not readily available in the literature with a single, universally quoted value, some studies indicate that the half-life of the Mn2+ complex is generally short [45], resulting in relatively short MR imaging duration for Mn-DOTA [46]. Furthermore, Mn2+ is rapidly oxidized to Mn3+ by H2O2 within the tumors, leading to a decrease in MR imaging signal intensity, resulting in lower relaxivity because of fewer unpaired electrons and less favorable electronic relaxation, a weaker or broader signal, and a less effective contrast agent. Fig. 5h displays the NIR-II fluorescence images. Over time, the fluorescence signals of tumors in the NIR-II range progressively increased until they reached their highest point at 24 h, after which they gradually decreased. The fluorescence intensity of the MIAM NPs was greater than that of the MIM NPs due to the presence of AS1411, which specifically binds to nucleolin on the tumor cell membrane, facilitating the accumulation of the nanoprobes in tumors. Following the imaging procedure, the mice were killed, and their hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, kidneys, and tumors were removed for ex vivo imaging. Fig. 5i demonstrates that the liver and tumors emitted the brightest light. This is because the liver is responsible for metabolizing fat-soluble compounds, and the nanoprobes can actively target malignancies. The quantitative data show that the intensity of the MIM NP signal was much lower than that of the MIAM NP signal. This difference in intensity is shown in Fig. 5j. Fig. 5k shows that the MIAM group had greater fluorescence signal intensity than the MIM group, as shown by the quantitative data. The maximum intensity of NIR-II fluorescence imaging occurs at 24 h. The major challenge in designing high-performance organic fluorescent probes for NIR-II biological imaging, in which a phenomenon is formally known as Aggregation-Caused Quenching (ACQ), which describes the dramatic loss or complete disappearance of fluorescence when the molecules transition from a dilute, dissolved state to a concentrated or aggregated state (e.g. forming a NP), which creates low-energy pathways for the excited-state energy to be released non-radiatively instead of fluorescence. However, for in vivo imaging, probes must be formulated as stable, water-dispersible NPs or aggregates, which were prevented by ACQ from being bright enough at the beginning of imaging [47]. Fig. 5l also shows that the liver and tumors had the highest signal intensity. By employing fluorescence intensity to determine the quantity of NPs entering the tumor, we were able to estimate that up to 16.73% of the MIAM NPs entered the tumor, which is higher than 13.15% of the MIM NPs if only considered the distribution in major organs and did not consider the loss of blood and urine. Because NPs inherently possess passive targeting capabilities and the MIM NPs are also taken up by tumors, the effect of AS1411 in vivo is not as strong as in vitro. Consequently, the MIAM NPs exhibited excellent imaging capability in vivo in terms of MR and NIR-II fluorescence, as shown by the findings.

In vivo synergistic chemo/PTT

Figure 6a shows the tumor treatment regimen of three-day cycles, for a total of 15 days of treatment. Fig. 6b shows that the tumor development of the MIAM group was slower within 72 h than that of the IAM and Saline groups because the MIAM NPs successfully accumulated in the tumors and that MLT, which had a suppressive influence on the tumors, lost its effect after 24 h. Fig. 6c visually demonstrates that the bioluminescence signals of the IAM and Saline groups were much greater than those of the MIAM NP group. This finding suggested that the MIAM NPs might partially ameliorate tumor hypoxia and prevent tumor development. The tumors in the Saline group and Laser (L) group exhibited rapid growth and exhibited the most intense bioluminescence signals, as shown in Fig. 6d. The MIAM NPs, which include MLT, had a specific inhibitory effect on tumors. Nevertheless, despite the absence of laser treatment, the tumors in this group of mice exhibited some level of suppression but continued to develop. In the IAM + L group, photothermal treatment had a more pronounced ability to suppress tumors. The MIAM + L group had the most pronounced effect as a result of the combination of MLT and PTT, which produced a substantial inhibitory effect on tumors. Upon reaching the advanced stage of therapy, the intensity of the bioluminescence signals emitted by the tumors in this group decreased, accompanied by a reduction in tumor size. This phenomenon was quantitatively analyzed (shown in Fig. 6e).

On the 15th day, after completing therapy, the mice were killed, and the tumors were surgically removed. A sample image of the excised tumor is shown in Fig. 6f, and other resected tumors are shown in Fig. S19. The tumors in the Saline and Laser (L) group exhibited the greatest size, which aligned with the findings of bioluminescence imaging shown in Fig. 6d. Compared with those in the other three groups, the tumors in the MIAM NP group exhibited a slight reduction in size, while the tumors in the IAM + L group showed a greater reduction in size, primarily due to the photothermal effect. In contrast, the tumors in the MIAM + L group were the smallest, indicating a more pronounced antitumor effect resulting from the combined influence of MLT and the photothermal effect.

Throughout the treatment procedure, the body weights of the mice in each group were recorded. As observed in Fig. 6g, the weights of the mice did not exhibit a noticeable decline but rather showed a gradual increase, suggesting that all treatments demonstrated a lack of adverse effects and had favorable biocompatibility. Fig. 6h illustrates the tumor volume development patterns of the mice in each group during the treatment period. The tumors in the Saline and Laser (L) group exhibited the most rapid growth, suggesting that the use of a single laser irradiation did not have any suppressive effect on the tumors. The tumors in the MIAM group had a reduced rate of growth and were partially suppressed. Both the MIAM + L and IAM + L groups had a suppressive effect on tumor development, with the MIAM + L group demonstrating a more pronounced effect. This suggests that the combined chemo/PTT approach provides highly efficient inhibition of tumor growth.

Image analysis of tissue sections

Tissue section analysis was conducted on organ and tumor tissues of mice after therapy. Fig. 6i shows that the H&E staining findings indicate little damage to the tumor tissues in both the Saline and Laser (L) groups. The tumor tissue in the MIAM group exhibited some level of damage, while that in the MIAM + L group and IAM + L group exhibited more severe damage. This is due to the combined effect of MLT, which alleviated tumor hypoxia, and laser irradiation, which enhanced PTT, resulting in significant damage to the tumor tissue and demonstrating a potent tumor-killing effect. As shown in Fig. 6j, the IHC analysis of HIF-1α revealed that the tumor cells in the Saline and Laser (L) groups exhibited hypoxia, whereas those in the MIAM + L group exhibited decreased hypoxia due to its ability to relieve hypoxia. Consequently, the expression of HIF-1α decreased in the MIAM + L group. The MIAM + L group had the most intense green fluorescence in the TUNEL fluorescence study, suggesting that it had the greatest degree of cellular death. The green fluorescence of the IAM + L group was evident, although it was not as intense as that of the MIAM + L group. This suggests that in addition to relieving hypoxia, MIAM NPs can induce cell apoptosis through PTT. These findings support the theory that PTT can not only trigger apoptosis in tumor cells but also alleviate intratumoral hypoxia [48, 49]. These findings demonstrated that mitigating tumor hypoxia might augment the PTT effect. Furthermore, Fig. S20 shows that H&E staining was conducted on the organ tissues of the mice, revealing no apparent tissue damage. This suggests that the nanoprobe exhibited favorable biosafety and biocompatibility.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study developed a nanocarrier with a size of 150 nm using IPD self-assembly that combined NIR-II fluorescence imaging with photothermal conversion capability, as well as MR imaging. It also encapsulated MLT, which helped regulate tumor HIFs, thereby effectively suppressing the effects of hypoxia in tumors. This nanocarrier utilized the IPD for NIR-II fluorescence imaging and Mn2+ for T1-weighted MR imaging, and PTT and delivered MLT were used to alleviate tumor hypoxia. Additionally, the aptamer AS1411 actively targeted tumors to facilitate the accumulation of nanocarriers. The findings from in vitro and in vivo experiments using purposefully modified A549-HRE-Luc cells demonstrated that the combination of MLT and PTT effectively suppressed the effects caused by tumor hypoxia. By employing tumor bioluminescence, these approaches could be demonstrated to regulate the expression of HIFs, stimulate tumor apoptosis, and ultimately restrain tumor growth. The effectiveness of this method could be verified through Western blot, flow cytometry, and tissue section analysis, indicating its potential as a synergistic therapeutic strategy.
