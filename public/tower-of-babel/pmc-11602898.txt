β’-COP mediated loading of PPARγ into trophoblast-derived extracellular vesicles
Authors: Xiaofang Luo, Hao Wang, Biyang Yin, Biao Huang, Jinfeng Cao, Hongbo Qi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Fetal growth restriction (FGR) is characterized by impaired fetal growth and dysregulated lipid metabolism. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have been proved playing a crucial role in transporting biomolecules from the mother to the fetus. However, the mechanisms underlying cargo sorting and loading into trophoblastic EVs remain elusive. This study focuses on examining how the essential fatty acid regulator, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), is sorted and loaded into EVs originating from trophoblasts. We conducted proteomic analysis on placenta-derived EVs from normal and FGR pregnancies. Interactions between PPARγ and coat protein complex I (COPI) subunit were evaluated using co-immunoprecipitation and bioinformatics simulation. Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to identify critical binding sites between β’-coat protein complex I (β’-COP), a subunit of COPI, and PPARγ. lentivirus-mediated knockout and overexpression techniques were employed to elucidate the role of β’-COP in PPARγ loading into EVs. Our findings demonstrate that PPARγ protein levels are significantly decreased in EVs from FGR placentas. β’-COP subunit directly interacts with PPARγ in trophoblasts, mediating its sorting into early endosomes and multivesicular bodies for EVs incorporation. Knockout of β’-COP impaired PPARγ loading into EVs. Molecular dynamics simulations identified critical binding sites for the interaction between β’-COP and PPARγ. Mutation of these sites significantly weakened the β’-COP-PPARγ interaction and reduced PPARγ levels in trophoblastic EVs. In conclusion, β’-COP mediates sorting and loading of PPARγ into trophoblastic EVs. This study provides insights into regulating EVs cargo loading and potential strategies for targeted cargo delivery from the maternal to the fetal circulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-024-05494-8.

Introduction

Fetal growth restriction (FGR1) is characterized by the failure of the fetus to achieve its full growth potential during intrauterine development. FGR affects up to 15% of pregnancies globally [1], while in China, the incidence of FGR stands at 8.7% [2]. FGR fetuses are at an elevated risk of health defects, including developmental delay, impaired development of neurological and cardiovascular system and metabolic disorders such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [3, 4]. Therefore, it is crucial to carry out in-depth studies on the pathogenesis of FGR. An important distinguishing factor between fetuses affected by FGR and those developing normally is the impaired fatty acid metabolism and lipogenesis disorder [5, 6]. FGR fetuses exhibit these metabolic disorders during gestation, highlighting the importance of timely intervention during pregnancy.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are released from late endosomal multivesicular bodies (MVBs) through fusion with the plasma membrane. This fusion enables the release of intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) contained within the MVBs into the extracellular space, thereby generating EVs [7]. The concentrations of EVs in the plasma of pregnant women are over 50 times higher than those in non-pregnant women [8, 9]. EVs play a crucial role as mediators between the mother and fetus, aiding in the regulation of cellular metabolic homeostasis [9, 10]. In addition, EVs are proved to be capable of directly transporting lipids and regulating lipid synthesis through the enrichment of enzymes associated with lipogenesis [11–13]. Alterations in both the circulating levels and content of placental EVs during pregnancy have been correlated with a range of pregnancy complications [14–16]. Specifically, the abundance of EVs isolated from plasma in FGR cases has been shown to be decreased compared to those from normal pregnancies [17–19].

In this study, EVs were isolated from placental tissues, and proteomic analysis was conducted to discern differences in EVs between the placental EVs of pregnancies with FGR and those from normal pregnancies. There were notable discrepancies in protein content related to lipid metabolism, particularly in the expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), which controls the expression of genes involved in the storage and mobilization of lipids [20]. PPARγ is a key regulator of adipogenesis, lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity and is highly expressed in adipocytes [21, 22]. It is also present in placental tissue, where it is crucial for proper placental function and fetal growth. In PPARγ-deficient mice, early developmental abnormalities such as abnormal vascularization, impaired terminal epithelial differentiation of the trophoblast and deficits in lipid accumulation within the placenta [23]. While global deletion of PPARγ in mice results in embryonic lethality, PPARγ-null mice can survive to term if the placental PPARγ is preserved through tetraploid rescue [23, 24]. Additionally, pharmacological inhibition of PPARγ in the latter stages of pregnancy has been shown to induce FGR in rats [25]. These findings underscore the critical role of placental PPARγ in regulating fetal growth throughout pregnancy. Therefore, it is rational to speculate that PPARγ in EVs released by placental trophoblast cells may play a role in lipid regulation in cases of FGR. Understanding the mechanisms by which trophoblastic EVs sort and load PPARγ is essential for unraveling the pathogenesis of FGR. Intervening in this process may present a promising strategy for regulating metabolism during pregnancy and potentially alleviating the impact of FGR on both the mother and fetus.

Materials and methods

Clinical characteristics and sample collection

This research recruited women with FGR, and matched pregnancies from the Department of Obstetrics, the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. FGR is evidenced by a birth weight below the 10th percentile of the growth curve specific to the population [26]. The study obtained informed consent from each participant. The sample collection procedures in the experiment were in accordance with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University (No: 2020 − 788). Human specimen sampling was conducted following a previously outlined procedure with certain modifications [27].

Primary cytotrophoblasts (CTBs) isolation and cell culture

The protocol for isolating primary CTBs from the placenta was described in a previous study [28]. In brief, the maternal surface of the placenta was washed and transferred to a flask containing 1x HBSS (Servicebio, China) and neutral protease II (Roche, Switzerland) for digestion for 45 min. After digestion, the liquid and tissue were filtered through a 70 μm cell filter and centrifuged for 10 min. The pellet was resuspended in 40 ml of platelet lysing solution (Roche, Switzerland). After resting for 8 min, the suspension was transferred to a centrifuge tube containing 5 ml of fetal bovine serum (FBS) and centrifuged. Following another round of centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in 5% percoll (Solarbio, China), filtered, and centrifuged at 1000 g for 20 min. The gradient layers were collected and diluted with DMEM/F12 (Sunncell, China), centrifuged again, and the pellet was resuspended in 2 ml of DMEM/F12. The suspensions were combined, diluted to a total volume of 10 ml, filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and then seeded to the cell culture dishes.

Over the past few decades, various cell lines have been established to investigate the intracellular molecular mechanisms and functions of trophoblasts, including BeWo, JEG-3, HTR8/SVneo, AC1-M59, AC1-M32, ACH-3P, and Swan71. However, none of these cell lines fully replicate the characteristics of primary trophoblast cells [29]. Among them, HTR8/SVneo, an immortalized cell line derived from first-trimester extravillous trophoblasts and infected with simian virus 40 large T antigen (SV40), is the most widely used. Therefore, HTR8/SVneo was selected for our in vitro experiments in this study. HTR8/SVneo was purchased from the ATCC cell bank. The cell culture incubator was set to 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cell culture medium consisted of RPMI 1640 (GIBCO, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (GIBCO, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin-gentamicin solution (Beyotime, China) and changed every other day.

EVs isolation and verification

Human placenta derived EVs were isolated according to the following process. In detail, human placental tissue (100 mg) was homogenized in 1 ml lysis buffer (Thermo Pierce Biotechnology, USA) and immediately diluted 10-fold with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) after homogenization, as shown in supplement Fig. 1A (Fig. S1A). The diluted lysate was centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove unhomogenized tissue and cells. Subsequently, centrifugation at 2,000 g for 10 min, 5,000 g for 30 min, and 10,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C was applied to remove dead cells and cell debris. Then, the supernatant was centrifuged at 100,000 g using an SW32Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter) for 1 h at 4 °C to pellet the EVs, which were then washed with PBS and filtered using a 0.22-µm filter before centrifugation at 100,000 g for 2 h at 4 °C to pellet them again [30, 31]. EVs were isolated from CTBs and HTR8/SVneo cells following established protocols [32, 33]. The morphology of EVs was examined using transmission electron microscopy (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Glacios 2 Cryo-TEM, USA), revealing a typical double-membrane structure. The density and size of EVs were analyzed through Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NS300 system, NanoSight technology, Malver, UK). The total protein content of EVs were quantified with BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Pierce Biotechnology, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instruction after extraction with Lysis Buffer. The content of specific proteins, including biomarkers in EVs, was quantified with western blot using speccific primary antibodies [31].

Proteomic analysis

Following reduction and alkylation, EVs underwent enzymatic digestion with trypsin (Beyotime, China) at a mass ratio of 1:50 for 20 h at 37 °C. The resulting enzymatic products were desalted, lyophilized, redissolved in a 0.1% formic acid solution (MedChemExpress, USA), and stored at -20 °C until further use. Solution A contained 0.1% formic acid in water, while Solution B comprised 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (MedChemExpress, USA) at an 84% concentration, serving as the organic solvent for peptide elution during chromatography. Prior to sample loading onto the Trap column from an autosampler (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Teledyne CETAC, USA), the column was equilibrated with 95% Solution A. Gradient 1 H, a gradient elution program, involved a gradual increase in the proportion of Solution B in the mobile phase over time, typically utilized for the separation of complex samples. After each full scan (MS2 scan), 20 fragment profiles were collected for the analysis of peptide and fragment mass-to-charge ratios.

Binding free energy (BFE) analysis

The study utilized AlphaFold2 for β’-COP structure prediction and retrieved the PPARγ structure from Protein Data Bank (6oni). Protonation was performed under neutral conditions using H + + 3. Protein structure refinement was carried out using PyMOL. Molecular docking was conducted with HawDOCK, which integrates homology search, template-based modeling, and docking. The HawDOCK server utilizes a hybrid algorithm for interaction prediction, employing Fast Fourier Transform-based search for global docking, followed by scoring and MMGBSA calculation. Graphical analysis was done using PyMOL and ChimeraX, while 2D interaction analysis was performed with LigPlus+.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

To investigate the protein-protein interaction between PPARγ and β’-COP, simulations were performed using Gromacs 2019.6. The Amber14SB force field and TIP3P solvent model were utilized, with the proteins contained in a periodic cubic box. The box had a boundary distance of 0.1 nm and was filled with n-decane, water molecules, and counter ions for neutralization. Energy minimization was carried out using the steep algorithm for 50,000 steps, with a force convergence criterion of 1000 kcal/mol/nm. Subsequent simulations were conducted under constant Number of particles, Volume, and Temperature and constant Number of particles, Pressure, and Temperature conditions for 100 ps each, at a temperature of 298 K and pressure of 1 bar. MD simulations utilized the V-rescale thermostat for temperature maintenance and the Parrinello-Rahman barostat for pressure control. Post-simulation analyses included assessing secondary structure, RMSD, RMSF, Rg and SASA.

Mutation analysis

Bioinformatics was used to simulate the mutation of ASP258, LYS273, GLN281, GLY282, PHE285, and ARG286 in the PPARγ protein with alanine. A comprehensive conservation analysis and 3D modeling using AlphaFold DB were conducted to analyze the spatial arrangement of amino acid residues and visualize potential structural changes with PyMOL software.

Immunofluorescence

The samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. Then the samples were blocked with 5% BSA and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied, followed by DAPI staining for nuclei. The samples were sealed with a fluorescence quencher before imaging with a fluorescence microscope to analyze protein localization and expression. The primary antibodies applied in immunofluorescence are available in supplement Table 1. The colocalization coefficients of interested proteins were calculated using Python. In detail, fluorescence images were converted to grayscale and reshaped into 1D arrays for analysis. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated to quantify the linear relationship between the pixel intensities of the two channels. Additionally, a linear regression model was fitted to the data to determine the R-squared value, which indicates the proportion of variance in Channel 2 that is predictable from Channel 1. A scatter plot of pixel intensities from both channels was generated to visually assess colocalization. The R-squared value was reported to provide a quantitative measure of the colocalization. To statistical the difference among groups, we repeated those experiment three times. To avoid bias, in every experiment, each sample were randomly acquired six pictures to calculate the R-squared value and then the average of those six values were settled as the real R-squared values in this sample.

Western blot

Western Blot analysis was conducted following established protocols [34]. Protein isolation was performed through electrophoresis, running at 100 V for 30 min followed by 150 V for 1 h The proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane at 4 °C and a constant current of 200 mA for 2 h. Finally, the membrane was imaged using a ChemiDoc XRS + Gel Imaging System and analyzed with Image Lab 5.2 software. The information about antibodies utilized in Western Blot is available in the supplement Table 2.

RT-qPCR

RNA extraction from HTR8/SVneo cells was performed followed RNA extraction protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RT-qPCR was performed followed the instructions of RT-qPCR kit (Vazyme, China). The primers applied in this study is listed in the supplement Table 3.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

The Co-IP of interested proteins was conducted utilizing a Pierce Crosslink Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The information about antibodies utilized in Co-IP is available in the supplement Table 4.

Virus transfection

Virus transfection was conducted according to the instructions of plasmid virus (OBiO, China). The β’-COP/α-COP/δ-COP-knockout plasmid or PPARγ overexpression plasmid viruses were introduced to the cells at MOI = 30 or MOI = 10. Following a 24 h infection period, the virus-containing medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Subsequently, the cells were treated with puromycin (Beyotime, China) at a concentration of 2 µg/ml for 48 h to establish stable transfected cells.

Statistical analyses

The unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was employed for comparing two groups, while the nonparametric one-way ANOVA was utilized for analyzing differences among multiple groups using GraphPad Prism 9.0. The mean ± SEM is used to present all results. The number of independent experiments carried out for each analysis is indicated in the figure legends. P < 0.05 was used to define significant differences. Each experiment was conducted at least three times.

Results

PPARγ protein expression in FGR placenta derived EVs is impaired

To explore the differences in human placenta derived EVs between normal and FGR pregnancy, the EVs were firstly isolated from normal and FGR term placenta via gradient centrifugation. Electron microscopy showed these that EVs exhibit a typical double-membrane structure (Fig. S1B). Western blot confirmed high expression of EV-specific biomarkers, including CD9, CD81, CD63 and HSP90β as well as the placenta trophoblast biomarker placental alkaline phosphatase (PLAP), While the negative marker Tubulin was barely detectable (Fig. S1C). Moreover, we ascertained that the size of the extracted EVs from normal and FGR term placenta was primarily within the 80 to 130 nm range, as measured by laser scattering using the NanoSight tracking system (NTA, supplement Table 5, Fig. S1D). To identify the proteins enriched in human normal and FGR term placenta derived EVs, we conducted proteomic analysis of EVs derived from human normal and FGR term placenta using Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS). Wayne’s analysis revealed that 634 proteins were commonly expressed in both normal and FGR placenta derived EVs (Fig. 1A). The Gene Ontology (GO) molecular function analysis of the commonly expressed proteins in normal and FGR term placenta derived EVs indicated a predominant focus on protein binding and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 1B). Among the fatty acid metabolism-related proteins, PPARγ was noted for the high expression level and its crucial role in adipogenesis (Tables 1 and 2).

To confirm the expression of PPARγ in the human placenta, primary cytotrophoblasts (CTBs) were isolated from normal and FGR placenta tissues at late pregnancy. The purity of the extracted CTBs was validated by immunofluorescence assay, demonstrating high levels of epithelial cell markers CK7 and E-cadherin (98.09 ± 0.56% and 97.89 ± 0.65% of the cell population, respectively), with minimal expression of the mesenchymal cell marker vimentin (1.85 ± 0.61%) (Fig. 1C). Subsequently, EVs were isolated from primary CTBs (Fig. S1E-G, supplement Table 5). Western blot results showed that PPARγ protein levels in EVs from FGR-derived CTBs as well as FGR CTBs were significantly lower compared to the normal group (Fig. 1D and E), aligning with the lipogenesis disorders in FGR fetuses reported in previous studies [5, 6]. Furthermore, a notable decrease in PPARγ protein expression was observed in EVs derived from FGR placental tissues compared to the normal group (Fig. 1F).

Coat protein complex I (COPI) subunit proteins were enriched in both normal and FGR trophoblast derived EVs

As PPARγ was identified within EVs, we investigated the mechanism by which PPARγ protein enters EVs in trophoblasts. Through proteomic analysis of EVs from normal and FGR term placenta, we identified proteins associated with intracellular protein transport, including COPB2 (β’-COP), ARCN1 (δ-COP), COPG1 (γ-COP), COPA (α-COP) and COPE (ε-COP), all of which are components of the COPI complex (Tables 3 and 4). Western Blot analysis showed that all COPI complex subunits were expressed in both normal and FGR term placenta tissues as well as in placental-derived EVs (Fig. 1G-H). There were no significant differences in their expression levels between the two groups, suggesting that the transportation of PPARγ into EVs may be linked to the COPI complex.

β’-COP interacts directly with PPARγ in trophoblast starting from the early stage of EVs formation

To investigate the role of the COPI complex in sorting PPARγ into EVs, Co-IP was conducted to confirm the interactions between COPI complex subunits and PPARγ. The interaction of each COPI complex subunit with PPARγ was individually examined in primary CTBs from human normal term placenta. The results revealed a direct interaction between PPARγ and the β’-COP subunit in primary CTBs, with no involvement of other subunits (Fig. 2A-G). This association was further supported by reciprocal immunoprecipitation of COPI complex subunits with PPARγ antibody (Fig. 2H). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated co-localization of β’-COP and PPARγ in human placenta tissues and primary CTBs from both normal and FGR groups (Fig. 2I), providing spatial evidence of their binding. These findings indicate a specific interaction between β’-COP and PPARγ in human trophoblast cells.

Studies have shown that EV formation begins with the transition from early endosomes to late endosomes and multivesicular endosomes (LE/MVEs), culminating in EV production [35]. To investigate the stage at which β’-COP and PPARγ interact during EV formation, we performed an immunofluorescence assay to assess their co-localization with the early endosome marker EEA1 and the LE/MVE marker CD63 in HTR8/SVneo trophoblast cells. The assay confirmed the co-localization of PPARγ and β’-COP with EEA1 and CD63 (Fig. 2J-K). Furthermore, a Co-IP assay demonstrated a direct interaction between PPARγ and β’-COP in HTR8/SVneo trophoblast cells (Fig. 2L). These results suggest that the interaction between PPARγ and β’-COP occurs early in EV formation. However, no co-localization was observed with LAMP1, a lysosomal marker, or LC3 puncta, an indicator of autophagosome, in HTR8/SVneo trophoblast cells, indicating that PPARγ and β’-COP specifically localize to early endosomes and MVEs rather than lysosomes or autophagosomes (Fig. 2M-N). In conclusion, β’-COP directly binds to PPARγ in trophoblast cells, influencing PPARγ localization to early endosomes and MVEs during EV formation.

β’-COP mediates ab initio loading of PPARγ into trophoblast EVs

Subsequently, we investigated the role of β’-COP in loading PPARγ into EVs. Through lentiviral transfection, we successfully generated a β’-COP knockout (β’-COP-KO) HTR8/SVneo trophoblast cell line (Fig. 3A-C). The EVs derived from HTR8/SVneo were also characterized (Fig. S1H-J, supplement Table 5). However, immunofluorescence staining revealed a lack of co-localization of PPARγ with EEA1 and CD63 following β’-COP knockout (Fig. 3D-E). Western Blot analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in PPARγ protein expression in trophoblast EVs from the β’-COP-KO group compared to the control group (Fig. 3F). These findings suggest that β’-COP facilitates the transport of PPARγ into EVs during the early stages of EV formation.

β’-COP mediated transportation of PPARγ into EVs relies on intact COPI complex

To investigate the relationship between β’-COP mediated loading of PPARγ into EVs and the COPI complex, we constructed δ-COP knockout (δ-COP-KO) and ε-COP knockout (ε-COP-KO) HTR8/SVneo trophoblast cell lines by lentiviral transfection (Fig. 3G-L). Co-IP assays indicated that the knockout of δ-COP or ε-COP could significantly interfere with the interaction of PPARγ and β’-COP (Fig. 3M-N). Importantly, the deficiency of δ-COP or ε-COP can also result in an inhibition in the EVs transportation of PPARγ in trophoblast which manifested as declined co-localization of PPARγ with EEA1 and CD63, as well as reduced PPARγ protein expression in δ-COP-KO and ε-COP-KO HTR8/SVneo trophoblast (Fig. 3O-S). These findings demonstrated the essential role of intact COPI complex in the loading of PPARγ proteins into EVs.

The binding of PPARγ to β’-COP depends on solvation free energy and van der Waals forces

To investigate the binding modes between PPARγ and β’-COP, we conducted molecular dynamics simulations using Gromacs 2019.6 software. The root means square deviation (RMSD) analysis revealed that equilibrium in the β’-COP-PPARγ binding was reached after 20 ns of simulation (Fig. 4A), with the RMSD value stabilizing around 1.0 nm. Further assessment of the stability of the complex was performed through radius of gyration (Rg) and solvent accessible surface area (SASA) analysis (Fig. 4B-C). The Rg fluctuated within 4.7 nm post 20 ns of simulation, indicating sustained stability of the β’-COP-PPARγ binding complex (Fig. 4B). Additionally, the SASA of the complex remained constant throughout the simulation (after 20 ns) (Fig. 4C), suggesting persistent binding of PPARγ to the β’-COP surface (Fig. 4D-E).

To investigate the binding force between β’-COP and PPARγ, we conducted simulations to analyze hydrogen bond distribution during the binding process (Fig. 4F). The results show that β’-COP-PPARγ typically forms 7–8 hydrogen bonds over the simulation period of 0-100ns, indicating stable binding of PPARγ to the β’-COP protein surface (Fig. 4F-H). Upon further analysis, the BFE between PPARγ and β’-COP was determined to be -38.55 kcal/mol, primarily driven by solvation free energy (ΔGSOLV, -32.87 kcal/mol) and Vander Waals forces (ΔVDWAALS, -31.60 kcal/mol) (Table 5). The Coulomb force was found to be the main repulsive force (ΔEEL, 25.92 kcal/mol) (Fig. 4I; Table 5). Decomposition of the BFE of the β’-COP-PPARγ complex into residues highlighted key amino acids crucial for their interaction (Fig. 4J-K). Nine of these amino acids were identified as hot spots with a binding energy less than − 1 kcal/mol, namely GLU175, HIS178, ASP203, ARG204, ASP207, ASN238, and ARG244 for β’-COP, and LYS273, GYN281, and PHE285 for PPARγ (Fig. 4J-K). This analysis and subsequent bioinformatics investigation identified the binding domains of PPARγ and β’-COP to be located between amino acid residues 258–286 on PPARγ.

In order to identify the essential amino acid binding sites for the PPARγ-β’-COP complex within the 258–286 binding domains, an analysis was conducted to assess the binding energies contributed by amino acid sites within this domain. Through mutation of all amino acids in the binding domain to alanine, we observed a significant impact on the BFE at positions ASP258, ILE279, PHE280, GLY282, PHE285, and ARG286 (Table 6). Overall, our findings highlight the critical role of ASP258, LYS273, GLN281, GLY282, PHE285, and ARG286 in facilitating the transport of PPARγ into EVs mediated by β’-COP.

The mutation of ASP258, LYS273, GLN281, GLY282, PHE285 and ARG286 of PPARγ weaken the binding between PPARγ and β’-COP

To elucidate the functions of the six amino acids in the β’-COP-PPARγ interaction, we employed bioinformatics to simulate the mutations of these six amino acids in the PPARγ protein to alanine. The stability of the system was assessed using RMSD, revealing initial stability from 0 to 30 ns, followed by significant fluctuations after 35 ns (Fig. 5A). These fluctuations were attributed to the impact of mutations on system stability, leading to partial dissociation and substantial RMSD fluctuations. Similar trends were observed for Rg and SASA, with Rg increasing significantly after 35 ns and SASA showing an increasing trend after 30 ns, indicating system instability post-mutation (Fig. 5B-C). Analysis of hydrogen bond distribution revealed a gradual decrease in the number of hydrogen bonds from 8 to 10 at the start of the simulation to approximately 1–2 by the end (Fig. 5D-H), aligning with the dissociation process observed in RMSD, Rg, and SASA.

Confocal microscopy revealed the high transfection efficiency of the overexpressed letivirus in HTR8/SVneo trophoblast cells (Fig. 6B). Additionally, Western Blot and RT-qPCR analysis revealed a significantly overexpression of mCherry, PPARγ and histag both in PPARγM-OE and PPARγ-OE HTR8/SVneo cells (Fig. 6C-D). Co-IP experiment show a lack of interaction between Histag (represent the mCherry-PPARγM-Histag fused protein) and β’-COP after PPARγ mutation (Fig. 6E). In addition, the colocalization of PPARγ and β’-COP in HTR8/SVneo cells also vanished in PPARγM-OE group compared to PPARγ-OE group (Fig. 6F). After mutation, the immunofluorescence assay revealed a significantly reduction in the co-localization of mCherry fluorescent protein with the EEA1 and CD63 in the PPARγM-OE HTR8/SVneo group (Fig. 6G-H). In contrast, the PPARγ-OE HTR8/SVneo group exhibited colocalization of mCherry protein with EEA1 and CD63 (Fig. 6G-H). Western Blot analysis also revealed a significantly reduction of PPARγ expression level in the EVs of PPARγM-OE HTR8/SVneo group compared to the PPARγ-OE HTR8/SVneo group (Fig. 6I). Taken together, these findings indicate that ASP258, LYS273, GLN281, GLY282, PHE285 and ARG286 of PPARγ are crucial amino acid sites for the β’-COP-mediated transport of PPARγ into EVs.

Discussion

In this study, we elucidated the mechanism by which PPARγ is sorted and loaded into trophoblastic EVs through mediation by the β’-COP subunit of the COPI complex. In addition, we demonstrated that the expression of PPARγ was significantly diminished in FGR placenta derived EVs compared to the control group. By employing bioinformatics prediction techniques and Co-IP assay for protein interaction exploration, we identified a direct interaction between β’-COP and PPARγ in human placenta and trophoblasts, which facilitates the recruitment of PPARγ into trophoblastic EVs. Additionally, amino acid mutations of PPARγ revealed that the interaction with β’-COP predominantly hinges on ASP258, LYS273, GLN281, GLY282, PHE285 and ARG286 amino acid residues.

ILV formation and cargo sorting in placental trophoblast cells involve the recruitment of endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) subcomplexes [36, 37]. Recent studies highlight the importance of ESCRT-independent mechanisms, such as lipid raft [38], RAB31 [39], LAMP2A [40], and ceramides [41]. The COPI vesicular coat plays a crucial role in retrograde trafficking from the cis-Golgi to the endoplasmic reticulum and intra-Golgi transport [42]. Cargo incorporation into COPI vesicles is mediated by ADP-ribosylation factor hydrolysis of GTP [43]. Additionally, COPI binds to cargo through recognition of arginine-based signaling motifs with the RXR sequence by β’-COP and δ-COP subunits [44], as confirmed by our findings. However, the mechanism of COPI in EVs sorting and loading, as well as its interaction with PPARγ, remains understudied.

Several potential mechanisms can be proposed based on its established functions in intracellular trafficking. COPI may be involved in the direct recruitment and assembly on precursor membranes, such as endosomal or plasma membranes, through interactions with small GTPases and specific lipid environments [45–47]. This recruitment could facilitate cargo recognition, membrane curvature, and vesicle budding, suggesting a role for COPI in the selective sorting of proteins and lipids into vesicles that are destined to become EVs. Alternatively, COPI could interact with other protein complexes like ESCRT, providing a complementary pathway for cargo selection and EV biogenesis [48, 49]. Additionally, COPI might influence EV formation by regulating the sorting and loading of specific cargo proteins via recognition of sorting motifs or lipid raft microdomains [50, 51]. Such interactions could facilitate the incorporation of selected proteins and lipids into EVs, either through direct involvement in vesicle budding or by modulating other vesicle trafficking pathways. Future studies, including co-immunoprecipitation, proteomics, live-cell imaging, and genetic manipulation, are necessary to elucidate the exact role of COPI in EV biogenesis and to validate these speculative mechanisms. Our research highlights the involvement of COPI in sorting and loading PPARγ, a critical protein in fatty acid metabolism, into EVs of placental trophoblast cells.

The utilization of nanoparticle drugs shows promise for targeted drug therapy aimed at the placenta [52], but limitations exist in their drug delivery potential and the range of materials they can encapsulate. EVs play a crucial role in intercellular communication, offering a mechanism for delivering biological cargo without immune rejection [35]. EVs, with their complex lipid and surface membrane protein composition, effectively target specific tissues, reducing potential drug-related adverse effects [35, 53]. The size distribution variability among EV subpopulations provides options for drug delivery. Challenges remain in standardizing EV production processes and scaling up production [54, 55], as well as addressing biological variability among different sources and types of EVs [56]. Hence, investigating the mechanisms of content sorting and loading by EVs can lay the foundation for regulating EV cargo sorting and advancing extracellular protein encapsulation technologies.

While our study shows promising results on protein sorting and loading into trophoblastic EVs, it is important to note its limitations. Our focus has been on FGR lipid metabolism, but further research on COPI transport for other proteins is needed. Moreover, this study only examined amino acid sites on PPARγ, while crucial binding sites on β’-COP were not investigated. Investigating the key binding site of β’-COP is essential for understanding how molecules are transported into EVs through the COPI complex.

To summarize, this study delves into the molecular mechanism of PPARγ transportation into EVs via β’-COP, offering potential strategies for regulating EVs cargo loading and innovative approaches for drug delivery to the placenta.
