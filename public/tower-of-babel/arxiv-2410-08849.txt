Causal inference targeting a concentration index for studies of health inequalities
Mohammad Ghasempour

Abstract
A concentration index, a standardized covariance between a health variable and relative income ranks, is often used to quantify income-related health inequalities. There is a lack of formal approach to study the effect of an exposure, e.g., education, on such measures of inequality. In this paper we contribute by filling this gap and developing the necessary theory and method. Thus, we define a counterfactual concentration index for different levels of an exposure. We give conditions for the identification of this complex estimand, and then deduce its efficient influence function. This allows us to propose estimators, which are regular asymptotic linear under certain conditions. In particular, we show that these estimators are n \sqrt{n} -consistent and asymptotically normal, as well as locally efficient. The implementation of the estimators is based on the fit of several nuisance functions. The estimators proposed have rate robustness properties allowing for convergence rates slower than n \sqrt{n} -rate for some of the nuisance function fits. The relevance of the asymptotic results for finite samples is studied with simulation experiments. We also present a case study of the effect of education on income-related health inequalities for a Swedish cohort.



Keywords:
Efficient influence function; Gini index; rate robustness; semiparametric efficiency bound; record linked data.

1
Introduction

In the health inequality literature, a concentration index is often used to quantify socioeconomic-related inequality in a given health variable
Y

(see, e.g.,
Ataguba 2022
;
O’Donnell et al. 2008
, and citations therein)
. If a continuous variable
I
(we use income in this paper), is used as a socioeconomic measure, the concentration index

\displaystyle G=\frac{2cov(Y,F_{I}(I))}{\mathbb{E}(Y)},

(1)

is a standardized (valued between -1 and 1) covariance between the health variable
Y\geq 0
and the relative income ranks
F_{I}(I)
; see, e.g.,
Koolman & van Doorslaer 2004
. In a case study described in detail in Section
4
, we use register data on all those born in Sweden during year 1950, and obtain an estimate (using observed relative ranks and sample moments)
\widehat{G}=\numprint{-0.37934}
(s.e.
\numprint{0.007235}
) when
Y
is an indicator of death between age 50 and 60, and
I
is the average yearly income while alive during the same time period. The observed negative sign for
\widehat{G}
can be interpreted as income-related inequality in survival for the 1950 Swedish cohort.
This kind of inequality has been observed for many health indicators earlier in the literature (
Wallar et al. 2020
;
Sommer et al. 2015
;
Schwendicke et al. 2014
;
Ngamaba et al. 2017
). While quantifying inequalities is of interest per say, an important question is to understand their causes. A hypothesis in this context is that increasing educational level in a population could potentially be used to decrease income-related inequalities in health (
Gerdtham et al. 2016
;
Heckley et al. 2016
).
Thus, if
E
denotes education level, a counterfactual scientific question of interest is whether exposing a group of individuals to a given higher level of education would lower inequality in health (less negative concentration index). A naive investigation of this hypothesis is to estimate
G
separately for individuals having different education levels. However, because of confounding this would most probably give a biased answer to the above counterfactual question of interest.

A related literature studies causal pathways explaining health disparities, where for instance, one may be interested in how education can affect health through income where the latter is then a mediator; see
Jackson 2021
;
Jackson & VanderWeele 2018
, and references therein. This is, however, a different question to the one addressed here where a concentration index considers health and income observed simultaneously in time, and thus income is not on the causal pathway (a mediator) between education and health.

In this paper, we thus contribute by developing the theory and methods in order to answer counterfactual questions on concentration index (
1
). Note that
Imbens & Rubin 2015
discussed a counterfactual Gini index
(
Milanovic 1997
)
, which is a specific case of (
1
), where
Y\equiv I
. As Imbens and Rubin, we use here the potential outcome framework
(
Rubin 1974
;
Holland 1986
)
to define a target counterfactual concentration index corresponding to (
1
). We contribute by giving conditions for identification, and deducing the efficient influence function (EIF) of this complex target estimand. This allows us to propose estimators of the counterfactual concentration index, which are regular asymptotic linear under certain conditions
(
van der Vaart 1998
;
Hines et al. 2022
;
Kennedy 2016
)
. In particular, these estimators are
\sqrt{n}
-consistent and asymptotically normal, as well as locally efficient. The implementation of the estimators is based on the fit of several nuisance functions: e.g., regressions of the health and exposure variables on covariates, as well as counterfactual ranks of the income variable. The estimators proposed have rate robustness properties allowing for convergence rates slower than
\sqrt{n}
-rate for some of the nuisance function fits. These results are, however, more complex than similar known rate robustness results for simpler causal estimands such as the widely studied average causal effect of an exposure on an outcome of interest
(
Smucler et al. 2019
;
Kennedy 2016
;
Moosavi et al. 2023
, see, e.g., the reviews by)
. We operationalize two different EIF-based estimators, which necessitate atypical procedures to fit some of the nuisance functions involved. The proposed estimators are compared with the superefficient plug-in estimator in simulation experiments, where robustness properties to misspecification of nuisance models are also investigated.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the target estimand, conditions for identification, deduces the efficient influence functions, propose EIF-based estimators and give asymptotic properties. Section
3
uses simulation experiments to study finite sample properties of the estimators proposed. Section
4
presents a case study where the effect of education on income-related health inequality for Swedes born in 1950 is studied. Section
5
concludes the paper.

2
Theory and method

2.1
Assumptions and identification

For a sample of
n
individuals, we observe two outcomes of interest: a health variable
Y\geq 0
(e.g., survival, disease incidence, hospitalization, etc.), and a socioeconomic variable
I
(continuous, typically income), which we want to relate to study health inequality. Further, we consider an exposure of interest (categorical, e.g., education level
E
), as well as a vector of observed pre-exposure variables
X
. Let
E=0,1,\ldots,K-1
, i.e.
K
levels of exposure are observed. The observed data distribution of
O=(Y,I,E,X)
is denoted
\mathcal{P}
.
For the outcome variables, we define their potential outcomes
(
Rubin 1974
)
under each exposure level:
Y(e)
and
I(e)
,
e=0,\ldots,K-1
. For each individual in the sample, only one pair
(Y(e),I(e))
of these potential outcomes will be observed. We assume consistency, that is the observed outcomes are
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(e)
and
I_{i}=I_{i}(e)
if
E_{i}=e
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, and that there is no interference between individuals
(
Rubin 1991
, see,)
.

We use a concentration index valued between -1 and 1 to measure health inequality with respect to a socioeconomic variable:

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\frac{2cov(Y_{i},F_{I}(I_{i}))}{\mathbb{E}(Y_{i})}=2\frac{\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}F_{I}(I_{i}))}{\mathbb{E}(Y_{i})}-2\mathbb{E}(F_{I}(I_{i}))=2\frac{\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}F_{I}(I_{i}))}{\mathbb{E}(Y_{i})}-1,

where
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i})>0
, and
F_{I}(\cdot)
is the cumulative distribution function of
I_{i}

(
Koolman & van Doorslaer 2004
, e.g.,)
. Interpretation of the index
G
corresponds to the Gini index
(
Milanovic 1997
)
when
Y\equiv I
.

We are interested in the causal effects of an exposure
E

and we use the potential outcomes to define counterfactual versions of the concentration index as follows:

\displaystyle G(e)=2\frac{\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(e)F_{e}(I_{i}(e)))}{\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(e))}-1,

(2)

where expectations are w.r.t. the distributions of the random variables
(Y(e),I(e))
, with
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}(e))>0
, and
F_{e}(\cdot)
is the cumulative distribution function of
I_{i}(e)
.
The causal parameters of interest are the contrasts

\theta(e)=G(e)-G(0),\hskip 28.45274pte=1,\ldots,K-1.

These correspond to the causal effects of different exposure levels.
In an observational study, where the exposure is not randomized, we make the following assumptions to obtain identification.

Assumption 1
.

For
e=0,\ldots,K-1
,

i)

Y(e)\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.69998pt}\hskip-2.5pt\rule[0.0pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-2.5pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.69998pt}\;\,E\mid X
,

ii)

I(e)\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.69998pt}\hskip-2.5pt\rule[0.0pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-2.5pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.69998pt}\;\,E\mid X
,

where ”

” stands for ”independent”
(
Dawid 1979
)
.

Assumption 2
.

Y(e)\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.69998pt}\hskip-2.5pt\rule[0.0pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-2.5pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.69998pt}\;\,I(e)\mid X
, for
e=0,\ldots,K-1
.

Assumption 3
.

Y(e)F_{e}(I(e))\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.69998pt}\hskip-2.5pt\rule[0.0pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-2.5pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.69998pt}\;\,E\mid X
, for
e=0,\ldots,K-1
.

Assumption 4
.

\pi_{e}(x):=\Pr(E=e\mid X)>0
, for
e=1,\ldots,K-1

Assumption
1
states that given a set of observed covariates, the exposure is ignorable for the health outcome

and the socioeconomic outcome
I(e)
.
Assumption

guarantees that all individuals in the study can be exposed to the different levels of

, and are therefore comparable. These are typical assumptions in observational studies, unless other identification information is available, e.g. the existence of mediators or instruments
(
Gorbach et al. 2023
;
Fulcher et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
.
Finally, Assumptions

and
3
are two different alternative assumptions for the identification of the numerator of

.

Note, that a sufficient condition for Assumption
3
to hold is
\{Y(e),I(e)\}\;\,\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.69998pt}\hskip-2.5pt\rule[0.0pt]{6.49994pt}{0.29999pt}\hskip-2.5pt\rule[0.0pt]{0.29999pt}{6.69998pt}\;\,E\mid X
, for
e=0,\ldots,K-1
(a slightly stronger version of Assumption
1
). Assumption
2
may be less realistic than Assumption
3
. However, we consider it as an alternative because it yields simpler estimators, which happen to be robust and behave well when Assumption
3
holds instead of
2

Proposition 1

(Identification)
.

Let Assumptions
1
and

hold, as well as Assumption

. Then,

\displaystyle G(e)\equiv\Psi_{j}(\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=2\frac{A_{j}(\mathcal{P})}{B(\mathcal{P})}-1,

\displaystyle A_{1}(\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}(Y\mid X,E=e)\mathbb{E}(\Xi_{e}(I,\mathcal{P})\mid X,E=e)\},

(4)

\displaystyle A_{2}(\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}(Y\Xi_{e}(I,\mathcal{P})\mid X,E=e)\},

and

Proof.

In the sequel we will not index the expectation operator

as long as the context is clear.
That the identification of

follows from Assumptions

is identified for a given value

We can write:

\displaystyle F_{e}(i)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}(I(e)\leq i)),

where

is the indicator function.
Further,

\displaystyle F_{e}(i)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}(I(e)\leq i)\mid X)\},

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}(I(e)\leq i)\mid X,E=e)\},

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}(I\leq i)\mid X,E=e)\}=:\Xi_{e}(i,\mathcal{P}).

The first equality follows from the law of total expectation, and the second equality is due to Assumptions
1
ii) and
4
, and the last one is due to consistency. The last expression is a functional of the observed data distribution
\mathcal{P}
.

Consider now first the case when Assumption
2
holds, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y(e)F_{e}(I(e)))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}(Y(e)F_{e}(I(e))\mid X)\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}(Y(e)\mid X)\mathbb{E}(F_{e}(I(e))\mid X)\}.

Using further consistency, no interference, and Assumptions

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y(e)F_{e}(I(e)))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}(Y\mid X,E=e)\mathbb{E}(\Xi_{e}(I,\mathcal{P})\mid X,E=e)\}.

The latter expression is a functional of the observed data distribution
\mathcal{P}
.

Consider, finally, the case when Assumption
3
holds instead of
2
. Then, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y(e)F_{e}(I(e)))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}(Y(e)F_{e}(I(e))\mid X,E=e)\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\mathbb{E}(Y\Xi_{e}(I,\mathcal{P})\mid X,E=e)\},

which is a functional of the observed data distribution.
∎

Thus, by this proposition, the two statistical parameters

and

2.3
Asymptotic properties

The estimators (
6
) and (
7
)
are bias corrections of

justified by the von Mises expansion:

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left\{\left[\Psi_{j}(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})-\Psi_{j}(\mathcal{P})\right]+\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\varphi_{j}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})\right]\right\}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\varphi_{j}(O,\mathcal{P})\right]

(8)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{n}(\mathbb{E}_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}})\left[\varphi_{j}(O,\mathcal{P})-\varphi_{j}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})\right]

\displaystyle-\sqrt{n}R_{j}(\mathcal{P},\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}),

\displaystyle N(0,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}[\varphi(O,\mathcal{P})^{2}]),

\Lambda(h):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[M_{e}(X)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[h(I)|X,E=e\right]-\hat{M}_{e}(X)\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(\tilde{I}\mid X)}{\left[h(\tilde{I})|X,E=e\right]}\right],

equipped with the norm
||\Lambda||_{sup}=\sup_{||h||\leq 1}{|\Lambda(h)|}
, assume:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}{[(\Xi_{e}(I,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})-\Xi_{e}(I,\mathcal{P}))^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}}||\Lambda||_{sup}^{\frac{1}{2}}\asymp o_{\mathcal{P}}(\sqrt{n}^{-1}).

x)

The quantities
\hat{M}_{e}(\tilde{X})\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(\tilde{I}\mid\tilde{X})}{\left[\Xi_{e}(\tilde{I},\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})|\tilde{X},E=e\right]}
and
\hat{M}_{e}(\tilde{X})\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(\tilde{I}\mid\tilde{X})}{\left[\Xi_{e}(\tilde{I},\mathcal{P})|\tilde{X},E=e\right]}
belong to a Donsker class, and both are weakly consistent estimators of

M_{e}(X)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}{\left[\Xi_{e}(I,\mathcal{P})|X,E=e\right]}
.

xi)

For the nuisance functional
\Lambda_{2}(h)
, which is defined by

\Lambda_{2}(h):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[Yh(I)|X,E=e\right]-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(\tilde{Y},\tilde{I}\mid X)}{\left[\tilde{Y}h(\tilde{I})|X,E=e\right]}\right],

equipped with the norm
||\Lambda_{2}||_{sup}=\sup_{||F||\leq 1}{|\Lambda_{2}(F)|}
, assume:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}{[(\Xi_{e}(I,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})-\Xi_{e}(I,\mathcal{P}))^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}}||\Lambda_{2}||_{sup}^{\frac{1}{2}}\asymp o_{\mathcal{P}}(\sqrt{n}^{-1}).

xii)

The quantities
\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(\tilde{Y},\tilde{I}\mid\tilde{X})}{\left[\tilde{Y}\Xi_{e}(\tilde{I},\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})|\tilde{X},E=e\right]}
and
\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(\tilde{Y},\tilde{I}\mid\tilde{X})}{\left[\tilde{Y}\Xi_{e}(\tilde{I},\mathcal{P})|\tilde{X},E=e\right]}
belong to a Donsker class, and both are weakly consistent estimators of

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}{\left[Y\Xi_{e}(I,\mathcal{P})|X,E=e\right]}
.

Assumption
5
i) is a product rate condition for the plug-in estimators
B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})
and
\Psi_{1}(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})
. It allows both estimators to be estimated at a lower rate than
\sqrt{n}
.
Assumption
5
iii) is the usual product rate condition for the estimation of
B(\mathcal{P})

(
Farrell 2015
)
.
Assumptions
5
iv)-vii) are further product rate conditions needed for the estimation of
A_{j}(\mathcal{P})
,
j=1,2
. Further, Assumption
5
viii) is a stronger version of the identification Assumption
3
.
Finally, further rate conditions and Donsker conditions are used to control the remainder terms: Assumption
5
ix)-x) for
A_{1}(\mathcal{P})
and Assumption
5
xi)-xii) for
A_{2}(\mathcal{P})
.
As earlier, the Donsker conditions can be discarded if sample splitting is used to estimate the nuisance models in a separate split of the data.

Note that the remainder term can be written as:

\sqrt{n}R_{j}(\mathcal{P},\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})=\sqrt{n}\left(\Psi_{j}(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})+{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\varphi_{j}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})\right]-\Psi_{j}(\mathcal{P})\right),

and can be controlled as follows.

Theorem
2

(Controlling the remainder term)
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
,
4
,
5
i)-v), and
5
viii)-x)

\sqrt{n}\left(\Psi_{1}(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})+{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\varphi_{1}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})\right]-\Psi_{1}(\mathcal{P})\right)\asymp o_{\mathcal{P}}(1).

Further, under Assumptions
1
,
4
,
5
i)-iii),
5
vi)-viii), and
5
xi)-xii)

\sqrt{n}\left(\Psi_{2}(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})+{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\varphi_{2}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})\right]-\Psi_{2}(\mathcal{P})\right)\asymp o_{\mathcal{P}}(1).

See Supplementary Material Section
2
for a proof.

The direct consequence of this last result is that under the assumptions mentioned in this subsection the one-step estimator is regular asymptotic linear, and has asymptotically normal distribution (
9
). Note that this theorem also applies to the estimating equation estimator (
7
), but where the assumptions have to hold for
\hat{\mathcal{P}}^{*}_{n}
, a different empirical distribution. Because the latter is not explicit the assumptions are difficult to verify.

Remark
1
.

The product conditions used in the theorem above imply the following rate robustness properties.
If the nuisance functions
\pi_{e}(X)
and
N_{e}(I,\cal P)
are correctly specified (i.e. fitted at
\sqrt{n}
-rate) then the asymptotic results hold even if the outcome model
M_{e}(X)
and other nuisance functions involving the outcome
Y
are fitted at a slower rate. In such situations, the estimator based on Assumption
2
will be robust, i.e. behave well even when only the weaker and more realistic Assumption
3
holds.
Furthermore, if we have a correct parametric model for the joint distribution of
(Y(e),I(e))\mid X
, then we can estimate the propensity score
\pi_{e}(X)
at a slow rate. Finally, the product conditions in Assumptions
5
ix) and xi) imply that root-
n
convergence for one of the two terms in the products is needed.

3
Simulation study

We perform numerical experiments in order to study the finite sample properties of the estimators proposed.
We consider an exposure
E
with three levels, for which we define three potential incomes
I(0)
,
I(1)
, and
I(2)
, as well as three potential health-related outcomes
Y(0)
,
Y(1)
, and
Y(2)
. All potential outcomes are assumed to be determined by values of two covariates
X_{1}\sim N(1,1)
, and
X_{2}\sim N(10,1)
plus additive normally distributed noises as follows:

\begin{aligned} I(0)&=X_{1}-0.1X_{2}+\epsilon_{0},\\
I(1)&=X_{1}-1.5X_{2}+\epsilon_{1},\\
I(2)&=20X_{1}-X_{2}+10+\epsilon_{2},\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} Y(0)&=10X_{1}+X_{2}+\epsilon_{3},\\
Y(1)&=10X_{1}+X_{2}+8+\epsilon_{4},\\
Y(2)&=10X_{1}+X_{2}+18+\epsilon_{5},\end{aligned}

where
\epsilon_{j}\sim N(0,2)
for
j=0,\dots,5
.
Treatment levels (
E=0,1,2
) are assigned with the probabilities:

\begin{split}\pi_{0}(X)&=\frac{1}{1+\exp(l_{1})+\exp(l_{2})},\\
\pi_{1}(X)&=\frac{\exp(l_{1})}{1+\exp(l_{1})+\exp(l_{2})},\\
\pi_{2}(X)&=\frac{\exp(l_{2})}{1+\exp(l_{1})+\exp(l_{2})},\end{split}

where
l_{1}=(5(X_{1}+X_{2})-55)/(10\sqrt{2})
, and
l_{2}=(3\sqrt{2}X_{1}-43\sqrt{2}+4\sqrt{2}X_{2})/(10\sqrt{2})
.

To approximate the potential concentration indexes for this data generating process, we use a random sample of size one million and obtain
G(0)\approx\numprint{0.12486}
,
\theta(1)\approx\numprint{-0.1887868}
and
\theta(2)\approx\numprint{0.02209007}
. These are used as true values when computing biases and empirical coverages.

We use sample sizes
n=1000,2000
, and 1000 replicates. The estimators studied are the plug-in, and two efficient influence function (EIF) based estimators: the one-step estimator (
6
) (denoted EIF_1S) and the estimating equation estimator (
7
) (denoted EIF_EQ).
For this purpose we use the EIF obtained in Theorem
1
under Assumption
2
, although robustness to the assumption is studied below.
The deployment of these estimators is described in the Supplementary Material, Section
3
, including how the nuisance functions are fitted. All computations are done using the software R
(
R Core Team 2019
)
.

Results on bias, standard errors (both Monte Carlo and estimated), and coverage (using the estimated standard errors) for
\theta(1),\theta(2)
are displayed in Table
1
and
2
for
n=1000,2000
respectively. For completeness, we give also results on the estimation of
G(0)
in Supplementary Material Section
4
, Tables
5
and
6
.
Estimated standard errors are obtained by computing the sample variance of the fitted efficient influence function.
Note that this corresponds to the asymptotic semiparametric efficiency bound.
The plug-in estimator is on the other hand superefficient if the correct nuisance models are used
(
Moosavi et al. 2023
)
and thus using this same standard error may yield conservative empirical coverages in such cases.
To investigate robustness to misspecification of the nuisance functions (Remark
1
) we replace the covariates used to simulate the data with transformations:
\tilde{X}_{j}=\hbox{log}X_{j}^{2}
,
j=1,2
. Thus, the tables include results on four situations: when all nuisance models are correctly specified (labeled ”Correct models”); when only exposure probabilities
\pi_{e}(X)
are misspecified (”Incorrect model for
\pi
”); when only outcome models,
M_{e}(X)
are misspecified (”Incorrect model for
Y
”); when all nuisance functions mentioned in Assumption
5
are misspecified (”Incorrect model for all”).
The results confirm the following expected results from the theory presented: a) the plug-in estimator performs well when
M_{e}(X)
is correctly specified
but can be seriously biased when the latter nuisance model is misspecified, yielding in such cases low coverages; b) the EIF_1S estimator behaves well when only one of the models for
E
or
Y
given
X
is misspecified, yielding low bias and correct coverages; c) none of the methods is unbiased and/or give expected coverage when all models are misspecified. Let us expand on an important aspect of point b) above: using
\tilde{X}_{j}=\hbox{log}X_{j}^{2}
in a model for
Y\mid\tilde{X},E=e
corresponds to a situations where Assumption
2
does not hold because
\tilde{X}_{j}
is not a one-to-one transformation of
X_{j}
. Thus, the results are in line with Remark
1
, where we mentioned the robustness of the estimator EIF_1S to Assumption
2
.

Note that EIF_EQ does not behave as EIF_1S in all our experiments. When the plug-in estimator is biased, while EIF_1S corrects this bias, EIF_EQ corrects it only partially (one can show analytically that the bias correction is approximately half the one of EIF_1S). This is an interesting result per se since these estimators are in many situations equivalent, for instance, for the estimation of
B(\mathcal{P})

(
Hines et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
.

5
Conclusions

We have introduced the theory and method to study counterfactual concentration indexes. This is useful in observational studies of socioeconomic-related health inequalities, where one want to study the effect of exposures and interventions.
In particular, we propose estimators which are regular asymptotic linear and locally efficient under certain conditions. We give their asymptotic normal distribution including the asymptotic variance which can be estimated thereby allowing for valid inference. The estimators proposed require the fit of several nuisance functions, and they have some rate robustness properties, where some of these functions can be fitted at a lower convergence rate than the parametric
\sqrt{n}
-rate. We run finite sample experiments illustrating the behavior of the estimators and their inference.

An interesting observation is the difference in behaviour between the one-step estimator and the estimating equation estimator. While both are based on the efficient influence function, the latter does not fully correct for the bias of the plug-in estimator when nuisance models are misspecified. As we have noted, verifying the assumptions of Theorem
2
is not trivial for the estimating equation estimator, and we suspect that the observed difference in bias is due to the assumptions of Theorem
2
not holding in the misspecified situations studied in our numerical experiments.

In a case study we have applied the methods proposed to study income-related health inequalities for different levels of education. The results indicate that such inequalities exist after correcting for confounding in the Swedish cohort born in 1950, and that inequality decreases for increasing education level.

The terms

and

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}(X)}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}(I\mid X)}\{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}(\tilde{I}\mid\tilde{X})}(\mathbbm{1}(\tilde{I}\leq I)\mid\tilde{X},\tilde{E}=e)\mid X,E=e\}].

The last two items can be fitted with the same method as in the previous section, i.e. by regressing
\mathbbm{1}(i\leq\tilde{i})
on
x_{i},x_{\tilde{i}}
, while
\Xi_{e}(i,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})
can be fitted with the following algorithm for a given income
i
:

1.

Start by regressing
\mathbbm{1}(\tilde{I}\leq i)
on
\tilde{X}

for
(\tilde{X},\tilde{I})\in\{(x_{j},i_{j}),j=1,\dots,n_{e}\}
(subsample with E=e), with, e.g., a logit link.

2.

Use the fitted model to predict the values:
\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}(\tilde{I}\leq i)\mid\tilde{X})
for the whole sample
\{(x_{j},i_{j}),j=1,\dots,n\}
.

3.

Take the average
\mathbb{E}_{n}\{\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n}}(\mathbbm{1}(\tilde{I}\leq i)\mid\tilde{X})\}
, to obtain the estimation of
\Xi_{e}(i,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})
.

The terms
A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})
and
B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})
are the plug-in estimators described in the previous section. Finally,
\varphi{{}_{B}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})
is estimated using
\hat{\pi}_{e}(X)
and
\hat{M}_{e}(X)
.

3.3
Estimating equation estimator

The estimating equation estimator solves (
7
). We can write:

\mathbb{E}_{n}[\varphi{{}_{j}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})]=0\Rightarrow\frac{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}\left(\frac{E_{n}[\varphi{{}_{A}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})]}{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}-\frac{E_{n}[\varphi{{}_{B}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})]}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}\right)=0.

Thus for the uncentered influence function, the equation is:

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{E}_{n}[\varphi{{}_{j}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})]+2\frac{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}=2\frac{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}\\
\Rightarrow\frac{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}\left(\frac{E_{n}[\varphi{{}_{A}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})]}{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}-\frac{E_{n}[\varphi{{}_{B}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})]}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}\right)+2\frac{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}=2\frac{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}\\
\Rightarrow\frac{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}\frac{E_{n}[\varphi{{}_{A}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})]}{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}+2\frac{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}=\frac{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}\left(\frac{E_{n}[\varphi{{}_{B}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})]}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}+2\right).\end{array}

The equation can be solved by equating the following ratios:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{n}[\varphi{{}_{A}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})]+2A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}=\frac{A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}\left[\frac{\mathbb{E}_{n}[\varphi{{}_{B}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})]+2B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}\right]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\widehat{\left(\frac{A}{B}\right)}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{n}[\varphi{{}_{A}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})]+2A(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}{\mathbb{E}_{n}[\varphi{{}_{B}}(O,\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})]+2B(\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{n})}.

The latter yields an estimator for
\Psi
when using the fits of nuisance models described in the previous section.

3.4
Further details on fitting the nuisance functions

In the simulation study, a multinomial logistic regression is used to fit the propensity score, and a linear model is used for the potential health outcome. The remaining nuisance models are fitted as described above.

In the case study, the only differences are that for the potential health outcomes, a zero-inflated Poisson regression including all second order interaction terms is considered with
L_{1}
regularization, using the R-package
mpath
, and similarly for the multinomial model for the propensity score, using the R-package
glmnet
. All analyses in this case study are done using R
(
R Core Team 2019
)
, and SparkR
(
The Apache Software Foundation 2024
)
. The latter is needed to fit the binary response regressions in the first algorithm described above.
For instance, for the second education level the size of the training set is 2’250’079’225, which makes it almost impossible to do it on typically available RAM when using regular packages in R.
