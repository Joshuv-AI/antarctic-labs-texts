Spatiotemporal deformation trend assessment based on ICOPS and ICA methods using multitemporal InSAR data in industrial development zones
Authors: Muhammad Fulki Fadhillah, Wahyu Luqmanul Hakim, Seul-ki Lee, Bokyun Ko, Young-Hoon Jung, Kwang-Jae Lee, Seung-Jae Lee, Sung-Ho Chae, Chang-Wook Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The expansion of the industrial sector has created a need for comprehensive infrastructure development. Remote sensing technology, particularly synthetic aperture radar (SAR), has shown substantial importance in the analysis of surface deformation in the context of urban development. A comprehensive study was conducted in the Bugok industrial area, South Korea, in which improved combined scatterer interferometry with optimized point scatterer (ICOPS) methodology was implemented. The ICOPS methodology demonstrated the reliability of using machine learning algorithms in refining postprocessing time series. This investigation utilized Sentinel-1 SAR satellite data from ascending and descending tracks, and Cosmo SkyMed (CSK) acquired from 2014 to 2022. Furthermore, the independent component analysis (ICA) method was used to decompose the dominant trend and clustering of deformation data. The implementation of the ICOPS method facilitated the visualization of linear and nonlinear spatiotemporal deformation during the observation period. The time-series analysis indicated subsidence with a magnitude of 0.76–2.83 cm/year, reaching a maximum cumulative deformation of approximately 20 cm. Based on the principal component data on vertical deformation, there are seasonal, linear‒seasonal, and exponential trend characteristics in the study area. The contributing factors include the high density of industrial structures and intensive groundwater extraction, resulting in increased loads on soil structures and consequently continued subsidence in the linear cluster area. Furthermore, this study underlines the potential application of the InSAR methodology in the context of postconstruction infrastructure monitoring, contributing significantly to risk management and estimation.

Introduction

Land reclamation in coastal areas is a method of solving land availability problems in several countries, such as China1,2, Singapore3, the Netherlands4, and Indonesia5. The process of land reclamation converts coastal wetlands for various uses, including agricultural development6, industrial activities7,8, and urban expansion9–11. Topographic conditions, economic factors, tourism, and sea-level rise make reclamation necessary in coastal areas12,13. Areas were reclaimed by filling in regions of the coastal zone14,15. This infilling process threatens the stability and security of structures built on reclaimed land. The subsequent construction of infrastructure and facilities on this newly added alluvium compresses the soil, resulting in land subsidence16. Several previous studies have monitored the subsidence rate of reclaimed land at specific times. The Noksan National Industrial Complex, South Korea, a reclaimed area, has experienced subsidence of approximately 20.9 mm/year since it was first built17. Similarly, the subsidence rate in reclaimed regions in the Jakarta coastal area ranged from 30 to 40 mm/year between 2017 and 202018. Considering the potential losses, ground deformation monitoring in reclaimed areas is needed to prevent future damage.

In the last few decades, remote sensing data have contributed to increased research on the deformation of the Earth’s surface. One such method is interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR), which has proven to be a reliable technique and has been used to investigate deformation changes caused by natural or anthropogenic processes19. The goal of ​​utilizing time series in InSAR analysis is to overcome intrinsic geometric and temporal decorrelation problems, which simultaneously reduce the estimation of atmospheric effects commonly encountered with conventional InSAR methods20,21. The time-series InSAR technique has demonstrated its ability to explore land surface changes in various applications, such as mining, groundwater extraction, and land reclamation, such as Chek Lap Kok Airport in Hong Kong, Sibari in southern Italy, and the Venice Coast22–26.

In general, the InSAR time-series approach uses a point scatterer technique as a base, divided into persistent point and distributed scatterers. A persistent scatterer-based technique can evaluate high-quality pixels in the stable phase region to form a time series of deformation and deformation rates at multiple temporal resolutions, which the advantages can be seen in Table 127. However, due to the need for a stable phase state over a long temporal period, the number of analyzable pixels can be reduced. To overcome this problem, distributed scatters (DS) points are combined with PS points to increase the density of the target points introduced by Ferretti28. This new generation method was developed to maximize the spatial capacity of the deformation signal by combining the DS and PS deformation signals based on the statistical characteristics of the pixel. Although a promising approach has been presented, the process is not without its limitations, such as the large image dataset required and the absence of postprocessing to ensure measurement accuracy29–31.

The increasing availability of big data from remote sensing has presented processing challenges and increased interest in developing more efficient methods for generating susceptibility maps. Recently, machine learning approaches have been widely utilized to generate susceptibility maps for disasters such as landslides, land subsidence, and forest fires34–36. One key aspect of concern is the reliability of big data processing, where deep learning offers an effective solution to address the challenges in developing disaster susceptibility maps. One prominent approach is the use of the U-Net architecture, which is effective in segmenting spatial data to generate vulnerability maps with high accuracy37. Furthermore, hybrid approaches combining deep learning with metaheuristic optimization have been applied to improve the reliability and efficiency of big data processing, enabling more optimal model refinement in handling the complexity of spatial data38,39.

The Bugok Industrial Complex, a prominent industrial hub in South Korea, is situated on reclaimed coastal land and hosts a dense concentration of heavy machinery industries. Between 2018 and 2019, tunneling operations were conducted to upgrade high-voltage power infrastructure; however, these activities affect significant land subsidence, prompting government intervention to halt construction. A critical challenge in assessing this hazard lies in the complex geological context: as a reclaimed area, the site is naturally subject to long-term consolidation driven by sediment compaction and structural loading. Consequently, the observed ground subsidence cannot be exclusively attributed to construction activities without rigorous analysis. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to investigate the specific contribution of cable trench excavation to the subsidence in the vicinity. The novelty of this research lies in utilizing the improved combined scatterer interferometry with optimized point scatterer (ICOPS) approach to distinguishing these deformation mechanisms. This method allows for the distinct characterization of deformation patterns: anthropogenic construction impacts such as constructions activity are expected to manifest as sudden deformation, nonlinear deformation phases were detected, whereas natural consolidation or groundwater-related compaction is hypothesized to exhibit a continuous, linear trend over time.

Then, ICOPS method was utilized to generate high-density time-series data, followed by independent component analysis (ICA) to decompose the dominant deformation signals. Furthermore, to provide a comprehensive risk management context, this study incorporates a land subsidence susceptibility map (LSM) using a hybrid deep learning approach as a complementary tool to validate the relationship between observed deformation and environmental influencing factors. Thus, in this study, we attempt to integrate machine learning-based optimization techniques, specifically ICOPS, with the development of the InSAR method. PS and DS were combined to create the foundation for time-series surface deformation analysis, resulting in the establishment of combined scatterers interferometry (CSI). To increase reliability, we then employed a convolutional neural network (CNN) and the gray wolf optimization algorithm, a machine learning approach, for postprocessing at the measurement points. Then, we utilized independent component analysis (ICA) to estimate the different dominance trends in the surface deformation from the time-series InSAR data according to the source deformation characteristics40. This study describes the initial implementation of the ICOPS method in the Bugok Industrial Complex, South Korea, utilizing SAR data from 2014 to 2022. Combining the Sentinel-1 (C-band) and CSK (X-band) datasets provides a detailed description of ground surface deformation. We compare the small baseline and subset (SBAS) as the DS-based time-series method, ICOPS, and in situ analysis (leveling) methodologies to validate the InSAR time-series data and suggest enhancements for the ICOPS. Moreover, time series decomposition was applied to analyze the appropriate approach to describe the surface deformation pattern in this area41. Also, for comprehensive knowledge, we generate the land subsidence susceptibility map based on a hybrid-UNet al.gorithm using integrated remote sensing data. This study includes exploratory data that links surface deformation observations with the influencing factors of anthropogenic and natural processes around the study area.

Data and methods

Study area

The Asan National Industrial Complex is located on the southern coast of Asan Bay, Chungcheonnam Province, South Korea (Fig. 1a). The study area (Fig. 1e) is the result of intensive land reclamation in the 1980s, now housing over 30 heavy industrial factories. Between 2018 and 2019, tunneling was carried out to support high-voltage power needs, but this caused land subsidence around the Bugok Industrial Complex which raise the government to stop the constructions. Then, two sites associated with the underground cable tunnel were identified for detailed deformation monitoring shown in Fig. 1e. the vertical shaft launch pit (referred to as P1) and the receiver pit (referred to as P2). These sites represent distinct environmental contexts; P1 is situated in the primary zone of construction activity, influenced by anthropogenic excavation process. In contrast, P2 is located in an area with overlying historical stream channels that existed prior to reclamation, where surface stability is primarily governed by the geological consolidation of the underlying soft alluvial soil. The area is surrounded by small hills with rivers flowing into Asan Bay, where annual tides (1.98–8.39 m) affect groundwater fluctuations42.

The geology of the study area consists of Precambrian metamorphic rocks and Quaternary alluvium43. According to their composition and metamorphic grade, the metamorphic rocks are divided into Songak schist and Pyeongtaek magmatic gneiss. The Songak schist consists mainly of mica schist, banded gneiss, and calc-schist, and minor amounts of quartzite and lime silicate are present. The Pyeongtaek magmatic gneiss gradually contacts the Songak schist and consists of banded gneiss with a small amount of graphite. Industrial development was fully completed by 2000, resulting in the current topography, and the Bugok industrial district of the Asan National Industrial Complex was built on reclaimed land in 2000. A detailed description of the geological characteristics of the study area is provided in Fig. 1b. According to the investigation of the industrial district in 2000, the subsurface structure is composed of five layers: a reclaimed layer, a sediment layer, a weathered layer, soft rock, and hard rock. Another investigation in 2020 indicated a decrease in the thickness of the reclaimed and sediment layers during the last 20 years9. Furthermore, some parts of soft rock have low rock quality designation (RQD) values (< 10%) and are classified as fracture zones. The fracture zones are also detected by tomography using cross-well seismic data44, as shown in Table 2. The fracture zone’s coefficient of permeability (k = 1.76 × 10⁻⁴ cm/sec) is lower than that of soft rock (k = 0.523 × 10⁻⁴ cm/sec), leading to impeded drainage and groundwater overflow during tidal or rainfall events44. Land reclamation (infilling) was completed in the mid-1980s, but full conversion to industrial use occurred gradually. Fig. 1c (1991) shows residual salt pans and agricultural land, reflecting the transitional phase. Fig. 1c,d shows aerial images: 1991 (Fig. 1c) when the area was still salt pans and agricultural land, and 1999 (Fig. 1d) after land reclamation was completed. The development of the industrial complex was completed in 2000, creating the current topography. Long-term land subsidence is influenced by compaction of loose reclaimed soil and consolidation of soft clay beneath the embankment, exacerbated by construction activities and groundwater dynamics.

Datasets

Two SAR missions, one set of X-band data and two tracks of C-band data, were used for surface deformation analysis. The first dataset corresponds to 85 Cosmo SkyMed (CSK) images acquired from 27 June 2014 to 01 August 2021, along ascending tracks. The second dataset included 164 and 180 Sentinel-1 images from an ascending and descending orbit, respectively, between October 2014 and January 2022. The descriptions of the parameters used in Sentinel-1 and CSK are shown in Table 3. In this study, we used a Copernicus digital elevation model (DEM) with a resolution of 30 m to create a topographical phase for the Bugok industrial complex45. For the Sentinel-1 data in the ascending and descending tracks, we generated 580 and 595 interferogram pairs, respectively, and for the CSK data, we analyzed the 115 interferogram pairs in this area.

Method

To reveal the surface deformation characteristics in the study area, we utilized the ICOPS method for deriving the time-series analysis from the multitemporal SAR data. The ICOPS method utilizes the PS and DS information and refines it with the machine learning approach to obtain the final solution. Moreover, the source deformation analysis was conducted using the ICA algorithm. Figure 3 presents an illustration of the main processing workflow used in this study. The comparison between ICOPS, SBAS, and in situ leveling serves two purposes: (1) to validate the accuracy and reliability of ICOPS in capturing both linear and nonlinear deformation in mixed scatterer environments, and (2) to demonstrate the advantage of machine learning-based point optimization in reducing noise and improving the spatial density of reliable measurements.

This approach is optimized with machine learning algorithms to overcome the limitations of the point density of measurement points (MP) and improve the determination of MP through spatial cluster analysis33.

A land subsidence susceptibility map was produced at 10 m spatial resolution using fourteen conditioning factors selected for their established influence on consolidation settlement and long-term ground deformation in the study area shown in Table 4. The subsidence inventory was constructed from InSAR time-series analysis namely ICOPS, with subsidence points delineated where deformation rates exceeded 1.5 cm/yr. Land subsidence related factors descriptions.

The generation of the land subsidence susceptibility map relied on the rigorous integration of fourteen conditioning factors, each selected to represent specific geomorphological, hydrological, and geotechnical controls on ground deformation. Elevation was utilized as a primary proxy for the depositional environment, as lower topographic elevations typically correspond to basins filled with thick accumulations of under-consolidated16. Slope angle was included because steeper gradients induce greater gravitational shear stress, thereby promoting lateral strain and increasing the susceptibility of soft soils to creep and differential settlement27. The LS-factor was incorporated to quantify the combined impact of slope length and steepness on hydrological processes, specifically serving as an indicator of runoff velocity and the potential for indirect pore-pressure buildup in toe-slope areas. Plan curvature was analyzed because concave surface geometries effectively converge subsurface flow pathways, sustaining elevated soil moisture levels that degrade soil stiffness and shear strength over time59. Profile curvature was selected to characterize the rate of slope change, where convex profiles are mechanically associated with tension zones prone to cracking, while concave profiles facilitate water retention and sediment saturation. The topographic wetness index (TWI) was integrated to explicitly model the spatial distribution of saturation zones; high TWI values correlate with prolonged pore-water pressure, which directly reduces the effective stress acting on the soil skeleton60. Then, terrain ruggedness index (TRI) was considered to capture high-frequency topographic variability, which serves as a reliable indicator of heterogeneous sediment thickness and complex consolidation patterns that lead to uneven surface deformation.

Regarding subsurface material properties, soil drainage was mapped because the hydraulic conductivity of the soil matrix governs the rate of consolidation; poorly drained soils resulting in prolonged primary settlement and significant secondary compression. Soil thickness was included as a critical geometric variable, as the magnitude of total settlement is directly proportional to the depth of the compressible soil column under self-weight or external loading. Soil material was incorporated to account for lithological variations in compressibility, specifically identifying clay-rich and highly organic soils that possess high void ratios making them far more susceptible to volume reduction than coarse-grained materials61.

Furthermore, anthropogenic factor was accounted for through Land Use and Land Cover (LULC), which differentiates areas subjected to the sustained static surcharge of dense urban infrastructure from those with negligible loading, such as agricultural or forested lands5. Distance to roads was also included to represent the dynamic impact of transportation networks, where traffic-induced cyclic loading and vibration can induce particle reorientation and densification in adjacent soils62. Depth to groundwater was included to capture the fundamental mechanics of effective stress; shallower water tables exert buoyant forces, and any subsequent fluctuation whether natural or pumping-induced triggers stress cycles that accelerate63.

Moreover, Areas under active construction were excluded from the subsidence inventory to avoid conflating transient construction-induced displacement with long-term geotechnical subsidence, ensuring that the susceptibility map reflects persistent risk factors such as soil type and groundwater dynamics rather than temporary loading effects.

Training (70%) and validation (30%) sets of the inventory data were randomly selected. A land subsidence model was created using the U-Net algorithm and a meta-heuristic approach64,65. We evaluated the topographical factors, hydro-geological, and anthropogenic factors to evaluate the land subsidence susceptibility map. Prior to modeling, factors were categorized into five classes, and the association between each factor and past land subsidence events was evaluated using spatial correlation analysis61. Additionally, factor class weights were assessed using spatial correlation analysis, which computed frequency ratios to quantify spatial correlation for selecting land subsidence factors. The percentage of cells with land subsidence in each class relative to the overall area is shown by this ratio. The factors were then reclassified, with a ratio > 1 denoting a strong relationship and a ratio < 1 indicating the opposite. A learning scheme using U-Net and meta-heuristics produced a model once the data had been transformed into a statistical format. To ensure the accuracy of the vulnerability map, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to assess the model’s predictive ability. To improve the predictability and transparency of the results, SHAP analysis as Explainable AI (xAI) was utilized to explain the role of each factor in the model66.

Time-series deformation analysis

Surface deformation analysis in the Bugok industrial complex using SAR data from Sentinel-1 (S-1) and Cosmo SkyMed (CSK) for the period 2014–2022 shows the spatial distribution and trend of land subsidence. Time series data were analyzed using the ICOPS and SBAS methods to evaluate vertical deformation in the P1 and P2 areas by neglecting the contribution of horizontal movement. Structurally, P1 corresponds to the launch pit for the underground cable tunnel, while P2 represents the receiver pit cable tunnel. Figure. 5a–d present deformation graphs from S-1 on the ascending and descending trajectories. In P1, situated in the primary construction zone, the deformation trend is nonlinear and driven largely by anthropogenic activities, specifically the tunneling constructions. Slow deformation was recorded in 2014–2017, with a subsidence of 1.8 cm (ICOPS, ascending track) and 2.1 cm (ICOPS, descending track). From mid-2017 to 2019, deformation increased sharply to a maximum of 11.9 cm and 11.2 cm. After that, there was a rebound in 2019–2020, followed by stability until 2022. This indicates a transition from active subsidence to relative stabilization halt(stop)-construction. SBAS recorded a maximum deformation of 10.7 cm (ascending) and 9.9 cm (descending), indicating a nonlinear trend that is likely related to construction activities. Conversely, P2 is located in an area overlying historical stream channels that existed prior to reclamation. Consequently, the deformation here is primarily influenced by geological and groundwater condition factors, specifically the continuous compaction of the soft alluvial soil. In this area (northern Dangjin), cumulative subsidence reached 8.1 cm (ascending) and 7.8 cm (descending) according to ICOPS, with linear rates of 1.2 cm/year (ascending) and 1.1 cm/year (descending) from 2014 to 2022. Significant subsidence was detected post-2017, consistent on both S-1 trajectories44,67. The CSK analysis (2014–2021) in Fig. 5e–f confirms these findings. In P1, the CSK shows a nonlinear trend: slow deformation (2014–2017), acceleration (2017–2019), and subsidence (2019–2021), with an accumulation of 10.3 cm and a rate of 1.7 cm/year. This acceleration is correlated with the construction in 2017–2019. In P2, a maximum subsidence of 8.7 cm was recorded with a rate of 1.2 cm/year, consistent with soft soil prone to compaction.

The nonlinear dynamics of deformation are closely coupled with the construction chronology. During the active construction phase (2017–2019), the tunnel excavation required significant dewatering, which reduced pore pressure and triggered rapid subsidence. Following the halt of excavation in June 2019, the active extraction of groundwater ceased. This cessation allowed for the gradual recovery of the local water table and a restoration of hydrostatic pressure within the aquifer system. The subsequent recovery of hydrostatic pressure triggered a poroelastic rebound, resulting in stabilization and slight uplift trends observed in the P1 time series from late 2019 to 2021. This contrasts with the background linear subsidence trend driven by long-term natural soil consolidation. These are supported by local hydrogeological reports and references44,67. While causal links are inferred from spatiotemporal correlation, ongoing monitoring is recommended to assess long-term stability and inform risk management protocols. Furthermore, there are some limitations during the time series processing from two methods in complex deformation environments. SBAS typically employs temporal smoothing and linear deformation assumptions to maximize coherence, which reduces its sensitivity to abrupt or nonlinear changes. Consequently, it may produce smoothing artifacts or lag in detecting rapid deformation onsets. In contrast, ICOPS, by integrating optimized point scatterers and machine learning refinement, effectively captures these nonlinear dynamics, providing a more precise delineation of the deformation timeline compared to the smoothed SBAS results.

Spatiotemporal trend component clustering analysis

Further analysis was carried out by processing the ICOPS time-series deformation measurements from Sentinel-1, which ascending track and descending track via decomposition to 2-dimensional deformation in the horizontal (east‒west) direction and vertical directions68. All the results were resampled using the Copernicus DEM with a 30-m resolution for horizontal and vertical time-series surface deformation data from the Sentinel-1 datasets. The decomposition method was evaluated after generating the mean deformation in the ascending and descending tracks of Sentinel-1, as shown in Fig. 6a and b, respectively. This study used 6,856 measurement points from 164 to 180 Sentinel-1 datasets (ascending and descending trajectories) to analyze surface deformation in the Bugok industrial complex. The data were decomposed into horizontal and vertical components, then processed with PCA to reduce complexity, resulting in four principal components per direction. ICA then separated independent signals from the time series for deformation clustering, as shown in Fig. 6c f. Figure. 6e displays four horizontal components: IC1 and IC2 show positive trends (east) with moderate slopes, while IC3 and IC4 show negative trends (west). IC4 in particular displays a linear pattern in 2016–2019. Horizontal deformation was estimated using 2D decomposition of LOS displacements from combined ascending and descending Sentinel-1 data. East-west motion was minor ( < ± 3 mm/yr), consistent with dominant vertical consolidation in soft reclaimed soils. While shallow tunneling can induce horizontal displacement, the observed patterns were primarily vertical, likely due to uniform loading and high compressibility of alluvium.

In the vertical direction (Fig. 6f), the independent components reveal distinct hydro-mechanical mechanisms. IC1 and IC2 exhibit strong seasonal periodicities with weak linear trends. These are interpreted as the poroelastic response of the aquifer system to seasonal groundwater fluctuations (natural recharge and discharge cycles), rather than thermal effects. IC3 reflects a linear decline superimposed with seasonal effects, indicative of the long-term consolidation of the soft alluvial soil influenced by variations in the water table. IC4, specifically, captures a rapid exponential subsidence trend from 2017 to 2019, followed by stabilization until 2022. This component is spatially concentrated in the north and south of the excavation area and represents the direct impact of anthropogenic dewatering. The active pumping during construction reduced pore pressure within the high-permeability fracture zones (k = 1.76 × 10-4 cm/sec), accelerating consolidation. The statistical independence of these components was verified using the FastICA algorithm by maximizing non-Gaussianity, confirming that the seasonal groundwater signals (IC1–IC2) are distinct from the construction-induced dewatering trend (IC4)27,69. These findings underscore the complexity of surface deformation, influenced by geological and development factors.

Discussion

Subsidence driving mechanism

The physical mechanism driving this subsidence is primarily attributed to the reduction of pore water pressure caused by construction-related groundwater usage for dewatering44. The excavation process required the extraction of groundwater, which increased the effective stress on the highly compressible Quaternary alluvium and reclaimed soil layers, leading to rapid hydro-mechanical consolidation63,70. This phenomenon is consistent with findings in other regions characterized by similar soft soil conditions. For instance, Yoo et al. (2016) observed in Seoul metro that groundwater drawdown during tunnelling in saturated soil greatly increases surface settlement through consolidation caused by rising effective stress71. Similarly, Shen et al. (2014) demonstrated that in soft alluvial environments, groundwater leakage and dewatering during tunnelling operations are the critical drivers of increasing effective stress, which directly amplifies surface subsidence72. Furthermore, Zhang et al. (2016) highlighted that in water-rich soft ground, the disturbance of the hydro-geological equilibrium significantly contributes to long-term deformation73. The temporal correlation in our study where rapid subsidence coincided with the active dewatering phase and stabilized upon its cessation or stop aligns with these findings, confirming that pore-pressure reduction in the soft alluvial soil was the dominant deformation mechanism. The influence of groundwater dynamics on surface deformation is the dominant factor in the observed spatiotemporal patterns. Hydrogeological data indicates the presence of extensive fracture zones in the bedrock with a permeability coefficient of k = 1.76 × 10⁻⁴ cm/sec, significantly higher than that of the surrounding soft rock44. During the tunnel excavation (2017–2019), these high-permeability zones acted as conduits for groundwater inflow, necessitating intensive dewatering measures. The resulting drawdown of the water table caused a reduction in pore water pressure within the overlying quaternary alluvium. According to the principle of effective stress, this reduction transferred the overburden load to the soil skeleton, triggering rapid consolidation and subsidence74.

The analysis of subsidence distribution based on historical aerial image data from 1946, as shown through a map of the distribution of deformation measurement points in the study area (Fig. 8a). This map illustrates the spatial distribution of measurement points using colocation analysis, where areas experiencing subsidence (red) are concentrated around the historical stream path before reclamation, especially along the river flow, while insignificant areas (yellow) are scattered in higher areas. Physically, this active drainage reduced the pore water pressure within the soft alluvial layers identified in the historical aerial imagery (Fig. 8a). According to Terzaghi’s principle of effective stress, this reduction in pore pressure resulted in a corresponding increase in the effective stress acting on the soil74. In the context of the highly compressible paleochannel deposits found in the study area, this stress increase triggered significant consolidation settlement, effectively reducing the soil’s void ratio and stability during the excavation phase. Furthermore, a correlation analysis was carried out to evaluate the relationship between non-anthropogenic land deformation (other than cluster 4) and the presence of historical streams, as shown in the scatter plot and histogram (Figure. 8b). The scatter plot revealed a strong positive correlation between land deformation and variables related to historical streams, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.719. The histogram on the right side of the scatter plot shows a left-skewed distribution of land deformation, with most deformation values ranging from − 40 to 0 mm/year, indicating that subsidence predominantly occurs in areas adjacent to historical streams. This approach allows for a clearer identification of the relationship between subsidence and historical streams, thereby improving the accuracy of subsidence susceptibility analysis and supporting more effective mitigation planning in the study area.

Analysis of the InSAR time-series performance

InSAR time series deformation data from Sentinel-1 and Cosmo SkyMed were validated through cross-correlation to assess the consistency and accuracy of the SBAS and ICOPS methods. To ensure geometric consistency, this comparison utilized only the Sentinel-1 Ascending track to align with the Cosmo SkyMed Ascending track. For both datasets, the Line-of-Sight (LOS) displacement was projected to a vertical direction by neglecting the horizontal contribution. This single-geometry approach ensures a direct comparison of the sensor capabilities without the complexity of 2D vector decomposition used in the trend analysis. The average deformation map was resampled at 30 m resolution, and the LOS displacement was converted to vertical direction to standardize the satellite data using ascending tracks data The correlation analysis followed a rigorous spatial collocation procedure to ensure geometric and statistical consistency between the datasets. First, the optimized Measurement Points (MPs) were filtered using the ICOPS quality thresholds specifically requiring a temporal coherence (TCOH) > 0.65 and a amplitude stability > 0.7 to retain only pixels with high phase stability. To address the varying spatial resolutions of the sensors, the resulting deformation maps were resampled to a common 30-m grid resolution. Subsequently, a spatial intersection was performed to identify common grid cells that contained valid MPs in both the Sentinel-1 ascending and Cosmo SkyMed ascending datasets. Only these co-located, high-quality points were extracted for the linear regression analysis in the scatter plot. The correlation results of ICOPS on Sentinel-1 and Cosmo SkyMed (Fig. 10a) showed a coefficient of 0.839, while the comparison of ICOPS and SBAS (Fig. 10b) reached 0.894, with ICOPS as reference. These values ​​confirm the reliability and consistency of the method, supporting the use of satellites for long-term land subsidence monitoring. ICOPS data can be integrated with other models for damage mitigation.

Quantitative analysis of surface deformation at three selected sites (2014–2021) identified three phases: pre-construction, construction, and post-construction shown in Fig. 12. At P1, the time series plot shows slow subsidence until late 2017, followed by significant subsidence of ~ 8 cm (2018–2019), then rebound and stabilization until 2022. Tunnel construction data records excavation started on October 17, 2017, with excess groundwater inflow in December 2017, temporarily halting work. Groundwater recovery until May 2018 and completion of excavation in June 2019 correlated with a decrease in deformation rate, stabilizing in 2020–2021. The uplift (~ 2–3 cm) observed near the end of construction (June 2019) represents poroelastic rebound following load removal and groundwater recovery after drainage cessation. This transition to stability is evident in 2020–2022 data.

The comparison with SBAS was designed to assess whether ICOPS offers measurable improvements in deformation estimation, particularly in areas with transient scatterers affected by construction. Conclusion

Analysis of the changes in surface deformation that occurred in the Bugok industrial complex, South Korea, was carried out using the ICOPS method with Sentinel-1 and Cosmo SkyMed SAR satellite data from 2014 to 2022. The results of the time-series analysis revealed that land subsidence ranged from 0.76 to 2.83 cm/year, and the results were compared with ground truth data, with RMSE values ​​of approximately 0.6 and 0.7 cm/year for Sentinel-1 and CSK, respectively. In general, the rate of land subsidence increased during the construction period from 2017 to 2019. Furthermore, the key factors causing subsidence in this area were identified as the local geological conditions and the construction projects that occurred during this study. Natural compaction in soft soils plays a role in land subsidence, as it occurred at the beginning of the observation period. There was an increase in the rate of land subsidence during the construction period, but there was a decrease in the subsidence rate after the construction process. In addition, heavy industrial conditions and excessive use of groundwater exacerbate subsidence. Therefore, future studies on ICOPS time series InSAR could be extended by improving several aspects, such as the applications of deep learning algorithms with feature extraction, which can aid in the construction of deformation models and increase the optimization reliability. Therefore, a limitation of this study is the absence of independent in-situ horizontal measurements (e.g., continuous GNSS, extensometers, or borehole inclinometers) within the Bugok Industrial Complex. Consequently, the horizontal components reported here should be interpreted with caution; they primarily reflect the mathematical outcome of the 2D decomposition rather than conclusively proven ground motion. Shallow tunneling and lateral stress release during the 2018–2019 construction phase could have induced localized horizontal displacements that are below the reliable detection threshold of the current dataset. Future investigations incorporating continuous GNSS stations would be required to confidently quantify horizontal deformation patterns in this area. Despite these limitations, these results are valuable for the study of nonlinear surface deformation using InSAR to improve planning, monitoring, and post-project observations in infrastructure as a risk management effort in the future.
