Rotating Electromechanical System Dataset for Condition Monitoring
Authors: Juan José Saucedo-Dorantes, Miguel Delgado-Prieto, Joan Valls Pérez, David Alejandro Elvira-Ortiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This dataset provides monitoring data from a rotating electromechanical system under controlled and faulty conditions. The system is equipped with heterogeneous sensors, including accelerometers, current and temperature to capture its physical behavior across a range of stationary and non-stationary rotation’s speeds. Different faults, and some of them even with different severities, were systematically introduced in components of the electromechanical drivetrain—such as misalignments, bearing defects, and unbalances—to simulate degradation scenarios typically encountered in industrial settings. The resulting multivariate time series data are suitable for a variety of applications, including machine learning-based diagnostics, signal processing, and condition monitoring. The availability of multiple sensor modalities enables advanced techniques such as information fusion and multi-sensor data analysis. The experiments include variable speed conditions, introducing dynamic complexities that enhance the dataset’s realism and usefulness for robust algorithm development. This paper describes the experimental setup, sensor placement, fault injection procedures, and data acquisition parameters in detail.

Background & Summary

Condition monitoring (CM) of rotating machinery is essential for industrial maintenance to ensure reliability, reduce downtime, and extend asset lifespan1,2. As industries transition towards predictive and prescriptive maintenance paradigms, CM provides a foundation for data-driven decision-making by enabling early fault detection through the analysis of vibration, electrical, thermal, and acoustic signals3.

Despite the growing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) tehcniques to enhance diagnostic accuracy and automate condition assessment, which is supported by the increasing availability of high-frequency sensing technologies and the adoption of Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) infrastructures, the development of robust CM algorithms is hindered by a scarcity of realistic, diverse, and publicly accessible datasets.

The implementation of CM in industrial environments is increasingly guided by international standards and regulatory frameworks that ensure consistency, safety, and interoperability. Key among these are standards such as ISO 17359, which provides a general guideline for the implementation of CM in industrial machinery4, the IEC 60034 series for methods relevant to health assessment of rotating electrical machines5, and ISO 10816/20816 for vibration severity for evaluating mechanical condition in rotating equipment6. Furthermore, newer standards like ISO/IEC 301417 and ISO/TR 239518 are expanding to cover digitalization, data formats, and AI integration.

To address the data scarcity, this work introduces a comprehensive, open-access dataset collected from a flexible electromechanical test bench, featuring time-synchronized measurements of vibration, electrical current, and temperature under various operational speeds and fault scenarios (e.g., looseness, bearing degradation). This multivariate dataset enables the training, evaluation, and benchmarking of robust CM algorithms, particularly those dealing with non-stationary dynamics.

Several studies have leveraged this dataset to explore various aspects of condition monitoring and fault diagnosis in electromechanical systems. Following, such studies are presented from three distinct perspectives, highlighting the polyvalence of the described dataset.

Regarding signal processing and feature extraction techniques, different approaches focus on obtaining relevant information out of raw physical signals (vibration, current, temperature, and thermal images) into representative features. For instance, statistical feature extraction is widely used across multiple domains, including vibration analysis for gearbox wear9, infrared thermography for thermal distribution10, and multisensor approaches (vibration, current, temperature)11. Alternatively, Time-Frequency and Signal Decomposition techniques, such as Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD), have been applied to vibration signals to generate high-dimensional fault-related features12, alongside other signal decomposition strategies for prognosis13. Likewise, entropy-based analysis and derived variants have proven effective for characterizing non-linear vibration and current dynamics in motor and gearbox faults.

Regarding dimensionality reduction and feature optimization techniques, several studies applied specialized algorithms to isolate the most relevant fault indicators. On one hand, linear transformations such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) have been successfully used to reduce feature spaces and extract fault patterns from vibration and thermal data9. On the other hand, advanced selection algorithms such as Sequential Floating Forward Selection (SFFS)11 and statistical evaluations such as the Fisher discriminant score and Kruskal–Wallis test have been implemented to optimize feature sets for multifault diagnosis.

Regarding ML and Deep Learning (DL) approaches, the dataset has served as a benchmark for various predictive models. For instance, clustering and topographical models, such as Self-Organizing Maps (SOM), have demonstrated strong capabilities in modeling nominal distributions and classifying multifault scenarios using thermal and multisensor data10,11. In contrast, different types of Neural Networks have been employed for classifying wear conditions, in the form of Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), and also for deep feature learning in processing infrared thermography images, namely Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) and Stacked-AutoEncoders. Other AI approaches include adaptive novelty detection—which identifies faults using only healthy baseline data—and neuro-fuzzy modeling for predicting fault progression13.Publicly Available Datasets for Condition Monitoring

While numerous datasets have been developed for the condition monitoring of rotating machinery, few encompass the comprehensive diversity and depth required for advanced diagnostic research. In particular, publicly available datasets that include multiple fault types—ranging from electrical anomalies to mechanical imbalances and thermal deviations—are scarce. Moreover, most existing datasets tend to focus on a single physical domain, often limiting the analysis to vibration signals alone, therefore constraining the applicability of modern signal processing techniques and multimodal deep learning frameworks, which are suitable for leveraging cross-domain information.

To address these limitations, the introduced dataset provides a comprehensive, highly realistic resource characterized by the following key innovations. First, the integration of synchronized mechanical (vibration), electrical (current), and thermal (temperature), allowing researchers to leverage sensor fusion and cross-domain information for a system-wide understanding of machine degradation. Second, this collection offers high-resolution, unprocessed inputs. This is crucial for training and benchmarking advanced data-driven models like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Third, data is captured from a reconfigurable test bench under a wide variety of fault types and load variations, under both stationary and non-stationary speed conditions, enabling the evaluation of diagnostic algorithms in scenarios that more accurately reflect real-world machine operation.

Methods

The experimental test bench is based on a modular electromechanical system designed to replicate typical industrial machinery configurations. It consists of three main components: a three-phase induction motor (IM), a gearbox (GB), and a direct current (DC) generator. The induction motor is powered by means of a variable frequency drive (VFD) and acts as the driving element, transmitting mechanical power through the gearbox to the DC generator, which operates as a passive load at fixed speeds; these elements are mechanically coupled through two rigid couplings (C1 and C2). This configuration allows for flexible adjustment of speed operating conditions, thereby enabling the emulation of different industrial scenarios under both healthy and faulty states. The electromechanical system was instrumented with a variety of sensors aimed at capturing key physical quantities that reflect the machine’s operating condition. Specifically, the sensor array enables the acquisition of vibration signals, electrical current, temperature and additionally, rotational speed, providing a comprehensive overview of mechanical, electrical and thermal behaviour. This multisensory configuration supports the detection and analysis of a wide range of fault signatures across different physical domains. The sensors were strategically placed on critical components such as the motor housing, bearing supports, and power lines to ensure representative measurements under all test conditions. Certainly, as observed in Fig. 1, an accelerometer was placed on the top of the gearbox to acquire the vibration response of the whole system, three AC current sensors were located between the VFD and the induction motor to measure the electric current consumption, six resistance temperature detectors (RTDs) sensors were installed in the induction motor to quantify its temperature on both sides and its front and, an encoder was coupled at the free end of the DC generator to acquire the rotating speed of the electromechanical system. The data acquisition process was carried out using a data acquisition system (DAS) based on FPGA technology. The DAS corresponds to a proprietary design and employs four Texas Instruments ADS7841 analog-to-digital converters (ADC), each providing a maximum of 4-channel 12-bit serial outputs. This configuration allows for high-resolution signal acquisition across multiple physical quantities. The system was designed to capture vibration signals at a sampling frequency of 3 kHz, stator current at 4 kHz, temperature data from RTDs at 1 kHz, ensuring adequate resolution for each parameter. In addition, thermal imaging was performed with a sampling rate of 10 images per minute (one image every 6 seconds), which were stored in a commercial memory card.

The selection of sampling frequencies was predicated on the expected frequency content generated by the investigated fault conditions. This study focuses exclusively on mechanical and electromechanical anomalies, specifically broken rotor bars, bearing defects, unbalance, misalignment, and gearbox wear. For vibration and current signals, fault signatures are primarily correlated with the rotational frequency of the system (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{r}$$\end{document}fr)23 and the fundamental supply frequency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{s}$$\end{document}fs), respectively, along with their corresponding harmonics. Furthermore, since power-quality disturbances and switching-related harmonics—which typically introduce high-frequency components into the current spectrum—are outside the scope of this research, the relevant fault signatures are expected to manifest within a low-frequency band24. Consequently, given that the most demanding operating condition operates at a fundamental frequency of 60 Hz (equivalent to a rotational speed of 3600 rpm), the selected sampling frequencies were set to twice the tenth harmonic (i.e., frequencies above 1.2 kHz). Conversely, temperature variations in electromechanical systems exhibit slow transient behaviors due to the thermal inertia of the materials and components. Therefore, the sampling frequencies for thermal instrumentation are considerably lower: 1 kHz for RTDs connected to the DAS, and 1/6 Hz for the infrared camera, which corresponds to the maximum acquisition rate supported by the device. Finally, as speed measurements were acquired solely for the traceability of the operational state rather than for dynamic fault identification, a very low acquisition rate of 1/30 Hz was established. The temporal synchronization of the heterogeneous signals comprising the dataset is determined by the time vectors provided for each modality (current, vibration, temperature, speed and the and infrared image timestamp), which were generated by both the DAS and thermographic camera’s recording system, which enable temporal alignment of infrared frames with the remaining signals. Using the thermographic images as a temporal baseline, the recorded segments are sufficiently long (i.e., 6,000 samples for temperature, 18,000 samples for vibration, and 24,000 samples for current) to allow for the robust calculation of variations in each physical magnitude during the intervals between consecutive thermal image captures.

The electromechanical system was subjected to several fault conditions in order to evaluate the impact of different mechanical and electromechanical degradations. For the induction motor, six distinct conditions were established: a healthy baseline (HLT), a bearing defect (BD) created by a 1.191 mm hole drilled into the outer race, and rotor bar damages simulated by a 6 mm drilled hole. These rotor bar faults included a half-broken state (1/2 BRB), reaching a 3 mm depth that removed approximately 22% of the cross-section, and a fully broken state (1BRB) at a 14 mm depth. Additionally, a rotor unbalance (UNB) was induced by attaching a 10 g bolt to the motor-gearbox coupling, while shaft misalignment (MIS) was generated via a 5 mm horizontal displacement that caused a 1.5° angular deviation between the shafts. Concurrently, the gearbox was subjected to simulated progressive degradation by uniformly removing material from the active gear teeth surfaces. Each of the faults was introduced gradually and observed under different operating conditions to assess their progressive impact on the electromechanical system. For each fault condition evaluated, both in the induction motor and the gearbox, two types of tests were conducted to acquire and store signals from the system’s current, vibration, speed, and temperature: (i) start up transient test and, (ii) stationary test.

The start-up transient tests consisted of a 30-second duration, where the first 10 seconds corresponded to the start-up transient (acceleration ramp in the variable frequency drive, VFD), and the remaining 20 seconds represented the steady-state operation of the induction motor. For each of the four evaluated operating condition, five repetitions were performed: (i) IM speed set-point 300 rpm (equivalent to a transient from 0 Hz to 5 Hz in VFD), (ii) IM speed set-point 900 rpm (equivalent to a transient from 0 Hz to 15 Hz in VFD), (iii) IM speed set-point 3.000 rpm (equivalent to a transient from 0 Hz to 50 Hz in VFD) and, (iv) IM speed set-point 3.600 rpm (equivalent to a transient from 0 Hz to 60 Hz in VFD). During each repetition, measurements were taken for vibration, current and speed. The goal of this test was to analyse the changes occurring during the start-up transient, including the motor’s response to initial load conditions and any early-stage fault symptoms. For the transient tests, RTDs and thermographic image recording was not considered due to the short duration of the experiment.

The stationary tests involved continuous data acquisition for 45 minutes of operation. Only one acquisition was performed for each fault condition, with measurements recorded for vibration, current, temperature with RTDs, temperature via thermographic images, and speed. The primary objective of this test was to observe provided insights into the system’s performance over an extended period, including the operational stability of the system in terms of thermal evolution of the components within the system, as well as to analyse mechanical and electrical physical magnitudes.

A methodical procedure was applied to the electromechanical study system considered to perform several operations involving component replacement to introduce different failure conditions. In this regard, it should be noted that, despite the positioning and tightening checks after each replacement, some differences in the patterns for each faulty condition may appear, which are specific to the disassembly and assembly operations that have been performed.

Data Records

The dataset has been uploaded to CORA.RDR25, a federated and multidisciplinary data repository for Catalan universities, CERCA research centres, and other research institutions. Detailed information about the files comprising the dataset and the experimental conditions is provided in.TXT files. In addition, for each experiment, a set of .csv files containing the physical magnitudes acquired are provided. It is important to note that in the case of stationary tests,.mat files containing the RGB data of thermographic images are provided, together with examples in.jpg format for reference.

The names of the .csv files, .mat files and .jpg files encode the experimental conditions as follows: the first part indicates the fault condition (i.e., ‘H’, ‘BD’, ‘HB’, ‘OB’, ‘U’, ‘M’, ‘W25’, ‘W50’, and ‘W75’ referred to Healthy, Bearing Defect, Half Rotor Bar (1/2BRB), One Rotor Bar (1BRB), Unbalance, Misalignment, and Geartooth wear at 25%, 50% and 75%, respectively. The second part correspond to the frequency set point (i.e., ‘F5’, ‘F15’, ‘F50’ and ‘F60’ referred to 5 Hz –300 rpm-, 15 Hz−900 rpm-, 50 Hz−3000 rpm-, and 60 Hz−3600 rpm-). The third part corresponds to the regime of operation, that is Stationary speed condition (i.e., ‘S’), or Transient speed condition (i.e., ‘T’). The fourth part corresponds to each of the considered physical magnitudes (i.e., ‘C1’, ‘C2’, ‘C3’, ‘Vx’, ‘Vy’, ‘Vz’, ‘T1’, ‘T2’, ‘T3’, ‘T4’, ‘T5’, ‘T6’, ‘IR’, ‘RPM’, ‘AT’, ‘TimeCu’, ‘TimeVi’, ‘TimeTemp’ and ‘TimeRPM’), corresponding to Stator current 1, Stator current 2, Stator current 3, Vibration axis X, Vibration axis Y, Vibration axis Z, Temperature probe 1, Temperature probe 2, Temperature probe 3, Temperature probe 4, Temperature probe 5, Temperature probe 6, Infrared, Speed and, Ambient temperature, time for current, time for vibration, time for rotational speed, time for temperature, respectively.

These first four fields are preserved for the variables contained within each .csv, .mat file and .jpg image files. This ensures that the naming convention of both the data variables and the image files remains consistent, with additional identifying fields appended as explained below.

Where applicable - namely, in the transient tests and the thermographic images from stationary tests - a fifth field is included, corresponding to the iteration or sample number. Specifically, this field ranges from 1 to 5 in the case of transient tests, and from 1 to 450 in the case of thermal images obtained during stationary tests.

For example, in the file named “BD_F5_T_C1_4”, the ‘BD’ is Bearing Defect Condition, the ‘F5’ is the 5 Hz speed setpoint, the ‘T’ is the transient condition, the ‘C1’ is the Stator Current 1, and ‘4’ is the fourth iteration. This naming convention ensures that each file name concisely conveys the key parameters of the corresponding experiment, making it easy to identify and retrieve specific datasets.

Technical Validation

Technical validation of data has been conducted by a combination of visual inspection, statistical analysis and signal processing. Building on this approach, we implemented a structured validation workflow designed to consolidate the database into a coherent, readable, and machine-actionable schema while enforcing signal integrity by removing elementary errors and filtering out physically impossible values. Our aim was to produce a dataset that is immediately usable by other researchers without any additional cleaning or preprocessing.

The visual inspections focused on reviewing the scope signals to detect and eliminate anomalous errors and avoid gaps in the database. These checks were critical to obtaining a consistent dataset, which is essential for accurate analysis. Meticulously performed, these visual checks ensured that the data was free of discrepancies.

Then, a complete Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) was performed using MATLAB scripts with data cleaning purposes, investigating the integrity of the files, as well as the coherence of each data signal regarding their respective physical magnitude. First, regarding the integrity of the information, files were checked for unreadable data, zero-length signals and duplicate files. Then, signals were scanned for null or invalid values (e.g., NaN, inf, null), and for outliers with respect to the expected value range for each signal.

The length of the signals was also checked to ensure consistency throughout the database. For the transient signals, each signal contains a number of samples equivalent to 30 seconds, whereas, for the stationary signals, the number of samples must be the equivalent of 45 minutes. This consistency is critical for comparative analysis and modeling purposes, ensuring that each dataset is comparable to the others. Any discrepancy in signal length could lead to difficulties in analysis and potential inaccuracies in the modeling of electromechanical faults.

Furthermore, statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB scripts, including verifying the contained variance in mechanical and thermal signals for the stationary regime, as well as verifying essential signal properties for current (i.e., mean equal to zero, periodic behaviour), which is an essential aspect of ensuring the validity of the measurement.

Additionally, both faulty and healthy signals were segmented into multiple shorter duration signals (i.e., 1 minute) and analysed using MATLAB’s Diagnostic Feature Designer app to extract statistical features of the signals that are capable of distinguishing between healthy and faulty conditions.

Within the framework of the stationary experiments, features were extracted from data segments from healthy and all faulty conditions and operating points (i.e., F5, F15, F50 and F60). Then, hypothesis testing regarding the similarity of the average value for the computed features was conducted, with the aim of underscoring the distinguishability of each of the faulty conditions with respect to the healthy condition. High T-test values between healthy and faulty conditions indicate the distinguishability of these two groups. Usage Notes

For the analysis and interpretation of the provided dataset, the use of both MATLAB and Python is recommended. Considering the experimental nature of the dataset, it is advisable to apply an appropriate filtering technique to deal with the presence of electrical and mechanical noise, such as a band-pass filter, to improve the signal-to-noise ratio and ensure more accurate results.
