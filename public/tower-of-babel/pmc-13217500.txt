Immunoglobulin G exacerbates endothelial–microglial crosstalk, promoting secondary injury in high-altitude cerebral edema
Authors: Yan Xue, Meijuan Qian, Dongzhi Wang, Baolan Wan, Jun Wang, Mingming Zhou, Xiaoxiao Chen, Yuhua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypobaric hypoxia (HH)-induced blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption is a critical pathological event in high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE). However, the mechanistic role of extravasated immunoglobulin G (IgG) in this process remains insufficiently characterized. This study elucidates that leaked IgG actively contributes to neuroinflammation and BBB impairment under HH conditions, functioning beyond being a passive biomarker. Using both in vivo and in vitro models, we demonstrated that HH rapidly induces Caveolin-1–dependent transcytosis of IgG across endothelial cells as an early event, preceding the degradation of TJs. Internalized IgG activated NF-κB signaling in endothelial cells, leading to the downregulation of Claudin-5 and ZO-1 and promoting NLRP3 inflammasome assembly. Concurrently, extravasated IgG drove microglial polarization toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, marked by increased IL-1β expression and p65 phosphorylation. Inhibition of Caveolin-1 using methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD) significantly reduced IgG uptake, suppressed endothelial inflammatory signaling, and attenuated microglial activation, thereby preserving BBB integrity. These findings support the “two-hit” model, in which Caveolin-1-mediated IgG transcytosis initiates a feed-forward loop of neuroinflammation and BBB disruption. Our results identify Caveolin-1 as a promising therapeutic target and provide mechanistic insights into HACE pathogenesis, offering a foundation for novel preventive and treatment strategies.Keywords: Immunoglobulin G, High-altitude cerebral edema, Blood–brain barrier, Neuroinflammation, Caveolin-1, Microglia, Endothelia

Introduction

Hypobaric hypoxia (HH), a central concern in high-altitude medicine research, impairs blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity (Huang et al., 2015). Emerging evidence suggests that BBB disruption is an early pathological event in various neurological disorders, including Alzheimer's disease, multiple sclerosis, and traumatic brain injury (TBI) (Inoue et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2021, Tabet et al., 2022). The BBB, a critical protective structure of the central nervous system (CNS), maintains homeostasis through tight junctions (TJs) between endothelial cells and regulates transcellular transport (Singh and Vellapandian, 2023). Under pathological conditions, the downregulation of TJ proteins, such as Occludin, Claudin-5, and ZO-1, leads to increased paracellular permeability. Simultaneously, vesicular transport is upregulated, facilitating macromolecular leakage via transcellular pathways, with transcellular transport occurring earlier and independently of TJ disintegration (Jiang et al., 2018). Our preliminary data revealed that 24 h exposure to HH simulating an altitude of 7600 m resulted in a 40% reduction in Claudin-5 expression in murine brain tissue (Xue et al., 2022). Complementary in vitro studies confirmed that hypoxia disrupts BBB integrity by activating the NRF1–Caveolin-1–Claudin-5 endocytosis–autophagy axis. These findings align with the imaging features observed in clinical cases of high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) (Urushida et al., 2021, Hackett, 1999), providing mechanistic insights into HACE pathogenesis and informing potential intervention strategies. Notably, the role of immunoglobulin G (IgG), a macromolecular protein (∼150 kDa) that extravasates following BBB disruption, remains poorly understood in the context of HACE onset and progression. Therefore, building on previous work, this study aims to further characterize the spatiotemporal patterns of IgG leakage and its relationship with neuroinflammation.

Traditionally, the presence of IgG in the CNS has been interpreted as a passive marker of BBB compromise (Hou et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2022a, Yuan et al., 2023). However, recent studies suggest that IgG may play an active role in disease pathogenesis. Specifically, IgG leakage through the BBB has been implicated in the initiation of neuroinflammatory responses (Lee et al., 2021). Microglia, the primary immune effector cells in the CNS, exhibit functional plasticity and polarize toward either a pro-inflammatory M1 or an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (Hsu et al., 2023). The mechanisms by which extravasated IgG influences microglial polarization and contributes to neuroinflammation in HH conditions remain unclear. Elucidating these underlying mechanisms is crucial for understanding the interplay between BBB disruption and immune activation. Previous studies have suggested that microglial polarization is regulated by signaling pathways such as NF-κB and JAK–STAT (Tian et al., 2022, Ye et al., 2025). Activation of these pathways modulates the expression and secretion of inflammatory mediators, thereby driving neuroinflammatory cascades. Martin Scholz, et al. summarize the current understanding of the "second hit," the recent findings that may explain the pathophysiologic neutrophil-BBB interactions occurring after severe trauma (Scholz et al., 2007). We hypothesize that a "second-hit" mechanism in HACE—where initial BBB disruption is compounded by IgG-induced microglial activation. IgG leakage may initiate a vicious cycle between HH-induced BBB disruption and microglial-mediated inflammation, a “second-hit” mechanism that warrants further investigation. Therefore, comprehensively elucidating the mechanisms of IgG action may reveal novel therapeutic targets and intervention strategies for HH-induced neuroinflammation.

Caveolae-mediated endocytosis has recently garnered increasing attention. Caveolin-1, the principal scaffolding protein of caveolae, drives vesicle formation (Li et al., 2024) and regulates TJ stability via autophagy-mediated degradation, thereby influencing TJ assembly and barrier function (Xue et al., 2022). Our previous study delineated the dynamic crosstalk between Caveolin-1 and the TJ protein Claudin-5, as well as their roles in HACE, thereby addressing a significant research gap in understanding hypoxia-induced BBB disruption. Under HH conditions, endothelial dysfunction triggers the downregulation of TJ proteins, weakening BBB integrity and potentially permitting macromolecules such as IgG to infiltrate the CNS. However, the relative contribution of transcellular transport to IgG leakage remains unclear. In cerebral vascular endothelial cells, the upregulation of Caveolin-1 correlates with increased caveolar density and enhanced vesicular transport of macromolecules, such as transferrin (Perisa et al., 2016). Consequently, whether a “time-dependent biphasic relationship” exists between early caveolae-mediated transcytosis and later TJ impairment under HH warrants further investigation.

To address these gaps, this study primarily investigated three key aspects: first, the spatiotemporal dynamics of BBB disruption and IgG leakage under HH conditions; second, the impact of IgG leakage on microglial polarization and associated signaling pathways; and third, the therapeutic potential of intervening in IgG leakage or modulating microglial polarization to mitigate HH-induced neuroinflammation and BBB disruption. These investigations aim to advance our understanding of HACE pathogenesis and inform the development of targeted interventions for high-altitude-related neurological disorders in the future.

Materials and methods

Construction of the HACE animal model

Male C57BL/6 J mice (8 weeks old; 20 ± 2 g) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Nantong University. Prior to experimentation, the animals underwent a 3-day acclimatization period in a temperature-controlled facility (26 ± 1 °C) with a 12-h light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were randomly allocated into weight-matched groups and maintained on equal rations throughout the experiment. To induce HACE, each cohort was placed in a hypobaric chamber (ProOx - 810) calibrated to simulate an altitude of 7600 m for 24 h (ascent/descent speed 20 m/s, cabin pressure −63.8 kPa, oxygen partial pressure 9 kPa, temperature 25℃, humidity 40%, light-dark ratio 12:12 h), as described previously (Gong et al., 2018, Lu et al., 2022). Control mice were housed at ambient pressure (sea level) under identical environmental conditions. At the end of the exposure period, the animals were immediately processed for downstream analyses.

Animal experiment grouping and drug administration

Twelve male C57BL/6 J mice were randomly assigned to three groups (each group consists of n = 4 mice, and the modeling process is repeated 4 times): normobaric normoxia (NN), hypobaric hypoxia (HH), and Methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD)treated hypoxia (MβCD + HH) (drug-administered group). To ensure reproducibility, the experiment was repeated in four independent cohorts. Twenty-four hours before HH exposure, mice in the MβCD + HH group received a single intravenous injection of MβCD (300 mg/kg) (Xue et al., 2022) through the tail vein. Mice in the HH and MβCD + HH groups were placed in a hypobaric oxygen chamber simulating a 7600 m altitude for 24 h. Immediately following hypoxia, the MβCD + HH group received a second intravenous tail-vein injection of MβCD. Approximately 1 h after the second dose, all animals were immediately anesthetized and perfused for downstream analyses.

Brain water content

Brain water content was measured using the wet-dry weight method. Briefly, brain tissues were immediately weighed after collection (wet weight) and then dried at 80°C for 48 h to obtain the dry weight. Brain water content (%) = [(wet weight - dry weight) / wet weight] × 100%.

BBB permeability assay

BBB permeability was assessed by measuring the extravasation of Evans blue (EB, Sigma Aldrich, CAS: 314–13–6). Mice were injected with EB dye (2%, 4 mL/kg) via the tail vein following HH exposure and allowed to circulate for 1 h. Mice were then transcardially perfused with physiological saline (0.9%) through the left ventricle to remove any remaining intravascular dye. One-half of the brain was weighed, homogenized, and placed in polyethylene tubes containing 1 mL of 50% trichloroacetic acid solution formamide. The supernatants were collected and diluted with ethanol at a 1:3 ratio, and their absorbance was measured at 620 nm using a plate reader (Synergy 2™, Bio-Tek, US). A standard curve was established to determine the dye quantity, expressed as micrograms per gram (µg/g) of brain tissue. The other half of the brain was fixed and sectioned for confocal imaging using a 647 nm laser on a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

Primary microglial culture

Isolation and culture

Primary microglial cells were isolated from the cerebral cortices of postnatal day 0–3 (P0–P3) Sprague-Dawley rat pups. Briefly, after careful removal of the meninges, cortical tissues were minced and digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, USA) for 15 min at 37°C. Digestion was terminated by adding an equal volume of DMEM/F12 medium (HyClone, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime, China). The cell suspension was filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were seeded into poly-L-lysine-coated T75 culture flasks at a density of 2 × 10⁶ cells/mL and maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The medium was replaced every 3 days. After 10–14 days, when mixed glial cultures reached confluence, microglia were isolated by shaking the flasks at 200 rpm for 2 h at 37°C. The detached cells (microglia) were collected, centrifuged, and re-seeded into appropriate culture plates for subsequent experiments.

Purity assessment

Microglial purity was assessed by immunofluorescence staining using the microglia-specific marker ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba-1; 1:200, Abcam, ab5076). Briefly, cells cultured on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, blocked with 5% BSA, and incubated with anti-Iba-1 antibody overnight at 4°C, followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500, Invitrogen, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany). Purity was determined by counting Iba-1-positive cells relative to total DAPI-stained nuclei in at least 10 random fields per coverslip. Only cultures with purity > 95% were used for experiments (Supplementary Figure S1A).

Functional characterization

To verify that the isolated microglia maintained their functional responsiveness, the cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL, Sigma, USA) for 24 h to induce classical (M1) activation. Western blot analysis was conducted on TNF-α, IL-1β, and CD206 in the microglia. Meanwhile, the conditioned media were collected for TNF-α measurement using ELISA (430901, BioLegend) following the manufacturer's instructions (Supplementary Figure S1B–F).

Establishment of an in vitro hypoxia model

When bEnd.3 murine brain endothelial cell cultures reached 80–90% confluence, monolayers were allocated into normoxic control (21% O₂, 5% CO₂, 74% N₂) and hypoxic treatment (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, 94% N₂) groups. Hypoxia was induced using a modular chamber at 37 °C for specified durations. Normoxic controls were maintained in a conventional incubator.

IgG treatment in cellular experiments

For in vitro experiments, primary microglia and bEnd.3 cells were treated with purified rat IgG (Catalog No. I4131, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at a concentration of 5 μg/mL. IgG was dissolved in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and added directly to the culture medium. Cells were treated with IgG for 24 h under either normoxic (21% O₂) or hypoxic (1% O₂) conditions.

Physiological relevance

In C57BL/6 mice, total serum IgG is approximately 2.4 mg/mL (Klein-Schneegans et al., 1989). Under pathological conditions with BBB disruption, the concentration of IgG leaking into the brain parenchyma has been estimated to reach approximately 0.1–1% of serum levels (Villaseñor et al., 2016). The 5 μg/mL concentration falls within the lower end of this estimated pathological range, mimicking the IgG exposure that microglia might encounter in the brain interstitium during early or mild BBB compromise. In preliminary experiments, we tested a range of IgG concentrations (1, 5, 10, 50, 100 μg/mL) on primary microglia under normoxic conditions. The 5 μg/mL concentration produced a consistent and reproducible induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) without saturating the response. Compared to untreated controls, 1 μg/mL IgG showed no significant effect on the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β). While higher concentrations (≥10 μg/mL) produced near-maximal stimulation under hypoxic conditions, potentially masking modulatory effects (Supplementary Figure S2A-B). Therefore, 5 μg/mL was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments.

MβCD treatment in cellular experiments

MβCD (Catalog No. C4555, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in sterile PBS at a stock concentration of 100 mM and stored at −20℃. As previously described (Xue et al., 2022), bEnd.3 cells were pretreated with 5 mM MβCD for 1 h prior to IgG stimulation and/or hypoxic exposure. After pretreatment, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the same concentration of MβCD for the duration of the experiment (24 h) The 1-hour pretreatment was chosen to allow sufficient time for cholesterol depletion and disruption of caveolae before experimental stimulation. The continued presence of MβCD during stimulation was maintained to prevent cholesterol re-accumulation, as cholesterol synthesis and replenishment can occur within hours.

In vitro experimental design and treatment

bEnd.3 mouse brain endothelial cells and primary murine microglia were cultured as described above and used when they reached 80–90% confluence. Cells were allocated to five experimental groups: Nor (normoxia control), Nor + IgG, Hyp (hypoxia control), Hyp + IgG, and Hyp + IgG + MβCD. For IgG treatment, cells in the Nor + IgG, Hyp + IgG, and Hyp + IgG + MβCD groups were pre-treated with 5 μg/mL of IgG. Following this pre-treatment, the Hyp, Hyp + IgG, and Hyp + IgG + MβCD groups were exposed to hypoxia (1% O₂, balanced with N₂ and CO₂) for 24 h. Immediately after hypoxic exposure, the cells in the Hyp + IgG + MβCD group were washed once with PBS and incubated for 1 h in a medium containing 10% FBS supplemented with 5 mM MβCD.

Immunofluorescence

Brain sections (40 µm) and cultured cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 and blocked with 10% donkey serum for 1 h. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-Claudin-5 (Thermo, 35–2500), anti-Laminin (Abcam, ab11575), anti-Iba1 (Abcam, ab5076), anti-IgG (Abcam, ab133470), anti-ASC (CST, 60824S), anti-NLRP3 (Life Science, mAG-20B-0014), anti-ZO-1 (Proteintech, 21773–1-AP), anti-P65 (Cell Signaling, 8242), and anti-Caveolin-1 (CST, 3267). After washing, the samples were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 555–conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo, A31572), Alexa Fluor 647–conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG (Thermo, A32787), or Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated donkey anti-goat IgG (Abcam, ab150133). The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (2 µg/mL) in the dark for 10 min. Imaging was performed using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

Protein isolation and western blot

Tissue and cell samples were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing 1% phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) protease inhibitor and 1% phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were homogenized thoroughly and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as the soluble protein fraction. The pellets were resuspended in RIPA buffer supplemented with 20% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 24 M urea, and 1% PMSF, then homogenized, and centrifuged. The resulting supernatant was collected as an insoluble protein fraction.

The proteins were denatured, separated by SDS–PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-IgG (Abcam, ab133470), anti-AQP4 (Cell Signaling, 59678S), anti-NLRP3 (Life Science, mAG-20B-0014), anti-ASC (CST, 60824S), anti-p65 (Cell Signaling, 8242), anti-p-p65 (Cell Signaling, 3033S), anti-IL-1β (Abcam, ab9722), anti-CD63 (Santa Cruz, sc-5275), and anti-β-actin (Sigma, A5316). After washing, membranes were incubated with either goat anti-rabbit HRP–conjugated secondary antibody (Jason, 115–035–033) or goat anti-mouse HRP–conjugated secondary antibody (Jason, 111–035–003).

Transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER)

Transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) was used to quantify TJ integrity in bEnd.3 cell monolayers (Srinivasan et al., 2015). The cells were cultured in Corning Transwell inserts (3401, 0.4 μm) until they were confluent. After equilibrating the Millicell ERS-2 Volt–Ohm Meter (Merck Millipore), the short electrode was placed in the apical chamber and the long electrode in the basolateral chamber, avoiding contact with the cell layer or insert walls. Resistance readings were recorded for each insert and a blank (cell-free insert). TEER (Ω·cm²) was calculated as: TEER = (total resistance − blank resistance) (Ω) × insert area (cm²).

Transcytosis assay

An intact endothelial monolayer with well-preserved TJs restricts FITC–dextran passage, whereas TJ disruption permits leakage, serving as an indicator of endothelial barrier integrity. The concentration of dextran in the basolateral chamber serves as a metric for assessing the barrier function (Robinson et al., 2018). bEnd.3 cells were cultured in Corning Transwell inserts (3401) until a confluent monolayer was established. FITC–dextran (40 kDa) was applied at 100 µg/mL to the apical chamber and incubated for 30 min. Thereafter, the medium from the basolateral chamber was collected, and FITC-dextran fluorescence was quantified using a microplate reader (excitation 490 nm, emission 520 nm). The amount of FITC–dextran detected in the lower chamber was used to quantify monolayer permeability.

Endothelial cell–mediated pinocytosis assay

bEnd.3 cells were seeded in the apical chamber of the Transwell inserts to establish a confluent monolayer. A solution of FITC–dextran (40 kDa; 100 µg/mL) was added to the upper chamber and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Both the upper and lower chambers were rinsed with PBS, and fresh culture medium was added. The inserts were then returned to the incubator for an additional 3 h. Finally, the medium from the lower chamber was collected, and the FITC-dextran concentration was quantified by measuring the fluorescence at 490 nm excitation and 520 nm emission using a microplate reader.

Endocytosis assay

Endothelial cells were passaged at a 1:3 ratio and plated by adding 200 μL aliquots of the diluted suspension to the center of glass-bottomed dishes. Cells were incubated in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere until they reached approximately 60–70% confluence and then subjected to hypoxic treatment. Following hypoxia, the monolayer was incubated with 100 μL of FITC–dextran (40 kDa; 100 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the dishes were washed twice with 500 μL of pre-warmed PBS. Finally, 600 μL of phenol red–free DMEM was added, and live-cell confocal imaging was performed immediately using a Leica SP8 microscope with 488 nm excitation.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Brain sections and bEnd.3 cells were fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffer and stored at 4 °C overnight. After rinsing with PBS, the samples were dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (50%, 75%, and 95%) and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were cut and stained with uranyl acetate, followed by lead citrate. The sections were examined using an HT7700 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan), and autophagic vacuoles within individual cells were quantified.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software v10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired t-tests. For multiple group comparisons, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance is denoted as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001; n.s. indicates not significant.

Results

Hypoxia promotes IgG extravasation and microglial activation in mouse brain

To construct the HACE animal model, C57BL/6 J mice were exposed to HH (7600 m above sea level) for 24 h. The brain water content of HH-treated mice was significantly increased (Fig. 1A). Results in Fig. 1B and C showed that the AQP4 was upregulated significantly in HH-treated mice, demonstrating the success of the model. To observe BBB integrity, EB was injected via the tail vein and allowed to circulate for 1 h. The mice were then perfused with saline to remove the intravascular EB. The results demonstrated that the residual EB content in the mouse brains was higher after HH exposure (Fig. 1D and E). To investigate the effects of HH-induced TJ disruption on BBB permeability (Xue et al., 2022), we examined the distribution of IgG in the cerebral vasculature. Compared with NN, HH-exposed mice exhibited a marked increase in IgG immunoreactivity within both the cerebral cortex (Fig. 1F–I) and hippocampal CA1 region (Fig. 1J–K), indicating substantial IgG leakage from the vasculature into the brain parenchyma. These findings confirm that HH compromises BBB integrity and facilitates IgG extravasation. To assess the cellular consequences of IgG leakage, we evaluated microglial activation in the hippocampal CA1 region of the brain. At 24 h post-HH exposure, Iba-1+ microglia in the hippocampal region exhibited pronounced morphological changes relative to NN controls, including hypertrophic cell bodies and elongated processes (Fig. 1L–M). Notably, these activated microglia were spatially colocalized with perivascular IgG deposits, suggesting direct interaction. The observed colocalization provides morphological evidence that extravasated IgG may be internalized by microglia via phagocytosis, thereby contributing to their activation under hypoxic conditions.

Early hypoxia enhances endothelial IgG uptake through endocytosis prior to TJ disruption

Previous studies have demonstrated that TJs in endothelial cells remain structurally intact for up to 12 h of hypoxic exposure, despite a marked increase in transcellular permeability (Xue et al., 2025). To investigate whether hypoxia-induced endothelial permeability is associated with enhanced endocytic activity, bEnd.3 cells were exposed to 1% O₂ for 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, and 48 h. Both intercellular resistance and transmembrane transport capacity were assessed (Fig. 2A and B). Compared with normoxic controls, endothelial intercellular resistance began to decline after 9 h of hypoxia and displayed a time-dependent negative correlation with the duration of hypoxia (Fig. 2C). Concurrently, the transcellular transport of IgG increased significantly after 9 h (Fig. 2D), suggesting that hypoxia enhances IgG permeability through mechanisms other than TJ disruption. To further characterize this process, bEnd.3 cells were incubated with IgG for 30 min, followed by incubation under hypoxic conditions for various durations. As shown in Fig. 2E and F, IgG uptake via endocytosis progressively increased beginning at 6 h of hypoxia and peaked at 24 h. Notably, TJ integrity remained intact during the first 12 h of exposure, suggesting that the observed increase in IgG uptake preceded junctional breakdown. Based on these findings, cells exposed to hypoxia for 24 h were selected for live-cell imaging following a 30 min IgG incubation. Endothelial phagocytosis of IgG was detectable as early as 35 min after IgG incubation (Fig. 2G). TEM revealed a significant increase in vesicular structures within bEnd.3 cells after 24 h of hypoxia (Fig. 2H and I), consistent with elevated endocytic activity. Our previous studies demonstrated that Caveolin-1 expression in cerebral microvascular endothelial cells increases after 12 h of hypoxia and peaks at 24 h (Xue et al., 2022), which is consistent with the observed vesicle accumulation. Collectively, these results indicate that hypoxia compromises BBB integrity through a biphasic mechanism: an early phase characterized by increased endocytosis and IgG uptake, followed by a later phase characterized by TJ disruption and paracellular leakage.

Hypoxia enhances exocytosis capability in bEnd.3 cells

To evaluate the impact of hypoxia on endothelial exocytosis, bEnd.3 cells were exposed to 1% O₂ for 24 h, followed by a 30 min incubation with IgG. After washing with PBS, the cells were maintained in fresh medium for an additional 3 h. Live-cell dynamic tracing revealed exocytotic activity as early as 180 min after IgG incubation, indicating active vesicular release under hypoxia (Fig. 3A). To quantify exocytosis, Transwell assays were performed to measure IgG levels in the basolateral chamber. IgG release increased after 9 h of hypoxia and peaked at 24 h, confirming a time-dependent enhancement of exocytotic capacity (Fig. 3B and C). In parallel, bEnd.3 cells were cultured under hypoxic conditions (1% O₂) for 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, and 48 h to assess CD63 expression, a tetraspanin protein associated with exosome formation and vesicle trafficking. Total CD63 protein levels began to decline after 3 h of hypoxia exposure. Intracellular CD63 expression decreased after 6 h, whereas membrane-associated CD63 expression increased after 12 h of exposure (Fig. 3D–G). These protein-level changes were mirrored in the CD63 mRNA expression patterns (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hypoxia promotes a shift in CD63 localization and significantly enhances the exocytotic activity of endothelial cells, contributing to increased transcellular transport under stress.

Exogenous IgG amplifies hypoxia-induced endothelial injury via inflammatory signaling

In the early stages, IgG extravasation accelerates due to TJ disruption, and permeability changes intensify after 24 h of hypoxia exposure. To investigate the impact of IgG transport on endothelial barrier integrity, bEnd.3 cells were pre-treated with 5 μg/mL exogenous IgG prior to hypoxic challenge. Compared with hypoxia-only controls, IgG pre-treatment further reduced transcellular resistance and significantly increased the transport of Alexa Fluor® 488–labeled IgG across the endothelial monolayer (Fig. 4A and B), indicating increased permeability under combined stress. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we examined the expression of TJ proteins. Western blot analysis revealed that exogenous IgG exacerbated the hypoxia-induced downregulation of Claudin-5 (Fig. 4C–E). Immunofluorescence staining showed that IgG pre-treatment significantly promoted NLRP3 inflammasome formation and intensified ZO-1 disruption in hypoxia-exposed endothelial cells (Fig. 4F–H). Further analysis demonstrated that IgG pre-treatment markedly increased p65 phosphorylation and upregulated the expression of downstream pro-inflammatory mediators, including NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β, compared to hypoxia alone (Fig. 4I–M). These findings suggest that exogenous IgG amplifies hypoxia-induced inflammatory signaling, leading to aggravated TJ degradation and enhanced endothelial permeability.

Targeting Caveolin-1 exerts dual protective effects on BBB integrity under hypoxic conditions by attenuating IgG endocytosis and preserving TJs

This study explored the alterations in the cerebral vasculature subsequent to hypoxic exposure and Caveolin-1-mediated endocytosis. bEnd.3 cells were subjected to a hypoxic environment, and 5 mM MβCD was employed to suppress Caveolin-1-dependent internalization. In comparison with the normoxic control group, the hypoxic environment notably augmented the uptake of IgG by endothelial cells (Fig. 5A), accompanied by an upregulation of Caveolin-1 expression (Fig. 5F). After the MβCD-mediated inhibition of Caveolin-1, the hypoxia-induced endocytosis of IgG was significantly diminished, and the retained IgG mainly accumulated at the cell periphery (Fig. 5A and B). This confirms that Caveolin-1-mediated endocytosis is one of the principal pathways for IgG transport across the cell membrane. Notably, Caveolin-1 not only regulates endocytosis but also takes part in the membrane localization of tight junction proteins (Fig. 5G). Therefore, we further assessed the overall influence of MβCD on barrier function. The results indicated that MβCD pretreatment significantly mitigated the hypoxia-induced decline in TEER (Fig. 5C) and decreased the transmembrane transport of Alexa Fluor® 488-labeled IgG (Fig. 5D), while IgG endocytosis was significantly attenuated (Fig. 5E). Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that MβCD pretreatment, while reducing Caveolin-1 expression, on the one hand alleviated NLRP3 inflammasome activation (Fig. 5F, H–I), and on the other hand reversed the disruption of Claudin-5, resulting in partial restoration of tight junction integrity in both the membrane and cytoplasmic regions (Fig. 5G, J–K). In summary, targeted inhibition of Caveolin-1 may safeguard the blood-brain barrier via a dual mechanism: it reduces Caveolin-1-mediated IgG endocytosis and the subsequent inflammatory response, while also directly enhancing the integrity of tight junctions. These two pathways likely act in synergy to alleviate hypoxia-induced increased permeability.

IgG leakage contributes directly to microglial activation and drives neuroinflammation in hypoxic conditions

In vivo experiments confirmed that HH exposure facilitated IgG extravasation from cerebral microvessels into the brain parenchyma, where internalized IgG was detected within activated microglia. To determine whether the pro-inflammatory phenotype observed in vivo is directly driven by IgG or solely by hypoxia, we performed an in vitro experiment isolating microglia from these confounding factors. As shown in Fig. 6A, even in the absence of hypoxia (Normoxia+IgG group), IgG treatment significantly increased the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines compared to the normoxic control. This confirms an intrinsic, hypoxia-independent effect of IgG. Furthermore, the combination of hypoxia and IgG produced an effect significantly greater than hypoxia alone, suggesting a synergistic amplification (Fig. 6A–E). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that both hypoxia and IgG treatment independently promoted nuclear translocation of p65, with the combined treatment producing a synergistic effect (Fig. 6F–G). Morphological assessment using circularity coefficient analysis revealed that IgG pre-treatment intensified hypoxia-induced microglial activation. (Fig. 6H). Inflammasome formation was also enhanced, as evidenced by increased NLRP3 and ASC expression and elevated inflammasome counts in IgG-pre-treated hypoxic microglia (Fig. 6I, K–M). Transwell co-culture experiments were performed to assess the impact of endothelial permeability on microglial activation. Microglia co-cultured with hypoxia-exposed endothelial cells pre-treated with IgG exhibited elevated levels of p65, p–p65, and pro–IL-1β. However, when endothelial cells were treated with 5 mM MβCD, the inflammatory response in the co-cultured microglia was markedly reduced (Fig. 6J, N–O). These findings suggest that IgG amplifies hypoxia-induced NF-κB signaling in microglia, promoting inflammatory cytokine production and exacerbating neuroinflammatory response.

Inhibition of Caveolin-1 effectively suppresses IgG leakage from endothelial cells, reduces microglial recruitment to cerebral blood vessels, and mitigates BBB permeability

In vivo experiments revealed that HH exposure significantly increased microglial activation in the cerebral cortex and hippocampal CA1 region compared to NN controls. Internalized IgG was detected in these activated microglia, indicating active uptake. However, pre-treatment with MβCD via tail vein injection prior to HH exposure markedly reduced the number of activated microglia in both brain regions (Fig. 7A–F). Furthermore, 3D reconstruction demonstrated that HH-induced recruitment of Iba1+ microglia to the cerebral vasculature was significantly attenuated by MβCD treatment (Fig. 7G). These findings suggest that Caveolin-1 inhibition decreases BBB permeability, in part, by suppressing microglial chemotaxis toward sites of IgG extravasation.

Discussion

In the present study, we first validated that our HH exposure protocol successfully recapitulated the core pathological features of HACE, as evidenced by increased BBB permeability, elevated brain water content, and upregulated AQP4 expression. This provides a solid foundation for investigating the underlying mechanisms. HH-induced disruption of the BBB is a hallmark of HACE; however, the downstream consequences, particularly the role of extravasated immunoglobulins, remain poorly defined. Our findings demonstrate that IgG, traditionally regarded as a passive biomarker of BBB injury, actively contributes to neuroinflammation and barrier dysfunction in HH conditions. Mechanistically, IgG activates NF-κB signaling in endothelial cells, leading to the downregulation and mislocalization of TJ proteins. In parallel, IgG promotes microglial activation and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby amplifying the neuroimmune responses. These results expand upon prior observations and support the “second-hit” hypothesis, wherein IgG leakage not only reflects BBB compromise but also drives secondary injury in hypoxic environments. This concept is reinforced by parallels in acute ischemic stroke, where IgG extravasation correlates with BBB disruption and may serve as a biomarker of vascular injury (Loucks et al., 2023, Guo et al., 2021). Our study advances this understanding by establishing IgG as a pathogenic effector in HACE rather than a passive consequence of barrier breakdown. This aligns with emerging evidence from TBI, where serum protein leakage, including that of IgG, triggers microglial activation and exacerbates neuroinflammatory cascades (Green et al., 2024). Collectively, these insights reposition IgG-related pathways as promising therapeutic targets for hypoxia-induced neurological disorders. This study links the previously identified molecular mechanism (Caveolin-1 autophagy) with the overall pathological outcome (nerve injury), filling the evidence gap between vascular damage and nerve injury. Although the route of IgG entry is known, the pathological consequences of its interaction with microglia represent the incremental contribution of this work, emphasizing that the focus is on the consequences of leakage rather than the mechanism of leakage to highlight the uniqueness of this research.

A key innovation of this study is the identification of a time-dependent biphasic mechanism of IgG leakage across the BBB. We observed that Caveolin-1–mediated IgG endocytosis facilitates early transcellular IgG transport, preceding TJ disruption. Specifically, endothelial IgG uptake increased as early as 6 h post-hypoxia, peaking at 24 h, whereas significant TJ impairment, evidenced by reduced transendothelial resistance and Claudin-5 downregulation—only became apparent between 9 and 12 h. Collectively, these findings suggest that transcellular transport dominates the early phase of IgG leakage, whereas paracellular leakage becomes prominent later due to junctional degradation of the endothelial barrier. This biphasic model reframes the conventional views of BBB dysfunction, elevating transcytosis from a passive bystander to an active initiator of barrier compromise. It also provides a temporal framework for therapeutic intervention, enabling the targeted modulation of IgG transport at distinct stages of disease progression. Notably, this pattern mirrors the observations in TBI. In focal cerebral ischemia models, Caveolin-1 upregulation precedes TJ disruption and facilitates early macromolecular transport, such as albumin transport (Krueger et al., 2019). Our study definitively links Caveolin-1–dependent endocytosis to IgG trafficking in the HH model, reinforcing Caveolin-1's role as a central regulator of early BBB dysfunction.

Targeting Caveolin-1 has emerged as a promising strategy for interrupting the pathological cascade underlying BBB disruption and neuroinflammation. Specific inhibition of Caveolin-1 using MβCD partially reversed this cycle, resulting in reduced IgG uptake, attenuated endothelial inflammation, and diminished microglial activation. This dual protective effect aligns with our previous findings on Caveolin-1's role in maintaining BBB homeostasis. We previously demonstrated that restoring Claudin-5 expression via the NRF1–Caveolin-1–Claudin-5 endocytosis–autophagy axis, in combination with Caveolin-1 inhibition, ameliorates high-pressure hypoxia-induced BBB damage (Xue et al., 2022, Xue et al., 2025). The current study extends these insights by linking Caveolin-1 inhibition to reduced IgG-dependent neuroinflammation, a mechanism that has not been previously explored under hyperbaric hypoxia conditions. Notably, in TBI models, Caveolin-1 inhibition has been shown to suppress transcellular leakage and microglial activation, thereby improving neurological outcomes (Zhang et al., 2022b, Wang and Head, 2019, Zhang et al., 2024). In these models, MβCD treatment led to decreased Caveolin-1 expression, reduced albumin extravasation, and lower levels of microglia-derived pro-inflammatory cytokines (Li et al., 2025; Atış et al., 2019). The consistent efficacy of Caveolin-1 inhibition across diverse pathological contexts reinforces its therapeutic potential in HACE and other diseases characterized by BBB compromise and neuroinflammatory signaling.

This study had several limitations that warrant consideration. First, our experimental design focused on acute hypoxic exposure (24 h), which may not accurately reflect the adaptive or pathological responses observed in populations experiencing prolonged high-altitude exposure. Future investigations employing chronic hypoxia models are needed to determine whether IgG-mediated inflammatory responses persist, attenuate or evolve over time. Second, although prior studies have implicated FcγRIIB as a key mediator of IgG-induced microglial activation, promoting pro-inflammatory signaling in various neuroinflammatory contexts (Quan et al., 2009), our study lacks direct empirical evidence (such as FcγRIIB gene knockout or knockdown) to confirm its role under hypoxic conditions. Incorporating these approaches would strengthen the mechanistic framework and validate receptor-specific contributions. Third, the translational relevance of Caveolin-1 inhibition requires further evaluation in large animal models of HACE, as rodent models may not fully replicate human pathophysiological responses to high-altitude hypoxia. Finally, although MβCD effectively disrupts Caveolin-1–mediated IgG transport, its non-specific interaction with lipid rafts raises concerns about off-target effects (Hsiao et al., 2024). This study has not yet verified the functional role of IgG in HACE through specific blockade or exogenous supplementation. Although the MβCD intervention experiment supports the significance of the Caveolin-1 pathway, it cannot serve as direct evidence for the functional role of IgG. Therefore, our conclusion regarding IgG-driven secondary damage is mainly based on in vitro experiments (Fig. 6A shows that IgG directly induces microglial polarization) and in vivo observations (colocalization of IgG deposits with activated microglia), rather than on MβCD intervention. Future studies utilizing selective Caveolin-1 inhibitors (such as caveolin-1 siRNA or peptide-based inhibitors) would improve therapeutic specificity and reduce unintended cellular perturbations. We also plan to directly validate the functional role of IgG using Fcγ receptor-blocking antibodies or intravenous IgG administration.

The accumulation of IgG in perivascular regions raises important questions about the role of the vascular basement membrane as a secondary barrier. As a large molecular weight protein, IgG must traverse not only the endothelial barrier but also the vascular basement membrane to reach the brain parenchyma. The prominent perivascular retention observed in our study suggests that the basement membrane may function as a molecular sieve or secondary barrier that impedes the free diffusion of extravasated IgG (Zhang et al., 2017). Future studies should investigate whether matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)-mediated degradation of basement membrane components (e.g., laminin, collagen IV) facilitates the penetration of IgG beyond the perivascular space under hypoxic conditions. Additionally, the perivascular accumulation of IgG may also reflect dysfunction of the glymphatic system, a perivascular network responsible for clearing solutes from the brain interstitium. Recent studies have demonstrated that the glymphatic system plays a critical role in removing waste products along perivascular pathways, and its impairment has been implicated in various neurological disorders (Zahran et al., 2026, Xu et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2024, Braun and Iliff, 2020). The retention of IgG around cerebral vessels may indicate that hypoxic exposure inhibits cerebrospinal fluid-interstitial fluid exchange, leading to inefficient clearance of extravasated plasma proteins. This hypothesis is consistent with our observation that IgG deposition persists around vessels rather than diffusing freely into the parenchyma. While these mechanistic questions extend beyond the scope of the current study, we have emphasizd the significance of perivascular IgG accumulation as a potential spatial hub connecting BBB disruption to downstream neuroinflammation. We believe this will provide valuable insights for fellow researchers investigating the pathophysiology of HACE and related disorders.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that IgG internalized via the Caveolin-1–dependent endocytic pathway plays a central role in activating both endothelial cells and microglia, thereby driving HH-induced neuroinflammation and BBB dysfunction. These findings underscore the importance of simultaneously targeting IgG transcytosis and its downstream inflammatory signaling to disrupt the pro-inflammatory cycle underlying HACE. Caveolin-1 has emerged as a key molecular mediator and potential therapeutic target in this context. Collectively, our results advance the understanding of BBB–immune crosstalk during hypoxic stress and provide a theoretical foundation for developing novel strategies to prevent and treat high-altitude-associated neurological disorders.
