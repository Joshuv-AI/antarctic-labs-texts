The impact of caregiver burden on benefit finding: exploring the mediating role of psychological resilience in family caregivers of hip fracture patients
Authors: Dan Zhao, YingLi Liu, MeiLiang Chen, HuiQin Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the process of caring for elderly patients with hip fractures, caregivers are prone to experience caregiver burden. Excessive burden not only affects the physical and mental health of the caregivers themselves but also impacts the quality of care, hinders the patient’s recovery process, and reduces patient’s quality of life. This study explores the relationship between benefit finding, caregiver burden, and psychological resilience, while verifying the mediating effect of psychological resilience between caregiver burden and benefit finding. The findings provide a theoretical basis for improving the quality of care provided by caregivers. This multicenter, cross-sectional study recruited 501 caregivers of older hip-fracture patients from four hospitals in China between January 10 and July 15 2025. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the Benefit Finding Scale, Resilience Scale, and the Caregiver Burden Scale. Pearson’s correlation and mediation analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 27) and the PROCESS macro (version 5.0). The mean total score of benefit finding was (73.01 ± 17.48). Benefit finding was negatively correlated with caregiver burden (r = -0.289, P < 0.001) and positively correlated with psychological resilience (r = 0.267, P < 0.001), whereas caregiver burden was negatively correlated with psychological resilience (r = − 0.356, P < 0.001). Caregiver burden influenced benefit finding directly through psychological resilience: the indirect effect via psychological resilience was significant (β = − 0.045, 95% CI [− 0.092, − 0.012], P < 0.001). Psychological resilience plays a crucial mediating role between caregiver burden and benefit finding. Healthcare providers should pay close attention to caregivers’ burden and implement effective interventions to enhance their psychological resilience. Such efforts can help caregivers experience positive feelings during caregiving, ultimately improving both the quality of care and the patient’s quality of life.Keywords: Hip fracture in the elderly, Family caregiver, Benefit finding, Psychological resilience, Caregiver burden

Introduction

The world is currently facing the challenge of population aging. According to data from the World Health Organization (WHO), the global population aged 60 and above reached 1 billion in 2019. This figure is projected to increase to 1.4 billion by 2030 and to 2.1 billion by 2050. This growth is occurring at an unprecedented pace and is expected to accelerate in the coming decades, especially in developing countries1. As population aging intensifies, hip fractures among the elderly have emerged as a significant global public health concern2. As the world’s largest developing country, China must pay particular attention to the issue of hip fractures in the elderly population.

Hip fractures represent one of the most severe types of fractures among the elderly, characterized by acute onset, slow recovery, and poor prognosis3. Statistics indicate that approximately 1.5 million hip fractures occur annually among individuals aged 65 and above worldwide, with projections suggesting a sharp increase to 6.3 million cases by 20504. Hip fractures currently result in 4.5 million cases of disability globally each year, with estimates predicting this number will rise to 21 million within 40 years, ranking among the top ten causes of disability globally5. Research demonstrates that mortality rates following hip fractures in the elderly range from 15 to 20%, with only 39.3% of patients regaining their pre-fracture activity levels post-surgery6–8. Additionally, hip fractures are often accompanied by complications such as pneumonia, deep vein thrombosis, and cardiovascular events, leading to increased dependency of the patient, imposing substantial burdens on caregivers and adversely affecting their physical and mental health, as well as the quality of care provided, ultimately worsening patient outcomes9,10. Therefore, it is of great importance to focus on the physical and mental health of caregivers of elderly hip fracture patients11.

Previous studies have primarily focused on caregiver burden and negative emotions such as anxiety and depression12–14. In recent years, positive psychology has introduced the concept of "benefit finding," emphasizing the potential for positive psychological changes that caregivers may experience during the caregiving process. Benefit finding refers to the positive experiences and gains perceived by caregivers during caregiving15, which can alleviate negative emotions, promote the seeking of social support, enhance disease understanding, and thereby improve the quality of care16,17. However, research on benefit finding among caregivers of elderly hip fracture patients remains unexplored.

Based on existing literature, significant intercorrelations exist among benefit finding, caregiver burden, and psychological resilience. Caregiver burden encompasses the total physical, psychological, emotional, and social pressures experienced by caregivers during the caregiving process18. Studies have found a negative correlation between caregiver burden and benefit finding: the higher the burden, the more difficult it is to perceive positive experiences in caregiving19,20. Furthermore, caregivers with good psychological adaptation under long-term heavy caregiving tasks perceive lower burdens, indicating a negative correlation between caregiver burden and psychological resilience21–23. Psychological resilience refers to an individual’s positive adaptation process when facing significant stressors such as adversity, trauma, tragedy, or threats, essentially the ability to “bounce back” from difficulties24. Research also indicates that caregivers with high psychological resilience are more likely to actively cope with challenges, with their adaptive responses closely related to higher levels of benefit finding16. Therefore, this study aimed to explore whether psychological resilience among caregivers of older adults with hip fractures mediates the relationship between caregiver burden and benefit finding, while also examining the association between caregiver burden and benefit finding in this population. The study is grounded in Taylor’s Cognitive Adaptation Theory (CAT) as its theoretical framework25. When confronting the adverse event of a relative’s hip fracture, caregivers are not entirely passive or negative in their responses. Many actively engage in cognitive adaptation to derive benefits from this adversity, reflecting the positive significance embedded within stressful events. The construct of benefit finding encompasses multiple dimensions, including personal growth and social support. In this study, caregiver burden is conceptualized as the stressor that activates cognitive processes, prompting caregivers to engage in psychological adjustment, whether actively or passively. Psychological resilience serves as a crucial personal resource; individuals with higher levels of resilience demonstrate stronger capabilities in cognitive appraisal and adjustment. This significantly facilitates the effective operation of three adaptive processesis: ① Self-enhancement: Caregivers are more likely to affirm their own efforts and capabilities in coping with challenges rather than falling into self-blame. ② Meaning making: They are more inclined to perceive the trauma as an opportunity to "re-examine family relationships" or "discover personal resilience." ③ Sense of mastery: They are more adept at acquiring caregiving knowledge and planning rehabilitation schedules, thereby regaining a sense of control amid uncertainty. Ultimately, caregiver benefit finding represents the positive adaptive outcome, wherein caregivers experience not merely pure burden and loss, but various aspects of benefit finding, such as "personal growth," "positive health behavior changes", and "enhanced family cohesion".

Methods

Study design

This study featured a multicentre, cross-sectional design. The study design and reporting process followed the STROBE guidelines (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) for observational research (refer to the supplementary material 1).

Sampling and setting

Between 10 January 2025 and 15 July 2025, three hospitals in Hohhot, Inner Mongolia (Second Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University, Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University, and Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region People’s Hospital) and one hospital in Taiyuan (Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital) were selected. We used convenience sampling to identify 536 potential participants who met the inclusion criteria at these 4 medical institutions. Among these individuals, 35 older people were excluded because they met the exclusion criteria or because the investigation was interrupted. Finally, 501 participants regarding whom no data were missing were included in this study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

During the study period, participants were recruited from the orthopedic inpatient departments of 4 hospitals.

Inclusion criteria: (1) The patient had a hip fracture confirmed by X-ray (diagnostic criteria according to the 4th edition of Practical Orthopaedics)26; (2) The patient was aged ≥ 60 years27; (3) The participant was aged ≥ 18 years; (4) The participant was designated by the patient as the primary caregiver during the treatment period and could be the patient’s spouse, child, sibling, or other relative; (5) The participant had adequate reading and comprehension ability; (6) The participant provided informed consent and signed the informed consent form.

Exclusion criteria: (1) The participant had experienced a major negative life event within the past 3 months (e.g., traffic accident, bereavement); (2) The participant had a psychiatric or mental disorder; (3) The participant was involved in other research studies.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was calculated using the G-Power 3.1.9.7 software package. In the analysis, with three predictor variables, a significance level of 0.05 (alpha), a statistical power of 0.95 (1 − β), and an anticipated medium effect size of 0.15 (Effect size f2), the minimum number of participants required for the study was calculated to be 11928. Considering a 20% invalid response rate, the calculation indicated a need for at least 143 samples. In this context, the sample size of 501 participants included in this study can be considered to provide relatively strong statistical power.

Data collection

The data cited in this study were collected through paper-based questionnaires administered by two uniformly trained graduate students with medical backgrounds. In light of the issue of potential illiteracy among some participants, the researchers conducted interview-style surveys with all eligible participants to ensure comprehensive data collection without omitting any information.

To ensure the quality of the data thus collected, this study implemented quality control measures before, during, and after the surveys. Before the surveys, all investigators received standardized training to ensure consistency in terminology and assessment criteria. During the surveys, 2 investigators completed the questionnaires by simultaneously conducting face-to-face interviews with the same subject. And the questionnaire was checked immediately after the survey was completed to ensure that no survey questions were missed. After the survey, 2 investigators double-entered the questionnaires pertaining to the same period to ensure the accuracy of the entered data.

Measurement tools

General information questionnaire

The General Information Questionnaire was self-designed by the researchers. The general information survey for participants included: gender; age; education level; marital status; occupational status; monthly household income; place of residence; relationship to the patient; daily caregiving hours.

Chinese version of the Benefit Finding Scale (BFS)

The present study employed the Chinese version adapted cross-culturally by Liu Zhunzhun et al.29. The scale comprises six dimensions: Acceptance (3 items), Family Relationships (2 items), Worldview (4 items), Personal Growth (7 items), Social Relationships (3 items), and Health Behaviors (3 items), totaling 22 items. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“not at all”) to 5 (“extremely”). Total scores range from 22 to 110, with higher scores indicating greater perceived benefit finding. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the entire scale was 0.956, indicating excellent internal consistency. The reliability coefficients for each dimension were: Acceptance 0.799, Family Relationships 0.710, Worldview 0.814, Personal Growth 0.890, Social Relationships 0.793, and Health Behaviors 0.790, demonstrating excellent internal consistency. A confirmatory factor analysis was subsequently conducted. The initial model fit indices were as follows: χ2/df = 1.561, CFI = 0.962, TLI = 0.955, SRMR = 0.047, and RMSEA = 0.051, confirming satisfactory reliability and validity. Taken together, the Chinese version of the BFS is appropriate for assessing the level of benefit finding among family caregivers of older adults with hip fracture.

Resilience Scale (RS)

This scale was refined by the American scholar Wagnild30 in 2009 based on the original Resilience Scale. It was subsequently culturally adapted into Chinese by the Chinese scholar Ni Qianyu et al.31. The scale comprises two dimensions: Personal Competence (8 items) and Acceptance of Self and Life (6 items), totaling 14 items. It employs a Likert 7-point scoring method, ranging from “Strongly Disagree” (1 point) to “Strongly Agree” (7 points). The total score ranges from 14 to 98, with higher scores indicating better resilience levels. This scale has been widely used among adult caregivers in China and can be regarded as a well-established instrument. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the overall scale was 0.978, and the reliabilities for the Personal Competence and Positive Cognition subscales were 0.962 and 0.947, respectively, indicating excellent reliability and validity.

Zarit Caregiver Burden Inventory (ZBI)

This study employed the Chinese version of the ZBI scale translated by the domestic scholar Wang Lie et al.32 in 2006. It comprises two dimensions: Personal Strain(12 items) and Role Strain (10 items), totaling 22 items. The scale uses a Likert 5-point scoring method (0–4), representing "Not at all burdened" to "Extremely burdened." The total score ranges from 0 to 88, with the following clinical thresholds: ≥ 40: Severe burden; 21 ~ 39: Moderate burden; 0 ~ 20: No-to-mild burden. Widely applied in adult caregiver populations in China, this scale is considered a well-established instrument. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the entire scale was 0.971, with 0.951 for the Personal Burden dimension and 0.930 for the Role Burden dimension, confirming its good reliability and validity.

Other covariates

Confounders were evaluated on the basis of previous studies and an expert review through the use of directed acyclic graphs (Fig. 2). The following covariates were considered for inclusion in the final model: age, sex (male or female), level of education (junior high school and below, junior college degree and below, bachelor degree or above), monthly per capita income (≤ 3000, 3001 ~ 5000,5001 ~ 8000, ≥ 8000), relationship with the patient (spouse, son or daughter, siblings, daughter-in-law or son-in-law), average duration of care (< 6 h, 6-12 h, > 12 h). To evaluate these factors, a purpose-built, general Information questionnaire , which was developed speciffcally for this study, was employed.

Directed Acyclic Graph. A directed acyclic graph represents the associations between covariates and the primary exposure and outcome. The blue circles represent outcomes, the green circles represent exposures, and the pink circles represent ancestors of the exposures and outcomes (i.e., confounders). The green lines represent causal paths, and the pink lines represent biasing paths.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Inc., Chicago, IL, USA)and the PROCESS macro (version 5.0). A P value < 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Skewness and kurtosis were tested to verify the normality of the data. Continuous variables conforming to normal distribution were described as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess potential common method variance. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine relationships among caregiver burden, psychological resilience, and benefit finding.

The mediating effect of psychological resilience between caregiver burden and benefit finding was tested using Model 4 in Hayes’ PROCESS macro (version 5.0). To evaluate the mediating effects, this study adopted the Bootstrap method with 5000 resamples33. If the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the mediating effect did not include 0, the effect was considered statistically significant. The significance level for this study was set at P < 0.05, and two-sided tests were used.

Ethical recognition

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University (Approval No.: EFY20250095). Prior to the commencement of the study, written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were required to read and sign an informed consent form, clearly stating their right to withdraw from the study at any time without adverse consequences. All collected data were anonymized, stored using encryption methods, and strictly confined to the purposes of this research.

Results

Common method bias

In this study, the results of Harman’s single-factor test revealed that 5 factors exhibited eigenvalues greater than 1, and one factor explained 33.52% of the total variance, which was under the threshold of 40%34. Thus, no significant common method biases were detected.

Characteristics of the participants

This study included a total of 501 participants, with females accounting for 50.7% and married individuals comprising 88.0%. In terms of educational level, the majority of participants had received junior high school education (63.8%), followed by those with college or undergraduate education (28.0%). The monthly household income per capita was highest in the 5,000–8,000 yuan range (46.4%), while the proportion of households with less than 3,000 yuan was the lowest (8.3%). Regarding place of residence, rural households accounted for 64.7%. The employment status of caregivers showed that only 21.4% were currently employed, whereas more than half were unemployed (78.6%). In terms of relationship to the patient, spouses constituted the highest proportion (76.4%), followed by children (11.2%). Detailed characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1.

Discussion

Analysis of participant general information

In the present study, a substantial proportion of caregivers were spouses (76.4%), and the majority resided in rural areas (64.7%). Spousal caregivers often assume primary and long-term caregiving responsibilities, which may intensify both emotional involvement and caregiving demands. The predominance of rural households and relatively modest income levels may further reflect limited access to formal caregiving resources, potentially increasing reliance on family-based care. These contextual factors may partially explain the observed levels of caregiver burden.

At the same time, the high proportion of married participants (88.0%) and the relatively stable family structure may provide emotional or instrumental support that contributes to resilience and benefit finding. Additionally, the predominance of participants with junior high school education suggests a population with moderate educational attainment, which may influence coping styles and health literacy. These sample characteristics provide context for understanding the observed associations between caregiver burden, psychological resilience, and benefit finding.

Relationship between caregiver burden, psychological resilience and benefit finding

This study identified a significant negative correlation between caregiver burden and benefit finding, a significant positive correlation between psychological resilience and benefit finding, and a significant negative correlation between caregiver burden and psychological resilience. Mediation analysis further demonstrated that psychological resilience partially mediated the relationship between caregiver burden and benefit finding, supporting the proposed hypothesis.

The overall level of benefit finding among caregivers of elderly patients with hip fractures was moderate to high (73.01 ± 17.48). Among the subdimensions, personal growth scored highest. This may reflect the adaptive processes caregivers undergo during the recovery period. Following hip fracture surgery, functional recovery is often limited in the early postoperative stage, with recovery rates remaining below 50% at one month and persistent deficits at three months postoperatively35. During this period, caregivers must rapidly assume new responsibilities and acquire medical and rehabilitation knowledge through professional guidance and practical experience. Increased role competence and caregiving mastery may contribute to enhanced perceptions of personal growth.

Consistent with previous research, caregiver burden was negatively associated with benefit finding16. Persistent functional limitations substantially increase caregiving intensity and disrupt caregivers’daily routines and occupational responsibilities. When caregiving demands exceed available coping resources, physiological and psychological strain accumulates12,36. Elevated burden may impair caregivers’ cognitive and emotional capacity to engage in adaptive reflection, thereby reducing their ability to perceive positive meaning in the caregiving experience. Prolonged burden may also compromise caregivers’ health and caregiving quality, ultimately affecting patient recovery outcomes.

The inverse association between caregiver burden and benefit finding is consistent with findings from a cross-sectional study of caregivers of patients with heart failure in the United States37. When caregivers perceive their burden as excessive, emotional distress and functional impairment may follow, limiting sustained caregiving engagement and reducing opportunities for benefit finding38,39. A meta-analysis of 12 randomized controlled trials demonstrated that mindfulness-based interventions grounded in positive psychology effectively reduced caregiver burden among caregivers of adults with chronic illnesses40. In cultural contexts influenced by norms emphasizing emotional restraint and family privacy, caregivers may be less likely to seek external support, potentially exacerbating accumulated strain. These findings support the integration of caregiver burden assessment into routine clinical evaluation and the provision of structured psychosocial interventions.

Psychological resilience emerged as a significant partial mediating factor, consistent with findings from studies conducted in Turkey41 and among caregivers of patients with cancer and inflammatory bowel disease42,43. Caregivers with high burden levels may adopt avoidance-based coping strategies and exhibit limited adaptive regulation. In contrast, individuals with stronger psychological resilience are more capable of cognitive reappraisal and proactive coping, enabling positive reinterpretation of caregiving challenges. This process may mitigate perceived burden and facilitate benefit finding. Prior studies have also reported a positive association between resilience and benefit finding44 and an inverse association between resilience and caregiver burden45.

Overall, caregiver burden appears to influence benefit finding both directly and indirectly through psychological resilience. Early identification of caregivers experiencing high burden is therefore essential. Interventions should extend beyond burden reduction to include resilience-enhancing strategies. Approaches aimed at strengthening self-efficacy, promoting adaptive coping, and facilitating meaning-making may improve caregivers’ psychological well-being and contribute to more sustainable and higher-quality caregiving outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference among benefit finding, caregiver burden, and psychological resilience. Although the mediation model was theoretically grounded, mediation analysis based on cross-sectional data reflects statistical associations rather than causal pathways. The temporal ordering of the variables cannot be determined. For example, higher levels of benefit finding may contribute to increased resilience over time, or reciprocal relationships may exist. Therefore, the identified mediating effect should be interpreted as statistical mediation rather than evidence of causal mediation. Longitudinal or prospective designs are needed to clarify the directional and potentially bidirectional relationships among these variables. Second, the study was conducted in four hospitals across two cities in northern China using a non-probability sampling method. This sampling strategy may introduce selection bias, as caregivers who agreed to participate may differ systematically from those who declined. In addition, the limited geographic coverage restricts the representativeness of the sample and constrains the generalizability of the findings to other regions, healthcare systems, or cultural contexts. Future studies should employ probability sampling techniques and recruit participants from multiple regions to enhance external validity.

Third, although we controlled for various covariates in the mediation analysis, some variables might have been overlooked, thereby possibly leading to confounding biases.

Conclusions and implications

The study findings indicate that, despite the stress and challenges inherent in caregiving, caregivers can experience positive changes, including psychological growth, improved interpersonal relationships, and an enhanced sense of personal value. Caregivers with higher psychological resilience and lower perceived burden are more likely to derive these positive experiences.

Healthcare professionals can support both patients and caregivers in two primary ways. First, the health education system should be strengthened during the early hospitalization stage. This initial period often represents the phase of greatest emotional instability for both patients and caregivers. Providing systematic and comprehensible medical information at this critical time can effectively reduce anxiety and feelings of helplessness. Establishing timely, interactive communication channels further enhances caregivers’ understanding of the disease and their sense of control. Second, hospitals or individual departments should organize regular health-education sessions for patients and caregivers. These sessions should cover disease-related knowledge, caregiving skills, and psychological adjustment strategies, using live demonstrations and case analyses to increase engagement and practical relevance. Additionally, distributing easy-to-read booklets and leaflets, and establishing department-based WeChat public accounts or chat groups, can facilitate continuous dissemination of caregiving information. Such resources help caregivers stay informed, improve their caregiving competence, and build confidence in providing care.

Relevance to clinical practice

Given the mediating role of psychological resilience in the relationship between caregiver burden and benefit finding, future research could develop clinical intervention trials based on this framework, adopting a “burden reduction and empowerment” dual-pathway strategy. This approach would aim to alleviate objective caregiving burden through practical support while simultaneously enhancing caregivers’ self-efficacy, mastery, and psychological resources via targeted skills training and empowerment programs.

Such interventions are expected to validate the practical utility of the theoretical model proposed in this study and provide evidence-based guidance for promoting caregivers’ mental health and improving the quality of care.
