Fundamental Limitations of QAOA on Constrained Problems and a Route to Exponential Enhancement
Chinonso Onah, Kristel Michielsen

Abstract
We study fundamental limitations of the generic Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) on constrained problems where valid solutions form a low-dimensional manifold inside the Boolean hypercube, and we present a provable route to exponential enhancements via constraint embedding. Focusing on permutation-constrained objectives, we show that the standard “generic” QAOA ansatz—with transverse-field mixer and diagonal, commuting, r r –local cost—faces an intrinsic feasibility bottleneck: even after optimizing angles, depth– p p circuits with p = O ⁡ ( n ) p=O(n) cannot raise the total probability mass on the feasible manifold much above the uniform baseline | Π | / 2 N |\Pi|/2^{N} , up to polynomial slack in n n . Against this envelope we place a minimal constraint-enhanced kernel (CE–QAOA) that operates directly inside a product one–hot subspace and mixes with a block–local X ​ Y XY Hamiltonian. For permutation constrained problems on n n variables, we prove an angle-robust, depth-matched exponential enhancement of the form P p ( CE ) P p ( gen ) = exp ⁡ [ Θ ⁡ ( n 2 ) ] \frac{P_{p}^{\mathrm{(CE)}}}{P_{p}^{\mathrm{(gen)}}}\;=\;\exp\!\bigl[\Theta(n^{2})\bigr] (1) for all depths p p up to a linear fraction of n n under a polynomial incidence–growth condition on the interaction hypergraph. Introduction

Combinatorial optimization problems (COPs) with global constraints pose a specific challenge for shallow variational quantum circuits. Feasible solutions occupy a vanishing fraction of the full Hilbert space, so any method that explores the entire hypercube pays an intrinsic feasibility penalty. This paper isolates that bottleneck for the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)
[
1
]
and develops a transparent way to assess the state prepared by the algorithm for arbitrary shallow schedules of length
p=O(n)

[
1

as the generic QAOA ansatz in order to faciliate a clear comparison with a minimal constraint-aware variant that bakes constraint structure and symmetries into its quantum dynamics. The distinction is made explicit in Subsection
1.2

Instead of monitoring only expectation values and approximation ratios, we study the full probability profile of depth-
p
QAOA states through four lines of analyses. First, a Walsh–Fourier calculation on the Boolean cube reveals that the one-hot constraints behind permutation encodings have exponentially small low-degree Fourier mass. At
p=1
the
X
-mixer acts by pure phases in this Walsh/Fourier basis, so the generic ansatz cannot significantly lift feasible mass above the uniform baseline, up to polynomial slack, and this holds uniformly over angles. Second, a gentle cost-angle averaging argument, applicable after a simple rescaling that holds for standard integer or rational instances, pins the feasible baseline at
|\Pi|/2^{N}
. Third, a hypercontractive (
l_{4}
) bound on the output amplitudes then show that most angles concentrate around this baseline, which is useful for understanding shallow-depth training without assuming anything about gradients. Finally, locality enters through light-cone growth. With commuting,
r
-local diagonal costs, information propagates at most one neighborhood per layer. A dependency–graph argument turns this into a bound on the joint probability
that all row and column constraints hold, and the same limitation persists

1.2.1
Generic QAOA

The generic QAOA

1
]

\ket{\psi_p(\vec\gamma,\vec\beta)}=\bigl[\prod_{\ell=1}^{p}e^{-i\beta_{\ell}\sum_{j}X_{j}}\,e^{-i\gamma_{\ell}H_{C}}\bigr]\ket{+}^{\otimes N},

i.e., a depth–

X

are optimized (classically) to maximize an empirical objective. For the algorithm to be performant, typical schedules need to be short (
p=O(1)
or
p\ll N
) without going to the adiabatic limit
[
40
]
. Common ideas to improve algorithmic performance include linear/adiabatic ramps
[
39
]
, parameter transfer across sizes/instances to reduce training cost while keeping the same mixer/cost structures
[
24
]
, recursive elimination to iteratively fix variables based on low–depth runs, shrinking the instance before a final classical solve
[
8
,
25
,
26
]
, warmstarting QAOA to help landscapes and convergence
[
10
,
27
]
.

The

state.

2
Results

2.1
Fundamental Limitations

Our point of departure is the following question. By how much can a shallow variational quantum circuit concentrate probability mass on the feasible manifold of a globally constrained problem? We answer this for the canonical case of a permutation constrained problem of size
n
one-hot encoded into
N=n^{2}
qubits (e.g., assignment/TSP) by proving that
generic
QAOA initialized on
|+\rangle^{\otimes N}
with a
X
mixer and a diagonal cost cannot raise the feasible mass above the uniform baseline
|\Pi|/2^{N}
by more than polynomial factors at any fixed constant depth, and under standard locality, not even at linear depth
p=\alpha n
. Where
|\Pi|=n!
and
\alpha<1.

Recall that the problem Hamiltonian is diagonal in computational basis and is denoted as
H_{C}
. For any
r
–local diagonal
H_{C}
and all angles
(\beta,\gamma)
, we show that single payer
p=1
generic QAOA circuits cannot generate the long–range correlations required by the feasible solution space by more than polynomial slack:

P_{\Pi}^{\mathrm{(gen)}}(\beta,\gamma)\ \leq\ \frac{|\Pi|}{2^{N}}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n).

(2)

The reason is twofold. Structurally, the
p{=}1
light cone confines each block one–hot projector to a finite neighborhood, so the
O(n)
constraints interact only through short dependencies. This caps any joint boost to at most polynomial over uniform. Spectrally, the indicator
1_{\Pi}
is overwhelmingly high-degree in Walsh/Krawtchouk space, while a single
X
-mixer layer only adds phases. Consequently, average over
\gamma
pins the baseline exactly and fourth-moment bounds show typical angles concentrate near it.

In contrast, a
constraint-enhanced
variant (CE-QAOA) that operates inside a block one-hot subspace and mixes with an all-to-all
XY
block mixer achieves an
\exp[\Theta(n^{2})]
improvement over generic QAOA at the same depth. We arrive at the conclusion by establishing an upper envelope for generic QAOA and a lower envelope for CE–QAOA. To derive the upper envelopes, we employ harmonic analysis, angle-averaging, hyperconstrative fourth-moment, and light-cone analyses. Detailed proofs are presented in Methods and Materials ( Section
3
). Currently we summarize the main conclusions.

Setup

We refer to a COP of size
n
as permutation constrained if the valid solutions can be identified as a
n\times n
permutation matrix. For such problems, the one-hot encoding requires
N=n^{2}
qubits. Let
N
be the number of qubits and
\Pi=n!
the size of the feasible permutation sector (row/column one–hot constrained). Consider the Boolean cube
\{0,1\}^{N}
and write any function
f:\{0,1\}^{N}\!\to\!\mathbb{C}
in the Walsh/Krawtchouk basis

f(x)=\sum_{S\subseteq[N]}\widehat{f}(S)\,\chi_{S}(x)
with characters
\chi_{S}(x)=(-1)^{\langle S,x\rangle}
and
degree

|S|
. This is a complete and unique Fourier expansion for any function
f(x)
with unique coefficients
\widehat{f}(S)
[
29
]
.

To connect this formalism the probability amplitudes in generic QAOA, we write

H_{C}=\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{N}}C(y)\,|y\rangle\!\langle y|\quad\Rightarrow\quad U_{C}(\gamma)=e^{-i\gamma H_{C}}=\sum_{y}e^{-i\gamma C(y)}\,|y\rangle\!\langle y|.

With the phase field
\phi_{\gamma}(y):=e^{-i\gamma C(y)}
and any basis vector
|x\rangle
we obtain the probability profile

a_{\beta,\gamma}(x)=\langle x|U_{X}(\beta)U_{C}(\gamma)|+\rangle^{\otimes N}=2^{-N/2}\sum_{y}\underbrace{\langle x|U_{X}(\beta)|y\rangle}_{K_{\beta}(x,y)}\,\phi_{\gamma}(y).

Because
U_{X}(\beta)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}e^{-i\beta X_{i}}
, its
Z
-basis kernel factors as

K_{\beta}(x,y)\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{N}\langle x_{i}|e^{-i\beta X}|y_{i}\rangle\;=\;(\cos\beta)^{\,N-d(x,y)}(-i\sin\beta)^{\,d(x,y)}\;=\;(\cos\beta)^{N}\,(-i\tan\beta)^{\,d(x,y)},

(3)

where
d(x,y)=|\;x\oplus y\;|
is the Hamming distance.

Thus, we obtain a generic representation of the probability of obtaining a feasible permutation from generic QAOA as

a_{\beta,\gamma}(x)\;=\;2^{-N/2}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{N}}K_{\beta}(x,y)\,\phi_{\gamma}(y).

(4)

For the indicator
1_{\Pi}
on the permutation submanifold, we show (Materialos and Methods Sections
3.2
and
3.3
) that the total probability mass is tiny up to logarithmic degree. To derive this we define the
low-degree mass
up to level
d
as

\|f\|^{2}_{\leq d}\;:=\;\sum_{|S|\leq d}\!|\widehat{f}(S)|^{2},\qquad\text{and high-degree mass }\|f\|^{2}_{>d}=\|f\|_{2}^{2}-\|f\|^{2}_{\leq d}.

We introduce Krawtchouk analysis to calculate the low–degree weight in the one-hot sector (Lemma
13
), and, after tensorizing rows/columns and convolving to obtain the weight of indicator
1_{\Pi}
on the permutation submanifold (Section
3.4
). Our calculations show that both low–degree and high-degree mass remain exponentially suppressed. We denote the total output pmf as
p(x)=|a_{\beta,\gamma}(x)|^{2}
. To compute the total feasible mass, we choose a degree truncation
d=T=\Theta(\log n)
and split
p(x)
as low and high degree parts follows
p=p^{\leq 2T}+p^{>2T}
. The low degree part accounts for all the contributions to the Fourier mass from degrees below the
T
threshold and the high degree split captures all degree contributions above
T
. For the
low–degree
part we have (Lemma
17
),

\frac{1}{2^{N}}\langle\mathbf{1}_{\Pi},p^{\leq 2T}\rangle\leq\frac{C_{T}n^{O(T)}}{2^{N}}.

For the
high–degree
tail is equally suppressed (Lemma
18
). We have

\frac{1}{2^{N}}\big|\langle\mathbf{1}_{\Pi},p^{>2T}\rangle\big|\leq\frac{\sqrt{|\Pi|}}{2^{N}}.

Consequently,
p=p^{\leq 2T}+p^{>2T}
yields the harmonic bound in Theorem
19
:

P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\;\leq\;\frac{|\Pi|}{2^{N}}.

The angle averaging version of the result follows from an averaging over the phase field
\phi_{\gamma}(y)
if the spectrum of
H_{C}
is rescaled to lie on a lattice (after standard rescaling). This arises because then
\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}[\phi_{\gamma}(y)\overline{\phi_{\gamma}(y^{\prime})}]=\mathbf{1}_{\{C(y)=C(y^{\prime})\}}
leading cross–terms to cancel and

\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\big[P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\big]=\frac{|\Pi|}{2^{N}}.

The third bound is derived the fourth–moment (hypercontactivity) analysis of the probability profile. As outlined in Sec.
3.6
, let
a(x)=a_{\beta,\gamma}(x)
and recall
P_{\Pi}=\sum_{x\in\Pi}|a(x)|^{2}
.
By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\sum_{x\in\Pi}|a_{x}|^{2}\;\leq\;\sqrt{|\Pi|}\,\Bigl(\sum_{x}|a_{x}|^{4}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

(5)

Thus, any bound on
\sum_{x}|a_{x}|^{4}
yields an upper bound on
P_{\Pi}
.

Averaging over
\gamma
(phase field), the one-bit calculation tensorizes to

\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\!\Big[\sum_{x}|a(x)|^{4}\Big]\;\leq\;\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\sin^{2}(2\beta)\Big)^{\!N}\;\leq\;\Big(\tfrac{3}{4}\Big)^{\!N},

since
\max_{\beta}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\sin^{2}2\beta\big)=\tfrac{3}{4}
(achieved at
\beta=\pi/4
).
Thus, for typical angles we have,

P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\;\lesssim\;\sqrt{|\Pi|}\,\Big(\tfrac{3}{4}\Big)^{N/2}\;=\;\frac{|\Pi|}{2^{N}}\cdot\underbrace{2^{N}\,|\Pi|^{-1/2}\,\Big(\tfrac{3}{4}\Big)^{N/2}}_{\text{exponentially small in }N},

i.e. exponentially close to the uniform baseline.

The light-cone analysis provides some structural intuition for the above analysis. With
U=U_{X}(\beta)U_{C}(\gamma)
and
H_{C}=\sum_{j}H_{j}
diagonal,
r
–local, conjugation preserves diagonality and expands support by at most one
r
–local hyperedge. Each block projector
P_{B}
depends only on its radius-1 neighborhood in the interaction hypergraph; the
2n
one-hot constraints therefore form a bounded-dependency family. Standard cluster/dependency bounds then cap any feasible-mass boost to a polynomial over the uniform product baseline—fully consistent with the harmonic and moment bounds above.

All four routes—harmonic truncation with explicit low–degree suppression (Lemma
16
+ Lemmas

,

\tfrac{P^{(\mathrm{CE})}_{1}}{P_{p}^{\mathrm{(gen)}}}=\exp\bigl(\Theta(n^{2})\bigr).

In this asymptotic regime the constant

.

If instead one insists on tracking the

Materials and Methods

3.1
Walsh–Fourier/Krawtchouk Analysis

We seek to develop a Walsh–Fourier/Krawtchouk analysis that upper bounds the feasible mass of one–layer generic QAOA. Throughout,
N=n^{2}
,
\Pi\subset\{0,1\}^{N}
is the feasible set of
n\times n
permutation–matrix decoded from the sampled bitstrings, hence
|\Pi|=n!
and

\ket{\psi_1^{\mathrm{(gen)}}(\beta,\gamma)}\;=\;U_{X}(\beta)\,U_{C}(\gamma)\,\ket{+}^{\otimes N},\quad U_{X}(\beta)=e^{-i\beta\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}},\quad U_{C}(\gamma)=e^{-i\gamma H_{C}}.

With
H_{C}
diagonal in the computational basis, we can write

H_{C}=\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{N}}C(y)\,|y\rangle\!\langle y|\quad\Rightarrow\quad U_{C}(\gamma)=e^{-i\gamma H_{C}}=\sum_{y}e^{-i\gamma C(y)}\,|y\rangle\!\langle y|.

Acting on the uniform superposition gives

U_{C}(\gamma)|+\rangle^{\otimes N}=2^{-N/2}\sum_{y}e^{-i\gamma C(y)}|y\rangle.

With the phase field
\phi_{\gamma}(y):=e^{-i\gamma C(y)}
and any basis vector
|x\rangle
we obtain

a_{\beta,\gamma}(x)=\langle x|U_{X}(\beta)U_{C}(\gamma)|+\rangle^{\otimes N}=2^{-N/2}\sum_{y}\underbrace{\langle x|U_{X}(\beta)|y\rangle}_{K_{\beta}(x,y)}\,\phi_{\gamma}(y).

Because
U_{X}(\beta)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}e^{-i\beta X_{i}}
, its
Z
-basis kernel factors as

K_{\beta}(x,y)\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{N}\langle x_{i}|e^{-i\beta X}|y_{i}\rangle\;=\;(\cos\beta)^{\,N-d(x,y)}(-i\sin\beta)^{\,d(x,y)}\;=\;(\cos\beta)^{N}\,(-i\tan\beta)^{\,d(x,y)},

(22)

where
d(x,y)=|\;x\oplus y\;|
is the Hamming distance. Thus the output amplitudes are

a_{\beta,\gamma}(x)\;=\;2^{-N/2}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{N}}K_{\beta}(x,y)\,\phi_{\gamma}(y).

(23)

For normalization, note that
K_{\beta}
are matrix elements of the unitary
U_{X}(\beta)
, and compute

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}|a_{\beta,\gamma}(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=2^{-N}\sum_{x}\left|\sum_{y}K_{\beta}(x,y)\,\phi_{\gamma}(y)\right|^{2}\;=\;2^{-N}\,\|\,U_{X}(\beta)\,\phi_{\gamma}\,\|_{2}^{2}

(24)

\displaystyle=2^{-N}\,\|\,\phi_{\gamma}\,\|_{2}^{2}\;=\;2^{-N}\sum_{y}|\phi_{\gamma}(y)|^{2}\;=\;2^{-N}\cdot 2^{N}\;=\;1,

(25)

because
|\phi_{\gamma}(y)|\equiv 1
. Thus the amplitudes
a_{\beta,\gamma}(x)
are correctly normalized.

3.2
Walsh–Fourier primer

We write
[N]=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
. For a subset
S\subseteq[N]
, let
s\in\{0,1\}^{N}

be its indicator vector,
s_{i}=\mathbf{1}_{\{i\in S\}}
, and we freely identify
S
with
s
. The Walsh characters on the Boolean cube are
[
29
]

\chi_{S}(x)\ :=\ (-1)^{S\cdot x},\qquad S\subseteq[N].

For
x\in\{0,1\}^{N}
, define the mod-2 (parity) dot product

S\cdot x\;\coloneqq\;\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}x_{i}\Bigr)\bmod 2\;\in\;\{0,1\}.

For
S,T\subseteq[N]
, write
S\oplus T
for the symmetric difference
(bitwise XOR of indicators). Therefore:

S\oplus T\;\coloneqq\;(S\setminus T)\cup(T\setminus S),

and

\displaystyle(S\oplus T)\cdot x

\displaystyle\equiv(S\cdot x)+(T\cdot x)\pmod{2}

(26)

\displaystyle\equiv(S\cdot x)\oplus(T\cdot x).

(27)

So if
t
is the indicator of
T
, then
S\oplus T
corresponds to
s\oplus t
. The group law
\chi_{S}(x)\chi_{T}(x)=\chi_{S\oplus T}(x)
will be used repeatedly.

We use the (normalized) Walsh–Fourier transform is given as

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(S)\ :=\ 2^{-N}\!\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}f(x)\,\chi_{S}(x),\qquad f(x)\ =\ \sum_{S\subseteq[N]}\widehat{f}(S)\,\chi_{S}(x).

(28)

With this normalization, the hat
\widehat{\phantom{f}}
denotes the Walsh–Fourier transform on the Boolean cube with characters

\chi_{S}(x):=(-1)^{S\cdot x}
for
S\subseteq[N]
. Parseval/Plancherel reads
[
29
]

\sum_{x}|f(x)|^{2}\ =\ 2^{N}\sum_{S}|\widehat{f}(S)|^{2},\qquad 2^{-N}\!\sum_{x}\overline{f(x)}\,g(x)\ =\ \sum_{S}\overline{\widehat{f}(S)}\,\widehat{g}(S).

For functions
f:\{0,1\}^{N}\to\mathbb{C}
we use the counting inner product

\langle f,g\rangle:=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\overline{f(x)}\,g(x)

(and the normalized version

\langle\!\langle f,g\rangle\!\rangle:=2^{-N}\langle f,g\rangle

when convenient).

One could view
K_{\beta}
from Eq.
22
as a convolution on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{N}
:

(K_{\beta}f)(x)\ :=\ \sum_{y}K_{\beta}(x,y)\,f(y)\ =\ \sum_{z}k_{\beta}(z)\,f(x\oplus z),

with the one-bit kernel
k_{\beta}(0)=\cos\beta
and
k_{\beta}(1)=-i\sin\beta
and

k_{\beta}(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}k_{\beta}(z_{i})
.

Lemma 11

(Diagonalization of
K_{\beta}
in the Walsh basis)
.

For all
S\subseteq[N]
,

\widehat{(K_{\beta}f)}(S)\ =\ \lambda_{\beta}(|S|)\,\widehat{f}(S),\qquad\lambda_{\beta}(|S|)\ =\ e^{-i\beta\,(N-2|S|)}.

In particular,
|\lambda_{\beta}(|S|)|=1
, so
K_{\beta}
preserves Walsh magnitudes

|\widehat{f}(S)|
.

Proof sketch.

On one bit, the convolution theorem with our normalization gives

\widehat{k*f}(t)=2\,\widehat{k}(t)\,\widehat{f}(t)
for
t\in\{0,1\}
, where

\widehat{k}(t)=2^{-1}\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}}k(z)(-1)^{tz}
.
With
k(0)=\cos\beta
,
k(1)=-i\sin\beta
,

2\,\widehat{k}(0)=\cos\beta-i\sin\beta=e^{-i\beta},\qquad 2\,\widehat{k}(1)=\cos\beta+i\sin\beta=e^{i\beta}.

By tensoring over
N
bits, frequencies factorize. For a mask
S
with
|S|
ones,

\lambda_{\beta}(|S|)=e^{-i\beta}
on zero-frequencies and
e^{i\beta}
on one-frequencies,
hence
\lambda_{\beta}(|S|)=e^{-i\beta(N-|S|)}e^{i\beta|S|}=e^{-i\beta(N-2|S|)}
.
∎

Permutation–feasibility indicator.

We write
\mathbf{1}_{A}:\{0,1\}^{N}\to\{0,1\}
for the
indicator
of a set
A
, i.e.

\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x\in A,\\
0,&x\notin A.\end{cases}

In the permutation encoding (e.g. for TSP) we fix
N=n^{2}
and identify each bitstring

x\in\{0,1\}^{N}
with an
n\times n
binary matrix

X(x)\;=\;\bigl(x_{i,j}\bigr)_{1\leq i,j\leq n},

where
x_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
records whether city
j
is placed at tour position
i
.
We say that
x
is
permutation–feasible
if and only if
X(x)
is a
permutation matrix, i.e.

\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{i,j}\;=\;1\quad\text{for all rows }i\in[n],\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,j}\;=\;1\quad\text{for all columns }j\in[n].

Let
\Pi\subset\{0,1\}^{N}
denote the set of all such bitstrings, so

|\Pi|=n!
. The permutation–feasibility indicator is

\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}(x)\;=\;\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }x\in\Pi\text{ (i.e.\ $X(x)$ is a permutation matrix)},\\[2.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Equivalently, if we write

R(x)\;:=\;\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\Bigl[\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{i,j}=1\Bigr],\qquad C(x)\;:=\;\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,j}=1\Bigr],

for the row– and column–one–hot indicators, then

\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}(x)\;=\;R(x)\,C(x),\qquad\|\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}\|_{2}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{x}\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}(x)\;=\;|\Pi|\;=\;n!.

With this notation, the feasible probability mass can be written as the Walsh correlation

P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\;=\;2^{-N}\sum_{x}\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}(x)\,|g(x)|^{2}.

Then

P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\ =\ 2^{-N}\sum_{x}\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}(x)\,|g(x)|^{2}\ =\ \sum_{S\subseteq[N]}\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}}(S)\,\widehat{|g|^{2}}(S).

where
g=K_{\beta}\phi_{\gamma}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}}
is the Walsh transform
of the permutation–feasibility indicator. Writing
b:=\widehat{\phi_{\gamma}}
and using Lemma
11
,

\widehat{|g|^{2}}(S)\ =\ \sum_{T\subseteq[N]}\lambda_{\beta}(|T|)\,\overline{\lambda_{\beta}(|T\oplus S|)}\;b(T)\,\overline{b(T\oplus S)},\qquad\sum_{T}|b(T)|^{2}\ =\ 1,

so
P_{\Pi}
is controlled by the Walsh autocorrelations of the phase field and the Fourier profile of the feasible indicator.

3.3
Krawtchouk Polynomials on the Hamming sphere

Let
g_{n}
be the “exactly–one” (one–hot) indicator on
n
bits, and let

\Pi
be the intersection of
2n
block one–hot constraints plus the
row–column bijection (permutation) constraint. The Walsh spectrum of
g_{n}
is explicit via Krawtchouk polynomials
[
38
,
29
]
. In particular,it allows us to show via explicit computation that low degrees carry
exponentially small mass. To see this, we work on the Boolean cube
\{0,1\}^{n}
with Walsh characters
\chi_{S}(x)=(-1)^{S\cdot x}
for
S\subseteq[n]

and the normalization

\widehat{f}(S)\ :=\ 2^{-n}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}f(x)\,\chi_{S}(x),\qquad f(x)\ =\ \sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)\,\chi_{S}(x)

as in Eq.
28
.

Let
g_{w}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
denote the Hamming–sphere indicator
g_{w}(x)=\mathbf{1}_{\{|x|=w\}}
.
In particular, the one–hot indicator is
g_{1}
(support
\{x:|x|=1\}
). For
0\leq w\leq n
, the (unnormalized) binary Krawtchouk polynomials are

K_{w}^{(n)}(r)\ :=\ \sum_{j=0}^{w}(-1)^{j}\binom{r}{j}\binom{n-r}{\,w-j\,},\qquad r=0,1,\dots,n.

(29)

They satisfy the following generating function and orthogonality identities respectively
[
38
]

\displaystyle\sum_{w=0}^{n}K_{w}^{(n)}(r)\,z^{w}\

\displaystyle=\ (1+z)^{\,n-r}(1-z)^{\,r},

(30)

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}\,K_{w}^{(n)}(r)\,K_{w^{\prime}}^{(n)}(r)\

\displaystyle=\ 2^{n}\binom{n}{w}\,\delta_{w,w^{\prime}}.

(31)

Lemma 12

(Krawtchouk transform of a Hamming sphere)
.

For
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|=r
,

\sum_{x:\,|x|=w}\chi_{S}(x)\;=\;K_{w}^{(n)}(r).

Consequently,

\widehat{g_{w}}(S)\;=\;2^{-n}\,K_{w}^{(n)}\!\bigl(|S|\bigr).

(32)

Proof.

Partition the coordinates into the
r
positions where
S
has 1s and the
n-r
positions where
S
has 0s.
To form an
x
with
|x|=w
, choose
j
ones among the
r
“active” positions and
w-j
ones among the remaining
n-r
positions.
There are
\binom{r}{j}\binom{n-r}{w-j}
such
x
, and each contributes a phase
(-1)^{j}
because
\chi_{S}(x)=(-1)^{S\cdot x}
counts parity on the
r
active positions only.
Summing over
j
gives (
29
), hence the first claim.
Applying the Walsh transform definition to
g_{w}
,

\widehat{g_{w}}(S)=2^{-n}\sum_{x}g_{w}(x)\chi_{S}(x)=2^{-n}\sum_{|x|=w}\chi_{S}(x)=2^{-n}K_{w}^{(n)}(|S|).

∎

K_{1}^{(n)}(r)\ =\ \binom{r}{0}\binom{n-r}{1}-\binom{r}{1}\binom{n-r}{0}\ =\ (n-r)-r\ =\ n-2r.

Thus, for
g_{1}
(the “exactly one” indicator),

\widehat{g_{1}}(S)\ =\ 2^{-n}\bigl(n-2|S|\bigr),\qquad S\subseteq[n],

(33)

which matches the direct computation
\widehat{g_{1}}(S)=2^{-n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{\mathbf{1}_{\{i\in S\}}}=2^{-n}(n-2|S|)
.

Normalization/Parseval sanity check using (
32
) and (
31
) gives,

\sum_{x}|g_{w}(x)|^{2}=\binom{n}{w}\ =\ 2^{n}\sum_{S}|\widehat{g_{w}}(S)|^{2}\ =\ 2^{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}\,\bigl(2^{-n}K_{w}^{(n)}(r)\bigr)^{2},

which is exactly (
31
) with
w^{\prime}=w
. We also have

\|g_{1}\|_{2}^{2}=\binom{n}{1}=n
.

Lemma 13

(Low–degree Fourier weight for
g_{1}
)
.

For any fixed degree cutoff
d\geq 0
, where
d\in\mathbb{N}
denotes a
Fourier degree cutoff

\sum_{\,|S|\leq d}\!\bigl|\widehat{g_{1}}(S)\bigr|^{2}\ =\ 2^{-2n}\sum_{r=0}^{d}\binom{n}{r}\,(n-2r)^{2}\ \leq\ 2^{-2n}\,C_{d}\,n^{\,d+2},

for some constant
C_{d}
depending only on
d
(e.g.
C_{d}=2(d+1)
for all
n\geq 2
).
Equivalently, the degree–
\leq d
weight is at most
C_{d}\,n^{\,d+1}\,2^{-n}
of the total
\|g_{1}\|_{2}^{2}=n
.

Proof.

Insert (
33
) and group by
r=|S|
:

\sum_{|S|\leq d}\!\bigl|\widehat{g_{1}}(S)\bigr|^{2}=2^{-2n}\sum_{r=0}^{d}\binom{n}{r}\,(n-2r)^{2}.

For
r\leq d
we have
\binom{n}{r}\leq n^{r}
and
(n-2r)^{2}\leq n^{2}
, hence

\sum_{r=0}^{d}\binom{n}{r}\,(n-2r)^{2}\ \leq\ n^{2}\sum_{r=0}^{d}n^{r}\ =\ n^{2}\,\frac{n^{d+1}-1}{n-1}\ \leq\ 2(d+1)\,n^{\,d+1}\qquad(n\geq 2).

Multiplying by
2^{-2n}
yields the claimed bound with
C_{d}=2(d+1)
.
∎

The Krawtchouk identity
\widehat{g_{w}}(S)=2^{-n}K_{w}^{(n)}(|S|)
shows that the Walsh spectrum
of any Hamming–sphere indicator is a radial (shell–dependent) polynomial of
|S|
.
For the one–hot layer (
w=1
),
\widehat{g_{1}}(S)
is exactly linear in
|S|
,
and its low–degree weight decays exponentially in
n
after summation over shells, as quantified in Lemma
13
. Tensorizing across
m=2n
blocks and incorporating the bijection constraint yields an additional sparse factor that does not increase low–degree mass beyond a polynomial.

3.4
Route 1: Harmonic Analysis of the probability profile

To go beyond
n-
bit rows and make the Fourier weight decay on the permutation feasible subspace explicit, we shall use the following technical lemmas. Lemma
14
is an adaptation from Alon and Spencer (Lemma 2, p. 64,
[
36
]
) while Lemma
15
is a technical tool to control the resulting inequality.

Lemma 14
.

For every integer
n\geq 2
,

\bigl((n-1)!\bigr)^{\,n(n+1)}\cdot\bigl((n+1)!\bigr)^{\,n(n-1)}\;<\;\bigl(n!\bigr)^{\,2(n^{2}-1)},

equivalently,

\left(\frac{n^{n}}{n!}\right)^{\!2}\;>\;\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^{\,n(n-1)}.

Proof.

The two forms are equivalent by dividing both sides of the first inequality by

\bigl((n-1)!\,(n+1)!\bigr)^{\,n(n-1)}
and simplifying.

Base case
n=2
.
We have

\left(\frac{2^{2}}{2!}\right)^{\!2}=\left(\frac{4}{2}\right)^{2}=4\quad\text{and}\quad\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{9}{4},

so the inequality holds.

Case
n\geq 3
.
By Arithmetic Mean–Geometric Mean inequality ( AM–GM),

n!\;=\;\prod_{k=1}^{n}k\;\leq\;\left(\frac{1+\cdots+n}{n}\right)^{\!n}=\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{\!n}.

Hence

\left(\frac{n^{n}}{n!}\right)^{\!2}\;\geq\;\left(\frac{n^{n}}{\bigl(\tfrac{n+1}{2}\bigr)^{n}}\right)^{\!2}=\left(\frac{2n}{n+1}\right)^{\!2n}.

Using
(1+\tfrac{1}{n})^{n}<e
we get

\frac{2n}{n+1}=\frac{2}{1+\tfrac{1}{n}}>2e^{-1/n},\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\left(\frac{2n}{n+1}\right)^{\!2n}>4^{n}e^{-2}.

For
n\geq 3
we have
4^{n}>e^{\,n+1}
, hence
4^{n}e^{-2}>e^{\,n-1}
. Therefore

\left(\frac{n^{n}}{n!}\right)^{\!2}>e^{\,n-1}.

On the other hand,
(1+\tfrac{1}{n})^{n}<e
implies

\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^{\,n(n-1)}=\bigl((1+\tfrac{1}{n})^{n}\bigr)^{\,n-1}<e^{\,n-1}.

Combining the last two displays yields

\left(\frac{n^{n}}{n!}\right)^{\!2}\;>\;\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^{\,n(n-1)}.

∎

Lemma 15
.

Let
c_{T}>0
and set
a:=\tfrac{c_{T}\ln 2}{2}
. Then for all

n\;\geq\;N(c_{T})\ :=\ \max\!\Bigl\{9,\ \Bigl\lceil\tfrac{4}{a^{2}}\Bigr\rceil\Bigr\}

we have
\,n^{n}\,2^{-c_{T}n^{2}/2}<1
.

Proof.

For
n\geq 9
we have
\ln n<\sqrt{n}
, and for
n\geq 4/a^{2}
we have

\sqrt{n}\leq an
. Hence
\ln n<an
, so

n\ln n-an^{2}\;<\;0,

which implies
n^{n}2^{-c_{T}n^{2}/2}=\exp(n\ln n-a n^2)<1
.
∎

Lemma 16

(Low–degree mass of
\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}
)
.

Let
N=n^{2}
and
\Pi\subset\{0,1\}^{N}
be the set of
n\times n
permutation
matrices (row– and column–one–hot). For every fixed degree cutoff
d\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
constants
c_{d},C_{d}>0
such that

\sum_{|S|\leq d}\,|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}}(S)|^{2}\;\leq\;C_{d}\,n^{O(d)}\,2^{-cn^{2}}\,|\Pi|^{2}R_{n};\qquad\|\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}\|_{2}^{2}=|\Pi|=n!\ \textit{and }R_{n}\;>\;\Bigl(\tfrac{n+1}{n}\Bigr)^{n(n-1)}

Proof.

Write the indicator of
row
one–hot as

R(x)\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{n}g^{(i)}_{1}\!\bigl(x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,n}\bigr),

where each
g_{1}^{(i)}
is the (one–bit–weight) indicator on the
i
-th row block
(size
n
). Similarly, the
column
one–hot indicator factors as

C(x)\;=\;\prod_{j=1}^{n}g^{(j)}_{1}\!\bigl(x_{1,j},\dots,x_{n,j}\bigr).

Then
\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}(x)=R(x)\,C(x)
and, in Walsh–Fourier space,

\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}}\;=\;\widehat{R\,C}\;=\;\widehat{R}*\widehat{C},

where
*
is convolution on the frequency group
(\{0,1\}^{N},\oplus)
:

(f*g)(S)=\sum_{T}f(T)\,g(S\oplus T)
.

Step 1: Tensorization across blocks (row side).

By block disjointness of the rows and the factorization of characters,
the Walsh transform
factorizes
:
for
S\subseteq[N]
and
S_{i}:=S\cap\{\text{positions of row }i\}
,

\widehat{R}(S)\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{g_{1}}\bigl(S_{i}\bigr).

By Lemma
13
(the Krawtchouk transform with
w=1
),

\widehat{g_{1}}(T)=2^{-n}\bigl(n-2|T|\bigr)
for any
T\subseteq[n]
. Note that since a single
n
-bit one–hot block
g_{1}
has
\widehat{g_{1}}(T)=2^{-n}(n-2|T|)
.
Tensoring across
n
disjoint rows gives
\|\widehat{R}\|_{2}^{2}=2^{-n^{2}}n^{n}
, and
similarly for columns
\|\widehat{C}\|_{2}^{2}=2^{-n^{2}}n^{n}
.
Hence

\widehat{R}(S)\;=\;2^{-n^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(n-2|S_{i}|\bigr),\qquad\|\widehat{R}\|_{2}^{2}\;=\;2^{-N}\,\|R\|_{2}^{2}\;=\;2^{-N}\,n^{n}.

(34)

An analogous factorization holds for
C
, partitioning
S
by columns:

\widehat{C}(S)=2^{-n^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bigl(n-2|S^{(j)}|\bigr)
and

\|\widehat{C}\|_{2}^{2}=2^{-N}n^{n}
.

Step 2: Convolution bound onto a low–degree window.

Let
E_{d}:=\{S\subseteq[N]:|S|\leq d\}
denote the degree–
\leq d
frequency window.
For any
S
,

(\widehat{R}*\widehat{C})(S)=\sum_{T}\widehat{R}(T)\widehat{C}(S\oplus T).

By Cauchy–Schwarz in the
T

S\in E_{d}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in E_{d}}|(\widehat{R}*\widehat{C})(S)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S\in E_{d}}\Bigl(\sum_{T}|\widehat{R}(T)|^{2}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{T}|\widehat{C}(S\oplus T)|^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sum_{T}|\widehat{R}(T)|^{2}\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{S\in E_{d}}\sum_{T}|\widehat{C}(S\oplus T)|^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\|\widehat{R}\|_{2}^{2}\;\bigl|E_{d}\bigr|\;\|\widehat{C}\|_{2}^{2}\;=\;\bigl|E_{d}\bigr|\;\cdot\;2^{-2N}\,n^{2n},

using
\sum_{T}|\widehat{C}(S\oplus T)|^{2}=\|\widehat{C}\|_{2}^{2}
(shift invariance).
The window size satisfies, for fixed
d
, the standard bound

|E_{d}|=\sum_{d=0}^{d}\binom{N}{d}\;\leq\;\Bigl(\frac{eN}{d}\Bigr)^{\!d}\;=\;N^{O(d)}\;=\;n^{O(d)}.

Therefore

\sum_{|S|\leq d}|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}}(S)|^{2}\;\leq\;C_{d}\,N^{O(d)}\;\cdot\;2^{-2N}\,n^{2n}\;=\;C_{d}\,n^{O(d)}\;2^{-2n^{2}}\,n^{2n}.

(35)

Step 3: using Lemma
14
on (
35
)

\sum_{|S|\leq d}|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}}(S)|^{2}\;\leq\;C_{d}\,n^{O(d)}\,2^{-2n^{2}}\,n^{2n}.

Insert the exact factorization

n^{2n}\;=\;(n!)^{2}\!\left(\frac{n^{n}}{n!}\right)^{\!2}\;=\;(n!)^{2}\,R_{n},\qquad R_{n}:=\left(\frac{n^{n}}{n!}\right)^{\!2}.

By Lemma
14
(equivalent form),

R_{n}\;>\;\Bigl(\tfrac{n+1}{n}\Bigr)^{n(n-1)}\;=\;\exp\!\bigl(\Theta(n)\bigr),

so the multiplicative gap between
n^{2n}
and
(n!)^{2}
is only
\exp(\Theta(n))
.
Therefore,

\sum_{|S|\leq d}|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}}(S)|^{2}\;\leq\;C_{d}\,n^{O(d)}\,2^{-2n^{2}}\,(n!)^{2}R_{n}.

Using
\|\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}\|_{2}^{2}=|\Pi|=n!
, we get

\sum_{|S|\leq d}|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}}(S)|^{2}\;\leq\;C_{d}\,n^{O(d)}\,2^{-cn^{2}}\,|\Pi|^{2}R_{n}.

∎

The
2^{-n}
per block across
n
rows and
n
columns is the source of the
2^{-\Theta(n^{2})}
decay. To bound the total probability profile, write the low/high–degree split of the contributuions to toal probability as
p=p^{\leq 2T}+p^{>2T}
, where
p^{\leq 2T}
denotes the Walsh truncation to degrees
\leq 2T
and effectively identify
d=2T
. We use Lemma
14
to replace
n^{2n}

by
(n!)^{2}R_{n}
, exposing the
(n!)^{2}
factor explicitly.
R_{n}
remains suppressed by

. Additional suppression will come from the normalization terms as we shall see in the next Lemmas.

Lemma 17

(Low–degree probability mass contribution)
.

For every cutoff degree
T\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist a constant
C_{T}>0
s.t.

2^{-N}\,\bigl\langle\mathbf{1}_{\Pi},\;p^{\leq 2T}\bigr\rangle\;\leq\;\frac{C_{T}}{2^{N}}\;n^{O(T)}

Proof.

By Plancherel,

2^{-N}\,\langle\mathbf{1}_{\Pi},p^{\leq 2T}\rangle=\sum_{|S|\leq 2T}\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}}(S)\,\overline{\widehat{p}(S)}\;\leq\;\Bigl(\sum_{|S|\leq 2T}|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}}(S)|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{|S|\leq 2T}|\widehat{p}(S)|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2},

Using Lemma
16
in
fractional-energy
form

\sum_{|S|\leq 2T}|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}}(S)|^{2}\leq C_{T}n^{O(T)}2^{-c_{T}n^{2}}\cdot|\Pi|^{2}R_{n}.

For the second factor, use

\sum_{S}|\widehat{p}(S)|^{2}=2^{-N}\|p\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2^{-N}
since
p
is a pmf.
Collecting terms gives

2^{-N}\,\bigl\langle\mathbf{1}_{\Pi},\;p^{\leq 2T}\bigr\rangle\;\leq\;\Bigl(C_{T}n^{O(T)}2^{-c_{T}n^{2}}\tfrac{|\Pi|}{2^{N}}\Bigr)^{\!1/2}\Bigl(\tfrac{R_{n}\Pi}{2^{N}}\Bigr)^{\!1/2}\;\leq\;\frac{{|\Pi|}}{2^{N}}\;\sqrt{R_{n}}.C_{T}n^{O(T)}2^{-c_{T}n^{2}/2}

Recall that
R_{n}:=\left(\frac{n^{n}}{n!}\right)^{\!2}
so that
\sqrt{R_{n}}=\left(\frac{n^{n}}{n!}\right)=\frac{n^{n}}{|\Pi|}.

Therefore:

2^{-N}\,\bigl\langle\mathbf{1}_{\Pi},\;p^{\leq 2T}\bigr\rangle\;\leq\;\frac{{n^{n}}}{2^{N}}\;.C_{T}n^{O(T)}2^{-c_{T}n^{2}/2}\;\leq\;\frac{{C_{T}}}{2^{N}}\;.n^{O(T)}

Where we have used the fact that
n^{n}\cdot 2^{-c_{T}n^{2}/2}<1
from Lemma
15
in the last step.
∎

Lemma 18

(High–degree contribution)
.

For all
T\in\mathbb{N}
and all angles
(\beta,\gamma)
,

2^{-N}\,\bigl|\langle\mathbf{1}_{\Pi},\;p^{>2T}\rangle\bigr|\;\leq\;\frac{\|\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}\|_{2}}{2^{N}}\,\|p^{>2T}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{\sqrt{|\Pi|}}{2^{N}}.

Moreover, without additional structure on
\phi_{\gamma}
, one cannot obtain a
uniform
T
–decaying bound here because the Walsh multipliers of
K_{\beta}
satisfy

|\kappa_{\beta}(t)|=1
for all

(pure phases), so there is no degree–attenuation at
p=1
.

Proof.

Cauchy–Schwarz gives

|\langle\mathbf{1}_{\Pi},p^{>2T}\rangle|\leq\|\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}\|_{2}\,\|p^{>2T}\|_{2}
.
Then normalize by
2^{N}
to get the first inequality. Since
p^{>2T}
is the orthogonal Walsh projection of
p
,
\|p^{>2T}\|_{2}\leq\|p\|_{2}
.
Because
p
is a pmf,
\|p\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{x}p(x)^{2}\leq\sum_{x}p(x)\|p\|_{\infty}=1
, hence
\|p^{>2T}\|_{2}\leq 1
.
Also
\|\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}\|_{2}=\sqrt{|\Pi|}
.
For the remark,
\kappa_{\beta}(t)=e^{-i\beta(N-2t)}
so
|\kappa_{\beta}(t)|=1
.
∎

Adding column one-hot (turning “row-one-hot” into permutation matrices) multiplies by a sparse indicator that enforces n extra constraints. In Fourier space that becomes a convolution that can at worst blow up the low-degree mass by a polynomial factor in n and d (Cauchy–Schwarz on the convolution over the degree simplex).

Theorem 19

(Harmonic–analysis baseline)
.

There exist absolute constants
c,C>0
such that taking the cutoff degree
T\in\mathbb{N}
with
T=\lceil c\log n\rceil
,

P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\;\leq\;\frac{|\Pi|}{2^{N}}.

Proof.

Choosing
T=\lceil c\log n\rceil
, split
p=p^{\leq 2T}+p^{>2T}
. Then

P_{\Pi}=2^{-N}\langle\mathbf{1}_{\Pi},p^{\leq 2T}\rangle+2^{-N}\langle\mathbf{1}_{\Pi},p^{>2T}\rangle.

Low–degree part.

By Lemma
17
, setting
C=C_{T}
,

2^{-N}\,\bigl\langle\mathbf{1}_{\Pi},\;p^{\leq 2T}\bigr\rangle\;\leq\;\frac{{C}}{2^{N}}\;.n^{O(T)}

High–degree part.

By Lemma
18
,

2^{-N}\bigl|\langle\mathbf{1}_{\Pi},p^{>2T}\rangle\bigr|\;\leq\;\frac{\|\mathbf{1}_{\Pi}\|_{2}}{2^{N}}\;\|p^{>2T}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{\sqrt{|\Pi|}}{2^{N}}.

Summing the two contributions gives

P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\;\leq\;\frac{{C}}{2^{N}}\;.n^{O(T)}\;+\;\frac{\sqrt{|\Pi|}}{2^{N}}\;=\;\frac{({C\cdot n^{O(T)}+\sqrt{|\Pi|})}}{2^{N}}\;\;\leq\;\frac{|\Pi|}{2^{N}}.

Where we have used that the numerator sums to a quantity less that
|\Pi|
for any large
n
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.5
Route II: Parameter–Averaging (Exact Expectation and a Typical–Angles Bound)

In this section, we show that averaging over the
cost angle
pins the baseline feasible mass to the uniform value
|\Pi|/2^{N}
, independently of the mixer angle, provided the cost spectrum lies on a lattice (which holds after rescaling for the usual integer/rational Ising/TSP costs). This insight comes simple Fourier-averaging.

Recall that averaging over a
wide
distribution of the cost angle
\gamma
makes the oscillatory
phases
e^{-i\gamma\,\Delta}
cancel out for every fixed nonzero frequency
\Delta
.
When we expand
\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\!\left[|a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)|^{2}\right]
, the cross terms
carry factors
e^{-i\gamma\,[C(y)-C(z)]}
. A wide average over
\gamma
drives these
cross terms to
0
whenever
C(y)\neq C(z)
, leaving only the diagonal (unitarity) terms.
Thus the averaged mass returns to the uniform baseline. Exact degeneracies

C(y)=C(z)
cannot be averaged away (their phase is identically
1
), but they only

add
nonnegative contributions, so uniform baseline is recovered for
nondegenerate spectra (or after tiny tie-breaking), and baseline-or-higher otherwise. The main lattice averaging result is the following Lemma.

Lemma 20

(Exact angle–average under lattice spectrum)
.

Assume there exists
\omega>0
and integers
h(y)
such that
C(y)=\omega\,h(y)

for all
y\in\{0,1\}^{N}
(i.e.,
\mathrm{spec}(H_{C})\subseteq\omega\mathbb{Z}
and the spectrum lies on a lattice upon renormalization
[
39
]
).
Let
\gamma\sim\mathrm{Unif}\,[0,2\pi/\omega]
(independent of
\beta
). Then, for every fixed
\beta
and every
x
,

\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\bigl[\,|a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)|^{2}\,\bigr]\;=\;2^{-N},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\bigl[P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\bigr]\;=\;\frac{|\Pi|}{2^{N}}.

Proof.

Write
U_{xy}(\beta)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\langle x_{i}|e^{-i\beta X}|y_{i}\rangle
.
Then

a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)=2^{-N/2}\sum_{y}U_{xy}(\beta)\,e^{-i\gamma C(y)}=2^{-N/2}\sum_{y}U_{xy}(\beta)\,e^{-i\gamma\omega h(y)}.

Hence

\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\bigl[|a_{x}|^{2}\bigr]=2^{-N}\!\sum_{y,z}U_{xy}(\beta)\,U^{*}_{xz}(\beta)\,\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\!\bigl[e^{-i\gamma\omega(h(y)-h(z))}\bigr].

(36)

With
\gamma\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,2\pi/\omega]
and
m=h(y)-h(z)\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}[e^{-i\gamma\omega m}]=\mathbf{1}[m=0]
, killing all cross terms.
Thus
\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}[|a_{x}|^{2}]=2^{-N}\sum_{y}|U_{xy}(\beta)|^{2}=2^{-N}
by unitarity.
Summing over
x\in\Pi
gives the second claim.
∎

Remark 21

(Discrete grid averaging)
.

If
C(y)/\omega\in\tfrac{1}{L}\mathbb{Z}
for some
L\in\mathbb{N}
(common denominator),
then the
discrete
average over the grid

\Gamma=\{0,\tfrac{2\pi}{\omega L},\dots,\tfrac{2\pi(L-1)}{\omega L}\}
already yields

\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}|a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)|^{2}\;=\;2^{-N},\qquad\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\;=\;\frac{|\Pi|}{2^{N}}.

Lemma
20
yields an
expectation
baseline

|\Pi|/2^{N}
. To assert that
most
angles are near this baseline, we use
a one–liner.

Proposition 22

(Typical–angles upper bound via Markov)
.

For any
\beta
and any
t>1
,

\Pr_{\gamma}\!\Bigl[P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\;\geq\;t\cdot\frac{|\Pi|}{2^{N}}\Bigr]\;\leq\;\frac{1}{t}.

In particular, for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Pr_{\gamma}\!\Bigl[P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\;\leq\;n^{k}\cdot\frac{|\Pi|}{2^{N}}\Bigr]\;\geq\;1-\frac{1}{n^{k}}.

Proof.

Markov’s inequality with Lemma
20
.
∎

Lemma 23

(Riemann–Lebesgue averaging for nonlattice spectra)
.

Let
\{C(y)\}_{y\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary and fix
\beta
.
Let
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be any probability density, and for
s>0
define the widened
density
f_{s}(\gamma):=\frac{1}{s}\,f(\gamma/s)
. If
\gamma\sim f_{s}
is independent of
\beta
, then

\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\!\bigl[\,|a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)|^{2}\,\bigr]\;=\;2^{-N}\!\sum_{y,z}U_{xy}(\beta)\,U^{*}_{xz}(\beta)\;\widehat{f}\!\bigl(s\,[C(y)-C(z)]\bigr),

where
\widehat{f}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\,e^{-i\xi t}\,dt
is the Fourier transform.
By the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma,
\widehat{f}(\xi)\to 0
as
|\xi|\to\infty
, hence for every fixed
nonzero difference
\Delta=C(y)-C(z)\neq 0
the corresponding cross term vanishes as
s\to\infty
:

\widehat{f}(s\Delta)\to 0
. In particular, if the spectrum is nondegenerate,

\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\!\bigl[\,|a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)|^{2}\,\bigr]\;=\;2^{-N}\sum_{y}\bigl|U_{xy}(\beta)\bigr|^{2}\;=\;2^{-N},

and consequently
\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\!\bigl[P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\bigr]=|\Pi|/2^{N}
.

Remark 24

(Two concrete averaging choices.)
.

Let
\Delta_{\min}:=\min\{\,|C(y)-C(z)|:\ y\neq z\,\}
denote the minimal nonzero spectral gap
(if it exists). Two convenient distributions give explicit decay:

1.

Wide uniform window.
If
\gamma\sim\mathrm{Unif}[-S,S]
, then for any fixed
\Delta\neq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-i\gamma\,\Delta}\big]\;=\;\frac{1}{2S}\!\int_{-S}^{S}e^{-i\Delta\gamma}\,d\gamma\;=\;\frac{\sin(S\Delta)}{S\Delta}\;\xrightarrow[S\to\infty]{}\;0,

with the bound
\big|\sin(S\Delta)/(S\Delta)\big|\leq\min\{\,1,\;1/(S|\Delta|)\,\}
.
In particular, if
\Delta_{\min}>0
, then the worst cross-term magnitude is
\leq 1/(S\Delta_{\min})
.

2.

Wide Gaussian.
If
\gamma\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
, then for any fixed
\Delta\neq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-i\gamma\,\Delta}\big]\;=\;e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\Delta^{2}}\;\xrightarrow[\sigma\to\infty]{}\;0,

and the worst cross-term magnitude is
\leq e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\Delta_{\min}^{2}}
when
\Delta_{\min}>0
.

Exact degeneracies (
\Delta=0
) produce phase
e^{-i\gamma\cdot 0}=1
and are not suppressed by averaging; they yield “baseline or higher.” Equality to the uniform baseline holds under nondegeneracy (or after benign tie-breaking). One can still recover the baseline in the limit by enlarging the averaging window or by averaging over a measure whose characteristic function vanishes on the set

\{C(y)-C(z):y\neq z\}
(Riemann–Lebesgue Lemma
23
). For our purposes, the lattice assumption
(or its discrete-grid variant) is satisfied by the standard integer/rational
QAOA costs after a global rescaling of
\gamma
.

3.6
Route III: A Fourth–Moment (Hypercontractive–Style) Bound

The third route upper bounds
P_{\Pi}
for
most

(\beta,\gamma)
using the
\ell^{4}
–norm
[
29
]
of the output amplitude vector. Recall
from Eq.
23

a(x)=a_{\beta,\gamma}(x)=2^{-N/2}g(x)
with
g=K_{\beta}\phi_{\gamma}
. By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\sum_{x\in\Pi}|a_{x}|^{2}\;\leq\;\sqrt{|\Pi|}\,\Bigl(\sum_{x}|a_{x}|^{4}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

(37)

Thus, any bound on
\sum_{x}|a_{x}|^{4}
yields an upper bound on
P_{\Pi}
. The following holds.

Lemma 25

(Fourth moment under
\gamma
–averaging)
.

For any fixed
\beta
,

\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\!\Bigl[\sum_{x}|a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)|^{4}\Bigr]\;\leq\;\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}+\cos^{2}\beta\,\sin^{2}\beta\Bigr)^{N}\;=\;\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\sin^{2}(2\beta)\Bigr)^{N}.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\bigl[P_{\Pi}(\beta,\gamma)\bigr]\;\leq\;\sqrt{|\Pi|}\,\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}+\cos^{2}\beta\,\sin^{2}\beta\Bigr)^{N/2}.

Proof.

Recall from (
23
) that

a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)\;=\;2^{-N/2}\,g_{\beta,\gamma}(x),\qquad g_{\beta,\gamma}\;=\;K_{\beta}\phi_{\gamma},

where
K_{\beta}
is the mixer kernel and
\phi_{\gamma}(x)=e^{-i\gamma C(x)}
is the cost–phase field. We also have the explicit expression

a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)\;=\;2^{-N/2}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{N}}U_{xy}(\beta)\,e^{-i\gamma C(y)},

with

U_{xy}(\beta)\;=\;\prod_{j=1}^{N}\,\bra{x_j}e^{-i\beta X}\ket{y_j}

the product mixer matrix element. For convenience set

f_{y}(\gamma)\;:=\;e^{-i\gamma C(y)},\qquad\Delta(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})\;:=\;C(y_{1})-C(y_{2})+C(y_{3})-C(y_{4}).

Step 1: Expand the fourth power explicitly.

Fix
x\in\{0,1\}^{N}
. Then

\displaystyle|a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)|^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)\,\overline{a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)}

\displaystyle=2^{-N}\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}}U_{xy_{1}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{2}}(\beta)}\,f_{y_{1}}(\gamma)\,\overline{f_{y_{2}}(\gamma)}

\displaystyle=2^{-N}\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}}U_{xy_{1}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{2}}(\beta)}\,e^{-i\gamma\,[C(y_{1})-C(y_{2})]}.

(38)

Squaring once more and using independent dummy indices
(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})
we get

\displaystyle|a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)|^{4}

\displaystyle=\bigl(|a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)|^{2}\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=2^{-2N}\sum_{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}}U_{xy_{1}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{2}}(\beta)}\,U_{xy_{3}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{4}}(\beta)}

\displaystyle\hskip 70.0001pt\times e^{-i\gamma\,[C(y_{1})-C(y_{2})+C(y_{3})-C(y_{4})]}

\displaystyle=2^{-2N}\sum_{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}}U_{xy_{1}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{2}}(\beta)}\,U_{xy_{3}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{4}}(\beta)}\,e^{-i\gamma\,\Delta(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})}.

(39)

Summing over all
x
gives the exact fourth moment of the amplitude vector:

\sum_{x}|a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)|^{4}\;=\;2^{-2N}\!\!\sum_{x}\sum_{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}}U_{xy_{1}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{2}}(\beta)}\,U_{xy_{3}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{4}}(\beta)}\,e^{-i\gamma\,\Delta(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})}.

(40)

Step 2: Average over the cost angle
\gamma
.

Assume
C(y)=\omega h(y)
with
h(y)\in\mathbb{Z}
and

\gamma\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,2\pi/\omega]
as in Lemma
20
.
Then for any integer
m
,

\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\!\bigl[e^{-i\gamma\omega m}\bigr]\;=\;\mathbf{1}[m=0].

Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\Bigl[\sum_{x}|a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)|^{4}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=2^{-2N}\sum_{x}\sum_{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}}U_{xy_{1}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{2}}(\beta)}\,U_{xy_{3}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{4}}(\beta)}\,\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\!\bigl[e^{-i\gamma\omega\,\Delta(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3},h_{4})}\bigr]

\displaystyle=2^{-2N}\sum_{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}:\\
h(y_{1})-h(y_{2})+h(y_{3})-h(y_{4})=0\end{subarray}}U_{xy_{1}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{2}}(\beta)}\,U_{xy_{3}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{4}}(\beta)}.

(41)

The lattice averaging has therefore projected the quadruple sum onto those

(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})
whose costs satisfy the balancing condition

h(y_{1})+h(y_{3})=h(y_{2})+h(y_{4})
.

For an
upper bound
we can discard this constraint and simply observe that
the indicator is at most
1
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}\Bigl[\sum_{x}|a_{x}(\beta,\gamma)|^{4}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-2N}\sum_{x}\sum_{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}}\Bigl|U_{xy_{1}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{2}}(\beta)}\,U_{xy_{3}}(\beta)\,\overline{U_{xy_{4}}(\beta)}\Bigr|

\displaystyle=2^{-2N}\sum_{x}\sum_{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}}|U_{xy_{1}}(\beta)|\,|U_{xy_{2}}(\beta)|\,|U_{xy_{3}}(\beta)|\,|U_{xy_{4}}(\beta)|.

(42)

This step is where we deliberately lose the precise information about the cost
structure and retain only the mixer structure.

Step 3: Factorization over qubits.

Recall that

U_{xy}(\beta)\;=\;\prod_{j=1}^{N}u_{\beta}(x_{j},y_{j}),\qquad u_{\beta}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}\cos\beta&-i\sin\beta\\
-i\sin\beta&\cos\beta\end{pmatrix}.

Hence

|U_{xy}(\beta)|\;=\;\prod_{j=1}^{N}|u_{\beta}(x_{j},y_{j})|.

For each fixed
x
, the inner sum in (
42
) factorizes over the
qubits:

\displaystyle\sum_{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}|u_{\beta}(x_{j},y_{1,j})|\,|u_{\beta}(x_{j},y_{2,j})|\,|u_{\beta}(x_{j},y_{3,j})|\,|u_{\beta}(x_{j},y_{4,j})|

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4}\in\{0,1\}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{4}|u_{\beta}(x_{j},b_{\ell})|.

Define the one–bit quantity

A_{x_{j}}(\beta)\;:=\;\sum_{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4}\in\{0,1\}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{4}|u_{\beta}(x_{j},b_{\ell})|.

Then

\sum_{y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}}|U_{xy_{1}}(\beta)|\,|U_{xy_{2}}(\beta)|\,|U_{xy_{3}}(\beta)|\,|U_{xy_{4}}(\beta)|\;=\;\prod_{j=1}^{N}A_{x_{j}}(\beta).

Summing over

4
Conclusion and Outlook

This work reframes the computational power of shallow variational quantum algorithms through the lens of
feasibility concentration
. For permutation encodings on
n^{2}
qubits we prove that the generic QAOA cannot lift feasible mass much beyond the uniform baseline. The ceiling persists at constant depth and, under bounded degree, up to linear depth. In the same regime a minimal constraint–enhanced surrogate, CE–QAOA, achieves a depth-matched and angle-robust exponential gain in feasible mass. The separation follows from three transparent controls on the output distribution—angle averaging with low order moments, Boolean harmonic analysis, and light-cone locality.

The CE–QAOA kernel is not engineered here. We use it merely as a clean illustration of problem–algorithm co-design as a route to exponential enhancements. As we have demonstrated, even the minimal symmetry based bound we explored here was enough to achieve exponential enhancement. Beyond this baseline enhancement, we showed a parameter-transfer amplification result that combines the spirit of warmstarting QAOA and parameter transfer in QAOA. If
(\beta^{\star},\gamma^{\star})
maximize the feasible mass for generic
p{=}1
, then reusing those same angles inside the encoded kernel boosts success by at least

P^{(\mathrm{CE})}\ \geq\ \sqrt{2\pi n}\,e^{\,n}\;P^{(\mathrm{gen})}_{\max},

which makes parameter reuse a principled and testable strategy rather than a heuristic.

These results suggest that a simple design rule for globally constrained problems is to build feasibility into the ansatz and make the mixer operate
within
the constraint manifold. This converts a global coordination task that shallow generic circuits cannot sustain into a local exploration inside the encoded space, where light-cones grow at the block level and the baseline is information-theoretically favorable. Typical-angle and fourth-moment controls also serve as diagnostics when a generic shallow circuit appears to “fail to train.” The bounds here show that the obstacle is structural, not an artifact of optimizer choice, sampling noise, or unlucky initialization.

Our assumptions are mild but explicit. We work with diagonal commuting costs of arbitrary degree and the standard
X
mixer. We analyze permutation encodings because they capture a broad family of assignment and routing objectives and make the obstruction sharp. CE-QAOA–style kernels offer one principled path in this broader space, but other constraint-preserving designs are possible and worth exploring. We conclude that while feasibility concentration is the bottleneck for generic shallow VQA on global constraints, algorithms that
respect
those constraints in part or in full convert that bottleneck into a computational resource and unlock exponential gains. A natural next step is to build the same harmonic and Boolean–cube toolkit inside the CE–QAOA manifold to track how the block–
XY
mixer reshapes Fourier weight, and identify which degrees are actually reachable at fixed depth. This would give matching upper bounds for CE–QAOA and expose its ultimate limitations rather than only its advantages.
