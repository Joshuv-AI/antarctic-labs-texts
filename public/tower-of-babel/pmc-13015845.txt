Nested pediatric low-grade glioma cerebral organoid avatars reveal glutamatergic neuron stromal growth dependency
Authors: Enquan Xu, Christina Zhang, Fausto J Rodriguez, Sonika Dahiya, Corina Anastasaki, David H Gutmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, Xu et al. developed an assembloid system to model pilocytic astrocytoma (PA) tumorigenesis, a pediatric glioma comprised of PA cells nested in human iPSC-derived, cerebral organoids. The model recapitulates PA histopathology and growth and can be integrated with activatable neural progenitors, providing a valuable tool for not only determining the cell-intrinsic and cell-extrinsic growth dependencies of PA but also the preclinical assessment of potential therapeutic interventions.Keywords: cerebral organoid, assembloid, human induced pluripotent stem cell, cancer neuroscience, pilocytic astrocytoma, low-grade glioma

Brain tumors are the most common solid tumor in children, with low-grade pilocytic astrocytomas (PAs) comprising the majority of these cancers (Fangusaro et al. 2024). Unlike their malignant counterparts, World Health Organization grade 1 PAs are typically nonfatal tumors, exhibit unique histopathologic features (piloid morphology with fibrous fibrous processes, microcysts, and biphasic tissue architecture with some multinucleated cells) (Tihan et al. 2021). Additionally, these tumors harbor few genomic alterations (KIAA1549:BRAF fusion or biallelic NF1 loss), low proliferative rates (1%–4% Ki67+ cells), and a high dependence on their local microenvironment for support (Pfister et al. 2008; Raabe et al. 2011; Jones et al. 2013; Pan et al. 2018; Buhl et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2019). In this regard, we and others have successfully generated primary human PA cell lines, which in many circumstances fail to propagate due to premature cellular senescence (Raabe et al. 2011; Buhl et al. 2019; Selt et al. 2023) or grow for a limited time (5–7 days) as xenografts in zebrafish larvae (Yuan et al. 2021). For this reason, much of our understanding of the pathobiology of PAs derives from the use of the only authenticated preclinical platforms that model low-grade optic pathway gliomas arising in children with NF1 (Bajenaru et al. 2003; Zhu et al. 2005). These models have been useful for drug discovery and translation (Hegedus et al. 2008; Kaul et al. 2015), but their applicability to sporadic PAs or those tumors arising in other brain locations is limited.

In an effort to generate humanized models of sporadic PAs and NF1-associated PAs arising outside of the optic pathway, we previously leveraged both human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) engineering (Anastasaki et al. 2022b) and human PA tumor xenografting into immunocompromised Rag1-deficient or Cxcl10-null mice in vivo (Anastasaki et al. 2022a). While NF1-null or KIAA1549:BRAF-expressing hiPSC-derived progenitors and human primary PA lines form low-grade gliomas in mice in vivo, the resulting tumors fail to exhibit some of the characteristic histological features of the native human tumors, such as eosinophilic granular bodies and Rosenthal fibers.

Capitalizing on the successes of adult (glioblastoma) and pediatric (diffuse midline glioma) malignant brain tumor organoid modeling (Golebiewska et al. 2020; Pine et al. 2020; Mangena et al. 2025), we sought to develop tumor–cerebral organoid assembloids and authenticate them as accurate in vitro models of pediatric PAs. Following extensive experimental optimization, we generated mixed PA–human iPSC-derived cerebral organoid assembloids (PANCOs) as avatars of both NF1-associated and sporadic pediatric PAs. Similar to human PAs, the tumor cells in these PANCOs mimic some of the characteristic histopathological features of their native counterparts and exhibit cell-intrinsic MEK-driven growth dependency, and their growth is blocked by MEK inhibitors (mirdametinib and trametinib) currently in clinical trials (NCT04923126, NCT02684058, and NCT02124772). Moreover, the integration of designer receptor exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADD)-activatable neural progenitors into these PANCOs reveals preferential glutamatergic neuron generation and tumor infiltration, as well as a cell-extrinsic neuronal glutamate-driven PA growth dependency.

Results and Discussion

To develop a human preclinical platform that recapitulates human PA histopathology for the evaluation and targeting of PA cell-intrinsic and cell-extrinsic growth dependencies, we generated mixed 3D assembloids composed of PA cells nested within human iPSC-derived cerebral organoids (PANCOs). As the majority of PAs arise either sporadically harboring KIAA1549:BRAF fusion alterations or in individuals with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) cancer predisposition syndrome with germline and somatic NF1 mutations, we employed a series of sporadic (WUPA8 and WUPA11) and NF1-associated (WUPA3 and WUPA4) primary PA cell lines (Washington University pilocytic astrocytomas [WUPAs]) generated from operative specimens at St. Louis Children's Hospital (Fig. 1A; Anastasaki et al. 2025). The PA cells were lentivirally infected to express nuclear-targeted red fluorescent protein (RFP) under the control of the EF1α promoter. These PA cells were not 100% RFP-positive, as they purposely did not undergo antibiotic (e.g., puromycin) selection in order to preserve some of the innate PA cell population heterogeneity. Additionally, WUPA cells were strongly immunopositive for GFAP; a small portion (<10%) was faintly immunopositive for OLIG2, and all were negative for synaptophysin (neuronal marker) (Fig. 1B) expression, as is typical for these tumors (Rodriguez et al. 2008; Tihan et al. 2021). To model sporadic PA tumors, we leveraged a commercially available control human iPSC line (BJFF.6), while isogenic CRISPR/Cas9-engineered or patient-derived heterozygous NF1 -mutant human iPSCs (Anastasaki et al. 2022b) were used to recapitulate the genetics of NF1-PAs (Fig. 1C). hiPSCs were differentiated into human cerebral organoids (hCOs), which spontaneously formed organized ventricular zones (SOX2+ cells) and comprised neurons (TUJ-1+ cells) and astrocytes (GFAP+ cells) after 4 weeks in vitro (WIV) (Fig. 1D), as described previously (Anastasaki et al. 2022b).

To generate PANCOs in which PA cells are fully encapsulated within the hCO parenchyma, we performed numerous optimization experiments that included modifying the (1) coculture methods, (2) starting PA cell number and density, (3) age of the host hCOs, and (4) coculture conditions. First, we changed the conditions of the PA–hCO coculture for the first 48 h. In one approach, we started with PA cells grown as a single-cell suspension in low-attachment plates before introducing 4 WIV hCOs and incubating the coculture on an orbital shaker at 80 rpm for 48 h. In the second approach, we started with PA cells pelleted onto U-bottom low-cell attachment plates, prior to transferring 4 WIV hCOs directly on top of the pellet and proceeding with a static coculture for the following 48 h. In both cases, after 48 h, cocultured hCOs/PAs were transferred to new low-attachment multiwell plates and maintained for an additional 2 WIV under continuous orbital shaking (Fig. 2A). We found that, although floating cell cocultures did not produce any PANCOs, initial static cocultures generated three types of hCO–PA assembloids: (1) fused hCO–PA cell assembloids, which were connected on only a single plane; (2) hCO–PA cell assembloids with small integrated PA areas within hCOs (integrated small [IS]); or (3) PANCOs where PA cells formed tumors within the hCO parenchyma and occupied >2% of the hCO area (Fig. 2B). Second, we altered the initial amount of PA cells (1 × 104, 3 × 104, or 1 × 105 cells) used for static coculture with 4 WIV hCOs and found that only coculturing hCOs with 1 × 104 cells generated PANCOs (Fig. 2C; Supplemental Fig. S1A). Third, we modified the age of the recipient hCOs from 4 to 12 WIV and determined that coculture with 4 WIV hCOs was required for efficient PANCO generation (Fig. 2D; Supplemental Fig. S1B). Fourth, we changed the duration (48, 72, or 96 h) of the initial coculture of 1 × 104 PA cells with recipient 4 WIV hCOs and found that 72 h of initial infusion was optimal for successful PANCO generation (75% PANCOs) after 8 weeks of coculture (Fig. 2E; Supplemental Fig. S1D). Of note, this 72 h infusion duration period was selected because earlier transfers from static cultures to the orbital shaker sheared the PANCOs, while longer infusion periods resulted in PANCOs that were large and could not be maintained successfully. Fifth, to define the importance of the PA pellet diameter during the initial coculture period, we minced the 1 × 104 PA cell pellets into 100 µm spheroids and found an improved rate of PANCO generation (86%) after 8 weeks of coculture (Fig. 2F). Sixth, we varied the coculture duration of the minced spheroids from 1 to 8 WIV (Fig. 2F; Supplemental Fig. S1D) and determined that the 8 WIV coculture period was optimal for robust PANCO generation. Additionally, initial coculture with minced PA cell spheroids resulted in PANCOs nested within healthy hCOs containing intact ventricular zones (SOX2+) and differentiated neurons (TUJ1+), as opposed to PANCOs generated from a single large PA cell pellet, which had no discernable hCO features with few SOX2- or TUJ1-immunopositive cells (Fig. 2G). Taken together, we experimentally determined that generation of reproducible (>80%) PANCOs, where the PA tumor area is fully surrounded by developing hCO tissue, required that 1 × 104 WUPA cells be pelleted and physically dissociated into 100 µm diameter spheroids prior to coculture with 4 WIV hCOs for 72 h and then maintained for an additional 8 WIV (Fig. 2H). Using this optimized method, we generated PANCOs with similar success rates for seven separate batches with the same hCO/PA cell genotype combination (NF1-PANCO-3) (Supplemental Fig. S1E). We additionally generated multiple batches of sporadic PANCOs (Sp-PANCO-1 and Sp-PANCO-2) where control hCOs were cocultured with sporadic WUPA cells (WUPA8 or WUPA11), as well as NF1-PANCOs (NF1-PANCO-1 and NF1-PANCO-2) in which heterozygous NF1 mutant (NF1C383X/+ or NF1Y489C/+) hCOs were cocultured with NF1-PA cells (WUPA3 or WUPA10) to mimic the genetics of their respective clinical counterparts (Supplemental Fig. S1F,G; Pfister et al. 2008; Gutmann et al. 2013).

As part of the authentication process, we found that the resulting Sp-PANCO and NF1-PANCO tumors recapitulated many of the characteristic histopathological features seen in their parental tumors (Fig. 3A): All tumor-bearing PANCOs contained well circumscribed tumoral areas identified by RFP immunopositivity. Similar to typical PA surgical biopsy specimens, the tumors within these PANCOs exhibited a biphasic tissue architecture (densely packed cellular regions interspersed with less compact areas containing cystic components), multiple piloid processes, and some multinucleated cells. Additionally, there were rare mitotic figures and apoptotic bodies with occasional necrotic areas, mostly observed in the organoid portion of the PANCO (Rodriguez et al. 2008). PANCO tumors also demonstrated strong GFAP immunopositivity, faint OLIG2 immunopositivity, and a lack of synaptophysin (Syn) expression (Fig. 3B; Supplemental Fig. S2A). It should be noted that the surrounding hCO component of the PANCOs mimics some of the cellular diversity of the developing brain, including neurons and many differentiated astrocyte cells that are also positive for GFAP expression (Figs. 1D, 3B).

Following standardization, which ensured consistent production of reproducible PANCOs, an entirely human ex vivo platform was available to study PA growth dependencies. Similar to typical PA tumor biopsy specimens, both Sp-PANCOs and NF1-PANCOs exhibited low proliferative indexes (4% Ki67+ cells), which remained stable over time (2–8 weeks) (Fig. 3C; Supplemental Fig. S2B,C), consistent with the slow growth rates of these tumors in children (Tu et al. 2018). Unlike their malignant counterparts, PANCOs maintained their structural integrity and PA architecture, as well as low proliferative indices (Ki67+ cells), even after 24 WIV (Supplemental Fig. S2D). This is similar to PA monocultures grown as spheroids in vitro (Supplemental Fig. S2E). Additionally, these PA cells retained immunopositivity for GFAP expression (Supplemental Fig. S2F) and ERK activation (phosphorylated ERK 1/2 [pERK1/2]) (Supplemental Fig. S2G) after 8–24 WIV.

Because both KIAA1549:BRAF and NF1-null NF1-PAs have increased MEK activation due to either dysregulated BRAF activity (sporadic PA) or neurofibromin loss of RAS regulation (NF1-PA), we assessed the ability of two MEK inhibitors (mirdametinib [NCT04923126] and trametinib [NCT02684058 and NCT02124772], currently in clinical trials for children with low-grade gliomas) (Manoharan et al. 2020; Selt et al. 2020) to suppress PANCO–PA growth. Both agents reduced ERK1/2 phosphorylation (pERK 1/2) (Fig. 3D; Supplemental Fig. S3A) as well as NF1-PANCO (Mir, 48% reduction and Tra, 87% reduction in %Ki67+ cells) (Fig. 3E; Supplemental Fig. S3B) and Sp-PANCO (Mir, 50% reduction in %Ki67+ cells) (Supplemental Fig. S3C) tumor cell proliferation without affecting apoptosis (Supplemental Fig. S3C,D). One micromolar mirdametinib was selected as the minimum concentration that reduced ERK phosphorylation and cell proliferation in PA cell cultures in vitro (Supplemental Fig. S3B) without affecting non-PA-containing hCO proliferation (Supplemental Fig. S3C). This mirdametinib concentration reduced ERK phosphorylation in both PANCOs (Fig. 3D) and hCOs (Supplemental Fig. S3C).

The finding that MEK inhibitors suppress Sp-PA and NF1-PA cell-intrinsic growth is consistent with prior studies reporting the sensitivity of these tumors to RAF/MEK pathway interruption (Pfister et al. 2008; Kaul et al. 2012, 2015), which served as preclinical evidence to launch RAF/MEK-targeted clinical trials for children with low-grade glioma (NCT01089101, NCT03363217, NCT04485559, and NCT02684058). Future studies using PANCOs could focus on antisenolytic compounds (Buhl et al. 2019; Selt et al. 2023), dual inhibitors (e.g., MEK and mTOR inhibition) (Kaul et al. 2015), and pharmacological inhibitors that block other downstream effectors of the RAS/RAF signaling pathway (Ullrich et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2025).

In addition to cell-intrinsic growth dependencies, PA cells are heavily reliant on stromal cues and often exhibit cellular senescence in their absence (Raabe et al. 2011; Buhl et al. 2019). One of the critical cells in the glioma microenvironment is the neuron (Mancusi and Monje 2023), which has been shown to drive pediatric and adult high-grade glioma growth through the elaboration of neurotransmitters (Venkataramani et al. 2019; Venkatesh et al. 2019; Huang-Hobbs et al. 2023; Taylor et al. 2023).

Building on recent studies from our laboratory revealing that glutamatergic neurons regulate PA tumor growth in vitro and in vivo through glutamate receptor signaling (Anastasaki et al. 2025), we modified the PANCO generation pipeline to incorporate genetically engineered iPSC-derived DREADD-expressing neural progenitor cells (iNPCs) within the hCOs prior to PA cell coculture. These iNPCs were lentivirally infected with hM3DqR-GFP and introduced into hCOs prior to PA cell coculture. These iNPCs expanded and differentiated into neurons within the host hCOs. Following spontaneous differentiation into neurons in situ, the iNPC-derived neurons were responsive to clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) DREADD-mediated activation (Fig. 4A), resulting in PANCOs with traceable GFP+ neurons (Supplemental Fig. S4A).

To assess the proximity of the iNPC-derived GFP+ neurons to the tumor cells within these PANCOs, we leveraged confocal microscopy: We found GFP/synapsin-I+ and GFP/bassoon+ presynaptic structures surrounding PA cells (Fig. 4B; Supplemental Fig. S4B). Because the PA cells are labeled with nuclear-targeted EF1α-RFP, the distance between the GFP+ presynaptic neurons and the PA cell surface is likely underestimated. Additionally, we performed several proximity ligation assay (PLA) sets to assess the proximity of GFP+ iNPC-derived neurons (TUJ1+ cells) expressing the presynaptic protein bassoon to PA-RFP+ tumor cells (PDGFRA+ cells) expressing the postsynaptic protein homer (Fig. 4C). Puncta were observed, indicating that presynaptic TUJ1+ and bassoon+ neurons are within 40 nm of the postsynaptic PDGFRA+/homer+ PA cells and suggesting synaptic-width proximity between neuronal terminals and tumor cells.

Using PANCOs containing DREADD-activatable neurons in close proximity to the tumor cells, we found that 24 h of 10 µM CNO treatment was sufficient to induce a 10-fold increase in neuronal activation (GFP/c-Fos+) within the iNPC-GFP+-containing PANCOs (Fig. 4D). In contrast, CNO treatment of PANCOs devoid of hM3DqR-GFP+ iNPC-derived neurons had no effect on native neuron activation (c-Fos+ cells) (Supplemental Fig. S4C), highlighting the specificity of the designer drug (CNO) for selective activation of integrated engineered neurons. Moreover, while 87% of activated c-Fos+ cells were NeuN+ neurons, PA cells were c-Fosneg (Supplemental Fig. S5A), demonstrating that CNO-driven neuronal activation does not stimulate PA cells, similar to our prior findings using these PA cells in vitro (Anastasaki et al. 2025).

As iNPCs can spontaneously differentiate into either excitatory (glutamatergic) or inhibitory (GABAergic) neurons within the hCOs, we characterized the neuronal identity of hM3DqR-GFP+ iNPC-derived neurons in PANCOs. Eighty percent of the iNPC-derived GFP+ neurons were glutamatergic (v-Glut 1+), while only 1% were GABAergic (GAD67+) (Fig. 5A–C). Moreover, 58% of GFP+ neurons were activated by CNO and were c-Fos+, and among these neurons, only 1% were GABAergic (Fig. 5C). Together, these data support that CNO-activated iNPC-derived neurons are glutamatergic. Consistent with the presence of glutamatergic neurons, CNO induced a 6.9-fold increase in secreted glutamate after 24 h (Fig. 5D) without affecting GABA levels (Supplemental Fig. S5B).

Next, we sought to determine whether glutamatergic neuron activation regulates PA cell proliferation in PANCOs. In these experiments, we evaluated PA cell proliferation (Ki67+ cells) in PANCOs following vehicle or CNO treatment with or without glutamate receptor (memantine) inhibition: CNO addition increased PA proliferation in both NF1-PANCOs and Sp-PANCOs (Fig. 5E,F; Supplemental Fig. S5C,D), whereas subsequent memantine administration restored PA cell proliferation to vehicle-treated control levels (Fig. 5E,F). Additionally, to ensure that the CNO-mediated activation was specific to the hM3DqR-GFP+ iNPC-derived neurons, we treated PANCOs without integrated hM3DqR-GFP+ iNPCs and found that CNO had no effect on PA cell proliferation (Fig. 5E,F). Because neuronal glutamate induces PA cell proliferation by ERK activation (Anastasaki et al. 2025), we assessed pERK1/2 expression in PANCOs with and without integrated hM3DqR-GFP+ iNPCs following vehicle or CNO treatment (Fig. 5E). We demonstrated that CNO-mediated glutamatergic neuron stimulation enhanced PA ERK activation (pERK1/2 expression) only in PANCOs containing hM3DqR-GFP+ iNPCs.

Taken together, the close proximity of neuronal presynaptic proteins to tumoral postsynaptic proteins (revealed by confocal microscopy and puncta colocalization in PLAs) supports the existence of structural synapses. Further ultrastructural and electrophysiological studies will be required to determine whether bona fide synapses or gap junctions are responsible for this unique neuron–tumor growth dependency.

The finding that PA growth in PANCOs is regulated in a glutamate receptor (GluR)-dependent manner opens the door to the evaluation of more selective GluR antagonists currently in clinical use for children with epilepsy or mood disorders (NCT03194906), as well as other neurotransmitter cell-extrinsic growth dependencies (Venkataramani et al. 2019; Venkatesh et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2024; Barron et al. 2025; Drexler et al. 2025). In addition, PANCOs could serve as in vitro platforms to dissect the mechanisms underlying the physical interaction between neurons and glioma cells involving tumor microtubes and gap junctions (Venkataramani et al. 2022), enabling future investigations focused on the specificity of neuronal population–tumor interactions, the mechanisms mediating neuronal attraction to tumors, and the role of other brain stromal cell types in neuron regulation of PA growth (e.g., oligodendrocytes and astrocytes). In this regard, PANCOs can be engineered to incorporate additional cellular components, such as tumor-associated monocytes (TAMs), which are pivotal in regulating T-cell infiltration (Chatterjee et al. 2025) as well as regulating glioma cell growth (Guo et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2023). In this manner, PANCOs represent an accurate avatar amenable to the exploration of preclinical therapeutic agents as well as basic science investigations relevant to the management of this common pediatric brain tumor.

Materials and methods

Human PA cells

Operative specimens from children with NF1-associated and sporadic PA tumors were collected under an approved Human Studies (Institutional Review Board) protocol at Washington University School of Medicine and used to derive primary PA cells (Anastasaki et al. 2025). These PA cells were maintained in tissue culture-treated, uncoated, and vented T75 flasks in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, GlutaMAX and Pen/Strep and passaged upon reaching confluence using Accutase. The PA cells were lentivirally infected to express core histone protein H2B-red fluorescent protein (RFP) under the EF1α promoter (RRID: 201121) within the nucleus, as described below. The cells comprised a mixed population of ∼80% RFP+/20% RFPneg cells, did not undergo selection to maintain primary PA cell heterogeneity, and were passaged at high density once per week. Some PA cells were maintained as spheroids by passaging and pelleting 30,000 cells at the bottom of low-attachment U-bottom plates, allowing the tumor cells to aggregate overnight prior to maintaining the spheroids in low-attachment containers under static (nonshaking) conditions.

Human cerebral organoids

Cerebral organoid cultures were generated as described previously (Anastasaki et al. 2022b) with minor adaptations. Human NF1 patient-derived iPSCs heterozygous for an NF1 mutation (NF1Y489C/+), iPSCs CRISPR–Cas9-engineered to harbor a heterozygous NF1 patient-derived NF1 mutation (NF1C383X/+), and an isogenic control line (BJFF.6) were initially expanded on Matrigel-coated T25 flasks and detached using ReLeSR. Embryoid bodies (EBs) were generated by seeding 40,000 live hiPSCs per well into a 96 well low-attachment plate, followed by centrifugation at 300g for 3 min to promote cell aggregation. Neural induction was initiated by replacing mTeSR medium with STEMdiff neural induction medium supplemented with 4 ng/mL bFGF and 20 µM ROCK inhibitor. Each well contained 200 µL of media. On day 2, EBs were examined by bright-field microscopy to confirm their formation. The medium was refreshed on day 4 with bFGF and ROCK inhibitor and again on day 7 without supplements. On day 10, EBs were transferred to ultralow-attachment 24 well plates on an orbital shaker at 80 rpm in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 and maintained in organoid culture media:1:DMEM/F12:neurobasal medium supplemented with antibiotic/antimycotic solution, N-2 supplement, B-27 Plus supplement, GlutaMAX, MEM NEAA (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 250 µL of 100 U/mL humulin (in 1 L), and 4 µL of 2-mercaptoethanol (in 1 L; Sigma). After 4 weeks in vitro (WIV), mature cerebral organoids (COs) were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C. Fixed organoids were then washed in PBS and placed in 30% sucrose for 48 h of cryoprotection. To generate frozen sections, COs were placed in a mixture of 1:2 (v/v) Tissue-Tek OCT compound and 30% sucrose in 15 mm × 15 mm × 5 mm plastic molds. Tissues were snap-frozen using a slurry of dry ice and 2-methylbutane. Frozen samples were sectioned at 10 µm thickness using a cryostat, and two sections were mounted per glass slide for analyses.

Differentiation and maintenance of induced neural progenitor cells (iNPCs)

To induce neural progenitor differentiation, hiPSCs were plated onto poly-L-ornithine-coated (Sigma-Aldrich) and laminin-coated (Fisher Scientific) tissue culture flasks. Cells were initially cultured for 3 days in NPC base media consisting of 50% DMEM/F12 and 50% neurobasal media supplemented with N-2, B-27 Plus, and 2 mM GlutaMAX (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific). During this phase, the medium was supplemented with 10 ng/mL human LIF, 4 µM CHIR99021, 3 µM SB431542, 2 µM dorsomorphin, and 0.1 µM compound E (Stem Cell Technologies) prior to growth for an additional 5 days in the same NPC base medium containing 10 ng/mL LIF, 4 µM CHIR99021, 3 µM SB431542, and 0.1 µM compound E but omitting dorsomorphin. Thereafter, iNPCs were maintained in NPC base medium supplemented with 10 ng/mL LIF, 3 µM CHIR99021, and 2 µM SB431542. The medium was refreshed daily, and cells were passaged as required using Accutase (Stem Cell Technologies) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Lentiviral production and cell transduction

Lentiviral particles encoding H2B-RFP under the EF-1α promoter (RRID: 201121) and hM3D(Gq) DREADD receptors under the SYN1 promoter (VectorBuilder VB241016-1583wgf) were generated using a standard third-generation packaging system. Plasmid constructs and packaging vectors were cotransfected into HEK293T cells using Fugene HD (Promega) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Viral supernatants were harvested 48 h after transfection, passed through a 0.45 µm filter, and immediately applied to either patient-derived PA cells or induced neural progenitor cells (iNPCs). Target cells were exposed to viral supernatants for 72 h, after which infection efficiency was assessed by fluorescence microscopy: RFP+ for PA cells and EGFP+ for iNPCs. Transduced cells were maintained in vitro until they were used for coculture with hCOs.

PANCO generation

Human iPSC-derived cerebral organoids (hCOs) maintained for a minimum of 4 weeks in 24 well ultralow-attachment plates were assessed for neuronal (TUJ1+), neural stem cell (SOX2+), and astrocytic (GFAP+) marker expression. WUPA cells previously transduced with RFP-expressing lentivirus were seeded at a density of 10,000–100,000 cells per well in U-bottom 96 well ultralow-attachment plates 1 day prior to incubation with COs. After 24 h, PA cells formed compact spheroids. For optimal PANCO generation, aggregates from 10,000 cells were gently dissociated using a 200 µL pipette tip to produce smaller spheroids (<100 µm in diameter). For coculture experiments, hCOs were resuspended using organoid culture medium, transferred into 96 well plates containing PA spheroids, and incubated for 72 h to facilitate integration. Following this initial period, cocultured assembloids were returned to the 24 well ultralow-attachment plates and maintained under standard culture conditions for an additional 8 weeks. PA integration was monitored weekly by live-cell imaging of RFP+ PA cells within the PANCOs. If PA-RFP+ cells were not observed after 1 week, the coculture procedure was repeated to enhance the likelihood of successful PA cell integration.

Integration of hM3D(Gq)-GFP iNPCs into human cerebral organoids

Following infection of iPSC-derived NPCs (iNPCs) with hM3D(Gq)-GFP lentiviral particles, the expression efficiency of hM3D(Gq) DREADD receptor (under the SYN1 promoter) expression in iNPCs was assessed by fluorescence. Once GFP-positive cells exceeded 80%, iNPCs were dissociated using Accutase, and live cells were counted. iNPCs were then resuspended in cerebral organoid culture medium and plated at a density of 50,000 cells per well into U-bottom 96 well ultralow-attachment plates 1 h prior to coculture. Once the iNPCs had settled and formed a pellet, hCOs cultured in 24 well plates were gently transferred onto the iNPC pellet. After a 48 h incubation period, iNPC–hCOs were returned to the 24 well plates. Successful integration was monitored by live-cell imaging starting at 1 week after coculture. If integration was not achieved, the coculture procedure was repeated once to improve the rate of successful integration.

Sequential integration and DREADD activation in PANCOs

Human iNPC-hM3D–PANCOs were generated via sequential integration of PAs in iNPC–hCOs. iNPC–hCOs (2–4 WIV) were cocultured with WUPA cells as described above for PA cells. Functional activation of hM3DGq iNPC-derived cells was initiated 8 weeks after PANCO integration. For DREADD activation, 10 µM clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) was added to the culture medium for 1 h, followed by a medium change and a second 1 h CNO treatment 24 h later. After a second medium change, PANCOs were maintained for an additional 48 h before collection and immunohistochemical analysis. For memantine treatments, PANCOs were pretreated with 10 µM CNO for 1 h, followed by 24 h of recovery. A second 1 h CNO treatment was then applied, followed by 2 µM memantine addition to the media. PANCOs were collected 24 h after memantine treatment following a total treatment duration of 48 h.

Mirdametinib and trametinib treatments

PANCOs were treated with 0.5–1.5 µM mirdametinib (MedChem Express) and 50 nM trametinib (MedChem Express), with medium replenished daily for a total of 3 days. Treated PANCOs were then collected for histopathological analyses. hCOs were treated with 1 µM mirdametinib for 72 h prior to collection for histopathological analyses. Unlabeled WUPA cell monolayer cultures were treated with 0.1–1.0 µM mirdametinib for 24 h for proliferation assays and Western blot analyses.

Immunocytochemistry, immunofluorescence, and immunohistochemistry

Adherent WUPA cells were washed and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Fixed cells and frozen sections were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. After blocking with 10% goat serum, samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (Supplemental Table S1) diluted in 2% goat serum according to the manufacturer-recommended concentrations. Alexa fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200) were then applied, and nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33258 (Thermo Scientific). Samples were mounted with Immu-Mount (Fisher) and imaged using a Leica DMi1 fluorescence microscope with LAS X. For synapsin-I and bassoon immunostaining, high-resolution confocal imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 880 with Airyscan to assess synaptic proximity between integrated neuronal and PA cells.

For DAB immunohistochemical staining, frozen tissues were serially sectioned (5 µm) and immunostained with Ki67, GFAP, OLIG2, phospho-ERK1/2, and synaptophysin antibodies (Supplemental Table S1) using the VectaStain ABC kit and the appropriate biotinylated secondary antibodies.

Proximity ligation assays

Proximity ligation assays (PLAs) were performed to detect protein–protein interactions between PDGFRA:TUJ1, homer::bassoon, homer::PDGFRA, and PDGFRA::bassoon. Each assay was conducted using the Duolink in situ detection kit (Millipore Sigma DUO92013) according to the manufacturer's specifications. Briefly, all reagents were freshly prepared. PANCO tissue cryosections were washed and blocked using Duolink blocking solution, The appropriate primary antibodies (Supplemental Table S1) were diluted 1:100 in Duolink antibody diluent. After the staining was complete, the slides were mounted using Duolink mounting medium containing DAPI before undergoing confocal imaging.

Western blotting

Treated WUPA cells were collected, snap-frozen, and lysed in protease/phosphatase inhibitor-supplemented RIPA buffer for subsequent Western blotting as described previously (Anastasaki et al. 2025). Briefly, 20 µg of each sample was blotted using pERK1/2, total ERK1/2, and GAPDH primary and appropriate HRP- and NIR-conjugated secondary antibodies (Supplemental Table S1). Images were captured and analyzed using Li-COR Image Studio Lite (version 5.2).

Resazurin proliferation assays

Thirty-thousand WUPA cells were seeded in each well of a 96 well plate 24 h prior to treatment with vehicle (DMSO) or 0.1–1 µM mirdametinib for a total of 24 h. During the last 6 h of treatment, 10% (v/v) resazurin (Thermo Fisher) was added to each well according to the manufacturer's instructions. Metabolized resazurin produced resorufin as PA cells proliferated. Resorufin fluorescence was analyzed on a Varioskan LUX multimode microplate reader (Thermo Fisher) using SkanIt (Thermo Fisher).

Quantification and statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 5. Depending on the comparison, either two-tailed Student's t-tests or one-way ANOVA with Dunnett's or Tukey's post hoc corrections were used. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with exact P-values reported in the corresponding figure panels. Each analysis included data from a minimum of three independently generated biological replicates. Each biological replicate represented the average of a minimum of two technical replicates.
