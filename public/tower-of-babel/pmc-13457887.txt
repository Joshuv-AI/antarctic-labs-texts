From rigidity to collapse in soft coral slumping within the World Heritage coral gardens in Korean waters
Authors: Anna B Jöst, Taihun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoral communities (i.e., soft and hard corals) in Korean waters persist in temperate coastal environments characterized by strong seasonal variability and episodic hydrographic extremes. Within a protected coastal seascape of high ecological and socio-economic value, designated as a UNESCO natural heritage site for its vibrant soft coral gardens, we report the first observation of widespread collapse in soft corals. Based on in situ photographs, we document a previously undescribed loss-of-structural-integrity phenomenon in soft corals. We term this a “slumping event.” We define slumping in organisms lacking rigid skeletal support as collapse of the hydrostatic skeleton. The event affected multiple species of non-symbiotic octocorallian soft corals (family Nephtheidae), for which we describe four morphological stages: drooping, dangle, deflation, and disintegration, with a potential additional “inflation” stage. These represent descriptive categories based on cross-sectional observations rather than a confirmed temporal sequence. Environmental analyses indicate that the event coincided with anomalous hydrographic conditions characterized by sustained low salinity and elevated temperature. To quantify prolonged freshwater exposure, we introduce the Degree Freshening Week (DFW) metric, capturing the cumulative duration and intensity of hypo-saline stress, highlighting the importance of stress persistence rather than short-term extremes. This study provides the first field-based documentation and definition of soft coral slumping in situ. Introduction

“Soft corals” is an informal but widely used term referring to a diverse assemblage of octocorals (Cnidaria: Anthozoa: Octocorallia) characterized by the absence of a rigid, continuous aragonite skeleton. Instead, they possess fleshy, flexible bodies supported by microscopic internal calcite spicules embedded within their tissues1. Structural integrity in soft coral polyps is maintained largely through a hydrostatic skeleton, wherein muscle contractions exert pressure on an enclosed volume of coelenteric fluid2. In aquarium settings, partial deflation and drooping of soft coral polyps are commonly reported on reefing forums (e.g., Singapore Reef Club, Reef2Reef, ReefChasers, WetWebMedia.com), typically attributed to acclimation stress or suboptimal water conditions. However, complete deflation, marked by apparent changes in coelenteric fluid inferred from morphology (“slumping”) has not been previously documented in wild populations, particularly with photographic evidence. For non-symbiotic soft corals, in which classic signs of stress (e.g., bleaching) are absent, assessing mortality in situ remains challenging3. Prior studies have inferred mortality from long-term declines in colony cover, without resolving the intermediate morphological processes leading to disappearance. Here, we document a sequence of morphological categories observed across wild soft coral colonies in situ, which may represent progressive changes preceding death. However, individual colonies were not tracked longitudinally, nor were the exact same colonies re-visited, so this sequence is inferred from comparative observations across multiple colonies representing different apparent stages of decline. These categories should therefore be interpreted as descriptive stages rather than confirmed temporal progression.

Korean coastal waters lie near the poleward extent of shallow-water coral distribution in the East China Sea4. This northern limit is primarily constrained by declining sea surface temperature (SST) and solar irradiance with increasing latitude. While irradiance critically limits photosymbiotic hard corals (typically found shallower than 45 m), most shallow-water soft corals in Korea, including the dominant species, are non-symbiotic. Consequently, SST acts as the primary environmental driver of soft coral distribution. In recent decades, Jeju’s southern waters have experienced episodic periods of elevated summer temperatures, where soft coral communities are particularly well developed. Additionally, during summer, these waters are strongly influenced by freshwater inflow from the Changjiang Diluted Water (CDW), which intrudes into the hyper-saline Jeju Current via wind-driven Ekman transport5–7. The resulting stratified water column, with a low-salinity surface layer overlying cooler, saltier deep water, may influence stratification and surface temperature structure8 and has been associated with altered microbial assemblages9. Long-term hydrographic observations (KHOA, 2000–2023) indicate that Jeju sea surface salinity (SSS) follows a pronounced seasonal cycle (January–April winter average 33.5 ± 1.03 PSU; June–September summer average 31.1 ± 1.74 PSU). Jeju’s southern coastal waters, particularly around Seogwipo, are renowned for their vibrant soft coral gardens, protected under the Seogwipo Marine Provincial Park, a designated Marine Protected Area (MPA) and part of the Jeju Island UNESCO Biosphere Reserve (www.khs.go.kr). The MPA covers a sea surface area of 19.54 km2 (http://www.data.go.kr) around the islets of Beomseom, Munseom, and Seopseom10. South Korea hosts 78 recorded species of octocorallian Anthozoa, with the family Acanthogorgiidae being the most diverse (19 species; www.kbr.go.kr). In Jeju, the dominant octocoral species are Dendronephthya gigantea (Verrill, 1864), typically found at depths of 10–20 m11, and Scleronephthya gracillima (Kückenthal, 1906), dominating deeper depths (15–40 m)3. Both belong to the family Nephtheidae, the second most diverse octocorallian family in Korea (16 species; http://www.kbr.go.kr). In Jeju waters, both species have also been observed at shallow reef habitats at depths of approximately 5 m. Although the area has been extensively monitored and is frequently visited by divers4,10,12–16, large-scale slumping of soft corals in the wild has not previously been reported. We hypothesize that soft coral slumping is triggered by the combined effects of prolonged thermal stress and sustained low-salinity exposure, potentially exacerbated by microbial shifts associated with Changjiang Diluted Water (CDW) intrusion. Specifically, we (1) document the morphological progression of slumping in octocoral nephtheidean species, (2) quantify relevant environmental stressors using long-term sea surface temperature (SST) and salinity (SSS) records, and (3) explore, theoretically, the potential role of microbial changes as an additional stress factor. We further propose Degree Freshening Weeks (DFW) as a novel metric to characterize freshwater stress and quantify prolonged low-salinity exposure. These findings aim to fill a critical gap in soft coral ecology and contribute to a better understanding of octocoral responses to compound environmental stressors in marginal coral ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Survey area

Jeju Island lies approximately 100 km off the southwest coast of the Korean mainland (Fig. 1). It is the only oceanic location globally classified as “climatically extreme,” characterized by dry, polar winters and moist, tropical summers17. The lowest sea surface temperatures (SST) typically occur in February and March, whereas August consistently represents the annual thermal maximum. Over the 2000–2023 period, mean SST in February and March were 16.7 ± 2.6 °C and 16.5 ± 2.5 °C, respectively, while August averaged 22.4 ± 4.2 °C (KHOA data). In contrast, the most recent decade (2015–2024) shows a marked intensification of summer warming, with August mean SST rising to 26.1 ± 1.8 °C. This shift indicates a strengthening seasonal thermal amplitude, particularly driven by higher summer extremes rather than changes in winter minima. Such an increase in peak summer temperatures suggests enhanced heat stress potential for marine ecosystems during the warm season, even if winter conditions remain relatively stable.

The phenomenon investigated in this study was observed along the southern coast of Jeju Island, within the broader areas of Songaksan and Seogwipo Marine Provincial Park between August and September 2024. These areas are partially marine protected (Fig. 1C, E, light grey area) and are well-established dive sites used for recreational and scientific purposes, including long-term monitoring of soft coral communities, which have attracted increasing scientific and public interest since the 1970s4,10,12–16.

Field surveys

Occurrences were documented using underwater photography (Olympus TG-6, Sony DSC-RX100M2, and Sony ILCE-7M4) equipped with flash and strobes. Surveys were conducted by trained citizen scientist SCUBA divers at depths of 5–20 m along the coastline during August (15th, 16th, and 19th) and September (3rd, 4th, 14th, and 16th) of 2024, under daylight conditions between 10:00 and 15:00.

At Beomseom (~ 33°13’02” N, 126°30’52” E), Munseom (~ 33°13’40” N, 126°34’02” E), and Songaksan (~ 33°11’41” N, 126°34’01” E), surveys were conducted by SCUBA along the coastline in a west-to-east direction over approximately 200–300 m. Survey routes were determined by the coastline configuration and predefined depth strata. At Beomseom and Munseom, two dives were conducted per site, targeting depth ranges of 5–10 m and 10–20 m. At Songaksan, two dives were conducted spanning the full 5–20 m depth range. No formal transects or quadrats were employed. Instead, colonies encountered along each survey route were documented opportunistically using in situ photography, with the aim of capturing the observed range of morphological variation across sites and depth ranges.

All sites have been monitored using the above-described survey approach at approximately three-month intervals since 2023. The slumping phenomenon was exclusively observed during the August and September 2024 surveys. Although isolated instances affecting individual colonies were noted after the event in summer 2025, their spatial extent and frequency were negligible compared to the widespread occurrence documented in 2024.

Environmental data

Sea surface temperature (SST) and sea surface salinity (SSS) data for the Seogwipo station, Jeju Island, from 2015 to 2023, were obtained from the Korea Hydrographic and Oceanographic Agency (KHOA; Ministry of Oceans and Fisheries; http://www.khoa.go.kr/oceangrid). Data for 2024 were retrieved from the KHOA tide level observatory (http://www.khoa.go.kr/oceangrid/koofs/eng/observation/obs_real.do).

Hourly sea surface temperature (SST, °C) and sea surface salinity (SSS, PSU) observations spanning June–September 2015–2024 were subjected to a multi-step quality control (QC) procedure to remove erroneous measurements while retaining physically meaningful variability17. All data were first ordered chronologically and records containing missing SST or SSS values were excluded from further analysis. Initial plausibility checks were applied using conservative environmental bounds representative of coastal surface waters. Salinity values outside the range 23–40 PSU and temperature values outside 10–40 °C were flagged as implausible and removed. These thresholds were selected to encompass expected natural variability while excluding values likely arising from sensor malfunction or recording errors. Values below 23 PSU were classified as non-physical, as such low salinities are not expected in open coastal waters at 5–15 m depth in the study region, even under strong freshwater influence. For context, observed minima associated with the Changjiang River plume in 1997 and 1998 around Jeju were 27.5 and 25.5 PSU, respectively18, whereas salinities below 25 PSU are typical within the Changjiang River estuary19, indicating that a 23 PSU threshold provides a conservative margin. Values outside the 10–40 °C range were classified as non-physical, as the most extreme single SST readings for Seogwipo of the past two decades were 12.2 °C (in February 2013, KHOA data) and 29.5 °C (in June 2008, KHOA data), respectively. Therefore, our 10 °C minimum threshold provides a conservative margin, especially for a summer data set.

Temporal consistency was evaluated through rate-of-change tests. Absolute differences between consecutive hourly measurements were calculated, and observations exhibiting changes greater than 1.5 PSU h⁻¹ for salinity or 2 °C h⁻¹ for temperature were flagged as spikes. These thresholds reflect typical upper limits of physically realistic short-term variability in coastal environments, ensuring that abrupt, non-physical excursions were identified without removing genuine high-frequency signals. To account for systematic diurnal variability, a time-of-day baseline was established by calculating mean salinity for each hour of the day within each calendar month, thereby preserving seasonal structure. Individual observations were compared against these month-specific hourly means, and measurements deviating by more than − 1.5 PSU during daytime hours (11:00–18:00) were flagged as potential diurnal anomalies. This approach targets biases associated with solar heating, which can influence sensor performance during periods of high insolation. In addition to point-based checks, structured temporal patterns were assessed to identify gradual, multi-hour drift behavior indicative of sensor artifacts. Consecutive sequences of decreasing salinity were grouped and evaluated, with runs of at least four hours and cumulative decreases exceeding 2 PSU flagged as drift events, particularly when occurring during daytime periods (supplementary Fig. S2). To further support the identification of sensor-related bias, recovery phases were also detected as sequences of increasing salinity lasting at least three hours with total increases greater than 1.5 PSU, representing the expected rebound following transient sensor heating effects. A final QC flag was assigned to each observation by combining all criteria, including plausibility limits, spike detection, diurnal deviation, structured drift, and recovery patterns. Observations failing any of these tests were excluded from subsequent analyses, while all remaining data were retained as robust representations of in situ environmental variability. This multi-tiered QC approach integrates both instantaneous and temporally structured diagnostics, enabling the identification of not only isolated outliers but also systematic sensor biases, thereby ensuring a high-quality dataset for further analysis. Daily and hourly SST and SSS observations were visualized to illustrate temporal variability, while statistical analyses were conducted on either monthly aggregated means or full-resolution hourly data depending on the analysis objective. Following quality control, the filtered dataset was used to derive monthly summary statistics for each year within the study period (June–September, 2015–2024). For each variable (SST and SSS), arithmetic means were calculated separately for each month and year, ensuring that only validated observations contributed to the aggregated values. To quantify variability and uncertainty associated with the monthly means, measures of dispersion were also calculated. Specifically, standard deviations were computed for each month–year combination, and these were used to represent variability in graphical outputs. Where appropriate, standard errors (standard deviation divided by the square root of the number of observations) were derived to provide an estimate of the uncertainty of the mean. Temporal trends and variability were visualized using dot plots of daily mean SST and SSS from June until September for the past decade (Fig. 3), as well as line plots of monthly mean SST and SSS for each of the respective years (Fig. 4). In the monthly mean plot, error margins were included in the form of ± 1 standard deviation to illustrate intra-month variability and confidence in the estimated means (Fig. 4). This approach allows for clear identification of both seasonal progression within individual years and interannual differences across the study period. All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using the R programming environment (packages dplyr for data handling, lubridate for handling dates and times, readxl for reading Excel files, ggplot2 for plotting, RColorBrewer and viridis for colorblind-safe palettes, and readr for exporting CSV files).

Statistical analyses

The following R packages were used for data processing, statistical analysis, and visualization: tidyverse (data handling), lubridate (date handling), car (model diagnostics), emmeans (post-hoc comparisons), ggplot2 (visualization), and cowplot and patchwork (figure assembly, multi-panel layout). Non-parametric tests and auxiliary functions were applied using base R.

Sea surface temperature (SST) and sea surface salinity (SSS) time series from June to September (2015–2024) were quality-controlled prior to analysis by removing observations flagged as implausible values, sensor spikes, or excluded records based on pre-defined QC criteria.

Climatological aggregation and seasonal structure

To reduce temporal autocorrelation and ensure appropriate statistical independence, the primary unit of analysis was monthly means. SST and SSS were aggregated into monthly averages for each year (June–September). This monthly climatology was used for all interannual and seasonal statistical comparisons (Fig. 5). Interannual differences in mean summer SST and SSS were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with year as a fixed factor. No significant effects were detected; therefore, no pairwise post-hoc comparisons using Tukey-adjusted estimated marginal means were conducted. Seasonal structure and potential year-specific seasonal modulation were assessed using two-way ANOVA with year, month, and their interaction term. Long-term trends in summer SST and SSS over 2015–2024 were assessed using ordinary least squares linear regression applied to monthly means, with year treated as a continuous predictor.

Extreme event definition and frequency

Extreme warm SST conditions and low-salinity events were defined relative to a climatological baseline (2015–2023). Thresholds were computed as the 90th percentile of SST and the 10th percentile of SSS from the baseline period. These thresholds were used to construct binary event series identifying extreme conditions at each observation time step. The frequency of extreme SST and SSS conditions was quantified as the proportion of observations within each year-month classified as exceeding the respective thresholds. These monthly proportions were used as the statistical replication unit to avoid pseudoreplication. Interannual summer differences in event frequency were assessed using one-way ANOVA. No statistical significance was found. Thermal anomalies were defined relative to a temperature threshold of Minimum Monthly Mean (MMM) + 1 °C, and the number and proportion of anomalous days were calculated for each year. Summary statistics (means, standard deviations, and anomaly frequencies) were derived to quantify variability within and between summers.

Persistence of extreme conditions

Persistence of extreme events was quantified using a run-based segmentation approach. Continuous sequences of extreme conditions were identified using a run-length grouping method based on transitions in binary exceedance states. For each event, duration was defined as the number of consecutive time steps within a single extreme episode. From these event sequences, annual metrics including maximum event duration, mean event duration, and number of discrete events were calculated (Fig. 5E). Differences in event duration among years were assessed using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test. When the overall test was significant, pairwise differences were intended to be evaluated using Dunn’s post-hoc tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction; however, no significant overall effect was detected, and therefore post-hoc comparisons were not conducted.

Mean-state intensity and anomaly ranking

Summer months (June–September) mean SST and SSS were calculated as indices of mean-state intensity. Years were ranked according to these values to contextualize the relative position of 2024 within the 2015–2024 climatological distribution. To evaluate whether 2024 differed from the historical baseline, SST and SSS distributions in 2024 were compared against all other years combined (2015–2023) using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. These comparisons are visualized using violin and boxplots showing the full distribution of hourly observations (Fig. 7).

Statistical inference

All statistical tests were two-sided with a significance threshold of α = 0.05. Parametric methods (linear models and analysis of variance) were used for trend and interannual comparisons at the aggregated monthly scale. Non-parametric methods were applied for distributional and event-based analyses, including Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for comparing 2024 against the 2015–2023 baseline and Kruskal–Wallis tests for assessing differences in event duration among years. Where multiple comparisons were planned, p-values were adjusted using Tukey or Benjamini–Hochberg corrections as appropriate. No statistically significant effects were detected for trend or ANOVA-based comparisons, while significant differences were only observed in the Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Newly coined terms

Definition of slumping event

We define a slumping event in organisms without exo- or endoskeleton as loss of structural integrity by collapse of their hydrostatic skeleton after apparent changes in coelenteric fluid inferred from morphology, leading from drooping to deflation, death, and disintegration. However, this staging classification is based on visual morphology, and future work incorporating quantitative physiological measurements would be required to confirm the mechanistic basis of these changes and refine the staging framework.

Characteristics of soft coral slumping and classification of stages

Based on photographic documentation, we identify four key features of coral slumping attributed to morphological changes corresponding to the pathological appearance during specific stages, the drooping stage (Fig. 2A), the dangle stage (Fig. 2B), the deflation stage (Fig. 2C), and the disintegration stage (Fig. 2D). A fifth stage, the inflation stage (Supplementary Fig. S1), needs to be further explored.

Definition and calculation of Degree Freshening Week (DFW)

Following the established metric of Degree Heating Week (DHW), which quantifies cumulative thermal stress in reef-building corals, we propose Degree Freshening Week (DFW) as a time-integrated measure of cumulative hypo-saline stress in soft coral environments. DFW is designed to quantify both the magnitude and duration of reduced sea surface salinity (SSS) over time, providing a comparable framework for assessing osmotic stress. Degree Freshening Weeks (DFW) quantify cumulative low-salinity exposure relative to a climatological baseline. The baseline is defined as the minimum monthly mean (MMM) sea-surface salinity (SSS), representing the lowest long-term average monthly salinity at the study location. Values below this threshold are accumulated over time to represent the intensity and persistence of freshening conditions.

For the calculation of DFW, sea-surface salinity (SSS) data were quality-controlled by removing implausible values, spikes, and flagged observations. A climatological baseline salinity was defined as the Minimum Monthly Mean (MMM), representing the lowest monthly mean SSS derived from long-term averages across all study years. Degree Freshening Weeks (DFW) were calculated in R using the packages dplyr, lubridate, and zoo. Daily mean sea-surface salinity (SSS) was first derived from quality-controlled observations. A salinity climatology was constructed by averaging monthly mean SSS across all years, and the minimum monthly mean (MMM) was defined as the lowest value of this climatology. Freshening intensity was quantified as the positive deviation of MMM − SSS (i.e., values < 0 set to zero). These daily anomalies were converted to weekly units (PSU·weeks) and accumulated over an 84-day (12-week) right-aligned rolling window to yield DFW. This approach captures both the magnitude and persistence of low-salinity anomalies relative to the climatological baseline. Freshening events were defined as consecutive days with SSS below MMM, and for each event, cumulative DFW and maximum rolling DFW were calculated.

Results

Description of slumping event

In August and September of 2024, the soft coral beds at the three islets within Seogwipo Marine Provincial Park (Munseom, Beomseom), as well as around Songaksan further to the west (Fig. 1), were found severely slumping after apparent changes in coelenteric fluid inferred from morphology (Fig. 2). The phenomenon, for which we propose the term “slumping event”, appeared to particularly affect individuals and colonies occurring in the shallows between 5 and 10 m water depth, and belonging to species of the non-symbiotic malacalcyonacean soft coral family Nephtheidae (Cnidaria: Octocorallia: Malacalcyonacea), particularly within the genus Dendronephthya Kükenthal, 1905.

There were cases where the stalks were drooping due to loss of rigidity, while the polyps were still extended and operating normally (i.e., the drooping stage) (Fig. 2A). In other cases, only the peduncles appeared affected, leading to healthy-looking stalks and crowns dangling from sagging base-parts (i.e., the dangle stage) (Fig. 2B). In the most striking cases, the stalks were completely deflated and limp, with polyps barely visible (i.e., the deflation stage) (Fig. 2C). Traces of collapsed basal stalks were observed left behind on the rocky substrate after loss of crown parts (i.e., the disintegration stage) (Fig. 2D). Slumping soft corals were observed with simultaneously normal and deflated stalks in close proximity (Fig. 2E). As a consequence of deflated and elongated peduncles, some of the dangling soft corals ripped off, leading to stalks with crowns observed rolling around on the seafloor (Fig. 2F).

Overall, we observed and define four morphological categories associated with a slumping event. These categories are based on cross-sectional observations of different colonies at different times. They are presented in the order in which they are described in the text for clarity. This ordering does not imply a temporal sequence, and no progression between stages has been directly demonstrated. Environmental data

Daily sea-surface salinity (SSS) and temperature (SST) exhibit clear and coherent intra-seasonal trends across all summers (2015–2024), with substantial short-term variability superimposed (Fig. 3). SSS shows a progressive decline from early June (~ 31–33 PSU) into mid–late summer, reaching lowest values in August (Fig. 3, top). Minimum salinities frequently fall below 29 PSU and, in several summers (notably 2016 and 2024), decrease to ~ 25–27 PSU. The timing of minimum SSS is concentrated between late July and mid-August. Following this minimum, salinity generally increases through September, although the magnitude and rate of recovery differ between summers. Short-term fluctuations of ~ 1–2 PSU are common throughout the record, particularly during the freshening phase.

SST increases steadily from June (~ 18–21 °C) through July and reaches peak values in August (~ 25–28 °C) (Fig. 3, bottom). Maximum temperatures are typically observed between early August and early September, depending on the year. After the peak, SST stabilizes or gradually declines into late September.

Compared to SSS, SST displays a smoother temporal evolution, although short-term variability on the order of ~ 1 °C is evident. Warmer summers (e.g., 2023–2024) consistently exhibit higher temperatures throughout the season, with peak values approaching or exceeding 27.5 °C, whereas cooler summers (e.g., 2015 and 2018) remain below these levels. Across all summers, the period of lowest salinity coincides with the interval of highest temperature in August (Fig. 3).

Monthly mean SSS and SST display consistent intra-seasonal patterns across all summers (2015–2024), with variability in magnitude between years and the occurrence of anomalies (Fig. 4). Anomalies are defined as monthly values deviating by more than one standard deviation from the multi-year summer mean for a given month. Anomalies are indicated by triangular symbols, whereas circular symbols denote values within ± 1 standard deviation. SSS is highest in June (~ 31–33 PSU), decreases in July, and reaches a minimum in August (~ 29–30 PSU), followed by partial recovery in September. The magnitude of the August minimum varies between summers. The lowest values occur in 2016 and 2024 (approximately 26.5–27 PSU), where August values are classified as negative anomalies (> 1 SD below the mean). In contrast, higher salinity conditions persist throughout summers such as 2017 and 2021, although these generally remain within one standard deviation of the mean. September values range from ~ 29.5 to > 31 PSU, with occasional anomalously high values (e.g., 2017).

SST increases from June (~ 19–21 °C) to a maximum in August (~ 26–28 °C) in all summers, with slight stabilization or decline in September (Fig. 4). The highest temperatures are observed in 2023 and 2024 (~ 27.5–28 °C), where August–September values exceed one standard deviation above the mean and are therefore classified as positive anomalies. In contrast, cooler conditions in some summers (e.g., 2018) include September values that fall below one standard deviation of the mean.

Anomalies exceeding one standard deviation occur most frequently in August for both SSS and SST, with SSS anomalies predominantly negative and SST anomalies predominantly positive (Fig. 4).

Monthly mean SST did not differ significantly among years (one-way ANOVA, F9,30 = 0.47, p = 0.885), and Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons confirmed no significant differences between individual years (all p > 0.9). Despite this, SST exhibited a consistent upward tendency over the study period, increasing from 22.1 °C in 2015 to 25.0 °C in 2024, with 2024 representing the highest mean value in the record (Fig. 5A). Similarly, mean summer SSS did not differ significantly among years (one-way ANOVA, F9,30 = 1.19, p = 0.336), and no significant pairwise differences were detected (all p > 0.16). However, SSS showed a pronounced decline in 2024, reaching 28.7 PSU compared with ~ 29.8–31.3 PSU in all preceding years, representing the lowest summer salinity in the 10-year record (Fig. 5B). Therefore, although monthly minima were not consistently lowest across all summer months, the integrated seasonal mean (June–September) indicated that 2024 experienced the lowest overall salinity in the record, reflecting an extended period of anomalously low salinity during July–August rather than a uniform month-by-month shift.

Within-summer structure of variability

SST variability was significantly structured by both month (June–September) and year (two-way ANOVA without interaction: year, F9,27 = 4.98, p < 0.001; month, F3,27 = 97.68, p < 0.001). The effect of month was substantially larger than that of year, indicating that SST is primarily governed by a consistent within-summer progression, with additional but smaller differences among summers. SSS variability was likewise significantly influenced by both year and month (year, F9,27 = 3.97, p = 0.0026; month, F3,27 = 24.29, p < 0.001). As for SST, the month effect dominated, although the relative contribution of year was more pronounced for SSS. Including month as a factor revealed differences among summers that were not detected in one-way analyses, indicating that the strong within-summer structure can obscure variability between summers when not explicitly accounted for.

Extreme event frequency

The frequency of extreme warm SST conditions (defined relative to the 2015–2023 baseline 90th percentile) showed no statistically significant differences among years (F9,30 = 1.11, p = 0.388). However, estimated marginal means indicated a directional increase toward recent years, with the highest proportions of time spent above the threshold in 2023 (0.315; i.e., 31.5% of summer observations classified as “hot”) and 2024 (0.387; i.e., 38.7%) (Fig. 5C). Notably, this pattern differs from event counts, which peaked in 2022, indicating that recent years were characterized by fewer but more prolonged extreme events rather than a higher number of discrete events (see also Table 1).

Persistence of extreme warm events

The duration of warm SST events, quantified using a run-length approach, showed a marginally non-significant interannual effect (Kruskal–Wallis, χ² = 14.32, df = 8, p = 0.074). Despite high variability, summary statistics indicated longer and more persistent events in recent years, with the highest maximum duration recorded in 2024 (Fig. 5E). This suggests a tendency toward increasing persistence of warm conditions, although variability among years remains high.

Degree freshening week

Daily SSS exhibited a consistent within-summer decline followed by partial recovery across all study years (2015–2024) (Fig. 6A). Mean summer SSS ranged from 28.89 PSU (2024) to 31.22 PSU (2017), with standard deviations between 0.89 and 1.88 PSU (Table 2). The lowest mean salinity occurred in 2024, coinciding with the highest variability and the most frequent freshening conditions. Monthly distributions revealed a pronounced intra-summer structure (Fig. 6B). Salinity decreased from early summer values (~ 31–32 PSU) to minima in August (~ 29–30 PSU), followed by partial recovery in September. Variability increased during mid–late summer, reflected by broader distributions and frequent low-salinity outliers. Between-year comparisons showed relatively stable central tendencies but strong differences in the occurrence of anomalies (Fig. 6C; Table 2). The proportion of freshening days ranged from 1.64% (2018) to 57.38% (2024), with elevated frequencies also observed in 2019 (39.81%) and 2016 (29.17%). In contrast, heat anomalies (SST > MMM + 1 °C) varied from 0% (2015, 2019) to 38.52% (2024), indicating that both thermal and freshening extremes were most pronounced in 2024. Freshening intensity, expressed as maximum Degree Freshening Weeks (DFW), varied substantially among years (Fig. 6D; Table 3). The highest cumulative freshening was recorded in 2024 (max DFW ≈ 15 PSU·weeks), followed by 2016 (~ 9.6 PSU·weeks) and 2019 (~ 5.6 PSU·weeks). Most other years exhibited moderate maxima (< 5 PSU·weeks).

Long-term trends

Linear regression indicated a weak positive trend in SST over time (0.23 °C yr⁻¹), but this was not statistically significant (p = 0.12; R² = 0.06). Similarly, SSS showed a weak negative trend (− 0.10 PSU yr⁻¹), which was also not significant (p = 0.165; R² = 0.05). These results indicate that interannual summer variability is dominated by short-term fluctuations rather than monotonic long-term change.

The summer of 2024 as an anomalous hydrographic regime

Despite the absence of significant linear trends in SST and SSS over 2015–2024, 2024 emerged as a statistically distinct year in terms of its hydrographic distributions. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests comparing 2024 against all other years combined indicated highly significant differences for both variables (SST: W = 7,259,241, p < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶; SSS: W = 2,854,303, p < 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶) (Fig. 7). These results demonstrate a strong systematic shift in the overall distribution of temperature and salinity conditions rather than a monotonic long-term trend. This shift is evident in the full distribution of observations, with 2024 showing a broader and elevated range of SST values compared to the combined baseline years (Fig. 7).

This is consistent with multiple lines of evidence across analyses: 2024 exhibited the highest mean SST, the lowest mean SSS, the highest frequency of extreme warm and low-salinity conditions, and the greatest persistence of warm events. Overall, across multiple independent indicators, 2024 showed the most extreme values within the study period, including elevated SST, reduced SSS, increased frequency of extreme events, and enhanced persistence of warm conditions (Figs. 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7). The summer of 2024 also exhibited the most extreme freshening structure in terms of persistence, with the longest recorded event (50 days) and the highest cumulative Degree Freshening Weeks (DFW), indicating that prolonged exposure rather than short-lived salinity minima dominated the hydrographic regime.

Discussion

The causes of the slumping event affecting Jeju’s soft corals remain uncertain. This uncertainty is compounded by the observational nature of the dataset, which relies on non-standardized photographic documentation without concurrent environmental logging, tissue sampling, or direct physiological measurements. As such, causal inferences must be interpreted cautiously, and remain necessarily correlative. However, multiple environmental drivers likely acted in combination. The most plausible contributors include (1) elevated summer sea surface temperatures within the upper range of the local climatology, (2) prolonged reductions in salinity resulting in sustained hypo-osmotic stress, and (3) indirect effects associated with freshwater input, such as altered water quality, nutrient imbalance, and shifts in microbial communities. At present, direct experimental evidence linking these factors to the observed morphology is lacking, and the following interpretations should therefore be considered as mechanistic hypotheses rather than confirmed causal relationships.

Temperature is a key environmental constraint on coral distribution and performance. While optimal temperature ranges of approximately 25–29 °C have been reported for scleractinian corals21, the shallow-depth, temperate soft corals appear to exhibit slightly lower optima, typically between 22 °C and 27 °C3. Summer temperatures in Jeju frequently approached or exceeded the upper end of this range, particularly in recent years. In situ temperatures ranged from 28.6 °C to 30.5 °C at the time and depth of the observed slumping event, although the absence of additional, specific in situ physiological measurements and continuous environmental monitoring limits our ability to directly link these thermal conditions to organismal stress thresholds in the field. However, our analyses indicate that mean SST did not differ significantly among years, and long-term trends were weak and non-significant. Instead, temperature variability was expressed primarily through episodic extremes and their persistence rather than shifts in average conditions.

Experimental evidence for thermal stress responses in non-symbiotic soft corals remains limited. Studies on photosymbiotic taxa demonstrate that elevated temperatures can induce bleaching and mortality22, but these responses are not directly transferable to non-symbiotic species. More relevant insights are provided by Woo and Yum (2022), who investigated the temperate, non-photosymbiotic Scleronephthya gracillima under controlled conditions3. They reported morphological changes, including increased mucus production and reduced tissue tension, at 30 °C, whereas colonies maintained structural integrity at 26 °C and 28 °C. In the present study, in situ temperatures ranged from 28.6 to 30.5 °C, closely bracketing this experimentally identified threshold. These findings suggest that elevated temperatures may contribute to physiological stress in temperate soft corals, but are unlikely to act as the sole driver of the observed slumping.

In contrast, salinity represents a direct and potentially more critical stressor. Soft corals are osmoconforming cnidarians whose internal osmotic conditions closely track those of the surrounding seawater23. As a result, they are generally stenohaline and highly sensitive to salinity fluctuations. Although, current understanding of osmoregulatory processes in cnidarians largely derives from studies on sea anemones (Anthozoa: Hexacorallia: Actiniaria)22–26 and jellyfish (Scyphozoa)2. Exposure to hypo-osmotic conditions promotes water influx into tissues, initially causing swelling. Under prolonged exposure, however, osmotic imbalance can lead to cellular damage, loss of structural integrity, and eventual tissue collapse3,27,28. This mechanistic pathway is consistent with the observed sequence of morphological changes, including potential swelling, obvious drooping, and eventual disintegration. Photographs taken during this survey show instances of markedly swollen soft coral stalks, potentially documenting an early inflation phase preceding structural failure (Supplementary Fig. S1). These observations suggest the existence of an “inflation stage” in the progression of soft coral slumping. However, this proposed staging framework is based on cross-sectional photographic evidence rather than longitudinal tracking of individual colonies. Therefore, the inferred progression from inflation to collapse remains hypothetical and requires validation through repeated in situ monitoring or experimental exposure trials. Additionally, it should be noted that observed morphological states, including swelling and collapse, occurred across a range of colony orientations (upright, horizontal, and inverted), suggesting that orientation alone is unlikely to explain the slumping pattern, although this requires experimental confirmation.

Species of the genus Dendronephthya are known to be particularly sensitive to reduced salinity, especially under conditions of freshwater influx28. Although this previous study did not report severe morphological collapse comparable to the slumping observed here, it demonstrates the susceptibility of these taxa to hypo-saline stress. However, it should be noted that apparent morphological variation among colonies, including differences in coloration and visible condition (as illustrated in Fig. 2), cannot be reliably interpreted as species-level or functional differences due to the absence of taxonomic verification from collected specimens. Therefore, any apparent heterogeneity in morphological expression among colonies should be interpreted cautiously and not assumed to reflect interspecific differences in sensitivity or response. The photographic evidence presented here, including instances of swollen colony stalks, suggests a possible transition from initial inflation to structural failure under sustained osmotic stress, supporting the hypothesis of a progressive collapse of the hydrostatic skeleton.

The hydrographic analyses provide strong support for the importance of salinity-driven stress. Across all years, salinity followed a consistent seasonal pattern, declining from early summer into August before recovering in September. However, the magnitude and persistence of freshening varied substantially among years. Notably, 2024 exhibited the lowest mean salinity, the highest frequency of low-salinity extremes, and the most prolonged freshening events. Although interannual differences in mean summer salinity were not statistically significant in ANOVA, distribution-based comparisons revealed that 2024 differed strongly from the 2015–2023 baseline, indicating a shift in the overall salinity regime rather than a simple change in mean values. However, it should also be noted that these hydrographic metrics do not capture finer-scale microhabitat variability experienced by individual colonies, particularly within benthic boundary layers where physiological stress may differ from bulk water measurements.

The Degree Freshening Weeks (DFW) metric provides further insight into the role of persistence. Freshening intensity was strongly controlled by event duration rather than short-lived extremes. The most extreme event occurred in 2024, lasting approximately 50 days and producing the highest cumulative DFW (~ 15 PSU·weeks). This demonstrates that prolonged moderate anomalies can impose greater cumulative stress than brief extreme events. Similar, though less intense, patterns were observed in 2016 and 2019, indicating that extended freshening is a recurring but variable feature of the system. Importantly, the coincidence of high DFW values with elevated temperatures in 2024 indicates the presence of compound environmental stressors. Sensitivity analyses indicate that the overall temporal pattern and interannual ranking of summer DFW values are robust to reasonable variations in threshold definition and temporal aggregation. In particular, while absolute values vary depending on the selected salinity baseline and averaging window, the identification of 2024 as the dominant freshening year remains consistent. This suggests that the observed signal reflects a persistent hydrographic feature rather than a methodological artefact. However, DFW should be interpreted as a hydrographic proxy rather than a direct measure of biological stress. The metric does not incorporate species-specific salinity tolerance thresholds, fine-scale spatial variability within benthic boundary layers, or organismal acclimation capacity. As such, it cannot resolve physiological tipping points or differential susceptibility among taxa. Instead, it provides a first-order estimate of cumulative environmental exposure, which must be interpreted alongside biological observations and experimental evidence.

This compound nature of stress is critical for interpreting the ecological response. In a general climate-warming context, rising temperatures at higher latitudes are often expected to favor warm-affinity taxa, including subtropical or tropical species that may expand poleward as thermal barriers relax. However, this expectation does not directly translate to the present system, which is currently dominated by native temperate taxa rather than established warm-water assemblages. For these resident species, warming does not necessarily confer a fitness advantage, particularly when it occurs alongside reduced salinity. Freshening imposes osmotic stress that disrupts ion balance, cellular homeostasis, and membrane integrity, requiring substantial energetic investment in regulation. Elevated temperatures can intensify these effects by increasing metabolic demand and accelerating physiological processes, thereby compounding the energetic costs of maintaining homeostasis. Under such conditions, warming may shift from being a potentially beneficial driver to an additional stressor. Importantly, while these interpretations are consistent with known physiological principles, they remain indirect in the absence of experimental validation, species-level sensitivity data, and tissue-level confirmation of stress responses. Additionally, even in the context of future range expansions, the co-occurrence of warming and freshening may constrain the establishment and performance of incoming warm-affinity taxa. Although many tropical and subtropical coastal species are adapted to variable salinity regimes, this tolerance is often context-dependent and may not extend to sustained reductions in salinity or to conditions where osmotic stress is coupled with elevated temperatures. The combined effects can increase energetic demands for osmoregulation while simultaneously elevating metabolic rates, potentially narrowing physiological margins. As a result, tolerance to one stressor in isolation does not necessarily confer resilience to their interaction, and the realized niche of both resident and colonizing taxa may be restricted in coastal systems experiencing concurrent warming and freshening.

Overall, the ecological outcome is not determined by temperature in isolation, but by the interaction between multiple co-occurring stressors. However, this conclusion is derived from observational hydrographic correlations and photographic evidence rather than controlled experimentation, and should therefore be considered a working hypothesis rather than a confirmed mechanistic explanation. Nevertheless, recognizing these compound effects is essential for predicting both the responses of existing communities and the potential for future biogeographic shifts in shallow, temperate soft coral assemblages. These communities may be highly sensitive to concurrent changes in temperature and salinity, where even modest environmental shifts can have disproportionate ecological consequences. Accounting for such interactions is therefore critical for understanding and predicting community responses in coastal systems experiencing simultaneous warming and increased freshwater input.

Additional factors may have contributed to the observed phenomenon. Freshwater input associated with the Changjiang Diluted Water (CDW) can introduce altered nutrient regimes, pollutants, and distinct microbial communities9,29. Such changes may compromise immune function and increase susceptibility to opportunistic infections23,30,31. Although direct measurements of these variables were not available in this study, their potential role cannot be excluded. Reports of disease in soft corals remain limited, but documented cases include tissue degradation and disintegration resembling aspects of the slumping phenotype32. Whether the observed phenomenon represents a disease, a stress response, or a combination of both remains unresolved.

Hydrodynamic conditions may also modulate colony morphology. Soft corals can actively adjust posture in response to flow, influencing tissue rigidity and polyp expansion33,34. Reduced flow could exacerbate the effects of thermal and osmotic stress by limiting oxygen supply and waste removal, whereas strong currents may temporarily counteract tissue collapse. However, these effects are likely secondary to the dominant influence of prolonged low salinity and elevated temperature.

Overall, the results indicate that environmental variability in the study region is characterized less by long-term trends than by episodic extremes and their persistence. The summer of 2024 stands out as a statistically distinct hydrographic regime, defined by the co-occurrence of elevated temperatures, reduced salinity, high frequency of extreme conditions, and prolonged event duration. This combination of factors likely produced sustained physiological stress on soft coral communities.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the observed slumping reflects the combined influence of persistent hypo-saline conditions and elevated temperatures within the upper bounds of the local climatology. Reduced salinity likely represents the primary physiological stressor, acting directly on osmotic balance and tissue integrity, while elevated temperature may function as an amplifying factor that increases vulnerability to secondary stressors. Rather than a response to a single extreme event, the slumping phenomenon appears to be associated with the cumulative and persistent nature of compound environmental stress.

Future directions

Further work is required to resolve the mechanistic basis of this phenomenon. In particular, controlled experiments and physiological analyses will be necessary to distinguish between stress-induced responses, disease processes, and potential adaptive mechanisms. Experimental approaches should include single-stressor manipulations (independent control of salinity and temperature), as well as fully factorial designs combining low salinity and elevated temperature to explicitly test for interactive effects. Additional experiments simulating sedimentation and pollution exposure representative of Changjiang Diluted Water (CDW) conditions would help assess the role of water quality in driving observed responses. At the organismal and cellular level, targeted assays, including histological analyses, measurements of osmotic regulation capacity, and metabolic performance, will be essential for identifying physiological thresholds and failure points. Microbiome characterization and pathogen screening are also needed to evaluate the role of microbial dysbiosis or opportunistic infection in mediating tissue degradation and colony collapse. More broadly, improved baseline data on species-specific tolerance ranges, long-term monitoring of community dynamics, and assessments of post-disturbance recovery potential will be critical for determining whether these events represent transient disturbances or directional ecosystem change. Together, these approaches will help constrain causal mechanisms and improve predictions of how temperate soft coral assemblages respond to increasingly frequent compound environmental stress.

Limitations of study

This report is the first documentation of severe morphological changes in wild non-symbiotic soft corals providing detailed underwater photographs, and documenting them as a soft coral slumping event. However, as scientific study, it shows several limitations. These methodological constraints are not peripheral to the interpretation of results, but directly influence the strength of causal inference presented in this study.

First, the classification of morphological categories is based on cross-sectional photographs of different colonies collected at different times. No longitudinal tracking of individual colonies was performed. This limits the ability to confirm whether observed morphological categories represent sequential stages of a single process or independent responses across colonies. As such, the four categories should be interpreted as descriptive morphological states rather than verified sequential stages.

Second, the staging classification is based on visual morphology, and future work incorporating quantitative physiological measurements would be required to confirm the mechanistic basis of these changes and refine the staging framework.

Third, observations were collected opportunistically by citizen scientist SCUBA divers without a standardized sampling design (e.g., transects or quadrats), and without longitudinal tracking or repeated visits to the same colonies. As a result, the health status of soft coral beds was documented in a non-systematic manner, and the spatial extent and progression of colony decline cannot be quantitatively assessed.

Fourth, no measurable taxonomic information was collected. Based on the photographs, it is clear that the affected colonies are Nephtheidaen soft corals, including several different species. However, no attention was paid to whether or not other, perhaps less abundant and widely-distributed, families were also affected, and whether some species were affected more severely than others. Therefore, no indication about sensitive or resistant species can be given. This limits the ability to determine whether observed responses are species-specific or representative of broader taxonomic patterns.

Fifth, the choice of soft coral to photograph was biased by the subjectivity of the respective diver. While the loss of rigidity was the most notable and striking, and therefore photographed in abundance, the possibly initial inflation stage was inconspicuous, and thus considerably less often depicted on the photographs. Consequently, early-stage or sub-lethal responses are likely underrepresented, potentially biasing interpretation toward later-stage collapse morphologies. Therefore, we refrained from including it as defined stage until further evidence can be provided.

Sixth, this report relies exclusively on photographic documentation and does not include biological sampling to confirm species composition or assess tissue-level responses to environmental stressors. This precludes direct assessment of physiological damage, microbial involvement, or histopathological confirmation of stress pathways.

Seventh, no scientific in situ environmental logging was conducted during the study period, which limits the temporal resolution and contextual precision of the hydrographic interpretation. Although satellite-derived and remotely sensed datasets provide useful broad-scale estimates of sea surface temperature and salinity variability, they cannot resolve fine-scale or short-lived fluctuations at the reef or colony level, particularly within benthic boundary layers where organisms experience the actual physiological forcing. As a result, transient extremes, diel variability, and microhabitat heterogeneity may not be fully captured. This constraint reduces the ability to directly match observed biological responses to precise environmental conditions and limits mechanistic attribution to coarser-scale hydrographic patterns.

These limitations reflect the primary aim of the study, which was to provide a first field-based report of this phenomenon and to document its occurrence in situ. Our intention was to combine these observations with a hypothesis-driven discussion to stimulate interest within the scientific community and encourage further investigation.

Conclusion

The causes of the observed soft coral slumping event remain unresolved and likely involve multiple interacting factors. While a range of potential drivers, including thermal stress, osmotic stress, and microbial or pathogenic processes, may contribute, definitive attribution requires targeted follow-up studies.

The hydrographic analysis presented here provides critical environmental context. Although no significant long-term trends in sea surface temperature or salinity were detected, summer conditions (June–September) are characterized by pronounced intra-seasonal variability and episodic extremes. Within this framework, 2024 emerged as an anomalous year, marked by the lowest seasonal mean salinity, elevated temperatures approaching the upper bounds of the local climatology, and an increased frequency and persistence of extreme conditions. Prolonged freshening events, quantified using Degree Freshening Weeks (DFW), indicate sustained exposure to low-salinity stress rather than short-lived fluctuations, highlighting the importance of event duration as well as intensity.

Taken together, these observations suggest that the slumping of native soft coral assemblages is most consistently explained by compound environmental stress, with reduced salinity acting as the primary physiological driver and elevated temperature functioning as an amplifying factor. For these temperate, non-symbiotic communities, warming does not appear to confer a benefit. Instead, its interaction with persistent freshening likely exceeds physiological tolerance limits. This combination may impair osmotic regulation, compromise tissue integrity, and increase susceptibility to secondary stressors, including microbial imbalance or opportunistic infection.

More broadly, this study demonstrates that ecological responses in shallow, temperate soft coral communities are governed not by gradual warming alone, but by the increasing persistence and co-occurrence of hydrographic extremes. The patterns observed here point to a risk of continued decline in native fauna under future climate scenarios, as compound stress regimes intensify. At the same time, key uncertainties remain regarding species-specific resilience, the potential for recovery following extreme events, and the extent to which such disturbances may restructure community composition and ecosystem function in coastal systems such as Jeju Island.
