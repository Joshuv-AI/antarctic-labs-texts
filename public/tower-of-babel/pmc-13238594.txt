Five‐Year Success of Endodontics Plus Vital Pulp Therapy in a Symptomatic Maxillary Molar: A Case Report
Authors: Talal Al‐Nahlawi, Hadi Assadian, Ziba Ghorvei Nossrat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case report details the integration of nonsurgical endodontic treatment (NSET) and vital pulp therapy (VPT) for symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (SIP) and symptomatic apical periodontitis (SAP) in a mature maxillary molar, with 5‐year follow‐up. A 23‐year‐old female presented with sensitivity in tooth #26 due to extensive distal caries. Initial pulpotomy with Five Mineral Oxides (5MO) failed after 1 month, revealing SIP/SAP. Selective NSET was performed on buccal canals (MB1, MB2, DB), while 5MO preserved vitality in the palatal canal. Buccal canals were obturated; the tooth was restored with amalgam. Annual follow‐ups over 60 months showed no symptoms, intact periodontal ligament spaces, and transient sensibility response in the palatal canal on electric pulp testing a flexible treatment plan Selective NSET plus VPT provided long‐term clinical and radiographic success in this case, suggesting a conservative, biology‐based alternative to full pulpectomy in selected multirooted teeth with localized pathology.

Introduction

Pulpitis is classified as reversible when inflammation resolves after caries removal, and it is classified as irreversible when recovery is impossible, requiring partial or complete removal of affected tissue [1]. Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (SIP) and symptomatic apical periodontitis (SAP) are interrelated inflammatory conditions responsible for over 35% of emergency dental visits, mainly caused by active deep caries or traumatic pulp exposure [2]. SIP displays persistent, spontaneous pain and increased intra‐pulpal pressure, and SAP is categorized clinically by percussion sensitivity and radiographically by periapical bone loss [3], However, pain, although common, does not always correlate with histological severity [4]. The pathophysiology of these conditions highlights the importance of timely diagnosis and flexible therapeutic interventions addressing both pulp vitality and periapical health [5]. Untreated SIP and SAP can affect quality of life through persistent pain and complications such as abscess formation [3].

Despite advances in diagnostic tools like CBCT and laser Doppler flowmetry, accurately staging pulpitis remains challenging, as 20%–40% of asymptomatic teeth show histological irreversible inflammation [6]. The persistent inflammation in SAP contributes to tissue destruction and highlights the need for therapies targeting both pulp and periapical tissues [7].

Traditionally, these conditions are managed with pulpectomy or conventional root canal therapy (RCT) [8]. These treatment modalities have primarily emphasized nonsurgical endodontic treatment (NSET) to manage the infection and inflammation associated with SIP and SAP [5]. NSET focuses on eliminating infection within the root canal to prevent the progression of periapical pathology [2]. While highly effective, this approach is invasive and involves complete pulp tissue removal [3].

VPT, a biologically conservative approach, preserves remaining pulp vitality through indirect and direct pulp capping, and partial or full pulpotomy [9, 10]. By selectively removing infected pulp, VPT facilitates healing and regeneration of the remaining healthy pulp [11]. Recently, a growing interest has developed in using pulpotomy for SIP management. Advances in biomaterials and pulp biology understanding have substantially improved pulpotomy success, particularly in mature teeth [12]. Evidence demonstrates long‐term VPT success rates of 81%–93%, suggesting it as a viable alternative to conventional endodontic procedures [13]. Despite these promising outcomes, some VPT cases may fail, requiring investigation into contributing factors [14, 15]. Specifically, apical periodontitis on posttreatment radiographs is a key indicator of VPT failure [7]. Recent studies show that combining VPT with minimally invasive root canal disinfection can resolve SAP in mature teeth while avoiding complete pulp removal, shifting from traditional “all‐or‐nothing” treatments [8]. This method preserves pulp vitality while dealing with periapical infection, offering a less invasive alternative to complete pulp removal [2]. However, long‐term success in advanced inflammatory cases requires further investigation.

VPT efficacy in SIP with periapical pathology remains debated. This case report presents successful 5‐year management of a maxillary molar with SIP and SAP using combined NSET and VPT. Contrary to traditional full pulp removal via root canal therapy, this conservative approach preserved pulp vitality while managing infection. The patient remained asymptomatic over 5 years with no recurrence or periapical pathology, demonstrating long‐term success potential for this innovative approach.

Case Examination

A 23‐year‐old female patient presented to a private dental clinic in Damascus with intermittent cold sensitivity in the maxillary left molar region. The sharp, brief episodes subsided spontaneously within seconds. She reported no spontaneous pain, swelling, or chewing difficulty. Her medical history was unremarkable. The patient was motivated to seek early treatment due to a previous unfavorable experience with endodontic therapy on an adjacent tooth. The patient provided written consent for publication.

Methods

Investigation

Clinical and radiographic evaluations identified extensive distal caries in the maxillary left first molar (Figure 1). CBCT was performed to better visualize the extent of caries proximity to the pulp chamber and to assess the periapical region, given the tooth's proximity to the maxillary sinus floor. The cold sensibility test, performed using Endo Ice (Coltène/Whaledent AG, Switzerland) with the contralateral first molar serving as a control, elicited a positive response in the affected tooth that subsided within 10–15 s. Further diagnostic assessments, including evaluations for mobility, palpation, and percussion, yielded results within normal physiological limits, with no radiographic or CBCT evidence of periapical pathology. At this baseline visit, the diagnosis of reversible pulpitis was based on the patient's chief complaint of short, stimulus‐dependent sensitivity to cold, the absence of spontaneous or lingering pain after stimulus removal, and the lack of tenderness to percussion or palpation. These findings are consistent with current diagnostic criteria indicating a reversible inflammatory status of the pulp rather than irreversible pulpitis. Accordingly, a vital pulp therapy option in a mature tooth with deep caries and reversible pulpitis was put under consideration. It should be noted that electric pulp testing (EPT) was not performed at this initial visit, which represents a limitation in baseline vitality assessment.

Initial parallel periapical radiography.

The treatment plan included complete caries excavation followed by restoration. The patient was informed that pulp exposure would necessitate either vital pulp therapy or root canal therapy, depending on the exposed pulp's condition.

Treatment

Following informed consent, local anesthesia was administered using 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (New Stetic S.A., Colombia), and rubber dam isolation was applied to maintain aseptic conditions throughout the procedure. Distal caries was excavated under microscopic magnification (SmartOptic, Diplomat Dental, Piestany, Slovakia). A 2 mm pulp exposure revealed yellowish tissue suggesting early irreversible changes.

The primary objective was to achieve hemorrhage control and assess the health condition of remaining radicular pulp tissue. Deeper pulpotomy was performed in the palatal canal to ensure removal of superficially inflamed tissue and reach potentially healthy pulp. Slow, fresh bleeding from all canal orifices was controlled within 5 min using saline‐soaked sterile cotton, indicating non‐chronic inflammation (Figure 2A).

(A) Palatal orifice bleeding is a low flow crimson blood, indicating a normal ooze of blood with no inflammation. (B) Cement covering the orifice under isolation and cleaned pulp chamber.

Five Mineral Oxides (5MO) (Golden Yatti LLC, Muscat, Oman), a silicate‐based Portland cement derivative containing titanium oxide and sodium fluoride with enhanced antimicrobial properties compared to conventional MTA, was selected as it is widely available in the Middle East region and demonstrates favorable biological properties [16]. The material was mixed with distilled water according to manufacturer instructions (setting time: 3–3.5 h, comparable to MTA) and applied via micro‐apical placement system (MAP, Dentsply Tulsa, Switzerland) under maintained rubber dam isolation to ensure moisture control (Figure 2B). The 5MO was gently condensed with moist sterile cotton.

To prevent material displacement during setting, a protective base layer of light‐cure glass ionomer cement (Vitrebond, 3M ESPE) was immediately applied over the 5MO before final amalgam restoration in the same visit (Figure 3).

Application of 5MO following complete pulpotomy, accompanied by glass ionomer cement (GIC) and the final restoration.

Conclusion

Outcome and Follow‐Up

One‐month posttreatment, the patient reported severe spontaneous pain and biting discomfort localized to tooth #26. Clinical examination revealed pronounced percussion tenderness and buccal palpation pain at the apical region. These findings represented a diagnostic shift from the initial presentation to symptomatic irreversible pulpitis with symptomatic apical periodontitis, meeting contemporary AAE criteria: spontaneous lingering pain, marked percussion sensitivity, and apical involvement. This progression indicated pulpotomy failure in the buccal canals, necessitating root canal treatment.

Following standard anesthesia and isolation, the amalgam restoration and glass ionomer base were removed. Inspection revealed that 5MO had washed out from the buccal canals but remained fully set over the palatal canal orifice, suggesting the palatal pulp tissue remained protected and potentially vital.

The MB1, MB2, and DB canals were instrumented using the 2Shape rotary system (Micro‐Mega SA, France) to TS2 (size #25/0.06 taper) for MB1 and DB, and TS1 (size #25/0.04 taper) for MB2. Non‐setting calcium hydroxide dressing (Sultan, Sultan Chemists Inc., USA) was placed, and the tooth was temporarily restored with IRM (Dentsply, Tulsa, OK).

Fourteen days later, the patient was asymptomatic. Electric pulp testing (Pulp Tester, Foshan COXO Medical Instrument Co. Ltd., China) elicited a positive response from the palatal canal. While sensibility tests assess neural function rather than true histological vitality, the positive response combined with the absence of symptoms suggested preserved nerve integrity. The patient consented to monitor the palatal canal without endodontic intervention.

The buccal canals were obturated with single‐cone gutta‐percha (Sure Endo, Sure Dent Corporation, Korea) and bioceramic sealer (CeraSeal, Meta Biomed Co. Ltd., South Korea). The tooth was restored with amalgam over an IRM base (Figure 4A). Annual follow‐ups over 60 months confirmed absence of clinical symptoms or radiographic pathology. CBCT evaluation at 60 months revealed intact periodontal ligament spaces around all roots with no apical lesions (Figure 4B).

(A) Periapical X‐ray taken at the 5‐year recall, showing intact periodontal ligament (PDL) and healthy periapical tissues. (B) CBCT image (coronal section) of the palatal and disto‐buccal roots, demonstrating no periapical lesions at the 5‐year follow‐up.

Electric pulp testing continued to elicit transient responses, indicating maintained neural function in the palatal canal. These findings suggest, though do not definitively confirm without histological examination, that vital pulp tissue may have been preserved in the palatal root following the combined VPT‐RCT approach.

Discussion

This case shows that a flexible combination of VPT and selective NSET can successfully manage SIP/SAP in a multirooted molar. After pulpotomy failure in the buccal canals, NSET resolved symptoms while the palatal root, protected by intact 5MO, remained functional over 5 years. This suggests that, in selected cases, conservative and evolving mixed approaches may be a viable alternative to full RCT.

Inflammation in SIP is often localized [17], and radicular pulp may retain regenerative capacity despite coronal degeneration [18, 19]. High success rates for VPT in SIP/SAP have been reported [12], although outcomes remain controversial when apical involvement is present [3]. Evidence supporting selective retreatment is growing, Asgary et al. [20] showed that most VPT failures can be salvaged through selective NSET or re‐VPT, achieving over 90% success. Similar findings have been reported in other studies, including long‐term success in SIP with periapical involvement [3] and tissue regeneration in immature teeth treated with combined therapies [13].

In this case, washout of 5MO from the buccal canals likely contributed to treatment failure, while material stability in the palatal canal may have aided healing. Material displacement was likely influenced by the inability to delay final restoration due to the patient's travel schedule. Although bioceramic materials support pulp healing [21], their effectiveness depends on proper sealing and protection from contamination. Hemostasis alone couldn't predict clinical success, and failures could arise from misdiagnosis, insufficient disinfection, or microleakage [8, 22].

Diagnosis in this case relied on symptoms, cold testing, and radiography, which do not accurately reflect histological pulp status. The positive long‐term EPT response in the palatal root indicates preserved nerve function but cannot confirm true vascular vitality [23]. Thus, biological interpretation must remain cautious. CBCT is justified due to the palatal root's proximity to the sinus and the need for precise monitoring.

This report is limited by its single‐case design and lack of histological confirmation. Longer follow‐up periods and larger controlled studies are needed to define when selective NSET plus VPT is preferable to full RCT. Nevertheless, this case supports individualized, tissue‐preserving treatment planning, showing that selective intervention can maintain function and radiographic health in carefully chosen SIP/SAP cases.
