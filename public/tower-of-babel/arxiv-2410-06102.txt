An Anisotropic Model for the Universe
Morgan Le Delliou, Maksym Deliyergiyev, Antonino Del Popolo *

Abstract
Motivated by the back-reaction debate, and some unexplained characteristics of the CMB, we investigate the possibility of some anisotropy in the universe observed around us. To this aim, we build up a novel prediction for the Hubble law for the late universe from a Bianchi type I model, taken as proof of concept, transcribing the departure of such model from a Λ \Lambda CDM model. We dicussed the redshift measurement in this universe, and finally formalized the Hubble diagram.

Introduction

One of the main assumption of the
\Lambda
CDM model is that, on large
scales, an isotropic and homogeneous spacetime can describe accurately
the universe, at least at the background level. While most of observations
agree with this assumptions, they show a clumpy matter distribution
on small scales. This point is at the core of a debate concerning
the magnitude of backreaction of the large scale structure on the
background dynamics. Anisotropy and inhomogeneity might either explain
cosmic acceleration
Rasanen (2004)
;
Rasanen (2006)
;
Marozzi and
Uzan (2012)
,
or, according to other authors
Flanagan (2005)
, have negligible
effects. Apart from this point, anisotropies in cosmological expansion
have been discussed since the early works of
Misner (1968)
;
Gibbons and
Hawking (1977)
,
although their early models were favoring the late smearing of such
departures from isotropy. Together with the result that fundamental
observers measuring isotropic Cosmic Microwave Background
Penzias and
Wilson (1965)
;
Dickeet al. (1965)
[CMB]
radiation implied, we are in a spatially almost homogeneous and isotropic
region
Stoeger et al. (1997)
;
Maartens et al. (1995a)
;
Maartens
et al. (1995b)
, this
favored the dominant idea that the universe is an almost Friedman–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker

Friedman (1922)
;
Lemaitre (1931)
;
Robertson (1933)
;
Walker (1933)
[FLRW]
spacetime over keeping a fading out anisotropic behavior, as in Ref.
(
Collins and
Hawking, 1973
)
.

However, since the measurements of the CMB anisotropies in COBE
Mather et al. (1990)
,
WMAP
Bennett et al. (2003)
and Planck
Ade et al. (2011)

satellites, hints of a power hemispherical asymmetry have been found
and studied since the results of WMAP
Eriksen et al. (2004)
;
Hansen et al. (2004)
;
Jaffe et al. (2005)
;
Hoftuft et al. (2009)
,
continuing on Planck
Ade et al. (2014)
;
Akrami et al. (2014)
, revealing
a “preferred axis”
Zhao and Santos (2017)
for the low angular resolution
part of the radiation temperature spectrum and its polarization
Contreras et al. (2018)
;
O’Dwyer et al. (2019)
,
a quadrupole–octupole alignment
Schwarz et al. (2004)
;
Copi et al. (2006)
;
Copi et al. (2007)
;
Copi et al. (2010)
;
Copi et al. (2015)
;
Marcos-Caballero and Martínez-González (2019)

and a cold spot
Cruz et al. (2005)
;
Cruz et al. (2007)
;
Cruz et al. (2006)
. Moreover,
a recent emergence of a tension in the Hubble parameter measurements

(
Bolejko, 2018
)
between the values inferred from large scale
expansion of the Planck measured CMB
Aghanim et al. (2018)

H_{0}=67.4\pm 0.5

km/s/Mpc at 68% C.L., assuming the standard
\Lambda
CDM model,
and those obtained from the more local SNIa measurements
Riess et al. (2018a)
;
Riess et al. (2018b)
;
Riess et al. (2019)
,
the latest yielding
H_{0}=74.03\pm 1.42
km/s/Mpc at 68% C.L. Those
anomalies seem to persist, although statistical effects for the
CMB
Bennett et al. (2011)
, and systematic errors in SNIa have been
invoked
Rameez and Sarkar (2019)
.

Interrogations are thus piling up to reopen the case for anisotropic
expansion in the local universe. Bianchi models have been proposed,
escaping the prescriptions of Ref.
(
Collins and
Hawking, 1973
)
, to explain
the CMB anomaly
Pontzen and Challinor (2007)
;
Sung et al. (2011)
;
Russell et al. (2014)
.
A recent PhD thesis was even produced, discussing anisotropic universes

(
Macpherson, 2019
)
, and other types of non-FLRW models are
being explored
Cea (2020)
.

Our study, which aims at producing a Hubble law for the late universe
from a Bianchi type I model, and is amply motivated by all the above
hints, is therefore very timely and would be eminently useful in future
direct confrontation with observations of SNIa. Since this is
a proof of concept study, we choose to investigate the simplest anisotropic
model away from the FLRW model that is with one anisotropy direction.
The novelty of the work resides in the construction of the confrontation
of this simple model with Hubble diagram observations.

We organize the paper following Section
2
,
describing the model we used to transcribe the Bianchi I model into
an appearant almost
\Lambda
CDM model.
Section
3

discuss the measurement of redshifts in such universe, while Section
4

5

proposes a preliminary test of the model, before concluding in Section
6
.

2
Anisotropic
\Lambda
CDM Model

Our aim is to propose a model capable of including a level of anisotropies
compatible with observations that is looking very much like a
\Lambda
CDM
model in the past and developing anisotropies into the present. To
do so, we propose an expression of a Bianchi I model in a form similar
to the FLRW solution, and we develop its solution in order to keep
as close as possible to the derivations of the FLRW.

2.1
Model Setup

We want to investigate the simplest anisotropic model away from the
isotropic and homogeneous FLRW model. We are thus led to concentrate
on a flat Bianchi type I metric
Jacobs (1968)
which we choose
in the form

\displaystyle ds^{2}=

\displaystyle-dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)\left[\left(1+\epsilon(t)\right)^{2}dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}\right],

(1)

where
a
gives a global scale factor and we have chosen one direction
to expand anisotropically from the others, for which this departure
from isotropy is measured by
\epsilon
, the anisotropic perturbation
parameter. It is a measure of the anisotropic departure of the
model from the flat FLRW model by only multiplying the FLRW scale
factor
a(t)
by an amount
\left(1+\epsilon(t)\right)
in the chosen

x
direction of anisotropy. The Einstein’s field equations, in
this metric, for a perfect fluid of energy density
\rho
and pressure

p
give

\displaystyle G_{\>t}^{t}=3\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2}+2\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\frac{\dot{\epsilon}}{\left(1+\epsilon\right)}=

\displaystyle\kappa\rho+\Lambda,

(2)

\displaystyle G_{\>y}^{y}=G_{\>z}^{z}=-2\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}-\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2}=

\displaystyle\kappa p-\Lambda,

(3)

\displaystyle G_{\>x}^{x}=-2\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}-\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2}-\frac{\ddot{\epsilon}}{1+\epsilon}-3\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\frac{\dot{\epsilon}}{1+\epsilon}=

\displaystyle\kappa p-\Lambda,

(4)

while the Bianchi identity yields

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}+\left(3\frac{\dot{a}}{a}+\frac{\dot{\epsilon}}{\left(1+\epsilon\right)}\right)\left(\rho+p\right)=

\displaystyle 0.

(5)

We further restrict to
\Lambda
CDM a dust fluid with a cosmological
constant, for which the pressure equations become purely geometrical,

\displaystyle 2\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}+\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2}+\frac{\ddot{\epsilon}}{1+\epsilon}+3\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\frac{\dot{\epsilon}}{1+\epsilon}=

\displaystyle\Lambda,

(6)

\displaystyle 2\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}+\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2}=

\displaystyle\Lambda,

(7)

while the remaining inhomogeneous set remains as

\displaystyle 3\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2}+2\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\frac{\dot{\epsilon}}{\left(1+\epsilon\right)}=

\displaystyle\kappa\rho+\Lambda,

(8)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}+\left(3\frac{\dot{a}}{a}+\frac{\dot{\epsilon}}{\left(1+\epsilon\right)}\right)\rho=

\displaystyle 0,

(9)

The Friedmann-like Equation (
7
) can be rewritten in

\displaystyle\left(a\dot{a}^{2}\right)^{\cdot}=

\displaystyle\frac{\Lambda}{3}\left(a^{3}\right)^{\cdot},

to integrate into

\displaystyle\dot{a}^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{0}^{3}H_{0}^{2}\Omega_{0}}{a}+\frac{\Lambda}{3}a^{2}=H_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{a_{0}^{3}\Omega_{0}}{a}+\Omega_{\Lambda}a^{2}\right),

(10)

where the constant of integration is written with
a_{0}
, the scale
factor with the
0
index denoting values taken nowadays,
H_{0}=\frac{\dot{a}_{0}}{a_{0}}
,
the Hubble parameter at present, and an arbitrary parameter
\Omega_{0}
,
a relative energy density to critical, and where we have used the
definition
\Lambda=3H_{0}^{2}\Omega_{\Lambda}
. The Bianchi identity
(
9
) integrates into

\displaystyle\rho=

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{0}}{1+\epsilon}\right),

(11)

and we can combine (
7
) with (
6
)
to get the anisotropic perturbation parameter derivative:

\displaystyle\frac{\ddot{\epsilon}}{1+\epsilon}+3\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\frac{\dot{\epsilon}}{1+\epsilon}=

\displaystyle 0.

(12)

We then assume
\epsilon
to represent a vanishing perturbation at
some initial time just before the recombination (
\epsilon\underset{t\to t_{i}}{\longrightarrow}0
,
setting
\frac{a_{r}}{a_{i}}=10^{n}
, with
\epsilon_{r}\sim 10^{-5}

at
\frac{a_{r}}{a_{0}}=10^{-3}

Smoot et al. (1992)
;
Ade et al. (2011)
;
Ade et al. (2014)
)
Here, we use index
i
to designate values at this initial time and
index
r
to indicate values at recombination. The recombination
scale ratio and magnitude of fluctuations are extracted from the references,
and we assume that fluctuations caused by anisotropy imply such anisotropy
to be of the same magnitude. We use the power
n
of 10 to mark the
scale growth between the initial and recombination epochs. Its value
will be determined from the fit of the model to the data. We can rearrange and integrate the derivative equation into

\displaystyle\dot{\epsilon}=

\displaystyle\dot{\epsilon}_{0}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\right)^{3},

(13)

with the constant of integration reformulated such as to appear with

\dot{\epsilon}_{0}
, the anisotropic perturbation parameter derivative
at present. Combining it with the Bianchi identity solution (
11
)
allows, from (
8
), for finding the scale evolution
equation

\displaystyle 3\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2}+2\frac{\dot{a}}{a^{4}}\frac{\dot{\epsilon}_{0}a_{0}^{3}}{1+\epsilon}=

\displaystyle\kappa\rho_{0}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{0}}{1+\epsilon}\right)+\Lambda.

(14)

Anisotropy-Scale Relation Interpretation

From Equation (

) of Appendix

,
we can obtain an exact form for
\epsilon
such that the constraints
in the CMB
Smoot et al. (1992)
;
Ade et al. (2011)

lead to a current order of magnitude expected for the model: since
\frac{a_{r}}{a_{0}}=10^{-3}\ll 1

so
\frac{a_{i}}{a_{0}}=10^{-\left(n+3\right)}\ll 1
(

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\epsilon_{r}}{\epsilon_{0}}=

3
Redshift in Anisotropic Models

Since we want to produce a Hubble diagram for our model that is a
Hubble parameter evolution with redshift capable of confrontation
with the observable redshift vs magnitude relationship, we need to
make the connection of our model with the measurements of redshifts explicit.

In our anisotropic model, the redshift
z
depends on the direction
of observation: along any direction orthogonal to the
x
-axis, we
can expect, by analogy with FLRW, that
z=\frac{1}{a}-1
, while, along
the
x
axis, we can expect
z=\frac{1}{a\left[1+\epsilon\left(a\right)\right]}-1
.
In general, we need to define the comoving distance in any given direction
to measure real distances as we expect the redshift to be defined
in terms of observed and emitted wavelengths
\lambda_{o}
and
\lambda_{e}

by the form
z=\frac{\lambda_{o}}{\lambda_{e}}-1
.

3.1
Comoving Distance

We place the observer at the center of the frame and measure the comoving
distance in an arbitrary direction. We start from the line element
of lightlike trajectories proceeding from Equation (
1
)
for
ds=0

\displaystyle 1=

\displaystyle a^{2}(t)\left[\left(1+\epsilon(t)\right)^{2}\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)^{2}\right].

(17)

We then define
l
, the projected comoving coordinate orthogonal
to the
x
-axis and
R
, the comoving distance, as well as the angle

\alpha
of the observed comoving source with respect to the
x
-axis, with

\displaystyle dl^{2}=

\displaystyle dy^{2}+dz^{2},

(18)

\displaystyle dR^{2}=

\displaystyle dx^{2}+dl^{2},

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{dl}{dx}=

\displaystyle\tan\alpha.

(20)

In these terms, the comoving distance obeys

\displaystyle 1=

\displaystyle a^{2}\left(\left(1+\epsilon\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\alpha+\sin^{2}\alpha\right)\left(\frac{dR}{dt}\right)^{2}

(21)

which integrates, between emission and reception times
t_{e}
and

t_{o}
, into

\displaystyle R=

\displaystyle\int_{t_{e}}^{t_{o}}\frac{dt}{a\sqrt{\left(1+\epsilon\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\alpha+\sin^{2}\alpha}}.

(22)

This allows us to calculate the redshift in this anisotropic framework.

3.2
Redshift Calculation

The comoving distance covered by light emitted at
t_{e}
from
R_{e}

and received at
t_{o}
by an observer at
R_{o}=0
is the same
as that emitted after one period at
t_{e}+\delta t_{e}
from
R_{e}

and received at
t_{o}+\delta t_{o}
by an observer at
R_{o}=0
.
From Equation (
22
), we express the previous statement as:

\displaystyle\int_{t_{e}}^{t_{o}}\frac{dt}{a\sqrt{\left(1+\epsilon\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\alpha+\sin^{2}\alpha}}=\int_{t_{e}+\delta t_{e}}^{t_{o}+\delta t_{o}}\frac{dt}{a\sqrt{\left(1+\epsilon\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\alpha+\sin^{2}\alpha}},

(23)

which is equivalent, from the properties of integrals, to equating
the comoving distances covered by light in one period (comoving wavelengths)
at emitter and observer

\displaystyle\int_{t_{e}}^{t_{e}+\delta t_{e}}\frac{dt}{a\sqrt{\left(1+\epsilon\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\alpha+\sin^{2}\alpha}}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\delta t_{0}}\frac{dt}{a\sqrt{\left(1+\epsilon\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\alpha+\sin^{2}\alpha}}.

(24)

For any wavelength much shorter than the distance to the source (recall

c=1
),
\lambda=\delta t\ll t
, we can consider that, over such
interval of time, the values of the scale factors are constant, so
the integrals above can be approximated by

\displaystyle\frac{\delta t_{e}}{a_{e}\sqrt{\left(1+\epsilon_{e}\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\alpha+\sin^{2}\alpha}}\approx\frac{\delta t_{o}}{a_{o}\sqrt{\left(1+\epsilon_{o}\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\alpha+\sin^{2}\alpha}}.

(25)

Dropping the emitter’s index and observing nowadays, we get

\displaystyle z\equiv\frac{\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}-1\approx

\displaystyle\frac{a_{0}}{a}\sqrt{\frac{1+\left[2+\epsilon_{0}\right]\epsilon_{0}\cos^{2}\alpha}{1+\left[2+\epsilon\right]\epsilon\cos^{2}\alpha}}-1.

(26)

Further restricting to linear order in
\epsilon
, we obtain the linearized
relation between redshift, direction, and both scale factors

\displaystyle 1+z=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{0}}{a}\sqrt{\frac{1+2\epsilon_{0}\cos^{2}\alpha}{1+2\epsilon\cos^{2}\alpha}}.

(27)

3.3
Angle Averaging and Scale-Small Anisotropic Deviation Redshift Relation

As observations are not generally taking into account the possibility
of a direction dependent expansion, we need to produce a direction
independent evaluation of the impact of anisotropies. We start from
the general Equation (
26
) and proceed to average
over all angles: expressing the redshift-scale relation as

\displaystyle 1+\left[2+\epsilon\right]\epsilon\cos^{2}\alpha=\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\left(1+z\right)^{2}}\left(1+\left[2+\epsilon_{0}\right]\epsilon_{0}\cos^{2}\alpha\right),

(28)

averaging over all
\alpha
s produces factors of 1/2
for each
\cos^{2}\alpha
factor, so we get

\displaystyle\left(1+z\right)^{2}\overline{\left(\frac{a}{a_{0}}\right)^{2}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1+\left[1+\frac{\overline{\epsilon_{0}}}{2}\right]\overline{\epsilon_{0}}}{1+\left[1+\frac{\overline{\epsilon}}{2}\right]\overline{\epsilon}},

(29)

which, to a linear order, can be written as the angle-averaged, linearized,
redshift-scale relation

\displaystyle\left(1+z\right)^{2}\overline{\left(\frac{a}{a_{0}}\right)^{2}}\simeq

\displaystyle 1+\left(\overline{\epsilon_{0}}-\overline{\epsilon}\right)=1+\overline{\epsilon_{0}}\left(1-\frac{\overline{\epsilon}}{\overline{\epsilon_{0}}}\right).

(30)

Note that we recover the isotropic
z=0
at the present time.

4
Hubble Law in Anisotropic Models

The usual FLRW model produces a Hubble law by computing the Hubble
parameter evolution as a function of redshift. In order to easily
confront our model with the isotropic standard, we need to express
in the framework of our Bianchi I anisotropically expanding model
a similarly formulated Hubble law.

4.1
Generalized Hubble Parameter

Now, we want the Friedmann-equivalent Hubble parameter to confront
FLRW-based measurements done in an anisotropic universe. We can define
the Hubble parameter as the rate of relative volume change. In FLRW
models, such rate is related to the expansion scalar and to the FLRW
Hubble parameter straightforwardly

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{V}}{3V}=

\displaystyle\frac{\Theta}{3}=H_{FLRW}.

(31)

Assuming the FLRW model, Hubble measurements averaged with the angle give
access to the expansion rate. In this anisotropic model, the expansion
rate can be found in the Bianchi identity, which can be recast as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\ln\rho=

\displaystyle-\Theta=-\left(3\frac{\dot{a}}{a}+\frac{\dot{\epsilon}}{1+\epsilon}\right).

(32)

Thus, defining the FLRW-like angle averaged Hubble parameter from the
expansion
\mathcal{H}=\frac{d\ln V}{3dt}=\frac{1}{3}\Theta
, we
get from Equation (
32
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{a}}{a}+\frac{\dot{\epsilon}}{3\left(1+\epsilon\right)}.

(33)

Solving Equation (
8
) for
\frac{\dot{a}}{a}=H
,
and selecting the positive root (recall from (
11
)

\kappa\rho+\Lambda=3H_{0}^{2}\left(\Omega_{m}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{0}}{1+\epsilon}\right)+\Omega_{\Lambda}\right)
,
with
3H_{0}^{2}\Omega_{m}=\kappa\rho_{0}
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{a}}{a}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\left(\frac{\dot{\epsilon}}{3\left(1+\epsilon\right)}\right)^{2}+H_{0}^{2}\left(\Omega_{m}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{0}}{1+\epsilon}\right)+\Omega_{\Lambda}\right)}

\displaystyle-\frac{\dot{\epsilon}}{3\left(1+\epsilon\right)}

(34)

From (
10
) taken now, we can write

\displaystyle 1=

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}+\Omega_{\Lambda},

while the same treatment applied to Equation (
8
)
yields

\displaystyle 1+\frac{2}{3H_{0}}\frac{\dot{\epsilon}_{0}}{1+\epsilon_{0}}=

\displaystyle\Omega_{m}+\Omega_{\Lambda}.

The two previous relations yield

\displaystyle\Omega_{\Lambda}=

\displaystyle 1-\Omega_{0}=1-\Omega_{m}+\frac{2}{3H_{0}}\frac{\dot{\epsilon}_{0}}{1+\epsilon_{0}}.

(35)

We can thus rewrite, from Equations (
33
) and (
34
)
and the previous expression for
\Omega_{\Lambda}
, the Hubble parameter
into

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{2}=

\displaystyle H_{0}^{2}\left(\Omega_{m}\left[\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{0}}{1+\epsilon}\right)-1\right]+1+\frac{2}{3H_{0}}\frac{\dot{\epsilon}_{0}}{1+\epsilon_{0}}\right).

(36)

4.2
Hubble-Scale-Redshift Relation

At this point, we shall use Equations (

)
and (

) to introduce the redshift and anisotropic

\displaystyle\frac{a_{0}}{a}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\right)^{3}\frac{1+\epsilon_{0}}{1+\epsilon}\simeq

The Hubble parameter from Equation (

)
then reads, in terms of the above redshift expressions, the derivative

) from Appendix

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\simeq

\displaystyle H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{m}\left[\left(1+z\right)^{3}\left(1+\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}\left[\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon_{0}}-1\right]\right)-1\right]+1+\frac{2}{3}\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\Delta_{0}}}

\displaystyle\textrm{with }\Delta_{0}=\frac{2}{\Omega_{m}}\left[\sqrt{\Omega_{m}\left(\left(1+z_{i}\right)^{3}-1\right)+1}-1\right].

(41)

4.3
Hubble-Redshift Relation

From Equation (

,
we can further obtain an integral form for

\displaystyle\epsilon\simeq

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}\frac{\left[\sqrt{\Omega_{m}\left(\left(1+z_{i}\right)^{3}-1\right)+1}-\sqrt{\Omega_{m}\left(\left(1+z\right)^{3}-1\right)+1}\right]}{\left[\sqrt{\Omega_{m}\left(\left(1+z_{i}\right)^{3}-1\right)+1}-1\right]}.

We now have the tools to get the Hubble redshift relation, simplifying
the expression (

) of Appendix

,
in a shape close to the FLRW form

\displaystyle I\left(z\right)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Omega_{m}\left(\left(1+z\right)^{3}-1\right)+1},

\displaystyle I\left(z_{r}\right)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Omega_{m}\left(10^{9}-1\right)+1},

\displaystyle I_{0}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Omega_{m}\left(10^{3\left(n+3\right)}-1\right)+1},

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we have constructed a Hubble law capable of being confronted
with observations that were designed for an homogeneous and isotropic
expanding universe, while allowing for global anisotropy in expansion.
Such anisotropy would appear in observations designed without taking
it into account as distortions of the redshift-distance behavior.
This tool is obtained from solving the Einstein Field Equations for
a Bianchi I model that is almost FLRW. For this model, the effect of anisotropy on redshift is obtained which is then synthesized for
isotropy – assuming observation by an angle averaged redshift
expression. We finally produce a Hubble function of redshift that
we propose can be used in future confrontation with observations.
Such a tool, we argue, is needed now as we have several reasons, starting from the back-reaction debate, and going on with
some unexplained characteristics of the CMB that hint at the possibility
that the role of anisotropy in the universe is not trivial. The model
built in this paper discussed an anisotropic universe model that could be checked against CMB, or SNIa, predictions. As a preliminary example of how to use the model,
we confronted it with three data sets and found no conclusive results.
However, we argue that, with a more thorough approach, this method
could be a useful complement to other approaches such as that of
a Bolejko
Bolejko et al. (2016)
late model measurement diagram.
The model we build up will be checked in the next paper against cosmological
data.
