Exploring Large Language Model as an Interactive Sports Coach: Lessons from a Single-Subject Half Marathon Preparation
Kichang Lee

Abstract
. Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as domain-adaptive assistants, yet their prospective role as longitudinal, everyday virtual coaches remains underexplored. This single-subject, two-month case study examines an LLM-guided preparation for a half marathon (July 25–September 28, 2025). The participant interacted with a general-purpose LLM primarily via text, uploading training logs and occasional screenshots from consumer apps. We analyze the coaching interaction along three roles— planner, explainer, motivator —and quantify outcomes with session- and week-level metrics. Empirically, running performance improved from sustaining only ∼ \sim 2 km at 7 ′ ​ 54 ′′ 7^{\prime}54^{\prime\prime} /km to completing 21.1 km at ∼ \sim 6 ′ ​ 30 ′′ 6^{\prime}30^{\prime\prime} /km on race day; cadence rose from ∼ \sim 160 spm toward 170–180 spm; pace–heart rate relationships shifted favorably; and a derived efficiency index (speed/HR) exhibited upward trends in 7- and 28-day medians. While causal attribution is limited absent a control condition, these convergent indicators document meaningful progression under LLM-guided planning. Beyond outcomes, we surface recurring limitations that bound present-day LLM coaching: (i) modality and latency gaps —no in-run pose/HR context and predominantly post hoc dialogue; (ii) outbound feedback constraints —text-only guidance for embodied skills; (iii) motivation asymmetry —support largely user-initiated , weakest when most needed; and (iv) model specialization and safety —limited persistence, guardrails, and personalization. We outline design requirements for next-generation systems: persistent athlete models with safety-aware progression logic; multimodal, on-device sensing for near–real-time cues; audio/haptic/visual feedback loops; proactive, consented motivation scaffolds; and privacy-preserving personalization (e.g., federated learning, test-time adaptation). Taken together, this study offers grounded evidence and a concrete design agenda for evolving LLMs from retrospective advisors toward closed-loop, safe, and personalized coaching companions.



1.
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have rapidly evolved from general-purpose chatbots into domain-adaptive assistants that can reason over goals, constraints, and evolving context. Across diverse sectors, their ability to synthesize expert knowledge, generate actionable plans, and support reflective decision-making has been repeatedly demonstrated. Recent deployments in safety-critical domains such as healthcare
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
and in cyber-physical operations such as building HVAC optimization
(
Lee et al. 2025
)
underscore an emerging pattern: when paired with user-specific constraints and feedback, LLMs can function not merely as information-retrieval tools but as
interactive, situated collaborators
. This observation motivates an inquiry into their prospective role in everyday human performance settings—particularly in endurance training, where adherence, progressive overload, and recovery management are pivotal yet often under-institutionalized for non-elite participants.

Sports-related applications of LLMs are growing, from rule clarification and exercise-prescription summaries to lightweight coaching embedded in consumer platforms. However, current practice is still dominated by
episodic
interactions: one-off queries about form cues (e.g., “how to run a tempo session?”), nutrition heuristics (e.g., “what to eat before a long run?”), or injury-prevention checklists. While such micro-interventions are useful, they fall short of what human coaches provide—a
longitudinal
understanding of the athlete’s objectives, history, constraints, fatigue, and behavioral patterns, coupled with timely adjustments and motivational scaffolding. In short, despite surging enthusiasm, we lack systematic analyses of whether, when, and how an LLM can sustain the role of a virtual coach over
weeks to months
, continuously ingesting user states and outcomes to recommend, adapt, and motivate.

Running provides a compelling testbed for this question. As one of the most accessible forms of exercise, running requires minimal equipment, is logistically simple, and enjoys widespread participation. Yet for recreational runners, performance plateaus, overuse symptoms, and program non-adherence are common. Typical pain points include uncertainty about periodization (how to phase base, tempo, interval, taper), pacing (how to regulate intensity within and across sessions), and recovery (how to modulate load in the presence of soreness or life constraints). Traditional remedies—books, generic templates, or sporadic forum advice—offer limited personalization and little real-time reactivity to an individual’s fluctuating capacity and schedule. A dialogic agent that can track goals, read training logs, and reason about trade-offs could therefore address an unmet need:
dynamic, context-aware guidance
for non-elite runners.

This paper presents a two-month, single-subject case study probing the feasibility and limitations of LLM-assisted half-marathon preparation. From July 25, 2025 through race day on September 28, 2025 (Incheon Songdo International Marathon, half course), the author engaged an LLM (hereafter, “the agent”) as an interactive coach. The author supplied the agent with evolving constraints (e.g., weekly availability, session durations), short-term goals (e.g., target paces for upcoming workouts), and a longitudinal objective (half-marathon completion at a specific pace). In return, the agent proposed periodized plans, made session-level adjustments in light of fatigue and scheduling changes, provided educational rationales to support adherence, and offered motivational prompts when setbacks occurred. The study’s intent is
not
to claim superiority of LLM coaching over human coaching, but to document, in situ, the interaction patterns, decision points, and athlete outcomes that can inform future, larger-scale investigations.

Empirically, the author began the training block with modest capacity, able to sustain only a short continuous effort (approximately
2\,\mathrm{km}
) at a pace of
7^{\prime}54^{\prime\prime}/\mathrm{km}
, equivalent to about
7.59\,\mathrm{km/h}
. Over the subsequent weeks—following agent-guided progressions in easy mileage, strides, aerobic intervals, and long-run structure, together with recovery and strength suggestions—the author completed the target event, covering
21.1\,\mathrm{km}
at approximately
6^{\prime}30^{\prime\prime}/\mathrm{km}
(
\approx 9.23\,\mathrm{km/h}
). While a single case cannot establish causality, the magnitude and
specificity
of improvement, occurring under explicit schedule and fatigue constraints negotiated through dialogue, provide a concrete probe of where LLM-based coaching presently helps or hinders.

Our objective is deliberately pragmatic: we document how an LLM was used in practice during a two-month half-marathon build and, from that lived experience, identify concrete limitations and sketch what future sports-training applications will need. Rather than offering a comprehensive HCI analysis of “interaction mechanics,” we ground claims in observed use: where the agent’s planning, explanations, and motivational support helped; where they fell short; and how those shortcomings manifested amid real scheduling, fatigue, and recovery constraints.

Based on this experience, we emphasize three key limitations of current LLM-based training assistance.
First
, while the agent effectively produced a long-term plan and kept the user aligned with high-level goals, it lacked access to the runner’s physical state
during
training sessions (e.g., heart rate, cadence, posture). Without additional modalities from wearable or motion sensors, the LLM cannot provide accurate in-situ adjustments or semi–real-time feedback; future systems should integrate sensor streams and calendar information to deliver context-aware, adaptive coaching.
Second
, the communication channel remained text-only, constraining the expressiveness of feedback and instruction. Subtle elements such as form corrections or pacing cues are difficult to convey in text alone; multimodal feedback (visual, auditory, haptic) will be required to make coaching actionable in the moment.
Third
, even the best-designed plans are meaningless if athletes do not execute them. The case study underscored that sustained motivation and adherence are the hardest parts of training. While the LLM provided encouragement when prompted, future applications should embed more robust mechanisms for long-term engagement (e.g., gamification, progress visualization, adaptive motivational interventions).

In light of these limitations, we outline functional requirements for next-generation LLM coaching systems: (i) integration with multimodal sensing devices to capture real-time physical states; (ii) mechanisms for semi–real-time feedback delivery during exercise, going beyond text to richer modalities; and (iii) motivational frameworks that sustain long-term adherence, ensuring that plans translate into consistent training behavior. These requirements, distilled from practical use in this single-subject study, provide a roadmap for evolving LLMs from static advisors into truly interactive, effective virtual coaches.

The present work makes three contributions.
First
, it offers a richly annotated, longitudinal account of LLM-assisted endurance preparation, articulating concrete points where dialogic planning and explanation supported adherence and learning—and where they did not.
Second
, it proposes an analysis framework for
virtual coaching roles
(planner / explainer / motivator) and links these roles to measurable process and outcome variables relevant to recreational sport.
Third
, it distills design implications for next-generation LLM coaching systems that integrate real-time sensing, safety-aware progression logic, and reflective prompts to sustain motivation. Collectively, these contributions provide groundwork for controlled studies with larger cohorts, stronger baselines (e.g., certified coach programs, adaptive training apps), and objective endpoints (e.g., field tests and validated readiness scales).

In summary, this single-subject case study does not claim generality by itself; rather, it
constructively problematizes
what effective, safe, and motivating LLM coaching should entail for everyday athletes. By tracing how an agent and runner co-adapted plans under real constraints to reach a specific event goal within two months, we illuminate both the promise and the boundaries of current LLMs as virtual coaches, motivating a research agenda toward real-time, personalized, and ethically responsible AI support in recreational endurance training.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Large Language Models in Applied Domains

Large language models (LLMs) have progressed from general conversational systems to domain-adaptive assistants that scaffold concrete workflows. In healthcare, a recent JAMA systematic review synthesizing 126 studies finds that evaluations overwhelmingly emphasize examination- or vignette-style testing rather than end-to-end clinical use with real patient data; moreover, fairness, safety, and deployment readiness are inconsistently addressed
(
Bedi et al. 2025
)
. Subsequent surveys echo these gaps, noting that while LLMs show competence on diagnostic questions and summarization tasks, prospective evaluations embedded in clinical workflows remain rare
(
Bedi et al. 2025
)
.

Beyond medicine, applied work targets cyber-physical settings where language interfaces can bridge expert tooling. In building energy modeling,
EPlus-LLM
fine-tunes a text-to-specification pipeline that translates natural-language requirements into EnergyPlus models and automates simulations, improving efficiency for routine modeling tasks
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
. Complementary efforts use LLMs to normalize and map Energy Efficiency Measures across heterogeneous taxonomies, facilitating analytics and retrofit planning
(
Khanuja and Webb 2024
)
. Across these domains, LLMs reduce friction at
task boundaries
(e.g., specification, documentation, schema translation) and provide integration scaffolding for existing tools.

However, the evidence base still skews toward short, controlled trials, ablation-style demonstrations, or case reports without sustained outcome tracking
(
Bedi et al. 2025
;
Jiang et al. 2024
;
Khanuja and Webb 2024
)
. In contexts where progress accrues over weeks to months—such as endurance training—the absence of
longitudinal
evaluations constrains claims about real-world effectiveness, safety, and adherence. This gap motivates our event-focused, two-month case study that treats an LLM as a training assistant and records both process (plans, adjustments) and outcomes (pace changes, race completion).

2.2.
Conversational Agents for Health and Fitness

Conversational agents for physical activity and lifestyle change predate the LLM era and have been synthesized by mixed-methods and quantitative reviews. Luo et al. report feasibility and user acceptability for agents promoting physical activity, while highlighting repetitive content, variable study quality, and sparse long-term follow-up
(
Luo et al. 2021
)
. A meta-analysis by Singh et al. covering chatbot interventions across behaviors (physical activity, diet, sleep) finds small-to-moderate efficacy, with notable heterogeneity and limited durability of effects
(
Singh et al. 2023
)
. Aggarwal et al. similarly observe short study durations prioritizing usability metrics over rigorous, longitudinal outcomes
(
Aggarwal et al. 2023
)
.

LLM-era prototypes push toward richer counseling and personalization.
GPTCoach
examines LLM-delivered coaching aligned with health-coaching practices and tool use over wearables; however, its evaluation is short-term and centers on experience measures rather than month-scale performance endpoints
(
Jörke et al. 2025
)
. In parallel,
Coaching Copilot
blends an LLM chatbot with a human coach to support reflection and behavior change, demonstrating promise for engagement over a two-week window in a non-athletic leadership context
(
Arakawa and Yakura 2024
)
.

Taken together, prior work establishes that conversational agents—including LLM-powered systems—are usable, can convey evidence-based guidance, and may improve short-term engagement. Yet across health and fitness,
longitudinal, athlete-level
evaluations that trace coaching interactions, safety constraints (e.g., load progression), and objective performance trajectories (e.g., pacing across micro-/mesocycles, event completion) remain scarce
(
Luo et al. 2021
;
Singh et al. 2023
;
Aggarwal et al. 2023
;
Jörke et al. 2025
)
. Our study addresses this gap by documenting two months of LLM-assisted half-marathon preparation with concrete process and outcome measures, complementing short-horizon usability studies with an event-focused, real-world training narrative.

3.
Study Design

We describe the study design for a two-month, event-focused investigation of LLM-assisted half-marathon preparation.

3.1.
Objective and Research Questions

The objective was to examine whether an LLM-assisted training protocol could enable a novice runner to complete a half-marathon (21.1 km) within a nine-week build
and
to characterize the decision-level interactions that occurred in practice. We formalize three research questions:

RQ1:

Over a two-month build, how did key training/process metrics change under LLM-assisted planning?

RQ2:

In what concrete situations did the LLM help or hinder decisions (e.g., pace setting, session selection, load progression)?

RQ3:

What functional gaps emerged regarding safety, progression, and in-situ feedback?

3.2.
Participant and Context

The participant was a single male, age 25, height 174 cm (
\approx
5’7"), baseline body mass 85 kg, with minimal endurance background (occasional weight training; no structured aerobic conditioning). The training period extended from July 25 to September 28, 2025, culminating in the Incheon Songdo International Half Marathon (21.1 km). During preparation, daily bioimpedance measurements (InBody H20) indicated a
\sim
4–5 kg body-mass reduction with an estimated
\sim
1 kg increase in lean mass.

This study adopts an
auto-ethnographic
design: the participant is also the author. The approach enabled fine-grained documentation of lived interaction with an LLM coach but limits generalizability and introduces potential self-report bias; we discuss threats to validity in Section
7
.

3.3.
Data Sources

We relied on three primary data categories:

(i)

Structured training logs.
Each session included date, session type, distance, average pace, and session RPE, with free-text notes on fatigue, soreness, and sleep. Average heart rate (HR) was included when the watch was available; sessions without HR are flagged as missing and excluded from HR-derived metrics (e.g., efficiency index).

(ii)

LLM interaction logs.
The participant used the publicly available
ChatGPT
service for accessibility and familiarity. Screenshots from Nike Run, iPhone Fitness, and Zepp were regularly uploaded to supply structured context (date, distance, pace; HR when recorded). Dialogues covered meso/micro planning, weekly adjustments, explanatory rationales, and motivational support.

(iii)

Milestones and outcomes.
We recorded adherence lapses (missed/truncated sessions), notable fatigue or soreness episodes, and race-day outcomes.

Devices.
Early sessions were recorded on an Apple Watch SE (HR and cadence). Midway, device failure prompted a switch to an Amazfit Balance 2 paired with the Zepp ecosystem; subsequent HR/pace data were collected from this device. The smartphone was an iPhone 16 Pro for app logging and synchronization. Representative screenshots are shown in Figures
1
and
2
. Nike Run Club consistently surfaced date, distance, pace, and route, forming the baseline input to the LLM after each session (Figure
1
). In contrast, Zepp provided richer training signals via the Amazfit Balance 2—continuous HR, cadence, stride length, estimated
\mathrm{VO_{2}max}
, and recovery indices—enabling more precise contextualization when these data were uploaded (Figure
2
). The mid-study device change is noted as a potential source of measurement heterogeneity and considered in Section
7
.

Role expression in practice.

Beyond the concise definitions in Table
2
, the agent exhibited:

(i) Planner
—constructing micro/meso cycles and repairing schedules (e.g., swapping a tempo run after reported fatigue while preserving weekly volume);

(ii) Explainer
—attaching training-theory rationales (aerobic base, threshold, taper) that increased transparency and adherence; and

(iii) Motivator
—reframing lapses as load redistribution opportunities and offering simple prompts that supported continuity.

4.
Metrics and Analysis

We quantified outcomes at three levels:
(i) primary performance metrics
,
(ii) process and safety/progression indicators
, and
(iii) decision-level qualitative mappings
to the LLM’s functional roles. Unless noted, all time-series summaries are reported as distance-weighted daily values aggregated to weekly medians with 7-day and 28-day rolling windows.

4.1.
Primary Outcomes

Primary performance outcomes included improvements in pace (min/km), distance capacity, and completion of the target half marathon (21.1 km). We report baseline vs. post-block comparisons using session- or week-level

\text{pace}_{\mathrm{min/km}}\;=\;\frac{60}{v_{\mathrm{km/h}}},\qquad\Delta\text{pace}\;=\;\text{pace}_{\text{baseline}}-\text{pace}_{\text{post}},\qquad\%\Delta\text{pace}\;=\;\frac{\Delta\text{pace}}{\text{pace}_{\text{baseline}}}\times 100.

We also summarize
distance capacity
as the longest continuous run completed each week and report race-day pace and finish time as terminal outcomes.

4.2.
Process Metrics

We tracked exposure and distributional characteristics of training:

•

Weekly mileage
(km) and
long-run distance
(km).

•

Intensity distribution
by session type (easy/tempo/interval/long/recovery/strength) and by HR zones when available (Z1–Z5 based on estimated maximal HR).

4.3.
Cardiovascular Efficiency Metric

To probe aerobic adaptation, we focused on a single, interpretable metric capturing speed–HR coupling.

Efficiency Index (EI).

v\;=\;\frac{60}{\text{pace}_{\mathrm{min/km}}}\quad(\mathrm{km/h}),\qquad\mathrm{EI}\;=\;\frac{v\;(\mathrm{km/h})}{\mathrm{HR}\;(\mathrm{bpm})}\quad\big(\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}\,bpm^{-1}}\big).

Higher EI indicates greater speed per unit cardiac cost. We report daily EI together with 7- and 28-day rolling medians to visualize short- and medium-horizon trends in cardiovascular efficiency.

Prior to this program, the participant—like many recreational runners—assumed that performance hinges primarily on “training hard” (maximizing pace and elevating heart rate). The observed trajectories contradict this misconception: a high share of
easy
sessions enabled sustainable progression and recovery, while strategically placed higher-intensity sessions delivered focused adaptations. The resulting balance between low- and high-intensity work aligns with contemporary recommendations for endurance training distribution
(
Seiler 2010
;
Stöggl and Sperlich 2014
)
.

Cadence (spm) is a biomechanical marker of economy. The participant began near 165 spm, which at a given pace typically implies longer strides, increased braking, and higher joint loading. The LLM recommended progressive cadence elevation toward 170–180 spm. Figure

efficiency index (EI)
—the ratio of speed (km/h) to average HR (bpm) for each session:

\mathrm{EI}\;=\;\frac{\mathrm{speed\;(km/h)}}{\mathrm{avg\_HR\;(bpm)}},\qquad\mathrm{speed}\;=\;\frac{60}{\mathrm{pace\;(min/km)}}.

Higher EI denotes more speed per unit cardiac cost. We computed daily EI and summarized trends via distance-weighted 7- and 28-day medians.

5.3.
LLM as Planner: Constraint-Aware Micro/Meso Structuring (RQ2)

Across routine and exceptional circumstances, the LLM consistently operated as a
planner
, integrating constraints and restructuring sessions to preserve load distribution, safety, and goal alignment. The following instances illustrate how micro-level edits respected mesocycle intent.

(1) Symptom-aware adjustments.

When mild right-hip discomfort emerged (Figure
14
), the model linked symptoms to actionable modifications: immediate form cues (reduce overstriding; slight forward lean), warm-up/cool-down augmentation, short-term reductions in tempo/interval stress, and targeted accessory work (gluteus medius activation; hip-flexor isometrics). This
symptom-to-structure
mapping modulated the microcycle without derailing the mesocycle’s specificity.

(2) Recovery-informed weekly restructuring.

Following an unplanned rest day and a lower-body weights session (Figure
15
), the planner redistributed intensity across the week (easy
\rightarrow
tempo
\rightarrow
recovery
\rightarrow
LSD
\rightarrow
active recovery), increasing total mileage only within conservative bounds. The sequence preserved intended stimuli (threshold and long endurance) while spacing stressors to mitigate cumulative fatigue.

(3) Calendar-aware rescheduling with intensity separation.

Given a Saturday conflict (baseball game), the model advanced the LSD to Thursday and shifted tempo to Sunday (Figure
16
), maintaining separation among interval, long, and tempo sessions. This exemplifies pragmatic
plan–calendar co-optimization
that protects polarization principles while honoring external commitments.

(4) Guardrails and progressive overload.

Across scenarios, the planner role applied implicit guardrails—e.g., halving interval volume when symptomatic, extending recoveries, constraining LSD to Zone 2—and upheld progressive overload at the mesocycle level (weekly totals
\sim
32–45 km with long-run and tempo anchors). These controls lowered injury risk while maintaining directional progression.

Implications.

The planner translated high-level goals into feasible schedules under evolving constraints (symptoms, fatigue, calendar), preserving stimulus spacing, gradual progression, and specificity. Where disruptions occurred, it proposed concrete, safety-aware alternatives rather than cancelations. As discussed in Section
6
, residual limitations (e.g., missing in-run HR/cadence/posture signals; reliance on user-reported context) motivate tighter multimodal integration and explicit safety logic (caps on weekly load changes; automated flags for back-to-back intensity) in future systems.

5.4.
LLM as Explainer: From Prescriptions to Didactic Rationales (RQ2)

Beyond supplying session prescriptions, the LLM recurrently acted as an
explainer
, attaching concise rationales and step-by-step instructions that supported understanding, adherence, and self-efficacy. We observed three complementary didactic functions.

(1) Conceptual grounding of training decisions.

As illustrated in Figures
18
and
19
, the model contextualized base-building priorities (e.g., a Zone 2 emphasis) in terms of energy-system development, recovery management, and progressive overload. This transformed otherwise opaque directives (e.g., “run slower, run more”) into transparent trade-offs (mitochondrial adaptation, injury risk, taper dynamics), enabling acceptance of conservative prescriptions without the perception of undertraining.

(2) Procedural instruction for skill components.

When queried about post-run routines or cadence drills (Figures
17
and
20
), the LLM provided operational details—set/hold times, body cues, and common faults to avoid—that could be executed immediately. These micro-protocols (e.g., 40–45 s static holds; 30 s on/30 s off cadence bouts for 4–6 sets) reduced friction between intention and action, particularly for a novice unfamiliar with ancillary work.

(3) Explanations that scaffold adaptive planning.

In response to concerns about “low” mileage (Figure
19
), the LLM coupled justification (injury risk, recovery constraints) with concrete progression steps (adding an easy run; extending the long run by 1–2 km). Pairing the
why
with the
how
facilitated safe load increases while preserving the participant’s sense of agency.

Implications.

The explainer role made the logic of training explicit at two time scales: (i)
within-week
—how to execute a stretch, interval set, or drill—and (ii)
across weeks
—why base-first periodization and gradual mileage increases sustain long-term gains. In combination with planner and motivator roles, these didactic interactions helped bridge the gap between generic guidance and individualized, actionable coaching. At the same time, explanatory adequacy remained bounded by available data (e.g., absent HR or cadence during a session), suggesting that tighter multimodal integration (wearable HR/cadence, sleep summaries) would further refine and personalize future explanations (cf. Section
6
).

5.5.
LLM as Motivator: Limits of Passive, User-Initiated Support (RQ2)

While the agent provided encouraging language when prompted, its motivational impact was fundamentally bounded by a
passive, user-initiated
interaction model.

(1) Opaque motivational state without explicit disclosure.

The agent could not infer waning motivation unless the participant stated it explicitly. In practice, periods of low engagement (busy schedules, fatigue, dampened affect) were invisible because no behavioral or physiological signals were streamed automatically. As a result, the system lacked a basis for timely prompts (e.g., reframing a missed session, suggesting a lighter substitute) precisely when motivation was at risk.

(2) Asymmetric contact: help least available when most needed.

During high-motivation phases, the participant executed plans diligently and rarely requested motivational content; the LLM’s role collapsed to confirmation and minor edits. Conversely, during low-motivation weeks the participant often
did not initiate
a chat at all, precluding any intervention. This asymmetry highlights a structural limitation of pull-based designs: when support is most needed, the channel is least likely to be opened.

(3) Ad hoc encouragement vs. proactive, just-in-time support.

Because encouragement was typically triggered by explicit requests, support appeared
ad hoc
rather than continuous or context-aware. The participant perceived a gap relative to human coaching, where coaches proactively check in after missed sessions, detect early signs of dropout, and reframe short-term goals to preserve adherence.

Implications.

Effective digital motivation likely requires a shift from passive, user-pull interaction to a
proactive, sensing-enabled
model. Two design directions emerge:
(i)
Lightweight check-in scaffolds
(scheduled micro-prompts; push notifications after missed/truncated sessions; brief weekly reflection forms) to elicit motivational state even when the user would not open the chat; and
(ii)
Multimodal state estimation
(calendar load, sleep summaries, missed-run patterns, HR variability, step counts) that triggers
just-in-time
interventions (short replacement workouts, reframing messages, commitment reminders) without requiring explicit requests.
Integrating such mechanisms—with opt-in consent and privacy controls—appears necessary to close the motivational gap observed here and to sustain adherence in real-world training.

6.
Limitations of the Current Approach and Design Directions for Next-Generation Systems

This case study shows that an LLM—engaged primarily through text and retrospective logs—can support planning, explanation, and some adherence scaffolding during a two-month half-marathon build. At the same time, observed benefits were bounded by structural limitations in sensing, feedback channels, motivation, and model specialization. We distill these constraints and outline design directions for systems aspiring to function as safe, effective, and personalized virtual coaches in real-world training.

6.1.
Insufficient Modalities and Lack of Real-Time Context (Human
\to
LLM)

A core constraint was the
post hoc
, text-first nature of most interactions. Although daily logs (date, distance, pace, occasionally HR/cadence) enabled high-level plan adaptation, they were insufficient for
in-session
decision-making. During workouts, the coach had no direct visibility into form (pose, overstriding, asymmetries), breathing patterns, transient HR drift, heat stress, or terrain-induced load. As a result, the system could not (i) detect technique faults as they emerged, (ii) issue timely safety prompts (e.g., back off when HR spikes under heat), or (iii) exploit opportunities for micro-adjustments (e.g., shorten recoveries when HR stabilizes quickly).

Design implication.

Future systems should ingest
multimodal, low-latency
signals: on-body sensors (PPG/ECG HR, IMU for cadence/impact, footpods for ground-contact time), phone/glasses cameras for
on-device
pose estimation, and environmental telemetry (temperature, humidity, grade). A lightweight
streaming fusion
layer can map these to interpretable states (e.g., “probable overstriding,” “rising aerobic decoupling,” “heat-adjusted HR target”). To close the loop
within
a run, coaches must operate under tight compute/power budgets—arguing for
edge inference
with intermittent cloud sync. Architecturally: (i)
event-driven
pipelines that trigger micro-interventions when thresholds are crossed (e.g., HR–pace decoupling
>
5%); (ii)
graceful degradation
when sensors are missing; and (iii) explicit
uncertainty handling
so the agent can act (low risk) or query (high ambiguity).

6.2.
Richer, Embodied Feedback Channels (LLM
\to
Human)

Instructions were delivered almost exclusively as text, leaving a gap between
verbal prescriptions
and
embodied execution
. For motor skills—cadence changes, foot strike, hip control—language-only guidance imposes cognitive load and risks misinterpretation; post-run explanations cannot remediate within-run faults that accumulate fatigue and load.

Design implication.

Coaching outputs should be
multimodal and situated
: short
audio
cues (metronome/beat-synced prompts) for cadence;
haptic
cues on wearables for pace/zone drift; and
visual
overlays (phone or glasses) for pose targets with traffic-light semantics (“stable knee track,” “reduce overstride”). To avoid overload, feedback must be
bandwidth-aware
: prioritize one cue at a time, escalate as risks grow, and throttle prompts during high-RPE segments. Between sessions, brief video exemplars or stick-figure replays (from IMU/pose data) can support actionable reviews. Outbound messages should include
rationale snippets
and
expected sensations
(“hips should feel engaged; breathing steady”) to help users map text to bodily cues.

6.3.
Motivation as a First-Class Design Target

A recurring limitation was dependence on
user-initiated
contact for motivational support. When intrinsically motivated, the participant executed plans with minimal coaching; during busy or low-affect periods, the participant often did not open the chat at all—precluding timely reframing or adherence rescue. Thus, the current pull-based interaction fails precisely when motivational interventions are most needed.

Design implication.

Next-generation coaches should (i)
proactively
sense motivational risk and (ii) deliver
just-in-time
interventions. Signals can include missed/shortened runs, reduced step counts, poor sleep, rising HR at fixed paces, or calendar load spikes. With explicit consent,
check-in scaffolds
(micro-surveys, weekly reflection prompts) and
push notifications
after detected lapses can elicit state even when the user would not initiate chat. Behavioral design elements—
gamification
(streaks with compassionate resets),
social accountability
(opt-in micro-groups), and
commitment devices
(pre-committed time slots, soft pledges)—should be available as optional layers. Motivational prompts must remain
non-coercive
, with user controls for frequency, modality, and quiet hours.

6.4.
Security, Personalization, and Model Specialization

We intentionally used a general-purpose LLM without domain-specific training or privileged data flows. This minimized setup but limited personalization, robustness, and transparency. A generic model cannot, by default, encode athlete-specific constraints (injury history, HR zones, gait idiosyncrasies) as a persistent, safety-aware state machine.

Design implication.

We envision a
coach stack
with:
(1) a
persistent athlete model
(goals, zones, injury flags, historical load) backed by explicit memory/schema;
(2)
safety logic
and guardrails (caps on weekly load increases; flags for back-to-back intensity; heat/humidity adjustments) that the LLM must respect;
(3)
privacy-preserving learning
:
federated learning
to adapt shared components without centralizing raw sensor data;
on-device adapters/fine-tuning
for personal style and constraints; and
test-time adaptation
to recalibrate HR–pace relations under acute changes (heat, altitude, illness);
(4)
auditable reasoning
via structured rationales (citing guardrail rules or load metrics), supporting user trust and clinician review where relevant.
Security controls should prioritize local retention by default, differential privacy for aggregated updates, and user-facing dashboards for consent, retention windows, and data export.

From prototype to platform.

Operationally, these directions suggest a layered architecture:

Sensing layer
(wearables, phone, camera)
\to

Edge inference
(pose, HRV, decoupling)
\to

State & guardrail layer
(athlete profile, rules, uncertainty)
\to

Dialogue/feedback layer
(LLM with audio/haptic/visual outputs).
This separation enables incremental deployment (e.g., start with HR+pace; later add pose) and clearer safety testing.

Key Takeaways.

•

Multimodal, real-time sensing.
Replace text-only, post hoc logs with low-latency signals (HR/HRV, IMU, footpods, on-device pose, environment). Use
edge inference
and event-driven triggers (e.g., HR–pace decoupling
>
5%) to enable within-run micro-adjustments under power/compute constraints.

•

Embodied, bandwidth-aware feedback.
Go beyond text by combining short
audio
(cadence cues),
haptics
(zone/pace drift), and
visual
overlays (traffic-light pose feedback). Throttle prompts during high-RPE segments, and pair guidance with brief rationale snippets and expected sensations.

•

Proactive motivation and JIT interventions.
Move from passive, user-initiated chats to sensing-enabled check-ins: detect risk via missed runs, sleep/HRV trends, step-count dips, and calendar spikes; trigger opt-in push prompts, micro-surveys, and short replacement workouts to prevent dropout.

•

Secure personalization with explicit guardrails.
Maintain a persistent athlete model (goals, zones, injuries) and safety logic (weekly load caps, flags for back-to-back intensity, heat/humidity adjustments). Adapt privately via federated learning and on-device adapters; support
auditable
rationales for trust and review.

7.
Threats to Validity

Threats include the single-participant design, self-report biases, external confounders (weather, course), and measurement error from consumer-grade devices. We mitigate these by releasing anonymized logs, pre-defining metrics, and clearly separating pre- and post-event comparisons.

8.
Conclusion

This study investigated what a general-purpose LLM can—and cannot—do as a running coach over a bounded, event-focused window. Using ordinary consumer tools and predominantly text-first interaction, the agent supported planning (micro/mesocycles and schedule repair), explanation (energy systems, taper rationales, drill instructions), and selective motivation (reframing and encouragement upon request). The participant progressed from
\sim
2 km at
7^{\prime}54^{\prime\prime}/\mathrm{km}
to completing a half-marathon at
\sim
{6^{\prime}30^{\prime\prime}/\mathrm{km}}
, accompanied by improvements in cadence (from
\sim
160 spm toward 170–180 spm), favorable shifts in the pace–HR relationship, and a steadily rising efficiency index. Although the single-subject design and absence of a control arm preclude causal claims, the combination of process traces and quantitative markers demonstrates that LLM-mediated planning and reflection can align with safe, measurable progress over weeks, not merely days.

Equally important are the boundaries we observed. First,
modality and real-time gaps
limited in-run safety and technique coaching: without pose, cadence fidelity, or heat-adjusted HR context, guidance arrived primarily before or after sessions. Second,
outbound feedback channels
were mismatched to embodied skill learning: text alone struggled to convey motor cues as effectively as audio, haptics, or visualizations. Third,
motivation remained user-pull
: when engagement waned, the chat channel often remained unopened, blunting adherence rescue. Fourth,
model specialization and guardrails
were implicit rather than explicit: persistence of athlete state, caps on weekly load changes, and calendar-aware risk flags were emulated conversationally rather than enforced systematically.

From these findings, we derive a
design program
for next-generation coaching systems: (i) a persistent, longitudinal athlete model (goals, HR zones, injury flags, historical load) coupled to explicit, auditable safety logic (weekly load caps, back-to-back intensity checks, heat/humidity adjustments); (ii) multimodal,
on-device
sensing and inference (HR/HRV, IMU, optional pose) that enable
near–real-time
cues under tight power/latency budgets; (iii) bandwidth-aware outbound feedback (audio metronomes, haptics for zone drift, lightweight visual overlays) with concise rationales that connect sensations to prescriptions; (iv) proactive, privacy-preserving motivation scaffolds (opt-in check-ins, streaks with compassionate resets, small-group accountability) triggered by consented signals (missed runs, sleep, calendar load); and (v) privacy-advancing personalization via federated learning, on-device adapters, and test-time adaptation to maintain performance without centralizing raw data.

In sum, this work contributes (1) a richly annotated account of LLM-assisted, event-driven endurance preparation, (2) an analysis framework that maps
planner/explainer/motivator
roles to concrete training decisions and outcomes, and (3) a set of actionable requirements that translate observational gaps into system features. We view these as pragmatic groundwork for controlled, multi-participant studies with stronger baselines, objective field tests, and explicit safety evaluations—steps needed to move LLM coaching from promising prototypes to trustworthy, everyday training companions.
