Wake modeling for offshore floating wind farms incorporating time-lag effects in yaw and pitch states
Authors: Liye Zhao, Feiyang Qiao, Qianqian Li, Xiangyang Mu, Yingyue Pan, Yongxiang Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCompared to fixed-bottom offshore wind farms, offshore floating wind farms experience unstable platform motions that induce rotor misalignment. Such misalignment causes wake redirection, necessitating its consideration in wake modeling for enhanced accuracy. This study focuses on: developing an analytical solution for wake center deflection incorporating rotor misalignment based on counter-rotating vortex pair kinematics; proposing a 3D wake model using this deflection solution for wind speed prediction; and validating the analytical model against high-fidelity simulations with the coupled SOWFA-OpenFAST framework. The results computed by the dynamic wake model exhibit excellent agreement with Large Eddy Simulation (LES) data, confirming its strong capability in predicting spatial wake distributions under rotor misalignment conditions and accurately identifying wake positions. Finally, a dynamic wake model is derived using the Lagrangian approach. This research provides a predictive framework for offshore floating wind farm wakes, enhances wind farm power output forecasting accuracy, and establishes a computational foundation for effective wake steering control.Keywords: Wake model, Offshore floating wind farms, Rotor misalignment, Wake redirectionSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

According to statistics, wake interference causes annual energy production losses exceeding 5% in wind farms1–3. Active yaw misalignment control can modify wake center deflection, reduce wake impingement on downstream turbines and mitigate wake effects effectively4–8. This methodology has been widely adopted in wake control research for offshore floating wind turbines (OFWTs)9,10.

Wave-induced platform motions introduce operational instabilities11-12. Compared to land-based fixed turbines, OFWTs exhibit six degrees of freedom motion responses under combined wind-wave loading13. Motion-induced wake deflection alters wake center positions at various downstream locations, consequently changing wake velocity profiles. Research demonstrates that far-wake velocity distributions of conventional fixed-bottom horizontal-axis wind turbines follow symmetric Gaussian profiles14,15, while the dual-Gaussian wake model enhances velocity prediction in near-wake regions16.

For OFWTs, downstream wakes display complex meandering, deflection, and distortion due to inflow inhomogeneity and additional wave-induced platform motions17,18. Although this influence has limited impact on wake evolution dynamics19–21, it modifies velocity calculations in far-wake regions and alters wake center positions significantly19. Resolving OFWTs wake fields represents a sophisticated multi-physics coupling problem requiring further investigation20,21.

Engineering wake models are extensively utilized in operational wind farms owing to their computational efficiency, with the classical Jiménez model deriving wake deflection through initial cross-stream velocity calculation, assumed gradual decay during wake expansion, obtained analytical solutions from conservation equations, and final velocity integration, however, subsequent studies22,23 have shown that the model exhibits a systematic overestimation compared to experimental data. The Bastankhah model24 enhanced prediction accuracy by incorporating turbulence intensity and near-wake length effects, while the Shapiro model25 treated yawed rotors as lifting bodies to determine initial cross-stream velocity via lateral-force circulation, achieving comparable precision through spanwise momentum integration. Bastankhah et al.26 further modeled wake edges as vortex sheets, deriving empirical deflection formulas via counter-rotating vortex pair kinematics. Yaw-misaligned wakes exhibit asymmetric kidney-shaped velocity deficits, documented by Bastankhah27, and Howland28, which resist simple analytical characterization, with atmospheric turbulence further complicating wake morphology. While high-fidelity models like the curled wake model29 and point-vortex transport model30 reproduce these complex structures accurately, they incur computational costs orders of magnitude higher than analytical approaches.

Bastankhah et al.27 demonstrated that rotor misalignment-induced non-zero lateral forces generate transverse velocities within wake profiles. These velocities transport fluid replenished from upper and lower regions, forming counter-rotating vortex pair structures that drive lateral deflection of wake centers.

Bossuyt et al.31 further revealed that rotor tilt produces analogous vertical deflection effects, causing wake centers to shift vertically. Although active rotor tilt control remains unrealized, accurately determining wake positions under diverse inflow wind directions is critical for both wake steering strategies and power forecasting in floating wind farms.

This study enhances the double-Gaussian wake model framework by:

(1) Optimizing analytical solutions for wake center prediction.

(2) Incorporating offshore-specific wake diffusion characteristics for spatial wind condition modeling.

(3) Developing a dynamic wake model through Lagrangian formulation.

Space-time wake dynamics

While the deflection model derived in Sect. 2.2 assumes constant yaw and pitch angles (steady‑state rotor misalignment), the dynamic evolution of the wake under time‑varying platform motions is captured by the Lagrangian tracking method described in this section. Each time step generates a new wake control volume, whose center is advected downstream, allowing the accumulation of time‑lag effects.

Take the wake generated at each time step as a control volume, then the center of this control volume can be considered as a tracking point that moves with the wake.

Figure 5 illustrates the dynamic movement of the wake center. As shown in Fig. 5, at the first time step, the tracking point at the center of the wake generated behind the rotor is labeled P₁. At the second time step, after duration of Δt, the tracking point P₁ moves to a new position, while a new wake is simultaneously generated behind the rotor, with its center tracking point labeled P₂. After another time steps Δt, both tracking points P₁ and P₂ advance to new positions. This process repeats iteratively until, at the k-th time step, the wake centers behind the rotor correspond to the positions of tracking points P₁, P₂, …, Pk.

Schematic diagram of space-time wake dynamics.

As shown in Fig. 5, from time step n to n + k, the wind turbine yaw operation causes the wake to deflect, resulting in corresponding changes in the wake center’s trajectory.

The tracking points at the wake center generated at each time step move under the streamwise flow velocity. Their positions are determined by the time-integral of the wake velocity, where the cross-sectional wake velocity is solved based on the method described in Sect. 3.3.

Simulation settings

The simulations in this study are performed using the Simulator for Wind Farm Applications (SOWFA)34, a large eddy simulation (LES) wind farm solver developed by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). SOWFA is built upon the OpenFOAM computational fluid dynamics (CFD) toolbox (version 2.4.x)35 and solves the spatially filtered incompressible Navier–Stokes equations with a finite-volume discretization on a staggered grid. The filtered governing equations for continuity and momentum are given by:

where is the resolved velocity, the modified pressure, the deviatoric subgrid-scale stress, and the turbine-induced body force. The Coriolis and buoyancy terms are included to account for planetary rotation and thermal stratification. No modifications are made to the base solver; the standard SOWFA implementation is employed as described in Refs35,36. All simulations are conducted on a high-performance computing cluster using the Slurm workload manager. Jobs are submitted to the amd_256 partition, with a single node allocated per simulation.

To capture the changes in the wind alignment angle of the floating offshore wind turbine’s rotor caused by yaw and platform pitch, SOWFA is integrated with OpenFAST (v2.6.0)36 through a two-way coupling framework37,38. OpenFAST computes the rotor, tower, and floating platform dynamics, while SOWFA updates the flow field and the position of the actuator line model (ALM) at each time step. Simulation description

A fixed time step of is used for all simulations. The maximum Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) number remains below \:0.5 throughout the simulation, ensuring numerical stability. The total physical simulation time is 3,600 s. The first 400 s are discarded to eliminate start-up transients.

Statistical convergence is verified by monitoring the time-averaged streamwise velocity at multiple points in the wake. The running average is considered converged when the relative variation over a 500 s window falls below . All wake statistics presented are based on the remaining time series.

The study assumes a neutral atmospheric stratification for all simulations. Under neutral conditions, the Monin–Obukhov length is effectively infinite, and the surface heat flux is set to zero. The choice of neutral stratification is justified by the fact that many offshore wind farms experience neutral conditions during periods of peak power production43. The results are strictly applicable only to neutral conditions. The limitations of this assumption are acknowledged in the conclusions. It should be noted that a formal grid independence study was not conducted in the present work, mainly due to the following considerations. First, the refined grid resolution employed, (i.e., ), exceeds the widely accepted threshold for resolving wind turbine wakes documented in the literature. For instance, Sarlak et al44. suggested that approximately 30 grid points along the rotor blade are sufficient to capture the main dynamics of the tip vortices. Martínez-Tossas et al41.performed a systematic grid sensitivity study with , 32, and 64, and concluded that is adequate to resolve the near-wake velocity deficits and large-scale coherent structures; further refinement to 64 resulted in differences of less than 2% in the mean streamwise velocity. Second, the computational domain used in this study is relatively large, containing approximately cells. A single simulation requires about 72 h on a 64-core HPC cluster, and a full three-level grid sensitivity study would demand more than 200 h of computing time, which exceeds the resources available for this work. Nevertheless, we fully acknowledge the importance of grid convergence verification for LES studies. In future work, we plan to conduct a systematic grid independence analysis when computational resources permit, so as to further strengthen the methodological rigor.

It is important to note that the present validation is performed against high-fidelity LES data, not against experimental or field measurements. In the near and intermediate wake regions (x/D = 2–6), the model shows good predictive capability: RMSE ranges from 0.55 to 0.79 m/s, R² exceeds 0.71 (reaching 0.82), and NRMSE lies between 0.11 and 0.15, indicating that the wake width and center position are captured reasonably well. However, performance deteriorates significantly in the far wake (x/D ≥ 7): R² drops to 0.47 at x/D = 7, becomes negative (− 0.046) at x/D = 8, and is only 0.042 at x/D = 9, while both RMSE and MAE increase continuously (peaking at 1.16 m/s and 0.93 m/s, respectively). This trend suggests that the model overestimates the velocity deficit in the wake superposition region (x/D > 7), particularly on the y/D < 0 side, likely due to the simplified treatment of vortex pair dissipation and turbulent mixing. Therefore, the model is suitable for engineering predictions for x/D ≤ 6, but further refinement is required for longer downstream distances (Table 2).

Conclusions

This study develops a dynamic wake modeling framework for offshore floating wind farms that explicitly accounts for wake effects induced by rotor yaw and pitch motions. By employing an analytical solution for wake center deflection based on a counter-rotating vortex pair (CVP), the proposed model is able to compute the wake distribution under rotor misalignment conditions. Validation against high-fidelity SOWFA-OpenFAST simulations shows that the model predicts lateral wake center offsets with low mean error in the near-wake region and accurately reproduces the vertical deflection caused by rotor tilt. On vertical cross-sections, the normalized root mean square error (NRMSE) of streamwise velocity remains within 5% for and below 9% at , confirming the model’s accuracy for engineering applications. However, for the model overestimates the velocity deficit in the wake superposition region (with R² becoming negative at ), indicating that further refinement is required for far-wake predictions.

Furthermore, the Lagrangian-based dynamic model reproduces the time-lag propagation of wakes induced by rotor misalignment, capturing both the downstream advection of the wake front after a yaw maneuver and the sustained deflection of the wake center. Nevertheless, the model has the following limitations: all simulations assume neutral atmospheric stratification without considering thermal stability effects, so the results are strictly applicable only to neutral conditions and the tested operational range; the model does not resolve the instantaneous effects of atmospheric turbulence and is not a fully dynamic wake model in the true sense, being limited to time-lag effects. It should also be noted that the validation presented in this paper is performed by comparing the proposed model with other computational models (high-fidelity LES), rather than against real experimental or field data; therefore, the validation should be understood as demonstrating consistency with a trusted numerical benchmark. Future work will include systematic grid sensitivity analysis, extension of validation to non-neutral stratifications (stable and unstable), investigation of wake interactions in multi-turbine arrays, and further improvement of model credibility using wind tunnel or field data.
