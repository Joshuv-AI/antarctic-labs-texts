Regret and Sample Complexity of Online Q-Learning via Concentration of Stochastic Approximation with Time-Inhomogeneous Markov Chains
Rahul Singh, Siddharth Chandak, Eric Moulines, Vivek S. Borkar, Nicholas Bambos

Abstract
We present the first high-probability regret bound for classical online Q-learning in infinite-horizon discounted Markov decision processes, without relying on optimism or bonus terms. We first analyze Boltzmann Q-learning with decaying temperature and show that its regret depends critically on the suboptimality gap of the MDP: for sufficiently large gaps, the regret is sublinear, while for small gaps it deteriorates and can approach linear growth. To address this limitation, we study a Smoothed ϵ n \epsilon_{n} -Greedy exploration scheme that combines ϵ n \epsilon_{n} -greedy and Boltzmann exploration, for which we prove a gap-robust regret bound of near- O ~ ​ ( N 9 / 10 ) \tilde{O}(N^{9/10}) . To analyze these algorithms, we develop a high-probability concentration bound for contractive Markovian stochastic approximation with iterate- and time-dependent transition dynamics. This bound may be of independent interest as the contraction factor in our bound is governed by the mixing time and is allowed to converge to one asymptotically.

Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) provides a principled framework for sequential decision-making and has enabled major advances across domains ranging from large language models (LLMs)
(
32
)
to robotics
(
23
)
and healthcare
(
42
)
. Among the many RL algorithms developed over the past decades, Q-learning
(
41
)
remains a seminal method due to its simplicity and model-free nature, and underlies influential modern approaches such as Deep Q-Networks (DQN)
(
30
)
. Classical analyses
(
38
;
3
)
established convergence guarantees only in the asymptotic regime, which provide limited insight into practical, data-limited settings and motivate a growing body of finite-sample and finite-time analyses
(
1
;
35
;
24
)
.

In this work, we study the sample complexity and regret of online Q-learning in infinite-horizon discounted Markov decision processes. The majority of existing work on the sample complexity of Q-learning focuses on the offline setting, where state-action transitions
\{(s_{n},a_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are generated by a fixed behavioral policy and the objective is to estimate the optimal
Q^{\star}

(
35
;
25
;
24
)
. In contrast, online Q-learning updates Q-value estimates while interacting with the environment, with the evolving estimates directly determining the actions selected by the agent. Offline analyses primarily focus on controlling the estimation error
\|Q_{n}-Q^{\star}\|_{\infty}
and, as such, do not provide guarantees on the performance of the policy executed during learning. On the other hand, existing regret analyses for online Q-learning in this setting either consider model-based methods
(
16
)
or variants of Q-learning augmented with explicit optimism
(
18
)
.

Despite the practical importance of online Q-learning with simple exploration strategies, prior work does not provide regret or sample complexity guarantees for classical, fully model-free Q-learning without optimism terms. Most notably, Deep Q-Networks (DQN)
(
30
;
29
)
, one of the most influential algorithms in modern reinforcement learning, employs
\epsilon
-greedy exploration. Similarly, Boltzmann (softmax) exploration has been widely adopted in reinforcement learning applications due to its natural differentiability and smooth exploration properties
(
37
)
. Yet even for these foundational exploration strategies, no theoretical guarantees on sample complexity or regret have been established.

To bridge this gap, we establish sample complexity and regret guarantees for Q-learning with standard Q-value updates under different exploration policies. We first analyze the widely used Boltzmann Q-learning algorithm, in which actions are selected according to a Boltzmann (softmax) exploration rule. We then study a Smoothed
\epsilon_{n}
-Greedy (S
\epsilon
G) Q-learning algorithm, whose exploration policy is a convex combination of the uniform policy and the softmax policy. The smoothing is introduced solely to facilitate the analysis and does not alter the practical behavior of the algorithm, which closely mirrors standard
\epsilon
-greedy exploration. Our results rely on a novel concentration bound for a Markovian stochastic approximation (SA) framework that allows the contraction factor to converge to one asymptotically.

1.1
Main Contributions

We summarize the main contributions of this work below. In addition to establishing regret and sample complexity guarantees for classical online Q-learning in infinite-horizon discounted Markov decision processes, we develop a novel analytical framework for Markovian stochastic approximation that may be of independent interest.

Regret Guarantees for Boltzmann and S
\epsilon
G Q-learning.
We establish the first regret guarantees for classical, fully model-free online Q-learning without relying on additional optimism terms. We first characterize how the regret of Boltzmann Q-learning depends on the suboptimality gap of the Markov decision process, showing that it can be arbitrarily close to linear for small gaps. To address this limitation, we analyze the Smoothed
\epsilon_{n}
-Greedy (S
\epsilon
G) Q-learning algorithm and show that it achieves a regret bound of near-
\tilde{O}(N^{9/10})
. While this rate is worse than the
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{N})
dependence achieved by optimism-based methods, it demonstrates that sublinear regret is attainable for unmodified online Q-learning.

Sample Complexity of Online Q-learning.
We provide high-probability sample complexity guarantees exhibiting sub-Gaussian tail behavior for classical online Q-learning under Boltzmann and S
\epsilon
G exploration. Our results show how the exploration parameters, specifically the temperature schedule for the softmax exploration and the sequence
\epsilon_{n}
for
\epsilon
-greedy policies, affect the convergence rate of the learned Q-values to
Q^{\star}
. Moreover, our concentration bounds enable us to establish almost sure convergence of the Q-learning iterates, which is nontrivial for online Q-learning with decaying exploration. Together with the regret guarantees, these results illustrate the exploration-exploitation tradeoff in classical Q-learning.

Novel Concentration Bound for SA with Time-Inhomogeneous Markov Chains.
We develop a novel concentration bound for contractive Markovian stochastic approximation with time-inhomogeneous dynamics, where the transition probabilities depend on both the current iterate and an external, time-varying control sequence
\zeta_{n}
. Our concentration bound allows the mixing time of the underlying Markov chain, which depends on
(\zeta_{n},x_{n})
, to grow over time. This is essential for analyzing online Q-learning, where suboptimal actions are chosen with vanishing probability as
n\to\infty
. As a result, the contraction factor is allowed to approach one asymptotically. To handle this time-inhomogeneity, we combine novel Poisson equation techniques for controlling Markovian noise with a noise-averaging argument that yields sharp concentration bounds despite the weakening contraction. Beyond Q-learning, the resulting concentration inequality may be of independent interest for reinforcement learning and optimization algorithms with adaptively generated data.

There is also a growing literature on high-probability guarantees for reinforcement learning algorithms via contractive stochastic approximation. Some works

Q-learning was introduced in

2
Concentration Bounds for Stochastic Approximation

In this section, we first introduce the stochastic approximation (SA) formulation and then state the corresponding concentration bound. Consider the iteration

\displaystyle x_{n+1}=x_{n}+\beta_{n}\left(F(x_{n},y_{n})-x_{n}+M_{n+1}\right),

(1)

for
n\geq 0
. Here,
x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the iterate, where
\mathcal{X}
is a compact set. The function
F:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the update direction. The sequence
\{y_{n}\}
is the underlying Markov chain that induces the Markovian noise, and
\{M_{n+1}\}
is a martingale difference sequence.
\beta_{n}
denotes the stepsize sequence and is of the form

\displaystyle\beta_{n}=\frac{\beta}{(n+n_{0})^{1-\mathfrak{a}}},n\in\mathbb{N},

(2)

where
\beta,n_{0}>0
and
\mathfrak{a}\in[0,1)
.

We next state the assumptions required for our concentration bound. These assumptions are motivated by online Q-learning and are standard in the SA literature. The first two assumptions discuss the Markov noise and its transition dynamics.

Assumption 1
.

The process
\{y_{n}\}
is a controlled Markov chain with a finite state space
\mathcal{Y}
whose transition kernel depends on
(x_{n},\zeta_{n})
, where
\zeta_{n}\in\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
. Let
\{\mathcal{F}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
denote the filtration generated by
\{(y_{m},x_{m},M_{m})\}_{m=0}^{n}
. Then, for all
n\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}(y_{n+1}=y^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n})=p^{(\zeta_{n},x_{n})}(y_{n},y^{\prime})\quad\text{a.s.}

The sequence
\{\zeta_{n}\}
represents an external control process that influences the Markovian noise
\{y_{n}\}
. The dynamics of
\{y_{n}\}
also depend on the stochastic approximation iterates
\{x_{n}\}
. In the context of Boltzmann Q-learning,
\zeta_{n}
corresponds to the temperature schedule
\lambda_{n}
, while for S
\epsilon
G Q-learning it corresponds to the pair
(\epsilon_{n},\lambda_{n})
.

Assumption 2
.

For each
(\zeta,x)\in\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{X}
, the Markov chain with transition kernel
p^{(\zeta,x)}
on the state space
\mathcal{Y}
is irreducible and admits a unique stationary distribution, denoted by
\mu^{(\zeta,x)}=\{\mu^{(\zeta,x)}(i)\}_{i\in\mathcal{Y}}
. Define

\mu_{\min}(\zeta):=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\min_{i\in\mathcal{Y}}\mu^{(\zeta,x)}(i).

We assume that
\mu_{\min}(\zeta)>0
for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Moreover, there exists a designated state
i^{\star}\in\mathcal{Y}
such that, for any
(\zeta,x)\in\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{X}
and any initial state, the expected hitting time of
i^{\star}
is upper bounded by
\mathfrak{c}_{1}/\mu_{\min}(\zeta)
, for some constant
\mathfrak{c}_{1}>0
.

The condition
\mu_{\min}(\zeta)>0
is standard for irreducible Markov chains on finite state spaces, and ensures that every state has positive stationary probability uniformly over
x\in\mathcal{X}
. The second part of the assumption controls the sensitivity of the Markovian noise through the expected hitting time of a designated state
i^{\star}
. An equivalent condition could be formulated in terms of the mixing time, since for irreducible finite-state Markov chains, bounds on expected hitting times and mixing times are equivalent up to constants
(
22
)
. In particular, the expected hitting time of
i^{\star}
scales as
1/\mu^{(\zeta,x)}(i^{\star})
, yielding the stated bound of order
1/\mu_{\min}(\zeta)
. Unlike previous works
(
7
)
, we do not assume a uniform bound on the hitting time, which is necessary for online Q-learning where hitting times may diverge as suboptimal actions are played with vanishing probability.

We are interested in the fixed point of the stationary average of map
F(\cdot,\cdot)
, where
F(\cdot,i)
is Lipschitz continuous in the first argument for each
i\in\mathcal{Y}
. We define the stationary average of the function
F(\cdot,i)
under the stationary distribution corresponding to
(\zeta,w)
, with
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z},w\in\mathcal{X}
, as

\displaystyle\bar{F}^{(\zeta,w)}(\cdot):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{Y}}\mu^{(\zeta,w)}(i)\,F(\cdot,i).

Then we have the following key contractive assumption.

Assumption 3
.

For each
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
and
w\in\mathcal{X}
, the mapping
\bar{F}^{(\zeta,w)}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
is contractive with contraction factor
\alpha(\zeta)\in[0,1)
, i.e.,

\bigl\|\bar{F}^{(\zeta,w)}(x)-\bar{F}^{(\zeta,w)}(z)\bigr\|\;\leq\;\alpha(\zeta)\,\|x-z\|,\quad\forall x,z\in\mathcal{X},

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes any compatible norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, the fixed point of
\bar{F}^{(\zeta,w)}
does not depend on
(\zeta,w)
and we denote this common fixed point by
x^{\star}
.

The assumption that the stationary averaged map is contractive and admits a common fixed point is standard in the analysis of Q-learning; see, e.g.,
(
35
;
7
)
. In the case of Q-learning, this common fixed point is precisely the optimal Q-value function
Q^{\star}
. While our concentration bound applies under any compatible norm, the contraction property for Q-learning holds with respect to the
\ell_{\infty}
norm. The following assumption specifies how the contraction factor depends on the transition dynamics of the Markov chain.

Assumption 4
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}(\zeta):=1-\alpha(\zeta)
. We assume that
\tilde{\alpha}(\zeta)=\mathfrak{c}_{2}\,\mu_{\min}(\zeta),

for some constant
\mathfrak{c}_{2}>0
.

The above assumption states that the contraction factor approaches one as
\mu_{\min}(\zeta)
decays or equivalently as the mixing time explodes. Such dependence is often made explicit in analyses of Q-learning, and also appears in other algorithms such as variants of TD learning, where it is typically implicit
(
8
)
. By incorporating this dependence directly into the stochastic approximation framework, we extend concentration bounds for SA to time-inhomogeneous Markov chains.

Our next assumption is the key assumption that characterizes the time-inhomogeneous nature of the Markov chain induced by the control process and specifies how the control process is allowed to vary over time.

Assumption 5
.

The control process
\{\zeta_{n}\}
satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

Time-varying contraction factor.

\tilde{\alpha}(\zeta_{n})\geq\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{3}}{(n+n_{0})^{\kappa_{1}}},\quad\mathfrak{c}_{3}>0,\kappa_{1}\in[0,1].

(b)

Temporal smoothness.
For all
i\in\mathcal{Y}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{Y}}|p^{(\zeta_{n},x)}(i,j)-p^{(\zeta_{n+1},x)}(i,j)|\leq L_{1}(n),

such that

L_{1}(n)=\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{4}}{(n+n_{0})^{1-\kappa_{2}}},

where
\mathfrak{c}_{4}>0,\kappa_{2}\in[0,1]
.

(c)

Iterate sensitivity.
For each
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}
, the map
x\mapsto p^{(\zeta,x)}(i,j)
is Lipschitz, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{Y}}|p^{(\zeta,x)}(i,j)-p^{(\zeta,x^{\prime})}(i,j)|\leq L_{2}(\zeta)\|x-x^{\prime}\|,

for all
i\in\mathcal{Y}
and
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle L_{2}(\zeta_{n})\leq\mathfrak{c}_{5}(n+n_{0})^{\kappa_{3}}\log(n+n_{0}),

where
\mathfrak{c}_{5}>0,~\kappa_{3}\in[0,1)
.

We use the same
n_{0}
in the stepsize definition and in Assumption
5
for notational simplicity; these constants can be chosen independently without affecting the analysis.

Condition (a) controls the rate at which the contraction factor approaches one and can be interpreted as a constraint on the growth of the mixing time, since the previous assumptions imply
\mu_{\min}(\zeta_{n})\geq\mathfrak{c}_{3}/(\mathfrak{c}_{2}(n+n_{0})^{\kappa_{1}})
. Conditions (b) and (c) ensure that the transition kernel
p^{(\zeta_{n},x_{n})}
varies slowly enough over time and with the iterate to allow effective averaging of the Markovian noise in the concentration analysis. For online Q-learning, the parameters
\mathfrak{a}
,
\kappa_{1}
,
\kappa_{2}
, and
\kappa_{3}
are determined by the choice of stepsize and exploration policy, and play a central role in shaping both the concentration bounds and the resulting regret guarantees.

While the divergence of the mixing time is intrinsic to vanishing exploration in online Q-learning, we emphasize that the growth of the Lipschitz constant
L_{2}(\zeta)
is also unavoidable for softmax-based exploration. As the temperature decays to promote exploitation, the induced transition kernel becomes increasingly sensitive to changes in the iterate. This behavior is illustrated in Figure

\mathbb{E}[M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}]=0\quad\text{a.s.}

2.1
Concentration Bound

We establish the following high-probability concentration bound on
\|x_{n}-x^{\star}\|
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any
\delta>0
, iteration (
1
) satisfies the following with probability at least
1-\frac{\pi^{2}d}{6}\delta
:

\|x_{n}-x^{\star}\|\;\leq\;\tilde{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log\!\left(\frac{n^{2}}{\delta}\right)}{n}}\;n^{\,2\kappa_{1}+\max\{\mathfrak{a}+\kappa_{3},\;\kappa_{2}\}}\right),

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, the iterates converge almost surely to the fixed point, i.e.,
x_{n}\to x^{\star}
as
n\to\infty
.

We make the dependence on
\delta
explicit here to illustrate how it enters the bound; henceforth, we suppress this dependence as it appears in the same form in all subsequent results. The above result is a maximal or uniform concentration bound which holds for all time
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We make a few remarks on the conditions under which the bound holds and its dependence on the hyperparameters.

Remark
.

The bound in Theorem
2.1
holds for sufficiently large
n_{0}
(Appendix
D.8
) and for hyperparameter choices satisfying the conditions in Appendix
B
. These conditions are mild and are met by standard stepsize schedules and exploration policies used in online Q-learning.

Remark
.

The optimal rate is achieved for
\mathfrak{a}=0
, corresponding to the stepsize
\Theta(1/n)
, a phenomenon commonly observed in the SA literature
(
11
;
35
)
. The dependence on the hyperparameters
\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{3}
is also intuitive: smaller
\kappa_{1}
(slower increase of the contraction factor) and smaller
\kappa_{2}
and
\kappa_{3}
(slower variation of the transition kernel) lead to improved rates.

The resulting optimal convergence rate is

\tilde{O}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log\!\left(\frac{n^{2}}{\delta}\right)}{n}}\right),

achieved when
\mathfrak{a}=\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{3}=0
. This matches known concentration bounds for Markovian SA with time-homogeneous Markov chains
(
12
)
.

At a high level, the key challenge in our analysis is handling time-varying transition kernels together with a contraction factor that grows and approaches one. To address the resulting time-inhomogeneous Markovian noise, we use solutions of the Poisson equation and develop a novel analysis of how these solutions vary as the transition kernel evolves with the iterates and the control process. This is combined with a noise-averaging argument that yields sharp bounds on the decay of the noise despite these variations. Crucially, the noise-averaging argument allows us to decouple the stochastic fluctuations from the weakening contraction, leading to sharp concentration bounds even as the contraction factor approaches one.

3
Online Q-learning

Consider a Markov Decision Process (MDP)
\mathcal{M}
with finite state and action spaces
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
, respectively. The controlled transition probabilities are given by
p:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to[0,1]
, where

\mathbb{P}(s_{n+1}=s^{\prime}\mid s_{n}=s,a_{n}=a)=p(s,a,s^{\prime}),
for
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S},\,a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
be the discount factor, and let
r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,R_{\max}]
denote the reward function. The objective is to choose actions
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
so as to maximize the expected discounted return

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{n}r(s_{n},a_{n})\right]
, where
s_{n}
and
a_{n}
denote the state and action at time
n
, respectively. We denote the MDP by
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,r,\gamma)
.

Let
V^{\star}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the optimal value function,

V^{\star}(s)=\sup_{\pi}\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{n}r(s_{n},a_{n})\mid s_{0}=s\right],

where the supremum is taken over all stationary (possibly randomized) policies. The corresponding optimal action-value function
Q^{\star}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

Q^{\star}(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s,a,s^{\prime})V^{\star}(s^{\prime}).

Equivalently,
Q^{\star}
is the unique fixed point of the Bellman optimality operator
\mathcal{T}:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}
defined by

(\mathcal{T}Q)(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s,a,s^{\prime})\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

The optimal policy can be characterized directly in terms of
Q^{\star}
. In particular, actions that maximize the expected discounted return in state
s
are given by the maximizers of
Q^{\star}(s,\cdot)
. We define the set of optimal actions in state
s
as

\mathcal{A}^{\star}(s):=\left\{a\in\mathcal{A}:Q^{\star}(s,a)=\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{\star}(s,a^{\prime})\right\}.

Any stationary policy
\pi^{\star}
satisfying
\pi^{\star}(s)\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
is optimal. Consequently, the problem of computing an optimal policy reduces to finding the optimal action-value function
Q^{\star}
. If the reward function
r
and transition kernel
p
were known,
Q^{\star}
could be computed using dynamic programming. In contrast, when the MDP is accessed through a single trajectory of the controlled Markov chain, the Q-learning algorithm uses the following Q-value updates.

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}(s,a)=Q_{n}(s,a)+\beta_{n}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{s_{n}=s,a_{n}=a\right\}}

\displaystyle\times\left(r(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{n}(s_{n+1},a^{\prime})-Q_{n}(s,a)\right),

(3)

for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
and for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The indicator
\mathbbm{1}{\left\{s_{n}=s,a_{n}=a\right\}}
arises from the asynchronous nature of Q-learning, since at each time step only the action-value corresponding to the currently visited state–action pair
(s_{n},a_{n})
is updated, while all other components remain unchanged.

This asynchronous update can be studied in both offline and online settings: in the offline setting actions are generated according to a fixed behavior policy
\pi_{b}
, whereas in the online setting studied here the Q-values learned up to time
n
are used to select the action
a_{n}
. We focus on online Q-learning, in which both convergence to
Q^{\star}
and the accumulated reward along the learning trajectory are of interest. This setting is particularly relevant in applications where data are generated sequentially through interaction with the environment and poor decisions during learning incur real costs, such as control, robotics, and adaptive decision-making systems.

3.1
Regret in Discounted Q-Learning

We next define the regret of an online learning algorithm in the discounted MDP setting.

Definition 3.1

(Regret)
.

The regret incurred by an online learning algorithm up to time
N
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{N}:=(1-\gamma)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(V^{\star}(s_{n})-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{m=n}^{\infty}\gamma^{m-n}r(s_{m},a_{m})\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\right).

This definition is standard in the literature; see, for example, Definition 4.1 of
(
16
)
. Throughout this work, we establish bounds on
\mathcal{R}_{N}
that hold with high probability over the randomness of the learning trajectory. Intuitively, the regret
\mathcal{R}_{N}
measures the cumulative performance shortfall of the learning algorithm along the realized trajectory. At each time
n
, it compares the optimal discounted value
V^{\star}(s_{n})
with the expected discounted achieved obtained by the algorithm from that time onward and aggregates this loss over time. The normalization factor
(1-\gamma)
converts this discounted future loss into a quantity comparable to a single time-step reward, so that each term in the sum represents the loss incurred at time
n
.

This regret definition highlights a subtle tension in online Q-learning. On the one hand, the algorithm must quickly reduce the probability of suboptimal actions, since such actions directly incur regret. On the other hand, changing the policy parameters too aggressively can harm exploration and, more subtly, cause the induced policy, defined jointly by
Q_{n}
and the external control process, to drift away to a possibly worse policy. Balancing rapid exploitation, sustained exploration, and controlled policy drift is a key challenge in the discounted regret analysis.

4
Performance of Boltzmann and S
\epsilon
G Q-learning

We characterize the performance of Boltzmann Q-learning and S
\epsilon
G Q-learning through regret and sample complexity guarantees. The two algorithms share the same Q-learning update rule (
3
) and differ only in their exploration policies.

4.1
Boltzmann Q-Learning

Boltzmann Q-learning selects actions according to a softmax (Boltzmann) distribution over the current Q-values. At each time step, actions with higher estimated Q-values are selected with higher probability, while suboptimal actions continue to be explored depending on the temperature parameter. This exploration strategy provides a smooth tradeoff between exploration and exploitation and has been widely used in reinforcement learning due to its simplicity.

Let
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|}\mapsto\Delta(\mathcal{A})
denote the softmax function
(
37
)
. Then in Boltzmann Q-learning, the actions are sampled at time
n
according to the distribution
\sigma\left(Q_{n}(s_{n},\cdot)/\penalty\lambda_{n}\right)
, i.e., the action
a
is chosen at state
s_{n}
with probability proportional to
\exp\left(Q_{n}(s_{n},a)/\lambda_{n}\right)
. Here
\{\lambda_{n}\}
denotes the temperature sequence or schedule. We choose the temperature schedule to be of the form

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{b}\log(n+n_{0})},~\mathfrak{b}>0,n_{0},n\in\mathbb{N}.

This choice is tuned to optimize the regret bound in our analysis, and logarithmic temperature schedules of this form are known to be optimal in the stochastic multi-armed bandit setting; see, for example,
(
6
)
. The complete algorithm is presented in Algorithm
1
. The parameter
\mathfrak{b}
controls the exploration-exploitation tradeoff, with larger values leading to faster decay of the temperature and hence reduced exploration.

We present the sample complexity result next, followed by a discussion of its dependence on the hyperparameters.

Our regret guarantees depend on the suboptimality gap of the MDP, defined as

Formally, in S

5
Conclusion

In this work, we study classical, fully model-free Q-learning in the online setting and establish high-probability concentration and regret guarantees without relying on optimism, variance reduction, or model-based modifications. Our analysis covers Boltzmann and smoothened
\epsilon
-greedy exploration schemes and yields the first sublinear regret guarantees for unmodified online Q-learning and convergence of Q-values to
Q^{\star}
. A key technical contribution is a novel high-probability concentration result for SA with controlled, time-inhomogeneous Markov noise, which allows the mixing time to diverge and the contraction factor to approach one. This result recovers the best known finite-time guarantees for offline Q-learning as a special case and may be of independent interest in RL and optimization.

We highlight two future directions: first, it remains open whether the
\tilde{O}(N^{9/10})
regret guarantees obtained here can be improved to the
\tilde{O}(N^{2/3})
rates obtained for
\epsilon
-greedy exploration in bandits, or whether a fundamental separation exists. Second, it would be interesting to extend our framework to average-reward RL algorithms.

Lemma I.1
.

The difference between the action probabilities of
f^{(\epsilon,\lambda,Q)}
and the optimal policy

can be bounded as follows,

\displaystyle\|f^{(\epsilon,\lambda,Q)}-f^{(0,0,Q^{\star})}\|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\frac{\|Q-Q^{\star}\|}{\lambda}+|\mathcal{A}|e^{\left(-\frac{gap(\mathcal{M})}{\lambda}\right)},

\displaystyle\mbox{ where }(\epsilon,\lambda,Q)\in(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathcal{Q}.

Proof.

We have,

\displaystyle\|f^{(\epsilon,\lambda,Q)}-f^{(0,0,Q^{\star})}\|

\displaystyle=\|f^{(\epsilon,\lambda,Q)}-f^{(0,\lambda,Q)}+f^{(0,\lambda,Q)}-f^{(0,\lambda,Q^{\star})}+f^{(0,\lambda,Q^{\star})}-f^{(0,0,Q^{\star})}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|f^{(\epsilon,\lambda,Q)}-f^{(0,\lambda,Q)}\|+\|f^{(0,\lambda,Q)}-f^{(0,\lambda,Q^{\star})}\|+\|f^{(0,\lambda,Q^{\star})}-f^{(0,0,Q^{\star})}\|

(36)

where the second inequality follows since from Lemma
J.2

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(Q^{\star}(s,a)/\penalty\lambda\right)}{\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\exp\left(Q^{\star}(s,b)/\penalty\lambda\right)}
