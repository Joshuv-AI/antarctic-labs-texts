Atypical Lhermitte–Duclos Disease Presenting as a Malignant Cerebellar Tumor: NeuN-Positive Neuronal Differentiation as a Diagnostic Clue
Authors: Jignesh Joshi, Srikant Banumathy, Srikant Balasubramaniam, Sweety Shinde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lhermitte–Duclos disease (LDD), or dysplastic cerebellar gangliocytoma, is a rare benign cerebellar lesion that may present with atypical imaging and histopathological features, resulting in diagnostic uncertainty and potential overtreatment. We report a 32-year-old woman who presented with progressive headache, vomiting, vertigo, and right-sided cerebellar ataxia. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a large right cerebellar lesion extending into the vermis, middle cerebellar peduncle, and hemipons, with inhomogeneous contrast enhancement and diffusion restriction, closely mimicking a malignant posterior fossa tumor. The patient underwent suboccipital craniotomy and decompression. Histopathology demonstrated distorted cerebellar folia with depletion of the Purkinje cell layer and expansion of the granular layer by neurocytic cells, raising a diagnostic dilemma with medulloblastoma. Immunohistochemistry showed NeuN and synaptophysin positivity with a very low K i -67 index, supporting LDD. Postoperative imaging demonstrated subtotal resection, and the patient had an uneventful recovery. This case highlights the importance of recognizing atypical presentations of LDD and the critical role of immunohistochemistry in establishing the diagnosis.

Introduction

Lhermitte–Duclos disease (LDD), also known as dysplastic cerebellar gangliocytoma, is a rare, benign, and slowly progressive lesion of the cerebellum characterized by abnormal enlargement of cerebellar folia and dysplastic neuronal proliferation. It typically presents in young or middle-aged adults with features of raised intracranial pressure and cerebellar dysfunction. Although regarded as a hamartomatous process, LDD often poses a significant diagnostic challenge because its clinical and radiological appearance may overlap with true neoplastic conditions of the posterior fossa. Classically, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrates a laminated or “tiger-stripe” pattern on T2-weighted images with little or no contrast enhancement, allowing a confident preoperative diagnosis in many cases. 1 2

However, recent reports have highlighted that LDD can present with atypical imaging features such as patchy enhancement, diffusion restriction, and marked mass effect, closely simulating high-grade gliomas or embryonal tumors. 3 Furthermore, although LDD is well recognized in association with PTEN-related disorders, particularly Cowden syndrome, a substantial proportion of patients present with apparently sporadic disease, further increasing diagnostic uncertainty in routine neurosurgical practice. 4 5

Histopathological confirmation may also be challenging, especially in cases lacking conspicuous dysplastic ganglion cells, and immunohistochemistry is often required to support neuronal differentiation and exclude malignant entities. We report an adult patient with LDD showing atypical radiological and histopathological features, underscoring the importance of recognizing this rare but important diagnostic pitfall.

Case Report

A 32-year-old woman presented with a 3-month history of progressively worsening holocranial headache associated with recurrent vomiting, vertigo, and imbalance while walking, with a tendency to sway toward the right. There was no relevant past medical history and no family history suggestive of an inherited syndrome.

On examination, the patient was conscious, alert, and oriented. Cerebellar examination revealed a broad-based gait with right-sided ataxia. Right-sided finger–nose and heel–shin testing showed dysmetria, with dysdiadochokinesia of the right upper limb. Tandem gait was not possible. No nystagmus or speech abnormality was noted. Cranial nerve examination was unremarkable. Motor and sensory examination were normal. There were no cutaneous or mucocutaneous abnormalities. Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, and coagulation profile, were within normal limits. All other systemic investigations were normal.

MRI of the brain demonstrated a large, ill-defined, mildly expansile lesion predominantly involving the right cerebellar hemisphere with extension into the vermis, right middle cerebellar peduncle, and hemipons, reaching up to the pontomedullary junction. The lesion measured ∼65 × 62 × 50 mm. It appeared iso- to hypointense on T1-weighted images and heterogeneously hyperintense on T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences, with small internal cystic areas. Patchy areas of diffusion restriction were present. Post-contrast images showed irregular, inhomogeneous enhancement. There was significant mass effect with effacement of the posterior fossa cisterns and compression of the fourth ventricle, with minimal right tonsillar descent. Based on the imaging features, a neoplastic lesion such as a cerebellar glioma or an embryonal tumor was considered ( Fig. 1 ).

The patient underwent a midline suboccipital craniotomy. Following dural opening, the cerebellum appeared tense and swollen. After gentle cerebellar retraction, an ill-defined, pale gray-white lesion was encountered within the right cerebellar hemisphere. The lesion was soft to firm in consistency, mildly vascular, and poorly demarcated from the surrounding cerebellar parenchyma, with no clear capsular plane. The abnormal tissue was interdigitating with normal folial architecture, making identification of tumor margins difficult. Internal decompression was performed in a piecemeal manner. Due to the infiltrative appearance and lack of a safe cleavage plane, aggressive resection was avoided and decompression with representative tissue sampling was undertaken.

Histopathological examination showed cerebellar folia in continuity with the lesion, with marked depletion of the Purkinje cell layer and expansion of the granular layer by small-to-medium-sized oval to carrot-shaped cells with coarse chromatin and scant cytoplasm. Focal rosette-like structures were noted with adjacent apoptotic bodies. Some cells showed nuclear enlargement with fine chromatin suggestive of neurocytic differentiation. Thick-walled blood vessels were seen at the corticomedullary junction, and the medullary white matter appeared relatively hypercellular with oval to elongated cells. Based on these features, the principal histopathological differentials considered were involvement of cerebellar folia by medulloblastoma and dysplastic cerebellar gangliocytoma (LDD; Fig. 2 ).

Histopathology showing preserved cerebellar folial architecture with depletion of the Purkinje cell layer and expansion of the granular layer by dysplastic neurocytic cells, consistent with Lhermitte–Duclos disease.

Immunohistochemistry demonstrated diffuse nuclear positivity for NeuN and strong cytoplasmic positivity for synaptophysin in the expanded granular layer cells, confirming neuronal differentiation. Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP) highlighted only the background glial framework. The K i -67 proliferative index was very low (<1%), and the lesional cells were negative for OTX2 and LIN28A. Taken together, the immunoprofile supported the diagnosis of dysplastic cerebellar gangliocytoma and effectively excluded medulloblastoma. Genetic testing did not identify a pathogenic germline PTEN mutation, and in the absence of clinical stigmata of a PTEN-related syndrome, the lesion was considered to represent apparently sporadic LDD.

Postoperative contrast-enhanced MRI performed in the early postoperative period demonstrated residual nonenhancing and minimally enhancing abnormal cerebellar tissue along the right cerebellar hemisphere and vermis, consistent with subtotal resection, with adequate posterior fossa decompression and restoration of fourth ventricular patency ( Fig. 3 ). The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient showed progressive improvement in headache and gait imbalance and was discharged ambulant and neurologically stable.

Discussion

LDD is an uncommon cerebellar lesion characterized by dysplastic neuronal proliferation and abnormal enlargement of cerebellar folia. Although classically described as a hamartomatous lesion, its clinical and radiological presentation frequently overlaps with neoplastic posterior fossa lesions, leading to diagnostic and therapeutic uncertainty in neurosurgical practice. Recent neurosurgical case series have shown that most patients present with headache and cerebellar dysfunction secondary to posterior fossa mass effect, similar to the presentation observed in our patient. 1 2

The characteristic MRI appearance of LDD is the laminated or “tiger-stripe” pattern on T2-weighted images corresponding to thickened cerebellar folia, usually with little or no contrast enhancement. In typical cases, this pattern allows a confident preoperative diagnosis and may obviate the need for extensive resection. 1 2 However, a significant subset of patients demonstrates atypical radiological features, including irregular or patchy enhancement, diffusion restriction, cystic change, and pronounced mass effect, closely simulating malignant posterior fossa tumors. 3 In the present case, the presence of inhomogeneous enhancement, diffusion restriction, and extensive involvement of the cerebellar hemisphere, vermis, and brainstem structures led to a strong initial impression of a high-grade glioma or embryonal tumor.

An additional factor contributing to diagnostic difficulty is the variable association of LDD with PTEN-related syndromes. LDD is a recognized neurological manifestation of Cowden syndrome and related disorders; nevertheless, many patients present with apparently sporadic disease and lack overt mucocutaneous or systemic features. 4 5 McMahon and colleagues emphasized that the absence of clinical stigmata of Cowden syndrome should not exclude the diagnosis of LDD, particularly in adult patients presenting with atypical imaging findings. 4 Recent surgical case series have similarly reported a substantial proportion of sporadic cases without demonstrable syndromic association. 5

Histopathological confirmation of LDD can be challenging. Classical features include thickened cerebellar folia, depletion of the Purkinje cell layer, and expansion of the granular layer by dysplastic neurons with preservation of the overall folial architecture. However, in some cases, conspicuous dysplastic ganglion cells may be sparse or absent, creating overlap with other posterior fossa tumors, particularly medulloblastoma. 3 This difficulty was evident in our patient, in whom routine histology raised two principal differential diagnoses—medulloblastoma and dysplastic cerebellar gangliocytoma. In such situations, immunohistochemistry is crucial. Demonstration of neuronal differentiation with NeuN and synaptophysin, together with a very low proliferative index, supports the diagnosis of LDD and helps exclude embryonal tumors, which typically show high mitotic activity and elevated K i -67 indices.

Earlier neuroradiological and neuropathological reports have stressed that recognition of preserved folial architecture and neuronal immunophenotype is central to accurate diagnosis, particularly when imaging is atypical. 6 7 Carlson and colleagues reported similar diagnostic uncertainty in cases lacking classic imaging features, where correlation between radiology, histology, and immunohistochemistry was required to establish the correct diagnosis. 6 Otheman et al further demonstrated that LDD may present with nonclassical clinical manifestations and radiological appearances, reinforcing the importance of multidisciplinary assessment in equivocal cases. 7

Surgical management of LDD is primarily directed toward relieving mass effect rather than achieving complete tumor clearance. Most contemporary neurosurgical series recommend decompression or subtotal resection because of the ill-defined margins of the lesion and its intimate interdigitation with normal cerebellar folia. 1 2 5 Attempts at radical excision carry a significant risk of neurological morbidity, as a safe cleavage plane is usually absent. Our intraoperative findings of poorly demarcated, folia-based abnormal tissue closely reflect those described in previous surgical series. 1 5 8 Importantly, subtotal resection is generally associated with favorable functional outcomes and low recurrence rates.

Long-term prognosis for patients with LDD is usually excellent. Recurrence is uncommon and malignant transformation has not been convincingly documented. 8 9 10 Alawaji and Aweja, in a recent pediatric case, highlighted that even in younger patients, limited surgical decompression followed by radiological surveillance is sufficient in most cases. 9 Earlier neuropathological studies have similarly demonstrated the indolent biological behavior of LDD, supporting conservative surgical strategies whenever feasible. 10

This case highlights several important teaching points. LDD should be considered in the differential diagnosis of large cerebellar masses even when classical radiological features are absent. Histopathological overlap with medulloblastoma may occur, necessitating immunohistochemical confirmation. Awareness of this diagnostic pitfall is essential to avoid unnecessary aggressive resection in a lesion with a generally benign clinical course.

Conclusion

LDD remains a rare but important diagnostic consideration in patients presenting with large cerebellar masses. This case highlights that LDD may present with atypical radiological features, including contrast enhancement and diffusion restriction, closely mimicking malignant posterior fossa tumors and leading to significant preoperative diagnostic uncertainty. Furthermore, histopathological overlap with embryonal tumors, particularly medulloblastoma, may occur when classical dysplastic ganglion cells are inconspicuous. In such circumstances, immunohistochemical confirmation of neuronal differentiation with a low proliferative index is essential for establishing the correct diagnosis. Awareness of these atypical presentations is crucial for neurosurgeons, radiologists, and neuropathologists to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary aggressive resection, and to guide an appropriate, function-preserving surgical strategy for this benign and indolent lesion.
