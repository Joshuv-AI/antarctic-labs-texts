Geodesics and Shadows in the Kerr–Bertotti–Robinson Black Hole Spacetime
Xinyu Wang 1,2 , Yehui Hou 3 , Xi Wan 4 , Minyong Guo 1,2∗ , Bin Chen 4,5

Abstract
In this work, we investigate geodesics and black hole shadows in the Kerr–Bertotti–Robinson spacetime. We show that the equations of motion for null geodesics are separable and admit analytical treatment, whereas timelike geodesics are generally non-separable. Approximate analytical expressions for the photon sphere and the innermost stable circular orbit are derived via perturbative expansions in the magnetic field strength. We further explore the black hole shadow using both numerical and analytical methods, examining the effects of the magnetic field, the observer’s inclination angle and radial position. Deviations from the standard Kerr shadow are quantified, and a physical interpretation is provided by introducing asymptotic regimes defined relative to the magnetic field strength.

When the magnetic field is relatively weak, its backreaction on the spacetime geometry can be safely neglected. In this regime, Wald identified a solution to the source-free Maxwell equations that describes a weak, uniform magnetic field in the Kerr spacetime

More recently, Podolský and Ovcharenko introduced a new class of exact solutions describing a Kerr black hole embedded in the Bertotti–Robinson (BR) universe, permeated by a uniform Maxwell field

These properties make KBR black holes a more physically realistic model of a black hole immersed in an external electromagnetic field.

To quantify deviations from the Kerr shadow, we introduce a deformation parameter and analyze its dependence on the magnetic field strength, the observer’s inclination angle, and radial position.

Geodesics of the KBR Exterior

In this section, we examine the geodesic motion in the KBR spacetime.
The general Hamilton–Jacobi equation takes the form
[
63
,
64
,
65
]

\frac{\partial S}{\partial\lambda}+H=0,

(3.1)

where
S
is the Hamilton principal function, and
\lambda
denotes the affine parameter. For a freely moving particle subject to no external forces, the Hamiltonian
H
is given by

H=\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\frac{\partial S}{\partial x^{\mu}}\frac{\partial S}{\partial x^{\nu}}=-\frac{1}{2}\mu^{2}\,,

(3.2)

where
\mu=1
for massive particles and
\mu=0
for massless particles.
The four-momentum of the particle is defined by
p_{\mu}=\partial S/\partial x^{\mu}
. In a stationary and axisymmetric spacetime, the existence of two Killing vectors leads to the conservation of energy and angular momentum, expressed as
\mathcal{E}=-p_{t}
,
\mathcal{L}=p_{\phi}
, respectively.
The Hamiltonian can be rewritten in the form
2H=\mathcal{C}+g^{rr}p_{r}+g^{\theta\theta}p_{\theta}
, where the function
\mathcal{C}
is defined as
\mathcal{C}=g^{tt}\mathcal{E}^{2}-2g^{t\phi}\mathcal{E}\mathcal{L}+g^{\phi\phi}\mathcal{L}^{2}
.
Substituting the specific form of the KBR metric from Eq. (
2.10
) into
\mathcal{C}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{C}}{\Omega^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{C^{2}Qa\sin^{2}{\theta}\mathcal{E}^{2}+Q\csc^{2}{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{2}-Pa^{2}\mathcal{L}^{2}-C^{2}P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r^{2}+a^{2}}}\right)^{2}-2aCQ\mathcal{E}\mathcal{L}+2aCP\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r^{2}+a^{2}}}\right)\mathcal{E}\mathcal{L}}{C^{2}PQ\Sigma}

(3.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r^{2}+a^{2}}}\right)\mathcal{E}-a\mathcal{L}}}\right]^{2}}{C^{2}Q\Sigma}+\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Ca\sin^{2}{\theta}\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{L}}}\right)^{2}}{C^{2}P\Sigma\sin^{2}{\theta}}\,.

This leads to the Hamilton–Jacobi equation in the following form:

\frac{1}{2}\mu^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\Sigma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial r}}}\right)^{2}-\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(a^{2}+r^{2})\mathcal{E}-aC^{-1}\mathcal{L}}}\right]^{2}}{Q}+P\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial\theta}}}\right)^{2}+\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a\sin^{2}{\theta}\,\mathcal{E}-C^{-1}\mathcal{L}}}\right]^{2}}{P\sin^{2}{\theta}}}}\right]=0.

(3.4)

Although the terms within the square brackets are separable in
r
and
\theta
, the prefactor
\Sigma^{-1}\Omega^{2}
, which depends on both variables, generally prevents full separation of variables in the Hamilton–Jacobi equation for timelike particles (
\mu\neq 0
). Consequently, the geodesic equations are, in general, not separable for massive particles. However, for null geodesics

\mu=0

S=S_{r}(r)+S_{\theta}(\theta)-\mathcal{E}t+\mathcal{L}\phi
. This separability arises from the fact that the conformal factor does not affect the trajectories of null geodesics.
Finally, we note that the parameter

can be absorbed into the angular momentum by the rescaling

in Eq. (

). Without loss of generality, we set
C=1
in the remainder of this analysis.

3.1
Null geodesics and photon region

In this subsection, we focus on null geodesics. In this case, the radial and angular components of the four-momentum are given by
p_{r}=S_{r}(r)
and
p_{\theta}=S_{\theta}(\theta)
, respectively. Setting
\mu=0
in Eq. (
3.4
), the equations of motion in the

and
\theta

Qp_{r}^{2}-\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(a^{2}+r^{2})\mathcal{E}-a\mathcal{L}}}\right]^{2}}{Q}=-\kappa,\quad Pp_{\theta}^{2}+\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\sin^{2}{\theta}\,\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{L}}}\right)^{2}}{P\sin^{2}{\theta}}=\kappa,

(3.5)

where
\kappa
is a separation constant arising from the separability of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation. This constant is independent of the particle’s mass, energy, or angular momentum. It is convenient to define a shifted conserved quantity,

\mathcal{Q}=\kappa-(\mathcal{L}-a\mathcal{E})^{2},

(3.6)

which allows the equations of motion to be recast in the form

Q^{2}p_{r}^{2}=R(r),\quad P^{2}p_{\theta}^{2}=\Theta(\theta),

(3.7)

where the functions
R
and
\Theta
represent the effective radial and angular potentials, respectively:

\displaystyle R(r)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(a^{2}+r^{2})\mathcal{E}-a\mathcal{L}}}\right]^{2}-\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{Q}+(\mathcal{L}-a\mathcal{E})^{2}}}\right]-B^{2}r^{2}\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{Q}+(\mathcal{L}-a\mathcal{E})^{2}}}\right]\,,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\Theta(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{L}^{2}\csc^{2}\theta-a^{2}\mathcal{E}^{2}}}\right)\cos^{2}\theta+B^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{I_{2}}{I_{1}^{2}}-a^{2}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(\mathcal{L}-a\mathcal{E})^{2}+\mathcal{Q}}}\right]\cos^{2}\theta\,.

The full set of equations governing null geodesics is then given by

\displaystyle\frac{\Sigma}{\Omega^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2}+a^{2}}{Q}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{E}(r^{2}+a^{2})-a\mathcal{L}}}\right]+\frac{a}{P}(\mathcal{L}-a\mathcal{E}\sin^{2}\theta),

(3.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\Sigma}{\Omega^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}

\displaystyle=\pm_{r}\sqrt{R(r)},

\displaystyle\frac{\Sigma}{\Omega^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}

\displaystyle=\pm_{\theta}\sqrt{\Theta(\theta)},

\displaystyle\frac{\Sigma}{\Omega^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{Q}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{E}(r^{2}+a^{2})-a\mathcal{L}}}\right]+\frac{\mathcal{L}}{P\sin^{2}\theta}-\frac{a\mathcal{E}}{P}.

These equations reduce to those of the Kerr spacetime in the limit

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a^{2}-\lambda^{2}-\eta-B^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a^{2}-\frac{I_{2}}{I_{1}^{2}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(a-\lambda)^{2}+\eta}}\right]}}\right]\,,

(3.12)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a^{2}-B^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a^{2}-\frac{I_{2}}{I_{1}^{2}}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(a-\lambda)^{2}+\eta}}\right]}}\right]\,,\quad u_{\pm}=\frac{A_{1}\pm\sqrt{A_{1}^{2}+4\eta}}{2A_{2}}\,.

For small values of

The radial potential

Combining this condition with Eq.(

(3.17)

represents the first-order correction to the photon region due to the magnetic field in the KBR spacetime.

3.2
Circular timelike geodesics and the ISCO

In general, the equations of motion for timelike particles are not separable in the same manner as those for null geodesics. In this subsection, we briefly analyze the circular motion of timelike geodesics confined to the equatorial plane. Within this plane,
\theta=\pi/2
and
p_{\theta}=0
, which simplifies the equations of motion. Under these conditions, the radial equation of motion can be recast in the form

p_{r}^{2}+V(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L},r)=0
,
where the effective potential
V
is given by

V(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L},r)=\frac{r^{2}}{Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+B^{2}r^{2}}}\right)}-\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(a^{2}+r^{2})\mathcal{E}-a\mathcal{L}}}\right]^{2}}{Q^{2}}+\frac{(a\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{L})^{2}}{Q}\,.

(3.18)

Circular orbits correspond to extrema of the effective potential and are determined by the conditions

V=\partial_{r}V=0
, which constrain the energy
\mathcal{E}
and angular momentum
\mathcal{L}
in terms of the orbital radius.
The angular velocity
\Omega_{c}
of a particle in a circular orbit can be obtained from the condition
\partial_{r}g_{tt}+2\partial_{r}g_{t\phi}\Omega_{c}+\partial_{r}g_{\phi\phi}\Omega_{c}^{2}=0
. For a weak magnetic field
B\ll 1
, the angular velocity can be expanded as

\Omega_{c}=\frac{1}{a\pm r^{3/2}}+B^{2}\Omega_{(B)}\,,

(3.19)

where the first term corresponds to the Kerr angular velocity, and the second term
\Omega_{(B)}
represents the leading-order correction due to the magnetic field:

\Omega_{(B)}=\frac{-4a^{5}+2a^{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r^{3}+2}}\right)-4ar^{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+2r^{2}+r^{3}}}\right)\pm 3a^{4}r^{3/2}\pm 4r^{9/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r^{2}+1}}\right)\pm a^{2}r^{3/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(3r^{3}+4r^{2}-4}}\right)}{4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r^{3}-a^{2}}}\right)^{2}}\,.

V=0,\quad\partial_{r}V=0,\quad\partial_{r}^{2}V=0\,.

(3.21)

\displaystyle Z_{1}

\displaystyle=1+(1-a^{2})^{1/3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(1+a)^{1/3}+(1-a)^{1/3}}}\right],

(3.22)

\displaystyle Z_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{3a^{2}+Z_{1}^{2}},

\displaystyle r_{0}

In this section, we explore the properties of the shadow cast by the KBR black hole. Firstly, it is essential to construct the observer’s local orthonormal frame. Here, we adopt the widely used Zero Angular Momentum Observer (ZAMO) frame
[

,
70

\displaystyle\hat{e}_{(0)}=\zeta\,\partial_{t}+\Gamma\,\partial_{\phi},\quad\hat{e}_{(1)}=-\frac{\partial_{r}}{\sqrt{g_{rr}}},\quad\hat{e}_{(2)}=\frac{\partial_{\theta}}{\sqrt{g_{\theta\theta}}},\quad\hat{e}_{(3)}=-\frac{\partial_{\phi}}{\sqrt{g_{\phi\phi}}},

(4.1)

where

\begin{split}\zeta=\frac{g_{\phi\phi}}{\sqrt{g_{\phi\phi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g_{\phi t}^{2}-g_{\phi\phi}g_{tt}}}\right)}},\qquad\Gamma=\frac{-g_{\phi t}}{\sqrt{g_{\phi\phi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g_{\phi t}^{2}-g_{\phi\phi}g_{tt}}}\right)}}.\end{split}

(4.2)

The photon’s four-momentum in the ZAMO frame is expressed as
p_{(a)}=p_{\nu}e_{(a)}^{\nu}
.
To facilitate the reconstruction of the black hole shadow on the observational screen, we employ the stereographic projection method as outlined in
[

]
. The celestial coordinates
\Theta
and

\displaystyle\cos\Theta=\frac{p^{(1)}}{p^{(0)}},\quad\tan\Psi=\frac{p^{(3)}}{p^{(2)}}\,.

(4.3)

It is worth noting that, under the definition, the range of
\Psi
spans from
0

\pi

(x,y)
on the image plane are then obtained via the stereographic projection formulas:

\displaystyle x=-2\tan\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Theta}{2}}}\right)\sin\Psi,\quad y=-2\tan\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\Theta}{2}}}\right)\cos\Psi\,.

(4.4)

4.3
Derivations from Kerr

In this subsection, we proceed to study the derivation of critical curves in KBR spacetime, comparing them with those of the Kerr black hole. To provide a quantitative characterization of this discrepancy, and in accordance with our earlier work
[

]
, we introduce the coordinates
(x_{c},y_{c})
on the observation screen, defined as

where
x_{\min}

x_{\max}
,
y_{\min}
, and
y_{\max}
denote the minimal and maximal horizontal and vertical coordinates of the critical curve, respectively. These coordinates correspond to the geometric centre of the projected region. Given that for the same pair
(\xi,\eta)

to assume either a positive or negative sign—as allowed by Eq. (
3.9

y_{\min}+y_{\max}=0=y_{c}

5
Summary

In this work, we investigated the geodesics and the shadow cast by a black hole in the spacetime described by a newly proposed solution
[
10
]
for a Kerr black hole immersed in a uniform magnetic field.
Through the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, we found that for null geodesics, the method of separation of variables can be employed to derive an analytic expression for the four-momentum. Subsequently, we analysed and discussed the radial and angular potentials governing null geodesics. In the regime of a small magnetic field
B
, we obtained an approximate analytic expression for the photon ring. In contrast, for timelike geodesics, the separation of variables proves inapplicable; thus, we focused on equatorial motion. Under the assumption of a weak magnetic field, we derived approximate expressions for the angular velocity of circular orbits and the ISCO radius.

Subsequently, we conducted a systematic investigation into the shadow structure of the KBR black hole. By employing both numerical and analytical methods, we performed a cross-validation of our results and found them to be consistent. We not only carried out a qualitative analysis of how the magnetic field strength, observer distance, inclination angle, and black hole spin influence the KBR black hole shadow, but also provided a quantitative assessment of the deviations between the KBR and Kerr shadows as these parameters vary.

We observed that as the observation angle approaches
90^{\circ}
, the degree of deviation becomes more pronounced. While variations in the observer’s distance and magnetic field strength generally lead to increased deviations, certain subtle distinctions arise when the magnetic field is relatively weak or the observer is positioned closer to the black hole. By introducing a characteristic radius and defining the asymptotically defined near zone,
r_{+}\leq r\ll R_{B}
, and the far zone,
r\gg R_{B}
, we found that the spacetime within the near zone is approximately asymptotically flat, thereby yielding shadow structures closely resembling those of Kerr black holes. In contrast, the far zone exhibits an asymptotically AdS-like structure induced by the magnetic field, resulting in more significant deviations. This classification offers a self-consistent explanation for the observed differences.

We conclude this paper with several prospects for future exploration. Firstly, upcoming studies could further investigate the images of KBR black holes with accretion disks serving as the light source
[
20
,
75
]
, focusing on how the inner shadow structure diverges from that of the Kerr case. Secondly, given that the KBR spacetime self-consistently incorporates a magnetic field, it presents a natural avenue for exploring polarization images induced by the magnetic field, thereby deepening our understanding of such phenomena. Moreover, drawing comparisons between the findings in the KBR spacetime and those in the Kerr–Melvin spacetime would be a highly worthwhile endeavor.
