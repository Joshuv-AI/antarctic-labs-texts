Three decades of transformation: a bibliometric study of gender and geographic trends in international orthopaedic journal publications
Authors: Per Jolbäck, Antonia F Chen, Chloe E H Scott, Sebastian Mukka, Maziar Mohaddes, Anne Garland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAcademic journals are essential for advancing knowledge, yet gender disparities in orthopaedic authorship persist. While most previous studies focus authorship trends on mainly North American journals, this study analyzes gender distribution among primary and senior authors, along with the geographical origins of articles, in three orthopaedic journals based in Europe and North America.MethodsA retrospective, cross-sectional analysis was conducted on articles published from 2000 to 2004, 2010 to 2014, and 2020 to 2024 in the following journals: Acta Orthopaedica (AO), The Bone & Joint Journal (BJJ), and Clinical Orthopaedics and Related Research (CORR). The gender of primary and senior authors, as well as the geographical and developmental status of their countries, were collected. The Namsor application, along with additional sources, was used to assign author gender. Country development status was determined by the Human Development Index.ResultsThe study analyzed 7,381 articles. Female authorship significantly increased over time in all journals analyzed. Female primary authorship rose from 238 (8.9%) in the first period to 495 (24.7%) in the third, and senior female authorship increased from 133 (5.1%) to 287 (14.3%). In AO, female primary authorship saw a 3.4-fold rise, while senior authorship increased 4.9-fold. For BJJ, female primary authorship rose 2.3-fold, while senior authorship increased 1.7-fold. In CORR, primary authorship grew 2.7-fold and senior authorship 3.6-fold. Geographically, Europe was the most prominent contributor to AO and BJJ, while North America led in CORR. Articles from developing countries showed small increases, with China and India being key contributors, especially in BJJ and CORR.ConclusionThe study highlights a notable rise in female authorship in three orthopaedic journals, based in Europe and North America. The increase in female authors is more closely associated with specific countries rather than entire continents. Despite low representation, articles from developing countries increased in CORR and BJJ. Introduction

The publication of academic journals plays a crucial role in advancing and refining evidence-based practice. It provides professionals with a platform to share exemplary practices and research findings. These scientific publications serve as invaluable reservoirs of knowledge and evidence for students, practitioners, and researchers. To fulfill these roles effectively, it is imperative that manuscripts submitted to academic journals are subjected to thorough scrutiny. This process evaluates their value, quality, methodological rigour, utility, and suitability for publication.

Traditionally, medicine has been a male-dominated field. However, in recent years, women have made important strides and now represent the majority of medical students.1-3 The increase in female medical students may also be reflected in a growing number of female orthopaedic surgeons. Female representation in orthopaedics varies considerably across countries.4 Women often encounter challenges such as gender bias, limited access to mentorship, work-life imbalance, and under-representation in leadership roles—factors that can hinder career advancement and contribute to higher attrition rates.5-7 However, the growing presence of women in the field is also evident in orthopaedic research, as the proportion of female primary authors has increased in publications in North American and UK-based orthopaedic journals.8-15 However, while female senior authorship has also increased, the growth is not as pronounced as that of female primary authorship.8 It is important to recognize that most prior studies were primarily centred on journals from the USA where, historically, the presence of female orthopaedic surgeons has been limited, potentially skewing the results. There is a gap in our understanding of authorship trends in academic journals originating from regions beyond North America. Furthermore, there is limited knowledge in the contribution of developing countries in orthopaedic journals. The purpose of this study was to elucidate trends in the gender distribution of primary and senior authors. We also describe the geography and development status of the countries of origin in publications from three orthopaedic journals based in Europe and North America.

Methods

A retrospective cross-sectional analysis was conducted using information from the public websites of three orthopaedic journals (Acta Orthopaedica (AO), The Bone & Joint Journal (BJJ), and Clinical Orthopaedics and Related Research (CORR)) to assess the gender of primary and senior authors, as well as the geographical origins and development status of the countries of origin.

The study origin was determined based on the primary author’s affiliation at the publication time of the article of interest. Authors were assigned first (primary) or last (senior) authorship based on the order of the complete author list for each publication. For studies with only one author, the author was classified as the primary author, while studies with no individual author (i.e. only a group listed as authors) were excluded from the analysis. The time periods used in the analysis were framed as 2000 to 2004 (first period), 2010 to 2014 (second period), and 2020 to 2024 (third period).

In BJJ articles, the first period included reviews of articles labelled with Hip, Knee, Spine, Trauma, Shoulder, Hand, Upper Limb, Arthroplasty, Children’s Orthopaedics, General Orthopaedics, or Research. During the second period, articles were reviewed if they were labelled with Hip, Knee, Spine, Foot & Ankle, Trauma, Shoulder, Upper Limb, Oncology, Arthroplasty, Children’s Orthopaedics, General Orthopaedics, or Research. In the third period, reviews focused on articles labelled with Hip, Knee, Spine, Foot & Ankle, Trauma, Shoulder and Elbow, Oncology, Arthroplasty, Children’s Orthopaedics, General Orthopaedics, or Research. In CORR, only original articles in Section II – Original Articles were reviewed in the first period, articles in the sections Clinical research, Survey (Systematic review) and Case reports were reviewed in the second period, and clinical research and basic research articles were reviewed in the third period.

Gender attribution (binary gender male/female) for primary and senior authors was based on their first and last names at the time for publications. The Namsor internet application (Namsor, France) was employed to determine authors’ genders, utilizing its algorithm to assess the likelihood of a name being associated with either gender (probability > 55%). In cases where the probability of gender identification (for unisex names) in the Namsor application fell between 45% and 55%, supplementary sources such as LinkedIn (USA), ResearchGate (Germany), hospital websites, or general internet searches were consulted to verify author gender.

Our definition of developed countries is based on the annual Human Development Index (HDI) created by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP).16,17 The HDI ranges from 0.000 (lowest development) to 1.000 (highest development). Using the 2022 HDI scores, we classified countries with scores between 0.800 and 1.000 as ‘developed’. Countries with scores below 0.800 were classified as ‘developing’.

Acta Orthopaedica

AO, formerly titled Acta Orthopaedica Scandinavica until 2004, was established in 1930 by Scandinavian orthopaedic surgeons and is currently owned by the Nordic Orthopaedic Federation (NOF). The journal publishes articles from around the globe, encompassing basic research and clinical studies in orthopaedics and related subdisciplines. Annually, AO receives approximately 700 manuscripts, with a publication acceptance rate of around 20%. Over the years for this study, the impact factor has ranged from 0.8 (in 2003) to 3.9 (in 2022). Since 2005, AO has been freely accessible online immediately after publication, and since 2022, the journal no longer produces printed editions. In 2018, an article publishing cost (APC) was introduced, applicable only to articles from non-NOF countries.18

The Bone & Joint Journal

BJJ, formerly known as The Journal of Bone & Joint Surgery (Br), was first published in 1948. Initially, it shared the title The Journal of Bone & Joint Surgery with the American volume (JBJS). In 2013, the British version was renamed The Bone & Joint Journal. Since its inception in 1949, it has been owned by The British Editorial Society of Bone & Joint Surgery. BJJ receives over 2,000 submissions each year and accepts approximately 250 for publication. Over the years for this study, its impact factor has varied, ranging from 1.5 in 2001 to 4.6 in 2022. There are no submission or publication fees to publish in BJJ, unless open access publication is selected by the authors.

Clinical Orthopaedics and Related Research

CORR, previously known as Clinical Orthopaedics until 1963, was founded in 1953 under the auspices of the Association of Bone and Joint Surgeons (ABJS). The journal contains three sections: Section I – Symposium, Section II – Original Articles, and Section III – Regular and Special Features. CORR selects less than 25% of unsolicited manuscripts for publication. Over the years for this study, its impact factor has spanned from 1.2 (in 2001) to 4.8 (in 2021).

Statistical analysis

The initial record of the reviewed articles was stored in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft, USA). Descriptive data were presented as proportions (%) with a 95% CI. Chi-squared tests compared categorical variables. The function Custom Tables and Layer Column Percent in SPSS v29 (IBM, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The threshold for statistical significance was established at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 7,381 articles (Figure 1) were analyzed: 2,660 from the first period, 2,714 from the second period, and 2,007 from the third period.

Flowchart for article inclusion.

Authors’ gender

In AO, female primary authorship saw a 3.4-fold increase between the first and third periods, rising from 9.6% (95% CI 7.4 to 12.2) in the first period to 21.8% (95% CI 18.6 to 25.4) in the second, and reaching 32.3% (95% CI 28.4 to 36.3) by the third. Senior authorship showed a 4.9-fold increase over the same timeframe, growing from 3.9% (95% CI 2.5 to 5.7) in the first period to 7.4% (95% CI 5.5 to 9.8) in the second, and further to 18.9% (95% CI 15.8 to 22.4) by the third period (Figure 2).

a) Proportion of female primary authors presented as percentage with 95% CIs. b) Proportion of female senior authors presented as percentage with 95% CIs.

For BJJ, there was a 2.3-fold increase in female primary authorship between the first and third periods, from 9.0% (95% CI 7.4 to 10.9) in the first period to 13.4% (95% CI 11.6 to 15.3) in the second, and to 21.1% (95% CI 18.5 to 23.8) in the third. Senior authorship rose more modestly, with a 1.7-fold increase over the same period, from 6.5% (95% CI 5.1 to 8.2) in the first period to 8.3% (95% CI 6.8 to 9.9) in the second, and 10.7% (95% CI 8.8 to 12.8) in the third period (Figure 2).

In CORR, female primary authorship increased 2.7-fold between the first and third periods, rising from 8.5% (95% CI 6.9 to 10.3) in the first period to 16.7% (95% CI 14.3 to 19.2) in the second, and 23.3% (95% CI 19.9 to 26.9) in the third. Senior authorship saw a 3.6-fold increase, from 4.4% (95% CI 3.3 to 5.7) in the first period to 7.2% (95% CI 5.6 to 9.0) in the second, and reaching 15.8% (95% CI 12.9 to 19.0) by the third period (Figure 2).

The increases in both primary and senior female authors were statistically significant across all three journals (p < 0.001). Overall, in the first period, 8.9% (95% CI 7.9 to 10.1) of primary authors were female, increasing to 24.7% (95% CI 22.8 to 26.6) by the third period—a 2.8-fold rise. Similarly, the proportion of senior female authors grew from 5.1% (95% CI 4.3 to 6.0) to 14.3% (95% CI 12.8 to 15.9) over the same timeframe, marking a 2.8-fold increase. These findings demonstrate a statistically significant rise in the representation of female authors, both primary and senior, over the decades (p < 0.001).

Continent origin

The most prominent continent of origin for articles published in AO across all three periods was Europe, accounting for 78.8% (95% CI 75.3 to 81.9) in the first period, which increased to 92.5% (95% CI 90.0 to 94.5) in the third period. Asia was the second most prominent continent of origin, with 14.8% (95% CI 12.2 to 17.9) in the first period, decreasing to 3.2% (95% CI 1.9 to 4.9) in the third period, yet still maintaining its position as the second most published continent in AO.

In BJJ, Europe was the most prominent continent of origin, accounting for 64.7% (95% CI 61.7 to 67.6) in the first period, with a corresponding figure of 60.4% (95% CI 57.2 to 63.5) in the third period. In the first period, Asia was the second most prominent continent, with 20.5% (95% CI 18.0 to 23.0), followed by North America at 10.2% (95% CI 8.5 to 12.2). However, by the third period, North America’s contribution increased to 17.1% (95% CI 14.8 to 19.7), almost matching that of Asia at 17.7% (95% CI 15.3 to 20.3).

In CORR, the most prominent continent of origin was North America, with the proportion of articles not exceeding 50% in any period: 45.8% (95% CI 42.8 to 48.8) in the first period, 46.9% (95% CI 43.7 to 50.2) in the second period, and 49.3% (95% CI 45.1 to 53.4) in the third period. Europe was the second most published continent in all periods, followed by Asia in third place (Table I).

Country development status

Most of the articles originated from developed countries. In AO, there was a non-statistically significant increase in articles from developing countries, rising from 0.8% (95% CI 0.3 to 1.8) in the first period to 1.7% (95% CI 0.8 to 3.1) in the third period. Similarly, BJJ saw a statistically significant increase in articles from developing countries, from 4.0% (95% CI 2.9 to 5.4) in the first period to 7.8% (95% CI 6.2 to 9.6) in the third period. In CORR, 1.3% (95% CI 0.8 to 2.1) of the articles were from developing countries in the first period, increasing to 10.6% (95% CI 8.2 to 13.3) in the third period (p < 0.001, Table III). Additional analysis of developing countries revealed that China and India were the two primary contributors, especially in BJJ and CORR (Supplementary Table v).

Discussion

Our study analyzed trends in gender distribution of primary and senior authors, as well as the geographical and developmental status of countries of origin, in three orthopaedic journals during the last three decades. We found a susbtantial increase in female primary and senior authors across all three journals, with AO showing the largest increase in female authorship, followed by CORR, and BJJ showing the smallest increase. The origins of published papers align with the journals’ geographical origins: AO and BJJ predominantly featured European contributions, while CORR primarily featured North American contributions. Additionally, BJJ and CORR had a higher proportion of publications from developing countries compared to AO, which has only a small portion of such contributions.

We selected three orthopaedic journals for our study based on their geographical diversity, contrasting with earlier studies on author gender trends that focused mostly on North American-based journals.8-14 Additionally, we chose general orthopaedic journals that publish articles across a broad range of orthopaedic topics, rather than those focused on subspecialties, to try and reduce the likelihood of bias. The journals’ lack of gender diversity may be a consequence of the subspecialties’ ability to attract female surgeons.19 To our knowledge, our study is the first to explore gender authorship in orthopaedic journals from outside North America or the UK.

The results in our study of an increased number of female first authors align with the results of previous orthopaedic studies originating mainly form North America.8-15 Despite the increased proportion of female authors, notable differences in proportions remain between journals. AO saw the largest increase in both primary and senior female authors, followed by CORR, while BJJ experienced the smallest increase. The greater increase in female authorship in AO may be attributed to the large number of articles from five European countries, all of which rank in the top ten on the 2022 Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) Gender Index.20 The increase in female authorship seems to be more concentrated in specific countries, where a significant proportion of articles are authored by women. This indicates that the overall rise in female representation may be driven by countries or regions with greater gender equity in academic publishing, rather than reflecting a uniform trend across all continents.

Addressing barriers within the orthopaedic field could involve implementing structured mentorship and sponsorship programmes, promoting equitable recruitment and promotion processes, and offering flexible training and work arrangements. Such strategies could support career development, strengthen representation in academic and leadership roles, and contribute to a more inclusive and effective workforce.

Our findings of a higher proportion of articles from developing countries in CORR and BJJ align with an earlier study.21 This may be partly explained by the considerable number of articles from China and India. AO has a limited number of publications from both of these countries. Nonetheless, it is not surprising that these two nations, the most populous countries in the world, are the primary contributing developing countries in our study. Differences in editorial policies between journals, as well as changes within a journal’s policies, may impact the number of articles from developing countries. Due to the design of this study, we cannot determine whether the low number of articles from these regions is a result of low submission rates or low acceptance rates. If major orthopaedic journals are committed to increasing publications from developing countries, analyzing the acceptance rate for manuscripts submitted from different countries or regions could identify areas for improvement.

The strength of our study lies in the selection of journals within a similar impact factor range, each with broad content coverage. Additionally, our inclusion period—focusing on the first five years of each decade—ensures robust analysis that is less susceptible to annual fluctuations. However, there are limitations to acknowledge. First, we determined author gender based on names rather than self-reported identification, which may lead to inaccuracies, and does not account for non-binary or gender non-conforming individuals, potentially skewing our data. There is a risk that the Namsor internet application may misclassify names and their associated genders, particularly when handling names from diverse cultural and linguistic backgrounds, due to variations in name structures. We have used the term ‘gender’ in our manuscript and associated names with gender, with the intention that to reflect gender identity rather than merely biological sex. This method does not adequately reflect the sociocultural complexity of gender, and could obscure the presence of diverse gender identities, thereby potentially introducing systematic bias into the analysis.

Second, our use of the HDI as a metric for development status may introduce bias into the results, and the developmental analysis should be interpreted with caution. China’s HDI score of 0.788 is just below the threshold separating ‘developed’ from ‘developing’ countries, while India’s HDI score of 0.644 is substantially lower. The HDI, which considers a broad range of factors including economic growth, life expectancy, health, education, and quality of life, was selected as the metric for assessing a country’s development status, as opposed to the World Bank’s classification based on nominal gross national income (GNI) per capita, where a GNI below USD $12,696 is classified as a developing country. These two systems are not always in sync; a country classified as developed by the HDI metric might be classified as developing by the World Bank’s metric. China has a GNI of $12,850 and is classified as a developed country by the World Bank, but not by the UNDP.16,17 Therefore, our results on articles from developing countries may be biased by the selection of metrics used. A further limitation is the absence of gender distribution data by level of evidence, which may obscure disparities linked to variations in study design and funding mechanisms. Lastly, the study is limited by the selection of only three journals. This narrow scope may restrict the generalizability of our findings, as the results may not fully represent the broader academic publishing landscape. Including a wider range of journals would offer a more comprehensive understanding and improve the applicability of our conclusions.

The study reveals an increase in female authorship across three orthopaedic journals from Europe and North America. This rise is more strongly linked to certain countries rather than broader continental trends. Although female representation remains low, the number of articles from developing nations has grown in CORR and BJJ. While advancements in gender diversity are encouraging, regional imbalances persist.
