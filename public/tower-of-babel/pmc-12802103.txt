Introduction

Orthopedic surgery, particularly hip and knee arthroplasty, is a demanding job requiring physical stamina, fine dexterity, and emotional resilience. Arthroplasty surgeons operate for hours in physically demanding positions, make high-stakes decisions on a daily basis, and must navigate growing administrative burdens. Performing hip and knee arthroplasty may seem routine; however, surgeons must meet high patient expectations and risk penalties for complications. While we strive to promote musculoskeletal health and optimize outcomes for our patients, little is done to protect our own well-being, which is reflected in increasing rates of burnout among orthopedic surgeons [1]. It’s time we prioritize surgeon health by evaluating physical, mental, and social strains and creating tools to promote and preserve surgeon wellness.

The physical toll

Performing hip and knee arthroplasty is inherently strenuous. Prolonged standing, repetitive movements, and heavy instruments contribute to musculoskeletal fatigue and high rates of work-related injuries in arthroplasty surgeons [[2], [3], [4]]. Studies involving wearable sensors have shown high energy expenditure and cardiorespiratory strain in surgeons performing primary and revision total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [[5], [6], [7]]. This physical workload is comparable to and at times exceeds that of manual laborers or athletes, yet is often performed without the same focus on training, recovery, and ergonomics. Compounding this challenge is the steady rise in surgical caseloads driven by increasing demand for hip and knee arthroplasty procedures, alongside a growing population of patients with higher body mass index (BMI), which adds significant physical strain to each case [8,9].

Training for physical performance

Performing hip and knee arthroplasty requires strength, endurance, flexibility, and fine motor control, all of which parallel the physical requirements of high-level athletic performance. Just as athletes engage in “sport-specific” training to prepare for the unique movements and stresses of their discipline, surgeons should consider targeted training to optimize posture, core stability, general strength, and stamina. Athletes rely on coaches and trainers to provide feedback, refine techniques, and develop individualized training plans. Surgeon-athletes should also utilize outside perspectives. Consider hiring a coach or personal trainer to optimize physical training. Film yourself operating and review videos, analyzing movement patterns, and identifying areas for improvement. Surgery is a team sport: delegate tasks, utilize assistants, and ask for feedback from the surgical team. You can enable anesthesiologists, circulating nurses, implant company representatives, or other team members to speak up when the table height needs to be adjusted or when they notice ergonomically unfavorable situations. Other strategies include utilizing wearable technologies that can monitor posture and fatigue. Orthopedic surgery departments and affiliated institutions can support surgeon performance through education, equipment, and cultural shifts for the entire team that promote optimal performance.

Mental health matters

Recent studies have reported burnout rates in orthopedic surgeons as high as 40-60%, with younger surgeons and those in high-volume practices particularly at risk [10]. Contributing factors include long hours, increasing administrative burdens, reductions in procedural reimbursement, rising case volume and complexity, and the emotional toll of complications or dissatisfied patients [1]. The pressure to meet increasing patient demands and expectations, along with the threat of malpractice claims when complications arise, adds to surgeons feeling the need to aim for an unachievable level of perfection. Arthroplasty surgeons are also tasked with promoting value-based care and have been challenged with a variety of pilot projects aimed at increasing value while decreasing costs. These projects require additional efforts to develop and implement in an already mentally demanding field [1]. When equating higher stressors plus lower reimbursement, we get surgeons with emotional exhaustion. Early-career surgeons experiencing burnout are likely to change jobs, which can have significant financial implications, may require moving, and is disruptive to providing continuity of care for patients [11]. Critically, this can also lead to depression and suicidality [12]. Further, orthopedic surgeons may fear negative stigma or have concerns about confidentiality and potential effects on licensure or privileging, which prevent them from seeking help or psychiatric care [13].

Promoting mental wellness

Unlike many other high-stress professions, orthopedic surgeons often operate without structured mental health support, performance coaching, or formal recovery time. Promoting mental wellness among arthroplasty surgeons requires a proactive, systematic approach that goes beyond individual resilience. One critical step is destigmatizing mental health concerns within our professional culture. This begins by normalizing conversations about stress, depression, and burnout. We need private and confidential spaces to discuss complications and challenges freely and without fear of professional repercussions. Other high-stress professions, such as the military, aviation, and athletics have implemented structured mental health protocols, peer support teams, and mandatory wellness assessments and check-ins, which aim to identify issues early and intervene before performance is affected. High-performing musicians and athletes also use performance coaches, therapists, and mental conditioning as standard practices. Adopting similar programs for surgeons can help build a culture where mental health is supported, protected, and prioritized. Find a colleague, mentor, or partner with whom you can be vulnerable and who can provide support when you encounter stressful situations. We must move beyond reactive wellness interventions and adopt proactive strategies that integrate mental health into our professional development. Just as we refine our surgical techniques and invest in technology to support improving outcomes for our patients, we need to invest similar resources on an institutional, professional, and individual basis to support our own well-being.

Social stressors

Surgical excellence should not come at the expense of family life, hobbies, or personal identity. As arthroplasty surgeons, the demands of our profession often extend well beyond the operating room, placing significant strain on our personal lives. Studies have found personal time outside of work to be critical in psychological wellness, yet long hours and unpredictable scheduling make this time difficult to protect [14]. Hazy digital boundaries make it difficult to truly disconnect, and the emotional intensity of surgical practice can make it hard to sustain meaningful relationships, be fully present outside of work, or engage in hobbies that bring balance and fulfillment. While it may seem beneficial to have EMR access on your phone, the constant barrage of text, chat, and e-mail notifications can make it impossible to have critical time off. There are also social strains on arthroplasty surgeons due to the shifting work culture. The traditional sense of camaraderie among surgical teams has eroded in some settings as hospitals increasingly rely on temporary or traveling staff. This leads to fragmented teams and diminished connection among colleagues, which can negatively impact patient outcomes as well as the surgeons who are used to functioning as team leaders and bear responsibility for these outcomes [15]. At the same time, arthroplasty surgeons may feel a loss of autonomy with decisions increasingly driven by administrators, insurance policies, and productivity metrics rather than surgeon clinical judgment [16]. As such, work-life imbalance, loss of autonomy, and decreased physician engagement are major contributors to burnout among orthopedic surgeons [17].

The social network: leadership, networking, and community building

It is important to set boundaries to protect our personal time and mental bandwidth. Establishing clear limits on digital engagement, prioritizing time off, and flexible scheduling are essential not only for our personal lives but for sustainable surgical careers. Creating space for restorative activities and hobbies benefits work performance and emotional balance. This may require financial planning and fiscal responsibility, especially in a climate of decreasing reimbursements and for early practice surgeons who may feel pressure to increase productivity to pay off student loans. However, rebuilding work culture and improving physician engagement can improve overall well-being and result in increased efficiencies that allow for more time to focus on life away from work. Engaging in leadership roles, serving on volunteer committees, and participating in national meetings and specialty societies can be deeply rewarding and help combat the isolation that sometimes accompanies surgical practice. Social networking is a powerful way for arthroplasty surgeons to build professional relationships, share knowledge, and foster a sense of community within our specialty. Platforms such as Instagram, Facebook, LinkedIn, and X (formerly Twitter) are valuable spaces for discussion, mentorship, and staying connected with peers across the globe. The idea of work-life balance might seem unrealistic for arthroplasty surgeons given the intensity of our profession, but we can be intentional about blending professional and personal priorities and should work to build habits and systems that protect against burnout and aim to maintain joy in our profession.

Conclusions

Surgeon wellness should not be considered separate from surgical success or patient outcomes, rather it is foundational to it. As arthroplasty surgeons, we dedicate our lives to improving others’ mobility and quality of life. However, we face extraordinary physical, mental, and social demands and we often neglect to apply the same principles of optimization and care to ourselves that we routinely provide to our patients. The physical demands of joint replacement surgery rival those of elite athletes, yet we rarely train, recover, or prepare with the same intentionality. Orthopedic surgeons have high rates of burnout and depression, which underscores the need for systematic cultural shifts that prioritize wellness, destigmatize mental health, and protect surgeon autonomy. To preserve the arthroplasty surgeon, we must embrace strategies drawn from other high-performance fields in ways that align with the realities of surgical practice. This will require a comprehensive and proactive cultural shift. Institutions must invest in resources and policies that support the whole surgeon, and we must prioritize our personal health and identity beyond the operating room. Surgeon preservation is not a luxury - it is a necessity.

It is crucial that as surgeons, we prioritize our own well-being so that we can better care for our patients and maintain a healthy work-life balance.
