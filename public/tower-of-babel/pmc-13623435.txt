Treatment adherence in acne vulgaris: systematic review and narrative synthesis
Authors: Miriam Santer, Emma Maund, Taeko Becque, Rosie Essery, John S Barbieri, Beth Stuart, Tracey H Sach, Fathema Miah, Matthew J Ridd, William Price, Joanna Pang, Sophie Dove, Irene Soulsby, Kim S Thomas, Kate Henaghan-Sykes, Mary Steele, Steph Easton, Sebastien Pollet, Angel Umensofor, Sinéad M Langan, Nick A Francis, Andrew R Thompson, Paul Little, Alison M Layton, Ingrid Muller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcne vulgaris is common and has a substantial impact on patients’ quality of life. Low adherence to acne treatments is a barrier to successful management.ObjectivesTo examine the prevalence of adherence to oral and topical treatments for acne, factors associated with adherence and reasons for nonadherence.MethodsWe included primary quantitative studies that investigated acne-related experiences and behaviours, including predictors, determinants and outcomes associated with treatment adherence. We searched the following databases from inception until 24 March 2025, with no language restrictions: MEDLINE, PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO and CINAHL. Quality was appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool. We present the findings related to observational studies, analysed using narrative synthesis.ResultsTwenty-six eligible papers reported studies in the Americas, Europe and Asia. Thirteen papers reported quantitative descriptive studies (8111 survey participants plus 2 studies that reported data from electronic health records) and 13 papers (12 studies, 37 884 participants) reported other designs such as analytic cross-sectional or cohort studies. Methods for evaluating adherence varied: 21 studies used self-report with differing questionnaire designs, 3 used prescription data to evaluate medication possession ratio, 3 assessed drug refills, 1 carried out database drug survival analysis and 1 used pill count or tube weighing. Four studies used more than one method. The prevalence of adherence estimates varied from 6% to 84%, with larger studies estimating 50–65% adherence. Adherence was lower among younger people, but there was inconsistent evidence for differences between men and women, or between topical and oral treatments. Reasons for nonadherence included cost, side effects, perceived treatment ineffectiveness or symptom resolution.ConclusionsImproving treatment adherence in acne remains a priority, particularly for younger people. Interventions addressing the management of side effects and time to effectiveness of treatments may be beneficial.

Acne vulgaris (or acne) is common, affecting 90% of teenagers at some point.1 In approximately 50% of people, acne persists for several years and in approximately 20% it leads to scarring.2,3 Acne can have a substantial impact on patients’ quality of life, including an increased risk of depression and suicidal thoughts.2 Antimicrobial resistance, resulting from long courses of oral antibiotics for acne, is a concern.4

There is good evidence for the effectiveness of topical treatments for acne, such as topical retinoids, benzoyl peroxide (BPO) or topical antibiotics (used alongside topical retinoid or BPO), and these are widely recommended as the first-line treatment for mild-to-moderate acne.5,6 However, for medication to work it must be taken or used regularly for several weeks.7 Nonadherence is a hidden problem in many conditions, with treatment underuse being a barrier to improved outcomes.8 Treatment nonadherence has been categorized into issues around ‘initiation’, ‘implementation’ and ‘discontinuation’.9 There is a common perception that there is a particular problem with low treatment adherence to topical treatments for acne,2 but, to date, there has been relatively little exploration of whether the main barriers to treatment adherence in patients with acne are around ‘initiation’, ‘implementation’ or ‘discontinuation’.

A 2014 systematic review on adherence to acne medications in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) found adherence rates ranging from 7% to 98%, but the authors commented on methodological weaknesses and the fact that estimates from trial participants are likely to differ from other populations.10 A recent narrative review of methods used to measure adherence to dermatological treatments in general found financial considerations to be the most frequently mentioned barrier.11 This review also concluded that, although topical treatments are generally thought to be associated with lower adherence across a range of conditions, the evidence for this is weak in acne.11

We initially aimed to carry out a systematic review to review and synthesize the quantitative literature investigating people’s acne-related experiences and behaviours, including treatment adherence, help-seeking and personal expenditure. However, the breadth of the evidence was such that this paper focuses on observational studies that report adherence to topical and oral treatments for acne, addressing the following questions:

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

The review protocol was registered with the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic reviews (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42022377900).

Search strategy and information sources

We searched the following databases from inception until 2 June 2023: MEDLINE (OvidSP), PubMed, Embase (OvidSP), PsycINFO (EBSCOhost) and CINAHL (EBSCOhost). The same searches were rerun on 24 March 2025 to update the review.

The MEDLINE search strategy (Appendix S1; see Supporting Information) was developed first and then reviewed by an experienced health librarian. This strategy was then adapted by one researcher for the remaining databases (see Appendix S1). Experts in the field were consulted to ask whether they were aware of any additional relevant studies.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened all titles and abstracts using the eligibility criteria stated below, with disagreements resolved through discussion and arbitration with a third reviewer if necessary. We obtained full articles where titles or abstracts met the inclusion criteria. One researcher independently assessed whether these full papers met the inclusion criteria, with a 40% sample of full-text papers screened by a second reviewer, as well as whenever the first researcher was uncertain. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion and arbitration with a third reviewer. See Appendix S2 (Supporting Information) for a list of studies excluded on the basis of full-text screening, along with reasons for exclusion.

Eligibility criteria

The planned systematic review included primary studies that used quantitative data collection or analysis methods and presenting quantitative data (descriptive and/or inferential statistics), either standalone or as a distinct part of a mixed-methods study investigating acne-related experiences and behaviours, including treatment adherence, help-seeking and personal expenditure. However, due to the large number of studies retrieved, and their clinical and methodological heterogeneity, this paper reports on primary studies that reported data on treatment adherence. Furthermore, given the previous systematic review of clinical trials,10 and likelihood of trial participants being less reflective of ‘real-life’ adherence, this paper focuses on ­non-RCTs only. Examples of nonrandomized studies include ­nonrandomized experimental studies, analytical cross-sectional ­studies, case–control studies, cohort studies, or before-and-after studies [following the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) algorithm for selecting study categories].12 A systematic review of RCTs of interventions to promote treatment adherence will be published separately. For information purposes, the retrieved RCTs are presented in Appendix S3 (see Supporting Information), and papers included for the systematic review of RCT evidence are listed in Appendix S4 (see Supporting Information).

Population

We included studies of people of all ages with acne, provided at least some participants were aged between 13 and 25 years (their data did not need to be reported separately), health practitioners treating acne and carers or parents of children with acne. We included observational studies concerning multiple skin conditions if data for acne alone were reported separately. We excluded general public samples where it was unclear what proportion of the sample had acne and where their data were not reported separately.

We excluded studies concerning only oral isotretinoin as a previous systematic review has shown good treatment adherence to oral isotretinoin,10 although we included studies where isotretinoin was one of several treatments. We excluded studies concerning the mental health impact of acne alone, unless this was related to treatment adherence. We excluded studies concerning acne scarring or postinflammatory hyperpigmentation as the treatments and related adherence issues differ substantially from the treatment of active acne. As described above, given the volume and heterogeneity of studies identified, this paper reports on studies that included data on adherence to topical and oral treatments for acne.

Language and publication types

There were no language restrictions. Articles written in a language other than English were translated into English. We excluded studies reported in conference abstracts only as scoping revealed that these were unlikely to contain the quantitative outcome-related data of interest. We excluded case studies.

Data extraction process

Data extraction was performed by one researcher into a standardized prepiloted form created in Microsoft Excel. Extracted data were checked by a second researcher.

Assessment of study quality

Each study was assessed by one researcher against the MMAT.12 All assessments were then checked by a second researcher. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion. No studies were excluded on the basis of quality criteria, but assessments were used to guide confidence in study findings.

How is treatment adherence for acne measured?

Twenty-one studies based their measure of adherence on self-report, three papers used medication possession ratio (the number of days for which medication was dispensed divided by the number of days for which medication was needed during the study period), three objectively assessed drug refills (repeat prescriptions), one assessed pill counts and tube weight, and one carried out a drug survival analysis based on database data. Four studies used more than one method of assessment15,19,37,40 and in some papers the description of how adherence was assessed was not clear (see Table 4).

Self-report

Assessment of acne treatment adherence using self-report included the use of validated scales (Morisky Medication Adherence Scale, which focuses on recent medication-taking; ‘implementation’ adherence),35 and some other bespoke questionnaires reporting current adherence (‘implementation’ adherence).25,26,30,31,34,38 Some focused on self-report of whether refills (or repeat prescriptions) had not been collected (‘discontinuation’ adherence),17 and some focused on a combination of implementation and discontinuation.22,28 One study focused on cost barriers to adherence.39 In some studies, little detail was given of the self-report questions.19,36 For several studies including self-­reported treatment adherence, this was not really their main focus but was assessed in relation to treatment preference or treatment satisfaction (see Table 4 for details).

Eight studies reported on versions of the ‘Élaboration d’un outil d’évaluation de l’observance des traitements médicamenteux’ (ECOB), developed with support from the pharmaceutical industry as a mini-questionnaire to assess adherence, with separate questions for oral vs. topical acne treatments.41 Good adherence is defined as answering all four questions of the ECOB as expected (i.e. if one or more questions differs from the expected answer then adherence is considered to be ‘poor’). Question 1 of the ECOB asks ‘Do you remember the name of the (last) drug(s) you took?’ Questions 2, 3 and 4 ask about tolerability, stopping the drug and whether the drug has been useful.41 It can be seen from some studies that used the ECOB that the answer that most frequently differs from expected is Question 1.23,24,29 However, some studies did not report the responses to individual items within the ECOB20,21,33 so this cannot always be assessed. Some authors21 stated that if participants recalled the colour of the treatment packaging then it was accepted as a correct answer to Question 1, whereas others did not mention this.

Medication possession ratio or ‘refills’

The medication possession ratio (MPR) (the ratio of the number of days for which medication was dispensed divided by the number of days for which medication was needed during the study period) assumes that, if a prescription was obtained, the drugs were taken (oral treatments) or applied (topical treatments). Clearly for topical treatments this can be a more problematic calculation due to variations in skin coverage (face only or face and torso). One study attempted to assess the approximate surface area of acne in relation to the weight of topical treatment used,37 whereas others did not include data on surface area.15,16 Similarly, these two related studies dichotomized the MPR to categorize patients as adherent (≥0.8) or nonadherent (<0.8), although it is not described how this cutoff was chosen.15,16 Others argue for viewing the MPR as a continuous measure of adherence to avoid arbitrarily characterizing participants as ‘compliers’ or ‘noncompliers’.37

Three papers assessed adherence based on the number of prescription ‘refills’ over a given time period.15,18,19 ‘Refills’ occur where the patient obtains additional prescribed medication for ongoing treatment without further consultation, otherwise known as ‘repeat prescription’ or ‘repeat dispensing’. A drug survival analysis in Belgium looked at how long patients remained on acne treatments by reviewing medical records.27

Objective assessment

One study carried out among 100 people with acne in a tertiary dermatology clinic used both objective assessment by pill count (oral medication) and tube weighing (topical treatments), as well as self-report with the ECOB questionnaire.40

What is the prevalence of treatment adherence in acne?

The estimated prevalence of acne treatment adherence varied widely from 6% to 84% (see Table 4). Primary nonadherence, or noninitiation of treatment, appears to be a common form of nonadherence, with one study finding that 27% of participants reported not filling all prescriptions prescribed at their previous clinical appointment for acne.17 Most other studies were less clear on whether adherence rates related to initiation, implementation or discontinuation.

The study reporting the lowest treatment adherence rates (6% adherence to oral acne medication and 17% to topical acne treatments) included only 96 young people aged 12–17 years with acne and was the only study to use the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (implementation), so comparisons are limited.35 Among studies reporting ECOB scores (implementation and discontinuation), reported adherence ranged from 15% to 83.9%. The study reporting the lowest overall treatment adherence (15%) was from Iran and the authors suggest this may have been related to greater reliance on traditional or alternative medicine in this population.32 Larger cross-sectional surveys that used the ECOB to look at oral and topical treatments found adherence to be around 50%,21 whereas in the context of a cohort study, treatment adherence was much higher at 83.9%.24 Few studies allow international comparisons other than the large study by Dréno et al., who estimated the risk of poor adherence as follows: Americas 43%, Asia 48% and Europe 58%.21

A drug survival analysis (discontinuation nonadherence) carried out in over 1000 patients in 1 hospital and 3 private practices in Belgium reported a median duration of all topical acne treatments of 2 months, with only 50% continuing treatment at 3 months.27 A large US database study showed that the mean number of acne-related drug refills annually was 2.07 (SEM 0.17), but in the absence of information on the volume of each prescription, these findings are difficult to interpret.18

For studies where treatment adherence was assessed using MPR, the prevalence of good treatment adherence was estimated at approximately 12% for studies where MPR was dichotomized to adherent (≥0.8) or nonadherent (<0.8).15,16 Looking at the reported mean MPR, rather than dichotomized outcome, this was 0.34 among US Medicaid enrollees, suggesting that most patients took their treatment less than half of the time.15 In a UK dermatology setting the mean MPR was found to be 0.65, suggesting that most patients took their treatment most of the time.37

A cross-sectional survey of college students in China asked, ‘What is the shortest duration for which you have continuously used prescribed topical medication?’ and found that 63% of those using acne treatments reported a period of <1 month, suggesting still greater rates of discontinuation, although the methodology and population differed from other studies.28

The one study that weighed topical treatment used at 12 weeks found adherence rate of 64.5% of expected for 5% BPO gel and 51.9% of expected for 0.05% tretinoin cream, based on estimating the acne surface area with one ‘hand area’ requiring half a fingertip unit, or 0.25 g, topical treatment: for oral antibiotics, pill counts showed 94.3% expected treatment use.40

Factors associated with treatment adherence in acne

Factors examined for possible associations with treatment adherence in acne are shown in Table 4. Many of the variables associated with treatment adherence are themselves correlated (e.g. acne severity with treatment type, or healthcare provider, health insurance with geography), so we therefore focused on studies reporting multivariate analyses.

Of the studies that assessed the association between age and treatment adherence, two large studies found that younger age (<15 years or <12 years) was associated with lower adherence.16,42 Others found no difference in adherence in relation to age, but these were studies with smaller or more select samples, or only included participants aged ≥18 years.17,23,25 Most studies found no difference in treatment adherence on the basis of gender when included as part of a multivariate analysis,16,17,21,23,25 with the exception of one smaller study where young men were found to be less adherent.30 Conversely, the study that addressed cost barriers to treatment adherence found these to be greater among individuals who were assigned female sex at birth.39

There were conflicting findings regarding whether treatment adherence is more likely with topical or oral acne treatments (oral acne treatments other than isotretinoin). In one large survey using the ECOB, adherence to topical treatments was found to be better than to oral treatments for people who had been prescribed both.21 A large US database study using MPR found good adherence to the contraceptive pill and topical retinoids but low adherence to oral antibiotics amongst Medicaid enrollees.15 In a study on the same database, but only including people aged <18 years, good adherence to topical retinoids was found.16 A large survey in Turkey also found no difference between self-reported adherence to topical treatments and oral antibiotics.25 One US study found that participants were less likely to report filling prescriptions for topical ­retinoids than other acne treatments.17 Interestingly, the one study that used both objective and subjective assessments of treatment adherence reported a discrepancy between adherence to oral acne treatments measured using an objective assessment (pill count) and self-report (ECOB).40 Adherence was better in the objective assessment and the authors attribute this to the wording of the ECOB: ‘Question 3 asks if the subject ever stopped taking topical drugs because they thought it would do more harm than good.’ Where participants missed topical medication this was not thought to be due to the harmful effects of treatment, whereas participants taking oral treatments would answer ‘yes’ to this question even if they had only missed 1 day in 12 weeks.40

Reasons given for nonadherence

Eight studies reported reasons that participants gave for non­adherence to acne treatments.25–28,33,34,36,37 Although the nature of the questions and the settings vary, forgetfulness, perceived ineffectiveness, side effects and treatment costs were commonly cited (see Table 4). One Turkish study (n = 500) reported that 40% of patients stopped because they observed rapid improvement.25 However, a larger Belgian study (n = 1029) found that only 9% of patients reported stopping due to controlled acne, with 52% citing perceived ineffectiveness and 9% citing side effects.27

Discussion

Estimates of treatment adherence in acne vary widely, with variation relating to study design and population, whether the focus was on initiation, implementation or discontinuation treatment non­adherence, which treatments were being assessed and how adherence was assessed. Contrary to popular belief, there is little ­evidence for lower adherence to topical treatments than oral treatments, although this may be related to the heterogeneity by which adherence is assessed, as the one study that used both objective measures and self-report found higher adherence to oral treatment by pill count but lower adherence when assessed using questionnaire.40 In common with findings from other reviews, younger age (<15 years) is associated with low treatment adherence for acne, but there is no consistent evidence for differences between men/boys and women/girls. Common reasons given for discontinuing treatment are cost issues, side effects, perceived ineffectiveness or, conversely, rapid improvement, highlighting one of the challenges of assessing treatment adherence for this condition, in that nonadherence can be related to resolution of acne.

The 2014 systematic review of RCTs on treatment adherence in acne found a similarly broad range of adherence prevalence estimates ranging from 7% to 98%, but commented on methodological weaknesses and that clinical trial participants are more likely to be adherent than other populations.10 They found that low adherence was more common among younger patients and no significant difference between self-reported adherence to topical vs. oral treatments.10 Our review goes further in systematically examining associations with nonadherence and using observational studies, where estimates of adherence are more likely to reflect everyday use. Our findings regarding nonadherence are in line with previous reviews of quantitative and qualitative literature.11,43 The review by Lo et al.11 highlights that access to treatments is an important factor in dermatological treatment adherence, which may account for some of the variations seen in this study. For instance, the high cost and insurance approval process for topical retinoids in the US contrasts with the UK, where prescriptions incur no cost for people on a low income and for most people aged <19 years, and where a cost is incurred, this is a fixed rate. Furthermore, the availability of treatments available over the counter without a prescription varies between countries.

More robust findings probably relate to the MPR taking the affected surface area into account, where adherence was estimated as 65%.37 Similarly, drug survival analysis, showing that 50% of patients continue to use treatments at 3 months, is not dissimilar.27 These findings reflect a large study of general practitioner prescribing for acne in the UK, which showed that after an initial prescription for acne medications, 60% of patients were prescribed no further acne medications.44

This was a rigorous review, taking a methodical approach to assessing treatment adherence and the first, to our knowledge, to review factors associated with nonadherence.

While some studies asked about acne treatments obtained without a prescription,17,31,38 most focused on prescribed treatments. This is a limitation of our study as it limits the population to people who have consulted rather than people who self-manage their acne. It is possible that adherence to treatments purchased without prescription may be different, although there are likely to be large international variations due to differences in the availability of acne treatments without prescription in different territories.

As with any systematic review, the conclusions are limited by the number and limitations of the papers identified. One limitation is the different approaches to assessing adherence identified in the papers in this review, illuminating different aspects of treatment adherence such as initiation, implementation and discontinuation,9 and measures based on ‘refills’ may reflect noninitiation while self-report may be more appropriate for assessing implementation. Many of the studies reported on could have reflected more explicitly on these categories, which are embedded within reporting guidelines for studies on medication adherence.45

Another limitation is that most of the studies in the review had links with pharmaceutical organizations, which may raise questions. Relying on recall of the name of a treatment when assessing adherence makes it difficult to draw conclusions from these studies using the ECOB, as this method of assessment may favour products with more prominent branding or easier names, rather than how regularly treatments are used.

While we initially aimed to carry out a mixed-methods study including all quantitative and qualitative studies on treatment adherence in acne, we have focused on observational quantitative data here, in order to allow a full exploration of the data. The qualitative evidence will be reported separately and will allow greater exploration of people’s perceptions and experiences around acne treatments. A systematic review of randomized trials will examine which approaches to enhancing adherence have or have not been shown to be effective.

Our findings highlight the need for future research to establish more standardized measures for self-reporting treatment adherence in acne and explore different aspects of treatment adherence such as initiation, implementation and discontinuation. Our findings also point to the clinical importance of addressing treatment adherence in acne, and that potential solutions should be aimed at mitigating against side effects, ensuring that people with acne understand that treatments take several weeks of regular use to take effect, offering tools to support adherence and offering proactive review.
