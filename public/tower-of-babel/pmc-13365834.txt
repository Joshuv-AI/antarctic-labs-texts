Comprehensive cross-cohort analysis reveals global gut microbiome signatures of celiac disease
Authors: Peter J Prendergast, Haig V Bishop, Craig W Herbold, Elena F Verdu, Renwick C J Dobson, Andrew S Day, Olivia J Ogilvie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCeliac disease affects ~1–2% of people and remains incurable, requiring lifelong dietary restriction. The gut microbiome is thought to contribute to the development and progression of celiac disease. However, findings across previous studies are fragmented, making it difficult to understand exactly how the gut microbiome is involved.MethodsWe integrate over 900 samples from global datasets spanning different disease stages (before onset, during active disease, and after treatment), body sites, and research methods. Datasets produced using both 16S rRNA gene sequencing and shotgun metagenomics profile the gut microbiome. Alpha and beta diversity analyses and differential abundance testing identify consistent changes in bacterial communities linked to celiac disease. Machine learning tests how well microbiome data predicts disease status.ResultsHere, we show that celiac disease is not marked by large changes in gut microbiome diversity. Instead, there are subtle, consistent changes in specific bacteria, including a reduction in beneficial butyrate producers (Faecalibacterium, Prevotella, Agathobacter, Gemmiger), changes in mucin-associated microbes (Akkermansia muciniphila), and an increase in potentially harmful bacteria (Helicobacter, Campylobacter, Haemophilus parainfluenzae). These changes are seen before and during active disease and persist on a gluten-free diet. Microbiome-based disease prediction is moderately accurate for active disease and weaker for prospective performance, likely constrained by training data.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that celiac disease is linked to specific changes in gut bacteria that are not fully resolved by diet alone. Future treatments may need to focus on restoring healthy gut bacteria, not just avoiding gluten, to better manage the disease.Subject terms: Microbiome, Metagenomics

Introduction

Celiac disease (CeD) is an immune-mediated disease affecting 1–2% of the world’s population1,2, with its prevalence rising in recent decades3 and 75–83% of cases remaining undiagnosed4,5. The disease is characterized by an immune response to dietary gluten, causing small intestinal inflammation and damage, along with a range of both intestinal and extra-intestinal chronic symptoms1,6. The only treatment is a strict gluten-free diet (GFD), though in up to 22–25% of cases this fails to ease symptoms, with 40% of these patients still having histological alterations7,8. Furthermore, strict GFD adherence is associated with a set of micronutrient deficiencies and downstream health issues9,10, highlighting the need for improved treatment. Novel pharmaceutical treatments are being explored; however, most candidates target downstream inflammation, rather than upstream triggers11–13.

CeD has a strong genetic component, requiring Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA)-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8 for development, yet these variants occur in 30–40% of the population14,15. Their presence, along with other genetic factors, is critical to CeD development, but fails to fully explain CeD onset, indicating a significant contribution from non-genetic and environmental factors16,17. The gut microbiome, a complex ecosystem dominated by microorganisms co-existing with their host and influencing digestion, metabolism, and immune modulation, is increasingly implicated in CeD18,19. Research into the gut microbiome of people with CeD has demonstrated a loss of protective taxa20–22, increases in putative pathobionts20–24, alterations in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) producing taxa25–27, and mechanistic interactions between gluten and bacteria28–30.

Microbiome community profiling, widely used to study the gut microbiome in CeD, enables the investigation of gut microbiome composition, but is undermined by inter-study variability. Inconsistencies between results are often found. For instance, early duodenal-biopsy studies report increased diversity and higher abundances of Bacteroides in Italian children with CeD31 and elevated Pseudomonadota (Proteobacteria) in Spanish children with active CeD24. However, subsequent cohorts from Finland, the Netherlands, and Spain found no significant differences in small-bowel mucosal communities between individuals with CeD and controls32–34. Some of this variation may be explained by factors that have been shown to shape microbiome composition, such as geography, age, sex, fiber intake, and medication usage35. Importantly, variability also arises from sampling location and sample type, for instance, in samples from the same patients, duodenal biopsies versus stool samples capture markedly different communities, and even within the duodenum, luminal versus mucosa-associated samples and different locations along the duodenum exhibit distinct microbial profiles36. Other variation originates from methodological differences, such as sequencing technique, primer selection, DNA extraction kit, and choice of data processing method, all of which are known to influence the results of these studies37–39. Without a cross-cohort analysis that captures the gut microbiome’s global diversity and mitigates methodological heterogeneity, the global CeD-specific microbial signal remains largely hidden behind this variation.

Arcila-Galvis et al.40 conducted a cross-cohort analysis focusing on CeD-related gut microbiome sequencing studies, including data from articles published between 2010 and 2019. Based on this data, they found that CeD was characterized by an expansion of Pseudomonadota (Proteobacteria) and an overall depletion of Bacillota (Firmicutes) in both stool and duodenal samples, as well as an increase of Actinomycetota (Actinobacteria) specific to the duodenum. Their study reported the collection of 190 samples from individuals with CeD, making it the largest cross-cohort analysis on this subject. However, due to unavailability at the time, their analysis lacked the broader sample diversity available today. The available samples from people with CeD have climbed sharply and persistently, with 1276 CeD samples accessible by July 2025. For the body sites of stool and duodenum combined, Arcila-Galvis et al.40 were able to include just four datasets containing samples from patients with CeD. Two of these datasets included individuals with significant complicating factors, including 12 people with CeD with hookworm infections41 and 30 individuals with non-celiac gluten sensitivity42, with no mention of their exclusion. Since 2019, the number of CeD-related gut microbiome sequencing studies has increased dramatically, presenting an unprecedented opportunity to identify global trends of the gut microbiome in CeD.

This study comprehensively curates all publicly available CeD gut microbiome datasets into the open Celiac Microbiome Repository and applies harmonized analysis to 963 stool and duodenal samples spanning prospective, active, and treated disease stages. Processing is standardized across studies, integrating evidence across 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene amplicon and shotgun metagenomic profiles. Random-effects meta-analysis and dataset-conditioned multivariate modeling resolves stage- and site-specific compositional shifts, isolating the study-invariant disease component. The generalizability of microbiome-based disease prediction is assessed using cross-cohort evaluations of machine learning. This integrated analysis defines the present-day limits of cross-cohort prediction based solely on gut microbiome composition and identifies reproducible, disease-specific microbial signatures. These findings clarify the role of the gut microbiome in CeD and provide a foundation for targeted therapeutic strategies, grounded in cross-cohort evidence.

Methods

Obtaining data

On 15th July 2025, online searches were made on NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) and Scopus to retrieve potentially eligible datasets. The search on SRA for BioProjects with keywords “celiac”, “celiac”, “gluten enteropathy” or “sprue” yielded 179 search results. The Scopus search for (“celiac”, “celiac”, “gluten enteropathy” or “sprue”) and (“metagenomic”, “microbiome”, “microbiota”, “16S”, “rRNA”, “shotgun”, “sequencing” or “metagenomics”) and limited to Articles only, resulted in 927 publications. To refine the search results, the eligibility criteria were defined as 16S rRNA and whole metagenomic sequencing datasets of the human gut microbiome described in peer-reviewed publications, which include in vivo samples of individuals who are diagnosed with or who will be diagnosed with CeD. From 1,106 search results, 58 eligible datasets were manually identified (Supplementary Fig. 2).

When available, raw sequencing data and metadata from public databases were retrieved, and in the case of incomplete data requests via email were made to corresponding authors up to three times at fortnightly intervals. Out of the 58 eligible datasets, 30 were excluded due to the unavailability of suitable raw data and metadata, with specific exclusion rationales (Supplementary Data 5). The resulting 28 datasets able to be included in the Celiac Microbiome Repository, along with their study descriptions, can be found in Supplementary Data 5. Sample metadata was compiled, capturing body site (stool, duodenum, stomach, saliva, or oropharynx), GFD, country of residence, and CeD diagnosis/prospective diagnosis. Where available, the sequencing technology, age, sex, and the DNA extraction kit and primers used in sample preparation were also collected.

Processing 16S rRNA gene and shotgun data

All raw sequencing data available on SRA were downloaded using SRA toolkit (2.11.3)43. FastQC (0.11.9)44 was used to determine the presence of any adapter sequences, and if required, Trimmomatic (0.39)45 or Cutadapt (5.1)46 was used to remove them. For each 16S rRNA gene amplicon (16S) dataset, DADA2 (1.36.0)47 was used to identify amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and their relative abundances in the samples. For each shotgun metagenomic (shotgun) sequencing dataset, host read removal was performed using Bowtie2 (2.4.4)48. Taxonomic profiles were produced by MetaPhlAn4 and converted to GTDB taxonomy using MetaPhlAn’s gb_to_gtdb_profile.py tool (4.2.2)49. The dataset-specific parameters used for trimming of reads, DADA2, Bowtie2 and MetaPhlAn4 are detailed in Supplementary Data 5.

Shotgun taxonomic relative-abundance profiles were converted to estimated integer counts using per-sample total read counts from the SRA metadata (Total Spots ×2 for paired-end runs, ×1 for single-end runs). For each sample, relative abundances were first expressed as proportions (restricted to species/strain clades and total sum scaling (TSS)-normalized), then deterministically allocated to counts via the largest-remainder method so that the summed counts matched the library size. Samples with zero species/strain mass were excluded. The resulting estimated count matrix, taxonomy table, and sample metadata were combined into a phyloseq object for each dataset. Both the deterministic estimated-count and the relative-abundance per-dataset phyloseq objects were then merged into a single composite object by sample grouping (stool active and stool treated). The only exception was the longitudinal SG_118_Leonard dataset26, which was retained as its own phyloseq object because it is the sole prospective shotgun stool dataset. Deterministic estimated counts were used for differential-abundance analyses, whereas MetaPhlAn relative abundances were used for all other analyses.

Sample selection for analysis

This work focused on only stool and duodenal samples sequenced by either shotgun sequencing or 16S rRNA sequencing that captured the V4 region. Some samples included significant factors, warranting exclusion from analysis, including a recent short-term gluten challenge, NCGS, possible CeD, a recent hookworm infection, and an autoimmune condition other than CeD. Similarly, all samples with less than 1000 reads remaining after the processing of raw 16S and shotgun data were excluded. A single Ion Torrent pyrosequencing dataset was omitted from analysis because platform-specific biases produced community profiles that diverged significantly from the Illumina cohorts, consistent with prior reports that premature truncation and high error rates distort community structure in Ion Torrent 16S data50. The control group in the 16S_1211_Milletich dataset51 (N = 1211, CeD=26) was randomly down-sampled to 26 samples, achieving a more suitable case-control balance. Duodenal samples from the SG_80_Mouzan dataset52 could not be used due to all having very high percentages of unclassified reads ( > 93%, median of 100%). Finally, datasets with an inadequate case-control balance (defined as at least seven cases and seven controls) were excluded from analysis, resulting in 963 samples (487 CeD) across 14 datasets26,42,51–62 to be used in analysis, consisting of four patient groups: stool prospective, stool active, stool treated, and duodenum active.

Dataset combination

For each 16S analysis group, ASV sequences were aligned to the SILVA (138.1)63 full-length 16S rRNA gene alignment using Mothur (1.47.0)64. The resulting alignment was truncated using a custom R script (‘truncate_asvs.R‘ on GitHub) at the consistent overlapping V4 region. The truncated V4 ASVs were then aggregated, summing the abundance values for identical sequences. V4 ASVs were filtered based on prevalence within each analysis group separately, requiring that each V4 ASV had a prevalence of ≥10% in at least one dataset. Filtering reduced the number of V4 ASVs within each group, from 2147 to 203 in stool prospective, 7890 to 509 in stool active, 4371 to 439 in stool treated, and 5469 to 457 in duodenum active. These filtered V4 ASVs are utilized by all analyses, except for alpha diversity, which uses the unfiltered V4 ASVs. The truncated ASVs were taxonomically classified by DADA2 using the SBDI Sativa curated 16S GTDB database (Release 226)65. Both a 10% and a 15% prevalence filter were applied to the shotgun data. 10% and 15% filtering reduced the number of shotgun features within each group, from 912 to 257/183 in stool prospective, 1706 to 598/437 in stool active, and 2453 to 741/599 in stool treated, respectively. The 10% filtered features were used for beta diversity, while the 15% prevalence features were used for differential abundance and unfiltered features were used for alpha diversity.

Alpha diversity and phylum composition

Alpha diversity and phylum-level composition metrics were calculated in R (4.5.1)66 using the phyloseq (1.52.0)67, microbiome (1.30.0)68, meta (8.1.0)69, and metafor (4.8.0)70 packages. Meta-analysis was performed separately for each dataset, using unfiltered V4 16S ASVs and unfiltered shotgun relative-abundance features for group-wise alpha-diversity analyses, and 10%-prevalence–filtered features for phylum-level metrics. Four alpha diversity metrics were calculated, including Shannon diversity, observed diversity, Simpson dominance, and rare abundance. Four phylum-level metrics were calculated, including Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Pseudomonadota abundance along with the Bacillota/Bacteroidota abundance ratio. Beta diversity

Beta diversity analysis was conducted in R (4.5.1)66 using the phyloseq (1.52.0)67, microbiome (1.30.0)68 and vegan (2.7.1)71 packages. Filtered V4 ASVs were used for 16S, and filtered relative abundance features for shotgun across all groups. For each dataset, and again after pooling each sample within each group, unconstrained β-diversity was conducted using two normalization approaches: TSS, centered-log-ratio (CLR), combined with three distance measures: Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (Bray), Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD), and Euclidean distance. The resulting four distance-normalization pairs (Bray-TSS, JSD-TSS, Euclidean-TSS, and Euclidean-CLR) were analyzed separately. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations was used to test the significance of CeD status and where samples were pooled dataset variation. R2 and p-values were extracted for each model and adjusted for multiple testing using Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Full results for each distance-normalization paring are available in Supplementary Data 1–4.

Constrained β-diversity was calculated using canonical analysis of principal coordinates (CAP) using the distance-normalization pair that maximized variation explained by CeD status in the unconstrained β-diversity analysis across all groups (JSD-TSS). The same CAP model was used for all groups:

Differential abundance analysis

Differential abundance analysis was conducted in R (4.5.1)66 at multiple taxonomic levels for both the 16S and shotgun metagenomic datasets. For the 16S data, analyses were performed at the ASV, genus, and species levels. For the shotgun data, analyses were performed at the genus and species levels. The analytical pipeline utilized phyloseq (1.52.0)67 for data handling, ANCOM-BC2 (2.10.1)72 for differential abundance testing, and metafor (4.8.0)70 for random-effects meta-analysis. The preprocessing described above ensured all samples had > 1000 reads, feature prevalence > 10% (16S) and >15% (shotgun) of retained samples and removed features with zero variance. For every individual dataset within each group, feature counts were analyzed using the model: CeD status [+ sex] as the fixed effect and log library size as the offset; the sex term was fitted only when metadata was available. Significance per feature within a dataset was defined as p < 0.05 and FDR-adjusted q < 0.05, with effect direction from bias-corrected log fold change (LogFC). Dataset-level LogFC estimates and standard errors for each feature were then combined across datasets using a REML, producing the pooled effect, its standard error, τ2, Cochran’s Q, and I2. Meta-analysis significance was denoted with q < 0.05 after Benjamini-Hochberg correction. To complement the pooled results approach and to utilize pooled sample power, all samples within a group were additionally analyzed in a pooled data ANCOM-BC2 run that used the same settings except included dataset id in the fixed-effects formula (along with CeD status), thereby adjusting for between-dataset effects in composition. For both the pooled results and pooled data models, features meeting p < 0.05, FDR-adjusted q < 0.05, and LogFC ± 0.50 were deemed differentially abundant; effect direction was taken from the corresponding model’s bias-corrected LogFC estimates.

Agreement analysis

The agreement of differentially abundant taxa between groups and across sequencing methods was performed using a pairwise concordance analysis at both the genus and species levels. Python (3.12.2)73 along with pandas (2.2.3)74, numpy (2.2.1)75, and scipy (1.16.1)76 were used for data analysis. Two metrics were applied: sign agreement, which is the proportion of shared significant taxa that exhibited the same direction of change, and Pearson’s correlation to quantify the linear relationship between the magnitudes of the effect sizes. This analysis used significantly differentially abundant taxa only, with effect size direction from its bias-corrected LogFC. For the within-group concordance (16S vs shotgun), Pearson’s correlation was applied. Both the sign agreement and Pearson’s correlation were applied across groups for both the 16S and shotgun data.

Machine learning

For each analysis group, machine learning models were trained to predict CeD status based on ASV relative abundances, excluding the shotgun data. Unfiltered ASV abundance values were transformed by TSS before being filtered by prevalence as described above. Three evaluation methods were used: 1) 10-fold stratified cross-validation (K-fold), 2) leave-one-dataset-out validation (LODO), and 3) cross-dataset validation (X-Set). Several hyperparameter configurations (Supplementary Data 6) were trialed for five models: Support Vector Machine (SVM), Logistic Regression (LR), Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Random Forest (RF). For each model, an exhaustive hyperparameter grid-search was conducted, recording mean areas under the curve (AUCs) from five randomized replicates of the given evaluation method for each hyperparameter configuration. For X-Set, the mean AUC across all dataset pairs is reported. Finally, all five models were retrained using their optimal hyperparameters, recording the best of the five models along with its mean AUC as final performances.

Variable definitions

The primary outcome is CeD status (case vs controls), as defined by the original studies. Secondary outcomes include gut microbiome diversity (alpha and beta diversity), differential abundance of microbial taxa, and machine learning classification performance. Main exposures are disease status (CeD vs controls), disease stage (prospective, active, treated), and body site (stool, duodenum). Predictors include microbial taxon abundances (ASVs, genera, species), sequencing method, and available metadata (age, sex). Age and sex (where available) were considered as potential confounders and included as covariates. Dataset was included as the main conditioning factor to account for study-level effects. Country was not used for most comparisons as most datasets originated from unique countries, making it effectively synonymous with dataset adjustments. Sequencing method was not treated as a confounder as analyses were stratified by sequencing method. Disease stage, body site, and sequencing method are considered effect modifiers, analyses are stratified accordingly. CeD cases and controls diagnostic criteria were defined by the inclusion/exclusion criteria in their original study (Supplementary Data 5). In addition, samples with significant confounding factors were excluded according to the study-specific criteria in the Sample Selection section above.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using standardized and widely accepted methods, as detailed in the relevant sections above. Meta-analyses, linear modeling, differential abundance testing, and multivariate analyses were performed using R, with multiple testing correction applied where appropriate. Agreement analysis was performed using Python. Machine learning models were evaluated using cross-validation and cross-cohort validation approaches. Sample sizes for each group are provided in the Results, with specific dataset sample contributions in Supplementary Data 5. Individual samples are defined by unique sample identifiers provided in each individual dataset. Samples collected from the same individual at different time points or under different conditions are treated as independent biological replicates, consistent with the definitions used in the original studies. All analyses use these as independent biological replicates. No technical replicates are included. Data processing and analysis pipelines were harmonized across datasets to ensure reproducibility, and all code and processed data are available in the Celiac Microbiome Repository.

Results

Repository overview and analysis cohort

Of the 58 eligible datasets, a total of 28 datasets from 13 different countries and five different body sites were successfully retrieved, processed, and included in version 1.0 of the Celiac Microbiome Repository (Supplementary Data Fig. 1). The data included a total of 3,245 samples, 1,140 being from individuals with CeD, a figure that has steadily increased since 2014 (Supplementary Data Fig. 1a). Individuals on a GFD contributed 513 samples, comprising 458 people with CeD and 55 healthy individuals (Supplementary Data Fig. 1e). Across four prospective datasets, 136 samples were from individuals who later developed CeD (Supplementary Data Fig. 1f). Due to its widespread use, most data was generated from sequencing 16S, with just four datasets generated from shotgun sequencing (Supplementary Data Fig. 1c, d). Illumina MiSeq was the most common sequencing platform for 16S datasets, employed for 96% of samples. Across all 16S datasets, six unique amplicon regions were targeted, with 75% of all samples targeting the V3-V4 region, 13.5% targeting the V4 region and 7.2% targeting the V3 region. Consistent with the etiology of CeD, stool and the duodenum were the most commonly sampled body sites (Supplementary Data Fig. 1b) amongst a range of sampling configurations: stool-only (n = 17), duodenum-only (n = 3), stool + duodenum (n = 4), duodenum + stomach (n = 1), saliva-only (n = 2), and oropharynx-only (n = 1).

The subset of the repository selected for the present study comprised 963 stool and duodenal samples (487 CeD) from across ten 16S datasets and four shotgun datasets across nine countries (Fig. 1a, b). All included datasets derived from 16S rRNA sequencing captured the V4 region, and all included datasets had balanced case-control ratios within each group to ensure comparability. This selection consisted of four patient groups: stool prospective (N = 238, CeD = 117), stool active (N = 274, CeD = 102), stool treated (N = 417, CeD = 204) and duodenum active (N = 118, CeD = 64) (Fig. 1c). The prospective group comprised children aged from birth to five years who were followed for a minimum of five years after sample collection to determine their final CeD status26,51,57. All treated CeD samples were from individuals maintaining a GFD for at least three months42,54,55,58,59,62. Owing to study heterogeneity and sequencing-method differences, a unified processing workflow was utilized to harmonize all datasets. Each dataset was processed individually to enable re-analyses (e.g., alpha diversity and pooled results differential-abundance tests), and cross-study combinations were performed within sequencing modality (16S or shotgun). All downstream models accounted for dataset as a covariate/random effect to control batch structure, focusing inference on the CeD signal in each clinical group (Fig. 1d).

Celiac disease is associated with modest shifts in gut community structure

A random-effects pooled meta-analysis was performed to test for differences in within-sample (α) diversity between patient groups and sequencing methods (Fig. 2a). Overall, α-diversity did not differ between people with CeD and healthy controls. The only replicated signal was in the 16S data: in stool active 16S, richness-oriented metrics Observed (Log₂FC = −0.42; SE = 0.17; q = 0.03; τ² = 0.001; I² = 0%), and Rare-abundance (Log₂FC = −0.46; SE = 0.17; q = 0.03; τ² = 0; I² = 0%) were reduced in people with CeD compared to healthy controls. Consistent but non-significant trends were noted towards lower Simpson dominance and higher Shannon diversity across stool prospective, stool active shotgun, stool treated 16S, and duodenum active groups. In the duodenum active group specifically, there were no differences in any α-diversity metric between individuals with CeD and healthy controls (q > 0.05) (Fig. 2a).

At the phylum level, a broad trend emerged: the Bacillota-to-Bacteroidota ratio was consistently reduced across most disease stages and sequencing methods (Fig. 2b). This pattern was driven by a general decrease in Bacillota abundance co-occurring with an increase in Bacteroidota abundance. These shifts were most pronounced in the stool treated shotgun and stool active 16S groups, which showed a significant decrease in Bacillota abundance, respectively (Log₂FC = −0.18; SE = 0.06; q = 0.009) (Log₂FC = −0.18; SE = 0.07; q = 0.04). A distinct signature of stool active shotgun was an increase in Pseudomonadota abundance (Log₂FC = +2.67; SE = 0.97; q = 0.01), with similar but non-significant tendencies in the stool prospective, and stool active 16S groups (Fig. 2b).

To quantify disease-associated shifts in community structure independent of inter-study heterogeneity, CAP was applied to TSS-normalized JSD while conditioning on dataset (Fig. 3). Across groups, dataset identity accounted for the largest share of variance in the unconstrained space (R² = 6–38%, all P = 0.001, Table 1), whereas disease status explained only modest fractions (R² = 0.9–4%, Table 1). After conditioning on dataset, the disease-associated component remained uniformly small (partial R² ≤ 1.2%), with evidence differing by group and method. In stool prospective 16S, no adjusted effect was observed (CAP partial R² = 0.9%, P = 0.273, Fig. 3a); in stool active, a small 16S signal persisted (unconstrained 1.7%, P = 0.006; CAP partial 1.2%, P = 0.001, Fig. 3b, Table 1), whereas shotgun showed no adjusted effect (CAP partial 0.9%, P = 0.346, Fig. 3e); in stool treated, both assays produced modest but statistically robust adjusted effects (16S CAP partial 1.0%, p = 0.001; shotgun CAP partial 1.1%, P = 0.001, Fig. 3c, f) despite sizeable dataset contributions (11–13%, P = 0.001, Table 1); and in duodenum active, an unconstrained disease effect (4.1%, P = 0.001, Table 1) diminished to borderline after adjustment (CAP partial 1.1%, P = 0.051, Fig. 3g). Collectively, these analyses show that CeD status is associated with small dataset-independent shifts in composition that are detectable in stool treated (both methods) and in stool active by 16S but are overshadowed by dataset heterogeneity. This pattern, consistent with the shifts observed in α-diversity, aligns with modest compositional changes in those with CeD rather than a broad community overhaul (Fig. 3, Table 1).

In the prospective stool group, a combined differential abundance analysis of 16S and shotgun sequencing data identified 71 unique species and 47 unique genera at q < 0.05, with a LogFC range of −3.70 to +1.85. Taxa significantly depleted in individuals with CeD included a Faecalibacterium ASV, Gemmiger qucibialis, Blautia ammoniilytica, and the genus Prevotella. Conversely, taxa enriched in prospective people with CeD included the genus Lactococcus, species Hominimerdicola aceti, Mediterraneibacter torques, and Clostridium lentum (Fig. 4a). Shotgun sequencing provided species-level resolution that corroborated the depletion of the genus Faecalibacterium along with multiple Faecalibacterium spp. and the genus Prevotella, and it further identified significant reductions in Agathobacter faecis, Faecalibacillus faecis, Bacteroides uniformis, Bacteroides ovatus, Phocaeicola vulgatus, and Akkermansia muciniphila. Additional taxa enriched in prospective people with CeD included Thomasclavelia ramosa, Bifidobacterium kashiwanohense, and Collinsella sp. (Fig. 4d). Only three genera were shared between 16S and shotgun datasets, yielding weak concordance in effect sizes (Pearson’s r = 0.29, n = 3). At the species level, only two overlapping taxa were identified (n = 2), precluding robust correlation analysis and underscoring the complementary nature of the two profiling approaches (Supplementary Data 1).

Compared with the prospective group, the stool active group displayed more numerous and larger shifts (222 species, 126 genera; LogFC −4.74 to +4.48), with more taxa enriched than depleted. Like the prospective group, samples from individuals with CeD showed a depletion of a Faecalibacterium ASV and Gemmiger qucibialis. However, in contrast to the prospective cohort, other taxa, such as the genus Roseburia and Phocaeicola dorei, were significantly enriched in people with CeD. Other enriched taxa included the genera Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, and Haemophilus, as well as the species Ligilactobacillus salivarius (Fig. 4b). Shotgun sequencing data further highlighted an enrichment of specific species in individuals with CeD, including Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum, Bifidobacterium bifidum, Prevotella sp., and Haemophilus parainfluenzae. Notably, shotgun analysis also revealed a large increase in Akkermansia muciniphila during active disease, a finding that contrasts with its depletion in the prospective group. The shotgun data also identified further reductions in Bacteroides uniformis and Blautia wexlerae (Fig. 4e). These complex taxonomic shifts resulted in only moderate concordance between sequencing methods at the genus level (Pearson’s r = 0.70, n = 8) and a weak negative correlation at the species level (Pearson’s r = −0.21, n = 11) (Supplementary Data 2).

In the stool treated group, 253 species and 132 genera were detected at q < 0.05 (LogFC −4.42 to +2.57). Taxa significantly depleted in individuals with CeD included the genera Agathobacter, Bifidobacterium, Collinsella, and species Phascolarctobacterium faecium, and Anaerobutyricum hallii. Conversely, taxa enriched in cases included the genera Akkermansia and Alistipes, along with the species Phocaeicola coprocola (Fig. 4c). Shotgun sequencing provided species-level resolution that corroborated these findings and further identified significant depletions in Agathobacter rectalis, Bifidobacterium adolescentis, Bifidobacterium infantis, Collinsella sp., Faecalibacterium hattorii, and Gemmiger qucibialis. Additional taxa enriched in people with CeD included several Bacteroides species, such as, B. stercoris, B. xylanisolvens, B. cellulosilyticus, and Haemophilus parainfluenzae (Fig. 4f). These taxonomic shifts resulted in moderate and significant agreement between sequencing methods at both the genus (Pearson’s r = 0.66, n = 15, p = 0.008) and species levels (Pearson’s r = 0.54, n = 18, p = 0.02), indicating a strong level of concordance (Supplementary Data 3).

Finally, duodenal active 16S profiling revealed a smaller set of changes (38 genera, 22 species; LogFC −4.39 to +2.28). Taxa significantly depleted in people with CeD included several Prevotella species, such as Prevotella pallens, Prevotella histicola, and Prevotella vespertina. Other depletions included Alloprevotella sp., Rothia aeria, and Rothia mucilaginosa. Conversely, taxa enriched in cases included the genera Bifidobacterium, Helicobacter, Campylobacter, and Lactococcus, as well as species Porphyromonas endodontalis, and Prevotella intermedia (Fig. 4g, Supplementary Data 4).

To assess the consistency of microbial alterations across the study groups, the directionality (sign agreement) and correlation (Pearson’s r) of differentially abundant taxa were compared. Overall, the concordance of microbial changes was low in both 16S and shotgun datasets. The strongest signal of agreement was observed between the stool active and treated groups. In the shotgun data, 52 shared differentially abundant species showed a modest but significant positive correlation (sign agreement = 0.71; Pearson’s r = 0.35, q = 0.03). This significant correlation was not seen for 20 shared taxa from the 16S data (sign agreement = 0.75; r = 0.28, q = 0.38) (Fig. 5a, b).

Cross-dataset prediction reveals partial generalizability of celiac classification

To assess microbiome-based prediction of CeD, machine learning models were evaluated on 16S datasets using three cross-validation methods: (1) K-fold, where all datasets were pooled, models were trained on 90% of samples and tested on the rest; (2) LODO, where models were trained on all but one dataset and tested on the unseen dataset; and (3) X-set, where models were trained on a single dataset and tested on another. Of these, LODO is the most clinically relevant because it evaluates generalization to entirely unseen cohorts. Model performance was measured by the AUC and averaged across training runs. The highest K-fold performance was seen in the stool treated group, yielding a mean AUC of 0.913 (Fig. 6a, Table 2). Decreasing in line with the number of available datasets and samples, the other K-fold mean AUCs were 0.792, 0.744 and 0.599 for the stool active, duodenal active, and stool prospective groups, respectively (Fig. 6a, Table 2). Models demonstrated difficulty in generalizing across datasets when trained on only one dataset, as shown by the mean AUCs given by X-Set, which range from 0.556-0.658 (Table 2). As shown by LODO mean AUCs, training models on multiple datasets markedly improved classification of samples from unseen datasets, though performance was modest compared to K-fold (Fig. 6b, Table 2). Accuracies were closely aligned with volume of training data, with LODO mean AUCs of 0.773 for stool treated (four datasets, 215 samples), 0.759 for stool active (three datasets, 158 samples), 0.645 for duodenal active (three datasets, 118 samples), and 0.601 for stool prospective (two datasets, 120 samples).

Discussion

This cross-cohort analysis, integrating 16S rRNA gene amplicon and shotgun metagenomic data, provides an extensive characterization of the gut microbiome differences in CeD. The findings quantitatively confirm a long-standing challenge: the biological signal of CeD in the gut microbiome is modest and often obscured by technical variation between studies. Inter-study variation explains a substantial portion of microbial community variance (R2 = 6–38%), compared to disease status itself (R2 = 0.9–4%). By statistically conditioning on these effects, a reproducible, disease-associated signature was isolated in the large stool cohorts of both active CeD (partial R2 = 1.2%, P = 0.001) and treated CeD (partial R2 = 1.1%, P = 0.001). No adjusted effect was seen in the prospective stool group (p = 0.27), indicating that a broad pre-diagnostic microbiome shift was not resolved, although subtler or taxon-specific shifts cannot be excluded. In contrast the adjusted effect was borderline in the active duodenum group (P = 0.051), suggesting that with larger and more harmonized small-intestinal cohorts, disease-associated alterations at the intestinal site of pathology may be more clearly resolved.

Given that CeD is a disease of the small intestine and that stool captures only a modest signal, along with prior evidence of strong differences between duodenal and stool microbiomes36, future studies should include and, where feasible, prioritize small-intestinal sampling. The overwhelming host DNA content in the duodenum impedes whole metagenomic sequencing, necessitating the exclusion of one dataset in this study. While enrichment protocols are emerging77, taxonomic profiling using 16S rRNA gene amplicons remains the most robust approach for these low-biomass samples. Overall, these results demonstrate that CeD is not characterized by a broad collapse in microbial diversity, but rather by modest changes in specific taxa. Observed compositional differences should be interpreted cautiously. It is important to note that concordance between 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and shotgun metagenomic sequencing is often limited, reflecting differences in taxonomic resolution, detection sensitivity, and methodological biases inherent to each approach. As such, conflicting signals between methods should be interpreted with caution, with greater confidence placed in findings that are consistent across both platforms. Moreover, most included datasets were stool-derived, and microbial profiles in stool can be influenced by factors beyond disease status, including stool consistency, gut transit, diet, medications, and other host factors that could not be uniformly modeled. The present findings are best viewed as robust cross-cohort associations, not direct evidence of causal pathophysiological mechanisms in CeD.

The most substantial finding was a consistent alteration in metabolically important taxa across both stool and duodenal samples, with stage-specific patterns. Before disease onset, butyrate-producing species, such as Faecalibacterium sp., Agathobacter faecis, and Gemmiger qucibialis78–80 were depleted, while taxa associated with lactate, acetate, and propionate, including the genus Lactococcus, and species Hominimerdicola aceti, and Collinsella sp. were increased81–83. During active disease, the microbial signal became more complex, with further depletion of Faecalibacterium and Gemmiger qucibialis, and increases in Prevotella spp., Phocaeicola dorei, Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum, Lactobacillus, and Streptococcus84–87. In individuals on a GFD, depletions persisted for Agathobacter rectalis, Anaerobutyricum hallii, Faecalibacterium sp., and Gemmiger qucibialis, alongside reduced acetate-producing Bifidobacterium species like B. adolescentis and B. infantis88,89. In the duodenum during active disease, multiple Prevotella spp. were depleted while lactate-associated genera like Lactococcus increased, suggesting that altered fiber metabolism may occur at the active site of CeD pathology. This observation is consistent with previous duodenal biopsy–based work reporting impaired fiber fermentation and reduced SCFA-producing capacity in CeD patients90. While these compositional shifts are consistent with impaired SCFA production, functional consequences and causality remain to be established.

The functional consequence of this microbial shift may be reduced fiber fermentation in the small intestine and systemic butyrate, both important for gut health90. Butyrate is a key energy source for colonocytes and promotes immune tolerance by inhibiting histone deacetylases and supporting regulatory T cell differentiation91–94, which helps maintain epithelial integrity and immune regulation, features likely implicated in CeD pathogenesis26,95,96. In a healthy gut, lactate produced by bacteria, such as Lactobacillus is converted to butyrate by cross-feeding with butyrate-producing species97,98. The enrichment of lactate producers and depletion of lactate-utilizing butyrate producers in CeD suggests an alteration in this metabolic link. This disruption may precede diagnosis and persist after GFD initiation, indicating that microbial normalization may require longer follow-up than captured in the included datasets, as mucosal recovery in CeD is often slow and incomplete99. Consistent with this, evidence from duodenal biopsies in CeD demonstrates impaired local capacity for fiber metabolism90, suggesting that this functional loss may compound the inflammatory damage occurring at the mucosal surface in the small bowel.

Dynamic, stage-specific shifts in the gut microbiome, were identifed, reflecting the evolving host-microbe ecology of CeD. These microbial responses are highly specific to the disease stage and intestinal site, with each phase presenting a distinct microbial signature. For example, the mucin-degrading bacterium A. muciniphila, was depleted before diagnosis but became enriched during active disease and remained elevated in those treated with GFD (Fig. 4c). Its initial depletion may reflect a primary defect in the mucosal barrier, which could increase vulnerability to gluten, though this remains speculative. The subsequent enrichment and persistence may reflect an opportunistic response to inflammation-induced mucus production100. The current findings also noted a duodenal-specific loss of commensal oral microbes, such as Rothia aeria and Rothia mucilaginosa, which possess enzymes capable of hydrolyzing gluten. Their depletion at the site of inflammation could impair local gluten processing, potentially increasing the concentration of immunogenic peptides and contributing to the local inflammatory response101,102. Acute increases in potentially opportunistic taxa at the mucosal surface, namely Helicobacter and Campylobacter, may contribute to the inflammatory response in the duodenum, though further studies are required to clarify their roles103,104.

Strong K-fold performance was observed for CeD prediction in the stool treated group (0.913 mean AUC). However, this may partly reflect the classifiers distinguishing dietary effects rather than disease status, given that GFD significantly remodels the stool microbiome105. Classification accuracy significantly dropped under the more rigorous LODO cross-validation, which better simulates real-world deployment, and on the more clinically relevant groups, stool active (0.759 mean AUC) and stool prospective (0.601 mean AUC). Therefore, rigorous cross-validation methods, such as LODO, with cautious inclusion of samples from people on a GFD, should be considered essential for all evaluations of prediction of CeD with the aim of real-world usage. Across all groups, LODO performance was closely tied to training data volume, suggesting that higher accuracy prediction of CeD may be feasible given more data. However, microbiome community composition data is inherently high-dimensional and heterogeneous, invoking the curse of dimensionality and limiting predictive accuracy without sufficiently large datasets106. While labeled CeD-specific microbiome datasets are currently limited, vast amounts of unlabeled microbiome data already exist, such as the >168,000 stool samples107. In other domains, such as natural language processing and computer vision, models pretrained on large unlabeled datasets have demonstrated strong downstream performance108. Therefore, unsupervised approaches should be considered for building effective microbiome-based machine learning models for CeD prediction and more broadly to address some of the biggest questions facing microbiome research. Regardless of the machine learning approach, the Celiac Microbiome Repository establishes critical infrastructure for applying machine learning to understand the gut microbiome’s role in CeD, hopefully one day resulting in clinically useful predictions.

In summary, the current analysis reveals consistent changes in the gut microbiome between people with CeD and controls across multiple temporal landscapes and along the gastrointestinal tract. Although between-study heterogeneity remained substantial, accounting for cohort-level variation identified reproducible alterations in the gut microbiome consistent with hypothesized alterations in microbial-metabolic function among distinct stage-specific progression. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as key confounders, including age, sex, diet information beyond GFD status, medications, serology, and symptom severity, were inconsistently available and could not be uniformly modeled, limiting causal inference. Nonetheless, the patterns reported here align with other gastrointestinal diseases, with key changes in taxa hypothesized to maintain gut integrity and immune modulation by the production of SCFAs, namely butyrate95,109. As such, cross-cohort consistency supports testable hypotheses that current treatment paradigms may be biologically incomplete and that restoring gut microbiome function could provide a useful therapeutic target. Future therapeutic studies with deeply phenotyped metadata are needed to validate these associations, define their clinical value, and test whether treatment strategies should move beyond simple gluten elimination and mucosal healing towards restoration of microbial function. These findings provide a rationale for clinical trials of targeted interventions, including rationally designed prebiotic fibers, specific probiotic consortia, or postbiotics like butyrate, to address underlying metabolic and immunomodulatory deficits and improve long-term outcomes for patients with CeD.
