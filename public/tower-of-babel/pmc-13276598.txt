Effects of different polysaccharides on the anti-Retrogradation of buckwheat Wantuan
Authors: Zhe Cheng, Ruiling Shen, Jiawen Zhu, Xin Yi, Junjun Hu, Yunlong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHere, we elucidate the anti-retrogradation mechanism of oat β-glucan (OG), seaweed polysaccharide (SP), and konjac glucomannan (KGM) in buckwheat wantuan, by evaluating their impacts on the retrogradation behavior and structure of buckwheat starch, as well as the storage quality of the final product. All polysaccharides significantly decreased starch retrogradation value, with SP-added starch showing the highest reduction of 38.73%. They induced crystalline transformation from A-type to B + V-type and reduced crystallinity, accompanied by hydrogen-bond red-shifts and lower R1047/1022 ratios. SEM observations revealed cross-linking between polysaccharides and starch, forming a dense and ordered network structure. Storage studies demonstrated that polysaccharide addition significantly reduced hardness, chewiness, and gelatinization enthalpy (P < 0.05) of wantuan while inhibiting water migration from bound to free states. Among them, SP exhibited the best anti-staling effects, reducing hardness by 16.05% and ΔHge by 56.5%. Correlation analysis further confirmed that polysaccharides inhibit wantuan aging through controlling water mobility.Keywords: Buckwheat starch, Buckwheat Wantuan, Starch retrogradation, Polysaccharide, Qulity

Introduction

Fagopyrum belonging to the Polygonaceae family, is rich in nutrients(Sakac et al., 2011) such as protein, starch, dietary fiber, and vitamins, as well as bioactive compounds(Wang et al., 2024)like flavonoids and polyphenols, which contribute to its hypoglycemic, hypolipidemic, and antioxidant properties(Li & He, 2022; Qian et al., 2023). Buckwheat is commonly processed into various food products, including buckwheat noodles(Shi et al., 2023), steamed buns(Deng, 2022), buckwheat wine and tartary buckwheat tea(Liu et al., 2022), etc. Among these, buckwheat wantuan (a traditional starch-based convenience food) is a high-starch food primarily made from buckwheat flour, with a unique taste, soft and chewy. And it is particularly popular in northern China. However, due to the high starch content (> 70%) in wantuan, starch undergoes aging and retrogradation during storage, which not only deteriorates its eating quality and product taste, but also shortens its shelf life. Therefore, delaying or inhibiting the staling of buckwheat wantuan is of great significance for the advancement of buckwheat processing industries. The aging of wantuan is essentially the retrogradation of buckwheat starch. When starch is gelatinized in an aqueous solution under heating, the granules swell, internal hydrogen bonds break, and amylose leaches out, transforming the molecular structure from an ordered to a disordered state. Upon cooling and storage, the starch molecules realign into ordered double-helical structures, forming precipitates or gels(Wang et al., 2015). Starch aging is helpful to accelerate the gel forming of wantuan, but with the deepening of aging degree, it will cause the quality change of food during storage, resulting in dry and hard taste, water loss and dry cracking on the surface, short shelf life and other problems.

The retrogradation characteristics and inhibition methods of starch and starch-based foods have long been a key focus and challenge for researchers worldwide. Common anti-staling methods include the addition of emulsifiers, hydrocolloids, and enzymatic agents. Extensive studies have demonstrated that polysaccharides can significantly suppress starch retrogradation. For instance, hydrophilic polysaccharides such as seaweed polysaccharides and pullulan can influence the gelatinization, rheological properties, digestibility, and other physicochemical characteristics of rice starch(Chen et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2023), The incorporation of hydrocolloids improves product texture and inhibits both short-term and long-term starch retrogradation(Chen et al., 2016; Meng et al., 2014). Xu Lei(Xu et al., 2024)and Zhang Ning(Zhang et al., 2024)et al. developed crosslinked sugar beet pectin-pea starch composite systems and xylitol-glutinous rice flour blends, respectively. They found that these composite systems exhibited pseudoplastic fluid behavior, with significantly increased peak viscosity, final viscosity, and enhanced viscoelasticity. However, current research on starch retrogradation inhibition primarily focuses on common foods like rice and wheat, with limited attention given to high-starch foods such as buckwheat wantuan. Zhang Yangjie(Zhang, 2022) compared the anti-staling effects of nine different compounds on buckwheat wantuan and identified monoglycerides, guar gum, salt, and β-amylase as the most effective anti-staling agents. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanisms remain unexplored.

This study investigated the effects of seaweed polysaccharide (SP), oat β-glucan (OG), and konjac glucomannan (KGM) on the gelatinization and retrogradation properties of buckwheat starch, as well as their impacts on starch structural characteristics. Furthermore, the variations in water migration, textural properties, and thermal characteristics of wantuan during storage were systematically examined. Through correlation analysis of retrogradation indicators during storage, this research explored the intrinsic regulatory mechanisms of polysaccharides in delaying starch retrogradation in buckwheat wantuan. The findings provide theoretical insights and technical references for the development and quality preservation of starch-based foods like buckwheat wantuan.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Buckwheat flour was purchased from Meet All Supermarket (Taiyuan, China); Buckwheat starch was isolated in laboratory; oat β-glucan (OG, content >80%, weight-average molecular weight ranged from 200 to 400 kDa and the viscosity of 1% aqueous solution was 500–2000 mPa·s at 25 °C) were purchased from Shaanxi Shuoyang Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; seaweed Polysaccharide (SP, content >98%, weight-average molecular weight of 120–200 kDa and the apparent viscosity of its 1% (w/v) aqueous solution at 25 °C was 80–150 mPa·s) were purchased from Xi'an Jinzhou Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd.; konjac glucomannan (KGM, > 95%, weight-average molecular weight of 800–1200 kDa and the apparent viscosity of its 1% (w/v) aqueous solution at 25 °C was 1200–1800 mPa·s) were purchased from Hubei Johnson Konjac Technology Co., Ltd.; edible salt was sold on the Meet All Supermarket (Taiyuan, China). All chemicals were of analytical grade unless specified: Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), Petroleum ether, Sulfuric acid (H2SO4), Boric acid (H3BO3), Hydrochloric acid (HCl) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.; Potassium bromide (KBr, spectroscopic grade) was purchased from Merck KGaA.

Experimental methods

Preparation of buckwheat starch

Buckwheat starch was extracted using the dilute alkaline method according to Zhang et al.'s protocol with modifications(Zhang & Zhang, 2019). Briefly, buckwheat flour was mixed with 0.15% NaOH solution (1:5, w/v) and stirred at room temperature for 24 h. The slurry was sieved through a 0.150 mm mesh, and the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was resuspended in fresh 0.15% NaOH solution and stirred for 3 h at room temperature. After centrifugation (3000 ×g, 10 min), the sediment was washed with distilled water until neutral pH (7.0) was achieved, followed by repeated centrifugation until a colorless supernatant was obtained. The purified starch was dried in a petri dish at 40 °C for 48 h, then ground and sieved to obtain final starch powder with >80% starch content (determined by Ewers polarimetric method).

Determination of basic composition of buckwheat flour

The basic nutritional components of buckwheat flour were determined according to Chinese National Standards or commercial assay kits. Specifically: Protein content was determined according to GB 5009.5–2016 (Nitrogen analyzer distillation unit: KDN-103F, Shanghai Qianjian Instruments, China; Intelligent digestion furnace: HYP-3, Shanghai Hongyu Instruments, China); Fat content was measured following GB 5009.6–2016 (Soxhlet extractor fat meter: FY-SXT-6 A, Shanghai Fuyu Analytical Instruments, China); Ash content was analyzed according to GB 5009.4–2016 (Muffle furnace, Shanghai Yiheng Technoligy Co., Ltd.); Moisture content was determined as per GB 5009.3–2016 (Electric thermostatic drying oven: FCD-3000, Tianjin Tongli Xinda Instruments Co., Ltd.); Total starch and amylose content were measured using commercial assay kits (Megazyme International, Ireland).

Effect of polysaccharides on gelatinization and Retrogradation of buckwheat starch

The influence of polysaccharides on starch gelatinization characteristics was analyzed according to the method of Cai et al.(Cai et al., 2020) with modifications and using rapid visco analyzer (RVA 4500, Perten Instruments, Sweden). Briefly, 3.0 g (dry basis) of buckwheat starch was accurately weighed and mixed with 2% (w/w, starch basis) of different polysaccharides (oat β-glucan, OG; seaweed polysaccharide, SP; or konjac glucomannan, KGM) in an RVA canister. Distilled water (24.0 mL) was added, and the mixture was gelatinized following GB/T 24853–2010 with the following temperature profile: (1) holding at 50 °C for 1 min, (2) heating to 95 °C at 6 °C/min, (3) maintaining at 95 °C for 5 min, (4) cooling to 50 °C at 6 °C/min, and (5) holding at 50 °C for 2 min. The stirring speed was set at 960 rpm for the first 10 s, then maintained at 160 rpm throughout the gelatinization process. The resulting starch pastes containing polysaccharides (designated as SP-S, OG-S, and KGM-S for SP-, OG-, and KGM-modified starches, respectively) were cooled to form gels. Control samples without polysaccharides (CON-S) were prepared following the same procedure. All gel samples were stored at 4 °C for 48 h for retrogradation. The treated starch gels were divided into two groups: one group was maintained in gel form for relevant indicators, while the other group was freeze-dried, ground and sieved for subsequent characterization.

Effect of polysaccharides on water migration in buckwheat starch

The starch gels prepared in Section 2.3.3 were transferred to 2 mL centrifuge tubes, and water migration during retrogradation was monitored using nuclear magnetic resonance imaging (Low-field NMR analyzer, NM120, Niumag Corporation, China). Measurement were conducted at 18 MHz, with 375, 006 sampling points and the number of scans (NS) fixed at 4. The inversion spectrum of T2 and peak area ratios (A2) were obtained through invers Laplace transformation (ILT) of the NMR data.

Effect of polysaccharides on structural properties of buckwheat starch

Effect of polysaccharides on crystallinity of buckwheat starch

The influence of polysaccharides on the crystallinity of retrograded buckwheat starch was determined using wide-angle X-ray diffraction (X-ray diffractometer (XRD), PW-1710, Philips, Netherlands) following Li's method with modifications(Li et al., 2017). The measurement were performed at 25 °C with an applied voltage of 40 kV and a current of 40 mA. The scanning rate was 2°/min, the 2θ diffraction angle ranged from 5 to 40°, and the step size was set at 0.02°. The relative crystallinity (Rc) was calculated using Jade 6.0 software.

Effect of polysaccharides on FTIR spectra of buckwheat starch

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR), Vertex 70, Bruker, Germany) was performed according to Guo's method to analyze the chemical structure of retrograded buckwheat starch. The spectra were recorded from 400 to 4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 64 scans(Guo et al., 2018).

Effect of polysaccharides on microstructure of buckwheat starch

The retrograded starch samples with polysaccharides were freeze-dried and observed by scanning electron microscopy (Scanning electron microscope (SEM), Regulus 8100, Hitachi, Japan). The samples were mounted on conductive adhesive, sputter-coated with gold, and imaged at 500× magnification.

Storage characteristics of buckwheat Wantuan

Preparation of polysaccharide-containing buckwheat Wantuan

Buckwheat flour was mixed with 2% polysaccharides (w/w, based on flour weight), 0.35% NaCl, and distilled water (flour-to-water ratio 1:2.5). The mixture was steamed for 20 min to prepare φ50 × 5 mm wantuan samples. After cooling, samples were vacuum-packed, sterilized (100 °C, 5 min), and stored at 4 °C for 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20 days. Samples were labeled SP-B, OG-B, KGM-B (polysaccharide-added), and CON-B (control).

Effect of polysaccharides on texture properties

Texture parameters were measured using a texture analyzer (TA-XT plus, Stable Micro Systems, UK) with a 50 mm cylindrical probe. Settings: pre-test speed 1 mm/s, test speed 1 mm/s, post-test speed 10 mm/s, trigger force 5 g. The probe compressed the central redion of the gel to 50% of the original sample height.

Effect of polysaccharides on water migration

Wantuan samples were cut into uniform strips, placed in 2 mL tubes, and stored at 4 °C. Water migration was monitored by NMR imaging (parameters as in Section 2.2.4). T2 relaxation spectra and peak area ratios (A2) were obtained. Proton density images were acquired using MSE sequence (repetition time(TR) 300 ms, echo time(TE) 18.125 ms, matrix size 256 × 192, field of view(FOV) 80 × 80 mm). Images were pseudo-colored using Osiris software and saved as BMP files.

Effect of polysaccharides on thermodynamic properties

Using a differential scanningcalorimeter (DSC, Q20, TA Instruments, USA), freeze-dried wantuan samples (3.0 mg) were mixed with 6 μL deionized water in DSC pans, equilibrated at 4 °C for 12 h, and analyzed (20–120 °C, 10 °C/min). Gelatinization enthalpy (ΔHgel) and peak temperature (Tp) were calculated using DSC software.

Effect of polysaccharides on short-range molecular order

FTIR spectra (parameters as in 2.2.5.2) were deconvoluted (800–1200 cm−1, half-width 31 cm−1, enhancement factor 2.1) using OMNIC 8.2.0.387. The R1047/1022 ratio was calculated from peak intensities at 1047 cm−1 and 1022 cm−1.

Correlation analysis of Retrogradation indicators

Pearson correlation analysis was performed for texture, thermal, gelatinization, and water migration parameters. Heatmaps were generated using Origin 2021.

Data analysis

Triplicate data were processed with Origin 2021. Statistical significance (P < 0.05) was determined by ANOVA and Tukey's test in SPSS 23.0, expressed as mean ± SD.

Effect of polysaccharides on the pasting properties of buckwheat starch

The RVA curves in Fig. 1 characterize the gelatinization process of starch. As shown in Fig. 1, since starch cannot dissolve in cold water, the initial viscosity of the starch dispersion system was very low. With increasing temperature, starch began to gelatinize, leading to a rise in viscosity. Table 2 presents the RVA characteristic values of buckwheat starch under the influence of different polysaccharides. Compared with the control group, the retrogradation values of buckwheat starch in all polysaccharide-treated groups decreased significantly (P < 0.05). Specifically, the retrogradation value of buckwheat starch in the CON-S control group was 6161 cP, while those in the SP-S, KGM—S, and OG-S groups decreased by 38.73%, 17.29%, and 11.51% respectively. The retrogradation value reflects the short-term retrogradation of starch, which is associated with the recrystallization of starch molecules (particularly amylose) during cooling. These results are consistent with the findings of Yang K.(Yang et al., 2021), indicating that polysaccharides can reduce the damage to starch granules during gelatinization and inhibit starch retrogradation. Furthermore, the breakdown value of buckwheat starch in the control group was 786 cP, while those in the SP-S and OG-S groups decreased by 46% and 66% respectively. A lower breakdown value indicates stronger shear resistance of starch granules during heating(Luo et al., 2017). The reduction in both breakdown and retrogradation values suggests that the addition of polysaccharides enhances the thermal and cold paste stability of buckwheat starch(Liu et al., 2017). Notably, seaweed polysaccharide (SP) exhibited a significant inhibitory effect on both retrogradation and breakdown values (See Table 2).

Effect of polysaccharides on water migration in buckwheat starch gel

The spin-spin relaxation time (T2) is related to the free gregree of hydrogen protons and its binding force. Shorter T2 values indicate tighter water-substrate binding and greater stability(Cheng et al., 2019). Based on T2, water states are classified as: strongly bound water T20 (0.1–1 ms), weakly bound water T21 (1–10 ms), low mobile water T22 (10–100 ms), and free water T23 (>100 ms)(Dai et al., 2018). Fig. 2 shows T2 relaxation changes in starch gels containing different polysaccharides after 48 h aging at 4 °C. Polysaccharide addition caused rightward shifts in T21 (weakly bound water), particularly in SP-S and OG-S groups, indicating conversion of strongly bound to weakly bound water. In OG-S group, T22 and T23 showed leftward shifts, while other polysaccharides caused rightward shifts. This demonstrated that oat β-glucan (OG) enhanced water-binding capacity, whereas other polysaccharides increased water mobility in starch gels.

Effect of polysaccharides on crystalline properties of buckwheat starch

The XRD patterns and relative crystallinity (RC) of buckwheat starch containing different polysaccharides are shown in Fig. 3. Buckwheat starch exhibited a typical cereal-type A-type crystalline structure. During gelatinization, the crystalline structure was disrupted, followed by recrystallization through hydrogen bonding between starch molecular chains during cooling, forming a gel, which was consistent with previous studies(Kiatponglarp et al., 2015). Starch recrystallization occurred during aging, with distinct characteristic peaks appearing at 16.9° and 20.8°, indicating transformation into B + V-type polymorphic starch. The addition of polysaccharides did not alter the crystalline configuration of gelatinized buckwheat starch, which remained as B + V-type polymorphic starch. The relative crystallinity (RC) was used to evaluate the degree of aging and recrystallization(Zheng et al., 2021). The calculated RC values of SP-S, KGM-S and OG-S (16.28%, 18.01% and 7.67%, respectively) were all lower than that of the control group (33.33%). Notably, the crystallinity of β-glucan-added buckwheat starch decreased by 76.9%. This demonstrated that polysaccharide addition reduced the ordered reorganization of buckwheat starch and significantly inhibited starch recrystallization.

Effect of polysaccharides on short-range ordered structure of buckwheat starch

The FT-IR spectra of samples are shown in Fig. 4. FT-IR was used to characterize interactions between starch molecules and polysaccharides, analyzing their effects on chain conformation, helicity and double-helical structure of buckwheat starch. In starch systems, hydroxyl groups can form intra- and intermolecular hydrogen bonds. The strong and broad absorption peak at 3100–3600 cm−1 (Fig. 4) corresponds to hydroxyl stretching vibrations(Qian et al., 2009). Compared with control, polysaccharide addition caused a red shift of this peak, indicating enhanced hydrogen bonding interactions within and between starch molecules. However, only peak position shifts were observed without new characteristic peaks, suggesting no covalent bonding or new group formation between polysaccharides and starch molecules during aging.

Effect of polysaccharides on microstructure of buckwheat starch

The buckwheat starch added with polysaccharides was freeze-dried after aging, and the microstructure were observed as shown in Fig. 5. SEM images revealed that control starch transformed from disordered to ordered state after gelatinization and rearranged to form crystals(Lee & Rhee, 2007). During aging, water migration led to loose structure with irregular networks and cavities, resulting from starch granule rupture during gelatinization and subsequent molecular rearrangement. Polysaccharide addition resulted in denser, more ordered starch gels with significantly reduced cavities, indicating that the polysaccharide improved internal structure through cross-linking with starch molecules to form large aggregates to inhibite water migration and enhanced water-holding capacity. Specifically, SP-S group showed uniform molecular distribution with dense pores, while KGM-S and OG-S groups exhibited looser structures, attributed to varying molecular weights of polysaccharides affecting aggregation degree. This explains why seaweed polysaccharide showed optimal anti-retrogradation effect during storage. Liu et al. proposed that amylose promotes retrogradation by forming crystalline networks, while polysaccharide-water interactions inhibit starch swelling and prevent amylose recrystallization(Liu et al., 2018).

Effect of polysaccharides on storage characteristics of buckwheat Wantuan

Effect of polysaccharides on hardness of buckwheat Wantuan

Table 3 shows the effect of polysaccharides on hardness changes of buckwheat wantuan during storage. Hardness, as an important parameter reflecting starch retrogradation, can characterize the aging process. After 8 days of storage, hardness increased significantly (P < 0.05, shown in Table 3), with the rising trend slowing down subsequently. By day 20, compared with control, SP-B, OG-B and KGM-B groups showed 16.05%, 4.23% and 2.36% reductions in hardness respectively. Chewiness peaked at day 12 before decreasing variably. Liu wei found that molecular chain conformation contracted during retrogradation, tending toward ordered arrangement, while trehalose addition inhibited this contraction, hindering free water release and recrystallization(Liu et al., 2023). Long-term retrogradation mainly resulted from amylopectin recrystallization. With the increase of recrystallization degree of amylopectin, the gel strength and hardness of starch system increased progressively. Trehalose addition significantly reduced both hardness and chewiness of the starch system, with this advantage becoming more pronounced over time. Thus, polysaccharides (especially seaweed polysaccharide) could not only effectively inhibit the short-term retrogradation of starch, but also had a good inhibitory effect on long-term retrogradation. In addition, during the aging and retrogradation of buckwheat wantuan, with the rearrangement of starch molecules, the starch structure was destroyed, which limited the interaction between starch chains, and the firmness and strength increased, showing an increase in hardness and chewiness. After continuous storage for a period of time, the equilibrium state was reached, the upward trend of hardness slowed down, and the chewiness fell back.

Effect of polysaccharides on water migration in buckwheat Wantuan

The transverse relaxation time (T2) reflects water molecule mobility, with smaller T2 values indicating tighter starch-water binding and lower mobility(Tang et al., 2013). As shown in Fig. 6, T2 decreased with storage time for all samples, indicating reduced water mobility. This aligns with Niu Haili et al.'sfindings that T2 reduction during storage may result from increased structural order in starch systems, enhancing water binding to crystalline lamellae and restricting molecular movement(Niu et al., 2017). Fig. 7 displays water composition ratios in buckwheat wantuan, while Fig. 8 presents pseudocolor images of water migration. Whiter central regions indicate higher free water content, with grayer colors representing increased bound water. Results showed gradual decrease in T21 (bound water) and increase in T22 (low mobile water) during storage. Among them, the water loss of buckwheat wantuan T21 without polysaccharide was more serious. This is because the polysaccharide molecule contains more hydroxyl groups that form hydrogen bonds with water molecules easily, weakening starch-water interactions and enhancing water mobility(Ding et al., 2019). Furthermore, polysaccharides interact with starch molecules and compete for water in the system to inhibite starch recrystallization and delay starch aging(Yan & Lu, 2020).

Effect of different polysaccharides on thermal properties of buckwheat Wantuan

The changes in thermal properties of buckwheat wantuan during storage are shown in Table 4. During low-temperature storage, starch molecules transitione to lower energy states, and the molecular chains are gradually ordered by rearrangement and hydrogen bonding. The gelatinization enthalpy (ΔHgel) represents the energy required for melting crystalline starch polymers and reflects retrogradation degree. Higher ΔHgel indicates more recrystallization and severe retrogradation(Xu et al., 2012). ΔHgel of buckwheat wantuan increased gradually during storage. After 20 days, the control group showed a 4.09-fold increase in ΔHgel. Compared with control, SP-B, KGM-B and OG-B groups exhibited 56.5%, 43.7% and 49.1% reductions in ΔHgel, respectively. The gelatinization temperature decreased slightly but not significantly. The decreased ΔHgel suggests polysaccharides weakened starch crystal packing ability, reduced retrogradation and the energy required for the melting of starch crystal polymers. Liu's study demonstrated that trehalose's hydroxyl groups with small steric hindrance could enter the starch molecules, forming hydrogen bonds with amylose and entangling with amylopectin, thereby effectively inhibiting amylopectin interactions and preventing long-term retrogradation(Liu et al., 2023).

Short-range order analysis of buckwheat Wantuan

The R-values (relative intensity ratio of integrated peaks at 1047 cm−1 and 1022 cm−1) for different samples are presented in Table 5. The absorption peaks of starch molecules in the 800–1200 cm−1 range primarily relate to C—C and C—O bonds stretching. The bands at 1047 cm−1 and 1022 cm−1 reflect the quantities of ordered and amorphous regions, respectively(Yu et al., 2018), with their intensity ratio serving as an indicator of short-range structure strength of double-helical. Higher 1047/1022 cm−1 peak ratio indicates greater short-range order and more severe retrogradation(Guo et al., 2018). After refrigerated storage at 4 °C, the control group showed a significant 2.72-fold increase in R-value (P < 0.05). Compared with control, SP-B, KGM-B and OG-B groups exhibited 23.5%, 22.5% and 11.8% reductions in R-values, respectively. The decreased R-values with polysaccharide addition demonstrate their inhibitory effect on starch molecular ordering and reduction of double-helical structure formation.

Correlation analysis of storage indicators in buckwheat Wantuan

Fig. 9 presents the correlation analysis of various parameters during buckwneat wantuan aging. Control wantuan showed significant positive correlations (P < 0.05) between hardness, ΔHgel, R1047/1022 and storage time, while cohesiveness and gelatinization temperature (Tp) exhibited negative correlations with storage time (P < 0.05). This indicated the hardness, enthalpy values and short-range ordering of wantuan increased with prolonged storage. Weakly-bound water (A21) area negatively correlated with storage time (P < 0.05), whereas low-mobile water (A22) and free water (A23) areas showed opposite trends, demonstrating water state conversion during aging. While polysaccharide-added wantuan displayed different correlation patterns, primarily the correlations of Tp (−0.76, 0.12, −0.79 for SP-B, KGM—B, OG-B respectively) and A23 (0.47, 0.71, 0.33) with storage time were insignificant. That suggested water state transformation is the primary factor during starchy food aging. During aging, polysaccharides' superior water absorption reduced available water around starch granules, inhibiting swelling and increasing gelatinization temperature. Meanwhile, gelatinized starch molecules intertwined with polysaccharides, reducing binding opportunities with hydrogen bond and restricting water migration, thereby retarding starch retrogradation.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of different polysaccharides on the retrogradation properties of buckwheat starch and wantuan, with correlation analysis of storage indicators. The results showed that polysaccharide addition significantly reduced retrogradation value, gelatinization enthalpy, recrystallization rate and relative crystallinity in buckwheat starch. Seaweed polysaccharide showed the greatest reduction in retrogradation value, while oat β-glucan most effectively decreased crystallinity. Furthermore, the starch system exhibited increased relaxation time, with A21 (weakly-bound water) showing significant negative correlation with storage time (P < 0.05), while A22 (low-mobile water) and A23 (free water) displayed opposite trends. Reduced A23 correlation after polysaccharide addition indicated that could enhance water-binding capacity, thereby retarding starch retrogradation. Polysaccharides interacted with starch molecules to form network structures that: reduced molecular cavities, decreased short-range ordering, inhibited double-helix formation.The starch molecules in the seaweed polysaccharide group were more compact with more uniform pores, the polyhydroxyl structure interacts with starch molecules through hydrogen bonds limiting polymerization rearrangement of starch chains. These dual mechanisms of seaweed polysaccharide effectively suppressed starch recrystallization. During buckwheat wantuan storage, polysaccharide-amylose interactions inhibited water state conversion from bound to free water, reducing hardness and chewiness. Variation in anti-retrogradation efficacy among polysaccharides correlated with structural and molecular weight differences - higher molecular weight polysaccharides more effectively reduced short-range ordering, with seaweed polysaccharide exhibiting optimal performance. These findings provide valuable insights for developing and storing of convenience gel foods with high-starch.
