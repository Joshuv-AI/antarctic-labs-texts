Incidence of unintentional injuries among children aged 3–12 in China: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiujuan Li, Ziyue Tan, Linlin Xie, Roumei Lin, Fancun Meng, Pan Wen, Liping Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the occurrence of unintentional injuries among children aged 3 to 12 in China over the past 5 years, and to provide a scientific basis for the prevention and control of such injuries.DesignA systematic review and meta-analysis. A random-effects model was specified a priori to synthesise study estimates given anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies.Data sourcesWe searched CNKI, WanFang and PubMed for studies published between 1 January 2018 and 30 June 2024.Eligibility criteria for selecting studiesWe included observational studies conducted in China with children aged 3–12 years, reporting incidence or person-time incidence of unintentional injuries and using clear injury definition criteria. Exclusion criteria included reviews, studies on special populations, duplicated datasets or incomplete data.Data extraction and synthesisTwo independent reviewers screened studies, extracted data and assessed study quality using Loney’s criteria. Heterogeneity was quantified with the I² statistic and Q test. Publication bias was assessed with funnel plots, Egger’s regression and fail-safe N. Certainty of evidence for primary outcomes was rated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.ResultsA total of 16 studies involving 37 877 participants were included. The results indicated that the combined effect size of the incidence of unintentional injuries among children aged 3 to 12 in China was 14.0% (95% CI 9.5% to 20.3%), and the person-time incidence was 15.3% (95% CI 8.6% to 25.8%), with falls demonstrating the highest combined effect size. Further subgroup analysis revealed several trends: the incidence of unintentional injuries was higher in boys (16.1%) than in girls (11.2%), among children aged 3–6 years (15.2%) compared with those aged 6–12 years (12.4%), in the western region (14.3%) versus the eastern region (13.1%) and when applying the 1996 injury criteria (17.7%) compared with the 2004 criteria (15.6%). All differences were statistically significant (p<0.05). Study quality (Loney) ranged from 4–6 (median=6); Egger’s test showed no evidence of small-study bias (p=0.87). GRADE assessment rated the certainty of evidence for both primary outcomes as very low, primarily due to observational design, high heterogeneity and imprecision. Sensitivity analyses excluding lower quality studies and leave-one-out analyses did not materially change effect direction or conclusions.ConclusionsThe incidence of unintentional injuries among Chinese children aged 3–12 remains high, especially from falls. Injury rates differ significantly by gender, age, region and other factors. Future efforts should prioritise the safety of kindergarten children in western China, with a strong emphasis on preventing falls, which are a common type of injuries among children.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024604890.

Introduction

Unintentional injuries are external, sudden and unintended events that negatively impact children’s health and well-being.1 According to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, unintentional injuries are categorised by external causes of disease and death, including falls, blunt instrument injuries, road traffic accidents, drowning, sharp object injuries, burns and scalds, poisoning, animal bites, electric shock injuries and foreign body cavities.2

According to the death-cause monitoring data of China in 2021, the mortality rate of injuries among children and adolescents was 8.14 per 100 000, and it reached 10.26 per 100 000 in the western regions.3 Notably, the preschool and school age stages are the two peak stages for the occurrence of unintentional injuries in children.4 Children aged 3 to 12 in China fall within these developmental stages and are in a crucial phase of motor and sensory function development.5 Due to their strong curiosity and active nature, coupled with poor judgement regarding danger and a lack of safety awareness and life experience, they are more susceptible to injuries than children in other age groups.6

The issue of unintentional injuries in children is severe and ranks among the top health threats. Death is the most serious outcome of unintentional injuries; however, numerous non-fatal injuries can result in physical trauma, disabilities and even long-term incapacitation.7 8 These injuries impose significant burdens on families and society while also causing serious trauma to children’s mental and psychological health.911 These losses underscore the urgency of implementing comprehensive prevention and control measures for such incidents.

Unintentional injuries are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among children in China, yet most syntheses have focused on under-five mortality or mixed paediatric age bands, leaving key gaps for school-aged children (3–12 years). Recent national surveillance analyses show under-five unintentional injury mortality declined from 2010 to 2020 but remains a major public health burden, with pronounced urban–rural disparities and cause patterns dominated by suffocation in infants and drowning/traffic injuries in 1–4 year-olds.9

Prior systematic reviews have primarily examined (1) Mortality in younger children or (2) Broad associations and determinants in preschoolers, rather than population-level incidence in school-aged children and person-time incidence compatible with trend comparison and prevention planning. For example, a national meta-analysis of unintentional injury and death in 0–5 year-olds highlighted higher risks in boys and in rural areas, but did not quantify incidence for older age groups or provide person-time metrics.12

More recently, a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-guided review of determinants in preschool children synthesised child, parent, household and area-level risk factors (eg, male sex, developmental and behavioural characteristics, parental mental health, household socioeconomic status and area deprivation), underscoring the multi-level nature of risk;13 however, the evidence base centres on children ≤5 years and does not resolve national incidence patterns among those aged 3–12 years.

Collectively, these reviews and surveillance reports indicate progress in injury prevention among the youngest children while also revealing an evidence gap: up-to-date, nationally representative incidence and person-time incidence for school-aged children, with structured exploration of heterogeneity by sex, setting, age band, region, sampling method and injury case definition. Addressing this gap is essential for targeting resources (eg, kindergarten vs primary-school settings), tailoring interventions (eg, fall prevention) and benchmarking progress across regions.

Rationale and contribution of this study. We respond to this gap by conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis focused specifically on Chinese children aged 3–12 years (2018–2024), a period aligned with recent national initiatives and contemporary reporting standards. Our study extends the current evidence base in three ways: (1) We estimate pooled incidence and person-time incidence for overall unintentional injuries and for major injury types, (2) We quantify and interpret between-study heterogeneity via subgroup and meta-regression analyses and (3) We appraise study quality and the certainty of evidence (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)) to clarify how much confidence policymakers and practitioners can place in the pooled estimates.

Objectives. We aimed to: (1) Estimate pooled incidence and person-time incidence of unintentional injuries among children aged 3–12 years in China, (2) Summarise incidence by injury type (eg, falls, traffic, drowning, burns/scalds, sharp/blunt objects, poisoning, asphyxiation), (3) Explore sources of heterogeneity through predefined subgroup analyses (sex, sample source, age band, sampling method, region, judgement criteria) and meta-regression, (4) Evaluate study quality with an established tool and assess publication bias and (5) Rate the certainty of evidence for primary outcomes using the GRADE approach.

Methods

This study was conducted under the guidance of the PRISMA guidelines. The study’s protocol and plan have been registered in PROSPERO,14 the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration number CRD42024604890; available at: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?ID=CRD42024604890). The certainty of evidence for the primary outcomes was evaluated using the GRADE approach.15

Search strategy

We systematically searched both Chinese and English literature on unintentional injuries among children aged 3 to 12 in China, covering studies published from 1 January 2018 to 30 June 2024, in the CNKI, WanFang and PubMed databases. The search used the Medical Subject Headings terminology to identify relevant subject terms, combining both subject terms and keywords. The Chinese search strategy included the following keywords: injury/unintentional injury/accidental injury/trauma; full text terms: children/kids/kindergarten/preschool children/elementary school students; abstract term: incidence and publication date: from 1 January 2018, to 30 June 2024. The corresponding English search strategy was: (child OR pupil OR ‘Kindergarten student’ OR ‘child, preschool’ OR preschooler) AND (injury OR hurt OR ‘unintentional injury’ OR ‘accidental injuries’ OR accident OR trauma) AND incidence AND (China OR ‘People’s Republic of China’) AND (2018/1/1:2024/6/30)’. The literature type selected was ‘article,’ and the search timeframe spanned from 1 January 2018 to 30 June 2024. Additionally, a literature trace-back method was employed to collect as complete a set of relevant literature as possible. The detailed search strategies are shown in the online supplemental file 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Literature inclusion criteria: (1) The study subjects were Chinese children aged 3 to 12, (2) The study design was observational, (3) The observation period was at least 1 year, (4) Injury definition criteria were reported and (5) The outcome indicator was the incidence of injuries, and the literature reported the occurrence of injuries, the number of samples, the total number of injured individuals and/or person-times of injuries among children aged 3 to 12 in the past year, or the data could be used to calculate the required information.

Literature exclusion criteria: (1) Review articles, commentaries and conference papers, (2) Literature with unavailable data, (3) Studies involving special samples that cannot represent the general population, such as left-behind children or children from migrant worker schools, (4) Literature reporting only one type of injury, (5) Literature based on the same data or duplicated publications and (6) Literature with unclear, incomplete or erroneous information.

Study outcomes indicators

The outcome indicators of this study include the incidence of injuries and the person-time incidence of injuries. The incidence of injuries refers to the ratio of the number of injured individuals to the total population within a unit time (usually 1 year). The person-time incidence of injuries refers to the ratio of the total number of injury events to the total population within the same period. These indicators are commonly used in injury research and surveillance.

The injury case judgement criteria employed in existing literature include 1996, 2004 and other versions. The 1996 version of injury definition criteria states that an event can be classified as an ‘injury’ if it meets any of the following three conditions:16 (1) The individual seeks diagnosis and treatment at a hospital or school clinic, (2) The individual takes rest or leave for half a day or more due to injury or (3) The individual receives emergency treatment or care from parents, teachers or classmates. The 2004 version specifies that an event can be classified as an ‘injury’ if it meets any of the following two conditions:17 (1) The individual goes to a medical institution and is diagnosed with a specific type of injury or (2) The individual takes leave (absent from school or resting) for 1 day or more due to injury. Additional injury definition criteria include two types: (1) Being diagnosed as an injury by a medical facility or taking leave (absent from school or resting) for more than 0.5 days due to injury and (2) Meeting any of the following conditions: (a) Seeking diagnosis and treatment at a hospital or school clinic, (b) Receiving emergency treatment or care from parents, teachers or classmates or (c) Taking leave for more than 1 day due to injury.

Literature screening and data extraction

Initially, duplicates were removed from the search results. Titles and abstracts were then screened based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria to eliminate ineligible studies. The full texts of the remaining studies were subsequently reviewed to confirm eligibility.

During the full-text review process, the following data were extracted: first author, publication year, sample source, sampling method, study region, sample size, participants’ age range and gender ratio, judgement criteria, main outcome indicators and measurement data (including the overall incidence of unintentional injuries and the incidence of different types of unintentional injuries). The literature screening, data extraction and quality assessment were conducted independently by two researchers. Then, the two researchers cross-verified the results. Any disagreements that arose during the assessment process were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third party. All studies included in this review were retrospective and were grouped according to gender, sample source, age group, sampling method, region and judgement criteria.

Assessment of literature quality

This study adopted the quality assessment criteria for disease prevalence/incidence studies proposed by Loney et al18 to evaluate the quality of the included literature. The assessment criteria consist of eight items across three dimensions: methodological validity, rationality of result interpretation and applicability. Each item met is awarded one point, with a total score ranging from 0–8. The higher the score, the better the quality of the literature. Based on the score, the literature is categorised into three levels of quality: low (1–3 points), moderate (4–6 points) and high (7–8 points).

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted using CMA V.3.3 software. The incidence and person-times incidence of unintentional injury were used as combined effect sizes respectively, and heterogeneity was tested by I2 statistic. When p≥0.1 and I2≤50%, the fixed-effects model was employed to combine the data; otherwise, the random-effects model was used. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using the leave-one-out method. Funnel plot, fail-safe number and Egger’s regression analysis were used to assess publication bias. Meta-regression analysis was then conducted to examine the impact of sample size on the effect size of the incidence of unintentional injuries. Additionally, subgroup analysis was conducted to explore the impact of gender, sample source, age group, sampling method, study region and judgement criteria on the effect sizes of unintentional injuries in children.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Literature screening process and results

A total of 886 relevant articles published between 1 January 2018 and 30 June 2024, were retrieved, of which 16 ultimately met the inclusion criteria (see figure 1). The specific steps and results of the literature screening are illustrated in figure 1.

Study characteristics and assessment of risk bias

The 16 included studies were all retrospective surveys. Among these, seven studies used the 1996 version of the injury criteria, three used the 2004 version, and six employed other versions of the injury criteria. The total sample size across all included studies was 37 877 participants, with individual study sample sizes ranging from 373–10 909. Ten studies reported the incidence of unintentional injuries, and six studies reported the person-time incidence of unintentional injuries. In terms of quality, eight studies received a score of 6 points, two studies scored five points and six studies scored four points. The most common issue identified in the published literature was that most of the studies did not report the 95% CI of the incidence of injury, and some studies did not explain the reasons for invalid questionnaires and missing values. Certainty of evidence assessment

Applying the GRADE framework, the certainty of evidence for both the incidence and the person-time incidence of unintentional injuries was judged to be very low. Downgrading decisions were based on: (1) Risk of bias—all studies were cross-sectional with incomplete reporting of missing data handling and variable outcome ascertainment, (2) Inconsistency—very large between-study heterogeneity (I²>90%) that could not be fully explained by subgroup analyses and (3) Imprecision—wide pooled 95% CIs for several outcomes. Publication bias was not downgraded (Egger’s p=0.87; funnel plot symmetric). Details are presented in table 2.

Meta-analysis of effect sizes

The heterogeneity test indicated significant heterogeneity (Q=1829.21, p<0.001; I²=99.13%), necessitating the selection of the random-effects model for analysis. As shown in figure 2, the combined effect size of the incidence of unintentional injuries among children aged 3 to 12 in China was 14.0% (95% CI 9.5% to 20.3%), and the person-time incidence was 15.3% (95% CI 8.6% to 25.8%) (figure 2).

We extracted the incidence of different types of injuries from the included literature and combined the effect sizes, as detailed in table 3. Meta-regression analysis results

Using the sample size of each study as the independent variable, a univariate meta-regression analysis was conducted employing the restricted maximum likelihood estimation. The analysis revealed that larger sample sizes were associated with lower reported incidence of injuries, and this difference was statistically significant (B=–0.0005, 95% CI (–0.0007 to –0.0003), p<0.001) (figure 3).

Subgroup analysis results

Subgroup analyses were conducted on the included studies based on gender, sample source, age group, sampling method, region and judgement criteria, respectively. The results indicated that differences in these factors could lead to variations in effect sizes. According to the data presented in table 4, the incidence and person-time incidence of injuries in males (incidence: 16.1%, person-time incidence: 24.9%) were significantly higher than those in females (incidence: 11.2%, person-time incidence: 17.9%). The incidence of injuries for samples sourced from kindergartens (27.6%) was significantly higher than that from other sources, with childcare institutions reporting the lowest incidence (7.6%). However, the incidence of injuries in primary schools was higher than that in kindergartens. The incidence of injuries for children aged 3–6 years (15.2%) was significantly higher than that for children aged 6–12 years (12.4%). Conversely, the person-time incidence of injuries for children aged 6–12 years (15.7%) was significantly higher than that for children aged 3–6 years (15.0%). Studies using convenience sampling had the highest incidence of injuries (43.4%), but this difference was not statistically significant. Cluster sampling (19.3%) followed, which was significantly higher than other random sampling methods. Studies conducted in central regions reported the highest incidence and person-time incidence (incidence: 20.2%, person-time incidence: 31.9%), though the difference was not statistically significant. The western regions followed (incidence: 14.3%, person-times incidence: 26.5%), and the eastern regions had the lowest rates (incidence: 13.1%, person-times incidence: 9.1%), with statistically significant differences. The incidence of injury for the 1996 version, 2004 version and other criteria was 17.7% (95% CI 8.7% to 32.7%), 15.6% (95% CI 5.5% to 36.9%) and 10.5% (95% CI 4.9% to 20.9%), respectively. The corresponding person-time incidence of injury was 30.6% (95% CI 18.8% to 45.6%), 5.8% (95% CI 2.7% to 12.3%) and 12.0% (95% CI 5.8% to 23.0%), respectively. In summary, differences across subgroups were statistically significant. These results are detailed in table 4.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding each included study and re-merging the effect sizes (see online supplemental figure S1). The results indicated that the effect sizes remained statistically significant even after the exclusion of any single study, and the combined effect values did not change significantly. This suggests that the meta-analysis is not easily influenced by changes in the number of studies, demonstrating low sensitivity and strong robustness. These results support the reliability of the analysis, indicating the conclusions are not affected by any single study.

Assessment of publication bias

The results of Egger’s test showed a t-value of 0.17 and a p value of 0.87, indicating no publication bias. The fail-safe N test yielded a z-value of 98.78. According to the formula (5k+10), where k is the number of studies included (in this case, k=16), the threshold for no publication bias is 90, and since 90 <z-value, it indicates no publication bias. The funnel plot also demonstrated essentially symmetrical on both sides, suggesting a minimal impact from publication bias (see figure 4). Overall, multiple results consistently indicated that publication bias had a minimal effect on the meta-analysis results in this study.

Discussion

The results of this study suggest that the incidence of unintentional injuries among children aged 3–12 in China over the past 5 years is 14% (95% CI 9.5% to 20.3%), and the person-time incidence of injuries is 15.3% (95% CI 8.6% to 25.8%). These figures are lower than the incidence (18.94%) and person-time incidence (24.29%) of unintentional injuries in children aged 0 to 19 reported by Wang Li et al19 This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in data collection timeframes, as Wang Li et al’s meta-analysis covering literature published between 2002 and 2012. Over time, changes in children’s safety awareness and improvements in preventive measures may have influenced the incidence of unintentional injuries. Additionally, the decline in the incidence of unintentional injuries among children may be a result of effective prevention and control strategies implemented in China. The ‘Outline for the Development of Children (2011–2020)’,20 released in 2011, explicitly included the prevention and control of children’s injuries in the national development plan, setting a target to ‘reduce mortality and disability caused by injuries among children, with the mortality rate of children under 18 years decreasing by one-sixth from the base year of 2010’, and has been promoted and implemented nationwide.21 Consistent with findings from multiple studies,22 23 this study finds that falls have the highest incidence rate among childhood injuries, highlighting the need for continued focus on fall prevention while implementing comprehensive injury prevention and control measures.

The subgroup analysis results show that there are differences in the incidence of unintentional injuries among children in China across gender, sample source, age group, sampling method, region and injury definition criteria, which is consistent with many studies.24 25 Given the very high heterogeneity across pooled estimates (I²>99%), these variations are expected and likely reflect differences in injury definitions, sampling frames and regional contexts. We prespecified a random-effects model and performed subgroup, sensitivity and leave-one-out analyses; although pooled point estimates should be interpreted with caution, the direction of findings remained consistent, reinforcing the robustness of key conclusions (eg, falls as the predominant injury type). Boys exhibited a significantly higher incidence of injuries than girls, potentially due to differences in activity levels, curiosity and risk-taking behaviours between genders,26 27 giving boys more opportunities to be exposed to injury risks. Consistent with the findings of Gong Tian et al28 children aged 3 to 6 demonstrated a significantly higher injury incidence than those aged 6 to 12, likely because younger children are at a stage where their cognitive and motor skills are still developing, making them more vulnerable to injuries (eg, falls or collisions during play).29 Future research should explore targeted interventions to reduce the incidence of unintentional injuries across age groups. Furthermore, the incidence of unintentional injuries for children in the western region of China was higher than that in the eastern region, likely due to factors such as economic underdevelopment, inadequate safety infrastructure, weak safety awareness among parents and children, and different natural and geographical environments in the west.25 30 Consistent with previous studies,19 25 this study found that the incidence of injuries using the 1996 version of the injury definition criteria is higher than that using the 2004 version, as the latter is stricter in its criteria for injury diagnosis.16 17 Additionally, this study found that cluster sampling resulted in higher incidence of injuries than other random sampling methods, and the incidence of injuries was significantly higher in samples from kindergartens compared with other sources (eg, communities, childcare institutions, primary schools). These findings suggest that the incidence of injuries is influenced by sampling methods, sample sources and injury definition criteria. Consequently, future research should address sources and injury definition criteria to ensure comparability.

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the incidence of unintentional injuries among children aged 3 to 12. However, there are several limitations. First, all the included studies were cross-sectional studies, which may be subject to recall bias. To improve data accuracy, it is recommended that future studies should adopt cohort study methods and use multiple data sources, such as medical records, school records and unintentional injury surveillance systems. Second, most of the included study subjects were from kindergartens and primary schools, meaning that children who may have dropped out of school due to injuries were not captured, potentially leading to an underestimation of the incidence of unintentional injuries. Future research should conduct community-based studies, including home visits and community surveys, to identify children who are not enrolled in school and understand their health status and injury experiences. Third, the included studies exhibited considerable heterogeneity, partly due to differences in injury definition criteria and factors such as time and region. Future studies should adopt uniform definitions and classification standards for injuries to ensure the comparability of injury data across studies.

In conclusion, the incidence of unintentional injuries among Chinese children aged 3 to 12 is relatively high, particularly for falls. Furthermore, the incidence of unintentional injuries varies significantly based on gender, sample sources, age groups, sampling methods, regions and judgement criteria. Future efforts should particularly focus on kindergarten-aged children in western China and develop injury prevention and control measures and strategies tailored to the region and age group, taking into account the local cultural background, environmental characteristics and level of economic development. Strengthening coordination among various departments is also essential to reduce the incidence of unintentional injuries in children.
