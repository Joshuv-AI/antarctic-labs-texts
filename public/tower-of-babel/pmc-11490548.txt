Effect of costus lucanius bagasse fibre on fresh and hardened concrete using RSM modelling
Authors: Naraindas Bheel, Charles Kennedy, Shahnawaz Zardari, Waleligne Molla Salilew, Abdulrazak H. Almaliki, Omrane Benjeddou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This investigation purposes to use the recycling waste materials in concrete to produce a sustainable environment for construction. The Costus lucanius bagasse fiber (CLBF) was utilized as supplementary cementitious material (SCM) to analyse the workability and mechanical properties of blended cement concrete. The concrete samples were made with the inclusion of 5–20% of CLBF at several w/c ratio by applying RSM modelling and optimization. Moreover, the cubical specimens were cast to study the compressive strength (CS), while beams were used to investigate the flexural strength (FS) on 28th days correspondingly. The outcomes show that the compressive and flexural strengths were decreased with rise in proportion replacement in concrete at various w/c ratio. The highest flexural and compressive strengths was attained at 5% of CLBF at 28 days respectively. Across the tested specimens, the values of compressive strength and flexural strength ranged from 30.20 to 53.92 N/mm2 and 2.15–8.81 N/mm2 for CLBF correspondingly. Besides, the workability and water absorption of concrete is getting decreased as the content of CLBF increases while it is recorded increasing as w/c ratio increases from 0.20 to 0.40. Furthermore, response model predictions were constructed and verified applying ANOVA at an acceptable level of significance of 95%. The coefficients of R2 for the mathematical models ranged from 98 to 99.99%. The research study also indicated that cement can be substituted by CLBF in the production of concrete for various construction purposes.

Introduction

Concrete is widely recognised as one of the most commonly used construction materials worldwide for infrastructure projects, and it is manufactured primarily from the mixing of Portland cement (PC), aggregates, and water1–5. Cement is an imperative constituent in the making of concrete, and its utilization in the construction industry increases daily across the globe6, which as a result, has also put pressure on its purchase cost. In view of cost reduction and proper utilization of some waste materials7, which also has assisted in the managing of environmental pollution8, various agricultural wastes have been investigated as SCM in the production of concrete9–11.

Several agricultural plants, which are found locally, such as banana fiber, plantain leaf, rice husk, palm kernel shell, cassava waste, coconut husk, corn cob, and sugarcane bagasse have been used as SCM for making concrete12–16. They can be pulverized or burnt to ashes. Most of the investigations have revealed that sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA) performed excellently as SCM in concrete production13, 17. Replacement of cement with 5 to 15% of various materials like metakaolin18–20, silica fume (SF)21–23, fly ash24, wheat straw ash25 and SCBA yielded concrete with compressive strength comparable with concrete produced from ordinary Portland cement, and it also increased the workability of concrete26–28. Some authors have also shown that the crushing strength of concrete replaced between 5% and 15% SCBA was greater than concrete with 0% bagasse ash29–34.

Costus englerianus bagasse is a kind of sugarcane bagasse that is often found in natural environments and has more robust fibres compared to standard sugarcane bagasse. It has received little consideration as SCM in construction materials. Therefore, the selection of Costus lucanius bagasse fibre (CLBF) as an additional cementitious material is likely based on many significant criteria. Initially, CLBF can be easily obtained as a by-product of agriculture in the area, which makes it a financially viable and environmentally friendly alternative. The chemical composition of the material, namely its silica concentration, has the capability to augment the concrete’s strength and toughness by promoting pozzolanic activity. The use of a natural and renewable resource, such as CLBF, has substantial environmental advantages. By partly substituting cement manufacturing with this sustainable alternative, it aids in lowering the carbon footprint related with PC manufacturing. In addition, the mechanical characteristics of CLBF might enhance the overall performance of the composite material, providing superior toughness and resistance to cracking. This study demonstrates the effectiveness of CLBF in cementitious applications, offering a scientific rationale for its use. Additionally, cultural or traditional practices may have a significant impact, particularly if CLBF has a historical precedent in the region’s building practices. Besides, SCBA has demonstrated usefulness in enhancing the characteristics and durability of concrete35–37. Malyadri and Supriya38 found that consuming SCBA as SCM was augmented the strength of the concrete by 5%. A comparable outcome was also documented by Mangi et al.39. An appropriate concrete strength can be formed by substituting 5–20% of the ash with sugarcane bagasse29, 40. Additional research has found that sugarcane bagasse is an excellent substitute component for structural concrete13. Furthermore, 5 to 15% SCBA boosted the crushing strength of concrete26–28, while some research scholars reported compressive strength derived from SCBA that was better than cement concrete alone at 5% substitution or greater30, 31, 34.

A significant increment in the various percentages of bagasse ash may cause a decrease in concrete strength41–43. Bhanja and Sengupta44 examined the consequence of SF on the characteristics of concrete. However, the CS, FS and split tensile strength of all the mix proportions were performed at the water–binder ratios of 0.26–0.42 and SF–binder ratios of 0.0–0.3 in composites. Bheel et al.2 reported that the usage of 5% CLBA, 10% CLBA, and 10% CLBF were provided the higher CS and FS as compared to the CS and FS of concrete blended with hydrated lime. As a partial substitution ingredient for making concrete, CLBA was performed better than CLBF. Elahi et al.45 observed the hardened concrete blended with GGBFS, FA, and SF as binary and ternary mechanisms. However, in this research work, the GGBFS was used to 70%, FA consumed to 40% and SF utilized up to 15%. The concrete blended with 7.5% of SF and 50% of FA or GGBFS as ternary cementitious ingredients had the better resistance to chloride diffusion of all the mixtures. For strength development, silica fume (7.5%) outperforms other cementitious constituents. Prasad et al.46 recommended an artificial neural network (ANN) for the prediction of high-performance concrete’s 28-day compressive strength. The greater R2 values designated that the suggested ANN approach was proficient of accurately estimating CS values which were very near to actual measurements. Hassan et al.47 conducted an experimental investigation utilizing SF and FA to evaluate the assets of superplasticizer high strength concrete (10%, 30% by weight of cement). The strength development of the SF concrete was comparable to plain concrete, but with slightly greater values at 1, 3, 7, 28, 365 days. However, concrete reinforced with FA had the smallest compressive strength at initial days, was comparable to OPC concrete on 28 days, and had a higher compressive strength at 365 days. Ghorpade48 conducted experiments on 4 mixes containing 0–1.5% by volume of glass fiber, SF (0%, 10%, 20%, and 30% by mass of the cement), a W/B ratio of 0.35, aggregate/binder ratio of 2.0, and superplasticizer at 1% by mass of cement. The optimal proportion is 1% fiber volume to 10% SF to maximize crushing strength, flexural and split tensile strengths.

Therefore, the main objective of this investigational study is to examine the potential of fresh concrete and evaluate compressive strength and flexural strength of concrete blended with various concentrations of CLBF at various w/c ratio. These parameters were investigated by applying response surface methodology (RSM) as an approach for modelling and optimization. Besides, Costus lucanius is a species of sugarcane (Costus Lacerus) and has not widely gained attention as SCM in concrete. Thus, the study was used to decrease the usage of PC in concrete and produce a sustainable environment by recycling waste in concrete.

After getting bagasse, it dried under the atmosphere for 72 h to eliminate the humidity content. However, the dried bagasse was burnt in the open air to till when it converts into ash and then it was sieved to eliminate undesirable waste materials. The burned ashes were finely processed. The residual component of the dry samples (CLBF) was similarly finely powdered. The finely powdered CLBF were sieved to uniform sizes of 90 μm and kept in sealed containers. Experimental procedures

This experimental work was adopted to prepare concrete samples inclusion with 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% of CLBF as replacement for PC to determining mechanical characteristics concrete mixture. The samples were mixed at a PC content of 550 kg/m3 and water binder ratios of 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4. Three concrete cubes were cast for each mix to test the compressive strength test with dimensions 150 mm x 150 mm x 150 mm, while three concrete beams were cast for each mix to determine the flexural strength test with dimensions 500 mm x100mm x 100 mm. The cubes and beams were completely immersed in the water tank at room temperature. At 28 days, three cubes with 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% cement replacement was randomly selected and tested for CS, while for FS assessment was conducted at 28 days curing. Designing investigations using RSM

The RSM is a straightforward statistical technique used to design, analyze, optimize, and validate experimental outcomes. In the concrete sector, it is frequently employed for the purpose of designing experiments, constructing statistical models, and developing a relationship between responses (dependent variables) and factors (independent variables)49–52. The research applied Expert Design software due to its exceptional skills in constructing optimal experiments involving processes, mixtures, or combinations of elements and components. In addition, the experimental work was designed utilizing a Central Composite Design (CCD) approach with the assistance of RSM. The CCD utilized the w/c ratio and CLBF as the two input values, while the slump test, CS, and FS were regarded as the response variables. The input values for CLBF were restricted to a range of 0–20%, while the w/c ratio was limited to a range of 0.20–0.40. The output characteristics obtained during the CCD utilizing RSM were the slump test, CS, and FS. The statistical technique of RSM was utilized to analyze the slump test, CS, and FS. The importance of the model led to the proposal of linear models and quadratic polynomials for predicting the Hardened concrete. Testing procedure

The slump test was achieved on fresh concrete accumulation with various proportions of CLBF as replacement for PC for measuring the workability of concrete at various w/c ratio by confirming the BS EN 12,35053. However, the hardened states like CS and FS. However, CS assessment was conducted on the cubical samples of concrete inclusion with various percentages of CLBF as PC substitution ingredients in concrete by using the BS EN 1235 part 354 code procedure and these cubical samples were cured at 28 days. Similarly, the prism samples were cast for analysing the FS of concrete with accumulation of different contents of CLBF as SCM in concrete on 28 days by obeying the BS EN 12390-555 standard. Furthermore, the water absorption was performed on CLBF concrete at various w/c ratio after 28 days by using BS 1881: Part 12256 code procedure.

Results and discussion

The performance of CLBF as cementitious material for the making of concrete has been investigated various curing ages and water binder ratios.

Slump test

Figure 2 displays the mixture of concrete with addition of various content of CLBF at 0.20, 0.30 and 0.40 w/c ratio for determining the workability of concrete. The highest slump was noted by 48 mm, 56 mm and 62 mm at 0.20, 0.30, and 0.40 water-cement ratio by using 0% of CLBF while the lowest slump value was observed by 24 mm, 32 mm, and 36 mm at 0.20, 0.30 and 0.40 water-cement ratio at 20% of CLBF as cementitious material individually. The outcome has declared that the consumption of CLBF as SCM rises in concrete which cause drop in workability while the workability of concrete blended with CLBF is improved with increases in the water-cement ratio. This drop in slump is accompanying to the high specific surface area of CLBF which absorb more amount of water as compared to PC while the consumption of CLBF increases in concrete which absorbs high quantity of water than that of PC that results in dropping the workability of concrete. Moreover, utilizing superplasticizers or implementing sophisticated placement procedures may help to alleviate the effects of decreased workability. By carefully managing the workability difficulties, using CLBF in construction might be a feasible choice because of its greater sustainability and better mechanical qualities. Although drop workability is a concern that must be addressed, it does not automatically exclude the use of CLBF in real-world applications. According to the Bheel et al.2, the usage of SCBA rises in concrete which reduces the workability of concrete. Channa et al.57 reported that the workability is getting dropped when the consumption of SCBA rises in concrete. Dayo et al.58 reported that the utilization of SCBA in concrete mixtures is declined the slump flow of concrete. Comparable kind of observation was conveyed by Ige et al.59, Kirshnan60 and Lakshmi et al.61.

Compressive strength (CS)

It was performed on concrete inclusion with CLBF at various percentage replacement and water-cement ratios. The CS of CLBF concrete was associated to ascertain their performances after 28 days. At 28 days, the w/c ratio was kept varies from 0.20, 0.30, and 0.40 while the percentage replacement was varied from 5 to 20% of CLBF. Figure 5 demonstrations the CS of mix formed at 5–20% of CLBF against water-cement ratio at 28 days curing age. From the profiles, the CS is reduced by improving the w/c ratio at 28 days. Thus, from Fig. 5, the CS was observed by 53.92 MPa, 47.43 MPa, 43.08 MPa, 38.43 MPa, and 35.50 MPa while using 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% of CLBF at 0.20% w/c ratio after 28 days correspondingly. Similarly, the CS of concrete combined with 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% of CLBF at 0.30% w/c ratio was recorded by 50.23 MPa, 44.55 MPa, 40.50 MPa, 35.92 MPa, 33.37 MPa after 28 days consistently. Moreover, CS of concrete was noted by 45.74 MPa, 39.87 MPa, 36.62 MPa, 32.68 MPa, 30.20 MPa at CLBF0, CLBF5, CLBF10, CLBF15, and CLBF20 at 0.40 w/c ratio after 28 days respectively. These results exhibited that the performance of the CS concrete was highest at a 0.20 w/c ratio while the CS decreased as the rise in proportion replacement in concrete. From the outcomes, the CS of 5% CLBF concrete was performed slightly greater as compared to other mixes blended with CLBF concrete. This greater CS is accompanying to the fineness of CLBF than that of other ingredients of concrete therefore CLBF seals the spaces left by other components of concrete. Moreover, the reduction of CS with the addition of CLBF in concrete which associated to the dilution effect of CLBF on PC while using in concrete. Meanwhile, the CS of concrete was prepared with only PC (control sample) at the corresponding curing ages was higher than those produced from the CLBF. The CS of concrete can be classified under normal strength concrete (less than 50 N/mm2), according to American Concrete Institute62. Bheel et al.2 itemised that the usage of CLBA used as replacement for PC is offering good results for building work. Kumari and Kumar63 specified that the ingesting of SCBA increases in the concrete mixture was getting compressive strength reduced after every curing period. Channa et al.57 attained investigational study on concrete blended with 5–15% SCBA is recording the decreased CS after 7, 28, 56 and 90 days. A comparable kind of observation was provided by Kishore et al.64 and Khan et al.65.

Flexural strength (FS)

The FS of CLBF concrete was compared at 28 days, with different water binder ratios and different percentage replacements. Figure 8 illustrations the FS was achieved on concrete combined with several contents of CLBF as SCM at 28th days. As shown in Fig. 8, the FS was observed by 8.81 MPa, 6.91 MPa, 5.95 MPa, 5.47 MPa, and 4.85 MPa while using 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% of CLBF at 0.20% w/c ratio after 28 days correspondingly. Similarly, the FS of concrete combined with 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% of CLBF at 0.30% w/c ratio was noted by 6.86 MPa, 5.14 MPa, 4.76 MPa, 4.36 MPa, and 3.77 MPa after 28 days consistently. Moreover, FS of concrete was noted by 6.07 MPa, 3.87 MPa, 3.24 MPa, 2.61 MPa, 2.15 MPa at CLBF0, CLBF5, CLBF10, CLBF15, and CLBF20 at 0.40 w/c ratio after 28 days respectively. Moreover, the optimum FS of CLBF concrete was observed at 5% of CLBF as compared to the other proportions of CLBF at 0.20 to 0.40 w/c ratio after 28 days respectively. The enhanced strength of concrete mixed with CLBF is ascribed to the higher specific surface area of CLBF compared to other concrete elements, which effectively occupies the empty spaces created by the other elements of concrete. Further accumulation of CLBF in concrete, the strength gets dropping. This drop in FS of concrete with accumulation of CLBF as SCM is owing to the clinker effect of CLBF on PC when using in concrete mixture. The range of flexural strength recorded in this study agreed with those reported by some authors for SCBA32, 33, 66 and palm oil fuel ash67, but below the range reported by Rerkpiboon et al.68 for sugarcane bagasse ash. Also, the flexural strength was below those reported by Sofri et al.69 using palm oil fuel ash and Abdulkadir et al.11 using fly ash.

Water absorption (WA)

The WA of CLBF concrete was observed on concrete samples at various w/c ratio after 28 days as revealed in Fig. 13. RSM modelling and optimization
