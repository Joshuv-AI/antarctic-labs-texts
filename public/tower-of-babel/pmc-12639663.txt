Estimating treatment effect in randomised controlled trials with continuous outcomes subject to non-compliance: a logistic regression-based multiple imputation approach
Authors: Ruochen Du, Jialiang Li, Junxian Zhu, Mark Chan, Bee-Choo Tai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Non-compliance in randomised controlled trials poses a significant challenge to accurately estimate treatment effects. Traditional methods of analysis, such as intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses, are known to have limitations. The ITT analysis tends to underestimate the effect of treatment, while PP is prone to selection bias. The Complier Average Causal Effect (CACE) framework has been proposed to address the issue of non-compliance. In this paper, we propose a novel method that assumes the CACE framework based on logistic regression using multiple imputation technique to estimate the unknown compliance status, with a lemma that provides mathematical proof of identification. Its performance is compared with ITT, PP, instrumental variable method and latent class regression method. The performance of these five methods was evaluated via intensive simulations assuming varying compliance rates, sample sizes and effect sizes. Scenarios that account for selection bias are also considered. The simulation results demonstrate that our proposed method has smaller bias and mean squared error, with wider coverage and larger power, even in the presence of significant selection bias. The methods were also compared using data from the JOBS II randomised clinical trial on depression, where the non-compliance rate was 55%. The performance aligned with simulation results and demonstrated the good potential of LMI in estimating treatment effect in RCTs subject to non-compliance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13063-025-09203-z.

Introduction

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are widely considered the gold standard for estimating the effects of treatment in healthcare due to the highest level of evidence they provide [1]. However, there are practical challenges in conducting them, and thus, the results from these trials must be interpreted with caution [2]. One such challenge is non-compliance, where participants may not adhere to their assigned treatment groups. This may result in a violation of the randomisation assumption, leading to biased estimates of the treatment effect [3].

The as-randomised or the intention-to-treat (ITT) approach is commonly employed to report the results of RCTs in the presence of non-compliance [4]. The ITT approach estimates treatment effect based on the group that the participant is randomised to, irrespective of their actual compliance status. It provides a valid statistical test of the null treatment effect hypothesis. However, the ITT approach tends to underestimate the treatment effect when there is non-compliance [5, 6]. While ITT mimics the real-world practice by providing a pragmatic estimate of the effectiveness of treatment, taking into consideration non-compliance, this advantage can be questionable when the study objective is to estimate the efficacy of the treatment that participants actually receive.

Another commonly used method is the per-protocol (PP) analysis [7], which considers only participants who comply with their treatment allocation according to the study protocol. PP analysis aims to assess the treatment effect under the ideal or experimental conditions. However, it introduces selection bias when participants who do not comply have very different baseline characteristics compared to those who comply [8]. For example, participants with certain medical histories may be more susceptible to side effects from treatment, leading to a higher likelihood of non-compliance. As a result, the estimated treatment difference obtained from PP analysis may be confounded by the baseline characteristics.

To address the limitations of ITT and PP approaches, several statistical methods have been developed recently. One applicable method is the complier average causal effect (CACE) framework, originally introduced by Angrist et al. [9]. This framework categorises the participants into four groups based on their compliance to treatment: always-takers (always take treatment regardless of groups assigned to), never-takers (never take treatment regardless of groups assigned to), compliers (only take treatment if assigned to treatment group) and defiers (take opposite actions to assigned groups). Under the CACE framework, the objective is to estimate the treatment effect specifically among the compliers [9–12].

The instrumental variable (IV) method, an approach within the CACE framework, is sometimes used for addressing non-compliance in RCTs with continuous outcomes. The randomised assignment is regarded as an IV for the actual treatment received. The structural equation modelling, which treats the compliance status as a latent variable, has also been proposed [13]. It extends the traditional latent variable in psychometrics by specifying a model with three components: a compliance model, a disease model and a joint model [14, 15]. Additionally, the Stata gsem program has provided a tool for fitting the latent class regression (LCR) model to estimate the CACE [16].

In this paper, we propose a novel two-stage approach based on the CACE framework by treating the compliance status in the control group as a missing value problem. This allows us to differentiate the compliers from the never-takers. The logistic regression with multiple imputation (LMI) is used to impute the noncompliance status in the first stage. Next, the causal effect of treatment on the continuous outcome is analysed based on the imputed data sets. We compare the performance of LMI with ITT, PP and the CACE approaches based on IV and LCR, under various scenarios, including settings that are subject to selection bias, through extensive simulations.

In our methodological development, we focus our research interest specifically on the type of RCTs where non-compliance is one-sided. This is prevalent in studies investigating the effect of novel treatment. In such trials, the new drug or intervention is restricted to the treatment group while the control group is limited to receiving the standard treatment or placebo. A recent systematic review has shown that among the 17 papers examined, 11 studies defined non-compliance in the intervention group only [17]. For example, in a cancer trial assessing the benefit of adding chemotherapy to radiotherapy, only four instances of non-compliance to radiotherapy were noted in the control group, all of which were minor. Conversely, the intervention groups exhibited a more significant incidence of complex non-compliance, with over 38 documented cases [18]. This focus on one-sided non-compliance elucidates its nuanced dynamics and underscores its critical implications for the design and interpretation of RCTs assessing novel interventions.

We also illustrate the performance of these methods using data from the JOBS II randomised trial. The JOBS II trial aimed to assess the efficacy of a mental health intervention in reducing the risks of depression amongst unemployed workers [19]. By applying these methods to real-world data, we aim to provide practical insights to their performance and applicability in addressing non-compliance in RCTs.

Assumption 1 implies that there is no interference between each participant. This assumption is usually not directly testable but is assumed to be plausible by the RCT design. Assumption 2 restricts the effect of assignment on outcome only through treatment received. This is not directly testable as well and requires one to consider the nature of the study design if there are other ways that the assignment can affect the outcome independently (such as participants’ perceived expectations or indirect psychological suggestions from research staff). Assumption 3 on monotonicity can be assured by rigorous RCT double-blind design, so it is more likely to occur in poorly conducted RCTs, open-label or behaviour intervention studies. Assumption 4 is testable empirically by examining the data and checking the participants’ status on treatment received. Finally, assumption 5 relating to randomisation is directly testable by comparing baseline covariates and can be ensured by proper implementation of the randomisation process.

Let Y (D) be the potential outcome of a participant receiving treatment D (under assumption 1). The counterfactual outcomes may be generated for each participant depending on whether he/she has received the treatment. The CACE estimator is E [Y (1) − Y (0) | D (1) − D (0) = 1], namely the expected difference in outcome between treatment and control groups amongst compliers. We consider the one-sided non-compliance situation mentioned above, and the compliance rate is dependent on a set of covariates Xc. Furthermore, to assess the performance in handling selection bias, we allow the mean outcome Y to be dependent on unobserved covariates.

Performance measures

The performance of the above five methods is assessed in terms of bias, mean squared error (MSE), power at the 5% level test and the 95% coverage probability (denoted by CVG). Bias is defined by the difference between the estimated treatment effect and the true effect. Power is the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis that the true effect size is zero. MSE is the sum of variance and the square of bias. The 95% coverage probability is the proportion of simulations that the 95% confidence interval includes the true effect [24, 25].

The number of Monte Carlo simulations is set at 1000. The simulations and other statistical analyses are performed using Stata version 16 [26].

JOBS II trial data set

A total of 502 participants were included in the JOBS II data set, with 167 randomised to control group and 335 to intervention group based on a 1:2 allocation ratio. Only 183 (54.6%) participants complied to the intervention assigned to them. The intervention consisted of seminars on job-search and problem-solving skills and provided inoculation against setbacks. The control group received a booklet briefly describing methods and tips on job-search for self-reading. The primary endpoint was an 11-item scale that measured the level of depression. The mean depression score was - 0.38 (SD = 0.81) for the control group, - 0.46 (SD = 0.76) for the intervention group and - 0.42 (SD = 0.78) for the overall participants. Covariates including age, education, motivate, assertiveness, marital status, economic hardship and race were observed and used to predict the compliance status. The logistic regression results showed that these covariates were significantly associated with the compliance status except for economic hardship and race. The likelihood ratio test was significant with P-value < 0.0001. The Pearson goodness of fit test reported a P-value of 0.21, suggesting the compliance model was a good fit.

Discussion

The CACE has been one of the most commonly applied statistical methods for handling non-compliance in RCTs [27]. Owing to its flexibility, CACE has been applied to various types of study designs and outcomes, including longitudinal studies with time-dependent non-compliance [28], and RCTs with univariate binary response [29], among others. In this paper, we propose a novel CACE-based method, LMI, to estimate treatment effects on continuous outcomes when there is substantial non-compliance. We utilise multiple imputation to estimate the compliance status in the control group. Its performance was compared with traditional ITT and PP methods, as well as IV and LCR, two widely applied CACE-based approaches. Through rigorous simulations, we demonstrated that the proposed LMI approach had smaller bias and MSE, and larger coverage for most of the simulated scenarios, including when compliance rates were low or moderate.

While ITT is often recommended as the primary analysis for pragmatic RCTs, it has been criticised for its tendency to underestimate the true average treatment effects, leading to reduced coverage and power [30–32]. Consistent with these concerns, our simulation results indicated that the performance of ITT was severely compromised for high non-compliance rates and large effect sizes.

PP has been used as an alternative approach to address the issues that may arise from solely relying on the ITT principle [33]. However, several studies have demonstrated that it may be susceptible to selection bias and produce estimates that diverge from the null when there are prognostic differences in the treatment and control groups [34]. In such cases, the PP estimate is often larger than the ITT estimate, which further limits its application [35].

The CACE framework addressed the selection bias concern of the PP approach by comparing only the compliers. The two-stage least squares IV method was first developed for econometric applications and later adopted by biostatisticians for the estimation of causal treatment effects in RCTs [36]. The extension of the CACE framework based on the generalised linear latent and mixed model was first developed by Muthén and was available in Mplus (15) and later in Stata with gllamm and gsem (LCR method) commands [14, 16]. One of the difficulties in applying this method is the complexity in coding and interpretation of the statistical models. The simulation study showed LMI outperformed IV and LCR regardless of the effect sizes, compliance rates and sample sizes. Its performance remained robust even when selection bias was severe. Whereas ITT, PP, and IV tended to underestimate the treatment effect, LCR overestimated it, while the estimate of LMI lay between PP and LCR. This was consistent with the results of the JOBS II data. As compared to LCR, the conceptual interpretation of LMI is straightforward: it includes only the observed compliers in the treatment group and estimates the compliers in the control group by multiple imputation.

The LMI has several limitations due to its assumptions. First, its exclusion restriction assumption states that randomisation to the treatment group does not have any effect on the potential outcome. While this assumption is not verifiable, it is reasonable in double-blind RCTs. However, for open-label trials such as JOBS II, the participants randomised to treatment group may have certain beliefs about the intervention, and the depression score could be affected. In other words, knowing that one is in the treatment group may change an individual’s compliance behaviour, which may violate the exclusion restriction assumption. Additionally, the random treatment assignment assumption presupposes equal proportion of compliers for treatment and control group. This may not hold if non-compliance is involuntary, such as when induced by the side effect of the treatment. Unfortunately, it is not possible to verify the exclusion restriction assumption. Besides, due to the lack of information on the non-compliers, we would not know if an individual’s behaviours were involuntary or not. Hence it is important to assess the design of the trial and consider these assumptions before employing CACE methods and assess the robustness of the performance via sensitivity analyses, if possible. Also, our method leverages the use of logistic regression and multiple imputation to predict the compliance probabilities, and this means that if the predictive model (8) is compromised, the imputation model (9) would not be accurate and in turn the estimation model (10) would be biased. This reliance on model specification is analogous to that in standard imputation approaches for missing data problem. However, the LMI method does not require perfect specification, as it would not be realistic with real-world data. This limitation is shared by LCR and LV methods, and other methods for handling non-compliance in RCT. However, the imputation should perform sufficiently well if the main predictors are included in the model and the proportion of missingness is not extreme [38]. In practice, the simple structure of LMI allows for monitoring the predictive performance of model (8) and varying model specifications accordingly for improvement in prediction. Lastly, the proof of (7) relies on the fact that the estimated compliance indicator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{\mathrm C}$$\end{document}C^, is generated from binomial distribution. The LMI method recodes the indicator using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I(P\left(C=1|X,Z=0\right)>0.5)$$\end{document}I(PC=1|X,Z=0>0.5) for the control group. Here, the binomial distribution is approximated through multiple imputation technique, which inherently carries the limitations associated with this method [37].

To address these limitations, further studies are needed to explore the performance of LMI under special conditions when the assumptions are violated. Furthermore, it may be worthwhile to investigate methods of adjusting for potential bias introduced by the violation of exclusion restriction assumptions [29]. One of the key stages of LMI is the estimation of the association between compliance and covariates. Thus, it would be beneficial to examine the performance of LMI under various degrees of association.

Our study is also limited to situations where the treatment is restricted and available only to the treatment group. Thus, LMI is not applicable to studies with spillover non-compliance or multi-arm trials. Future research to adapt LMI to these types of studies may be necessary.

The application of LMI may have some indications for the design and data collection of RCTs. When non-compliance is anticipated, researchers should take preventive measures and record information on covariates that affect compliance, so that the compliance status could be better estimated for the control group.

In conclusion, we have demonstrated the potential of LMI in estimating the treatment effect of RCTs with continuous outcomes in the presence of non-compliance. It exhibited smaller bias, larger coverage and greater power as compared to ITT, PP, IV and LCR. The LMI may be a useful alternative for researchers in the analysis of RCTs with non-compliance.
