Association Between the Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio and in-Stent Neoatherosclerosis and Plaque Vulnerability: An Optical Coherence Tomography Study
Authors: Ning Gu, Panke Chen, Xi Wang, Changyin Shen, Yi Deng, Jianling Chen, Yi Ma, Shuai Ma, Xingwei Hu, Ranzun Zhao, Bei Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has revolutionized the treatment of patients with coronary heart disease.1 Compared with bare metal stents (BMS), drug-eluting stents (DES) substantially reduce angiographic and in-stent restenosis (ISR).2,3 However, late and very late ISR still occur, which is attributed to in-stent neoatherosclerosis (ISNA), which proceeds more rapidly in DES compared with that of BMS.4,5 Several studies have shown that local and systemic inflammatory responses play an important role in the development of ISNA and are predictors of ISR.6,7 Therefore, a reliable and easy-to-measure biomarker of inflammation is needed to predict ISNA progression.

Neutrophil and lymphocyte counts are obtained during routine peripheral blood tests. An elevated neutrophil count is associated with an inflammatory response, whereas a reduced lymphocyte count is associated with physiological stress.8 Many studies have shown that the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) is a biomarker of the inflammatory response and a promising predictor of disease severity and long-term prognosis in patients with coronary atherosclerosis.9,10

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is a catheter-based, high-resolution vascular imaging technique that shows the detailed structure of the coronary arterial lumen.11 OCT imaging enables precise visualization of the features of ISR within the lumen, identification of the lipid-laden neointima within the stent, and accurate measurement of the thickness of the neointima and fibrous cap.12,13 However, to our knowledge, no studies to date have used OCT to assess the relationship between the NLR and ISNA. Therefore, we analyzed ISNA incidence and in-stent plaque vulnerability in patients with varying NLRs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Patient Characteristics

This retrospective study included 580 patients with ISR following DES implantation who were admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University between July 2018 and November 2022. Excluding 340 patients who did not undergo OCT, 240 patients were included in this study. In addition, 7 patients were excluded from the study owing to a lack of baseline clinical data, 8 were excluded because of poor quality of OCT images, and 9 were excluded because no routine blood test results were available. Eventually, 216 patients with 220 ISR lesions following DES implantation were included in this study (Fig. 1). ISR was defined as stenosis of more than 50% within the stent or within 5 mm of either end of the stent. Based on NLR at admission, the 33rd and 66th percentiles were identified as cutoff points, and all eligible patients were divided into 3 groups: tertile 1 (NLR ≤2.273; n = 72), tertile 2 (2.273 < NLR <3.581; n = 72), and tertile 3 (NLR ≥3.581; n = 72). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (approval no. KLL-2021-323).

Study flowchart of enrollment. According to their NLR tertile level, the patients were divided into 3 groups (tertile 1 [NLR ≤2.273], n = 72; tertile 2 [2.273 <NLR<3.581], n = 72; and tertile 3 [NLR ≥3.581], n = 72).

Data Collection and Angiographic Characteristics

Data on age, sex, medical history, smoking status, and medication use were obtained from hospital electronic medical records. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure >140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure >90 mm Hg, or treatment with antihypertensive medication. Smoking was determined based on whether the patient was a current smoker or had quit smoking within the previous 6 months. Diabetes was diagnosed based on a history of diabetes, medication, a fasting blood glucose level >7 mmol/L, or random blood glucose level >11.1 mmol/L.

The NLR was calculated by dividing the absolute value of neutrophils by the absolute value of lymphocytes. The clinical blood counter used in our hospital is the XN-9000 fully automatic blood analyzer manufactured by Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan. A 2-mL peripheral venous blood sample was obtained from all patients on the day of the angiography after an overnight fast (fasting time >8 hours). Blood samples were analyzed using standard hospital laboratory techniques. Blood investigations included analyses of routine blood indicators (white blood cells, neutrophils, hemoglobin, lymphocytes, and platelets), blood biochemistry indicators (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, total and direct bilirubin, blood urea nitrogen, and estimated glomerular filtration rate), lipid indicators (total cholesterol [TC], triglycerides [TG], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C]), and N-terminal pro-B–type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP).

The patients in this study underwent coronary angiography in the catheterization laboratory of the Department of Cardiology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University. Experienced interventional cardiologists used catheters inserted through the radial or femoral arteries and obtained multiple matched angiographic views for each patient after intracoronary injection of nitrates. Coronary angiographic images were collected and analyzed by interventional cardiologists using Syngo AX software (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The angiographic pattern of ISR was assessed according to the Mehran classification14: type I, focal (≤10 mm); type II, diffuse in-stent (>10 mm, confined to the stent); type III, proliferative (>10 mm, beyond the stent edge); and type IV, complete occlusion.

OCT Acquisition and Assessment

OCT is an intravascular imaging technique that uses near-infrared light. After intracoronary nitrates were delivered, OCT was performed using a frequency-domain OCT imaging system (ILUMIEN OPTIS Intravascular Imaging System; St. Jude Medical, St. Paul, MN). An OCT imaging catheter (Dragonfly Duo; St. Jude Medical) was advanced along the guidewire beyond the stent segment, and contrast was injected at a rate of 3–5 mL/sec through the catheter to flush blood from the target vessel. An OCT pullback was then performed on the entire stent segment. The OCT images were analyzed by 2 experienced reviewers who kept the patients' clinical information confidential. A third independent investigator was invited to obtain consensus in cases of disagreement between the 2 reviewers.

OCT Analyses

An OCT analysis software (LightLab Imaging, Inc., Westford, MA) was used to perform quantitative and qualitative analyses of the entire stent segment at 1-mm intervals. For qualitative analysis, the presence of any target feature on the OCT vessel cross-section was considered a positive feature for that lesion. There are 3 types of OCT-guided restenosis histology15: (1) homogeneous, with homogeneous high-signal bands without backscatter patterns; (2) heterogeneous, with mixed-signal bands showing various backscatter patterns; and (3) layered, with superficial signal bands and deep low-signal bands around the stent beam. ISNA was defined as neointima with lipids or calcification at the stent implantation site.16 Thin-cap fibroatheromas (TCFAs) were defined as lipid-containing neointima, with fibrous caps having a minimum thickness of ≤65 µm.17

For quantitative analysis, the structural features of the plaque and multiple lumen area features of ISR were the primary indicators, including the following: minimum stent area, minimum stent diameter, minimum lumen area, minimum lumen diameter, neointimal hyperplasia (NIH) area, and NIH% ([stent area−lumen area] × 100%/stent area). The distal and proximal reference areas were defined as the areas of maximum canal lumen within 5-mm distal and proximal to the stenosis within the same segment, respectively. The area of the stent was measured at the midpoint of the connecting strut. If the stent strut could not be observed, it was clarified by comparing adjacent slices. Statistical Analyses

Normally distributed data are expressed as means (SDs); one-way analysis of variance was used to compare NLR tertiles, non-normally distributed data are presented as medians (interquartile ranges), and nonparametric tests were performed to compare differences between groups. Categorical data are expressed as frequencies (%), and χ2 tests were performed to detect differences. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to analyze the associations between the NLR and ISNA or TCFA. Model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; model 3 was adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and chronic cardiac insufficiency; and model 4 was further adjusted for leukocytes, hemoglobin, platelets, neutrophil absolute value, lymphocyte absolute value, NT-proBNP, ejection fraction (EF), creatinine clearance, TG, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, apolipoprotein A1, NIH ≥50, stent implantation time, and minimum lumen area. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). A P-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Post hoc comparisons were made using the Bonferroni correction if the overall test was significant (P < 0.05). When the Bonferroni correction was used, the significant P-value in the subcomparison was <0.017. We have calculated a statistical power of 0.913 under the current sample sizes using PASS 15 software (NCSS, Kaysville, UT) based on ISNA as the primary outcome indicator.

RESULTS

Clinical Characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the patients included in this study are summarized in Table 1. A total of 216 patients with 220 ISR target lesions were included. The mean age of the patients was 63.3 years, and 80.09% of them were male. No significant differences were observed between the NLR tertiles in terms of sociodemographics, medical history, or medication use. The main baseline differences among the 3 groups were in the results of laboratory tests. Leukocyte and neutrophil counts, levels of direct bilirubin, TC, LDL-C, and NT-proBNP increased progressively as the NLR increased (P < 0.05). By contrast, TG levels gradually decreased as the NLR increased (P < 0.05).

Table 3 summarizes the differences in the distribution of tissue patterns among the 3 groups in ISR measured using OCT. DISCUSSION

This study retrospectively analyzed the characteristics of ISR following DES implantation using OCT and assessed the relationship between the NLR, ISNA, and plaque vulnerability. Greater levels of inflammation were found in patients with ISNA; an elevated NLR was an independent risk factor of ISNA and in-stent TCFA in patients with ISR. Our findings suggest that the routinely calculated NLR may provide important information for the prediction of ISNA in patients with ISR, enabling early intervention. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use OCT to investigate the relationship between the NLR and ISNA in patients with ISR.

PCI with stenting has become the main treatment for atherosclerotic coronary artery disease.18 Nevertheless, late ISR and in-stent thrombosis can lead to stent failure and seriously affect the patient prognosis.19 Increasing pieces of clinical and pathological evidence suggest that ISNA is an important factor in late ISR and in-stent thrombosis20,21; ISNA is also strongly associated with major adverse cardiac events after stent implantation.20,22 Previous studies have demonstrated that during the development of atherosclerosis, macrophages phagocytose large amounts of lipids, becoming foam cells. As foam cells accumulate, the necrotic core expands, leading to the formation of TCFA and eventually plaque rupture.23 ISNA has a similar pathological process to that of de novo atherosclerosis, with typical fragile morphological features such as a necrotic core, intraplaque hemorrhage, and a thin fibrous cap infiltrated by foam cells.16 Patients with plaques with longer lipid lengths, wider lipid arcs, and higher stenosis are considered to have a higher risk of future coronary events.24,25 Therefore, exploring the causes of ISNA after stent implantation may help prevent late ISR and improve the long-term prognosis of patients after PCI.

The inflammatory response plays an important role in the development of ISNA and can predict the occurrence of adverse events after stent implantation.26 Stent implantation leads to cytokine activation, releasing large amounts of inflammatory factors that adhere to monocytes and neutrophils, ultimately creating a local “inflammatory storm”.27 With the release of stent medication, endothelial cell proliferation is inhibited and the healing of the injured site is delayed, which can lead to a chronic inflammatory response.28 In summary, long-standing chronic inflammation, which plays an important role in the development of ISNA, is present throughout the process of coronary artery injury and repair after stenting and ultimately leads to late adverse cardiovascular events. Increased levels of inflammatory mediators or biomarkers can predict the formation and development of ISNA.29 Therefore, to enable early detection and intervention, a simple and readily available indicator of inflammation is needed to predict ISNA and plaque vulnerability.

Many studies have found high monocyte and low lymphocyte counts combined with the NLR to be a composite parameter of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress in vivo.30 The NLR can be used as a biological marker for levels of inflammation and oxidative stress.31 Previous studies have consistently shown a significant correlation between the NLR and coronary atherosclerotic heart disease. A retrospective study including 4110 patients with unstable angina showed a linear positive correlation between the NLR and SYNTAX scores, suggesting that the NLR can predict the severity of coronary artery lesions.32 Another study showed that the NLR was independently associated with in-hospital mortality in patients with acute coronary syndrome with higher in-hospital mortality in patients with acute coronary syndrome and a high NLR.33 In addition, the NLR is considered to be a predictor of long-term prognosis and death in patients with coronary heart disease.34 A previous study showed that the NLR may also reflect coronary microvascular dysfunction in patients with type 2 diabetes.35

However, to our knowledge, no studies have compared the relationship between different NLRs and the plaque characteristics of de novo atherosclerosis. In our study, high-resolution OCT was used for the first time to better identify neointimal tissue within the stent. The results of our study illustrate the association of the NLR with ISNA incidence and plaque vulnerability in patients with ISR. The mechanism underlying the association between the NLR and ISNA may be related to the inflammatory response and oxidative stress; the inflammatory response plays an important role in the development of ISNA. Studies have shown that systemic markers of inflammation, such as C-reactive protein levels, are positively correlated with the NLR.36 Further studies have shown that C-reactive protein levels are associated with the development of ISNA.29 The mechanism by which the NLR reflects the progression and vulnerability of ISNA may be related to the fact that neutrophil counts reflect and contribute to inflammation, releasing oxidative factors and prothrombotic substances, leading to endothelial damage and platelet aggregation. By contrast, lymphocytes can stimulate the secretion of anti-inflammatory factors by monocytes, and a decrease in lymphocyte numbers reflects increased levels of oxidative stress. Thus, the NLR combines the inflammatory response of neutrophils and the oxidative stress response of lymphocytes and can be used as a predictor of ISNA progression and plaque vulnerability after PCI.

Our study revealed an association between the NLR and ISNA and plaque vulnerability; however, the exact mechanism of action requires further validation. Our study provides a new direction for exploring the pathogenesis of ISNA and predicting its progression. This may contribute to the early diagnosis of ISNA and provide a theoretical foundation for the prevention of ISNA progression and early intervention.

Limitations

Our study had several limitations, and the conclusions should be interpreted with caution. First, this was a retrospective study conducted at a single center, and the sample size was relatively small. Therefore, the generalizability of the results to the population is a concern. Second, this study was limited to patients with ISR who underwent OCT. Not all patients with ISR underwent OCT because of economic factors, leading to selection bias. Third, this was a cross-sectional study, and we were unable to distinguish the time at which the NLR increased in relation to ISR; therefore, the results are not sufficient to prove a causal relationship between the NLR and ISNA. Therefore, only 1 phenotype was observed in this study, and a prospective cohort study with a large sample size is needed to validate the relationship between the NLR and ISNA.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, the findings of our study showed that an increased NLR was associated with ISNA and in-stent plaque fragility in patients with ISR following DES implantation. The NLR can be used as a predictive biomarker for the occurrence of ISNA.
