A Nationwide Assessment of Anticholestatic Therapy Uptake in Patients With Primary Biliary Cholangitis: Opportunities for Optimisation

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOptimal care delivery for primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) has become challenging due to new therapies and more individualized biochemical treatment goals.AimTo evaluate uptake of ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) and second‐line therapy (SLT) in a nationwide PBC cohort and identify factors associated with suboptimal therapeutical management.MethodsThe Dutch PBC Cohort Study is a retrospective study including every identifiable patient with PBC in the Netherlands from 1990 onwards. Use of UDCA and SLT was assessed among patients in clinical follow‐up on 1 January 2019. Biochemical response was defined as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) ≤ 1.5 × ULN and total bilirubin (TB) ≤ ULN. SLT included off‐label fibrates, budesonide and/or obeticholic acid (not reimbursed).ResultsIn total, 2638 patients with PBC were included; median age was 64.0 (IQR 55.0–72.0) years, 2361 (89.5%) were female and 2143 (81.2%) were treated in a non‐academic center. Overall, 2532 (96.0%) patients used UDCA. The recommended UDCA dose of ≥ 13 mg/kg/day was used by 1363/2205 (61.8%). Age (aOR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.02, p = 0.02), body weight (aOR 1.04, 95% CI 1.03–1.05, p < 0.01) and biochemical response (aOR 1.35, 95% CI 1.09–1.67, p = 0.01) were associated with UDCA dosage < 13 mg/kg/day. Among patients with an incomplete response, 101/638 (15.8%) were prescribed SLT. Younger age, female sex, treatment in an academic centre and higher levels of ALP and AST were positively associated with use of SLT.ConclusionsDuring recent years, UDCA was insufficiently dosed in Dutch patients with PBC. The results indicate that first‐line treatment can be optimized, especially in those with a biochemical response and higher body weight.Keywords: primary biliary cholangitis, second‐line therapy, ursodeoxycholic acid

Introduction

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a rare cholestatic immune‐mediated liver disease. The most recent pooled global incidence rate of PBC was 1.97 per 100.000 persons/year, with a prevalence of 19.89 per 100.000 persons [1]. In the Netherlands, based on the nation‐wide perspective on the PBC population, the most recent (2018) estimates with respect to incidence and point prevalence were 1.74 per 100.000 persons/year and 21.53 per 100.000 persons, respectively [2].

As a result of liver disease progression, patients with PBC are at risk of an impaired survival [3]. Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) in a dosage of 13–15 mg/kg/day is the internationally recommended first‐line therapy and has been proven to reduce alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and to delay fibrosis development [4, 5]. More recently, UDCA was associated with improved survival free of liver transplantation [6]. This association depends on the dosage of UDCA, with better outcomes in patients treated with a daily dose of at least 13 mg/kg as compared to those with a dose below 13 mg/kg/day. Nevertheless, the clinical outcome remains impaired in a substantial group of patients who have an incomplete biochemical response to UDCA [6, 7]. These patients may benefit from add‐on second‐line therapy to optimize their prognosis [6, 8, 9, 10].

Following the progress in the field, PBC management has evolved. Due to new treatment options and more individualized biochemical treatment goals, optimal care delivery for PBC patients has become challenging [8, 11, 12]. At the same time, the rarity of the disease could limit physicians to gain substantial clinical experience. Many patients are managed outside of expert centers [13]. Although audit data are scarce, recent assessments in the UK and Germany indeed showed there is room to improve the therapeutic management for those affected by PBC [14, 15, 16].

Over the recent years, a national collaboration among all hospitals in the Netherlands resulted in an extensive clinical dataset including every identifiable patient with PBC in the Dutch health care system, making this Dutch PBC cohort highly suitable for the evaluation of real‐world uptake of anticholestatic drug therapy. The aim of this study was to evaluate the use of optimal UDCA dose and second‐line therapy among patients with PBC and to identify factors which are associated with suboptimal drug therapy.

Methods

Study Design and Population

The Dutch PBC cohort study (DPCS) is a nation‐wide retrospective cohort study in which every identifiable patient with an established diagnosis of PBC in the Netherlands was included, regardless of the date of diagnosis. The study was conducted in all 71 hospitals in the Netherlands. With use of an electronic case record form, data were collected following clinical chart review by two medical doctors between 2019 and 2023. During this time period, each centre was visited for structured case identification of individuals with PBC and subsequent clinical data collection from the date of diagnosis up to the date of data collection. Details about case identification were previously described [2].

For this study, individuals with PBC were eligible for inclusion if they were under clinical follow‐up in a Dutch hospital on 1 January 2019, as data collection started shortly after this date. By using 1 January 2019 as a reference date, it prevents bias due to varying data collection periods and ensures comparability of persons receiving clinical care across all sites. People were included regardless of their date of diagnosis (Figure 1). Patients who underwent a liver transplantation prior to the reference date were excluded.

People were included if they were under clinical follow‐up on 1 January 2019, and had been diagnosed prior to 1 January 2017, to ensure a minimum UDCA treatment period of 1 year (up to 1 January 2018) as well as a window of 1 year thereafter to evaluate the biochemical response to UDCA and the need for add‐on second‐line therapy. After 1 January 2019, clinical data were collected of individuals with PBC from date of diagnosis up to the date of data collection.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Assessment Committee (METC) of the corresponding centre and approved by the research board of each participating centre, in accordance with their local regulations.

Data Collection

Collected clinical data included sex, age at diagnosis, antimitochondrial antibody status, body weight and details on the use of PBC treatments, including dosage and duration.

Presence of cirrhosis was based on histological evaluation, on a transient elastography result ≥ 15 kPa (based on Corpechot et al. 2022 [17], Lam et al. 2024 [18]) or on a minimum of two of the following signs of cirrhosis and/or portal hypertension: radiology (surface nodularity, caudate hypertrophy, splenomegaly), laboratory (thrombopenia, hypoalbuminemia), upper endoscopy (gastroesophageal varices) and/or ascites. Liver‐related laboratory values were registered once yearly, among which ALP, total bilirubin and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were included for these analyses.

Statistical Analyses

The use and dosage of UDCA (in mg/kg/day) were assessed for all patients on 1 January 2019 (Figure 1A). On this reference date, use of any second‐line therapy was assessed as well, but only among patients without PBC‐autoimmune hepatitis variant syndrome (either as reported and treated by the local physician or in case the Paris criteria were met). Evaluation of second‐line therapy was performed for individuals with at least 12 months of follow‐up on 1 January 2018. This methodology allowed a time‐interval of at least 12 months for physicians to initiate second‐line therapy on 1 January 2019 (Figure 1B). Second‐line therapy included the off‐label use of agonists of the peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor (PPAR; either bezafibrate, ciprofibrate or gemfibrozil), budesonide and/or obeticholic acid (OCA). At present, the new PPAR‐agonists elafibranor and seladelpar are not reimbursed in the Netherlands, and thus not available. Obeticholic acid has not been reimbursed in the Netherlands and was only available for a small number of patients following clinical trial participation. The role of budesonide in PBC remains debated. Current practice favours optimizing UDCA and adding OCA (if available) and/or PPAR agonists [8]. Nonetheless, budesonide is included as second‐line therapy in this study due to real‐world practice.

In order to calculate the UDCA dosage in mg/kg/day, the patient's most recent recorded weight was considered. Based on the World Health Organization estimate of an average annual weight gain of 400 g, a sensitivity analysis was performed in which body weight was adjusted for the time between the last known measurement and 1 January 2019 [19, 20]. In addition, a last observation assessment was performed as a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the use of both UDCA and SLT after 1 January 2019.

Laboratory data are presented in times the upper limit of normal (ULN) which were site, time, and sex specific. The last laboratory data preceding the reference date have been assessed to determine the biochemical response (to UDCA) (Figure 1). Biochemical response was defined according to the Paris‐II criteria (ALP and AST ≤ 1.5 × ULN and total bilirubin ≤ ULN).

To analyse differences in type and size of hospitals, hospitals were categorized into academic (n = 8, hospitals affiliated with a university) and non‐academic hospitals (n = 63), as well as by volume. Volume was classified as small, medium or large, based on the 33rd and 66th percentile of the number of patients with PBC being managed in each hospital in 2019.

Complete case analyses were performed. Continuous variables are described using medians and Interquartile Ranges (IQR). Categorical data are presented as counts and proportions. Differences between independent groups are assessed by the Chi‐square test in case of categorical data. Student's t and Mann–Whitney‐U tests were used for differences in continuous data between groups. Logistic regression models were fitted to evaluate which factors were associated with a UDCA dosage of at least 13 mg/kg/day as well as to evaluate which factors were associated with the prescription of second‐line therapy. Results were reported in Odds Ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The cutoff of at least 13 mg/kg/day was chosen for its association with improved outcomes compared to a dose below 13 mg/kg/day [6]. For the statistical analysis, IBM SPSS Statistics v28.01 was used.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

In total, 2638 individuals with PBC who received in hospital‐based care on 1 January 2019 were included. Among the 71 treatment centres, the number of patients in care ranged from 7 to 101 patients (median 37, IQR 19–47). Of these patients, 2143 (81.2%) were treated in non‐academic hospitals and 495 (18.8%) in academic hospitals. Table 1 describes the characteristics of the study population; 2361 (89.5%) patients were female and the median age was 64.0 years (IQR 55.0–72.0).

UDCA Dosage

On 1 January 2019, 2532 (96.0%) patients used UDCA for a median of 7.6 years (IQR 3.1–12.9). Of the 106 patients not taking UDCA, 58 (54.7%) had discontinued UDCA and 48 (45.3%) patients never used UDCA. Of those who never used UDCA, 28/48 (48.3%) started UDCA after 1 January 2019. Among the 2205 (87.1%) patients with an available body weight, the median dosage of UDCA was 13.8 mg/kg/day (IQR 12.0–15.3). A dosage of at least 13 mg/kg/day was used by 1363 (61.8%). Overall, the UDCA dosage on 1 January 2019 was the prescribed dosage for a median of 4.5 years (IQR 1.8–9.1). In total, 1483/2532 (58.6%) of people using UDCA never had any dose change. This was 508/842 (60.3%) in those who were prescribed a dosage < 13 mg/kg/day at 1 January 2019. Of the 842 (38.2%) patients with a dose < 13 mg/kg/day, 134 (15.9%) previously had a dose reduction from a dose of ≥ 13 mg/kg/day. Compared to January 2019, 59/836 (7.1%) changed from a dosage < 13 mg/kg/day to a dosage of at least 13 mg/kg/day at the end of follow‐up (median 2021, IQR 2020–2021). In addition, among the newly diagnosed patients after 1 January 2019, the UDCA dose at the end of follow‐up was at least 13 mg/kg/day in 257/383 (67.1%) individuals. Combined, at the last observation, 2998/3086 (97.1%) people used UDCA of whom 1691/2689 (62.9%) individuals were prescribed a daily dose ≥ 13 mg/kg.

In a sensitivity analysis, in which the patient's body weight was increased by 400 g per year up to the date of evaluation, the median dose of UDCA was 13.2 mg/kg/day (IQR 11.5–14.7), with 1106 (53.5%) patients using at least 13 mg/kg/day on 1 January 2019.

With respect to the type of hospital, a dosage of ≥ 13 mg/kg/day was prescribed to 1063 (60.4%) patients treated in non‐academic hospitals versus 300 (67.4%) in academic centres (p = 0.006). When focusing on centre volume, the numbers of patients with a dose ≥ 13 mg/kg/day in low, medium and large volume centres were 135 (51.9%), 473 (62.6%) and 755 (63.5%), respectively (p = 0.002). These results remained when focusing on the difference in volume of the non‐academic centres alone: 114 (50.9%) patients, 376 (62.4%) patients and 573 (61.4%) patients used an UDCA dosage of ≥ 13 mg/kg/day in the low, medium and large volume non‐academic centres, respectively (p = 0.007).

Among those with a UDCA dosage < 13 mg/kg/day, 331 (42.7%) patients had a normal ALP level and 433 (66.9%) met Paris II criteria. In comparison, among those with a dose ≥ 13 mg/kg/day, 454 (35.4%) had a normal ALP level and 638 (57.6%) met Paris II criteria (p < 0.01 for both). Table 2 presents the factors associated with an UDCA dosage of < 13 mg/kg/day. In a multivariable regression model, age on 1 January 2019 (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.02, p = 0.02), body weight (OR 1.04, 95% CI 1.03–1.05, p < 0.01) and biochemical response to UDCA according to the Paris II criteria (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.09–5.1.67, p = 0.01) remained statistically significantly associated with a UDCA dosage < 13 mg/kg/day. Similar results were seen when evaluating the UDCA dosage at the last observation (Table S1). When categorizing patients according to these factors, at least 30% were prescribed a dosage of < 13 mg/kg/day except for those with a bodyweight of below 60 kg (Figure 2). When centre volume was stratified at the 20th percentile instead of tertiles to increase statistical power, patients in the 20% of centres with the lowest volumes were statistically significantly more often prescribed a UDCA dosage < 13 mg/kg/day (adjusted OR 1.85, 95% CI 1.19–2.86, p = 0.007).

Second‐Line Therapy

On 1 January of 2019, the use of second‐line therapy was assessed in 2004 patients. Second‐line therapy was used by 117 (5.8%) of whom 3 (2.6%) did not use UDCA. An additional 44 (2.2%) patients had started but stopped second‐line therapy before that date. Overall, 103 (5.1%) patients had started a PPAR agonist, 49 (2.4%) had started budesonide, and 26 (1.3%) had started OCA prior to 1 January 2019. Similar results were found in a last observation assessment in which 309/2589 (11.9%) were ever prescribed SLT.

With respect to biochemical evaluation, 638 (34.3%) of the 1858 patients with an available ALP, AST and total bilirubin measurement in 2018 did not meet Paris II criteria after at least 12 months of follow‐up. Among these patients, which may be considered to have an indication for second‐line therapy, 101 (15.8%) were using or had previously used second‐line therapy on 1 January 2019. This was 146/720 (20.3%) in the last observation assessment. In the non‐academic setting, 42/482 (8.7%) patients with an inadequate response were prescribed second‐line therapy versus 59/156 (37.8%) in the academic setting (p < 0.001). When focusing on centre volume, the number of these patients in low, medium and large volume centres was 7 (10.3%), 28 (14.0%) and 66 (17.8%), respectively (p = 0.203).

Factors which were independently associated with the use of second‐line therapy among those with an incomplete response according to the Paris II criteria included age, sex, type of centre and the level of ALP and AST (Table 3 and Table S1 for the last observation assessment). Replacing type of centre with centre volume yielded comparable results. Centre volume was not statistically significantly associated with the use of second‐line therapy, either when divided into tertiles (p = 0.847) or when stratified according to the 20th percentile (p = 0.111).

Discussion

This nationwide real‐world cross‐sectional assessment on the uptake of anticholestatic drug therapy in individuals with PBC showed that 96% was treated with UDCA. In fact, less than 1% of the Dutch PBC population had never been prescribed UDCA. This finding is reassuring since UDCA therapy has recently been associated with a markedly improved survival free of liver transplantation in patients with PBC [6]. Yet, our assessment also indicated that UDCA treatment can still be improved. As many as 38% of the patients were using a UDCA dosage below the minimally recommended 13 mg/kg/day. Older patients and patients with higher body weight had an increased risk of a suboptimal UDCA dose, indicating that physicians should monitor the body weight of their patients and adapt the UDCA dose over time. Second‐line therapy was only used by a minority of PBC patients at the time of this evaluation, even though this assessment was restricted to those with an incomplete biochemical response after at least 12 months of follow‐up.

The UDCA treatment rates in the Netherlands can be considered very high, and were in line with a recent report from Germany in which all patients with PBC were treated with UDCA [16]. However, cohort studies from the US and UK reported UDCA treatment rates ranging from only 72.8 to 88%, respectively [14, 15, 21]. While the momentum to refine the indication for second‐line therapy in PBC is increasing, the international community should not forget to focus on improving the uptake and/or optimization of UDCA as a safe, affordable, and clinically effective first‐line treatment option.

In the last decades, there has been a worldwide rise in body weight, overweight and obesity [22, 23, 24]. Specifically, in those aged above 50 years, more than half of the population is now overweight [23]. In our cohort, higher body weight was associated with a suboptimal dosage of UDCA (below 13 mg/kg/day). We could not identify the reason for this finding, but we hypothesize that patients have gained weight over time while their UDCA dosage remained stable if they were not already started on a suboptimal UDCA immediately. It is important to keep track of the body weight of an individual patient and adjust the dosage when indicated. Only a minority (n = 134) reduced their UDCA dosage from an adequate dose to < 13 mg/kg/day, perhaps because of side effects. Substantial side effects are, however, infrequent. Prior to reducing the daily dosage of UDCA, we would recommend splitting the daily dose into two administrations or switching from UDCA manufacturer. In our clinical experience, this has successfully kept patients on their recommended daily UDCA dosage.

In line with the positive narrative on biochemical responders in the recent literature, a complete biochemical response to UDCA may not trigger physicians to critically evaluate their drug therapy. Indeed, our analyses showed that patients with a complete response according to the Paris II criteria more frequently had suboptimal UDCA dosages below 13 mg/kg/day. Patients with a Paris II response may represent a group with favourable outcomes, but this does not necessarily mean that these patients should not be treated with a daily UDCA dosage of at least 13 mg/kg/day. While suboptimal dosages still improve clinical outcomes, the clinical benefit of UDCA in terms of survival was substantially greater in the case of adequate daily dosages ≥ 13 mg/kg/day (HR 0.50 versus 0.26, respectively), irrespective of the on‐treatment biochemistry [6]. In addition, we recently learned that ALP normalization rather than an ALP below 1.5 × ULN (as incorporated in the Paris II criteria) may be the preferred biochemical treatment goal, at least for younger patients and/or those with signs of hepatic fibrosis [11, 12]. Furthermore, it is relevant to realize that multiple mechanisms of action contribute to the positive effect of UDCA, which goes beyond reduction in cholestasis alone [25]. The clinical relevance of these additional effects of UDCA was suggested by a follow‐up analysis by Harms et al., in which PBC patients without an ALP or bilirubin improvement after 1 year of UDCA still had a statistically significantly better survival as compared to untreated patients [6, 26]. Thus, considering the arguments above as well as the negligible incremental costs of up‐titrating the UDCA dose to ≥ 13 mg/kg/day, we consider this to be relevant for all patients with PBC.

Less than 6% of patients with PBC were prescribed second‐line therapy, which was assessed in those with at least 1 year of follow‐up on 1 January 2018. Considering the real‐world perspective of our study, this methodology allowed for a 12 months time interval to evaluate UDCA response and take required action. The low proportion with second‐line therapy was not explained by a favourable biochemical profile with UDCA alone. Among the subgroup of patients who did not meet the Paris II criteria in 2018, only 15.8% received second‐line therapy. Although it was reassuring that higher levels of ALP and/or AST were associated with prescription of second‐line therapy, as these patients are in most need of add‐on therapy, the numbers are too low and need to be improved. However, it should be noted that OCA has not been available in the Netherlands due to reimbursement restrictions. Also, the timing of the current evaluation may have influenced the results. The first landmark randomized controlled trial evaluating bezafibrate in patients with an incomplete response to UDCA was only published in the year of our assessment [27]. In PBC, however, bezafibrate remains off‐label use. Healthcare professionals across the Netherlands may not have been familiar and/or at ease with off‐label prescribing at that time which may explain the therapeutic inertia. Our last observation assessment indeed shows an increase in the prescription of SLT, but this improvement is rather small. Therefore, we consider that the clinical implications of the findings of our study are also relevant for the situation today. The timing of our primary analyses may have contributed to a possible centre bias, with better uptake in more specialized centers. We recently showed that the use of bezafibrate exponentially increased after 2019 and therefore, re‐evaluation of more recent second‐line therapy use would be relevant [28]. New data collection within the Dutch PBC cohort study is currently ongoing.

Our results suggest that medical management, especially with respect to the UDCA dose, may be related to clinical experience with PBC. This was, however, only crudely assessed based on centre‐specific data rather than on physician‐related parameters. These results contribute to previous studies in the UK and the US which showed that dedicated hepatologists felt more competent in their understanding of PBC management compared to general gastroenterologists [14, 29]. Still, differences were small and also the largest centres did not meet the EASL's clinical standard of 90% of patients on the adequate UDCA dose [8]. These results thus indicate that further educational efforts are relevant and that implementation of tools to optimize care delivery may be worthwhile [15, 30, 31].

One of the strengths of our study is the nationwide perspective, giving us a reliable view on general PBC management with maximally detailed data as possible on UDCA dosage. Also, in contrast to recent studies, in which data were reported by the local centres, we evaluated clinical data which were extracted from electronic patient records by dedicated physicians of the study team [14, 15, 16]. As a result, however, this time‐consuming methodology delayed assessments. A limitation is therefore that full data for the entire Dutch PBC population was available in 2019 at latest, with variable last follow‐up for each centre thereafter. However, we found only a few dosage adjustments after 2019. The last observation assessment also indicated similar UDCA dose estimates, suggesting that our conclusion is still valid and relevant for PBC care today. The retrospective design of the study also results in some limitations. For instance, body weight close to 1 January 2019 was frequently missing, so that we had to rely on the latest reported weight in the medical chart. Considering that people tend to gain weight during their adult life, we have included a sensitivity analysis to model the average weight gain according to WHO estimates as the main estimates could underestimate inadequate UDCA use. While this modelling assessment indicates that almost half of the individuals with PBC have a suboptimal UDCA dosage, it should be acknowledged that we do not account for individual weight trajectories. Further, where guidelines provide a framework for medical management, individual patient characteristics and preferences which influence therapeutic decisions could not all be accounted for. In part, deviation from guideline recommendations may thus be considered adequate medical management. However, we do not believe that this should impact our report which primarily aims to raise awareness, and not to value deviations from guideline recommendations.

Taken together, our results in a nationwide cohort show that anticholestatic drug therapy of PBC, including first‐line therapy with UDCA, can be optimized. Patients at risk of suboptimal UDCA dosing are those with higher body weight, with a relatively favorable biochemical profile, those who are older and when managed in low volume centers. The field should thus continue to improve care delivery for people with PBC, which remains a rare disease, with educational efforts directed at both physicians and patients.
