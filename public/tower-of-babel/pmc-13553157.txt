The Effect of Personalised Feedback in Simulation on Knowledge and Skills in the Management of Bleeding During Pregnancy: A Parallel Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Mine Gökduman Keleş, Sema Göçmez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCombining simulation‐based training with structured feedback enhances skill acquisition and professional competence. Simulation‐based education is essential for preparing nurses and midwives to manage obstetric haemorrhage and other critical emergencies requiring rapid clinical decision‐making.AimThis study was conducted to determine the effect of personalised feedback in simulation on knowledge and skills related to the management of bleeding during pregnancy.Study DesignThis randomized clinical trial. All students received theoretical training on bleeding management. Simulation sessions used a high‐fidelity simulator and standardised protocols. Outcomes were measured using the Student Satisfaction and Self‐Confidence in Learning Scale and the Simulation Design Scale.ResultsIn total 58 students participated, (n = 29 in intervention, n = 29 in control group. Post‐intervention theoretical knowledge scores were higher in the intervention group than in the control group (75.6 ± 7.6 vs. 71.7 ± 11.1); however, the difference was not statistically significant (t = 1.588, p = 0.118). Satisfaction and self‐confidence scores were significantly greater in the intervention group (44.8 ± 3.0 vs. 38.8 ± 9.0, U = 245.0, p = 0.006). On the Simulation Design Scale, the intervention group scored significantly higher in Nursing Process (33.9 ± 6.5 vs. 29.9 ± 7.0, p = 0.03), Patient Safety (27.1 ± 6.0 vs. 24.5 ± 3.5, p = 0.01) and Reflective Behaviour (19.2 ± 5.1 vs. 16.1 ± 3.3, p = 0.02).ConclusionsPersonalised feedback in simulation significantly improves knowledge, satisfaction and clinical performance. Integration with traditional teaching methods is recommended as a reinforcement strategy for bleeding management education during pregnancy.Relevance to Clinical PracticePersonalised video‐assisted feedback in simulation‐based training improves midwifery students' knowledge, clinical skills and self‐confidence in managing bleeding during pregnancy. This approach supports reflective learning by helping students recognise and correct their own clinical errors. Integrating such feedback into education programmes can enhance clinical competence and preparedness for obstetric emergencies. Ultimately, this may contribute to safer maternal care and improved patient outcomes.Keywords: clinical simulation, personalised feedback, pregnancy bleeding management, simulation‐based education

Introduction

Obstetric emergencies, including haemorrhage during pregnancy, represent life‐threatening events that require rapid, coordinated responses not only from midwives but also from critical care nurses who may encounter pregnant or postpartum patients in intensive care units or emergency settings [1]. The ability to recognise and respond to such emergencies is therefore relevant across multiple clinical disciplines [2]. It is essential for midwives and critical care nurses alike to possess the knowledge and skills necessary to effectively manage obstetric emergencies, as this plays a crucial role in reducing perinatal morbidity and mortality. In addition to acquiring theoretical knowledge, it is vital that midwifery and nursing students also develop practical skills in managing obstetric emergencies during their education [3]. It is important for midwifery and nursing students to gain competence by acquiring the relevant knowledge, skills and behaviours. The concept of competence encompasses a holistic approach, including pregnancy, childbirth and the postpartum period [4]. There are various teaching and learning methods that support the transfer of knowledge from theory to practice [1]. Simulation is commonly applied in midwifery and nursing training and increasingly recommended for critical care nursing education to support learning processes and enhance the effectiveness of clinical practices [3, 5, 6].

Background

Simulation‐based training fosters active interaction and collaboration among participants. Evidence from interdisciplinary studies confirms that this approach significantly strengthens essential soft skills, such as communication and teamwork, across a range of healthcare disciplines including midwifery and critical care nursing [3, 6]. Clinical reasoning is the ability of midwifery and nursing students to apply knowledge when performing tasks and solving problems. It is cognitive in nature and involves logical, intuitive and creative thinking. Practical experiences such as applying skills and using relevant materials, tools and methods develop these abilities. These abilities reflect the outcome of applying learned knowledge through both cognitive and hands‐on experiences, a process equally critical for students preparing for high‐acuity clinical environments such as intensive care units [6, 7]. Additionally, feedback is recognised as an essential component of clinical education for both midwifery and nursing students, supporting the development of competencies required in emergency and critical care settings [8].

Obstetric haemorrhage remains the leading cause of maternal mortality worldwide, and severe bleeding during pregnancy frequently necessitates intensive care unit admission, where critical care nurses play a central role in haemodynamic monitoring, early recognition of deterioration and implementation of massive transfusion protocols. Given that an estimated 70% of maternal deaths from obstetric haemorrhage are considered preventable, the timely and competent management of this condition is of paramount importance across all clinical settings, including critical care environments [9, 10]. It has been reported in the literature that providing information through simulation‐based training scenarios and giving feedback positively affects skill development across healthcare disciplines, including midwifery and critical care nursing [11, 12]. Students and clinical practitioners should feel safe and comfortable in using their skills during case management in simulations [12]. The literature shows that simulation‐based education positively affects participants' perceived readiness and clinical skills, with implications extending to nurses working in emergency and intensive care settings where obstetric emergencies may be encountered [13, 14, 15, 16]. However, most existing studies focus primarily on the general effectiveness of simulation or the benefits of collective debriefing sessions [8, 15]. There is a noticeable lack of specific evidence regarding how ‘personalised’ feedback mechanisms tailored to the individual student's specific cognitive and psychomotor performance influence long‐term skill acquisition in high‐risk obstetric emergencies, particularly among both midwifery and nursing students preparing for critical care roles.

Personalised feedback in simulation refers to individualised, performance‐specific information directed at a learner's cognitive, psychomotor and affective domains, with the aim of improving understanding and guiding targeted skill development [17]. Unlike standardised group debriefing, personalised feedback addresses each learner's unique errors, gaps and strengths, thereby enabling more precise remediation and deeper clinical learning [18]. While standardised feedback is common, the nuances of individualised instruction in managing pregnancy‐related bleeding remain insufficiently explored across multiple clinical training contexts [11, 15]. This gap in the literature suggests that current training models may not be optimising the clinical reasoning potential of each student, nor adequately preparing critical care nurses for the obstetric emergencies they may face in intensive care or emergency settings. This study was conducted to determine the effect of personalised feedback used in simulation techniques on knowledge and skills in the management of bleeding during pregnancy, thereby addressing this gap and providing a more targeted pedagogical framework for midwifery and nursing education.

Purpose of the Research

This study was conducted to determine the effect of personalised feedback used in simulation techniques on knowledge and skills in the management of bleeding during pregnancy.

Hypotheses of the Study

Personalised feedback used in the simulation of managing bleeding during pregnancy significantly improves the knowledge and skills of participants in the experimental group compared to those in the control group.

Personalised feedback used in the simulation of managing bleeding during pregnancy does not have a significant effect on the knowledge levels and skills of the experimental group compared to the control group.

Design and Methods

Study Design

The study is a parallel randomized controlled trial.

Setting and Sample

This study was conducted between February and March 2025 with second‐year midwifery students at a higher education institution in the Mediterranean region of Türkiye during the 2024–2025 academic year.

Inclusion Criteria: who attended the second year in the spring semester of 2024–2025 and took the prenatal period II course.

Exclusion Criteria: Failed prenatal period I course.

Study Protocol

Application

The simulation laboratory is equipped with various facilities, including a control room where instructors manage scenarios and observe and record applications, as well as a debriefing room where feedback is provided on students' performance. The laboratory houses essential equipment such as the Noella S551 mannequin, hospital bed, bedside cabinet, monitor, bedside oxygen systems, wardrobe, emergency cart and a camera system for scenario management and recording by the instructors.

To ensure the internal validity of the trial and isolate ‘personalised video‐assisted feedback’ as the sole independent variable, the following three‐stage standardised protocol was implemented.

Stage 1. (Preparation): In the Antenatal Care II course, the topic of bleeding management during pregnancy was delivered to both the intervention and control groups by the researchers through a PowerPoint presentation developed based on current literature [19, 20, 21]. Following the theoretical training, clinical skills laboratory sessions were organised in groups of 5 and 6 students. The design of the simulation sessions and the developed scenario adhered to the training standards outlined in the Ministry of Health's guidelines on emergency obstetric care [19]. Prior to the simulation, each student completed an introductory form, a knowledge assessment form and scales measuring satisfaction and self‐efficacy. The total session duration for each student in both groups was strictly standardised to 25 min (5‐min pre‐briefing, 10‐min active simulation and 10‐min debriefing/reflection) to eliminate time‐on‐task bias.

Stage 2. (Intervention and Standardised Feedback): The simulation and debriefing sessions were conducted by two researchers who routinely provide training with this specific simulation modality and are experts in obstetric emergencies. During the active simulation, students in both groups received identical immediate verbal feedback solely to ensure patient safety and to correct critical technical errors in real time. The main difference between the groups is the personalised video‐assisted feedback method applied during the debriefing phase.

Control Group: Students performed a 10‐min intervention individually on a high‐fidelity simulator. During this period, students used standard clinical checklists to reflect on their own performance, estimation of blood loss and adherence to the emergency bleeding protocol. To mitigate performance bias and maintain ethical educational standards, they were provided with a 10‐min structured self‐debriefing period in the debriefing room immediately following their intervention (during which students independently reviewed their performance using a structured reflection form, identified strengths and areas for improvement, and evaluated their adherence to the emergency bleeding management protocol).

Control Group: Students performed a 10‐min intervention individually on a high‐fidelity simulator. During this period, students used standard clinical checklists to reflect on their own performance, estimation of blood loss and adherence to the emergency bleeding protocol. To mitigate performance bias and maintain ethical educational standards, they were provided with a 10‐min structured self‐debriefing period in the debriefing room immediately following their intervention (during which students independently reviewed their performance using a structured reflection form, identified strengths and areas for improvement, and evaluated their adherence to the emergency bleeding management protocol).

Study Group: Students performed the same 10‐min intervention while being video‐recorded. During this period, students used standard clinical checklists to reflect on their own performance, estimation of blood loss and adherence to the emergency bleeding protocol. Immediately after the performance, a 10‐min video‐assisted debriefing session was conducted in the debriefing room. During this session, video recordings were used as reflective tools to evaluate students' performance. The video review enabled students to identify individual errors in blood loss estimation and adherence to the emergency bleeding protocol, thereby enhancing self‐awareness and facilitating the development of clinical skills. This approach ensured that the structured, video‐assisted feedback constituted the sole point of differentiation between the two groups.

Stage 3. (Post‐Intervention Evaluation): All participants completed the post‐test, including the Knowledge Assessment Form, the Evaluation of Simulation‐Based Learning Scale and the ‘Student Satisfaction and Self‐Confidence in Learning Scale’ [8, 21].

Data Collection and Research Instruments

Data were collected at two different time points during the study. Prior to the simulation sessions, all participants completed the Student Identification Form, the Knowledge Assessment Form on the Management of Bleeding in Pregnancy and the Student Satisfaction and Self‐Confidence Scale in Learning as baseline (pre‐test) assessments. Immediately following completion of the simulation intervention, participants completed the Simulation‐Based Learning Assessment Scale and the Student Satisfaction and Self‐Confidence in Learning Scale as post‐test assessments.

Assessment of Simulation‐Based Learning Scale

There are five sub‐dimensions and 37 items in the Evaluation of Simulation‐Based Learning Scale. The responses are scored on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 and the total score ranges between 37 and 185. Within the scope of the scale, the concepts of nursing process, patient safety, professional knowledge, communication and reflected behaviour, which are the basic competencies that nurses should have, are evaluated. The increase in the score obtained from the sub‐dimensions in the scale means that the goal of that sub‐dimension is realised for the student in the scenario [21].

Student Satisfaction and Self‐Confidence in Learning Scale

The Student Turkish validity and reliability were conducted by Ünver et al. in 2017. The scale, which, was revised to 12 items during the Turkish validity and reliability study. The 5‐point Likert‐type scale consists of two sub‐dimensions: ‘Satisfaction with Current Learning’ with five items and ‘Confidence in Learning’ with seven items. The scale, which does not contain any negative items, is scored by dividing the sum of the sub‐dimensions by the number of items [22].

Data Analysis

The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For variables that were normally distributed, the Independent‐Samples t‐test was used for between‐group comparisons. For variables that did not meet the assumption of normality, the Mann–Whitney U test and the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test were applied. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical Approvals

Ethical approval was obtained from the Non‐Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee prior to the commencement of the study (Decision No: GO 2025/1062, Meeting No: 2025/2, Date: 05 February 2025). All participants were informed about the purpose of the research, the voluntary nature of their participation and the confidentiality of the data. In accordance with the study protocol, participants provided written informed consent explicitly agreeing to the video recording of their simulation performances for educational and feedback purposes.

Trial Registration

This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov with the identifier NCT06832319.

Results

A total of 58 students participated in the study, including 29 students in the study group and 29 students in the control group. Participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention (study) group or the control group (Figure 1) using a simple randomization procedure. The allocation sequence was generated using an online automated tool (random.org) to ensure unbiased group assignment. Each participant had an equal probability of being allocated to either group, resulting in two comparable groups (n = 29 per group).

There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of place of longest residence (χ2 = 3.99, p = 0.13) and preference for the midwifery profession (χ2 = 0.36, p = 0.54), indicating that the groups were comparable at baseline. While there was no significant difference in theoretical knowledge scores before the bleeding management intervention (U = 389.5, p = 0.629), post‐intervention theoretical knowledge scores were higher in the study group than in the control group (75.6 ± 7.6 vs. 71.7 ± 11.1); however, this difference was not statistically significant (t = 1.588, p = 0.118).

Discussion

This study examined the effect of personalised feedback used in simulation‐based training on students' knowledge and skills in managing bleeding during pregnancy. While there is a growing body of evidence supporting simulation‐based education, there appears to be limited research specifically evaluating the combined impact of high‐fidelity simulators and personalised video‐assisted feedback on pregnancy bleeding management within the current midwifery literature. Although various studies have explored the efficacy of video debriefing and structured feedback in broader clinical settings and healthcare disciplines, research specifically tailored to this obstetric emergency in midwifery education remains relatively scarce.

Simulation‐based learning experiences for health professionals support the development of specific clinical skills in participants and the development of decision‐making and critical thinking skills in a safe, controlled environment through simulated learning scenarios [23]. These benefits are not limited to midwifery education alone; simulation‐based training has been increasingly recognised as a critical pedagogical tool in nursing and critical care education, where clinical decision‐making under pressure is equally essential [6, 24]. In the literature, simulation has been reported to improve students' clinical performance, increase students' adherence to protocols through scenarios and contribute to reducing morbidity from obstetric emergencies [25, 26]. It has been reported that applying simulation to the diagnosis and management of US‐based postpartum haemorrhage (PPH) teams significantly increased cognitive knowledge for members of obstetric teams, knowledge of blood transfusion protocols was relatively high and the team generally used the protocols appropriately during the scenarios [25]. A US‐based multicentre cohort study reported that high‐fidelity simulation for PPH management resulted in a reduction in bleeding‐related morbidity, curettage rates and hysterectomy rates. In addition, an increase in the diagnosis of PPH after simulation training was reported to increase awareness of this condition [26]. In a study conducted in the United States with staff involved in maternity care, it was reported that PPH simulation training improved team performance in the treatment of the underlying causes of PPH [27]. These findings are particularly relevant for critical care nurses, who may encounter obstetric haemorrhage in intensive care or emergency settings and must be equally prepared to recognise and respond to such emergencies. A simulation training programme conducted among nurses in Saudi Arabia demonstrated that targeted haemorrhage simulation significantly improved nurses' knowledge and clinical competencies in PPH management, underscoring the transferability of simulation‐based approaches beyond midwifery to broader nursing and critical care contexts [1]. In line with these findings, the present study, conducted in Turkey, demonstrated that both the intervention and control groups exhibited significant within‐group improvements in knowledge and clinical skills following the simulation sessions, highlighting the inherent effectiveness of simulation‐based training itself. However, it is noteworthy that the group receiving personalised feedback through video‐assisted debriefing achieved greater gains compared to the control group. This suggests that while simulation alone is a powerful educational tool, the integration of structured and reflective feedback mechanisms may further optimise learning outcomes by enhancing self‐awareness, clinical reasoning and performance refinement with implications for nursing and critical care education programmes seeking to strengthen obstetric emergency preparedness across clinical disciplines.

In the literature, feedback devices in adult education are generally reported to improve the quality of performance in training across multiple healthcare disciplines, including nursing and critical care [26, 27]. This is particularly relevant in critical care settings, where performance feedback during simulation has been shown to enhance clinical decision‐making and procedural accuracy among nurses managing life‐threatening emergencies. In a study conducted in the United States examining visual attention during paediatric resuscitation with feedback devices, it was reported that the quality of the animation improved significantly. However, the shift from other equipment to the feedback device reduced attention to the manikin and increased workload [28]. A study conducted in Saudi Arabia among nurses demonstrated that simulation‐based feedback in haemorrhage management training significantly improved both theoretical knowledge and clinical performance, findings that align closely with the results of the present study conducted in Turkey [1]. Similar to this study, the fact that personalised feedback used in the simulation technique to teach bleeding management during pregnancy is associated with higher levels of theoretical knowledge may be due to the fact that the student's performance is videotaped, and then both midwifery and nursing students can see their mistakes. The incorporation of structured reflection into simulation‐based education may be especially beneficial in critical care nursing, as the ability to identify and correct procedural errors during obstetric emergencies is fundamental to improving clinical decision‐making and patient outcomes.

In contrast to the significant improvements observed in clinical management, no statistically significant difference was found between the study and control groups in the communication and professional knowledge subdimensions (p > 0.05). The lack of a significant difference in professional knowledge may be due to both groups receiving the same comprehensive theoretical training before the simulation, which may have resulted in a ceiling effect. Arrogante et al. [24] reported that high‐accuracy simulation improved communication skills in nursing students, although this study showed no statistically significant difference between the intervention and control groups. In this study, personalised feedback appears to have a stronger effect on higher level competencies such as clinical performance, self‐confidence and reflective behaviour, rather than directly affecting communication and professional knowledge scores.

Midwives have the duties of determining, coordinating and managing care needs within the emergency obstetric care team, as well as consulting with gynaecologists, anaesthesiologists and physicians from various disciplines [20]. In addition, it is crucial to equip midwifery and nursing students with skills in the management of risky pregnancies during undergraduate and postgraduate education programmes, as these students will be the ones advancing both the midwifery and nursing professions, including critical care nursing practice, in the future [29]. This is especially pertinent for nursing students who may pursue careers in intensive care or emergency settings, where the ability to recognise and manage obstetric emergencies such as haemorrhage during pregnancy is an essential yet often undertrained competency. Personalised video‐assisted feedback supports a learning process in which midwifery and nursing students move beyond passive observation to active cognitive engagement, enabling a deeper understanding of their clinical practices. Video recordings allow students to review their own performance, directly identify technical errors and deficiencies, and thereby function as a powerful pedagogical tool that promotes reflective practice across healthcare disciplines. Through this process, both midwifery and nursing students are able to recognise the discrepancy between their internalised ideal of practice and their actual performance, fostering the development of self‐assessment and self‐regulation skills. These competencies are of particular relevance in critical care nursing education, where reflective practice and rapid self‐correction are essential for safe and effective management of high‐acuity obstetric emergencies.

In addition, this approach not only enhances individual awareness but also offers substantial potential for the integration of peer learning and peer coaching. Collaborative analysis of video recordings among students can deepen the learning process and strengthen collective self‐efficacy through social interaction.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted within the context of several limitations. First, the research was conducted with a specific group of midwifery students at a single university; this may limit the generalisability of the results to other institutional settings or to professional midwives in clinical practice. Second, the study used self‐report questionnaires to measure student satisfaction and self‐esteem.

Future Research

The higher levels of satisfaction and self‐confidence observed among students receiving personalised feedback further underscore the pedagogical value of actively engaging learners in self‐evaluation processes. By promoting insight into performance gaps and encouraging reflective thinking, such feedback mechanisms appear to support the development of self‐regulatory capacities essential for effective clinical decision‐making in emergency obstetric contexts.

Notably, the results emphasise that the educational impact of simulation is not solely contingent upon the level of technological fidelity. Rather, the presence of structured, reflective and learner‐cantered feedback processes emerges as a key determinant of learning effectiveness. This finding carries significant implications for critical care nursing education, suggesting that even within resource‐limited simulation environments, the quality and personalisation of feedback rather than the sophistication of equipment alone may be the primary driver of meaningful clinical skill acquisition among nurses preparing for high‐acuity obstetric emergencies.

Accordingly, systematically integrating personalised feedback strategies into both undergraduate and graduate midwifery and nursing curricula including critical care nursing training programmes can be a critical step in optimising clinical training and preparing future midwives and nurses for the complexities of high‐risk care. In particular, critical care nursing programmes should consider incorporating obstetric emergency simulation modules with structured personalised feedback, given that nurses in intensive care units are frequently the first responders to deteriorating obstetric patients and must be equipped with both the theoretical knowledge and practical skills to act decisively. Future research is recommended to be designed as multicentric randomized controlled trials with larger and more heterogeneous sample groups, including nursing and critical care populations, to enhance the generalisability of findings across clinical disciplines. Furthermore, mixed‐methods research, using both quantitative and qualitative data, is suggested to gain a deeper understanding of the underlying reasons for quantitative findings and participant experiences. Future studies should also explore the specific impact of personalised feedback on critical care nurses' readiness to manage obstetric emergencies, as this remains an understudied area in the simulation literature. Finally, longitudinal follow‐up studies should be planned, with participants followed for 6 months, 1 year and 2 years, to assess the sustainability of the results obtained in this study across both midwifery and nursing education contexts.

Implications for Clinical Practice

The higher levels of satisfaction and self‐confidence observed among students receiving personalised feedback further underscore the pedagogical value of actively engaging learners in self‐evaluation processes. By promoting insight into performance gaps and encouraging reflective thinking, such feedback mechanisms appear to support the development of self‐regulatory capacities essential for effective clinical decision‐making in emergency obstetric contexts. For critical care nurses, whose clinical encounters with obstetric haemorrhage may be infrequent yet immediately life‐threatening, the cultivation of these self‐regulatory and reflective competencies through personalised simulation feedback represents a particularly valuable strategy for maintaining readiness and ensuring safe, timely intervention.

Conclusion

The findings of this study provide robust evidence that the integration of personalised video‐assisted feedback into simulation‐based education substantially enhances midwifery and nursing students' clinical competence in the management of pregnancy‐related bleeding. Participants exposed to individualised feedback demonstrated superior performance not only in knowledge acquisition but also across critical clinical domains, including the nursing process, patient safety and reflective behaviour. These outcomes suggest that facilitating midwifery and nursing learners' ability to systematically observe and critically appraise their own performance strengthens both technical skills and higher order clinical reasoning competencies that are equally fundamental for nursing students preparing for roles in critical care and emergency settings where obstetric emergencies may be encountered. These findings are of particular significance for critical care nurse educators, as they provide evidence‐based support for incorporating personalised, video‐assisted feedback into simulation curricula designed to prepare critical care nurses for the rapid recognition and management of obstetric haemorrhage in intensive care and emergency settings.

All participants were informed about the purpose of the research, the voluntary nature of their participation and the confidentiality of the data. In accordance with the study protocol, participants provided written informed consent explicitly agreeing to the video recording of their simulation performances for educational and feedback purposes.

Consent

All participants were informed about the purpose, procedures and voluntary nature of the study. They were assured that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Conflicts of Interest

The authors declare no conflicts of interest.

Data Availability Statement

The data that support the findings of this study are available on request from the corresponding author. The data are not publicly available due to privacy or ethical restrictions.
