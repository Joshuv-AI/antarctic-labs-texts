Pirenzepine exhibits anti-prostate cancer activity and enhances checkpoint inhibitor-based immunotherapy by targeting STING
Authors: Jinge Xu, Junyu Li, Junwen Zhang, Yongsheng Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTherapeutic options targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) have been approved for use in human malignancies, showing clinical benefits. Nonetheless, a significant number of patients, particularly those with prostate cancer (PCa), show poor response to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies, highlighting the necessity to explore supportive strategies that could enhance conventional PD-1/PD-L1-targeting immunotherapy.MethodsPCa cell lines DU145 and RM1 were used to investigate the effects of pirenzepine (PZP) on the proliferation, colony formation, and migration of PCa cells in vitro. A subcutaneous tumor model bearing DU145 and RM1 was established to evaluate the anti-tumor effect in vivo. Lentivirus transfection and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assays were conducted to investigate the role of STING (endoplasmic reticulum-associated protein stimulator of interferon genes) in the antitumor mechanisms of PZP. The infiltration of CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues was examined using immunohistochemistry. The RM1 subcutaneous tumor model was employed to assess the combined effects of PZP and anti-programmed cell death 1 antibody (anti-PD1) immune checkpoint blockade therapy.ResultsThe in vitro and in vivo experiment results indicated that PZP suppressed the proliferation, colony formation, and migration of PCa cells in a dose-dependent manner. Notably, PZP also promoted CD8+ T cell infiltration and enhanced the anti-PD1 therapeutic effect on PCa. Mechanistically, the results preliminarily indicated that PZP impeded PCa progression and stimulated tumor immune response by upregulating STING expression.ConclusionsOur results revealed that PZP impaired the malignant biological behavior of PCa and enhanced antitumor immunity. These findings may provide a theoretical basis for combining PZP with anti-PD1 immune checkpoint blockade therapy on PCa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03520-4.Keywords: Pirenzepine, STING, Anti-PD1, Prostate cancer

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most common malignant tumors in the male urogenital system. According to the latest research statistics from the National Cancer Institute of the United States in 2022, prostate cancer has the highest incidence rate among all male malignant tumors, accounting for 27% of new tumor diagnoses in men [1]. Its mortality rate ranks second among all male malignant tumors, only after lung cancer [1]. Most prostate cancer cases progress slowly and have a long natural course of disease. Considering that its occurrence and development rely on the presence of androgens, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) has emerged as an effective treatment option [2]. Some prostate cancer patients can achieve remission through surgery or endocrine castration therapy. However, many patients eventually progress to a refractory subtype known as castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) [3]. Progression to the castration-resistant state is characterized by endocrine therapy resistance, negatively affecting the prognosis of patients and potentially leading to death [4]. In the past few years, significant advances have been made in the treatment of castration-resistant prostate cancer. New drugs, including docetaxel, cabattaxel, abiraterone acetate, enzalutamide, olaparib, etc., have been developed. These new drugs have shown good clinical efficacy and can improve the overall median survival of patients [5]. Nonetheless, most patients with CRPC eventually experience disease progression and die from prostate cancer [4]. Therefore, more effective treatment options for refractory prostate cancers, such as CRPC, require further research.

After decades of development, immunotherapy has emerged as a pivotal cancer treatment modality [6]. Immune checkpoints are a group of inhibitory pathways in the immune system. Through the binding of ligands and receptors on the cell surface, they play an essential role in maintaining the homeostasis of the immune system and preventing autoimmune inflammation and tissue damage under physiological conditions. However, tumor cells can utilize immune checkpoints to suppress the function of the immune system, facilitating immune escape [7]. Immune checkpoint inhibition therapy targets immune checkpoints to prevent T cell inhibition and promote the activation and proliferation of effector T cells, revitalizing the anti-tumor immune response [8]. At present, immune checkpoint blockades (ICBs) targeting cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4) [9] and PD-1/PD-L1 [10] have been approved for clinical application and have achieved certain clinical effects. For instance, anti-PD1 therapy targets the immune checkpoint PD-1/PD-L1, which inhibits the interaction between PD-1 on the surface of T cells and PD-L1 expressed by tumor cells. This effectively reactivates effector T cells and revitalizes the anti-tumor immune response [11]. However, many patients develop drug resistance against anti-PD1 immunotherapy and do not benefit from the treatment, which limits the prospects of anti-PD-1 treatment. Notably, low infiltration of effector T cells in the tumor microenvironment of CRPC contributes to strong resistance to immune checkpoint blockade. Clinical trials of anti-PD-1 /PD-L1 monotherapy have also shown limited clinical benefits for patients with prostate cancer [12–14]. Currently, no antibody against PD-1 or PD-L1 has been approved for clinical application in CRPC. Still, a recent Phase III clinical trial demonstrated efficacy in a small number of patients with advanced prostate cancer [15], highlighting the potential of immune checkpoint therapy for treatment-resistant prostate cancer. Furthermore, elderly and frail patients with prostate adenocarcinoma show better tolerance to immunotherapy targeting PD-1/PD-L1 [16]. Therefore, future studies may focus on developing more effective anti-PD1 /PD-L1 treatment strategies or combined therapies to enhance the therapeutic effect of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 for advanced prostate cancer.

Pirenzepine (PZP), also known as pyrazine or piperazine, is a selective anticholinergic drug and belongs to the category of acid-suppressing and gastric mucosal protective drugs [17]. PZP competitively inhibits the M1 receptor of the gastric mucosa, selectively reducing gastric acid secretion, and simultaneously suppressing the activity of pepsin while protecting the gastric mucosa. Hence, PZP is widely used for gastric ulcers, duodenal ulcers, and acute gastric mucosal hemorrhage [18]. Nevertheless, the clinical application scope of PZP is very limited, and its role in tumor progression remains largely unknown. A growing number of studies have reported that PZP exhibited potential anti-tumor activity [19]. For example, Naznin et al. [20] reported that PZP suppresses human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells in vitro. For PCa, several reports have suggested that PZP inhibits PCa cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro [21, 22]. For example, Yin et al. [21] reported that PZP inhibited the migration and invasion of PCa cells PC-3 and LNCaP in vitro, which may be mediated by the classic receptor muscarinic acetylcholine receptor M1 (CHRM1). However, the in vivo anti-tumor effects of PZP have not been further verified. Unidentified targets may potentially be involved, and the effects of PZP on the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) remain unknown. In conclusion, previous studies have demonstrated that PZP may exert anti-tumor effects in various types of cancer. Studies on PCa were limited to in vitro experiments. Therefore, its function in vivo, in TIME, related mechanism, and its clinical value in PCa therapy, especially in tumor immune therapy, remains poorly understood.

A recent study employed oblique incidence reflectance difference (OI-RD) and small-molecule microarray (SMM)-based assays and reported that PZP may bind to STING; however, no functional verification was carried out. STING (endoplasmic reticulum-associated protein stimulator of interferon genes) plays a crucial role in signal transduction and regulates cell death and the innate immune response [23–25]. Specifically, STING regulates multiple types of cell death, including apoptosis [26, 27], necroptosis [28, 29], pyroptosis [30–32], ferroptosis [33, 34], mitotic death [35, 36], and immunogenic cell death [37, 38]. Nevertheless, the specific type of cell death possibly mediated by PZP-STING remains unclear, warranting further research. Furthermore, STING is a crucial signal transduction molecule in the innate immune response [23, 24]. Upon detecting DNA from an invading pathogen or host genomic DNA, the DNA sensor cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate synthetase (cGAS) catalyzes the production of cyclic dinucleotide (CDN) 2 ′3'-cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate (2 ′3' -cGAMP) in the cytoplasm [39]. Subsequently, 2 ′3' -cGAMP, as a second messenger, binds to STING, thereby triggering the production of type I interferons (IFN) and pro-inflammatory cytokines [40]. This signaling pathway participates in immune surveillance and defense [41]. A previous study revealed that interferon is essential for the maturation and activation of dendritic cells (DCS) and macrophages, which promotes the activation of CD8+ T cells and anti-tumor immunity [42]. Li et al. [43] reported that arsenic trioxide activates the cGAS-STING-IFN pathway and facilitates CD8+ T cell infiltration, thereby potentiating the anti-PD1 anti-hepatocellular carcinoma effect. However, whether STING is essential for PZP’s anti-tumor effect remains unknown, warranting verification and further research.

Thus, this research aimed to explore the potential anti-tumor effects of PZP and the associated mechanisms involved in the progression of PCa. Additionally, this research examined the functional impact of PZP on the immune microenvironment and ICB (anti-PD1) therapy.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

Human PCa cell lines, namely LNcap, PC3, DU145, prostate epithelial cell line PZ-HPV-7, and murine cell line RM-1, were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The PCa cells were maintained in complete medium, which consisted of 1640 medium (Gibco, China) enriched with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Sangon, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone). PZ-HPV-7 was cultured in keratinocyte serum-free medium (K-SFM) supplemented with bovine pituitary extract and human recombinant epidermal growth factor (EGF). All the cell lines were incubated at a temperature of 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 200 cells per well and cultured for a duration of 2 weeks. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, the colonies were stained using Giemsa dye for 10 min. Colonies, defined as those containing over 50 cells each, were then counted. This procedure was replicated in three independent experiments. The PZP (CAT#HY-17037A, MedChemExpress) was dissolved in DMSO. The negative control, referred to as the Control group in this study, was also treated with the same concentration of DMSO. The concentration of PZP used in this experiment was based on several published reports [21, 22].

Cell wound healing assay

Cell motility was assessed using a wound healing assay. A total of 1.2 × 106 cells was plated in 6-well tissue culture dishes and incubated for 24 h. Scratch wounds were generated using a 10 μL pipette tip, followed by three rinses with cold PBS. Wound healing was then observed at 0, 9, 18, and 36 h. Photographs were captured to evaluate the migration levels of cells in each group. Cell motility was quantified by measuring the distance between the advancing edges of the cells in three randomly chosen microscopic fields (× 100) at each time interval. The concentration of PZP used in this experiment was based on several published reports [21, 22].

RNA interference and plasmid transfection

To achieve RNA interference, cells were transfected with 20 nmol/L small interfering RNA (siRNA) (GenePharma, Suzhou, China) specifically targeting human STING. The sequence was as follows: GTTTACAGCAACAGCATCTAT. The short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequence used for Sting downregulation in murine PCa cells was as follows: CAACATTCGATTCCGAGATAT. Lipofectamine 3000 (CAT#L3000015, ThermoFisher, MA, USA) was utilized following the manufacturer's protocol for the transfection process. The efficacy of inhibition was assessed by qRT-PCR detection. The concentration of PZP used in this experiment was based on several published reports [21, 22]. In these articles, researchers suggested that the effective concentration of PZP on PCa tumor cells, such as PC3, is around 100 ug/ml [21, 22]. The plasmid PCDNA3.1-mouse-Sting was sourced from Genechem (Shanghai, China). Initially, the packaging cells (HEK293) were transfected with plasmids for 24 h using Lipofectamine 2000 (Cat#11668019, Invitrogen, USA). Following this, the old medium was discarded and substituted with fresh, complete culture medium. After approximately 2 days, the targeted recombinant lentiviruses were harvested and then introduced into RM1 cells.

In vivo tumor model

C57BL/6c and nude mice aged 3–4 weeks were sourced from the Medical Experimental Animal Center of Guangdong Province in Guangzhou, China, and were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. All animal procedures strictly adhered to the guidelines set by the Chinese National Institute of Health for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The research was approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Guangzhou Medical University in Guangzhou, China. The right armpit and inguinal regions of each C57BL/6c or nude mouse received subcutaneous implantation of 2 × 106 RM-1 or DU145 cells. As the tumor size reached around 50 mm3, mice were administered either the anti-PD1 treatment (CAT#BE0146, 200 µg per mouse, BioXCell) or an IgG isotype control (CAT#BE0089, BioXCell) intraperitoneally once every 2 days. After a feeding period of 20–25 days, all mice were euthanized, and the tumor weight and volume were measured promptly. Tumor volumes were calculated by the following equation: V = [Length (mm)* Width2 (mm)]/2. The STING agonist group received 25 μg STING agonist c-di-AMP (CAT#HY-12326A, MedChemExpress) injected into the tumor mass every 2 days. The dose of PZP used in vivo experiment was determined by converting animal doses to human equivalent doses based on body surface area, following the methods outlined in previously published reports [44–46]. Considering that the clinical dosage of PZP is around 75 mg–150 mg/kg [18, 47], the PZP dosage in mice was calculated as (75/60–150/60)*12.3 = 15.4–30.75 mg/kg. Therefore, the dose gradient for the in vivo experiment was set to 16–32 mg/kg.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assays

Total RNA was obtained from cultured cells and human tissues utilizing TRIzol reagent (Takara Bio, Beijing, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Beijing, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. For qRT-PCR, reverse transcription was performed using the SuperScript First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR analysis was conducted employing complementary DNA and SYBR Green II (Takara Bio, Beijing, China). The relative expression levels of mRNAs in the cells and tissues were normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH and computed using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The sequences of primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor specimens were surgically removed, preserved in 10% formalin, treated with ethanol and xylene, and then embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, 4-micron-thick tumor sections were produced using a Leica RM2235 rotary microtome. Following antigen retrieval with sodium citrate buffer (pH = 6.0), the sections were stained with the UltraSensitiveTM SP (Mouse/Rabbit) IHC kit (Maxim Biotechnologies, Kit-9730). The tumor sections were incubated with the primary antibodies (mouse CD8a, eBioscience, 14-0808-82; Nkp46, CAT#ab283505, Abcam, USA; CD206, CAT#ab64693, Abcam, USA), overnight at 4 °C. Thereafter, a DAB kit (Maxim Biotechnologies, DAB-1031) was used for development for 1 min, and the tissue sections were counterstained with hematoxylin solution (Sigma, HHS16) for 10 min. Image analysis was conducted using Cellsens Standard software (Olympus, Japan).

Western blotting (WB) analysis

Proteins sourced from cell and tissue lysates were meticulously separated using SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). Following this separation process, the proteins were electrotransferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane, provided by Pall Corp located in Port Washington, NY. To optimize the conditions for antibody binding, the membranes underwent a blocking step utilizing a 5% solution of skimmed milk. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies specifically targeting a range of proteins, including GAPDH (1:2000, Cat#TA-08, ZSbio, China). Additionally, antibodies against Sting (1:1000 dilution, Cat# 13647), TBK1(1:1000 dilution, Cat#3504), phosphorylated TBK1 (p-TBK1, 1:1000 dilution, Cat# 5483), IRF3(1:1000 dilution, Cat#4302), phosphorylated IRF3(p-IRF3, 1:1000 dilution, Cat# 4947) were applied, all of which were sourced from Cell Signaling Technology in Beverly, MA. Following the incubation with the primary antibodies, the membranes were then treated with the corresponding secondary antibodies, specifically anti-rabbit IgG at a dilution of 1:3000 (#7074; Cell Signaling). To visualize the protein signals, an enhanced chemiluminescence detection method from Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA, was employed.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The murine PCa RM cells were cultured at a density of 2 × 106 cells in a 12-well plate. The cells were subsequently treated with varying concentrations of PZP, specifically 10 and 100 µg/ml, for a duration of 48 h. Following the treatment period, the culture supernatants were collected for analysis using the ELISA method. To assess the levels of specific biomarkers, mouse interferon beta 1 (IFNB1) and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10(CXCL10) ELISA kits were sourced from Elabscience (Cat#E-EL-M0033, Cat#E-EL-M0021, China). The experiments were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s detailed instructions to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the results.

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluations were conducted using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Each assay was executed a minimum of three times, and the results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group differences were assessed using ANOVA or a Student’s t-test for continuous data. The statistical significance of differences between groups was indicated by asterisks (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001). A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

PZP inhibited the cell proliferation and migration ability of CRPC cells in vitro

PZP is a small molecule compound (MF: C19H21N5O2, MW:351.4 g/mol) that is commonly used in the treatment of gastric ulcers, duodenal ulcers, and acute gastric mucosal hemorrhage (Fig. 1A). First, the impact of PZP on the proliferation of human CRPC cells DU145 and murine PCa cells RM1 was investigated. As demonstrated in Fig. 1B, C, PZP significantly inhibited the growth of human CRPC cells DU145 and murine CRPC cells RM1 in a concentration-dependent manner. The plate clonality assays further verified that different concentrations of PZP (10, 100 µg/ml) suppressed the proliferation abilities of RM1 (Fig. 1D, E) and DU145 cells (Fig. 1F, G). Moreover, the cell wound healing assay indicated that the migratory abilities of RM1 cells were significantly impeded (Fig. 1H, I). Collectively, these findings revealed that PZP remarkably inhibited the cell proliferation and migration ability of CRPC cells in vitro.

The concentration of PZP used in this experiment was based on several published reports [21, 22]. In these reports, researchers suggested that the effective concentration of PZP on PCa tumor cells, such as PC3, was around 100 µg/ml [21, 22]. Considering the difference of compound purity, a concentration gradient (10, 100 µg/ml) was set to explore the anti-tumor activity of PZP.

PZP impaired the tumorigenicity abilities of CRPC cells in vivo

Next, in vivo experiments were performed to determine the anti-tumorigenicity abilities of PZP (Fig. 2A). Palpable tumors were detected, and PZP was initiated on the seventh day after CRPC cell injection (Fig. 2B). As shown in Fig. 2C, D, RM1 tumors treated with PZP (16 mg/kg, 32 mg/kg) grew at a slower rate and exhibited a smaller size at day 25 compared to the control group. The weight of C57BL/6c mice demonstrated no significant change (Fig. 2E). Moreover, the cell-derived xenograft assay (Fig. 2F) revealed that DU145 tumors treated with PZP (16 mg/kg, 32 mg/kg) grew significantly more slowly compared to the control group (Fig. 2G, I). The weight of nude mice demonstrated no significant change (Fig. 2J).

STING may be essential for PZP anti-tumor effect and inflammatory cytokines secretion

The data from the human protein atlas (HPA) database revealed a lower expression of STING in PCa tissue compared to normal prostate tissues (Fig. 4A). In addition, qPCR detection indicated significantly lower STING expression in PCa cells compared to PZ-HPV-7 prostate epithelial cells (Fig. 4B). Moreover, the addition of PZP (10,100 µg/ml) not only significantly increased STING expression but also induced IFNB1 and CXCL10 expression in DU145 and RM1 cells (Fig. 4C, D). Consistently, WB analysis indicated that PZP treatment increased the phosphorylation level of STING downstream signaling molecules, including TANK-binding Kinase 1 (TBK1) and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) (Fig. 4E). The result of qPCR detection suggested that PZP treatment significantly upregulated the expression of apoptosis-related genes B-cell lymphoma-2 associated X(BAX) and B-cell lymphoma-2 homologous antagonist/killer -1 (BAK-1) in RM1 cells (Fig. 4F).

Subsequently, STING was successfully knocked down in DU145 cells (Fig. 5A), and the impact of PZP on cell growth and the expression of IFNB1 and CXCL10 was assessed. As shown in Fig. 5B, the results of the CCK-8 assay indicated that PZP (100 µg/ml) significantly impeded the cell growth of DU145 compared to the control group, while STING knockdown neutralized the PZP anti-tumor effect. Moreover, treatment with PZP (100 µg/ml) induced the expression of IFNB1 and CXCL10, while STING knockdown abrogated PZP-induced expression of IFNB1 and CXCL10 at the protein level (Fig. 5C). A similar outcome was observed in murine PCa RM1 cells. The PZP-mediated inhibition of cell growth and the upregulation of IFNB1 and CXCL10 were neutralized by Sting knockdown (Fig. 5D–F). STING was also successfully overexpressed in RM1 cell (oeSting cell) (Fig. 5G). Compared to the control group, the PZP group and oeSting group exhibited lower cell viability and higher IFN and CXCL10 expression. When compared to the oeSting group, the cell viability and IFN and CXCL10 expression of the oeSting + PZP group showed no significant difference (Fig. 5H, I). In conclusion, STING may play an essential role in the anti-tumor effect of PZP and inflammatory cytokine secretion.

PZP enhanced the anti-PD1 therapeutic effect in a Sting-dependent manner

The initiation of type I interferon responses, including the expression and secretion of IFNB1 and CXCL10, is crucial for mounting effective anti-tumor immune reactions, such as promoting CD8+ T cell infiltration. Notably, Sting has emerged as a promising target in cancer immunotherapy, especially in immune checkpoint blockade therapy. The IHC analysis revealed that the PZP-treated group exhibited greater CD8+ T cell infiltration compared with the control group (Fig. 6A, B), while the infiltration of other immune cells, such as macrophages and NK cells, demonstrated no significant change (Fig. 6C–F).

A, B IHC analysis of CD8+ T cells in RM1 tumor tissues after PZP treatment (16 mg/kg or 32 mg/kg, i.p. daily), data were presented as mean ± SD (n = 3), **P ≤ 0.01, ****P ≤ 0.0001, scale bar, 25 μm; D Representative immunohistochemistry data of CD206+ cell and in RM1 tumors treated with PZP (32 mg/kg, i.p. daily) in C57BL/6c mice. The quantification data is shown in E, scale bar, 25 μm; F Representative immunohistochemistry data of NKp46+ cell and in RM1 tumors treated with PZP (32 mg/kg, i.p. daily) in C57BL/6c mice, scale bar, 25 μm

In addition, the growth and mass of tumors formed by RM1 were significantly inhibited by PZP, while the anti-PD1 therapy exhibited no significant effect. Importantly, the combined therapy led to a greater inhibition of RM1 tumor growth compared to PZP or anti-PD1 monotherapy (Fig. 7A–D). The role of Sting in the observed synergistic effect was further investigated. Sting KO RM1 cells were injected subcutaneously in mice to form tumors. However, the administration of PZP did not influence the proliferation of tumors derived from shSting RM1 cells. Additionally, the combination therapy failed to impede tumor growth (Fig. 7E–G). In summary, Sting may be largely responsible for the combined effects of PZP and anti-PD1 in inhibiting tumor growth in vivo.

Discussion

This study is the first to demonstrate that PZP, a drug commonly used for gastric ulcers, duodenal ulcers, and acute gastric mucosal hemorrhage, exhibits significant anti-tumor effects by inhibiting PCa cell proliferation, migration. Moreover, PZP enhances CD8+ T cell infiltration by promoting IFNB1 and CXCL10 expression through the upregulation of STING. These findings indicate an alternative mechanism for the PZP-mediated anti-tumor effects and provide a theoretical basis for the treatment of PCa by combining PZP and anti-PD1immune checkpoint blockade. The present study presented a minimum of three benefits: (1) the results reinforce PZP's status as the preferred medication for PCa patients experiencing gastric and duodenal ulcers, as well as acute gastric mucosal hemorrhage during cancer therapies, particularly in combination with anti-PD1 therapy; (2) the research provides innovative perspectives on developing therapeutic approaches for prostate cancer utilizing commonly used medications; and (3) the study provides evidence supporting the safety of PZP in PCa prevention. The combination may facilitate the application of PZP in the immunotherapy of PCa.

Discovering compounds with anti-tumor activity from non-anti-tumor drugs provides an effective avenue to develop tumor treatment methods. This approach involves low research and development costs, short development time, and reliable safety. Additionally, new targets and new pathways can be further studied. Recently, a growing number of non-anti-tumor drugs have been found to exert anti-tumor effects and enhance tumor immune therapy. For example, Wu et al. [48] revealed that the commonly used antihypertensive drug nifedipine suppressed colorectal cancer progression and potentiated anti-PD1 therapy. Previous reports demonstrated that PZP can inhibit malignant biological behavior, such as the proliferation, migration, and invasion of various types of tumors, via its classic receptor muscarinic acetylcholine receptor M1 (CHRM1) [21, 22]. Nevertheless, Dang et al. [49] recently utilized oblique incidence reflectance difference (OI-RD) and small-molecule microarray (SMM)-based assays, revealing that PZP may bind to STING. Nonetheless, no functional verification was conducted. Yin et al. [21] conducted a series of in vitro experiments, including MTT and transwell assay, and reported that PZP inhibited PC-3 and LNCaP PCa cell proliferation and invasion. Another similar report also verified that PZP inhibited PCa cells’ growth in vitro. Overall, previous cancer research on PZP focuses on targeting CHRM1 and the direct inhibition of PCa proliferation and invasion in vitro. Our research further verified that PZP exerted antitumor effects in vivo and reshaped the tumor immune environment via a novel target STING [22]. The present study further verified STING as a new target of PZP, which can not only directly target tumor cells but also potentially act on the tumor immune microenvironment. Consequently, STING promotes the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and inhibits tumor progression.

The present study indicated that PZP increased IFNB1 expression and CXCL10 secretion by upregulating STING, promoting CD8+ T cell infiltration, and potentiating the anti-PD1 therapeutic effects. Growing evidence indicates that classic STING agonists, such as cyclic dinucleotide analogues, face significant limitations in clinical applications due to their limited accessibility in the cytoplasm and susceptibility to degradation by phosphodiesterase [50]. Over the past few decades, non-cyclic dinucleotide STING agonists have been developed, such as dimethoxoxazinylacetic acid (DMXAA) and aminobenzimidazole (ABZI). Noncyclic dinucleotide agonists MSA-2 and SR-717 have been developed for activating STING and treating cancer. MSA-2 has been developed into nano-liposome vesicles or nano-adjuvants, which can rapidly activate STING and reshape the tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing the anti-tumor effect [51, 52]. However, the clinical efficacy and safety of STING agonists are limited due to challenges in drug delivery and pharmacology. Hence, no STING agonist has been approved for clinical use at present. Therefore, the development of STING agonists may enhance the efficacy of cancer treatments that rely on STING pathway activation [53]. Our results demonstrate that PZP synergizes with anti-PD1 therapy, suggesting its potential as a combination therapeutic regimen for PCa. PZP is commonly used in the treatment of gastric ulcers, duodenal ulcers, and acute gastric mucosal hemorrhage, and its clinical safety is well established [54]. Collectively, our findings suggest that PZP may serve as a safe and effective enhancer of anti-PD1-based agents in PCa patients.

Limitations

Our study offers a theoretical foundation for utilizing PZP in the treatment of PCa; however, several limitations must be acknowledged. For instance, further direct evidence is required to confirm that PZP effectively inhibits tumor growth through STING. Additionally, no long-term in vivo toxicity or pharmacokinetic analyses have been conducted regarding PZP; Considering the time and financial constraints, only relatively simple and practical experimental approaches were employed. Further in vivo and in vitro studies are essential to substantiate the findings of this research. We intend to delve deeper into this topic in the next phase.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our work suggests that PZP shows significant anti-tumor activity by suppressing PCa via upregulating STING expression. Further basic research, along with clinical trials, is essential for additional validation. More research groups are encouraged to investigate other analogous drugs and various tumor types. We believe that these studies will ultimately benefit numerous cancer patients.
