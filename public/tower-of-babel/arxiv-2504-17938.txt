Machine Learning-Based Prediction of Quality Shifts on Video Streaming Over 5G
Raza Ul Mustafa, Sesha Dassanayake, Noman Ashraf

Abstract
The Quality of Experience (QoE) is the user’s satisfaction while streaming a video session over an over-the-top (OTT) platform like YouTube. QoE of YouTube reflects the smooth streaming session without any buffering and quality shift events. One of the most important factors nowadays affecting QoE of YouTube is frequent shifts from higher to lower resolutions and vice versa. These shifts ensure a smooth streaming session; however, it might get a lower mean opinion score. For instance, dropping from 1080p to 480p during a video can preserve continuity but might reduce the viewer’s enjoyment. Over time, OTT platforms are looking for alternative ways to boost user experience instead of relying on traditional Quality of Service (QoS) metrics such as bandwidth, latency, and throughput. As a result, we look into the relationship between quality shifting in YouTube streaming sessions and the channel metrics RSRP, RSRQ, and SNR. Our findings state that these channel metrics positively correlate with shifts. Thus, in real-time, OTT can only rely on them to predict video streaming sessions into lower- and higher-resolution categories, thus providing more resources to improve user experience. Using traditional Machine Learning (ML) classifiers, we achieved an accuracy of (77%), while using only RSRP, RSRQ, and SNR. In the era of 5G and beyond, where ultra-reliable, low-latency networks promise enhanced streaming capabilities, the proposed methodology can be used to improve OTT services.



I
Introduction

The rapid increase in multimedia traffic is the most significant factor affecting the next-generation cellular network 5G and beyond; therefore, OTT platforms, such as YouTube, Netflix, etc., require more bandwidth and better signal quality to provide end-users the maximum perceived quality
[
1
]
. Video traffic is the center of these core Quality of Service (QoS) metrics, e.g., bandwidth and signal strength, streamed through various applications such as Video on Demand (VoD), live streaming, and 360 videos. Moreover, multimedia traffic’s recent standards (compression), such as High Efficiency Video Coding (H.265/HEVC) and the availability of Ultra High Definition (UHD), increase the demand for 4K videos. In addition, multimedia in fifth-generation (5G) and beyond networks centers around mobile broadcasting, remote surgery, and augmented reality, demanding real-time and ultra-high quality. Thus, quality is crucial to providing end-users maximum satisfaction, known as Quality of Experience (QoE).

QoE can be defined as Subjective or Objective: In Subjective, a rating is given to a video on a scale of 1-5 after the streaming session, known as a Mean Opinion Score (MOS); whereas in Objective, various QoE metrics are considered, such as i) quality switching (a change in resolution from higher to lower and vice-versa), ii) number of stalling events, iii) dominant resolution throughout the streaming session among many others. These aspects are essential for several reasons: engagement with high-quality content keeps end-users streaming for longer, and perceived quality can also influence the Service Level Agreement (SLA), potentially increasing overall QoE and loyalty
[
2
,
3
]
.

Predicting subjective QoE is a non-trivial task and requires a lot of resources. Therefore, Mobile Network Operators (MNOs) actively seek alternative ways of estimating end-users’ QoE to meet SLA agreements. Within this context, this work presents a novel approach to predict video quality by only relying on Channel Level Metrics (CLM). We conducted real 5G experiments in the wild, considering static and mobility use cases with YouTube as a baseline. We also consider both deployment technologies (Non-standalone – NSA and Standalone – SA) during the dataset collection campaign to generalize the work in predicting objective QoE. In summary, we address the following research questions.

•

RQ1:
Quality shifting plays a significant role in the QoE during video streaming. Do channel metrics such as RSRQ, RSRP, and SNR correlate to shifts (changes in quality)?

•

RQ2:
Can we accurately predict the quality of a receiver by categorizing streaming content into lower or higher resolutions using only channel metrics?

•

•

State of the Art

YouTube plays video in different resolutions, starting from 144p, 240p, 360p, 480p, 720p (HD), 1080p (Full HD), 1440p (2k) and 2160p (4K)
[
4
]
. Typically divided into two main categories, i) Lower- and ii) Higher resolutions. Based on network conditions, the YouTube player adapts to different resolutions.
Lower resolutions are typically considered for playing video in lower quality, such as 144p or 240p. Resolutions below 720p are generally not labeled as High Definition (HD) and are typically considered
lower
in quality or standard definition (SD). 720p is referred to as
HD Ready
, with a resolution of 1280x720 pixels, marking it as the entry-level for high-definition quality.
Therefore, we categorize the qualities into two classes: i) Lower and ii) Higher. Lower as 144p, 240p, 360p, and 480p – low quality or binary class – 0, and 720p, 1080p, 1440p, and 2160p resolutions are considered the video played in higher quality or binary class – 1.

II-B

Subjective QoE Modeling

After the video session, viewers give MOS scores to the video streaming session. In most real-life applications, video quality, initial delay and stalling events influence the MOS score
[
8
,
9
]
. Usually, MOS score is scaled to 1-5, where 5 presents the maximum perceived quality with the highest score. However, subjective testing is time-consuming, requires resources, and is not scalable. Moreover, subjective techniques are also not suitable for predicting real-time QoE
[
10
]
. We also argue subjective techniques depend on many other factors, such as age, sex, and socioeconomic status. For instance, in
[
11

, authors highlight various factors affecting the QoE, including age and sex. In addition, these factors are challenging to model in real-time systems. Therefore, MNOs rely on objective QoE assessment techniques for prediction in real-time.

II-C

Objective QoE Models

Objective QoE has gained tremendous popularity in recent years. MNOs look for ways to assess real-time QoE with a minimal interface of subjective MOS scores and fewer resources. Moreover, traditional techniques such as Peak Signal to Noise Ratio (PSNR) and Structural Similarity Index Matrix (SSIM) are criticized for being unreliable for QoE evaluation and also require original video to compare
[
10
]
. In the same context, another QoS and QoE mapping function is proposed by Hsu and Lo

for cloud-based multicast video streaming
using a simulated platform.
The work, which is very close to the objective QoE modeling, is our prior work to predict stalling events using CLM
[
13
]
. We used only channel metrics to predict stall in real-time YouTube traffic. We collected channel metrics in the wild and predicted stalls using the most recent patterns found in RSRQ, RSRP, and SNR. However, this work uses only CLM and predicts video flows into two categories: played in lower resolution or higher in real-time for YouTube. Moreover, compared to our previous work, where the dataset only considered one technology – 5G (NSA), in this work, we collected CLM metrics in Mobility and Static use cases using both technologies SA and NSA. Our technique leveraged subjective QoE assessment information and has been validated using ground truth in a realistic 5G environment.

IV-A

Correlation Between Channel Metrics and Quality Shifting

It is important to note that the channel metrics RSRP, RSRQ, and SNR are quantitative variables, while Resolutions is an ordinal variable with seven naturally ordered categories: 244p, 360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p, 1440p, and 2160p. We performed pair-wise correlations between (i) RSRP and Resolutions, (ii) RSRQ and Resolutions, and (iii) SNR and Resolutions. Since Resolutions is an ordinal variable, we calculated the Spearman’s Rank Correlation Coefficient, a non-parametric measure of rank correlation, for each pair of these variables. The Spearman’s Rank Correlation Coefficient takes values ranging from
-
to
+
1 and measures the strength and direction of association between two ranked variables. Furthermore, we employed the accompanying Spearman’s rank correlation test to determine whether there were significant correlations between each pair of variables. The results from Spearman’s rank correlation tests for data collected in Mobility (SA) and Indoor (NSA) cases are shown in Tables
II
and
III
, respectively. As shown in Tables
II
and
III
, highly significant correlations exist within each of the three pairs of variables, as evidenced by the small p-values. Furthermore, weak positive correlations also exist within each of the three pairs of variables. The magnitude of the correlation coefficient shows the strength of the association between the two variables, while the sign indicates the direction of the relationship. Since the correlations are positive, when each of the channel metrics RSRP, RSRQ, and SRN increase, perceived quality (Resolutions) also increases; when each of the metrics RSRP, RSRQ, and SRN decreases, perceived quality also decreases.

IV-B

Machine Learning Classifiers Results

In machine learning, binary classification is a supervised learning method that categorizes new observations into one of two classes. We utilized eight machine-learning binary classifiers to categorize the resolution of a running video as either high or low, using the three channel metrics – RSRP, RSQR, and SNR – as inputs. The eight binary classifiers we used are the following: (i) Decision Tree (DT), (ii) Random Forest (RF), (iii) Logistic Regression (LR), (iv) Support Vector Machine (SVM), (v) XGBoost (Extreme Gradient boosting), (vi) Stacking, (vii) Voting, and (viii) Neural Network (NN). Of the eight methods, method (vi) – Stacking –integrates multiple models through a meta-model, while method (vii) – Voting – aggregates predictions or probabilities. We use default parameter settings for all traditional ML classifiers. However, for NN, we used
pytorch
, and the model’s architecture consists of two hidden layers, with 32 output neurons from the first layer and 100 neurons from the second hidden layer. Three input features are forwarded as input to the first layer, and the last layer provides logits of possible probabilities where we use the
sigmoid
activation function to classify the logits into two classes we have. Finally, NN trained on 10,000 epochs; the loss function is
BCEWithLogitsLoss
and the learning rate is
0.0001
. Please note: We tried other combinations during the training, such as increasing the number of epochs, changing the loss function, and playing with the learning rate. However, the parameters mentioned above provide the best results.

V
Conclusion

From the perspective of Mobile Network Operators (MNOs), ensuring Quality of Experience (QoE) poses a significant challenge in fulfilling Service Level Agreements (SLAs). Therefore, MNOs seek alternative metrics to forecast end-users’ QoE instead of relying on traditional Key Performance Indicators (KPIs). In this work, we provide classifiers to predict objective QoE perceived quality on real 5G data using both technology Standalone (SA) and (Non-Standalone). We achieved an accuracy of 77% on both scenarios, such as static Indoor (NSA) and Mobility (SA). We also find that the channel metrics RSRP, RSRQ, and SNR correlate with objective QoE perceived quality in both technology SA and NSA in both scenarios, Static and Mobility.
Moreover, the model performance varies across Static – NSA and Mobility – SA use cases. In Static, models demonstrate strong predictive capability for Class - High, as reflected in the confusion matrix
1
. However, in Mobility, the model performs better in identifying Class - Low, achieving improved classification metrics for this category see confusion matrix
2
. Our results suggest that the effectiveness of ML classifiers is influenced by the characteristics of NSA and SA along with motion (Static and Mobility).
This study has limitations, which we aim to address in future work. First, frequent quality switching is extremely challenging to capture while collecting datasets in the wild in a 5G environment, as streaming sessions predominantly occur at either 2160p or 1440p resolutions. Therefore, future work could use generative AI to generate more realistic instances of quality shifting that will be helpful for the training of ML classifiers. Second, this study also considers YouTube as a baseline therefore, there is scope to broaden this study to consider other OTT platforms that would enhance understanding of 5G NSA and SA impact on video streaming, especially quality shifting. Finally, with the recent rise of omnidirectional videos, future work includes investigating the impact of CLMs on buffering and quality shifts in 5G settings.
