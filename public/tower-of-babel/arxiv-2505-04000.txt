Interval-closed set rowmotion and homomesy on products of two chains
Nadia Lafrenière, Joel Brewster Lewis, Erin McNicholas, Jessica Striker, Amanda Welch

Abstract
We study rowmotion dynamics on interval-closed sets. Our first main result proves a simplification of the global definition of interval-closed set rowmotion from (Elder, Lafrenière, McNicholas, Striker, and Welch 2024). We then completely describe the orbits of interval-closed set rowmotion on products of two chains [ 2 ] × [ n ] [2]\times[n] and use this understanding to prove a homomesy conjecture from (ELMSW 2024) involving the signed cardinality statistic.

Introduction

Rowmotion is a natural action
with surprisingly nice dynamical properties in certain cases.
An interesting feature is that this action has both global and local definitions; globally, it may be seen as a convex closure operation, while locally, it is given as a composition of toggle operators.
Rowmotion was originally studied on order ideals of a finite poset (equivalently, distributive lattices)
[
23
]
and has recently been extended to generalized realms
[
9
,
3
]
, other combinatorial objects
[
22
,
15
]
, and other types of lattices
[
2
,
24
,
7
]
. Rowmotion has even been categorified
[
14
]
. Many foundational results in dynamical algebraic combinatorics relate to this action; see the survey articles
[
17
,
21
,
13
]
.

Recently, Elder, Lafrenière, McNicholas, Striker, and Welch
[
11
]
defined rowmotion on the set
\mathcal{IC}(P)
of
interval-closed sets
(or convex subsets) of a poset, a natural superset of order ideals, giving both local and global characterizations.
The local characterization is a natural toggle action for interval-closed sets, analogous to the definition for order ideals: for
x\in P
, the toggle
t_{x}
acts on an interval-closed set
I
by taking the symmetric difference of
\{x\}
and
I
, provided this is an interval-closed set, and as the identity otherwise. The local definition of rowmotion applies all toggles in the poset from top to bottom, that is, in the reverse order of any linear extension. The global characterization of ICS rowmotion, though, is much more complicated than the analogous definition for order ideals.
Our first main theorem is a simplification of this global definition of rowmotion on interval-closed sets; see Section
2
for the notation.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given an interval-closed set
I\in\mathcal{IC}(P)
, rowmotion on
I
is given by

\mathrm{Row}(I)=\Big(\mathrm{Inc}(\mathrm{Ceil}({I}))-I\Big)\cup\Big(\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I})-\Delta(\mathrm{Min}(I)\cap\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I}))\Big).

The orbits of rowmotion on

2
A simpler global definition of rowmotion

In
[
11
]
, rowmotion on interval-closed sets was defined in two ways. It was first defined as a composition of
toggles
through which we add or remove individual elements in an interval-closed set, i.e., the
local
definition. Rowmotion was then characterized
globally
(
[
11
, Theorem 2.20]
) and it was shown that the two definitions are equivalent. The global definition sped up computations significantly, allowing the authors to search more effectively for dynamical phenomena. We recall this result now; see Definition
1.5
for necessary notations.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
11
, Theorem 2.20]
)
.

Given an interval-closed set
I\in\mathcal{IC}(P)
, rowmotion on
I
is given by

\mathrm{Row}(I)=\mathrm{Inc}(I)\cup\Big(\Delta\mathrm{Inc}_{I}\big(\mathrm{Ceil}({I})\big)-\big(I\cup\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I})\big)\Big)\cup\Big(\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I})-\Delta(\mathrm{Min}(I)\cap\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I}))\Big).

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
, giving a simpler characterization of the global definition of rowmotion, presented as the union of two sets rather than three.

Theorem
1.1
.

Given an interval-closed set
I\in\mathcal{IC}(P)
, rowmotion on
I
is given by

\mathrm{Row}(I)=\Big(\mathrm{Inc}(\mathrm{Ceil}({I}))-I\Big)\cup\Big(\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I})-\Delta(\mathrm{Min}(I)\cap\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I}))\Big).

Proof.

Recall from Theorem
2.1
above that

\mathrm{Row}(I)=\mathrm{Inc}(I)\cup\Big(\Delta\mathrm{Inc}_{I}\big(\mathrm{Ceil}({I})\big)-\big(I\cup\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I})\big)\Big)\cup\Big(\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I})-\Delta(\mathrm{Min}(I)\cap\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I}))\Big).

So we need to show the following (where we have labeled the sets in each union for brevity):

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathrm{Inc}(I)}_{A}\cup

\displaystyle\underbrace{\Big(\Delta\mathrm{Inc}_{I}\big(\mathrm{Ceil}({I})\big)-\big(I\cup\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I})\big)\Big)}_{B}\cup\underbrace{\Big(\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I})-\Delta(\mathrm{Min}(I)\cap\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I}))\Big)}_{C}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Big(\mathrm{Inc}(\mathrm{Ceil}({I}))-I\Big)}_{D}\cup\underbrace{\Big(\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I})-\Delta(\mathrm{Min}(I)\cap\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I}))\Big)}_{C}.

We proceed by double inclusion. Since the last set of each union is
C
, we only need to show that
A\cup B\subseteq D\cup C
and that
D\subseteq A\cup B\cup C
. We show below that
D\subseteq A\cup B
,
A\subseteq D\cup C
and that
B\subseteq D
.

We consider
x\in\mathrm{Inc}(\mathrm{Ceil}({I}))-I
(so
x\in D
). Then,
x
is incomparable with the ceiling of
I
, and not in
I
. So,
x
cannot be in the order filter spanned by
I
, so it is either incomparable with
I
, or in its order ideal (but not in
I
). In the first case,
x\in A
. In the second case,
x
is in the order ideal of
I
but not in
I
, and incomparable with the ceiling. Hence,
x\in B
.

Now let
x
be in
\mathrm{Inc}(I)
. Because
x
is incomparable with
I
, it cannot be in the order filter of
I
, so it is not in the ceiling of
I
nor above. It can be either incomparable with the ceiling of
I
(so in
D
), or below the ceiling of
I
but not in the order ideal of
I
, so
x\in C
.

The last case to consider is when
x
is in
\Big(\Delta\mathrm{Inc}_{I}\big(\mathrm{Ceil}({I})\big)-\big(I\cup\Delta\mathrm{Ceil}({I})\big)\Big)
(so in
B
). We need to show that
x\in D\cup C
, and it suffices to show that
x\in D
, so this is what we do. In that case,
x
is below an element of
I
that is incomparable with the ceiling, but
x
itself is neither in
I
nor below the ceiling. Therefore,
x
is incomparable with the ceiling and also not in
I
, so
x\in D
.
∎

Any interval-closed set that is neither the empty set nor the full poset is in exactly one of the types

Proof.

Let

and

•

If
b_{1}=b_{2}

–

if

–

otherwise,
a_{1}=a_{2}

•

Otherwise,
b_{1}>b_{2}

–

If
a_{1}=a_{2}
, then
I

–

Otherwise,
a_{1}<a_{2}

*

3.2
Calculating the signed cardinality

Using the notation from Subsection
3.1
, we now compute the signed cardinality for the ICS of
[2]\times[n]
.
Recall from Definition
1.14
, that for all
x\in P
, the
signed cardinality statistic

\mathrm{sc}(x):P\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
is

\mathrm{sc}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if }\textrm{rk}(x)\textrm{ is even, }\\
-1&\textrm{if }\textrm{rk}(x)\textrm{ is odd. }\end{cases}

For
I\subseteq P
, let
\mathrm{sc}(I)=\sum_{x\in I}\mathrm{sc}(x)
.
Using the
parity indicator function

\delta_{x}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $x$ is odd,}\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1)

we can directly calculate the signed cardinality for ICS in
P=[2]\times[n]
, as follows.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
P=[2]\times[n]
with
n\geq 2
and let
I=[b_{1},i_{1},a_{1}:b_{2},i_{2},a_{2}]\in\mathcal{IC}(P)
. Then

\mathrm{sc}(I)=\delta_{i_{1}}(-1)^{b_{1}}+\delta_{i_{2}}(-1)^{b_{2}+1}.

(2)

Proof.

The ICS
I:=[b_{1},i_{1},a_{1}:b_{2},i_{2},a_{2}]
has entries on the lower diagonal in ranks
b_{1},\ldots,b_{1}+i_{1}-1
and on the upper diagonal in ranks
b_{2}+1,\ldots,b_{2}+i_{2}
. Since
\sum_{k=r}^{s}(-1)^{k}=\frac{(-1)^{r}+(-1)^{s}}{2}
, it follows that the signed cardinality of
I
is
\frac{(-1)^{b_{1}}+(-1)^{b_{1}+i_{1}-1}}{2}+\frac{(-1)^{b_{2}+1}+(-1)^{b_{2}+i_{2}}}{2}=\delta_{i_{1}}(-1)^{b_{1}}+\delta_{i_{2}}(-1)^{b_{2}+1}.

∎

Using Lemma
3.3
, it is easy to prove the following propositions, which are integral in proving Theorem
1.3
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
P=[2]\times[n]
with
n\geq 1
. Then
\mathrm{sc}(P)=0
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
P=[2]\times[n]
with
n\geq 1
and let
I\in\mathcal{IC}(P)
. Then
\mathrm{sc}(I)+\mathrm{sc}(\overline{I})=0
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
P=[2]\times[n]
with
n\geq 1
, let
I
be an ICS in
P
that does not contain the top element in either chain, and let
I^{\prime}
be the result of shifting
I
up by one rank. Then
\mathrm{sc}(I)+\mathrm{sc}(I^{\prime})=0
.

3.5
The quadratic orbits

In this section, we examine the third class of orbits under rowmotion, those that have large size that grows quadratically with
n
. These orbits are unlike other orbit classes, which are either of size 2 or grow linearly with
n.
Most orbits in this third class have lower-chain singleton representatives, i.e., interval-closed sets of the form
I=[x-1,1,n-x:\varnothing]=\{(1,x)\}.

We give a characterization of these orbits in Theorem
3.26
, while in Theorem
3.27
, we prove the average value of the signed cardinality statistic for each of these orbits is zero when
n
is odd.
To prove Theorems
3.26
and
3.27
, we study commonly appearing orbit substructures, ultimately focusing our attention on substructures between consecutive appearances of lower-chain singleton ICS. In particular, we find that for
1<x\leq n-6,
the next lower-chain singleton appearing in an orbit after
\{(1,x)\}
is
\{(1,x+6)\}
(Lemma
3.23
). A more complicated process determines the next lower-chain singleton appearing in an orbit after
\{(1,n-y)\}
for
y<6
(Lemma
3.25
). These two results allow us to use the ordering of lower-chain singleton representatives to stitch together the larger orbits based on values of
x
and
n\bmod{6}.
In particular, we are able to determine the size and lower-chain representatives of these orbits from subcycles of a weighted permutation.

The first substructures we observe give rules for mapping between low and high interval-closed sets under applications of rowmotion, Propositions
3.20
and
3.21

3.2

,

Remark 3.29
.

This characterization of the orbit lengths includes representatives for all orbits (see the theorems of this section cited in the proof). Our characterization shows that if an orbit contains no low interval-closed sets (those that only have elements in the first chain) or high interval-closed sets (those that have only elements in the second chain), then the orbit is of size dividing
n+3
. The vast majority of orbits are of this type. All orbits of other sizes contain either the empty interval-closed set or an interval on a single chain. In particular, a given single-chain ICS belongs to an orbit of the following size:

•

n+3
, if the ICS is either of the singleton sets
\{(1,1)\}
or
\{(2,n)\}
, i.e., the minimum element or the maximum element of the poset;

•

n+5
or
\frac{n+5}{3}
, if the ICS is an interval of size at least
4
and either on the first chain and not including
(1,1)
,
(1,2)
or
(1,3)
, or on the second chain and not including
(2,n)
,
(2,n-1)
or
(2,n-2)
;

•

quadratic size, if the ICS is an interval of size at least
4
and includes any of
(1,1)
,
(1,2)
,
(1,3)
,
(2,n)
,
(2,n-1)
or
(2,n-2)
;

•

quadratic size, if the ICS has size at most
3
, and it is neither
\{(1,1)\}
nor
\{(2,n)\}
. (In particular, all the orbits of quadratic size contain a singleton representative, except for one orbit with no singleton when

It is worth noting that homomesy only occurs for the
[2]\times[n]
poset when
n
is odd, as shown in the proposition below.

Proposition 3.30
.

Let
n
be positive and even. Then, the signed cardinality statistic is not homomesic under rowmotion for the poset
[2]\times[n]
.

Proof.

For homomesy, we need the global average of signed cardinality to be the same as the average over every orbit. Under rowmotion, every nonempty poset has an orbit of size
2
containing the whole poset and the empty ICS, which both have signed cardinality
0
. However, the global average of signed cardinality is not
0
for the poset
[2]\times[n]
when
n
is even. Indeed, the average would be
0
only if the sum of the signed cardinality over all ICS would be
0
. We show here that the total signed cardinality is
\frac{-n^{2}(n+1)}{4}
over ICS of
[2]\times[n]
with
n
even.

Interval-closed sets of
[2]\times[n]
are of one of the following types, which are disjoint:

•

an interval-closed set of
[2]\times[n-1]
embedded in the
[2]\times[n]
poset. Since rowmotion is
0
-mesic for ICS of
[2]\times[n-1]
when
n
is even, the sum of the signed cardinality for these ICS is
0
.

•

a low ICS
[b,n-b,0:\varnothing]
or a high ICS
[\varnothing:b,n-b,0]
, for
0\leq b\leq n-1
. For any given value of
b
, the low ICS
[b,n-b,0:\varnothing]
has opposite signed cardinality value of that of the high ICS
[\varnothing:b,n-b,0]
, so the total sum of the signed cardinality for these ICS is
0
.

•

an interval-closed set with elements in both the lower and upper chains, and containing
(1,n)
. This is the ICS
[b_{1},n-b_{1},0:b_{2},i_{2},n-i_{2}-b_{2}]
with
1\leq i_{2}\leq n
,
0\leq b_{1}\leq n-1
and
0\leq b_{2}\leq n-i_{2}
. Following Lemma
3.3
, the total signed cardinality for these ICS is

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n}\sum_{b_{1}=0}^{n-1}\sum_{b_{2}=0}^{n-i_{2}}\left(\delta_{b_{1}}(-1)^{b_{1}}+\delta_{i_{2}}(-1)^{b_{2}+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n}\sum_{b_{1}=0}^{n-1}\sum_{b_{2}=0}^{n-i_{2}}-\delta_{b_{1}}+n\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n}\sum_{b_{2}=0}^{n-i_{2}}\delta_{i_{2}}(-1)^{b_{2}+1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{n}{2}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{n}(n-i_{2}+1)+0

\displaystyle=-\frac{n^{2}(n+1)}{4},

where the second-to-last equality comes from parity analysis: when
n
is even and
i_{2}
is odd, there is an even number of values of
b_{2}
between
0
and
n-i_{2}
, meaning that the sum of
(-1)^{b_{2}+1}
over these values of
b_{2}
is
0
.

Therefore, signed cardinality cannot be homomesic under rowmotion for
[2]\times[n]
with
n
even.
∎

In this section, we discuss some conjectures and directions for future work.

4.1
Rowmotion orbits of interval-closed sets of
[m]\times[n]

Experimental evidence suggests that it will not be possible to fully catalogue rowmotion orbits in
[m]\times[n]
for
m>2
and arbitrary
n
. Nevertheless, some steps in this direction may be possible. It follows from Theorem
1.2
that for large
n
, the vast majority of ICS of
[2]\times[n]
belong to orbits of size
n+3
. Experimental evidence suggests that this extends to
[m]\times[n]
, in the following sense.

Conjecture 4.1
.

Fix any positive integer
m
. Let
r(n)
be the fraction of interval-closed sets of
[m]\times[n]
that belong to orbits of size
m+n+1
. Then
r(n)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.

One may view this conjecture as asserting that, although the overall behavior of rowmotion on ICS is complicated and of large order, its behavior on
typical
ICS is very nice. It would be possible in principle to prove Conjecture
4.1
by identifying a sufficiently large family of orbits of size
m+n+1
, and comparing with the enumeration of all ICS. We outline a strategy in this direction here.

Say that an ICS
I
in
[m]\times[n]
is
good
if

I=[(1,a_{1}),(1,b_{1})]\cup[(2,a_{2}),(2,b_{2})]\cup\cdots\cup[(m,a_{m}),(m,b_{m})]

such that no two of
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\}
differ by less than
2
. The number of good ICS is
C_{m}\cdot\binom{n-2m+1}{2m}
, where
C_{m}
is the
m
th Catalan number
C_{m}=\frac{1}{m+1}\binom{2m}{m}
. The binomial coefficient
\binom{n-2m+1}{2m}
counts the number of ways to choose
2m
distinct elements from
\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that no two differ by
1
, and there are
C_{m}
ways to assign these
2m
distinct values to be
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}
(the conditions
a_{1}>a_{2}>\cdots>a_{m}
,
b_{1}>\cdots>b_{m}
, and
a_{i}<b_{i}
on the assignment are essentially those of a standard Young tableau of shape
\langle m,m\rangle
). If
m
is fixed, then as
n\to\infty
, this number grows like
\frac{n^{2m}}{m!(m+1)!}+O(n^{2m-1})
. Then
if
one could show that all such elements belong to orbits of size
m+n+1
,
and
one could show that the total number of ICS of
[m]\times[n]
for fixed
m
and
n\to\infty
is asymptotic to
\frac{n^{2m}}{m!(m+1)!}
, that would be sufficient to prove the conjecture. One should be able to extract the latter (enumerative) piece from the formulas in
[
12
]
.
