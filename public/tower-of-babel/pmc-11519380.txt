Predictive power of the eTBI score for 30 day outcome in elderly patients with traumatic brain Injury
Authors: Mehdi Shafiei, Shiva Maleki, Mehdi Nasr Isfahani, Alireza Amin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a common problem in elderly individuals, with significant morbidity and mortality. The elderly Traumatic Brain Injury (eTBI) score, a novel tool for predicting outcomes in elderly patients with TBI, has shown promising results in previous studies. This study aimed to validate the eTBI score in a larger cohort of elderly patients with TBI in the Middle East. We conducted a retrospective study on 337 TBI patients with a mean age of 73.04 ± 8.73, admitted to a tertiary care hospital between March 2021 and November 2022. Within 30 days of admission, the patients’ conditions, including mortality and entering a vegetative state, were evaluated. The study population was split into three groups based on eTBI score: low, medium, and high risk; then patients were divided into two subgroups based on their Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS ≤ 2, GOS > 2) in 30 days from hospital admission. Poor outcomes (mortality and entering a vegetative state) occurred in 24.3% of the study population. Within 30 days of hospital admission, 88% of low-risk patients experienced some degree of improvement, while 100% of high-risk patients died or fell into a vegetative state. In the medium-risk group, there was a significant correlation between unresponsive pupil (P = 0.006), initial GCS score (P = 0.003), need for a ventilator device (P = 0.015), need for surgical treatment (P = 0.031) and poor outcomes. Despite having a low sensitivity (21% vs. 57%), the eTBI score performed well in terms of accuracy (81% vs. 88%), specificity (100 vs. 98%), positive predictive value (100% vs. 90%), and negative predictive value (80% vs. 88%) for both eTBI ≤ 0 and eTBI ≤ 3 thresholds. The eTBI score is a reliable tool for predicting outcomes in elderly patients with TBI. This scoring system has a positive predictive value of 100% in the eTBI ≤ 0 group, which shows that 100% of the patients who are predicted by the eTBI score to have a poor outcome will indeed have a poor outcome. Patients in the high-risk group should be closely monitored and provided with intensive care, while those in the low-risk group can be reassured about their prognosis. The eTBI score can also be used in conjunction with other clinical factors to inform treatment decisions for patients in the medium-risk group.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a condition that occurs when the brain suffers damage, either physically or functionally, as a result of an external impact1. It is a significant global health concern, contributing to approximately 30% of all injury-related deaths. Each year, between 54 and 60 million new cases of TBI are reported worldwide2–4. Furthermore, trauma-related deaths account for 4.48 million people each year, with TBI responsible for 2 million of these fatalities5,6. It is estimated that each year, 10 million individuals either die or require hospitalization due to TBI7.

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is used to assess the severity of TBI. Mild TBI, defined by GCS scores ranging from 13 to 15. Moderate TBI is classified by GCS scores of 9 to 12, and severe TBI by scores of 8 or below8–10.

While TBI affects individuals across all age groups, older adults represent a disproportionately high number of cases11. In the United States, for example, elderly individuals accounted for 43.9% of all TBI-related hospitalizations and 38.4% of TBI-related deaths in 2017, despite constituting only 15.2% of the population12. Falls and motor vehicle accidents are the primary causes of TBI in this age group, with mortality rates reaching 16% for hospitalized elderly patients13,14. The severity of TBI correlates strongly with in-hospital mortality, ranging from 5% in mild cases to 65% in severe cases14.

There is considerable controversy in the literature over the age obstacle used to define the elderly population. Various studies have found that this threshold ranges from 60 to 80 years15–17. This variation highlights the challenges of establishing a common definition across different research contexts, as well as the importance of precisely stating the criteria used in each study.

Advanced age is a well-established independent predictor of poor outcomes following TBI18,19. Managing TBI in older patients is further complicated by the presence of comorbidities such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and the use of anticoagulant medications, which make the clinical course less predictable12.

The GCS is a clinical tool used to assess the level of consciousness in TBI patients, with scores ranging from 3 to 15, where higher scores indicate less severe injury7,20. However, in elderly patients, even those with relatively normal GCS scores may experience severe brain injury or death21. In response to these challenges, the elderly traumatic brain injury (eTBI) score was developed in 2019 to better predict outcomes in older TBI patients. The eTBI score incorporates four components: the GCS motor score (GCS-M), platelet count, red cell distribution width (RDW-CV), and the presence of comorbid conditions such as cardiac, pulmonary, or renal dysfunction, or malignancy22,23. Considering the increasing mortality rates reported among elderly patients with TBI and the challenges associated with their management, particularly due to comorbid conditions and the use of antithrombotic medications, our study aims to validate the predictive power of the eTBI score. The objective of this study was to assess the performance of the eTBI scoring system in predicting outcomes within this vulnerable population.

Method

Study design

From March 2021 to November 2022, we performed a retrospective case-control study on elderly traumatic brain injury patients who were referred to the level-1 trauma center of Kashani in Isfahan. This study defined elderly patients as individuals over 60 years of age, based on World Health Organization and United Nations guidelines24.

Participants

We obtained patient information from the available medical documents in the recoded archives of the hospital. Patients with the discharge diagnosis code S06 according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) were recruited.

The study included elderly patients with TBI, all of whom underwent a brain CT scan that showed evidence of skull fractures and/or brain injury. The exclusion criteria for this study included: patients who died on arrival at the hospital due to multiple traumas and severe associated injuries before undergoing an initial brain CT scan, Individuals with a history of head trauma but no evidence of skull fractures or brain injuries on brain CT scans, Patients observed for only 24 h and discharged without an initial brain CT scan, individuals with missing medical histories or laboratory results, and patients who died within 30 days of admission due to causes unrelated to TBI, such as myocardial ischemia or acute respiratory disease (including COVID-19). The patients’ selection process is shown on the study diagram flowchart (Fig. 1). The research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Ethical considerations

The research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Research Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences approved the utilization of anonymous data without requiring patient consent (Approval No.: IR.MUI.MED.REC.1402.350).

For the initial phase of the study, researchers only had access to anonymous data, which did not permit the identification of individuals. However, for the follow-up phase, where it was necessary to determine the causes of mortality, explicit informed consent was obtained from the patients or their legal guardians before contacting their families. This consent process was documented and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB).

The follow-up information was collected separately and was not linked to the anonymous data set used for the main analysis. This ensured that the anonymity of the initial data was maintained. All ethical standards and guidelines were strictly adhered to throughout the study.

Data collection

We collected and documented participants’ demographic information such as age and gender, medical histories, anticoagulant use, trauma mechanisms, neurological status, initial GCS score, associated injuries, radiological findings, and laboratory results, in addition to information on the patients’ hospitalizations from their medical records. Mortality or entering a vegetative state (which we defined as a poor outcome) in elderly patients with traumatic brain injuries was assessed up to 30 days after admission to the hospital. Assessment of the patients who were discharged in less than 30 days was performed through a phone call with their families.

Patient classification

We divided our study population based on eTBI score risk stratification into three groups: low risk (eTBI ≥ 4), medium risk (1 ≤ eTBI ≤ 3), and high risk (eTBI ≤ 0); then patients were separated into two subgroups according to their Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS ≤ 2, GOS > 2) in 30 days of hospital admission.

The Glasgow Outcome Scale was used to assess patient disability levels post-injury. This validated scale assigns scores ranging from 1 (death) to 5 (good recovery), with higher scores indicating better functional status25,26. For this study, poor outcome was defined as GOS score 1 (death) or 2 (vegetative state), concordant with previous research investigating TBI prognosis27. In this investigation, we determined the degree of recovery as a GOS of 3–5.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 27. Quantitative data were summarized using means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on data distribution, while qualitative data were presented as frequencies with percentages. To explore associations between GOS scores (≤ 2 vs. >2) and categorical study variables, chi-square tests were utilized. For comparisons of continuous variables between groups, independent samples t-tests and Mann-Whitney U tests were employed as appropriate.

To evaluate the predictive value of the main components of the eTBI score for 30-day poor outcomes (GOS 1–2), binary logistic regression analysis was conducted. The model was adjusted for multiple potential confounders, including demographic factors (age and gender), clinical factors (presence or absence of associated injuries such as pulmonary contusion, large bone fractures, pelvic fractures, and organ damage), radiological findings (subarachnoid hemorrhage, subdural hemorrhage, epidural hemorrhage, intracranial hemorrhage, intraventricular hemorrhage, brain contusion), laboratory data (prothrombin time, partial thromboplastin time, international normalized ratio), use of anticoagulant drugs, and treatment interventions (requirement for surgical intervention, admission to the intensive care unit, and the need for ventilator support).

These control variables were included to isolate the independent effects of the eTBI score components on the outcome, thereby minimizing confounding bias. Statistical significance was determined using a threshold of p < 0.05, and the strength of associations was quantified by estimating odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

The primary objective of this study was to assess the predictive power of the eTBI score in forecasting poor outcomes (mortality or entering a vegetative state) in elderly TBI patients. Additionally, at two thresholds (eTBI ≤ 0 and eTBI ≤ 3), we measured the accuracy (the overall proportion of correct predictions for both poor and good outcomes), sensitivity (the ability of the eTBI score to correctly identify patients with a poor outcome), specificity (the ability of the eTBI score to correctly identify patients with a good outcome), positive predictive value (the proportion of patients with a poor outcome among those predicted to have a poor outcome by the eTBI score), and negative predictive value (the proportion of patients with a good outcome among those predicted to have a good outcome by the eTBI score).

Result

A total of 920 patients were initially enrolled in the study. However, 575 patients were excluded based on predefined criteria. Specifically, 67 patients were excluded due to death on arrival from multiple traumas and severe injuries before a brain CT scan could be performed. Additionally, 397 individuals were excluded because they had a history of head trauma without skull fractures or brain injuries on brain CT scans. After 24 h of monitoring, 79 patients were discharged without undergoing an initial brain CT scan; 32 patients had incomplete medical histories or laboratory results; and 8 patients died within 30 days of admission due to causes unrelated to TBI, such as myocardial ischemia or acute respiratory disease (including COVID-19).

Finally, 337 patients met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study, with a mean age of 73.04 ± 8.73 years. Of these, 81 (24%) were female and 256 (76%) were male. Figure 1 illustrates the study flow diagram and patient selection process.

According to Table 2, the most prevalent cause of TBI among the elderly in this study was car accidents, accounting for 183 cases (54.3%), followed by falls, which accounted for 142 cases (42.1%). Mild TBI, defined by an initial GCS score of 13–15, was observed in 242 individuals (71.8%). The median length of stay in the hospital was 4 days (range 1–101), and the median ICU stay was 2 days (range 0–84). Surgical treatment was required for 60 patients (17.8%), while 96 patients (28.5%) needed ventilator support. The most common radiological finding was subarachnoid hemorrhage, present in 173 patients (51.3%). The mortality rate was 14.8%, with 50 patients succumbing to their injuries, while 287 patients (85.2%) survived.

The study population was divided into three groups based on eTBI score: low, medium, and high risk. 286 patients were in the low-risk (the largest group in the study); and 34 and 17 patients had medium and high risk, respectively (Table 4).

In the study population, the prevalence of poor outcomes (mortality and vegetative state) was 24.3%. None of the patients in the high-risk group recovered from TBI, and all died or entered a vegetative state. The median of the initial GCS in this group was 3, with a range of 3 to 4. In the same way, the median of GCS-M was 1, with a range of 1 to 2. 15 (88.2%) patients had unresponsive pupils on initial neurologic examinations. 12 (70.6%) patients were admitted to the ICU, while all the patients in the high-risk group needed a ventilator device. 11 (64.7%) of the 17 patients in the high-risk group died.

In the medium-risk group, only 5 patients showed any degree of recovery within the initial 30 days following their admission. In this group, 30-day mortality or entering a vegetative state was significantly associated with the following factors: the presence of an unresponsive pupil (P = 0.006), the initial GCS score (P = 0.003), the need for ventilatory support (P = 0.015), and the requirement for surgical intervention (P = 0.031).

In the low-risk group, two patients (1.47%) with an eTBI score of 6, five patients (4.62%) with an eTBI score of 5, and nine patients (21.42%) with an eTBI score of 4 died within the first 30 days following hospital admission.

The predictive performance of the eTBI high-risk group and combination of high and medium risk for a 30-day poor outcome (GOS 1–2) in elderly TBI patients is shown in Table 4. Despite relatively low sensitivity (21% vs. 57%), the eTBI score demonstrated great performance in accuracy (81% vs. 88%), specificity (100 vs. 98%), positive predictive value (100% vs. 90%), and negative predictive value (80% vs. 88%) for both eTBI ≤ 0 and eTBI ≤ 3 thresholds.

Discussion

This study shows that the eTBI score has significant predictive power for poor outcome (GOS = 1–2) in elderly TBI patients within 30 days of hospital admission. Bobeff et al. introduced the eTBI scoring system in 201922. In 2022, their research team conducted a study to validate the eTBI score specifically for elderly TBI patients23. To ensure reliability and comparability with their previous findings, we used the same 3-tier risk stratification. This current study serves as a second validation of the eTBI score’s effectiveness in predicting 30-day outcomes for elderly TBI patients.

We found that 100% of high-risk patients died or entered a vegetative state, and 88% of low-risk patients had some degree of recovery in the first 30 days following their admission, as shown in Table 5. The result of our study was in the same overall direction as the previous validation study. Some of the other studies in the field of elderly TBI have used only the initial GCS score for the prediction of the GOS of their population, and have suggested that lower GCS scores have a significant association with poor outcomes for the elderly TBI patients28,29.

The inclusion of the other components in the eTBI score (platelet count, RDW-CV, and comorbid conditions) illustrates a multifactorial approach to assessing patient risk, even though in our study they did not individually reach statistical significance (Table 2). Each component may contribute to the overall score’s ability to stratify patients, especially when considered in conjunction with GCS-M.

The fact that the eTBI score performs reasonably well in differentiating outcomes, despite the significance of only GCS-M, suggests that the score may capture a broader clinical picture that incorporates both neurological and systemic factors. This holistic approach is particularly important in the context of elderly patients, where comorbidities and physiological reserve can significantly influence outcomes.

Wan et al. conducted a study on 137 elderly patients to assess the efficiency and utility of the International Mission for Prognosis and Analysis of Clinical Trials in TBI (IMPACT) model for assessing the outcome of severe TBI. They observed that the real results in a sample of elderly patients with severe TBI were better than those predicted by the IMPACT model. Their model indicated a probability of more than 70% for the unfavorable outcome, whereas the actual risk was more than 80%30. In a retrospective study of 206 patients aged ≥ 65 years who underwent craniotomy after acute TBI, Hazare et al. found pupillary reaction and motor score to be important predictors of mortality. The study found that 116 patients (56.3%) died after 6 months; 1.2% of patients who developed a vegetative state survived; and dilated pupils and no response increased the risk of death31.

In our study, all 24 patients with an initial GCS of 3 and 12 patients with an initial GCS of 4 died or fell into a vegetative state (GOS = 1–2). 84% of patients were classified as low-risk. In the latter group, the initial GCS score, GCS-M score, length of stay in hospital, admission in the ICU and ICU length of stay, need for a ventilator device, and need for surgical intervention all had a significant association with a poor outcome in 30 days from hospital admission (P ≤ 0.001 for each variable). This may further help clinicians to understand those patients who may do poorly even with a low-risk eTBI score. It has been suggested by other authors that neurosurgical intervention after mild TBI could be beneficial, and intervention after the failure of conservative management has a worse outcome than immediate intervention32.

While the eTBI scoring system demonstrated strong predictive power for unfavorable outcomes in moderate and high-risk patients, its performance in prediction of good outcomes (GOS > 2) in low-risk patients (eTBI > 3) appears less reliable. Despite a high sensitivity (98%) and a reasonable negative predictive value (90%), the specificity (57%) and positive predictive value (87.7%) were lower in this subgroup. Notably, a small number of low-risk patients still died or entered a vegetative state within 30 days post-TBI.

This suggests that factors beyond the components of the eTBI score may be at play in these outcomes. Non-trauma-related elements, such as other pre-existing comorbidities, age-related frailty, or acute systemic events, likely contribute to unfavorable outcomes in low-risk patients. These results indicate that while the eTBI score functions well in predicting outcomes in moderate and high-risk patients, it may require further refinement for low-risk individuals. A future version of the score could consider additional factors tailored to this population or limit the use of the eTBI score primarily to moderate and high-risk categories, where it demonstrates greater predictive accuracy.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the eTBI score has significant predictive power in determining poor outcomes such as mortality or entering a vegetative state in elderly patients with TBI. We found a strong association between lower GCS motor scores and the likelihood of a poor outcome within 30 days. The eTBI score effectively stratifies patients into low, medium, and high-risk groups, with the high-risk group having a 100% likelihood of poor outcomes. The score showed good accuracy, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value in predicting poor outcomes. The findings support the use of the TBI score as a tool for identifying high-risk patients and guiding treatment decisions. Further research is needed to validate the findings of this study in larger, multicenter studies and to explore the applicability of the eTBI score in different healthcare settings.

Limitation

This study has certain limitations. It was a study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the study relied on retrospective data collection and analysis, which introduces the possibility of bias and incomplete information.

One of the key limitations of this study is the exclusion of 397 patients who experienced head trauma but showed no evidence of skull fractures or brain injuries on their initial CT scans. While these patients presented with a GCS score of 13–15, which is typical of older patients seen in clinical practice following fall-related trauma, more advanced imaging techniques such as diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) may have revealed subtle microstructural damage to the brain, particularly in the white matter. As such, the exclusion of these patients limits the generalizability of our findings, as the study may not fully represent the broader population of older TBI patients commonly encountered in clinical settings. Future studies should incorporate advanced imaging modalities to provide a more comprehensive assessment of TBI, including cases where conventional imaging fails to detect brain injuries.

Implications for practice

Healthcare professionals can use the eTBI score to stratify elderly TBI patients into low-, medium-, and high-risk groups, predicting the likelihood of poor outcomes.
