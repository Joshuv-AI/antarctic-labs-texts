Influence of diabetes on short-term mortality of patients with acute type A aortic dissection: a meta-analysis
Authors: Zhiheng Liu, Ning Wu, Fan Wu, Tao Liu, Kai Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is a life-threatening cardiovascular emergency with substantial early mortality. Whether diabetes mellitus (DM) independently influences short-term mortality in ATAAD remains uncertain. We conducted a meta-analysis to synthesize the available data.MethodsPubMed, Embase, and Web of Science were searched from inception to June 22, 2025, for longitudinal studies reporting short-term mortality (in-hospital or 30-day) in ATAAD patients with and without DM. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using a random-effects model accounting for the influence of heterogeneity. Subgroup, sensitivity, and meta-regression analyses explored heterogeneity. The study quality was assessed with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS).ResultsTwenty-one retrospective cohort studies involving 19,291 patients were included. DM was associated with a modestly increased short-term mortality risk (OR = 1.48; 95% CI 1.14 to 1.91; p = 0.003; I2 = 44%). The results were consistent in sensitivity analyses restricted to surgical patients (OR = 1.52; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.96; p = 0.001). Subgroup analyses showed no significant differences by region, follow-up duration, analytic model, or NOS score (all p for subgroup differences > 0.05). Meta-regression analysis showed that sample size was negatively correlated with the association (coefficient = − 0.000074, p = 0.02), which fully explained the source of heterogeneity (adjusted R2 = 100%).ConclusionsThis meta-analysis of retrospective studies suggests that DM may be associated with higher short-term mortality in ATAAD. Given the observational nature of the evidence, further prospective studies are needed to clarify this relationship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03448-9.Keywords: Type A aortic dissection, Diabetes, Mortality, Survival, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is a catastrophic cardiovascular emergency characterized by a tear in the intimal layer of the ascending aorta, leading to separation of the aortic wall layers and potential rupture [1, 2]. The annual incidence of acute aortic dissection (AAD) ranges from 2.6 to 4.6 per 100,000 persons, with ATAAD accounting for approximately two-thirds of all cases [3, 4]. Despite advances in diagnostic imaging, surgical techniques, and perioperative care, ATAAD continues to carry a high early mortality risk, reported to approach 50% within 48 h if untreated and approximately 15–30% even after surgical repair [5]. When compared with acute type B aortic dissection (ATBAD), which primarily involves the descending aorta and is often managed medically or with endovascular interventions, ATAAD almost universally requires emergency open surgery due to its proximal location and risk of catastrophic complications such as cardiac tamponade, aortic rupture, and coronary malperfusion [6]. Nevertheless, early mortality in ATAAD remains significantly higher than in ATBAD, underscoring the importance of identifying prognostic factors that could guide early risk stratification and improve clinical outcomes [7].

Diabetes mellitus (DM) affects approximately 10–20% of patients with AAD, including those with ATAAD [8]. Although DM is a well-established risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases, its impact on the prognosis of ATAAD remains controversial [9, 10]. Some studies suggest that DM may exacerbate vascular inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and coagulopathy, thereby contributing to worse outcomes, although others propose potential protective effects through vascular remodeling and reduced aortic wall stress [8]. Existing evidence is limited to retrospective studies with inconsistent findings, small sample sizes, and heterogeneous adjustments for confounders [11–31]. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, no prior meta-analysis has comprehensively synthesized the available data to clarify the relationship between DM and short-term mortality in ATAAD. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies to evaluate whether the presence of DM is associated with increased early mortality risk in patients with ATAAD, aiming to provide a more robust evidence base to inform clinical risk stratification and future research.

Methods

This study followed the PRISMA 2020 [32] and Cochrane Handbook guidelines [33] for conducting systematic reviews and meta-analyses, covering study design, data collection, statistical methods, and interpretation of results. The protocol was also registered in PROSPERO under the identifier CRD420251136865.

Database search

To identify studies pertinent to this meta-analysis, we searched PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases using an extensive array of search terms, which involved the combined terms of (1) "diabetes" OR "diabetic" OR "T1DM" OR "T2DM"; (2) "dissection" OR "aortic dissection" OR "dissecting aneurysm"; and (3) "mortality" OR "death" OR "deaths" OR "died" OR "survival" OR "prognosis" OR "outcome". The search was restricted to studies on human subjects and included only full-length articles published in English in peer-reviewed journals. We also manually checked the references of related original and review articles to find additional relevant studies. The search covered all records from database inception up to June 22, 2025. The detailed search strategy for each database is shown in Supplementary File 1.

Study eligible criteria

We applied the PICOS framework to define the inclusion criteria:

Population (P): Adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with ATAAD confirmed by imaging (e.g., CT, MRI, echocardiography) or surgical findings.

Intervention/Exposure (I): Presence of DM (type 1 or type 2), as defined by the original studies using clinical records, laboratory criteria, or self-reports.

Comparator (C): Patients with ATAAD without DM.

Outcomes (O): Short-term mortality was defined as death occurring during the index hospitalization or within 30 days after the initial diagnosis or surgical repair of ATAAD, whichever came first. This pragmatic definition is widely used in cardiovascular surgery outcomes research and facilitates consistent extraction and pooling of data across studies [34].

Study design (S): Longitudinal follow-up studies, including prospective or retrospective cohort studies, post-hoc analyses of clinical trials, and nested case–control studies.

We excluded studies that did not report short-term mortality outcomes separately for patients with and without DM, studies involving mixed populations without clear stratification for ATAAD, cross-sectional studies, case reports, reviews, editorials, letters, and studies with insufficient data to calculate effect estimates. Studies focusing solely on chronic aortic conditions or type B dissections were also excluded. If studies had overlapping populations, we included the one with the largest sample size in the meta-analysis.

Study quality evaluation

Two authors independently performed the literature search, study selection, quality assessment, and data extraction. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with the corresponding author. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [35], which rates selection, control of confounders, and outcome evaluation. Scores range from 1 to 9, with scores of 8 or higher considered good quality.

Data collection

The data collected for analysis included the study details (author, year, design, and study country), participant characteristics (number of patients included in each study, diagnosis, mean ages of the patients, and the proportion of men), exposure analysis (type of DM, methods for validation, and numbers of patients with DM), main treatment for ATAAD, follow-up durations, numbers of patients who died during follow-up, and covariates adjusted in the regression models for the analysis of the association between DM and short-term mortality risk of patients with ATAAD.

Statistical analysis

We used odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to assess the association between DM and short-term mortality of patients with ATAAD. ORs and standard errors were directly extracted or calculated from 95% CIs or p values, then log-transformed to stabilize variance and normalize the data [33]. If multiple ORs were reported from different models, we used the one with the most complete adjustment. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochrane Q test and I2 statistic [36], with a p value < 0.10 suggesting significant heterogeneity and I2 values of < 25%, 25–75%, and > 75% indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity. A random-effects model was used to pool the data, accounting for heterogeneity between studies [33]. Sensitivity analyses were done by removing one study at a time to evaluate the robustness of the findings [33]. Predefined subgroup analyses were conducted based on the study country (Asian vs. Western countries), sample size, follow-up duration (in-hospital or 30-day mortality), analytic model (univariate vs. multivariate analyses), and NOS. Continuous moderators were dichotomized at their dataset-specific medians to provide balanced subgroups and avoid post-hoc thresholding when no clinical cut-point exists. Accordingly, for sample size, the median was 300 in our dataset. In addition, a univariate meta-regression analysis was also performed to evaluate the influence of study characteristics on the results, such as sample size, mean ages of the patients, proportions of men, and study quality scores [33]. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and visual inspection for asymmetry, along with Egger’s test [37]. All analyses were performed using RevMan (Version 5.3; Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK) and Stata (Version 17.0; Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Study inclusion

The study selection process is shown in Fig. 1. We first identified 3007 records from the three databases. After removing 624 duplicates, 2383 articles were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 2342 were excluded primarily for not meeting the aims of the meta-analysis. The full texts of the remaining 41 articles were reviewed by two independent authors, and 20 were excluded for various reasons (see Fig. 1). In the end, 21 studies were included in the quantitative analysis [11–31].

Characteristics of the included studies

Table 1 summarizes the key characteristics of the 21 retrospective cohort studies included in this meta-analysis, which were published between 2001 and 2025 across diverse regions, including Europe (Italy, France, Germany, Belgium, Czech Republic, Finland, Spain, UK, and Turkey), Asia (China and Taiwan), and the United States. A total of 19,291 patients with ATAAD were analyzed. The mean ages varied from 48.3 to 77.0 years, and the proportion of male participants ranged between 47.7 and 77.1%. DM was identified mainly via clinical records or database codes, with both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T1DM and T2DM) reported where available in 19 studies [12–24, 26–31]; although in the other two studies, only patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes were considered as exposure [11, 25]. As for the treatment, most patients underwent surgical repair, including ascending aorta replacement, hemiarch or total arch replacement, Bentall procedure, and frozen elephant trunk technique, although two studies also included medical or interventional management besides surgery [21, 23]. Follow-up durations were primarily in-hospital or 30-day mortality. Confounder adjustment varied: earlier or smaller studies often provided unadjusted estimates [11, 13, 19–22, 24, 26, 28–31], while larger and more recent cohorts adjusted for age, sex, comorbidities, surgical details, and hemodynamic parameters etc. [12, 14–18, 23, 25, 27].

Eight studies achieved scores of 8 or 9 [12, 14, 15, 17, 18, 23, 25, 27], reflecting good selection of cohorts, robust ascertainment of exposure, and reliable outcome assessment. Results of meta-analysis and sensitivity analysis

The pooled results of 21 studies [11–31] with a random-effects model showed that overall, DM was associated with a higher short-term mortality risk of patients with ATAAD (OR: 1.48, 95% CI 1.14 to 1.91, p = 0.003; Fig. 2A) with moderate heterogeneity (p for Cochrane Q test = 0.02; I2 = 44%). Sensitivity analyses were performed by removing one dataset at a time, and the results remained stable (OR: 1.42 to 1.58, p all < 0.05; Table 3). Specifically, the sensitivity analysis limited to studies of patients who received surgical treatment only [11–20, 22, 24–31] also showed consistent results (OR: 1.52, 95% CI 1.18 to 1.96, p = 0.001; I2 = 40%).

Results of subgroup analysis

Further subgroup analyses indicated that the association between DM and an increased short-term mortality was not significantly different between studies from Asian and Western countries (OR: 1.11 vs. 1.55, p for subgroup difference = 0.42; Fig. 2B). Interestingly, a stronger association was observed in studies with sample size < 300 than those ≥ 300 (OR: 2.06 vs. 1.26), although the between subgroup difference is non-significant (p = 0.05; Fig. 3A). Similar results were observed for studies reporting in-hospital and 30-day mortality (OR: 1.52 vs. 1.34, p for subgroup difference = 0.73; Fig. 3B), using univariate or multivariate analysis (OR: 1.69 vs. 1.39, p for subgroup difference = 0.47; Fig. 4A), or with NOS scores of 6 ~ 7 or 8 ~ 9 (OR: 1.73 vs. 1.29, p for subgroup difference = 0.29; Fig. 4B).

Results of univariate meta-regression analysis

The results of the univariate meta-regression analyses are shown in Table 4. The study sample size was found to be negatively correlated with the association between DM and the risk of short-term mortality of patients with ATAAD (coefficient = − 0.000074, p = 0.02), which fully explained the source of heterogeneity (adjusted R2 = 100%; Table 4 and Fig. 5). Other variables, including mean age, proportion of men, or study quality scores did not seem to significantly modify the association between DM and the risk of short-term mortality of these patients (p all > 0.05; Table 4).

Discussion

This meta-analysis, based on 21 retrospective cohort studies including over 19,000 patients, suggests that DM may be associated with an increased risk of short-term mortality in patients with ATAAD. Although the effect size was moderate, the consistency across multiple sensitivity analyses supports the robustness of this association. Given the catastrophic nature of ATAAD and persistently high early mortality despite advances in surgical techniques and perioperative care, these findings raise important questions about the prognostic role of DM in this population.

Several pathophysiological mechanisms may underlie the observed association between DM and worse outcomes in ATAAD. Chronic hyperglycemia induces endothelial dysfunction, promotes vascular inflammation, and accelerates atherosclerosis, all of which can compromise the structural integrity and reparative capacity of the aortic wall [38]. Histopathological studies have reported more severe aortic wall atherosclerosis and medial degeneration in ATAAD patients with DM when compared with those without, suggesting a more fragile aortic substrate that may be less resilient during the acute dissection process and surgical repair [39]. In addition, DM is strongly associated with microvascular disease and impaired collateral circulation, which may predispose to organ malperfusion and ischemic complications during the acute phase [40].

Perioperative complications likely mediate part of the excess mortality risk. Multiple studies have linked DM to postoperative stroke [41], spinal cord ischemia and neurological injury [42, 43], acute kidney injury (AKI) [44, 45], and pulmonary complications [46] after ATAAD repair. Neurological injury is particularly relevant, as postoperative stroke occurs in up to 10–20% of ATAAD patients and doubles early mortality risk [41]. Mechanisms include perioperative hypoperfusion, embolization from the dissected aorta, and cerebral malperfusion at presentation—all of which may be aggravated by DM-related vasculopathy and coagulation abnormalities [47]. Similarly, AKI affects up to 23% of ATAAD surgical patients and independently predicts both early and late mortality [44, 45]. Diabetes is a well-known risk factor for postoperative AKI due to baseline renal microangiopathy, increased susceptibility to ischemia–reperfusion injury, and heightened systemic inflammation [44, 45]. Postoperative pulmonary complications, reported in nearly 30% of ATAAD patients, are also more frequent in those with DM and strongly associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation, ICU stay, and in-hospital mortality [46]. Together, these findings suggest that the adverse impact of DM in ATAAD is likely multifactorial, involving both direct effects on the aortic wall and indirect effects via increased perioperative morbidity.

Subgroup analyses showed no significant differences by geographic region, follow-up duration, or study quality. The comparison between Asian and Western cohorts is clinically relevant because these populations differ in the prevalence and management of diabetes, cardiovascular comorbidities, and surgical and perioperative practices. Such differences could plausibly modify the impact of diabetes on short-term outcomes in ATAAD; however, the lack of a significant regional difference in our analysis suggests that the prognostic influence of diabetes may be broadly consistent across diverse healthcare settings. Importantly, the pooled association was also consistent between studies using unadjusted versus multivariate-adjusted analyses, even though the latter generally provide more reliable effect estimates by partially accounting for baseline risk differences. The similar findings across these analytic subgroups suggest that the observed association between diabetes and short-term mortality may not be driven solely by unadjusted analyses, lending additional credibility to the robustness of the overall pooled result despite the observational nature of the evidence. Sensitivity analyses restricted to surgically treated patients confirmed the robustness of the findings, indicating that the prognostic relevance of DM persists even when focusing on the standard-of-care treatment for ATAAD. Meta-regression suggested that smaller sample size was associated with a stronger estimated association and statistically “explained” the observed heterogeneity (adjusted R2 = 100%). However, this result should be interpreted cautiously because univariate meta-regression in a relatively small set of studies can overfit the data and yield spurious findings. The apparent explanatory effect may thus reflect chance or other unmeasured factors correlated with sample size. Otherwise, these results are consistent with the notion that early single-center reports with limited adjustment for confounders may have produced more variable results, whereas larger multicenter cohorts yield more reliable estimates. Nevertheless, large-scale prospective studies are still needed to validate the finding.

Several strengths enhance the credibility of this meta-analysis. We performed an up-to-date and comprehensive literature search across major databases, retrieving up-to-date evidence regarding the association between DM and short-term mortality of patients with ATAAD. Multiple prespecified subgroup, sensitivity, and meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. The overall methodological quality of included studies, assessed by the NOS, was moderate to high, with most studies achieving scores of 7 or above. Furthermore, publication bias was formally assessed and not detected, lending additional confidence to the validity of the findings. Nevertheless, important limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting these results. First and foremost, all included studies were retrospective observational cohorts, which inherently carry risks of selection bias, recall bias, and residual or unmeasured confounding [48]. This study-level design limitation underscores that the pooled association should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than establishing a causal link. Although some studies adjusted for major baseline variables such as age, sex, comorbidities, and hemodynamic status, residual confounding remains likely. Because diabetic patients comprised a relatively small minority (< 10%) in most cohorts, even a few additional deaths could substantially shift the estimated OR, particularly in smaller studies. Such studies are also more prone to chance variation and selective reporting, which may exaggerate the apparent association. Moreover, diabetic patients tended to be older and to have more comorbidities, and these baseline imbalances may have been inadequately accounted for in unadjusted analyses. In addition, although comorbidities such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, renal dysfunction, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and prior myocardial infarction etc. are strongly associated with both diabetes and perioperative outcomes, their evaluation in our meta-analysis was limited. Definitions of these conditions varied among the included studies, and, more importantly, none reported their distributions separately for diabetic versus non-diabetic patients. As a result, we were unable to extract harmonized data to evaluate whether imbalances in these comorbidities might have contributed to the observed association with short-term mortality. Second, individual patient data were unavailable, precluding detailed analyses stratified by diabetes severity, type 1 versus type 2 DM, or perioperative glucose fluctuations, all of which may have prognostic implications. The included studies generally grouped patients with T1DM and T2DM together as “diabetes” despite their distinct pathophysiology and perioperative risk profiles. T1DM is often associated with younger age at onset but longer disease duration and potentially greater microvascular burden, whereas T2DM is typically linked to older age, obesity, and more metabolic comorbidities. The lack of stratified data prevented us from evaluating whether these subtypes confer different risks for short-term mortality in ATAAD. This heterogeneity may have introduced unmeasured variability into the pooled estimates and warrants further investigation in future studies. Third, perioperative technique likely contributed to residual heterogeneity. Key procedural factors—including the extent of arch/root reconstruction, use of frozen elephant trunk, cannulation site, cerebral perfusion strategy and temperature, and operative times—were variably reported and rarely stratified by diabetes status. Because studies did not provide mortality by both procedure category and DM vs. non-DM, we could not perform meaningful per-procedure subgroup analyses without individual patient data (IPD). Although the surgery-only sensitivity analysis showed similar results, unmeasured differences in surgical strategy may still have influenced the pooled estimates; future IPD meta-analyses or registries with standardized procedural variables are needed to test for effect modification by operative technique. Finally, the observational design of all included studies precludes causal inference; therefore, the association between DM and early mortality in ATAAD should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the importance of recognizing DM as a potential marker of increased perioperative risk rather than a direct therapeutic target in ATAAD. Current risk prediction models for early mortality in ATAAD largely focus on age, hemodynamic instability, malperfusion syndromes, and surgical factors; incorporating DM status might modestly improve risk stratification and perioperative decision-making. However, given the observational nature of the evidence and lack of data on glycemic control or diabetes-specific interventions, our results should not be interpreted as supporting any specific glucose-lowering strategy in the acute setting of ATAAD. Future research should prioritize large prospective multicenter registries or collaborative studies with standardized definitions of DM, detailed perioperative variables, and long-term follow-up. Individual patient data meta-analyses could enable more granular exploration of interactions between DM, surgical techniques, perioperative complications, and outcomes. Mechanistic studies integrating imaging, histopathology, and biomarker data may further clarify how DM influences aortic wall biology, organ perfusion, and postoperative recovery in ATAAD.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this meta-analysis of retrospective studies suggests that DM may be associated with increased short-term mortality in patients with ATAAD. Although results were consistent across multiple sensitivity and subgroup analyses, the observational design and residual confounding warrant cautious interpretation. Further prospective investigations are needed to validate these findings, elucidate underlying mechanisms, and refine risk prediction in this high-risk population.
