Abstract
. In these notes, we introduce the concept of self-similar graph, associated with groups acting on graphs. We define the corresponding C ∗ C^{*} -algebra using different complementary approaches, to understand its basic properties. We also analyze various generalizations that appear in the literature and, in particular, review the relationship of this construction with Zappa-Szép products. Finally, we present very recent results on homology and K K -theory for these algebras.

Introduction

These notes were originally intended to be complementary material for an introductory course on self-similar graphs and their algebras, presented by the author at the CIMPA School “K-theory and Operator Algebras”, held in La Plata and Buenos Aires (Argentina) from 28 July to 8 August 2025.

Self-similar graphs and their algebras were introduced in 2017 by Ruy Exel and the author
[
21
]
as a generalization of two previous constructions: self-similar groups
[
42
]
and Katsura algebras
[
27
]
. Although it is a recent topic, in the last years several authors have studied various generalizations, extending the scope of these objects to a huge variety of algebras. However, so far no survey has given a general overview of this topic and its results.

Given the extent of the existing material and since we did not pretend to write an exhaustive monograph, we had to make some choices to keep the notes manageable enough. Thus, we decided:

(1)

Do not include proofs of the results. Instead, we included precise references of all the results stated, to facilitate this information to the interested reader.

(2)

To avoid some topics and so reduce the size of the notes. For example, we do not talk about self-similar actions of compact groups on topological graphs
[
5
]
, bicategorical descriptions of self-similar groups
[
2
]
,
kk
-theoretical results
[
11
]
, among others.

(3)

In each section, the original notation used by the authors of the corresponding reference is kept, even if it does not fit the standard introduced in
[
21
]
, to facilitate the work for potential readers who browse the original sources.

Hence, we concentrate ourselves in studying the original concept from an inverse semigroups/groupoid approach, and to look at its natural generalizations from the most elementary situation to the most abstract one: from actions of groups on finite graphs to Zappa-Szép products of groupoids on left cancellative small categories.

We hope these notes will help the reader to have a global vision of the concept.

1.
The origins

In this section, we revisit two previous constructions, which were source of inspiration for self-similar graph definition.

1.1.
Self-similar groups and Nekrashevych algebras

Self-similar groups appeared as a class of groups whose presentation is easily encoded by automata, providing examples of exotic behaviors (nontrivial torsion, intermediate growth, etc.). The simplest way of describing these groups is through their action on
n
-regular rooted trees (equivalently, on words written on an alphabet of
n
symbols). we follow
[
24
,
43
]
for definitions and results.

To be precise: given a (finite) set
X
, regarded as an alphabet, we can define
X^{*}
to be the set of finite words, and
X^{\omega}
to be the set of infinite words on this alphabet. The set
X^{\omega}
becomes an ultrametric space when equipped with the metric
d(u,v)=2^{-|u\wedge v|}
, where
u\wedge v
denotes the longest common prefix of
u
and
v
.

Since every closed ball of
X^{\omega}
equals the set
uX^{\omega}
for some given prefix
u\in X^{*}
, every isometry
f
of
X^{\omega}
induces a permutation of
X^{*}
. As this permutation preserves inclusion of balls, it is an automorphism of the Cayley graph of
X^{*}
that preserves lengths (and so levels). This means that, if
X
is a finite set, and
G
is a discrete group acting on
X^{\omega}
by isometries, then it induces an action of
G
on
X^{*}
by
|X|
-ary tree automorphisms.

Now, let
g:X^{*}\rightarrow X^{*}
be an automorphism of the rooted tree
X^{*}
. Given a vertex
v\in X^{*}
, we consider the subtrees
vX^{*}
and
g(v)X^{*}
of
X^{*}
, which are connected through the restriction of
g:vX^{*}\rightarrow g(v)X^{*}
. Since both
vX^{*}
and
g(v)X^{*}
are naturally isomorphic to the whole tree
X^{*}
, we can represent the above restriction map as an automorphism
g{|_{v}}:X^{*}\rightarrow X^{*}
, which is uniquely determined by the rule
g(vw)=g(v){g|_{v}}(w)
.

Definition 1.1
.

The element
g{|_{v}}\in\text{Aut}(X^{*})
is the restriction of
g
in
v
. It satisfies two obvious properties:

(1)

g|_{v_{1}v_{2}}=(g{|_{v_{1}}}){|_{v_{2}}}
for all
v_{1},v_{2}\in X^{*}
.

(2)

(g_{1}g_{2}){|_{v}}={g_{1}}{|_{g_{2}(v)}}{g_{2}}{|_{v}}
for all
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
and
v\in X^{*}
.

This means that the restriction map is a
1
-cocycle
{-}_{|-}:G\times X^{*}\rightarrow G
.

Now, we are ready to introduce the main definition:

Definition 1.2
.

Given a finite set
X
, a
faithful
action of a (discrete) group
G
on
X^{\omega}
by isometries is self-similar if for all
g\in G
and all
x\in X
there exists
g{|_{x}}\in G
such that, for all
w\in X^{\omega}
, we have
g(xw)=g(x)g{|_{x}}(w)
. Notice that this is equivalent to the definition of a self-similar action of
G
on
X^{*}
by (level-preserving) automorphisms; since the action is faithful, the element
g{|_{x}}
is unique. Moreover, the restriction rules described in Definition
1.1
allow us to inductively extend the definition to all
v,w\in X^{*}
.

Definition
1.2
means that
(G,X)
is self-similar if
G
is a subgroup of
\text{Aut}(X^{*})
invariant by the restriction
1
-cocycle. We can formalize this fact by using the permutational wreath product construction:
G\leq\text{Aut}(X^{*})
is self-similar if
G\leq S(X)\wr G
(for more details, see e.g.
[
43
, Sections 1.4-1.5]
).

Let us illustrate this definition with a classical example:

Example 1.3
.

The Grigorchuk group is a group
G
acting on
X^{*}
for the alphabet
X=\{0,1\}
, generated by four automorphisms
a,b,c,d\in\text{Aut}(X^{*})
, encoded by the automaton
c
a
b
d
e
x\mid x
1\mid 0
0\mid 1
0\mid 0
1\mid 1
0\mid 0
1\mid 1
1\mid 1
0\mid 0

The group also can be defined recursively by the corresponding action and restriction of these generators on
X
:

(1)

a(0)=1
,
a{|_{0}}=e
&
a(1)=0
,
a{|_{1}}=e
.

(2)

b(0)=0
,
b{|_{0}}=a
&
b(1)=1
,
b{|_{1}}=c
.

(3)

c(0)=0
,
c{|_{0}}=a
&
c(1)=1
,
c{|_{1}}=d
.

(4)

d(0)=0
,
d{|_{0}}=e
&
d(1)=1
,
d{|_{1}}=b
.

Nekrashevych
[
41
,
42
]
defined a
C^{*}
-algebra associated to a self-similar group (also known as Nekrashevych algebra), as follows:

Definition 1.4
.

Let
(G,X)
be a self-similar group. We define
{\mathbb{C}}(G,X)
to be the universal complex
\ast
-algebra generated by

\{u_{g}\mid g\in G\}\cup\{s_{x}\mid x\in X\}

satisfying the following relations:

(1)

s_{x}^{*}s_{y}=\delta_{x,y}
for all
x,y\in X
.

(2)

\sum\limits_{x\in X}s_{x}s_{x}^{*}=1
.

(3)

The map
g\mapsto u_{g}
is a unitary representation of
G
.

(4)

u_{g}s_{x}=s_{g(x)}u_{g_{|x}}
for all
g\in G
and all
x\in X
.

We define the
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{O}(G,X)
to be the universal
C^{*}
-algebra given by the same set of generators and relations; it turns out to be the norm-completion of
{\mathbb{C}}(G,X)
in a suitable norm.

Given any field
K
, we can also define
K(G,X)
to be the universal
K
algebra with involution
\ast
satisfying the same relations as above (see e.g.
[
25
]
for a general definition when the field
K
is replaced by a unital commutative ring
R
). For this
K
-algebra, we use the notation
{\mathcal{O}}_{G,X}^{K}
.

When
G=\{1\}
,
{\mathbb{C}}(G,X)
is the complex Leavitt algebra
L_{|X|}
, and
\mathcal{O}(G,X)
is the Cuntz algebra
\mathcal{O}_{|X|}
(see e.g.
[
52
]
); in fact, it is easily seen that these graph algebras are subalgebras of
{\mathbb{C}}(G,X)
and
\mathcal{O}_{|X|}
(respectively) for any self-similar group
(G,X)
.

Nekrashevych gave presentations of
\mathcal{O}_{|X|}
both as Cuntz-Pimsner algebras
[
41
]
(which allowed stating the nuclearity of the algebra) and as groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras associated with a certain ample, second countable groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{(G,X)}

[
42
]
. Since the action of
G
on
X^{*}
is faithful, the groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{(G,X)}
is effective, while the fact that the underlying graph of
(G,X)
has a unique vertex implies that
\mathcal{G}_{(G,X)}
is minimal. As we see later on, this forces the algebra
\mathcal{O}(G,X)
to be simple in reasonable situations (e.g., when
\mathcal{G}_{(G,X)}
is Hausdorff
[
8
]
), but not in general (see e.g.
[
10
, Section 5]
).

1.2.
Katsura algebras

Katsura
[
26
]
constructed a family of
C^{*}
-algebras of combinatorial nature that includes, up to isomorphism, all Kirchberg algebras in the UCT. We recall the definition and basic properties of Katsura algebras that will be needed in the sequel, borrowed from
[
26
]
.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
N\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
, let
A\in M_{N}({\mathbb{Z}}^{+})
and
B\in M_{N}({\mathbb{Z}})
be row-finite matrices. Define a set
\Omega_{A}
by

\Omega_{A}:=\{(i,j)\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}\times\{1,2,\dots,N\}\mid A_{i,j}\geq 1\}.

For each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, define a set
\Omega_{A}(i)\subset\{1,2,\dots,N\}
by

\Omega_{A}(i):=\{j\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}\mid(i,j)\in{\Omega_{A}}\}.

Notice that, by definition,
\Omega_{A}(i)
is finite for all
i
. Finally, fix the following relation:

(0)
\Omega_{A}(i)\neq\emptyset
for all
i
, and
B_{i,j}=0
for
(i,j)\not\in{\Omega_{A}}
.

With these data we can define Katsura algebras:

Definition 1.6
.

Define
{\mathcal{O}_{A,B}}
to be the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
mutually orthogonal projections
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, partial unitaries
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with
u_{i}u_{i}^{*}=u_{i}^{*}u_{i}=q_{i}
,
and partial isometries
\{s_{i,j,n}\}_{(i,j)\in{\Omega_{A}},n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
satisfying the relations:

(1)

s_{i,j,n}^{*}s_{i,j,n}=q_{j}
for all
(i,j)\in{\Omega_{A}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

(2)

q_{i}=\sum\limits_{j\in{\Omega_{A}}(i)}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{A_{i,j}}s_{i,j,n}s_{i,j,n}^{*}
for all
i
.

(3)

s_{i,j,n}u_{j}=s_{i,j,n+A_{i,j}}
and
u_{i}s_{i,j,n}=s_{i,j,n+B_{i,j}}
for all
(i,j)\in{\Omega_{A}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Given any field
K
, we can also consider
{\mathcal{O}}_{A,B}^{K}
to be the universal K-algebra with the same presentation as above (see also
[
25
]
).

Remark 1.7
.

The following facts holds:

(1)

The
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathcal{O}_{A,B}}
is separable, nuclear, in the UCT class
[
26
, Proposition 2.9]
.

(2)

If the matrices
A,B
satisfy the following
additional properties:

(a)

A
is irreducible, and

(b)

|A_{ii}|\geq 2
and
B_{i,i}=1
for every
1\leq i\leq N
,

then the
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathcal{O}_{A,B}}
is purely infinite simple, and hence a Kirchberg algebra
[
26
, Proposition 2.10]
.

(3)

The
K
-groups of
{\mathcal{O}_{A,B}}
are
[
26
, Proposition 2.6]
:

(a)

K_{0}({\mathcal{O}_{A,B}})\cong\mbox{coker}(I-A)\oplus\mbox{ker}(I-B)
, and

(b)

K_{1}({\mathcal{O}_{A,B}})\cong\mbox{coker}(I-B)\oplus\mbox{ker}(I-A)
.

(4)

Every Kirchberg algebra can be represented, up to isomorphism, by an algebra
{\mathcal{O}_{A,B}}
for matrices
A,B
satisfying the conditions in
1.7
(2)
[
27
, Proposition 4.5]
.

At first sight, it could seem that there is no relation between the definition of Nekrashevych algebras and that of Katsura algebras. Let us show that, in fact, we can change Katsura’s picture in such a way that both definitions almost match, at least when
N<\infty
.

As in
[
26
]
, let us assume that we have two
N\times N
matrices
A
and
B
with integer entries, and such that
A_{i,j}\geq 0
, for all
i
and
j
. We may then consider the graph
E
with vertex set

E^{0}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\},

and such that, for each pair of vertices
i,j\in E^{0}
, the set of edges from vertex
j
to vertex
i
is a set with
A_{i,j}
elements, say

\{e_{i,j,n}:0\leq n<A_{i,j}\}.

Moreover, assuming that
A
has no identically zero rows, it is easy to see that
E
has no sources.

Define an action
\sigma
of
{\mathbb{Z}}
on
E
, which is trivial on
E^{0}
, and which acts on edges as follows: given
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, and
e_{i,j,n}\in E^{1}
, let
(\hat{k},\hat{n})
be the unique pair of integers such that

mB_{i,j}+n=\hat{k}A_{i,j}+\hat{n}\text{ and }0\leq\hat{n}<A_{i,j}.

That is,
\hat{k}
is the quotient and
\hat{n}
is the remainder of the Euclidean division of
mB_{i,j}+n
by
A_{i,j}
. We then put

\sigma_{m}(e_{i,j,n})=e_{i,j,\hat{n}}.

In other words,
\sigma_{m}
corresponds to the addition of
mB_{i,j}
to the variable “
n
” of “
e_{i,j,n}
”, taken modulo
A_{i,j}
. In turn, it is easy to check that the map
\varphi:{\mathbb{Z}}\times E^{1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
defined by

\varphi(m,e_{i,j,n})=\hat{k}

is a
1
-cocycle in the sense of Definition
1.1
.

Observe that if
A_{i,j}=0
, then there are no edges from
j
to
i
, so the value
B_{i,j}
is entirely irrelevant for the above construction. Therefore, it makes no difference to assume that

A_{i,j}=0\Rightarrow B_{i,j}=0.

So, Katsura algebra
{\mathcal{O}_{A,B}}
seems to be isomorphic to a sort of Nekrashevych algebra
{\mathcal{O}}_{({\mathbb{Z}},E)}
, defined via an action of
{\mathbb{Z}}
on
E
by the automorphism
\sigma
, “twisted” by the 1-cocycle
\varphi
, just by sending each
u_{m}
to the
m^{\text{th}}
power of the unitary

u:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}

in
{\mathcal{O}_{A,B}}
, and sending
s_{e_{i,j,n}}
to
s_{i,j,n}
; the formalization of this idea is the basis of the definition of a self-similar graph.

Notice that, when
N=1
, the graph
E
for Katsura’s algebras is the same as the one we used above in the description of Nekrashevych’s example. However, the former is not a special case of the latter because, contrary to what is required in
[
42
]
, the group action might not be faithful.

2.
Self-similar graphs and their algebras

Looking at the previous constructions, we can extract common elements to both families of algebras: a (finite) directed graph
E
, a discrete group
G
acting on
E^{0}\sqcup E^{1}
by graph automorphisms, and a length-preserving
1
-cocycle
\varphi:G\times(E^{0}\sqcup E^{1})\rightarrow G
allowing to define a sort of self-similar identity: for all
g\in G
and all
x\in E^{0}\sqcup E^{1}
we have

g\cdot x:=g(x)\varphi(g,x).

Using this identity, we could then define a
C^{*}
-algebra with generators corresponding to a graph
C^{*}
-algebra
C^{*}(E)
extended by a self-similar action of a (non necessarily faithful) unitary copy
u(G)
of
G
in such a way that, for all
g\in G
and all
x\in E^{0}\sqcup E^{1}
we have

u_{g}s_{x}=s_{g(x)}u_{\varphi(g,x)}.

Building a coherent algebra will require to take care of some technical details. Let us formalize the idea. We follow
[
21
]
throughout this section.

2.1.
Self-similar graphs

Let
E=(E^{0},E^{1},r,d)
be a directed graph, where
E^{0}
denotes the set of
vertices
,
E^{1}
is the set of
edges
,
r
is the
range
map, and
d
is the
source
, or
domain
map.

By definition, a
source
in
E
is a vertex
x\in E^{0}
, for which
r^{-1}(x)=\emptyset
. Thus, when we say that a graph has
no sources
, we mean that
r^{-1}(x)\neq\emptyset
, for all
x\in E^{0}
.

By an
automorphism
of
E
we shall mean a bijective map

\sigma:E^{0}\sqcup E^{1}\to E^{0}\sqcup E^{1}

such that
\sigma(E^{i})\subseteq E^{i}
, for
i=0,1
, and moreover such that
r\circ\sigma=\sigma\circ r
, and
d\circ\sigma=\sigma\circ d
, on
E^{1}
. It is evident that the collection of all automorphisms of
E
forms a group under composition.

By an action of a group
G
on a graph
E
we mean a homomorphism of the group from
G
to the group of all automorphisms of
E
.

If
X
is any set, and if
\sigma
is an action of a group
G
on
X
, we shall say that a map

\varphi:G\times X\to G

is a
one-cocycle
for
\sigma
, when

\varphi(gh,x)=\varphi\big(g,\sigma_{h}(x)\big)\varphi(h,x),

for all
g,h\in G
, and all
x\in X
. In particular,
\varphi(1,x)=1
for every
x
.

The
Standing Hypothesis
in the classical construction we define is that
G
will be a discrete group,
E
will be a finite graph with no sources,
\sigma
will be an action of
G
on
E
,
\varphi:G\times E\rightarrow G
will be a
1
-cocycle for the restriction of
\sigma
to
E^{1}
, which must satisfy

(1)

\sigma_{\varphi(g,e)}(x)=\sigma_{g}(x)\hskip 5.69046pt\forall g\in G,\hskip 5.69046pt\forall e\in E^{1},\hskip 5.69046pt\forall x\in E^{0}.

As we shall see later, the finiteness of
E
could be removed from the hypotheses. Also, as remarked in
[
5
]
,
(\ref{equa:StanHypSSG})
can be replaced by the weaker requirement

(2)

\sigma_{\varphi(g,e)}(s(e))=\sigma_{g}(s(e))\hskip 5.69046pt\forall g\in G,\hskip 5.69046pt\forall e\in E^{1}.

By
path
in
E
of
length

n\geq 1
we mean any finite sequence of the form

\alpha=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\ldots\alpha_{n},

where
\alpha_{i}\in E^{1}
, and
d(\alpha_{i})=r(\alpha_{i+1})
, for all
i
(this is the usual convention when treating graphs from a categorical point of view, in which functions compose from right to left). The
range
of
\alpha
is defined by
r(\alpha)=r(\alpha_{1})
, while the
source
of
\alpha
is defined by
d(\alpha)=d(\alpha_{n})
. A vertex
x\in E^{0}
will be considered a path of length zero, in which case we set
r(x)=d(x)=x
.

For every integer
n\geq 0
we denote by
E^{n}
the set of all paths in
E
of length
n
. Finally, we denote by
E^{*}
the sets of all finite paths, and by
E^{\leq n}
the set of all paths of length at most
n
, namely

E^{*}=\bigcup\limits_{k\geq 0}E^{k}\text{ and }E^{\leq n}=\bigcup\limits_{k=0}^{n}E^{k}.

It is a straightforward computation to extend
\sigma
and
\varphi
to finite paths. From now on, we adopt the shorthand notation

g\alpha=\sigma_{g}(\alpha).

Then, we can write

Proposition 2.1

(c.f.
[
21
, Proposition 2.4 & Equation 2.5]
)
.

For every
g,h\in G
, for every
x\in E^{0}
, and for every
\alpha
and
\beta
in
E^{*}
such that
d(\alpha)=r(\beta)
, one has that

(1)

(gh)\alpha=g(h\alpha)
,

(2)

\varphi(gh,\alpha)=\varphi(g,h\alpha)\varphi(h,\alpha),

(3)

\varphi(g,x)=g
,

(4)

r(g\alpha)=gr(\alpha)
,

(5)

d(g\alpha)=gd(\alpha)
,

(6)

\varphi(g,\alpha)x=gx
,

(7)

g(\alpha\beta)=(g\alpha)\varphi(g,\alpha)\beta
,

(8)

\varphi(g,\alpha\beta)=\varphi(\varphi(g,\alpha),\beta)
.

Once we have the correct definition for self-similar graphs, we are ready to define a
C^{*}
-algebra associated with it.

2.2.
Algebras of self-similar graphs

Now, we fix a graph
E
, an action of a group
G
on
E
, and a
1
-cocycle
\varphi
under (
1
). We proceed to build a
C^{*}
-algebra from these data.

Definition 2.2
.

We define
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
to be the universal unital
C^{*}
-algebra generated by a set

\{p_{x}\mid x\in E^{0}\}\cup\{s_{e}\mid e\in E^{1}\}\cup\{u_{g}\mid g\in G\},

subject to the following relations:

(1)

\{p_{x}\mid x\in E^{0}\}\cup\{s_{e}\mid e\in E^{1}\}
is a Cuntz-Krieger
E
-family
[
52
]
,

(2)

the map
u:G\rightarrow{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
, defined by the rule
g\mapsto u_{g}
, is a unitary representation of
G
,

(3)

u_{g}s_{e}=s_{ge}u_{\varphi(g,e)}
, for every
g\in G
, and
e\in E^{1}
,

(4)

u_{g}p_{x}=p_{gx}u_{g}
, for every
g\in G
, and
x\in E^{0}
.

Given any field
K
, we can also define
{\mathcal{O}}_{G,E}^{K}
to be the universal
K
algebra with the same presentation as above (see
[
25
]
for a general version).

This construction generalizes our inspiration examples, as well as some other well-known constructions, as follows:

Example 2.3

3.
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
as groupoid algebra

One of the main tools for understanding the structure of

is to represent it as a (full) groupoid

-algebra of a second countable ample groupoid. To do this, we use the classical approach due to Exel
[

The first step is to show that
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
is universal for tight representations of
{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}
; roughly speaking, given an inverse semigroup
S
and a unital ring
R

\ast
-representation
\phi:S\rightarrow R
is said to be tight if whenever
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\}
is a cover of
e
(that is, for any
0\neq g\leq e

such that
g\cdot f_{j}\neq 0
), then
\phi(e)=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\phi(f_{i})
. For a precise definition of the concept, see
[
17
, Definition 13.1]
(also
[
19
]
).

(
[
21
, Proposition 6.2 & Theorem 6.3]
)

Let
(G,E,\varphi)
be a self-similar graph under (
1

(1)

The map

\pi:{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}\to{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}},

defined by
\pi(0)=0
, and

\pi(\alpha,g,\beta)=s_{\alpha}u_{g}s_{\beta}^{*},

is a tight representation.

(2)

Let
A
be a unital
C^{*}
-algebra and let
\rho:{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}\to A
be a tight representation. Then there exists a
unique unital *-homomorphism
\psi:{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}\to A
, such that the diagram

commutes.

Hence,

Corollary 3.2

(
[
21
, Corollary 6.4]
)
.

Let
(G,E,\varphi)
be a self-similar graph under (
1
). Then:

(1)

{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
is isomorphic to the (full)
C^{*}
-algebra of the tight groupoid of the inverse semigroup
{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}
, denoted
C^{*}(\mathcal{G}_{\text{tight}}({\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}))

(2)

If

is any field, then

is isomorphic to the Steinberg

Thus, we need to define
\mathcal{G}_{\text{tight}}({\mathcal{S}_{G,E}})
, and describe its topology. The groupoid
{\mathcal{G}_{\text{tight}}({\mathcal{S}_{G,E}})}
is the quotient (by the so-called germ relation) of the transformation groupoid of the action of
{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}
on the space of tight filters over the idempotent semi-lattice
{\mathcal{E}}
of
{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}
(see e.g.
[
17
, Section 4]
). Since we need a clear picture of this construction, let us be more precise in this description.

By an
infinite path
in
E
we shall mean any infinite sequence of the form

\xi=\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\ldots,

where
\xi_{i}\in E^{1}
, and
d(\xi_{i})=r(\xi_{i+1})
, for all
i
. The set of all infinite paths will be denoted by
E^{\infty}
. Given an infinite path

\xi=\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\ldots\in E^{\infty},

and an integer
n\geq 0
, denote by
\xi|_{n}
the finite path of length
n
given by

\xi|_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\ldots\xi_{n},&\hbox{ if }n\geq 1,\\
r(\xi_{1}),&\hbox{ if }n=0.\end{array}\right.

Proposition 3.3

(
[
21
, Proposition 8.1]
)
.

There is a unique action

(g,\xi)\in G\times E^{\infty}\mapsto g\xi\in E^{\infty}

of
G
on
E^{\infty}
such that

{(g\xi)}|_{n}=g(\xi|_{n}),

for every
g\in G
,
\xi\in E^{\infty}
, and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Given an infinite path
\xi\in E^{\infty}
, we may look at the subset

\mathcal{F}_{\xi}=\{f_{\xi|_{n}}\mid n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{E}},

which turns out to be an ultrafilter over
{\mathcal{E}}
. Denoting the set of all ultrafilters over
{\mathcal{E}}
by
\widehat{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\infty}
, one may also show
[
17
, Proposition 19.11]
that the correspondence

\xi\in E^{\infty}\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{\xi}\in\widehat{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\infty}

is bijective, and we use it to identify
E^{\infty}
and
\widehat{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\infty}
. Furthermore, this correspondence may be proven to be a homeomorphism when
E^{\infty}
is equipped with the product topology.

Since
E
is finite,
E^{\infty}
is compact by Tychonov’s Theorem, and consequently so is
\widehat{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\infty}
. Being the closure of
\widehat{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\infty}
within
\widehat{{\mathcal{E}}}
the space
\widehat{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\text{tight}}

Identifying

{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}
from the former to the latter, resulting in the following: for each element
(\alpha,g,\beta)\in{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}

whose domain is the

As before, we do not use any special symbol to indicate this action, using module notation instead:

(\alpha,g,\beta)\eta=\alpha g\xi\,,\forall(\alpha,g,\beta)\in{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}\,,\forall\eta=\beta\xi\in Z(\beta).

Then, we have the transformation groupoid

{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}\times E^{\infty}:=\{(\alpha,g,\beta;\xi)\mid\xi\in Z(\beta)\}

and

(s;\xi)\sim(t;\omega)
if and only if

and there exists

. If we denote the equivalence class of

{\mathcal{G}_{\text{tight}}({\mathcal{S}_{G,E}})}:=\left\{[\alpha,g,\beta;\xi]\mid(\alpha,g,\beta)\in{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}},\ \xi\in Z(\beta)\right\}.

Now, let us look at the topology of
{\mathcal{G}_{\text{tight}}({\mathcal{S}_{G,E}})}
. Recall from
[
17
, Proposition 4.14]
that, if
\mathcal{S}
is an inverse semigroup acting on a locally compact Hausdorff topological space

is in turn defined by

\Theta(s,U)=\left\{[s,x]\in\mathcal{G}\mid x\in U\right\}.

Notice that, by definition,

is an open bisection, and it is compact if so is

If we restrict the choice of the

A simple computation shows that, if it is not empty, then it coincides with
\Theta(\alpha,g,\beta;\beta)
. Thus, we redefine

\Theta(\alpha,g,\beta):=\left\{(\alpha,g,\beta;\xi)\in{\mathcal{G}_{\text{tight}}({\mathcal{S}_{G,E}})}:\xi\in Z(\beta)\right\},

and we conclude

Proposition 3.4

(
[

, Proposition 9.4]
)

The collection of all sets of the form

Now, we are ready to give characterizations of the topological properties enjoyed by

We study conditions under which

The other relation we have in mind is simply the orbit relation, defined by

\rightharpoonup

We may then consider the smallest transitive relation extending both “
\rightharpoonup
” and “

Then, we have

Proposition 3.7

(
[

)

Let

A=\left[\begin{matrix}2&1&0\\
1&2&1\\
1&1&2\end{matrix}\right]\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636ptB=\left[\begin{matrix}1&2&0\\
2&1&2\\
0&2&1\end{matrix}\right].

Let
E_{A}=(E_{A}^{0},E_{A}^{1},r,s)
be the directed graph whose incidence matrix is
A
. The matrix
B
determines an action
{\mathbb{Z}}\curvearrowright E_{A}
and a cocycle
\varphi:{\mathbb{Z}}\times E^{1}_{A}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
, as described after Remark

. This action and cocycle are described for

as follows:

\displaystyle 1\cdot e_{ii}^{0}=e_{ii}^{1},\hskip 28.45274pt

\displaystyle\varphi(1,e_{ii}^{0})=0\quad\text{ for }i=1,2,3,

\displaystyle 1\cdot e_{ii}^{1}=e_{ii}^{0},\hskip 28.45274pt

\displaystyle\varphi(1,e_{ii}^{1})=1\quad\text{ for }i=1,2,3,

\displaystyle 1\cdot e_{12}=e_{12},\hskip 28.45274pt

\displaystyle\varphi(1,e_{12})=2,

\displaystyle 1\cdot e_{21}=e_{21},\hskip 28.45274pt

\displaystyle\varphi(1,e_{21})=2,

\displaystyle 1\cdot e_{32}=e_{32},\hskip 28.45274pt

\displaystyle 1\cdot e_{23}=e_{23},\hskip 28.45274pt

Let
({\mathbb{Z}},E_{A},\varphi)
be the Katsura triple associated to the matrices (

be the associated groupoid. Then

is minimal, effective and non-Hausdorff
[

. But

satisfies an additional condition
[

, Lemma 5.15]
that guarantees the simplicity of

[

, Theorem 5.16]
. The same result holds for the algebraic Katsura algebra
{\mathcal{O}}_{A,B}^{K}
over any field

.

In general, it is possible to construct self-similar groups
(G,X)
such that, even if
\mathcal{G}_{(G,X)}
is minimal and effective,
{\mathcal{O}}(G,X)
fails to be simple. Or even more strange,
{\mathcal{O}}(G,X)
is simple, but

fails to be simple for fields

having nonzero characteristic.

Example 3.33

(
[
10
, Subsection 5.6]
)
.

Let
X=\{0,1\}
, and let
a,b,c,d
be the length-preserving bijections of
X^{*}
determined by the formulas

\begin{array}[]{lll}a\cdot(0w)=1w,&&c\cdot(0w)=0(a\cdot w),\\
a\cdot(1w)=0w,&&c\cdot(1w)=1(d\cdot w),\\
b\cdot(0w)=0(a\cdot w),&&d\cdot(0w)=0w,\\
b\cdot(1w)=1(c\cdot w),&&d\cdot(1w)=1(b\cdot w),\end{array}

for every

. The group

generated by the set of bijections
\{a,b,c,d\}
is called the Grigorchuk group. We have that
(G,X)
is a faithful self-similar action with restrictions given by

\begin{array}[]{lll}a|_{0}=e,&&c|_{0}=a,\\
a|_{1}=e,&&c|_{1}=d,\\
b|_{0}=a,&&d|_{0}=e,\\
b|_{1}=c,&&d|_{1}=b.\end{array}

Each of the elements
b,c,
and
d
have infinitely many minimally fixed words, whence
\mathcal{G}_{G,X}
is a minimal, effective, non Hausdorff ample groupoid. According to
[
10
, Theorem 5.22]
, the Steinberg
K
-algebra

for any field

of characteristic different from

is simple, and so is the

-algebra
{\mathcal{O}}_{G,X}
.

But if
\text{char}(K)=2
, then
A_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{G,X})
is shown that it is not simple, by explicitly constructing a nonzero singular function
f\in A_{K}(\mathcal{G}_{G,X})

[
10
, Corollary 5.26]
.

Example 3.34

(c.f.
[
60
]
)
.

Let
X=\{0,1\}
, and let

be the length-preserving bijections of

\begin{array}[]{lll}a\cdot(0w)=1w,&&c\cdot(0w)=0w,\\

Each of the elements

and

These are clear examples that simplicity for these kind of algebras is far to be characterized using only topological properties of the underlying groupoid. Steinberg and Szakács

did a thorough study of this particular phenomenon, providing families of examples that enjoy properties similar to Examples

and

4.
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
as Cuntz-Pimsner algebra

Inspired by Nekrashevych’s paper
[
41
]
, we give a description of
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
as a Cuntz-Pimsner algebra
[
50
]
. With this we will be able to prove that
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
is nuclear and that
{\mathcal{G}_{\text{tight}}({\mathcal{S}_{G,E}})}
is amenable when
G
is an amenable group. As before, we work with self-similar graphs
(G,E,\varphi)
under (
1
).

We begin by introducing the algebra of coefficients over which the relevant Hilbert bimodule, also known as a
correspondence, will later be constructed.

Since the action of
G
on
E
preserves length, we see that the set of vertices of
E
is
G
-invariant, so we get an action of
G
on
E^{0}
by restriction. By dualization,
G
acts on the algebra
C(E^{0})
of complex valued functions on
E^{0}
(notice that, since
E^{0}
is a finite set,
C(E^{0})
is nothing but
{{\mathbb{C}}}^{|E^{0}|}
). We may therefore form the crossed-product
C^{*}
-algebra

A=C(E^{0})\rtimes G.

Since
C(E^{0})
is a unital algebra, there is a canonical unitary representation of
G
in the crossed product, which we denote by
\{v_{g}\}_{g\in G}
. On the other hand,
C(E^{0})
is also canonically isomorphic to a subalgebra of
A
and we therefore identify these two algebras without further warning.

For each
x
in
E^{0}
, we denote the characteristic function of the singleton
\{x\}
by
q_{x}
, so
that

\{q_{x}\mid x\in E^{0}\}

is the canonical basis of
C(E^{0})
, and thus
A
coincides with the closed linear span of the set

\left\{q_{x}v_{g}\mid x\in E^{0},g\in G\right\}.

For later reference, notice that the covariance condition in the crossed product reads

v_{g}q_{x}=q_{gx}v_{g}\hskip 5.69046pt\forall x\in E^{0}\hskip 5.69046pt\forall g\in G.

Our next step is to construct a correspondence over
A
. In preparation for this, we denote by
A^{e}
the right ideal of
A
generated by
q_{d(e)}
, for each
e\in E^{1}
. In technical terms,

A^{e}=q_{d(e)}A.

With the obvious right
A
-module structure, and the inner product defined by

\langle y,z\rangle=y^{*}z\hskip 5.69046pt\forall y,z\in A^{e},

one has that
A^{e}
is a right Hilbert
A
-module. Notice that this is not necessarily a full Hilbert module since

\langle A^{e},A^{e}\rangle
is the two-sided ideal of
A
generated by
q_{d(e)}
, which might be a proper ideal in some cases.

Since
A
is spanned by the elements of the form
q_{x}v_{g}
,
A^{e}
is spanned by the elements of the form

q_{d(e)}q_{x}v_{g}
, but, since the
q
’s are mutually orthogonal, this is either zero or equal to
q_{d(e)}v_{g}
. Therefore, we see that

A^{e}=\overline{\hbox{span}}\{q_{d(e)}v_{g}\mid g\in G\}.

Introducing the right Hilbert
A
-module, which will later be given the structure of a correspondence over
A
, we define

M=\bigoplus_{e\in E^{1}}A^{e}.

Observe that if
x
is a vertex which is the source of many edges, say

d^{-1}(x)=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\},

then

A^{e_{i}}=q_{d(e_{i})}A=q_{x}A,

for all
i
, so that
q_{x}A
appears many times as a direct summand of
M
. However, these copies of
q_{x}A
should be suitably distinguished, according to which edge
e_{i}
is being considered. On the other hand, notice that if
d^{-1}(x)=\emptyset
, then
q_{x}A
does not appear among the summands of
M
.

Addressing the fullness of
M
, observe that

\langle M,M\rangle=\sum\limits_{x\in E^{0},d^{-1}(x)\neq\emptyset}Aq_{x}A.

So, when
E
has no
sinks
, that is, when
d^{-1}(x)
is nonempty for every
x
, it follows that
M
is
full.

Given
e\in E^{1}
, the element
q_{d(e)}
, when viewed as an element of
A^{e}\subseteq M
, will play a very special role in what follows, so we give it a special notation, namely

t_{e}:=q_{d(e)}.

There is a small risk of confusion here in the sense that, if
e_{1},e_{2}\in E^{1}
are such that

x:=d(e_{1})=d(e_{2}),

then we assign
q_{x}
to both
t_{e_{1}}
and
t_{e_{2}}
. However, the coordinate in which
q_{x}
appears in
t_{e_{i}}
is determined by the corresponding
e_{i}
. So, if
e_{1}\neq e_{2}
, then
t_{e_{1}}\neq t_{e_{2}}
.

In order to completely dispel any confusion, here is the technical definition:

t_{e}=(m_{f})_{f\in E^{1}},

where

m_{f}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}q_{d(e)},&\text{if }f=e,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

We should notice that
t_{e}q_{d(e)}=t_{e}
, and that any element
y\in M
can be written uniquely as
y=\sum\limits_{e\in E^{1}}t_{e}y_{e}
, where each
y_{e}\in A^{e}
.

As the next step in constructing a correspondence over
A
, we have to define a certain
\ast
-homomorphism from
A
to the algebra
\mathcal{L}(M)
of adjointable linear operators on
M
. Since
A
is a crossed product algebra, this will be accomplished once we produce a covariant representation
(\psi,V)
of the
C^{*}
-dynamical system
\left(C(E^{0}),G\right)
on
M
. We begin with the group representation
V
.

Definition 4.1
.

For each
g\in G
, let
V_{g}
be the linear operator on
M
given by

V_{g}\left(\sum\limits_{e\in E^{1}}t_{e}y_{e}\right)=\sum\limits_{e\in E^{1}}t_{ge}v_{\varphi(g,e)}y_{e},

whenever
y_{e}\in A^{e}
, for each
e\in E^{1}
.

By the uniqueness of the representation of
y
above, it is clear that
V_{g}
is well defined.

Proposition 4.2

(
[
21
, Proposition 10.7]
)
.

Each
V_{g}
is a unitary operator on
M
. Moreover, the correspondence
g\mapsto V_{g}
is a unitary representation of
G
.

In order to complete our covariant pair, we must now construct a
\ast
-homomorphism from
C(E^{0})
to
\mathcal{L}(M)
. With this in mind, we give the following:

Definition 4.3
.

For every
x\in E^{0}
, let

M_{x}=\bigoplus_{e\in r^{-1}(x)}A^{e},

which we view as a complemented submodule of
M
. In addition, we let
Q_{x}
be the orthogonal projection from
M
to
M_{x}
, so that

Q_{x}(t_{e}y)=[r(e)=x]t_{e}y\hskip 5.69046pt\forall e\in E^{1}\hskip 5.69046pt\forall y\in A.

Observe that the
Q_{x}
are pairwise orthogonal projections and that
\sum\limits_{x\in E^{0}}Q_{x}=1
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\psi:C(E^{0})\to\mathcal{L}(M)
be the unique unital
\ast
-homomorphism such that

\psi(q_{x})=Q_{x}\hskip 5.69046pt\forall x\in E^{0}.

From our working hypothesis that
E
has no sources we see that, for every
x\in E^{0}
, there is some
e\in E^{1}
such that
r(e)=x
. So,

Q_{x}(t_{e})=t_{e},

whence
Q_{x}\neq 0
. Consequently
\psi
is injective.

Proposition 4.5

(
[
21
, Proposition 10.10]
)
.

The pair
(\psi,V)
is a covariant representation of the
C^{*}
-dynamical system
\left(C(E^{0}),G\right)
in
\mathcal{L}(M)
.

Then, there exists a
\ast
-homomorphism

\Psi:C(E^{0})\rtimes G\to\mathcal{L}(M),

such that

\Psi(q_{x})=Q_{x}\hskip 5.69046pt\forall x\in E^{0},\text{ and }\Psi(v_{g})=V_{g}\hskip 5.69046pt\forall g\in G.

Equipped with the left-
A
-module structure provided by
\Psi
, we then have that
M
is a correspondence over
A
.

Our next goal is to prove that
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
is naturally isomorphic to the Cuntz-Pimsner algebra associated to the correspondence
M
, which we denote by
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
. As a first step, we identify a certain Cuntz-Krieger
E
-family.

Proposition 4.6

(
[
21
, Proposition 10.12]
)
.

The following relations hold within
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
:

(1)

For every
x\in E^{0}
, one has that
\sum_{e\in r^{-1}(x)}t_{e}t_{e}^{*}=q_{x}
.

(2)

\sum_{e\in E^{1}}t_{e}t_{e}^{*}=1
.

(3)

The set
\{q_{x}\mid x\in E^{0}\}\cup\{t_{e}\mid e\in E^{1}\}
is a Cuntz-Krieger
E
-family.

Thus,

Proposition 4.7

(
[
21
, Proposition 10.13]
)
.

There exists a unique surjective
\ast
-homomorphism

\Lambda:{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{M}

such that
\Lambda(p_{x})=q_{x}
,
\Lambda(s_{e})=t_{e}
, and
\Lambda(u_{g})=v_{g}
.

Let us now prove that
\Lambda
is bijective by providing an inverse to it. Since
A
is the crossed product
C^{*}
-algebra
C(E^{0})\rtimes G
, the universal property of
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
guarantees the existence of a
\ast
-homomorphism

\theta_{A}:A\to{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}},

sending
q_{x}
to
p_{x}
, and
v_{g}
to
u_{g}
. For each
e\in E^{1}
, consider the linear mapping

\theta_{M}:M\to{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}},

given, for every
m=(m_{e})_{e\in E^{1}}\in M
, by

\theta_{M}(m)=\sum_{e\in E^{1}}s_{e}\theta_{A}(m_{e})\in{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}.

Notice that
\theta_{M}(t_{e})=s_{e}
, for all
s\in E^{1}
, because

\theta_{M}(t_{e})=s_{e}\theta_{A}(q_{d(e)})=s_{e}p_{d(e)}=s_{e}.

Lemma 4.8

(
[
21
, Lemma 10.14]
)
.

The pair
(\theta_{A},\theta_{M})
is a representation of the correspondence
M
in the sense of
[
50
, Theorem 3.4]
, meaning that for all
y\in A
and all
\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in M
,

(1)

\theta_{M}(\xi)\theta_{A}(y)=\theta_{M}(\xi y),

(2)

\theta_{A}(y)\theta_{M}(\xi)=\theta_{M}(y\xi),

(3)

\theta_{M}(\xi)^{*}\theta_{M}(\xi^{\prime})=\theta_{A}(\langle\xi,\xi^{\prime}\rangle).

It is well known
[
50
, Theorem 3.4]
that the Toeplitz algebra for the correspondence
M
, usually denoted
\mathcal{T}_{M}
, is universal for representations of
M
, so there exists a
\ast
-homomorphism

\Theta_{0}:\mathcal{T}_{M}\to{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}},

coinciding with
\theta_{A}
on
A
and with
\theta_{M}
on
M
.

Theorem 4.9

(
[
21
, Theorem 10.15]
)
.

The map
\Theta_{0}
, defined above, factors through
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
, providing a
\ast
-isomorphism

\Theta:{\mathcal{O}}_{M}\to{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}},

such that
\Theta(q_{x})=p_{x}
,
\Theta(t_{e})=s_{e}
, and
\Theta(v_{g})=u_{g}
, for all
x\in E^{0}
,
e\in E^{1}
, and
g\in G
.

When
G
is amenable,
C(E^{0})\rtimes G
is nuclear. Then, from Theorem
4.9
, the fact that Toeplitz-Pimsner algebras over nuclear coefficient algebras are nuclear
[
9
, Theorem 4.6.25]
, and so are quotients of nuclear algebras
[
9
, Theorem 9.4.4]
, we obtain:

Corollary 4.10

(
[
21
, Corollary 10.16]
)
.

If
G
is amenable then
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
is nuclear.

Remark 4.11
.

Since
E^{0}
is finite, the nuclearity of
C(E^{0})\rtimes G
is equivalent to the amenability of
G
. However, if the present construction is generalized to infinite graphs, one could produce examples of non-amenable groups acting amenably on
E^{0}
, in which case
C(E^{0})\rtimes G
would be nuclear. The proof of
Corollary
4.10
could then be adapted to prove that
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
is nuclear.

Corollary 4.12

(
[
21
, Corollary 10.18]
)
.

If
G
is amenable, then
{\mathcal{G}_{\text{tight}}({\mathcal{S}_{G,E}})}
is an amenable groupoid.

5.
Self-similar graphs of countable graphs

In this section, we extend the construction of a
C^{*}
-algebra associated to a self-similar graph to the case of arbitrary countable graphs. We reduce the problem to the row-finite case with no sources by using a desingularization process. Finally, we characterize the properties associated with simplicity in this case.

The results of this section, contained in
[
22
]
, are essential to extend the scope of the results obtained in
[
21
]
for Katsura algebras over finite matrices to the general case; this guarantees, by
[
26
]
, that every Kirchberg algebra in the UCT is the full groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra of a second countable amenable ample groupoid.

5.1.
Extending self-similar graphs to countable graphs

First, we look at the problem of extending our class to the case of countably infinite graphs with no restrictions (i.e. sources, sinks, and infinite receivers are admitted).

Notice that, if
E^{0}
is countably infinite, then the algebra
C^{*}(E)
is no longer unital. Thus, if we intend to get a unitary representation of the group
G
associated to the algebra, we need to consider unitary representations of
G
in the multiplier algebra
\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{O}_{G,E}})
. This approach does not give us an intrinsic definition of the object, but it is very helpful to fix it precisely. In our search for such a definition, we follow the model of Katsura algebras
[
26
]
, where the unitary associated to an element of
{\mathbb{Z}}
is written in terms of partial unitaries associated to the projections
p_{x}
for
x\in E^{0}
.

5.1
.

The basic data for our construction is a triple
(G,E,\varphi)
composed of:

(1)

A countable directed graph
E=(E^{0},E^{1},r,s)
.

(2)

A discrete group
G
acting on
E
by graph automorphisms.

(3)

A 1-cocycle
\varphi:G\times E^{1}\rightarrow G
satisfying the property

\varphi(g,a)\cdot x=g\cdot x\text{ for every }g\in G,a\in E^{1},x\in E^{0}.

We will refer to that triple as a self-similar graph under (
1
).

As remarked before, this condition can be weakened to

\varphi(g,a)\cdot s(a)=g\cdot s(a)\text{ for every }g\in G,a\in E^{1},

(see e.g.
[
30
, Appendix A]
). Notice that all the results in Subsection
2.1
are true for
(G,E,\varphi)
as in (
5.1
). So, we assume that we have such a triple
(G,E,\varphi)
.

In order to build our algebra, we again resort to a set of generators

\{p_{x}:x\in E^{0}\}\cup\{s_{a}:a\in E^{1}\}

as in the case of classical self-similar graphs, generating a copy of
C^{*}(E)
into our algebra. However, once we allow
E^{0}
to be infinite, the lack of units prevents us from having unitary elements in our algebra, and hence the inclusion of the missing
u_{g}
becomes problematic.

The strategy we follow to circumvent this problem is to look at the elements
\{u_{g}\colon g\in G\}
into the multiplier algebra
{\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{O}_{G,E}})}
of our algebra
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
, and suppose that the assignment
g\mapsto u_{g}
defines a unitary group representation
u:G\rightarrow\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{O}_{G,E}})
. Then, in the multiplier algebra, we shall have relations of the form

u_{g}s_{a}=s_{ga}u_{\varphi(g,a)}

for every
g\in G,a\in E^{*}
. Since
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
is an ideal of
{\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{O}_{G,E}})}
, the product
u_{g,x}:=u_{g}p_{x}
is an element of
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
. In addition, the fact that the
p_{x}
add up to the identity of
{\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{O}_{G,E}})}
in the strict topology ensures that
\sum\limits_{x\in E^{0}}u_{g,x}=u_{g}
strictly. So, we add to our generators a collection of symbols

\{u_{g,x}:x\in E^{0},g\in G\},

which would play the role of proxies for the unitary elements we are prevented to consider.

Using the relations in
{\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{O}_{G,E}})}
(inspired by the relations in the original definition), we conclude that each
u_{g,x}
is a partial isometry satisfying:

(1)

u_{g,x}u_{g,x}^{*}=p_{g\cdot x}
.

(2)

u_{g,x}^{*}u_{g,x}=p_{x}
.

(3)

u_{g,s(a)}s_{a}=s_{g\cdot s(a)}u_{\varphi(g,a),s(a)}
.

(4)

u_{g,x}p_{x}=p_{g\cdot x}u_{g,x}
.

If we take the above identities as relations of our definition of
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
, we see that
\sum\limits_{x\in E^{0}}u_{g,x}
indeed converges to an element
u_{g}\in\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{O}_{G,E}})
, and it is easy to see that it is unitary. Since we are interested in getting a unitary representation of
G
in
\mathcal{M}({\mathcal{O}_{G,E}})
, we need to be sure that
u_{g}u_{h}=u_{gh}
for every
g,h\in G
. A
simple computation shows that this occurs exactly when

u_{gh,h^{-1}\cdot x}=u_{g,x}u_{h,h^{-1}\cdot x}

for every

g,h\in G
and every
x\in E^{0}

Given a triple

(2)

(a)

(b)

(c)

(d)

Remark 5.3

When

(1)

(2)

\ast
-representation of

(3)

u_{g,x}=u_{g}p_{x}
for every

and

.

(4)

u_{g}p_{x}=p_{gx}u_{g}
for every

and

.

(5)

Following the method adopted in the classic case, we wish to associate an

verbatim here:

Definition 5.4
.

Given a self-similar graph
(G,E,\varphi)
as in (

), we define a
\ast
-semigroup
{{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}}
as follows: as a set,

{{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}}=\{(\alpha,g,\beta):\alpha,\beta\in E^{*},\ g\in G,\ s(\alpha)=g\cdot s(\beta)\}\cup\{0\}.

With small adaptations, the previous results from the case of finite graphs hold here as well. Moreover, there is a semigroup representation

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\pi:&{\mathcal{S}_{G,E}}&\longrightarrow&{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}\\
&(\alpha,g,\beta)&\mapsto&s_{\alpha}u_{g,s(\alpha)}s_{\beta}^{*}\end{array}.

The essential point is the following result:

Theorem 5.5

(
[

, Theorem 2.5]
)

Let

be a self-similar graph as in (

(2)

In order to extend the results in

5.2.
Desingularizing self-similar graphs

Suppose that we have a self-similar graph
(G,E,\varphi)
as in (

), and let

denote the desingularized graph of

obtained in
[
16
]
. In this section, we show that we can define an action

and a
1
-cocycle

extending the ones in the original triple, such that
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{G,F}
are strong Morita equivalent

-algebras.

Remark 5.6
.

Let
(G,E,\varphi)
be a triple as in (
5.1
), and let

be a vertex. Then:

(1)

If

is a source, then so is
gx
for every

(2)

If

is an infinite receiver, then so is

for every

In view of this, when defining the desingularization, we need to keep track of the fact that, for any vertex in the orbit of a singular vertex, we must define the tails added to it as orbit-connected parts of the graph.

5.2.1.
Desingularizing a source

Now, we explain how to construct a triple
(G,F,\varphi)
that desingularizes a source in a triple
(G,E,\varphi)
.

Let
x\in E^{0}
be a source, and let
\widehat{G}
be a set of representatives of the orbits of

under the action
G\curvearrowright E^{0}
. We add an infinite tail into

and to all vertices in the orbit of
x
. Specifically, define

F^{1}=E^{1}\bigsqcup\{e_{i,g}\}_{g\in\widehat{G},i\geq 1}

F^{0}=E^{0}\bigsqcup\{v_{i,g}\}_{g\in\widehat{G},i\geq 1}

We extend the range map, the source map, and the action of
G
to

as follows:

(1)

s(e_{i,g})=r(e_{i+1,g})=v_{i,g}
for every

, while
ge_{i,1_{G}}=e_{i,g}
for every

.

(2)

For any

with

, we let
he_{i,1_{G}}=e_{i,1_{G}}
for every

.

At this point, we have applied the Drinen-Tomforde desingularization construction in such a way that it is coherent with the action
G\curvearrowright E

The next step is to extend the
1
-cocycle. To do this, for any

,

and any

we define
\widehat{\varphi}(h,e_{i,g}):=h
. Once this is done, what we have obtained is:

(3)

A graph
F
that extends

, constructed using the Drinen-Tomforde desingularization process.

(4)

An action
G\curvearrowright F
that extends the original action
G\curvearrowright E
.

(5)

A

-cocycle
\widehat{\varphi}:G\times F\rightarrow G
extending

Then, the self-similar graph

is the desingularization of
(G,E,\varphi)
at the source

5.2.2.
Desingularizing an infinite receiver

Now, we explain how to construct a triple
(G,F,\varphi)
that desingularizes an infinite receiver in a triple

Let

be an infinite receiver, let
\widehat{G}
be a set of representatives of the orbits of

under the action

We define
\alpha_{i,g}=e_{1,g}e_{2,g}\cdots e_{i-1,g}f_{i,g}

to

as follows:

(1)

For each

and

, while

(2)

For any

Again, we have applied the Drinen-Tomforde desingularization construction in such a way that it is coherent with the action

The next step is to extend the
1
-cocycle. To do this, we define for any

and any

(3)

\widehat{\varphi}(h,e_{i,g}):=h
.

(4)

\widehat{\varphi}(h,f_{i,g})=\varphi(h,a_{j})

In particular,
\widehat{\varphi}(h,\alpha_{i,g})=\varphi(h,a_{j})

(5)

A graph

that extends

, constructed using the Drinen-Tomforde desingularization process.

(6)

An action

that extends the original action

\widehat{\varphi}:G\times F\rightarrow G

Then, the self-similar graph

on the infinite receiver

5.2.3.
The desingularization result

Now, we check that the results of Drinen and Tomforde about their desingularization process for

extend to this context.

A simple inspection shows that
[
16
, Lemmas 2.9 & 2.10]
extend to our context. Now, we arrange the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 2.11]
in order to obtain the desired Morita equivalence between
{\mathcal{O}_{G,E}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{G,F}
. We follow the notation of the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 2.11]
. Let
E
be a graph with a singular vertex
v_{0}
, let

\{t_{e},q_{v}\mid e\in F^{1},v\in F^{0}\}

be the canonical set of generators for
C^{*}(F)
, and let

\{s_{e},p_{v}\mid e\in E^{1},v\in E^{0}\}

be the Cuntz-Krieger
E
-family constructed
inside

C^{*}(F)
in
[
16
, Lemma 2.9]
. Recall that
\{s_{e},p_{v}\}
is defined as follows:

(1)

For every

,

.

(2)

For every
e\in E^{1}
such that

,

.

(3)

For every

such that

for some

, and thus

Define
B:=C^{*}(\{s_{e},p_{v}\})
and

. Then,
[
16
, Theorem 2.11]
shows that

C^{*}(E)\cong B\cong pC^{*}(F)p

and that

is a full projection.

Now, observe that:

(1)

For every
g\in G
and

we have that
u_{g}p_{x}=u_{g}q_{x}=q_{gx}u_{g}=p_{gx}u_{g}

(2)

If

u_{g}s_{e}=u_{g}t_{e}=t_{ge}u_{\widehat{\varphi}(g,e)}=s_{ge}u_{{\varphi}(g,e)}

\widehat{\varphi}

(3)

If

u_{g}s_{e}=u_{g}t_{\alpha_{j}}=u_{g}t_{e_{1}}t_{e_{2}}\cdots t_{e_{j-1}}t_{f_{j}}=t_{ge_{1}}t_{ge_{2}}\cdots t_{ge_{j-1}}u_{g}t_{f_{j}}=

Thus, the

-algebra isomorphism

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\Phi:&C^{*}(E)&\rightarrow&C^{*}(\{s_{e},q_{x}\})\\
&P_{x}&\mapsto&q_{x}\\
&T_{e}&\mapsto&s_{e}\end{array}

(1)

For every

and

.

(2)

For every

Hence, by the universal property of

-algebra isomorphism

Moreover, following
[
16

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\Psi:&\Phi(C^{*}(E))&\rightarrow&pC^{*}(F)p\\
&s_{\alpha}s_{\beta}^{*}&\mapsto&ps_{\alpha}s_{\beta}^{*}p\\

Notice that in

-algebra isomorphism

Theorem 5.7

(
[

)

Hence, up to Morita equivalence, we can assume that

graphs. To define the corresponding algebras, they use a Cuntz-Pimsner algebra model. They also shall be acknowledged for coining the term
Exel-Pardo algebras
for referring to self-similar graph algebras.

Other generalizations are the following:

6.1.
Self-similar
k
-graphs

Graph
C^{*}
-algebras have been a successful source of knowledge for operator algebraists, as allowed to easily link properties of algebras with the combinatorial properties of “simpler” objects
[
52
]
. But they also have limitations on the scope of the families of algebras that fall under their umbrella. So, quickly appeared new generalizations of directed graphs covering the gaps: ultragraphs, labelled graphs, etc.

One of the most successful constructions has been higher rank graphs (or
k
-graphs, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
). The difference between this construction and the previous ones is that to define it, Kumjian and Pask deliberately avoided using a graph language, and preferred a categorical approach, which will turn out to be useful in the sequel.

Definition 6.1

(
[
29
, Definitions 1.1]
,
[
53
, Definitions 2.1]
)
.

Given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, a graph of rank
k
(or
k
-graph)
(\Lambda,d)
consists of a
countable category
\Lambda=(\text{Obj}(\Lambda),\text{Hom}(\Lambda),r,s)
together with a functor
d:\Lambda\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}^{k}
, called the degree map, which satisfies the
unique factorization property
(UFP): for every
\lambda\in\Lambda
and
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{k}
with
d(\lambda)=m+n
, there are unique elements
\mu,\eta\in\Lambda
such that
\lambda=\mu\eta
,

d(\mu)=m
and
d(\eta)=n
. Elements
\lambda\in\Lambda
are called paths. For
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{k}
and
v\in\text{Obj}(\Lambda)
, we define
\Lambda^{m}:=\{\lambda\in\Lambda\mid d(\lambda)=m\}
and
\Lambda^{m}(v):=\{\lambda\in\Lambda^{m}\mid r(\lambda)=v\}
. A morphism between two
k
-graphs
(\Lambda_{1},d_{1})
and
(\Lambda_{2},d_{2})
is a functor
f:\Lambda_{1}\rightarrow\Lambda_{2}
that respects the degree maps.
(\Lambda,d)
is row-finite if for each
v\in\text{Obj}(\Lambda)
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{k}
, the set
\Lambda^{m}(v)
is
finite;
(\Lambda,d)
has no sources if
\Lambda^{m}(v)\neq\emptyset
for all
v\in\text{Obj}(\Lambda)
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{k}
.
The unique factorization property says that there is a unique path of degree
0
at each vertex, and hence allows us to identify
\text{Obj}(\Lambda)
with
\Lambda^{0}
.

Then, we can construct a
C^{*}
-algebra associated to this new object.

Definition 6.2

(c.f.
[
35
, Definition 2.4]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a
k
-graph. Then, a family of partial isometries
\{S_{\mu}\}_{\mu}\in\Lambda
in a
C^{*}
-algebra
B
is called a Cuntz-Krieger
\Lambda
-family if:

(1)

\{S_{\mu}\}_{\mu}\in{\Lambda^{0}}
is a family of mutually orthogonal projections;

(2)

S_{\mu\nu}=S_{\mu}S\nu
if
s(\mu)=r(\nu)
;

(3)

S_{\mu}^{*}S_{\mu}=S_{s(\mu)}
for all
\mu\in\Lambda
; and

(4)

S_{v}=\sum_{\mu\in v\Lambda^{n}}S_{\mu}S_{\mu}^{*}
for all
v\in\Lambda^{0}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{k}
.

The
C^{*}
-algebra
{\mathcal{O}}_{\Lambda}
generated by a universal Cuntz-Krieger
\Lambda
-family

is called the
k
-graph
C^{*}
-algebra of
\Lambda
.

Li and Yang
[
35
]
extended the definition of classical self-similar graphs from actions of groups on graphs to actions of groups on
k
-graphs
.

Definition 6.3

(
[
35
, Definition 3.1]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a
k
-graph. A bijection
\varphi:\Lambda\to\Lambda
is an automorphism of
\Lambda
if:

(1)

\varphi(\Lambda^{n})\subseteq\Lambda^{n}
for all

;

(2)

s\circ\varphi=\varphi\circ s
and
r\circ\varphi=\varphi\circ r
.

If
G
is a (discrete countable) group, an action of

on

is a homomorphism from
G
to
\text{Aut}(\Lambda)
. For
g\in G
and
\mu\in\Lambda
, we write
g\cdot\mu:=\varphi(g)(\mu)
.

Using that notion, they define their generalization.

Definition 6.4

(
[
35
, Definition 3.2]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a
k
-graph, and let
G
be a group acting on
\Lambda
. The action is self-similar if there exists a restriction map
G\Lambda\to G
defined by
(g,\mu)\mapsto g|_{\mu}
such that:

(1)

g\cdot(\mu\nu)=(g\cdot\mu)(g|_{\mu}\cdot\nu)
for all
g\in G
,
\mu,\nu\in\Lambda
with
s(\mu)=r(\nu)
;

(2)

g|_{v}=g
for all
g\in G
,

;

(3)

g|_{\mu\nu}={g|_{\mu}}|_{\nu}
for all
g\in G
,
\mu,\nu\in\Lambda
with
s(\mu)=r(\nu)
;

(4)

e|_{\mu}=e
for

the neutral element, and all
\mu\in\Lambda
;

(5)

(gh)|_{\mu}=g|_{h\cdot\mu}h|_{\mu}
for all

\mu\in\Lambda
.

Then,

is called a self-similar

-graph over

is called a self-similar group on

.

Furthermore, they define the universal
C^{*}
-algebra associated to it
when
\Lambda
as a finite set of objects
:

Definition 6.5

(
[
35
, Definition 3.2]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
with

be a self-similar
k
-graph over a group

-graph

-algebra

-algebra generated by a family of unitaries

satisfying:

(1)

for all

;

(2)

for all

.

They showed that

can be realized as a Cuntz-Pimsner algebra associated to a suitable product system
[

. Under the additional condition of

being pseudo-free, they construct a Deacoun-Renault groupoid

[

, and showed that, if

-algebra associated to

An interesting generalization was given by Larki
[

. His approach is based on the association of a suitable inverse semigroup

with the self-similar

-graph

(which generalizes the semigroup

introduced in
[

). This allows us to avoid restrictions about finiteness conditions on
\Lambda
, or pseudo-freeness of

. Since the notation in
[
31
]
slightly changes from that in
[
35
]
, let us fix it explicitly:

Definition 6.6
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned
k
-graph and

be a discrete group. A triple

is called a

-graph

(1)

(2)

If there is no ambiguity, we often write a self-similar

Standing assumption:
Throughout this subsection, we work with self-similar
k
-graphs

over finitely aligned

-graphs
\Lambda

Definition 6.7
.

Let
(G,\Lambda)
be a self-similar
k
-graph. A

-family
is a set

\{s_{\mu}:\mu\in\Lambda\}\cup\{u_{v,g}:v\in\Lambda^{0},g\in G\}

The

-algebra

-algebra generated by a

Then, he defined his inverse semigroup: given a self-similar

Property

and

Definition 6.8
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{G,\Lambda}
be the collection of all finite sets

6.2.
Self-similar actions of groupoids on graphs

Laca, Ramagge, Raeburn, and Whittaker
[
30
]
extended the definition of self-similar graphs to include
faithful
actions of groupoids on the path space of
finite
graphs. Since a groupoid
G
has a large number of “neutral” elements (the units), we need to establish a suitable definition of the action of
G
on a finite graph
E
.

Given a graph
E
, and any
v\in E^{0}
, the subgraph
vE^{*}=\{\mu\in E^{*}\colon r(\mu)=v\}

is a rooted tree with root
v\in E^{0}
, and
T_{E}=\bigsqcup_{v\in E^{0}}vE^{*}
is a disjoint union of trees, or
forest
. A
groupoid
is a small category with inverses. Thus, a groupoid consists of a set
G^{0}
of objects, a set
G
of morphisms, two functions
c,d:G\to G^{0}
, and
a partially defined product
(g,h)\mapsto gh
from
G^{2}:=\{(g,h):d(g)=c(h)\}
to
G

such that
(G,G^{0},c,d)
is a category and such that each
g\in G
has an inverse
g^{-1}
.

Since the groupoid operation is
partial
, to define the action of the groupoid
G
on
E
, we need to deal with partial automorphisms of
E^{*}
. These can be codified as partial automorphisms of the forest
T_{E}
, defined on each subtree and restricting to isomorphisms on its domain and range.

Definition 6.9

(
[
30
, Definition 3.1]
)
.

Suppose
E=(E^{0},E^{1},r,s)
is a directed graph. A
partial isomorphism
of
T_{E}
consists of two vertices
v,w\in E^{0}
and a bijection
g:vE^{*}\to wE^{*}
such that

(5)

g|_{vE^{k}}\text{ is a bijection onto }wE^{k}\text{ for }k\in{\mathbb{N}},\text{ and }g(\mu e)\in g(\mu)E^{1}\text{ for all }\mu e\in vE^{*}.

For
v\in E^{0}
, we write
\operatorname{id}_{v}:vE^{*}\to vE^{*}
for the partial isomorphism given by

\operatorname{id}_{v}(\mu)=\mu
for all
\mu\in vE^{*}
.
We denote the set of all partial isomorphisms of
T_{E}
by
\text{PIso}(E^{*})
.
We define domain and codomain maps
d,c\colon\text{PIso}(E^{*})\to E^{0}
so that
g:d(g)E^{*}\to c(g)E^{*}
;
thus in (
5
) we have
d(g):=v
and
c(g):=w
.

The key point is

Proposition 6.10

(
[
30
, Proposition 3.2]
)
.

Suppose that
E=(E^{0},E^{1},r,s)
is a directed graph with associated forest
T_{E}
. Then
(\text{PIso}(E^{*}),E^{0},c,d)
is a groupoid in which: the product is given by composition of functions, the identity isomorphism at
v\in E^{0}
is
\operatorname{id}_{v}:vE^{*}\to vE^{*}
, and the inverse of
g\in\text{PIso}(E^{*})
is the inverse of the function
g:d(g)E^{*}\to c(g)E^{*}
.

Given a directed graph
E
and a groupoid
G
with unit space
E^{0}
, an
action
of
G
on the path space
E^{*}
is a (unit-preserving) groupoid homomorphism
\phi:G\to\text{PIso}(E^{*})
; the action is
faithful
if
\phi
is one-to-one.
If the homomorphism is fixed, we can write
g\cdot\mu
for
\phi_{g}(\mu)
.

Definition 6.11

(
[
30
, Definition 3.3]
)
.

Suppose
E=(E^{0},E^{1},r,s)
is a directed graph and
G
is a groupoid with unit space
E^{0}
which acts
faithfully
on
T_{E}
. The action is
self-similar
if for every
g\in G
and
e\in d(g)E^{1}
, there exists
h\in G
such that

(6)

g\cdot(e\mu)=(g\cdot e)(h\cdot\mu)\text{ for all }\mu\in s(e)E^{*}.

Since the action is faithful, there is then exactly one such
h\in G
, and we write
g|_{e}:=h
.

Definition (
6.11
) has some immediate and important consequences.

Lemma 6.12

(
[
30
, Lemma 3.4]
)
.

Suppose
E=(E^{0},E^{1},r,s)
is a directed graph and
G
is a groupoid with unit space
E^{0}
acting self-similarly on
T_{E}
. Then for
g,h\in G
with
d(h)=c(g)
and
e\in d(g)E^{1}
, we have

(1)

d(g|_{e})=s(e)
and
c(g|_{e})=s(g\cdot e)
,

(2)

r(g\cdot e)=g\cdot r(e)
and
s(g\cdot e)=g|_{e}\cdot s(e)
,

(3)

if
g=\operatorname{id}_{r(e)}
, then
g|_{e}=\operatorname{id}_{s(e)}
, and

(4)

(hg)|_{e}=(h|_{g\cdot e})(g|_{e})
.

Notice that Lemma
6.12
(
2
) implies that
the source map may not be equivariant:
s(g\cdot e)\not=g\cdot s(e)
in general. Indeed,
g\cdot s(e)
will often not make sense:
g
maps
d(g)E^{*}
onto
c(g)E^{*}
, and
s(e)
is not in
d(g)E^{*}
unless
s(e)=d(g)
. This non-equivariance of the source map is one of the main points of difference with the original definition of self-similar action, and also differs from that in
[
5
]
.

With this new construction at hand, they construct the Toeplitz and Cuntz-Krieger algebras associated to this self-similar graph
[
30
, Section 4]
, and recover the Katsura algebras
[
30
, Example 7.7]
as a particular case. Moreover, it is shown
[
30
, Appendix A]
that if a classical self-similar graph
(G,E,\varphi)
satisfies a certain faithfulness condition, then it gives rise to faithful self-similar groupoid actions; to be coherent with
[
30
]
notation, let me write
(G,E,\sigma,\varphi)
for
(G,E,\varphi)
with action
\sigma:G\curvearrowright E
.

Proposition 6.13

(
[
30
, Proposition A.2]
)
.

Suppose

is a self-similar action of a group on a graph and let

be the transformation groupoid with

,

, and unit space

, and consider the maps

(7)

\displaystyle(g,v)\cdot\mu

\displaystyle:=\sigma_{g}^{*}(\mu)\quad\text{ for $\mu\in vE^{*}$ and}

\displaystyle(g,v)|_{\mu}

\displaystyle:=(\varphi^{*}(g,\mu),s(\mu))\quad\text{ for $\mu\in vE^{*}$}.

Then (

on

Moreover, if

.

6.3.
Self-similar graphs with a twist

One topic in groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras theory which attracts a lot of interest is that of
twisted groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras
, and their algebraic siblings,
twisted Steinberg algebras
(see
[
3
,
4
]
, and references herewith).

Cortiñas
[
11
]
defined a twisted version of self-similar graphs using an algebraic approach, showed that it relates to the classical definition associated to twisted groupoid extensions, and studied simplicity, pure infiniteness, as well as
kk
-theoretical and homological results. Let us give a view of his work.

Fix a self-similar graph
(G,E,\phi)
(he termed it
Exel-Pardo tuple
), and write
\mathcal{U}(\ell)
for the group of invertible elements of our ground ring
\ell
. A
twisted Exel-Pardo tuple
is an Exel-Pardo tuple
(G,E,\phi)
together with a
1
-cocyle
c:G\times E^{1}\to\mathcal{U}(\ell)
. Remark that

\phi_{c}:G\times E^{1}\to\mathcal{U}(\ell)G\subset\mathcal{U}(\ell[G]),\ \phi_{c}(g,e)=c(g,e)\phi(g,e)

is a
1
-cocycle with values in the multiplicative group of the group algebra
\ell[G]
. We write
(G,E,\phi_{c})
for the twisted EP-tuple
(G,E,\phi,c)
.

Lemma 6.14

(
[
11
, Lemma 2.3.1]
)
.

Let
(G,E,\phi_{c})
be a twisted Exel-Pardo tuple. Then

extends uniquely to a
1
-cocycle
G\times\mathcal{P}(E)\to\mathcal{U}(\ell)
satisfying

(9)

c(g,v)=1,\,\,c(g,\alpha\beta)=c(g,\alpha)c(\phi(g,\alpha),\beta)

for all concatenable
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{P}(E)
.

Remark 6.15
.

Lemma

imply that, for extension of
\phi_{c}
to a map
\ell[G]\times\mathcal{P}(E)\to\ell[G]
,
\ell

\phi_{c}(g,\alpha\beta)=\phi_{c}(\phi_{c}(g,\alpha),\beta).

Let
\mathcal{S}
be an inverse semigroup with inverse operation

, pointed by a zero element
\emptyset
. A (normalized)
2

with values in the pointed inverse semigroup

\omega:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{U}(\ell)_{\bullet}

such that for all

and

(10)

\displaystyle\omega(st,u)\omega(s,t)=\omega(s,tu)\omega(t,u),

(11)

\displaystyle\omega(\emptyset,s)=\omega(s,\emptyset)=0,\ \ \omega(x,x^{*})=\omega(xx^{*},x)=\omega(x,x^{*}x)=1.

The
twisted semigroup algebra

\ell[\mathcal{S},\omega]
is the
\ell

\ell[\mathcal{S}]=(\bigoplus_{s\in S}\ell s)/\ell\emptyset
equipped with the
\ell
-linear multiplication
\cdot_{\omega}
induced by

(12)

A straightforward calculation shows that

is an associative algebra and that for every

,

s\cdot_{\omega}s^{*}\cdot_{\omega}s=s.

Let
(G,E,\phi_{c})
be a twisted Exel-Pardo tuple. On the inverse semigroup
\mathcal{S}(G,E,\phi)
we can define a map

(13)

\displaystyle\omega:\mathcal{S}(G,E,\phi)\times\mathcal{S}(G,E,\phi)\to\mathcal{U}(\ell)_{\bullet}

\displaystyle\omega(0,s)=\omega(s,0)=0\ \ \forall s\in\mathcal{S}(G,E,\phi),

\displaystyle\omega((\alpha,g,\beta),(\gamma,h,\theta))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c(h,h^{-1}(\beta_{1}))&\text{ if }\beta=\gamma\beta_{1},\\
c(g,\gamma_{1})&\text{ if }\gamma=\beta\gamma_{1},\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Lemma 6.16

(
[
11

)
.

The map
\omega
of (

2
-cocycle.

Now, we proceed to define twisted Exel-Pardo algebras. First, we define the
Cohn algebra
of the twisted Exel-Pardo tuple
(G,E,\phi_{c})
to be the quotient

7.1.
Basic definitions

Given a small category
\Lambda
, we denote by
\Lambda^{\circ}
its objects, and we identify
\Lambda^{\circ}
with identity morphisms, so that
\Lambda^{\circ}\subseteq\Lambda
. Given

\Lambda^{-1}:=\{\alpha\in\Lambda\mid\exists\beta\in\Lambda\text{ such that }\alpha\beta=s(\beta)\}\,.

Definition 7.1
.

Given a small category
\Lambda
, and let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\Lambda
:

(1)

\Lambda
is

if
\alpha\beta=\alpha\gamma
then
\beta=\gamma
,

(2)

\Lambda
is

\beta\alpha=\gamma\alpha
then
\beta=\gamma
,

(3)

\Lambda

has no inverses
if
\alpha\beta=s(\beta)
then
\alpha=\beta=s(\beta)
.

A
category of paths
is a small category that is right and left cancellative and has no inverses.

Examples of left cancellative small categories (LCSC for short) are:

(1)

Higher-rank graphs, and arbitrary subcategories of them.

(2)

The positive cone in a discrete ordered group (and, in particular, quasi-
lattice ordered groups).

(3)

P

Notice that if
\Lambda
is either left or right cancellative, then the only idempotents in

\Lambda^{\circ}
.

(a)

(b)

if

(c)

T_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}^{*}T_{\beta}T_{\beta}^{*}=\bigvee_{\gamma\in\alpha\vee\beta}T_{\gamma}T_{\gamma}^{*}

for every

and for all

Spielberg presented a natural groupoid
\mathcal{G}|_{\partial\Lambda}
associated to

7.2.
The inverse semigroup of a LCSC

We start by recalling some basic concepts that we will need in the sequel.

Definition 7.11
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be an inverse semigroup, and let
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{S})
be its semi-lattice of idempotents. We say that
s,t\in\mathcal{S}
are compatible, denoted by
s\sim t
, if both
s^{*}t
and
st^{*}
belong to
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{S})
.

Lemma 7.12

(
[
32
, Lemma 1.4.16]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be an inverse semigroup, and let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathcal{S}
. If
\bigvee_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\alpha\in\mathcal{S}
(the least-upper bound with the above defined partial order), then the elements of
\Sigma
are pairwise compatible.

We say that an inverse semigroup
\mathcal{S}
is
(finitely) distributive
if whenever
\Sigma
is a (finite) subset of
\mathcal{S}
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
, if
\bigvee_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\alpha\in\mathcal{S}
then
\bigvee_{\alpha\in\Sigma}s\alpha\in\mathcal{S}
and
s\left(\bigvee_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\alpha\right)=\bigvee_{\alpha\in\Sigma}s\alpha
.

we say that
\mathcal{S}
is
(finitely) complete
if for every (finite) subset
\Sigma\subseteq\mathcal{S}
of pairwise compatible elements we have that
\bigvee_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\alpha\in\mathcal{S}
.

Notice that this property is not necessarily inherited by its inverse subsemigroups. Indeed, the point is that given
\Sigma\subseteq\mathcal{S}
a set of pairwise compatible elements, and
s\in\mathcal{S}
:

(1)

Not necessarily
\bigvee_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\alpha\in\mathcal{S}
,

(2)

even if
\bigvee_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\alpha\in\mathcal{S}
, it can happen that
\bigvee_{\alpha\in\Sigma}s\alpha\notin\mathcal{S}
.

Now, given a LCSC
\Lambda
, we define an inverse semigroup associated to
\Lambda
. We denote by
\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
the inverse semigroup of partial bijections of
\Lambda
.

Definition 7.13
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC. For any
\alpha\in\Lambda
, we define two maps:

(1)

\sigma^{\alpha}:\alpha\Lambda\to s(\alpha)\Lambda
given by
\alpha\beta\mapsto\beta\,,

(2)

\tau^{\alpha}:s(\alpha)\Lambda\to\alpha\Lambda
given by
\beta\mapsto\alpha\beta\,.

Clearly
\sigma^{\alpha}
is injective, and since
\Lambda
is left cancellative so is
\tau^{\alpha}
, whence both maps belong to
\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
. Moreover,

\sigma^{\alpha}=\sigma^{\alpha}\tau^{\alpha}\sigma^{\alpha}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tau^{\alpha}=\tau^{\alpha}\sigma^{\alpha}\tau^{\alpha}\,,

for every
\alpha\in\Lambda
.

Lemma 7.14

(
[
46
, Lemma 2.3]
)
.

Given a LCSC
\Lambda
, we define the semigroup

\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}:=\left\langle\sigma^{\alpha},\tau^{\alpha}:\alpha\in\Lambda\right\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{I}(\Lambda).

Then
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
is an inverse semigroup.

Now, we connect
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
to the semigroups appearing in
[
15
,
55
,
56
]
.

Definition 7.15
.

Let
\Lambda
be a small category. A
zigzag
is an even tuple of the form

\xi=(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})

with
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\Lambda
,
r(\alpha_{i})=r(\beta_{i})
for every
1\leq i\leq n
and
s(\alpha_{i+1})=s(\beta_{i})
of every
1\leq i<n
. We denote by
\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda}
the set of zigzags of
\Lambda
. Given
\xi\in\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda}
, we define
s(\xi)=s(\beta_{n})
,
r(\xi)=s(\alpha_{1})
and
\bar{\xi}=(\beta_{n},\alpha_{n},\ldots,\beta_{1},\alpha_{1})
.

Every
\xi\in\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda}
defines a zigzag map
\varphi_{\xi}\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
by

\varphi_{\xi}=\sigma^{\alpha_{1}}\tau^{\beta}_{1}\cdots\sigma^{\alpha_{n}}\tau^{\beta_{n}}\,.

We denote
\mathcal{Z}(\Lambda)=\{\varphi_{\xi}:\xi\in\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda}\}
.

Remark 7.16
.

(1)

For every
\alpha\in\Lambda
we can define
\xi_{\alpha}:=(r(\alpha),\alpha)
. Notice that
\varphi_{\xi_{\alpha}}=\tau^{\alpha}
and
\varphi_{\bar{\xi}_{\alpha}}=\sigma^{\alpha}
.

(2)

\mathcal{Z}(\Lambda)
is closed by concatenation, and
\varphi_{\xi_{1}}\circ\varphi_{\xi_{2}}=\varphi_{\xi_{1}\xi_{2}}
.

(3)

For every
\xi\in\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda}
, then
\varphi_{\bar{\xi}}=\varphi_{\xi}^{-1}
.

Thus,

Lemma 7.17

(
[
6
, Section 7.2]
)
.

If
\Lambda
is a LCSC, then
\mathcal{Z}(\Lambda)
is an inverse semigroup. Moreover,
\mathcal{Z}(\Lambda)=\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
.

To understand the structure of
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
, we need to restrict ourselves to the case of finite aligned LCSC. First, we introduce a definition.

Definition 7.18
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned LCSC, and let
s\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
. We say that a presentation
s=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\beta_{i}}
is
irredundant
if for all
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
we have
\alpha_{i}\not\in[\alpha_{j}]
and
\beta_{i}\not\in[\beta_{j}]
.

Lemma 7.19

(
[
55
, Lemma 3.3 & Theorem 6.3]
)
.

If
\Lambda
is a finitely aligned LCSC, then every
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
is the supremum of a finite family of elements of the form
\tau^{\alpha}\sigma^{\beta}
with
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
and
s(\alpha)=s(\beta)
. Moreover, if such a decomposition is irredundant, then it is unique (up to permutation).

Notice that, given any finite family
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}\subset\Lambda
, the elements
\{\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\alpha_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
are pairwise compatible, so that
\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\alpha_{i}}\in\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
, but not necessarily to
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
. Thus, in order to do some essential arguments, we need to consider a new object.

Definition 7.20
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned LCSC. We define

\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}=\left\{\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\beta_{i}}\mid\{\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\beta_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}\text{ are pairwise compatible}\right\}.

By Lemma
7.19
,
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}\subset\mathcal{I}(\Lambda)
. Moreover, by
[
32
, Proposition 1.4.20 & Proposition 1.4.17]
,
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
is closed by composition and inverses, and moreover, is finitely distributive. Thus,

Lemma 7.21

(
[
46
, Lemma 2.8]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned LCSC. Then,
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
is the smallest finitely complete, finitely distributive, inverse semigroup containing
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
.

Now, we proceed to understand who are the elements in
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda})
and the order relation, in order to be able to work with filters on
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{S})
.

Lemma 7.22

(
[
46
, Lemma 2.9]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned LCSC. Then
e\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda})
if and only if
e=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\alpha_{i}}
for some
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\Lambda
.

Proposition 7.23

(
[
46
, Proposition 2.10]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned LCSC, and let
e=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\alpha_{i}}
,
f=\bigvee_{j=1}^{m}\tau^{\beta_{j}}\sigma^{\beta_{j}}
be idempotents of either
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
or
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
(written in irredundant form). Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

e\leq f
,

(2)

for each
1\leq k\leq n
, there exists
1\leq l\leq m
such that
\beta_{l}\leq\alpha_{k}
.

7.3.
A space of filters

Now, we describe the spaces of filters on
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
we need to define our groupoid. But, instead of using the classical description, we produce a new model, associated to the order properties of our category
\Lambda

To guarantee that every filter has such a path model, we introduce a restriction on
\Lambda

Definition 7.24
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned LCSC. We say that a filter
\eta\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
enjoys condition
(*)
if given
\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\alpha_{i}}\in\eta

\tau^{\alpha_{j}}\sigma^{\alpha_{j}}\in\eta
4
4

4

This property corresponds to the filter being
prime

.

Notice that, if
\Lambda
is singly aligned, then every filter enjoy condition
(*)
(see e.g.
[
14

). Before showing how to construct the path model of

Lemma 7.25

(
[

)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned LCSC. Then, any
\eta\in\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{tight}
satisfies condition
(*)
. In particular, every
\eta\in\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\infty}
satisfies condition
(*)

Now, we proceed to introduce a set of paths, inspired in a idea of Spielberg
[

Definition 7.26
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned LCSC. A nonempty subset
F
of
\Lambda
is:

(1)

Hereditary
, if
\alpha\in\Lambda
,
\beta\in F
and
\alpha\leq\beta

(2)

(upwards) directed

\gamma\in F

\alpha,\beta\leq\gamma

We denote by
\Lambda^{*}
the set of nonempty, hereditary, directed subsets of
\Lambda

The elements of
\Lambda^{*}
are our paths. We remark here that, in the context of partially ordered abelian groups (with interpolation), this kind of sets are called

to refer to them in our context.

Definition 7.27
.

Given

Lemma 7.28

(

Let
\Lambda

\Delta_{\eta}\in\Lambda^{*}

Definition 7.29
.

If
\Lambda
is a finitely aligned LCSC, we define

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{*}=\{\eta\in\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\mid\eta\text{ satisfies condition }(*)\}.

Thus,

(
[

If
\Lambda
is a finitely aligned LCSC, then

\begin{array}[]{rl}\Phi:\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{*}&\longrightarrow\Lambda^{*}\\
\eta&\longmapsto\Delta_{\eta}\,,\end{array}

is a well-defined map.

Now, we construct an inverse for this map.

Definition 7.31
.

Given
F\in\Lambda^{*}
, we define

\eta_{F}:=\{f\in{\mathcal{E}}\mid f\geq\tau^{\alpha}\sigma^{\alpha}\text{ for some }\alpha\in F\}.

Lemma 7.32

(
[

, Lemma 3.18]
)
.

If
\Lambda
is a finitely aligned LCSC, then for every

we have that

Corollary 7.33

(
[

)
.

If
\Lambda
is a finitely aligned LCSC, then

\begin{array}[]{rl}\Psi:\Lambda^{*}&\longrightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{*}\\

Lemma 7.34

(
[

, Lemma 3.20]
)
.

If
\Lambda
is a finitely aligned LCSC, then
\Phi

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\infty}

Definition 7.35

Let
\Lambda

Lemma 7.36

(
[

)

Let

\{\mathcal{U}(X,Y)\mid X=\{\tau^{\alpha}\sigma^{\alpha}\}\,,Y=\{\tau^{\beta_{i}}\sigma^{\beta_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}\},

Hence, both

(

)
.

Let

.

Given

(

)
.

Let

(2)

7.4.
The action

Since we have a homeomorphism
\Psi:\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{*}\to\Lambda^{*}
with inverse
\Psi
(Lemma
7.37
), we can transfer the action of
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
on
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{*}
to
\Lambda^{*}
. First, we fix the domain and range.

Definition 7.40
.

Let
s=\tau^{\alpha}\sigma^{\beta}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
. Then, we define

E_{\alpha}:=E_{ss^{*}}=\Phi(D_{ss^{*}})=\Phi(\mathcal{U}(\{\alpha\},\emptyset))=\{C\in\Lambda^{*}\mid\alpha\in C\}\,

and

E_{\beta}:=E_{s^{*}s}=\Phi(D_{s^{*}s})=\Phi(\mathcal{U}(\{\beta\},\emptyset))=\{C\in\Lambda^{*}\mid\beta\in C\}\,.

Given
s=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\beta_{i}}
, we define

E_{s^{*}s}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{\beta_{i}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\Phi(D_{\tau^{\beta_{i}}\sigma^{\beta_{i}}})=\Phi(D_{s^{*}s})\,.

The sets
E_{s^{*}s}
and
E_{ss^{*}}
are the natural candidates for being the domain and range of the partial action of
s\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
on
\Lambda^{*}
.

The next step is to define the action. We start by defining the action in the particular case of
s=\tau^{\alpha}\sigma^{\beta}
. In this case,
F\in E_{\beta}
if and only if
\beta\in F
. Then, we define

\tau^{\alpha}\sigma^{\beta}\cdot F=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\leq\gamma\\
\gamma\in F\end{subarray}}[\alpha\sigma^{\beta}(\gamma)]\,,

where
[\delta]
is the set of initial segments of
\delta\in\Lambda
, which clearly belong to
\Lambda^{*}
.

Moreover,
\tau^{\alpha}\sigma^{\beta}\cdot F\in E_{\alpha}
. Thus, in this case, we have that

\tau^{\alpha}\sigma^{\beta}\cdot:E_{\beta}\to E_{\alpha}\,,\qquad F\mapsto\tau^{\alpha}\sigma^{\beta}\cdot F\,,

is a well-defined map.

Now, set
s=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\beta_{i}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
, with

E_{s^{*}s}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{\beta_{i}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad E_{ss^{*}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{\alpha_{i}}\,.

By Lemma
7.12
,
\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\beta_{i}}
and
\tau^{\alpha_{j}}\sigma^{\beta_{j}}
are compatible for
1\leq i,j\leq n
, and then
\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\beta_{i}}\tau^{\beta_{j}}\sigma^{\alpha_{j}}
and
\tau^{\beta_{i}}\sigma^{\alpha_{i}}\tau^{\alpha_{j}}\sigma^{\beta_{j}}
are idempotents in
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
.

For every
1\leq i\leq n
and every
F\in E_{\beta_{i}}
, we define

s\cdot F=\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\beta_{i}}\cdot F\,.

The point is that, if
F\in E_{\beta_{i}}\cap E_{\beta_{j}}
for
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
, then
\tau^{\alpha_{i}}\sigma^{\beta_{i}}\cdot F=\tau^{\alpha_{j}}\sigma^{\beta_{j}}\cdot F
.

Because of this fact, we define the map

s\cdot:E_{s^{*}s}\to E_{ss^{*}}\,,

as follows: given
F\in E_{s^{*}s}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}E_{\beta_{i}}
we can assume, after re-indexing, that
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}\in F
and
\beta_{k+1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\notin F
. Thus,

s\cdot F=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}\beta_{i}\leq\gamma\\
\gamma\in F\end{subarray}}[\alpha_{i}\sigma^{\beta_{i}}(\gamma)]\,.

Now, we prove a result that is essential to fix the dictionary.

Lemma 7.41

(
[
46
, Lemma 4.6]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned LCSC. Then, for every
s\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
and any
\eta\in D_{s^{*}s}\cap\widehat{\mathcal{E}_{*}}
we have that
s\cdot\Delta_{\eta}=\Delta_{s\cdot\eta}
.

Corollary 7.42

(
[
46
, Corollary 4.7]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a countable, finite aligned LCSC. Then, given
s\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
:

(1)

s\cdot
restricts to an action on
\Lambda^{**}
,

(2)

s\cdot
restricts to an action on
\Lambda_{tight}
.

7.5.

We are ready to give a nice description of

Lemma 7.43

(
[

)
.

Let

The next result is a refined version of

Lemma 7.45

(
[
46
, Lemma 5.1]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finitely aligned LCSC, let

and
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda

\alpha\delta=\beta\gamma

Let us compare this with Spielberg’s groupoid. First, we recall the definition of Spielberg’s groupoid associated to a small category: we start by defining an equivalence relation on
\Lambda\times\Lambda\times\Lambda^{*}
by saying that
(\alpha,\beta,F)\sim(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},F^{\prime})
if there exist
G\in\Lambda^{*}
,
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Lambda
such that
F=\tau^{\gamma}\cdot G
,
F^{\prime}=\tau^{\gamma^{\prime}}\cdot G
,
\alpha\gamma=\alpha^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime}
and
\beta\gamma=\beta^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime}
. Denote
\mathcal{G}=\Lambda\times\Lambda\times\Lambda^{*}/\sim

\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{G}^{(2)}:=\{([\alpha,\beta,F],[\gamma,\delta,G])\mid\tau^{\beta}\cdot F=\tau^{\gamma}\cdot G)\}\,,

and define
[\alpha,\beta,F]^{-1}=[\beta,\gamma,F]
. Given a pair
([\alpha,\beta,F],[\gamma,\delta,G])\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}

[\alpha,\beta,F][\gamma,\delta,G]=[\alpha\xi,\delta\eta,H]\,,

where
\xi\in F
and
\eta\in G
are the elements given in Lemma
7.45
such that
\beta\xi=\gamma\eta
, and
H=\sigma^{\xi}\cdot F=\sigma^{\eta}\cdot G
. Finally, the sets
[\alpha,\beta,U]:=\{[\alpha,\beta,F]:F\in U\}
for

an open subset of
\Lambda^{*}
form a basis for the topology of
\mathcal{G}
, under which
\mathcal{G}
is an étale groupoid. By Corollary
7.42

\mathcal{G}_{|\partial\Lambda}=\{[\alpha,\beta,F]\in\mathcal{G}\mid F\in\Lambda_{tight}\}
.

Proposition 7.46

(
[
46
, Proposition 5.2]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a countable, finitely aligned LCSC. Then the map

As a final remark, Spielberg

8.
Zappa-Szép products of groups acting on LCSC categories

Bédos, Kaliszewski, Quigg, and Spielberg
[
6
]
extended the notion of self-similar action of a group on a graph to
left cancellative small categories
. But instead of giving a generalization of the original definition, they chose an approach related to a more general construction, called the Zappa-Szép product, introduced by Brin
[
7
]
; Lawson used it to describe self-similar groups via Zappa-Szép products of groups on free monoids
[
33
]
. The relevant fact is that, in this particular case, the new construction turns out to be a left-cellative small category, too. Then, they defined an algebra associated to their self-similar object by using a Cuntz-Pimsner construction.

Ortega and Pardo
[
46
]
studied the
C^{*}
-algebras associated to Zappa-Szép products of groups on left cancellative small categories, using the groupoid approach introduced in Section
7
. Subsequently, they extended the construction to Zappa-Szép products of
groupoids
acting on left cancellative small categories
[
47
]
, using ideas similar to those of
[
30
]
; we will present this construction in the next section.

In this section we analyze the notion of Zappa-Szép products of groups acting on left cancellative small categories.

If
G
is a discrete group (with unit
\mathbf{1}_{G}
), we use multiplicative notation for the group operation. We say that the group
G
acts on a small category
\Lambda
by permutations when

r(g\cdot\alpha)=g\cdot r(\alpha)\text{ and }s(g\cdot\alpha)=g\cdot s(\alpha)\text{ for every }\alpha\in\Lambda,\,g\in G\,.

For the remainder of the section, we assume that
G
acts by permutations on
\Lambda
.

A
1-cocyle
for the action of
G
on a small category
\Lambda
is a function
\varphi:G\times\Lambda\to G
that satisfies the 1-cocyle identity

\varphi(gh,\alpha)=\varphi(g,h\cdot\alpha)\varphi(h,\alpha)\text{ for all }g,h\in G,\,\alpha\in\Lambda\,.

In particular, the 1-cocycle identity says that
\varphi(\mathbf{1}_{G},\alpha)=\mathbf{1}_{G}
for every
\alpha\in\Lambda
.

Definition 8.1
.

A 1-cocycle
\varphi:G\times\Lambda\to\Lambda
on a small category
\Lambda
is a
category cocycle
if for all
g\in G
,
v\in\Lambda^{0}
, and
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
with
s(\alpha)=r(\beta)
we have:

(1)

\varphi(g,v)=g
.

(2)

\varphi(g,\alpha)\cdot r(\alpha)=g\cdot r(\alpha)
.

(3)

g\cdot(\alpha\beta)=(g\cdot\alpha)(\varphi(g,\alpha)\cdot\beta)
.

(4)

\varphi(g,\alpha\beta)=\varphi(\varphi(g,\alpha),\beta)
.

We call
(\Lambda,G,\varphi)
a
category system
.

The next definition comes from
[
6
]
:

Definition 8.2
.

Let
(\Lambda,G,\varphi)
be a category system. We denote by
\Lambda\rtimes^{\varphi}G
the small category with

\Lambda\rtimes^{\varphi}G:=\Lambda\times G\text{ and }(\Lambda\rtimes^{\varphi}G)^{0}=\Lambda\times\{\mathbf{1}_{G}\}\,,

and
r,s:\Lambda\rtimes^{\varphi}G\to(\Lambda\rtimes^{\varphi}G)^{0}
maps defined by

r(\alpha,g)=(r(\alpha),\mathbf{1}_{G})\text{ and }s(\alpha,g)=(g^{-1}\cdot s(\alpha),\mathbf{1}_{G})\,.

Moreover, for
(\alpha,g),(\beta,h)
with
s(\alpha,g)=r(\beta,h)
, the composition is defined by

(\alpha,g)(\beta,h)=(\alpha(g\cdot\beta),\varphi(g,\beta)h)\,.

We call
\Lambda\rtimes^{\varphi}G
the
Zappa-Szép product of
(\Lambda,G,\varphi)
.

It was proven that
\Lambda\rtimes^{\varphi}G
is left cancellative whenever so is
\Lambda

[
6
, Proposition 3.5]
, and as observed in
[
6
, Remark 3.9]
, the elements of the form
(v,g)
(where
v\in\Lambda^{0}
and
g\in G
) are units of
\Lambda\rtimes^{\varphi}G
. Then, given
(\alpha,g)\in\Lambda\rtimes^{\varphi}G
and
h\in G
, we have that

(\alpha,g)(g^{-1}\cdot s(\alpha),g^{-1}h)=(\alpha,h)\,,

so
(\alpha,g)\approx(\alpha,h)
.
Moreover,
\Lambda\rtimes^{\varphi}G
is finitely aligned (singly aligned) whenever
\Lambda
is finitely aligned (singly aligned)
[
6
, Proposition 3.12]
. In particular,

(\alpha,g)\vee(\beta,h)=(\alpha\vee\beta)\times\{\mathbf{1}_{G}\}\,.

Definition 8.3
.

A category system
(\Lambda,G,\varphi)
is called
pseudo free
if, whenever
g\cdot\alpha=\alpha
and
\varphi(g,\alpha)=\mathbf{1}_{G}
, then
g=\mathbf{1}_{G}
.

Proposition 8.4

(
[
21
, Proposition 5.6]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC, and let
(\Lambda,G,\varphi)
be a pseudo-free category system. Then, for all
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
, and
\alpha\in\Lambda
, one has that

g_{1}\cdot\alpha=g_{2}\cdot\alpha\text{ and }\varphi(g_{1},\alpha)=\varphi(g_{2},\alpha)\Rightarrow g_{1}=g_{2}\,.

Remark 8.5
.

Given a category system
(\Lambda,G,\varphi)
on
\Lambda
LCSC, where

To end this section, we will have a look on the case of
\Lambda=E^{*}
, where
E
is a countable graph. In order to fix the relation between
\mathcal{G}_{tight}(\mathcal{S}_{G,E})
and
\mathcal{G}_{tight}(\mathcal{S}_{{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G})
, we first need to state the relation between
\mathcal{S}_{G,E}
and
\mathcal{S}_{{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G}
. On the one hand, we have

\mathcal{S}_{G,E}=\{(\alpha,g,\beta):\alpha,\beta\in E^{*},g\in G,s(\alpha)=g\cdot s(\beta)\}.

On the other hand,

\mathcal{S}_{{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G}=\langle\tau^{(\alpha,g)}\sigma^{(\beta,h)}:\alpha,\beta\in E^{*},g,h\in G,g^{-1}\cdot s(\alpha)=h^{-1}\cdot s(\beta)\rangle.

Since
(x,g)\in({{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G})^{-1}
for all
x\in E^{0}
,
g\in G
, we have that

\tau^{(\alpha,g)}\sigma^{(\beta,h)}=\tau^{(\alpha,g)}\sigma^{(h^{-1}\cdot s(\beta),h^{-1})}\tau^{(s(\beta),h)}\sigma^{(\beta,\mathbf{1}_{G})}=\tau^{(\alpha,gh^{-1})}\sigma^{(\beta,\mathbf{1}_{G})}.

Moreover, since
E^{*}
is singly aligned, so is
{{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G}

[
6
, Proposition 3.12(ii)]
. Thus, by
[
14
, Theorem 3.2]
,

\mathcal{S}_{{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G}=\{\tau^{(\alpha,g)}\sigma^{(\beta,\mathbf{1}_{G})}:\alpha,\beta\in E^{*},g\in G,s(\alpha)=g\cdot s(\beta)\}\,.

Hence, the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\pi:&\mathcal{S}_{G,E}&\rightarrow&\mathcal{S}_{{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G}\\
&(\alpha,g,\beta)&\mapsto&\tau^{(\alpha,g)}\sigma^{(\beta,\mathbf{1}_{G})}\end{array}

is a well-defined, onto
\ast
-semigroup homomorphism. Let us characterize when
\pi
is injective. To this end, take
(\alpha,g,\beta),(\gamma,h,\delta)\in\mathcal{S}_{G,E}
such that

\tau^{(\alpha,g)}\sigma^{(\beta,\mathbf{1}_{G})}=\pi(\alpha,g,\beta)=\pi(\gamma,h,\delta)=\tau^{(\gamma,h)}\sigma^{(\delta,\mathbf{1}_{G})}.

Being both equal functions, they must have the same domain, i.e.
(\beta,\mathbf{1}_{G})({E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G)=(\delta,\mathbf{1}_{G})({E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G)
. Since
({{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G})^{-1}=E^{0}\times G
, we conclude that
\beta=\delta
. Moreover,
\tau^{(\alpha,g)}=\tau^{(\gamma,h)}
on their common domain, so that for every
\lambda\in(g^{-1}\cdot s(\alpha))E^{*}
and for every
\ell\in G
we have

(\alpha(g\cdot\lambda),\varphi(g,\lambda)\ell)=\tau^{(\alpha,g)}(\lambda,\ell)=\tau^{(\gamma,h)}(\lambda,\ell)=(\gamma(h\cdot\lambda),\varphi(h,\lambda)\ell).

Since the self-similar action of
G
on
E^{*}
preserves lengths of paths, we conclude that
\alpha=\gamma
, and that for every
\lambda\in(g^{-1}\cdot s(\alpha))E^{*}
we have
g\cdot\lambda=h\cdot\lambda
and
\varphi(g,\lambda)=\varphi(h,\lambda)
. Thus, the existence of nontrivial kernel for
\pi
is equivalent to the existence of
g\in G
,
\alpha\in E^{*}
such that for all
\lambda\in s(\alpha)E^{*}
satisfies
g\cdot\lambda=\lambda
and
\varphi(g,\lambda)=\mathbf{1}_{G}
; in other words, injectivity of
\pi
is equivalent to the fact that the self-similar action of
G
on
E^{*}
is faithful on vertex-based trees of
E
. Notice that if
(E,G,\varphi)
is pseudo-free, then the above condition is trivially fulfilled, so that
\pi
will be an isomorphism in this case. Moreover, being
{{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G}
singly aligned, we see that it is right cancellative exactly when
(E,G,\varphi)
is pseudo free. In this case, not only
\mathcal{S}_{G,E}\cong\mathcal{S}_{{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G}
, but also they are weak semilattices, so their associated tight groupoids are Hausdorff.

Now, we look at the relation between the corresponding tight groupoids
\mathcal{G}_{tight}(\mathcal{S}_{G,E})
and
\mathcal{G}_{tight}(\mathcal{S}_{{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G})
. First, notice that the idempotent semilattices of
\mathcal{S}_{E}
,
\mathcal{S}_{G,E}
and
\mathcal{S}_{{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G}
coincide, so that the spaces of filters, ultrafilters and tight filters are the same (up to natural isomorphism). Furthermore, the partial actions
\mathcal{S}_{G,E}\curvearrowright\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
and
\mathcal{S}_{{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G}\curvearrowright\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
are
\pi
-equivariant. Moreover, the germ relationship is compatible with
\pi
. Thus,
\pi
induces a continuous, open, onto groupoid homomorphism

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\Phi:&\mathcal{G}_{tight}(\mathcal{S}_{G,E})&\rightarrow&\mathcal{G}_{tight}(\mathcal{S}_{{E^{*}}\rtimes^{\varphi}G})\\
&[\alpha,g,\beta;\eta]&\mapsto&[\tau^{(\alpha,g)}\sigma^{(\beta,\mathbf{1}_{G})};\eta].\end{array}

Using the Morita equivalence reduction shown in Theorem
5.7
(
[
22
, Theorem 3.2]
), we can assume that
E
is row-finite with no sources or sinks, whence
\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\infty}=\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{tight}}=E^{\infty}
; let us reduce to this case, to simplify the computations. We now show that
\Phi
is injective. To do this, let
[\alpha,g,\beta;\eta]\in\ker\Phi
. This means that
\tau^{(\alpha,g)}\sigma^{(\beta,\mathbf{1}_{G})}
is an idempotent. According to the calculations done before, this happens exactly when
\alpha=\beta
and for every
\lambda\in s(\alpha)E^{*}
we have that
g\cdot\lambda=\lambda
and
\varphi(g,\lambda)=\mathbf{1}_{G}
. Pick
\lambda
any initial segment in
\eta\in E^{\infty}
. Notice that
\lambda\in s(\alpha)E^{*}
, and thus
(\alpha\lambda,\mathbf{1}_{G},\alpha\lambda)\in\alpha\eta
(seen as a filter), while

(\alpha,g,\alpha)\cdot(\alpha\lambda,\mathbf{1}_{G},\alpha\lambda)=(\alpha(g\lambda),\varphi(g,\lambda),\alpha\lambda)=(\alpha\lambda,\mathbf{1}_{G},\alpha\lambda).

Hence, by the germ relation, if
\eta=\lambda\hat{\eta}
, then

[\alpha,g,\alpha;\alpha\eta)]=[\alpha\lambda,\mathbf{1}_{G},\alpha\lambda;\alpha\lambda\hat{\eta})]\in\mathcal{G}_{tight}(\mathcal{S}_{G,E})^{(0)}.

Thus,
\Phi
is a homeomorphism and an isomorphism of groupoids. This guarantees that, independent of the choice for representing the self-similar graph system
(G,E,\varphi)
, their associated tight groupoids -and hence their algebras- are the same.

9.
Zappa-Szép products of groupoids acting on LCSC categories

An obvious generalization of the previous construction, as well as that of
[
30
]
, is to consider Zappa-Szép products of
groupoids
acting on left cancellative small categories. This require to state the right definition of what a groupoid action means.

By technical reasons (associated with some applications of the results in this section), it is convenient to introduce a notion of
length function
defined on a left cancellative small category.

Definition 9.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC and let
\Gamma\subseteq Q
be a submonoid of a group
Q
with unit element
\mathbf{1}_{Q}
, and such that
\Gamma\cap\Gamma^{-1}=\{\mathbf{1}_{Q}\}
. A map
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
is called a
length function
if:

(1)

\mathbf{d}(\alpha\beta)=\mathbf{d}(\alpha)\mathbf{d}(\beta)
for every
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
with
s(\alpha)=r(\beta)
.

A length function is said to satisfy the
weak factorization property

(WFP)
if for every
\alpha\in\Lambda
and
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma
with
\mathbf{d}(\alpha)=\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}
, there are
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\Lambda
with
s(\alpha_{1})=r(\alpha_{2})
,
\mathbf{d}(\alpha_{i})=\gamma_{i}
for
i=1,2
, such that
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
, and moreover for every
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\Lambda
with
s(\beta_{1})=r(\beta_{2})
,
\mathbf{d}(\beta_{i})=\gamma_{i}
for
i=1,2
, such that
\alpha=\beta_{1}\beta_{2}
, there exist
g_{1},g_{2}\in\Lambda^{-1}
such that
\beta_{1}=\alpha_{1}g_{1}
and
\beta_{2}=g_{2}\alpha_{2}
.

Observe that, given a LCSC
\Lambda
, there always exists what we call the
trivial length function

\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, defined by
\mathbf{d}(\alpha)=\mathbf{1}_{Q}
for every
\alpha\in\Lambda
.

The above definition is motivated by the one given in
[
34
, Section 3]
, where the authors only consider the case when
\Gamma={\mathbb{N}}^{k}
.

We recall that a length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
satisfies
unique factorization property

(UFP)
if, for all
\alpha\in\Lambda
and
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\Gamma
with
\mathbf{d}(\alpha)=\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}
, there are unique
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\Lambda
with
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
and
\mathbf{d}(\alpha_{i})=\gamma_{i}
for
i=1,2
(see e.g.
[
29
]
).

Remark 9.2
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC and let
\Gamma\subseteq Q
be a submonoid of a group
Q
with unit element
\mathbf{1}_{Q}
such that
\Gamma\cap\Gamma^{-1}=\{\mathbf{1}_{Q}\}
. Then, given a length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
satisfying the WFP, we have:

(1)

\mathbf{d}^{-1}(\mathbf{1}_{Q})=\Lambda^{-1}
.

(2)

\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
satisfies the UFP if and only if it satisfies the WFP and
\Lambda
has no inverses.

Definition 9.3
.

A LCSC
\Lambda
is called
action-free
if the action of
\Lambda^{-1}
on
\Lambda
is free: whenever
g\gamma=\gamma
for some
\gamma\in\Lambda
and
g\in\Lambda^{-1}
, then
g=r(\gamma)
.

Observe that if
\Lambda
has no inverses, then
\Lambda
is action-free.

Lemma 9.4

(
[
47
, Lemma 2.10]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC and let
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
be a length function satisfying the WFP. Then,
\Lambda
is right cancellative if and only if
\Lambda
is action-free.

Now, we define the action of a groupoid on a LCSC, essentially following the same schema as in
[
30
]
. The first step is to define the notion of partial isomorphism of a small category
\Lambda
, inspired by
[
30
, Definition 3.1]
.

Definition 9.5
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
. Let
\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
be the set of partial isomorphisms of
\Lambda
,
f\in\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
such that satisfies:

(1)

f:v\Lambda\rightarrow w\Lambda
is a bijection for some
v,w\in\Lambda^{0}
,

(2)

f(\alpha\Lambda)=f(\alpha)\Lambda
for every
\alpha\in v\Lambda
,

(3)

f(v)=w
, and

(4)

\mathbf{d}(f(\alpha))=\mathbf{d}(\alpha)
for every
\alpha\in v\Lambda
.

We denote by
v=:d(f)
(the
domain of
f
) and by
w=:c(f)
(the
codomain of
f
).

Remarks 9.6
.

(1)

Observe that condition
(3)
does not follow from the previous conditions.
Indeed, given
g\in\Lambda^{-1}
, the map
f:s(g)\Lambda\to r(g)\Lambda
given by
f(\gamma)=g\gamma
for every
\gamma\in s(g)\Lambda
satisfies conditions
(1)
-
(2)
but not
(3)
, whenever
g\in\Lambda^{-1}\setminus\Lambda^{0}
.

(2)

Given
f:v\Lambda\to w\Lambda
in
\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
and
g\in v\Lambda^{-1}
, we have that
f(gg^{-1})=w
by condition
(3)
, and hence by condition
(2)
there exists
h\in\Lambda
such that
f(g)h=w
, so
f(g)\in\Lambda^{-1}
.

Lemma 9.7

(
[
47
, Lemma 3.3]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
. Then, given
f\in\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
and
\alpha\in d(f)\Lambda
, there exists a unique function
f|_{\alpha}:s(\alpha)\Lambda\to s(f(\alpha))\Lambda
in
\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
such that
f(\alpha\beta)=f(\alpha)f|_{\alpha}(\beta)
for every
\beta\in s(\alpha)\Lambda
.

Lemma 9.8

(
[
47
, Lemma 3.4]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
. Then, given
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
with
s(\alpha)=r(\beta)
and
f\in\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
with
r(\alpha)=d(f)
, we have that:

(1)

f|_{d(f)}=f
.

(2)

s(f(\alpha))=f|_{\alpha}(s(\alpha))=c(f|_{\alpha})
.

(3)

\operatorname{id}_{s(\alpha)}=(\operatorname{id}_{r(\alpha)})|_{\alpha}
.

(4)

f|_{\alpha\beta}=(f|_{\alpha})|_{\beta}
.

The next step is to show that
\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
has a natural groupoid structure.

Lemma 9.9

(
[
47
, Lemma 3.5]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
. Then, given
f,g\in\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
such that
d(f)=c(g)
, the composition
f\circ g\in\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
.

For each
v\in\Lambda^{0}
, we define
\operatorname{id}_{v}:v\Lambda\rightarrow v\Lambda
as the identity map on
v\Lambda
, so that
\operatorname{id}_{v}\in\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
.

Lemma 9.10

(
[
47
, Lemma 3.6]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, and let
f\in\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
. Then
f^{-1}\in\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
.

Now, using Lemmas
9.9
and
9.10
, we can define a groupoid structure in
\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
:

Definition 9.11
.

If
\Lambda
is a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, then
\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
is a discrete groupoid where, given
f,g\in\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
, the product
fg
is defined as the composition
f\circ g
whenever
d(f)=c(g)
, and
f^{-1}
is the set-theoretical inverse of
f
. Moreover, we can identify the unit space of
(\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d}))^{(0)}=\{\operatorname{id}_{v}:v\in\Lambda^{0}\}
with
\Lambda^{0}
.

With this in mind, we can define the notion of action of a groupoid on a LCSC that we need:

Definition 9.12
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, and let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete groupoid. An action of
\mathcal{G}
on
\Lambda
is a groupoid homomorphism
\phi:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
. Given
g\in\mathcal{G}
and
\alpha\in d(\phi(g))\Lambda
, we write the action of
g
on
\alpha
by
g\cdot\alpha:=\phi(g)(\alpha)
.

The identification
(\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d}))^{(0)}=\Lambda^{0}
implies that
\phi(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\subseteq\Lambda^{0}
, and
\phi(s(g))=d(\phi(g))
and
\phi(r(g))=c(\phi(g))
for any
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Thus,

\phi(g):\phi(s(g))\Lambda\rightarrow\phi(r(g))\Lambda\,.

Remarks 9.13
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, and let
\phi:\mathcal{G}\to\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
be a groupoid action on
\Lambda
. Then:

(1)

Suppose that
\Delta^{0}:=\phi(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})\subsetneqq\Lambda^{0}
. Let us define

\Delta:=\{\alpha\in\Lambda\mid s(\alpha),r(\alpha)\in\Delta^{0}\}\,.

Then
\Delta
is a LCSC, and
\phi(\mathcal{G})\subseteq\text{PIso}(\Delta,\mathbf{d})
.
However,
\Delta
is not necessarily finitely aligned if so is
\Lambda
. Thus, such a restriction will affect the arguments. Hence, we always assume that, given an action of
\mathcal{G}
on
\Lambda
, either
\phi(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})=\Lambda^{0}
, or that the restricted subcategory
\Delta
inherits the finitely aligned property. In practice, this means that, after restricting if necessary, we assume that
\phi(\mathcal{G}^{(0)})=\Lambda^{0}
.

(2)

Suppose that
\phi_{|\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
is not an injective map. Then it can happen that there exists
(g,h)\notin\mathcal{G}^{(2)}
(so that
s(g)\neq r(h)
), but
(\phi(g),\phi(h))\in(\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d}))^{(2)}
, (and thus
\phi(s(g))=d(\phi(g))=c(\phi(h))=\phi(r(h))
). Hence,
\phi(g)\phi(h)\in\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})\setminus\phi(\mathcal{G})
. This will affect defining composability of elements in the corresponding Zappa-Szép product. Since our model should include non-faithful self-similar actions of a groupoid
\mathcal{G}
on
\Lambda
, we cannot skip that case. Then, for a non-injective action, we need to include the condition that for every
g,h\in\mathcal{G}
, then
s(g)=r(h)
if and only if
d(\phi(g))=\phi(s(g))=\phi(r(h))=c(\phi(h))
, or equivalently, that
\phi_{|\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
is injective.

According to Remarks
9.13
, we assume that an action of a discrete groupoid
\mathcal{G}
on a small category
\Lambda
through a (not necessarily injective) groupoid homomorphism
\phi:\mathcal{G}\rightarrow\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
, satisfies that
\phi_{|\mathcal{G}^{(0)}}
is a bijection. Therefore, we identify
\mathcal{G}^{(0)}=\Lambda^{(0)}=\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})^{(0)}
, omitting
\phi
.

Now, we are ready to define the Zappa-Szép product of a left cancellative small category. This is inspired by the construction of the self-similar graph of a groupoid on a finite graph
[
30
, Section 3]
and the Zappa-Szép product of a group acting on a left cancellative small category
[
6
,
46
]
. This has also been done recently in
[
34
]
, where they construct Zappa-Szép products of groupoids acting on higher-rank graphs.

First, we state the abstract notion of self-similar action, in order to boil up the exact definition of a 1-cocycle that we need.

Definition 9.14
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete groupoid acting on a LCSC
\Lambda
with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
. We say that the action is
self-similar
if, for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
and for every
\alpha\in s(g)\Lambda
, there exists
h\in\mathcal{G}
such that

g\cdot(\alpha\mu)=(g\cdot\alpha)(h\cdot\mu)

for every
\mu\in s(\alpha)\Lambda
.

Clearly, in the above definition
h
depends on
g
and
\alpha
. A natural question is to decide whether such a
h
is unique. Suppose that
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
satisfies
g\cdot(\alpha\mu)=(g\cdot\alpha)(h_{i}\cdot\mu)
(
i=1,2)
for every
\mu\in s(\alpha)\Lambda
. Then

(g\cdot\alpha)(h_{1}\cdot\mu)=(g\cdot\alpha)(h_{2}\cdot\mu).

Since
\Lambda
is left cancellative, then

(h_{1}\cdot\mu)=(h_{2}\cdot\mu)

for every
\mu\in s(\alpha)\Lambda
.
Hence, we can only guarantee that
\phi(h_{1})=\phi(h_{2})
and
h_{1}=h_{2}
hold only when
\phi
is injective.

We use this fact to define a suitable notion of a cocycle for such an action. We essentially follow
[
6
, Section 4]
, taking care of the fact that the action is partial.

Definition 9.15
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, and let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete groupoid acting on
\Lambda
. Consider the set

\mathcal{G}{}_{s}\times_{r}\Lambda:=\{(g,\alpha)\in\mathcal{G}\times\Lambda\mid s(g)=r(\alpha)\}\,.

A
(partial) cocycle of the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
\Lambda
is a function
\varphi:\mathcal{G}{}_{s}\times_{r}\Lambda\rightarrow\mathcal{G}
satisfying the cocycle identity

\varphi(gh,\alpha)=\varphi(g,h\cdot\alpha)\varphi(h,\alpha)

for any
(g,h)\in\mathcal{G}^{(2)}

and any
\alpha\in\Lambda
such that
s(h)=r(\alpha)
. Observe, in particular, that
\varphi(r(\alpha),\alpha)\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}
for every
\alpha\in\Lambda
.

Now, we state the properties that a cocycle must have (in a similar list to that of
[
46
, Section 7]
). But instead of imposing them, we will try to deduce from the definition, as in
[
30
, Section 3]
.

The first step is to fix the requirement to guarantee that the action of
\mathcal{G}
is compatible with the composition in
\Lambda
. We introduce minimal requirements to guarantee this fact when constructing our “self-similar” actions, and also to ensure that we can associate a suitable small category to the action and the cocycle.

Definition 9.16
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, and let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete groupoid acting on
\Lambda
. A (partial) cocycle
\varphi
for the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
\Lambda
is said to be a
category cocycle
if for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
\alpha\in\Lambda
with
s(g)=r(\alpha)
and every
\beta\in s(\alpha)\Lambda
we have that:

(CC1)

\varphi(g,d(\phi(g)))=g
.

(CC2)

\varphi(g,\alpha\beta)=\varphi(\varphi(g,\alpha),\beta)
.

(CC3)

g\cdot(\alpha\beta)=(g\cdot\alpha)(\varphi(g,\alpha)\cdot\beta)
.

If we have an action of
\mathcal{G}
on
\Lambda
, and
\varphi
is a category cocycle for this action, we say that
(\Lambda,\mathbf{d},\mathcal{G},\varphi)
is a
category system
.

Remark 9.17
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete groupoid acting on
\Lambda
, and let
(\Lambda,\mathbf{d},\mathcal{G},\varphi)
be a category system. Then, by condition
(CC3)
in Definition
9.16
,
the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
\Lambda
is self-similar. Moreover, given
g\in\mathcal{G}
and
\alpha\in s(g)\Lambda
, we have that
\phi(\varphi(g,\alpha))=\phi(g)|_{\alpha}
.

Lemma 9.18

(
[
47
, Lemma 4.5]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, and let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete groupoid acting on
\Lambda
. Let
\varphi
be a category cocycle for the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
\Lambda
. Then for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
\alpha\in\Lambda
with
s(g)=r(\alpha)
we have that:

(CC4)

s(g\cdot\alpha)=\varphi(g,\alpha)\cdot s(\alpha)=r(\varphi(g,\alpha))
.

(CC5)

s(\alpha)=\varphi(r(\alpha),\alpha)
.

Remark 9.19
.

Similarly,
(CC1)
and
(CC2)
will follow from
(CC3)
, modulo the action homomorphism
\phi
. In particular,
when the action homomorphism is injective
,
(CC1)
and
(CC2)
are direct consequences of
(CC3)
.

Example 9.20
.

If
\Lambda
is a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, then
\varphi:\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d}){}_{d}\times_{r}\Lambda\to\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d})
defined by
\varphi(f,\alpha)=f|_{\alpha}
is a category cocyle (Lemmas
9.7
&
9.8
), and thus
(\Lambda,\mathbf{d},\text{PIso}(\Lambda,\mathbf{d}),\varphi)
is a category system.

The property of being a category cocycle is the correct version of
[
21
, (2.3)]
. Let us determine which are the basic properties satisfied by a category cocycle. These properties, jointly with Definition
9.16
, are analogous to those proved in
[
30
, Lemma 3.4 & Proposition 3.6]
.

Proposition 9.21

(
[
47
, Proposition 4.8]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete groupoid acting on
\Lambda
, and let
(\Lambda,\mathbf{d},\mathcal{G},\varphi)
be a category system. Then, for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
, every
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
with
s(g)=r(\alpha)
,
s(\alpha)=r(\beta)
, we have that:

(1)

r(g\cdot\alpha)=g\cdot r(\alpha)
.

(2)

s(\varphi(g,\alpha))=s(\alpha)
.

(3)

\varphi(g,\alpha)^{-1}=\varphi(g^{-1},g\cdot\alpha)
.

Now, we fix the definition of Zappa-Szép product for a category system
(\Lambda,\mathcal{G},\varphi)
, similar to that in
[
6
, Section 4]
.

Definition 9.22
.

Let
\Lambda
be an LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete groupoid acting on
\Lambda
, and let
(\Lambda,\mathbf{d},\mathcal{G},\varphi)
be a category system. We denote by
\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}
the set

\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}:=\Lambda{}_{s}\times_{r}\mathcal{G}=\{(\alpha,g)\in\Lambda\times\mathcal{G}\mid s(\alpha)=r(g)\}\,,

with distinguished elements

(\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G})^{(0)}:=\Lambda^{0}\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\,.

We equip this pair of sets with range and source maps
r,s:\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}G\rightarrow(\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}G)^{(0)}
defined by

r(\alpha,g):=(r(\alpha),r(\alpha))\text{ and }s(\alpha,g):=(s(g),s(g))=(g^{-1}\cdot s(\alpha),s(g)),

for every
(\alpha,g)\in\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}
. Observe that the latter equality holds because
g\cdot s(g)=r(g)=s(\alpha)
, whence
g^{-1}\cdot s(\alpha)=g^{-1}\cdot r(g)=s(g)
.

Moreover, given
(\alpha,g),(\beta,h)\in\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}
with
s(\alpha,g)=r(\beta,h)
, we define the composition of two elements as follows:

(\alpha,g)(\beta,h):=(\alpha(g\cdot\beta),\varphi(g,\beta)h).

We now adapt the arguments of
[
6
, Proposition 4.6]
and
[
6
, Proposition 4.13]
to prove the next results.

Proposition 9.23

(
[
47
, Proposition 4.10]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete groupoid acting on
\Lambda
, and let
(\Lambda,\mathbf{d},\mathcal{G},\varphi)
be a category system. If given
(\alpha,g),(\beta,h)\in\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}
with
s(\alpha,g)=r(\beta,h)
, we define the composition of these two elements by

(\alpha,g)(\beta,h):=(\alpha(g\cdot\beta),\varphi(g,\beta)h)\,,

then
\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}
is a small category.

Lemma 9.24

(
[
47
, Lemma 4.11]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete groupoid acting on
\Lambda
, and let
(\Lambda,\mathbf{d},\mathcal{G},\varphi)
be a category system. Then,
(\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G})^{-1}=\Lambda^{-1}{}_{s}\times_{r}\mathcal{G}
.

Proposition 9.25

(
[
47
, Proposition 4.12]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete groupoid acting on
\Lambda
, and let
(\Lambda,\mathbf{d},\mathcal{G},\varphi)
be a category system. Then:

(1)

\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}
is left cancellative.

(2)

If

Remark 9.31
.

Let
\Lambda
be a LCSC with length function
\mathbf{d}:\Lambda\to\Gamma
, let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete groupoid acting on
\Lambda
, let
(\Lambda,\mathbf{d},\mathcal{G},\varphi)
be a category system, and let
\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}
be the associated Zappa- Szép product.

(1)

Let
(\alpha,g),(\beta,f)\in\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}
, and suppose that
(\alpha,g)\leq(\beta,f)
. Then, there exist
(\delta,h)\in\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}
with
r(\delta,h)=s(\alpha,g)
, that is,
r(\delta)=g^{-1}\cdot s(\alpha)
such that

(\beta,f)=(\alpha,g)(\delta,h)=(\alpha(g\cdot\delta),\varphi(g,\delta)h)\,.

Hence,
\alpha(g\cdot\delta)=\beta
and
f=\varphi(g,\delta)h
. Thus,
g\cdot\delta=\sigma^{\alpha}(\beta)
by left cancellation, and so
\delta=g^{-1}\cdot\sigma^{\alpha}(\beta)
and
h=\varphi(g,g^{-1}\cdot\sigma^{\alpha}(\beta))^{-1}f=\varphi(g^{-1},\sigma^{\alpha}(\beta))f
because of the cocycle identity. Therefore,

(\alpha,g)\leq(\beta,f)\text{ if and only if }\alpha\leq\beta\,,

and then we have that

\sigma^{(\alpha,g)}(\beta,f)=(g^{-1}\cdot\sigma^{\alpha}(\beta),\varphi(g^{-1},\sigma^{\alpha}(\beta))f)\,.

The above observation, together with Proposition
9.25
(2)(a), shows that the map

is a bijection between

and

(\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G})_{tight}

\Lambda_{tight}
.

(2)

Let
(\alpha,g),(\beta,f)\in\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}
with
s(\alpha,g)=s(\beta,f)
, and let
\tau^{(\alpha,g)}\sigma^{(\beta,f)}\in\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}}
. Observe that, since
(\beta,f)(\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G})=(\beta,s(f))(\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G})
, we have that

\tau^{(\alpha,g)}\sigma^{(\beta,f)}=\tau^{(\alpha,gf^{-1})}\sigma^{(\beta,s(f))}.

(3)

Given
F\in\Lambda_{tight}
and
(\alpha,g),(\beta,f)\in\Lambda\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}
with
s(\alpha,g)=s(\beta,f)
, we have that

\tau^{(\alpha,g)}\sigma^{(\beta,f)}\cdot(F\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G})=F^{\prime}\Join^{\varphi}\mathcal{G}\,,

where
F^{\prime}:=\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\leq\gamma\\
\gamma\in F\end{subarray}}[\alpha((gf^{-1})\cdot\sigma^{\beta}(\gamma))]\in\Lambda_{tight}
.

10.
Homology and K-theory for self-similar graph algebras

Homology and K-theory play a central role in understanding the structure of
C^{*}
-algebras, as well as in their classification. In the particular case of groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras, the groupoid homology of an ample groupoid
\mathcal{G}
shares many similarities with the K-theory of its reduced
C^{*}
-algebra, which is closely related to the K-theory when the isotropy groups are torsion-free
[
51
,
39
]
. Matui had conjectured
[
37
]
a connection
K_{i}(C^{*}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{G}))\cong\bigoplus_{q\geq 0}H_{2q+i}(\mathcal{G})
for
i=0,1
, known as the HK property. However, torsion in the isotropy becomes an obstruction
[
54
]
for the HK property, even for principal groupoids
[
13
]
.

When specializing to self-similar graph
C^{*}
-algebras, the homology of the groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{(G,X)}
associated to a self-similar group action
(G,X,\sigma)
, and the K-theory of its Nekrashevych algebra
\mathcal{O}_{(G,X)}
, provide fundamental invariants for the self-similar group. The K-theory is particularly relevant in light of the Kirchberg–Phillips Theorem
[
28
,
49
]
, as Nekrashevych algebras are often purely infinite and simple. Nekrashevych computed the K-theory of Nekrashevych algebras in the case of certain iterated monodromy groups
[
42
]
. He used a two-step approach: first, he related the K-theory of
\mathcal{O}_{(G,X)}
with that of its gauge-invariant subalgebra, and then described it as an inductive limit of matrix amplifications of
C^{*}(G)
. A similar approach, at the groupoid level, was used by Ortega and Sánchez
[
48
]
to study the homology of the groupoid associated to a certain self-similar action of the infinite dihedral group. Deaconu also outlined a general strategy along these lines for both K-theory and homology
[
12
]
.

Miller and Steinberg compute homology and K-theory in much greater generality, by relating the groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{(G,X)}
and the
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{O}_{(G,X)}
of a self-similar group action
(G,X,\sigma)
directly to
G
and
C^{*}(G)
, respectively. This provides a way to compute the homology and K-theory in entirely group-theoretic terms. Their strategy is to use that self-similar groupoid actions are exactly self-correspondences of discrete groupoids
[
2
]
, and to take advantage of their induced mappings in homology
[
38
]
and K-theory. This allows them to extend the results from self-similar groups to self-similar graphs, and even to self-similar groupoid actions on graphs, without extra effort. Once this is done, they show that Matui’s computation of the homology of graph groupoids
[
36
]
(see also
[
44
]
) and Nyland and Ortega’s computation of the homology of Exel–Pardo–Katsura groupoids
[
45
]
) follow directly from their results.

Let us have a quick look at their results. First, let us recall the definitions for étale correspondences and their composition
[
2
]
, which are basic for understanding this work.

Definition 10.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{H}
be ample groupoids. An étale correspondence
\Omega\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
is a
\mathcal{G}
-
\mathcal{H}
-bispace
\Omega
such that the right action
\Omega\curvearrowleft\mathcal{H}
is free, proper and étale. We write
\textbf{r}\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{G}^{0}
and
\textbf{s}\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{H}^{0}
for the anchor maps. That
\Omega\curvearrowleft\mathcal{H}
is étale means that
\textbf{s}\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{H}^{0}
is étale, while free and proper together mean that the map
\textbf{r}\times\textbf{s}\colon\Omega\rtimes\mathcal{H}\to\Omega\times\Omega
is a closed embedding. We say
\Omega
is proper if the induced map
\Omega/\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{G}^{0}
is proper.

For an ample groupoid
\mathcal{H}
and a free, proper, étale right
\mathcal{H}
-space
\Omega
and
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\Omega\times_{\Omega/\mathcal{H}}\Omega
, we write
\langle\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\rangle
for the unique
h\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying
\omega_{2}=\omega_{1}h
. The map

\langle-,-\rangle\colon\Omega\times_{\Omega/\mathcal{H}}\Omega\to\mathcal{H}

is continuous
[
2
, Lemma 3.4]
.

One formulation of the notion of self-similar groups is via a proper correspondence from a group to itself (see Nekrashevych
[
43
]
where the term “covering bimodule” is used).

Definition 10.2
.

A self-similar groupoid action
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{X})
consists of a discrete groupoid
\mathcal{G}
and an étale correspondence
\mathcal{X}\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}
with anchor maps
\textbf{r},\textbf{s}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{G}^{0}
. We may refer to
\mathcal{X}
as a self-similarity of
\mathcal{G}
. The self-similar groupoid action is called row-finite if
\mathcal{X}
is proper. An object/vertex
v\in\mathcal{G}^{0}
is called regular if
0<|\textbf{r}^{-1}(v)/\mathcal{G}|<\infty
; otherwise
v
is called singular. One says that a singular vertex
v
is a source if
\textbf{r}^{-1}(v)=\emptyset
, and an infinite receiver otherwise.

Regular objects form an invariant subset
\mathcal{G}^{0}_{\mathrm{reg}}
of the unit space and thus determine an invariant subgroupoid
{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{reg}}}=\mathcal{G}|_{\mathcal{G}^{0}_{\mathrm{reg}}}
. To be consistent with
[
21
]
, we say that
\mathcal{X}
is pseudo-free if the left action of
\mathcal{G}
is free.

There is a reformulation of this notion in terms of actions on graphs that can be found in
[
2
, Example 4.4]
.

Definition 10.3
.

A self-similar groupoid action
(\mathcal{G},E,\sigma)
is a discrete groupoid
\mathcal{G}
whose unit space is the vertex set
E^{0}
of a directed graph
\textbf{r},\textbf{s}\colon E\to E^{0}
, with a left action
\mathcal{G}\curvearrowright E
(not necessarily an action by graph partial automorphisms, see
[
2
]
) with anchor
\textbf{r}\colon E\to E^{0}
, written
(g,e)\mapsto g(e)
, and a
1
-cocycle
\sigma\colon\mathcal{G}\times_{E^{0}}E\to\mathcal{G}
, written
(g,e)\mapsto g|_{e}
, such that
\textbf{r}(g|_{e})=\textbf{s}(g(e))
,
\textbf{s}(g|_{e})=\textbf{s}(e)
and
(hg)|_{e}=h|_{g(e)}g|_{e}
(equivalently,
\sigma\colon\mathcal{G}\ltimes E\to\mathcal{G}
is a groupoid homomorphism with unit space map
\sigma|_{E}=\textbf{s}\colon E\to E^{0}
). The element
g|_{e}
is called the section of
g
at
e
.

The translation between the two definitions is as follows:

(1)

If
(\mathcal{G},E,\sigma)
is a self-similar groupoid action in the sense of Definition
10.3
, then the associated correspondence is
\mathcal{X}_{(\mathcal{G},E)}=E\times_{E^{0}}\mathcal{G}
with
\textbf{r}(e,g)=\textbf{r}(e)
,
\textbf{s}(e,g)=\textbf{s}(g)
, right action
(e,h)g=(e,hg)
and left action
g(e,h)=(g(e),g|_{e}h)
. Moreover,
E
is a set of representatives for
\mathcal{X}_{(\mathcal{G},E)}/\mathcal{G}
.

(2)

If
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{X})
is a self-similar groupoid action in the sense of Definition
10.2
, then we can choose a transversal
E\subseteq\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{X}/\mathcal{G}
.
By freeness of the right action, each
x\in\mathcal{X}
can be uniquely written in the form
eg
with
e\in E
,
g\in G
. Putting
E^{0}=\mathcal{G}^{0}
, we can define
\textbf{r},\textbf{s}\colon E\to E^{0}
by restriction. If
g\in\mathcal{G}
and
\textbf{s}(g)=\textbf{r}(e)
, then
ge=g(e)g|_{e}
for a unique
g(e)\in E
and
g|_{e}\in\mathcal{G}
. This defines a left action of
\mathcal{G}
with anchor
r
isomorphic to the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{X}/\mathcal{G}
, and
\sigma(g,e)=g|_{e}
defines a
1
-cocycle with
\textbf{r}(g|_{e})=\textbf{s}(g(e))
,
\textbf{s}(g|_{e})=\textbf{s}(e)
and
(hg)|_{e}=h|_{g(e)}g|_{e}
. Thus,
(\mathcal{G},E,\sigma)
is a self-similar groupoid action in the sense of definition
10.3
and
\mathcal{X}_{(\mathcal{G},E)}\to\mathcal{X}
given by
(e,g)\mapsto eg
is an isomorphism of correspondences. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{X}_{(\mathcal{G},E)}
is proper if and only if
E
is row-finite. Moreover, a vertex
v\in E^{0}
is regular if and only if it is not a source and not an infinite receiver.

By a
self-similar group action
we shall always mean in these notes
(G,\mathcal{X})
with
\mathcal{X}
a nontrivial proper correspondence over the group
G
, equivalently
(G,E,\sigma)
with finite alphabet
E
of size
|E|\geq 2
(although the case of nonproper correspondences was considered in
[
60
]
).

Now, we define the corresponding
C^{*}
-algebra. Given an étale correspondence
\mathcal{X}\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{H}
of a discrete groupoids, we can extend the map
\langle-,-\rangle\colon\mathcal{X}\times_{\mathcal{X}/\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}
to a map
\langle-,-\rangle\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}\cup\{0\}
by

\langle x,y\rangle=\begin{cases}h,&\text{if}\ xh=y,\\
0,&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Trivially,
\langle x,yh\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle h
,
\langle y,x\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle^{-1}
,
\langle x,x\rangle=\textbf{s}(x)
and
\langle gx,y\rangle=\langle x,g^{-1}y\rangle
.
For example, if
(\mathcal{G},E,\sigma)
is a self-similar groupoid action in the sense of Definition
10.3
, then for
\mathcal{X}_{(\mathcal{G},E)}
, one has
\langle(e,g),(f,h)\rangle=g^{-1}h\delta_{e,f}
.

We write
M_{\mathcal{X}}\colon C^{*}(\mathcal{G})\to C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
for the
C^{*}
-correspondence of a groupoid self-similarity
\mathcal{X}\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}
. The space
M_{\mathcal{X}}
is densely spanned by elements
m_{x}
for
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and
C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
is densely spanned by the partial isometries
u_{g}\in C^{*}(\mathcal{G})
for

with
u_{0}=0
, and thus the
C^{*}
-correspondence
M_{\mathcal{X}}
is determined by the relations:

(1)

u_{g}\cdot m_{x}=m_{gx}\delta_{\textbf{s}(g),\textbf{r}(x)}
,

(2)

m_{x}\cdot u_{g}=m_{xg}\delta_{\textbf{s}(x),\textbf{r}(g)}
, and

(3)

\langle m_{x},m_{y}\rangle=u_{\langle x,y\rangle}

for
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
. The regular objects determine an ideal

in

. Moreover, this ideal acts by compact operators on

because for each

the projection

acts as the compact operator

Recall the definition of
groupoid homology
. If
X
is a space with a basis of compact (Hausdorff) open sets and
A
is an abelian group (written additively), then
AX
denotes the abelian group of mappings
X\to A
spanned by elements of the form
a1_{U}
where
a\in A
and
1_{U}
is the characteristic function of a compact open set
U\subseteq X
. If
X
is Hausdorff, these are precisely the compactly supported locally constant mappings
X\to A
.

The construction
X\mapsto AX
is functorial with respect to étale maps and contravariantly functorial with respect to proper maps. If
f\colon X\to Y
is étale, then
f_{\ast}\colon AX\to AY
is given by
f_{*}(g)(y)=\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(y)}g(x)
. If
p\colon X\to Y
is a proper map, then
p^{*}\colon AY\to AX
is given by
p^{*}(f)=f\circ p
. Notice that an open inclusion
i
is étale with
i_{*}
extension by
0
, and a closed inclusion
i
is proper with
i^{*}
restriction of functions.

We recall now the definition of the homology of an ample groupoid
\mathcal{G}
with coefficients in an abelian group
A
via the formulation of Matui
[
36
]
. There are étale maps
d_{i}\colon\mathcal{G}^{n}\to\mathcal{G}^{n-1}
for
n\geq 2
and
i=0,\ldots,n
given by

(32)

d_{i}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})=\begin{cases}(g_{2},\ldots,g_{n}),&\text{if}\ i=0,\\
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{i}g_{i+1},\ldots,g_{n}),&\text{if}\ 1\leq i\leq n-1,\\
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n-1}),&\text{if}\ i=n.\end{cases}

We define
d_{0},d_{1}\colon\mathcal{G}^{1}\to\mathcal{G}^{0}
by
d_{0}(g)=\textbf{s}(g)
and
d_{1}(g)=\textbf{r}(g)
, which are again étale.
It is well known that these maps satisfy the semisimplicial identities,
and so we can define a chain complex
C_{\bullet}(\mathcal{G},A)
with
C_{n}(\mathcal{G},A)=A\mathcal{G}^{n}
for
n\geq 0
, and
\partial_{n}\colon C_{n}(\mathcal{G},A)\to C_{n-1}(\mathcal{G},A)
given by
\partial_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}(d_{i})_{*}
for
n\geq 1
. As usual, we take
\partial_{0}=0
. The homology of this chain complex is denoted
H_{\bullet}(\mathcal{G},A)
. When
A=\mathbb{Z}
, we often write
C_{\bullet}(\mathcal{G})
and
H_{\bullet}(\mathcal{G})
. There is a picture of groupoid homology in terms of the Tor functor with
H_{\bullet}(\mathcal{G},A)\cong\text{Tor}^{{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}({\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{G}^{0},A\mathcal{G}^{0})
, and for general
{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{G}
-modules
M
we set
H_{\bullet}(\mathcal{G};M)=\text{Tor}^{{\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{G}}_{\bullet}({\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{G}^{0},M)
; this is the left derived functor of the
\mathcal{G}
-coinvariants
M\mapsto M_{\mathcal{G}}=M/\langle 1_{U}m-1_{\textbf{s}(U)}m\rangle
, where
U
ranges over compact open bisections of
\mathcal{G}
and
m
ranges over
M
. It is known that groupoid homology is invariant under Morita equivalence.
