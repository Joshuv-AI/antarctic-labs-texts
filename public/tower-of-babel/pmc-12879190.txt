Is he really my relative? Kinship cases in substructured populations: a study on the Lebanese population
Authors: Ansar El Andari, Issam Mansour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe probabilistic analysis performed in DNA kinship cases is affected by factors such as the number of loci analyzed, the STR allelic relative frequency dataset, and the population genetic characteristics of the individuals involved in the analysis. The Lebanese population shows high rates of endogamy and consanguinity, which challenges the power of discrimination of DNA profiles and is a potential source of misleading conclusions. The present study aims to evaluate the probability of false positives in relationship testing, especially siblingship, avuncular, and grandparent–grandchildren relationships, when cases are subject to different constraints, including DNA profile sizes (15 vs. 23 STR loci) and inbreeding coefficients (FST). A total of 126 794 kinship comparisons were performed for each type of kinship test using a Lebanese database of 505 unrelated healthy individuals. To do so, DNA analysis results were assessed under a pair of propositions involving the stated relationship link between the two individuals vs. unrelatedness, offering quantification through the likelihood ratio (LR), a widely accepted metric to assess the value of scientific results. Among the simulated cases, false associations for full siblingship relationships were observed in 1 995 (1.6%) instances using a DNA profile size of 15 STRs (LR > 1, or log(LR) > 0). False associations decreased to 340 (0.3%) cases when the DNA profile size was increased to 23 STRs, and to 299 (0.2%) and 91 (0.07%) cases using the co-ancestry coefficient for the Lebanese population (0.002) and that recommended by the National Research Committee (0.02), respectively. Simulations of half siblingships (representing half-siblingship, avuncular, and grandparent–grandchildren relationships) showed similar results. Conversely, 41 full biological siblingship cases, 24 true full-siblingship laboratory cases, and 124 true half-siblingship laboratory cases were used as controls to assess the effects of implementing the conditions applied in the simulations (profile size and co-ancestry coefficient (FST) values) to lower false associations. Results for the positive controls showed that the implementation of the National Research Committee-recommended FST value of 0.02 decreased the LR by an average of 102 for 15-STR DNA profiles, and by an average of 104 for 23-STR DNA profiles, which did not lead to misleading results. However, the use of a DNA profile size of 23 STRs with the Lebanese co-ancestry coefficient showed the most meaningful results. This study could help to set national recommendations for the interpretation of kinship testing in the Lebanese population.Keywords: forensic sciences, forensic genetics, DNA profiling, kinship testing, siblingship testing, F ST , consanguinity, Lebanon

Introduction

DNA analysis helps assess whether individuals are genetically related. Kinship testing, which includes genetic testing of parentage, siblingship, avuncular, and other relationships, is usually evaluated using likelihood ratios (LRs). An LR is a statistical measure that quantifies the probability of the observed genetic results under two competing hypotheses: that the individuals have the given genetic relationship, or that they are unrelated. Kinship tests thus usually involve quantifying the probability of the two DNA profiles occurring if the subjects have the given genetic relationship, and if they are unrelated but have the same ethnic background. The evaluation of genetic evidence is affected by many factors, including the short tandem repeat (STR) allele frequency estimates of the reference population, the number of loci used in the analysis, and the genetic make-up of the reference population, particularly the presence of any genetic stratification.

Lebanon is a relatively small country located on the Eastern shore of the Mediterranean Sea, with a population that has been shaped by a sequence of many different civilizations [1], and forced moves of neighboring populations such as Armenians, Palestinians, Syriacs, Caledonians, and recently, Syrians. Consequently, nowadays the Lebanese population is characterized by the presence of more than 18 religious communities that live in relative geographic isolation and are unevenly distributed throughout the Lebanese geographic area. The Lebanese population practices endogamous marriage—the custom of marrying only within the limits of a given religious subcommunity—with a rate up to 88% [2], as well as consanguineous marriages—marriages between related individuals, usually second or third generation—which could reach up to 36% [3, 4]. Lebanese citizens tend to practice marriages with individuals of the same religion, village, and/or family because of social, political, and religious beliefs. These factors have contributed to the development of population stratification and subpopulations in Lebanon, which are currently being underestimated when calculating the evidential value of DNA profile interpretations.

The stratification of the Lebanese population could lead to erroneous kinship testing results, and the inbreeding practices could affect STR allele frequencies as well as the power of discrimination of an STR profile of a given size [5]. Indeed, endogamy and consanguinity shape the genetic structure of the population [6] so that the probability of observing matching allele types in different individuals is high [7], in addition to a loss in systemic heterozygosity. In kinship analysis, such as siblingship relationship testing, individuals belonging to the same subpopulation can exhibit more shared alleles than they would if no substructure was present, leading to misleading results. Observing one allele in the subpopulation increases the chance of observing another of the same type. Hence, within a subpopulation, DNA profiles with matching allele types are more common than expected when assuming independence, even when two people are not directly related [8].

The underestimation of co-ancestry coefficients or population stratifications introduces bias to DNA interpretations. If a population shows significant genetic stratification, then the LR and other statistical calculations are affected; therefore, to take this aspect into account in any analysis or disputes, mathematical formulae are modified to accommodate substructure coefficients, such as co-ancestry coefficient (FST) corrections [7, 9]. Also, the number of STR markers used to generate a DNA profile plays a crucial role in increasing the power of discrimination between individuals in a study. The more STR markers studied in a genetic analysis, the lower the risk of misinterpretation in DNA investigations.

Various studies have shown the effect of genetic seclusions between the different Lebanese subpopulations on STR frequencies and thus on the interpretation and evidential value of DNA profiles [6, 10–12]. Many studies have discussed the effect of the inbreeding coefficient in DNA interpretations and questioned the number of genetic markers used [9, 13–18]. However, no recommendations have yet been suggested for the evidential value of DNA interpretation in the Lebanese population. Lebanese relationship testing laboratories currently use STR allele frequencies that assume Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium [5], and so no inbreeding corrections are applied for any DNA interpretation. However, kinship LR calculations should account for the presence of population stratifications and substructure coefficients should be factored in while assigning LRs [7, 9]. None of these constraints have yet been considered or adopted in Lebanese laboratory practices. Moreover, Lebanon lacks legislation concerning kinship testing, allowing relationship testing laboratories to choose whether to follow international recommendations. Relationship testing may therefore give false-positive results in such cases [10, 19].

This study aimed to assess the probability of false kinship associations. The kinship investigations were analyzed under different situations, including different profile sizes (15 vs. 23 STRs), and incorporating inbreeding coefficients (FST) either specific to the population [20] or based on international recommendations [21]. The results of this study would help in setting national recommendations for kinship testing laboratories.

Materials and methods

Simulations

A database of 505 unrelated healthy Lebanese individuals was used to estimate the allele frequencies of 23 STR loci in the Lebanese population [5] and to carry out full- and half-siblingship simulations, allowing an LR value to be assigned for each selected pair of individuals. The study received ethical approval from the Ethical Committee for Research of the American University of Science and Technology, namely the Institutional Review Board (IRB; IRB-Biomedical-2013). All participants provided written informed consent before participation and were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. The Lebanese database is structured around the seven major Lebanese religious communities (Christian Catholics, Christian Maronite, Christian Greek Orthodox, Muslim Druze, Muslim Shiaa, Muslim Sunni, and Christian Armenians), considering their geographic distribution and proportional importance. The DNA profiles in the Lebanese database were generated using a 23-STR system (including the loci D3S1358, THO1, D21S11, D18S51, Penta E, D5S818, D13S317, D7S820, D16S539, CSF1PO, Penta D, VWA, D8S1179, TPOX, FGA, D19S433, D2S1338, D22S1045, D1S1656, D10S1248, D2S441, D12S391, and SE33) tested using the PowerPlex 16 HS and ESI 17 Fast systems (Promega Corporation; Madison, WI, USA). Siblingship and avuncular simulations were performed between samples in the database, totaling 126 794 simulations for each case. Conditions implemented in the investigations were: (i) 15 STRs or (ii) 23 STRs; each with and without (iii) the Lebanese FST correction (0.002) [20], and (iv) the U.S. National Research Committee (NRC)-recommended FST correction (0.02) [21]. The STR loci used for analysis of the 15-STR DNA profiles were those included in the Identifiler kit (Applied Biosystems, Bedford, MA, USA) because these loci are most commonly used by relationship testing laboratories in Lebanon. The STRs used for the 23-STR DNA profiles were those obtained by the combination of the PP16 HS and ESI 17 systems (Promega). An LR was calculated under the following pair of propositions: (i) the selected individuals are related (e.g., siblings); (ii) the selected individuals are unrelated but from the same subpopulation.

Controls

Two types of controls were used in this study: true cases of full siblingship obtained from known paternities; and laboratory cases showing positive full- and half-siblingship results. The DNA profiles of 82 individuals belonging to 41 true cases of full biological siblingship were used as a control to assess the effects of implementing the conditions applied in the simulations to true siblingship analyses.

Additionally, 24 full-siblingship and 124 half-siblingship laboratory cases were used as controls. These controls were used to evaluate the effect of implementing various conditions, aimed at minimizing false associations observed in kinship simulations, on the results of previously tested laboratory cases that indicated a positive LR.

Results

Simulations

A total of 126 794 comparisons were performed for each full- and half-siblingship analysis.

Full-siblingship cases where the data indicated the subjects were unrelated

When using 15 STRs for the DNA profiles, 1 995 (1.6%) cases showed full siblingship LRs ranging from 1 to 103. When the Lebanese co-ancestry correction was applied to these cases, the number of LRs larger than 1 decreased to 1 852 (1.5%) cases; when the NRC-recommended FST value of 0.02 was used, the number of cases with LRs larger than 1 decreased to 1 073 (0.8%) cases with LRs ranging up to 102 (Supplementary Figure S1).

However, the number of false associations significantly dropped to 340 (0.3%) cases when 23 STR markers were used for analysis. When the Lebanese FST correction was applied using 23 STRs, the number of false associations decreased to 299 (0.2%) of the cases, and when the NRC-recommended value of 0.02 was used, the false associations decreased to 91 (0.07%) of the cases (SupplementarySupplementary Figure S2).

The overall distribution and comparison of the results obtained for the full-siblingship simulations using 15 STR vs. 23 STR markers, with and without FST corrections, are shown in Figure 1.

Likelihood ratios (LRs) obtained in simulated full-siblingship cases where subjects are unrelated, using 15-STR vs. 23-STR DNA profiles with and without FST corrections (zoomed-in section, showing the false-positive correlations). NRC: National Research Committee.

Simulated half-siblingship cases

When using 15 STRs for the DNA profiles, 15 561 (12.3%) simulated cases showed positive half-siblingship LRs within the range of 101 to 104. When Lebanese FST corrections were applied to these cases, the number of misleading conclusions decreased to 14 823 (11.7%) cases, and when the NRC-recommended FST was used, the number of cases where the LR supported the false proposition decreased to 9 668 (7.6%) (Supplementary Figure S3).

However, the number of cases with misleading conclusions dropped significantly to 7 857 (6.2%) when 23 STR markers were used for analysis (Supplementary Figure S4). When Lebanese FST corrections were applied to 23-STR analysis, the number of misleading conclusions decreased to 7 236 (5.7%) cases, and when the NRC-recommended FST was used for corrections, the number of cases where the LR supported the false proposition decreased to 3 612 (2.8%) simulated cases.

The overall distribution and comparison between the results obtained for the half-siblingship simulations when using 15 STR vs. 23 STR markers with and without FST corrections are shown in Figure 2.

Controls

Two types of controls were used in this study: true full-siblingship cases obtained from positive Trio paternity cases where several siblings were available; and positive casework cases with LR greater than 1 for full- and half-siblingships.

True siblingships

Forty-one known biological sibling pairs were analyzed using 15-STR vs. 23-STR DNA profiles, with and without the implementation of FST corrections using either the Lebanese (0.002) or NRC-recommended (0.02) FST value, illustrated in Supplementary Figures S5 and S6.

The overall distribution and comparison between the results obtained for the full-siblingship simulations and true siblingships using 23 STR markers with and without FST corrections are shown in Figure 3.

Siblingship laboratory cases

Also used as controls were 24 full-siblingship and 124 half-siblingship laboratory cases showing positive associations with LRs higher than 1, using different conditions: 15-STR vs. 23-STR DNA profiles, with and without the implementation of FST correction using either the Lebanese (0.002) or NRC-recommended (0.02) FST values. The results of the controls, true sibling relationships and positive laboratory sibling cases demonstrated that the application of 23-STRs, with or without FST corrections, is suitably conservative and did not lead to misinterpretation of the cases or false-negative results. Instead, the application of these conditions when analyzing the cases reaffirmed their positive association.

Discussion and conclusion

Testing 126 794 simulated full- and half-siblingship relationships was important to assess potential factors that may cause misleading results in kinship investigations in different real-life situations used by Lebanese testing laboratories. Most Lebanese relationship testing laboratories use 15-STR DNA profile systems and do not apply corrections for inbreeding. This introduces uncertainty when interpreting the evidential value of DNA profiles because of the specific genetic structure of the Lebanese population. Here, our results show that false associations significantly decrease when using DNA profiles containing 23 STRs, as depicted in Figures 1–2. Adopting co-ancestry corrections minimized misleading results, especially when using the NRC-recommended FST value of 0.02, which showed the lowest number of false positives for both half- and full-siblingship simulations. However, when these conditions were applied to true siblingship cases, the application of a conventional FST value reduced the LRs by an average of 102 for DNA profiles composed of 15 STRs, and by an average of 104 for DNA profiles composed of 23 STRs. Despite these reductions, we observed no false negatives (log10(LR) value less than 0) for true siblingship couples. As shown in Figures 3–7 and Supplementary Figures S5, and S6, which present data from the positive siblingship controls, using a 23-STR system is a conservative approach, with or without FST correction, because most cases retained high LR values.

Because Lebanon lacks legislation concerning relationship testing and DNA-testing laboratories follow guidelines of their choice, the current study could help provide a baseline for setting national recommendations for the interpretation of kinship investigations, and provide local laboratories with a compendium of research results to be taken into consideration when interpreting kinship investigations in local Lebanese laboratories.

In conclusion, our results show that it is highly recommended that Lebanese DNA testing laboratories adopt a minimum of 23 STR markers when generating DNA profiles for evaluating kinship investigations, to lower the risk of misinterpretations. Additionally, it is also recommended to adopt adequate FST corrections in kinship analysis, in this case the Lebanese FST (0.002) [20], so that the interpretation of Lebanese DNA kinship testing can be as robust, reliable, and accurate as possible.
