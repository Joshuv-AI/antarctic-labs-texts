Scenarios for magnetic X-point collapse in 2D incompressible dissipationless extended magnetohydrodynamics
Alain J. Brizard

Abstract
The equations of 2D incompressible dissipationless extended magnetohydrodynamics (XMHD) extend the equations of incompressible Hall MHD (HMHD) by retaining finite-electron inertia. These XMHD equations couple the fluid velocity 𝐕 = 𝗓 ^ × ∇ ϕ + V z ​ 𝗓 ^ {\bf V}=\widehat{\sf z}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\times$}\,\nabla\phi+V_{z}\,\widehat{\sf z} with the magnetic field 𝐁 = ∇ ψ × 𝗓 ^ + B z 𝗓 ^ {\bf B}=\nabla\psi\,\mbox{\boldmath$\times$}\,\widehat{\sf z}+B_{z}\,\widehat{\sf z} in a process that is known to support dissipationless solutions that exhibit finite-time singularities associated with magnetic X-point collapse in the magnetic plane ( B x = ∂ ψ / ∂ y , B y = − ∂ ψ / ∂ x ) (B_{x}=\partial\psi/\partial y,B_{y}=-\,\partial\psi/\partial x) . Here, by adopting a 2D self-similar model for the four XMHD fields ( ϕ , ψ , V z , B z ) (\phi,\psi,V_{z},B_{z}) , we obtain five coupled ordinary differential equations that are solved in terms of the Jacobi elliptic functions based on an orbital classification associated with particle motion in a quartic potential. Excellent agreement is found when these analytical solutions are compared with numerical solutions, including the precise time of a magnetic X-point collapse.

I.1
Incompressible dissipationless extended MHD equations

The extended MHD study of the dissipationless collapse of a magnetic X-point is based on the following coupled incompressible fluid equations (normalized to standard Alfvénic units)
[
10
]
: the fluid momentum equation

\frac{\partial\bf V}{\partial t}+{\bf V}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}\,\nabla{\bf V}\;=\;-\,\nabla P\;+\;{\bf J}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\times$}\,{\bf B}\;-\;{\sf d}_{e}^{2}\;{\bf J}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}\,\nabla{\bf J},

(1)

where
{\bf V}
denotes the normalized center-of-mass fluid velocity, the normalized fluid mass density
\varrho=1
has been set to unity (so that the fluid continuity equation yields the incompressibility condition
\nabla\,\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}\,{\bf V}\equiv 0
),
P
denotes the normalized total fluid pressure, and
{\bf J}\equiv\nabla\,\mbox{\boldmath$\times$}\,{\bf B}
denotes the normalized Ampère current density obtained from the normalized magnetic field
{\bf B}\equiv\nabla\,\mbox{\boldmath$\times$}\,{\bf A}
; the generalized Ohm’s law

{\bf E}+{\bf V}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\times$}\,{\bf B}\;=\;{\sf d}_{i}\left({\bf J}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\times$}\,{\bf B}-\nabla P_{e}\right)\;+\;{\sf d}_{e}^{2}\left(\frac{\partial\bf J}{\partial t}+{\bf V}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}\,\nabla{\bf J}+{\bf J}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}\,\nabla{\bf V}\right)\;-\;{\sf d}_{i}{\sf d}_{e}^{2}\,{\bf J}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}\,\nabla{\bf J},

(2)

where
{\bf E}\equiv-\,\nabla\phi-\partial{\bf A}/\partial t
denotes the normalized electric field and
P_{e}
denotes the normalized electron pressure; and Faraday’s law

\frac{\partial\bf B}{\partial t}\;=\;-\;\nabla\,\mbox{\boldmath$\times$}\,{\bf E},

(3)

which is subject to the condition
\nabla\,\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}\,{\bf B}\equiv 0
. Here, terms that involve
{\sf d}_{i}\neq 0
represent Hall corrections while terms that involve
{\sf d}_{e}\equiv\sqrt{\delta}\,{\sf d}_{i}\neq 0
represent finite electron-inertia corrections.

While the nature of three-dimensional (3D) magnetic reconnection can be very different from two-dimensional (2D) magnetic reconnection

{\bf A}\equiv\psi\,\widehat{\sf z}+{\bf A}_{\bot}

{\bf J}=-\,\nabla^{2}\psi\,\widehat{\sf z}+\nabla B_{z}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\times$}\,\widehat{\sf z}

(4)

I.2
Standard 2D self-similar XMHD model

Since the detailed solution of the 2D nonlinear XMHD equations (
4
)-(
7
) requires extensive numerical simulations, it would be advantageous to proceed with a simple representation of the XMHD fields
(\phi,V_{z},\psi,B_{z})
in order to extract the key elements of finite-time singularities associated with dissipationless X-point magnetic collapse. For this purpose, a standard 2D self-similar XMHD model
[

,
19
,

,
16
,
17

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}\phi(x,y,t)&=&\gamma(t)\,xy\\

\psi^{*}(x,y,t)&=&\imath(t)\,x^{2}\;-\;\jmath(t)\,y^{2}\\
{\sf d}_{i}\,B_{z}(x,y,t)&=&b(t)\,xy\end{array}\right\},

(11)

where the five coefficients
(\imath,\jmath,u,v,b)
are arbitrary functions of time. Here, Eq. (
5

\nabla^{2}\phi=0=\nabla^{2}B_{z}

\psi=\imath\,x^{2}-\jmath\,y^{2}+2\,{\sf d}_{e}^{2}\,(\imath-\jmath)
yields
a uniform parallel current density
J_{z}=-\,\nabla^{2}\psi=2\,(\jmath-\imath)
so that
\nabla(\nabla^{2}\psi)=0
. We note, however, that the 2D self-similar XMHD model (
11
) is not unique in studying magnetic reconnection and other models
[

,
30
,

,
32

have been used to study Hall MHD reconnection (with or without finite resisitivity) while using axisymmetric magnetic geometry

)-(

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2b\,(\imath-\delta\;u),

(12)

\displaystyle\dot{\jmath}+2\gamma\,\jmath

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\;2b\,(\jmath+\delta\;v),

(13)

\displaystyle\dot{u}-2\gamma\,u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\;2\,b\,\imath,

(14)

\displaystyle\dot{v}+2\gamma\,v

\displaystyle-\;2\,b\,\jmath,

\displaystyle\dot{b}

I.3
Normalized XMHD equations

In Eqs. (
12
)-(
16
), we note that the free coefficient
\gamma
can actually be absorbed as an integrating factor into the definitions

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\imath(t)\\
\jmath(t)\\

are independent of
\gamma(t)

\gamma
is well behaved (i.e., it doesn’t have singularities of its own), the exponential growth (or decay) associated with
\Gamma(t)
reaches infinity (or zero) only as

\displaystyle-\;2\,b\,(J+\delta\;V),

\displaystyle\dot{U}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\;2\,b\,I,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\;2\,b\,J,

I.4
Purpose of the present work and organization

The purpose of the present work is to extend previous works
[
19
,
20
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
, where
{\sf C}_{0}>0
was the only case considered, to include the case
{\sf C}_{0}<0
. We will introduce a classification of bounded and unbounded solutions for the magnetic coefficient
b(t)
associated with orbits in a quartic potential that are parametrized by
{\sf C}_{0}
and the real parameter
\epsilon
. Each of these analytical solutions for
b(t)
will be expressed in terms of Jacobi elliptic functions
[
34
,
35
]
, which will yield an explicit expression for the finite singularity time
T_{\infty}({\sf C}_{0},\epsilon)
for the unbounded solutions as functions of
({\sf C}_{0},\epsilon)
. For the bounded (periodic) solution, the period will also be explicitly expressed as a function of
({\sf C}_{0},\epsilon)
. We will also show how these magnetic solutions can be used to obtain solutions of the Hall MHD equations (with
\delta=0
), as well finite electron-inertia corrections, for the XMHD coefficients
(I,J,U,V)
.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, the magnetic equation (
26
) [Eq. (
51
)] is solved in terms of the Jacobi elliptic functions
[
34
,
35
]
for all possible values for
({\sf C}_{0},\epsilon)
, where
\epsilon
is a real parameter. We note that Janda
[
16
]
has previously solved the magnetic equation in terms of the Weierstrass elliptic function
[
35
,
36
]
for the case
{\sf C}_{0}>0
and
\epsilon<1
. (See Refs.
[
17
,
18
]
for comments on Janda’s work
[
16
]
.) In the present work, we use a standard energy method to obtain a simple classification of “particle” orbits in a quartic potential
[
37
,
38
]
, which allows bounded periodic solutions as well as unbounded solutions that exhibit finite-time singularities. In Sec.
III
, we explore how finite electron inertia modifies the solutions of the normalized XMHD coefficients
(I,J,U,V)
once the magnetic coefficient
b(t)
is solved. In Sec.
IV
, the numerical solutions of the XMHD equations (
18
)-(
22

\delta=1/1836

is confirmed and the Jacobi elliptic solutions match the numerical results exactly, including the finite-time singularities predicted at times
T_{\infty}({\sf C}_{0},\epsilon)
that are expressed in terms of complete elliptic integrals. We summarize our work in Sec.
V
and, in App.

[
16

for the bounded and unbounded orbits expressed in terms of the Weierstrass elliptic function.

II
Jacobi Elliptic Solution for the XMHD Magnetic Equation

In the present Section, we show that the solutions for Eqs. (
18
)-(
21
) can be determined from the solutions of the magnetic coefficient
b(t)
. Here, by introducing the integrating factors
e^{\pm 2\beta}
, where
\beta(t)\equiv\int_{0}^{t}b(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}
, with the transformations
I(t)\equiv{\mathcal{I}}(t)\,e^{2\beta(t)}
and
J(t)\equiv{\mathcal{J}}(t)\,e^{-2\beta(t)}
, we obtain the coupled reduced XMHD equations

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{\mathcal{I}}\\
\dot{V}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d(e^{-2\beta})}{dt}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\delta\,U\\
{\mathcal{J}}\end{array}\right)\;=\;-2\,b\,e^{-2\beta}\,\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\delta\,U\\
{\mathcal{J}}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{\mathcal{J}}\\
\dot{U}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\,\frac{d(e^{2\beta})}{dt}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\delta\,V\\
{\mathcal{I}}\end{array}\right)\;=\;-2\,b\,e^{2\beta}\,\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\delta\,V\\
{\mathcal{I}}\end{array}\right),

which can be expressed in vector form as

\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}_{\pm}\;=\;-2\,b\,e^{\pm 2\beta}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\delta\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{\mp},

(42)

where the reduced coefficient vectors
\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{+}\equiv({\mathcal{J}},U)^{\top}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{-}\equiv({\mathcal{I}},V)^{\top}
are the two linearly-independent solutions of their respective second ordinary differential equations

\ddot{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}_{\pm}\;=\;\frac{e^{\mp 2\beta}}{b}\frac{d}{dt}\left(b\,e^{\pm 2\beta}\right)\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}_{\pm}\;+\;4\,\delta\,b^{2}\;\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{\pm}\;=\;\left(\frac{\dot{b}}{b}\;\pm\;2\,b\right)\dot{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}_{\pm}\;+\;4\,\delta\,b^{2}\;\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{\pm},

(43)

subject to the initial conditions
(I_{0},J_{0},U_{0},V_{0},b_{0})
. Hence, the solution
b(t)
of the magnetic equation (
26
) can be used to generate solutions for the reduced coefficients
\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{\pm}
.

For a given solution for
b(t)
, which is still determined from the initial conditions
(I_{0},J_{0},U_{0},V_{0},b_{0})
, the reduced XMHD equations (
II
)-(
II
) can be shown to satisfy the following conservation laws
d{\sf C}_{k}/dt=0
, where

\displaystyle{\sf C}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}\;{\mathcal{J}}\;-\;\frac{\delta}{4}\;b^{2},

(44)

\displaystyle{\sf C}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U\;V\;-\;\frac{1}{4}\;b^{2},

(45)

\displaystyle{\sf C}_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({\mathcal{I}}\,e^{2\beta}\;+\;U\right)\;\left({\mathcal{J}}\,e^{-2\beta}\;-\;V\right)\;-\;\frac{\delta}{4}\;b^{2}\;\equiv\;\left({\sf C}_{1}-{\sf C}_{2}\right)\;+\;U\,{\mathcal{J}}\;e^{-2\beta}\;-\;V\,{\mathcal{I}}\;e^{2\beta}\;-\;\frac{1}{4}\;b^{2},

(46)

and the initial conditions for
({\mathcal{I}}_{0},{\mathcal{J}}_{0})=(I_{0},J_{0})
are independent of
b(t)
. We now introduce the time-dependent Hamiltonian

{\sf H}_{b}(U,V,{\mathcal{I}},{\mathcal{J}};t)\;\equiv\;2\,\left({\sf C}_{1}-{\sf C}_{2}-{\sf C}_{3}\right)\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,b^{2}(t)\;+\;2\,\left(V\,{\mathcal{I}}\;e^{2\beta(t)}\;-\;U\,{\mathcal{J}}\;e^{-2\beta(t)}\right),

(47)

which satisfies
\dot{\sf H}_{b}\equiv 0
, with
\dot{b}\equiv-\,4\left(V\,{\mathcal{I}}\;e^{2\beta}+U\,{\mathcal{J}}\;e^{-2\beta}\right)
, and the time-dependent canonical 4D Poisson bracket

\left\{F,\;G\right\}_{b}\;\equiv\;b(t)\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial U}\;-\;\frac{\partial F}{\partial U}\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial V}\right)\;+\;\delta\,b(t)\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathcal{I}}\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial\mathcal{J}}\;-\;\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathcal{J}}\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial\mathcal{I}}\right),

(48)

Equations (
II
)-(
II
) can thus be expressed in Hamiltonian form as

\displaystyle\left(\dot{\mathcal{I}},\;\dot{\mathcal{J}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\,b(t)\left(\frac{\partial{\sf H}_{b}}{\partial\mathcal{J}},\;-\,\frac{\partial{\sf H}_{b}}{\partial\mathcal{I}}\right)\;\equiv\;\left(\left\{{\mathcal{I}},\;{\sf H}_{b}\right\}_{b},\frac{}{}\left\{{\mathcal{J}},\;{\sf H}_{b}\right\}_{b}\right),

(49)

\displaystyle\left(\dot{U},\;\dot{V}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b(t)\left(-\,\frac{\partial{\sf H}_{b}}{\partial V},\;\frac{\partial{\sf H}_{b}}{\partial U}\right)\;\equiv\;\left(\left\{U,\;{\sf H}_{b}\right\}_{b},\frac{}{}\left\{V,\;{\sf H}_{b}\right\}_{b}\right),

(50)

where it is immediately clear that
({\mathcal{I}},{\mathcal{J}})
are constants of the motion for Hall MHD (
\delta=0
). We note that an attempt at deriving a Hamiltonian structure for the 5D XMHD model
(\imath,\jmath,u,v,b)
, with
\gamma
appearing as a free parameter, was presented in a recent paper by Abdelhamid and Lingham
[
39
]
, but it was later revealed
[
40
]
that their proof of the Jacobi identity in 5D space was invalid.

II.1
Energy method

We now proceed with the solution
b(t)
of the magnetic equation (
26
), which can be transformed into
\ddot{q}=2\,q^{3}-2{\sf C}_{0}\,q
, with the definition
q(t)\equiv\sqrt{1+4\,\delta}\;b(t)
, which takes into account a finite electron-inertia correction. If we multiply this equation by
\dot{q}
and integrate it over time, we obtain the energy-like conservation law
[
37
,
38
]

\frac{1}{2}\,\dot{q}^{2}\;+\;U(q)\;=\;E\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,\dot{q}_{0}^{2}\;+\;U(q_{0})\;\equiv\;\frac{1}{2}\,{\sf C}_{0}^{2}\,\epsilon,

(51)

where the energy parameter
\epsilon
is any real number and the “potential” energy is defined by the even quartic polynomial

U(q)\;=\;{\sf C}_{0}\,q^{2}\;-\;\frac{1}{2}\,q^{4}.

(52)

When
{\sf C}_{0}>0
(solid curve in left figure in Fig.
3
), the potential energy (
52
) has two maxima
{\sf C}_{0}^{2}/2
at
q=\pm\,\sqrt{{\sf C}_{0}}
and a local minimum
0
at
q=0
. When
{\sf C}_{0}<0
, the potential energy has a single maximum
0
at
q=0
(solid curve in right figure in Fig.
3
). As seen in Fig.
3
(left), the case
{\sf C}_{0}>0
allows for a bounded periodic solution (Orbit II:
-\,\sqrt{{\sf C}_{0}}<q<\sqrt{{\sf C}_{0}}
) for
0<\epsilon<1
, with
\epsilon=1
corresponding to a separatrix solution. We also have unbounded orbits for
0<\epsilon<1
, with a single turning point for
{\sf C}_{0}>0
(Orbit III:
q(t)>\sqrt{{\sf C}_{0}}
or
q(t)<-\,\sqrt{{\sf C}_{0}}
), and without turning points for
{\sf C}_{0}<0
(Orbit VI). For
\epsilon>1
, the unbounded solutions have no turning points for both cases
{\sf C}_{0}>0
(Orbit I) and
{\sf C}_{0}<0
(Orbit V). For
\epsilon<0
, the unbounded solutions have a single turning point for both cases
{\sf C}_{0}>0
(Orbit IV) and
{\sf C}_{0}<0
(Orbit VII).

III
Finite Electron Inertia XMHD Solutions

While the electron-proton mass ratio is very small
(\delta=1/1836\ll 1)

II
)-(
II

b(t)
will play a crucial role. We note that the finite singularity time
T_{\infty}({\sf C}_{0},\epsilon)
has an implicit dependence on the electron-inertia parameter

\delta

{\sf C}_{0}(\delta)
and
\epsilon(\delta)
and, in general,

at
\delta=0

III.2
Finite electron-inertia corrections

When
\beta(t)
approaches infinity as
t\rightarrow T_{\infty}
(assuming that
\sigma=+1
), the HMHD coefficients
I(t)\rightarrow\infty
and
U(t)\rightarrow\infty
are expected to explode exponentially, according to Eqs. (
68
) and (
70
), respectively, and, therefore, do not require finite electron inertia corrections, so that we may assume
I(t)\simeq I_{H}(t)
and
U(t)\simeq U_{H}(t)
. Because
\lim_{t\rightarrow T_{\infty}}V(t)=V_{0}-J_{0}
is finite at the zeroth order in
\delta
, its finite electron inertia correction may not be important and we may again assume that
V(t)\simeq V_{H}(t)
. In the case of the Hall solution
J_{H}(t)\rightarrow 0
, however, it is important to consider corrections due to finite electron inertia, so that

\lim_{t\rightarrow T_{\infty}}J(t)\;=\;\overline{J}(\delta)\;=\;\delta\,\overline{J}^{(1)}\;+\;\cdots

(80)

may approach a finite value at
t=T_{\infty}
.

To derive an expression for the first-order correction
\overline{J}^{(1)}
in Eq. (
80
), we begin with the expression

J(t)\;=\;J_{H}(t)\;+\;\delta\,\overline{J}^{(1)}\;\left(1\;-\;e^{-2\beta(t)}\right)\;+\;\cdots,

(81)

where
J_{H}(t)
is given by Eq. (
69
) and the initial condition
J(0)=J_{0}
is preserved. By inserting Eqs. (
68
) and (
81
) into the conservation law (
23
):
I\,J-I_{0}J_{0}=\delta(b^{2}-b_{0}^{2})/4
, we obtain the first-order expression

\overline{J}^{(1)}\;\left(1\;-\;e^{-2\beta(t)}\right)\;=\;\frac{1}{4I_{0}}\;\left(b^{2}(t)\;-\frac{}{}b_{0}^{2}\right)e^{-2\beta(t)},

which is expected to be finite at
t=T_{\infty}
:

\overline{J}^{(1)}\;=\;\frac{1}{4I_{0}}\;\lim_{t\rightarrow T_{\infty}}\left(b^{2}(t)\;-\frac{}{}b_{0}^{2}\right)e^{-2\beta(t)},

(82)

with
b(t)
given in Table
2
and
e^{-2\beta(t)}
given in Table
4
in the zero electron-inertia limit
(\delta=0)
. When an unbounded orbit has a single turning point (i.e.,
b_{0}\neq 0
), for Orbit III
(0<\epsilon<1)
, we find

\left(b^{2}(t)\;-\frac{}{}b_{0}^{2}\right)e^{-2\beta(t)}\;=\;b_{0}^{2}\,\left(\frac{{\rm dn}^{2}(\Omega t,k)\;-\;{\rm cn}^{2}(\Omega t,k)}{(1\;+\;{\rm sn}(\Omega t,k))^{2}}\right)\;\rightarrow\;\frac{b_{0}^{2}}{4}\,(1-k^{2})\;=\;\frac{{\sf C}_{0}}{2}\,\sqrt{1-\epsilon},

(83)

while for Orbits IV and VII
(\epsilon<0)
, we find

\left(b^{2}(t)\;-\frac{}{}b_{0}^{2}\right)e^{-2\beta(t)}\;=\;b_{0}^{2}\,\left(\frac{{\rm sn}(\Omega t,k)}{{\rm dn}(\Omega t,k)+k^{\prime}\;{\rm sn}(\Omega t,k)}\right)^{2}\;\rightarrow\;\frac{b_{0}^{2}}{4\,(1-k^{2})}\;=\;\frac{|{\sf C}_{0}|}{2}\,\sqrt{1-\epsilon},

(84)

where
k=\sinh(\alpha/2)/\sqrt{\cosh\alpha}
and
k=\cosh(\alpha/2)/\sqrt{\cosh\alpha}
, respectively.

For unbounded orbits without turning points (i.e.,
b_{0}=0
), on the other hand, we expect
b^{2}(t)\,e^{-2\beta(t)}
to be finite at
t=T_{\infty}
. For Orbit I
(\epsilon>1)
, for example, we find

\displaystyle b^{2}(t)\,e^{-2\beta(t)}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle{\sf C}_{0}\,\sec\Phi\;e^{i\Phi}\,\left(1-e^{i\Phi}\right)^{2}\;\lim_{z\rightarrow 2{\sf K}}\left(\frac{{\rm sn}(z-i\,{\sf K}^{\prime},e^{i\Phi})}{{\rm dn}(z-i\,{\sf K}^{\prime},e^{i\Phi})\;-\;e^{i\Phi}\;{\rm cn}(z-i\,{\sf K}^{\prime},e^{i\Phi})}\right)^{2}

(85)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sf C}_{0}\,\sec\Phi\;e^{i\Phi}\,\left(1-e^{i\Phi}\right)^{2}\;\lim_{z\rightarrow 2{\sf K}}\left(\frac{e^{-i\Phi}}{i\,({\rm cn}z\;-\;{\rm dn}z)}\right)^{2}\;=\;\frac{{\sf C}_{0}}{2}\left(\sqrt{\epsilon}\;-\frac{}{}1\right),

where
\sec\Phi=\cosh\alpha=\sqrt{\epsilon}
, while for Orbit VI
(0<\epsilon<1)
, on the other hand, we find

b^{2}(t)\,e^{-2\beta(t)}\;=\;\frac{Q_{0}^{2}\,(1+k^{\prime})^{2}\;{\rm sn}^{2}(\Omega t,k)}{({\rm dn}(\Omega t,k)+k^{\prime})^{2}}\;\rightarrow\;\frac{Q_{0}^{2}\,(1+k^{\prime})^{2}}{4\,k^{\prime 2}}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\,|{\sf C}_{0}|\;(1+\sqrt{\epsilon}),

(86)

where
k^{\prime}=\tan(\theta/2)
and
\sin\theta=\sqrt{\epsilon}
.

Finally, we note that the present analysis would be reversed for the case
\sigma=-1
, i.e.,
e^{2\beta(t)}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow T_{\infty}
. Hence,
J(t)\rightarrow\infty
and
V(t)\rightarrow\infty
would explode exponentially at the finite singularity time
T_{\infty}
,
U(t)\rightarrow U_{0}+I_{0}
would reach a finite value, and
I(t)\rightarrow\overline{I}(\delta)=\delta\,\overline{I}^{(1)}+\cdots
would reach a finite value at first order in
\delta
, where

\overline{I}^{(1)}\;=\;\frac{-\,1}{4J_{0}}\;\lim_{t\rightarrow T_{\infty}}\left(b^{2}(t)\;-\frac{}{}b_{0}^{2}\right)e^{2\beta(t)}.

(87)

This expression can, of course, also be used when
b_{0}=0
for orbits I and VI. We also note that, independently of the asymptotic sign of
b(t)
, i.e., whether

or

blows up exponentially at the finite-time singularity, the magnetic X-point collapse occurs.

Summary

In the present paper, we considered the analytical and numerical solutions for the five coupled ordinary differential equations (

)-(

) associated with a 2D self-similar model of the incompressible extended MHD equations. One of the major results of our work was the crucial role played by the

, represented by the time-dependent coefficient

, which controlled the timing of the finite-time magnetic collapse. Here, we separated the equation for the magnetic coefficient

{\sf C}_{0}
. For each unbounded orbit, we were able to calculate the time

associated with each finite-time singularity at which the magnetic X-point collapse occurs. In our work, we have shown that the effects of finite electron inertia

T_{\infty}(0)
, where

In Sec.

Appendix A
Weierstrass Elliptic Representation

Several problems in classical mechanics are solved in terms of doubly-periodic elliptic functions
[
37
,
38
]
: for a particle moving in a cubic potential, its bounded and unbounded orbits are naturally expressed in terms of the Weierstrass elliptic functions
[
36
]
; and for a particle moving in a quartic potential, its bounded and unbounded orbits are naturally expressed in terms of the Jacobi elliptic functions
[
34
]
. Based on mathematical grounds
[
43
]
, however, a general elliptic function can either be expressed in terms of Weierstrass or Jacobi elliptic functions, which implies that there is a connection between these two classes of elliptic functions. In this Appendix, we will show that, while the twelve different Jacobi elliptic functions
[
34
]
can be replaced by the single Weierstrass elliptic function
[
36
]
, this explicit simplicity hides an implicit complex structure of roots and singularities.

The purpose of the present Appendix is to present a self-contained general description of the Weierstrass elliptic function
\wp(t)
, which satisfies the Weierstrass elliptic equation
[
36
]

\dot{\wp}^{2}\;=\;4\,\wp^{3}\;-\;g_{2}\,\wp\;-\;g_{3}\;\equiv\;4\,(\wp-e_{1})\,(\wp-e_{2})\,(\wp-e_{3}),

(93)

where the parameters
(g_{2},g_{3})
are known as the lattice invariants and
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
denote the roots of the cubic polynomial
w(s)=4\,s^{3}-g_{2}\,s-g_{3}
, i.e.,
e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}=0
, while
g_{2}=-4\,(e_{1}e_{2}+e_{2}e_{3}+e_{3}e_{1})
and
g_{3}=4\,e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}
. The assignment of these roots and their associated half-periods
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})
, where
\wp(t+2\,\omega_{k})=\wp(t)
and
\wp(\omega_{k})=e_{k}
(for
k=
1, 2, or 3), is determined by a complex dependence on the lattice invariants
(g_{2},g_{3})
. Lastly, the Weierstrass elliptic function
\wp(t)
has singularities at 0 and
(2\,\omega_{1},2\,\omega_{2},2\,\omega_{3})
, where
(2\,\omega_{1},2\,\omega_{3})
are known as lattice generators
[
36
]
, with
\omega_{2}\equiv-\,\omega_{1}-\omega_{3}
.
