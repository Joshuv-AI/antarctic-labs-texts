Genetic testing practices across European epilepsy centers: An ERN EpiCARE survey
Authors: Sébile Tchaicha, Johanna Van Hulle, Eva Brilstra, Andreas Brunklaus, Gaetano Cantalupo, Martin Geroldinger, Amy McTague, Reetta Kälviäinen, Eulalia Turón‐Viñas, Alexis Arzimanoglou, Rikke S. Møller, Rima Nabbout

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGenetic testing plays an increasing role in the diagnostic pathway for rare and complex epilepsies. However, significant heterogeneity persists in access, implementation, and interpretation across Europe. This study aimed to assess genetic testing practices, accessibility, and challenges across expert epilepsy centers within the European Reference Network for Rare and Complex Epilepsies (ERN EpiCARE) and to identify key challenges and areas for harmonization.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey was developed by the ERN EpiCARE Clinical Genetics Working Group and distributed to 50 EpiCARE member centers across 27 European countries. The questionnaire collected quantitative and qualitative information on available genetic testing modalities, turnaround times, use of rapid testing, multidisciplinary team (MDT) organization, genetic counseling practices, and perceived challenges. Survey findings were complemented by a structured discussion held during the ERN EpiCARE General Assembly.ResultsResponses were received from 46 centers (51 responses). Most centers reported access to genetic testing, predominantly through in‐house facilities. Whole‐exome sequencing was available in 85% of centers, and gene panels were available in 78%. Whole‐genome sequencing was available in 59% of centers, frequently restricted to research or performed externally. Turnaround times for standard genetic testing were most commonly between 1 and 6 months. Genetic testing strategies varied by epilepsy subtype, with gene panels most frequently used as first‐tier testing, and exome sequencing preferentially applied in developmental and epileptic encephalopathies. Considerable heterogeneity was observed in MDT organization, access to genetic counseling, reimbursement, data‐sharing and registry infrastructures.SignificanceAlthough genetic testing is widely available across ERN EpiCARE centers, substantial disparities persist in its organization, accessibility, and implementation. European collaboration and harmonized practices are needed to support equitable access to high‐quality genetic care for people living with epilepsy.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a disabling neurological condition that affects 65 million individuals worldwide. 1 , 2 The incidence of epilepsy follows a bimodal age distribution, with the highest rates observed in early childhood and in older adults (over 65 years). Despite advances in diagnostics, the underlying etiology remains unknown in approximately 60% of epilepsy cases. Genetic factors play an important role in both common and rare epilepsies. Common epilepsy syndromes such as genetic generalized epilepsies (GGE) are often due to polygenic causes while early‐onset epilepsies, including developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs), are often monogenic. 3 A genetic cause is identified in up to 40% of individuals with DEEs. 4 The introduction of next‐generation sequencing (NGS) technologies has transformed the etiological evaluation of epilepsy. Nevertheless, many patients with suspected genetic etiologies remain undiagnosed or experience substantial diagnostic delays, underscoring persistent gaps in current diagnostic pathways. Despite notable progress, significant challenges remain in ensuring equitable access to genetic testing, harmonizing diagnostic practices, and effectively integrating genetic findings into routine clinical care. 4 , 5

The European Reference Network for Rare and Complex Epilepsies (ERN EpiCARE) was established in 2017 to improve the quality, accessibility, and cost‐effectiveness of healthcare for patients with rare and complex epilepsy and to optimize the use of specialized resources across Europe. One of the major missions of the ERNs, including the ERN EpiCARE, is to reduce the heterogeneity of care and to ensure all EU citizens equal access to optimal care. 6

In line with its objectives, the EpiCARE Working Group on Clinical Genetics conducted a survey to evaluate current practices, accessibility, and challenges in genetic testing in the epilepsies across EpiCARE centers. This survey aimed to identify areas for improvement, facilitate harmonization of clinical genetics testing practices, and ultimately improve the diagnostic and therapeutic landscape for patients with genetic epilepsies across Europe.

Challenges, state of the art in genetic testing practices

Genetic testing practices in epilepsy remain highly heterogeneous across centers, including among epilepsy expert centers. While genetic testing was indicated primarily for early‐onset epilepsies and often excluded cases with structural brain anomalies, its scope has expanded substantially and now encompasses a broad spectrum of epilepsies, including almost all epilepsy types. The most recent report of the ILAE genetic commission 5 recommends genetic testing in the following conditions when no other etiology has been identified: (1) Severe childhood‐onset epilepsies, particularly DEEs, (2) Epilepsy with intellectual disability, autism, and/or other comorbidities, (3) Progressive myoclonus epilepsies and progressive phenotypes generally, and (4) Non‐acquired focal epilepsies in specific familial syndromes. These recommendations were accompanied by a proposal to perform genetic testing in non‐acquired focal, drug‐resistant epilepsies in the setting of presurgical evaluation and in the setting of malformations of cortical development (possibly with the need of DNA from the brain tissue or from other tissues than blood). These last indications are now accepted in many epilepsy surgery expert centers as a standard indication for genetic testing. 7 , 8

To date, targeted single‐gene testing may still be justified for a limited number of monogenic epilepsies in which a predominant causative gene is well established, such as SCN1A in Dravet syndrome, particularly in resource‐limited settings. However, the decreasing cost of NGS increasingly supports the use of larger gene panels, exome, or even genome sequencing. 6 , 9

This change in our practice has substantially increased the number of patients who may benefit from testing and has moved our needs from selecting specific genes to test a priori to interpreting large‐scale sequencing data from exomes or genomes. Although exome and genome sequencing offer broader coverage, these approaches may add interpretative challenges and require integrated clinical and genetic expertise. Finally, the expanded identification of variants, both in genes not previously associated with epilepsy and variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in previously established epilepsy genes, highlights the urgent need for actionable networks for an optimal analysis of these variants. Such networks emerging in single expert centers between geneticists and clinicians with expertise in the field of genetic epilepsies and in some instances in specific genetic epilepsies are essential to validate genetic findings and to refer in some cases to dedicated research groups that can explore the functional effect of the variants and confirm their causal role in the phenotype.

METHOD

This study employed a cross‐sectional, mixed‐methods design combining a structured survey with a complementary expert discussion. It was conducted within the framework of ERN EpiCARE. The survey was developed by the Clinical Genetics Working Group (WG2), comprising neurologists and clinical geneticists with expertise in epilepsy genetics. The questionnaire was designed through an expert‐driven process and pilot‐tested among WG2 members prior to dissemination. It was administered via SurveyMonkey.

The questionnaire aimed to capture both quantitative and qualitative aspects of genetic testing practices across centers, including test availability, turnaround times, use of rapid testing, multidisciplinary team (MDT) involvement in patient selection and test interpretation, and genetic counseling practices. Respondents were also asked to describe perceived challenges and unmet needs in the genetic diagnosis of rare epilepsies in their institution.

The questionnaire consisted mainly of structured questions, including closed and semi‐closed items with single‐ and multiple‐choice responses, ordinal rating scales 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 to assess the relative use of diagnostic methods, and a limited number of open‐ended questions to capture qualitative information.

The survey was distributed on December 13, 2023, to 50 EpiCARE member hospitals in 27 different European countries via the email list of EPICARE healthcare professionals (HCPs). A reminder was sent on March 7, 2024. The survey was closed on June 22, 2024. In cases where multiple responses were received from the same center, entries were retained as independent observations when they represented distinct clinical pathways (e.g., adult vs. pediatric). Otherwise, responses were consolidated at the center level where appropriate.

To complement the survey findings, a hybrid meeting was held during the EpiCARE General Assembly dedicated to the Clinical Genetics WG in June 2024 after the survey closed. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize survey responses. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

Analyses were primarily conducted at the center level. For analysis of diagnostic strategies by epilepsy subtype, responses were evaluated at the center level. Ordinal rankings 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 were analyzed by calculating the proportion of centers assigning the highest rank (“5”) to each diagnostic modality.

No inferential statistical analyses were performed, given the exploratory nature of the study.

RESULTS

The survey was distributed to 50 EpiCARE centers across 27 European countries. A total of 51 responses were collected from 46 centers, with 41 centers submitting one response and 5 centers providing two responses (reflecting separate pediatric and adult pathways). Respondents included 28 pediatric neurologists, 17 adult neurologists, and 7 geneticists, offering an overview of genetic testing practices across the ERN‐EpiCARE network (Figure 1).

Countries represented among respondents to the Clinical Genetics Working Group survey within the ERN EpiCARE network. National flags indicate the countries of origin of participating EpiCARE centers that responded to the survey.

Most centers reported access to genetic testing predominantly in‐house (n = 39; 84.78%) or through external providers. As shown in Figure 2, whole‐exome sequencing (WES) was available in 85% of centers (39/46), while gene panels including CNV detection data were available in 78% (36/46). Gene panels without CNV detection were generally not used. Whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) was available in 59% of centers (27/46) (Figure 3), although its use was frequently restricted to research settings or outsourced, underscoring persistent barriers to its integration into routine clinical practice.

Turnaround times for genetic testing in non‐emergency situations are generally between 1 and 6 months, with 49% of centers reporting results within 3 months (Figure 4 and Table 1). Overall, 50% of centers reported turnaround times within 1–3 months, 35% within 3–6 months, 13% within 6–12 months, and 2% exceeding 1 year. The estimated median turnaround time fell within the 1–3 months category.

Rapid genetic testing

Rapid genetic testing was available in 70% of centers with rapid panel testing and trio exome sequencing being the most common approaches (45.71 and 42.86% respectively). Rapid genome sequencing for rapid testing was rare (8.57%).

Rapid testing was prioritized for neonates with seizures (86%) and patients under 12 months with DEE (46%). It was also frequently considered for patients with epilepsy and regression (54%) and PICU patients with difficult‐to‐treat seizures (40%). Patients aged <2 years and <5 years with epilepsy were less frequently selected (20 and 6% respectively). Precision medicine emerged as a critical determinant of eligibility for rapid testing, particularly for treatable genetic epilepsy syndromes. However, varying interpretations of severity and eligibility criteria were reported.

Diagnostic methods by epilepsy type

For this specific analysis, 48 center‐level responses were considered. This reflected the inclusion of two centers (one in Belgium and one in Spain) that provided distinct adult and pediatric clinical pathways, which were analyzed independently to accurately reflect differences in diagnostic practices. This explains the discrepancy with the total of 46 unique centers reported elsewhere in the manuscript. Respondents ranked diagnostic methods on a scale of 1 (least used) to 5 (most used), and the following analysis focuses on methods most frequently rated as “5” across the 48 analyzed center‐level responses (Figure 5).

First‐tier genetic diagnostic methods by epilepsy subtype across EpiCARE centers (n = 48 analyzed center‐level responses). The total of 48 corresponds to the inclusion of two centers (Belgium and Spain) that provided separate adult and pediatric responses, which were analyzed independently to reflect pathway‐specific diagnostic practices.

Across the 48 participating centers, genetic testing was consistently part of routine care for the majority of epilepsy phenotypes. Gene panel testing was the most frequently used first‐line approach across several epilepsy subtypes, particularly in self‐limited neonatal/infantile epilepsies (49.02%), progressive myoclonic epilepsies (43.08%), magnetic resonance imaging‐negative focal epilepsies (42.59%), and generalized genetic epilepsies (GGE/IGE, 41.86%).

WES was preferentially used in more severe and genetically heterogeneous phenotypes, notably in Early Infantile Developmental and Epileptic Encephalopathies (EIDEEs, onset <3 months; 28.79%), other DEEs with onset after 3 months (27.69%), and neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) with epilepsy (21.74%).

Combined diagnostic strategies integrating CNV analysis with gene panel or exome sequencing were also frequently reported, particularly in DEEs, EIDEEs, NDD with epilepsy, and epilepsies associated with structural brain malformations.

Single‐gene testing was rarely used as a first‐tier approach and remained mainly confined to progressive myoclonic epilepsies (10.77%) and, to a much lesser extent, epilepsies associated with structural brain malformations (1.67%).

Regarding testing for somatic variants, 48% of centers perform deep sequencing on resected brain tissue from epilepsy surgery patients, with 18% performing this testing as part of the diagnostic work‐up and 30% of them in a research setting.

Conversely, genome sequencing was consistently ranked among the least used first‐line approaches across most epilepsy subtypes, reaching the highest rank (“5”) in only 0–4% of centers depending on the phenotype. Similarly, CGH/SNP‐array was very rarely prioritized in DEEs and in EIDEEs (<3 months), where it was ranked as the most used method in only 2% of centers. This confirms that, despite occasional complementary use, these approaches are generally considered low‐priority first‐line investigations in routine clinical practice.

Patient communication and multidisciplinary roles

Pre‐test information for patients and their families was most frequently provided by pediatric neurologists (84.31%), followed by clinical geneticists (63%) and adult neurologists (52%). Multiple professionals could be involved within the same center. Pediatric neurologists also led result disclosure (82%), with clinical geneticists (76%) and adult neurologists (65%) playing substantial roles. Genetic counselors and nurses had limited involvement. 32% of centers offered genetic counseling only when results were clinically relevant, whereas 68% always provided counseling. This variability reflects substantial heterogeneity in the organization and systematic integration of genetic counseling across centers.

Pre‐ and post‐testing MDT meetings were reported inconsistently (Figure 6). Thirteen centers lacked pre‐testing meetings (from Estonia, Belgium, Spain, Italy, Hungary, Czech Republic, Croatia, Austria, and Germany), and five lacked post‐testing meetings (from Estonia, Italy, Denmark, Hungary, Croatia). Pediatric neurologists, adult neurologists, and clinical geneticists were the most common MDT participants, while genetic counselors, bioinformaticians, and nurses were underrepresented. Genetic scientists played a more prominent role in post‐testing MDTs, reflecting their critical expertise in interpreting results.

Data management and international/European collaboration

Overall, 64% of respondents reported maintaining local registries for genetic epilepsy. Of these, 84.85% were institutional, 6% regional, and 21.21% national. However, only 45.71% of these registries were reported to be interoperable with the ERN EpiCARE registry. In parallel, 73.47% of centers indicated that they lacked access to disease‐specific European or international registries.

Regarding codification practices, 72.97% of centers reported using standardized coding systems for rare and complex epilepsies. Because centers could report more than one coding system, ICD‐10 and Orphanet codes were the most commonly used (64.86%), while national or institutional coding systems distinct from ICD‐10 and Orphanet were reported by 8% of the participants.

Concerning international initiatives, 49% of respondents reported being aware of the SOLVE‐RD platform, and among them, 53% used it for gene sequencing. Respondents were also members of other collaborative initiatives including the Epi25 project (29.95%) and the 1+ Million Genomes Initiative (8%).

Challenges in genetic testing

Twenty‐four respondents reported challenges related to genetic testing. The main barriers identified were funding and reimbursement issues (25%), including insufficient public coverage, administrative reimbursement constraints, and requirements for patient self‐payment. Long turnaround times were reported by 20.83% of respondents, particularly for whole‐exome and WGS. A lack of MDTs was noted by 17% of participants, highlighting gaps in structured collaborative interpretation of results.

Difficulties related to the interpretation of VUS were reported by 12% of respondents, reflecting ongoing challenges in variant validation and clinical interpretation. Limited access to clinical geneticists was also reported by 12.5% of centers. Ethical and administrative challenges were cited by 16.67%, including ethical issues related to consent, complex administrative procedures for reimbursement, and the absence of clearly defined eligibility criteria for genetic testing.

Concerns regarding the management of secondary or incidental findings were reported by 4.17% of respondents. Limited integration of genetic testing into healthcare systems, particularly through insufficient adoption of standardized coding systems, was reported by 12.5% of participants. Challenges related to the dissemination and accessibility of genetic testing for patients outside of referral centers were reported by 8.33%. Finally, a lack of dedicated staff for the analysis and interpretation of genetic test results was reported by 8.33%, contributing to delays in diagnostic reporting.

Outcomes of genetic testing

Among the 51 respondents, all respondents (100%) recognized the value of genetic counseling as the primary benefit of genetic testing, underscoring its role in tailored patient support and family guidance. Diagnostic closure was reported by 98.04% of respondents, while changes in management were noted by 94.12%, reflecting the impact of genetic results on treatment personalization. Precision therapy and avoidance of unnecessary investigations were highlighted by 82.35% of respondents, demonstrating the utility of genetic testing in streamlining diagnostics and guiding targeted therapeutic strategies.

Reimbursement

Genetic testing was fully covered by national/regional health services in 86% of centers and partially by private insurance in 6.34%. Two centers reported research funding as a source of support for genetic testing. Five centers (Cyprus, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, and Croatia) reported that patients and families may be required to contribute to the cost of genetic testing. This contribution coexisted with other reimbursement mechanisms in four centers, whereas one center reported patient‐funded testing only.

DISCUSSION

The survey confirms that genetic testing is now available in the vast majority of EpiCARE centers, marking a significant advancement in the diagnostic pathway for people with rare and complex epilepsies. Most centers reported access to key diagnostic modalities, including gene panels and exome sequencing. The progressive integration of genetic testing into routine clinical care reflects its growing importance in patient management and the recognition of its role in shaping precision medicine strategies in rare and complex epilepsies.

At the same time, several important challenges persist:

Variability in testing approaches and resources

There is marked heterogeneity in the availability and use of genetic testing strategies across centers and countries. While targeted gene panels are the most commonly used due to their efficiency and cost‐effectiveness, WES is increasingly adopted for more complex phenotypes, such as DEEs and NDDs. WGS, however, remains less frequently used, likely due to limited access and higher costs. In addition, the turnaround times were not aligned and varied from 1 to over 12 months, showing the difficulty of some centers to accelerate such results. This variability was also observed within an individual country, emphasizing the lack of harmonized national frameworks.

Rapid genetic testing is prioritized for neonates and NICU/PICU patients with severe, unexplained, or drug‐resistant seizures, aligning with the need for early intervention in these critical cases. The advances of precision medicine and related targeted therapies support this approach and underscore its central role in the management of these patients. However, the risk of being “left behind” with very long turnaround times in older patients or patients with less severe phenotypes may disadvantage these groups where precision therapies are also available.

Expanding rapid testing to additional patient groups, where precision medicine could significantly impact outcomes, should be explored and decreasing the turnaround time that goes beyond 6 months should be a key objective.

Diagnostic methods and clinical impact

Our findings reveal a preference for gene panels and WES over single‐gene testing, mainly in complex conditions like DEEs and EIDEEs. Conversely, single‐gene testing remains highly specific to specific PMEs, where known monogenic etiologies drive targeted approaches in the context of specific phenotypes.

In surgical candidates, genetic testing is increasingly moving beyond peripheral blood sequencing, particularly in focal cortical dysplasia and other structural epilepsies, where resected brain tissue may be informative. The increasing recognition of somatic mosaicism in focal epilepsies supports this approach. Emerging approaches using CSF or tissue material obtained from SEEG electrodes may further expand the role of genetic testing in structural epilepsies. 10 , 11

The impact of genetic testing on clinical care is well‐established: nearly all respondents reported that results supported diagnostic confirmation, limited unnecessary investigations, and introduced in a high number of cases significant changes in treatment plans.

Multidisciplinary collaboration and genetic counseling

The successful implementation of genetic testing goes beyond test availability; it relies on the expertise required for optimal indication and interpretation.

These findings also illustrate the multidisciplinary nature of genetic counseling pathways in many centers, where responsibilities are often shared between neurologists and clinical geneticists rather than attributed to a single professional group. Structured multidisciplinary discussions, including experts in both genotype and phenotype, are essential for accurate interpretation, particularly in cases involving VUS or atypical presentations. Our results suggest that such MDT meetings are lacking or under‐resourced in several centers. Strengthening collaborative expertise through dedicated staffing, regular meetings, and training programs is a key priority. In addition, these MDT meetings are mainly based on (pediatric) neurologists and clinical geneticists but often lack genetic counselors, bioinformaticians, and laboratory scientists that may have a key role in this setting. The exchange of expertise within EPICARE centers could play a major role to strengthen this multidisciplinary collaboration sharing additional expertise to the countries and the centers where it is still lacking.

Financial and logistical barriers

In a significant proportion of centers, financial constraints continue to hinder access to genetic testing. One in four respondents cited insufficient reimbursement, including insufficient public coverage, or the need for patient self‐payment. Although most EpiCARE centers reported full reimbursement through national or regional healthcare systems, five centers indicated that patient contribution to testing costs may still be required. These findings highlight persistent healthcare‐system inequalities in access to genomic medicine across the EU.

Such disparities may contribute to diagnostic delays, delayed access to precision therapies, and reduced access to specialized multidisciplinary expertise and clinical trial participation. Although our survey did not demonstrate systematically longer laboratory turnaround times in centers where patient contribution may be required, delays in access to testing before samples are submitted may still occur. In addition, laboratory turnaround times exceeding 6 months were reported by several centers, regardless of reimbursement model, further limiting the timely implementation of genetic findings into clinical care. Although these downstream consequences were not specifically assessed in the present survey, they are likely to contribute to inequalities in patient care across Europe.

Overcoming these barriers will require national and European‐level efforts to secure funding, ensure reimbursement, and streamline access to genetic diagnostics. ERN EpiCARE can support these efforts by providing expertise, encouraging case discussions, and supporting education in this field, facilitating cross‐border collaboration, promoting harmonized referral pathways and genomic infrastructures, and advocating for more equitable access across Europe. Collaboration with patient representatives (EpiCARE Patient Advocacy Group; ePAGs), national health authorities, and European genomic initiatives will also be essential to promote sustainable reimbursement policies and reduce inequalities in access to genetic testing across Europe.

Data management and codification systems

Despite the availability of registries in 64% of centers, only 45.71% of these are interoperable with the ERN EpiCARE registry, and 73.47% of centers lack access to disease‐specific European or international registries. This highlights the need for harmonized coding systems, such as Orphanet or ICD‐10 codes, and the development of robust, interoperable databases. Such systems are vital for tracking outcomes, facilitating research, and fostering collaboration across networks. They are also crucial for timely patient identification, particularly for emerging clinical trials.

The limited awareness and use of platforms like SOLVE‐RD (49%) and European initiatives such as the 1+ Million Genomes Project (8%) suggest opportunities for increased participation in these collaborative efforts. Among centers reporting reimbursement‐related barriers, participation in collaborative research initiatives such as EPI25 and EuroEPINOMICS was reported by some institutions, illustrating that research collaborations may provide additional opportunities for selected patients. However, these initiatives depend on research participation or specific eligibility criteria and therefore cannot replace sustainable and equitable access to routine clinical genetic testing within national healthcare systems. Promoting greater integration into international collaborative initiatives, together with interoperable registries and harmonized coding systems, could enhance data sharing, accelerate genetic discoveries, and improve access to genomic medicine for people with epilepsy.

Future perspectives

Emerging technologies including long‐read sequencing, transcriptomics, multi‐omics integration, and AI‐assisted variant interpretation are expected to further improve diagnostic yield and variant classification in epilepsy genetics. These developments align with the recently defined ERN EpiCARE research priorities, 12 which emphasize genetics, targeted therapies, AI‐supported diagnostics, and harmonized European data infrastructures as key areas for future progress. In parallel, interoperable registries and genomic infrastructures may facilitate data sharing, accelerate research collaborations, and improve equitable access to precision medicine across Europe. These infrastructures are particularly relevant for rare and complex epilepsies, where fragmented datasets and small patient populations remain major barriers.

The role of ERN EpiCARE

EpiCARE plays a central role in supporting rare and complex epilepsy care across Europe. Beyond offering expert consultation and case review, the network facilitates knowledge exchange, promotes best practices, and strengthens collaboration between centers with differing levels of resources. Through initiatives such as the epiREG registry 13 and collaborative genetic research platforms, 14 EpiCARE can support harmonized data collection, improve patient identification, and facilitate multicenter research across Europe.

The active involvement of ePAGs is essential to ensure that genetic information, counseling, and care pathways remain aligned with the needs of patients and families, and to support advocacy toward more equitable national policies. EpiCARE actions in education through courses, workshops, and webinars, research coordination, registry development, and advocacy for equitable care are vital to reducing disparities in access and ensuring consistent policy for genetic etiologies' diagnostic quality across EU.

Limitations

Some methodological considerations should be acknowledged. The survey was limited to ERN EpiCARE centers, which are highly specialized epilepsy referral institutions. The findings therefore likely reflect best‐case practices in Europe, while access to advanced genomic technologies, multidisciplinary expertise, and structured genetic pathways may be more limited in non‐reference centers and lower‐resource settings.

The high center‐level response rate (46/50 centers; 92%) supports the representativeness of the survey within ERN EpiCARE, although non‐response bias cannot be entirely excluded. As the study was based on self‐reported practices and designed as a descriptive survey, no inferential statistical analyses were performed. The findings provide a descriptive overview of current practices across ERN EpiCARE centers and should not be interpreted as formal comparative analyses between countries or healthcare systems.

CONCLUSION

Exploring the scientific, logistical, financial, and systemic barriers to genetic testing is the cornerstone to support the development of a coordinated action at both national and European levels. Expanding access to testing, emphasizing the need for rapid testing, reinforcing multidisciplinary expertise, ensuring sustainable reimbursement models, and strengthening interoperable data infrastructures are critical priorities. Leveraging the expertise and collaborative capacity of ERN EpiCARE will be instrumental in achieving equitable access to high‐quality genetic diagnostics and optimizing outcomes for patients with genetic epilepsy.
