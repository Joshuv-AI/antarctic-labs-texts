Molecular effects of transposable element sequences in mammalian cells
Authors: Ming-Ching C Wen, Joshua D Welch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransposable elements (TEs) are often epigenetically repressed in eukaryotic cells, but still affect the molecular state of the cell in certain contexts. A flurry of recent studies have elucidated new effects of TE sequences in eukaryotic cells. We review these emerging molecular effects of TEs, including a variety of new mechanisms by which TE sequences affect the cell, including pre- and post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression; cell-to-cell transmission of genes within a multicellular organism through virus-like activity; and RNA-guided DNA insertion. Recent demonstration of TE-guided genome editing underscores the importance of these investigations for both basic and translational research. Future work is needed to continue to unravel the molecular effects of TE sequences across developmental stages, across cell types, and in various diseases.

Background

Transposable elements (TEs) are ubiquitous in mammalian genomes, occupying nearly 50% of the human genome [1–3] and 30% of the mouse genome [4, 5]. Such sheer abundance indicates the potential for TEs to influence the cell. Since TEs were first characterized [6], great strides have been made in annotating and identifying these repeated sequences in different genomes. More recent efforts have focused on identifying molecular mechanisms of TE activity in regulating gene expression [7].

In general, TE activity is silenced by heterochromatinization at TE locations [8] or specific repressive factors, such as SABF proteins which have been shown to prevent retrotransposition and the exonization of TEs in order to maintain splicing integrity [9]. Nevertheless, TE activity can be de-repressed under certain biological conditions. Mounting evidence reveals the consequences of TE activity in development [10–12], cancer [13–15], aging, and age-related degenerative disease [16, 17]. In recent years, multiple papers have reviewed varied aspects of TE activity, including the regulatory function of TEs in mammalian transcription [18, 19]; roles of TEs in disease and aging [7, 8, 20]; TEs in chromatin organization [21]; and TEs in development [22, 23]. The last three years have seen a flurry of exciting new discoveries about how TEs function in mammalian cells. These remarkable studies have largely not been reviewed. Here, we offer an up-to-date review of the molecular mechanisms by which TEs may alter gene expression and shape the molecular states of cells. We close by discussing important future directions and their potential for advancing our understanding of human disease and providing new tools for genome engineering.

TEs can affect gene expression at the pre-transcriptional stage

Mammalian gene expression is under combinatorial control of multiple pre- and post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. Recent studies have shed new light on how TEs exert multifaceted effects on gene expression at the pre-transcriptional stage.

TEs function as enhancers

TEs can often serve as regulatory elements in different genomic contexts [18, 24]. In a recent study on immune cell tissue adaptation, Simon and colleagues investigated the TE topology of immune cells acquired from various tissues and found that TEs– particularly long terminal repeats (LTRs)– reside in enhancer regions and are related to tissue adaptation. In addition, they noted a distinct, cell-type-dependent accessibility pattern, an organ-specific accessible chromatin pattern and tissue regulatory T cell adaptation program in the TE landscape. These findings offer a potential for future organ-specific immune interventions [25].

Primate-specific TEs, especially ERVs, contain transcription factor (TF) binding sites for lineage-specific TFs, which activate expression during human gastrulation [11]. Such ERV-enriched TF binding sites also play a role in later stages of embryonic development. TEs function as enhancers to promote the transcription of KRAB-zinc finger proteins only in specific cell types, which in turn govern lineage-specific activity of TE-inserted regulatory sequences [11]. Also, regulatory sequences containing TE insertions may regulate the subsequent steps of embryonic development by recruiting TFs, such as GATA6 and SOX17, that determine germ layers [11]. In a separate study on hippocampal neurogenesis, species-specific ERVs and SINE-VNTR-Alu (SVA) elements were also found as candidate enhancers to regulate gene expression for human and chimpanzee intermediate progenitor cells [26]. ERVs can contain a well-defined cis-regulatory element structure, such as their own RNA polymerase II promoters [27], which could explain their consistent regulatory effects as enhancers on the expression of developmental genes.

Additionally, studies have shown how TEs regulate the expression of oncogenes as onco-exapted enhancers [28, 29]. Recently, LTRs were found to be significantly enriched in the active enhancer regions in colon cancer cells, liver cancer cells, and retinal pigmented epithelial cells. Distinct TEs as enhancers were found to bind to different TFs in a cell-type-specific manner. For example, while TE enrichment arrangement was noticed to be similar in both liver and retinal cells, abundance of specific TE types, such as MER61 and LTR10, decreased after knocking out p53 in colon cancer cells [14]. Furthermore, comparing colon cancer and liver cancer cells indicated both shared and cell type-specific TE-derived enhancers. Differentially upregulated TE subfamilies in each respective cell type were found to be more similar to the TE family consensus sequence than the shared TEs. These findings suggest that cancer cells can exploit TEs as new enhancers to trigger transcriptomic alterations in epigenetically modified cells.

The role of LTRs as pervasive enhancers to boost human gene expression has been well established [26, 30–33]. In addition to LTRs, long interspersed nuclear element-1 (LINE-1) elements can exhibit enhancer activity. Remarkably, LINE-1 also shows widespread effects on distal gene expression (Fig. 1a) [34]. Similarly, the male-specific lethal (MSL) complex specifically acetylates histone 4 lysine 16 (H4K16ac) at LINE-1s and ERVs in some cell types, enabling these TE subfamilies to perform enhancer-like functions. These activated subfamilies often are located at topologically associated domains (TAD) and form loops with genes. Using CRISPR to epigenetically perturb and genetically delete LINE-1s showed the involvement of H4K16ac-marked LTR and LINE-1s in regulating gene transcription in cis, suggesting that H4K16ac-enriched TEs may preserve chromatin structures that result in cis-regulatory function at specialized genomic locations [35].

Interestingly, TEs may sometimes attenuate enhancers to exert repressive effects, even though TEs with RNA polymerase II promoters often increase expression of nearby genes. Recent work by Kojima and colleagues identified TE-insertion expression quantitative trait loci–TEs with varying insertion positions across the human population whose presence or absence correlates with a change in gene expression [36]. Among the mobile element variants that correlated with gene expression changes and overlapped likely enhancer regions, the majority of them (30 out of 45) negatively regulate nearby genes. This suggests that the insertion of TEs into enhancers mostly attenuates enhancer activity. An Alu element occurs in distal enhancer-like elements between genes DGKE and TRIM25. This insertion by Alu is the primary variant in expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) of DGKE, and shows high linkage disequilibrium with the primary variant in one of the TRIM25’s eQTLs. For both eQTLs, Alu insertion attenuates enhancer activity as evidenced by the association between the haplotype that contains Alu insertion and downregulated gene expression.

In addition to the examples listed above demonstrating a few key targets regulated by TEs, studies have identified roles for TEs in shaping the overall gene expression landscape more broadly [37, 38]. In human cells, Alu elements tend to increase gene expression, while other types of TEs tend to reduce gene expression. The extent and direction of gene expression changes regulated by TEs depend on the type of TE and the genomic context (promoter vs. UTR) and whether the TE is species-specific. These studies suggest that, in general, species-specific TEs seem to play a larger role in regulating gene expression than TEs shared across many species.

TEs compete with super-enhancer loci for transcriptional condensates

A remarkable recent study [39] demonstrated a new molecular mechanism by which transcripts from ERVs compete with super-enhancers to rearrange transcriptional condensates (Fig. 1b). When ERVs are repressed, transcriptional condensates containing RNA Pol II, mediator coactivator, transcription factors, and transcripts nucleate around super-enhancer loci. However, when ERVs are de-repressed in embryonic stem cells, they reshape the genomic distribution of transcriptional condensates, disabling super-enhancer-driven transcription of pluripotency genes impacting normal development, and possibly resulting in disease.

TEs function as promoters

TEs can function as cryptic promoters, providing an initiation site for RNA polymerase. Once polymerase initiates at such a cryptic TE promoter, it can continue to transcribe or splice into downstream protein-coding genes (Fig. 1c). These chimeric products from TE promoters are termed TE-chimeric transcripts. A recent study [41] using Smart-seq5 + reveals that all TEs, including DNA transposons, can drive the formation of chimeric transcripts with prevalent transcriptional activity mostly regulated by LINE, SINE and LTR families during embryonic genome activation. The study further shows that many of these TE insertions possess promoter-like transcription start sites (TSSs) to initiate chimeric transcripts. LTRs and SINE elements use sharp TSSs to initiate chimeric transcripts whereas LINEs show global effects through antisense transcription. Multiple LTRs are specifically upregulated in embryonic development, but some of them are downregulated afterward. Overall, these findings not only reveal the effects of TEs on global gene expression but delineate different expression properties of TEs in mammalian embryonic development.

In cancers, LTR and SVA families are the most frequent contributors to TE-chimeric transcripts. Lower global methylation levels of LTRs and SVAs correlate with more frequent TE-chimeric transcription [13]. Furthermore, tumor-specific TE-chimeric transcripts can contribute to protein translation and TE-chimeric transcripts involving membrane proteins can be used as immunotherapy targets. With long-read sequencing technologies, TE transcripts, including previously uncharacterized transcripts, have been found to encode novel proteins and antigens. Because different tumors often use different cryptic TE promoters, tumors produce many unique TE-chimeric transcripts. These TE-chimeric transcripts in turn can produce tumor-specific antigens at the cell surface, thus offering promise for targeted tumor therapy.

A recent paper by Jang and colleagues demonstrates the viability of implementing epigenetic treatment to induce TE-chimeric antigens in glioblastoma [42]. The study used CRISPR activation to enable targeted histone acetylation of TE-chimeric transcripts to reactivate TE promoters and subsequently produce tumor-specific antigens in glioblastoma.

TEs facilitate enhancer-promoter interactions through RNA-RNA interactions

TEs can facilitate 3D chromatin folding, which is important for gene regulation. TEs may give rise to species-specific and cell-type-specific 3D chromatin looping and gene expression by shifting enhancer-promoter loops at the sub-TAD level [43]. Species-specific TE activity can trigger the deposition of new CTCF binding sites and facilitate novel 3D genome folding (TAD or a loop) in a species-specific manner that rewires local gene regulation. Notably, about half of the human-specific enhancer-promoter loops involving TEs have been shown to bring a stronger yet distal enhancer close to an active promoter [44]. By contrast, a recent whole-genome unbiased screen found that TEs function as CTCF-motif-free DNA sequences to modulate genome folding and exert stronger impacts on adjacent chromatin interactions than regular CTCF sites. Moreover, TE insertion, especially Alu and endogenous retrovirus-K (ERVK) elements, changed chromatin contacts, with most changes not overlapping with CTCF binding sites [45].

A recent study identified a striking new function for TEs in facilitating enhancer-promoter selectivity by forming RNA-RNA interactions [40]. Liang et al. developed a new genome-wide sequencing method for detecting interactions among noncoding RNAs from enhancers and promoters. While the main purpose of the study was to look at these RNA-RNA interactions in an unbiased fashion rather than to investigate TEs, they found a striking overlap between RNA-driven enhancer-promoter interactions and TEs. In fact, 38% of all RNA-RNA enhancer-promoter interactions overlapped with Alu sequences. The interacting Alu transcripts tended to have complementary RNA sequences and had strong potential to form RNA-RNA duplexes, based on RNA secondary structure prediction (Fig. 1d). To functionally validate the effects of Alu on enhancer–promoter looping, the authors performed both Alu insertion and tethering mediated by CRISPR–dCasRx. Either insertion or tethering of Alu elements was sufficient to forge new loops for previously unregulated promoters and homologous enhancers. Inserting an Alu family sequence at the FGF22 gene produced a >10-fold increase in long-range looping with SE351. Conversely, knocking out Alu at the enhancer site substantially decreased promoter-promoter loops between SE351 and CNN2. Although a super enhancer hub, CCAT1-5 L, was previously reported to modulate long-range chromatin looping via interaction with MYC promoters [46], a recent study [40] demonstrates the unique role of Alu elements in mediating these interactions.

TEs can affect gene expression at the post-transcriptional stage

The role of TEs in some types of post-transcriptional regulation is well-established [47]; nevertheless, recent studies have identified additional mechanisms of post-transcriptional gene regulation by TEs. Lately, the involvement of LINE-1 at the post-transcriptional stage during cortical development was reported [12]. LINE-1s encode long non-coding RNAs that coalesce efficient translation factors to support cellular growth during corticogenesis. In addition, LINE-1s are essential for proper transcription during differentiation, maturation, and migration of neurons along the cortical plate. Therefore, corticogenesis can be affected by LINE-1 silencing and LINE-1 can remodel neuronal transcriptional and chromatin landscapes by compromising cell maturation. LINE-1 also exerts post-transcriptional effects by forming double-stranded products that trigger hybrid immune responses. A LINE-1 subfamily element named Lx9c11, residing in the remote promoter area of Schlafen 1 (Slfn1) was found to regulate early immune response after viral infection. Lx9c11 non-coding RNA hybridizes with the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) and the 3’ UTR of Slfn1 to function as natural antisense transcripts to post-transcriptionally modulate virus response genes. Deletion of Lx9c11 in mice resulted in excessive expression of multiple immune genes of the Slfn family. These observations demonstrate Lx9c11’s post-transcriptional modulation of the immune response to viral infection, avoiding excessive immunoreactivity [48]. Similarly, Alu exonization generates isoforms of interferon receptors to mediate immune signaling in human cells [49]. Furthermore, the exonization of TEs was found to reshape protein secondary structure by boosting α helix content to facilitate the translation of functional proteins [50].

In human primary cardiac precursor cells, mammalian-wide interspersed repeats (MIR) TEs play a crucial role in smooth muscle cell specification. A conserved long non-coding RNA controlling smooth muscle cell specification, CARMEN-201, contains an alternatively spliced exon with a MIRc element. CARMEN-201 is transcribed and vital during the process of cardiogenesis [51]. The MIRc element forms an RNA-protein interaction with a transcriptional repressor, REST (RE1 Silencing Transcription Factor). At the same time, the CARMEN-201 transcript forms RNA-DNA interactions near key cardiogenic genes, such as ISL1, IRX1, IRX5, and SFRP1. This effectively tethers the REST repressor near these genes, blocking transcription of cardiomyocyte genes (Fig. 2a), which in turn pushes cells toward the smooth muscle cell lineage.

TEs from the Alu SINE family were also recently found to have post-transcriptional regulatory significance in tumorigenesis [52]. Inverted Alu repeats embedded in 3’ UTRs post-transcriptionally silence genes responsible for signaling pathways by moving them into paraspeckles, preventing their nuclear export and translation (Fig. 2b). Additionally, 3’ UTR shortening caused by the exclusion of inverted Alu repeats performs gene silencing to suppress p53 activation in lung adenocarcinomas. Global UTR lengthening in neural progenitor cells employs inverted Alu repeats at 3’ UTR to forge neuronal cell-specific regulation at the post-transcriptional stage. Notably, the majority of the genes exhibiting neural progenitor cell-specific gene silencing are associated with the development of neurodegenerative disease.

TEs can also modulate splicing and polyadenylation (Fig. 2c). A human-specific SVA retrotransposon located upstream of agouti signaling protein gene (ASIP) has been found to create a premature polyadenylation site, resulting in decreased expression of the ASIP protein-coding sequence [53]. However, a second SVA element, if present, can base-pair with the first, reducing the amount of splicing into the SVA element and producing more of the protein-coding ASIP transcript. The SVA insertions in ASIP vary across the human population, suggesting that the insertion of SVA in an early intron of ASIP may have repeatedly altered human skin pigmentation. In addition, the junctions between TEs and exons exhibit tumor-specific splicing variants and encode peptides on MHC-1 [54]. The peptides derived from the TE-exon junctions hold the potential for immunization to curb tumor growth. Similarly, intronic LINE-1 elements maintain T cell quiescence by creating non-canonical isoforms of genes that normally would activate CD4 + T cells. As shown in a recent study, LINE-1-containing transcripts found in naive CD4 + T cells are controlled by a TF named IRF4. Dysfunctional T cells triggered by tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes accumulated transcripts containing LINE-1, but reducing the amount of these LINE-1 transcripts restored tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte effector function [55].

While these new pieces of evidence reveal tumor-specific proteins or peptides derived from TE transcripts in multiple cancers, interestingly, another study demonstrates increased TE transcription after chemotherapy to induce melanoma differentiation-associated proteins 5 (MDA5) to release inflammatory cytokines. As a result, hematopoietic stem cells are forced to exit quiescence and initiate hematopoietic regeneration [56]. The findings imply that while TEs are abundantly expressed during tumorigenesis, treatments may involve TE expression to trigger the needed inflammatory mechanism for hematopoietic regeneration.

Virus-like TE activity enables cell-to-cell gene transmission within an organism

It has long been appreciated that viral infection and integration into a genome provides a mechanism of horizontal gene transfer across species [57, 58]. A subset of retrotransposons in mammalian genomes still retain protein-coding capacity and–like viruses–are capable of cell-to-cell gene transfer [59]. In fact, horizontal gene transfer of both retrotransposons and DNA transposons across species has been reported in mammals [60–62].

However, there is growing appreciation of the important role that TEs play in cell-to-cell transfer within the same multicellular organism through virus-like activity. Our current review thus focuses on the recent results elucidating such within-organism gene transfer activity by TEs, rather than on the more well-studied horizontal gene transfer across species. It was previously demonstrated that a human endogenous retrovirus, HERV-K (HML-2, or HK2), remains capable of producing virus particles. HK2 can package viral RNAs and dispatch them to other cells [63].

The neuronal gene, Arc, bears considerable similarity to a retrotransposon. It has long been established that Arc is crucial for mammalian long-term memory and synaptic plasticity [66] and can perform retroviral-like behavior to traffick a signal from presynaptic site to postsynaptic point [67]. Nevertheless, the precise function of Arc was only recently discovered [68]. Intriguingly, like Gag, Arc proteins assemble into virus-like capsids that encapsulate the Arc RNA. Cells then release these capsids as extracellular vesicles for intercellular transmission of Arc RNA in an activity-dependent fashion (Fig. 3a) [64]. Because Arc is an immediate early gene (rapidly transcribed and translated when neurons fire), its cell-to-cell transmission may promote the local spreading of neuron activity. Arc shares similar molecular characteristics with the elements found within viral Gag proteins. Yet, it was recently discovered that Arc capsids are secreted through a different pathway; that is, Arc capsids assemble on phosphatidylinositol-3- phosphate (PI3P) lipid-enriched endosomal and multivesicular body membranes. By contrast, HIV Gag assembles at and sprouts from the plasma membrane [69].

In addition to transferring mRNA through extracellular vesicles between neurons in the healthy brain, Arc proteins and mRNA are also found in extracellular vesicles produced by glioma cells, suggesting that the RNA transfer mechanism of Arc could impact cancer progression [70]. In other types of cancer, TEs also transfer among human cells [71, 72]. In multiple cancer types, circulating tumor DNA released from tumor cells is taken up by other cells, transported into the nucleus, and integrated into the genome [71]. Interestingly, a recent study showed that these circulating tumor DNA sequences are preferentially recognized and taken up by cells of the same tumor type that the DNA originated from [71]. Multiple TEs, including ERV-Ls, SINEs (Alu), LINEs, and LTRs present in circulating tumor DNA, show this selective uptake. For example, AluSp and MER11C transposons enable myeloma circulating tumor DNA to efficiently enter myeloma cells, but not other types of cancerous cells. A recent study further demonstrated that in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), LINE-1, HSATII, and human ERVs are packaged into extracellular vesicles (EVs) which are secreted into the tumor microenvironment [72]. These repeat-containing EVs are taken up by nearby recipient cells, and induce cell-type-specific transcriptional changes. The uptake results in transcriptomic alterations and loss of cell identity in the recipient cells. These discoveries highlight that TE sequences perform virus-like behavior to mediate horizontal intercellular gene transfer within the same organism without viral vectors.

Another Gag homolog, PEG10, also forms virus-like particles. It binds its own mRNA and boosts vesicular secretion of the RNA cargo [73]. In fact, PEG10 virus-like particles can be harnessed to transfer programmed cargo mRNAs flanked with UTR packing signals. Feng Zhang’s lab engineered PEG10 to package, secrete, and deliver specific RNAs. Thus, PEG10 may be an efficient modality for therapeutic delivery. However, highly elevated PEG10 expression during neurodevelopment may impair neuronal migration, implying its involvement in brain development [74]. In addition to RNA, PEG10 also binds to ataxia-related proteins and is secreted via extracellular vesicles. In Angelman syndrome, a neurodevelopmental disorder, the absence of neuronal UBE3A gene promotes PEG10 protein levels, suggesting PGE10 to be a target of human UBE3A in Angelman syndrome pathology [74].

Mammalian ERVs contain virus-like components and are largely repressed. Nonetheless, the de-repression of ERVs has been detected in aging tissues and organs of mammals. Specifically, HERVK (HML-2) can enable the transcription of viral genes and the production of retrovirus-like particles that trigger innate immune responses in aging cells [65]. Furthermore, the transmissible aging message produced by the HERVK virus-like particles can produce premature senescence phenotypes even in young cells. Neutralization with the anti-HERVK-Env antibody can abrogate cellular senescence induced by HERVK retrovirus-like particles (Fig. 3b) [65]. Thus, ERVs can be a biomarker of aging and a druggable therapeutic target for alleviating aging. Notably, ERVs are not the only type of TEs involved in aging. Multiple reports have shown that LINE-1 activation is also associated with aging and age-related degenerative conditions [34, 75, 76].

TEs also enable intracellular gene transfer during development. A recent study revealed that an LTR retrotransposon inserted upstream of the Fgf8 gene can bypass epigenetic repression, adopt enhancers and other regulatory properties from its neighboring genome, and co-express with Fgf8 [77]. When the LTR is transcribed, the LTR retrotransposon’s Gag assembles into virus-like particles within cells where Fgf8 is expressed. The virus-like particles can then enter neighboring cells of the developing limb. The presence of virus-like particles triggers apoptosis, causing digit loss [77]. Unlike classic cell autonomous effects caused by a TE inserting into the wrong part of the genome, this study indicates that LTRs can spread to neighboring cells in a non-cell-autonomous fashion by forming virus-like particles. These particles can ultimately exert significant effects on the organism, such as digit loss during organogenesis.

TEs can perform RNA-guided insertion

Targeted gene integration is an area of active research in the burgeoning field of genome editing. While most TEs do not exhibit sequence specificity in their integration site preference, some elements have recently been shown to interact with RNA molecules that guide them to specific integration sites. In fact, guided TE insertion has been demonstrated even in mammalian cells [78, 79]. Insertion sequences (ISs) are abundant autonomous bacterial TEs that only encode genes needed for transposition. Among ISs, IS200- and IS605-family transposons encode TnpB proteins. IS200/IS605-encoded TnpB proteins show high similarity to class 2 type-V CRISPR effectors based on phylogenetic analyses [80] and are among the most compact genomic editors [81]. The compact TnpB transposon-encoded endonuclease uses a single long noncoding RNA (ωRNA) to recognize target-adjacent motifs (TAMs) similar to the protospacer-adjacent motifs recognized by Cas proteins. With the guidance of right-end element RNA, TnpB recognizes a TAM site and triggers the cleavage of target DNA loci that are complementary to the ωRNA guide, suggesting that the TnpB-ωRNA system can be optimized for gene editing efficiency [82]. Remarkably, recent findings have confirmed that these transposon-encoded endonucleases can perform guided insertion in mammalian cells [78, 83, 84]. In fact, an optimized ISDra2-TnpB design that extends the original targeting range has considerably enhanced the efficiency of gene editing in mammalian cells [81]. Recently, a computational model used neural networks to predict ωRNA efficiency for TnpB prior to in vivo gene-editing experiments, thereby facilitating the design of optimal ωRNAs [81].

An optimized CRISPR-associated transposon system termed “evoCAST” was recently developed to offer high-efficiency and precise RNA-guided insertion of kilobase-sized DNA into human genomes without double-strand breaks [88]. EvoCAST uses a CRISPR targeting module to target DNA sites with an RNA guide and employs components of a bacterial TE, Tn7, to catalyze the integration of a donor DNA sequence at a fixed position downstream of the R-loop. By engineering Tn7’s key proteins, EvoCAST achieves consistent integration at varying loci with minimal indels and off-target effects.

Fanzors are TE-encoded proteins similar to TnpB IS200/ IS605 in eukaryotes, interspersed with TnpBs, and share similar structures with TnpB and Cas12 [89]. Fanzors can cleave DNAs using the flanking sequence of the transposon 3’ end region to function as a guide sequence to steer Fanzor to their target location. While TnpBs and Fanzors share many similarities [90], recent reports have shown that Fanzor proteins insert a unique R loop to join the TAM and the guide sequences, resulting in DNA unwinding. Such a strategy differs from those used by TnpB and Cas12 proteins; the former basically utilizes the inherent DNA properties and the latter employs a short lysine helix-loop to unwind DNA [79, 85, 89]. These studies demonstrate that as compact RNA-guided DNA endonucleases, Fanzors have the capacity to program RNA-guided DNA cleavage in a TAM-specific and target-site-focused fashion (Fig. 4a). In addition to the structural and functional properties, because of their small size, Fanzors are considered better options for convenient delivery into human cells than the far larger CRISPR/Cas endonucleases [80].

IS200 and IS605 transposons not only encode various TnpB-family proteins, they also use a ‘peel-and-paste’ mechanism catalyzed by TnpA transposases. As shown in a recent study [91], co-expression of TnpA and TnpB retains much greater transposon activity, compared with TnpA-only expression. Moreover, TnpA and TnpB jointly encourage more frequent recombination that exceeds what TnpB alone can generate. These observations show how TnpA-mediated excision/integration transposases and RNA-guided TnpB nucleases synergize to increase their activity.

A subsequent study by the same group [92] showed that IStron family elements encode introns with homing endonucleases, which facilitate intron mobilization by cleaving and recombining DNA at predefined target locations. IStron transcripts indicate multifaceted functionality of transposon-encoded noncoding RNAs.

IS110 is another TE that exerts a “cut-and-paste” mechanism to remove itself from the genome and generates a unique circular product. Distinct properties of IS110–including a DEDD catalytic motif, an RuvC-like domain, and a Tnp domain with a well-conserved serine residue [93, 94]--suggest that IS110 may adopt a different mechanism of action. Indeed, a recent discovery revealed that the IS110 locus encodes a single-protein RNA-guided RNA recombinase with a bispecific guide molecule termed a “bridge RNA”. The bridge RNA contains two internal binding looping structures individually identifying the donor DNA section and the target insertion site of IS110 DNA (Fig. 4b) [94, 95]. Assisted by the IS110 recombinase, this bi-specific feature of the bridge RNA promotes DNA recombination by complementary base pairing between the donor and target DNA sequences. Notably, in the bridge RNA, each binding loop can be separately engineered to attach to and re-incorporate disparate DNA sections, which allows flexible, modular programming of DNA reorganization by inserting, inverting or excising targeted sequences. In fact, it was recently demonstrated that the four ingredients (bridge RNA, recombinase, donor DNA and target DNA) suffice to construct the intended recombination object [94]. Notably, bridge editing has not yet been demonstrated in eukaryotes, though it may be possible to engineer IS elements for human cells, opening exciting new therapeutic strategies.

Like IS110, IS111 also has a non-coding region. The non-coding region determines a short seeker RNA which copurifies with transposase. The seeker RNA plays a pivotal role in transposition of IS110 and IS111 or another payload molecule. If another payload is used, it must match both part of the IS and part of the target region to be edited. Just like IS110, the IS111 element has a seeker RNA that matches both strands of the target DNA site, though the strand-specific matches are in the opposite order for IS110 and IS111. Given these observations, seeker RNA may be programmable to guide target selection by IS110 and IS111 [96]. The advantage of employing the IS110/IS111 and seeker RNA system is its simplicity: Only a single modest-sized protein and a short seeker RNA are required.

Retrotransposon insertion can likewise mediate integration of the targeted gene in mammalian cells. Zhang and colleagues designed a gene delivery protocol involving template RNA that encodes a transgene and a messenger RNA encoding the R2 protein that guides transgene insertion into a multicopy safe locus [97]. The R2 protein recognizes the target site, performs single-strand cleavage, and ultimately inserts the payload by enabling DNA synthesis from the RNA template. These R2-based strategies have several advantages, including comparably inexpensive, fast assembly and flexibility of RNA-only delivery with the additional strength of no extra-genomic DNA, avoiding provoking detrimental genetic mutations and intrinsic immune activation. Similarly, a recent study showed that R2 retrotransposons, which are non-LTR retrotransposons, can integrate specifically into 28 S rDNA loci. The authors further engineered the R2 protein and donor RNA to enhance R2-mediated gene integration efficiency and on-target integration specificity (Fig. 4c) [87].

Remarkably, a recent study demonstrates that R2 elements, including R2Tg and R2Tocc, packaged in a module system, termed Site-specific Target-Primed Insertion through Targeted CRISPR Homing of Retroelements (STITCH), can be harnessed and reprogrammed for precise and scarless gene insertion at new genomic locations [98]. Specifically, in STITCH, R2 retrotransposons are fused to a CRISPR-Cas9 nickase to reprogram the insertion site by retargeting the homology arms of the RNA payload as well as by Cas9 nicking directed by the guide RNA. STITCH can modify sequences as small as one nucleotide or as large as 12.7 kilobases. This new method establishes a paradigm for gene insertion tools that do not require systems derived from bacteria. Another similar study used R2Tg to engineer an all-RNA system for DNA insertion [99], which is different from the STITCH system that involves CRISPR–Cas9 nickase fusion. These works developed a compact, high-efficiency, and RNA-only retrotransposon platform with menial scars in mammalian cells based on mechanistic understanding of RNA-guided TE insertion.

Discussion and conclusion

Accumulating evidence has indicated the versatile mechanisms by which TE sequences may reshape the molecular environments of cells, including at pre-transcriptional and post-transcriptional stages of gene expression. Although these mechanisms may contribute to disease in some contexts [13, 15, 17, 100], in other contexts, TEs may serve helpful roles [48, 56]. While many such mechanisms have already been discovered, many more likely remain as yet uncharacterized. It is interesting to note that many of these recent discoveries involve molecular interactions that remain challenging to measure in high throughput, including RNA-protein interactions, RNA-DNA interactions, and RNA-RNA interactions (and sometimes two or three of these types of interactions at once). As experimental technologies for measuring these interactions improve, we expect more discoveries of molecular functions for TEs.

In addition to experimental efforts to elucidate TEs’ mechanisms, computational modeling has achieved impressive results in predicting TnpB ωRNA efficiency and designing IS200/IS650 elements [81, 101]. Moreover, the improvement in computational prediction of protein structure and protein-nucleic acid interactions from sequence also has great promise for identifying new molecular roles for TEs. More comprehensive investigation engaging multi-omic sequencing techniques would be useful to further our understanding of TE activity across cell types, developmental stages, and disease conditions. Computational re-analysis of publicly available bulk and single-cell sequencing datasets also holds great promise for unraveling TE epigenetic and transcriptional features across organisms, tissues, cell types, developmental stages, and disease states.

Recent work on the pre- and post-transcriptional effects of TEs in human and mouse cells has primarily focused on LTR, LINE, or SINE families. In contrast, many recent studies using TEs for RNA-guided insertion primarily examined DNA transposons. The demonstrated precision and efficiency of RNA-guided TE insertion techniques mentioned in our review appear promising. Yet, the discovery that such RNA-guided TE insertion mechanisms have been operating in nature raises interesting questions about the role such mechanisms may have played in organismal adaptation. In addition, for biological systems rich in cellular and molecular heterogeneity, to what extent the same RNA-guided TE insertion techniques can be applied is largely an unanswered question. All of these questions are of particular importance considering that TE-driven guided-RNA insertion could be a useful tool for gene therapy. Hence, more structural and functional exploration of TE-derived guided RNA mechanisms is urgently needed.

Lately, there have been some intriguing studies on virus-like activity of TEs that transfer molecules between cells within the same organism. As with the discovery of RNA-guided TE insertion, this raises interesting questions about how widespread and significant the effects of cell-to-cell transmission are within physiological and disease conditions. Additionally, little is known about the cellular states or conditions under which TEs can mediate such cell-non-autonomous effects, which is an open question for future research. New experimental technologies such as spatial transcriptomics and spatial proteomics measure the locations of RNA and proteins within tissues. At least one study has already used spatial transcriptomics to identify cell crosstalk mediated by extracellular vesicles carrying TE-derived transcripts [77]. Further analyzing TE-derived transcripts and proteins with this type of data is an interesting direction for future work and may reveal new examples of TEs and cellular contexts in which virus-like activity plays a crucial role.

Finally, there is a growing interest in leveraging our understanding of these molecular TE mechanisms to develop new therapies for human disease. For example, increased antigen repertoire was found after TE activation by epigenetic treatment in glioblastoma, suggesting that epigenetically promoted TE-elicited antigens could be a potential target for immunotherapy [42]. Moreover, as mentioned previously, recent studies have shown that TEs hold promise for efficient and safe RNA-guided gene editing. TEs such as Fanzor, IS110, and R2–which can perform RNA-programmable targeted insertion–have great potential for genetic engineering and genetic therapies. Bridge editing in particular could open the door to new types of genome editing, but must first be adapted to eukaryotic and mammalian cells. Additional future endeavors are necessary to improve the accuracy and safety of these editing approaches.
