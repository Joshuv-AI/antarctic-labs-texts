A dual-approach analysis of parenting styles and their differential impact on proactive and reactive aggression in Chinese adolescents
Authors: Annis Lai Chu Fung, Weiye Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile parenting style is a well-established correlate of adolescent aggression, prior research has rarely compared specific parenting behaviours across distinct proactive and reactive aggression profiles. The present study addressed this gap by integrating specific parenting behaviours and proactive and reactive aggression within both subgroup-based and variable-centred analytic frameworks.MethodsA total of 1,300 Chinese adolescents and their parents from four middle schools in Hong Kong completed questionnaires. Adolescents were classified into non-aggressive, reactive, proactive, and co-occurring reactive-proactive groups using a composite classification based on standardized parent- and self-reported aggression scores. Parenting was assessed at both the global style level (authoritative, authoritarian, and permissive) and the behaviour level (warmth and acceptance, reasoning and induction, democratic participation, physical coercion, verbal hostility, and non-reasoning or punitive discipline). Subgroup-based multivariate analyses of covariance were conducted to compare parenting patterns across aggression groups, followed by hierarchical regression models predicting proactive and reactive aggression from parenting variables.ResultsSubgroup-based analyses showed that, compared with non-aggressive adolescents, aggression groups generally experienced more maladaptive parenting patterns, especially higher authoritarian and permissive parenting. The proactive group reported the lowest levels of positive parenting, whereas the co-occurring reactive-proactive group showed the highest mean levels on several negative parenting dimensions, although overlap among aggression groups remained substantial. Variable-centred analyses further showed that parenting explained substantially more variance in parent-reported than in self-reported aggression. Across parent-reported models, permissive parenting, physical coercion, and non-reasoning or punitive discipline emerged as the most consistent positive correlates of both reactive and proactive aggression.ConclusionsTogether, these findings suggest that parenting-aggression associations are overlapping but not uniform across adolescent aggression profiles. Compared with broad parenting styles, behaviour-level indicators provided a more specific account of the parenting practices associated with aggression. These results identify potentially modifiable family correlates that may help guide future longitudinal and intervention research on adolescent aggression.

Introduction

Adolescent aggression is an important public health and developmental concern [1, 2]. In Chinese contexts, aggression remains common among adolescents, with a recent large-scale study reporting a 26.4% prevalence of self-reported aggression [3]. Thus, aggression is better understood as a dimensional pattern of behavioural variation rather than a purely clinical phenomenon in community samples. Furthermore, different subtypes of aggression may be associated with distinct family correlates and therefore require different explanatory frameworks. Existing evidence suggests that family processes, particularly parenting, are consistently associated with adolescent aggression [4, 5]. However, much of this literature has examined aggression as a global construct without differentiating its subtypes, while relying solely on variable-centred approaches without identifying distinct aggression profiles. Meanwhile, most studies have treated parenting merely as broad global styles without pinpointing specific parenting behaviours, leaving it unclear whether these behaviours are differentially associated with distinct aggression subtypes. Therefore, the present study focuses on proactive and reactive aggression, examining how both global parenting styles and specific parenting behaviours are associated with these patterns among Chinese adolescents in Hong Kong.

Proactive and reactive aggression are widely regarded as functionally distinct subtypes of aggressive behaviour [6, 7]. Reactive aggression is typically defined as an impulsive, affectively charged response to perceived provocation or threat, whereas proactive aggression is more deliberate, goal-directed, and instrumental [8, 9]. This distinction is theoretically important because it implies different underlying cognitive processes. Within the Social Information Processing (SIP) theory, reactive aggression has been linked to biased encoding and interpretation of social cues in the early stages of information processing, particularly hostile attribution bias, which is the tendency to infer hostile intent in ambiguous situations [8, 10]. By contrast, proactive aggression has been linked more closely to later stages of processing, including instrumental goal selection and positive outcome expectancies for aggression [8]. This distinction has also been widely supported in Chinese samples, with relevant measures demonstrating acceptable psychometric properties and distinct correlates for the two subtypes [11, 12]. Furthermore, it is particularly relevant to parenting research because different parenting behaviours may shape different stages of social information processing, thereby contributing to different aggression subtypes.

Parenting has traditionally been conceptualised in terms of three broad styles: authoritative, authoritarian, and permissive [13]. Authoritative parenting is typically characterised by warmth, reasoning, and democratic participation; authoritarian parenting by harsh control, verbal and physical coercion, and punitive discipline; and permissive parenting by low structure, low monitoring, and indulgent or inconsistent limit-setting [14, 15]. In the context of aggression, these styles are relevant not simply as broad family climates, but because their behavioural components may map onto the different developmental processes underlying the two subtypes of aggression. Harsh and punitive parenting may heighten threat sensitivity, frustration, and hostile interpretations of others’ behaviour, making it especially relevant to reactive aggression [8, 16]. Similarly, coercive parenting may model aggression as a legitimate means of control, thereby strengthening instrumental beliefs relevant to proactive aggression [17–19]. By contrast, warmth, reasoning, and inductive discipline may support emotion regulation, empathy, and more accurate social-cognitive processing, which are associated with a reduction in both subtypes of aggression [5, 20, 21]. Permissive parenting may operate through a different route, as insufficient supervision and weak behavioural boundaries can undermine self-regulation, although the specific mechanisms and expected subtype-specific patterns remain less clear [1, 22].

Empirical evidence broadly supports the relevance of parenting behaviours to both aggression subtypes, but the literature remains inconsistent. Authoritarian parenting has demonstrated the most consistent positive association with both proactive and reactive aggression across samples and age groups [12, 23–26]. Authoritative parenting is generally associated with lower levels of aggression, but findings are more mixed, with some studies reporting significant negative associations [5, 12, 27, 28] and others finding weak or non-significant relations [25, 26]. Permissive parenting is the least well-understood. Some accounts suggest that under-regulation and weak supervision may be associated with higher aggression, particularly where rule internalisation is poor [1, 22], whereas other findings suggest that permissive parenting may not uniformly predict maladjustment and may operate differently depending on cultural context, developmental stage, or whether low control is accompanied by warmth versus disengagement [26, 29]. Accordingly, permissive parenting should not be treated as a residual category included merely because it appears in a parenting measure. Instead, it represents a theoretically important but underspecified domain requiring further empirical clarification.

Potential variation by gender and reporter also warrants consideration in interpreting parenting-aggression associations, but both issues are best treated cautiously. Boys generally report higher levels of aggression than girls, but evidence on whether parenting-aggression associations differ by gender is mixed [12, 30–32]. Likewise, associations between parenting and aggression may depend on whether aggression is reported by adolescents or by parents, partly because self-reported aggression may be more vulnerable to underreporting, whereas parent reports may be more sensitive to such observable behaviour [5, 33]. Such discrepancies may also vary by aggression subtype, as reactive aggression is often more overt, whereas proactive aggression may be less readily observable in some contexts. These issues are relevant to interpretation, but because prior findings are inconsistent, they are treated here as exploratory rather than central theoretical predictions.

Based on the previous literature, three main research gaps can be identified. First, most prior studies either treat aggression as a single construct without differentiating between the two subtypes or examine proactive and reactive aggression solely through variable-centred approaches. Such approaches are informative but do not reveal whether adolescents with different aggression profiles (e.g., predominantly reactive, predominantly proactive, or co-occurring reactive-proactive elevations) also differ in their parenting environments. This limits the identification of family factors associated with adolescents showing elevated aggression profiles in community samples. Second, much of the literature focuses on global parenting styles rather than concrete behaviours, such as verbal hostility and emotional warmth. It therefore remains important to test whether specific parenting behaviours account for different aggression subtypes, because such an approach can provide a more granular understanding of the precise family processes associated with distinct aggressive responses. Third, as previously noted, the relation between permissive parenting and the two subtypes of aggression remains unclear due to a paucity of research and inconsistent findings. Therefore, further evidence concerning permissive parenting is required to better elucidate its potential association with aggression. Together, these gaps highlight the need for a research design that combines group-based comparisons with behaviour-level modelling of parenting.

Against this background, the present study examined how parenting styles and specific parenting behaviours are associated with proactive and reactive aggression among Chinese adolescents in Hong Kong. To address the gaps outlined above, the study combined group-based comparisons across four aggression profiles (non-aggressive, reactive, proactive, and co-occurring reactive-proactive) with variable-centred regression analyses of continuous aggression scores. It aimed to clarify whether parenting-aggression associations are more clearly captured at the level of broad styles or specific behaviours, and whether these associations differ across aggression subtypes and subgroup profiles. In addition, exploratory analyses examined whether the observed associations varied by gender and compared parent- and self-reported aggression.

Three main hypotheses guided the study. First, relative to non-aggressive adolescents, those with elevated aggression profiles were expected to experience less adaptive parenting overall, specifically higher levels of authoritarian parenting and lower levels of authoritative parenting. Second, adolescents with a co-occurring reactive-proactive aggression profile were expected to show the most maladaptive parenting profile, given prior evidence that co-occurring aggression is associated with broader psychosocial difficulties and more severe maladjustment [1]. Third, at the variable-centred level, coercive parenting behaviours were expected to show stronger and more consistent positive associations with both aggression subtypes than global styles, whereas warmth, reasoning, and democratic participation were expected to show negative associations. Finally, given the inconsistencies in previous research, results involving permissive parenting, as well as reporter and gender differences, were treated more cautiously and examined in an exploratory manner.

Methods

Participants

A total of 1300 adolescents (744 boys, 556 girls; age range = 11–17 years, M = 13.03, SD = 1.08) from four randomly selected middle schools in Hong Kong and one of their parents or caregivers participated in the study. The four schools were randomly selected from a pool of 50 secondary schools that had previously participated in the authors’ related research on child and adolescent aggression. Within each selected school, all Chinese students in Grades 7 to 9 were invited to participate.

One parent or caregiver completed the parent questionnaire for each participating adolescent. Among parent respondents, 920 (70.8%) were biological mothers, 237 (18.2%) were biological fathers, 42 (3.2%) were other caregivers (e.g., stepparent, adoptive parent, grandparent, or other relative), and 101 (7.8%) did not report their relationship to the adolescent.

Both students and parents were informed that the study aimed to understand the behaviours and needs of adolescents in Hong Kong. Participation was voluntary. Parental consent and student assent were obtained through a reply slip attached to the school notice before questionnaire administration. Students and parents were also informed that all data would be collected anonymously and treated confidentially. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the City University of Hong Kong. Written consent was obtained from school principals, vice-principals, relevant administrators, parents, and students.

Measures

Reactive and proactive aggression

Both students and parents completed the Reactive-Proactive Aggression Questionnaire (RPQ: [9]; Chinese version: [11]). The RPQ is a 23-item questionnaire assessing reactive aggression (11 items, e.g., “reacted angrily when provoked by others”) and proactive aggression (12 items, e.g., “hurt others to win a game”) on a 3-point scale (0 = never, 1 = sometimes, 2 = often). Sum scores were computed for reactive, proactive, and total aggression. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alphas were 0.86 (total), 0.85 (proactive), and 0.79 (reactive) for child-report, and 0.86 (total), 0.87 (proactive), and 0.81 (reactive) for parent-report.

Parenting styles and behaviours

Parents completed the Parenting Styles and Dimensions Questionnaire-Short Form (PSDQ-32: [15]; Chinese version: [34]). The PSDQ-32 is a 32-item parent-report instrument rated from 1 (never) to 5 (always), yielding three global parenting styles: authoritative (15 items), authoritarian (12 items), and permissive (5 items) parenting. Subscales include warmth/acceptance (5 items), reasoning/induction (5 items), and democratic participation (5 items) within authoritative parenting; physical coercion (4 items), verbal hostility (4 items), and non-reasoning/punitive (4 items) within authoritarian parenting; and permissive/indulgent subscale (5 items). Sum scores were computed for each subscale and for the three global styles. In this sample, internal consistency was acceptable to good: authoritative-warmth/acceptance α = 0.85, reasoning/induction α = 0.84, democratic participation α = 0.82, total α = 0.90; authoritarian-physical coercion α = 0.73, verbal hostility α = 0.76, non-reasoning/punitive α = 0.71, total α = 0.78; permissive α = 0.61.

Data analysis

Missing data were minimal (maximum item-level missingness = 1.2%; all measures < 5%). Given the low level of missingness, estimates were expected to remain unbiased [35].

For subgroup-based analyses, adolescents were classified using a multi-informant, rule-based composite classification strategy. Parent- and child-reported reactive aggression scores were first standardized across the full sample and then averaged to form a composite reactive aggression index. The same procedure was used to form a composite proactive aggression index. High aggression on a given dimension was defined as a composite standardized score of + 1 SD or greater [6]. Adolescents were then classified into four mutually exclusive groups: reactive-proactive (RPA; z ≥ + 1 on both dimensions), reactive (RA; z ≥ + 1 on reactive only), proactive (PA; z ≥ + 1 on proactive only), and non-aggressive (NA; z < + 1 on both dimensions). Group sizes were NA = 1,102 (84.8%), RA = 72 (5.5%), PA = 59 (4.5%), and RPA = 67 (5.2%). To examine the robustness of this subgrouping strategy, alternative classifications based on parent-only and child-only aggression scores were also inspected, yielding highly similar overall patterns of subgroup differences.

To compare parenting across aggression subgroups, we first conducted two multivariate analyses of covariance (MANCOVAs). The first MANCOVA examined the three global parenting styles (authoritative, authoritarian, and permissive), and the second examined the six specific parenting behaviours (warmth and acceptance, reasoning and induction, democratic participation, physical coercion, verbal hostility, and non-reasoning or punitive discipline). In both models, aggression group (NA, RA, PA, RPA), sex, and school were entered as fixed factors, and age was included as a covariate. To account for potential school-level clustering, school was modelled as a fixed factor rather than a higher-level random effect in all subgroup comparisons, as students were nested within only four schools. Furthermore, although the composite classification reduced reliance on a single informant, parenting remained parent-reported, and subgroup differences should therefore not be interpreted as entirely free of reporter-related variance.

Given the unequal subgroup sizes and the observed violation of the equality of covariance matrices assumption, Pillai’s Trace was used as the primary multivariate test statistic because it is generally more robust to such violations [36]. When the multivariate main effect was significant, follow-up univariate ANCOVAs were examined for each parenting outcome. Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were then used to identify group differences. Partial η2 values are reported as indicators of effect size.

To examine variable-centred associations between parenting and aggression, we conducted hierarchical multiple linear regressions for reactive and proactive aggression using both child- and parent-reported outcomes. Because students were nested within four schools, we first estimated intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) for each aggression outcome. ICCs were 0.061 and 0.073 for child-reported reactive and proactive aggression, and 0.015 and 0.023 for parent-reported reactive and proactive aggression, indicating modest school-level clustering for child-reported outcomes and low clustering for parent-reported outcomes. Given the small number of schools, we addressed school-level dependency by including school as a set of fixed-effect dummy variables in all regression models.

For each outcome, age, sex (female = 1, male = 0), and school dummy variables (with School 1 as the reference group) were entered in Block (1) Parenting predictors were then entered simultaneously using the Enter method in Block (2) To address potential multicollinearity between global parenting styles and their constituent behaviours, we estimated two complementary model families: (a) total-score models including authoritative, authoritarian, and permissive parenting totals; and (b) behaviour-level models including warmth and acceptance, reasoning and induction, democratic participation, verbal hostility, physical coercion, non-reasoning or punitive discipline, and permissive parenting. The total-score models were intended to capture broader parenting climate, whereas the behaviour-level models were used to identify the specific practices underlying those associations.

For all regression models, we report model R², adjusted R², change in R² relative to the covariate-only models, and unstandardized coefficients (B), standardized coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals, and t values. Prior to modelling, we inspected bivariate correlations and examined Durbin-Watson and variance inflation factor (VIF) values to assess serial autocorrelation and multicollinearity. All analyses were conducted in SPSS 31.

Results

Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations), group means, and bivariate correlations for all key variables are presented in Table 1. Prior to the subgroup analyses, assumptions relevant to the multivariate models were examined. For both MANCOVAs, Box’s M tests were significant, indicating that the assumption of equality of covariance matrices across groups was violated (global styles: Box’s M = 246.27, F(144, 8450) = 1.50, p < .001; parenting behaviours: Box’s M = 656.50, F(441, 13572) = 1.21, p = .002). Given the unequal subgroup sizes, Pillai’s Trace was therefore used as the primary multivariate criterion. Levene’s tests were non-significant for all follow-up ANCOVA outcomes except physical coercion (p = .011), indicating that homogeneity of variance was met for most outcomes. For the regression analyses, Durbin-Watson values ranged from 1.89 to 2.08, and VIF values ranged from 1.02 to 3.30, indicating no substantial concerns regarding serial autocorrelation or multicollinearity.

MANCOVAs

Two MANCOVAs were conducted to examine whether parenting differed across the four aggression groups while controlling for sex, school, and age. For the global parenting styles model, there was a significant multivariate main effect of aggression group, Pillai’s Trace = 0.07, F(9, 3801) = 9.34, p < .001, partial η² = 0.022. The multivariate effects of sex and the aggression group × sex interaction were not significant, whereas age was significant, Pillai’s Trace = 0.031, F(3, 1265) = 13.35, p < .001, partial η² = 0.031. Furthermore, the multivariate effect of school was small and at the threshold of significance, Pillai’s Trace = 0.013, F(9, 3801) = 1.88, p = .05, partial η² = 0.004. For the specific parenting behaviours model, there was again a significant multivariate main effect of aggression group, Pillai’s Trace = 0.080, F(18, 3792) = 5.81, p < .001, partial η² = 0.027. As in the global styles model, the multivariate effects of sex and the aggression group × sex interaction were not significant, whereas age was significant, Pillai’s Trace = 0.051, F(6, 1262) = 11.32, p < .001, partial η² = 0.051. Again, the multivariate effect of school was small and not statistically significant, Pillai’s Trace = 0.023, F(18, 3792) = 1.60, p = .051, partial η² = 0.008. These findings indicate that parenting varied significantly across aggression subgroups even after accounting for demographic and school-level differences. Furthermore, the significant effect of age indicates that parents of older adolescents reported lower scores across all parenting dimensions.

Follow-up univariate ANCOVAs are summarized in Table 2, and Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons are shown in the post-hoc column of Table 1. Across the global parenting styles, the aggression-group effect was significant for authoritative parenting, F(3, 1267) = 7.83, p < .001, partial η² = 0.018, authoritarian parenting, F(3, 1267) = 18.53, p < .001, partial η² = 0.042, and permissive parenting, F(3, 1267) = 14.11, p < .001, partial η² = 0.032. The post-hoc comparisons indicated that adolescents in the PA group reported significantly lower authoritative parenting than those in the NA and RA groups, whereas the RPA group did not differ significantly from the other groups on the total authoritative score. In contrast, the NA group reported significantly lower authoritarian parenting than all three aggression groups, and significantly lower permissive parenting than the RA and RPA groups.

A similar pattern emerged for the specific parenting behaviours, as the main effect of aggression group was significant for all of them: warmth and acceptance, F(3, 1267) = 10.11, p < .001, partial η² = 0.023; reasoning and induction, F(3, 1267) = 2.97, p = .031, partial η² = 0.007; democratic participation, F(3, 1267) = 8.45, p < .001, partial η² = 0.020; verbal hostility, F(3, 1267) = 8.51, p < .001, partial η² = 0.020; physical coercion, F(3, 1267) = 17.09, p < .001, partial η² = 0.039; and non-reasoning or punitive discipline, F(3, 1267) = 19.41, p < .001, partial η² = 0.044. Post-hoc comparisons showed that adolescents in the PA group reported significantly lower warmth and acceptance and democratic participation than those in the NA and RA groups, and significantly lower reasoning and induction than those in the NA group. For the authoritarian dimensions, the NA group consistently reported less maladaptive parenting than the aggression groups, as it scored significantly lower than all three aggression groups on physical coercion and non-reasoning or punitive discipline, and significantly lower than the RA group on verbal hostility. In addition, the RA group reported significantly lower non-reasoning or punitive discipline than the PA group.

Overall, the subgroup-based analyses revealed a clear pattern: compared with non-aggressive adolescents, those in the aggression groups generally experienced more maladaptive parenting, particularly in terms of authoritarian and permissive parenting. Within the aggression groups, the PA group tended to report the lowest levels of positive parenting, whereas the RPA group showed the highest levels on several negative parenting dimensions. Pairwise comparisons also indicated that the subgroup differences were clearer for contrasts between NA and aggression groups than for contrasts among the aggression groups themselves. However, although the aggression group effects were statistically significant, most effect sizes were small to modest in magnitude, warranting cautious interpretation of the results.

Across outcomes, parenting variables explained substantially more variance in parent-reported aggression than in child-reported aggression. Relative to the covariate-only models, the addition of parenting variables increased explained variance by 0.103 to 0.183 in parent-reported models, compared with only 0.012 to 0.021 in child-reported models. Thus, parenting-aggression associations were markedly stronger when aggression was rated by parents than when it was self-reported by adolescents. However, this discrepancy should be interpreted cautiously, as reporter-specific variance may also contribute to these differences.

For parent-reported reactive aggression, the total-score model explained 19.7% of the variance (Adjusted R² = 0.197), with authoritarian parenting (β = 0.23) and permissive parenting (β = 0.26) emerging as significant positive predictors. The behaviour-level model provided a slightly better fit (Adjusted R² = 0.199), indicating that these associations were primarily driven by permissive parenting (β = 0.25), verbal hostility (β = 0.12), physical coercion (β = 0.08), and reasoning and induction (β = 0.09). These results suggest that both low-structure parenting and coercive discipline were associated with higher levels of parent-reported reactive aggression.

For parent-reported proactive aggression, the total-score model explained 12.2% of the variance (Adjusted R² = 0.122). In this model, authoritarian parenting (β = 0.20) and permissive parenting (β = 0.08) were positively associated with aggression, whereas authoritative parenting was negatively associated with aggression (β = − 0.16). The behaviour-level model showed better fit (Adjusted R² = 0.145) and indicated that higher proactive aggression was associated primarily with non-reasoning or punitive discipline (β = 0.18), physical coercion (β = 0.15), and permissive parenting (β = 0.10), whereas democratic participation (β = − 0.09) and verbal hostility (β = − 0.10) showed negative associations when all parenting behaviours were entered simultaneously.

In contrast, the child-reported models showed much smaller effects. For child-reported reactive aggression, the total-score model explained 6.7% of the variance overall (Adjusted R² = 0.067), but parenting variables contributed only a small increment beyond the covariates (ΔR² = 0.012). Within this model, authoritarian parenting was the only significant positive predictor (β = 0.07). The behaviour-level model explained a similarly small proportion of variance (Adjusted R² = 0.068; ΔR² = 0.015), and no individual parenting behaviour emerged as a clearly robust unique predictor after simultaneous entry.

For child-reported proactive aggression, the total-score model explained 8.4% of the variance overall (Adjusted R² = 0.084), with parenting variables contributing a modest increment beyond the covariates (ΔR² = 0.013). In this model, authoritative parenting was negatively associated with aggression (β = − 0.09). The behaviour-level model explained 9.0% of the variance (Adjusted R² = 0.090; ΔR² = 0.021), and this association was primarily accounted for by warmth and acceptance, which was negatively associated with proactive aggression (β = − 0.13). No other behaviour-level predictors were significant.

Taken together, the regression analyses suggest two broad patterns. First, parenting variables were much more strongly associated with parent-reported than with child-reported aggression. Second, behaviour-level models generally provided equal or slightly better fit than total-score models and offered a more specific account of which parenting practices were associated with aggression. In particular, permissive parenting, physical coercion, and non-reasoning or punitive discipline emerged as the most consistent positive correlates in the parent-reported models, whereas the child-reported models showed only weak and selective associations.

Discussion

The present study examined how parenting styles and specific parenting behaviours are associated with proactive and reactive aggression among Chinese adolescents using both subgroup-based and variable-centred analyses. Across the subgroup analyses, differences were clearer between non-aggressive adolescents and aggression groups than among the aggression groups themselves. Within the aggression groups, adolescents in the proactive subgroup tended to report the lowest levels of positive parenting, whereas adolescents in the co-occurring reactive-proactive subgroup showed the highest mean levels on several negative parenting dimensions. The variable-centred analyses further indicated that parenting variables were much more strongly associated with parent-reported than self-reported aggression, and behaviour-level models provided a more specific account of these associations than global-style models, with permissive parenting, physical coercion, and non-reasoning or punitive discipline emerging as the most consistent positive correlates of both reactive and proactive aggression.

Subgroup-based findings

The subgroup-based analyses suggest that maladaptive parenting was more characteristic of adolescents showing elevated aggression than of their non-aggressive peers. In particular, non-aggressive adolescents generally reported lower levels of authoritarian parenting than the aggression groups. This pattern is broadly consistent with prior work linking harsh and coercive parenting to elevated reactive and proactive aggression [12, 23, 25, 26]. At the same time, the present findings also suggest that subgroup differences were not categorical. Rather than revealing fully distinct parenting environments for each aggression subtype, the data point to overlapping but differently weighted parenting correlates across proactive, reactive, and co-occurring aggression subgroups.

Within this general pattern, the co-occurring reactive-proactive subgroup showed the highest mean levels on several negative parenting dimensions. This finding is compatible with a cumulative-risk perspective, in which exposure to multiple adverse family processes may be associated with broader behavioural maladjustment [37]. However, the observed overlap across pairwise comparisons also indicates that this group should not be interpreted as categorically distinct from the reactive-only or proactive-only subgroups. The present data support stronger relative maladjustment in the co-occurring subgroup, but they do not establish a unique developmental pathway.

The most differentiated positive-parenting pattern emerged for the proactive subgroup, which reported lower levels of warmth and acceptance, democratic participation, and, to a lesser extent, reasoning and induction than the non-aggressive and reactive groups. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that lower parental warmth, reduced participatory family processes, and weaker inductive guidance may be more closely associated with goal-oriented and instrumental forms of aggression than with reactive aggression alone. This interpretation aligns with prior work suggesting that proactive aggression may be more closely tied to social-learning processes and to weaker internalization of prosocial norms [12, 25, 26]. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the present analyses identify subgroup differences in parenting correlates but not distinct etiological mechanisms, and the differences between the reactive and proactive groups were modest rather than absolute.

A related implication is that lower positive parenting did not characterize all aggression subgroups to the same degree. In the current data, reactive aggression appeared to be differentiated more clearly by elevated negative parenting than by reduced positive parenting. This is consistent with the view that harsh, coercive, and poorly regulated parenting may be more salient correlates of reactive aggression than the mere absence of warmth or democratic family interaction [5, 12]. However, the present cross-sectional design does not permit conclusions about developmental precedence. Thus, the findings are best understood as showing that different aggression profiles are associated with partially different constellations of parenting experiences, rather than with fully separate developmental pathways.

Although boys reported higher overall aggression, sex did not meaningfully moderate subgroup-based parenting patterns. This aligns with evidence that sex differences are more robust in aggression than in parenting [12, 32], indicating that parenting differences across aggression subgroups are broadly similar for both sexes.

Variable-centred findings

A notable finding was the large discrepancy between parent- and child-reported aggression models, with parenting variables explaining substantially more variance in parent-reported aggression. Rather than indicating greater validity, this likely reflects informant effects, as shared reporter variance inflates associations when parents report both parenting and aggression. Conversely, the weaker associations in child-reported models may also reflect differences in perspective, salience, or willingness to disclose aggressive behaviours [33, 38]. Ultimately, these findings highlight that conclusions regarding parenting-aggression links depend heavily on the chosen informant.

With that caution, the parent-reported models still provide useful information about how parents’ own reported parenting practices co-occur with their perceptions of their children’s aggression. Across those models, permissive parenting, physical coercion, and non-reasoning or punitive discipline emerged as the most consistent positive correlates of aggression. These findings extend prior work suggesting that aggression is associated not only with overt harshness, but also with family environments characterized by weak behavioural boundaries and inconsistent regulatory practices [5, 12, 26].

Another implication of the variable-centred analyses is that behaviour-level models were often slightly more informative than the global-style models. This suggests that the explanatory value of parenting styles may lie largely in the specific practices they contain. In the present data, coercive and punitive behaviours were more consistently associated with aggression than were broad style labels. At the same time, some coefficients became weaker, changed direction, or lost significance when conceptually related parenting behaviours were entered simultaneously. This pattern suggests conceptual overlap among parenting practices rather than simple one-to-one mappings between single behaviours and single aggression outcomes. Accordingly, the current results are more consistent with overlapping behavioural correlates than with sharply differentiated subtype-specific predictors.

Finally, the cultural context of the study remains important, but it should be interpreted cautiously. Parenting practices such as control, harsh discipline, and family hierarchy have long been discussed as salient features of Chinese family socialization [12, 39]. The present findings are broadly consistent with prior work suggesting that coercive and low-structure parenting may be meaningful correlates of adolescent aggression in Chinese contexts. However, since the study did not directly test cultural values or social change processes, cultural interpretations should remain contextual and conservative.

Implications

At the level of screening relevance, the subgroup-based analyses suggest that adolescents with elevated aggression profiles, especially those showing co-occurring reactive and proactive aggression, may warrant closer attention in future prevention-oriented work. The results do not justify clinical classification, but they do suggest that aggression profiles are not identical in their parenting correlates and that subgroup differentiation may be useful when identifying adolescents with greater family-related difficulty patterns associated with aggression.

At the level of intervention hypotheses, the results point to several parenting dimensions that appear especially worthy of attention in future intervention development. Across the variable-centred analyses, permissive parenting, physical coercion, and non-reasoning or punitive discipline emerged as the most consistent positive correlates of aggression, whereas positive parenting dimensions such as warmth, democratic participation, and authoritative parenting were more selectively associated with lower aggression. These findings suggest that low-structure and coercive parenting processes may represent plausible targets for future family-based or school-linked intervention development, although the present findings did not test causal effects and more applied recommendations still need future experimental or longitudinal research.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the study was cross-sectional, and the observed associations cannot establish directionality. Parenting may be associated with adolescent aggression, but adolescents’ aggressive behaviour may also shape how parents respond to them over time [40]. Second, although the subgroup-based analyses no longer relied on a single informant, parenting remained parent-reported, and the composite classification still partly incorporated parent-reported aggression. Thus, the subgroup findings should not be interpreted as entirely free of reporter-related variance and may still partly reflect shared perceptual biases. Similarly, the stronger variable-centred associations in the parent-reported models may partly reflect informant effects rather than stronger behavioural prediction. Third, the subgroup comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because the aggression subgroups remained relatively small compared with the non-aggressive group, which may have reduced stability and power for some pairwise comparisons. Finally, the reliability of the permissive parenting scale was modest, which should remain prominent because permissive parenting played an important conceptual role in the study and emerged as a consistent correlate in several analyses. The present findings therefore suggest that permissive parenting may be relevant, but they do not remove the need for continued measurement refinement.

These limitations highlight several critical directions for future research. A primary priority is longitudinal, multi-informant research to clarify the temporal ordering and reciprocal associations between parenting and aggression. Replication using independent informants for subgroup classification is also crucial to further mitigate concerns regarding shared reporter variance. Additionally, experimental or intervention-based studies are needed to determine whether specific, behaviour-level parenting processes serve as effective targets for reducing adolescent aggression. Finally, broader investigations into instrument refinement, cross-cultural comparisons, and differences in parent roles would be highly valuable.

Conclusions

Using both subgroup-based and variable-centred analyses, the present study examined how parenting styles and specific parenting behaviours are associated with proactive and reactive aggression among Chinese adolescents in Hong Kong. The findings suggest that parenting-aggression associations are overlapping but not uniform across aggression profiles. Compared with non-aggressive adolescents, aggression groups generally reported more maladaptive parenting, with the strongest subgroup differentiation appearing in contrasts between non-aggressive adolescents and aggression groups rather than among the aggression groups themselves. Within the aggression groups, proactive aggression was more clearly associated with lower positive parenting, whereas co-occurring reactive-proactive aggression was associated with higher mean levels on several negative parenting dimensions. At the variable-centred level, the findings further suggest that behaviour-level indicators provided a more specific account of parenting-aggression associations than global parenting styles, and that permissive parenting, physical coercion, and non-reasoning or punitive discipline were among the most consistent positive correlates, particularly in the parent-reported models. At the same time, the substantial discrepancy between parent- and self-reported aggression models underscores the importance of informant effects. Overall, the study identifies a set of statistically supported parenting correlates that are compatible with partially differentiated patterns of parenting-aggression association. These findings may help guide future longitudinal, multi-informant, and intervention-oriented research on family processes associated with proactive and reactive aggression.
