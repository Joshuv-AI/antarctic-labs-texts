Agreement between people with obesity and their treating physicians on behavioral and psychological phenotypes in real-world obesity care
Authors: David M Savastano, Pamela Appel, Andrea Leith, Giorgio Castellano, Lewis Harrison, Victoria Higgins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAwareness is growing that body mass index (BMI) alone does not capture the behavioral, psychological and physiological heterogeneity of obesity. We investigate the recognition and alignment of four existing obesity phenotype descriptor domains (emotional hunger, hungry brain, hungry gut, and slow burn) among people with obesity (PwO) and physicians.MethodsThis was a real-world, cross-sectional survey study, which included elements of retrospective data collection. Data were collected from physicians and PwO in the United States between October 2023 and April 2024 as part of the Adelphi Real World Obesity Disease Specific Programme™. Physicians provided clinical data and PwO demographic information; both reported on predefined obesity phenotype descriptors. Agreement between PwO and physician reports was evaluated using Cohen's κ; PwO characteristics were compared using bivariate analyses.ResultsPwO (n = 215) mean age was 48.8 ± 13.9 years, and 69.3% were female. Significant differences between comorbidity prevalence were observed across phenotype descriptors; depression, stress and hypothyroidism being notably common in PwO reporting emotional hunger or hungry brain descriptors. Physicians (n = 151) most commonly reported hungry brain descriptor (52.6%), whereas PwO most commonly reported emotional hunger (45.1%). Patient-physician agreement was slight for hungry brain (κ = 0.176) and hungry gut (κ = 0.113), fair for emotional hunger (κ = 0.207) moderate for slow burn (κ = 0.410). There was significantly greater weight loss among PwO where physicians aligned on the presence of the hungry brain descriptor compared with those who did not align.ConclusionsObesity phenotype descriptors relating to emotional hunger, hungry brain, hungry gut and slow burn may provide a useful framework for characterizing behavioral, psychological and physiological aspects of obesity that are not reflected by BMI. However, PwO-physician recognition and agreement were limited and varied across descriptor domains. Greater concordance on selected descriptors was associated with improved weight-loss outcomes, suggesting value in supporting shared understanding of obesity presentation and management.Keywords: Obesity phenotyping, PwO-physician alignment

Introduction

Obesity is a complex and multifaceted disease, influenced by biological, psychological, and behavioral factors. The prevalence of obesity among adults in the United States (US) reached 40.3% in 2023 [1]. Although body mass index (BMI) has historically been used to diagnose and manage obesity, it is increasingly recognized as insufficient for diagnosing and capturing the full spectrum of obesity-related health risks, as it does not account for differences in body composition [2,3].

To address this complexity, organizations such as the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists, American Diabetes Association and the European Association for the Study of Obesity have suggested definitions of obesity incorporate physical, mental, and social well-being factors to improve diagnostic accuracy [[4], [5], [6]]. The 2025 American Diabetes Association Standards of Care for Overweight and Obesity emphasize a comprehensive, person-centered approach that goes beyond BMI, incorporating the assessment of functional health, comorbidities, individual goals, and strategies to reduce weight stigma [7].

Building on these updates to the clinical guidance beyond BMI assessment alone, an alternative approach to the classification of obesity is detailed in previous research by Acosta et al. and has identified four primary obesity phenotypes based on behavioral, physiological, and psychological characteristics, which includes emotional hunger, hungry brain, hungry gut, and slow burn [8]. Evidence suggests that tailoring obesity treatments according to these phenotypes can improve clinical outcomes [8]. Consequently, phenotype-guided selection of treatment is gaining traction as a more effective and personalized approach to obesity management [8,9].

Shared decision-making relies on a mutual understanding of the underlying factors contributing to disease, and misalignment may lead to poor adherence, frustration, or suboptimal treatment selection. In this context, understanding whether physicians and people living with obesity (PwO) share a common view of obesity phenotypes becomes critical. Given that phenotype-guided interventions can improve clinical outcomes, identifying where physician and PwO perceptions diverge represents an important first step toward closing this gap. To address this, the current study aimed to describe obesity phenotypes as reported by both physicians and PwO, to assess the degree of alignment between physicians and PwO in their identification of these obesity phenotypes, and to evaluate any differences in PwO characteristics in those with an aligned or non-aligned phenotype descriptor.

Methods

Study design and data source

This was a real-world, cross-sectional survey study, which included elements of retrospective data collection. Data were collected from physicians and PwO in the US between October 2023 and April 2024 as part of the Adelphi Real World Obesity Disease Specific Programme (DSP)™. The DSP has been previously described [10,11], validated [12], and demonstrated to be representative and consistent over time [13], and is well-established.

Eligibility criteria

Physicians were eligible for inclusion if they were personally responsible for the management and treatment decisions of a minimum of 10 PwO in a typical month and were accepting of all survey requirements.

Physicians recruited 8 PwO deemed eligible for inclusion if they were aged ≥18 years, had a physician-confirmed diagnosis of obesity (defined as on a weight management program and had a previous or at time of data capture BMI ≥30 kg/m2 or BMI ≥27 kg/m2 with at least one weight-related comorbidity), and were not involved in an obesity clinical trial at the time of data collection. To ensure sufficient representation of PwO using obesity medications, at least two out of the eight PwO included were required to be receiving these.

Data collection

Participating physicians (primary care physicians, internists, endocrinologists, cardiologists, obstetrician/gynecologists, and gastroenterologists) were instructed to complete a questionnaire for their next eight consecutively consulting eligible PwO who visited them for routine care. The physician-reported questionnaire contained questions on demographic and clinical characteristics including height, weight, weight loss attempts, comorbidities, and use of obesity medications. Physicians also selected pre-defined obesity phenotype descriptors for each PwO (see section ‘Identification of phenotypes and descriptions’). BMI was retrospectively categorized.

Each PwO for whom the physician completed a questionnaire for was then invited to complete a voluntary self-reported questionnaire and, upon agreement, provided their informed consent to participate. The PwO-reported questionnaire contained questions on insurance type, educational attainment, and employment status. Socioeconomic status (SES) was retrospectively categorized as low or high based on combined assessment of insurance type, educational attainment, and employment status. PwO receiving Medicaid were automatically classified as low SES, regardless of other factors (see Supplementary Table 1 for full criteria). PwO also self-identified pre-defined obesity phenotype descriptors (details below).

Identification of phenotypes and descriptions

Four phenotypes were applied from previous research: emotional hunger (hedonic eating), hungry brain (abnormal satiation), hungry gut (abnormal satiety), and slow burn (decreased metabolic rate) [8]. In the original framework developed by Acosta et al. [8], phenotypes were identified using objective pathophysiological measurements such as indirect calorimetry and gastric emptying tests. Because such specialized testing is rarely feasible in routine clinical practice, the current study adapted these biological phenotypes into pragmatic, descriptor-based clinical profiles. This approach enabled evaluation of how these underlying physiological concepts are recognized and applied as behavioral archetypes by both physicians and PwO in a real-world setting. The specific descriptors provided to each group are presented in Table 1, with clinical wording from prior research used for physicians and PwO-appropriate language used for PwO surveys. More than one phenotype could be selected per PwO, and as outlined in the original source, due to low counts for some phenotype combinations, these were consolidated into a single ‘Other’ group [8].

Data analysis

To be included in the analysis, PwO were required to have both physician-reported and PwO self-reported phenotype descriptor data. PwO for which either was not available were excluded.

Continuous variables are reported as means with standard deviations (SDs) or median with interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate, and categorical data ware reported as frequency counts and percentages.

Differences in PwO characteristics between phenotype groups were investigated with bivariate analyses, using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and Fisher's exact or chi-square tests for categorical variables.

The level of agreement between PwO- and physician-reported phenotypes was assessed using Cohen's kappa (κ) coefficient. Cohen's kappa accounts for agreement expected to occur by chance and was calculated as:

where Po represents the observed proportion of agreement and Pe represents the expected proportion of agreement due to chance, estimated from the marginal distribution of physician and PwO reported classifications. The kappa statistic was determined between −1 and 1, and interpreted as poor (<0.00), slight (0.00–0.20), fair (0.21–0.40), moderate (0.41–0.60), substantial (0.61–0.80), or almost perfect (0.81–1.00) [14]. A one-sided Z-test was used to assess whether the observed κ values were significantly different from zero. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, with no adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Demographic and clinical differences between aligned and misaligned groups in the context of PwO-physician alignment were also assessed. For these comparative analyses, alignment was treated as a binary variable. The ‘aligned’ group included cases where both the physician and the PwO agreed on the presence or absence of a specific phenotype. The ‘misaligned’ group encompassed all instances of discordance, regardless of whether the phenotype was selected solely by the physician or solely by the PwO. For these comparisons, t-tests were used for continuous variables and Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables.

All analyses were conducted in Stata version 18.5 (Stata Corp LP, College Station, TX, USA) and UNICOM Intelligence Reporter version 7.5 (UNICOM Systems Inc., Mission Hills, CA). Missing data were not imputed; therefore, the base of PwO for analysis could vary from variable to variable and was reported separately for each analysis.

Ethical approval

Data collection was undertaken in line with the European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association (EphMRA) Code of Conduct [15]. This research was submitted to and obtained an exemption from ethical approval from Pearl Institutional Review Board (22-ADRW-136). In addition, each survey was performed in full accordance with relevant legislation at the time of data collection, including the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act 1996 [16], the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act [17], and the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and subsequent revisions.

Participants provided informed consent at the time of data collection using a checkbox. Data were collected in such a way that participants could not be identified directly. Physician and PwO data were pseudo-anonymized, and a code was assigned when data was collected. Upon receipt by the study team, data were pseudo-anonymized again to mitigate against tracing them back to the individual. Data were aggregated and de-identified prior to analysis and publication.

Results

PwO characteristics

Overall, 151 physicians reported data for 215 PwO who had available data for both physician- and PwO-reported phenotype descriptors. PwO characteristics are shown in Table 2. PwO mean age was 48.8 ± 13.9 years, and 69.3% were female. There was a significant difference (p = 0.007) in the proportion of female PwO by phenotype descriptor, ranging from 47.8% in the slow burn with hungry brain group to 86.0% in the hungry brain only group. PwO were majority White (80.5%), employed (85.6%) and of high SES (83.6%) with no significant difference between groups.

The median number of comorbidities reported for PwO varied significantly (p < 0.001) between phenotype descriptor groups, ranging from 1.0 (0.0, 4.0) for hungry brain only to 3.0 (3.0, 5.0) for emotional hunger with hungry brain. There were also significant differences in the prevalence of specific comorbidities (Fig. 1). In psychological comorbidities (Fig. 1a), anxiety was the most commonly reported comorbidity (20.0%), with no significant differences. However, there were significant differences in the prevalence of depression, which ranged from 8.0% in hungry brain only to 41.2% in emotional hunger with hungry brain (p = 0.023) and stress, ranging from 0.0% in slow burn only to 35.3% in emotional hunger with hungry brain (p = 0.008).

In physical comorbidities (Fig. 1b), hypertension (47.0%), dyslipidemia (45.1%) and type 2 diabetes without chronic complications were most commonly reported, with no differences between phenotype descriptors. The only common physical comorbidity with a significant difference in prevalence was hypothyroidism, prevalence of which varied from 4.0% in slow burn only to 35.3% in emotional hunger with hungry brain (p = 0.039). There was also a significant difference in the proportion of PwO with no comorbidities (Fig. 1c), from 0.0% in emotional hunger with hungry brain to 32.0% in hungry brain only (p < 0.001). PwO's most common Charlson comorbidity index was 0 (87.0%, Table 2).

Median BMI at obesity diagnosis was 35.5 (32.5, 39.5) kg/m2, while at time of survey, this was 33.5 (30.3, 37.8) kg/m2. BMI at survey differed significantly between groups (p = 0.043), from 31.6 (29.1, 33.6) kg/m2 for emotional hunger only to 35.3 (31.4, 39.8) kg/m2 in those with another phenotype descriptor, though in all groups, most PwO still had class I obesity (40.0%). Median weight loss since diagnosis was 6.8 (1.4, 11.8) kg, with a median weight loss since the start of the most recent weight loss attempt of 4.8 (0.9, 10.0) kg.

At the time of survey, PwO were receiving a median of 1.0 (1.0, 1.0) weight loss drugs. The majority of PwO receiving a weight loss drug were prescribed a GLP-1 RA (87.7%).

Phenotype descriptor prevalence and PwO-physician alignment

The proportions of physicians and PwO reporting phenotype descriptors are showing in Fig. 2a. Physicians most commonly reported hungry brain phenotype descriptor (52.6%), which was reported by 38.1% of PwO. The phenotype descriptor most frequently reported by PwO was emotional hunger (45.1%), which was reported by 34.9% of physicians. Hungry gut was reported by 11.2% of physicians and 24.7% of PwO, while for slow burn, these proportions were 37.2% and 33.5%, respectively.

PwO, people living with obesity.

Physicians and PwO agreed on the presence of the hungry brain phenotype descriptor in 57.6% of cases (expected agreement: 48.5%), with a Cohen's kappa of 0.176 (Fig. 2b), indicating only slight agreement, though this was statistically significant (Z = 2.46, p = 0.007). In the emotional hunger phenotype descriptor (Fig. 2c), there was physician-PwO agreement in 60.5% of cases (expected agreement: 50.1%), with a Cohen's kappa of 0.207, reflecting fair agreement and reaching statistical significance (Z = 2.78, p = 0.003). Although there was no significant alignment as per Cohen's kappa (Κ = 0.113, Z = 1.62, p = 0.053) for the hungry gut phenotype descriptor (Fig. 2d), physicians and PwO nevertheless agreed on its presence in 69.8% of cases (expected agreement: 65.9%). In slow burn, agreement between PwO and physician reports was 71.5% (expected agreement: 71.7%) and Cohen's kappa was 0.41, indicating moderate agreement; this was highly significant (Z = 5.411, p < 0.001).

PwO characteristics by phenotyping alignment

A number of differences in PwO characteristics were found between those who aligned on their phenotype descriptor with their treating physicians and those who did not (Table 3). There were no significant differences in the demographic characteristics between PwO who were aligned and not aligned with their treating physician, with the exception of a significantly higher proportion of those with a South Asian ethnicity and a slow burn phenotype descriptor (aligned: 0.0%; not aligned 6.1%, p = 0.022).

There were significant differences between alignment groups in recorded weight loss in those with the hungry brain phenotype descriptor. Discussion

This real-world study extends obesity assessment beyond BMI alone by comparing physician-reported and PwO-reported descriptors of phenotype classifications, with important clinical implications for understanding how obesity is conceptualized in routine care.

There were notable differences in the prevalences of psychological comorbidities between PwO with different phenotype descriptors, with high rates of depression (41.2%), anxiety (29.4%), and stress (10.8%) in those with the emotional hunger and hungry brain phenotype descriptors. This aligns well with previous evidence that emotional eating and poor appetite control are closely linked to mood and stress-related disorders [18], and further validates this phenotyping.

The level of PwO-physician agreement on phenotype descriptor identification varied substantially across phenotypes, indicating meaningful differences between PwO and physician perceptions. The only phenotype descriptor where there was moderate agreement between PwO and physicians was slow burn. The relatively good level of agreement may be due to its defining characteristics, such as slow metabolism and gradual weight gain, being more visible and objectively measurable [8]. The relatively “medical” nature of this phenotype descriptor may also make it more likely to be discussed during clinical consultations.

There was fair agreement on the emotional hunger phenotype descriptor, suggesting that, while many PwO and physicians can identify emotional eating as a contributing factor, consistent assessment remains challenging, likely because it relies on insight into internal emotional states rather than observable behaviors. Similarly, there was only slight agreement on the hungry brain phenotype descriptor, suggesting that cognitive aspects of eating may be less obvious to physicians and are more likely to be under-recognized.

There was no statistically significant alignment between PwO and physicians for the hungry gut phenotype descriptor, despite a raw agreement of 69.8%. This apparent discrepancy is consistent with the well-documented kappa paradox, in which high raw agreement can co-occur with low kappa when the prevalence of a category is skewed [19,20]. In practical terms, this indicates that although both PwO and physicians frequently selected this phenotype descriptor, they may have done so based on different interpretations of what hungry gut represents.

Overall, these findings show that while some phenotypes descriptors, particularly slow burn, are relatively well recognized from both PwO and physician perspectives, others involving emotional or cognitive drivers (emotional hunger and hungry brain) are less consistently identified. This highlights an important opportunity to improve communication and education around behavioral and psychological contributors to obesity, ensuring that both PwO and physicians share a common understanding of phenotype characteristics, and are able to identify these. It also points to the need for greater physician awareness of the value of phenotyping beyond standard clinical guideline frameworks, which traditionally emphasize weight, BMI, and comorbidity thresholds, including cognitive and emotional aspects of obesity.

Recent updates to clinical guidance from the Lancet Obesity Commission and the American Diabetes Association emphasize the importance of understanding biological and behavioral heterogeneity, rather than relying on BMI alone [7,21]. The current findings provide empirical support for this direction by demonstrating that phenotype descriptor recognition is feasible in routine practice, even if consistency between physicians and PwO requires improvement. Improving shared understanding and communication between PwO and physicians, particularly in terms of phenotype classification, could therefore help support more personalized and effective obesity care.

No significant differences were observed between PwO who were or were not aligned with their physicians in terms of overall comorbidity burden, number of obesity medications, or use of GLP-1 RA, indicating that current treatment practices remain consistent regardless of phenotype agreement. However, as this was an observational study, physicians would not necessarily have based their treatment decisions on obesity phenotype descriptors, in which case descriptors or alignment levels may not impact on treatment practices.

Nevertheless, among those with the hungry brain phenotype descriptor, PwO who were aligned with their physician lost significantly more weight, both since diagnosis and from the start of their most recent attempt. This highlights the importance of PwO-physician agreement in effective obesity treatment. This also aligns with previous work highlighting the importance of effective PwO-physician communication and physician communication style in achieving favorable outcomes in obesity [22]. However, given the cross-sectional design of the study, we cannot draw any causative conclusions based on this observation. For instance, it may be the case that the PwO's weight loss history may influence physicians' phenotypic classification.

Incorporating phenotypic assessment into prescribing decisions could be key to selecting targeted therapies, optimizing drug response and improving long term outcomes. Given that phenotype-guided interventions have been associated with up to a 1.75-fold increase in weight loss [8], the misalignment observed in this study suggests that a meaningful proportion of PwO may not be receiving optimally targeted treatment.

Future prospective studies incorporating validated behavioral assessment instruments, objective phenotype measures where feasible, and adjustment for relevant clinical and treatment-related confounders will be important to further evaluate the validity and clinical utility of obesity phenotyping.

Limitations

The study has several limitations that should be considered. The DSP is not based on a true random sample of physicians or PwO, and participation is influenced by willingness to complete the survey. The requirement that at least two of eight PwO per physician be receiving obesity medications may have enriched the sample for certain phenotype descriptor profiles, potentially affecting prevalence estimates. The study sample was also predominantly White, employed, and of high SES, which may limit generalizability to more diverse or underserved populations. Recall bias may have affected questionnaire responses, though data collection at the time of consultation is expected to reduce this risk.

Phenotype descriptors were assigned based on narrative descriptors rather than objective physiological testing. The agreement measured here therefore reflects shared perception of behavioral profiles rather than concordance on objectively validated biological subtypes. While this is a pragmatically relevant construct for guiding treatment discussions, future research incorporating validated diagnostic tools could clarify whether alignment improves with more structured assessment. We must also acknowledge the corrigendum on the original Acosta et al. study, which clarified that their study was not a fully randomized and blinded clinical trial. Further interventional trials would be needed to further validate phenotype-guided obesity treatment strategies.

No adjustments for multiple testing were made as the analysis is explorative in nature. The reported p-values should be considered nominal and interpreted with caution. Additionally, we did not control for possible confounders, such as treatment prescribed or treatment intensity.

Finally, because data were collected while many PwO were actively receiving obesity medications, pharmacological effects could have masked or altered the presentation of certain behavioral phenotype descriptors at the time of data capture.

Conclusion

In summary, this study highlights the real-world value of obesity phenotyping, beyond physiological and including behavioral and psychological characteristics, in understanding the diverse presentation and experiences of PwO alongside clinical assessment. The variation in comorbidities and the inconsistent alignment between PwO- and physician-reported phenotype descriptors underscore the limitations of relying on BMI alone to guide obesity assessment and management. Structured phenotype descriptor assessment tools, integrated into routine clinical workflows, could help close this alignment gap and ensure that behavioral and psychological drivers are consistently recognized.

These findings are consistent with the direction of recent clinical practice guideline updates, which advocate for moving beyond BMI-based frameworks toward more individualized and behaviorally informed models of care. Embedding phenotype recognition into clinical practice, supported by shared language between physicians and PwO, could enhance the precision of obesity management, enhance behavioral and psychological needs, strengthen therapeutic dialogue, and guide more targeted use of pharmacologic and behavioral interventions.
