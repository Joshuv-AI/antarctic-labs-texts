Advanced LC-MS/MS Technique for Environmental Ivermectin Detection
Authors: Alicia Maria Carrillo Heredero, Giulia Segato, Simonetta Menotta, Elena Butovskaya, Annalisa Borra, Simone Bertini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIvermectin is a widely used broad-spectrum antiparasitic agent employed in both companion animals and livestock. While generally safe for mammals, its acute and chronic toxicity has been proven in many nontarget fish and insect species at low concentrations. The present study aimed to develop, validate, and test a more accurate method for ivermectin detection in various matrices (feces, soil, and sewage) for environmental studies. The method was validated using the Eurachem network. The applicability of the method was established at 1.5–500 μg/L for feces and 1.5–15 μg/L for sewage and soil. No dilution effect was observed at 5000 μg/L. The limit of detection was 0.66, 0.54, and 0.36 μg/kg for feces, soil, and sewage, respectively. The limit of quantification was set at 1.5 μg/kg and verified experimentally. Overall recovery was 92.27 ± 12.01% for feces and 96.19 ± 13.78% in soil, reaching a linearity of at least 0.99. The present method was successfully applied to 228 feces, 20 sewage, and 105 soil samples. This validated method enables the precise detection and quantification of ivermectin in the tested matrices, thereby envisaging potential applications in environmental monitoring studies with improved sensitivity and specificity.

Introduction

Ivermectin (IVM) is an antiparasitic pharmaceutical agent member of the macrocyclic lactone family, derived from avermectin, a compound produced by the bacterium Streptomyces avermitilis.1,2 It is a mixture primarily consisting of 22,23-dihydroavermectin-B1a in quantities exceeding 80%, accompanied by less than 20% of 22,23-dihydroavermectin-B1b.2−5 Its extensive efficacy across various parasites, notable safety profile, and economic advantage contributed to its widespread usage in animal husbandry. Furthermore, its pronounced lipophilic properties underscore its pharmacological characteristics, potentially influencing its distribution and pharmacokinetic behavior.2 IVM is proved to be barely metabolized and excreted at 99% in feces and 1% in urine of several domestic mammals, such as pigs, cattle, dogs, cats, sheep, goats, and horses.5,6 It is a very stable compound as it is resistant to UV light, low temperature, and high temperature up to 70 °C for 24 h.7−12

The spectrum of activity of IVM encompasses a wide range of parasites, both ectoparasites and endoparasites, including nematodes and arthropods.1,13,14 Ivermectin holds approval for use in both human and veterinary medicine by the European Medicine Agency and Food and the Drug Administration.13,14 In human medicine, it is primarily employed for the treatment of onchocerciasis.15 Conversely, within veterinary practice, this pharmacological agent demonstrates efficacy across various species, including cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, horses, and dogs. Its availability extends across numerous formulations, ranging from feed premixes to spot-on and injectable preparations, facilitating versatile administration in various clinical settings.16 While being very safe for mammals, IVM has been proven to be toxic for several nontarget animals.5,17−21 Both chronic and acute toxicity have been tested and confirmed experimentally on Daphnia magna by Di Paola et al.20 Dung beetles have also been found to be sensitive to IVM concentrations.17 Bai et al. have established ecotoxicity consequences for soil invertebrates considered a sentinel for soil conditions, such as earthworms.22

Even if the Environmental Risk Assessment of pharmaceuticals did not identify IVM as posing a significant concern, several works have underlined the possible consequences of the wide use of IVM, which could then be found in feces, sewage, and followingly, onto the fields thus representing a threat to nontarget species.6,20,23 Possible accumulation in soil has also been suggested by previous studies: Mancini et al. have underlined the need for a mindful and sustainable use of this drug.6,21 For all these reasons, the massive and systematic use of IVM in livestock has been questioned.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) has been traditionally used to detect IVM in food and environmental samples.25−28 For instance, HPLC with a fluorescent detector has been used to detect IVM in feces after derivatization with N-methylimidazole and trifluoroacetic anhydride.25,26,29 Our research team has previously applied HPLC with a diode array detector (HPLC-DAD) to detect IVM in organic matter such as feed, soil, and water. On the other hand, liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) offers more sensitive, specific, potentially less time-consuming IVM detection in complex biological and environmental matrices.2,31−34 LC-MS/MS has been already applied to study the pharmacological properties of drugs in plasma of various animals35,36 and quantify residues of IVM in fish and bovine tissues,37 milk,38 and eggs.31 Liquid–liquid extraction,35 solid-phase extraction,11 as well as extraction through dried blood spots11 have been used to prepare samples for mass spectrometry analysis. To date, multiple efforts have been made to detect IVM in samples, such as soil, feces, and water at concentrations low enough to undertake ecotoxicological studies.26,40−46 However, complex, time-consuming extraction and purification procedures are applied, and there is a need for simple and reliable analytical methods based on LC-MS/MS to analyze complex environmental matrices at concentrations in the μg/kg range.13,23

The aim of the study was to develop and validate a method for the detection and quantification of ivermectin through LC-MS/MS in environmental samples: feces, soil, and sewage. The extraction procedure was based on our previous studies for IVM detection with HPLC-DAD and adapted for LC-MS/MS detection to reach higher sensitivity in the μg/kg concentration range.

Results and Discussion

Sample Preparation

The object of the study involves complex matrices containing vegetal, mineral, and starchy components whose proportions are highly variable based on the animals’ diet and soil type (mainly sandy rather than loamy or with high contents of clay). Due to the highly lipophilic nature of IVM, extraction was performed easily in feces and soil samples, while sewage recovery rates were lower because IVM has a low affinity for aqueous solutions. Indeed, the recoveries of fortified samples were very good in the fecal and soil samples, with overall recoveries of 92.27 ± 12.01% and 96.19 ± 13.78%, respectively. Our findings thus confirm that the use of methanol was effective for IVM extraction from feces and soil samples, as it reported in previous studies, while it needs to be followed by-solid phase extraction and purification in feces. The recovery of IVM was not as satisfactory for sewage-spiked samples (33.04 ± 8.43%). Such a low recovery rate is a limitation of the validation study, but it serves as a valuable starting point for future studies: sewage samples are mainly water solutions with variable percentages of the solid fraction; part of the analyte can be removed with the solid fraction by centrifugation during sample preparation. Further investigations are needed to improve the recovery rates, focusing on the extraction procedures of the analyte from solid components. While the previous method facilitated analysis in water, it dealt with significantly higher concentrations, which may not fully address the complexities encountered in more challenging matrices, such as feces and soil. Future research should aim to refine the current method to improve recovery rates, particularly by investigating the role of corpuscular components, ultimately enhancing the method’s applicability in environmental and residue analyses at lower concentrations.31 Future studies could investigate the particulate matter of sewage and analyze the pellet formed after centrifugation when removing the water fraction.

Method Validation

The applicability of the method for fecal samples was evaluated across a concentration range of 1.5–500 μg/L, with no dilution effect observed even at 5000 μg/L. For sewage and soil samples, the method was assessed within a concentration range 1.5–15 μg/L. Calibration curves were built by plotting the measured concentration against the instrumental response (chromatographic peak area) across five concentration levels (0.05–0.1–0.2–0.4–1 μg/mL).

A remarkable matrix effect was detected only for soil samples, and no interfering signals were detected at the analyte’s retention time.

This method was applied to real samples in an environmental study on IVM residues. The results demonstrate that this updated method enables efficient extraction and purification of IVM from feces and soil, representing a significant improvement over a previously published procedure. For soil, especially, the extraction process is notably faster and more cost-effective for laboratories. In the case of feces, the procedure was refined to achieve greater magnification than was possible with the feed in earlier methods. Enhanced selectivity and sensitivity were achieved through mass spectrometry, resulting in significantly lower LOD and LOQ. The use of LC-MS/MS, the gold standard for such analyses, ensures the highest accuracy and reliability, making this method highly suitable for environmental and residue analyses in complex matrices. The successful application of this method confirms its effectiveness for such research.

Conclusions

In the present study, an analytical method for the quantification of IVM using the LC-MS/MS technique was developed and validated for the environmental matrices: feces, sewage, and soil. The validation process followed the standards described in the Eurachem guidelines to ensure the fitness for purpose of the method.47 In soil samples, the presence of the matrix effect required calibration curves to be prepared in blank soil extracts instead of solvent mixtures. The LOQ for all studied matrices was determined to be 1.5 μg/kg. The validated method is particularly suitable for application in feces and soil and can be used in such environmental samples for monitoring traces of IVM, which can threaten nontarget organisms, and contributes to the mitigation of the risks derived from environmental contamination with veterinary pharmaceuticals.

Such methods are crucial for sustainability goals, particularly for monitoring of macrocyclic lactones like IVM. The approach of this method ensures a One Health perspective, protecting human and animal health while promoting environmental stewardship, and contributing to planetary well-being.

Methods

Equipment and Consumables

The equipment used consisted of standard laboratory material (scales, micropipettes, vortex, ultrasonic bath, reservoirs, vacuum pump, centrifuge), and additionally it comprehended homogenizer, manifold, and sample shaker Geno/Grinder. Samples were analyzed in a Waters UHPLC model Acquity I Class system (Waters, USA) coupled to a Waters model TQS-micro mass spectrometer. MassLynx V4.1 by Waters Corporation (Milford, MA 01757 USA) was the software used for instrumental data acquisition and data elaboration.

The necessary glassware consisted of the following materials: 10 mL graduated glass test tubes with conical bottoms (Saniver Srl, Italy), 10 mL glass test tubes (Isolab, Italy), class A glass pipettes from 1 to 20 mL (Blaubrand, Germany), Pasteur glass pipettes (VWR International Srl, Italy), and 100 and 50 mL graduated glass cylinders (Poulten and Graf Wertheim, Germany), 2, 5, 50, and 1000 mL class A glass flasks (Hirschmann Laborgeräte, Germany).

The consumables used were spatulas, spoons, frits (Phenomenex, USA), plastic bags, 250 mL centrifuge plastic jars with screw caps (Nalgene, Rochester, NY, USA), 50 mL polypropylene test tubes with screw caps (Biosorfa, China), 15 mL polypropylene test tubes with screw cap (Biosorfa, China), and a C-18 SPE Cartridge of 500 mg/6 mL (code: COC186500;Biocomma, China), silica SPE Cartridge 500 mg/6 mL (code: COSIL6500; Biocomma, China), and micropipet tips (Gilson Middleton, USA).

Chemicals and Reagents

Chemicals and reagents used were methanol, acetonitrile, and formic acid (all HPLC grade) purchased from Carlo Erba, Italy. Ammonium formate for HPLC was acquired from Merck (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany), deionized and filtered water was obtained from Millipore Corporation (Merck, Germany), and ivermectin standard (96% 22,23-dihydroavermectin B1a + 1% 22,23-dihydroavermectin purity) was obtained from LGC Labor GmbH (Augsburg, Germany).

The aqueous mobile phase (mobile phase A) was a buffer solution of 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid, while the organic mobile phase (mobile phase B) was a 0.1% formic acid solution in acetonitrile. For extract dilution, a solvent mixture of methanol/mobile phase A (80:20 v/v) was used.

Standard Preparation

Ivermectin powder was used for stock solution preparation. An appropriate amount of ivermectin powder was solubilized in methanol to reach a concentration of 1 mg/mL. Intermediate and working solutions were freshly prepared before each analysis by diluting the stock solution. The intermediate solution was prepared at 10 μg/mL. To prepare the standard working solutions for the calibration curve, the intermediate solution was properly diluted in methanol to reach final concentrations of 0.05–0.1–0.2–0.4–1 μg/mL. After preparing the standard solutions, the IVM standard curve for calibration was prepared in vials by diluting it 1:100.

Preparation of Feces Samples

A portion of 10 g of homogenized fecal sample was weighed into a plastic jar and 50 mL of methanol was added. The mixture was ultrasonicated for 20 min and then shaken on Geno/Grinder for 4 min at 1500 oscillations/min. The supernatant (40 mL) was transferred in to the 50 mL plastic tubes and centrifuges at 1006 x g for 5 min. A volume of 15 mL of the centrifuged extract was loaded into the reservoirs prepared with 5 g of activated neutral aluminum and frits. The volume was collected in 15 mL plastic tubes.

The SPE C-18 columns were initially conditioned with 5 mL of dichloromethane, dried for 1 min under vacuum; subsequently 5 mL of a 50:50 water/acetonitrile mixture (v/v) was allowed to pass through the column. The conditioned columns were loaded with 10 mL of the extract and 5 mL of a water/acetonitrile mixture, allowing them to percolate by gravity without drying. The columns were washed with 1 mL of a water/acetonitrile mixture followed by 15 s of vacuum. Finally, column walls were washed with 2 mL of the same mixture and dried under vacuum for 1 min.

The SPE silica columns were conditioned with 5 mL of acetonitrile and 5 mL of dichloromethane, drying the columns for 1 min after the application of each sample. The SPE silica columns were placed on the manifold alongside the C-18 columns. Five mL plus 5 mL of the 9:1 dichloromethane/acetonitrile mixture (v/v) were added to the SPE C-18 column, and vacuum was maintained for 1 min before discarding the column.

The tube holders with glass V-bottom tubes were added to the manifold. The IVM was eluted by gravity from the SPE silica column into a conical tube containing 5 mL of acetonitrile without allowing it to dry. Another 2 mL of acetonitrile was eluted using vacuum. The eluate was evaporated to dryness under nitrogen flow at 50 ± 5 °C. The dried extract was resuspended in 0.5 mL of solvent mixture, vortexed for 1 min, sonicated for 5 min, and transferred to an eppendorf test tube for ultracentrifugation at 17530 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was placed in a vial and stored in the freezer at −20 °C prior to the analysis which occurred within 24 h. For each analytical batch, an IVM-free blank control sample and the sample fortified with 2.5 μg/L IVM were processed under the same analytical conditions.

Preparation of Soil Samples

Into a plastic jar, 10 g of sample was weighed, 50 mL of methanol was added with a class A flask for analysis. Ultrasonication was then performed for 20 min, after which samples were shaken on the Geno/Grinder for 4 min at 1500 oscillations/min. Once stirring was complete, 40 mL of the supernatant was placed in plastic test tubes with screw caps and centrifuged at 1006 x g for 5 min.

Twenty milliliters of the extract was then set to dry under nitrogen flow at 50 ± 5 °C. The extract was then resuspended in 0.5 mL of solvent mixture, vortexed for 1 min, and sonicated for 5 min. The extract was placed in an eppendorf test tube and ultracentrifuged for 10 min at 17 530 x g at 4 °C. The supernatant was placed in a vial with insert and stored in the freezer at −20 °C prior to the analysis which occurred within 24 h.

For each analytical batch, an IVM-free blank control sample and the sample fortified with 2.5 μg/L of IVM were processed in the same analytical conditions.

Preparation of Sewage Samples

Ten milliliters of the sample was ultrasonicated for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 1006 x g for 5 min. Next, 1.5 mL of the sample was transferred to an eppendorf-type tube and ultracentrifuged at 17530 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was then transferred to a vial and immediately analyzed, and it was stored in the freezer at −20 °C within 24 h after analysis. For each analysis, both a blank sample and a fortified sample were prepared and processed identically to the real samples. To prepare the fortified sample, 25 μL of IVM solution (1 μg/mL) was added to a 10 mL flask partially filled with blank sewage, and the volume with blank sewage.

Analytical Conditions

A Supelcosil C-18 reverse phase chromatographic column, 25 cm long, 4.6 mm internal diameter, and 5 μm average particle diameter (Supelco, USA) was used together with a Supelguard C-18 reverse phase chromatographic precolumn, 2 cm long, 4.6 mm internal diameter, and 5 μm average particle diameter (Supelco, USA). Both columns were maintained at 40 ± 5 °C using a thermosetting system.

A volume of 4 μL of each sample was injected, and the chromatographic separation was performed in an 8.5 min-long run. During the analytical run, the binary gradient scheme indicated in Table 2 was performed.

IVM calibration curve was constructed by plotting the concentration of the sample on the x-axis against the area response on the y-axis. The calibration curve and instrument sensitivity were deemed satisfactory when the linear correlation coefficient (r2) was equal to or exceeded 0.99. The recovery of fortified samples had to be in the range of 80–120%. The signal/noise ratio (S/N) had to be higher than or equal to 20 for the standard and fortified samples. The analysis had to be repeated if the requirements were not met.

Validation Parameters and Method Validation

The method was validated according to “The Fitness for Purpose of Analytical Methods” by Eurachem,47 which lays down the requirements for method validation to ensure adequacy to international standards, providing definitions of terms and quality measures. The following criteria were assessed: accuracy, applicability, limit of detection, limit of quantification, precision, repeatability, recovery, selectivity, linearity, and matrix effect.

If a matrix effect was observed, the calibration curve used for all tests involving that matrix was made in matrix extract instead of a mixture of solvents.

The limit of detection was defined as the smallest concentration of a substance that can be detected using standard tests, and it was calculated using the ADVeRSE 2.0 (Analytical methoDs Validation Results Speedy Elaboration) software package for the R statistical environment.48,49 The limit of quantification was defined as the minimum concentration of a substance that can be measured with certainty using standard tests and calculated using the ADVeRSE software.48,49 For each matrix, the ADVeRSE software calculations for LOD were based on six samples for each of the three levels on the 3 days of validation. LOQ, set at 1.50 μg/mL, was verified experimentally on the same samples. Therefore, the calculation was based on a total of 54 samples.

Selectivity is the ability to clearly identify the analyte in the presence of other expected components such as impurities, degradants, or the matrix. To assess the specificity of the method, 18 blank samples were analyzed. The linearity of an analytical procedure is its capability, within a specified range, to produce test results that are directly proportional to the concentration (amount) of the analyte in the sample. Linearity was evaluated building 3 replicas of the calibration curve in the concentration range. The calibration curve and instrumental sensitivity were considered acceptable when two conditions occurred: the correlation coefficient (r2) was ≥0.99 and the peak relating to the first point of the curve appeared to be quantifiable.

The matrix effect was intended as the combined influence of all components in the sample, other than the analyte, on the measurement of its quantity. To test the matrix effect, the calibration curve was constructed in solvent and in the blank extract in the same session. If the comparison of the two calibration curves did not differ by more than 20%, then the matrix effect was null. A difference higher than 20% implicated a matrix effect.

Accuracy refers to how accurately the input data reflect the closeness of estimates to actual unknown values. The method can be applied to data for a single entity as well as similar data across comparable entities. The applicability of the method concerned feces, sewage, and soil. The range of applicability was chosen based on preliminary results from an unpublished study tracing IVM residues in the environment. Accuracy was evaluated through the recovery percentage at 3 levels within the scope of the method: 1.5, 250, and 500 μg/L for feces, 1.5, 5, and 15 μg/L for sewage and soil samples.

Precision, which was intended as the degree of agreement between independent test results obtained under specified conditions, was assessed by calculating the standard deviation and the coefficient of variation (CV%) by conducting 6 replicate tests for each of the aforementioned levels. Specificity was evaluated by applying the entire tested method on 6 samples of all matrices on 3 separate days of analytical sessions.

Repeatability was defined as the degree of agreement between independent test results obtained using the same method on identical test material in the same laboratory by the same operator with the same equipment within short time intervals (such as 1 day or one analytical run). The samples were tested on three different days across the 18 samples. Recovery was expressed as the quantity measured as a percentage of the initial amount of analyte added to the sample.

Uncertainty of measure was calculated using the ADVeRSE software.48 the estimation of combined (ucomb) and expanded uncertainty (U) at the 3 validation levels was considered. U was obtained by multiplying ucomb for a coverage factor k = 2. The uncertainty is expressed as the maximum relative extended uncertainty.

Six blank samples for each matrix were processed and examined using the method parameters to confirm that there was no notable instrumental reaction occurring at the retention times of the IVM.

When analyzing and quantifying the actual samples, quantification calculations were carried out using software. Through interpolation, the concentration of IVM present in the sample was calculated based on the response obtained from the chromatogram report. If a positive sample exceeded the highest concentration on the calibration curve, it was diluted to bring it within the measurable range and applicability of the method.
