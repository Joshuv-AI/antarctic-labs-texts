Advances in New Approach Methodologies at FDA's Division of Applied Regulatory Science
Authors: Ksenia Blinova, Kimberly Chiu, Anik Chaturbedi, Jahan Cooper, Tromondae K. Feaster, Paula L. Hyland, Lidiya Stavitskaya, Wendy W. Wu, Jeffry Florian, Rodney Rouse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) represent a paradigm shift in drug development and regulatory science, offering human‐relevant alternatives to traditional preclinical models. This mini‐review highlights recent advances in NAM development, validation, and regulatory application from FDA's Division of Applied Regulatory Science (DARS). We describe in silico NAMs including quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) modeling for opioid overdose scenarios, (quantitative) structure–activity relationship ((Q)SAR) modeling for toxicity prediction and drug–drug interaction assessment, and multi‐omics approaches for pharmacodynamic biomarker discovery. Additionally, we present in vitro NAMs including human‐induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)‐derived neural networks for opioid pharmacology assessment, microphysiological systems (MPS) for pulmonary drug permeability, gastrointestinal models for intestinal transport, and standardized cardiac ion channel and hiPSC‐CM MEA assays for proarrhythmia risk prediction. These case studies demonstrate how NAMs can reduce reliance on animal studies while improving translation to clinical outcomes. The NAMs described here range from exploratory research tools to approaches actively informing regulatory submissions review. The integration of in silico and in vitro NAMs into regulatory decision‐making frameworks represents a critical step toward more efficient, human‐relevant drug development and safety assessments.

The disconnect between preclinical animal models and clinical outcomes, particularly for large molecules and complex biological systems, has driven increased emphasis on developing, qualifying, and integrating New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) as physiologically relevant approaches to complement, reduce the size of, or replace traditional preclinical studies. NAMs encompass a broad range of technologies including computational models, in vitro cell‐based systems, microphysiological systems, and multi‐omics platforms that can provide mechanistic insights into drug pharmacology and toxicology while reducing animal use.

FDA's Division of Applied Regulatory Science (DARS) 1 has been at the forefront of developing and applying NAMs to support regulatory decision‐making. This mini‐review highlights recent advances across computational and experimental NAM platforms, demonstrating their utility in addressing critical questions in drug development and safety assessments.

DARS's NAM activities are part of FDA's broader strategic commitment to modernize drug evaluation through human‐relevant science, as reflected in the FDA Predictive Toxicology Roadmap and the FDA Modernization Act 2.0, which explicitly encourages the use of alternatives to animal testing in drug development. DARS serves as an internal scientific resource for CDER, providing NAM‐based consultations, developing and validating new platforms, and contributing to regulatory guidance development – all in direct support of FDA's agency‐wide NAM integration goals.

The NAMs presented in this review were selected based on DARS's internal priorities, which center on addressing critical regulatory gaps where traditional preclinical approaches are insufficient, associated with high animal use, or poorly predictive of human outcomes. DARS selection criteria include scientific plausibility; availability of human‐relevant biological systems; feasibility of standardization and reproducibility assessment; and alignment with active regulatory needs within CDER. IN SILICO NAMs

Quantitative systems pharmacology modeling of opioid overdose

DARS developed and applied a computational model to evaluate opioid overdose scenarios and reversal strategies, addressing a critical gap in the treatment of community opioid overdose. Integrating opioid and antagonist pharmacokinetics, receptor binding, pharmacodynamics, and cardiopulmonary physiology, 2 DARS showed that fast‐absorbing antagonists and earlier administration are important for acute opioid overdose reversal and that antagonists with slow clearance may help prevent renarcotization. 3 , 4 Afterward, this translational model was also used to support pediatric dosing. The model was later extended to incorporate rescue breathing and used to show that timely rescue breathing reduced naloxone dosing needed for reversal and extended the rescue window, while naloxone administration reduced the required rescue breathing duration and shortened the time to recovery of spontaneous respiration. 5 Altogether, these studies provide quantitative evidence to inform antagonist product development and dosing recommendations to help address community opioid overdose.

(Q)SAR modeling for regulatory toxicology assessment

(Quantitative) structure–activity relationship ((Q)SAR) modeling is an increasingly prevalent in silico method that employs machine‐learning techniques to identify associations between chemical structure and biological activity. As part of the regular regulatory safety review of pharmaceutical products, DARS' (Q)SAR experts and Computational Toxicology Consultation Service (CTCS) apply (Q)SAR models to rapidly generate toxicity predictions for pharmaceutical impurities with limited experimental data. CTCS handles hundreds of consult requests annually from across CDER. Three common review scenarios are described below.

Mutagenic impurity assessment

CTCS reviews industry submissions characterizing the mutagenic potential of drug product impurities by generating predictions of bacterial mutagenicity from expert‐rule and statistical‐based (Q)SAR models. This approach, combined with the application of expert knowledge, is recommended in the ICH M7(R2) guideline as an alternative to experimental testing, which may be infeasible for products with many identified impurities. 6 , 7

Extractables and Leachables (E&L) read‐across assessment

For E&Ls with limited general toxicity data requiring safety qualification, DARS applies a ‘read‐across’ approach using information from structurally similar surrogate compounds to fill experimental data gaps and inform permissible daily exposure limits. 8 DARS utilizes various structure–activity relationship (SAR) workflows to determine chemical similarity based on structural, physicochemical, mechanistic, and biological factors.

Nitrosamine impurity assessment

Nitrosamines present in pharmaceuticals are a unique impurity class that requires special safety considerations due to their potential high carcinogenic potency and limited compound‐specific toxicology data. DARS applies the Carcinogenic Potency Categorization Approach (CPCA), an SAR model that identifies activating and deactivating substructural features known to influence the main pathway of carcinogenesis, to assign nitrosamine impurities to potency categories that inform acceptable intake limits. DARS expertise was central in developing the CPCA, which has been adopted internationally by several drug regulatory authorities as a more practical approach to nitrosamine impurity control than conventional read‐across. 9 , 10

(Q)SAR modeling for drug–drug interaction assessment

More recently, to support metabolite evaluation for CYP enzyme inhibition potential, DARS worked with a Research Collaboration Agreement partner to develop complementary (Q)SAR models identifying structural alerts associated with mechanism‐based inhibition. 11 Specifically, a SAR profiler containing 58 alerts and five QSAR models was developed to predict reversible inhibition of CYP3A4, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP2D6 as well as time‐dependent inhibition of CYP3A4. QSAR models were constructed using a non‐proprietary database of 10,129 unique chemicals harvested from FDA drug approval packages and published literature. Cross‐validation performance statistics for the new CYP QSAR models ranged from 78% to 84% sensitivity and 79% to 84% normalized negative predictivity. External validation using 231 approved drugs and 33 non‐proprietary chemicals showed sensitivities up to 75% and normalized negative predictivity up to 80%. 11 These newly developed models provide a faster and effective evaluation of potential drug–drug interactions caused by metabolites and facilitate the identification of structural alerts for CYP inhibition.

IN VITRO NAMs

Cardiac MPS NAMs for regulatory safety pharmacology assessment

Human‐induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived cardiomyocyte (hiPSC‐CM) platforms are increasingly used to assess cardiac safety in drug development. DARS has validated hiPSC‐CMs with multielectrode array (MEA) to detect drug‐induced cardiotoxicity. Through the Comprehensive in vitro Proarrhythmia Assay (CiPA) initiative, these platforms demonstrated reliable prediction of torsade de pointes (TdP) risk categorization across a multisite study. Recent studies show the platform can evaluate drug combinations. For example, cobicistat reduced moxifloxacin‐induced repolarization prolongation and lowered TdP risk categorization. 12 Advanced cardiac microphysiological systems (MPS) and organ‐on‐a‐chip (OoC) platforms enable simultaneous measurement of voltage, calcium handling, and contractility, improving assessment of cardiac excitation‐contraction coupling. 13 , 14 These systems successfully predicted proarrhythmic effects and revealed rate‐dependent responses. Standardized protocols now address variability in cell culture and OoC system assembly, supporting reproducibility and broader adoption of these predictive nonclinical cardiac safety models. Future studies will evaluate cardiac repolarization concordance across nonclinical assays and clinical repolarization outcomes.

Cardiac ion channel pharmacology – patch clamp studies for regulatory safety pharmacology assessment

CiPA proposes to integrate data from multiple cardiac ion channels to improve proarrhythmia risk prediction. Patch clamp assessment using human‐based cell lines provides direct characterization of a drug's electrophysiological effects; however, diversity in experimental practices has highlighted a critical need for protocol standardization. Research conducted by DARS has focused on quantifying data variability and developing experimental approaches that improve nonclinical‐to‐clinical translation. 15 A multi‐laboratory study 16 evaluated hERG channel block for 28 drugs across five laboratories, confirming that systematic differences can persist even with standardized protocols and quantifying assay variability at approximately 5‐fold. 16 Drug loss due to nonspecific binding was identified as a major factor leading to underestimation of potency. Beyond standardization, enhancing physiological relevance is crucial. 17 Physiologically relevant protocols helped resolve nonclinical‐clinical discrepancies for diltiazem, revealing it as a multi‐ion channel blocker rather than a selective Ca2+ channel blocker. 17 Similarly, physiologically relevant protocols and accounting for active metabolites improved risk assessment for certain drugs. 18 These contributions enhance the reliability of nonclinical data and bolster confidence in using ion channel data for regulatory decision‐making under the CiPA framework.

Omics as NAMs for pharmacodynamic biomarker discovery

Omics technologies can support comprehensive, systems‐level evaluation of drug response, toxicity, and biological effects following therapeutic exposure. Additionally, in contrast to traditional single analyte assays, omics approaches can detect molecular changes and capture downstream biological responses in systems, especially when baseline molecular activity is low. 19 , 20 , 21

DARS has evaluated omics technologies, including proteomics and small RNA transcriptomics, for biomarker discovery in drug development through the creation and application of a structured framework for biomarker identification and characterization. This framework has potential applicability to areas where conventional endpoints may be limited, such as rare diseases. Using high‐throughput plasma proteomics, DARS showed that drug exposure is associated with coordinated, pathway‐level changes across the proteome, enabling identification of response biomarkers and mechanistic signatures of drug activity. 22 Applying this approach across additional drug doses demonstrated reproducible molecular responses with clear dose dependence, supporting robust characterization of pharmacodynamic biomarker responses. 20 Integration with miRNA sequencing further illustrated how multi‐omics approaches can link regulatory layers by connecting transcriptional and protein‐level responses. 19 These studies suggest that, given the diversity of platforms, workflows, and analytical approaches within omics technologies used as NAMs, these methods are best evaluated and applied within fit‐for‐purpose frameworks, with emphasis on study design, analytical performance, reproducibility, and demonstration of biological relevance to support interpretation and downstream application in drug development.

Neural NAMs for opioid pharmacology assessment

The opioid crisis necessitates better understanding of opioid effects on neural networks and evaluation of reversal agents. DARS developed a neural NAM combining hiPSC‐derived glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons with astrocytes on MEA platforms. 23 Serna et al. established the context of use of this NAM and demonstrated that the μ‐opioid receptor agonist DAMGO modulated neural electrophysiological activity in a concentration‐dependent manner, with effects reversed by naloxone. Key findings included: DAMGO disrupted higher‐order neural network burst patterns; baseline network activity influenced DAMGO‐induced responses; and naloxone (10 μM) reversed DAMGO effects within 30 minutes. Principal component analysis revealed a distinct separation between DAMGO‐treated and vehicle‐treated groups, with clustering of DAMGO plus naloxone with vehicle controls, demonstrating reversal. This platform provides a high‐throughput, human‐relevant NAM for evaluating opioid agonists, antagonists, and combinations, enabling human‐derived pharmacological data to inform in silico models. 23 Future studies are investigating clinically relevant opioids (e.g., fentanyl) and illicit polypharmacy combinations to further characterize the pharmacological landscape of opioid overdose.

Pulmonary NAMs

DARS evaluated a small airway MPS for assessing inhaled drug permeability. 24 The system incorporates primary human lung epithelial cells cultured at an air–liquid interface (ALI) in the apical channel and microvascular endothelial cells in the basolateral channel. The MPS recapitulated relevant cell types and many morphological features in the human lung. Permeability studies with bronchodilators for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and asthma treatment were then performed, resulting in the identification of nonspecific binding of lipophilic drugs requiring material considerations and the characterization of in vitro permeability with established quality control parameters. The study provides a basic paradigm to culture, characterize, and evaluate drug permeability with the small airway MPS. 24 Future studies are extending lung NAMs to additional structural and functional regions including lung alveolus MPS.

Gastrointestinal NAMs

DARS comprehensively characterized Caco‐2 cells, a widely used model that has demonstrated utility for assessing passive transport of drugs in intestinal permeability studies. 25 While Caco‐2 cells formed robust barriers with functional P‐glycoprotein and metabolizing enzymes, targeted gene expression analysis revealed substantial disparities in drug‐metabolizing enzymes (CYP3A4, CYP3A5, CES2) and drug transporters compared to human intestinal tissues. Principal component analysis demonstrated distinct transcriptomic clustering of Caco‐2 cells separate from both small intestine and colon tissues. These findings underscore potential limitations of the static Caco‐2 monolayer model for predicting active transport and first‐pass metabolism. 25 Future studies are evaluating 3D complex in vitro NAMs incorporating dynamic perfusion (e.g., flow), and mechanical stretch to better recapitulate complex intestinal biology.

DISCUSSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

The case studies presented in this review demonstrate the breadth and impact of NAM development and application at FDA's DARS. These efforts span the drug development continuum from early safety and efficacy assessment through regulatory submission and post‐marketing evaluation, addressing critical gaps in safety assessment, efficacy prediction, and mechanistic understanding.

Several key themes emerge from this work. First, computational NAMs provide powerful tools for rapid assessment and mechanistic insight, particularly when experimental data are limited or infeasible to obtain. The (Q)SAR and QSP modeling examples demonstrate how in silico approaches can inform regulatory decisions and guide product development. Second, experimental NAMs offer human‐relevant platforms. The neural, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, and cardiac NAMs described here provide physiologically relevant systems for assessing drug effects. Third, standardization and validation are critical for regulatory acceptance and confidence in NAM‐derived data, as illustrated by the cardiac ion channel work. Fourth, omics technologies represent an important class of NAMs that enable systems‐level characterization of drug response and biological effects, supporting mechanistic interpretation and biomarker discovery, and future integration across NAMs platforms.

It is important to note that the NAMs presented here are not uniformly positioned for regulatory decision making. In silico tools, such as (Q)SAR models and QSP models, are most mature and are already embedded in routing regulatory review. For example, (Q)SAR predictions for mutagenic impurities are explicitly supported by ICH M7. In vitro platforms such as hiPSC‐CM MEA and patch clamp assays play a supportive role within the CiPA framework under ICH S7B, while MPS‐based and neural NAMs remain exploratory, actively building the evidence base needed for broader regulatory integration.

Looking forward, key gaps remain that future work may address: the need for more physiologically mature hiPSC‐derived cell types; broader chemical applicability domains for (Q)SAR models; integration of dynamic flow and mechanical stimuli in GI and pulmonary MPS; and validated multi‐omics analytical pipelines. Drug and model developers can accelerate regulatory readiness by submitting NAM‐derived data alongside traditional endpoints in INDs and NDAs, enabling prospective co‐validation against clinical outcomes. DARS engages with external partners through research collaboration agreements and the FDA's Innovative Science and Technology Approaches for New Drugs (ISTAND) Pilot Program, which provides a mechanism for sponsors to qualify new drug development tools, including NAMs.

Global collaboration remains essential to realizing the regulatory potential of NAMs. DARS's work contributes to several active international harmonization efforts, including ICH S7B/E14 Q&A updates for cardiac safety pharmacology, ICH M7 for mutagenic impurity assessment, and alignment with OECD (Q)SAR validation principles. Future harmonization priorities include establishing internationally recognized qualification criteria for emerging NAMs – particularly MPS and omics platforms – and developing standardized data reporting formats that enable cross‐agency evaluation.

CONCLUSIONS

NAMs represent a transformative approach to drug development and regulatory science, offering the potential to reduce animal use while improving human relevance and predictive accuracy. The work highlighted in this review demonstrates FDA's commitment to advancing NAM science and integrating these approaches into regulatory decision‐making. Continued investment in NAM development, validation, and standardization will be essential to realize the full potential of these methodologies.
