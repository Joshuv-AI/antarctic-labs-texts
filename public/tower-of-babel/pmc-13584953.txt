Extracellular Vesicles for Androgenetic Alopecia: Current Evidence, Mechanisms, and Clinical Prospects
Authors: Wei-Zhen Tang, Chong-Yi Liao, Hong-Yu Xu, Wen-Ting Huang, Zi-Han Lan, Zhi-Xian Wu, Yu-Han Yang, Ming-Si Li, Tai-Hang Liu, Yong-Heng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile mainstream therapies for androgenetic alopecia (AGA), including pharmacological treatments, mesotherapy, low-level light therapy, and hair transplantation, often fall short of clinical needs due to limited efficacy, side effects, or invasiveness, regenerative medicine based on stem cells shows promise yet faces challenges related to immunogenicity, standardization, and safety. Against this backdrop, extracellular vesicles (EVs)—nanoscale signaling vesicles enclosed by a lipid bilayer—have rapidly emerged as an innovative “cell-free” therapeutic paradigm. By delivering bioactive “cargo” such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, EVs mimic the therapeutic functions of their parent cells and precisely regulate recipient cells. This approach combines high biosafety, low immunogenicity, and good stability while avoiding the risks associated with live-cell therapies. Distinct from previous reviews largely centered on mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs, this review offers a broader synthesis that encompasses emerging sources including somatic cells, blood components, plants, and milk, with comparative mechanistic and translational analyses. In this narrative review, we comprehensively synthesize the current evidence from preclinical and preliminary clinical studies identified through a literature search across major databases (PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library), covering diverse EV sources, isolation methods, engineering strategies, and administration routes. We identify that the therapeutic actions converge on several major mechanistic themes, including synergistic activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, suppression of the TGF-β/SMAD and androgen signaling, and restoration of the perifollicular niche through anti-oxidative, anti-inflammatory, and pro-angiogenic effects. Nevertheless, we critically highlight the key translational limitations,—noting that the field remains predominantly preclinical with only limited early-phase clinical data—including the absence of standardized manufacturing protocols, inconsistency in dosing and administration regimens, and the urgent need for rigorous large-scale clinical trials to confirm long‑term safety and efficacy. Looking ahead, through interdisciplinary collaboration to advance manufacturing processes, conduct rigorous clinical trials, and develop engineered strategies for enhanced efficacy, EVs are poised to become an efficient, minimally invasive, and accessible emerging therapeutic option within the AGA treatment landscape.Keywords: androgenetic alopecia, extracellular vesicles, exosome, mesenchymal stem cells

Introduction

Androgenetic alopecia (AGA), the most common form of chronic hair loss, represents a significant global public health and aesthetic medicine challenge. Epidemiological data indicate its prevalence increases markedly with age, affecting up to 50% of women and 80% of men, and exerting a profound negative impact on patients’ psychosocial well-being and quality of life.1–3 The core pathophysiological mechanism involves an abnormal sensitivity of hair follicles to dihydrotestosterone (DHT), leading to excessive activation of androgen receptor signaling, dysfunction of dermal papilla cells, and disruption of the hair follicle cycle. This ultimately results in progressive follicular miniaturization and hair loss.4,5

Currently, oral finasteride and topical minoxidil are the first-line therapies for AGA approved by the FDA, yet their clinical application faces significant limitations. Oral finasteride, a 5α-reductase inhibitor, can reduce serum DHT levels but may cause side effects such as decreased libido and erectile dysfunction. It is also contraindicated in women of childbearing potential, limiting its user population and long-term adherence.6 Although topical minoxidil can promote local hair growth, its efficacy varies among individuals (response rate approximately 30–40%) and is often accompanied by local adverse reactions like scalp itching and contact dermatitis, which may discourage continued use.7 Furthermore, these medications typically require long-term or even lifelong use to maintain benefits, and discontinuation often leads to the shedding of newly regrown hair. These limitations underscore the urgent clinical need for novel therapeutic strategies characterized by high efficacy, safety, and patient compliance (Figure 1). Regenerative medicine strategies based on tissue engineering and stem cell transplantation have brought new hope for AGA treatment, demonstrating the potential to reconstruct the hair follicle niche through cellular therapies.8,9 However, stem cell therapies face their own bottlenecks—such as immune rejection, tumorigenicity risks, survival uncertainty, and difficulties in standardized preparation—which hinder their clinical translation.10 Synthetic nanomedicine strategies, in addition to stem cell therapy, have also been explored as potential interventions for AGA, aiming to enhance the transdermal delivery efficiency and follicular targeting of drugs such as minoxidil through carrier systems like liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles, nanostructured lipid carriers, and polymeric nanoparticles.11 However, the application of these synthetic nanocarrier systems in dermatology is limited by poor skin barrier penetration efficiency, questionable biocompatibility, and a lack of long-term safety data, resulting in slow clinical translation progress.12–14 These shared challenges in synthetic nanomedicine and regenerative medicine underscore the urgent need to develop novel nanoplatforms that combine inherent targeting capabilities with the potential for standardization.

EVs, as key mediators of intercellular communication, are rapidly emerging as a highly promising “cell-free” therapeutic paradigm.15 EVs are nanometer-scale membranous vesicles actively secreted by cells, capable of carrying and delivering bioactive “cargo” such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids (eg, miRNAs). They precisely regulate recipient cell functions, thereby mimicking the therapeutic effects of their parent cells while circumventing the risks associated with live-cell therapies.16,17 Compared to other emerging biological products such as platelet-rich plasma (PRP)—which is limited by compositional complexity, donor variability, and lack of standardized protocols, with multiple randomized controlled trials failing to replicate early positive findings18–20—and stem cell-conditioned media, whose active components are unstable, have short half-lives, and are prone to degradation,21 EVs, encapsulating their cargo within a lipid bilayer, exhibit superior stability and lower immunogenicity.22 Preliminary studies have confirmed the encouraging potential of EVs from various sources in promoting hair growth.23,24 A recent pilot study also showed that plasma- and sonophoresis-assisted topical delivery of exosomes improved hair density and diameter in 25 AGA patients with a favorable safety profile.25 The relevance of EVs to AGA has never been more timely: recent breakthroughs in scalable isolation, engineered cargo loading, and targeted delivery have substantially advanced their translational feasibility, while the persistent shortcomings of current pharmacological and cell-based therapies have created an urgent demand for safer, more standardized off-the-shelf alternatives.

Currently, it is well established that EVs from diverse sources promote hair follicle regeneration in preclinical models via convergent mechanisms involving Wnt/β‑catenin activation, TGF‑β/SMAD and androgen signaling suppression, and perifollicular microenvironment restoration. However, substantial uncertainties remain: the comparative potency of different EV sources, the optimal isolation and engineering protocols, the key functional cargo molecules, dose‑response relationships, and long‑term safety and efficacy in humans have yet to be rigorously defined; moreover, the clinical application protocols, production standardization, and regulatory pathways for EV-based therapies remain poorly defined.26–29 To fill these critical knowledge gaps, this narrative review comprehensively synthesizes the current evidence on EVs for AGA based on a thorough examination of the published literature, covering their biogenesis, preparation strategies, source‑specific mechanisms, and translational challenges, with the overarching goal of providing a coherent framework to guide future mechanistic research and clinical development.

Methods

Search Strategy and Study Selection

This narrative review was conducted using a structured approach to identify relevant studies on EV-based therapies for AGA. A comprehensive literature search was performed across several major databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science Core Collection, and the Cochrane Library. Articles published in English between January 1, 2020, and July 20, 2026, were retrieved using the following search terms: “extracellular vesicle”, “exosome”, “nanovesicle”, “microvesicle”, “apoptotic body”, and “EV”, combined with “androgenetic alopecia”, “pattern hair loss”, “hair follicle”, “hair regrowth”, “hair cycle”, and “follicular miniaturization”. To further investigate mechanisms underlying EV-based interventions, additional search terms such as “dermal papilla cell”, “hair follicle stem cell”, “anagen”, “telogen”, “Wnt/β-catenin”, “TGF-β/SMAD”, “BMP”, and “Shh” were incorporated into the search strategy.

The study selection process was conducted in two stages. The first stage involved title and abstract screening to identify studies relevant to EV-based therapy for AGA. Articles with unclear relevance or insufficient information were advanced to the second stage for full-text review. During the full-text assessment, studies were included if they provided preclinical or clinical evidence on extracellular vesicles, exosomes, or exosome-like nanoparticles for the treatment of AGA. Exclusion criteria were applied to remove studies unrelated to AGA, conference abstracts lacking detailed methodology, and studies with redundant/duplicated data.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was performed using a standardized form to ensure consistency and accuracy. For preclinical studies, the extracted data included study characteristics such as author, year, country, EV source, isolation method, modification, and cargoes. Additionally, in vitro/ex vivo outcomes, in vivo animal models, delivery routes, and in vivo therapeutic outcomes were recorded. Biological Characteristics and Preparation of EVs

EVs are nanometer-scale membranous vesicles actively secreted by cells, characterized by a lipid bilayer structure. They serve as core mediators of both short- and long-range intercellular communication. Their ability to regulate recipient cell function by delivering bioactive “cargo” has garnered significant attention in the field of regenerative medicine.68,69 Based on their biogenesis pathways and physical size, EVs are primarily classified into three categories: exosomes (approximately 30–150 nm in diameter), which originate from the endocytic pathway and are released upon fusion of multivesicular bodies with the plasma membrane; microvesicles (approximately 100–1000 nm), which are generated by direct outward budding of the plasma membrane; and apoptotic bodies, produced during the process of apoptosis (Figure 2).70,71 The formation mechanism of exosomes is relatively well-defined, involving the inward budding of the limiting membrane to form intraluminal vesicles, the maturation of multivesicular bodies, and the process of exocytosis. Their membranes are enriched with tetraspanin proteins such as CD63 and CD81, which are often used as specific markers for their identification.72,73 The core function of EVs lies in their role as complex biological information packages, capable of carrying and delivering functional molecules—including proteins, lipids, DNA, and various RNA species (such as mRNA, miRNA, lncRNA)—to target cells.74,75 Among these, non-coding RNAs like miRNA and lncRNA are particularly crucial due to their ability to precisely regulate gene expression networks at the post-transcriptional level. Through EV-mediated delivery, they can extensively influence multiple biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, metabolism, and microenvironment remodeling.76,77 EVs can be taken up by target cells via various mechanisms, such as direct membrane protein-receptor interaction, membrane fusion, or endocytosis, enabling efficient cargo delivery and biological effects.78 Compared to traditional live-cell therapies, EV-based therapeutics circumvent issues like immune rejection, tumorigenic potential, and cell survival uncertainty. They also offer advantages such as superior stability, ease of storage, and enhanced tissue penetration due to their nano-scale size, highlighting their unique potential as a “cell-free” therapeutic strategy.17,79

Exosomes (30–150 nm) are formed via the endosomal multivesicular body (MVB) pathway. Microvesicles (100–1000 nm) are generated through outward budding of the plasma membrane. Apoptotic bodies (50–5000 nm) are released from cells undergoing programmed cell death. Right panel: Intracellular biogenesis of EVs within a parental cell. Key organelles involved include the nucleus (DNA source), mitochondria (energy metabolism), Golgi complex (protein modification and trafficking), and the endosomal–lysosomal system. The isolation of high-purity EVs from complex biological samples is a prerequisite for functional studies and clinical translation. Currently used isolation techniques each have their own characteristics and limitations. Ultracentrifugation is the most widely applied method and does not introduce exogenous reagents, but it is time-consuming, potentially damaging to vesicles, and difficult to scale up.80 Density gradient centrifugation can achieve higher purity but suffers from low throughput and operational complexity.81 Size-based separation techniques, such as ultrafiltration and size-exclusion chromatography, are relatively simple and gentle, but they face challenges like membrane clogging (leading to low purity) or co-isolation of contaminants, respectively.82,83 Polymer-based precipitation is suitable for preliminary large-scale enrichment but introduces chemical reagents that are difficult to completely remove.84 The universal challenge of balancing yield, purity, and scalability across these methods is a key bottleneck urgently needing resolution for the clinical translation of EVs.85 Notably, the yield, composition, and function of EVs are not fixed but are dynamically regulated by the microenvironment of their parent cells. Studies have confirmed that pre-treating parent cells with external stimuli is an effective strategy to enhance specific functions of EVs. For example, applying mechanical stress (eg, fluid shear stress, stretching),86,87 altering culture conditions (eg, hypoxia, three-dimensional culture),88 or using biochemical stimuli (eg, cytokines, lipopolysaccharides)89,90 can significantly influence EV secretion rates and alter the abundance of their cargo (such as specific miRNAs or proteins), thereby directionally enhancing their efficacy in promoting angiogenesis, anti-inflammation, or tissue repair.91,92 This strategy of producing functionally enhanced, “customized” EVs by modulating cell states provides an important pathway for obtaining high-quality therapeutic EVs. To further overcome the limitations of natural EVs regarding yield, standardization, and drug loading, strategies for engineering EVs have emerged. These primarily include: 1) Loading exogenous therapeutic molecules (such as small-molecule drugs or nucleic acids) into isolated EVs using methods like electroporation, sonication, or extrusion; and 2) Genetically engineering parent cells to secrete EVs expressing targeting peptides or ligands on their surface, thereby conferring active targeting ability to diseased tissues.93,94 Although these engineering approaches show great application potential, they currently face multiple challenges, including loading efficiency, scalable production, quality control, and long-term safety validation. In the future, combining cell preconditioning strategies with engineering technologies, and establishing reproducible, low-cost, high-throughput industrial production and quality control standards, will be key to driving EVs towards clinical application for conditions like AGA.

EVs from Mesenchymal Stem Cells (MSCs)

MSCs derived from various tissues, such as adipose tissue, umbilical cord, bone marrow, and hair follicles,95 secrete EVs whose “cargo” composition and therapeutic properties exhibit diversity and specificity, influenced by their unique microenvironment. Nonetheless, they commonly intervene in the core pathological network of AGA through the synergistic regulation of key signaling pathways, predominantly by activating the pro-hair growth Wnt/β-catenin pathway and inhibiting suppressive signals like the TGF-β/SMAD pathway (Figure 3). EVs from Adipose-Derived Stem Cells

EVs derived from adipose-derived stem cells (ADSC-EVs), including exosomes and engineered nanovesicles, have become a research focus in cell-free therapy for AGA due to their wide availability, ease of isolation, low immunogenicity, and potent paracrine regenerative potential. Their therapeutic mechanism is not limited to a single pathway but involves multi-dimensional antagonism against DHT-induced pathological damage through the coordinated regulation of two pivotal signaling networks: Wnt/β-catenin and TGF-β/SMAD. Research in this area is deepening and has begun to advance toward clinical translation and efficacy enhancement via engineering.

Deepening Molecular Mechanisms: Studies have revealed the precise regulatory roles mediated by ADSC-EVs’ cargo. A previous study systematically elucidated the mechanism by which ADSC-derived exosomes (ADSC-Exos) antagonize DHT effects by delivering enriched miR-122-5p.30 Their research found that while promoting dermal papilla cell (DPC) proliferation and upregulating anagen markers like β-catenin, ADSC-Exos significantly downregulated TGF-β1 and its downstream signaling. Mechanistically, ADSC-Exos deliver miR-122-5p to inhibit the TGF-β/SMAD3 pathway. Another study further confirmed the activating effect of ADSC-Exos on the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. They found ADSC-Exos activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling via GSK-3β phosphorylation.31 From a proteomics perspective, a team identified Cell Division Cycle 42 (CDC42), a functional protein carried in human ADSC-Exos, as crucial for activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.32 Additionally, lncRNA AC010789.1-mediated epigenetic regulation has also been reported to promote HFSC proliferation via Wnt/β-catenin.33

Clinical Translation and Innovative Delivery Strategies: Preliminary clinical reports have verified their safety and initial efficacy. A retrospective study by Byung-Soon Park showed that microneedle-assisted delivery of ADSC-Exos for 12 weeks led to statistically significant improvements in hair density and thickness in 39 AGA patients.58 A prospective study also reported that treatment with ADSC-Exos (ASCE+HRLV®) for over 24 weeks in 30 patients resulted in a significant increase in total hair density, high patient satisfaction, and no serious adverse events.65 Researchers further confirmed that a single microneedling treatment followed by application of an ADSC-derived exosome preparation led to an average hair density increase of 35 hairs/cm2 in 16 patients over 12 months of follow-up, with good tolerability.66 To overcome the limitations of natural exosomes, such as low yield and short half-life, engineering strategies are widely explored. A study evaluated ADSC-derived nanovesicles (ADSC-NVs) prepared by mechanical extrusion. These NVs exhibit a biconcave disc-like structure similar to exosomes and demonstrated hair growth-promoting efficacy comparable to minoxidil both in vitro and in vivo, while also reducing sebum secretion, offering a high-yield alternative.96 More advanced studies have developed smart delivery systems. For instance, engineered ADSC-NVs overexpressing the Junctional Adhesion Molecule A (JAM-A) protein were loaded into a thermosensitive hydrogel. This system not only enabled sustained release but also delivered JAM-A protein, which enhanced autophagy in DPCs to combat DHT and inflammatory damage, significantly improving treatment efficiency in an AGA mouse model.34 Despite the promising outlook, ADSC-EV therapy still faces challenges. Its long-term safety, optimal dosage and regimen, and full elucidation of mechanisms need to be established through large-scale randomized controlled trials.97,98 In the future, combining gene-editing technologies to optimize EV cargo loading or integrating them with existing therapies like lasers or drugs holds promise for further efficacy enhancement.99,100 To date, the first randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial evaluating ADSC-exosomes combined with microneedling for AGA has been reported by Luna-Garza et al.59 In this study, 48 AGA patients were randomized to receive four sessions of microneedling plus topical ADSC-exosomes or microneedling plus saline placebo.59 At 12-week follow-up, the exosome group showed no statistically significant improvements over microneedling alone in any trichoscopic parameter, including hair density, total hair count, shaft thickness, or vellus-to-terminal ratio (all p > 0.05).59 Both groups achieved significant DLQI improvements, and no serious adverse events were reported.59 The authors noted that the negative result may reflect the short follow-up, single-donor exosome preparation, suboptimal dosing, or patient heterogeneity, emphasizing the need for larger studies with standardized exosome potency assays and longer observation periods.59 This rigorous trial provides a critical counterbalance to earlier positive reports and underscores the necessity of sham-controlled designs to distinguish true therapeutic effects from the confounding impact of microneedling.59 In summary, ADSC-EVs counteract the core pathology of AGA through multi-pathway synergy and are steadily advancing toward clinical practicality aided by engineering strategies, representing a highly promising direction in the field of AGA treatment.

EVs from Umbilical Cord Mesenchymal Stem Cells

Exosomes derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSC-Exo) have garnered significant attention due to their abundant source, minimal ethical controversy, low immunogenicity, and potent paracrine and regenerative regulatory capabilities.101 Research demonstrated that in a testosterone-induced AGA mouse model, topical application of hUCMSC-Exo significantly ameliorated the hair loss phenotype. This was evidenced by increased hair length and diameter, a greater number of hair follicles, and reversal of miniaturization, with effects comparable to positive control drugs.35 Further histological analysis revealed upregulation of hair follicle stem cell-associated markers (K15, CD200) and the cell proliferation marker PCNA in the skin of the treatment group, alongside decreased AR expression. This suggests that hUCMSC-Exo may concurrently promote hair follicle regeneration and antagonize androgen signaling.35

The core molecular mechanism underlying its hair growth-promoting effect lies in the precise activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, central to hair follicle regeneration. An in-depth study elucidated the details of this action: hUCMSC-Exo is specifically enriched with microRNAs such as miR-21-5p and let-7b-5p.36 These miRNAs activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in DPCs.36 Furthermore, hUCMSC-Exo can improve the perifollicular microenvironment by modulating ECM, angiogenic, and fibrotic factors.36 Recent mechanistic investigations have further expanded this understanding. Research team demonstrated that hUC-MSC-exosomes deliver let-7b-5p to target deubiquitinase USP12, promoting AR ubiquitination and degradation; simultaneously, let-7b-5p targets DKK3 to activate Wnt/β-catenin from multiple dimensions, while let-7f-5p targets Smad2 to synergistically inhibit TGF-β/Smad signaling, counteracting DHT-induced DPC apoptosis.37 Additionally, researchers reported that hypoxia-preconditioned hUCMSC-derived exosomes (EH-MSCs) upregulated GM-CSF and downregulated IL-1α in a DHT-induced mouse model in a dose-dependent manner, revealing dual regenerative and anti-inflammatory effects via cytokine modulation.38 It is noteworthy that, although hUCMSC-Exo demonstrates clear efficacy, a comparative study indicated that hADSC-Exos might exhibit superior potency over hUCMSC-Exos in promoting DPCs proliferation.32 This difference may be mechanistically linked to protein components specifically carried by hADSC-Exos, such as CDC42, which could more effectively activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and inhibit GSK-3β.32

In summary, hUCMSC-EVs demonstrates significant potential in treating AGA by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway through the delivery of specific miRNA cargos and by improving the follicular microenvironment through multiple dimensions. Building upon these promising preclinical findings, related clinical translation research has been formally initiated. Researchers designed and registered a single-center, prospective, randomized controlled clinical trial to systematically evaluate the safety and efficacy of hUCMSC-EVs for the treatment of AGA in young male patients.102 The study plans to enroll 59 patients, employing a two-phase design: the first phase involves dose escalation to determine the optimal therapeutic dose, and the second phase will compare hUCMSC-EVs (at the optimal dose) with the standard topical therapy of 5% minoxidil. As the first randomized, dose-escalation clinical trial of hUCMSC-EVs for this specific indication, the publication of its results will provide crucial evidence-based medical support for the first extracellular vesicle-based therapy in the field of AGA. These holds promise for establishing a novel regenerative therapeutic strategy for hair restoration.102

EVs Derived from Amniotic Membrane Mesenchymal Stem Cells

Exosomes derived from human amniotic membrane mesenchymal stem cells (hAMSC-exo) have attracted significant attention due to their unique therapeutic potential. Compared to mesenchymal stem cells derived from adipose tissue or bone marrow, hAMSC-exo possess distinct advantages, including freedom from ethical controversies, low immunogenicity, absence of tumorigenic risk, and favorable storage stability, rendering them a more promising candidate for clinical translation.103,104 Research provided the first evidence that both hAMSC-exo and their conditioned medium can effectively mitigate hair follicle miniaturization and significantly promote hair growth in an AGA mouse model. Notably, the therapeutic effect of the exosomes surpassed that of the conditioned medium, suggesting that hAMSC-exo constitutes a key active component in the paracrine action of hAMSCs.39 This study employed single-cell RNA sequencing to analyze treated skin tissue. The results revealed a complex cellular landscape and delineated the differentiation trajectory of hair follicle cells under the influence of hAMSC-exo. A core finding was that hAMSC-exo enhances signaling crosstalk between DPCs and cycling hair follicle cells. DPCs serve as critical signaling hubs within the hair follicle stem cell niche, but their function is impaired in AGA due to persistent DHT stimulation, leading to hair cycle disruption.105 hAMSC-exo was shown to specifically reverse DHT-induced DPC apoptosis and senescence. This action helps to reconstruct a favorable microenvironment for the activation and proper differentiation of HFSCs.105 Further investigation focused on mitochondrial dysfunction as a potential central pathological link in AGA. Hair follicle regeneration is a high-energy-demand process heavily dependent on mitochondrial respiration.106 DHT-induced DPC damage is accompanied by impaired mitochondrial function and excessive ROS accumulation, creating a vicious cycle of oxidative stress and declining cellular function.106,107 This study confirmed that hAMSC-exo can significantly alleviate DHT-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in DPCs. At the molecular level, this protective effect is highly dependent on the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. The study found that β-catenin expression was upregulated in DPCs treated with hAMSC-exo, and inhibiting this pathway attenuated the mitochondrial protective effect of hAMSC-exo.39 These findings are consistent with prior research establishing the Wnt/β-catenin pathway as central to regulating the hair follicle cycle and DPC function, with small-molecule agonists of this pathway already entering clinical trials for hair loss treatment.108,109

EVs Derived from Foreskin Mesenchymal Stromal Cells

The application of exosomes derived from foreskin mesenchymal stromal cells (F-MSC-Exos) for the treatment of androgenetic alopecia represents an exploratory yet directly translatable approach within the field of cell-free stem cell therapy. Preliminary supportive evidence comes from a prospective clinical study conducted by Mert Ersan et al.23 This study evaluated the effect of scalp injections with F-MSC-Exos in 30 male AGA patients classified as Norwood-Hamilton types III–VI. Characterization of the administered vesicles confirmed their exosomal identity, with an average particle size of approximately 139.7 ± 2.3 nm. The results demonstrated both objective and subjective improvements. Objectively, a statistically significant increase in hair density was observed at both the 4-week and 12-week follow-up time points. Subjectively, patient-reported satisfaction scores also showed significant enhancement over the treatment period. Notably, no side effects or complications were reported throughout the observation period, providing initial evidence for the safety and hair growth-promoting efficacy of topical F-MSC-Exos application.23 The mechanism is presumed to involve delivery of pro-regenerative miRNAs and growth factors to DPCs and HFSCs, as commonly reported for MSC-EVs.110,111 This facilitates the precise delivery of their cargo, enabling the regulation of key signaling pathways involved in the hair follicle cycle, cell proliferation, and anti-apoptosis. Additionally, by potentially delivering anti-inflammatory factors, they may help ameliorate the perifollicular microinflammatory environment associated with AGA progression.112

EVs from Hair Follicle Mesenchymal Stem Cells

HFSCs and their derived EVs demonstrate unique potential for AGA treatment, owing to their inherent follicular tropism and niche-regulating capabilities. Current research is advancing along two complementary directions: first, the clinical exploration of minimally manipulated micrografts (MCGs) rich in HF-MSCs and native EVs; and second, the engineering of HFSC-EVs to construct multifunctional synergistic therapy platforms. At the clinical application level, evidence from a multicenter retrospective study supports the safety and preliminary efficacy of the MCG-based approach.60 This study followed 60 patients with mild-to-moderate AGA for one year. Quantified assessment using standardized trichoscopy revealed a significant increase in hair density within the target area post-treatment (an increase of 30 ± 5 hairs/cm2 for male pattern hair loss and 28 ± 4 hairs/cm2 for female pattern hair loss). The treatment was well-tolerated, with no reports of severe adverse events.60 The core principle of this therapy lies in the MCGs—obtained via mechanical dissociation of autologous scalp tissue—which preserve the active components of the hair follicle niche. These include CD44+ HF-MSCs, CD200+ hair follicle epithelial stem cells, and various paracrine cells.113,114 Research further confirmed the presence of EVs expressing typical mesenchymal markers (eg, CD9, CD63, CD81) within the MCG suspension, promoting fibroblast proliferation/migration and resisting oxidative stress, as a key effector mediating the regulation of the follicular microenvironment and regenerative repair.115,116

To address the limitations of natural EVs in terms of scalable production, stability, and drug-loading capacity, and to integrate multiple therapeutic mechanisms, research has progressed into the engineering phase. A team developed an innovative hybrid vesicle platform (Hybrid/Au@Fi).67 This system was created by fusing HFSC-derived EVs with artificial liposomal membranes, enabling the efficient co-loading of gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) and the drug finasteride. This platform integrates EV-mediated signaling, finasteride’s pharmacological action, and AuNP-induced photothermal stimulation.67,117 In an AGA animal model, this hybrid vesicle platform demonstrated superior efficacy in promoting hair follicle cycle transition and enhancing hair quality compared to conventional 5% topical minoxidil, highlighting the synergistic advantage of engineered EVs in integrating “biological signaling”, “pharmacological treatment”, and “physical stimulation”.67

EVs Derived from Dental Pulp Stem Cells

Extracellular vesicles derived from dental pulp stem cells (DPSC-EVs), recognized for their robust tissue repair and regenerative potential, demonstrate unique value in developing integrated therapeutic strategies for the complex pathology of AGA. In an innovative approach, researchers constructed a multifunctional bio-composite microneedle system (Se-HEVs-AMN). This system is designed to synergistically target three core pathological aspects of AGA—ROS accumulation, microvascular damage, and persistent inflammation—through a single platform.40 Its core strategy involves using hypoxia-preconditioned DPSC exosomes as carriers to encapsulate selenium nanozymes (SeNP) with potent antioxidant and potential anti-androgenic activity, which are then delivered efficiently via astragalus polysaccharide microneedles (AMN).

Within this integrated system, each component is assigned a distinct and complementary role based on its biological properties. Hypoxic preconditioning activates hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), upregulating the expression of pro-angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor within DPSC-EVs. This boosts the inherent ability of HEVs to improve follicular microcirculation.118,119 SeNPs serve as the core active component against oxidative stress. Mimicking the function of selenoproteins like glutathione peroxidase in the human body, they efficiently scavenge local ROS in the hair follicle microenvironment. Derived from selenocysteine, they also offer good biocompatibility.120,121 The AMN, functioning as the delivery vehicle, achieve non-invasive and efficient transdermal delivery of the Se-HEVs complex to the target dermal layer. Moreover, the astragalus polysaccharide matrix itself has been shown to exert anti-inflammatory effects by modulating pathways such as Nrf2/HO-1, helping to quell perifollicular inflammation.122,123 In an AGA mouse model, this multi-component system validated its synergistic therapeutic potential. Following application, the microneedle patch dissolved rapidly, releasing the Se-HEVs complex. The composite exerted multiple effects locally at the hair follicles, ultimately resulting in significant improvements in hair density and increased hair follicle diameter.40 Complementing this engineering strategy, Research team systematically investigated the therapeutic mechanism of naïve DPSC-Exos in AGA.41 In a DHT-induced mouse model, DPSC-Exos significantly promoted hair regeneration, increasing follicular density and dermal thickness with efficacy comparable to minoxidil.41 Mechanistically, DPSC-Exos activated the PI3K/Akt pathway to upregulate VEGFA and other pro-angiogenic factors (FGF2, ANGPT1), thereby restoring the perifollicular vascular network and reversing DHT-induced DPC dysfunction.41 The necessity of PI3K/Akt activation was confirmed by complete abrogation of these effects upon PI3K inhibition.41 This study positions DPSC-Exos as a promising cell-free therapeutic strategy for AGA with a clearly defined mechanistic basis.41

EVs Derived from Bone Marrow Mesenchymal Stem Cells

The clinical potential of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (BMSC-EVs) for AGA treatment received preliminary validation in a retrospective, open-label study conducted by Sasaki et al.22 This study evaluated a single intradermal injection of an allogeneic human bone marrow MSC-EV preparation (XoFlo) in 31 patients with early or stable AGA. Six-month follow-up confirmed safety and efficacy in promoting hair regrowth.22 Efficacy analysis revealed several key clinical covariates. Treatment response exhibited correlations with demographic and disease characteristics: better responses were observed in women over 50 and men under 50, patients with a shorter history of hair loss (<8 years), and those at earlier Norwood-Hamilton/Ludwig classification stages. This suggests that younger male patients, who typically present with active hair follicle miniaturization and shorter disease duration, may represent a more responsive demographic for allogeneic BMSC-EV therapy. The superior efficacy in this subgroup could be attributed to the relatively preserved regenerative capacity of their hair follicle niches and the distinct endocrine milieu compared to older patients. However, further mechanistic studies are required to validate this hypothesis and optimize treatment protocols specifically for younger AGA patients.22 Furthermore, treatment parameters and prior treatment history also held predictive value. Greater benefit was associated with the use of a higher-dose, undiluted EV preparation and a previous positive response to minoxidil, finasteride, or platelet-rich plasma (PRP). Conversely, the presence of systemic factors potentially interfering with hair growth, such as concurrent autoimmune or endocrine disorders, was linked to a poorer treatment response.22 However, the study also highlighted important unresolved questions and limitations. First, the mechanistic basis for the observed gender- and age-based dichotomy in efficacy remains unclear, potentially relating to the complex endocrine milieu in women.124,125 Second, the precise biological endpoints induced by the EVs—such as prolongation of the anagen phase or promotion of vellus-to-terminal hair conversion—have yet to be elucidated.22 Additionally, the study’s conclusions warrant further validation in prospective, large-scale trials due to its relatively small sample size, the fact that nearly half of the patients did not complete the final quantitative assessment, and the lack of long-term follow-up data.

EVs from Induced Mesenchymal Stem Cells Derived from Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells

In the context of EV-based strategies for AGA treatment, a key challenge lies in sourcing homogeneous, scalable, and potent vesicles. While MSC-derived EVs show therapeutic promise, the inherent heterogeneity, limited expansion capacity, and potential immunogenicity of MSCs themselves constrain their clinical application 30561809. To address this, induced mesenchymal stem cells (iMSCs) differentiated from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) have emerged as an ideal alternative cell source for producing therapeutic-grade EVs, owing to their superior homogeneity and near-unlimited expansion potential.126,127 A study introduced an innovative “cell-preconditioning” strategy involving the stimulation of iMSCs with hyaluronic acid (HA) prior to EV collection.24 HA, a natural polysaccharide, possesses various biological functions, including immunomodulation and promotion of cell migration.128 The research demonstrated that EVs obtained from these HA-pretreated iMSCs (HA–iMSC–EVs) exhibited significantly enhanced efficacy in treating AGA compared to EVs from untreated iMSCs (iMSC-EVs). The enhancement mechanism primarily involves the synergistic reversal of core AGA pathological pathways. HA-iMSC-EVs dually suppress AR/TGF-β/IL-6 signaling and activate Wnt/β-catenin via GSK3β inhibition.24 In contrast, iMSC-EVs from untreated cells failed to induce these significant changes. This synergistic action was validated in an animal model. Topical application of HA–iMSC–EVs significantly restored hair growth in AGA mice, with efficacy comparable to the positive control drug finasteride. Furthermore, it reversed the testosterone-induced decrease in the proportion of anagen hair follicles—an effect not achieved by iMSC-EVs alone.24 This study demonstrates that the functional properties of EVs can be actively designed and enhanced by preconditioning parent cells with biomaterials like HA. HA–iMSC–EVs achieve more effective multi-target therapy for AGA through the synergistic modulation of the AR signaling and Wnt/β-catenin pathways.

EVs Derived from Somatic Cells and Blood Components

EVs from somatic cells and blood components offer precise therapeutic strategies for AGA by leveraging the inherent properties of functional cells or their engineered modifications. Unlike stem cell-derived vesicles, these approaches directly utilize mature cells with specific functions. Through engineering, their targeting capability, signaling complexity, or scalable production is enhanced for more precise intervention in the hair follicle microenvironment. EVs from Dermal Papilla Cells

DPCs, as the central signaling source regulating the hair follicle cycle, play a key role whose dysfunction is critical in AGA pathogenesis. Recently, EVs secreted by DPCs themselves (DPC-EVs) have emerged as highly promising cell-free therapeutic tools, owing to their ability to deliver the full bioactive repertoire of their parent cells (eg, hair growth-promoting cytokines and miRNAs). Research in this area primarily advances along two strategies: first, obtaining functionally enhanced EVs through biointerventional preconditioning of DPCs; and second, engineering DPC-EVs into advanced targeted delivery platforms.

For functional EV acquisition, a previous study preconditioned DPCs with a traditional Chinese herbal compound (LTF) and collected the secreted exosomes (LTF-DPC-EXO).42 In an AGA mouse model, these exosomes promoted hair regeneration by regulating the PI3K/AKT pathway to protect DPCs, inhibit apoptosis, and induce anagen transition (molecular details in Table 2).42 This exemplifies “customizing” EV therapeutic effects through external intervention.

To address limitations of natural EVs—such as limited drug loading and short cutaneous retention—research has moved into engineering. A research team developed HDAC1-overexpressing engineered nanovesicles (HDAC1OE@NVs) via genetic modification of DPCs, based on findings of suppressed HDAC1 in balding scalps.44 These vesicles enhanced proliferation and migration while mitigating DHT-induced cytotoxicity through p53 and Wnt/β-Catenin modulation. Loaded onto a dissolvable microneedle platform, they enabled sustained release and outperformed minoxidil in an AGA animal model.44 Separately, another group constructed a hybrid system fusing minoxidil-loaded flexible liposomes with DPC-derived exosomes, embedded in a hydrogel for topical application.43 This design integrates enhanced penetration, follicle targeting, and sustained release, promoting follicle cycling, density, and angiogenesis with reduced irritation compared to conventional minoxidil (Table 2).43,129 This marks the transition of DPC-EVs from “bioactive supplements” to “intelligent targeted delivery systems”.

EVs from Human Embryonic Kidney Cells

The engineered exosome platform EX104, derived from human embryonic kidney (HEK)-293 cells, represents an advanced targeted therapeutic strategy designed from a profound understanding of AGA’s pathological mechanisms. This platform is based on genetically engineering HEK-293 cells to stably co-express and secrete exosomes enriched with three key hair growth-promoting factors: WNT10B, VEGFA, and FGF7.45 This design co-delivers pleiotropic signals within a single nanocarrier: WNT10B activates DPC and stem cell proliferation;130 VEGFA potently promotes perifollicular angiogenesis, improving nutrient supply;131 and FGF7 helps enlarge hair follicle size and maintain the anagen phase.132 This multi-factor strategy concurrently targets DPC dysfunction, microvascular atrophy, and keratinocyte apoptosis.133–135 In preclinical models, EX104 reversed DHT-induced miniaturization in human DPCs and downregulated key negative regulators including AR, DKK1, and IL-6.45 In DHT-induced AGA mice, topical EX104 achieved hair regeneration comparable to minoxidil, with additional advantages in stimulating perifollicular angiogenesis and follicle cell proliferation.45 Mechanistically, it activates β-catenin signaling while inhibiting local inflammation and apoptosis (Table 2).45 This multimodal effect enables EX104 to achieve “reprogramming” of the pathological hair follicle microenvironment.

In a complementary approach, a team developed a dual-factor engineered exosome platform co-delivering epidermal growth factor (EGF) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) using the LAMP2B membrane-anchoring technology.46 Clinical sample analysis confirmed that EGF and FGF expression are significantly downregulated in the dermal papilla cells of AGA patients, accompanied by reduced expression of NOTCH signaling pathway proteins critical for hair follicle regeneration.46 Using 293T cells as donor cells, the researchers constructed LAMP2B fusion proteins to anchor EGF and FGF onto the exosomal membrane surface, generating EXO-EGF/FGF with significantly higher growth factor loading than naïve exosomes.46 In vitro, EXO-EGF/FGF promoted the proliferation and migration of HDPCs by reactivating the cell cycle and enhancing migration-related programs.46 In a testosterone-induced AGA mouse model, local injection of EXO-EGF/FGF effectively restored hair coverage density and follicular structural integrity, significantly increasing the number of anagen-phase hair follicles without eliciting immunogenic responses or systemic toxicity.46 Mechanistically, EGF directly interacts with NOTCH signaling to maintain hair follicle stem cell pluripotency and drive cell cycle progression,136,137 while FGF activates the FGFR3-ERK1/2 pathway to promote β-catenin nuclear translocation.138 This dual-factor synergistic strategy overcomes the inherent limitations of conventional therapies and naïve exosomes—particularly their low endogenous growth factor content and lack of follicular targeting—offering a novel, safe, and clinically translatable therapeutic approach for AGA.46

EVs from Salmon

Beyond mammalian cell sources, a recent study has explored the therapeutic potential of extracellular vesicles derived from salmon (Oncorhynchus keta) for AGA treatment. Unlike human MSC‑derived EVs, which face limitations of donor variability, high production costs, and scalability challenges, salmon‑derived EVs offer a cost‑effective and readily available alternative. Mechanistically, these non-engineered EVs promote hair follicle stem cell proliferation and enhance perifollicular angiogenesis via the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway (Table 2).36,139 When combined with microneedle therapy, salmon EVs synergistically enhance hair growth through a dual mechanism: direct stimulation of hair follicle stem cells and indirect promotion of angiogenesis via controlled micro‑injury‑induced VEGF and PDGF release.140 Although clinical translation remains preliminary and requires larger controlled trials to confirm efficacy, salmon‑derived EVs represent a promising, economically viable, and scalable platform for AGA treatment, particularly as an alternative to human cell‑derived products.23,141

EVs from Red Blood Cells

Among the various strategies for EV-based AGA therapy, extracellular vesicles derived from red blood cells (RBC-EVs) constitute a highly promising therapeutic dimension due to their unique origin and functions, offering significant resource potential and novel biological insights. As the most abundant cell population in the human body (approximately 20–30 trillion), mature red blood cells, which lack a nucleus and common organelles, can themselves be viewed as a class of highly specialized natural “vesicles”, rich in hemoglobin with a unique membrane composition.142 RBC-EVs released by these cells carry hemoglobin, glycophorins, and a rich array of miRNAs and lipids (such as ceramides). They are involved not only in systemic redox and immunoregulatory balance,143 but recent research further reveals they serve as important messengers and material carriers connecting the circulatory and integumentary systems.

The link between anemia and impaired skin/hair homeostasis has long been observed,144 and a group of researchers further provided mechanistic insight while pioneering RBC-EV therapy for AGA.47 Local injection of autologous RBC-EVs promoted hair growth through a dual mechanism: “structural reconstruction” (incorporating hemoglobin and keratins into the hair shaft) and “programmatic activation” (delivering miRNAs to upregulate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and drive telogen-to-anagen transition) (specific miRNAs in Table 2).47 Notably, they proposed that skin and hair follicles represent a physiological clearance and integration endpoint for RBC-EVs47,145—EVs from daily erythrocyte turnover are actively taken up by skin, supplying barrier lipids146 and maintaining microenvironmental homeostasis. Thus, RBC-EV therapy goes beyond “drug delivery”: it harnesses the body’s most abundant intrinsic cellular resource to repair the degenerated follicle niche by mimicking and amplifying a natural physiological process, offering a resource-efficient, mechanism-aligned therapeutic direction.

EVs Derived from Platelet-Rich Plasma

Extracellular vesicles derived from platelet-rich plasma (PRP-EVs) represent both a key effector component responsible for the regenerative effects of PRP therapy in AGA and an emerging cell-free therapeutic strategy. Compared to PRP itself, which is complex in composition and difficult to standardize, PRP-EVs selectively encapsulate the diverse growth factors (such as VEGF, PDGF18) within biomembrane vesicles, offering low immunogenicity, high stability, and targeted delivery potential.147–149 A study demonstrated that PRP-EVs are internalized by DPCs, promoting proliferation, migration, and cell cycle progression; they prolong anagen in hair follicle explants; and in AGA models, topical application induced telogen-to-anagen conversion with efficacy comparable to minoxidil but without skin irritation.48,150 Mechanistically, this is achieved through Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation via downregulation of SFRP1 (Table 2),151,152 echoing the mechanism of DPC-EVs and highlighting the central role of Wnt signaling in EV-mediated hair regeneration,153,154 although other pathways like TGF-β/BMP and Shh may also contribute to the complex regulatory network.155–157

However, clinical translation of PRP-EVs faces substantial standardization challenges. Primary challenges include donor heterogeneity (age,158 health status159), which affects EV bioactivity; lack of consensus on platelet activation methods (eg, calcium vs thrombin160), which alters yield and proteomic profiles;161,162 and isolation technique variability—ultracentrifugation may co-isolate contaminants,163 SEC improves purity but risks stripping surface protein corona,164,165 while tangential flow filtration may better balance yield and integrity.164 Additionally, the vague definition of active ingredients complicates quality standard establishment.166

To advance clinical application, future efforts should focus on: 1) Adhering to standardization guidelines (eg, ISEV-MISEV167) and sharing protocols via EV-TRACK;168 2) Exploring scalable isolation methods that preserve functional protein corona;166 3) Developing delivery systems like hydrogels169,170 or microneedles for sustained release and improved compliance; and 4) Systematically elucidating the full signaling network beyond Wnt and exploring synergies with existing therapies like minoxidil.

Plant-Derived Exosome-Like Nanoparticles

Plant-Derived exosome-like nanoparticles (PENs) offer a unique “cross-kingdom regulation” strategy for AGA therapy. Benefiting from their natural origin, excellent biocompatibility, and superior stability,171–173 PENs not only serve as efficient nanocarriers for delivering plant-specific active ingredients but also enable the cross-kingdom delivery of plant miRNAs to human hair follicle cells, allowing direct transcriptional-level intervention against core targets such as the androgen receptor. Furthermore, owing to their small size, strong permeability, and negative surface charge,174,175 PENs can function as intelligent platforms to enhance the transdermal absorption and synergistic efficacy of conventional drugs, with some formulations already demonstrating clinical potential. This strategy organically integrates the multi-target advantages of medicinal plants with the precision delivery of nanotechnology, paving a highly promising path for developing novel, nature-derived AGA therapies (Figure 3).

Nano-Vesicles Derived from Platycladus Orientalis Leaves

Nano-vesicles derived from Platycladus orientalis leaves (PcNs) serve as a paradigm due to their dual attributes as both “active ingredients” and “intelligent carriers”. In an innovative study, a group utilized PcNs as carriers to load the conventional drug finasteride, constructing a synergistic therapeutic nanop platform (FIN-PcNs) and systematically evaluating its efficacy.49

The core advantage of this strategy lies in the multiple synergistic effects of PcNs. Firstly, PcNs are derived from Platycladus orientalis leaves, which have a long history of use in hair growth applications. They naturally carry active components such as flavonoids and terpenoids, known for their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and hair growth-promoting properties.176,177 Secondly, as nanocarriers, PcNs significantly enhance the transdermal penetration and local retention of the loaded drug FIN, promoting its targeted accumulation in the epidermis and dermis while reducing systemic exposure risk.49 In a testosterone-induced AGA mouse model, topical application of FIN-PcNs demonstrated significantly superior efficacy in reversing hair follicle miniaturization and stimulating hair growth compared to the positive control minoxidil.49 Mechanistically, this synergy combines PcNs’ intrinsic bioactivity—scavenging ROS and modulating inflammation—with FIN’s efficient local 5α-reductase inhibition, ultimately upregulating hair follicle regeneration-related genes.49 This research offers a functional alternative to traditional plant extracts or single-drug formulations by utilizing natural nano-vesicles as a multifunctional platform. PcNs not only address the challenge of low local delivery efficiency for FIN, but their own contributed therapeutic activity synergizes with the drug’s effect. This provides a new paradigm for AGA treatment: using modern pharmacologically validated plant-derived natural vesicles as “intelligent” delivery systems in combination with conventional or novel drugs to achieve enhanced efficacy and reduced toxicity. Nevertheless, their long-term safety and efficacy in humans require further validation.

Beyond their role as drug carriers, the intrinsic bioactivity of Platycladus orientalis‑derived vesicles (PO-EVs) has been further leveraged in a complementary MN-based strategy for co-delivering PO-EVs and minoxidil nanoparticles (MXD NPs) for AGA treatment.50 PO-EVs possess intrinsic antioxidant, anti‑inflammatory, and pro‑angiogenic activities that directly address the oxidative and inflammatory microenvironment limiting conventional topical therapies.50 The MN patch, fabricated from chondroitin sulfate and carboxymethyl chitosan, exhibits sufficient mechanical strength for skin insertion and rapid dissolution for efficient intradermal delivery.50 In an AGA mouse model, dual‑loaded MN treatment synergistically promoted telogen‑to‑anagen transition, increased hair coverage and follicular density, and restored normal follicular morphology.50 Mechanistically, the platform activated HFSCs, reduced perifollicular oxidative stress and inflammation, and enhanced microvascular density without significant toxicity.50 Notably, while the PcNs platform primarily utilized Platycladus orientalis vesicles as carriers for finasteride, the PO-EV strategy directly leverages the vesicles as active therapeutic agents, illustrating the dual functionality of plant‑derived vesicles as both active therapeutic agents and intelligent delivery platforms. By overcoming limitations of poor cutaneous penetration and transient retention, this MN‑based co‑delivery strategy offers a translatable approach for AGA and potentially other inflammation-driven skin disorders.50

Plant Exosome-Like Nanoparticles from Polygonum Multiflorum

Research on Polygoni Multiflori Radix-derived exosome-like nanoparticles (PMENs) has revealed a novel therapeutic pathway based on “cross-kingdom gene regulation” that extends beyond traditional active compounds.51 A previous study demonstrated that PMENs can be efficiently internalized by DPCs via caveolin- and heparan sulfate proteoglycan-mediated endocytosis.51 The core mechanism involves PMENs delivering plant-derived miRNAs—notably aof-miR168a and osa-miR164a—that directly target and suppress human AR gene expression by binding to conserved sites in its 3’-UTR.51 AR downregulation inhibits its downstream target DKK1 (a Wnt/β-catenin pathway inhibitor), while PMENs treatment also increases GSK3β phosphorylation to stabilize β-catenin, synergistically activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway essential for hair follicle regeneration.51 These dual actions synergistically and potently activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which is essential for hair follicle regeneration. In a testosterone-induced hair regrowth delay model using C57BL/6 mice, topical application of PMENs demonstrated efficacy superior to minoxidil in restoring hair growth, validating its in vivo effectiveness.51

This study expands the understanding of the therapeutic material basis of the traditional Chinese herb Polygonum multiflorum from traditional small-molecule compounds like stilbenes178 to bioactive nano-vesicles and their carried genetic information. It provides the first evidence that plant-derived vesicles can perform cross-kingdom regulation by delivering miRNAs to directly intervene against AR, a core pathological factor in AGA. Although a portion of internalized PMENs enters the lysosomal pathway, the protective effect of their lipid bilayer and potential non-canonical endosomal escape mechanisms are sufficient to ensure the successful delivery of functional miRNAs.179,180 This research not only offers a modern molecular biological explanation for the traditional efficacy of Polygonum multiflorum but also establishes a solid theoretical foundation for developing precise, low-toxicity novel therapies based on plant vesicles. Future studies need to further elucidate the cross-kingdom regulatory network of their miRNAs, optimize their delivery efficiency, and conduct systematic safety and efficacy evaluations in models more closely resembling humans.

Exosomes Derived from Germinated Hemp Seed Callus Culture

Exosomes derived from a germinated hemp (Cannabis sativa) seed callus culture (referred to as E40 in the study) exhibit a unique, integrated mode of synergistic action combining multi-target gene regulation, promotion of follicular differentiation, and immunomodulation. Compared to a traditional germinated hemp seed extract (GHSE, or GE2000), E40 demonstrated significantly greater biological potency at a much lower dose (40 µg/mL vs 2000 µg/mL), highlighting the advantage of plant-derived vesicles as highly efficient, nano-scale bioactive complexes.52

Its core mechanism lies in multi-level intervention targeting DPCs and the local immune microenvironment. Under DHT exposure, E40 reversed DHT-induced gene imbalance by upregulating pro-hair growth axes (Wnt, β-catenin, TCF) while suppressing hair loss-related genes (STAT1, 5α-reductase type 1, IL-15R). Notably, E40 suppressed cytotoxicity- and inflammation-associated genes (NKG2DL, IL2-Rβ, JAK1, STAT1) in CD8+ T cells, suggesting mitigation of immune attacks on hair follicles via JAK-STAT modulation (Table 2).52 Furthermore, E40 promoted the differentiation of DPCs into functional hair follicle stem cells and DPCs, thereby strengthening the foundation for follicular self-renewal and regeneration.52 In a DHT-induced chicken embryo feather loss model, topical E40 restored feather length and density while coordinately upregulating both SHH (PTCH1, SMO, GLI1) and Wnt/β-catenin pathways and downregulating BMP4 and AR (Table 2).53 This confirmed that E40 can antagonize the pathogenic effects of DHT through multi-pathway synergy, with efficacy superior to that of the traditional extract.

An Exosome-Based Formulation Containing Ecklonia Cava and Thuja Orientalis Extracts

In exploring the clinical translation pathway for PENs in AGA treatment, a study evaluated the efficacy of an exosome-based formulation containing extracts from the brown seaweed (Ecklonia cava) and the oriental arborvitae (Thuja orientalis).61 This randomized controlled trial involving 20 male AGA patients (Norwood grade 2–3) demonstrated that after 16 weeks (spanning 4 treatment sessions), the ECPE group showed a statistically significant increase in hair count from baseline, whereas no significant change was observed in the placebo group. Intergroup comparison further confirmed that the hair growth-promoting effect of ECPE was significantly superior to that of placebo, accompanied by a large effect size (Cliff’s Delta: Δ = 0.73) and improved patient-reported satisfaction. No significant adverse events were reported.61 This positive clinical outcome provides initial human evidence for the therapeutic strategy combining plant-derived active ingredients with an exosomal carrier.

The potential efficacy of the ECPE formulation stems from the complementary mechanisms through which its two core botanical components act on the follicular microenvironment. The Ecklonia cava extract, rich in compounds like phlorotannins, possesses antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. It can reduce oxidative stress in DPCs and inhibit 5α-reductase activity as well as androgen-induced TGF-β1 secretion.181 The Thuja orientalis extract has been shown to activate the β-catenin and SHH signaling pathways, both crucial for hair follicle development and cycle regulation.176 The key innovation lies in integrating these synergistic actives into an exosomal carrier, which protects bioactive molecules from degradation and may enhance tropism toward follicular target cells, enabling more precise multi-pathway regulation (Table 2). This study marks an important step for plant-derived nano-vesicle therapy, moving from preclinical mechanistic research towards initial clinical validation. It exemplifies a research and development strategy that fuses modern pharmacologically validated plant actives with advanced bio-nanocarrier technology. While its precise mode of action (eg, whether the exosomes serve as the primary active source or an efficacy-enhancing delivery vehicle) requires further in-depth mechanistic elucidation, and larger-scale, longer-term clinical trials are needed to confirm its long-term safety and efficacy, the preliminary success of ECPE undoubtedly points to a highly promising direction. It guides the development of novel AGA therapies that combine the multi-target regulatory advantages of natural products with the precision delivery characteristics of nanotechnology.

Exosomes Derived from Poria Cocos

Exosome-like nanoparticles derived from the medicinal fungus Poria cocos (PCEVs) have recently emerged as a distinctive therapeutic platform for AGA.54 To overcome the limitations of poor local retention and unstable release, researchers developed an injectable polysaccharide hydrogel (CMC–Na/SA/HA) for localized PCEV delivery, which stabilizes vesicular cargo, enables sustained release, and creates a permissive regenerative microenvironment.54 The matrix components—HA, SA, and CMC—synergistically support cell adhesion, vascularization, and fibroblast proliferation.182–184

In a DHT‑induced AGA mouse model, PCEV@CMC/SA significantly accelerated hair regeneration and increased follicular density.54 Mechanistically, PCEVs activate the Wnt/β-catenin axis and downstream transcription factors, orchestrating HFSC activation and lineage progression via modulation of quiescence and differentiation markers (Table 2).54,185 Notably, unlike plant EVs that primarily target inflammation or short‑term proliferation, this platform directly addresses HFSC homeostasis and structural reconstruction, with tangential flow filtration ensuring scalable production.54 While further studies are needed to establish dose‑response relationships and identify active molecular components, the PCEV@CMC/SA system represents a robust and clinically promising strategy for AGA treatment.54

Ginger-Derived Exosome-Like Nanoparticles

Ginger-derived exosome-like nanoparticles (GELNs) have recently emerged as a promising plant-based platform for AGA treatment, offering dual functionality as both active therapeutic agents and efficient drug delivery vehicles.55 GELNs, with sizes of 30–150 nm, possess intrinsic anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and bio-regulatory properties while exhibiting low immunogenicity and high delivery efficiency.186,187 To overcome the limitations of poor solubility and systemic adverse effects associated with oral finasteride—researchers developed a finasteride-loaded GELN system integrated into a thermosensitive poloxamer gel (FIN@GELNs-Gel) for enhanced transdermal delivery.55 The poloxamer-based gel exhibits sol‑gel transition at body temperature, facilitating drug loading at low temperature and sustained release upon topical application.188,189 In vitro, FIN@GELNs-Gel promoted cell migration and angiogenesis, suggesting improved perifollicular microenvironment remodeling.55 In an AGA mouse model, the formulation significantly reduced DHT levels in both skin and serum, upregulated VEGF and Ki67 expression, and effectively accelerated telogen‑to‑anagen transition (Table 2).55 Importantly, FIN@GELNs-Gel demonstrated superior hair regeneration efficacy compared to oral finasteride, with reduced dosing frequency and minimized systemic toxicity risk.55 Although industrial scale‑up and drug loading capacity require further optimization, this integrated nanovesicle‑hydrogel platform offers a safe, effective, and patient‑friendly topical therapeutic strategy for AGA, with promising implications for improving treatment adherence and clinical translation.55

Exosomes Derived from Rose Stem Cells

Exosomes derived from rose stem cells (RSCEs), as an emerging plant-derived therapeutic agent, have demonstrated their potential for AGA treatment through preliminary clinical cases, revealing possible additional benefits beyond promoting hair regeneration. Two cases provided direct evidence for the clinical application of RSCEs.63,64 In the first case, a 54-year-old male AGA patient (Hamilton-Norwood grade V), who declined conventional therapies, underwent a treatment regimen combining non-invasive electroporation with topical application of RSCEs for a total of 12 sessions administered every 3 weeks.63 The electroporation technique aims to temporarily enhance skin permeability via pulsed electric fields to facilitate the transdermal delivery of large molecules like exosomes.190 This combined protocol showed favorable efficacy: an increase in hair density and thickness was observed starting from the third session, with significant improvement achieved by the end of the treatment cycle. The results were maintained 3 months after the final session, and no serious adverse events were reported throughout the course.63 In another case involving a 38-year-old male patient with concurrent AGA and localized canities (graying hair), a treatment regimen combining RSCEs with fractional picosecond laser (FPL) was employed.64 The results indicated that after completing four treatment sessions, not only was the alopecic area improved, but repigmentation of the localized white hair patches on the scalp was also observed.64 These cases suggest that RSCEs combined with physical enhancement delivery techniques may represent a safe and effective novel strategy for AGA treatment.

The efficacy of RSCEs likely stems from their multifaceted biological activities. In terms of composition, these plant-derived vesicles (approximately 100–200 nm in size) contain a specific proteome (206 proteins have been identified) and microRNAs (such as miR-8485, miR-574-5p, etc), which are thought to play roles in regulating cell proliferation and inflammation.73,191 In vitro, RSCEs safely promote human DPC proliferation and activity. Mechanistically, they are believed to activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway while delivering growth factors and exerting anti-inflammatory/antioxidant effects.192,193 The mechanism behind the observed repigmentation of white hair is more complex. It may involve the modulation of hair follicle melanocyte stem cells or pigmentation pathways by RSCEs.192,194 However, this phenomenon could also be related to the micro-injury stimulation and subsequent wound-healing response induced by the co-administered fractional laser.64,195

Milk-Derived EVs

In the exploration of innovative therapies for AGA, milk-derived EVs (mEVs) have emerged as a promising new direction with significant clinical translation potential, owing to their wide availability, low cost, high safety profile, and suitability for scalable production.99 Compared to EVs derived from stem cells, which require complex cultivation, mEVs can be directly sourced from abundant dairy resources, circumventing high production costs and potential immunogenicity risks, thereby laying a foundation for large-scale clinical application.196,197

The therapeutic value of mEVs is first reflected in their naturally carried bioactive “cargo”, with researchers demonstrating that exosomes isolated directly from bovine colostrum (Milk-exo) can effectively promote the proliferation of human DPCs and antagonize DHT-induced hair follicle growth arrest in vivo.56 In animal models, their efficacy in inducing hair growth was comparable to the first-line clinical drug minoxidil, with no significant adverse reactions reported.56 Mechanistically, Milk-exo drives telogen-to-anagen transition by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, an effect attributed not to a single component (eg, lactoferrin) but to a “multifunctional active molecular complex” comprising over 1000 growth factors, cytokines, and miRNAs.198,199

Beyond their direct use as natural actives, the more significant strategic value of mEVs lies in their potential as efficient drug delivery platforms. A study provided proof of concept for this: they constructed engineered drug-loaded vesicles (Cur-mEVs) by loading the potent anti-inflammatory molecule curcumin into mEVs via electroporation.57 In an AGA mouse model, Cur-mEVs demonstrated superior synergistic effects: retaining mEVs’ intrinsic Wnt/β-catenin activation while delivered curcumin downregulated TGF-β1 and reduced perifollicular inflammation (Table 2).57 This “vesicle-based drug delivery” combined with “intrinsic vesicle activity” achieves a synergistic “two-in-one” effect, overcoming curcumin’s poor solubility and low bioavailability while comprehensively remodeling the follicular microenvironment.57,200 The study also confirmed the favorable biosafety profile of Cur-mEVs under experimental conditions.57

mEVs offer a dual solution for AGA treatment, serving as both an “off-the-shelf” therapeutic agent and a customizable delivery platform. Both unmodified natural mEVs and drug-loaded engineered mEVs have demonstrated hair growth efficacy and safety in preclinical models. Their ability to retain activity after lyophilization further addresses the storage and transportation challenges associated with such biologics.56 While their precise molecular mechanisms of action, optimal administration routes, and efficacy in humans await confirmation through large-scale clinical studies, mEVs, based on their resource accessibility, cost-effectiveness, and safety, undoubtedly open an attractive translational pathway for developing accessible, effective, and safe novel AGA therapies.

Challenges in the Clinical Translation of EVs

EV-based therapies demonstrate clear potential in the regenerative medicine of AGA; however, their translation from the laboratory to widespread clinical application is significantly lagging, with no EV-based therapy currently formally approved for this condition.201,202 This status quo reveals a series of interconnected and systemic challenges.

The primary and fundamental challenge lies in the bottlenecks of standardization and scalability in manufacturing. The biological origin of EVs dictates that their production is a complex process involving upstream cell culture and downstream isolation and purification. Variables such as the cell source, passage number, culture medium composition, and even physical (eg, shear stress, stretching86,87) or biochemical (eg, hypoxia, cytokines88,91) preconditioning can significantly impact the final product’s yield, purity, active components, and functionality.203,204 Hyaluronic acid preconditioning has been shown to significantly enhance the therapeutic efficacy of iMSC-EVs in improving AGA outcomes, primarily through the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. In contrast, EVs derived from non-preconditioned cells demonstrated no significant therapeutic effect,24 underscoring the critical dependence of EV functionality on the microenvironmental state of the parent cells. Traditional laboratory methods (eg, ultracentrifugation) have inherent limitations in scalability, reproducibility, and avoiding vesicle damage, making it difficult to meet the stringent requirements for batch-to-batch consistency of clinical-grade products.26,27 Although regenerative effects such as Wnt/β-catenin activation, angiogenesis, and immunomodulation have been widely attributed to EVs, their precise molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. A more fundamental issue lies in the inability of current isolation techniques to completely eliminate non-vesicular contaminants. In the absence of functional potency assays, it is unclear whether the observed biological effects are due to the active cargoes within EVs or co-isolated protein aggregates.98,139 Establishing GMP-compliant industrial-scale production with stable quality control remains one of the most pressing challenges.205

The heterogeneity inherent in production directly contributes to significant inconsistency in subsequent research quality and clinical protocols. To enhance research rigor and comparability, the ISEV has established the MISEV guidelines. However, adherence to these guidelines varies widely across existing studies. Some studies exhibit shortcomings in the transparency of isolation methods, the exclusion of contaminants (eg, Calnexin), and multi-technique characterization, which weakens data reliability and cross-study comparability.23,206 This “fragmented” state is particularly pronounced in clinical research, manifesting as extreme inconsistency in key treatment parameters: the dose reporting of EVs (particle number vs protein mass) and the numerical range vary enormously (109 to 1011 particles/mL), and there is a lack of consensus on the route of administration (topical, injection, microneedling), frequency, and treatment duration (4 to 26 weeks).23,207 Furthermore, most studies are limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods (often ≤12 weeks), and homogeneous subject populations, severely restricting the evaluation of efficacy generalizability and long-term safety.29 A notable concern in the current literature is the lack of systematic reporting on neutral or negative outcomes, which may reflect publication bias rather than definitive evidence of efficacy. Existing clinical data on EV-based AGA therapies are predominantly derived from single-arm studies, with only two RCTs included in systematic reviews, both of which had limited sample sizes.208 Similar to other paracrine therapies, PRP has been demonstrated to be completely ineffective in treating advanced AGA cases (Hamilton-Norwood grades 4–6),209 suggesting that EV-based therapies may also face efficacy limitations in patients with severely miniaturized hair follicles. However, no EV studies to date have conducted stratified analyses to address this issue. Furthermore, a systematic review on EVs in wound healing revealed no significant differences between EV-treated groups and placebo in terms of average healing time,210 highlighting the context-dependent nature of EV-mediated biological effects and cautioning against oversimplified extrapolations across indications. Ultimately, all the aforementioned uncertainties converge on a regulatory approval pathway that remains unclear. Regulatory agencies (eg, FDA, EMA) have not yet definitively classified EV-based products (as biologicals, advanced therapy medicinal products, or drugs), leaving product developers with a murky approval landscape and high compliance costs.28 Safety data reported in published studies exhibit significant “survivorship bias”, characterized by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and the absence of independent safety monitoring, making it nearly impossible to capture rare or delayed adverse events.211 More critically, neither the FDA nor the EMA has approved any EV-based products for the treatment of hair loss. The widespread commercialization of unregulated “exosome” products marketed as cosmetics bypasses the rigorous safety and efficacy evaluations required for biologics,212 further exacerbating the regulatory ambiguity surrounding EV-based therapies. Concurrently, industry-specific guidelines and pharmacopoeial standards for unique EV attributes—such as defining functional active ingredients and potency assays—are still absent. The lack of unified quality control standards and a clear regulatory framework constitute the ultimate obstacle that must be overcome for EV therapies to progress to large-scale, multi-center clinical trials and eventual market approval.

In summary, the successful translation of EV-based therapies in the field of AGA requires far more than just validating their biological effects. It urgently demands synergistic breakthroughs across three key dimensions: standardization and quality control in manufacturing, optimization of clinical research protocols, and innovation in regulatory science. Future progress depends on close collaboration among academia, industry, and regulatory bodies to jointly establish a complete, transparent, and verifiable system—from production to evaluation—thereby propelling this highly promising cell-free therapeutic strategy towards genuine clinical application.

Critical Comparison of EV Sources and Future Perspective

EVs, as an innovative “cell-free” therapeutic paradigm, represent a promising but still investigational approach in the treatment of AGA. This review comprehensively elucidates the diverse sources of EVs—particularly those derived from various MSCs, somatic and blood components, as well as emerging plant- and milk-derived vesicles—and their multifaceted, multi-target synergistic mechanisms to counteract the core pathology of AGA.

A critical comparison of the various EV platforms reveals a clear hierarchy of translational readiness. ADSC-EVs and mEVs emerge as the most clinically translatable candidates in the near term, owing to their abundant raw material availability, established scalability, and encouraging preliminary safety and efficacy data from both preclinical models and early-phase clinical studies. In particular, mEVs offer unique advantages as an “off-the-shelf” product with cost-effective production and lyophilization compatibility, while ADSC-EVs benefit from extensive mechanistic validation and ongoing clinical exploration. Among MSC-derived EVs, ADSC-EVs have the most robust preclinical support with multiple early-phase clinical studies, though donor variability and lack of standardized potency assays remain unresolved. UCMSC-EVs offer abundant sources and minimal ethical concerns, but comparative studies suggest inferior efficacy to ADSC-EVs, and clinical data remain limited to a single registered trial. BMSC-EVs face challenges of invasive harvesting and limited expansion capacity, while iPSC-derived iMSC-EVs are constrained by complex, costly production. Among somatic sources, DPC-NVs offer unparalleled targeting specificity due to homologous origin but are constrained by limited tissue availability. HEK-293/293T-derived engineered exosomes enable off-the-shelf production of rationally designed biologics; however, immunogenicity and long-term safety require rigorous investigation. PRP-EVs are supported by clinical studies but face significant standardization challenges due to donor heterogeneity and protocol variability, with limited efficacy in advanced AGA. Plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles offer cross-kingdom regulation, intrinsic bioactivity, and low immunogenicity, with PCEVs demonstrating a distinct advantage in directly targeting HFSC homeostasis and GELNs providing dual functionality as both active agents and drug carriers. However, evidence remains predominantly preclinical, and the molecular complexity of plant-derived vesicles poses challenges for batch-to-batch consistency and identification of active ingredients. Salmon-derived EVs are economically viable and scalable but remain the most exploratory platform, with only descriptive evidence and unknown immunogenicity in humans.

Nevertheless, despite substantial preclinical evidence and encouraging early-phase safety signals, the field remains predominantly experimental, with full clinical translation hindered by critical bottlenecks in standardized manufacturing, quality control, dosing optimization, and regulatory clarity, compounded by significant underreporting of adverse events and negative outcomes—a publication bias that may overestimate efficacy. From a clinical perspective, EV-based therapies should currently be viewed as experimental, with routine adoption contingent upon successful completion of rigorous trials and regulatory approval. To overcome these hurdles, regulatory agencies such as the FDA and EMA must establish explicit guidance on product classification, potency assays, and quality release criteria, while large-scale, long-term multicenter RCTs are indispensable to definitively establish efficacy and safety. Several cross-cutting limitations compromise the overall evidence base: heavy reliance on acute preclinical models that may not recapitulate chronic human AGA pathology; lack of standardized EV characterization and functional potency assays; clinical data predominantly derived from single-arm, small-sample studies; and the absence of stratified analyses by disease severity. Critical unanswered questions include optimal dosing regimens, identification of essential molecular mediators for potency biomarkers, long-term safety of non-autologous and plant-derived sources, and synergy with existing therapies. Future progress depends on interdisciplinary collaboration, with a focus on: 1) Establishing GMP-compliant, scalable production processes and potency-based quality control standards; 2) Conducting well-designed, large-scale, long-term RCTs to confirm their efficacy and safety across diverse AGA populations; and 3) Further exploring engineering strategies to enhance the precision and efficacy of the treatment, including investigating how to enhance and achieve targeted delivery of exosomes to the hair follicle, as this represents a crucial direction for improving therapeutic outcomes. Ultimately, over the next 5–10 years, the convergence of these priorities—with regulatory clarity and high-quality clinical evidence at the core—will be essential for translating EV-based therapies into routine practice.

Conclusion

EVs represent a promising but still investigational cell-free therapeutic paradigm for AGA, capable of delivering complex bioactive cargo that synergistically activates Wnt/β-catenin, suppresses TGF-β/SMAD signaling, and restores the perifollicular microenvironment. ADSC-EVs and mEVs are the most clinically translatable near-term candidates, while most other platforms remain exploratory. The field is limited by the lack of standardized manufacturing, validated potency assays, and regulatory clarity. Future success depends on GMP-compliant production, large-scale RCTs, and engineering strategies for targeted delivery.
