Spectral Shrinkage of Gaussian Entropic Optimal Transport
Ho Yun

Abstract
We present a functional calculus treatment of Entropic Optimal Transport (EOT) between Gaussian measures on separable Hilbert spaces, providing a unified framework that handles infinite-dimensional degeneracy. By leveraging the notion of proper alignment and the Schur complement, we reveal that the Gaussian EOT solution operates as a precise spectral shrinkage : the optimal coupling is uniquely determined by contracting the spectrum of the correlation operator via a universal scalar function. This geometric insight facilitates an algorithmic shift from iterative fixed-point schemes (e.g., Sinkhorn) to direct algebraic computation, enabling efficient multi-scale analysis, where a single spectral decomposition allows for the exact evaluation of entropic costs across arbitrary regularization parameters ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 at negligible additional cost. Furthermore, we investigate the asymptotic behavior as ε ↓ 0 \varepsilon\downarrow 0 in settings where the unregularized Optimal Transport problem admits non-unique solutions. We establish a selection principle that the regularized limit converges to the most diffusive optimal coupling –characterized as the centroid of the convex set of optimal Kantorovich plans. This demonstrates that in degenerate regimes, the entropic limit systematically rejects deterministic Monge solutions (extremal points) in favor of the optimal solution with minimal Hilbert-Schmidt correlation, effectively filtering out spurious correlations in the null space. Finally, we derive stability bounds and convergence rates, recovering established parametric rates ( ε ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) \varepsilon\log(1/\varepsilon) ) in finite dimensions while identifying distinct non-parametric rates dependent on spectral decay in infinite-dimensional settings.

Introduction

The Entropic Optimal Transport (EOT) problem has emerged as a computational and theoretical cornerstone in modern data science, bridging the gap between optimal transport (OT) theory and efficient numerical algorithms via the Schrödinger bridge framework
[
27
,
7
,
35
]
. Within this landscape, the Gaussian setting serves as a canonical prototype, offering one of the few regimes where the EOT problem admits closed-form algebraic solutions
[
26
]
. However, the geometric structure of these solutions is often obscured by the dominant analytical approach: matrix differential calculus
[
26
,
28
,
30
,
4
,
16
]
. By treating covariance matrices as generic variables to be differentiated, standard derivations typically yield complex Riccati-type algebraic equations that hide the intrinsic geometry of the problem.

We investigate the Gaussian EOT problem between two arbitrary centered Gaussian measures
\mu=N(0,\mathbf{A})
and
\nu=N(0,\mathbf{B})
on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, where the covariances
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
are non-negative definite (n.n.d.) and trace-class
[
12
]
. We assume centered measures without loss of generality, as the mean vectors contribute only a trivial affine shift separable from the covariance transport. In contrast to the literature of Gaussian EOT, which often imposes non-degeneracy assumptions (i.e.,
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})=\{0\}
) to ensure the differentiability of the objective function, our framework imposes no nullity conditions nor inclusion assumptions (e.g.,
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\subseteq\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{B}))
. Instead, we propose a coordinate-free geometric formulation based on properly aligned square roots. This perspective trivializes the algebraic complexity, replacing opaque matrix equations with explicit spectral operations.

Proper Alignment and Bures-Wasserstein Geometry

The core difficulty in comparing two covariance operators
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
lies in the ambiguity of their square roots, which we call the Green’s operators
\mathbf{G},\mathbf{M}
satisfying
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{*}=\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{*}=\mathbf{B}
. While
\mathbf{A}^{1/2}
is undoubtedly the canonical choice for a single operator, the geometry of the 2-Wasserstein distance suggests that
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
must be treated interactively. Recall that the 2-Wasserstein distance is given by
[
17
,
2
,
37
,
33
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\tr[\mathbf{A}]+\tr[\mathbf{B}]-2\tr[(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{1/2})^{1/2}]\leq{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{A}^{1/2}-\mathbf{B}^{1/2}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{2}^{2},

where
{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\cdot|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{2}
denotes the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. The equality holds if and only if
\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}^{1/2}\succeq\mathbf{0}
, a condition that fails in general unless the operators commute. This discrepancy motivates our notion of
proper alignment
: among the class of Green’s operators, we select the pair satisfying
\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}\succeq\mathbf{0}
. This choice of pair recovers the 2-Wasserstein distance as a Hilbert-Schmidt distance
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})={|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{M}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{2}
, and renders the analysis robust to degeneracy—a regime where classical Kantorovich duality or density-based arguments often become intractable due to the failure of Brenier’s theorem
[
39
,
38
,
40
]
. From the perspective of shape analysis
[
14
,
36
,
29
]
, the Green’s operator can be viewed as a preshape lying in the fibre of the covariance. The 2-Wasserstein distance then corresponds naturally to the Procrustes distance: it is the infimum of the Hilbert-Schmidt distance over the unitary orbits of
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{M}
. Nonetheless, while the operator product
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{M}^{*}
may vary with the specific choice of the aligned pair, the spectrum of
\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}
remains invariant (coinciding with that of
(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{1/2})^{1/2}
). Consequently, any optimal Kantorovich coupling admits a covariance of the form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{G}\mathbf{M}^{*}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{M}\mathbf{G}^{*}&\mathbf{B}\end{array}\right).

Uniqueness is guaranteed if and only if the generalized Schur complement (denoted
\mathbf{B}/\mathbf{A}
or
\mathbf{A}/\mathbf{B}
in our coordinate-free approach) vanishes, implying no residual information in one marginal remains unexplained by the other at least in one direction
[
40
]
.

Spectral Characterization of Entropic Solutions

Leveraging this alignment, we characterize the entire class of joint Gaussian couplings equivalent to the independent coupling
\mu\otimes\nu
through the Felman-Hájek dichotomy. We show that minimizing the entropic cost is equivalent to maximizing a quadratic
entropic interaction
term with the Fredholm determinant:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{R})=2\tr[\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M})]+\varepsilon\log\det(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R}^{*}\mathbf{R}),\quad\varepsilon>0,

where
\mathbf{R}
is the Green’s correlation operator with respect to
(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{M})
. Unlike the canonical non-interactive pair
(\mathbf{A}^{1/2},\mathbf{B}^{1/2})
, the properly aligned pair decouples this optimization spectrally, admitting a closed-form solution
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}=f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M})
. Here,
f_{\varepsilon}(x)=2x/(\sqrt{4x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}+\varepsilon)\in[0,1)
is a universal
spectral shrinkage function
that monotonically shrinks the correlation eigenvalues as regularization
\varepsilon
increases. This result demystifies the Gaussian EOT solution: it is simply a spectral filter applied to the optimal Kantorovich coupling, yielding the unique solution invariant to the specific choice of properly aligned pair:

\displaystyle\pi_{\varepsilon}=N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon})\in\Pi(\mu,\nu),\quad\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{M}^{*}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{G}^{*}&\mathbf{B}\end{array}\right).

Beyond theoretical clarity, this framework enables a fundamental algorithmic shift. While standard Gaussian EOT solvers rely on Sinkhorn iterations
[
26
,
28
]
, which are iterative fixed-point schemes, our approach reduces the problem to a direct algebraic computation. By recognizing that the cost is determined purely by the singular spectrum of
\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}
, the exact solution can be recovered using standard linear algebra routines (e.g., SVD) in finite time, eliminating iterative approximation errors. This advantage is particularly pronounced in multi-scale simulations: while the Sinkhorn-based algorithm reiteraterate for each
\varepsilon>0
where the required iterations typically diverge as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0

[
11
,
19
,
8
]
, in our approach once the eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}
is computed for any properly aligned pair—a single
\mathcal{O}(d^{3})
operation when
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
—the solution for any subsequent
\varepsilon>0
can be evaluated in
\mathcal{O}(d)
time.

Limiting Behavior and Diffusivity

Our framework elucidates the convergence behavior as

\varepsilon\to 0
. The sequence of spectral shrinkage functions
\{f_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
converges pointwise to the indicator function
f_{\varepsilon}(x)\to f_{0}(x):=I(x>0)
, but fails to converge uniformly. This discontinuity at zero explains the emergence of non-unique optimal Kanotorovich solutions in degenerate regimes: the behavior on the null space of
\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}
becomes ill-posed. Crucially, we establish that the EOT coupling limit
\pi_{0}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\pi_{\varepsilon}
with respect to the 2-Wasserstein distance is the
most diffusive
Gaussian solution among all possible optimal Kantorovich couplings. Specifically,
\pi_{0}
corresponds to the centroid of the convex set of optimal couplings, constructed via the orthogonal projection
\mathbf{R}_{0}=f_{0}(\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M})
onto the closure of the range of
\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}
.
This implies that, whenever the Schur complement does not vanish, the optimal Kantorovich solution is non-unique, among which the limit of EOT coupling is never derived from a Monge solution (extremal point of the solution set)
[
27
,
40
]
:

\displaystyle\pi_{0}=N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})\in\Pi(\mu,\nu),\quad\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}_{0}\mathbf{M}^{*}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}_{0}\mathbf{G}^{*}&\mathbf{B}\end{array}\right).

Finally, owing to the explicit form of
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}=f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M})
, we provide sharp stability bounds for the entropic bias
\mathcal{W}_{2,\varepsilon}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})^{2}-\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})^{2}
. In the Euclidean setting
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we recover the convergence rate of
\varepsilon\log(1/\varepsilon)
, a well-established rate in various setups under certain regularity conditions
[
27
,
5
,
31
,
6
,
15
,
20
,
26
,
23
]
. However, in infinite-dimensional settings, we show that the rate depends on the spectral decay of
(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{1/2})^{1/2}
, exhibiting slower rates. For instance, we show that the sharpest rate for
n
-th and
m
-th integrated Brownian motions is given by
\varepsilon^{1-1/(2\lceil(n+m)/2\rceil)}
.
Furthermore, we extract a closed-form formula for the squared Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon},\pi_{0})
between EOT coupling and OT couplings. In finite dimensions
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this distance typically decays at the parametric rate
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, whereas infinite-dimensional settings may again exhibit slower, non-parametric rates.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notations

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote a real Hilbert space, which is assumed to be separable throughout this work. For any closed subspace
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{H}
, we denote the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{V}
by
\mathbf{\Pi}_{\mathcal{V}}
. The symbol
†
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. For an operator
\mathbf{T}
, we denote its null space and its range by
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{T})
and
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{T})
, respectively. For a densely defined closable operator
\mathbf{T}
,
\overline{\mathbf{T}}
denotes its closure
[
9
]
.

The Banach space of bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H})
and is equipped with the operator norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{B}_{0}(\mathcal{H})
the Banach space of compact operators. For
1\leq p<\infty
, the Banach space of
p
-Schatten class operators on
\mathcal{H}
is defined as:

\mathcal{B}_{p}(\mathcal{H}):=\left\{\mathbf{K}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}(\mathcal{H}):{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{K}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{p}:=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}{\left\langle{e_{l}},{(\mathbf{K}^{*}\mathbf{K})^{p/2}e_{l}}\right\rangle}\right)^{1/p}<+\infty\right\},

where
\{e_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
is any orthonormal basis (ONB) of
\mathcal{H}
. We denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
the Banach space of non-negative definite (n.n.d.) bounded operators, and similarly define
\mathcal{B}_{0}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{B}_{p}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
for
p\in[1,\infty)
. For
p=2
, the class of
2
-Schatten operators is referred to as the Hilbert–Schmidt operators. For
p=1
, the class consists of the trace-class operators. For
\mathbf{K}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
, the trace is defined as

\tr(\mathbf{K}):=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}{\left\langle{e_{l}},{\mathbf{K}e_{l}}\right\rangle},

which coincides with the trace norm
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{1}
if and only if
\mathbf{K}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
.

For the analysis of EOT, we utilize the functional calculus for self-adjoint compact operators
[
25
]
. Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}(\mathcal{H})
be a self-adjoint compact operator. By the spectral theorem,
\mathbf{A}
admits a spectral decomposition into distinct eigenvalues
\sigma(\mathbf{A})=\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\subset\mathbb{R}
and associated orthogonal projections
\{\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{I}}
onto the eigenspaces
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}-\lambda_{k}\mathbf{I})
. The index set
\mathcal{I}
is a countable subset of
\mathbb{N}
. To explicitly handle infinite-dimensional degeneracy, we adopt the following convention:

1.

Injective Case:
If
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})=\{0\}
, we set
\mathcal{I}=\mathbb{N}
and order the eigenvalues strictly by magnitude:
|\lambda_{1}|>|\lambda_{2}|>\cdots>0
.

2.

Degenerate Case:
If
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\neq\{0\}
, we include the zero eigenvalue. We set
\lambda_{1}=0
, implying
\mathbf{\Pi}_{1}=\mathbf{\Pi}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})}
, and order the remaining non-zero eigenvalues as
|\lambda_{2}|>|\lambda_{3}|>\cdots>0
.

In both cases, the projections form a resolution of the identity,
\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}=\mathbf{I}_{\mathcal{H}}
, where the series converges in the Strong Operator Topology (SOT).
For any bounded Borel function
f:\sigma(\mathbf{A})\to\mathbb{R}
, the functional calculus is defined by the series:

f(\mathbf{A}):=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}f(\lambda_{k})\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}.

Since the projections are mutually orthogonal, this series always converges in the SOT; that is,
f(\mathbf{A})v=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}f(\lambda_{k})(\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}v)
for all
v\in\mathcal{H}
. The resulting operator
f(\mathbf{A})
is bounded and self-adjoint. Furthermore, if
f
satisfies the condition
f(0)=0
and is continuous at
0
, then the coefficients
f(\lambda_{k})
decay to
0
, ensuring that
f(\mathbf{A})
is compact (
\in\mathcal{B}_{0}(\mathcal{H})
) and the series converges in the operator norm topology
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}
.
Finally, we recall the following convergence properties: given a sequence of uniformly bounded functions
\{f_{n}:\sigma(\mathbf{A})\to\mathbb{R}\}
that converges pointwise to
f
, then the operator sequence converges in SOT,
f_{n}(\mathbf{A})\xrightarrow{SOT}f(\mathbf{A})
. If the scalar convergence
f_{n}\to f
is uniform on the spectrum, the operator convergence holds in the norm topology,
f_{n}(\mathbf{A})\xrightarrow{{|\kern-0.75346pt|\kern-0.75346pt|\cdot|\kern-0.75346pt|\kern-0.75346pt|}_{\infty}}f(\mathbf{A})
.

2.2
Review of Gaussian OT

We briefly review key results from our recent work
[
40
]
that are essential for this paper. Any centered Gaussian measure
\mu=N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{A})
is fully determined by its trace-class covariance
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})

.

Definition 2.1

(Partial Isometry)
.

An operator
\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H})
is called a
partial isometry
if
\|\mathbf{U}h\|=\|h\|
for any
h\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{U})^{\perp}
. The subspaces
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{U})^{\perp}
and
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{U})
are called the
initial space
and the
final space
, respectively.

The final space of a partial isometry
\mathbf{U}
is closed. The operators
\mathbf{U}^{*}\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{*}
are the orthogonal projections onto the initial and final spaces, respectively. Furthermore, the adjoint
\mathbf{U}^{*}=\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}
is also a partial isometry with the initial and final spaces swapped
[
9
]
.

Definition 2.2

(Green’s Operator)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
. An operator
\mathbf{G}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
satisfying
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{*}=\mathbf{A}
is called a
Green’s operator
for
\mathbf{A}
. We denote the set of such operators by
\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{A})
.

For any Green’s operator
\mathbf{G}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{A})
,
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger/2}\mathbf{G}
is the partial isometry with the initial space
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{G}})^{\perp}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})^{\perp}=\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{G}^{*})}
and the final space
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{G}})=\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{G})}=\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A})}
.
Consider the orthogonal decomposition of
\mathcal{H}
induced by
\mathbf{A}
:

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2},\quad\mathcal{H}_{1}:=\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A})},\quad\mathcal{H}_{2}:=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}).

We remark that the operator
\mathbf{B}^{1/2}\mathbf{\Pi}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}^{1/2})}\mathbf{B}^{1/2}
vanishes on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
is the invariant subspace.

Definition 2.3

(Schur Operator)
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
. We define the
\mathbf{A}
-
Schur operator
of
\mathbf{B}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}/\mathbf{A}:=[\mathbf{B}^{1/2}\mathbf{\Pi}_{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}^{1/2})}\mathbf{B}^{1/2}]\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}:\mathcal{H}_{2}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}.

To interpret the Schur operator, express
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
as block operators:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{{11}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{B}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}_{{11}}&\mathbf{B}_{{12}}\\
\mathbf{B}_{{21}}&\mathbf{B}_{{22}}\end{pmatrix}:\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{H}_{1}\\
\oplus\\
\mathcal{H}_{2}\end{array}\rightarrow\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{H}_{1}\\
\oplus\\
\mathcal{H}_{2}\end{array},

where
\mathbf{A}_{{11}}:=\mathbf{A}\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}
is injective, and the block components of
\mathbf{B}
are given by
\mathbf{B}_{{ij}}:=\mathbf{\Pi}_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}\mathbf{B}\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{j}}:\mathcal{H}_{j}\to\mathcal{H}_{i}
for
i,j\in\{1,2\}
. It can be shown that
\mathbf{B}/\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}_{{22}}-(\mathbf{B}_{{11}}^{\dagger/2}\mathbf{B}_{{12}})^{*}(\mathbf{B}_{{11}}^{\dagger/2}\mathbf{B}_{{12}}):\mathcal{H}_{2}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}/\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{0}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{B}^{1/2})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}^{1/2})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{B}^{1/2})\cap\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}^{1/2})=\{\mathbf{0}\}.

Definition 2.4

(Proper Alignment)
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
. We say that
\mathbf{G}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{B})

if
\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
.

By von Neumann’s trace inequality and the polar factorization

9
]
, we have

\displaystyle\tr[\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}]\leq\tr[(\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{G})^{1/2}]=\tr[(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{1/2})^{1/2}]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})\leq{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{M}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{2}^{2},

(1)

and the equality holds if and only if
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{M}
are properly aligned. In this case, the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
coincides with that of
(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{1/2})^{1/2}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
.
We also remark that if
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
commute, then
\mathbf{A}^{1/2}
and
\mathbf{B}^{1/2}
are properly aligned. Additionally, if
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{0}

and

A coupling of

and

is optimal for the Kantorovich problem if and only if its covariance operator admits the form

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{G}\mathbf{M}^{*}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{M}\mathbf{G}^{*}&\mathbf{B}\end{array}\right)\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}),

for some properly aligned Green’s operators
\mathbf{G}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{B})

.

Definition 2.5

(Reachability)
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
. We say that
\mathbf{B}
is
reachable
from
\mathbf{A}
, denoted by
\mathbf{A}\leadsto\mathbf{B}
, if there exists a (possibly unbounded) operator
\mathbf{T}:\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A}^{1/2})\subset\mathcal{H}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
satisfying transportability and optimality conditions:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{A}^{1/2})(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{A}^{1/2})^{*}=\mathbf{B},\quad\tr(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{A})=\tr[(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{1/2})^{1/2}].

In such cases, there exists an extension
\tilde{\mathbf{T}}
of
\mathbf{T}
that optimally pushes forward
\mu=N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{A})
to
\nu=N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{B})
, satisfying:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=\int_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}}\|x-\tilde{\mathbf{T}}(x)\|^{2}\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\tr[\mathbf{A}]+\tr[\mathbf{B}]-2\tr[(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{1/2})^{1/2}].

It has been established in
[
40
]
that for arbitrary
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
,
\mathbf{A}\leadsto\mathbf{B}
holds if and only if

\displaystyle\dim[\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{B}^{1/2})\cap\mathcal{H}_{2}]\leq\dim[\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{B}^{1/2}\mathbf{A}^{1/2})\cap\mathcal{H}_{1}],

and thus either
\mathbf{A}\leadsto\mathbf{B}
or
\mathbf{B}\leadsto\mathbf{A}
; that is, a Monge solution always exists in at least one direction. In the finite dimensional case
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the rank nullity theorem reduces the above condition to
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{A})\geq\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B})
, and in this case, there always exists an optimal linear transport map

3
Gaussian EOT

We formally introduce the Entropic Optimal Transport (EOT) problem in the Gaussian setting. Given two centered Gaussian measures
\mu=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{A})
and
\nu=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{B})
on
\mathcal{H}
, the EOT problem with regularization parameter
\varepsilon>0
with respect to mutual information is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2,\varepsilon}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}):=\left[\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\left\{\int_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}}\|x-y\|^{2}\pi(\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y)+2\varepsilon\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})\right\}\right]^{1/2},

where the reference measure is the independent coupling:

\displaystyle\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}:=\mu\otimes\nu=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}),\quad\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{B}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}).

While the definition allows
\pi
to be any probability measure, we can restrict our search to Gaussian couplings without loss of generality due to the following argument.
Let
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
be any feasible coupling with finite second moments, and let
\pi_{G}
be the centered Gaussian measure with the same covariance operator as
\pi
. Since the quadratic cost function depends solely on the second moments,
\pi
and
\pi_{G}
yield identical transport costs.
Furthermore, since the reference measure
\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}
is Gaussian, the KL divergence satisfies the well-known Pythagorean identity
[
10
]
:
\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})=\KL(\pi\|\pi_{G})+\KL(\pi_{G}\|\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})
.
Since
\KL(\pi\|\pi_{G})\geq 0
with equality if and only if
\pi=\pi_{G}
, the Gaussian coupling
\pi_{G}
strictly dominates any non-Gaussian candidate with the same covariance.
Finally, the uniqueness of the solution is guaranteed by the strict convexity of the mapping
\pi\mapsto\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})
on the convex set of couplings
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, combined with the linearity of the transport cost.
Thus, we assume
\pi=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma})
where
\mathbf{\Sigma}
has marginals
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
.
For the entropic cost to be finite, the coupling
\pi
must be absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}
so that
\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})<\infty
. Our first task is to completely characterize the geometry of such Gaussian couplings.

Proposition 3.1

(Generalized Baker-Douglas Theorem
[
13
,
1
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space and
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
be trace-class covariance operators. Fix any Green’s operators
\mathbf{G}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{B})
.
The Gaussian coupling of
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{A})
and
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{B})
on
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
admits a covariance of the form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{C}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{C}^{*}&\mathbf{B}\end{array}\right)\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}),

for some shrinkage
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H})
(i.e.,
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{R}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}\leq 1
) such that the cross-covariance is given by
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{M}^{*}
. This operator
\mathbf{R}
is unique if one imposes the following constraints, in which case we call it the
Green’s correlation operator
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{R})\supset\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{M})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{R})\subset\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})^{\perp}.

Explicitly, it is given by
\mathbf{R}=\left[\mathbf{M}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}\mathbf{C})^{*}\right]^{*}=\overline{\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{M}^{*})^{\dagger}}
.

Proof.

The condition
\mathbf{\Sigma}\succeq\mathbf{0}
implies that for any
u,v\in\mathcal{H}
,
|\langle u,\mathbf{C}v\rangle|^{2}\leq\langle u,\mathbf{A}u\rangle\langle v,\mathbf{B}v\rangle
. This inequality necessitates the range inclusions
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{C})\subset\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A}^{1/2})
and
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{C}^{*})\subset\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{B}^{1/2})
.
Since
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{A}^{1/2})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{G})
and
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{B}^{1/2})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{M})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{M}^{\dagger*})
, the Douglas Factorization Lemma guarantees the existence of a bounded operator
\mathbf{R}
such that
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{M}^{*}
.
Furthermore, the Douglas lemma states that among all such operators, there exists a unique solution satisfying the geometric constraints
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{R})\supset\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{M})
and
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{R})\subset\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})^{\perp}
. This specific solution coincides with the Moore-Penrose formulation
\overline{\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{M}^{*})^{\dagger}}
. Additionally, it possesses the minimal operator norm among all valid factors; since there exists at least one contraction satisfying the factorization (by the Cauchy-Schwarz argument on
\mathbf{\Sigma}\succeq\mathbf{0}
):

\displaystyle|{\left\langle{(\mathbf{G}^{*}u)},{\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{M}^{*}v)}\right\rangle}|^{2}=|\langle u,\mathbf{C}v\rangle|^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{G}^{*}u\|^{2}\|\mathbf{M}^{*}v\|^{2},

this unique minimal-norm solution must satisfy
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{R}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}\leq 1
.
∎

Theorem 3.2

(Feldman-Hájek Dichotomy for Couplings
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
and fix any Green’s operators
\mathbf{G}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{B})
. Let
\pi
be a centered Gaussian measure on
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
defined by the covariance operator:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{M}^{*}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}^{*}\mathbf{G}^{*}&\mathbf{B}\end{array}\right)\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}),

where
\mathbf{R}
is the Green’s correlation operator for
\mathbf{\Sigma}
. Then,
\pi
is equivalent to
\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}
if and only if

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H})\quad\text{and}\quad{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{R}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}<1;

(4)

otherwise, they are mutually singular.

Proof.

By the classical Feldman-Hájek theorem,
\pi
and
\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}
are equivalent if and only if:

1.

Range Equivalence:

\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1/2})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}^{1/2})
.

2.

Hilbert-Schmidt Defect:
The operator
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}^{\dagger/2}(\mathbf{\Sigma}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}^{\dagger/2}
is Hilbert-Schmidt on the Cameron-Martin space of
\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}
, and
\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{T}
is invertible,

otherwise mutually singular.

Step 1: Range Equivalence (
\Longleftrightarrow\ {\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{R}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}<1
).

Let
\mathbf{S}=\text{diag}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{M})
. We observe that
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{*}
. We can factor the coupled covariance
\mathbf{\Sigma}
similarly:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{S}\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{R}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{R}^{*}&\mathbf{I}\end{array}\right)}_{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{S}^{*}.

Since
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}^{1/2})=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{S})
, the ranges coincide if and only if the core matrix
\mathbf{K}
is a bounded invertible operator on the support of
\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}
.
Using Schur complements,
\mathbf{K}\succ\mathbf{0}
if and only if
\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R}^{*}\mathbf{R}\succ\mathbf{0}
. This condition is equivalent to the spectrum of
\mathbf{R}^{*}\mathbf{R}
being strictly bounded below
1
, which implies
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{R}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}<1
. Note that this strict inequality automatically guarantees that
\mathbf{K}
(and thus
\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{T}
) is invertible.

Step 2: Hilbert-Schmidt Condition (
\Longleftrightarrow\ \mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
).

We examine the relative covariance operator
\mathbf{T}
. Since the measures may be degenerate:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\dagger/2}&\mathbf{0}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{B}^{\dagger/2}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{M}^{*}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}^{*}\mathbf{G}^{*}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\dagger/2}&\mathbf{0}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{B}^{\dagger/2}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{0}&(\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\dagger/2}\mathbf{G})\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{B}^{\dagger/2}\mathbf{M})^{*}\\
\hline\cr(\mathbf{B}^{\dagger/2}\mathbf{M})\mathbf{R}^{*}(\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\dagger/2}\mathbf{G})^{*}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{M}}^{*}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{R}^{*}\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{G}}^{*}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right),

where we invoke the partial isometries defined in the preliminaries:
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{G}}:=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger/2}\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{M}}:=\mathbf{B}^{\dagger/2}\mathbf{M}
. Thus,
\mathbf{T}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H})
if and only if its off-diagonal block
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{M}}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
is Hilbert-Schmidt.
The partial isometry
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{M}}^{*}
maps
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{B})^{\perp}
isometrically to
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{M})^{\perp}
. The operator
\mathbf{R}
maps
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{M})^{\perp}
to
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})^{\perp}
. Finally,
\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{G}}
maps
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})^{\perp}
isometrically to
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})^{\perp}
.
Since the partial isometries are isometric on the support and range of
\mathbf{R}
(due to the geometric constraints of the Green’s correlation operator), they preserve the Hilbert-Schmidt norms:
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{M}}^{*}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{R}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}
.
Therefore,
\mathbf{T}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H})
if and only if
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
.
∎

As a direct consequence, it suffices for the Gaussian EOT to consider the couplings where the Green’s correlation operator lies within the following convex set:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{M}):=\left\{\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H})\;\big|\;\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{R})\supset\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{M}),\,\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{R})\subset\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})^{\perp},\,{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{R}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}<1\right\}.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
and fix any Green’s operators
\mathbf{G}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{B})
. Let
\pi
be a centered Gaussian coupling of
\mu=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{A})
and
\nu=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{B})
on
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
, and let
\mathbf{R}
be the Green’s correlation operator for its covariance. Then, the KL divergence is given by:

\displaystyle\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R}^{*}\mathbf{R})&,\quad\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{M}),\\
+\infty&,\quad\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\det
denotes the standard Fredholm determinant for trace-class perturbations
[
21
]
.

Proof.

From
Theorem

3.2
, it is immediate that
\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})=\infty
if
\mathbf{R}\notin\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{M})
, as the measures are mutually singular.
Let
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{M})
. Consider the operator
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}_{\mathbf{M}}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
constructed in the proof of
Theorem

3.2
, which shares the same singular values
\{s_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
as
\mathbf{R}
, satisfying
s_{k}<1
and
\sum s_{k}^{2}<\infty
.
We construct a sequence of finite-rank approximations. Let
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{n}
be the operator formed by the first
n
singular components of
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}
. We define the approximate Gaussian coupling
\pi_{n}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
via the covariance:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{n}\mathbf{B}^{1/2}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{B}^{1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{*}\mathbf{A}^{1/2}&\mathbf{B}\end{array}\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

The relative covariance operator
\mathbf{T}_{n}:=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}^{\dagger/2}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp}^{\dagger/2}
takes the form
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{n}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{*}&\mathbf{0}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
Since
\mathbf{T}_{n}
is finite-rank, the standard Gaussian KL formula applies. Crucially, the non-zero eigenvalues of
\mathbf{T}_{n}
occur in pairs
\{\pm s_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
, causing the linear trace terms to cancel exactly (
\tr(\mathbf{T}_{n})=0
). Thus:

\displaystyle\KL(\pi_{n}\|\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\tr(\mathbf{T}_{n})-\log\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{T}_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\log(1+s_{k})+\log(1-s_{k})\right]=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log(1-s_{k}^{2}).

We now justify the limit
n\to\infty
.
Since
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{n}\to\tilde{\mathbf{R}}
in
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
, it follows that
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{n}^{*}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{n}\to\tilde{\mathbf{R}}^{*}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}
in
\mathcal{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H})
. The Fredholm determinant
\mathbf{A}\mapsto\det(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{A})
is continuous on
\mathcal{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H})

[
21
]
, so the RHS converges to
-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R}^{*}\mathbf{R})
.
For the LHS, we invoke the martingale convergence of the Radon-Nikodym derivatives. The sequence of measures
\{\pi_{n}\}
corresponds to the restriction of the full measure
\pi
to the filtration generated by the first
n
singular modes. By the monotonicity of the KL divergence and the martingale convergence theorem for densities of equivalent Gaussian measures
[
3
]
, we have:

\displaystyle\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\KL(\pi_{n}\|\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})=-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R}^{*}\mathbf{R}).

∎

3.2
Spectral Shrinkage of Correlations

The Gaussian EOT problem seeks to minimize the regularized transport cost:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2,\varepsilon}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\left\{\int_{\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}}\|x-y\|^{2}\pi(\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y)+2\varepsilon\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\perp\mkern-9.5mu\perp})\right\},\quad\varepsilon>0.

Expanding the quadratic cost and the entropy term using the Green’s correlation operator
\mathbf{R}
, this is equivalent to maximizing the
entropic profit
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{C}_{\varepsilon}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{C}_{\varepsilon}^{*}&\mathbf{B}\end{array}\right)\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}),\quad\mathbf{C}_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{M}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}:=\argmax_{\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{M})}\left\{\underbrace{2\tr[\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{M}^{*}\mathbf{G})]+\varepsilon\log\det(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R}^{*}\mathbf{R})}_{=:\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{R})}\right\}.

(7)

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
, and let
\mathbf{G}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{B})
be properly aligned Green’s operators.
Then, the unique solution to (
7
) is given by the universal spectral shrinkage:

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}=f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M})\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H}),\quad f_{\varepsilon}:x\in[0,\infty)\ \mapsto\ \frac{2x}{\sqrt{4x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}+\varepsilon}\in[0,1).

Proof.

We derive the solution via explicit spectral construction.

Step 1: Diagonalization via Alignment.

By the proper alignment condition,
\mathbf{K}:=\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}^{*}\mathbf{G}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
is non-negative definite. By the Spectral Theorem for compact self-adjoint operators,
\mathbf{K}
admits the decomposition:

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{e}_{j}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{j}),\quad\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots>0,

where
\{\mathbf{e}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
forms an orthonormal basis for
\overline{\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{K})}\subset\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{M})^{\perp}
.

Step 2: Von Neumann Trace Inequality.

Consider the singular value decomposition of a candidate Green’s correlation operator
\mathbf{R}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{G},\mathbf{M})
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}s_{j}(\mathbf{g}_{j}\otimes\mathbf{h}_{j}):\mathbf{h}_{j}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{M})^{\perp}\mapsto s_{j}\mathbf{g}_{j}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{G})^{\perp},

where
1>s_{1}\geq s_{2}\geq\cdots>0
. The objective functional splits into linear and entropic parts:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{R})=2\tr(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{M}^{*}\mathbf{G})+\varepsilon\tr\log(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{R}^{*}\mathbf{R})=2\tr(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M})+\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\log(1-s_{j}^{2}),

The entropic term depends strictly on the singular values
\{s_{j}\}
. By the von Neumann Trace Inequality, the linear term is maximized for fixed singular values if and only if the singular vectors align with the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{g}_{j}=\mathbf{h}_{j}=\mathbf{e}_{j}
. Thus, the optimal
\mathbf{R}
must be self-adjoint and share the spectral basis of
\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M}
.

Step 3: Scalar Optimization.

With the basis fixed, the problem reduces to a scalar optimization for each eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}

(The Singularity of

0&0&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0&0\\
\rho&0&0&1\end{array}\right),\quad|\rho|\leq 1,

shows that, for any

, the EOT solution is the independent coupling (

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}\equiv\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|cc}1&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{array}\right),

The regularization explicitly rejects the deterministic transport plans in favor of the maximum entropy (independent) plan.

\mathbf{M}_{0}

Proposition 3.8

(One-Step Coordinate-Free Update)
.

Let

with
\mathbf{A}\leadsto\mathbf{B}

\mathbf{K}_{0}
nor
\mathbf{M}_{0}
do not depend on the choice of
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{B})
. Furthermore, the second correction term vanishes if and only if

coincides with the canonical choice in (

Proof.

Recall from

Remark 3.2

(Construction of Proper Alignment)
.

Notably,
Proposition

3.8
generalizes to a constructive method for obtaining a properly aligned Green’s operator from arbitrary initial choices. Given
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
, let
\mathbf{G}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{A})
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{B})
be any Green’s operators (e.g., obtained via Cholesky decomposition). We can update
\mathbf{M}
to satisfy the alignment condition with
\mathbf{G}
by defining:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{M})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{G})^{1/2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{L}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^{*}).

Then, setting
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}:=\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{L}
guarantees that
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{B})
and
\mathbf{G}^{*}\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}=(\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{G})^{1/2}\succeq\mathbf{0}
. This construction admits an interpretation as an operator-valued Pythagorean theorem with respect to the Bures-Wasserstein geometry:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{*}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{total covariance}\\
(=\mathbf{B})\end{subarray}}=\underbrace{\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^{*}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{aligned covariance}\\
(\text{projection onto }\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{M}^{*}\mathbf{G}))\end{subarray}}+\underbrace{\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{*}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{residual variance}\\
(=\text{diag}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{B}/\mathbf{A}))\end{subarray}}.

This intrinsic geometric structure remains obscured in the dominant literature, which typically relies on the assumption
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A})\subseteq\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{B})
or non-singular marginals. In such settings, the Schur complement vanishes, leading standard approaches to default to symmetric square roots
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{A}^{1/2}
and
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{B}^{1/2}
—which still fail to be properly aligned in the non-commutative case.

2.

\hline\cr\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}&\mathbf{I}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\\

Conjecture 3.1

(Universal Bounds)
.

While the asymptotic scaling
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon},\pi_{0})=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
is guaranteed in the non-degenerate finite-dimensional setting (
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\succ\mathbf{0}
on
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
) from (
13
), determining the sharp universal constant governing this rate remains an open problem. Specifically, does there exist a dimension-dependent constant
C_{d}>0
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\pi_{\varepsilon},\pi_{0})}{\varepsilon}\leq C_{d}\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\succ\mathbf{0}?

Analytical results for the identity transport case (
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}
) in (
12
) gives a lower bound of
C_{d}\geq d/2
. However, our numerical experiments with randomly generated full-rank covariances (see
Fig.

3
) consistently exhibit a strictly smaller rate, typically concentrating around
\approx 0.36d
. It remains unclear whether the identity case represents a theoretical worst-case scenario, or if the observed lower rates are simply an artifact of the concentration of measure in random matrix ensembles.

4
Discussion

This work establishes an operator-theoretic framework for Gaussian Entropic Optimal Transport, moving beyond the opacity of standard matrix calculus to reveal the problem’s intrinsic geometry. By characterizing the solution through properly aligned Green’s operators, we demonstrated that the Gaussian EOT problem admits a fully explicit spectral resolution. A notable theoretical consequence of this structure is the gain in regularity inherent to the optimal solution. While the entropic cost remains finite for any Hilbert-Schmidt correlation operator with operator norm strictly less than one, the optimal correlation
\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{H})
is proven to be a n.n.d. trace-class operator satisfying the explicit bound
{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{R}_{\varepsilon}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{1}\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\tr[(\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{1/2})^{1/2}]
. Our stability analysis also bridges the gap between finite-dimensional and infinite-dimensional theory. While we recover standard parametric rates for finite-rank operators, our derivation of the non-parametric rate for processes like Integrated Brownian Motion quantifies the price of roughness. This result highlights that in the Hilbert space setting, the convergence of the entropic approximation is strictly governed by the spectral decay of the marginals, serving as a cautionary tale against applying finite-dimensional convergence intuitions to functional data.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\mathbf{G},\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
. If
\{\mathbf{T}_{n}\}_{n}
is a sequence of bounded operators such that
\mathbf{T}_{n}\to\mathbf{T}
in the SOT, then the product converges in the trace norm:

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}\mathbf{T}_{n}\mathbf{M}^{*}\to\mathbf{G}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{M}^{*}\quad\text{in}\quad\mathcal{B}_{1}(\mathcal{H}).

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}:=\mathbf{T}_{n}-\mathbf{T}
. Since
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\xrightarrow{SOT}\mathbf{0}
, the sequence of pointwise norms
\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}x\|
is bounded for every
x\in\mathcal{H}
. By the Uniform Boundedness Principle, the sequence is uniformly bounded in the operator norm:
C:=\sup_{n}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}<\infty
.
We must show
{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{M}^{*}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{1}\to 0
. Since finite-rank operators are dense in
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathcal{H})
with respect to
{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\cdot|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{2}

\mathbf{G}_{F},\mathbf{M}_{F}
such that
{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{G}_{F}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{2}<\delta
and
{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{M}_{F}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{2}<\delta
.
We decompose the error term and apply the generalized Hölder inequality
{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{1}\leq{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{A}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{B}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{C}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{2}

\displaystyle{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{G}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{M}^{*}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\delta\cdot C\cdot{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{M}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{2}+{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{G}_{F}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{2}\cdot C\cdot\delta+{|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\mathbf{G}_{F}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{M}_{F}^{*}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|}_{1}.

Lemma A.2
.

Consider the function in

:

\displaystyle g_{1}(x)=\left[2x(1-f_{1}(x))-\log(1-f_{1}(x)^{2})\right],\quad f_{1}(x)=\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{1}{2x}\right)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2x},\quad x>0.

Then,
g_{1}
is monotonely increasing and we have

\displaystyle\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\frac{g_{1}(x)}{2x}=1,\quad\lim_{x\to\infty}(g_{1}(x)-\log x)=1-\log 2.

Proof.

1.

It is convenient to express
g_{1}
in terms of
f_{1}
. We use the identity
1-f_{1}(x)^{2}=\frac{f_{1}(x)}{x}
.
Let
y=f_{1}(x)
. Since

is strictly decreasing, it suffices to show

increases with
y
. Substituting

into the expression for
g_{1}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{g_{1}}(y)=g_{1}(x)=2\left(\frac{y}{1-y^{2}}\right)(1-y)-\log(1-y^{2})=\frac{2y}{1+y}-\log(1-y^{2}).

Differentiating with respect to
y

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{g_{1}}}{\mathrm{d}y}=\frac{2(1+y)-2y}{(1+y)^{2}}-\frac{1}{1-y^{2}}(-2y)=\frac{2}{(1+y)^{2}}+\frac{2y}{(1-y)(1+y)}>0,

i.e.,
g_{1}(x)
is monotonically increasing.

2.

For the limits, first observe that:

\displaystyle\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\frac{g_{1}(x)}{2x}=\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\left((1-f_{1}(x))-\frac{\log(1-f_{1}(x)^{2})}{2x}\right)=1-\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\frac{-f_{1}(x)^{2}}{2x}=1-0=1.

For the other limit, let
u=1/(2x)
. As

. We can rewrite

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)=\frac{1/u}{\sqrt{1/u^{2}+1}+1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}+u}=\sqrt{1+u^{2}}-u.

For small

.
This implies
1-f_{1}\approx u
.
Now substitute into
g_{1}(x)
:

\displaystyle g_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{u}(1-f_{1})-\log(1-f_{1}^{2}).

Using
1-f_{1}^{2}=(1-f_{1})(1+f_{1})\approx u(2-u)\approx 2u
, we get:

\displaystyle g_{1}(x)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{u}(u)-\log(2u)=1-(\log 2+\log u)=1-\log 2-\log(1/(2x))=1+\log x.

Thus,
\lim_{x\to\infty}(g_{1}(x)-\log x)=1
.

∎

Lemma A.3
.

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\left(1+\sqrt{1+u^{2}}-u\right)}=\sqrt{4-2u+\mathcal{O}(u^{2})}=2-\frac{u}{2}+\mathcal{O}(u^{2}),
