Adherence to 24-hour movement guidelines and mental health outcomes in Chinese university students
Authors: Yongtao Yan, Xiangli Zhong, Yang Wang, Sha Liao, Xiangyu Du, Qiyuan He, Yunhan Qing, Cheng Chen, Yisong Yao, Yongyu Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Mental health problems such as depression, anxiety, and stress are highly prevalent among university students and may lead to long-term adverse outcomes. Emerging evidence suggests that 24-hour movement behaviors—including physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep—play an important role in psychological well-being. However, limited research has examined these behaviors collectively within the 24-hour movement framework among Chinese university students.Methods A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 836 full-time university students in 2025. Physical activity and sedentary behavior were assessed using the IPAQ-SF, and sleep duration was measured using items from the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. Mental health was evaluated using the DASS-21. Compliance with the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines was categorized by the number and types of recommendations met. Multivariable linear and logistic regression models were used to examine associations between guideline adherence and psychological symptom scores and risks, adjusting for demographic covariates.Results Overall, 23 students met none of the guidelines, while 140, 379, and 294 met one, two, and all three guidelines, respectively. The prevalence of stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms was 84.4%, 61.4%, and 78.0%. Compared with meeting none of the guidelines, meeting all three was associated with significantly lower prevalence of anxiety (OR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.17–0.96) and depressive symptoms (OR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.08–0.50). Meeting two guidelines were also related to lower odds of depressive symptom (OR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.14–0.77). Among behavior combinations, strongest relationship was shown between meeting both PA, sedentary behavior and anxiety (OR = 0.33), depressive symptoms (OR = 0.28).Conclusions Adherence to multiple 24-hour movement guidelines is strongly associated with better mental health outcomes among Chinese college students. These findings highlight the potential of integrated movement-based strategies in university mental health promotion.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Mental health issues among college students, particularly depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and perceived stress, are exhibiting a high prevalence globally and have become a significant public health problem affecting academic performance and even the well-being of young adults. Epidemiological data indicated that the overall prevalence of depressive symptoms among college students is 33.6%, while over 39.0% for anxiety symptoms [1]. The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated this trend, with post-pandemic studies showing the prevalence of depressive and anxiety symptoms rising to 35.9% and 40.7%, respectively [1]. A large-scale survey in Slovenia also revealed that more than half of higher education students reported high levels of perceived stress [2]. In China, encompassing 1,292,811 college students, a systematic review and meta-analysis showed that the pooled prevalence of depressive symptoms among college students was 26.0% (95% CI: 23.3–28.9%) [3].

These mental health issues not only affect college students’ academic performance, current emotions, and functioning but may also lead to a range of long-term adverse outcomes. Persistent symptoms during them can significantly increase the risk of multiple adverse consequences in early adulthood, including other mental disorders, substance use disorders, suicidal ideation, and attempts [4, 5]. Furthermore, depression and anxiety during adolescents have also been identified as potential common origins of “diseases of despair” and cardiometabolic risk in middle age [6]. Functionally, depressive and anxiety symptoms are often accompanied by difficulties in daily functioning, such as self-care and communication, further limiting their social participation and development opportunities [7]. Therefore, it is important to explore the influencing factors of mental health among college students.

“24-hour movement” refers to the complete combination of physical activity behaviors an individual engages in over a 24-hour day, including components such as moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), sedentary behavior, and sleep [8, 9]. According to previous studies, unsatisfactory 24-hour movement are usually characterized by high sedentary time, low MVPA, late and short sleep, and high screen time, with self-reported data often significantly overestimating physical activity and underestimating sedentary time [10, 11]. Evidence from a series of systematic reviews, meta-analyses and longitudinal studies have shown that the specific patterns of “24-hour movement” were associated with multiple dimensions of children and adolescents’ well-being, spanning across academic performance [12, 13], physical health [14], cognitive function or developmental health [12, 13], quality of life [15], especially mental health [16].

In recent years, a growing body of research has focused on the association between children and adolescents’ 24-hour movement and their mental health status. Studies indicate that students who meet the 24-Hour Movement Guidelines (24-HMG) exhibit lower levels of depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and stress [16, 17]. For instance, numerous integrated lifestyle interventions promoting adherence to the 24-HMG have proven effective in improving the mental health of children and adolescents [18]. A study on American adolescents found that those who met the recommended amounts of physical activity, screen time, and sleep as per the 24-HMG had significantly lower depressive symptoms compared to those who did not [19]. Furthermore, higher levels of physical activity can not only directly alleviate depression, anxiety, and perceived stress levels in students but may also indirectly promote mental health through mediating pathways such as improved dietary behaviors [20]. Meanwhile, sedentary behavior, particularly prolonged screen time, is considered a modifiable mental health risk factor, although its association with depression and anxiety has shown inconsistencies across different studies [21].

Although previous studies have explored the positive effects of physical activity and mental health, there are still research gaps remained. Firstly, most studies have focused on the independent effects of a single factor (such as only physical activity or sleep) on depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and perceived stress. Secondly, despite some studies explored the association between 24-hour movement and mental health, existing research has focused primarily on the general population, older adults, children and adolescents, and there is relatively insufficient research on college students. Compared to general population or older adults, college students is a special group that is in a critical period of psychological development and has a high incidence of mental health problems. The psychological needs, decision-making logic, and dependence on family of college students are fundamentally different from those of children and adolescents who rely on their families. In addition, as a relatively subjective and novel variable, perceived stress has rarely been examined in relation to 24-hour movement .

This study uses a cross-sectional survey of college students to examine the associations between both the number of 24‑hour movement guidelines met and specific guideline‑adherence combinations and mental health. This study helps deepen the understanding of the intervention mechanism for college students’ mental health and provides a theoretical basis and practical guidance for universities to develop scientifically effective, comprehensive 24-hour movement intervention strategies.

Methods

Participants

A convenience sampling approach was adopted. From May to July 2025, students enrolled in a physical fitness course at a university were invited to complete a questionnaire survey and undergo physical examinations. The university where the survey was conducted is a comprehensive public institution in southern China, and students taking the physical fitness course come from multiple majors across the university. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary and was not linked to course grades or course requirements. A total of 893 full-time university students were initially recruited. After data cleaning and removal of 57 invalid questionnaires, 836 valid responses were retained, yielding a valid response rate of 93.6%. All participants provided written informed consent and voluntarily took part in the study. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Polytechnic University (Approval No. SPU-2025-041).

24-hour movement behaviors

The 24-hour movement behavior framework includes sleep, sedentary behavior, and physical activity [8]. The following instruments were used for assessment:

Physical activity and sedentary behavior

Physical activity and sedentary behavior were assessed using the Chinese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Short Form (IPAQ-SF) [22]. Based on participants’ self-reported time spent in vigorous-intensity and moderate-intensity physical activity, the average daily duration of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA; minutes/day) over the past week was calculated. Daily sedentary time (minutes/day) was also derived from the questionnaire.

Sleep duration

Two items from the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (Cronbach’s α = 0.92) were used to assess actual nocturnal sleep duration on weekdays and weekends [23]. The average daily sleep duration (hours/day) was calculated across the week.

Compliance with movement guidelines

Compliance with movement guidelines was determined according to the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for Adults, which recommend at least 150 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity per week, 7–9 h of sleep per day, and no more than 8 h of sedentary time per day [8]. Each participant’s behaviors were compared with these thresholds to determine guideline adherence. Two indicators were derived: the number of guidelines met, representing the total count of behaviors meeting recommendations (range: 0–3), and the types of guideline compliance, in which participants were classified into eight mutually exclusive categories based on their specific combinations of adherence—none met, MVPA only, sleep only, sedentary only, MVPA plus sleep, MVPA plus sedentary, sleep plus sedentary, and all three met.

Mental health

Mental health was assessed using the simplified Chinese version of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) [24], which has been validated in Chinese populations. The scale consists of three subscales—depression, anxiety, and stress—each containing seven items rated from 0 (“did not apply to me at all”) to 3 (“applied to me most of the time”). Following the standard scoring procedure, subscale scores were summed and then multiplied by two to obtain final scores (range: 0–42), with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. Consistent with the recommended cut-offs for the validated Chinese version, mild-level thresholds were used to classify participants at risk: ≥10 for depression, ≥ 8 for anxiety, and ≥ 15 for stress. The DASS-21 has demonstrated strong reliability and validity in both Chinese and international adult samples, with internal consistency coefficients typically ranging from 0.91 to 0.96.

Covariates

A self-administered questionnaire was used to collect basic demographic information, including age, sex, grade level, and area. Height and weight were measured using standardized procedures, and body mass index (BMI) was subsequently calculated.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare continuous variables across groups defined by the number of guidelines met, while chi-square tests were used to examine differences in categorical variables.

Multivariable linear regression models were employed to examine the associations of the number and types of guideline compliance with depression, anxiety, and stress scores, adjusting for demographic covariates. To further explore the relationship between guideline compliance and symptom risk, each DASS-21 subscale score was dichotomized according to established clinical cut-offs (presence vs. absence of symptoms), and multivariable logistic regression models were applied. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.2).

Results

Baseline characteistics

As shown in Table 1, a total of 836 university students participated in the survey. Their ages ranged from 17 to 25 years, with a mean age of 19.3 ± 3.8 years. Of the participants, 514 (61.5%) were male. Most students were freshmen or sophomores, had a normal body weight (79.5%), and reported an urban household registration (68.1%). Regarding guideline compliance, 23 students did not meet any of the recommendations, whereas 140, 379, and 294 students met one, two, and all three guidelines, respectively. A chi-square test indicated significant sex differences in the number of guidelines met (p < 0.001). The prevalence of stress, anxiety, and depression among participants was 84.4%, 61.4%, and 78.0%, respectively, with mean scores of 8.3 ± 7.1 for stress, 6.7 ± 6.1 for anxiety, and 5.4 ± 6.0 for depression.

Associations of guideline compliance with stress, anxiety, and depression scores

After adjusting for all covariates, multivariable linear regression analyses revealed significant negative associations between the number of movement guidelines met and scores for stress, anxiety, and depression (all p for trend < 0.05). Using students who met none of the guidelines as the reference group, psychological symptom scores decreased as the number of guidelines met increased.

For stress scores, the regression coefficients (B) for meeting one, two, and three guidelines were −0.32 (95% CI: −3.43, 2.80; p = 0.841), −1.29 (95% CI: −4.26, 1.68; p = 0.396), and −2.04 (95% CI: −5.04, 0.96; p = 0.183), respectively. For anxiety scores, the corresponding B values were −1.17 (95% CI: −3.89, 1.54; p = 0.396), −2.43 (95% CI: −5.01, 0.15; p = 0.065), and −2.71 (95% CI: −5.33, −0.10; p = 0.042). For depression scores, the B values were −2.43 (95% CI: −5.08, 0.22; p = 0.072), −2.98 (95% CI: −5.51, −0.46; p = 0.021), and −4.14 (95% CI: −6.69, −1.59; p < 0.001), respectively (see Table 2).

Regarding types of guideline compliance, compared with the “none met” group, meeting both the MVPA and sedentary behavior guidelines was associated with lower depression scores (B = −3.51, 95% CI: −6.31, −0.71; p = 0.014). Meeting both the sedentary behavior and sleep guidelines was associated with lower anxiety (B = −2.69, 95% CI: −5.32, −0.06; p = 0.045) and depression scores (B = −3.05, 95% CI: −5.62, −0.48; p = 0.020).

Associations of guideline compliance with stress, anxiety, and depression status

Multivariable logistic regression analyses adjusting for covariates showed that, compared with meeting none of the guidelines, meeting one guideline was associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms, although the association did not reach statistical significance (OR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.16, 1.02; p > 0.05). Meeting two guidelines was significantly associated with a reduced risk of depressive symptoms (OR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.14, 0.77; p = 0.009). Meeting all three guidelines was significantly associated with lower risks of anxiety symptoms (OR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.17, 0.96; p = 0.040) and depressive symptoms (OR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.08, 0.50; p < 0.001) (see Table 3).

A statistically significant trend was observed indicating that meeting a greater number of guidelines was associated with lower risks of stress (p = 0.028), anxiety (p = 0.002), and depression (p < 0.001) symptoms. Regarding types of guideline compliance, meeting both the MVPA and sedentary behavior guidelines was significantly associated with lower risks of anxiety symptoms (OR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.12, 0.85; p = 0.023) and depressive symptoms (OR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.10, 0.76; p = 0.012). Meeting both the sedentary behavior and sleep guidelines was significantly associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms (OR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.13, 0.75; p = 0.008)

Discussion

The results of this study indicated a significant negative correlation between meeting 24-hour movement guidelines and mental health outcomes. Compared with participants who met none of the guidelines, those who met one, two, or three guidelines showed a progressively decreasing risk of mental health outcomes. These findings are consistent with previous research, which has shown that individuals who simultaneously meet all three requirements of the 24-hour movement guidelines have a significantly reduced risk of psychological symptoms [25, 26].

The study did not provide robust evidence for single-guideline associations after adjustment, a conclusion consistent with other global studies analyzing the 24-hour movement guidelines [27, 28]. The results showed that a significant effect was only observed when at least two out of the three guidelines were met. This can be attributed to the cumulative effect of the benefits of each health behavior, as well as the potential synergistic associations between these behaviors and mental health outcomes when analyzed in combination [29]. This may also be due to the limited statistical power caused by the small reference group.

Notably, there were varying relationships between different combinations of 24-hour movement behaviors and mental health outcomes. Among the combinations meeting two guidelines, adherence to both the physical activity and sedentary behavior guidelines was most strongly associated with lower anxiety and depressive symptom scores, and with lower odds of these symptoms. This finding is closely related to the actual behavioral patterns of Chinese college students. A recent study exploring the composition of 24-hour movement behaviors associated with optimal mental health among Chinese college students pointed out that college students spend considerable time on sedentary behavior and sleep, while the time devoted to physical activity is relatively insufficient [30]. Existing research has confirmed that physical activity can counteract the negative impact of sedentary behavior on mental health through complex biological pathways, with mechanisms including regulating the expression of neurobiological molecules, optimizing blood and cellular metabolic processes, and enhancing connectivity between functional brain regions [31]. Research by Werneck et al. further demonstrated that physical activity plays a significant moderating role in the relationship between sedentary time and depressive symptoms among adults [32]. However, the associations between physical activity, sedentary behavior and mental health may vary depending on the specific context: leisure-time physical activity may alleviate depressive symptoms [33], whereas passive sedentary behavior, such as prolonged social media scrolling or watching videos, may pose significant harm to mental health [34]. Because physical activity and sedentary behavior are distinct behaviors that alternate throughout the day, a high level of sedentary behavior may persist even when overall physical activity levels meet recommendations [35]. Moreover, even after accounting for the influence of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), the detrimental health effects of prolonged sedentary behavior remain evident [36]. Therefore, the stronger association observed for this combination may reflect the potential synergistic benefits of meeting both guidelines simultaneously.

Furthermore, we found that when all three guidelines were met simultaneously, the strength of the association was greater for depressive symptoms than for anxiety symptoms. Several potential mechanisms proposed in prior literature may help to interpret the links between the different behaviors mentioned in the 24-hour movement guidelines and psychological symptoms, although these mechanisms were not directly measured in the present study and should therefore be considered speculative. Firstly, physical activity, sedentary time, and adequate sleep are associated with changes in hippocampal function and structure, thereby influencing neuroplasticity to some extent [37]. Secondly, components of the movement guidelines have been shown to correlate with inflammation [38], which is itself associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms [39]. Thirdly, previous studies have pointed out that movement behaviors are related to the regulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which is linked to immune regulation and subsequently to anxiety and depressive symptoms. Additionally, meeting the 24-hour movement guidelines does not occur in a social vacuum. Prior experimental research has shown that perceived campus norms can influence individuals’ intentions toward health-related behaviors [40]. Moreover, ingroup norms may shape individuals’ normative expectations, thereby promoting adherence to the 24-hour movement guidelines [41]. Therefore, this association may reflect not only individual-level behavioral patterns and physiological mechanisms, but also the broader social embeddedness of these behaviors. Subtle differences among these potential mechanisms may partly help to explain why the associations differed between anxiety and depressive symptoms.

However, the combination of physical activity and sleep duration did not show robust evidence of association with any of the three psychological symptoms after adjustment, and no combination was significantly associated with stress symptoms. Taken together, these findings suggest that the associations between meeting the 24-hour movement guidelines and mental health may be outcome-specific rather than uniform across all psychological domains. These findings may also be related to measurement limitations, context-specific variations in the associations, restricted variability or possible ceiling effects in stress scores, or limited statistical power due to the small number of participants meeting these specific combinations.

Additionally, it is noteworthy that perceived stress appeared to be less strongly associated with meeting the 24-hour movement guidelines than were depressive and anxiety symptoms. One possible explanation is that perceived stress may represent a more proximal construct, reflecting short-term emotional changes and individuals’ cognitive appraisal of recent stressful circumstances. Unfavorable 24-hour movement patterns may be associated with poorer emotional health outcomes, but may not necessarily lead to a significant change in individuals’ subjective perceptions of stress. In this sense, habitual movement patterns may be more closely related to relatively stable symptom profiles than to short-term appraisals of stress.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents any inference about temporal ordering or causality. Because mental health symptoms may themselves influence physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep, reverse causality is highly plausible. Second, participants were recruited from a physical fitness course at a single university, which may introduce selection bias. These students may differ from the broader Chinese university population in physical activity habits, health awareness, or institutional environment; therefore, caution is needed when generalizing the findings. In addition, the guideline-adherence groups were imbalanced, and the reference group of students meeting none of the guidelines was relatively small. This limited sample size may reduce the stability of the regression estimates and widen confidence intervals. Third, although several demographic variables were adjusted for, a number of potentially important confounders—such as socioeconomic status, academic pressure, chronic illness, dietary habits, alcohol use, smoking, prior mental health conditions, medication use, and sleep quality—were not assessed in this study, and residual confounding may therefore remain. Finally, the measurement of 24-hour movement behaviors relied on self-reported instruments, which may introduce recall bias and misclassification. Physical activity and sedentary behavior were assessed using the IPAQ-SF, which is known to overestimate physical activity, and sleep duration was derived from only two PSQI items. Sedentary behavior was measured as total sedentary time without distinguishing specific contexts, which may have different associations with mental health.

Future studies should consider longitudinal designs, incorporate objective measurement tools (e.g., accelerometers or wearable devices), and include more comprehensive assessments of sleep and sedentary behavior to improve measurement precision. In addition, as 24-hour movement behaviors are inherently interdependent components of a finite time budget, future research would benefit from applying more advanced analytical frameworks—such as compositional data analysis (CoDA)—to better capture the proportional structure of the 24-hour movement system [42]. Integrating these methodological improvements, together with recruiting students from more diverse regions, will help strengthen the generalizability and theoretical rigor of future work.
