From lecture hall to clinic: dental students’ AI readiness and anxiety across educational stages
Authors: Kübra Nur Çakan, İrem İpek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly shaping modern healthcare, its integration into dental education remains underexplored—particularly with regard to how students’ readiness and anxiety toward AI evolve throughout their academic progression. Existing studies primarily focus on medical cohorts, leaving a critical knowledge gap in understanding how dental students across different years perceive and adapt to these technological transformations. This study aims to address this gap by assessing both cognitive readiness and emotional responses to AI among current and future dental professionals.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted with 420 participants, including first- to fifth-year dental students and recent graduates, using purposive sampling. Two validated instruments were employed: the Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale and the Artificial Intelligence Anxiety Scale. Descriptive statistics and one-way ANOVA were used to analyze differences across academic year, gender, age, AI familiarity, and prior training experience.ResultsSignificant differences in AI readiness were observed based on academic year, with second-year students demonstrating the highest readiness across subdomains (Cognition, Ability, Vision), while fifth-year students scored the lowest. Participants with prior AI-related knowledge or training consistently showed higher readiness and lower anxiety levels, although the reduction in anxiety was not always statistically significant. Gender-based analysis revealed that female participants reported higher AI anxiety in specific subdomains, including Learning and Surveillance, while overall readiness scores did not significantly differ by gender or age.ConclusionFindings emphasize the need for early, continuous, and practice-oriented AI education in dental curricula. Structured exposure to AI-related concepts not only enhances competence but may also reduce anxiety. Tailoring pedagogical approaches to different stages of training could foster a more balanced integration of AI in dentistry—addressing both technological proficiency and emotional resilience.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming healthcare through innovations in data analytics, machine learning, and automation. In clinical contexts, AI is being applied to support diagnosis, treatment planning, image interpretation, and electronic health records, offering potential benefits in efficiency, precision, and patient outcomes [1–4]. However, the adoption of AI technologies in healthcare also raises critical concerns, including ethical, legal, and psychological challenges such as data privacy, algorithmic bias, accountability, and user trust [5, 6].

In dentistry, AI-supported systems have been increasingly utilized in fields such as radiological interpretation, early caries detection, periodontal disease monitoring, and even administrative automation [4, 7, 8]. As AI technologies become more prevalent in clinical practice, it is essential to assess the readiness of future and current dental professionals to adopt and integrate such innovations into their workflows.

AI readiness refers to the level of competence, awareness, and motivation required to engage with AI technologies in professional contexts. This encompasses the ability to understand AI concepts, critically evaluate AI-driven tools, and implement these technologies ethically within healthcare settings [9].

In addition to readiness, psychological responses to AI—particularly anxiety—represent another critical factor influencing the successful adoption of technology in healthcare. Such anxiety may manifest as fears of job loss, diminished autonomy, or distrust of autonomous systems. Elevated levels of AI-related anxiety can hinder the integration of these technologies, even when technical competence is sufficient [2].

While AI readiness and anxiety have been examined among medical students and mixed healthcare cohorts, there is limited research focusing specifically on dental students, and even fewer studies have compared these attitudes across different stages of dental education. However, no prior studies have specifically explored how AI readiness and anxiety vary between different stages of dental education, such as the transition from preclinical to clinical training. This lack of stratified insight limits the development of stage-appropriate educational strategies. Understanding how readiness and anxiety evolve throughout the educational continuum—from early preclinical years to post-graduation—is essential for designing targeted curricular interventions and continuing professional development programs.

This study addresses this gap by evaluating and comparing AI readiness and AI-related anxiety among first- through fifth-year dental students and practicing dentists. Using validated scales, it examines the influence of academic stage, demographic variables, and prior AI exposure. The findings aim to inform curriculum design, promote early AI literacy, and guide strategies to reduce anxiety while fostering readiness for technology-driven practice in dentistry.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Fırat University (approval number: [2025/05 − 01]). Before participation, all individuals were provided with detailed information regarding the purpose, content, and voluntary nature of the study.

Participation was anonymous and voluntary, and completion of the online questionnaire was considered as an indication of informed consent. Respondents were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any negative consequences.

No personal identifiers were collected at any stage of the research. Data were analyzed in aggregate form and stored securely in accordance with data protection regulations. The study involved no clinical intervention, and no personal health information was collected.

Study design and participants

This descriptive, cross-sectional survey study was conducted to assess and compare AI readiness and AI-related anxiety among dental students and licensed dentists. The target population included individuals involved in the field of dentistry in Türkiye, grouped into six categories: first-, second-, third-, fourth-, and fifth-year undergraduate dental students and practicing dentists.

Participants were recruited via purposive sampling using institutional email lists and social media platforms (e.g., WhatsApp student groups and alumni networks). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) currently enrolled in a dental faculty (1st − 5th year) or holding a dentistry degree; (2) voluntary participation; and (3) full completion of the survey with no inconsistent responses. Incomplete or invalid responses were excluded from the analysis.

A priori power analysis was performed using G*Power (v3.1, University of Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on a medium effect size (f = 0.25), an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 222 participants. The survey was distributed to 526 individuals. A total of 420 participants voluntarily completed the questionnaire, resulting in an overall response rate of approximately 79.8%. Among them, 349 were undergraduate dental students (1st year: 68; 2nd year: 67; 3rd year: 72; 4th year: 77; 5th year: 65), and 71 were practicing dentists.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected between March and June 2025 via an online survey hosted on Google Forms. The survey link was distributed through institutional email announcements and professional social media networks targeting dental students and practicing dentists. This was a closed survey, accessible only to the intended population. No personal identifiers or IP addresses were collected, and no cookies or IP tracking were employed. Data were stored in a secure, password-protected environment accessible only to the research team. Duplicate entries were prevented by allowing only one submission per Google account.

The introductory page outlined the study’s objectives, estimated completion time (approximately 10–12 min), and the voluntary and anonymous nature of participation. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Proceeding to the survey after reading this information was considered as implied informed consent. Written consent was not sought due to the fully online nature of the survey, which made obtaining signed forms impractical. The introductory page explicitly stated that continuing to the questionnaire indicated voluntary participation, while those who declined could exit freely.

No formal pilot testing was conducted. However, all items were reviewed by two dental faculty members to ensure linguistic clarity, conceptual consistency, and contextual relevance to dental education. All items were mandatory, ensuring completeness prior to submission. Respondents were also able to review and change their responses before final submission.

Data collection instruments

Medical artificial intelligence readiness scale (MAIRS)

The Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale developed by Karaca et al. [9] is designed to assess medical students’ preparedness to engage with AI technologies across cognitive, applied, visionary, and ethical domains within healthcare.

The scale comprises 22 items distributed across four dimensions: Cognition, Ability, Vision, and Ethics. The Cognition dimension evaluates respondents’ conceptual understanding of AI-related principles; Ability assesses practical competence in utilizing AI tools in medical contexts; Vision measures the capacity to anticipate the future impact of AI on healthcare delivery; and Ethics reflects awareness of ethical considerations surrounding AI integration in medicine.

The Cognition and Ability dimensions each include eight items, with scores ranging from 8 to 40 points. The Vision and Ethics dimensions each consist of three items, with scores ranging from 3 to 15 points. Accordingly, the total score ranges from 22 to 110, with higher scores indicating greater readiness to adopt and utilize AI in medical settings.

All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). The original scale demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.87). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) supported the four-factor structure, yielding acceptable model fit indices: χ²/df = 3.81, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) = 0.094, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) = 0.057, Comparative Fit Index (CFI) = 0.938, and Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI) = 0.928. These values collectively indicate satisfactory construct validity.

Artificial intelligence anxiety scale (AIAS)

The Artificial Intelligence Anxiety Scale, originally developed by Wang et al. [2], was employed in this study using its Turkish adaptation validated by Yazıcı and Yıldırım [10]. The scale aims to assess individuals’ emotional and cognitive responses to the integration of AI into daily life, professional environments, and broader sociotechnical systems.

The Turkish version of the scale consists of 16 items and is structured into four distinct subdimensions: Learning, Job Replacement, Surveillance, and Dehumanization. The Learning subdimension evaluates anxiety about the increasing demand to understand and keep up with AI technologies. The Job Replacement dimension assesses concerns related to the potential of AI to replace human roles and professions. The Surveillance dimension reflects fears of being constantly monitored or tracked by AI-driven systems. The Dehumanization subdimension captures anxieties related to the erosion of human values and social relationships in an AI-dominated world. Higher scores across these subdimensions indicate greater levels of anxiety toward the integration of AI into professional and societal contexts.

Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree), with higher scores indicating higher levels of AI-related anxiety. The total score ranges from 16 to 80.

In the Turkish validation study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.90). CFA supported the four-factor model with acceptable fit indices (χ²/df = 2.54, RMSEA = 0.072, SRMR = 0.056, CFI = 0.941, and TLI = 0.926), indicating that the scale possesses satisfactory construct validity for use in Turkish-speaking populations.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables, with continuous variables reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as frequency (percentage).

The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and supported by visual inspection of histograms. As the data met the assumption of normality, parametric tests were applied. Between-group differences in AI readiness and AI anxiety subdimensions were examined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant differences were identified, Tukey’s HSD post hoc test was performed for pairwise comparisons.

A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes (η²) were calculated for ANOVA results to assess the magnitude of observed differences.

Results

A total of 420 participants took part in the study, of whom 66% were female and 34% male. The majority were between 21 and 24 years old (60.2%), followed by those over 25 (21.4%) and under 20 (18.3%). In terms of educational status, all five academic years of dental education were represented, as well as graduated dentists. The highest proportions were observed among fourth-year (18.3%) and third-year (17.1%) students. Regarding AI familiarity, 49% reported having prior knowledge of artificial intelligence applications, while only 7.4% had received formal training or attended a course related to AI in medicine (Table 1).

The results indicated statistically significant differences in Medical AI Readiness and its subdimensions based on several demographic and experience-based variables. This downward trend contrasts with the elevated readiness observed in second-year students and may reflect shifting educational priorities or increasing clinical burdens in later years. No statistically significant differences were observed between males and females or among different age groups in overall readiness (p >.05), although trends favored those over 25 in some dimensions, as detailed in Table 3. Discussion

The integration of AI into dental education is becoming increasingly important for both individual learning processes and professional practice. In this context, healthcare professionals’ levels of readiness for AI and the anxiety they experience toward this technology are considered critical factors influencing the effectiveness of digital transformation in healthcare. This study examined dental students’ and graduates’ attitudes toward AI in relation to various sociodemographic and educational variables. The findings were interpreted within the framework of existing literature and contribute to a deeper understanding of how AI-related perceptions evolve throughout professional development.

The data obtained indicate that attitudes toward AI vary significantly across different academic year levels. These differences are particularly pronounced among early-year students, forming the starting point of the discussion. The high level of AI readiness observed among second-year students suggests that this stage offers a critical opportunity for the development of technological competence and professional awareness. This period typically marks the transition from basic sciences to clinical practice, during which students begin to construct their professional identity and are increasingly exposed to digital learning materials and simulation-based systems. Such exposure appears to positively influence their cognitive awareness of AI, perceived self-efficacy, and future-oriented vision.

In contrast, the lower levels of AI readiness observed among fifth-year students may be attributed to the dominance of clinical responsibilities and the lack of structured AI content in the later stages of the curriculum. As clinical priorities take precedence, students may become less engaged with emerging technologies, making it difficult to sustain AI-related learning. Similar observations have been reported elsewhere: for example, a scoping review found that many dental curricula struggle to integrate technological advances into clinical training years, often due to increasing clinical workload and insufficient faculty capacity [3, 11]. These findings indicate that raising early awareness alone is insufficient; instead, AI education should be designed as a gradual and practice-oriented process that spans the entire course of dental training.

Existing literature frequently emphasizes the importance of appropriate guidance and supportive instructional strategies to help students effectively transfer their knowledge and skills to clinical practice during this formative period [12].

Accordingly, it is recommended that AI education in dentistry begin in the early years with digital literacy modules focused on fundamental concepts and ethical dimensions [13]. AI literacy could be introduced through elective courses, complemented by awareness-raising seminars on the use of AI in healthcare and dentistry, as well as short online learning modules that allow students to progress at their own pace [14]. In the intermediate years, learning can be reinforced through problem-based learning approaches using clinical scenarios, while in the clinical years, AI-assisted decision support systems should be integrated into case-based applications [15]. This progressive model would allow students to experience AI not merely as a theoretical subject but as a practical professional skill directly contributing to patient care. Following graduation, this educational trajectory should be supported by continuing professional development programs to ensure the long-term and sustainable integration of AI into dental practice.

The number of studies applying the MAIRS specifically to dental students remains quite limited in the literature. Most existing research has been conducted with medical students or mixed groups of healthcare students, with few studies offering class-level comparisons. For instance, a study conducted at Qatar University focused exclusively on dental students and reported overall readiness scores but did not include analyses based on academic year [16]. Similarly, a study from Ardabil University in Iran compared students and faculty members but did not differentiate among student cohorts by year level [17]. In both cases, general cognitive awareness of AI was found to be low. In a large-scale study conducted in Pakistan, medical and dental students were evaluated together, and no significant differences in AI readiness were found based on academic year or field of study [18]. From this perspective, our study is pioneering, offering one of the first class-level insights in dental education.

With regard to study year, no statistically significant differences were observed across the AI anxiety subdimensions. Nonetheless, mean score trends suggest a slight increase in anxiety during the later years, particularly among fifth-year students, who reported relatively higher values in the Dehumanization and Learning dimensions. This tendency may reflect increased perceptions of uncertainty and diminished autonomy stemming from escalating clinical responsibilities during the final stages of dental education. Such a pattern is widely documented in the literature as a contributor to elevated occupational stress, especially during the transition from preclinical to clinical training, which is often accompanied by role ambiguity and intensified workload [19–21]. In this context, the observed anxiety levels among senior students may be linked not only to technical unfamiliarity with AI but also to psychosocial pressures encountered during this critical phase of professional identity formation.

On the other hand, the graduate group demonstrated the lowest mean anxiety scores in the “job replacement” subdimension, which may be attributed to their clearer professional role definitions and stronger perceptions of job security. Although this association has not been specifically studied in the context of dentistry, the broader occupational psychology literature suggests that variables such as job security and role clarity can mitigate technology-related anxiety [22]. In this context, the more firmly established professional identity of graduate dentists may serve as a buffering factor against perceived threats posed by artificial intelligence.

Indeed, the theoretical framework of the AIAS developed by Wang and Wang supports this trend, emphasizing that individuals’ anxiety toward AI is influenced not only by a lack of technical knowledge but also by socio-cognitive factors such as professional identity, role ambiguity, and perceived employment security [2]. This highlights the notion that throughout their education, students require not only cognitive input but also psychological reassurance.

Similarly, recent evidence from dental education further supports this view. Özbey and Yaşa [23] demonstrated that students with higher levels of anxiety had greater difficulties in accepting AI, and that individual personality traits such as openness and conscientiousness predicted more positive orientations, whereas neuroticism was associated with increased anxiety. These findings are in line with our results and reinforce the notion that AI-related anxiety is not merely a cognitive deficit but is shaped by broader psychosocial characteristics, underlining the importance of addressing both emotional and individual differences in educational strategies.

Therefore, even in the absence of statistically significant differences across academic years, the content and intensity of AI-related anxiety appear to vary according to stages of professional development. This finding underscores the need to address anxiety as an integrated component of the educational process and suggests that targeted educational interventions—particularly during the clinical years—are essential for recognizing anxiety and mitigating it through structured, competence-building technology training.

In this study, no statistically significant differences were found regarding age in either the MAIRS or AIAS. This finding aligns with prior research suggesting that age alone is not a decisive factor in shaping attitudes toward AI [17, 24]. However, an analysis of mean scores revealed that participants over the age of 25 reported higher scores in the “Cognition” and “Ability” subdimensions of the readiness scale, along with lower scores across all subdimensions of the anxiety scale.

This trend may indicate that increasing age—often accompanied by greater clinical experience and professional foresight—enhances individuals’ perceptions of technological competence while reducing feelings of uncertainty and threat. Consistent with this, existing literature suggests that as experience accumulates, AI is perceived less as a source of anxiety and more as a practical tool within professional roles [25–27].

Nevertheless, the absence of statistically significant age differences suggests that attitudes toward AI are not solely shaped by fixed demographic factors such as age, but are also influenced by more dynamic and modifiable elements, including curricular content, levels of technological exposure, clinical application experience, and individual motivation. In this regard, it may be more effective to develop training models based on experience and exposure rather than age per se, with the aim of enhancing readiness and reducing anxiety.

In this study, no statistically significant differences were observed in AI readiness levels based on gender. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted by Hamad et al. in Saudi Arabia [24]. and Yazdi et al. in Pakistan [17] both of which reported no significant gender-based differences in AI readiness. These studies suggest that perceptions of technological competence are shaped more by personal exposure to technology and educational experiences than by biological sex.

However, a separate study conducted in Pakistan by Baseer et al. [18] found that male students had significantly higher AI readiness scores compared to their female counterparts. This discrepancy may reflect the influence of contextual factors such as cultural norms, gender roles, and disparities in access to technological resources. Therefore, the impact of gender on AI readiness should not be considered a universal phenomenon but rather a context-sensitive variable that can vary across different sociocultural settings.

On the other hand, analyses based on the AIAS revealed that female participants scored significantly higher than male participants across all subdimensions. This finding aligns with existing literature suggesting that women tend to exhibit stronger affective responses to technological innovations [28, 29]. However, this does not imply that women are less capable of adopting new technologies; rather, it reflects a more critical and cautious attitude toward potential risks.

In conclusion, the findings indicate that gender does not play a determining role in cognitive readiness for AI, but it may contribute to significant differences in affective responses to technological change. Therefore, AI education should incorporate gender-sensitive, emotionally supportive, and confidence-enhancing components. Pedagogical interventions such as structured teaching strategies, experiential learning environments, and risk-oriented discussion sessions may be particularly effective in addressing and reducing anxiety among female students, thereby contributing to more equitable engagement with emerging technologies.

When examining participants’ self-reported AI knowledge (Question 4) and prior participation in AI-related education or seminars (Question 5) in relation to both AI Readiness and AI Anxiety, notable trends were observed. Participants who answered “yes” to these items scored significantly higher across all subdimensions of the AI Readiness Scale, suggesting that both knowledge acquisition and experience-based learning directly enhance cognitive awareness, self-efficacy, and future-oriented vision. This finding is consistent with the study conducted by Hamad et al. [24], which also reported significantly higher readiness levels among individuals who had received formal AI education.

These results underscore the value of structured and targeted AI training programs in health sciences education. Such interventions not only enhance theoretical knowledge but also strengthen technological competence and professional adaptability. In today’s rapidly evolving healthcare environment, the integration of AI-focused training into the curriculum may help students become more resilient and better equipped to engage with emerging technologies.

On the other hand, although no statistically significant differences were found in AI Anxiety scores for either variable, participants who reported prior knowledge or had attended AI-related seminars consistently exhibited lower mean scores across all subdimensions. This suggests that educational exposure may foster a sense of affective security, reducing perceived uncertainty and threat. These findings indicate that knowledge does not only operate at the cognitive level but also plays a regulatory role in shaping emotional responses to emerging technologies [30].

Accordingly, AI education should be designed not solely to deliver conceptual content, but also to mitigate technology-related anxiety through pedagogically informed strategies. The integration of accessible, multi-layered, and practice-oriented AI modules into the dental curriculum may serve as an effective approach to enhancing readiness while simultaneously addressing affective resistance.

Limitations

While this study represents one of the pioneering efforts to evaluate dental students’ readiness and anxiety levels regarding AI, it is not without limitations. First, its cross-sectional design restricts the findings to a single point in time, limiting the ability to assess evolving trends or infer causal relationships. Additionally, the study was conducted with students from a single public university, which may constrain the generalizability of the results to other institutions or regions. The use of purposive sampling may also have introduced sampling bias, and the lack of information regarding the response rate further limits the ability to assess the representativeness of the sample. The voluntary nature of participation could also have introduced selection bias into the sample. Given that data collection relied on self-report measures, the potential influence of social desirability bias should also be considered. Moreover, although attitudes toward AI may be shaped by a wide range of factors, the study did not account for variables such as digital literacy, general attitudes toward technology, or socioeconomic status. For these reasons, future research utilizing more diverse samples and longitudinal designs is warranted and may provide valuable contributions to the field.

Conclusion

This study highlights stage-specific dynamics in dental students’ readiness and anxiety toward AI, demonstrating that early peaks in readiness are followed by later declines as clinical demands increase. While readiness has been the primary driver for curricular reform, our results underscore that AI-related anxiety—shaped by gender, role uncertainty, and professional identity—is equally influential in technology adoption. Therefore, curricular strategies should not only embed AI literacy modules early and reinforce them through practice-oriented clinical exposure but also incorporate affective support to reduce anxiety. Future studies should adopt longitudinal and intervention-based designs to track attitudinal changes over time, test the effectiveness of structured AI modules, and extend investigations across multiple institutions. Complementary qualitative approaches may further illuminate the nuanced psychological factors influencing readiness and anxiety, thereby informing comprehensive educational strategies.
