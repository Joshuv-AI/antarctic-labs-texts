Renal ultrasound after first febrile urinary tract infection in hospitalized children: The ROUTINE prospective observational study protocol

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrinary tract infection (UTI) is a common reason for hospitalization in young children. American and Canadian guidelines recommend universal renal ultrasound after a first febrile UTI to identify genitourinary abnormalities that require further management, despite limited supporting evidence. The Renal ultrasOund after first febrile Urinary Tract INfEction (ROUTINE) study aims to (a) determine the incidence of clinically important and insignificant genitourinary abnormalities on renal ultrasound, (b) describe the cascade of healthcare interventions following the detection of abnormalities, (c) explore parents’ experiences, and (d) identify clinical factors associated with clinically important abnormalities.MethodThis multicentre, prospective, observational study with an embedded qualitative component will enroll children <24 months of age hospitalized at 17 children's and community hospitals in Canada and the United States after a first febrile UTI, with 1‐year follow‐up. The primary outcome is detection of genitourinary abnormalities on renal ultrasound, classified by clinical importance using a standardized system. Secondary outcomes include clinical (recurrent febrile UTI), radiologic (vesicoureteral reflux), healthcare interventions (surgery, antibiotic prophylaxis, imaging, specialist follow‐up), and parent‐reported outcomes (anxiety, involvement in decision making). Parent experiences will be explored through semistructured interviews. Primary analysis will estimate the incidence of clinically important abnormalities and potential overdiagnosis with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Secondary analysis will summarize radiologic, clinical, healthcare intervention, and parent‐reported outcomes and assess predictors of clinically important abnormalities using multivariable logistic regression.DiscussionThis multicentre prospective study with longitudinal follow‐up will generate evidence on the utility of universal renal ultrasound after the first febrile UTI. Findings will inform evidence‐based management, shared decision‐making, and clinical practice guidelines.

INTRODUCTION

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is a common reason for hospitalization in young children across both community and children's hospitals. 1 , 2 UTIs account for 5%–8% of all febrile episodes in infants and occur in approximately 2% of boys and 8% of girls by 7 years of age. 1 , 2 , 3 In both Canada and the United States, UTI is a leading cause for pediatric hospitalization and accounts for substantial hospitalization costs. 4 , 5

The Canadian Paediatric Society (CPS) and American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guidelines recommend universal screening ultrasound for all children less than 2 years after a first febrile UTI. 6 Renal ultrasound is a noninvasive, radiation‐free, and relatively low‐cost imaging modality used to detect anatomic abnormalities and urinary tract dilatation suggestive of high‐grade vesicoureteral reflux (VUR). 6 Abnormal findings may prompt additional testing (e.g., voiding cystourethrogram [VCUG], repeat ultrasound), therapeutic interventions (e.g., antibiotic prophylaxis for high‐grade VUR, surgery), and specialist referrals. CPS and AAP guidelines further advise using ultrasound results to guide VCUG decisions, and to consult pediatric urology or nephrology for abnormal findings, although standardized criteria for interpreting abnormal findings are lacking. 1 , 6 , 7 , 8

The value of routine renal ultrasound after a first UTI remains controversial. Routine screening risks overdiagnosis, that is, the detection of true abnormalities that would not otherwise cause symptoms or morbidity, while selective imaging strategies may risk missing clinically important abnormalities. Renal ultrasound has limited diagnostic accuracy for high‐grade VUR (sensitivity 40%, specificity 75%) compared with the gold standard VCUG. 9 , 10 , 11 Moreover, the widespread use of prenatal ultrasonography has led to earlier detection of serious genitourinary abnormalities, further questioning the incremental value of first‐episode UTI ultrasound screening. 6 , 7 , 12 Simulation models also suggest that universal screening may not be cost‐effective. 13 In contrast to CPS and AAP guidelines, the United Kingdom's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline does not recommend universal screening; rather, ultrasound is advised only for infants under 6 months after a first febrile UTI, or for older children with atypical or recurrent infection. 14 Reflecting this uncertainty, the CPS notes that it is “controversial whether there is a need to obtain a renal ultrasound” in this era of routine prenatal screening. 8

A recent meta‐analysis of 29 studies reported that 22.1% (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.3%–8.1%) of renal ultrasound exams revealed an abnormality, while the pooled yield of clinically important abnormalities in eight studies was only 3.1% (95% CI, 0.5%–12.0%) following a first febrile UTI. 15 However, significant heterogeneity across studies, inconsistent classification of ultrasound findings, limited follow‐up, and a lack of patient‐centered outcomes underscored the need for high‐quality prospective studies. 13

The Renal ultrasOund after first febrile Urinary Tract INfEction (ROUTINE) study aims to determine the clinical utility of the universal screening renal ultrasound in children 0–24 months of age hospitalized with a first febrile UTI and no previously identified genitourinary abnormality. The study objectives are to: (a) determine the incidence of clinically important abnormalities and overdiagnosis, (b) describe the cascade of healthcare interventions associated with overdiagnosis, (c) explore parents’ experiences, and (d) identify clinical predictors of clinically important abnormalities.

METHODS

Study design

This is a multicentre, pragmatic, prospective observational study, with an embedded qualitative component, across 16 Canadian hospitals and one US hospital, including both children's and community centres. The study enrolls infants from birth to 24 months of age hospitalized for their first febrile UTI with 1‐year follow‐up (see Table 1 for schedule of study activities). In addition, semistructured virtual interviews will be conducted with a purposeful sample of parents to explore their experiences and preferences. Recruitment at the first site began in June 2023, with all sites recruiting patients by December 2024.

Study setting and population

Participants will be recruited from General Pediatric Inpatient Units (GPIUs) and/or the hospital day‐patient unit. Day‐patient units (or referred to as medical day hospital), included at several sites, serve to manage children requiring parenteral antibiotics and close follow‐up while avoiding inpatient admission. While children managed in the primary care outpatient setting with a first‐episode UTI would also be relevant to our study question, we focused on hospitalized children because they are the population represented within our research network and because tracking renal ultrasound results performed in community radiology sites for outpatients would be logistically challenging. Language interpretation services will be available for families as needed.

Data sources and management

Data will be obtained from electronic health records and parent reports by trained research assistants at the 17 hospitals within the Canadian Paediatric Inpatient Research Network (https://www.pirncanada.com). The Ontario Child Health Support Unit (http://www.ochsu.ca), a Strategy for Patient‐Oriented (SPOR) funded center, will serve as a data management centre. Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) will be used for data entry, storage, and management.

Baseline characteristics

The following patient characteristics will be collected: age (in days), sex, gestational age, causative organism (E. coli vs. other bacteria), C‐reactive protein (CRP) level (normal vs. elevated), presence of bacteremia, presence of sepsis, circumcision status (males only), timing of renal ultrasound (days from initiation of antibiotic therapy), and elevated serum creatinine for age (yes/no). Prenatal imaging variables will include whether a prenatal ultrasound was performed and, if so, the trimester and results (normal, abnormal for renal findings, or uncertain). Family and sociodemographic factors will be collected, including family history of genitourinary abnormalities. Parent gender, race, and ethnicity will be collected to support diverse qualitative sampling and contextual interpretation of parent‐reported experiences.

Outcomes

Outcomes will include radiologic, clinical, healthcare intervention, and parent‐reported outcomes.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the detection of genitourinary abnormalities on renal ultrasound following the first febrile UTI. Abnormalities will be classified using a standardized system for febrile UTI assessment in children younger than 2 years (Table 2), which incorporates the Society for Fetal Urology (SFU) grading of hydronephrosis. 2 , 20 De‐identified reports and images of the index ultrasound will be obtained for each participant. Two central study pediatricians will classify all ultrasound reports independently using the standardized system. Discrepancies will be resolved by consensus between the two study pediatricians; if consensus is not achieved, a third study pediatrician will serve as adjudicator. De‐identified ultrasound images will also be independently reviewed by two pediatric radiologists at the central site, blinded to patient care, to classify hydronephrosis using the SFU system.

Parent‐reported outcomes

Anxiety will be measured using a single‐item question adapted from the adult State‐Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). 21 Parents will rate their anxiety after receiving renal ultrasound results on a 4‐point Likert‐type scale (1 = not at all, 4 = very much). This measure was used in a prior PIRN study. 22 Shared decision‐making will be assessed using the validated collaboRATE measure around the UTI diagnosis and renal ultrasound testing. 23 This three‐item questionnaire captures parental perceptions of clinician effort to (i) explain the child's condition, (ii) elicit parental preferences, and (iii) integrate those preferences into care decisions.

Parent experiences and preferences of universal screening renal ultrasound

To explore parent's lived experiences, we will conduct 30–45 min, semistructured virtual interviews with parents within 4–8 weeks and 10–12 months after discharge. Participants will be purposefully sampled to ensure diverse perspectives based on gender, race, ethnicity, hospital site, and ultrasound findings. The interview guide, developed with input from a parent partner, the research team, and a literature review, will address experiences before, during, and after the ultrasound (e.g., understanding of rationale, decision‐making involvement, child experience, communication of results, perceived value, and process improvement). Interviews will be conducted by a trained research coordinator with qualitative interview experience, through a secure virtual platform, transcribed verbatim in English or French, and de‐identified before analysis. For non‐English or French‐speaking participants, interviews will be conducted with an interpreter in their preferred language, and the English components of the interviews will be transcribed verbatim.

Statistical analysis

The incidence of clinically important genitourinary abnormalities and of overdiagnosis on renal United States (primary outcome) will be estimated with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) using the Wilson‐score method. The incidence of overdiagnosis will be calculated as the number of children with class A and B abnormalities divided by the total number of children, while the incidence of clinically important abnormalities will be calculated as the number of children with class C abnormalities divided by the total number of children.

Subgroup analysis will estimate the incidence of clinically important abnormalities and overdiagnosis by sex, age (birth to <2 months, 2 months to <6 months, 6 months to 2 years), urine sample method of collection, and urine bacterial growth thresholds (10,000–100,000 CFU/mL vs. greater than 100,000 CFU/mL), each with 95% CI calculated using the Wilson‐score method. In addition, the proportion and 95% CI of each specific renal abnormality will be reported across the study population.

To describe the cascade of health interventions associated with overdiagnosis (secondary outcome), we will report results for all participants and stratify by: (i) overdiagnosis of genitourinary abnormalities (Class A or B), (ii) clinically important abnormalities (Class C), and (iii) no renal ultrasound abnormality. For each group, we will calculate the percentage and 95% CI (Wilson‐score method) for: surgery within 1 year of the index UTI; initiation of antibiotic prophylaxis for prevention of recurrent UTI; completion of secondary imaging investigations; identification of incidental non‐genitourinary imaging findings; and specialist follow‐up.

A multivariable logistic regression will examine the association between clinically important genitourinary abnormality and the following potential predictors: age, sex, prenatal ultrasound abnormal, type of infection (Escherichia coli vs. non‐E. coli), timing of ultrasound (days on antibiotics when ultrasound was performed), and circumcision (female = 0, male = 0 or 1). To account for clustering of patients within hospitals, we will calculate clustered standard errors using a sandwich estimate. 24 Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals will be reported. Two‐sided p‐values < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Qualitative analysis

For the qualitative component, data from parent interviews will be analyzed using thematic analysis following the six‐phase approach described by Nowell et al.: (1) familiarization with the data; (2) generation of initial codes; (3) searching for themes; (4) reviewing themes; (5) defining and naming themes; and (6) producing the final report. 25 An inductive analytic approach will be used. Transcripts will be read iteratively and coded to identify similarities and differences in content. Codes will be organized into a coding tree and analytical framework, which will be refined through ongoing comparison and discussion among members of the research team. Key themes and subthemes will be developed by integrating related codes and finalized by consensus.

Sample size

For the quantitative component, a sample size range of 300–600 participants was targeted based on anticipated feasibility at the 17 sites over a 24‐month period. With this sample size range of 300–600, we will be able to estimate the incidence of clinically important genitourinary abnormalities (expected at approximately 10%) with a margin of error of about ±2.4 to ±3.4%, and the incidence of overdiagnosis (expected at approximately 30%) with a margin of error of about ±3.7 to ±5.2%. The final sample size will be based on the observed overall recruitment rate, such that the sample size is within the target range. No outcome data will be examined in making this decision.

For the qualitative component, we will conduct 30 interviews (each 30–45 min, 1–2 caregiver participants from each site) at 4–6 weeks and 10–12 months after discharge from the hospital. The sample size is consistent with established qualitative research standards and is expected to achieve thematic saturation given the study objectives and context. 26

We anticipate minimal missing data ( < 5%) for the primary outcome, as renal ultrasound is performed in almost all children after the first febrile UTI in the hospital at the study sites. Secondary outcomes will be similarly ascertained through the electronic health records and supplemented by parent phone follow‐up. Participants with any missing primary outcome data will be excluded from the primary outcome analysis. We also anticipate minimal missing data for patient characteristics obtained from medical records and parents during index admission. Missing covariate data will be dealt with using multiple imputation by chained equation (MICE). 27

Integrated knowledge translation

This study follows an integrated knowledge translation approach, in which researchers and knowledge users, such as clinicians, parents, and policy partners, are engaged throughout the research process to ensure the study questions, methods, and dissemination strategies are relevant, useful, and readily translatable into practice. 28

Clinicians and policy partners

The ROUTINE study was vetted at the PIRN network, with feedback and refinement of the study concept by practicing hospitalists and general pediatricians, in addition to study concept presentations at participating hospitals. At the study concept stage, professional societies and organizations such as the Canadian Paediatric Society (authors of Canadian UTI guidelines) and Choosing Wisely Canada were engaged around the need for the study and provided letters of support for the grant funding applications.

Patient and public involvement

A parent partner with lived experience around young childhood hospitalization as a parent and partners with PIRN in research, will continue to provide input into the study design, outcome selection, protocol implementation, and knowledge dissemination from a parent perspective. In addition, the PIRN patient–engagement coordinator, a parent with lived experience around childhood hospitalization, will provide input in all phases of the study. Their engagement will ensure the research remains patient‐centred, feasible, and relevant to families’ experiences and priorities.

End of grant dissemination

Study findings will be disseminated through the PIRN network, collaborating institutions, and professional societies. The end of grant dissemination will be conducted in collaboration with Children's Healthcare Canada knowledge mobilization services. 29 The design of dissemination messages will be tailored to each audience, and dissemination approaches will be knowledge‐user driven. Dissemination strategies will include publication in peer‐reviewed, open‐access journals; presentations at local, national, and international conferences; and development of webinars, video abstracts, and infographics for social media. In collaboration with the parent partner, infographics and summary pamphlets tailored for families will be created to promote patient and public engagement. Finally, study results will be shared with the CPS to inform updates to clinical practice guidelines on imaging after a first febrile UTI.

Ethics statement

The study has received ethics approval from the Hospital for Sick Children's Research Ethics Board through the Clinical Trials Ontario Streamlined Research Ethics Review System for Ontario sites, and from individual institutional review boards at other participating hospitals across other provinces and the US site. Informed consent will be obtained from parents or caregivers before enrollment by research staff. This is a minimal‐risk study examining standard clinical care practices without altering clinical management. Accordingly, the Research Ethics Boards of 12 participating sites approved implementation of verbal consent using a one‐page information sheet.

DISCUSSION

This multicentre, pragmatic, prospective observational study with an embedded qualitative component aims to determine the clinical utility of universal renal ultrasound screening in children 0–24 months of age hospitalized with their first febrile UTI. The findings will inform evidence‐based management of UTIs in hospitalized children, support clinical practice guideline recommendations, and promote standardization of care with the overarching goal of improving outcomes after a first febrile UTI. This study builds on the existing literature through its multicentre design, the inclusion of both community and children's hospitals to enhance generalizability, the use of a standardized classification system for renal ultrasound findings, and longitudinal follow‐up for 1 year to capture downstream clinical, health services, and parent‐reported outcomes, while exploring parental experiences and preferences. Together, these elements will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the benefits of universal ultrasound screening in children 0–24 months of age hospitalized with their first febrile UTI and potential unintended consequences, such as overdiagnosis and unnecessary interventions.

We elected to first conduct this observational study to understand clinical utility, rather than conduct a randomized clinical trial comparing imaging strategies (e.g., universal vs. targeted imaging) for several reasons. First, renal ultrasound is a well‐established component of current clinical care in hospitalized children with UTI and is recommended in major guidelines, making randomization challenging without prior evidence of equipoise. Second, clinicians within the PIRN network expressed strong support for first generating observational evidence on outcome incidence and practice. These data will provide essential context for determining whether a trial is warranted and feasible. If a trial is deemed necessary and feasible based on this project, the results of this study will provide interim guidance for parents and clinicians in shared decision‐making by quantifying the likelihood of detecting clinically important abnormalities versus the likelihood of findings with limited clinical relevance. Moreover, this pragmatic observational approach facilitates clinician engagement and allows for assessment of trial feasibility in real‐world practice.

Notably, the number of hospital admissions for first febrile UTI in most of our study sites has been lower than initially estimated. This may be partly due to the publication of the updated AAP and CPS fever management guidelines, which recommend outpatient management for well‐appearing, low‐risk infants older than 4 weeks following emergency department assessment for fever. 30 , 31 Other studies have pointed to a trend to a lower incidence of UTI over time, which may be related to stricter diagnosis criteria (e.g., requirement for pyuria). 32 , 33 To mitigate the lower‐than‐expected number of UTI admissions and reach our target sample size, we have extended our recruitment period.

This study aims to generate high‐quality evidence on the diagnostic yield and downstream implications of universal renal ultrasound screening after a first febrile UTI in young children. The findings will help inform evidence‐based imaging strategies that balance the early detection of clinically important abnormalities with the need to minimize overdiagnosis, unnecessary interventions, and parental anxiety. By addressing existing evidence gaps and reflecting real‐world practice across diverse hospital settings, this study will provide data to guide both clinicians and policymakers towards more judicious and patient‐centered approaches to UTI imaging.
