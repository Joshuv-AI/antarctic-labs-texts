L-cysteine enhances heat-stress adaptation in meat-type chickens through modulation of cysteine-metabolic and nutrient transporter genes
Authors: Bikash Aryal, Oluwatomide W Ariyo, Josephine Kwakye, Selorm Sovi, Ahmed FA Ghareeb, Evan Hartono, Marie C Milfort, Alberta L Fuller, Romdhane Rekaya, Samuel E Aggrey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat stress (HS) is a major challenge in poultry production due to its negative effects on growth, metabolism, and redox balance. Excessive reactive oxygen species generated during HS contribute to oxidative damage, emphasizing the need for nutritional strategies that enhance antioxidant capacity. This study evaluated the effects of dietary L-cysteine supplementation on growth performance, carcass traits, hepatic redox-related gene expression, and ileal nutrient transporters in commercial broilers. A total of 1,080 Cobb500 chickens (d 28–35) were assigned to diets containing deficient (0%), control (0.3%), or excess (0.6%) L-cysteine and exposed to thermoneutral (TN; 25°C), pair-feeding (PF; 25°C), or cyclic HS (35°C for 12 h daily). As expected, HS adversely affected (P < 0.05) BWG, feed efficiency, and carcass yields; however, L-cysteine supplementation did not improve (P > 0.05) any performance variable under HS. Hepatic cystathionine-β-synthase (CBS) expression increased under cysteine-deficient conditions, indicating enhanced endogenous cysteine synthesis via the transsulfuration pathway. In contrast, expression of glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC), cysteine dioxygenase-1 (CDO1), and glutathione synthetase (GSS) did not differ (P > 0.05) among dietary cysteine levels, suggesting similar glutathione synthetic capacity across treatments. Ileal nutrient transporters, including SGLT1, GLUT5, FATP1, FABP2, PEPT1, and PEPT2, exhibited variable expression patterns in response to diet and temperature, reflecting differences in feed intake and intestinal physiological adaptation. Overall, dietary L-cysteine did not mitigate HS-induced declines in performance. Increased CBS expression under cysteine deficiency suggests robust endogenous regulation of cysteine supply, and further studies integrating protein abundance and enzyme activity are needed to elucidate L-cysteine’s biological effects under HS.Keywords: Heat stress, Chicken, Cysteine, Transsulfuration, Gene

Introduction

Heat stress (HS) has become a significant challenge in poultry farming, especially in tropical or subtropical climates affecting all types of poultry species, regardless of age or strain/breed. Under HS, production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) exceeds the natural antioxidant capabilities, resulting in oxidative damage to proteins, lipids, carbohydrates and nucleic acids (Aryal et al., 2025; Chauhan et al., 2014; Habashy et al., 2019). Since HS is fundamentally a management-related challenge rather than an infectious condition, there are no targeted therapeutic interventions comparable to those used for microbial diseases. Effective mitigation instead depends on implementing integrated, multi-component strategies that reduce the overall heat load experienced by poultry. These strategies may include various approaches such as genetic improvement for heat tolerance (Gowe and Fairfull, 2008; Lin et al., 2006), physical cooling methods (Renaudeau et al., 2012; Yahav and McMurtry, 2001), and nutritional manipulations (Ariyo et al., 2023; Attia et al., 2016; Kwakye et al., 2023; Saeed et al., 2017; Suganya et al., 2015). However, implementing improved housing and physical cooling measures can raise the fixed costs of production (Gous and Morris, 2005). On the other hand, genetic selection is a lengthy process. Alternatively, nutritional strategies and supplements can mitigate the impacts of HS in a more advantageous and convenient manner, without necessitating alterations to the fundamental structure of the diet (Ariyo et al., 2023; He et al., 2018; Kwakye et al., 2025).

L-Cysteine is a semi-essential amino acid that can be either produced from methionine through the transsulfuration pathway or obtained directly through diet. Because cysteine can be produced endogenously from the transsulfuration pathway (Aggrey et al., 2018; D'mello, 2003), cysteine is not considered an essential amino acid in poultry diets. Glutathione (GSH) is considered as the primary natural antioxidant in the cells, protecting them from oxidative stress (Meister, 1995). GSH consists of three amino acids namely cysteine, glutamine, and glycine. During oxidative stress, there is a greater requirement for GSH, and cysteine may become rate-limiting in producing sufficient GSH levels (Bannai and Tateishi, 1986; Ishii et al., 1987). Similarly, Habashy et al. (2017b) found that cysteine was incorporated into tissues more than any other amino acid in chickens under HS. This increased incorporation of cysteine into tissue protein may reduce the pool of free cysteine, thereby limiting the capacity to meet the increased need for GSH synthesis under HS. Therefore, increasing the availability of cysteine has become a primary focus to meet the high demand for reduced GSH during oxidative stress (Kerksick and Willoughby, 2005). Determining whether cells preferentially utilize cysteine derived from dietary supplementation or that generated endogenously from methionine is essential for understanding how they support the biosynthesis of glutathione, sulfide species, pyruvate, and taurine. In vitro studies using rat liver cells have shown that both sources of cysteine are utilized equally in the synthesis of these metabolites, suggesting that methionine is not a superior substrate to preformed cysteine for hepatic GSH synthesis (Stipanuk et al., 1992). The processes governing cysteine digestion, absorption, and metabolic transformation can modulate the expression of multiple nutrient transporters through diverse regulatory mechanisms. The role of GSH in neutralizing ROS aids in the restoration of intestinal integrity (Hou et al., 2012; Koo et al., 2023), pyruvate production may affect energy metabolism (Gray et al., 2014), while sulfide production can have both positive and negative effects on intestinal epithelial cells (Buret et al., 2022; Li et al., 2016).

Multiple studies have investigated the protective roles of various cysteine isoforms, such as N-acetylcysteine (Cao et al., 2023; Pertiwi et al., 2023; Yi et al., 2016) and S-allyl-cysteine (Wang et al., 2021) in alleviating HS-related dysfunction. However, no research to date has specifically evaluated whether dietary L-cysteine supplementation can mitigate the adverse effects of chronic HS in commercial broilers. In the present study, we examined three dietary inclusion levels of L-cysteine in finisher stage broiler chickens exposed to cyclical HS, assessing both phenotypic responses and molecular adaptations. The finisher stage of broiler chicken is characterized by rapid growth and high metabolic activity, making this stage more susceptible to oxidative stress that may render cysteine availability limiting for GSH synthesis. Elucidating the effects of dietary L-cysteine supplementation on central metabolic networks, including the transsulfuration pathway, glutathione biosynthesis, and the regulatory mechanisms governing nutrient transporter activity is critical for assessing its viability as a targeted nutritional intervention in poultry production.

Materials and methods

Animals

All experiments in this study were implemented under the Animal Use Proposal number A2015 04-005 approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Georgia. A total of 1,080 Cobb500 broiler chickens were used for this study and raised on fresh pine shavings (0.074 m2/bird) from d 1 until 35 d of age. Birds received crumbled starter (22% C.P, 12.45 MJ/kg M.E.), pelletized grower (20% C.P, 12.97 MJ/kg M.E.) and finisher diet (19.12% C.P, 13.38 MJ/kg M.E.) from d 0–14, d 15–28 and d 28–35, respectively. Lighting and relative humidity management practices were in accordance with Cobb500 standards (Cobb, 2021). Until d 28, birds had ad libitum supply of feed and water, temperature was set in accordance with Cobb500 standards. The chickens’ health and behavior were monitored twice daily.

Experimental design

Day-old chicks were weighted and allocated into pens to maintain a similar initial body weights across pens. At d 28, pens were randomly divided into three dietary treatments related to cysteine supplementation, categorized as cysteine deficient (D), cysteine control (C), and cysteine excess (E). The digestible cysteine levels in the finisher diet were 5.6 g/kg, 8.6 g/kg, and 11.6 g/kg for the deficient, control, and excess treatment groups, respectively (Table 1). Chickens in each dietary treatment were raised at either thermoneutral (TN) condition (25°C) or cyclic-heat stress (HS) condition (35°C from 7:00 AM to 7:00 PM and 25°C from 7.00 PM until 7.00 AM). Additionally, to understand the effect of HS independent of the reduction in feed intake (FI), birds under TN condition were subdivided into pair-fed (PF) groups. Pair feeding involves restricting the FI of the TN group to match the reduced appetite observed in the HS group. Thus, the amount of feed provided to the PF group was adjusted to match the quantity consumed by their counterparts raised under HS condition. Therefore, there were total nine treatment groups in a 3 (Temperature) x 3 (Diet) design.

Pens served as the experimental unit, and since initial body weight was used as a blocking factor to minimize variation among experimental units, this study was conducted as a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with factorial treatments. There were six replicates per treatment and 20 birds per replicate within each dietary and temperature combination. Given that the most pronounced impact of HS in broiler chickens manifest as reductions in body weight, the experimental period was focused on the finisher phase (d 28–35), when growth rate and nutrient demand are highest. In this study, the finisher diet was formulated to provide 95% of the established standardized ileal digestible (SID) methionine requirement, a strategy intended to favor the remethylation of homocysteine and thereby limit its flux through the transsulfuration pathway (Aggrey et al., 2018; Vilar da Silva et al., 2020). This dietary design enabled a clearer assessment of how varying levels of supplemental cysteine influence transsulfuration-related metabolism under HS conditions.

Performance parameters

On d 28, prior to initiating HS exposure, all birds within each replicate (pen) were weighed together. Mortality was monitored and recorded throughout the experimental period. Body weight gain (BWG) was calculated as the difference between the initial body weight on d 28 and the final body weight on d 35. FI was determined by weighing feed offered and feed leftovers at the beginning and end of the study. Water intake (WI) was quantified using the same approach, based on the difference between water provided and water remaining. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was computed as total FI divided by BWG, whereas water conversion ratio (WCR) was calculated as the ratio of WI to BWG from d 28 to d 35, following the methodology described by Aggrey et al. (2023).

Carcass evaluation and sample collection

On d 35 (7 d post-HS), birds from each replicate were randomly selected and humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation. Three birds per replicate were randomly selected for processing at the University of Georgia Processing Plant (Athens, GA, United States) to evaluate carcass characteristics. Birds were processed using a commercial inline system, during which they were electrically stunned (11 V, 11 mA for 8 s), slaughtered, and defeathered. Carcasses were subsequently eviscerated, manually rinsed, and chilled at 4 °C for 24 h. Following chilling, the carcass weight (CW) was recorded, along with the weights of Pectoralis (P.) major, P. minor, thigh, drumstick, wings, and abdominal fat. Carcass yield (CY) was calculated as a percentage of live body weight, whereas Pectoralis major yield (PMY), Pectoralis minor yield (PMiY), thigh yield (TY), drumstick yield (DY), wing yield (WY), and abdominal fat yield (AbFY) were calculated as a percentage of CW. For statistical analysis, measurements from the three birds were averaged to obtain a single value per replicate (pen). In addition, for RNA extraction and downstream gene expression analysis, one bird per replicate was randomly selected and freshly culled for tissue collection. Liver and ileal samples were immediately excised, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen (−196 °C), and stored at −80 °C until subsequent analyses.

RNA extraction

The TRIzol-chloroform method was used to extract the RNA from liver and ileal tissue samples. Briefly, 100 mg of frozen ileal tissue was homogenized with 1 mL of TRIzol using the Benchmark BeadBlaster Microtube Homogenizer® (OVERSTOCK Lab Equipment, NH, USA). This was followed by phase separation by adding 200 μL of chloroform. The mixture was vortexed, incubated at room temperature for 3 min, and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 4°C, for 15 min). Subsequently, 550 μL of the aqueous phase was transferred into a tube containing 0.5 mL of isopropanol, vortexed, and incubated at room temperature for 10 min to precipitate the RNA. The tubes were then centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 4°C, for 10 min), the supernatant was discarded, and the resulting pellet was washed with 1 mL of 75% ethanol by vortexing, followed by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 4°C, for 5 min). After removing the supernatant, the tube containing the RNA pellet was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The pellet was then dissolved in 100 μL of nuclease-free water and incubated at 55-60°C for 10 min before being stored at −80°C. Finally, the RNA was purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. Clean RNA was diluted in nuclease-free water, and concentration was measured and adjusted to 200 ng/μL using a NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE). This purified RNA was stored at −80°C until cDNA synthesis.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

10 μL of clean diluted RNA (200 ng/μL) was used for reverse transcription (RT) with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, CA), based on manufacturer's protocol. A gradient thermocycler (Eppendorf, NY) used for cDNA synthesis was adjusted for repeated cycles: 10 min at 25°C, 120 min at 37°C, 5 min at 85°C, and the final cycle at 4°C. The concentration of cDNA was measured, and the samples were adjusted to 20 ng/μL using NanoDropTM 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). The cDNA was stored at −20°C until further processing. The real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using the StepOnePlusTM Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, CA). Each qPCR reaction consisted of a total volume of 20 μL; made up of 2 μL cDNA (20 ng/μL), 0.6 μL forward and reverse primer each (10 μM), 6.8 μL nuclease-free water, and 10 μL FAST SYBRTM Green Master Mix® (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA). All samples were replicated three times on the same plate. The thermocycling conditions used for qPCR were as follows: UDG (Uracil-DNA Glycosylase) incubation step at 50°C for 120 sec to prevent carryover contamination, followed by AmpliTaq® Fast DNA Polymerase activation and initial denaturation at 95°C for 120 sec. This was followed by 40 amplification cycles consisting of denaturation at 95°C for 15 sec and annealing/extension at 60°C for 60 sec, as described previously (Ghareeb et al., 2022). Fluorescence data (Ct values) were collected at the end of each cycle, and a melt curve analysis was performed at the end of the run to confirm amplification specificity. The Ct values for genes of interest were normalized against the Ct values of the β-actin gene (reference gene), and the fold increase was calculated versus the control group (TNC). The relative mRNA expression of different genes in the transsulfuration pathway and cysteine metabolism were measured. These genes include cystathionine-beta-synthase (CBS), cystathionine-gamma-lyase (CGL), glutamate cysteine ligase-catalytic subunit (GCLC), cysteine dioxygenase-1 (CDO1), and glutathione synthetase (GSS). We also measured the relative mRNA expression of different nutrient transporters in ileal samples, including sugar transporters sodium/glucose cotransporter 1 (SGLT1) and glucose transporter 5 (GLUT5); the fatty acid transporters, including fatty acid transport protein 1 (FATP1) and fatty acid binding protein 2 (FABP2); and the peptide transporters which include peptide transporter 1 (PEPT1; SLC15A1) and peptide transporter 2 (PEPT2; SLC15A2). The qPCR data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). The detail of primer pairs used in this study are summarized in Table 2.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed at the pen-level (mean value per pen) in the SAS OnDemand for Academics (SAS Institute Inc, 2024), using a two-way ANOVA model. Outliers were detected using the Grubb’s test via the GraphPad online calculator (https://www.graphpad.com/quickcalcs/grubbs1/), and normality of the data was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, with p > 0.05 indicating a normal distribution. The statistical model used for data analysis is mentioned below.

where μ is the overall mean of the response, αi is the fixed effect of temperature, where i= thermoneutral (TN) or pair-fed (PF) or heat stress (HS); βj is the fixed effect of different cysteine levels (diet), where j = deficient (D) or control (C) or excess (E); (αβ)ij represents the interaction effect of temperature and diet; and the ɛijk is the random error. Differences among treatments were tested using the “Tukey’s HSD” option. The significant differences between treatment groups were declared at P < 0.05.

Only the main effect of temperature significantly affected performance parameters. Exposure to HS significantly reduced (P < 0.05) FI and BWG, while concurrently increasing (P < 0.05) FCR and WCR compared with birds maintained under TN conditions. WI, however, did not differ (P > 0.05) among the TN, pair-fed (PF), and HS groups. Carcass characteristics

Table 4 summarizes the carcass characteristics of broilers exposed to varying thermal and dietary treatments. A significant diet x temperature interaction (P < 0.05) was observed for CW and TY. Among the treatment combinations, birds fed the deficient cysteine diet under thermoneutral conditions (TND) exhibited the highest CW (1714 g), whereas those receiving the excess cysteine diet under heat stress (HSD) showed the lowest CW (1379.27 g). For TY, the pair-fed group receiving excess cysteine (PFE) demonstrated the highest percentage (18.07%), while birds provided the excess cysteine diet under thermoneutral conditions (TNE) exhibited the lowest TY (15.73%). No other carcass parameters were significantly (P > 0.05) affected by the interaction between temperature and dietary cysteine. Regarding main effects, both HS and PF birds had significantly higher WY (P < 0.05) compared with TN birds. CY was also significantly increased in the PF group (72.6%) compared to birds in the TN (69.98%) and HS (70.64%) groups. Other carcass traits, including AbFY, PMY, PMiY, and DY, did not differ among TN, PF, and HS treatments (P > 0.05). Additionally, dietary cysteine level had no significant (P > 0.05) main effect on any carcass characteristic measured.

Transsulfuration pathway and cysteine metabolism

The relative mRNA expression levels of CBS and CGL are presented in Fig. 1A and B, respectively. For both genes, a significant temperature × diet interaction was detected (P < 0.05). Within each thermal environment (TN, PF, or HS), birds receiving the cysteine-deficient diet (D) exhibited the highest (P < 0.05) CBS expression compared with those fed the control diet. Moreover, relative to all TN groups (TNC, TND, TNE), CBS expression was consistently upregulated in all PF groups (PFC, PFD, PFE), whereas within the HS treatments, only the HSD group showed increased CBS expression compared with the TN groups. In contrast, expression of the CGL gene did not differ significantly (P > 0.05) among cysteine levels within each temperature condition (TN, PF, HS). However, birds in the PFD group exhibited consistently higher CGL expression than all TN (TNC, TND, TNE) and HS (HSC, HSD, HSE) groups. A significant temperature × diet interaction (P < 0.05) was also observed for the expression of GCLC (Fig. 2A) and GSS (Fig. 2B). Expression of GCLC did not differ (P > 0.05) among cysteine levels within TN, PF, or HS conditions. Nevertheless, GCLC expression was consistently upregulated in the PFC and PFE groups relative to all TN groups. Likewise, all HS groups (HSC, HSD, HSE) exhibited significantly higher GCLC expression (P < 0.05) compared with the TN groups. For GSS, mRNA expression was significantly higher (P < 0.05) in birds fed with cysteine control diet under heat stress (HSC) than in all other treatment groups. However, within TN (TNC, TND, TNE) and PF (PFC, PFD, PFE) conditions, GSS expression did not differ significantly (P > 0.05) among cysteine dietary levels. Temperature × diet interaction for the expression of CDO1 was not significant (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3A). Only the main effect of temperature was significant (Fig. 3B), with HS and PF groups exhibiting higher (P < 0.05) CDO1 expression compared with the TN group. Discussion

Identifying nutritional strategies that bolster resilience to environmental stressors is increasingly important for modern poultry systems. HS remains a primary constraint on productivity and welfare, necessitating interventions that enhance redox capacity and sustain nutrient utilization. In this study, we evaluated whether dietary L-cysteine modulates production outcomes and the transcriptional regulation of redox-associated pathways and nutrient transporters in broilers subjected to TN, PF, or HS conditions. Our findings indicate that while L-cysteine altered the expression of selected genes, it did not translate into measurable improvements in performance or carcass traits under HS. Instead, the dominant drivers of physiological and molecular responses were FI and heat load, with PF serving as an important intake-matched comparator.

Performance responses

Consistent with prior literature, HS birds exhibited reduced BWG and FI, together with increased FCR and WCR compared with TN birds (Ariyo et al., 2023; Ghareeb et al., 2022; Habashy et al., 2017a). The BWG depression is plausibly explained by both lower FI and impaired feed efficiency, as birds divert energy to dissipate excess heat, thereby constraining energy available for protein accretion (Papanikolaou et al., 2025). Recent evidence also points to HS-associated downregulation of mTOR, energy-sensing, and autophagy pathways that collectively suppress protein synthesis and growth (Kwakye et al., 2025). Additional mechanisms include compromised gut integrity and mucosal morphology that limit nutrient absorption (Gabler et al., 2018; Tajima et al., 2007), as well as suppressed digestive enzyme activities (Al Wakeel et al., 2017).

Interestingly, WI did not differ between TN and HS in our study; thus, higher WCR under HS largely reflects lower BWG rather than increased water use. Importantly, no performance variable differed among cysteine dietary levels. This deviates from reports of improved performance with N-acetyl-cysteine (NAC) in HS birds (Omid et al., 2018; Pertiwi et al., 2023; Yi et al., 2016). Biochemical differences between NAC and L-cysteine likely underpin this discrepancy: NAC is relatively resistant to oxidation in the lumen, whereas L-cysteine is rapidly oxidized to cystine, whose uptake via cystine/glutamate exchange can deplete intracellular glutamate (Yildiz et al., 2009). Although L-cysteine’s bitter taste has been implicated in feed aversion (Kawai et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2013), we observed no FI differences among cysteine diets, suggesting limited palatability impact under our conditions.

Carcass characteristics

CW tended to be lower in both PF and HS groups compared with TN, consistent with reduced live body weight arising from lower FI (and poorer feed efficiency in HS). CY (% live BW) was higher in PF than in TN and HS, potentially due to reduced visceral mass (e.g., liver, heart, gastrointestinal tract) and/or lower gut fill at slaughter, a recognized consequence of restriction regimens where birds quickly consume ration and experience longer inter-meal intervals (Zeferino et al., 2016). Notably, this contrasts with Teyssier et al. (2022), who reported the lowest CY in PF birds; differences in restriction severity, strain, age, and environmental context may explain the divergence.

TY was higher in PF cysteine-excess (PFE) than in TN cysteine-excess (TNE), with no difference versus HSE, suggesting that under feed restriction, excess cysteine may be preferentially partitioned toward thigh protein accretion, whereas under ad libitum feeding it may be diverted toward oxidative or non-protein pathways. WY was higher in PF and HS versus TN, consistent with the notion that limited nutrients favor allocation to locomotory musculature, and that HS-induced peripheral vasodilation to wings and limbs for heat dissipation may enhance nutrient delivery to wing muscles (Emami et al., 2021). In contrast to several reports documenting shifts in PMY, PMiY, DY, and TY under prolonged HS (Hosseini-Vashan et al., 2020; Jankowski et al., 2015; Rosa et al., 2007; Temim et al., 2000), we detected no significant differences among TN, PF, and HS for these traits, again underscoring the variable interaction of strain, age, stress duration, and diet across studies. Overall, cysteine level exerted no main effect on carcass parameters, suggesting limited impact of L-cysteine (within tested ranges) on carcass composition across environments.

Regulation of cysteine metabolism and redox-related genes

CBS expression was highest in cysteine-deficient birds across thermal conditions, consistent with a compensatory activation of the transsulfuration pathway to maintain intracellular cysteine for GSH synthesis (Vilar da Silva et al., 2020). Although dietary methionine was set at 95% of requirement to bias homocysteine toward remethylation and isolate cysteine effects (Vilar da Silva et al., 2019), CBS upregulation in the cysteine-deficient group indicates a cysteine-specific response that overrides methionine-mediated suppression. In contrast, CGL expression did not differ by cysteine level within TN, PF, or HS, suggesting CBS is more transcriptionally responsive to cysteine supply than CGL in broilers.

Across cysteine diets, CBS expression was higher in PF and HS versus TN, implicating reduced FI and associated hormonal shifts (lower insulin-to-glucagon ratio) in its regulation (Unger, 1971), corroborated by HS-associated hyperglycemia (Ariyo et al., 2024; Garriga et al., 2006). Prior studies show insulin inhibits transsulfuration and elevates homocysteine (Chiang et al., 2009; Jacobs et al., 1998), whereas glucagon enhances homocysteine clearance via transsulfuration (Jacobs et al., 2001). The greater CBS expression in PF than HS likely reflects longer inter-meal fasting in PF birds (restricted ration consumed rapidly), versus ad libitum but reduced intake in HS.

Mammalian work describes reciprocal regulation of CDO (up with higher protein/sulfur amino acids) and GCL (down) (Bagley and Stipanuk, 1994, 1995; Bella and Stipanuk, 1996). In our study, GCLC and CDO1 expression did not differ by cysteine level under TN, PF, or HS, but both were elevated in PF and HS relative to TN. Given that GCLC encodes the rate-limiting enzyme for GSH synthesis, its upregulation under PF and HS points to increased antioxidant demand under energy restriction and heat-induced oxidative stress, consistent with activation of Nrf2-dependent responses (Lettieri-Barbato et al., 2020; Miao et al., 2020). The parallel increase in CDO1 despite its usual upregulation with abundant cysteine, suggests that HS- and PF-driven reductions in FI may override substrate-driven reciprocity, promoting transcription of both pathways. To our knowledge, HS- or FI-induced CDO1 upregulation has not been documented previously in chickens.

GSS displayed HS-specific, U-shaped regulation by dietary cysteine: lower expression in cysteine-deficient and excess groups compared with control under HS, but not under TN or PF. This suggests optimal GSS expression at moderate cysteine during HS, independent of energy status. The broader pattern, higher CBS in deficiency; no consistent cysteine effect on GCLC or CDO1; HS-specific modulation of GSS supports homeostatic maintenance of intracellular cysteine and a robust GSH synthetic capacity across cysteine supplies. Where excess cysteine was not reflected in GCLC/CDO1 transcription, it may have been inefficiently absorbed or diverted into cysteine desulfuration via CBS/CGL, generating sulfide species (Sbodio et al., 2019). Collectively, these results support the interpretation that GSH synthesis proceeded similarly across cysteine treatments, aligning with the absence of dietary effects on performance and carcass traits. Nevertheless, because HS elevates oxidative load, protein abundance and enzyme activity (not measured here) may still differ among diets and should be quantified in future work.

Expression of nutrient transporters

SGLT1 (apical Na⁺-glucose/galactose cotransporter) showed a temperature × diet interaction, with no cysteine effect under TN (Wright et al., 2018). Under PF, SGLT1 was higher in cysteine-deficient and excess groups than control, consistent with negative energy balance (reduced FI) and higher glucagon favoring greater intestinal glucose absorption. Conversely, under HS, HSE showed lower SGLT1 than HSC/HSD. One plausible mechanism is microbial H₂S generated from excess cysteine (Blachier et al., 2019), which can stimulate GLP-1 release (Pichette et al., 2017), increasing insulin secretion (Creutzfeldt, 1979; Harada and Inagaki, 2012), and thereby suppressing SGLT1 to limit glucose uptake. Consistent with this, PFE exhibited higher SGLT1 than all TN groups, reflecting the combined effects of restriction and sulfur amino acid supply.

GLUT5 (SLC2A5), the apical fructose transporter (Uldry and Thorens, 2004), showed no interaction, but a significant temperature effect: PF > HS > TN. This pattern mirrors earlier reports in HS broilers (Ebrahimi et al., 2015; Ghareeb et al., 2022; Habashy et al., 2017b) and likely reflects adaptation to reduced FI, increasing fructose uptake as an alternative energy source. The absence of dietary cysteine effects suggests limited direct regulation of ileal GLUT5 by cysteine.

FATP1 (apical long-chain fatty acid transporter) and FABP2 (cytosolic long-chain fatty acid chaperone) exhibited temperature × diet interactions. Under TN, both were higher in cysteine-deficient than control or excess groups, indicating enhanced lipid uptake/trafficking when cysteine is limited, potentially supporting compensatory energy metabolism (Dutta-Roy, 2000; Gajda, 2015). Under PF and HS, cysteine-specific differences disappeared, consistent with stress-induced metabolic reprogramming overriding dietary regulation. Across temperature treatments, FATP1 increased in PF and HS versus TN (except TND), aligning with reports that insulin downregulates FATP1 (Hui et al., 1998) and that HS elevates its expression (Habashy et al., 2017b; Zhou et al., 2018). A similar intake-/insulin-related mechanism may explain FABP2 upregulation in PF and HS relative to TN (except TND).

PEPT1 (SLC15A1), a high-capacity, low-affinity di/tripeptide transporter, showed higher expression in cysteine-deficient birds across TN, PF, and HS consistent with a compensatory increase in peptide uptake under amino acid limitation (Chen et al., 2002). Although PEPT1 has been reported to decline on low crude protein diets and increase on high-protein diets (Chen et al., 2005), our results highlight the specific role of cysteine availability and FI in shaping PEPT1 expression. Reduced PEPT1 in cysteine-excess groups under TN and HS may reflect detrimental effects of excess luminal H₂S on mucosa. All PF groups exhibited elevated PEPT1 compared with TNC/TNE, in line with enhanced transporter expression during restricted feeding to scavenge luminal nutrients (Madsen and Wong, 2011).

PEPT2 (SLC15A2), a low-capacity, high-affinity transporter (Boll et al., 1996; Terada et al., 2000), displayed a distinct pattern: it decreased in cysteine-deficient groups under PF and HS relative to control/excess, suggesting coordinated affinity–capacity balancing between PEPT1 and PEPT2 under cysteine limitation. Like PEPT1, PEPT2 was higher in PF than TN/HS, consistent with compensatory uptake during restriction.

Limitations and future directions

We focused on mRNA abundance without measuring protein levels or enzyme activities; thus, post-transcriptional regulation remains unresolved. Ion transporter activity (e.g., SGLT1), GSH redox status (GSH/GSSG, redox potential), and key metabolic hormones such as insulin were not quantified in this study. Additionally, microbial H₂S was inferred mechanistically but not measured. Future work should integrate transcriptomics–proteomics–enzymology, direct redox biochemistry, hormonal profiling (insulin, glucagon, incretins), and gut microbial/metabolite analyses to test the proposed mechanisms, including FI-mediated and H₂S-mediated regulation of transporters and sulfur amino acid metabolism.

Conclusions and implications

Dietary L-cysteine did not improve growth performance or carcass traits under HS, despite discrete transcriptional effects. The data indicates strong homeostatic control of intracellular cysteine and GSH synthesis across cysteine supplies supported by elevated CBS in deficiency and largely unchanged GCLC/CDO1 across cysteine levels—while PF and HS broadly upregulated antioxidant and sulfur metabolism genes (GCLC, CDO1), likely in response to energy restriction and oxidative stress. Nutrient transporter responses were shaped primarily by FI (PF and HS) and, under cysteine excess, plausibly by H₂S-incretin-insulin signaling, rather than by cysteine per se.

From a practical perspective, these results suggest that L-cysteine alone at the levels tested is unlikely to rescue HS-induced performance losses. More promising strategies may include combinations that stabilize FI, support redox buffering (e.g., NAC or other thiol donors less prone to luminal oxidation), and modulate the gut microbial sulfur metabolome. Rigorous multi-omic and physiological phenotyping will be essential to delineate the most effective nutritional programs for HS resilience in commercial broilers.
