Advancing Problem-Based Learning in Biomedical Engineering in the Era of Generative AI
Micky C. Nnamdi, J. Ben Tamo, Wenqi Shi, May D. Wang

Abstract
Problem-Based Learning (PBL) has significantly impacted biomedical engineering (BME) education since its introduction in the early 2000s, effectively enhancing critical thinking and real-world knowledge application among students. With biomedical engineering rapidly converging with artificial intelligence (AI), integrating effective AI education into established curricula has become challenging yet increasingly necessary. Recent advancements, including AI’s recognition by the 2024 Nobel Prize, have highlighted the importance of training students comprehensively in biomedical AI. However, effective biomedical AI education faces substantial obstacles, such as diverse student backgrounds, limited personalized mentoring, constrained computational resources, and difficulties in safely scaling hands-on practical experiments due to privacy and ethical concerns associated with biomedical data. To overcome these issues, we conducted a three-year (2021–2023) case study implementing an advanced PBL framework tailored specifically for biomedical AI education, involving 92 undergraduate and 156 graduate students from the joint Biomedical Engineering program of Georgia Institute of Technology and Emory University. Our approach emphasizes collaborative, interdisciplinary problem-solving through authentic biomedical AI challenges. The implementation led to measurable improvements in learning outcomes, evidenced by high research productivity (16 student-authored publications), consistently positive peer evaluations, and successful development of innovative computational methods addressing real biomedical challenges. Additionally, we examined the role of generative AI both as a teaching subject and an educational support tool within the PBL framework. Our study presents a practical and scalable roadmap for biomedical engineering departments aiming to integrate robust AI education into their curricula. This strategy not only prepares students effectively for future healthcare innovation but also systematically addresses educational disparities and resource limitations encountered in conventional learning approaches.

Despite these benefits, traditional PBL approaches face critical limitations, including scalability challenges, difficulties providing personalized learning, and resource inefficiencies. Concurrently, artificial intelligence (AI) has experienced a rapid resurgence in recent years, exemplified by its recognition through the 2024 Nobel Prize, highlighting unprecedented advancements in scalability, adaptability, and broad applicability across diverse domains

Specifically, advanced AI technologies, such as large language models (LLMs), have shown significant potential in biomedical research and education by addressing predictive modeling, clinical decision support, experimental design, and automated literature analysis. Yet, despite parallel advances in both biomedical education and AI technology, there remains a significant gap in effectively integrating AI education within biomedical engineering curricula. Current educational practices primarily fall into two insufficient categories: traditional biomedical programs offering superficial AI electives, and computer science programs lacking meaningful biomedical contexts

In response to this gap, our study presents a systematic implementation of problem-based learning specifically tailored for teaching advanced AI concepts in biomedical engineering. Unlike previous educational efforts that treated biomedicine merely as an application area for AI, our approach embeds biomedical contexts as the fundamental framework for introducing and mastering AI principles. We detail a three-year case study (2021–2023) conducted at Georgia Tech, demonstrating how carefully designed PBL modules enable students to simultaneously acquire technical AI proficiency and navigate healthcare-specific considerations, including ethical implications, regulatory frameworks, and clinical integration complexities.

Our approach thus addresses critical educational needs in biomedical AI, offering scalable solutions adaptable to diverse student backgrounds and variable institutional resources. Furthermore, recognizing the transformative potential and associated risks of generative AI technologies, we comprehensively analyze their role both as educational content and as tools to enhance learning experiences. Specifically, we discuss the benefits and challenges of integrating LLMs with PBL, providing practical insights and mitigation strategies from an educator’s perspective.

II
Related Works

The intersection of artificial intelligence and biomedical education has markedly evolved over the past decade. This section reviews two central topics: the development of biomedical AI education programs and the emerging role of generative AI within biomedical education.

II-A

Biomedical AI Education

Educational programs in biomedical AI have experienced considerable changes due to the widespread adoption of AI technologies across healthcare. Historically, traditional biomedical programs primarily emphasized fundamental medical knowledge and clinical skills, often treating computational methods as secondary or supplementary tools
[
17
,
18
]
. However, the accelerated growth and broader integration of AI applications into healthcare have necessitated significant curriculum adaptations.

Medical schools and biomedical engineering departments have progressively responded by creating specialized curricula that systematically integrate clinical knowledge with foundational AI competencies (Tables
I
and
II
). For example, Stanford Medical School developed a specialized biomedical AI track, explicitly designed to train students with dual expertise in clinical medicine and machine learning methodologies
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
. Such initiatives illustrate the transition toward more cohesive and rigorous approaches to biomedical AI education.

Current educational practices in biomedical AI generally follow one of three distinct frameworks:

•

Integration Model:
AI concepts and computational tools are systematically embedded within existing biomedical curricula. This approach introduces AI within the context of relevant clinical and biomedical scenarios, ensuring students understand AI applications directly in clinical practice
[
14
]
.

•

Specialization Model:
Dedicated academic tracks or specialized concentrations focus explicitly on biomedical applications of AI. Typically, this model demands additional, intensive coursework in computer science, machine learning, data analytics, and their biomedical applications, thus preparing students for specialized careers at the intersection of medicine and AI
[
22
]
.

•

Hybrid Model:
Combining elements of integration and specialization approaches, hybrid models offer core biomedical curricula alongside optional advanced AI coursework and research opportunities. It provides flexibility to accommodate diverse student backgrounds and interests, enabling tailored education pathways
[
23
]
.

In this study, we conceptualize our PBL as a form of the hybrid model, evaluating its effectiveness in biomedical AI education through rigorous empirical assessment. By clearly distinguishing these educational frameworks, our study positions PBL within the broader landscape of biomedical AI education, elucidating its potential to effectively prepare students for real-world interdisciplinary challenges.

II-B

Biomedical Education in the Generative AI Era

The emergence of generative AI, particularly LLMs and advanced human-computer interfaces such as virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR), is reshaping biomedical education. These technologies facilitate personalized instruction, enhanced simulation environments, and improved educational assessments.

Generative AI tools have demonstrated considerable educational benefits. First, they enable personalized instruction by dynamically adapting educational content to students’ individual learning preferences and backgrounds, thus enhancing engagement and comprehension
[
24
]
. Second, AI-driven virtual patient simulations provide diverse clinical scenarios, surpassing traditional limitations in case-based teaching and allowing students to develop sophisticated diagnostic and decision-making skills
[
25
]
. Third, AI-powered assessment systems deliver immediate, targeted feedback on student performance, effectively guiding personalized improvements throughout the learning process.

However, integrating generative AI into biomedical education presents significant challenges. Chief among these are issues of accuracy and reliability—AI systems may generate plausible yet incorrect information, necessitating rigorous validation processes
[
26
]
. Furthermore, institutions face ethical challenges in maintaining academic integrity, clearly defining appropriate AI use, and fostering responsible practices. To address these challenges, educational programs must develop students’ AI literacy, enabling critical evaluation of AI systems, their capabilities, limitations, and ethical implications.

Overall, successfully incorporating generative AI into biomedical education requires balancing personalized, scalable educational delivery with rigorous standards for accuracy, validation, critical thinking, and ethical responsibility. Our case study contributes to this discourse by inspiring effective solution to integrate generative AI technologies into a structured problem-based learning framework.

III-A

Background: Problem-Based Learning

PBL is a student-centered educational strategy in which learners utilize authentic problem scenarios (“triggers”) to define learning objectives, investigate knowledge gaps, and collaboratively develop solutions
[
27
,
28
]
. Originating from medical education, PBL emphasizes not only the final solutions but also the learning process itself, mirroring healthcare’s shift toward outcome-based assessment
[
4
,
29
]
.

PBL typically involves small student groups working closely together, enhancing communication, teamwork, problem-solving capabilities, independent learning skills, and mutual respect
[
29
]
. By engaging with real-world clinical challenges, students directly connect theoretical knowledge to practical clinical applications, preparing them effectively for interdisciplinary biomedical careers
[
30
,
31
]
(Figure

). Prior work, including Medford et al.’s pilot study at Georgia Tech, demonstrated that vertically integrated PBL teams, combining undergraduate, graduate, and industry participants, successfully addressed complex technical problems
[
32
]
.

However, despite these successes, traditional PBL faces significant scalability challenges, particularly in technical fields like biomedical engineering. Typically, such constraints limit PBL activities to theoretical planning or preliminary computational prototypes rather than complete, functional implementations
[
33
]
.

III-B

Case Study: PBL for Biomedical AI Education

To address these scalability and complexity challenges, we conducted a three-year case study (2021–2023) at Georgia Tech, systematically implementing an enhanced PBL model for biomedical AI education. The study involved 248 students across three courses: Biomedical Health Informatics (BHI) and Medical Image Processing (MIP) at the graduate level, and Biostatistics (BioStats) at the undergraduate level. Specifically, the study engaged 156 graduate students (62 BHI and 94 MIP) and 92 undergraduate students (BioStats), organized into a total of 62 interdisciplinary teams (41 graduate teams, 21 undergraduate teams).

Teams addressed real-world biomedical AI problems that required integrating multidisciplinary knowledge, innovative computational techniques, and practical implementation strategies. This structure intentionally reflected professional research environments typical in biomedical AI academia and industry

III-C

Discussion: Impact of PBL in Biomedical AI Education

Our case study extends prior evidence supporting the effectiveness of PBL in medical education
[
4
,
29
]
, demonstrating its applicability and value in technically demanding biomedical AI contexts. The observed outcomes align with broader evidence indicating that students trained using PBL perform better in real-world problem-solving scenarios compared to traditional lecture-based methods
[
59
,
60
]
.

This work provides two significant contributions to the current literature. First, it successfully adapted the PBL methodology for complex biomedical AI education, shifting beyond theoretical exercises toward fully realized computational implementations. Second, it addressed inherent scalability limitations of PBL by integrating advanced generative AI technologies, such as LLMs and immersive VR/AR interfaces, facilitating personalized and scalable student learning without substantially increasing faculty workload.

By preserving PBL’s collaborative, interdisciplinary core while strategically incorporating generative AI tools, our enhanced approach offers a practical solution for scaling effective biomedical AI education. Such integration ensures that students not only gain technical competencies but also develop critical thinking and collaborative skills necessary for real-world biomedical innovation.

IV
Challenges and Opportunities

While our case study illustrates the efficacy of PBL in biomedical AI education, widespread adoption of PBL remains limited due to inherent scalability constraints. Traditional PBL approaches demand considerable faculty resources, domain-specific expertise, and continuous curricular updates to align with rapidly evolving biomedical technologies. Emerging large foundation models—such as LLMs and vision-language models (VLMs)—offer opportunities to address these constraints. Here, we discuss the potential of AI-enhanced PBL and outline the key challenges alongside mitigation strategies, highlighting relevant educational resources.

IV-A

Opportunities: Enhancing PBL Scalability with AI

\bullet

Guided Problem Exploration.

Foundation models can facilitate personalized guidance traditionally provided by faculty. For instance, LLMs can prompt students to critically analyze complex biomedical scenarios, formulating clear learning objectives without prescribing direct solutions
[
61
,
62
]
. This approach maintains the learner-directed essence of PBL while significantly reducing faculty facilitation burdens.

\bullet

Knowledge Acquisition and Integration.

Foundation models support efficient navigation of extensive biomedical literature by summarizing current research, highlighting critical studies, and interpreting complex biomedical concepts and visual information
[
63
,
64
]
. This streamlines the self-directed learning phase, enabling students to allocate more time toward critical appraisal and innovative problem-solving.

\bullet

Prototype Development and Technical Support.

Generative AI models trained on programming languages can assist students by providing initial computational frameworks, debugging assistance, and suggestions for optimization
[
65
,
66
]
. Consequently, students can concentrate on higher-order problem-solving skills, while educators can direct their attention to conceptual guidance rather than routine technical support.

\bullet

Automated and Continuous Assessment.

AI-driven formative assessment systems provide timely, detailed, and individualized feedback on student progress throughout the PBL cycle. Such automated formative evaluations accelerate learning iterations, ensuring timely identification of conceptual misunderstandings and facilitating corrective action
[
67
,
68
]
.

\bullet

Personalized Learning Pathways.

AI’s analytical capability allows customization of learning experiences by identifying students’ specific learning patterns, strengths, and gaps, and recommending tailored resources and instructional approaches. This personalization addresses challenges arising from varied educational backgrounds common in interdisciplinary biomedical AI courses
[
69
,
70
]
.

IV-B

Challenges and Mitigation Strategies

\bullet

Ensuring Accuracy and Managing Hallucinations.

Foundation models, particularly LLMs, may produce plausible yet inaccurate information (often referred to as “hallucinations”), posing risks in biomedical education contexts where accuracy is critical. Mitigation strategies include implementing rigorous validation protocols
[
71
]
, integrating retrieval-augmented generation methods that reference verified biomedical literature
[
72
]
, and explicitly training students to critically evaluate AI outputs
[
73
]
. These approaches ensure the reliability of AI-generated content in educational settings.

\bullet

Balancing AI Use and Critical Thinking Development.

While generative AI can significantly scale biomedical education, excessive dependence may inhibit students’ deep conceptual understanding and independent critical thinking. Educational strategies should clearly define appropriate use-cases for AI assistance
[
74
]
, establish periods of independent problem-solving without AI support, and design assessment methods emphasizing critical evaluation of AI-generated solutions rather than simple adoption
[
75
]
. To foster creativity, instructors should incorporate challenging scenarios specifically designed to encourage innovative problem-solving and divergent thinking beyond standard AI recommendations
[
63
]
.

\bullet

Equity, Inclusion, and Accessibility in AI-Enhanced Education.

Generative AI models often display performance biases or reduced effectiveness for non-native English speakers and individuals from diverse cultural backgrounds, potentially reinforcing existing educational inequalities
[
76
]
. Institutions must proactively ensure equitable access to AI-enhanced education by developing inclusive prompting strategies, providing multilingual model interfaces
[
77
]
, and consistently monitoring the performance and effectiveness of AI tools across varied demographic groups
[
78
]
. Such measures are critical for ensuring equitable benefits and reducing disparities in AI education outcomes.

\bullet

Ethical, Legal, and Professional Responsibility.

The educational use of AI technologies presents ethical and legal challenges related to privacy, data security, intellectual property, and academic integrity
[
79
]
. Addressing these concerns requires explicit institutional policies governing AI usage in educational settings
[
80
]
, robust frameworks for data management, and clear delineation of responsibilities regarding AI-generated content. Transparency and clear communication regarding AI’s role in educational processes are essential to maintain academic integrity and uphold professional and ethical standards in biomedical education
[
81
]
.

Conclusion

Our pedagogical study demonstrates the effectiveness of a PBL framework tailored specifically for biomedical AI education, addressing critical gaps in traditional biomedical engineering curricula. Through a three-year case study conducted at Georgia Tech and Emory University, involving 248 undergraduate and graduate students, we showed that PBL substantially enhances student capabilities in addressing real-world biomedical challenges. The educational success of this approach is reflected by the high scholarly output (16 peer-reviewed student publications), consistently positive peer evaluations, and robust interdisciplinary collaboration.
PBL, as implemented, successfully integrates foundational biomedical knowledge with advanced AI techniques, effectively bridging theoretical understanding and practical application. By explicitly embedding generative AI into the PBL model, this approach provides a scalable solution to address common educational challenges such as diverse student backgrounds, limited faculty resources, and rapidly evolving biomedical technologies. Our findings thus offer a practical roadmap for biomedical engineering programs aiming to effectively incorporate AI education, ultimately preparing the next generation of engineers to responsibly develop innovative solutions that improve healthcare outcomes.
