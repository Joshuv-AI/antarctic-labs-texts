Comment on “A machine learning approach to predicting severe diabetes distress in adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus”
Authors: Yindan Song, Linwei Xia
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Sakane et al. 1 , which developed machine learning models to predict severe diabetes distress in adults with type 1 diabetes and identified the neural network as the best‐performing approach. This work is clinically important because earlier recognition of distress could help integrate psychosocial risk stratification into routine diabetes care. However, we have several methodological concerns that potentially threaten the validity of the conclusions.

First, the validation strategy is insufficiently clear and may have produced optimistic performance estimates. The Methods section states that the dataset of 259 participants was split into 80% training and 20% testing, while also stating that all models were trained and evaluated using stratified 5‐fold cross‐validation with SMOTE and class weighting; yet it is not specified whether oversampling and model selection were performed strictly within each resampling loop. In a small dataset with only 30.9% events, evaluating 10 algorithms and selecting the apparent “best” model without a clearly nested framework can inflate discrimination estimates and distort risk estimates, especially when SMOTE is used 2 . A fully nested resampling strategy, with all preprocessing confined to the training folds and followed by calibration assessment on untouched test data, would provide more credible internal validation.

Second, the study's claim of screening utility seems overstated because model choice was driven mainly by accuracy, although the outcome prevalence was imbalanced and screening typically prioritizes sensitivity, calibration, and clinical utility. Notably, the neural network had the highest accuracy (0.744) and AUC (0.728), but only moderate recall (0.500), whereas the SGDClassifier achieved higher recall (0.750) and a higher F1 score (0.571). Moreover, several CGM variables showed no significant between‐group differences in Table 1, which further tempers confidence in immediate clinical deployment. Current guidance recommends evaluating discrimination together with calibration and decision‐relevant measures before advocating implementation 3 .

In summary, this promising study should be interpreted with caution until the validation workflow is clarified and clinically appropriate performance criteria are emphasized. Addressing these issues is essential for correct interpretation of the evidence and for guiding future research and practice.
