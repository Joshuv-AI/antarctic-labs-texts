Generative multi-fidelity modeling and downscaling via spatial autoregressive transport maps
Alejandro Calle-Saldarriaga, Paul F.V. Wiemann, Matthias Katzfuss

Abstract
Spatial fields are often available at multiple fidelities or resolutions, where high-fidelity data is typically more costly to obtain than low-fidelity data. Statistical surrogates or emulators can predict high-fidelity fields from cheap low-fidelity output. We propose a highly scalable Bayesian approach that can learn the joint non-Gaussian distribution and nonlinear dependence structure of nonstationary spatial fields at multiple fidelities from a small number of training samples. Our method is based on fidelity-aware autoregressive GPs with suitably chosen regularization-inducing priors. Exploiting conjugacy, the integrated likelihood is available in closed form, enabling efficient hyperparameter optimization via stochastic gradient descent. After training, the method also characterizes in closed form the distribution of higher-fidelity fields given lower-fidelity data. In our numerical comparisons, we show that our approach substantially outperforms existing methods and that it can be used to characterize and simulate high-fidelity fine-scale climate behavior based on output from coarse (low-fidelity) global circulation models.



Keywords:
climate-model emulation; Gaussian process; nonstationarity; regional climate model; statistical downscaling

1
Introduction

Multi-fidelity modeling

Multi-fidelity modeling integrates numerical models or observations that describe the same underlying quantity at different resolutions, time points, or spatio-temporal scales. This kind of modeling is used in myriad application areas
(
Umakant et al., 2007
;
Huang et al., 2013
;
Ho et al., 2021
;
Ma et al., 2022
;
Bird et al., 2023
;
Bhouri et al., 2023
;
Mole and Laizet, 2024
, e.g.,)
. Higher fidelities (e.g., finer resolutions) are more computationally expensive, and so a key goal of statistical multi-fidelity modeling is to develop surrogates that leverage inexpensive, low-fidelity data to predict high-fidelity fields
(
Fernández-Godino, 2023
;
Bocquet, 2023
)
. Probabilistic (i.e., generative) models are crucial for this task as they account for the stochastic nature of many processes, such as atmospheric physics at high resolutions
(
Selz and Craig, 2015
, e.g.,)
. Generative models also enable error propagation and statistical inference
(
Peherstorfer et al., 2018
)
by providing a joint or conditional distribution across fidelities.

However, training data for such multi-fidelity models are often scarce.
Due to the computational cost of running high-fidelity models, few high-fidelity samples may be available for training multi-fidelity emulators.
Similarly, while automated and remote sensing have enabled the collection of large numbers of observations of Earth systems, the relevant or effective amount of training data for many important tasks is small, especially when considering localized or extreme events, which often have the most impact.
Hence, there is a crucial need for methods that can learn or probabilistically emulate the properties of large and complex spatio-temporal fields such as temperature or precipitation from only a few multiple-fidelity training samples. Current approaches are often too simplistic or restrictive (e.g., assuming Gaussianity or linear dependence), are insufficiently scalable, do not properly account for uncertainty, or require more training data than is available in many settings (e.g., for localized impact assessments).

Multi-fidelity Earth-systems models

An example of low-fidelity models are global circulation models (GCMs), which can be run forward to produce samples of both short-term forecasts (e.g., hours to weeks) and long-term projections of potential future climates. GCMs are too spatially coarse to resolve many fine-scale climate processes with crucial impacts. Hence, they must be connected to higher-fidelity features, including those produced by regional forecast or climate models or other fine-scale numerical models
(
Mearns et al., 2009
;
Mearns et al., 2017
)
, or to remote-sensing or in-situ observational data.
Dynamical downscaling of coarse models
(
Giorgi and Bates, 1989
;
Coppola et al., 2021
;
Giorgi et al., 2023
, e.g.,)
using regional climate models (RCMs) requires considerable computational resources. Figure
1
shows samples of GCM-RCM pairs.

Statistical downscaling

Statistical multi-fidelity emulators
(
Tabari et al., 2021
)
often rely on simple Gaussian approaches
(
Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2000
;
Le Gratiet and Garnier, 2014
, e.g.,)
. A Bayesian nonparametric treatment of the relationship between low- and high-fidelity outputs
(
Perdikaris et al., 2017
)
can lead to non-Gaussian posteriors in higher-fidelity models.
Han and Görtz, 2012
use a hierarchical kriging approach, where GPs fitted on lower-fidelity observations serve as the model trends for GPs fitted to higher-fidelity observations.
Ma et al., 2019
model high-fidelity outputs via a learned GP conditional on the lower-fidelity model outputs. The parallel partial cokriging emulator
(
Ma et al., 2022
)
can model large spatial domains that may be non-nested.
Cheng et al., 2023
argues that these methods based on cokriging are computationally infeasible for very large datasets and proposes a recursive nearest-neighbor cokriging method to alleviate that cost.
Ekanayaka et al., 2025
jointly model spatio-temporal high-resolution remote-sensing data and coarse-resolution GCM outputs with a basis function representation.
Many of the multi-fidelity models in the literature are geared towards having a single training replicate (i.e., just one observation per spatial location), whereas ensemble data, which our method is designed for, is increasingly common and useful for assessing uncertainty of complex models, such as Earth-system models.

Machine-learning approaches

Some recent multi-fidelity approaches rely on deep learning. The multi-fidelity residual neural process
(
Niu et al., 2024
)
shares residual outputs of lower-fidelity surrogates with their higher-fidelity counterparts to improve the accuracy of their predictions. Generative hierarchical neural processes
(
Wu et al., 2022
)
construct a latent variable model and predictions across fidelities are conditionally independent given the latent states.
Buster et al., 2024
use a fully convolutional neural network to produce high-resolution spatial observations given the outputs of a GCM.
Mardani et al., 2023
propose a two-step approach, in which an initial mean prediction is corrected by a diffusion step.
However, approaches based on deep neural networks typically require massive training data. Their application is challenging in data-scarce regimes, often forcing indiscriminate pooling of training data over different spatial regions or diurnal and seasonal cycles, or requiring sophisticated techniques with substantial expert user input such as transfer learning. For example, extreme weather events—rare by definition and varying substantially by location and season—thus pose a major challenge from a training-data standpoint.
Deep-learning approaches based on synthetic data
(
Nikolenko, 2021
)
are limited in our setting due to potential biases, poor generalization to real data, their inability to fully capture the complexity and variability of real-world Earth systems, and challenges with uncertainty quantification
(
Hao et al., 2024
)
. Additionally, these models can suffer from long-term instability
(
Chattopadhyay and Hassanzadeh, 2023
;
Tague, 2024
, e.g.,)
and high computational costs. For example, even very-high-resolution numerical weather models struggle to simulate hazardous and extreme convective rainfall
(
NASEM, 2024
, i.e., from thunderstorms;)
, limiting their usefulness for generating synthetic data for subsequent deep learning.
Many deep-learning approaches also encode implicit assumptions or inductive biases that result in unrealistic marginal distributions or overly smooth spatial fields
(
Rahimi et al., 2024
, e.g.,)
.

Our method

We develop a probabilistic emulator that learns the joint distribution and nonlinear dependence structure of nonstationary spatial fields at multiple scales, fidelities or resolutions. Our method requires only a small number of training samples by employing fidelity-aware autoregressive GPs with carefully crafted regularization-inducing priors. Our approach builds upon recently proposed Bayesian triangular transport maps
(
Katzfuss and Schäfer, 2023
)
, which capture complex and non-Gaussian distributions of a (single-fidelity) spatial field by learning a transport map from the distribution of interest to a simple reference distribution. In our approach, the information between fidelities is shared in a hierarchical fashion — we learn the conditional distribution at each fidelity given lower-fidelity fields. The original BTM method uses the maximin ordering, which can be interpreted as a multi-resolution decomposition of the observations, so extending the Bayesian transport map method to multi-resolution observations seems quite natural: we use a conditional maximin ordering, which orders the coarser fidelities first, ensuring an appropriate conditioning structure. Our model is trained using multiple spatial fields at multiple fidelities as input. We propose methods that encourage data-dependent sparsity to achieve scalability, extending the priors in the original BTM so that we can model fidelity-specific processes.
Our approach accurately models behavior at different scales and captures complex spatial distributions arising from a wide range of physical phenomena, as opposed to many competing methods, which assume the spatial distribution (or some latent representation of it) is Gaussian.

Methodology

2.1
Scalable Bayesian transport maps: a review

Motivation and core idea

Traditional Gaussian process (GP) models for spatial fields assume Gaussian marginal and joint distributions, which can be overly restrictive for many environmental and physical processes.

Katzfuss and Schäfer, 2023
present a flexible probabilistic approach based on Bayesian transport maps that can learn non-Gaussian distributions and nonlinear dependence structures from limited training data, via estimation of a triangular transport map to a simple reference measure
(
Baptista et al., 2024
;
Irons et al., 2022
;
Wang and Marzouk, 2022
, e.g.,)
. The map is inferred by fitting a GP (auto-)regression at each spatial location. Carefully designed priors and Vecchia-type
(
Vecchia, 1988
)
approximations allow the Bayesian transport map (BTM) approach to scale to large spatial fields while fitting limited training data and quantifying uncertainty.

Maximin ordering

Consider a centered spatial field
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})^{\top}
on the domain
\mathcal{D}
, where
y_{i}=y(\mathbf{s}_{i})
for
\mathbf{s}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}
.

Katzfuss and Schäfer, 2023
propose to use a maximum-minimum-distance (maximin) ordering
(
Guinness, 2018
)
of
y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}
based on their spatial locations
\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{N}
. The maximin ordering is such that coarse scales are ordered early and fine scales later. We assume that the entries of
\mathbf{y}
are ordered according to the maximin ordering of
\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{N}
.

Bayesian specification and inference

The BTM approach assumes a conditional Gaussian structure for each observation

\textstyle y_{i}|\mathbf{y}_{<i},f_{i},d_{i}^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(y_{i}|f_{i}(\mathbf{y}_{<i}),d_{i}^{2})

and places conjugate GP-inverse-gamma priors on the mean functions
f_{i}
and the standard deviations
d_{i}
:

\displaystyle f_{i}|d_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle ind}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{GP}(0,d_{i}^{2}K_{i}),

\displaystyle d_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle ind}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{IG}(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}),\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha_{i}>1,\beta_{i}>0,

for
i=1,\dots,N
.
The inverse-gamma prior parameters
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
encode a parametric decay in the
d_{i}^{2}
induced by the maximin ordering.
The covariance kernels
K_{i}
are designed to encode two principles:
(i) functions
f_{i}
are pushed toward linearity as the minimum distance to previously ordered points decreases as
i
increases;
(ii) input relevance of the
k
-th neighbor
y_{c_{i}(k)}
decays exponentially with neighbor order
k
. The latter property enables a (data-dependent) reduction in the conditioning sets
\mathbf{y}_{<i}
encoding the screening effect
(
Stein, 2011
)
— the diminishing relevance of distant neighbors in the presence of close neighbors.

The specific form of the map components and the normal-inverse-gamma conjugacy enables factorization and closed-form evaluation of the marginal log-likelihood of the global hyperparameter vector
\bm{\theta}
driving the inverse gamma prior on
d_{i}
and the kernels
K_{i}
.
The factorization of the model evidence (i.e. the marginal log-likelihood) enables mini-batch training for scalable inference.
Alternatively, full Bayesian inference via MCMC can be carried out when prior uncertainty in
\bm{\theta}
is important.

2.2
Multi-fidelity modeling via autoregressive transport maps

We extend the BTM framework from Section
2.1
to handle multi-fidelity spatial data by proposing fidelity-aware autoregressive GPs that can learn joint distributions across multiple resolutions or scales from limited training samples.

Multi-fidelity formulation

Consider observations
\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{y}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{R}^{\top})^{\top}
at
R
fidelities or resolutions ordered from low/coarse to high/fine, with the corresponding sets of spatial locations
\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{R}
. In other words, we have
\mathbf{y}_{r}=(y_{r,1},\ldots,y_{r,N_{r}})^{\top}
, where
y_{r,i}
corresponds to spatial location
\mathbf{s}_{r,i}\in\mathcal{S}_{r}\subset\mathcal{D}
. We typically have
N_{1}\leq\ldots\leq N_{R}
.
We write the
N
-variate joint density, with
N=\sum_{r=1}^{R}N_{r}
, using an autoregressive decomposition as

\textstyle p(\mathbf{y})=\prod_{r=1}^{R}p(\mathbf{y}_{r}|\mathbf{y}_{<r}),

where our goal is to learn these conditional distributions from a training ensemble
\{\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{n}
sampled independently from
p(\mathbf{y})
, in the large-
N
, small-
n
regime.
We make the typical Markov assumption
p(\mathbf{y}_{r}|\mathbf{y}_{<r})=p(\mathbf{y}_{r}|\mathbf{y}_{r-1})
across fidelities
(
Le Gratiet and Garnier, 2014
;
Ma et al., 2022
;
Perdikaris et al., 2017
)
for notational simplicity, but relaxing it is straightforward.

Conditional maximin ordering

We extend maximin ordering to the multi-fidelity setting through a conditional approach
(
Schäfer et al., 2021
;
Chen et al., 2025
)
, where points with lower-fidelity are ordered first, and conditional on those being ordered first, we sequentially order each subsequent fidelity. The multi-fidelity maximin ordering is illustrated in Figure
2
. The goal is to ensure that lower-resolution points anchor the ordering, so that every fine-scale location has nearby lower-fidelity neighbors in its conditioning set. Formally, let
d(x,\mathcal{A}):=\min_{y\in\mathcal{A}}\|x-y\|
, and let
\mathcal{A}_{r-1}=\bigcup_{q<r}\mathcal{S}_{q}
, with
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\emptyset
. For each fidelity, we produce the permutation
\pi_{r}:\{1,\dots,N_{r}\}\to\{1,\dots,N_{r}\}
such that

\displaystyle\pi_{r}(1)

\displaystyle={\arg\max}_{i}d(\mathbf{s}_{r,i},\mathcal{A}_{r-1}),

\displaystyle\pi_{r}(k+1)

\displaystyle={\arg\max}_{i\notin\pi_{r}(\{1:k\})}d(\mathbf{s}_{r,i},\mathcal{A}_{r-1}\cup\{\mathbf{s}_{r,\pi_{r}(1)},\dots,\mathbf{s}_{r,\pi_{r}(k)}\}),\hskip 10.00002ptk=1,\ldots,N_{r}-1.

For
\pi_{1}(1)
we pick an arbitrary location, usually near the center of the lowest-fidelity grid. The conditional maximin ordering is then given by the global permutation
P:\{1,\dots,N\}\to\bigcup_{r=1}^{R}\{(r,i)\}
, where

\textstyle P(\sum_{q=1}^{r}N_{q}+k)=(r,\pi_{r}(k)),\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq r\leq R,\>1\leq k\leq N_{r}.

Hereafter,
\mathbf{y}
and locations
\mathcal{S}
are assumed to be ordered according to this conditional maximin formulation.
We define the lengthscales (i.e., distance to the nearest previously ordered point) as

\textstyle\ell_{r,i}=d(\mathbf{s}_{r,i},\mathcal{A}_{r-1}\cup\{\mathbf{s}_{r,j}:j<i\}).

The conditional maximin ordering can be constructed in
\mathcal{O}(N\log^{2}(N))
time and
\mathcal{O}(N)
space
(
Chen et al., 2025
;
Schäfer et al., 2021
)
. The ordering is precomputed once before training, and hence it is usually not a computational bottleneck.

Multi-fidelity transport map

We propose to learn the multi-fidelity distribution
p(\mathbf{y})
via a transport map
\mathcal{T}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
to the standard multivariate normal distribution:
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{N}_{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{N})
, in an extension of
(
Katzfuss and Schäfer, 2023
)
. Without loss of generality, we consider a lower-triangular map

\textstyle\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{y})=\begin{bmatrix}[l]\mathcal{T}_{1,1}(y_{1,1})\\
\quad\vdots\\
\mathcal{T}_{1,N_{1}}(y_{1,1},\dots,y_{1,N_{1}})\\
\mathcal{T}_{2,1}(y_{1,1},\dots,y_{1,N_{1}},y_{2,1})\\
\quad\vdots\\
\mathcal{T}_{R,N_{R}}(y_{1,1},\dots,y_{1,N_{1}},y_{2,1},\dots,y_{2,N_{2}},\dots,y_{R,1},\dots,y_{R,N_{R}})\end{bmatrix}.

(1)

To ensure invertibility of the map, we define the monotonic (in the last argument) map components

\textstyle\mathcal{T}_{r,i}(y_{1,1},\ldots,y_{r,i})=\frac{y_{r,i}-f_{r,i}(\mathbf{y}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}})}{d_{r,i}},

(2)

where the conditioning set
\mathbf{y}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}}=(\mathbf{y}_{r,c_{r,i}},\mathbf{y}_{r-1,c^{\prime}_{ri}})
contains the
m_{r}
nearest previously ordered neighbors of the same fidelity and
m^{\prime}_{r}
nearest neighbors of the previous fidelity to location
\mathbf{s}_{r,i}
, with
c_{r,i}
and
c^{\prime}_{r,i}
denoting the corresponding index sets.
This yields a factorization where each element in
\mathbf{y}
can be expressed by a normal distribution when conditioning on values
\mathbf{y}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}}
as well as the relevant function
f_{r,i}
and variance term
d^{2}_{r,i}
,

\textstyle y_{r,i}|\mathbf{y}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}},f_{r,i},d^{2}_{r,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(y_{r,i}|f_{r,i}(\mathbf{y}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}}),d_{r,i}^{2}).

Using these small conditioning sets is motivated by the so-called screening effect
(
Stein, 2002
;
Stein, 2011
)
, where the relevance of distant neighbors decays when conditioning on closer neighbors (see Figure
3
). Under the conditional maximin ordering, the rates at which these relevances decay are exponential in certain settings, as shown in
Schäfer et al., 2021
and
Chen et al., 2025
, but with constants depending on the specific data-generating process. Similarly,
Chen and Sanz-Alonso, 2024
show that methods similar to our transport map approach only need a polylogarithmic number of samples (in
N
) to accurately estimate the distribution in the Gaussian setting.

Fidelity-aware priors

We specify independent conjugate priors for each fidelity,

\displaystyle f_{r,i}|d_{r,i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,d_{r,i}^{2}K_{r,i}),

\displaystyle d_{r,i}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{IG}(\alpha_{r,i},\beta_{r,i}),

whose specific parameterizations are motivated by the behavior of aggregated Matérn Gaussian fields (as illustrated in Figure
3
), with hyperparameters that are shared within fidelities but differ across fidelities to capture fidelity-dependent behavior.

Following the decay principles from the BTM reviewed in Section
2.1
, we parameterize the inverse-gamma parameters using the minimum neighbor distance
\ell_{r,i}
by

\textstyle\mathbb{E}[d_{r,i}^{2}]=e^{\theta^{d_{1}}_{r}}\ell_{r,i}^{\theta^{d_{2}}_{r}},

where the hyperparameters
\theta^{d_{1}}_{r}
and
\theta^{d_{2}}_{r}>0
control fidelity-specific decay rates of the residual variance. Note that we use superscripts on
\theta
s to denote hyperparameter names, not exponents. This polynomial decay specification is motivated by theoretical results showing that the large class of stochastic processes with quasi-quadratic log-likelihood functions exhibit polynomial decay of conditional variances under the conditional maximin ordering
(
Schäfer et al., 2021
, Lemmas 5.10 and 5.15)
, where GPs with covariance functions given by the Green’s function of elliptic PDEs of order
s
have conditional variances of order
\ell_{r,i}^{2sr}
measured at fidelity
r
.

Empirical support for this decay is illustrated in Figure
3
. To parameterize the gamma prior for
d_{r,i}^{2}
, we assume that the prior standard deviation equals
g
times the prior mean, resulting in
\alpha_{r,i}=2+1/g^{2}
and
\beta_{r,i}=\exp(\theta^{d_{1}}_{r})\ell_{r,i}^{\theta^{d_{2}}_{r}}(1+1/g^{2})
. We let
g=4
to obtain a relatively vague prior for the conditional variances.

The covariance kernels
K_{ri}
extend the
K_{i}
from
Katzfuss and Schäfer, 2023
to handle inputs from multiple fidelities and by introducing fidelity-specific parameters, resulting in

\textstyle K_{r,i}(\mathbf{y}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}},\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}})=\mathbf{y}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{r}\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}}+\sigma_{r,i}^{2}\rho\left(\sqrt{(\mathbf{y}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}})^{\top}\mathbf{Q}_{r}(\mathbf{y}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}})}/\gamma_{r}\right),

(3)

where
\gamma_{r}=\exp(\theta_{r}^{\gamma})
is the fidelity-specific range parameter.
The nonlinearity variance
\sigma_{r,i}^{2}=e^{\theta^{\sigma_{1}}_{r}}\ell_{r,i}^{\theta^{\sigma_{2}}_{r}}
in the kernels decays when
r
and
i
increase, pushing functions toward linearity at fine scales. This behavior is justified from the fact that, for many physical processes, if we condition on averages over subdomains of decreasing diameter, we observe that the conditional distributions quickly become Gaussian, as argued by
Katzfuss and Schäfer, 2023
as a consequence of the Poincaré inequality
(
Adams and Fournier, 2003
)
. These averages over subdomains can be viewed as observations in lower fidelities or earlier-order points in the conditional maximin ordering in the current fidelity.

We propose the relevance matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{r}
as the diagonal matrix with weights
q_{r,j}
,
j=1,\dots,m_{r}+m^{\prime}_{r}
, decaying exponentially with neighbor number following the screening effect with respect to each fidelity,

\textstyle q_{r,j}=\begin{cases}\exp(\theta^{q_{0}}_{r}-\theta^{q_{1}}_{r}j),&j\leq m_{r},\\
\exp(\theta^{q^{\prime}_{0}}_{r}-\theta^{q^{\prime}_{1}}_{r}(j-m_{r})),&m_{r}<j\leq m_{r}+m^{\prime}_{r}.\end{cases}

The conditioning set sizes are determined adaptively as
m_{r}=\max\{k:\exp(\theta_{r}^{q_{0}}-\theta_{r}^{q_{1}}k)\geq\varepsilon\}

and similarly for
m^{\prime}_{r}
,
where
\varepsilon\geq 0
is specified upfront.
In our numerical experiments, we set
\varepsilon=0.01
, which typically results in conditioning sets of size less than 20 while maintaining high inferential accuracy. The intercept terms
\theta_{r}^{q_{0}},\theta_{r}^{q_{0}^{\prime}}
allow extra flexibility for the model, as it is a data dependent way to pick fidelity-specific truncation radii.

We motivate regularization and sparsity by exploiting the so-called screening effect
(
Stein, 2002
;
Stein, 2011
, e.g.,)
.
For multiresolution spatial observations,
Schäfer et al., 2021
establish exponential screening rates for processes derived from elliptic boundary-value problems observed at different scales, induced by the conditional maximin ordering.
Under the assumption that observation points are approximately homogeneously distributed within each fidelity level, we expect similar conditional independence properties to hold at fine scales.
Importantly, the arguments presented by
Schäfer et al., 2021
specifically accommodate settings where the coarser-fidelity observations represent local averages rather than point-wise evaluations of the underlying function, which matches the empirical scenarios examined in Section
3.2
and illustrated in Figure
3
.

Scalable inference

The multi-fidelity model requires
9R-2
hyperparameters total (9 per fidelity except for the first, which has no cross-fidelity terms), which are estimated from training data
\mathbf{y}^{(1:n)}=\{\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{n}
. The normal-inverse-gamma conjugacy enables closed-form marginal likelihood evaluation, allowing empirical Bayes inference via gradient-based optimization of
\log p(\mathbf{y}^{(1:n)}|\bm{\theta})
, where it can be shown by extending results in
Katzfuss and Schäfer, 2023
that

\textstyle p(\mathbf{y}^{(1:n)}|\bm{\theta})=\prod_{r=1}^{R}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{r}}(|\mathbf{G}_{r,i}|^{-1/2}\times(\beta_{r,i}^{\alpha_{r,i}}/\tilde{\beta}_{r,i}^{\alpha_{r,i}})\Gamma(\tilde{\alpha_{r,i}})/\Gamma(\alpha_{r,i})),

(4)

with
\mathbf{G}_{r,i}=\mathbf{K}_{r,i}+\mathbf{I}_{n}
,
\mathbf{K}_{r,i}=\big(K_{r,i}(\mathbf{y}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}}^{(j)},\mathbf{y}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}}^{(l)})\big)_{j,l=1,\dots,n}
,
\tilde{\alpha}_{r,i}=\alpha_{r,i}+n/2
, and
\tilde{\beta}_{r,i}=\beta_{r,i}+\mathbf{y}_{r,i}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{r,i}^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{r,i}/2
.
We write
\bm{\theta}
for the vector of all hyperparameters, and
\bm{\theta}_{r}
for the hyperparameters relevant for the
r
-th fidelity.
Because
\bm{\theta}_{r}
and
\bm{\theta}_{s}
for
r\neq s
do not appear in the same terms in (
4
), training across fidelities can proceed independently and can be parallelized with computational cost
\mathcal{O}(N_{r}(n^{3}+\tilde{m}_{r}n^{2}))
per fidelity, where
\tilde{m}_{r}=m_{r}+m^{\prime}_{r}
typically remains below 20.
Within each fidelity, mini-batch optimization provides additional speedups, reducing the computational cost to
\mathcal{O}(B(n^{3}+\tilde{m}_{r}n^{2}))
per gradient update for batch size
B
.

After training (i.e., obtaining the empirical Bayes estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
), the posterior predictive distribution

\textstyle p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{y}^{(1:n)},\hat{\bm{\theta}})=\prod_{r=1}^{R}p(\mathbf{y}_{r}|\mathbf{y}_{r-1},\mathbf{y}^{(1:n)},\hat{\bm{\theta}})=\prod_{r=1}^{R}\prod_{i=1}^{N_{r}}p(y_{r,i}|\mathbf{y}_{\tilde{c}_{r,i}},\mathbf{y}^{(1:n)},\hat{\bm{\theta}})

can be used for efficient marginal likelihood evaluation at test samples, posterior sampling, and conditional inference. Individual product terms can be obtained by straightforward extension of
Katzfuss and Schäfer, 2023
.
Importantly, one can readily compute the distribution of higher-fidelity observations conditional on lower-fidelity data. When modeling uncertainty quantification is critical, the framework can be extended using MCMC or Laplace approximation to account for hyperparameter uncertainty.

Simulation study

3.1
Comparison metrics and methods

We compare several different estimators
\hat{p}
of true data-generating distributions
p(\mathbf{y})
and associated conditionals
p(\mathbf{y}_{r}|\mathbf{y}_{<r})
. To evaluate the performance of an estimator, we compute the average negative log-density of the learned distribution on a set of held-out test samples; for one test sample
\mathbf{y}^{*}
, we have
-\log\hat{p}(\mathbf{y}^{*})=-\sum_{r=1}^{R}\log\hat{p}(\mathbf{y}_{r}^{*}|\mathbf{y}_{<r}^{*})
. This is a strictly proper scoring rule for probabilistic prediction, called the log-score
(
Gneiting and Katzfuss, 2014
, e.g.,)
, and provides an approximation, up to an additive constant, of the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence
KL(p\|\hat{p})
. This allows us to compare the accuracy and goodness-of-fit of the learned distribution
\hat{p}
against the true data-generating distribution
p
, evaluating both the joint and the conditional distributions (i.e.,
p(\mathbf{y}_{r}|\mathbf{y}_{<r})
). We also plot generated conditional spatial fields from our method for a qualitative check on its accuracy.

The methods to be compared (see Appendix
A
for implementation details) are:

MF BTM:

Our multi-fidelity Bayesian transport map method, as described in Section
2.2
.

HK:

Our implementation of hierarchical kriging
(
Han and Görtz, 2012
)
.

NARGP:

Our implementation of the nonlinear autoregressive GP
(
Perdikaris et al., 2017
)

VAE:

A multi-fidelity variational auto-encoder, inspired by
Cheng et al., 2024
.

Fits a GP with Matérn covariance to the highest-fidelity observations and assumes the lower-fidelities are block-wise averages of the higher-fidelities, similar to
(

. Not shown for all comparisons because of poor performance when this assumption is not met.

3.2

In many downscaling and multiresolution problems in the geosciences, the average of the fine-scale values in a given cell is equal to the corresponding value at a coarser scale. With this in mind, we consider three fidelities such that we observe the highest fidelity on a regular grid of size
N_{3}=30\times 30=900

N_{2}=10\times 10=100

N_{1}=5\times 5=25

f_{3i}

f_{3,i}(\mathbf{y}_{3,<i})=\mathbf{b}_{3,i}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{c_{3,i}}+2\sin(4(b_{3,i,1}y_{c_{3,i}(1)}+b_{3,i,2}y_{c_{3,i}(2)})),

where
\mathbf{b}_{3,i}=(b_{3,i,1},\dots,b_{3,i,|c_{3,i}|})^{\top}

4
Emulating regional climate models

Climate models are essential computational tools for simulating and studying Earth’s climate and are used for climate research and policy-making. They are, in essence, computer code that models Earth-system behavior via differential equations. Producing ensembles of samples using such models is very computationally costly
(
Kay et al., 2015
, e.g.,)
, but they are needed to assess uncertainty related to different initial conditions. Statistical emulators, such as the one presented in this work, allow us to summarize, study, and replicate the distribution to generate additional samples at much lower computational cost.

However, as noted by
Yu et al., 2020
, to capture some quantities of interest, especially those with severe impacts, fine-scaled grid spacing is necessary, usually accomplished via dynamical downscaling, where GCM output drive or force high-resolution RCM
(
Xu et al., 2019
)
. However, this increase in resolution in climate models incurs a substantial computational cost, and a
\times 10
decrease in grid spacing can lead to a
\times 1000
increase in computational cost
(
Schneider et al., 2024
)
.

We interpret GCMs and RCMs as low-fidelity and high-fidelity encodings, respectively, of Earth’s climate distribution, and an RCM run forced by GCM output as a sample from the conditional distribution of the high-fidelity field given the coarse low-fidelity GCM field. Our method can be used as a statistical emulator that produces RCM-like samples given GCM outputs.

We use the 50 GCM members of the CanESM2
(
Chylek et al., 2011
)
Large Ensemble (CanESM2-LE)
(
Kushner et al., 2018
;
Kirchmeier-Young et al., 2017
)
produced by the Canadian Centre for Climate Modeling and Analysis under the RCP8.5 emission scenario.
Leduc et al., 2019
uses the CanESM2-LE output to drive the Canadian Regional Climate Model (CRMC5)
(
Martynov et al., 2013
)
and obtain the CRCM5 Large Ensemble (CRMC5-LE), which we use as our RCM data.

We focus our attention on emulating maximum daily temperatures (
K
). We consider a longitude-latitude regional grid (over Europe) of size
N_{1}=24\times 14=336
for the CanESM2 output, and a grid of size
N_{2}=280\times 280=78{,}400
for the CRCM5 output. We obtain temperature anomalies by standardizing the data at each grid location to zero mean and unit variance. Examples are shown in Figures
1
and the first row of Figure
7
. We limit our analysis to a single day, January 25th 1996, but extending the model to include temporal structure is straightforward (see brief discussion in Section
5
).

5
Conclusions

We have proposed a Bayesian approach for learning non-Gaussian, high-dimensional distributions of multi-fidelity spatial fields based on a relatively small number of training samples. We do so via transport maps, whose components are modeled using autoregressive Gaussian processes, which have a spatially informed parametrization that only requires a small number of hyperparameters to be fitted. The autoregressive nature of our map allows us to naturally learn the conditional distributions of higher fidelities given lower fidelities, which, for example, enables cheap sampling of (otherwise costly) high-fidelity fields given only (relatively cheap) low-fidelity observations. Our Bayesian approach probabilistically regularizes the model, resulting in sparse maps that are scalable in high dimensions. The method can be fitted quickly and was highly accurate in our numerical comparisons, outperforming all considered competing methods. It is therefore a promising approach for many spatial multi-fidelity emulation tasks, especially when low-fidelity models are much cheaper to run than their high-fidelity counterparts. Potentially, deep models such as the VAE could outperform the MF BTM method in data-rich regimes, where much more training samples are available than in the settings considered here.

For future work, it is straightforward to combine the proposed multi-fidelity approach with existing spatio-temporal (Lei and Katzfuss, in prep.) and multiple-process
(
Wiemann and Katzfuss, 2023
)
extensions of the single-fidelity BTM method, which rely on dimension-expanded input spaces. We can also relax the conditional Gaussianity assumption to allow more flexible maps (Brachem, Katzfuss, Wiemann, in prep.) or consider parametric shrinkage for scenarios where we have only one or very few training samples
(
Chakraborty and Katzfuss, 2024
)
. Possible applications include multi-fidelity climate model emulation (especially the GCM-RCM paradigm presented in Section
4
), RCM parameter calibration given GCM outputs, or attribution of regional climate-change-related extremes.

Appendix A
Competitors: Implementation details

Matérn

If we assume that the averages of higher fidelities correspond to observations in lower fidelities, as in
Ma et al., 2019
, we can write

\textstyle\mathbf{y}_{r-1}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}_{r}+\mathbf{y}_{r}^{h},

where
\mathbf{A}
is an averaging matrix, and
\mathbf{y}_{r}^{h}
is a vector containing one hold-out fine-scale value from each coarse grid cell (and so
\mathbf{y}_{r}
only contains the fine-scale values that are not held-out). Note that, in practice, we do not have access to
\mathbf{A}
, nor do the relationships between the fidelities ought to be linear. We can model all fidelities jointly by assuming a GP prior with Matérn covariance on
\mathbf{y}_{R}
(with left out points at all fidelities except the lowest, to obtain non-singular joint covariances for all fidelities), and finding hyperparameters via second-order optimization. The conditional distributions in this scenario will be given by the usual conditional multivariate Gaussian formulas, and the left-out observations can be filled in deterministically.

Hierarchical kriging (HK)

For
R
fidelities,
Han and Görtz, 2012
propose setting the prior on the first fidelity as

\textstyle y_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{1})\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{1}^{\prime})),

and, for the subsequent fidelities, they recursively set

\textstyle y_{r}(\mathbf{s}_{r})\sim\mathcal{GP}(\beta_{0,r}\hat{y}_{r-1}(\mathbf{s}_{r}),K_{r}(\mathbf{s}_{r},\mathbf{s}_{r}^{\prime})),

where
\beta_{0,r}
is a scaling factor which measures correlation between the
r
-th and
r-1
-th fidelities, and
\hat{y}_{r-1}(\mathbf{s}_{r})
is the posterior mean of the GP at the
r-1
-th fidelity evaluated at the locations of the
r
-th fidelity. They use a spline correlation function
(
Lophaven et al., 2002
)
for their kernels
K_{1},\dots,K_{R}
, but in our implementation we choose a more common Matérn kernel, due to the spatial nature of our data. We chose a smoothness of
\nu=0.5
, which we found resulted in better log-scores. Also, they find their hyperparameter estimates using genetic algorithms, but we tune them using SGD with auto-differentiation.

One key assumption of the paper is that they only observe one spatial field per fidelity. In our settings, we observe
n
spatial fields per fidelity, so we get
\beta_{0,r,1},\dots,\beta_{0,r,N_{r}}
estimates of the correlations between spatial fields at different fidelities for each fidelity. In order to accommodate for these differing prior means, we normalize each field at fidelity
r
,
\mathbf{y}_{r}^{(i)}
, using the corresponding
\beta_{0,r,i}\hat{y}_{r-1}(\mathbf{s}_{r})
as the average of the field, and we put a prior mean at zero at each fidelity.

For the climate-model application in Section
4
, where the number of locations in higher fidelities is large, we fit the lower-fidelity (GCM data) using an exact Matérn kernel, but for the RCM outputs we use a variational strategy to approximate the posterior with interpolation kernels
(
Wilson and Nickisch, 2015
)
and inducing points
(
Hensman et al., 2013
)
.

Non-linear auto-regressive GP (NARGP)

Perdikaris et al., 2017
retain the standard GP prior on the
coarsest level,

\textstyle y_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{1})\sim\mathcal{GP}\!\bigl(0,\;K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{1}^{\prime})\bigr),

but model every subsequent-fidelity by augmenting the spatial input with
the posterior mean prediction of the previous level. Let

\hat{y}_{r-1}(\mathbf{s}_{r})
denote the GP posterior mean at fidelity

r\!-\!1
evaluated at the locations of fidelity
r
and define the
augmented input

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{r}:=\bigl[\hat{y}_{r-1}(\mathbf{s}_{r})\;,\;\mathbf{s}_{r}^{\!\top}\bigr]^{\!\top}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.
The
r
-th fidelity is then assigned the GP prior

\textstyle\mathbf{y}_{r}(\mathbf{s}_{r})\;\sim\;\mathcal{GP}\!\bigl(0,\;K_{r}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{r},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{\prime})\bigr),\hskip 17.00024pt\hskip 8.50012ptr=2,\dots,R,

with a kernel that factorizes into a product-sum form

\textstyle K_{r}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\;=\;\,k_{a}\!\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{r-1}(\mathbf{s}_{r}),\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{r-1}(\mathbf{s}_{r}^{\prime})\bigr)\,k_{x}\!\bigl(\mathbf{s}_{r},\mathbf{s}_{r}^{\prime}\bigr)\;+\;k_{x}\!\bigl(\mathbf{s}_{r},\mathbf{s}_{r}^{\prime}\bigr),

where
k_{a}
acts on the low-fidelity prediction (capturing possibly
non-linear relations across fidelities) and
k_{x}
is a spatial kernel.
We again use an exponential Matérn kernel (
\nu\!=\!0.5
) for

k_{x}
and
k_{a}
, as it yielded lower log-scores on our climate data than smoother choices. Each kernel has its own ARD weights for each dimension.

For
R>2
, since the posterior distribution at all fidelities except
r=1
is no longer Gaussian, we propagate uncertainty along each recursive step, and approximate the posterior likelihood using Monte Carlo integration, as
Perdikaris et al., 2017
.

For the climate example, the posterior for the higher-fidelity GP is too costly to learn, so we use the same variational strategies as used in HK.

R
-fidelity variational autoencoder (VAE)

Inspired by
Cheng et al., 2024
, for a multi-fidelity variational autoencoder we treat the collection of spatial fields observed at increasing resolutions as a single hierarchy generated from shared latent structure. We introduce an independent latent vector
\mathbf{z}_{r}
for each fidelity, which is drawn from a standard-normal prior, and all of them are concatenated into a global latent vector
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{z}_{R}^{\top})^{\top}
.

This latent vector is passed through convolutional layers to learn a common feature map, and then we have
R
decoder heads that up-samples the shared feature map so that it matches the target grid size at the
r
-th fidelity, which outputs the mean of a diagonal Gaussian likelihood for
\mathbf{y}_{r}
. A learnable variance
\sigma^{2}_{r}
completes the generative model

\textstyle\mathbf{y}_{r}|\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(f_{\theta}^{(r)}(\mathbf{z}),\sigma_{r}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{N_{r}}).

Inference is amortized with
R
separate convolutional encoders, which maps its input field to the mean and log-variance of a diagonal Gaussian posterior
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}_{r}|\mathbf{y}_{r})
. Under a mean-field assumption
(
Blei et al., 2017
)
, the global variational distribution factorizes as the product of these
R
Gaussians. This leads to a evidence lower bound (ELBO) that is a sum over fidelities of reconstruction terms (one log-likelihood per fidelity) minus a sum of KL divergences that regularize every fidelity, with some
\beta_{r}\in[0,1]
terms that allows us to tune how hard we push towards the prior
(
Higgins et al., 2017
)
. We optimize the resulting objective with the usual reparameterization trick
(
Kingma and Welling, 2014
;
Rezende and Mohamed, 2015
)
and ADAM, using mini-batches that carry observations from all resolutions.

To estimate predictive performance comparable with our methods here, we approximate the marginal log-likelihood of a multi-resolution observation via importance-weighted sampling
(
Burda et al., 2015
)
. For every held-out test set member, we draw a few thousand latent samples from the variational posterior and evaluate their joint likelihood across all fidelities, and average the resulting importance weights.

In practice, we use smaller latent dimensions at lower-fidelity fields and larger ones at higher fidelities, together with ELU activations and batch normalization. Thus, the model learns a single coherent latent representation that explains all fidelities simultaneously, but allows each resolution to express its own detail through their dedicated decoder.
