On graphs coverable by chubby shortest paths
Meike Hatzel, Michał Pilipczuk

Abstract
Dumas, Foucaud, Perez, and Todinca [SIAM J. Disc. Math., 2024] proved that if the vertex set of a graph G G can be covered by k k shortest paths, then the pathwidth of G G is bounded by 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ⋅ 3 k ) \mathcal{O}(k\cdot 3^{k}) . We prove a coarse variant of this theorem: if in a graph G G one can find k k shortest paths such that every vertex is at distance at most ρ \rho from one of them, then G G is ( 3 , 12 ​ ρ ) (3,12\rho) -quasi-isometric to a graph of pathwidth k 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ) k^{\mathcal{O}(k)} and maximum degree 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ) \mathcal{O}(k) , and G G admits a path-partition-decomposition whose bags are coverable by k 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ) k^{\mathcal{O}(k)} balls of radius at most 2 ​ ρ 2\rho and vertices from non-adjacent bags are at distance larger than 2 ​ ρ 2\rho . We also discuss applications of such decompositions in the context of algorithms for finding maximum distance independent sets and minimum distance dominating sets in graphs.



1
Introduction

The aim of
coarse graph theory
is to study the metric structure in graphs. Recently, Georgakopoulous and Papasoglu
[
GP23
]
launched a systematic investigation of the coarse counterpart of the theory of Graph Minors, with the following overarching conjecture in mind: If a graph
G
excludes some fixed graph as a
fat minor
, then the metric structure of
G
resembles that of a graph excluding a classic minor (there are subtleties regarding this statement, see the discussion in
[
DHIM24
]
). Here, fat minors are a coarse counterpart of classic minors, and the resemblance is measured through the existence of a
quasi-isometry
: a roughly bijective mapping between graphs that roughly preserves distances.

The investigation of the theory of coarse minors naturally leads to studying coarse counterparts of classic graph parameters. The general principle of the coarse theory is that disjointness corresponds to farness, and intersection corresponds to closeness.
Hence, in the coarse counterpart of treewidth, we would consider tree decompositions where the bags are not of bounded size but can be covered by a bounded number of balls of bounded radius. Very recently, Nguyen, Scott, and Seymour
[
NSS25a
]
, and independently Hickingbotham
[
Hic25
]
proved that the existence of such a tree decomposition is equivalent to being quasi-isometric to a graph of bounded treewidth, and the natural analogue of this statement also works for pathwidth
[
Hic25
]
. Also very recently, Abrishami, Czyżewska, Kluk, Pilipczuk, Pilipczuk, and Rzążewski
[
ACK+25
]
studied the connection between such tree decompositions and the existence of balanced separators consisting of a bounded number of bounded-radius balls.

The drive permeating the recent work on coarse graph theory is to understand which results from classic structural graph theory can be lifted to the coarse setting and to what extent. See
[
GP23
]
for a broad overview and
[
AGHK25
,
AHJ+24
,
BS24
,
DHIM24
,
DJMM24
,
Hic25
,
NSS25a
,
NSS25b
]
for examples of individual works of this kind. In this paper, we add one more result to this growing list. Precisely, we examine the following elegant theorem proved recently by Dumas, Foucaud, Perez, and Todinca
[
DFPT24
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
DFPT24
]
)
.

Suppose
G
is a graph in which one can find a family
\cal P
of
k
shortest paths so that every vertex of
G
belongs to some path of
\cal P
. Then
G
has pathwidth bounded by
\mathcal{O}(k\cdot 3^{k})
.

The main contribution of this work is the following coarse counterpart of
theorem

1.1
. Here, for positive integers
k,\rho
, we call a graph
G

(k,\rho)
-geodesic-coverable
if in
G
there is a family
\cal P
of
k
shortest paths so that every vertex is at distance at most
\rho
from some path of
\cal P
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose
G
is a
(k,\rho)
-geodesic-coverable graph, for some positive integers
k,\rho
. Then
G
is
(3,12\rho)
-quasi-isometric to a graph of pathwidth at most
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
and maximum degree less than
26k
. Furthermore, the vertices of
G
can be partitioned into parts
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{\ell}
so that every part can be covered by
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
balls of radius
2\rho
, and every two vertices at distance at most
2\rho
belong to the same part or to two consecutive parts.

The proof of
theorem

1.2
follows the strategy proposed by Dumas et al.
[
DFPT24
]
and lifts it naturally to the coarse setting. Namely, we fix a root vertex
v
and partition the graph into
breadth first search
(BFS) layers starting from
v
. The key technical statement is the following: every BFS layer
S
can be covered by
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
balls of radius
2\rho
. Inspired by
[
DFPT24
]
, the idea here is to classify the shortest paths from
v
to the vertices of
S
according to their
types
.
Very roughly, every shortest path from
v
to a vertex of
S
can be technically massaged so that it first travels for a significant distance along one shortest path
P_{1}
from the family
\mathcal{P}
witnessing geodesic-coverability, then jumps through a short “connector” to another shortest path
P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
and travels for a significant distance along it, and so on; the sequence
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots
is the type of
Q
.
There are only
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
relevant types to consider, and it turns out that paths of the same type starting in the same vertex lead to vertices that are at distance at most
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}\cdot\rho
from each other. This allows us to cover
S
with
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
balls of radius
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
, and an additional argument is needed to cover each of those balls with
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
balls of radius
2\rho
. Once the coverage property of the BFS layers is established, we naturally construct the partition
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{\ell}
so that each
C_{i}
consists of
2\rho
consecutive layers. The quasi-isometry statement is obtained by combining all these observations with a generic construction. We note that the proof naturally gives a linear-time algorithm to construct a partition
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{\ell}
with the asserted properties.

Compared to
theorem

1.1
,
theorem

1.2
gives a
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
bound instead of
2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
. In
[
DFPT24
]
, Dumas et al. first gave a simpler argument giving a bound of
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
on the number of relevant types and then proceeded with a more complicated argument reducing the bound to
2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
.
In our proof, we use the simpler argument because we lose an additional
k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
factor later in the proof as well when proving that paths of the same type lead to vertices not far from each other.
Compared to
theorem

1.1
, this part of the proof gets far more complicated in the coarse setting.

The partition
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{\ell}
as described in
theorem

1.2
shall be called a
distance-
2\rho
path-partition-decomposition
of
G
. We see it as the natural coarse counterpart of the linear variant of
tree-partition-decompositions
, underlying the graph parameter
tree-partition width
; see an overview of this parameter e.g. in the work of Wood
[
Woo09
]
. More generally, a
distance-
\rho
tree-partition-decomposition
of a graph
G
would be a tree
T
together with a partition of the vertex set of
G
into parts
\{C_{x}\colon x\in V(T))\}
so that whenever
\mathsf{dist}(u,v)\leqslant\rho
,
u
and
v
must belong to the same part
C_{x}
or to two parts
C_{x},C_{y}
so that
x,y
are adjacent in
T
. We observe that such a decomposition can be used to efficiently solve some computational problems of metric character in
G
, by means of dynamic programming. We showcase this on the examples of the computation of
\alpha_{2\rho}(G)
— the maximum number of disjoint balls of radius
\rho
that can be packed in
G
— and
\gamma_{\rho}(G)
— the minimum number of balls of radius
\rho
needed to cover all the vertices of
G
.

Theorem 1.3
.

There is an algorithm that, given an
n
-vertex graph
G
and a
distance-
\rho
tree-partition-decomposition of
G
of width at most
k
and degree at most
\Delta
, computes
\alpha_{2\rho}(G)
in time
n^{\mathcal{O}(\Delta k)}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

There is an algorithm that, given an
n
-vertex graph
G
and a
distance-
\rho
tree-partition-decomposition of
G
of width at most
k
and degree at most
\Delta
, computes
\gamma_{\rho}(G)
in time
n^{\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2}k)}
.

By combining
Theorems

1.2
,
1.3
and
1.4
, we obtain the following algorithmic consequence.

Corollary 1.5
.

Suppose
G
is an
n
-vertex
(k,\rho)
-geodesic-coverable graph, for some positive integers
k,\rho
. Then
\alpha_{4\rho}(G)
and
\gamma_{2\rho}(G)
can be computed in time
n^{k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}}
.

In the language of
parameterized complexity
, this places the computation of
\alpha_{4\rho}(G)
and
\gamma_{2\rho}(G)
in the complexity class
\mathsf{XP}
(
slicewise polynomial
) when parametrised by the smallest
k
such that the graph is
(k,\rho)
-geodesic-coverable.

2
Preliminaries

Paths and walks.

A
walk

P
of length
\ell
in a graph
G
is a sequence of vertices
v_{0},\dots,v_{\ell}
such that
v_{i}v_{i+1}\in E(G)
for all
0\leqslant i<\ell
; and
P
is a
path
if the vertices
v_{0},\ldots,v_{\ell}
are additionally pairwise different.
We sometimes identify
P
with the subgraph of
G
containing these edges and vertices.
We identify
v_{0}
as the
startvertex
,
\mathsf{start}(P)
, and
v_{\ell}
as the
endvertex
,
\mathsf{end}(P)
, in order to distinguish the two ways of traversing a walk:
forward
from
\mathsf{start}(P)
to
\mathsf{end}(P)
, or
backward
from
\mathsf{end}(P)
to
\mathsf{start}(P).

We refer to
P
as a
v_{0}
-
v_{\ell}
-walk (or
v_{0}
-
v_{\ell}
-path, if it is a path).
If
x,y\in V(P)
, then we write
xPy
for the subwalk of
P
starting in
x
and ending in
y
.
We write
xP
instead of
xP\mathsf{end}(P)
and
Px
instead of
\mathsf{start}(P)Px
.
For two walks
P
and
Q
with
\mathsf{end}(P)=\mathsf{start}(Q)
, we write
P\cdot Q
for their concatenation.
We also combine these two notations to write
PxQ
instead of
Px\cdot xQ
.

Metric aspects.

The
distance
between two vertices
u,v\in V(G)
, denoted
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(x,y)
, is the length of the shortest
u
-
v
-path in
G
.
We drop the index in case the graph is evident from the context.
We extend the notation to sets
X,Y
of vertices in the expected way:
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,X)\coloneqq\min_{v\in X}\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)
and
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(Y,X)\coloneqq\min_{u\in Y}\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,X)
.
If
P
and
Q
are paths, we simply write
\mathsf{dist}(u,P)
and
\mathsf{dist}(Q,P)
instead of
\mathsf{dist}(u,V(P))
and
\mathsf{dist}(V(Q),V(P))
.
A
geodesic
in
G
is a path that is a shortest path between its endpoints.
Note that every subpath of a geodesic is a geodesic as well.
A path/walk
P
is a
\gamma
-almost shortest path/walk
if
||P|-\mathsf{dist}(\mathsf{start}(P),\mathsf{end}(P))|\leqslant\gamma
.
Note that every subpath/subwalk of a
\gamma
-almost shortest path/walk is again a
\gamma
-almost shortest path/walk.

For a vertex
u
and a non-negative integer
\rho
, the radius-
\rho
ball around
v
is

\mathsf{B}_{\rho}(v)\coloneqq\{u\in V(G)\mid\mathsf{dist}(u,v)\leqslant\rho\}.

A collection of paths
\mathcal{P}
is a
distance-
\rho
path-cover
if every vertex of
G
lies at distance at most
\rho
of some vertex of a path in
\mathcal{P}.

We say that
G
is
(k,\rho)
-geodesic coverable
if there is a distance-
\rho
path-cover
\mathcal{P}
of size
k
and all paths in
\mathcal{P}
are geodesics. We then also say that
\mathcal{P}
is a
distance-
\rho
geodesic-cover
, or a
(k,\rho)
-geodesic-cover
of
G
.

A
distance-
\rho
independent set
in
G
is a set
I\subseteq V(G)
such that
\mathsf{dist}(u,v)>\rho
for all distinct
u,v\in I
.
The maximum size of a distance-
\rho
independent subset of a set
A\subseteq V(G)
is denoted by
\alpha_{\rho}(A,G)
, and the distance-
\rho
independence number of
G
is defined as
\alpha_{\rho}(G)\coloneqq\alpha_{\rho}(V(G),G)
.

A
distance-
\rho
dominating set
of a set
A\subseteq V(G)
in a graph
G
is a set
D\subseteq V(G)
such that for every
u\in A
there exists
d\in D
satisfying
\mathsf{dist}(u,d)\leqslant\rho
; we also say that
D

distance-
\rho
dominates

A
.
The minimum size of a distance-
\rho
dominating set of
A
is denoted as
\gamma_{\rho}(A,G)
. We say that
A
is
(k,\rho)
-coverable
if
\gamma_{\rho}(A,G)\leqslant k
.
The
distance-
\rho
domination number
of
G
is defined as
\gamma_{\rho}(G)\coloneqq\gamma_{\rho}(V(G),G)
.

We note the following simple relation between the quantities defined above.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any graph
G
, set
A\subseteq V(G)
, and positive integer
\rho
, we have

\alpha_{2\rho}(A,G)\leqslant\gamma_{\rho}(A,G)
.

Proof.

Let
I\subseteq A
be a maximum-size distance-
2\rho
independent subset of
A
, and
D
be a minimum-size distance-
\rho
dominating set of
A
. Observe that for every
v\in D
, there is at most one vertex
u\in I
such that
\mathsf{dist}(u,v)\leqslant\rho
, for otherwise, by triangle inequality, we would have two vertices of
I
at distance at most
2\rho
from each other. Since every vertex of
I
is at distance at most
\rho
from some vertex of
D
, this proves that
|I|\leqslant|D|
.
∎

Decompositions.

We make use the following coarse counterparts of
path-partition-decompositions
and
tree-partition-decompositions
.
Similarly to other notions in coarse graph theory, these are obtained by requiring the parts of the decomposition to be coverable by a small number of balls.
Fix a positive integer
\rho
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
distance-
\rho
path-partition-decomposition
of a graph
G
is a sequence
C_{1},\dots,C_{\ell}
of subsets of
V(G)
such that

\blacktriangleright

\{C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{\ell}\}
is a partition of
V(G)
, and

\blacktriangleright

for all
u,v\in V(G)
with
\mathsf{dist}(u,v)\leqslant\rho
and
u\in C_{i},v\in C_{j}
, we have
|i-j|\leqslant 1
.

The
width
of this path-partition-decomposition is defined as

\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\{\gamma_{\rho}(C_{i},G)\}
.

Definition 2.2
.

A
distance-
\rho
tree-partition-decomposition
of a graph
G
consists of a tree
T
and, for every node
x\in V(T)
, a set of vertices
C_{x}\subseteq V(G)
such that

\blacktriangleright

\{C_{x}\colon x\in V(T)\}
is a partition of
V(G)
, and

\blacktriangleright

for all
u,v\in V(G)
with
\mathsf{dist}(u,v)\leqslant\rho
and
u\in C_{x},v\in C_{y}
, the nodes
x
and
y
are either equal or adjacent in
T
.

The
width
of this tree-partition-decomposition is

\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\{\gamma_{\rho}(C_{i},G)\}
, and its
degree
is the maximum degree of
T
.

Thus, the width of a path- or tree-partition-decomposition is at most
k
if and only if every part
C_{x}
is
(k,\rho)
-coverable. Let us also recall the classic notions of
path-decompositions
and
pathwidth
.

Definition 2.3
.

A
path-decomposition
of a graph
G
is a sequence
D_{1},\dots,D_{\ell}
of subsets of
V(G)
satisfying the following:

\blacktriangleright

for every
e\in E(G)
there is an
i\in[\ell]
with
e\subseteq D_{i}
, and

\blacktriangleright

for all
i\leqslant j\leqslant k
holds
D_{i}\cap D_{k}\subseteq D_{j}.

The
width
of such a decomposition is the size of its largest set, and the
pathwidth
of
G
is the minimal width over all path-decompositions of
G
.

Quasi-isometries.

Let
G
and
H
be graphs.
A function
\varphi~\colon V(G)\to V(H)
is an
(m,a)
-quasi-isometry from
G
to
H
if
for all
u,v\in V(G)
we have

m^{-1}\cdot\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)-a\leqslant\mathsf{dist}_{H}(\varphi(u),\varphi(v))\leqslant m\cdot\mathsf{dist}_{G}(u,v)+a,

and for every
w\in V(H)
there exists
u\in V(G)
with

\mathsf{dist}_{H}(w,\varphi(u))\leqslant a
.
We say
G
is
(m,a)
-quasi-isometric
to
H
if there is an
(m,a)
-quasi-isometry from
G
to
H
.

We use the following generic construction of a quasi-isometry between a graph, and its “coarsening” induced by a maximal distance independent set. We use the terminology from
[
ACK+25
]
, but the method is folklore; see e.g.
[
GP23
, Observation 2.1]
or
[
AHJ+24
, Theorem 4]
.

Let
G
be a graph and
I
be a maximal distance-
\rho
independent set in
G
.
The
(I,\rho)
-distance graph
of
G
, denoted
H_{\rho}(I,G)
, is defined as follows

\blacktriangleright

V(H_{\rho}(I,G))\coloneqq I
, and

\blacktriangleright

for all
x,y\in I
we have
xy\in E(H_{\rho}(I,G))
if and only if
\mathsf{dist}_{G}(x,y)\leqslant 3\rho
.

Note that
H_{\rho}(I,G)
is unweighted, so single edges in this graph roughly correspond to distances between
\rho
and
3\rho
in
G
. To get a closer connection between the shortest path metrics, we also define
\widehat{H}_{\rho}(I,G)
as the graph obtained from
H_{\rho}(I,G)
by replacing every edge with a path of length
3\rho
. With this definition, we have that
G
and
\widehat{H}_{\rho}(I,G)
are quasi-isometric in the sense below.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
ACK+25
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph and
I
be an inclusionwise maximal distance-
\rho
independent set in
G
. For every
u\in V(G)
, let

\varphi(u)
be a vertex of
I
closest to
u
in
G
. (If there are several such vertices, choose an arbitrary one.) Then
\varphi
is a
(3,3\rho)
-quasi-isometry from
G
to
\widehat{H}_{\rho}(I,G)
.

We note that in
[
ACK+25
]
, Abrishami et al. derive
\widehat{H}_{\rho}(I,G)
from
H_{\rho}(I,G)
slightly differently: instead of replacing edges by paths of length
3\rho
, they just consider
\widehat{H}_{\rho}(I,G)
to be an edge-weighted graph obtained from
H_{\rho}(I,G)
by assigning weight
3\rho
to every edge. This minor difference is immaterial for the proof of
lemma

2.4
.

Snapwalks

In this section we discuss the concept of
snapwalks
. Intuitively, a snapwalk in a graph
G
is a sequence consisting alternately of subpaths of geodesics from a fixed geodesic-cover
\mathcal{P}
of
G
, and short “connectors” joining the endpoints of those subpaths in order. The idea is that every shortest path
Q
in
G
can be “snapped” to a snapwalk by (roughly) snapping every vertex on
Q
to the closest geodesic from
\mathcal{P}
. However, we want to avoid reusing the same geodesic of
\mathcal{P}
several times in a snapwalk, so every segment between two consecutive “snappings” to some
P\in\mathcal{P}
is replaced by simply sliding along
P
.

We proceed to a formal description.
Let
G
be a
(k,\rho)
-geodesic-coverable graph and
\mathcal{P}
a
(k,\rho)
-geodesic-cover of
G
.
A
\rho
-snapwalk

Q
is a sequence
(R_{0},Q_{1},R_{1},Q_{2},

R_{2},\dots,Q_{k^{\prime}},R_{k^{\prime}})
with
k^{\prime}\leqslant k
such that

\blacktriangleright

every
Q_{i}
is a subpath of a geodesic in
\mathcal{P}
;

\blacktriangleright

for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
there is at most one
i
such that
Q_{i}
is a subpath of
P
;

\blacktriangleright

every
R_{i}
is walk in
G
of length at most
2\rho+1
, and moreover,
R_{0}
and
R_{k^{\prime}}
are of length at most
\rho
; and

\blacktriangleright

\mathsf{end}(R_{i-1})=\mathsf{start}(Q_{i})
and
\mathsf{end}(Q_{i})=\mathsf{start}(R_{i})
, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k^{\prime}\}
.

We define
\mathsf{concat}(Q)\coloneqq R_{0}\cdot Q_{1}\cdot R_{1}\cdot Q_{2}\cdot R_{2}\cdot\dots\cdot Q_{k^{\prime}}\cdot R_{k^{\prime}}
.
We generalise notation for paths and walks to snapwalks by applying it to the concatenation instead wherever sensible, for example,
|Q|\coloneqq|\mathsf{concat}(Q)|
.
The geodesics of
\mathcal{P}
that the paths
Q_{i}
are subpaths of are called
visited
by
Q
.

The following lemma establishes that every path in
G
lies “near to” a snapwalk.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a
(k,\rho)
-geodesic-coverable graph and
\mathcal{P}
a
(k,\rho)
-geodesic-cover of
G
.
For every shortest path
P
in
G
there exists a
\rho
-snapwalk
Q
with
\mathsf{start}(Q)=\mathsf{start}(P)
,
\mathsf{end}(Q)=\mathsf{end}(P),
and
||P|-|Q||\leqslant 4\rho k
.

Proof 3.2
.

Let
\pi\colon V(G)\to\bigcup_{P_{i}\in\mathcal{P}}V(P_{i})
map every vertex of
G
to a vertex on a path in
\mathcal{P}
minimising the distance to it. (If there are multiple vertices minimising the distance, select an arbitrary one.) Note that
\mathsf{dist}(x,\pi(x))\leqslant\rho
, for
\mathcal{P}
is a distance-
\rho
geodesic-cover.
Also, let
\pi^{\mathcal{P}}\colon V(G)\to\mathcal{P}
map every vertex
x
to a path of
\mathcal{P}
containing
\pi(x)
, i.e.
\pi(x)\in\pi^{\mathcal{P}}(x)
. For a walk
S
, we write
\pi^{\mathcal{P}}(S)\coloneqq\pi^{\mathcal{P}}(V(S))
.

We now prove the following statement, which implies the desired one, by induction on
k^{\prime}\coloneqq|\pi^{\mathcal{P}}(P)|
:
There is a
\rho
-snapwalk
Q
with
\mathsf{start}(Q)=\mathsf{start}(P)
,
\mathsf{end}(Q)=\mathsf{end}(P)
, and
||P|-|Q||\leqslant 4\rho k^{\prime}
which only visits paths in
\pi^{\mathcal{P}}(P)
.

If

Lemma 4.1

Let
