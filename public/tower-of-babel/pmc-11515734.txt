Dynamics of fermentation quality, bacterial communities, and fermentation weight loss during fermentation of sweet sorghum silage
Authors: Haiwen Xu, Nier Wu, Na Na, Haijun Ding, Lin Sun, Yongyu Fang, Dongyang Li, E Li, Baozhu Yang, Xiaobin Wei, Yanlin Xue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSweet sorghum is used mainly as an energy crop and feed crop in arid and semiarid regions, and ensiling is a satisfactory method for preserving high-quality sweet sorghum. The aim of this study was to reveal the dynamics of the fermentation quality, bacterial communities, and fermentation weight loss (FWL) of sweet sorghum silage during fermentation.MethodsSweet sorghum was harvested at the first inflorescence spikelet stage and ensiled without (CK) or with lactic acid bacterial (LAB) additives (L). After ensiling, samples were collected on days 0, 1, 3, 5, 15, 40, and 100 to assess the fermentation quality, bacterial communities, and FWL.ResultsFor CK and L, on day 1, the pH was 5.77 and 5.57, respectively, and the lactic acid (LA) was 1.30 and 2.81 g/kg dry matter (DM), respectively. Compared with CK, L had a lower pH and higher LA from days 1 to 5 (P < 0.05), a lower FWL from days 5 to 100 (P < 0.05), and a greater abundance of Lactiplantibacillus from days 1 to 15 (P < 0.05). The main bacterial genera were Leuconostoc and Weissella in CK and Lactiplantibacillus, Leuconostoc, and Weissella in L on day 1; Lactiplantibacillus in all silages from days 3 to 40; and Lactiplantibacillus and Lentilactobacillus in all silages on day 100.ConclusionsSweet sorghum silage fermented relatively slowly during the first day. Moreover, inoculation with LAB accelerated fermentation and optimized bacterial communities during the initial fermentation phase. Inoculation with LAB also reduced the silage FWL, and the LAB succession relay occurred in the silage throughout the fermentation process.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03573-1.

Introduction

Sweet sorghum (Sorghum bicolor L. Moench) has developed primary root and secondary root systems, which contribute to its strong tolerance to drought and saline alkaline soil with enormous biomass yield [1]. Currently, it is used mainly as an energy crop and feed crop in arid and semiarid regions [2]. After large biomass is harvested, ensuring high-quality raw materials throughout the year is a problem. Ensiling is a satisfactory method for preserving high-quality sweet sorghum [3] because 60%—70% of its water-soluble carbohydrate (WSC) are preserved, and the crude protein concentration changes minimally after ensiling [4–6].

Sweet sorghum silage without any additives has good fermentation quality because of the high WSC concentration of fresh forage [4, 5, 7, 8]. Moreover, inoculation of sweet sorghum with lactic acid bacteria (LAB) at ensiling can accelerate fermentation and reduce fermentation weight loss (FWL) during fermentation [4, 6, 8, 9]. Ensiling sweet sorghum with grape pomace and LAB improves the fermentation quality and aerobic stability of silage [7]; interestingly, adding rumen fluid at ensiling improves the fermentation quality and optimizes the silage bacterial communities [9]. Inoculation with LAB isolated from sweet sorghum silage or elephant silage has a satisfactory effect on fermentation quality and microbial counts in sweet sorghum silage during the first 5 days after ensiling [4, 8]. Replacing corn silage with sweet sorghum silage in a total mixed diet does not affect the milk production and quality of dairy cows or the production performance of sheep [1, 10, 11]. However, there is limited information about the fermentation process during the first 5 days and the bacterial community succession during whole fermentation in sweet sorghum silage.

We hypothesized that the ensiling of sweet sorghum with LAB inoculants would have a satisfactory effect on the fermentation quality, the bacterial community, and the FWL of the silage. Thus, the objectives of this study were to determine the dynamics of fermentation quality, the bacterial community, and FWL during the fermentation process.

Materials and methods

Preparing silage

Sweet sorghum [Sorghum dochna (Forssk.) Snowden] was harvested at the first inflorescence spikelet stage from four different fields as replicates on 29 September 2022 at an experimental farm in Huhhot, China (40°750 N, 111°670 E). The forages from each field were immediately chopped into 1 to 2-cm pieces. After mixing thoroughly, the chopped forages from each field were divided into two batches as treatments. One batch (CK) was sprayed with 20.0 ml/kg fresh weight (FW) of distilled water, and the other batch (L) was sprayed with 5 g/t FW (recommended amount) of commercial LAB additives (Lactiplantibacillus plantarum 550 and 360 (≥ 1.3 × 1010 CFU/g) and Lentilactobacillus buchneri 225 (≥ 7.0 × 109 CFU/g)); Zhuanglemei. Sichuan Gaofuji Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Chengdu, China) and 20.0 ml/kg FW of distilled water. After thorough mixing, the forages (500 g) were packed into a polyethylene bag and sealed with a vacuum sealer. Twenty-eight bags of silage were prepared for each treatment (seven bags per field). The ensiling samples were collected on days 0, 1, 3, 5, 15, 40, and 100 for analysis of dry matter (DM), nutritional composition, fermentation quality, bacterial communities, buffering capacity (BC), and FWL.

Analysis

The DM content was assessed after drying the samples at 65 °C for 48 h in a forced-air oven. The crude protein (CP) concentration was assessed by the Kjeldahl method with an autoanalyzer using copper as a catalyst [12]. The water-soluble carbohydrate (WSC) concentration was determined via anthrone-sulfuric acid colorimetry using a spectrophotometer [13]. Neutral detergent fibre (NDF) and acid detergent fibre (ADF) concentrations were analysed via an Ankom 2000 fibre analyser without the use of a heat-stable amylase and are expressed inclusive of ash [14]. The ash concentration was assessed with a muffle oven by burning at 550 °C for 3 h after carbonizing the dried samples [15].

The three extracts per sample were performed by blending 20 g of silage with 180 mL of distilled water for 1 min using a flap-type sterile homogenizer for 100 min and then filtering the suspension through 4 layers of cheese cloth [16]. A pH meter was used to measure the pH of the first silage extract. The second extract was used for assessing the concentrations of lactic acid (LA), acetic acid (AA), propionic acid, and butyric acid via high-performance liquid chromatography with a column (Shodex RS Pak KC-811, Showa Denko K.K., Kawasaki, Japan) using 3 mM HClO4 as the mobile phase with a 1.0 mL/min flow rate set at 50 °C [6]. The third extract was used for analysing the AN using an autoanalyzer via an adaptation of the Kjeldahl method [17]. The BC of the silage was assessed via acid‒base titration [18]. The FWL of the silage was assessed by calculating the different weights of the bags between day 0 and day x (x = 1, 3, 5, 15, 40, and 100) according to Xu et al. [6] and Samarasinghe et al. [19].

The counts of lactic acid bacteria (LAB), coliforms, total aerobic bacteria (TAB), and yeasts were assessed by culturing on Man–Rogosa–Sharpe agar, violet red bile agar, nutrient agar, and potato dextrose agar, respectively, at 30 °C for 72 h in an incubator [20]. DNA extraction was performed via the E.Z.N.A. Stool DNA Kit (D4015, Omega, Inc., United States) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3–V4 region of the bacterial rRNA gene was amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) according to Logue et al. [21]. The PCR products were purified and quantified via AMPure XT beads and Qubit, respectively [22]. The sequence of the PCR products was analysed by LC-Bio (Hangzhou Lianchuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) with an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The paired-end reads were merged using FLASH. The sequence alignment of species annotation was performed via BLAST. The sequencing data were submitted to the Genome sequence archive (GSA) (accession code: CRA017600).

Statistical analyses

The effects of ensiling day per treatment and the effects of inoculants per ensiling day were analysed via the GLM procedure of SAS (SAS System for Windows, version 9.1.3; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Fermentation quality

During fermentation, all the silages presented a decreasing pH (P < 0.05) and an increasing LA, AA, AN, BC, and FWL (P < 0.05) (Table 2). Compared with CK, L presented a lower pH and higher LA and BC from days 1 to 5 (P < 0.05), a lower FWL from days 3 to 100 (P < 0.05), a lower pH on days 40 and 100 (P < 0.05), and a lower LA on day 40 (P < 0.05). Compared with that in the CK treatment, the AA in the L treatment was lower on day 3 but greater on day 100 (P < 0.05). Compared with that in the CK treatment, the AN in the L treatment was greater on day 1 and lower from days 5 to 15. Inoculation with LAB at ensiling sped up the early fermentation of sweet sorghum silage. Microbial counts

For all the silages, the counts of LAB and TAB increased during the first 3 days but decreased subsequently (P < 0.05) (Table 3); the counts of yeasts increased during the first 5 days and decreased from days 5 to 40 but increased slightly from days 40 to 100 (P < 0.05). The coliform counts increased on the first day and then decreased (P < 0.05) and was measurable until day 5 in CK and day 3 in L. Compared with CK, L had higher LAB counts on days 0, 1, and 40 (P < 0.05), lower coliform counts on day 3 (P < 0.05), higher TAB counts on day 1 but lower counts on day 40 (P < 0.05), and higher yeast counts on days 1 and 40 (P < 0.05). Inoculation with LAB at ensiling inhibited early coliform proliferation in sweet sorghum silage. Bacterial communities

The main bacterial genera in fresh forage (F) were Pantoea (56.1%), Erwinia (11.2%), Enterobacteriaceae noname (6.27%), Pseudomonas (6.41%), and Acinetobacter (5.41%). The abundances of those bacterial genera decreased during fermentation. The abundance of Lactiplantibacillus increased from 0.09% in F to 94.9% in CK and 97.3% in L on day 40 and then decreased to 38.9% in CK and 63.7%in L on day 100 (Fig. 1). The abundance of Lentilactobacillus increased from 0.01% in F to 21.7% in CK and 35.3% in L on day 100. The abundance of Leuconostoc increased from 0.001% in F to 39.0% in CK and 17.2% in L on day 1, then decreased to 0.49% in CK and 0.20% in L on day 100. The abundance of Weissella increased from 0.002% in F to 7.54% in CK and 4.48% in L on day 1, then decreased to 0.003% in CK and 0% in L on day 100. The abundance of Klebsiella increased from 0.89% in F to 16.1% in CK and 13.4% in L on day 1, then decreased to 0.61% in CK and 0.07% in L on day 100. Compared with CK, L presented a higher abundance of Lactiplantibacillus from days 1 to 15 (P < 0.05) and lower abundances of Klebsiella, Leuconostoc, and noname Enterobacteriaceae from days 3 to 100 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). Discussion

Although fresh sweet sorghum has relatively high BC, it is suitable for ensiling because of the sufficient WSC of fresh forage and the greater fermentation quality of silage [4, 6, 8]. Similar results were observed in the present study (Tables 1 and 2). Moreover, LAB were minor bacteria on fresh sweet sorghum because of their lower counts and abundance (Table 3 and Fig. 1). Pantoea was the main bacterial genus in fresh sweet sorghum in this study (Fig. 1) and in a previous study [6] because the materials were harvested from the same experimental farm. Previous studies reported that Bacillus or Pantoea was the main bacterial genus in fresh sweet sorghum [9, 23]. These differences might be caused by differences in geographical conditions because geographical location mainly affects the microbial communities of fresh forage [24–26].

The fresh sweet sorghum had ideal ensiling characteristics (Table 1), but on day 1, the CK and L presented higher pH values and lower LA values in the present study (Table 2). From day 3, the fermentation of all the silages proceeded as normal because of the lower pH and greater LA content (Table 2). Sifeeldein et al. reported a higher pH on days 5 and 7 in sweet sorghum silages [4]. Nevertheless, from day 1 after ensiling, a lower pH and greater LA were observed in whole-plant corn silages, oat silage, and sorghum-sudangrass silage without or with LAB [22, 27, 28]. These results indicate that during the initial fermentation phase, sweet sorghum silage can be observed to have slower fermentation than the other silages. Further study is needed to determine the cause of the unique initial sweet sorghum silage fermentation conditions.

The L had a lower pH and higher LA from days 1 to 5, a higher LAB count on day 1, and a higher abundance of Lactiplantibacillus from days 1 to 15 (Tables 2 and 3 and Figs. 1 and 2). These results suggested that inoculation with Lact. plantarum at ensiling can promote fermentation and optimize bacterial communities during the early fermentation phase because Lact. plantarum is a homofermentative LAB with the ability to produce greater amounts of LA by fermenting WSC in silage [29]. Moreover, on day 100, L contained higher AA and more Lentilactobacillus than did CK (Table 2 and Fig. 1). A previous study also revealed a higher abundance of Lent. buchneri in sweet sorghum silage with the same additives at different silo densities on day 100 [6]. Lent. buchneri in a silage system can ferment WSC into LA, AA, alcohol, and gas when WSC are sufficient and has the ability to convert LA into AA and alcohol under low-WSC conditions [29–31]. The terminal silages contained more than 200 g/kg DM of WSC in the present study (Table 1), indicating that Lent. buchneri exhibited heterofermentative activity during the late fermentation phase in sweet sorghum silage inoculated with Lent. buchneri.

Sun et al. first defined the LAB fermentation relay in whole-plant corn silage during fermentation; the dominant LAB are Weissella, Lactococcus, and Leuconostoc during the first day and Lactobacillus from day 2 [22]. Zhao et al. reported that Weissella and Lactococcus on day 1 and Lactobacillus from day 3 dominated the bacterial communities in sorghum-sudangrass silage [28]. Moreover, in oat silage, the LAB that dominated fermentation were Lactiplantibacillus and Pediococcus during the first 3 days, Lactiplantibacillus from days 3 to 15, and Lactiplantibacillus and Lentilactobacillus from day 15 [27]. In the present study, the main bacterial genera were Leuconostoc and Weissella in CK and Lactiplantibacillus, Leuconostoc, and Weissella in L on day 1; Lactiplantibacillus in all silages from days 3 to 40; and Lactiplantibacillus and Lentilactobacillus in all silages on day 100 (Fig. 1). These results indicate that the LAB fermentation relay is a common phenomenon in silage during fermentation.

The formation of lactates, acetates, and NA in silage significantly increases BC during fermentation [18]. In the present study, the LA and BC in all the silages increased rapidly during the first 15 days and then remained high (Table 2). However, the AA and AN increased slowly during the first 40 days and increased significantly from days 40 to 100 (Table 2). These results were also reflected in the rho values (0.9730, 0.9092, and 0.8561) of the correlation analysis and the R2 values (0.9467, 0.8266, and 0.7329) of the linear equation of BC with LA, AA, and AN (Figure S1). Moreover, previous studies reported that LA and BC exhibited similar trends of change during fermentation in Leymus chinensis silage and oat silage [16, 27]. These results indicated that the LA concentration strongly contributed to the BC in the silage during fermentation.

A previous study reported that the production of gas primarily contributes to the FWL of sweet sorghum silage during anaerobic fermentation [6]. The gas escapes from the silo into the air [31] because polyethylene has very low transmission rates of gas permeability [32]. Gas production in silage is related to the activities of heterofermentative LAB and undesirable microorganisms during fermentation [27, 33]. In the present study, the CK presented higher abundances of Leuconostoc and Enterobacteriaceae from days 3 to 100 and Lactococcus from days 3 to 40 (Figs. 1, 2, and S1). Furthermore, inoculation with LAB at ensiling reduces the amount of gas produced during fermentation [27, 33, 34], which explains the lower FWL in L than in CK from days 3 to 100 (Table 2).

Conclusions

Sweet sorghum silage has relatively slow fermentation during the first day, and the fermentation quality of the terminal silage is satisfactory. Inoculation with Lact. plantarum promotes fermentation and optimizes bacterial communities during the initial fermentation phase. Lent. buchneri exhibited heterofermentative activity during the late fermentation phase. During fermentation, the LAB fermentation relay is a common phenomenon in silage; the LA concentration contributes most strongly to the BC of the silage, and inoculation with LAB reduces the FWL of the silage.
