Determinants of caregiver burden among family caregivers of post-intensive care syndrome patients at 2 weeks post-discharge: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jiali Luo, Bolin Xiang, Sui Su, You Yuan, Ziqian Zhang, Dandan Song, Huiming Gao, Rujun Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith improved intensive care unit (ICU) survival rates, the number of patients with post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) is on the rise. The Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) recommends screening within 2–4 weeks post-discharge. However, in China, most ICU survivors rely on family caregivers, while data on caregiver burden specifically at 2 weeks post-discharge remain scarce. This cross-sectional study aimed to identify the factors influencing family caregiver burden in PICS patients 2 weeks after hospital discharge.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in the intensive care unit (from March 2024 to June 2025). Consecutive patients with post-intensive care syndrome and their primary family caregivers were included 14 days after discharge. Potential predictive factors were collected using a self-made standardized questionnaire and the Zarit Burden Interview Scale. Multivariate ordinal logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with the severity of caregiver burden.ResultsConducted at a tertiary hospital from March 2024 to June 2025, the study included 299 patient-primary caregiver pairs. Using the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI), the median caregiver burden score was 38.0 (IQR:25.0, 52.0), with 26.1% reporting minimal/no burden, 34.1% mild to moderate, 25.4% moderate to severe, and 14.4% severe burden. Multivariate ordinal logistic regression identified seven independent risk factors: caregiver age ≤ 45 years (OR = 3.196, 95% CI = 1.351–7.591), rural residence (OR = 2.894, 95% CI = 1.089–7.723), Dedicated care 2 weeks after ICU (OR = 6.844, 95% CI = 3.029–15.482), patient not returning to work (OR = 3.019, 95% CI = 1.459–6.251), caregiver cohabiting with spouse and children (OR = 6.758, 95% CI = 1.166–39.200), presence of other stressors (OR = 2.228, 95% CI = 1.155–4.305), and caregiver cohabiting with the patient (OR = 3.116, 95% CI = 1.483–6.546). Protective factors included caregiver being married/cohabiting, patient’s ICU stay ≤ 30 days, first ICU admission, and prognosis aligning with expectations (all p < 0.05).ConclusionsTwo weeks post-discharge, family caregivers of PICS patients predominantly bear mild to moderate burden; multivariate analysis identifies key risk and protective factors, addressing the gap in early-stage evidence and providing a basis for targeted clinical interventions.Trial registrationChiCTR2500100968 (https://www.chictr.org.cn); retrospectively registered on April 17, 2025.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04823-5.Key words: Intensive care units, Post-intensive care syndrome, Family caregivers, Caregiver burden, Post-discharge

Introduction

As intensive care unit (ICU) survival improves worldwide, the number of critically ill survivors is rising steeply. In 2010, the Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) coined the term post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) to describe new or worsening physical, cognitive and psychological impairments that appear after discharge [1]. Studies have shown that 12 months after discharge, 40%-64% of patients experience physical functional impairment, 30%-78% exhibit cognitive decline, and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) reach 21%-56%, 8%-42%, and 13%-56% respectively [2–4]. Driven by “early transfer, early rehabilitation” policies [5], approximately half of patients are sent home before physiological stability is fully restored, allowing PICS to be detected within 14 days after discharge. This makes the second week a critical window for early identification.

China’s transitional-care system is still nascent; around 90% of ICU dischargees rely on informally trained family members for assistance with activities of daily living, medication management, psychological support and emergency response [6, 7]. Prolonged caregiving precipitates anxiety, depression, PTSD, fatigue, sleep disturbance and financial strain in caregivers, a constellation termed post-intensive care syndrome-family (PICS-F) [8]. Reported rates range from 20% to 60% and correlate positively with the severity of patient PICS [9]. Higher caregiver burden is associated with increased patient readmission and poorer functional recovery, creating a mutually reinforcing cycle of impairment.

Existing studies have largely examined caregiver distress 3–6 months after ICU discharge; however, quantitative data on caregiver outcomes within the 2–4 week post-discharge window officially recommended by the 2020 SCCM International Consensus Conference and 2023 international multimodal rehabilitation guideline remain extremely scarce globally [10–12]. Furthermore, most existing evidence on PICS family caregivers is derived from Western countries with well-developed transitional care systems, while large-scale systematic studies in the Chinese context, where over 90% of ICU survivors rely on informally trained family caregivers for post-discharge care, remain notably lacking [6, 7]. Additionally, most previous studies have been conducted independently of routine clinical follow-up workflows, limiting the direct translatability of their findings into clinical practice.

The 2020 Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) International Consensus Conference on PICS officially recommends that follow-up diagnostics of physical, cognitive, and psychological impairments in PICS patients should be performed 2–4 weeks after hospital discharge [12]. Clinical recommendations further support that screening using patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) within this window helps identify post-ICU impairments at an early stage [1, 13]. While these recommendations focus primarily on patient outcomes, unaddressed caregiver burden will directly compromise the effectiveness of these early rehabilitation programs, given the mutually reinforcing cycle of impairment between caregivers and patients established earlier. This 2–4 week timeframe also aligns with a critical stress-adaptation phase for family caregivers: longitudinal evidence shows that while family stress begins to gradually ease from 2 weeks to 6 months after ICU discharge, caregivers at the 2-week mark have exited the acute ICU environment but have not yet established stable home care routines [14, 15]. This transitional period, before burden can stabilize or become chronic, therefore represents the optimal window for early identification of caregiver burden and targeted interventions to prevent the progression of PICS-F.

Therefore, the purpose of this study is to measure caregiver burden and analyze its influencing factors exactly at the SCCM-recommended two-week post-discharge timepoint. This study aims to fill the evidence gap in caregiver burden within the internationally standardized early follow-up window, provide data support for transitional care in the Chinese context, and generate findings that can be directly integrated into existing post-ICU clinical workflows to alleviate caregiver stress, promote patient recovery, and maintain family caregiving capacity.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted. From March 2024 to June 2025, pairs of patients and their caregivers were recruited from different intensive care units (emergency ICU plus another comprehensive ICU divided into ICU1 and ICU2) of a tertiary hospital in Zunyi City, Guizhou Province. A total of 299 pairs of patients and their caregivers were finally included in the study; recruitment was carried out two weeks after the patients’ discharge. The design and reporting of this study followed the requirements of the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement.

Setting and participants

We recruited 299 patient-caregiver pairs from a tertiary hospital in Zunyi(Guizhou, China). A consecutive enrollment strategy was adopted to minimize selection bias: trained researchers screened all patients discharged from the two ICUs daily according to the pre-specified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria included: age ≥ 18 years, ICU length of stay ≥ 48 hours, a score of ≥ 25 on the 41-item Post-ICU Syndrome Assessment Scale [16], ability to communicate in Chinese, and signed written informed consent. Primary family caregivers (spouses, children, or relatives providing unpaid care) also needed to understand the patient’s condition, have the ability to communicate in Chinese, and agree to participate. Exclusion criteria included pairs that could not be contacted by phone, patients who died or were readmitted within 14 days after discharge, caregivers with psychiatric/cognitive impairments, and cases without a caregiver for post-discharge care.

Sample size calculation followed Kendall’s recommendation: each variable should include 5–10 cases when performing multivariate modeling. The maximum number of candidate predictors was 52 (including patient and caregiver demographic variables and ZBI scale items). Taking the upper limit (52 × 5 = 260) and reserving a 10% dropout rate, the required sample size is determined to be at least approximately 290 pairs.

During the study period (March 2024 to June 2025), a total of 3349 accessible samples from three ICUs (ICU1, ICU2, and EICU) were available. Of these, a total of 769 samples were actually contacted and subjected to qualification screening. After initial screening, 448 patient-caregiver pairs were excluded (including those who did not meet inclusion criteria or met exclusion criteria). The remaining 321 pairs completed the baseline assessment, among whom 22 were lost to follow-up. Finally, 299 patient-caregiver pairs were included in this study. Data collection

From March 2024 to June 2025, nursing staff caring for consecutive ICU patients were randomly sampled and invited; written informed consent was obtained. Patient demographic data and admission/discharge assessment scores were extracted from the Haitai Electronic Medical Record System (V4.0); caregiver demographic information and the 22-item Zarit Burden Interview questionnaire were collected by trained nurses through face-to-face or telephone interviews. To ensure data accuracy, strict quality control measures were implemented throughout the study. All data were independently double-entered by two trained researchers using a standardized electronic case report form, and cross-checked for consistency. Any discrepancies or abnormal values were verified against the original electronic medical records and follow-up forms. All data were screened by two people, entered into Excel 2020, and then cleaned and logically validated in SPSS 29.0. Missing data occurred in the variables “Monthly income, Employment status, APACHE II score at ICU admission, and Distance from residence to hospital”, with a proportion of less than 3% for each variable. Given the very low level of missingness and the assumption that missing values were missing completely at random, a complete-case analysis was used for all statistical models without imputation.

Measurement tools

Patient and caregiver demographic data, care environment, and clinical variables were collected using a self-developed general information questionnaire, which was designed based on relevant literature and reviewed by an expert team. The questionnaire included patient-related information (e.g., clinical characteristics, ICU admission details, and post-discharge care needs) and caregiver-related information (e.g., sociodemographic characteristics, caregiving experience, and perceived stressors). The full questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Material.

The Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI) was originally developed by Zarit and colleagues for caregivers of elderly patients with dementia in the United States. It was later revised from a 29-item 4-point Likert scale to the currently widely used 22-item 5-point Likert scale [17]. The ZBI-22 is a commonly used tool for assessing care burden in clinical practice and research, with free public access. It includes two dimensions: personal burden and role burden. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = Never, 4 = Almost always; some items: 0 = Not at all, 4 = Very much), with a total score ranging from 0 to 88. Higher scores indicate greater care burden. The grading criteria are as follows: 0–21 points (minimal or no burden), 22–40 points (mild to moderate burden), 41–60 points (moderate to severe burden), and 61–88 points (severe burden). The ZBI-22 has demonstrated good internal consistency across populations, with Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.92 for the original scale and 0.875 for the Chinese version [18]. The scale has been widely used and validated in caregiver populations for various diseases and has multiple language versions [19]. In this study, the ZBI-22 was used to assess the care burden of family caregivers of PICS patients.

PICS was assessed using the Chinese version of the post-intensive care syndrome assessment scale developed by Ao Man [16]; a tool rated as Grade A recommended in a COSMIN based systematic review [20] and specifically designed and validated for Chinese ICU survivors. The scale consists of 41 dichotomous items (0 = absent, 1 = present) across four domains: Disease Status (10 items), Clinical Manifestations (12 items), Psychological Status (10 items), and Activities of Daily Living (9 items), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 41. It demonstrates strong psychometric properties, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.806, content validity of 0.867, and established structural validity. Criterion validity is supported by a strong correlation between the Psychological Status domain and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (r = 0.73, p < 0.001). ROC analysis showed an area under the curve of 0.817 (95% CI: 0.767–0.853). For clinical interpretation, the result judgment criterion was defined as a scale score reaching 63% of the total possible score, which corresponds to 25 points, and this value was used as the diagnostic threshold for PICS in this study.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 29.0 software (IBM Corp. Armonk, New York, USA). All continuous variables underwent Shapiro-Wilk normality tests. Variables conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x±s); comparisons between two groups were conducted using independent samples t-tests, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by least significant difference-t test (LSD-t) post-hoc tests. Variables not conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as median (M) and interquartile range (Q1, Q3); comparisons between two groups were conducted using Mann-Whitney U tests, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using Kruskal-Wallis H tests followed by Dunn post-hoc tests with Bonferroni correction. Categorical variables were presented as number (percentage) [n(%)], and group comparisons were conducted using χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact probability test. Trend tests for ordinal categorical variables were performed using linear trend χ2 tests.

For multivariate analysis, variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance were first calculated to diagnose multicollinearity (VIF < 5 and tolerance > 0.1 were considered acceptable). With caregiver burden level (1 = minimal or no burden, 2 = mild to moderate burden, 3 = moderate to severe burden, 4 = severe burden) as the dependent variable, variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the cumulative ordinal multinomial logistic regression model. Model diagnosis included: ① Test of Parallel Lines, where p ≥ 0.05 indicated that the proportional odds assumption was satisfied; ② Pearson and Deviance goodness-of-fit tests, where p ≥ 0.05 suggested a good model fit; ③ Calculation of pseudo R2 (Nagelkerke R2) to evaluate the degree of explained variance. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI). All tests were two-sided, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

Registration

This observational cross-sectional study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500100968; https://www.chictr.org.cn) during data collection, post first participant enrolment and prior to enrolment completion and database lock. We acknowledge that this is a retrospective registration, but no modifications to the study protocol were made after enrolment of the first participant and the research process.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 299 patient-caregiver pairs were included in this study, all assessed at two weeks after ICU discharge (Fig. 1).

Patient characteristics

Patients were predominantly diagnosed with surgical diseases (170, 56.9%), with a median age of 56 years (IQR: 43–68). Most had low educational attainment (189, 63.2% primary school or lower) and low monthly household income (199, 66.6%<3,000 yuan). 81.6% received mechanical ventilation during ICU stay, and the median APACHE II score at admission was 21 (IQR: 15–25) (Table 1).

Caregiver characteristics

Caregivers were mostly middle-aged men (60.5%) residing in rural areas. 80.6% were unpaid family caregivers living with the patient. Over one-third reported additional life stressors (38.1%), and 36.1% and 31.8% experienced negative impacts on their daily life and work, respectively (Table 1).

Overall caregiver burden

The ZBI-22 scores of 299 caregivers ranged from 12 to 79 points, with a median of 38.0 points (IQR: 25.0–52.0), indicating an overall mild-to-moderate burden. Notably, 39.8% of caregivers experienced moderate-to-severe or severe burden.

Univariate analysis of caregiver burden

Since the caregiver burden score was not normally distributed, the Kruskal-Wallis H test identified 28 factors associated with this score (p < 0.05)(Table 2). To avoid missing any potential related variables, the score was converted into a 4-level severity category; subsequently, 18 factors associated with burden severity were confirmed using chi-square/Fisher’s exact test. After Bonferroni correction (p < 0.000877), 11 variables remained statistically significant: patient’s age, residence, ICU LOS, need for full-time care at 2 weeks after ICU discharge, first admission to ICU, ability to return to work, and caregiver’s marital status, living situation, living with the patient, additional stressors, and patient’s prognosis is as expected (Table 3).

Ordinal multivariate logistic regression analysis of caregiver burden

Taking the severity of caregiver burden (graded 1–4) as the dependent variable, two ordered logistic models were constructed sequentially:① Model 1 included 11 variables that remained significant after Bonferroni correction. Tolerance > 0.1 and VIF < 5 excluded multicollinearity. Both the parallel-lines and Pearson tests yielded p > 0.05, confirming the proportional-odds assumption and adequate fit. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically analyze family caregiver burden at the clinically critical two-week window following ICU discharge of patients with PICS. This early transition period, explicitly recommended by the 2020 SCCM International Consensus Conference on PICS as the standardized timepoint for early post-discharge follow-up, represents a unique and understudied phase where the full impact of ICU-related impairments first manifests at home. Research results show that 39.8% of caregivers experienced moderate to severe or higher levels of burden, a proportion significantly higher than the reported levels among caregivers of diabetes patients (27%) and stroke patients (32.7%) [21, 22]. This indicates that the early caregiving pressure faced by family caregivers of PICS patients cannot be overlooked. Initially, the study used 11 adjusted variables for regression analysis; however, the model fit was poor (p < 0.05). This may be due to overly strict correction during the variable selection process, leading to the loss of key information. After incorporating all 28 single variables, the model fit was significantly improved (p > 0.05), indicating that caregiver burden is the result of the combined action of multiple-dimensional factors, which is consistent with other research findings [23]. Strict correction of variables may have missed some potential influencing factors, particularly the interactions between patients’ clinical characteristics and caregivers’ social attributes.

The study found that caregivers aged ≤ 45 years had a significantly higher risk of burden (OR = 3.196), which is consistent with previous findings that “middle-aged caregivers bear the dual pressure of family responsibilities” [24, 25]. Caregivers in this age group are mostly in the critical stage of career development and simultaneously face the dual tasks of caring for patients and raising children; conflicts between work and care easily exacerbate their burden. Caregivers living in rural areas had a heavier burden (OR = 2.894), which may be related to the lack of medical resources, inconvenient transportation (rural caregivers in this study were an average of 102.04 km away from the hospital), and insufficient rehabilitation support in rural areas, a point confirmed by many studies [25–28]. Additionally, 80.6% of rural families without hired caregivers had to bear all care responsibilities independently, further intensifying their burden [29, 30]. Furthermore, married/cohabiting status was a protective factor (OR = 0.010). It is hypothesized that emotional support and division of labor between spouses can buffer part of the care pressure, while single or divorced caregivers are more likely to fall into a burden dilemma due to a lack of emotional support [31], which is consistent with the results of a study on dementia family caregivers in Malaysia [32]. This suggests that the integrity of the family support system is a crucial foundation for reducing the burden [33]. In this study, the odds ratio for “Living alone” was high (OR = 26.346, 95% CI = 3.348–207.20), suggesting a potential association with increased burden; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. The very wide confidence interval is attributable to the small number of caregivers living alone in our sample (n = 18, 6.0%), which limits the statistical precision of the estimate.

ICU length of stay ≤ 30 days was an important protective factor, and the shorter the stay, the lighter the burden, confirming the hypothesis that “shortening ICU length of stay can reduce family care pressure” [34]. However, it is worth noting that 81.6% of patients in this study received mechanical ventilation; such patients often experience long-term sequelae, including respiratory dysfunction [35, 36]. Even with a short hospital stay, the complexity of post-discharge care remains high. Caregivers of patients admitted to the ICU for the first time experience a lighter burden (OR = 0.289). As shown in Zhang Hongwei’s research [37], the SOFA score during the initial ICU admission is positively correlated with 90-day prognosis; better patient outcomes reduce the caregivers’ burden. Meanwhile, family members of patients with multiple admissions are prone to accumulate traumatic stress due to repeatedly experiencing crises [38]. The phenomenon that “patients not returning to work (OR = 3.019) exacerbates caregiver burden” reflects the dual impact of the loss of the family’s economic pillar on caregivers: they must not only undertake care tasks but also face economic pressure from reduced income [39]. Additionally, 74.9% of caregivers reported that medical expenses exceeded their financial capabilities, resulting in a “double burden” effect of economic pressure and caregiving tasks, which is consistent with the results of breast cancer survivor studies [40].

“Need for Full-Time Care at 2 Weeks After ICU Discharge” was unexpectedly a risk factor for burden (OR = 6.844). This result is somewhat inconsistent with the research by Toby O Smith [41], which suggests that caregivers who have received professional care training experience a reduced burden. However, our study may indicate that patients often have more severe conditions and higher care needs; even with partial professional support, family members of such patients still need to bear the primary caregiving responsibilities. Moreover, improper coordination between professional nursing and family care may increase the coordination burden [42]. Both cohabitation with the patient (OR = 3.116) and cohabitation with spouse and children (OR = 6.758) exacerbated burden, reflecting the dilemma of single care resources in nuclear families [39]. Although cohabitation facilitates care implementation, the lack of a rotation mechanism easily leads to long-term caregiver fatigue; furthermore, the family structure of “supporting both the elderly and raising children” [43] further compresses the self-regulation space of caregivers, highlighting the urgent need for external support resources.

Caregivers with other stressors had a 1.2-fold increased risk of burden (OR = 2.228), suggesting a superimposed effect of care stress and life stress—consistent with the mechanism of “multiple stressors reducing psychological resilience” in PICS-F [8]. In contrast, patient prognosis consistent with expectations (OR = 0.399) was a protective factor, emphasizing the importance of doctor-patient communication in reducing care burden. Clear rehabilitation expectations can reduce caregivers’ anxiety caused by uncertainty, providing a basis for early prognostic communication in clinical practice [44].

Limitations

Although this study systematically analyzed the factors influencing family caregiver burden among relatives of patients diagnosed with PICS at two weeks post-discharge, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, the study design lacked a comparator group of caregivers of ICU survivors without PICS. Consequently, we cannot establish a causal relationship between PICS itself and caregiver burden, nor can we determine whether the observed burden levels are specifically attributable to PICS rather than general post-ICU recovery challenges. Our findings therefore describe the distribution and correlates of burden within the PICS caregiver population during a critical early transition period, rather than demonstrating that PICS uniquely causes greater burden compared to non-PICS ICU survivors.

Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary ICU in Guizhou Province, and 59.9% of participating caregivers resided in rural areas. This geographic and institutional concentration limits the generalizability of our findings to urban settings or regions with more robust post-ICU support systems. The observed burden profile may reflect the specific socioeconomic and healthcare access conditions of rural southwestern China rather than broader national or global contexts.

Third, the cross-sectional design only captures the burden of caregivers at a single point in time. Prior evidence suggests that caregiver burden often peaks around three months after ICU discharge, coinciding with the emergence of persistent neurocognitive and psychological symptoms [41]. Our single assessment cannot capture the dynamic trajectory of burden over time, nor can it distinguish transient stress reactions from chronic strain.

Fourth, our inclusion criteria required patients to be able to complete the screening questionnaire and communicate in Chinese, which likely excluded individuals with severe cognitive impairment, profound physical disability, communication disorders, or unstable mental health conditions. As these excluded patients are among the most severely affected by PICS, their caregivers presumably face even greater caregiving burden, and omitting them may have reduced the overall observed burden level in our sample. This selection bias could therefore lead to an underestimation of the true burden magnitude in the broader PICS population. Future studies should aim to include more diverse patient groups to better reflect the full spectrum of caregiver experiences. Additionally, while the PICS assessment scale includes items on mood and anxiety symptoms, we did not use a dedicated validated tool to comprehensively evaluate pre-existing or current mental health conditions, which limits our ability to fully explore their impact on caregiver burden. Furthermore, in this study, the presence of “additional stressors” was identified as a significant predictor of caregiver burden. However, these stressors were only presented as binary yes/no variables, and the research did not delve into detailed information about specific stressors. Future studies are recommended to adopt qualitative or mixed-methods designs to explore the long-term impact of specific stressors on caregiver well-being.

Collectively, these limitations underscore the necessity for future multicenter longitudinal studies that enroll both PICS and non-PICS ICU survivor cohorts drawn from a wide range of urban and rural as well as socioeconomic settings, and that track caregiver outcomes repeatedly for a minimum of six months after discharge.

Conclusion

We focused on family caregivers of patients with post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) and observed that most experience mild to moderate burden within two weeks after discharge. Through multivariate logistic regression analysis, we identified the key risk and protective factors associated with this burden. This work addresses the gap in early-stage evidence regarding caregiver burden in the context of PICS, confirms the multidimensional nature of its influencing factors, and provides robust evidence to support future related clinical research and targeted interventions.
