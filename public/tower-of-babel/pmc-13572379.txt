Safety and immunogenicity of inactivated human Rotavirus vaccine in young children and infants: a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, phase I clinical trial
Authors: Yan Zhou, Jiebing Tan, Yan Liu, Jing Pu, Jinyuan Wu, Xinling Liu, Shan Yi, Xiaoqing Hu, Rong Chen, Jun Ye, Na Yin, Xiaochen Lin, Jiamei Gao, Qiumeng Tong, Xiangjing Kuang, Yongli Yang, Weiping Zhang, Yan Wang, Maosheng Sun, Guangming Zhang, Yanxia Wang, Jingsi Yang, Zhongyu Hu, Hongjun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inactivated rotavirus vaccines (IRV) are an effective approach for providing protection against the virus. This double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled, dose-escalation phase I trial evaluated the safety and immunogenicity of IRV in 288 healthy children aged 2–71 months without prior rotavirus vaccination or HIV infection. We stratified participants by age (7–71 months and 2–6 months) and schedule (2 or 3 doses), before randomising them 3:1 to receive one of three antigen doses (80, 160, or 320 ELISA Unit [EU]), or aluminium adjuvanted placebo. The primary endpoints included adverse reactions/events within 30 min post-dose, adverse reactions/events during days 0–7 and 8–28/30 post-dose, and serious adverse events (SAEs) 6 months after the full course. The secondary endpoints were neutralizing antibody (NTA) geometric mean titres (GMT) and seroconversion rates (≥4-fold rise) at day 28 post-final dose. IRV was well tolerated, with no vaccine-related SAEs in any of the cohorts; we observed clinically manageable transient mild-to-moderate fever in the high dose infant group. Dose-dependent increases in NTA and IgG antibody responses were observed across all cohorts. In the 3-dose infant group, the 320 EU dose yielded a GMT of 551.98 (95% CI 338.39 ~ 900.40) compared to 36.17 for placebo, and a seroconversion rate of 83.33% (vs. 10.00% for placebo); the 3-dose schedule outperformed the 2-dose regimen. Our findings confirm that IRV has a favourable safety and immunogenicity profile. This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04626856).

Introduction

Diarrhea is the second leading cause of mortality in children <5 years of age, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), causing approximately 500,000 deaths annually in infants and young children1. Rotavirus, which belongs to the Reoviridae family, is the major cause of rotavirus gastroenteritis in children <5 years of age worldwide, resulting in 120,000–220,000 deaths annually2,3. Specific treatments are not available for rotavirus infection, and vaccination is the most cost-effective preventive method.

Eight live rotavirus vaccines (LRVs) have so far been approved, including Rotarix (GlaxoSmithKline Biologicals, Rixensart, Belgium), RotaTeq (Merck & Co. Inc., Whitehouse Station, NJ, USA), ROTASIIL (Serum Institute of India, India), Rotavac (Bharat Biotech International Ltd., India), Lanzhou lamb rotavirus vaccine (LLR, Lanzhou Institute of Biomedical Products, Lanzhou, China), ROTALAN (LLR3, Lanzhou Institute of Biomedical Products, Lanzhou, China), Wusheng Erlunbao (RV6, Wuhan Institute of Biological Products, Wuhan, China) and Rotavin-M1 (Polyvac, Vietnam). Rotarix, RotaTeq, Rotavac, and ROTASIIL are prequalified by the WHO. Rotavirus vaccines in China and Rotavin-M1 in Vietnam are approved for use in vaccine-producing countries. In China, all licensed rotavirus vaccines are available as self-paid vaccines in both public and private vaccination settings4–6. The data collected by the Global Rotavirus Surveillance Network showed that the prevalence of rotavirus decreased by 39.6% after the introduction of a live-attenuated rotavirus vaccine in the national immunization program. In addition, rotavirus hospitalization, all-cause acute gastroenteritis hospitalization, and gastroenteritis mortality decreased in many countries7. LRVs have played a crucial role in alleviating the burden of severe disease and death caused by rotavirus gastroenteritis.

Nonetheless, the performance of live oral rotavirus vaccines (ORVs) is relatively poor in LMICs. In high-income countries, rotavirus vaccines provide 80%–90% protection against severe disease in young children, which drops to 50%–60% in LMICs8. Several factors have been hypothesized to be responsible for their lower efficacy, including differences in histo–blood group antigens, coinfection with other enteric pathogens, rotavirus antibodies from the placenta or breast milk4, interference from simultaneous oral polio vaccine immunization, stomach acid9, the microbiome, and other enteric pathogens10–12. However, the exact reason for the reduced effectiveness of oral vaccines in LMICs remains to be determined. Furthermore, clinical findings have shown that LRVs cause intussusception in vaccinated infants. Therefore, new vaccines, alternative immunization strategies such as parenteral vaccine immunization combination with ORVs, and new systems to deliver viral antigens are required to overcome the barriers to effectiveness and safety13.

Since 2010, we have been attempting to develop a nonreplicating rotavirus vaccine candidate named the inactivated rotavirus vaccine (IRV) (Vero cells). It is a fully IRV strain (ZTR-68-A, G1P[8]) with good immunogenicity in mice14 and rhesus macaques15,16. Moreover, the vaccine was well-tolerated and showed promising immunogenicity when administered intramuscularly on Day 0 and Day 28 to adults and adolescents in Phase Ⅰ clinical trials17,18. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and tolerability of an IRV in healthy Chinese infants (2–6 months) and young children (7–71 months) and to assess its dose-escalation immunogenicity in these cohorts.

Results

Trial participants

A total of 288 eligible participants were enrolled and followed up from December 3, 2020, to February 8, 2022. Of these, 92 young children (22 in the 80 ELISA unit (EU), 23 in the 160 EU, 23 in the 320 EU, and 24 in the placebo groups) received a second dose on Day 28 and completed the Day-56 safety assessment; 93 infants received a second dose on Day 28 and completed the Day-56 safety assessment; and 87 infants received a third dose on Day 56 and completed the Day-84 safety assessment (Fig. 1). Safety analysis

In the 7–71 months cohort, 96 young children who received at least one dose of the vaccine or the placebo were included in the safety analysis. A total of 71 AEs (28 vaccine-related) were recorded within 0–30 days after completing the vaccination. The overall proportions of ARs in the placebo, low-dose, medium-dose, and high-dose groups were 8 (33%, 15.63–55.32) of 24, 4 (17%, 4.74–37.38) of 24, 8 (33%, 15.63–55.32) of 24, and 8 (33%,15.63–55.32) of 24, respectively (Supplementary Table 7). No significant differences were observed between the trial groups and the placebo group in terms of local/systemic AE, total AE, or AR proportions. Similarly, no statistically significant differences were noted across the various dose groups. ARs within 28 days postvaccination (Table 2) predominantly involved injection site pain, cough, and fever, whose proportions were nonsignificant. Details of AEs are furnished in the supplementary Data 1. The reactions were generally common and limited to Grade 1 severity. Six Grade 3 AEs occurred in the 7–71 months age group postvaccination, including one case of placebo-related fever on Day 7 post–first vaccination. The proportions of Grade 3 ARs were 4% for placebo and 0% for low/medium/high dose (Supplementary Table 14). Six unrelated SAEs were reported within 28 days post-injection (one in the placebo, two in the 80 EU, one in the 160 EU, and two in the 320 EU groups), with no significant differences in the SAE rate In the 2–6 months age cohort (2-dose), 96 infants who received at least one dose of the vaccine or the placebo were included in the safety analysis. A total of 52 AEs (34 vaccine-related) were recorded within 0–30 days after completing the vaccination. The overall proportions of ARs in the placebo, low-dose, medium-dose, and high-dose groups were 5 (21%, 7.13–42.15) of 24, 6 (25%, 9.77–46.71) of 24, 8 (33%, 15.63–55.32) of 24, and 15 (63%, 40.59–81.20) of 24, respectively (Supplementary Table 8). No significant differences in local/systemic/total AEs or ARs were observed between low/medium-dose and placebo groups. Nonetheless, the high-dose group exhibited significantly higher proportions of systemic ARs (p = 0.002), total AEs (p = 0.039), and total ARs (p = 0.003) compared with the placebo. ARs until Day 28 (Table 2) primarily comprised injection site erythema, cough, fever, and diarrhea, with the proportion of fever being considerably higher in the high-dose group. Most reactions were of Grade 1–2 severity (Grade 1: 21% (80 EU), 13% (160 EU), 33% (320 EU), and 21% (placebo); Grade 2: 4% (80 EU), 29% (160 EU), 33% (320 EU), and 4% (placebo)), although one high-dose recipient developed Grade 3-related fever on Day 1 postvaccination (Supplementary Table 15). No significant between-group differences occurred in Grade 3 AE proportions. Four vaccine-unrelated SAEs recorded within 30 days postvaccination showed no significant differences in SAE proportions across groups (complete AE details: Supplementary Data 2).

In the 2–6 months (3-dose) cohort, a total of 96 infants who received at least one dose of the vaccine or the placebo were included in the safety analysis. During the 0–30-day post–full vaccination period, 76 AEs were recorded, including 50 vaccine-related cases. The overall proportions of ARs in the placebo, low-dose, medium-dose, and high-dose groups were 11 (46%, 25.55–67.18) of 24, 9 (38%, 18.80–59.41) of 24, 13 (54%, 32.82–74.45) of 24, and 17 (71%, 48.91–87.38) of 24, respectively (Supplementary Table 9). No significant differences in local/systemic/total AEs or ARs were evident between the low/medium-dose and placebo groups. Vaccine-related ARs until Day 28 postvaccination primarily encompassed injection site erythema, cough, fever, diarrhea, and vomiting (Table 2). The high-dose group exhibited a significantly greater incidence of Grade 1 fever compared with the placebo group (50% (29.12–70.88) vs. 21% (7.13–42.15); p = 0.035) (Supplementary Data 3). Most total reactions were Grade 1 (80 EU: 4/24 (17%, 4.74–37.38), 160 EU: 9/24 (38%, 18.80–59.41), 320 EU: 12/24 (50%, 29.12–70.88), placebo: 7/24 (29%, 12.62–51.09)) or Grade 2 (80 EU: 6/24 (25%, 9.77–46.71), 160 EU: 8/24 (33%, 15.63–55.32), 320 EU: 12/24 (50%, 29.12–70.88), placebo: 8/24 (33%, 15.63–55.32)) (Supplementary Table 16). Four Grade 3 AEs occurred, which included one vaccine-related fever in a medium-dose recipient on Day 1 post-dose 3 (Supplementary Table 9). No significant between-group differences were observed in the proportions of Grade 3 AEs. Seven SAEs occurred within 30 days postvaccination, of which one placebo-group case (functional gastrointestinal disorder on Day 1 post-dose 1) was assessed as vaccine-related (leading to discontinuation). The SAE proportion showed no significant dose-group differences (complete AE data: Supplementary Data 3).

Immunogenicity analysis

A total of 81 young children (22 in the placebo, 20 in the 80 EU, 19 in the 160 EU, and 20 in the 320 EU groups) received two doses of either the vaccine or the placebo and were included in the immunogenicity analysis. The remaining 15 were excluded for the reasons shown in Fig. 1. The GMT values of prevaccination NTA (NTA) for the 80 EU, 160 EU, 320 EU, and placebo groups were 181.74, 270.62, 423.23, and 218.89, respectively (Table 3, Supplementary Table 18). After 2-dose injection, the GMT values of NTA in the 80 EU, 160 EU, 320 EU, and placebo groups were 495.18 (192.20–1275.79), 782.07 (590.07–1036.53), 1147.95 (610.31–2159.24), and 269.46 (111.96–648.52), respectively. (Table 3, Supplementary Table 24). Adjusted NTA seroconversion proportions were 5 (25%) of 20 (95% CI 8.66–49.10) in the 80 EU, 5 (26%) of 19 (9.15–51.20) in the 160 EU, and 8 (40%) of 20 (19.12–63.95) in the 320 EU groups compared with 2 (9%) of 22 (1.12–29.16) in the placebo group (Table 3, Supplementary Table 27). A total of 76 infants (16 in the placebo, 21 in the 80 EU, 17 in the 160 EU, and 22 in the 320 EU groups), who received two doses of either the vaccine or the placebo, were included in the immunogenicity analysis. The remaining 20 were excluded for the reasons shown in Fig. 1. The GMT values of prevaccination NTA (NTA) for the 80 EU, 160 EU, 320 EU, and placebo groups were 50.98, 32.83, 41.09, and 19.59, respectively (Table 3, Supplementary Table 18). After 2-dose injection, the GMT of NTA in the 80 EU, 160 EU, 320 EU, and placebo groups were 103.63 (58.05–185.03), 103.66 (57.30–187.55), 192.06 (100.92–365.52), and 25.07(12.32–51.03), respectively (Table 3, Supplementary Table 24). Adjusted NTA seroconversion proportions were 2 (10%) of 21 (95% CI 1.17–30.38) in the 80 EU, 7 (41%) of 17 (18.44–67.08) in the 160 EU, and 12 (55%) of 22 (32.21–75.61) in the 320 EU groups compared with 1 (6%) of 16 (0.16–30.23) in the placebo group (Table 3, Supplementary Table 27). A total of 64 infants (20 in the placebo, 15 in the 80 EU, 17 in the 160 EU, and 12 in the 320 EU groups) who received three doses of either the vaccine or the placebo was included in the immunogenicity analysis. The remaining 32 were excluded for the reasons shown in Fig. 1. The GMT values of prevaccination NTA (NTA) for the 80 EU, 160 EU, 320 EU, and placebo groups were 23.11, 31.94, 22.74, and 29.67, respectively (Table 3, Supplementary Table 18). After 3-dose injection, the GMT values of NTA in the 80 EU, 160 EU, 320 EU, and placebo groups were 114.32 (58.84–222.14), 173.36 (86.24–348.48), 551.98 (338.39–900.40), and 36.17 (15.56–84.08), respectively (Table 3, Supplementary Table 24). Adjusted NTA seroconversion proportions were 8 (53%) of 15 (26.59–78.73) in the 80 EU, 9 (53%) of 17 (27.81–77.02) in the 160 EU, and 10 (83%) of 12 (51.59–97.91) in the 320 EU groups compared with 2 (10%) of 20 (1.23–31.70) in the placebo group (Table 3, Supplementary Table 27). Significant statistical differences were observed in the adjusted NTA seroconversion rate of the 80 EU group compared with the placebo group (p = 0.008), as presented in Table 3. Discussion

This study examines the safety and immunogenicity of a nonreplicating, whole-virus inactivated vaccine to prevent rotavirus-induced diarrhea in children. IRVs were well tolerated in 2–71-month-old children. In young children aged 7–71 months, no statistically significant differences were observed between the 80 EU, 160 EU, and 320 EU vaccine groups and the placebo group in the proportions of total reactions within 28 days postvaccination course completion, local reactions, vaccine-related Grade ≥3 AEs, and SAEs throughout the study period. In infants aged 2–6 months, no statistically significant differences were seen between the 80 EU, 160 EU, and 320 EU vaccine groups and the placebo group in the proportions of total vaccine-related AEs within 28 days after the full course of vaccination, local reactions, and Grade ≥3 vaccine-related AEs throughout the study period. In the high-dose group, an increased frequency of Grade 1 and 2 fever was observed compared with the placebo group under the 3-dose vaccination schedule. However, these febrile events were predominantly mild to moderate in severity, transient, and resolved without sequelae. No serious adverse events were considered related to vaccination. The safety profile of the high-dose group, while notable, remained within acceptable limits for a phase I trial. Whether fever should be considered a safety concern requires validation in future clinical trials. Overall, the AEs in each age group were mainly Grade 1 and Grade 2, signifying that the safety profile of the trial vaccine is within the acceptable range.

All young children aged 7–71 months enrolled in this study were NTA seropositive across all vaccination dose cohorts. Infants aged 2–6 months displayed a preimmunization NTA seropositivity of >75%. Statistically significant differences were not evident in NTA seropositivity proportions or GMTs of prevaccination between adjacent dose groups (low vs. placebo, medium vs. low, and high vs. medium). Young children aged 7–71 months demonstrated higher preimmunization GMT levels of NTA than infants aged 2–6 months. Despite differences in preimmunization NTA GMT levels between children aged 7–71 months and infants aged 2–6 months, postimmunization neutralizing antibodies showed a dose-dependent increase in both groups. Cellular immune profiling revealed that only the medium-dose group had a substantial increase in the absolute counts of CD3⁻CD16⁺CD56⁺ NK cells and their subsets compared with baseline. The constraint of blood sampling sites led to a delay in the timing of cellular immunity assays, potentially contributing to the attenuated cellular immune response. The role of IRV immunization in inducing NK cell-mediated immune responses must be explored further.

Twenty-eight days post–full immunization, all age cohorts showed substantially higher NTA GMT levels than the placebo group. Across all age groups, the medium-dose group exhibited elevated NTA and IgG GMT values compared with the low-dose group. Nonetheless, no statistically significant differences appeared in seropositivity or seroconversion proportions. Within each age group, the high-dose group exhibited significantly higher NTA/IgG seroconversion proportions and GMT values than the medium-dose group. In addition, the high-dose regimen elicited stronger immune responses in infants and young children. In all groups, NTA and IgG responses revealed a significant dose–response effect. It is worth noting that no statistically significant dose-response association was observed between vaccine dosage and IgA levels in infants aged 2-6 months, whereas such a relationship was evident in children aged 7-71 months. This difference likely reflects the lack of prior rotavirus exposure and immature mucosal priming in younger infants, rather than inadequate protection. Maternal antibodies from breastfeeding may also contribute, but they would be expected to affect IgG responses similarly; however, this was not observed for IgG.

The correlate of protection (CoP) for rotavirus immunity remains incompletely defined. For live oral rotavirus vaccines, serum IgA is widely considered the primary CoP, yet clinical trial data indicate it explains only 32.7%–43.6% of vaccine efficacy, with substantial protection observed even in IgA-seronegative recipients19,20. Epidemiological studies in low-income countries suggest IgA contributes only about 7.8% to vaccine-induced protection, with IgG and IgM playing compensatory roles21. Studies in IgA-deficient mouse models have further shown that α4β7 integrin-mediated B cell homing to the intestine represents an IgA-independent mechanism of protective humoral immunity, supporting the view that serum IgA serves more as a marker of immune response than an essential mediator of protection22. Notably, a 2024 review proposed that IgG may be a more relevant CoP for parenteral non-replicating rotavirus vaccines, a finding particularly relevant to our inactivated rotavirus vaccine (IRV)23. In this study, IRV primarily induced high levels of IgG and neutralizing antibodies, along with low-level IgA responses that reached the protective threshold (≥20 U/mL) in a subset of infants. Despite these findings, further molecular immunological studies and large-scale phase III trials are required to validate whether IgG and neutralizing antibodies can serve as reliable correlates of protection (CoPs) for IRV.

Two-dose vaccination failed to evoke a strong immune response in infants aged 2–6 months, which could be attributed to their immature immune systems. Three-dose immunization with high-dose (320 EU) IRV considerably augmented NTA and IgG levels in the 2–6 months age group, with notably higher antibody seroconversion proportions. However, owing to sample size limitations in Phase Ⅰ, the robust immunogenicity observed in the 320 EU dose should be validated in Phase Ⅱ trials. A 3-dose immunization arm for the 7–71-month age group was not included because of sample size constraints. Hence, the optimal immunization schedule for children aged 7–71 months remains undefined. Dose-ranging studies must be included in future Phase II trials to prioritize establishing appropriate target populations and vaccination strategies for IRV.

Our study also had some limitations. The per-protocol set for the high-dose, three-dose group included only 12 infants. This was largely because enrollment for this group coincided with a peak season of upper respiratory infections in young children. Several infants developed colds during the study period, which led to delayed vaccinations or missed blood sampling windows and therefore to their exclusion from the per-protocol analysis according to the prespecified criteria. As a result, the sample size for this subgroup was limited. That said, the findings across the other dose groups were generally consistent, which supports the overall conclusions. Nevertheless, the immunogenicity results for the three-dose schedule in infants aged 2–6 months need to be further validated in a phase II trial.

The LRV has played a significant role in preventing gastroenteritis caused by rotavirus infection–induced gastroenteritis. According to the findings of an epidemiological study, RV positivity was the highest in children aged 6 months to 2 years in Western China24. Approximately 10% of children aged 24–71 months are susceptible to rotavirus infection or disease, emphasizing an unmet need for enhanced vaccination coverage. During years of live vaccine use, intussusception has remained the greatest safety risk25,26. The World Health Organization expanded its recommendations in 2013, stating that the rotavirus vaccine dose can be administered after 6 weeks of age and that it could be administered to children up to 24 months of age27. Nevertheless, the package inserts for Rotarix® and RotaTeq®, the two market-leading rotavirus vaccines, still specify that 32 weeks of age should be completed. Clearly, in such settings, current live oral vaccines alone are insufficient to entirely control rotavirus disease in children <5 years of age28. Next-generation rotavirus vaccines must be explored. The inactivated rotavirus vaccine IRV9 developed by the US CDC demonstrated broad cross-protection against multiple G-serotype rotaviruses in the gnotobiotic piglet model, while both the virus-like particle vaccine from Baylor College of Medicine and the VP6 protein vaccine from Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center showed good immunogenicity in animal models29–33.PATH has been developing a rotavirus vaccine named parenteral trivalent P2-VP8 subunit vaccine since 2011. Clinical trials have indicated that the vaccine is safe, and vaccinated infants have elicited strong IgG and NTA responses34–36. However, the development of this vaccine has been halted due to poor performance in the Phase Ⅲ trial (NCT04010448). This scenario highlights the translational challenges in developing nonreplicating rotavirus vaccine platforms. Nevertheless, unlike subunit vaccines, IRVs, as inactivated whole-virus nonreplicating vaccines, theoretically eliminate the risk of intussusception and potentially offer broader protection. These vaccines protect both the conventional target population of live vaccines and the previously underserved 7–71-month age group. However, its specific protective efficacy must be validated in future Phase Ⅲ clinical trials involving larger cohorts. Unlike many other countries, China has not included rotavirus vaccines in the National Immunization Program, and the currently available oral vaccines have a narrow age window and variable efficacy. The findings of this phase I trial provide a basis for considering an inactivated rotavirus vaccine across a broader age range (2–71 months) and support the feasibility of more flexible dosing schedules.

To effectively control rotavirus infection–induced diarrhea, a sequential immunization strategy similar to the current inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPV) approach is needed. The IRV should be combined with live vaccines to augment the protection for infants and young children. Using multivalent combined vaccines can streamline vaccination procedures. Immunization coverage can be enhanced, supply chain and cold-storage management challenges can be eased, and the vaccination time of healthcare facilities can be reduced. In the future, combining the IRV with other injectable vaccines, such as the IPV, or combination vaccines, such as pentavalent or hexavalent vaccines, is a highly promising immunization approach.

In conclusion, IRV demonstrates favourable safety and immunogenicity in infants and young children aged 2–71 months, laying the foundation for future Phase II clinical trials. Specifically, the 3-dose immunization strategy using a 320 EU dose of IRV should be further explored and validated in Phase II clinical trials.

Methods

Ethics statement

All procedures of this clinical trial complied with relevant ethical regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Henan Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (Ethics approval number: 2020-YM-004-02). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all minor participants prior to enrolment. Consistent with applicable regulatory requirements, nutritional and transportation compensation was offered to participants, with full disclosure of all relevant compensation details provided to legal guardians during the informed consent procedure.

Study design

This double-blinded, randomized, placebo-controlled Phase Ⅰ dose-escalation trial assessed IRV tolerability and immunogenicity at Suixian County CDC (Henan, China). The study was registered with ClinicalTrial.gov, NCT04626856. The study complied with the China Good Clinical Practice guidelines. A Data Safety Monitoring Committee (DSMB) was established for trial oversight and risk assessment. The participants were stratified into three cohorts by their age and regimen: 96 young children aged 7–71 months (2-dose regimen), 96 infants aged 2–6 months (2-dose regimen), and 96 infants aged 2–6 months (3-dose regimen). Three cohorts were enrolled separately. The dose-escalation was designed first in young children and then in infants. All cohorts underwent antigen dose-escalation (low: 80, medium: 160, high: 320 ELISA Unit (EU)/dose). Progression from one dose to the next and from one cohort to the next required a review by DSMB up to 7 days following the first injection at each dose or in each age group. Enrollment in the next trial vaccine dose group or the next age group was required when the initial safety assessment met the protocol with no vaccine-related Grade 4 serious adverse reactions (ARs) or Suspected Unexpected Serious ARs (SUSARs), or the number of ARs with Grade 3 of severity did not exceed 15% of the vaccinated subjects in this group (Supplementary pp 49-51). For the 7–71-month (2-dose regimen) and 2–6-month (2-dose regimen) groups, antibody samples were collected at: Day 0, 14, 28, 42, 56, 112, and 196. For the 2–6-month (3-dose regimen) group, antibody samples were collected at: Day 0, 56, 84, 146, and 224. Cellular immunity (lymphocyte counts and cytokines) was assessed only in the 7–71-month group, with samples taken at Day 0 and Day 42.

Participants

Eligibility was assessed through medical history and physical examination. Healthy young children (aged 7–71 months) and infants (aged 2–6 months) who provided voluntary informed consent (guardian-signed) with protocol compliance commitment and without previous receipt of rotavirus vaccination were enrolled. In this study, eligibility was determined based on the infant’s chronological age in natural months. No additional variation was allowed beyond the defined age windows. The exclusion criteria included prevaccination axillary temperature >37.0°C; history of intussusception; congenital/developmental disorders or severe malnutrition; immunodeficiency (congenital/acquired including HIV, lymphoma, leukemia) or autoimmune diseases (SLE, JRA); neurological/psychiatric conditions (convulsions, epilepsy, cerebral palsy); vaccine allergy; acute illness/chronic disease exacerbation within 3 days; recent immunosuppressive therapy (>14 days within 3 months) or vaccination (live-attenuated ≤14 days; other ≤7 days); bleeding disorders; organ resection history (thyroid/pancreas/liver/spleen) or thyroid treatment within 12 months; concurrent clinical trial participation; or investigator-assessed unsuitability. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in the supplementary pp 51–53. None of the young children or infants enrolled in this study had previously received any live oral rotavirus vaccine. The age, gender, length, weight, and ethnicity data were collected through their legally authorized representatives. All children were enrolled if their parents/guardians were literate and had provided consent for them. Subjects in this study and the public were not involved in the study design, conduct, and reporting. Throughout the study, the participants received the IRV co-administered with other vaccines. These essential immunizations were administered on schedule, maintaining protocol-defined intervals for the study vaccine: 7 days for inactivated vaccines and 14 days for live-attenuated vaccines.

Randomization and masking

Stata 13.0 was used to generate computer-generated randomization sequences. Participants were stratified into three cohorts: the 7–71-month group, the 2–6-month (2-dose) group, and the 2–6-month (3-dose) group. Within each cohort, a sequential dose-escalation design was implemented. The first cohort was randomly assigned to receive either the 80 EU vaccine or placebo in a 3:1 ratio using block randomization with a fixed block size of eight (24 vaccine:8 placebo per dose group). Following DSMB review of safety data collected up to 7 days after the first injection, the second cohort was opened and randomly assigned to the 160 EU vaccine or placebo, also in a 3:1 ratio. The same process was repeated for the 320 EU dose cohort. Cumulatively, the final allocation ratio across the three vaccine dose groups and the placebo group was approximately 1:1:1:1. The Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, provided qualified vaccines (80 EU, 160 EU, 320 EU) and placebo. The three vaccine formulations and placebo used identical blinded packaging, distinguished only by a “Y + 4-digit” code. Subject IDs and randomization results were loaded into a central randomization system, which managed vaccine warehousing and allocation. The randomized grouping and vaccine assignment results were then linked using three-letter codes C, D, and E: C for those aged 7–71 months, D for those aged 2–6 months (2-dose schedule), and E for those aged 2–6 months (3-dose schedule). Staff involved in allocation concealment were not permitted to participate in the clinical trials, nor were they allowed to disclose any content regarding allocation concealment to any trial staff. All participants, guardians, clinical, data, and laboratory staff were blinded to treatment allocation.

Procedures

This trial was performed on 288 participants aged 2–71 months. Young children (aged 7–71 months) received an injection of either the vaccine or a placebo in the anterolateral mid-thigh or the deltoid muscle of the upper arm on Days 0 and 28. Infants (aged 2–6 months) received an injection of vaccine or placebo in the anterolateral midthigh or deltoid muscle of the upper arm on Days 0 and 28 or Days 0, 28, and 56. The first dose was administered immediately upon enrollment. The interval between doses was 28 days, with a permitted delay of up to 7 days for subsequent doses. All cohorts underwent antigen dose-escalation (80 EU, 160 EU, and 320 EU/ dose). For safety evaluation, all participants were observed for 30 min post-injection. Investigators monitored adverse events (AEs) from Days 0 to 7 post-vaccination via telephonic calls and in-person visits. The body temperatures and any AEs were recorded for all subjects on diary cards; these were then assessed for safety on Day 8. AE observation continued from Days 8 through 30, with comprehensive records maintained. The severity grading scale is detailed in the supplementary materials (Supplementary pp 53–57).

The participants were followed for up to 6 months post-full vaccination, during which only serious AEs (SAEs) were recorded. The occurrence of any one of the following events triggered a safety assessment by the independent Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) to determine trial suspension/termination: a confirmed vaccine-related Grade 4 serious AR, a SUSAR, or a Grade 3 AR affecting >15% of the vaccinated participants. For immunogenicity assessment, serum samples were collected at the baseline and then at 4 weeks after the final injection. The assays of NTA to ZTR-68-A (G1P[8]) and anti-RV IgG and IgA were described in the “Laboratory tests” section and on Supplementary pages 7–8. The rotavirus strain ZTR-68-A (G1P[8]) was isolated from a diarrheal stool sample of a 5-month-old infant in Zhaotong, Yunnan, China, in 2010. Testing was performed at the National Institutes for Food and Drug Control in Beijing, China, and the Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Science in Kunming, China. Statistical analyses were performed by the Computer and Health Statistics Unit, School of Public Health, Zhengzhou University.

Laboratory tests

A cell culture-integrated enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was employed to quantify neutralizing antibodies in serum samples. Briefly, MA104 cells were counted and seeded into 96-well microplates at a density of 1×105 cells per well, cultured until a confluent monolayer formed. Serum samples were two-fold serially diluted starting from 1:4, alongside positive serum control wells, negative serum control wells, virus back-titration wells, cell control wells, and virus control wells. The homotypic indicator virus was activated with trypsin (2.5%, GIBCO, 15090046) at a final concentration of 20 μg/mL for 1 h at 37 °C. The virus was subsequently neutralized using a 96-well plate: 50 µL of the viral indicator solution (100 CCID50/well, neutralization unit) was mixed with an equal volume of serially diluted serum and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Then, the neutralization products were cultured in a 5% CO₂ incubator at 37 °C for 7 days. After incubation, the plates were placed in a low-temperature refrigerator and subjected to two freeze-thaw cycles. After thawing, the contents of the neutralized plates were transferred to 96-well plates pre-coated with goat anti-RV antibody and incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour. Institute-produced HRP-labeled anti-rotavirus antibodies (diluted 1:2000, IMBCAMS, China) were added at 100 µL per well, followed by a 1-hour incubation at 37 °C to promote binding. The plates were then washed with PBST (Solarbio, China) using a plate washer, blotted dry with filter paper, and thoroughly air-dried before color development. Color development was initiated by adding 100 µL of TMB (3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine, Solarbio, China) to each well, followed by incubation in the dark at room temperature for 2–10 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 100 µL of 2 M H₂SO₄ per well. Absorbance values were measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 650 nm using a microplate reader. The cutoff value was calculated as 2.1 times the mean absorbance of control cell wells. A well was classified as positive for rotavirus neutralizing antibody when the detected value was below this threshold.

For IgG/IgA, microplates were coated with inactivated homotypic rotavirus overnight at 4 °C. After washing, 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) was added for blocking at 37 °C for 1 hour. Following PBS washes, two-fold serial dilutions of sera were added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour. Subsequently, HRP-conjugated donkey anti-human IgG antibody (diluted 1:12000, KPL, 709-035-149, Cambridge, UK) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-human IgA antibody (diluted 1:12000, Abcam, ab97215, UK) was added, followed by a 1-hour incubation at 37 °C. After three washes with PBS, the plates were developed with 100 μL/well of TMB substproportion (Solarbio, China) for 5 minutes in the dark, and the reaction was terminated with ELISA Stop Solution (Solarbio, China). Absorbance values were measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 650 nm using an EPOCH microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Absorbance values ≥ 0.195 were classified as positive for IgG antibodies, whereas those with absorbance values≥ cutoff value were designated as IgA antibody-positive. The cutoff value of IgA was calculated as 2.1 times the mean absorbance of control cell wells (the mean OD value of the blank control is less than 0.050, calculated as 0.050). The reciprocal of the highest positive dilution was defined as the antibody titer.

Outcomes

The primary endpoints across all three study groups included the proportions of any AEs/reactions occurring within 30 min post-injection; local and systemic reactions occurring within 7 days post-injection; AEs occurring within 28/30 days post-injection; SAEs occurring up to 6 months after the last injection. The secondary endpoints included the geometric mean titers (GMTs) of anti-rotavirus neutralizing antibodies (NTA) at 28 days post-final injection, and the proportion of infants achieving NTA seroconversion (defined as either an increase in antibody titer from <8 to ≥8 or a ≥ 4-fold rise from baseline at 4-week post-final vaccination). The exploratory endpoints included anti-rotavirus IgG/IgA GMTs at 28 days post-final injection, the proportion of infants achieving IgG/IgA seroconversion (defined as IgG: titer change from ≤16 to >16 or ≥4-fold increase; IgA: titer change from <8 to ≥8 or ≥4-fold increase), and lymphocyte subset (B lymphocyte, CD3+, CD4+, CD8+ T lymphocytes and NK cells)/cytokine profiles (interleukin-2 [IL-2] (UniProt:P60568),IL-4 (UniProt:P05112), IL-6 (UniProt:P05231), IL-10 (UniProt:P22301), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) (UniProt:P01375), interferon gamma (IFN-γ) (UniProt:P01579)) in 7–71-month-old participants. Considering the decay of maternal antibodies, the proportion of seroconversion and Geometric Mean Increase (GMI) for NTA and IgG titers was adjusted using half-life calculations derived from placebo-group participants with baseline titers higher than the post-injection levels, while maintaining the same seroconversion criteria as pre-adjustment.

Statistical analysis

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size” was included in the Statistical analysis section. Within each cohort, the participants were randomly allocated to receive either low-, medium-, or high-dose vaccine or placebo at a 3:1 ratio (24 vaccine:8 placebo per dose group). For analysis, placebo recipients from all 3-dose groups within each group were pooled, achieving a balanced 1:1:1:1 analytical ratio among the placebo, low-, medium-, and high-dose groups. The study followed unified standard operating procedures and utilized electronic data capture (EDC) systems (Version 2.4) for data collection within the same timeframe. All analysis was performed using SAS 9.4. Quantitative data were described using the mean, median, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum. Frequency and percentage were used to describe the qualitative data. The demographics and baseline characteristics of all subjects were summarized by groups. Confidence intervals (CIs) for event proportions were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson exact method, and proportion differences were estimated via normal approximation. For subjects aged 2–6 months and 7–71 months, monthly age was calculated as (enrollment date – date of birth)/365 × 12. No statistical testing was performed for the baseline variables.

The safety dataset included all subjects who received at least one dose of vaccine or placebo. AEs and ARs were enumerated in both the treatment groups, with proportions and 95% CIs computed. Adverse reaction classifications were presented as compositional ratios. Statistical comparisons between the vaccine and placebo groups within each age stratum, including categorized/symptom-specific AE/AR proportions, severity grade distributions, grade-specific proportions, and SAE proportions (overall and symptom-specific), employed Pearson’s chi-square, the corrected chi-square, or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate (Supplementary p21–31)27.

Subjects meeting all protocol eligibility criteria were included in the immunogenicity analyses. For each participant, blood samples were collected at the indicated time points. Each sample was tested independently. All antibody assays (neutralizing antibody, IgG, and IgA) were performed in duplicate wells, and the mean value of the two replicates was used for further analysis. The antibody titer was logarithmically transformed. Minimum, maximum, median, and quartile spacing, as well as GMT and 95% CI, were calculated. The antibody levels before immunization were summarized by groups. Post-vaccination analyses employed independent-samples t-tests (equal variances) or Welch’s t-tests (unequal variances) to compare GMTs of rotavirus NTA/IgG/IgA between sequential dose groups (low vs. placebo; medium vs. low; high vs. medium or low vs. placebo; medium vs. placebo; high vs. placebo) at specified time points; seroconversion and seropositivity proportions were compared using Pearson’s chi-square, the corrected chi-square, or Fisher’s exact tests. GMTs with 95% CIs were calculated for all vaccine groups per time-point/stratum. In cellular immunity analysis (7–71-month age group), pre-immunization levels were compared among all dose groups using rank-sum tests, whereas post-immunization (Day 14 post-dose 2) levels were analyzed using rank-sum tests between adjacent dose groups; within-group pre/post-immunization changes were analyzed by Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. To compensate for the simultaneous decline of maternal antibodies alongside the IgG and NTA immune responses induced by the vaccine in infants, NTA and IgG antibody post-injection titers were adjusted for decay in maternal antibodies using the half-life calculations derived from placebo-group participants with baseline titers higher than the post-injection levels (Supplementary pp 4–6)34.
