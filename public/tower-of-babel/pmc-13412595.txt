Topical Mongolian Lycium ruthenicum extract attenuates oxidative stress, inflammation, and itch signaling in atopic dermatitis models
Authors: Himeno Saito, Chizuki Usui, Reo Matsuzaka, Chiharu Ohira, Mao Kaneki, Yoshiichi Takagi, Yoshikazu Hoshino, Tomoki Fukuyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMongolian Lycium ruthenicum (LR) are rich in anthocyanins and oligomeric proanthocyanidins, which possess potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. However, their therapeutic potential in inflammatory skin diseases, such as atopic dermatitis (AD), remains unclear. This study investigated the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of topical LR extract and its therapeutic potential in experimental models of AD. Antioxidant activity was assessed in UVB-irradiated human keratinocytes (HaCaT) by measuring intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the expression of Nrf2 and HO-1. Anti-inflammatory effects were examined in TNF-α/IFN-γ–stimulated HaCaT and LPS-induced DC2.4 cells. In vivo efficacy was evaluated in NC/nga and HR-1 mouse models of AD. LR extract significantly suppressed UVB-induced ROS production and enhanced Nrf2 and HO-1 expression. It reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine and chemokine levels by inhibiting ERK phosphorylation in HaCaT cells. Topical LR treatment in vivo attenuated trans-epidermal water loss, epidermal hyperplasia and immune cell infiltration, and Th2 cytokine levels in the lymph nodes. Dorsal root ganglion calcium imaging revealed decreased neuronal responsiveness to pruritogens following LR application. Topical application of LR extract ameliorated AD-like inflammation and pruritus through antioxidative and immunomodulatory mechanisms. These findings highlight LR as a natural therapeutic agent for managing oxidative stress-related skin inflammation and barrier dysfunction.Keywords: Mongolian Lycium ruthenicum, atopic dermatitis, antioxidant, anti-inflammation, anti-itch

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD), a chronic, relapsing, and pruritic inflammatory skin disorder, has been steadily increasing in prevalence, particularly in industrialized countries, affecting up to 20% of children and 10% of adults globally,(1) AD is characterized by impaired epidermal barrier function, recurrent eczematous lesions, and severe itching, all driven by complex interactions between genetic, immunological, and environmental factors.(2) Conventional therapies, including topical corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, and systemic immunosuppressants, are effective in many AD cases but are limited by side effects, safety concerns with long-term use, and variable patient responses.(3,4) These limitations highlight the need for alternative or adjunctive treatments, particularly those derived from natural products with strong safety profiles and multifunctional bioactivities.

Oxidative stress is a central mechanism implicated in AD pathogenesis and exacerbation. Elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the skin disrupt keratinocyte differentiation, compromise epidermal barrier integrity, and activate pro-inflammatory signaling cascades. These events lead to increased production of cytokines like interleukin (IL)-4, IL-13, and TNF-α, which further perpetuate inflammation and barrier dysfunction. Additionally, oxidative stress amplifies pruritus by sensitizing peripheral sensory nerves, creating a vicious cycle of itching and scratching, which worsens skin lesions. Therefore, targeting oxidative stress at the skin level may be an effective therapeutic strategy for AD management.(5)

Plant-derived polyphenols have attracted considerable attention because of their potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Compounds such as resveratrol, epigallocatechin gallate, and proanthocyanidins can neutralize ROS, modulate immune signaling, and enhance skin barrier recovery.(6) However, despite increasing support for the dermatological benefits of polyphenols, the therapeutic potential of certain anthocyanin- and proanthocyanidin-rich natural products remains unexplored. Among these, the Mongolian Lycium ruthenicum (LR), also known as the black wolfberry, is of particular interest.

LR grows naturally in the highlands of Mongolia and northern China and has traditionally been consumed in health-promoting teas and beverages.(7) Recent phytochemical analyses have revealed high levels of anthocyanins and oligomeric proanthocyanidins (OPCs) in LR.(8) OPCs are also abundant in blueberries, grape seeds, and pine bark, and are recognized for their strong antioxidant capacity and regulatory effects on inflammation and immune responses.(9–11) Notably, LR is distinguished from other anthocyanin-rich fruits based on its abundance of both anthocyanins and OPCs, providing a unique phytochemical profile with potential synergistic therapeutic effects.(12)

Recent studies have demonstrated that LR exerts potent antioxidant effects, in part through activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) signaling pathway, a central regulator of cellular redox homeostasis.(13–15) These findings suggest that LR may enhance endogenous antioxidant defenses by inducing downstream targets such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), thereby mitigating oxidative stress-related tissue damage. However, despite this emerging evidence, the therapeutic potential of LR—particularly via topical application—in inflammatory skin diseases such as AD remains largely unexplored. In addition, whether NRF2 activation by LR contributes to the modulation of cutaneous inflammation, barrier dysfunction, and itch signaling in AD has not been fully elucidated. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of topical LR extract in experimental models of AD, with a focus on its antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and anti-pruritic mechanisms.

Although oral consumption of LR has been linked to systemic antioxidant effects, topical application offers distinct advantages for skin diseases like AD. In this setting, LR extract is expected to restore skin barrier integrity by enhancing lipid synthesis and keratinocyte differentiation, reducing ROS-induced cellular damage, suppressing pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, and stabilizing mast cells to alleviate pruritus.

In this study, we focused on AD as a representative oxidative stress-driven skin disease and investigated the therapeutic potential of topical LR extract. Specifically, we evaluated whether LR extract could inhibit or ameliorate inflammatory and pruritic responses in AD and explored the underlying mechanisms via keratinocyte activation and immune modulation both in vitro and in vivo.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of LR extract

LR was obtained from PROM STYLE Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). The LR powder used in this study contained 10.4% OPCs and 0.86% anthocyanins. The antioxidant capacity of the LR preparation was evaluated using oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC) and Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity (TEAC) assays, which yielded values of 47 ‍mmol TE/100 ‍g and 26 ‍mmol TE/100 ‍g, respectively. For in vitro experiments, the LR extract was prepared by homogenization in the appropriate culture medium using a bead beater homogenizer (μT-12; TAITEC CORPORATION, Koshigaya, Japan) for 10 ‍min, followed by sonication with an ultrasonic disintegrator (Bioruptor II; BM Equipment Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for an additional 10 ‍min. For in vivo mouse experiments, the LR extract was incorporated into a gel formulation by dissolving the powder in a mixture of glycerin and cellulose gum (Sosin Co., Ltd., Kiyose, Japan) to achieve a final concentration of 3% or 10%. A vehicle gel composed solely of glycerin and cellulose gum without LR was used as a control to ensure matching physical properties between the experimental and control groups.

Ultraviolet (UV) B-induced ROS measurement in HaCaT cells

Intracellular ROS levels were measured as previously described with minor modifications.(16) Human epidermal keratinocytes (HaCaT cells) were obtained from the Cell Lines Service GLRH (Eppelheim, Germany) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and penicillin-streptomycin (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation). HaCaT cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/ml in 12-well plates and cultured until they reached approximately 70% confluence. The cells were then pretreated with LR extract at concentrations of 0, 1.6, or 8 ‍mg/ml for 24 ‍h prior to UVB exposure. UV irradiation was performed at 254 ‍nm using a BLX-Multichannel Bio-Link crosslinker (Vilber, Collégien, France) at 60 mJ/cm2, followed by incubation for 60 ‍min. Intracellular ROS production was assessed using an ROS Assay Kit (R252; Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence signals were detected and visualized using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X800; Keyence Corporation, Osaka, Japan).

Anti-inflammatory potential of LR extract in TNFα/IFNγ-induced HaCaT cells

HaCaT cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates and cultured to approximately 70% confluence under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). Cells were pretreated with LR extract at concentrations of 0, 1.6, 8, or 40 ‍mg/ml for 24 ‍h, followed by stimulation with TNFα (10 ‍ng/ml) and IFNγ (10 ‍ng/ml) (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA). Cytotoxicity was evaluated by quantifying the release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) using a Cytotoxicity LDH Assay Kit-WST (Dojindo Laboratories). β-actin, NF-E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) gene expression was analyzed using quantitative PCR (qPCR). Total RNA was extracted and cDNA was synthesized using specific primers (Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan). Amplification was performed using PowerUpTM SYBRTM Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) on a CFX Duet Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA). Relative expression levels were normalized to β-actin. Cytokine and chemokine production in culture supernatants was quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using DuoSet ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). The analytes included IL-6, IL-8, monocyte chemotactic protein-1 (MCP-1), and thymus- and activation-regulated chemokine (TARC/CCL17).

Inhibitory effects of LR extract on TNFα/IFNγ-induced mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) phosphorylation in HaCaT cells

MAPK phosphorylation in HaCaT cells was analyzed using Western blotting. Cells were pretreated with LR extract (0, 1.6, or 8 ‍mg/ml) for 24 ‍h, then stimulated with TNFα (10 ‍ng/ml) and IFNγ (10 ‍ng/ml) for 1 ‍h. Total cellular proteins were isolated using M-PERTM Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), and protein concentrations were quantified. Equal amounts of protein (30 ‍μg) were separated using SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes using a Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc.). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2), total p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2), and β-actin (all from Cell Signaling Technology Inc., Danvers, MA). Following incubation with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, protein bands were visualized using ImmunoStar® Zeta (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation). Bands were detected using the iBright Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), and phosphorylation levels were quantified relative to total MAPK and normalized to β-actin.

To investigate the involvement of NRF2 signaling in the anti-inflammatory effects of LR extract, HaCaT cells were treated with the selective NRF2 inhibitor ML385 (Sigma-Aldrich). The cells were pretreated with LR extract (8 ‍mg/ml) for 24 ‍h, followed by co-treatment with ML385 (5 ‍μM) for 1 ‍h prior to stimulation with TNF-α (10 ‍ng/ml) and IFN-γ (10 ‍ng/ml). After 24 ‍h of stimulation, culture supernatants were collected, and IL-6 and IL-8 levels were quantified. Cytotoxicity was assessed in parallel using an LDH cytotoxicity assay to exclude nonspecific cytotoxic effects.

Anti-inflammatory potential of LR extract in LPS-induced DC2.4 cells

The murine dendritic cell line DC2.4 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA) and in RPMI-1640 medium (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) supplemented with 10% FCS and penicillin–streptomycin under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/100 ‍μl when the cultures reached 70–80% confluence. They were pretreated with LR extract at concentrations of 0, 1.6, 8, or 40 ‍mg/ml for 24 ‍h, followed by stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 1 ‍μg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich). IL-1β and IL-6 concentrations in the culture supernatants were measured using DuoSet ELISA kits (R&D Systems), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Animals and housing conditions

Seven-week-old female NC/nga mice and four-week-old female HR-1 mice were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. (Hamamatsu, Japan) and Hoshino Laboratory Animals, Inc. (Bando, Japan), respectively. A maximum of four animals were housed per cage under controlled environmental conditions (12 ‍h light/dark cycle; temperature, 22 ± 3°C; relative humidity, 55 ± 15%). NC/nga mice were provided a certified pellet diet, whereas HR-1 mice were maintained on a polyunsaturated fatty acid-free diet (HR-AD; Hoshino Laboratory Animals, Inc.). All animals had free access to tap water throughout the experimental period. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Program of Azabu University (Approval No. 220607-6). Group allocation was performed randomly and the investigators conducting outcome assessments were blinded to the treatment conditions.

Topical treatment of LR extract in preventive and therapeutic settings of NC/nga mice with AD

The antiallergic effects of topical LR extract were evaluated in an NC/nga mouse model of AD. AD-like skin lesions were induced by repeated dermal application of a house dust mite (HDM) ointment (Biostir AD; Biostir Inc., Osaka, Japan) following pretreatment with a 4% sodium dodecyl sulfate solution (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To evaluate the preventive potential of LR extract, mice were topically treated once daily with either vehicle gel (without LR extract; n = 8) or 10% LR gel (n = 8), beginning one week before HDM sensitization and continued for 21 days. An additional group of untreated mice (n = 3) received neither the HDM nor the LR extract. For therapeutic evaluation, LR extract treatment was initiated after AD-like lesions appeared. From Day 0 (onset of visible lesions), the mice received a daily topical application of either vehicle gel (n = 7) or 10% LR gel (n = 8) until the end of the experiment on Day 39. On the days of HDM challenge, LR gel (0% or 10%) was applied approximately 30 ‍min after HDM application. AD progression was monitored weekly by measuring ear thickness, back skin thickness, trans-epidermal water loss (TEWL), and clinical scores. The clinical severity of the ears and back was scored on a scale of 0 to 3 (0, no symptoms; 1, mild; 2, moderate; 3, severe), and the combined scores were used for analysis.(17) Scratching behavior was recorded for 60 ‍min immediately after the final HDM application on the day before sacrifice. A scratching bout was defined as one or more consecutive hind limb strokes directed toward the HDM-treated area. At 24 ‍h after the final HDM challenge, back skin, pinnae, auricular lymph nodes (LN), and blood samples were collected. Tissues and serum were processed for histological evaluation, cytokine measurement, IgE quantification, and flow cytometry. Further, primary sensory neurons were isolated from the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) to compare the responses evoked by histamine and compound 48/80, which are dependent on itch signaling.

Topical treatment with LR extract in therapeutic settings using HR-1 mice with AD

The therapeutic efficacy of LR extract was evaluated in a second mouse model of AD using HR-1 mice. Four-week-old female HR-1 mice were maintained on a polyunsaturated fatty acid-free diet (HR-AD; Hoshino Laboratory Animals, Inc.) until 12 weeks of age.(18,19) AD-like skin lesions were induced by repeated dermal application of 0.5% tolylene-2,4-diisocyanate (TDI; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), following a previously described protocol.(20) From Day 0 (onset of visible AD-like lesions), the mice were treated once daily with the vehicle gel (n = 8), 3% LR gel (n = 6), or 10% LR gel (n = 8) for 21 consecutive days. Disease progression was monitored by measuring the TEWL and back skin thickness at weekly intervals. Sample collection at the end of the experiment was performed in the same manner as described for the NC/nga mouse model of AD, including harvesting skin tissues, LN, and blood for histological, immunological, and biochemical analyses.

Histopathology of skin samples

Skin tissue specimens were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 ‍μm thickness. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) following a previously established method.(20) Semiquantitative histopathological evaluation was performed in a blinded manner. Epidermal hyperplasia, crust formation, parakeratosis, cellular infiltration, ulceration, and vascular dilation were assessed. Each parameter was graded on a 4-point scale: 0, within normal limits; 1, mild; 2, moderate; and 3, severe.

Flow cytometric analysis of auricular LNs

Single-cell suspensions from auricular LNs were prepared as previously described.(21,22) The total number of isolated cells was determined using a CellDropTM Cell Counting System (DeNovix Inc., Wilmington, DE). For flow cytometry, 1 × 106 cells were first incubated with 1 ‍μg of mouse Fc Block (Miltenyi Biotec K.K., Tokyo, Japan) to minimize nonspecific antibody binding. The cells were then stained with the following monoclonal antibodies: PE/Cyanine7-conjugated anti-mouse CD3, PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD4, PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD11b, APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD11c, PerCP/Cyanine5.5-conjugated anti-mouse CD19, APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD44, APC/Cyanine7-conjugated anti-mouse CD62L, FITC-conjugated anti-mouse IgE, FITC-conjugated anti-mouse MHC class II, and DAPI. All antibodies were purchased from BioLegend Inc. (San Diego, CA), Miltenyi Biotec K.K., and Sony Biotechnology Inc. (San Jose, CA). After staining, the cells were washed and analyzed on a BD FACSAriaTM III cell sorter (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ).

Cytokine release assay of LNs

Single-cell suspensions from auricular LNs were used to assess cytokine production by T cells. Cells were seeded at 5 × 105 cells/well and stimulated with mouse T-activator CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) for 24 or 96 ‍h under standard culture conditions. IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, and TARC/CCL17 concentrations in the culture supernatants were measured using DuoSet ELISA kits (R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Gene expression analysis in back skin tissue

The frozen back skin tissue was homogenized using a bead-beater homogenizer (μT-12; TAITEC CORPORATION). Total RNA was extracted using the NucleoSpin® RNA kit (Takara Bio Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For cDNA synthesis, 500 ‍ng of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using PrimeScriptTM RT Master Mix (Takara Bio Inc.). qPCR was performed to assess the expression of β-actin, IL-4, IL-13, IL-33, filaggrin (Flg), thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), and TNFα using gene-specific primers (Takara Bio Inc.) and PowerUpTM SYBRTM Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) on a CFX Duet Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method and normalized to β-actin as a housekeeping gene.

Measurement of total IgE in serum

Serum total IgE concentrations were determined using an ELISA kit (BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Calcium imaging of mouse DRG neurons

Activation of small-diameter DRG neurons was assessed using Fura 2-AM (Dojindo Laboratories) ratiometric Ca2+ imaging (Nikon Solutions Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Calcium imaging was performed on cultured mouse DRG neurons as previously described, with minor modifications.(23) Fluorescence excitation was alternated between 340 and 380 ‍nm UV light and the emitted light was collected every 200 ms using an ORCA-Fusion BT Digital CMOS camera (Hamamatsu Photonics K.K., Hamamatsu, Japan). During imaging, cells were perfused with Locke buffer (pH ‍7.4; 136 ‍mmol/L NaCl, 5.4 ‍mmol/L KCl, 2.9 ‍mmol/L CaCl2, 2.5 ‍mmol/L MgCl2, 10.9 ‍mmol/L d-glucose, 0.6 ‍mmol/L NaHPO4, and 14.3 ‍mmol/L NaHCO3) through a temperature-controlled perfusion system. Neuronal activation was induced by administering histamine (100 ‍μmol/L) or Compound 48/80 (100 ‍μmol/L) (both from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation). At the end of each experiment, neuronal viability was confirmed by the Ca2+ influx response to 150 ‍mM KCl. Cells were considered responsive if the Fura-2 fluorescence ratio after chemical stimulation increased by more than 10% compared to that at the baseline.

Statistical analyses

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t test. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Šídák’s multiple comparison test was used. For measuring AD progression, including clinical score, skin thickness, and TEWL, two-way ANOVA with Šídák’s multiple comparison test was used. Proportional data such as the DRG analysis results were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. All statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05 or p<0.01.

Results

Antioxidant effects of LR extract in UVB-induced HaCaT cells

The antioxidant activity of LR extract in UVB-irradiated HaCaT cells was assessed by measuring intracellular ROS levels and the expression of antioxidant-related genes. As expected, UVB irradiation markedly increased intracellular ROS production compared to that in the non-irradiated control. Pretreatment with 1.6 and 8 ‍mg/ml LR extract significantly reduced ROS levels in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1A and B). Further, Nrf2 and HO-1 were significantly upregulated after UVB exposure. Interestingly, treatment with LR extract enhanced the expression of both Nrf2 and HO-1 compared with that in the UVB-only vehicle control group, suggesting that LR suppressed ROS accumulation and activated the endogenous antioxidant defense system (Fig. 1C and D). Notably, treatment with LR extract alone, in the absence of UVB exposure, did not significantly alter intracellular ROS levels or the expression of Nrf2 and HO-1, indicating that LR primarily modulates oxidative stress responses under stimulated conditions rather than inducing basal activation (data not shown).

Anti-inflammatory potential of LR extract in TNFα/IFNγ-induced HaCaT cells

To investigate the anti-inflammatory effects of LR extract, HaCaT cells were stimulated with TNFα/IFNγ, and cytotoxicity, pro-inflammatory cytokine/chemokine secretion, and MAPK phosphorylation were assessed. Pretreatment with LR extract significantly improved cytotoxicity while dose-dependently reducing IL-6 and IL-8 secretion, with the strongest suppression observed at 40 ‍mg/ml compared with the untreated group (Fig. 1E–G). Additionally, MCP-1 (which recruits monocytes and lymphocytes) and TARC (which attracts Th2 cells) secretion was markedly decreased by the LR extract in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1H and I). To further examine the upstream regulatory mechanisms, phosphorylation of p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) was evaluated and LR extract significantly suppressed Erk1/2 phosphorylation in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1J).

To further investigate the involvement of NRF2 signaling in the anti-inflammatory effects of LR extract, we evaluated cytokine production in the presence of the NRF2 inhibitor ML385. HaCaT cells were pretreated with LR extract followed by co-treatment with ML385 prior to TNF-α/IFN-γ stimulation. As expected, LR extract significantly suppressed IL-6 and IL-8 production compared with the stimulated control group. Notably, inhibition of NRF2 with ML385 markedly reversed these suppressive effects, restoring IL-6 and IL-8 levels to those comparable to the vehicle-treated control (Supplemental Fig. 1*).

Anti-inflammatory potential of LR extract in LPS-stimulated DC2.4 cells

To evaluate the anti-inflammatory effects of LR extract in AD-related immune cells, DC2.4 dendritic cells were stimulated with LPS, and cytokine production was measured. Pretreatment with LR extract significantly reduced LPS-induced IL-1β and IL-6 secretion in a dose-dependent manner, with a more pronounced inhibitory effect observed for IL-6 (Fig. 2A and B).

Preventive efficacy of topical LR extract in an NC/nga mouse model of AD

To evaluate the preventive effects of LR extract, topical treatment was initiated before the onset of AD lesions (Fig. 3A). Mice receiving 10% LR extract exhibited significantly lower AD clinical scores, skin thickness (both back and ear), and TEWL than in the vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 3B–F). Furthermore, topical LR treatment markedly reduced scratching behavior, as evidenced by a significant decrease in the number of scratching bouts recorded during the 30-min observation following the final HDM challenge (Fig. 3G). Histopathological analysis of the ear and back skin confirmed the protective effects of 10% LR extract. The treated mice showed markedly reduced epidermal hyperplasia, parakeratosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration compared with those in the vehicle group, indicating amelioration of cutaneous inflammation and improved barrier integrity (Fig. 3H and Table 1).

To further explore the immunological changes, flow cytometric analysis of auricular LNs was performed. Although the total number of dendritic cells (DCs) was not significantly affected by LR treatment, the proportions of effector Th cells, B cells, and IL-4–producing T cells were significantly reduced in the LR-treated group (Fig. 4A–D). Consistent with these findings, serum total IgE level, a systemic indicator of allergen sensitization, was not significantly altered by LR treatment. However, the mRNA expression levels of IL-4, IL-13, and IL-33 in the back skin were significantly suppressed in LR-treated mice compared with those in the vehicle controls (Fig. 4E–H). Filaggrin (FLG), a key barrier protein, showed a mild but nonsignificant increase in expression following LR treatment (Fig. 4I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that preventive topical application of LR extract effectively attenuates the development of AD-like symptoms by suppressing local Th2-type inflammation and improving epidermal homeostasis.

Therapeutic efficacy of topical LR extract in an NC/nga mouse model of AD

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of LR extract, topical treatment was initiated after the onset of AD-like skin lesions (Fig. 5A). Although the AD clinical scores and skin thickness (ear and back) showed a decreasing trend in the LR-treated group, these changes were not statistically significant (Fig. 5B–E). In contrast, TEWL was significantly reduced after the topical application of 10% LR, suggesting an improvement in skin barrier function. Histopathological assessment of the ear and back skin showed results consistent with the clinical observations. Although epidermal hyperplasia, parakeratosis, and inflammatory infiltration were moderately alleviated by LR treatment, these improvements were not significant (Fig. 5I and Table 2). Similarly, scratching behavior showed a decreasing tendency in LR-treated mice, although the difference was not statistically significant compared with the vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 5F). Interestingly, calcium imaging analysis of DRG neurons revealed that the proportion of neurons responding to the pruritogens histamine and compound 48/80 was significantly reduced in mice treated with the 10% LR extract (Fig. 5G and H). These results suggest that LR treatment may modulate the peripheral neuronal excitability associated with itch signaling.

Therapeutic efficacy of topical LR extract in the HR-1 mouse model of AD

To further confirm the therapeutic efficacy of topical LR extract, with an emphasis on skin barrier restoration, experiments were conducted using a polyunsaturated fatty acid-free diet and a Th2 hapten-induced AD model in HR-1 mice (Fig. 7A). TEWL was significantly ameliorated by topical LR treatment in a dose-dependent manner, whereas the back skin thickness remained unchanged among the groups (Fig. 7B–D). Histopathological examination revealed that inflammatory cell infiltration in the dermis was markedly suppressed by topical application of the 10% LR extract, whereas 3% LR treatment showed no significant effects (Fig. 7E and Table 3). These findings suggest that a higher concentration of the LR extract exerts a more pronounced therapeutic effect on cutaneous inflammation.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that the LR extract exerts potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects both in vitro and in vivo, providing compelling evidence for its potential as a topical therapeutic and preventive agent for AD. Using complementary models—including UVB-induced oxidative stress in HaCaT keratinocytes, TNF-α/IFN-γ-induced inflammatory keratinocyte activation, LPS-stimulated DCs, and two distinct murine AD models (NC/nga and HR-1 mice)—we systematically evaluated the multi-layered biological effects of LR extract on the skin barrier, cutaneous inflammation, and neuroimmune interactions. The results consistently indicate that the LR extract ameliorates oxidative damage, attenuates inflammatory signaling, suppresses immune activation, and improves cutaneous barrier integrity, highlighting its broad mechanistic versatility in skin protection and repair.

Keratinocytes are the primary sensors of environmental stressors such as UVB irradiation, and play a pivotal role in initiating cutaneous inflammation.(24) Excessive ROS production following UVB exposure induces oxidative stress, DNA damage, and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which contribute to skin barrier disruption and chronic inflammation.(25) In the present study, LR extract significantly attenuated UVB-induced intracellular ROS accumulation in HaCaT cells, demonstrating strong antioxidant capacity. Notably, the LR extract suppressed ROS levels and upregulated the gene expression of Nrf2 and HO-1, two key regulators of cellular antioxidant defense systems.(26) Nrf2 activation orchestrates the transcription of cytoprotective enzymes, including HO-1, which mitigate oxidative injury and maintain redox homeostasis.(27) The dual action of LR extract—scavenging ROS and enhancing endogenous antioxidant pathways—suggests that its bioactive components may directly activate the Nrf2 pathway, as observed for other plant-derived polyphenols and flavonoids.(28) This activation may contribute towards the protection of keratinocytes against UVB-induced oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby preventing subsequent skin barrier impairment.

Inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IFN-γ are major mediators of AD-related skin inflammation, acting synergistically to stimulate keratinocytes for producing a range of cytokines and chemokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1, and TARC.(29) These mediators perpetuate the recruitment of immune cells and amplify the chronic inflammatory loop in AD lesions.(30,31) Our results clearly show that pretreatment with LR extract significantly suppressed the production of these pro-inflammatory mediators in a concentration-dependent manner, without inducing cytotoxicity. In particular, suppression of MCP-1 and TARC suggests an inhibitory effect on monocyte and Th2 cell recruitment, both central to AD pathophysiology.(32–34) Mechanistically, LR extract decreased TNF-α/IFN-γ-induced phosphorylation of Erk1/2 MAPK, a signaling axis involved in cytokine transcription and keratinocyte activation.(35) Thus, the LR extract appears to modulate upstream inflammatory pathways by attenuating MAPK signaling, leading to reduced cytokine and chemokine production. Collectively, these findings indicate that LR extract exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects in keratinocytes by downregulating both oxidative stress and MAPK-driven inflammatory cascades.(36,37)

DCs orchestrate cutaneous immune responses, bridging innate and adaptive immunity.(38) Upon stimulation by microbial or allergenic signals such as LPS, DCs release IL-1β and IL-6, which drive Th2 and Th17 polarization and sustain allergic inflammation.(39) Our findings reveal that LR extract markedly inhibited LPS-induced IL-1β and IL-6 secretion by DC2.4 cells, suggesting a direct regulatory effect on DC activation. This anti-inflammatory modulation of DCs was consistent with the reduced antigen presentation and downstream T-cell activation observed in LR-treated animals. Therefore, the LR extract may act on an early immune checkpoint by restraining DC-mediated cytokine signaling, thereby dampening the subsequent Th2-dominant immune responses in AD.

In addition to its antioxidant effects, the anti-inflammatory activity of LR extract may be partially mediated through NRF2 signaling. NRF2 is known to suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6 and to regulate inflammatory responses via cytoprotective gene expression.(40) Consistent with this, LR extract significantly reduced cytokine and chemokine production in stimulated keratinocytes and DCs. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of NRF2 using ML385 partially reversed the suppressive effects of LR on IL-6 and IL-8 production in TNF-α/IFN-γ–stimulated HaCaT cells, supporting a role for NRF2 in its anti-inflammatory action. In parallel, LR also inhibited MAPK (Erk1/2) phosphorylation, suggesting involvement of additional signaling pathways. Together, these findings indicate that LR exerts anti-inflammatory effects through both NRF2-dependent and NRF2-independent mechanisms. However, as ML385 inhibition does not fully replicate genetic ablation, further studies using NRF2-deficient models will be required to definitively establish causality and clarify the relative contribution of NRF2 signaling.

The NC/nga mouse model recapitulates several immunological and histopathological features of human AD, including Th2 polarization, elevated IgE production, and skin barrier dysfunction.(41) In the preventive regimen, topical application of LR extract before allergen sensitization significantly suppressed AD development, as evidenced by reduced clinical scores, skin thickness, TEWL, and scratching behavior. Histological analyses confirmed the attenuation of epidermal hyperplasia and parakeratosis, indicating restoration of epidermal structure and function. Immunologically, LR extract-treated mice exhibited decreased numbers of effector Th and B cells in the auricular LN, along with reduced IL-4 production, suggesting dampened Th2-driven inflammation. Interestingly, serum levels of total IgE were not significantly altered, indicating that LR extract primarily modulated local rather than systemic immune responses. Furthermore, the gene expression of IL-4, IL-13, and IL-33 in the back skin was significantly reduced, reinforcing the suppression of Th2 cytokine networks and epithelial-derived alarmins.(42) The modest increase in Flg expression, although not statistically significant, implies a partial recovery of barrier integrity. These findings highlight the strong preventive efficacy of the LR extract, acting through both antioxidant and immunomodulatory mechanisms to maintain cutaneous homeostasis prior to allergen-induced inflammation.

When administered therapeutically after the onset of AD lesions, LR extract showed moderate but consistent improvements. Although the reductions in AD scores and skin thickness were not statistically significant, TEWL was markedly improved, indicating partial restoration of the skin barrier. This finding is clinically relevant, as TEWL reflects epidermal integrity and hydration, which are key determinants of disease severity in AD.(43) Importantly, topical LR treatment significantly reduced the proportion of DRG neurons responsive to histamine and compound 48/80, suggesting a reduction in peripheral itch sensitivity.(44) This neuroprotective effect may contribute to the decreased scratching behavior, even in the absence of significant histological improvement. Furthermore, LR treatment significantly reduced the DC, effector Th, and B cell populations in the draining LN, demonstrating attenuation of local immune activation. Among the cytokine genes in the skin, IL-33 expression was notably downregulated, consistent with the suppression of epithelial alarmin signaling contributing to chronic itch and inflammation.(45) Together, these results indicate that the LR extract confers therapeutic benefits primarily by modulating neuroimmune pathways and skin barrier recovery, rather than systemic immunosuppression.

To strengthen evidence for the therapeutic potential of LR, we evaluated its effects in an alternative AD model of HR-1 mice maintained on a polyunsaturated fatty acid-free diet and repeatedly exposed to TDI. This model is characterized by dry inflamed skin and a dominant Th2 cytokine response, closely mimicking intrinsic AD.(46) Consistent with the findings in NC/nga mice, topical application of the 3% and 10% LR extract significantly ameliorated TEWL in a dose-dependent manner, reflecting an improvement in skin barrier function. Histological analysis further confirmed the suppression of inflammatory cell infiltration in the 10% LR-treated group. Flow cytometric and cytokine analyses of auricular LNs revealed dose-dependent inhibition of DCs, effector Th cells, B cells, and Th2 cytokine secretion (IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13), indicating robust local immunosuppression. Moreover, LR treatment significantly downregulated the IL-33, TSLP, and TNFα gene expression in skin tissue, suggesting that LR acts on keratinocyte-derived cytokines to disrupt the vicious cycle of inflammation and barrier dysfunction. These findings validate that LR extract is a multifunctional topical agent capable of both barrier restoration and immunomodulation across distinct AD models.

The biological effects of LR can be attributed, at least partly, to its rich composition of OPCs, which are known for their strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. OPCs have been reported to inhibit lipid peroxidation, scavenge ROS, and regulate immune signaling pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK.(47,48) These properties may underlie the observed suppression of cytokine and chemokine secretion.(49) Simultaneously, anthocyanins and OPCs may contribute to barrier protection by stabilizing keratinocyte membranes and influencing lipid metabolism, thereby supporting epidermal homeostasis.(50,51) Although the precise molecular targets of LR remain to be fully elucidated, our findings provide a compelling rationale for further detailed phytochemical and mechanistic studies.

From a clinical standpoint, the dual effects of LR on inflammation and barrier integrity are particularly relevant in AD, which is characterized by both immune dysregulation and barrier dysfunction. Conventional topical therapies, including corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors, are effective at suppressing inflammation but do not directly strengthen the skin barrier, and their long-term use is limited by side effects, such as skin thinning and immune suppression.(52–55) Natural formulations based on LR may thus offer a complementary or adjunctive approach by simultaneously supporting barrier health and reducing inflammatory responses. Such dual-action strategies are being increasingly sought in dermatology and cosmeceutical development, particularly as patients and consumers express a growing interest in plant-derived, low-toxicity alternatives.

Despite these promising results, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although the current study identified the major pathways modulated by LR extract, detailed molecular characterization of its bioactive constituents remains to be performed. Future studies employing LC–MS/MS and metabolomic profiling may identify the specific compounds responsible for Nrf2 activation and MAPK inhibition. Second, although in vivo models closely mimic human and canine AD, interspecies differences in skin structure and immune responses may affect translational applicability. Future studies should use reconstructed human skin or ex vivo explant systems to validate these findings. Third, although our findings suggest activation of the NRF2-associated antioxidant response, this conclusion is based primarily on the upregulation of HO-1, a representative downstream target. However, NRF2 regulates a broad network of cytoprotective genes, including NQO1, GCLC, GCLM, and various glutathione S-transferases, which were not evaluated in the present study. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as indicative of partial activation of NRF2-related pathways rather than comprehensive pathway engagement. Future studies incorporating a wider panel of canonical NRF2 target genes, as well as direct assessment of NRF2 nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity, will be necessary to more definitively establish the extent and specificity of NRF2 pathway activation by LR extract. Finally, long-term safety evaluation and mechanistic studies on neuroimmune modulation will provide further insights into its anti-pruritic potential.

In summary, the present study provides comprehensive evidence that LR extract possesses strong antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory activities that collectively prevent and attenuate AD-like pathology. Future studies focusing on bioactive compound isolation, formulation optimization, and clinical validation will advance its development toward dermatological applications.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that LR extract exerts potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects on AD. LR activates the Nrf2–HO-1 antioxidant pathway, suppresses ROS accumulation, and inhibits pro-inflammatory cytokine and chemokine production in keratinocytes and DCs. In murine AD models, topical application of LR improved skin barrier function, reduced TEWL and inflammatory infiltration, and downregulated Th2-related cytokines and epithelial alarmins. Moreover, LR treatment diminished neuronal responsiveness to pruritogens, indicating its potential anti-itch activity. These findings suggest that LR extract acts through multi-target mechanisms involving oxidative stress reduction, immune modulation, and barrier restoration. Owing to its favorable safety profile and plant-derived origin, LR is a promising candidate for topical prevention and adjunctive therapy in AD and related inflammatory skin disorders. However, further research is needed to optimize these formulations and evaluate their efficacy in clinical settings.
