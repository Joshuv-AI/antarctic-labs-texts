Lacunar stroke with incidental congenital absence of the internal carotid artery: importance of recognising a rare anatomical variant
Authors: Woei Ling Lai, Danushka Ratnayake
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe report a case of a woman in her 50s who presented with a left lacunar infarct and was incidentally found to have congenital absence of the ipsilateral internal carotid artery (ICA). This rare vascular anomaly was confirmed by the presence of a hypoplastic bony carotid canal on imaging, consistent with congenital origin. Cerebral perfusion was preserved, and no conventional vascular risk factors were identified. This case highlights the diagnostic importance of recognising congenital ICA absence on neurovascular imaging, particularly in stroke assessment, to prevent misinterpretation and to guide appropriate management.Keywords: Vascular surgery, Radiology, Stroke, Neuroimaging

Background

Congenital absence of the internal carotid artery (ICA) is a rare vascular anomaly, with an estimated prevalence of less than 0.01% in the general population.1 It results from developmental failure during embryogenesis, typically involving the third aortic arch or dorsal aorta.2 A left-sided predominance has been reported, with a left:right ratio of approximately 3:1.3

Most cases are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally on neurovascular imaging.4 In rare instances, it has been reported in association with subarachnoid haemorrhage, aneurysm formation or ischaemic stroke.5 Cerebral perfusion is generally maintained via robust collateral pathways through the circle of Willis.6

Differentiation between congenital absence and acquired conditions such as atherosclerotic occlusion or dissection is critical. The absence or hypoplasia of the bony carotid canal on CT imaging is a key distinguishing feature of congenital aetiology.7 This distinction is particularly important in acute stroke presentations, where misinterpretation can lead to inappropriate interventions.8

Case presentation

A previously healthy woman in her 50s presented with sudden onset right facial droop and slurred speech. Earlier that day, she had fallen from a horse while wearing a helmet. She reported no loss of consciousness or immediate neurological symptoms. She is a non-smoker with no known vascular risk factors.

Examination revealed a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of 3, with findings of right facial weakness (2 points) and mild dysarthria (1 point). No limb weakness, sensory deficits or external injuries were observed.

Investigations

A non-contrast CT of the head showed no acute intracranial pathology. Subsequent CT angiography of the head and neck revealed the complete absence of the left cervical and cavernous segments of the ICA, with hypoplasia of the left carotid canal, confirming a congenital origin7 (figures1 2).

Collateral circulation was evident through a prominent left posterior communicating artery and a single azygous anterior cerebral artery (figure 3). These findings corresponded to Type A of Lie’s classification of ICA agenesis.9 Perfusion to bilateral middle cerebral arteries and posterior circulation territories was preserved. There was no evidence of dissection, atherosclerosis or stenosis.

MRI of the brain showed a small acute infarct in the left posterior limb of the internal capsule, consistent with a lacunar pattern (figure 4). Carotid duplex ultrasound confirmed the absence of the left ICA and showed no signs of significant stenosis or plaque in the contralateral vessels. Non-HDL (Non-High-Density Lipoprotein) cholesterol was 2.44 mmol/L. A comprehensive young stroke panel, including thrombophilia and autoimmune screening, was negative.

Differential diagnosis

The clinical presentation and radiological findings confirmed an acute ischaemic stroke. The infarct pattern was consistent with a lacunar stroke, classically associated with small vessel disease (SVD).10 However, the absence of classical SVD risk factors prompted consideration of alternative mechanisms.

Arterial dissection was considered due to the history of trauma. However, the absence of clinical signs such as neck pain and lack of radiological features of dissection such as mural haematoma, dissection flap or pseudoaneurysm, made this unlikely.11

The presence of a hypoplastic carotid canal and absence of acquired vascular pathology confirmed the diagnosis of congenital ICA absence.7 No embolic source was identified, and further investigations for vasculitis, cardioembolism and hypercoagulability were unremarkable.

Although congenital ICA absence has been described in association with cerebrovascular events, current evidence does not establish a causal link between this anomaly and lacunar infarction.12 In the absence of risk factors or alternative aetiology, an idiopathic small vessel infarct remains a recognised and evidence-based diagnostic possibility for lacunar infarction.13

Treatment

The patient was started on high-dose aspirin 300 mg once daily for 2 weeks, followed by lifelong clopidogrel 75 mg once daily for secondary stroke prevention. High-intensity statin therapy with atorvastatin 80 mg once daily was initiated for lipid management and secondary prevention. Although the patient’s non-HDL cholesterol was only borderline elevated, the decision to start high-dose atorvastatin was based on three key factors: the patient’s age (around 60), the acute onset of ischaemic stroke and the recognised pleiotropic benefits of statins in this context. A neurovascular multidisciplinary team review concluded that no surgical intervention was required, given the congenital nature of the vascular anomaly and the absence of associated large vessel pathology.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient was discharged the following day. At 9-month follow-up, she had full neurological recovery with no recurrent events.

Discussion

Congenital absence of the ICA is a rare developmental anomaly, occurring in less than 0.01% of the population.1 It arises due to failure in the embryological development of the third aortic arch or dorsal aorta.2 A left-sided predominance has been reported, with a 3:1 ratio compared with the right.3

In most cases, collateral circulation via the circle of Willis maintains cerebral perfusion, and the anomaly remains asymptomatic.4 It is frequently detected incidentally on imaging performed for unrelated indications.5 However, reported complications include intracranial aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations and, less commonly, ischaemic stroke.6 A direct causal relationship between ICA agenesis and infarction remains unproven.7

Radiologically, the absence or hypoplasia of the carotid canal on CT imaging is a key diagnostic feature that differentiates congenital ICA absence from acquired ICA occlusion or dissection.8 This differentiation is clinically important, particularly in the acute stroke setting, where misinterpretation may lead to unnecessary investigations or inappropriate management.9

In this case, Type A collateral configuration was present, as described in Lie’s classification, with the anterior cerebral artery supplied by the anterior communicating artery and the middle cerebral artery perfused by a dominant posterior communicating artery.9 10 This configuration has been considered adequate for cerebral perfusion in the literature.

A small acute infarct was detected within the ipsilateral posterior limb of the internal capsule, in keeping with a lacunar infarct. There was no radiological evidence of arterial insufficiency, vasculopathy or embolism. Although congenital vascular anomalies have been hypothesised to contribute to haemodynamic vulnerability, there is insufficient evidence to establish a link between ICA agenesis and lacunar stroke.12 Idiopathic SVD, even in the absence of conventional risk factors, is recognised in the literature.13

This case reinforces the importance of recognising rare congenital vascular anomalies and their implications in stroke work-up. Accurate identification of a hypoplastic carotid canal aids in distinguishing congenital from acquired pathology, ensuring appropriate investigation and avoiding misdiagnosis.

Patient’s perspective

I was fortunate to make a full recovery and have since returned to my normal daily activities. I was initially surprised to learn about the congenital absence of my internal carotid artery, as I had never encountered this term before. By the time I received my follow-up letter, I had resumed my usual routine and had almost forgotten that I had experienced a stroke.
