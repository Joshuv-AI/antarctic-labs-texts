Uncovering the impact of co-infections on disease severity and mortality in pulmonary cryptococcosis
Authors: Luling Wu, Xuemin Fu, Ling Weng, Benno Pütz, Renfang Zhang, Li Liu, Yueming Shao, Zhihang Zheng, Jingna Xun, Ximei Han, Ting Wang, Yinzhong Shen, Hongzhou Lu, Bertram Müller-Myhsok, Jun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIncreasing case reports and monocentric studies suggest that co-infections may be associated with poorer outcomes in patients with pulmonary cryptococcosis (PC), including both people living with HIV (PLWH) and HIV-negative individuals. However, systematic cohort-based evidence evaluating the prognostic impact of co-infections remains limited.MethodsTotal of 454 patients with PC (239 PLWH and 215 non-HIV) were included to compare clinical characteristics, CT imaging features, and relevant prognostic factors according to their co-infection status.ResultsAmong non-HIV patients, co-infections were linked to older age (53.42 vs. 45.46 years, p = 0.02), while no age difference was observed among PLWH. No HIV-negative patient had multiple co-infections, and all 1-year deaths occurred in those with bacterial co-infection; in contrast, 20% of PLWH had dual co-infections, most commonly tuberculosis. PLWH more frequently presented with fever and central nervous system symptoms, and co-infected PLWH had greater disease severity, reflected by higher SOFA, CURB-65, and APACHE II scores. Among co-infected patients, disease severity and 52-week median survival were comparable across HIV and dissemination status, although non-HIV patients experienced a markedly longer diagnostic delay [13.5 vs. 2.0 days; p < 0.0001]. Over 52 weeks, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP), identified only in PLWH, was independently associated with mortality (HR = 3.81; p = 0.002), whereas bacterial co-infection showed a non-significant increase in mortality risk (HR = 1.51; p = 0.44). In the overall cohort, a high-risk APACHE II score was associated with more than a 15-fold increase in mortality, while nodular-plus-patchy CT lesions were associated with a greater than fivefold increase. PLWH had an 11.3-fold higher mortality risk than non-HIV patients, with SOFA score ≥ 2, high-risk APACHE II score, and absence of standard antifungal therapy further associated with poorer survival.ConclusionCo-infection patterns differed by HIV status: multiple and diverse co-infections were common in PLWH, whereas bacterial co-infections predominated in non-HIV patients. Among co-infected patients, survival was comparable regardless of HIV status or extrapulmonary dissemination. Earlier recognition and timely diagnosis may improve the clinical management of pulmonary cryptococcosis.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14444-7.

Introduction

Cryptococcosis is a severe fungal infection with a global presence, contributing significantly to morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Each year, over 15,000 new cases of cryptococcal meningitis are reported, resulting in approximately 112,000 deaths worldwide [3]. Pulmonary cryptococcosis (PC), the most prevalent form, arises from the inhalation of cryptococcal yeast or spores [4]. Several factors, including advanced age, uncontrolled diabetes, high cryptococcal antigen (CrAg) titers, and multiple comorbidities, have been associated with poorer outcomes in PC patients [5, 6]. It’s also identified that IL-1RA potentially be the key biomarker for predicting three-year survival in patients with HIV-associated disseminated cryptococcosis [7]. The infection predominantly affects immunocompromised individuals, including people living with HIV (PLWH), organ transplant recipients, and those with malignancies, who are at heightened risk for extrapulmonary spread and increased mortality [8, 9]. In immunodeficient patients, especially those with significantly reduced CD4+ T cell counts, co-infections with bacteria, tuberculosis, or non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are common and often worsen prognosis [10–12].

Recent research highlights a rising incidence of PC in immunocompetent patients, particularly in China, where most cases occur in HIV-negative individuals—the trend that contrasts with patterns observed in many other countries [2, 13, 14]. Furthermore, diverse co-infections also pose additional burdens and challenges, often leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment [10, 15]. Limited evidence suggests that patients co-infected with Cryptococcus neoformans and Mycobacterium tuberculosis face significantly higher mortality risks than only-Cryptococcus suffer, especially in cases of severe immunosuppression [1, 16]. However, these findings are primarily derived from single-center case reports [17–20]. Previous animal study added further complexity to the understanding of co-infections, demonstrating that Pseudomonas aeruginosa co-infection was detected with less fungal loads in the mice brain, likely by enhancing macrophage activity and inhibiting fungal growth, thereby delaying the spread of Cryptococcus to the central nervous system [13]. All these findings highlight distinct dynamics and clinical significance in co-infection interactions, therefore robust observational studies are urgently needed to validate and expand upon these insights.

To address these gaps, we systematically collected data on clinical symptoms, imaging findings, disease severity, and outcomes from two specialized infectious disease hospitals in eastern coastal China. Our study aims to compare with/without co-infection patterns in non-HIV and HIV-related PC and to identify key prognostic factors influencing survival and prognosis.

Materials and methods

Human ethics

The study received approval form the Research Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center (approval number 2022-S055-01), and the Ethics Committee of Fuzhou Pulmonary Hospital, Fujian Provincial Hospital for Tuberculosis Prevention and Control (approval number 2022-005-01). All data analyzed were anonymous.

Cohort design and patient enrolment

This retrospective cohort study included patients diagnosed with PC at the participating centers between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2022, who met the predefined inclusion criteria. PLWH were recruited from the Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center, while the non-HIV cohort was drawn from the Fuzhou Pulmonary Hospital. PC was diagnosed in both inpatient and outpatient patients with pulmonary lesions and either histopathological confirmation of cryptococcal infection or detection of Cryptococcus in sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. Co-infection was defined as the presence of at least one additional infection, including bacterial infection, tuberculosis, non-tuberculous mycobacteria, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP), or Talaromyces marneffei. Positive pathogenic diagnoses were confirmed by identifying specific pathogens (e.g., bacteria, fungi, tuberculosis, or non-tuberculous mycobacteria) through microbial culture or metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS).

This study collected demographic data, clinical characteristics, laboratory results, computed tomography (CT) imaging features, and disease severity metrics from all patients (Fig. 1a). Participants with more than 20% missing values across study variables were excluded from the analysis. Within 24 h of admission, disease severity was assessed using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), CURB-65, and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) scores, which provide complementary measures of organ dysfunction, pneumonia severity, and overall physiological illness, respectively [21]. The Veterans Aging Cohort Study (VACS) Index 2.0 was additionally calculated for PLWH to account for HIV-specific disease burden [22]. In accordance with established guidelines [2, 23], antifungal therapy was initiated upon diagnosis of cryptococcosis, and antiretroviral therapy (ART) was started after 4–6 weeks of antifungal treatment.

Statistical analysis

In this study, a difference with p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). For normally distributed numeric data, comparisons between groups were performed using paired or unpaired t-tests; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was employed. To compare categorical variables (proportions between groups), the two-sided Fisher’s exact test or the chi-square test (χ²-test) was utilized. Differences in survival between specific subsets were analyzed using the log-rank test. Kaplan-Meier survival estimates were computed to evaluate survival probabilities within one year.

Variables with p < 0.10 in the univariate analysis were further evaluated using univariable Cox proportional hazards regression. Variables with p < 0.15 in the univariable Cox analysis were then included in the multivariable Cox regression model. This analysis was achieved through stepwise selection, driven by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) improvements, wherein variables were iteratively added or removed to optimize the AIC value. Given the potential overlap among disease severity scores, multicollinearity among variables retained in the multivariable model was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs). A VIF > 5 was considered indicative of potentially substantial multicollinearity. Variables with acceptable VIF values were retained in the model. When substantial multicollinearity was identified between clinically overlapping variables, these variables were not included simultaneously; instead, the variable with greater clinical relevance was retained, and the model was refitted and reassessed for multicollinearity. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.3 [24], and the following packages: tidyverse [25], ggplot2 [26], gtsummary [27], and survival [28].

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

In this study, we analyzed 454 patients with PC (Table 1 and STable 2), dividing them into four subgroups based on co-infection status: (1) HIV+PC+ without co-infections (N = 145); (2) HIV+PC+ with co-infections (N = 94); (3) HIV−PC+ without co-infections (N = 203); (4) HIV−PC+ with co-infections (N = 12). As shown in Fig. 1(b and c), among the 94 co-infected PLWH, 20% had multiple simultaneous co-infections, most commonly involving tuberculosis or PCP. In contrast, none of the 12 co-infected non-HIV patients had multiple co-infections; bacterial infections predominated (75%), and all 1-year deaths occurred in this group. PCP was identified exclusively among PLWH. Based on the demographic data, individuals in the non-HIV groups with co-infections were older than those without co-infections (53.42 vs. 45.46 years, p = 0.02). There was no significant age difference observed in PLWH group (Table 1). Extrapulmonary dissemination was more statistically frequent in PLWH and sole cryptococcus, whereas the reverse was noted in non-HIV patients (p = 0.02). In the non-HIV group, CD8+ T cell counts were significantly higher in the non-coinfected group compared to the co-infected group (539.77 ± 27.00 vs. 351.00 ± 43.66, p = 0.04), but there was no significant difference in the PLWH group (STable 2 and STable 3). Furthermore, CD4+ T cell counts did not differ significantly between PLWH and non-HIV patients, regardless of coinfection status. In the presence of co-infections, a significantly higher proportion of severe cases were observed in the PLWH group, as reflected by SOFA, CURB-65, and APACHE II scores (p = 0.01, 0.02 and 0.02, respectively). In contrast, co-infections had no significant impact on disease severity in the non-HIV group. Combination antifungal therapy was more common in PLWH sole cryptococcus group, while monotherapy was prevalent in the non-HIV group and HIV co-infected individuals.

Medical traits and chest imaging features

Asymptomatic or acute respiratory symptoms were mostly observed in the non-HIV group, whereas fever and central nervous system (CNS) symptoms, like headache, nausea, vomiting, and even unconsciousness, predominated in the PLWH group (Fig. 2a). When PC was accompanied by co-infections, over 20% of patients in both groups experienced dyspnea, a significantly higher rate compared to those without co-infections (p < 0.01). The prevalence of meningeal irritation (22.8% vs. 21.3%; p = 0.79) and neck stiffness (7.6% vs. 3.2%; p = 0.16) did not differ significantly between patients with and without co-infections.

No neurological symptoms were observed in the non-HIV group. Additionally, 81.8% of nodules appeared in the non-HIV group without co-infections, significantly more than in those with co-infections (Fig. 2b). Multiple lesions were common in both groups. The left upper lobe was associated with co-infections in both PLWH and non-HIV groups, while the right middle lobe was more common in PLWH without co-infections (P = 0.02).

Disease progression and kaplan-Meier survival-analysis curves for 1-year mortality

Among co-infected patients, disease severity and 52-week median survival time were similar regardless of whether HIV infection and extrapulmonary dissemination were present (Fig. 3b and d). Despite comparable disease severity at symptom onset, the median (IQR) time from admission to diagnosis in the non-HIV group with co-infections was 13.5 (8.5, 19.3) days, significantly longer than in the PLWH group, which was 2 (1.3, 3) days (Fig. 3j, p < 0.0001). Similarly, in the non-disseminated group with co-infections, the median (IQR) diagnosis time was significantly prolonged at 3 (2, 13) days, compared to 2 (1, 4) days in the disseminated group (Fig. 3l, p = 0.007). Interestingly, even without considering extrapulmonary dissemination, PLWH and non-HIV patients had similar survival times (SFig 1a). In contrast, in patients without co-infection, both HIV and extrapulmonary dissemination reduced the 52-week median survival time (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3a and c). These factors also shortened the time from symptoms-to-admission and admission-to-diagnosis (Fig. 3e, g, i, and k), while significantly increasing the length of admission-to-discharge (Fig. 3m and o).

Prognostic factors for 1-year mortality in PC patients

Total of 317 patients with PC and definitive survival outcomes, 9% (29/317) died within 52 weeks. Univariate analysis showed that a high-risk APACHE II score increased mortality risk by over 15-fold (HR = 16.4) compared with a low-risk score (Table 2). Patients with both nodular and patchy lesions on lung CT had a more than five-fold higher risk of death (HR = 5.8) compared with those with nodular lesions alone. All cases of co-infection with PCP occurred in the HIV-positive cohort; such co-infection was identified as an independent risk factor for 1-year mortality (HR = 3.81, 95%, CI:1.63–8.93). Notably, the overall mortality did not appear to correlate with the number of concurrent co-infections. Several clinical severity indicators, including SOFA score ≥ 2 (HR = 2.59), hemoptysis (HR = 4.43), and radiological involvement of the right upper lobe (HR = 2.56), were consistently associated with increased mortality risk (HR > 1), indicating worse prognosis. In contrast, BMI showed a protective association (HR = 0.87). Other variables, including CD4 count and time from admission to diagnosis, did not show a consistent independent effect on survival. Among all patients with PC, the single co-infection was associated with increased mortality risk (HR = 2.04, p = 0.06), whereas estimates for dual co-infections were limited by the small sample size.

To enhance the prediction of survival outcomes, we developed a multivariate survival model optimized for the lowest AIC. The model incorporated key variables, including body mass index, SOFA score ≥ 2, hemoptysis, right upper lung lesions on CT imaging, CD4 count, and the time from admission to diagnosis. In addition, according to the result revealing that PLWH faced a significantly higher mortality risk-11.3 times greater than HIV-negative individuals (Table 2), we performed a focused analysis of 10-week mortality in PLWH. Additionally, given the inclusion of both APACHE II and SOFA in the final model, we examined potential multicollinearity among the retained variables. VIFs were low for APACHE II (1.26), SOFA (1.21), and antifungal therapy (1.16), indicating no substantial multicollinearity. The results identified several critical prognostic factors: a SOFA score ≥ 2 (HR = 7.27), a high-risk APACHE II score (HR = 12.7), and the absence of treatment with amphotericin B, flucytosine, or fluconazole (HR = 4.84). These findings highlight the urgent need for timely, targeted antifungal therapy to improve survival outcomes in this high-risk population (STable 4).

Discussion

Previous studies have identified co-infection as an emerging challenge in the diagnosis and management of cryptococcal disease, particularly among PLWH. However, the available evidence is derived largely from case reports and small case series, with limited cohort-based observational studies to date (Supplementary Methods and STable 1). To our knowledge, this is the first observational study focused on co-infections in patients with PC, involving a cohort of 454 patients recruited from two major infectious disease centers in eastern coastal China. Aligning with previous case report studies [17–20], our findings highlight the increasing clinical burden of co-infections, particularly among PLWH, who are disproportionately affected by multiple opportunistic pathogens.

Consistent with prior studies [29, 30], we found that PLWH exhibited a higher risk of extrapulmonary dissemination than non-HIV patients. Interestingly, in our cohort, PLWH with isolated cryptococcal infections had higher dissemination rates than those with co-infections. This aligns with animal studies showing that Pseudomonas aeruginosa co-infection can inhibit cryptococcus growth and enhance macrophage activity, thereby limiting spread to the CNS [31]. We hypothesize that, in PLWH, co-infections might elicit a more robust immune response that curtails fungal dissemination. While the opposite trend was observed in non-HIV patients: those with co-infections showed increased extra pulmonary spread, albeit with a limited sample size. These findings collectively point to a nuanced role for co-infections, which both complicate clinical management and, in some instances, may provide a protective effect against dissemination.In the overall cohort, the single co-infection showed a trend toward increased mortality risk (HR = 2.04; p = 0.06), whereas the effect of dual co-infections could not be reliably estimated because of the small number of patients in this subgroup. Larger prospective studies are therefore needed to clarify the prognostic impact of multiple co-infections. Additionally, our finding that co-infection with PCP observed exclusively in PLWH is an independent risk factor for mortality underscores the varying impacts of specific co-infecting pathogens, such as PCP and tuberculosis, on disease severity highlight the importance of pathogen-specific diagnostic and management strategies. Symptom profiles also differed by HIV status and co-infection. Non-HIV patients predominantly presented with asymptomatic or localized respiratory symptoms, whereas PLWH more often experienced fever or CNS manifestations, likely reflecting their immunocompromised state and systemic infection spread. In co-infected PLWH, severe symptoms—such as cough, unconsciousness, and dyspnea—were more common, while in co-infected non-HIV patients, sputum production and dyspnea predominated. Imaging findings revealed that nodules and patchy lesions were frequent in the non-HIV co-infection group, complicating diagnostic differentiation and necessitating more attentive radiological evaluation.

Our data also indicate that delayed recognition of cryptococcal disease and co-infections contributes to worse outcomes. Patients in the non-HIV group with co-infections experienced a longer time to diagnosis compared to those in the PLWH group. These delays may result from atypical clinical presentations, underrecognition of co-infections in immunocompetent populations, or a lower index of suspicion for cryptococcosis in non-HIV settings. Consequently, co-infected PC patients exhibited no significant difference in 1-year survival between PLWH and non-HIV groups, likely due to comparable poor outcomes stemming from delayed diagnosis and treatment.

Mirroring with previous studies, traditional HIV severity markers such as viral load and CD4+ T cell counts did not fully capture the risk profile in PLWH [32, 33]. Although the VACS Index, has shown promise in Western populations, its applicability in our Chinese PLWH cohort was limited and did not correlate with co-infection status [22, 34]. This highlights the need to adapt or develop more comprehensive and locally relevant assessment tools that account for regional patterns of co-infection and host factors. In current study, due to the limited sample size in the non-HIV co-infected group, developing a reliable prediction model for this population was challenging. Instead, our developed predictive model across the entire cohort identified key mortality prognostic factors, including BMI, SOFA score ≥ 2, hemoptysis, and RUL lesions on CT imaging, CD4 count, and time from admission to diagnosis, encompassing physiological, radiological, and severity-related dimensions. Among PLWH, we further identified that a SOFA score ≥ 2, high-risk APACHE II score, and non-standard antifungal therapy markedly elevated 10-week mortality, in line with prior evidence indicating the prognostic value of these severity tools in immunocompromised patients. The link between suboptimal antifungal regimens and higher mortality underscores the importance of guideline-adherent treatment protocols, particularly in resource-limited settings where co-infections can divert clinical focus.

While our study provides valuable insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. The observational design limits causal inference regarding the association between co-infection and mortality. The small sizes of several subgroups also restricted assessment of additive risks, and these findings should therefore be considered exploratory. Exclusion of patients without follow-up data may have introduced selection bias. Although diagnostic delay was included in the survival models, its potential confounding effect should be examined further in larger cohorts. Future studies integrating advanced immunologic and microbiologic approaches may also help clarify the mechanisms underlying adverse outcomes in co-infected patients. In addition, HIV-positive and HIV-negative patients were recruited from different centers, and potential center-specific effects could therefore not be fully separated from those associated with HIV status. Finally, despite the inclusion of two centers, the generalizability of our findings to other populations and settings may remain limited. Larger prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate these results and refine clinical management strategies. In summary, our findings contribute to a growing framework for addressing the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of co-infections in pulmonary cryptococcosis. Stratifying patients by specific co-infection statuses and tailoring interventions for high-risk groups may help improve clinical outcomes.
