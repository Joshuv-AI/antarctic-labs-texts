Redox reactions in subducted CaCO3–Fe–SiO2 as a potential source of superdeep diamond inclusions
Authors: Mengqi Guo, Xinyang Li, Zhu Mao, Fangfei Li, Liang Li, Qiang Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaCO3 is one of the most abundant carbonates at the Earth’s surface. It is transported into the mantle in substantial quantities via subducted slabs and participates in the deep mantle carbon cycle along with other mantle materials. Our experiments utilized the CaCO3–Fe–SiO2 system to simulate the environment of subducted oceanic crust entering the deep mantle. In situ X-ray diffraction experiments were performed on the CaCO3–Fe–SiO2 system at pressures of 25–53 GPa and temperatures of 1200–2250 K using a laser-heated diamond anvil cell. The results indicate that redox reactions occur within the pressure range of 25–53 GPa and the temperature range of 1500–2000 K, leading to the formation of CaSiO3 + FeO + Fe3C. At higher temperatures of 2000–2250 K and pressures of 25–53 GPa, the products are CaSiO3 + Fe3O4 + Fe7C3. The mineral assemblage of Fe3O4 and Fe7C3, crystallizing under temperature conditions corresponding to the slab-mantle interface, can be encapsulated as inclusions in superdeep diamonds, such as those from the Juina area of Brazil. Introduction

Carbonates are typically found in the sedimentary layers overlying subducted oceanic crust. Although a portion of these sediments may be scraped off during subduction, the remaining carbonates within the sediments and the altered oceanic crust continue to be transported into the mantle via the subducted slab1. Among these carbonates, CaCO3 is one of the most abundant on the Earth’s surface. It exists as calcite at surface conditions and undergoes multiple phase transitions during subduction into the mantle. Under high-pressure and high-temperature conditions, it sequentially transforms into aragonite, the CaCO3-VII phase, post-aragonite, and the CaCO3-P21/c-h phase2–5. The carbon in the CaCO3-P21/c-h phase is in the sp3 hybridization state, which exhibits higher stability compared to sp2 carbon5. During subduction, CaCO3 may react with mantle pyroxene and/or olivine and could become destabilized upon slab melting, which are factors limiting the transport of surficial CaCO3 into the deeper mantle6,7. However, significant amounts of carbonate are still preserved within colder subducted slabs and altered oceanic crust. The previous discovery of CaCO3 inclusions in diamonds from the lower mantle in Brazil demonstrates that CaCO3 can survive transport to the lower mantle8. Carbonates are predominantly hosted in the subducted slab sediments, which also contain substantial amounts of SiO2 (approximately 30–50 wt%)9. Due to silicate disproportionation, the mantle at depths greater than 250 km is generally saturated with metallic iron (Fe0)10,11. Consequently, when subducted slab enters the deep mantle, interactions within the CaCO3-Fe binary system and the CaCO3-Fe-SiO2 ternary system become inevitable at the slab sediment-mantle interface.

Regarding the interaction between CaCO3 and Fe, previous studies using multi-anvil apparatus have investigated their redox reactions at the slab-mantle interface. These works revealed that at 4 GPa, 6 GPa, and 16 GPa and 923–1673 K, Fe7C3 and CaFe2O3 form12. High-temperature experiments at 6 GPa showed that between 1273 and 1473 K, CaCO3 and Fe produce FeCa2O3 and Fe3C; at 1473–1673 K, the products are CaCO2.5 (Liquid, L, hereafter), Fe4CaO5, Fe3C (L), and C; while at 1873 K, CaCO2.5 (L), Fe4CaO5, and C are generated13. Redox experiments on (Mg, Ca)CO3 + Fe conducted at 6.5–7.5 GPa and 1273–1923 K demonstrated the formation of diamond, (Fe, Mg)O, Fe3C, and (Ca, Mg, Fe)CO314. Further investigations into the reaction between (Mg, Ca)CO3 and Fe at 51–113 GPa and 1800–2500 K confirmed redox reactions yielding diamond, MgO, FeO, and Fe7C315. In contrast, studies on the CaCO3 + SiO2system are relatively limited, with only Li et al2. reporting that CaCO3 + SiO2 at 20–60 GPa and 1200–2300 K produced CaSiO3 and CO2.

We investigated the redox reactions of CaCO3 + Fe at 46 GPa and 1380–1820 K, and CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 at 25–53 GPa and 1200–2250 K to simulate reactions at the slab-mantle interface in the lower mantle. In situ laser-heated X-ray diffraction experiments on the CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 system revealed the formation of CaSiO3 + FeO + Fe3C within the pressure range of 25–53 GPa and temperature range of 1500–2000 K, and the formation of CaSiO3 + Fe3O4 + Fe7C3 at 25–53 GPa and 2000–2250 K. The reaction products Fe3O4 and Fe7C3 may be encapsulated as inclusions in superdeep diamonds, such as those from the Juina area, Brazil, providing critical constraints on the pressure and temperature conditions of diamond origin.

Results

We conducted in-situ laser heating and X-ray diffraction experiments on the CaCO3-Fe binary system and the CaCO3-Fe-SiO2 ternary system, respectively. For the CaCO3-Fe binary system, the CaCO3 + Fe mixture was compressed to 46 GPa at room temperature, and its redox reactions were investigated within the temperature range of 1380–1820 K. For the CaCO3-Fe-SiO2 ternary system, the CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 mixture was compressed to 26.3 GPa, 34.5 GPa, 42.2 GPa, and 50.4 GPa at room temperature before being heated, and its redox reactions were studied under conditions of 25–53 GPa and 1200–2250 K.

CaCO3–Fe binary system

In the CaCO3 + Fe experiment (Fig. S1), compression to 46 GPa at room temperature yielded diffraction peaks corresponding to CaCO3, Fe, and NaCl. Upon heating to 1380 K, the appearance of FeO diffraction peaks was detected. Additionally, numerous other diffraction peaks emerged that could not be indexed to any known iron carbides (e.g., Fe3C, Fe7C3, Fe2C) or calcium oxides (e.g., CaO, CaFe2O3). Examination of the diffraction patterns showed no evidence of sodium carbonate or other Na-containing products, indicating that the NaCl used as thermal insulation and pressure-transmitting medium on both sides of the sample chamber did not react with the sample under the experimental conditions. This suggests the possible formation of previously unreported iron carbide and calcium oxide phases with distinct stoichiometries, which resulted in the incomplete indexing of the reaction products in this CaCO3 + Fe high-pressure and high-temperature experiment.

CaCO3–Fe–SiO2 ternary system

In the CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 experiment (Fig. 1), diffraction peaks of CaCO3, Fe, and NaCl were detected upon compression to 34.5 GPa at room temperature, while SiO2 remained amorphous, as evidenced by the absence of distinct diffraction peaks. When laser-heated to 1580 K and 36.6 GPa, SiO2 crystallized, and stishovite appeared in the XRD patterns, along with many other new peaks, indicating that the initial mixture of CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 underwent a redox reaction. Phase identification was carried out by matching the experimentally observed diffraction peaks with previously reported crystal structures2,16–20, through which the newly appeared peaks were identified as belonging to FeO, Fe3C and CaSiO3. In Fig. 1, the characteristic reflections associated with each phase are labeled with their respective hkl indices, indicating the specific diffraction planes used for phase assignment. When the system was compressed to 42.2 GPa at ambient temperature and subsequently heated to 2070 K and 44.1 GPa, the diffraction peaks underwent significant changes again, indicating the occurrence of a second redox reaction: 6CaCO3 + 23Fe + 6SiO2 = 6CaSiO3 + 3Fe3O4 + 2Fe7C3 (Fig. 1). In Fig. 1, the pattern at 40.9 GPa and 300 K was obtained after quenching. The diffraction peaks of CaSiO3 + Fe3O4 + Fe7C3 remained stable, and strong diffraction peaks of Fe3O4 were observed. As shown in Fig. 2, when CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 was compressed and heated to 28.6 GPa and 1607 K, 36.6 GPa and 1580 K, and 53.0 GPa and 1753 K, respectively, the mixture underwent a redox reaction, producing CaSiO3 + FeO + Fe3C. When heated to a higher temperature of 2070 K at 44.1 GPa, a second redox reaction occurred, producing CaSiO3 + Fe3O4 + Fe7C3. Detailed pathway information for these redox reactions is provided in Table S1 of the Supplementary Information.

Discussion

Previous studies have primarily focused on binary systems such as CaCO3 + Fe or CaCO3 + SiO22,12,13. Regarding the investigation of CaCO3and Fe, Martirosyan et al13. reported that at 6 GPa and 1273–1473 K, FeCa2O3 and Fe3C formed; between 1473 and 1673 K, the products were CaCO2.5 (L), Fe4CaO5, Fe3C (L), and C; while at 1873 K, CaCO2.5 (L), Fe4CaO5, and C were generated. Martirosyan et al12. further observed the formation of Fe7C3 and CaFe2O3 under conditions of 4–16 GPa and 923–1673 K, where FeCa2O3, Fe4CaO5, and CaFe2O3 are identified as solid solutions of CaO and FeO. In the CaCO3 + SiO2 system, Li et al2. found that CaSiO3 and CO2 formed at 20–60 GPa and 1200–2300 K. These results indicate that the presence or absence of Fe significantly influences the carbon valence state in the system. Our experimental study focuses on the ternary system CaCO3–Fe–SiO2. Upon laser heating to approximately 1500 K, the reaction CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 → CaSiO3 + FeO + Fe3C occurs, in which carbon exists in the form of iron carbide (Fig. 2), and calcium from CaCO3 transforms into CaSiO3. Compared with previous studies on the CaCO3 + Fe reaction, where CaO combined with FeO to form CaFe2O312, in our system CaO binds with SiO2 to form CaSiO3, indicating the high reactivity of CaO under high pressure and temperature. When the temperature was increased to approximately 1900 K, the form of carbon in the reaction products shifted from Fe3C to Fe7C3.This transition is likely attributable to the intrinsic transformation of Fe3C at elevated temperatures18: Fe3C → Fe7C3 + Liquid.

The redox reaction involving CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 may proceed via two distinct pathways. The first possible sequence involves the initial reaction between CaCO3 and Fe, forming Fe–C compounds, Fe–O compounds, and Ca–Fe–O compounds, followed by the reaction of Ca–Fe–O compounds with SiO2 to yield CaSiO3 and Fe–O compounds identical to those formed in the preceding step. The second possible sequence begins with the reaction between CaCO3 and SiO2, producing CO2 and CaSiO3, after which CO2 reacts with Fe to form Fe–C and Fe–O compounds. Regarding the first pathway where CaCO3 and Fe react initially, our experiments on the CaCO3 + Fe system at 46 GPa and 1380–1820 K demonstrate the formation of FeO, but no Fe3C was detected (Fig. S1). In contrast, in the CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 system, the products include CaSiO3, FeO, and Fe3C. If CaCO3 and Fe had reacted first, both FeO and Fe3C should have been observed. The absence of Fe3C in the binary system suggests that this pathway is not operative. This indicates that in the CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 system, the reaction between CaCO3 and SiO2 to form CO2 and CaSiO3 occurs first, followed by the reaction between CO2 and Fe to produce Fe–C and Fe–O compounds (Fig. 2). Therefore, the fundamental nature of the redox reaction in CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 is essentially the reaction between CO2 and Fe.

Geological implications

The carbon in CaCO3 is reduced to iron carbide, which is often found in diamond inclusions, indicating the complex environment of diamond formation8,23. Diamond is a product of the carbon cycle between the Earth’s surface and the mantle. The mineral inclusions within diamonds serve as primary samples that directly reveal the composition, temperature, and redox conditions of the Earth’s deep interior8,24–29. As a unique carbon reservoir, diamonds record the complete deep carbon cycle process, whereby surface carbon is subducted into the mantle via plate tectonics and returned to the surface through volcanic eruptions8,24,26,28,30. Most diamonds discovered to date originate from the lithospheric mantle at depths of 150–240 km, accounting for 95% of observed specimens24. Their formation is primarily attributed to C–O–H fluids from the upper mantle24,31. Diamonds found below 250 km are referred to as superdeep diamonds. Due to a shift in the mantle’s redox conditions toward a more reduced state at such depths27,32, their formation mechanisms are more complex and can only be inferred by analyzing the types of inclusions within diamonds combined with high-pressure and high-temperature laboratory experiments. Diamond inclusions act as natural depth recorders33–37. Previous studies have determined the formation pressures and temperatures of diamonds by analyzing the chemical composition of inclusion minerals in shallow diamonds and applying the principles of phase equilibrium, which describe the stable coexistence of specific mineral assemblages under unique pressure and temperature conditions33–37. However, determining the depth origin of superdeep diamonds through inclusion analysis remains challenging due to complex mineral assemblages and redox conditions in the transition zone and lower mantle. Currently, only limited studies provide such constraints. For instance, the discovery of hydrous ringwoodite inclusions in diamonds from the Juina area, Brazil, indicates a mantle transition zone origin at depths of 525–660 km38. The presence of bridgmanite inclusions suggests a lower mantle origin39. Similarly, the identification of the perovskite-structured polymorph of CaSiO3 in diamonds from the South African Cullinan kimberlite implies formation at depths greater than 660 km40. Additionally, Fe–Ni–C–S metallic melt inclusions indicate diamond crystallization between 360 and 750 km20,41. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the stability fields of inclusion minerals provide crucial constraints for determining the formation depths of superdeep diamonds20,38–42.

We investigated the redox reactions involving CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 in the lower mantle, where CaCO3 is one of the primary carbonates in the sediments of subducted oceanic crust1, SiO2 is an abundant mineral in sediments9, and Fe is present in a saturated state in the mantle at depths greater than 250 km due to the disproportionation of silicate minerals10,11. Our study demonstrates that during the redox reaction among CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2, the reaction between CaCO3 and SiO2 occurs first, and the resulting CO2 subsequently reacts with Fe. Specifically, after the subducted slab enters the lower mantle, CaCO3 in the sediments reacts with SiO2 to form CaSiO3 and CO2. The CO2 then migrates out of the slab and reacts with metallic iron in the mantle, forming Fe3O4 + Fe7C3 in the mantle (Fig. 3).

The encapsulation of reaction products within diamond enables the determination of the pressure-temperature conditions at its source. Previous studies have discovered diamonds from the lower mantle in the Juina area of Brazil, with inclusions comprising iron carbides, native iron, magnetite, and graphite8. The iron carbides exhibit multiple stoichiometries, including Fe7C3, which matches the Fe3O4 + Fe7C3 assemblage generated in our experiments from the CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 reaction at the lower mantle. The native iron and graphite in these inclusions may have exsolved from an iron-carbon melt, while other stoichiometries of iron carbide may have formed through the decomposition of Fe7C3 during decompression8,20,41,43. In previous studies of diamond inclusions containing iron, iron carbides, and magnetite, magnetite has often been interpreted as a secondary phase formed by the partial oxidation of iron carbide and native iron8,23. This is because iron and iron carbides typically form under relatively reducing conditions, whereas magnetite is stable under more oxidized environments. In contrast, our study demonstrates that redox reactions in the lower mantle can simultaneously produce both magnetite and iron carbides. These phases may be encapsulated as inclusions within superdeep diamonds formed in the lower mantle, thereby constituting a mineral assemblage indicative of the pressure-temperature conditions of diamond formation. This finding suggests that diamonds from the Juina area, Brazil, containing assemblages of iron carbide + native iron + magnetite + graphite, may have originated within the pressure-temperature range corresponding to the stability field of Fe3O4 + Fe7C3 in our experiments (25–53 GPa and 2000–2250 K), which corresponds to depths of approximately 700–1300 km in the mantle. Our experiments thus provide a well-constrained reference for both the formation depth and the mineral paragenesis of diamond inclusions, offering key experimental mineralogical evidence for interpreting diamonds originating from the upper part of the lower mantle.

Methods

Laser-heated diamond anvil cell

The starting materials consisted of mixtures of CaCO3 powder, SiO2 powder, and Fe powder. These three powders were dried overnight in an oven at 120 °C. Two powder mixtures were then prepared with mass ratios of CaCO3:Fe at 100:7 and CaCO3:Fe: SiO2 at 100:195:7, respectively. Each mixture was ground under an inert atmosphere for 5 h. The homogenized powders were subsequently pressed into foils with a thickness of 10 μm and loaded into the sample chamber. The experiments employed 300 μm diameter diamond anvils to generate pressure. Rhenium gaskets were pre-indented to a thickness of 40 μm, and holes with diameters of 160 μm were drilled at the centers of the indentations to serve as sample chambers. During the loading of the diamond anvil cell (DAC), NaCl foils with a thickness of 3–5 μm were placed on both sides of the sample foil to act as both thermal insulation and a pressure-transmitting medium. The Fe powder present in both starting material mixtures served as a laser-absorbing material. Due to the multiplicity of materials loaded in the sample chamber, NaCl was employed as a pressure calibrant to preclude potential extraneous reactions. Pressure was obtained by fitting the lattice volumes of NaCl in the high pressure and temperature diffraction patterns to its established high pressure and temperature equation of state44, with the associated uncertainties summarized in Table S1.

X-ray diffraction

High-pressure XRD data collection for the CaCO3 + Fe system was conducted at the Extreme Conditions Beamline (ECB, P02.2) of the PETRA III synchrotron radiation source at DESY. Experiments were conducted using an X-ray energy of 42.7 keV, with the beam focused to a spot size of 3 × 8 µm2 (horizontal × vertical). Detector parameters, including sample-to-detector distance and tilt angles, were calibrated using XRD patterns of a NIST CeO2 standard reference material (674b). XRD data collection for the CaCO3 + Fe + SiO2 system was performed at the GeoSoilEnviroCARS (GSECARS) beamline of the Advanced Photon Source (APS), Argonne National Laboratory. Diffraction patterns were collected using a 165MAR-CCD detector with an X-ray energy of 37 keV and a beam size focused to 3 × 4 µm2 (horizontal × vertical). Data processing was carried out using the Dioptas software45. Temperature measurement was performed by fitting the thermal radiation spectra, collected from both sides of the sample after background subtraction, to the Planck radiation function. Spectral analysis and temperature reading were conducted using the T-Rax software46, and the corresponding uncertainties are reported in Table S1.
