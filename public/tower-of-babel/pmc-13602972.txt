Two‐Decade Trends in Radiotherapy Patterns and Clinical Outcomes for Soft Tissue Sarcoma: A Nationwide Study in South Korea (2005–2023)
Authors: Tae Hyung Kim, Jun‐Hyuk Lee, Kyungtae Hong, Hyuntak Koh, Seo Hee Choi, Hong In Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSoft tissue sarcoma (STS) is a rare malignancy with increasing utilization of advanced radiotherapy (RT). We evaluated nationwide transitions in RT patterns and clinical outcomes for STS in South Korea, focusing on the impact of national healthcare policies.MethodsUtilizing the National Health Insurance Service database (2005–2023), we identified 52,245 patients with STS. Temporal trends in RT modalities (3D‐conformal RT [3D‐CRT] vs. intensity‐modulated RT [IMRT]) and fractions per course were analyzed using joinpoint regression with a pre‐specified breakpoint at 2014. Survival analysis was performed for 45,465 patients using Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsThe annual incidence of STS increased from 2338 to 3066 cases. A definitive shift toward IMRT occurred following the 2015 insurance expansion; by 2023, IMRT utilization reached 17.5%, while 3D‐CRT declined to 0.3%. A sharp increase in mean fraction count was identified in 2012, with mean fractions rising from < 8 to > 16, indicating a marked shift in fractionation practice. In survival analysis, RT groups showed higher hazard ratios (HRs) for mortality (3D‐CRT: 1.24; IMRT: 1.32), reflecting confounding by indication where RT was reserved for high‐risk patients. However, in the non‐surgery subgroup, IMRT was associated with a lower mortality risk than 3D‐CRT (HR 1.20 vs. 1.25). Similarly, in pediatric patients, IMRT demonstrated a lower HR compared with 3D‐CRT (3.35 vs. 4.19).ConclusionRT for STS in Korea has transitioned to an IMRT‐centered paradigm, catalyzed by supportive insurance policies. In non‐surgery and pediatric cases, IMRT showed a lower HR than 3D‐CRT, though this hypothesis‐generating finding does not establish a survival benefit.Keywords: intensity‐modulated radiotherapy, National Health Insurance Service, soft tissue sarcoma, survival analysis

Introduction

Soft tissue sarcoma (STS) is a rare and highly heterogeneous group of mesenchymal malignancies, representing approximately 1% of all adult solid cancers [1, 2]. Comprising more than 50 distinct histological subtypes, STS can arise from almost any anatomical site, including muscle, fat, and nerve tissues. In Korea, the total incidence of STS is estimated at 2.49 per 100,000 person‐years, with a 5‐year survival rate of 56.4% for all cases and 82.4% for localized disease [3]. Due to its rarity and diversity, establishing standardized management protocols remains a significant challenge in clinical oncology.

The standard of care for localized STS is surgical resection, which is increasingly integrated into a multidisciplinary framework including chemotherapy and radiotherapy (RT) [4, 5]. RT plays a critical role in optimizing local control, and its utilization has grown significantly over time; institutional data in Korea shows that the use of perioperative RT increased from a mere 1.4% in the pre‐2000 era to over 22% in the 2010s [6]. This growth has been accompanied by a major technological transition from three‐dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D‐CRT) to advanced modalities such as intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), which allows for superior dose distribution while sparing adjacent normal tissues [7].

The adoption of these advanced RT technologies in Korea has been decisively shaped by national healthcare governance. A pivotal milestone occurred in 2015, when the National Health Insurance (NHI) expanded its coverage for IMRT to include all solid tumors. Prior to this expansion, the high cost of IMRT served as a significant barrier for many STS patients. Additionally, in September 2015, coverage for proton therapy was expanded to all pediatric cancers and specific adult malignancies, further broadening the landscape of precision RT in Korea. These policy shifts were intended to facilitate migration toward more precise treatment modalities and improve the overall quality of oncological care.

Despite these advancements, existing STS research in Korea has primarily focused on institutional registries or specific clinical subgroups within limited observation periods [3, 6]. While these studies have provided valuable pathological insights, they are often constrained by their single‐center nature or may not fully reflect the long‐term, nationwide transition of treatment patterns. Crucially, there is a paucity of longitudinal evidence detailing how advanced RT technologies have been adopted across the entire nation in response to evolving healthcare policies.

To address these gaps, this study utilizes the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database to analyze an unprecedented, nationwide cohort of over 50,000 patients with STS. By tracing the transition from 3D‐CRT to IMRT alongside changes in fractionation practice and clinical outcomes over the 2005–2023 period, we aim to characterize how the 2015 insurance expansion reshaped RT delivery for this rare malignancy at a national scale.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study utilized claims data from the Korean NHIS database, which provides universal health coverage to the entire Korean population and maintains comprehensive records of diagnostic codes, treatment procedures, and vital status [8]. The NHIS supports 40%–70% of medical expenses based on its criteria and assigns V codes to reduce costs for severe diseases to 5%. Mortality data are linked to Statistics Korea for NHIS‐registered patients.

Study Population

The study was conducted in two analytic frameworks: a full cohort for examining temporal trends in RT utilization and fractionation patterns, and an analytic cohort with a 3‐month lag period for survival analysis. A total of 52,245 patients newly diagnosed with soft tissue sarcoma between January 2005 and December 2023 were initially identified, including 41,759 who did not receive RT, 4130 treated with 3D‐CRT, and 6356 who received IMRT. For the survival analysis, patients who died within 3 months of diagnosis were excluded (n = 6780; 13.0%), yielding a final analytic cohort of 45,465 patients (no‐RT, n = 35,285; 3D‐CRT, n = 3975; IMRT, n = 6205).

Identification of Soft Tissue Sarcoma

Patients with soft tissue sarcoma were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes C22.3, C22.4, C47, C48, C49, and C54.2 in conjunction with Korea's cancer‐specific insurance claim code V193, which is assigned to patients with a confirmed diagnosis of malignancy. Detailed disease‐to‐code mappings are provided in Table S1.

Because codes C22.3, C22.4, and C54.2 capture entities that are not exclusively soft‐tissue sarcoma (hepatic angiosarcoma, other malignant connective‐tissue tumors of the liver, and myometrial malignancy, respectively) but may also represent true STS, a sensitivity analysis was performed excluding all patients who carried any of these three codes, irrespective of concurrent core soft‐tissue‐sarcoma coding (Table S4).

Classification of RT Modality

RT modality was determined based on the specification codes within the NHIS claims database. 3D‐CRT was identified using procedure codes for low‐, intermediate‐, and high‐energy RT (HD051, HD052, and HD053), and IMRT was identified using the dedicated procedure code HZ271. Patients who did not have any RT‐related claims during the study period were classified into the no‐RT group.

RT Timing

To characterize the temporal relationship between RT and surgery, claim dates were used to classify the sequence of RT relative to surgery among patients with a captured surgical procedure within a peri‐diagnostic episode window (90 days before to 365 days after diagnosis). RT was categorized as preceding surgery, following surgery, occurring on the same date, or of indeterminate order when both procedures were recorded within the same claim period. Because the NHIS database does not capture treatment intent, this classification reflects claim‐date sequence only and should not be interpreted as neoadjuvant, adjuvant, definitive, or palliative intent.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the temporal transitions in RT modality utilization and the mean number of fractions per course, and the secondary outcome was all‐cause mortality. For the survival analysis, the follow‐up period for each patient was calculated from the date of soft tissue sarcoma diagnosis to the date of death or censored at December 31, 2023, whichever came first.

Covariates

Covariates included sex, age at diagnosis, low‐income status, residential area. Low‐income status was defined as receiving medical aid or falling within the lowest quintile of NHIS insurance premiums. Residential area was classified as urban, including Seoul, Gyeonggi‐do, and metropolitan cities, or rural. Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) score was calculated. Baseline surgery was defined using EDI procedure categories captured from 90 days before diagnosis through the diagnosis date, including procedures such as skin‐tumor excision and lymph‐node surgery; further detail is provided in Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of the study population were compared across the three RT groups using chi‐square tests for categorical variables and analysis of variance for a continuous variable. Temporal trends in the incidence of soft tissue sarcoma and the utilization of each RT modality were evaluated using joinpoint regression analysis with a pre‐specified breakpoint at 2014 to evaluate the impact of the 2015 expansion of national health insurance coverage for advanced RT techniques, and the annual percentage change (APC) with 95% CIs was estimated for each segment. Trends in IMRT adoption rate were additionally modeled using logit‐linear regression, with results expressed as odds ratios per year (OR/yr) with 95% CIs. Changes in RT fractionation patterns over the study period were described using the mean number of fractions per course per year for each modality.

For survival analysis, Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for all‐cause mortality according to RT modality, with attained age incorporated as the underlying time scale. Three models were constructed with sequential adjustment: an unadjusted model, Model 1 adjusted for sex, and Model 2 adjusted for sex, low‐income status, residential area, and CCI score. Stratified analyses were performed to examine whether the association between RT modality and mortality differed by anatomical site (extremity versus other sites), surgical status (surgery versus no surgery), and adult status (age < 19 years versus age ≥ 19 years). To further evaluate the impact of surgery while avoiding immortal‐time bias, an exploratory Cox model was fitted with surgery entered as a time‐varying covariate (Table S2).

To address potential confounding by indication inherent to this observational design, we additionally performed propensity‐score‐based analysis, including inverse‐probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and 1:1 propensity‐score matching (PSM). The propensity score model included age, age squared, diagnosis year, diagnosis era, sex, low‐income status, residential area, CCI category, extremity site, claims‐based baseline surgery, and pediatric status. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with |SMD| ≥ 0.10 pre‐specified as indicating imbalance; analyses that failed to achieve balance were reported as supportive or exploratory only. Three sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) a 90‐day landmark period combined with an active‐comparator design comparing IMRT versus 3D‐CRT directly; (2) analyses stratified by diagnosis era and after exclusion of rare diagnostic codes; and (3) age‐restricted analyses comparing adult and pediatric subgroups.

To evaluate whether pooling across the 19‐year study period confounded the principal findings, we performed diagnosis‐era‐stratified analyses using the breakpoints identified in the joinpoint analyses (2005–2011, 2012–2014, and 2015–2023, corresponding to the pre‐expansion, transition, and post‐expansion periods, respectively; Table S4).

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide (version 7.1; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R (version 4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a two‐sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the Study Population

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study population, highlighting distinct clinical profiles according to the receipt and modality of RT. Overall, patients receiving RT (both 3D‐CRT and IMRT) were more likely to be male and younger compared to the no‐RT group (p < 0.001). The mean age at diagnosis was lowest in the 3D‐CRT group (53.1 ± 17.7 years), followed by the IMRT group (55.9 ± 17.4 years) and the no‐RT group (55.3 ± 19.6 years; p < 0.001). Individuals aged 70 years or older constituted the largest proportion in the no‐RT group (25.4%), whereas the 60–69 age group was the most represented in the IMRT group (24.3%) and the 50–59 age group in the 3D‐CRT group (21.3%). Urban residence was more prevalent in the RT groups (3D‐CRT, 69.3%; IMRT, 70.1%) compared with the no RT (66.6%, p < 0.001). Regarding comorbidity burden, a CCI score of 4 or higher was observed in 95.4% of the 3D‐CRT group and 94.6% of the IMRT group, compared with 92.0% in the no‐RT group (p < 0.001).

Temporal Trends in RT Modalities and Fractionation

Figure 1 shows the temporal trends in soft tissue sarcoma incidence and RT modality utilization over the 19‐year study period. The annual incidence of soft tissue sarcoma increased from 2338 cases in 2005 to 3066 cases in 2023 (Figure 1A). Joinpoint regression analysis identified a breakpoint in 2014; the APC was 1.6% (95% CI, 0.4–2.9; p = 0.015) from 2005 to 2014, while no statistically significant increase was observed after the 2014 joinpoint (APC, 0.7%; 95% CI, −0.1 to 1.6; p = 0.093).

A shift in RT modality utilization was observed during the study period (Figure 1B). In 2005, 3D‐CRT and IMRT accounted for 14.5% and 2.2% of all cases, respectively. By 2015, the utilization rate of IMRT (12.9%) exceeded that of 3D‐CRT (6.2%). By 2023, 3D‐CRT utilization declined to 0.3%, while IMRT increased to 17.5%. Joinpoint regression with a pre‐specified breakpoint at 2014 showed that the OR per year for IMRT utilization was 1.33 (95% CI, 1.21–1.47; p < 0.001) during 2005–2014 and 1.41 (95% CI, 1.28–1.56; p < 0.001) during 2015–2023.

Figure 2 presents the temporal changes in fractionation patterns. Between 2005 and 2011, the mean number of fractions per course for 3D‐CRT ranged from 3.1 to 7.7. In 2012, coinciding with the expansion of national health insurance coverage for advanced RT techniques, the mean fraction count increased to 16.3 for 3D‐CRT and 19.4 for IMRT. Following this period, the mean number of fractions for both modalities stabilized, with 3D‐CRT averaging 15–17 fractions and IMRT averaging 18–19 fractions per course through 2023.

Annual changes in the mean number of radiotherapy fractions per treatment course by modality (2005–2023). The Y‐axis represents the mean number of fractions delivered per treatment course. Blue and red lines indicate 3D‐CRT and IMRT, respectively; error bars represent standard errors. The dotted vertical line at 2012 denotes the “intensity jump,” representing a marked shift in fractionation practice, characterized by a significant increase in the mean number of fractions from < 8 to > 16. This transition reflects the nationwide implementation of standardized, high‐dose treatment courses for soft tissue sarcoma in South Korea.

RT Timing

Among 3356 RT recipients with a captured surgical procedure in the peri‐diagnostic episode window, RT followed surgery in 3089 patients (92.0%) and preceded surgery in 193 (5.8%); the remaining 74 patients (2.2%) had RT and surgery on the same date, RT both before and after surgery, or RT on multiple dates spanning the surgical date (Table S2), indicating a predominantly postoperative sequence overall. In an exploratory time‐dependent Cox model, surgery was associated with lower all‐cause mortality (HR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.49–0.53; Table S2).

Risk Factors for All‐Cause Mortality

During a median follow‐up of 3.5 years (interquartile range [IQR], 1.3–8.5), a total of 20,524 deaths were identified: 15,426 in the no‐RT group, 2497 in the 3D‐CRT group, and 2601 in the IMRT group. Table 2 and Figure 3 present the HRs for all‐cause mortality according to the RT modality. In the unadjusted analysis, both 3D‐CRT (HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.21–1.32; p < 0.001) and IMRT (HR, 1.34; 95% CI, 1.29–1.40; p < 0.001) showed a higher risk of mortality compared with the no‐RT group. These associations remained consistent after adjusting for sex in Model 1 (3D‐CRT: HR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.21–1.31; IMRT: HR, 1.34; 95% CI, 1.28–1.40) and for sex, low income, residential area, and CCI score in Model 2 (3D‐CRT: HR, 1.24; 95% CI, 1.19–1.30; IMRT: HR, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.26–1.37; all p < 0.001).

Regarding surgical status, patients in the surgery group (n = 7961) showed HRs of 1.32 (95% CI, 1.18–1.47) for 3D‐CRT and 1.79 (95% CI, 1.59–2.02) for IMRT. In contrast, among patients in the non‐surgery group (n = 37,504), the HRs for 3D‐CRT and IMRT were 1.25 (95% CI, 1.19–1.31) and 1.20 (95% CI, 1.15–1.26), respectively. Notably, in this non‐surgery subgroup, IMRT was associated with a numerically lower HR compared with 3D‐CRT.

Stratified Risk of Death by Age Group

Table S2 presents the mortality risk stratified by age group. Among patients aged < 19 years (n = 2515), the adjusted HRs in the multivariable model (Model 2) were 4.19 (95% CI, 3.25–5.25) for 3D‐CRT and 3.35 (95% CI, 2.66–4.22) for IMRT, relative to the no‐RT group. For the adult population (aged ≥ 19 years, n = 42,950), the corresponding Model 2 HRs were 1.20 (95% CI, 1.15–1.25) for 3D‐CRT and 1.27 (95% CI, 1.22–1.33) for IMRT. Notably, within the pediatric and adolescent subgroup (< 19 years), the IMRT group was associated with a numerically lower HR compared with the 3D‐CRT group. These associations remained consistent across the unadjusted analysis and both adjusted models—Model 1 (adjusted for sex) and Model 2 (adjusted for sex, low income, residential area, and CCI score).

Propensity‐Score‐Based and Sensitivity Analyses

To address potential confounding by indication, propensity‐score‐based analyses were performed. In the primary analysis (Table S3), the head‐to‐head comparison of IMRT versus 3D‐CRT showed balanced covariates and concordant estimates: IPTW HR, 0.626 (95% CI, 0.571–0.687; maximum |SMD|, 0.026) and 1:1 PSM HR, 0.784 (95% CI, 0.712–0.865; 1694 pairs; maximum |SMD|, 0.045). For the comparisons versus no RT, PSM achieved acceptable balance (3D‐CRT vs. no RT: HR, 1.484; 95% CI, 1.386–1.588; IMRT vs. no RT: HR, 1.195; 95% CI, 1.124–1.270; all |SMD| < 0.10), whereas the corresponding IPTW models retained residual imbalance (maximum |SMD|, 0.214 and 0.242, respectively) and are therefore presented as supportive only.

In sensitivity analyses using a 90‐day landmark period and active‐comparator design (Table S5), the balanced PSM estimates versus no RT were essentially null (3D‐CRT: HR, 1.015; 95% CI, 0.925–1.115; IMRT: HR, 1.001; 95% CI, 0.911–1.100), as was the IMRT‐versus‐3D‐CRT comparison (HR, 1.016; 95% CI, 0.858–1.203); the corresponding timing‐aligned IPTW models did not achieve balance (maximum |SMD| up to 0.878) and are reported for transparency only. In the age‐restricted analysis (Table S6), results in the adult subgroup were consistent with the primary analysis (PSM IMRT vs. 3D‐CRT: HR, 0.760; 3D‐CRT vs. no RT: HR, 1.421; IMRT vs. no RT: HR, 1.209), whereas the pediatric PSM analysis did not achieve acceptable covariate balance (maximum |SMD| up to 0.719) and is reported as exploratory only. Diagnosis‐era‐stratified analyses showed HRs that varied by era, with values relative to no RT of 1.54 (95% CI, 1.45–1.62) for 3D‐CRT and 0.78 (95% CI, 0.69–0.89) for IMRT during 2005–2011; 1.24 (95% CI, 1.12–1.37) and 0.95 (95% CI, 0.84–1.07) during 2012–2014; and 1.34 (95% CI, 1.21–1.49) and 1.28 (95% CI, 1.22–1.35) during 2015–2023, with the final stratum corresponding to the modern IMRT‐standard era (Table S4). A sensitivity analysis excluding 357 patients who carried any of codes C22.3, C22.4, or C54.2—all of whom also carried a core soft‐tissue‐sarcoma code—did not materially change the estimates (3D‐CRT vs. no RT: HR, 1.35; 95% CI, 1.29–1.40; IMRT vs. no RT: HR, 1.33; 95% CI, 1.27–1.39), confirming that the main results were robust to their inclusion (Table S4).

Across all balanced analyses, adjusted HRs for both RT modalities remained above 1.0 relative to no RT, consistent with substantial residual confounding by indication in the no‐RT comparisons.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the longitudinal evolution of RT patterns for STS in Korea, highlighting a substantial transition from 3D‐CRT to IMRT over two decades. The adoption of IMRT accelerated following the expansion of national health insurance coverage in 2015, leading to its emergence as the predominant RT modality by 2016. Furthermore, the marked increase in the mean number of fractions per course observed in 2012 signifies a fundamental shift in clinical practice from a predominantly palliative approach toward curative‐intent RT. This evolution reflects the synergy between technological maturation and administrative policy frameworks in optimizing the management of STS.

The accelerated adoption of IMRT observed in our study highlights the significant influence of national healthcare policy on the clinical dissemination of advanced medical technologies. The OR for IMRT utilization, which was 1.33 per year during the 2005–2014 period, increased to 1.41 per year following the 2015 coverage expansion. This rapid transition aligns with the nationwide survey by Rim et al. [9], which documented the extensive integration of high‐precision RT resources into the Korean clinical infrastructure, reinforcing IMRT as a primary treatment modality across the country. Consequently, by 2023, 3D‐CRT utilization in our STS cohort had declined to 0.3%, while IMRT reached a utilization rate of 17.5%, establishing it as the current standard of care for patients receiving RT. A comparable acceleration in IMRT adoption following the 2015 coverage expansion has also been reported across five major cancer types (lung, stomach, colorectal, breast, and liver) in a nationwide claims‐based analysis [10], supporting the generalizability of this policy‐driven modality shift beyond the rare‐tumor setting examined in the present study.

Survival analysis indicated that patients receiving RT (either 3D‐CRT or IMRT) had a higher risk of mortality compared to the no‐RT group. These findings may potentially reflect an underlying selection bias inherent in administrative claims databases [11]. Specifically, it is plausible to consider that this observation represents confounding by indication, as RT is typically reserved for patients presenting with higher disease severity and adverse prognostic factors—such as larger tumor size, deep‐seated location, or higher histological grade—that are known to be independent predictors of poor survival [12]. Therefore, elevated HRs in RT groups act as surrogate markers for higher disease severity rather than indicating a failure of the therapeutic modality.

Stratified analysis by surgical status and anatomical site further supports the presence of confounding by indication. Among patients who underwent surgery, the HR for IMRT was 1.79 (95% CI 1.59–2.02), which was higher than that for 3D‐CRT (HR 1.32; 95% CI 1.18–1.47). This likely reflects the preferential application of IMRT in clinically challenging scenarios, such as cases with positive surgical margins, complex anatomical constraints, or a high perceived risk of local recurrence requiring precise dose modulation [13]. Similarly, the higher HRs observed in extremity STS compared to other sites may reflect the selective use of RT in limb‐salvage procedures for high‐grade or deep‐seated tumors. Conversely, in the nonsurgical subgroup—representing inoperable or advanced‐stage disease—IMRT (HR 1.20) was associated with a lower mortality risk than 3D‐CRT (HR 1.25). This within‐group difference could reflect either a genuine contribution from the enhanced precision and normal tissue sparing offered by IMRT [14], or differential residual confounding by indication between the two RT modalities. Given that adjusted HRs for both modalities remained above 1.0 relative to no‐RT, and that the database lacks essentially all major prognostic covariates in STS, this finding should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory of a clinical benefit.

The stratified analysis by age group provides further insight into the clinical context of RT utilization in STS. In the pediatric and adolescent subgroup (< 19 years), we observed high HRs for mortality in the RT groups (HR 4.19 for 3D‐CRT and 3.35 for IMRT). These findings must be interpreted with caution due to the inherent limitations of the NHIS database, which lacks granular clinical information such as stage and histological grade. In clinical practice, RT for pediatric STS—including rhabdomyosarcoma or high‐grade small round cell tumors—is primarily reserved for patients with advanced disease or aggressive biological subtypes. Consequently, the elevated HRs in the RT groups likely reflect the higher baseline clinical risk of these patients—a phenomenon known as confounding by indication—rather than a negative impact of RT on survival. Notably, within this high‐risk pediatric cohort, IMRT demonstrated a relatively lower HR compared to 3D‐CRT. This difference could reflect either a genuine contribution from IMRT's superior dose distribution in managing these vulnerable patients, or differential residual confounding by indication between the two RT modalities. Given the markedly elevated HRs for both modalities relative to no‐RT and the absence of histology and staging data in this database, this finding should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory of a clinical benefit.

To further probe the head‐to‐head comparison between RT modalities, we performed propensity‐score‐based analyses with time alignment. While the primary propensity‐score models (IPTW and PSM, without landmark adjustment) suggested a lower mortality risk for IMRT relative to 3D‐CRT, this apparent advantage was attenuated to near‐null once a 90‐day landmark period and active‐comparator design were applied (HR, 1.016; 95% CI, 0.858–1.203). This discrepancy suggests that the unadjusted head‐to‐head comparison may be susceptible to immortal‐time or guarantee‐time bias, whereby patients must survive long enough after diagnosis to receive and be classified under a given RT modality. We therefore interpret the time‐aligned, landmark‐adjusted estimate as the more methodologically robust reference point, and regard it as reinforcing our overall conclusion that observed differences between RT modalities are more consistent with residual confounding and time‐related bias than with a true differential treatment effect.

The 2012 “intensity jump” marks a critical inflection point in Korean radiation oncology. Since its inception, IMRT has consistently maintained a higher mean number of fractions (18–19) than 3D‐CRT. Given the capability of IMRT to deliver conformal, dose‐escalated regimens to complex target volumes while sparing adjacent critical organs [13], this sustained fractionation pattern may be reasonably interpreted as a population‐level signal of a broader shift toward more definitive‐intent RT delivery for STS in Korea. This interpretation, however, should be qualified at the individual‐patient level: the NHIS database does not record the clinician's stated therapeutic goal, and RT delivered alone, without surgery, is more often used in clinical practice for unresectable disease or radiotherapy‐sensitive histological subtypes and, in some cases, for palliation—settings in which shorter fractionation courses may be selected despite substantial disease burden. We therefore present the fractionation trend as suggestive of an evolving treatment paradigm at the national level, while acknowledging that fraction number alone cannot serve as a definitive surrogate for intent in any individual case [15].

Similarly, although this database does not capture treatment intent directly, claim‐date sequencing allowed us to characterize the temporal relationship between RT and surgery. Among RT recipients with a captured surgical procedure, the large majority (92.0%) received RT after surgery, consistent with a predominantly postoperative treatment pattern at the population level. However, this timing‐based classification cannot be equated with neoadjuvant, adjuvant, definitive, or palliative intent, as clinical decision‐making regarding treatment sequence depends on factors—such as resectability, margin status, and disease extent—that are not captured in this database.

Unlike clinical trials with strict inclusion criteria, this study provides robust real‐world evidence that reflects the actual clinical practice patterns of an entire nation. By including over 50,000 patients, our analysis captures the complete transition of RT modalities and identifies key inflection points, such as the 2012 fractionation shift and the 2015 insurance expansion. This high level of external validity ensures that our findings represent the true landscape of soft tissue sarcoma management in a real‐world setting, offering valuable insights into how healthcare policies directly govern the adoption of precision medicine.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be noted. First, the NHIS database lacks granular clinical data—including tumor size, depth, stage, histological grade, surgical margin status, and systemic therapy regimen and timing—that are established independent prognostic factors in STS. Consequently, the survival associations reported in this study are subject to substantial residual confounding by these unmeasured variables and should be interpreted as descriptive of real‐world outcome patterns rather than as unbiased estimates of comparative treatment efficacy. Chemotherapy data were similarly unavailable and represent an important unmeasured confounder. Second, granular radiotherapy parameters—including dose, biologically effective dose, target volume, dose constraints, and image‐guided RT use—are not captured in claims data. Era‐stratified analyses indicated that HRs varied across the 19‐year study period, with estimates in the most recent era (2015–2023, which contributes the majority of person‐time) consistent with the primary pooled analysis, suggesting our principal findings are not dominated by the earliest, most atypical era. Third, the NHIS claims database relies on ICD‐10 diagnosis and procedure codes rather than pathology‐based morphology classification (ICD‐O‐3), precluding histology‐specific analysis; a sensitivity analysis excluding the small subset identified through non‐exclusive diagnostic codes (C22.3, C22.4, and C54.2) did not materially change the results. Fourth, specialized modalities such as proton therapy, often preferred for pediatric patients to minimize late toxicities, may be underrepresented or omitted in the database, particularly for the period preceding its insurance coverage expansion in 2015. Fifth, we utilized all‐cause mortality rather than cancer‐specific survival, as the cause of death is often insufficiently adjudicated in administrative data. Lastly, the database lacks the clinical rationale behind RT modality selection, which may contribute to confounding by indication. Although claim‐date sequencing enabled characterization of RT timing relative to surgery, the database cannot distinguish neoadjuvant, adjuvant, definitive, or palliative RT intent, nor does it capture the quality of treatment planning or the clinical rationale behind selecting a specific modality for each patient.

In conclusion, RT for STS in Korea has undergone a definitive transition toward an IMRT‐centered paradigm, catalyzed by technological advancements and supportive national insurance policies. Our large‐scale real‐world analysis underscores that RT remains a pivotal pillar of the multidisciplinary management of STS, alongside surgery and chemotherapy. These findings highlight that the integration of advanced RT techniques into the broader therapeutic landscape is essential for optimizing clinical outcomes and ensuring high‐quality, comprehensive care for patients with STS.
