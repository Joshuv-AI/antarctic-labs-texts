The integral Hasse principle for stacky curves associated to a family of generalized Fermat equations
Juanita Duque-Rosero, Christopher Keyes, Andrew Kobin, Manami Roy, Soumya Sankar, Yidi Wang

Abstract
. We characterize the integral Hasse principle for an infinite family of spherical stacky curves with genus g ∈ [ 2 / 3 , 1 ) g\in[2/3,1) that are defined using generalized Fermat equations, extending a result of Darmon and Granville. We then apply our methods to find that a positive proportion of curves in our family satisfy the integral Hasse principle.

Primary 11D45, 14G12, 14D23; Secondary 11D41, 14G05, 14Q25

1.
Introduction

An algebraic curve
X
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
is said to satisfy the Hasse principle if the existence of a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-point on
X
is equivalent to the existence of points over all completions
{\mathbb{Q}}_{v}
of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. There is a dichotomy in the arithmetic of the curve: when
X
has genus
0
, the Hasse principle holds for
X
by the Hasse–Minkowski theorem. On the other hand, when its genus is at least
1
there are counterexamples to the Hasse principle
[
Poo10
]
(in fact, they exist over every global field).

When the curve is replaced by a
stacky curve
,
\mathcal{X}
, the nature of the problem changes. If
\mathcal{X}
has coarse space
X
, then away from the stacky locus, the coarse moduli map
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow X
is an isomorphism, so understanding
\mathcal{X}({\mathbb{Q}})
is nearly equivalent to understanding
X({\mathbb{Q}})
. However the following
integral
version of the Hasse principle exhibits more interesting phenomena.

Definition 1.1
.

A (relative) stacky curve
\mathcal{X}
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies the integral Hasse principle if
\mathcal{X}({\mathbb{Z}})\not=\emptyset
is equivalent to
\mathcal{X}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})\not=\emptyset
for all primes
p
and
\mathcal{X}({\mathbb{R}})\not=\emptyset
.

This problem was first studied in
[
BP22
]
, where the authors show that the integral Hasse principle holds for all tame stacky curves of genus
g<\frac{1}{2}
. They also exhibit counterexamples to the Hasse principle for
g=\frac{1}{2}
. More counterexamples to the Hasse principle may be constructed using
[
DG95
, Prop. 8.1]
, which characterizes local and global solutions to certain
generalized Fermat equations
, i.e. Diophantine equations of the form

(1.2)

Ax^{p}+By^{q}=Cz^{r}

for some
A,B,C,p,q,r\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
ABC\neq 0
and
p,q,r\geq 2
. See §
2.3
for more background on these equations, which are well-studied in the literature.

In this article, we study the case when
A=1
and
(p,q,r)=(2,2,n)
for
n
odd. For each odd
n
, we construct a stacky curve
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}
of genus
\frac{n-1}{n}
such that integral points on
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}
correspond exactly to primitive solutions of (
1.2
). We extend the local characterization of
[
DG95
]
to all pairs of non-zero integers
(B,C)
in Proposition
3.1
. Extending their criterion for global points to allow
B
to be non-squarefree or in any congruence class modulo
4
turns to be quite subtle: it requires working with norm forms of and factorizations in arbitrary orders in quadratic fields. Our first main result towards a characterization of the existence of global integral points on the curves
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}
is as follows.

Theorem A

(Theorem
5.2
)
.

Let
B=f^{2}B_{0}
, with
-1\neq B_{0}
squarefree and
\gcd(f,C)=1
. Let
K={\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sqrt{-B_{0}}\right)
with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Then there exist a localization
R_{K}
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, an order
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
, and a finite and explicitly computable set
A_{B,C}\subseteq H^{1}(R_{K},\mu_{n})
such that the following are equivalent.

(i)

There exists an integral point on
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}
, corresponding to a primitive integer solution
(x,y,z)
to the equation
x^{2}+By^{2}=Cz^{n}
, such that
x+f\sqrt{B_{0}}y\notin p\mathcal{O}_{K}
for all prime numbers
p
.

(ii)

Each prime number
p
divides
C
at most once if
p
is ramified in
K
(resp. zero times if
p
is odd and inert, or if
p=2
and
B_{0}\equiv 3\pmod{4}
) and
A_{B,C}
is nonempty.

(iii)

In the ring
\mathcal{O}_{K}
,
C
splits as a product of coprime ideals
C\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathfrak{j}_{+}\mathfrak{j}_{-}\mathfrak{r}
, where
\mathfrak{j}_{\pm}
are each products of split primes,
\mathfrak{r}
is a product of ramified primes,
\mathfrak{j}_{+}=\overline{\mathfrak{j}_{-}}
, and
[\mathfrak{j}_{+}\cap\mathcal{O}]\in n\Cl(\mathcal{O})
.

We denote by
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0})
the groupoid of integral points satisfying Theorem
A
(i)
. When
B_{0}\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, slight modifications are required, but we show that a similar statement holds (Theorem
5.3
). The results we highlight in the rest of the introduction have analogous versions for the modified set when
B_{0}\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.

In Section
6
, we develop methods that allow us to use Theorem
A
to handle equations
x^{2}+By^{2}=Cz^{n}
for general
(B,C)
, and detect when
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}})\neq\emptyset
, rather than just
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0})
. The only constraints that we must impose on the coefficients are the ones described in §
6.4
. We also discuss how to approach the problem when
A\neq 1
in (
1.2
) (see §
6.7
).

Theorem B
.

Fix
(B,C)
outside of an explicit excluded set
\mathbb{E}
(see Definition

) of natural density at most
\sum_{p\mid n}\frac{1}{p^{(p+1)/2}}
. There is a finite, explicitly described set of pairs

such that

\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}})\neq\emptyset\iff\coprod_{(B^{\prime},C^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}}S_{B^{\prime},C^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset,

where
S_{B^{\prime},C^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{B^{\prime},C^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}})
is a subgroupoid (depending on

) described explicitly in terms of divisibility conditions in Section

The subgroupoids

are in fact contained in the points described in Theorem

, with additional divisibility conditions needed on the

All together, Proposition

\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}

1.1.
Approach via descent

Our approach to Theorem
A
, inspired by
[
PSS07
]
,
involves identifying an étale
\mu_{n}
-torsor
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\to(\mathcal{Y}_{B,C})_{R_{K}}
and computing a set of admissible twists
\{\mathcal{C}_{d}^{\prime}\to(\mathcal{Y}_{B,C})_{R_{K}}\}
, whose
R_{K}
-points descend to points in
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0}).

In particular, the set
A_{B,C}
in Theorem
A
(ii)
consists of those
d
for which
\mathcal{C}_{d}^{\prime}\to(\mathcal{Y}_{B,C})_{R_{K}}
is admissible, so that the equivalence of parts
(i)
and
(ii)
of Theorem
A
says that
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0})\neq\emptyset
if and only if there exists an admissible twist
\mathcal{C}_{d}^{\prime}\to(\mathcal{Y}_{B,C})_{R_{K}}
.
In parallel to this,
[
DG95
, Prop. 8.1]
provides a class group obstruction to the existence of
{\mathbb{Z}}
-points on
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}
. Theorem
A
illustrates the connection between these two obstructions.

Having a clean description in terms of the class group of
\mathcal{O}
also explains why Theorem
A
restricts to the case
\gcd(f,C)=1
and only detects points in
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0})
. Indeed, the candidates for points that descend to
{\mathbb{Z}}
come from ideals trivial in
\Cl(\mathcal{O})
and these principal ideals are typically of the form
(a+bf\sqrt{-B_{0}})
with
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\gcd(a,f)=1
(see §
2.4
). Theorem
B
makes the jump to the more general case using primarily elementary techniques. This splitting of results is a consequence of our choice of torsors and methods, rather than an intrinsic one. It might be possible to combine Theorems
A
and
B
into one by making different choices, but we choose not to do so in the interest of readability.

Theorem
A
can be interpreted as showing that an explicitly computable
refined
descent obstruction is the only obstruction to the Hasse principle for points in
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0})
.
Indeed, Santens showed that for a stacky curve
\mathcal{Y}
of genus less than
1
there exists a finite étale group scheme
G
and a
G
-torsor
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{Y}
such that the descent along
\pi
obstruction is the only obstruction to the integral Hasse principle (
[
San23
, Thm. 1.1]
). However, in practice, it is difficult to study the integral Hasse principle in this way as it is hard to construct non-trivial étale covers of
\mathcal{Y}
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

To get around this issue, we compute a smaller descent locus from the subset
A_{B,C}\subseteq H^{1}(R_{K},\mu_{n})
(which can be viewed as a “refined Selmer set”) that allows one to describe the obstruction to
{\mathbb{Z}}
-points from the usual descent locus for
R_{K}
-points. More precisely, define the refined descent locus as

\mathcal{Y}^{\pi,\textnormal{refined}}({\mathbb{A}}_{R_{K}})\coloneqq\coprod_{d\in{A}_{B,C}}\pi_{d}(\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{A}}_{R_{K}}))\subseteq\coprod_{d\in H^{1}(R_{K},\mu_{n})}\pi_{d}(\mathcal{C}_{d}({\mathbb{A}}_{R_{K}})).

Then, Theorem
A
characterizes the following refined Hasse principle:
\mathcal{Y}^{\pi,\textnormal{refined}}({\mathbb{A}}_{R_{K}})\neq\emptyset\implies\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0})\neq\emptyset
.

1.2.
Statistics

Fixing the signature
(2,2,n)
and letting
B
and
C
vary, we can study the following counting functions for
T\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}

\displaystyle N^{\mathrm{loc}}_{n}(T)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\#\{(B,C)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:|B|,|C|<T,\ \mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})\neq\emptyset\text{ for all primes }p\},

\displaystyle N_{n}(T)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\#\{(B,C)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:|B|,|C|<T,\ \mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}})\neq\emptyset\}.

For all odd

)

We have

\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{N_{3}(T)}{N_{3}^{\mathrm{loc}}(T)}>0.00988.

That is, a positive proportion of the stacky curves
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}
of signature
(2,2,3)
non-vacuously satisfy the Hasse principle for integral points

Stacky curves

A

A

\Spec L

\mathcal{X}

with

The

2.3.
Generalized Fermat equations

A
generalized Fermat equation
is a Diophantine equation of the form

(2.4)

Ax^{p}+By^{q}=Cz^{r},

where
A,B,C\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
p,q,r\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 2}
. The integer solutions to (
2.4
) have been studied extensively in the literature; see
[
PSS07
]
for a survey in the case
A=B=C=1
(and a complete description of the solutions in the case
(p,q,r)=(2,3,7)
) and
[
DG95
,
Beu98
]
for a general treatment.

Of principal interest are the
primitive solutions
, i.e. solutions
(x,y,z)
with
\gcd(x,y,z)=1
. There may be a finite or an infinite number of primitive solutions to (
1.2
) depending on whether
\chi=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}-1
is positive, in which case there are either no primitive solutions or infinitely many
[
Beu98
]
, or
\chi
is negative, in which case there are at most finitely many primitive solutions
[
DG95
]
. In many cases when
\chi<0
, the full set of solutions has been found; see
[
RG25
]
for a recent survey. The case
\chi=0
also occurs — in fact only when
(p,q,r)=(2,3,6),(2,4,4)
, or
(3,3,3)
, or permutations of these — and reduces to classical descent techniques; see
[
DG95
, Sec. 6]
.

Set
d=\gcd(qr,pr,pq)
. Using (
2.4
), we can define the scheme
S
as in (
2.2
) so that
S({\mathbb{Z}})
is the set of nontrivial primitive integer solutions to (
2.4
). Each choice of parameters determines a
1

2.3.1.

Let

be a Dedekind domain (not necessarily a PID) with fraction field

\mathfrak{p}
a prime ideal of

denote the completion of

at
\mathfrak{p}
with normalized discrete valuation

. Let

be the weighted projective stack with weights

.

An

-point
(resp.

-point
) of

is a morphism of stacks

(resp.
\Spec F_{\mathfrak{p}}\to\mathbb{P}(a,b,c)
). A

of such a point is an object in the isomorphism class of

in the groupoid
\mathbb{P}(a,b,c)(R)
, which can be identified with a tuple of
(x,y,z)
defined over

. We write

, to be thought of as weighted homogeneous coordinates.

A point

is called
\mathfrak{p}
-integral
if

\min\left\{\frac{v_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)}{a},\frac{v_{\mathfrak{p}}(y)}{b},\frac{v_{\mathfrak{p}}(z)}{c}\right\}\in{\mathbb{Z}}

for any representative
(x,y,z)
. Further, a representative
(x,y,z)
is called
\mathfrak{p}
-minimal
if the above minimum is in the interval
[0,1)
. In particular, if
R
is a PID and
(x,y,z)
is a fixed representative of a point in

with

, then it is

-minimal if and only if

.

Returning to the stacky curve
\mathcal{Y}=[S/\mathbb{G}_{m}]
from (
2.5
), for a PID

, a point
[x:y:z]\in\mathcal{Y}(R)
is by definition
\mathfrak{p}
-integral at all primes
\mathfrak{p}
. Taking a representative which is
\mathfrak{p}
-minimal at all
\mathfrak{p}
yields a
primitive
solution, i.e. we have
(x,y,z)\in R^{3}
with
\gcd(x,y,z)=1
.

Remark 2.7
.

The property of
\mathfrak{p}
-integrality belongs to a point of
\mathbb{P}(a,b,c)
, i.e. the equivalence class. A representative of that class can be taken to be
\mathfrak{p}
-minimal. As we are chiefly interested in the case where
R
is a PID, namely the cases

,
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, or

for

a quadratic number field, we will use “

” to mean that

is a particular primitive representative.

Lemma 4.20
.

Let
f,

B_{0},
and
C
be nonzero integers with
B_{0}
squarefree and
\gcd(f,C)=1
. Suppose
p
is an odd prime.
If
[x:y:z]\in\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*_{p}^{r})
for
r>0
, then
p^{r}\mid x
and exactly one of the following holds:

(a)

p^{r}\mid y
,
p^{2r}\mid C
, and
p\nmid z
;

(b)

p\nmid y
,
p^{r}\mid f
, and
p^{\lceil 2r/n\rceil}\mid z
.

Moreover, in case
(b)
, if
n\nmid r
, then we have
p^{r}\parallel f
.

Proof.

By hypothesis,
u\in p^{r}\mathcal{O}_{K}
, so
\overline{u}\in p^{r}\mathcal{O}_{K}
as well.
Since
u+\overline{u}=2x
and
p
is odd, we have
p^{r}\mid x
. To see that
p^{r}|fy
, we note that
u-\overline{u}=2f\sqrt{-B_{0}}y\in p^{r}\mathcal{O}_{K}
. If
p\nmid B_{0}
, then
p^{r}\mid fy
. If
p\mid B_{0}
, then
p
is ramified,
v_{\mathfrak{p}}(\sqrt{-B_{0}})=1
, and
p\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathfrak{p}^{2}
so that
v_{\mathfrak{p}}(u-\overline{u})\geq 2r
must be odd. In particular,
fy\mathcal{O}_{K}\in\mathfrak{p}^{2r}
and
p^{r}|fy
.

Suppose that
r>0
and that
p\mid\gcd(f,y)
. Then
p\nmid C
, so we must have
p\mid z
. This contradicts
[x:y:z]
being primitive, so we have either
p^{r}\mid y
and
p\nmid f
or
p^{r}\mid f
and
p\nmid y
.

In case
(a)
, we have
p\nmid z
is implied by primitivity of
[x:y:z]
. That
p^{2r}\mid C
follows from the equation for
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}
.

In case
(b)
, again we have
p^{2r}\mid Cz^{n}
, and by hypothesis that
\gcd(f,C)=1
it must be
p^{2r}\mid z^{n}
. Since the latter is an
n
-th power, we deduce that
p^{\lceil 2r/n\rceil}\mid z
.

For the moreover statement, suppose that
n\nmid r
and to the contrary that
p^{r+1}\mid f
. In this case, we use the fact that
\lceil 2r/n\rceil n>2r
. We can now examine the equation for
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}
satisfied by
[x:y:z]
, modulo
p^{2r+1}
:

x^{2}+f^{2}B_{0}y^{2}\equiv Cz^{n}\pmod{p^{2r+1}}.

The right-hand-side vanishes by the observation we just made, and the
f^{2}B_{0}y^{2}
term vanishes by hypothesis. This means
p^{2r+1}\mid x^{2}
, so
p^{r+1}\mid x
. However, this produces a contradiction, as now
p^{r+1}\mid u
.
∎

Remark 4.21
.

It follows from Lemma

that if

The story for

is different because

Lemma 4.22
.

Let
f,

B_{0},
and
C

squarefree and

. Setting

4.4.
Detecting integral points

In this subsection, we show that
*^{0}
points on
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}
are detected by admissible twists, i.e. are contained in the refined descent obstruction from §
1.1
.

Lemma 4.27
.

Suppose
B=f^{2}B_{0}
for
B_{0}\neq-1
squarefree and
\gcd(f,C)=1
. Then under the map
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}})\to\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}(R_{K})
we have an inclusion of groupoids

\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0})\subseteq\coprod_{d\in A_{B,C}}\pi_{d}^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}_{d}^{\prime}(R_{K})).

In particular, if
A_{B,C}=\emptyset
then
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Let
[x:y:z]\in\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0})
. Let
[u:v:w]
be its image in
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}(R_{K})
and note that
u=\bar{v}
. By Corollary
4.11
, we have
[u:v:w]=\pi^{\prime}_{d}([U:V:W])
for some
d\in R_{K}^{\times}/(R_{K}^{\times})^{n}
and
[U:V:W]\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{d}(R_{K})
. Thus for any
\lambda\in R_{K}^{\times}
,

[u:v:w]=\left[\frac{U^{n}}{dC^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}:\frac{V^{n}}{d^{n-1}C^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}:W\right]=\left[\frac{\lambda^{n}U^{n}}{dC^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}:\frac{\lambda^{n}V^{n}}{d^{n-1}C^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}:\lambda^{2}W\right].

Taking
\mathfrak{p}
-adic valuations for primes
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{S}
and reducing modulo
n
, we have

(4.28)

\displaystyle v_{\mathfrak{p}}(u)

\displaystyle\equiv-v_{\mathfrak{p}}(d)-\frac{n-1}{2}v_{\mathfrak{p}}(C)\pmod{n},

(4.29)

\displaystyle v_{\mathfrak{p}}(v)

\displaystyle\equiv v_{\mathfrak{p}}(d)-\frac{n-1}{2}v_{\mathfrak{p}}(C)\pmod{n},

which are well-defined since
d\in R_{K}^{\times}/(R_{K}^{\times})^{n}
. We will show that these imply
d\in A_{B,C}
.

First, we consider
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{S}
ramified. Since
u=\bar{v}
we have
v_{\mathfrak{p}}(u)=v_{\mathfrak{p}}(v)
. Subtracting (
4.28
) from (
4.29
) we have
2v_{\mathfrak{p}}(d)\equiv 0
, which implies
v_{\mathfrak{p}}(d)\equiv 0\pmod{n}
. Similarly, if
\mathfrak{p}=p\mathcal{O}_{K}
is inert, we have
v_{\mathfrak{p}}(u)=v_{\mathfrak{p}}(v)
and deduce that
v_{\mathfrak{p}}(d)\equiv 0\pmod{n}
.

Assume now that
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{split}}
, i.e. there exists a prime
\bar{\mathfrak{p}}\neq\mathfrak{p}
with
\mathfrak{p}\bar{\mathfrak{p}}=p\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Condition
*^{0}
implies that
\min\{v_{\mathfrak{p}}(u),v_{\bar{\mathfrak{p}}}(u)\}\equiv 0\pmod{n}
. Hence,
\min\{v_{\mathfrak{p}}(u),v_{\mathfrak{p}}(v)\}\equiv 0\pmod{n}
. Up to (possibly) swapping
\mathfrak{p}
and
\bar{\mathfrak{p}}
, we may assume
v_{\mathfrak{p}}(v)\equiv 0\pmod{n}
.

If
p\nmid C
then we have
v_{\mathfrak{p}}(d)\equiv 0\pmod{n}
. Otherwise we have
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{C,\mathrm{split}}
, so

v_{\mathfrak{p}}(d)\equiv\frac{n-1}{2}v_{\mathfrak{p}}(C)\equiv\frac{n-1}{2}v_{p}(C)\pmod{n};

v_{\bar{\mathfrak{p}}}(d)\equiv-\frac{n-1}{2}v_{\bar{\mathfrak{p}}}(C)\equiv\frac{n+1}{2}v_{p}(C)\pmod{n}.

Swapping the roles of
\mathfrak{p},\bar{\mathfrak{p}}
merely swaps the sign. This shows
[x:y:z]\in\pi_{d}^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(R_{K}))
for some
d
satisfying the conditions in (
4.14
).

To show
\mathrm{div}(d)\in\im(\psi_{\mathcal{O}})
, there is a slight difference depending on the residue of
B_{0}
modulo
{8}
and the parity of
C
. Here
\psi_{\mathcal{O}}
is defined in (
2.20
). First, assume
B_{0}\not\equiv 3\pmod{8}
, so that the order
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
defined in Definition
4.12
has conductor
\mathfrak{f}=f
. For
p\mid f
, the condition
*_{p}^{0}
implies
u
is coprime to
f
, so
u\mathcal{O}_{K}=\psi_{\mathcal{O}}(u\mathcal{O})
by the definition of
P(\mathcal{O},f)
. To see
d\mathcal{O}_{K}\in\im(\psi_{\mathcal{O}})
, we observe that
[u:v:w]\in\im(\pi_{d}^{\prime})
implies

ud=\frac{U^{n}}{C^{(n-1)/2}}

for some
U\in K^{\times}
. Since
\gcd(f,C)=1
and
\frac{n-1}{2}
is coprime to
n
, we have

d\mathcal{O}_{K}\equiv\psi_{\mathcal{O}}((C^{\frac{n-1}{2}}u\mathcal{O})^{-1})\pmod{nP(\mathcal{O}_{K})},

hence
d\in A_{B,C}
.

If
B_{0}\equiv 3\pmod{8}
, then by Remark
4.24
the conclusion is vacuously satisfied for
C
even. When
C
is odd,
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
has conductor
\mathfrak{f}=2f
. By the same argument as above,
u
is coprime to
f
. Since
x,y
are not both even by the
*^{0}
condition, the congruence
x^{2}+f^{2}B_{0}y^{2}\equiv 0\pmod{8}
has no solutions. Thus,
z
is odd and
u
is coprime to
2f
.

This completes the inclusion of
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0})
into
\coprod_{d\in A_{B,C}}\pi^{\prime}_{d}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{d}(R_{K}))
. By hypothesis,
-B
is not a square, so by Remark
2.9
, these groupoids are sets, so there is nothing extra to check.
∎

Similarly, recall the definition of the subset
\widetilde{A}_{B,C}
from Definition
4.15
.

Lemma 4.30
.

Suppose
B=f^{2}B_{0}
for
B_{0}\neq-1
squarefree,
B_{0}\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, and
\gcd(f,C)=1
. Then under the map
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}})\to\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}(R_{K})

In particular, if
\widetilde{A}_{B,C}=\emptyset
then
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};\tilde{*}^{0})=\emptyset

The proof is nearly identical to that of Lemma
4.27
, with nothing changing at the odd primes. When
B_{0}\equiv 3\pmod{8}
, by Remark
4.24
the conclusion is vacuously satisfied unless
4\parallel C
. Since 2 is inert we have
v_{2}(d)\equiv 0\pmod{n}
, showing that

satisfies (

). Since in this case
\mathcal{O}
has conductor

, we need not check that

is coprime to 2, and the verification that the image of

lies in that of

is the same as in the proof of Lemma

Suppose now that
B_{0}\equiv 7\pmod{8}
. At the split prime 2, since our points satisfy
\tilde{*}^{0}_{2}
, we instead find that that
v_{\mathfrak{p}_{2}}(u)
or

is congruent to 1 mod

. Assuming
v_{\mathfrak{p}_{2}}(v)\equiv 1\pmod{n}

v_{\mathfrak{p}_{2}}(d)\equiv 1+\frac{n-1}{2}v_{\mathfrak{p}_{2}}(C)\pmod{n}.

Conjugating gives the value of

5.
Main theorems

In the next two sections we prove the main results about the Hasse principle for integral points on the stacky curves

(Theorems

For many cases,

Example 5.6

Consider the equation

5.2.
Cohomological obstructions to the Hasse principle

Theorems
5.2
and
5.3
show that the refined descent obstruction, as defined below, is the only obstruction to the integral Hasse principle for
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0})
(resp.
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};\tilde{*}^{0})
).

In
[
San23
, Thm. 1.1]
, Santens proved that the finite descent obstruction is the only obstruction to strong approximation for tame stacky curves
X
over a number field
k
of genus
g<1
and therefore the only obstruction to the Hasse principle for all the integral models
\mathcal{X}
of
X
. That is, there exists a finite étale group scheme
G
and a
G
-torsor
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{X}
such that the descent along
\pi
locus

\mathcal{X}({\mathbb{A}}_{U})^{\pi}=\coprod_{\sigma\in H^{1}(U,G)}\pi_{\sigma}(\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}({\mathbb{A}}_{U}))\neq\emptyset\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathcal{X}(U)\neq\emptyset

for a Dedekind domain
U
with fraction field
k
. Here
{\mathbb{A}}_{U}
is the subring of integral adèles of
{\mathbb{A}}_{k}
, and

\mathcal{X}({\mathbb{A}}_{U})\coloneqq\prod_{v\in S}\mathcal{X}(k_{v})\times\prod_{v\not\in S}\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{O}_{v})

endowed with the product topology
(see
[
Chr20
, Prop. 13.0.2]
),
where
S
is the finite set of archimedean places of
k
and the product is over places of
U
.

Although descent along
\pi
captures the failure of the integral Hasse principle for a stacky curve
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}
, it is hard in practice to use it to show whether
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}
satisfies the integral Hasse principle over
U={\mathbb{Z}}
, since it is difficult to construct étale covers of
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
. We are able to construct étale covers over
{\mathbb{Z}}[1/2n]
, and to make the twists of this cover easy to compute, we pass to the ring
R_{K}
.
We note that

\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}})\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}\Big({\mathbb{Z}}\Big[\frac{1}{2n}\Big]\Big)\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}(R_{K}),

and
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{A}}_{{\mathbb{Z}}})\subset\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{A}}_{R_{K}})\neq\emptyset
. Descent along
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\to(\mathcal{Y}_{B,C})_{R_{K}}
now only allows one to describe the obstruction to the existence of
R_{K}
-points instead of
{\mathbb{Z}}
-points.
Our main theorems thus provide a “refined” descent locus,

\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}^{\pi,\textnormal{refined}}({\mathbb{A}}_{R_{K}})\coloneqq\coprod_{d\in{A}_{B,C}}\pi_{d}^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}_{d}({\mathbb{A}}_{R_{K}}))\subseteq\coprod_{d\in H^{1}(R_{K},\mu_{n})}\pi_{d}^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}_{d}({\mathbb{A}}_{R_{K}}))

whose non-emptiness implies that of
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0})
.

6.
The “cascade”: reduction to the base cases

The goal of this section is to reduce to the case where the conditions of one of the main theorems (Theorems
5.2
and
5.3
) are satisfied. More precisely, we would like to prove that for any
B,C\in{\mathbb{Z}}\smallsetminus\{0\}
, there exists a finite set of pairs
\mathcal{P}=\{(B^{\prime},C^{\prime})\}
such that

\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}})\neq\emptyset\iff\mathcal{Y}_{B^{\prime},C^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}};\bullet)\neq\emptyset\text{ for some }(B^{\prime},C^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}

for some condition
\bullet
depending on
B
and
C
that can be tested using the main theorems in Section
5
. For a given
(B,C)
with
\gcd(f,C)=1
, the set of pairs
\mathcal{P}
depends on two kinds of primes: the first kind where
p^{2}\mid\gcd(B,C)
, which are dealt with using Lemma
3.4
. The second kind are those for which
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{r}_{p})
is non-empty for some
r>0
. In §
4.3
, we proved some preliminary lemmas characterizing divisibility conditions for points in
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*_{p}^{r})
for such primes.
In §
6.1
and §
6.2
we use these to prove isomorphisms between groupoids of the form
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};\bullet)
and
\mathcal{Y}_{B^{\prime},C^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}};\bullet^{\prime})
which in turn, help us compute the set
\mathcal{P}
. We consider the case
\gcd(f,C)=1
in §
6.1
and the case when primes
p\mid\gcd(f,C)
in §
6.2
. In §
6.3
, we present a modification of Theorem
5.2
so that we can construct a point
[x:y:z]
with
y
relatively prime to a fixed integer in certain situations. In §
6.4
, we describe the set
\mathbb{E}
of
(B,C)
that are excluded from our analysis. In §
6.5
, we provide an algorithm that takes as inputs and outputs whether
\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}})
is empty or not. Together, these results prove Theorem
B
.

6.1.

The lemmas in this section fall naturally into two categories: Lemmas

Observation 6.1
.

We begin with a simple, yet useful observation that follows from the definition of
*^{0}
points. Let
f,B_{0},
and
C
be non-zero integers with
B_{0}
squarefree and let
B=f^{2}B_{0}
. Suppose

but

. Then

\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0}_{p})\cong\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0}_{p},p\nmid y).

Indeed, for any

if and only if

. The

. Similarly, Lemma
4.22
implies that

\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};\tilde{*}^{0})=\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};\tilde{*}^{0},2\nmid y).

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
f,

B_{0},
and

Let

5.6

. This implies that

\mathcal{Y}_{3^{5},3\cdot 31}(\mathbb{Z})=\mathcal{Y}_{3^{5},3\cdot 31}(\mathbb{Z};*_{3}^{2})\overset{\sim}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{Y}_{3,31}(\mathbb{Z};*_{3}^{0},3\nmid y)=\emptyset.

6.3.
Points with restricted
y
-coordinates

In several of the results in the previous section, the target groupoids are of the form
\mathcal{Y}_{B^{\prime},C^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}};*_{p}^{0},p\nmid y)
for
p
an odd prime or
\mathcal{Y}_{B^{\prime},C^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}};\tilde{*}_{2}^{0},2\nmid y)
. It is only when Observation
6.1
applies that we can conclude that these are equal to
\mathcal{Y}_{B^{\prime},C^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}};*_{p}^{0})
or
\mathcal{Y}_{B^{\prime},C^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}};\tilde{*}_{2}^{0})
, respectively. In general, as illustrated in Example
5.6
, it appears to be possible to have
B^{\prime},C^{\prime}
with
p\nmid C^{\prime}
, but
p\mid y
for every
[x:y:z]\in\mathcal{Y}_{B^{\prime},C^{\prime}}({\mathbb{Z}})
.

In light of these observations,
we have to modify Theorem
5.2
so that we can always construct a point
[x:y:z]
with
y
coprime to a fixed integer. The following proposition allows us to do so in the case that this integer is coprime to
nC
.

Proposition 6.14
.

Let
\gcd(f,C)=1
and fix an integer
N
satisfying
\gcd(N,nC)=1
. Then for any integer
M
,

\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0},\gcd(z,M)=1)\neq\emptyset\iff\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0},\ \gcd(y,N)=1,\ \gcd(z,M)=1)\neq\emptyset

and

\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};\tilde{*}^{0},\gcd(z,M)=1)\neq\emptyset\iff\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};\tilde{*}^{0},\ \gcd(y,N)=1,\ \gcd(z,M)=1)\neq\emptyset.

Proof.

The reverse implication is immediate. For the forward implication, it suffices to take
N
positive and squarefree. The conclusion is vacuous when
N=1
, so it suffices to proceed by induction on the number of prime divisors of
N
. Equivalently, if we have a point
[x:y:z]
with
\gcd(y,N)=1
and a prime
p
satisfying
p\nmid nCN
, we must find a point
[x^{\prime}:y^{\prime}:z^{\prime}]
with
\gcd(y^{\prime},Np)=1
. We will first focus on the case of
B_{0}\equiv 1,2\pmod{4}
, then note the necessary adjustments for that of
B\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.

Suppose we have
[x:y:z]\in\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}};*^{0},\ \gcd(y,N)=1,\ \gcd(z,M)=1)
and a prime
p\nmid nCN
. Recall
\mathcal{O}={\mathbb{Z}}[f\sqrt{-B_{0}}]
and write
\mathcal{O}_{p}={\mathbb{Z}}[pf\sqrt{-B_{0}}]
for the order of relative conductor
p
in
\mathcal{O}
, and similarly
\mathcal{O}_{N}
,
\mathcal{O}_{Np}
. By Theorem
5.2
, we have
C\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathfrak{j}_{+}\mathfrak{j}_{-}\mathfrak{r}^{2}\mathfrak{i}^{2}
for

6.4.

In light of Observation

.

For fixed, odd

, let the set of excluded cases

, denote the set of pairs of non-zero integers

with

•

•

. Note that in the first case, if

We believe that it is not a coincidence that the cases we need to exclude involve primes dividing

, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|B|\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|C|\leq T\\
\gcd(2B,C)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(C)^{2}}{2^{\omega(C)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\kappa_{1}T}{\sqrt{\log T}}\sum_{|B|\leq T}\left(\mu(B)^{2}\prod_{p\mid 2B}\left(1+\frac{1}{2p}\right)^{-1}\right)+o\left(\frac{T^{2}}{\sqrt{\log T}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{16\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}T^{2}}{5\sqrt{\log T}}+o\left(\frac{T^{2}}{\sqrt{\log T}}\right),

where the last line follows by Lemma
7.8
below.

Lemma 7.8
.

There exists a positive constant
\kappa_{2}
such that

\sum_{1\leq B<T}\mu(B)^{2}\prod_{p\mid B}\left(1+\frac{1}{2p}\right)^{-1}\sim\kappa_{2}T.

Proof.

Consider the Dirichlet series given by

\displaystyle L(s)=\sum_{B>0}\frac{\mu(B)^{2}}{B^{s}}\prod_{p\mid B}\left(1+\frac{1}{2p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{B>0}\mu(B)^{2}\prod_{p\mid B}\left(1+\frac{1}{2p}\right)^{-1}p^{-s}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\left(1+\left(1+\frac{1}{2p}\right)^{-1}p^{-s}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\left(1+p^{-s}\left(1-\frac{1}{2p}+\frac{1}{(2p)^{2}}\ldots\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\left(1+p^{-s}-\frac{1}{2}p^{-s-1}\sum_{i\geq 0}\left(\frac{-1}{2p}\right)^{i}\right).

Now note that

L(s)\zeta(s)^{-1}=L(s)\prod_{p}(1-p^{-s})=\prod_{p}\left(1-p^{-2s}-\frac{1}{2}p^{-s-1}(1-p^{-s})\sum_{i\geq 0}\left(\frac{-1}{2p}\right)^{i}\right)=M(s),

which converges on the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)>1/2
. Thus
L(s)
converges for
\mathrm{Re}(s)>1
with a simple pole at
s=1
and residue
\kappa_{2}=M(1)
. The lemma follows from a standard application of a Tauberian theorem (see e.g.
[
PTBZ25
]
or
[
BD04
, Chapter 7]
).
∎

7.1.3.
Evaluation of the constants

It is useful to estimate the constants
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
. The constant
\kappa_{1}
is given in
[
FI10
, Lemma 1]
by

\kappa_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\prod_{p}\left(1+\frac{1}{2p}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{1/2}.

This is equivalent to

{\pi}\kappa_{1}^{2}=\prod_{p}\left(1+\frac{1}{2p}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{3}{4p^{2}}-\frac{1}{4p^{3}}\right).

Since the Euler factors are decreasing in
p
, we immediately get an upper bound by truncating the product after finitely many primes. On the other hand, observe that

\left(1-\frac{3}{4p^{2}}-\frac{1}{4p^{3}}\right)-\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{4p^{2}}-\frac{1}{4p^{3}}>0.

In particular, for any real
X>0
,

\pi\kappa_{1}^{2}\geq\prod_{p\leq X}\left(1-\frac{3}{4p^{2}}-\frac{1}{4p^{3}}\right)\frac{\zeta(2)^{-1}}{\prod_{p\leq X}(1-p^{-2})}.

Computing this for
X=50,000
, we find

(7.9)

\displaystyle 0.4581814

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{1}\leq 0.4581819.

For
\kappa_{2}=M(1)
from Lemma
7.8
, we use the same idea (the Euler factors are still decreasing), now comparing it to
\zeta(1.95)
instead of
\zeta(2)
. This gives us

(7.10)

\displaystyle 0.526859

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{2}\leq 0.526861.

In particular, taking
\alpha=1/2+10^{-5}
, we see that

\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{N_{n}^{\mathrm{loc}}(T)}{\frac{T^{2}}{\sqrt{\log T}}}\leq\frac{4\pi^{4}\kappa_{1}}{9(1-\alpha)^{1/2}}\leq 28.05267255,

and

\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{N_{n}^{\mathrm{loc}}(T)}{\frac{T^{2}}{\sqrt{\log T}}}\geq\frac{16\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}}{5}\geq 0.772470381.

7.2.
Lower bounds for global points

In this section, we prove Theorems
7.3
and
7.4
.
We begin with some notation. Let
n
be an odd prime and define
\delta_{n},\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{im}}
to be the density of squarefree
B
for which
\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{B}))[n]
is nontrival, given by the limits

\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{im}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq B\leq T:B\text{ squarefree},\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-B}))[n]\neq 0\}}{\#\{1\leq B\leq T:B\text{ squarefree}\}},

\displaystyle\delta_{n}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\#\{1\leq B\leq T:B\text{ squarefree},\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{B}))[n]\neq 0\}}{\#\{1\leq B\leq T:B\text{ squarefree}\}}.

Davenport and Heilbronn showed in the family of imaginary (resp. real) quadratic fields
K={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-B})
counted by discriminant, the average size of
{\Cl(\mathcal{O}_{K})}[3]
is 2 (resp.
\frac{4}{3}
)
[
DH71
, Thm 3]
. It follows from the fact that these averages are well behaved in residue classes
[
NH88
, Thm. 1]
that the same average holds when counting instead by the squarefree
B
instead of the discriminant.

For an odd prime
n>3
, the Cohen–Lenstra heuristics (
[
CL84
, Section 9]
) predict that the average size of
{\Cl(\mathcal{O}_{K})}[n]
, when ordered by discriminant, is
2
for imaginary quadratic fields and (
1+\frac{1}{n}
) for real quadratic fields. It is expected that these averages also behave well in residue classes of discriminants, leading to the same values when ordered by squarefree
B
as by discriminant. We record this as Assumption
7.11
below.

Assumption 7.11

(Modified Cohen–Lenstra)
.

Let
n>3
be an odd prime. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\end{subarray}}\#\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-B}))[n]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2T}{\zeta(2)}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\end{subarray}}\#\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{B}))[n]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{n+1}{n}\cdot\frac{T}{\zeta(2)}

Lemma 7.12
.

For
n\geq 3
an odd prime, under Assumption
7.11
if
n>3
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{im}}\leq\frac{1}{n-1},\quad\text{ and }\quad\delta_{n}\leq\frac{1}{n(n-1)}.

Proof.

For the imaginary case, observe that,

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\Bigg(\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
\mathrm{rk}_{n}\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-B}))=r\end{subarray}}n^{r}\Bigg)\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\end{subarray}}1\Bigg)^{-1}

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{T\to\infty}\Bigg({\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-B}))[n]=0\end{subarray}}1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
\mathrm{rk}_{n}\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-B}))[n]\neq 0\end{subarray}}n}\Bigg)\Bigg({\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\end{subarray}}1}\Bigg)^{-1}

\displaystyle=1-\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{im}}+n\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{im}},

which shows that
\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{im}}\leq\frac{1}{n-1}.
The proof for
\delta_{n}
follows similarly.
∎

In order to give a lower bound on
N_{n}(T)
,
we will be interested in
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])
for
B
squarefree. Since this order is not always maximal, we define
\widetilde{\delta_{n}}^{\mathrm{im}}
(resp.
\widetilde{\delta_{n}}
) to be the density of positive (resp. negative) squarefree integers
B
such that
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[n]\neq 0
. By Lemma
2.22
, we have
\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-B}))[n]=\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[n]
for all odd primes
n>3
, so in this case we have
\widetilde{\delta_{n}}^{\mathrm{im}}=\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{im}}
and
\widetilde{\delta_{n}}=\delta_{n}
.

When
n=3
, an application of Lemma
2.22
shows
\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-B}))[3]=\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[3]
whenever
B\not\equiv 3\pmod{8}
; when
B\equiv 3\pmod{8}
we have
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[3]\neq 0
. By
[
NH88
]
, the average ranks are well behaved in residue classes, and we have

(7.13)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{3}^{\mathrm{im}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\Bigg({\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
B\not\equiv 3\pmod{8}\\
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[n]\neq 0\end{subarray}}1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
B\equiv 3\pmod{8}\end{subarray}}1}\Bigg)\Bigg({\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\end{subarray}}1}\Bigg)^{-1}=\frac{5}{6}\delta_{3}^{\mathrm{im}}+\frac{1}{6}\leq\frac{7}{12},

(7.14)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\delta}_{3}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\Bigg({\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
-B\not\equiv 3\pmod{8}\\
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{B}])[n]\neq 0\end{subarray}}1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
-B\equiv 3\pmod{8}\end{subarray}}1}\Bigg)\Bigg({\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\end{subarray}}1}\Bigg)^{-1}=\frac{5}{6}\delta_{3}+\frac{1}{6}\leq\frac{11}{36}.

Proof of Theorem
7.3
.

As in the proof of Theorem
7.1
, we achieve a lower bound by restricting our attention to
B
and
C
squarefree, with
C
odd. Let

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{im}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\left\{(B,C)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:B\geq 1,\gcd(2B,C)=1,BC\text{ squarefree},\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[3]=0\right\},

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\left\{(B,C)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:B\leq-1,\gcd(2B,C)=1,BC\text{ squarefree},\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[3]=0\right\},

and define for
T>0

\displaystyle N_{3}^{\mathrm{im}}(T)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\#\left\{(B,C)\in S^{\mathrm{im}}:\max\{|B|,|C|\}\leq T,\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})\neq\emptyset\text{ for all }p\right\},

\displaystyle N_{3}(T)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\#\left\{(B,C)\in S:\max\{|B|,|C|\}\leq T,\mathcal{Y}_{B,C}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})\neq\emptyset\text{ for all }p\right\}.

We have

N_{3}(T)\geq N_{3}^{\mathrm{im}}(T)+N_{3}(T),

since when
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[3]
is trivial, there is no obstruction to the Hasse principle by Theorem
5.2
. We can then produce lower bounds for
N_{3}^{\mathrm{im}}(T)
and
N_{3}(T)
by modifying our local solubility counts from the proof of Theorem
7.1
.

Starting in the same manner as (
7.7
), we have

\displaystyle N_{3}^{\mathrm{im}}(T)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[3]=0\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|C|\leq T\\
\gcd(2B,C)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(C)^{2}}{2^{\omega(C)}}\prod_{p\mid C}\left(1+\left(\frac{-B}{p}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[3]=0\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|C|\leq T\\
\gcd(2B,C)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(C)^{2}}{2^{\omega(C)}}\sum_{D\mid C}\left(\frac{-B}{D}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[3]=0\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|C|\leq T\\
\gcd(2B,C)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(C)^{2}}{2^{\omega(C)}}+\sum_{|C|\leq T,\text{ odd}}\frac{\mu(C)^{2}}{2^{\omega(C)}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}D\mid C\\
D>1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[3]=0\\
\gcd(B,C)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{-B}{D}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\kappa_{1}T}{\sqrt{\log T}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[3]=0\end{subarray}}\prod_{p\mid 2B}\left(1+\frac{1}{2p}\right)^{-1}+o(T)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\kappa_{1}T}{\sqrt{\log T}}\left(\sum_{1\leq B\leq T}\prod_{p\mid 2B}\left(1+\frac{1}{2p}\right)^{-1}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[3]\neq 0\end{subarray}}\prod_{p\mid 2B}\left(1+\frac{1}{2p}\right)^{-1}+o(T)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2\kappa_{1}T}{\sqrt{\log T}}\left(\frac{4\kappa_{2}T}{5}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq B\leq T\\
B\text{ squarefree}\\
\Cl({\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{-B}])[3]\neq 0\end{subarray}}1+o(T)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\kappa_{1}T^{2}}{\sqrt{\log T}}\left(\frac{4\kappa_{2}}{5}-\frac{6\widetilde{\delta_{3}}^{\mathrm{im}}}{\pi^{2}}\right)+o\left(\frac{T^{2}}{\sqrt{\log T}}\right).

Combining this with the estimates (
7.10
) for
\kappa_{2}
and (
7.13
) for
\widetilde{\delta_{3}}^{\mathrm{im}}
, we see that
\frac{4\kappa_{2}}{5}-\frac{6\widetilde{\delta_{3}}^{\mathrm{im}}}{\pi^{2}}>0
. The same calculation shows

N_{3}(T)\geq\frac{2\kappa_{1}T^{2}}{\sqrt{\log T}}\left(\frac{4\kappa_{2}}{5}-\frac{6\widetilde{\delta_{3}}}{\pi^{2}}\right)+o\left(\frac{T^{2}}{\sqrt{\log T}}\right).

Finally, using our lower bounds computed for
N_{3}(T)
and our upper bounds for
N_{3}^{\mathrm{loc}}(T)
, we find

\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{N_{3}(T)}{N_{3}^{\mathrm{loc}}(T)}\geq\frac{4\kappa_{1}\left(\frac{4\kappa_{2}}{5}-\frac{3(\widetilde{\delta_{3}}^{\mathrm{im}}+\widetilde{\delta_{3}})}{\pi^{2}}\right)}{\frac{4\pi^{4}\kappa_{1}}{9(1-\alpha)^{1/2}}}=\frac{9(1-\alpha)^{1/2}}{\pi^{4}}\left(\frac{4\kappa_{2}}{5}-\frac{3(\widetilde{\delta_{3}}^{\mathrm{im}}+\widetilde{\delta_{3}})}{\pi^{2}}\right).

Taking
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}+10^{-5}
yields a value of at least 0.00988.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.4
.

Given Assumption
7.11
, we have
\widetilde{\delta_{n}}^{\mathrm{im}}=\delta_{n}^{\mathrm{im}}\leq\frac{1}{n-1}
and
\widetilde{\delta_{n}}=\delta_{n}\leq\frac{1}{n(n-1)}
. Following the proof of Theorem
7.3
yields

\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{N_{n}(T)}{N_{n}^{\mathrm{loc}}(T)}\geq\frac{9(1-\alpha)^{1/2}}{\pi^{4}}\left(\frac{4\kappa_{2}}{5}-\frac{3(n+1)}{\pi^{2}n(n-1)}\right)>0.

In the limit as
n\to\infty
, the lower bound approaches 0.0275.
∎
