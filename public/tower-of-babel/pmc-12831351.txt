The emerging role of non-coding RNAs in the pathogenesis of infantile hemangioma
Authors: Najmaldin Saki, Narjes Sadat Sadati, Zeinab Deris Zayeri, Masoud Kargar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfantile hemangioma (IH) is a common benign tumor that occurs in children, affecting both blood vessels and soft tissues. Its pathological features include abnormal proliferation of endothelial cells and an irregular vascular structure. However, the exact genetic and molecular mechanisms behind the development and progression of IH are not yet fully understood. Recent studies suggest that the dysregulation of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), such as microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), is closely related to the causes of various cancers, including IH. Altered expression of these ncRNAs can influence cell proliferation, viability, invasion, clonal expansion, migration, cell cycle progression, and angiogenesis while inhibiting apoptosis. This regulation is crucial for the formation and growth of IH. This article provides an overview of the potential use of ncRNAs as biomarkers or treatment targets in the context of IH development.Keywords: Infantile hemangioma, MiRNA, LncRNA, CircRNA, Biomarker, NcRNA

Introduction

Hemangioma (HA) is a benign neoplasm characterized by the abnormal proliferation of endothelial cells within blood vessels [1, 2]. In 2022, the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) updated the classification methodology for vascular anomalies, including HA [3]. Infantile hemangioma (IH) is the most common type of benign vascular tumor in infants, particularly those born with low birth weight and premature infants. The incidence of IH is 5–10% in newborns, and approximately 75%−80% of individuals diagnosed with IH are female [4, 5]. IH follows a well-defined clinical progression through three distinct phases: the proliferative, plateau, and involuting stages. It typically emerges within three to six months after birth, swiftly advancing into the proliferative phase. During this stage, a surge in blood flow fuels rapid tumor expansion, often alleviating pressure on surrounding tissues and organs. As IH transitions into the involuting phase, the vascular landscape undergoes a striking transformation—some capillary-like vessels enlarge, while others regress, marking the gradual resolution of the tumor [6, 7]. The potential adverse outcomes associated with IH comprise permanent disfigurement, ulceration, hemorrhage, visual impairment, airway constriction, congestive heart failure, and mortality [8, 9]. In comparison to localized lesions of IH, deep-seated lesions pose a higher risk of ulceration and dysfunction. Therefore, these lesions require immediate and aggressive clinical intervention and treatment [10]. The prominent methods to treat IH include pharmacological interventions, surgical procedures, and laser treatments [11]. Propranolol is the first-line treatment for severe IH, offering multiple therapeutic effects, including vasoconstriction, inhibition of cell proliferation, and induction of apoptosis. However, its use comes with potential side effects, including hypoglycemia, respiratory issues, hyperkalemia, bradycardia, hypotension, and sleep disturbances, highlighting the need for careful monitoring during treatment [12, 13]. Moreover, some evidence indicates that HA cells tend to develop resistance to propranolol [14–17]. The exact genetic and molecular mechanisms driving the onset and progression of HA remain incompletely understood. However, research points to a complex interplay of genetic mutations, dysregulated cellular signaling pathways, epigenetic alterations, and immune system imbalances as key contributors to their development and pathogenesis [18–20]. The crucial influence of microRNAs (miRNAs) in stem cell development has been extensively explored in recent studies, with a strong focus on their role in post-transcriptional gene regulation through interactions with the 3'-UTR of target mRNAs [21]. Among the identified miRNAs, miR-21 stands out as a key regulator, frequently overexpressed across various cancer types. Its consistent upregulation strongly suggests a pivotal role in driving cancer progression, making it a crucial focus for oncogenic research and potential therapeutic targeting [22]. Recent evidence suggests that dysregulated function and expression of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), such as miRNAs, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), are closely linked to the etiology of various cancers, including HA [23–25]. Disturbed expression of these ncRNAs can enhance cell proliferation, survival, invasion, clonal expansion, migration, cell cycle progression, and angiogenesis while inhibiting apoptosis, which is crucial for HA formation and growth [26–28]. This article provides an overview of the potential uses of ncRNAs as either biomarkers or targets for treatment in the formation of HA.

The functions and mechanisms of non-coding RNAs

In recent decades, with the help of the human genome project and advanced high-throughput sequencing technology, we have learned that only 2% of the human genome produces around 20,000 proteins [29]. On the other hand, the non-coding regions of the genome are transcribed into ncRNAs over 90%. Although ncRNAs are transcribed and do not code for any protein, it does not mean they are non-functional [30]. In normal physiological conditions, the expression of ncRNAs is specific to the cell type and tissue [31]. These molecules play crucial roles in various biological processes by regulating gene expression at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels [32]. In addition to their normal biological functions, ncRNAs have been implicated in various physiological and pathological processes. The abnormal expression of these molecules is prevalent in different types of cancers and significantly impacts the onset and advancement of tumors [33, 34]. Based on their length, ncRNAs can be classified into two categories: small or short ncRNAs that are less than 200 nucleotides long and long ncRNAs that are greater than 200 nucleotides long. Small ncRNAs include miRNA, endogenous small interfering RNA (siRNA), and P-element-induced wimpy testis (PIWI) interacting RNA (piRNA). Among these, miRNAs are the most abundant small ncRNA molecules formed from hairpin loop structures during transcription [35, 36]. Also, there is another class of ncRNAs called circRNAs that, unlike linear RNAs, possess a distinct structural attribute of being covalently closed, without 5' caps and 3' poly(A) tails, which arise through non-canonical splicing reactions [37]. Currently, the categories of ncRNAs that receive the most research attention constitute miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs (see Fig. 1).

An overview of the biogenesis of ncRNAs. MiRNA biogenesis begins with gene transcription by RNA polymerase II/III, producing a pri-miRNA transcript. Then, the microprocessor complex produces pre-miRNA and is transported to the cytoplasm by Exportin-5 and Ran-GTP, where Dicer cleaves it to produce double-stranded miRNA duplexes. One strand becomes functional, while the other degrades. Within the RISC region, miRNAs duplex and Ago2 protein incorporate, producing a single-stranded mature miRNA. LncRNAs are transcribed from various locations in the genome, including intergenic and exonic regions and distal protein-coding regions, using RNA polymerase II/III. Unlike miRNAs, they are not processed by the Dicer and Drosha enzymes. LncRNAs have complex 3D structures that determine their functions, and they act as sponges by binding to miRNAs. CircRNAs originate mainly from pre-mRNAs and have several biogenesis mechanisms, but they all share a common feature of back-splicing. They are produced via 3'−5' reverse splicing during pre-transcriptional processing of mRNAs by RNA polymerase II. Unlike linear RNA, circRNA does not possess a cap structure at its 5' end or a polyadenylation tail at its 3' end. CircRNA is exported into the cytoplasm after it forms an uninterrupted RNA loop. It acts as an miRNA sponge in the cytoplasm, where it inhibits miRNAs that regulate the expression of target genes. It is worth noting that a complex network of ncRNAs can be observed due to the direct interaction between lncRNAs and miRNAs and between circRNAs and miRNAs through a process known as sponging

MiRNAs

MiRNAs are small ncRNA molecules that are approximately 22 nucleotides in length. They are derived from RNA transcripts and fold back on themselves to form short hairpins. This process occurs in the nucleus through a multistep process [38]. MiRNA responsive elements (MREs) are short sequences of 2–8 nucleotides that are found in various RNA molecules, including transcribed pseudogenes, lncRNAs, circRNAs, mRNAs, and 5' and 3' untranslated regions (UTRs) of coding sequences [39]. MiRNAs can inhibit gene expression by binding to the 3'-UTR of targeted mRNAs through MREs. This complementary sequence matching usually results in mRNA cleavage and represses the expression of post-transcriptional proteins or/and inhibition of translation [40–42]. A gene’s promoter is the DNA segment located upstream of the coding sequence, essential for initiating transcription. MiRNAs are primarily recognized for their role in binding to mRNA post-transcriptionally; however, certain studies indicate that they may also influence transcription by interacting with transcription factors or gene promoter regions. This is said to induce either repression or activation, but it is seen much less commonly than post-transcriptional regulation [43, 44]. MiRNAs also can bind to the coding sequence (CDS) although that is much rarer than attaching to the 3'-UTR. As with 3'-UTR binding, binding to the CDS may cause either mRNA degradation or inhibition in translation. The precise mechanisms may differ but they tend to interfere with translation machinery [45]. The biogenesis of miRNAs involves several processing steps in the nucleus and cytoplasm. It begins with the transcription of the miRNA gene by RNA polymerase II/III, a process that produces a primary miRNA (pri-miRNA) transcript. The pri-miRNA processing occurs within the nucleus, where a microprocessor complex comprising Drosha (RNase) and its cofactor DGCR8 produces pre-miRNA. These pre-miRNAs are then transported to the cytoplasm by Exportin-5 and Ran-GTP via the nuclear pores. In the cytoplasm, Dicer, another RNase, cleaves pre-miRNAs at the stem-loop structure to produce 20–22 nucleotide double-stranded miRNA duplexes. Following the formation of the duplex, one of the strands transforms into a functional miRNA strand, whereas the other strand gets decomposed. MiRNAs duplex and argonaut-2 (Ago2) protein incorporated in the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) region. This incorporation produces a single-stranded mature miRNA with a length of 22 nucleotides from one strand [46, 47]. The roles of miRNAs are connected to their physiological surroundings and the type of disease they are associated with. They can act as tumor suppressors (oncosupressor miRs) to prevent tumor cell proliferation or as oncogenes (oncomiRs) to stimulate tumorigenesis [48, 49].

LncRNAs

Compared to miRNAs, lncRNAs have longer sequences (>200 nt), a complex spatial structure, and diverse information content. Thus, they may form the basis of the genetic regulatory network [50]. LncRNA expression is tissue-specific, most found in the nucleus and some in the cytoplasm [51]. The lncRNA categorization is based on their location relative to protein-coding transcripts. These include sense, antisense, bidirectional, intragenic, and intergenic lncRNAs [52]. LncRNAs regulate their target genes via interactions with proteins, RNAs, and DNAs at transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels. There are several mechanisms involved in a particular process. These include recruiting transcription factors to gene promoters and binding to enhancers and the 3'-UTRs of genes. Also, these molecules compete for miRNA binding sites with mRNAs, contributing to the regulation of mRNA splicing and function. Furthermore, they serve as precursors to other types of ncRNAs [53, 54]. The biogenesis of lncRNAs does not involve the processing pathways mediated by Dicer and Drosha enzymes. They can be transcribed from various locations in the genome, including intergenic and exonic regions and distal protein-coding regions. RNA polymerase II/III is involved in the transcription process. The mature lncRNAs have complex 3D structures that determine their various functions. Additionally, lncRNAs can act as sponges by binding to miRNAs [55, 56]. Like miRNAs, numerous studies have shown that lncRNAs function as oncogenes or tumor suppressors in the modulation of tumorigenesis and progression [57, 58].

CircRNAs

Recently, circRNAs have emerged as a prominent class of ncRNAs and garnered significant attention within the scientific community [59, 60]. CircRNAs are ncRNA molecules characterized by their covalently closed structure and are present in abundant quantities within the transcriptomes of eukaryotic organisms [61]. CircRNAs are typically classified based on their origin into six distinct categories, namely Exonic circRNAs (EcircRNAs), Intronic circRNAs (ciRNAs), Exon-intron circRNAs (EIciRNAs), Read-through circRNAs (rt-circRNAs), Fusion circRNAs (f-circRNAs), and tRNA intronic circRNAs (tricRNAs). Exonic circRNAs represent the most prevalent category among these molecules [62]. In addition, circRNAs are produced via 3'−5' reverse splicing during pre-transcriptional processing of mRNAs by RNA polymerase II. Unlike linear RNA, circRNA does not possess a cap structure at its 5' end or a polyadenylation tail at its 3' end. This unique characteristic enables circRNA to resist degradation by exonuclease RNase R, augments its stability, and prolongs its half-life compared to linear RNA [63]. Various investigations on the unique attributes and multifarious cellular roles of circRNAs, such as their ability to sponge miRNAs, influence reciprocal interactions and undergo protein translation, regulate gene transcription, and interfere with linear mRNA splicing, have illuminated their significance in numerous cancer hallmarks [64, 65]. These findings suggest that circRNAs have the potential to function as essential novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets in the treatment of cancer.

MiRNAs and hemangioma

Several studies have provided evidence for the involvement of miRNAs in a wide range of biological processes, including tumor angiogenesis and vascular-related disorders [66–68]. These molecules can function as either promoters or suppressors. Also, miRNAs can affect genes that regulate angiogenesis, such as endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and vascular remodeling [69]. Research involving microarray profiling and bioinformatic analyses has revealed that miRNA plays a significant role in the development of HA (see Fig. 2). For example, Li et al. conducted a study on IH using high-throughput sequencing and identified 142 miRNAs that were differentially expressed. Among these, 42 miRNAs were up-regulated, while 100 were down-regulated [70]. In another study, miRNA expression profiling was performed on hemangioma endothelial cells (HemECs), which revealed that 15 miRNAs were up-regulated and 18 miRNAs were down-regulated compared to regular endothelial cell control lines [71]. Additionally, Fu et al. conducted a miRNA microarray analysis on proliferative IH and found 68 differentially expressed miRNAs. Of these 68 miRNAs, 65 were up-regulated, while three were down-regulated compared to normal skin tissues [72]. Extensive research has explored the role of miRNAs in regulating critical cellular processes in HA, including cell viability, proliferation, invasion, colony formation, migration, and apoptosis. These studies, following the identification of key miRNAs, shed light on their intricate involvement in HA progression and potential therapeutic targets.

Up/Downregulated miRNAs

The first studies on miRNAs’ role in angiogenesis focused on miR-132. This particular miRNA is found in high levels in the endothelium of human tumors and in vitro conditions. It acts as a switch to promote angiogenesis by targeting p120RasGAP, a tumor suppressor belonging to the RAS family. This leads to the activation of Ras and the induction of neovascularization. Moreover, administering anti-miR-132 can restore p120RasGAP expression in the tumor endothelium and suppress angiogenesis [73].

MiR-501 is another oncogenic miRNA whose expression is up-regulated in HA cell lines (HDEC and EOMA). The downregulation of this miRNA inhibited HA cell proliferation, cell cycle progression, colony formation, migration, and invasion in vitro. Homeobox D10 (HOXD10), a transcription factor from the homeobox gene family, has been recognized as a direct target of miR-501. Researchers have found that downregulating HOXD10 effectively nullified the impact of the miR-501 inhibitor on HA cell activities, leading to a complete reversal of the observed effects [74]. Similar results were obtained on this regulatory mechanism in endometrial cancer [75].

Ma et al. demonstrated that extracellular vesicles (EVs) released by hypoxic Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were taken up by HemECs and promoted the growth and migration of these cells while inhibiting their programmed cell death. They found that the high expression of miR-210 in the EVs derived from hypoxic HUVECs played a significant role in promoting the growth of HemECs. Moreover, by silencing miR-210 expression in hypoxic induced-HUVEC-EVs, the development of HA was suppressed in hem-nude mice models. The researchers have also discovered that miR-210 targets the HOXA9 gene [76]. The HOXA9 gene belongs to the homeobox gene family, and research has shown that abnormalities in this gene are linked to cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in different types of cancer [77–79]. Interestingly, inhibition of miR-210 expression in hypoxic induced-HUVEC-EVs was associated with elevated mRNA and protein levels of HOXA9 in HemECs [76]. Thus, investigating the role of EVs derived from HUVECs, explicitly focusing on the miR-210/HOXA9 axis, may provide new insights into the understanding and potential treatment of HA.

Several other miRNAs also have been discovered to function as tumor suppressors, and their expression is down-regulated in HA. For instance, The study conducted by SUN et al. found that the expression of miR-195-5p is significantly reduced in HA cell lines, specifically XPTS‑1 and EOMA. The overexpression of this miRNA resulted in the inhibition of HA cell viability and hindered the colony formation and proliferation of HA cells. Additionally, it induced HA cell apoptosis by reducing cell proliferation genes, such as Bcl-2, and promoting apoptosis-inducing genes, such as Bax [80]. V-ski sarcoma viral oncogene homolog, also known as V-SKI or SKI, is a cellular protein encoded by the SKI gene. This protein has been recognized as an oncogene, and its abnormal or activated state has been implicated in various tumors, including HA [81–83]. Notably, researchers observed that the expression of SKI is notably increased in HA cells. They further concluded that miR‑195‑5p has the potential to target the SKI gene. Their findings supported the idea that the overexpression of miR-195-5p led to reduced levels of SKI expression. On the other hand, the overexpression of SKI counteracted the impacts of miR-195-5p on the cell proliferation and apoptosis of HA cells [80].

MiR-424 is another tumor suppressor miRNA whose expression is down-regulated in HemECs. The results of bioinformatic analysis verified that Vascular endothelial growth factor 2 (VEGFR-2) is a direct target of miR-424 in HemECs [84]. VEGFR-2 is a potent angiogenic factor that plays a crucial role as a receptor within the VEGF signaling pathway, controlling the processes of cell migration, proliferation, and angiogenesis [85, 86]. Overexpression of miR-424 significantly decreases expression of VEGFR-2 and cell proliferation and promotes cell apoptosis of HemECs. On the contrary, its down-regulation promotes cell growth and VEGFR-2 expression and suppresses cell apoptosis in these cells. On the other hand, overexpression of miR-424 significantly inhibits the phosphorylation of AKT and ERK1/2 in HemECs [84]. Phosphorylation of AKT and ERK can result in increased cell proliferation and survival and enhanced angiogenesis, all of which contribute to tumor growth and progression [87, 88]. Another study verified the tumor suppressor impact of miR-424 in the HA. MiR-424 has been discovered to directly affect the regulation of fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1) expression [89]. FGFR1, upon binding with its ligand basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), triggers the activation of various functional proteins. This leads to involvement in several signal transduction pathways responsible for regulating critical cellular processes such as cell growth, differentiation, viability, and angiogenesis [90, 91]. Notably, forced overexpression of miR-424 has diminished cell proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and ERK1/2 phosphorylation by blocking the bFGF/FGFR1 pathway in HemECs. Conversely, miR-424 inhibition promoted these functions by promoting the bFGF/FGFR1 pathway in these cells [89].

Hu et al. identified the tumor suppressor role of miR-203a-3p in HemECs. Up-regulation of miR-203a-3p by miR-203a-3p mimics transfection effectively reduced cell proliferation and invasion of HemECs while significantly promoted apoptosis by suppressing and elevating the expression of BCL-2 and C-caspase 3 in these cells. However, the transfection of the miR-203a-3p inhibitor notably reversed these effects in HemECs [26]. Studies have shown that the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is critical in developing HA pathogenesis [92, 93]. In line with this, overexpression and inhibition of miR-203a-3p significantly reduced and elevated the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT in HemECs, respectively [26]. Similar results are also in line with the data previously reported in other tumors, such as renal cell carcinoma [95] and glioblastoma [94]. Recent studies have revealed that VEGFA is crucial in developing HA, particularly in regulating cell proliferation, invasion, apoptosis, and angiogenesis [95–97]. The researchers also demonstrated that miR-203a-3p directly targets Vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), the prominent member of the VEGF protein family. Moreover, treatment with miR-203a-3p mimics can reduce cell proliferation, invasion, and PI3K/AKT phosphorylation while promoting apoptosis in HemECs. On the other hand, overexpression of VEGFA can counteract these effects, indicating that miR-203a-3p/VEGF/PI3K/AKT axis could potentially be used as a biomarker for diagnosing and treating HA [26].

MiR-200c-3p is another down-regulated miRNA in IH tissues compared with healthy tissues. Transfecting a miR-200c-3p inhibitor in human dermal microvascular endothelial cells (HDMEC) resulted in downregulating miR-200c-3p expression. This ultimately led to a significant increase in cell proliferation [98]. The Notch signaling pathway plays a crucial role in promoting angiogenesis during the progression of IH. It demonstrates a pronounced activation state within the corresponding tissue [99, 100]. The researchers also found that miR-200c-3p can influence the expression of LFNG and NCOR2 genes that are involved in the Notch signaling pathway and regulate the proliferation of endothelial cells in HA. In particular, overexpression of miR-200c-3p results in decreased expression of LFNG and NCOR2, while inhibition of miR-200c-3p leads to an increase in the expression of these genes. These findings suggest that miR-200c-3p can promote the proliferation of vascular endothelial cells in HA by modulating the Notch signaling pathway [98]. LncRNAs and hemangioma

LncRNAs can participate in physiological processes as activators or inhibitors. The mechanism of action of lncRNAs is manifested by interaction with various molecules such as DNA, RNA, miRNA, or proteins [131, 132]. Thus, they play a significant role in the development of human cancers. Moreover, the effect of lncRNA in the pathogenesis of diseases on the one hand and the possibility of detecting them in the contents of extracellular fluid such as serum and urine on the other hand, suggests the biomarker potential of lncRNAs while being quickly accessible [133, 134]. Studies have shown that aberrant lncRNA expression is a key factor in the development and progression of HA, particularly in regards to angiogenesis and vasculogenesis (see Fig. 2). For instance, Liu et al. conducted a lncRNA microarray analysis and found that in HA tissues, 1259 lncRNAs are upregulated while 857 lncRNAs are downregulated compared to adjacent normal tissues. Moreover, they identified specific lncRNAs and their target genes via bioinformatics analysis. Among the 292 known mRNAs, 228 were upregulated, and 64 were downregulated [135]. Similarly, Yang et al. performed a microarray analysis of lncRNAs and identified 146 differentially expressed (DE) lncRNAs and 374 DE mRNAs in both proliferating and involuting phases of HA. The researchers found that 42 lncRNAs and 217 mRNAs form a co-expression network, which suggests a correlation between lncRNAs and mRNAs [136]. After identifying the lncRNAs involved in the pathogenesis of HA, various studies have been conducted to clarify their target molecules and introduce their different axes and signaling pathways.

Up/Downregulated lncRNAs

Like miRNAs, multiple lncRNAs have been reported to function as oncogenes, and their dysregulation influences the growth and progression of HA cells. Taurine upregulated gene 1 (TUG1) is a 7.1 kb gene located on chromosome 22q12.2, which is upregulated in mouse retinal cells upon exposure to taurine treatment [137]. TUG1 expression has been altered in various cancer types [138]. A study by Zhou et al. has demonstrated that TUG1 expression is elevated in IH tissues compared to normal tissues. The increased levels of TUG1 promote the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HemECs, particularly during the proliferative phase of IH. TUG1 acts as a sponge for miR-137, inhibiting its function and promoting the expression of Insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 5 (IGFBP5), thereby activating the miR-137/IGFBP5 axis in HemECs [114].

Nuclear enriched abundant transcript 1 (NEAT1) is a crucial lncRNA that was initially recognized as a vital element of nuclear paraspeckles, serving as a structural component [139]. Studies have shown that NEAT1 is overexpressed in several types of cancers and is associated with their progression and development [140]. In HA, Yu et al. reported that NEAT1 acts as a ceRNA, and its expression is significantly higher in proliferating phase tissues compared to involuting phase and normal skin tissues. Moreover, NEAT1 is a sponge of miR-361-5p. By negatively regulating miR-361-5p, it increases the expression of VEGFA and activates the miR-361-5p/VEGFA pathway in HemECs, which eventually promotes proliferation and migration and inhibits apoptosis of these cells [115]. In another study, Li et al. confirmed that NEAT1 is a ceRNA highly expressed during the proliferating stage of IH tissues compared to the involuting stage and normal subcutaneous tissues. Moreover, the introduction of si-NEAT1 in HemECs results in a significant reduction in cell proliferation, migration, and invasion compared to the control group. As a sponge for miR-33a-5p, NEAT1 negatively regulates miR-33a-5p, promotes the expression of HIF-1α and NF-kB, and activates the HIF-1α/NF-kB signaling pathway in HemECs [116]. Alkylation repair homolog protein 5 (ALKBH5) is an enzyme that can remove N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modifications from RNA molecules [141]. In IH, Peng et al. found that ALKBH5 promotes the expression of NEAT1 in XPTS-1 cells by reducing the m6A modification of NEAT1. Moreover, miR-378b is downregulated in IH tissues; NEAT1 is a sponge of miR-378b, and there is a negative correlation between them. FOS-like antigen 1 (FOSL1) is a target gene regulated by miR-378b, and the NEAT1/miR-378b/FOSL1 axis promotes the progression of IH by ALKBH5 [117]. Similar results are also in line with the data reported in hepatocellular carcinoma [142], colon cancer [143], and gastric cancer [144].

Metastasis-associated lung cancer transcript 1 (MALAT1) is an intergenic transcript located at 11q13.1 and is closely associated with the occurrence and development of various human cancers [145]. The expression level of this lncRNA is higher in the proliferating phase of IH tissue compared to the involuting phase and normal skin tissue. Moreover, MALAT1 is a sponge of miR-424 and negatively regulates miR-424. It regulates the IKK/NF-kB signaling pathway by deactivating and inhibiting miR-424 on its target Mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 3 (MEKK3). MALAT1 silencing has enhanced miR-424 levels and reduced MEKK3 levels. Moreover, MALAT1 silencing has suppressed proliferation, cell viability, cell migration, invasion, and tube formation ability of HemECs while increasing their apoptosis. In addition, this lncRNA affects the expression of genes associated with proliferation and apoptosis, including c-Myc, CyclinD1, Ki-67, Bcl2, and Bax [118]. Another study also confirmed the oncogenic function of this lncRNA and revealed its ability to promote cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and vasoformation in HemECs. In line with this, MALAT1 acts as ceRNA through competitively binding to miR-206 to upregulate its target VEGFA in HemECs. Moreover, inhibiting MALAT1 expression results in downregulating various cellular processes, such as cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and vasoformation, while promoting apoptosis in HemECs. This effect is achieved by regulating the miR-206/VEGFA axis [97]. In general, this finding states that MALAT1, as an oncogenic lncRNA, plays an essential role in the progression of HA cells.

In HA, a few lncRNAs have been found to function as tumor suppressors. Maternally expressed gene 3 (MEG3) is a lncRNA encoded by a gene located on human chromosome 14q32.3 [146]. Previous studies have shown that MEG3 has a tumor-suppressive effect, and its expression is inhibited in various tumor tissues like glioma [147], breast cancer [148], gastric cancer [149], and melanoma [150]. Dai et al. reported that the expression level of this lncRNA is significantly decreased in the proliferating phase of HAs compared with the involuting phase and normal skin tissues. Silencing MEG3 increased cell proliferation and colony formation and accelerated the S phase in the cell cycle in HDEC and CRL-2586 EOMA cells. Conversely, overexpression of MEG3 suppressed the expression of VEGF and Cyclin D1 and reversed these effects. Moreover, the results of qRT-PCR analysis showed that MEG3 acts as a sponge for miR-494, and there is a negative correlation between them. Also, MEG3 upregulates the expression level of Phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) as a tumor suppressor protein and inactivates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by sponging miR-494 in HAs cells. However, the transfection of the miR-494 mimic notably decreased the expression level of PTEN and reversed these effects in these cells. Finally, a in vivo study indicated that overexpression of MEG3 restrained HAs xenograft tumor growth in volume and weight [128].

Cancer susceptibility candidate 2 (CASC2) is another lncRNA that is generally considered to be a tumor suppressor gene. Studies have shown that CASC2 expression is diminished in multiple cancer types [151–153]. Moreover, this lncRNA plays an essential role in regulating angiogenesis, cell proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance [154]. In HA, Dai et al. found that the expression level of this lncRNA is significantly downregulated in the proliferating phase of HAs as compared with the involuting phase and normal tissues. In the proliferating phase of HAs, low expression of the CASC2 was found to be negatively associated with the expression of VEGF. As a sponge for miR-18a-5p, CASC2 binds and inhibits miR-18a-5p, promotes the expression of F-box and leucine rich repeat protein 3 (FBXL3), activates the miR-18a-5p/FBXL3 axis in HemECs, and finally suppresses the growth, cell viability, and colony formation of these cells [129]. CircRNAs and hemangioma

Numerous genetic studies have provided compelling evidence of the crucial role of circRNAs in regulating various fundamental biological processes, including cell cycle control, proliferation, invasion, migration, and apoptosis [155]. CircRNAs have emerged as a promising class of non-coding RNAs that can uniquely regulate gene expression. These molecules exhibit a remarkable ability to act as efficient “miRNA sponges” by competitively binding miRNAs and inhibiting their interaction with target mRNAs. This competition for miRNA binding can lead to alterations in gene expression, making circRNAs potentially essential regulators of cellular processes and disease development [156]. The aberrant expression of certain circRNAs can significantly contribute to the advancement of HA (see Fig. 2). In line with this, microarray profiling and bioinformatics data analysis have revealed a significant number of circRNAs implicated in regulating HA. For example, the first study conducted by Fu et al. on 13,617 IH tissues and adjacent skin tissues with microarray analysis showed that 234 circRNAs are up-regulated and 347 circRNAs are down-regulated. In both groups, exonic circRNAs have allocated the largest amount. The results of the qRT-PCR analysis also revealed an increase in the expression of upregulated hsa-circRNA-100933 and hsa-circRNA-100709, as well as a decrease in the downregulation of hsa-circRNA-104310 [157]. Another group of researchers using bioinformatics data revealed that in the IH patient group, compared to normal skin tissues, 343 circRNAs were up-regulated while 512 circRNAs were down-regulated. They also found that circRNAs in the ceRNA network may function through HIF-1 and Notch signaling pathways [158]. On the biological side, Yuan et al. reported that CircAP2A2 is upregulated in IH tissue samples and exerted an oncogenic role by opposing miR-382-5p activities and up-regulating the expression of VEGFA. The suppression of the CircAP2A2 has been found to inhibit various cellular processes such as proliferation, migration, colony formation, and invasion in both HemECs and HUVEC cells [96]. Interestingly, circRNA ATP5SL also exerted an oncogenic role by acting as a sponge for miR-873-5p, leading to upregulating IGF1R expression in HemECs and HUVECs cell lines. Knockdown of circATP5SL promotes apoptosis and inhibits proliferation, invasion, and migration in these cells [28]. In summary, while circRNAs play a significant role in HA, research on their connection to HA is currently limited. It is strongly recommended that further studies be conducted to investigate the involvement of circRNAs and their downstream target genes or signaling pathways in the development of HA. Role of ncRNAs in drug therapy for HA

Propranolol, a nonselective β-adrenergic receptor (ADRB) antagonist, is the recommended initial treatment option for severe IH [159]. Currently, propranolol is the only medication approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treating complicated IH. It exhibits multiple mechanisms of action, such as inducing apoptosis, inhibiting cell growth, and causing vasoconstriction. However, there are several reported side effects of its use, such as hypoglycemia, respiratory symptoms, hyperkalemia, bradycardia, hypotension, and sleep disturbances [12, 13, 160, 161]. Some evidence indicates that HA cells tend to develop resistance to propranolol [14–17]. In line with this, a few studies have shown that ncRNAs such as miRNA and circRNA contribute to propranolol resistance or sensitivity by regulating cell proliferation, apoptosis, and viability of HA cells, making them potential markers for diagnosis and prognosis. The chromosome 19 microRNA cluster (C19MC) is a cluster of miRNAs specific to primates and located on the human chromosome 19q27.3 [162]. A study by Strub et al. revealed that C19MC miRNAs were highly expressed in IH compared to normal tissues. Additionally, the study identified that C19MC miRNAs were produced and secreted exclusively by IH endothelial cells that tested positive for glucose transporter 1 (GLUT-1). The levels of these miRNAs were associated with the size of IH, its proliferation rate, and its resistance to propranolol treatment. This suggests that it could be a valuable tool for the precise prognosis of IH [163]. Mong et al., in a subsequent study, indicated that propranolol treatment reduced the expression of both LIN28B and miR-498(46) and increased the expression of let-7, leading to reduced cell proliferation and induced epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in involuted IH samples and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). This implies propranolol induces IH involution by modulating the LIN28B/let-7 signaling pathway [164]. In XPTS-1 cells, treatment with propranolol resulted in the decrease of miR-382 via the PTEN/AKT/mTOR pathway [105]. In contrast, propranolol treatment caused a significant reduction in cell proliferation and migration, induced G1 arrest, and stimulated apoptosis of HUVECs through the upregulation of miR-206 and the downregulation of VEGF, respectively. Treatment with miR-206 antagomir significantly improved the cells’ resistance to propranolol by reversing these effects [165]. Similarly, propranolol treatment reduced XPTS-1 cell viability and invasion and increased apoptosis by upregulating miR-424 and downregulating its target VEGFA, respectively [108]. Notably, miR-187-3p is downregulated in IH tissues and promotes propranolol sensitivity of HemECs by suppressing NIPBL expression. The upregulation of miR-187-3p has effectively impeded the viability and proliferation of HemECs while promoting apoptosis in response to propranolol treatment [166]. In a study, it was found that M2-polarized macrophages inhibit the sensitivity of HeM2 polarized macrophages, which has been observed to hinder the effectiveness of propranolol on HemECs. This is due to the release of exosomes containing miR-27a-3p. This exosomal miR-27a-3p is derived from M2-polarized macrophages and inhibits the sensitivity of HemECs to propranolol by binding directly to DKK2. This has been demonstrated both in vitro and in xenograft mouse models of IH, where HemSCs exposed to propranolol exhibit a significant increase in cell proliferation and a decrease in apoptosis [106]. Circ-0000915, a circRNA upregulated in HemECs with chemoresistance to propranolol, was demonstrated to reduce propranolol sensitivity by transcriptionally inducing the IL6/STAT3 pathway. On the other hand, circ-0000915 can upregulate RNF187 by sponging miR-890, thus leading to propranolol resistance in HemECs [130].

Conclusion and future perspectives

The exact cause of HA is not yet fully understood. However, studies involving animals and humans suggest that a combination of factors contributes to its development. These factors include genetic mutations, cellular signaling pathways, epigenetic modifications, and immune dysregulation. Recent research has indicated that alterations in ncRNAs play a significant role in the pathogenesis and progression of HA. Many of these dysregulations impact essential pathways related to cell proliferation, viability, invasion, colony formation, migration, the cell cycle, angiogenesis, and apoptosis. Furthermore, some studies have shown that ncRNAs, such as miRNAs and circRNAs, can influence propranolol resistance or sensitivity by regulating the proliferation, apoptosis, and viability of HA cells, making them potential markers for diagnosis and prognosis. However, the precise mechanisms by which ncRNAs contribute to HA remain unclear. To address this gap in understanding, several measures are suggested. First, the technical tools used to investigate the biological mechanisms of ncRNAs in HA should be improved. Second, it is essential to identify unknown targets of ncRNAs, including miRNAs targeted by lncRNAs and circRNAs, as well as mRNAs influenced by these ncRNAs. Finally, developing new medications that can effectively target ncRNAs and conducting clinical trials to treat HA is crucial. By exploring the complex molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways associated with ncRNAs in HA, we may be able to develop novel clinical diagnostic markers and targeted therapeutic interventions for this condition.
