Ruptured pulmonary arteriovenous malformation causing hemothorax during pregnancy: a case report
Authors: Xueqian Wang, Bing Liu, Lianghai Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary arteriovenous malformation (PAVM) is a rare pulmonary vascular anomaly. During pregnancy, physiological hemodynamic changes and hormonal fluctuations substantially increase the risk of PAVM rupture. Spontaneous hemothorax secondary to ruptured PAVM is extremely uncommon and often presents with nonspecific manifestations, frequently resulting in misdiagnosis and delayed treatment.Case presentationA 33-year-old woman at 31+ 6 weeks of gestation presented to the emergency department with a 30-minute history of sudden-onset left-sided chest pain radiating to the shoulder and back. Initial vital signs were relatively stable; however, progressive hypoxemia developed after admission. Following multidisciplinary consultation, using Triple-Rule-Out Computed Tomography Angiography (TRO-CTA) protocol demonstrated rupture of an aneurysmally dilated pulmonary arteriovenous malformation (PAVM) in the left lower lobe, complicated by hemothorax. Emergency transcatheter pulmonary artery embolization was successfully performed, resulting in rapid hemostasis and clinical stabilization. The pregnancy was subsequently continued, and a healthy neonate was delivered at 36 weeks of gestation.ConclusionsPregnancy increases the risk of PAVM rupture and hemorrhage. Clinicians working in emergency settings should be aware of this rare but potentially fatal condition. Triple-Rule-Out Computed Tomography Angiography (TRO-CTA) is a valuable modality for both diagnosis and therapeutic planning, and transcatheter embolization represents an effective treatment option for this life-threatening complication.

Background

Pulmonary arteriovenous malformation (PAVM) is a rare pulmonary vascular disorder characterized by direct communication between one or more pulmonary arteries and veins in the absence of an intervening capillary bed, resulting in a right-to-left shunt. The most common underlying etiology is hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT), an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by abnormal vascular development affecting the skin, mucosa, lungs, liver, and brain [1]. Approximately 80% of PAVM cases are associated with HHT [2], also known as Osler–Weber–Rendu syndrome, which has an estimated prevalence of 1 in 5,000 individuals and occurs more frequently in women [3, 4]. PAVMs may also occur sporadically or in association with other pathological conditions, including trauma, mitral stenosis, liver cirrhosis, metastatic malignancy, systemic amyloidosis, and infections such as actinomycosis and schistosomiasis [5] (Table 1). During pregnancy, the risk of PAVM rupture is increased and may lead to fatal hemothorax. Hemothorax resulting from PAVM rupture is rare and frequently misdiagnosed. Herein, we report a rare case of ruptured PAVM occurring in late gestation and provide a narrative review of previously published cases, summarizing their clinical characteristics, diagnostic pitfalls, and management strategies.

Case presentation

A 33-year-old primigravida at 31+ 6 weeks of gestation presented to the emergency department at 02:40 on October 10, 2025, with sudden onset of left-sided chest pain lasting 30 min. The pain was localized to the left upper chest, described as a persistent dull ache, and radiated to the left shoulder and back. The patient denied cough, fever, hemoptysis, dyspnea, nausea, vomiting, or recent trauma. Her past medical history and family history were unremarkable.

On admission, vital signs were stable: heart rate 88 beats/min, respiratory rate 20 breaths/min, blood pressure 105/67 mmHg, and peripheral oxygen saturation 95% on room air. Physical examination revealed decreased tactile fremitus and diminished breath sounds over the left lower lung field. Cardiac examination was unremarkable. Obstetric examination showed a fundal height of 29 cm, fetal heart rate of 135 beats/min, cephalic presentation, intact membranes, and no uterine contractions.

Initial differential diagnoses included acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, and pleural disease. Arterial blood gas analysis on room air demonstrated pH 7.42, PaCO₂ 33.9 mmHg, and PaO₂ 88.9 mmHg. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 11 × 10⁹/L, hemoglobin level of 125 g/L, and D-dimer level of 1.1 mg/L. Cardiac biomarkers, liver and renal function tests, and electrocardiography were unremarkable.

After administration of acetaminophen for analgesia and supportive care in the emergency department, the patient reported partial relief of chest pain. Chest computed tomography was recommended by the emergency physician to further clarify the diagnosis; however, the patient and her family declined the examination due to concerns about fetal radiation exposure. Magnetic resonance imaging was also not performed at that time for financial reasons. At 07:00, the patient was reassessed and continued to report mild chest pain on deep inspiration. A repeat electrocardiogram showed no abnormalities, and the patient was subsequently admitted to the obstetrics ward for further management.

After admission to the obstetrics ward, the patient developed tachycardia, with a heart rate of 112 beats per minute, accompanied by hypoxemia. Peripheral oxygen saturation ranged from 92% to 94% on room air and increased to 97%–98% following supplemental oxygen administered via nasal cannula at 3 L/min. Given the unclear etiology of chest pain in combination with hypoxemia, a multidisciplinary consultation involving thoracic surgery, cardiology, and respiratory medicine was convened. After repeated discussions with the patient and her family, consent was obtained to perform Triple-Rule-Out Computed Tomography Angiography (TRO-CTA) .

At 16:00 on October 10, 2025, TR0-CTA revealed a nodular lesion measuring approximately 25 mm × 15 mm in the anterior basal segment of the left lower lobe. The lesion was contiguous with adjacent pulmonary arteries and veins, with indistinct margins. A moderate left-sided hemothorax was also observed, accompanied by compression atelectasis of the adjacent lung parenchyma (Figs. 1 and 2). Based on these imaging findings, rupture of a pulmonary arteriovenous malformation with aneurysmal dilatation was suspected.

Given the patient’s critical condition, an emergency multidisciplinary consultation involving cardiothoracic surgery, interventional radiology, anesthesiology, and intensive care medicine was immediately initiated. Emergency transcatheter pulmonary artery embolization was subsequently planned. At 18:00, transcatheter pulmonary artery embolization was performed. Intraoperative angiography demonstrated focal dilatation and aneurysmal enlargement of the left lower pulmonary artery, with a prominent arteriovenous shunt. Two abnormal feeding arteries were identified; one markedly enlarged and the other relatively slender, consistent with a diagnosis of pulmonary arteriovenous malformation of the left lower lobe with aneurysmal dilatation and a high-flow arteriovenous fistula (Fig. 3). Embolization was successfully achieved by the slow deployment of detachable coils into the distal segments of both abnormal feeding arteries via a microcatheter (Figs. 4 and 5). The procedure was completed at 18:50, after which the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for further monitoring and management.

The patient showed good clinical recovery and was transferred back to the obstetrics ward on postoperative day 2. She was discharged in stable condition on October 15, 2025. An elective cesarean section was performed on November 11, 2025, and both the mother and the neonate have remained free of complications to date.

Discussion

In the present case, the patient developed symptoms during late pregnancy and presented with unilateral hemorrhagic pleural effusion. Pregnancy-related physiological changes, including increased blood volume, elevated cardiac output, and vascular remodeling, may have increased the susceptibility of pre-existing PAVMs to rupture. During pregnancy, circulating blood volume and cardiac output increase substantially, resulting in increased pulmonary blood flow through pre-existing PAVMs, which may increase wall stress and the risk of rupture (6). In addition, hormonal changes, particularly elevated levels of estrogen and progesterone, may promote vascular remodeling and vasodilation, thereby increasing shunt flow and the risk of hemorrhagic complications. These mechanisms are biologically plausible and have been proposed to explain the increased risk of PAVM rupture during pregnancy. However, a causal relationship between pregnancy-related physiological changes and PAVM rupture cannot be established on the basis of a single case.

Most PAVMs are asymptomatic; however, a minority of patients present with dyspnea, palpitations, migraine, hemoptysis, cyanosis, congestive heart failure, or respiratory failure. During pregnancy, chest pain and dyspnea are the most common manifestations of PAVM rupture, whereas hemoptysis, a classic symptom of PAVM, is relatively uncommon. Because these clinical manifestations are often nonspecific, PAVM may be mistaken for pulmonary embolism, pleuritis, acute coronary syndrome, or isolated pleural effusion. Therefore, PAVM should be included in the differential diagnosis of pregnant women presenting with unexplained chest pain, dyspnea, hemoptysis, or pleural effusion. For women with a known family history of PAVM or HHT, preconception multidisciplinary counseling involving pulmonologists, obstetricians, and interventional radiologists is recommended. Preconception evaluation should include contrast-enhanced chest CT, brain magnetic resonance imaging, and screening for visceral vascular malformations, when clinically indicated. Whenever feasible, treatment of identified vascular lesions before pregnancy may reduce the risk of rupture during gestation and improve maternal and fetal outcomes [7].

Triple-rule-out computed tomography angiography (TRO-CTA) integrates pulmonary, aortic, and coronary CT angiography into a single examination. Using a single contrast injection and a single acquisition, TRO-CTA simultaneously visualizes the pulmonary arteries, thoracic aorta, and coronary arteries, enabling rapid evaluation of the three major life-threatening causes of acute chest pain: acute coronary syndrome (ACS), pulmonary embolism (PE), and acute aortic syndrome (AAS). In addition, it provides a comprehensive assessment of adjacent intrathoracic structures, facilitating the identification of alternative causes of chest pain. Owing to its diagnostic efficiency, TRO-CTA can expedite evaluation in the emergency setting and support timely therapeutic decision-making in appropriately selected patients. By combining multiple diagnostic assessments into a single examination, TRO-CTA may reduce the need for multiple imaging studies, thereby potentially decreasing cumulative radiation exposure, contrast medium use, examination time, hospital length of stay, and overall healthcare costs [8].Consequently, TRO-CTA has become an important imaging modality for selected patients presenting with acute chest pain in the emergency department [9]. During pregnancy, concerns about fetal radiation exposure among patients and their families may delay diagnostic imaging, resulting in delayed diagnosis, missed opportunities for timely intervention, and potentially adverse maternal and fetal outcomes. Previous studies have shown that when the fetus is outside the scan field, such as during head or chest CT, fetal radiation exposure is negligible. The fetal radiation dose associated with most diagnostic CT examinations is well below the threshold associated with fetal malformations or pregnancy loss, which is generally considered to be 50 mGy. Moreover, fetal susceptibility to radiation-induced injury is substantially lower during the second and third trimesters than during early gestation [10]. The subsequent development of persistent chest pain, new-onset tachycardia, and progressive hypoxemia substantially increased the clinical suspicion of a life-threatening thoracic condition. At this stage, the potential benefits of timely diagnosis and definitive treatment were considered to outweigh the potential risks of fetal radiation exposure, particularly during the second and third trimesters.A retrospective study involving 18 patients with PAVM found that 8 patients (44.4%) were misdiagnosed at initial presentation because CTA was not performed, leading to non-targeted treatment [11]. Among patients presenting with hemothorax, the misdiagnosis rate reached 80%, with some cases initially diagnosed as lung cancer, isolated pleural effusion, or acute myocardial infarction. Among patients presenting with hemoptysis, three underwent bronchial artery embolization without prior CTA confirmation, and PAVM was subsequently diagnosed due to limited therapeutic response.Existing literature indicates that misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis of PAVM may result in severe complications, including massive hemothorax and hemorrhagic shock, and in rare cases, maternal cardiac arrest or fetal death.

Interventional management of pulmonary arteriovenous malformations includes transcatheter embolization and surgical resection. Owing to its minimally invasive nature and high reproducibility, transcatheter embolization has become the preferred first-line treatment for PAVM [20]. Procedure-related complications are relatively uncommon and mainly include ectopic embolization resulting from migration or dislodgement of embolic materials [21], as well as recanalization of the fistula or residual shunting. Gershon et al. reported 13 cases of pregnancy-associated PAVM, among which 7 patients underwent transcatheter embolization. The estimated fetal radiation dose ranged from 0.50 to 2.2 mGy, and all patients subsequently delivered healthy neonates [22]. Surgical intervention is generally reserved for cases in which embolization fails, lesions are anatomically complex, or PAVM rupture results in life-threatening hemorrhage. Compared with conventional open thoracotomy, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) offers advantages including reduced surgical trauma, improved safety, and faster recovery [23], and has increasingly become the primary surgical approach for PAVM management.

In this case, effective hemostasis was achieved and a favorable clinical outcome was obtained with transcatheter embolization alone after the diagnosis was established, without the need for additional chest tube drainage or surgical intervention. This outcome may be attributable to the timely diagnosis of PAVM rupture and the relatively stable hemodynamic status of the patient. Following transcatheter embolization, the patient’s hemoglobin level and pleural effusion were closely monitored. The hemoglobin level was 108 g/L after the procedure and increased to 112 g/L at discharge. Imaging performed on postoperative day 5 demonstrated a reduction in left-sided pleural effusion compared with baseline (Fig. 6), and follow-up imaging on postoperative day 13 showed complete resolution of the pleural effusion (Fig. 7). At the 8-month postoperative follow-up (June 2026), TRO-CTA showed the embolization coils in a stable position at the treated site. The previously noted moderate left pleural effusion had nearly completely resolved, and no abnormalities were detected in the pulmonary arteries, thoracic aorta, or coronary arteries (Fig. 8). During follow-up, the patient did not develop symptoms such as dyspnea or hypoxemia attributable to pleural effusion. Litzler et al.[24] reported that chest tube drainage before embolization is not recommended in patients with hemothorax, as pleural decompression may exacerbate active bleeding or even precipitate re-rupture of malformed vessels; their study also suggested that closed chest drainage should be performed after embolization.However, in the present case, effective hemostasis was achieved by transcatheter embolization, and the pleural effusion gradually resolved without chest tube drainage. This favorable outcome should not be interpreted as supporting conservative management in all patients with PAVM-associated hemothorax. Conservative management of a moderate hemothorax may be associated with potential risks, including delayed resolution, retained hemothorax, secondary infection, and the possible need for subsequent intervention. Therefore, the decision to perform chest tube drainage should be individualized based on the patient’s hemodynamic status, respiratory status, evidence of ongoing bleeding, and the radiological evolution of the hemothorax. Careful clinical monitoring and serial imaging are essential to guide post-embolization management.

In this case, the patient presented with atypical clinical symptoms, while vital signs and fetal heart rate monitoring were initially normal. Nevertheless, a high level of clinical vigilance was maintained. Following comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation involving obstetrics, respiratory medicine, cardiology, and cardiothoracic surgery, contrast-enhanced computed tomography was promptly performed to establish the diagnosis. Rupture of a pulmonary arteriovenous malformation in the left lower lobe complicated by hemothorax was identified, and emergency transcatheter embolization was subsequently undertaken. Intraoperative catheter angiography precisely localized the bleeding site, and successful hemostasis was achieved using microcoils. The favorable outcome in this case may be attributed to several factors: first, timely implementation of contrast-enhanced CT and interventional therapy, enabling rapid identification of the underlying cause and effective hemorrhage control; second, close multidisciplinary collaboration involving obstetrics, anesthesiology, interventional radiology, and related specialties, which facilitated prompt and accurate clinical decision-making; Lastly, systematic perioperative monitoring, supportive care, and nursing management.Recent case reports have similarly highlighted that successful management of complex obstetric emergencies depends on timely diagnostic evaluation or intervention, together with close multidisciplinary collaboration among obstetricians, radiologists, anesthesiologists, surgeons, and critical care physicians [25, 26]. These principles are consistent with the management strategy adopted in the present case, in which multidisciplinary discussion and dynamic risk–benefit assessment facilitated timely diagnosis and definitive treatment, contributing to favorable maternal and fetal outcomes.

An important limitation of this case is that genetic testing for hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) was not performed. Although the patient had no clinical features suggestive of HHT, including recurrent epistaxis, mucocutaneous telangiectasia, or a family history of HHT, and did not fulfill the Curaçao diagnostic criteria, HHT cannot be definitively excluded. Given that approximately 80% of PAVMs are associated with HHT, genetic testing and appropriate family screening should be considered, when clinically indicated, to establish a definitive diagnosis and guide long-term management.

Conclusions

Pulmonary arteriovenous malformation (PAVM) is a rare but potentially life-threatening condition that should be considered in pregnant women presenting with unexplained chest pain or hemothorax. Prompt recognition, timely imaging with triple-rule-out computed tomography angiography (TRO-CTA) when clinically indicated, and multidisciplinary management are essential for optimizing maternal and fetal outcomes. Transcatheter embolization represents an effective treatment option in appropriately selected patients.
