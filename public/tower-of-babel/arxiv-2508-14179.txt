Nonadiabatic force matching for alchemical free-energy estimation
Jorge L. Rosa-Raíces, David T. Limmer

Abstract
We propose a method to compute free-energy differences from nonadiabatic alchemical transformations using flow-based generative models. The method, nonadiabatic force matching, hinges on estimating the dissipation along an alchemical switching process in terms of a nonadiabatic force field that can be learned through stochastic flow matching. The learned field can be used in conjunction with short-time trajectory data to evaluate upper and lower bounds on the alchemical free energy that variationally converge to the exact value if the field is optimal. Applying the method to evaluate the alchemical free energy of atomistic models shows that it can substantially reduce the simulation cost of a free-energy estimate at negligible loss of accuracy when compared with thermodynamic integration.



Introduction

Computational schemes for accurate, high-throughput free-energy estimation are highly coveted and could enable scientific breakthroughs in materials design
Alberi et al. 2019
and drug discovery
Sadybekov and Katritch 2023
.
Presently, widespread methods adopt an alchemical approach, in which the state of a system is transformed along a thermodynamic path that connects the two equilibrium states,
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
, between which the free-energy difference
\Delta F
is sought
York 2023
.
Changes in the system’s state are driven by a switching potential
U(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
, where the switch
\lambda
runs from
\lambda_{\mathsf{A}}
to
\lambda_{\mathsf{B}}
and indexes a family of equilibrium probability densities

\pi(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)\equiv\exp\{\beta[F(\lambda)-U(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)]\}

(1)

over system configurations
\boldsymbol{x}
, with
\beta^{-1}
the temperature times Boltzmann’s constant and
F(\lambda)
the absolute Helmholtz free energy at
\lambda
.
If the system is driven at a slow enough rate to prevent dissipation throughout the switching, the free-energy difference between its end states is
Kirkwood 1935

\Delta F\equiv F(\lambda_{\mathsf{B}})-F(\lambda_{\mathsf{A}})=\int_{\lambda_{\mathsf{A}}}^{\lambda_{\mathsf{B}}}\negthinspace\mathrm{d}\lambda\thinspace\langle\partial_{\lambda}U\rangle_{\lambda}

(2)

where the angled brackets
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle_{\lambda}
denote a configurational average under the probability density in
Eq.

1
.
The duration of this adiabatic alchemical transformation approaches the quasistatic limit, exceeding the system’s longest relaxation timescales and allowing it to reach equilibrium at every
\lambda
.

Free-energy estimation by quadrature of
Eq.

2
, known as thermodynamic integration (TI), underpins the computation of solubility curves and binding affinities for molecular systems of modest size
Gobbo et al. 2019
;
Cournia et al. 2017
.
However, TI entails sampling a locus of high-dimensional, multi-modal equilibrium states along the thermodynamic path
Mey et al. 2020
, and is seldom tractable at the scale of modern campaigns for drug discovery and materials design.
An alternative avenue for free-energy estimation is suggested by Jarzynski’s work theorem
Jarzynski 1997

\exp(-\beta\Delta F)=\langle\exp(-\beta\mathcal{W}_{\mspace{-2mu}\tau})\rangle

(3)

which relates
\Delta F
to the work
\mathcal{W}_{\mspace{-2mu}\tau}
done on the system to drive it through the thermodynamic path via a switching process of duration
\tau
.
The expression in angled brackets
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle
in
Eq.

3
denotes an average over nonequilibrium work measurements; its evaluation enables free-energy estimation from nonadiabatic alchemical transformations, which are those whose duration is short compared to the quasistatic limit.
Seeking computational expediency beyond that of TI without sacrificing accuracy, a flurry of schemes have emerged to evaluate
Eq.

3
with maximum efficiency and minimum bias
Oberhofer et al. 2005
;
Shirts et al. 2007
;
Dellago and Hummer 2014
;
Wan et al. 2023
.

Naive evaluation of Jarzynski’s equality in
Eq.

3
is far from efficient, as converging the exponential average on its right-hand side often requires exponentially many work measurements
Jarzynski 2006
.
Instead, modern schemes for free-energy estimation based on Jarzynski’s equality target the exponentially rare work measurements that dominate the average, deploying probabilistic generative modeling techniques
Ho et al. 2020
;
Papamakarios et al. 2021
;
Albergo et al. 2023
;
Vargas et al. 2024
to mine quasi-adiabatic paths connecting states
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}

Noé et al. 2019
;
Wirnsberger et al. 2020
;
Zhao and Wang 2023
;
Zhong et al. 2024
;
Máté et al. 2024
;
He et al. 2025a
.
To address scenarios where such paths are intractable due to computational limitations, it is desirable to extend generative modeling approaches to leverage typical nonequilibrium work measurements from simulation or experiment
Liu et al. 2014
;
Ciliberto 2017
;
Ross et al. 2018
;
Bechhoefer et al. 2020
;
Barker et al. 2022
.
Necessary to this end are theoretical perspectives that clarify the dissipative contributions to the exponential average in
Eq.

3
in terms of quantities that can be estimated from readily accessible trajectory data.

In this article, we examine flow matching models (FMMs) as an emergent platform for dissipative free-energy estimation.
After formulating an alchemical interpretation of the FMM archetype and laying out the stochastic thermodynamics of the nonadiabatic transformation that it induces, we elucidate descriptors of the dissipation incurred along the transformation that yield sharp bounds on the free-energy difference.
The bounds are saturated by a flow field that extends a stationary analogue introduced in Ref.
Rosa-Raíces and Limmer 2024
to evaluate log-likelihood functions for active systems, and that can be learned by training a FMM.
Numerical demonstrations on atomistic models of fast-growth solvation and of Frenkel–Ladd solid formation confirm that, given a nonadiabatic alchemical transformation and an associated FMM, our variational bounds can produce a free-energy estimate with comparable accuracy to TI at a substantially reduced simulation cost.

The stochastic thermodynamics

of flow matching models

FMMs learn a two-sided mapping between distributions of
data
and
noise
that allows generating samples from either distribution by transporting samples from the other.
The mapping is comprised of a pair of stochastic processes, of which the
forward
process is given and the
backward
process is its learned time-reversal
Song et al. 2021a
.
Here we specify an alchemical FMM paradigm in which the map is determined by a switch
\lambda(t)
of duration
\tau
with the boundary values
\lambda(t\mathord{\mspace{2mu}\scalebox{0.67}[1.0]{$=$}\mspace{3mu}}0)\equiv\lambda_{\mathsf{A}}
and
\lambda(t\mathord{\mspace{2mu}\scalebox{0.67}[1.0]{$=$}\mspace{3mu}}\tau)\equiv\lambda_{\mathsf{B}}
, and where the forward process

\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t}=-\beta D\boldsymbol{\nabla}U\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{(}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},\lambda(t)\mathclose{}\boldsymbol{)}\thinspace\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2D}\thinspace\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{w}_{\mspace{-1mu}t}

(4)

has an initial configuration
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}0}
drawn from the data distribution with density
\pi(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda_{\mathsf{A}})
.
Here and throughout,
D
denotes a diffusion coefficient,
\{\boldsymbol{w}_{\mspace{-1mu}t}\}_{t=\mspace{1mu}0}^{\tau}
a standard Brownian motion, and
U\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{(}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},\lambda(t)\mathclose{}\boldsymbol{)}
the switching potential introduced in
Eq.

1
.
The forward process in
Eq.

4
maps the data distribution onto the noise distribution with density
\rho(\boldsymbol{x},\tau)\thinspace\mathord{\equiv}\thinspace\langle\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}\tau}\thinspace\mathord{-}\thinspace\boldsymbol{x})\rangle
, where
\delta
is the Dirac delta and where the angled brackets
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle
denote an average over realizations of the forward process.
The noise density satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation

\partial_{t}\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t)=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot[D\boldsymbol{\nabla}V(\boldsymbol{x},t)\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t)]

(5)

subject to the initial condition
\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t\mathord{\mspace{2mu}\scalebox{0.67}[1.0]{$=$}\mspace{3mu}}0)\equiv\pi(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda_{\mathsf{A}})
, and the nonadiabatic potential

V(\boldsymbol{x},t)\equiv-\ln\frac{\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t)}{\pi\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{(}\boldsymbol{x},\lambda(t)\mathclose{}\boldsymbol{)}}

(6)

advects probability density from state
\mathsf{A}
toward state
\mathsf{B}
.

Physically, the forward process in
Eq.

4
corresponds to a nonadiabatic alchemical transformation where a system, initially at thermal equilibrium in state
\mathsf{A}
, is mechanically driven toward state
\mathsf{B}
in an irreversible manner.
Although this transformation occurs far from equilibrium, the free-energy difference
\Delta F
remains well-defined within the framework of stochastic thermodynamics
Limmer 2024
;
Seifert 2025
.
By the first law, the change of energy along the transformation is

\Delta U\equiv\langle U\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{(}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}\tau},\lambda(\tau)\mathclose{}\boldsymbol{)}-U\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{(}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}0},\lambda(0)\mathclose{}\boldsymbol{)}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{W}_{\mspace{-2mu}\tau}\rangle-\langle\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}\rangle

(7)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mspace{-2mu}\tau}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{\mspace{-1mu}\tau}\negthickspace\mathrm{d}t\thinspace\partial_{t}U\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{(}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},\lambda(t)\mathclose{}\boldsymbol{)}

(8a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}

\displaystyle\equiv-\int_{0}^{\mspace{-1mu}\tau}\negthickspace\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t}\mspace{2mu}\mathord{\circ}\mspace{2mu}\boldsymbol{\nabla}U\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{(}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},\lambda(t)\mathclose{}\boldsymbol{)}

(8b)

the work exerted on the system and the heat dissipated by the system, respectively, along a realization of the process.
Using
Eq.

1
, the first equality in
Eq.

7
reduces to an expression for the Helmholtz free-energy difference, namely
(
SI
, Sec. I)

\Delta F=\Delta U-\beta^{-1}[\Delta S+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tau)]

(9)

where

\Delta S\equiv\langle-\ln[\rho(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}\tau},\tau)/\pi(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}0},\lambda_{\mathsf{A}})]\rangle

is the entropy change of the system along the transformation; the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(t)\equiv\biggl\langle\ln\frac{\rho(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},t)}{\pi\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{(}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},\lambda(t)\mathclose{}\boldsymbol{)}}\biggr\rangle=\langle-V(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},t)\rangle

(10)

quantifies the nonadiabatic lag between the state of the system at each time
t\mspace{5mu}\mathord{\in}\mspace{5mu}[0,\tau]
and its counterpart in the quasistatic limit.
The second law of thermodynamics provides a decomposition of
\Delta S
through an entropy balance that clarifies
Eq.

9

Seifert 2005
;
Muratore-Ginanneschi and Schwieger 2017
.
Since the system evolves at a fixed temperature, entropy changes in the ideal, implicit bath are reversible and we find
(
SI
, Sec. II)

\Delta S=\biggl\langle\int_{0}^{\mspace{-1mu}\tau}\negthickspace\mathrm{d}t\thinspace D|\boldsymbol{\nabla}V(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},t)|^{2}\biggr\rangle-\beta\langle\mathcal{Q}_{\tau}\rangle

(11)

where the first term is the entropy produced along the transformation, which is nonnegative, and the second is the dissipated heat introduced in
Eq.

7
.
Combining
Eqs.

7
,
9
,
10
and
11
, we finally obtain

\Delta F=\langle\mathcal{W}_{\mspace{-2mu}\tau}\rangle-\beta^{-1}\biggl\langle\int_{0}^{\mspace{-1mu}\tau}\negthickspace\mathrm{d}t\thinspace D|\boldsymbol{\nabla}V(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},t)|^{2}-V(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}\tau},\tau)\biggr\rangle

(12)

an equality relating
\Delta F
to the dissipation along a nonadiabatic alchemical transformation from state
\mathsf{A}
toward state
\mathsf{B}
.

Equation

12
furnishes a refinement of Jensen’s bound on Jarzynski’s equality in
Eq.

3
,

\Delta F\leqslant\langle\mathcal{W}_{\mspace{-2mu}\tau}\rangle

(13)

in that it explicitly states the dissipative terms that close the gap from Jensen’s bound, rendering the inequality an equality.
These terms depend on the nonadiabatic potential
V(\boldsymbol{x},t)
in
Eq.

6
, whose gradient can be regressed from the forward process by minimizing the loss function
(
SI
, Sec. III)

\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})\equiv\biggl\langle\int_{0}^{\mspace{-1mu}\tau}\negthickspace\mathrm{d}t\thinspace D|\boldsymbol{\nabla}V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},t)|^{2}-2\negthinspace\int_{0}^{\mspace{-1mu}\tau}\negthickspace\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t}\mspace{2mu}\mathord{\circ}\mspace{2mu}\boldsymbol{\nabla}V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},t)\biggr\rangle

(14)

with respect to the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
of the differentiable ansatz
V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
If
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}\thinspace\mathord{\equiv}\thinspace\argmin\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})
are optimal parameters, then the optimal nonadiabatic potential
V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}
satisfies

V(\boldsymbol{x},t)=V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)-\ln\langle\exp[V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},t)]\rangle

(15)

where the cumulant shift ensures correct normalization.
For the initial condition in
Eq.

5
, the optimal nonadiabatic potential must satisfy the boundary condition
V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}(\boldsymbol{x},t\mathord{\mspace{2mu}\scalebox{0.67}[1.0]{$=$}\mspace{3mu}}0)\thinspace\mathord{\equiv}\thinspace 0

Taken together,
Eqs.

12
,
14
and
15
suggest a scheme to estimate

by training FMM to quantify the dissipation along a nonadiabatic alchemical transformation.
Given an ensemble of realizations of the forward process, a nonadiabatic potential ansatz

can be trained by minimizing

14

Eq.

15

Eq.

12

If the learned nonadiabatic potential is optimal, then this procedure yields a statistically unbiased estimate of
\Delta F
at no greater simulation cost than evaluating the work bound in
Eq.

13
.

Flow matching models enable

variational free-energy estimation

Since the exact nonadiabatic potential in
Eq.

6
is seldom known, unbiased free-energy estimation with
Eq.

12
is all but intractable in realistic applications.
Here, we argue that the forward and backward processes that comprise an alchemical FMM are associated with variational upper and lower bounds on
\Delta F
.
These bounds control the systematic bias incurred by a suboptimal nonadiabatic potential and, by virtue of their variational character, are simultaneously saturated by the equality in
Eq.

12
for an optimal potential.

Probabilistic inference in FMMs is achieved through a map from noise to data that is enabled by a learned flow field.
In alchemical FMMs, this map is effected by the backward process

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t}=

\displaystyle\mathord{-}D[\beta\boldsymbol{\nabla}U\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{(}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},\lambda(\tau\mspace{-1mu}\mathord{\mspace{2mu}\scalebox{0.67}[1.0]{$-$}\mspace{3mu}}\mspace{-1mu}t)\mathclose{}\boldsymbol{)}+2\boldsymbol{\nabla}V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},\tau\mspace{-1mu}\mathord{\mspace{2mu}\scalebox{0.67}[1.0]{$-$}\mspace{3mu}}\mspace{-1mu}t)]\thinspace\mathrm{d}t

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbin{+}\sqrt{2D}\thinspace\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{w}_{\mspace{-1mu}t}

where the role of the flow field falls on the learned nonadiabatic force
-\boldsymbol{\nabla}V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
A physical system evolving under the backward process has an initial configuration
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}0}
drawn from the noise distribution with density
\rho(\boldsymbol{x},\tau)
and is driven by the time-reversed switch
\lambda(\tau\mspace{-1mu}\mathord{\mspace{2mu}\scalebox{0.67}[1.0]{$-$}\mspace{3mu}}\mspace{-1mu}t)
toward the equilibrium distribution at state
\mathsf{A}
.
If the learned nonadiabatic force is optimal, then the backward process maps the system directly onto state
\mathsf{A}
, enabling the generation of equilibrium samples through the time-reversed nonadiabatic alchemical transformation.

A connection between the backward process and the free-energy difference
\Delta F
can be gleaned from the flexible trainability of FMMs through either flow matching or minimization of the relative entropy between forward and backward processes
Song et al. 2021b
.
Specifically, the nonadiabatic potential
V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}
that enters the backward process can be trained by minimizing the loss in
Eq.

14
or by minimizing the
backward
Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence

\biggl\langle\negthinspace\mathord{-}\ln\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{\mspace{-5mu}\tau}[\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}\tau-t}\}_{t=\mspace{1mu}0}^{\tau}]}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{\mspace{-2mu}\boldsymbol{\theta},\tau}[\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t}\}_{t=\mspace{1mu}0}^{\tau}]}\biggr\rangle_{\negthickspace\boldsymbol{\theta}}\geqslant\thinspace 0

(17)

between forward and backward path ensembles, where the symbol

\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{\mspace{-5mu}\tau}[\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}\tau-t}\}_{t=\mspace{1mu}0}^{\tau}]/\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{\mspace{-2mu}\boldsymbol{\theta},\tau}[\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t}\}_{t=\mspace{1mu}0}^{\tau}]

denotes the likelihood of a backward trajectory
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t}\}_{t=\mspace{1mu}0}^{\tau}
relative to its time-reversal
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}\tau-t}\}_{t=\mspace{1mu}0}^{\tau}
, and the angled brackets
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
denote an average over realizations.
As shown in Sec. IV of Ref.
SI
, the relative likelihood takes the form

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{\mspace{-5mu}\tau}[\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}\tau-t}\}_{t=\mspace{1mu}0}^{\tau}]}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{\mspace{-2mu}\boldsymbol{\theta},\tau}[\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t}\}_{t=\mspace{1mu}0}^{\tau}]}=\exp\bigl[V(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}0},\tau)-\beta\bigl(\overline{\mathcal{W}}_{\mspace{-2mu}\tau}+\Delta F\bigr)-\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\tau}\bigr]

(18)

where the reverse-time work and surprisal are given by

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{W}}_{\mspace{-2mu}\tau}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{\mspace{-1mu}\tau}\negthickspace\mathrm{d}t\thinspace\partial_{t}U\mathopen{}\boldsymbol{(}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},\lambda(\tau\mspace{-1mu}\mathord{\mspace{2mu}\scalebox{0.67}[1.0]{$-$}\mspace{3mu}}\mspace{-1mu}t)\mathclose{}\boldsymbol{)}

(19a)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta},\tau}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{\mspace{-1mu}\tau}\negthickspace\mathrm{d}t\thinspace D|\boldsymbol{\nabla}V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},\tau\mspace{-1mu}\mathord{\mspace{2mu}\scalebox{0.67}[1.0]{$-$}\mspace{3mu}}\mspace{-1mu}t)|^{2}

(19b)

\displaystyle{\hskip 25.00003pt}\mathord{-}\medspace\sqrt{2D}\thinspace\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{w}_{\mspace{-1mu}t}\thinspace\mathord{\cdot}\thinspace\boldsymbol{\nabla}V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},\tau\mspace{-1mu}\mathord{\mspace{2mu}\scalebox{0.67}[1.0]{$-$}\mspace{3mu}}\mspace{-1mu}t)

respectively.
Due to the nonnegativity of the KL divergence, one infers from
Eqs.

17
and
18
the relation
(
SI
, Sec. V A)

\displaystyle\Delta F\geqslant

\displaystyle\langle\mathord{-}\overline{\mathcal{W}}_{\mspace{-2mu}\tau}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\beta^{-1}\biggl\langle\int_{0}^{\mspace{-1mu}\tau}\negthickspace\mathrm{d}t\thinspace D|\boldsymbol{\nabla}V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},\tau\mspace{-1mu}\mathord{\mspace{2mu}\scalebox{0.67}[1.0]{$-$}\mspace{3mu}}\mspace{-1mu}t)|^{2}\biggr\rangle_{\negthickspace\boldsymbol{\theta}}

(20)

\displaystyle\mathbin{+}\beta^{-1}\bigl[\langle V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}0},\tau)\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\ln\langle\exp[V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}0},\tau)]\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\bigr]

which provides a
backward variational bound
on
\Delta F
in terms of the work done and the dissipation incurred along the backward process.
A similar argument starting from the

KL divergence between forward and backward processes, laid out in Sec. V B of Ref.

\displaystyle\Delta F\leqslant

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{W}_{\mspace{-2mu}\tau}\rangle-\beta^{-1}\biggl\langle\int_{0}^{\mspace{-1mu}\tau}\negthickspace\mathrm{d}t\thinspace D|\boldsymbol{\nabla}V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}t},t)|^{2}\biggr\rangle

\displaystyle\mathbin{+}\beta^{-1}\bigl[\langle V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}\tau},\tau)\rangle-\ln\langle\exp[V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}\tau},\tau)]\rangle\bigr]

and thus recasts

Efficiency gains from nonadiabatic flow matching for free-energy estimation

We provide a numerical demonstration of alchemical FMMs for free-energy estimation by evaluating the backward variational bound in

20

We use a simple linear switch

\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
at the endpoints of the alchemical transformation.
This switch recovers the quasistatic limit as

, allowing us to compare the simulation cost to evaluate the backward variational bound in
Eq.

20

Eq.

We set the diffusion coefficient to unity and use the Euler–Maruyama integrator

WCA monomer solvation

As a canonical benchmark problem, we consider the free energy to solvate a point particle in a three-dimensional Weeks–Chandler–Andersen (WCA) solvent.
In our simulations, the solvent is comprised by
N\thinspace\mathord{=}\thinspace 512
point particles with positions
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, their interactions governed by the pairwise energy function

u_{\mathrm{WCA}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}i},\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}j}|\mspace{2mu}\sigma)\equiv 4\epsilon\thinspace(r_{ij}^{-12}-r_{ij}^{-6}+1/4)\thinspace\boldsymbol{1}\{r_{ij}\!\leqslant 2^{1/6}\}

(22)

with
\sigma
the solvent size,
\epsilon
the interaction energy scale,
r_{ij}\thinspace\mathord{\equiv}\thinspace|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}j}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}i}|/\sigma
the dimensionless distance between particles
i
and
j
, and
\boldsymbol{1}\{r_{ij}\thinspace\mathord{\leqslant}\thinspace\,\cdot\,\}
a distance cutoff indicator.
The solvent occupies a periodic cubic box with a side length of
8.110\,\sigma
and is held at a temperature that corresponds to
\beta^{-1}\thinspace\mathord{=}\thinspace 1.214\,\epsilon
.
These parameters define the equilibrium distribution of the system at state
\mathsf{A}
, whose density is proportional to the Boltzmann factor
\smash{\exp\bigl[-\beta\sum\nolimits_{i<j}u_{\mathrm{WCA}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}i},\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}j}|\mspace{2mu}\sigma)\bigr]}
, where the sum runs over solvent atom pairs.

The alchemical path to the solvated state starts with the insertion of a small solute into a random pocket of the equilibrium solvent.
The free-energetic cost of this operation, which we neglect, is well captured by a Poisson point process approximation of Widom’s scheme that takes advantage of the solute’s small size at insertion

In general, physical insight into the role of excess work informs the design of efficiently trainable nonadiabatic potential ansätze for accurate free-energy estimation.
We use an ansatz that captures energetic fluctuations due to solute-solvent interactions along the solute growth direction while ignoring higher-order contributions due to solvent-solvent interactions.
The ansatz is comprised of a Behler-style neural centrosymmetric forcefield
Behler 2011
whose inputs are comprised by
N
solute-solvent pair distances
\{\phi(|\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{S}}\thinspace\mathord{-}\medspace\boldsymbol{x}_{\mspace{-1mu}i}|/\sigma)\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where

\phi(r)\equiv\frac{1}{2}[1+\cos(\pi r^{2}\negthinspace/4)]\exp(-r^{2}\negthinspace/4)\thinspace\boldsymbol{1}\{r\leqslant 2\}

(25)

is a radial symmetry function.
These inputs are passed to a shallow, fully-connected neural network (NN) with
N
neurons in the input layer,
4N
neurons in a hidden layer, and a scalar output layer that yields the nonadiabatic potential
V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
.
To ensure that the learned potential satisfies the boundary condition
V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x},t\mathord{\mspace{2mu}\scalebox{0.67}[1.0]{$=$}\mspace{3mu}}0)\equiv 0
, the NN inputs are multiplied by the instantaneous switching value
\lambda(t)
, the linear bias of each layer is fixed at zero, and exponential linear units
Clevert et al. 2016
are used as activation functions in all layers.
The layer weights
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are optimized by minimizing
Eq.

14
with the Adam optimizer
Kingma and Ba 2015

V_{\!\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
is used to produce
100
statistically independent Euler–Maruyama solutions of
Eq.

16

Eq.

4

Although this may result in a reasonable approximation of the fluctuating velocity field near the quasistatic limit, it rapidly degrades at solute growth rates faster than those considered here.

Concluding remarks

We present a stochastic-thermodynamic framework for free-energy estimation along a nonadiabatic alchemical transformation that hinges on training a flow-based generative model.
Within this framework, we derive variational estimators for the free-energy difference that, coupled with an efficient neural ansatz for the nonadiabatic force field, can reduce the simulation cost of a statistically unbiased free-energy estimate by orders of magnitude relative to methods that operate within the quasistatic limit, such as thermodynamic integration.
In our applications of the variational free-energy bound in
Eq.

20
, we use computationally inexpensive ansätze for the nonadiabatic force to resolve its fluctuations along dominant dissipation pathways while ignoring subdominant higher-order channels that contribute negligibly to the overall dissipation. In doing so, we illustrate the cost benefits of using system-adapted neural ansätze that intuitively capture the physics of the alchemical transformation.
All numerical results presented in this work, along with generating code, are available in the GitHub repository
jrosaraices/nonadiabatic-force-matching
.

The current work adds to a rapidly growing collection of free-energy estimation schemes that leverage controlled diffusions.
Of note are Máté
et. al.

Neural TI

Máté et al. 2025

et. al.

He et al. 2025a

In these works, a counterdiabatic potential is sought to advect a molecular system along a set of equilibrium states connecting
\mathsf{A}

\mathsf{B}

In contrast, our approach to free-energy estimation evolves from Hummer’s

Leaving an explicit performance comparison for the future, we believe that the fast-growth approach exhibits a computational advantage over the escorted approach in settings where training-adapted data generation would be too expensive or done
