Association of second-hand tobacco smoking with liver cancer, and interaction with HBV infection in a Chinese population-based case–control study
Authors: Xing Liu, Aileen Baecker, Ming Wu, Jin-Yi Zhou, Zi-Yi Jin, Fang Fang, Su Yon Jung, Lina Mu, Na He, Weidong Qu, Xuehong Zhang, Liming Li, Jin-Kou Zhao, Zuo-Feng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Although tobacco smoking is one of the established risk factors for liver cancer, results from epidemiological studies remain inconclusive on the relationship between second-hand smoke (SHS) and liver cancer, particularly among nonsmokers.Objectives:This study aimed to examine the association between SHS and liver cancer, and to assess its potential interaction with major risk factors such as hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection.Methods:A population-based case–control study was conducted in Jiangsu, China, from 2003 to 2010, including 2011 newly diagnosed primary liver cancer cases and 7933 population-based controls. Data on SHS exposure at home and in the workplace, along with other major liver cancer risk factors such as alcohol consumption, were collected through self-reported questionnaires. SHS exposure was assessed as a dichotomous variable, categorizing participants as either exposed or unexposed to SHS. HBV infection status was determined by testing serum samples for serum hepatitis B virus surface antigen. Multivariable unconditional logistic regression models were used to examine the association between SHS and risk of liver cancer. Besides stratified analyses, interactions on additive and multiplicative scales were further evaluated between SHS and other risk factors for liver cancer.Results:Exposure to SHS was associated with liver cancer, shown as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) of 1.84 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.56, 2.18) in the overall population and 2.11 (95% CI: 1.67, 2.65) among never smokers. Stratified analyses showed that the association between SHS and liver cancer was stronger among HBV-positive participants (OR: 2.09, 95% CI: 1.48, 2.93) compared with HBV-negative participants (OR: 1.87, 95% CI: 1.54, 2.27) (P = 0.02). Both super-additive and super-multiplicative interactions were observed between SHS and HBV infection, with relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) of 12.56 (95% CI: 6.60, 18.53) and ratio of ORs of 1.55 (95% CI: 1.07, 2.23) in the total population, and RERI of 12.87 (95% CI: 4.93, 20.81) and ratio of ORs of 1.78 (95% CI: 1.06, 2.99) among never smokers.Conclusion:SHS is independently associated with liver cancer, and this association is further modified by HBV infection, leading to a substantially elevated risk, particularly among nonsmokers.

What this study adds

In a Chinese population with one of the world’s highest second-hand smoke (SHS) exposure, we provide the first large-scale evidence that passive smoking is an independent liver-carcinogen, doubling risk among lifetime never smokers. We further quantify a strong super-additive interaction between household SHS and hepatitis B virus infection, indicating that viral infection and SHS-derived carcinogens act synergistically to increase the absolute burden of liver cancer among nonsmokers.

Introduction

Liver cancer poses a major public health burden, ranking sixth in incidence and third in mortality among all cancers, claiming over 758,444 lives worldwide in 2022.1 Approximately 42% of the liver cancer incident cases and deaths occur in China,2 due to the high prevalence of established risk factors in the Chinese population,3 including chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection,4 heavy alcohol use,5 and aflatoxin-contaminated food intake.6 Accumulated evidence indicates that active tobacco smoking is positively associated with liver cancer risk.7 Tobacco smoking remains one of the most important and preventable risk factors for human diseases worldwide, yet its prevalence continues to rise in some countries.8 According to a 2018 national survey, 26.6% of adults in China (50.5% of men and 2.1% of women) were current smokers. On the other hand, the prevalence of exposure to environmental tobacco smoke (ETS), also known as secondhand smoke (SHS), is approximately 68.1%.9 In the absence of effective smoking restrictions, SHS exposure can be as prevalent, or even more widespread, than active smoking.

Cigarette smoking endangers not only smokers themselves, but also those exposed to their smoke. Studies have shown that the side-stream smoke, the primary source of SHS, contains higher concentrations of toxic elements than mainstream smoke, which is mainly inhaled by passive smokers.10 Furthermore, even low levels of mainstream and side-stream smoke extracts can induce DNA damage in vivo.11 Consistent with this, SHS has been identified as a risk factor for several smoking-related cancers,12 including lung cancer13–16 and head and neck cancer.17–19

The question of whether SHS influences liver cancer risk has long received attention. Early evidence pointed to no association: a 2016 meta-analysis of two cohort and four case–control studies gave a pooled relative risk (RR) of 0.84 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.63, 1.12).20 Since then, three Chinese investigations have reported overall positive findings with summarized RR of 1.37 (95% CI: 1.08, 1.73),21 among which a Shanghai cohort reported RR of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.47, 1.70).12 Whether these discrepancies reflect population-specific factors—most notably the high prevalence of chronic HBV infection—remains unclear. Yet the total number of liver cancer cases in these studies remains modest (<100 in most single reports), limiting the efficiency to control for key confounders. Therefore, there is still an urgent need to clarify the extent to which SHS contributes to liver cancer risk, particularly in the Chinese population.

SHS contains carcinogens, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and N-nitrosamines, that undergo hepatic metabolic activation via cytochrome P450 enzymes to form DNA adducts.22 HBV-induced chronic hepatic inflammation and oxidative stress create a microenvironment that amplifies the carcinogenic effects of exogenous chemicals.23 Similarly, alcohol causes hepatic damage through CYP2E1-mediated lipid peroxidation and inflammatory pathways.24 In previous analyses, we reported positive associations for tobacco smoking (odds ratio [OR] = 1.43), HBV infection (OR = 9.94), and family history of liver cancer (OR = 4.32) in this population.25,26 We hypothesized that pre-existing liver damage from HBV infection or alcohol use may amplify the carcinogenic potential of SHS, leading to synergistic rather than merely additive effects on liver cancer risk. This biological plausibility underpins our examination of effect modification by HBV status, alcohol consumption, and other established risk factors, using both stratified analyses to assess heterogeneity and interaction analyses on additive and multiplicative scales to quantify joint effects.

We conducted a large, population-based case–control study in Jiangsu Province, China, including 2011 incident liver cancer cases (902 nonsmokers) and 7933 population-based controls (4085 nonsmokers) to investigate whether SHS exposure increases the risk for liver cancer. We also tested whether SHS acts synergistically with these established risk factors to further increase liver cancer incidence.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a population-based case–control study to explore risk factors for four major cancers including lung, stomach, liver, and esophagus in four counties (Dafeng, Ganyu, Chuzhou, and Tongshan) of Jiangsu Province, China, between 2003 and 2010. These cancers represent the leading causes of cancer related deaths in this eastern coastal province. The incidence and mortality rates of liver cancer in Jiangsu were 25.3 and 23.4 per 100,000, respectively, in 2015.27

Subjects

Details of the study design and participant recruitment have been described previously.28 In brief, incident primary liver cancer cases were identified through four county-level cancer registries from 2003 to 2010. Eligibility for cases included (1) age 18 years or older; (2) residence in the county for at least 5 years; (3) clinical or pathological diagnosis of primary liver cancer within 12 months before study enrollment.

Population-based controls were individually matched to cases by sex and age (±5 years) and were randomly selected from local household registries. In addition to liver cancer, cases and controls for esophageal, stomach, and lung cancers were enrolled. For the present analysis, all controls from the four parallel cancer studies were pooled to increase statistical power.

Participants with any previous cancer diagnosis were excluded. A total of 2011 incident liver cancer cases and 7933 controls were included in the current analyses. Of the eligible participants approached for the study, the response rates were between 30% and 40% among liver cancer cases during the study period, and greater than 85% among controls throughout the study period.

Ethics, consent, and permissions

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jiangsu Provincial Health Department and the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before enrollment.

Data collection

Epidemiological data were collected by a standardized questionnaire administered through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained interviewers. The questionnaire gathered information on sociodemographic characteristics, alcohol consumption, tobacco smoking, dietary habits, and other lifestyle factors.

Information on exposure to SHS was also collected. The participants were asked: “Do any of your family members or people living with you (parents, relatives, spouse, boarders, etc.) smoke?” and “At your workplace, are you exposed to other people’s smoking for over 15 minutes daily?” Participants who reported no SHS exposure at home or at work were classified as having no SHS exposure, while those who reported SHS exposure in either setting were classified as exposed to SHS.

For active smoking, the participants were asked whether they had ever smoked during their lifetime. Individuals who reported regular smoking with a cumulative consumption of at least 100 cigarettes were classified as ever smokers; others were considered never smokers. For theose who had smoked, additional details were collected on age at smoking initiation, age at cessation, duration of smoking, and average number of cigarettes smoked per day.

For alcohol consumption, participants first indicated lifelong drinking frequency (never, occasional, regular, or daily); never drinkers skipped the remaining items, while drinkers reported age at initiation, current drinking status, any history of quitting with corresponding ages and duration of abstinence, and decade-by-decade (1960s–1990s) plus past-year consumption of seven alcoholic beverages (Chinese liquor [baijiu] of high (≥38% alcohol by volume) and low (<38% alcohol by volume) alcohol content, beer, home-brewed barley wine, rice wine, grape wine, fruit/sparkling wine, and any other type) in bottles or grams per week. Finally, they noted whether drinking habits had changed during the last 12 months and the reason (health, economic, or other). These data were used to calculate ethanol intake (g) and to classify participants as never, former, or current drinkers.

As individual-level dietary aflatoxin intake was not measured, we used participants’ responses to the question “In your observation, is there any possibility of mildew in stored food at home?” as a proxy for aflatoxin exposure, with responses categorized as yes or no.

Blood sample collection and serological measurements

Among those who completed the interview and were included in the current analyses, 7745 (78%) participants, including 1216 (61%) cases and 6529 (82%) controls, provided blood samples for this study. Participants from different study sites had varying response rates for blood collection: 90% from Tongshan, 79% from Dafeng, 68% from Ganyu, and 67% from Chuzhou had their blood sample drawn and stored. Blood sample collection rates also differed significantly by sex, age, and family income per capita 10 years ago (P < 0.05).

Between 6 and 8 mL of blood was collected from participants who provided consent by trained personnel. The samples were centrifuged, and sera were stored at −20 °C. Serum hepatitis B virus surface antigen (HBsAg) and hepatitis C virus antibody (HCV-Ab) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Shanghai Kehua Diagnostic Medical Products Co., Ltd., China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Each 96-well plate included triple-negative controls and one positive control provided in the kits. Sensitivity and specificity of the HBsAg testing were 100% and 95.4%, respectively.29

Statistical analyses

Chi-square tests were used to compare the distribution of categorical variables. Unconditional logistic regression models were applied to estimate the crude and adjusted ORs and their 95% CIs to evaluate liver cancer risk across different exposure levels. The following variables were included as potential confounders: study site, sex, age, education level, marital status, family income per capita 10 years ago, body mass index, pack-years of smoking, weekly alcohol intake in the 1990s, history of raw water drinking (proxy for possible exposure to microcystins), HBsAg status, HCV-Ab status, and family history of liver cancer.

To more thoroughly control for confounding by active smoking, we examined the association between SHS and liver cancer not only in the total study population but also separately among never smokers. Stratified analyses were also prespecified across study region, sex, age (stratified at 65 years), marital status, education, family income, body mass index, smoking, alcohol use, and family history of liver cancer. To further examine interaction effects, relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion due to interaction (AP), and synergy index (S) with 95% CIs were calculated on the additive scale, while the ratio of the odds ratios (ROR) was calculated on the multiplicative scale.30 Let ORij represent the odds ratio for exposure category i, j. OR11 denotes the OR for participants jointly exposed to both factors (e.g., exposure to ETS and HBV positive), OR10 and OR01 denote the ORs for participants exposed to only ETS or only HBV, respectively, and OR00 (the reference) is set to 1, then:

RERI = OR11 – OR10 – OR01 +1,

AP = RERI/OR11, and

S = [OR11 – 1]/[(OR10 – 1) + (OR01 – 1)].30

On the additive scale, sub-additive interaction was indicated when the 95% CI of RERI was entirely below 0, or the 95% CI of AP was entirely below 0, or the 95% CI of S was entirely below 1, suggesting that the combined effect of two exposures was significantly less than the sum of their individual effects; conversely, super-additive interaction was indicated when the 95% CI of RERI was entirely above 0, or the 95% CI of AP was entirely above 0, or the 95% CI of S was entirely above 1. On the multiplicative scale, sub-multiplicative interaction was indicated when the 95% CI of ROR was entirely below 1, suggesting that the combined effect was significantly less than the product of the individual effects, while super-multiplicative interaction was indicated when the 95% CI of ROR was entirely above 1.30

A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.3 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC)

The mean age was 58.7 years (standard deviation: 12.2) for liver cancer cases and 63.9 years (standard deviation: 11.4) for controls. Males account for 76.3% of the cases and 71.9% of the controls. HBsAg positivity was observed in 43.2% of the cases and 6.5% of the controls. Eleven cases and 53 controls tested positive for HCV antibody. Ever alcohol consumption was reported by 56.0% of the cases and 46.4% of the controls, while ever tobacco smoking was reported by 51.7% of the cases and 46.5% of the controls. In addition, 13.7% of the cases and 3.1% of the controls reported a family history of liver cancer. Overall, 45.9% of liver cancer cases and 37.6% of the controls reported lifetime exposure to SHS (P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Ever exposure to SHS was positively associated with liver cancer (OR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.56, 2.18) after controlling for major confounders. When stratified by sex, the associations were similar for males (OR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.51, 2.25) and females (OR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.34, 2.47) (Table 2). SHS exposure at home only (OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.62, 2.33) and exposure both at home and at work (OR = 2.19, 95% CI: 1.61, 2.98) were each associated with higher odds of liver cancer compared with no SHS exposure, whereas SHS exposure only at work (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 0.83, 1.68) did not show a clear association. Furthermore, strong positive associations were observed between SHS and liver cancer among nonsmokers (OR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.67, 2.65), nonsmokers and nondrinkers (OR = 2.51, 95% CI: 1.89, 3.33), and HBsAg-negative nonsmokers and nondrinkers (OR = 2.63, 95% CI: 1.89, 3.65). These associations were consistent for males and females, with a similar magnitude of risk (Table 2). Given that only 64 participants were HCV-positive, no further stratified analysis by HCV was performed.

The potential interactions between SHS and major liver cancer risk factors were further examined on both the additive scale and multiplicative scale (Table 3). First, for the interaction between SHS and HBsAg status, SHS showed super-additive (RERI = 12.56, 95% CI: 6.60, 18.53; AP = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.45, 0.71; S = 2.54, 95% CI: 1.81, 3.55) and super-multiplicative (ROR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.07, 2.23) interactions among all participants. Even stronger interactions were observed among nonsmokers. Second, SHS demonstrated sub-multiplicative interaction with alcohol consumption among all participants (ROR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.41, 0.79). Among nonsmokers, SHS showed both sub-additive (RERI = −1.42, 95% CI: −2.65, −0.19]; S = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.32, 0.95) and sub-multiplicative (ROR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.26, 0.66) interactions with alcohol consumption. Third, among smokers, SHS exhibited sub-additive (RERI = −0.85, 95% CI: −1.50, −0.21; AP = −0.39, 95% CI: −0.71, −0.07; S = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.40, 0.85) and sub-multiplicative (ROR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.40, 0.75) interactions with active smoking. Lastly, no obvious interaction was detected between SHS and family history of liver cancer.

Discussion

This large, population-based case–control study, leveraging comprehensively collected epidemiologic data, identified a positive association between SHS exposure and liver cancer risk. This association was most pronounced among never smokers and was further amplified by HBV infection in the Chinese population.

Abundant evidence links SHS to lung cancer,7,13–16,31 with suggestive but limited data for pharynx and larynx,17–19,32 esophageal,33 and cervical cancers;34 while its association with breast cancer remains controversial.7,35–38 For liver cancer, one early meta-analysis reported a null association (0.84, 95% CI: 0.63, 1.12).20 Later, two Chinese case–control studies reported positive findings, while one Shanghai cohort reported an RR of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.47, 1.70).21 We found that exposure to SHS was associated with approximately a two-fold higher risk of liver cancer after adjustment for major confounders in overall analysis as well as among nonsmokers. Our study adds new evidence for liver cancer, demonstrating an independent association between SHS exposure and liver cancer, particularly among nonsmokers and HBV-negative subjects without tobacco or alcohol use. Moreover, our findings of super-additive and super-multiplicative interactions between SHS and HBV suggest that HBV-infected individuals may face an even higher risk of developing liver cancer when also exposed to SHS.

The components, concentration, and health hazards of SHS have been well studied. During cigarette smoking, the side-stream smoke released from the burning end mixes with exhaled mainstream smoke, together forming SHS that affects both active smokers and bystanders. The International Agency for Research on Cancer has reviewed the carcinogenic components in both smoke types.7 Side-stream smoke contains multiple group 1 carcinogens, including benzene, cadmium, 2-aminoaphthalene, nickel, chromium, arsenic and 4-aminobiphenyl, as well as numerous group 2A and 2B carcinogens. Early studies reported that most of these components occur at higher concentration in side-stream smoke than in mainstream smoke.13,39 These may partly explain why the magnitude of association between SHS and liver cancer observed in our study was greater in nonsmokers than the association between active smoking and liver cancer (OR = 1.43) found in the same population.25

Although most of our analyses focused on ever versus never exposure to SHS, we also examined exposure locations, at home and at work. Consistent positive associations were observed for exposure at home and both at home and work, but not for exposure only at work. This was plausible because most participants were farmers and workplace exposure might have been diluted in open-air environment. Therefore, the lack of association of SHS at work and liver cancer risk may not be generalizable to other occupational settings. In contrast, exposure to SHS at home was the primary source of exposure in the population and showed a significant positive association with liver cancer. Similar findings, showing a stronger cancer risk associated with SHS exposure at home than at the workplace, have been reported in previous studies.13,15

Given the high prevalence of HBV infection and alcohol consumption in the Chinese population, as well as potential early-life exposure to mildew-contaminated food in this study area, it is crucial to control for these variables as confounding factors when assessing liver cancer risk.40 Leveraging the study’s relatively large sample size and comprehensive questionnaire that captured detailed information on multiple risk factors, we examined the association between SHS and liver cancer using multivariable regression models with rigorous adjustment for these confounders. Simultaneously, recognizing that these same factors may also modify the effect of SHS, we conducted stratified analyses and interaction testing. We consistently observed positive associations between SHS and liver cancer. For example, we observed heterogeneity by study sites in the SHS–liver cancer association, plausibly reflecting regional differences in background incidence and SHS exposure intensity. Importantly, this positive association remained consistent across all four sites in both HBV-positive and HBV-negative subgroups, supporting the robustness of the finding regardless of regional HBV prevalence or other contextual factors. Furthermore, our interaction analyses revealed that HBV infection significantly modifies the effect of SHS on liver cancer risk, mostly detecting a strong super-additive interaction between SHS and HBV infection among nonsmokers (RERI = 12.87). This provides the first population-based evidence that viral infection and SHS-derived carcinogens act synergistically to increase the absolute burden of liver cancer. By contrast, we observed sub-additive interactions between SHS and alcohol drinking and between SHS and active smoking, possibly reflecting competing risks or healthy-survivor bias in the high-exposure strata, rather than any genuine biological resistance to multiple carcinogens. All these observations warrant further investigation into the underlying biological mechanisms.

There are several limitations in this study. First, as a population-based case–control study, cases were identified through the cancer registry, where delays in reporting and recruitment were inevitable. Given the rapid progression of liver cancer, selection bias is a particular concern if patients with better clinical conditions were more likely to participate, while those with poorer status were less likely to complete the interview. If clinical severity correlates with both nonparticipation and ETS exposure history, then selection bias could either exaggerate or dilute the true association. Specifically, if less severe cases (who are more likely to respond) have systematically different ETS exposure patterns compared with severe cases, and if the most severe cases represent a high-exposure subgroup, our estimates might be biased toward the null. Conversely, if responsive patients are more likely to be from households with smoking restrictions (hence lower ETS exposure) while nonresponding severe cases have higher exposure, the observed association could be biased away from the null. Nevertheless, the low response proportion remains a limitation, and our findings should be interpreted cautiously as they may not be applicable to the most severe, rapidly fatal cases who were unable to participate. Second, it is a challenge to measure SHS exposure based on self-reported recall, as smoke concentration varies considerably with space and ventilation. Owing to space constraints on the questionnaire, we were unable to capture more granular quantitative data on SHS exposure intensity or duration. Like SHS, both alcohol consumption and aflatoxin exposure were ascertained by self-report. Alcohol intake may therefore be underestimated because of social-desirability bias, while aflatoxin was approximated by reporting visibly moldy grain stored at home instead of individual biomarkers. These misclassifications of confounders leave room for residual confounding that could modestly inflate the observed SHS association. The potential measurement error might have an impact on the observed association. However, it is most likely that the distortion of the association might be toward the null. Third, while the association between ETS exposure and liver cancer observed in the overall study population remained consistent with findings among never smokers, we caution that effect estimation among ever smokers is inherently problematic. The strong correlation between active and passive smoking histories creates substantial overlap in exposure profiles, making it difficult to disentangle the independent contribution of ETS from that of personal smoking habits. Consequently, residual confounding by active smoking likely persists despite statistical adjustment. Therefore, we consider the stratified analysis among never smokers—and particularly, the stratum further restricted to never-drinkers without HBV infection (i.e., those free of the three major liver-cancer risk factors)—as the most valid basis for causal inference regarding ETS. In this triple-negative subgroup, where competing carcinogenic exposures are minimized, the observed association between ETS exposure and liver-cancer risk likely reflects a more unconfounded relationship.

Conclusions

The high prevalence of cigarette smoking in China results in widespread exposure to SHS. Our findings demonstrate a positive association between SHS and liver cancer, particularly among never smokers and individuals infected with HBV. These results underscore the need for further research to support these associations and highlight the importance of strengthened interventions to reduce tobacco exposure, not only in public spaces but also within households, for effective liver cancer prevention.
