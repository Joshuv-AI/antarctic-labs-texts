Modularity theorems for abelian surfaces
Toby Gee

Abstract
This is a brief account of my results with George Boxer, Frank Calegari and Vincent Pilloni on the (potential) modularity of abelian surfaces.

Introduction

This article gives an overview of the proofs of the main results of
[
7
,
8
]
.
I have attempted to complement the introductions to those papers, and to concentrate on aspects of the proofs that are not already covered in other survey articles.
Consequently, I devote little space to innovations in the Taylor–Wiles method (in particular, the Calegari–Geraghty method), for which I refer the reader to Calegari’s excellent surveys
[
16
,
15
]
.

We begin by recalling the main theorems of the papers. Our first paper
[
7
]
proves the Hasse–Weil conjecture for abelian surfaces (and genus 2 curves) over totally real fields. To recall what this means, let
X
be a smooth, projective variety over a number field
F
with good reduction outside a finite set of primes
S
.
Associated to
X
, one may write down a global Hasse–Weil zeta function:

\zeta_{X}(s)=\prod\frac{1}{1-N(x)^{-s}},

where the product runs over all the closed points
x
of some (any) smooth proper integral model
\mathcal{X}/\mathcal{O}_{F}[1/S]
for
X
.
(Different choices of
S
only change
\zeta_{X}(s)
by a finite number of Euler factors. For curves and abelian varieties there is a natural definition of the Euler factors at all places, and our modularity results are compatible with these factors, but we ignore this point from now on.)
The function
\zeta_{X}(s)
is absolutely convergent for
\mathrm{Re}(s)>1+\dim X
. We have the following:

Conjecture 1.1
(Hasse–Weil Conjecture, cf.
[
43
]
, in particular Conj. C9).

The function
\zeta_{X}(s)
extends to a meromorphic function of
\mathbf{C}
. There exists a positive real number
A\in\mathbf{R}^{>0}
, non-zero rational functions
P_{v}(T)
for
v|S
, and infinite Gamma factors
\Gamma_{v}(s)
for
v|\infty
such that:

\xi(s)=\zeta_{X}(s)\cdot A^{s/2}\cdot\prod_{v|\infty}\Gamma_{v}(s)\cdot\prod_{v|S}P_{v}(N(v)^{-s})

satisfies the functional equation
\xi(s)=w\cdot\xi(\dim X+1-s)
with
w=\pm 1
.

(In Serre’s formulation of the conjecture, the Gamma factors are also given explicitly in terms of the Archimedean Hodge structures of
X
.)

If
F=\mathbf{Q}
and
X
is a point, then
\zeta_{X}(s)
is the Riemann zeta function,
and Conjecture
1.1
follows from Riemann’s functional equation.
By work of Hecke and Brauer, the conjecture is known if
X
is zero-dimensional, or if more generally
the Galois representations associated to the
l
-adic
cohomology of
X
are
potentially
abelian (e.g. an abelian
variety with CM).
All subsequent progress on Conjecture
1.1
has been via the Langlands program.
Write
\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}
for the absolute Galois group of
\mathbf{Q}
; more generally, for any field
K
, we write
\overline{K}
for a separable closure, and
\mathrm{Gal}_{K}\coloneqq\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{K}/K)
for the absolute Galois group.
If
K
is a local field, then we write
I_{K}
for the inertia subgroup of
\mathrm{Gal}_{K}
.
Via the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula, one writes for each prime
\ell
(at least up to a finite number of Euler factors)

\zeta_{X}(s)=\prod_{n=0}^{2\dim X}L(H^{n}(X_{\overline{F}},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}),s)^{(-1)^{n}}

where
L(H^{n}(X_{\overline{F}},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}),s)
is the
L
-function of the
\ell
-adic representation
H^{n}(X_{\overline{F}},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell})
of
\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}
.
The Langlands conjectures predict that each
L(H^{n}(X_{\overline{F}},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}),s)
is a product of automorphic
L
-functions; more precisely, we have
L(H^{n}(X_{\overline{F}},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}),s)=\prod_{j}L(\pi_{j},s)
, where each
\pi_{j}
is an automorphic representation of
\operatorname{GL}_{n_{j}}(F)
for some integers
n_{j}
with
\sum_{j}n_{j}=\dim_{\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}}H^{n}(X_{\overline{F}},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell})
. Since (completed) automorphic
L
-functions have meromorphic (usually holomorphic) continuations and functional equations, this prediction implies the Hasse–Weil conjecture.

Definition 1.2
.

If
X/F
is a curve or an abelian variety, we write
L(X,s)\coloneqq L(H^{1}(X_{\overline{F}},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}),s)
, and we say that
X
is
modular
if
L(X,s)
is a product of automorphic
L
-functions. We say that
X
is
potentially modular
if there is a finite extension
F^{\prime}/F
such that
X_{F^{\prime}}
is modular.
Similarly, we say that a representation
\rho:\mathrm{Gal}_{F}\to\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell})
is modular if
L(\rho,s)
is a product of automorphic
L
-functions, and we say that a representation
\overline{\rho}:\mathrm{Gal}_{F}\to\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{l})
is modular if it is the reduction modulo
\ell
of a modular Galois representation.

Remark 1.3
.

The notion of potential modularity was introduced by Taylor
[
46
]
, who observed that Brauer’s methods apply in this setting, so that if
X
is potentially modular, then
L(X,s)
has the expected meromorphic continuation and functional equations.
However one cannot in general say anything about the poles (or lack thereof) of this meromorphic continuation.

If
X
is a curve of genus zero, then (up to bad Euler factors)
\zeta_{X}(s)=\zeta_{F}(s)\zeta_{F}(s-1)
, and Conjecture
1.1
follows immediately. The fundamental work of Wiles
[
49
,
47
]
and the subsequent
work of Breuil, Conrad, Diamond, and Taylor
[
20
,
12
]
proved Conjecture
1.1
for curves
X/\mathbf{Q}
of genus one,
since if we write
E=\operatorname{Jac}(X)
, then
\zeta_{X}(s)=\zeta(s)\zeta(s-1)/L(E,s)
so the modularity of
E
implies the holomorphy and functional
equation for
L(E,s)
. More generally, Taylor’s potential modularity results
[
46
]
prove Conjecture
1.1
for curves
X/F
of genus one over any totally real field.

The methods used in these papers have been vastly generalized over the past 30 years
due to the enormous efforts of many people, and as a consequence one knows for example that if
F
is totally real and
X
is such that the Hodge numbers
h^{p,q}=\dim H^{p,q}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)=\dim H^{q}(X,\Omega^{p})
of
X
are at most
1
for all
p
and
q
with
p+q=n
, then
L(H^{n}(X_{\overline{F}},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}),s)
has the expected meromorphic continuation and functional equation (see
[
31
, Cor. B]
).

Unfortunately, this multiplicity one condition on Hodge numbers is fundamental to the original Taylor–Wiles method, and there is a paucity of natural geometric examples satisfying this condition. In particular, it fails for curves of genus
g>1
and for abelian varieties of dimension
g>1
, where
h^{1,0}=h^{0,1}=g
.
The main theorem of
[
7
]
is the following.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
X
be either a genus two curve or an abelian surface over a totally real field
F
. Then
X
is potentially modular, and Conjecture
1.1

holds for
X
.

(The deduction of Conjecture
1.1
from potential modularity is straightforward in this case, using that the cohomology of
X
is given by the wedge powers of the cohomology in degree
1
, and known Langlands functoriality results for wedge powers.)

While Theorem
1.4
resolves the Hasse–Weil conjecture for abelian surfaces
A/\mathbf{Q}
, for many purposes (e.g. applications to the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture) one wishes to know modularity.

Modularity is known in some cases, using the results recalled above (in particular, the modularity of elliptic curves).
More precisely, by
[
8
, Thm. 10.2.1]
, it is known unless
A/\mathbf{Q}
is “challenging” in the sense of
[
7
, §9.2]
, which means that either

1.

\mathrm{End}(A_{\overline{\mathbf{Q}}})=\mathbf{Z}
, or

2.

there exists a quadratic field
K/\mathbf{Q}
so that
\mathrm{End}(A)=\mathbf{Z}
but
\mathrm{End}(A_{K})\otimes\mathbf{Q}
is either
\mathbf{Q}\oplus\mathbf{Q}
or a real quadratic field.

A natural source of abelian surfaces of type
2
are those of the form
{\operatorname{Res}}_{K/\mathbf{Q}}(E)
for a non-CM elliptic curve
E
which is not isogenous to its
\mathrm{Gal}(K/\mathbf{Q})
-conjugate. In this case the modularity of
A
would follow from the modularity of
E
.
If
K/\mathbf{Q}
is real quadratic, then
E
is modular by Freitas–Le Hung–Siksek
[
23
]
, while if
K
is imaginary quadratic, then the modularity of
E
is known in many cases by work of Caraiani–Newton
[
18
]
.
On the other hand, the endomorphism algebra
\mathrm{End}(A_{K})\otimes\mathbf{Q}
could also be a real quadratic field
E
rather than
\mathbf{Q}\times\mathbf{Q}
, in which case
A/K
will be a simple abelian surface of
\operatorname{GL}_{2}
-type, and the modularity of such abelian surfaces remains open in general even for real quadratic fields
K
.

In view of this, we concentrate from now on the “typical” case that
\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathbf{Q}}}(A)=\mathbf{Z}
, where one has the more precise expectation that
L(A,s)=L(\pi,s)
for some cuspidal automorphic representation
\pi
of
\operatorname{GSp}_{4}/\mathbf{Q}
, and Brumer and Kramer
[
13
]
formulated the
paramodular conjecture
, which gives a precise prescription for the “optimal” level structure for an automorphic form corresponding to a given abelian surface; in particular, this in principle reduces the
conjecture for a given
A
to an explicit computation of a (finite-dimensional) space of Siegel modular forms. Using the Faltings–Serre method, and elaborate explicit computations of low weight Siegel modular forms,
developed in part by Poor and
Yuen
[
36
,
35
,
11
]
, the modularity of (finitely many, up
to twist) abelian surfaces
A
with
\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathbf{Q}}}(A)=\mathbf{Z}
was established in the papers
[
36
,
14
,
3
]
.

If
A/F
is an abelian surface, we write
\rho_{A,p}
for the Galois representation associated to
H^{1}(A_{\overline{F}},\mathbf{Z}_{p})
, which we often think of as a representation

\rho_{A,p}:\mathrm{Gal}_{F}\to\operatorname{GSp}_{4}(\mathbf{Q}_{p})

with multiplier given by the inverse cyclotomic character
\varepsilon^{-1}
.
By definition,
A
is modular if and only if
\rho_{A,p}
is modular for some
p
(equivalently, for all
p
).
We also let
\overline{\rho}_{A,p}
denote the Galois
representation associated to
H^{1}(A_{\overline{F}},\mathbf{F}_{p})
. If
A
admits a principal polarization
of degree prime to
p
, then we can and do think of
\overline{\rho}_{A,p}
as a representation

\overline{\rho}_{A,p}:\mathrm{Gal}_{F}\to\operatorname{GSp}_{4}(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{p}).

The main theorem of
[
8
]
is as follows.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
A/\mathbf{Q}
be an abelian surface with a polarization of degree prime to
3
. Suppose
the following hold:

1.

The mod
3
representation

\overline{\rho}_{A,3}:\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}\rightarrow\operatorname{GSp}_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{3})

is surjective.

2.

The representation
\overline{\rho}_{A,3}|_{\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{2}}}}
is unramified, and the characteristic polynomial
of
\overline{\rho}_{A,3}(\mathop{\mathrm{Frob}}\nolimits_{2})
is not
(x^{2}\pm x+2)^{2}
.

3.

A
has good ordinary reduction at
3
, and
the characteristic polynomial
of
\mathop{\mathrm{Frob}}\nolimits_{3}
does not have repeated roots.

Then
A
is modular.

More precisely, there exists a cuspidal automorphic representation
\pi

of
\operatorname{GL}_{4}/\mathbf{Q}
(the transfer of a cuspidal
automorphic representation of
\operatorname{GSp}_{4}/\mathbf{Q}
of weight
2
)
such that
L(s,H^{1}(A))=L(s,\pi)
. Consequently,
L(s,H^{1}(A))
has a holomorphic continuation to
\mathbf{C}
and satisfies the expected functional equation.

Remark 1.6
.

We claim that Theorem
1.5
applies to a positive proportion of abelian surfaces over
\mathbf{Q}
, counted in any reasonable sense.
As one justification of this, suppose that one samples genus two curves

X:y^{2}+h(x)y=f(x)

with
h(x),f(x)\in\mathbf{Z}[x]
of degrees
\leq 3
and
\leq 6
in any way in which the distributions modulo
2
and
3
are equidistributed, and considers those curves
X
with the following properties:

1.

\overline{\rho}_{\operatorname{Jac}{X},3}:\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}\rightarrow\operatorname{GSp}_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{3})

is surjective,

2.

X
has good reduction at
2
,

3.

X
has good ordinary reduction at
3
,

4.

\overline{\rho}_{\operatorname{Jac}(X),3}(\mathop{\mathrm{Frob}}\nolimits_{2})
does not have characteristic polynomial
(x^{2}\pm x+2)^{2}
,

5.

The characteristic polynomial of
\mathop{\mathrm{Frob}}\nolimits_{3}
has distinct eigenvalues.

Theorem
1.5
proves the modularity of
\operatorname{Jac}(X)
for any such
X
, and one can check
[
8
, §10.1]
that these
X
form a subset of density

\frac{5551}{46656}=0.1189\ldots
Another point of comparison is with the curves in the database
[
48
]
(see also
[
6
]
). This contains
63107
genus two curves
X/\mathbf{Q}
with
\mathrm{End}\operatorname{Jac}(X)_{\overline{\mathbf{Q}}}=\mathbf{Z}
, and Theorem
1.5
applies to
11384
of them.

2
The 2–3 switch

The proof of Theorem
1.5
follows Wiles’s strategy for proving the modularity of semistable elliptic curves, and in particular, we make use of an analogue of
the
3
-
5
switch used by Wiles
[
49
]

to prove residual modularity. That switch exploited the rationality of certain twists of the modular curve
X(5)/\mathbf{Q}
. In our case, we use a rational moduli
space of abelian surfaces to carry out a
2
-
3
switch.

In outline, the
2
-
3
switch proving Theorem
1.5
divides into three steps as follows.

1.

Show that
\overline{\rho}_{B,2}
is modular for many abelian surfaces
B/\mathbf{Q}
. (See Lemma
2.3
.)

2.

Show that for any abelian surface
A
as in Theorem
1.5
, there exists
B
as in (
1
) with
\overline{\rho}_{B,3}\cong\overline{\rho}_{A,3}
. (See Lemma
2.4
.)

3.

Prove the following (imprecisely stated) modularity lifting theorem, which applies in particular to the representations
\rho_{B,2}
and
\rho_{A,3}
for
A,B
as in the previous two steps:

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that
\rho:\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}\to\operatorname{GSp}_{4}({\overline{\mathbf{Z}}_{p}})
is unramified at all but finitely many primes and de Rham at
p
, and:

(a)

\overline{\rho}:\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}\to\operatorname{GSp}_{4}(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{p})
is modular.

(b)

\overline{\rho}(\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}})
is large.

(c)

\rho
is pure.

(d)

\rho|_{\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}}
is ordinary,
p
-distinguished, and has Hodge–Tate weights
0,0,1,1
.

Then
\rho
is modular.

Proof 1
(Proof of Theorem
1.5
, given these steps).

Suppose that
A
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
1.5
, and let
B
be as in
2
.
Then
\overline{\rho}_{B,2}
is modular by
1
, so that
\rho_{B,2}
is modular by Theorem
2.1
.
Equivalently,
\rho_{B,3}
is modular, so that
\overline{\rho}_{B,3}
is modular.
Since
\overline{\rho}_{A,3}\cong\overline{\rho}_{B,3}
by assumption, we can apply Theorem
2.1
to deduce that
\rho_{A,3}
is modular, as required.

Remark 2.2
.

Hypothesis
3b
of Theorem
2.1
is responsible for assumption
1
in Theorem
1.5
, while the more serious hypothesis
3d
corresponds to assumption
3
there (and is also responsible for
2
).

Our supply of abelian surfaces
B/\mathbf{Q}
for
1
will be certain Jacobians
B=\operatorname{Jac}(X)
, where
X/\mathbf{Q}
is a genus two curve. Let
r_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,6
be the Weierstrass points of
X
over
\overline{\mathbf{Q}}
; then the non-zero elements of
B[2]
are given by the
r_{i}-r_{j}
for
i<j
.
Considering the action of
\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}
on the
r_{i}
, one has an identification

S_{6}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\operatorname{Sp}_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{2})=\operatorname{GSp}_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{2})
. There are two conjugacy classes of subgroup
S_{5}\subset S_{6}
; we denote by
S_{5}(b)
the standard copy of
S_{5}
in
S_{6}
(and below we write
A_{5}(b)
for the copy
of
A_{5}
in
S_{5}(b)
).
Thus
X
has a rational Weierstrass point (so that
X
can be written in the form
y^{2}=f(x)
with
f
quintic) if and only if
\overline{\rho}_{A,2}
factors through a conjugate of
S_{5}(b)
.

The following lemma, which exploits some coincidences in the representation theory of
A_{5}
, allows us to find many
X
for which we know that
\overline{\rho}_{B,2}
is modular.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
X/\mathbf{Q}
is a genus two curve with a rational Weierstrass point, and that
B\coloneqq\operatorname{Jac}(X)
has semistable ordinary or good ordinary reduction at
2
.
Suppose also that

\overline{\rho}_{B,2}:\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}\rightarrow\operatorname{GSp}_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{2})\simeq S_{6}

has image
S_{5}(b)
, and that the image of complex conjugation has conjugacy class
(**)(**)
.
Then
\overline{\rho}_{B,2}
is modular, arising from an ordinary weight 3 Siegel modular form.

Proof 2
.

If
F^{+}
is the quadratic field given by the kernel of the composite
\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}\to S_{5}(b)\to\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z}
, then
\overline{\rho}(\mathrm{Gal}_{F^{+}})=A_{5}(b)
, and
F^{+}
is real by the assumption on complex conjugation.
Let

\overline{\varrho}:\mathrm{Gal}_{F^{+}}\rightarrow\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{4})\simeq A_{5}

denote the residual
2
-dimensional Galois representation associated to
this
A_{5}
-extension. (There are two such representations which are
permuted by the outer automorphism; choose either.) Either by an easy Brauer character
computation, or as a consequence of the Steinberg tensor product
theorem for
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{4})
, we have

\overline{\rho}_{B,2}\cong\mathrm{Sym}^{3}\overline{\varrho}.

By a theorem of Tate
the composite
\overline{\varrho}:\mathrm{Gal}_{F^{+}}\rightarrow A_{5}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})

lifts to an odd representation

\varrho:\mathrm{Gal}_{F^{+}}\to\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})

with finite image (which will be some central extension of
A_{5}
).
By the odd Artin conjecture for
\operatorname{GL}_{2}
(i.e. by the main results
of
[
34
]
or
[
40
]
),
\varrho
is modular. By Hida theory, it follows that
\overline{\varrho}
is modular, coming from an ordinary Hilbert modular eigenform of parallel weight
2
.
By symmetric cube functoriality
[
27
]
,

\overline{\rho}_{B,2}|_{\mathrm{Gal}_{F^{+}}}
is modular, arising from an ordinary weight 3 Hilbert–Siegel modular form.
By solvable base change and a standard use of the Khare–Wintenberger method
[
26
]
,
\overline{\rho}_{B,2}
itself is modular, arising from an ordinary weight 3 Siegel modular form, as required.

2
is provided by the following lemma (see
[
8
, Lem. 9.4.2]
).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A/\mathbf{Q}
be an abelian surface with a polarization of degree prime to
3
. Assume that
\overline{\rho}_{A,3}|_{{\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}_{2}}}}
is unramified, and
the characteristic polynomial
of
\overline{\rho}_{A,3}(\mathop{\mathrm{Frob}}\nolimits_{2})
is not
(x^{2}\pm x+2)^{2}
.

Then there exists a genus two curve
X/\mathbf{Q}
with a rational Weierstrass point,
with
B=\mathrm{Jac}(X)
having the following properties:

1.

\overline{\rho}_{B,3}\cong\overline{\rho}_{A,3}
.

2.

B
has good ordinary or semistable ordinary reduction at
2
,
and is
2
-distinguished.

3.

B
has good ordinary reduction at
3
.

4.

\mathrm{End}(B_{\overline{\mathbf{Q}}})=\mathbf{Z}
.

5.

The representation

\overline{\rho}_{B,2}:\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}\rightarrow\operatorname{GSp}_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{2})

has image
S_{5}(b)
, and the image of complex conjugation has
conjugacy class
({*}{*})({*}{*})
.

Proof 3
.

We consider the moduli space
M(\overline{\rho}_{A,3})/\mathbf{Q}
of genus-two curves
X
equipped with a fixed rational Weierstrass point and a symplectic isomorphism
\overline{\rho}_{\operatorname{Jac}(X),3}\cong\overline{\rho}_{A,3}
.
By definition, any
\mathbf{Q}
-point of
M(\overline{\rho}_{A,3})
gives
B=\operatorname{Jac}(X)
satisfying (
1
), and
\overline{\rho}_{B,2}(\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}})\subseteq S_{5}(b)
(due to the rational point).

By
[
7
, Lem. 10.2.4]
, the variety
M(\overline{\rho}_{A,3})/\mathbf{Q}
is rational. (The proof of this lemma is very similar to that of
[
45
, Lem. 1.1]
: namely, a Galois cohomology argument reduces to checking that the corresponding moduli space for the representation
1\oplus 1\oplus\overline{\varepsilon}^{-1}\oplus\overline{\varepsilon}^{-1}
is
\operatorname{PGL}_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{3})
-equivariantly rational over
\mathbf{Q}
, which is known by
[
5
, Theorem 0.0.1]
.)
In particular, by weak approximation (combined with Hilbert irreducibility as in
[
44
, §3.4, §3.5]
), for any finite set of places
S
and any nonempty open subsets
\Omega_{\ell}\subset M(\overline{\rho}_{A,3})(\mathbf{Q}_{l})
(in the
\ell
-adic topology) for each
\ell\in S
, we can find a
\mathbf{Q}
-point of
M(\overline{\rho}_{A,3})
lying in
\Omega_{\ell}
for each
\ell\in S
, for which
\overline{\rho}_{B,2}(\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}})=S_{5}(b)
(which in turn implies that
\mathrm{End}(B_{\overline{\mathbf{Q}}})=\mathbf{Z}
).
We then take
S=\{2,3,\infty\}
; the hypothesis that the characteristic polynomial
of
\overline{\rho}_{A,3}(\mathop{\mathrm{Frob}}\nolimits_{2})
is not
(x^{2}\pm x+2)^{2}
is used to guarantee the existence of suitable points over
\mathbf{Q}_{2}
, by writing down appropriate abelian surfaces over
\mathbf{Q}_{2}
for each of the other possibilities for this characteristic polynomial.

For Theorem
1.4
, we use a variant of the above strategy, where we choose primes
p,q
splitting completely in our totally real field
F
, with the further properties that
A
admits a polarization of degree prime to

, the representations
\overline{\rho}_{A,p}
and
\overline{\rho}_{A,q}
have large image, and
A
has good ordinary reduction at all places dividing

.
Then we have:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A/F
be a challenging abelian
surface over a totally real field. Then there is a finite Galois extension of
totally real fields
F^{\prime}/F
such that
p
and
q
split completely in
F^{\prime}
, and an abelian surface
B/F^{\prime}
with good ordinary reduction at all places dividing

, such that

\overline{\rho}_{B,p}\cong\overline{\rho}_{A,p}|_{\mathrm{Gal}_{F^{\prime}}}
, while
\overline{\rho}_{B,q}
is modular of parallel weight
2
, and has large image.

Proof 4
.

We consider the moduli space
Y
of abelian surfaces
B
equipped with isomorphisms
B[p]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}A[p]
and
B[q]\cong\overline{\rho}_{q}
, for any choice of
\overline{\rho}_{q}:\mathrm{Gal}_{F}\to\operatorname{GSp}_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{q})
. There is no reason that

should be nonempty, but a theorem of Moret-Bailly
[

guarantees that we can find

as above so that

is nonempty, and satisfies the required local conditions at primes dividing

Choosing
\overline{\rho}_{q}
to be induced from a 2-dimensional representation, we are able to arrange (using the (known) potential modularity of elliptic curves) that after a further extension of

is modular.

Remark 2.6
.

As well as the (important!) difference between modularity and potential modularity, another significant difference between the Lemma
2.5
and Lemmas
2.3
and
2.4
is that Lemma
2.5
shows residual potential modularity in weight
2
, while Lemma
2.3
shows residual modularity in weight
3
. (which is regular).

The Galois representations associated to Siegel modular eigenforms of weight
k\geq 2
have Hodge–Tate weights
0,k-2,k-1,2k-3
(see Section
3.1
below for the definition of Hodge–Tate weights), while the Galois representations associated to abelian surfaces have Hodge–Tate weights
0,0,1,1
.
It follows that if an abelian surface is modular, corresponding to a Siegel modular form, that modular form must be of weight
2
; so when we have residual modularity in weight
3
, we have to “change weight” in some way (see Remark
2.7
below for more discussion of this).
On the other hand, if
k>2
then the Hodge–Tate weights

are pairwise distinct, and we say that
k
is a “regular weight”, while
k=2
is an “irregular weight”.
The irregular weight cases behave quite differently (and are in general much more complicated than the regular weight cases) on both the automorphic and Galois sides of the Langlands correspondences, as we will see below.

The remainder of this survey is devoted to
3
(and its analogue in
[
7
]
). This step divides into two parts. Firstly, we use the Taylor–Wiles method to show that
\rho
is
p
-adically modular, in the sense that it contributes to a Hida family of Siegel modular forms. This is relatively standard, although the need to consider the primes
p=2,3
causes some pain. Secondly, we prove a classicality criterion for weight
2
ordinary Siegel
p
-adic modular
forms. It is in this step that the difference mentioned in Remark
2.6
becomes significant. In
[
7
]
, the relevant classicality theorem is a “small slope implies classical” theorem in the style of Coleman, but this approach does not suffice in the situation considered in
[
8
]
. In this case the proof, building upon work of Rodríguez Camargo
[
38
]
, is a generalisation from
\operatorname{GL}_{2}
to
\operatorname{GSp}_{4}
of a part of Lue Pan’s remarkable work
[
30
]
.
In the remainder of this survey, we concentrate on the classicality theorems, before briefly returning to the Taylor–Wiles method in Section
5
.

.

It was well-known for many years that Theorem

3
Galois representations

3.1
Sen theory

Let
{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})\coloneqq\cup_{n}{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{n}})
be the cyclotomic extension of
{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}
.
Then
\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})}
is the kernel of the cyclotomic character
\varepsilon:\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}\to\mathbf{Z}_{p}^{\times}
.
By the Ax–Sen–Tate theorem, we have
\mathbf{C}_{p}^{\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})}}=\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})}
, the completion of
{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})
.
Let
V/{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}
be a finite-dimensional
{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}
-vector space equipped with a continuous action of
\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}
, so that
\rho:\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
is a Galois representation.
Then
V\otimes_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}\mathbf{C}_{p}
has a semilinear action of
\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}
, and Sen showed that it descends to
\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})}
, in the sense that

(V\otimes_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}\mathbf{C}_{p})^{\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})}}\otimes_{\widehat{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})}}\mathbf{C}_{p}\cong V\otimes_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}\mathbf{C}_{p}.

Furthermore, it descends to
{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})
: there is a unique
\mathrm{Gal}({\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})/{\mathbf{Q}_{p}})
-stable
{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})
-subspace
\mathbf{D}_{\operatorname{Sen}}(V)
of
V\otimes_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}\mathbf{C}_{p}
such that

\mathbf{D}_{\operatorname{Sen}}(V)\otimes_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})}\mathbf{C}_{p}\cong V\otimes_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}\mathbf{C}_{p}.

In fact,
\mathbf{D}_{\operatorname{Sen}}(V)
is the union of the finite-dimensional
\mathrm{Gal}({\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})/{\mathbf{Q}_{p}})
-stable
{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})
-subspaces of
V\otimes_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}\mathbf{C}_{p}
.

The
{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})
-vector space
\mathbf{D}_{\operatorname{Sen}}(V)
has an action of
\mathrm{Gal}({\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})/{\mathbf{Q}_{p}})
, and thus a linear action of
{\operatorname{Lie}\,}(\mathrm{Gal}({\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})/{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}))
.
More explicitly, we have the Sen operator
\Theta_{V}
, which is the
{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})
-linear map given by
\Theta_{V}\coloneqq\log(\gamma)/\log_{p}(\varepsilon(\gamma))

for any
\gamma\in\mathrm{Gal}({\mathbf{Q}_{p}}(\zeta_{p^{\infty}})/{\mathbf{Q}_{p}})
sufficiently close to
1
.

By definition,
V
is Hodge–Tate, with Hodge–Tate weights
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}\in\mathbf{Z}
, if there exists an isomorphism of
\mathbf{C}_{p}[\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}]
-modules

V\otimes_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}\mathbf{C}_{p}\cong\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{C}_{p}(-h_{i}),

where
\mathbf{C}_{p}(n)
is the
n
th Tate twist; thus
V
is Hodge–Tate if and only if the Sen operator
\Theta_{V}
is semisimple and has eigenvalues in
\mathbf{Z}
, in which case the Hodge–Tate weights are the negatives of the eigenvalues of
\Theta_{V}
.

3.2
Ordinary Galois representations

There are various definitions in the literature of ordinary Galois representations; the following will be convenient for us.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that
\rho:\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
is ordinary if there are integers
h_{1}\leq h_{2}\dots\leq h_{n}
such that
\rho
is conjugate to an upper-triangular representation

\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{1}&*&\dots&*\\
0&\chi_{2}&\dots&*\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dots&\chi_{n}\end{pmatrix}

where each
\chi_{i}:\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}\to\mathbf{Q}_{p}^{\times}
is a character with
\chi_{i}|_{I_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}}=\varepsilon^{-h_{i}}
.

It is straightforward to check that an ordinary Galois representation is de Rham if and only if it is Hodge–Tate, i.e. if and only if the Sen operator
\Theta_{V}
is semisimple.
If
h_{1}<\dots<h_{n}
, this is automatic (e.g. because the eigenvalues of the Sen operator are distinct), but in general it need not hold.
Indeed, a standard example of a representation which is not Hodge–Tate but whose Sen operator has integral eigenvalues is the ordinary representation

\begin{pmatrix}1&\log_{p}\varepsilon\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

which by definition has Sen operator
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
.

4
p
-adic modular forms and classicality

We now review the approach to
p
-adic modular forms and classicality theorems taken in
[
8
]
, which relies on the work of Pan
[
30
]
and its generalisations to other Shimura varieties by Rodríguez Camargo
[
38
]
, as well as the higher Coleman and Hida theories of Boxer–Pilloni
[
9
,
10
]
.
There is a long history of such classicality theorems, going back in the case of Coleman theory to
[
19
]
; we highlight in particular Kassaei’s paper
[
24
]
, and its generalisation in
[
4
]
.
Another important ingredient is the families of
p
-adic automorphic forms introduced by Andreatta–Iovita–Pilloni
[
1
]
(see
[
2
]
for a survey).

For simplicity we say almost nothing below about compactifications of Shimura varieties, or about the distinction between cuspidal and usual cohomology.
These both play an important role in the foundations of the theory, but disappear in our main results, as we always localise at a non-Eisenstein maximal ideal of a Hecke algebra.
Similarly, we will sometimes elide the difference between functors on the abelian and derived level where it makes no difference for our final statements.
We will also only work with
p
-adic modular forms of integral weight, as this is all that is needed for our main theorems and allows us to simplify the exposition in places. We say very little about families of
p
-adically varying weight, although these are an important ingredient in the proofs of some results that we state.

We will also be extremely informal in our treatment of
p
-adic functional analysis and condensed mathematics. A justification for this is that at the time of writing, the “correct” foundations for the constructions we discuss are not yet available. In particular, the arguments below use a
p
-adic version of Beilinson–Bernstein localisation; ideally, this should be defined in the framework of the analytic de Rham stack of Rodríguez Camargo
[
39
]
, similarly to Scholze’s
treatment of classical Beilinson–Bernstein localisation in
[
42
]
. This formalism is expected to be available soon, but in the meantime
[
8
]
proceeds in a somewhat ad hoc fashion.

While the only Shimura varieties considered here are the Siegel threefolds associated to the group
\operatorname{GSp}_{4}
, many of the results that we explain below are proved in
[
8
]
for Hodge-type Shimura varieties, or (in the case of results requiring a non-Eisenstein localisation) are expected to hold in this generality. Accordingly, where possible we phrase our results without reference to specific features of
\operatorname{GSp}_{4}
, although we caution the reader that
they should turn to
[
8
]
to see the precise hypotheses under which each result is proved.

Accordingly, from now on we write

; when we eventually need to be concrete, we will realise

as the subgroup of

acting on the free

where

We let

We

The sets of

.
We let

.

We let

, and write

4.1
Modular forms and the Hodge–Tate period map

We fix throughout a tame level
K^{p}\subset\operatorname{GSp}(\mathbf{A}^{\infty,p})
(which we ultimately choose in order to guarantee that various spaces of
p
-adic modular forms are 1-dimensional, using the theory of newforms developed in
[
37
]
), and for each open compact subgroup
K_{p}\subset\operatorname{GSp}_{4}({\mathbf{Q}_{p}})
, we let
\mathrm{Sh}_{K_{p}}/\mathbf{C}_{p}
be the analytic adic space attached to (a toroidal compactification of) the Siegel Shimura variety of level
K_{p}K^{p}
.

For each
\kappa\in X^{*}(T)^{M,+}
and each finite level Shimura variety
\mathrm{Sh}_{K_{p}}
, we have the usual sheaf
\omega^{\kappa}
of modular forms of weight
\kappa
on
\mathrm{Sh}_{K_{p}}
.
The coherent cohomology
\mathrm{R}\Gamma(\mathrm{Sh}_{K_{p}},\omega^{\kappa})
has an action of a Hecke algebra
\mathbf{T}
(generated by the Hecke operators at the places where
K^{p}
is hyperspecial), and the corresponding eigenclasses can be computed in terms of automorphic forms on
G
. More precisely, cuspidal automorphic representations contribute according to their Archimedean components
\pi_{\infty}
, and the essentially tempered
\pi_{\infty}
which contribute to coherent components are the so-called
non-degenerate limits of discrete series. The upshot for us is that in order to prove that a Galois representation is modular, it suffices to show that its corresponding system of Hecke eigenvalues contributes to some
\mathrm{R}\Gamma(\mathrm{Sh}_{K_{p}},\omega^{\kappa})
.

We let
\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty}=\lim_{K_{p}}\mathrm{Sh}_{K_{p}}
, which is a perfectoid space with an action of
\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}
,
admitting a
\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}
-equivariant Hodge–Tate period map

\pi_{\operatorname{HT}}:\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty}\to\mathcal{FL},

where
\mathcal{FL}
is the base change to
\mathbf{C}_{p}
of the (partial) flag variety
P\backslash G
. This was introduced by Scholze
[
41
]
, and has revolutionised the study of
p
-adic modular forms.
As a first illustration of this, let
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}
be the
G
-equivariant sheaf on
\mathcal{FL}
whose fibre at
e
is the inflation from
M
to
P
of the irreducible representation of
M
of highest weight
\kappa
, and we set
\omega^{\kappa,\mathrm{sm}}\coloneqq(\pi_{\operatorname{HT}}^{*}\mathcal{L}_{\kappa})^{\mathrm{sm}}
.
By the definition of
\pi_{\operatorname{HT}}
, one finds that the sheaf
\omega^{\kappa,\mathrm{sm}}
descends to
\omega^{\kappa}
on each
\mathrm{Sh}_{K_{p}}
. Thus
\mathrm{R}\Gamma(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{\kappa,\mathrm{sm}})
is a complex of smooth admissible
G(\mathbf{Q}_{p})
-representations, equal to
\operatorname{colim}_{K_{p}}\mathrm{R}\Gamma(\mathrm{Sh}_{K_{p}},\omega^{\kappa})
.
This suggests the possibility of proving results about modular forms by working on the flag variety
\mathcal{FL}
, an idea exploited to great effect by Scholze in
[
41
]
.

Returning to the problem of the modularity of Galois representations, a basic difficulty now presents itself: there is no Galois action on
\mathrm{R}\Gamma(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{\kappa,\mathrm{sm}})
, thus no direct connection to Galois representations.
This difficulty was resolved in the case of the modular curve by Pan
[
30
]
, who invented geometric Sen theory and combined it with Scholze’s ideas to prove remarkable new “
p
-adic Eichler–Shimura” results, relating the
\mathrm{R}\Gamma(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{\kappa,\mathrm{sm}})
to étale cohomology groups, which naturally have an action of
\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}
.
Furthermore, as we will see below, Pan’s theory often allows one to reduce to questions on the flag variety, and thus to explicit computations.

In fact, the spaces of classical modular forms
\mathrm{R}\Gamma(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{\kappa,\mathrm{sm}})
do not directly show up in
p
-adic Eichler–Shimura theory; rather, one sees spaces of
p
-adic modular forms which are defined using the flag variety, and are closely related to the higher Coleman theory of Boxer–Pilloni
[
9
]
.
From our point of view, this is a feature rather than a bug: it is these spaces of
p
-adic modular forms to which we can apply the Taylor–Wiles method in order to prove our modularity lifting theorems, and we then consider separately the problem of proving the classicality of a
p
-adic modular form.
This classicality problem is solved by a generalisation of Pan’s ideas, in combination with results of Boxer–Pilloni.

Theorem 4.1

,

We write

.

For each

, we can consider the composite of
\operatorname{Loc}
with restriction to

{\mathcal{O}}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{b})_{\mathrm{alg}}

(\mathfrak{g},B)
-action.
This stabiliser admits an explicit description as follows.
The action of
(\mathfrak{g},B)

Write

It is then elementary to check that

(P_{w}\cap B)_{e}\backslash(P_{w,e}\times B_{e})\rtimes(P_{w}\cap B).

The corresponding

(4.6)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{b})_{\mathrm{alg}}}

For any

E_{1}^{p,q}=\oplus_{w\in{}^{M}W,\ell(w)=p}H^{p+q}(HC_{w}(\mathbf{C}_{p}\otimes^{L}_{\mathfrak{u}_{\mathfrak{p}_{w}}}M))

4.5
The ordinary part

Let
T^{+}({\mathbf{Q}_{p}})\coloneqq\{t\in T({\mathbf{Q}_{p}}),\ \forall\alpha\in\Phi^{+},\ v_{p}(\alpha(t))\geq 0\}

Given a smooth representation
V
of
B({\mathbf{Q}_{p}})
, there is a Hecke action of
T^{+}({\mathbf{Q}_{p}})
on
V^{U({\mathbf{Z}_{p}})}
; as usual, the action of
t\in T^{+}({\mathbf{Q}_{p}})
is defined via the (normalised) trace
V^{tU({\mathbf{Z}_{p}})t^{-1}}\to V^{U({\mathbf{Z}_{p}})}

acts invertibly.
For any

, we say that the slopes of

(respectively, are equal to

The spaces

for a precise statement). The following key slope bound was proved by Boxer–Pilloni (it is essentially
[
10

It is proved by a careful analysis of integral models of Hecke correspondences (and in particular, it is not deduced from a statement on the flag variety).
We will see below that it immediately implies classicality theorems and
p
-adic Eichler–Shimura decompositions for the ordinary part of the cohomology (and more generally for the “small slope” part, although we do not discuss that here).

Theorem 4.3

Let

be a module generated by a highest weight vector of weight

Then the slopes of

are at least

.

We fix from now on a character

such that the slopes of

are at least

-\lambda

Write

]
,
the input to our modularity lifting theorem is a class in
H^{0}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(2,2;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
, i.e. an ordinary Siegel modular form of weight
2
(see Remark
2.6
). Accordingly in that paper we work with
\mathrm{R}\Gamma_{\mathrm{id},Q}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(2,2;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
, which has cohomology in degrees
0,1
, and by Theorem
4.5
agrees with the classical ordinary cohomology
\mathrm{R}\Gamma(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(2,2;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
.

However in
[
8
]
our input is a class in weight
3
, rather than weight
2
.
Using Hida theory, we can produce a congruence to a class in
H^{0}_{\mathrm{id}}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(2,2;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
, and ultimately the modularity lifting machinery produces another class in the same cohomology group. We then need to understand when such a class extends to
H^{0}_{\mathrm{id},Q}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(2,2;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
.
We now briefly explain how we do this; in fact for technical reasons we found it more convenient in
[
8
]
to study the problem of extending from
H^{2}_{{{}^{3}w}}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
to
H^{2}_{{{}^{3}w},Q}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
, so we do the same here.

From now on we work on
C_{{}^{3}w,Q}\coloneqq C_{{}^{3}w}\cup C_{{}^{2}w}
.
We have a short exact sequence of sheaves over
\pi_{HT}^{-1}(C_{{}^{3}w,Q})
, corresponding to the stratification of
C_{{}^{3}w,Q}
into
B
-orbits, with
j_{{}^{3}w,\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty}}:\pi_{HT}^{-1}(C_{{}^{3}w})\hookrightarrow\pi_{HT}^{-1}(C_{{}^{3}w,Q})
:

(4.14)

0\rightarrow(j_{{}^{3}w,\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty}})_{!}\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}}|_{\pi_{HT}^{-1}(C_{{}^{3}w})}\rightarrow\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}}|_{\pi_{HT}^{-1}(C_{{}^{3}w,Q})}\rightarrow\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}}|_{\pi_{HT}^{-1}(C_{{}^{2}w})}\rightarrow 0

Thus
\mathrm{R}\Gamma_{{{}^{3}w},Q}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
is computed by the following complex in degrees
2,3
, where
Cous
is induced by the class of the extension (
4.14
):

(4.15)

[H^{2}_{{}^{2}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}\xrightarrow{\text{Cous}}H^{3}_{{}^{3}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}].

On the other hand, as noted just below (
4.12
), the Sen operator
\Theta
on
H^{3}(\mathop{\mathrm{RHom}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{g}}\bigl(M_{\lambda},\mathcal{O}^{\mathrm{la}}_{\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty}}))_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
acts by
\langle\mu,\kappa_{{}^{2}w}\rangle=\langle\mu,\kappa_{{}^{3}w}\rangle=0
on each of
H^{2}_{{}^{2}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
and
H^{3}_{{}^{3}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
, so it induces a map:

(4.16)

H^{2}_{{}^{2}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}\xrightarrow{\text{Sen}}H^{3}_{{}^{3}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}.

The key result is the following theorem, showing that “Sen equals Cousin”.
It is proved by an explicit calculation on the flag variety
\mathcal{FL}
.

Theorem 4.8
.

The two maps

\mathrm{Cous},\mathrm{Sen}:H^{2}_{{}^{2}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}\to H^{3}_{{}^{3}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}

agree up to a non-zero scalar.

Combining Theorem
4.8
and (
4.15
), we see that a class
c\in H^{2}_{{}^{2}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
extends to
H^{2}_{{{}^{3}w},Q}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
(equivalently, to
H^{2}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
) if and only if
\operatorname{Sen}(c)=0
.
It only remains to relate this condition to the Sen operator on the Galois representation associated to a
p
-adic modular form.
We do this using the Eichler–Shimura relations; more precisely, we use the following lemma, which is deduced from results of Nekovář,

[
29
]
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
f\in H^{0}_{{}^{0}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(2,2;2),\mathrm{sm}})^{\mathrm{ord}}
be an ordinary overconvergent modular eigenform with corresponding Galois representation

\rho_{f}:\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}\rightarrow\mathrm{GSp}_{4}({\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{p}})
. Let
\mathfrak{m}_{f}
be the corresponding maximal ideal of the Hecke algebra
\mathbf{T}[1/p]
.
We assume that
\overline{\rho}_{f}
is irreducible and the Zariski closure of
\rho_{f}(\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}})
contains
\mathrm{Sp}_{4}
. Then
H^{3}(\mathrm{RHom}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\lambda,\mathrm{R}\Gamma(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},{\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{p}})^{\mathrm{la}}))^{\mathrm{ord}}[\mathfrak{m}_{f}]=\rho_{f}\otimes_{{\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{p}}}W
for some finite-dimensional
vector space
W\neq 0
.

Finally, we deduce from this the following classicality theorem.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
f\in H^{0}_{{}^{0}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{K^{p}},\omega^{(2,2;2),\mathrm{sm}})^{\mathrm{ord}}
be an ordinary overconvergent modular eigenform with Galois representation

\rho_{f}:\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}\rightarrow\mathrm{GSp}_{4}({\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{p}})
. Let
\mathfrak{m}_{f}

be the corresponding maximal ideal. We assume that:

1.

the Zariski closure of
\rho_{f}(\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}})
contains
\mathrm{Sp}_{4}
.

2.

The representation
\rho_{f}|_{\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}
is de Rham.

3.

We have
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbf{C}_{p}}H^{i}_{{}^{i}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(2,2;2),\mathrm{sm}})^{\mathrm{ord}}[\mathfrak{m}_{f}]=1
for
i=0,1
and
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbf{C}_{p}}H^{i}_{{}^{i}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})^{\mathrm{ord}}[\mathfrak{m}_{f}]=1
for
i=2,3
.

4.

The representation
\overline{\rho}_{f}
is irreducible.

Then
f
is a classical Siegel modular form.

Proof 6
.

Assumption
3
guarantees that the passage to the
\mathfrak{m}_{f}
torsion is exact, so that we have induced maps

(4.17)

\mathrm{Cous},\mathrm{Sen}:H^{2}_{{}^{2}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})^{\mathrm{ord}}[\mathfrak{m}_{f}]\to H^{3}_{{}^{3}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})^{\mathrm{ord}}[\mathfrak{m}_{f}].

These two maps agree by Theorem
4.8
.
Since
\rho_{f}|_{\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}
is de Rham, the corresponding Sen operator is semisimple (see Section
3.2
), which by Lemma
4.9
implies that the maps (
4.17
) vanish.
As noted above, this implies that
H^{2}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(1,1;2),\mathrm{sm}})^{\mathrm{ord}}[\mathfrak{m}_{f}]\neq 0
, which by Arthur’s multiplicity formula implies that
H^{0}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(2,2;2),\mathrm{sm}})^{\mathrm{ord}}[\mathfrak{m}_{f}]\neq 0
, whence it is a one-dimensional
\mathbf{C}_{p}
-vector space spanned by
f
, as required.

5
Modularity lifting

Finally, we very briefly explain the use of modularity lifting theorems in our two papers.
In
[
8
]
, we needed to prove Theorem
2.1
, which we deduce from Theorem
4.10
. We work with the ordinary higher Coleman families
\mathrm{R}\Gamma_{{}^{i}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{\kappa,\mathrm{sm}})^{\mathrm{ord}}
, or rather their integral versions, the higher Hida theories of
[
10
]
.
Since for each
i
, the complex
\mathrm{R}\Gamma_{{}^{i}w}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{\kappa,\mathrm{sm}})^{\mathrm{ord}}
has cohomology only in degree
i
, a standard application of the usual Taylor–Wiles method is able to prove modularity lifting theorems for each of these higher Hida theories.
(In practice we find it useful to work with Hida families in which the weight varies, for example in proving local-global compatibility, but this is again standard.)
Furthermore, following Diamond
[
21
]
, the Taylor–Wiles method is able to prove multiplicity one theorems for spaces of modular forms, and we use it to verify hypothesis
3
in Theorem
4.10
.
(It is here that we use the hypothesis of
p
-distinguishedness in assumption
3d
of Theorem
2.1
.)
The only significant difficulty that we have to overcome in applying the Taylor–Wiles method is that we have to consider residual Galois representations with rather small image, namely
A_{5}
(when
p=2
) and
\operatorname{GSp}_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{3})
(when
p=3
).
This causes us considerable pain, but does not involve any significant innovations.

As we explained above, in our earlier paper
[
7
]
we worked with the complexes
\mathrm{R}\Gamma_{\mathrm{id},Q}(\mathrm{Sh}_{\infty},\omega^{(2,2;2),\mathrm{sm}})_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathrm{ord}}
, which have cohomology in degrees
0,1
.
Again, there is an integral version, the higher Hida theory introduced by Pilloni in
[
32
]
.
Since there is cohomology in multiple degrees, the usual Taylor–Wiles method does not work in this case, and instead we use the version of the Taylor–Wiles method introduced by Calegari–Geraghty
[
17
]
.
Our main difficulty now is that rather than working over
\mathbf{Q}
, we are over an arbitrary totally real field
F
of degree
d
, say, and the analogous complexes (constructed from the cohomology of Hilbert–Siegel Shimura varieties) now have cohomology in degrees
0,\dots,d
;
but we do not know how to prove the local-global compatibility results needed for the Calegari–Geraghty method unless
d=1
. We get around this difficulty by working only with primes
p
which split completely in
F
, and for each
v|p
, considering complexes which at
v
behave like
\mathrm{R}\Gamma_{\mathrm{id},Q}
, and at the other places above
p
behave like
\mathrm{R}\Gamma_{\mathrm{id}}
.
We are able to compare these complexes, and we show that an appropriately compatible set of cohomology classes combine to give a classical cohomology class.

6
Future directions

It is hopefully clear that there is considerable scope for improving on Theorem
1.5
.
Firstly, by working with Hilbert–Siegel Shimura varieties, it should be possible to extend Theorem
1.5
to abelian surfaces over arbitrary totally real fields.
By making a solvable base change to a totally real field with suitable behaviour at the primes above
2
, such an improvement would allow us to remove Hypothesis
2
in that theorem, which arose due to the interaction between the local conditions at
2
and
3
in Lemma
2.4
. Secondly, our theorems should not require the full strength of the ordinarity condition, but only a suitable “small slope” condition, which would allow us to relax Hypothesis
3
in Theorem
1.5
.
Finally, many of the arguments in
[
8
]
are written for more general Shimura data, and it would be interesting to prove classicality theorems beyond the case of
\operatorname{GSp}_{4}
.
We intend to pursue all of these ideas in future sequels to
[
8
]
.
Finally, one other possible route to the modularity of abelian surfaces would be to deduce it from an appropriate version of Serre’s conjecture for
\operatorname{GSp}_{4}
. Such a deduction would be analogous to Khare’s theorem
[
25
]
, which showed that Serre’s conjecture implies the Artin conjecture for odd
2
-dimensional representations of
\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}
. With this in mind, we use our theorems to prove the following implication (see
[
8
, Lem. 10.4.1]
).

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose that for every residual representation:

\overline{\rho}:\mathrm{Gal}_{\mathbf{Q}}\rightarrow\operatorname{GSp}_{4}(\mathbf{F}_{p})

satisfying the following conditions:

1.

\overline{\rho}
has multiplier
\overline{\varepsilon}^{-1}
,

2.

\overline{\rho}
is absolutely irreducible, and

3.

the semi-simplification of
\overline{\rho}|_{\mathrm{Gal}_{{\mathbf{Q}_{p}}}}
is a direct sum of characters,

there exists an ordinary classical Siegel modular form
f
for
\operatorname{GSp}_{4}/\mathbf{Q}
of weight at least
3
such that

\overline{\rho}_{f,p}\cong\overline{\rho}.

Then all abelian surfaces
A/\mathbf{Q}
are modular.
