Fueling failure or fostering fusion? A critical review of the potential effect of ketogenic, intermittent fasting, and low-carbohydrate diets on dental implant osseointegration
Authors: Faris A Alshahrani, Noha Taymour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo synthesize current evidence (2010–2025) on ketogenic, intermittent fasting, and low-calorie diets' effects on bone health, inflammation, osseointegration and identifying research gaps relevant to dental implantology. A systematic literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for human and animal studies, mechanistic work, and systematic reviews. Evidence was extracted and summarized, prioritizing studies on diet patterns, bone biology, and dental implant outcomes. Mechanistic and animal studies suggest dietary influence on bone remodeling, inflammation, and angiogenesis, crucial for osseointegration. Vitamin D level correlates with implant outcomes, and moderate intermittent fasting appears not to harm systemic bone markers short-term. Major gaps include peri-implant mechanistic data, comparative trials, and optimal dietary timing. Current evidence indicates a potential influence of different diets on dental implant success factors. Definitive dietary recommendations for implant patients are premature due to insufficient human clinical data. Introduction

Dental implant therapy has revolutionized modern dentistry, with survival rates exceeding 90% in most clinical settings. Successful dental implant outcomes are multifactorial, relying on a complex interplay of factors including surgical technique, implant design, surface modifications biomaterials and oral hygiene (Taymour et al. 2022, 2024). While technological advancements have significantly improved implant survival rates, systemic nutrition remains a silent determinant of clinical implant success (Mahendra et al. 2025). Nutrition is emerging as a key modifiable factor that can profoundly modulate the biological processes affecting implant success, particularly osseointegration and long-term stability (Tobias et al. 2025).

The biological cascade of osseointegration, involving inflammation, angiogenesis, osteogenesis, and remodeling. (Chandwani et al. 2025) This complex process places high demands on the host's metabolic reserves (Abu Alfaraj et al. 2023). Consequently, the role of general nutrition in bone healing is well-established. Adequate protein intake is paramount for the synthesis of the collagen matrix, which provides the structural scaffolding for mineralization; indeed, protein-energy malnutrition has been directly linked to impaired bone formation and compromised mechanical fixation of titanium implants (Dayer et al. 2006, 2007). Similarly, micronutrients play indispensable roles: calcium and vitamin D are essential for mineral homeostasis and osteoblast differentiation (West et al. 2023), while magnesium acts as a cofactor for enzymes involved in bone matrix formation (Del Barrio et al. 2010). Beyond structural components, dietary patterns significantly influence systemic inflammation. Pro-inflammatory states, often exacerbated by diets high in refined sugars or deficient in antioxidants, can elevate circulating cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, potentially disrupting the delicate balance between bone resorption and formation required for successful implant integration (Zhang et al. 2025a). Thus, a nutrient-dense diet supports the physiological environment necessary for optimal wound healing and skeletal repair.

In recent years, there has been a surge in the popularity of specific dietary regimens, such as the ketogenic diet (KD), intermittent fasting (IF), and low-calorie diets (LCD), driven by their purported benefits for weight management, metabolic health, and overall well-being (Ashtary-Larky et al. 2022; Martínez-Montoro et al. 2025) Patients are increasingly adopting these diets, often without a full understanding of their systemic effects, creating a new clinical and research imperative for the dental community. (Graphical abstract) The fundamental question arises: how do these popular dietary patterns, which significantly alter metabolic pathways and nutrient intake, affect the processes of bone healing, inflammation, and osseointegration that are paramount to dental implant success?

Existing literature establishes that general malnutrition negatively impacts bone turnover; however, the specific effects of modern dietary regimens like ketogenic diet (KD), intermittent fasting (IF), and low-calorie diets (LCD) on the highly specific biological process of osseointegration remain poorly understood The ketogenic diet, characterized by a high-fat, low-carbohydrate intake, has been shown to have anti-inflammatory effects but also raises concerns about its impact on bone metabolism (Ashtary-Larky et al. 2022). Intermittent fasting, which involves cycles of eating and fasting, has demonstrated benefits in reducing inflammation and improving metabolic health, but its long-term effects on bone are still under investigation (Clayton et al. 2023). Low-calorie diets, while effective for weight loss, can lead to nutrient deficiencies and have been associated with negative effects on bone turnover and clinical healing (Liu and Rosen 2023). As patients increasingly adopt these diets, clinicians lack evidence-based guidelines to advise them during the critical peri-implant healing period.

This review aims to systematically synthesize the existing literature on the potential correlation between ketogenic, intermittent fasting, and low-calorie diets and the biological processes relevant to dental implant success. By examining the physiological and mechanistic findings related to these diet regimens, this article explores their potential to either enhance or impair implant outcomes. Furthermore, this review identifies critical translational gaps, including the absence of randomized controlled trials and long-term clinical data, and proposes practical, evidence-based recommendations for patient counseling and management. Hence, the review provides dental professionals with a comprehensive understanding of this evolving area, enabling them to offer informed guidance to patients and optimize clinical outcomes in an era of increasing dietary diversity.

Methodology

A structured search was conducted across different databases; PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (2010–2025) using Boolean operators combining diet terms (“ketogenic,” “low carbohydrate,” “intermittent fasting,” “caloric restriction”) with implantology outcomes (“osseointegration,” “bone healing,” “implant stability,” “peri-implant bone loss”). The core search concept was built around two main components: (1) dietary patterns and (2) implantology outcomes. The exact search strings used for each database were presented in supplementary Table 1 (search syntax). Grey literature was screened through ClinicalTrials.gov to identify ongoing or unpublished trials. Inclusion criteria were established to select studies that provided insights into the physiological and molecular mechanisms by which these diets could influence osseointegration. This included animal and clinical studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. Studies were included if they reported on at least one of the following outcomes: bone mineral density, markers of bone formation or resorption, inflammatory markers, or direct assessments of osseointegration. Exclusion criteria included articles not published in English, case reports, and studies focusing on diets not relevant to this review.

Results

The search strategy of recent literature is summarized in Table 1. Few studies directly examined diet and implant healing. Evidence was indirect, focusing on systemic bone health under carbohydrate restriction or fasting protocols (improved insulin sensitivity, altered bone turnover markers). Animal studies including rat and rabbit models under ketogenic or fasting regimens showed mixed effects on bone regeneration, with some studies reporting reduced bone mineral density, while others indicated enhanced anti-inflammatory responses facilitating early healing. Other Mechanistic studies highlighted pathways include altered osteoblast/osteoclast balance, modulation of RANKL/OPG, reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α), and variable angiogenic effects. Recent studies acknowledge the potential benefits of dietary modulation on peri-implant bone metabolism.

Ketogenic diet

The evidence investigating the direct impact of a ketogenic diet (KD) on dental implant osseointegration is derived primarily from animal models and presents a complex and often conflicting picture. The findings can be broadly categorized into direct investigations of osseointegration, mechanistic studies on bone metabolism, and analyses of systemic effects like inflammation and the gut microbiome.

Direct investigations into osseointegration under high-fat, ketogenic-style diets yield contradictory results, highlighting a critical dependency on dietary composition and experimental design. Studies demonstrating a negative impact are compelling. For instance, King et al. (2020) showed that a diet high in both fat and fructose significantly compromised osseointegration in rats, evidenced by reduced bone-to-implant contact (BIC) and diminished biomechanical properties. This was linked to decreased osteoblast activity and reduced peri-implant trabecular bone volume, independent of systemic glucose levels (King et al. 2020). Similarly, Xu et al. (2019) reported that both continuous KD and an Every-Other-Day KD (combining ketosis with intermittent fasting) groups showed significantly reduced bone quality, lower bone volume/total volume (BV/TV) of approximately 22% compared to the control's 35%, and altered mechanical properties in femurs compared to the control group. The EODKD group specifically demonstrated the most significant inhibition in bone marrow stromal cell (BMSC) osteogenic differentiation, with lower alkaline phosphatase activity on day 7 and reduced mineralization on day 21 compared to control and KD groups.

Conversely, Dündar et al. (2016) reported that a 12-week high-fat diet (HFD) did not impair osseointegration or decrease Bone-to-Implant Contact (BIC) and Bone Area (BA) within implant threads in a rabbit model The high-fat diet group showed BIC of 51.05% ± 11.23% and BA of 44.57% ± 11.83%, statistically similar to control values of 49.40% ± 10.74% (BIC) and 41.85% ± 10.37% (BA). Kaya et al. (2025) also determined that a high-fat diet (42% of calories from fat)did not statistically significantly affect bone-implant osseointegration in the tibias of rats (p > 0.05).

Liu et al. (2024) elucidated a clear pathway, showing that a KD induces severe bone loss in mice by impairing the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow stem cells (BMSCs) through upregulation of the mTOR pathway and downregulation of autophagy (Liu et al. 2024). On the other hand, research into specific metabolites reveals potential benefits. Liu et al. (2023) investigated alpha-ketoglutarate (α-KG), a key intermediate in the ketogenic metabolic pathway (Liu et al. 2023). In an osteoporotic mouse model, systemic α-KG supplementation was shown to enhance osseointegration by upregulating autophagic activity, improving BMMSC function, and down-regulating osteoclast differentiation. This creates a fascinating scientific paradox: while the overall state of ketosis may impair bone health, a specific metabolic byproduct of that state may be profoundly beneficial for bone regeneration.

In female subjects who experienced a 10% weight loss, KD was associated with a significant increase in bone resorption markers, such as serum C-telopeptide (CTX), coupled with a decrease in bone formation markers like procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (P1NP) (Yu et al. 2022). This biochemical shift suggests a net catabolic state for bone during active weight loss, even without a concurrent diagnosis of osteoporosis.

This concern is echoed in specific populations. A study by Heikura et al. (2019) on elite endurance athletes found that a short-term (3.5-week) ketogenic diet negatively impacted markers of bone modeling and remodeling, specifically reporting a reduction in the bone formation marker osteocalcin by approximately 25% compared to baseline. Conversely, animal studies more consistently raise concerns. Multiple rat models under KD have demonstrated lower BMD and compromised cortical and cancellous bone microarchitecture, findings not always replicated in human studies but highlighting a potential long-term risk. Kong et al. (2022) demonstrated that KD ameliorates inflammation by directly inhibiting the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, a key driver of IL-1β and IL-18 production. This systemic reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines could theoretically create a more favorable environment for the early stages of bone healing.

Intermittent Fasting (IF)

Kaya et al. (2025) investigated the effects of both intermittent fasting and a high-fat diet on osseointegration in a rat model. Their findings were reassuring, concluding that IF did not negatively affect bone-implant osseointegration. However, the oral implications are complex; while reduced meal frequency can decrease the number of acid attacks on teeth, prolonged fasting periods may lead to a more acidic oral environment due to reduced salivary flow, potentially increasing enamel erosion risk.

Clayton et al. (2023) analyzed studies on Time-Restricted Eating (TRE) and Alternate-Day Fasting (ADF). It found that when practiced for up to six months, these IF regimens generally did not adversely affect bone outcomes. Notably, in studies where participants experienced modest weight loss (defined as less than 5% of baseline body weight), IF appeared to offer a slight protective effect against bone loss compared to continuous caloric restriction. This suggests that the metabolic benefits of IF may offset the catabolic effects of mild weight loss on skeletal tissue.

The anti-inflammatory effects of IF are a consistent finding. Studies have shown IF can reduce systemic levels of key inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α (Pappe et al. 2025; Lira-Junior et al. 2024). In contrast to the conflicting data surrounding ketogenic diets, the emerging evidence on intermittent fasting (IF) presents a more cautiously optimistic, though still preliminary, picture for its effects on bone health and osseointegration. The available literature, primarily derived from animal models, suggests that IF may not impair, and could potentially enhance, the biological processes critical for implant success, particularly in compromised systemic conditions.

Kaya et al. (2025) and Yavuz et al. (2024) systematically investigated the effects of IF and a high-fat diet (HFD) on bone-implant connection (BIC) in rat tibias. Across both studies, which included groups for fasting, HFD, and a combination of both, the key finding was neither intermittent fasting nor a high-fat diet adversely affected the bone-implant connection or new bone formation in the peri-implant defects after a 12-week period. Thus, the metabolic stress of IF does not translate into a negative outcome for the structural integration of titanium implants in a healthy animal model.

The most compelling evidence for IF emerges from studies investigating its effects in pathophysiological states known to impair bone healing and implant outcomes. In a mouse model of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM), a condition that severely compromises mandibular bone regeneration, Zhang et al. (2025b) demonstrated that periodic fasting created a more favorable metabolic environment. By alleviating systemic lipid dysregulation, IF promoted the osteogenesis of implanted mesenchymal stromal cells and safeguarded mandibular bone repair. This suggests IF could be a valuable adjunctive therapy to improve implant success in diabetic patients.

Similarly, in a rat model of glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIO), Alrowaili et al. (2021) showed that IF had a corrective effect on bone loss. The intervention worked by stimulating osteoblast activity while simultaneously inhibiting osteoclastogenesis and parathyroid hormone secretion, leading to improved bone mineral content and density. This direct modulation of bone remodeling towards a net anabolic state is highly relevant to the osseointegration process, which relies on a delicate balance of these same cellular activities (Albrahim et al. 2024).

Low-Calorie Diet (LCD)/Caloric Restriction (CR)

Similar to the ketogenic diet, no direct studies were found that specifically investigate the effects of LCD or CR on dental implant osseointegration in humans or relevant animal models. The implications for implant patients are therefore inferred from the established negative effects of severe caloric deficit on bone biology.

Liu and Rosen 2023 reported that caloric restriction can suppress bone growth and increase fracture risk. A key mechanism identified is the significant increase in Bone Marrow Adipose Tissue (BMAT). The accumulation of BMAT is not merely a space-filling phenomenon; these adipocytes secrete a cocktail of factors, including RANKL, adipsin, and Pparγ, that actively inhibit osteogenic differentiation and promote osteoclastogenesis, directly compromising the bone remodeling balance essential for osseointegration.

This is supported by Rinne et al. (2024) who reported in male mice that while CR improved the endocrine function of existing bone marrow adipocytes, it was associated with a reduction in trabecular bone loss during aging in male mice. For an implant patient, however, the primary concern is not the long-term aging process but the acute need for good bone formation and mineralization in the weeks and months following surgery. A severe caloric deficit during this critical window could deprive the body of the necessary energy and substrates (protein, calcium, vitamin D) for effective bone matrix deposition and mineralization, thereby jeopardizing implant stability.

In contrast to the mixed findings for macronutrient manipulation, the evidence for severe dietary restriction, encompassing overall caloric deficit, specific protein deficiency, and critical mineral insufficiency, presents a more consistent and concerning picture for osseointegration. The literature strongly suggests that a catabolic nutritional state creates an environment hostile to the establishment and maintenance of a good bone-implant interface.

Experiments by Dayer et al. (2006, 2007) in rat models demonstrated that an isocaloric low-protein diet significantly impairs titanium implant osseointegration. This was quantified by a 29% reduction in the pull-out strength of titanium rods in the protein-restricted group compared to controls. This finding establishes a clear, negative correlation between protein intake and the mechanical fixation of implants. Critically, these studies also explored therapeutic interventions, finding that systemic treatment with parathyroid hormone (PTH) not only prevented the negative effects of protein malnutrition but also significantly improved mechanical fixation, outperforming both renutrition and bisphosphonate therapy (pamidronate).

The impact of general caloric restriction (CR) on the jawbone and surrounding tissues is complex and appears to be gender-dependent. Liu et al. (2025) provided direct evidence that CR leads to trabecular and cortical bone loss in the mandibles of female mice. The underlying mechanism involved a detrimental shift in bone marrow lineage commitment, where adipogenesis (fat cell formation) was accelerated at the expense of osteogenic differentiation.

However, CR's effects on inflammation are more nuanced. Research in non-human primates by Reynolds et al. (2009) found that long-term CR reduced inflammatory mediators and the risk of inflammatory periodontal disease in male rhesus monkeys, who exhibited less periodontal pocketing and lower levels of inflammatory markers. This gender-specific finding was supported by Parveen (2021), who noted that CR dampens the inflammatory response and reduces circulating TNF-α, but highlights the complexity of its effects across different biological systems.

Beyond macronutrients, the adequacy of micronutrients is paramount. The study by Del Barrio et al. (2010) on severe dietary magnesium deficiency illustrates this point perfectly. The research demonstrated that a 90% reduction in dietary magnesium content led to decreased systemic bone density and, critically, a reduced removal torque of already osseointegrated implants. This indicates that severe mineral deficiencies can compromise not only the initial healing phase but also the long-term stability and maintenance of the bone-implant interface.

Discussion

The biological plausibility linking popular dietary patterns to osseointegration is multifaceted. The systemic effects of ketogenic, low-carbohydrate, and intermittent fasting diets, ranging from improved insulin sensitivity and reduced systemic inflammation to potential concerns about long-term bone density, (Sbricoli et al. 2022) raise compelling questions for the field of implantology. Carbohydrate restriction demonstrably reduces systemic inflammation and improves glycemic control, factors beneficial to wound healing. However, chronic ketosis may negatively impact long-term bone mass, raising caution (Lennerz et al. 2021). Similarly, while intermittent fasting appears generally safe for bone health over moderate durations, (Garofalo et al. 2023; Longo and Mattson 2014) ketogenic and low-carbohydrate diets, especially when combined with low energy availability or in vulnerable populations, show signals of reduced bone formation or microarchitectural compromise. (Song and Kim 2023) Given that dental implant healing requires a tightly orchestrated sequence of inflammation, angiogenesis, matrix deposition, and remodeling, (Saghiri et al. 2016) any dietary pattern that disrupts these processes could impair osseointegration or marginal bone stability (Kabisch et al. 2025).

The Studies on the ketogenic diet present a complex picture regarding its effects on bone metabolism. Human studies generally report no significant short-term changes in bone mineral density (BMD) or markers of bone resorption and formation (Garofalo et al. 2023). Conversely, animal studies frequently demonstrate lower BMD and abnormal cortical and cancellous bone mass under KD, findings not consistently replicated in human cohorts (Sherman et al. 2022; Gelles et al. 2025). Concerns have been raised that KD may impair bone health and elevate osteoporosis risk by influencing the growth hormone-insulin-like growth factor 1 (GH-IGF-1) axis, acid load, and weight loss (Luo et al. 2025). For instance, a short-term KD in elite endurance athletes was shown to negatively affect markers of bone modeling and remodeling (Heikura et al. 2019) (Fig. 1).

Dual effect of ketogenic diet

The discrepancy between animal models (which often show bone loss) and human studies (which sometimes show preservation) may be attributed to species-specific metabolic rates. Rodents have a significantly higher bone turnover rate than humans; thus, the catabolic stress of ketosis may manifest more rapidly and severely in animal models than in the human skeletal system (Liu et al. 2025).

Regarding inflammation, KD has demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties. Kong et al. (2022) showed that KD can ameliorate inflammation by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Additionally, Ruskin et al. reported that KD treatment significantly reduced tactile allodynia, suggesting broader anti-inflammatory effects (Ruskin et al. 2021). From an oral health perspective, KD has been associated with benefits such as reduced plaque buildup due to lower sugar intake, which can contribute to improved gum health markers through its anti-inflammatory effects and stabilization of blood sugar levels (Taher et al. 2024; Woelber et al. 2016). Despite these insights, direct studies investigating the correlation between KD and dental implant osseointegration were still limited identified.

Intermittent fasting (IF) is recognized for its potential in weight management and metabolic health improvement, yet its impact on bone health is less investigated. Observational studies have suggested a possible link between certain IF practices, such as breakfast omission, and compromised bone health; however, these findings are often limited by confounding factors (Lewis et al. 2021; Clayton et al. 2016; Yu et al. 2023). More encouragingly, interventional studies focusing on time-restricted eating (TRE) regimens, when practiced for up to six months, generally did not adversely affect bone outcomes and, in some cases, offered a slight protective effect against bone loss during modest weight loss (less than 5% of baseline body weight). Similarly, most studies on alternate-day fasting (ADF) reported no adverse effects on bone outcomes (Clayton et al. 2023; Song and Kim 2023). However, current interventional studies are often limited by their short duration, small and diverse population samples, and a primary focus on total body bone mass, lacking detailed clinically relevant bone assessments. IF has consistently been shown to reduce systemic inflammation and may also contribute to reduced gingival inflammation (Papathanasiou et al. 2023; Martínez-García and Hernández-Lemus 2021). Kaya et al. (2025) suggested that IF did not negatively affect bone-implant osseointegration in rats. While this study represents a valuable first step, but its findings of "no negative effect" require validation in human clinical trials with relevant endpoints like implant stability quotient (ISQ) or marginal bone loss.

Caloric restriction (CR), often synonymous with low-calorie diets (LCD), has a more consistently reported negative impact on bone health, particularly when severe (Rinne et al. 2024). Liu and Rosen (2023) reported that CR can suppress bone growth and increase the risk of fracture, with the underlying mechanisms not yet fully elucidated. Key factors implicated in CR-induced bone loss include reduced mechanical loading and a notable increase in bone marrow adipose tissue (BMAT). The accumulation of BMAT, observed in both animal models and clinical conditions like anorexia nervosa, is significant because increased adipocytes in the bone marrow can secrete various cytokines and adipokines (Pparγ, adipsin, RANKL) that negatively influence the skeletal system and inhibit osteogenic differentiation. This highlights the importance of adequate calorie intake for healing processes, including those following orthopedic surgery (Briguglio et al. 2019). A balanced diet rich in calcium and vitamin D is crucial for preserving bone health (Voulgaridou et al. 2023).

While severe LCDs pose risks to bone integrity, anti-inflammatory diets, which are often inherently low in calories, can contribute to reducing systemic inflammation (Kökten et al. 2021; Khodarahmi et al. 2025). However, further direct studies specifically investigating the effects of LCDs on dental implant osseointegration are needed. The implications for bone turnover, potential nutrient deficiencies, and inflammation are highly relevant to the success of dental implants, and a healthy, nutrient-dense diet is generally recommended for optimal recovery and long-term care following implant procedures.

Adherence to strict diets poses challenges. Clinicians might need to weigh short-term peri-implant benefits (reduced inflammation, improved glycemic control) against possible long-term risks (reduced calcium intake, altered bone density). While systemic skeletal outcomes serve as a proxy for metabolic health, the localized process of osseointegration involves distinct mechanical and biological factors. By bridging nutritional science with dental implantology, the review proposes a roadmap for the integration of systemic health with dental care, evidence-based practice, and translational clinical research.

Clinical significance vs. statistical significance

For the practicing clinician, the paramount question is one of clinical significance: Do the observed biochemical changes translate into a meaningful difference in implant survival or longevity? The current literature often reports statistically significant changes in biomarkers (e.g., a 15% reduction in IL-6), but the clinical relevance of this for osseointegration remains speculative (Bhukal et al. 2025). A small, statistically significant improvement in a bone formation marker may be less clinically important than ensuring a patient has adequate protein and caloric intake to support the formation of the bone-implant interface. The ultimate clinical outcomes, implant stability, marginal bone level over 1, 5, and 10 years, and incidence of peri-implantitis, are the metrics that matter (Pérez-Pevida et al. 2025). Until research directly addresses these endpoints, any dietary recommendations remain theoretical.

Potential confounding factors

The relationship between diet and bone health outcomes is not isolated; it is embedded within a complex web of patient behaviors and biological factors that can confound observed associations. Key confounding variables include:

Physical activity

Physical activity

Exercise is a potent anabolic stimulus for bone. A patient on a caloric restriction diet who maintains a rigorous resistance training regimen may have entirely different bone outcomes than a sedentary individual on the same diet (Murphy and Koehler 2020).

Overall Micronutrient Profile

Overall Micronutrient Profile

The focus of these diets is on macronutrients (fat, carbohydrates, protein). However, the intake of critical micronutrients for bone health such as calcium, vitamin D, magnesium, and vitamin K2, can vary dramatically based on food choices within each dietary pattern. A “well-formulated” ketogenic diet rich in leafy greens and fatty fish is vastly different from one consisting of bacon and butter (West et al. 2023).

Lifestyle Factors

Lifestyle Factors

Smoking and alcohol consumption are powerful negative modifiers of bone healing and implant success. Their presence can easily mask any potential benefit of a dietary intervention (Xu et al. 2021; Alharethi 2024).

Oral Hygiene

Oral Hygiene

The effect of diet on plaque accumulation (e.g; reduced sugar on a ketogenic diet) is only one side of the coin. Meticulous oral hygiene remains the primary determinant of peri-implant health, and poor hygiene can negate any systemic anti-inflammatory benefits of a diet (Taher et al. 2024).

Effect modifiers: Who benefits most and who is at risk?

The impact of these dietary patterns is unlikely to be uniform across all patient populations. Specific characteristics can act as effect modifiers, altering the risk–benefit calculus for implant patients.

Age and gender

Older adults, particularly post-menopausal women, already face a baseline of compromised bone homeostasis due to hormonal changes and age-related sarcopenia. For these individuals, any dietary pattern that further tips the balance toward bone resorption (e.g., severe caloric restriction, a poorly planned ketogenic diet) could pose a significant risk to implant stability (Dominguez et al. 2025).

Systemic health status

Diabetes mellitus

Patients with poorly controlled diabetes have impaired wound healing and higher implant failure rates. The improved glycemic control offered by ketogenic diets and intermittent fasting could be particularly beneficial in this cohort, potentially outweighing risks to bone metabolism (Alarim et al. 2020).

Obesity

While weight loss is often desirable, rapid or severe caloric restriction in obese patients can lead to a significant catabolic state and bone loss, potentially jeopardizing implant osseointegration (Liu et al. 2025).

Medications

Patients on medications that affect bone metabolism present a complex clinical picture. Those on bisphosphonates or denosumab have suppressed bone turnover, which could potentially mask any negative dietary effects but also raises concerns about impaired healing. (Anastasilakis et al. 2018) Conversely, patients on long-term corticosteroids are already at high risk for osteoporosis, and any additional dietary stressor could be detrimental (Sawamura et al. 2017).

Limitations of existing evidence: The translational gap

The most significant limitation is the preliminary indirect clinical evidence with potential confounders. While animal models (primarily rats and rabbits) offer controlled environments to investigate mechanisms like RANKL/OPG modulation or local inflammation, their findings cannot be directly extrapolated to humans. The metabolic rate, bone remodeling dynamics, and oral microbiome of rodents differ significantly from humans. For instance, a diet that reduces bone mineral density (BMD) in a rat over 12 weeks may not have the same magnitude or even direction of effect in a human jawbone over a 4-month osseointegration period. The absence of human clinical trials investigating KD, IF, and LCD impact on dental implant outcomes represents a significant barrier to definitive clinical guidance. Consequently, considerations for the practicing clinician should be cautious until comprehensive implant-specific data becomes available." Future research should prioritize the clinical trials in implant patients even small pilot RCTs that assess implant stability, marginal bone loss, and possibly bone biomarker correlates, comparing diet intervention vs control.

Conclusion

The intersection of nutrition and dental implantology represents a promising but underexplored field. While mechanistic plausibility is high, Comprehensive clinical trials are needed to establish diet-specific protocols for dental implant patients. Dietary counseling should be individualized, balancing systemic health benefits with implant-specific concerns.

From a practical standpoint, clinicians should assess patients' nutritional status pre-implant (protein, vitamin D, calcium, metabolic health) and exercise caution when patients follow extreme diets. In the future, dental implant specific clinical trials and translational animal studies are needed to bridge the gap between mechanistic theory and clinical reality, finally answering the question of how we can use nutrition to optimize the success rate of dental implant.

The intersection of nutrition and dental implantology represents a promising but currently underexplored field. While mechanistic plausibility is highly supported by animal data showing that dietary patterns can modulate inflammation and bone turnover, there is a distinct lack of direct human evidence linking specific diets like KD, IF, or LCD to dental implant osseointegration. The available literature is characterized by a translational gap, relying heavily on systemic bone health proxies that may not fully reflect the localized environment of a healing dental implant.

Therefore, while this review synthesizes critical biological pathways, definitive dietary recommendations for implant patients remain premature. From a practical standpoint, clinicians should prioritize a general assessment of nutritional status (protein, vitamin D, calcium, and metabolic health) and exercise caution when patients follow extreme diets that may compromise bone substrate availability. Moving forward, dental implant-specific clinical trials and translational animal studies are prioritized to bridge the gap between mechanistic theory and clinical reality, finally answering the question of how we can use nutrition to optimize the long-term success of dental implants.
