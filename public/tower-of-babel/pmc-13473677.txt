Optoelectronic performance of diphenylamine-helicene derivatives as hole transport materials in perovskite solar cells: a DFT/TD-DFT study
Authors: Shima Shabani, Rahim Ghadari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAchieving high efficiency in perovskite solar cells depends critically on the design of small-molecule hole-transport materials (HTM), and leveraging theoretical design alongside in-depth structure–property analysis offers a cost-effective screening strategy. In this work, we employ DFT at the B3LYP/6-311G(d) and TD-DFT at CAM-B3LYP/6-311G(d) level to evaluate a series of helicene-based diphenylamine derivatives (the HEL family) as potential HTMs. Nine members with helicene-based structure, including unsubstituted structures as well as structures with substituents (F, Cl, Br, Methyl, Ketone, and Amine in the ortho position and Methyl and Amine in the meta position), show optimal HOMO/LUMO alignments for efficient hole extraction, while the others appear unsuitable. Their computed absorption peaks lie between 354.98 and 368.96 nm, making them compatible with n–i–p cell architectures. Among these, the structure that is containing amine functional group in meta position has the narrowest band gap (2.70 eV), the most red-shifted λmax (365.28 nm), and excited-state metrics (D = 0.93 Å, H = 4.66 Å, t = − 2.75 Å, Sr = 70) that signal excellent charge separation. Its transport and thermodynamic descriptors—net charge = 0.44 e⁻, hole contribution = 52.28%, Eb ≈ 0.46 eV, IPa = 5.34 eV, ΔGsol = − 0.59 eV, λhole = 0.298 eV, mobility parameter η = 2.35 eV—further underline its superior hole-generation ability, solubility, and low reorganization energy. Collectively, these characteristics identify that structure containing amine functional group in meta position can be regarded as a highly promising HTM for MAPbI₃-based n–i–p perovskite solar cells.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54954-7.Keywords: Perovskite solar cells, Helicene, Hole-transporting materials, DFTSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

The global energy demand is on the rise, while the finite nature of fossil fuel reserves renders them an unsustainable option. Solar energy stands out as the most plentiful energy source, possessing the capability to fulfill worldwide energy requirements at a low cost and with minimal ecological repercussions. Perovskite-based photovoltaic devices have gained popularity by offering a set of attractive features: near-theoretical limits of photoelectric conversion efficiency, tunable band gaps, strong light harvesting capability, long carrier diffusion lengths, low-cost production routes, and simple prototyping1. Beyond these advantages, ongoing research is focused on improving long-term stability and reducing lead toxicity to pave the way for commercial deployment. The versatility of perovskite materials has attracted considerable interest, attributed to their diverse compositions, ease of fabrication, and a broad spectrum of desirable characteristics2–4. Since their 2009 debut, perovskite solar cells have quickly pushed power-conversion efficiencies beyond 25 percent, positioning them at the forefront of emerging photovoltaic technologies. Research today is concentrated on improving their long-term operational stability, identifying lead-free material systems, and developing scalable production methods that can translate laboratory achievements into market-ready products5. Halide perovskites used in photovoltaic devices follow an ABX₃ configuration, where A is an organic cation (such as methylammonium or formamidinium), B is typically a divalent metal ion like Pb2⁺, and X represents a halide anion (Cl⁻, Br⁻, or I⁻). A standard perovskite solar cell is built from three successive functional layers: an electron‐transport layer at the front, the central perovskite light‐absorbing film, and a hole‐transport layer at the rear2,6,7. When sunlight hits a perovskite, it excites electrons and holes to form bound pairs (excitons). Due to the small binding energy of perovskite excitons, they easily dissociate into free charge carriers, which produce an electric current8. Holes then migrate into the hole‐transport material (HTM) while electrons are funneled into the electron‐transport material (ETM). In the final step, each charge carrier is collected at its respective electrode9–11. Hole‐transport materials are responsible for capturing positive charge carriers from the perovskite layer and conveying them to the metal electrode12. In both n-i-p and p-i-n layouts, hole-transport materials are crucial for maintaining the stability and performance of perovskite solar cells. In the standard n-i-p design, they are especially valued for their straightforward synthesis and the ready availability of precursors. The best HTMs combine outstanding thermal and photochemical robustness with high hole mobility to accelerate charge transport and suppress recombination. Their HOMO level sits just above the perovskite’s valence band to ensure efficient hole extraction, while their LUMO lies well above the absorber’s conduction band to block electrons from reaching the gold counter-electrode7,13. Although strong adsorption is not essential for the effectiveness of HTMs, high solubility is beneficial for the formation of the layers. Durable planar HTMs exhibit intermolecular π-π interactions that enhance hole mobility and overall cell efficiency. To minimize recombination losses through the integration of structural units containing heteroatoms and to improve the interactions between perovskite and HTM, a successful approach is needed to create high-efficiency, impurity-free hole transport materials (HTMs)14,15. The HOMO energy in a molecule is dictated by π-electron distribution across its conjugated framework. Electron-releasing groups—such as alkyl chains or nonbonding lone pairs on nitrogen, oxygen, or sulfur—boost electron density through resonance or inductive influence, raising the HOMO level while lowering the ionization energy. In contrast, electron-attracting substituents like fluorine, cyano, or carbonyl units pull down the energies of both the HOMO and the LUMO, thereby enhancing electron affinity11,16. Changes in molecular properties such as mobility, energy levels, dipole moment and band gap can be achieved by substituting small functional groups or introducing heteroatoms5,12,17. A diverse range of hole-transporting materials has been developed, including small organic compounds, inorganic materials, and organic polymers, all of which have shown remarkable performance in perovskite solar cells (PSCs). In particular, helicene-derived semiconductor molecules, defined by their twisted π-surfaces, are promising due to their improved mobility resulting from larger molecular aggregation domains and multidimensional hole transport. Investigations of carbohelicene- and azahelicene–based hole-transport materials, aimed at producing low-cost, long-lasting perovskite solar cells, have demonstrated pronounced CH–π interactions in their crystal lattices and outstanding phase stability18. Previous investigations have confirmed that density functional theory offers a reliable and efficient approach for forecasting the structural configurations, electronic properties, and various other characteristics of conjugated systems4,19. DFT and TD-DFT in particular allows the evaluation of critical properties of materials without the need for experimental synthesis. Such predictive capabilities enable the design of materials with customized absorption characteristics, properly aligned electronic energy levels, and fine-tuned band gaps that facilitate efficient charge movement. Additionally, it is crucial to probe the mechanisms of hole transport and create advanced HTMs featuring superior hole mobility and elevated HOMO energies tailored for perovskite applications4,17. This study employs density functional theory at the B3LYP/6-311G level of theory to explore a series of small-molecule hole-transport materials tailored for perovskite photovoltaics. Each candidate features a helicene backbone adorned with diphenylamine moieties and is systematically modified at ortho and meta sites with substituents: F, Cl, Br, Me, CN, COOH, COMe, NH₂, and NO₂. Their structural, optical, and electronic attributes—in particular, their hole-shuttling capabilities—are characterized through both DFT and time-dependent DFT calculations. Excited-state (S₀→S₁) charge-transfer channels are mapped using the interfragment charge transfer (IFCT) approach, while electron–hole interaction analyses of the first excited state gauge separation efficiency. By merging these electron–hole metrics with IFCT findings, the work delivers deeper insight into HTM transport mechanisms and offers design principles for next-generation hole-transport materials4,20,21.

Computational techniques

The molecular structures of HTM were built and rendered in Avogadro and their electronic properties were evaluated by DFT and TD-DFT calculations in Gaussian 0922. Gas-phase optimization of the ground state geometries (S₀) for all compounds was performed using the widely accepted B3LYP functional and for UV–vis analyses, the CAM- B3LYP functional was used, both with the 6-311G(d) basis set23. Electron–hole distribution maps and the associated charge transfer matrix were generated using Multiwfn 3.8 for comprehensive wave function analysis24. The solvent environment was simulated using the Polarizable Continuum Model (PCM) with dichloromethane as the implicit solvent. Time-dependent DFT analysis in the gas phase was performed to capture the dynamics of the adsorption process25,26. Results and discussion

Refined ground‐state geometries

In each HTM design, the linkage of diphenylamine units to the helicene core produces two specific dihedral angles. The first angle, Φ1, spans from 120.6 to 129.8°—a deviation of 50.2° to 59.4° from perfect planarity—while the second, Φ2, lies between – 130.2 and – 142°, placing it 38° to 49.8° away from a straight 180° alignment. These significant twists result in an overall nonplanar configuration that disrupts uniform π–π stacking and reduces conjugation throughout the aromatic framework, thereby affecting the materials’ physical traits and electronic performance. For effective hole extraction, the HOMO of HTM must be positioned above the valence band of absorber35,36. The molecular structure of helicene-based diphenylamine derivatives tunes the HOMO–LUMO energy levels through both electronic and conformational effects. The electron-donating diphenylamine units mainly raise the HOMO level, while the twisted helicene backbone modulates π-conjugation and fine-tunes orbital energies. This allows better energetic alignment between the HOMO of the hole transport material and the Valence Band Maximum of MAPbI3, reducing the hole-injection barrier and facilitating efficient hole extraction. In addition, substituent further adjust the frontier orbitals, improving interfacial charge transfer and suppressing recombination37. The valence and conduction bands of MAPbI₃ are reported at − 5.43 eV and − 3.93 eV, respectively38. Notably, several HTMs (HEL, HEL1, HEL3, HEL5, HEL7, HEL8, HEL13, HEL15, and HEL16) exhibit HOMO levels higher than MAPbI₃. Among them, HEL16—featuring a potent electron-donating group at the DPA meta-position—achieves the highest HOMO energy of − 5.27 eV35,39. An electron-donating amine at meta position of DFA elevates the HOMO level, strengthening its ability to pull holes from the perovskite layer. HEL3, which bears a chlorine substituent, showcases one of the lowest HOMO energies at –5.41 eV. Placing Cl at the ortho site withdraws electron density through an inductive effect, deepening the HOMO, which in turn boosts oxidative resilience but can hinder hole extraction due to a wider energy gap with the perovskite’s valence band. As a result, both the unsubstituted HEL and the ortho-substituted variants (HEL1, HEL3, HEL5, HEL7, HEL13, HEL15) featuring F, Cl, Br, CH₃, COOR, and NH₂ groups—as well as the meta-substituted HEL8 and HEL16 (CH₃ and NH₂)—all align favorably with MAPbI₃. This optimal alignment promotes rapid hole injection from the absorber into the HTM28,40,41. Among the HTMs bearing meta-position substituents (HEL2, HEL4, HEL6, HEL10, HEL12, HEL14, HEL18) and those with ortho-position CN, COOH, or NO₂ groups (HEL9, HEL11, HEL17), the HOMO resides below valence band of MAPbI₃, rendering them ineffective as hole transport layers42. Ensuring an LUMO of HTM sits above the perovskite conduction band is equally vital to block electron flow toward the counter electrode and curb charge recombination28,43. Therefore, all HTMs can prevent the return of electrons44. Comparison of the HOMO energy levels in HTMs shows that all HTMs except HEL7 and HEL8, which have CH3 substitutions in the ortho and meta positions, and HEL16, which has NH2 substitution in the meta position, have lower HOMOs than the HEL HOMO (base structure). Absorption spectrum of the engineered HTMs

Time-dependent TD-DFT calculations in the gas phase were performed at the CAM-B3LYP/6-311G(d) level of theory to characterize the optical properties of compounds. because it provides a more accurate description of the long-range charge-transfer interactions that are important in helicene-based hole-transport materials and it leads to more accurate electronic and optical predictions25,52. Table 3 compiles the principal photophysical metrics for each HTM, listing vertical excitation energies (Eex), oscillator strengths (f), absorption maxima (λmax), light-harvesting efficiencies (LHE), and the dominant molecular orbital (MO) transitions responsible for the key absorption bands14. The optical absorption of MAPbI3 is in the range of 550–800 nm53–55. To prevent competition in light absorption with the perovskite solar cells (PSCs), it is generally advantageous for the absorption spectrum of hole transport materials (HTMs) to be concentrated within the 400 nm range53. Optical analysis of the synthesized HTMs shows that their absorption maximum is in the range of 351.49–368.96 nm and does not interfere with the absorption band of MAPbI356. The ultraviolet absorption of helicene-based HTMs minimizes the spectral overlap with the perovskite absorber, which is beneficial for efficient light harvesting in n-i-p perovskite solar cells. Consequently, this factor makes them suitable as hole-transporting materials in conventional n-i-p perovskite solar cells28,57. The absorption λmax of HEL7, HEL8, HEL15 and HEL16 is higher than that of HEL (3.46, 0.49, 5.57 and 1.89 nm, respectively). In contrast, the absorption λmax of HTMs (HEL1-HEL6, HEL9-HEL14 and HEL17-HEL18) shows a decrease compared to the absorption λmax of HEL (8.41, 4.03, 6.64, 5.22, 5.59, 4.74, 7.07, 8.53, 9.18, 6.93, 1.98, 7.59, 11.16 and 11.90 nm, respectively). Furthermore, the excitation energy (Eex) of HTMs has been evaluated, which results in the following sequence of values: HEL17 and HEL18 (3.52 eV) > HEL11 (3.50 eV) > HEL1 and HEL10 (3.49 eV) > HEL14 (3.48 eV) > HEL3, HEL9 and HEL12 (3.47 eV) > HEL4 and HEL5 (3.46 eV) > HEL2 and HEL6 (3.45 eV) > HEL13 (3.43 eV) > HEL (3.41 eV) > HEL8 (3.40 eV) > HEL16 (3.39 eV) > HEL7 (3.37 eV) > HEL15 (3.36 eV). The excitation energies are strongly affected by the nature and position of the substituent on the DPA units attached to the dithiahelicene core. Compounds bearing electron-withdrawing nitro groups at either ortho or meta position show higher Eex values due to the reduction of electron density in the π-system. In contrast, the presence of an electron-donating amino group at the ortho position leads to lower Eex, which can be attributed to enhanced electron donating and stronger conjugation, promoting intramolecular charge transfer (ICT)58. Lower vertical excitation energies in HTMs favor efficient charge separation by facilitating the transition of charge from bonding to antibonding orbitals. When benchmarked against the parent HEL molecule, HTMs HEL1–HEL6, HEL9–HEL14, HEL17 and HEL18 exhibit higher Eex values, whereas HEL7, HEL8, HEL15 and HEL16 show lower Eex. The oscillator strength (f) measures the intensity of the HOMO→LUMO transition, and the light-harvesting efficiency (LHE) is given by LHE = 1 − 10(–f)14,59. According to Table 3, HTMs HEL1, HEL10, HEL13, HEL14 and HEL17 have increased f and LHE relative to HEL; HEL3, HEL5- HEL9, HEL11, HEL12, HEL15, HEL16 and HEL18 display decreased values; and HEL2 and HEL4 retain the same f and LHE as HEL.

The computed molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) distributions for the designed HTMs

Electrostatic potential levels (ESP) were calculated using the B3LYP method with the 6-311G(d) basis set. This approach is useful in accurately determining acidic and basic sites that may participate in intermolecular interactions, especially in polar environments60. Red regions represent electron-rich regions that are favorable for electrophilic reactions. In contrast, blue regions that are electron-deficient are favorable for nucleophilic reactions60. The electrostatic potential levels of the structures HEL, HEL1, and HEL2 are shown in Fig. 5, and those for the remaining structures are shown in Fig. S3. According to Fig. 5, in the HEL structure, the negative regions are mainly located on the DPA, dithiahelicene rings, and the methoxy oxygen, due to the high electronegativity and lone pairs of the N and O atoms, as well as the π-electron cloud of the aromatic system. Strong positive potential concentrates on the methyl hydrogens because the electron cloud shifts toward the carbon atom, leaving those hydrogens partially electropositive. In HEL1–HEL6, negative potential areas appear over the diphenylamine (DPA) unit, the dithiahelicene rings, the methoxy oxygen, and the halogens (F, Cl, Br) due to their high electronegativity and lone‐pair electrons. However, in the structures of HEL3 and HEL5, more negative charge is distributed on the dithiahelicene rings. This is attributed to the combined effect of their weaker inductive effect compared to F, their possible contribution to the mesomeric resonance, and the higher polarizability of chlorine and bromine atoms, which facilitates charge decentralization in the conjugated helicene system. The presence of methyl in HEL7 and HEL8 did not significantly change the positive and negative potential distribution compared to HEL. This is due to the weak inductive effect of the methyl group, the absence of resonance interactions with the π-system and its minimal steric interference on the helicene framework. In HEL9 and HEL10, the presence of the CN group with a negative electrostatic potential is strongly concentrated due to the strong inductive effect and high electronegativity of the CN group, especially on the N atom. For this reason, we observe a different charge distribution compared to the previous structures. As a result, the negative charge is more concentrated in the CN groups and we observe a relatively smaller charge distribution in the DPA, dithiahelicene and methoxy oxygen groups. For HEL11–HEL14, the regions of negative electrostatic potential are predominantly situated on the carbonyl oxygen atoms, reflecting the high electronegativity and inductive influence of the carboxyl and ketone groups61. However, in HEL11 and HEL12, due to the presence of two oxygens in the carboxyl group, the negative charge is more concentrated than in HEL13 and HEL14, which have one oxygen in the carbonyl groups. In HEL15 and HEL16, negative potential is still localized on the diphenylamine core, the dithiahelicene rings, and the ketone oxygen, but the presence of electron-donating NH₂ groups uniquely concentrates positive potential around the amine hydrogens62. And finally, HEL17 and HEL18, which have NO2 substitution. The localization of negative charge in these two molecules mirrors that seen in HEL11 and HEL12—both structures feature two electronegative oxygen atoms similar to a carboxyl group—but the nitro moiety concentrates negative potential more intensely than the carboxyl, thanks to its nitrogen atom being more electronegative than carbon48. As a result, the defects on the perovskite surface are expected to be reduced by the defined areas of negative potential, especially the Lewis base anchors, through coordination with the uncoordinated lead (Pb)63.

Analysis of electron excitation

Multiwfn 3.8 was employed to compute hole–electron distributions and wavefunction differences by contrasting the excited‐state and ground‐state electron densities24,64. The VMD visualization tool was then used to display these distributions24. Interfragment charge transfer (IFCT) analysis quantified the extent of electron transfer65. The transport of charge and the optoelectronic characteristics of hole transport materials (HTMs) are crucial for comprehending their prospective applications in devices. A recent investigation explored how the molecular architecture of various hole-transport materials (HTMs) influences their photophysical and electronic behaviors86,87. Their charge-carrying capabilities and optical properties are compiled in Table 8 to pinpoint the most effective candidates. Key descriptors in this assessment are the HOMO energy (EHOMO), LUMO energy (ELUMO), and the resulting gap (Egap)86. For optimal hole extraction, HOMO of HTM must lie above that of MAPbI₃, and its LUMO must exceed conduction band of MAPbI₃ to prevent electron back-transfer to the gold electrode36. The data show that HEL2, HEL4, HEL6, HEL9, HEL10, HEL11, HEL12, HEL14, HEL17, and HEL18—each bearing terminal substituents like –F, –Cl, –Br, –CN, –COOH, –COR, or –NO₂—have HOMO levels misaligned with HOMO of MAPbI₃, disqualifying them for practical use. In contrast, HEL, HEL1, HEL3, HEL5, HEL7, HEL8, HEL13, HEL15, and HEL16, which carry –F, –Cl, –Br, –CH₃, –COR, or –NH₂ groups, exhibit HOMO and LUMO energies that align favorably with those of MAPbI₃, making them promising for device integration. Therefore, these HTMs (HEL, HEL1, HEL3, HEL5, HEL7, HEL8, HEL13, HEL15, and HEL16) are considered suitable. In hole transport materials (HTMs) such as HEL, HEL1, HEL3, HEL5, HEL7, HEL8, HEL13, HEL15, and HEL16, the maximum absorption wavelength (λmax) varies from 354.98 to 368.96 nm, which is in the visible spectrum. Conclusion

In this research, innovative helicene-based hole-transporting materials (HTMs) were developed. To assess the effectiveness of designed compounds as hole-transport material, density functional theory (DFT) investigations were undertaken. The calculated frontier molecular orbital energies for HEL, HEL1, HEL3, HEL5, HEL7, HEL8, HEL13, HEL15, and HEL16 closely match those of MAPbI₃. Also, confirming their suitability The selected HTMs have a more favorable HOMO/LUMO distribution, stronger electron scattering, and improved intramolecular charge transport properties, which leads to improved hole transport capability and reduced charge recombination compared to other structures. Conversely, the HTMs (HEL2, HEL4, HEL6, HEL9, HEL10, HEL11, HEL12, HEL14, HEL17, and HEL18) are deemed unsuitable. The HTMs exhibit peak absorption between 354.98 and 368.96, making them well‐suited for integration into conventional n–i–p perovskite solar cells. HEL16 features the narrowest band gap among the series, a trait that typically boosts photovoltaic performance. Insights from electron–hole density maps and interfacial charge‐transfer analysis reveal that its molecular architecture supports especially efficient carrier mobility. Across the HTM set, the hole reorganization energies span 0.190–0.353 eV, and lower λhole values—such as that found for HEL16—favor faster hole hopping. Its calculated electron–hole binding energy is also among the lowest, indicating superior exciton dissociation. With an adiabatic ionization potential of just 5.34 eV, HEL16 stands out for facile hole formation. Finally, the incorporation of electron‐donating substituents (with energy levels in the 2.35–2.53 eV range) further enhances HTM robustness, contributing to overall device stability and efficiency. HEL16 demonstrates high solubility, a critical factor for achieving uniform cell formation. HEL16 exhibits a higher hole generation capacity due to the electron-donating effect of the amine group, which increases electron delocalization and intramolecular charge transfer. In addition, the meta configuration reduces spatial distortion and helps improve thermodynamic stability and solubility. HEL16’s frontier orbital energies are − 5.27 eV (HOMO) and − 2.57 eV (LUMO), giving a band gap of 2.70 eV and an absorption maximum at 365.28 nm. Its electron–hole distribution metrics are D = 0.93 Å, H = 4.66 Å, t = − 2.75 Å, and Sr = 0.70 Å. The molecule carries a net charge of 0.44 e– with a 52.28% hole contribution, an energy barrier of 0.46 eV, an IPa of 5.34 eV, a solvation free energy of − 0.59 eV, a hole reorganization energy (λhole) of 0.298 eV, and a mobility (η) of 2.35 eV. Because its orbital energies align with MAPbI₃, coupled with its favorable electron–hole distribution, high solubility, and enhanced hole mobility, HEL16 is proposed as an effective HTM for n–i–p perovskite solar cells.
