International fiscal architecture for advancing sexual and reproductive health and rights
Authors: Attiya Waris, Juan Pablo Bohoslavsky, Gorik Ooms, Rachel Hammonds
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Delivering sexual and reproductive health justice requires a new independent and neutral global body where global rights obligations are prioritised, write Attiya Waris and colleagues

The freefall in development assistance for health has captured most of the attention of the global health community and could indeed lead to “tens of millions of excess deaths” in low and middle income countries (LMICs). Reductions in development assistance will contribute to reversing progress on achieving the sustainable development goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3.1 (reduce the global maternal mortality ratio) and SDG 3.2 (end preventable deaths of newborns and children under 5 years).1 Cuts in US funding alone are predicted to contribute to “40-55 million additional unplanned pregnancies and 12-16 million unsafe abortions across 51 countries, and 2.5 million (1.3-4.5) additional child deaths from causes other than HIV and tuberculosis across 24 countries.”2

Preventing these avoidable deaths will require countries dependent on development assistance to increase domestic government spending on sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) to compensate for the funding shortfall.3 LMICs are diverse, but many have tighter fiscal constraints and weaker regulatory and industrial capacity than high income countries, largely because of external factors, including the international fiscal architecture (box 1). This article, part of a BMJ Collection examining the effects of shifts in the multilateral system on SRHR, considers how broader macroeconomic and fiscal trends will constrain LMICs’ capacity to increase government expenditure on health systems and will contribute to increases in avoidable morbidity and mortality, especially among women and children, undermining progress on SRHR.

We focus on three structural issues: externally imposed austerity measures (which require a global approach), tax related solutions (which are domestic but have elements of global implications through the web of bilateral tax treaties on corporate taxation), and addressing debt burdens (usually negotiated at a global level with international institutions or other member states and often in a foreign currency). Focusing on these three issues highlights why a piecemeal approach will not fix the combined pressure of these trends on LMICs’ fiscal space.1 To advance SRHR requires tackling these financial obstacles through systemic reforms to the international financial architecture.2 Addressing them requires a new international financial architecture and institution—one with a true human face.3 4

Harms of structural adjustment inspired measures

In the 1980s, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank began requiring that LMICs implement “structural adjustment” polices in order to receive development assistance (box 2). Economic analysis of IMF and World Bank data shows that these conditions for accessing financing never really went away and simply changed form.5 The gendered consequences of these policies, including increases in maternal mortality, were highlighted in a 1987 Unicef report.6

Considerable research shows how these policies have continued to disproportionately affect women’s and girls’ health by constraining reproductive health choice, undermining access to quality and affordable healthcare and the social determinants of health, including income and food availability.7 8 9 For example, sub-Saharan African nations that received an IMF structural adjustment loan tended to have higher levels of maternal mortality than sub-Saharan African nations that did not.8

The effects of debt can extend beyond health spending. For example, high levels of public debt and pressure to meet debt repayments can reduce investment in climate mitigation and environmental protection. This contributes to climate related risks, which in turn can harm SRHR by disrupting access to healthcare, increasing gender based violence, and worsening pregnancy outcomes during extreme weather events.

Austerity

Austerity policies are particularly relevant for SRHR because cuts to health and care budgets reduce the quality and quantity of care workers available to treat child and maternal health conditions.7 In addition, they disproportionately reduce women’s employment prospects as women comprise the majority of the health and care workforce.2

Even during the covid-19 pandemic, the IMF and the World Bank continued encouraging countries to adopt “structural adjustment” measures. Analysis in September 2022 estimated that 143 countries (including 94 LMICs) were implementing structural adjustment measures and that in 2023, 85% of the world population would be living under austerity measures which would continue at least until 2025.10 These measures affect the wellbeing of all people, but disproportionally affect women, for example through cuts to reproductive healthcare services worsening pre-existent gender inequalities.11

The first sectors that usually suffer from budget cutbacks are education, health, and care, all of which are highly feminised in labour terms. These differential and discriminatory effects occur through a series of causal channels, including the loss of access to public services, fewer opportunities to access (or keep) jobs with decent conditions, a larger burden of unpaid care work, the increase of debt in households, and the weakening of food safety, among others. The adverse effects of structural adjustment on the realisation of SRHR derives not only from budget cuts in the health sector but also from other policies, such as privatisation, deregulation of financial and labour markets, and lowering environmental protection standards.12

Taxation

Fair and progressive taxation is essential for generating the domestic revenues needed to finance public services, including the development of health systems that guarantee equitable access to quality services that advance SRHR. Insufficient domestic revenue often leads to underfunded health systems, which can limit access to contraceptive counselling and choices (eg, offering only hormonal methods), thereby reducing reproductive health rights.9

The number of billionaires and trillionaires worldwide is growing rapidly, with billionaire wealth increasing by over 16% in 2025, three times faster than the average for the previous five years.13 At the same time, there is an increase in illicit financial flows (IFF) draining LMICs of much needed financial resources 14 Africa alone is losing about $88bn a year, which represents about 3.7% of the continent’s total gross domestic product.15

Efforts to conclude a UN Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation are hampered by high income countries. These countries favour guidelines developed under the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), a club of 38 mostly high income countries. Crucial areas of disagreement are not only which organisation should host (UN or OECD) but also on taxing rights (taxation where profits are made versus taxation where companies are registered), for example.16 Pro-poor, pro-women approaches to domestic revenue raising require a focus on progressive measures such as wealth taxes and taxing corporate profits rather than regressive measures such as value added tax, which increase the prices of essential commodities, including food.15

Debt

Action on the debt crisis cannot wait for an effective UN tax treaty. The external debt of LMICs hit a record of $11.4tn in March 2025, and has continued growing since.17 High debt servicing limits governments’ ability to invest in public services including health and care services, education, and climate adaptation, leading to calls to reform the international financial architecture. Feminist analysis has highlighted the gendered and unequal impact of debt related cuts and austerity, including the impacts on women’s health and education expanding to the health related impact of unpaid domestic and care work on women and girls.18 19 When governments reduce public care expenditures because of debt servicing or austerity, the expectation is that care will be delivered unpaid, largely by women or girls.20 The attendant gendered consequences of time poverty and the diminished opportunities for schoolwork or rest are not factored into such policy decisions.

A human rights based approach to managing sovereign debt recognises that debt sustainability should be assessed not only by a country’s ability to repay its debts, but also by its ability to fulfil its human rights obligations. The Interagency Task Force on Financing for Development has made clear that debt and debt service levels must not compromise a country’s ability to respect, protect, and fulfil its human rights obligations, including the right to health, the right to life, and the prohibition of gender discrimination.21 This requires that the legal principle of pacta sunt servanda (“agreements must be kept”) of financial law must be harmonised with the international human rights and gender equality obligations, which may suggest that debt relief is needed.22 Extensive research shows how and why IMF debt relief policies do not sufficiently engage with the differential effect of their policies on women.12 23 Despite this evidence the World Bank and IMF, as private creditors, have not extended their fiscal and short term criteria to assess debt sustainability to consider effects on rights. In the absence of global rules for sovereign debt restructuring that incorporate human rights, gender equality, and development considerations, creditors often define debt as sustainable simply because governments can continue making repayments in the short term, even when doing so requires measures that undermine human rights or long term development. For example, governments may be forced to undertake extreme fiscal adjustment that weakens social protection.

Why does this matter for SRHR?

Better resourced health systems improve women’s and children’s health.24 Although the relation between the “inputs” of healthcare systems—including government expenditure—and their “outputs”—improved health and increased life expectancy—are complex, increasing inputs generally leads to greater outputs, and this correlation is stronger in countries with lower health expenditure. In an era of fast declining development assistance for health, increasing domestic government health expenditure in LMICs is particularly important.

States have legally binding obligations under international treaties, including the International Covenant on Economic, Social, and Cultural Rights, which enshrines the right to health.25 In ratifying these treaties states commit to using their domestic resources to realise the right to health, and those in a position to assist commit to providing assistance to ensure the right to health can be realised universally. National budgets must have clear financial allocations for healthcare in general, including for women’s and children’s health.

During fiscal crises, states have been seen to push health to the bottom of their priorities despite it being a driving force to any country’s growth.26 Insufficient budget allocations lead to shortages of medicines, medical equipment, and hospital beds, or even health worker strikes. Closely tied to this is that even if citizens have access to insurance cover, they may still have to pay out-of-pocket for some procedures, such as birth related medical interventions, because they are excluded from what is covered.13 In the Abuja Declaration of 2001, African leaders pledged to increase public health expenditure by channelling at least 15% of their budgets into healthcare. However, in 2021 about one third of African countries had allocated only about 10% of their national annual budget to the health sector because of factors including limited fiscal space, debt obligations, corruption and resource mismanagement, a lack of accountability and proper governance, and donor dependence.27

Ways forward

Ensuring universal access to SRHR in a context of shrinking aid budgets requires ensuring that LMICs’ fiscal space is not constrained by external pressure. Without sufficient fiscal space, these countries will be unable to increase domestic expenditure on health and the health of their citizens, especially marginalised groups, will suffer. Another article in this collection discusses the actions required to prevent these ongoing rights violations.28 Given that high income countries have failed to fulfil their legal obligations to engage in international cooperation and assistance for 60 years, the time has come to consider enforcement mechanisms, and a new international financial architecture could contribute to that. Furthermore, to emphasise that international assistance is not charity, LMICs may have to recast their claims as reparations.

Our proposal for a new international fiscal architecture aligns with a recent analysis calling for major reforms to the World Bank and IMF, a complete restructuring of the voting system to shift decision making power to the global south, decoupling financial assistance from macroeconomic conditionalities, and the creation and strengthening of alternative, decolonial financial institutions.29

One reason that undemocratic policies persist is that the IMF and the World Bank are not governed by the United Nations principle of “one country, one vote”; final decisions are taken or improved on the basis of a shareholder logic, which puts developing countries in a minority position.30 The Government of Barbados’s 2022 Bridgetown Initiative is helping to lead a shift in the “discourse on scaling capital flows and reshaping the financing system to achieve the SDGs and spur climate action.”31

While we welcome the calls for reform, the challenges cannot be resolved with incremental change. What is required is a new international financial architecture, including the creation of a global fiscal institution that is human centred.5 This new global fiscal institution should be governed on the basis of the principle of “equal sovereignty,” which means “one country, one vote.” To be able to harmonise international rules that affect all countries’ ability to provide the full enjoyment of all human rights, particularly economic, social, and cultural rights, or to allow the making of such rules if they do not yet exist, this new global fiscal institution should be empowered to have a say in, at least, reparations (instead of development assistance), global tax justice and debt restructuring, and debt relief when needed to ensure the realisation of minimum core rights.

Considering the ongoing geopolitical crisis, calling for such a global fiscal institution may seem naive. However, hoping for this crisis to go away without a deep reform of the international financial architecture may be even more naive. As the so called “rules based international order” has lost its appearance of benevolence and fairness, LMICs are using their power to say no to forms of international assistance and cooperation that do not serve their interests fairly, and some high income countries now present (or understand) themselves as “middle powers”—that is, with diminishing global influence and facing a rather unpleasant future in a “might is right” world. These shifts create both an opportunity and an urgent need to rethink the international fiscal architecture to better advance SRHR.
