Differential physiological responses to electroacupuncture at ST36 and SP6 in CFA-induced inflammatory arthritis: serum metabolomic evidence for acupoint-specific redox regulation
Authors: Seung Heon Jeon, Moongi Ji, Vishnumolakala Sindhuri, Min-Jae Koo, Ki-Tae Ha, Seungtae Kim, Man Jeong Paik, Sungtae Koo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electroacupuncture (EA) modulates neuroimmune and metabolic pathways, but acupoint-specific physiological responses remain poorly characterized. This study investigated acupoint-dependent differences in nociceptive behavior, synovial inflammation, and serum metabolomic profiles in rats with complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA)-induced knee arthritis. Animals were assigned to three groups: CFA without EA, CFA with EA at SP6, and CFA with EA at ST36. EA at ST36 significantly improved hindlimb weight-bearing (74.1% vs. 45.3% at post-injection day 8; p < 0.01), whereas EA at SP6 did not reach significance. Both acupoints comparably reduced synovial inflammation scores. Targeted GC-MS/MS serum metabolomics identified 14 significantly altered metabolites; 2-hydroxybutyric acid (2-HBA) showed the largest fold-change and was selectively normalized by EA at ST36. Intra-articular 2-HBA injection did not alter pain behavior, indicating it represents a systemic physiological consequence of inflammatory-metabolic burden rather than a nociceptive mediator. These findings demonstrate acupoint-specific modulation of redox-metabolic homeostasis by EA in inflammatory arthritis.

Introduction

Inflammatory arthritis is characterized by persistent synovial inflammation, joint nociception, and systemic metabolic dysregulation, collectively reflecting a disruption of neuroimmune and redox homeostasis that extends beyond the local joint environment [1], [2]. Current pharmacological approaches, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and corticosteroids, provide symptomatic relief but carry significant risks of gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, and renal adverse effects with long-term use, and fail to address the underlying pathophysiology [3], [4], [27], [28], [29]. The physiological mechanisms underlying these systemic changes, and the extent to which they can be normalized by neuromodulatory interventions such as EA, remain incompletely understood.

EA modulates pain and inflammation through somatosensory afferent activation, engaging spinal and supraspinal circuits that regulate nociception, autonomic tone, and neuroimmune function. Clinical and preclinical evidence demonstrates significant improvements in pain and functional outcomes in knee osteoarthritis, with pooled effect sizes showing substantial pain reduction (SMD −1.86, 95% CI −2.33 to −1.39) and total WOMAC score improvements (SMD −1.34, 95% CI −1.85 to −0.83) compared to control groups [7]. A review of 10 randomized controlled trials encompassing 1456 participants confirmed acupuncture as an effective treatment for pain and physical dysfunction associated with knee osteoarthritis [8]. Furthermore, higher intensity EA stimulation (>2 mA) has been shown to enhance analgesic efficacy in chronic pain through strengthening of conditioned pain modulation function [9]. Despite this evidence, the acupoint-specific physiological mechanisms by which EA modulates systemic metabolic homeostasis remain poorly characterized, and the extent to which different acupoints engage distinct neurohumoral pathways is not well understood [10], [11].

Inflammatory pain states are intimately coupled to systemic metabolic reprogramming. Arthritis-associated inflammation is characterized by oxidative stress, altered energy metabolism, and perturbations of amino acid, organic acid, and lipid pathways [5], [6]. Metabolomics studies in rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis have identified at least 50 metabolites that differ significantly between patients and healthy controls, spanning the citrate cycle, amino acid catabolism, and lipid oxidation pathways [5], [6]. Targeted GC-MS/MS-based metabolomics, which enables simultaneous quantification of amino acids, organic acids, and fatty acids from biofluids, provides a powerful and sensitive approach for capturing treatment-responsive metabolic shifts [12]. Applying such metabolomics frameworks to EA research offers the potential to characterize the systemic physiological responses to acupoint stimulation that conventional inflammatory markers cannot capture. Identifying acupoint-specific metabolic signatures would provide mechanistic insight into how EA modulates redox-metabolic homeostasis and neuroimmune regulation in inflammatory disease.

Among metabolites linked to oxidative stress and inflammatory joint disease, 2-hydroxybutyric acid (2-HBA) has attracted growing interest as a clinically relevant biomarker. 2-HBA is generated as a byproduct of the transsulfuration and glutathione biosynthesis pathways: under conditions of oxidative stress, increased demand for glutathione drives elevated flux through cysteine catabolism, generating alpha-ketobutyrate, which is subsequently reduced by lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) to yield 2-HBA [13], [14]. Consequently, elevated serum 2-HBA reflects heightened hepatic NADH/NAD+ ratio and systemic oxidative stress burden, and has been established as an early biomarker of insulin resistance, impaired glucose metabolism, and inflammatory conditions [13]. Importantly, 2-HBA is elevated in the blood of both animal models and patients with rheumatoid arthritis, suggesting its relevance as an indicator of arthritis-associated redox dysregulation [5], [6].

The selection of acupoints ST36 and SP6 for this study was guided by their established roles in modulating pain and inflammation. ST36 exhibits prominent anti-inflammatory and anti-nociceptive effects through modulation of MAPK signaling pathways and neuroendocrine-immune networks, with demonstrated efficacy in reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhancing immune regulation [15]. SP6 mediates analgesic and autonomic regulatory effects, and combined ST36 and SP6 stimulation has been reported to show synergistic anti-inflammatory activities via sciatic nerve-mediated pathways [15]. The inclusion of both acupoints in a parallel-group comparative design allows assessment of whether the metabolic effects of EA are acupoint-specific or reflect a generalized neuromodulatory response, an important question for understanding the mechanistic basis of EA-induced analgesia. This acupoint-specific comparison provides a controlled physiological framework for examining how differential peripheral innervation and segmental afferent input translate into distinct systemic metabolic and neurohumoral responses, independent of local anti-inflammatory effects.

The present study aimed to (1) compare the acupoint-specific effects of EA at ST36 and SP6 on pain behavior and synovial inflammation in a CFA-induced knee arthritis rat model, and (2) characterize the acupoint-dependent serum metabolomic responses using targeted GC-MS/MS metabolomics, with a focus on redox-related metabolite changes. The CFA-induced arthritis model provides a highly reproducible inflammatory joint pain model that shares key histological and biochemical features with human inflammatory arthritis, making it well-suited for mechanistic investigations [16], [17]. Our primary hypothesis was that EA at ST36 would more effectively improve weight-bearing capacity and reduce synovial inflammation than EA at SP6. Our secondary hypothesis was that reductions in serum 2-HBA levels in EA-treated animals would reflect acupoint-specific normalization of redox-metabolic homeostasis, providing physiological evidence for differential neurohumoral responses to ST36 versus SP6 stimulation.

Methods

Animals

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (180–200 g; Hyochang Science, Daegu, Korea) were used in this study. Rats were housed at a constant room temperature of 22 ± 1˚C with relative humidity of 50 ± 5% under a 12:12-h reversed light-dark cycle (dark cycle: 8:00 A.M. – 8:00 P.M.). All animals were allowed free access to food and water ad libitum and were acclimatized for 1 week before experiments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Study of Pusan National University (approval No. PNU−2022–3161) and all animals were treated according to the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health for care and use of laboratory animals.

Experimental design and groupings

A total of 24 rats were used for the main experiment. To investigate the analgesic effect and metabolic changes induced by repeated EA on CFA-induced inflammatory pain, rats were randomly assigned to one of three groups using a random number generator: CFA+ANES (anesthesia only control, n = 8), CFA+SP6 (SP6 electroacupuncture, n = 8), and CFA+ST36 (ST36 electroacupuncture, n = 8). Inflammation was induced in all rats by intra-articular CFA injection. The CFA+SP6 and CFA+ST36 groups received EA applied to the SP6 or ST36 acupoints, respectively, while the CFA+ANES group received anesthesia only without needle insertion. Weight bearing force was measured at baseline (Day −1) and on post-injection days (PID) 2–8. EA treatment was administered daily for 6 consecutive days from PID 2–7. An additional cohort of rats was used for the 2-hydroxybutyric acid (2-HBA) injection experiment (n = 5 per group). Two experiments were conducted: (1) intra-articular injection of 2-HBA or distilled water (DW; vehicle control) into the right knee of normal (non-arthritic) rats to assess whether exogenous 2-HBA induces pain; and (2) the same injection administered PID 8, when pain had partially subsided through natural recovery, to assess whether 2-HBA exacerbates residual arthritis pain.

Sample size calculation

Sample sizes were determined based on our pilot studies and previous publications using the same CFA-induced arthritis model [16], [17], which consistently demonstrated that a minimum intergroup difference of approximately 20% in weight-bearing ratio was associated with meaningful differences in histological joint pathology and was detectable with n = 8 per group (statistical power >0.80, α=0.05, β=0.20). This threshold was considered both statistically detectable and physiologically meaningful. No animals were excluded from the final analysis.

Randomization and blinding

Animals were randomly allocated to experimental groups using a computer-generated random number sequence. Behavioral testing was performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation. However, blinding was not feasible for EA treatment administration due to the distinct acupoint locations.

Induction of knee arthritis

Rats were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane (JW Pharmaceutical Corp., Seoul, Korea) in a mixture of nitrous oxide and oxygen (3% for induction and 1.5% for maintenance). CFA-induced knee arthritis was established by intra-articular injection of 100 µL of complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at a 1:1 ratio (oil:saline) into the joint space of the right hind limb on experimental Day 0, as previously described [18], [19]. The injection was performed using a 26-gauge needle inserted through the patellar ligament into the knee joint cavity.

Electroacupuncture treatment

Under gaseous anesthesia as described above, the rats’ hair was shaved around the acupoint area. A pair of stainless-steel acupuncture needles (0.3 mm diameter, 13 mm length) was inserted into the contralateral (left) hindlimbs at the SP6 acupoints (CFA+SP6 group) or ST36 acupoints (CFA+ST36 group) at a depth of 5 mm. Electrical stimulation was delivered using a Grass S88 stimulator (Grass Instrument Co., Quincy, MA, USA) at an intensity of 1 mA, frequency of 10 Hz, and pulse width of 1 ms for 30 min. EA stimulation was applied once daily from PID 2 through PID 7 (6 consecutive days; Day 0 = CFA injection, baseline measurement on Day −1, final assessment on PID 8). The CFA+ANES control group received identical anesthesia exposure without needle insertion to control for potential effects of anesthesia. The specific locations of SP6 and ST36 acupoints were determined using a standardized rat acupoint atlas [18]. SP6 is located approximately 3 mm proximal to the medial malleolus, at the posterior border of the tibia. ST36 is located approximately 5 mm below and lateral to the anterior tubercle of the tibia.

Weight-bearing test

The level of arthritic pain was assessed by measuring the weight bearing forces (WBF) of the affected limb. Rats were placed in a rectangular acrylic apparatus (length × height × width: 80 × 10 × 10 cm) with a pressure sensor plate (BIOMEDI, Seoul, Korea) positioned in the center of the path and allowed to move freely. The weight borne on the affected right hind limb was recorded using a CED Power 1401 A/D board (Cambridge Electronic Design, Cambridge, UK) and Spike2 data acquisition software version 7.08 (Cambridge Electronic Design). At least three stable measurements were obtained for each animal at each time point, and the average was used for analysis. WBF (%) was calculated as:

WBF (%) = (weight borne on right hind limb / body weight) × 100. WBF at each time point was normalized to each animal's own pre-CFA baseline (Day −1) to yield the weight-bearing ratio (WBR) used throughout this study: WBR (%) = (WBF at a given time point / WBF at pre-CFA baseline) × 100. A WBR of 100% corresponds to pre-CFA baseline weight-bearing, and lower values indicate greater pain-related functional impairment. Body weight was recorded at each measurement time point.

Measurements were performed before CFA injection (baseline, Day −1) and on PID 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8. The area under the curve (AUC) for weight bearing over PID 2–8 was calculated using the trapezoidal rule to assess the overall treatment effect.

Intra-articular 2-HBA injection

To determine whether 2-HBA itself induces or exacerbates joint pain, two separate experiments were conducted using intra-articular injection of 2-HBA or distilled water (DW; vehicle control). 2-Hydroxybutyric acid (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in sterile distilled water at a concentration of 0.0005 ng/μL (osmolarity: approximately 4.8 × 10⁻⁶ mOsm/kg, essentially equivalent to distilled water), and 50 μL (total dose: 0.025 ng) was injected into the right knee joint cavity under brief isoflurane anesthesia. In the first experiment, 2-HBA or DW was injected into normal rats to assess whether exogenous 2-HBA alone is sufficient to induce pain. In the second experiment, the same injection was administered on PID 8 (1 week after CFA injection), when CFA-induced pain had partially subsided through natural recovery, to assess whether 2-HBA exacerbates residual arthritis pain. Weight-bearing was assessed at 30 min and 1 h post-injection.

Histopathological evaluation and scoring

Rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, JW Pharmaceutical Corp.) and immediately perfused transcardially with cold heparinized 0.9% saline (100 mL). The affected knee joint was carefully dissected, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 48 h, decalcified in 10% EDTA solution for 3 weeks with solution changes twice weekly, and embedded in paraffin. Serial Section (4 μm thickness) were prepared, deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol series, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Muto Pure Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan) according to standard protocols. Histopathological analysis was performed on 6 animals per group (CFA+ANES: n = 6, CFA+SP6: n = 6, CFA+ST36: n = 6): four animals per group were drawn from the main behavioral cohort (non-overlapping with the four animals per group used for serum metabolomics; see Section 2.2), and two additional animals per group were used exclusively for histological evaluation.

Synovial inflammation was assessed using a semi-quantitative scoring system based on the method described by Krenn et al. [19]. Serum sample collection and preparation

On PID 8, immediately following the final weight bearing measurement, rats were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and blood samples (3–5 mL) were collected via cardiac puncture into serum separator tubes. Blood was allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, then centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. Serum was separated, aliquoted into cryogenic vials, and stored at −80°C until metabolomics analysis.

Gas chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS)

GC-MS/MS analysis was performed using a Shimadzu GCMS-TQ8040 system (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) consisting of a GC−2010 Plus gas chromatograph coupled to a TQ−8040 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with an Ultra−2 capillary column (25 m × 0.20 mm I.D., 0.11 μm film thickness; Agilent Technologies, Atlanta, GA, USA). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, and argon was used as the collision gas. Sample injection volume was 1.0 μL in split mode (split ratio 10:1).

For amino acid profiling analysis, the oven temperature program was: initial temperature 140°C held for 3 min, ramped to 300°C at 8 °C/min, and held at 300°C for 5 min. For organic acid and fatty acid profiling analyses, the initial temperature was 100°C held for 2 min, increased to 250°C at 10 °C/min, then to 300°C at 20 °C/min, and finally held at 300°C for 5 min. Ionization was performed in electron impact (EI) mode at 70 eV. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode with optimized precursor-to-product ion transitions for each metabolite.

Sample preparation for metabolite profiling

Profiling analyses of amino acids (AAs), organic acids (OAs), and fatty acids (FAs) were performed by GC-MS/MS following derivatization to ethoxycarbonyl (EOC)-tert-butyldimethylsilyl (TBDMS), methoxime (MO)-TBDMS, and TBDMS derivatives, respectively, as previously described [12].

For amino acid analysis: Deproteinization was performed by adding acetonitrile (60 μL) to serum (30 μL) containing norvaline as internal standard (IS, 0.2 μg). After vortexing and centrifugation at 13000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was collected. Dichloromethane (2.0 mL) containing ethyl chloroformate (ECF, 20 μL) was added to the supernatant, followed by addition of deionized water (1 mL). The pH was adjusted to ≥ 12 using 5.0 M sodium hydroxide solution. Two-phase EOC derivatization was conducted with vortexing for 10 min at room temperature. The organic phase was collected, evaporated to dryness under nitrogen stream at 40°C, and reconstituted in MTBSTFA (N-methyl-N-(tert-butyldimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide; 20 μL) for TBDMS derivatization at 60°C for 60 min.

For organic acid analysis: Following deproteinization, the supernatant was transferred to a clean tube containing methoxyamine hydrochloride (1 mg) and deionized water (1 mL). The pH was adjusted to ≥ 12 with 5.0 M sodium hydroxide, and MO derivatization was performed at 60°C for 1 h. The pH was then adjusted to ≤ 2 using 10% H₂SO₄, saturated with sodium chloride, and sequentially extracted with diethyl ether (3 mL) and ethyl acetate (2 mL). The combined organic extracts were evaporated to dryness under nitrogen at 40°C and derivatized with MTBSTFA as described above.

For fatty acid analysis: Following deproteinization with pentadecanoic acid as IS (0.1 μg), the extract was directly derivatized with MTBSTFA without additional chemical modification.

All derivatives were transferred to autosampler vials and analyzed by GC-MS/MS in MRM mode. Metabolite concentrations were calculated using calibration curves prepared with authentic standards (Sigma-Aldrich) at concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 100 μg/mL. Quality control samples were analyzed every 10 samples to ensure analytical reproducibility.

Star pattern recognition analysis

The levels of AAs, OAs, and FAs were quantified using calibration curves constructed from authentic standards. For visualization purposes, the mean values of each metabolite in the CFA+ANES, CFA+SP6, and CFA+ST36 groups were normalized to the corresponding mean values of the control group (set as 1.0). The levels of each normalized metabolite were represented by lines radiating from a common center point in a polar coordinate system (star plot), allowing visual comparison of metabolic profiles across groups [20].

Multivariate statistical analysis and biomarker exploration

Metabolite data were log₁₀-transformed and Pareto-scaled prior to multivariate analysis. Hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated using Euclidean distance and average linkage methods to visualize metabolite abundance patterns and sample clustering. Partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed to identify metabolites that discriminate between experimental groups. The variable importance in projection (VIP) score was calculated for each metabolite, with VIP > 1.0 indicating high discriminative power. Model performance was assessed using seven-fold cross-validation, and model validity was confirmed by permutation testing (1000 permutations). All multivariate analyses were performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (www.metaboanalyst.ca) and R software version 4.2.1.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise stated. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.2 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) and R version 4.2.1.

For weight bearing data, group comparisons over time were analyzed using two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Greenhouse-Geisser correction for sphericity violations, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. AUC was compared using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test or non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's multiple comparisons when data did not meet normality assumptions (assessed by Shapiro-Wilk test).

For metabolomics data, group differences across the four experimental groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA. When significant differences were detected (P < 0.05), pairwise comparisons were performed using Duncan's multiple range test. Metabolites with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

To examine the relationship between individual serum 2-HBA levels and pain-related behavior, Pearson and Spearman rank correlation analyses were performed between individual serum 2-HBA concentrations and weight-bearing ratio (WBR AUC across PID 2–8, and WBR at PID 8) across all CFA-treated animals with paired metabolomics and behavioral data (CFA+ANES, CFA+SP6, and CFA+ST36 combined; n = 12). Correlation coefficients are reported with 95% confidence intervals.

For histopathological analysis, the experimental unit was the individual animal. Given the ordinal nature of the Krenn synovitis score, group differences were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test as the primary statistical method. For reference, one-way ANOVA (Shapiro-Wilk test P = 0.242; Brown-Forsythe test P = 1.000) yielded consistent results. For each animal, the mean score across three sections from both observers was used as a single data point. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient.

For 2-HBA injection experiments, weight bearing was compared between treatment groups (2-HBA vs DW) using two-way repeated measures ANOVA with time (30 min, 1 h) as the within-subject factor and treatment (2-HBA vs DW) as the between-subject factor, followed by Sidak's multiple comparison test.

Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all analyses. All tests were two-tailed unless otherwise specified.

Results

Electroacupuncture at ST36 improves weight-bearing capacity

To evaluate the analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects of EA in an experimental arthritis model, we measured hindlimb weight-bearing ratio and histopathological changes in knee synovium following CFA injection. Intra-articular CFA injection markedly reduced ipsilateral hindlimb weight-bearing ratio to approximately 10–11% of body weight by PID 2 (baseline), compared to approximately 100% prior to injection (PID 0), confirming successful induction of inflammatory arthritis in all groups.

The CFA+ST36 group showed a significant improvement beginning at PID 3 (Fig. 2B) (CFA+ST36: 25.19 ± 4.04% vs CFA+ANES: 9.89 ± 2.27%; p < 0.01), with progressively greater differences through PID 4 (CFA+ST36: 45.63 ± 7.05% vs. CFA+ANES: 17.29 ± 2.65%; p < 0.01), reaching maximum significance at PID 5 (CFA+ST36: 54.82 ± 2.68% vs. CFA+ANES: 30.02 ± 4.58%; p < 0.001), PID 6 (CFA+ST36: 63.93 ± 1.98% vs. CFA+ANES: 36.44 ± 4.82%; p < 0.001), and PID 7 (CFA+ST36: 69.58 ± 2.25% vs. CFA+ANES: 38.49 ± 4.77%; p < 0.001), before returning to p < 0.01 at PID 8 (CFA+ST36: 74.08 ± 2.97% vs. CFA+ANES: 45.32 ± 5.95%). Although the CFA+SP6 group showed a trend toward improved weight-bearing from PID 4 onwards, reaching 53.61 ± 4.78% at PID 8 (vs. CFA+ANES: 45.32 ± 5.95%), the differences from the CFA+ANES group did not reach statistical significance at any individual timepoint (all p > 0.05). To summarize the overall magnitude of functional recovery across the full post-injection assessment period, we calculated the area under the curve (AUC) of WBR for each animal (PID 2–8; Fig. 2C). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant overall group effect (F = 10.79, p = 0.0006). Tukey's post hoc test showed that the CFA+ST36 group had a significantly greater AUC than both the CFA+ANES group (303.27 ± 15.72 vs. 160.05 ± 19.09; p < 0.001) and the CFA+SP6 group (209.67 ± 29.30; p = 0.018), whereas the CFA+ANES and CFA+SP6 groups did not differ significantly (p = 0.274). This AUC analysis confirms that ST36 produced the greatest overall functional recovery across the treatment period, significantly exceeding both CFA+ANES and CFA+SP6.

EA attenuates synovial membrane inflammation

Histological examination of H&E-stained knee joint synovium confirmed marked inflammation in the CFA+ANES group, characterized by pronounced synovial lining cell hyperplasia, increased stromal cellularity, and dense inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 3A). Quantification using the Krenn synovitis scoring system revealed a mean synovitis score of 8.5 ± 0.34 in the CFA+ANES group (Fig. 3B). EA treatment significantly reduced synovitis scores in both the CFA+ST36 group (4.5 ± 0.34; p = 0.0025 vs. CFA+ANES) and the CFA+SP6 group (5.0 ± 0.37; p = 0.0228 vs. CFA+ANES; Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc correction). Discussion

The present study characterized acupoint-specific physiological responses to EA in a rat model of CFA-induced inflammatory arthritis, examining differences in nociceptive behavior, synovial pathology, and systemic metabolic profiles between EA at ST36 and SP6. The principal findings were threefold. First, EA at ST36, but not SP6, significantly improved pain-related functional impairment, whereas both acupoints reduced synovial inflammation — a physiological dissociation suggesting that acupoint identity determines the central neuromodulatory pathway engaged, rather than the local anti-inflammatory response. Second, targeted serum metabolomics revealed 14 significantly altered metabolites, among which 2-HBA showed the largest fold-change and was selectively normalized by EA at ST36, reflecting acupoint-specific restoration of redox-metabolic homeostasis. Third, intra-articular administration of 2-HBA did not alter pain behavior, indicating that elevated 2-HBA represents a systemic physiological consequence of inflammatory-metabolic burden rather than a direct nociceptive signal.

Acupoint-specific analgesic effects of ST36 and SP6

Repeated EA at ST36 produced a robust and progressive recovery of hindlimb weight-bearing beginning at PID 3, with maximal effects observed during PID 5–7. In contrast, EA at SP6 showed only a non-significant trend toward improvement and did not reach statistical significance at any time point. These findings indicate that, under the present experimental conditions, ST36 exerts a clear analgesic effect, whereas SP6 does not produce measurable functional recovery despite a similar experimental context. This distinction highlights the importance of acupoint-specific mechanisms in modulating pain behavior.

Neuroanatomical basis of differential analgesia between ST36 and SP6

Histological analysis demonstrated that both ST36 and SP6 significantly reduced synovial inflammation, with no difference between the two acupoints in synovitis scores. This dissociation between histological improvement and functional recovery is a key finding of the present study. A plausible neuroanatomical explanation lies in the different peripheral innervation territories of these two acupoints. ST36, located over the tibialis anterior muscle, is innervated primarily by the deep peroneal nerve and the common peroneal nerve, with afferent input projecting to L4-L5 spinal segments [21]. This segmental correspondence closely matches the articular innervation of the knee joint and the central nociceptive processing circuitry for hindlimb pain. In contrast, SP6, situated over the medial aspect of the lower leg, is innervated predominantly by the saphenous nerve and the tibial nerve, which project to partially overlapping but distinct spinal levels (L3-S1), and may engage different spinal and supraspinal circuits [22]. Direct comparisons of ST36 and SP6 support the existence of such acupoint-specific effects independent of segmental overlap: functional neuroimaging has shown that although ST36 and SP6 project to overlapping spinal segments, their stimulation evokes a distinct pattern of central activation not observed with acupoints from other segments [23], and direct experimental comparisons of the two points have revealed differential effects on visceral physiological outcomes, including gastrointestinal radiotracer bioavailability [24] and small intestinal myoelectric activity [25]. These differences in segmental distribution, although partially overlapping, may contribute to why ST36 more effectively modulates dorsal horn nociceptive processing, descending inhibitory pathways, and supraspinal pain-regulatory circuits. An additional and potentially important factor is the tissue composition at the two acupoint sites: ST36 overlies the tibialis anterior muscle, which is rich in group III and IV muscle afferents (Aδ and C fibers) critical for EA-induced analgesia, whereas SP6 is situated over predominantly bony and tendinous structures where the density and distribution of deep tissue afferents differ substantially. This difference in recruited afferent fiber type and quality may independently contribute to the differential central modulatory effects. Together, both the partial segmental difference and the tissue composition difference likely contribute to the observed differential analgesic and metabolic outcomes. The absence of significant analgesic effects in the SP6 group, despite clear suppression of synovial inflammation, thus indicates that reduction of local inflammatory pathology alone is insufficient to restore pain-related function. In this context, it is also noteworthy that the SP6 group itself served as an active comparator to ST36, providing functional evidence for acupoint specificity without the confound of needle insertion artifact inherent to sham acupuncture controls.

Serum 2-HBA as the principal treatment-responsive metabolite

Serum metabolomics analysis revealed broad alterations in amino acids, organic acids, and fatty acids in CFA-induced arthritis, reflecting systemic metabolic perturbation associated with inflammatory pain. Among the 14 significantly altered metabolites, 2-HBA was particularly notable because it exhibited the largest fold-change and a distinct pattern of normalization. Serum 2-HBA levels were markedly elevated in CFA+ANES animals, remained elevated in the CFA+SP6 group, and were selectively reduced toward control levels in the CFA+ST36 group. Importantly, this pattern paralleled the presence of significant functional recovery only in the ST36 group, suggesting that 2-HBA is more closely associated with effective analgesic response than with inflammation per se. This acupoint-specific pattern of 2-HBA normalization may be mechanistically linked to the differential properties of ST36 and SP6 discussed in Section 4.2: effective central modulation by ST36, mediated through favorable segmental alignment and muscle afferent recruitment, may reduce the systemic pro-inflammatory and oxidative burden that drives 2-HBA elevation via the transsulfuration-glutathione pathway, whereas the less effective central modulation by SP6 allows ongoing redox imbalance and sustained 2-HBA elevation despite comparable local anti-inflammatory effects.

Redox-metabolic basis of 2-HBA elevation and normalization

The biological relevance of 2-HBA in this context is supported by its role in redox-related metabolism. 2-HBA is generated from alpha-ketobutyrate during threonine and methionine catabolism and is closely linked to the transsulfuration pathway and glutathione synthesis (Supplementary Figure 1 C) [30]. Under conditions of increased oxidative stress, enhanced demand for glutathione can increase flux through this pathway, resulting in elevated circulating 2-HBA levels. Elevated serum 2-HBA has previously been described as a marker of systemic metabolic stress and impaired glucose oxidation in the contexts of insulin resistance, pre-diabetes, and cardiovascular risk, with prospective studies demonstrating that higher fasting 2-HBA predicts incident type 2 diabetes and relates to elevated oxidative burden in non-diabetic individuals [13], [26]. The present study extends this established metabolic context to inflammatory arthritis and acupuncture research, suggesting for the first time that 2-HBA dynamics may reflect EA-induced normalization of systemic redox-metabolic homeostasis. In inflammatory arthritis, where oxidative and metabolic disturbances are prominent, the elevation of 2-HBA observed in CFA-treated animals likely reflects a systemic response to inflammatory burden. The selective reduction of 2-HBA in the ST36 group is therefore consistent with normalization of redox-associated metabolic imbalance accompanying effective treatment. However, because direct measures of oxidative stress were not performed in this study, this interpretation should be considered inferential.

Additional support for this interpretation comes from transcriptomic analyses of inflammatory conditions. In our supplementary bioinformatic analysis of a rheumatoid arthritis dataset, genes involved in glutathione metabolism, including CBS, GCLC, GCLM, and LDHA, were significantly upregulated, together with enrichment of oxidative stress-related pathways (Supplementary Figure 1 A, B, D). These findings demonstrate that enhanced flux through glutathione-related pathways is not limited to the rodent CFA model but is also a feature of human RA, thereby providing clinical plausibility for the elevated 2-HBA observed in our animal model and supporting the translational relevance of targeting this pathway. Because 2-HBA elevations arise through the same glutathione-linked biochemical route, the RA transcriptomics data provide mechanistic coherence between the human disease context and the metabolite changes observed in the present study. Nonetheless, these observations should be regarded as supportive rather than definitive, and further studies incorporating direct biochemical measurements will be necessary to confirm the link between EA, redox regulation, and 2-HBA dynamics.

The acupoint-specific normalization of 2-HBA further suggests that systemic metabolic responses may differ according to the functional efficacy of EA. While both ST36 and SP6 reduced synovial inflammation, only ST36 produced significant behavioral recovery and corresponding normalization of 2-HBA. This pattern indicates that serum 2-HBA may reflect the overall effectiveness of treatment at the level of integrated physiological response, rather than local inflammatory changes alone. From this perspective, 2-HBA may serve as a candidate biomarker for functional therapeutic efficacy in acupuncture treatment of inflammatory arthritis.

2-HBA as a candidate biomarker for EA efficacy

The 2-HBA injection experiments provide additional insight into its functional role. Intra-articular administration of 2-HBA did not induce or exacerbate pain behavior either before or after CFA-induced arthritis, indicating that it does not act as a direct local pronociceptive mediator under the conditions tested. This dissociation supports the interpretation that 2-HBA is a downstream metabolic consequence of inflammatory and oxidative stress rather than a causative factor in pain generation [31], [32]. However, these results should be interpreted with caution, as the experiment assessed only acute effects following local administration, and more complex systemic or chronic roles of 2-HBA cannot be excluded. Additionally, the weight-bearing values in Fig. 7B (∼60–65%) appear somewhat higher than those of the CFA+ANES group in Fig. 2B at the equivalent time point. We consider this apparent discrepancy unlikely to reflect a genuine analgesic effect of either injected solution, for three reasons. First, Fig. 2B and Fig. 7 represent independent experiments performed in separate, non-randomized cohorts under different designs: Fig. 2B reflects the primary EA efficacy time-course in the main study cohort, whereas the Fig. 7 experiment was performed in a separate cohort specifically to test intra-articular 2-HBA at a single defined time point (PID 8) following CFA injection alone, and the two experiments were not designed or powered for direct comparison. Second, within the Fig. 7B experiment itself, the DW and 2-HBA groups did not differ in weight-bearing ratio at baseline, prior to injection, indicating an equivalent degree of residual arthritis between groups at the outset. Third, if 2-HBA had exerted a delayed pronociceptive effect, the 2-HBA-injected group specifically — not the DW-injected group — would be expected to show a lower weight-bearing ratio than the Fig. 2B reference value; instead, both groups showed comparably elevated weight-bearing, arguing against a 2-HBA-specific effect. Consistent with this, both the 2-HBA and DW groups showed essentially identical weight-bearing profiles at 30 min and 1 h post-injection, confirming that 2-HBA itself does not exacerbate or alleviate pain beyond effects common to both injected groups.

Taken together, the present findings support 2-HBA as a candidate serum biomarker for assessing EA efficacy in inflammatory arthritis. Notably, 2-HBA was elevated in the disease state, showed the largest metabolic shift among altered metabolites, and was selectively normalized in the group exhibiting significant functional recovery. Unlike conventional inflammatory markers, 2-HBA may provide insight into systemic metabolic and redox-related aspects of treatment response - a dimension that has not been captured by conventional outcome measures in acupuncture research. However, validation in larger cohorts, multiple time points, and clinical populations will be required to establish its utility as a biomarker.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the metabolomics analysis was conducted with a relatively small sample size (n = 4 per group), which may limit statistical robustness and generalizability. In particular, the correlation analyses between serum 2-HBA and weight-bearing outcomes revealed a consistent negative trend but did not reach statistical significance, likely due to insufficient power at this sample size. Second, serum metabolites were measured at a single time point, precluding temporal analysis of metabolic changes during disease progression and treatment [33], [34]. Third, tissue-specific metabolic changes were not assessed, limiting mechanistic interpretation at the local level. Fourth, direct measurements of oxidative stress and antioxidant capacity were not performed, so conclusions regarding redox regulation remain indirect. Fifth, the CFA model reflects inflammatory arthritis-like pathology and does not fully represent chronic osteoarthritis. Finally, blinding was not feasible during EA administration due to acupoint-specific procedures, and sham acupuncture controls were not included; however, the inclusion of SP6 as an active acupuncture comparator provides functional evidence for acupoint specificity and partially mitigates the absence of a sham condition, as the differential outcomes between two needle-insertion groups cannot be attributed to needling artifact alone. In addition, the high inter-individual variance observed in the SP6 behavioral data may reflect technically more demanding afferent recruitment at this bony/tendinous site; future studies should include larger sample sizes and optimize stimulation parameters for SP6 before concluding that it lacks analgesic potential in this model.

In conclusion, EA at ST36, but not SP6, significantly improved pain-related function despite comparable reductions in synovial inflammation in CFA-induced knee arthritis, an acupoint-specific dissociation that may be explained in part by distinct peripheral innervation territories and segmental spinal inputs. These effects were accompanied by distinct changes in serum metabolite profiles, among which 2-HBA emerged as a key candidate associated with functional therapeutic response. Because 2-HBA did not directly induce pain behavior, it is more plausibly interpreted as a marker of inflammatory-metabolic burden and treatment efficacy rather than a direct mediator of nociception. These findings suggest that serum metabolomics may provide a useful framework for identifying objective, redox-sensitive biomarkers of acupuncture efficacy - an approach that could ultimately support the development of precision acupuncture protocols in which treatment selection is guided by metabolic phenotyping. These findings demonstrate that acupoint identity shapes the systemic physiological response to EA, with distinct metabolic outcomes arising from stimulation at sites with different segmental innervation. Future studies incorporating direct measures of redox status, autonomic function, and neurohumoral mediators across multiple time points will be required to fully characterize the physiological pathways by which acupoint-specific EA normalizes systemic metabolic homeostasis in inflammatory disease.
