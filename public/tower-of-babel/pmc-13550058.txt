Robot-assisted versus manual percutaneous vascular interventions across vascular territories: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaotian Wu, Shuaiwei Guo, Yi Zhang, Xiao Zhang, Yingfeng Li, Xinjie Lin, Shengyan Cui, Zixuan Xing, Linsen Zhang, Xiangyu Li, Yixin Sun, Jichang Luo, Mingqian Qin, Liqun Jiao, Shiqi Liu, Tao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRobot-assisted percutaneous vascular intervention (R-PVI) has expanded beyond coronary procedures, but previous reviews were largely coronary-focused and observational. Recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs) warrant broader reassessment of R-PVI versus manual percutaneous vascular intervention (M-PVI) across vascular territories. PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials were searched from database inception to January 31, 2026, following PRISMA guidelines. RCTs and observational studies including ≥10 adult patients in total were eligible. Comparative studies informed primary analyses, while single-arm studies provided supportive evidence. Primary outcomes were clinical success rate and major adverse cardiovascular/cerebrovascular events (MACE) rate. Secondary outcomes included mortality rate, technical success rate, procedural time metrics, contrast volume, and radiation exposure. Random-effects models were used. Forty studies were included: 3 RCTs, 10 comparative observational studies, and 27 single-arm observational studies, comprising 3,870 patients undergoing R-PVI and 1,142 undergoing M-PVI. Comparative analyses showed similar clinical success rates (RR 1.00, P = 0.46), MACE rates (RR 0.72, P = 0.43), and mortality. Single-arm pooled estimates for clinical and technical success were 98.76% and 96.09%, respectively. R-PVI prolonged total procedure time overall (MD 15.92 min, P = 0.01), with consistent increases in the neurovascular, RCT, and non-RCT subgroups. Fluoroscopy time was also longer (MD 1.91 min, P = 0.04), mainly in the RCT subgroup (MD 2.83 min, P = 0.001). In contrast, intravascular intervention time was unchanged overall and in RCTs, but was prolonged in non-RCTs (MD 8.72 min, P = 0.006). Operator radiation exposure was markedly reduced (MD −33.97 μSv, P < 0.001), whereas patient radiation exposure and contrast volume were similar. R-PVI appears feasible and safe across selected vascular procedures. Introduction

Cardiovascular, neurovascular, and peripheral vascular diseases collectively account for a substantial share of deaths and disability worldwide, placing sustained pressure on health-care systems [1–3]. Percutaneous vascular intervention (PVI) has become a cornerstone of contemporary treatment because of its minimally invasive nature and proven clinical efficacy [4–8]. However, conventional manual percutaneous vascular intervention (M-PVI) still faces several important limitations. First, the technical complexity and steep learning curve of these procedures restrict their wider dissemination, particularly in resource-limited settings [9]. Second, even in experienced hands, manual manipulation is inherently constrained by physiologic tremor, operator fatigue, and limited visual–haptic precision, which may affect procedural accuracy in complex cases [10]. The occupational burden is also substantial: repeated exposure to scattered radiation and prolonged work in heavy protective apparel contribute to cumulative radiation risk and work-related musculoskeletal injury [11, 12].

Robotic technology has been introduced into PVI in response to advances in robotics and the growing need to overcome these limitations [13, 14]. In these systems, the primary operator controls interventional devices from a shielded console rather than remaining continuously at the table, thereby increasing separation from the radiation field and potentially reducing the physical demands of prolonged bedside work. At the same time, robotic platforms may provide more stable and precise control of guidewires, catheters, and treatment devices, which could be particularly advantageous during technically demanding procedures [15]. Building on these potential advantages, encouraging early clinical experience has led to the gradual expansion of robot-assisted percutaneous vascular intervention (R-PVI) from coronary applications to neurovascular and peripheral vascular settings, suggesting a broader role for this technology in contemporary endovascular practice [16–20].

Despite this promise, the clinical value of R-PVI remains uncertain. Existing studies have yielded mixed results: reductions in operator radiation exposure have been reported consistently, but evidence regarding procedural efficiency, technical performance, and clinical outcomes remains inconclusive [21–24]. Much of the available literature consists of small cohorts, uncontrolled series, and narrowly selected procedures, which limits both the precision and generalizability of the evidence. Several previous evidence syntheses have only partly addressed these uncertainties. Most were restricted to coronary interventions, evaluated a limited range of clinical outcomes, or relied predominantly on observational studies, thereby providing an incomplete assessment of the field [25, 26]. With continued technological development and the emergence of recent randomized controlled trials (RCTs), a broader and updated synthesis is needed to evaluate safety, effectiveness, procedural performance, and radiation-related outcomes across vascular territories.

To address these gaps, we synthesized comparative evidence on R-PVI versus M-PVI across coronary, neurovascular, and peripheral vascular procedures. We assessed clinical success, safety, procedure-related efficiency, contrast use, and radiation exposure, while pooling single-arm R-PVI data separately to describe feasibility and current platform performance. This cross-territory approach was designed to clarify which benefits are consistently supported, which outcomes remain uncertain, and which limitations may influence wider clinical adoption.

Methods

Study design and registration

Reporting followed the PRISMA 2020 statement, while methodological decisions were guided by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [27, 28]. The protocol was entered in PROSPERO before screening began (CRD420251231572) and was subsequently published [29].

Eligibility criteria

RCTs and observational studies evaluating R-PVI in adults with cardiovascular, neurovascular, or peripheral vascular disease were included. Both comparative studies (R-PVI vs. M-PVI) and single-arm R-PVI studies were eligible. We excluded case reports, case series with fewer than 10 patients overall, reviews, conference abstracts, editorials, animal studies, and evaluations of robotic platforms limited to catheter steering or navigation without robotic guidewire advancement, such as the Hansen Medical Magellan system. All studies published up to January 31, 2026 were considered eligible, and no language restrictions were imposed.

This threshold of including at least 10 participants was applied to the entire study population rather than to each treatment arm. It was used as a pragmatic evidence-scope criterion to distinguish cohort-level evidence from case reports and very small exploratory series, rather than as a universally validated minimum sample-size requirement. Similar thresholds have been applied in systematic reviews of endovascular procedures [30, 31]. This consideration was particularly relevant for outcomes approaching 0% or 100%, for which estimates from very small series are highly unstable.

Search strategy and study selection

PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials were searched from their respective inception dates through January 31, 2026. Reference lists of relevant articles and eligible trial registries were examined to identify additional records. Database-specific queries incorporated controlled vocabulary, including Medical Subject Headings and Emtree terms, together with free-text expressions covering robotic technology and endovascular procedures. Search terms included, but were not limited to, “Robotics,” “Robotic Surgical Procedures,” “robot*,” “robot-assisted,” “robotic system,” “robot-enhanced,” “Endovascular Procedures,” “Interventional Radiology,” “Catheterization,” “Vascular Surgical Procedures,” “Percutaneous Coronary Intervention,” “Angioplasty,” “Stenting,” “Thrombectomy,” “endovascular,” “catheter-based,” “percutaneous,” and “intravascular.” The complete strategy for each database is presented in Supplementary File 1. After duplicate removal, 2 reviewers (XW and XZ) separately assessed records at the title-and-abstract stage and subsequently reviewed potentially eligible full-text reports. Disagreements were first discussed between the two reviewers; cases that remained unresolved were referred to a third reviewer. Figure 1 summarizes the selection pathway.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were clinical success rate and major adverse cardiovascular/cerebrovascular events (MACE) rate. Clinical success was defined as the successful completion of the intended procedure without major peri-procedural complications. MACE encompassed all-cause death, myocardial infarction, stroke, or unplanned target-vessel revascularization occurring during the periprocedural period or within 30 days of intervention. Secondary endpoints addressed technical performance, procedural efficiency, contrast use, and radiation exposure. Technical success indicated completion of the planned robotic procedure without unplanned conversion to manual intervention. Total procedure time generally extended from establishment of vascular access to sheath removal. Intravascular intervention time represented the core catheter-based phase, beginning with guide-catheter engagement and ending with its removal, and usually excluded initial robotic setup. Fluoroscopy time referred to cumulative fluoroscopic exposure, whereas contrast volume represented the total amount of contrast medium administered. Radiation outcomes comprised operator exposure and patient exposure, with the latter assessed using air kerma (AK) and dose–area product (DAP).

Because endpoint definitions varied across studies, study-specific definitions of clinical success, technical success, MACE/safety composites, total procedure time, intravascular intervention time, and radiation exposure are summarized in Supplementary Tables 7A and 7B. For procedural-time endpoints, the reported start and end points and the inclusion of workflow components such as vascular access, robotic setup/docking, or device exchange are also documented when specified in the original studies.

Data extraction

Two reviewers separately extracted the relevant information into a structured electronic data form. The collected items covered study design and setting, participant characteristics, procedural details, and all prespecified outcomes. Missing data were sought from corresponding authors when necessary. The two extractors reconciled differences by reviewing the source article together, with unresolved cases adjudicated by a third reviewer.

For non-randomized comparative studies, propensity score–matched analytic populations or otherwise adjusted comparative data were preferentially extracted when available. Unadjusted data were used only when matched or adjusted data were unavailable.

Risk-of-bias assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated separately by two reviewers using tools selected according to study design. Randomized trials were examined with the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool (RoB 2), whereas non-randomized comparative studies were assessed with the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions tool (ROBINS-I). Disagreements were reconsidered jointly, and a third reviewer adjudicated any unresolved rating.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative analyses were undertaken in R version 4.1.3 and Review Manager version 5.4.1. Because variation was expected across vascular territories, study designs, procedures, and robotic platforms, random-effects pooling was prespecified. Comparative binary outcomes were expressed as risk ratios (RRs), whereas continuous variables were summarized as mean differences (MDs); both estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Uncontrolled R-PVI cohorts were synthesized separately to obtain pooled proportions or pooled means with corresponding 95% CIs. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran’s Q and the I2 statistic. For outcomes represented by at least 10 studies, potential small-study effects were examined using funnel plots together with Egger’s and Begg’s tests. Dependence of the pooled estimates on individual studies was explored through leave-one-out analyses. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, prespecified subgroup analyses were performed according to vascular territory (cardiovascular, neurovascular, and peripheral vascular), study design (RCTs vs non-RCTs), and robotic platform (CorPath 200, CorPath GRX, and other systems). For safety outcomes, including MACE and all-cause mortality, subgroup analyses were performed according to follow-up duration (in-hospital and 30-day follow-up). For outcomes with sufficient numbers of studies, univariable meta-regression was additionally undertaken using publication year, mean patient age, and proportion of male participants as candidate moderators. Evidence certainty for the outcomes was rated according to the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.

Results

Study selection and baseline characteristics

A systematic search of four electronic databases—PubMed (n = 3,133), Embase (n = 6,217), Web of Science (n = 2,714), and the Cochrane Library (n = 160)—identified 12,224 records. Deduplication removed 4,381 records, leaving 7,843 for title and abstract screening. Full-text reports were obtained for 265 potentially relevant records. Of these, 225 were excluded, principally because they were trial registration records (n = 28), inappropriate study design (n = 67), conference abstracts only (n = 50), ineligible populations (n = 21), follow-up studies (n = 6), and potential duplicate data (n = 11). Ultimately, the remaining 40 studies formed the final quantitative evidence base. Figure 1 presents the complete selection pathway.

Among the 40 included studies, 3 were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (Lu [32], Yu [21], and Zhang [22]), whereas the remaining 37 were observational studies. Overall, 23 studies adopted a prospective design and 17 were retrospective. Thirteen studies provided direct comparisons between R-PVI and M-PVI, while 27 were single-arm studies evaluating R-PVI only. The included studies encompassed three major clinical areas: coronary procedures (n = 26), predominantly percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), with one study evaluating diagnostic coronary angiography; neurovascular interventions (n = 12), including diagnostic cerebral angiography (DCA; n = 4), carotid artery stenting (CAS; n = 3), aneurysm treatment (n = 2), mixed studies of DCA and CAS (n = 2), and spinal angiography (n = 1); and peripheral vascular interventions (n = 2). The robotic platforms evaluated were CorPath GRX (n = 26), CorPath 200 (n = 6), ETcath200 (n = 2), PANVIS-A (n = 1), Robocath R-One (n = 3), the VIR-2 prototype (n = 1), and YDHB-NS01 (n = 1). Across all included studies, 3,870 patients underwent R-PVI and 1,142 underwent M-PVI. Most studies were published between 2017 and 2025. Risk of bias assessment

RoB 2 evaluation assigned an overall rating of “some concerns” to each of the three RCTs. In every trial, this judgment arose from the domain concerning deviations from the intended intervention, reflecting the practical inability to blind operators and participants during interventional procedures. Ratings were low risk for other RoB 2 domains in all three trials.

Among the ten non-randomized comparative studies evaluated with ROBINS-I, six received an overall moderate-risk rating and four received a serious-risk rating. Confounding related to non-random treatment allocation was the principal concern. The four studies rated as serious overall also had serious confounding concerns, mainly because propensity score matching or an equivalent method of adjustment was not used. Several studies had additional moderate concerns regarding deviations from the intended intervention, and one had moderate risk in outcome measurement. Ratings in the remaining ROBINS-I domains were generally low risk. Figure 2 presents the study-level judgments.

Meta‑analysis results

Clinical success rate

Clinical success rates were reported in 12 comparative studies comprising 1,550 patients. The pooled estimate was neutral between R-PVI and M-PVI (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.99–1.01; P = 0.46), with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) (Table 2; Fig. 3). None of the prespecified subgroup comparisons materially altered this result. Separately, 26 uncontrolled R-PVI studies involving 3,609 patients yielded a pooled clinical success rate of 98.76% (95% CI 97.68%–99.34%; I2 = 0%). No significant subgroup effect was observed according to vascular territory (test for subgroup differences, P = 0.4889) (Supplementary Table 5).

MACE

MACE data were available from 11 comparative studies encompassing 1,786 patients. R-PVI was not associated with a statistically detectable change in MACE relative to M-PVI (RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.31–1.64; P = 0.43; I2 = 0%) (Table 2; Fig. 4). Similar findings were observed in subgroup analyses of 30-day and in-hospital MACE, both of which showed no significant between-group differences (Supplementary Table 5). In addition, a pooled single-arm analysis of 30 studies reporting R-PVI outcomes, comprising 3,504 patients, showed a very low overall MACE rate of 0.66% (95% CI 0.28%–1.55%; I2 = 12.9%) (Supplementary Table 5).

MACE with R-PVI versus M-PVI, stratified by vascular territory

All-cause mortality

The same 11 comparative studies, comprising 1,786 patients, reported all-cause mortality. Only one death occurred across these studies, resulting in an imprecise comparative estimate with no statistically detectable difference between R-PVI and M-PVI (RR 0.33, 95% CI 0.01–8.15; P = 0.50) (Table 2). Furthermore, pooled single-arm analyses of 29 studies consistently showed that mortality remained extremely low following R-PVI (0.03%) (Supplementary Table 5).

Technical success rate

A total of 38 studies involving 4,104 procedures reported the technical success rate of robot-assisted interventions. A pooled single-arm analysis revealed an overall technical success rate of 96.09% (95% CI 93.42%-97.71%), with moderate between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 36.4%, p = 0.0148). Rates exceeded 95% in each vascular territory, without evidence of a between-subgroup difference (p = 0.5514) (Supplementary Table 5).

Total procedure time

Total procedure time was available from 23 studies, four of which directly compared R-PVI with M-PVI. In the comparative synthesis, robotic intervention prolonged total procedure time by 15.92 min (95% CI 3.27–28.57; P = 0.01), although heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 88%) (Table 2; Fig. 5). Longer procedure times were also observed in the neurovascular subgroup and in analyses stratified into RCTs and non-RCTs (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2).

Total procedure time with R-PVI versus M-PVI, stratified by vascular territory

In addition, a single-arm pooled analysis of 23 studies showed that the mean total procedure time for R-PVI was 64.99 min (95% CI 54.19–75.80; I2 = 98.9%). Among vascular territories, the neurovascular subgroup had the longest total procedure time (92.65 min), followed by the cardiovascular subgroup (59.31 min) and the peripheral vascular subgroup (45.81 min). Significant differences in total procedure time were observed across subgroups (Supplementary Table 5).

Intravascular intervention time

A total of 26 studies reported intravascular intervention time, 10 of which were comparative studies. The overall comparative estimate did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between R-PVI and M-PVI (MD = 4.58 min, 95% CI −0.95 to 10.11; P = 0.10; I2 = 84%) (Table 2; Fig. 6). However, non-RCTs favored M-PVI, with robotic procedures taking 8.72 min longer (95% CI 2.49–14.95; P = 0.006). By contrast, in the RCT subgroup, intravascular intervention time was not significantly increased and showed a trend toward reduction (MD = −1.72 min, 95% CI −5.74 to 2.30; P = 0.40) (Supplementary Table 2).

Intravascular intervention time with R-PVI versus M-PVI, stratified by vascular territory

A single-arm pooled analysis of 26 studies demonstrated a mean intravascular intervention time of 48.24 min (95% CI 39.72–56.75; I2 = 98.6%). Significant differences in intravascular intervention time were observed across vascular territories (P < 0.0001). The neurovascular subgroup had the longest duration (58.36 min), followed by the cardiovascular subgroup (43.63 min), whereas the peripheral vascular subgroup had the shortest duration (26.82 min) (Supplementary Table 5).

Fluoroscopy time

Fluoroscopy time was reported in 31 studies, including 12 head-to-head comparisons. R-PVI increased fluoroscopy time by a pooled mean of 1.91 min relative to M-PVI (95% CI 0.06–3.75; P = 0.04; I2 = 76%) (Table 2; Fig. 7). Stratification by study design showed that the difference was confined to the RCT subgroup (MD = 2.83 min, 95% CI 1.10–4.55; P = 0.001; I2 = 0%) (Supplementary Table 2).

Fluoroscopy time with R-PVI versus M-PVI, stratified by vascular territory

A single-arm pooled analysis of 31 studies showed a mean fluoroscopy time of 19.81 min (95% CI 16.20–23.41). Similarly, significant differences in fluoroscopy time were observed across vascular territory subgroups. The neurovascular subgroup had the longest duration (25.41 min), followed by the cardiovascular subgroup (18.35 min), whereas the peripheral vascular subgroup had the shortest duration (7.06 min) (Supplementary Table 5).

Patient radiation exposure

Patient air kerma was reported in 16 studies, including four comparative studies, whereas dose–area product was available from 14 studies, including eight comparisons. Neither patient-radiation measure differed between R-PVI and M-PVI: the pooled MD was −200.88 mGy for AK (95% CI −417.19 to 15.44; P = 0.07; I2 = 77%) and −258.04 cGy·cm2 for DAP (95% CI −937.07 to 420.99; P = 0.46; I2 = 62%) (Table 2; Figs. 8 and 9).

Patient dose–area product with R-PVI versus M-PVI, stratified by vascular territory

A single-arm pooled analysis of 16 studies showed a mean AK of 1055.8 mGy (95% CI 756.89–1354.71). Similarly, a single-arm pooled analysis of 14 studies showed a mean DAP of 6006.7 cGy·cm2 (95% CI 4217.47–7795.93) (Supplementary Table 5).

Contrast agent volume

A total of 29 studies reported contrast agent volume. Pooled analysis of 11 comparative studies showed no material difference between approaches (MD = 0.80 mL, 95% CI −5.01 to 6.61; P = 0.79; I2 = 29%) (Table 2; Fig. 10).

Contrast agent volume with R-PVI versus M-PVI, stratified by vascular territory

Across 29 single-arm R-PVI studies, the pooled mean contrast volume was 130.67 mL (95% CI 113.58–147.76). Mean values differed across vascular territories: 141.21 mL for cardiovascular procedures, 114.41 mL for neurovascular procedures, and 65.91 mL for peripheral vascular procedures (Supplementary Table 5).

Operator radiation exposure

Three comparative studies reported operator radiation exposure. Relative to manual intervention, R-PVI reduced exposure by a pooled mean of 33.97 μSv (95% CI −47.95 to −19.99; P < 0.001; I2 = 83%) (Table 2; Fig. 11).

Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out analyses showed that some secondary outcomes were influenced by individual studies. The direction of effect remained toward longer procedure time, but statistical significance was lost after exclusion of Zhang 2025, indicating limited robustness of the overall estimate [22]. The pooled estimate for intravascular intervention time became significant after exclusion of Yu [21], whereas for fluoroscopy time, significance was not driven by any single study, but was lost after removal of several individual studies, suggesting limited robustness. The effect estimate for air kerma became significant after exclusion of Hirai et al [33] or Yu [21]. In contrast, the pooled reduction in operator radiation exposure remained stable throughout the leave-one-out analyses. These findings suggest that the reduction in operator radiation exposure associated with R-PVI is robust, whereas the evidence for procedure-related efficiency outcomes and patient radiation exposure is less stable.

Publication bias

Assessment of publication bias showed no clear evidence of significant small-study effects for most outcomes with available statistical testing, including contrast agent volume, fluoroscopy time, and intravascular intervention time. However, for clinical success, although Egger’s test was not significant, Begg’s test indicated potential asymmetry (P = 0.040), suggesting that small-study effects or selective reporting cannot be completely ruled out for this endpoint. More generally, the limited number of studies contributing to several outcomes reduced the power of the available tests, preventing publication bias from being confidently excluded (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Meta-regression analysis

Univariable meta-regression was used to examine heterogeneity in fluoroscopy time and intravascular intervention time. For fluoroscopy time, publication year was identified as a significant moderator (β = 0.6952, SE = 0.3163, 95% CI 0.0753 to 1.3152; P = 0.0279), explaining 37.80% of the between-study heterogeneity. In contrast, mean patient age and the proportion of male patients were not significantly associated with fluoroscopy time. For intravascular intervention time, none of the tested covariates reached statistical significance, although mean patient age showed a borderline association (β = 0.9712, SE = 0.5853, 95% CI −0.1759 to 2.1183; P = 0.0970). These findings suggest that publication year may partly explain the heterogeneity observed for fluoroscopy time, whereas the heterogeneity in intravascular intervention time could not be adequately explained by the covariates examined in the present meta-regression (Supplementary Table 6).

Certainty of evidence

GRADE ratings differed according to study design and outcome. In randomized trials, evidence certainty was high for total procedure time, fluoroscopy time, and operator radiation exposure, but low for clinical success, MACE, intravascular intervention time, air kerma, and contrast agent volume, mainly because of risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision (Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, evidence from non-randomized comparative studies was rated as very low across all outcomes, largely due to potential confounding, serious risk of bias, heterogeneity, and imprecision (Supplementary Fig. 3). Therefore, the current evidence most robustly supports a reduction in operator radiation exposure with R-PVI, whereas estimates for clinical outcomes, patient radiation exposure, and procedural efficiency remain less certain.

Discussion

Across the comparative evidence base, R-PVI and M-PVI yielded similar clinical success and short-term safety outcomes. The clearest signal favoring R-PVI was lower operator radiation exposure; patient radiation dose and contrast volume, however, were comparable between approaches. Comparative analyses suggested longer total procedure and fluoroscopy times with R-PVI, although these estimates showed substantial heterogeneity and limited robustness in sensitivity analyses. The duration of the core intravascular intervention was similar, raising the possibility that workflow-related components contribute to the observed difference, although heterogeneous procedural-time definitions preclude firm attribution. Single-arm evidence further supported the procedural feasibility of R-PVI, with high pooled clinical and technical success rates, although these uncontrolled estimates should be interpreted cautiously.

Compared with previous syntheses, which largely focused on coronary intervention and relied predominantly on observational evidence, the present study provides an RCT-era reassessment across coronary, neurovascular, and peripheral vascular procedures. Its contribution is therefore not merely the inclusion of additional studies, but a refinement of how the existing evidence should be interpreted [25, 34]. In contrast to earlier suggestions of broader procedural and patient-side radiation benefits, the current evidence most robustly supports occupational radiation protection, while reductions in patient radiation exposure, contrast volume, and procedural duration remain unproven [25, 26]. Moreover, separating total procedure time from intravascular intervention time indicates that the observed efficiency disadvantage may be workflow-related and potentially modifiable. Nevertheless, because the evidence remains dominated by observational studies and is limited outside coronary intervention, cross-territory estimates should be regarded as complementary rather than definitive.

Current data indicate that R-PVI can be completed successfully in appropriately selected procedures. Clinical success was comparable between robotic and manual intervention, while the pooled technical success rate exceeded 95%, indicating that the planned robotic component could be completed in most procedures. A small proportion of procedures still required bedside assistance or conversion to manual operation, usually because of challenging anatomy, inadequate guide support, limited working length, device-delivery difficulty, mechanical malfunction, or incompatibility with required techniques [15, 18, 35–37]. In particular, device compatibility also remains an important constraint. Most current robotic systems were initially designed for coronary intervention and are therefore optimized for standardized guidewire and rapid-exchange device workflows, rather than for the broader device compatibility, greater distal reach, and complex catheter coordination often required in neurovascular procedures [15, 22, 35]. Robotic intervention has also been attempted in technically demanding settings, such as bifurcation disease, heavily calcified segments, chronic total occlusion, carotid artery stenting, and aneurysm embolization; however, these procedures appear more likely than routine cases to require manual support or conversion [13, 33, 36]. Taken together, these findings suggest that current robotic systems are feasible and safe in selected vascular procedures, but their routine use will likely remain case-dependent until further refinements in device compatibility, mechanical reliability, and workflow integration are achieved.

The short-term safety findings were similarly reassuring, with no significant differences in MACE or mortality between robotic and manual intervention and low event rates in single-arm studies. Moreover, limited comparative data also showed no evident excess in adverse outcomes at 1 year after robotic PCI [23, 24]. The consistently favorable safety outcomes observed in our analysis help alleviate concerns that the absence of haptic feedback in robotic systems might increase the risk of vascular injury, dissection, or perforation. Several factors may help explain why safety outcomes remained favorable despite this limitation. Mechanical stability, incremental device control, continuous fluoroscopic visualization, and elimination of physiological tremor may partly compensate for this limitation [35, 38]. More importantly, the favorable safety profile probably reflects careful patient selection, experienced operators, continued bedside oversight, and selective manual takeover during technically demanding steps. Accordingly, our results support the short-term safety of R-PVI, but they should not be interpreted to mean that current systems have fully overcome the sensory and technical limitations inherent to endovascular robotics.

Procedural efficiency requires a more nuanced interpretation than simply concluding that robotic procedures are slower. In the pooled analysis, R-PVI prolonged total procedure time, whereas intravascular intervention time—which more closely reflects the core intravascular phase of catheter, guidewire, and device manipulation—did not differ overall. This pattern is compatible with a possible contribution from workflow-related components, such as device loading and exchange, robotic-arm docking, transitions between bedside and console, and team coordination. However, definitions of these time metrics varied across studies, and the two outcomes were not reported by identical study sets; therefore, the pooled data cannot definitively distinguish the contribution of workflow-related processes from that of core intravascular manipulation [13, 18, 21]. Thus, although robotic intervention currently appears less time-efficient at the whole-procedure level, the available evidence does not clearly indicate that the core intravascular phase itself is intrinsically slower.

Subgroup patterns further suggest that this time penalty is influenced by the learning curve and workflow maturity. Intravascular intervention time was prolonged in non-RCTs but not in RCTs, suggesting that operator experience, workflow standardization, and center-level familiarity with robotic systems may substantially influence procedural efficiency. Early clinical experience has similarly shown that selected time metrics improve as teams gain experience and robotic workflows become more standardized [18, 39]. Learning-curve effects may therefore account for part of both the observed time disadvantage and the substantial heterogeneity across studies, although differences in case complexity, vascular territory, and robotic platform are also likely contributors. Overall, current R-PVI remains less efficient at the whole-procedure level, but the available evidence suggests that this limitation is at least partly modifiable through experience and workflow optimization.

Fluoroscopy time showed a similar pattern. The pooled analysis showed a modest increase with R-PVI; although this association was not driven by any single study, its statistical significance was lost after the exclusion of several individual studies in leave-one-out analyses, indicating limited robustness. The increase in fluoroscopy time may again reflect workflow inefficiencies during the robotic learning phase, more frequent confirmation imaging during remote manipulation, or additional imaging required when transitioning between robotic and manual steps [23, 39]. Nevertheless, this increase did not translate into higher patient radiation exposure, as neither air kerma nor dose-area product differed significantly between groups. Notably, the air kerma estimate became significantly lower with R-PVI after exclusion of two individual studies, whereas the dose-area product result remained neutral, indicating uncertainty rather than a consistent patient-side radiation benefit [21, 23, 24]. Overall, current evidence does not establish that R-PVI reduces patient radiation burden, despite its robust reduction in operator exposure.

The most consistent comparative benefit of R-PVI was lower radiation exposure to the operator, although this outcome was reported in only three comparative studies and the overall pooled estimate showed substantial heterogeneity. Nevertheless, all contributing studies favored R-PVI, and the reduction remained statistically significant throughout the leave-one-out sensitivity analyses; notably, the two contributing RCTs showed no detectable heterogeneity. Shifting device control to a shielded console outside the immediate radiation field reduces the operator’s proximity to scattered radiation. This advantage is clinically meaningful given the cumulative burden of ionizing radiation exposure, musculoskeletal strain from prolonged lead apron use, and operator fatigue inherent to conventional bedside intervention [21, 22]. From the standpoint of real-world adoption, occupational protection may currently be the strongest justification for robotic vascular intervention, particularly in lengthy, complex, or high-radiation procedures.

From a practical clinical perspective, these findings suggest that R-PVI should currently be considered a selective rather than universal alternative to manual intervention. Its principal rationale may be greatest when prolonged fluoroscopy or substantial occupational radiation exposure is anticipated, provided that the procedural anatomy and required devices remain compatible with the capabilities of the robotic platform. This potential benefit must be weighed against workflow-related demands, including vascular access, robotic setup and docking, device loading and exchange, transitions between the bedside and console, the need for trained bedside assistance, and their potential effects on procedure-room turnover. Importantly, procedures associated with the greatest radiation burden are often also among the most technically demanding, creating an inherent clinical trade-off. In complex PCI, particularly CTO intervention, guidewire advancement and lesion crossing may require subtle assessment of resistance and frequent changes in wire or support strategy; the absence of tactile feedback in current robotic platforms may therefore become particularly relevant during these steps and may necessitate bedside manipulation or conversion to manual intervention. Accordingly, anticipated radiation burden alone should not determine case selection, and procedural complexity, device compatibility, operator experience, and the ability to rapidly transition to manual control should also be considered. Nevertheless, the lack of haptic feedback is not necessarily a permanent limitation of endovascular robotics. Emerging systems are incorporating force sensing, force/torque estimation, and haptic interfaces to restore or enhance operator perception of device–vessel interaction, and continued development of these technologies may progressively mitigate this limitation in future generations of robotic platforms [40, 41].

Health-economic considerations represent another important determinant of the real-world adoption of R-PVI. Available comparative evidence remains limited and heterogeneous. A secondary economic analysis of the CORA-PCI cohort found that R-PCI was associated with higher direct supply costs, largely attributable to single-use robotic components, whereas total hospitalization and catheterization-laboratory costs were not significantly different from manual PCI after adjustment [13]. In carotid artery stenting, robotic assistance was similarly associated with higher personnel, supply, and overall procedural costs [39]. Conversely, during the COVID-19 pandemic, R-PCI reduced personal protective equipment use and associated costs by reducing the number of scrubbed personnel required; however, this context-specific benefit may not be generalizable to routine practice [42]. Beyond direct procedural costs, implementation of robotic intervention requires capital investment, recurring disposable and maintenance costs, dedicated staff training, and workflow adaptation [43]. Additional setup requirements and longer whole-procedure times may also affect cath-lab throughput, particularly in high-volume centers, although these inefficiencies may decrease as teams gain experience and workflows become more standardized. Overall, the available evidence is insufficient to establish the cost-effectiveness of R-PVI or to determine whether occupational benefits, including reduced operator radiation exposure, offset the additional resources required. Future studies should therefore incorporate standardized economic evaluations that consider total system costs, staffing and training, procedural throughput, occupational health benefits, and downstream healthcare utilization.

Robotic performance varied across vascular territories. In the single-arm analyses, neurovascular procedures had longer total procedure, intravascular intervention, and fluoroscopy times than coronary or peripheral procedures. This pattern may reflect the longer and more tortuous access routes, greater reliance on multi-catheter support, and more demanding device coordination in neurointervention, together with the incomplete adaptation of predominantly coronary-designed platforms to these requirements [22, 35]. However, these cross-territory comparisons were indirect and may also have been influenced by differences in procedure type, case complexity, operator experience, and definitions of procedural time. The limited number of peripheral studies further restricts firm comparisons in this subgroup. Overall, coronary intervention remains the most mature application of vascular robotics, whereas broader adoption in neurovascular practice will require improved device compatibility, distal reach, multi-device control, and feedback mechanisms. However, the very high heterogeneity of the single-arm procedural-time estimates substantially limits their clinical interpretability. These pooled values should therefore be regarded as descriptive summaries of the currently reported R-PVI experience rather than representative procedural benchmarks, and apparent differences across vascular territories should not be interpreted as direct comparative effects.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although three recent randomized trials were included and propensity score–matched data were used in comparative observational studies where available, most of the evidence remained observational. Residual confounding, selection bias, and center-level learning-curve effects therefore could not be fully eliminated. Second, the inclusion of studies across different vascular territories inevitably resulted in some variation in the definitions of clinical and technical success. We partly addressed this limitation through vascular territory–specific subgroup analyses, although residual endpoint heterogeneity could not be completely eliminated. Third, several endpoints were informed by a small number of comparative studies and showed substantial heterogeneity, which limited statistical power and precluded reliable assessment of publication bias. The very high heterogeneity of single-arm procedural-time estimates limits their generalizability and supports their interpretation as descriptive evidence only. Fourth, most studies reported only peri-procedural or short-term outcomes, with limited data on long-term clinical endpoints. Fifth, the pragmatic exclusion of studies enrolling fewer than 10 participants may have omitted early feasibility experience or rare adverse events. Finally, quantitative pooling of economic outcomes was not feasible because the limited available studies differed substantially in their clinical settings, costing methodologies, and economic or resource-utilization endpoints. The health-economic evidence was therefore summarized narratively and should be regarded as exploratory, highlighting the need for standardized prospective economic evaluations of R-PVI.

In conclusion, R-PVI appears feasible and safe across selected vascular procedures, with clinical success and short-term adverse outcomes comparable to manual intervention. The most consistent comparative signal favoring R-PVI is reduced operator radiation exposure, although this finding is based on a limited number of studies. Comparative evidence also suggests a potential disadvantage in whole-procedure efficiency, but the magnitude and robustness of this effect remain uncertain, which likely reflected setup demands, workflow complexity, and incomplete adaptation to certain vascular territories. These findings support the continued development and selective clinical adoption of robotic vascular intervention, while underscoring the need for larger high-quality comparative studies, longer follow-up, and next-generation robotic systems better tailored to the procedural demands of pan-vascular endovascular practice.
