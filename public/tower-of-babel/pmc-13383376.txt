Efficacy of artemether-lumefantrine for uncomplicated plasmodium falciparum malaria treatment in Mozambique: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Abel Nhama, Pedro Aide, David Torres-Fernandez, Rosauro Varo, Arsénio Nhacolo, Quique Bassat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a significant public health concern in Mozambique. Early diagnosis and the prompt use of effective drugs are essential for malaria control in endemic regions. This review aims to synthesize the existing evidence concerning the efficacy of artemether-lumefantrine (AL) in treating uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum malaria in Mozambique.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis include studies evaluating the efficacy of AL using the World Health Organization standard protocol in Mozambique across children under 5 years of age and adults, regardless of publication year. The main outcome was efficacy, defined as the proportion of participants who had an adequate clinical and parasitological response, without or with Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) correction. Other outcomes included day 3 positivity rate, early treatment failure, late clinical failure and late parasitological failure. Data were obtained from electronic searches of PubMed and ScienceDirect citations. The Rayyan software assessed the adherence of all studies to the stipulated inclusion criteria. The studies' risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. Pooled efficacy was calculated based on the per-protocol population. The heterogeneity between studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic, the τ2, and the I2 statistic, and all statistical analyses were performed using the R software.ResultsSix studies, comprising 20 single-arm trials and including 1862 participants, were included in the analysis. Studies were conducted across Mozambique and enrolled both children and adults from the country’s three main regions (South, Centre, and North). The pooled uncorrected efficacy of AL was 91.0% (95% CI 84.2–94.6), while the PCR-corrected efficacy was 98.3% (95% CI 96.9–99.1). During the 28-day follow-up period, early treatment failure was observed in 0.05% (95% CI 0.01–0.38) of participants. Late clinical failure and late parasitological failure occurred in 2.73% (95% CI 1.65–4.50) and 5.3% (95% CI 3.10–8.96) of participants, respectively. Recrudescence was observed in 1.8% (95% CI 1.20–2.70) of cases, whereas new infections accounted for 8.6% (95% CI 7.30–10.10). The day 3 parasite positivity rate remained low, at about 0.78% (95% CI 0.21–2.81).ConclusionsAL remains efficacious for the treatment of uncomplicated malaria with an efficacy above 90%, and may consequently continue to be used as the first-line treatment option in Mozambique among all vulnerable groups.Registration number prospero: 369991.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05931-y.Keywords: Efficacy, Artemether-lumefantrine, Plasmodium falciparum, Mozambique, Meta-analysis, Malaria

Background

Malaria represents a significant major public health concern. In 2024, globally, there were around 282 million malaria cases and 610000 malaria-related deaths, and the majority of these cases (94%) and deaths (95%) occurred in the African region [1]. In Mozambique, approximately 10 million malaria cases (representing 3.6% of global cases) and approximately 17,690 malaria deaths (equaling 2.9% of global deaths) were estimated to have occurred in 2024 [1], particularly affecting children and pregnant women.

In Mozambique, malaria remains endemic throughout the country, with most regions experiencing year-round transmission, albeit peaking during the rainy season from November to April. Among the different Plasmodium parasites that cause human disease, Plasmodium falciparum is by far the deadliest, as it is the most common species circulating and causing disease in Mozambique [2, 3]. According to the 2022–23 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), the prevalence of P. falciparum infection among children under 5 years of age was 32% [4]. Malaria prevalence is different between regions, decreasing from north to south, reaching 55% in Nampula, the most affected province in the northern region, 35% in Zambezia, the most affected province in the central region, and 16% in Inhambane [4] (Fig. 1). In malaria-endemic areas like Mozambique, one of the key strategies for malaria control involves the rapid diagnosis and treatment of malaria with efficacious antimalarials. Nevertheless, the emergence and propagation of P. falciparum strains that are resistant to antimalarials pose a significant challenge to these treatment efforts and other malaria control measures. In the Asian region, an emerging body of evidence suggests a degree of resistance to antimalarials containing artemisinin derivatives [5–7]. Novel markers of artemisinin resistance in the K13-propeller gene have been identified. Mutations in this gene were detected in artemisinin-resistant parasites from Southeast Asia, the same region where resistance to chloroquine and sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) first emerged and subsequently spread to Africa [6, 8]. The emergence of partial resistance to artemisinin derivatives has been reported in some locations of Sub-Saharan Africa, such as Rwanda [9] and Uganda [10], and there is great concern that these could rapidly spread to the rest of the countries in the continent, jeopardizing the therapeutic management of cases.

Since the beginning of this millennium, the World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) [11]. Following the adoption of ACT in 2006, Mozambique introduced artemether-lumefantrine (AL) and artesunate-amodiaquine (AS-AQ) as the first-line treatment for uncomplicated malaria in 2009, and these drugs continue to be recommended for the treatment of uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria [12–14]. The WHO has issued guidelines recommending the regular monitoring of first-line antimalarials at 2-year intervals [15]. Despite the absence of any indication of resistance to the ACT in the country so far [16–18], it is imperative to employ a multifaceted approach that incorporates monitoring the efficacy of the medication and routinely screening for the prevalence of molecular markers associated with parasite resistance to anti-malarial drugs. This strategy is crucial for assessing the efficacy of current treatment recommendations and ensuring a prompt response to the development of parasite resistance to support its continued use or the need for replacement in the country. The purpose of this review was to systematically synthesize available cumulative evidence on the efficacy of AL for the treatment of children and adults diagnosed with uncomplicated P.falciparum malaria in Mozambique, assessed on parasite clearance rate or recovery rate, to support its continued use or the need for replacement in the country.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

The present review encompasses all studies on AL conducted in Mozambique and published by July 15, 2025. The review focuses exclusively on the efficacy of AL and parasite clearance rate, which included participants (children under five and adults) diagnosed with uncomplicated malaria, and as reported in accordance with the WHO standard protocol [19] or a modified version thereof. There were no language, patients’ age, or date restrictions imposed, and a focus on human studies.

Information sources

The review protocol has been formulated following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guideline [20]. The studies were selected through electronic searches of the websites.

Search strategy

The following search terms were used to access relevant literature on the topic: combinations of words as ‘therapeutic’, ‘efficacy’, ‘artemether-lumefantrine’, and ‘Mozambique’, without language restriction, regardless of the year of publication, and restricted to human studies. The search was conducted on the PubMed website and the ScienceDirect citations. The last search was conducted on July 15, 2025.

Data selection, collection and extraction process

All studies captured by the search strategy were assessed to verify that they met the inclusion criteria using the Rayyan software [21], by two independent reviewers. The inclusion criteria (reporting on the efficacy of AL, based on the WHO protocol or a modified version, conducted in humans and in Mozambique) were assessed in three sections of the studies. First, the title was examined. Second, the abstract was assessed. Finally, the full text was examined. Based on these three assessments, only studies that met these criteria were included in this review. The discrepancies in the outputs were resolved in an open discussion between the reviewers.

After selecting the articles, two authors independently extracted the data using a Microsoft Excel® 2021 sheet to minimize errors, employing a predefined list of variables (study design, study region, follow-up period, study period, sample size, including age). For studies whose databases were available from the authors, they were automatically converted to Excel format. For those for which we did not have the data, they were manually extracted. Discrepancies found in the outputs prompted discussion between reviewers to resolve the issue.

Data items

A dataset was extracted from the included articles, covering key elements of the review. The main outcome was adequate clinical and parasitological response (ACPR) to AL on day 28 in children and adults with uncomplicated P. Falciparum malaria, as defined by WHO [19]. The secondary outcomes were as: early treatment failure (ETF), late clinical failure (LCF), late parasitological failure (LPF) of AL in the children and adults diagnosed with uncomplicated P. Falciparum malaria, as defined by the WHO protocol [19].

Early treatment failure: presence of danger signs or severe malaria during the first 3 days of treatment and the presence of parasitaemia, higher parasitaemia on Day 2 compared to Day 0 regardless of axillary temperature (≥ 37.5 °C), parasitaemia on Day 3 accompanied by fever and parasitaemia on Day 3 greater than 25% of the Day 0 count.

Late clinical failure: presence of danger signs or severe malaria in the presence of parasitaemia on any day between day 4 and day 28 and the presence of parasitaemia on any day between day 4 and day 28 with axillary temperature ≥ 37.5 °C in patients who did not previously meet any of the criteria of early treatment failure.

Late parasitological failure: presence of parasitaemia on any day between day 7 and day 28 with axillary temperature < 37.5 °C in patients who did not previously meet any of the criteria of ETF or LCF.

Adequate clinical and parasitological response is defined as the absence of parasitaemia on day 28, irrespective of axillary temperature, in patients who did not previously meet any of the criteria of ETF, LCF, or LPF.

Therapeutic efficacy is expressed as the cumulative success rate, i.e., the proportion of ACPR before and after polymerase chain reaction (PCR) correction. PCR-correction refers to the process of distinguishing between a new infection and a recrudescence using a molecular method. Uncorrected efficacy is calculated by dividing ACPR by a denominator (ACPR + ETF + LCF + LPF), where the lost to follow-up and withdrawals are removed from the denominator. For the PCR-corrected efficacy, new infections are additionally removed from the denominator.

Other important outcomes gathered were the positivity rate on day 3, indicating that the participant maintained a positive blood slide for P. falciparum by the third day of follow-up, and Recurrent parasitaemia was classified as recrudescence if it was related to the same parasite strain as on day 0 and the day of failure and as a new infection if it was due to a genetically different strain between day 0 and the day of failure.

Study risk of bias assessment

The studies’ risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [22]. The Cochrane risk-of-bias tool was used to assess the quality of the studies through the following domains: (1) randomization process, (2) deviations from intended interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) measurement of the outcome, and (5) selection of the reported result [22], by two independent reviewers. The overall risk bias for each study was determined and classified as low risk, some concern, or high risk.

Effect measures

The primary endpoint was day 28 ACPR to AL, assessed both without correction and with PCR correction. The primary outcome was ACPR at day 28, and ETF, LCF and LPF were secondary outcomes, according to the WHO protocol [19]. Efficacy estimates were calculated according to the per-protocol population, which included all patients who met the protocol's eligibility criteria, who completed the 3-day course of study medication, who underwent the assessment on day 28 and who had an assessable PCR in the event of recurrent parasitaemia. Another important outcome was the day 3 positivity rate, which is used as an indicator of suspected artemisinin partial resistance. Descriptive statistical analysis, including simple counts, averages, ranges, standard deviations and percentages, was used to present the efficacy data and day 3 positivity rate.

Synthesis methods

In this review, a random effects meta-analysis model was applied to account for between-study heterogeneity. This model assumes that the observed treatment effects vary across studies due to differences in populations, settings, or implementation, and estimates the average treatment effect across a distribution of true effects.

The meta-analysis was implemented using the metaprop function from the R package meta version 8.1 [23]. The analysis was conducted on logit-transformed proportions and used inverse-variance weighting within a random intercept logistic regression framework. This transformation stabilizes variances and reduces bias when dealing with extreme proportions. To address studies with zero events or events equal to the total sample size, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to both the numerator and denominator. The between-study variance (τ2) was estimated using the maximum likelihood estimator.

Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic, the between-study variance (τ2), and the I2 statistic, which quantifies the proportion of total variability attributable to heterogeneity. Confidence intervals were calculated for all heterogeneity estimates.

Two separate analyses were performed: one for uncorrected efficacy and another for corrected efficacy. For each, the primary outcome was the pooled proportion of treatment success with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Forest plots were generated to visualize individual study estimates and the pooled effect. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 [24], and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Reporting bias assessment

Two authors worked independently to assess the risk of bias using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool [22], in five domains. Each question was verified in each domain according to the tool for each study included in the review. At the end, after answering all the questions, the studies included in the systematic review were finally classified according to the Cochrane bias tool.

Certainty assessment

Certainty of evidence for each outcome was checked using the Handbook for grading the quality of evidence [25], by two authors. First, the outcomes were classified according to their importance, being categorized as: critical, important but not critical, and of limited importance, assessing what and why the outcomes were important. Secondly, the previously classified outcomes were judged, and discrepant results were openly discussed to resolve the dispute. The WHO acceptable threshold for PCR-corrected efficacy is 90% [19] and the positivity rate on day 3 is 10%, and this rate is used as an indicator of suspected partial resistance to artemisinin in an area [26].

Results

Study selection

A total of 91 studies (PubMed n = 17, Science Direct Citations n = 74) were found with the search strategy. After the exclusion of 5 duplicate articles, 86 were assessed for the title, 14 were screened for the abstract, and lastly, 11 articles were assessed for the complete text, of which two were systematic reviews, and four articles did not include the disaggregated data for Mozambique [27–30]. Finally, five of them met the inclusion criteria for the review (Fig. 2). Thus, in this analysis, six studies were included: [12–14, 31–33].

Study selection flowchart

Study characteristics

All studies included in this review were prospective, using the WHO protocol for antimalarial drug efficacy monitoring [19], and evaluated the efficacy of AL among other drugs in Mozambique. The six studies were conducted throughout the different regions of the country: the South, the Centre, and the North. Four of them (66.6%), [12–14, 33] were prospective, non-randomized, open-label, single-arm clinical trials and were conducted at the same sites, and recruited children under five years of age in three regions of the country (south, centre and north). One study (16.6%; 1/6) [32] was a randomised open-label and single-site study in Mozambique, although it was multicentre, taking place at five African sites (Nanoro, Burkina Faso; Kilifi, Kenya; Manhiça, Mozambique; Mbarara, Uganda; and Ndola, Zambia) in children under 5 years of age. This study included a follow-up of up to 42 days, but only data until day 28 were used for this analysis. The last (16.6%; 1/6) [31] study was also conducted as a prospective, non-randomized, open-label, single-arm clinical trial, recruiting adult subjects, however, the precise site was not specified, and the study medication was not directly observed, as recommended by the WHO protocol. Except for this study, [31], which included adult participants, all other studies included participants under 5 years of age. Two studies, [12, 14], reported two arms (AL and AS-AQ), while [32] reported AL and dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (DP); [33] reported four arms (AL, AS-AQ, DP and artesunate-pyronaridine (AS-PY). Finally, [13, 31] both reported only on AL efficacy. Further details of each study included are summarised in Table 1, and the excluded studies are in the supplementary Table 1.

Characteristics of individual studies

Risk of bias in studies

Despite few studies were included in the analysis, the overall risk of bias in individual studies was considered low, with some concerns across all domains considered (Supplementary Table 2). Although the site-specific efficacy of the study [32] had not previously been calculated for Mozambique alone, access to the database was granted, and the results were calculated to ensure their accuracy. For the other four studies in children, we had full access to the locked databases [12–14, 33]. In the study [31], we did not have database access, so we extracted the data directly from the article and entered it into an Excel spreadsheet, then calculated efficacy, as we have done for other studies.

Day 3 parasite positivity rates were generally low across the included studies in the review, with no positivity reported in [31, 32]. Low positivity rates were observed in [33] with 1.1%, [13, 33] with 0.9%, and with 0.4%. However, one study, [33] reported a notably higher day 3 positivity rate (approximately 7%).

Data from the synthesis of different studies

A total of six studies comprising 20 single-arm trials, totalling 1862 participants, were included in this analysis (Table 2). The overall random-estimated uncorrected efficacy and PCR-corrected efficacy of AL in the country were 91.0% (95% CI 0.84–0.95) and 98.3% (95% CI 0.97–0.99), respectively (see Table 2).

In this review, the overall ETF rate was very low, at approximately zero (0.05%). Late clinical failure and late parasitological failure occurred in 2.73% and 5.3% of participants, respectively. Recrudescence was observed in 1.8% of cases, whereas new infections accounted for 8.6% (Table 2).

The pooled estimate of the day 3 positivity rate was 0.78% (Table 2), meaning that fewer than 1% of patients remained positive on day 3, although the true rate may vary across settings.

The heterogeneity efficacy between studies varied, being high for uncorrected efficacy heterogeneity (81.8%), while PCR-corrected efficacy was moderately, close to 44.4%, as illustrated in Figs. 3A, 3B.

Discussion

Mozambique is one of the countries with a higher malaria burden [34], so it is critical that highly effective antimalarials are available and used as first-line treatment for the management of clinical malaria. The country approved the use of ACT in 2006 following WHO recommendations, introducing AL as the first-line treatment for uncomplicated malaria in 2009, and has been used uninterruptedly since then [35]. This review showed a high PCR corrected efficacy of AL (98.3%) and its good suitability to remain as the preferred first-line option, given its efficacy remaining above the WHO cut-off of 90% [19]. This result is also consistent with other studies conducted in Mozambique, which also document efficacy above 90%, and not included in this review due to the absence of disaggregated Mozambique-specific data [12–14, 27, 32, 36]. Beyond Mozambique, the pooled efficacy results are also similar to those found in studies/reports conducted in other countries from the African continent [29, 30, 36].

Also, in this review, the overall treatment failure was low, with ETF being almost zero (0.05%). Late treatment failures were common, with LPF the most frequent at 5.3%, whereas LCF was at 2.73%. Treatment failure refers to the inability to eliminate parasites after administration of an adequate dose of antimalarial medication. It can be due to intrinsic host factors (malabsorption) or to the parasite escaping the action of the medicine, since all drugs used in clinical trials were certified and administration procedures ensured. The low ETF rate (0.05%) reported in this review emphasizes that AL remains effective against P. falciparum. These results are aligned with those from previous studies conducted in Mozambique [29, 30] and systematic reviews [37, 38].

Another important result was the recurrent parasitaemia rate, indicating that the participant had a negative blood slide and returned to a positive during follow-up, which can result from reinfection or recrudescence. In this review, among participants with recurrent parasitaemia, 8.6% were reinfections, indicating that the AL still functions properly. These findings are aligned with other systematic reviews in sub-Saharan Africa [36–38] and indicate that health authorities and partners should increase coverage and improve utilization of malaria prevention measures in Mozambique.

Unfortunately, 1.8% of recurrent parasitaemias were due to recrudescence. Recrudescence may result from drug resistance or inadequate exposure to the drug due to sub-optimal dosing, poor adherence, vomiting, unused pharmacokinetics in an individual, or substandard drugs [39]. Although no signs of partial resistance to artemisinin derivatives to date have been reported in the country [40, 41], these findings call for close monitoring of first-line antimalarials in Mozambique, as other African countries have already reported the emergence of partial resistance to artemisinin derivatives [9, 10]. Additionally, in this review, the day 3 positivity rate was 0.78% (Table 2). The day 3 positivity rate is an important indicator of suspected artemisinin partial resistance, and its WHO cut-off is 10% [26]. Our results did not detect partial resistance to artemisinin derivatives (below 10%). This result is in line with previous studies conducted in the country [27–30], but contrasts with reports from some African countries [9, 10] and the Asian region [6, 8] on the presence of partial resistance to artemisinin derivatives. Finally, an important result is the heterogeneity of the studies included in this review. The heterogeneity of efficacy across studies is very high, with uncorrected efficacy heterogeneity of 81.8% and PCR-corrected efficacy heterogeneity of approximately 44.4%. The difference in heterogeneity is due, on the one hand, to the difference in malaria prevalence at the different sites where the studies were conducted and, on the other hand, to the period of the year in which the study was conducted, given that malaria is endemic with peaks during the rainy season in the country. Another concern is the number and age of participants evaluated in each study included in the analysis, as some studies were single-site and others were multi-site. These results are consistent with previous systematic reviews [36, 41] but contrast with a study conducted in Ethiopia years ago [42].

Thus, this review suggests that AL can be used for the moment as a first-line treatment of uncomplicated malaria without major concerns in Mozambique. Nevertheless, monitoring of the efficacy of antimalarial drugs should be continued at least every two years according to the WHO [19], including molecular markers, to detect and mitigate the spread of mutant parasites if they are found and take specific decisions in consequence. In this range, the WHO recommends that countries with a high malaria burden [1], such as Mozambique, to use Multiple First-Line Treatments (MFT) to reduce the risk of resistance spreading or emerging locally [43]. In fact, the country is preparing to introduce AS-PY as the third first-line treatment, in accordance with WHO recommendations [43].

Limitations

The most significant limitation in this review is the limited number of studies conducted to date in Mozambique that report on AL efficacy. Second, some of these few studies were multi-site, and disaggregated data from Mozambique were not available to perform the respective analysis; thus, these studies were excluded. Third, it was not possible to disaggregate the reasons why participants did not successfully complete the study. An important limitation was the heterogeneity of the studies. Another important limitation was that the method for performing molecular correction was not included in this review. Finally, one study [31] was of very low quality, due to the high rate of loss to follow-up, the methods applied and the lack of availability of individual participant data, but it was included in this review because the variables were available.

Conclusion

AL remains an effective treatment for uncomplicated P falciparum malaria for children and adults in Mozambique, and should therefore continue to be recommended as the preferred first-line treatment. Monitoring the efficacy of first-line treatment should be continued every 2 years, as recommended by the WHO.
