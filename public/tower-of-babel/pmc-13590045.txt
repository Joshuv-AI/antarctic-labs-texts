Progesterone in feminizing gender affirming hormone therapy: study protocol for a randomized placebo-controlled trial
Authors: David R. Cromer, Daniela S. Diego, Thomas Luke, Elle Deppe, Zhenmei Wu, Kelly O'Sullivan, Ryan J. Kann, Jose Nilo G. Binongo, Vin Tangpricha

Introduction

Transfeminine hormone replacement therapy (tfHRT) refers to the use of estrogen-based hormone regimens to support feminization in transgender women. Current clinical guidelines recommend estrogen therapy combined with a testosterone-lowering agent as first-line treatment for transfeminine individuals [1,2]. However, several additional therapies are commonly requested or used in clinical practice despite limited supporting data, including progesterone and nonsteroidal anti-androgens, such as bicalutamide [3].

Progesterone is frequently requested by transgender women as part of gender-affirming hormone therapy, despite limited evidence to support its use. In a recent United States-based study of transgender women and gender-diverse adults assigned male at birth receiving hormone therapy, a substantial proportion reported experience with progestogen use [4]. However, progesterone use has been associated with potential cardiovascular risks, including an increased risk of thromboembolic events, particularly when combined with estrogen therapy [5,6]. Evidence supporting clinical benefit in transgender women remains sparse. A prospective study of oral micronized progesterone demonstrated no significant improvement in breast development, sleep quality, or psychological distress compared with standard care, although a subset of participants reported improved quality of life [7]. Data from cisgender populations further raise concerns, as combined estrogen–progestin therapy has been associated with increased cardiovascular risk compared with estrogen alone [8]. As a result, the role of progesterone in tfHRT remains uncertain and is not currently recommended as a first-line agent in clinical guidelines.

The objective of this pilot randomized controlled trial is to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and preliminary clinical effects of oral micronized progesterone as part of gender-affirming hormone therapy in transgender women receiving stable estradiol and testosterone-lowering therapy. By evaluating micronized progesterone, a formulation associated with a more favorable safety profile in cisgender populations, this study seeks to address an important evidence gap and inform clinical practice. This study is intended to inform the design of future adequately powered randomized controlled trials evaluating progesterone in transfeminine care.

Methods

Study design and intervention

This is a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial designed to evaluate the addition of oral micronized progesterone to standard tfHRT. Standard tfHRT is defined as estradiol administered via transdermal, oral, subcutaneous, or intramuscular routes in combination with a testosterone-lowering agent, typically spironolactone. Participants will be randomized via simple randomization in a 1:1 ratio by the Emory Investigational Drug Service (IDS) to receive either oral micronized progesterone 200 mg daily at bedtime or a matched placebo. Both the study drug and placebo will be formulated, stored, and dispensed by the Emory IDS. The study medication will be formulated by encapsulating 2 tablets of 100 mg of commercially available micronized progesterone. The placebo will be formulated by encapsulating lactose. Both capsules have the identical external appearance. Participants, clinicians, and data analysts will be blinded. Only the IDS will be unblinded.

Regulatory approval

Approval from the Emory University Institutional Review Board (IRB #00006161) and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (IND #171225) was obtained prior to subject recruitment in April 2025. A HIPAA waiver for screening for potential participants was also approved.

Participants

Prospective participants are identified through pre-screening of clinic schedules and electronic medical records of patients receiving care at the Emory Clinic. Individuals meeting preliminary eligibility criteria are invited for an in-person screening visit at the Emory University Hospital General Clinical Research Center, where informed consent is obtained, a detailed medical history is reviewed, and screening laboratory studies are performed. A log of screened individuals who are deemed ineligible is maintained.

Transgender women aged 18–60 years who have been receiving stable tfHRT for at least six months are eligible for enrollment. Exclusion criteria include medical conditions associated with gynecomastia (e.g., thyroid disease, chronic kidney disease, cirrhosis), use of medications associated with gynecomastia (e.g., ACE inhibitors, statins, haloperidol, tricyclic antidepressants, atypical antipsychotics), congestive heart failure, cardiovascular disease, poorly controlled hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia ≥500 mg/dL, history of breast cancer, known BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutation, uncontrolled depression or active suicidal ideation, cannabis use within the past 3 months of greater than 1 use/week, current smoking ≥25 cigarettes per day, prior progesterone use, peanut allergy, or estradiol levels outside the predefined stable range (100–300 pg/mL).

Study procedures

Participants will continue their existing estradiol and testosterone-lowering regimens throughout the study. Participant gender identity, race, ethnicity, educational attainment, and employment status are collected at enrollment. Height and weight will be measured to calculate body mass index, and blood pressure is recorded at study visits. Breast hemi-circumference measurements will be obtained at baseline and at the end of the study as a noninvasive estimate of breast development. Breast measurements will collected from the medial to lateral breast inflection points across the nipple for each breast.

Blood samples will be collected at the screening visit and at the final study visit. Serum will be analyzed for estradiol, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides, creatinine, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST).

Participants will complete three validated questionnaires at specified time points during the study. Psychological distress will be assessed using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10), a 10-item instrument with scores ranging from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating greater distress. Sleep quality is assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a 19-item questionnaire yielding a total score of 0–21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. Gender congruence and life satisfaction are assessed using the Gender Congruence and Life Satisfaction Scale (GCLS), a 38-item measure with total scores ranging from 38 to 190, with higher scores indicating higher satisfaction.

Participants will be followed for a total of six months (Fig. 1). The baseline (“Month 0”) visit will be conducted in person and includes physical measurements, breast hemi-circumference assessment, and administration of all three surveys. Participants will be randomized and receive the study drug at this visit. Monthly follow-up visits will be conducted thereafter. The study visits at months 1, 2, 4, and 5 visits are completed virtually and include assessment of medication adherence through participant self-report, evaluation of medication side effects and adverse events, and reinforcement of study procedures. Potential side effects of concern are focused on venous thromboembolism and cardiovascular diseases. The month 3 visit will also include repeat administration of all surveys. The study visit at month 6 will also be conducted in person and will include repeat physical measurements, breast hemi-circumference assessment, survey administration, laboratory testing, and return of any remaining study medication to further assess adherence.Fig. 1SPIRIT schedule for enrollment, interventions, and assessments.

Participants may discontinue the study intervention due to adverse events, participant request, investigator discretion, or withdrawal of consent.

Data safety monitoring

A Data safety monitoring board (DSMB) was appointed prior to initiation of the study. The committee was chaired by a faculty member from the Department of Biostatistics, Emory School of Public Health and two physicians with expertise in gender affirming hormone therapy. The committee will meet every 6 months to review enrollment data and any AEs/SAEs. They will determine if completion of the study is futile if enrollment is inadequate and/or not safe to continue if the AEs/SAEs occur more frequently than expected.

Outcomes and planned analyses

Feasibility outcomes include participant recruitment, retention, adherence to study medication, and completion of study visits and questionnaires. These outcomes will be summarized descriptively and used to inform the design and implementation of future definitive randomized controlled trials. All analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle, whereby all randomized participants will be analyzed in the group to which they were assigned regardless of adherence to the intervention. This study is a pilot randomized controlled trial, and the sample size is based on the primary outcome under optimistic assumptions; therefore, all findings will be interpreted as preliminary. All statistical tests will be two-sided with a significance level of α = 0.05.

Baseline characteristics will be summarized by treatment group using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables will be presented as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, as appropriate based on distributional characteristics. Categorical variables will be summarized using frequencies and percentages. No formal statistical comparisons will be performed for baseline characteristics.

The primary outcome is psychological distress at 6 months, measured using the K10 scale. The primary analysis will use analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with 6-month K10 score on the original scale as the dependent variable, treatment group as the primary independent variable, and baseline K10 score included as a covariate. The treatment effect will be reported as the adjusted mean difference between groups, with corresponding 95% confidence interval and two-sided p-value. To assess robustness to departures from model assumptions, a sensitivity analysis will be conducted using log-transformed K10 scores. In this analysis, both baseline and 6-month K10 values will be log-transformed, and an analogous ANCOVA model will be fitted. Results from this analysis will be interpreted as supportive.

Secondary outcomes include sleep quality measured by the PSQI, gender congruence and life satisfaction measured by the GCLS, breast hemi-circumference, blood pressure, lipid measures, estradiol, creatinine, alanine aminotransferase, and aspartate aminotransferase. Continuous secondary outcomes will be analyzed using ANCOVA models analogous to the primary analysis, with the 6-month value as the dependent variable, treatment group as the primary independent variable, and the corresponding baseline value included as a covariate when available. Given the pilot design and limited sample size, these analyses will be considered exploratory, with emphasis on estimation of effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals rather than formal hypothesis testing. For outcomes measured at multiple time points (baseline, 3 months, and 6 months), exploratory longitudinal analyses may be conducted using linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects for treatment group, time, and treatment-by-time interaction, and a subject-specific random intercept to account for within-subject correlation.

No formal interim analyses or stopping guidelines are planned due to the pilot nature and limited sample size of the study. Adverse events and safety outcomes will be summarized descriptively by treatment group using counts and percentages. Comparisons of categorical safety outcomes will use chi-square tests or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. Continuous safety laboratory measures at 6 months will be analyzed using ANCOVA models as described above. Safety analyses will be interpreted descriptively.

Model assumptions, including linearity, homoscedasticity, and approximate normality of residuals, will be evaluated for all linear models. If substantial violations are observed, appropriate transformations or non-parametric sensitivity analyses will be considered. Missing data will be assessed for extent and pattern. The primary analysis will be based on complete cases; if missingness exceeds a minimal threshold, multiple imputation using chained equations will be performed as a sensitivity analysis under a missing-at-random assumption.

A secondary per-protocol analysis may be conducted including participants who complete the study and remain adherent to the assigned intervention. Consistent with the pilot randomized controlled trial design, all secondary, sensitivity, and per protocol analyses will be interpreted as exploratory and supportive.

Sample size estimation was informed by published data from a comparable transgender population, which reported a mean K10 score of 22.7 with a standard deviation of 8.09. Because this study was cross-sectional, only a single time-point estimate of variability was available, and no information on within-subject change or longitudinal correlation was reported. Accordingly, the standard deviation of 8.0 was used as an estimate of the variability of K10 at both baseline and 6 months. To implement an ANCOVA framework, an additional assumption regarding the correlation between baseline and 6 month K10 scores was required; a conservative correlation of 0.3 was assumed. Under these assumptions, and specifying a two-sided alpha of 0.05, 80% power, and a minimum detectable adjusted between-group difference of 8 points in mean K10 at 6 months, 15 participants per group are required. To account for an anticipated 10% loss to follow-up, the sample size was inflated to 17 participants per group, for a total of 34 participants. The primary analysis will be conducted on the original K10 scale; however, to assess the robustness of the findings to potential departures from normality, a sensitivity analysis will be performed using log-transformed K10 scores within the same ANCOVA framework.

Data management

Study data will be collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at Emory University. Participants will be assigned unique study identification numbers to maintain confidentiality. Access to identifiable participant information will be restricted to authorized study personnel. Data will be stored on secure password-protected institutional servers in accordance with HIPAA and institutional regulatory requirements. Periodic data quality checks will be performed to assess completeness and accuracy.

Discussion

Despite increasing clinical use, progesterone is not recommended for initial tfHRT by most professional societies due to limited high-quality evidence supporting its efficacy and safety. Existing studies in transgender women are largely observational, retrospective, or uncontrolled. Current guidelines emphasize the need for randomized trials to clarify progesterone's role in clinical care [1,2,5]. To address this evidence gap, we designed a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial evaluating oral micronized progesterone as an adjunct to standard feminizing hormone therapy. This pilot randomized controlled trial is designed to systematically assess feasibility, safety, and preliminary patient-reported, physical, and cardiometabolic outcomes, as well as adverse events, to inform the safety, feasibility, and potential clinical role of progesterone in transfeminine care.

Progesterone is an endogenous steroid hormone with physiological effects that extend beyond reproduction, including roles in breast development, neurocognitive function, sleep regulation, and cardiometabolic health in cisgender women [9]. In the breast, progesterone acts synergistically with estradiol to support ductal branching and lobuloalveolar maturation, providing biological plausibility for its proposed role in feminizing hormone therapy [10]. Progesterone metabolites such as allopregnanolone also function as neurosteroids that modulate γ-aminobutyric acid type A receptors, which may contribute to anxiolytic and sleep-promoting effects observed with micronized progesterone use [11,12].

Progesterone is available in multiple formulations and routes of administration, and the physiological effects and safety profile vary by formulation. Oral micronized progesterone is the most commonly used bioidentical preparation and appears to have a neutral or mildly favorable cardiometabolic profile in cisgender women [13]. In contrast, synthetic progestogens such as medroxyprogesterone acetate and cyproterone acetate exhibit additional androgenic or antiandrogenic properties and have been associated with less favorable metabolic and cardiovascular effects in some populations [5,6,[14], [15], [16]]. Micronized progesterone is typically prescribed at doses of 100–200 mg daily and has a pharmacologic profile that more closely resembles endogenous progesterone than synthetic progestins [17].

Prior research on the role of progesterone in tfHRT remains controversial due to limited and heterogeneous evidence. Existing studies are largely retrospective or observational, use variable dosing strategies and formulations, and lack standardized outcome measures, limiting comparability across studies [5]. A prospective case–control study of oral micronized progesterone demonstrated no significant differences in breast development, sleep quality, or psychological distress compared with standard care over a short follow-up period [7]. In contrast, retrospective observational data have suggested higher self-reported satisfaction with breast development and mental health among individuals receiving progesterone. However, these findings are limited by the absence of randomization and potential selection bias [16]. To date, only one randomized study has been initiated, although it lacked blinding and placebo control and has not yet reported results [18]. Collectively, existing studies highlight the paucity of rigorous evidence and underscore the need for controlled trials to clarify the clinical role of progesterone in gender-affirming hormone therapy.

While oral micronized progesterone has widespread clinical use and a comparatively favorable safety profile in cisgender populations [19], extrapolation from cisgender populations remains limited. As such, controlled trials are necessary to evaluate whether these physiological effects translate to clinically meaningful benefits or risks in transgender women receiving gender-affirming hormone therapy. Restricting the intervention to a single formulation and dosing strategy allows for a clearer assessment of safety and feasibility within transfeminine hormone therapy.

This study has several strengths, including the double-blind nature, randomization, use of a single progesterone formulation, and six-month study participant follow-up. In addition, various outcome measures will be assessed, including psychological distress, sleep quality, gender congruence and life satisfaction, and breast development. This builds upon the study by Nolan et al. which utilized a lower dose of micronized progesterone (100 mg vs 200 mg in our study), had a shorter follow-up period (3 months vs. 6 months in our study). In addition, their study assessed breast development via Tanner staging, which can be subjective, whereas our study uses a more objective breast hemi-circumference measurement. Our study also incorporates the assessment of gender congruence and life satisfaction as an outcome measure. Lastly, we will be assessing patients’ side effects on the study medication as well as any potential changes in their cardiometabolic health via physical and laboratory assessments, as previously described.

This study has several limitations. The planned sample size of 34 participants limits statistical power, particularly for secondary outcomes such as breast development and cardiometabolic parameters. The six-month follow-up period may be insufficient to capture full breast maturation or longer-term metabolic effects. Survey-based measures of psychological distress and sleep rely on self-report and may be influenced by social desirability or placebo effects. Because the study includes only adults receiving stable gender-affirming hormone therapy for at least six months, findings may not be generalizable to transgender youth, nonbinary individuals, or those initiating hormone therapy. Additionally, participants continue individualized estradiol and testosterone-lowering regimens that may vary in route and dose, which could contribute to outcome variability.

The findings from this pilot study may inform the design, outcome selection, and feasibility considerations of future definitive multicenter randomized controlled trials. Results from this pilot study will provide important data to inform power calculations of outcomes declared as secondary in this pilot study for future studies. Future studies should build on this work by evaluating different dosing strategies and formulations of progesterone in combination with estradiol to better define dose–response relationships and potential clinical effects. Larger, multicenter trials with longer follow-up periods will be needed to assess longer-term outcomes (beyond 6 months), including cardiometabolic risk and breast development. Future research should also consider broader inclusion criteria, including individuals with diverse gender identities and varying stages of gender-affirming hormone therapy, to improve generalizability. Incorporation of more objective outcome measures, such as advanced imaging techniques for breast development, may further strengthen future studies.

In conclusion, despite increasing clinical interest, the evidence base supporting progesterone use in tfHRT remains limited. Well-designed randomized controlled trials are needed to clarify the safety, feasibility, and potential clinical role of progesterone as part of gender-affirming care. This study represents an important step toward addressing these knowledge gaps and informing future clinical practice.
