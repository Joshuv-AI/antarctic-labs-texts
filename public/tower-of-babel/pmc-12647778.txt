Parental educational aspirations and children’s sleep: a mediation analysis
Authors: Jun Yamashita, Kenji J Tsuchiya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractJapanese children tend to have short sleep durations, influenced by the educational environment. However, the empirical evidence of this association remains limited. This study examined the relationship between parental educational aspirations and children’s sleep duration among Japanese school-aged children and investigated whether cram school attendance mediates this relationship. Using the Japanese Longitudinal Study of Children and Parents (JLSCP), a nationally representative sample was analyzed. The current study included children in grades 5–9 (ages 10–15 years) and their mothers, and revealed three key findings. First, higher parental educational aspirations were significantly associated with shorter sleep duration among junior high school students, while no such association was observed among elementary school students. Second, this relationship was partially mediated by children’s attendance at cram school. Third, an inverse association between parental educational aspirations and sleep duration was evident in non-metropolitan areas. These findings suggest that parental educational aspirations reduced sleep duration in junior high school students via increased cram school attendance. The results highlight the need to revisit the role of cram schools and promote sleep hygiene education to support children’s sleep, particularly in non-metropolitan areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25781-z.Keywords: Children’s sleep, Parental educational aspirations, Educational environment, Cram school attendance, Mediation analysisSubject terms: Public health, Human behaviour

Introduction

Sleep is important for maintaining mental and physical health. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommended 9–12 h of sleep for elementary school students and 8–10 h for middle and high school students1. However, international comparative surveys conducted by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) have revealed that children fall short of these recommendations, especially East Asia, including Japan and China2.

Children’s sleep duration in East Asia may be shorter owing to different influencing factors than in other regions. International research suggests that determinants of children’s sleep duration vary by cultural background3. Specifically, in Western countries, electronic media factors influence children’s sleep more than academic factors4. In East Asian contexts, particularly in South Korea and possibly other similar settings, educational factors—especially private tutoring time—tend to influence children’s sleep more than electronic media factors5. However, a systematic review6 of longitudinal studies mainly identified behavioral, biological, and family-environmental determinants of children’s sleep (e.g., physical activity, screen use), but it paid little attention to factors rooted in the educational context, such as parental aspirations or academic demands. This gap underscores the need to examine sleep duration from an educational perspective6.

East Asian cultures emphasize academics, valuing diplomas and academic credentials7,8 and consequently a social norm prioritizing studying has become widespread9,10. In In Japan, there was a time when students preparing for university entrance examinations were told to “sleep four hours to succeed and five hours to fail”10,11 whereas in South Korea, a similar saying suggests “sleep three hours to succeed, four hours to fail”5.

In such an intensely competitive educational environment, parental educational aspirations—defined as the level of educational achievement parents desire for their children—have been widely studied and influence children’s educational and occupational attainment12–14. However, excessive parental educational aspirations exert negative effects, such as excessive pressure on children15. Hence, parents with high educational aspirations may encourage or pressure children to devote more time to studying, reducing sleep durations.

Furthermore, in East Asian educational environment, attendance at private cram schools—specialized out-of-school education program known as “juku” in Japan—is an important factor to consider. Although cram schools serve as a “remedial strategy” in most countries, in East Asia, high-achieving students often attend them as an “enrichment strategy” to gain additional educational opportunity16. In Japan, attending cram schools aligns with this enrichment approach. The country’s compulsory education consists of six years of elementary school and three years of junior high school, after which more than 99% of students continue to high school17. This educational system is often described as egalitarian with standardized curricula and limited formal tracking18,19. Many parents enroll their children in juku during the compulsory education stage to prepare for competitive entrance examinations, and this early participation has become a structural feature of the Japanese educational system20. Children with high parental educational aspirations are more likely to enroll in cram schools in response to intense competition in admission tests20,21. Thus, considering that parental educational aspirations influence children’s attendance at cram schools, attendance at cram schools can be regarded as a mediator in the association between parental educational aspirations and children’s sleep. Furthermore, previous studies on Japanese cram schools have shown that access to cram schools differs markedly between metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas, which may in turn influence children’s daily time use20,22. Considering this context, it is important to examine whether the associations observed in this study vary by residential area.

Parental educational aspirations are plausible determinants of a children’s sleep duration, with attendance at cram school serving as a mediator in this relationship. Therefore, the main objective of this study was to explore whether parental educational aspiration is associated with children’s sleep duration among Japanese elementary and junior high school students. Specifically, the present study examined the following hypotheses: (1) Children whose parents hold higher educational aspirations would be more likely to have shorter sleep duration; (2) Children whose parents hold higher educational aspirations would be more likely to attend cram schools after regular school, which in turn would reduce their sleep duration; and (3) Children living in metropolitan areas would be more strongly affected by these associations than those living in non-metropolitan areas, reflecting regional differences in cram school attendance. Parental socioeconomic status and screen time have been widely recognized as important determinants of children’s sleep5,23–26. Therefore, these factors were statistically controlled for when testing the hypotheses.

Methods

Study design

This study was a secondary analysis of a publicly available dataset from the Japanese Longitudinal Study of Children and Parents (JLSCP) 201527,28, The JLSCP is a nationally representative sample of Japanese children and parents that has been conducted by the Institute of Social Science (ISS), the University of Tokyo29 and the Benesse Educational Research and Development Institute (BERD)30 since 2015. The JLSCP aims to survey changes in children’s daily lives, learning, and parenting over multiple years to understand current issues and explore better approaches to education and parenting27,28. For this study, a dataset from the first wave of the JLSCP (JLSCP 2015) was used.

Participants

The JLSCP included 1st grade students to 12th grade students and their parents across Japan. Questionnaires were distributed to 21,569 parent-child pairs, with a response rate of 77.7% (16,761/21,569). The first wave survey was conducted from July to August 2015. Further details on the survey methodology can be found in previous28,31.

The current study targeted children and parents included in the JLSCP, specifically children who were in the 5th grade of elementary school to the 9th grade of junior high school in the 2015 academic year, and their mothers. The focus on school-aged children in 5th grade and above as the target for analysis was based on nationwide32, which indicated that from 5th grade onward in public schools, spending on supplementary learning (e.g., cram school attendance) surpassed that on other extracurricular activities (e.g., experiential activities). This trend suggested that cram school attendance typically begins at this stage.

The analysis focused on mothers for the following reasons: (1) approximately 90% of the guardian respondents were mothers and (2) the exposure in this study, “educational aspirations for children,” may differ between mothers and fathers33. Therefore, in studies investigating the relationship between parental educational aspirations for their children and children’s sleep duration, it is essential to consider which parent provides the response. Thus, this study focused only on mothers, and all parent-related variables used in the analysis specifically pertained to mothers. Listwise deletion was used to manage missing data.

Outcome variable

Children’s sleep duration was assessed using a single measure in which children reported their average sleep duration on school days. This was calculated as the difference between self-reported bedtime and wake-up time. Wake-up time was measured on a 7-point scale (in 30-minute intervals), ranging from before 6:00 a.m. to after 8:00 a.m. Bedtime was measured on an 11-point scale (in 30-minute intervals), ranging from before 22:00 p.m. to after 2:00 a.m. Minutes were converted to hours (e.g., 6 h and 30 min was converted to 6.5 h), and the variable was treated as continuous5.

Exposure variable

Parental aspirations were assessed by a single item in which the parent reported the level of education they wished their child to achieve33–35 (“To what level of education do you wish your child to advance in the future?”). A binary variable was created to indicate parents aspiring for their child to attain a university degree or higher, coded as 1 for “university or graduate school,” and 0 otherwise34,35.

Mediator variable

Cram school (juku) attendance was assessed using a single item in which children reported the frequency of their attendance at such schools during a regular (non-summer vacation) week (“How many times per week do you usually attend cram schools?”). A binary dummy variable was created, coded as 0 for those who answered, “I do not attend” and 1 for those who answered, “1 or more times25.”

Covariates

The covariates included parental socioeconomic status (SES), children’s sex, grade, screen time, residence area, and school operator. SES was measured via parental reports on educational background, family income, and occupation. Parental education was a binary variable coded as 1 if the mother had a university degree or higher, and 0 otherwise. Household income was measured based on the reported income for the survey year. Maternal occupation was a binary variable coded as 1 for full-time homemakers and 0 otherwise. This distinction was made because stay-at-home mothers, compared to working mothers, tend to spend more time engaging with their children together36, reflecting a more proactive educational involvement. Sex was a binary variable coded as 1 for males and 0 for others. Grade was included as a dummy variable indicating children’s grade level. Screen time was assessed using a single item where children reported the amount of time spent on activities such as “watching TV or DVDs,” “playing video games or handheld gaming devices,” “using a mobile phone or smartphone,” and “using a computer or tablet.” A binary dummy variable was created, coded as 1 for those who spent ≥ 2 h on these activities and 0 otherwise37,38. Residence area was a binary variable coded as 1 if the residence was in metropolitan areas (Tokyo’s 23 Special Wards and cities of more than 0.5 million inhabitants) and 0 otherwise (non-metropolitan areas). The school operator was a binary variable coded as 1 if the student attended a public school and 0 otherwise39.

Statistical analysis

First, the association between parental educational aspirations and children’s sleep duration was examined using a linear regression model (ordinary least squares), with and without covariates. Second, whether attendance at cram schools mediated the relationship between parental educational aspirations and children’s sleep duration was investigated using causal mediation analysis. Third, a causal mediation analysis was conducted, stratified by residential area.

Causal mediation analysis40–42, based on a counterfactual approach (potential outcomes framework), was used to estimate the total effects (TE) of parental aspirations on children’s sleep duration. This framework is commonly used to examine the impact of one variable on another under different scenarios. If A represents a binary exposure (maternal educational aspirations: high vs. low), M represents a binary mediator (cram school attendance: attend vs. not attend), and Y represents a continuous outcome (children’s sleep duration), we can define counterfactual outcomes based on the potential outcome framework. Let Ya denote a children’s sleep duration if the parental educational aspirations A were set, possibly contrary to fact, to a. Let Ma denote the counterfactual value of the mediator M if A were set to a. Finally, let Yam denote the counterfactual value of Y if A were set to a and M were set to m.

The natural direct effect (NDE) represents the effect of changing exposure (A) from a* to a, while fixing the mediator (M) to the value it would naturally take under A = a*. Mathematically, this can be expressed as NDE = E [YaMa*] − E[Ya*Ma*]. The natural indirect effect (NIE) represents the effect of changing the mediator (M) to the value it would naturally take under A = a while keeping the exposure constant at A = a. This is expressed as NIE = E [YaMa] − E[YaMa*]. The total effect (TE) is the sum of natural direct and indirect effects: TE = NDE + NIE. The proportion mediated (PM), representing the effect of parental aspirations on sleep duration mediated by cram school attendance, was calculated as NIE divided by TE.

The outcome model in the causal mediation analysis was implemented using a linear regression model, as the outcome was a continuous variable. In contrast, the mediator model employed a logistic regression model because the mediator was binary. To simplify the interpretation of the model, the exposure-mediator interaction variable was omitted from the mediation analyses if their removal did not meaningfully change either of the effect estimates (NDE and NIE) by more than 10%41,43,44. StataNow 19.5 (StataCorp)45 was used for the statistical analysis.

Ethical procedures

Ethical approval was not required for this study as it used publicly available secondary datasets46. We submitted a data request to the data provider, the Center for Social Research and Data Archives (CSRDA), Institute for Social Science, The University of Tokyo47. Upon approval, the data provider approved our application and granted access to the microdata for use in this research. The survey underlying these datasets was conducted with the approval of the Research Ethics Committee of the Institute for Social Science, The University of Tokyo.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to investigate whether parental educational aspirations predict sleep duration among Japanese elementary and junior high school students. Our findings showed that parental educational aspirations negatively affected children’s sleep duration. However, negative effects were evident only among junior high school students, with no effect on elementary school students. For junior high school students, both direct and indirect effects mediated via cram school attendance were identified, suggesting partial (23.4%) mediation by the indirect effect. These associations were observed exclusively in non-metropolitan areas.

The association between parental educational aspirations and sleep duration was observed in junior high and not elementary students, likely attributed to the increased effort required for high school entrance exam preparation, which typically begins during junior high school in Japan. According to the FY2023 Basic School Survey17, the proportion of students advancing to private or national junior high schools is estimated to be below 10%. In contrast, over 98% of junior high school students attend high school, indicating that almost all of them take entrance exams. Therefore, among elementary school students who have not yet begun intensive exam preparation, parental educational aspirations on children’s sleep duration can be considered insignificant.

The finding that parental educational aspirations influence sleep duration via the mediation of cram school attendance among junior high school students is supported by previous studies. Specifically, parental educational aspirations have been shown to affect cram school attendance among junior high school student21, and cram school attendance negatively impacts children’s sleep duration5.

Several factors may account for the variation in association patterns across residential areas. First, a comparison between metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas reveals that parents in non-metropolitan areas are less likely to aspire for their children to obtain a university degree or higher (see Supplemental Table 1). In this context, mothers in non-metropolitan areas who do hold such aspirations may be more actively involved in their children’s education as an enrichment strategy16. This involvement aims to prevent their children from falling behind in educational competition, particularly, entrance exams, while also considering the higher cram school attendance rates in metropolitan areas. Consequently, in non-metropolitan areas, parental educational aspiration influenced children’s sleep duration both directly and indirectly via increased cram school attendance, whereas this effect was less pronounced in metropolitan areas.

The study organizers (ISS and BERD) compared the baseline characteristics of the survey respondents with national statistics. The results showed that while sex distribution, household income, and regional representation closely matched national averages, parents’ educational attainment was marginally higher, and the proportion of junior and senior high school students attending public schools was marginally lower. However, these differences were minor and did not introduce substantial bias that would hinder the validity of the analysis28.

Parental socioeconomic status is associated with parental educational aspirations, set as the exposure21,34,35, and should be considered a covariate affecting children’s sleep duration, set as the outcome24. It is also a covariate in the association between cram school attendance and children’s sleep duration25. However, the crude associations we identified were not fully accounted for by the covariates alone (Table 2).

Screen time is another important covariate to consider in this study5,25,26. After adjusting for screen time, both the association between the exposure and the outcome, and the association between the mediator and the outcome, were examined. However, screen time alone did not fully account for the observed associations (Table 2).

This study showed that parental educational aspirations are inversely associated with children’s sleep. However, treating parental educational aspirations as a variable that can be intervened is unrealistic. Instead, future research should focus on the practical issue of attending cram schools as a mediating variable. East Asian countries other than Japan, such as South Korea and China, have already implemented regulations on cram school48,49. These regulations could provide valuable references for reconsidering the use of cram schools for children of parents with high aspirations in Japan. Excessive parental educational aspirations for children may foster a mindset of “studying at the expense of sleep9.” This study demonstrated that the impact of parental educational aspirations, mediated by cram school attendance, on children’s sleep duration is more pronounced in non-metropolitan areas than in metropolitan areas. This finding highlights the importance of proactively implementing sleep hygiene education, particularly in non-urban regions.

The strengths of this study are as follows: First, it contributed to the accumulation of empirical research on the determinants of sleep duration from an educational environment perspective, particularly in East Asia contexts, such as Japan, where academic achievement is highly emphasized, and children’s sleep determinants vary across countries and cultures. Second, the study used nationally representative parent-child paired data. Third, it evaluated both direct and indirect effects, providing a more comprehensive analysis.

However, this study has some limitations. Although we used the term parental educational aspirations in this study, it should be noted that the data reflect maternal educational aspirations, as the information was reported by mothers. In addition, the measurement of sleep variables can be a source of biases, as sleep duration was assessed based on children’s self-reports and coded into categories (e.g., 7–8 h) rather than exact hours, potentially leading to misclassification. Objective measures such as actigraphy would be a preferable measure, which will help reduce measurement errors related to subjective data50. Sleep was measured solely by duration, and future research should include assessments of the qualitative aspects of sleep51,52. Another limitation is that the data used in this study were collected in 2015, a decade ago. As such, it may not fully capture recent changes in digital media use and educational contexts. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution when generalizing to the current situation. It should be added that enrolment of school-age children to the cram school in Japan has been stable approximately since 201553, implying that the findings are likely to represent the current situation of children in Japan.

Conclusion

Using a national representative dataset of Japanese parent-child pairs, this study demonstrated that parental educational aspirations negatively influence the sleep duration of junior high school students directly and indirectly via cram school attendance. This relationship was less prominent in metropolitan areas than elsewhere. These findings suggest that in addition to traditional approaches promoting better sleep hygiene for children, such as encouraging lifestyle improvements and limiting screen time, raising awareness about the impact of parental educational aspirations on children’s sleep is essential.
