Remarks on the inverse Littlewood conjecture
Thomas F. Bloom, Ben Green

Abstract
. The Littlewood conjecture, proven by Konyagin and McGehee-Pigno-Smith in the 1980s, states that if A ⊂ ℤ A\subset\mathbb{Z} is a finite set of integers with | A | = N \lvert A\rvert=N then ‖ 1 A ^ ‖ 1 ⩾ c ​ log ⁡ N \|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\geqslant c\log N for some absolute constant c > 0 c>0 . We explore what structure A A must have if ‖ 1 A ^ ‖ 1 ⩽ K ​ log ⁡ N \|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\leqslant K\log N for some constant K K . Under such an assumption we prove, for instance, that A A contains a subset A ′ ⊆ A A^{\prime}\subseteq A with | A | ⩾ N 0.99 \lvert A\rvert\geqslant N^{0.99} such that | A ′ + A ′ | ≪ K O ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ | A ′ | \lvert A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}\rvert\ll K^{O(1)}\lvert A^{\prime}\rvert . As a consequence, for any k ⩾ 3 k\geqslant 3 , if N N is sufficiently large depending on k k and K K , then A A must contain an arithmetic progression of length k k . A byproduct of our analysis is a (slightly) improved bound for the constant c c .



1.
Introduction

For a finite set
A\subset\mathbf{Z}
the Fourier transform
\widehat{1_{A}}:\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
is defined by
\widehat{1_{A}}(\theta)=\sum_{n\in A}e(-n\theta)
, where
e(x):=e^{2\pi ix}
. Define

\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}=\int_{0}^{1}\lvert\widehat{1_{A}}(\theta)\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

A famous conjecture of Littlewood stated that
1

f\gg g
, equivalent to
g=O(f)
, to denote the existence of some absolute constant
c>0
such that
\left\lvert f(x)\right\rvert\geqslant c\left\lvert g(x)\right\rvert
for all sufficiently large

.

\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\gg\log N
for any set
A
of
N
integers, which is achieved for example by an arithmetic progression of length

. This was proved in 1981 independently by Konyagin
[
11
]
and McGehee, Pigno, and Smith
[

]
. A discussion of the problem and its history may be found in

.

This lower bound is universal, yet rarely achieved – in fact generically we expect
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}
to be closer to the trivial upper bound
N^{1/2}
, which is an immediate consequence of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Parseval’s identity. This is achieved when
A
is lacunary, for example, or a random positive density subset of an interval (with high probability). In fact, the only examples we know that achieve
O(\log N)
are arithmetic progressions and simple variations, for example the union of
O(1)
arithmetic progressions and some small unstructured set.

It is therefore natural, especially from the viewpoint of modern additive combinatorics, to ask the following inverse question.

Question 1.1
.

Let
K>0
be some large constant. What can we say about the structure of sets
A\subset\mathbf{Z}
with
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=N
and
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\leqslant K\log N
?

This question was asked by the second author
[
7
]
and one possible precise conjecture in this direction was formulated by Petridis
[
13
, Question 5.1]
(see Section
6
for further details). While we are some way from a full resolution of this question, in this paper we offer the following weak partial progress.

Recall that the
additive energy

E(B)
of a finite set
B\subset\mathbf{Z}
is defined by

E(B)=\#\{(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},b_{4})\in B^{4}:b_{1}+b_{2}=b_{3}+b_{4}\}.

(1.1)

We have

E(B)=\int^{1}_{0}|\widehat{1_{B}}(\theta)|^{4}d\theta,

(1.2)

as can be checked using the orthogonality relation for characters. We also write
\omega[B]:=E(B)/|B|^{3}
for the normalised additive energy of
B
, which satisfies
0<\omega[B]\leqslant 1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
N
be a sufficiently large positive integer. Let
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
K>0
. If
A
is a set of
N
integers such that
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\leqslant K\log N
then there is a subset
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
of size
\left\lvert A^{\prime}\right\rvert\gg N^{1-\delta}
such that
\omega[A^{\prime}]\gg(\delta/K)^{2}
.

The set
A^{\prime}
produced in Theorem
1.2
is in fact an initial segment of
A
.

The following two corollaries are almost immediate consequences of this and standard results in additive combinatorics.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
N
be a sufficiently large positive integer. Let
K>0
and suppose that
A\subset\mathbf{Z}
is a finite set of size
N
such that
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\leqslant K\log N
. Then there exists an arithmetic progression
P
of length
\geqslant N^{c_{K}}
such that
\left\lvert A\cap P\right\rvert\geqslant c_{K}\left\lvert P\right\rvert
, where
c_{K}>0
depends only on
K
.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
1.2
with
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
. Applying the Balog-Szemerédi-Gowers theorem (see, for example,
[
16
, Theorem 2.31]
) to the resulting set
A^{\prime}
, we obtain a set
A^{\prime\prime}\subseteq A
,
|A^{\prime\prime}|\gg K^{-O(1)}N^{1/2}
, with
\left\lvert A^{\prime\prime}+A^{\prime\prime}\right\rvert\leqslant K^{O(1)}\lvert A^{\prime\prime}\rvert
. By the Freiman-Ruzsa theorem (see, for example
[
16
, Theorem 5.33]
), there are arithmetic progressions
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}
with
r=O_{K}(1)
such that
A^{\prime\prime}\subseteq P_{1}+\cdots+P_{r}
and

\left\lvert P_{1}+\cdots+P_{r}\right\rvert=\left\lvert P_{1}\right\rvert\cdots\left\lvert P_{r}\right\rvert\ll_{K}\left\lvert A^{\prime\prime}\right\rvert.

By averaging there exists some
i
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
, and
x
such that

\left\lvert(P_{i}+x)\cap A^{\prime\prime}\right\rvert\gg_{K}\left\lvert P_{i}\right\rvert\gg_{K}\left\lvert A^{\prime\prime}\right\rvert^{1/r}.

Since
N
is large, the length of
P_{i}
is bounded below by
N^{c_{K}}
for some
c_{K}>0
.
∎

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
K>0
and let
k\geqslant 3
be an integer. Let
N
be sufficiently large in terms of
k,K
and let
A\subset\mathbf{Z}
be a finite set of size
N
such that
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\leqslant K\log N
. Then
A
contains an arithmetic progression of length
k
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Corollary
1.3
and Szemerédi’s theorem.
∎

Remark
.

The key feature of Theorem
1.2
is that the lower bound on
E(A^{\prime})
is a constant times
|A^{\prime}|^{3}
. If one is prepared to tolerate logarithmic losses then a one line application of Hölder’s inequality on the Fourier side shows that we in fact have
E(A)\geqslant N^{3}/(K\log N)^{2}
. However, this would certainly be too weak to deduce Corollary
1.4
given our current knowledge of bounds in Szemerédi’s theorem when
k\geqslant 4
.

1.2.
Bounds for the Littlewood conjecture

The lower bound
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\gg\log N
is the best possible up to an absolute constant, since this is achieved when
A
is an arithmetic progression of length
N
. In this case, one may compute (see, for example,
[
18
, II.12.1]
) that

\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}=\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\log N+O(1).

One may ask (and Hardy and Littlewood did ask
[
10
, p 167]
) whether this leading constant is the best possible or even whether
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\geqslant\|\widehat{1_{P}}\|_{1}
where
P
is an arithmetic progression of length
N
. This statement, or the slightly weaker statement that
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\geqslant(\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}-o(1))\log N
, is usually called the
Strong Littlewood Conjecture
.

Less ambitiously, one can seek to prove the best possible value of
c
in an inequality

\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\geqslant(c-o(1))\log N.

The argument of McGehee, Pigno, and Smith
[
12
]
achieved
c=1/30
. The numerics were optimised by Stegeman
[
15
]
and Yabuta
[
17
]
, the latter proving a value of
c\approx 0.1295
. We give a slight improvement on this constant.

Theorem 1.5
.

If
A\subset\mathbf{Z}
is a finite set of size
N
then

\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\geqslant(c-o(1))\log N

where
c=0.1422917\cdots
.

For comparison note that
4/\pi^{2}\approx 0.405
so we are still about a factor of
3
away from the optimal value. The value of
c
is found as the maximum of a complicated two-variable expression and presumably has no nicer form.

The proofs of Theorems
1.2
and
1.5
both hinge on a detailed analysis of the test functions used by McGehee, Pigno, and Smith. This is the main business of the paper.

In the final section of the paper we make some additional comments and record some further questions and conjectures.

Notation

For any function
f\in\ell^{1}(\mathbf{Z})
we define the Fourier transform
\widehat{f}:[0,1]\to\mathbf{C}
by

\widehat{f}(\theta)=\sum_{n}f(n)e(-n\theta),

where
e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}
. The convolution is defined in the usual way, so that if
f,g\in\ell^{1}(\mathbf{Z})
then

f\ast g(x)=\sum_{y}f(y)g(x-y).

Note that if
A=\mathrm{supp}(f)
and
B=\mathrm{supp}(g)
then
\mathrm{supp}(f\ast g)\subseteq A+B
. All of the functions we consider will have finite support, so there are no issues of convergence. We note here the useful property that
\widehat{f\ast g}=\widehat{f}\cdot\widehat{g}
. It is convenient to use inner product notation, so that if
f,g:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
we write

\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}f(n)\overline{g(n)},

and
\langle\widehat{f},\widehat{g}\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}\widehat{f}(\theta)\overline{\widehat{g}(\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}\theta
. By Parseval’s identity

\langle f,g\rangle=\langle\widehat{f},\widehat{g}\rangle.

2.
McGehee-Pigno-Smith test functions

The overall strategy to prove Theorem
1.2
is to mimic proofs of the Littlewood conjecture, aiming to prove a lower bound of the shape
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}>K\log N
. Since this lower bound is false by assumption, some stage in our purported proof must fail, which will reveal structural information about
A
.

All proofs of the Littlewood conjecture follow a similar strategy, obtaining a lower bound for
\|\widehat{1}_{A}\|_{1}
via construction of a suitable test function. In particular, our goal will be to try to construct a test function
\mathcal{R}:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
such that

(1)

\|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
and

(2)

\langle 1_{A},\mathcal{R}\rangle>K\log N
.

By Parseval’s identity and the triangle inequality these facts combined imply
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}>K\log N
. This contradicts our hypothesis, so the construction of such a test function must fail. The new observation is that, examining the details of the construction of the test function, this yields some additive information about
A
.

We will use the test functions of McGehee, Pigno, and Smith
[
12
]
. These are not the only ones capable of proving the Littlewood conjecture but, of the ones we are aware of, they perform the best from the quantitative point of view. For a discussion of other possible test functions we refer to the survey paper of Fournier
[
4
]
.

The following lemma details the construction of these test functions.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
b>0
and set
c=1-e^{-b}
. If
f:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{R}
is a finitely supported function then there exists a function
\mathcal{R}_{f}:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}

(
which depends on
b
)
with the following properties:

(1)

\mathcal{R}_{f}
is supported on
\mathbf{Z}_{\leqslant 0}
;

(2)

\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{f}\right\rVert_{2}\leqslant 2^{1/2}b\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{2}
;

(3)

\|\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{f}}+1\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
; and

(4)

if
\|\widehat{f}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
then, for any
g:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
with
\|\widehat{g}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
, the function

h:=g+cf+g\ast\mathcal{R}_{f}

satisfies
\|\widehat{h}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
.

Proof.

Since
\lvert\widehat{f}\rvert
is a
1
-periodic symmetric function we can write

\lvert\widehat{f}(\theta)\rvert=\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}c_{n}e(n\theta)

for some
c_{n}\in\mathbf{R}
with
c_{n}=c_{-n}
, and define
h_{f}:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
by

h_{f}(n)=\begin{cases}c_{0}&\textrm{if }n=0\\
2c_{n}&\textrm{ if }n<0\textrm{, and}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise,}\end{cases}

so that
h_{f}
is supported on
\mathbf{Z}_{\leqslant 0}
. Furthermore, since

\widehat{h_{f}}(\theta)=c_{0}+2\sum_{n<0}c_{n}e(n\theta),

for any
\theta\in\mathbf{R}/\mathbf{Z}
we have

\Re\widehat{h_{f}}(\theta)=c_{0}+\sum_{n<0}c_{n}(e(n\theta)+e(-n\theta))=\lvert\widehat{f}(\theta)\rvert\geqslant 0.

By Parseval’s theorem,

\left\lVert h_{f}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=c_{0}^{2}+2\sum_{n\neq 0}c_{n}^{2}\leqslant 2\sum_{n}c_{n}^{2}=2\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

We may now define

\mathcal{R}_{f}(n)=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\frac{(-b)^{j}}{j!}h_{f}^{(j)}(n),

where
h_{f}^{(j)}=h_{f}\ast\cdots\ast h_{f}
denotes the
j
-fold convolution. Equivalently, we may define
\mathcal{R}_{f}
via its Fourier transform by

\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{f}}(\theta)=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\frac{(-b)^{j}}{j!}\widehat{h_{f}}(\theta)^{j}=e^{-b\widehat{h_{f}}(\theta)}-1.

Since
\mathrm{supp}(g_{1}\ast g_{2})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(g_{1})+\mathrm{supp}(g_{2})
for any pair of functions
g_{1},g_{2}:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
, it is clear that
\mathcal{R}_{f}
is supported on
\mathbf{Z}_{\leqslant 0}
, which is item (1).

The inequality
\left\lvert e^{-z}-1\right\rvert\leqslant\left\lvert z\right\rvert
, valid whenever
\Re z\geqslant 0
, implies

\lvert\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{f}}(\theta)\rvert\leqslant b\lvert\widehat{h_{f}}(\theta)\rvert

pointwise, and hence in particular

\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{f}\right\rVert_{2}\leqslant b\left\lVert h_{f}\right\rVert_{2}\leqslant 2^{1/2}b\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{2},

which is item (2).
We also have

\lvert\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{f}}(\theta)+1\rvert=e^{-b\Re\widehat{h_{f}}(\theta)}=e^{-b\lvert\widehat{f}(\theta)\rvert}\leqslant 1

pointwise, which is (3).

Finally, suppose
\|\widehat{f}\|_{\infty},\|\widehat{g}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
and consider

h=cf+g+g\ast\mathcal{R}_{f},

where
c=1-e^{-b}
.
Taking the Fourier transform, we have the pointwise inequality

\lvert\widehat{h}\rvert\leqslant\lvert\widehat{g}\rvert\lvert 1+\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{f}}\rvert+c\lvert\widehat{f}\rvert\leqslant e^{-b\lvert\widehat{f}\rvert}+(1-e^{-b})\lvert\widehat{f}\rvert\leqslant 1,

which is (4).
In the final step here we used the inequality
e^{-bx}+(1-e^{-b})x\leqslant 1
, which is valid for all
x
with
0\leqslant x\leqslant 1
.
∎

The form of item (4) lends itself to an iterative argument, the details of which are as follows.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
b>0
and
c=1-e^{-b}
. Let
J\in\mathbf{N}
and suppose that
g_{1},\ldots,g_{J}:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{R}
are finitely supported functions such that
\|\widehat{g_{i}}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
for all
i=1,\dots,J
. Then there exists a test function
\mathcal{R}:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbf{C}
such that
\|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
and

\mathcal{R}=c\sum_{j=1}^{J}g_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}D_{j},

(2.1)

where the function
D_{j}
has the shape

D_{j}=c\sum_{1\leqslant i<j}g_{i}\ast F_{ij},

(2.2)

where

\|F_{ij}\|_{2}\leqslant 2^{1/2}b\left\lVert g_{j}\right\rVert_{2},

(2.3)

and
F_{ij}
is supported on
\mathbf{Z}_{\leqslant 0}
.

Proof.

We iteratively define functions
\mathcal{R}_{i}:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{C}
by
\mathcal{R}_{1}:=cg_{1}
and

\mathcal{R}_{i+1}:=\mathcal{R}_{i}+cg_{i+1}+D_{i+1}

for
i\geqslant 2
, where

D_{i+1}:=\mathcal{R}_{i}\ast\mathcal{R}_{g_{i+1}}.

We then set
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{J}
. The fact that
\mathcal{R}
has the form (
2.1
) is a trivial induction.

By the final property of Lemma
2.1
and a trivial induction, we have
\|\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{i}}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
for all
i
. A downwards induction on
i
using the identity

\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{i}}=c\widehat{g_{i}}+\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{i-1}}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{g_{i}}}+1)

yields, for any
i<j
,

\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{j}}=c\sum_{i<k\leqslant j}\widehat{g_{k}}\prod_{k<\ell\leqslant j}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{g_{\ell}}}+1)+\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{i}}\prod_{i<\ell\leqslant j}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{g_{\ell}}}+1),

where the product over
k<\ell\leqslant j
is interpreted as
1
when
k=j
.
In particular, taking
i=1
we have

\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{j}}=c\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant j}\widehat{g_{i}}\prod_{i<\ell\leqslant j}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{g_{\ell}}}+1).

From the definition of
D_{j}
it then follows that

\widehat{D_{j}}=\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{j-1}}\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{g_{j}}}=c\sum_{1\leqslant i<j}\widehat{g_{i}}\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{g_{j}}}\prod_{i<\ell<j}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{g_{\ell}}}+1).

Defining

\widehat{F_{ij}}:=\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{g_{j}}}\prod_{i<\ell\leqslant j}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{g_{\ell}}}+1),

we see that (
2.2
) holds.

The bound (
2.3
) now follows using Lemma
2.1
(2) and (3) and Parseval’s identity
\|\widehat{F_{ij}}\|_{2}=\|F_{ij}\|_{2}
. The fact that
\mathcal{R}
is supported on
\mathbf{Z}_{\leqslant 0}
follows from Lemma
2.1
(1).
∎

3.
A lower bound for
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}

We will now apply the McGehee-Pigno-Smith test function from the previous section to prove the following technical proposition. It will be the key input in the proof of our two main results, Theorems
1.2
and
1.5
. An initial segment of
A
is a non-empty subset of the shape
A\cap(-\infty,x]
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
b>0
be a real parameter, and let
J\in\mathbf{N}
. Let
A\subset\mathbf{Z}
be a finite set and suppose that
A_{1},\dots,A_{J}
are initial segments of
A
with
A_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{J}
. Let
\lambda>1
be a parameter, and suppose that
\left\lvert A_{j+1}\right\rvert\geqslant\lambda\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert
for
j=1,\dots,J
. Then

\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\geqslant(1-e^{-b})\left(J-\frac{b\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\lambda}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{J}\omega[A_{i}]^{1/2}\right),

where
\omega[\cdot]
denotes normalised additive energy.

Proof.

For
j=1,\dots,J
, let
\mu_{j}=\frac{1}{\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert}1_{A_{j}}
. Then
\left\lVert\mu_{j}\right\rVert_{1}=1
and
\|\widehat{\mu_{j}}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
. Let
\mathcal{R}
be the associated McGehee-Pigno-Smith test function given by Lemma
2.2
, with parameter
b
, so that
\|\widehat{\mathcal{R}}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
and

\mathcal{R}=c\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mu_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}D_{j},

where
c=1-e^{-b}
. Since
\langle\mu_{j},1_{A}\rangle=1
for all
j
, we deduce that

\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\geqslant\left\lvert\langle\mathcal{R},1_{A}\rangle\right\rvert\geqslant cJ-\Big|\sum_{j}\langle D_{j},1_{A}\rangle\Big|.

(3.1)

It therefore suffices to prove an upper bound on
\big|\sum_{j}\langle D_{j},1_{A}\rangle\big|
of sufficient quality. By (
2.2
) we have
D_{j}=c\sum_{i<j}\mu_{i}\ast F_{ij}
,
and so

\sum_{j=1}^{J}\langle D_{j},1_{A}\rangle=c\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant J}\langle 1_{A},\mu_{i}\ast F_{ij}\rangle.

(3.2)

Since
F_{ij}
is supported on
\mathbf{Z}_{\leqslant 0}
, from the nesting property of the
A_{i}
we have

\langle 1_{A},\mu_{i}\ast F_{ij}\rangle=\langle 1_{A_{i}},\mu_{i}\ast F_{ij}\rangle=\langle 1_{A_{i}}\ast\mu_{i}^{\circ},F_{ij}\rangle,

where
g^{\circ}(x):=\overline{g(-x)}
.
Moreover by (
2.3
) we have

\|F_{ij}\|_{2}\leqslant 2^{1/2}b\left\lVert\mu_{j}\right\rVert_{2}=2^{1/2}b\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert^{-1/2}.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we thus have

\big|\langle 1_{A},\mu_{i}\ast F_{ij}\rangle\big|=\big|\langle 1_{A_{i}}\ast\mu_{i}^{\circ},F_{ij}\rangle\big|\leqslant 2^{1/2}b|A_{j}|^{-1}\|1_{A_{i}}\ast\mu_{i}^{\circ}\|_{2}.

(3.3)

However one may compute that

\|1_{A_{i}}\ast\mu_{i}^{\circ}\|_{2}=\frac{E(A_{i})^{1/2}}{|A_{i}|}.

Combining this with (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) yields

\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{J}\langle D_{j},1_{A}\rangle\Big|\leqslant c2^{1/2}b\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant J}\frac{E(A_{i})^{1/2}}{|A_{i}||A_{j}|^{1/2}}.

Substituting into (
3.1
) gives

\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\geqslant(1-e^{-b})J\Big(J-2^{1/2}b\sum_{i=1}^{J}\frac{E(A_{i})^{1/2}}{\left\lvert A_{i}\right\rvert}\sum_{i<j\leqslant J}\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert^{-1/2}\Big).

By the assumption in the lemma we have
|A_{j}|^{-1/2}\leqslant\lambda^{(i-j)/2}|A_{i}|^{-1/2}
for
j\geqslant i
. Summing the geometric series yields

\sum_{j>i}|A_{j}|^{-1/2}\leqslant\frac{\left\lvert A_{i}\right\rvert^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{\lambda}-1},

and the stated conclusion follows using the definition
\omega[A_{i}]=E(A_{i})/|A_{i}|^{3}
.
∎

4.
Improved constants for Littlewood’s conjecture

In the next two sections we deduce our two main theorems from Proposition
3.1
, starting here with Theorem
1.5
, the improved constant for Littlewood’s conjecture.

One may deduce Littlewood’s conjecture (with some constant) from Proposition
3.1
by taking
A_{i}
to be roughly the
\approx\lambda^{i}
smallest elements of
A
for all
i
and using the trivial bound
\omega[A_{i}]\leqslant 1
, choosing
b>0
and
1/\lambda
suitably small absolute constants yields
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\gg J
, where
\lambda^{J}\asymp N
and hence
J\gg_{\lambda}\log N
as required.

To extract a decent constant from this is a delicate optimisation in both
b
and
\lambda
, and this was the task considered by
[
15
,
17
]
. To get the slightly better constant in Theorem
1.5
, we will use a slightly less trivial bound for the
\omega[A_{i}]
, obtained using a rearrangement inequality.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
A,B
are finite sets of integers with cardinalities
n,m
respectively, where
m\leqslant n
. Then

E(A,B)=\#\{(a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2})\in A\times B\times A\times B\}\leqslant nm^{2}\big(1-\frac{m}{3n}\big)+\frac{m}{3}.

In particular,
\omega[A]\leqslant\frac{2}{3}+o_{n\rightarrow\infty}(1)
.

Proof.

This follows from a well-known rearrangement inequality (see Gabriel
[
5
, Theorem 3]
or
[
9
, Theorem 376]
3

3

It is important to note, when reading this reference, the convention established on the previous page: ‘We may agree that, when there is no indication to the contrary, sums involving several suffixes are extended over values of the suffixes whose sum vanishes’.
). A quick proof is as follows. Let the elements of
A
be
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{n}
, and the elements of
B
be
b_{1}<\cdots<b_{m}
. Writing
r(x)
for the number of pairs
(a,b)\in A\times B
with
a+b=x
, we have

r(a_{i}+b_{j})\leqslant 1+\min(m-i,j-1)+\min(n-j,i-1).

(4.1)

Indeed, the constant
1
counts the representation
a_{i}+b_{j}
itself. The
\min(m-i,j-1)
term is an upper bound for representations
a_{i}+b_{j}=a_{i^{\prime}}+b_{j^{\prime}}
with
i<i^{\prime}\leqslant m
, which must have also have
1\leqslant j^{\prime}<j
; for each
i^{\prime}
(or
j^{\prime}
) there is at most one such representation. The
\min(n-j,i-1)
term is an upper bound for representations
a_{i}+b_{j}=a_{i^{\prime}}+b_{j^{\prime}}
with
1\leqslant i^{\prime}<i
(and hence
j<j^{\prime}\leqslant n
).
Now sum (
4.1
) over
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
and
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
. The left-hand side becomes
E(A,B)
, and (after a computation) the right-hand side becomes
nm^{2}\big(1-\frac{m}{3n}\big)+\frac{m}{3}
.
∎

We now give the deduction of Theorem
1.5
from Proposition
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
b>0
and
\lambda\geqslant 2
be some constants to be chosen later, and fix some
A\subset\mathbf{Z}
of size
N
, for some large
N
(where the notion of ‘large’ may depend on
\lambda
and
b
). We now construct nested initial segments
A_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{J}
of
A
, for a certain parameter
J
.

Let
A_{1}
be the set consisting of the smallest
\lfloor\log N\rfloor
elements of
A
. In general, if
A_{1},\ldots,A_{j}
have been constructed, let
A_{j+1}
be the set of the smallest
\lceil\lambda\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert\rceil
elements of
A
and continue. Otherwise, we set
J=j
and halt. Furthermore we have
\left\lvert A_{j+1}\right\rvert\geqslant\lambda\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert
for
j=1,\dots,J
. Additionally,
\left\lvert A_{j+1}\right\rvert\leqslant\lambda\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert+1
for
j=1,\dots,J
, and hence by induction

|A_{j}|\leqslant\lambda^{r}\big(|A_{j-r}|+\lambda^{-1}+\lambda^{-2}+\cdots+\lambda^{-r}\big)

for any
r\geqslant 1
.
Taking
r=j-1
(and since
\lambda\geqslant 2
) we have

\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert\leqslant\lambda^{j-1}(1+\log N).

By definition, the parameter
J
was minimal such that

N<\lambda\left\lvert A_{J}\right\rvert.

It follows that

N<\lambda^{J}(1+\log N)

and so

J\geqslant(1-o(1))\frac{\log N}{\log\lambda}.

(4.2)

Here, and below,
o(1)
means a quantity tending to
0
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.

Applying Lemma
4.1
to
A_{1},\dots,A_{J}
and substituting in to Proposition
3.1
, and recalling our bound (
4.2
) on
J
, we deduce that

\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\geqslant(1-o(1))f(b,\lambda)\log N

where

f(b,\lambda)=\frac{1-e^{-b}}{\log\lambda}\left(1-\frac{2b}{\sqrt{3}(\sqrt{\lambda}-1)}\right).

It remains to choose
b>0
and
\lambda\in[2,\infty)
to maximise
f(b,\lambda)
. Computational investigations tell us that the maximum value is

f(b,\lambda)=0.1422917\cdots

achieved at

b=1.3766505\cdots\quad\textrm{and}\quad\lambda=36.1127893\cdots.\qed

5.
A structural inverse result

Now we turn to the deduction of Theorem
1.2
.
In deducing Theorem
1.2
we are not concerned with constants, and so we record the following simpler consequence of Proposition
3.1
.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
\eta\in(0,1/4]
. Let
A\subset\mathbf{Z}
be a finite set. Suppose we have a nested sequence
A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{J}
of initial segments of
A

such that

\left\lvert A_{i-1}\right\rvert\leqslant(1-\eta)\left\lvert A_{i}\right\rvert.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that

\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\gg J-c\eta^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{J}\omega[A_{i}]^{1/2}.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Proposition
3.1
with
b=1
and
1/\lambda=1-\eta
, so that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}-1}\ll\eta^{-1}
.
∎

We may now prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We may assume that
K\geqslant\frac{1}{100}
(say) since the Littlewood conjecture is true. If
\delta/K\leqslant N^{-1/2}
then the result is trivial by taking
A^{\prime}=A
, since any set of size
N
has at least
N^{2}
additive quadruples. If
\delta\leqslant C(\log N)^{-1}
for some absolute constant
C>0
then the result is again true with
A^{\prime}=A
by applying Hölder’s inequality on the Fourier side, noting that
E(A)^{1/4}
is the
L^{4}
-norm of
\widehat{1}_{A}
.

In what follows we suppose that
\delta/K>N^{-1/2}
and that
\delta\geqslant C(\log N)^{-1}
(where
C>0
is some absolute constant to be chosen later).
Set
\eta:=c\delta/K
, where
c\in(0,\frac{1}{4}]
is an absolute constant to be specified later. In particular,
\eta^{-1}\ll N^{1/2}
.

We construct nested initial segments
A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{J}
of
A
in the following fashion. Let
A_{1}
be the smallest
\lfloor N^{1-\delta}\rfloor
elements of
A
. In general, if
A_{1},\ldots,A_{j}
have been constructed, then let
A_{j+1}
be the set of the smallest
\lceil(1-\eta)^{-1}\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert\rceil
elements of
A
if
A
has this many elements, or else halt with
j=J
. By construction it is clear that
\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert\leqslant(1-\eta)\left\lvert A_{j+1}\right\rvert
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
. Furthermore,
\left\lvert A_{j+1}\right\rvert\leqslant(1-\eta)^{-1}\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert+1
for
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
, and hence by the same induction leading to (
4.2
) we have

\left\lvert A_{j}\right\rvert\leqslant(1-\eta)^{1-j}(N^{1-\delta}+\eta^{-1})\ll(1-\eta)^{1-j}N^{1-\delta}.

(5.1)

The parameter
J
was the minimal
j\geqslant 1
such that

N<(1-\eta)^{-1}\left\lvert A_{J}\right\rvert,

and hence by (
5.1
) we have
N^{\delta}\ll(1-\eta)^{-J}\leqslant e^{2\eta J}
. Since
\delta\geqslant C(\log N)^{-1}
(and
N
is sufficiently large), provided
C>0
is chosen sufficiently large, it follows that
J\gg\eta^{-1}\delta\log N
.

By Corollary
5.1
we see that either

\sum_{i=1}^{J}\omega[A_{i}]^{1/2}\gg\eta J

(5.2)

or
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\gg\eta^{-1}\delta\log N
. If the constant
c
is chosen appropriately then, since
\eta=c\delta/K
, the latter option is contrary to our assumption. Thus (
5.2
) holds and so there exists some
i
such that

\omega[A_{i}]\gg\eta^{2}\gg(\delta/K)^{2}.

The conclusion now follows taking
A^{\prime}=A_{i}
(noting that since
A_{1}\subseteq A^{\prime}
we always have
\left\lvert A^{\prime}\right\rvert\geqslant\lfloor N^{1-\delta}\rfloor
).
∎

6.
Speculations about stronger structure

The structure found by Theorem
1.2
is, although non-trivial, still quite weak compared to the kind of structure one expects to find. For instance, we do not know of a counterexample to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 6.1
.

Let
A\subset\mathbf{Z}
be a finite set of size
N
. If
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\leqslant K\log N
then for some
m,r\ll_{K}1
the following holds. There are finite arithmetic progressions
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}\subset\mathbb{Z}
, each of length at most
N
, and finite sets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}\subset\mathbf{Z}
such that
\big|\bigcup_{j}X_{j}\big|=o_{K}(N)
,
together with
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{m},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{r}\in\{-1,1\}
such that

1_{A}=\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}\epsilon_{i}1_{P_{i}}+\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}\eta_{j}1_{X_{j}}.

A very similar (but slightly stronger) conjecture was put forward in print by Petridis
[
13
, Question 5.1]
. Analogous results are known to be true in finite field settings due to work of Sanders and the second author
[
6
]
.

A consequence of Conjecture
6.1
, still much stronger than anything we can prove, would be that there exists an arithmetic progression
P
of length at most
N
such that
|A\cap P|\gg_{K}N
.

A third possible inverse statement concerns the ‘dimension’ of
A
, defined as the size of the largest dissociated subset of
A
. As noted in the introduction, Pichorides
[
14
]
has proved that if
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\leqslant K\log N
then

\dim A\ll_{K}(\log N)^{3}.

In the other direction, there are examples where
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\ll\log N
and yet
\dim(A)\gg(\log N)^{2}
– simply take the union of an arithmetic progression
P
of size
\approx N
and a dissociated set of size
\approx X=\lfloor(\log N)^{2}\rfloor
, and use the triangle inequality together with the observation that

\|\widehat{1_{X}}\|_{1}\leqslant\left\lvert X\right\rvert^{1/2}\ll\log N.

We conjecture that this is the best possible.

Conjecture 6.2
.

Let
K>0
and
N
be sufficiently large depending only on
K
. Let
A\subset\mathbf{Z}
be a finite set of size
N
. If
\|\widehat{1_{A}}\|_{1}\leqslant K\log N
then
\dim A\ll_{K}(\log N)^{2}
. Furthermore,
A
contains a subset of size
\gg_{K}N
and dimension
\ll_{K}\log N
.
