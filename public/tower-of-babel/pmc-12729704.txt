Impact of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) in pediatric emergency departments: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Mohammed Alsabri, Mohammed Tarek Hasan, Shree Rath, Ahmed Bostamy Elsnhory, Mohamed Ismaeil Elnady, Ahmed Farid Gadelmawla, Suhel F Batarseh, Mohammed Alaswad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoint-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is a bedside diagnostic tool clinicians use to provide immediate insights and guide therapeutic interventions. It has become increasingly significant in pediatric emergency departments (EDs) for diagnosing conditions, managing critical scenarios, and guiding procedures due to its portability, ease of use, and lack of radiation. This study aims to systematically review and analyze the efficacy of POCUS compared to conventional diagnostic methods in pediatric emergency settings. A literature search was conducted across PubMed, SCOPUS, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library up to February 2025. The inclusion criteria were pediatric patients aged 1 month to 18 years in EDs, with studies comparing POCUS to conventional methods. Primary outcomes included first-attempt procedural success and overall success rates. Secondary outcomes included time to procedure completion, mean number of attempts, hospitalization rates, and discharge rates. Data analysis was conducted in R employing a random-effects model, with dichotomous data analyzed as risk ratio (RR) and 95% confidence interval (CI), and continuous data as unbiased standardized mean difference (SMD). Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05. Eighteen randomized controlled trials involving 2264 patients met the inclusion criteria. POCUS significantly improved first-attempt success (RR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.09–1.43). The overall procedural success showed a significant benefit with POCUS (RR = 1.12; 95% CI: 1.03–1.22). However, no significant differences were noted in the time to procedure completion, number of attempts for a successful procedure, and rates of hospitalization and discharge to home. POCUS significantly improves first-attempt and overall procedural success rates in pediatric emergency settings, although it does not significantly reduce procedure times or the number of attempts. These findings underscore the importance of integrating POCUS into pediatric emergency care to enhance diagnostic accuracy and procedural success, though further research is needed to optimize its implementation across different age groups and procedures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-025-02159-5.Keywords: Point-of-care ultrasound, Emergency department, Pediatrics, Intravenous access, Lumbar puncture

Background

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is a goal-directed, bedside diagnostic modality performed by clinicians directly involved in patient care. It provides immediate diagnostic insights and guides therapeutic interventions [1–3]. In pediatric emergency departments, POCUS plays a pivotal role in diagnosing conditions, managing critical care scenarios, and providing procedural guidance [4]. The advantages of portability, ease of use, and lack of radiation make it particularly well-suited for pediatric populations, where conventional imaging methods can be logistically challenging or pose additional risks [5, 6].

POCUS is invaluable for rapidly assessing critically ill children, identifying reversible causes of clinical deterioration, and guiding resuscitation efforts [7]. Its applications extend to managing fractures, evaluating bladder fullness for catheterization, guiding lumbar punctures, and facilitating intravenous (IV) access [8]. For instance, POCUS can accurately detect fractures, guide reduction procedures, and confirm alignment [9]. A study highlighted POCUS's high sensitivity and specificity in detecting distal forearm fractures in children, with benefits such as reduced pain and shorter procedural durations compared to X-rays [10]. Similarly, POCUS improves bladder catheterization success rates by minimizing attempts, thereby reducing discomfort and procedural time [11]. Particularly in settings of multiorgan trauma, POCUS has been recognized as a vital tool to aid in rapid assessment of patients in trauma [2].

Despite its advantages, challenges such as limited equipment, training gaps, time constraints, and resistance to adoption hinder POCUS integration into pediatric emergency care [12–15]. The American Academy of Pediatrics and the American College of Emergency Physicians emphasize the need for structured training and standardization to ensure effective implementation [8, 16]. This systematic review aims to evaluate the efficacy of POCUS compared to conventional diagnostic methods in pediatric emergency settings. By synthesizing evidence from RCTs, it seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of POCUS's role in enhancing pediatric emergency care.

Materials and methods

Study design

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [17] and adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [18].

Search strategy

A comprehensive and exhaustive literature search was performed across multiple databases, including PubMed, SCOPUS, Web of Science (WOS), EMBASE, and Cochrane Library, from inception to February 2025. The search strategy combined medical subject headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to “Point-of-Care Ultrasound,” “POCUS,” “Pediatrics,” and “Emergency Department.” Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to refine the search, and filters were applied to include only RCTs published in English. The detailed search strategy for each database is outlined in Supplementary Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following PICO framework:

Population (P): Pediatric patients aged 1 month to 18 years presenting to emergency departments.

Intervention (I): Use of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) for diagnostic or procedural purposes.

Comparison (C): Conventional diagnostic methods, including radiography, physical examination, or standard care.

Outcomes (O): Primary outcomes included first-attempt procedural success and overall success rates. Secondary outcomes included time to procedure completion, mean number of attempts, hospitalization rates, and discharge rates.

Exclusion criteria encompassed non-English publications, studies without full-text availability, non-randomized designs, and those not involving pediatric populations.

Study selection

Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts to identify eligible studies. Full-text articles were retrieved for detailed evaluation. Discrepancies during the screening process were resolved through consensus or consultation with a third senior reviewer.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data from the included studies were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted data included study characteristics such as the place of study, study design, total sample size, and allocation to POCUS or conventional groups. Details about the study duration, inclusion and exclusion criteria, intervention specifics (e.g., frequency specifications, transducer location, and planes of measurement), pre-procedure requirements, control modality, and conclusions were also collected. Additionally, baseline patient demographics, including age, sex, race, and symptoms (e.g., abdominal pain, dysuria, flank pain, hematuria, and vomiting), were recorded for both POCUS and conventional groups, along with any differences in outcomes between the two modalities. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB-2) [19]. Each study was evaluated for potential biases in randomization, blinding, outcome assessment, and reporting.

Statistical analysis

We used R version 4.5.0 and R Studio version 2024.12.1 + 563. Dichotomous data were analyzed as risk ratio (RR) and 95% confidence interval (CI), and continuous data as unbiased standardized mean difference (SMD) or Hedges’ g [20] and 95% CI. Statistical heterogeneity among the studies was assessed by visual inspection of the forest plot, besides using I-squared (I2) and chi-squared (Chi2) statistics. I2 values of 50% were indicative of high heterogeneity. A random-effects model was applied when there was a significant variation in the data. Other than that, the fixed-effect model was applied. We used Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment for random-effects to improve CI coverage [21]. Prediction intervals (PI) were incorporated into forest plots, particularly for outcomes with random variables. To formally identify significant outliers, we used Baujat plots [22]. For assessment of publication bias and small-study effects, we used the Doi plot and LFK index [23]. Trim-and-fill method was used to estimate the number of missing studies and calculate adjusted pooled estimates. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

Results

Screening and study selection

A comprehensive search strategy identified a total of 17,746 records across multiple databases. Following the removal of 6,991 duplicate records, 10,755 titles and abstracts underwent screening. Of these, 10,679 were excluded for failing to meet the predefined eligibility criteria. A total of 73 full-text articles were assessed for inclusion, with 55 excluded for reasons such as ineligible study designs, population mismatches, or insufficient POCUS-related data. Ultimately, 18 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis [24–41]. Study characteristics

Included studies consisted of 18 RCTs, with a total sample size of 2264 patients. Patients were stratified into groups based on whether they received point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) (N = 1068) or conventional methods (N = 1060). The studies were conducted in various countries, including the USA, Canada, Australia, and Turkey, spanning from 2007 to 2020. Detailed characteristics of included studies are summarized in Table 1. The clinical contexts varied; some studies included infants and children requiring lumbar puncture, bladder catheterization, or venous access, while others focused on specific subgroups such as those with spinal deformities or requiring urgent/emergency access. The inclusion criteria typically targeted pediatric populations (neonates to adolescents), with the age range across studies spanning from 1 day to 18 years. Several studies noted improved success rates and reduced number of attempts or procedural time in the POCUS group compared to conventional methods. Summary of included studies

Primary outcomes

First-attempt success

The pooled analysis, including thirteen studies, covering a total of 1,607 patients, showed that using POCUS significantly improved the success rate of first attempt cannulation compared to conventional techniques (RR = 1.25; 95% CI: 1.09–1.43; p = 0.001). This means there was a 25% higher chance of success on the first try, which could help reduce complications and make vascular access procedures more efficient (Fig. 2A). However, there was substantial heterogeneity across the studies (I2 = 72.3%, τ2 = 0.0398). Sensitivity analysis, using a leave-one-out approach, showed that the results remained consistent (RR range: 1.19–1.29), confirming the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Figure 1). The Doi plot revealed major asymmetry (LFK index = 2.57), indicating potential publication bias (Supplementary Figure2). Additionally, the Baujat plot highlighted studies like Curtis 2015 and Vinograd 2019 as having a strong influence on overall heterogeneity (Supplementary Figure3).

Forest plot of primary outcomes: A. First-attempt success; B. Overall success rates

Overall procedural success

A total of nine studies were included in the analysis. Using a random effects model, the results showed a statistically significant benefit of POCUS, with a risk ratio (RR) of 1.12 [95% CI: 1.03–1.22], translating to a 12% increase in the likelihood of success compared to conventional methods (p = 0.0093) (Fig.2B). There was moderate heterogeneity among the studies, reflected by an I2 value of 54.6%, Tau2 = 0.0089, and a Chi2 of 17.62 (df = 8, p = 0.0242).To check the stability of the findings, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed. The overall effect remained statistically significant no matter which study was removed, with the pooled RR ranging from 1.09 [1.01–1.17] to 1.15 [1.03–1.27]. The heterogeneity estimates fluctuated slightly but stayed within a reasonable range (I2 between 43.7% and 59.9%) (Supplementary Figure 4). When assessing for publication bias, the DOI plot and LFK index pointed toward major asymmetry (LFK = 2.91), suggesting that bias could be present (Supplementary Figure 5). Further, the funnel plot showed some asymmetry, supporting the possibility of missing or unpublished studies (Supplementary Figure 6). The Baujat plot (Supplementary Figure 7) highlighted Neal 2017 as having the strongest influence on the overall result. Zummer 2020 and Kessler 2018 also stood out for contributing more to heterogeneity compared to the other included studies.

Secondary outcomes

Time to completion of procedure

For the mean time to completion of the procedure, seven studies were included, with a total of 932 patients (462 in the POCUS group and 470 in the conventional group). The pooled analysis, using a random effects model, found a nonsignificant mean difference of −9.32 seconds (95% CI: −20.05 to 1.42; p = 0.0889), slightly favoring POCUS (Fig. 3A). Heterogeneity was considerable (I2 = 89.1%, Tau2 = 175.47), indicating substantial variability between studies. Sensitivity analyses using a leave-one-out approach confirmed the robustness of the results, with no single study significantly altering the overall findings (all p > 0.05) (Supplementary Figure 8). The Doi plot revealed major asymmetry (LFK index = −5.87), suggesting potential publication bias (Supplementary Figure 9). Similarly, the funnel plot with trim and fill analysis (Supplementary Figure 10) showed visual asymmetry, with missing studies likely on the right side, although no studies were imputed, implying limited impact on the pooled estimate. A Baujat plot (Supplementary Figure 11) identified Curtis 2015 as contributing most to the observed heterogeneity and influence. While Vinograd 2019 and Dessie 2018 also had a moderate influence, excluding Curtis 2015 or any other study did not materially change the overall results.

Forest plot of: A. Mean time to completion of procedure; B. Mean number of attempts to success

Mean number of attempts for successful procedure

For the mean number of attempts for a successful procedure, three studies (n = 492) compared POCUS versus conventional. The mean difference was −0.49 (95% CI: −1.33 to 0.36), favoring POCUS but not reaching statistical significance (Z = −1.13, p = 0.26) (Fig. 3B). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 84.8%, τ2 = 0.50, p = 0.0014), indicating considerable variability in effect estimates. Leave-one-out analysis showed consistent results, with mean differences ranging from −0.06 to −0.77. No study significantly altered the overall estimate or reduced heterogeneity (Supplementary Figure 12). The Baujat plot indicated that Doniger 2009 contributed the most to the observed heterogeneity, while Curtis 2015 had the greatest influence on the overall effect size (Supplementary Figure13). The funnel plot based on the trim and fill method suggested potential publication bias or small study effects, showing asymmetry with missing studies on the positive side (Supplementary Figure 14). Similarly, the DOI plot demonstrated major asymmetry (LFK index = −4.52), supporting the likelihood of publication bias (Supplementary Figure 15).

Hospitalization

Two randomized controlled trials (N = 210) compared POCUS (n = 106) with conventional assessment (n = 104). The pooled risk ratio was 1.04 (95% CI: 0.69–1.57), with no significant difference between groups (Z = 0.19, p = 0.8473) (Fig.4A). Heterogeneity was negligible (I2 = 0%, p = 0.8519), supporting the fixed effect model. The LFK index indicated minor asymmetry (−1.69), suggesting low risk of publication bias (Supplementary Figure 16).

Forest plot of A. Hospitalization; B. Discharge to home

Discharge to home

The analysis showed no significant difference between POCUS and conventional modalities regarding discharge home (Risk Ratio [RR] = 1.00, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.84 to 1.18; Z = −0.04, p = 0.9688). Heterogeneity among the studies was negligible (Tau2 = 0; Chi2 = 0.00, df = 1, p = 0.9742; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 4B). Assessment of publication bias using the Doi plot and LFK index revealed major asymmetry (LFK index = 2.3), suggesting potential bias in the reporting or selection of studies (Supplementary Figure 17).

Quality assessment

We used the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool to evaluate the overall risk of bias in 18 randomized controlled trials. The results revealed that 12 studies (67%) had a low risk of bias, indicating strong methodological quality. However, two studies (11%) raised some concerns, mainly due to biases with randomization and allocation concealment. Four studies (22%) were classified as having a high risk of bias. Specifically, Bair 2008, Dessie 2018, and Witt 2004 exhibited biases in randomization and allocation concealment, which could introduce selection bias, while Lennon 2024 demonstrated deviations from the planned interventions (Supplementary Figures 18, 19).

Discussion

This meta-analysis assessed the efficacy of POCUS in improving patient outcomes and ease of procedures in pediatric patients across 18 RCTs involving 2,264 pediatric patients. POCUS significantly improved rates of first-attempt success over conventional techniques by nearly 25%. Similar procedure times were noted across both techniques, however, a slightly lesser number of attempts were needed when using POCUS. Overall procedural success rates were improved significantly as well. Length of hospital stay and discharge rates were similar across both groups.

POCUS aids in diagnosing multiple conditions, acting as a screening test for detecting different concurrent injuries or pathologies [42]. This holds significant importance, especially in the emergency department, when patients are more likely to suffer from polytrauma [43]. In these settings, achieving procedural success within fewer attempts aids in the administration of early intervention, thus reducing the risk of mortality. POCUS improves diagnostic accuracy, which can help detect early changes in organ systems like cardiac tamponade [44]. In addition, by providing visualization aid, POCUS helps to administer invasive interventions like lumbar puncture and catheterization faster than standard techniques. Particularly for lumbar puncture, ultrasound guidance can increase amount of CSF collected, and lower the risk of traumatic taps [34]. Ultrasound aided cannulation allows for exploration of the anatomical structures of artery and the adjacent structures, and helps guide the direction of insertion of the cannula [45].

Our analysis noted significantly higher first-attempt success rates on the use of POCUS vs the standard of care. Included studies encompassed procedures like bladder catheterization, detection of abdominal conditions, intravenous access, and lumbar puncture, implying that the use of POCUS helped achieve access faster than the routine care methods. The overall procedural success was significantly improved by the use of POCUS, signifying lower rates of overall procedural failure, which is per a previous meta-analysis by Ye et al. [46] Our findings are supported by the study by Kleidon et al., which noted significantly greater first-time insertion success on use of ultrasound guided measures for intravenous catheterization (risk difference, RD: 53.6%; 95%CI: 41.7%-65.4%; p < 0.001) [47]. Similarly, POCUS was associated with a significantly higher first-attempt success of lumbar puncture in the EDs in children, as noted in a previous meta-analysis by Ćwiek et al. [48] On the other hand, few included studies demonstrated a slightly higher, but insignificant improvement on use of POCUS, like that noted by Zummer et al. [41] This is likely due to the underpowered study, as demonstrated by its small sample size (166 patients). Ultrasound-guidance is particularly crucial in infants, wherein a failure to secure access may be associated with errors and iatrogenic injuries to nearby structures. Procedures like lumbar puncture become technically difficult when performed in newborns, with a failure rate of over 50% [49]. Achieving intravenous access is more complicated, too, due to the smaller veins and higher subcutaneous fat [50, 51]. Therefore, the use of ultrasound-guided measures can significantly improve visualization and accurate estimation of the ideal puncture site and depth. Our analysis, however, showed high heterogeneity. This may be because of the varying sample sizes across different studies. The different procedures performed across these studies may also account for this variability. Additionally, the age of the patient may play an important role in determining the usefulness of POCUS; a previous meta-analysis concluded ultrasound-guided lumbar punctures are more efficacious in infants than in children [52]. This meta-analysis was, however, limited by the inclusion of only two studies in the older children subgroup, which may explain the higher effect size, but it did not reach statistical significance. Further exploration is crucial to determine the efficacy of POCUS techniques across all age groups and specifically for different procedures.

Our analysis also noted no significant improvement in the mean time to completion of the procedure. The procedural time may be lengthened by the use of ultrasound itself, thus resulting in significant variability across different studies. The ultrasound technique may differ too and may employ different planes and axes, which contribute to the overall time of the procedure. Procedures like intravenous cannulation employ simpler ultrasound techniques, and may explain the faster time to completion, as supported by the studies of Lau et al. [52] and Aouad-Maroun et al. [53] On the other hand, ultrasound techniques for an abdominal survey, trauma, or catheterization of the bladder employ 3 to 6 planes of measurement, which may improve the time to completion vs its faster success rate.

Additionally, no significant improvement was noted in the number of attempts across both groups. While effect size favored POCUS, the difference failed to reach statistical significance. While Doniger et al. noted a significantly reduced number of attempts (p = 0.004), Curtis et al. noted no significant change across both groups. Doniger et al. [29] exclusively evaluated children below 10, while Curtis et al. [27] evaluated the procedure across all age groups till 18. This implies that the number of attempts may be significantly reduced in younger children than those older. The analysis is limited by the inclusion of only 3 studies, and requires further exploration in future trials. Finally, no significant difference was noted in the discharge outcomes of patients, with a similar number of children being hospitalized or discharged. Discharge outcomes would be relevant in conditions of trauma or catheterization of the bladder, similar rates were consistently noted across included studies, implying no change in the recovery of patients [28]. However, both outcomes included two to three studies; further trials are necessary to evaluate the efficacy of POCUS in admitted children. While POCUS did not significantly alter disposition outcomes such as hospital admission or discharge rates, it enhanced procedural success and reduced failed attempts. This reflects a process-level benefit: faster, more efficient procedures reduce patient distress, staff burden, and procedural repetition, even if the ultimate clinical outcome remains unchanged. Prior studies have similarly noted that while diagnostic or interventional ultrasound may not influence disposition directly, it plays a vital role in improving care delivery, particularly in high-stakes settings like the ED [54].

Our meta-analysis provides a comprehensive assessment of POCUS, assessing its efficacy across multiple procedures like lumbar puncture, intravenous access, catheterization, and trauma. This analysis helps provide a generalized conclusion on the improvements in patient outcomes.

Study limitations and future directions

Our analysis is limited by the heterogeneity across multiple outcomes. This heterogeneity may be explained by the different age groups included and the varied procedures performed. We attempted to resolve heterogeneity via leave-one-out analysis, but the effect size remained largely unchanged. There was a lack of detailed operator training data and standardization of ultrasound techniques, limiting our ability to account for skill-related variability. Additionally, this meta-analysis did not account for the varied ultrasound techniques used across each study, which may significantly influence visualization and, hence, success rates. Further, the small sample size across a few included studies may have contributed to the heterogeneity. Another important limitation of our analysis is the limited geographic diversity of included studies. Nearly all RCTs were conducted in high-income countries such as the United States, Canada, and Australia, with only one study originating from Turkey. This lack of representation from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) in Asia, Africa, and the Middle East restricts the generalizability of our findings, as resource availability, operator training, and patient demographics may differ significantly across regions. Given that pediatric emergency care practices and access to ultrasound technology vary globally, future research should prioritize multicenter RCTs in diverse healthcare settings to provide a more representative assessment of the role of POCUS in pediatric populations worldwide.

Despite the predominance of randomized trials from high-income countries in our meta-analysis, growing evidence from other regions supports the feasibility and clinical utility of POCUS in varied healthcare settings. Implementation and curriculum studies from low- and middle-income contexts have documented increased POCUS use, shorter lengths of stay, and lower local costs after structured training interventions, indicating that benefits observed in RCTs may translate beyond North America and Australia [55, 56].

However, many of these non-RCT reports occur as single-site or observational studies and vary in methodology, highlighting the need for multicenter, pragmatic trials across Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America to determine external validity and context-specific effect sizes. Expanding trial geography would also allow comparisons of how system factors (staffing models, triage practices, referral pathways) modify POCUS effectiveness [57].

Barriers and cost constraints are important determinants of real-world adoption and help explain geographic differences in uptake. Global surveys and longitudinal implementation studies repeatedly identify three dominant obstacles: (1) limited access to ultrasound machines and durable devices at point of care, (2) insufficient local training and supervised practice opportunities to achieve and maintain competence, and (3) competing time and workflow pressures that limit clinicians’ ability to perform and teach POCUS in busy ED environments [58, 59].

Cost considerations extend beyond the initial purchase price to include maintenance, consumables, software updates, credentialing and faculty time for training, and telemedicine or cloud storage fees for image review. Systematic economic reviews show that POCUS can be cost-saving or cost-effective in targeted applications (for example, pediatric appendicitis and specific trauma workflows), but these benefits depend on procedure mix, device choice (handheld vs cart-based), and implementation strategy; therefore cost-effectiveness observed in high-income settings may not directly generalize to resource-constrained hospitals without tailored deployment plans [60].

Practical strategies to overcome these barriers include development of concise, competency-based curricula and longitudinal supervision (including tele-mentoring), deployment of low-cost handheld probes with local maintenance plans, pooled or phased procurement to reduce upfront costs, incorporation of POCUS into routine ED workflows, and funding models that recognize training and faculty time as implementation costs. Embedding standardized POCUS protocols in multicenter trials and registries will both improve external validity and generate the context-specific cost and outcome data necessary to inform national policy and scale-up decisions [61].

Further randomized trials are needed to assess the efficacy of POCUS across each age group, particularly in infants, those below 3, young children, and older children, considering the differences in their physiology. More studies are needed to assess the efficacy of POCUS in patients with trauma, where POCUS can be a useful tool for primary and secondary survey. Training programs should emphasize age-specific techniques and ensure operator competency. Additionally, future research should explore standardized ultrasound protocols and investigate long-term outcomes such as complication rates, procedural satisfaction, and healthcare resource utilization.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis highlights the substantial benefits of integrating point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) in pediatric emergency departments for various procedures. Its significant impact on first-attempt and overall procedural success rates underscores its efficacy in improving patient outcomes. Although time to procedure completion and the number of attempts did not show significant differences, the overall enhancement in procedural success emphasizes the value of POCUS in pediatric care. Future research should focus on standardizing techniques and expanding training to maximize the potential of POCUS across different age groups and settings, ultimately aiming for broader adoption and optimized patient care in pediatric emergency medicine.
