Self-management-based health coaching for sustainable behavior change in chronic disease care: A concept analysis using Walker and Avant’s framework
Authors: Rian Adi Pamungkas, Kathleen Potempa, Kanittha Chamroonsawasdi, Andi Mayasari Usman, Samoraphop Banharak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelf-management-based health coaching is increasingly applied in chronic disease management; however, conceptual ambiguity persists regarding its defining characteristics and theoretical boundaries. A clear and consistent conceptualization is essential to strengthen its application in nursing research and practice.ObjectiveThis study aimed to clarify the concept of self-management–based health coaching in chronic disease care.MethodA concept analysis was conducted using Walker and Avant’s framework. A systematic literature search was conducted across multiple databases (PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar) for studies published between January 2000 and June 2025, resulting in 49 articles included in the analysis. Conceptual elements were extracted and synthesized using iterative content analysis and constant comparison.ResultsThe analysis identified seven core attributes (health-focused, partnership and collaboration, client-centered, goal-oriented, process-driven, enlightening, and empowering) that reflect a collaborative, client-centered, and process-oriented approach that facilitates insight and empowerment in behavior change. Antecedents included individual readiness and supportive contextual conditions, while consequences encompassed psychological, behavioral, and clinical outcomes. Model and additional cases further illustrated the concept’s application and boundaries.ConclusionThis study provides a clearer conceptual understanding of self-management–based health coaching and highlights its relevance for guiding theory-informed research and practice in chronic disease management. However, further empirical and theoretical work is needed to refine and validate the concept across diverse contexts.

Background

Health coaching has been widely applied to facilitate health behavior change and improve health outcomes through motivation and a supportive partnership between patients and health professionals. This partnership is characterized by collaborative goal setting, active listening, shared decision-making, and ongoing feedback, all of which aim to enhance individuals’ capacity for self-management (Huffman, 2007). Health behavior change is fundamentally shaped by the quality of interaction between patients and healthcare providers, underscoring the relational foundation of effective coaching interventions.

Despite its increasing use, the concept of health coaching remains inconsistently defined and is often used interchangeably with related terms such as wellness coaching or life coaching. Although these approaches share common elements, including motivational strategies and partnership-based interactions, they differ in focus and application. Health coaching is typically embedded in a clinical setting and focused on managing or preventing chronic diseases, such as diabetes and hypertension, with an emphasis on measurable health outcomes and sustained behavior change. In contrast, wellness coaching is broader and largely non-clinical, focusing on general lifestyle enhancement among healthy individuals, including fitness, stress management, and personal growth (Olsen, 2014). This conceptual ambiguity limits the clarity and effectiveness of health coaching interventions, particularly in chronic disease care.

The use of health coaching has expanded rapidly across healthcare programs and interventions. It has been applied to support behavior change and improve clinical outcomes among individuals with chronic conditions through client-centered and partnership-oriented processes (Wolever & Eisenberg, 2011). However, the term “health coaching” is often used loosely in practice, with considerable variation in intervention duration, frequency, delivery modes, and underlying strategies. Many programs describe educational or counseling activities as health coaching without clearly articulating their defining attributes or theoretical foundations. Moreover, there is limited conceptual clarity about integrating health coaching with self-management, despite self-management being a central component of chronic disease care.

The conceptual integration between health coaching and self-management is also supported by established behavior change theories. For example, Social Cognitive Theory emphasizes the role of self-efficacy, observational learning, and self-regulation in behavior change (Bandura, 1986). Health coaching interventions often aim to strengthen these mechanisms by facilitating reflective dialogue, goal-setting, and self-monitoring. Similarly, Self-Determination Theory highlights the importance of autonomy, competence, and relatedness in supporting sustained motivation for behavior change (Deci & Ryan, 2000). Coaching approaches that emphasize collaboration and client empowerment closely align with these psychological needs, enabling individuals to take an active role in managing their health behaviors. In addition, Lorig’s Self-Management Model emphasizes the development of problem-solving skills, action planning, and decision-making abilities among individuals living with chronic conditions (Lorig & Holman, 2003). These elements are consistent with the process-oriented and goal-directed nature of health coaching, suggesting that coaching may function as a facilitative strategy to operationalize self-management support in clinical practice. Overall, these theoretical perspectives provide a stronger conceptual foundation for understanding how coaching interactions can facilitate the development of self-management capabilities and support sustainable health behavior change. Concept analysis provides a systematic approach to clarifying complex or evolving concepts and establishing precise operational definitions. The Walker and Avant model is a widely accepted framework in nursing science that offers structured steps for identifying defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents of a concept. This approach is particularly suitable for analyzing health coaching within the context of chronic disease management, where behavioral, psychological, and clinical dimensions intersect.

Unlike previous concept analyses of health coaching, e.g., Olsen (2014), which examined coaching broadly, this study specifically integrates self-management theory and focuses on chronic disease behavior change. Consequently, our analysis identifies attributes such as health-focused orientation and process-driven structure that are not emphasized in earlier work.

By clarifying the concept of self-management–based health coaching, this analysis aims to establish a coherent and theoretically grounded framework that can guide research, education, and practice. A refined conceptual understanding provides a foundation for developing effective, culturally sensitive, and context-specific interventions-such as motivational interviewing and personalized goal setting-that align with individuals’ values, readiness for change, and long-term self-management needs. Ultimately, conceptual clarity is essential for advancing theory-driven interventions and promoting sustainable health behavior change among individuals with chronic disorders.

Methods

Study Design

This concept analysis was conducted using Walker and Avant’s approach to clarify the meaning, defining attributes, antecedents, and consequences of self-management–based health coaching in the context of chronic disease prevention (Walker & Avant, 2005). The primary aims were to delineate the core characteristics of self-management integrated with health coaching and to provide a comprehensive conceptual explanation of the intervention. In addition, this study developed a conceptual framework for self-management–based health coaching in chronic disease care.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

Relevant literature was identified through systematic searches of PubMed, Science Direct, and Google Scholar. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., MeSH) and free-text keywords related to health coaching and self-management. The controlled descriptors included “Health Coaching,” “Self-Care,” and “Self-Management.” Free-text keywords included health coaching, health coach, self-management, self-care, and behavior change. Search strategies were adapted to the indexing structure of each database, including the use of MeSH terms in the database (Table 1).

Databases and search strategy

Search strategies were adapted to the specific indexing systems of each database. The literature search was conducted in December 2024 and included publications from January 2000 to June 2025. In PubMed and ScienceDirect, descriptors were combined with free-text keywords using Boolean operators. Google Scholar was used as a supplementary source to identify additional relevant literature not indexed in major databases. To maintain methodological transparency, screening of Google Scholar results was limited to the first 200 records sorted by relevance, consistent with recommendations for structured grey literature searches.

Titles and abstracts were systematically screened to determine their relevance to self-management integrated with health coaching. Articles that met the study’s conceptual criteria were retrieved and reviewed in full text to ensure alignment with the aims of the concept analysis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were established prior to the search process. Articles were included if they: (1) described definitions or defining attributes of self-management integrated with health coaching; (2) addressed antecedents, consequences, or empirical referents of the concept; (3) were published in English with full-text availability; and (4) were peer-reviewed. Articles were excluded if they were unpublished works, dissertations or theses, commentaries, editorials, or letters to the editor.

Data Collection Process and Article Selection

The article selection process was conducted in several sequential stages. Initially, literature searches were conducted across multiple electronic databases to identify articles on self-management and health coaching. All records retrieved from the databases were compiled, and duplicate articles were removed. In the first screening stage, titles and abstracts were independently reviewed to assess their relevance to the concept of self-management integrated with health coaching. Articles that clearly did not address health coaching, self-management, or behavior change in a healthcare context were excluded at this stage. In the second stage, full-text articles were retrieved and examined in detail. Full-text screening focused on identifying studies that provided conceptual definitions, described defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, or empirical referents of self-management–based health coaching. Articles lacking conceptual relevance, not addressing self-management or coaching as a core component, or lacking sufficient theoretical or empirical insight were excluded.

The remaining articles were included in the final analysis and used to inform the identification of defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, model cases, and empirical referents of self-management–based health coaching. This stepwise selection process ensured that only conceptually relevant and methodologically appropriate literature contributed to the development of the concept.

Study Extraction and Screening Description

Although this study is not a systematic review, the process of literature identification and selection was conducted according to the principles of transparent reporting, adapted from the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework (Figure 1) (Page et al., 2021). The PRISMA approach was used to structure the reporting of database searching, screening, eligibility assessment, and study inclusion to enhance transparency and reproducibility of the literature identification process.

The article selection process

Initially, 1,167 records were retrieved from the selected databases. After removing duplicate records, 1,015 articles remained for further screening. Titles and abstracts were then carefully reviewed for relevance to self-management-based health coaching, resulting in the exclusion of 822 articles and the selection of 193 articles for detailed evaluation. Among these, 144 articles were excluded after full-text review because they did not address health coaching, self-management, self-care, health behavior change, or chronic disease contexts. In addition, a third-stage search was conducted by screening the reference lists of the selected full-text articles to identify additional relevant studies. Following the full screening and selection process, 49 primary research articles were included in the final concept analysis.

Conceptual Analysis Procedure

Following the identification and selection of relevant articles, the conceptual analysis was conducted using Walker and Avant’s framework (Walker & Avant, 2005). Conceptual statements related to health coaching and self-management were extracted from each included study, including descriptions of coaching processes, interaction patterns, behavioral mechanisms, and theoretical explanations. These statements were analyzed using iterative content analysis and constant comparison to identify recurring conceptual patterns across the literature. Similar concepts were grouped into preliminary categories, which were then synthesized to determine the defining attributes of self-management–based health coaching.

Conditions described prior to the implementation of coaching interventions were categorized as antecedents, while outcomes occurring after the coaching process were classified as consequences. Observable indicators of the defining attributes in practice were identified as empirical referents. Through this process of conceptual extraction, categorization, and synthesis, the attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents of self-management–based health coaching were systematically identified and refined.

The conceptual synthesis was conducted using thematic coding and constant comparative analysis to systematically identify conceptual patterns across the literature. Conceptual statements extracted from the included articles were coded and compared iteratively to identify similarities and differences in how health coaching and self-management processes were described. Through this process, recurring themes were grouped into conceptual categories. These categories were then interpreted within Walker and Avant’s concept analysis framework, allowing the coded themes to be organized into defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents. In this way, empirical descriptions from the literature were linked with the framework to clarify the concept of self-management–based health coaching.

Reliability of Study Extraction

All retrieved abstracts were imported into EndNote for reference management, and duplicates were removed before exporting the records to Rayyan (https://www.rayyan.ai) for screening. Inter-rater reliability was assessed during the title and abstract screening stage. Two independent reviewers (RAP and KC) reviewed 49 articles based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and agreement was quantified using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ = 0.78), indicating substantial agreement.

Results

Identifying All Uses of the Concept and Its Definition

Self-management-based health coaching does not appear in the literature as a single, unified term. Instead, its components of self-management and health coaching are discussed separately across disciplines such as psychology, nursing, health sciences, and organizational studies. Therefore, this concept analysis examines these related terms individually to clarify their meanings and support their integration in behavioral change interventions for chronic disease management.

Definition of Self-Management

Dictionary definitions describe self-management as taking responsibility for one’s behavior, health, and daily activities (Oxford Learner’s Dictionary, 2023) and as the ability to regulate behavior in pursuit of specific goals, including structured behavioral strategies such as problem identification and goal setting (APA Dictionary of Psychology, 2023). The literature indicates that no single gold-standard definition exists (Barlow et al., 2002). In healthcare, self-management is commonly defined as the day-to-day tasks individuals perform to control or reduce the impact of chronic illness on physical and psychosocial functioning (Clark et al., 1991). It involves integrating biological, psychological, and social strategies to support self-regulation and functional independence (Hennessey & Suter, 2011). Unlike self-care, which is often preventive and applied to healthy individuals, self-management emphasizes active disease control in collaboration with healthcare providers. In summary, self-management refers to an individual’s capacity to regulate health-related behaviors through integrated biopsychosocial strategies to minimize disease impact and maintain daily functioning.

Definition of Coaching

Dictionary sources define coaching as intensive, personalized instruction aimed at improving skills or performance (Cambridge Dictionary, 2023; Merriam-Webster Dictionary, 2009; Oxford Learner’s Dictionary, 2023). The term “coach” originated in Hungarian as kocsi, referring to a carriage used to transport people from one place to another. Metaphorically, coaching represents a process of facilitating individuals’ movement from their current state toward a desired goal. In athletics, coaching is a structured process in which a coach supports athletes or teams in improving performance, developing skills, and achieving competitive goals through guidance, feedback, and training strategies. In organizational contexts, executive coaching is defined as a helping relationship between a coach and a client with managerial responsibility aimed at improving professional performance, leadership capability, and organizational effectiveness through goal-oriented behavioral development (Joo, 2005).

From a psychological perspective, coaching psychology is defined as the scientific study of behavior, cognition, and emotion within coaching practice to enhance human functioning, well-being, and performance (Passmore & Theeboom, 2016). The American Psychological Association further describes coaching as specialized training designed to enhance individual performance across personal, academic, and professional domains (APA Dictionary of Psychology, 2023).

In healthcare, coaching has evolved into health coaching, focusing on facilitating health behavior change. It is a partnership helping individuals maximize their personal and professional potential through reflective and creative processes. Health coaching emphasizes patient-centered communication, empowerment, and readiness for change. Olsen (2014) conceptualizes health coaching as a health-focused, partnership-based, client-centered, goal-oriented, process-driven, enlightening, and empowering approach.

Across these disciplines, coaching consistently involves collaborative partnership, goal-oriented guidance, reflective learning, and facilitation of behavioral change. These elements form the foundation for understanding health coaching as an approach that supports sustainable health behavior change. These recurring elements suggest that coaching is fundamentally a facilitative and relational process rather than a directive intervention, distinguishing it conceptually from traditional education or instruction-based approaches.

Definition of Self-Management-Based Coaching

Self-management coaching combines self-regulation and coaching to empower individuals in managing personal goals, responsibilities, and health-related behaviors. It emphasizes skill development in goal setting, prioritization, stress management, and behavioral regulation (Kemp, 2012). This process enhances cognitive, emotional, and behavioral awareness, enabling individuals to challenge maladaptive patterns and strengthen self-directed effectiveness (Kemp, 2012). In healthcare, self-management-based coaching supports patient empowerment, collaborative goal setting, and sustained behavior regulation in chronic disease management.

Attributes of self-management-based health coaching

Batson and Yoder (2012) highlighted managerial coaching elements such as mutual trust, clear expectations, feedback, goal setting, and the availability of resources, which reflect a structured interpersonal process. Vitale and Germini (2021) identified components of health coaching relevant to behavior change, including motivational interviewing, nurse–patient relationships, leadership, and supportive environments. O’Connor et al. (2014) emphasized directive and non-directive coaching strategies: goal-focused and treat-to-target approaches (directive), as well as personalized and patient-driven approaches (non-directive).

Although empowerment, client-centeredness, and self-efficacy are conceptually related, they are distinct in coaching. Client-centeredness reflects an approach that prioritizes the individual’s preferences and experiences during the interaction. Empowerment is a process-oriented attribute that facilitates autonomy, active engagement, and shared decision-making within the coaching interaction. In contrast, self-efficacy is conceptualized as a psychological outcome that emerges from the coaching process rather than as an inherent characteristic of the interaction itself. Similarly, self-awareness is a consequence of the coaching process, reflecting the client’s growing understanding of behavioral patterns, not an inherent attribute of the coaching interaction.

To avoid conceptual overlap between attributes and consequences, this analysis distinguishes between the coaching process and the outcomes produced by that process. Attributes refer to the defining characteristics that are observable during the coaching interaction, such as reflective questioning, collaborative goal setting, shared decision-making, and client participation in action planning. In contrast, self-efficacy and self-awareness are conceptualized as psychological consequences that may emerge after individuals engage in the coaching process. Therefore, self-efficacy and self-awareness were not treated as attributes, but as outcomes resulting from empowering and enlightening coaching interactions.

Model Case

Walker and Avant (2005) described the model case to demonstrate all defining attributes of the concept. In this concept analysis, the case demonstrates how coaching facilitates improved self-management behaviors by strengthening motivation, self-efficacy, and sustainable lifestyle changes, ultimately supporting better hypertension outcomes.

“Ms. A is a 50-year-old woman with a 7-year history of hypertension. Her blood pressure has remained uncontrolled (170/100 mmHg), and she struggles with several self-management behaviors, including limiting salty food, maintaining regular physical activity, and adhering to medication. She reports competing demands at home, psychological stress, and low confidence in her ability to sustain healthy habits. Previous attempts at weight loss and exercise were unsuccessful, leading to frustration and disengagement.”

The health-coaching interaction between the coach and client is illustrated in this session. Session 1: Assessment & Establishing Partnership. The coach begins by building rapport, actively listening to Ms. A’s concerns, and validating her experiences. Through collaborative dialogue, Ms. A identifies her main challenges: lack of time, low motivation, and inconsistent routines. This session establishes a trusting partnership as an essential attribute of coaching while maintaining a health-focused orientation. Session 2: Client-Centered Goal Setting. Using motivational interviewing techniques, the coach explores Ms. A’s personal values and readiness to change. Together, they co-create a realistic goal: reducing daily salt intake and walking for 15 minutes three times per week. This illustrates the attributes of client-centeredness and goal-orientation. Session 3: Process-Driven Action Planning & Enlightening. The coach guides Ms. A in identifying barriers, such as family meal preferences, emotional eating during stress, and inconsistent routines. Through reflective dialogue, Ms. A gains insight into her behavioral triggers (enlightening attribute). They develop a practical action plan that includes meal modifications, scheduling short walks, and stress management strategies.

This case demonstrates the defining attributes of self-management-based health coaching through their observable manifestations in the coaching process. Health-focused orientation is reflected in the consistent emphasis on dietary regulation, physical activity, and hypertension management. Partnership and collaboration are evident in the establishment of trust and shared dialogue between the coach and the client. Client-centeredness is demonstrated through the exploration of the client’s personal values and preferences in goal setting. Goal orientation is reflected in the development of specific, achievable behavioral targets. The process-driven attribute is illustrated through the structured progression of coaching sessions, while the enlightening attribute is observed as the client gains insight into behavioral triggers. Finally, empowerment is reflected in the client’s active participation in developing and implementing self-management strategies.

Related Case

A related case describes a situation conceptually associated with the concept, but that does not include its defining attributes.

“A hospital implemented a structured health education program for patients with chronic illness. Patients attended group sessions in which nurses delivered standardized information on disease management, medication use, and healthy lifestyles.”

Although the program aimed to improve patient knowledge, interactions were largely one-directional, and no individualized coaching or collaborative goal setting occurred. The focus was on information transfer rather than empowerment or facilitation of self-management skills. While related to health behavior change, this case reflects health education rather than self-management-based coaching, as the defining attributes of partnership, empowerment, and individualized coaching were not present.

Borderline Case

A borderline case contains some but not all the defining attributes of the concept. In this scenario, elements of coaching are present, but the process does not fully reflect the defining characteristics of self-management–based coaching. For example:

“Mr. B is a 55-year-old patient with type 2 diabetes who attends a lifestyle counseling session at a primary healthcare clinic. During the session, the nurse encourages Mr. B to consider improving his diet and physical activity. The nurse asks several reflective questions and briefly discusses the importance of setting personal health goals. Mr. B expresses interest in walking regularly and reducing sugar intake.”

However, the interaction remains largely structured and time-limited. Although some dialogue occurs, the session is primarily guided by standardized counseling protocols rather than a collaborative partnership. Individualized exploration of barriers is minimal, and there is no ongoing follow-up or structured coaching process to support sustained behavior change. This case reflects some attributes of self-management-based coaching, such as health-focused dialogue and goal discussion. However, it lacks key attributes including sustained partnership, empowerment through reflective exploration, and a process-driven coaching structure. Therefore, it represents a borderline case rather than a full example of self-management–based coaching.

Contrary Case

A contrary case is one in which none of the defining attributes of the concept are evident.

“During the COVID-19 pandemic, healthcare services were severely strained. A critical nursing staff shortage, coupled with full bed occupancy, substantially increased nurses’ workload and reduced their ability to provide individualized care. Interactions between nurses and patients became brief, task-focused, and largely impersonal, as nurses had very limited time for direct communication. Patients felt their concerns were not fully heard or addressed, and they expressed dissatisfaction with the care they received. Under these circumstances, the heavy workload, limited interaction time, and lack of a therapeutic relationship hindered the development of a collaborative partnership between the nurse and the patient. There was no opportunity for individualized support, empowerment, shared goal setting, or guidance to support self-management behaviors. As a result, patients’ needs remained unmet, and no meaningful self-management behavior change occurred.”

This situation stands in clear contrast to the concept of self-management-based coaching, as none of its defining attributes were present. This case helps clarify the conceptual boundaries of self-management–based coaching by illustrating a situation in which the defining attributes of coaching are absent.

Antecedents

According to Walker and Avant (2005), antecedents are events or conditions that must exist before the concept occurs. In the context of self-management–based coaching, antecedents are the conditions that enable the coaching process to begin. These conditions occur before the coaching interaction begins and provide the necessary context for the concept to unfold (Olsen, 2014; Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013).

Internal antecedents are primarily related to the client’s readiness to engage in behavior change. These include awareness of a health condition requiring self-management, prior exposure to health information, intrinsic motivation to improve health, and a willingness to participate in a behavior change process. Such readiness reflects an individual’s openness to receiving guidance and engaging in reflective dialogue to improve self-management practices (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Lorig & Holman, 2003; Prochaska & DiClemente, 1984; Ryan & Deci, 2017).

External antecedents are conditions in the care environment, including the availability of a trained coach or healthcare professional with behavior-change and communication competencies; a supportive and trusting relationship; sufficient time and continuity for structured sessions; access to resources; and a chronic condition or modifiable health risk that warrants sustained self-management support. Reviews of health coaching consistently describe trained coaches, ongoing relationships, patient-centered communication, and structured follow-up as enabling conditions for coaching interventions (Kivelä et al., 2014; Singh et al., 2022; Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013).

Overall, internal readiness and external support create the context in which self-management–based health coaching can be initiated and sustained. Their absence may limit engagement, weaken collaborative goal setting, and reduce the feasibility of implementing the coaching process as intended (Olsen, 2014; Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013).

Consequences

Consequences refer to events or outcomes that occur as a result of the concept being enacted. According to Walker and Avant (2005), identifying consequences is essential for understanding the observable effects of a concept and its relevance for research and practice. In the context of self-management–based coaching, the consequences include psychological outcomes, improvements in self-management behaviors, successful goal attainment, enhanced quality of life, and measurable improvements in clinical health indicators. These outcomes reflect the impact of coaching after the coach-client interaction.

Psychological consequences represent the foundational outcomes of the coaching process. These include enhanced self-efficacy and self-awareness, which support individuals’ confidence in managing their health and making informed decisions (Carden et al., 2022; Cinar & Schou, 2014; Eom & Lee, 2017; Rosland et al., 2022; Sutton & Crobach, 2022; Tülüce & Kutlutürkan, 2018). Improvements in psychological functioning often precede and support sustained behavioral change.

Behavioral consequences are reflected in improved self-management practices, such as dietary regulation, increased physical activity, and better medication adherence (Carden et al., 2022; Sutton & Crobach, 2022). These practices indicate the translation of psychological readiness into concrete health behaviors. In addition, self-management-based coaching has been associated with successful goal attainment, as clients actively engage in setting and achieving health-related goals aligned with their individual needs and preferences (Biswas-Diener, 2009; Lipscomb, 2006). Collectively, these outcomes contribute to improved quality of life (Nelson et al., 2023). Goal orientation shapes the coaching process, while goal attainment results from successful goal-directed coaching.

At the clinical level, self-management-based coaching is associated with improvements in objective health indicators. Studies have reported reductions in body weight, HbA1c levels, blood pressure, and cholesterol following coaching interventions (Berzins et al., 2024; Patja et al., 2012; Yun et al., 2020). These changes suggest that sustained behavioral and psychological improvements can translate into meaningful clinical benefits. Each category of consequence is further described below:

Psychological consequences refer to outcomes that occur after the coaching process has been enacted. In this analysis, self-efficacy and self-awareness are not considered defining attributes of self-management–based health coaching, but rather psychological outcomes that may result from empowering and enlightening coaching interactions. Empowering interactions provide clients with opportunities for autonomy, shared decision-making, and action planning, which may strengthen self-efficacy. Similarly, enlightening interactions involve reflective questioning and exploration of barriers, which may enhance self-awareness over time

Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to perform behaviors required to achieve desired outcomes successfully. Individuals with higher self-efficacy tend to exert greater effort and persist longer when facing challenges. Evidence suggests that health coaching plays a critical role in strengthening self-efficacy by continuously encouraging individuals to maintain health-related behaviors (Eom & Lee, 2017). Health coaching has also been shown to foster positive beliefs and confidence that facilitate behavioral adjustment (Cinar & Schou, 2014). Moreover, coaching interventions have been associated with improved medication adherence through enhanced self-efficacy among individuals with chronic pulmonary diseases (Tülüce & Kutlutürkan, 2018)

Self-awareness is a key psychological outcome that supports healthy lifestyle change. Individuals with higher self-awareness are better able to reflect on their behaviors, regulate their emotions, and align their health actions with their personal values. Research highlights the role of self-awareness in promoting self-acceptance, confidence, and positive relational engagement within coaching contexts (Carden et al., 2022). Group-based coaching has also been shown to enhance self-awareness through reflective self-development, acceptance of self and others, and increased proactivity and engagement (Sutton & Crobach, 2022).

Self-Management Practice is a central behavioral consequence of self-management-based coaching. Evidence indicates that coaching interventions enhance individuals’ capacity to manage their condition through increased confidence and sustained engagement in health behaviors (Pamungkas & Chamroonsawasdi, 2020). Enhanced self-management practices have also been linked to improvements in overall quality of life, reflecting the feasibility and effectiveness of coaching-based approaches (Huang et al., 2017).

Goal Attainment is a key consequence of health coaching, reflecting the achievement of client-centered and meaningful health goals. Goals are tailored to individual health priorities and are applicable across diverse disciplines and contexts (Biswas-Diener, 2009; Lipscomb, 2006). Health coaching has been shown to facilitate goal attainment by strengthening positive psychological resources, increasing self-efficacy, and supporting purposeful modifications in daily health behaviors. Through guided reflection, collaborative planning, and ongoing support, individuals can translate intentions into sustained behavioral changes, ultimately contributing to the achievement of personally meaningful health goals. This outcome has been observed not only in clinical populations but also in broader contexts of personal development and well-being.

Clinical Biomarkers provide objective indicators of health outcomes and disease management. Improvements in biomarkers such as weight, blood pressure, glycemic control, and lipid profiles occur after health coaching interventions (Lawson et al., 2013; Pamungkas & Chamroonsawasdi, 2020; Patja et al., 2012). These findings suggest that self-management-based coaching not only influences psychological and behavioral domains but also contributes to measurable improvements in clinical health indicators.

Conceptual Model of Self-Management-Based Coaching

The antecedents consist of internal and external conditions that must be present prior to the coaching process. Internal antecedents include patients’ experience, commitment, motivation, and consistency, which reflect individual readiness to engage in behavior change (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Prochaska & DiClemente, 1984). External antecedents relate to the coach’s actions and the care environment, including trust building, active listening, positive reinforcement, targeted goal setting, and the provision of consistent coaching support (Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013). These antecedents enable the defining attributes of self-management–based coaching, characterized by a health-focused, client-centered partnership that is goal-oriented, empowering, enlightening, and motivating. Through this collaborative process, clients are supported in achieving meaningful health goals.

The consequences of self-management–based coaching extend across psychological, behavioral, and clinical domains. Expected outcomes include improvements in self-efficacy and self-awareness, sustained behavior changes, successful goal attainment, and improvements in clinical biomarkers such as body weight, blood pressure, glycemic control, and lipid profiles. Collectively, this model provides a conceptual framework for supporting chronic disease management and promoting sustainable health behavior change (Figure 2).

Empirical Referents

Empirical referents represent observable indicators that demonstrate the presence of a concept in practice. According to Walker and Avant (2005), empirical referents provide ways to recognize when the defining attributes of a concept occur in real-world settings. In the context of self-management–based health coaching, empirical referents reflect observable interaction patterns, communication strategies, and behavioral processes that indicate the implementation of the defining attributes of coaching during coaching sessions (Olsen, 2014; Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013).

The health-focused orientation is empirically evident when coaching interactions consistently address disease-specific self-management and modifiable health behaviors, such as diet, physical activity, medication adherence, symptom monitoring, stress management, and risk reduction. Observable evidence may be found in session content, coaching notes, or structured checklists documenting health-related goals and action plans (Conn & Curtain, 2019; Pamungkas & Chamroonsawasdi, 2020; Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013).

Partnership and collaboration are reflected in bidirectional dialogue, active listening, mutual trust, shared decision-making, and joint exploration of barriers and solutions. These behaviors demonstrate the relational foundation of coaching and distinguish it from one-directional education or prescriptive counseling (Kivelä et al., 2014; Olsen, 2014; Thom et al., 2015).

The client-centered attribute can be recognized when the client’s experiences, preferences, readiness, abilities, and personal priorities guide the coaching interaction. Empirical indicators include individualized goal discussions, adaptation of strategies to the client’s context, and the client’s active involvement in identifying feasible solutions (Conn & Curtain, 2019; Olsen, 2014; Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013).

Goal-oriented guidance becomes evident through client-generated, specific, and achievable goals; documented action plans; and repeated review of progress. Coaching records, goal-attainment forms, and follow-up logs can therefore serve as observable evidence of this attribute (O’Connor et al., 2014; Pamungkas et al., 2022; Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013).

The process-driven attribute is reflected in a structured yet flexible sequence of assessment, planning, action, feedback, progress review, and adaptation across repeated sessions. Observable indicators include use of a coaching protocol, session attendance and fidelity records, reflective questioning, and documentation of iterative revisions to the action plan (Olsen, 2014; Pamungkas & Chamroonsawasdi, 2020; Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013).

The enlightening attribute is empirically observed when the coach uses reflective questions, guided exploration, clarification, and feedback to help clients identify beliefs, barriers, motivations, and behavioral triggers. Recorded-session coding, narrative coaching notes, and client reflection records can document these self-discovery processes, whereas increased self-awareness is regarded as a consequence rather than the referent itself (Conn & Curtain, 2019; Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013).

Finally, the empowering attribute is empirically evident when the coach supports autonomy through shared decision-making, client-generated goals and solutions, collaborative action planning, problem-solving support, and accountability. Observable documentation may include client-owned action plans, participation in decisions, and records of self-selected strategies and follow-up commitments; increased self-efficacy and confidence are subsequent psychological outcomes (Lorig & Holman, 2003; Olsen, 2014; Ryan & Deci, 2017; Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013) (Table 3).

Discussion

This concept analysis of self-management–based health coaching contributes to a clearer understanding of its application in chronic disease prevention and management. Although the term “health coaching” is often used interchangeably with wellness coaching, executive coaching, or life coaching, this analysis highlights important conceptual distinctions. Life coaching primarily emphasizes personal growth and goal attainment unrelated to health outcomes, while wellness coaching broadly focuses on general well-being, such as stress management, sleep, and nutrition. In contrast, self-management–based health coaching is explicitly oriented toward clinical and behavioral goals relevant to chronic conditions, including adherence to treatment, disease-specific education, and sustained health behavior change aimed at improving measurable health outcomes.

The defining health-focused attribute underscores that this form of coaching is anchored in clinical relevance and evidence-based targets. Unlike broader coaching models, self-management–based health coaching aligns coaching goals with indicators such as blood pressure control, glycemic regulation, lipid management, and adherence to chronic care plans (Olsen, 2014; Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013). This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that health coaching interventions in chronic disease care are commonly directed toward improving self-management behaviors and clinical outcomes such as self-efficacy, adherence, and cardiometabolic control (Dejonghe et al., 2017; Kivelä et al., 2014). This orientation ensures that coaching outcomes are observable, measurable, and meaningful within healthcare practice.

Partnership and collaboration emerged as central attributes of the concept. A reciprocal and trust-based relationship between the coach and the client enables shared decision-making and mutual goal setting. This finding is consistent with previous literature emphasizing that coaching is not a directive educational approach, but a collaborative process that supports patient autonomy, self-efficacy, and personalized behavior change (Kivelä et al., 2014). Such collaborative engagement is essential for sustaining long-term involvement in self-management behaviors, particularly in the context of chronic disease, where ongoing motivation and adaptation are required (Dejonghe et al., 2017; Kivelä et al., 2014).

Client-centeredness and goal orientation further reinforce the importance of tailoring coaching interventions to individuals’ needs, preferences, and readiness for change. These attributes align with established behavior change theories. The Transtheoretical Model explains that behavior change occurs through progressive stages and requires interventions matched to an individual’s readiness to change (Prochaska & DiClemente, 1984). Self-Determination Theory emphasizes autonomy, competence, and relatedness as essential conditions for strengthening intrinsic motivation and sustained behavior change (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Ryan & Deci, 2017). Social Cognitive Theory further supports this framework by highlighting the role of self-efficacy, observational learning, and self-regulation in behavior change, all of which are facilitated through coaching interactions (Bandura, 1986). Through this alignment, self-management-based coaching remains both structured and flexible, allowing it to adapt to diverse patient contexts while maintaining conceptual coherence. These findings suggest that the effectiveness of self-management–based health coaching is not solely dependent on behavioral techniques but also on the relational and contextual dynamics embedded within the coaching interaction (Kivelä et al., 2014; Olsen, 2014; Wolever & Eisenberg, 2013).

The implementation of self-management–based coaching reflects a stepwise and systematic process, encompassing assessment, goal formulation, action planning, and monitoring. This structured yet adaptable framework supports progressive behavior change while accommodating individual variability. Enlightenment and empowerment function as key psychological mechanisms within this process, fostering knowledge, awareness, autonomy, and confidence (Dejonghe et al., 2017; Ryan & Deci, 2017). As a result, clients are better equipped to make informed decisions, enhance self-efficacy, and maintain health behaviors over time (Dejonghe et al., 2017; Kivelä et al., 2014).

The consequences of self-management–based health coaching extend across psychological, behavioral, and clinical domains. Psychological improvements, such as enhanced self-efficacy and reduced emotional distress, support sustained engagement in self-management practices. These psychological changes facilitate behavioral modification, including increased physical activity, improved dietary adherence, and consistent medication use, which may contribute to goal attainment. Collectively, these outcomes contribute to improved quality of life and favorable clinical biomarkers, such as reduced HbA1c levels and improved blood pressure control among individuals with chronic conditions.

From a practical perspective, this concept analysis highlights the need for standardized training in self-management–based health coaching for health professionals. Training should emphasize communication skills, motivational interviewing, behavioral change theories, and cultural competence to ensure adaptability across diverse populations. Interprofessional collaboration is also essential; nurses may lead ongoing coaching and follow-up, while community health workers can provide culturally grounded reinforcement at the community level.

Future research should focus on developing and testing intervention models that explicitly integrate self-management principles within health coaching frameworks. Further studies are also needed to establish valid and reliable instruments for measuring self-management practices and coaching processes. Additionally, empirical research should continue to explore the relationships between antecedents, defining attributes, and consequences of self-management–based coaching to strengthen its theoretical and practical foundations.

Limitation of the Study

This study has several limitations. First, although multiple databases were searched, the inclusion of Google Scholar and the restriction to English-language publications may have introduced selection bias and limited the comprehensiveness of the literature. Second, as a concept analysis, the findings are inherently interpretative and subject to researcher judgment, which may influence the identification and synthesis of defining attributes. Third, variations in terminology and conceptual usage across studies may have affected the consistency of concept interpretation. Despite these limitations, efforts were made to enhance transparency and rigor through systematic search procedures, independent screening, and iterative analytical processes.

Conclusion

This concept analysis contributes to a clearer understanding of self-management–based health coaching in the context of chronic disease care by identifying its defining attributes, antecedents, and consequences. Seven core attributes characterize this approach, reflecting its collaborative, client-centered, and process-oriented nature. These attributes, shaped by both individual readiness and contextual conditions, distinguish self-management-based health coaching as a facilitative approach to behavior change. The findings provide a conceptual basis that may inform the development of theory-driven interventions and guide future research aimed at refining and empirically validating the concept across diverse healthcare settings.
