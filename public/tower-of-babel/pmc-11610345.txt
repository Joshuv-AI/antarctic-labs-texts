Triglyceride glucose index: a significant prognostic marker of heart failure in patients with chronic kidney disease
Authors: Shijie Liu, Xuefu Chen, Qian Guo, Jingwen Dou, Jiaxin Huang, Lewen Jia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTriglycerides and glucose (TyG) index, a relatively convenient method for assessing insulin resistance, has been confirmed as an independent predictor both for chronic kidney disease (CKD) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) in previous studies. However, a few studies focus on the prognostic value of TyG index for CVD in patients diagnosed with CKD, especially in advanced CKD patients. In this study, we aim to investigate the prognostic value of TyG index for heart failure (HF) in patients with non-dialysis CKD stage 3–5.Methods and resultsThis retrospective study was conducted at The First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University. Patients with non-dialysis CKD stage 3–5 between 1 January 2016 and 1 January 2023 were included in our study. The endpoints included cardiovascular death and HF-related rehospitalization. Finally, a total of 284 subjects were enrolled in our cohort. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that TyG index was one of the independent risk factors both for HF-related rehospitalization and cardiovascular death in non-dialysis CKD stage 3–5 patients. Furthermore, Cox regression model showed a favorable predictive efficiency of TyG index for adverse clinical events. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that TyG index was one of the positive independent predictors of HF.ConclusionsNon-dialysis CKD stage 3–5 patients with higher TyG index level experience worsening cardiovascular outcomes, and TyG index is associated with the occurrence of HF. Highlighting that TyG index may be a significant marker for assessing cardiovascular prognosis in CKD patients.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is one of the common causes of death. With the progress of disease, kidney injury and insufficiency in CKD will influence homeostasis and result in a serial of pathologic changes [1,2], including electrolyte disturbances, abnormal hormone release, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system activated, oxidative stress, and elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α, transforming growth factor-β, interleukin-6, etc. These pathologic changes, especially in CKD stage 3–5 patients, can cause cardiac damage, and eventually lead to heart failure (HF), which will significantly influence the prognosis and living quality of CKD patients [1,3]. Several studies [4,5] have demonstrated that patients with CKD stage 3–5 experienced 2–3 times higher risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). In order to promote the cardiovascular prognosis in CKD patients, previous studies [6–8] have identified several factors in CKD patients which could increase the risk for cardiovascular events, such as decline of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), elevated albuminuria level, and abnormal pulse wave velocity (PWV). However, as is mentioned above, the pathologic physiology of HF in CKD is complex. In addition to uremic toxins, increased proteinuria, abnormal neurohumor, and elevated arterial stiffness, other nontraditional pathogenic mechanism such as abnormal glucolipid metabolism plays important role in promoting the development of HF in CKD patients [3]. Therefore, exploring more predicting markers of HF are important for the management of CKD patients, especially in CKD stage 3–5.

Blood glucose and insulin homeostasis change were confirmed to be related to CKD, which could lead to insulin resistance (IR) by inflammation and oxidative stress [9]. Several studies have confirmed that IR is not only associated with CKD process, but also promote the development of CVD [10,11]. Considering IR was a significant risk factor of CVD, identifying IR was important during the course of CKD. Triglycerides and glucose (TyG) index, which was based on laboratory test of fasting plasma glucose and triglyceride levels, was a relatively convenient method for assessing IR compared to traditional homeostasis model assessment (HOMA) [12]. A number of studies [13,14] have indicated that TyG index was non-inferior than HOMA in assessing IR. What’s more, several studies have demonstrated that TyG index was an independent factor for predicting the occurrence of CVD and the development of CKD [15,16]. However, there is hardly any study focus on the association of TyG index and cardiac function in non-dialysis CKD patients. Considering patients with CKD stage 3–5 experienced worser cardiovascular prognosis, we aim to investigate the prognostic value of TyG index for HF and clinical prognosis in non-dialysis CKD stage 3–5 patients in our study.

Methods

Clinical data and study materials are available by reasonable request from Nephrology Department, The First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University.

Study population

This study was approved by the institutional review board of The First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University. The ethical review procedures were conducted with the Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments. All participants have signed informed consent prior to the collection of clinical data.

This is a retrospective study, which included patients diagnosed with CKD stage 3–5 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University between 1 January 2016 and 1 January 2023. Patients who were younger than 18, lacked baseline echocardiography examination, received renal replacement therapy or renal transplantation, lost follow-up were excluded from our cohort. Baseline demographics, laboratory examination results, imageological examination findings, and therapeutic schemes were all collected from Electronic Medical Record System at the First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University.

Clinical definition

HF was defined as a clinical syndrome consisting of symptoms and/or signs, objective evidence of abnormal cardiac structure and function by echocardiography findings according to 2022 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology Foundation/Heart Failure Society of America heart failure guideline [17]. CKD stage 3–5 was defined and calculated according to 2024 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) clinical guideline for CKD [18].

TyG index calculation

TyG index was defined according to previous study [19]. The computational formula is shown as follows: TyG index=ln [fasting triglyceride (mg/dL)×fasting blood glucose (mg/dL)2]

Endpoints and follow-up

The clinical endpoints included cardiovascular death and HF-related rehospitalization. The deadline for follow-up was 1 January 2023 or the occurrence of clinical endpoints defined in this study, whichever occurred first. For these patients who failed to conduct outpatient follow-up, we evaluated their physical condition via telephone.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed by SPSS Statistical Software (IBM Corp., Released 2019, IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while continuous variables with non-normal distribution were expressed with median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were presented as percentage. Cox regression analysis was used to assess the risk of clinical endpoints. The covariates included in multivariate Cox regression analysis were the independent risk factors which showed statistical significance in univariate analysis. Clinically relevant risk factors were also adjusted in multivariate Cox regression model, including age, male, smoking, drinking, and hypertension. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) were used to assess the predictive value of TyG index for clinical endpoints. Kaplan–Meier’s analysis was performed to calculate incidence of clinical endpoints at different levels of TyG index, the cutoff value of TyG index was calculated via Youden index from ROC curves and AUC. And log-rank test was used for evaluating statistical differences in Kaplan–Meier’s analysis. Logistic regression analysis was used to confirm the independent predictors of HF. The covariates showed statistical significance in univariate logistic regression analysis was adjusted in multivariate analysis. A two-sided p value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 2749 patients were diagnosed with CKD stage 3–5 between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2021 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University. Two thousand four hundred and sixty-five patients who met excluded criteria were excluded from our study, and 284 subjects were finally enrolled in our cohort. Of these, 169 (59.5%) patients were men, median age was 65.00 (58.00–72.00). One hundred and nine (38.4%) patients experienced diabetes mellitus, and 223 (78.5%) participants have a medical history of hypertension. Adverse clinical outcomes on follow-up

During the follow-up, a total of 23 patients (8.1%) experienced clinical endpoints. Among them, 20 patients (7.1%) experienced HF-related rehospitalization, five patients (1.8%) experienced cardiovascular death. In the analysis of risk factors for HF-related rehospitalization, multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that TyG index (hazard ratio (HR) = 1.790, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.095–2.927, p = .020), left ventricular ejection fraction (HR = 0.896, 95% CI = 0.829–0.969, p = .006), and nitrates (HR = 5.728, 95% CI = 2.210–14.848, p < .001) were associated with HF-related rehospitalization (Tables 2a and 2b). In the analysis of risk factors for cardiovascular death, multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that urea nitrogen (HR = 1.021, 95% CI = 1.010–1.031, p < .001) and TyG index (HR = 2.379, 95% CI = 1.015–5.578, p = .046) were independent risk factors of cardiovascular death (Table 3).

Independent predictors of HF

There was not any participant complicated with HF at baseline. However, a total of 28 (9.9%) participants were diagnosed with HF during the follow-up. Among them, 26 subjects (92.9%) were diagnosed with HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), one subject (3.6%) was diagnosed with HF with reduced ejection fraction, and one subject (3.6%) was diagnosed with HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction. Additionally, we have detected predictive factors for HF in our study. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that TyG index (odds ratio (OR) = 1.827, 95% CI = 1.088–3.070, p = .023), age (OR = 1.063, 95% CI = 1.012–1.116, p = .014), systolic blood pressure (OR = 1.021, 95% CI = 1.004–1.039, p = .013), creatinine (OR = 1.004, 95% CI = 1.001–1.007, p = .008), left atrial diameter (OR = 1.140, 95% CI = 1.052–1.236, p = .001), and nitrates (OR = 5.342, 95% CI = 1.779–16.040, p = .003) were positive independent predictors of HF (Table 4).

Discussion

The main findings in our study are: (1) CKD stage 3–5 patients with high TyG index level experienced higher risk of adverse clinical events and (2) TyG index is a significant marker for predicting HF in patients with CKD stage 3–5.

Patients with CKD, especially stage 3–5, have a much larger burden of CVD compared with those without CKD. Previous studies have revealed the prevalence of CVD was 75.3% in patients with CKD stage 4–5 and 66.6% in patients with CKD stage 3, which were higher than stage 1–2 and normal population [1]. Therefore, identifying risk factors for predicting CVD plays important role in improving cardiovascular prognosis in CKD patients. Previous studies [8,20,21] have revealed that uremic toxins, increased oxidative stress, and hemodynamic abnormalities including elevated arterial stiffness would affect cardiovascular outcomes. However, these studies paid less attention on abnormal metabolism, including glucolipid metabolism, which play a crucial part in worsening cardiovascular prognosis. What’s more, there is an inevitable measurement bias in assessing arterial stiffness calculated by PWV. Hence, identifying an easily obtainable and objective laboratory marker, especially in metabolic mechanism, is essential for predicting cardiovascular risk in CKD patients.

In our study, we found that CKD stage 3–5 patients with higher TyG index experienced higher risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. TyG index is calculated to assess IR, and it is associated with the level of blood glucose and blood lipid. Previous studies [22,23] have revealed that inflammation and fibrosis in CKD, which were resulted from several mechanisms including IR, were related to various clinical conditions. Inflammation caused by IR in CKD is associated with cardiac remodeling and myocardial fibrosis, which lead to worsen cardiovascular outcomes [1,24]. Several studies [25,26] have confirmed that IR, caused by metabolic alteration including hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia, promote the development of CVD. 2022 KDIGO guidelines for diabetes management in CKD [27] have emphasized improving glycemic control is crucial in the management of diabetic nephropathy, patients with diabetes and CKD are supposed to treated with sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, a kind of hypoglycemic agent, to reduce cardiovascular risk. A scientific statement from the American Heart Association [28] has recommended hemoglobin A1c goals in HF patients with diabetes, which revealed the relationship between blood glucose level and cardiovascular outcomes. Previous studies have confirmed the prognostic value of TyG index for cardiovascular events. Zhou et al. have conducted a retrospective study [29] which revealed that chronic HF patients with higher TyG index level experienced increasing risk of cardiovascular death (HR = 1.54, 95% CI = 1.38–1.72, p < .001). Another study [30] conducted by Yu et al. showed a U-shape association between TyG index and cardiovascular death in general population, which confirmed the value of TyG index for risk assessment in CVD.

HF is an end-stage of CVD, which seriously influenced the prognosis of patients. Exploring independent predictors of HF and removing variable clinical risk factors were significant in clinical practice. In our study, we found that TyG index was an independent predictor of HF. The pathophysiology mechanism [31] promoting HF is based on ventricular remodeling, and the molecular changes associated with ventricular remodeling included abnormal neurohumor activation, increased cytokine expression, immune changes, inflammatory response, and metabolic alteration. Metabolic influence, especially IR, plays an essential role in the development of HF and CKD [32]. 2023 American College of Cardiology Expert Consensus for HFpEF [33] has revealed that obesity is a strongly risk factor of HFpEF, and IR may explain the association between obesity and HFpEF. Previous studies [34,35] have revealed that IR could decrease myocardial glucose uptake and utilization for energy production, which would result in an increased reliance on fatty acid β-oxidation for energy production. The metabolic shift comes with increased myocardial oxygen consumption and lower cardiac efficiency, which would lead to myocardial hypertrophy, cardiac fibrosis, and diastolic dysfunction [35,36]. Considering TyG index is calculated for assessing IR, it is reasonable to regard TyG index as independent predictor of HF in CKD patients. What’s more, TyG index is associated with blood glucose level. 2023 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for CVD in patients with diabetes [37] has revealed that hyperglycemia can cause myocardial damage and result in cardiomyopathy via multiple pathways, and it is an important risk factor for HF, especially HFpEF. Previous studies have verified our point. Xu et al. [38] have revealed that patients with highest quartile of TyG index experienced 24% higher risk of HF (HR = 1.24, 95% CI = 1.07–1.44). A meta-analysis conducted by Li et al. [39] also showed that high TyG index was an independent predictor for HF in general population.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the selected bias cannot be unavoidable due to the retrospective nature. Second, we just included participants with CKD stage 3–5 in our study, and explored the association between TyG index and CVD prognosis. Future studies need to enroll more participants, including CKD stage 1–2 patients. What’s more, this study aimed to explore the effect of TyG index for CVD in patients with CKD. However, considering some comorbidities may be associated with TyG index level, such as diabetes mellitus. Future studies are supposed to explore predictive value of TyG index in different comorbidity situations. Third, considering the relatively small sample size in our study, future large cohort studies are supposed to be conducted to confirm our results. Finally, this is a retrospective study, some parameters of cardiac structure and function, including left atrial volume, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion, and right ventricular hemodynamics feature, cannot be acquired. It is necessary for future studies to include more cardiac parameters to confirm the findings in our study.

Conclusions

A higher level of TyG index is associated with worsening CVD prognosis, and TyG index can be considered as a marker to predict the occurrence of HF events in non-dialysis CKD stage 3–5 patients. Highlighting clinicians should focus IR in the management of CKD for improving prognosis and quality of life.
