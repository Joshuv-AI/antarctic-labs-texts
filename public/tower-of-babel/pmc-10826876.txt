Mental health morbidities in Kerala, India: Insights from National Mental Health Survey, 2015–2016
Authors: Sonakshi Jyrwa, T. M. Shibukumar, Jayakrishnan Thavody, P. K. Anish, Thomas Bina, K.R. Rajith, Pradeep S. Banandur, Girish N. Rao, Gopalkrishna Gururaj, Mathew Varghese, Vivek Benegal
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:The National Mental Health Survey was borne out of the felt need for a comprehensive epidemiological survey on mental health to understand the magnitude of psychiatric morbidities in India to aid in mental health policymaking, service planning, and delivery. Kerala was one of the 12 surveyed states, representing southern India.Aims:To estimate the prevalence and pattern of various mental illnesses and substance use disorders in a representative sample from Kerala state.Settings and Design:A household survey using a multi-stage, stratified, random cluster sampling technique, with selection based on probability proportionate to size at each stage.Materials and Methods:The community-based survey was carried out by trained field staff on individuals from systematically selected households from three randomly selected districts of Kerala. The instruments used in the survey included M.I.N.I adult version 6.0, a modified version of the Fagerström Nicotine Dependence Scale and questionnaires to screen for epilepsy, intellectual disability, and autism spectrum disorders.Results:A total of 2479 respondents aged >18 years were interviewed. The lifetime and current prevalence of mental morbidity (excluding tobacco use disorders) was 14.14% and 11.36%, respectively. Neurotic/stress-related disorders and depressive disorders were 5.43% and 2.49%, respectively, while severe mental disorders were prevalent in 0.44% of the sample. The prevalence of high risk for suicide was 2.23%.Conclusions:The survey revealed high rates of common mental illnesses and suicide risk in the state when compared to national estimates.

INTRODUCTION

Understanding the prevalence and pattern of psychiatric morbidities is fundamental to the formulation of mental health policies and strategies for the need-based allocation of the limited resources available for mental health care in our country. Community-based epidemiological studies and surveys have been conducted in India in the past to study[123456789101112] the magnitude of mental health morbidities, but the results were highly variable, ranging from 9.5[1] to 102[7] per thousand population, rendering the findings non-generalizable. The variable findings were postulated to be due to several reasons such as methodological differences, varying case definitions and diagnostic criteria, lack of reliability and validity of the assessment tools used in the studies, to name a few.[1314] The prevalence rates estimated from the two meta-analyses of epidemiological studies conducted in India nearly 2 decades ago were 58.2/1000[15] and 73/1000.[16] Some researchers believe that these estimates do not truly reflect the actual prevalence in the population considering the possibilities of systematic underreporting of mental morbidities due to lack of awareness, stigma and discrimination.[1314] Thus, a modest estimation of psychiatric morbidities was calculated to be 100–200/1000, which is nearly 20%, a sizeable population in need of active mental health intervention.[14]

Recognizing the socio-demographic and cultural diversity across states and regions in India, mental health policies, services, and interventions must be tailored to cater to the population-specific mental health requirements. There are no representative estimates on mental health from Kerala other than a community-based study in rural Kerala 2 decades back which roughly estimated the prevalence of primary psychiatric disorders to be 14.5/1000.[17]

This paucity of representative statistics on mental morbidities has been a major roadblock to mental health policy and infrastructure development in developing countries such as ours.[18] Hence, the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW) in collaboration with 12 partner institutions across the country under the leadership of the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS), Bengaluru, sponsored the first and largest epidemiological study on mental health in India – the National Mental Health Survey (NMHS), 2015–2016. The initiative was undertaken with the primary objective of estimating the prevalence and patterns of mental illnesses, studying the pattern of healthcare-seeking behavior, healthcare utilization, and treatment gap, and assessing the mental health burden among respondents with current mental illness.[1920]

In the current paper, we present the findings of the NMHS from the state of Kerala with emphasis on the prevalence and pattern of psychiatric disorders including substance use disorders in the state.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This community-based household survey was undertaken to study the prevalence and pattern of various mental health morbidities and substance use disorders in the state of Kerala as part of the National Mental Health Survey (NMHS). Qualitative assessments through focused group discussions (FGDs) and key informant interviews (KIIs) were also carried out to obtain data difficult to obtain by the quantitative household survey.

Under the guidance of NIMHANS, Bengaluru, the Department of Psychiatry at the Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (IMHANS), Kozhikode, in collaboration with the Department of Community Medicine, Government Medical College, Kozhikode (GMCK), conducted the survey in Kerala. Ethical approvals for the survey were obtained from the institutional ethics committees of GMCK (Ref No: GMCKKD/RP 2015/IEC/98) and NIMHANS (Letter No. NIMHANS/DO/98th IEC/2015 dated 16/07/2015 of Member Secretary, NIMHANS Institutional Ethics Committee).

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on the estimates in the pilot study undertaken in the Kolar district of Karnataka.[19] The prevalence of any mental health morbidity among adults in the pilot study was found to be 7.5%. The final sample size was calculated to be 3000, which was derived with a design effect of 3 and an absolute error of 2% at the confidence level of 95%.[20]

Sampling design

We adopted a multi-stage, stratified, random cluster sampling technique, with random selection based on probability proportionate to size at each stage (MSRS-PPS).[20] Briefly, multi-stage sampling was adopted (District→Taluk→Village/Ward/Cluster→Household (HH)→Individual), with the state as the sampling frame and districts and talukas as primary and secondary sampling units, respectively, and the individuals within the enlisted households formed the unit of analysis. Three districts, Thrissur, Palakkad, and Pathanamthitta, were selected using a stratified random sampling technique based on district-level poverty estimates. A total of 6 talukas (2 from each district) and 60 clusters (10 clusters per taluka) were selected across the state. The numbers of rural and urban (metro and non-metro) clusters selected were proportionate to their state contribution as per the 2011 census.[21] Within each cluster, the households were selected after mapping and listing exercises using a systematic random sampling method. All adults (≥18 years) belonging to the selected households, who gave written informed consent, were interviewed. Additional two visits were made in the event of the non-availability of a member of the household, after which if unavailable, the individual was considered a non-responder.

Field Survey Procedure: The data were collected by eight trained field data collectors (FDCs), of whom one was assigned as a field coordinator. The FDCs were persons having postgraduate qualifications in one of the following: psychology, social work, social sciences, or psychiatric nursing. They received 6 weeks of extensive training (theoretical and practical orientation) on mental illnesses, survey methodology, the use of survey instruments, and data collection on hand-held digital devices configured for the survey.

Qualitative assessments by FGDs and KIIs were carried out at the end of field data collection for qualitative inquiry, such as patterns of substance abuse (both licit and illicit substances), the perceived treatment gap, stigma, and the barriers to mental health care delivery. Four of the FGDs were done in the survey districts, and a selected group of people from the locality participated. One FGD in which participants were doctors was conducted at Kozhikode. Three of the KII were done with psychiatrists, one with a physician, and three were done with pharmacists.

Quality Control: For quality assurance, a robust monitoring mechanism was built into the protocol, which included (i) daily monitoring of activities by the field coordinator, weekly by principal investigators (PIs) and co-investigators (Co-PIs) at IMHANS, and fortnightly monitoring and review by NIMHANS through video-conferencing; (ii) certification of training for data collectors to ensure high levels of inter-rater reliability; (iii) regular data checks and feedback; and (iv) 5% validation of re-interviews by field data supervisors and PIs. The re-interview data were analyzed to study the agreement between the interviews and re-interviews and had a kappa value of 0.54, suggesting moderate agreement, indicating that the quality of data obtained from the survey was satisfactory, despite the limitations inherent to surveys of mental health disorders.[19]

Instruments: All survey instruments were validated Malayalam translations, developed using a standard translation-back translation protocol.[1920] Translated instruments were checked for social and cultural appropriateness and field-tested before use. Details of survey instruments are provided elsewhere.[1920]

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (frequencies and proportions) provided estimates of the prevalence of mental illnesses coded by using the International Classification of Disease, 10th revision, Diagnostic Criteria for Research (ICD-10-DCR)[22] criteria for classifying psychiatric disorders, and has operationally defined specific terminologies. The probability of selection of districts and tehsils (design weight) and non-response to arrive at prevalence estimates of psychiatric disorders as per ICD-10-DCR was used to weigh the estimated results. As the data were digitally collected, data merging and cleaning was undertaken and analysis was done using SPSS 18.0[23] and STATA 11.0[24] for Windows.

RESULTS

A total of 3149 eligible individuals were contacted, of which 2479 consented and were interviewed during NMHS (National Mental Health Survey), Kerala, 2015–2016. The overall response rate was 78.7%. The socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents are given in Table 1. There is almost an equal representation of all age groups, with the geriatric population (above 60 years) comprising about 26% of the sample. The observed female preponderance in the sample (57.6%) was proportionate to state census 2011 findings of gender distribution (females constituting 52.02% as per the census). Of the respondents, 52% were rural residents (52.30% of the population were rural residents in the 2011 census). Nearly 75% were married. Occupation-wise, nearly 60% of female respondents were family workers, while 33% of males were employees. Literacy rates were also comparable between the survey population and census data of 2011 for males (97.53% vs 96.11%) and females (92.07% vs 93.84%) taken separately as well as total (95.41% vs 94%).[21] Nearly 85% of respondents were at least high-school-educated.

The overall lifetime prevalence of any mental morbidity was 14.14% (CI 13.8-14.28), and the current mental morbidity among individuals above 18 years of age was 11.36% (CI 11.05-11.67).

Table 2 shows the weighted prevalence (in %) of psychiatric morbidities in Kerala. Among psychiatric morbidities, common mental disorders (CMDs) which include neurotic/stress-related disorders, mood, and substance abuse disorders (excluding tobacco) were reported in 11% of the individuals. The prevalence of severe mental disorders (SMDs), which include bipolar affective disorders, psychotic disorder, and severe depression with psychotic features, was found to be 0.44%. The lifetime and current prevalence of mood disorders was 6.22% (CI 5.98–6.45) and 2.62% (CI 2.47–2.78), respectively. Among the mood disorders, the lifetime prevalence of depressive disorders was 5.54% (CI 5.31-5.76) and the current prevalence was 2.49% (CI 2.34-2.65). The prevalence of any substance use disorder was 10.12% (CI 9.82–10.41), out of which tobacco use disorder, 7.22% (CI 6.96–7.47), was the most common, followed by alcohol use disorder, 4.82% (CI 4.61–5.03). Neurotic and stress-related disorders (predominantly phobic anxiety disorder and agoraphobia), depressive disorders, and substance use disorders (predominantly tobacco and alcohol) were the most prevalent psychiatric disorders in the survey (in descending order) [refer to Table 2]. Nearly 0.4% of individuals screened positive each for epilepsy and intellectual disability (including autism spectrum disorder).

The prevalence rates of mental health morbidities show a rising trend with the decreasing level of education, the highest being among illiterates. Individuals who were widowed/separated/divorced had a higher prevalence of mental morbidities. Common mental disorders were found to be most prevalent in the 50–59 years age group (current 13.94%) and nearly 2 times more prevalent in males (current 14.96%) when compared to females (current 8.12%). Among the CMDs, depressive disorders had the highest current prevalence in this age group at 4.73% (CI 4.22–5.23). Similar trends were observed with SMDs, with the highest among those aged 60 years and above (current 0.97%) and males (current 0.55%). Higher prevalence rates of both common and severe mental disorders were found in those residing in urban metros (current 12.73% and 0.65%, respectively).

Concerning the prevalence of specific psychiatric morbidities as per age, neurotic and stress-related disorders were the most common in the 18–29 years age group (current 6.25%, CI 5.75–6.75). Tobacco use disorder showed increasing prevalence with increasing age and was the most prevalent in those aged ≥50–59 years (current 10.63%, CI 9.9–11.36) with a slight dip in those above 60 years of age (10.08%). Alcohol use disorder was more prevalent among 30–39-year (6.71%) and 50–59-year-old groups (6.65%). Concerning gender, neurotic and stress-related disorders and mood disorders were more prevalent in females (6.51% vs 3.98% in males), whereas severe mental illnesses (schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders 0.25% vs 0.15%) and substance use disorders were more prevalent in males (alcohol use disorder 11.26% vs 0.07% in females; tobacco use disorder 14.16% vs 2.11% in females).

High suicide risk (a score of 17 on module B of MINI) and moderate suicide risk (Score 9-16) were found in 2.23% (CI 2.08-2.37) and 0.96% (CI 0.86-1.05) of the respondents, respectively. As per Kerala state Crime Records Bureau,[25] the overall suicide incidence rate for the year 2014–15 was 23.9/100,000, with 4 times higher incidence in males (40.1) than in females (11.7). The records also revealed a rising trend in suicide incidence rates with increasing age, the highest among those aged 60 and above (42.16). Corresponding with the incidence trends in the state, suicide risk increased with age from 1.81% in those aged 18–29 and peaked in 40–49 age group (2.63%), with a slight dip to 2.2% in those aged 60 years and above [Figure 1]. Males had a higher suicide risk than females (2.59% and 1.74%, respectively). Urban non-metros (2.85%) reported a marginally higher rate than the rural (2.26%) and metro urban (2.05%) areas.

DISCUSSION

Kerala surpasses other states of India and is closer to high-resource countries in terms of physical health and human development indicators.[26272829] The advances made in the health sector are partially the result of a comparatively well-organized public health system, a health model that is widely lauded.[2930] With regard to mental health, there is a dearth of epidemiological studies focusing on mental health morbidities in Kerala. NMHS is the largest comprehensive survey on mental health to be conducted in India as well as the state of Kerala.

Although many sample characteristics were representative of the general population, there was a marginally higher representation of females. Over 1/4th of the sample population was over 60 years of age, which is nearly twice the state's population distribution.

The survey revealed that the lifetime prevalence of any mental morbidity among adults in Kerala was 14.14% and the current prevalence was 11.36%. As per the projected population of Kerala in 2016, this translates to nearly 3 million persons living with mental illnesses (excluding tobacco use) in Kerala. Approximately, 1 in every 8 persons in Kerala has a mental morbidity warranting active mental health intervention. If tobacco use disorders are included, then the estimated numbers are massive, nearly 4.5 million. The overall prevalence of psychiatric morbidities in Kerala was higher than the national estimates (lifetime: 13.67% and current: 10.56%).[20] Common mental disorders constituted the largest proportion (11%) of psychiatric morbidities in the state, of which substance use disorders (tobacco > alcohol) were the most prevalent, followed by mood disorders (depressive disorders) and neurotic and stress-related disorders (mainly phobic anxiety disorder and agoraphobia). The state findings nearly echoed the NMHS national estimates except for tobacco use disorders and severe mental disorders which were just about half the national rates, while neurotic and stress-related disorders and suicide risk were nearly 1.5 and 2 times, respectively [Figure 2].

The weighted current prevalence of mental morbidities in Kerala in comparison with national estimates

The high rates of neurotic and stress-related disorders in the state, especially in the 18–29-year-olds, could be an indicator of rising mental health issues potentially contributed by modernization, urbanization, consumerism, unemployment or lack of education-commensurate jobs, gender inequality, academic pressures, and substance use (especially alcohol).[2930] The lower prevalence of tobacco use disorders could be explained by the education status of the population. Kerala has the highest literacy rate in India. Several studies have observed a significant association between low educational levels with tobacco use behavior and knowledge and awareness of the detrimental health effects of tobacco.[31323334]

Mental morbidity distribution across socio-demographic characteristics revealed the highest prevalence in those aged 50 years and above, males, the widowed/separated/divorced, those currently employed, and residents of urban Kerala. The high prevalence of psychiatric morbidities, including substance use disorders in the elderly population and a substantially high suicide risk of 2.2%, is concerning. The factors were not particularly studied in this survey. However, the geriatric population is known to be biologically vulnerable and economically disadvantaged, often needing support, for financial and health needs. A large percentage of this vulnerable section, nearly 30%, is below the poverty line,[35] and the government pension schemes currently benefit only 10% (2.76 million out of 28 million) of elderly people, predominantly those residing in urban areas.[36] The state of Kerala is witnessing a rising trend in migration among the youth, with one of the highest rates of Gulf and other international labor migration in India, possibly due to the unavailability of jobs commensurable to education and expectations.[3031323334353637] Due to the migration of offspring to foreign countries and the shift in the family structures/organization from joint to nuclear type, the elderly suffer social isolation and neglect. Besides the psycho-social adversities faced, the age-related rise in medical morbidities might also contribute to a high prevalence of psychiatric co-morbidities. The bio-psycho-social determinants of mental health morbidities in the elderly need further exploration, and targeted interventions need to be planned accordingly.

NMHS survey is one of the few epidemiological studies in India to assess suicidal risk. Early identification of an individual with suicide risk is vital for the formulation and planning of suicide prevention policies. The survey revealed an alarming prevalence of ‘suicide risk’ (2.23%), nearly twice the national estimates, and the highest among the 12 surveyed states. Previous studies have also documented similar findings.[38] The prevalence positively correlates with the suicide incidence rates in the state. Based on the prevalence rates, the projected estimates were 587,385 and 252,865 individuals with high and moderate suicidal risk, respectively. A sizeable section of the population is in dire need of active psychiatric intervention.

Suicide risk was found to be higher in males and residents of urban non-metros and the highest in those aged 40–49 years. Similar socio-demographic characteristics were identified as determinants of suicide in previous studies as well.[39] Substance use disorders, especially alcohol use disorders, one of the determinants of suicide, have a higher prevalence in males and are known to be highly co-morbid with psychiatric disorders. This could explain the gender variation. The age and gender variation in suicide risk did not correlate with variation in overall psychiatric morbidity or prevalence of any psychiatric disorder; in particular, this finding suggests other factors playing a role. Studies in the past have indicated the role of psycho-social stress as an important risk factor for suicides.[39404142] National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) data report that family problems, illness, alcohol/drug misuse, and financial difficulties are major drivers of suicide in India[43] and in Kerala[25] too.

The paradox that the state is a leader in physical health and socio-demographic indicators (namely, literacy rates, quality of life) and among the top five states in suicide rates in the country has been baffling researchers and clinicians. One theory postulated that high literacy rates and unavailability of education-commensurate jobs in the state resulted in underemployment, wherein the individual is overqualified for his job and possibly underpaid and dissatisfied.[30] A study on unemployment in Kerala considered underemployment and ‘educated unemployment’ in women to be serious issues for the state.[44] Low levels of underreporting of suicide in the state were postulated as a possible reason for higher statistics when compared to other states.[45] The overall high prevalence of psychiatric morbidities and implicated psycho-social factors could explain the increased suicide risk in the state. The evaluation of risk factors or psycho-social determinants of suicidality was not the primary objective of the current survey, and more studies are warranted to understand the bio-psycho-social and economic underpinning of suicide in the state. This will aid in the formulation of preventive strategies.

The study despite its robust methodology and rigorous monitoring has certain limitations. As the geriatric population had one of the highest psychiatric burden in the state, neuro-cognitive disorders such as dementia, one of the common causes of psychiatric morbidity in the geriatric population, were not assessed for specifically.

The NMHS survey in Kerala revealed high psychiatric morbidity in the state, with a high prevalence of neurotic and stress-related disorders and findings of the highest suicide risk among the surveyed states. The high prevalence of psychiatric morbidities in the elderly population (>50 years), including substance use disorders and substantially high suicide risk of 2.2%, is alarming. These findings warrant further exploration of factors and planning targeted intervention in this vulnerable population. Low rates of tobacco use disorders and severe mental illness in the state when compared to national estimates are worth mentioning and exploring further.
