External validation of the modified Brain Injury Guidelines: an observational study
Authors: Giorgio Colombo, Anna Giuliani, Francesca Gianni, Rosa Casella, Giulio Andrea Bertani, Giovanni Casazza, Giorgio Costantino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) is a frequent cause of emergency department (ED) admission, with most cases being uncomplicated. However, a subset of patients presents with intracranial findings on CT, such as cerebral hemorrhage or skull fracture, that raise concerns for potential clinical deterioration. The modified Brain Injury Guidelines (mBIG) provide a risk-stratification framework for managing such patients but do not address cerebral hygromas or indeterminate radiological lesions, both commonly encountered in clinical practice. This study aimed to externally validate the mBIG criteria, including patients with cerebral hygromas and indeterminate radiological lesions on CT scan, in order to assess their prognostic accuracy for severe neurological outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective single-center observational study of 451 adult patients presenting to the ED with a blunt head trauma who underwent initial CT imaging and clinical evaluation. Patients were classified as mBIG 1, 2, or 3 based on CT findings and clinical criteria. Outcomes were extracted from electronic medical records, with the primary outcome defined as a composite of death due to mTBI, neurosurgical intervention, or admission to intensive care unit.ResultsAmong 237 patients classified as mBIG 1, 38.8% exhibited an indeterminate radiological lesion and 35% had cerebral hygromas. The primary outcome was observed in only one mBIG 1 patient (0.4%; 95% CI: 0.0%–2.3%), demonstrating high sensitivity (94.7%; 95% CI: 74%-99.9%) and low negative likelihood ratio (0.1; 95% CI: 0.01-0.65), albeit with wide confidence intervals due to limited sample size. Radiological progression occurred in 6.3% of mBIG 1 patients, though none required neurosurgical intervention or intensive care admissionConclusionThe mBIG criteria appear to be a safe and efficient approach for managing complicated mTBI, even in cases involving cerebral hygromas and indeterminate radiological lesions. This preliminary validation suggests good prognostic performance, but further large-scale prospective studies are needed to confirm applicability in routine ED practice.

Background

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a frequent cause of emergency department (ED) access worldwide, with varying incidence rates between developed and developing countries [1]. The majority of TBIs are classified as mild (mTBI) [2], a condition variably defined by a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score greater than 12, or more commonly a score > 13 measured 30 min after the injury [3]. Although most mTBIs are uncomplicated a subset of patients may present with complications such as cerebral hemorrhage or skull fractures [4-5]. These patients are typically discharged in good health either directly from the ED or following a short hospital stay; only a small proportion of patients experience disability or mortality.

Patients with mTBI who show abnormalities on CT imaging represent a heterogeneous group. Despite the overall low risk for clinical deterioration or severe adverse outcomes some cases may warrant increased clinical attention [6]. This uncertainty often compels emergency physicians to prolong ED observation, consult neurosurgery services, and request repeated CT scans—potentially exposing patients to additional risks without proportional clinical benefit [7].

The modified Brain Injury Guidelines (mBIG) provide a clinical framework to guide decisions about hospital admission, neurosurgical consultation, and repeated CT imaging in patients with complicated mTBI. The mBIG stratify patients into three prognostic categories (mBIG 1, 2, or 3), considering factors such as anticoagulant use, alcohol intoxication, and the type and extent of intracranial hemorrhages or skull fractures [8]. According to these criteria, mBIG 1 patients may be safely discharged without urgent neurosurgical consultation or routine repeat CT scanning.

Derived from the original Brain Injury Guidelines (BIG), mBIG appear to offer improved sensitivity for ruling out severe outcomes in complicated mTBI⁸. A internal validation has been performed by the authors who proposed the guidelines, and some external validations has also been reported [9, 10, 11].

Although BIG and mBIG are the most extensively studied tools for risk stratification in complicated mTBI, alternative approaches with comparable outcomes exist [12].

It is important to note that mBIG do not address two common post-traumatic conditions: cerebral hygromas and indeterminate radiological lesions.

Post-traumatic cerebral hygroma typically presents as an extra-axial collection with attenuation values similar to cerebrospinal fluid. These lesions are thought to arise from disruption of the dural-arachnoid interface, accompanied by subdural cerebrospinal fluid leakage [13]. While generally benign and self-resolving, a minority may evolve into hemorrhagic transformations or require surgical evacuation [14, 15]. Radiographically, post-traumatic subdural hygromas can be distinguished from subdural hematomas by their imaging characteristics and clinically indolent course [13].

Uncertainty in diagnosing minor hemorrhages stems from the limitations of CT scan resolution—particularly for intracerebral hemorrhages measuring less than 5 mm. Studies have demonstrated poor inter-rater reliability even among experienced neuroradiologists for hemorrhages below this threshold [16]. In practice, this often results in indeterminate or equivocal CT reports, leading to additional scans and neurosurgical consultations, and extending ED observation without clear clinical benefit. Despite the inclination toward conservative management, there is no established consensus for patients with an indeterminate radiological lesion.

Cerebral hygromas and indeterminate radiological lesions are commonly encountered in clinical settings, yet no current guidelines or previous study in literature offer a clear recommendations for the ED management of mTBI patients affected by these conditions. The mBIG criteria do not explicitly account for these scenarios, potentially limiting their applicability and external validity and no study in literature has dealt with the problem.

A recent meta-analysis [17] has demonstrated high sensitivity for the BIG criteria in ruling out the need for neurosurgical intervention among BIG 1 patients. However, neither subdural hygromas nor indeterminate radiological lesions were specifically addressed. Notably, mBIG criteria may offer superior diagnostic performance compared to BIG [8]. A recent retrospective study by Freeman and colleagues has studied the diagnostic accuracy of mBIG in TBI patients, but it does not have dealt with either cerebral hygromas or indeterminate radiological lesions [11].

The aim of the current study is to externally validate the mBIG framework with specific consideration for post-traumatic cerebral hygromas and indeterminate radiological lesions.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective, single-center observational study involving patients who underwent a head computed tomography (CT) following blunt traumatic brain injury at the Emergency Department (ED) of the Fondazione IRCCS Ca’Granda Ospedale Maggiore Policlinico di Milano, between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2019. The ED sees approximately 56,000 visits annually and serves as a referral center for neurological and neurosurgical conditions. A consultant neurologist is available 24/7, while neuroradiologists, interventional neuroradiologists, and neurosurgeons are present from 08:00 to 20:00 and remain on-call overnight, every head CT scan report is issued by a consultant neuroradiologist.

Study population, inclusion and exclusion criteria

To identify the initial study cohort, all head CT scans performed during the study period with the diagnostic indication of “head injury” were reviewed. Associated ED reports were then examined to determine eligibility.

The study included all the adult patients (≥ 18 years) who presented to the ED, underwent a head CT scan and a medical evaluation after a mTBI and presented a definite or possible post-traumatic intracranial finding at the CT scan report.

Patients with mild TBI (mTBI) and post-traumatic intracranial findings on initial CT were categorized according to the modified Brain Injury Guidelines (mBIG) as mBIG 1, 2, or 3 [9]. In cases lacking criteria for mBIG 2 or 3, isolated hygromas were classified as mBIG 1 due to their low risk of lesion progression. A radiological lesion was considered indeterminate when there was any degree of uncertainty for the presence of a post-traumatic lesion according to what has been written in the CT scan report. Indeterminate radiological lesions were assigned to the higher mBIG category in accordance with the entity and location of the lesion for which the suspect was raised. For instance, a small dubious petechial hemorrhage was categorized as mBIG1, whereas a dubious intraparenchymal hyperdense lesion with its major diameter bigger than one centimeter and suspected to be possibly hemorrhagic was categorized as mBIG 3.

For each patients electronic medical records were reviewed to extract outcomes of interest and demographic and clinical variables including age, sex, and comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, coronary artery disease, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, active malignancy, cognitive impairment, prior neurosurgical intervention, previous cerebral hemorrhage). Additional data collected included antithrombotic therapy (anticoagulant and/or antiplatelet agents), injury type from CT findings, timing of repeat CT scans (during ED stay or post-discharge), alcohol intoxication, and ED length of stay (measured in minutes from admission to discharge).

Patients classified as mBIG 2 and 3 were included solely for demographic comparisons and for evaluating the primary outcome against the mBIG 1 cohort.

Outcomes of interest

The primary outcome was a composite of admission to the intensive care unit, neurosurgical intervention or death related to mTBI in patients classified as mBIG 1. These events were compared with corresponding outcomes in mBIG 2 and mBIG 3 patients to assess prognostic accuracy.

Secondary outcomes in the mBIG 1 cohort included:

In cases requiring admission, the destination ward (non-neurosurgical, neurosurgical, or neuro-intensive) and reason for hospitalization (clinical deterioration, radiological changes, or precautionary observation) were identified. Reasons for hospitalization and mortality were independently assessed by two authors (GCol and AG) via detailed chart review. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus with a third author (GCos).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize population characteristics. Categorical variables were reported as absolute frequencies (N) and percentages (%). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median and inter-quartile range (IQR). Outcome measures were reported as proportions with corresponding 95% confidence intervals, calculated via the Clopper-Pearson exact method.

To evaluate mBIG prognostic accuracy, we calculated sensitivity (SE), specificity (SP), positive and negative likelihood ratios (LR⁺ and LR⁻), and positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV), each with 95% confidence intervals (C.I.).

A total of 72 mBIG 1 patients were discharged after a single CT scan with no subsequent ED visits for clinical deterioration. All were discharged in their pre-trauma neurological state.

Indeterminate radiological lesions

A total of 140 possibly post-traumatic indeterminate radiological lesions was found. Ninety-two out of 140 patients were categorized as mBIG 1 and 48 out of 140 patients as non-mBIG 1.

As to the 92 mBIG 1 patients, they were all afflicted by very small dubious lesions with sub-cortical, cortical or subdural location.The radiological differential diagnosis was between a small hemorrhage and radiological artifact (28 out of 92 patients), a calcification (15 out of 92 patients), an hemangioma (3 out of 92 patients) or a vascular structure (7 out of 92 patients). The differential diagnosis was not clearly stated in 39 out of 92 patient and there was no radiological report with a high suspect for an hemorrhagic lesion (rated as probable or as “suggestive of an hemorrhagic lesion”).

As to the 48 non-mBIG 1 patients, they were all afflicted by small dubious lesions. The radiological differential diagnosis was between a small hemorrhage and radiological artifact (12 out of 48 patients), a calcification (7 out of 48 patients), an hemangioma (2 out of 48 patients), a meningioma (1 patients out of 48) or a vascular structure (3 out of 48 patients). The differential diagnosis was not clearly stated in 18 out of 48 patients. There were only five report with a higher suspicion of an hemorrhagic lesion (stated as “probable” in the original report). Of these five patients, two had very small probable extra-axial hemorrhages (not further described), 3 had a very small probable subarachnoid hemorrhage and a patient had a probable petechial contusion.

35 patients were considered non-mBIG 1 because of extra-radiological criteria (i.e. antithrombotic drugs assumption, alcohol consumption), whereas 13 patients were categorized as non-mBIG 1 because of a radiological criteria (i.e. size or location of the indeterminate radiological lesion).

Radiological progression of intracranial lesions was observed in 15 of 237 mBIG 1 patients (6.3%; 95% CI: 3.6%–10.2%). Of these, 8 patients were admitted to the hospital and 7 patients were directly discharged from the ED, with no further deterioration noted.

A total of 49 mBIG1 patients (20.9%; 95% CI: 15.7%–26.4%) were hospitalized, but only 11 admissions (4.6%; 95% CI: 2.3%–8.2%) were attributed directly to mTBI. Among these only one patient was admitted to the neurosurgery unit and just for clinical observation. Three patients were hospitalized due to worsening CT findings, and seven for clinical and radiological observation in non-neurosurgical wards. No admissions occurred due to in-ED clinical deterioration. None required surgical intervention or intensive care unit admission.

The patient admitted to the neurosurgical unit had a subdural hematoma and was discharged in good condition following spontaneous radiological resolution. As to the three patients admitted for lesion progression, they were all discharged after the radiological stabilization of the lesions. Of the seven patients admitted for observation, six improved without further treatment. One patient -initially admitted to the internal medicine ward- developed neurological worsening due to hemorrhagic transformation of a hygroma, compounded by urinary sepsis and pulmonary embolism, and subsequently died during neurosurgical admission.

Two additional deaths occurred in mBIG1 patients hospitalized for unrelated medical conditions. One, diagnosed with a pulmonary malignancy, died of cardiovascular complications; another, admitted for a sepsis-related delirium, died from systemic infection. Thus, three deaths were recorded in the mBIG 1 cohort, only one of which was potentially related to mTBI. The attributable mortality rate for mBIG 1 patients was 0.4% (95% CI: 0.0%–2.4%) though patient death depended on multiple clinical factors.

All other mBIG 1 patients were discharged with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15 and without new neurological deficits. Eventually only one patient (0.4%; 95% CI: 0.0%–2.3%) experienced brief episodes of non-convulsive seizures, successfully managed with anti-epileptic therapy.

Discussion

This study yields several noteworthy findings. First, patients classified as mBIG 1 exhibit a very low—but not null—risk of clinical progression toward severe neurological outcomes, even when cerebral hygromas and indeterminate radiological lesions are considered. Second, the mBIG criteria demonstrate good prognostic accuracy in predicting death from mTBI, neurosurgical intervention, and intensive care unit admission, including indeterminate radiological lesions and cerebral hygromas. However, due to the limited sample size, the confidence intervals for sensitivity and negative likelihood ratio remain broad, compelling for a validation through larger-scale studies before these results can inform clinical practice.

Third, cerebral hygromas and indeterminate radiological lesions were highly prevalent, comprising approximately 74% of the mBIG 1 population. Importantly, application of mBIG in this context may have prevented 167 repeat CT scans, 11 hospital admissions attributable to mTBI, multiple hours of ED observation, and numerous neurological consultations—potentially reducing patient exposure to unnecessary imaging, prolonged observation, and hospitalization.

Hospital admissions and mortality in mBIG 1 patients were only partially attributed to mTBI, highlighting that this cohort consists largely of medically or socially fragile individuals. Frailty appears to be a hallmark of complicated mTBI, as reflected by the high prevalence of multiple comorbidities. It is well-established that extended ED stays—particularly overnight—can lead to adverse outcomes in elderly and vulnerable patients [17]; thus, every hour spared through efficient triage may reduce complication rates, and mBIG may serve as a valuable tool in this regard.

Previous studies evaluating mBIG have not accounted for cerebral hygromas or indeterminate radiological lesions. Notably, in our cohort, 73.8% of complicated mTBI cases involved these two lesion types. Incorporating them into the mBIG framework has broadened its clinical applicability. While the clinical progression potential of indeterminate radiological lesions is inherently uncertain, cerebral hygromas typically progress slowly and are generally benign [14, 15]; it is of interest to note that the only clinically significant event in our population was associated with a cerebral hygroma.

We found a quite high number of indeterminate radiological lesions (i.e. 38,8%). This could be explained by the fact that each CT scan was reported by senior radiologists, which are prone to be very prudent during CT scan reporting, especially when issuing the report of very small lesions. Actually, the literature has already shown a low concordance in reporting small lesion (i.e. <5 mm), even in well-trained neuroradiologist [16]. The high rate of small indeterminate radiological lesions could partially explain the low rate of radiological progression of our study (i.e. 6,3%). Also, it has to be underscored that a very comprehensive definition of indeterminate radiological lesions was used to include patients in our study, perhaps widening the applicability of the study results to all kind of indeterminate radiological lesions. The current study is one of the few, or perhaps the only one that deals with prognostic stratification of indeterminate radiological lesion in mTBI. One of the major strength of this study lays in the fact that is faces the presence of indeterminate reports as a matter of fact in the clinical practice, trying to give a prognostic stratification to uncertainty.

The original mBIG derivation study by Khan et al. reported a radiological progression rate of 11% in the mBIG 1 population [8], slightly higher than the 6.3% (15 of 237 patients) observed in our cohort. This discrepancy may reflect the predominance of hygromas and indeterminate radiological lesions in our population, which carry lower progression risks. Other studies report comparable outcomes. For example, Khan’s validation study found radiological progression in 13.2% of mBIG 1 patients [9]. A recent study by Freeman and colleagues reported a CT scan repetition rate of 95.2% in mBIG 1 population, along with a radiological deterioration rate of 17%. These data are quite higher if compared to what have been reported in our study (i.e. repetition rate 70,5% and radiological deterioration rate 6,3%). This difference could be due to the fact that poly-trauma patients were excluded in our study (necessity of total body CT-scan for traumatic issues at the first ED evaluation was an exclusion criteria), furthermore, patients with a GCS < 13 at ED presentation were excluded from the study. We choose to focus on pure mTBI issues and the more severely injured patients could have been not enrolled in our study.

Regarding critical endpoints, Khan et al. reported neurosurgical intervention or death in 0 of 98 patients (0%; 95% CI: 0%–3.7%) and in 1 of 496 patients (0.2%; 95% CI: 0.0%–1.1%) in subsequent research. Our study found an equivalent rate of 0.4% (95% CI: 0.0%–2.3%), aligning closely with published data [19, 20].

Only a recent study by Freeman and colleagues has formally evaluated mBIG prognostic accuracy, even if this paper does not deal specifically with hygromas or indeterminate radiological lesions [11]. The authors found that mBIG 2 + 3 criteria were 100.0% sensitive for neurosurgical intervention, whereas their specificity was 37.2% and 18.1%, respectively. In our study we found a quite lower sensitivity (i.e. 94%) and a little higher specificity (i.e.54%). These differences could be due to two factors: the first one is that the primary outcome of the current paper included also ICU admission due to TB and TBI related death, the second factor is normal sample variability among different studies. The authors of the current paper believe that these differences do not change the meaning or the interpretations of the study results. The lower mortality rate in mBIG1 patients among the current paper (0.4%) and the one by Freeman et al. (2.4%) has to be related with the selection of patients in the two studies, as it was yet discussed previously. The study by Freeman and colleagues contain a brief meta-analysis on the need of neurosurgical intervention in TBI patients with a cumulative sample size of 1169 TBI patients: no mBIG 1 patients required intervention across these studies; these data are in line with what has been found in the current paper.

A multicenter retrospective study by Marincowitz et al. examined BIG performance, reporting sensitivity of 99.5% (95% CI: 98.1%–99%), LR⁻ of 0.1 (95% CI: 0.02–0.4), specificity of 4.8% (95% CI: 3.7%–6.3%), and a positive likelihood ratio of 1.05 (95% CI: 1.03–1.06) [12]. A recent meta-analysis [17]confirmed a BIG 1 sensitivity for neurosurgical intervention of 98% (95% CI: 93%–99%), but with low specificity and a single recorded death among 1,493 BIG 1 patients. These findings suggest BIG may yield more false positives than mBIG, potentially because BIG classifies patients on aspirin therapy as high risk—unlike mBIG.

The papers referenced above have employed various endpoints, such as radiological deterioration [8], clinical decline, ICU admission, neurosurgical intervention, or death [9, 11]. While we reviewed all these variables, we selected a composite outcome—neurosurgical intervention, intensive care unit admission, or mTBI-related death—as our primary endpoint. This approach reflects a more robust and clinically meaningful criterion, reducing the ambiguity associated with radiological or clinical progression, which can vary in reliability and impact.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective nature limits outcome ascertainment, particularly for patients discharged without scheduled neurosurgical or radiological follow-up. Thus, under-reporting of adverse events is possible. However, considering that the Fondazione IRCCS Ca’ Granda Ospedale Maggiore Policlinico di Milano is a tertiary referral center for neurosurgical care, it is unlikely that complications would have presented at external facilities. Second, the sample size was insufficiently powered, leading to wide confidence intervals and limiting the precision of prognostic estimates. Nevertheless, the point estimates of sensitivity and LR⁻ remain consistent with prior research, and this is the first study to evaluate mBIG performance in cases involving cerebral hygromas and indeterminate radiological lesions, for which sample size calculations were not feasible a priori. Third, this is a single center study conducted in the Italian reality, this could limit the generalizability of the study.

Conclusion

The mBIG criteria represent a promising tool for stratifying risk in complicated mTBI, particularly among fragile patients. Individuals classified as mBIG 1 could have a very low likelihood of progression to severe outcomes, even in the presence of indeterminate radiological lesions or cerebral hygromas. These findings support the potential utility of mBIG for improving ED resource allocation and clinical decision-making. A large-scale, prospective multicenter validation study is warranted to confirm these results and facilitate implementation in routine clinical practice.
