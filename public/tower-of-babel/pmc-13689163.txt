ApoB/ApoA-I ratio and all-cause mortality in maintenance hemodialysis: a cohort study
Authors: Zhen Zhang, Fangfang Xiang, Wuhua Jiang, Xuesen Cao, Xiaoqiang Ding, Bo Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraditional lipid markers have limited and inconsistent prognostic value in maintenance hemodialysis (MHD), a phenomenon often described as the lipid paradox. We evaluated whether lipid and apolipoprotein-related markers, particularly the ApoB/ApoA-I ratio, were associated with mortality in MHD patients.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included 409 MHD patients. Lipid and apolipoprotein-related markers were analyzed as standardized continuous variables. Cox regression was used for all-cause mortality, and cause-specific Cox and Fine–Gray models were used for cardiovascular mortality.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 55.7 months, 148 all-cause deaths occurred, including 61 cardiovascular deaths. ApoB/ApoA-I was not associated with all-cause mortality in the unadjusted model, but was independently associated with all-cause mortality in Model 3 (hazard ratio [HR] 1.23, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.05–1.44; p = 0.012). The association was similar in sensitivity analyses. ApoB/ApoA-I was not significantly associated with cardiovascular mortality in exploratory Fine–Gray regression (subdistribution hazard ratio [SHR] 1.12, 95% CI 0.89–1.43; p = 0.337) and did not improve discrimination beyond the base clinical model.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that ApoB/ApoA-I may contribute to risk characterization and etiologic understanding in MHD, but do not support its use as a stand-alone clinical prediction marker.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05224-1.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease accounts for approximately 40% of deaths among maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients. However, conventional lipid markers, including total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglycerides (TG), have shown limited and inconsistent associations with mortality in dialysis cohorts [1, 2]. This lipid paradox has been attributed to reverse epidemiology, in which inflammation, poor nutritional status, and protein-energy wasting may be linked to both lower lipid levels and higher mortality [2]. However, inverse lipid-mortality associations may persist even after adjustment for inflammation and malnutrition markers [3]. Large randomized trials of statin-based LDL-C lowering have shown limited cardiovascular benefit in dialysis or advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) populations [4–6], and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines do not recommend initiating statin or statin/ezetimibe therapy in most prevalent dialysis patients [7].

The ApoB/ApoA-I ratio reflects the balance between ApoB-containing atherogenic particles and ApoA-I-containing anti-atherogenic particles. In the general population, this ratio has been associated with cardiovascular risk and may outperform several cholesterol-based measures [8, 9]. Evidence in dialysis patients remains limited [10, 11]. Therefore, we evaluated whether selected lipid and apolipoprotein-related markers, including the ApoB/ApoA-I ratio, were associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in maintenance hemodialysis patients using sequentially adjusted survival models.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study in China. The inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years, maintenance hemodialysis for at least 3 months, and available baseline clinical, lipid, and apolipoprotein measurements between July and August 2017. The exclusion criteria were active infection within 1 month before baseline assessment or previous kidney transplantation. Of 451 patients screened, 38 were excluded because lipid profile results were unavailable, and four patients were excluded because other required laboratory data were incomplete. The final complete-case cohort therefore included 409 patients.

Clinical and laboratory measurements

Baseline demographic and clinical data were extracted from medical records. Routine lipid markers included TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C. Selected lipid and apolipoprotein-related markers included apolipoprotein A-II (ApoA-II), apolipoprotein C-II (ApoC-II), free fatty acids (FFA), lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], apolipoprotein B (ApoB), apolipoprotein A-I (ApoA-I), and the derived ApoB/ApoA-I ratio. ApoA-II, ApoC-II, and FFA were measured by immunoturbidimetric assay. Lp(a) was measured by latex-enhanced immunoturbidimetry. ApoB and ApoA-I were measured separately, and the ApoB/ApoA-I ratio was calculated from their concentrations. Additional laboratory measurements included serum creatinine, albumin, and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP). NT-proBNP was log-transformed for analysis because of its right-skewed distribution.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. The secondary outcome was cardiovascular mortality, defined as death attributed to myocardial infarction, heart failure, sudden cardiac death, stroke, or other vascular causes. Causes of death were assigned from medical records according to the physician’s clinical judgment at the time of outcome documentation. Formal blinded adjudication was not available; however, outcome classification was based on contemporaneous clinical information recorded during routine care, which helped limit retrospective classification bias. Patients were followed until death or censoring due to kidney transplantation, transfer to another dialysis center, loss to follow-up, or the administrative end of follow-up on April 30, 2022.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate associations between lipid markers and all-cause mortality. All continuous lipid markers were standardized, and hazard ratios were reported per SD increase. For consistency, regression analyses modeled lipid markers as continuous standardized variables. Median-based grouping was used only for graphical display in Kaplan–Meier and cumulative incidence curves. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, diabetes mellitus, and dialysis vintage; and Model 3 further adjusted for serum creatinine, albumin, and log-transformed NT-proBNP. Model 3 was used as the primary model for inference because it included demographic, clinical, cardiac, and nutrition-related covariates without simultaneous adjustment for highly collinear lipid variables.

A full lipid-adjusted model, with and without variance inflation factor (VIF)-reduced adjustment, statin adjustment, stratified Cox analysis, proportional hazards diagnostics, exploratory adjusted subgroup analyses, competing-risk analyses, and bootstrap C-index comparisons were performed as sensitivity or exploratory analyses. The full lipid-adjusted model was interpreted cautiously because of substantial multicollinearity. Exploratory subgroup analyses were fitted using adjusted Cox models aligned with Model 3, excluding the subgroup-defining variable from the adjustment set where appropriate. Interaction p values were obtained from adjusted multiplicative interaction models. For cardiovascular mortality, non-cardiovascular death was treated as a competing event. Cause-specific Cox regression and Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models were performed. Fine–Gray models were fitted using the survival package by expanding the data into start–stop intervals with the finegray function. The resulting inverse probability-of-censoring weights were applied in weighted Cox models, and robust sandwich standard errors clustered by patient identifier were used. This approach assumes independent censoring for construction of the Fine–Gray risk set. C-index comparisons used 1000 bootstrap resamples, and p values for C-index differences were calculated empirically from paired bootstrap distributions [12, 13]. All analyses were performed in R 4.5.0.

Sensitivity analyses and model diagnostics

Because simultaneous adjustment for TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C introduced substantial multicollinearity in the full lipid-adjusted model, Model 3 was retained as the primary multivariable-adjusted model for inference. The association was similar in a VIF-reduced lipid-adjusted sensitivity model including TG and HDL-C (HR 1.34, 95% CI 1.12–1.60; p = 0.001; maximum VIF = 1.8). No evidence of proportional hazards violation was observed for ApoB/ApoA-I. Additional adjustment for statin use did not materially change the primary estimate (HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.05–1.44; p = 0.012). Detailed sensitivity analyses and proportional hazards diagnostics are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Adding ApoB/ApoA-I did not improve discrimination beyond the base clinical model (C-index 0.7767 vs. 0.7755; delta −0.0012 (95% CI, −0.0046 to 0.0076); p = 0.992; Table 4). The exploratory full lipid model showed a higher C-index, but this model included multiple correlated lipid variables and was interpreted only as a completeness analysis rather than as evidence supporting routine prediction. Exploratory adjusted subgroup analyses did not show consistent evidence of effect modification and are presented in Supplementary Table S3. Discussion

In this cohort of maintenance hemodialysis patients, the ApoB/ApoA-I ratio was independently associated with all-cause mortality after adjustment for demographic, clinical, cardiac, and nutrition-related covariates. This association was not evident in the unadjusted model but became apparent after multivariable adjustment. This adjustment-dependent pattern should be interpreted cautiously. It may reflect the complex interrelationships among lipid metabolism, nutritional status, inflammation, cardiac stress, comorbidity burden, and overall illness severity, rather than a proven biological suppression effect.

One possible interpretation is that the ratio summarizes information from both ApoB and ApoA-I. However, the component analyses do not establish incremental predictive utility of the ratio beyond ApoB or ApoA-I alone. Thus, ApoB/ApoA-I may provide an integrated lipid-related summary, although this interpretation remains observational.

The absence of a significant association with cardiovascular mortality suggests that the all-cause mortality finding should not be interpreted as direct evidence of an atherosclerotic cardiovascular pathway. Instead, ApoB/ApoA-I may capture lipid-related information within a broader metabolic, nutritional, inflammatory, and illness-severity phenotype in maintenance hemodialysis. Because the association became apparent after adjustment for albumin, creatinine, NT-proBNP, and other clinical covariates, the ratio may provide information that is not fully represented by conventional nutrition-related indicators. However, this does not mean that ApoB/ApoA-I measures nutritional status, nor should it replace established nutritional, inflammatory, or body-composition assessment tools. Rather, ApoB/ApoA-I may complement these assessments by adding a lipid- and apolipoprotein-related perspective in a population where conventional lipid markers are often difficult to interpret.

Consistent with this cautious interpretation, ApoB/ApoA-I did not improve C-index discrimination beyond the base clinical model. Although the full lipid model showed numerically higher discrimination, its interpretation is limited by multicollinearity and by the exploratory nature of simultaneous lipid-marker adjustment. Overall, these findings indicate that statistical association does not necessarily translate into clinically meaningful incremental prediction. Therefore, the potential value of ApoB/ApoA-I may lie in etiologic understanding and contextual risk characterization rather than in routine clinical risk prediction.

Other lipid-related markers also showed attenuation or directional shifts after multivariable adjustment. ApoA-II, ApoC-II, and triglycerides were inversely associated with mortality in crude analyses, but these associations were attenuated after adjustment for clinical and nutrition-related covariates. These findings are consistent with the need for cautious interpretation of lipid biomarkers in dialysis patients, in whom crude lipid–mortality associations may be influenced by nutritional reserve, inflammation, and reverse epidemiology [14].

Future multicenter studies should validate these findings in larger cohorts and determine whether longitudinal ApoB/ApoA-I trajectories provide more clinically meaningful prognostic information than a single baseline measurement. Additional inflammatory biomarkers, formal nutritional assessments, body-composition measures, imaging data, and dialysis-related variables may help clarify whether ApoB/ApoA-I identifies a reproducible high-risk phenotype.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design limits generalizability, and residual confounding, selection bias, and lack of external validation cannot be excluded. Although the cohort reflects patients from a large Chinese dialysis center, its representativeness of broader Chinese maintenance hemodialysis populations cannot be assumed. Differences in referral patterns, dialysis practice, comorbidity burden, nutritional status, and laboratory measurement protocols may limit external validity. Second, cause-of-death classification was based on the responsible physician’s clinical judgment at the time of outcome documentation rather than formal blinded adjudication, so outcome misclassification bias cannot be fully ruled out. This limitation is particularly relevant to the cardiovascular mortality analyses, which included only 61 cardiovascular deaths and therefore had limited precision. Even modest outcome misclassification could have attenuated associations and may partly explain the neutral cardiovascular findings. Third, albumin and creatinine were used as indirect nutritional markers but may also reflect inflammation, hydration status, muscle mass, comorbidity burden, or frailty; inflammatory biomarkers and formal nutritional or body-composition assessments were unavailable. Fourth, cardiovascular mortality and subgroup analyses had limited event counts and should be interpreted as exploratory. Finally, lipoprotein markers were measured once at baseline, and serial measurements may provide additional information.

Conclusion

In this cohort of 409 MHD patients, ApoB/ApoA-I was independently associated with all-cause mortality after adjustment for demographic, clinical, and nutrition-related covariates. However, ApoB/ApoA-I was not significantly associated with cardiovascular mortality in exploratory competing-risk analysis and did not improve C-index discrimination beyond the base clinical model. These findings suggest that ApoB/ApoA-I may provide complementary lipid-related context for risk characterization in MHD, but they do not support its use as a stand-alone clinical prediction marker or as a replacement for established nutritional and inflammatory assessment.
