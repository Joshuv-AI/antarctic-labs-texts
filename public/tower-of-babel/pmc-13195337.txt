Transmission of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus from a dog to humans and a cat in a household cluster
Authors: Dongyu Ma, Wangjun Li, Tao Liao, Yongqing Song, Jun Ma, Hecheng Lu, Hanbing Liu, Wei Liu, Qi Zhang, Tingting Hu, Yaping Zhu, Wei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The growing proximity and contact between companion animals and humans underscore the significant public health risk of zoonotic spillover, a core concern of the One Health framework. Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS), an emerging tick-borne zoonosis of increasing incidence, is capable of direct animal-to-human transmission; however, evidence detailing its transmission within households remains limited. This study documents a transmission chain involving a domestic dog, two owners, and a cohabiting cat. The owners developed symptoms approximately one week after exposure to their symptomatic dog, and the cat subsequently succumbed two weeks after the dog's death. High viral loads of SFTS virus (SFTSV) were consistently detected in samples from the patients and deceased animals. The S-fragment sequences from the patients showed 100% identity with those from the pets, confirming a common source. Phylogenetic analysis classified the virus as genotype D, and SFTSV was also detected in the shared household environment, indicating its role as a transmission hub. These findings provide clear evidence of multi-directional household transmission, demonstrating that companion animals can mediate cross-species transmission to humans and other susceptible species. Transmission likely occurred via infectious secretions, fomites, or potentially aerosols. In line with the One Health concept, our results emphasize the urgent need to implement enhanced surveillance of companion animals in SFTS-endemic regions and to establish integrated systems that disrupt transmission cycles among ticks, animals, and humans, thereby safeguarding public and animal health in tandem.

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS), caused by the novel bunyavirus called SFTS virus (SFTSV), is an emerging acute infectious disease affecting humans and animals, clinically characterized by fever, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia [1]. Severe cases progress rapidly with fatality rates reaching 35% [2]. The disease has been attracting increasing global attention due to its expanding geographical distribution and rising incidence worldwide [3], [4]. SFTSV, a member of the genus Bandavirus (family Phenuiviridae, order Bunyavirales) [5], is a negative-sense, single-stranded RNA virus with a tri-segmented genome (designated L, M, and S) [1]. Six genotypes (A-F) have been identified, with genotypes F, A, and D predominating in China [6]. The natural transmission cycle of SFTSV involves two primary routes: the tick-borne tick cycle and the tick-borne mammal cycle [7], [8]. Multiple tick species act as both reservoir hosts and vectors, among which Haemaphysalis longicornis (H. longicornis) demonstrates the highest vector competence for zoonotic transmission [9]. Notably, a 2025 study reported the first molecular evidence of SFTSV in deer keds from wild ungulates in South Korea, thereby expanding our knowledge of its potential vectors [10].

The life cycle of SFTSV involves not only ticks but also wild and domestic animals. These animals can serve as amplification hosts for the virus, which poses direct and indirect transmission risks to humans [11]. Previous epidemiological investigations show that SFTSV can be detected in various animals, mainly including domestic animals (dogs, cats, goats, sheep, cattle, pigs, etc.) and wild animals (rodents, hedgehogs, deer, wild boars, weasels, and birds, etc.) [12], [13], [14], [15]. Recent investigations suggest that camels may serve as efficient intermediate hosts for human infection [16]. Notably, companion animals (domestic dogs and cats) that maintain close contact with humans exhibit higher seroprevalence rates [17], [18], [19], thereby increasing the potential for intrahousehold SFTSV transmission. SFTSV is not transmitted exclusively through tick vectors. Human-to-human transmission has long been documented [20]. Importantly, accumulating evidence indicates that infected companion animals—particularly cats and dogs—can also directly transmit the virus to humans. For example, case reports have described veterinarians and pet owners acquiring SFTS via close contact with diseased cats and dogs [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]. Collectively, these findings highlight the complexity of SFTSV transmission routes and have sparked extensive debate regarding the pivotal role of companion animals in SFTSV cross-species transmission, underscoring their potential importance in viral dissemination dynamics.

In this study, we report two human cases of SFTSV infection and an infected domestic cat, with epidemiological and genomic evidence confirming that transmission originated from an infected dog. Through integrated epidemiological investigations combined with whole-genome sequencing and phylogenetic analyses, we characterized the cross-species transmission dynamics among these hosts. The results provide compelling evidence that companion animals carrying SFTSV can serve as significant infection sources, facilitating multi-directional transmission among family members, from animals to humans, and between animals within households.

Materials and methods

Case identification and epidemiological investigation

On 1 April 2025, two patients were admitted to Hospital B with intermittent fever. Diagnosis of SFTSV infection was confirmed through reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) and clinical evaluation. We retrospectively investigated confirmed cases and their household contacts, collecting data on demographics, medical histories, living environments, animal exposures, and tick bites. Serum samples were obtained from 32 healthy residents of Village A, where the patients resided, and were tested for SFTSV antibodies. Ticks were collected from two sources in Village A: environmental sampling via flag-dragging in residential and livestock areas and parasitic ticks harvested from domestic animals.

SFTSV RNA detection by RT-PCR

The samples for detection included blood samples from patients with SFTS, tissue and blood samples from companion animals, environmental samples, and collected tick samples. Total RNA was extracted from all samples using the GeneRotex 96 Automated Nucleic Acid Extraction System (Xi'an Tianlong Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. SFTSV RNA was detected using the SFTSV RT-PCR Detection Kit (Catalog No. 202411245, Beijing Zhuocheng HuiSheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum samples were obtained from 32 healthy participants residing in the same village (Village A) as the patients. Following collection, all samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to separate serum components. SFTSV-specific antibody levels were quantified using a commercial human SFTSV IgG/IgM ELISA kit (Catalog No. 20250408, Tianjin Saier Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using a DNM-9606 Microplate Reader (Beijing Pulang New Technology Co., Ltd., China).

Gene sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

The complete genome of SFTSV from nucleic acid-positive samples was sequenced and analyzed using second-generation sequencing technology. Reverse transcription amplification and library preparation were performed using the Berger New Bunyavirus Genome Capture Kit (Cat. No. SJ-CX-616-2) and the Multiplex DNA Library Prep Kit (Cat. No. 940–00017-00). Sequencing was performed on the MGI Tech FCS chip (Cat. No. 1000020207). The raw sequencing data were assembled into complete genome sequences using MGAP software (v2.0) on the MGI platform. For phylogenetic analysis, six SFTSV reference sequences representing different genotypes (A-F) were retrieved from GenBank. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT v7.0.26, and a maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed with IQ-TREE v2.3.6, with the best-fit substitution model automatically selected by ModelFinder. Branch support was assessed using the ultrafast bootstrap approximation method with 1000 replicates.

Results

Case presentation

Patients

Patient 1, a 48-year-old female power plant worker residing with her husband (Patient 2) in Village A, Anhui Province, initially presented with anorexia, fatigue, and dizziness around March 20, 2025, which she initially disregarded. On March 30, she presented to the emergency department of Hospital B (Northern Campus) with one week of poor appetite and intermittent chills/fever. Laboratory tests revealed leukopenia and thrombocytopenia, with decreased white blood cell (WBC) count of 1.7 × 109/L and platelet (PLT) counts of 56 × 109/L, prompting leukocyte-stimulating therapy. On March 31, due to persistent fever, the patient visited the fever clinic of Hospital B (main campus), where she was diagnosed with fever, hepatic dysfunction, and worsening thrombocytopenia. By April 1 (Day 12 of illness), her condition deteriorated, leading to re-evaluation at the emergency department, Hospital B (main campus). SFTS was clinically suspected and subsequently confirmed by RT-PCR testing of blood samples (Ct value: 27.10). The patient was immediately transferred to the infectious disease unit for isolation and targeted treatment.

Patient 2, a 62-year-old retired male and the spouse of patient 1 developed intermittent high-grade fever (peak: 40.1 °C), fatigue, dizziness, and anorexia beginning March 23, 2025. Despite symptom persistence, he delayed medical attention until March 30, when he presented to the emergency department of hospital B (Northern Branch) with a one-week history of fever. Initial laboratory analysis demonstrated leukocytopenia (WBC: 2.21 × 109/L) and thrombocytopenia (PLT: 73 × 109/L). On March 31, due to inadequate response to antipyretic treatment, Patient 2 presented to the fever clinic at hospital B (main campus), where he was diagnosed with persistent fever and leukopenia. By April 1 (day 9 of illness), his clinical deterioration prompted re-evaluation at the emergency department of hospital B (main campus). The clinical suspicion of SFTS was subsequently validated through RT-PCR detection of SFTSV RNA in blood samples (Ct value: 25.11), and he was admitted to the infectious disease unit for specialized management.

Companion animals

The household of two patients kept three cats and one dog. Around March 15, the dog developed anorexia, vomiting, and abnormal urination/defecation. On March 18, the owners presented the dog to a veterinary clinic for evaluation (Table 2). Based on clinical signs, imaging findings, and elevated lipase/amylase levels, the dog was diagnosed with pancreatitis and chronic renal failure. After fluid therapy, it was discharged but deteriorated and died at home on March 19. The owners and their son buried the dog along with its bedding in their vegetable garden. Follow-up surveillance of close contacts and companion animals revealed that one of the three domestic cats in the affected household developed clinical symptoms around April 3 and died on April 5, presenting with nasal and oral hemorrhage upon death. The carcass was buried in the same vegetable garden. The remaining two cats exhibited no clinical signs throughout the observation period. Epidemiological investigation

Both patients reported stable daily routines. Patient 2, a retiree, primarily engaged in gardening and tending to a home vegetable plot, while Patient 1 assisted after work. Neither patient reported any travel history or contact with febrile individuals or wild animals within the 14 days preceding symptom onset. Physical examinations upon hospital admission revealed no definitive evidence of tick bites. It is worth noting that both patients reported multiple unprotected close-contact exposures to the symptomatic domestic dog, including daily feeding, direct contact during play, and handling of the dog's vomit. Moreover, Patient 2 participated in the unprotected burial of the deceased dog, posing a postmortem exposure risk.

The domestic dog in the household exhibited a wide activity range, with unrestricted movement between indoor (living room) and outdoor areas (grassy yard and vegetable garden). It consistently retreated nightly to a storage room adjoining the bedroom and was occasionally walked on-leash within the village perimeter, during which no contact with other canines was documented. In contrast, the three cats had restricted movement, strictly confined to the living room yet showing spatial overlap with the dog's activity patterns. All cats had prolonged cohabitation with both patients until hospitalization, exhibiting frequent physical contact. The SFTSV-infected cat had a documented history of feline panleukopenia and feline infectious peritonitis comorbidity, while the other two cats remained clinically healthy. No animals had received antiparasitic prophylaxis in the preceding three months.

Environmental, vector, and host investigation

The two patients resided in a rural village of approximately 300 permanent residents, characterized by ponds, vegetated slopes, and mixed farmland-grassland terrain. Their single-story house comprised a living room, two bedrooms, and a utility room. We observed poor ventilation due to misaligned doors and sealed front windows, which impaired air circulation. The property had a private courtyard with grass, vegetable gardens, small plots, and a pond, though some pathways were overgrown with weeds. To characterize the natural foci of SFTS in the local area, we conducted a systematic epidemiological investigation in the patient's residential area, focusing on SFTSV infection status in vector ticks, animal hosts, and healthy human populations. A total of 168 questing H. longicornis ticks were collected from four monitoring sites in the village using the flagging method. The mean village tick density was 12.74 per 100m2, with no ticks detected in the patients' residential area. The deceased dog and cat underwent postmortem examination at 14 days and 2 days after death, respectively, and no tick infestation was observed. In contrast, screening of dogs from other households in Village A detected 18 parasitic ticks, with morphological identification revealing 17 Haemaphysalis flava and 1H. longicornis. Additionally, venous blood samples were collected from 32 healthy village residents and four domestic dogs for SFTSV infection status evaluation.

Laboratory confirmation and genomic analysis

We systematically collected samples from exhumed dog and cat carcasses in the patient's courtyard, obtaining multiple biological specimens (including liver tissue samples, cardiac tissue samples, and blood samples), along with three environmental samples from animal living areas (desktop surfaces, door handles, and cat bedding swabs). RT-PCR analysis detected high viral loads of SFTSV RNA in all animal specimens, comprising three canine blood swabs and two liver tissue samples from the domestic dog, as well as two feline blood swabs, one cardiac tissue sample and one liver tissue sample from the cat. SFTSV RNA was also detected in all environmental samples, indicating not only active animal infections but also environmental viral contamination. All tick specimens (n = 186) were negative for SFTSV nucleic acid. Serum analysis showed no detectable SFTSV nucleic acid in the other four domestic dogs from the same village under investigation. All 32 serum samples from healthy individuals were negative for SFTSV RNA, while three cases were positive for both SFTSV-specific IgM and IgG antibodies.

We successfully obtained complete genome sequences from two patients and deceased cat/dog cases revealed >99.8% pairwise nucleotide identity among all strains, with complete conservation (100%) in S segments, confirming intra-cluster transmission. Strikingly, the dog-origin strain showed 99.7% M-segment identity to human/cat isolates (vs. >99.9% in human-cat pairs), suggesting host-specific divergence. The L segment of the current isolates showed 99.4% similarity to 20-china_Henan-77 from Henan Province (OM453534.1), while the M segment exhibited 99.3% similarity to reference OM452855, and the S segment displayed 99.4% similarity to Henan prototype OM452156.1. Discussion

Although SFTS is defined as a tick-borne zoonosis, only 21% of patients in China—the country bearing the highest SFTS burden-report definitive tick exposure histories [26]. This low proportion likely underestimates the true extent of tick exposure, as bites from nymphal ticks frequently go unnoticed due to their minute size, painless nature and the absence of immediate symptoms, leading to poor patient recall [27], [28], [29]. However, even accounting for this underestimation, a proportion of cases remain unexplained by tick exposure alone. Accumulating evidence indicates the existence of tick-independent transmission routes for SFTSV, including animal-to-human, animal-to-animal, and human-to-human spread [30]. Workers on fur farms in Weihai, experienced direct transmission of SFTSV from farm-raised animals [31]; furthermore, outbreaks on arctic fox farms underscore significant intra-species transmission risks [32]. It is noteworthy that most previously documented household clusters originated from human-to-human transmission, primarily through direct contact with infected patients' blood/bodily fluids, with aerosol transmission representing a potential secondary route [33], [34], [35]. In contrast, clusters triggered by companion animals remain rarely reported. Of particular significance, while cats and dogs have been extensively implicated as sources of human infection in Japan and South Korea [23], [25], [36], comparable evidence from China remains limited—and the role of domestic dogs as transmission sources has not been laboratory-confirmed. In the present study, we have documented the laboratory-confirmed household transmission chain of SFTSV, involving sequential spread from an infected domestic dog to human cohabitants and a contact cat. Epidemiological investigation identified the index case as a domestic dog that died after symptom onset. Two owners developed symptoms after close contact, followed by a fatal infection in a cohabiting cat. Whole-genome alignment demonstrated 100% nucleotide identity across the complete S segment among all isolates, which molecularly confirms this transmission pathway. This study confirms that domestic dogs can serve as a source of SFTSV infection and reveals the new dynamic of multi-directional transmission from an infected dog to both humans and cats within households. The absence of tick exposure in secondary cases strongly suggests contact rather than vector-borne transmission as the predominant route in this cluster.

Previous studies have detected SFTSV RNA in infected dogs [37]. While most SFTSV-positive dogs remain asymptomatic, acute clinical manifestations such as fever, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and gastrointestinal symptoms have been reported [38]. Natural fatal cases of SFTSV infection in dogs have been documented in Japan [39]. Interestingly, viral loads in dog urine exceed those in blood or feces by orders of magnitude and may persist for over two months [40], suggesting prolonged shedding potential. In the present study, the index dog exhibited typical clinical signs and died within five days of onset. High viral loads of SFTSV were still detected in blood and tissue samples collected from the canine carcass two weeks postmortem, demonstrating its strong infectious potential and probable role as a transmission source. Intriguingly, the dog was concurrently diagnosed with pancreatitis—a condition linked to elevated amylase/lipase levels in SFTSV-infected canines [41] and rarely observed in human SFTS cases [42]. These findings underscore the need for diagnostic screening for SFTSV in endemic areas when dogs with pancreatitis present with thrombocytopenia or an outdoor exposure history. Epidemiological investigation revealed that the domestic dog had not received any tick prevention treatment, despite the village's high tick density and the dog's history of roaming within the community. Although no ticks were found on the deceased dog or in the household courtyard—potentially due to postmortem tick detachment and regular yard maintenance before illness onset—multiple studies have nonetheless identified outdoor activity with tick exposure as a significant risk factor for canine SFTSV infection [39], [40]. Furthermore, a confirmed SFTS case had been reported in the same village the previous year, involving a neighbor of the current case. Given the absence of other symptomatic dogs or cats in the village during the recent surveillance period, we conclude that tick-borne transmission represents the most probable route of SFTSV infection in this canine case.

Multiple transmission routes of SFTSV from animals to humans have been identified, extending beyond classical tick-borne pathways. A Japanese cohort study found that 48% of SFTSV patients reported close contact with domestic pets (including exposure to bodily fluids or saliva) without documented tick bites [43]. Additional routes include animal scratches/bites and potential aerosol transmission [24], [44], [45]. Importantly, the incubation period varies by transmission route: as short as 2 days after feline scratches/bites [24], [45], approximately 10 days following blood/body fluid exposure [22], and potentially extending to 2 weeks in suspected aerosol transmission [44]. In our investigation, two secondary human cases developed symptoms 6 and 9 days after the onset of illness in the dog index case, with both patients having provided intensive care to the infected dog. Several lines of evidence collectively support canine-to-human transmission via infectious secretions: (1) the 100% similarity of the S fragment sequences between human and canine isolates; (2) Neither patient reported tick bites and physical examinations ruled out tick-attachment signs; (3) The dog exhibited vomiting and died with unidentified secretions in its kennel; both patients participated in its care; and (4) No contact with other symptomatic individuals prior to illness onset,and the 3-day interval between the two cases precludes the possibility of human-to-human transmission. The observed 6–9-day incubation period aligns with established timelines for fluid exposure. These findings provide compelling evidence for direct dog-to-human SFTSV transmission in a household setting, most likely through contact with the dog's vomit or other secretions. From a One Health perspective, we propose a triad of integrated interventions: first, protecting humans through guided use of personal protective equipment during animal care; second, establishing early warning via enhanced veterinary surveillance of SFTSV in canines; and third, breaking the transmission cycle through rigorous decontamination of shared environments. This integrated approach is critical for building resilient defenses at the human-animal-environment interface—the central focus of One Health.

As companion animals cohabiting with humans, domestic cats exhibit heightened epidemiological significance in SFTSV transmission. Feline infections often result in more severe clinical outcomes and higher mortality rates compared to canine cases [46]. Recent epidemiological studies have confirmed that close contact with infected cats, exposure to their blood, or scratches by infected cats can transmit SFTSV to humans [22], [23], [24]. Furthermore, infected cat urine has been demonstrated as a potential transmission vehicle [47]. Molecular evidence from a Japanese veterinary hospital confirmed nosocomial transmission, where two cats acquired SFTSV through contact with a pulse oximeter probe contaminated by an infected dog [41]. In this household cluster of SFTSV infections, a cat cohabitating with an infected dog developed a fatal SFTSV infection following the canine's death—with high viral loads detected in its blood and tissues. We propose that interspecies transmission likely occurred through direct or indirect contact with infected canine secretions, or prolonged exposure to a contaminated household environment. This hypothesis is supported by three key lines of evidence: (1) Whole-genome sequencing revealed >99.8% nucleotide identity among viral strains isolated from the dog, patient, and cat, with 100% conservation in the S segment. (2) The cat, which was strictly indoors and showed no evidence of tick bites, shared substantial activity overlap with both the patient and the infected dog. (3) SFTSV RNA was detected on multiple household surfaces (including cat bedding, furniture and electronic devices), indicating prolonged environmental persistence of the virus. It is noteworthy that the cat developed symptoms two weeks after the dog's death. This delayed onset, combined with poor ventilation in the household, raises the possibility of aerosol transmission.These findings underscore the necessity of integrating feline surveillance into existing public health frameworks for effective outbreak control. First, investigations of household clusters should account for the potential of cross-species transmission. Second, cohabiting animals should undergo extended monitoring (for at least two weeks) after an index case is identified. Finally, households with multiple pets must implement strict environmental decontamination following a confirmed infection.

By demonstrating that domestic dogs sustain SFTSV infections and transmit the virus to humans and cohabiting cats, our study confirms multi-directional cross-species transmission in household settings, thereby establishing companion animals as critical reservoirs and transmission sources within these ecosystems. Future research should focus on quantifying transmission risks and developing targeted interventions to disrupt these newly identified transmission chains. Collectively, our findings significantly advance the understanding of SFTSV transmission dynamics in household environments and highlight the necessity of integrating companion animals surveillance into One Health-aligned public health frameworks to enhance outbreak monitoring and control.
