Nasogastric vs. nasojejunal feeding in stroke patients under a DRG payment system: balancing clinical benefits and economic costs
Authors: Jing Mei, Jiyu Chen, Fei Xie, Yang Xu, Jia Li, Yi He, Bin Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnder the DRG payment system, the choice of enteral nutrition for stroke patients requires balancing clinical efficacy and economic costs. Nasogastric (NG) and nasojejunal (NJ) feeding are widely used, but comprehensive comparisons of nutritional outcomes, complications, and DRG‑related economic indicators remain limited.ObjectiveTo compare the clinical effects and economic burden of NG versus NJ enteral nutrition in stroke patients under DRG, and to provide evidence for individualized clinical decision‑making.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted in stroke patients receiving enteral nutrition at a tertiary hospital in central China from January 2021 to December 2024. A total of 322 patients were initially enrolled (193 in NG group, 129 in NJ group), and 149 patients were eligible for final DRG economic analysis (83 in NG group, 66 in NJ group). To confirm model robustness, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was performed (n = 66 pairs). Nutritional indicators, complications, length of stay, hospitalization costs, nutritional costs, and DRG cost overrun rates were compared.ResultsThe NJ group had lower pretreatment hemoglobin, but showed significantly greater improvements in hemoglobin and albumin than the NG group (both P < 0.001). The incidence of malnutrition and inflammatory burden index were significantly lower in the NJ group (both P < 0.05). In DRG analysis, total hospitalization costs and nutritional costs were significantly higher in the NJ group (both P < 0.001), with a higher DRG overrun rate (77.3% vs. 62.7%, P = 0.055). Length of stay was similar between groups (P > 0.05).ConclusionNJ is superior to NG in improving nutritional status and reducing malnutrition and inflammatory burden in stroke patients, but is associated with higher short‑term costs and greater DRG overrun risk. A risk‑stratified strategy is recommended under DRG to balance clinical benefits and economic sustainability.Keywords: DRG, Nasogastric feeding, Nasojejunal feeding, Stroke

Background

In recent years, the global healthcare system has undergone significant reforms in payment models aimed at curbing rising healthcare expenditures and enhancing the efficiency of health resource allocation. As a cornerstone reform strategy, the Diagnosis-Related Groups (DRG)–based payment system has been widely adopted across high-income and middle-income countries, and China initiated nationwide DRG pilot programs in 2019—followed by phased implementation and scale-up beginning in 2021 [1]. Previous studies conducted in China have demonstrated that DRG payment can effectively reduce hospitalization costs for both providers and patients while promoting standardized clinical management and improving operational efficiency [2, 3]. The DRG payment system establishes prospective, case-weighted reimbursement rates for clinically homogeneous disease groups—defined by principal diagnosis, major procedures, comorbidities, and resource utilization patterns—thereby incentivizing hospitals to standardize clinical pathways, enhance cost efficiency, and minimize preventable complications [4, 5].This payment model holds significant implications for managing high-resource-demand diseases, with stroke being a prime example—characterized by high incidence rates, prolonged hospital stays, frequent complications, and substantial rehabilitation needs. Globally, stroke remains the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability, with a 70.0% increase in incident cases between 1990 and 2019 [6, 7]. In China, stroke patients account for a considerable proportion of inpatient expenditures, making it a key population for cost control and efficiency improvement under the DRG payment system. Malnutrition is a common complication in hospitalized stroke patients, with reported rates ranging from 6.1% to 62%, depending on assessment methods and patient characteristics [8]. Stroke-related dysphagia, affecting approximately 25%–75% of patients, further increases the risk of malnutrition and aspiration pneumonia [9, 10]. These nutrition-related complications are closely associated with longer hospital stays, increased nursing workload, higher treatment costs, and worse functional outcomes [11, 12], which makes enteral nutrition intervention a crucial component of clinical management and inpatient resource allocation for stroke patients.

Enteral nutrition (EN) is therefore not only a clinical intervention but also a critical component of inpatient resource allocation. Early enteral nutrition has been shown to reduce mortality and stroke-associated pneumonia, potentially lowering downstream healthcare costs [13–15].In clinical practice, enteral nutrition in stroke patients is primarily delivered via nasogastric (NG) or nasojejunal (NJ) tubes. NG feeding is widely used in clinical practice due to its simple operation and low upfront cost, while NJ feeding can bypass gastric retention, reduce the risk of aspiration and improve the efficiency of nutrient delivery, showing potential advantages in the nutritional support of high-risk stroke patients [16, 17]. However, NJ feeding involves greater operational complexity, longer nursing time commitments, and higher initial medical resource consumption, potentially leading to differences in hospitalization costs between the two feeding methods. Under the DRG payment system, the choice of enteral nutrition route is not only a clinical decision but also an economic decision involving hospital cost control and patient out-of-pocket expenses. Balancing clinical benefits with economic costs has become a critical issue for both clinical practitioners and healthcare administrators [18].

Although existing studies have compared the clinical efficacy of nasogastric tube feeding versus nasojejunal tube feeding, most research has focused on short-term in-hospital clinical endpoints such as nutritional indicators and complication rates. There remains a lack of comprehensive evaluations integrating clinical outcomes, nursing resource utilization, and DRG-related economic metrics [19, 20]. Moreover, economic analyses of enteral nutrition pathways remain limited to describing total hospitalization costs, lacking in-depth examination of cost composition, cost-effectiveness, and reasons for DRG cost overruns. This hinders the provision of sufficient evidence-based support for individualized clinical decision-making and optimization of DRG payment policies [21]. Evidently, the economic consequences of selecting enteral nutrition routes under the DRG payment model remain under-explored.

Against this backdrop, conducting comparative research on the clinical efficacy and health economics of nasogastric (NG) versus nasojejunal (NJ) tube feeding for stroke patients under the DRG payment system holds significant theoretical and practical implications. Integrating the research context with clinical practice, the core study subjects are defined as follows: Enteral nutrition via NG/NJ tubes for stroke patients under the DRG payment system refers to nutritional support interventions administered through nasogastric or nasojejunal tubes to stroke patients covered by DRG reimbursement. This aims to improve nutritional status, reduce complications, and balance clinical treatment outcomes within the constraints of fixed DRG payment costs. This retrospective cohort analysis utilizes electronic medical records from inpatients with stroke at a tertiary hospital in central China. It systematically compares baseline characteristics, nutrition-related clinical indicators, and DRG-related economic metrics between NG and NJ groups. Specifically, this study contributes as follows:

It represents the first domestic research on NG/NJ enteral nutrition under the DRG payment system, elucidating differences between NG and NJ feeding methods in improving key nutritional indicators and stroke patient malnutrition incidence.

It represents the first domestic research on NG/NJ enteral nutrition under the DRG payment system, elucidating differences between NG and NJ feeding methods in improving key nutritional indicators and stroke patient malnutrition incidence.

It employs multivariate analysis to evaluate the impact of different nutritional support interventions on clinical outcomes, revealing the positive role of nutritional intervention in improving nutritional status and reducing complication rates.

Methods

Study design

This study was a single-center, retrospective cohort study conducted among hospitalized stroke patients receiving enteral nutrition under the Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) payment system. Data were extracted from the electronic medical records of neurology inpatients at a tertiary hospital in central China between January 2021 and December 2024. Patients were categorized into the nasogastric (NG) group or the nasojejunal (NJ) group according to the primary enteral nutrition route used during hospitalization. Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data, informed consent was waived.

Patients

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) admitted to the neurology department with a confirmed diagnosis of stroke who scored Grade III or higher on the Watanabe Drinking Test, had an NRS-2002 score ≥ 3, and required enteral nutrition support were eligible for inclusion. Patients were stratified based on the enteral nutrition method received (NG or NJ feeding). Only patients with complete clinical, nursing, and cost-related data were included. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-stroke diagnoses confirmed by imaging or clinical records; (2) Patients not covered by the DRG reimbursement system; (3) History of gastric/intestinal resection or receiving total parenteral nutrition; (4) Incomplete or missing key clinical or economic data. Using DRG overpayment rate as the primary outcome, with α = 0.05, β = 0.2, and an effect size OR = 0.45, the calculation indicates a minimum sample size of 120 cases is required. A total of 322 participants were enrolled, of whom 149 were included in the DRG analysis (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart

Data were independently extracted by two trained researchers using standardized data collection forms to minimize information bias. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus. All data were anonymized prior to analysis. Collected variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex, education level, marital status, insurance type), clinical characteristics (comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes), nutritional risk assessed by the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002), with scores less than 3 indicating good nutritional status and scores greater than or equal to 3 indicating nutritional risk that may require nutritional support. Swallowing function was evaluated using the Kubota Water Swallowing Test, graded into three levels: level one as normal, level two as suspicious abnormal, and levels three to five as abnormal. Additionally, DRG-related information included DRG group classification and cost thresholds.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcomes focused on healthcare efficiency under the DRG payment system, including: 1)Length of hospital stay (LOS); 2)Total hospitalization costs; 3)Rate of DRG cost overruns. Secondary outcomes included nutrition- and nursing-related clinical outcomes, comprising: 1)Nutrition-related complications, including aspiration pneumonia, diarrhea, tube blockage, and feeding intolerance; 2)Inflammatory Burden Index (IBI); 3)out-of-pocket expenses and nutrition-specific costs; 4)Hemoglobin and serum albumin levels prior to discharge.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) according to distribution, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were conducted using independent samples t-tests, Kruskal-Wallis tests, and chi-square tests as appropriate. Given the limited sample size, propensity score matching (PSM) was not applied to the overall cohort for nutritional outcome comparisons. To account for potential baseline confounding in economic outcomes, PSM (1:1 ratio, caliper = 0.05) was performed exclusively for cost and DRG-related analyses, adjusting for age, sex, consciousness status, nutritional risk (NRS-2002), swallowing function, and comorbidities. Post-matching balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD). Missing data (< 5%) were handled via multiple imputation.

Results

A total of 322 patients requiring enteral nutrition were initially included, with 193 cases in the nasogastric (NG) group and 129 cases in the nasojejunal (NJ) group. Additionally, 173 patients were excluded from DRG calculations. Among the remaining 149 patients, there were 83 cases in the NG group and 66 in the NJ group.

Baseline characteristics

The baseline demographic, clinical, and socioeconomic characteristics of the two groups were partially similar, but there was a significant disparity in consciousness status, with 62.69% (121) of NG group patients being clear-headed compared to only 29.46% (38) in the NJ group (P < 0.0001). In the assessment of malnutrition risk, 29.53% (57) of the NG group were classified as low-risk, while 70.47% (136) were considered high-risk. In contrast, the NJ group had only 8.53% (11) in the low-risk category and a striking 91.47% (118) in the high-risk category, indicating significant differences between the groups (P < 0.001). Comparison of nutrition-related indicators

A statistical analysis of nutritional indicators in 322 enrolled patients revealed no significant differences in hemoglobin and albumin levels at admission between those receiving NG tube feeding and those receiving NJ tube feeding (Table 2). At admission, the hemoglobin level in the NG group was129.09 ± 22.41 g/L, while in the NJ group it was132.15 ± 21.67 g/L(t = 1.485, P = 0.224). The results suggest that although there are differences in consciousness status and malnutrition risk between the two groups, the baseline nutritional status is balanced, and the interference of baseline nutritional differences on the study results can be excluded. However, prior to treatment, the NJ group had significantly lower levels than the NG group(104.38 ± 29.37 g/L vs. 116.5 ± 22.94 g/L; t= -17.190, P < 0.001), suggesting that the NJ group may have a higher baseline risk of anemia. After treatment, the levels were similar between the two groups(NG group 124.24 ± 19.53 g/L vs. NJ group 123.21 ± 22.12 g/L; t= -0.191, P = 0.663), indicating that both nutritional methods effectively improved hemoglobin levels, whereas the NJ group recovered to a comparable level from a lower baseline.

Similar, there were no significant differences in albumin levels at admission, or after treatment between the two groups (Table 3), suggesting that both enteral nutrition methods maintained albumin levels similarly, with no statistical differences between the groups. Significant differences were observed in the changes in nutritional status and the incidence of complications between the two groups (Table 4). The change in hemoglobin (post-treatment - pre-treatment) was significantly higher in the NJ group than in the NG group (18.84 ± 20.3 g/L vs. 7.74 ± 13.44 g/L, t = 34.840, P < 0.001), indicating a more pronounced improvement in anemia in the NJ group. The change in albumin was slightly higher in the NJ group (2.89 ± 3.19 g/L vs. 1.91 ± 3.12 g/L, t = 7.49, P < 0.001), statistical significance.

Changes in inflammatory markers showed statistically significant differences between groups (NG group 218.22 ± 437.91 vs. NJ group 115.45 ± 142.95; t= -6.626, P = 0.011). In categorical indicators, the incidence of malnutrition was significantly lower in the NJ group compared to the NG group (17.05% vs. 26.94%; χ²=1.791, P = 0.043), suggesting that the NJ method is more effective in preventing malnutrition. However, there were no significant differences in the incidence of hypoalbuminemia (24.35% vs. 21.71%; χ²=1.161, P = 0.686) and anemia (6.74% vs. 3.10%; χ²=2.252, P = 0.205) between the NG and NJ groups. Comparison of DRG-related indicators

Only 149 patients met the inclusion criteria for the cost statistics under DRG payment. Combining the baseline DRG payment category data(Table 1), in NG group, there were 110 cases (56.99%) of non-DRG payment, 31 cases (16.06%) of normal balance, 36 cases (18.65%) of normal overspending, and 16 cases (8.29%) of abnormal high amount. In NJ group, there were 63 cases of non-DRG payment (48.84%), 15 cases of normal balance (11.63%), 31 cases of normal overspending (24.03%), and 20 cases of abnormal high amount (15.50%). There was no statistically significant difference in DRG payment categories between the two groups (P = 0.0823). The two groups of patients included in the cost analysis were ensured to be comparable in terms of fee-related baseline characteristics.

Under the DRG payment system, significant differences in economic indicators were observed between the two groups (Table 5). Total hospitalization costs were significantly higher in the NJ group than in the NG group (81527.36 ± 67047.42 CNY vs. 40877.87 ± 41648.00 CNY; t = 20.560, P < 0.001), and total nutritional costs also reflected this trend (2879.29 ± 1868.47 CNY vs. 1207.66 ± 830.38 CNY; t = 53.280, P < 0.001),indicating that the materials and nursing costs associated with nasojejunal feeding are higher. The incidence of DRG excess costs was greater in the NJ group (77.3% vs. 62.7%; χ²= 3.683, P = 0.055), approaching significance, which may be related to the higher baseline risks in this group. There were no significant differences in the length of hospital stay (NG group 10.54 ± 5.72 days vs. NJ group 10.73 ± 5.57 days; t = 0.087, P = 0.768), suggesting that both methods demonstrate comparable efficiency within the expected length of stay (LOS) under the DRG system.

Discussion

This study is a retrospective analysis based on the DRG payment system, comparing the clinical outcomes and economic differences between nasogastric (NG) and nasojejunal (NJ) enteral nutrition support in stroke patients. After balancing baseline characteristics via propensity score matching, the results showed that the NJ group demonstrated superior outcomes in improving nutritional indicators, reducing the incidence of malnutrition, and alleviating the inflammatory burden. However, this was accompanied by higher nutrition-related costs, patient out-of-pocket expenses, and a higher risk of DRG cost overruns (differences approaching statistical significance). There was no statistically significant difference in length of hospital stay between the two groups. These findings suggest that, under DRG cost-control constraints, the selection of enteral nutrition pathways requires a precise balance between clinical benefits and economic costs, thereby providing a basis for standardized and individualized decision-making regarding nutritional support for stroke patients.

Clinical implications of nutritional outcomes

The results of this study indicate that NJ feeding leads to a more significant improvement in nutritional status among stroke patients compared to NG feeding. There were no significant differences in baseline hemoglobin and albumin levels between the two groups at admission; however, the NJ group had lower hemoglobin levels, higher nutritional risk, and more severe impaired consciousness prior to treatment. Following intervention, the increase in hemoglobin levels was significantly greater in the NJ group than in the NG group (t = 34.840, P = 0.000), indicating a significant advantage of NJ feeding in terms of nutritional improvement. This result is consistent with previous studies, which indicate that NJ feeding bypasses gastric stasis, thereby delivering nutrients more effectively, reducing the risk of gastric stasis and aspiration, ensuring continuous nutrient infusion and absorption efficiency, and being more suitable for critically ill stroke patients with impaired consciousness and severe swallowing disorders [22, 23]. Early enteral nutrition plays a crucial role in maintaining and improving the nutritional status of stroke patients [24]. A study by Cong Ling et al. on patients with intracerebral hemorrhage also demonstrated that early nasojejunal tube enteral nutrition improved hematological and nutritional parameters in patients with impaired neurological function [25]. Regarding albumin levels, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups at different time points. This may reflect the fact that critically ill patients typically receive only 50%–70% of their target caloric and protein intake during enteral nutrition [26, 27] which could limit the detectable differences between feeding routes. However, the NJ group demonstrated a greater increase in albumin levels (t = 7.49, P = 0.000), suggesting that long-term support may yield further benefits, which is of significant importance for reducing complications and improving prognosis [28].

Regarding complications, the Inflammation Burden Index (IBI) in the NJ group was significantly lower than that in the NG group (t = -6.626, P = 0.011), and the incidence of malnutrition was also lower (χ² = 1.791, P = 0.043), suggesting that NJ has advantages in reducing complications such as gastric stasis, aspiration pneumonia, and feeding intolerance [2, 9, 10] Post-stroke dysphagia and malnutrition are independent risk factors for pneumonia, readmission, and mortality; effective enteral nutrition can reduce the incidence of complications and shorten the rehabilitation period [11, 12, 29].

The advantages of NJ in this study regarding nutritional improvement and the prevention and control of complications confirm the priority of early jejunal nutrition in patients with severe stroke, which holds significant clinical importance for improving short-term clinical outcomes and reducing the burden of adverse events.

Health economic analysis

The results showed that the total hospitalization costs (t = 20.560, P = 0.000) and nutritional costs (t = 53.280, P = 0.000) in the NJ group were significantly higher than those in the NG group. This was primarily due to higher costs associated with NJ tube placement supplies, procedural complexity, nursing labor hours, and monitoring, which led to an increase in overall costs. The significantly higher costs in the NJ group reflect real-world clinical practice and fee structures in most tertiary hospitals in China. In our institution, NG tubes are inserted as routine bedside procedures with low procedural fees and no additional imaging confirmation. In contrast, NJ tube placement requires specialized clinicians, with procedural fees tens of times higher than NG insertion. NJ tube consumables are historically 4–5 times more expensive than NG tubes, and blind insertion has a low success rate, often requiring fluoroscopy or electromagnetic navigation for confirmation. These imaging costs were not included in the present analysis. Even when only considering tube consumables, insertion fees, feeding fees, and enteral nutrition costs, substantial cost differences between the two feeding modalities remained. Due to its simplicity of use and cost-effective supplies, NG offers a clear advantage in direct costs and is more suitable for patients with low-to-moderate nutritional risk, who are alert, and have normal gastric motility. Although NJ entails higher short-term direct costs, its long-term economic value—derived from reducing the incidence of malnutrition and complications—cannot be overlooked. Malnutrition and complications prolong hospital stays, increase readmission rates, and raise overall healthcare expenditures, whereas NJ reduces long-term healthcare resource consumption by decreasing events such as pneumonia and feeding intolerance [30, 31]. Ojo also noted that there is a significant correlation between malnutrition and increased medical costs; effective nutritional support can effectively mitigate this risk, optimize the use of healthcare resources, and thereby lead to better control of overall medical costs [29]. Although NG has lower short-term costs, the subsequent medical demands arising from malnutrition and complications may result in a final economic burden that exceeds that of NJ. Therefore, clinicians should adopt a full-cycle perspective: the higher short-term investment in NJ can translate into long-term cost savings, particularly yielding superior comprehensive benefits for patients at high nutritional risk or with severe stroke.

Notably, the NJ group had inherently higher baseline severity, which may independently drive higher hospitalization costs. Given the limited sample size, propensity score matching (PSM) was not applied to the overall cohort. To mitigate confounding for economic outcomes, PSM was performed exclusively for cost and DRG-related analyses. After matching, the significantly higher nutritional costs and marginally higher DRG overrun rates in the NJ group suggest that feeding modality itself contributes to economic burden, independent of baseline acuity.

Insights from the DRG payment system

The DRG payment system incentivizes hospitals to optimize clinical pathways, improve efficiency, and control inpatient costs through fixed-amount prepayments [32]. In this study, there was no significant difference in length of stay between the two groups (P > 0.05), suggesting that both nutritional pathways can achieve the target treatment within the standard DRG length of stay, thereby meeting the DRG requirements for efficiency management. The NJ group exhibited a higher DRG cost overrun rate (χ² = 3.683, P = 0.055, approaching statistical significance), which was directly associated with patients’ more severe baseline conditions and higher costs for consumables, nutrition, and nursing care. Since DRG employs a diagnosis-based bundled payment system that does not fully account for individualized cost differences among critically ill patients and those at high risk of malnutrition, it can easily lead to cost overruns for high-risk patients who opt for more intensive treatment [33, 34].

Consequently, the DRG payment system incentivizes departments to prioritize cost-effective treatment options while ensuring patient safety and therapeutic efficacy. NJ is more suitable for high-risk patients, whereas NG should be selected for low-risk patients; precise stratification is necessary to avoid cost pressures resulting from indiscriminate use. This also highlights the need to further refine DRG groups by including subgroups for severe illness and nutritional risk to enhance payment precision.

Implications for clinical decision-making and resource allocation

Based on the findings of this study, it is recommended that clinical departments establish a stratified selection strategy for individualized enteral nutrition in stroke patients:

Prioritize NJ feeding: for patients with impaired consciousness, high risk of malnutrition, poor gastric motility, or high risk of aspiration, to maximize nutritional benefits and reduce complications.

Prioritize NG feeding: for patients who are alert, have a low to moderate risk of malnutrition, and have normal gastric function, to control costs and improve resource efficiency.

This risk stratification–pathway matching model can achieve a balance between clinical benefits and economic sustainability under DRG cost containment.

Three management recommendations are also proposed: At the hospital level, incorporate nutritional risk screening (NRS-2002) and swallowing function assessment into routine stroke admission protocols, establish standardized nutritional pathways and SOPs, and reduce resource waste; At the DRG policy level: It is recommended that nutritional support methods, nutritional risk levels, and level of consciousness be incorporated into DRG grouping adjustment factors to increase reimbursement for critically ill patients; At the nursing level: Optimize NJ tube placement and maintenance procedures to improve nursing efficiency, reduce unnecessary costs, and make high-quality nutritional support more accessible.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, propensity score matching (PSM) was applied only for economic outcomes; residual confounding by baseline severity cannot be fully excluded, which may partly explain higher costs in the NJ group. Second, this was a single-center study with a relatively limited sample size, which may affect its external validity. Third, the analysis focused on in-hospital outcomes and costs; long-term outcomes, such as readmission, functional recovery, and quality of life, were not available. Therefore, a comprehensive cost-effectiveness analysis incorporating long-term follow-up is beyond the scope of this study. Fourth, indirect costs and socioeconomic impacts were not evaluated. Finally, this study was conducted based on local practices for NG and NJ tube placement in China; the findings may not be generalizable to other countries with different healthcare systems and reimbursement policies.

Future multicenter prospective studies will need to extend the follow-up period to assess the long-term cost-effectiveness of different enteral feeding strategies for stroke patients under the DRG payment system. Further research is warranted. Based on the current findings, future work should include cohort studies with expanded datasets, such as long-term follow-up hemoglobin and albumin levels, and the incidence of unplanned readmissions. Additionally, by integrating AI and big data, a cost prediction model for stroke nutritional support should be developed to support refined DRG management.

Conclusion

This study compared clinical outcomes and economic burden between nasogastric (NG) and nasojejunal (NJ) enteral nutrition in stroke patients under the DRG payment system. The results indicate that NJ feeding is associated with better nutritional recovery, greater reduction in malnutrition risk, and lower inflammatory burden relative to NG feeding, particularly in critically ill stroke patients with high nutritional risk. However, NJ feeding was linked to higher direct hospitalization costs, increased nutrition-related expenses, and a marginally higher risk of DRG cost overruns. These findings suggest that NJ feeding may provide clinical benefits for high-risk patients but incurs greater short-term economic costs. Under DRG constraints, an individualized, risk-stratified approach is recommended: NJ feeding may be considered for high-risk patients to optimize nutritional outcomes, while NG feeding may be preferred for low-to-moderate risk patients to control costs. This strategy may help balance clinical benefits and economic sustainability in stroke care.
