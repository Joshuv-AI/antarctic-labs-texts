Neonatal Giant Cardiac Fibroma With Coronary Involvement
Authors: Elena Panaioli, Margaux Bernardini, Damien Bonnet, Diala Khraiche, Regis Gaudin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cardiac fibroma is the second most common childhood cardiac tumor. Coronary arteries are typically unaffected. A 5-month-old infant received a diagnosis of giant cardiac fibroma with coronary involvement. Ventricular arrhythmias prompted a surgical procedure, which was successful. At follow-up, systolic function was normal, with no arrhythmias.

Cardiac fibroma is the second most common childhood cardiac tumor.1 Although it is benign, patients may become symptomatic because of arrhythmias, valvular dysfunction, heart failure, intracavitary obstruction, and conduction defects, which may lead to sudden death. Cardiac fibromas are masses, typically well defined, originating in the ventricular septum or ventricular free wall; they can invade the ventricular conduction system and lead to ventricular arrhythmia, with an incidence of ≤64%.2 Although the coronary arteries are typically unaffected, there have been reports of large fibromas causing coronary compression.Take-Home Message•This case highlights the importance of multimodality imaging for procedural planning in a case of cardiac mass and the beneficial effects of a surgical procedure, although partial, guided by epicardial echocardiography because of complex anatomy.

We herein describe the case of an infant with a large infiltrating cardiac tumor arising from the free wall of the left ventricle and with coronary arteries coursing inside the mass.

An asymptomatic 5-month-old boy underwent transthoracic echocardiography for a cardiac murmur that showed a hyperechoic mass predominantly along the lateral wall of the left ventricle (LV) (Video 1). The suspected cardiac fibroma was subsequently confirmed by cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) showing a not well-defined intramural mass in the same location (Figure 1A), 46 × 39 × 34 mm with pericardial effusion and homogeneous signal isointense to myocardium on T1-weighted and cine sequences. On postcontrast dynamic CMR, the mass showed on the delayed image, with avid contrast enhancement (Figure 1B). Cardiac CT evidenced involvement of the left anterior descending artery (LAD) in the infiltrating mass (Figures 1C and 1D). A first 24-hour Holter monitoring showed sinus rhythm without any arrhythmia, but a subsequent control revealed ventricular arrythmias. The patient was operated on via a median sternotomy using conventional cardiopulmonary bypass with bicaval cannulation. The white LV mass was inspected. Epicardial echocardiography was used to analyze the intratumoral course of the LAD. The aorta was then cross-clamped, and cold crystalloid cardioplegia was given. A large left ventriculotomy incision was made lateral to the LAD, and most of the mass was shelled out, leaving only residual fibrotic tissue under the left main coronary artery and along the LAD. Reconstruction of the LV was realized by direct closure of the ventriculotomy with interrupted 5.0 polypropylene sutures reinforced with 2 heterologous pericardial strips and a running suture. Before closure, pledgeted sutures were passed through the LV wall to prevent the “pocket” of the mass from filling up (Video 2).

Because ventricular arrhythmias persisted at the end of surgery, the patient was supported by venoarterial-extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) for 1 day. In the postoperative course, the inotropic drugs were stopped after 10 days, and left ventricular systolic function fully recovered despite the presence of a residual cardiac mass. The patient was discharged from the hospital 23 days after surgery. Microscopic analysis confirmed the diagnosis of fibroma with noncapsulated peripheral borders.

After discharge the postoperative course was uneventful. After 3 months, the child remained asymptomatic. No recurrence of arrhythmia was noted during follow-up. Transthoracic echocardiography showed normal ventricular function and a residual cardiac mass of 12 mm with stable dimensions (Video 3).
