Deciphering the Impact of Lignin on Anaerobic Digestion: Focus on Inhibition Mechanisms and Methods for Alleviating Inhibition
Authors: Ziyan Sun, Qiang Liu, Yeqing Li, Mahmoud Mazarji, Lu Feng, Junting Pan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
China has abundant lignocellulosic biomass resources. These resources are converted into biogas by anaerobic digestion (AD), which not only realizes the comprehensive utilization of waste resources but also obtains abundant biomass energy. However, the low biodegradability of lignocellulosic biomass caused by the complex structure has seriously hindered its utilization by enzymes and microorganisms, resulting in low biogas production and limited development of biogas engineering. The purpose of this work is to analyze the mechanism of lignin inhibiting AD and summarize the main methods for alleviating inhibition. Based on this, this review examines the factors influencing lignin’s inhibition of methane production during AD from two key perspectives: (1) discussing lignin’s biodegradability challenges, with a focus on its structure, functional groups, and the impact of lignin content in lignocellulosic biomass on its methanogenic potential, and (2) analyzing lignin’s impact on each stage of AD. In addition, we provide insights into future research directions in this field.

Introduction

According to a report from the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization, approximately 3.3 billion tons of cereal straws are produced worldwide each year.1 As a major agricultural nation, China’s annual crop straws yield remained steady at 970 × 106 t from 2015 to 2019, and the comprehensive utilization rate of crop straws reached 85.5% in 2019.2 In 2021, the production of straws reached 865 million tons, an increase of over 35 million tons compared to 2018. The production of corn, rice, and wheat straws reached 321 million tons, 222 million tons, and 179 million tons, respectively, accounting for a total of 83.5%.3 During the period from 2022 to 2030, the estimated supply of corn straws is expected to range from 207 million tons per year to 246 million tons per year in the United States.4

Crop straws have multiple uses, including direct combustion and conversion to clean fuels such as biomethane.5,6 Anaerobic digestion (AD) is a technology for processing biowaste such as animal manure, crop straws, sludge, food waste, etc., through microbial communities to recover energy.7,8 It converts organic matter into methane-rich biogas under anaerobic conditions.9 In comparison to other methods, the biogas produced after AD of crop straws can be used as fuel and for electricity generation. The digestate can be used as fertilizer, achieving comprehensive resource utilization and creating a zero-pollution system.10 Biomethane produced from crop straws through AD (solid-state AD) has excellent development potential as a kind of renewable energy.2,11,12 It is reported that the potential production of biomethane was estimated at 82.25 × 109 m3/a, which could provide 29.2% of China’s annual natural gas consumption in 2018 and 2.5% of its annual energy consumption in 2019.2

While AD of crop straws holds competitive potential, the inherent components of straws often limit this potential.13 Crop straws are primarily composed of lignocellulose, which is composed of cellulose (35–50%), hemicellulose (20–35%), lignin (10–20%), and other compounds.14 Cellulose and hemicellulose can be converted into fermentable sugars and degraded.15 However, lignin, as the most abundant energy-rich aromatic polymer on Earth, is considered the main factor restricting the efficient utilization of lignocellulose biomass.16,17 This impedes the conversion of lignocellulose biomass to methane and restricts the industrial development.14,18,19 It is reported that the activities of hydrolysis, acetogenesis, and methanogenesis decreased by 35%, 10%, and 15%, respectively, when the lignin concentration reached 5 g/L.20 Such inhibition is attributed to the irreversible adsorption of cellulase on lignin.

At present, determining how to break the natural barrier of lignocellulose biomass and improve methane production has become a research hotspot.19 There is a lack of understanding regarding the inhibition mechanisms of lignin in AD of lignocellulosic biomass. This review discusses the reasons for lignin inhibition of methane production in AD from two perspectives: the biodegradation issues of lignin itself and the effect of lignin on the various stages of AD.

Results and Discussion

Characteristics and Methanogenic Potential of Lignin

Structure and Composition of Lignin

Lignin is a biopolymer with a three-dimensional network structure, composed of three phenylpropane units connected by ether bonds and carbon bonds. Due to the different precursors of coniferyl, sinapyl, and p-coumaric alcohol, lignin can be divided into three types of monomers, including guaiacyl (G), syringyl (S), and para-hydroxy-phenyl (H) units.21−23 The proportion of units varies among different plant species, which affects the structure and properties of lignin. Generally, monocotyledonous plants contain G, S, and H units; gymnosperms mainly contain G units, while dicotyledonous plants mainly contain G and S units.22,23Figure 1 shows the plant cell structure of crop straws and the three monomers of lignin.

Function of Lignin

The plant cell wall is a thick wall surrounding the cells, which supports and protects cells. It contains cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, and proteins.24−26 It has a primary and secondary wall. The primary wall is formed during rapid cell elongation and consists primarily of cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin, and some structural proteins. Secondary wall deposition begins when the plant cells stop growing. The secondary wall contains a high concentration of lignin, which improves the cell wall’s water resistance, strength, and ability to resist the invasion of external organisms and abiotic agents. In summary, lignin’s primary functions are as follows: (1) forming the main component of the plant skeleton with cellulose and hemicellulose, providing structural strength for plants; (2) filling the cellulose framework to enhance the mechanical strength of the plant body, facilitating water transportation; and (3) resisting attack from the adverse external environment.22,27 Lignin has the aforementioned functions, which limit the utilization of lignocellulosic biomass. Due to its unique structural characteristics, lignin acts as a barrier between cellulose and cellulase, making lignocellulose difficult to degrade by enzymes and microorganisms. Usually, pretreatments are used to dissolve the lignin barrier.28

Functional Groups of Lignin

The properties of lignin are mainly determined by its functional groups.29 Lignin is primarily divided into two types of functional groups. One of these are the functional groups on the benzene ring, which are also the primary functional groups reflecting the characteristics of lignin, particularly the phenolic hydroxyl group (−OH) and methoxyl group (−OCH3). The second group is composed of functional groups that are attached to the side chains of benzene rings, primarily including aliphatic hydroxyl groups (−OH) and carboxylic acid groups (−COOH).

The phenylpropane structural unit of lignin can be identified based on the number of methoxyl groups, which is a characteristic functional group of lignin. The H unit has no methoxyl, while the G unit and the S unit possess one and two, respectively, as shown in Figure 1. Anaerobic bacteria degrade lignin through a variety of processes, including methanogenesis, ring cleavage, and demethoxylation, according to studies.22 Methoxyl may be the main point of attack for anaerobic microorganisms.22,30 Pan found no apparent correlation between the methoxyl group and the inhibition effect.31 However, Usman Khan et al. found that demethoxylation correlated highly with lignin degradation.32 Barakat et al. highlighted the fact that methoxyl was more biodegradable than side chains and aromatic ring structures.33

The hydroxyl groups are the most common functional group in lignin, which can be divided into two types. One is the phenolic hydroxyl group directly connected to the benzene ring, which is connected to other lignin structural units by an ether bond. Another type of aliphatic hydroxyl group, located on the side chain of the benzene ring, may exist as a free group or be attached to other units by a bond. Phenolic hydroxyl groups are required for the inhibition of lignin model compounds on enzymatic hydrolysis.34 Pan found that phenolic hydroxyl groups are the key to the inhibition effect of lignin, and the inhibition can be eliminated by chemical blocking of free phenolic hydroxyl groups by hydroxypropylation.31 Phenolic hydroxyl groups may form nonproductive bonds through hydrogen bonding, playing an essential role in lignin inhibition.35

As a result, the phenolic hydroxyl group in lignin determines its inhibition effect, while the methoxy group, as a unique functional group in lignin, may be the point at which the inhibition is disrupted.

Content and Methanogenic Potential of Lignin in Lignocellulosic Biomass

The characteristics of lignin are highly dependent on the S/G ratio.36 Substrates with low lignin content and a high S/G ratio can be more easily hydrolyzed by enzymes and produce a high methane yield.37,38 Softwoods generally contain more lignin and mainly include G units.39,40 As a result, they cannot be used as an AD substrate. Herbaceous plants (such as corn straws, wheat straws, rice straws, etc.) have the lowest contents of lignin,41 which are commonly used as substrates for AD. As shown in Table 1, cellulose is more readily broken down than hemicellulose, resulting in higher methane production. However, lignin is difficult to break down. When lignin was codigested with cellulose and hemicellulose, methane production of cellulose and hemicellulose was significantly reduced compared to digestion of cellulose or hemicellulose alone.19 Crop straws are complex organic materials, which may be due to the lack of linear change in the amount of lignin or methane produced. The content of lignin has an impact on the methanogenic potential as well. Triolo et al. investigated the effect of biomass on biochemical methane potential and highlighted that biochemical methane potential had the most extraordinary dependence on the concentration of lignin.24 Li et al. found that the lignin inhibition index was positively correlated to the ratio of lignin to cellulose content. Reducing this ratio was the optimal strategy to overcome lignin inhibition.47

Based on the analysis of the structure, functional groups, and content of lignin mentioned above, it was found that lignin itself is difficult to degrade. Consequently, biomass with a high cellulose content and a low lignin content is regarded as a substrate for AD with high potential for biomethane.48

Effect of Lignin on Various Stages in AD: Inhibition or Promotion

Hydrolysis Process: Nonproductive Adsorption of Lignin

Hydrolysis, acidogenesis, and methanogenesis are the main components of AD. Hydrolysis is generally considered to be the limiting step in the AD of lignocellulosic biomass. The presence of lignin prevents the AD of lignocellulose to produce methane, primarily by restricting the hydrolysis process.12,49 It was found that when the concentration of dissolved lignin was 5 g/L, the hydrolysis, acetogenesis, and methanogenesis activities decreased by 35%, 10%, and 15%, respectively, compared with the control.20 The nonproductive adsorption of lignin has always been considered the main reason for it inhibiting cellulose hydrolysis. Nonproductive adsorption refers to the binding that occurs when cellulase or anaerobic microorganisms adsorb on the surface of lignin rather than cellulose or hemicellulose (Figure 2). The adsorption capacity of cellulase on lignin is almost irreversible. Moreover, cellulase has a greater adsorption capacity for lignin than cellulose.42,50 Only a small amount of cellulase reacts with the cellulose or hemicellulose, which affects glycosylation and reduces methane production.28 In some comprehensive studies on optimizing AD processes, three main nonproductive adsorption forces for lignin and cellulase have been demonstrated: hydrophobic interaction, electrostatic interaction, and hydrogen bonding interaction, following the order of hydrophobic interaction > electrostatic interaction > hydrogen bonding interaction.27,50 Hydrophobic interactions exist between the aromatic groups of lignin and the aromatic amino acids of cellulase. Electrostatic interaction is mainly due to electrification of the surface of the enzyme and lignin. Hydrogen bonding interaction can occur between the amide group of the enzyme and the aromatic phenol hydroxyl group of lignin. Hydrophobic interaction and hydrogen bonding interaction can further enhance nonproductive adsorption.

Lignin inhibits hydrolysis through nonproductive adsorption.

Hydrophobic interaction is generally considered the main cause of the nonproductive lignin adsorption on cellulase, composed of proteins.50−52 It is well-known that when proteins (cellulase) are dissolved in water, their natural tendency is to minimize the exposure of hydrophobic groups to the water, making them more likely to adsorb when hydrophobic proteins come into contact with lignin or cellulose. Lignin has a strong hydrophobicity and a larger contact angle than cellulose.53,54 When hydrophilic cellulose meets them, cellulase is preferentially adsorbed on lignin, which reduces the availability of cellulase to cellulose and results in a decrease in the cellulose hydrolysis rate. Qin et al. used atomic force microscopy tips with hydrophobic, −OH, and −COOH chemical properties and immobilized cellulase to express hydrophobic, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions, respectively.55 The forces between the hydrophobic tips and cellulase were found to be 43% and 13% higher than those between cellulase and tips with −OH and −COOH groups, respectively.55 This indicated that cellulase’s attraction to lignin is primarily due to hydrophobic interaction.

The combination of cellulase and lignin is influenced by the electrostatic interaction. In general, enzymes and polymers carry chemical groups with different charges on their surfaces, which affect the electrification of the surface of the compound. The charge of lignin and cellulase will affect their adsorption and be affected by the solution environment. The positive or negative charge of amino acids exposed to the enzyme surface depends on the pH value of the environment. According to the principle that like things repel and opposites attract, protein molecules and polymers repel each other when their charge is the same and attract each other when they have opposite charges. The acidic groups in lignin (sulfonic and carboxylic acids) were found to increase the electrostatic repulsion between lignin and the enzyme. With an increase in pH, it can reduce the nonproductive adsorption of cellulose.56

Hydrogen bonding interaction is another important cause of cellulase adsorption on lignin. The formation of hydrogen bonds is mainly due to the presence of hydroxyl groups in lignin.42 In plant tissues, lignin is surrounded by extra water, where it combines with carbohydrate polymers to create intricate structures with a variety of chemical connections. Compared with carbohydrates in plant tissue, lignin is hydrophobic. However, the hydroxyl groups attached to lignin, especially those near methoxy groups, participate in hydrogen bonding with water molecules. The mechanism involves lignin interacting with nucleophilic sites on enzymes and/or carbohydrates, forming an intermediate quinone methyl compound that inhibits cellulose hydrolysis. Phenol hydroxyl is a necessary condition for the formation of quinone methyl. Suppose that the phenolic hydroxyl group of lignin occupies one or more adsorption sites of the catalytic channel by forming hydrogen bonds or quinone methyl groups. In that case, the adsorption mode between cellulose and cellulase is destroyed, affecting cellulase hydrolysis. The presence of carboxyl groups in lignin increases its hydrophilicity and reduces its adverse effects when enzymatically hydrolyzing the substrate. As previously stated, enzymatic hydrolysis efficiency is positively correlated with the content of aliphatic hydroxyl groups and carboxylic acid groups and negatively correlated with the content of phenolic hydroxyl groups.

Methanogenesis Process: Low Methanogenic Potential for Lignin

The methanogenesis process refers to methanogens converting small molecules such as acetic acid, CO2, and one-carbon compounds (formic acid, methylamine, methanol, etc.) into CH4. As shown in Table 1, cellulose has a methanogenic potential higher than those of hemicellulose and lignin. Lignin has limited methanogenic potential and has adverse effects on the methanogenic process. Sulfur-containing lignin, in particular, has a greater inhibition effect on methanation in AD than non-sulfur-containing lignin.59 Additionally, certain structures of lignin, such as quinones, compete for electrons with methanogens, thereby inhibiting the process of methanogenesis.60 Banu et al. found that adding surfactant can prevent methanogens from secreting enzymes to adsorb lignin and increase methane production by 350 mL/g COD.61 In addition to mitigating inhibition by adding exogenous additives, existing research has attempted to elucidate the metabolic connections between lignin-degrading bacteria and methanogens through metagenomic analysis. This provided a potential theoretical basis for enhancing lignin methanogenesis.62

Other Factors of Inhibition

In addition to lignin, the high carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C/N) of lignocellulosic biomass, the crystallinity of cellulose, and the hydrolysis of hemicellulose are also factors that inhibit AD to produce methane. When the C/N ratio is too low, excess dissolved nitrogen is produced, which can lead to ammonia inhibition. When the C/N ratio is too high, it causes a nutritional imbalance and difficulties with cellulose degradation. Therefore, codigestion of low C/N ratio biomass and high C/N ratio biomass is a viable option for increasing methane production.40 The crystalline part of cellulose is dense and complex to be degraded by enzymatic hydrolysis.22 As a result, the high crystallinity of cellulose has a negative impact on methane production.59 Hemicellulose hydrolyzes quickly and easily causes acid accumulation. Table 2 shows that the VFA contents of xylan and glucomannan were both greater than 7000 mg/L.19 Therefore, codigestion of cellulose-rich substrates and hemicellulose-rich substrates is recommended to relieve VFA accumulation of hemicellulose.59

Methods for Alleviating Lignin Inhibition on AD

Pretreatment is a promising approach to overcome the natural recalcitrance of lignocellulose.51 Physical, chemical, and biological methods are the three main pretreatment techniques.63−69

Physical Methods

Physical pretreatment refers to reducing the size of the raw material by mechanical actions without using chemical agents or microorganisms.70,71 This is primarily accomplished through conventional mechanical grinding or radiation, which reduces cellulose’s crystallinity, eliminates the lignin and hemicellulose binding layer, and increases the accessible surface area.72 This method has the advantages of simple operation, less pollution, and no/few inhibitors but has high energy consumption and high cost.73 The typical physical techniques include extrusion, comminution, and irradiation (ultrasound and microwave).72,74 Physical pretreatment can generally increase the methane yield of biomass in AD.18

Chemical Methods

Chemical pretreatment is the process of modifying the physical and chemical characteristics of lignocellulosic biomass with chemicals. Additionally, it is the one that garners the most interest and shows the most promise.75 The typical chemical methods include acid and alkali pretreatment, ionic liquids, oxidative pretreatment with peroxides, and wet oxidation.76,77 A study by Usman Khan et al. showed that pretreatment with 4% oxygen and 2% NaOH at 220 °C (wet oxidation) significantly promoted lignin degradation, ultimately resulting in 35% lignin conversion to methane.78 It should be noted that chemical pretreatments can produce inhibitors, leading to equipment corrosion. The development of waste liquid recovery techniques contributes to the use of chemical pretreatment.79

Biological Methods

Biological pretreatment methods include fungal pretreatment, microbial consortium pretreatment, and enzymatic pretreatment. Specifically, biological pretreatment is the use of microbial metabolites.80,81 Previous studies have shown that methane yield from lignocellulosic biomass (such as straws) pretreated with Penicillium aurantiogriseum demonstrated a 48% increase compared to untreated biomass.82 Biological pretreatment has advantages over physical and chemical pretreatments, including lower energy requirements, the absence of chemicals, fewer inhibitors, and more tolerant environmental conditions.83−86 However, the extended pretreatment time limits the application of these processes in commercial applications.76,87,88

Combined Strategies

To overcome the limitations of a single pretreatment technique, combining two or more of the same type of pretreatment technology or different types of pretreatment technology has been studied.89−91 Zhang et al. found that microbial combined dilute acid pretreatment is the most effective method to deal with water hyacinth, resulting in the highest cellulose content and reducing sugar production compared with single pretreatment.92 Guan et al. discovered that combined biological and chemical pretreatment of rice straws increased the methane yield by 57.56% compared to the control.93 Combining single pretreatments can complement their shortcomings, which is beneficial for increasing methane production and comprehensive biomass utilization efficiency.94,95 Cost is the limiting factor for combined pretreatment use.

Conclusions and Future Remarks

The advancement of biomass energy and biogas engineering offers a viable solution to address the energy shortage. The inhibition of lignin remains a main obstacle in the commercial operation of lignocellulose AD. This work summarizes the mechanism of lignin inhibition in AD from two perspectives: lignin biodegradability and its impact on various stages of the AD process. The hydroxyl functional groups present in lignin lead to nonproductive adsorption with hydrolytic enzymes, which is the primary cause of inhibition in AD. This adsorption mainly occurs during the hydrolysis stage of AD. Methoxy groups are more susceptible to biodegradation than side chains and aromatic ring structures, making them the primary target for anaerobic microorganisms. Microbial–chemical combined pretreatment is a promising method to enhance lignocellulosic biomass methanogenesis.

To accelerate the development of projects converting lignocellulosic biomass into biogas, we propose the following research directions:1.The characteristic functional groups in lignin are the limiting factors affecting AD. Specifically, the influence of methoxy groups on AD needs to be studied further, as their effects are yet to be fully understood. At present, we consider that elucidating the mechanisms of these functional groups is crucial theoretical support for promoting the conversion of lignocellulosic biomass.2.From a process analysis perspective, pretreatment remains a relatively simple and efficient engineering method to enhance the conversion rate of lignocellulosic biomass. China has a vast geography and significant climate differences between the north and south, so it is essential to adapt pretreatment methods to the specific characteristics of locally produced lignocellulosic biomass. To meet the diverse demands for effective AD of these biomasses, there is a recognized need for an economical and feasible comprehensive pretreatment technology to complement single pretreatment methods.3.In fact, lignin has a wide range of applications. We also support exploring lignin separation and extraction technology combined with AD, where the non-lignin portion after separation serves as the AD substrate, and lignin itself is utilized as a raw material for high-value applications.
