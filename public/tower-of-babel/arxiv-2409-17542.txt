Evidence for Type Ib/c origin of the supernova remnant G292.0 + + 1.8
Takuto Narita, Hiroyuki Uchida, Jacco Vink, Satoru Katsuda, Hideyuki Umeda, Takashi Yoshida, Toshiki Sato, Kai Matsunaga, Takeshi Go Tsuru

Abstract
Circumstellar material (CSM) produced by mass loss from massive stars ( ≳ 10 ​ M ⊙ \gtrsim 10~M_{\odot} ) through strong stellar winds or binary stripping provides rich information for understanding progenitors of core-collapse supernova remnants. In this paper we present a grating spectroscopy of a Galactic SNR G292.0 + + 1.8, which is claimed to be a Type Ib/c remnant in a binary system according to recent studies. If G292.0 + + 1.8 was experienced a strong mass-loss via binary interactions before its explosion, an oxygen-rich material produced in the He-burning layer is expected to be observed in the central belt-like structure formed by shock-heated CSM. Using the Reflection Grating Spectrometer onboard XMM-Newton, we detect N VII Ly α \alpha line (0.50 keV) for the first time in G292.0 + + 1.8 and find that the abundance ratio of nitrogen to oxygen is significantly lower (N/O = 0.5 ± 0.1 =0.5\pm 0.1 ) than the solar value. This low N/O suggests that the progenitor of G292.0 + + 1.8 experienced strong mass-loss and ended up to a Wolf-Rayet (WR) star exposing the He-burning layer at the pre-supernova. Comparing our result and the evolution models of single stars and binaries, we conclude that the progenitor of G292.0 + + 1.8 experienced strong mass-loss enough to occur a Type Ib/c supernova. Our finding is another crucial piece of evidence for a stripped-envelope supernova such as Type Ib/c as the origin of G292.0 + + 1.8.



I
Introduction

Circumstellar material (CSM) around core-collapse (CC) supernovae (SNe) of massive (
\gtrsim 10~M_{\odot}
) stars have rich information on progenitor parameters such as a mass of zero age main sequence (
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
), an initial rotation velocity, and convection
(
Fransson et al. 2005
;
Maeder et al. 2014
;
Chiba et al. 2020
, e.g,)
.
This is because the mass-loss history of massive stars is affected by their stellar properties; particularly abundances of the CSM is significantly changed during the stellar evolution
(
Maeder 1983
, e.g,)
.
For instance, main-sequence (MS) massive stars emit a hydrogen(H)-rich wind and post-MS stars emit a stellar wind rich in helium (He) and nitrogen (N), since elements such as N produced by the CNO-cycle in the H-burning layer are carried to the surface by the convection
(
Owocki 2004
;
Przybilla et al. 2010
, e.g,)
.

CC SNe are roughly classified by the presence of lines, i.e., the abundance of the CSM; H-rich (Type II), H-poor (Type Ib), and H/He-poor (Type Ic) SNe.
Among them, Type Ib/c SNe are also known as stripped SNe because the progenitors are Wolf-Rayet (WR) stars experiencing strong mass-loss and exposing inner layers where the H/He burning takes place
(
Smith 2014
, e.g,)
.
Therefore, the progenitor of Type Ib/c SNe is expected to have blown the N-rich winds produced in the H-burning layer and oxygen (O) rich winds produced by the triple-
\alpha
reaction and
12
C(
\alpha
,
\gamma
)
16
O reaction in the He-burning layer.
Although the CSM around SNe thus gives us rich information on their progenitors, due to a lack of observational constraints, many questions still remain about the evolution of massive stars and their explosion mechanisms.

Supernova remnants (SNRs) are also essential to determine the origin of CC SNe, since abundances of shock-heated ejecta and CSM reflect progenitor parameters such as stellar mass and mass loss, which are useful to constrain their explosion scenario, i.e., their progenitors or progenitor systems.
The most common method employed so far to estimate
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
is to compare ejecta abundance ratios of such as O, neon (Ne), magnesium (Mg), silicon (Si), sulfur (S), and iron (Fe) with theoretical calculations
(
Katsuda et al. 2018
, cf.)
.

Lighter elements contained in the CSM, Carbon (C), N, and O (hereafter, CNO elements) are also useful to constrain the progenitors or progenitor systems.
Several previous observations with the Reflection Grating Spectrometer (RGS) onboard XMM-Newton successfully detected C V Ly
\alpha
and N VII Ly
\alpha
in nearby SNRs
(
Uchida et al. 2019
;
Kasuga et al. 2021
, e.g,)
.
Once we can accurately measure the amount of these CNO elements in the CSM, we can directly access the progenitor’s mass-loss history since the mass-loss rate at each stage of the stellar evolution changes the amount of CNO elements blown out from the stellar surface
(
Maeder 1983
, e.g,)
, which will be a good tracer of progenitor parameters or progenitor systems
(
Chiba et al. 2020
;
Tateishi et al. 2021
, e.g,)
.
Under this consideration, in our previous paper we established a new method for constraining these parameters using a high-energy-resolution spectroscopy and determined
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
and the rotation velocity of a magnetar-hosting SNR RCW 103
(
Narita et al. 2023
)
.

Here, we focus on an oxygen-rich CC SNR G292.0
+
1.8, which has a belt-like structure produced by CSM
(
Park et al. 2004
;
Park et al. 2002
)
and hosts a rapid moving pulsar
(
Hughes et al. 2001
;
Long et al. 2022
)
.
Previous studies show various estimates for
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
of the progenitor with ejecta abundances: 25–40
M_{\odot}
(
Park et al. 2004
;
Gonzalez & Safi-Harb 2003
;
Kamitsukasa et al. 2014
)
, 13–30
M_{\odot}
(
Bhalerao et al. 2019
)
,
<15~M_{\odot}
(
Katsuda et al. 2018
)
., and 12–16
M_{\odot}
(
Temim et al. 2022
)
.
Interestingly a recent study of hydrodynamical simulations of G292.0
+
1.8 suggests that a total mass of the ejecta requires
\lesssim 3~M_{\odot}
, which likely results in a remnant of a massive star in a binary system
(
Temim et al. 2022
)
.

In this paper, we perform a grating spectroscopy of CSM-dominant filaments in G292.0
+
1.8 (Section
II
and
III
), estimate the progenitor in the cases of a single star or a binary system (Section
IV.1
and
IV.2
), and then summarize the conclusions in Section
V
.
Throughout the paper, we assume the age and distance of G292.0
+
1.8 to be 3000 yr
(
Ghavamian et al. 2005
;
Winkler et al. 2009
)
and 6.2 kpc
(
Gaensler & Wallace 2003
)
, respectively.
Errors of parameters are defined as 1
\sigma
confidence intervals.

II
observations

We selected an observation with the longest exposure time available for G292.0
+
1.8 (ID: 0400330101) from the XMM-Newton Science Archive since it is inappropriate to combine the spectrum from different observations with different roll angles.
A nearby blank-sky observation (ID: 0823031601) was applied for estimating the background contribution.
RGS and MOS data were used for the following analysis.
We also used an ACIS image of G292.0
+
1.8 obtained with Chandra (ID: 6677) for estimating the spatial broadening of the remnants to modify the RGS response.
We reprocessed the MOS data using pipeline tool
emchain
in the XMM-Newton Science Analysis System (SAS) version 20.0.0.
The RGS data was reprocessed using the pipeline tool
rgsproc
in SAS.
Remaining exposure times after the standard event screening are 60.7 ks and 61.0 ks for the MOS and RGS, respectively.

III
analyses and results

As shown in Figure
1
, we observe a clear belt-like structure, which is thought to be a product of stellar winds due to its low metal abundance compared to that in the metal-rich ejecta elsewhere in the remnant
(
Park et al. 2002
)
.
We selected three compact bright regions in the belt-like filament (namely, Reg A, B, and C in Figure
1
) so that we can extract high resolution spectra with the RGS.
The angular size of each region is small (
\lesssim 1^{\prime}
) enough to resolve low energy lines of the CNO elements.

For the following spectral analysis, we used the
XSPEC
software version 12.12.1
(
Arnaud 1996
)
and the maximum likelihood W-statistic
(
Wachter et al. 1979
)
.
MOS1, MOS2, and combined RGS data (RGS1
+
2; first and second order spectra) were simultaneously fitted.
RGS response matrices (RMFs) were modified for taking into account the spatial broadening using
rgsrmfsmooth
in
FTOOL
.
We convolved the RMFs to an ACIS image for each emission line and consequently obtained 30 different modified RMFs applied for different emission line bands.

We fitted the spectra with an absorbed two-component non-equilibrium ionization (NEI) model with variable abundances,
tbabs*(vnei+vnei)
since according to previous observations
(
Gonzalez & Safi-Harb 2003
;
Vink et al. 2004
;
Bhalerao et al. 2019
, e.g,)
the analyzed regions are explained by emissions from both the forward-shocked CSM and reverse-shocked ejecta.
We set the solar abundance to that of
Wilms et al. 2000
defied as
({n_{x}}/{n_{H}})_{\odot}
, where
n_{X}
and
n_{H}
represent the number density of each element.
The hydrogen column density (
N_{\rm{H}}
) was set to free and the electron temperature (
kT_{\rm e}
), the ionization timescale (
n_{\rm e}t
), and the normalization for each NEI component were allowed to vary.
We also varied abundances of O (=Ne), Mg, Si, S, and Fe (=Ni) for the hot component and those of N, O, Ne, and Fe (=Ni) for cold components.
Here, we define ”abundance” as the so-called solar abundance ratio:
(n_{\rm{x}}/n_{\rm{H}})/(n_{\rm{x}}/n_{\rm{H}})_{\odot}
.
We also added a power-law component to the two-component NEI models for Reg C since this region is contaminated by emission from the pulsar wind nebula
(
Kamitsukasa et al. 2014
, e.g,)
.

We found that the above model was not able to represent the continuum in the high energy band (
\gtrsim 5
keV).
According to the previous Suzaku observation of G292.0
+
1.8,
Kamitsukasa et al. 2014
suggests that the ejecta can be explained by a multi-temperature model (
\sim 1.1
keV and
\sim 2.7
keV).
We therefore added another NEI component to the above model, allowing the normalization,
kT_{\rm e}
and
n_{\rm e}t
to be free (hereafter, three-component NEI model).
The abundance of Fe of this NEI component was linked to that of the above hot component and the other elements were fixed to the solar value.
The goodness of fit is improved (W-statistics value/d.o.f.: 8,651/8,586 to 8,597/8,579) without any other significant change in the other parameters.
We thus applied the three-component NEI model for the following analysis.

2
shows the fits of three-component NEI model to the spectra.
Table
1
displays the best-fit parameters.
The obtained
N_{\rm{H}}
,
kT_{\rm{e}}
, and
n_{\rm{e}}t
are consistent with the pervious studies
(
Park et al. 2002
;
Gonzalez & Safi-Harb 2003
;
Vink et al. 2004
;
Kamitsukasa et al. 2014
;
Bhalerao et al. 2019
)
.
The abundances of medium
kT_{\rm{e}}
(
\sim 0.7~\rm{keV})
and low
kT_{\rm{e}}
(
\sim 0.2~\rm{keV})
components are similar to the previous measurements of the ejecta
(
Park et al. 2002
;
Gonzalez & Safi-Harb 2003
;
Kamitsukasa et al. 2014
;
Bhalerao et al. 2019
)
and the CSM
(
Park et al. 2002
;
Kamitsukasa et al. 2014
;
Bhalerao et al. 2019
)
, respectively.
We found no evidence of line broadening, reported in the previous study for Ne X Ly
\alpha

(
Vink et al. 2004
)
, which might be due to the difference of the spectral extraction regions or statistics.
Notably we detected the N VII line (
=0.50

+
1.8.
Measured N-rich abundance ratio (N/Fe
\geq 1.3

IV
discussion

IV.1
Evidence of CSM and its Composition

Previous studies
(
Katsuda et al. 2015
;
Tateishi et al. 2021
;
Narita et al. 2023
, e.g,)
claim that the N-rich material observed in SNRs is clear evidence of CSM.
This is because N is mainly produced by the CNO cycle in the H-burning shell and blown out from the stellar surface via mass loss through the life of progenitors
(
Maeder 1983
, e.g,)
.
Our results are consistent with the conclusion that the N-rich material found in the belt-like structure of G292.0
+
1.8 originates from the shock-heated CSM as suggested by
Park et al. 2002
.
Such N-rich CSM is presumably from a red super giant (RSG) or a WR star
(
Maeder 1983
, e.g,)
.

Recently we showed a method for constraining progenitor parameters such as
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
, rotation velocities, metallicities, and convections by using the abundance ratio N/O of the CSM.
Initial state of progenitors changes the mass-loss rate in each stage of the stellar evolution having a positive relationship to the amount of C, N, and O of CSM.
In the case of relatively low
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
(
\lesssim 30~M_{\odot}
) progenitors, the N/O is higher than the solar value since the N-rich layer produced by CNO-cycle in H-burning layer is blown out from the stellar surface.
On the other hand, in the case of relatively high
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
(
\gtrsim 30~M_{\odot}
) progenitors, the N/O is lower than the solar value since the O-rich layer produced by triple
\alpha
in the He-burning layer is blown out from the stellar surface by the strong mass loss.
The N/O of rapid rotating progenitors is similar to that of heavier progenitors with slow initial rotation velocity since rotation enhances the mass-loss rate
(
Heger et al. 2000
, e.g.,)
.
Metallicity and convection also change the N/O since the metallicity has a positive relationship to the mass-loss rate
(
Maeder et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
and convection can carry the N-rich material to the outer layer of the progenitor
(
Luo et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
.
Under this consideration, we calculated the N/O by integrating the amounts of elements of stellar winds blown out from various type of progenitors with the stellar evolution codes and estimated the progenitor of RCW 103 (
{\rm N/O}=3.8\pm 0.1
) to be a low-mass (
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}=10
–12
M_{\odot}
) progenitor with medium rotation velocity (
\lesssim 100~\rm{km~s^{-1}}
)
(
Narita et al. 2023
)
.
In the same manner, we constrain the properties of the progenitor of G292.0
+
1.8 for the following analysis.
The weighted average N/O measured in the three regions is calculated to
0.5\pm 0.1
with confidence contour map between N and O of the soft component.
Of particular note is a significantly low N/O of G292.0
+
1.8 (
=0.5\pm 0.1
) compared with RCW 103, which implies that the progenitor exposed the O-rich core before the SN explosion.

For constraining the progenitor of G292.0
+
1.8, it is important to know what material is contained in the belt-like region.
The abundances of CSM produced by stellar winds are same to that produced by other mechanism such as the binary stripping since the abundances of the material producing CSM is uniform across the entire stellar surface.
Under this consideration, we constrained the CSM composition of G292.0
+
1.8 assuming the spherically symmetric CSM.
Figure
3
illustrates two conceivable cases of environments around the progenitor at the pre-supernova stage for G292.0
+
1.8.
In accordance with
Narita et al. 2023
, we made a simple assumption for calculating the bubble sizes by applying CSM hydrodynamic evolution
(
Chevalier 1999
;
Chevalier 2005
, e.g,)
and stellar evolution
(
Takahashi et al. 2013
;
Takahashi et al. 2014
;
Takahashi et al. 2018
;
Yoshida et al. 2019
, e.g,)
codes of single stars.
In both cases, the outer most cavity is produced by MS winds, which sweep up the interstellar matter (ISM) in the MS phase
(
Weaver et al. 1977
;
Chen et al. 2013
)
.
Assuming the small progenitor (
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
=10~M_{\odot}
) and the pressure divided by Boltzmann’s constant of Galactic ISM
(
Berghöfer et al. 1998
,
\sim 10^{4}~\rm{cm^{3}~K}
;)
, we calculated the minimum bound of the size of the wind bubble to be
\sim 15.6~\rm{pc}
since the bubble size has a positive linear relationship to
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
(
Chen et al. 2013
)
.
We also calculated the bubble size around a binary system with the hydrodynamic simulation of wind morphology around twin-star binaries
(
MacLeod & Loeb 2020
)
and binary evolution models
(
Yoon et al. 2010
)
.
From the mass-loss rate (
\sim 10^{-8}~M_{\odot}~\rm{yr^{-1}}
) and the speed of wind (
\sim
300–1000 km s
-1
) emitted from the binary models, the stellar wind bubble size around a binary system is similar to that around a single star.
Since the radius of G292.0
+
1.8 is
\sim 8.0
pc, we infer that the observed CSM component contain no ISM.

We consider two cases with different CSM structures for estimating the swept-up stellar wind material in the observed CSM filaments.
In the case of a relatively low mass (
\lesssim 20~M_{\odot}
) progenitor, the RSG wind material is concentrated near the progenitor (
r_{\rm{RSG}}=2.5
pc) with a radius of
r_{\rm{RSG}}=2.5
pc due to a slow wind
(
Goldman et al. 2017
,
\sim 10~\rm{km~s^{-1}}
,)
.
We thus consider that the CSM component originates from a swept-up material produced by mass loss through the RSG phase.
In the case of a relatively high mass (
\gtrsim 20~M_{\odot}
) progenitor, a fast material produced by strong mass loss through the WR phase
(
Chevalier & Imamura 1983
;
Chevalier 2005
, 100–200
\rm{km~s^{-1}}
,)
expands up to
r_{\rm{WR}}=8.0
pc (
\geq 60~M_{\odot}
) and sweeps up the RSG mass-loss materials.
Given this, we assume that the resultant CSM component contains either all of the RSG/WR mass-loss material produced or only a portion of the WR mass-loss material if the progenitor was a high-mass star.

IV.2
Progenitor Constraint with N/O

To constrain the progenitor parameters, we use a method established in our previous study
(
Narita et al. 2023
)
.
Figure
4
shows the relationship between the measured N/O and the progenitor parameters calculated with stellar evolution code of single stars
(
Ekström et al. 2012
, Geneva code;)
varying progenitor
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
and initial rotation velocities (
v_{\rm{init}}/v_{\rm{crit}}=0,0.4
, where
v_{\rm{init}}
is the initial surface rotation velocity and
v_{\rm{crit}}
is the critical velocity reaching when the gravitational acceleration is exactly counterbalanced by the centrifugal force).
We also vary the metallicity (
=1

Z_{\odot}
and 0.5
Z_{\odot}
) since G292.0
+
1.8 is a young Galactic SNR, which is possibly in a solar- or slightly low-metallicity
(
Bhalerao et al. 2019
)
environment.
Another parameter that can modify the value of N/O is a convective overshoot, which is the extrusion of convective motion due to non-zero velocities of material on a boundary between the convection zone and the radiative zone in stars.
The N-rich convection layer above the H-burning shell expands due to the strong convective overshoot and, therefore, the amount of N is larger than that of progenitor with the weak convective overshoot.
The N/O is enhanced in the case of the middle mass range (15–20
M_{\odot}
)
(
Narita et al. 2023
, see Figure 8 of)
.
Another effect of the convective overshoot is an increase in the He core mass and, therefore, the minimum mass of a progenitor becoming an WR star is probably a few
M_{\odot}
smaller than that of the progenitor with the weak overshoot
(
Yoshida et al. 2019
, cf)
.

As shown in Figure
4
, we compared our result (N/O
=0.5\pm 0.1
) with the above models.
The result indicates that the progenitor of G292.0
+
1.8 is likely a massive star with
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
of
\gtrsim 30~M_{\odot}
regardless of the rotation velocity and the metallicity.
It is inconsistent with most of the previous estimations of
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
with ejecta abundances; 25–35
M_{\odot}

(
Park et al. 2004
;
Kamitsukasa et al. 2014
)
,
<15~M_{\odot}

(
Katsuda et al. 2018
)
, 13–30
M_{\odot}

(
Bhalerao et al. 2019
)
, and 12–16
M_{\odot}

(
Temim et al. 2022
)
.
We thus investigate if the observed low N/O could be explained by a relatively low-mass star experiencing strong mass-loss in a binary system, or something similar.

One of the possibilities in this scenario is a gravitational effect of a companion star.
According to recent theories, if a progenitor is in a binary system, a star with

\lesssim 20~M_{\odot}

This scenario resolves the discrepancy between our result based on the CSM composition (
\gtrsim 30~M_{\odot}

From these results we conclude that the progenitor of G292.0

V
Conclusions

We performed a high-resolution spectroscopy of G292.0
+
1.8 with the RGS onboard XMM-Newton and robustly detected the N VII line for the first time.
Detailed spectral analysis indicates that the N-rich (
{\rm N/Fe}\gtrsim 1.3
), low-temperature component originates from shock-heated CSM produced by the CNO-cycle in the H-burning layer.
One of the intriguing findings is that G292.0
+
1.8 has an O-rich CSM based on the measured abundance ratio of N/O (
=0.5\pm{0.1}
).
Comparing this with the available stellar evolution codes, we found that none of the models for single low-mass progenitors can account for the observation; instead a high-mass star (
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
\gtrsim 30~M_{\odot}
; potentially a WR star) seems a more likely explanation.
Since the observed O-rich CSM implies a considerable mass loss, another possible scenario is a gravitational effect of a companion star in a binary system.
Many recent theories suggest that a low-mass star (
M_{\rm{ZAMS}}
\lesssim 20~M_{\odot}
) tends to end up as a WR star because of the mass stripping
(
Yoon et al. 2010
;
Yoon 2017
;
Woosley 2019
;
Tauris et al. 2015
)
.
If this is the case, the mass-loss rate is significantly enhanced and the O-rich material is blown off, providing a natural explanation for the observed low N/O ratio and other previous studies on the progenitor mass estimation of G292.0
+
1.8.
Future studies of stellar and binary evolutions will allow us to constrain the progenitor parameters for remnants of such stripped-envelope SNe.
The method using the CNO element abundances will be useful to reveal explosion mechanism of stripped SNe with upcoming microcalorimeter missions such as LEM
(
Kraft et al. 2022
)
, Athena
(
Barret et al. 2018
)
, and Lynx
(
Gaskin et al. 2019
)
.
