Density of shapes of periodic tori in the cubic case
Nguyen-Thi Dang, Nihar Gargava, Jialun Li

Abstract
. Consider the compact orbits of the ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} action of the diagonal group on SL ⁡ ( 3 , ℝ ) / SL ⁡ ( 3 , ℤ ) \mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z}) , the so-called periodic tori. For any periodic torus, the set of periods of the orbit forms a lattice in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . Such a lattice, re-scaled to covolume one, gives a shape point in SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) / SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℤ ) \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}) . We prove that the shapes of all periodic tori are dense in SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) / SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℤ ) \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}) . This implies the density of shapes of the unit groups of totally real cubic orders.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we investigate the shapes of periodic tori on
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
.

Denote by
A
the subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})
of diagonal matrices with positive entries.
Such a group is a locally compact topological group isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
A
periodic torus
is a compact orbit for the left action of
A
on
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
.
The set of periods of such an orbit forms a discrete cocompact subgroup of
A
.
Any cocompact subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be uniquely written as
g\mathbb{Z}^{2}
for some
g\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
The
shape
of a periodic torus is the projection of
g\mathbb{Z}^{2}
in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
See
Definition

2.3
for a more precise definition.

Why study the shapes of periodic tori?

In the
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
case, the diagonal subgroup is one dimensional.
Its left action on
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
is the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle of the modular curve
\mathbb{H}/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
Periodic tori in this case are periodic orbits of the geodesic flow.
Huber
[
16
]
, Margulis
[
19
]
and Sarnak
[
22
]
among others have computed the asymptotic number of periodic geodesics of bounded length.

In our case, periodic tori are cocompact quotients of
A
. We endow
A
with the Euclidean metric induced by the Killing form on the Lie algebra of
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})
.
Now cocompact quotients of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are topologically equivalent to genus 1 surfaces and are equipped with the induced quotient Euclidean metric.

Consequently, periodic tori may be ordered using different parameters; volume or systole - length of the shortest geodesic - for example.
Any such ordering will lead to a different counting problem i.e. to count the number of periodic tori for which the chosen parameter is bounded.
The following are some orderings that were previously used for counting periodic tori :

•

The volume ordering, or geometric ordering
[
12
]
.

•

The systole ordering, or dynamical ordering—the length of the shortest one-parameter periodic orbit
[
26
,
11
,
18
,
9
,
15
]
.

Our main motivation to study the shapes of tori is to understand the relation between volume orderings and systole orderings. For instance, when the shape lies within a compact region of the moduli space, the ratio between the systole and the square root of the volume cannot be too small. This relationship might aid in bridging the gap between counting results with the systole ordering to the volume ordering, offering a unified perspective on these two counting results.

Another motivation comes from number theory.
Let
M
be the subgroup of diagonal matrices with entries in
\{\pm 1\}
.
Periodic tori in
M\backslash\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
correspond to arithmetic objects, namely the unit groups of totally real cubic orders, as explained further in
Section

2.2
.
Therefore the shape of a periodic torus is the shape of the unit group of a totally real cubic order. This bijection is the central theme of the seminal work of
[
13
]
where an “arithmetic ordering” of tori based on this connection is considered instead of geometric parameters like volume or systole. The present paper heavily relies on this bijection.

The space of Weyl chambers:

The double coset
M\backslash\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
is called the space of Weyl chambers, which is a generalisation of the unit tangent bundle of a hyperbolic surface for higher-rank Lie groups (e.g.,
\mathrm{SL}(d,\mathbb{R})
for
d\geq 3
), and the action of
A
generalises the geodesic flow. Periodic tori also occur as compact orbits for
A\curvearrowright M\backslash\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
.

Arithmetically, periodic tori in
M\backslash\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
are in bijection with the unit groups of totally real cubic orders, as explained further in
Section

2.2
.
Hence we will mainly consider periodic tori in
M\backslash\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
.
The case without quotienting by
M
, which requires some modifications, will be treated in
Section

5.4
.

Main result:

The main result of the paper is the following
Theorem

1.1
.
This solves a conjecture put forth in the paper
[
10
]
, where the authors conjectured the following statement, motivated by numerical experiments
and questions of Margulis and Gromov.

Theorem 1.1
.

The shapes of periodic tori in
M\backslash\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
are dense in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

Previously, Cusick
[
8
]
proved the closure of shapes contains an arc in the moduli space.
David-Shapira
[
10
]
provided a description of additional curves that appear in this closure.
Both Cusick and David-Shapira rely on constructing a specific family of cubic polynomials to make cubic orders, which is also a key step in our proof (cf.
Eq.

11
).

With
Theorem

1.1
, we can now convince ourselves that any relationship between systole ordering and volume ordering will not be straightforward.
Also,
Theorem

1.1
tells us that the possible shapes of unit groups of orders of totally real cubic fields can be arbitrarily close to any given shape. This is of independent interest in number theory and is a partial result towards the conjecture stated in a MathOverflow discussion
[
21
]
.

One can also consider the periodic tori of
A\curvearrowright\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
and study their shapes.

It is a
4
-fold covering of the space of Weyl chambers and the action of
A
is equivariant for this covering.
Therefore, periodic tori in
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
are covering of periodic tori in the space of Weyl chamber.
Since the covering may have some non-trivial index, the shapes are not necessarily preserved.
However, we can still prove density of shapes.

Theorem 1.2
.

The shapes of periodic tori in
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
are dense in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

See
Section

5.4
for further details.

Remark 1.3
.

(1)

There is a related notion of a shape studied by Bhargava-Harron
[
2
]
.
Shape points of
[
2
]
take values in
\mathrm{SO}(m)\backslash\mathrm{SL}(m,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(m,\mathbb{Z})
, that is lattices up to scaling and isometry with
m
being
\deg K-1
.
In
[
27
]
, a related notion of a “grid” is introduced to consider the analogous construction in
\mathrm{SL}(m,R)/\mathrm{SL}(m,Z)

Bhargava-Harron and Yifrach study the shape of the lattice coming from the additive group of an order in a number field whereas our work studies the lattices coming from the multiplicative group of units in the order, using Dirichlet’s unit theorem.
In the case of a totally real number field
K
of degree
n
, both our notion and theirs associate a point in
\mathrm{SL}(n-1,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(n-1,\mathbb{Z})
to an order in
K
.

The overloading of the same name is regrettable, but at the same time we fail to find a more appropriate name. We hope that a possible confusion can be quickly
remedied by the use of the adjectives “multiplicative shapes” vs “additive shapes”.

(2)

To our knowledge, there are no known tools to prove an equidistribution statement for our notion of shapes.
However, as conjectured in
[
21
]
, we expect similar equidistribution results to also hold in some natural orderings for totally real cubic orders.
Any input from the community in pursuing this direction is very welcome.

(3)

Corso–Rodriguez Hertz in a recent preprint
[
6
]
prove with different methods that the set of shapes is unbounded, which is a consequence of
Theorem

1.1
.
They also present a comparison of the approaches.

Sketch of the proof:

By a classical observation (cf.
[
12
]
), the periods of a periodic torus are in correspondence with the units of a totally real cubic order.
The proof of the main theorem is based on a special family of cubic orders already studied by Cassels
[
3
]
and Ankeny–Brauer–Chowla
[
1
]
. The family of cubic orders considered here is

(2)

\cal O=\frac{\mathbb{Z}[X]}{\langle f(X)\rangle},\text{ where }f(X)=X(X-a_{1})(X-a_{2})-1,

for some integers
0<a_{1}<a_{2}
in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Using the “best lower bound” of Cusick
[
7
]
between the regulator and the discriminant in the cubic case, we compute the generators of the unit group of these orders in
Proposition

3.4
.
As already observed by Cusick
[
8
]
and David-Shapira
[
10
]
, this provides a family of shapes whose projections on
\mathbb{H}/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
are concentrated on the geodesic line between the square torus and the hexagonal torus in the moduli space of flat tori
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. (See
Fig.

1(a)
).
The proof of the compactness of these shapes is given in
Section

3
. This makes it seem like an obstruction to show density of shapes, however the final proof of
Theorem

1.1
uses this compactness as an essential step instead of an obstruction.

In order to obtain more shapes, one can consider finite index subrings / suborders of the above cubic orders. Namely, for an integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and any cubic order from the above family
\cal O
, one can try to work with
\cal O_{n}=\mathbb{Z}+n\cal O
. Overall,
\cal O_{n}^{\times}
depends highly on the prime factorization of
n
.
Assuming that
n=p_{1}^{r_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{r_{k}}
is the prime factorization for
n
, one can separately analyse the orders
\cal O_{i}=\mathbb{Z}+p_{i}^{r_{i}}\cal O
to get
\cal O_{n}=\bigcap_{i}\cal O_{i}
(see
Lemma

4.1
). To control
\cal O_{n}^{\times}
, we try to work with a fixed set of primes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
and increase the exponents
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}
. When
\cal O
is fixed and
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}
are varied, it seems to be a difficult problem in
p
-adic Diophantine approximation to show the density of the shapes of
(\cal O_{n})^{\times}
.

What was needed to prove
Theorem

1.1
was to impose, for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, local conditions on the coefficients of the polynomial
f_{N}(X)
up to a depth
N
(that is, congruence conditions modulo
p_{1}^{N}p_{2}^{N}p_{3}^{N}
) as we do in
Section

5.3
.
The outcome of these congruence conditions is twofold. Firstly, in
Proposition

4.4
, we are able to explicitly compute the unit group
(\cal O_{p_{1}^{c}p_{2}^{d}p_{3}^{r}})^{\times}
, and hence its shape, for
0\leq c,d,r\leq N
whenever these congruence conditions are satisfied. Secondly, if
\cal O^{\times}
could be fixed, this gives roughly
\sim N^{3}
shape points that are finite index sublattices
of
\cal O^{\times}
.
We prove that the closure of such shape points satisfy an invariance under the diagonal group and contain a horospherical piece.
By using techniques similar to the “banana trick” argument due to Margulis (see
[
17
,
23
]
), this implies the density of shapes when we restrict our argument to a stationary sequence of shape points of
\cal O^{\times}
. This is where the proof uses the compactness of the shapes
\cal O^{\times}
when the polynomial
f
is allowed to vary by changing
a_{1},a_{2}
in
Eq.

11
satisfying our local conditions modulo
(p_{1}p_{2}p_{3})^{N}
. The exact choice of
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
is not critical for our proof and we work with the primes
2,3,5
(but at least 3 primes seem to be necessary).

To sum up, the family of orders that allows us to prove the density is
\mathbb{Z}+2^{c}3^{d}5^{r}\cal O
, where
c,d,r\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,N]
and
\cal O=\mathbb{Z}[X]/\langle f_{N}(X)\rangle
where

N
goes to infinity.

1.1.
Some side remarks

(1)

For the higher dimensional case of
\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
with
n\geq 4
, there exist similar inequalities as Cusick’s inequality in cubic case such as
[
20
]
and
[
24
]
. But the bound is not very good. With the same method, we can only locate the lattice
\cal O^{\times}
up to finite index. Therefore, our method cannot be directly generalized to higher dimension, although we can obtain some unboundedness result of shapes.

(2)

It is interesting that we can add non-Archimedean local conditions to obtain a collection of shapes that admit diagonal and horospherical invariance, which is the key to the density.
We believe that these techniques indicate a deeper arithmetic structure of shapes of orders which might shed more light on the distribution of shapes of units of orders in the future.

(3)

Our work was born out of efforts to understand the effect of considering Hecke neighbours of periodic tori. We owe it to the work of
[
25
]
as an inspiration
to investigate this direction. They use Hecke correspondence on periodic tori to construct interesting limit measures of diagonally invariant probability measures.

However, our main direction of investigation is the space of shapes (
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
in our case) and not the space of the periodic tori themselves (
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
in our case). The exact same technique therefore does not apply. Indeed, we need to use suborders
\mathbb{Z}+2^{c}3^{d}5^{r}\cal O
with at least three different primes to get our result and a single prime does not suffice. Another main difference is that we make no use of equidistribution of Hecke points
[
4
]
.

We reemphasize that the idea of using the polynomials
X(X-a_{1})(X-a_{2})-1
is itself very classical and goes back to Cassels, Shapira and many other authors.

Background

2.1.
Number theoretic preliminaries

Let
(K,\sigma)
be a totally real cubic field with ordered real embeddings, where
K
denotes the cubic field and
\sigma:=(\sigma_{i})_{1\leq i\leq 3}
is a fixed ordering of the three real embeddings
\sigma_{1},\ \sigma_{2},\ \sigma_{3}
of
K
.
Let
\cal O
be an order in
K
, which is a finite index subring of the ring of integers
\cal O_{K}
.
The map
\sigma
gives an embedding of
\cal O
into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as a rank-three free abelian group, that is, a rank-three lattice.
The squared covolume of
\sigma(\cal O)
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the
discriminant
of the order
\cal O
denoted by
\disc(\mathcal{O})
.

Example 2.1
.

Fix a monic irreducible polynomial
f(X)\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
of degree 3 with three real roots. Then
K=\mathbb{Q}[X]/\langle f(X)\rangle
is a cubic field in which
\cal O=\mathbb{Z}[X]/\langle f(X)\rangle
is an order. The discriminant of this order is given by

(3)

\disc(\cal O)=\prod_{i<j\in\{1,2,3\}}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})^{2},

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
are the three roots of
f
.

Let
\cal O^{\times}
be the subset of invertible elements in
\cal O
, which is a multiplicative group. We recall an important theorem concerning the structure of
\cal O^{\times}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

(
Dirichlet’s unit theorem)

Given an order
\cal O\subseteq\cal O_{K}
for a totally real cubic field
K
, the group
\cal O^{\times}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times\{\pm 1\}
. That is, there exist two generators
\alpha,\beta\in\cal O^{\times}
such that

(4)

\cal O^{\times}=\langle\alpha,\beta,\pm 1\rangle.

For any
x\in\cal O^{\times}
, we define the map
\psi
by

(5)

\psi(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\log|\sigma_{1}(x)|&&(0)\\
&\log|\sigma_{2}(x)|&\\
(0)&&\log|\sigma_{3}(x)|\end{pmatrix}.

Since
x\in\cal O^{\times}
, we know that the product
\sigma_{1}(x)\sigma_{2}(x)\sigma_{3}(x)=\pm 1
, hence the image of
\psi
is inside the hyperplane
\mathfrak{a}=\{\mathrm{diag}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),\ x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\ x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=0\}
.
We know by Dirichlet’s unit theorem that the image
\sigma(\cal O^{\times})
is a rank-
2

\mathbb{Z}
-lattice under multiplication and hence maps to a
2
-lattice in
\mathfrak{a}
via
\psi
.
We fix one identification of
\mathfrak{a}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
from now on. We denote the image of
\psi(\cal O^{\times})
by
\Lambda(\cal O)
, a
2
-lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The covolume of the lattice
\psi(\cal O^{\times})
in
\mathfrak{a}
is
\sqrt{3}
times the
regulator

R(\cal O)
of the order
\cal O
.

Definition 2.3

(Definition of shapes)
.

For any
2
-lattice
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there exists an element
g\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
such that
g\,\mathbb{Z}^{2}=c\cdot\Lambda

.
We denote by
[\Lambda]:=g\,\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
its shape, which is in the space of covolume-one
2
-lattices
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

The

of
\psi(\cal O^{\times})
is defined to be
[\Lambda(\cal O,\sigma)]
.
In the rest of the paper, for every totally real cubic field we consider, the choice of ordered real embeddings
\sigma
will be fixed.
Abusing notation, we omit
\sigma
and denote the shape point by

[\Lambda(\cal O)]
.

2.2.
Units of orders and periods of periodic tori

Using the ring structure of the order
\cal O
, we can realize
\cal O^{\times}
as a commutative subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Similarly as above, the map
\Psi

\Psi(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}(x)&&(0)\\
&\sigma_{2}(x)&\\
(0)&&\sigma_{3}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
\psi=\log|\Psi|

The action of
\cal O^{\times}

\Psi(x)\sigma(y)=\sigma(xy)

This action is linear and preserves the

\sigma(\cal O)

For every oriented basis

\mathbb{Z}

h_{y}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{disc}(\cal O)^{1/6}}\big(\sigma(y_{1}),\sigma(y_{2}),\sigma(y_{3})\big)

h_{y}\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{R})

We construct a map
\gamma

\cal O^{\times}

For all

\gamma(x)=h_{y}^{-1}\Psi(x)h_{y}.

A different choice of oriented basis only changes

Lemma 2.5
.

For all orders
\cal O

h_{y}^{-1}(MA)h_{y}\cap\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z})=\gamma(\cal O^{\times})\cap\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R}).

From the construction, we know that

is a subset of

For the other direction, notice that
\mathrm{disc}(\mathcal{O})^{-\frac{1}{6}}\sigma(\cal O)=h_{y}\mathbb{Z}^{3}

Let

\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z})\cap h_{y}^{-1}(MA)h_{y}
.
Since

Consequently

The same argument also works for

Lemma 2.6
.

The orbit

Remark 2.7
.

(1)

Most suborders of a given order will give, up to homothety, a different oriented basis for the
3
-lattice.
As a consequence, they will correspond to another periodic torus, i.e. an abelian subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
of dimension
2
.

(2)

The above construction starts from a totally real cubic order and yields a periodic torus.
The correspondence may be generalised to orders of totally real polynomials of higher degree
(Cf.
[
12
]
).

(3)

Two different periodic tori can have the same set of periods, hence the same shape.

Indeed, consider any two ideals
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
of the maximal order
\cal O_{K}\subseteq K
of a totally real cubic field
K
. When viewed as points in
M\backslash\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
,
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}
are in the same
A
-orbit if and only if
\mathcal{I}=\alpha\mathcal{J}
for some element
\alpha\in K^{\times}
. Hence, if
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}
are in two different ideal classes, they are not in the same diagonal orbit. However, one can check that their shapes are the same since

\{\alpha\in K^{\times}\mid\alpha\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}\}=\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}=\{\alpha\in K^{\times}\mid\alpha\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}\}.

(4)

We consider
M\backslash\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
instead of
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
for the following reason: the periods of periodic tori in
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
correspond to totally positive units, that is
\cal O^{\times,+}=\{x\in\cal O^{\times}:\ \sigma_{j}(x)>0\text{ for }j=1,2,3\}
. From a number-theoretic perspective, the shape of
\cal O^{\times}
is more natural, which aligns with the setting of
M\backslash\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
.

For completion, we will show the density of shapes of periodic tori in
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
in
Section

5.4
.

4.

For any three distinct prime numbers

Lemma 4.1

We have

\mathbb{Z}+p_{1}^{c}p_{2}^{d}p_{3}^{r}\cal O=\left(\mathbb{Z}+p_{1}^{c}\cal O\right)\cap\left(\mathbb{Z}+p_{2}^{d}\cal O\right)\cap\left(\mathbb{Z}+p_{3}^{r}\cal O\right).

It is clear that

(52)

\mathbb{Z}+p_{1}^{c}p_{2}^{d}p_{3}^{r}\cal O\subseteq\left(\mathbb{Z}+p_{1}^{c}\cal O\right)\cap\left(\mathbb{Z}+p_{2}^{d}\cal O\right)\cap\left(\mathbb{Z}+p_{3}^{r}\cal O\right).

lies in the set on the right side of

\displaystyle b\in(a_{1}+p_{1}^{c}\cal O)\cap(a_{2}+p_{2}^{d}\cal O)\cap(a_{3}+p_{3}^{r}\cal O).

\displaystyle a\in(a_{1}+p_{1}^{c}\mathbb{Z})\cap(a_{2}+p_{2}^{d}\mathbb{Z})\cap(a_{3}+p_{3}^{r}\mathbb{Z}),

Proposition 4.4
.

Let

be three distinct primes.
If one of

or

.

Then there exists

\displaystyle\cal O_{c,d,r}^{\times}

Remark 4.5
.

From the geometric perspective, taking suborders is equivalent to taking Hecke neighbors of a periodic torus. The shapes of new tori are given by the shapes of units of suborders.

5.
Density of a family of shapes

In this section we set the triplet of primes
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})=(2,3,5)
in
Proposition

4.4
.
We first calculate the sub-lattice
L_{c,d,r}
for all

.
In

, we express

as the image of a sub-lattice of

This expression motivates us to study, up to a fixed matrix action, the family of shapes of

5.2
.
Finally, in the last section, we prove

5.1.
The suborders matrix equations for

Let us try to evaluate the values of

.

f(X)=X(X-a_{1})(X-a_{2})-1\equiv X^{3}-X^{2}-1\pmod{2}.

This is an irreducible polynomial. We can then calculate that in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[X]/\langle X^{3}+X^{2}+1\rangle
the multiplicative order of

is 7.
In
\mathbb{Z}[X]/\langle X^{3}-X^{2}-1\rangle

(72)

X^{7}=3+2X+4X^{2}\text{ and }X^{14}=41+28X+60X^{2}.

Thus

and

lemma

Similarly, for

The smallest degree polynomial of the type

Let us denote the set

(73)

L_{c,d,r}=\left\{\begin{tabular}[]{c|ccl}\hbox{\multirowsetup$\begin{pmatrix}m\\
n\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}$}&$m+n$&$\equiv\,0$&$\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c-1}}$\\

Lemma 5.1
.

Denote by

g=8\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&1\\
0&-1\end{array}\right)\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}-1&1\\
2&-1\end{array}\right).

Then for every
c\geq 4
and
d\geq 2
and
r\geq 1
, the sub-lattice
L_{c,d,r}
as defined in
Eq.

73
satisfies

L_{c,d,r}=g\begin{pmatrix}3^{d-2}&0\\
0&5^{r-1}\end{pmatrix}\bigg\{\binom{x}{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\;\bigg|\;3^{d-2}x-5^{r-1}y\equiv 0\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c-4}}\bigg\}.

Proof.

By
Eq.

73
,
L_{c,d,r}
is the set of integer vectors
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
that solve a rank-two equation in
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, i.e., such that

\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&1\\
1&-2\\
2&-1\end{array}\right)\binom{m}{n}\in\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}7\cdot 2^{c-1}&&(0)\\
&8\cdot 3^{d-1}&\\
(0)&&24\cdot 5^{r-1}\end{array}\right)\mathbb{Z}^{3}.

We compute the Smith normal form on the
3\times 2
matrix on the left and denote by

U=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}0&-1&1\\
0&2&-1\\
1&1&-1\end{array}\right),\quad M=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&1\\
1&-2\\
2&-1\end{array}\right),\quad V=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&1\\
0&-1\end{array}\right).

Then
U\in\mathrm{GL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
and
V\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
and

M=U^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&0\\
0&3\\
0&0\end{array}\right)V^{-1}.

Now,
V^{-1}L_{c,d,r}
is the set of
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that there exists
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
such that

(74)

\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&0\\
0&3\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\binom{m}{n}=U\left(\begin{array}[]{r}7\cdot 2^{c-1}\;x\\
8\cdot 3^{d-1}\;y\\
24\cdot 5^{r-1}\;z\end{array}\right).

The first two lines of the matrix equation
Eq.

74
yield the following rank-
2
linear equation

(75)

\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&0\\
0&3\end{array}\right)\binom{m}{n}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}-1&1\\
2&-1\end{array}\right)\binom{8\cdot 3^{d-1}\;y}{24\cdot 5^{r-1}\;z}.

Hence using that
d\geq 2
, we deduce that

V^{-1}L_{c,d,r}\subset 8\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}-1&1\\
2&-1\end{array}\right)\begin{pmatrix}3^{d-2}&0\\
0&5^{r-1}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

The last line of the matrix equation
Eq.

74
is a hyperplane equation in
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
.
It will induce a sub-lattice in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
which will be mapped to
L_{c,d,r}
by an element in
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})
.
By the matrix equation,
(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
satisfy

(76)

7\cdot 2^{c-1}\;x+8\cdot 3^{d-1}\;y-24\cdot 5^{r-1}\;z=0.

Equivalently,

-7\cdot 2^{c-4}\;x=3^{d-1}\;y-3\cdot 5^{r-1}\;z.

Since
c\geq 4
and using that
3
and
7\cdot 2^{c-4}
are coprime and
d\geq 2
, the integers
(y,z)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
solve the congruence equation

3^{d-2}\;y-5^{r-1}\;z\equiv 0\;\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c-4}}.

Finally, combining the hyperplane relation with the rank
2
part, we deduce that

L_{c,d,r}=8V\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}-1&1\\
2&-1\end{array}\right)\begin{pmatrix}3^{d-2}&0\\
0&5^{r-1}\end{pmatrix}\bigg\{\binom{y}{z}\;\bigg|\;3^{d-2}y-5^{r-1}z\equiv 0\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c-4}}\bigg\}.

∎

5.2.
Density of shapes for the solutions of the
(2,3,5)
-equations

For all
c,d,r\in\mathbb{N}
with
c\geq 4
,
d\geq 2
and
r\geq 1
, denote by

\Lambda_{c,d,r}=\begin{pmatrix}3^{d-2}&0\\
0&5^{r-1}\end{pmatrix}\bigg\{\binom{y}{z}\;\bigg|\;3^{d-2}y-5^{r-1}z\equiv 0\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c-4}}\bigg\}.

Note that

L_{c,d,r}=g\Lambda_{c,d,r}
, where
g
is defined in
Lemma

5.1
.
The sets
L_{c,d,r}
and
\Lambda_{c,d,r}
are auxiliary lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that, up to a linear transformation, give the sets of periods of periodic tori, as explained in the proof of
Theorem

1.1
in
Section

5.3
.
For now, we prove that these lattices are dense.

Proposition 5.2
.

The collection of points
\{[\Lambda_{c,d,r}]\}_{c,d,r\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
is dense.

Proof.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
be the closure of
\{[\Lambda_{c,d,r}],\ c,d,r\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
We will first prove that
\Omega
is invariant under the diagonal flow
\{a(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
,
then we prove it contains the horospherical piece
u([0,1])\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, where

a(t)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{t/2}&0\\
0&e^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\,,\,\,u(s)=\begin{pmatrix}1&s\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Then we can apply Margulis’ “banana trick”
to conclude the density of
\Omega
. (See for example
[
17
, Proposition 2.2.1 and Proposition 2.4.8]
or
[
23
, Theorem 1.4 ]
).

Diagonal invariance:

Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
We want to show that
\Omega
is invariant under
a(t)
. It is sufficient to show that for every
[\Lambda_{c,d,r}]
where
c,d,r\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
a(t)[\Lambda_{c,d,r}]\in\Omega
. By a diagonalization argument, it suffices to prove that there exist
(c_{n},d_{n},r_{n})
such that
[\Lambda_{c_{n},d_{n},r_{n}}]\rightarrow a(t)[\Lambda_{c,d,r}]
.
Note that

a(t)\Lambda_{c,d,r}=\begin{pmatrix}3^{d-2}e^{t/2}&0\\
0&5^{r-1}e^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}\bigg\{\binom{y}{z}\;\bigg|\;3^{d-2}y-5^{r-1}z\equiv 0\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c-4}}\bigg\}.

We will choose
c_{n},d_{n},r_{n}
such that the rank-
2
lattice solving the hyperplane equation
3^{d_{n}-2}y-5^{r_{n}-1}z\equiv 0\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c_{n}-4}}

remains satisfied and such that we also have the following convergence in
\mathrm{PGL}(2,\mathbb{R})
:

(77)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}3^{d_{n}-2}&0\\
0&5^{r_{n}-1}\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{pmatrix}3^{d_{n}-2}/5^{r_{n}-1}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\;n\rightarrow+\infty\;]{}\begin{pmatrix}e^{t}3^{d-2}/5^{r-1}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\sim\begin{pmatrix}3^{d-2}e^{t/2}&0\\
0&5^{r-1}e^{-t/2}\end{pmatrix}.

Let
c_{n}=c
for all
n\geq 1
.
Denote by
s_{1}
(resp.
s_{2}
) the order of
3
(resp.
5
) in
(\mathbb{Z}/7\cdot 2^{c-4}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.
To preserve the hyperplane equation,
(d_{n},r_{n})
must satisfy the following conditions.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cr}3^{d_{n}}\equiv 3^{d}&\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c-4}}\\
5^{r_{n}}\equiv 5^{r}&\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c-4}}\end{array}\right.\Longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{cr}d_{n}\equiv d&\pmod{s_{1}}\\
r_{n}\equiv r&\pmod{s_{2}}\end{array}\right..

Denote by
p_{n}=(d_{n}-d)/s_{1}
and
q_{n}=(r_{n}-r)/s_{2}
.
Taking logarithms in (
77
), we deduce that the positive integers
(p_{n},q_{n})
must equivalently satisfy the following convergence.

(78)

\displaystyle p_{n}\cdot s_{1}\log(3)-q_{n}\cdot s_{2}\log(5)-t\xrightarrow[\;n\rightarrow+\infty\;]{}0.

Since
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are positive integers, the ratio of
s_{1}\log(3)
and
s_{2}\log(5)
is irrational.
Hence every forward orbit of the translation
s_{1}\log(3)
in
\mathbb{R}/(s_{2}\log(5)\mathbb{Z})
is dense. In particular, as
p_{n},q_{n}
vary in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, the left-hand side in (
78
) starting from
-t
visits infinitely often any neighbourhood of
0
.
Therefore, we may pick a sequence of positive integers
(p_{n},q_{n})
with
p_{n},q_{n}\rightarrow+\infty
, satisfying the convergence (
78
).
Then
d_{n}=d+s_{1}p_{n}
and
r_{n}=r+s_{2}q_{n}
are positive integers and we have constructed a sequence
\big([\Lambda_{c,d_{n},r_{n}}]\big)_{n\geq 1}
that converges towards
a(t)[\Lambda_{c,d,r}]
.

Horospherical piece:

For every integer
c\geq 3
, denote by
S_{c}
the finite set of representatives in
[0,7\cdot 2^{c}]\cap\mathbb{N}
of the multiplicative group generated by
5
in
\big(\mathbb{Z}/(7\cdot 2^{c})\mathbb{Z}\big)^{\times}
, namely,

S_{c}=\Big\{f\in[0,7\cdot 2^{c}]\cap\mathbb{N}\;\Big|\;\exists r\in\mathbb{N}\;\text{such that}\;5^{r}\equiv f\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c}}\Big\}.

The first step is to prove that as
c\rightarrow+\infty
the set
\frac{S_{c}}{7\cdot 2^{c}}
becomes dense in
[0,1]
.

Then we prove that for every
c\in\mathbb{N}
with
c\geq 3
,

(79)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{S_{c}}{7\cdot 2^{c}}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{2}\subset\Omega.

By combining the two steps in a diagonal argument, we can deduce that
\Omega
contains the horospherical piece
u([0,1])\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Let us first calculate the cardinality of
S_{c}
, i.e., the order of
5
in the multiplicative group
\big(\mathbb{Z}/7\cdot 2^{c}\mathbb{Z}\big)^{\times}
.
By induction, starting from the fact that
5^{2^{1}}-1=2^{3}\cdot 3
and using that for every
k\geq 1

5^{(2^{k})}-1=\Big(5^{(2^{k-1})}-1\Big)\Big(5^{(2^{k-1})}+1\Big),

we deduce that for every
c\geq 3,
the order of
5
in
(\mathbb{Z}/2^{c}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is
2^{c-2}
.
Furthermore,
5
is a generator of
(\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
and has order
2\cdot 3
.
Therefore,
S_{c}
is a set of
3\cdot 2^{c-2}
elements.
When
r
is even (resp. odd), then
5^{r}\equiv 1\pmod{2^{3}}
and
5^{r}\equiv 1,2,4\pmod{7}
(resp.
\equiv 5\pmod{2^{3}}
and
\equiv 3,5,6\pmod{7}
).
By definition, any element in
S_{c}
will satisfy either the even system of congruence equations, or the odd one.
By the Chinese remainder theorem, there are
3\cdot 2^{c-3}
integer solutions to the even system of congruences in
(0,7\cdot 2^{c})
, and likewise for the odd one.
Consequently, the two systems of congruences describe all the elements in
S_{c}
.
Indeed, by explicit computation, for every integer
c\geq 3

S_{c}=\big\{7\cdot 2^{3}k+j\;\big|\;j\in\{1,5,9,13,25,45\}\text{ and }k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\cap[0,2^{c-3}-1]\big\}.

Hence
\min S_{c}=1
and
\max S_{c}=7\cdot 2^{c}-11
and the gaps between consecutive elements are at most
20
.
Consequently,
S_{c}/(7\cdot 2^{c})
is dense in
[0,1]
as
c
goes to infinity.

Now, for the second step of the proof, we fix an integer
c\geq 3
and an element
f\in S_{c}
.
Let us denote by
s_{1}
(resp.
s_{2}
) the order of
3
(resp.
5
) in the multiplicative group
\big(\mathbb{Z}/7\cdot 2^{c}\mathbb{Z}\big)^{\times}
.
We want to find a sequence of positive integers
(d_{n},r_{n})
such that

\big([\Lambda_{c+4,d_{n}+2,r_{n}+1}]\big)_{n\geq 1}
converges towards
u\big(\frac{f}{7\cdot 2^{c}}\big)\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Denote by
r_{f}
an integer such that
5^{r_{f}}\equiv f\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c}}
.
Assume
d_{n},r_{n}
satisfy the following equivalent conditions.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cr}3^{d_{n}}\equiv 1&\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c}}\\
5^{r_{n}}\equiv f&\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c}}\end{array}\right.\Longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}d_{n}\equiv 0&\pmod{s_{1}}\\
r_{n}\equiv r_{f}&\pmod{s_{2}}\end{array}\right..

Then
3^{d_{n}}y-5^{r_{n}}z\equiv y-fz\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c}}
for all
(y,z)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Hence the solutions to the hyperplane equation for
\Lambda_{c+4,d_{n}+2,r_{n}+1}
, noting that
7\cdot 2^{c}|y-fz
if and only if
y\in 7\cdot 2^{c}\mathbb{Z}+fz
can be written as follows.

\bigg\{\binom{y}{z}\;\bigg|\;3^{d_{n}}y-5^{r_{n}}z\equiv y-fz\equiv 0\pmod{7\cdot 2^{c}}\bigg\}=\begin{pmatrix}7\cdot 2^{c}&f\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

As a consequence,

\Lambda_{c+4,d_{n}+2,r_{n}+1}=\begin{pmatrix}3^{d_{n}}&0\\
0&5^{r_{n}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}7\cdot 2^{c}&f\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{2}=7\cdot 2^{c}\cdot 3^{d_{n}}\begin{pmatrix}1&f/(7\cdot 2^{c})\\
0&\frac{5^{r_{n}}}{7\cdot 2^{c}\cdot 3^{d_{n}}}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

To end the proof, it is sufficient to choose
r_{n}\in r_{f}+s_{2}\mathbb{N}
and
d_{n}\in s_{1}\mathbb{N}
such that the following convergence holds.

(80)

\displaystyle\frac{5^{r_{n}}}{7\cdot 2^{c}\cdot 3^{d_{n}}}\xrightarrow[\;n\rightarrow+\infty]{}1.

Set
p_{n}=d_{n}/s_{1}
and
q_{n}=(r_{n}-r_{f})/s_{2}
. Taking logarithms, we deduce the equivalent convergence in
(p_{n},q_{n})
:

r_{f}\log(5)+q_{n}\cdot s_{2}\log(5)-p_{n}\cdot s_{1}\log(3)-\log(7\cdot 2^{c})\xrightarrow[\;n\rightarrow+\infty]{}0.

As in the proof of diagonal invariance, the forward orbit starting at
r_{f}\log(5)-\log(7\cdot 2^{c})
for the translation
s_{2}\log(5)
in
\mathbb{R}/s_{1}\log(3)\mathbb{Z}
is dense, so there exist sequences
p_{n},q_{n}\rightarrow+\infty
such that the above convergence holds.
∎

5.3.
Density of shapes for periodic tori

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N\geq 10
, we choose a pair of integers
(a_{1,N},a_{2,N})
such that
a_{1,N},a_{2,N}-a_{1,N}>C
for
C
from
Lemma

3.5
and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ cccccl}a_{1,N}&\equiv&0&\equiv&a_{2,N}-1&\pmod{2^{N}}\\
a_{1,N}-1&\equiv&0&\equiv&a_{2,N}&\pmod{3^{N}}\\
a_{1,N}-1&\equiv&0&\equiv&a_{2,N}-1&\pmod{5^{N}}\end{array}\right.

Recall that
b_{1}=\theta_{0}
and
b_{2}=\theta_{0}-a_{1}
. Applying
Lemma

3.5
and enlarging
C
if necessary, we know that up to the same scaling
\psi(b_{1})=v_{1}
,
\psi(b_{2})=v_{2}
with
v_{1},v_{2}
given in
Lemma

3.5
. Moreover, the matrix
g_{N}
with
(\psi(b_{1}),\psi(b_{2}))=(u_{1},u_{2})g_{N}
and its inverse
g_{N}^{-1}
is bounded in
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})^{+}
, where
(u_{1},u_{2})
is a fixed oriented basis of
\mathfrak{a}
from its identification with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Therefore the lattice is represented by
[\Lambda(\cal O^{(a_{1,N},a_{2,N})})]=[g_{N}\mathbb{Z}^{2}]
.

From
Proposition

4.4
, we know that for
c,d,r\leq N

(81)

\bigg[\Lambda\Big(\cal O^{(a_{1,N},a_{2,N})}_{c,d,r}\Big)\bigg]=\bigg[\Big\{m\psi(b_{1})+n\psi(b_{2})\;\Big|\begin{pmatrix}m\\
n\end{pmatrix}\in L_{c,d,r}\Big\}\bigg]=[g_{N}L_{c,d,r}].

From
Lemma

5.1
, we further obtain that

(82)

[\Lambda(\cal O^{(a_{1,N},a_{2,N})}_{c,d,r})]=[g_{N}g\Lambda_{c,d,r}]=g_{N}g[\Lambda_{c,d,r}],

where the action of
g_{N}g
is through
\mathrm{PGL(2,\mathbb{R})}
.

Due to the uniform boundedness of
g_{N}g
for all
N
, we are able to choose a subsequence of
\{g_{N}g\}_{N\geq 10}
that converges to some fixed
g_{0}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, up to normalization by the determinant. Therefore, by
Proposition

5.2
, the set of shapes

(83)

\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\{[\Lambda(\cal O^{(a_{1,N},a_{2,N})}_{c,d,r})],\ c,d,r\leq N\}=\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\{g_{N}g[\Lambda_{c,d,r}],\ c,d,r\leq N\}

is also dense in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
∎

5.4.
Density of shapes of positive units

As mentioned in
Remark

2.7
-
4
, the shapes of periodic tori of
A
-orbits in
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})

form the set

\{[\psi(\cal O^{\times,+})]\;|{\cal O\text{ a totally real cubic order}}\}
. One can observe without much difficulty that for the totally positive units, in
Proposition

3.4
we would get

\mathcal{O}^{\times,+}=\langle\theta_{0},(\theta_{0}-a_{1})^{2}\rangle.

With this, we get that the totally positive units of the order
\cal O_{c,d,r}
, in the notation of
Proposition

4.4
, are given by

\cal O_{c,d,r}^{\times,+}=\left\{b_{1}^{m}b_{2}^{2n}\;\bigg|(m,n)\in L_{c,d,r}^{\prime}\right\},

where
L_{c,d,r}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is the lattice of pairs
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
satisfying the congruence conditions

\begin{array}[]{ cccl}m+2n&\equiv&0&\pmod{l_{1}p_{1}^{\max(c-j_{1},0)}},\\
m-4n&\equiv&0&\pmod{l_{2}p_{2}^{\max(d-j_{2},0)}},\\
2m-2n&\equiv&0&\pmod{l_{3}p_{3}^{\max(r-j_{3},0)}}.\end{array}

This leads to the following coincidence.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\cal O_{c,d,r}
be as defined in
Section

4
with
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})=(2,3,5)
.
Then the following shape points are equal in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

[\psi(\cal O_{c,d,r}^{\times})]=[\psi(\cal O_{c,d,r}^{\times,+})].

Proof.

When we substitute
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})=(2,3,5)
as in the discussion at the beginning of
Section

5
, we get the same values of
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},l_{1},l_{2},l_{3}
as in the case of
L_{c,d,r}
given in
Eq.

73
. Then, observe that
L_{c,d,r}
and
L^{\prime}_{c,d,r}
differ only by the resubstitution
n\mapsto 2n
. This gives us

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)L_{c,d,r}^{\prime}\subseteq L_{c,d,r}.

However, because
l_{3}=24
, the third condition in
Eq.

73
shows that
n
must be even and in fact

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)L_{c,d,r}^{\prime}=L_{c,d,r}.

In fact, what this means for
\cal O^{\times}_{c,d,r}
is that if
b_{1},b_{2}
are as in
Proposition

4.4
, then
b_{1}^{m}b_{2}^{n}\in\cal O^{\times}_{c,d,r}\Rightarrow b_{1}^{m}b_{2}^{n}\in\cal O^{\times,+}_{c,d,r}
. Since the map
\psi:\cal O^{\times}\rightarrow\mathfrak{a}
defined in
Eq.

5
satisfies
\psi(b_{1}^{m}b_{2}^{n})=\psi(-b_{1}^{m}b_{2}^{n})
, we can conclude the statement of the lemma.
∎

With these facts, the proof of
Theorem

1.1
adapts directly to prove the following statement.

Theorem 5.4
.

The set of shapes of periodic tori for the left action of the subgroup of diagonal matrices with positive entries
A\subseteq\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})
on
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
is dense in
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
