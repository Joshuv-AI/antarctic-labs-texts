Frailty Among the Older Adults in Bangladesh: A Comparative Analysis of Rural and Urban Populations
Authors: Mohammad Kamal Hossain, Shihab Mostafa, Sabbir Hossain, Nazrul Islam, Mohammed Taj Uddin, Mohammad Meshbahur Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe increasing incidence of frailty among older adults in Bangladesh is a significant public health concern. This study aims to compare the level and determinants of frailty among older adults living in rural and urban areas of Bangladesh.MethodsA cross‐sectional survey of 944 older adults aged 55 years and above, conducted in the Sylhet District of Bangladesh between 2022 and 2023, selected through a two‐stage random sampling method in urban areas and a multistage random approach in rural areas. The health status of participants was assessed using a 33‐indicator Frailty Index (FI33), which categorized individuals into four groups: good health (0 < = FI33 < = 12), mild frailty (0.12 < FI33 < = 0.24), moderate frailty (0.24 < FI33 < = 0.36), and severe frailty (FI33 > 0.36). Data analysis involved descriptive statistics, Pearson χ 2 tests, and multinomial logistic regression.ResultsThe prevalence of moderate to severe frailty was higher in rural areas (57.6%) compared to urban areas (49%). This difference was statistically significant (χ² = 37.09, p < 0.001). Rural older adults also had a higher likelihood of severe frailty than urban older adults (AOR: 1.80, 95% CI: 0.92–3.52). Age was a significant predictor of frailty, with adults aged 65 years and above having a much higher risk (AOR: 9.06, 95% CI: 4.33–18.94). Women were also more likely to experience moderate frailty than men (AOR: 4.43, 95% CI: 2.27–8.62).ConclusionThese findings highlight the need for targeted interventions in rural areas and among older women to address frailty in Bangladesh's aging population.Keywords: aging population, Bangladesh, frailty, frailty index, rural–urban differences

Introduction

Frailty is widely recognized as a geriatric syndrome characterized by reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes such as disability, hospitalization, and mortality [1]. It reflects the cumulative decline of multiple physiological systems that occurs with aging. Rather than being a single disease, frailty represents a biological state in which older adults have a lower capacity to cope with physical or psychological stressors [2]. In many South Asian countries, geriatric healthcare services remain limited, which means many older adults suffer from psychological issues, high blood pressure, heart disease, vulnerability, pain, vision loss, obesity, and hearing loss [3, 4, 5]. Therefore, many older adults experience poverty and restricted access to healthcare, and frailty has become an important public health concern [6]. In addition, differences in living conditions between rural and urban areas may play an important role in shaping frailty among older adults, as health outcomes are strongly influenced by socioeconomic and environmental contexts [7]. Rural populations in South Asia often face limited access to healthcare services, lower socioeconomic conditions, and reduced health awareness, which increase their vulnerability to adverse health outcomes [8]. In contrast, urban populations generally benefit from relatively better infrastructure, services, and living conditions, contributing to improved access to healthcare and other essential resources [9]. These disparities contribute to variations in health outcomes and frailty patterns, making rural–urban comparison an important area of investigation in South Asian ageing research [7].

Previous studies in South Asia indicate that frailty is a common and growing concern among older adults, although the prevalence varies across countries. For example, the prevalence of frailty has been reported as 38.8% in India, 15.2% in Malaysia, 55.4% in Pakistan, and 46.2% in Nepal [10, 11, 12, 13]. Literature found that most of the risk factors are occupation, education level, older adults' age, loneliness, respiratory issues, falls, fatigue, lack of sleep, and fractures [14].

In South Asia, specifically in Bangladesh, where older adults' care services and resources are not abundant, public health policymakers and doctors face difficulties related to the increasing number of older adults and their health issues. Bangladesh is undergoing a demographic transition, with one in ten citizens aged 60 years or older in 2025, a proportion projected to rise to one in five in the coming decades, nearly half of whom suffer from various forms of infirmity and malnutrition [15, 16, 17]. Research showed that older adults' inactive lifestyles impair their mental and physical abilities, making them unable to do daily work and increasing the risk of accidents, falls, and a poor quality of life [18]. Additionally, other common illnesses among older adults are anxiety, depression, and isolation [16]. Based on the sociodemographic structures and living conditions (i.e., age, education, sex, occupation, income, family, and lifestyle), their mental and physical health, as well as the use of health services, vary. Older adults' fear of becoming dependent on family members due to illness is greater than their fear of becoming economically dependent [17]. Although research on aging patterns and diseases is sufficient in Bangladesh, the health status of older adults is still not well evaluated in light of their frailty, which leads to a lack of guidelines when it comes to public health and clinical decisions [7]. The frailty phenotype and the frailty index (FI), two diagnostic definitions of frailty, have given rise to a conceptual framework for evaluating frailty. The FI is determined by several cumulative health deficiencies [19] and has a higher prediction ability for mortality than individual elements. The FI is one of the alternative metrics of biological aging and is associated with falls, impairments, hospitalization, and high risk of mortality [20, 21, 22, 23].

Existing studies have not fully explored differences across urban and rural settings, nor have they examined frailty using a validated FI approach in this population. Therefore, the present study aims to address this gap by applying the FI to assess the health status of older adults in Sylhet District, Bangladesh. Specifically, the study investigates frailty prevalence and associated sociodemographic factors and compares patterns between rural and urban settings. By doing so, the study provides insights into frailty in Bangladesh, contributing to regional and global discussions on aging and public health.

Methods

Ethics and Permission for Data Collection

Ethics approval for this study (Project Number: PS/2018/1/11 and PS/2019/1/35) was granted by the University Research Center, Shahjalal University of Science & Technology, Bangladesh, and formal permission for data collection was sought from the Sylhet Civil Surgeon's Office, Bangladesh. In accordance with the standards of the Helsinki Declaration of 2000 (revised version), both verbal and written informed consent were obtained from each of the participants before data collection commenced. The study objectives were briefly explained to ensure respondents understood the purpose of their participation. For illiterate older adults, the written consent form translated into the local language was read aloud to facilitate comprehension. To maintain confidentiality, all collected data were anonymized.

Data Source and Study Design

The data for this study were collected from a cross‐sectional investigation of Bangladeshi males and females aged 55 years and older, residing in the Sylhet District. A standardized questionnaire covering various topics related to the health of older individuals was used to gather data through a multi‐indicator survey approach. The sampling methodology involved multistage random sampling in rural areas and two‐stage random sampling in urban areas. In rural settings, multistage sampling was used due to the dispersed nature of villages and larger geographic coverage, which required selection at multiple levels. In contrast, two‐stage sampling was appropriate in urban areas where populations are more concentrated and organized within defined administrative units, allowing for more efficient sampling. A total of 944 older adults were included in the sample, with equal representation from urban and rural regions (n = 472 for each). Urban and rural areas were defined based on the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) classification [24, 25]. Urban areas included city corporations and municipalities, while rural areas comprised villages under union councils. An equal number of participants were selected from each setting (n = 472), ensuring balanced representation. Standardized data collection procedures were applied across both groups to maintain consistency.

In the analysis, urban–rural residence was included as an adjustment variable, and stratified analyses were performed where appropriate to account for contextual differences such as access to healthcare, income levels, and education. Calculation of Frailty Index

Thirty‐three health indicators that were obtained from the same respondent were used to calculate the FI. When there was a deficit, the FI variable was coded 1, and when it was missing, it was coded 0 [27, 28]. The Frailty index (FI33) is computed using the following mathematical formula:

The data sets contained no missing values. The range of FI is 0–1 and is computed as a continuous score. For analysis purpose, the continuous FI was divided into categories based on the following thresholds: FI33 less than or equal to 0.12 was categorized as non‐frail or fit/good health, FI33 greater than 0.12 and less than or equal to 0.24 as mild frailty/slightly poor health, FI33 greater than 0.24 and less than or equal to 0.36 as moderate frailty/poor health, and FI33 greater than 0.36 as severe frailty/very poor health [7, 27, 28]. Moreover, additional details regarding how covariates, aside from the FI, were measured were explained in the Supporting Information S1: details 1,2). These thresholds were inspired by a study that was conducted in Bangladesh in rural settings [7]. On the other hand, these thresholds have been empirically tested in older Bangladeshi adults, demonstrating their ability to distinguish frailty severity levels associated with functional decline, hospitalization, and mortality risks. Aligning our categorization with both locally validated evidence and international standards ensures that our findings are contextually relevant to the Bangladeshi population while maintaining global comparability and interpretability [7, 28].

Statistical Analysis

To summarize the socio‐demographic and frailty measuring variables, descriptive analysis was utilized. The overall distribution of frailty according to rural and urban regions was presented through a clustered bar chart. Furthermore, the Pearson χ 2 test was used to find the factors that are significantly associated with frailty and residence. Finally, a multinomial logistic regression model was employed between different categories of frailty, which was the dependent variable, and other variables were the independent variables. In the multinomial logistic regression model, reference categories were defined for all categorical variables. The 55–59‐year age group was used as the reference for age. For gender, the male group served as the reference; for education, the illiterate category was used; for occupation, the service/business was selected; for marital status, unmarried/widowed/divorced individuals were included; for residence, urban areas were designated; and for religion, the muslim was specified. All AORs are interpreted relative to these baseline categories. On the other hand, adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) were employed to check the individual impact of categorical variables. Moreover, before conducting the multinomial logistic regression analysis, relevant model assumptions were assessed. Multicollinearity among independent variables was examined using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and no significant multicollinearity was observed (all variables were less than 10). Additionally, this study considered the statistical significance at the 5% level with a 95% confidence interval. Descriptive and inferential statistical methods and instruments were employed in the study to analyze the data. The Statistical Packages for Social Sciences (version 26) and MS Excel were utilized in data analysis.

Outcome and Independent Variables

The primary outcome variable of this study was the FI, categorized into four levels (non‐frail, mild, moderate, and severe frailty). The main independent variable of interest was place of residence (urban vs. rural). In addition, several sociodemographic variables, including age, gender, education, occupation, marital status, religion, and other relevant factors, were included as explanatory variables in the analysis.

Reliability

Cronbach's alpha was calculated to assess the internal consistency of the FI, as it is constructed from multiple health deficit variables [29]. Separate alpha values for rural and urban samples were reported to evaluate the consistency of the index across different population settings, where variations in health conditions and reporting patterns may exist [30]. Here, Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the frailty index (FI33) was 0.73 for the combined sample, 0.80 for the rural sample, and 0.64 for the urban sample, indicating acceptable to good internal consistency overall. The variation between rural (0.80) and urban (0.64) samples may be attributed to differences in population heterogeneity, health conditions, and reporting patterns across settings.

Descriptive Information of Frailty Index

The frailty index (FI33) ranged from 0.03 to 0.67, where the average FI33 was 0.26 with a standard deviation of 0.10 (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). However, formal normality testing was not considered necessary, as the main analyses (χ 2 test and multinomial logistic regression) do not require the assumption of normality [31, 32]. Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence and risk factors associated with physio‐psychosocial health and frailty in older adults living in rural and urban areas of Bangladesh using the Frailty Index (FI33). The higher level of frailty observed among rural older adults may be explained by differences in access to healthcare services, socioeconomic conditions, and health awareness. Rural populations often experience limited healthcare infrastructure, lower income, and reduced access to preventive services, which may contribute to increased vulnerability and poorer health outcomes. These findings were related to a previous study that concluded in China, adults in rural and urban areas [33]. There were variations in frailty prevalence across countries (Pakistan, Nepal, Sri Lanka, and India) compared to Bangladesh [34, 35, 36, 37]. It is important to note that these prevalence estimates, like this study, were derived using the deficit accumulation model (Frailty Index). The similarities and differences in frailty prevalence between Bangladesh and other countries may be explained by several contextual factors, as frailty prevalence varies across countries due to differences in sociodemographic and healthcare contexts [38]. Second, differences in healthcare systems play an important role, as populations living in regions with better healthcare performance and access tend to have lower frailty levels due to improved management of chronic conditions [38]. Third, socioeconomic conditions such as income level, education, and access to nutrition and healthcare services can significantly affect frailty outcomes, with lower socioeconomic status consistently associated with higher frailty prevalence [39]. In Bangladesh, particularly in rural areas, limited healthcare infrastructure and lower socioeconomic status may contribute to higher levels of frailty compared to some other settings, consistent with evidence showing higher frailty in disadvantaged and rural populations [38]. These results also suggest that the FI can be a useful tool for identifying vulnerable older populations and supporting clinical and public health decision‐making.

The strong association between age and frailty reflects the cumulative nature of health deficits, where physiological decline increases with advancing age. This highlights the importance of early interventions to delay the progression of frailty. Moreover, other studies also highlighted that frailty significantly impacts the health and well‐being of older adults [40, 41, 42]. According to a study, frailty was also linked to the progression and development of age‐related diseases, and frailty might be the final phase, moving directly to some significant age‐related illnesses like osteoporosis as age increases [43]. From the study, a higher level of frailty was observed among rural older adults; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Another study also showed the same scenario of frailty in both rural and urban areas [44]. These age‐related differences may be more pronounced in rural settings, where limited access to healthcare services, delayed diagnosis, and lower health awareness can accelerate the progression of frailty compared to urban populations [45, 46]. On the other hand, many senior people after the age of 50 years faced moderate frailty or poor health conditions, whereas, after the age of 70, the condition of health got worse (severe frailty or very poor health) than before, in both rural and urban areas. This study's findings have similarities with other studies conducted in India and Sri Lanka [34, 47].

Gender was another factor associated with frailty in older individuals [35, 48]. This study showed similar results, which are that both rural and urban females have moderate frailty or poorer health than males, and an existing study confirmed this finding [49]. These gender disparities may also vary across rural and urban settings, as women in rural areas often face greater barriers to healthcare access, lower socioeconomic status, and reduced social support compared to urban women, largely due to limited healthcare infrastructure, geographic constraints, and gender inequality in access to services in South Asia [50]. One possible reason for this gender difference could be that older women who live alone are more likely to experience poor health conditions [51, 52]. Differential reporting could also have been another reason for the observed difference in health between men and women [48]. On the other hand, the difference in the prevalence of gender between rural and urban areas was found by Hamiduzzaman [53], where rural regions had greater health problems than urban regions across gender.

This study found a significant association between religion and frailty, which is consistent with findings from previous studies [54, 55]. Furthermore, non‐muslims had a lower frailty rate, which was from moderate to severe frailty (poor to very poor health) than Muslims. These differences may reflect broader social and cultural factors, including variations in lifestyle, socioeconomic conditions, and healthcare utilization [54]. On the other hand, rural areas showed poorer health conditions or higher frailty compared to urban areas across religious groups. This pattern may be explained by differences in access to healthcare services, health awareness, and availability of preventive care, which are generally more limited in rural settings. In contrast, urban populations often benefit from better healthcare infrastructure and more frequent health monitoring [56]. Therefore, the observed association between religion and frailty should be interpreted within the rural–urban context, as these underlying structural differences may play an important role.

The literature, along with this study, indicates that the overall health and well‐being of older adults are influenced by their marital status. Marital status is an important social factor influencing self‐rated health both in India and other countries [57]. Most studies showed that married older adults tend to have better health compared to unmarried, widowed, or divorced individuals [58]. This finding also highlighted the same scenario. A reason that marriage played a protective role in risky health behaviors (i.e., smoking, alcohol abuse, and excessive drinking) was due to the social inclusion and social regulations it provides [59]. Importantly, this relationship should be considered within the rural–urban context, as differences in family structure, availability of social support, and access to healthcare services may influence how marital status affects frailty in different settings. This study suggested that rural individuals have a higher risk of frailty than urban individuals across marital status, which matched other study findings [60]. In rural areas, limited healthcare access and socioeconomic constraints may weaken the protective effects of marital status, whereas in urban areas, better access to services and support systems may enhance these benefits [61].

Strengths

This study has several important strengths. First, it used a relatively large sample size (n = 944), which is larger than many previous studies conducted in Bangladesh, particularly in the Sylhet region. Second, the study included both rural and urban populations, allowing for a direct comparison of frailty across different settings. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies in Bangladesh to examine frailty using the FI in both rural and urban contexts. This provides valuable insights into the epidemiological and demographic patterns of frailty among older adults in the country.

Limitations

Despite these strengths, the study has several limitations. First, the cross‐sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between risk factors and frailty. Second, the data were based on self‐reported information, which may introduce reporting bias and affect the accuracy of some variables. Third, the study was conducted among community‐dwelling older adults in a specific geographic region, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations in Bangladesh.

Recommendations

Providing better healthcare and social support as individuals age may help reduce the risk of poor health outcomes. Particular attention should be given to vulnerable groups identified in this study, including older women and individuals living in rural areas. However, the observed association between religion and frailty should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect underlying socioeconomic, cultural, or healthcare access differences rather than a direct effect. Therefore, further research is needed to explore these relationships in greater detail. Policymakers can use these findings to develop inclusive and equitable health services for older adults in both rural and urban settings. Although knowledge about the public health implications of frailty is still evolving, these results highlight the need to incorporate frailty assessment into future public health and healthcare strategies in Bangladesh.

Conclusions

The FI is a valuable tool for assessing the overall health status of older adults and identifying vulnerable populations in both rural and urban settings. This study demonstrates that frailty is influenced by key sociodemographic factors and that older adults in rural areas showed a higher level of frailty, although this difference was not statistically significant. Beyond these findings, the study has important implications for public health practice. There is a need to strengthen geriatric healthcare services, particularly in rural areas, by improving access to preventive care, early screening, and community‐based interventions targeting high‐risk groups such as older women and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. Integrating frailty assessment into routine healthcare services could support early identification and better management of health risks among older adults. In addition, future research should focus on longitudinal and nationally representative studies to better understand causal relationships and regional variations in frailty across Bangladesh. Further investigation into healthcare access, social support systems, and lifestyle factors will also help to design more effective and context‐specific interventions. Given the rapidly aging population in Bangladesh, addressing frailty should be considered a key public health priority to improve quality of life and reduce the burden on healthcare systems.
