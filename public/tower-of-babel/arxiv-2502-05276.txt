Homotopy Types of Small Semigroups
Dennis Sweeney

Abstract
. We present a software package for quickly calculating the integral homology of finite semigroups and monoids by a novel approach of exploiting structure in projective resolutions. We describe the usage of this package on semigroups and monoids of small orders. From the results of these calculations, we present a wealth of counterexamples in finite semigroup theory: we give a finite aperiodic semigroup with large torsion in its homology, two finite semigroups with certain Moore spaces as classifying spaces, and a finite semigroup and with nontrivial rational homology in infinitely many dimensions, refuting conjectures of William Nico. We further show that the set of homotopy types of classifying spaces of finite semigroups is closed under suspension.



1.
Introduction

The classifying space
BG
of a (discrete) group
G
is
an Eilenberg-MacLane space of type
K(G,1)
: it satisfies

\pi_{1}(BG)\cong G
, and
\pi_{k}(BG)=0
for
k\neq 1
.
However,
[
McD79
]
showed that
the classifying space
BM
of a monoid
M

has no such homotopical restriction:
BM

can attain the homotopy type of any connected CW complex.

Finite semigroups and monoids, and especially their actions
on a finite set of states, have long been
studied in automaton theory
[
KR65
,
AKR68
]
,
and much is known about the properties and structure of finite semigroups

[
Gre51
,
CP61
,
RS09
]
.
Fiedorowicz conjectured
[
Fie84
, p. 323]

that any finite simply connected
CW complex is homotopy equivalent to the
classifying space of some finite monoid,
but to date, the classifying spaces of the finite monoids considered
have generated relatively few unique homotopy types
(though this is growing
[
MS26
]
).
The classifying spaces of finite groups are well-studied
[
Bro82
]
,
and
[
Fie84
,
Fie02
]

notes that a
2
-by-
2
rectangular band semigroup
(with an adjoined
1
)
has a classifying space homotopy equivalent
to the
2
-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
while
[
Ste25
]
computes more generally the classifying spaces of completely simple semigroups with techniques from
[
GS22
]
.
Although
[
AR67
]
and
[
Nic69
]
consider certain reductions
to smaller monoids, and
[
Nic71
,
Nic72
]

gives bounds on cohomology of finite
regular
semigroups
in high dimensions,
the exact homotopy types of classifying spaces of individual
general finite semigroups nonetheless remain elusive.

To begin to rectify this, we uniformly investigate
semigroups of small orders.
We use the
minion
solver
[
GJM06
,
Jef+24
]
to generate
finite semigroups and monoids,
and then we use a new

fast_semigroup_homology

Python package developed by the author
[
Swe26
]

to compute homology using projective resolutions.
Our novel methods of constructing
small projective resolutions,
splitting resolutions into multiple branches,
and caching pieces of resolutions
offered substantial performance improvements compared
to computations involving the bar resolution
(see Table
6
),
which allowed many more homology groups to be computed.

Using the
fast_semigroup_homology
package, we compute homology groups

H_{0}(S),\dots,H_{8}(S)
for all semigroups of order at most 8,
and then we reduce to a restricted
class of monoids (those with no one-sided ideals that are monoids)
and compute several homology groups of such monoids of order at most 11.
We also search through a further restricted class of monoids
(those with no one-sided ideals that are monoids and
at most 4 non-unit elements outside the minimal ideal)
and continue computing homology for such monoids of order at most 16.

For finite nontrivial groups

is annihilated by multiplication by

[

, and

2.
Theoretical Background

2.1.
Semigroups and Monoids

For a general background in semigroup theory, see

[
How95
]
,
[
CP61
]
,
Section 1.1 of
[
HS12
]
,
or Section 1 of
[
Ste16
]
.

A semigroup
S
is a set equipped with an associative binary operation.
A monoid
M
is a semigroup that contains an identity element, denoted
1
.
Given a semigroup
S
, we will write
S^{\text{1}}
for
the monoid formed by adjoining an identity element to
S
,
even if
S
already has an identity, so
S^{\text{1}}=S\sqcup\{1\}

with
1x=x1=1
for all
x\in S^{\text{1}}
. A
subgroup
of
S

is a subsemigroup isomorphic to a group. An
aperiodic
semigroup
S

is a semigroup such that each
x\in S
satisfies
x^{n+1}=x^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
A finite semigroup is aperiodic if and only if it has only trivial subgroups.
For a semigroup

S
, we will write
S^{\operatorname{op}}
for the semigroup with the same underlying set as
S

but with the dual operation
x\cdot y\coloneq yx
. An element
z\in S

is called a
left zero
(resp.
right zero
) if
zx=z
(resp.
xz=z
)
for all
x\in S
.

A
left ideal
(resp.
right ideal
) of a semigroup
S
is
a nonempty subset
A\subseteq S
satisfying
SA\subseteq A
(resp.
AS\subseteq A
).
An
ideal
of
S
is a subset that is both a left ideal
and a right ideal.
Finite intersections
of ideals are ideals, so if
S
is finite and nonempty, then the intersection
of all ideals of
S
is an ideal. This
minimal ideal
(or “kernel” on p.67 of
[
CP61
]
)
of a finite nonempty semigroup
S
is denoted
K(S)
,
and
K(S)
is a
simple
semigroup in the sense that it has no proper ideals.

Per
[
Sus28
]
and
[
Ree40
]
(see also Section 3.2 of
[
How95
]
or Theorem 1.64 of
[
HS12
]
),
the classification of finite simple (or more generally,
completely simple
)
semigroups is well-understood, up to the classification of groups:
each finite simple semigroup
is isomorphic to a
Rees matrix semigroup
, denoted

\mathcal{M}(H;I,J;C_{(-,-)}),

where
H
is some finite group,
I
and
J
are finite sets,
and
C_{(-,-)}\colon J\times I\to H
is any function,
called the
sandwich matrix
. This semigroup
has the underlying set
I\times H\times J
with
operation

(i,h,j)(i^{\prime},h^{\prime},j^{\prime})\coloneq(i,hC_{(j,i^{\prime})}h^{\prime},j^{\prime}).

Each
\{i\}\times H\times\{j\}
is then a subgroup isomorphic to
H
,
with identity
(i,C_{(j,i)}^{-1},j)
.
Furthermore, every finite simple semigroup
is isomorphic to a Rees matrix semigroup with a
normalized

sandwich matrix, meaning that there is some
i_{0}\in I

and
j_{0}\in J
such that for all
i\in I
and
j\in J
we have

C_{(j_{0},i)}=C_{(j,i_{0})}=1_{H}
.
If
H
is trivial then
\mathcal{M}(H;I,J;C_{(-,-)})

is called an
|I|
-by-
|J|

rectangular band
, while if
C_{(-,-)}

is a constant function then
\mathcal{M}(H;I,J;C_{(-,-)})

is a direct product of a rectangular band and a group.
More generally, a Rees matrix semigroup can be understood
as a “twisted product” of a rectangular band and a group.

For any finite nonempty semigroup
S
, if we write

K(S)=\mathcal{M}(H;I,J;C_{(-,-)})
,
then each set
I\times H\times\{j\}

is a minimal left ideal of
S
,
and each set
\{i\}\times H\times J
is a minimal right ideal;
see the statement of Theorem 1.64 in
[
HS12
]
.
Thus, for each
(i,h,j)\in K(S)
and
x\in S
,
we have
(i,h,j)x=(i,h^{\prime},j^{\prime})
for some
h^{\prime}\in H
and
j^{\prime}\in J
,
and likewise
x(i,h,j)=(i^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime\prime},j)
for some
i^{\prime\prime}\in I
and
h^{\prime\prime}\in H
.

The
group completion
of a semigroup
S

is a group
GS
with a semigroup homomorphism
\gamma\colon S\to GS

such that any semigroup homomorphism
S\to\Gamma
to a group

\Gamma
factors uniquely as
S\xrightarrow{\gamma}GS\to\Gamma
.
The group completion
is unique up to isomorphism, and the functor
G\colon\mathsf{Sgrp}\to\mathsf{Grp}

from the category of semigroups to the category of groups
is the left adjoint of the forgetful functor
\mathsf{Grp}\to\mathsf{Sgrp}

from groups to semigroups. The group
GS
can also
be called the
maximal homomorphic group image
of
S

[
HM03
]
, the
enveloping group
of
S

[
MS76
]
,
or when
S
is a commutative monoid, the
Grothendieck group
of
S
.
The group completion of a finite semigroup is readily computed:

Lemma 2.1

(
[
Lar72
]
, Lemme II.3;
[
HM03
]
, p. 848)
.

For any finite nonempty semigroup
S
, identifying the minimal
ideal
K(S)
with some Rees matrix semigroup
\mathcal{M}(H;I,J;C_{(-,-)})

with normalized sandwich matrix as described above, we have

\displaystyle GS=G(K(S))

\displaystyle=H/\langle\langle C_{(j,i)}\mid j\in J,i\in I\rangle\rangle,

\displaystyle=H/\langle\langle C_{(j,i)}\mid j\in J\setminus\{j_{0}\},i\in I\setminus\{i_{0}\}\rangle\rangle

where
\langle\langle{\cdots}\rangle\rangle
denotes the normal closure

N\trianglelefteq H

of a subset of
H
, and where the group completion map
S\to GS

sends each
x\in S
to
[h_{x}]\in H/N

such that
(i_{0},1_{H},j_{0})x(i_{0},1_{H},j_{0})=(i_{0},h_{x},j_{0})
.

Note in particular that the group completion of a finite semigroup is finite,
and
G(S^{\text{1}})=GS
. The group completion of a monoid is the same as its group completion
as a semigroup.

For a monoid
M
, the
group of units

is the set of
x\in M
such that some
y,z\in S
satisfy

yx=xz=1
. The group of units is the unique maximal subgroup of
M
containing
1

[
CP61
, Theorem 1.10]
. If
M
is a finite monoid,
the set of non-units is an ideal of
M

[
RS09
, Exercise A.2.2]
.

2.2.
Classifying Spaces

The classifying space of a monoid is the realization of a simplicial set,
and the classifying space of a general semigroup is more naturally described as a Delta set.
For an introduction to Delta sets and simplicial sets,
see
[
Fri12
]
, or see
Section 2.1 and the
Simplicial CW Structures
appendix of
[
Hat02
]
.
Simplicial sets are discussed more thoroughly in
Section 8.1 of
[
Wei94
]
and in
[
May93
]
.
For an overview of classifying spaces of categories,
refer to
[
Seg68
]
or Section IV.3 of
[
Wei13
]
.

Recall that a
Delta set

X

is a sequence of sets

X^{0},X^{1},X^{2},\dots

equipped with face maps
d^{i}_{j}\colon X^{i}\to X^{i-1}
satisfying certain identities,
and a
simplicial set
is a Delta set additionally
equipped with degeneracy maps
s^{i}_{j}\colon X^{i}\to X^{i+1}

satisfying additional identities.

The
nerve

NM
of a monoid
M

is the same simplicial set as its nerve when viewed
as a category with one object, and thus has an
n
-simplex for each
list of
n
elements of
M
. We will write such an
n
-simplex as
[m_{1}|\cdots|m_{n}]
.
The face maps of
NM

remove the vertical bars in the notation to replace
m_{i}|m_{i+1}

with the product
m_{i}m_{i+1}
(or delete
m_{1}
or
m_{n}
),
and the degeneracy maps insert the identity
1\in M
,
so any
n
-simplex that includes
1
is degenerate.
We then define the classifying space
BM:=|NM|
to be the
geometric realization of the nerve,
working in the category of compactly generated weak Hausdorff spaces.
Recall that natural transformations between functors
descend to homotopies between classifying spaces
[
Seg68
, Prop. 2.1]
.

If
S
is a semigroup instead of a monoid, we could similarly define its
classifying space
B_{\mathsf{Sgrp}}S

as the realization of the Delta set with similar
n
-simplices
[x_{1}|\cdots|x_{n}]

and face maps, but without degeneracy maps, but this is identical to
B(S^{\text{1}})
.
Furthermore, if
S
is a monoid, then there is a homotopy equivalence

BS\simeq B_{\mathsf{Sgrp}}S
, since
these are the thick and standard realizations of the same simplicial set.
Hence, there is no essential difference
between results here on semigroups and on monoids,
and we will simply write
BS\coloneq B_{\mathsf{Sgrp}}(S)\cong B(S^{\text{1}})
.

2.3.
Homology as Tor

Since
[
McD79
]
showed that connected CW homotopy types have discrete
algebraic representations as monoids, it is desirable
to find correspondences between algebraic properties
of monoids and topological properties of their classifying spaces.
The first well-known result in this direction is that
the fundamental group of
BM
is
GM

[
McD79
, Lemma 1]
.
Another class of results, described for example in
[
Nun95
]
,
is that some standard algebraically-defined homology
theories for
M
agree with the homology of
BM
.

For a monoid
M
, we define the
monoid ring

\mathbb{Z}M
to be the (unital) ring
of finite formal
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combinations of
M
elements.
Given a left
\mathbb{Z}M
-module
D
, we define the
homology of
M
with coefficients in
D
:

H_{i}(M;D)\coloneq\operatorname{Tor}^{\mathbb{Z}M}_{i}(\mathbb{Z},D).

Above,
\mathbb{Z}
is a right
\mathbb{Z}M
-module with trivial
\mathbb{Z}M
action.
This is identical to the definition given in Section X.5 of
[
Mac95
]
,
with the exception that we use a left module of coefficients,
as is done for groups in Chapter 6 of
[
Wei94
]

and Section 9.4 of
[
Rot09
]
.
We will most often consider the case
D=\mathbb{Z}
and write

H_{i}(M)\coloneq H_{i}(M;\mathbb{Z})=\operatorname{Tor}^{\mathbb{Z}M}_{i}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}).

We now have the following:

Proposition 2.2

(From Proposition 0.4.2 in
[
Nun95
]
)
.

For a monoid
M
, and any
GM
-module
D
, we have

H_{i}(BM;D)\cong H_{i}(M;D),

where the left side is interpreted as homology
with local coefficients,
and
D
is interpreted as a
\mathbb{Z}M
-module via the group completion
map
\gamma\colon M\to GM
, so
m\cdot v\coloneq\gamma(m)v

for
m\in M
and
v\in D
.

In particular, we have the following, which is well-known (e.g. by
[
Fie02
]
):

Corollary 2.3
.

For any monoid
M
, we have isomorphic integral homology

H_{i}(M)\cong H_{i}(BM)\cong H_{i}(BM^{\operatorname{op}})\cong H_{i}(M^{\operatorname{op}}).

Proof.

For the first and third isomorphisms, take
D=\mathbb{Z}
in the previous proposition.
For the middle, there is an obvious homeomorphism
BM\cong BM^{\operatorname{op}}
.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

For any semigroup
S
, the integral semigroup homology,
defined as
H_{i}(S)\coloneq H_{i}(S^{\text{1}})
,
is isomorphic to
H_{i}(B_{\mathsf{Sgrp}}S)
.

Proof.

Since
B(S^{\text{1}})\cong B_{\mathsf{Sgrp}}S
,
we have
H_{i}(S^{\text{1}})\cong H_{i}(BS^{\text{1}})\cong H_{i}(B_{\mathsf{Sgrp}}S)
.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

As pointed out by the proof of Proposition 4.4 of
[
Fie84
]
,
the universal cover
\tilde{BM}
of the classifying space
BM
of a monoid
has homology
H_{i}(\tilde{BM};\mathbb{Z})\cong\operatorname{Tor}^{\mathbb{Z}M}_{i}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}GM)
.
By the Hurewicz theorem, we have

\pi_{2}(BM)\cong\pi_{2}(\tilde{BM})\cong H_{2}(\tilde{BM})\cong\operatorname{Tor}^{\mathbb{Z}M}_{i}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}GM),

so the computations described in Section
3

can be easily adapted to compute the second homotopy group
of the classifying space of a finite semigroup or monoid.

2.4.
Reduction to Smaller Semigroups

[
AR67
]

give the following way to reduce homology computations
of large semigroups to homology computations of certain subsemigroups
(see also Lemma 2.1 of
[
Nic69
]
):

Lemma 2.6

(Theorem 2 of
[
AR67
]
)
.

Let
S
be a semigroup and let
M

be a left (right) ideal of
S
that is a monoid.
Let
D
be any left (right)
S
-module (defined as a
\mathbb{Z}S^{\text{1}}
-module).
Let
e
be the identity element of
M
. Then for
all
n\geqslant 0
, we have

H^{n}(S,D)\cong H^{n}(M,D)=H^{n}(S,eD)=H^{n}(M,eD).

Based on this, with the note that the first isomorphism above
is induced by the map
S\to M
given by
x\mapsto exe
,
we see that this semigroup homomorphism induces
a function
BS\to BM
that induces isomorphisms
on cohomology with all local coefficient systems
(i.e.
\mathbb{Z}G(S)
-modules)
D
, so this function
is a homotopy equivalence
BS\simeq BM
.
The following is an original direct proof of the same fact:

Theorem 2.7
.

If
S
is a semigroup with some idempotent
e
such that
eSe=Se

or
eSe=eS
, then
BS\simeq B(eSe)
.

Proof.

By replacing
S
with
S^{\operatorname{op}}
as necessary,
assume without loss of generality that
eSe=eS
.
Write
M\coloneq eS
,
and note that
e
is an identity for
M
,
so
exe=ex
for all
x\in S
. Define
r\colon S\to M

by
r(x)=ex
. The map
r
is a semigroup
homomorphism because
exey=exy
. Write
\iota\colon M\to S

for the inclusion, so then
r\circ\iota=\operatorname{id}_{M}
.
We will show that
\iota
and
r
define a homotopy equivalence.
Since
B_{\mathsf{Sgrp}}S\cong BS^{\text{1}}
is a natural homeomorphism,
it suffices to show that
r^{\text{1}}\colon S^{\text{1}}\to M^{\text{1}}

and
\iota^{\text{1}}\colon M^{\text{1}}\to S^{\text{1}}

define a homotopy equivalence
Br^{\text{1}}\colon BS^{\text{1}}\to BM^{\text{1}}

and
B\iota^{\text{1}}\colon BM^{\text{1}}\to BS^{\text{1}}
.

Interpreting
M^{\text{1}}
and
S^{\text{1}}
as categories
each with a single object, we provide natural transformations as homotopies.
The first composition
r^{\text{1}}\circ\iota^{\text{1}}=(r\circ\iota)^{\text{1}}=\operatorname{id}_{M}^{\text{1}}=\operatorname{id}_{M^{\text{1}}}
is the identity functor already, so no homotopy is needed.
For the second, define a natural transformation

\eta
from the identity functor on
S^{\text{1}}

to
\iota^{\text{1}}\circ r^{\text{1}}
by taking the unique component

\eta_{*}=e
. The naturality square for each
x\in S^{\text{1}}
is:

Computing Kernels

To compute the kernel of a map of projective

There is already substantial literature on computing kernels of integer matrices,
many of which have been incorporated
into the SageMath system of

for dense integer matrices

However, when compared to alternative packages in the computational ecosystem,

The
mutable_lattice.relations_among
method computes
the left kernel of an integer matrix, given as a list of integer row vectors.
Its implementation begins by removing duplicate vectors,
adding relations
(\dots,1,\dots,-1,\dots)

to the result that witness any duplication. It then searches
to identify columns in which there is only one nonzero entry
and removes that row and column from consideration.
Next, it partitions the remaining rows and columns
into smaller subproblems based on which rows and columns
intersect along nonzero entries, potentially
reducing the kernel computation to a number of smaller
kernel computations. After these reductions,
the subproblem kernels are computed via standard Gaussian elimination:
an identity matrix
is adjoined on the right to the given list of row vectors,
imagining it as separated by a vertical bar.
Then (generalized) row operations are applied to
put the matrix in Hermite normal form (HNF).
For example, given vectors
(2,4),(4,8),(0,3)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
we compute:

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|ccc}2&4&1&0&0\\
4&8&0&1&0\\
0&3&0&0&1\\
\end{array}\right)\xrightarrow{\text{row operations}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|ccc}2&1&1&0&-1\\
0&3&0&0&1\\
\hline\cr 0&0&2&-1&0\\
\end{array}\right)

We separate the bottom
k
rows so that the rows
with only zero entries to the left of the vertical bar
are exactly the rows below the separation.
The row operations on the left of the vertical bar
replaced a matrix
A
by a new matrix
UA
with the same
row span, where
U
is an invertible integer matrix representing
the row operations. The matrix
U
is recorded
to the right of the vertical bar because we applied
the same row operations to the identity matrix.
Because the top left portion of the matrix is in HNF,
its rows are linearly independent, and so
\{w\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}:w(UA)=0\}

is spanned by the last
k
standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
.
Hence, the left kernel of
A
is

\displaystyle\{w\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}:wA=0\}

\displaystyle=\{w^{\prime}U:w^{\prime}UA=0\}

\displaystyle=\{w^{\prime}:w^{\prime}(UA)=0\}\cdot U

\displaystyle=\langle\text{the last $k$ standard basis vectors of $\mathbb{Z}^{r}$}\rangle\cdot U

\displaystyle=\langle\text{the bottom $k$ rows of $U$}\rangle.

Thus, the rows in the bottom right separation
give a basis for the kernel.
In the displayed example, we found one relation:
2(2,4)-1(4,8)+0(0,3)=0

3.2.
Covering by a New Module

Once we have computed an ordered
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\}

for the kernel of the outgoing map

we are left to cover this kernel by the image of some
new map

out of a new projective module
P_{n+1}
.
One inefficient method of doing this is to take
P_{n+1}=(\mathbb{Z}M)^{k}

and define
d_{n+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\coloneq x_{1}w_{1}+\dots+x_{k}w_{k}
.
The image

is then
\mathbb{Z}Mw_{1}+\cdots+\mathbb{Z}Mw_{k}
, which certainly
contains
\ker d_{n}
, and is a subset of
\ker d_{n}

because
\ker d_{n}
is closed under left multiplication by
\mathbb{Z}M

by the left
\mathbb{Z}M
linearity of

To construct a smaller module

,
we can search for a subset

\{w_{i_{1}},\dots,w_{i_{r}}\}\subseteq\{w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\}

with the same
\mathbb{Z}M
-span, so then

(
\star
)

\mathbb{Z}Mw_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\mathbb{Z}Mw_{i_{r}}=\ker d_{n}=\mathbb{Z}w_{1}+\cdots+\mathbb{Z}w_{k}.

To search for such a subset, we maintain
a representation of a subgroup

L\coloneq\mathbb{Z}w_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\mathbb{Z}w_{i_{r_{0}}}
of
\ker d_{n}
.
For each original basis vector
w_{i}\in\{w_{1},\dots,w_{r}\}
,
if
w_{i}
is not already in
L
then we add
w_{i}

to our solution subset and add the vectors of
\mathbb{Z}Mw_{i}

to
L
. The subgroup
L
is stored as a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
using a
mutable_lattice.Lattice
object,
and adding
\mathbb{Z}Mw_{i}
involves adding each vector

for

.
The result of this pass ensures that each
vector in our solution is not in the
\mathbb{Z}M
span of previously added vectors:

w_{i_{j}}\notin\mathbb{Z}Mw_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\mathbb{Z}Mw_{i_{j-1}}.

To further ensure that

w_{i_{j}}\notin\mathbb{Z}Mw_{i_{j+1}}+\cdots+\mathbb{Z}Mw_{i_{r}},

we repeat a similar process of filtering
by iterating over the remaining basis in reverse order.
It is possible to further ensure that the chosen subset
is minimal, in the sense that

w_{i_{j}}\notin\mathbb{Z}Mw_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\mathbb{Z}Mw_{i_{j-1}}+\mathbb{Z}Mw_{i_{j+1}}+\cdots+\mathbb{Z}Mw_{i_{r}},

but this is more computationally expensive, so we only do this when

Once we have found an appropriately small subset satisfying (

We then use

3.3.
mutable_lattice

The covering process described in subsection
3.2

involves maintaining a representation of a sublattice of some integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
.
An existing implementation
2
2

2

Specifically SageMath’s
FreeModule_submodule_pid_with_category

type prodiced by the expression
span([vector([1])])

in SageMath was immutable,
so new vectors could not be added without substantial copying.
This led the author to develop the
mutable_lattice
package,
available at
[
Swe26a
]
.

For performance,
mutable_lattice
is implemented primarily in the C language,
using Python’s C API. Because the integers we use are typically small,
we use a system of tagged pointers to allow efficient storage
of both small integers that fit into one machine word and
pointers to unbounded Python integer objects.

The
mutable_lattice
package provides two classes,

Vector
and
Lattice
, as well as the
function
relations_among
described in subsection
3.1
.
The
Vector
class stores a sequence of integers,
and the
Lattice
class provides a mutable representation
of a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
. A
Lattice
object
L
provides
an efficient
L.__contains__(v)
method
to check for the presence of a vector in a lattice
and an
L.add_vector(v)
method for
replacing a subgroup
L
by the subgroup
L+\mathbb{Z}v
.

A
Lattice
object stores a basis in Hermite normal form (HNF).
To add a vector
v
, we first apply row operations between the existing
basis and
v
to increase the number of leading zeros in
v
,
until the leading nonzero entry of
v
cannot be reduced—this
nonzero entry becomes a pivot, and the row is inserted into the basis
so that each basis vector has more leading zeros than the previous.
Once inserted, additional row operations are applied so
that the entries above each pivot are normalized to be nonnegative
and less than the pivot value.
A normalization order inspired by
[
CC82
]

is used to maintain smaller integer entries:
we reduce the second-to-last row by the pivot of the last row,
then reduce the third-to-last row by the pivots of the last two
rows, and so on, until all entries above pivots are reduced.

.

0&0&0&70&0&2\\

\end{pmatrix}

To check for containment of a vector

3.4.
Splitting and Caching

When constructing projective resolutions of the
form described so far, it is sometimes the case
that the kernel of a map

d\colon\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{Z}Me_{j}\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m^{\prime}}\mathbb{Z}Mf_{i}

splits along the given summands: we can partition

\{1,\dots,m\}=A_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup A_{q}

so that
\ker d=\iota_{A_{1}}(K_{1})+\cdots+\iota_{A_{q}}(K_{q})
,
where each
\iota_{A_{\ell}}
is the inclusion

\iota_{A_{\ell}}\colon\bigoplus_{j\in A_{\ell}}\mathbb{Z}Me_{j}\hookrightarrow\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{Z}Me_{j},

and each
K_{\ell}
is some submodule of
\bigoplus_{j\in A_{\ell}}\mathbb{Z}Me_{j}
.
When such splitting occurs,
we can solve the the corresponding covering
problem for each

individually, since each
\iota_{A_{1}}(K_{\ell})
is closed
under the
\mathbb{Z}M
action. The

method
on

objects is used to find such a splitting.

BS\simeq\mathbb{S}^{2}\vee\mathbb{S}^{2}
by the
uniqueness of Moore spaces.

The preceding example may be optimized further by noting that
the two summands of
\ker d_{1}
are the same subset
\mathbb{Z}S\subset\mathbb{Z}S^{\text{1}}
,
and so we can re-use previously created parts of the resolution
by maintaining a cache of which subsets of which projective modules
are covered by which new maps.
The
fast_semigroup_homology
package applies this optimization,
which is useful in the following notable case:

Example 3.4
.

For the cyclic group
C_{k}=\{1,a,\cdots,a^{k-1}\}
,
we have the following classical projective resolution
of the trivial left
\mathbb{Z}C_{k}
-module
\mathbb{Z}
:

\mathbb{Z}\xleftarrow{\varepsilon}\mathbb{Z}C_{k}\xleftarrow{\cdot(a-1)}\mathbb{Z}C_{k}\xleftarrow{\cdot N}\mathbb{Z}C_{k}\xleftarrow{\cdot(a-1)}\mathbb{Z}C_{k}\xleftarrow{\cdot N}\mathbb{Z}C_{k}\xleftarrow{\cdot(a-1)}\mathbb{Z}C_{k}\leftarrow\cdots

Here
N=1+a+\cdots+a^{k-1}
.
However, when constructing this resolution,
the
fast_semigroup_homology
package
identifies that
\ker(\cdot N)
and
\ker\varepsilon

are the same subset of
\mathbb{Z}C_{k}
, so we can re-use the same
module in dimension
1
as the module in dimension
3
.
The result is that the above infinite resolution can
be represented as the following finite graph with a cycle:

\mathbb{Z}\xleftarrow{\varepsilon}\mathbb{Z}C_{k}
\mathbb{Z}C_{k}\xleftarrow{N}\mathbb{Z}C_{k}
a-1

Removing the augmentation and tensoring with
\mathbb{Z}
results in the complex

\mathbb{Z}
\mathbb{Z}\xleftarrow{k}\mathbb{Z}
0

from which we compute the usual homology groups

H_{0}(C_{k})=\mathbb{Z}
,
H_{i}(C_{k})=C_{k}
for odd
i
,
and
H_{i}(C_{k})=0
for even
i\geqslant 2
.

The implementation of this technique of caching and re-using
parts of a resolution is implemented by

projective_resolution.py

in the
fast_semigroup_homology
package.
More examples of this technique
are presented in Section
5
, particularly Example
5.3
,
in which the technique provided an exponential speedup.

3.5.
Tensoring and Homology

Once we have found a sufficiently long partial projective resolution
P_{*}

of the trivial left
\mathbb{Z}M
-module
\mathbb{Z}
,
to compute the homology groups
H_{i}(M)=\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{\mathbb{Z}M}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z})
,
we need to first find the groups and boundary maps
in the chain complex
\mathbb{Z}\mathrel{\otimes}_{\mathbb{Z}M}P_{*}
.

For the projective modules
P_{n}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{Z}Me_{j}
we use,
because
\mathbb{Z}\mathrel{\otimes}_{\mathbb{Z}M}\mathbb{Z}Me\cong\mathbb{Z}

via the isomorphism
1\otimes x\mapsto 1
for
x\in Me
,
we have
\mathbb{Z}\mathrel{\otimes}_{\mathbb{Z}M}P_{n}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.
Working along the direct sums,
each map

d\colon\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{Z}Me_{j}\to\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m^{\prime}}\mathbb{Z}Mf_{j}

defined by
p_{i}(d(\iota_{j}(x)))=xa_{j}^{i}
,
descends to the tensored map

\operatorname{id}\mathrel{\otimes}d=\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathrel{\otimes}_{\mathbb{Z}M}d

which may be defined by

p_{i}((\operatorname{id}\mathrel{\otimes}d)(\iota_{j}(1\mathrel{\otimes}x))=(1\mathrel{\otimes}x)\varepsilon(a_{i}^{j})
,
where
\varepsilon\colon\mathbb{Z}M\to\mathbb{Z}
has
\varepsilon(x)=1
for
x\in M
.
Thus, the matrix defining the tensored map
\mathbb{Z}^{m}\to\mathbb{Z}^{m^{\prime}}

representing
\operatorname{id}\mathrel{\otimes}d
has entries
\varepsilon(a_{i}^{j})
.
The boundary matrices in the tensored complex
\mathbb{Z}\mathrel{\otimes}_{\mathbb{Z}M}P_{*}

can therefore be computed by summing the coefficients
within each
\mathbb{Z}M
element entry of the boundary matrices for
P_{*}
.
If there is a splitting
in the resolution as described in subsection
3.4
,
we still have the same splitting after tensoring with
\mathbb{Z}
,
as seen in Example
3.3
.

To finally compute the homology of a chain complex,
the standard technique uses Smith normal forms
[
Rot88
, p.60]
:
given a chain complex

\mathbb{Z}^{m^{\prime\prime}}\xleftarrow{d^{\prime}}\mathbb{Z}^{m^{\prime}}\xleftarrow{d}\mathbb{Z}^{m},

the homology at the middle term is given by

\ker d^{\prime}/\operatorname{im}d\cong\mathbb{Z}^{m^{\prime}-\operatorname{rank}{d^{\prime}}-\operatorname{rank}{d}}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/r_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}/r_{k},

where
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
are the nonzero diagonal entries of
some (not necessarily square) diagonal matrix representing
d

in some pair of bases. For a complex

\mathbb{Z}^{m^{\prime\prime}}\xleftarrow{d^{\prime}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{q}\mathbb{Z}^{m^{\prime}_{i}}\xleftarrow{d=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{q}d_{i}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{q}\mathbb{Z}^{m_{i}},

with splitting
structure as described in subsection
3.4
,
we can find such diagonal entries of each summand map
d_{i}
independently,
then concatenate the lists to find the diagonal entries of the sum map
d
.
This allows re-using the invariant factors
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}

for the tensored outgoing map from each module after computing them once,
even if the module is re-used
several times in the projective resolution.
The diagonal matrices are traditionally normalized
to satisfy the Smith normal form divisibility constraint
r_{1}\mid\dots\mid r_{k}
,
but this can be enforced after concatenation by repeatedly
replacing
\mathbb{Z}/a\oplus\mathbb{Z}/b

with the isomorphic abelian group
\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(a,b)\oplus\mathbb{Z}/\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)
.

The code used to find the invariant factors
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}

of a matrix is implemented by the
nonzero_invariants

method on
Lattice
objects in the
mutable_lattice
package,
which is based on the technique of alternating transposition with HNF
calculations described in
[
KB79
]
.
The code used to convert lists of invariants into homology
groups is implemented in the files
projective_resolution.py

and
normalized_invariants.py
in the
fast_semigroup_homology

package.

4.
Finding Semigroups and Monoids

The Smallsemi GAP package
[
DM24
]

already provides multiplication tables for all semigroups of order at most 8.
However, the numbers of semigroups of order
n
grows extremely quickly as
n
grows
(see Table
3
),
and databases of semigroups of larger orders have not been publicly available.
Fortunately, the vast majority of known semigroups have
some idempotent
e
satisfying the hypothesis of Theorem
2.7
,
so there is a monoid
eSe
with fewer elements having a homotopy equivalent
classifying space.
In searching for finite semigroups with unique classifying spaces,
we can therefore limit our search to the much smaller set of
only those semigroups of a particular order with no non-identity idempotent
e

satisfying
eSe=eS
or
eSe=Se
. If this was the only restriction,
our search would often include both a semigroup
S
of order
n

and the monoid
S^{\text{1}}
of order
n+1
,
which have homotopy equivalent classifying spaces; instead
we restrict our search to finite monoids, so each non-monoid semigroup’s
classifying space is found as the classifying space of the monoid
S^{\text{1}}
.
Finally, for each finite semigroup
S
, we examine
only one of
S
or
S^{\operatorname{op}}
, since these have
homotopy equivalent classifying spaces as well.

We follow techniques described in
[
Dis10
]

and in
[
DK09
]
to generate finite semigroups/monoids
using the
minion
solver
[
GJM06
,
Jef+24
]
.
The program used to generate semigroups/monoids is available
in the
semisearch
repository of
[
Swe26b
]
.
The numbers of semigroups and monoids
found are are summarized in Table
3
.

Because of Corollary
2.8
,
we generate finite semigroups or monoids
based on the possible structures of the minimal ideal
K(S)
.
For example, the smallest
K(S)
for which the
hypothesis of Corollary
2.8
does not hold is a
2
-by-
2

rectangular band
\{x_{00},x_{01},x_{10},x_{11}\}

with operation
x_{ij}x_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=x_{ij^{\prime}}
. By fixing this minimal
ideal as the first four elements of a multiplication table,
it suffices to search only for multiplication tables in the format of
Table
2
.
In general,
we can enforce the fact that
K(S)
is the minimal ideal
by choosing
K(S)
to be simple and applying the constraint
that it absorbs multiplication by other elements.
We can also enforce prior knowledge of the minimal
left and right ideals of
K(S)
based on the
Rees matrix semigroup structure of
K(S)
.

We also follow Section 3.1 of
[
Dis10
]
in separating
the search space by the isomorphism type of the diagonal
of the multiplication table, and we follow
[
DK09
]

in also separating by the group of units when searching for monoids.
Separating the search space into many parts with more structure has two main purposes:
(1) it allows the individual search processes to be easily run in parallel,
and (2) it reduces the number of permutations of the elements that fix that the structure,
so there are fewer isomorphic multiplication tables to compare against to remove duplicates.
If there are only a small number of structure-stabilizing permutations, we include
symmetry-breaking constraints directly into the
minion
problem
by requiring solution multiplication tables to be lexicographically minimal
among isomorphic tables with the given structure.
For larger stabilizers, we must apply a post-filtering step to individually
compare against each such isomorphic table. This follows
the “Complete Symmetry-Breaking” versus “Isomorph Rejection”
distinction defined in
[
DK09
]
.

is homotopy equivalent to
the suspension
\Sigma(\mathbb{R}P^{\infty})
of the infinite real projective space,
i.e.,
BM\simeq\Sigma(BC_{2})
. Note that
because suspensions have trivial cup products
but
M
has infinitely many nontrivial (co)homology groups,
the cohomology ring
H^{*}(BM)
cannot be finitely generated as a ring.

Definition 6.2
.

For a monoid
M
and a set
Y
, define a monoid
J^{Y}(M)

on the
(1+|Y|)|M|
symbols
M\sqcup\{ym:y\in Y,m\in M\}
,
with operation “
\cdot
” defined as follows:

\displaystyle m\cdot m^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneq mm^{\prime}\in M

\displaystyle m\cdot y^{\prime}m^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneq y^{\prime}m^{\prime}

\displaystyle ym\cdot m^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneq y(mm^{\prime})

\displaystyle ym\cdot y^{\prime}m^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneq ym^{\prime}

For
y\in Y
identify
y\coloneq y1\in J^{Y}(M)
, so
Y\subseteq J^{Y}(M)

is a subsemigroup satisfying
yy^{\prime}=y
.

Note that
J^{Y}(M)
is the union of the monoid
M

with the
|Y|
-by-
|M|
rectangular band
YM
,
where
M
acts as the identity on the left of
YM

and acts as multiplication in
M
on the right of
YM
.
This section will prove Theorem
1.1
,
which states that the classifying space
B\left(J^{Y}(M)\right)

is homotopy equivalent to the join of
BM
with the discrete set
Y
.

The
join
of
topological spaces is discussed in Section 5.7 of
[
Bro68
]
,
and joins of simplicial sets are discussed in
[
EP00
]
.
For simplicial sets
X,X^{\prime}
, each regarded as augmented
with a unique
(-1)
-cell
\varnothing
,
the set of
n
-simplices in the join
X*X^{\prime}
is

(X*X^{\prime})^{n}\cong\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a+1)+(b+1)=(n+1)\\
a,b\geqslant-1\end{subarray}}X^{a}\times X^{\prime b}.

Each simplex is denoted as
\sigma^{a}*\tau^{b}\in X^{a}\times X^{\prime b}\subseteq(X*X^{\prime})^{a+b+1}
.
Face maps are given by

d_{i}^{a+b+1}(\sigma^{a}*\tau^{b})\coloneq\begin{cases}d_{i}^{a}\sigma^{a}*\tau^{b}&0\leqslant i\leqslant a\\
\sigma^{a}*d^{b}_{i-(a+1)}\tau^{b}&a+1\leqslant i\leqslant a+b+1,\end{cases}

and degeneracy maps defined similarly. Joins respect geometric realization:

|X*X^{\prime}|\cong|X|*|X^{\prime}|

[
EP00
, Theorem 4.3]
.

The corollaries from Section
1
are immediate
from Theorem
1.1
:

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

By standard topological properties of joins,

BM*Y^{\text{discrete}}\simeq\Sigma\!\left(BM\wedge Y^{\text{discrete}}\right)\simeq\Sigma\!\left(\bigvee_{y\in Y\setminus\{y_{0}\}}BM\right)\cong\bigvee_{y\in Y\setminus\{y_{0}\}}\Sigma(BM).\qed

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

Taking
Y=\{1,2\}
, it is immediate from Theorem
1.1
that

B(J^{1,2}(M))\simeq\Sigma(BM)
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.4
.

To create a finite monoid
M
with
BM\simeq\bigvee_{k=1}^{r}\mathbb{S}^{n}
,
we can repeatedly apply
J^{\{1,2\}}
to reduce the problem
to finding a finite monoid
M
with
BM\simeq\bigvee_{k=1}^{r}\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
This is supplied by a
(r+1)
-by-
2
rectangular band with an adjoined unit,
directly generalizing Example
3.3
.
Alternately, we can appeal to
[
Ste25
]
.
∎

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we use the collapsing

]
for the nerve of a monoid with a

Proposition 6.3
.

J^{Y}(M)
is a monoid with a complete rewriting system
on the alphabet
(M\setminus\{1\})\sqcup Y
with rewriting rules

\displaystyle mm^{\prime}

\displaystyle\to m^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\text{for each $m,m^{\prime}\in M\setminus\{1\}$ where $mm^{\prime}=m^{\prime\prime}$ in $M$},

\displaystyle my

\displaystyle\to y

for each
m\in M\setminus\{1\},y\in Y
, and

\displaystyle yy^{\prime}

\displaystyle\to y

\displaystyle\text{for each $y,y^{\prime}\in Y$}.

Proof.

There are no infinite chains of reductions because each rule reduces
the length of a string. The system is confluent because
the critical pairs converge:

•

(mm^{\prime})y\to m^{\prime\prime}y\to y
agrees with
m(m^{\prime}y)\to my\to y
.

•

(my)y^{\prime}\to yy^{\prime}\to y
agrees with
m(yy^{\prime})\to my\to y
.

•

Critical pairs only in
S
or only in
Y
certainly converge.

The irreducible words in this system take the form of
m
,
y
,
ym
or
1

for any
m\in M\setminus\{1\}
and
y\in Y
,
and the rewriting rules give exactly the operation on
J^{Y}(M)
.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

There is a homotopy equivalence
B(J^{Y}(M))\to Q
,
where
Q
is the subcomplex of
B(J^{Y}(M))
consisting
of cells of the form
[m_{1}|\cdots|m_{k}|y_{1}|\cdots|y_{\ell}]
,
where each
m_{i}\in M\setminus\{1\}
and
y_{i}\in Y
, and
k,\ell\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Using the complete rewriting system of Proposition
6.3
,
the collapsing scheme of
[
Bro92
]

defines a homotopy equivalence from
B(J^{Y}(M))

onto a certain quotient complex with a cell for each
essential

cell of
B(J^{Y}(M))
.
Using our notation from Section
2.2
,
Brown’s essential cells
are lists
[w_{1}|\cdots|w_{n}]
of irreducible words over the alphabet,
with the first entry
w_{1}
a single letter, the concatenation

w_{i}w_{i+1}
of any pair of adjacent entries reducible,
and no proper prefix of any
w_{i}w_{i+1}
reducible.

For our system, the essential cells cannot include
[\cdots|m|ym^{\prime}|\cdots]

and cannot include
[\cdots|y|y^{\prime}m^{\prime}|\cdots]
because
my
and
yy^{\prime}

are reducible proper prefixes of the concatenations.
Thus, because these longer irreducible words cannot follow a single-letter word,
all entries of our essential cells are single letters.
Because any
ym
is irreducible,
our essential cells cannot include
[\cdots|y|m|\cdots]
either,
so we are left with only the listed cells of
Q
.

For an arbitrary complete rewriting system,
Brown’s quotient complex is a more general CW complex.
However, because our essential cells and their degeneracies
form a simplicial set subcomplex of
B(J^{Y}(M))
,
no stage of the collapsing scheme
alters the face maps on
Q
, so we can identify
Q

with the stated simplicial set subcomplex.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Using the simplicial set
Q
from Lemma
6.4
,
we show the following zigzag of homotopy equivalences:

BM*Y^{\text{discrete}}\underset{(1)}{\leftarrow}BM*\bigsqcup_{y\in Y}{BY}\underset{(2)}{\rightarrow}\frac{BM*\bigsqcup_{y\in Y}{BY}}{()*\bigsqcup_{y\in Y}{BY}}\underset{(3)}{\cong}\frac{Q}{BY}\underset{(4)}{\leftarrow}Q\underset{(5)}{\leftarrow}B\!\left(J^{Y}(M)\right).

Here,
Y\subseteq J^{Y}(M)
has operation
yy^{\prime}=y
,
so
BY
is contractible by Corollary
2.9
,
and so (4) is an equivalence.
Joins respect equivalences,
so (1) is an equivalence.
A join with a single point
()
is
a cone, and hence is contractible, so (2) is an equivalence.
The map (5) is an equivalence by the lemma,
so it remains to give the isomorphism (3) of simplicial sets.
The correspondence (3) on nondegenerate cells is

the unique
0
-cell

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\text{the unique $0$-cell}

\displaystyle\big[[m_{1}|\cdots|m_{k}]*\varnothing\big]

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\big[[m_{1}|\cdots|m_{k}]\big]

for
k\geqslant 1

\displaystyle\big[[m_{1}|\cdots|m_{k}]*\iota_{y_{0}}[y_{1}|\cdots|y_{\ell}]\big]

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\big[[m_{1}|\cdots|m_{k}|y_{0}|y_{1}|\cdots|y_{\ell}]\big]

\displaystyle\text{ for $k\geqslant 1,\ell\geqslant 0$}.

For the last case, the face map
d_{k}
is respected because

m_{k}y_{0}=y_{0}
, and
the face map
d_{k+1}

is respected because
y_{0}y_{1}=y_{0}
.
∎
