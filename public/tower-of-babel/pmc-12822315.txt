Mesenchymal stem cell therapy for endometrial injury: a meta-analysis of preclinical studies
Authors: YangYang Liu, Zahra Ghorbaninejad Koubanani, DaZhou Jia, Min Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndometrial injury is a common cause of menstrual changes, abortion, and even infertility in women, including intrauterine adhesions (Asherman’s syndrome) and a thin endometrium. Hysteroscopic surgery and drug treatment are currently the main treatment methods; however, their effect is not satisfactory. Increasingly, studies have demonstrated that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) may be a safe and promising option for treating endometrial injury. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of MSCs in an endometrial injury model.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and EMBASE from the establishment of the databases to December 2023. Preclinical studies examining the role of MSCs in treating endometrial injury were included. The main outcome measures were the endometrial thickness, fibrosis area ratio, and recovery of reproductive function. Risk of bias assessment was performed using the SYRCLE tool. RevMan 5.3 was used for the meta-analysis and testing heterogeneity.ResultsA total of 10 Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) involving 230 animals were included. The analysis showed that after treatment with MSCs, the endometrium was significantly thickened (WMD 115.01 μm, 95% CI (94.07–135.95 μm)), the area ratio of endometrial fibrosis reduced (WMD − 29.88, 95% CI (− 36.61–−23.15)), and the pregnancy rate increased (RR 4.60, 95% CI (2.09–10.13)). Subgroup analyses indicated that Human amniotic mesenchymal stem cells (HA-MSCs) seemed to be more effective than other types. In addition, we found that the treatment’s efficacy is also affected by the route of administration, the type of transplantation, and other factors.ConclusionsThe results of this study indicate that treatment with MSCs (HA-MSCs delivered via intrauterine injection) exerts beneficial effects on endometrial injury in rats.Xenogeneic MSCs showed enhanced therapeutic potential relative to allogeneic MSCs in our experimental models.Taken together, these data highlight MSCs as a potentially valuable approach warranting additional research for the treatment of endometrial injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04211-2.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cells, Endometrial injury, Intrauterine adhesion, Asherman’s syndrome, Meta-analysis

Background

In the normal menstrual cycle, the endometrium undergoes periodic proliferation, secretion, exfoliation, and regeneration under hormonal influence. Abnormal repair and regeneration occur under the influence of mechanical injury, infection, drugs, and other factors, namely endometrial injury. The main clinical manifestations are intrauterine adhesion (IUA), also known as Asherman’s syndrome (AS), and a thin endometrium. The incidence of IUA or AS is 2%–22% [1] while that of a thin endometrium is approximately 2.4% in the assisted reproductive cycle [2]. Mild injury can cause changes in women’s menstruation, and serious ones can lead to repeated miscarriages and even infertility. According to statistics, the incidence of infertility caused by endometrial injury is as high as 40% [3]. So far, the treatment mainly depends on hysteroscopic surgery; however, it has a high failure rate and a high risk of recurrence for severe injury [4]. Other methods, such as hormone-based drugs, hyaluronic acid gel, and amniotic membrane transplantation, are also more suitable for mild to moderate injuries. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a safer and more effective treatment method.

With the development of regenerative medicine, MSCs have been shown to have great potential in treating various diseases [5]. Increasing evidence suggests that MSCs exhibit anti-fibrotic effects and promote tissue repair in both preclinical and clinical studies [6, 7]. Recent studies have demonstrated that mesenchymal stem cells can promote endometrial regeneration through differentiation, immune regulation, and paracrine signaling [8]. Diseases such as IUA are also considered a form of endometrial fibrosis. However, due to the adverse consequences such as rejection and tumorigenicity after transplantation, its clinical application is limited [9].

Additionally, there were differences in the design of each study, including MSC doses, types, route of administration, and the source and timing of treatment [10]. The best way to use MSCs remains unclear, and their efficacy and safety still need to be further verified [11]. Therefore, we collected all relevant RCTs and conducted a meta-analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of MSCs for endometrial injury.

Methods

The author declares that all supporting data are available within this article and its online supplementary files. The study protocol was registered through PROSPERO (http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO), and the registration number is CRD42022331689. This registration can be found online at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/display_record.php?RecordID=331689.

Search strategies

To obtain the relevant literature for this meta-analysis, two researchers independently searched for eligible papers published on PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and EMBASE. The retrieval period spanned from the database’s establishment to December 2023. All references included in the literature were searched to determine the potential eligibility of the studies. The language was limited to English. The details of the search strategy are shown in the attached file.

Research selection

Inclusion criteria

(1) Animal models involving endometrial injury (all species); (2) All animal models of endometrial injury were treated using MSCs; (3) including therapeutic results, such as endometrial thickness, the percentage of the fibrotic area, pregnancy rate, etc.; (4) A control group was established.

Exclusion criteria

(1) The MSCs have been differentiated or modified to alter the expression of specific genes; (2) Summary of the meeting, case report, or case series; (3) Review or meta-analysis; (4) Duplicate study; (5) Studies not published in English.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two researchers independently searched the included studies. A unified data extraction table was developed to extract the primary data. The relevant information included the first author, publication year, country, sample size, animal characteristics (species, models, etc.), intervention details (the source and dose of MSCs, the way and time of transplantation), follow-up care (observation time), evaluation index (endometrial thickness, fibrotic area ratio, pregnancy rate). Two researchers resolved differences by conferring with each other, and, if necessary, discussing the differences in the group to reach a consensus while avoiding deviations caused by subjective factors of the extractors. The risk of bias was assessed by two independent reviewers using the SYRCLE risk of bias tool for animal studies [12], which evaluates random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding methods, selective outcome reporting, incomplete outcome data, and other potential deviations.

Statistical analysis

Review Manager 5.3 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK) was used for data analysis. The I2 test was used for heterogeneity analyses. When P > 0.1 and I-squraed statistis(I2)≤ 50%, the fixed effect model was adopted; however, when P ≤ 0.1 and I2 > 50%, significant heterogeneity was considered, and the random effect model was used. A subgroup analysis or sensitivity analysis was performed when inter-study heterogeneity was present. Due to the limited number of studies for each variable, it is difficult to conduct a meta-regression. Continuous and dichotomous outcome variables were described in terms of relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), while continuous outcome variables were defined in terms of weighted mean differences (WMDs). A funnel chart was used to determine whether there was publication bias. P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Search results

A total of 206 related references were retrieved, and 144 repetitive studies were deleted. After the preliminary screening of titles and abstracts, 45 studies were excluded. Seven studies were excluded after scanning the full text according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Finally, 10 RCTs were included [13–22] (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

A total of 10 RCTs were included [13–22], involving 230 experimental animals; 115 in the MSC treatment group and 115 in the control group. The basic characteristics of the studies are presented in Table 1. All the subjects were rats. Five types of MSCs were involved, which included human amniotic mesenchymal stem cells (HA-MSCs) [15–17], bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) [13, 22], umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UC-MSCs) [14, 21], adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADMSCs) [18, 20], and uterine-derived mesenchymal stem cells (uMSCs) [19]. The routes of administration included intrauterine injection [15–21] and tail vein injection [13, 14, 22]. Transplantation types included allogeneic [13, 18, 20, 22] and xenogeneic [14–17, 21]. Quality assessment

As evaluated using the SYRCLE bias risk tool, all included studies were of medium quality, as shown in Table 2. While all studies [13–22] used random allocation and reported baseline characteristics, the risk of bias for allocation concealment was unclear. It should be noted that random housing and blinding of investigators were not described in all included studies. Three studies mentioned the use of random outcomes for assessment[14, 16, 19], and only one study employed blinding for outcome assessment [19]. In addition, all included studies reported the outcome data altogether and avoided selective outcome reporting. Notably, the sources of bias were unclear in other areas.

Efficacy of MSC therapy on endometrial injury

Endometrial thickness

Main outcomes: The results of nine studies showed that the endometrium in the MSC treatment group was significantly thicker than that in the control group (WMD 115.01 μm, 95% CI (94.07–135.95 μm), P < 0.00001; χ2 = 41.72, I2 = 81%) (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis: The meta-analysis revealed high heterogeneity; therefore, we conducted subgroup analyses in terms of MSC type, MSC dose, route of administration, and type of transplantation to explore the source of heterogeneity (Table 3). We compared different types of MSCs and found that the endometrium was thicker in animals treated with HA-MSCs than in those treated with BMSCs and other stem cell types (WMD 141.64 μm, 95% CI (117.59–165.70 μm) vs. WMD 102.63 μm, 95% CI (64.00–141.25 μm) vs. WMD 88.05 μm, 95% CI (71.70–104.41 μm)). The subgroup heterogeneity decreased significantly, and the sensitivity analysis of studies on BMSC treatment (Additional file: Table 1 A) shows that excluding one study [20] resulted in a significant reduction in heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). Concerning the MSC dose, there was no significant difference between the two groups (WMD 115.03 μm, 95% CI (87.06–143.00 μm) and (WMD 115.34 μm, 95% CI (76.99–153.69 μm)); the sensitivity analysis indicated that when one study in each subgroup was excluded [14, 17], the heterogeneity of the two groups decreased significantly (Additional file: Table 1B). We also found that intrauterine injection was more effective than the tail vein injection (WMD 134.14 μm, 95% CI (109.30–158.98 μm) vs. WMD 85.42 μm, 95% CI (74.42–96.42 μm)); after excluding one study [18], the intrauterine injection subgroup showed slight heterogeneity (I2 = 11%) (Additional file: Table 1 C). At the same time, we found that MSCs from xenogeneic sources were better than those from allogeneic sources (WMD 130.66 μm, 95% CI (94.79–166.52 μm) vs. WMD 97.20 μm, 95% CI (78.13–116.26 μm)). After one study [14] was removed, the heterogeneity of the xenogeneic group was significantly reduced (I2 = 26%) (Additional file: Table 1D). In our analysis, removing the studies that contributed most to heterogeneity did not change the overall findings.

Fibrosis area ratio

Main outcomes: Four studies were included. Due to the high heterogeneity (I2 = 91%, P < 0.00001), a random effects model was used. The results showed that the fibrosis area ratio in the MSC treatment group was significantly lower than that in the control (WMD − 29.88, 95% CI (− 36.61–−23.15), χ2 = 32.04) (Fig. 3).

Forest plot showing the effect of MSC therapy on fibrosis area ratio

Subgroup analysis (Table 4) and sensitivity analysis: The analysis revealed significant heterogeneity; therefore, we focused on MSC types, MSC dose, administration method, and transplant type to investigate the source of heterogeneity. According to different types of MSCs, we divided them into two subgroups: HA-MSCs and other types. There was no significant difference between the two groups (WMD − 29.24, 95% CI (− 31.32–−27.17) vs. WMD − 27.98, 95% CI (− 51.70–−4.26)); Due to the small number of studies, no sensitivity analysis was conducted. In addition, we also found that there was little difference between dosage subgroups. It is worth noting that the analysis results suggest that the effect of the intrauterine injection is better than that of the tail vein injection (WMD − 27.65, 95% CI (− 32.01–−23.28) vs. WMD − 39.30, 95% CI (− 42.64–−35.96)). The sensitivity analysis (Additional file: Table 1E) suggested that there was no significant heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) after excluding one study [19], and removing this study did not change the overall findings. MSCs from xenogeneic sources were slightly better than those from allogeneic sources (WMD − 27.98, 95% CI (− 51.70–−4.26) vs. WMD − 29.24, 95% CI (− 31.32–−27.17)).

Pregnancy rate

Two studies examined the pregnancy rate, showing low heterogeneity between them (I2 = 0%, P = 0.35); therefore, we used the fixed-effects model for meta-analysis. The results showed that the pregnancy rate of the MSC treatment group was significantly higher than Control group (RR 4.60, 95% CI (2.09–10.13), P = 0.0002) (Fig. 4).

Publication bias

We used the endometrial thickness as an example to analyze the publication bias of the included studies. The funnel diagram displays a biased distribution, indicating a potential publication bias in the included studies (Fig. 5). Since all the included studies were positive, this may be one reason for the funnel bias. We suggested that future studies should include negative results or promote pre-registration to reduce publication bias.

Discussion

Injury to the endometrial basal layer impairs the regeneration of endometrial cells and glands, hinders neovascularization, and renders the endometrium difficult to repair, leading to fibrosis, scar formation, and adhesion. Uterine adhesion and a thin endometrium caused by endometrial injury are currently two of the main causes of female infertility. Therefore, the regeneration and repair of the endometrium are vital. In recent years, the multi-directional differentiation ability and paracrine function of MSCs have been widely focused on by scholars. Many studies have shown that MSCs play an anti-fibrotic role in many diseases [23–25]. MSC therapy is also considered a new method of treating endometrial injury. The study aims to evaluate the efficacy of MSCs in preclinical studies, with the goal of providing reliable evidence to support their clinical application.

This study is the first meta-analysis of preclinical studies to evaluate the efficacy of MSCs in the treatment of endometrial injury. Endometrial receptivity (ER) refers to the ability of the endometrium to allow blastocyst location, adhesion, penetration, implantation, and development, which is closely related to embryonic implantation. The endometrial thickness is the main index to evaluate ER. We primarily found that MSCs can promote endometrial thickening, facilitate uterine angiogenesis, and reduce the area of endometrial fibrosis, thereby promoting the repair and regeneration of the endometrium and improving its receptivity. The improvement of reproductive capacity can more directly reflect the effect of treatment. In our analysis, two studies were included in the evaluation of the pregnancy rate. The results demonstrate that MSCs can effectively enhance ER, promote embryo implantation, and partially restore reproductive capacity.

The primary cause of endometrial injury is damage to the basal layer, which leads to the regeneration of endometrial epithelial cells and stromal cells, the obstruction of neovascularization, the proliferation of connective tissue, and the formation of scars and fibrosis. MSCs mainly promote the recovery of the endometrial morphology and function and improve fertility through the following aspects: (1) Direct differentiation participates in tissue repair and regeneration: MSCs have the potential for multiple differentiation. When the endometrium is damaged, the stimulation of the inflammatory microenvironment upregulates the expression of homing receptors and chemokines, which promotes the migration of exogenous MSCs to the damaged area, and on the one hand, reactivates the differentiation potential of damaged endometrial stem cells by secreting cytokines such as stem cell factors and macrophage colony-stimulating factors. On the other hand, by regulating the differential expression of genes, it directly differentiates into various types of cells, such as endometrial epithelial cells, stromal cells, and vascular endothelial cells, thereby replacing damaged cells and achieving re-epithelialization of the endometrium [26]. Studies by SHI [27] have shown that human umbilical cord MSCs differentiate into endometrial epithelial cell-like cells and stromal cell-like cells under different induction environments; Alawadhi et al. [28] found that the fertility of adult IUA model mice transplanted with BM-MSCs was restored; Taylore [29] also found that transplanted BMSCs could reach the endometrium through migration and differentiate into endometrial cells. (2) Immune regulation ability: Studies [13, 20, 21] have shown that MSCs secrete a variety of cytokines, such as bFGF and VEGF, which induce angiogenesis and promote intimal regeneration; At the same time, MSCs upregulate anti-inflammatory cytokines (FGF- β, IL-10, IL-6, etc.) and downregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF α and IL-1 β) to regulate the inflammatory process and inhibit the immune response. (3) Paracrine action: MSCs can regulate macrophage phenotypes through paracrine signaling. The tumor necrosis factor-α-stimulated protein 6 (TNF-α-SP6) secreted by MSCs can promote the polarization of macrophages to the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype and inhibit the differentiation of the M2 phenotype into the M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype, thereby participating in the regulation of endometrial inflammatory responses to improve inflammation and promote endometrial regeneration [30]. MSCs can also play a role by regulating the ratio of helper T cells, which produce nitric oxide that inhibits the proliferation of pro-inflammatory Th1 cells and Th17 cells when stimulated by inflammatory cytokines. Additionally, MSCs can differentiate pro-inflammatory phenotype cells into Th2 cells with an anti-inflammatory phenotype. In addition, MSCs can also reduce the activation of NK cell receptors, thereby weakening the inflammatory response induced by cells of the adaptive immune system [31]. Recent studies have suggested that MSCs play an anti-fibrotic role through their paracrine and exocrine secretions, which can reduce extracellular matrix deposition, inflammatory cell infiltration, and the release of inflammatory factors [32].

There was significant heterogeneity in the study; therefore, we conducted a further analysis from the aspects of MSC type, dose, route of administration, and transplantation type. In the included studies, three items used HA-MSCs ([15–17], three items used BMSCs[13, 20–22], two items used UC-MSCs [14, 21], one item used ADSCs [18], and one item used uMSCs ([19]. The results showed that HA-MSCs appeared to have more advantages than other types in restoring endometrial thickness. HA-MSCs are superior to other types in reducing the area of fibrosis; however, the difference is not statistically significant. BMSCs were the first to come into view ([33]. Like BMSCs, HA-MSCs play a role by secreting cytokines and growth factors with anti-apoptotic, angiogenic, and immunomodulatory properties ([34]. There are also studies indicating that human amniotic mesenchymal stem cells have significant advantages over other stem cell therapies, such as promoting angiogenesis, cell proliferation, anti-inflammatory effects, anti-fibrosis, immune regulation, and other biological function [35, 36]. In addition, HA-MSCs have the advantages of being a rich source, causing no trauma, no ethical restrictions, a strong proliferative ability, no rejection, and no tumorigenicity ([37]. However, there is still a paucity of comparative studies on the efficacy of HA-MSCs and other types of MSCs. In combination with the results of this study, HA-MSCs may be a more suitable choice for conditions related to endometrial injury. Further studies are needed to verify this hypothesis.

The results showed that the effect of the intrauterine injection was greater than that of the caudal vein injection. Domnina et al. found that the therapeutic effect of MSCs was influenced by the method of cell administration, which is consistent with the findings of this paper [38], suggesting that intrauterine injection may be a more effective treatment. This may be because intrauterine injection of MSCs is more intuitive, which increases the concentration of MSCs in local tissues, to ensure the accuracy of the implantation site, make it easier to achieve the number of MSCs required for treatment, and can also avoid damage to other organs such as pulmonary embolism caused by intravenous transplantation [39].

Furthermore, our analyses show that the effect of xenogeneic MSCs (all of which are human) is greater, suggesting that the source of MSCs is also vital for the therapeutic effect. In the meta-analysis of MSCs in the treatment of pulmonary fibrosis by Li [10], it is also noted that the therapeutic effect of xenogeneic MSCs is superior to that of allogeneic ones. The meta-analysis of MSCs to evaluate the survival rate of the skin flap also found that xenogeneic MSCs from the human body showed better efficacy in preclinical studies [40]. However, this result should be approached with caution. The reasons for this include factors such as interspecies differences in immune responses. Further research is needed to investigate the differences between the two in the future. We also conducted subgroup analyses stratified by the dose of MSCs (≤ 5 × 106 and >5 × 10^6) and found no significant difference between the two groups. This may be due to the small sample size in this study. Our results show that cell types, doses, and routes of administration may be the determinants of heterogeneity. However, due to the limited number of included studies, no further meta-regression analysis was conducted.

To the best of our knowledge, MSCs have been used in clinical trials; however, the numbers involved in these trials are relatively small. Most studies have shown the desired effect of MSCs. Santamaria [41] et al. treated 16 patients with AS and endometrial atrophy (EA) using BMSCs. Finally, 15 patients resumed menstruation, three patients conceived naturally, seven patients conceived successfully after embryo transfer, and two patients delivered successfully. Singh [42] et al., in a bid to evaluate the role of BMSCs in AS and EA, treated 25 patients using autologous BMSC intrauterine transplantation and followed them up for five years. The results showed that 11 of the 25 patients had an endometrial thickness of 7 mm, and three patients had favorable pregnancy outcomes (two cases of natural pregnancy and one case of embryo transfer). Cao [43] et al. treated patients using UC-MSCs. Three months after treatment, the thickness of the endometrium increased, and the uterine adhesion score decreased. At the end of the follow-up, 10 out of 26 patients were pregnant, and eight of them gave birth to live babies. Interestingly, in a clinical trial conducted by Huang et al. [44] using UC-MSCs to manage endometrial injury, although the endometrial thickness and other indicators tended to improve, the difference was not statistically significant. It is worth noting that no obvious adverse reactions were observed in the aforementioned clinical studies. Research confirms the safety and long-term clinical effectiveness of mesenchymal stem cell injection therapy for multiple sclerosis, as well as its immunomodulatory effects, which can significantly improve the patient’s condition [45]. The research results of Pico OA et al. indicate that frozen UCMSCs have long-term therapeutic effects in treating osteoarthritis [46]. Therefore, we believe that the long-term efficacy of MSCs in treating endometrial injury is positive. However, the follow-up time of the studies included in this meta-analysis is relatively short, and it is hoped that more studies will clarify the long-term efficacy of MSC treatment.

Limitations

This meta-analysis has several advantages, including being the first systematic review of the efficacy of MSCs in treating endometrial injury. However, there are still some shortcomings. First, according to the findings, the results exhibit non-negligible heterogeneity. We employed the random effects model and conducted subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses to investigate the sources of heterogeneity. The type, route of administration, and dose of MSCs may be contributing factors to the high heterogeneity; therefore, our results need to be interpreted carefully. Second, according to the inclusion criteria, we included a small number of documents, and the sample size in each study was also small. Additionally, all experimental animals were rats, and there is a lack of experimental research on larger animals. All included studies used rats. The reason may be due to its low cost, rapid reproduction, and clear genetic background. However, it may limit the external validity of the findings. Studies have shown significant differences between rats and humans in terms of immune microenvironment, metabolic enzyme activity, and fibrogenesis mechanisms, among others [47]. These disparities raise critical questions about the direct translatability of preclinical findings from rat models to human clinical outcomes. Given that large animal models possess anatomical and physiological features more analogous to humans, they may serve as more robust platforms for evaluating therapeutic strategies. We therefore advocate for increased utilization of large animal models in future research to better bridge the gap between preclinical and clinical. Third, although MSCs have been used in human clinical studies, including preclinical studies, the type, dose, treatment time, and follow-up time of MSCs were not completely unified, and most available clinical studies are not high-quality RCTs, which highlights the need for further standardization in the future. Fourth, Potential confounding from varied injury induction methods (e.g., ethanol vs. mechanical damage).The lack of standardized protocols for MSC preparation/administration across studies. Fifth, the funnel chart suggests that this study may have certain publication biases, which could be due to unpublished and negative studies.

Conclusion

In summary, based on this meta-analysis, MSCs appear to be promising in effectively promoting endometrial thickening, reducing the proportion of endometrial fibrosis, and partially restoring ER expression and reproductive capacity in the included animal models. We also found differences in cell type, route of administration, and transplantation type. Therefore, these differences should be considered in future clinical studies to achieve the optimal therapeutic effect. In summary, MSCs may be a potential method for managing endometrial injury; however, considering the heterogeneity and small sample size of this meta-analysis, further investigation is needed to confirm this hypothesis.
