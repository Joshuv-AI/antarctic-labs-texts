Guiding vaccine policy for chikungunya in Brazil: a cost-effectiveness analysis of live-attenuated versus recombinant chikungunya vaccines in Brazilian adults ≥18 years
Authors: Christina Guo, Christopher Xiao, Juliana Evangelista Paiva, Yin Hong Lo, Emmanuel F Drabo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesChikungunya virus (CHIKV) causes substantial burden in Brazil, with 3.2 million cases and US$9.8 billion in costs from 2011 to 2020. This study evaluates the cost-effectiveness of live-attenuated and recombinant vaccines for adults ≥18 years to guide vaccination strategies.DesignA modified Susceptible–Vaccinated–Exposed–Infectious–Recovered–Deceased model was used to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of live-attenuated and recombinant chikungunya vaccines vs no vaccination in a cohort of 1000 Brazilian adults ≥18 years over 10 years. Costs (2025 US dollars, US$/Brazilian reais, R$) and quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) were estimated from both the Brazilian healthcare sector and societal perspective assuming a 5.00% annual social discount rate. Model inputs were derived from published data, clinical trials and assumptions.Main outcome measuresIncremental cost-effectiveness ratios and incremental net monetary benefits (INMB) were calculated for each vaccine compared with no vaccination. Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSA) were conducted to assess the impact of parameter uncertainty.ResultsIn the base case, and from both healthcare sector and societal perspectives, the live-attenuated vaccine was more effective (336 011 QALYs) and less costly (healthcare: US$0.12 million/R$0.68 million; societal: US$2.85 million/R$15.93 million) than the recombinant vaccine (335 787 QALYs; US$0.15 million/R$0.83 million; US$2.88 million/R$16.11 million) or no vaccination (335 472 QALYs; US$0.02 million/R$0.11 million; US$2.76 million/R$15.44 million), thus dominating both. At a societal willingness-to-pay threshold of US$10 306 (R$57 601) per QALY, its INMB was US$5.46 million (R$30.50 million) and US$5.47 million (R$30.58 million) from the healthcare and societal perspectives, respectively, compared with US$3.12 million (R$17.44 million) and US$3.13 million (R$17.49 million) for the recombinant vaccine. PSA and a battery of sensitivity analyses showed the live-attenuated vaccine remained dominant at this threshold.ConclusionsChikungunya vaccination is a highly cost-effective strategy to reduce the health and economic burden of CHIKV in Brazil, with the live-attenuated vaccine favoured over the recombinant formulation.

Introduction

Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), a mosquitoborne arbovirus transmitted primarily by Aedes species, is a growing global health threat driven by climate change, urbanisation and deforestation.1 Once confined largely to Africa and Southeast Asia, CHIKV has now been reported in over 100 countries. Brazil is among the most affected countries in South America, with over 3.2 million cases reported between 2011 and 2020, and an estimated economic burden exceeding US$9.8 billion.2 Since 2016, Brazil has experienced recurrent epidemics, highlighting the virus’s endemicity and persistent transmission.3

CHIKV infection progresses through acute, postacute and chronic phases.4 The acute phase, characterised by fever, arthralgia and rash, typically resolves within 7 to 10 days. However, 25% to 59% of patients develop postacute infection or chronic chikungunya disease (CCD).4 The postacute infection phase is characterised by arthralgia lasting up to 3 months, while CCD is characterised by persistent arthralgia lasting longer than 3 months. CCD closely resembles inflammatory rheumatic conditions and can significantly impair quality of life for months or even years.4

The burden of CCD is particularly high among older adults and individuals with pre-existing comorbidities, who often require ongoing medical attention, rehabilitation and informal caregiving. Studies suggest 60% of patients >40 years experienced persistent rheumatologic symptoms 1 year postinfection, and nearly a third sought further medical care, highlighting the persisting effects of CHIKV infection.5 This disabling chronic sequela remains largely neglected in outbreak responses and health system planning, despite its chief role in chikungunya’s long-term health and economic burden.2

CHIKV control has traditionally relied on vector control and symptomatic management.4 6 However, adherence to vector control measures is inconsistent,6 and insecticide resistance is rising.7 The recent licensure of two vaccines—a live-attenuated vaccine (VLA1553, IXCHIQ) and recombinant vaccine (PXVX0317, VIMKUNYA)—marks a shift from reactive control to potential sustained prevention.8 Both vaccines have demonstrated durable immunogenicity and favourable safety profiles in clinical trials.9 10 The live-attenuated vaccine had a seroresponse rate of 96.6% after 2 years and received regulatory approval in the United States, Europe, Canada and Brazil as recent as April 2025.9 11 The recombinant vaccine had a seroresponse rate of 85.5% after 6 months and was also approved in the USA and Europe, but not yet in Brazil.10

However, evidence on the cost-effectiveness and affordability of CHIKV vaccination in endemic low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as Brazil is lacking. Outside of North America, including US territories and Mexico,12 13 no peer-reviewed cost-effectiveness analyses of CHIKV vaccination have been conducted in LMICs. Given competing health priorities and fiscal constraints, robust economic evaluation is essential to inform vaccine policy in Brazil and similar countries.

The economic feasibility of CHIKV vaccination in LMICs remains uncertain. Beyond high vaccine prices, substantial costs related to delivery, cold chain, workforce, wastage and outreach challenge implementation in weak health systems.14 In Brazil, projected prices—based on dengue vaccine introduction and current caps—suggest the live-attenuated vaccine may cost about US$172 to US$231 per dose in the public and private sectors, respectively.15 In the USA, the live-attenuated and recombinant vaccines are priced at roughly US$160 to US$275 and US$200 to US$344 per dose.16 Determining whether these vaccines deliver sustained societal value is essential for equitable and financially viable deployment in endemic settings. Therefore, this study evaluated the cost-effectiveness of live-attenuated and recombinant CHIKV vaccines in preventing acute infection and CCD among Brazilian adults, using a dynamic transmission model that captures both acute, postacute and chronic disease outcomes.

Methods

Model structure

We developed a modified Susceptible-Vaccinated-Exposed-Infectious-Recovered-Deceased (SVEIRD) dynamic transmission model, extended with input from a clinician experienced in the Brazilian health system and context, to include a vaccine-exposed susceptible state (SV) for waning or failed immunity, and a chronic state (C) to capture CHIKV sequelae (figure 1). The SVEIRD structure extends the classic Susceptible-Infectious-Recovered (SIR) model by incorporating vaccination, exposure and mortality compartments. Similar SEIRD and SEIRD+V structures and other variants of the SIR model have been previously applied in COVID-19 transmission and economic impact analyses.1723

Model diagram. ψv, vaccination rate for vaccine V; ϵv, effectiveness of vaccine v (varies by vaccine: live or recombinant vaccine); ωv, rate of waning of vaccine-conferred immunity for vaccine v; βS, CHIKV transmission rate among vaccine-naïve susceptible individuals; βSV, CHIKV transmission rate among vaccine-exposed susceptible individuals; λ, rate of progression to the full-blown infection; γR and γC, removal rates from the infectious (I) state to the recovered (R) and chronic disease (C) states; κ, chronic disease recovery rate; μ, background mortality rate; δ, excess mortality risk from CHIKV complications. Notes: The ovals represent the discrete health states in the modified SVEIRD model—susceptible vaccine-naïve (S), susceptible vaccine-exposed (SV), vaccinated (V), exposed (E), infectious (I), recovered (R) and deceased (D). At each cycle (1 week), individuals can transition between health states at specific probabilities. The transitions are represented by arrows connecting the health states. Susceptible (vaccine naïve) individuals can become protected by the vaccine or get vaccinated but not develop antibodies (the vaccine exposed state), exposed to the disease, remain susceptible or die from all causes (background mortality risk). Similarly, vaccinated individuals can become exposed to the disease, die, remain in the vaccinated state or transition to the vaccine exposed state. Individuals in the vaccine-exposed state either never developed protective levels of antibodies or lost immunity due to vaccine waning. These individuals can stay in the vaccine-exposed state, die or become exposed to CHIKV. For the base case analysis, we assume a constant disease transmission rate, β=βS=βSV, for both vaccine-naïve and vaccine-exposed susceptible individuals, thus ignoring risk compensation and adverse selection. The rate β is calculated by multiplying the basic reproduction number by the recovery rate from acute CHIKV: β=γ×R0, where γ=γR+γC, with γR and γC denoting, respectively, the removal rates into the recovered and chronic disease states, and R0 denotes the basic reproduction number for CHIKV. Individuals in the exposed state can progress to the infectious state, die or remain in the exposed state. Individuals in the infectious state can recover, progress to CCD, die or remain in the infectious state. Individuals in a chronic disease state can recover, die or remain in chronic disease. Recovered individuals either stay recovered or die. CCD, chronic chikungunya disease; CHIKV, chikungunya virus.

The model simulated a hypothetical cohort of 1000 Brazilian adults (≥18 years) over 10 years (weekly cycles), using a deterministic approach in the base case. Individuals entered the model either as susceptible (vaccine-naive, S), exposed (E) or infectious (I), based on the annual incidence of CHIKV infection in Brazil. Once in the model, vaccine-naive susceptible individuals could become vaccinated with vaccine v at a rate ψv, and transition into the vaccinated state (V), acquiring full immunity with a probability εv (vaccine efficacy), or transition into the vaccine-exposed susceptible state (SV) with the complement probability 1-εv, where they remained susceptible to infection due to vaccine failure. In the base case analysis, both vaccines were assumed to diffuse at a constant rate ψv=ψ. The waning rates of vaccine-acquired immunity, ωv, were estimated by fitting vaccine-specific exponential decay models to trial-based seroprotection and seroresponse data, assuming a constant rate of decline from baseline seroresponse levels of 98.8% for the live-attenuated vaccine and 97.8% for the recombinant vaccine (online supplemental appendix A, section 2.2.2.3).9 10

Susceptible individuals (both vaccine-naïve and vaccine-exposed) could become exposed via mosquito bites with time-varying probabilities based on respective transmission risks βS and βSV, and infection prevalence (I(t)/N(t)), where I(t) and N(t) denote, respectively, the prevalence of infectious individuals and the total population. Exposed individuals could become infectious at a rate λ, and transition to state I. Similarly, infectious individuals could develop CCD, recover or die, with respective rates γC, γR, and μ+δ, where γC and γR denote the removal rates from the infectious state into the chronic and recovered states, and are defined such that the total removal rate is γ=γC+γR, μ is the background mortality rate, and δ denotes the excess mortality risk from CHIKV complications (online supplemental appendix A, section 2.2.1.1.2). Individuals who developed CCD could recover at a rate κ and transition to state R, die at a rate μ, or sustain lifetime CCD and remain in state C. We assumed recovery from CHIKV infection conferred life-long immunity,24 25 so that they could not transition from the recovered pool (R) back into any susceptible pool (S or SV).

Interventions

We compared vaccination with a live-attenuated or recombinant CHIKV vaccines and no vaccination.

Epidemiological and clinical inputs

Epidemiological and clinical input parameters were derived from published literature, CDC Yellow Book data, and phase 3 clinical trials and are summarised in table 1. Vaccine uptake (ψv) and effectiveness were informed by survey-based chikungunya vaccine acceptability and immunogenicity data, respectively (online supplemental appendix A section 2.2.2.1).26 27 Vaccine efficacy (εv) was proxied by the reported seroprotection and seroresponse rates from phase 3 trials, and longitudinal immunogenicity data were used to estimate vaccine-specific waning rates (ωv)).9 10 28 29 The transmission rate (β) was calculated from the basic reproduction number (R0) and the recovery rate from acute CHIKV (γ) assuming homogeneous mixing (βS=β=γ×R0), with vaccine-modified transmission defined as βSV=η×βS (η=1 in the base case). The estimate for R0 was obtained from prior outbreak analyses,30 while rates of infection (λ) and recovery from acute disease were sourced from the CDC Yellow Book.31 The recovery rate from chronic CHIKV disease (κ) was derived from a published systematic review and meta-analysis,32 and the proportion developing CCD (ρ) was informed by a prospective cohort study from the French West Indies.33 Background mortality risks were obtained from Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics life tables,34 and excess chikungunya-related mortality (δ) was based on estimates from India, due to limited data from Brazil and the Americas.35 36

Health state utilities

Effectiveness was measured in quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), based on utility weights reported in table 2. Country-specific population norms for baseline utility were set at 0.824 (range, 0.820 to 0.828),37 for susceptible and recovered individuals, and adjusted multiplicatively to derive utilities for the infectious, exposed and chronic disease states using state-specific utility multipliers. The multiplier for acute infection was approximated as the complement of the published disability weight for severe dengue fever (0.133; 95% CI 0.088 to 0.190),24 reflecting the absence of chikungunya-specific utility estimates. For CCD, the multiplier was assumed to be similar to that for rheumatoid arthritis38 and was set at 0.850 (range 0.638 to 1.000). Lastly, the multiplier for the exposed (latent) state was assumed to be intermediate between the susceptible and infectious states and set at 0.850 (range 0.638 to 1.000). Death was assigned a utility of zero.

Disutilities from vaccine adverse events (VAEs) were estimated by multiplying the probability of severe or serious VAEs,13 28 39 40 the published disutility for the acute disease stage from US-based studies,134144 and a Brazil-to-US utility multiplier (online supplemental appendix A table S1). The multiplier is defined as the ratio of utility weights for the acute disease state in Brazil versus the USA. Total vaccine-related disutility was calculated as the sum of disutilities from severe and serious adverse events and was estimated separately for each vaccine.

Resource uses and unit costs

Cost components included direct medical costs (vaccination costs, outpatient visits, hospitalisations, diagnostics, medications) for the healthcare sector perspective, and additionally indirect costs—consisting primarily of productivity and caregiving costs—for the societal perspective (table 3).

Vaccination costs were calculated separately for the live-attenuated and recombinant vaccines by combining vaccine acquisition, wastage and administration costs. Vaccine acquisition costs were derived from 2025 public and private-sector price caps for the live-attenuated vaccine (public sector: R$929; range 762 to 989 vs private sector: R$1246; range 1022 to 1327),45 adjusted by an assumed discount factor (0.50; range 0.00 to 0.75) to reflect actual transaction prices and weighted by the proportion of vaccinations delivered in the public (0.75; range, 0.56 to 0.94) versus private sector.13 46 For the recombinant vaccine, acquisition costs were estimated by applying a cost multiplier relative to the unit cost of the live-attenuated vaccine, derived either from the ratio of US list prices or from an assumed multiplier,13 47 and similarly adjusted for sector-specific pricing and price discounting. Total per-dose vaccination costs were then calculated for each vaccine by applying a vaccine wastage rate and adding a per-dose administration cost (table 3 and online supplemental appendix A section 2.2.4.1).

Other direct medical costs were estimated separately for the acute, postacute and chronic phases of illness,48 combining the postacute and chronic phases, and included inpatient care, outpatient visits (one, two and three visits for the acute, postacute and chronic phases, respectively), and medication costs (table 3). We calculated average inpatient hospitalisation costs as a weighted mean of public-sector and private-sector per-stay costs,48 using the proportion of inpatient admissions occurring in the public sector,13 46 and assuming an average hospitalisation length of stay of 11 (range 8 to 14) days.49 Phase-specific outpatient costs were calculated as the product of the assumed number of visits per case and the unit cost per medical appointment (2019 R$23.17; range, 17.38 to 28.96).48 In the acute phase, medication costs were estimated as a weighted average of treatments for minor or moderate pain vs severe pain,48 based on the proportion of acute cases with minor or moderate symptoms.48 50 In the postacute phase, medication costs reflected the share of cases with arthritis treated with prednisone, with remaining cases assigned the mean cost of alternative analgesic therapies.48 50 Lastly, in the chronic phase, medication costs were estimated as a weighted average of treatment for mild illness with hydroxychloroquine versus treatment for more severe illness with methotrexate and folic acid.48 50

We estimated indirect costs as the sum of productivity losses due to absenteeism and the economic value of informal caregiving during the infectious and chronic phases of illness. Productivity losses were calculated using a human-capital approach based on net annual earnings, derived from monthly gross earnings,51 after accounting for mandatory employee social security contributions52 and personal income taxes under the simplified deduction scheme (online supplemental appendix A section 2.2.4.3).53 54 Next, we calculated the daily productivity loss by dividing net annual income by 365 days and estimated absenteeism costs by multiplying daily productivity loss by the number of workdays lost in the infectious and remaining chronic phases, weighted by phase-specific absenteeism frequencies (table 3).2 We valued informal caregiving costs using gross daily earnings, calculated as the product of caregiving days and the probability of receiving care in each phase.2 From these, we calculated the total indirect costs by summing absenteeism and caregiving costs across phases.

All costs were inflation-adjusted to 2025 R$ using the Brazilian Broad National Consumer Price Index (IPCA),41 and converted to US$ using the average 2025 R$-to-US$ exchange rate of US$1.00=R$5.59 (table 4).55

Outcomes and measures

Baseline distributions of the cohort in the health states

In each cycle, we simulated the number of individuals in each health state.

Health effects

To estimate the total QALYs for each strategy, we multiplied the number of individuals in each state by the corresponding utility weights and expected duration, then summed across states and over the entire 10-year follow-up period. The total QALYs were discounted, assuming a 5.00% annual social discount rate.5659 Next, we calculated the incremental discounted QALYs between pairs of strategies to estimate the marginal health effects of the intervention. We calculated incremental discounted QALYs between pairs of strategies to estimate marginal health effects, comparing each vaccine with no vaccination and with each other.

Costs and incremental costs

We calculated the total costs associated with each comparator intervention, by aggregating costs across all individuals and health states, over the entire 10-year follow-up period. Costs were also discounted at the same rate as the QALYs, to avoid the Keeler-Cretin paradox.5660 We then calculated the incremental costs for each vaccination strategy relative to no vaccination and to each other, to capture the total marginal cost of adopting the intervention.

Net monetary benefit

We additionally calculated the net monetary benefit (NMB) of each comparator strategy by monetising the QALYs accumulated under the strategy, net of the opportunity costs of the resource uses required to produce those health outcomes. Monetisation of the QALYs was done by multiplying the QALYs by the average willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of US$10 306 (R$57 601) per QALY gained (one-time GDP per capita) in the base case analysis61: NMB=QALY×WTP–Cost. The intervention with the highest NMB is preferred.

Analyses: economic evaluation

Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio

The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) between pairs of alternatives i and j was calculated as a ratio of their incremental costs, ΔCostij, to their incremental effects, ΔQALYij: ICERij = ΔCostij/ΔQALYij. The ICERs were then compared with the WTP threshold of US$10 306 (R$57 601) per QALY gained to determine cost-effectiveness.61

Incremental NMB

As the ICER can be difficult to interpret without the cost-effectiveness plane and does not offer a straightforward approach for rank-ordering interventions in terms of their value, we also calculated the incremental NMB (INMB) for each alternative vaccination intervention, relative to no vaccination, and relative to each other. The INMB was calculated as the difference between the NMBs of the strategies being compared. The strategy with the highest NMB and INMB >0 with respect to the next-best alternative was considered the optimal intervention.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted one-way deterministic sensitivity analyses—sequentially varying each parameter over its uncertainty bounds, while holding all other parameters at their base case values—to assess its impact on the INMBs of live-attenuated and recombinant CHIKV vaccines vs no vaccination. We additionally conducted probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSA) with 1000 iterations to evaluate the joint uncertainty in model parameters and estimate the probability that each strategy is cost-effective across WTP thresholds.62 Model parameters were assigned probability distributions informed by the available evidence, and repeated simulations were performed to generate joint distributions of epidemiologic, costs and health outcomes. Total and incremental outcome values (ie, costs, QALYs and NMBs at the WTP threshold of US$10 306 (R$57 601) per QALY gained61 were calculated within each simulation. We summarised uncertainty using posterior means and 95% Bayesian credible intervals, defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the simulated distributions.

To examine the potential impact of postvaccination behavioural responses on infection risk and cost-effectiveness, we allowed transmission rates to differ between vaccine-naïve and vaccine-exposed susceptible individuals (online supplemental appendix A section 3.2.2). We further evaluated the impact of differential vaccine uptake, attenuated vaccine effectiveness, alternative analytic time horizons and vaccine price discounts (online supplemental appendix A section 2.2.2.1).

All sensitivity analyses were performed from both healthcare sector and societal perspectives, with results summarised using tornado plots, heatmaps and scenario comparisons (online supplemental appendix A section 3.2).

Model implementation

All analyses were conducted in the R programming language,63 and scripts are provided in online supplemental appendix C as well as in the Github repository (https://github.com/ChrisXiao12/Chikungunya-CEA).

Patient and public involvement

Neither patients nor the public were involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study.

Reporting

All analyses were conducted and findings reported in accordance with the National Commission for Health Technology Incorporation’s methodological standards for economic evaluations in Brazil,64 the 2022 Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards guidelines (online supplemental appendix D), and the recommendations from the Second Panel on Cost Effectiveness in Health and Medicine (impact inventory, online supplemental appendix E).65

Results

Base case

In the base-case analysis, the live-attenuated CHIKV vaccine dominated both recombinant CHIKV vaccine and no vaccination, as it was more effective and less costly than both across the healthcare sector and societal perspectives (table 5 and online supplemental appendix A table S4). Under the healthcare sector perspective, 335 472 QALYs were accumulated with no vaccination at the total discounted cost of US$0.02 million (R$0.11 million), resulting in an NMB of US$3.46 billion (R$19.32 billion) at the assumed WTP threshold of US$10 306 (R$57 601) per QALY gained. In comparison, vaccination with the live-attenuated vaccine produced 336 011 QALYs and cost US$0.12 million (R$0.68 million), translating to an NMB of US$3.46 billion (R$19.35 billion), whereas vaccination with the recombinant vaccine yielded 335 787 QALYs and cost US$0.15 billion (R$0.80 million), translating to an NMB of US$3.46 billion (R$19.34 billion). Therefore, compared with no vaccination, the live-attenuated vaccine produced 539 more QALYs at the additional cost of approximately US$0.10 million (R$0.57 million), resulting in an INMB of US$5.46 million (R$30.50 million). In comparison, the recombinant vaccine produced 315 more QALYs and saved US$0.13 million (R$0.72 million), resulting in an INMB of US$3.12 million (R$17.44 million).

Results from the societal perspective were qualitatively similar to those from the healthcare sector, but with larger absolute costs reflecting the inclusion of non-healthcare expenditures. The total discounted societal cost of not vaccinating was US$2.76 million (R$15.44 million), yielding an NMB of US$3.46 billion (R$19.31 billion). Compared with no vaccination, the live-attenuated vaccine had an incremental cost of US$0.09 million (R$0.49 million), corresponding to an INMB of US$5.47 million (R$30.58 million). The recombinant vaccine cost US$2.88 million (R$16.11 million), or US$0.12 million (R$0.67 million) more than no vaccination, and resulting in an INMB of US$3.13 million (R$17.49 million). As in the healthcare sector analysis, the live-attenuated vaccine dominated the recombinant vaccine, offering greater health benefits at lower overall societal cost. Combined, these results suggest that vaccination with the live-attenuated CHIKV vaccine was the most cost-effective strategy among all the strategies considered.

Sensitivity analyses

In one-way deterministic sensitivity analyses, changes in the probability of recovering from chronic disease, utility in the chronic disease state and the cost of infection had the largest influence on the INMBs of vaccination with both live-attenuated and recombinant CHIKV vaccines, relative to no vaccination (figure 2). Higher costs and greater likelihood of CHIKV-related chronic disease make vaccination both more effective and less costly, and vice versa. Notably, higher disease transmission rates reduced the INMB of both vaccines, relative to no vaccination, under our model’s implicit assumption of equal transmission rates (ie, βS=βSV=β) for vaccine-naive and vaccine-exposed susceptible individuals (S and SV).

Results were largely insensitive to modest behavioural responses following vaccination, including risk compensation or selective uptake, reflecting low modelled vaccine coverage rates. Analyses varying vaccine effectiveness, analytic time horizon and relative vaccine uptake confirmed that vaccination remained economically favourable under conservative assumptions, with consistently higher INMBs for the live-attenuated vaccine and larger gains under the societal perspective.

The PSA supported the favourable cost-effectiveness profile of the live-attenuated vaccine in almost 100% of simulations at the WTP threshold of US$10 306 (R$57 601) per QALY gained. The live-attenuated vaccine was preferred in almost 100% of simulations across the entire range (US$4942 to US$39 539; R$27 624 to R$220 990) of WTP threshold values (0.5 and 4 times GDP per capita).61 Results remained robust to parameter uncertainties, as evidenced by the means and CIs of the simulated INMB estimates for live-attenuated and recombinant vaccines relative to no vaccination, and for live-attenuated vaccine relative to recombinant vaccine (table 6 and online supplemental appendix A table S5). Combined, these results support a stability of the cost-effectiveness profile of vaccination across epidemiologic, behavioural and economic uncertainties.

Discussion

In this study, we used a dynamic transmission model (modified SVEIRD model) to assess the cost-effectiveness of two vaccination strategies against CHIKV compared with no vaccination among Brazilian adults (≥18 years). At a societal WTP threshold of US$10 306 (R$57 601) per QALY gained, the live-attenuated vaccine yielded the highest NMB and was the preferred strategy in almost 100% of PSA simulations. Although both vaccines were generally cost-saving, the live-attenuated vaccine remained dominant across a battery of deterministic sensitivity analyses and PSA.

As expected, higher postvaccination transmission due to risk compensation modestly reduced NMB, although cost-effectiveness profile remained largely robust under low vaccine uptake. This limited impact reflects the relatively low vaccination coverage (~35%) assumed in the model, which constrains population-level risk reduction and attenuates the economic consequences of behavioural responses. These findings underscore that, while small adverse behavioural changes have minimal economic impact at low coverage, achieving higher uptake would be necessary for vaccination strategies to realise their full population-level health and economic benefits. Additionally, and despite uncertainty in model inputs, the findings were robust. PSA (1000 iterations) confirmed the dominance of the live-attenuated vaccine across a wide range of scenarios. While small variations in utility weights or adverse events occasionally altered rankings, overall results were consistent.

CHIKV remains a neglected tropical disease, causing recurrent outbreaks and long-term disability. Its spread is accelerating due to climate change, urbanisation and global travel, underscoring the need for preventive strategies that extend beyond current endemic zones. Our findings offer timely evidence for policy action. Routine vaccination of at-risk populations in Brazil could yield substantial health and economic benefits. Given the growing global burden of CHIKV, these results are relevant for other middle-income, endemic countries considering vaccine adoption, as well as to clinicians advising travellers who may be considering chikungunya vaccination.66 Further research on vaccine uptake, long-term effectiveness and CHIKV-specific utility values is needed to support implementation.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study evaluating both the long-term clinical and economic outcomes of CHIKV vaccination in a chikungunya-endemic LMIC setting. By incorporating both acute and chronic disease phases and adopting a societal perspective, our model addresses critical gaps in prior research, which has largely focused on short-term outbreaks and vector control. Strengths of this study include the use of a dynamic transmission model with a 10-year horizon, incorporation of both acute and chronic health impacts, and a societal perspective that accounts for productivity loss and informal caregiving. We also used recent trial data for vaccine efficacy and adverse events, and Brazil-specific cost and utility inputs where available.

Limitations

Limitations include uncertainty around vaccine pricing, limited availability of CHIKV-specific utility values, potential waning of vaccine-induced immunity and assumptions about vaccine uptake. The model did not include vector control strategies or broader health system dynamics, which may influence real-world effectiveness. Our assumption of equal transmission rates for both vaccine-naive and vaccine-exposed susceptible individuals may not hold if vaccination influences protective behaviour. While some serious adverse events have been reported in postmarketing surveillance, mainly among older adults and immunocompromised individuals,67 68 no causal link has been established, and these populations were outside the scope of our analysis, which focused on working-age adults.

Conclusions

This study provides the first cost-effectiveness analysis of chikungunya vaccines in an endemic middle-income setting. Both the live-attenuated and recombinant vaccines were cost-effective compared with no vaccination, with the live-attenuated vaccine yielding greater NMB under base-case assumptions. However, given ongoing postmarketing safety evaluations of the live-attenuated product, policy decisions should weigh comparative effectiveness alongside emerging safety data and population-specific risk profiles. Overall, our findings indicate that chikungunya vaccination represents a potentially scalable cost-effective strategy to reduce the long-term health and economic burden of disease in endemic settings.
