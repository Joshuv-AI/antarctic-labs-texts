Circulating cell-free DNA as a biomarker in systemic lupus erythematosus: A narrative review
Authors: Klaudia Andrea Garai, Attila Szederjesi, Anna Bazsó, Péter Szodoray, Yehuda Shoenfeld, György Nagy, Emese Virág Kiss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multifactorial, clinically heterogenous autoimmune disease. Reliable biomarkers are essential for early diagnosis, disease activity monitoring, flare prediction, and guiding therapeutic decisions. Biomarkers currently used in clinical practice - such as inflammatory markers, complement levels, and anti–double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies - are valuable but lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity for predicting flares and disease progression. Circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) has emerged as a promising biomarker candidate.ObjectivesTo provide a comprehensive and critical overview of the current state of knowledge regarding cfDNA in relation to SLE, highlighting key findings, methodological advances, pathomechanistic insights, and areas requiring further research.MethodsThis narrative review summarizes key findings on cfDNA in SLE based on studies published between 1966 and 2025. Relevant articles were identified through PubMed and Google Scholar using keywords. Studies investigating cfDNA as a biomarker, methodological advances in cfDNA analysis, and key findings regarding the pathomechanistic relationship between cfDNA and SLE were prioritized.ResultsCfDNA originates principally from apoptosis, necrosis, and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation. Advances in molecular technologies have enabled detailed characterization of cfDNA concentration, fragmentation patterns, and epigenetic modifications. These features correlate with disease activity and immunological abnormalities in SLE. More recent studies have expanded the scope of nucleic-acid–based biomarkers.ConclusionsCirculating cfDNA and related nucleic-acid biomarkers represent a promising avenue for improving disease monitoring and personalized management in SLE. However, further large-scale studies and methodological standardization are required before these biomarkers can be routinely implemented in clinical practice.Keywords: Biomarkers, Circulating cell-free DNA, Epigenetics, Lupus nephritis, Systemic lupus erythematosus

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multifactorial autoimmune connective tissue disease with an exceptionally broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, capable of affecting virtually any organ system. It predominantly affects women of reproductive age, although it can develop at any age and in men as well. The most common manifestations include mucocutaneous, musculoskeletal, and renal involvement. The clinical course is typically characterized by alternating periods of relapse and remission. Severe complications may arise, including renal failure due to lupus nephritis, thromboembolic events, infectious complications, and central nervous system involvement. Although several aspects of its pathomechanism remain incompletely understood, ongoing research continues to expand our knowledge of the underlying mechanisms and to identify novel therapeutic targets for more individualized treatment strategies.

The central pathomechanistic events in SLE involve the loss of immune tolerance to self-antigens and the development of a multisystem inflammatory process mediated by autoantibody production and immune complex deposition. Triggering factors are diverse and include genetic, epigenetic, hormonal, and environmental elements; infections, certain medications, and ultraviolet (UV) radiation are particularly significant. These triggers lead to aberrant activation of both the innate and adaptive immune systems - especially autoreactive B and T lymphocytes - resulting in the production of pro-inflammatory mediators, most notably type I interferons [1]. Increased cellular necrosis and apoptosis result in the release of chromatin and nucleoproteins into the circulation, while their clearance and degradation become impaired [2]. This process contributes to the formation of anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) autoantibodies, and cell-free DNA (cfDNA) is generated through the same mechanism.

For a favorable prognosis in SLE, early and accurate diagnosis is essential, alongside regular monitoring through the evaluation of clinical symptoms, laboratory parameters, and disease activity indices. Timely selection of appropriate therapeutic strategies and monitoring of treatment response are equally critical. Biomarkers play an important role in facilitating each of these steps. Although they are rarely used in isolation for diagnosis, they are valuable for refining diagnostic assessments, estimating disease activity, and evaluating organ involvement. The concept of biomarkers encompasses a wide range of measurable parameters, including values obtained from blood tests, genetic analyses, and imaging.

In current clinical practice, inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), complement levels (C3 and C4), and anti-dsDNA titers are commonly used for prognostication and monitoring of SLE. In selected cases, more detailed immunoserological testing is performed, including detection of antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antiphospholipid antibodies. These parameters are useful and allow the calculation of validated disease activity indices, but they lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity. Furthermore, they do not reliably indicate specific organ involvement and have limited predictive value for disease flares.

Renal biopsy remains the gold standard for detection, classification, and monitoring of renal involvement in SLE. However, this is an invasive procedure that carries procedural risks, and the number of times it can be safely repeated is limited. Although urinary protein excretion can be quantified, this parameter does not delineate the specific histological class of renal involvement. The identification of non-invasive, sensitive, and specific biomarkers is therefore critical for the optimal management of these patients.

An ideal biomarker supports clinical diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making. It should be disease-specific, suitable for estimating disease activity and monitoring progression, and easy to detect in a cost-effective manner. When considered in the context of the remarkable heterogeneity of SLE, it becomes clear that no single, lupus-specific ideal biomarker exists [3]. Research efforts therefore focus on a broad range of candidates, including autoantibodies, cytokines, chemokines, adhesion molecules, serum and urinary proteins, and genetic and epigenetic markers [4]. Nevertheless, clinical practice still lacks a comprehensive biomarker panel that simultaneously captures organ involvement while remaining individually applicable. One potential candidate for such a role is cell-free DNA (cfDNA).

The purpose of this narrative review is to assess the current state of knowledge regarding cfDNA as a biomarker in SLE, focusing on the major milestones achieved over the last 60 years of research. In oncology, particularly in colorectal cancer, and in prenatal testing, liquid biopsy has already been implemented in clinical practice. Autoimmune diseases represent a promising emerging field for the application of cfDNA-based approaches, as personalized disease monitoring and treatment selection are of particular importance in these chronic and heterogeneous conditions. In this review, we reflect on the strengths and limitations of the available studies, identify current gaps in knowledge, and explore how cfDNA-based approaches could potentially be translated into clinical practice. Particular emphasis is placed on the potential translational applications of cfDNA for disease activity monitoring, assessment of organ involvement, monitoring of therapeutic response, patient stratification, and the development of more personalized management strategies in SLE.

This narrative literature review was based on a structured literature search and selection strategy, following the general principles of narrative medical reviews [4]. It is based on a literature search of studies published between 1966 and 2026, primarily identified through PubMed and Google Scholar, supplemented by additional web-based and journal-specific searches using combinations of terms related to cfDNA, systemic lupus erythematosus, and lupus nephritis. Relevant studies were selected based on their contribution to the understanding of cfDNA as a biomarker, methodological advances, and the pathomechanistic relationship between cfDNA and SLE, with particular emphasis on recent developments and potential clinical and translational applications.

Conceptual framework: cfDNA, nucleosomes, anti-dsDNA antibodies

Three interconnected analytes recur throughout the SLE biomarker literature, and their relationships merit explicit clarification before reviewing the evidence.

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) refers to DNA fragments of varying sizes that circulate freely - either in solution or enclosed within extracellular vesicles — in blood, serum, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, and other body fluids. It is the physical nucleic-acid substrate released during cell death and, in SLE, is disproportionately derived from apoptotic cells, necrotic debris, and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). CfDNA is the raw material that drives downstream autoimmune responses.

Nucleosomes are the primary structural unit of chromatin, consisting of approximately 147 base pairs of DNA wrapped around a histone octamer. During apoptosis, chromatin undergoes nucleosomal laddering, releasing nucleosome-bound DNA into the circulation. Circulating nucleosomes therefore represent a specific subform of cfDNA that retains its histone scaffold. Anti-nucleosome antibodies are autoantibodies that target this DNA–histone complex. Importantly, measuring anti-nucleosome antibodies is not the same as quantifying cfDNA itself: the antibody assay detects the immune response to nucleosomal cfDNA, whereas direct cfDNA assays measure the nucleic-acid substrate regardless of immune complex formation.

Anti-dsDNA antibodies are autoantibodies directed against double-stranded DNA epitopes and represent one of the hallmark serological features of SLE. They may bind to cfDNA circulating in plasma, forming immune complexes that are subsequently deposited in tissues - particularly the kidney - thereby driving end-organ damage. The inverse correlation observed in several studies between cfDNA levels and anti-dsDNA titers is explained by this sequestration: during active disease, rising anti-dsDNA antibodies bind and remove cfDNA from the soluble phase, reducing measurable circulating levels while worsening organ damage.

In summary, cfDNA is the source material; nucleosomes are a structurally intact subform of cfDNA; anti-nucleosome and anti-dsDNA antibodies are immune responses directed against these substrates. Elevated cfDNA drives autoantibody production, and the resulting immune complexes feedback to propagate tissue inflammation - a cycle central to SLE pathogenesis.

Sources and detection of cfDNA

The most well-characterized sources of cfDNA are apoptosis and necrosis; however, living cells may also actively release DNA through secretory mechanisms, typically within microvesicles. This phenomenon has been observed in proliferating tumor cell cultures, although non-dividing lymphocytes can also release cfDNA [5]. Some reports suggest that approximately 90% of circulating cfDNA is enclosed within exosomes, though this figure may vary considerably depending on the biological context [6].

CfDNA may also be generated through pyroptosis - a form of rapid, inflammatory cell death triggered by inflammasome activation in response to infectious stimuli. In SLE, NETosis, the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps, is particularly prominent and represents a quantitatively important source of cfDNA [6]. CfDNA may originate from both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes; the latter has distinct structural features, including a higher proportion of hypomethylated CpG dinucleotides, that confer particular immunostimulatory properties. Elevated cfDNA levels may occur in autoimmune conditions, malignancies, and benign lesions, but they can also be detected in healthy individuals as a result of physical exertion or age-related physiological processes [6]. It is established that production of cfDNA alone is not a pathological process. However, in SLE, it becomes a powerful driver of autoimmunity. The imbalance between the increased production by accelerated apoptosis and NETosis and the various defects of DNA elimination results in autoantibody formation against the cellular remnants, such as dsDNA, histones and nucleosomes. Poor elimination mainly stems from reduced DNaseI/DNASE1L3 enzyme activity, complement deficiencies, impaired NET degradation and hypomethylation that makes nucleosomes defective against degradation [7].

The characterization of cfDNA size, fragmentation patterns, and epigenetic modifications can help distinguish between different sources and etiologies. In particular, the analysis of DNA methylation patterns is highly relevant in SLE. Studies have demonstrated that both T-cell DNA and cfDNA exhibit global hypomethylation in this disease, which contributes to enhanced autoimmune activity, and that the degree of methylation correlates with disease activity [9].

The extraction of cfDNA from biological samples is often referred to as a liquid biopsy - a term that has become particularly widespread in oncology research. Following collection of body fluid samples, most commonly blood or serum, preservation of cfDNA can be challenging due to its rapid turnover and short half-life. Specialized collection tubes have been developed to address this issue, and some modern systems employ magnetic bead–based technologies to facilitate isolation. After collection, specimens are typically centrifuged to remove cellular components, and the isolated cfDNA is analyzed using various next-generation sequencing (NGS) and non-NGS–based techniques, including quantitative PCR (qPCR), mass spectrometry–based PCR, and microarray fluorescence analysis [10].

Mechanisms of action in SLE

One of the reasons why cfDNA is a promising biomarker candidate is that it is not only a passive bystander, but may also play an active role in the pathomechanism of SLE. In healthy individuals, some cfDNA is released as a consequence of physiological cell death and tissue damage and is subsequently cleared within a relatively short timeframe. CfDNA can originate from various cell types and tissues, depending on the underlying process and cellular origin - for example, as nuclear, mitochondrial, NET-derived, tumor-derived, immune cell-derived, or tissue-specific cfDNA, as well as fetal or pathogen-derived DNA, such as DNA from bacteria or viruses. In autoimmune diseases such as SLE, cfDNA may become immunostimulatory for two main reasons: 1) its biological and molecular characteristics may influence its immunogenicity, and 2) its clearance may be impaired.

Certain epigenetic features, such as hypomethylation, hydroxymethylation, and specific histone marks, as well as fragmentomic features, including fragment size, jagged ends, and 5′ end motifs, may influence the immunogenicity and immune-sensing properties of cfDNA [11]. These altered cfDNA forms may be more likely to activate innate immune pathways, including those leading to type I interferon production.

NETosis can directly contribute to plasmacytoid dendritic cell activation through TLR9 stimulation. cfDNA released during NETosis can form complexes with antimicrobial peptides such as LL-37, which can protect the DNA from degradation and facilitate its uptake by plasmacytoid dendritic cells. Furthermore, SLE patients can develop autoantibodies against DNA-associated proteins, leading to the formation of immune complexes. These cfDNA-containing immune complexes promote chronic plasmacytoid dendritic cell activation through endosomal TLR9, thereby inducing type I interferon production and contributing to further NET formation, creating a vicious circle [12].

In parallel, DNA associated with apoptosis-derived membrane vesicles has been shown to activate the cytosolic cGAS–STING pathway and induce type I interferon responses, providing another mechanism through which self-DNA can trigger innate immune activation in SLE [13]. Regardless of the sensing pathway involved, sustained type I interferon signaling promotes immune activation and autoantibody production, which can lead to immune complex formation and deposition, contributing to further tissue damage and additional release of immunogenic self-DNA.

Another important aspect is the impaired clearance of cfDNA. Loss-of-function mutations in DNASE1L3 can cause monogenic forms of SLE. In addition, Hartl et al. demonstrated that more than 50% of patients with sporadic SLE and renal involvement had reduced DNASE1L3 activity, which was associated with the presence of neutralizing anti-DNASE1L3 autoantibodies. In these patients, cfDNA accumulated in microparticles, with a higher proportion of polynucleosomal cfDNA fragments. These longer DNA fragments showed increased binding to autoantibodies and a greater capacity to induce type I interferon responses, suggesting that impaired cfDNA clearance and altered fragment characteristics may contribute to its pathogenic potential [14].

Methods

This narrative literature review was conducted according to a pre-specified search and selection strategy. The primary databases searched were PubMed and Google Scholar, supplemented by additional web-based and journal-specific searches in Frontiers, Genome Biology, Lupus Science & Medicine, and NPJ Genomic Medicine. The following MeSH terms and free-text keywords were used in varying combinations: “cell-free DNA,” “cfDNA,” “circulating DNA,” “nucleosome DNA,” “mitochondrial DNA,” combined with “systemic lupus erythematosus” or “lupus nephritis."

Studies published between 1966 and 2026 were considered, with particular emphasis on research published after 2021. Priority was given to English-language studies that: (1) investigated nuclear or mitochondrial cfDNA as a biomarker in SLE; (2) introduced novel analytical methods for cfDNA measurement with potential clinical applicability; or (3) provided pathomechanistic insight into the relationship between cfDNA and SLE pathogenesis, particularly findings with therapeutic implications. Early studies were included due to their historical significance in shaping the field.

Articles focusing specifically on the relationship between cfDNA and SLE were prioritized over studies addressing autoimmune diseases broadly. For studies indexed in PubMed, a citation threshold of at least 50 citations was applied as a surrogate marker of scholarly impact for older publications. For studies published after 2021, where citation counts have not yet matured, selection was based on journal impact factor and the novelty of methodological or conceptual contributions relative to prior work. Studies were excluded if they examined cfDNA exclusively in non-SLE autoimmune diseases, did not report original data, or lacked methodological detail sufficient to evaluate the validity of reported findings.

The review presents key developments in chronological order, organized thematically to highlight methodological progress, biomarker findings, pathomechanistic insights, and emerging therapeutic implications.

Results

Historical foundation: 1948-1966

In 1948, Mandel and Métais first identified circulating cell-free DNA in the serum of cancer patients [15]. This discovery eventually influenced multiple fields of medicine. Leon et al. were the first to systematically investigate cfDNA as a diagnostic tool in malignant disease, though in 1977 the technical infrastructure for broader clinical application was not yet available [16]. The advent of high-throughput parallel sequencing in 2011 catalyzed the translation of cfDNA analysis into clinical diagnostics; today approximately half of prenatal genetic tests are performed as non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) [17].

The earliest SLE-related study was published in 1966, when Tan et al. detected elevated cfDNA levels in the serum of patients with SLE and proposed a relationship between anti-DNA antibodies and cfDNA levels [18]. This observation established that cfDNA might be relevant in the context of autoimmune disease. In 1980, Raptis and Menard compared plasma cfDNA levels in healthy controls and patients with autoimmune diseases using enzymatic labeling with DNase I, a technique of greater sensitivity than its predecessors. As a result, the difference between groups was less pronounced than previously reported, except in clinically active SLE, leading the authors to propose cfDNA as a potential acute-phase reactant [19].

Early characterization studies: 1984-2003

In 1984, Steinman et al. provided evidence that circulating double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) is present in plasma in SLE, often in the form of immune complexes, and may contribute to disease pathogenesis. However, the study was limited by the low sensitivity of counterimmunoelectrophoresis (CIE) as the primary detection method; cfDNA was detectable only in a small subset of patients, predominantly those with clinically confirmed vasculitis [20].

In 1997, Amoura et al. investigated the relationship between circulating nucleosomes and disease activity, measuring nucleosome levels - representing DNA-histone complexes released from dying cells - alongside anti-nucleosome, anti-dsDNA, and anti-histone antibodies by ELISA. Disease activity was assessed using the SLEDAI index. An inverse correlation between nucleosome levels and autoantibody titers was observed; however, nucleosome levels did not correlate with disease activity, underscoring the need for longitudinal studies [21].

Building on these findings, Kiss et al., in 2009 conducted a study with a similarly large patient cohort, measuring anti-nucleosome, anti-dsDNA, and antinuclear antibodies (ANA), together with complement activity (CH50), in patients with biopsy-confirmed lupus nephritis followed for a mean of 8.5 years. A lower frequency of ANA positivity was observed compared with the Amoura cohort, attributable in part to geographic population differences and lower average disease activity over the prolonged follow-up period. Importantly, the different antibody specificities frequently co-occurred, and the strongest correlation with lupus nephritis was identified for anti-nucleosome antibodies, implicating them in renal pathogenesis [22].

In 2002, Min et al. identified a correlation between anti-nucleosome antibodies and disease activity, particularly among patients lacking detectable anti-dsDNA antibodies - suggesting that anti-nucleosome antibodies may have additive diagnostic value in seronegative cases. Approximately half of anti-nucleosome–positive patients exhibited renal involvement, whereas those testing negative did not, indicating potential utility for identifying nephritis risk [23].

A shift toward qualitative analysis emerged in 2003, when Galeazzi et al. characterized cfDNA from plasma, serum, and cellular fractions of healthy individuals, SLE patients, and patients with systemic sclerosis using gel electrophoresis. Distinct fragmentation patterns were observed between patient groups and healthy controls, and disease-specific differences were identified between SLE and systemic sclerosis [24].

Epigenetic contributions and pathomechanistic insights: 2004-2019

In 2004, Pisetsky et al. conducted an experimental mouse study to test the hypothesis that cfDNA serves as a marker of cell death in SLE. Apoptosis was induced by multiple methods with variable success: anti-Fas antibody and lipopolysaccharide administration resulted in measurable DNA release, whereas dexamethasone did not, suggesting that cfDNA release is not an invariable consequence of apoptosis. Macrophage-dependent mechanisms also contributed to cfDNA generation. The authors concluded that cfDNA may serve as a biomarker in SLE, but that the underlying mechanisms are complex and require further investigation [25].

In 2006, Pisetsky extended this line of inquiry by examining the roles of necrosis and inflammation in DNA release, proposing that macrophages may undergo necrosis during phagocytosis, releasing intracellular contents. Additionally, lower circulating DNA levels were observed in male mice, suggesting a potential modulatory role of estrogen. Glucocorticoids were also shown to influence circulating DNA levels [26].

A landmark twin study by Javierre et al., in 2010 examined epigenetic DNA modifications in SLE using monozygotic twin pairs discordant for disease. Hypomethylation was identified in genes already implicated in SLE pathogenesis, including interferon-gamma receptor 2 (IFNGR2) and matrix metalloproteinase genes. Gene-specific hypomethylation appeared more informative than global methylation changes, and epigenetic differences became more pronounced with age. Although the study could not resolve whether epigenetic modifications represent a cause or consequence of disease, it significantly advanced understanding of the role of epigenetics in autoimmunity [27].

In 2014, Tug et al. demonstrated that plasma cfDNA levels were significantly elevated in SLE patients compared with healthy controls, with significant inverse correlations with complement levels (C3 and C4), consistent with complement consumption during active inflammation. Notably, cfDNA showed an inverse relationship with anti-dsDNA antibody titers - likely reflecting sequestration of cfDNA into immune complexes during active disease. DNase I activity was reduced in SLE patients, suggesting impaired cfDNA clearance, although no statistically significant correlation with cfDNA levels was established [28].

A prominent study in 2015 by Chan et al. employed massively parallel sequencing to investigate plasma cfDNA from patients with active and inactive SLE and healthy controls, focusing on genomic aberrations, fragmentation patterns, and methylation profiles. The extent of genomic abnormalities correlated with anti-dsDNA autoantibody levels. Both cfDNA fragmentation and hypomethylation were positively associated with anti-dsDNA titers and SLEDAI-defined disease activity. IgG-bound DNA levels were higher in SLE patients, with preferential binding to hypomethylated, CpG-rich regions; bound fragments were shorter and exhibited greater genomic aberrations, suggesting that antibody binding influences DNA stability. Importantly, the study also cautioned that cfDNA-based diagnostic algorithms designed for other indications - such as NIPT or liquid biopsy in oncology - must be interpreted differently in SLE, as disease-related hypomethylation and fragmentation may confound these assays [29].

In 2016, Hendy et al. did not identify a significant correlation between cfDNA levels and SLEDAI scores, but observed a significant decline in cfDNA levels following treatment with cyclophosphamide, azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil, concurrent with reductions in anti-nucleosome antibody levels. This raised the possibility that cfDNA may serve as a pharmacodynamic biomarker for monitoring therapeutic response [30].

A comprehensive review by Truszewska et al., in 2016 synthesized accumulated findings, emphasizing the importance of cfDNA fragmentation patterns and hypomethylation, identifying apoptosis and NETosis as the two principal sources of cfDNA in SLE, and recommending methodological standardization to enable cfDNA to function as a reliable biomarker in this heterogeneous population. Lupus nephritis was identified as the organ manifestation most strongly associated with cfDNA levels [9]. The significance of renal disease activity was further emphasized in 2019 by Arneth et al. whose literature review primarily focused on disturbances in DNA degradation and clearance [9].

In 2019, Jeremić et al. investigated cfDNA in the context of NET-associated markers, demonstrating that NETosis represents a major source of autoantigens in SLE pathogenesis. Compared with healthy controls, all NET-associated markers - including cfDNA, myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity, anti-MPO antibodies, and DNase I concentration - were elevated in SLE patients, whereas NETolytic activity was paradoxically reduced. This apparent discrepancy was attributed to the presence of inhibitors of DNase I function, including actin and specific autoantibodies, despite higher enzyme concentrations. Treatment-naive patients had significantly higher cfDNA levels and MPO activity than treated patients. Among serological markers, cfDNA correlated with anti-dsDNA antibody levels and with B-cell activating factor (BAFF) levels - the latter being the therapeutic target of belimumab - highlighting potential pathomechanistic and therapeutic relevance [31].

Methodological advances: 2021-2022

From a methodological perspective, the 2021 study by Neuberger et al. introduced a direct quantification qPCR assay from blood plasma that omits the DNA isolation step, performed in accordance with ISO guidelines. The study exploited the known effect of physical exercise on cfDNA levels - which double in SLE patients after light walking and may increase up to tenfold after strenuous activity, returning to baseline within approximately 1 h of rest. In addition to reducing costs and the risk of extraction-related measurement error, this approach provides an indirect index of pre-analytical compliance: inadequately elevated levels may indicate prior physical exertion, whereas persistently low levels despite exercise may indicate remission [32].

In 2021, Giaglis et al. measured the copy number of circulating mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) in blood samples from SLE patients and healthy controls using quantitative PCR. mtDNA levels distinguished the two groups with high sensitivity and specificity, even in SLE patients in clinical remission. SLEDAI scores were influenced by mtDNA copy number, prednisolone dose, and anti-dsDNA titer, but not by nuclear cfDNA - suggesting that mtDNA captures a dimension of disease activity not reflected by nuclear cfDNA alone. Although the sample size was modest (103 SLE patients and 56 healthy donors), the results were promising, and the methodology was deemed readily replicable. mtDNA is not entirely SLE-specific, as copy numbers may also increase in other interferonopathies and following trauma, but it clearly distinguishes SLE patients from healthy individuals and serves as a meaningful predictor of disease activity. Mechanistically, mtDNA functions as a ligand for TLR9 and cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), activating the type I interferon signaling pathway and driving autoantibody formation, and can also prime neutrophils to release additional DNA, thereby sustaining a vicious cycle [33].

In 2022, Wang et al. enrolled 127 patients, of whom 64 had biopsy-confirmed lupus nephritis (LN), in a study aimed at characterizing the biological features of cfDNA from serological, fragmentomic, and epigenetic perspectives - with the goal of establishing liquid biopsy as a less invasive alternative to repeat renal biopsy. Patients with LN exhibited lower cfDNA concentrations and shorter fragment sizes compared with non-nephritis SLE patients, findings attributed to higher anti-dsDNA titers sequestering nucleic acids. Methylation profiling by next-generation sequencing and machine learning identified more aberrant methylation patterns in the LN subgroup; however, only three LN patients could be included in the methylation analysis due to insufficient cfDNA yield in the remainder. The principal contribution of this study lies in the application of modern fragmentomic and epigenomic approaches to the LN population - an underrepresented group in prior cfDNA research - rather than in definitive quantitative findings [34].

Expanding the biomarker landscape: 2022-2024

Lee et al. found that in active SLE, nuclear cfDNA levels are elevated whereas mitochondrial DNA copy number is decreased. Interpreting these findings through the lens of NETosis, the authors proposed that nuclear cfDNA is released into the circulation during NET formation while mitochondrial DNA remains partially bound within NETs, enriched in immunostimulatory hypomethylated CpG islands. The study also examined the role of reactive oxygen species (ROS) using 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) and malondialdehyde (MDA) as oxidative stress markers, with active patients exhibiting higher 8-OHdG levels - indicating oxidative DNA damage as an additional contributor to cfDNA generation [35].

In 2023, Che et al. conducted a large-scale study using shallow whole-genome sequencing (sWGS) combined with machine learning to identify distinctive cfDNA patterns in autoimmune diseases within a precision medicine framework. More than 80,000 samples from pregnant and non-pregnant individuals with autoimmune diseases or inflammatory bowel disease were analyzed. This approach is potentially integrable into routine NIPT workflows, enabling early identification of pregnant women at risk for autoimmune complications. Characteristic cfDNA patterns in autoimmune diseases arise because, while apoptosis is the predominant source of cfDNA in healthy individuals, NETosis and other inflammatory processes increase both the concentration and structural heterogeneity of cfDNA in autoimmune patients. Inconclusive NIPT results were more common among pregnant patients with autoimmune diseases, particularly SLE, attributable to higher maternal cfDNA fractions and correspondingly reduced fetal fractions [36].

Ding et al. characterized a novel structural feature of cfDNA: irregularities at the fragment ends - termed “jagged patterns” - reflecting the activity of nuclease enzymes. Three DNases were investigated: DNase I, DNA fragmentation factor subunit beta (DFFB), and DNase-1-like 3 (DNase1L3). Since genetic deficiency of DNase1L3 induces an SLE-like syndrome in murine models, alterations in nuclease-mediated cfDNA processing may contribute to disease predisposition. The degree of jaggedness correlated with disease activity: it was significantly reduced in active SLE compared with healthy controls, with remission patients showing intermediate values, consistent with nuclease dysfunction. This parameter therefore represents a potential biomarker and may serve as a predictive indicator of disease flares [37].

The research group of Whittal-Garcia examined NET remnants containing nuclear and mitochondrial DNA as well as nucleosomes, in patients with active and inactive lupus nephritis and healthy controls. NET remnant levels were elevated in LN patients compared with healthy controls, and those with particularly high levels during flares were less likely to achieve renal remission. Predictive accuracy for renal outcomes exceeded that of proteinuria, anti-dsDNA titers, and complement levels. Furthermore, NET remnant levels predicted outcome even in patients with preserved renal function at the time of flare, unlike serum creatinine. NETosis was more pronounced in proliferative than non-proliferative renal histological subtypes, suggesting utility for disease classification [38].

Li et al. investigated extrachromosomal circular DNA (eccDNA) as a potentially superior biomarker compared with linear cfDNA, given its greater structural stability. Using genome-wide sequencing (GWS), they characterized eccDNA in plasma from SLE patients and healthy controls. Unexpectedly, eccDNA levels were lower in SLE patients than in controls, yet showed a positive correlation with C3 complement levels and a negative correlation with anti-dsDNA titers - a pattern reminiscent of the complement consumption and immune complex formation seen with conventional cfDNA. Functional analysis further supported an association between SLE and apoptotic signaling pathways as the source of eccDNA [39]. This finding is particularly interesting given earlier work demonstrating that UV radiation - a well-established SLE trigger - is a primary driver of eccDNA generation from apoptotic cells [40].

Halfon et al. again investigated mitochondrial cfDNA as a biomarker in SLE and lupus nephritis, additionally addressing mitochondrial dysfunction as a pathogenetic factor and potential therapeutic target. Among mitochondria-targeting drugs, agents such as metformin and the mTOR inhibitor sirolimus are already in clinical use for other indications, and additional therapies are currently under development [41].

Regarding the importance of mitochondrial dysfunction in the pathogenesis of SLE and its potential as a therapeutic target, the work of Nagy et al. deserves particular attention. Their findings provide further evidence that mitochondrial dysfunction is a key driver of immune dysregulation in SLE. T cells from patients with SLE exhibit mitochondrial hyperpolarization, increased mitochondrial mass, and excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), resulting in oxidative stress and impaired cellular energy metabolism. These mitochondrial abnormalities promote mTOR activation, aberrant T-cell activation, and defective cell death pathways [42]. Furthermore, mitochondrial dysfunction facilitates the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA).

Tong et al. investigated 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) modification of cfDNA as a novel epigenetic biomarker. 5hmC is an oxidation product of 5-methylcytosine generated through the activity of TET enzymes and represents an intermediary step in active DNA demethylation. Its tissue-specific distribution has been shown to carry diagnostic information in oncological, metabolic, and cardiovascular disease contexts. In SLE, patients - particularly those with active disease - exhibited enriched 5hmC signatures. A panel incorporating 5hmC patterns together with genes involved in epigenetic regulation, such as TET2 and MYH9, was proposed as a candidate biomarker panel [43].

Redefining the role of cfDNA and therapeutic implications: 2025-2026

Research published in 2025 has further complicated - and enriched - the conceptual framework surrounding cfDNA in SLE. Ferrera et al. demonstrated that cfDNA may exert immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory effects under specific conditions, challenging the prevailing assumption that elevated cfDNA levels necessarily indicate a worse prognosis. In this study, exogenous cfDNA administered to lupus-model mice protected against disease progression. This effect was attributed to cfDNA binding to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecules, sterically preventing antigen presentation and thereby modulating T-cell responses. Additionally, macrophages interacting with cfDNA altered their gene expression toward an anti-inflammatory profile. The authors proposed a dual role for cfDNA: pro-inflammatory activity may require specific danger signals - such as a high density of CpG repeats, mitochondrial origin, or insufficient clearance - whereas in the absence of these factors, cfDNA may instead attenuate immune activation [44]. In the context of SLE, where these pro-inflammatory cofactors are typically present, cfDNA retains its status as both a pathogenic driver and a biomarker; however, this finding has potential therapeutic relevance in other disease settings, such as oncology.

Complementarily, Suárez et al. investigated cfDNA as a marker associated with NETosis and low-density granulocytes, demonstrating that cfDNA levels are elevated not only in proportion to disease activity and renal involvement severity, but also in the context of SLE-associated comorbidities - including subclinical atherosclerosis, classical cardiovascular risk factors, and reduced bone density. The association with subclinical atheroma formation is particularly relevant, as it suggests that cfDNA may help identify patients at elevated risk for major cardiovascular events before clinically manifest disease develops [45].

Expanding the biomarker landscape is important not only for diagnostic and prognostic purposes but also because it may reveal novel therapeutic targets. Zhu et al. developed a nanogel-based drug delivery system designed to accumulate preferentially in the kidney through a homing mechanism following intravenous administration. This system enables targeted renal delivery of dexamethasone, reducing systemic glucocorticoid toxicity. In addition to its drug delivery function, the cationic nanogel acts as a competitive binder of cfDNA, thereby modulating TLR9 activation, attenuating pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and suppressing pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization. In murine SLE models, this approach significantly reduced disease manifestations and improved serological markers [46].

In a parallel approach, Zhou et al. developed a helical molecular clip designed to trap cfDNA through a combination of electrostatic interaction and structural enclosure between the helical arms of the molecule. Intravenous administration in SLE model mice reduced acute inflammatory flares and produced significant improvements in lupus nephritis and splenomegaly, with a favorable systemic safety profile [47]. These proof-of-concept studies establish cfDNA sequestration as a mechanistically rational therapeutic strategy in SLE, though translation to human studies remains a future goal.

In 2026, Casares-Marfil et al. investigated the understudied population of patients with childhood-onset SLE and identified key epigenetic alterations in DNA derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells. DNA methylation analysis revealed significant hypomethylation of interferon-regulated genes, as well as genes involved in B-cell activation and cellular senescence, which was associated with higher SLEDAI scores. Furthermore, methylation patterns identified three distinct molecular subgroups of childhood-onset SLE, characterized by enrichment of pathways related to cell adhesion and growth factor response, cell differentiation and fate, and oxidative stress and Ras-associated protein-1 signaling. Sex-based analysis further revealed greater hypomethylation in male patients, suggesting potential sex-specific differences in the epigenetic regulation of childhood-onset SLE [48].

Several studies have suggested that circulating mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) concentrations may correlate more closely with disease activity than nuclear cfDNA. A recent review further integrates the available evidence and emphasizes that, beyond absolute concentration, the qualitative characterization of mtDNA may provide additional clinically relevant information. A multidimensional biomarker profile incorporating parameters such as fragment size distribution and mitochondrial DNA copy number may therefore offer further insight into renal damage and potentially aid in the assessment of lupus nephritis prognosis. The review also highlights the potential applicability of mtDNA-based analyses to other, relatively understudied manifestations of SLE, including neuropsychiatric and cutaneous involvement, as well as cardiovascular and pulmonary complications [49]. These findings further support the concept that the future clinical utility of cfDNA-based biomarkers may lie not in the measurement of DNA concentration alone, but in the integrated analysis of multiple quantitative and qualitative molecular characteristics.

Discussion

Systemic lupus erythematosus is a highly complex autoimmune connective tissue disease with diverse organ system involvement, a relapsing-remitting course, and significant inter-individual heterogeneity in both manifestations and prognosis. This complexity makes the identification of reliable biomarkers particularly challenging and particularly important. The biomarkers currently available in clinical practice - inflammatory parameters, complement levels, and anti-dsDNA autoantibodies - are useful but insufficient for accurate flare prediction and individualized disease management.

Across the studies reviewed, a consistent picture emerges: cfDNA is not merely a passive byproduct of cell death, but an active participant in SLE immunopathology. Released principally through apoptosis, necrosis, and NETosis, cfDNA - particularly when hypomethylated or of mitochondrial origin - activates innate immune receptors including TLR9 and cGAS/STING, driving type I interferon production and amplifying the autoimmune cascade. Anti-dsDNA and anti-nucleosome antibodies form immune complexes with cfDNA, and the resulting deposits in renal glomeruli underlie the pathogenesis of lupus nephritis.

Although cfDNA is not lupus specific, it could serve a more versatile biomarker than the ones in current clinical practice, because it reflects the current state of cellular injury and cell death processes. Other biomarkers, such as inflammatory parameters and antibodies may rise in latency of the actual flare, making prognostication difficult. Furthermore, there are cases when the autoantibody titers are high despite the scarcity of clinical symptoms or active disease occurs in the absence of significant changes in conventional serological markers.

A critical distinction that has emerged from recent literature is that nuclear and mitochondrial cfDNA do not behave interchangeably as biomarkers. Lee et al. demonstrated that in active SLE, nuclear cfDNA levels rise while mtDNA copy number decreases, and Giaglis et al. showed that mtDNA - but not nuclear cfDNA - correlated independently with SLEDAI score [33,35]. These divergent patterns likely reflect different mechanisms of generation: nuclear cfDNA is released during apoptotic nucleosomal laddering and NETosis, whereas mtDNA may be preferentially retained within NET scaffolds or released in response to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. The stronger and more specific association of mtDNA with interferon pathway activation further supports treating these two analytes as complementary rather than interchangeable. Future biomarker panels should measure and report them separately.

Unmet needs in cfDNA research regarding SLE

Despite growing interest in circulating cfDNA as a biomarker in SLE, its clinical utility remains limited by several unresolved challenges. Most studies focus on total cfDNA levels in small, cross-sectional cohorts, with insufficient attention to disease heterogeneity, organ-specific involvement, and treatment response. Conclusion

The investigation of circulating cfDNA and related nucleic-acid–based biomarkers represents one of the most rapidly evolving and scientifically productive frontiers in SLE research. Over more than five decades, the field has progressed from basic colorimetric detection to genome-wide epigenetic profiling and machine learning–assisted pattern recognition. Non-invasive biomarkers are intrinsically valuable in a disease requiring repeated monitoring over decades. If validated and standardized, cfDNA-based liquid biopsy could reduce the need for repeated renal biopsies and provide real-time pharmacodynamic information during treatment. The dual role of cfDNA - as a pro-inflammatory driver under most SLE conditions and as a potential immunomodulator under specific circumstances - also opens novel therapeutic avenues, as illustrated by the cfDNA-targeting nanogel and molecular clip strategies currently in preclinical development.

For clinical translation to be realized, the field requires: (1) prospective, multi-center studies with standardized pre-analytical and analytical protocols; (2) investigation across a broader range of SLE manifestations beyond lupus nephritis; (3) studies powered to evaluate cfDNA across diverse ethnic and demographic groups; (4) systematic evaluation of cfDNA as a pharmacodynamic biomarker under different therapeutic regimens; and (5) the development of reference ranges and decision thresholds applicable in routine clinical laboratory settings. Based on current evidence, a combination of qPCR (for quantification of nuclear and mitochondrial cfDNA separately) and gel electrophoresis (for fragment size assessment) represents the most pragmatic near-term platform for clinical standardization, with NGS reserved for research applications and selected complex cases.
