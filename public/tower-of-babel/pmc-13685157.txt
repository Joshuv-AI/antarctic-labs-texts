TyG index and TyG-WHtR as predictors of all-cause mortality in patients with prior hepatitis A or B virus infections
Authors: Fan Bai, Wen Li, Ni Luo, Yange Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the association between the triglyceride glucose (TyG) index and the TyG-to-waist-height ratio (TyG-WHtR) with the risk of all-cause mortality in patients with prior hepatitis A virus (HAV) or hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection.MethodThis study was a retrospective cohort study that included patients from 1999 to 2018 with available data on HAV/HBV, TyG-related indices, and survival and mortality information. Multiple imputation methods were utilized to address missing variables. A weighted multivariable Cox regression model was employed to analyze the relationship between TyG and TyG-WHtR with all-cause mortality in the study patients. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, gender, smoking, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and hypertension. All analysis results were presented as hazards with a 95% confidence interval (CI).ResultsThe study included a total of 6,413 patients, of which 1,692 were dead. The follow-up period concluded in December 2019, with a total duration of 119.31 ± 1.53 months. Analysis revealed that in patients with prior HAV or HBV infection, the TyG (HR: 1.22, 95%CI: 1.10–1.36) and TyG-WHtR (HR: 1.53, 95%CI: 1.34–1.75) were associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality. Separate studies conducted in patients with prior HAV or HBV infection also demonstrated an association between the TyG and TyG-WHtR indices with all-cause mortality (former: HR: 1.20, 95%CI: 1.07–1.34; latter: HR: 2.02, 95%CI: 1.49–2.74). Subgroup analysis results indicated that the association between TyG-WHtR and all-cause mortality was similar across age, gender, smoking, and hypertension groups (all P < 0.05).ConclusionIn patients with prior HAV or HBV infection, the TyG and TyG-WHtR indices were associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality. This study underscores the importance of the TyG and TyG-WHtR for patients with prior viral hepatitis, potentially aiding in the improvement of health management for this group.Clinical TrialOur research is not a clinical trial. Introduction

Viral hepatitis is an inflammation of the liver associated with viral infections [1, 2]. Over the past few decades, with advancements in medicine, numerous preventive measures, vaccines, and passive strategies have been developed and implemented, particularly with the World Health Organization (WHO) release of the 2030 plan to eliminate viral hepatitis as a public health threat [3, 4]. However, viral hepatitis should not be underestimated as it remains a leading cause of global mortality, impacting public health worldwide [5, 6]. It is reported that viral hepatitis affects 500 million people globally [7], causing up to 1.34 million deaths annually [8]. Moreover, studies have shown that individuals with a history of hepatitis A virus (HAV) or hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection face a 50–60% increased risk of significant liver fibrosis [9], with a higher risk of liver cancer and all-cause mortality [10, 11]. Even after viral clearance, these patients remain a high-risk group requiring long-term health monitoring and targeted management, yet effective and convenient prognostic indicators for risk stratification are still lacking in clinical practice.

The metabolic disorders associated with chronic HBV infection have been widely discussed [12, 13]. Emerging evidence shows that prior HBV infection is closely associated with insulin resistance (IR) and metabolic disorders, which further drive adverse clinical outcomes [14]. Research on the association between HAV and blood glucose metabolism was limited [15]. HAV infection was previously considered a self-limiting disease, manifesting only as acute clinical symptoms [16, 17]. However, recent studies have shown that previous HAV infection is significantly associated with an increased risk of diabetes, and individuals with HAV infection have notably higher levels of fasting blood glucose and glycated hemoglobin [18]. Additionally, the liver’s crucial role in blood glucose homeostasis has been established [19]. Liver damage resulting from previous viral hepatitis infections may lead to metabolic disorders [20, 21]. In light of this, individuals with a history of viral hepatitis infection may face a more severe risk of blood glucose metabolism abnormalities, and blood glucose metabolism abnormalities, particularly IR, were considered significantly associated with a range of multi-organ comorbidities and increased mortality risk [22].

The triglyceride glucose (TyG) index, recently proposed, serves as a novel indicator that can replace the Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) to some extent and functions as an effective tool for assessing IR levels [23, 24]. Studies have demonstrated that TyG exhibits good sensitivity and specificity in identifying IR [7, 25]. TyG index has been utilized in multiple studies to assess the risk of all-cause mortality in populations such as patients with diabetes [26], heart failure [27], and critically ill individuals following a stroke [28], demonstrating good predictive efficacy. Building on this foundation, some studies further integrated the TyG index with obesity indicators to form the TyG-waist height ratio (TyG-WHtR) and found that in the general population, both TyG and TyG-WHtR were superior to other TyG-related parameters in predicting the risk of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [29, 30]. Additionally, research has shown that compared to the TyG index alone, TyG-WHtR demonstrates superior performance in identifying early diabetes risk and predicting the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD)-related mortality [31, 32]. The TyG and TyG-WHtR indices, as easily obtainable metabolic markers, may fill this gap by reflecting the combined effects of IR and central obesity, which are closely linked to liver damage and systemic complications in viral hepatitis survivors.However, whether these two simple metabolic indicators can predict the prognosis of patients with prior HAV or HBV infection remains unclear, which limits the optimization of health management strategies for this vulnerable population.

This study used the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database, a large-scale, population-based, multistage stratified sampling database with long-term follow-up mortality data. The NHANES database provides high-quality, nationally representative data on demographic characteristics, physical examinations, laboratory tests, and survival status, which greatly reduces selection bias, ensures adequate sample size and follow-up duration, and significantly improves the validity, reliability, and generalizability of the study results. Based on this database, we aimed to investigate the association of the TyG index and TyG-WHtR with all-cause mortality in patients with prior HAV or HBV infection, so as to provide evidence-based indicators for prognostic risk stratification and precise health management of this population.

Method

Research data source

The data for this study were derived from the NHANES program in the United States. NHANES is a nationwide study that combines interviews and physical examinations to collect demographic, socioeconomic, dietary, and health-related data. The NHANES database employs a complex, multistage sampling method to draw a representative sample of approximately 5,000 individuals from about 15 counties across the country each year [33]. The specific research methods and data collection protocols used in the NHANES database are detailed by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). The NHANES database is released biennially, and all data can be freely accessed through the following link: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/tutorials/SampleDesign.aspx.

Yet it has several potential limitations: Some variables, including lifestyle factors and disease history, were collected by self-report, which may lead to recall bias. NHANES uses a complex multistage sampling design, which requires weighted analysis to ensure population representativeness. Detailed information on hepatitis treatment, viral load, exact duration of infection, and liver histology was not available. Loss to follow-up or missing data may exist despite multiple imputation.

Study design and population selection

This study was a retrospective cohort study, extracting all sample data from the NHANES database between 1999 and 2018. The specific inclusion criteria for the study patients were as follows: (1) Age ≥ 40 years; (2) Patients diagnosed with prior HAV or HBV infection; (3) Patients with complete information available for TyG and TyG-WHtR assessment; (4) Patients with complete survival information in the NHANES from 2001 to 2018. Exclusion criteria: (1) Missing information required for defining prior HAV or HBV infection; (2) Missing key covariate information. All Patients were granted their informed consent in writing, and the study protocol was endorsed by the Ethics Review Board of the NCHS.

The rationale for setting age ≥ 40 years is that individuals aged 40 years and older have a significantly higher risk of metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and all-cause mortality, and are more likely to develop long-term adverse outcomes related to prior viral hepatitis infection. This age threshold also ensures sufficient follow-up events and improves the reliability of survival analysis.

Definition of prior HAV and prior HBV infection

Serological criteria for prior hepatitis A and B virus infection were defined strictly according to standardized laboratory testing protocols:

Prior HAV infection: Serum total anti-HAV antibody positive, with negative anti-HAV IgM. Total anti-HAV IgG positivity indicates resolved past HAV infection, while IgM anti-HAV excludes acute active infection at enrollment.

Prior HBV infection: Two mutually exclusive serological profiles indicating a history of HBV exposure:

Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) negative + Hepatitis B core antibody (Anti-HBc) positive, regardless of Hepatitis B surface antibody (Anti-HBs) status;

HBsAg negative, Anti-HBc positive, Anti-HBs positive, consistent with remote cleared HBV infection.

Individuals with persistent HBsAg positivity were retained within the HBV exposure group. In this cohort focused on all-cause mortality rather than liver-specific endpoints, all participants with lifetime HBV exposure (either resolved remote infection or chronic HBsAg-positive infection) were collectively classified as having prior HBV infection. Participants missing any hepatitis serology markers were excluded per study exclusion criteria.

Definition of outcome

The primary outcome of this study was all-cause mortality. The mortality data for the study population was obtained through the following link: NCHS Data Linkage - Mortality Data - Public-Use Files. Follow-up was conducted through a combination of interviews and physical examinations, with follow-up ending in December 2019. The total duration of follow-up was 119.31 ± 1.53 months. The analysis of this study divided the study population into a study group and a reference group according to information on survival and death.

Statistical analysis

Given the complex, multistage, stratified sampling design characteristic of the NHANES database, the analysis was weighted by NHANES recommendations. The weighting factors included primary sampling units (SDMVPSU) and strata (SDMVSTRA). Additionally, the variables were weighted based on the year of the data, with the following rules: For data years 1999–2002, the weight was 2/10 * WTSAF4YR, and for 2009–2018, the weight was 1/10 * WTSAF2YR. Furthermore, the original database had instances of missing data. We addressed the missing variables using multiple imputation methods based on chained equations from random forests. Subsequent sensitivity analyses conducted on the data before and after imputation revealed no statistically significant differences (Table S1).

In this study, continuous data were described as the mean (standard error) [Mean(SE)], and comparisons between two groups were made using the independent samples t-test, while multiple groups were compared using analysis of variance. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and proportions N (%), with comparisons between groups conducted using the chi-square test, and ordinal data were compared using rank sum tests. A weighted univariate Cox regression model was employed to identify covariates for subsequent analysis, with variables that yielded positive results (shown below in the variables included in model II) in the analysis being included as final covariates. Separate weighted Cox regression models were constructed to assess the association between TyG/TyG-WHtR and all-cause mortality in patients with a history of HAV or HBV infection. Model I was unadjusted for any covariates. Model II was adjusted for age, gender, race, education, marital status, poverty-income ratio (PIR), smoking status, physical activity level, body mass index (BMI), diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, CVD, cancer, anemia, chronic kidney disease (CKD), unhealthy sleep duration (USFLO), fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) score, statin use, and total energy intake. Subgroup analysis of the total population was performed by age (≥ 60 years), sex, smoking, diabetes, CVD, and hypertension.

We formally tested the proportional hazards (PH) assumption for all weighted Cox regression models using two complementary approaches: (1) visual inspection of log(-log(survival)) curves stratified by TyG and TyG-WHtR quartiles; (2) time-dependent covariate interaction test, where each exposure variable was multiplied by follow-up time and incorporated into the Cox model. For all primary and subgroup Cox models, all time-interaction terms yielded P > 0.05, and parallel log(-log) survival curves were observed across quartile strata, confirming no violation of the proportional hazards assumption. All Cox models therefore satisfied the core statistical assumption for valid hazard ratio estimation.

All statistical tests were conducted using two-tailed tests with a significance level of α = 0.05, and effect sizes and assessment indicators were presented as a hazard (HR) [95% confidence interval (CI)]. Data cleaning and handling of missing values were performed using Python 3.9, while model statistical analysis was carried out using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Interaction and effect modification analyses

To evaluate potential effect modification by clinically relevant factors, we tested interaction terms between TyG-WHtR and each subgroup variable (age ≥ 60 vs. <60 years; sex; smoking; diabetes; CVD; hypertension) in weighted multivariable Cox regression models. A significant P for interaction indicated that the association between TyG-WHtR and all-cause mortality differed across subgroups. Subgroup analyses were performed to illustrate stratified associations only when the interaction was statistically significant or biologically meaningful. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study patients

Figure 1 illustrates the specific screening process for the study patients. Initially, 15,198 individuals aged 40 and above with a history of HAV or HBV were retrieved from the NHANES database. After excluding 8,785 patients who were missing key information based on the exclusion criteria, a final total of 6,413 patients were included for analysis. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the included patients. The average age of the total patients was 60.52 (0.21) years. Among them, there were 4,721 individuals (73.62%) with a survival status of alive and 1,692 individuals (26.38%) with a status of dead. We observed that the average age of the dead group was significantly higher than that of the alive group [70.39 (0.31) vs. 57.34 (0.25), P < 0.05]. The average values of the TyG [8.93 (0.02) vs. 8.75 (0.02)] and TyG-WHtR [5.43 (0.03) vs. 5.28 (0.02)] in the dead group were also higher than those in the alive group, with the former being more likely to have severe liver fibrosis (9.88% vs. 2.94%) (all P < 0.05). Additionally, we noted significant differences in clinical characteristics between the two groups, such as diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, CVD, cancer, anemia, chronic kidney disease, and FIB-4 score (all P < 0.05).

Baseline comparison between included and excluded eligible participants

Of the 15,198 initially eligible participants aged ≥ 40 years with prior HAV/HBV seropositivity, 8,785 (57.8%) were excluded due to missing TyG/WHtR laboratory data or incomplete mortality linkage records. Table S2 (Supplementary Material) presents weighted baseline comparisons between included and excluded individuals. Excluded participants were older (mean age 63.1 vs. 60.5 years), had lower socioeconomic status (higher proportion of poverty-income ratio ≤ 1.3), lower physical activity levels, higher prevalence of self-reported chronic kidney disease and anemia, and incomplete metabolic laboratory measurements (all P<0.05). No significant differences were observed in sex distribution, race composition, or rates of prior HAV versus HBV serostatus between the two groups (all P>0.05).

Association of TyG and TyG-WHtR with the risk of death in patients with prior HAV or HBV infection

As shown in Table 2, when TyG and TyG-WHtR were analyzed as continuous variables, each unit increase in their levels was significantly associated with an elevated risk of mortality in the total population (TyG: HR: 1.22, 95%CI: 1.10–1.36; TyG-WHtR: HR:1.53, 95%CI: 1.34–1.75; all P < 0.05). When TyG was analyzed as a categorical variable, with the lowest quartile group (TyG ≤ 8.65) as the reference, the highest quartile group (TyG > 9.16) was associated with a higher risk of mortality (HR: 1.28, 95%CI: 1.08–1.53, P < 0.05). Similarly, when TyG-WHtR was analyzed as a categorical variable, using the first quartile group (TyG-WHtR ≤ 4.64) as the reference, the third and fourth quartile groups (TyG-WHtR: 5.25–5.91 or > 5.91) were associated with an increased risk of mortality (former: HR: 1.37, 95%CI: 1.07–1.76; latter: HR: 2.02, 95%CI: 1.46–2.80; all P < 0.05).

Association of TyG with the risk of death in patients with prior HAV or HBV

Figure 2 demonstrates the association between TyG and the risk of mortality in individuals with a history of HAV or HBV infection. The study found that with each increment in the levels of TyG, there was a significant increase in the risk of death (Group HAV: HR: 1.20, 95%CI: 1.07–1.34; Group HBV: HR: 1.51, 95%CI: 1.18–1.95; all P < 0.05). When TyG was analyzed as a quartile variable, using the lowest quartile as the reference, the highest quartile of TyG showed a statistically positive correlation with mortality (Group HAV: HR: 1.21, 95%CI: 1.01–1.46; Group HBV: HR: 1.88, 95%CI: 1.22–2.90; all P < 0.05).

Association of TyG-WHtR with the risk of death in patients with prior HAV or HBV

As depicted in Fig. 3, TyG-WHtR exhibit positive and statistically significant associations with the risk of mortality, whether analyzed as continuous or categorical variables. The HR and 95%CI for Group HAV and Group HBV were 1.50 (1.30–1.73) and 2.02 (1.49–2.74), respectively (all P < 0.05). Subgroup analysis of the relationship between TyG-WHtR and the risk of death in patients with prior HAV or HBV infection

Table 3 presents the subgroup analysis of the association between TyG-WHtR and the risk of mortality in the overall population. The results indicated that significant positive statistical associations were observed between the two groups when stratified by age, gender, smoking, and hypertension (all P < 0.05). In the subgroup without diabetes, a significant positive correlation with mortality risk was observed when TyG-WHtR was analyzed as a continuous variable (P < 0.001). When analyzed as a categorical variable, a significant association was only found in the fourth quartile (P < 0.001), while the second and third quartiles did not reach statistical significance. Among those with diabetes, the results as a continuous variable were consistent with the aforementioned associations (HR: 1.30, 95%CI: 1.08–1.57), but as a categorical variable, the second quartile group showed a negative association when using the first quartile as the reference (HR: 0.46, 95%CI: 0.24–0.89). A statistical association was observed in the subgroup without CVD (all P < 0.05), while no significant association was found in those with CVD (all P > 0.05). Formal interaction tests were conducted to examine whether the association between TyG-WHtR and all-cause mortality was modified by age, sex, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, or CVD. Significant interactions were observed for diabetes (P for interaction = 0.028) and CVD (P for interaction = 0.011), indicating that the strength of association differed between subgroups. No significant interactions were found for age (P = 0.126), sex (P = 0.415), smoking (P = 0.782), or hypertension (P = 0.084). Consistent with interaction results, TyG-WHtR was significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality in participants without diabetes (HR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.39–2.01, P < 0.001) but not in those with diabetes (HR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.08–1.57, P = 0.007). Similarly, the association was robust in participants without CVD (HR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.42–1.95, P < 0.001) but not in those with CVD (HR = 1.16, 95% CI: 0.87–1.53, P = 0.308). In age, sex, smoking, and hypertension subgroups, the associations were directionally consistent without significant interaction.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this population-based retrospective cohort study is the first to systematically evaluate the prognostic performance of the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and TyG-waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHtR) for long-term all-cause mortality among adults with serologically resolved hepatitis A virus (HAV) or hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. After rigorous multivariable adjustment, elevated TyG index and TyG-WHtR were independently associated with increased long-term all-cause mortality. Stratified analyses revealed consistent adverse associations across subgroups defined by age, sex, smoking status, and hypertension. Notably, significant effect modification was observed for diabetes and cardiovascular disease (CVD), whereby the predictive values of TyG and TyG-WHtR were substantially stronger in individuals without these two comorbidities. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that concurrent glucolipid dysfunction and central obesity are robustly linked to poor long-term survival among patients with previously resolved viral hepatitis.

The following sections first elaborate on the underlying biological mechanisms supporting these associations, then contextualize our results against existing literature, interpret subgroup and interaction analyses, discuss translational clinical implications, acknowledge study limitations, summarize methodological strengths, and close with recommendations for future research.

Viral hepatitis represents a major global public health burden, triggering acute and chronic hepatic inflammation that may further cause multisystem organ damage [35, 36]. The liver acts as a central regulator of glucose and lipid homeostasis, with bidirectional crosstalk between viral hepatitis and metabolic dysregulation well documented [14, 37, 38]. Two interconnected pathways explain why patients with serologically cleared HAV/HBV develop persistent glucolipid disorders and elevated mortality risk, which collectively provide a biological rationale for the prognostic utility of TyG and TyG-WHtR. First, residual hepatic injury drives hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation and insulin resistance (IR). Viral clearance does not fully reverse prior liver damage; sustained hepatic inflammation disrupts extracellular matrix turnover and activates HSCs to promote progressive liver fibrosis [39, 40]. Post-hepatitic metabolic disturbance establishes a hyperglycemic, hyperinsulinemic microenvironment that further amplifies HSC activation via upregulated connective tissue growth factors and accumulated advanced glycation end products [39]. IR reciprocally exacerbates hepatic inflammatory cascades, sustains HSC-mediated parenchymal damage, and accelerates hepatocyte apoptosis through aberrant metabolic signaling pathways [41–43]. This self-perpetuating cycle of liver dysfunction creates chronic glucolipid imbalance long after viral resolution. Second, post-hepatitic IR disrupts vascular endothelial homeostasis to amplify systemic cardiometabolic risk. Under physiological conditions, insulin maintains vascular balance via two counter-regulatory pathways: the vasoprotective phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/nitric oxide (NO) cascade and the vasoconstrictive mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/endothelin-1 (ET-1) axis [44, 45]. IR impairs PI3K-NO-mediated vasodilation while overstimulating the detrimental MAPK-ET-1 pathway, triggering endothelial dysfunction, systemic glucolipotoxicity, and heightened susceptibility to fatal long-term adverse events [34, 46]. Central adiposity captured by WHtR potentiates this endothelial injury, which explains why TyG-WHtR—an integrated marker combining glucolipid status and central obesity—exhibits robust prognostic capacity alongside the standalone TyG index. Taken together, unresolved hepatic and vascular metabolic damage after HAV/HBV recovery creates a persistent high-risk phenotype comprehensively captured by TyG-related indices.

TyG-derived markers have been validated as reliable prognostic surrogates for disease progression and adverse endpoints across multiple chronic liver diseases, with abundant evidence established in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Zhao et al. demonstrated a positive correlation between the TyG index and coronary heart disease risk in NAFLD patients [46], while Chen et al. confirmed that TyG metrics independently predict all-cause, cardiovascular, and diabetes-associated mortality in NAFLD populations [47]. Extending this work, Yu et al. reported superior mortality predictive performance for TyG-WHtR in MASLD cohorts, supporting its application as a non-invasive metabolic biomarker for routine liver disease surveillance [48]. Our findings expand the clinical scope of TyG and TyG-WHtR beyond primary fatty liver disorders to survivors of resolved viral hepatitis, reinforcing their broad utility in chronic liver populations.

A substantial body of work has specifically linked chronic HBV infection to IR, a phenotype tightly correlated with liver fibrosis severity and hepatic disease progression [49]. Hepatotropic viruses disrupt hepatic glucose regulatory machinery, frequently inducing IR or incident type 2 diabetes [37, 50–52]. One prospective cohort further identified the TyG index as an independent risk factor for hepatocellular carcinoma among patients with HBV-related cirrhosis, highlighting the central role of TyG-reflected metabolic dysregulation in HBV-driven fatal outcomes [53]. Consistent with these data, we observed stable, independent associations between elevated TyG/TyG-WHtR and all-cause mortality in both resolved HBV and resolved HAV subgroups. This indicates that HBV-mediated hepatic and metabolic deterioration persists even after serological viral clearance to worsen long-term survival. By contrast, clinical data connecting prior HAV exposure to metabolic impairment remain scarce, though serological analyses have confirmed a meaningful association between HAV seropositivity and incident diabetes [18]. Our novel results fill this critical evidence gap, providing epidemiological support for the hypothesis that resolved HAV infection also triggers sustained IR and glucolipid imbalance that compromise long-term survival. Multiple multinational cohorts have further validated the consistent prognostic value of TyG and TyG-WHtR across diverse ethnicities and healthcare systems, including studies conducted in China, South Korea, Europe, and the United States. Despite cross-regional differences in race, lifestyle patterns, and medical infrastructure, the mortality-predictive capacity of TyG-based markers remains stable, supporting the generalizability of our findings to global clinical practice.

Stratified analyses confirmed consistent positive associations between elevated TyG-WHtR and all-cause mortality across age, sex, smoking status, and hypertension strata, demonstrating that the prognostic role of this composite marker applies broadly to most patients with resolved viral hepatitis. However, effect sizes were markedly attenuated among participants with pre-existing diabetes or CVD, with formal interaction analyses verifying significant effect modification by both comorbidities (all P < 0.05). No statistically meaningful interactions were detected for age, sex, smoking, or hypertension, consolidating the robustness of TyG-WHtR as a generalizable risk marker across these subgroups. Three complementary explanations account for the weakened predictive performance of TyG and TyG-WHtR in individuals with diabetes or CVD. Statistically, smaller sample sizes within these two comorbidity subgroups increase sampling variability and limit power to detect incremental risk associations. Second, long-term use of hypoglycemic, lipid-lowering, and antihypertensive medications introduces unmeasured confounding that may mask the independent prognostic signal of metabolic indices. Biologically, patients with established diabetes or CVD already exhibit severe baseline IR and widespread endothelial dysfunction; this metabolic “ceiling effect” diminishes the incremental risk information provided by TyG/TyG-WHtR, whereas the markers retain strong discriminatory power in metabolically uncomplicated viral hepatitis survivors free of these advanced cardiometabolic diseases. Collectively, these subgroup and interaction results highlight that TyG-WHtR-based metabolic risk stratification delivers the greatest clinical value for low-comorbidity patients with resolved HAV/HBV. Large-scale prospective studies are needed to externally validate this heterogeneous prognostic effect and develop tailored intervention strategies stratified by baseline cardiometabolic comorbidity burden.

TyG and TyG-WHtR possess distinct advantages for population-level cardiometabolic risk screening: measurement relies on routine laboratory and anthropometric parameters, with low cost, rapid turnaround, and excellent intraindividual repeatability. These properties render them ideal tools for long-term risk stratification in clinical follow-up of patients with remote HAV or HBV infection. Integrating TyG-related indices into standard outpatient liver panels enables early identification of metabolically high-risk individuals, facilitating timely lifestyle modification, targeted glycemic and lipid control, and intensified cardiovascular surveillance to reduce long-term population mortality burden.

Nevertheless, widespread routine clinical deployment faces notable practical constraints. Uniform standardized protocols for laboratory testing, calculation, and reporting of TyG and TyG-WHtR have not been universally adopted across clinical laboratories. Ethnicity-, age-, and comorbidity-specific optimal cutoff thresholds for risk stratification in viral hepatitis survivors are yet to be established. Additionally, standardized clinician education and structured patient health literacy programs are required to improve awareness and consistent uptake of these metabolic biomarkers in hepatology clinics.

While analyses leveraged the nationally representative multi-ethnic NHANES dataset, with core associations preserved after complex survey weighting and multiple imputation for missing covariates, several key limitations constrain interpretation and external validity. First, substantial selection bias arose from the high exclusion rate of eligible participants. Among 15,198 adults aged ≥ 40 years with complete HAV/HBV serology, 8,785 (57.8%) were excluded due to missing triglyceride, fasting glucose, waist circumference, height, or mortality follow-up data. Weighted baseline comparisons (Supplementary Table S2) indicated excluded participants were older, had lower socioeconomic status and reduced physical activity, and carried higher burdens of chronic kidney disease, anemia, CVD, and diabetes alongside marginally elevated FIB-4 scores suggestive of advanced liver fibrosis. The final analytical sample overrepresented metabolically healthier individuals with complete laboratory records. Although weighted statistical approaches and multiple imputation were applied to mitigate missing data bias, residual selection bias could not be fully eliminated. Systematic exclusion of frailer participants likely underestimates the true magnitude of the association between elevated TyG/TyG-WHtR and all-cause mortality, limiting extrapolation to patients with incomplete clinical workups. Second, unmeasured granular hepatitis-specific clinical variables introduce residual confounding. The NHANES database lacks critical mortality-modifying covariates, including longitudinal HBV viral load trajectories, detailed antiviral treatment histories (duration, adherence, virological suppression status), precise time intervals since initial HAV/HBV infection, formal clinical diagnoses of cirrhosis and decompensated liver disease, imaging-confirmed hepatocellular carcinoma, and serial longitudinal liver function panels. While FIB-4 was used as a non-invasive surrogate for liver fibrosis, it cannot replace histological staging or formal cirrhosis adjudication. Undocumented advanced hepatic disease acts as a powerful confounder: severe liver injury simultaneously exacerbates IR and raises mortality risk, potentially inflating the independent prognostic effect sizes of TyG and TyG-WHtR. Furthermore, self-reported covariates including smoking, alcohol intake, and chronic disease history are vulnerable to recall bias, and unrecorded lifestyle exposures such as long-term dietary patterns and routine liver surveillance add further residual confounding. Third, the clinical utility of TyG and TyG-WHtR as standalone standardized decision-making tools remains unvalidated. The present study only establishes statistical associations between elevated TyG/TyG-WHtR and all-cause mortality; we did not formally assess predictive discrimination, calibration, or net clinical benefit of these indices in independent external cohorts. Consensus risk-stratification cutoffs specific to resolved viral hepatitis populations are absent, and uniform measurement and calculation protocols differ across clinical centers. All analyses were restricted to a single U.S. population-based dataset, meaning multi-ethnic hospital-based prospective cohorts with detailed liver staging, serial viral load monitoring, and complete antiviral treatment records are mandatory to replicate and validate our findings. At this stage, TyG and TyG-WHtR should only be deployed as exploratory metabolic screening markers to identify candidates for intensified cardiometabolic monitoring and lifestyle intervention, rather than standalone tools guiding definitive risk stratification or therapeutic decisions for viral hepatitis survivors.

Despite the above limitations, this study possesses notable methodological strengths that strengthen internal validity and population representativeness. We utilized a large, nationally representative multi-ethnic U.S. population sample from NHANES, minimizing single-center selection bias common to hospital-based cohorts. Complex survey weighting was applied to account for stratified sampling design, and multiple imputation was implemented to handle missing covariate data, reducing statistical bias from incomplete laboratory and anthropometric records. All primary associations remained robust following sequential multivariable adjustment for a comprehensive panel of demographic, lifestyle, and clinical confounders, supporting the stability of our core study conclusions.

In conclusion, this population-based retrospective cohort study delivers novel epidemiological evidence that combined glucolipid dysfunction and central adiposity correlate with impaired long-term survival among adults with serologically resolved HAV or HBV infection. The low-cost, readily measurable TyG and TyG-WHtR indices can be integrated into routine outpatient liver laboratory panels to screen metabolically high-risk subpopulations and guide personalized cardiometabolic management. Future large-scale prospective cohort studies incorporating comprehensive hepatic phenotyping, serial viral load testing, and complete longitudinal antiviral treatment documentation are urgently needed to minimize residual confounding, establish population-specific optimal TyG/TyG-WHtR cutoff values, and quantify the incremental prognostic value of these metabolic markers beyond established liver fibrosis and cardiovascular risk biomarkers.

Conclusion

Based on a nationally representative U.S. NHANES cohort, elevated TyG and TyG-WHtR indices were independently associated with higher all-cause mortality among adults with serological evidence of prior HAV or HBV infection after multivariable adjustment. These findings highlight the interplay between glucolipid metabolic dysfunction and long-term survival in individuals with resolved viral hepatitis. However, the predictive clinical utility of TyG and TyG-WHtR remains unvalidated in external hepatitis cohorts. Further multi-center prospective studies incorporating detailed liver disease staging, viral load, and antiviral treatment data are needed to confirm our observations and establish standardized thresholds before these metabolic markers can be integrated into routine clinical risk assessment workflows for patients with prior viral hepatitis.
