Assembling peptides for treating Alzheimer's disease: a supramolecular perspective
Authors: Xiaohua Fang, Zhuobin Zhang, Xiaoyu Hu, Miaotiao Zhang, Han Yang, Junting He, Yajun Shi, Guanying Li, Yanyan Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder. Current clinical treatments for AD primarily focus on alleviating symptoms but still face limitations such as suboptimal efficacy, side effects, and irreversible neuronal loss. Misfolding of the Aβ peptide plays a key role in AD pathogenesis and progression. By targeting this AD pathology, peptide-based therapeutics, particularly peptide assembly strategies, hold immense potential in AD therapy. Unlike sequence-based conventional reviews, this review reorganizes relevant advances from the perspective of a unified supramolecular framework. We first illustrate the structural, kinetic, and thermodynamic molecular basis of the pathological Aβ assembly and its neurotoxicity. Three mainstream supramolecular intervention strategies are then discussed: peptides redirecting the regulation of aberrant Aβ assembly, neuroprotective peptide scaffolds, and drug-loading peptide hydrogels. Finally, a universal rational design workflow for multifunctional peptide therapeutics is summarized, and perspectives on advanced directions are outlined.

Introduction

Defined by a gradual and irreversible decline in cognitive functions, including memory, thinking, language, and problem-solving abilities, Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that primarily affects the the ageing population.1 It is the most common cause of dementia, accounting for 60–80% of all dementia cases. According to the systematic analysis for the Global Burden of Diseases (GBD) study,2 AD has become the most common neurodegenerative disease worldwide, with patient numbers reaching 56 million. As the aging population continues to grow, this number is expected to keep rising. The high prevalence of AD not only brings great suffering to patients but also places an enormous burden on families, healthcare systems, and society.

The primary pathological hallmark of AD is the formation of senile plaques in the human hippocampus and cerebral cortex, which are composed of insoluble β-amyloid (Aβ) peptides. The pathogenesis of AD is not entirely clear yet. The amyloid cascade hypothesis,3–6 although still under debate,7 has dominated research in this field. Briefly, it posits that the abnormal accumulation of Aβ peptides is the primary initiating event in AD. Aβ is a proteolytic product of amyloid precursor protein (APP). In the non-amyloidogenic pathway, APP is cleaved by α-secretase in the middle of the Aβ sequence within APP, precluding the formation of Aβ. On the contrary, in the amyloidogenic pathway, APP is cleaved by β- and γ-secretases at the N-terminus of the Aβ sequence, yielding several Aβ peptides of variable length.8 The aggregation of Aβ into plaques triggers a pathocascade of cellular events, including neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and activation of apoptotic pathways, ultimately leading to neuronal death and cognitive decline.

Current clinical treatments for AD primarily focus on alleviating its symptoms. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved several cognitive-improving drugs for AD treatment in clinical trials since 1993.9 Cholinesterase inhibitors, including tacrine (no longer used in clinical settings due to hepatotoxicity), donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine, can enhance acetylcholine levels to support cognitive abilities. Memantine is an N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist used to improve global functions by regulating glutamate levels. Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine remain the primary treatment for late-stage AD with mild, moderate to severe dementia. Recently, the FDA also approved the monoclonal antibodies lecanemab in 2023 and donanemab in 2024 for AD management. These anti-amyloid antibodies bind to and eliminate soluble amyloid beta (Aβ) oligomers and have proven efficacy in slowing cognitive decline in patients with mild cognitive impairment due to early-stage AD.10,11 Despite clinical benefits, these symptomatic therapies are still facing significant safety and access challenges. First, their clinical efficacy remains suboptimal, which is likely attributed to poor brain–blood barrier (BBB) penetration and narrow targeting of these anti-AD drugs. Second, treatment-related side effects, such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, severely impact the quality of life of patients. Third, the clinical trials of these drugs largely enroll patients with established AD pathology, where neuronal loss is irreversible. This therapeutic gap underscores the need for developing innovative platforms to address the complexity of AD.

Peptides have drawn increasing attention as potential therapeutics for AD due to their simplicity and reproducibility in chemical synthesis, potent target affinity and specificity, high BBB permeability and low immunogenicity. Existing reviews12–15 on anti-AD peptides generally classify peptides by sequence type or biological activity, lacking a unified supramolecular logical thread to connect molecular non-covalent interactions, peptide self-assembly morphology, and ultimate anti-AD functions. Through sophisticated molecular design, peptides can self-assemble into various nanostructures, such as fibrils, hydrogels, and vesicles, shifting the paradigm in the development of candidates for anti-AD therapeutics. These self-assembled peptide structures can interact with key pathological components in AD, such as amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau proteins, in multiple ways. By integrating anti-inflammatory activity with neuroprotective properties, peptide assemblies offer a multifaceted approach to AD therapy. Peptide-assembling materials have demonstrated therapeutic benefits in treating central nervous system injuries.16–19 Unlike conventional descriptive summaries, the present review adopts a unified supramolecular analytical framework centered on intermolecular recognition mechanisms to systematically reorganize and dissect the state-of-the-art progress of therapeutic peptide assemblies for AD (Fig. 1). We elaborate the supramolecular thermodynamic and kinetic basis of aberrant Aβ self-assembly. We then categorize three core therapeutic targets arising from multi-stages of Aβ aggregation, alongside matched supramolecular intervention strategies: peptide modulators that redirect the pathological Aβ assembly, neuroprotective peptide scaffolds, and self-assembled peptide hydrogels for localized sustained delivery of anti-AD bioactive agents. Finally, we summarize a universal rational design workflow for multifunctional peptide therapeutics and outline cutting-edge research avenues, including AI-driven de novo design of Aβ-affinity peptides and innovative Aβ-degrading technologies.

Overview of Aβ assembly

Aβ peptides recovered from AD brain tissue display heterogeneous sequence lengths, among which Aβ42 stands out as the most hydrophobic and aggregation-prone isoform compared to shorter variants such as Aβ40 and Aβ38. Two discrete hydrophobic supramolecular interaction domains dominate Aβ42's self-assembly propensity, including the central hydrophobic core (Aβ16–21) and the extended C-terminal hydrophobic segment (Aβ29–42) (Fig. 2A). These two hydrophobic regions possess a high intrinsic β-strand conformational propensity, which drives spontaneous intermolecular self-association to generate mature amyloid fibrils. The peptide segments within these two domains represent the core self-recognition motifs of Aβ.

Atomic-resolution structural characterization of Aβ assemblies has been enabled by a suite of advanced biophysical techniques, such as X-ray micro-crystallography,20,21 solid-state NMR,22,23 cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM),24–27 and cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET).28–30 These tools have unambiguously verified the widespread structural polymorphism of Aβ assemblies.31 The core architectural motif shared across all Aβ fibril polymorphs is the cross-β spine, where stacked β-strands form continuous β-sheet layers that constitute the rigid backbone supporting fibrillogenesis (Fig. 2B). Distinct Aβ42 fibril polymorphs exhibit divergent dimer packing arrangements within their cross-β spines. For example, cryo-EM mapping of Aβ filaments isolated from AD patients' brains revealed two types of Aβ42 filaments (Fig. 2C).24 In type I filaments, residues 19–42 adopt an S-shaped fold, and the two S-shaped domains fold around the central hydrophobic clusters and the C-terminal part. While in type II filaments, residues 20–42 adopt an S-shaped fold, and the second peptide reorients from the C-terminal segment (Fig. 2D). A conserved supramolecular structural unit termed the steric zipper31 is found across all variants. A steric zipper forms when two antiparallel or parallel β-strands interdigitate their hydrophobic side chains via tight non-covalent packing, requiring only 4–6 consecutive hydrophobic residues to establish stable intermolecular adhesion. Multiple short Aβ subsequences capable of forming independent steric zipper motifs have been identified via structural studies,20,21,31 including KLVFFA from Aβ16–21, NKGAII from Aβ27–32, AIIGLM from Aβ30–35 and GGVVIA from Aβ37–42 (Fig. 2E).

The supramolecular assembly pathway of Aβ proceeds through three well-defined kinetic phases (Fig. 2F) described by its thermodynamic principles:32,33 (1) lag phase in which monomeric Aβ undergoes slow nucleation, which is dependent on β-sheet stacking and hydrophobic interactions between transient steric zipper domains pairing; this step represents the highest kinetic barrier of the entire aggregation cascade, as unstable small oligomers carry substantial energetic frustration until sufficient zipper contacts accumulate to offset unfavorable free energy. (2) Exponential growth phase in which the nucleation barrier is overcome, and existing oligomers act as thermodynamic templates to recruit free Aβ monomers; progressive expansion of the aggregate-prone region-mediated hydrophobic packing and hydrogen bonding continuously reduces the overall system free energy, causing protofibrils to elongate rapidly. (3) Plateau phase in which monomer concentration depletes, fibril growth ceases, and mature insoluble plaques form.

Critically, the soluble Aβ oligomers generated during the lag and the early growth phases exhibit much stronger neurotoxicity than insoluble mature fibril Aβ plaques34 owing to their smaller size and higher membrane permeability (Fig. 2G). Soluble Aβ oligomers trigger a series of neurotoxic effects through direct neurotoxicity or indirect induced neuroinflammatory actions. They can disrupt the integrity of cell membranes,35,36 interfere with ion homeostasis,37,38 and increase oxidative stress within neurons, ultimately leading to neuronal dysfunction. They can also activate microglia, the immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), to release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), which can cause chronic neuroinflammation.39,40 This neuroinflammation further damages neurons and disrupts normal neural function. Aβ oligomers impair synaptic function,41 correlated with cognitive decline in AD patients.

Aβ′s intrinsic hydrophobic sequence features mediate its self-assembly into cytotoxic oligomeric intermediates, which sequentially trigger neuronal damage, neuroinflammation and synaptic failure. This chain of supramolecular causality not only provides insights into the pathogenic mechanism of AD but also guides the rational design of therapeutic strategies for AD therapy. The most potent disease-modifying intervention strategy targets the upstream supramolecular assembly process to eliminate toxic Aβ oligomeric intermediates before they initiate neurotoxic cascades.

Therapeutic targets of peptide assemblies

Overview of peptide assembly

Peptide assembly, a fundamental process in supramolecular chemistry, refers to the spontaneous organization of peptide molecules into well-defined, ordered supramolecular architectures via non-covalent interactions,42–45 including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interaction, π–π stacking, electrostatic interaction, and van der Waals forces. Comprehensive reviews have extensively summarized the types of non-covalent interactions and regulatory factors affecting peptide assembly.45–49 From a supramolecular perspective, peptide assembly is a double-edged sword. On the one hand, it fundamentally participates in the amyloid fibril formation involved in the AD pathogenesis; on the other hand, it offers new opportunities for the development of therapies for AD. Rationally designed peptide assemblies with tunable supramolecular interactions can be exploited to interrupt Aβ aggregation thermodynamics and rescue damaged neurons, creating viable AD therapeutic candidates. Unlike covalent polymer synthesis, which relies on irreversible chemical bond formation, peptide assembly is driven by dynamic, adjustable intermolecular forces, endowing them with unique stimulus responsiveness and adaptability, which are particularly valuable for biomedical applications, including AD therapy.

Peptide assembly is thermodynamically and kinetically tunable, with their final architectures primarily encoded by the peptide sequence and modulated by microenvironmental stimuli, such as pH, salt stimuli and enzyme stimuli.44 They can self-assemble into organized nanostructures, such as nanofibers, nanotubes, nanospheres, and micelles. Peptide nanofibers further entangle into three-dimensional porous networks, retaining water to form macroscopic peptide hydrogels. The structural diversity of peptide assemblies is one of their most attractive features for biomedical applications.50–53 They also provide a new opportunity for multi-modal intervention of AD therapy, given that different supramolecular forms exert differentiated therapeutic effects. For example, nanofibers can serve as inhibitors of amyloid fibril formation and remove mature amyloid fibrils via entrapment. Nanospheres and micelles act as nanocarriers for anti-AD drugs. Hydrogels with high water content mimic the mechanical properties of the CNS extracellular matrix (ECM), and the porous structure in peptide-assembling hydrogels not only supports neuron survival but also efficiently encapsulates and retains bioactive cargoes such as anti-oxidative species and anti-inflammatory drugs for localized and sustained therapy in the brain.

Peptide assemblies targeting multi-stage Aβ pathology

Guided by the supramolecular structural mechanism of Aβ self-assembly and its downstream neurotoxic cascades, designer self-assembled peptides can be rationally categorized into three therapeutic branches with distinct intervention targets: toxic Aβ oligomers, preformed Aβ fibrils, and Aβ-damaged neurons (Fig. 3).

Therapeutic targets for peptide assembly used for treating AD. Peptide assembly targets the Aβ supramolecular assembly by blocking the elongation of the Aβ monomer/oligomer, redirecting the Aβ assembly toward disordered amorphous aggregates trapped in kinetically stable states or breaking preformed fibrils to shift the overall Aβ assembly equilibrium away from cytotoxic oligomeric species. Peptide assembly can also target neurons by protecting them from Aβ-induced damage or promoting neuroregeneration, rooted in the ECM-mimicking properties of peptide assemblies.

Targeting toxic Aβ monomers/oligomers

Soluble Aβ oligomers represent the primary neurotoxic species in AD, originating from the lag-phase nucleation mediated by Aβ's central and C-terminal hydrophobic segments. Peptide assemblies neutralize oligomer toxicity via two distinct supramolecular regulatory pathways differentiated by their intermolecular binding modes. The first pathway involves the competitive blocking of Aβ's self-recognition motifs. Short peptide fragments matching Aβ's self-recognition sequences specifically bind to the hydrophobic pockets on Aβ monomers or oligomers, occupying the sites required for inter-peptide hydrophobic contact and cross-β formation. This competitive binding interrupts the lag-phase nucleation and completely arrests the generation of low-molecular-weight toxic oligomers. The second pathway for supramolecular co-assembly is conformational redirection. Rationally designed peptides are integrated into growing Aβ oligomers via balanced intermolecular forces, disrupting ordered cross-β stacking and redirecting Aβ folding toward disordered amorphous aggregates that are trapped in kinetically stable states. These co-assembled hybrid aggregates lose membrane-permeable cytotoxicity and can be readily cleared by cellular proteases and microglia without triggering microglial pro-inflammatory activation or neuronal membrane damage.

Targeting preformed Aβ fibrils

Mature insoluble Aβ fibrils deposit as senile plaques in late-stage AD brains; though less acutely toxic than oligomers, they continuously shed toxic oligomeric fragments and obstruct extracellular transport, forming persistent pathological reservoirs. Therapeutic intervention on mature fibrils is to fragmentize intact fibrils into small, non-amyloidogenic soluble fragments that lose amyloid seeding activity and can be efficiently eliminated via microglial phagocytosis. Furthermore, fibril disassembly curtails the continuous release of toxic oligomers from plaques, indirectly shifting the overall Aβ aggregation equilibrium away from cytotoxic oligomeric species and alleviating long-term progressive pathology.

Targeting Aβ-impaired neurons

Beyond direct modulation of the Aβ supramolecular assembly, peptide assemblies display ECM-mimicking properties and exert therapeutic effects at the cellular level by targeting Aβ-impaired neurons to reverse Aβ-induced irreversible neuronal loss in AD. One intervention approach is passive neuronal protection against Aβ oligomer-induced damage. Bioactive epitope-carrying peptide assemblies spontaneously form biomimetic 3D nanofiber networks that support the attachment of neurons, stabilize neuronal cytoskeletons, and downregulate the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, simultaneously alleviating direct neuronal toxicity and chronic neuroinflammation triggered by Aβ. Another approach focuses on active neuronal regeneration to compensate for lost neural circuits, which is mainly realized by combining supramolecular peptide scaffolds with neural stem cell (NSC) transplantation. The nanofibrous ECM-mimetic architectures provided by peptide assemblies significantly improve the adhesion and survival of grafted cells in the toxic AD brain microenvironment while guiding stem cell differentiation toward mature neurons rather than glial cells. Collectively, the unique ECM-mimicking properties of peptide assemblies provide a long-term regenerative solution to irreversible neuronal degeneration.

Peptides targeting Aβ to modulate Aβ aggregation

The Aβ oligomeric intermediates generated in the early phases of Aβ aggregation are now believed to be the most toxic species that lead to AD pathogenesis and progression. Strategies that modulate the Aβ aggregation processes have been developed to remove these toxic intermediates. Specifically, as shown in Fig. 4A, the peptide is designed to cap the core aggregation-prone regions of Aβ monomers or break the β-sheet of pre-formed Aβ oligomers (also called the β-sheet breaker strategy54–56). These two strategies prevent Aβ oligomers from interacting with each other and forming amyloid fibrillar plaques, thus inhibiting the Aβ aggregation processes. In addition, a peptide that promotes Aβ fibrillation through co-assembly with the Aβ monomer has also been proven effective in reducing toxic oligomers. Representative peptides modulating Aβ assembly

Arresting Aβ fibril formation

Structural evidence has revealed that β-sheet-prone sequences, such as the central hydrophobic core and the hydrophobic C-terminus, are the critical regions involved in amyloid fibrillogenesis. Short peptide fragments derived from these sequences are more favorable for the paired β-sheet interactions and bind more strongly to the homologous sequence of Aβ. Consequently, they block the hydrophobic core region of Aβ, preventing cross-β sheet extension and arresting Aβ fibril formation. Therefore, peptides based on these self-recognition motifs are widely investigated as amyloid inhibitors.13

Tjernberg et al.57 systematically mapped Aβ′s self-interaction regions by synthesizing 31 overlapping fragments of Aβ40 and screening their ability to bind full-length Aβ and identified that the central Aβ16–20 sequence (KLVFF) is the shortest peptide retaining high Aβ-binding affinity. A 7-mer peptide ligand Aβ15–20 AcQKLVFFNH2 derived from this sequence can block the formation of dense amyloid fibril bundles when co-incubated with Aβ40 at equimolar concentrations. Matsunaga et al.58 found that the 8-mer peptide Aβ15–22 (QKLVFFAE) can stop Aβ42 from aggregating at acidic pH and inhibit its serine proteinase-like activity at neutral pH. S. Zimbone et al.59 tested the Aβ8–20 fragment and demonstrated that Aβ8–20 does not form amyloid fibrils by itself. However, it interacts with the 4–11 region of Aβ42 and inhibits the formation of toxic oligomeric species and Aβ fibrils, therefore protecting neuron-like cells.

The B. Mandal group designed a flexible hairpin-shaped synthetic paratope peptide SP1 by integrating two N-methylated Aβ KLVFFA epitope strands connected via a PEG flexible linker, where alternating N-methylation blocks intermolecular hydrogen bonds to avoid the self-aggregation of SP1 itself.73 SP1 selectively binds the central KLVFFA and C-terminal hydrophobic motifs of Aβ40/Aβ42 to redirect its assembly toward large nontoxic amorphous off-pathway aggregates, simultaneously inhibiting early toxic oligomer nucleation and disassembling mature Aβ fibrils in a dose-dependent manner. In vitro membrane leakage assays verified that SP1-treated Aβ assemblies lose the capacity to perforate lipid bilayers, eliminating the primary source of oligomer-mediated neurotoxicity. Cellular experiments on SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells further confirmed that SP1 suppresses Aβ-induced ROS overproduction and apoptotic signaling cascades, while in vivo rat assays demonstrated that SP exhibits low systemic toxicity and effectively rescues Aβ-triggered hippocampal neuronal degeneration.

The C-terminal region of Aβ is critical in driving Aβ oligomerization as this hydrophobic stretch forms the core of Aβ oligomers.61,78 R. Mallesh et al.60 designed seven Aβ C-terminal fragment analogs pairing Aβ31–x (where x = 35–41) and screened them for anti-Aβ aggregation activity. The Thioflavin T (ThT) assay showed decreased ThT fluorescence when incubating Aβ42 with these fragments (schematic illustrated in Fig. 5A-1), while ACT-7 (sequence IIGLMVGGVVI) derived from Aβ31–41 emerged as the most potent one. Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) results demonstrated that ACT-7 exhibits high binding affinity to Aβ42 (Ka = 2.37 × 106 M−1). Dot blot experiments (Fig. 5A-2) and TEM observation (Fig. 5A-3) suggested that ACT-7 binds to Aβ42 and blocks the fibrillation process, preventing the formation of amyloid fibrils. CD and FTIR spectroscopy demonstrate that ACT-7 stabilizes Aβ42 in a random coil conformation, preventing β-sheet conversion. Moreover, ACT-7 is nontoxic to PC-12-derived neurons and protects them from Aβ42-induced toxicity. S. Bansal et al.79 evaluated the 6-mer Aβ32–37 fragment (IGLMVG-NH2), which showed complete mitigation of Aβ toxicity toward the PC12 cells at 2 µM. C. Peters et al.62 demonstrated that 5-mer GLMVG Aβ33–37 interacts with Aβ at the C-terminal region and interferes with its aggregation, association and perforation of the plasma membrane, reducing synaptotoxicity on hippocampal neurons. E. A. Fradinger et al.63 evaluated homologous C-terminal fragments (CTFs) Aβx–42 (where x = 28–39) and identified Aβ31–42 as the most potent Aβ aggregation inhibitor. In addition, Aβ39–42 (VVIA) is the shortest peptide on the C-terminal region of Aβ that can alter Aβ aggregation. VVIA inhibits Aβ-induced cell death and rescues Aβ42 oligomer-induced synaptic disruption at micromolar concentrations. Discrete molecular dynamics (DMD) simulations revealed that it co-assembles with Aβ42 to form nontoxic heterooligomers (Fig. 5B-2).

Disrupting Aβ aggregation

A pioneering design based on aggregation disruption was proposed by J. Ghanta et al. in 1996,64 who focused on combining an Aβ-specific recognition element with an amyloid-disrupting element to abrogate Aβ toxicity. This prototype inhibitor H2 integrates a recognition segment Aβ15–25 (QKLVFFAEDVG-) with a C-terminal oligolysine (-KKKKKK) disrupting element linked via a glycine and aminocaproate spacer (-Ga-). The recognition element enables specific binding to full-length Aβ39, while the hydrophilic, cationic K6 segment modulates the Aβ aggregation pathways. The TEM images reveal that H2 reduces the average fibril length and increases fibril entanglement, while the CD spectra show no significant alteration to the overall β-sheet structure of Aβ39. The MTT assay reveals that H2 completely blocks Aβ toxicity in PC12 neuronal cells. Pallitto et al.65 in 1999 optimized the recognition elements and the linker and developed KLVFF-KKKKKK and VLFKF-KKKKKK (with scrambled sequence to Aβ16–20), which completely protected against Aβ toxicity compared to the recognition motif KLVFF alone. In 2005, the same group further developed KLVFF-RRRRRR, having the R6 sequence as a disrupting element replacing the K6 sequence.66 Following a similar design, K. Watanabe et al.71 used a flexible hydrophilic repeating unit as a disrupting element. A hydrophilic chain containing aminoethoxy ethoxy acetic acid (AEEA) or AEEA-aspartate hydride derivatives was conjugated to the C-terminus of KLVFF.

C. Soto et al.67 incorporated proline residue in place of the V18 residue within the β-pleated structure Aβ17–21 (LVFFA). The introduction of proline residue impairs peptide bond interactions and generates steric hindrance, efficiently disrupting β-sheet formation. The lead inhibitor iAβ1 (11-residue sequence RDLPFFPVPID) binds Aβ with a relative dissociation constant of ∼75.9 nM and exhibits concentration-dependent inhibition of both Aβ40 and Aβ42 fibrillogenesis. Critically, iAβ1 also disassembles preformed fibrils, achieving near-complete disaggregation of Aβ40 fibrils. Shorter derivative iAβ5 (LPFFD) improves the inhibitory efficacy of Aβ fibrillogenesis in vitro and prevents neuronal cell death induced by Aβ fibrils in cell culture (Fig. 5C-2). Furthermore, iAβ5 significantly reduces Aβ deposition in vivo and completely blocks the formation of amyloid fibrils in a rat brain model of amyloidosis.68 To improve its stability in vivo, N-terminal acetylation and C-terminal protection were added to iAβ5, leading to iAβ5p (Ac-LPFFD-NH2). iAβ5p demonstrates improved stability against proteolytic degradation, a high rate of penetration across the blood–brain barrier, and the ability to reduce amyloid deposition in the brain of transgenic AD mice (Fig. 5C-3).69 To further enhance the in vivo half-life of iAβ5p, N-methylation was subsequently introduced at the cleavage sites to protect it from endoproteolytic degradation. The results indicated that a methyl group introduced at the nitrogen atom of one amide bond led to a 10-fold higher in vivo half-life and good brain uptake compared to iAβ5p.70

The B. Mandal group designed lactam-based tail-to-side-chain stapled macrocyclic peptides centered on Aβ′s LVFF aggregation motif, with variable dicarboxylic acid linkers and optional anthranilic acid turn units to lock rigid binding conformations.72 These stapled peptides bind Aβ and redirect its assembly toward large, nontoxic off-pathway amorphous aggregates, simultaneously inhibiting early oligomer nucleation and disassembling mature cross-β fibrils without generating harmful intermediate species. Lipid vesicle tests confirmed that the resulting hybrid assemblies lose their membrane-damaging capacity, eliminating Aβ oligomer-induced neurotoxicity.

Co-assembly with Aβ into non-toxic nanofibers/aggregates

Peptides with complementary β-sheet motifs co-assemble with Aβ, redirecting their aggregation into non-toxic structures. In Section 4.1.1, the Aβ-derived short peptide fragments possessing beta-sheet forming interactions generally co-assemble with Aβ, blocking the cross β-sheet formation to suppress Aβ fibrillogenesis. These co-assembling processes are directed toward the formation of heterooligomers that are less toxic than Aβ self-assembled oligomers. However, since oligomers are metastable, their subsequent fibrillation process has been rarely explored, raising concerns about their long-term biosafety. An alternative strategy to alleviate Aβ oligomer toxicity is to promote its aggregation. To this regard, the peptide co-assembles with Aβ to form nanofibers or amorphous aggregates, stable forms of molecular assembly that lie in the thermodynamic and kinetic traps, thus significantly neutralizing the toxicity of Aβ oligomers.

A. Yang et al.74 designed peptide A3 (sequence DDKSGYKVDDQD) utilising inter-peptide electrostatic forces as well as the hydrophilic and hydrophobic interactions with Aβ5–17. The ThT assay indicated that the Aβ aggregation process was significantly accelerated by the A3 peptide without a lag time (schematic illustrated in Fig. 5A-1). Both TEM and AFM images demonstrated that A3 co-assembled with Aβ into small amorphous aggregates. In a transgenic Caenorhabditis elegans model overexpressing amyloid precursor protein, A3 could significantly attenuate Aβ oligomer-induced paralysis.

The Aα peptides (also named p3) are C-terminal peptide fragments with the sequences of Aβ17–40/42. They are alternative cleavage products of the amyloid precursor proteins and were historically designated as non-amyloidogenic. However, recent evidence75,80 has suggested Aα can also form amyloid fibrils with kinetics dominated by secondary nucleation. When cross-seeded with full-length Aβ, a strong nucleation occurs with very fast aggregation speed, eventually generating co-assembling fibrils.801H–15N heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) NMR (Fig. 5B-1) was conducted to reveal the binding mode of Aα and Aβ, and the results demonstrated the most substantial changes in the S26, N27 and G37 residues.75 Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations reveal that Aα promotes increased β-sheet formation within the 17–42 segment of Aβ.81 The intra-layer salt bridge (such as D23–K28) and the inter-layer hydrophobic interactions play crucial roles in the co-assembly process of Aα and Aβ. Aα oligomers are cytotoxic primarily due to the formation of large ion channels across cellular membranes. However, the co-assembly of Aα and Aβ significantly neutralizes the toxicity of both Aα oligomers and Aβ oligomers.

K. Taş et al.76 modified amyloid core mimics (ACMs) Aβ15–40 by replacing the loop tripeptide segments (LTSs) Aβ24–26 VGS with β-sheet-propagating hydrophobic tripeptides such as tris-norleucine (Nle)3, tris-leucine (Leu)3 and tris-phenylalanine (Phe)3 to strengthen β-sheet interaction surfaces. Selective amide bond N-methylation on two alternate residues (Leu17, Val18 or Phe19) within one Aβ β-strand segment was incorporated to suppress intrinsic amyloidogenicity while preserving high-affinity binding to Aβ42. Additionally, the Met35 residue was replaced with Nle to avoid Met(O)-related side effects. Building on this design concept, they screened six lead ACMs, which were non-amyloidogenic in isolation but strongly co-assembled with Aβ42 with nanomolar affinity. The co-assembly of Aβ42 with ACM was visualized by two-photon stimulated emission depletion (STED) imaging (Fig. 5A-4) and FLIM-FRET imaging (Fig. 5A-5) and further confirmed by spectroscopic evidence. Images revealed that Aβ42/ACM co-assembles into µm-long heteromeric nanofiber bundles with widths between 0.5 and 2 µm. These heteromeric nanofibers are non-toxic, thermolabile, proteolytically degradable, and efficiently phagocytosed by microglia, facilitating their clearance in the brain. Ex vivo studies in murine hippocampal slices show they fully ameliorate Aβ42-mediated impairment of long-term potentiation (LTP).

O-Glycosylation at the Tyr10 site of Aβ is one of the post-translational modifications naturally occurring in Aβ fragments from AD patients' cerebrospinal fluid.82,83 The S. Dong group developed O-glycopeptides as an engineered strategy to modulate Aβ42 aggregation.84 They found that glycosylation of full-length Aβ42 with the GalNAc monosaccharide (msAβ), Galβ1-3GalNAc disaccharide (dsAβ), and sialyl Galβ1-3GalNAc trisaccharide (tsAβ) at the Tyr10 site inhibit the fibril formation of Aβ42, with tsAβ showing the most significant modulatory effect. Compared to Aβ42 fibrils, dsAβ fibrils are less stable and more vulnerable to Aβ-degrading enzymes. They further evaluated monosaccharide-modified Aβ9–21 fragments, which modulate Aβ42 aggregation by promoting the formation of non-toxic, stable fibrils (Fig. 5A-3), thereby reducing neurotoxic oligomeric species.77 β-GalNAc-modified Aβ9–21 exhibits the highest binding affinity for Aβ42 (KD = 17.4 µM). Cryo-EM structural analysis reveals that β-GalNAc-modified Aβ9–21 co-assembles with Aβ42 to form three distinct fibril polymorphs with unique core architectures. Specifically, β-GalNAc-modified Aβ9–21 adopts an elongated β-strand conformation, attaches to the fibril surface, and forms extensive interactions, including steric zippers, hydrophobic contacts, and π–π stacking with Aβ42 residues His13-Phe19, stabilizing the newly formed fibril structures (Fig. 5B-3 and 3B-5). β-GalNAc-modified Aβ9–21 exhibits potent neuroprotective effects by rescuing Aβ42-induced cytotoxicity in SH-SY5Y cells and rat primary neurons in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5C-1). In APP/PS1 transgenic mice, intravenous administration of liposome-encapsulated β-GalNAc-modified Aβ9–21 alleviates spatial cognitive impairment (Fig. 5C-4) and reduces hippocampal neuron loss without systemic toxicity.

Peptide assemblies protect Aβ-impaired neurons and promote neuronal regeneration

In addition to directly interacting with Aβ to modulate Aβ aggregation, peptide assembly can protect neurons from the toxic effects associated with AD. Peptide assembles into three-dimensional networks that entrap high volume of water, resulting in hydrogels similar to the native extracellular environment. Peptide-based hydrogels demonstrate high biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, adaptive mechanical properties, and versatility in fabrication, serving as scaffolds for cell growth and differentiation. These scaffolds can provide a physical support for cells attachment and also present biochemical cues to guide cell behavior, promoting neuronal survival and neurite outgrowth. Moreover, peptides can be tailored as bioactive ligands by sophisticated molecular design (Fig. 6A). These neuroactive ligands are usually short peptide fragments mimicking the functional domain from natural ECM components in the brain, such as fibronectin, collagen, and laminin. The most studied sequences16,85–88 include laminin-derived epitopes IKVAV and YIGSR and fibronectin-derived epitopes RGD and GRGDS. Peptide-assembling hydrogels have emerged as alluring biomedical platforms in neuroregenerative medicine16–18,89 (Table 2).

Peptide assemblies targeting neurons for treating AD

H. Yang et al.90,91 investigated IKVAV-PA (sequenced as C16H31O-A4G3E-IKVAV, as shown in Fig. 6B), a self-assembling peptide amphiphile that forms 3D nanofibers in vivo. This neuroactive peptide amphiphile can self-assemble into nanofibril-entangled hydrogels (Fig. 6C-D) with high epitope density to promote rapid neuronal differentiation in vitro.88 When injected into the hippocampus of APPswe/PS1dE9 transgenic AD mice, IKVAV-PA increases the proliferation (Fig. 6E) of neural progenitor cells (NPCs) and their differentiation into new neurons, and reverses hippocampal neurogenesis impairment. Morris water maze tests show reduced escape latencies and increased target quadrant occupancy, suggesting improved spatial learning and memory. Mechanistically, IKVAV-PA reduces the hippocampal Aβ plaque area and soluble Aβ40/Aβ42 levels in both the cortex and hippocampus and alters amyloid precursor protein (APP) processing by inhibiting BACE1 gene expression and upregulating genes for Aβ-degrading enzymes (IDE and NEP), without affecting total APP or phosphorylated tau levels.

G. Cui et al.92 modified a designer self-assembling peptide RADA16 with the laminin-derived YIGSR motif through a diglycine linker, obtaining DSP (sequenced as Ac-(RADA)4GGYIGSR-NH2, as shown in Fig. 6B). DSP can undergo spontaneous assembly into well-ordered nanofibers to optimize neural stem cell (NSC) transplantation efficacy. NSC cells seeded in the DSP hydrogel expressed higher levels of neuron-related markers such as neuron-specific enolase (NSE) (Fig. 6F) and β3-tubulin (Tju-1), suggesting that neuronal differentiation is promoted by DSP. In vitro, DSP boosts NSC viability, reduces Aβ40-induced apoptosis, and promotes neuronal differentiation. In Aβ40-injected AD rats, NSC transplantation combined with DSP yields superior cognitive rescue compared to NSC alone, unmodified self-assembling peptide (SP), or DSP alone. Moreover, DSP can modulate neuroinflammation by elevating neurotrophic factors (BDNF, CNTF, and IGF-1) and anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 while reducing pro-inflammatory TNF-α and IL-1β.

A. Adak et al.93 conjugated palmitic acid (PA) with a microtubule-stabilizing peptide NAPVSIPQK, and then introduced a triple lysine residue at the C-terminal to promote the amphiphilicity and generate cell adhesion, obtaining a self-assembling peptide amphiphile PA-NK (sequenced as PA-NAPVSIPQKKK, as shown in Fig. 6B). PA-NK self-assembles into a 3D cross-linked fibrillary hydrogel network. It is highly neuro-biocompatible, supporting 2D and 3D (neurosphere) neuron cell culture (Neuro-2a cells). In the presence of proteinase K, PA-NK enzymatically degrades to release the neuroprotective peptide NAPVSIPQ, which stabilizes microtubules by reducing the hyperphosphorylation of tau and promotes neurite outgrowth. PA-NK treatment increases neurite length by ∼50% vs. control and protects PC12 cells from anti-NGF-induced toxicity.

Peptide assembling hydrogels deliver anti-AD agents

Peptide hydrogels also serve as ideal delivery carriers for anti-AD drugs due to their porous architectures (Fig. 6A). Small-molecule drugs or even protein-based drugs can be physically entrapped within the hydrogel matrix. The release of the drugs from the hydrogel can be controlled by adjusting the properties of the hydrogel, such as its cross-linking density and the type of non-covalent interactions. Curcumin is a natural anti-AD compound with well-documented anti-amyloidogenic, microtubule-stabilizing, and neuroprotective activities but poor aqueous solubility and bioavailability.95–97 To overcome these pharmaceutical limitations, A. Adak et al.93 employed PA-NK hydrogels for delivering curcumin in treating AD. PA-NK (2 wt%) efficiently entraps curcumin via hydrophobic interactions within its fibrillary network without compromising the hydrogel's structural integrity. Curcumin is gradually released over 72 hours under physiological conditions (37 °C, PBS pH 7.4). Curcumin-loaded PA-NK exhibits enhanced efficacy compared to free curcumin. MAX8 is a β-hairpin peptide hydrogel (sequenced as VKVKVKVKV-DPro-PTKVEVKVKV) with unique shear-thinning and self-healing properties. S. Lindsey et al.94 employed a MAX8 hydrogel as an injectable delivery vehicle for neurotrophic growth factors (NGF and BDNF). MAX8 self-assembles under physiological conditions (pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl) into a mechanically robust hydrogel that encapsulates NGF and BDNF without disrupting gel formation or its rehealing ability. The results show that MAX8 enables sustained, tunable release of NGF and BDNF for at least 28 days (Fig. 6G). PC12 cells extend neurite-like extensions in response to released factors. Long-term studies confirm that NGF encapsulated in MAX8 retains its activity for 28 days (Fig. 6H), far exceeding its 3.5-day half-life in an aqueous solution. Cell culture results show that 86% of PC12 cells formed neurites after 28 days of encapsulation.

Concluding remarks and future perspectives

The systematic design of therapeutic peptide assemblies for AD follows two complementary engineering pipelines, separately optimized for Aβ-targeted amyloid regulation and neuron-focused neuroprotection, as summarized in the schematic workflow (Fig. 7).

General design workflow of multifunctional peptide assemblies for treating AD. The AI-guided design of Aβ-targeted peptides and peptide-mediated Aβ degradation technologies, highlighted in blue in the schematic, are elaborated as cutting-edge next-generation therapeutic strategies against AD.

For Aβ-targeting peptide ligands, four sequential design modules are integrated stepwise: Aβ-binding motif screening, Aβ aggregation modulation, stability enhancement, and multifunctional bioactivity integration. Researchers have constructed Aβ-binding peptides through structure-based design, such as Aβ-complementary sequences, sequences matching the self-recognition regions of Aβ, or other rationally designed peptides. Phage display technology14,98,99 is an established, powerful platform for the selection and identification of potent peptide candidates with high affinity for Aβ peptide. Next, a suite of molecular modification strategies, including proline insertion, hydrophilic group grafting, and glycosylation, are adopted to redirect aberrant Aβ cross-β self-assembly. Other post-modifications such as stapling, phosphorylation, sulfonation, and lipidation are also worth exploring to evaluate their effectiveness in modulating Aβ assembly as these modifications sharply reshape molecular lipophilicity–hydrophilicity. To overcome the intrinsic limitations of linear peptides, stability optimization is realized via chemical modifications such as terminal capping, backbone N-methylation bonds,70,76,100 and d-amino acid substitution,101–103 resisting proteolytic breakdown in physiological environments. Another strategy is to encapsulate therapeutic peptides within protective carriers, such as liposomes or nanoparticles. These carriers can shield peptides from the harsh physiological environment and prolong their circulation time in the body. Finally, functional moieties are incorporated to equip peptides with blood–brain barrier permeability, cellular clearance capacity, responsiveness to brain pathological microenvironments, and stimuli-triggered activity, laying the foundation for in vivo anti-amyloid therapy. Advances in the identification of brain shuttle peptides104–106 have led to considerable achievements in the development of therapeutic cargos for treating CNS diseases. Conjugating the anti-AD peptides with brain shuttle peptides can enhance their targeted transport across the BBB, not only improving their therapeutic efficacy in the brain but also reducing off-target assembly in healthy organs that causes unintended effects.

In parallel, neuron-targeted peptide assemblies are engineered through a matched four-stage design logic centered on bioactive neuronal epitopes, self-assembling building blocks, therapeutic cargo loading, and integrated biofunctional modification. This branch relies on ECM-derived, neuron-stabilizing or neuron-protein-specific bioactive epitopes as core units; these sequences are further developed into amphipathic designer peptides or hydrogel-forming supramolecular building blocks capable of fabricating biomimetic nanofiber networks. The peptide scaffolds serve as biocompatible carriers for loading diverse therapeutic cargos, including anti-AD agents, neurotrophic growth factors, as well as cells, to deliver combinatorial neuroprotective signals inside damaged brain lesions. Subsequent functional tuning assembles multiple therapeutic functionalities into a single supramolecular system, endows the scaffold with pathological stimuli responsiveness, and ensures full biodegradability to avoid long-term foreign-body accumulation in CNS tissue. Notably, matrix stiffness acts as a critical mechanical cue governing the fate specification of mature neurons and stem cells.107–109 Modulating the stiffness of peptide-based self-assembled hydrogels via supramolecular design provides a novel strategy for constructing regenerative scaffolds against Alzheimer's disease.

Across both design branches, sequence–structure–property correlation analysis acts as the core analytical framework, bridging peptide primary sequences, supramolecular assembly morphologies, and the final anti-AD performance. Every amino acid substitution, backbone modification and side-chain functionalization reshapes non-covalent interaction networks, such as hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic packing, and steric zipper interdigitation. These structural changes, alter assembly thermodynamics, serum stability, biological efficacy and binding affinity toward Aβ or neuronal receptors.

Traditional trial-and-error peptide screening is gradually being replaced by computational simulation pipelines. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations63,81 quantify the binding free energy between candidate peptides and Aβ, predict secondary structural transitions, and forecast the supramolecular topology of self-assembled nanofibers or hydrogel networks. Emerging artificial intelligence tools110–112 represent a transformative advance over conventional computational modeling, unlocking high-throughput de novo peptide design for dual Aβ and neuronal targeting. Machine learning and large biomolecular language models can be employed to mine massive amyloid structural datasets to generate custom peptide sequences with predefined properties, including high Aβ binding selectivity, BBB permeability, proteolytic resistance and ECM-mimetic self-assembly capacity. Reinforcement learning further iteratively optimizes peptide sequences through feedback collected from in silico aggregation kinetic simulations, directly generating lead candidates that redirect Aβ supramolecular folding or support neuronal regeneration.

Beyond redirecting Aβ assembly kinetics, innovative Aβ-degrading tools open a new disease-modifying therapeutic dimension. Biological degradation pathways of the Aβ peptide rely on the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway and lysosomal cascade. Engineered Aβ-binding peptides can be functionalized with targeting tags to shuttle bound Aβ oligomers and fibrils toward proteasomal degradation or trigger autophagosome formation to engulf large amyloid aggregates for lysosomal hydrolysis.113–116 Separately, chemical degradation pathways of the Aβ peptide centered on photooxidation introduce photosensitizer moieties into Aβ-affinity peptides117 or organic ligands.118,119 Upon light stimulation, these groups generate reactive oxygen species that oxidize Aβ′s hydrophobic and aromatic residues, irreversibly disrupting the closed contacts and breaking the peptide backbone to abolish amyloidogenic activity.

Collectively, the two interconnected peptide design routes enable dual intervention against AD core pathologies. Aβ-targeted peptide ligands suppress toxic Aβ oligomer generation and disassemble mature amyloid plaques by tuning supramolecular intermolecular forces, while neuron-targeted self-assembled peptide hydrogel scaffolds can construct supportive ECM-mimetic microenvironments to protect compromised neurons, facilitate neural regeneration, and alleviate neuroinflammation. Supported by systematic sequence–structure–property analysis, computational simulation, fast-evolving AI design platforms and novel Aβ-degrading peptide tools, rationally engineered supramolecular peptide assemblies form a multi-modal disease-modifying therapeutic toolkit for AD treatment that outperforms single-target peptide candidates.
