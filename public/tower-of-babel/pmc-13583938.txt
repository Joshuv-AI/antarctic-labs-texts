Too little, too late? The burden of delayed care in NTDT
Authors: Khaled M. Musallam, Thomas D. Coates, Ali T. Taher
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Cheng et al1 report the largest multicenter analysis from the United States specifically examining patients with non–transfusion-dependent thalassemia (NTDT) who were only referred to specialist thalassemia centers during adulthood. Their findings are both eye-opening and concerning. Nearly half of the patients ultimately required initiation of regular transfusion therapy after specialist evaluation, whereas substantial proportions had already developed iron overload, pulmonary hypertension, thrombosis, endocrine complications, or extramedullary hematopoiesis by the time they reached expert care.1

Importantly, these observations are not unique to the United States. Rather, they confirm what has gradually emerged over the past decade from cohorts in the Mediterranean region, the Middle East, and Asia: NTDT is not a benign condition that can simply be observed over time.2 Instead, it is a progressive disease characterized by cumulative pathophysiologic insults that require careful surveillance and, when appropriate, intervention.3,4

The evolution of terminology itself reflects this shift in understanding. Historically, patients were classified as having “thalassemia intermedia,” a term that implied a disease severity somewhere between thalassemia major and thalassemia trait. However, this classification failed to capture the underlying biology driving long-term complications. The adoption of the term NTDT emphasized transfusion requirement rather than perceived disease severity.5 Yet the absence of regular transfusions should never be mistaken for absence of disease burden.

We now recognize that chronic anemia, ineffective erythropoiesis, hemolysis, primary iron overload, and hypercoagulability interact throughout life to drive progressive organ damage.3,4 Unlike transfusion-dependent thalassemia, where transfusion-related complications dominate, NTDT complications often arise insidiously over decades. Pulmonary hypertension and thromboembolic disease, most common in splenectomized adults, remain among the most serious complications and are major contributors to mortality.6,7 Once established, many of these complications become difficult, or impossible, to fully reverse. Likewise, iron overload resulting from increased intestinal iron absorption accumulates silently over time and may ultimately contribute to advanced liver disease, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, another leading cause of mortality in NTDT.3,4,6

Paradoxically, the relatively slow and cumulative nature of NTDT pathophysiology presents a major opportunity. These processes evolve over years, creating a window during which intervention can modify disease trajectory. Perhaps the most alarming finding in the study is the extent of undertreated iron overload. Among patients with significant hepatic iron accumulation, only approximately one-quarter were receiving iron chelation therapy at the time of referral. This is particularly concerning because iron overload is one of the most readily identifiable and treatable risk factors in NTDT. Iron chelation therapy is now firmly established as standard of care, with robust evidence demonstrating effective reductions in liver iron concentration and favorable long-term safety profiles.4,6,9 Indeed, iron removal in NTDT can often be achieved efficiently because patients are not continuously accumulating transfusional iron. The low use of chelation observed in this cohort highlights a major implementation gap rather than a lack of therapeutic options.

The management of anemia has historically been more challenging. For many years, regular transfusion therapy represented the principal strategy for improving hemoglobin levels and suppressing ineffective erythropoiesis. The increasing recognition of anemia as an independent driver of morbidity in adulthood has undoubtedly influenced evolving treatment practices and guideline recommendations.4 The fact that nearly half of patients in this study were advised to initiate regular transfusions after specialist assessment reflects a growing awareness that waiting for complications to emerge may no longer be acceptable practice. Similar to adults, children with NTDT also require early evaluation by a pediatric thalassemia specialist because failure to recognize and appropriately manage anemia before ∼10 to 12 years of age may result in the loss of a critical window for achieving optimal linear growth, with deficits that may not be fully recoverable later in life.

Yet important questions remain. Intervention is relatively straightforward when patients present with symptomatic anemia, extramedullary hematopoiesis, pulmonary hypertension, or other established complications. The more challenging scenario involves patients with hemoglobin levels persistently below optimal thresholds but without overt symptoms or organ damage. Emerging data indicate that what matters most is not achieving a hemoglobin concentration >10 g/dL at a single point in time but maximizing the proportion of a patient’s life spent above this threshold, thereby reducing cumulative exposure to ineffective erythropoiesis and chronic anemia.10 However, recommending lifelong transfusion therapy solely to prevent complications that may occur decades later can be difficult for both physicians and patients. Concerns regarding transfusion burden and secondary iron overload remain legitimate, even in the era of effective chelation.

Fortunately, the therapeutic landscape is rapidly evolving. The recent emergence of disease-modifying therapies such as mitapivat offers the possibility of improving anemia and ineffective erythropoiesis without reliance on chronic transfusions. Such therapies may fundamentally alter treatment paradigms by enabling earlier intervention in patients who would previously have been observed until complications developed. However, access to these innovations remains highly variable worldwide, and cost considerations may limit their impact in many health care systems.

The study also highlights important genotype-specific considerations. Although patients with α-thalassemia generally experienced fewer complications than those with β-thalassemia or hemoglobin E/β-thalassemia in the study, disease burden was far from negligible, particularly among nondeletional forms. Additional natural history studies in α-thalassemia remain needed to better define risk stratification and optimal intervention thresholds.11

Another particularly noteworthy observation involves patients with heterozygous β-thalassemia mutations who nonetheless developed clinically significant disease. These cases illustrate an increasingly recognized challenge in modern thalassemia practice. The presence of severe anemia, iron overload, or ineffective erythropoiesis in an apparent carrier should prompt evaluation for modifying genetic factors, including mutations affecting the quantity of α-globin product, nonglobin mutations affecting erythropoiesis, membranopathies, enzymopathies, and erythrocyte-independent modifiers of complications relating to β-thalassemia. Failure to recognize these complex genotypes may lead to years of underdiagnosis and undertreatment.

Ultimately, the most important message from this study extends beyond the patients themselves. It is a message for health care providers. Many individuals with NTDT are managed by primary care physicians, pediatricians, general hematologists, internists, or other specialists who may encounter only a handful of such patients during their careers. Educational efforts must therefore focus on establishing clear referral triggers and increasing awareness of contemporary management principles. Persistent anemia, evidence of iron overload, splenectomy, extramedullary hematopoiesis, unexplained thrombosis, and cardiopulmonary complications should all serve as red flags warranting specialist evaluation.

The lesson from Cheng et al is clear: the greatest threat in NTDT may no longer be the absence of available therapies but, rather, delayed recognition of patients who need them. By identifying disease progression earlier and intervening before complications become irreversible, we now have an opportunity to fundamentally alter the natural history of NTDT. The challenge ahead is ensuring that this knowledge reaches every clinician caring for these patients.
