Tocilizumab Biosimilars versus Reference Tocilizumab in Rheumatoid Arthritis: A Meta-Analysis of Pharmacokinetic Biosimilarity, Efficacy, Safety, and Immunogenicity
Authors: Young Ho Lee, Gwan Gyu Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe aim of the study was to systematically evaluate the efficacy, safety, immunogenicity, and pharmacokinetics (PK) of tocilizumab biosimilars compared to reference tocilizumab (r-TCZ) in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and healthy volunteers.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane CENTRAL for phase I and III randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing tocilizumab biosimilars vs. r-TCZ. Primary outcomes included change from baseline in DAS28-ESR, ACR20/50/70 response rates, adverse events (AEs), serious AEs (SAEs), anti-drug antibody (ADA) positivity, and PK parameters (AUC0−inf, Cmax, and AUC0−last). Random-effects meta-analyses were performed using mean difference (MD), risk ratio (RR), and geometric mean ratio (GMR) with 95% or 90% confidence intervals (CIs). This systematic review and meta-analysis have been registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251270353).ResultsEight studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising 3 phase III RCTs (n = 1,696 RA patients) and 5 phase I RCTs (n = 1,363 healthy volunteers), for a total of 3,059 participants. In RA patients, biosimilars demonstrated equivalent efficacy to r-TCZ at week 24, with no significant differences in DAS28-ESR change (MD −0.06; 95% CI: −0.19 to 0.06; I2 = 0%) or ACR20 response rates (RR 1.03; 95% CI: 0.96–1.10; I2 = 0). Results for ACR50 and ACR70 were similarly balanced. Safety profiles were comparable (any AE: RR 1.01; 95% CI: 0.94–1.08), as was immunogenicity (ADA positivity: RR 1.28; 95% CI: 0.99–1.65). In healthy volunteers, PK biosimilarity was confirmed across intravenous and subcutaneous routes with pooled GMRs (90% CI) of 104.5% (101.2–107.9%) for AUC0−inf, 101.4% (98.5–104.4%) for Cmax, and 102.3% (97.8–106.9%) for AUC0−last; all pooled CIs lay entirely within the 80–125% regulatory acceptance range (I2 ≤ 35).ConclusionTocilizumab biosimilars exhibit therapeutic similarity in RA patients and pharmacokinetic biosimilarity in healthy volunteers compared to r-TCZ, with comparable safety and immunogenicity, supporting their use as credible, cost-effective alternatives in clinical practice.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by synovial inflammation, joint destruction, and systemic complications, affecting about 0.5–1% of the world’s population [1, 2]. The proinflammatory cytokine interleukin-6 (IL-6) is central to RA pathogenesis, driving immune activation, synovial inflammation, and bone erosion [3, 4]. Tocilizumab, a recombinant humanized monoclonal antibody that targets both soluble and membrane-bound IL-6 receptors, serves as a key biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) for moderate-to-severe RA, with proven long-term efficacy and safety across multiple conditions, including RA, giant cell arteritis, and systemic sclerosis-associated interstitial lung disease [5, 6]. Tocilizumab has received approval from major regulatory agencies such as the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and is administered via intravenous infusion (IV; 8 mg/kg every 4 weeks) or subcutaneous injection (SC; 162 mg weekly or every 2 weeks) [7]. It is typically prescribed in combination with methotrexate for patients who have an inadequate response to conventional synthetic DMARDs [8, 9].

Although tocilizumab is effective, its high cost restricts availability, particularly in settings with limited resources, thereby worsening health disparities [10]. Biosimilars – products highly similar to approved biologics with no clinically significant differences in efficacy, safety, or immunogenicity – provide a cost-efficient alternative and may reduce economic barriers while increasing patient access [10]. Regulatory agencies require robust evidence of biosimilar comparability in pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics, efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity, which must be established through a comprehensive clinical program typically encompassing phase I (pharmacokinetic), phase II (preliminary efficacy), and phase III (confirmatory efficacy and safety) randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [11]. For tocilizumab, biosimilar candidates such as CT-P47, MSB11456, and BAT1806/BI-IB800 have undergone comparative evaluation with reference tocilizumab (r-TCZ; EU-approved or US-licensed) in these trials, with phase I trials investigating PK in healthy volunteers and phase III trials assessing clinical outcomes in RA patients. Although regulatory approval has been granted for all included biosimilars, the objective of this work was not to reassess regulatory decisions, but to quantitatively synthesize publicly available randomized evidence across independent development programs. Compared to a narrative review, this meta-analysis offers enhanced methodological rigor. It provides pooled estimates with high precision, and facilitates the formal assessment of consistency across individual studies and biosimilar products. Additionally, it enables the performance of structured sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of the findings. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to consolidate findings from phase I and III RCTs that compare tocilizumab biosimilars against r-TCZ. No prior systematic review has jointly synthesized efficacy, safety, immunogenicity, and pharmacokinetic evidence across phase I and phase III randomized trials.

Methods

Protocol and Registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis have been registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251270353) and adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (online supplementary material; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552447 PRISMA Checklist) [12].

Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted from database inception to September 1, 2025, using PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH and Emtree terms) and free-text keywords related to the intervention, condition, and biosimilar concept without restricting study design at the search stage. The core search terms included: (“tocilizumab” OR “Actemra” OR “RoActemra”) AND (“biosimilar” OR “biosimilarity” OR “bioequivalent” OR “follow-on biologic” OR “CT-P47” OR “MSB11456” OR “BAT1806” OR “BI-IB800”) AND (“rheumatoid arthritis” OR “RA”). For pharmacokinetic-focused retrieval, additional terms were incorporated: (“pharmacokinetics” OR “AUC” OR “Cmax” OR “biosimilarity”). No filters for study design, language, or publication date were applied (online suppl. Appendix 1). The review was conducted by two independent reviewers (Y.H.L. and G.G.S.) who independently performed title/abstract screening, full-text eligibility assessment, and data extraction using Covidence software. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, consistent with established systematic review methodology.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were included if they met the following Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, Study design (PICOS) framework. Population: adults (aged ≥18 years) with moderate-to-severe RA defined by American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) criteria [13], or healthy adults for PK comparisons. Patients with RA were required for efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity endpoints, whereas healthy volunteers were allowed for PK assessments according to regulatory standards for biosimilar evaluation (e.g., FDA and EMA biosimilar guidance). Intervention: administration of any tocilizumab biosimilar (e.g., CT-P47, MSB11456, BAT1806/BI-IB800) via intravenous (IV) or subcutaneous (SC) routes at RA-approved dosages (such as 8 mg/kg IV every 4 weeks or 162 mg SC weekly/every 2 weeks). Comparator: use of reference tocilizumab (r-TCZ; EU-authorized or US-approved formulations).

We assessed efficacy outcomes (Disease Activity Score in 28 joints-erythrocyte sedimentation rate [DAS28-ESR]: Disease Activity Score in 28 joints using erythrocyte sedimentation rate, a composite measure of disease activity ranging from 0 to 10, ACR20/50/70 response rates: American College of Rheumatology improvement criteria representing ≥20%, ≥50%, and ≥70% improvement in specific clinical and laboratory parameters), as well as safety (adverse events [AEs], serious AEs [SAEs]), immunogenicity (anti-drug antibodies [ADAs]), and PK parameters (area under the curve [AUC0-inf], maximum concentration [Cmax], AUC0-last]) in both RA patients and healthy volunteers. For phase III trials involving patients with rheumatoid arthritis, the primary outcomes were defined as the change from baseline in DAS28-ESR and the ACR20/50/70 response rates at week 24. Secondary outcomes encompassed safety, measured through adverse events and serious adverse events, as well as immunogenicity, assessed via anti-drug antibodies between weeks 24 and 48. Safety outcomes were extracted at the longest available follow-up timepoint for each study: two studies reported safety at week 24 (primary efficacy timepoint) and one study reported extended safety data through week 48. Phase I (healthy volunteers): Primary: PK parameters (AUC0-inf, Cmax, AUC0-last). Safety: treatment-emergent adverse events and serious adverse events over the primary efficacy evaluation period. Immunogenicity: treatment-emergent ADA incidence up to Week 24, excluding baseline-positive subjects. The 24-week timepoint was specifically selected for the immunogenicity assessment for several reasons. This interval was consistently reported across all three phase III trials, which facilitated direct comparability. Furthermore, the majority of treatment-emergent anti-drug antibodies for tocilizumab typically manifest within the first 24 weeks of treatment, according to the drug’s established immunogenicity profile. Finally, employing the longest available follow-up timepoint would have likely introduced significant heterogeneity, as the maximum reporting durations varied across the included studies. PK: AUC and Cmax from phase I studies. Study design: RCTs, including phase I (for PK analysis) and phase III (for efficacy/safety/immunogenicity). Non-randomized, observational, and preclinical investigations were excluded. Studies restricted to device comparisons (e.g., auto-injector versus pre-filled syringe without biosimilar-reference efficacy comparison) were not eligible. There were no exclusions based on language, publication year, or geographic location. Where study populations overlapped, the analysis included only the most comprehensive report. Formal similarity margins (80–125%) were applied exclusively to pharmacokinetic outcomes, consistent with regulatory bioequivalence standards. For phase III clinical outcomes, individual trials were designed with trial-specific equivalence or non-inferiority margins that were heterogeneous across studies; therefore, pooled equivalence testing was not performed. Instead, consistency of effect estimates and confidence interval overlap were assessed to evaluate comparability of clinical outcomes between biosimilars and reference tocilizumab.

Data Extraction and Management

Two reviewers (Y.H.L. and G.G.S.) independently extracted data using a standardized, pilot-tested form in Microsoft Excel. The extracted data included study characteristics (such as author, year, design, duration, and funding), participant demographics (including age, sex, disease duration, baseline DAS28-ESR), intervention specifics (biosimilar agent, dosage, administration route, concomitant medications such as methotrexate), outcomes (means/SDs or proportions for efficacy/PK; adverse event rates for safety/immunogenicity), and statistical data (including 95% CIs for mean differences [MDs], risk ratios [RRs], or geometric mean ratios [GMRs] for PK similarity). For PK analyses, GMRs and 90% CIs were given priority; if not reported, means/SDs were used to estimate GMRs assuming a log-normal distribution. Missing data (such as SDs) were imputed using established approaches (e.g., from CIs or p values) [14]. We extracted the number of participants analyzed for each outcome. Data were extracted from the primary analyses as reported in each publication (predominantly ITT for efficacy, safety set for safety outcomes). For PK, geometric means are the gold standard for PK parameters. We prioritized extraction of GMRs and 90% CIs when reported; when only arithmetic means/SDs were available, we estimated GMRs assuming log-normal distribution [15]. For efficacy, data were extracted from the primary analysis (ITT or full analysis set), consistent with regulatory and trial reporting standards. When multiple analyses were available, we selected methodologically comparable analyses across studies (same timepoint, similar imputation rules). Per-protocol analyses were reviewed but not pooled due to inconsistent reporting.

Efficacy endpoints were extracted exclusively from primary phase III randomized controlled trials in patients with rheumatoid arthritis. To ensure comparability, ACR20/50/70 outcomes were consistently extracted at Week 24, using intention-to-treat or full analysis sets, with conservative imputation strategies (e.g., non-responder imputation or equivalent) applied across studies. Only the main efficacy publications for each biosimilar were included. Safety endpoints were derived from the same phase III trials and were based on the safety analysis set (all patients receiving ≥1 dose), with treatment-emergent adverse events assessed over exposure periods aligned with the primary efficacy evaluation window. Immunogenicity outcomes were harmonized using treatment-emergent ADA incidence, excluding baseline-positive subjects, and were extracted up to Week 24 in all phase III trials. Although assay methodologies differed in technical aspects, all studies employed validated, multi-tiered immunogenicity assays consistent with regulatory guidance, supporting comparability at the level of ADA incidence. We have clarified that the width of CIs is primarily influenced by sample size and number of events, rather than ADA incidence alone. For pharmacokinetic outcomes, five eligible phase I randomized studies were included. The higher number of biosimilar-treated subjects reflects the design of biosimilar PK trials, which often include a single biosimilar arm and two reference product arms (e.g., EU- and US-sourced originators). Phase I biosimilar trials often employ a three-arm design comparing one biosimilar against two reference products (EU-approved and US-licensed tocilizumab separately) to address different regulatory jurisdictions. In such designs, the biosimilar group may have a similar or larger sample size than each individual reference arm, but when reference arms are pooled for analysis, the overall reference group may be larger. In our meta-analysis, we pooled all reference tocilizumab arms (regardless of source) for comparison against biosimilars, resulting in a total of X biosimilar-treated and Y reference-treated participants across all PK studies. PK analyses were conducted using biosimilar-to-reference geometric mean ratios (GMRs). Although IV and SC administrations yield different absolute PK profiles, all similarity comparisons were performed within the same route of administration, and pooled on the ratio scale, which is independent of absolute exposure and consistent with EMA/FDA biosimilarity guidance.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias (RoB) was evaluated using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool for RCTs [16]. The assessment covered domains such as the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and the selection of reported results. Each domain received a rating of low, some concerns, or high RoB, which together informed the overall RoB judgment. The GRADE approach was used to assess the certainty of evidence for critical outcomes [17].

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

RRs were selected because safety and immunogenicity outcomes were in binary proportions assessed over comparable exposure windows. RR provides an interpretable and regulator-familiar measure for comparing event risks between biosimilars and reference products. We selected RR using the random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird method) for the following reasons: RRs were selected as the primary metric for several reasons. Primarily, risk ratios offer superior clinical interpretability and provide a more intuitive measure of relative risk for clinicians compared to odds ratios (ORs). Furthermore, they represent the standard metric in biosimilar comparative trials and regulatory submissions. Given the relatively low event rates for adverse events and anti-drug antibodies in the included studies, the choice between ORs and RRs had minimal practical impact on the final conclusions. Additionally, the balanced treatment allocation in the included randomized controlled trials ensured that RRs and ORs remained largely convergent. The inclusion of studies was restricted to phase I and phase III randomized controlled trials. This decision was based on the fact that these phases represent the body of publicly available, peer-reviewed comparative data for tocilizumab biosimilars. Moreover, phase II data for biosimilars are frequently limited or integrated directly into phase III protocols, making them less suitable for standalone synthesis. Random-effects models provide more conservative (wider) confidence intervals, which is appropriate when synthesizing evidence across different biosimilar development programs. Random-effects models are commonly used in biosimilar meta-analyses to account for potential between-study variability. Our a priori choice of random-effects was methodologically sound regardless of the observed heterogeneity. When I2 = 0%, random-effects and fixed-effects models yield very similar point estimates and confidence intervals, so this choice does not materially affect our conclusions.

Meta-analyses were conducted separately for phase III (efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity in RA patients) and phase I (PK in healthy volunteers) studies. Phase II trials and pharmacodynamic (PD) assessments were excluded from the analysis for several reasons. Phase II trials for tocilizumab biosimilars were generally not available as standalone studies or were incorporated into phase III protocols. Additionally, pharmacodynamic endpoints were not reported with sufficient methodological uniformity across different development programs to permit a robust pooled analysis. Furthermore, the focus on phase I pharmacokinetic and phase III clinical data aligns with core regulatory requirements for establishing biosimilarity and represents the most robust evidence currently available. For efficacy outcomes, MD was used for continuous data (e.g., DAS28-ESR change), whereas RR was applied to binary outcomes (e.g., ACR20/50/70 rates). Safety and immunogenicity analyses utilized RR for proportions (e.g., AEs, ADAs). For PK assessments, GMR was employed for AUC0-inf, Cmax, and AUC0-last (data log-transformed and subsequently back-transformed for reporting with 90% CIs). The random-effects model using the DerSimonian-Laird estimator was selected to address anticipated heterogeneity [18]. Heterogeneity was evaluated with the I2 statistic (thresholds: low < 25%, moderate 25–50%, high > 50%) and the χ2 test (significance set at p < 0.10) [19, 20]. Subgroup analyses were performed by stratifying phase I pharmacokinetic studies according to the route of administration, specifically comparing intravenous and subcutaneous delivery. For phase III efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity data, a descriptive examination was conducted for each individual biosimilar product. No cross-population comparisons (RA patients vs. healthy volunteers) were performed as these would not be methodologically appropriate. Forest plots were used for visual representation of results. Analyses were carried out using Review Manager (RevMan) 5.4 and R (version 4.3.0; meta and metafor packages). Statistical heterogeneity assessment included I2, τ2, and Cochran’s Q test [21]. For non-similarity outcomes, p values <0.05 were regarded as statistically significant, whereas PK analyses relied on similarity margins for interpretation. p values were not used to test superiority or difference but only reported where provided in original studies. The interpretation emphasizes CIs and similarity ranges, consistent with biosimilarity principles.

Results

Study Selection

In accordance with PRISMA guidelines, comprehensive searches of PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane CENTRAL yielded 1,123 total records. After the screening process and full-text review, 60 articles were evaluated for eligibility. Ultimately, 8 studies met the inclusion criteria for the meta-analysis: three phase III RCTs (N = 1,696 RA patients) examining efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity, and five phase I RCTs (N = 1,363 healthy volunteers) evaluating pharmacokinetics (PK) [22–29] (Table 1, Fig. 1). According to the Cochrane RoB 2 tool, all studies demonstrated low risk of bias (online suppl. Table S1). The evidence supports high certainty of therapeutic similarity for efficacy and moderate-to-high certainty for safety and pharmacokinetic biosimilarity, with low certainty for immunogenicity due to heterogeneity and imprecision (online suppl. Tables S2, S3, S4, and S5).

Study Characteristics

The three phase III RCTs evaluated tocilizumab biosimilars (CT-P47, MSB11456, and BAT1806/BI-IB800) versus reference tocilizumab (r-TCZ) in adults with moderate-to-severe rheumatoid arthritis (RA) (DAS28-ESR >3.2) and an inadequate response to methotrexate. A total of 1,696 patients were enrolled (Leng et al. [29]: N = 621; Smolen et al. [22]: N = 471; Zubrzycka et al. [23]: N = 604). Baseline characteristics were well balanced between biosimilar and reference arms within each trial. Patients had mean ages of 50.5–52.2 years, were predominantly female (80.7–86%), and exhibited high disease activity at baseline (mean DAS28-ESR 6.27–6.64). Mean disease duration ranged from 7.8 to 7.9 years in the two studies reporting this variable, and prior biologic exposure varied from 8.9% to 30.8%. Pharmacokinetic biosimilarity was evaluated for CT-P47, MSB11456, and BAT1806 in five phase I RCTs versus EU- and/or US-licensed r-TCZ in healthy adults using single-dose intravenous (8 mg/kg) or subcutaneous (162 mg) administration. A total of 1,363 healthy volunteers participated. Demographics reflected the geographic locations of the studies: European cohorts were older (mean age ∼51 years) and predominantly White, whereas Asian cohorts (Korea, China, Japan) were younger (mean age 26–36 years) and exclusively Asian, with lower body weight and BMI. Gender distribution varied (0–48% female).

Efficacy Outcomes (Phase III RCTs, RA Patients)

Meta-analyses of the three phase III randomized controlled trials (total N = 1,696 patients with moderate-to-severe RA; 848 assigned to tocilizumab biosimilar and 848 to reference tocilizumab) demonstrated therapeutic similarity at week 24 (Table 2). The pooled mean difference in change from baseline in DAS28-ESR was −0.06 (95% CI: −0.19 to 0.06; I2 = 0%; p = 0.21), confirming comparable improvement in disease activity (Fig. 2). The pooled results for the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria were as follows: ACR20 – the response rate was 68.7% (95% CI: 65.4%–71.8%) for biosimilars compared to 66.6% (95% CI: 63.3%–69.7%) for r-TCZ, yielding a pooled risk ratio (RR) of 1.03 (95% CI: 0.96–1.10; I2 = 0%; p = 0.33); ACR50 – biosimilars showed a response rate of 47.7% (95% CI: 44.3%–51.1%) versus 46.0% (95% CI: 42.6%–49.4%) for r-TCZ (RR, 1.04; 95% CI: 0.94–1.15; I2 = 0%; p = 0.46); ACR70 – the response rate was 27.0% (95% CI: 23.9%–30.3%) in the biosimilar group and 26.1% (95% CI: 23.0%–29.3%) in the r-TCZ group (RR, 1.03; 95% CI: 0.88–1.21; I2 = 0%; p = 0.67). No statistically detectable heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%); however, given the small number of included studies, this should be interpreted cautiously as the power to detect heterogeneity was limited.

Safety Outcomes (Phase III RCTs, RA Patients)

Safety and immunogenicity profiles were comparable between biosimilars and reference tocilizumab across all eight studies (N = 2,346). The incidence of any adverse event was 71.9% in the biosimilar groups versus 71.3% in the reference groups (RR 1.01; 95% CI: 0.94–1.08; I2 = 41%; p = 0.80) (Table 2). Serious adverse events occurred in 3.6% versus 3.8% of participants (RR 0.95; 95% CI: 0.70–1.30; I2 = 0%; p = 0.73).

Immunogenicity Outcomes (Phase III RCTs, RA Patients)

Anti-drug antibody positivity was low and not significantly different (7.8% vs. 5.9%; RR 1.32; 95% CI: 0.89–1.96; I2 = 60%; p = 0.16) (Table 2; Fig. 2). The moderate heterogeneity in immunogenicity was primarily attributable to higher detection rates in phase I studies using sensitive assays in healthy volunteers.

Pharmacokinetic Outcomes (Phase I RCTs, Healthy Volunteers)

Pharmacokinetic biosimilarity was evaluated in five phase I randomized controlled trials in healthy volunteers (total N = 1,363; 526 biosimilar, 837 reference tocilizumab). All individual studies demonstrated that the 90% CIs for the GMRs of the primary endpoints (AUC0-inf/∞, AUC0-last/t, and Cmax) were fully contained within the prespecified equivalence margin of 80.00–125.00% for both intravenous and subcutaneous routes (Table 3). Pooled meta-analysis confirmed pharmacokinetic similarity (Table 3; Fig. 3): AUC0-inf/∞: GMR 104.5% (90% CI: 101.2–107.9%; I2 = 0%); Cmax: GMR 101.4% (90% CI: 98.5–104.4%; I2 = 0%); AUC0-last/t: GMR 102.3% (90% CI: 97.8–106.9%; I2 = 0%). The 90% CIs of all pooled estimates lay entirely within the 80–125% regulatory acceptance range, with no evidence of heterogeneity across studies or routes of administration. These results provide robust evidence of pharmacokinetic similarity between the tocilizumab biosimilars (CT-P47, MSB11456, and BAT1806) and reference tocilizumab. Formal equivalence margins were applied only to PK outcomes because regulatory bioequivalence criteria (80–125% for GMRs) are predefined and universally accepted for phase I studies. In contrast, the included phase III trials were designed primarily as similarity or non-inferiority trials at the individual study level, but with heterogeneous margins and endpoints.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

Analyses were stratified a priori by study phase and study population, in accordance with regulatory principles for biosimilar development (online suppl. Tables S6, 7; Fig S1, S2). Specifically, phase III randomized controlled trials conducted in patients with RA were analyzed exclusively for efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity outcomes, whereas phase I randomized controlled trials conducted in healthy volunteers were analyzed exclusively for PK outcomes. Consequently, no statistical subgroup comparisons across different study populations (RA patients versus healthy volunteers) were performed as such comparisons are neither methodologically appropriate nor clinically meaningful.

For efficacy outcomes, each included biosimilar (CT-P47, MSB11456, and BAT1806/BI-IB800) was represented by a single phase III trial. As a result, formal subgroup analyses by biosimilar product were not statistically feasible. Instead, consistent direction and magnitude of effect across individual biosimilar products, with no detectable statistical heterogeneity were assessed descriptively by examining the direction, magnitude, and CIs of treatment effects. All biosimilars demonstrated effect estimates centered around similarity with reference tocilizumab, with overlapping CIs and no detectable between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), indicating a high degree of concordance across trials.

For PK outcomes, subgroup analyses were conducted on the basis of route of administration (intravenous vs. subcutaneous) using biosimilar-to-reference GMRs for AUC and Cmax. Although absolute PK profiles differ between IV and SC administration, similarity assessments were performed within the same route of administration in each study, and pooled estimates were derived using GMRs, which are independent of absolute exposure levels. This approach is consistent with regulatory guidance for biosimilar evaluation and allows aggregation of biosimilarity evidence across studies while preserving route-specific comparability. No meaningful heterogeneity was observed across PK analyses (I2 = 0%). Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding individual studies to evaluate the influence of any single trial on the pooled estimates. These analyses did not materially alter the results for efficacy, safety, immunogenicity, or PK outcomes, supporting the robustness of the findings (online suppl. Table S6). No imputation was performed for efficacy, safety, or immunogenicity outcomes, in line with regulatory guidance for biosimilar trials. Imputation was limited to selected PK parameters (AUC and Cmax) when summary statistics required for meta-analysis were not explicitly reported. All PK meta-analyses were repeated excluding imputed values; results remained directionally identical, with no material impact on pooled GMRs or conclusions of biosimilarity (online suppl. Table S7). Formal publication bias assessments (funnel plots, Egger’s test) were not performed when fewer than 10 studies were available.

Certainty of Evidence (GRADE Assessment)

In accordance with the GRADE framework, the overall certainty of evidence for therapeutic and pharmacokinetic similarity was assessed (Table 4). We found high-certainty evidence that tocilizumab biosimilars are equivalent to the reference product in terms of clinical efficacy (ACR20 and DAS28-ESR change) and pharmacokinetic parameters (AUC and Cmax), with all confidence intervals for these primary endpoints being narrow and well-contained within predefined similarity margins. Regarding safety, there was high-certainty evidence of comparable risks for treatment-emergent adverse events and serious adverse events. However, the certainty of evidence for immunogenicity was rated as low. This downgrading was due to imprecision (wide confidence intervals resulting from a low absolute number of ADA-positive events) and heterogeneity (I2 = 60%) likely stemming from variations in assay sensitivity across different development programs. Despite this, the low absolute incidence of ADAs across both groups suggests that the risk of immune-mediated loss of efficacy is minimal.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis shows that tocilizumab biosimilars (CT-P47, MSB11456, BAT1806/BI-IB800) are comparable to r-TCZ in efficacy, safety, immunogenicity, and PK. Among RA patients, there were no meaningful differences in efficacy outcomes at week 24. Safety outcomes were similar. Immunogenicity, assessed by ADA incidence, did not differ significantly, but the low event rates resulted in wide CIs. Among healthy volunteers, PK similarity was demonstrated, with all parameters remaining within the 80–125% margin for biosimilarity. These findings are consistent with regulatory requirements for biosimilars, which mandate the demonstration of no clinically meaningful differences compared to the reference product [30]. The observed low heterogeneity (I2 = 0% across all outcomes) further strengthens the reliability of results across various biosimilars, administration routes (IV and SC), and different populations, including RA patients and healthy volunteers [31]. Immunogenicity was comparable between biosimilars and the reference product, and despite the observed low ADA rates, there appears to be minimal risk of immune-mediated loss of efficacy, which is consistent with tocilizumab’s established low immunogenicity profile [32].

Pharmacokinetic studies were conducted in healthy volunteers, which may not fully reflect exposure profiles in patients with RA, where systemic inflammation, target-mediated drug disposition, and concomitant therapies can influence drug pharmacokinetics [33]. However, consistent with regulatory biosimilar development pathways, healthy-volunteer studies are considered the most sensitive setting for detecting relative pharmacokinetic differences between biosimilars and the reference product [34], while phase III trials in RA patients support clinical extrapolation. In addition, one pharmacokinetic study was conducted predominantly in an Asian population, which may limit generalizability to ethnically diverse RA populations. Although biosimilar evaluations rely on within-study biosimilar-to-reference comparisons that mitigate population-related confounding, differences in body weight and genetic background may influence absolute exposure, underscoring the importance of post-marketing and real-world data.

This study represents the first pooled analysis of multiple tocilizumab biosimilars, offering a comprehensive synthesis of efficacy, safety, immunogenicity, and PK data. The random-effects model used appropriately addressed potential heterogeneity, though low I2 values indicate strong result consistency. By including both phase I and III RCTs, the analysis facilitated an integrated assessment of biosimilarity that spans from early PK parameters to clinical outcomes in RA patients. The reliability of the findings is further reinforced by adherence to PRISMA guidelines and consistently low risk of bias, as evaluated using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool.

This systematic review and meta-analysis summarizes randomized evidence comparing tocilizumab biosimilars with r-TCZ across pharmacokinetic, efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity outcomes. The findings are interpreted in the context of the publicly available peer-reviewed literature, acknowledging that regulatory approvals are based on a broader totality-of-evidence framework that may include additional studies not captured in this review. We distinguished our findings of 'therapeutic similarity' from formal regulatory determinations of bioequivalence, or interchangeability [35]. Accordingly, conclusions are framed in terms of overall consistency with biosimilarity rather than completeness of regulatory evidence. Immunogenicity analyses showed comparable rates of treatment-emergent ADAs between biosimilars and r-TCZ. Wider CIs observed for immunogenicity outcomes primarily reflect the limited number of events and sample size, which is typical for rare-event analyses, rather than low ADA incidence alone. Random-effects models were applied throughout to account for potential clinical and methodological differences across studies. Although statistical heterogeneity was low, trials differed by biosimilar product and design features; under such conditions, random- and fixed-effect models yield similar estimates, and the random-effects approach was retained as a conservative and widely accepted choice. Phase I and phase III studies were analyzed in separate meta-analyses because they address distinct objectives and populations. Phase I trials inform pharmacokinetic similarity, whereas phase III trials evaluate confirmatory efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity in patients with rheumatoid arthritis. While each study evaluated a different biosimilar, meta-analysis provided value beyond narrative synthesis by enabling quantitative assessment of consistency, estimation of pooled effects with precision, and sensitivity analyses to assess robustness. In this context, the analysis was intended to assess alignment with reference tocilizumab across independent development programs, rather than to compare biosimilars with each other [36].

Demonstrating similarity between tocilizumab biosimilars and r-TCZ provides strong rationale for their use as cost-effective treatment options in the management of RA, potentially reducing economic barriers and enhancing patient access across healthcare systems. Clinicians may confidently prescribe biosimilars such as CT-P47, MSB11456, or BAT1806/BI-IB800, assured that efficacy and safety standards are not compromised. The demonstration of similar PK profiles in healthy volunteers further supports regulatory confidence in biosimilarity at the molecular level and facilitates streamlined regulatory approval. The included phase III trials were designed as similarity or non-inferiority trials, each with prespecified margins defined by their respective protocols. However, these margins were not uniform across studies, precluding a single pooled similarity margin for clinical outcomes [37]. We now clarify that the meta-analysis assesses consistency of effect estimates and CI overlap, rather than formally testing pooled similarity.

This meta-analysis included only one pivotal phase III trial per biosimilar product, focusing on three tocilizumab biosimilars (CT-P47, MSB11456, and BAT1806/BI-IB800) that have received regulatory approval from the US FDA and/or EMA. The findings of this study may not be generalizable to other tocilizumab biosimilars approved in different jurisdictions or currently under development. Furthermore, the results may not necessarily apply to biosimilars utilizing different manufacturing processes or formulations, nor to real-world patient populations that may diverge significantly from the participants selected for the included randomized controlled trials. Clinicians and policymakers should base decisions on the specific biosimilar products evaluated in this review and consult additional evidence for other biosimilar candidates.

This systematic review evaluated comparative pharmacokinetics, efficacy, safety, and immunogenicity between tocilizumab biosimilars and reference tocilizumab. Switching studies (transitioning patients from reference to biosimilar) were not included in this analysis and represent an important area for future research and real-world evidence generation. Interchangeability is a distinct regulatory designation that was not assessed in this meta-analysis.

Several limitations merit discussion. First, the absence of ACR50/70 data for BAT1806/BI-IB800 at week 24 restricted these meta-analyses to two studies, likely impacting the precision of effect estimates. Second, the low frequency of ADAs led to wider CIs, reflecting event sparsity and subsequently hindering the statistical power for immunogenicity comparisons. Third, phase I PK investigations enrolled healthy volunteers, with one study focusing on mainly Asian participants, which may not accurately capture the PK profile in RA patients who may exhibit disease-related physiological variations such as altered clearance due to inflammation [38]. Fourth, we selected 24 weeks for immunogenicity assessment. However, longer term immunogenicity data may provide additional insights [39]. Fifth, although two independent reviewers performed all stages of the review process using Covidence software, systematic reviews ideally benefit from involvement of a larger team to further reduce the risk of reviewer bias and error at each stage. While the two-reviewer approach employed here is consistent with established systematic review methodology and widely adopted in the literature, we acknowledge that the absence of a third independent reviewer or a broader team may limit the robustness of certain steps, particularly study selection and data extraction. This should be considered when interpreting the findings of this review. Lastly, the inability to include unpublished or ongoing trial data (such as results from additional phase III studies) introduces potential risk of publication bias.

In summary, tocilizumab biosimilars have demonstrated comparable efficacy, safety, immunogenicity, and PK relative to r-TCZ across phase I and III RCTs. These results support the utility of biosimilars as credible alternatives, potentially broadening patient access to IL-6 inhibitor treatment for those with RA worldwide. Ongoing research and more real-world evidence will be indispensable in reinforcing their role in standard care.
