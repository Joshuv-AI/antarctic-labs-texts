Survival analysis of post-war mortality in conflict-affected communities of Laelay Maichew Woreda, Tigray Region, Ethiopia
Authors: Yemane Asmelash Gebretensae, Yemane Hailu Fissuh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Examines post-conflict mortality using advanced survival analysis.•Identifies sociodemographic predictors of mortality risk.•Provides empirical evidence from vulnerable populations in a post-war context.•Utilizes robust statistical modeling of real-world health data.•Informs targeted public health interventions and policy decisions.

Introduction

War and military conflicts have far-reaching consequences for human populations, civilizations, and economies. These conflicts frequently render affected people more vulnerable, increasing mortality and morbidity during and after wars [1].

The armed conflict in Ethiopia's Tigray region, which began in November 2020, resulted in significant destruction of health care infrastructure, disruption of vital services, and a humanitarian crisis. The World Health Organization reported that only 3% of health facilities in Tigray were functioning during the conflict, while the availability of medicines, fuel, electricity, and medical supplies was severely limited [2]. Maternal care, immunization programs, and management of chronic diseases were substantially disrupted, making the population more susceptible to preventable illnesses and death.

Furthermore, the armed conflicts caused large population displacements, food insecurity, economic crises, and social instability in the region. The World Food Programme stated that 83% of people in Tigray faced food insecurity, with millions needing urgent relief [3]. Displacement, lack of nutrition, poor sanitation, and interruption of livelihoods all increased the mortality risk among vulnerable groups, including children, the elderly, pregnant women, and individuals with chronic illnesses.

However, limited evidence exists on post-conflict mortality trends in these populations. Therefore, post-war mortality analysis is crucial for understanding the long-term health effects of conflict, targeting the most vulnerable populations, and prioritizing recovery efforts. Survival analysis is an important statistical method for examining the determinants that influence population outcomes in post-conflict settings, where there are health inequities and limited resources [4].

In the war-impacted areas of Tigray, the Laelay Maichew Woreda is one of several areas used as a case study to analyze the survival factors among affected vulnerable populations. Survival analysis insights are critical for devising interventions that address both acute dangers (combat injuries) and long-term difficulties (lack of access to health care and disease epidemics). Existing vulnerabilities are frequently made worse by the aftermath of war, which exposes populations to increased risks of combat injuries, a shortage of medical services, and infectious disease epidemics [5]. In these situations, a complicated interaction of socioeconomic, demographic, and health-related factors affects survival outcomes. A framework for separating these factors by determining the most urgent problems for focused actions is provided by survival analysis.

Post-conflict mortality studies are critical for understanding the health consequences of armed conflict, especially among vulnerable populations such as children and disadvantaged groups. Violence-related deaths are considered direct mortality, whereas indirect mortality is caused by a collapsed healthcare system and an increased prevalence of illness [6,7].

Previous studies have shown ample evidence of gender differences in survival outcomes. Research indicates that, because of biological and behavioral differences, such as hormonal effects and healthcare-seeking behaviors, females often have higher survival odds than males [8]. However, cultural, social, and economic factors may alter these trends in countries afflicted by violence [9]. To guide equitable policy initiatives, a thorough investigation of the relationship between gender and healthcare access is necessary.

The root causes of mortality in conflict and post-conflict settings are complex and multifactorial, ranging from gender to violence, disease epidemics, and poor access to healthcare. For instance, direct violence may lead to significant numbers of early deaths, while the indirect aspects of conflict, namely infectious disease epidemics and the collapse of healthcare infrastructure, tend to cause delayed excess mortality [10].

Similarly, armed conflict also has negative effects on population health, as evidenced by previous studies showing that conflict causes severe damage to infrastructure, deterioration of services, and greater exposure to injuries and communicable diseases. Apart from direct deaths, there is a considerably increased risk of indirect mortality from maternal, neonatal, nutritional, and chronic diseases, especially in children below five years of age [11].

Furthermore, estimating mortality in conflict settings has proved challenging because of compromised civil registration systems and constraints on access to affected populations. Consequently, alternative methods, such as key informant interviews and household surveys, have been utilized to estimate mortality among displaced populations. Nonetheless, these proxies also need to be refined to improve their validity and timeliness [12]. Overall, the existing literature documents several different disease categories and estimates millions of civilian deaths worldwide [11].

Additionally, in settings like northeastern Nigeria, where armed conflicts and population displacements have continued for years, alternative estimation approaches have been used to estimate the number of excess deaths, emphasizing the serious public health impacts of prolonged insecurity and the urgent need for accurate mortality estimates in such settings [13].

Before the Tigray war broke out in November 2020, Tigray had a fairly well-functioning healthcare system with health clinics, hospitals, health posts, and community-based health services, which provided maternal, child, and preventive healthcare [14].

The Tigray war (2020-2022 G.C.) had devastating effects on health, food insecurity, and livelihood systems in Tigray, particularly in Laelay Maichew Woreda and elsewhere: health facilities were destroyed, essential medicines were unavailable, and maternal and child health services became difficult to access [14]. The war caused several crises, including food insecurity through the destruction of food production and markets, mass population displacement, and asset depletion [15,16], which eventually posed long-term threats to health and survival.

Overall, the purpose of this study was to assess survival outcomes and their determinants, with a particular emphasis on gender disparities, causes of death (conflict-related injuries, disease outbreaks, and healthcare shortages), and socioeconomic and health-related factors.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Laelay Maichew Woreda, a district in the Central Zone of the Tigray Regional State, northern Ethiopia, about 250 kilometers northwest of Mekelle. The woreda consists of 16 rural tabias (kebeles) and is mainly engaged in a mixed crop-livestock farming system [17].

Study population

The study population comprised civilians living in the 16 tabias of Laelay Maichew Woreda who were affected by the conflict between November 2020 and March 2024. The inclusion criteria comprised all identified conflict-affected civilian individuals in Laelay Maichew Woreda during the study period, while individuals with incomplete or missing survival information and combatants were excluded. In total, 303 participants were eligible for inclusion through complete enumeration of readily available cases from hospital records, community reports, and records of district administration and bureaus during the conflict period. The inclusion of all the districts (tabias’) provided comprehensive coverage of conflict-affected populations.

Study design

A retrospective mixed-methods research design was used for the study to assess mortality and its determinants among conflict-affected communities of Laelay Maichew Woreda, Central Zone, Tigray, Ethiopia, from November 2020 to March 2024. The quantitative component used survival analysis to assess mortality trends and determinants and descriptive analysis to describe the socioeconomic and demographic characteristics of the study population. The qualitative component provided supportive contextual information.

Data collection and validation procedures

Data were drawn from a variety of sources, such as hospital data records, structured interviews of households, and interviews with local government officials. A mortality survey was conducted at the household level to assess the role of socioeconomic, environmental, demographic, and health factors in survival. Other secondary data sources from Laelay Maichew Woreda and the Central Zone of the Tigray Administration Office were used to corroborate and validate the primary mortality survey data. Data from various primary and secondary sources were cross-checked to eliminate inconsistencies and provide more accurate, validated data through triangulation methods. The authors had access to personally identifiable data during the initial data collection from hospital records and household interviews, but the data were de-identified before analysis.

Variables of the study

Dependent variable

The outcome variable was the survival time to death (death = 1, censored = 0), in weeks, from the start of the war (November 2020) to the date of death. Subjects who failed to experience the event by the end of the observation period (March 2024) or who were lost to follow-up were considered censored events. (Censored cases included patients who were alive at the end of the observation period or who survived war-related injuries or disease outbreaks).

Independent variables

The factors studied included comprehensive variables that could possibly contribute to post-war mortality, including demographic characteristics (age and gender of the victims), socioeconomic status (education level, monthly income, and employment status of the victims), health indicators (pre-existing health conditions, type of health condition [acute or chronic], and access to health facilities before and during the war), and conflict- and environment-related factors (displacement, cause of death, and access to food supply during times of hardship).

Survival analysis

Survival analysis is a statistical technique used to study the length of time until the occurrence of an event of interest while accounting for censored observations [18]. In this study, survival models were used to analyze the impact of socioeconomic, demographic, and health variables on survival using a hazard-based approach.

The Cox proportional hazards (PH) regression model

The Cox PH regression model was first used to identify factors related to death [19]. The model associates the hazard function with a set of covariates as follows:(1)h(t,X,β)=h0(t)eβ′x……where: h(t,X)is the hazard function at time t for an individual with covariates(X); h0(t) is the baseline hazard function; β represents a vector of regression parameters; and exp(β′x) describes the multiplicative effect of the covariates on the hazard. The variable t is the failure time.

Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to quantify the effects of covariates. The variable (t)denotes the survival (failure) time.

Model diagnostics and assumption checking

The PH assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals and graphical approaches based on log-log survival plots [20]. Variables that violated the PH assumption were examined during model selection. Cox-Snell residuals were used to assess model adequacy, with a 45° line through the origin representing a good fit. When the PH assumption failed, stratified models were investigated. Both the PH and accelerated failure time (AFT) models were evaluated [21].

Parametric proportional hazards model (PPH)

To find the best-fitting model for the survival data, PPH and AFT models were fitted and compared [22].

The Weibull PH model assumes the following hazard function:(2)h0(t)=λγ(t)γ−1

Here, λ represents the baseline hazard rate and γ is the shape parameter. Therefore, the hazard increases if γ>1, remains constant if γ = 1, and decreases if γ < 1.

The Gompertz model assumes:(3)h(t|X)=λe(γt)exp(Xβ)where λ and γ denote the scale and shape parameters, respectively.

In addition, log-normal and log-logistic AFT models were used. In these models, covariates have a multiplicative effect on the survival time rather than on the hazard function.

Similarly, the overall health status of individuals was predictive of survival. Compared with participants reporting good health status, those who rated their overall health as bad had a 2.36 times higher hazard of death than participants who reported good overall health (HR=2.36,p=0.003). Those who rated their overall health as fair had a higher hazard of death than those who rated their overall health as good, but this was not statistically significant (HR=1.36,p=0.181).

We also found a significant interaction between chronic conditions and general health status. There was a 72% increase in the hazard of death for the interaction between chronic condition and poor general health (HR=1.72,p=0.013). There was also a 29.3% increase in the hazard of death for the interaction between chronic condition and fair general health (HR=1.293,p=0.031).

Moreover, the cause of death was significantly predictive of survival. Compared with the reference category, deaths due to disease outbreaks were significantly predictive of a lower hazard of death (HR=0.52,p=0.020), as were deaths due to shortages of medicine and health services (HR=0.45,p=0.005).

Overall, age, sex, health status, the interaction between chronic conditions and overall health status, and the underlying cause of death were identified as significant causes of mortality risk in the study population.

Again, deaths due to disease outbreaks (HR=0.54,p=0.027) and shortages of medicines and health facilities (HR=0.51,p=0.015) were associated with significantly lower hazards of death than deaths due to direct combat, thus demonstrating reduced hazard by non-combat measures. Males had an HR of 1.682, suggesting that males experienced a 68.2% higher hazard of death compared with females.

In addition, compared with having no healthcare access during the war period, having healthcare access was significantly inversely associated with the hazard of death (HR=0.49,p=0.025). This suggests that having access to healthcare is associated with a reduced risk of death.

For those with an acute health condition during wartime, there was a significantly higher hazard of death of 1.56 (p=0.018) than for those without an acute health condition. This translates to a 56% increased risk of mortality than that in the reference group.

A statistically significant interaction was found between chronic health status and general health status. Compared with individuals who had no chronic health condition and reported good health, those who had a chronic health condition and reported poor health had a 3.28 times higher hazard of death (HR=3.28,p=0.002). This indicates that a poor perception of health substantially worsens the impact of chronic disease on survival.

Finally, the estimated Gompertz shape parameter was significant (γ=0.085,p<0.001), showing a rising hazard of death over time. Overall, the model showed that being a child or young adult, having an acute health condition, and the interaction between a chronic health condition and poor health were associated with an increased probability of death.

Death associated with access to healthcare, a chronic health condition prior to the war, and non-combat causes of death (disease outbreaks and shortages of medicine supplies and health facilities) was associated with a decreased probability of death compared with death from direct combat. These findings highlight critical areas for intervention by emphasizing the importance of healthcare accessibility and addressing gender disparities and acute health conditions.

Mortality associated with access to health, premorbid chronic disease, and indirect mortality (such as disease epidemics and the shortage of medicine supplies and health facilities) was associated with a decreased risk of death compared with combat mortality. These findings highlight key areas for intervention, emphasizing the importance of access to healthcare and addressing gender differences and premorbid diseases.

Discussion

This study highlights key factors associated with mortality in a population affected by violence and adds to the evidence on factors associated with survival during war. The increased vulnerability of children and adolescents found in this research is consistent with other reports of increased mortality among children due to the implementation of war and its consequences, including the loss of essential services such as healthcare, food insecurity, forced displacement, and deteriorating living conditions [6].

Similarly, outbreaks of infectious disease and conflict- and combat-related causes of death in the study population emerged as significant prognostic factors. The findings of this research support the concept that both the direct impact of violence and the indirect effects resulting from war are detrimental to health. The breakdown of healthcare systems, a dearth of essential medicines, the collapse of promotive and preventive health services, and mass population displacement can all increase the risk of infectious diseases, and persistent violence itself can increase mortality. These findings are consistent with previous evidence of the disastrous impact of war on population health [10,23].

Males had a significantly increased hazard of death compared with females; these findings perhaps relate to increased exposure to conflict-related violence, the perception that male civilians are generally considered combatants and, as such, are at increased risk of conflict-related death and injury [8,24].

The association between socioeconomic status, as determined by household income, and mortality emphasizes the importance of the resilience of household resources in this population. Access to safe housing, transport, food, and healthcare can influence survival outcomes. Conversely, having access to healthcare was associated with a substantial reduction in the hazard of death, a proxy for functioning health systems in a conflict environment [12].

The association of acute health conditions with a higher hazard of death highlights the important role of timely surveillance, rapid response, and the availability and accessibility of emergency health services. This finding is consistent with previous evidence of the importance of functional healthcare systems in reducing preventable deaths in humanitarian crises [13], although the lack of statistical significance of the negative association between chronic health conditions and mortality suggests that further exploration of this prognostic factor may be warranted.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged when considering the results. Mortality estimates may have been influenced by the breakdown of reporting, surveillance, and health services during the war and may have been underreported. In addition, displacement, the exclusion of some non-household populations, recall bias, and incomplete records may have led to selection and information bias. To address these limitations, mortality data were triangulated with information obtained from surveys, hospital records, and local administrative sources.

Several survival models were tested to determine the most accurate fit and to assess the robustness of the findings. Goodness-of-fit criteria and diagnostics guided model selection. Since the sample was limited in size, some estimates may have been less precise, and this study should be validated in larger post-conflict populations.

These findings have serious implications for both the planning of post-conflict recovery and public health practice. The increased risk of death among children, males, those with poor health status, and those without access to health services points to the need for organized responses aimed at protecting vulnerable groups. These include strengthening the primary healthcare system and community services, rebuilding health infrastructure, improving the provision of essential medicines, and expanding community health programs, among other strategies. Programs designed to address specific health problems, such as levels of chronic disease, malnutrition, and mental health, may also be important. Cross-sector cooperation between government, humanitarian agencies, and local populations is crucial to enable populations to recover from the health impacts of armed conflicts.

Conclusions

This study assessed mortality and survival determinants among war-affected populations of Laelay Maichew Woreda, Tigray, Ethiopia. The results revealed excess mortality among children and young people, underscoring the overall fragile situation of young and adult populations during armed conflict and the importance of emergency medical and public health responses during and after conflict.

Males had higher rates of death, which was probably a reflection of increased exposure to conflict-related violence (both the threat of targeted violence and conflict-related injury), since civilian males are perceived as potential combatants. Socioeconomic stability, while less important, affected survival through access to food and healthcare. Improved medical infrastructure and equal healthcare access in Laelay Maichew greatly reduced mortality risks; however, acute health emergencies substantially increased mortality, highlighting the need for real-time data and quick interventions.

The results from this study suggested that the effect of conflict on post-conflict mortality is associated with demographic, socioeconomic, and health variables. The recovery strategies of the post-conflict era should focus on safeguarding vulnerable populations, restoring access to healthcare resources, and strengthening the health system. Policymakers should monitor regular mortality as well as disease-specific outcomes when developing post-conflict recovery policies.

Additional research efforts should focus on long-term health impacts, the pace of recovery, and assessing the success of intervention programs using large-scale longitudinal designs.

Recommendations

Interventions in conflict settings must directly address the key risk factors identified in this analysis of mortality determinants. Efforts should focus on reducing deaths due to armed conflict by supporting civilian protection and access to aid. In light of the evidence for the health impact of disease and the clear effects of inaccessibility, mobile clinics, medical logistics and medical supplies, and interventions via targeted vaccinations should be established in the most at-risk districts in the study.

Nutritional support should be aimed at the most vulnerable groups, including children, pregnant and lactating women, and internally displaced persons. Given the elevated mortality risk for males, there should also be an emphasis on emergency and trauma care for conflict-exposed populations. Interventions should be implemented to improve health services in the localities most affected and to recover services that have been eroded through damage or displacement, to reduce preventable mortality.

Finally, mortality and disease monitoring should be established in areas most burdened by conflict to allow for swift responsive interventions, and the implementation of strategic plans should follow the identified risk patterns using evidence-based approaches.
