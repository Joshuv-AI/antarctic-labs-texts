Evidence for high-frequency hearing in a Permian stem reptile
Authors: Kelsey M Jenkins, Rachel C Fleming, Juan D Daza, Hans-Dieter Sues
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-frequency hearing evolved in most amniote lineages in the Permian and Triassic. However, for reptiles, the evolutionary history of hearing is more elusive. Some have suggested that tympanic hearing evolved independently in each major lineage of reptile, largely based on gross observations of the stapes and outer ear region of fossils. On that basis, it was postulated that many Permian stem reptiles could not hear high frequencies. However, this hypothesis lacks quantitative support. To our knowledge, biomechanical analyses have not previously been conducted to determine the capacity for hearing in stem reptiles. Here, we conduct functional and morphometric analyses of the reptilian stapes including fossil data. Using harmonic response analyses, we demonstrate that the stapes of the stem reptile Youngina capensis could be displaced proximodistally at values similar to tympanic squamates. Additionally, Y. capensis is predicted to possess a tympanum based on a flexible discriminant analysis, while early-diverging stem reptiles are classified as low-frequency bone conductors. We show that hearing was facilitated by the formation of the annular ligament, improving stapedial movement, in addition to the evolution of a slender stapes and tympanum. Our results suggest that relatively high-frequency hearing was present in the common ancestor of reptiles, originating by the late Permian.Subject terms: Palaeontology, Auditory system, Phylogenetics

Introduction

Hearing is one of the most ubiquitous senses across Tetrapoda. From the many mammals and birds with sensitive ears capable of hearing high frequencies1,2 to ground-dwelling reptiles and amphibians with restricted frequency ranges3–5, sound allows animals to interpret their surroundings in a myriad of ways. Hearing provides animals with a unique mode of inter- and intraspecific communication6 as well as the ability to listen for prey and detect danger7. Even species with poorly developed hearing systems are known to sense sound waves and even vocally communicate with conspecifics effectively4,8,9.

Despite the ubiquity of hearing across Tetrapoda, there is controversy surrounding its evolutionary origins. The development of the middle ear from Meckel’s cartilage in extant mammals, and evolution of the postdentary bones of mandibles of non-mammalian synapsids into the bones of the middle ear of mammaliaforms are well established, e.g., refs. 10–12. However, the concurrent evolution of reptilian hearing involving a single ossicle, the stapes, has remained enigmatic. In extant reptiles, the transmission of sound waves from the tympanum to the inner ear is facilitated by the stapes and extrastapes. In brief, sound waves first encounter the tympanic membrane, causing it to move, and also forcing movement of the ossicle chain (i.e., extrastapes and stapes). As this movement occurs, the proximal portion of the stapes interacts with the fluids of the inner ear, transmitting sound waves within the inner ear, where they are detected by specialized hair cells and interpreted by the brain as sound. The slender morphology of the stapes in many taxa allows for the transmission of high-frequency sound waves, facilitating impedance matching between the airy medium by which sound initially travels to the liquid medium within the inner ear.

Only recently was it suggested that a slender stapes is ancestral to reptiles based on developmental data13 and fossils14. A larger body of work suggested there were multiple independent acquisitions of tympanic hearing in each major lineage of reptiles (i.e., lepidosaurs, archosaurs, turtles) in the Triassic15–17 (Fig. 1). This hypothesis was supported by the relatively large and “bulky” stapes present in many Permian stem reptiles, e.g., refs. 18,19, in addition to a lack of osteological correlates relating to a soft-tissue tympanum (e.g., otic notch). The “bulky” stapes of these early-diverging taxa are very different from the slender stapes—capable of transmitting higher frequencies—that is potentially the plesiomorphic condition for crown reptiles.

The “bulky” stapes described for most Permian stem reptiles, however, does not fully encapsulate the diverse morphologies seen in this bone across different taxa. While early-diverging clades, like captorhinids, have relatively large and robust stapes with a thick columella and broad footplate18, other Permian stem reptiles possess a more rod-like, albeit relatively large, columella with a much smaller footplate20. From what is known about squamate stapes, these morphological differences imply different capabilities for the transmission of vibrational energy from the external environment to the inner ear21. Furthermore, previous work on fossils often could not account for the presence of a tympanum, which is known to amplify sound and improve sensitivity22,23, though recent evidence suggests a possible tympanum in some stem reptiles14. Conversely, several extant squamates that dwell in totally different habitats and lack a tympanum (e.g., amphisbaenians [underground], chameleons [arboreal], snakes [terrestrial, aquatic, underground, and arboreal]) are typically less sensitive to airborne sound and primarily rely on bone conduction3,23,24. Because the tympanum is a soft-tissue structure, it is not likely to be preserved, and paleontologists have been largely unable to estimate the hearing abilities of extinct animals. Therefore, the dichotomy between low-frequency and high-frequency hearing used to categorize many stem reptiles is insufficient, clouding our ability to determine the ancestral condition for Reptilia.

Here, we calculated the capacity of stapedial movement in three stem reptiles in order to elucidate improvements in hearing from the early to the late Permian. We hypothesize that early-diverging taxa from the early Permian with enlarged stapedial footplates will show a lower capacity for stapedial movement, similar to that of squamates that utilize bone conduction and lack a tympanum. Squamates with this combination of characters are generally more sensitive to low frequencies within the range of 200–500 Hz23. Simultaneously, we hypothesize that the late Permian Youngina capensis, with its elongate stapes and small footplate, shows an increased capacity for movement that would suggest improved hearing, potentially within the range of tympanic squamates. Tympanic squamates typically have improved sensitivity at or above 500 Hz, and often have extended sensitivity ranges from 1000 to 2000 Hz or more23. To determine relative stapedial movement, we subjected the stapes of these taxa to Harmonic Response Analysis (HRA) and then compared those results to those from a diverse selection of stapes from squamates that inhabit varying environments and exhibit different morphologies. To further explore the form-function relationship of the reptilian stapes and the capacity to hear, we conducted a phylogenetic flexible discriminant analysis using a training dataset of 104 squamates from a variety of ecosystems and exhibiting varying levels of sensitivity. Our results suggest that high-frequency middle ear hearing, analogous to those of extant tympanic squamates, evolved by the late Permian, and is likely the plesiomorphic condition for crown reptiles. This challenges previous hypotheses that suggest tympanic hearing evolved independently in each major lineage of reptile, or that improved hearing sensitivity requires a tympanic middle ear.

Results

Harmonic response analyses in extant squamates and fossil taxa

We created and analyzed 3D models of the ear of two gecko species with known hearing sensitivity and frequency ranges to validate our HRA models, and we subsequently applied those model parameters to the stapes of extant and extinct species (Fig. 2, Table 1, Fig. S2, and Supplemental Movie 1). Compared to the previously published displacement curves25,26, our validation models show similar trends, where response decreases steadily at approximately 1000 Hz. As frequency increases, displacement decreases with occasional erratic peaks. Erratic peaks at higher frequencies are probably not interpreted by the animal as sound23, but instead indicate a lack of damping at resonate frequencies. Upon validation, we subjected the gecko models to further HRA where 2 Pa of pressure (~ 100 dB SPL of sound pressure) is applied to the tympanum, extrastapes, and stapes (Fig. 1C–E). While 2 Pa is louder than many sounds in nature, it was chosen to match the values of previous works for apt comparison25,26. For both species of gecko, displacement of the stapedial footplate was greatest when pressure was applied to the tympanum and lowest when applied to only the distal tip of the stapes (Fig. 3A, B). Intermediate values are seen for analyses in which pressure was applied to the extrastapes.

Using the same material properties and boundary conditions, we then conducted HRA for a series of squamates with differing stapedial morphologies that are thought to correlate with their hearing abilities. These HRA were only conducted on the stapes of these species. Generally, elongate, slender columellae with smaller footplates have been associated with relatively sensitive hearing and increased frequency ranges, while those with enlarged footplates tend to be associated with bone conduction21,23. However, we also find that the columella of bone-conducting squamates can be quite variable, ranging from quite elongate to very short (Fig. 4C). Despite these differences in the shape of the columella, bone-conducting squamates generally exhibit less displacement than those with a tympanum within the range of 100–1000 Hz (Fig. 3C and Supplemental Movie 2), though there is some overlap in displacement values in the lower range of tympanic species and upper range of bone-conducting species.

We subjected the stapes of three fossil taxa to HRA, and we find the magnitude of stapedial movement varies between species (Fig. 3C and Supplemental Movie 3). Captorhinus aguti and Bolosaurus major, two of the earliest-diverging members of the reptilian stem that lived during the early Permian, show small levels of displacement in the range of 100–1000 Hz, similar to that of bone-conducting squamates. B. major shows the lowest levels of displacement across all sampled taxa, suggesting very poor sensitivity in the range of 100–1000 Hz. Evolutionary modifications to its stapes resulting in poor sensitivity may be a trade-off for its powerful bite27, which has been hypothesized for many lepidosaurs with poor hearing, no tympanum, and strong bites28,29. Conversely, Y. capensis exhibits displacement values similar to those of tympanic squamates within the range of 100–1000 Hz. Of the sampled squamates with tympanic hearing, only Varanus komodoensis shows higher displacement values than Y. capensis within this frequency range (Fig. 3C and Supplemental Data 1). Our results suggest that Y. capensis was more sensitive to sound than earlier-diverging stem reptiles and may have possessed a tympanum.

While stapedial shape and the presence of a tympanic membrane strongly influence sound reception, we also wanted to explore the influence that suturing of the stapedial footplate has on stapedial displacement, and by extension, hearing sensitivity. The stapes of captorhinids is thought to be weakly sutured to the surrounding bones of the braincase18, while our validation models of extant squamates assume that the stapes is suspended in the oval window of the inner ear by the annular ligament23. The annular ligament is a fibrous ring of tissue that surrounds the stapedial footplate and attaches it to the oval window. It has a low stiffness (K = 120 N/m)30 particularly when compared to the stiffness of thin cranial bone (K = 390,000 N/m)31, which we applied as a proxy for light suturing seen in captorhinids. We hypothesize that the evolutionary transition from sutured bone to unsutured bone accompanied by the evolution of an annular ligament would allow for more movement of the stapes within the oval window. To this end, when we simulated all three fossil stapes with these different stiffness values, those with the lower K value showed displacement values several orders of magnitude greater than those of sutured bone (Figs. 3D and S1d). Realistically, those taxa with inflexible sutured bone (Captorhinus, Bolosaurus) should be modeled with the higher K value that results in greater stiffness, while the unsutured stapes of Youngina should be modeled with a more flexible, lower K value because it is not sutured to the surrounding bone. However, our results (Fig. 3D) demonstrate that increased flexibility between the stapes and the oval window could contribute to stapedial movement, regardless of stapedial shape, and suggests that the evolution of a flexible annular ligament would be crucial for improved movement.

Phylogenetic flexible discriminant analysis

The potential for tympanic hearing in Y. capensis is congruent with our flexible linear discriminant analysis (FDA). We supplied our FDA with a training dataset of 104 squamates for which we took linear measurements and positional data of the stapes (Fig. 4A). Based on these priors, the FDA predicted the placement of the fossil taxa within one of three groups: tympanic, atympanic, and bone conduction. A degree of overlap is observed between tympanic and bone-conducting squamates, though they largely occupy distinct regions of the discriminant space (Fig. 4B). While C. aguti and B. major were predicted to utilize bone conduction, Y. capensis was predicted to have tympanic hearing. Y. capensis exhibits many morphological similarities to squamates with tympanic hearing, including a low ratio of columellar length to footplate circumference (Fig. 4C), and its stapes holds a similar position in the skull as taxa with tympanic hearing (Fig. 4D). Tympanic species occupy a narrow range of positions, with the columella oriented posteriorly and ventrally at moderate angles (~85–133° measured from the anterior tip of the skull and ~83–127° measured from the dorsum of the skull). Conversely, the stapes of bone-conducting species exhibit a wide range of shapes and may be oriented in many different positions (Fig. 4C, D). Atympanic species also occupy a narrow range of shapes and positions, but they share the discriminant space with bone-conducting species, suggesting these squamates also utilize bone conduction.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that Y. capensis possessed a stapes with similar displacement capability and morphology as squamates with tympanic hearing, suggesting it also possessed a tympanum or at least demonstrated increased hearing sensitivity compared to more early-diverging stem reptiles. By extension, this implies that sensitive hearing, and potentially tympanic hearing, is ancestral to Reptilia and did not evolve independently in each major lineage of reptile as previously hypothesized (Fig. 1B). Previous work inferred that Y. capensis and other Permian stem reptiles did not possess a tympanum based on the morphology of the quadrate, in addition to the morphology of the stapes. In extant reptiles with a tympanum, the quadrate is bowed posteriorly to anchor the tympanic membrane23, and thus fossils with a bowed quadrate were interpreted to support a tympanum15, while those like Y. capensis with a vertical quadrate were said to lack a tympanum.

Despite common consensus for the origin of hearing in reptiles, widespread evidence for a tympanum in Carboniferous and Permian taxa is present on the amniote and lissamphibian stem14,32. It is only within Amniota, particularly on the reptile stem, that the tympanum was apparently lost, implying only low-frequency hearing for these taxa, akin to snakes that use bone conduction to sense vibrations through the ground33. For these taxa, bone conduction is thought to be facilitated by a connection between the stapes and the hyoid, as is present in Sphenodon and some squamates34,35. To this end, atympanic reptiles display distinct evolutionary paths toward sound transmission via unique connections between the stapes, the extrastapes, and frequently the lower jaw, though these pathways are overall less sensitive than those that utilize a tympanum23. While a similar connection between the stapes and the lower jaw was certainly possible for Permian stem reptiles utilizing bone conduction, it would not have greatly impacted movement of the stapes within the oval window. This is particularly the case where the footplate of the stapes is sutured to the surrounding bone or occupies the entirety of the oval window, as is the case for early-diverging taxa like C. aguti. This, in addition to the overall size and shape of the stapes, which is considerably larger than those of most squamates (Fig. S4), would still be a limiting factor impacting stapedial movement and, by extension, sensitivity.

Other authors suggested that the tympanum was not lost in early-diverging stem reptiles, but instead was greatly reduced or migrated to the underside of the skull, where it would not be framed by the quadrate36. Such is the case for the stem reptile Milleropsis pricei, in which a tympanic fossa is present on the posterior margin of the skull and supported in large part by the quadratojugal and squamosal rather than the quadrate37. Other early-diverging stem reptiles were also suggested to possess tympana14,38,39, though the phylogenetic implications of widespread tympanic hearing in the reptilian stem has gone unappreciated. Because the quadrate of Y. capensis is not concave, but the position, shape, and displacement capability of the stapes suggests tympanic capabilities, it is plausible that either an incipient or fully formed tympanum was present, possibly along the posterior margin of the skull14 (rather than laterally as in most extant reptiles). Though previous work suggested a tympanum was not present, the stapes does not appear to be in complete articulation with the quadrate in Youngina20, and we suggest its free margin could have been in contact with an incipient tympanum posteriorly. Similar configurations have been proposed for stem turtles, in which the stapes lies within a posteroventrally oriented groove in the quadrate, which guides the stapes towards the tympanum40. The possibility remains, however, that a tympanum was not present in Youngina. If this is the case, then it is still possible that modifications to the shape and flexibility of stapes could facilitate improved hearing ranges in the absence of a tympanum, though hearing would not be as high-frequency as in taxa that possess a tympanum. However, a single origin of a stapes that is capable of transmitting relatively high frequencies and a tympanum is a far more parsimonious explanation for the origin of hearing in Reptilia than independent acquisition in each of the three major lineages, particularly in light of the data presented here. Most likely, high-frequency tympanic hearing was more widespread along the reptilian stem than previously acknowledged.

It is well-established that the evolution of a tympanum and a stapes with a slender columella improved hearing sensitivity at higher frequency ranges, and our results also show greater displacement of the stapes with a tympanum and extrastapes rather than without these structures (Fig. 3A, B). However, these adaptations are not solely responsible for improved hearing capabilities. Modifications to the proximal portion of the stapes also improve hearing function, and a reduction in the size of the footplate as well as the evolution of a pressure-release window, would also facilitate improved sensitivity to high frequencies21,39. We demonstrate that in addition to these features, stapedial movement was facilitated by the loss of sutural contact between the stapedial footplate and neighboring bones, as well as the formation of the fibrous annular ligament at some point along the reptilian stem (Fig. 3D).

The amount of stapedial displacement at any particular frequency range does not fully encompass an animal’s ability to hear, and there are several other components of the ear that process sound that impact sensitivity at different frequency ranges (e.g., ligaments, musculature, inner ear anatomy). While we could not model all these various components of the ear, stapedial displacement is still a crucial component of the hearing mechanism to consider, particularly for impedance matching. The vibration of the stapes in response to sound waves is critical for the ability to hear, and this vibration is dependent on physical properties related to the mass, friction, and stiffness of the stapes. While the overall shape and size of the stapes is most frequently discussed in relation to the hearing abilities of fossil taxa, these other factors are seldom discussed. In FEA models of single-ossicle ears, the stiffness of the annular ligament is one of the most influential parameters in addition to that of the tympanic membrane and extracolumella41–43. Furthermore, the orientation of the stapes originating from the oval window is restricted to a narrower range in tympanic species than atympanic species (Fig. 4D), revealing the importance played by its orientation in transmitting sound waves from the outer to the inner ear. This demonstrates that high-frequency hearing is enabled by in-and-out rocking movement of the stapes, tangential to the midline of the skull, which is possible due to having an elastic annular ligament instead of sutured bone.

In our HRA, even fossils with “bulky” stapes (C. aguti, B. major) experienced greater displacement when modeled with an annular ligament instead of sutured bone, despite their greater overall size (Fig. 3D). In some fossorial squamates, the footplate of the stapes occupies the entire oval window and can be fused or tightly bound to the surrounding bones by a thick ligament. This greatly restricts the movement of the stapes and influencing the animal’s sensitivity range3,44, demonstrating the importance of stapedial movement and attachment in sound reception. We hypothesize that in addition to the formation of a tympanum, the annular ligament evolved early along the reptilian stem, facilitating movement of the footplate within the oval window. However, this evolutionary transition likely did not take place as a simple binary shift from lightly sutured to highly flexible. More likely, the stapediovestibular joint gradually gained better flexibility along the reptilian stem as stapedial shape changed, the tympanum formed, and sutural contact between the stapes and oval window became less stiff. Detailed examination of the braincase of additional stem reptiles to determine the nature of contact between the stapedial footplate and the surrounding bones of the braincase will be crucial for elucidating the earliest evolutionary phases of the reptilian ear.

Future work should consider the nature of the stapediovestibular joint in stem reptiles in order to elucidate the tempo and mode of the evolution of hearing within the lineage. In extant reptiles, reduced movement of the stapes within the oval window and varying stapedial orientations have been observed for fossorial squamates that secondarily evolved low-frequency hearing45. For these squamates, particularly amphisbaenians and snakes, the footplate may occupy the entirety of the oval window, greatly restricting stapedial movement in all directions. It was proposed that a large footplate may protect the ear from physical damage in burrowing squamates21, suggesting that a large stapedial footplate with restricted movement is adaptive and better facilitates bone conduction. To this end, we hypothesize that the annular ligament reduces rotation and displacement in alternate directions, which would reduce chaotic and damaging motions of the footplate at high frequencies. In other squamates restricted to hearing low frequencies, however, the fit between the stapes and the oval window is not as tight46. Such variation in stapedial orientation, morphology, and joint flexibility suggests that these traits are under less constraint in bone-conducting taxa than in those with a tympanum. However, more research into the nature of the stapediovestibular joint in extant reptiles is needed to clarify the role that each of these components play in hearing. Future work exploring stapedial movement in cadaveric reptiles could be conducted to elucidate how the annular ligament impacts stapedial movement depending on stapedial morphology.

Methods

Model validation in living squamates

We created 3D biomechanical models of the middle ear in two species of gecko (Rhacodactylus leachianus [CAS Herp 203066] and Chondrodactylus turneri [UF Herp 165490]) for which high-resolution diceCT scans are available on Morphosource.org. Our models include the tympanum and surrounding tissue, the extrastapes, and the stapes. Stapedial displacement is used here to determine the capability of the stapes for movement when sound waves interact with the tympanum. This in turn can be used as a proxy for interpreting a reptile’s relative hearing capabilities, because the in-and-out movement of the stapedial footplate within the oval window impacts sound detection by filtering the sound frequencies that cause vibrations in the inner ear15. However, this is largely true at lower frequency ranges (<1000 Hz)—erratic movements of the stapes at high frequencies are not thought to be interpreted by the animal as sound and often causes damage to the middle and inner ear23. To produce accurate models, we compared them to previous work that measured in vivo stapedial displacement in two species of gecko, Underwoodisaurus millii 25 and Gekko gecko26. In vivo measurements of stapedial displacement in those species used the Mössbauer technique, a method that permits measurement of small vibrational amplitudes, like those associated with vibrations of the middle ear. Though these species are different, most geckos exhibit similar peak sensitivities between 400 and 1000 Hz, but some species have higher peak sensitivities and more extensive sensitivity ranges23. Additionally, while these previous studies utilized entire specimens, our models only consisted of the tympanum, extrastapes, and stapes. Therefore, we aimed to have our models to show similar stapedial displacement trends to the previously studied geckos, and not necessarily provide a precise replication of the previously published curves. Our curves are likely an underrepresentation of the true stapedial displacement. As such, they should be viewed as relative measures.

To create our models, we segmented the tympanum, extrastapes, and stapes of Rhacodactylus leachianus and Chondrodactylus turneri in VG Studio Max v. 2024.2.1 (Volume Graphics) using the region growing tool. Surfaces were determined for each component of the ear and then exported as a surface mesh (Fig. 1). Following this, we cleaned and smoothed our models in Meshlab47 using Tutorial 1 of Clark et al.48. Meshes were then imported into Autodesk Fusion 360 to convert to a parasolid format (.step) and for further cleaning if necessary using the shrinkwrap, smoothing, and remeshing tools. At times, models had to be simplified and refined to meet the computational limitations of the biomechanical software. Once the models were completely cleaned, they were imported into OnShape (onshape.com) for assembly and reorientation. Assembled models were oriented proximodistally in X-axis, the Y-axis oriented dorsoventrally, and the Z-axis anteroposteriorly. Once this was accomplished, we imported the fully assembled ear models into SimScale for analysis (simscale.com).

In SimScale, we utilized a Harmonic Response Analyses (HRA)—a type of Finite Element Analysis (FEA)—to measure stapedial displacement along the X-axis at varying frequencies. HRA is a popular structural analysis often used in engineering to determine a structure’s response to steady-state periodic loads depending on its material properties and boundary conditions. We assigned material properties to each component of the ear, with the tympanum modeled as soft tissue, the extrastapes as cartilage, and the stapes as bone (Table 1). Then, we established boundary conditions (Fig. 2C–G): a 2 Pa pressure was applied to the tympanum according to in vivo studies25,26, fixed supports were placed on the soft tissue surrounding the tympanum where it contacts the quadrate anteriorly (Fig. 2F), and an elastic support with a stiffness (K) of 120 N/m was placed along the rim of the stapedial footplate to simulate the annular ligament (Fig. 2G), per previous work that established this value in human cadaveric specimens30. Currently, no studies have investigated this value in reptiles. We also established a bonded contact between the tympanum and the extrastapes, as the two are firmly attached to one another, and we established a sliding contact between the extrastapes and the stapes. While the contact between the extrastapes and stapes varies across different species of lizard, from firmly bonded by connective tissue to flexible23,49, we chose all our models to have a flexible sliding contact for ease of comparison between models and because we could not determine the nature of contact for both species of gecko. However, sensitivity analyses did not show major differences between trends, though impacted the severity of local optima (Fig. S1a, b). Displacement was measured from 100 to 10,000 Hz and sampled every 100 Hz, and we measured displacement for five positions on the stapedial footplate (Fig. S1c). Differences in displacement for these positions were negligible (Fig. S1c), so we report displacement only for the central position for all other HRA analyses herein. Our models show more variation and local minima and maxima compared to those previously published (Fig. S2). This is due to our increased sampling compared to earlier studies. For that reason, and because there are experimental conditions we cannot replicate as well as unique species differences, we did not aim to precisely replicate the previously published frequency response curves. Instead, we aimed for similar trends wherein displacement decreases with increasing frequency at a similar rate.

Displacement resulting from different components of the ear

To determine how each component of the ear interacts with sound waves and influences stapedial displacement, we conducted two additional analyses for R. leachianus and C. turneri. In the first, we removed the tympanum and applied a pressure of 2 Pa to the pars superior and the pars inferior of the extrastapes, which both stretch across the proximal side of the tympanum (Fig. 2D). In the second, the tympanum and extrastapes were removed, and a pressure of 2 Pa was applied to the distal tip of the stapes (Fig. 2E). All other settings were kept the same as the complete model outlined above. Additionally, the stapes-only analyses allow us to make equivalent comparisons to taxa for which only the stapes could be analyzed, particularly for fossil taxa, as described below.

Acoustical models of fossil taxa

We segmented the stapes of C. aguti OMNH 4481650 and B. major AMNH 446127, both of which have bulky stapes (see Fig. S3 for new interpretation of the stapes). We also included Y. capensis AMNH 556120, which has a stapes intermediate between the bulky form seen in early amniotes and the slender form of most crown reptiles. Captorhinids are traditionally thought to be early-diverging stem reptiles51, although some analyses recently recovered them on the amniote stem14,52. Regardless, both phylogenetic positions allow us to use captorhinids as an appropriate outgroup for assessing the functional and morphological changes of the stapes in evolution of early amniotes and the reptile line. B. major diverges between captorhinids and crown reptiles27. Y. capensis is well-accepted as a stem reptile that lies near the divergence of crown reptiles, e.g., refs. 53–55, making it an appropriate transitional taxon for understanding the changes that took place from the bulky stapes seen in the many early amniotes to the slender stapes of crown reptiles. We conducted HRA simulations according to the parameters described above, and we conducted another set of simulations for which the stiffness (K) of the elastic support representing the annular ligament was increased to 390,000 N/m. This value represents the lower range of skull stiffness31, and we used it to simulate the “light suturing” described for the captorhinid stapes in relation to the braincase18, which was likely present in many early-diverging stem reptiles. Cranial bone is very stiff, and there is potential that the “light suturing” seen in stem reptiles18 is not as stiff as the lower range of skull stiffness. Therefore, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis testing a series of intermediate K values (Fig. S1d), indicating that any increase in stiffness above 1000 N/m reduces stapedial displacement, with greater reduction seen in the highest K values.

Ecological comparisons among acoustical models

Many squamates, particularly fossorial species lacking a tympanum, have secondarily evolved stapes with enlarged footplates21. These species utilize bone conduction and have restricted frequency ranges and reduced sensitivity compared to species with a tympanum23. To determine the stapedial displacement of species inhabiting different ecological niches and hearing capabilities, we conducted HRA for 10 species of squamates according to the parameters outlined above, five of which have a tympanum, and five of which lack the tympanum and utilize bone conduction (Fig. S4). Those with a tympanum include R. leachianus, C. turneri, Agama agama, Anolis baleatus, and V. komodoensis. Those utilizing bone conduction are Amphisbaena alba, Anilius scytale, Anniella pulchra, Eryx colubrinus, and Zygaspis kafuensis. We further compared the results of these HRAs to those conducted for the fossil taxa.

Phylogenetic flexible discriminant analysis

To characterize potential ear types in fossil taxa for which the presence of tympanic hearing is unknown, we utilized a phylogenetic flexible discriminant analysis. This analysis incorporates phylogenetic signal to maximize the correlations between stapedial morphology and hearing abilities. We established classifications by applying the analysis to a training dataset of 104 squamate reptiles. We created this dataset by digitally isolating and measuring the stapes from CT scans downloaded from Morphosource.org. Those scans were imported into VG Studio Max v. 2024.2.1, where we segmented the stapes from each specimen and converted it to a surface mesh. We imported those surface meshes into Slicer v. 5.8.056 to take three-dimensional measurements. We measured the length proximo-distal length of the stapes, the circumference of the footplate, the circumference of the columella at the mid-shaft, and the circumference of the of the distal tip of the columella, which articulates with the extrastapes. All these measurements are in millimeters and were scaled by specimen head length. We also measured the anterior angle of the stapes and the dorsal angle of the stapes using ImageJ (Fig. 4A)57. All measurements, specimen numbers, and permissions can be found in Supplemental Data 2.

We created a phylogenetic metatree to conduct the analysis. We consulted a taxonomically broad squamate phylogeny to construct the backbone topology58, and additional phylogenies were consulted to determine the interrelationships of gekkotans59,60, amphisbaenians61, snakes62–64, varanids65, and iguanians66,67. We included three categories of ear type: tympanic, atympanic, and bone-conducting. While the presence or absence of a tympanum was easily noted in the CT scans of extant squamates, we had to make certain assumptions about the potential for bone conduction in all atympanic taxa. We assumed all snakes and amphisbaenians utilize bone conduction, which has been long established3,33,68; nothing we observed in CT scans contradicted this assumption. We also inferred that the few chameleons in our dataset (n = 3) utilized bone conduction via a unique stapedial-pterygoid connection that has been noted for some species9,24,69; the orientation of the stapes in the CT scans support this connection in our sampled taxa. All atympanic taxa for which we could not determine bone conduction were kept within the atympanic category. To conduct this analysis, we modified a published script that calculates the optimal lambda value and discriminant space of extant taxa, and predicts the ear type of our fossil taxa70. The analysis was conducted in R71 with a Pagel’s lambda of 0.17, which was determined to be the optimal value for correlation between ear type and stapedial morphology. For in-depth background information on this analysis, see ref. 72. This R script is available as Supplementary Code 1.
