The use of triptans and other acute treatments for the management of migraine in routine clinical practice in England: a retrospective cohort study using the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD)
Authors: Shuk-Li Collings, Amisha Patel, Hannah Gowman, Sarah Law, Aideen Ahern, Robert Pawinski, Robert Wood, Shazia K Afridi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigraine is a common and disabling neurological condition affecting around 10 million people in the UK. Optimal management has been indicated as a priority for improving migraine care in the UK, supported by clinical guidelines to provide clinicians with pathways for using acute and preventative treatments. There is a paucity of evidence on the real-world management of migraine, particularly with acute treatments.MethodsA retrospective cohort study using linked primary and secondary healthcare data in England (Clinical Practice Research Datalink [CPRD] Aurum and Hospital Episode Statistics [HES]). Adults with their first diagnosis of migraine between 1st January 2017 and 31st March 2019 were identified. Acute treatment patterns (prescribing rates, sequencing) and triptan use were described during a variable 12-month minimum follow-up period, up to 31st March 2020.ResultsA total of 62,205 patients met all eligibility criteria, of which 76.2% (n = 47,420) were female and the mean (SD) age at index was 39.8 (15.5) years. Almost 60% (n = 36,849; 59.2%) of the cohort received no prescription for acute treatment during follow-up, 23.1% (n = 14, 379) received acute treatment only and 17.6% (n = 10,977) received acute and preventative treatments. Triptans (25.7%; n = 15,971) and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (15.1%; n = 9,368) were the most frequently prescribed acute treatments during follow-up. Opioids were prescribed to 28.5% (n = 3,130) of the acute and preventative subgroup, and 15.8% (n = 2,267) of the acute only subgroup during follow-up. Only 1.8% (n = 1,127) were prescribed two different triptans with the median (IQR) time to second triptan of 7.8 (2.2–15.8) months. Of these, 17.2% (n = 198/1,149) subsequently cycled back to a previously prescribed triptan.ConclusionA large proportion of patients with migraine received no prescribed acute treatments, potentially indicating over-the-counter management or treatment with preventatives administered in secondary care. The most prescribed acute treatments were triptans. Very few were tried on more than one triptan, with some returning to earlier triptans. A significant proportion of our cohort were prescribed an opioid regimen, counter to UK clinical guidance. These observations suggest some variability to local guidance and international best practices in the treatment in migraine, highlighting a need for further guidance on the use of acute treatments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-03125-0.Keywords: Migraine, Acute treatment, Triptans, Opioids, Migraine epidemiology, Headache, Primary care, England, CPRD

Background

Globally, migraine is ranked the second most disabling disorder [1], with United Kingdom (UK) estimates suggesting that 10–15% of the population experience migraines [2, 3]; however, this is an under-representation as migraine often goes undiagnosed [4, 5]. The patient burden of migraine is high; increased migraine frequency is associated with poorer health-related quality of life, with recurring migraines leading to poorer work productivity, social relationships and mental health [6, 7]. Migraine can be related to a broad spectrum of factors, such as genetics, hormones, stress, and dehydration [6, 7].

In the UK, people experiencing migraine may start management with over-the-counter medications (OTC), such as paracetamol, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or aspirin; if these are ineffective, a triptan may be prescribed in primary care by a general practitioner (GP) [8]. Triptans, first approved in the UK over 30 years ago, can be used alone, or in combination with paracetamol or an NSAID at any stage of a migraine attack [9–12]. Antiemetics may also be prescribed to reduce nausea or improve absorption of acute migraine medications [13, 14]. Pharmacological interventions aim to reduce the frequency and severity of migraines or to stop attacks completely; however, clinical management with such pharmacological interventions must further consider the potential for medication overuse headache (MOH) caused by frequent use of acute medications [15, 16].

The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), which is the national health technology assessment body of England, recommend sumatriptan as the first-choice triptan, with other triptans and different formulations available to be tried if the initial triptan is ineffective [12, 17]. However, almost a third of patients will not respond to any triptans [18]. In 2023, an orally administered calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) receptor antagonist (also known as a gepant), was recommended for acute treatment of migraine if: at least two triptans were tried and were not adequately effective; triptans were contraindicated or not tolerated; NSAIDs and paracetamol were tried but did not work well enough [19]. The most common reasons for real-world discontinuation of triptans are lack of efficacy and concerns over side effects (most frequently dizziness, nausea and fatigue) [20]. Patients with insufficient response to triptans can experience poorer outcomes relative to responders such as greater disability [21], impaired quality of life [21] and increased risk of MOH [22]. Further, in the UK triptans are not licensed for those aged over 65 years, and should not be prescribed to those taking contraindicated medications (such as ergotamine and monoamine oxidase inhibitors) or those with contraindicated comorbidities (such as cardiovascular [CV] and cerebrovascular disease) [8, 10, 11].

Preventative treatments may be more appropriate for patients with frequent severe migraine as overuse of acute treatments increases the risk of developing MOH [23]. Common oral preventative treatments include beta-blockers, tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) and anticonvulsants [24, 25]. Other options are available in secondary care, including onabotulinum A (botox) or CGRP monoclonal antibodies (mABs), which are administered by injection on a monthly or quarterly basis [26–31]. More recently, gepants, have also been recommended by NICE for use within the NHS for the prevention of episodic [32, 33] and chronic migraine [32].

In the UK, clinical management of migraine is currently informed by guidelines from NICE and the British Association for the Study of Headache (BASH) [14, 34]. However, European evidence suggests disparities between treatment guidance recommendations and real-world clinical practice [35, 36]. Reasons include delayed reporting of symptoms to healthcare providers, delayed diagnoses, lack of access to migraine specialists and inadequate treatment options [37]. This study aimed to explore whether disparities between national guidelines and clinical practice in the acute treatment of migraine exist in UK clinical practice. Using routinely collected primary care data, this study describes the treatment patterns and sequencing of acute migraine treatments prescribed in the UK primary care setting.

Methods

Data source

A population-based retrospective cohort study was conducted using primary care data from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink database (CPRD Aurum – May 2022 release), linked secondary care administrative data from the Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) Admitted Patient Care (HES APC) and Outpatient (HES OP) datasets, and death registration data from the Office for National Statistics (ONS). Over 95% of CPRD Aurum patients can be linked to the HES datasets [38].

CPRD Aurum is an anonymised database of primary care electronic health records, capturing data collected as part of routine care from 16.5% of NHS primary care practices in England [39]. As of May 2022, over 41 million patient records were deemed to be of acceptable quality (data flagged as being accurate, complete and consistent), with more than 13 million patients active (still alive and registered with a GP practice) [39]. CPRD Aurum patients are broadly comparable to the English general population in terms of age, deprivation, geographical spread, and ethnicity [40, 41]. Data captured include demographics (e.g., age, sex, weight and ethnicity), medical diagnoses, referrals to specialists in secondary care, and all other types of care administered as part of routine primary care practice, such as prescriptions issued in primary care. The HES datasets were established to monitor secondary care activity and capture details on inpatient admissions (HES APC dataset) and outpatient appointments (HES OP dataset) from NHS funded centres in England. Death registration data from the ONS includes information on the official date and cause of death [42].

Study design and population

Adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) in England with migraine were identified via diagnoses recorded in the primary care setting between January 2017 and March 2019 (indexing period). The date of first (incident) migraine diagnosis, observed within the indexing period, including any diagnoses observed in secondary care (see supplementary Table 1), constituted the index date. To ensure patients were newly diagnosed in the indexing period, patients with a migraine diagnosis recorded in their medical record at any point prior to the indexing period were excluded from the study. To be included in the study, patients needed to be continuously registered with the same GP practice for at least 12 months prior to and following the index date, and for their primary care medical record to be eligible for linkage to the HES datasets.

Patient follow-up extended for a minimum of 12 months from index date to the earliest of: study period end date (31st March 2020), end of data availability (the earliest of the data the patient leaves the GP practice or last collection date of the practice) or patient death. The study period end date was chosen to avoid including data following the COVID-19 pandemic, whereby the access to and delivery of healthcare services were significantly disrupted.

Subgroups

Patients with migraine were further classified into four groups according to treatment for migraine prescribed in primary care during follow-up: acute only, preventative only, acute and preventative and no treatment (see supplementary Table 3 for list of medication classes).

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Sociodemographics were captured at index and included age, sex, ethnicity and social deprivation (measured using quintiles of the 2019 Index of Multiple Deprivation [IMD] score [43]). Migraine treatment during 12-months prior to migraine diagnosis

Migraine treatments prescribed during the 12-months prior to the index migraine diagnosis were observed (see supplementary Table 3 for full list of drug classes), as well as concomitant medications prescribed during follow-up.

Migraine treatment during follow-up

Prescriptions of treatments with an indication for migraine were reported during the 12-month minimum follow-up. The data does not contain the indication for a prescription – as such, it was assumed that prescriptions were for migraine rather than alternative indications – for example, if a patient was prescribed an NSAID during follow-up, it is assumed to be for migraine. In those prescribed at least two different acute treatments during follow-up, particular sequences of acute treatments of interest were assessed, centred around triptans, to understand whether specific treatment guidelines were being followed. Note that patients could receive any treatment prior to or following the sequences of interest but must have switched directly from one acute treatment of interest to another (i.e., without further classes of acute treatment prescribed in between).

The proportion of patients prescribed different triptans (1, 2 and ≥ 3 types of triptans) over follow-up was calculated. In those prescribed at least two different triptans during follow-up (omitting those prescribed multiple types of triptans on the same day), the time from index to the second triptan and the proportion of patients cycling back to a previously prescribed triptan was reported. Patients returning to the same formulation of triptan and those changing to a different formulation were also described. Contraindicated use of triptans was also captured, defined as the proportion of patients who were prescribed medications contraindicated for use with triptans within 7 days prior to or following a triptan prescription (see supplementary Table 4 for list of contraindicated medications). The number and classes of acute treatments prescribed in those with comorbidities potentially contraindicated with triptan use was also reported.

Data analysis

This was a descriptive study and as such, no hypotheses were tested. Frequencies and percentages were presented for categorical variables, while mean, standard deviation (SD), median and 1 st quartile (Q1) and 3rd quartile (Q3) were reported for numeric variables. To account for variable follow-up within the cohort, prescribing rates were presented as the number of patients with a healthcare event (e.g. prescription) over the total time at risk across all patients and reported as per 100-person-years. Primary suppression of data on counts with fewer than 5 patients, and secondary suppression where necessary, was implemented to ensure patient confidentiality was preserved in accordance with CPRD reporting rules.

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

In total, 62,205 patients newly diagnosed with migraine met all the eligibility criteria for inclusion in the study, of which 79.8% (n = 49 567) were coded with ‘migraine’, and 4.9% (n = 3,032) were coded with ‘migraine with aura’. The median length of follow-up among the cohort was 25.9 (Q1, Q3: 18.8, 32.7) months. The mean age at index (incident migraine diagnosis) was 39.8 (SD: 15.5) years, and most patients were female (n = 47,420; 76.2%), and of white ethnicity (n = 48,383; 77.8%). The proportion of patients across the IMD quintiles were similar (Table 1).

Depression (n = 7,049, 11.3%), anxiety (n = 5,399, 8.7%) and hypertension (n = 3,996, 6.4%) were the most commonly recorded comorbidities in the 12-months prior to migraine diagnosis. Of note, 7.8% (n = 4,856) of the cohort had at least one comorbidity indicative of potential contraindication for triptan use. Furthermore, 15.5% (n = 9,635) of the cohort had a prior headache diagnosis (see supplementary Table 2 for headache code lists), and 10.3% (n = 6,388) had at least one consultation with a neurologist in the 12-months prior to receiving a formal migraine diagnosis (Table 1). Additionally, 2.3% (n = 1, 451) of the cohort had a prior headache diagnosis, and a triptan prescribed in the 12-months prior to their index migraine diagnosis (Table 1).

Migraine treatment prior to diagnosis

In the 12 months prior to index, 24.3% (n = 15,137) of the cohort received a treatment indicated for migraine (Table 2); noting that many of these treatments are also indicated for other conditions. NSAIDs were the most commonly (n = 5,664, 9.1%) prescribed treatment prior to index, followed by paracetamol-containing regimens (n = 5,588, 9.0%), with paracetamol plus codeine prescribed to 6.9% (n = 4,295) of the cohort. Opioids were prescribed to 5.9% (n = 3,664), with 2.7% (n = 1,702) of patients prescribed codeine. Of note, 4.6% (n = 2,850) of the cohort received a triptan (mostly sumatriptan: 87.4% [see supplementary Table 5]) in the 12-months prior to their migraine diagnosis (Table 2).

Migraine treatment during follow-up

During the follow-up period, after index date, over half (n = 33,821, 54.4%) of the study cohort did not receive any prescriptions for migraine therapies in the primary care setting. Nearly a quarter (n = 14,379, 23.1%) of the cohort were prescribed acute migraine treatments only, 17.6% (n = 10,977) were prescribed acute and preventative treatments, and 4.9% (n = 3,028) received preventative treatment only. Contraceptives were prescribed to 17.9% (n = 8,490) of female patients with migraine. Progesterone only and combined (oestrogen containing) contraceptives, which are not recommended for those with migraine with aura due to increased risk of stroke [44], were prescribed to 14.8% (n = 7,019) and 4.5% (n = 2,154) of female patients with migraine, respectively (Table 1).

Overall, 21.8% (n = 13,576) of the cohort were prescribed only one type of acute treatment during follow up, 11.2% (n = 6,964) two acute treatments, and 7.7% (n = 4,816) ≥ 3 acute treatments. When stratifying by treatment subgroups, 61.2% (n = 8,933), 24.8% (n = 3,572) and 13.0% (n = 1,874) of the ‘acute only’ group received 1, 2, and ≥ 3 types of acute treatments, respectively. Of those in the ‘acute and preventative’ group, 42.3% (n = 4,643), 30.9% (n = 3,392) and 26.8% (n = 2,942) were prescribed 1, 2, and ≥ 3 types of acute treatments, respectively (Table 3).

The most prescribed migraine treatment classes were triptans (n = 15,971, 25.7%), NSAIDS (n = 9,368, 15.1%) and paracetamol-containing regimens (n = 8,266, 13.3%) (Table 3). Among the paracetamol-containing regimens, the majority were prescribed paracetamol with codeine (n = 6,938, 11.2%). Other opioids were prescribed to 8.7% (n = 5,397) of the overall cohort, rising to 28.5% (n = 3,130) in the ‘acute and preventative’ subgroup, and 15.8% (n = 2,267) of the ‘acute only’ subgroup. Triptans were commonly prescribed in the ‘acute only’ and ‘acute and preventative’ treatment subgroups with approximately two-thirds receiving triptans (63.3% [n = 1,900] and 62.6% [n = 6,871] respectively) (Table 3). When accounting for variable follow-up, the prescribing rates (per 100 person-years) of each treatment class by treatment subgroup followed similar patterns to those observed with the proportion-based analyses (Fig. 1).

Triptan use

Few patients were prescribed more than one type of triptan, overall and within the treatment subgroups – less than 2% overall (n = 1,171; 1.9%). Among this group, the median time from index date to second triptan was 7.8 (Q1, Q3: 2.2, 15.8) months (Table 4).

Of the 1,171 patients with more than one type of triptan prescribed during follow-up, 44 were removed from the analysis of triptan sequencing due to their triptan prescriptions being on the same day. Of the remaining 1,127 patients, the most frequently prescribed first and second triptans were sumatriptan (n = 826; 73.3%) and zolmitriptan (n = 434; 38.5%), respectively (Table 4). Cycling back (i.e., prescribing the first triptan again after a second one was tried) was observed in 17.0% (n = 192/1,127) of patients, with most returning to the same formulation (n = 165; 85.9%) (Table 4).

Of 11,780 patients who had been prescribed more than one type of acute treatment 62.6% (n = 7,377) were prescribed triptan as monotherapy followed by a triptan in combination with a paracetamol-containing regimen; 28.7% (n = 3,379) were prescribed an NSAID followed by a triptan. Of note, 61.8% (n = 7,285) were prescribed a triptan followed by an opiate and 21.7% (n = 2,556) were prescribed an opiate followed by a triptan (Table 5).

Triptan contraindications

There were 4,856 (7.8%) patients with comorbidities that could potentially contraindicate them from triptans. NSAIDs and paracetamol-containing regimens were the most prescribed treatment classes among this group, however triptan prescriptions were still observed in 13.7% (n = 666) (see supplementary Table 7).

Contraindicated medications (ergotamine, monoamine oxidase inhibitors or another triptan) were prescribed within 7 days prior to, or after, a triptan among 1.8% (n = 290) of 15,971 patients who received a triptan during follow-up (Table 4).

Discussion

Summary

In this population-based retrospective study, we described the prescribing patterns of acute migraine treatment in patients with incident migraine in England. Our study highlights potential inconsistencies between real-world clinical practice and the current treatment guidelines for acute management of migraine. Triptans are highly utilised in primary care, however, some patients may be receiving them against the guidelines. Further, triptans are being prescribed ahead of a documented migraine diagnosis and triptans are being cycled back to, despite an absence of guidelines recommending this approach. This study also highlights possible concerns around the use of opioids in patients with migraine.

Acute treatment prescribing and OTC use

Over 40% of patients in our study were prescribed treatments for acute management of migraine, comprising of paracetamol, NSAIDs, opioids and triptans. This is likely to be an under-representation of the wider migraine population, given that paracetamol and NSAIDs are available OTC in the UK, and OTC medications are not captured in this dataset. Sumatriptan may also be purchased OTC, in packs of two 50 mg tablets, if a patient has a clinically confirmed diagnosis [45]; again, this could lead to an underestimate of overall triptan use. As such, the true proportion of patients managing migraine with acute medications will be greater than 40%. Similarly, as some preventative migraine treatments, such as CGRP mABs, are not observable within the CPRD Aurum dataset, it is likely that we have underestimated the uptake of migraine treatment in the present study.

Treatment sequencing and guideline concordance

Clinical management of migraine in the UK is informed by the NICE and BASH guidelines [14, 34]. These guidelines advise that triptans can be tried as part of a stepped approach, i.e., try simple analgesics before triptans, or a stratified approach, i.e., where the treatment depends on migraine severity. We aimed to capture acute treatment sequencing in this study: we observed that almost 30% of patients prescribed more than one acute treatment did follow a stepped approach with NSAIDs followed by triptans. Again, this is likely higher if OTC medication is accounted for.

Triptan switching patterns

While triptans are an established and effective acute treatment for migraine, their use may be limited by factors such as adverse effects, including dizziness and nausea, and insufficient response in some individuals. Observational studies show patients achieve variable degrees of pain relief (9–45%) and pain freedom (25–75%) within 2 h of taking a triptan [46]. Similarly, it has been highlighted that across randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies, 6–53% of patients have headache recurrence within 24 h of triptan administration [46], and it is estimated that 30% of patients with migraine do not respond to any triptans [14, 46]. These limitations may explain triptan switching; however, this was only observed in fewer than 2% of patients following their migraine diagnosis. Our finding that most patients prescribed a triptan are not prescribed further alternative triptans may suggest that the initial triptan choice was effective; however, we also observed a much greater proportion of patients prescribed an opioid following a triptan (1.9% vs. 11.7% of the study cohort). This could suggest that management with triptans was insufficient for some patients, leading to the use of opioids against current guidelines.

In the UK, there is no guidance on triptan cycling i.e., returning to a previously tried triptan. A second triptan can be tried as non-response to one triptan does not predict response to other triptans [18] and it is recommended only following two failures with the initial triptan [14]. Our study showed that some patients move back and forth between different triptans, trying a new triptan and then cycling back to a previously prescribed triptan. Instances of this pattern were low, and it is not known from our data why patients cycle back. This pattern of cycling back warrants further investigation to determine the reasons for this approach and to inform further clinical guidance on whether this approach is appropriate.

Triptan use prior to documented diagnosis

We observed a small proportion of patients prescribed triptans prior to having a recorded diagnosis of migraine. Unlike many other treatments for migraine, triptans are not indicated for any other condition [47]. Therefore, receiving a triptan ahead of a formal migraine diagnosis could suggest that clinicians are using triptans to differentiate between headache disorders and support a migraine diagnosis. There are currently no guidelines to support or oppose this approach to diagnosis. It could also reflect incomplete data capture, however it is likely that if a GP is prescribing a triptan, that the corresponding diagnosis would also be recorded.

Triptan prescribing trends and influences on triptan selection

Sumatriptan was prescribed to almost 90% of patients who had been prescribed a triptan prior to their migraine diagnosis and was the first triptan prescribed in over 70% of patients who received two or more triptans following their diagnosis, in line with national guidance [8, 14]. Second to clinical recommendations, the cost of sumatriptan relative to other triptans may influence these observations [48], and might suggest cost-conscious prescribing is becoming an important component of clinical decision-making. Further, due in part to local prescribing restrictions, GPs often only have access to one or two different triptans, which may limit the choices that clinicians have had on which medications they are able to prescribe. We observed that sumatriptan, rizatriptan and zolmitriptan were prescribed as a first option triptan in 94% of patients, and second option in 83%.

Potential triptan contraindications

We aimed to identify patients who were contraindicated for triptans due to comorbidities, including ischaemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, previous myocardial infarction, and uncontrolled or severe hypertension. As a proxy, we were able to establish patients who had a record of any of the following conditions prior to diagnosis: hypertension, transient ischaemic attack, ischaemic heart disease, severe hepatic impairment, myocardial infarction peripheral vascular disease, coronary vasospasm and cerebrovascular accident. With the use of this proxy, 8% of patients may have been contraindicated for triptans and yet 14% of this group were still prescribed triptans. This observation may suggest inappropriate prescribing of triptans. However, it may be an overestimation due to the use of a broad proxy to define triptan contraindications, particularly influenced by the inclusion of general ‘hypertension’ diagnosis codes. Available diagnosis codes do not distinguish between controlled and uncontrolled hypertension, which limits the precision of our contraindication definition.

Opioid use

Although NICE and BASH guidelines clearly state opioids should not be used for the treatment of migraine due to the associated risk of opioid dependence and MOH [12, 17], over 40% of the acute treated migraine population were prescribed opioids after diagnosis and 7% prescribed prior to diagnosis. In addition, we observed patients were being prescribed an opioid ahead of a triptan, albeit a small proportion. The use of opioids is a concern, while there is a possibility that opioids were prescribed for a condition other than migraine, the large proportion of patients receiving opioids calls for additional investigation.

Emerging therapies and the evolving migraine treatment landscape

Since this study was conducted, new treatments in the form of CGRP antagonists have been approved for the acute management of migraine [49, 50]. Where this study has highlighted inconsistencies in the use of acute treatments in line with clinical guidelines, these new treatments could address an unmet need that may have been the cause of some of these inconsistencies. Clinical guidelines will be updated to incorporate advice on the use of these new treatments and once sufficient time has passed to capture real-world clinical practice, future research on treatment patterns should be conducted in the evolved treatment landscape for migraine.

Limitations

This study was limited by the data available within the datasets, most notably only medications prescribed in the primary care setting are captured within CPRD Aurum. Consequently, migraine medications initially prescribed in secondary care (hospital or outpatient settings) could not be observed. Although it is expected that most acute migraine medicines will be initiated in primary care, and any initiated in secondary care will be continued in primary care, the first instance of the prescription from secondary care will not be observed [51]. Further, medications exclusively administered in secondary care, such as mABs or botox, used for the preventative treatment of migraine are not captured in the dataset, which may have resulted in the misclassification of some patients in the acute-only treatment group, whom should have been categorised in the acute and preventative treatment group. Similarly, many treatments for migraine can be bought OTC, including paracetamol, NSAIDs, paracetamol plus codeine, and sumatriptan. Therefore, the treatment history of some patients may have been incomplete, and use of such treatments may be underestimated. Additionally, most treatments for migraine have multiple indications, and as prescription data does not specify the health condition to be treated, it is possible that some patients received treatment for another condition. This study does not capture all migraine patients in the population since some do not consult their GP about their migraine; additionally previous research suggest that a large proportion of migraine is misdiagnosed as headache [52] and as such, our results may not be fully representative of all people with migraine. Another limitation of this study is the incomplete diagnostic coding associated with outpatient neurology consultations. In the UK, diagnostic information is not required for reimbursement in the outpatient setting, resulting in high levels of missingness in linked diagnosis codes. It is therefore possible that neurology visits prior to migraine diagnosis may not have been related to migraine. Finally, our study may have overestimated the proportion of patients with comorbidities contraindicated with triptan use, as previously mentioned. This is due to the lack of granularity on the severity of hypertension; all patients with a diagnosis of hypertension were considered when defining our proxy for triptan contraindication.

Conclusions

To conclude, we observed heterogeneity in the treatment patterns of patients with migraine and the extent to which national guidelines on the management of migraine are followed varied across the cohort. An unexpectedly high rate of opioid prescribing was identified, particularly in those prescribed acute and preventative treatments during follow-up. While triptans were highly prescribed, very few patients were prescribed more than one type of triptan. These findings underscore a need for updated clinical guidelines that consider current prescribing patterns in the context of newly available treatment options and enhanced education on the optimal management of migraine in primary care.
