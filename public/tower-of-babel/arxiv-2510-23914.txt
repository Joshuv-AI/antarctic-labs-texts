Geometry-Inspired Unified Framework for Discounted and Average Reward MDPs
Arsenii Mustafin, Xinyi Sheng, Dominik Baumann

Abstract
The theoretical analysis of Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) is commonly split into two cases—the average-reward case and the discounted-reward case—which, while sharing similarities, are typically analyzed separately. In this work, we extend a recently introduced geometric interpretation of MDPs for the discounted-reward case to the average-reward case, thereby unifying both. This allows us to extend a major result known for the discounted-reward case to the average-reward case: under a unique and ergodic optimal policy, the Value Iteration algorithm achieves a geometric convergence rate.

1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation and Contribution

A Markov Decision Process (MDP) is a foundational framework for sequential decision-making and serves as the theoretical backbone of Reinforcement Learning. From the early stages of MDP theory, analysis split into two branches: the discounted-reward and average-reward cases. In the discounted-reward case, future rewards are valued less than immediate rewards, and performance is measured by the discounted sum of rewards over an infinite trajectory, with each reward multiplied by a discount factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
. This criterion is common when time preferences matter, for example, in economic applications. In contrast, the average-reward case treats all rewards as equally important and measures performance by the long-run average of the rewards, a setting often used in control theory.

In this work, we unify the analysis of these two cases by extending the MDP geometry framework
(
Mustafin et al. 2025a
)
, originally developed for the discounted-reward case, to the average-reward case. This extension enables our main result, which generalizes
Mustafin et al. 2025b
to the average-reward setting: under weak ergodicity and a unique optimal policy, the Policy Iteration algorithm achieves a
geometric convergence
rate.

1.2
Previous Works

MDPs were introduced in the late 1950s, together with foundational algorithms such as Value Iteration (VI)
(
Bellman 1957
)
and a comprehensive dynamic programming framework
(
Howard 1960
)
. Two standard performance criteria emerged: the discounted-reward criterion, extensively summarized by
Puterman 1994
, and the average-reward criterion, developed through the works of
Blackwell 1962
,
Yushkevich 1974
,
Denardo and Fox 1968
, and characterized further by
Schweitzer and Federgruen 1978
;
Schweitzer 1984
.

For discounted MDPs,
Howard 1960
showed that the convergence rate of VI is upper-bounded by the discount factor
\gamma
, and that this bound is attainable. Subsequent works refined and systematized these results and their assumptions
(
Puterman 1994
;
Feinberg and Huang 2014
)
, establishing VI as a central tool for computing value functions in the discounted case. More recently,
Mustafin et al. 2025b
applied a recently introduced MDP geometry framework
(
Mustafin et al. 2025a
)
to show that VI’s geometric convergence in the discounted case exceeds the discount factor
\gamma
assuming an ergodic optimal policy.

For average-reward MDPs, convergence of VI has been extensively studied. In the unichain case, linear convergence rates can be obtained via the
\delta
- and ergodicity coefficients
(
Seneta 2006
;
Hübner and Hinderer 1977
;
Puterman 2014a
, e.g.,)
, and
J
-stage span contractions yield linear rates every
J
iterations in the span seminorm
(
Federgruen and Schweitzer 1978
;
van der Wal 1981
;
Puterman 2014a
)
. In the multichain case, normalized VI iterates converge to the optimal average reward, although the policy error may not vanish
(
Puterman 2014a
, Thm. 9.4.1)
. Necessary and sufficient conditions for VI convergence, as well as asymptotic linear rates on the Bellman error, were established by
Schweitzer and Federgruen 1977
;
Schweitzer and Federgruen 1979
. To ensure convergence of iterates to the bias function
h^{\star}
,
White 1963
introduced Relative Value Iteration (RVI), with sufficient conditions studied by
Morton and Wecker 1977
, and additional conditions for VI convergence were studied by
Vecchia et al. 2012
.

2
Problem Statement

2.1
MDP Model

We employ a simple discrete MDP framework used by
Mustafin et al. 2025b
, which builds on the classical setup of
Puterman 2014b
, modifying it slightly by redefining the action space for convenience. An MDP is defined by the tuple
\mathcal{M}=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R},\gamma\rangle
, where

\mathcal{S}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
is a finite set of
n
states, and
\mathcal{A}
is the set of attainable state––action pairs with cardinality
m
. Following
Mustafin et al. 2025a
, state––action pairs are the main object of our analysis; we will use this concept extensively and, for convenience, refer to a state––action as a
SAP
. Since every SAP
a
is tied to exactly one state, we define a mapping
\mathrm{st}
such that
\mathrm{st}(a)=s
means that the state of SAP
a
is
s
(i.e.,
a
can be chosen only in state
s
). Each SAP
a
is characterized by a transition probability vector
\mathcal{P}(a)=(p^{a}_{1},\dots,p^{a}_{n})
drawn from the set of all transition probabilities
\mathcal{P}
and a deterministic reward
r^{a}
drawn from the set of all rewards
\mathcal{R}
. Lastly,

\gamma\in(0,1)
is a discount factor that specifies how much less a reward is valued if it is received one step later.

Given the set of SAPs, a choice rule (or
policy
) is a map
\pi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
that satisfies
\mathrm{st}(\pi(s))=s
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. We consider only deterministic stationary policies, which means that for each state
s
, the same action is chosen whenever the state is visited. Because each SAP is tied to a state, we can view a

policy as a set of SAPs
,
\pi=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
with
a_{i}=\pi(s_{i})
. If a policy
\pi
chooses SAP
a
, we write
a\in\pi
.

For a given
\mathcal{M}
and policy
\pi
, performance can be measured in two ways corresponding to the discounted- and average-reward criteria. We start with the discounted-reward case, as it is slightly simpler.

Discounted MDP

In the discounted reward case the value of the policy
\pi
in state
s
, denoted as
V^{\pi}(s)
, is defined as

V^{\pi}(s)=\E\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\right],

where
r_{t}
is the reward obtained at time
t
. The vector of values
V^{\pi}
is the unique vector satisfying the Bellman equation:

V^{\pi}(s)=r^{\pi(s)}+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}p_{s^{\prime}}^{\pi(s)}V^{\pi}(s^{\prime}),

which can also be written in matrix form as

V^{\pi}=R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}V^{\pi},

where
R^{\pi}
is a vector of all rewards of policy
\pi
, so that the
i
th entry
R^{\pi}(i)=r^{\pi(i)}
; and
P^{\pi}
is a transition kernel of the Markov chain induced by policy
\pi
. It is a square matrix whose
i
th row is equal to
P^{\pi}(i)=\mathcal{P}(\pi(i))=(p^{\pi(i)}_{1},\dots,p^{\pi(i)}_{n})
.

In the discounted reward case, the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
is a policy that attains the maximum values in every state:

V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\geq V^{\pi}(s),\quad\forall\pi,s.

Average reward MDP

The average-reward case is considered harder, as it involves two quantities associated with each policy: the
gain
and the
bias
. Before we start, note that the discount factor
\gamma
is usually omitted in this setting; to maintain generality, we set
\gamma=1
in this paper.

For a given policy
\pi
, the
average reward
(or
gain
) from state
s
is defined as

\rho^{\pi}(s)\;=\;\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\,\E\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r_{t}\,\middle|\,s\right].

If the MDP is unichain, then the average reward is state-independent, i.e.,
\rho^{\pi}(s)\equiv\rho^{\pi}
for any start state
s\in\mathcal{S}

(
Mahadevan 1996
)
. We say that
\rho^{*}
is the
optimal average reward
(or
optimal gain
) if
\rho^{*}(s)=\max_{\pi}\rho^{\pi}(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

For a given policy
\pi
, the
relative value function
(or
bias
) from state
s
is defined by

h^{\pi}(s)\;=\;\E\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\bigl(r_{t}-\rho^{\pi}\bigr)\,\middle|\,s\right].

Accordingly, this function satisfies the Bellman equation

T^{\pi}h^{\pi}\;=\;h^{\pi}\;+\;\rho^{\pi}\mathbbm{1},

where
\mathbbm{1}
is the all-ones vector, and for any vector
h
the (undiscounted) Bellman operator is

T^{\pi}h(s)\;=\;r_{\pi}(s)\;+\;\sum_{s^{\prime}}P\!\left(s^{\prime}\mid\pi(s)\right)h(s^{\prime}).

Note that we keep the same notation as in the discounted case, with the understanding that here
\gamma=1
.

There is not a unique fixed point for the Bellman equation: since

T^{\pi}\!\bigl(h^{\pi}+c\,\mathbbm{1}\bigr)\;=\;\bigl(h^{\pi}+c\,\mathbbm{1}\bigr)\;+\;\rho^{\pi}\mathbbm{1},

it follows that
h^{\pi}+c\,\mathbbm{1}
is also a solution for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
. We denote the set of solutions by

\{\,h^{\pi}+c\,\mathbbm{1}\;\mid\;c\in\mathbb{R}\,\}.

2.2
Advantages and Value Iteration Algorithm

Mustafin et al. 2025a
suggest that advantages are the key quantities of an MDP. The
advantage
of a SAP
a
at state
s
with respect to a policy
\pi
is defined as the gain (or loss) caused by a one-time deviation from policy
\pi
by choosing
a
at state
s
, compared to following
\pi
starting from
s
.

In the discounted-reward case, advantages are always well-defined and given by

\textrm{adv}(a,\pi)\;=\;r^{a}\;+\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{a}_{i}\,V^{\pi}(i)\;-\;V^{\pi}(s),

(1)

and this quantity exists for all SAPs and for all policies and pseudo-policies.

An MDP can be solved by the
Value Iteration
(VI) algorithm, which starts from an arbitrary value vector
V_{0}
and then iteratively performs the update

V_{t+1}(s)\;=\;\max_{\mathrm{st}(a)=s}\left\{\,r^{a}\;+\;\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{a}_{i}\,V_{t}(i)\right\}.

(2)

Mustafin et al. 2025b
suggest an alternative, advantage-based view of this update:

V_{t+1}(s)\;=\;V_{t}(s)\;+\;\max_{\mathrm{st}(a)=s}\,\textrm{adv}(a,V_{t}),

(3)

and we will adopt this perspective here, as it aligns naturally with the geometric view.

Similarly to the classic definition of the advantage function for the discounted case, in the average-reward case, we define the advantage function as in
Zhang and Ross 2020
;
Adamczyk et al. 2025
:
\textrm{adv}(a,\pi)=Q^{\pi}(s,a)-h^{\pi}(s)
, where

Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\E\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\left(r_{t}-\rho^{\pi}\right)\mid s,a\right]=r^{a}-\rho^{\pi}+\sum_{s^{\prime}}p^{\pi(s)}_{s^{\prime}}h^{\pi}(s^{\prime}).

Then

\displaystyle\textrm{adv}(a,\pi)=r^{a}-\rho^{\pi}+\sum_{s^{\prime}}p^{a}_{s^{\prime}}h^{\pi}(s^{\prime})-h^{\pi}(s).

Finally, following
Mustafin et al. 2025b
, we analyze the convergence of VI using the vector span seminorm, defined as the difference between the maximum and minimum entries of a vector. For a value vector
V
, we denote it by
\textrm{sp}(V)=\max_{i}V(i)-\min_{j}V(j)
.

New Geometric Interpretation

3.1
New Action and Policy Vectors

(a)
Example of a 2-State MDP in an old interpretation in the discounted reward case.

(b)
Example of a 2-State MDP in the new interpretation in the average reward case.

Figure 1:
Old and new visualizations of two two-state MDP with the same transition probabilities and rewards but different discount factors. The right panel also illustrates why the geometric interpretation developed for the discounted-reward case does not extend directly to the average-reward case: in the latter, the vertical value lines collapse into a single line, so all states have the same value, and this set of values does not define a unique hyperplane. At the same time, a hyperplane can still be constructed for an ergodic policy, and the geometric picture remains logically coherent in that case. To extend the geometric framework to the average-reward case, we propose measuring the values not on the inner but on the outer edges of the action zones. These new values
v
can be used in both the average- and discounted-reward cases.

While the geometric interpretation demonstrated its applicability in the discounted-reward case
(
Mustafin et al. 2025b
)
, it does not directly extend to the average-reward case. The geometric intuition is illustrated in Figure
1
. Algebraically, for any policy
\pi
, the matrix
I-\gamma P^{\pi}
becomes singular when
\gamma=1
, so the value vector
V^{\pi}
that would satisfy the Bellman equation:

V^{\pi}=R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}V^{\pi}\;\implies R^{\pi}\;=\;(I-\gamma P^{\pi})\,V^{\pi}

cannot, in general, be computed at
\gamma=1
.

As suggested by Figure
1(b)
, we propose measuring values along different vertical lines, which leads to the following definitions of action and policy vectors.

For a SAP
a
with
\mathrm{st}(a)=s
, define the
action vector

a^{+}
by

a^{+}\;=\;\Bigl(r^{a},\;\tfrac{\gamma p^{a}_{1}-\gamma}{C},\;\dots,\;\tfrac{\gamma(p^{a}_{s}-1)-\gamma}{C},\;\dots,\;\tfrac{\gamma p^{a}_{n}-\gamma}{C}\Bigr),

where
C=n\gamma+(1-\gamma)
is an MDP constant. For convenience, denote by
c_{i}^{a}
the
i
-th coordinate of
a^{+}
excluding the first element
r^{a}
, that is,

c_{i}^{a}=\begin{cases}\tfrac{\gamma p^{a}_{i}-\gamma}{C},&\text{if }i\neq s,\\[4.0pt]
\tfrac{\gamma(p^{a}_{s}-1)-\gamma}{C},&\text{if }i=s.\end{cases}

Note that the sum of all entries of
a^{+}
except the first one equals
-1
. The first entry is special; following
Mustafin et al. 2025a
, we refer to it as the
0
-th coordinate (or
height
).

For values, given a policy
\pi
, define the state values
v^{\pi}(s)
by

\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}\;=\;r^{\pi(s)}\;-\;\frac{\gamma\,v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C}\;+\;\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{\pi(s)}\frac{v^{\pi}(i)}{C},\quad\text{where }v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v^{\pi}(i).

(4)

Then define the
policy vector

v^{\pi}_{+}
is

v^{\pi}_{+}\;=\;\bigl(1,\;v^{\pi}(1),\;\dots,\;v^{\pi}(n)\bigr).

Below we relate these newly introduced quantities to the classical value definitions in both the discounted- and average-reward settings.

3.2
Discounted Reward Case

We start by showing the relationships between the old and new values in the discounted reward case. If
\gamma<1
, we can subtract the quantity
\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C(\gamma-1)}
from both sides of the value equation, which leads to:

\displaystyle\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}

\displaystyle=r^{\pi(s)}-\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C}+\gamma\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\pi(i)}\frac{v^{\pi}(i)}{C}\implies

\displaystyle\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}-\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C(\gamma-1)}

\displaystyle=r^{\pi(s)}-\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C}-\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C(\gamma-1)}+\gamma\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\pi(i)}\frac{v^{\pi}(i)}{C}

\displaystyle=r^{\pi(s)}-\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}((\gamma-1)+1)}{C(\gamma-1)}+\gamma\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\pi(i)}\frac{v^{\pi}(i)}{C}\implies

\displaystyle\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}-\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C(\gamma-1)}

\displaystyle=r^{\pi(s)}+\gamma\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\pi(i)}\left(\frac{v^{\pi}(i)}{C}-\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C(\gamma-1)}\right).

Therefore, the values
\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}-\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C(\gamma-1)}
satisfy the Bellman equation and, due to uniqueness,

V^{\pi}(s)=\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}-\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C(\gamma-1)}=\frac{(1-\gamma)v^{\pi}(s)}{C(1-\gamma)}+\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C(1-\gamma)}.

Then, we can sum this expression over all states to get

\sum_{s}V^{\pi}(s)=\sum_{s}\left[\frac{(1-\gamma)v^{\pi}(s)}{C(1-\gamma)}+\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C(1-\gamma)}\right]=\frac{(1-\gamma)v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C(1-\gamma)}+\frac{\gamma nv^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C(1-\gamma)}=\frac{v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{1-\gamma}.

Defining the mean values as
\bar{V}^{\pi}=(\sum_{i}V^{\pi}(s))/n
and similarly for
\bar{v}^{\pi}
, we obtain

\displaystyle V^{\pi}(s)-\bar{V}^{\pi}

\displaystyle=V^{\pi}(s)-\frac{\sum_{i}V^{\pi}(i)}{n}=\frac{(1-\gamma)v^{\pi}(s)}{C(1-\gamma)}+\frac{\gamma v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C(1-\gamma)}-\frac{v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{(1-\gamma)n}

\displaystyle=\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}+\frac{\gamma nv^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{Cn(1-\gamma)}-\frac{v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}(n\gamma+(1-\gamma))}{Cn(1-\gamma)}=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C}(v^{\pi}(s)-\bar{v}^{\pi}).

Having this, we can finalize the equivalence between old and new geometric interpretations in the discounted case.

Lemma 3.1
.

In the discounted case, the inner product of action vector
a
and policy vector
\pi
is equal to the advantage of
a
with respect to
\pi
:

\displaystyle a^{+}v^{\pi}_{+}

\displaystyle=r^{a}+\sum_{i}c^{a}_{i}v^{\pi}(i)=\textrm{adv}(a,\pi).

Proof.

We work with the expression
\sum_{i}-c^{a}_{i}v^{\pi}(i)
, which is the height at which the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}^{\pi}
crosses the vertical line with coordinates the same as
a
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}-c^{a}_{i}v^{\pi}(i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}-c^{a}_{i}v^{\pi}(i)+\bar{v}^{\pi}-\bar{v}^{\pi}=\bar{v}^{\pi}+\sum_{i}-c^{a}_{i}(v^{\pi}(i)-\bar{v}^{\pi})

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\bar{V}^{\pi}+C\sum_{i}-c^{a}_{i}(V^{\pi}(i)-\bar{V}^{\pi})

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\bar{V}^{\pi}+(V^{\pi}(s)-\bar{V}^{\pi})+\sum_{i}(\gamma-\gamma p^{a}_{i})(V^{\pi}(i)-\bar{V}^{\pi})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}\tilde{c}^{a}_{i}V^{\pi}(i).

∎

3.3
Average Reward Case

In our newly defined value function,
\gamma
can be
1
, which motivates investigating equivalence in average-reward cases. We recall the equivalence between the discounted value function and the relative value function from the literature in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2

(
Puterman 2014a
)
.

Given any policy
\pi
and state
s
,

V^{\pi}(s)\;=\;\frac{\rho^{\pi}(s)}{1-\gamma}\;+\;h^{\pi}(s)\;+\;f^{\pi}(s,\gamma),

where
V^{\pi}
and
h^{\pi}
are the old discounted value function and the old relative value function, respectively;
\rho^{\pi}(s)
is the average reward, which remains constant with respect to the policy; and
f^{\pi}
is a remainder term such that
\lim_{\gamma\to 1}f^{\pi}(s,\gamma)=0
.

As a consequence of
v_{\Sigma}^{\pi}=(1-\gamma)\sum_{s}V^{\pi}(s)
shown above for the discounted case, we can get the equivalence between the traditional average reward and the new defined value function when considering the average reward MDP. For convenience, we use
\rho^{\pi}
to represent
\rho^{\pi}(s)
for all
s
.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
\gamma=1
, then for a given
\pi
,

\rho^{\pi}=\frac{v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C}.

Proof.

From Lemma
3.2
we have

\displaystyle v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}=(1-\gamma)\sum_{s}V^{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\rho^{\pi}(s)+(1-\gamma)\sum_{s}h^{\pi}(s)+(1-\gamma)\sum_{s}f^{\pi}(s,\gamma).

If
\gamma=1
, the last two terms converge to
0
. Then
v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}=\sum_{s}\rho^{\pi}
and
C=|S|=n
. Therefore,

\rho^{\pi}=\frac{v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{n}=\frac{v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C}.

∎

Let
\gamma=1
in our new value function in Equation
4
. According to Lemma
3.3
, the new value function in the average case can be written as

\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}\;=\;r^{\pi(s)}\;-\;\rho^{\pi}\;+\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{\pi(s)}\frac{v^{\pi}(i)}{C}.

This new value function can satisfy the Bellman equation in the average reward MDP:

T^{\pi}\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}\;=\;r^{\pi(s)}\;+\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{\pi(s)}\frac{v^{\pi}(i)}{C}=\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}\;+\;\rho^{\pi}.

Consequently, our new value function
\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}
belongs to the solution set
\{h^{\pi}+c\mathbbm{1}\mid c\in\mathbbm{R}\}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

In the average reward case, the inner product of the action vector
a^{+}
and the policy vector
v^{\pi}_{+}
is equal to the advantage function with respect to policy
\pi
:

\displaystyle a^{+}v^{\pi}_{+}

\displaystyle=r^{a}+\sum_{i}c^{a}_{i}v^{\pi}(i)=\textrm{adv}(a,\pi).

Proof.

Let
\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}=h^{\pi}(s)+c
for some constant
c
and
\gamma=1
. Then,

\displaystyle a^{+}v^{\pi}_{+}

\displaystyle=r^{a}+\sum_{i}c^{a}_{i}v^{\pi}(i)

\displaystyle=r^{a}-\frac{v^{\pi}_{\Sigma}}{C}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{a}\frac{v^{\pi}(i)}{C}-\frac{v^{\pi}(s)}{C}

\displaystyle=r^{a}-\rho^{\pi}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{a}\left(h^{\pi}(i)+c\right)-\left(h^{\pi}(s)+c\right)

\displaystyle=r^{a}-\rho^{\pi}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{a}h^{\pi}(i)-h^{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle=\textrm{adv}(a,\pi).

∎

3.4
Policy Evaluation and MDP Normalization

As mentioned earlier, in the average-reward case, the matrix
I-\gamma P
is not invertible when
\gamma=1
, so a unique value vector
V^{\pi}
satisfying
R^{\pi}=(I-\gamma P^{\pi})V^{\pi}
does not exist. However, for the new values, we can obtain the corresponding equation by rewriting Equation
4
in matrix form and rearranging terms:

R^{\pi}\;=\;(I+E-\gamma P^{\pi})\,\frac{v^{\pi}}{C},

where
E=\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{\top}
is the all-ones matrix. This equation can be solved in the average-reward case (i.e.,
\gamma=1
) when the policy
\pi
induces a
unichain
Markov chain (a chain with a single closed irreducible class), as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\pi
be a policy with transition kernel
P^{\pi}
on a finite state space, and let
\mathbbm{1}
denote the all-ones vector with
E:=\mathbbm{1}\mathbbm{1}^{\top}
. Then, the following are equivalent:

1.

The Markov chain induced by
P^{\pi}
is unichain (i.e., it has a single closed irreducible class; all other states are transient).

2.

The matrix
I+E-P^{\pi}
is invertible.

In particular, in the unichain case, the values
v^{\pi}
defined via the linear system
(I+E-P^{\pi})\,v^{\pi}=\cdots
are uniquely determined.

Proof.

(
1\Rightarrow 2
) Assume the chain is unichain. Then
\ker(I-P^{\pi})=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}
for the (right) kernel. For any vector
x
, write
x=\alpha\mathbbm{1}+y
with
\mathbbm{1}^{\top}y=0
. Using
P^{\pi}\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}
and
Ey=(\mathbbm{1}^{\top}y)\mathbbm{1}=0
,

(I+E-P^{\pi})(\alpha\mathbbm{1}+y)=\underbrace{(I-P^{\pi})\alpha\mathbbm{1}}_{=0}+\underbrace{E\alpha\mathbbm{1}}_{=\alpha n\mathbbm{1}}+(I-P^{\pi})y+\underbrace{Ey}_{=0}=\alpha n\mathbbm{1}+(I-P^{\pi})y.

If
(I+E-P^{\pi})(\alpha\mathbbm{1}+y)=0
, taking the component along
\mathbbm{1}
gives
\alpha n=0
, hence
\alpha=0
. Thus,
(I-P^{\pi})y=0
with
\mathbbm{1}^{\top}y=0
. By
\ker(I-P^{\pi})=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}
, we get
y=0
. Hence,
x=0
is the only solution, so
I+E-P^{\pi}
is invertible.

(
2\Rightarrow 1
) Assume
I+E-P^{\pi}
is invertible. Suppose, for contradiction, that the chain is not unichain. Then there are at least two closed irreducible classes, and hence
\dim\ker(I-P^{\pi})\geq 2
. Therefore, there exists a nonzero
y\in\ker(I-P^{\pi})
with
\mathbbm{1}^{\top}y=0
(choose a nontrivial linear combination of basis vectors of the kernel with zero sum). For this
y
, we have
Ey=0
and

(I+E-P^{\pi})y\;=\;(I-P^{\pi})y\;+\;Ey\;=\;0,

which contradicts invertibility. Hence the chain must be unichain.

The final statement follows since invertibility of
I+E-P^{\pi}
guarantees a unique solution
v^{\pi}
to the corresponding linear system.
∎

Since we can compute values for any unichain policy
\pi
, we can compute the advantage of any SAP with respect to such a policy, which is equivalent to constructing the policy hyperplane
\mathcal{H}^{\pi}
. This, in turn, implies that the transformation
\mathcal{L}^{\Delta}
described in
Mustafin et al. 2025a
can be applied to the MDP. Therefore, if the optimal policy induces a unichain MDP (the case we analyze in this work), the MDP can be normalized, which significantly simplifies the analysis.

4
Main Result

Assumption 4.1
.

There is a
unique
optimal policy
\pi^{*}
. The MRP implied by
\pi^{*}
is
unichain
.

Now, assume the Value Iteration algorithm on the new values
v^{\pi}
. Starting from the arbitrary values
v_{0}
m the algorithm consist of iteratively performing an update similar to Equation
2
:

\frac{v_{t+1}}{C}=\frac{v_{t}}{C}+\argmax_{a}\textrm{adv}(a,v_{t})=R^{\pi_{t}}-E\frac{v_{t\Sigma}}{C}+P^{\pi_{t}}v^{t},

where
\pi_{t}
is a policy greedy with respect to values
v_{t}
,
\pi_{t}(i)=\argmax_{a,\st(a)=i}\textrm{adv}(a,v_{t})
.

We then show that the new Bellman operator has a contraction property with respect to
\textrm{sp}(v_{t})
, the span seminorm of the values vector. For the proof of this theorem, we first prove the following auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any stochastic matrices
P,P_{1},\cdots,P_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and all-ones matrix
E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, there is

\prod_{t=1}^{N}(P_{t}-E)=\prod_{t=1}^{N}P_{t}+E^{\prime},

where
E^{\prime}
is a matrix with identical rows and
E^{\prime}v
is a vector with identical entries for any vector
v
.

Proof.

We first consider any row-stochastic matrix
P
, any matrix
H
whose rows are all identical and equal to a row vector
h
, and any vector
v
. Let
E=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the all-ones matrix. Then:

•

PE=E
.

•

EP
is a matrix with identical rows. Moreover, writing
H=[\,h,\ldots,h\,]^{\top}
with
h
a row vector, we have

Hv=[\,hv,\ldots,hv\,]^{\top}
, i.e., a vector whose entries are all the same scalar
hv
.

•

For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
E^{m}=n^{m}E
. Hence,
E^{m}v
is a vector with identical entries.

Thus, for any
i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{k}\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
, we have

•

P_{i_{1}}P_{i_{2}}\cdots P_{i_{k}}E=E
as
PE=E
.

•

The matrix
EP_{i_{1}}P_{i_{2}}\cdots P_{i_{k}}
has identical rows. We note
EP_{i_{1}}=[h,\cdots,h]^{\top}
since it has identical rows
h
. Then, for
P_{i_{2}}=[v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}]
with column vectors
v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}
,
EP_{i_{1}}P_{i_{2}}=[h,\cdots,h]^{\top}[v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}]
shows that
EP_{i_{1}}P_{i_{2}}
has identical rows
[hv_{1},hv_{2},\cdots,hv_{n}]
. By this induction, we obtain the result.

•

P_{i_{1}}P_{i_{2}}\cdots P_{i_{j}}EP_{i_{j+1}}P_{i_{j+2}}\cdots P_{i_{k}}
has identical rows.

The product of matrices
\prod_{t=1}^{N}(P_{t}-E)
can be expanded as

\prod_{t=1}^{N}(P_{t}-E)=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}}(-1)^{|S|}\left(\prod_{t\in S}E\prod_{t\notin S}P_{t}\right),

where each subset
S\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
indicates the indices
where
E
is selected instead of
P_{t}
.
The first term corresponds to
\prod_{t=1}^{N}P_{t}
, and the following
terms

\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-1)^{k}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq N}P_{N}\cdots P_{i_{k}+1}EP_{i_{k}-1}\cdots P_{i_{1}+1}EP_{i_{1}-1}\cdots P_{1}

contain one or more
E
factors. The product of
E
with any stochastic matrices from the left can be simplified to
E
. Consequently, each term in the expansion that contains
k
matrices
E
can be reduced to
E^{k}
multiplied by the remaining right-hand stochastic matrices, which all have identical rows as shown above. Moreover, the sum of matrices with identical rows also has identical rows, denoted as
E^{\prime}
.

∎

Theorem 4.3
.

If Assumption
4.1
holds, the span of the value vector obtained after
N
steps of a standard Value Iteration algorithm satisfies the following inequality:

\textrm{sp}(v_{N})\leq\gamma^{N}\tau\,\textrm{sp}(v_{0}),

where
\tau\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

The proof strategy is the same as in
Mustafin et al. 2025b
. After normalization, all actions lie on or below the horizontal zero-level hyperplane; therefore, SAPs participating in the unique optimal policy
\pi^{*}
have
0
rewards, and SAPs that do not participate have negative rewards.

For clarity, we define
\tilde{v}_{t}=\frac{v_{t}}{C}
. Assume
a^{*}\in\pi^{*}
and let
a:=\arg\max_{a}\textrm{adv}(a,\tilde{v}_{t})
with
st(a^{*})=st(a)=s
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{t+1}(s)=\tilde{v}_{t}(s)+\textrm{adv}(a,\tilde{v}_{t})=r^{a}+\gamma\sum_{i}(p_{i}^{a}-1)\tilde{v}_{t}(i)\leq\gamma\sum_{i}(p_{i}^{a}-1)\tilde{v}_{t}(i),

and

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{t+1}(s)=\tilde{v}_{t}(s)+\textrm{adv}(a,\tilde{v}_{t})\geq\tilde{v}_{t}(s)+\textrm{adv}(a^{*},\tilde{v}_{t})=\gamma\sum_{i}(p_{i}^{a^{*}}-1)\tilde{v}_{t}(i).

Then, there is

\displaystyle\gamma\sum_{i}(P^{a^{*}}_{i}-1)\tilde{v}_{t}(i)\;\leq\;\tilde{v}_{t+1}(i)\;\leq\;\gamma\sum_{i}(P^{a}_{i}-1)\tilde{v}_{t}(i),

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\gamma(P^{*}-E)\tilde{v}_{t}\;\leq\;\tilde{v}_{t+1}\;\leq\;\gamma(P_{t}-E)\tilde{v}_{t},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\gamma P^{*}\tilde{v}_{t}\;\leq\;\tilde{v}_{t+1}+\gamma E\tilde{v}_{t}\;\leq\;\gamma P_{t}\tilde{v}_{t},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\tilde{v}_{t+1}+\gamma E\tilde{v}_{t}=\gamma P^{\prime}_{t}\tilde{v}_{t}=\gamma\left[D_{t}P^{*}+(I-D_{t})P_{t}\right]\tilde{v}_{t},

where
E
is the all-ones matrix and
P^{\prime}_{t}:=D_{t}P^{*}+(I-D_{t})P_{t}
is a convex combination of two endpoints. The diagonal matrix
D_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
has entries
D_{t}(s,s)\in[0,1]
.

Define the maximum advantage of non-optimal actions with respect to the optimal policy as
-\delta
:

\displaystyle-\delta=\max_{a^{\prime}\notin\pi^{*}}\textrm{adv}(a^{\prime},\pi^{*}),\quad\delta>0.

Hence,

\displaystyle\textrm{adv}(a^{\prime},\pi^{*})\leq-\delta<0,\quad\forall a^{\prime}\notin\pi^{*}.

Since
r^{a}=\textrm{adv}(a,\pi^{*})\leq-\delta<0
, we obtain a stricter upper bound for
\tilde{v}_{t+1}(s)
:

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{t+1}(s)

\displaystyle=r^{a}+\gamma\sum_{i}(P^{a}_{i}-1)\tilde{v}_{t}(i)\leq-\delta+\gamma\sum_{i}(P^{a}_{i}-1)\tilde{v}_{t}(i)<\gamma\sum_{i}(P^{a}_{i}-1)\tilde{v}_{t}(i).

In matrix form, this yields

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{t+1}+\gamma E\tilde{v}_{t}<\gamma P_{t}\tilde{v}_{t}.

As
P^{\prime}_{t}
is a convex combination of
P^{*}
and
P_{t}
, and since
P^{\prime}_{t}\neq P_{t}
during the iteration before convergence to the optimal policy, we have
I-D_{t}\neq I
and
D_{t}(s,s)\neq 0
. Then,

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{t+1}(s)+\gamma\tilde{v}_{t,\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\gamma\left(D_{t}(s,s)\sum_{i}P^{a^{*}}_{i}\tilde{v}_{t}(i)+(1-D_{t}(s,s))\sum_{i}P^{a}_{i}\tilde{v}_{t}(i)\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\delta+\gamma\sum_{i}(P^{a}_{i}-1)\tilde{v}_{t}(i)+\gamma\tilde{v}_{t,\Sigma}

\displaystyle\leq-\delta+\gamma\sum_{i}P^{a}_{i}\tilde{v}_{t}(i),

which implies

\gamma D_{t}(s,s)\sum_{i}(P^{a^{*}}_{i}-P^{a}_{i})\tilde{v}_{t}(i)\leq-\delta.

We have

-\textrm{sp}(\tilde{v}_{t})=\min_{i}\tilde{v}_{t}(i)-\max_{i}\tilde{v}_{t}(i)\leq\sum_{i}\left(P^{a^{*}}_{i}-P^{a}_{i}\right)\tilde{v}_{t}(i)\leq-\frac{\delta}{\gamma D_{t}(s,s)},

where the first inequality follows from
\max_{i}\tilde{v}_{t}(i)\geq\sum_{i}P^{a}_{i}\tilde{v}_{t}(i)
and
\min_{i}\tilde{v}_{t}(i)\leq\sum_{i}P^{a^{*}}_{i}\tilde{v}_{t}(i)
. Thus
D_{t}(s,s)\geq\frac{\delta}{\gamma\textrm{sp}(\tilde{v}_{t})}
. We now analyze the properties of the matrix
P^{\prime}_{t}
.

There exists a natural number
N\leq n^{2}-2n+2
such that all entries of
(P^{*})^{N}
are positive, and we denote the minimum of these entries by
\omega
. From the above,

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{t+1}+\gamma E\tilde{v}_{t}=\gamma P^{\prime}_{t}\tilde{v}_{t}.

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{t+1}=\gamma(P^{\prime}_{t}-E)\tilde{v}_{t}.

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\tilde{v}_{N}=\gamma^{N}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{N}(P^{\prime}_{t}-E)\tilde{v}_{0}\right).

We note
\Pi_{t=1}^{N}(P^{\prime}_{t}-E)=\Pi_{t=1}^{N}P^{\prime}_{t}+E^{\prime}
, where
E^{\prime}
has identical rows according to Lemma
4.2
. Considering only the product
\prod_{t=1}^{N}P^{\prime}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pi_{t=1}^{N}P^{\prime}_{t}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{t=1}^{N}\left(D_{t}P^{*}+(I-D_{t})P_{t}\right)\geq\Pi_{t=1}^{N}(D_{t}P^{*})

\displaystyle\geq\Pi_{t=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\delta}{\gamma\textrm{sp}(\tilde{v}_{t})}P^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{N}}{\gamma^{N}\Pi_{t=1}^{N}\textrm{sp}(\tilde{v}_{t})}(P^{*})^{N}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\omega\delta^{N}}{\gamma^{N}\Pi_{t=1}^{N}\textrm{sp}(\tilde{v}_{t})}E

\displaystyle=\phi E,

where
\phi=\frac{\omega\delta^{N}}{\gamma^{N}\Pi_{t=1}^{N}\textrm{sp}(\tilde{v}_{t})}
and
E
is the all-one matrix. Using the trick from
Mustafin et al. 2025b
with normalized differences, we define a stochastic matrix

Q=\frac{\Pi_{t=1}^{N}P^{\prime}_{t}-\phi\mathbbm{1}_{n\times n}}{1-n\phi}.

Then

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{N}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{N}\left(\Pi_{t=1}^{N}P^{\prime}_{t}+E^{\prime}\right)\tilde{v}_{0}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{N}\Pi_{t=1}^{N}P^{\prime}_{t}\tilde{v}_{0}+\gamma^{N}E^{\prime}\tilde{v}_{0}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{N}\left((1-n\phi)Q+\phi\mathbbm{1}_{n\times n}\right)\tilde{v}_{0}+\gamma^{N}E^{\prime}\tilde{v}_{0}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{N}(1-n\phi)Q\tilde{v}_{0}+\gamma^{N}(\phi\mathbbm{1}_{n\times n}+E^{\prime})\tilde{v}_{0}.

Let
\tau=1-n\phi
, then

\displaystyle\gamma^{N}\tau\min_{i}\tilde{v}_{0}(i)\leq\tilde{v}_{N}-\gamma^{N}(\phi\mathbbm{1}_{n\times n}+E^{\prime})\tilde{v}_{0}\leq\gamma^{N}\tau\max_{i}\tilde{v}_{0}(i).

Therefore,
\textrm{sp}(\tilde{v}_{N})=\textrm{sp}(\tilde{v}_{N}-\gamma^{N}(\phi\mathbbm{1}_{n\times n}+E^{\prime})\tilde{v}_{0})\leq\gamma^{N}\tau\textrm{sp}(\tilde{v}_{0})
. By the linearity of span seminorm, substituting
\tilde{v}_{t}=\frac{v_{t}}{C}
yields
\frac{\textrm{sp}(v_{N})}{C}\leq\gamma^{N}\tau\frac{\textrm{sp}(v_{0})}{C}
, which completes the proof of the theorem.

∎

5
Conclusion

We introduced a unified geometric framework for Markov Decision Processes that incorporates the average-reward setting alongside the discounted-reward case. Within this framework, we proved that Value Iteration achieves a geometric convergence rate under the assumption of a unique unichain optimal policy. Future work might relax this assumption and extend the analysis to more general settings, including multichain structures, non-ergodic dynamics, and broader classes of policies.
