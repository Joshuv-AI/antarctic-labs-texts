Scope 2 estimates of carbon dioxide emissions from electricity consumption at the US census block group scale
Authors: Kevin R. Gurney, Pawlok Dass, Anna Kato, Bhaskar Mitra, Modeste Kameni Nematchoua
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This paper introduces and describes a dataset representing United States carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from electricity consumption (scope 2 CO2 emissions) for the 2019–2021 time period separately for the residential, commercial, and industrial sectors. The spatial resolution is the U.S. census block group at annual time resolution. We also provide state-scale aggregate data. Given the increased interest in greenhouse gas mitigation at the urban landscape scale, this data offers the opportunity to better understand the emitting landscape and craft more targeted, efficient policy instruments. The approach starts with well-established estimates of scope 2 CO2 emissions at larger scales and performs a series of downscaling steps to allocate scope 2 emissions into block groups. This open-access scope 2 CO2 emissions dataset and methodology is valuable for a wide range of multidisciplinary studies, including climate science, environmental policy, urban planning, and socioeconomic research.

Background & Summary

The carbon dioxide emissions associated with the generation of electricity accounted for roughly 31% of the total energy-related U.S. carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions in 20221. Electricity consumers have limited independent information, beyond what is supplied by utilities, on how much CO2 is being emitted per amount of purchased electricity. These CO2 emissions are often referred to as “scope 2” emissions2. The information challenge is due, in part, to the fact that accurately identifying the precise mix of power generators that contribute to consumed electricity in a given house or business is difficult to quantify due to limited publicly available data and physical limitations to understanding the flow of electrons through the U.S. power grid.

Having a quantitative understanding of scope 2 CO2 emissions and their spatial distribution regionally or nationally facilitates strategies on CO2 mitigation policy and provides consumers with much-needed information about this component of their overall greenhouse gas (GHG) footprint3. Numerous efforts have quantified scope 1 CO2 emissions (direct emission within a specified geography) at sub-national scales4,5 and hence, sub-national scope 2 CO2 emissions offers further expansion of spatially-explicit GHG information to facilitate a more complete understanding of emissions at scales where decisions are often made.

The most common examples of scope 2 emissions estimation at sub-national are associated with individual companies, consumers, or communities. These are mostly “one-off” estimations and will use either a “location-based” or “market-based” allocation approach6. Market-based approaches reflect individual contracts with a known supplier who also has a known fuel mix (i.e. a single source such as wind or solar). Location-based approaches reflect use of an average emissions intensity (i.e., kgCO2/kWh) for a defined grid.

Few studies, by contrast, have quantified scope 2 CO2 emissions at sub-national spatial scales across larger areas or for multiple consumer groups. Chalendar et al.7, quantified electricity consumption and scope 2 CO2 emissions at the scale of a U.S. balancing authority for the continental U.S.. They quantify these attributes for 66 U.S. balancing authority areas within the U.S. which range in size from roughly a U.S. county to the size of a multistate area. Though a considerable advance in spatial resolution and comprehensiveness, there remains a need to estimate emissions at finer scales to capture both the variation in CO2 emissions intensity and the variation in electricity demand.

Here, we present the Vulcan scope 2 CO2 emissions data product, a partner to the Vulcan scope 1 CO2 emissions project4,8. This study describes the steps used to produce annual scope 2 emissions for the U.S. including Hawaii and Alaska for the 2019 to 2021 time period. The spatial scale is the U.S. census block group (BG) and emissions are separately defined for the residential, commercial, and industrial sectors. We provide assessment of these results relative to samples of utility service territory state-scale estimates.

Methods

The study domain is the United States, the third-largest country in the world (covering approximately 9.8 million square kilometers) with a population of over 333 million people as of 202231 The emission estimates presented here cover 50 states and the District of Columbia but do not include the U.S. territories. The geographic resolution of the data is the U.S. Census block group (BG) and the study period covers the years 2019 to 2021. These have also been aggregated to the state scale.

Input data

The Vulcan-scope 2 carbon dioxide (CO2) emission estimates presented here start with scope 2 CO2 equivalent (CO2-eq) emissions and electricity consumption estimates developed by Chalendar et al.7, for each U.S. Balancing Authority (BA) area. A balancing authority acts as an entity responsible for integrating resource plans ahead of time, maintaining load-interchange-generation balance within a BA, and supporting real-time power interconnection. The input emissions data cover the period July 2018 to the present. The emissions input data uses a grid-balance/MRIO model approach to estimate the mixture of power plants providing electricity to each individual BA. The emission factors used in this calculation vary by season, location and time of day. The scope 2 CO2-eq emissions used by Chalendar et al. do not include emissions associated with the heat component of combined heat and power (CHP) generation and reflect emission factors calculated using IPCC-LCA approach7,9. For further details on the construction of the input data, we refer readers to Calendar et al.7.

We spatially downscale the electricity consumption and scope 2 CO2-eq emissions from the BA-scale to the Census block-group (BG) scale using energy and infrastructure proxies. To do so requires geographical boundaries of both the BGs and the BAs.

The BA geographical boundaries were obtained from the Homeland Infrastructure Foundation Level Database (HIFLD)10. The HIFLD reflects information as of 2019 and provides the geographic boundaries for 72 BAs. The associated metadata includes BA identifiers (integer values assigned by the U.S. Energy Information Administration - EIA), BA names, and other BA-specific information. The BA IDs are essential in order to link the geographical boundaries to the BA electricity consumption and scope 2 emissions input data. The HIFLD includes 72 BAs while the electricity consumption and scope 2 emissions input data includes 68 BAs. The difference occurs due to a BA referred to as “GRID” included in the input emissions data but not included in the HIFLD data. Furthermore, the electricity and scope 2 emissions input data does not include information for five BAs; two BAs in Alaska (AMPL and CEA), one in Hawaii (HECO), and two small BAs in Oregon (CSTO) and New Mexico (GRIS), all of which are included in HIFLD data.

Because BA IDs and acronyms reported to the EIA change over time, the BA ID of the New Harquahala Generating Company (BA ID=55372) is updated to ID = 32790 and the BA acronym of the Turlock Irrigation District (“TID”, BA ID=19281) is updated to “TIDC” to reflect the recorded changes. Note that the “GRID” BA was reported as a “generation-only” BA though Chalander et al.7 assigned scope 2 CO2-eq emissions to this BA. The contribution of the GRID BA to both the scope 2 emissions and electricity consumption is very small, accounting for less than 0.005% of the total. Hence in order to conserve mass, we distributed the electricity consumption and scope 2 CO2-eq emissions to all BGs using the method described in the downscaling section below (i.e., treated as if GRID is covering the entire U.S. land area).

The electricity consumption and scope 2 CO2-eq emissions input data for Alaska and Hawaii are not available. Since these two states are geographically isolated, we assume that the power generated (and its associated emissions) within these states is consumed completely within the state boundaries. Following this assumption, we use the Vulcan-power v1.0 power plant CO2 emission totals for these two states for the BA-scale scope 2 emissions11.

Downscaling

As noted previously, the BA scale scope 2 CO2-eq emissions are downscaled to the target resolution of a U.S. block group (BG). We describe this downscaling method here in terms of the scope 2 CO2-eq emissions but this was also identically done for electricity consumption as this offers a means to assess the output in comparison to other published electricity consumption data. The comparison using the downscaled electricity consumption is described in the Technical Validation section.

Most BGs are within a single BA. However, many BGs are located geographically within more than one BA due to overlap in space of two to four BAs (see Fig. 1). This means that, while a given BG may be served by multiple BAs, a single customer in that BG is purchasing electricity from only one utility within the overlapping BAs. The majority of the Eastern half of the U.S. is served explicitly by one BA, whereas in the Western half of the U.S., there are up to four BAs potentially serving electricity to a particular geographic area (Fig. 1). Most areas of the U.S. are served by one BA (~70%, light grey in Fig. 1) or two (~20%, green in Fig. 1). To perform downscaling while simultaneously ensuring that overlapping BAs do not result in overallocation of scope 2 CO2-eq emissions into a BG, three input data elements are needed: 1) the total building floor area (TFA) in a BG categorized by building type and, 2) electricity energy use intensity (EUI) for that building type in the BG; 3) a simple count of the number of overlapping BAs potentially serving a given BG.

The BG TFA data are obtained from Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) Hazus v5.1 and Hazus v6.0 building inventory data (v5.1 is based on the 2010 U.S. Census; v6.0 is based on the 2020 U.S. Census) at the U.S. census block resolution12 and aggregated to block-groups. Year 2019 uses the 2010 census BG boundary definitions while years 2020 & 2021 use the 2020 census BG boundary definitions (the TFA data was available only for the two Census years). Therefore the TFA for all the intermediate years (2010–2020) were linearly interpolated at the BG scale. The 2020 TFA was used for the year 2021. The electricity EUI data are obtained from the national sample survey collected by the EIA for residential (Residential Building Energy Consumption Survey13–15), commercial (Commercial Buildings Energy Consumption Survey16), and industrial sectors (Manufacturing Energy Consumption Survey17). The EUI values are specific to climate zone, census division, and building type (industrial EUI values are a national average).

Data Records

The complete BG- and state-scale Vulcan-scope 2 CO2-eq emission datasets are published on Zenodo18. The three (one each for 2019, 2020, and 2021) BG-scale files (ELC2.CO2.BG.ann.smplst.mn.<year>.csv) are ascii formatted consisting of 5 columns and 217182 rows (in 2019; 239179 in 2020; 239179 in 2021). The columns contain the following information from left to right: Block group ID – the 12 digit block group identifier; Res CO2 (tC) – the block-group residential fossil fuel CO2-eq emissions in units of metric tons of carbon; Com CO2 (tC) – the block-group commercial fossil fuel CO2-eq emissions in units of metric tons of carbon; Ind CO2 (tC) – the block-group industrial fossil fuel CO2-eq emissions in units of metric tons of carbon. The rows represent each U.S. block-group (217182 BGs in 2019), ordered by the numerical U.S. Census block-group numerical identifier (small to large).

An additional three files (ELC2.CO2.BG.ann.smplst.mn.<year>.gpkg) contain the same content but formatted as geopackage for which an additional column (at end of column sequence) containing the shape (BG) geometry.

Finally, a third set of three files (ELC2.CO2.st.ann.smplst.mn.<year>.csv) contain the state-scale aggregated results in ascii format. The columns are identical to the ELC2.CO2.BG.ann.smplst.mn.<year>.csv files but there are only 51 rows, one for each U.S. state and the District of Columbia.

Technical Validation

Strict technical validation of the Vulcan-scope 2 CO2-eq emissions at the U.S. block group scale is not possible with existing publicly available data. However, there are data comparisons that offer some assessment in the sense that there should be a measure of agreement to reported data. Self-reported utility electricity consumption data as archived by the EIA offers such a comparative assessment19. The EIA reports electricity consumption by utility, sector, and year. This data is compared to the Vulcan electricity consumption data calculated here by aggregating block-groups within (fully contained within or exceeding 50% area within) utility service territories for which the service territory boundaries do not overlap with any other utility service territories (called “isolated utilities”, hereafter). Instances of overlap would make comparison difficult without knowing the precise allocation to consumers. Only survey data from 2019 will be selected for this comparison. 185 utilities satisfied the isolation criteria, as shown in Fig. 3. It is clear that these utilities cannot be considered a geographically representative sample given the clustering across the US landscape. By contrast the industrial sector relative differences exhibit a flat, random distribution. The industrial sector has undoubtedly higher uncertainty in the Vulcan estimate given the fact that the EUI values for industrial buildings is relying on national average values (unlike the residential and commercial EUIs).
