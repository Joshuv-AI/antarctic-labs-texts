Shared Decision making aids in palliative care: A systematic review & meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Joe El Khoury, Rachel Garofalo, Molly M Kilpatrick, Arya B Mohabbat, Maisha T Robinson, Carolyn Mead-Harvey, Zaheera Lukman, Larry J Prokop, Abd Moain Abu Dabrh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Shared Decision Making in palliative care encompassing patient-clinician collaboration has shown potential for improved patient outcomes. However, the impact of decision aids intended to support palliative care decision making discussions across clinical care settings remains uncertain.Aim:To assess the impact of shared of decision aids on decision making, clinical outcomes, and quality of life for patients with palliative care needs and their caregivers.Design:Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, reported according to PRISMA, with overall evidence quality assessed using GRADE.Data sources:Ovid MEDLINE (all segments), EMBASE, Cochrane CENTRAL, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and Scopus, from inception through September 20, 2024, without restrictions on publication date, sample size, or language. Eligible studies were randomized controlled trials that included adult patients with palliative care needs or their caregivers, using any form of decision aids. Outcomes encompassed decision-related, clinical, and quality of life measures.Results:Sixteen randomized controlled trials were included. Participant ages ranged from 44 to 85 years. Predominant decision aids were videos or printed materials addressing goals of care and treatment options, with aims that included improving understanding of prognosis and supporting values clarification. Decision aids significantly improved decision-related knowledge (SMD: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.14–0.67, p = 0.003) and lowered preference for cardiopulmonary resuscitation (OR: 0.28, 95% CI: 0.20–0.40, p < 0.001). Future research should prioritize integration into comprehensive shared decision making frameworks, cultural adaptations, and larger-scale studies to enhance patient-centered outcomes.Keywords: systematic review, decision making, shared, decision aid, goals of care, knowledge

Introduction

Palliative care involves frequent, high-stakes, values-sensitive decisions1,2—often under prognostic uncertainty and sometimes with surrogate involvement3,4—where aligning treatment with what matters most to patients and families is central to quality care.5 –7 Shared decision making has been proposed as an approach to support this alignment.8 –15

Shared decision making refers to a process of sharing information, deliberation, and decision making in a medium where patients and clinicians collaborate to identify care plans that are both medically sound and reflective of the patient’s preferences and values.8 –11 This approach enhances patient engagement, improves adherence, and supports informed treatment decisions.9,12 –15 Evidence indicates that shared decision making leads to better patient outcomes through discussions about options, risks, benefits, and personalized considerations combined with decision-support counseling. 16

Within the past two decades, decision aids have emerged as tools intended to strengthen shared decision making by presenting options in a balanced manner, clarifying potential outcomes, and—ideally—supporting values- and context-sensitive deliberation.11,16 –18 For example, a decision aid for cardiopulmonary resuscitation may compare attempting versus not attempting resuscitation, describe expected benefits and harms, and prompt patients to consider how each option aligns with their goals, whereas an informational handout may simply describe cardiopulmonary resuscitation without explicitly supporting choice deliberation. Decision aids are distinct from general educational materials or clinician counseling; they are designed to prepare patients (and caregivers, when applicable) to participate meaningfully in decision making. Yet in palliative care, decision aids may be used variably—sometimes as standalone materials rather than embedded within real-time clinical conversations—raising uncertainty about their effectiveness across patient-centered outcomes.2,19 –21

The purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to synthesize evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to evaluate the effectiveness of decision aids to manage patients with palliative care needs in any clinical care setting. The review aims to assess their impact on decision making, clinical outcomes, and quality of life measures for patients and their caregivers. Specifically, we evaluated whether decision aids, compared with usual care or alternative informational strategies, improve decision-related outcomes, clinical outcomes, and quality of life for patients with palliative care needs in any clinical care setting. By incorporating evidence from diverse populations, decision aid formats, and settings, the aim of this review is to provide clarity on the role of decision aids in supporting palliative care decision making discussions.

Methods

This meta-analysis followed an a priori protocol as well as reporting standards according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 22 statement and Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations guidelines (GRADE).23,24 The review was designed to address the following question: among adults receiving palliative care (or their caregivers), what is the effect of decision aids, compared with usual care or alternative informational approaches, on decision-related outcomes, clinical outcomes, and quality-of-life measures? The protocol was developed prior to study initiation and guided eligibility criteria, outcomes of interest, and analytic methods. The protocol was not registered in PROSPERO; however, all methods were specified a priori and adhered to throughout the review process (Appendix 1).

Search strategy and study eligibility

A medical librarian collaborated with the study’s principal investigator to design and conduct a comprehensive search strategy. The search included several databases from their inception through December 20, 2024, without restrictions on publication date, sample size, or language. Controlled vocabulary terms were supplemented with relevant keywords to identify studies of decision aids or decision-support tools in palliative or serious illness care to support shared decision making. The search strategy included three core concepts: (1) serious illness/palliative or end-of-life care, including hospice, goals of care, and advance care planning; (2) decision aids or decision-support tools; and (3) randomized or controlled study designs, systematic reviews, or meta-analyses. Synonymous terms within each concept were combined with OR, and the three concepts were combined with AND. Databases searched included Ovid MEDLINE Epub Ahead of Print, Ovid Medline In-Process & Other Non-Indexed Citations, Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid EMBASE, Ovid Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Ovid Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and Scopus. The full search strategy for Ovid MEDLINE is provided in Appendix 2.

The review focused exclusively on RCTs. A preliminary scoping search identified sufficient randomized controlled trial evidence to justify restricting the review to RCTs, as this design allows the most rigorous evaluation of the comparative effectiveness and causal impact of decision aids on patient-centered outcomes while minimizing confounding. Eligibility criteria were predefined according to the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome (PICO) framework and are summarized in Table 1.

Eligibility criteria for study inclusion.

Eligible interventions were those explicitly designated as “decision aids” or “decision tools” in any format (e.g. paper, electronic, or hybrid). For the purposes of this review, decision aids were defined as structured tools or media intended to support deliberation by presenting at least two healthcare options and their potential benefits and harms, with or without explicit values clarification components, and designed to prepare patients or caregivers to participate in shared decision making. These decision aids aimed to facilitate decision making by supporting patient-clinician discussions, providing information on risks and benefits of treatment options, or incorporating patient values and preferences into care planning. During screening, we did not rely solely on the terminology used by study authors. Each intervention was assessed against our operational definition of a decision aid. Tools were considered decision aids if they supported an actual healthcare decision by presenting options and their benefits/harms and, when applicable, facilitating values clarification or deliberation. Materials that only provided general disease or treatment information without supporting option comparison or choice deliberation were considered informational tools rather than decision aids. Multicomponent interventions were eligible only when they included a patient- or caregiver-facing decision aid component.

Outcomes of interest

Outcomes of interest were prespecified a priori. Eligible studies were required to report at least one of the following outcome types: (1) decision-related outcomes (including, but not limited to, decision process; disease or treatment knowledge, understanding, or preference; decision-aid acceptance or understanding; decisional conflict; satisfaction with decision, consultation, or decision-aid; decision regret; preference for decision involvement or information; encounter time, barriers, and facilitators; clinician satisfaction); (2) clinical outcomes (including, but not limited to, worsened or improved symptoms; anxiety levels; depression; hospitalizations or emergency department visits); and/or (3) quality of life-related outcomes (use of specific quality of life scales).

Data screening and extraction

Data screening and extraction were conducted independently and in duplicate by two reviewers working blindly to each other’s assessments.

Data extraction

For eligible studies, reviewers extracted data on patient demographics (e.g. sample size, age, race), baseline characteristics, study design, and relevant outcomes of interest. Discrepancies in extracted data were resolved through consensus or adjudication by a senior investigator (AMAD). Risk of bias assessment and quality of evidence

The risk of bias or individual study quality was evaluated by two reviewers independently using the Cochrane risk of bias tool. 25 Each article was rated as “high,” “unclear,” or “low” for performance, attrition, and reporting bias from each of six domains (random sequence generation; allocation concealment; blinding of participants and personnel; blinding of outcome assessment; incomplete outcome data, and selective reporting). The strength of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.23,24

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of outcomes at follow-up between decision aid and control groups were made using a random-effects meta-analysis for continuous outcome variables. 26 Because similar outcome domains (e.g. knowledge, decisional conflict, anxiety, quality of life) were assessed using different measurement instruments across studies (see Table 2), effect sizes were pooled using standardized mean differences (SMDs) and were estimated (using Hedges’ g) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each continuous outcome. Odds ratios (OR) with CIs were estimated for each binary outcome. Between-study heterogeneity was examined by estimating the I2 statistic, which measures the proportion of variation between studies due to heterogeneity beyond chance. 27 After applying a Bonferroni correction for multiple testing for the nine outcome measures, p-values <0.005 were considered as statistically significant. Comparators varied between usual care and active informational controls; however, data were insufficient to support formal subgroup analyses by comparator type. All statistical tests were two-sided. Statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Systematic review findings

The initial search strategy yielded 3561 citations, of which 16 RCTs28 –43 were deemed to be eligible and included in the final analysis as shown in the study search and selection flowchart (Figure 1). The number of randomized participants in the included studies ranged from 51 to 743, with a patient or surrogate age ranging from 44 to 85 years. Follow-up periods varied between same day and 12 months post-intervention. The studies encompassed diverse settings and geographic locations: of the 16 included studies, there were 10 trials conducted in the USA29 –33,35 –37,40,42; two in the Netherlands38,43; one study in Japan 39 ; one in Korea 41 ; one in Saudi Arabia 28 ; and one multisite study in Australia and Canada. 34

Flowchart of the study search and eligibility.

Of the 16 studies, 12 enrolled patients exclusively28 –32,34,37 –40,42,43; one enrolled both patients and caregivers/surrogates 35 ; and three enrolled only caregivers or surrogates.33,36,41 Most decision aids used videos and/or printed materials.28 –35,38 –41,43 Four studies34,36,42,43 actively supported shared decision making by facilitating bidirectional patient-clinician communication. The remaining studies provided decision aids for independent review, without structured shared decision making documentation. The included interventions were grouped into three broad typologies: (1) standalone patient- or caregiver-facing decision aids, most commonly videos and/or printed materials presenting options and benefits/harms; (2) clinician-facilitated decision aids embedded within structured shared decision making encounters, including multicomponent interventions that combined clinician training with patient-facing video or pamphlet tools; and (3) digital or online communication aids designed to prepare patients for consultation.

For decision aids formats, 13 studies used videos and/or printed materials (e.g. booklets)28 –35,38 –41,43; two studies combined physician training with a video and pamphlet decision aid36,42; and one study directed patients to an online communication aid, 37 as shown in Table 2. Comparators included usual care, verbal information alone, or standard clinician-led discussions without structured decision-support tools. Most study interventions were conducted in hospital or clinic settings and involved the presence of a clinician.

Reported decision making related outcomes included knowledge and understanding, treatment preferences, and decisional conflict. Clinical outcomes, such as anxiety, stress, and depression, were also frequently reported. Quality-of-life measures, though less common, were also assessed.

Baseline characteristics and main findings of the studies are detailed in Table 2. Risk of bias was generally moderate (Appendix 3), and overall evidence quality was graded as low, mainly due to indirectness and inconsistency.

Discussion

Main findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the impact of decision aids intended to support palliative care decision making discussions within clinical care settings. Findings suggest that while decision aids significantly improve patient knowledge and reduce stated cardiopulmonary resuscitation preference, they have limited impact on other decision-related outcomes, such as decisional conflict, satisfaction, or regret. The quality of evidence was rated generally low due to indirectness (i.e. reflecting variability across studies in patient populations, decision aid formats, clinical contexts, outcome measures, and the extent to which interventions reflected real-world shared decision making processes) and inconsistency (i.e. reflecting heterogeneity in effect estimates across trials).

These findings reinforce prior evidence that decision aids—while effective in improving knowledge—are only one component of shared decision making, and may be insufficient on their own to address the complex, emotional, and relational dimensions of serious illness decision making.11,16,17,43,44 In this review, decision aids were associated with significant improvements in knowledge and reduced preference for cardiopulmonary resuscitation, yet no consistent effects were observed for decisional conflict, satisfaction, or regret—outcomes that are more closely tied to values clarification, clinician–patient interaction, and emotional support.

For example, visual tools depicting cardiopulmonary resuscitation have been associated with changes in stated treatment preferences, including lower preference for cardiopulmonary resuscitation, likely by improving understanding of the intervention, its likely outcomes, and its potential burdens; However, such tools must be designed and implemented carefully to minimize framing effects and support voluntary, informed decision making rather than directing patients toward a particular choice.31,40 This improved understanding supports informed consent and facilitates deeper discussions about prognosis, trade-offs, and treatment goals.1,2,29 However, most included trials deployed decision aids as standalone interventions, with only a minority explicitly integrating them into clinician-facilitated shared decision making processes, which may help explain the limited impact observed on relational and affective outcomes. Rather than limiting shared decision making use to situations of clinical equipoise, the emphasis should be on how well these tools are implemented to support authentic patient engagement across care settings. 45

The central role of communication

Knowledge alone does not ensure values-congruent choices. Decisional conflict, regret, and satisfaction are shaped by relational and emotional factors—elements that static tools may not fully address.8,11,19,46 Tools that were not embedded in bidirectional, clinician-facilitated workflows showed minimal impact on these outcomes. In contrast, studies that combined decision aids with structured communication—such as clinician-guided values elicitation, shared agenda setting, iterative dialogue, or decision coaching—demonstrated better alignment with care preferences and greater patient satisfaction.34,36 These interventions allowed clinicians to contextualize information, address emotional concerns, and help patients deliberate in accordance with their values. Thus, decision aids should be integrated into shared decision making conversations—not used in isolation.9,10 Yet, despite professional advocacy, shared decision making is often inconsistently implemented as part of palliative care discussions due to systemic barriers and clinician discomfort with prognosis discussions, which can undercut the intended benefits of decision aids. 47

Importantly, decision aids did not increase anxiety or depression, supporting their emotional safety even in sensitive contexts.28,41 Still, reducing psychological distress likely requires adjunctive support, such as coaching or structured debriefs.48,49 Embedding decision aids into ongoing relational conversations, rather than one-off interventions, may provide the continuity and support patients need to process complex emotions over time. 50 The lack of improvement in values clarity points to gaps in either decision aid design or how these tools are implemented. Tools aligned with International Patient Decision Aid Standards (IPDAS) 51 that incorporate values clarification are more likely to support patient-centered decisions.16,48 Clinicians should fill these gaps by explicitly inviting patients to reflect on their hopes, fears, and factors that matter most. Without guided reflection or follow-up, patients may struggle to connect the information presented in decision aids to their own priorities. Thus, well-designed tools may fall short unless they are embedded within supportive communication that actively engages patients in clarifying their values.

Implementation gaps and the need for cultural tailoring

Most included studies deployed decision aids for independent use with minimal follow-up. Only a few incorporated meaningful, two-way communication. This likely explains the limited effect on broader outcomes like decisional conflict or satisfaction.9,10 Training clinicians to respond effectively to what patients learn from decision aids—and to deepen the conversation beyond factual comprehension—is essential. This includes developing professional competencies in recognizing and responding to patient cues, using structured follow-up questions (e.g. “What concerns you most about this decision?”), and incorporating patient-reported goals and values into the care plan. 49 Decision aids must be complemented by deliberate clinician behaviors—such as eliciting goals, exploring trade-offs, and clarifying values—if they are to fulfill their role in shared decision making.45,47

Lastly, decision aids, particularly in palliative care discussions, must reflect the social and cultural realities of care.10,14,16,44 Most trials were based in high-income, Western contexts and assumed individual decision making models. Yet many patients— especially in collectivist cultures— make decisions in partnership with family or spiritual advisors. Equity-oriented shared decision making approaches should prioritize cultural responsiveness and ensure tools are co-designed with communities to avoid perpetuating structural inequities. 52 Cultural tailoring may include involving family caregivers in the design process, offering multilingual formats, and framing decisions in ways that respect spiritual and cultural beliefs/values. Future tools should be designed for and tested in diverse populations and real-world care settings.21,44 Technologies like patient-reported outcome dashboards may help identify evolving symptoms and priorities to guide timely, values-aligned decisions 49 —especially in the face of declining decision making capacity.6,7,49

Limitations and future directions

This review is limited by substantial heterogeneity in study designs, outcome measures, intervention characteristics, and participant demographics, which complicate cross-study comparisons and limit the precision of pooled estimates. Decision aids varied widely in format, content, and degree of integration into clinical encounters, and similar outcome domains (e.g. knowledge, decisional conflict, quality of life) were assessed using different measurement instruments across trials. Most included studies were conducted in high-income settings with limited cultural diversity, reducing generalizability and transferability to more diverse populations and care models. Additionally, many studies provided only short-term follow-up, limiting insights into long-term outcomes such as patient empowerment, caregiver burden, downstream care utilization, and sustained alignment of care with patient values. The overall low quality of evidence, influenced by indirectness and inconsistency—reflecting variability in populations, interventions, implementation contexts, and heterogeneity in effect estimates—further constrains the strength and transferability of results.

Nonetheless, this review is strengthened by an a priori protocol, comprehensive search strategy, and quantitative synthesis across a range of decision-related outcomes with methodologically rigorous rating of evidence.24,25 The results remain valuable in illustrating current gaps and offering a roadmap for improving decision aid implementation in palliative care. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating decision aids specifically tailored to the nuanced demands of palliative care. This includes designing culturally sensitive decision aids that acknowledge family involvement in decision making, training clinicians to facilitate emotionally supportive dialogues, and incorporating patient values and patient-reported outcome data to guide real-time decisions, while acknowledging the diversity of shared decision making approaches that can be leveraged.53,54 Long-term studies and mixed-methods approaches through validated frameworks should be employed to understand both the quantitative impacts and the qualitative experiences of patients, caregivers, and clinicians using decision aids in palliative care.55 –59 Ultimately, improving decision aid implementation and integration into shared decision making frameworks may support patient- and family-centered care that respects individual values, goals, and the lived context of serious illness and end-of-life care.2,44,46

Conclusions

This systematic review highlights limited and generally low-quality evidence regarding the impact of including decision aids in palliative care discussions in diverse clinical settings. Decision aids were associated with improved knowledge and changes in stated treatment preferences, including lower preference for cardiopulmonary resuscitation, but these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of superior decision quality or values-concordant care. Their standalone use may be insufficient to support the emotional, relational, and contextual work of serious illness decision making. Future research should focus on co-designed, culturally tailored decision aids that minimize framing effects, support voluntary informed decision making, and are embedded within clinician-facilitated shared decision making workflows. Mixed-methods and real-world studies are needed to examine how decision aids shape understanding, deliberation, values clarification, patient experience, and goal-concordant care for patients and their care partners.

Protocol: Decision aids for shared decision making in palliative care

Background

Palliative care involves complex chronic medical care that encompasses high-load of information presenting advanced-care life decision, treatment plans, medication choice, advanced directives, and side-effects management. In addition to that, this process often accompanied with emotional, financial and social dimensions. Because patients are faced with these various challenges, the presence of decision aids that may guide them to make an informed and shared decision are valuable and helpful in shouldering all challenges they face while continuing to live their lives with the best quality possible.

Objective

We aim in this review to synthesize the evidence about the use of decision aids on different outcomes for patients with palliative care needs and/or their caregivers.

PICO 1

Population

Patients with palliative care needs using decision making aids or tools in any clinical setting (no simulation). We will include all patients age; in cases where the patient may not have agency to make an informed decision (e.g. young pediatrics/elderly with advanced dementia), the caregiver will be considered the decision-maker. Patients in eligible studies will need to be facing an actual decision of their care and lives.

Intervention

We will include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or systematic reviews and meta-analyses that included RCTs that evaluate any intervention aiming to improve decision making between clinicians and patients or their caregivers. We will include any decision making intervention type that aimed on informing patient decisions or focused on informed consent, reported information exchange between clinicians and patients encompassing the discussion of risks and benefits of treatment options as well as values and preferences.

Usual care or any modified comparable intervention
