Blowing-up solutions to competitive critical systems in dimension 3
Antonio J. Fernández, Maria Medina, Angela Pistoia

Abstract
. We study the critical system of m ⩾ 2 m\geqslant 2 equations − Δ u i = u i 5 + ∑ j = 1 , j ≠ i m β i ​ j u i 2 u j 3 , u i ≩ 0 in ℝ 3 , i ∈ { 1 , … , m } , -\Delta u_{i}=u_{i}^{5}+\sum_{j=1,\,j\neq i}^{m}\beta_{ij}u_{i}^{2}u_{j}^{3}\,,\quad u_{i}\gneqq 0\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\,, where β κ ​ ℓ = α ∈ ℝ \beta_{\kappa\ell}=\alpha\in\mathbb{R} if κ ≠ ℓ \kappa\neq\ell , and β ℓ ​ m = β m ​ κ = β < 0 \beta_{\ell m}=\beta_{m\kappa}=\beta<0 , for κ , ℓ ∈ { 1 , … , m − 1 } \kappa,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\} . We construct solutions to this system in the case where β → − ∞ \beta\to-\infty by means of a Ljapunov-Schmidt reduction argument. This allows us to identify the explicit form of the solution at main order: u 1 u_{1} will look like a perturbation of the standard radial positive solution to the Yamabe equation, while u 2 u_{2} will blow-up at the k k vertices of a regular planar polygon. The solutions to the other equations will replicate the blowing-up structure under an appropriate rotation that ensures u i ≠ u j u_{i}\neq u_{j} for i ≠ j i\neq j . The result provides the first almost-explicit example of non-synchronized solutions to competitive critical systems in dimension 3.

1.
Introduction

Let us consider the
critical
system

(1.1)

-\Delta u_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{ij}u_{i}^{2}u_{j}^{3}\,,\quad u_{i}\gneqq 0\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\,,

where
m\geqslant 2
is an integer and
\beta_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. This kind of systems naturally arise when looking for standing waves in critical systems of Schrödinger equations, see
e.g.
[
1
,
25
]
. From the physical point of view,
\beta_{ii}
discriminates between focusing and defocusing behavior of a single component
u_{i}
, and
\beta_{ij}
,
i\neq j
, describes the interspecies forces between particles of different components
u_{i}
and
u_{j}
. If
\beta_{ij}<0
the particles are in competition and repel each other, and if
\beta_{ij}>0
they cooperate and attract each other.

In this paper we are concerned with the focusing case, namely
\beta_{ii}>0
.
In some cases, it is straightforward to find solutions to this system. Indeed, let us introduce the function

(1.2)

U(x):=\frac{3^{\frac{1}{4}}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,,

and recall that all the
positive
solutions to the
Yamabe
equation

(1.3)

-\Delta u=u^{5}\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,

are given by

(1.4)

U_{\delta,\xi}(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}\,U\Big(\frac{x-\xi}{\delta}\Big)=\frac{3^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\delta}}{(\delta^{2}+|x-\xi|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,,\quad\textup{for }\delta>0\,,\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.

Then, setting
u_{i}:=s_{i}\,U_{\delta,\xi}
for some
\delta>0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, system (
1.1
) reduces to the algebraic system

s_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{ij}s_{i}^{2}s_{j}^{3}\,,\quad s_{i}>0\,,\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\,.

Whenever one is able to solve this algebraic system, one can then obtain solutions to (
1.1
). We refer to this kind of solutions as
synchronized
solutions. We are interested in the existence (or not) of finite energy
non–synchronized
solutions to (
1.1
).

Let us turn first to the case where
m=2
. Assuming
\beta_{11}=\beta_{22}=1
and
\beta_{12}=\beta_{21}=\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, system (
1.1
) reduces to

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u&=u^{5}+\beta u^{2}v^{3}\quad&&\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\\
-\Delta v&=v^{5}+\beta u^{3}v^{2}\quad&&\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\end{aligned}\right.\qquad u,v\gneqq 0\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.

In the attractive regime, i.e. when
\beta>0
, Guo and Liu proved in
[
14
, Theorem 3.2]
that any solution to (
1.5
) is a synchronized solution. In the repulsive regime, i.e. when
\beta<0
, the situation is however different and non-synchronized solutions do exist. In this case, there is a strong connection between solutions to (
1.5
) and sign-changing solutions to (
1.3
).
This was first pointed out in a series of papers by Terracini and her collaborators
[
8
,
18
,
21
,
23
,
24
]
. In particular, in
[
21
, Theorem 1.5]
, the authors proved that, if
(u_{\beta},v_{\beta})_{\beta}
is a family of solutions to (
1.5
) uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, then a segregation phenomenon happens. More precisely, for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, it follows that, up to a subsequence,

u_{\beta}\to w_{+}\quad\hbox{and}\quad v_{\beta}\to w_{-}\quad\hbox{in }H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap C^{0,\alpha}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,,\quad\textup{as }\beta\to-\infty\,.

Here,
w
is a sign-changing solution to (
1.3
) and, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
s_{+}:=\max\{s,0\}
and
s_{-}:=\max\{-s,0\}
. Having in mind this connection, it is natural to wonder if, whenever there exists a sign-changing solution
w
to (
1.3
), it is possible to find a solution to (
1.5
) whose components resemble the positive and negative part of
w
, as
\beta\to-\infty.

The first result in this direction was obtained by Clapp and Pistoia
[
5
]
using variational methods. More precisely, the authors adapted an argument by Ding
[
11
]
, where he established the existence of infinitely many sign-changing solutions to (
1.3
), and proved the existence of solutions to (
1.5
) exhibiting a segregation phenomenon as
\beta\to-\infty
. Let us point out that, aside from Ding’s sign-changing solutions to (
1.3
), there are many others built using perturbative methods
[
15
,
9
]
. At main order, the sign-changing solutions to (
1.3
) constructed by del Pino et al.
[
9
]
are given by

w_{k}=U-\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{\delta_{k},\,\xi_{j,k}}\,,

where
k
is a sufficiently large integer, the points
\xi_{j,k}
are the vertices of a regular polygon placed on a great circle on the
(x_{1},x_{2})
-plane, and
\delta_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. It seems then natural to attempt a similar construction in order to build solutions to (
1.5
). More precisely, one may try to construct solutions to (
1.5
) whose components resemble respectively
U
and
\sum_{j}U_{\delta_{k},\,\xi_{j,k}}
. A first attempt in this direction is due to Guo et al.
[
13
]
. Unfortunately, the proof contains a gap, and their construction does not seem to be feasible. In
[
13
, Proposition 2.3]
, the authors claim to be able to invert the corresponding linearized operator (the analogue to Proposition
2.4
in this work) as a consequence of the symmetries of the construction, without the need of projecting onto any space. However, this is not the case. The chain of identities in
[
13
, Equation (2.25)]
does not hold, and they cannot conclude the proof of
[
13
, Proposition 2.3]
.

Concerning the case where
m\geqslant 3
, there are very few results in the literature. The first in this direction was obtained by Clapp et al.
[
7
]
. There, the authors were able to generalize the results obtained by Clapp and Pistoia
[
5
]
to systems with
m\geqslant 2
components. Let us also mention the very recent result by Clapp et al.
[
6
]
for systems of Yamabe type equations on closed Riemannian manifolds. In both papers
[
7
,
6
]
the solutions do exhibit a segregation phenomenon as the competition parameters go to
-\infty
.

In this paper we construct a new type of solutions to (
1.1
) which are non-synchronized and whose components do not exhibit a segregation phenomenon in a large repulsive regime. Indeed, let us denote by

(1.6)

{\mathscr{R}}_{\theta}:=\left(\begin{matrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\
0&0&1\\
\end{matrix}\right),\quad\theta\in\mathbb{R}\,,

the rotation matrices on the
(x_{1},x_{2})
-plane. Also note that, as usual, the space
\dot{H}^{1}:=\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
denotes the completion of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with respect to the norm

\|\phi\|:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla\phi|^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,\quad\textup{for all }\phi\in\dot{H}^{1}\,.

We can prove the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that

(1.7)

\beta_{ii}=\beta_{mm}=1,\quad\beta_{ij}=\alpha\quad\hbox{and}\quad\beta_{\ell m}=\beta_{m\kappa}=\beta\quad\hbox{for }\ i,j,\kappa,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}\,,\ i\neq j\,,

for certain
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
\beta<0
. Then, for any fixed integer
k\geqslant 2
, there exists
\beta_{\star}<-\sqrt{2}
such that, for each
\beta\in(-\infty,\beta_{\star}]
, (
1.1
) has a finite energy solution
(u_{1,\beta},u_{2,\beta},\ldots,u_{m,\beta})
of the form

u_{1,\beta}=U+\phi_{\beta}\,,\quad u_{2,\beta}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,U\Big(\frac{\,\cdot\,-\xi_{\beta,j}}{\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}}\Big)+\psi_{\beta}\,,\quad u_{i,\beta}=u_{2,\beta}\Big({\mathscr{R}}_{\frac{(i-2)}{m-1}\frac{2\pi}{k}}\cdot\Big)\quad\textup{for }i\in\{3,\ldots,m\}\,.

Here,
U
is defined in (
1.2
),
{\mathscr{R}}_{\frac{(i-2)}{m-1}\frac{2\pi}{k}}
is given by (
1.6
),
\phi_{\beta},\psi_{\beta}\in\dot{H}^{1}
and
(\xi_{\beta,j})_{j}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfy

\|\phi_{\beta}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}+\|\psi_{\beta}\|_{\dot{H}^{1}}\to 0\quad\textup{ and }\quad\xi_{\beta,j}\to\Big({\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{2\pi(j-1)}{k}i},0\Big)\,,\quad\textup{as }\beta\to-\infty\,,

and
\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}\in(0,1/{\mathrm{e}}^{2})
satisfies

\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}|\log\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}|\sim-\frac{1}{\beta}\,.

It is worth pointing out that the solutions constructed in Theorem
1.1
are not uniformly bounded as
\beta\to-\infty
. Indeed, the
i-
th components for any
i\in\{2,\dots,m\}
blow-up at the
k
vertices of a regular planar polygon. Even more, these solutions do not exhibit a segregation phenomenon as
\beta\to-\infty
. In fact, the limiting profile of the first component is
U
(see (
1.4
)), which is positive everywhere, and the limiting profile of all the other components is a sum of Dirac delta masses at the
k
vertices of a regular planar polygon. This shows that the segregation result
[
21
, Theorem 1.5]
is somewhat sharp.

Let us also emphasize the fact that the presence of the parameter
\beta
is what allows us to keep
k
fixed. As it will be clear, the strategy used in the manuscript relies on a reduction procedure, where an equlibrium must be found. This is done by adjusting a free parameter, which happens to be
\beta
. This is in big contrast with the case of the single equation, treated in
[
9
,
10
]
. There, the absence of a natural parameter in the problem forces the authors to
artificially
introduce one, which is the number of peaks
k
. It is worth noting that the use of the number of peaks as a parameter, first introduced in
[
26
]
, has in fact been a winning choice in several problems.

1.1.
Strategy of the proof

Theorem
1.1
relies on a classical Ljapunov-Schmidt reduction argument. For simplicity, we first discuss the proof in the case where
m=2
and then explain how to reduce the general case
m\geqslant 3
to a different
nonlocal
system of two components, which can be treated in a similar fashion.

We start of with an approximated solution of the form

(1.8)

(U,V)=\bigg(U,\ \sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{\tilde{\delta}_{\beta},\xi_{\beta,j}}\bigg)\,,

where
k\geqslant 2
is a fixed integer and the coupling parameter
\beta\to-\infty
is a free term in the reduction process. The gist of the proof is to show that, for some
\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}
, there exists a true solution to (
1.5
) of the form

(u,v)=(U+\phi_{\beta},V+\psi_{\beta})

with
\phi_{\beta},\psi_{\beta}\to 0
in
\dot{H}^{1}
, as
\beta\to-\infty
. We will address the problem by linearizing around
(U,V)
and solving the corresponding system for
(\phi_{\beta},\psi_{\beta})
. It turns out that this is possible if the concentration parameter
\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}
is suitably chosen.

The choice of
\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}
becomes apparent in the study of the reduced energy (
2.47
). At main order, one needs to balance the interaction among
U
and
V
, which is of order

-\beta\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}^{\frac{3}{2}}|\log\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}|\,,

and the interaction among the different bubbles in
V
, which is of order

-\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}\,.

This balance provides the rate

\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}|\log\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}|\sim-\frac{1}{\beta}\,,

which is rather slow, but sufficient for our purposes. This is something special of the three-dimensional case we are dealing with.

It is folklore that the existence of blowing-up solutions in critical problems is strongly affected by the dimension. For instance, given a smooth bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 4
, and a parameter
\varepsilon>0
, the celebrated Brezis-Nirenberg problem
[
3
]

(1.9)

-\Delta u=|u|^{4\over n-2}u+\varepsilon u\quad\hbox{in }\Omega\,,\quad u=0\quad\hbox{on }\partial\Omega\,,

possesses positive and sign-changing solutions which blow-up as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
(see e.g.
[
20
,
17
,
16
]
).
On the other hand, if
n=3
and
\Omega
is star-shaped, the problem does not have any positive solutions when
\varepsilon>0
is small enough
[
3
]
. Moreover, the existence (or not) of sign-changing solutions to (
1.9
) with
\varepsilon>0
small is a challenging open problem

To elucidate more the role of the dimension in our construction, let us compare our result with the one by Chen et al.
[
4
]
. To that end, let us consider the critical system in general dimension, which reads as:

(1.10)

-\Delta u_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{ij}u_{i}^{\frac{2}{n-2}}u_{j}^{\frac{n}{n-2}}\,,\quad u_{i}\gneqq 0\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\,,\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}.

First, let us stress that, when all the coupling parameters are negative, solutions exhibiting segregation were found in
[
7
,
5
]
using variational methods. On the other hand, for
n=4
, Druet and Hebey proved in
[
12
, Proposition 3.1]
that any non-trivial solution to (
1.10
) is a synchronized solution if all the
\beta_{ij}
are positive and equal. Here, we want to address the attention to the construction in
[
4
]
, that considers again the case
n=4
. There, the authors built solutions to (
1.10
) in a small competitive regime using a Ljapunov-Schmidt reduction argument. More precisely, assuming (
1.7
), the authors built solutions to (
1.10
) for
\beta<0
with
|\beta|
sufficiently small. In the case where
m=2
, the first component of their solutions looks like
U
and the second one like
V
(see (
1.8
)), so our ansatz is very similar to theirs. However, in our case
\beta\to-\infty
, while in theirs
\beta\to 0^{-}
. If one tries to mimic our computations in the four-dimensional case when
\beta\to-\infty
, they would obtain a rate
|\log\tilde{\delta}_{\beta}|\sim-\frac{1}{\beta}
, which is too slow for the reduction method to succeed. This striking difference is a purely dimensional phenomenon, which highlights one more time the role of the dimension in the existence of blowing-up solutions in critical problems. Actually, the same is expected to hold in dimensions
n\geqslant 5
. Formal computations show that in high dimensions the reduction process also forces
\beta\to 0^{-}
. However, this case is tricky because of the nature of the interaction term. The first power becomes sublinear, and hence no longer contractive. This prevents us to apply fixed point-type arguments in a direct way as in the cases
n=3,4
. The problem is being treated in an ongoing work, where we expect to overcome this technical difficulty.

We now explain how to reduce the case
m\geqslant 3
to a
nonlocal
system with only two components. Let
{\mathscr{R}}_{\theta}
with
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
be given in (
1.6
) and
r,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Denote
q=m-1
. We set

(1.11)

{\mathscr{R}}_{r,k}:={\mathscr{R}}_{\frac{(r-1)}{q}\frac{2\pi}{k}}\,,

and consider the
nonlocal
system

(1.12)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta u=u^{5}+\beta u^{2}v^{3}+\beta u^{2}\sum\limits_{r=2}^{q}v_{r}^{3}\quad&&\hbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\\
&-\Delta v=v^{5}+\beta u^{3}v^{2}+\alpha v^{2}\sum\limits_{r=2}^{q}v_{r}^{3}\quad&&\hbox{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\qquad u,v\gneqq 0\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,

where

\displaystyle v_{r}(x):=v({\mathscr{R}}_{r,k}x)\quad\hbox{for }r\in\{1,\dots,q\}\,.

Then, using (
1.7
), one can check that, if
(u,v)
is a solution to (
1.12
) and satisfies

u(x)=u({\mathscr{R}}_{r,k}x)\quad\hbox{for all }r\in\{2,\dots,q\}\,,\quad\hbox{and}\quad v(x)=v(\mathscr{R}_{\frac{2\pi}{k}}x)\,,

the vector
(u_{1},\dots,u_{q+1})
, with

\displaystyle u_{i}(x)=v({\mathscr{R}}_{i,k}x)\quad\hbox{for all}\ i\in\{1,\dots,q\}\,,\quad\hbox{and}\quad u_{q+1}(x)=u(x)\,,

is a solution to (
1.1
). We refer to Sect.
3
for more details. Let us stress that the assumption (
1.7
) is crucial in the reduction. If we remove it, the reduction cannot be done. The key fact here is that the nonlocal terms appearing in (
1.12
) are of lower order with respect to the ones already present in (
1.5
). Using the symmetries above, we can then deal with (
1.12
) in a similar fashion as we did with (
1.1
). In particular, this allows the parameter
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
to be freely chosen.

This reduction is inspired by the one in
[
4
, Sect. 3]
. However, the symmetries we are considering are different from the ones there. In particular, we do not need to impose that
k
is even as they did. Actually, we strongly believe that our approach can lead to the construction of two new different families of solutions in the four-dimensional case when
\beta\to 0^{-}
. Moreover, this would allow to treat the case where
k
is odd and would substantially simplify the proofs in
[
4
]
.

.

x_{3}
, i.e.

(2.8)

\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\phi(x_{1},-x_{2},x_{3})=\phi(x_{1},x_{2},-x_{3})\,,\quad\textup{for all }(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.

•

Every
\phi\in X_{k}
is invariant under rotation of
2\pi/k
in the
x_{1},x_{2}
-variables, i.e.

(2.9)

\phi(r{\mathrm{e}}^{i\theta},x_{3})=\phi(r{\mathrm{e}}^{(\theta+\frac{2\pi}{k})i},x_{3})\,,\quad\textup{for all }\theta,x_{3}\in\mathbb{R}\textup{ and }r>0\,.

•

Every
\phi\in X_{k}
is invariant under the action of the Kelvin transform, i.e.

(2.10)

\phi(x)=\frac{1}{|x|}\phi\Big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\Big)\,,\quad\textup{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}\,.

In other words, we define

(2.11)

X_{k}:=\big\{\phi\in\dot{H}^{1}:\phi\textup{ satisfies \eqref{E.invarianceEven}, \eqref{E.invarianceRotation} and \eqref{E.invarianceKelvin}}\big\}\,.

Note that
X_{k}
is a Hilbert space endowed with the same scalar product and the same norm as
\dot{H}^{1}
.
By Sobolev inequality, we have that the embedding
\dot{H}^{1}\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is continuous. Hence we can define, via the Riesz representation theorem, the continuous operator

(-\Delta)^{-1}:L^{\frac{6}{5}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\to\dot{H}^{1}\,,\quad f\mapsto u\,,

where
u\in\dot{H}^{1}
is the unique solution to

-\Delta u=f\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.

Thus, we can reformulate (
2.2
) as

(2.12)

\left\{\begin{aligned} u&=(-\Delta)^{-1}(u_{+}^{5}+\beta u_{+}^{2}v_{+}^{3})\quad&&\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\\
v&=(-\Delta)^{-1}(v_{+}^{5}+\beta u_{+}^{3}v_{+}^{2})\quad&&\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\end{aligned}\right.

and we will look for a solution to (
2.12
) of the form

(2.13)

u=U+\phi\,,\quad v=V+\psi\,,

with
\phi,\,\psi\in X_{k}
sufficiently small. It is worth emphasizing that, if
u,v\in X_{k}
, then also the right hand side in (
2.12
) belongs to
X_{k}
. In particular, let us point out that

\widetilde{\phi}(x):=\frac{1}{|x|}\phi\Big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\Big)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta\phi(y)=\frac{1}{|y|^{5}}\,\Delta\widetilde{\phi}\Big(\frac{y}{|y|^{2}}\Big)\,.

In terms of the functions
\phi
and
\psi
we can rewrite (
2.2
) as

(2.14)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{1}\phi&={\mathcal{E}}_{1}+{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(\phi,\psi)\,,\\
\mathcal{L}_{2}\psi&={\mathcal{E}}_{2}+{\mathcal{N}}_{2}(\phi,\psi)\,,\end{aligned}\right.

where the linear operators
({\mathcal{L}}_{1},{\mathcal{L}}_{2})
are given by

(2.15)

{\mathcal{L}}_{1}\phi:=-\Delta\phi-5U^{4}\phi\,,\quad{\mathcal{L}}_{2}\psi:=-\Delta\psi-5V^{4}\psi\,,

the error terms
({\mathcal{E}}_{1},{\mathcal{E}}_{2})
are given by

(2.16)

{\mathcal{E}}_{1}:=\beta U^{2}V^{3}\,,\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{2}:=V^{5}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{t,j}^{5}+\beta U^{3}V^{2}\,,

and the coupling terms
({\mathcal{N}}_{1},{\mathcal{N}}_{2})
are given by

(2.17)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathcal{N}}_{1}(\phi,\psi):=(U+\phi)_{+}^{5}-U^{5}-5U^{4}\phi+\beta(U+\phi)_{+}^{2}(V+\psi)_{+}^{3}-\beta U^{2}V^{3}\,,

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathcal{N}}_{2}(\phi,\psi):=(V+\psi)_{+}^{5}-V^{5}-5V^{4}\psi+\beta(V+\psi)_{+}^{2}(U+\phi)_{+}^{3}-\beta V^{2}U^{3}\,.

Using the dual formulation, (
2.14
) is equivalent to:

(2.18)

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\phi,\psi)-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}^{*}(\phi,\psi)=0\,,\quad(\phi,\psi)\in X_{k}\times X_{k}\,.

Here, the linear operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{*}:=({\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{*},{\mathcal{L}}_{2}^{*})
is given by

{\mathcal{L}}_{1}^{*}\phi:=\phi-(-\Delta)^{-1}(5U^{4}\phi)\,,\quad{\mathcal{L}}_{2}^{*}\psi:=\psi-(-\Delta)^{-1}(5V^{4}\psi)\,,

the error term
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{*}:=({\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{*},{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{*})
is given by

{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{*}:=(-\Delta)^{-1}{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\,,\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{*}:=(-\Delta)^{-1}{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\,,

and the coupling term
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}^{*}:=({\mathcal{N}}_{1}^{*},{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{*})
is given by

{\mathcal{N}}_{1}^{*}(\phi,\psi):=(-\Delta)^{-1}{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(\phi,\psi)\,,\quad{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{*}(\phi,\psi):=(-\Delta)^{-1}{\mathcal{N}}_{2}(\phi,\psi)\,.

The first difficulty when trying to find a solution to (
2.18
) is that the operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
is in general not invertible. We will overcome this issue using a Ljapunov-Schmidt reduction argument.

We consider the linearization of the Yamabe equation around
U
(given in (
2.3
)), i.e.,

(2.19)

-\Delta\phi-5U^{4}\phi=0\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.

It is well known (see e.g.
[
2
]
) that the set of
\dot{H}^{1}
-solutions to (
2.19
) is
\operatorname{span}\{Z^{(\ell)}:\ell\in\{0,\ldots,3\}\}
, where

(2.20)

Z^{(0)}(x):=\frac{3^{\frac{1}{4}}(|x|^{2}-1)}{2(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,,\qquad Z^{(\ell)}(x):=\frac{3^{\frac{1}{4}}\,x_{\ell}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\quad\ell\in\{1,2,3\}\,.

Similarly to (
2.4
) and (
2.7
), for
\delta>0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we set

Z_{\delta,\xi}^{(0)}(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}\,Z^{(0)}\Big(\frac{x-\xi}{\delta}\Big)=\frac{3^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{\delta}\,(|x-\xi|^{2}-\delta^{2})}{2(\delta^{2}+|x-\xi|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,,

and define

(2.21)

Z_{k,t}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}Z^{(0)}_{t\delta_{\beta},\xi_{t,j}}(x)=\frac{3^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{t\delta_{\beta}}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{|x-\xi_{t,j}|^{2}-t^{2}\delta_{\beta}^{2}}{(t^{2}\delta_{\beta}^{2}+|x-\xi_{t,j}|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,.

Having at hand this notation, we set

(2.22)

{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}:=X_{k}\cap{\rm span}\{Z_{k,t}\}\,,\qquad{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}:=\big\{\phi\in X_{k}:\langle\phi,Z_{k,t}\rangle=0\big\}\,,

and consider the orthogonal projections

\Pi:X_{k}\times X_{k}\to X_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}\,,\qquad\Pi^{\perp}:X_{k}\times X_{k}\to X_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}\,.

Note that
{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}
is the space of
X_{k}
functions
\dot{H}^{1}
-orthogonal to
{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}
, and that one has the decomposition

X_{k}={\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}\oplus{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}\,.

We can then rewrite (
2.18
) as the equivalent system

(2.23)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \ &\Pi\big[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\phi,\psi)-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}^{*}(\phi,\psi)\big]=0\,,\\
&\Pi^{\perp}\big[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\phi,\psi)-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}^{*}(\phi,\psi)\big]=0\,,\end{aligned}\right.\qquad(\phi,\psi)\in X_{k}\times X_{k}\,.

The first equation is the so-called
auxiliary equation
and, as we will see later on, it is finite dimensional. The rest of the section is devoted to prove the existence of a solution
(\phi,\psi)
to (
2.23
).

Through the rest of the paper, we will systematically use
[
19
, Lemma A.1]
. For convenience, we state this result here with our notation, and for the particular case of dimension
n=3
.

Lemma 2.2
.

(
[
19
, Lemma A.1]
)

Let

(2.24)

\Omega_{1}:=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x-\xi_{t,1}|<|x-\xi_{t,j}|\textup{ for all }j\in\{2,\ldots,k\}\big\}\,.

For every
\nu,\gamma\geqslant 0
such that
\nu+\gamma\leqslant 6
and all
0<a<b
, there exits a constant
C>0
such that

\int_{\Omega_{1}}U^{6-\nu-\gamma}\,U_{t,1}^{\nu}\bigg(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}\bigg)^{\gamma}dx\leqslant C\Big(\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{\nu+\gamma}{2}}f_{1}(k,\nu,\gamma)+(k\log k)^{\gamma}f_{2}(\delta_{\beta},k,\nu,\gamma)\Big)\,,

for all
k\geqslant 2
and all
t\in[a,b]
. Here,

f_{1}(k,\nu,\gamma):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &k^{\gamma-1}&&\textup{if }\nu<2\,,\\
&k^{\gamma-1}(\log k)^{\gamma+1}&&\textup{if }\nu=2\,,\\
&k^{\nu+\gamma-3}(\log k)^{\gamma}&&\textup{if }\nu>2\,,\end{aligned}\right.\quad\textup{ and }\quad f_{2}(\delta_{\beta},k,\nu,\gamma):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{\nu+\gamma}{2}}k^{\nu-3}&&\textup{if }\nu<3\,,\\
&\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{3+\gamma}{2}}|\log\delta_{\beta}|&&\textup{if }\nu=3\,,\\
&\delta_{\beta}^{3+\frac{\gamma-\nu}{2}}&&\textup{if }\nu>3\,.\end{aligned}\right.

2.1.
The error of approximation

As a preliminary step, we quantify how well the ansatz
(U,V)
“solves” (
2.18
), or equivalently (
2.23
). In order to make the notation lighter, we will write
\|\cdot\|_{p}=\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

For all fixed integer
k\geqslant 2
and all
0<a<b
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

C^{-1}\Big(\|{\mathcal{E}}_{1}^{*}\|+\|{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{*}\|\Big)\leqslant\|{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}+\|{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\leqslant C\delta_{\beta}(1+|\beta|)\,,

for all
\beta<-\sqrt{2}
and
t\in[a,b]
.

Proof.

We first prove that there exists
C>0
(independent of
\beta
and
t
) such that

(2.25)

\|{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\leqslant C\delta_{\beta}|\beta|\,.

First, taking into account the definition of
\Omega_{1}
(see (
2.24
)), we immediately get that

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}^{\frac{6}{5}}

\displaystyle=|\beta|^{\frac{6}{5}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U(x)^{6-\frac{18}{5}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{t,j}(x)\bigg)^{\frac{18}{5}}dx=k|\beta|^{\frac{6}{5}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}U(x)^{6-\frac{18}{5}}\bigg(U_{t,1}(x)+\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}(x)\bigg)^{\frac{18}{5}}dx

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{\frac{13}{5}}k|\beta|^{\frac{6}{5}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}U(x)^{6-\frac{18}{5}}U_{t,1}(x)^{\frac{18}{5}}dx+2^{\frac{13}{5}}k|\beta|^{\frac{6}{5}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}U(x)^{6-\frac{18}{5}}\bigg(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}(x)\bigg)^{\frac{18}{5}}dx\,.

Once we have this decomposition, we estimate each term of the right hand side separately. First, applying Lemma
2.2
with
\nu=18/5
and
\gamma=0
, we get

\int_{\Omega_{1}}U(x)^{6-\frac{18}{5}}U_{t,1}(x)^{\frac{18}{5}}dx\lesssim\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{6}{5}}((\delta_{\beta}k)^{\frac{3}{5}}+1)\,.

Likewise, by Lemma
2.2
with
\nu=0
and
\gamma=18/5
, it follows that

\int_{\Omega_{1}}U(x)^{6-\frac{18}{5}}\bigg(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}(x)\bigg)^{\frac{18}{5}}dx\lesssim\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{9}{5}}(k^{\frac{13}{5}}+k^{\frac{3}{5}}\log(k)^{\frac{18}{5}})\,.

Thus, we infer that

(2.26)

\|{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\lesssim\delta_{\beta}|\beta|\left[\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{3}{5}}\big(k^{\frac{8}{5}}(1+\log(k)^{\frac{18}{5}})+k^{\frac{18}{5}}\big)+1\right]^{\frac{5}{6}}.

Note that here, and during the rest of the proof, the implicit constants may depend on
a
and
b
, but they are independent of
k
,
\beta
and
t
. Finally, since
k
is a fixed number, (
2.25
) immediately follows from (
2.26
).

Next, we prove that there exists
C>0
(independent of
\beta
and
t
) such that

(2.27)

\|{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\leqslant C\delta_{\beta}(1+|\beta|)\,.

To that end, we write

{\mathcal{E}}_{2}={\mathcal{E}}_{2,1}+{\mathcal{E}}_{2,2}\,,\quad\textup{ with }\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{2,1}:=\beta V^{2}U^{3}\quad\textup{and}\quad{\mathcal{E}}_{2,2}:=V^{5}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{t,j}^{5}\,,

and we estimate each
{\mathcal{E}}_{2,i}
separately.

First, arguing as in the proof of (
2.26
), we see that

\|{\mathcal{E}}_{2,1}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}^{\frac{6}{5}}\leqslant 2^{\frac{7}{5}}k|\beta|^{\frac{6}{5}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}U(x)^{6-\frac{12}{5}}U_{t,1}(x)^{\frac{12}{5}}dx+2^{\frac{7}{5}}k|\beta|^{\frac{6}{5}}\int_{\Omega_{1}}U(x)^{6-\frac{12}{5}}\bigg(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}(x)\bigg)^{\frac{12}{5}}dx\,.

Then, using twice Lemma
2.2
(first
\nu=12/5
and
\gamma=0
and then
\nu=0
and
\gamma=12/5
), we get

(2.28)

\|{\mathcal{E}}_{2,1}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\lesssim\delta_{\beta}|\beta|\left[k^{\frac{2}{5}}(1+\log(k)^{\frac{12}{5}})+k^{\frac{12}{5}}\right]^{\frac{5}{6}}\,.

On the other hand, by
[
19
, Equation (3.7)]
, we get that

\|{\mathcal{E}}_{2,2}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}^{\frac{6}{5}}\lesssim k\int_{\Omega_{1}}U_{t,1}(x)^{\frac{24}{5}}\bigg(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}(x)\bigg)^{\frac{6}{5}}dx+k\int_{\Omega_{1}}\bigg(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}(x)\bigg)^{6}dx\,.

Using once again Lemma
2.2
, we estimate each term of the right hand side and obtain that

(2.29)

\|{\mathcal{E}}_{2,2}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\lesssim\delta_{\beta}\left[\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{9}{5}}(k^{4}\log(k)^{\frac{6}{5}}+k^{6}+k^{4}\log(k)^{6})+k^{\frac{11}{5}}\log(k)^{\frac{6}{5}}\right]^{\frac{5}{6}}\,.

Since
k
is a fixed number, (
2.27
) immediately follows from (
2.28
) and (
2.29
).

Taking into account that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(2.30)

\|(-\Delta)^{-1}f\|\leqslant C\|f\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\,,\quad\textup{ for all }f\in L^{\frac{6}{5}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,,

the result follows combining (
2.25
) and (
2.27
) with the definition of
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}^{*}
,
i=1,2
.
∎

2.2.
The linear theory

Once we have quantified the error of approximation, we start our analysis of (
2.23
) by solving the second equation there. More precisely, we deal with

(2.31)

\Pi^{\perp}\big[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\phi,\psi)-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}^{*}(\phi,\psi)\big]=0\,,\quad(\phi,\psi)\in X_{k}\times X_{k}\,.

To that end, let us introduce the shortened notation

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{\perp}_{*}:=\Pi^{\perp}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{*}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{\perp}_{*}:=\Pi^{\perp}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\,,\quad\textup{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}^{\perp}_{*}:=\Pi^{\perp}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}^{*}\,.

The first step to solve (
2.31
) is to analyze the invertibility of the linear operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_{*}^{\perp}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

For all fixed integer
k\geqslant 2
and all
0<a<b
, there exist constants
\beta_{\star}<-\sqrt{2}
and
C>0
such that, for all
\beta\in(-\infty,\beta_{\star}]
and all
t\in[a,b]
,

(2.32)

\|(\phi,\psi)\|\leqslant C\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{\perp}_{*}(\phi,\psi)\|\,,\quad\textup{{for all} }(\phi,\psi)\in X_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}\,.

In particular, the inverse operator
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{\perp}_{*})^{-1}:X_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}\to X_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}
exists and is continuous.

Proof.

We argue by contraction and assume that there exist sequences
(\beta_{n})_{n}\subset(-\infty,0)
,
(t_{n})_{n}\subset[a,b]
and
(\phi_{n},\psi_{n})_{n}\subset X_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t_{n}}^{\perp}
such that
\beta_{n}\to-\infty
and

(2.33)

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_{*}^{\perp}(\phi_{n},\psi_{n})=(f_{n},g_{n})\in X_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t_{n}}^{\perp}\,,\quad\|\phi_{n}\|+\|\psi_{n}\|=1\,,\quad\textup{and}\quad\|f_{n}\|+\|g_{n}\|=o(1)\,.

More precisely, we assume that
\beta_{n}\to-\infty
, and that there exists a sequence
(c_{n})_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
(\phi_{n},\psi_{n})
solves the system

(2.34)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\phi_{n}-5U^{4}\phi_{n}&=-\Delta f_{n}&&\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\\
-\Delta\psi_{n}-5V_{n}^{4}\psi_{n}&=-\Delta g_{n}-c_{n}\Delta Z_{n}&&\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

Here,

V_{n}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}},\,\xi_{t_{n},j}}(x)\,,\qquad Z_{n}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}Z_{t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}},\,\xi_{t_{n},j}}^{(0)}(x)\,,

and we are assuming that

\|\phi_{n}\|+\|\psi_{n}\|=1\,,\quad\textup{and}\quad\|f_{n}\|+\|g_{n}\|=o(1)\quad\textup{as }n\to\infty\,.

For later purposes, we introduce the notation

U_{jn}(x):=U_{t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}},\,\xi_{t_{n},j}}(x)\quad\textup{and}\quad Z_{jn}(x):=Z_{t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}},\,\xi_{t_{n},j}}^{(0)}(x)\,,

so that

V_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{jn}\quad\textup{and}\quad Z_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}Z_{jn}\,.

We divide the rest of the proof into four steps for the sake of clarity.

Step 1. Up to a subsequence,
\phi_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
\dot{H}^{1}
and
\phi_{n}\to 0
in
L^{p}_{{}_{\mathrm{loc}}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for all
p\in[2,6)
.

Since
(\|\phi_{n}\|)_{n}
is bounded, we have the existence of
\phi\in X_{k}
such that, up to a subsequence,

\phi_{n}\rightharpoonup\phi\quad\textup{in }\dot{H}^{1}\,,\quad\textup{and}\quad\phi_{n}\to\phi\quad\textup{in }L^{p}_{{}_{\mathrm{loc}}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\textup{ for all }p\in[2,6)\,.

Moreover, taking into account (
2.34
), we infer that

-\Delta\phi-5U^{4}\phi=0\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.

Hence, by
[
2
, Lemma A.1]
, we get that
\phi\in\operatorname{span}\{Z^{(\ell)},\ \ell\in\{0,\ldots,3\}\}
. Taking into account the definition of
X_{k}
, it is then not difficult to conclude that
\phi\equiv 0
and so the first step. Indeed, note that

\langle\phi,Z^{(\ell)}\rangle=5\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U^{4}Z^{(\ell)}\phi\,dx\,,\quad\forall\ \ell\in\{0,\ldots,3\}\,.

Then, using (
2.8
) it is straightforward to see that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U^{4}Z^{(2)}\phi\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U^{4}Z^{(3)}\phi\,dx=0\,.

Moreover, combining that
\langle\phi,Z^{(2)}\rangle=0
with (
2.9
) and the fact that
k\geqslant 2
, we see that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U^{4}Z^{(1)}\phi\,dx=0\,.

Finally, using (
2.10
), we get that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U^{4}Z^{(0)}\phi\,dx=0\,,

and thus
\langle\phi,Z^{(\ell)}\rangle=0
for all
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,3\}
. This implies that
\phi\equiv 0
, as desired.

Step 2.

\|Z_{n}\|^{2}=\frac{15\sqrt{3}\pi^{2}}{64}k+o(1)
and
c_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

First of all, using that
Z^{(0)}
solves (
2.19
), we get that

\displaystyle\|Z_{n}\|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla Z_{jn}|^{2}dx+2\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla Z_{jn}\nabla Z_{in}\,dx=5\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U_{jn}^{4}Z_{jn}^{2}\,dx+10\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U_{jn}^{4}Z_{jn}Z_{in}\,dx

\displaystyle\quad=5k\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U(y)^{4}Z^{(0)}(y)^{2}dy+10\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U_{jn}^{4}Z_{jn}Z_{in}\,dx=\frac{15\sqrt{3}\pi^{2}}{64}k+10\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U_{jn}^{4}Z_{jn}Z_{in}\,dx\,.

Hence, to conclude that
\|Z_{n}\|^{2}=\frac{15\sqrt{3}\pi^{2}}{64}k+o(1)
as
n\to\infty
, we are just missing to prove that

(2.35)

10\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U_{jn}^{4}Z_{jn}Z_{in}\,dx=o(1)\quad\textup{as }n\to\infty\,.

We start pointing out that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
,

(2.36)

|Z_{jn}|\leqslant U_{jn}\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.

Likewise, if we assume that
i\neq j
and choose
r_{0}>0
small enough so that
B_{r_{0}}(\xi_{t_{n},j})\cap B_{r_{0}}(\xi_{t_{n},i})=\emptyset
, it is straightforward to check that

(2.37)

U_{jn}\leqslant\frac{3^{\frac{1}{4}}\sqrt{b}}{r_{0}}\sqrt{\delta_{\beta_{n}}}\quad\textup{in }B_{r_{0}}(\xi_{t_{n},i})\,.

Using these pointwise estimates, one can check that

\bigg|\int_{B_{r_{0}}(\xi_{t_{n},j})}U_{jn}^{4}Z_{jn}Z_{in}\,dx\bigg|\lesssim\delta_{\beta_{n}}\,,

and that

\bigg|\int_{B_{r_{0}}(\xi_{t_{n},i})}U_{jn}^{4}Z_{jn}Z_{in}\,dx\bigg|+\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus(B_{r_{0}}(\xi_{t_{n},i})\cup B_{r_{0}}(\xi_{t_{n},i}))}U_{jn}^{4}Z_{jn}Z_{in}\,dx\bigg|\lesssim\delta_{\beta_{n}}^{3}\,.

Note that here and through the proof, the implicit constants (in
\lesssim\,
) may depend on
r_{0}
,
a
and
b
, but they are independent of
n
. Thus, since
\beta_{n}\to-\infty
, by (
2.6
), we conclude that (
2.35
) holds and so that

(2.38)

\|Z_{n}\|^{2}=\frac{15\sqrt{3}\pi^{2}}{64}k+o(1)\quad\textup{as }n\to\infty\,.

Next, testing the second equation in (
2.34
) with
Z_{n}
, and using that
Z^{(0)}
is a solution to (
2.19
), that
g_{n}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t_{n}}^{\perp}
and (
2.38
), we get that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{jn}^{4}Z_{jn}-V_{n}^{4}Z_{n}\bigg)\psi_{n}\,dx=c_{n}\bigg(\frac{3\sqrt{3}\pi^{2}}{64}k+o(1)\bigg)\quad\textup{as }n\to\infty\,.

Moreover, using the multinomial theorem, and Hölder and Sobolev inequalities, we infer that

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{jn}^{4}Z_{jn}-V_{n}^{4}Z_{n}\bigg)\psi_{n}\,dx\bigg|\lesssim\|\psi_{n}\|\bigg[\sum_{j\neq i}\|U_{jn}^{4}Z_{in}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}+\sum_{j\neq i}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\Big(\|U_{jn}^{3}U_{in}Z_{ln}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}+\|U_{jn}^{2}U_{in}^{2}Z_{ln}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j\neq i\neq r}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\|U_{jn}^{2}U_{in}U_{rn}Z_{ln}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}+\sum_{j\neq i\neq r\neq p}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\|U_{jn}U_{in}U_{rn}U_{pn}Z_{ln}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\bigg]\,.

Since
k\geqslant 2
is a fixed integer, using the pointwise estimates (
2.36
)–(
2.37
), and arguing as we did to prove (
2.35
), we estimate each term on the right hand side and conclude that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{jn}^{4}Z_{jn}-V_{n}^{4}Z_{n}\bigg)\psi_{n}\,dx=O(\delta_{\beta_{n}})\quad\textup{as }n\to\infty\,.

Hence, it follows that

c_{n}\frac{3\sqrt{3}\pi^{2}}{64}k=o(1)\quad\textup{as }n\to\infty\,,

and so that
c_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. This concludes the proof of the second step.

Step 3.

Up to a subsequence,
\widetilde{\psi}_{n}(y):=\sqrt{t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}}}\psi_{n}(t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}}y+\xi_{t_{n},1})
satisfies
\widetilde{\psi}_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
in
\dot{H}^{1}
and
\widetilde{\psi}_{n}\to 0
in
L^{p}_{{}_{\mathrm{loc}}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for all
p\in[2,6)
.

First note that
\|\widetilde{\psi}_{n}\|=\|\psi_{n}\|
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, since
(\|\psi_{n}\|)_{n}
is bounded, there exists
\psi\in\dot{H}^{1}
such that, up to a subsequence,

\widetilde{\psi}_{n}\rightharpoonup\psi\quad\textup{in }\dot{H}^{1}\,,\quad\textup{and}\quad\widetilde{\psi}_{n}\to\psi\quad\textup{in }L^{p}_{{}_{\mathrm{loc}}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\textup{ for all }p\in[2,6)\,.

Moreover, taking into account the second step, we infer that

-\Delta\psi-5U^{4}\psi=0\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,

and thus, by
[
2
, Lemma A.1]
, we get that
\psi\in\operatorname{span}\{Z^{(\ell)},\ \ell\in\{0,\ldots,3\}\}
. Note that, by the definition of
\xi_{t_{n},1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\widetilde{\psi}_{n}
satisfies (
2.8
) for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, arguing as in the first step, we get that

\langle\psi,Z^{(2)}\rangle=\langle\psi,Z^{(3)}\rangle=0\,.

On the other hand, since
\psi_{n}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t_{n}}^{\perp}\subset X_{k}
,
Z^{(0)}
is a solution to (
2.19
) and
\widetilde{\psi}_{n}\rightharpoonup\psi
in
\dot{H}^{1}
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{n},Z_{n}\rangle=5\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{jn}^{4}Z_{jn}\bigg)\psi_{n}\,dx=5k\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U_{1n}^{4}Z_{1n}\psi_{n}\,dx

\displaystyle=5k\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U(y)^{4}Z^{(0)}(y)\widetilde{\psi}_{n}(y)\,dy=k\langle\widetilde{\psi}_{n},Z^{(0)}\rangle=k\langle\psi,Z^{(0)}\rangle+o(1)\quad\textup{as }n\to\infty\,.

Taking the limit as

\langle\psi,Z^{(0)}\rangle=0

To that end, using (
2.10

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{y_{1}}{(1+|y|^{2})^{\frac{7}{2}}}\,\widetilde{\psi}_{n}(y)\,dy=(t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}})^{\frac{7}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{x_{1}-(1-t_{n}^{2}\delta_{\beta_{n}}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(t_{n}^{2}\delta_{\beta_{n}}^{2}+|x-\xi_{t_{n},1}|^{2})^{\frac{7}{2}}}\,\psi_{n}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\quad=(t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}})^{\frac{7}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{z_{1}-(1-t_{n}^{2}\delta_{\beta_{n}}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}|z|^{2}}{(t_{n}^{2}\delta_{\beta_{n}}^{2}+|z-\xi_{t_{n},1}|^{2})^{\frac{7}{2}}}\,\psi_{n}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{y_{1}}{(1+|y|^{2})^{\frac{7}{2}}}\,\widetilde{\psi}_{n}(y)\,dy+\frac{(1-t_{n}^{2}\delta_{\beta_{n}}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1-|t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}}y+\xi_{t_{n},1}|^{2}}{(1+|y|^{2})^{\frac{7}{2}}}\,\widetilde{\psi}_{n}(y)\,dy\,.

Thus, it follows that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-t_{n}^{2}\delta_{\beta_{n}}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1-|t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}}y+\xi_{t_{n},1}|^{2}}{(1+|y|^{2})^{\frac{7}{2}}}\,\widetilde{\psi}_{n}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=(1-t_{n}^{2}\delta_{\beta_{n}}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}t_{n}\delta_{\beta_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1-|y|^{2}}{(1+|y|^{2})^{\frac{7}{2}}}\,\widetilde{\psi}_{n}(y)\,dy-2(1-t_{n}^{2}\delta_{\beta_{n}}^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{y_{1}}{(1+|y|^{2})^{\frac{7}{2}}}\,\widetilde{\psi}_{n}(y)\,dy\,.

Sending

, we conclude that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{y_{1}}{(1+|y|^{2})^{\frac{7}{2}}}\,\psi(y)\,dy=0\,,

and so that
\langle\psi,Z^{(1)}\rangle=0
, as desired.

Step 4. Conclusion of the proof.

We are going to prove that
\|\psi_{n}\|+\|\phi_{n}\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, reaching a contradiction with (
2.33
). First of all, we test the first equation in (

) with
\phi_{n}
, the second one with
\psi_{n}
, and we sum them both to get that

\displaystyle 1=\|\psi_{n}\|+\|\phi_{n}\|=5\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U^{4}\phi_{n}^{2}\,dx+\langle\phi_{n},f_{n}\rangle+5\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V_{n}^{4}\psi_{n}^{2}\,dx+\langle\psi_{n},g_{n}+c_{n}Z_{n}\rangle\,.

Combining (
2.33

\langle\phi_{n},f_{n}\rangle\to 0\quad\textup{and}\quad\langle\psi_{n},g_{n}+c_{n}Z_{n}\rangle\to 0\quad\textup{as }n\to\infty\,.

Likewise, using the first step and the decay of

, we infer that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U^{4}\phi_{n}^{2}\,dx\to 0\quad\textup{as }n\to\infty\,.

Hence, we have that

1=\|\psi_{n}\|+\|\phi_{n}\|=5\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V_{n}^{4}\psi_{n}^{2}\,dx+o(1)\quad\textup{as }n\to\infty\,,

and thus, to conclude the proof, we just have to show that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V_{n}^{4}\psi_{n}^{2}\,dx\to 0\quad\textup{as }n\to\infty\,.

Recalling the definition of

, see (

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V_{n}^{4}\psi_{n}^{2}\,dx=k\int_{\Omega_{1}}\bigg(U_{1n}+\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{jn}\bigg)^{4}\psi_{n}^{2}\,dx\leqslant 8k\int_{\Omega_{1}}U_{1n}^{4}\psi_{n}^{2}\,dx+8k\int_{\Omega_{1}}\bigg(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{jn}\bigg)^{4}\psi_{n}^{2}\,dx\,.

One one hand, by Lemma

applied with

Since

is a fixed integer, we conclude that

On the other hand, note that

8k\int_{\Omega_{1}}U_{1n}^{4}\psi_{n}^{2}\,dx\leqslant 8k\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U_{1n}^{4}\psi_{n}^{2}\,dx=8k\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U(y)^{4}\widetilde{\psi}_{n}(y)^{2}\,dy\,.

Since

is a fixed integer, using the third step and the decay of the function

and so that

as desired.
∎

2.3.
Nonlinear theory

Having at hand Propositions
2.3
and
2.4
we can now give the desired existence result for (
2.31
). More precisely, we prove the following:

Proposition 2.5
.

For all fixed integer
k\geqslant 2
and all
0<a<b
, there exist constants
\beta_{\star}<-\sqrt{2}
and
C>0
such that, for all
\beta\in(-\infty,\beta_{\star}]
and all
t\in[a,b]
, the system

(2.39)

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_{*}^{\perp}(\phi,\psi)-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{*}^{\perp}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}_{*}^{\perp}(\phi,\psi)=0\,,\quad(\phi,\psi)\in X_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}\,,

has a unique solution
(\phi[t],\psi[t])\in X_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}
such that

(2.40)

\|\phi[t]\|+\|\psi[t]\|\leqslant C\delta_{\beta}(1+|\beta|)\,.

Moreover, the map
t\mapsto(\phi[t],\psi[t])
is continuously differentiable.

The proof of this result follows from a standard application of the contraction mapping theorem, as a consequence of the following result:

Lemma 2.6
.

For all fixed integer
k\geqslant 2
, all
D>0
and all
0<a<b
, there exists
C>0
such that

(2.41)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}_{*}^{\perp}(\phi_{0},\psi_{0})\|=\|(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}_{*}^{\perp}(\phi_{0},\psi_{0}))_{1}\|+\|(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}_{*}^{\perp}(\phi_{0},\psi_{0}))_{2}\|\leqslant\frac{C}{|\log\delta_{\beta}|}\Big(\|\phi_{0}\|+\|\psi_{0}\|\Big)\,,

(2.42)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}_{*}^{\perp}(\phi_{1},\psi_{1})-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}_{*}^{\perp}(\phi_{2},\psi_{2})\|\leqslant\frac{C}{|\log\delta_{\beta}|}\Big(\|\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}\|+\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|\Big)\,,

for all
\beta<-\sqrt{2}
, all
t\in[a,b]
and all
(\phi_{0},\psi_{0}),\,(\phi_{1},\psi_{1}),\,(\phi_{2},\psi_{2})\in\dot{H}^{1}\times\dot{H}^{1}
satisfying

\|\phi_{i}\|+\|\psi_{i}\|\leqslant D\,\delta_{\beta}\big(1+|\beta|\big)\,.

Proof.

Firstly, note that there exists
C>0
such that

\begin{split}\|(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}_{*}^{\perp}(\phi,\psi))_{1}\|&\leqslant C\|(U+\phi)_{+}^{5}-U^{5}-5U^{4}\phi+\beta(U+\phi)_{+}^{2}(V+\psi)_{+}^{3}-\beta U^{2}V^{3}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\,,\\
\|(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}_{*}^{\perp}(\phi,\psi))_{2}\|&\leqslant C\|(V+\psi)_{+}^{5}-V^{5}-5V^{4}\psi+\beta(V+\psi)_{+}^{2}(U+\phi)_{+}^{3}-\beta V^{2}U^{3}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\,,\end{split}

for every
(\phi,\psi)\in\dot{H}^{1}\times\dot{H}^{1}
. Also, notice that, doing Taylor and using the mean value theorem, we get the existence of
t,\lambda,\tau\in[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\big|(U+\phi)_{+}^{5}-U^{5}-5U^{4}\phi\big|=\big|10(U+t\phi)_{+}^{3}\phi^{2}\big|\,,

\displaystyle\big|\beta(U+\phi)_{+}^{2}(V+\psi)_{+}^{3}-\beta U^{2}V^{3}\big|=\big|\beta\big((U+\phi)_{+}^{2}-U^{2}\big)(V+\psi)_{+}^{3}+\beta U^{2}\big((V+\psi)_{+}^{3}-V^{3}\big)\big|

\displaystyle\quad=\big|2\beta(U+\lambda\phi)_{+}\phi(V+\psi)_{+}^{3}+3\beta U^{2}(V+\tau\psi)^{2}_{+}\psi\big|\,,

and hence

(2.43)

\begin{split}\|(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}_{*}^{\perp}(\phi,\psi))_{1}\|&=O\Big(\big\|U^{3}\phi^{2}+|\phi|^{5}\big\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\Big)+O\Big(|\beta|\big\|(U+|\phi|)|\phi|(V^{3}+|\psi|^{3})+U^{2}(V^{2}+|\psi|^{2})|\psi|)\big\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\Big)\,.\end{split}

Likewise, it can be seen that

(2.44)

\begin{split}\|(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}_{*}^{\perp}(\phi,\psi))_{2}\|&=O\Big(\big\|V^{3}\psi^{2}+|\psi|^{5}\big\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\Big)+O\Big(|\beta|\big\|(V+|\psi|)|\psi|(U^{3}+|\phi|^{3})+V^{2}(U^{2}+|\phi|^{2})|\phi|)\big\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\Big)\,.\end{split}

Since
\beta\to-\infty
here, some of these terms are not obvious to handle, being the worst ones the terms that are linear in
|\phi|
and
|\psi|
. We focus on these terms and prove that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(2.45)

\displaystyle|\beta|\Big(\|U^{2}V^{2}\phi\|_{\frac{6}{5}}+\|U^{2}V^{2}\psi\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\Big)\leqslant C\frac{\sqrt{\delta_{\beta}}}{|\log\delta_{\beta}|^{\frac{1}{3}}}\Big(\|\psi\|+\|\phi\|\Big)\,,

\displaystyle|\beta|\Big(\|UV^{3}\phi\|_{\frac{6}{5}}+\|U^{3}V\psi\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\Big)\leqslant\frac{C}{|\log\delta_{\beta}|}\Big(\|\psi\|+\|\phi\|\Big)\,.

We estimate each term separately. Arguing as in the proof of Proposition
2.3
, using twice again Lemma
2.2
, we get that

\displaystyle|\beta|^{\frac{6}{5}}\|U^{2}V^{2}\psi\|_{\frac{6}{5}}^{\frac{6}{5}}\lesssim\|\psi\|^{\frac{6}{5}}|\beta|^{\frac{6}{5}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U^{3}V^{3}dx\bigg)^{\frac{4}{5}}

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\|\psi\|^{\frac{6}{5}}|\beta|^{\frac{6}{5}}\bigg(k\int_{\Omega_{1}}U^{3}U_{t,1}^{3}dx+k\int_{\Omega_{1}}U^{3}\bigg(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}\bigg)^{3}dx\bigg)^{\frac{4}{5}}\lesssim\big[\|\psi\||\beta|\delta_{\beta}\big]^{\frac{6}{5}}\Big(k^{3}+k\log(k)^{3}+k|\log\delta_{\beta}|\Big)^{\frac{4}{5}}\,.

Note that here, and during the rest of the proof, the implicit constants may depend on
a
and
b
, but they are independent of
k
,
\beta
and
t
. Since
k\geqslant 2
is a fixed integer, we immediately deduce that there exists
C>0
(independent of
\beta
and
t
) such that

|\beta|\|U^{2}V^{2}\psi\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\leqslant C\|\psi\|\delta_{\beta}|\beta||\log\delta_{\beta}|^{\frac{2}{3}}=C\|\psi\|\,\frac{\sqrt{\delta_{\beta}}}{|\log\delta_{\beta}|^{\frac{1}{3}}}\,.

The same proof also gives

|\beta|\|U^{2}V^{2}\phi\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\leqslant C\|\phi\|\,\frac{\sqrt{\delta_{\beta}}}{|\log\delta_{\beta}|^{\frac{1}{3}}}\,.

Similarly, we get that

\displaystyle|\beta|^{\frac{6}{5}}\|UV^{3}\phi\|_{\frac{6}{5}}^{\frac{6}{5}}\lesssim|\beta|^{\frac{6}{5}}\|\phi\|^{\frac{6}{5}}\bigg(k\int_{\Omega_{1}}U^{6-\frac{9}{2}}U_{t,1}^{\frac{9}{2}}dx+k\int_{\Omega_{1}}U^{6-\frac{9}{2}}\bigg(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}\bigg)^{\frac{9}{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{4}{5}}

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\big[\|\phi\|\sqrt{\delta_{\beta}}|\beta|\big]^{\frac{6}{5}}\Big(k+\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{3}{2}}(k^{\frac{9}{2}}+k^{\frac{5}{2}}\log(k)^{\frac{9}{2}})\Big)^{\frac{4}{5}}\,.

Since
k\geqslant 2
is a fixed integer, we deduce that there exists
C>0
(independent of
\beta
and
t
) such that

(2.46)

|\beta|\|UV^{3}\phi\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\leqslant C\|\phi\|\sqrt{\delta_{\beta}}|\beta|=\frac{C}{|\log\delta_{\beta}|}\|\phi\|\,.

By using twice Lemma
2.2
, first with
\nu=3/2
and
\gamma=0
, and then with
\nu=0
and
\gamma=3/2
, we get

|\beta|\|U^{3}V\psi\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\leqslant\frac{C}{|\log\delta_{\beta}|}\|\psi\|\,.

The other terms in (
2.43
) and (
2.44
) can be seen to be smaller in a similar fashion and thus (

does not play any role in the proof of (

2.44

The rest of the proof is analogous so we skip the details.

2.4.
Energy expansion

Given a fixed integer
k\geqslant 2
,
t\in[a,b]
and
\beta\in(-\infty,\beta_{\star}]
, let
(\phi,\psi)=(\phi[t],\psi[t])
be the unique solution to (
2.39
) provided by Proposition
2.5
. We consider the function

(2.47)

F_{\beta}:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\,,\quad t\mapsto{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U+\phi,V+\psi)\,,

where

{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(u,v):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big(|\nabla u|^{2}+|\nabla v|^{2}\big)\,dx-\frac{1}{6}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big(u_{+}^{6}+v_{+}^{6}\big)\,dx-\frac{\beta}{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}u_{+}^{3}v_{+}^{3}dx\quad\textup{for }(u,v)\in\dot{H}^{1}\times\dot{H}^{1}\,.

This section is devoted to prove an expansion of
F_{\beta}
in terms of
\beta
and a function of
t
. As a preliminary step, we prove the following:

Lemma 2.7
.

For all fixed integer
k\geqslant 2
and all
0<a<b
, there exist constants
\beta_{\star}<-\sqrt{2}
and
C>0
such that, for all
\beta\in(-\infty,\beta_{\star}]
and all
t\in[a,b]
, it holds

F_{\beta}(t)={\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U,V)+\theta_{1,\beta}(t)\,,

with
\theta_{1,\beta}:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

|\theta_{1,\beta}(t)|\leqslant C\delta_{\beta}^{2}(1+|\beta|)^{2}\quad\textup{for }t\in[a,b]\,.

Proof.

First note that, by the fundamental theorem of calculus,

F_{\beta}(t)-{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U,V)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{ds}{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U+s\phi,V+s\psi)ds\,.

Moreover, using Propositions
2.3
and
2.5
, we get that

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{d}{ds}{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U+s\phi,V+s\psi)\Big|_{s=0}\bigg|=\big|\partial_{1}{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U,V)\phi+\partial_{2}{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U,V)\psi\big|

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\|{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\phi\|_{1}+\|{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\psi\|_{1}\leqslant\|{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\|\phi\|_{6}+\|{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\|\psi\|_{6}\lesssim\|{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\|\phi\|+\|{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\|\psi\|\lesssim\delta_{\beta}^{2}(1+|\beta|)^{2}\,.

Note that here and during the rest of the proof the implicit constants are independent of
\beta
and
t
. Then, using again the fundamental theorem of calculus and exchanging the order of integration, we get that

(2.48)

F_{\beta}(t)-{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U,V)=\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U+s\phi,V+s\psi)ds+O(\delta_{\beta}^{2}(1+|\beta|)^{2})\,.

Now, using that
(\phi,\psi)
solves (
2.39
), we get that

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U+s\phi,V+s\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big({\mathcal{N}}_{1}(\phi,\psi)+{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\big)\phi\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big({\mathcal{N}}_{2}(\phi,\psi)+{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\big)\psi\,dx

\displaystyle\quad-5\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big((U+s\phi)_{+}^{4}-U^{4}\big)\phi^{2}dx-5\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big((V+s\psi)_{+}^{4}-V^{4}\big)\psi^{2}\,dx-6\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(U+s\phi)_{+}^{2}(V+s\psi)_{+}^{2}\phi\psi\,dx

\displaystyle\quad-2\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(U+s\phi)_{+}(V+s\psi)_{+}^{3}\phi^{2}\,dx-2\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(U+s\phi)_{+}^{3}(V+s\psi)_{+}\psi^{2}\,dx\,.

Arguing as in the proofs of Proposition
2.3
and Lemma
2.6
, and using Propositions
2.3
and
2.5
, one can estimate each term on the right hand side and get a constant
C>0
(independent of
\beta
and
t
) such that

(2.49)

\bigg|\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U+s\phi,V+s\psi)\bigg|\leqslant C\delta_{\beta}^{2}(1+|\beta|)^{2}\,.

Note that, since
\beta\to-\infty
, we need to be careful with the terms involving
\beta
. However, following the proof of Lemma
2.6
, if is not difficult to see that (
2.49
) holds true.

The result follows combining (
2.48
) and (
2.49
).
∎

Proposition 2.8
.

For all fixed integer
k\geqslant 2
and all
0<a<b
, there exist constants
\beta_{\star}<-\sqrt{2}
and
C>0
such that, for all
\beta\in(-\infty,\beta_{\star}]
and all
t\in[a,b]
, it holds

F_{\beta}(t)=\frac{k+1}{3}\|U\|_{6}^{6}+\delta_{\beta}\big(-\mathtt{c}_{1}t+\mathtt{c}_{2}t^{\frac{3}{2}}\big)+\theta_{2,\beta}(t)\,,

with
\theta_{2,\beta}:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

|\theta_{2,\beta}(t)|\leqslant C\frac{\delta_{\beta}}{|\log\delta_{\beta}|}\,,\qquad\mathtt{c}_{1}:=\sqrt{6}\,\pi k\sum_{j=2}^{k}\Big[1-\cos\Big(\tfrac{2\pi(j-1)}{k}\Big)\Big]^{-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\mathtt{c}_{2}:=\sqrt{6}\,\pi k\,.

Proof.

First note that we can decompose
{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U,V)
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{\beta}(U,V)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\|U\|_{6}^{6}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla V|^{2}dx-\frac{1}{6}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V^{6}\,dx-\frac{\beta}{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U^{3}V^{3}dx=:\frac{1}{3}\|U\|_{6}^{6}+{\mathcal{I}}_{1,\beta}(V)+{\mathcal{I}}_{2,\beta}(U,V)\,.

Having at hand this decomposition, we analyze each
{\mathcal{I}}_{i,\beta}
separately.

On one hand, following the proof of
[
19
, Lemma 3.4]
, we see that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{1,\beta}(V)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{3}\|U\|_{6}^{6}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\neq i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U_{t,j}^{5}U_{t,i}dx-\frac{1}{6}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg(V^{6}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{t,j}^{6}-6\sum_{j\neq i}U_{t,j}^{5}U_{t,i}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle 0

,

(3.10)

\|(\phi,\psi)\|\leqslant C\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}^{\perp}_{*}(\phi,\psi)\|\,,\quad\textup{{for all} }(\phi,\psi)\in\tilde{X}_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}\,.

In particular, the inverse operator
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}^{\perp}_{*})^{-1}:\tilde{X}_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}\to\tilde{X}_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}
exists and is continuous.

Proof.

The proof follows exactly as in Proposition
2.4
just by noticing that the operator
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
preserves the extra symmetry appearing in
\tilde{X}_{k}
. That is, using the fact that
U
is radial, one gets that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
maps
\tilde{X}_{k}\times X_{k}
into
\tilde{X}_{k}\times X_{k}
.
Since
\tilde{X}_{k}\subset X_{k}
, the rest of the proof can be analogously reproduced.
∎

3.3.
Nonlinear theory

Having at hand Propositions
3.4
and Proposition
3.5
we can now give the required existence result for

\tilde{\Pi}^{\perp}\big[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}^{*}(\phi,\psi)-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}^{*}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}}^{*}(\phi,\psi)\big]=0\,,\qquad(\phi,\psi)\in\tilde{X}_{k}\times X_{k}\,.

Indeed, we have the following:

Proposition 3.6
.

For all fixed integer
k\geqslant 2
and all
0<a<b
, there exist constants
\beta_{\star}<-\sqrt{2}
and
C>0
such that, for all
\beta\in(-\infty,\beta_{\star}]
and all
t\in[a,b]
, the system

(3.11)

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}}^{\perp}_{*}(\phi,\psi)-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}^{\perp}_{*}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}}^{\perp}_{*}(\phi,\psi)=0\,,\quad(\phi,\psi)\in\tilde{X}_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}\,,

has a unique solution
(\phi[t],\psi[t])\in\tilde{X}_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}
such that

\|\phi[t]\|+\|\psi[t]\|\leqslant C\delta_{\beta}(1+|\beta|)\,.

Moreover, the map
t\mapsto(\phi[t],\psi[t])
is continuously differentiable.

Proof.

As stated in Section
2
, once we have Propositions
3.4
and Proposition
3.5
, this result follows by
a standard fixed point argument. The fact that the nonlinear term is contractive follows in the same spirit as Lemma
2.6
. Hence, it only remains to check that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}^{\perp}_{*}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}}^{\perp}_{*}
maps
\tilde{X}_{k}\times{\mathcal{K}}_{k,t}^{\perp}
into itself.

By construction, for every
r\in\{1,\ldots q\}
,
V_{r}
satisfies the symmetries (
2.9
), (
2.10
), and

V_{r}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=V_{r}(x_{1},x_{2},-x_{3})\,.

Also, using that
V(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=V(x_{1},-x_{2},x_{3})
, it can be seen that

V_{r}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=V_{q-r+2}(x_{1},-x_{2},x_{3})\quad\textup{for }r\in\{1,\ldots q\}\,,

and hence in particular

\sum_{r=2}^{q}V_{r}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{\gamma}=\sum_{r=2}^{q}V_{r}(x_{1},-x_{2},x_{3})^{\gamma},\quad\gamma\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Likewise, if
\psi\in X_{k}
then
\psi_{r}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\psi_{q-r+2}(x_{1},-x_{2},x_{3})
and hence

\sum_{r=2}^{q}\big([V_{r}+\psi_{r}](x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\big)^{\gamma}=\sum_{r=2}^{q}\big([V_{r}+\psi_{r}](x_{1},-x_{2},x_{3})\big)^{\gamma},\quad\gamma\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Having at hand this information, it is straightforward to see that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}^{*}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}}^{*}(\phi,\psi)
satisfies (
2.8
)–(
2.10
) whenever
(\phi,\psi)\in\tilde{X}_{k}\times X_{k}
.

On the other hand, noticing that

V_{r}({\mathscr{R}}_{r,k}x)=V_{r+1}(x)\quad\textup{for }r\in\{2,\ldots,q\}\,,

we get that, for every pair
(\phi,\psi)\in\tilde{X}_{k}\times X_{k}
,

\Big[\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}^{*}_{1}+\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}^{*}_{1}(\phi,\psi)\Big]({\mathscr{R}}_{r,k}x)=\Big[\tilde{{\mathcal{E}}}^{*}_{1}+\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}^{*}_{1}(\phi,\psi)\Big](x)\quad\textup{for }r\in\{2,\ldots,q\}\,,

and the result easily follows.
∎

3.4.
Finite dimensional reduction

Given a fixed integer
k\geqslant 2
,
0<a<b
,
t\in[a,b]
,
\beta_{\star}<-\sqrt{2}
as in Proposition
3.6
and
\beta\in(-\infty,\beta_{\star}]
, we let
(\phi,\psi)=(\phi[t],\psi[t])
be the unique solution to (
3.11
) provided by Proposition
3.6
. The goal of this subsection is to find
t
in such a way that
(\phi,\psi)
solves the complete problem (
3.7
). In Section
2
we faced this by a variational approach, which is not available here. We will use the so-called direct method: we rewrite the first equation in (
3.7
) as

(3.12)

\mathcal{L}_{2}^{*}(\psi)-\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{*}-\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{*}(\phi,\psi)=c_{0}(t)Z_{k,t}\,,

where the function
Z_{k,t}
is given in (
2.21
), and hence we want to find
t
so that
c_{0}(t)=0
or, equivalently (see (
2.38
)), so that

\tilde{\mathbf{c}}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla(\mathcal{L}_{2}^{*}(\psi)-\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{*}-\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{*}(\phi,\psi))\nabla Z_{k,t}\,dx=0\,.

Proposition 3.7
.

For all fixed integer
k\geqslant 2
and all
0<a<b
, there exist constants
\beta_{\star}<-\sqrt{2}
and
C>0
such that, for all
\beta\in(-\infty,\beta_{\star}]
and all
t\in[a,b]
, it holds

\tilde{\mathbf{c}}(t)=-\tilde{\mathtt{c}}_{1}t\delta_{\beta}-\tilde{\mathtt{c}}_{2}\beta(t\delta_{\beta})^{\frac{3}{2}}|\log\delta_{\beta}|+\theta_{3,\beta}(t)\,,

where
\theta_{3,\beta}:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

|\theta_{3,\beta}(t)|\leqslant C\delta_{\beta}^{2}(1+|\beta|)^{2}\,,\qquad\tilde{\mathtt{c}}_{1}:=\sqrt{6}\,\pi k\sum_{j=2}^{k}\Big[1-\cos\Big(\tfrac{2\pi(j-1)}{k}\Big)\Big]^{-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\tilde{\mathtt{c}}_{2}:=\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{6}\,\pi k\,.

Proof.

First of all, it can be seen that

(3.13)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla(\mathcal{L}_{2}^{*}(\psi)-\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{*}-\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{*}(\phi,\psi))\nabla Z_{k,t}\,dx=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{*}\nabla Z_{k,t}\,dx+O\big(\delta_{\beta}^{2}(1+|\beta|)^{2}\big)\,.

Indeed, using the fact that

(3.14)

|Z^{(0)}_{t\delta_{\beta},\xi_{t,j}}|\leqslant CU_{t\delta_{\beta},\xi_{t,j}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad|Z_{k,t}|\leqslant CV\quad\mbox{ for some constant }C>0\,,

the problem satisfied for
Z_{k,t}
and Proposition
3.6
, we can estimate

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{2}^{*}(\psi)\nabla Z_{k,t}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\psi)Z_{k,t}\,dx=5\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Big(V^{4}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{t,j}^{4}\Big)\psi Z_{k,t}\,dx=O\big(\delta_{\beta}^{2}(1+|\beta|)\big)\,.

Also, proceeding as in the proof of Lemma
2.6
and using Proposition
3.6
, one can see that

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{*}(\phi,\psi)\nabla Z_{k,t}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}(\phi,\psi)Z_{k,t}\,dx\\
&\quad\lesssim\bigg|\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(V^{2}U^{2}\phi+U^{3}V\psi)Z_{k,t}\,dx\bigg|+|\alpha|\sum_{r=2}^{q}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(V^{2}V_{r}^{2}\psi_{r}+VV_{r}^{3}\psi)Z_{k,t}\,dx\bigg|+O(\delta_{\beta}^{2}(1+|\beta|)^{2})\,,\end{split}

and that

\bigg|\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(V^{2}U^{2}\phi+U^{3}V\psi)Z_{k,t}\,dx\bigg|\leqslant|\beta|(\|\psi\|+\|\phi\|)\Big(\|V^{3}U^{2}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}+\|V^{2}U^{3}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\Big)=O\big(\delta_{\beta}^{2}(1+|\beta|)^{2}\big)\,.

Furthermore,

|\alpha|\sum_{r=2}^{q}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(V^{2}V_{r}^{2}\psi_{r}+VV_{r}^{3}\psi)Z_{k,t}\,dx\bigg|\leqslant|\alpha|\|\psi\|\Big(\|V^{3}V_{r}^{2}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}+\|V^{2}V_{r}^{3}\|_{\frac{6}{5}}\Big)=O\big(\delta_{\beta}^{3}(1+|\beta|)\big)\,,

and thus (
3.13
) follows. Notice that the key point to get this estimate is the fact that

(3.15)

\mbox{there exists }\eta_{0}>0\mbox{ such that }|\xi_{r,t,j}-\xi_{s,t,i}|>\eta_{0}\mbox{ whenever }i\neq j\mbox{ or }r\neq s.

Now, let us estimate the projection of the error term:

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{*}\nabla Z_{k,t}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{2}Z_{k,t}\,dx\\
&\quad=\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Big(V^{5}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{t,j}^{5}\Big)Z_{k,t}\,dx}_{I_{1}}+\underbrace{\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}U^{3}V^{2}Z_{k,t}\,dx}_{I_{2}}+\underbrace{\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V^{2}\Big(\sum_{r=2}^{q}V_{r}\Big)^{3}Z_{k,t}\,dx}_{I_{3}}\,.\end{split}

We estimate each
I_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2,3\}
separately.

First, using the symmetries of the construction and (
3.14
) once again, we get that

\begin{split}I_{1}&=k\int_{\Omega_{1}}\Big(V^{5}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}U_{t,j}^{5}\Big)Z_{k,t}\,dx\\
&=5k\int_{\Omega_{1}}U_{t,1}^{4}\Big(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}\Big)Z^{(0)}_{t\delta_{\beta},\xi_{t,1}}\,dx+O\Big(\sum_{\gamma=0}^{4}\int_{\Omega_{1}}U_{t,1}^{\gamma}\Big(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}\Big)^{6-\gamma}\,dx\Big)\\
&=5k\int_{\Omega_{1}}U_{t,1}^{4}\Big(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}\Big)Z^{(0)}_{t\delta_{\beta},\xi_{t,1}}\,dx+O(\delta_{\beta}^{2})\,.\end{split}

Note that in the last step we have used once again Lemma
2.2
. Now, let
r_{0}>0
small enough such that
B_{r_{0}}(\xi_{t,1})\cap B_{r_{0}}(\xi_{t,j})=\emptyset
for every
j\neq 1
. Then,

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega_{1}}U_{t,1}^{4}\Big(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}\Big)Z^{(0)}_{t\delta_{\beta},\xi_{t,1}}\,dx=\int_{B_{r_{0}}(\xi_{t,1})}U_{t,1}^{4}\Big(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}\Big)Z^{(0)}_{t\delta_{\beta},\xi_{t,1}}\,dx+O(\delta_{\beta}^{3})\\
&\quad=t^{\frac{1}{2}}\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{B_{\frac{r_{0}}{\delta_{\beta}}}(0)}U^{4}(y)\Big(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}(\delta_{\beta}y+\xi_{t,1})\Big)Z^{(0)}(y)\,dy=t\delta_{\beta}\sum_{j=2}^{k}\frac{3^{\frac{1}{4}}}{|\xi_{t,j}-\xi_{t,1}|}\int_{B_{\frac{r_{0}}{\delta_{\beta}}}(0)}U^{4}Z^{(0)}\,dy+O(\delta_{\beta}^{2})\,,\end{split}

and thus

I_{1}=\tilde{\mathtt{c}}_{1}t\delta_{\beta}+O(\delta_{\beta}^{2})\,.

Next, by (
3.14
) and Lemma
2.2
, we get that

\begin{split}I_{2}&=\beta k\int_{\Omega_{1}}U^{3}V^{2}Z_{k,t}\,dx=\beta k\int_{\Omega_{1}}U^{3}V^{2}Z^{(0)}_{t\delta_{\beta},\xi_{t,1}}\,dx+O\bigg(|\beta|\sum_{\gamma=0}^{2}\int_{\Omega_{1}}U^{3}U_{t,1}^{\gamma}\Big(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}\Big)^{3-\gamma}\,dx\bigg)\\
&=\beta k\int_{\Omega_{1}}U^{3}U_{t,1}^{2}Z^{(0)}_{t\delta_{\beta},\xi_{t,1}}\,dx+O\bigg(|\beta|\sum_{\gamma=0}^{2}\int_{\Omega_{1}}U^{3}U_{t,1}^{\gamma}\Big(\sum_{j=2}^{k}U_{t,j}\Big)^{3-\gamma}\,dx\bigg)\\
&=\beta k\int_{B_{r_{0}}(\xi_{t,1})}U^{3}U_{t,1}^{2}Z^{(0)}_{t\delta_{\beta},\xi_{t,1}}\,dx+O\Big(|\beta|\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{3}{2}}\Big)\,.\end{split}

On the other hand, using
[
15
, Equation (2.11)]
, we get that

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{r_{0}}(\xi_{t,1})}U^{3}U_{t,1}^{2}Z^{(0)}_{t\delta_{\beta},\xi_{t,1}}\,dx=\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{3}{2}}\int_{B_{\frac{r_{0}}{\delta_{\beta}}}(0)}U^{3}(\delta_{\beta}y+\xi_{t,1})U^{2}(y)Z^{(0)}(y)\,dy\\
&\quad=\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{3}{2}}U^{3}(\xi_{t,1})\bigg[\int_{B_{\frac{r_{0}}{\delta_{\beta}}}(0)}U^{2}(y)Z^{(0)}(y)\,dy+O(1)\bigg]=\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{3}{2}}U^{3}(0,0,1)(1+O(\delta_{\beta}^{2}))\bigg[\frac{3^{\frac{3}{4}}}{2}\int_{B_{\frac{r_{0}}{\delta_{\beta}}}(0)}\frac{dy}{(1+|y|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}+O(1)\bigg]\,.\end{split}

and so, by (
2.52
), we conclude that

I_{2}=\tilde{\mathtt{c}}_{2}\beta(t\delta_{\beta})^{\frac{3}{2}}|\log\delta_{\beta}|+O\Big(|\beta|\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{3}{2}}\Big)\,\,.

Finally, using (
3.14
) and (
3.15
), it is straightforward to check that

|I_{3}|\leqslant\Big|\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}V^{3}\Big(\sum_{r=2}^{q}V_{r}\Big)^{3}\,dx\Big|=O\big(\delta_{\beta}^{3}|\log\delta_{\beta}|\big)\,.

Substituting the estimates for
I_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, into (
3.13
) the result follows.
∎

3.5.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

We now have all the needed ingredients to prove Theorem
3.1
. Here, given a fixed integer
k\geqslant 2
,
0<a<b
,
t\in[a,b]
,
\beta_{\star}<-\sqrt{2}
as in Proposition
3.6
and
\beta\in(-\infty,\beta_{\star}]
, we let
(\phi,\psi)=(\phi[t],\psi[t])
be the unique solution to (
3.11
) provided by Proposition
3.6
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

First of all, choosing
\delta_{\beta}
such that

\delta_{\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}|\log\delta_{\beta}|=-\frac{1}{\beta}\,,

from Proposition
3.7
, it follows that

\tilde{\mathbf{c}}(t)=\Big(-\tilde{\mathtt{c}}_{1}t+\tilde{\mathtt{c}}_{2}t^{\frac{3}{2}}\Big)\delta_{\beta}+\theta_{3,\beta}(t)\,,

with
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}(t)
defined in (
3.4
). Hence, we can choose

\tilde{t}_{*,\beta}=\Big(\frac{\tilde{\mathtt{c}}_{1}}{\tilde{\mathtt{c}}_{2}}\Big)^{2}+o_{\delta_{\beta}}(1)\,,

to have
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}(\tilde{t}_{*,\beta})=0
. Fixing
a,b
such that
0<a<t_{*}<b
, we conclude that

\mathcal{L}_{2}^{*}(\phi,\psi)-\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{2}^{*}-\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{*}(\phi,\psi)=0\,,

and therefore
(\phi_{\delta_{\beta}},\psi_{\delta_{\beta}})=(\phi[\tilde{t}_{*,\beta}],\psi[\tilde{t}_{*,\beta}])
solves (
3.7
).
∎
