Cardiac thrombus despite anticoagulation in a 76-year-old man
Authors: Harry Wang, Roy Khalife, Kevin Emery Boczar, Yan Xu
Published: 2025

A 76-year-old man on dabigatran for rate-controlled atrial fibrillation had follow-up transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) after bioprosthetic valve replacements. An echogenic, nonmobile mass (3.8 × 1.5 cm) in the left atrium was identified, which appeared to be attached adjacent to the pulmonary vein (Figure 1). Given the atypical attachment site, the mass was concerning for a thrombus and unlikely to be a myxoma or other solid mass. The patient reported increased lethargy, with no substantial change to his functional status and no other constitutional symptoms. He was admitted to cardiology for further workup and referred to our thrombosis service.

Transthoracic echocardiogram (apical 2-chambre view) from a 76-year-old man. Arrow indicates left atrial thrombus.

Six years before presentation, the patient had received the diagnosis of atrial fibrillation secondary to rheumatic heart disease, and anticoagulated with dabigatran (150 mg twice daily). A TTE at that time identified severe biatrial dilatation and multivalvular disease including aortic stenosis, mitral stenosis, and mitral regurgitation. He underwent bioprosthetic aortic and mitral valve replacement, tricuspid valve repair, and left atrial appendage ligation 1.5 years before presentation. One year before presentation, his anticoagulation was rotated to edoxaban (60 mg daily) because of a supply chain shortage.

Other medications included digoxin for rate control, rosuvastatin, pregabalin, and amitriptyline. Age-appropriate malignancy screening was up to date. On admission, the patient’s weight was 83 kg and his complete blood count was normal. His creatinine clearance was 85 mL/min, his international normalized ratio (INR) and partial thromboplastin time were within normal range, and blood cultures were negative.

The differential diagnosis of a left atrial mass is broad, including thrombus, infection, myxoma, lipoma, sarcoma, and metastatic tumour. Timely assessment of left atrial masses is crucial as left atrial appendage thrombi account for more than 90% of cardioembolic strokes in atrial fibrillation.

Initial evaluation for left atrial mass is typically done with TTE to assess size, location, mobility, hemodynamic effect, and pericardial involvement, but can be limited by image quality and tissue characterization. Transesophageal echocardiography (a) can be considered when valvular lesions are suspected (e.g., vegetations) and to provide characterization of a cardiac mass in terms of its size, morphology, and hemodynamic effects when TTE images are suboptimal.1 Given low suspicion for infective endocarditis in our patient, TEE was not indicated.

Cardiac MRI (b) is the preferred diagnostic test for noninvasive assessment of cardiac masses when there is uncertainty following echocardiography.2 Cardiac MRI allows for detailed evaluation of morphology, size, location, homogeneity, mass infiltration into surrounding tissues, and tissue characterization.2 Early gadolinium imaging can be helpful to identify thrombi, and first-pass perfusion can assess internal vascularity within a mass.

Cardiac CT is an alternative if cardiac MRI is unavailable or contraindicated (e.g., gadolinium contrast allergy, incompatible cardiac devices). It is useful in detecting and characterizing left atrial thrombi, small or calcified masses, and masses involving the great vessels.2 Thrombi typically demonstrate lower attenuation than the surrounding myocardium, unlike intracavitary tumours. Cardiac CT can be limited by artifact from devices or increased heart rate.2

Cardiac PET-CT using fluorine-18-labelled fluorodeoxyglucose tracer (c) can be used to differentiate between benign and malignant cardiac tumours and aid in the workup of suspected infective endocarditis but is not indicated in the first-line evaluation of cardiac masses when alternative conditions remain on the differential diagnosis.1 Although endomyocardial biopsy (d) is part of the diagnosis and management of cardiac tumours, noninvasive modalities represent first-line evaluations. Cardiac MRI, cardiac CT, and cardiac PET are all specialized tests that often require cardiology referral and interpretation.

Cardiac MRI showed a mass measuring 2.6 × 2.3 × 3.0 cm attached to the left atrial wall with T1 and T2 hyperintensity, with no enhancement on first-pass perfusion images and no late gadolinium enhancement (Figure 2). Overall features were in keeping with a thrombus.

Evaluation of a breakthrough thromboembolic event while a patient is on DOACs requires determination of potential causes of drug underexposure, including drug–drug interactions, previous bariatric surgery, and medication nonadherence (d).

Drug–drug interactions (a) through the cytochrome P450 3A4 and P-glycoprotein pathways may limit DOAC use.3 Antiepileptics including phenytoin, carbamazepine, and phenobarbital have been shown to decrease DOAC levels, while select macrolides and azoles can increase DOAC levels.

Bariatric surgery (b) can affect DOAC absorption through alterations in gastrointestinal anatomy, thus DOAC use is not recommended for patients who have undergone this procedure. Apixaban and edoxaban are partially absorbed in the proximal small intestine, whereas rivaroxaban has additional absorption in the distal stomach.3 Rivaroxaban bioavailability is improved with concurrent food intake, which can be limited after bariatric surgery. Patients with obesity may have lower DOAC concentrations because of increased volume of distribution.4

Although the INR is used to assess the therapeutic effect of warfarin and other vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), DOACs are not routinely monitored by coagulation testing and therefore lack validated therapeutic ranges. Thus, nonadherence to DOAC therapy can remain undetected (c). Across 7 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing DOACs with warfarin, rates of premature DOAC discontinuation and nonadherence ranged from 2.3% to 37%.5 Clinicians should assess DOAC adherence when patients develop thromboembolic events and consider interventions such as simplification of dosing regimens, reminder tools, and blister packaging.

Patients with thrombotic antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, mechanical heart valves, or atrial fibrillation in association with rheumatic heart disease are all at high risk of thromboembolism, even while on DOACs (d). Although DOACs are the cornerstone of therapy in nonvalvular atrial fibrillation and venous thromboembolism, VKAs (i.e., warfarin) remain the standard of care in these clinical situations.

In thrombotic antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (a), a meta-analysis of 4 RCTs comparing DOACs (3 with rivaroxaban, 1 with apixaban) with VKAs identified higher risks of subsequent arterial thrombotic events, especially ischemic strokes, among patients randomized to DOACs than among those on VKAs.6

Patients with mechanical heart valves (b) require lifelong anticoagulation. Two multicentre RCTs demonstrated increased risk of stroke and systemic thromboembolism among patients randomized to DOACs than among those on VKAs, including those with low-thrombogenicity aortic valves.7

Data on atrial fibrillation associated with valvular heart disease in the absence of mechanical heart valves are mixed. In an RCT involving 1005 participants with atrial fibrillation and bioprosthetic mitral valves, Guimarães and colleagues reported noninferiority of rivaroxaban 20 mg daily (15 mg for patients with a creatinine clearance of 30 to 49 mL/min) compared with warfarin (target INR 2.0 to 3.0) on a composite primary outcome of death, major cardiovascular events, or major bleeding at 12 months.8 Strokes occurred in 0.6% and 2.4% of patients in the rivaroxaban and warfarin groups, respectively (hazard ratio [HR] 0.25; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.07 to 0.88).8 In another RCT involving 4135 participants with rheumatic heart disease–associated atrial fibrillation (c), higher risks of ischemic stroke (1.1 v. 0.7 per 100 person-years; HR 1.53, 95% CI 1.06 to 2.20) and death (8.0 v. 6.4 per 100 person-years per year; HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.09 to 0.40) were found among patients treated with rivaroxaban compared with those treated with warfarin.9 Whether these trial findings are generalizable to patients after valvular repair or bioprosthetic replacement remains unclear.

Discussion

Cardiac thrombi often manifest as intracardial masses, with infectious and malignant causes as crucial differential diagnoses. Combining clinical context and advanced imaging techniques is key to appropriate confirmatory workup. Multimodal imaging (e.g., cardiac MRI) has enabled an increasing number of left atrial thrombi to be confirmed noninvasively, while pathological determination remains the gold standard in cases with diagnostic ambiguity.2 Left atrial thrombus is common among patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation, where DOACs have demonstrated similar rates of thrombus resolution as VKAs.10 However, patients with valvular atrial fibrillation require specific attention as DOACs are not recommended for patients with concurrent mechanical heart valves because of higher rates of stroke than with VKAs. Moreover, emerging data indicate inferior outcomes associated with DOACs compared with VKAs in rheumatic heart disease–associated atrial fibrillation.9

Although the efficacy and safety of DOACs have been well established through regulatory trials and post-marketing studies, a meta-analysis of 5 landmark phase III trials reported residual risks of ischemic stroke and systemic embolism among patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation, ranging from 0.6% to 2.5% per year depending on clinical risk factors.11 Thromboembolic events despite DOAC treatment requires the clinican to confirm patient adherence, review appropriate DOAC dosing, consider pharmacokinetics (drug–drug interactions, bariatric surgery, or volume of distribution), and evaluate the patient for acquired thrombophilic states (e.g., antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, occult malignancy). Contraindications specific to VKAs include acute heparin-induced thrombocytopenia due to risk of skin necrosis, and the presence of calciphylaxis.

Conclusion

For our patient, we discontinued edoxaban and started him on enoxaparin (1 mg/kg every 12 h) as a bridge to warfarin for long-term anticoagulation. His follow-up imaging showed a decrease in thrombus size. With the widespread adoption of DOACs over the past decade, thromboembolic events occurring despite therapy are increasingly encountered by clinicians. A structured, interdisciplinary approach that incorporates confirmatory imaging, review of indications and dosing, and evaluation for established causes of DOAC underexposure or failure is crucial to optimal anticoagulant management.
