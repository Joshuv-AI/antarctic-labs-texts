Exploring academic mobbing among graduate nursing students: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Serpil Özcan, Yasemin Erden, Bahar Çiftçi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMobbing in academic environments significantly affects individuals’ professional and psychological well-being. Due to their position in the educational hierarchy, graduate students may be more vulnerable to mobbing.ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine academic mobbing experiences among graduate nursing students and to explore the associated challenges and coping processes.MethodThis study used a mixed-methods design. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics, independent samples t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and Pearson correlation analysis. A total of 209 graduate nursing students participated in the quantitative component of the study. Twenty graduate students were included in the qualitative research. Qualitative data were obtained through semi-structured interviews and analysed using Colaizzi’s descriptive phenomenological method.FindingsQuantitative analyses revealed that doctoral students and research assistants had significantly higher total mobbing scores (p < 0.05), while female students reported significantly higher scores only in attacks on self-presentation and communication (p < 0.05). Qualitative findings indicated that communication barriers and professional pressures were prominent facilitators of mobbing. Qualitative analyses revealed that mobbing was primarily perpetrated by supervisors, administrators, and academic staff. In particular, interruptions, professional devaluation, excessive workload, and pressure via digital platforms were commonly reported. These findings highlight the role of academic hierarchy and tenure type in shaping mobbing experiences.ConclusionThe findings indicate that doctoral students and research assistants reported higher levels of academic mobbing, while female students were more vulnerable specifically to communication-based mobbing behaviors. Quantitative findings were complemented by qualitative data, which provided contextual insights into how participants perceived and reported mobbing experiences. However, this integration remains primarily interpretative and does not imply causal relationships between the identified factors and mobbing experiences. Specifically, mobbing was primarily experienced through communication barriers, professional pressures, and psychological coercion. These findings suggest consistency between quantitative and qualitative results and contribute to a better understanding of academic mobbing.Clinical trial numbernot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09561-5.Keywords: Graduate nursing students, Academic mobbing, Mixed methods

Introduction

Mobbing is defined as persistent and hostile behavior towards individuals in the workplace and has become a significant problem in various organizational contexts [8]. In this study, the term “mobbing” is used to describe systematic, prolonged, and group-based psychological harassment in the workplace, which is conceptually related to workplace bullying but often emphasizes collective and hierarchical dynamics within organizational settings. In academic environments, mobbing may manifest through sustained communication barriers, professional devaluation, and institutional power imbalances, distinguishing it from more individual and episodic forms of workplace bullying. The experience of mobbing can have a profoundly negative impact on individuals’ psychological well-being and significantly affect the organization’s overall productivity and atmosphere. Research indicates that the workplace environment is social, and interactions between members can lead to conflicts [12, 29]. The complexity of relationships and hierarchies can increase the likelihood of mobbing in educational settings. Individual competition, jealousy, and status differences can trigger hostile behaviour among colleagues [10, 17]. Moreover, leadership style in an organization plays a critical role in shaping the workplace climate. An inappropriate organizational climate, characterized by a lack of awareness of and support for mobbing, can facilitate the spread of such behavior [12, 29]. In education, where academic demands are high, this situation can create greater vulnerability to mobbing when intersecting with individual conflicts. The consequences of mobbing extend beyond individual suffering and can lead to significant organizational problems, including absenteeism, decreased job satisfaction, and a decline in the quality of education offered [10, 12].

For graduate students, mobbing experiences may lead to burnout, reduced interest in courses, and alienation from the academic environment. Graduate nursing students represent a particularly vulnerable group due to their dual roles as learners and healthcare providers, exposure to hierarchical academic and clinical environments, and dependence on supervisors for academic progression. This is extremely worrying, given that the profession depends on collaborative practices and supportive relationships for effective patient care and professional development. Research in Turkey indicates that mobbing is prevalent among academic staff and has a significant impact on mental health and organisational effectiveness [6, 7, 14]. The unique challenges that graduate students face, including high-risk assessments and the emotional demands of clinical practice, may further complicate their mobbing experience. Research indicates that younger professionals, including graduate students, are more likely to be exposed to mobbing behaviors [11, 13]. To address mobbing in graduate education, preventive strategies that foster a supportive, respectful academic environment may be necessary. This includes raising awareness about the signs of mobbing, encouraging open communication, and establishing clear policies that discourage such behavior. Furthermore, leadership in educational institutions may play an important role in promoting a positive organizational culture that values cooperation and mutual respect among all members.

In conclusion, the mobbing experiences of graduate students are shaped by a complex interplay of individual, organizational, and contextual factors. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for developing effective intervention strategies to protect students from the adverse effects of mobbing and to enhance their educational experience. Accordingly, this study aims to comprehensively examine academic mobbing experiences among graduate students using a mixed-methods approach. From a theoretical perspective, academic mobbing can be understood within the framework of organizational power dynamics and hierarchical theory. Academic institutions are inherently hierarchical structures in which power is distributed unevenly across roles such as supervisors, administrators, and graduate students. According to the power imbalance theory, individuals in subordinate positions are more vulnerable to systematic psychological pressure and exclusion. In addition, organizational behavior frameworks suggest that unclear role definitions, job insecurity, and competitive academic environments may increase the likelihood of mobbing behaviors. In this context, graduate students, particularly those dependent on supervisors for academic progression, represent a structurally vulnerable group. This theoretical positioning strengthens the interpretation of the findings and situates the study within the broader literature. Previous research indicates that exposure to workplace bullying may vary according to gender, age, academic seniority, and employment conditions. Female academics may be more vulnerable to communication-based and gendered forms of mobbing, whereas doctoral students and early-career academics may face increased pressure due to dependence on supervisors and career insecurity. Previous studies also suggest that gender may influence exposure to workplace mobbing in academic environments. Female academics and students may encounter greater communication barriers, interruptions in academic discussions, and higher levels of psychological pressure within hierarchical institutional structures. Therefore, examining whether mobbing experiences differ according to gender constitutes an important dimension in understanding communication-related mobbing in academic settings. Age may also be relevant because it can reflect differences in professional maturity, social positioning, and exposure to hierarchical academic environments. In addition, academic position, work experience, and institutional hierarchy may influence power dynamics, collegial support, and exposure to mobbing behaviours in academic settings.

Method

This study employed a convergent parallel mixed-methods design, in which quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently, analysed separately, and integrated during interpretation to provide a comprehensive understanding of academic mobbing. The quantitative component followed a cross-sectional survey design, while the qualitative component used a descriptive phenomenological approach based on Colaizzi’s method. Quantitative data were gathered using the Mobbing Scale for Academicians (MSA) to assess the level of mobbing experienced by graduate students. Concurrently, qualitative data were collected through semi-structured interviews to gain deeper insight into participants’ personal experiences and perspectives. The findings were integrated following independent analyses of both datasets—quantitative data using descriptive and inferential statistics, and qualitative data analysed through Colaizzi’s descriptive phenomenological method—to achieve a broader and richer interpretation of mobbing phenomena in the academic environment among graduate students. This methodological approach enabled a holistic exploration by capturing both generalizable trends (quantitative findings) and detailed personal experiences (qualitative findings), thereby providing a stronger basis for interpreting the findings and considering practical implications. The study was reported in accordance with the Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) checklist.

Population and sample of the study

The study population consisted of graduate nursing students enrolled in master’s and doctoral programs at universities in Türkiye between October 2024 and January 2025. For the quantitative component, participants were recruited through an online survey distributed to graduate nursing students enrolled in graduate programs at universities in Türkiye. The survey link was shared nationwide through open academic communication channels, including academic networks, graduate student groups, institutional mailing lists, and publicly accessible academic platforms. Because the survey was disseminated broadly across Türkiye rather than administered at a single institution, participants were not recruited from a single university. In addition, data were collected anonymously; therefore, institution-specific participant counts could not be reported. Because the survey was distributed through nationwide open academic communication channels rather than administered within a specific institution, additional institutional permission from individual universities was not required. Participation was voluntary, and students who agreed to participate completed the questionnaire anonymously. The sample size for the quantitative data in the research was calculated using G-Power to ensure reliable and suitable results for statistical analysis. A priori power analysis in G*Power 3.1.9.4 was used to determine the sample size. For the a priori power analysis, the effect size was estimated as moderate-to-large based on previous studies with similar designs. Based on the power analysis, a minimum sample size of 190 participants was required; accounting for potential data loss, 209 participants were included [4, 15]. Considering the possibility of 10% data loss [22], a sample size of 209 graduate students was finalised for this study. A criterion sampling method was used for the qualitative data, and individuals who met specific criteria were selected for interviews. Qualitative interviews were terminated upon reaching data saturation, and qualitative data were collected from 20 students. The criterion sampling method enabled the selection of individuals who met specific criteria, a process guided by Johnson and Christensen [20] and Yıldırım and Şimşek [30]. Of the participants, 71.8% were female, 50.7% were married, 52.6% were doctoral students, and 26.8% were research assistants employed under Article 50/d. In the Turkish higher education system, research assistants may be appointed under different legal statuses. Research assistants under Article 33/a hold relatively more stable staff positions, whereas research assistants under Article 50/d are employed on a more temporary basis and their appointments generally end upon completion of their graduate education. Because these two statuses differ in terms of job security and employment continuity, they were evaluated separately in this study. The mean age was 30.27 ± 4.36 years and the mean work experience was 6.26 ± 4.34 years.

Data collection tools

The research data were collected using forms prepared by the researchers, including the socio-demographic characteristics of graduate students (age, gender, etc.), open-ended semi-structured interview questions, and the Mobbing Scale for Academicians (MSA).

Graduate student information form

It includes questions on age, gender, marital status, and other relevant details.

Mobbing Scale for Academics (MSA)

To determine mobbing behaviors toward academic staff, Çöğen and Asunakutlu (2014) adapted the mobbing scale originally developed by Leymann [26] and developed the Mobbing Scale for Academics (MSA). The scale consists of 23 items and five subscales: Attacks on Self-Presentation and Communication (6 items), Attacks on Social Relationships (4 items), Reputational Attacks (3 items), Attacks on Professional Status (7 items), and Attacks on Psychological Health (3 items). Higher scores indicate greater exposure to mobbing behaviors. In the original study, the scale suggests excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.98), and subscale reliability coefficients ranged from 0.920 to 0.951. In the present study, the internal consistency of the scale was also assessed, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the total scale was 0.96, indicating high reliability in the current sample.

Semi-structured interview form

The qualitative data were collected using a semi-structured interview guide developed in parallel with the quantitative scale. The interview guide comprised five open-ended questions designed to explore graduate students’ experiences of academic mobbing, the challenges they encountered, their coping strategies, and their suggestions for preventing mobbing in academic environments (Appendix 3). The questions were developed by the researchers based on an extensive literature review. Probing questions were also used during the interviews to deepen the understanding of participants’ responses and to encourage more detailed descriptions of their experiences [30]. The initial draft of the interview form was evaluated by 10 experts in nursing, qualitative research, and academic workplace behaviour for content and semantic validity. The experts were asked to rate each item on a four-point scale (1 = not suitable, 4 = very suitable) and provide recommendations for improvement. Based on their feedback, the interview questions were revised and finalised. The English version of the semi-structured interview questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Pilot study

After the content and semantic evaluation by the experts, a pilot study was conducted with five graduate students to assess the clarity, comprehensibility, and flow of the semi-structured interview questions. During the pilot interviews, participants were asked whether any items were unclear, difficult to understand, or repetitive. Based on the feedback obtained, minor revisions were made to the wording and sequence of some questions to improve clarity and coherence. No major changes were made to the interview guide’s content. The data obtained from the pilot study were not included in the final analysis.

Data collection

Quantitative data were collected through an online survey including the Graduate Student Information Form and the Mobbing Scale for Academics (MSA). Before completing the questionnaire, participants were informed of the study’s aim and provided electronic informed consent.

For the qualitative phase, semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted with graduate students who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate. Participants who completed the survey and indicated their willingness to participate in the qualitative phase were subsequently contacted by the researchers to schedule interviews. The interviews were conducted online via Zoom to ensure accessibility and privacy. Each interview lasted approximately 30 min and was audio-recorded with participants’ verbal consent. The interviews focused on participants’ experiences of academic mobbing, the challenges they encountered, their coping strategies, and their suggestions for preventing mobbing in academic environments. All interviews were transcribed verbatim by the researcher, and participant quotations were presented in the results section using coded identifiers (e.g., P1, P2) to ensure anonymity.

Data analyses

Before conducting inferential statistical analyses, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were examined. Normality of the scale and subscale scores was assessed using skewness–kurtosis values and visual inspections of histograms and Q–Q plots. Homogeneity of variance was tested using Levene’s test. Since the data approximately met the assumptions of normal distribution and variance homogeneity, parametric tests were used in the analysis. The quantitative data obtained from the survey were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarise participants’ demographic characteristics and mobbing scale scores. Independent-samples t-tests were used to examine differences in mobbing scores according to gender and graduate degree level. One-way ANOVA was used to analyse differences across more than two groups by academic position. When significant differences were detected, LSD post-hoc tests were conducted to determine the source of the differences. Effect sizes were calculated and reported as Cohen’s d for t-tests and eta-squared (η²) for ANOVA to enhance the interpretability of the findings. Additionally, 95% confidence intervals were reported where appropriate to enhance the precision and interpretability of the findings. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine relationships between continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Justification of Colaizzi’s method

Colaizzi’s method was preferred in this study due to its robust analytical structure and ability to elicit the essence of the participants’ lived experiences in detail. Researching a sensitive topic such as mobbing based on individual perceptions requires accurately and authentically representing the participants’ voices.

This method provides a robust framework for understanding students’ mobbing experiences in the academic environment, identifying primary themes, and elucidating the cause-and-effect relationships associated with these experiences. The study’s mixed-methods approach enabled the measurement of the mobbing phenomenon using quantitative data, as well as its in-depth understanding through qualitative data. The study’s findings may inform preventive policies and interventions aimed at promoting a healthier academic environment.

Research ethics principles

Ethical approval was obtained from the Erzurum Technical University Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee for this study (Meeting No: 11, Decision No: 10, Date: 10.10.2024). The purpose of the research and the data collection method were explained in detail to the graduate students, and it was emphasised that their participation was completely voluntary. Verbal informed consent was obtained from those who volunteered to participate in the study. It was noted that the data would be used solely within the scope of this study, and no participant identity information was collected.

To ensure anonymity and participant confidentiality, participants were assigned codes (e.g., P1 and P2) in the report. All audio recordings and written documents are encrypted and accessible only by the research team. During the research process, the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki were fully adhered to, acting within the framework of established moral guidelines.

Results

The results are presented according to the research questions. First, the quantitative findings describing the level of academic mobbing and associated factors are presented. Then, qualitative findings are presented to deepen understanding of the quantitative results by explaining how mobbing was experienced, who perpetrated it, which challenges emerged, and what strategies and recommendations participants reported.

Qualitative findings

As a result of the data obtained from qualitative interviews, it was found that graduate students were mobbed mainly through other faculty members (P1, P2, P3, P6, P7, P9, P10, P11, P12, P14, P15, P16, P17), and then from their supervisors (P1, P6, P7, P8, P10, P12, P13, P15, P16, P17, P20) and also from institutional administrators and department heads (P4, P5, P7, P11, P12, P18). When the processes in which graduate students were mobbed were analyzed, it was determined that they were mobbed mainly in the course (P1, P2, P4, P6, P8, P10, P11, P12, P14, P16, P17) and during the thesis period (P1, P6, P7, P8, P10, P11, P13, P15, P16, P20).

As a result of the interviews conducted to evaluate the mobbing experience of graduate students in academia, seven themes and 24 codes were created (Table 3). Qualitative findings addressing the research questions “How do graduate students describe their experiences of mobbing in the academic environment?” and “What challenges do graduate students face due to mobbing in the academic environment?” showed that graduate students were exposed to mobbing mainly by other faculty members, followed by supervisors, institutional administrators, and department heads. These experiences occurred most commonly during coursework and the thesis period. Themes 1 to 5 addressed how graduate students described their mobbing experiences and the challenges they encountered in academic environments. Theme 6 reflected participants’ coping responses to academic mobbing, and Theme 7 covered their recommendations for preventing mobbing in academic settings.

Theme 1. attacks on self-presentation and communication

This theme reflects communication-based mobbing experienced by graduate students in academic interactions. Participants reported being interrupted during conversations, prevented from expressing their opinions, and subjected to harsh or humiliating communication styles by academic authorities. Such behaviors were perceived as attempts to suppress students’ voices and limit their participation in academic discussions. Five codes were determined for Theme 1. Interview examples for the determined codes are as follows;

P1: “I was prevented from communicating, I was interrupted, I was unfairly scolded.” (28 years old, female, married, PhD, research assistant 33/a).

P5:” …being constantly interrupted, scolded, and continuously disturbed by communication tools.” (32 years old, female, single, PhD, lecturer).

P10: “I receive tasks every hour via WhatsApp. I am scolded for creating a discussion environment while talking.” (31 years old, female, single, PhD, research assistant 33/a).

Theme 2. attacks on social relationships

This theme describes attempts to isolate graduate students socially within the academic environment. Participants reported that their relationships with colleagues were deliberately restricted, and in some cases they were portrayed as problematic individuals to discourage others from interacting with them. These practices created feelings of isolation and weakened professional collaboration. Two codes were identified for Theme 2. Interview examples for the determined codes are as follows;

P14: “My dialogue with other colleagues was prevented.” (38 years old, female, married, PhD, research assistant 50/d).

P19: “He prevents them from being with me by making me look like a problematic person and implying that those who are with me will be harmed.” (32 years old, female, single, master’s degree, lecturer).

Theme 3. attacks on reputation

This theme reflects practices aimed at damaging the professional reputation of graduate students. Participants described situations in which unfounded rumors were spread, their academic work was obstructed, and they were excluded from meetings or collaborative activities. These behaviors were perceived as attempts to undermine their academic credibility and professional image. Six codes were identified for Theme 3. Interview examples for the determined codes are as follows;

P7: “Unfounded rumors were deliberately spread among my colleagues. The assigned tasks were frequently changed without any justification. I was deliberately not invited to meetings.” (29 years old, male, married, PhD, lecturer).

P9: “I was subjected to gossip and insults, I was insistently given jobs that were not in our job description, my academic studies were prevented, and they tried to break up my relationship with the people I published with.” (32 years old, female, married, master’s degree, research assistant 33/a).

P11: “I was subjected to a sexist approach regarding breastfeeding leave, and my academic progress was hindered by being overloaded with work.” (32 years old, female, married, master’s degree, lecturer).

Theme 4. attacks on professional status

This theme represents actions that undermine graduate students’ professional roles and responsibilities. Participants reported being assigned tasks outside their academic duties or being forced to perform responsibilities unrelated to their qualifications. Such practices were perceived as attempts to diminish their academic status and autonomy. Three codes were identified for Theme 4. Interview examples for the determined codes are as follows;

P2: “The lecturers of the department say that you will come and see us every day. We are given the task of collecting publications in the institution” (26 years old, female, single, PhD, research assistant 33/a).

P18: “My manager forces me to do tasks for which I have no qualifications.” (28 years old, male, single, master’s degree, research assistant 50/d).

This theme reflects psychological pressure and emotional distress experienced by graduate students as a result of mobbing behaviors. Discussion

The mixed-methods design enabled the integration of quantitative and qualitative findings. While the quantitative analysis identified which groups were more exposed to mobbing and in which dimensions mobbing behaviors were more prominent, the qualitative findings provided contextual insights into how these experiences were reported and perceived in daily academic life. Beyond descriptive alignment, the integration of quantitative and qualitative findings enabled the development of meta-inferences regarding how academic hierarchy and organizational dependency may shape mobbing experiences among graduate students. Specifically, doctoral students and research assistants reported higher mobbing scores quantitatively, while qualitative findings revealed repeated experiences of communication restriction, excessive workload, professional devaluation, and hierarchical pressure. When interpreted together, these findings may suggest that individuals occupying structurally dependent positions within academic systems are more vulnerable to mobbing behaviors. Similarly, female students’ higher scores in communication-related mobbing dimensions were supported by qualitative narratives describing interruption, silencing, and exclusion from academic discussions. These convergent mixed-methods findings may indicate that mobbing experiences are shaped not only by individual interactions but also by broader organizational and hierarchical dynamics within academic environments. These interpretations should be considered with caution, particularly given the cross-sectional design and the relatively small-to-moderate effect sizes observed in the study.

Research findings indicate that female students reported higher levels of communication-related mobbing behaviors, particularly interruptions, scolding, and restricted academic expression. In the literature, it has been reported that female academics and students are more frequently victims of mobbing and face obstacles in academic communication processes than their male colleagues [2, 21, 24]. Almarwani et al., [2] reported that the impact of academic pressure on female students was more significant, with negative consequences for their academic achievement and self-confidence. Similarly, Zhou et al., [32] reported that female academics occupy lower academic positions, making them more vulnerable to mobbing. Qualitative data also support these results. In this respect, the qualitative findings help explain the quantitative result showing higher scores among female students in the communication-related mobbing dimension, as the interview data revealed repeated experiences of interruption, unfair scolding, and communication pressure. When the interview data were analysed, it was seen that female students were subjected to psychological pressure by being interrupted, prevented from communicating, and unfairly scolded in the academic environment. For example, P1 and P5 stated that they were constantly interrupted and pressurized by administrators while trying to express their opinions in academic meetings and platforms. P10 indicated that they were assigned tasks continuously through digital platforms, which created psychological pressure. These findings may indicate that academic mobbing can also be reflected in digital communication channels [31]. It is reported that female academics are exposed to more verbal attacks, academic devaluation, and verbal violence compared to their male colleagues [18, 25]. Keashly, [23] suggests that such pressures lead to academic burnout, loss of motivation, and slow career progression. Cieri et al., [9] emphasize that power imbalances in the academic hierarchy make women more vulnerable. Gendered academic hierarchies and unequal access to decision-making positions may contribute to women’s increased vulnerability to academic mobbing. From a power imbalance theory perspective, female graduate students may occupy more vulnerable positions within hierarchical academic structures, particularly in communication-based interactions where authority and legitimacy are negotiated. Organizational dynamics theory further suggests that unequal access to institutional power and decision-making processes may reinforce communication-related mobbing behaviors toward individuals in subordinate academic roles. These findings suggest the potential value of transparent reporting mechanisms, institutional support systems, and gender-sensitive academic policies.

The findings of this study suggest that academic status may be associated with differences in psychological pressure and communication barriers. Doctoral students appeared to report higher levels of psychological and professional pressure in academic processes. According to the literature, competition and expectations increase as one progresses through the academic hierarchy, thereby increasing the risk of mobbing at the doctoral level [2]. Qualitative findings support this result. These qualitative accounts help explain the quantitative finding that doctoral students reported higher mobbing scores by illustrating how communication barriers, administrative pressure, and non-academic task assignments are experienced during doctoral training. Doctoral students reported that their communication in the academic environment was deliberately hindered, and administrators exerted pressure on them. P1 reported being interrupted and scolded while trying to express her ideas. P5 stated that he was pressurised through verbal interventions and communication tools on academic platforms. P10 noted that he was constantly assigned tasks on digital platforms, which led to psychological pressure. These findings indicate that academic mobbing may not be limited to physical spaces and may also occur through digital communication tools [31]. Depending on the academic status, the types of mobbing experienced differ. Doctoral students primarily face attacks on their professional roles and more frequent systematic pressures related to their academic status. P2 stated that her department’s lecturers pressured her to be physically present and assigned her non-academic tasks. P18 stated that her supervisor required her to perform low-quality, irrelevant tasks that did not support her academic development. The literature reports that individuals lower in the academic hierarchy are pressured by more senior academics and are often tried to be excluded from research processes [32]. Increased academic competition, research expectations, and power dynamics may partially explain the higher mobbing scores reported by doctoral students. These findings may also be interpreted within frameworks of organizational hierarchy, which propose that individuals with high performance expectations but limited institutional authority are at greater risk of psychological pressure and role-related coercion. Doctoral students often remain academically dependent on supervisors and institutional evaluations, which may intensify vulnerability to communication barriers and professional pressure. With increased academic expectations and responsibilities, doctoral students may experience greater academic and professional strain. Master’s students, on the other hand, feel less academic pressure but may encounter communication barriers and challenges in social relationships.

The findings suggest that academic position may be associated with mobbing experiences. The fact that the total scores of research assistants employed under Article 33/a and Article 50/d, as well as lecturers, were significantly higher than those of specialist staff may suggest that individuals at lower levels in the academic hierarchy are exposed to greater psychological and professional pressure [32]. Especially the lack of job security for research assistants with 50/d status may be a main reason for this situation. Individuals in 50/d positions experience uncertainty in their academic careers because their positions typically end upon completion of their doctoral studies. This precariousness may increase vulnerability to workplace authority and potential exposure to mobbing. From an organizational dynamics perspective, insecure employment conditions may reinforce unequal power relations by limiting individuals’ ability to resist authority or report negative experiences. This may partially explain why research assistants with temporary or unstable contracts reported higher mobbing scores. Qualitative findings support this result. More specifically, the interview data help interpret the quantitative finding that research assistants and lecturers reported higher mobbing scores by illustrating experiences of communication barriers, administrative pressure, and non-academic task assignments. P1 stated that communication was deliberately blocked in academic processes and that she was subjected to scolding by managers. P5 noted that she was constantly interrupted on academic platforms and that administrators pressured her through communication tools. P18 stated that her manager forced her to perform low-quality, irrelevant tasks that hindered her academic development. The literature also indicates that individuals at lower levels in the academic hierarchy are forced to perform non-academic drudgery, which hinders their academic progress [2]. In addition, factors such as research assistants not receiving additional course fees despite attending classes and being assigned every job due to unclear job descriptions increase professional pressure.

In summary, research assistants, especially those in 50/d status, may be more vulnerable to mobbing experiences. The fact that their job descriptions are unclear and they are assigned to non-academic jobs also increases the pressure. Academic institutions may consider implementing measures to protect the rights of research assistants, clarify job descriptions, and support their academic development [31].

Weak positive associations were also observed for age and work experience. Age was associated with communication-related mobbing, whereas work experience was associated with attacks on social relationships. Although these relationships were limited in magnitude, they may suggest that positioning within academic settings, prolonged exposure to hierarchical structures, and changing collegial dynamics can shape communication patterns, authority relations, and vulnerability to exclusion. The qualitative findings also supported this interpretation, as participants described blocked communication, restricted self-expression, and social isolation within academic environments. The findings may suggest that individuals who remain in academic environments for extended periods can experience greater social isolation and professional exclusion. The literature supports this, showing that experienced academics are more susceptible to social exclusion and professional loneliness [2]. Qualitative data also confirm these findings. In other words, the interview data help clarify the weak but significant quantitative association between work experience and attacks on social relationships by illustrating experiences of exclusion, blocked communication, and social isolation. The participants reported that their communication with colleagues in the academic environment was limited, and they experienced deliberate isolation, being excluded from the educational community. For example, P14 noted that she was deliberately prevented from communicating with other academics.

In contrast, P19 stated that administrators and colleagues portrayed her as “problematic” and implied that those who communicated with her could be harmed. Such psychological pressures may contribute to individual isolation and reduced academic well-being [31]. One possible explanation for this situation may be the inherent power dynamics within the academic hierarchy. Long-term academics may experience greater risk of exclusion from management processes over time and may struggle to maintain their status. New staff recruitments and managerial changes in the academic world tend to marginalise existing academics [32]. In this context, academic institutions may consider developing policies that reduce social isolation, encourage academic collaboration, and support long-term academics in maintaining their professional networks.

Graduate students’ reactions to academic mobbing were generally perceived as remaining silent, applying to institutional complaint mechanisms, or exhibiting individual resistance. However, qualitative findings revealed that many participants complained to the administration but received no response (P5) or were prevented from speaking (P17), despite their attempts to defend themselves. These findings may reflect how academic mobbing is perceived and experienced at both individual and institutional levels. It has also been emphasized in previous studies that the hierarchical structure of academic institutions fosters mobbing, and the administration does not adequately support students [3]. It has been reported that victims of academic mobbing experience burnout, depression, and isolation in the long term, and even tend to abandon their academic careers or change countries ([1]; Fernández-Gutiérrez, [16]). Participants offered various suggestions for preventing mobbing. P6’s statement that “mobbing reporting units should be established” reveals the inadequacy of existing institutional complaint mechanisms. It was stated that the necessity of independent audit mechanisms can provide effective solutions against mobbing [2, 28]. P8’s suggestion that “awareness training on mobbing should be provided” reveals that academic institutions should raise awareness against this problem. Courtney-Pratt et al., [5] suggest that awareness training reduces the severity of mobbing in studies conducted in health sciences faculties. Participants also emphasised that legal regulations should be strengthened to prevent academic mobbing. P20’s statement that “legal regulations should be clearer and sharper” reveals that academic mobbing should be prevented not only through ethical rules but also through legal sanctions. However, many countries’ lack of specific laws on academic mobbing makes the victims legally disadvantaged [27]. It has been stated that academic institutions’ internal policies and regulations are generally insufficient in preventing mobbing, and more stringent control mechanisms are needed [19].

In summary, graduate students react differently to academic mobbing, but often struggle to find support. To address mobbing, institutional complaint mechanisms may need to be strengthened, awareness training expanded, and legal regulations clarified. The implementation of such measures may contribute to a healthier and safer academic environment. Overall, integrating quantitative and qualitative findings yielded a clearer, more coherent understanding of academic mobbing. While the quantitative results identified groups with higher mobbing scores and the dimensions in which mobbing was more prominent, the qualitative findings explained how these experiences manifested in daily academic life. In particular, the interview data provided concrete examples of communication barriers, professional pressure, social exclusion, and ineffective institutional responses, thereby offering a deeper understanding of the statistical patterns. This integrated interpretation may contribute to clearer alignment between statistical results and participants’ experiences. These integrated interpretations should be considered cautiously, particularly given the cross-sectional nature of the study and the relatively small-to-moderate effect sizes observed. The findings identify patterns of association between academic characteristics and mobbing experiences rather than definitive causal pathways. Therefore, the qualitative findings should be understood as contextual explanations that complement, rather than confirm causally, the quantitative results. Therefore, the statistical findings should not be interpreted as indicating strong deterministic relationships. Instead, the observed effect sizes may reflect the cumulative influence of multiple organizational, interpersonal, and structural factors contributing to mobbing experiences in academic settings. Even modest effects may become practically meaningful when repeated over long periods within hierarchical and high-pressure educational environments. From a practical perspective, these effect sizes suggest that mobbing experiences are likely influenced by multiple interacting factors rather than a single dominant variable. However, even small effect sizes may have meaningful implications in cumulative and organizational contexts, particularly in hierarchical academic environments where repeated exposure to mobbing behaviors can lead to substantial psychological and professional consequences over time. In the context of nursing education, even modest differences in mobbing exposure may have important implications for students’ psychological well-being, academic engagement, and professional development. Therefore, these findings may be relevant not only statistically but also to educational practice and institutional policy. The results highlight patterns of association rather than definitive causal relationships.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the research was conducted within a specific timeframe (October 2024 – January 2025) and included graduate nursing students from universities in Türkiye; therefore, the generalisability of the findings to different cultural and institutional contexts may be limited.

The use of nationwide open academic networks for participant recruitment may have introduced self-selection bias, as participation was voluntary and may have attracted individuals with prior experiences or interest in mobbing. In addition, because the survey was distributed through open networks rather than institutional channels, institution-specific representation could not be ensured, and the findings may not reflect all universities equally. The use of non-probability sampling further limits the sample’s representativeness. Inclusion criteria were defined as being enrolled in a graduate nursing program (master’s or doctoral level) and voluntarily agreeing to participate in the study. Although participants were recruited from different institutions across Türkiye, detailed institutional distribution could not be determined.

The qualitative component was limited to 20 participants. While criterion sampling ensured the inclusion of relevant participants, a larger and more diverse sample could have provided a broader range of perspectives and enhanced transferability. Additionally, data were based on self-reported experiences, which may be subject to recall bias and individual interpretation. The cross-sectional design limits causal inferences and the assessment of long-term effects. Furthermore, qualitative data were collected via online interviews (Zoom), which may have restricted the observation of nonverbal cues and influenced the depth of participants’ responses.

These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. Future studies using longitudinal designs and more representative sampling strategies are recommended.

Conclusion and recommendations

The findings of this study indicate that graduate students may experience academic mobbing in the academic environment. Doctoral students, research assistants, and female students appeared to be more vulnerable to communication-based mobbing behaviors, including communication barriers, social isolation, attacks on academic reputation, and professional pressures. These patterns may reflect the influence of hierarchical dependency, power imbalances within organizations, and role-related vulnerability in academic environments. According to participants’ reports, mobbing behaviors were most frequently attributed to senior academic staff, supervisors, and administrators, and were most commonly experienced during the course and thesis periods. Most students remain silent in the face of mobbing or resort to institutional complaint mechanisms; however, these attempts are largely ineffective. The findings of this study suggest that academic institutions may benefit from strengthening reporting mechanisms, establishing independent monitoring systems, and developing clearer institutional policies to address academic mobbing. Additionally, awareness training for academic managers may be enhanced, and legal regulations on mobbing may be clarified further.
