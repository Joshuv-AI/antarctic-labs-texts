Triple-armed oncolytic HSV enhances antitumor immunity by remodeling the tumor microenvironment in subcutaneous murine CRC
Authors: Lei Zhang, Kunpeng Zheng, Wu Tong, Fengnian Chen, Yanbing Zhu, Shuxin Han, Zhenghai Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryColorectal cancer (CRC) is characterized by therapeutic resistance and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). In the present study, five oncolytic herpes simplex virus type 1 (oHSV) variants were constructed based on the herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) strain 17 via deletion of the IR and ICP47 genes and insertion of the genes encoding mIL-12, mIL-15, and mCCL5. In vitro, these viruses exhibited replication kinetics comparable to those of the wild-type virus, stably expressed the transgenes, and exerted dose- and time-dependent oncolytic effects against colorectal cancer cells. In vivo, the triple-gene-armed oHSV RG2012m remodeled the tumor microenvironment by recruiting and activating immune cells as well as promoting their survival, thereby enhancing antitumor efficacy. Specifically, it elevated the levels of antitumor cytokines and cytotoxic molecules while reducing the abundance of immunosuppressive factors. These oncolytic herpes simplex viruses may function via an “oncolysis-chemotaxis-immune activation” cascade, providing a preclinical rationale for the treatment of colorectal cancer.Subject Areas: health sciences, medicine, oncology

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a highly prevalent malignant tumor worldwide, and its clinical treatment poses substantial challenges.1 CRC treatment faces two key challenges: the inherent resistance of tumor cells to traditional therapies and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) enriched with suppressive cells. Cancer viroimmunotherapy employs oncolytic viruses (OVs) to lyse tumor cells, remodel the TME, and induce systemic antitumor immunity.2,3,4

OVs selectively infect tumor cells through the recognition of specific receptors (e.g., integrins and CAR receptors), which are highly expressed on the surface of tumor cells. These viruses subsequently undergo conditional replication within tumor cells, ultimately resulting in tumor cell lysis; this phenomenon is defined as the “oncolytic effect”.5 This process eliminates tumor cells and releases immune danger signals, while viral infection disrupts the immune tolerance barrier in the tumor TME formed by regulatory T cells (Tregs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). Viroimmunotherapy induces systemic antitumor immunity through antigen-presenting cells (APCs), activates immune cells to clear residual tumor cells, circulating tumor cells, and metastases, and thereby enhances treatment efficacy.6,7,8 Engineered OVs function as gene delivery platforms, which carry cytokines to better reshape the TME and activate immune responses.9 Clinical studies have verified that viroimmunotherapy combined with other therapies exerts synergistic antitumor effects, markedly elevating response rates and improving survival outcomes in patients with advanced cancer.8,10,11

Genetic engineering techniques have been utilized to enhance the tumor selectivity and immunostimulatory properties of HSV-1.12,13,14,15 The replication capacity and tropism of HSV-1 can be regulated via genetic manipulation. The internal inverted repeat (IR) sequence of HSV-1 is essential for viral genome replication and capsid assembly. Deletion of the IR region conditions viral replication on the active DNA synthesis machinery of tumor cells, resulting in “conditional replication” and thereby reducing toxicity to normal cells.16 Furthermore, knockout of the ICP47 gene alleviates the inhibition of MHC-I-mediated antigen presentation, enabling tumor cells to present both viral antigens and tumor-associated antigens (TAAs); this in turn enhances the capacity of T cells to recognize target cells and become activated.17

A key “pioneer signal” molecule can specifically bind to the CCR5 receptor on the surface of T cells and NK cells, thereby directionally recruiting circulating immune cells to cross the vascular barrier and infiltrate the core area of the tumor. This process effectively disrupts the local immunosuppressive state of typical “cold tumors” (e.g., CRC).18,19 Interleukin-12 (IL-12) serves as the “activation hub” of the immune response. It drives the differentiation of naive T cells into effector T cells by activating the STAT4 signaling pathway. Meanwhile, it induces NK cells and CD8+ T cells to secrete IFN-γ, thereby triggering an “activation-amplification” immune cascade.20 Interleukin-15 (IL-15) plays a critical role in sustaining the survival of these cells. Binding to the IL-15Rα/βγc receptor complex significantly inhibits the apoptotic process of effector T cells and NK cells, thereby prolonging their survival in the tumor microenvironment and preserving their cytotoxic function. Preclinical studies have confirmed that IL-15 can extend the survival of tumor-infiltrating T cells and NK cells by 2–3-folds.21 The synergistic effect of these three cytokines is particularly crucial: CCL5 initiates the recruitment process of immune cells, IL-12 dominates the activation of effector cells, and the secretion of IFN-γ and IL-15 maintains the persistence of the immune effect. Compared with OVs that express only a single cytokine, specifically granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (e.g., T-VEC), the triple-armed oncolytic HSV-1 RG2012m (which co-expresses mIL-12, mIL-15, and mCCL5) demonstrates significant advantages in the treatment of CRC by addressing the key limitations of single-cytokine strategies. The single-cytokine approach of T-VEC targets only one link in immune activation and fails to cover the complete immune regulation process; thus, it exhibits limited efficacy in colorectal cancer characterized by a complex immunosuppressive TME.22,23,24

In this study, a oncolytic virus platform was constructed based on the HSV-1 17 strain. Using bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) recombination technology, the IR region and the ICP47 gene were deleted; this deletion enhanced tumor-selective replication and reduced viral immune escape. Furthermore, mIL-12, mIL-15, and mCCL5 were precisely integrated into the gene deletion sites, leading to the successful generation of a series of recombinant viruses (RG2001m, RG2002m, RG2005m, RG2011m, and RG2012m). It is worth noting that RG2012m, which co-expresses these three genes, may exhibit the capacity to mediate synergistic regulation of immune cell chemotaxis and effector cell activation. We systematically evaluated the replication kinetic characteristics, exogenous gene expression patterns, and in vitro oncolytic activity of these recombinant viruses in colorectal cancer cell lines. Additionally, we verified their in vivo antitumor efficacy and regulatory effects on the tumor immune microenvironment in a mouse CT26 tumor-bearing model. The objective of this study was to determine whether this multimodification strategy could synergistically enhance the oncolytic effect of HSV-1 and the antitumor immune response, thereby exploring the potential for clinical translation of viral immunotherapy in colorectal cancer.

Results

Gene engineering technology has successfully generated various oncolytic HSV-1 strains

We utilized the HSV-1 strain 17 as an oncolytic viral vector. This vector exhibits several advantages, including the capacity for conditional replication within tumor cells, high cargo-loading capacity, and controllability, which are primarily attributed to the deletion of its IR sequence and the ICP47 gene. With the aid of recombinant engineering technology based on the BAC vector, we first constructed a BAC vector harboring the HSV-1 strain 17 genome. Building on this foundation, we further modified the BAC vector via homologous recombination and successfully generated a series of recombinant viruses: (1) RG2001m, which carries the mIL15 expression cassette at the IR deletion site; (2) RG2002m, which carries the mIL12 expression cassette at the IR deletion site; (3) RG2005m, which carries the tandem expression cassette of mIL15 and mIL12 at the IR deletion site; (4) RG2011m, which carries the CCL5 expression cassette at the ICP47 deletion site; and (5) RG2012m, which carries the tandem expression cassette of mIL15 and mIL12 at the IR deletion site and the CCL5 expression cassette at the ICP47 deletion site. A schematic diagram depicting the construction strategy of these recombinant viruses is provided in Figure 1A. After infecting HSV-1-susceptible Vero cells with these recombinant viruses, distinct viral plaques were observed (Figure 1B). PCR identification of the viruses confirmed that the amplified bands corresponded to the expected sizes, thus verifying the successful generation of the correct recombinant oncolytic HSV-1 vectors (Figure 1C).

Retained replication kinetics and expression of carried exogenous genes in all genetically engineered oncolytic HSV-1 variants

To assess the impact of genetic engineering on the virus, we measured the titers (Figure 2A) and growth curves (Figure 2B) of HSV-1/WT and oHSV-1 (RG2001m, RG2002m, RG2005m, RG2011m, and RG2012m) in Vero cells. The results showed that after 48 h of culture, complete cytopathic effects were observed; however, the Vero cells did not lyse or die. At this time point, viral titer determination revealed that the titers of recombinant oncolytic HSV-1 strains were significantly lower than those of HSV-1/WT (p < 0.05), with an approximate 2-fold difference. No significant difference in titer was detected among the various oHSV-1 strains (p > 0.05). Further analysis of viral growth curves over the 48-h period demonstrated that oHSV-1 and HSV-1/WT exhibited a similar replication cycle and growth curve trend in Vero cells, with both reaching a peak at 24 h.

To investigate whether the aforementioned modifications affect the replication capacity of the virus in tumor cells, we performed verification experiments using colon cancer cell lines (CT26, HCT116, and HT29), with the results presented in Figures 2C–2F. In these tumor cells, the replication capacity of oHSV-1 was comparable to that of HSV-1/WT (p > 0.05). However, significant differences in replication capacity were observed between the recombinant virus and the wild-type virus across tumor cells derived from different sources (p < 0.05). Specifically, the replication capacity of the recombinant virus in human colon cancer cell lines was significantly higher than that in murine colon cancer cell lines (p < 0.05). These results indicate that our modifications to the virus did not alter its replication cycle, and the modified virus can maintain a high titer in tumor cells.

To determine whether oHSV-1 harboring exogenous genes can effectively express IL-15, IL-12, and CCL5—key factors for enhancing antitumor immune function—we measured the cytokine concentrations in the supernatants of Vero cells at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h post-infection (Figures 2G–2I). At 24 h post-infection, exogenous genes began to be expressed as the virus replicated. From 24 h to 48 h post-infection, the expression levels of these genes increased to a peak, which was associated with the enhanced viral replication and protein synthesis. After 48 h post-infection, Vero cells started to lyse; by 72 h post-infection, the cells were completely lysed, and the content of exogenous gene products (i.e., IL-15, IL-12, and CCL5) in the supernatant decreased due to degradation. Compared with HSV-1/WT, recombinant oHSVs carrying the corresponding gene cassettes exhibited significantly higher expression levels of IL-15, IL-12, or CCL5. Furthermore, no significant difference in the expression level of each target cytokine was observed between the single-cassette group and the multiple-cassette groups (p > 0.05). These results indicate that the expression of exogenous genes is positively correlated with the replication cycle of oHSV-1, and the expression of each exogenous gene does not interfere with that of the others.

Oncolytic HSV-1 variants demonstrate potent efficacy in eradicating colon cancer cells in vitro

To evaluate the antitumor activity at the cellular level, we assessed the efficacy of oHSV-1 in eliminating colon cancer cell lines (CT26, HCT116, and HT29). Initially, cell viability was determined at MOIs of 0.5, 1, and 5. The results showed that at 48 h post-infection (hpi), there was no significant difference in cell viability between the MOI = 1 and MOI = 5 groups (p > 0.05); however, the cell-killing efficacy in these two groups was significantly higher than that at MOI = 0.5 (p < 0.05) (see Figures 3A–3C). Additionally, the cytotoxic effect of oncolytic HSV-1 infection at MOI = 1 on colon cancer cells was monitored at 24, 48, and 72 hpi. The data demonstrated that oncolytic HSV-1 potently eradicated tumor cells at 48 hpi, with the killing effect persisting and further intensifying up to 72 hpi. Specifically, the cytotoxicity at 48 and 72 hpi was significantly greater than that at 24 hpi (p < 0.05). Furthermore, at the same MOI and time point, no significant difference was observed in the cytotoxicity of HSV-1/WT, RG2001m, RG2002m, RG2005m, RG2011m, or RG2012m against colon cancer cells (p > 0.05). These findings indicate that, at the cellular level, the elimination of colon cancer cells by oncolytic HSV-1 depends on the MOI and infection duration. Modifications to the virus and the expression of mIL-15, mIL-12, and mCCL5 did not enhance its in vitro tumor-killing capacity.

Oncolytic HSV-1 variants augment the therapeutic efficacy in a mouse CT26 xenograft tumor model

To evaluate the in vivo antitumor effect of oHSV-1, a subcutaneous tumor model was established using CT26 cells in the present study (Figure 4A). Thirteen tumor-bearing mice were assigned to each of the experimental group and the control group. Among these mice, 8 were used for tumor volume measurement and survival monitoring, while the remaining 5 were subjected to mouse dissection and photography, flow cytometry analysis, immunohistochemical staining analysis, and other relevant assays. The results of the intratumoral administration experiments indicated that, compared with the PBS control group, both the recombinant oncolytic HSV-1 group and the HSV-1/WT group exhibited significant antitumor activity (Figure 4B), with the difference being statistically significant (p < 0.05). Further survival analysis revealed that both recombinant oHSV-1 and HSV-1/WT significantly prolonged the survival time and increased the survival rate of tumor-bearing mice (Figure 4C). On the 21st day of the experiment, five tumor-bearing mice were randomly selected for euthanasia; subsequently, subcutaneous tumor tissues were dissected, isolated, and photographed (Figure 4D). These results demonstrated that the tumor volume in the oncolytic HSV-1 treatment group was significantly smaller than that in the PBS control group. To confirm viral replication within the tumor, immunohistochemical staining for the HSV-1 gD protein was performed on the tumor tissues (Figure S1). The results verify that both the recombinant oHSV-1 and HSV-1/WT strains can replicate efficiently in tumor tissues (Figure S1B). Additionally, HE staining results reveal that varying degrees of necrotic changes occur in the central region of tumor tissues in the virus treatment group (Figure S1A), suggesting that the virus may exert its antitumor effect by inducing tumor cell necrosis.

The results indicated that recombinant HSV-1 RG2001m and RG2002m, which express IL-15 and IL-12, respectively, exhibited significantly better antitumor effects than HSV-1/WT, suggesting that the expression of IL-15 and IL-12 is crucial for the enhanced efficacy. Further investigation revealed that the recombinant RG2005m virus, which carries both the IL-15- and IL-12-encoding genes, had significantly more vigorous antitumor activity than RG2001m (expressing only IL-15) or RG2002m (expressing only IL-12). These outcomes imply that the synergistic effects of IL-15 and IL-12 can enhance treatment efficacy in the antitumor process.

Furthermore, analysis of the recombinant HSV-1 RG2011m, which expresses CCL5, has demonstrated that its antitumor efficacy is superior to that of HSV-1/WT. This superiority is attributed to the ability of CCL5 to recruit T lymphocytes and NK cells to tumor tissues via chemotaxis, thereby augmenting the local antitumor immune response. Notably, the recombinant RG2012m virus—co-expressing IL-15, IL-12, and CCL5—exhibited the most potent antitumor activity among all tested viral constructs. These findings further validate the synergistic effects of cytokines and chemokines in remodeling the TME and enhancing antitumor immune responses, thereby providing an experimental foundation for the optimized design of engineered OVs.

Treatment with oncolytic HSV-1 variants enhanced the proliferation and activation of NK cells and T cells in the spleen

To investigate the impact of oHSV-1 on the body’s antitumor immunity, as well as the roles of IL-15 and IL-12 in enhancing the proliferation and activation of NK cells and T cells, this study initially monitored the dynamic changes in IL-15 and IL-12 levels in the peripheral blood of mice at three time points: before treatment (day 10), during treatment (day 16), and after treatment (day 21) (Figures S2A and S2B). The results showed that with the progression of treatment, the concentrations of IL-15 and IL-12 in mouse peripheral blood increased progressively. Notably, the cytokine levels in the groups treated with recombinant oHSV-1 (which harbored expression cassettes for IL-15 and IL-12) were significantly higher than those in the control groups (p < 0.05).

To further elucidate the regulatory effects of oHSV-1 on NK cells and T cells, flow cytometry analysis was performed on splenocytes isolated from tumor-bearing mice in this study. As shown in Figure S3, the flow cytometry results demonstrated that the proportion of CD45+CD69+ early-activated immune cells in the HSV-1/WT treatment group tended to be higher than that in the PBS control group. Compared with the HSV-1/WT treatment group, all other oncolytic HSV-1 treatment groups (which harbored therapeutic genes) exhibited significantly greater proportions of CD45+CD69+ early-activated immune cells, with the exception of the RG2011m treatment group (which only expressed CCL5). Notably, the RG2005m and RG2012m treatment groups—both of which co-expressed IL-15 and IL-12—exerted the most pronounced immune cell activation effects (p < 0.01). However, no significant difference was detected between these two groups (p > 0.05).

Furthermore, the proportions of CD4+ T cells (Figure 5B) and CD8+ T cells (Figure 5C) in each oncolytic virus treatment group were significantly higher than those in the PBS treatment group. The RG2005m and RG2012m groups were particularly prominent in increasing the proportion of T cells. At the NK cell level, oHSV-1 significantly promoted the proliferation of NK cells. Compared with the HSV-1/WT treatment group, the proportions of NK cells in the treatment groups expressing IL-15 and IL-12 (either alone or in combination) were significantly higher; notably, the proportion of NK cells in the treatment group co-expressing IL-15 and IL-12 was extremely significantly higher (p < 0.01) (Figure 5D). Analysis of CD107a+ NK cells (activated NK cells) also revealed a similar trend (Figure 5E).

Oncolytic HSV-1 variants enhance the migration and infiltration of NK and T cells into tumor tissues by expressing exogenous genes

The high expression of armed immunomodulatory genes in tumor tissues is crucial for enhancing the antitumor function of recombinant oHSV-1. We assessed the levels of IL-12, IL-15, and CCL5 in these tissues. Our findings indicated that as the virus replicated in the tumor tissues (Figure S1), the recombinant oncolytic HSV-1 specifically targeted and highly expressed these immunomodulatory genes. Notably, RG2001m, RG2005m, and RG2012m exhibited high expression of IL-15; RG2002m, RG2005m, and RG2012m showed high expression of IL-12; RG2011m and RG2012m demonstrated high expression of CCL5; and RG2012m simultaneously highly expressed IL-12, IL-15, and CCL5 (Figures 6A–6C). Meanwhile, the expression levels of IL-12, IL-15, and CCL5 in the RG2005m experimental group (which co-expresses IL-12 and IL-15) and the RG2012m experimental group (which co-expresses IL-12, IL-15, and CCL5) were slightly higher than those in the experimental group expressing a single exogenous gene; however, the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Subsequently, flow cytometry analysis (Figure S4) and immunohistochemical staining analysis (Figure S5) were conducted on the tumor tissues. Figures 6D–6G presents the flow cytometry data from the CT26 tumor model. Compared with the PBS-treated group, the oncolytic virus-treated group exhibited a significantly higher percentage of CD45+ immune cells in the tumor tissues. The proportions of infiltrating cytotoxic CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells also increased (p < 0.05). This compelling evidence confirms the potential of the viral preparation to enhance immune cell infiltration. Furthermore, the RG2012m-treated group showed a significantly greater enhancement of immune cell infiltration (p < 0.01). These results indicate that the upregulation of IL-12, IL-15, and CCL5 markedly promotes the infiltration of immune cells into tumor tissues, thereby contributing to the improvement of therapeutic efficacy.

Oncolytic HSV-1 variant therapy promotes the upregulation of proinflammatory mediators while concurrently downregulating immunosuppressive factors

IFN-γ is a key cytokine in the Th1-type immune response, capable of activating macrophages and enhancing the cytotoxicity of T cells. The IFN-γ level in the PBS group was relatively low. Infection with HSV-1/WT alone slightly upregulated its expression, but the effect was limited. In contrast, treatment with oncolytic HSV-1 carrying therapeutic genes led to a significant increase in IFN-γ levels (Figure 7A). Specifically, in the RG2005m and RG20012m groups, the secretion of IFN-γ was increased by approximately 1.5 to 2-fold compared with that in the HSV-1/WT group (p < 0.01), indicating that oncolytic HSV-1 can effectively activate the Th1-type immune response and enhance antitumor cellular immunity. Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) is involved in inducing tumor cell apoptosis and immune cell recruitment. The TNF-α level in the HSV-1/WT group was higher than that in the PBS group. Furthermore, significant upregulation of TNF-α expression was observed in the oncolytic HSV-1 treatment groups (RG2005m and RG20012m) (Figure 7B). These findings indicate that oncolytic HSV-1 can promote the development of a proinflammatory microenvironment and synergistically kill tumor cells. GZMB is a core molecule involved in the cytotoxicity of T cells and NK cells, and it can directly induce tumor cell apoptosis. The GZMB level in the PBS group was relatively low, and it was slightly increased in the HSV-1/WT group. However, after treatment with oncolytic HSV-1, the secretion of GZMB was significantly increased (Figure 7C). In the RG2005m and RG2012m groups, the GZMB level was approximately 2- to 3-fold higher than that in the HSV-1/WT group (p < 0.01), indicating that oncolytic HSV-1 can enhance the cytotoxic function of effector cells and directly strengthen the antitumor effect. IL-6 is involved in the regulation of the immune response, but its overexpression may lead to immunosuppression. The IL-6 level in the PBS group was relatively high, whereas the expression of IL-6 was downregulated in the oncolytic HSV-1 group, indicating that oncolytic HSV-1 can moderately regulate IL-6 to maintain immune balance (Figure 7D). IL-10 is a typical immunosuppressive cytokine. The IL-10 level in the HSV-1/WT group was lower than that in the PBS group, and the secretion of IL-10 was significantly inhibited after treatment with recombinant oncolytic HSV-1 (Figure 7E). Compared with that in the HSV-1/WT group, the IL-10 level in the RG2012m group was decreased by approximately 50%–70% (p < 0.05), indicating that oncolytic HSV-1 can inhibit the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment and tumor immune escape.

Discussion

CRC is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors worldwide, and its clinical management remains a significant challenge. The immunosuppressive barriers within the TME—including the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells, imbalances in cytokine levels, and aberrant expression of immune checkpoint molecules—are key factors contributing to the low response rates to conventional therapies (e.g., radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy) and poor prognosis in patients with CRC.25,26 Therefore, exploring treatment strategies that can effectively disrupt immunosuppression within the TME and trigger a systemic antitumor immune response has become an important direction in the field of CRC research.27 OVs are a class of viruses capable of selectively infecting and lysing tumor cells, while also stimulating the host’s antitumor immune response. This dual-action mode, which combines “oncolysis and immunomodulation,” provides a approach for the treatment of CRC.28 Numerous literature reports indicate that during tumor treatment, the anti-tumor immune system mainly relies on various immune cells (such as CD8+ T cells and NK cells) to exert its effector functions. Based on a comprehensive review of the literature and research experience in tumor therapy mediated by oHSV-1, we designed and conducted the relevant experimental verification work described in this article. We used OVs as therapeutic vectors to evaluate the anti-tumor immune effect.29,30,31,32The present study constructed a series of recombinant oncolytic vectors based on the HSV-1 17 strain. These vectors are characterized by the deletion of the ICP34.5 (inverted repeat, IR) and ICP47 regions, and carry the immunoregulatory genes encoding IL-12, IL-15, and CCL5. Our results preliminarily confirmed the efficacy of these vectors at both the cellular and animal levels, and verified the synergistic enhancement of antitumor effects by IL-12, IL-15, and CCL5 based on the “oncolysis-chemotaxis-immune activation” theory. This work may provide a feasible direction for the subsequent modification of OVs.

Previous studies have shown that the deletion of the IR region of HSV-1 does not significantly affect the viral replication kinetics.33 This phenomenon is closely associated with the functional characteristics of genes, including the γ34.5 gene (present as a single copy) and the transcripts of ICP0, ICP4, ICP22, and the latency-associated (LAT) gene, all of which are localized within the IR region. Specifically, the deletion of ICP0 alone does not impair the viral replication process.34,35 ICP4 mainly regulates the transcription of late viral genes and simultaneously suppresses the transcription of the immediate-early gene ICP0, which is indispensable for progeny virus production. ICP22 can downregulate the expression of UL41, US11, and UL38. Deletion of the ICP22 gene abolishes its inhibitory effect on ICP0 expression; this process may be achieved either by directly interfering with ICP22’s transactivation activity or indirectly by reducing its expression level.36 The LAT gene plays a crucial role in regulating the latent phase of the virus. Its sustained expression results in the production of microRNAs that target the viral ICP0 and ICP4 mRNAs, thereby inhibiting viral replication and contributing to the maintenance of the latent phase.

Furthermore, deletion of the LAT gene can, to some extent, disrupt the latent phase of HSV-1 and promote the transition of the virus to the lytic replication stage.33,37 This study’s results are consistent with the previously reported regulatory mechanisms. The recombinant HSV-1 vector maintained normal replication kinetics in Vero cells and colorectal cancer cells, with replication peaking at 24 h (Figure 2B), and retained high replication activity in the latter (Figures 2C–2F). The replication capacity of this recombinant HSV-1 vector was significantly higher than that of T-VEC (p < 0.05) and G47Δ, which indicates an enhanced replication potential in tumor cells.38,39

ICP47 deletion significantly impairs the immune evasion capacity of HSV-1. Wild-type HSV-1 utilizes ICP47 to bind transporters associated with TAPs, thereby blocking the translocation of antigenic peptides into the endoplasmic reticulum and evading detection by CD8+ T cells. In the engineered vector constructed in this study, deletion of ICP47 restores the MHC I-mediated antigen presentation pathway, allowing tumor cells to efficiently present viral antigens (e.g., gB, ICP27) and TAAs. This modification synergizes with cytokines delivered by the vector: IL-12 cross-presents viral/tumor antigens to CD4+ T cells, thereby enhancing their activation. The “oncolytic virus replication-antigen release-immune activation” cascade accounts for the robust T cell response observed in in vivo experiments (Figures 5A–5C).14,40,41

oHSV-1 can function as a gene delivery vector to transport and express exogenous therapeutic genes, thereby further activating and enhancing the body’s antitumor immune response.28 Previous studies by our research group have confirmed that IL-12 and IL-15 significantly promote the proliferation and activation of NK cells and T cells.33 Specifically, IL-12 promotes the differentiation of Th1 cells by activating the STAT4 pathway and induces the secretion of IFN-γ. This further upregulates the expression of primary MHC I molecules on the surface of tumor cells, ultimately activating the cytotoxic immune response.42 IL-15, on the other hand, binds to the interleukin-15 receptor α/βγc complex (IL-15Rα/βγc), thereby maintaining the survival of effector immune cells and effectively inhibiting the apoptosis of T cells and NK cells.43,44 However, previous studies have indicated that the effects of IL-12 and IL-15 on promoting the infiltration of NK cells and T cells into tumor tissues are relatively modest and has shortcomings.33 Consequently, in the present study, chemokine CCL5 was selected as a supplement to promote the extensive infiltration of NK cells and T cells into tumor tissues. As a “pioneer” chemokine, CCL5 can specifically recruit T cells and NK cells to the tumor microenvironment through binding to chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5).18,45 IL-12, IL-15, and CCL5 may play a synergistic role in reshaping the tumor microenvironment and enhancing tumor therapy, thereby forming a strengthened anti-tumor immune regulatory network. To verify the expression efficiency of the multiple genes carried by recombinant oHSV-1, this study detected their expression at the cellular level. Results showed that these genes were expressed at high levels, with expression dynamics matching the viral replication cycle: expression was initiated at 24 h post-infection, peaked at 48 h, and declined at 72 h. Multigene coexpression (e.g., in RG2012m) did not significantly affect the expression of individual genes, which verifies the compatibility of the multigene arming strategy (Figures 2G–2I). Furthermore, these genes achieved targeted expression in tumor tissues, ensuring their specific enrichment at lesion sites (Figures 6A–6C).

HSV-1 replicates in tumor tissues (Figure S1) and expresses therapeutic genes (Figures 6A–6C); the synergistic anti-tumor effect of IL-12, IL-15, and CCL5 is driven by interconnected positive feedback loops that enhance the recruitment, activation, and survival of immune cells.46 We found that the expression level of CCL5 of RG2002m in tumor tissues was slightly upregulated compared with the wild-type (WT) group, but the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Figure 6C). Secondly, the expression level of IFN-γ in the RG2012m treatment group was upregulated compared with the RG2011m group, but the difference in CCL5 expression level was not significant (p > 0.05) (Figures 7C and 6C). Therefore, IFN-γ induced by IL-12 has a certain promoting effect on CCL5 in tumor cells or stromal cells, but compared with the direct expression of CCL5, it cannot meet the requirements for exerting a strong chemotactic function. However, this can solve the problem of insufficient infiltration found in previous studies, where IL-12 and IL-15 can chemotact immune cells such as NK cells to a certain extent. We solved this problem through intratumoral delivery of CCL5.47 This dual effect enables synchronized kinetic consistency between oncolysis and immunomodulation, mitigates off-target cytokine toxicity, and ensures specific effects within the TME.48 Recombinant oHSV-1 (e.g., RG2012m) replicates efficiently in tumor cells, where it directly expresses the IL-12, IL-15, and CCL5 genes (Figures 6A–6C), leading to high cytokine accumulation in the TME. Circulating cytokines derive from two sources: overexpression in tumor tissue—whereby these cytokines penetrate the bloodstream via blood vessels—and secretion by systemic immune cells (e.g., NK cells and T cells in the spleen) that are stimulated by local tumor immune activation signals (Figure 5). This observation reflects the “spill-over effect” of local immune responses coupled with systemic immune activation. Cytokine concentrations in tumor tissue are markedly higher than those in the blood. This local high-concentration distribution is critical for the migration, infiltration, and functional activation of NK cells and T cells; this critical role is achieved by establishing precise chemotactic gradients, providing local activation signals, and sustaining cell survival-favorable microenvironments. Notably, high levels of CCL5 in tumors bind to CCR5 receptors on NK/T cells, forming potent chemotactic gradients that act as “navigation signals” to guide immune cells across the tumor vascular endothelial barrier.34 Compared to the lower levels of CCL5 in the blood, the high concentration of CCL5 in the local tumor microenvironment more efficiently triggers the directional migration mechanism of immune cells, thereby promoting their extravasation from blood vessels and infiltration into the tumor parenchyma. As shown in Figures 6D–6G, the infiltration ratios of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells in tumors from the RG2011m and RG2012m groups (which exhibit high CCL5 expression) were significantly increased. Furthermore, high concentrations of IL-12 in tumors can rapidly initiate the activation program of immune cells following their infiltration, while IL-15 sustains the survival of effector immune cells, Similarly, through multi-time-point sampling of blood and analysis of expression levels in tumor tissues, the expression level of IL-12 in RG2005m, which co-expresses IL-15 and IL-12, is higher than that in the RG2002m group (Figure 6A; Figure S2A). Therefore, T/NK cells maintained by IL-15 may secrete additional signals to enhance IL-12, which is consistent with our previous research results: IL-15 and IL-12 can enhance the anti-tumor effect.33,49

oHSV-1 exhibits comparable in vitro cell-killing efficacy but enhanced in vivo activity (Figures 3A–3C, 4B, and 4C), highlighting immune-mediated effects that remodel the TME in three sequential steps. First, viral replication induces ICD of tumor cells, leading to the release of TAAs, DAMPs, and cytokines (Figures 2G–2I). Viral replication and tumor necrosis were confirmed by immunohistochemistry (Figures S1A–S1B). Second, CCL5 recruits T cells and NK cells, while IL-12/IL-15 activates the expansion of splenic immune cells (Figures 5A–5E), upregulates the intratumoral levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α, and increases granzyme B levels by 2- to 3-fold (Figure 7C). Finally, the vector reduces TME immunosuppression by downregulating IL-6 and IL-10 (Figures 7D and 7E); specifically, RG2012m decreases IL-10 levels by 50%–70%. These effects counteract regulatory T cell (Treg)-mediated inhibition and prolong the survival of treated mice (Figure 5C).

Limitations of the study

Our multi-gene-armed HSV-1 vectors have advanced the development of oncolytic virotherapy by addressing the key limitations of single-modality approaches. Previous HSV-1 vectors, armed with only a single cytokine, exhibited limited efficacy due to incomplete immune activation. T-VEC, which is based on an HSV-1 backbone, only deletes the ICP34.5 gene to reduce neurotoxicity, partially deletes the ICP47 gene to weakly restore MHC-I antigen presentation, and harbors only a single transgene (GM-CSF), which solely mediates the recruitment of dendritic cells. In contrast, RG2012m is derived from the HSV-1 17 strain. It enhances tumor-selective replication via deletion of the IR region (containing multiple genes including ICP34.5), completely deletes the ICP47 gene to fully restore antigen presentation, and simultaneously carries three transgenes: mCCL5, mIL-12, and mIL-15. This configuration forms a complete “recruitment-activation-survival” immune regulatory cascade, overcoming the limitation of T-VEC’s single-mode immune activation, and is particularly suitable for the “cold tumor” microenvironment of CRC.50,51 In contrast, our three-gene-armed RG2012m integrates chemotaxis (CCL5), immune cell polarization (IL-12), and effector cell survival (IL-15), achieving a synergistic effect superior to that of single-gene or two-gene-armed vectors (Figures 4B and 4C). However, this study has limitations that necessitate further research. Efficacy has been tested in a murine CT26 model; however, human colorectal cancer presents a different TME, such as increased Treg infiltration.52 In the future, it will be necessary to verify the immune response in a human context using humanized mice (such as NSG mice carrying human PBMCs and patient-derived xenografts). Although the deletion of IR/ICP47 reduces neurotoxicity, latent reactivation in immunocompromised hosts remains a concern. The optimization of transgene regulation, such as controlling CCL5 with a hypoxia-responsive promoter targeting the hypoxic TME, may further enhance specificity. Additionally, the inclusion of antiangiogenic factors (such as anti-VEGF agents) may improve immune cell infiltration by normalizing tumor blood vessels.53,54

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines and cell culture

HSV-1-susceptible Vero cells were preserved in the laboratory in the early stage. CT26, HTC116, and HT29 cells were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China (Cat. No. CL-0071; Cat. No. CL-0096; Cat. No. CL-0118).

Vero, CT26, HTC116, and HT29 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 units/ml penicillin/streptomycin. All the tumor cell lines were identified via STR. Vero cells were used to remove the BAC sequence from HSV-1/BAC and to purify and amplify the virus.

Animal studies

Female BALB/C mice, aged 5–6 weeks, were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd., and housed in a clean animal facility. To establish a subcutaneous transplantation tumor model of murine CT26 colon cancer, CT26 cells were suspended in PBS (1×106 cells/ml) and subcutaneously inoculated into the right abdominal subcutis of 6-week-old female BALB/C mice (100 μL per mouse). When the average tumor volume reached 100 mm3, intratumoral (IT) virus administration was conducted. Each mouse received a total of four doses of virus (2∗107 pfu) at specified time points. The tumor volume was monitored three times every 3 days post virus injection. Blood, spleen, and tumor samples were collected at the specified time points. The animals were euthanized when the tumor burden equaled or exceeded 2000 mm3. All the tumor models used in this study adhered to relevant ethical norms and experimental animal management regulations and were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Xinjiang University (Approval Number: XJUAE-2024-027). Experimental operations are carried out strictly in accordance with the approved protocol, and relevant inspections and declarations are conducted in accordance with the Data S1.

No human participants, plant materials, or primary cell cultures were used in this preclinical study of oncolytic HSV-1 for colorectal cancer, which utilized animal models, cell lines, bacterial strains, and viral strains.

Animal Models: Only 5-6-week-old female BALB/C mice were used for CT26 tumor inoculation, with no male mice or sex-matched controls. Housed in a clean facility under standard protocols, experiments were approved by Xinjiang University’s Experimental Animal Ethics Committee (XJUAE-2024-027) and followed ARRIVE guidelines. The exclusive use of female mice limits result generalizability, precluding assessment of sex-based differences in oncolytic HSV-1’s antitumor efficacy and TME remodeling.

Cell Lines: Vero (African green monkey kidney, lab-preserved), murine CT26, and human HCT116/HT29 colorectal cancer cells (Wuhan Procell) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM with 10% FBS and antibiotics under standard mammalian conditions. Tumor cell lines were authenticated via STR profiling (Vero authentication unreported); mycoplasma testing was performed for any line. Vero cells supported viral manipulation, while tumor cells were used for in vitro assays, with CT26 for in vivo modeling.

Bacterial & Viral Strains: E. coli DH5α and three lab-preserved recombinant E. coli strains enabled BAC-mediated viral construction. Viral strains derived from HSV-1 strain 17 included wild-type HSV-1/WT and five engineered variants (e.g., triple-armed RG2012m) with IR/ICP47 deletions and murine IL-12/IL-15/CCL5 insertions, all purified/amplified in Vero cells and stored as stocks.

No clinical trials or human samples were involved; no registry numbers exist. All unique engineered reagents are available from corresponding author Zhenghai Ma (mzhxju@xju.edu.cn).

Method details

Recombinant viruses

To generate recombinant HSV-1 (RG2001m, RG2002m, RG2005m, RG2011m, and RG2012m), seamless cloning primers (Table S2) were used to amplify the target sequence from the vector plasmid containing the IL-15, IL-12, and CCL5 expression cassettes. Concurrently, the shuttle plasmids pko5/ΔIR and pko5/ΔICP47 were linearized via double digestion with EcoRI and EcoRV. Subsequently, the IL-15, IL-12, and CCL5 expression cassettes were inserted into the linearized shuttle plasmids using a seamless cloning kit, resulting in the construction of pko5/ΔIR/IL-15, pko5/ΔIR/IL-12, pko5/ΔIR/IL-15-IL-12, and pko5/ΔICP47/CCL5. The constructed shuttle plasmids were then electrotransformed into HSV-1/BAC recombinant engineered bacteria at 1.8 kV, 200 Ω, and 25 μF. Recombinant DNA was screened through a combination of sucrose selection, antibiotic selection (chloramphenicol at 25 μg/ml), and temperature cycling (43°C - 30°C - 37°C). Following screening, the recombinant DNA was transfected into Vero cells.55 After three rounds of plaque purification, purified virus stocks were obtained, and the identities of the target recombinant viruses were verified via PCR analysis. The primers used in all the above processes are shown in Table S1.

Determination of the titer and growth curves of oncolytic HSV-1

The titers of HSV-1/WT, RG2001m, RG2002m, RG2005m, RG2011m, and RG2012m were determined in VERO, CT26, HCT116, and HT29. The same amount of each virus was amplified and purified to infect confluent Vero cells. The mixture was washed, and the infected Vero cells were resuspended in 1 ml of PBS. The samples were then frozen in liquid nitrogen at 37°C and thawed three times. Vero, CT26, HCT116, and HT29 cells were serially diluted and inoculated at the same density. The virus dilution factor was observed, and the titer was calculated when there were approximately 100 plaque-forming units.

Virus titer (pfu/mL) = (virus plaques/volume of virus dilution) ∗ number of dilutions

The infection protocol was executed with precision to ensure the validity of the experiment. The recombinant virus was inoculated into Vero, CT26, HCT116, or HT29 cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1. Samples were collected at 0, 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, and 48 hours post-infection. The virus particles underwent repeated freeze‒thaw cycles to fully release them, and a growth curve was plotted based on the titers obtained at each time point, strictly following the infection protocol.

HE staining assay

Fixed tissues were dehydrated in an automatic dehydrator in this sequence: 75% alcohol 2h, 85% alcohol 1h, 95% alcohol 1h, 100% alcohol (I-IV) 20min each, clearing agent I 25min, clearing agent II 30min, paraffin I 30min, paraffin II-III 1h. Then tissues were embedded and sectioned. Samples were dewaxed with solutions: dewaxing solutions I-II 30min each, absolute ethanol I-II 5min each, 95%-75% alcohol 5min each, and rinsed with tap water 5min. Hematoxylin was applied 5 - 10min, samples washed with tap water till color removed. Differentiated with hydrochloric acid alcohol solution 3s, rinsed with tap water, treated with weak alkaline solution to turn blue and washed again. Stained with alcohol-soluble eosin 3min, dehydrated with gradient alcohol, cleared with clearing agent and mounted with neutral gum. Sections were collected using a digital three-eye microphotography system.

Immunohistochemical assay

Paraffin sections were immersed in dewaxing solutions I, II, III for 15 min each. Then transferred to absolute ethanol I, II for 5 min each, followed by 85% and 75% ethanol for 5 min each for gradient hydration. Washed with distilled water. Immersed in antigen retrieval solution, heated in microwave for 20 min, cooled naturally, and washed 3 times with PBS for 5 min each. Blocked endogenous peroxidase in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 25 min at room temperature in the dark, washed 3 times with PBS on shaker for 5 min each. Blocked with BSA for 20 min at room temperature, discarded serum, added primary antibody, incubated overnight at 4°C. Next day, washed 3 times with PBS for 5 min each. Added secondary antibody, incubated at 37°C for 30 min, washed 3 times with PBS for 5 min each. Added fresh DAB chromogenic solution, observed under microscope, stopped reaction with distilled water when positive signal was brown-yellow. Counterstained with hematoxylin for 3 min, rinsed with tap water, treated with bluing solution, rinsed with running water. Subjected to gradient dehydration and cleared in 75%, 85%, 95% ethanol, absolute ethanol, and xylene for 10 min each. Mixed with neutral gum. Images collected with microscopic imaging system.

Flow cytometry assay

CT-26 tumors were minced and digested in DMEM with 0.1% collagenase I, 0.1% collagenase V, and 1% DNase I at 37°C, gently mixed every 10 min and observed microscopically. The spleen was mechanically homogenized, passed through a 200-mesh strainer, red blood cell lysis buffer added, incubated, pelleted, counted, and resuspended in DPBS. DMEM was added to terminate, centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, supernatant discarded. Appropriate Fc receptor antibody was added and incubated at 4°C in the dark for 20 min, then PBS added, centrifuged, supernatant discarded. CD45, CD69, etc. were added, incubated in the dark for 30 min, PBS added to terminate, centrifuged, supernatant discarded. Resuspended in 300 μL DPBS, filtered through 200-mesh copper mesh into FACS tube for detection.

ELISA analysis

Analyses of mouse interleukin-12 (IL-12), mouse interleukin-15 (IL-15), mice regulated upon activation, normal T-cell expressed and secreted factor (RANTES, CCL5), mouse interleukin-6 (IL-6), mouse interleukin-10 (IL-10), mouse tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), mouse granzyme B (GzmB), and mouse interferon-γ (IFN-γ) were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Flow cytometry data were analyzed with CytExpert software. Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 9 software. The results are shown as the mean ± SEM. The datasets were compared via one-way ANOVA and Student's t-test, and p-values were calculated. P < 0.05 was considered significant. ns indicates no significant difference (P > 0.05), P < 0.05, ∗; P < 0.01, ∗∗; p < 0.001, ∗∗∗.
