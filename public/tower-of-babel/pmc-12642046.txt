Predict disabling severe attacks in neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders: warning symptoms and innovative machine learning models
Authors: Weizhe Zhen, Lei Cui, Yeqiong Zhang, Jinsong Jiao, Yujuan Jiao, Renbin Wang, Hongjun Zhen, Weihe Zhang, Dantao Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere disabling attacks neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders (NMOSD) severely affect patients’ quality of daily life and life safety.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled consecutive Chinese patients suffering from NMOSD who visited the China-Japan Friendship Hospital (Beijing, China) between October, 2010 and February, 2023. Correlation analysis was used to perform feature selection. The prediction model was constructed using the support vector machine (SVM) and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) algorithm.ResultsA total of 356 patients (mean [SD] age, 34.45[15.22] years) and 1291 NMOSD attacks were eligible for this study. ON and age were positively and linearly correlated, and TM and age were negatively and linearly correlated. Throbbing headache and neuralgia showed a significant linear relationship with disabling episodes, and circumventricular organ- area postrema syndrome (CVO-APS) showed a significant linear relationship with disabling episodes only in a few cases. We select top three high correlation variable (HCV) for predicting ON and TM models. Then, we constructed prediction models based on the XGBoost using age as a feature, and HCV and warning symptoms (WS) as features respectively. The ML models showed reasonable predictive performance for ON (Age + WS: AUC, 0.809; Age + HCV: AUC, 0.787) and TM (Age + WS : AUC, 0.817; Age + HCV: AUC, 0.854) We also constructed the first prediction model about severe NMOSD attacks using XGBoost and SVM. Among them, the fundamental model incorporated the 16 features with AUC of Xgboost: 0.830 and SVM: 0.775. On the basis of the fundamental model, we also incorporated the results of nadir and remission the expanded disability status scale (EDSS) or visual outcome scale (VOS) from the last time attack as features to construct 12 optimized models. As a whole, the optimized models showed higher predictive performance for severe attack. The AUC for the model adding the nadir EDSS scores as features were XGBoost: 0.862, SVM: 0.741, adding the nadir VOS scores as features were XGBoost: 0.870, SVM: 0.723, adding the remission EDSS scores as features were XGBoost: 0884, SVM: 0.806, adding the remission VOS scores as features were XGBoost: 0.9998, SVM: 0.700, adding both nadir and remission EDSS scores as features were XGBoost: 0.899, SVM: 0.794, adding both nadir and remission VOS scores as features were XGBoost: 0.905 and SVM: 0.705.ConclusionThis study innovatively identified associations between disabling attacks of NMOSD and age, warning symptoms and developed an easy-to-use, less costly and less invasive machine learning model for predicting NMOSD disabling attack symptoms and severe attacks.Keywords: Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders, Machine learning, Disabling severe attacks, Warning symptoms

Introduction

Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) is a rare autoimmune disorder of the central nervous system characterized by frequent relapses that lead to high rates of disability. Aquaporin-4 autoantibodies (AQP4-IgG) play a key role in NMOSD by targeting astrocytes and triggering inflammation [1]. Based on serum AQP4-IgG status, NMOSD can be classified into seropositive and seronegative forms [2]. Among the six core clinical features defined by the International Panel for NMO Diagnosis (IPND) [2], optic neuritis (ON), transverse myelitis (TM), and other brainstem-cerebellar or cerebral presentations generally manifest as disabling attacks. These associations between disabling attacks and age and warning symptoms have important implications for our assessment of severe attacks, but there is still a lack of relevant studies being reported.

Our previous study demonstrated that there are virtually no monophasic clinical courses in the natural history of NMOSD [3], indicating that almost all patients, if not properly treated with immunotherapy, will experience at least one clinical relapse, thereby increasing the risk of disability. Therefore, preventing relapse is the primary goal of treatment and a major focus of current research on sequential immunotherapies [4–8]. Early prediction of relapse risk and timely adjustment of immunotherapy are crucial for effective prevention.

Recently, certain biological markers such as AQP4-IgG titers [9–12], circulating blood B cells [13–16], glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) [10, 17]or neurofilament light chain (Nfl) [18, 19], and cytokines (like IL-6 or IL-17a) [15, 20, 21], have shown potential correlations with disease activity and severity in NMOSD patients. However, the predictive capabilities of these markers remain controversial, and they are insufficient for real-time prediction of attacks. Additionally, these markers require frequent, invasive, and technically demanding testing, making it challenging for clinicians to monitor patients’ conditions promptly. This highlights the urgent need for more accessible and reliable predictive methods that can facilitate timely intervention and improve patient outcomes.

To address these limitations, this study introduces a novel approach by constructing a machine learning model to predict disabling attacks in NMOSD based on clinical symptoms. This model aims to offer a more accessible and real-time predictive tool that clinicians can utilize, thereby facilitating early intervention and timely medication adjustments. Our single-center research, including a large population of Chinese patients with NMOSD, focuses on the development and validation of this innovative model, which promises to enhance patient care by providing a real-time, symptom-based assessment of relapse risk.

Methods and materials

Patient enrollment

This study enrolled 356 consecutive Chinese patients suffering from NMOSD who visited the China-Japan Friendship Hospital (Beijing, China) between October, 2010 and February, 2023. The 2015 International Consensus Diagnostic Criteria were used to diagnose NMOSD (with or without seropositive AQP4-IgG). An AQP4-IgG test was conducted using a cell-based assay (AQP4-IgG test kit; EUROIMMUN (China) Co., Beijing, China) by an independent medical inspection agency and the in-house laboratory.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of China-Japan Friendship Hospital (Ethics ID: Approval No. 2020–31-Y06-32), and the need for informed consent was waived by the committee because of the study’s retrospective nature.

Clinical attacks collection

We documented each clinical attacks in enrolled patients, with clinical attacks determined through medical history inquiries, ophthalmic examinations, and imaging data, confirmed by two neuroimmunology specialists. Neurological functions were assessed using the expanded disability status scale (EDSS) or visual outcome scale (VOS) score for all clinical attacks, including disease onset and every subsequent relapse identified in patients. Disabling attacks were characterized as new or worsening neurological symptoms, including ON, TM, and symptomatic brainstem-cerebellar and cerebral syndromes, persisting for more than 24 h and occurring at least 30 days after the previous attack. Unconfirmed relapses that due to insufficient medical records were excluded from the analysis. Furthermore, a severe disabling attack (major attack) was defined as having an EDSS score of ≥ 6 or a VOS score of ≥ 4 at the nadir of the attack. If these criteria were not met, the attack was classified as a minor attack.

Predictive factors definition

The participants reported predictive factors within a prespecified time interval of ≤ 30 days in relation to symptom onset, which were then recorded. Notably, preceding circumventricular organ (CVO) syndromes, such as area postrema syndrome (APS) and diencephalic syndromes, were considered universal non-disabling conditions that typically achieve complete recovery. These attacks were also included as predictive factors in the evaluation. In addition, we developed separate predictive models for two distinct types of disabling attacks (ON and TM) and severity of the attacks. Due to the low incidence of symptomatic brainstem-cerebellar and cerebral syndromes in our study cohort, we excluded these symptoms from the predictive analysis.

Model predictive variables selection

Based on prior literature and the incidence rates observed in our study data, and after excluding variables with high missing variables and rarely occurring variables, we selected the following 16 variables for model predictive analysis: age at onset, seropositive autoantibodies (AQP4-IgG, antinuclear antibodies (ANA)), comorbidity (Sjö’ren’s Syndrome (SS), other comorbidities), warning symptoms (throbbing headache, neuralgia, APS), attack symptoms (left ON (LON), right ON (RON), bilateral ON (BON), TM, CVO syndrome), history of longitudinal extensive transverse myelitis (LETM), reduction of glucocorticoid/immunosuppressive therapy (GC/IST reduction) prior to attack, major attacks at last time.

We utilized machine learning models to analyze the distribution of these 16 variables to predict the severe attacks. Furthermore, we categorized the 1,291 recorded attacks based on the occurrence of ON and TM, respectively, and assessed the frequency of warning symptoms in the attacks across different groups.

Correlation analysis is a common method to assess the strength of relationships between variables. We used the ‘cor’ function in R-4.3.1 to calculate correlation coefficients for the variables strongly associated with disabling severe attacks of NMOSD. The closer the absolute value of the correlation coefficient is to 1, the stronger the correlation between the variables. Based on the results of the correlation analysis, we performed feature selection. We selected the variables with the top three absolute values of correlation coefficients, excluding age, to construct a machine learning model for predicting ON and TM.

Model building and training

We split the dataset consisting of all recorded attacks into a training set(0.7) and a test set(0.3). For model construction, we employed two machine learning algorithms: support vector machine (SVM) and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). To ensure robustness and prevent overfitting, we performed ten-fold cross-validation on both the training and validation sets, enhancing the model’s performance. The area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity and specificity were applied to assess model performance in the test set. In our study, we selected the model with the highest AUC in the receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC) as the optimal model.

We developed two predictive models targeting the severe and disabling attacks of ON and TM, respectively. These models incorporated age as features, alongside warning symptoms (WS) or highly correlated variables (HCV). The performance of these models was then compared.

Next, we constructed models incorporating the 16 variables to predict severe attacks. Subsequently, we expanded our predictive models to twelve by adding more features and higher dimensionality. This was achieved by integrating the last time attack EDSS scores or VOS scores, performed according to patients’ attack symptoms, as new features on top of the initial 16 features.

The entire model building and training process were conducted using R-4.3.1studio.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, differences between groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27. A p-value of 0.05 was considered as the threshold for significance. For multiple hypothesis test, we adopted the Bonferroni correction to correct the p-value. The formula for the Bonferroni correction is p*(1/n), where p is the original threshold and n is the total number of tests. The relationship between disabling attack and age or warning symptoms was estimated using linear regression analysis using R-4.3.1studio.

For features with a small number of random missing values, we used Multiple Imputation (MI) to fill in the missing values to minimize bias due to missing data. The Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) method was used to impute these random missing values, ensuring a more complete dataset for model construction.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of patients

A total of 356 patients diagnosed with NMOSD were enrolled in the study, with 292 of them (82.0%) being female, and the average age at onset was 34.45 ± 15.22 years. 123 (34.6%) patients also exhibited one or more other SADs (mainly Sjögren’s syndrome, SS). Overall, 279 (78.4%) patients were positive for AQP4-IgG. In addition, 291 (81.7%) of patients had one or more organ-specific or non-organ-specific antibodies detected in their peripheral blood (mainly ANA). There were no familial cases observed.

Characteristics of clinical attacks

In total, we documented 1,337 clinical attacks. After adjusting for exclusion factors, such as incomplete clinical data or lack of objective examination evidence, we collected data from 1,291 clinical attacks for predictive analyses. This includes 518 (40.1%) ON attacks, 566 (43.8%) TM attacks, 184 (14.3%) CVO attacks, 113 (8.8%) brainstem-cerebellar syndromes or cerebral syndromes. Additionally, 172 (13.3%) of these attacks exhibited multiple symptoms. The clinical relevance of disabling attacks (ON and TM) is detailed in Table 1.

The association between disabling attacks and age

In Fig. 2, we can find that the incidence of ON is gradually decreasing with age, both for last time attack and this time attack (p < 0.05). In terms of the rate of decline in the incidence of ON, the rate of decline in the incidence of ON is faster in the case of a major attack than in the case of a minor attack, whether it is a last time attack or this time attack. However, in terms of the incidence of ON itself, both time-last attack and this time attack, the incidence of ON is higher for a major attack compared to a minor attack (Fig. 2A and B). For TM, the incidence of both last time attack and this time attack gradually increased with age, but in this major attack, the increase in TM was not statistically significant (p < 0.05). Compared to ON, the difference in the incidence of TM in the occurrence of major attack and minor attack was relatively smaller (Fig. 2C and D).

For throbbing headache, it was positively correlated with ON episodes for both last time attack and this time attack (p < 0.05). At the same frequency of throbbing headache, major attack will have a higher incidence of ON than minor attack. At the same time, the gap between the two was greater when throbbing headaches occurred less frequently, relative to when they occurred frequently (Fig. 3A and B). Similar to throbbing headache, the gap between the two was greater when neuralgia was less frequent, relative to when throbbing headache was frequent (Fig. 3C and D). For CVO-APS, there was no statistically significant positive or negative correlation between it and ON episodes in last time attack (p > 0.05). In this time attack, if major attack, CVO-APS had a significant positive correlation with the onset of ON, whereas when minor attack, CVO-APS did not have a statistically significant positive or negative correlation with ON (p > 0.05) (Fig. 3E and F).

Construction and comparison of ON prediction models

As shown in Fig. 5, we developed two prediction models for ON using the XGBoost algorithm. The first model was characterized by age and warning symptoms (Throbbing headache, Neuralgia, APS), while the second model utilized age and highly correlated variables.

ON prediction models based on different features. Legend: WS = warning symptoms. HCV = high correlation variable. ON = optic neuritis. auc = area under the working characterization curve

Notably, the prediction model based on age and warning symptoms demonstrated superior performance. This model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.809, with an accuracy of 0.736, a sensitivity of 0.743, and a specificity of 0.725. These metrics indicate a robust ability to predict ON, with a balanced trade-off between sensitivity and specificity.

In contrast, the prediction model that incorporated age and highly correlated variables (HCV) showed slightly lower predictive capability. This model attained an AUC of 0.787, with an accuracy of 0.770, a sensitivity of 0.873, and a specificity of 0.592. While this model exhibited higher sensitivity, indicating a strong ability to identify true positives, it had lower specificity, suggesting a higher rate of false positives compared to the first model.

The TM prediction model characterized by age and warning symptoms also performed well, achieving an AUC of 0.817. Fundamental models construction for predicting severe attacks

As shown in Fig. 7, we developed two machine learning models, employing the XGBoost classifier and SVM classifier, respectively, to predict severe attacks. These models utilized the selected 16 variables. To enhance performance and prevent overfitting, we employed ten-fold cross-validation.

Fundamental prediction models for severe attacks. Legend: SVM = Support Vector Machine. XGB = Extreme Gradient Boosting. auc = area under the working characterization curve

The XGBoost model demonstrated superior performance, with an AUC of 0.830, an accuracy of 0.724, a specificity of 0.890, and a sensitivity of 0.612. In comparison, the SVM model achieved an AUC of 0.775, an accuracy of 0.721, a specificity of 0.665, and a sensitivity of 0.769.

Incorporating neurological function impairment scores at last attack for improved predictive modeling

We conducted separate evaluations using nadir and remission EDSS scores or VOS scores for NMOSD attacks based on different attack symptoms. Utilizing the original 16 features, we augmented our models by integrating EDSS scores or VOS scores as additional features. We employed the XGBoost classifier and SVM classifier to construct higher-dimensional predictive models with the inclusion of these new scale scores.

When incorporating the nadir EDSS scores of the last attack into the original features, the XGBoost model exhibited the best predictive performance (AUC = 0.862), with an accuracy of 0.816, specificity of 0.786, and sensitivity of 0.835. Conversely, the SVM model showed slightly inferior predictive performance (AUC = 0.741), with an accuracy of 0.710, sensitivity of 0.807, and specificity of 0.557 (Fig. 8A).

Similar trends were observed when incorporating the nadir VOS scores of the last attack. As shown in Fig. 8B, The XGBoost model demonstrated superior predictive performance (AUC = 0.870), with an accuracy of 0.798, specificity of 0.855, and sensitivity of 0.741. In contrast, the SVM model showed inferior predictive capability (AUC = 0.723), with an accuracy of 0.679, sensitivity of 0.588, and specificity of 0.759.

Upon adding remission EDSS scores of the last attack to the original features, the XGBoost model outperformed the SVM model (AUC = 0.884 vs. AUC = 0.806). The XGBoost model achieved an accuracy of 0.820, specificity of 0.827, and sensitivity of 0.816, while the SVM model had an accuracy of 0.708, sensitivity of 0.876, and specificity of 0.466 (Fig. 8C).

As shown in Fig. 8D, an excellent prediction model is constructed when incorporating remission VOS scores of the last attack. The XGBoost model exhibited near-perfect predictive performance (AUC = 0.9998), with an accuracy of 0.991, sensitivity of 1, and specificity of 0.981, whereas the SVM model (AUC = 0.700) had an accuracy of 0.651, sensitivity of 0.531, and specificity of 0.754.

Furthermore, integrating both nadir and remission EDSS or VOS scores of the last attack into the original features resulted in improved predictive performance for the XGBoost model compared to the SVM model (AUC = 0.899 vs. AUC = 0.794 for EDSS scores; AUC = 0.905 vs. AUC = 0.705 for VOS scores). The XGBoost model demonstrated an accuracy of 0.840, specificity of 0.899, and sensitivity of 0.792 for EDSS scores, and an accuracy of 0.840, specificity of 0.852, and sensitivity of 0.827 for VOS scores, while the SVM model exhibited an accuracy of 0.760, sensitivity of 0.849, specificity of 0.623 for EDSS scores, and an accuracy of 0.623, sensitivity of 0.510, specificity of 0.719 for VOS scores (Fig. 8E and F).

Discussion

NMOSD is a highly disabling condition characterized by severe, recurrent attacks that often result in significant neurological damage and disability. Despite the availability of various effective pharmacologic interventions, such as therapeutic monoclonal antibodies [22–24], their high costs limit widespread use. Additionally, optimal dosage and interval adjustments remain unresolved, posing further challenges. For instance, administering low-dose rituximab to reduce costs may inadvertently increase the relapse rate [25, 26]. Moreover, these treatments, while effective, are typically reactive, administered after relapses have occurred. Thus, the early prediction of relapses would be far more clinically valuable. Current biomarkers, such as AQP4-IgG, B cells, GFAP, NfL, and cytokines, exhibit inconsistent efficacy in predicting relapses in NMOSD [27, 28] and demand rigorous detection protocols. Given the high potential for disability with NMOSD, it is crucial to establish a clinically feasible and real-time predictive model to anticipate relapses and promptly adjust treatment strategies.

The relationship between ON and TM, as important disabling episodes of NMOSD, and age and early warning symptoms is of great interest to us. When we clarify the relationship between them, we can recognize early warning symptoms in the clinic, pay attention to the patient’s age of onset and use it to assess the possibility of the emergence of disabling attacks. The results of linear regression suggest that the older the age of onset, the less likely ON is to occur, whether it is a last time attack or this time attack, whether it is a major attack or a minor attack. And the greater the likelihood of TM occurring. This has been documented in previous studies, where researchers in multiple sclerosis have found a significant correlation between ON attacks and younger age [29]. Although there is no clear evidence to support a positive correlation between the incidence of TM and age, there is some evidence to suggest that older age is associated with a more severe onset of TM [30]. There is a wide variation in the relationship between different warning symptoms and disabling attacks. This is reflected in the fact that throbbing headache and neuralgia showed a significant linear correlation with the occurrence of disabling episodes, whereas CVO-APS did not show a linear correlation. There are no previous studies on this topic, and this finding would be better supported by future basic research revealing the mechanism.

Few studies have explored machine learning-based predictive models specifically tailored for NMOSD. Existing research has primarily focused on differentiating NMOSD from multiple sclerosis (MS) [31, 32], an essential clinical distinction given the potential harm of MS treatments in NMOSD patients. However, these studies do not contribute to the early prediction of NMOSD attacks. Additionally, other investigations have sought to predict the onset of NMOSD using biomarkers but have overlooked addressing relapses in diagnosed NMOSD patients [33, 34]. Moreover, limited attention has been given to predictive modeling for NMOSD recurrence and the severity of symptoms during attacks. Previous studies, while analyzing factors such as ethnicity and age of onset in AQP4-IgG-positive NMOSD patients, did not incorporate pre-exacerbation warning symptoms, which are crucial for early intervention [9]. Furthermore, they did not utilize machine learning algorithms, resulting in suboptimal predictive performance. The absence of comprehensive predictive models that integrate clinical symptoms and demographic factors may impede the accuracy and effectiveness of NMOSD prediction.

In contrast, our study represents a novel approach by incorporating a wide range of clinical features, including pre-exacerbation warning symptoms, and employing advanced machine learning algorithms such as XGBoost and SVM. By leveraging these techniques, our predictive model demonstrates enhanced accuracy and sensitivity in anticipating NMOSD attacks, thus addressing a critical gap in the existing literature. This innovative methodology enables early intervention and personalized treatment strategies, ultimately improving patient outcomes and reducing the burden of NMOSD.

In this study, our focus was on non-biomarker variables, such as warning symptoms before attacks, attack symptoms, and medication tapering, to construct machine learning models for predicting severe NMOSD attacks and type of disabling attacks such as ON and TM. Our aim was to develop clinically feasible models that avoid invasive tests and reduce the financial burden on patients [35, 36].

Our correlation analysis revealed significant findings. The warning symptom of throbbing headache demonstrated a strong correlation with ON attacks, potentially indicating a link to peripapillary damage and inflammatory processes [37, 38]. Similarly, neuralgia exhibited the highest correlation with TM, highlighting the need for further basic research to illuminate this association. Moreover, we observed a robust correlation between severe attacks and previous severe attacks, hinting at a recurring clinical pattern that warrants deeper investigation into underlying mechanisms, such as those involving IL-6, the complement pathway, and B-cell-related pathways [39–42].

This work pioneered the introduction of the XGBoost and SVM machine learning algorithms into NMOSD disabling severe attacks prediction research. Our predictive models for ON attacks, utilizing warning symptoms or HCV, demonstrated commendable predictive performance. While the warning symptom-based model exhibited slight superiority overall, it’s crucial to note the intersecting ROC curves, suggesting potential differences in predictive effects at various thresholds. Conversely, in predicting TM, the model utilizing HCV surpassed the warning symptom-based model, underscoring the importance of tailored clinical applications of these predictive models. When patients do not manifest any of the three early warning symptoms, the prompt utilization of an alternative predictive model can avert potential false-negative predictions. This strategy underscores the robustness and applicability of our study’s methodology, ensuring thorough coverage and augmenting the clinical relevance of our predictive models.

These findings hold significant implications for clinical practice. By leveraging machine learning algorithms and incorporating readily available clinical data, our models offer a promising avenue for early prediction and intervention in NMOSD attacks. Clinicians can use these models to identify high-risk patients, enabling proactive management strategies and personalized treatment approaches. Furthermore, the ability to predict disabling attack manifestations, such as ON and TM, provides valuable insights for targeted interventions and monitoring.

Severe attacks in NMOSD are associated with grave outcomes, including disability and even mortality [43]. This study represents a pioneering effort in developing predictive models for severe NMOSD attacks utilizing machine learning techniques. Both the XGBoost and SVM models exhibited promising predictive capabilities, with XGBoost showing superiority in handling high-dimensional data. By integrating EDSS or VOS scores in nadir and in remission from the last attack, we constructed a total of 12 predictive models. Remarkably, the XGBoost model incorporating VOS scores in remission achieved near-perfect predictive performance. These findings indicate the significant potential of our models for clinical application. They pave the way for the development of evaluation systems or scales that can aid healthcare providers in the timely assessment and management of NMOSD patients, ultimately improving patient outcomes and enhancing the quality of care in clinical settings.

We should also acknowledge several limitations of this study. Firstly, the absence of relevant data from other healthcare organizations may hinder the generalizability of our prediction model. Additionally, the observational nature of our study may introduce inherent selection biases. These limitations warrant the need for future studies to validate our findings across diverse patient populations and healthcare settings, thereby enhancing the robustness and applicability of predictive models for NMOSD. In addition to this, we didn’t put all data from the same patient in the same data split to avoid the significant bias caused by the presence of missing data for the same individual within the same dataset whether it is the training or testing data sets. This inevitably introduces other biases and to some extent affects the generalizability of the results. After comprehensive evaluation, the current approach remains the most optimal. This also serves as a constant reminder that we must prioritize preventing data missing values in our future work.

In summary, this study has successfully developed and validated machine learning models for predicting severe NMOSD attacks, as well as specific types of disabling attacks such as ON and TM. The integration of neurological function impairment scores at the last attack has significantly enhanced predictive performance. These models represent a significant advancement in NMOSD research, offering the potential for early intervention and improved disease management in clinical practice. Moving forward, further research should focus on validating these models across diverse patient populations and exploring additional variables to further enhance predictive accuracy, thereby maximizing their clinical utility and impact.
