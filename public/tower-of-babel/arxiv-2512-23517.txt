Van der Waals interaction at short and long distances: a pedagogical path from stationary to time-dependent perturbation theory
L. Saba, C. D. Fosco

Abstract
The van der Waals interaction between neutral atoms is typically studied using stationary perturbation theory for the short-distance (London) limit, while long-distance (Casimir-Polder) results are usually derived via semiclassical, time-dependent approaches. In this pedagogical article, we demonstrate that reformulating stationary perturbation theory calculations in terms of time-ordered correlation functions significantly simplifies the mathematical treatment. This reformulation is particularly advantageous for higher-order calculations required in the long-distance regime, where retardation effects become important. Our approach provides a unified framework that connects both limiting cases while offering a clear conceptual picture suitable for advanced quantum mechanics courses.



1
Introduction

Van der Waals forces are responsible for phenomena as varied as the
adhesion of geckos to walls
[
1
]
and the stability of
biological
membranes.
[
2
]
Their theoretical
description requires connecting two different regimes: short-distance
(London) and long-distance (Casimir-Polder), a task that remains
nontrivial from both pedagogical and computational standpoints.

The theoretical understanding of these forces developed over
several decades. In 1873,
van der Waals introduced his famous equation of state to account for
deviations
from ideal gas behavior.
[
3
]

However, the microscopic origin of these forces remained
mysterious until the advent of quantum mechanics. Keesom first
explained the interaction between molecules with permanent dipole
moments,
[
4
]
but this could not account for forces between
nonpolar atoms.

A breakthrough was made by London,
[
5
]
who showed how
quantum fluctuations may induce forces between neutral atoms, even when
they
lack permanent dipole moments. London’s theory predicts an interaction
energy
proportional to
R^{-6}
, where
R
denotes the interatomic distance. His
treatment assumed an instantaneous (Coulomb) electromagnetic (EM)
interaction. However, this assumption is valid only
when
R\ll c/\Omega
, where
\Omega
is a characteristic atomic frequency.
For larger separations, retardation effects become important. Casimir and
Polder addressed this issue in 1948,
[
6
]
showing that at
large distances the interaction transitions to a
R^{-7}
law,
due to the finite speed of light. (The same year, Casimir also derived
the attraction between parallel conducting plates,
[
7
]

which bears his name.) For a comprehensive modern treatment,
see Ref.
[
8
]
. Note that the transition to the

R^{-7}
behavior by
no means implies that the system becomes time-dependent—the
Hamiltonian remains time-independent throughout.
However, most derivations of the long-distance result rely upon a
semiclassical approach where time dependence is introduced for the EM
field.

It is one of our goals in this paper to show how a time-independent
treatment of the system can be conveniently used to derive results for
both regimes. At the same time, we explain how a time-dependent formulation
greatly simplifies the inclusion of retardation effects, which in
stationary
perturbation theory corresponds to terms at fourth order in the
perturbation.

We also show how the time-dependent perturbation theory
framework helps study the London limit by including higher orders.

This approach is well-suited for advanced quantum mechanics courses, where
it can complement standard treatments of perturbation theory.

This paper is organized as follows: Section
2
presents
the framework used to describe the system, introducing the
atomic model and then the EM interaction.
Section
3
presents the construction of the Lagrangian in the
Coulomb gauge, used to evaluate corrections to the vacuum energy in
the instantaneous approximation. We then
introduce a time-dependent treatment, showing how it simplifies the
evaluation
of the vacuum energy in the dipole approximation to all orders in
perturbation theory. The stationary perturbation theory results are
summarily presented in an Appendix.

Then, in Sec.
4
, we consider the inclusion of retardation
effects, showing how the time-dependent perturbation theory framework also
simplifies the calculation. In Sect.
5
we present our
conclusions.

2
The system

The system we consider throughout most of this paper consists of
two static, identical, neutral atoms, labeled
A
and
B
, whose
centers of mass occupy fixed positions
\mathbf{R}_{A}
and

\mathbf{R}_{B}
, respectively. To an excellent approximation, which we
adopt, each center coincides with the location of the
respective nucleus. The positions of the least-bound
electrons (relative to those centers) are denoted by
\mathbf{r}_{A}

and
\mathbf{r}_{B}
; these, together with the EM field potentials, are the
dynamical variables we shall include in the model.

We take as our initial description the real-time action
{\mathcal{S}}
,
assumed to have the form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbf{r}}_{A},{\mathbf{r}}_{B},A\,;{\mathbf{R}}_{A},{\mathbf{R}}_{B})

\displaystyle=\;{\mathcal{S}}^{a}_{0}({\mathbf{r}}_{A})+{\mathcal{S}}^{a}_{0}({\mathbf{r}}_{B})+{\mathcal{S}}^{a}_{I}({\mathbf{r}}_{A},A\,;{\mathbf{R}}_{A})

\displaystyle+\,{\mathcal{S}}^{a}_{I}({\mathbf{r}}_{B},A\,;{\mathbf{R}}_{B})+{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm EM}_{0}(A)\;,

(1)

where
{\mathcal{S}}^{a}_{0}
is the action for one electron in its
binding potential,
{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm EM}_{0}(A)
is the action for the free
EM field, and
{\mathcal{S}}^{a}_{I}
contains the coupling of that field to
an electron.

Let us describe each of these terms in turn. The electrons have
identical actions. Using
\alpha
as a label running over the values

A
or
B
, we take
{\mathcal{S}}^{a}_{0}
to be

{\mathcal{S}}^{a}_{0}({\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha})\;=\;\frac{m}{2}\int dt\,\Big(\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}(t)-\Omega^{2}\,{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}(t)\Big)\;,

(2)

where
\Omega
is an effective frequency describing the relevant
dynamics of the electron in the atomic potential.

Although simplified, this harmonic oscillator model yields the correct
functional form for the polarizability and reproduces the
R^{-6}
and

R^{-7}
power laws. It is particularly appropriate
for noble gas atoms, where the least-bound electron experiences an
approximately harmonic potential near equilibrium. Quantitatively
reasonable results are obtained when
\Omega
is chosen to match
the atomic ionization energy.

The EM field action, before gauge fixing, takes the well-known form

\mathcal{S}_{0}^{\text{EM}}(A)=\frac{1}{2}\,\int dt\int d^{3}x\,\left({\mathbf{E}}^{2}(t,{\mathbf{x}})-{\mathbf{B}}^{2}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\right)=-\frac{1}{4}\,\int d^{4}x\,F_{\mu\nu}(x)F^{\mu\nu}(x)\;,

(3)

with
d^{4}x\equiv dx^{0}\,dx^{1}\,dx^{2}\,dx^{3}
,
x^{0}=ct
,

F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
,

{\mathbf{E}}=(F_{01},F_{02},F_{03})
, and

{\mathbf{B}}=(F_{32},F_{13},F_{21})
.

Indices from the middle of the Greek alphabet (
\mu,\nu,\ldots
)
run from
0
to
3
and are raised and lowered
with the spacetime metric
(g_{\mu\nu})={\rm diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)
.
In the four-potential, the temporal component
A^{0}
is the scalar potential

\phi
, while the spatial components
A^{j}
(
j=1,2,3
) form the
vector potential
{\mathbf{A}}
.

The interaction of a given atom with the EM field is described by

\mathcal{S}_{I}^{a}({\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha},A;{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha})=\int dt\,\left[\frac{q}{c}\,\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\cdot{\mathbf{A}}(t,{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}+{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t))-q\,\phi(t,{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}+{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t))+q\,\phi(t,{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha})\right]\;,

(4)

where
q
is the electron’s charge.

The electrons’ dynamics is described nonrelativistically. It is worth
noting that, up to this point, no dipole approximation has been
implemented.

3
Coulomb gauge and instantaneous approximation

3.1
Coulomb gauge

The action in Eq. (
2
) corresponds to a Lagrangian

L
(with
{\mathcal{S}}=\int dt\,L
) having the structure

L=\frac{m}{2}\sum_{\alpha}\big(\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}-\Omega^{2}{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}\big)+\int d^{3}x\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big({\mathbf{E}}^{2}-{\mathbf{B}}^{2}\big)-\frac{1}{c}J_{\mu}(t,{\mathbf{x}})A^{\mu}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\Big]\;,

(5)

with the current four-vector
J^{\mu}
:

\displaystyle J^{0}(t,{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=q\sum_{\alpha}\Big[\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}-{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\big)-\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}\big)\Big]\,c\equiv\rho(t,{\mathbf{x}})c\;,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{J}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=q\sum_{\alpha}\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}-{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\big)\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\;.

(6)

Gauge invariance of the action is guaranteed by current conservation,
manifested as the continuity equation
\partial_{\mu}J^{\mu}=0
. The total
charge
Q=\int d^{3}x\,\rho(t,{\mathbf{x}})
vanishes, as does the
total charge of each atom.

To proceed, we fix the gauge. Adopting the Coulomb gauge,
\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{A}}=0
, and noting that
A^{0}
is not
dynamical, we can eliminate it from the Lagrangian by solving
the equation it satisfies:
\nabla^{2}A^{0}=-\rho
.
Thus,

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{2}\sum_{\alpha}\big(\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}-\Omega^{2}{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle+\int d^{3}x\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big(\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}^{2}-{\mathbf{B}}^{2}\big)+\frac{1}{c}{\mathbf{J}}\cdot{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}\Big]-U_{\rm Coul}\;,

\displaystyle U_{\rm Coul}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\,\rho(t,{\mathbf{x}})\frac{1}{4\pi|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}|}\rho(t,{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}})\;.

(7)

We have introduced the transverse part,
{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}
, of the
vector potential:

{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}(t,{\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{A}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})+\nabla\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\,\frac{1}{4\pi|{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}|}\nabla^{\prime}\cdot{\mathbf{A}}(t,{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}})\;,

(8)

which satisfies
\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}=0
. The same notation
shall
be used for any vector field to denote its solenoidal part. Analogously, we
shall use
{\mathbf{A}}_{\parallel}
for the longitudinal component,
such that
{\mathbf{A}}_{\parallel}={\mathbf{A}}-{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}
.

3.2
The instantaneous approximation

When the speeds involved in the electrons’ motion are much smaller than

c
,
one can neglect the coupling between the current and the vector potential.
Only the Coulomb term remains to account for the interaction between the
two atoms. The dynamical (transverse) part of the EM field becomes
effectively
decoupled from the atoms; thus, the interaction between atoms is completely
encoded in the Lagrangian

L=\frac{m}{2}\sum_{\alpha}\big(\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}-\Omega^{2}{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}\big)-U_{\rm Coul}\;.

(9)

The Coulomb term may be written in terms of the
polarization.
[
9
]
Since each atom contains two equal
and opposite charges, we have

\displaystyle\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}-{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\big)-\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}du\,\frac{d}{du}\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}-u{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\big)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}du\;{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\cdot\nabla\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}-u{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\big)

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\int_{0}^{1}du\;{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\,\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}-u{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\big)\;,

(10)

so that

\rho(t,{\mathbf{x}})=-\nabla\cdot{\mathbf{P}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\;,

(11)

with polarization
{\mathbf{P}}=\sum_{\alpha}{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}
and

{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\int_{0}^{1}du\;q\,{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}-u{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\big)\;.

(12)

Equation (
11
) allows us to express the Coulomb energy in
the alternative form

U_{\rm Coul}=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x\,|{\mathbf{P}}_{\parallel}|^{2}\;,

(13)

which we shall use below.

The quantum version of this system yields London’s expression for the
interaction energy between dipoles when the dipolar limit is taken,
but it also contains contributions from higher electric multipoles.

The full quantum Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
in the instantaneous approximation is

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}^{\prime}\;,

(14)

with

\hat{H}_{0}=\sum_{\alpha,i}\hbar\Omega\,\big(\hat{a}_{\alpha,i}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\alpha,i}+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\;,

(15)

where
\hat{a}_{\alpha,i}
and
\hat{a}_{\alpha,i}^{\dagger}
are
annihilation and creation operators corresponding to each Cartesian
component of
{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}
:

\hat{r}_{\alpha}^{i}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\Omega}}\big(\hat{a}_{\alpha,i}+\hat{a}_{\alpha,i}^{\dagger}\big)\;,\quad[\hat{a}_{\alpha,i},\hat{a}_{\beta,j}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{ij}\;,

(16)

and

\hat{H}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\,\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{x})\,\frac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|}\,\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\;.

(17)

The perturbation
\hat{H}^{\prime}
contains self-energy contributions, which
do not affect the interaction between atoms. Setting

\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{H}_{\rm self}+\hat{H}_{\rm int}
and
discarding
\hat{H}_{\rm self}
, we obtain

\hat{H}_{\rm int}=\int d^{3}x\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\,\hat{\rho}_{A}(\mathbf{x})\,\frac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|}\,\hat{\rho}_{B}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\;,

(18)

where

\hat{\rho}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=q\Big[\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}\big)-\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}\big)\Big]\;,\quad\alpha=A,B\;.

(19)

3.3
Perturbative corrections to the vacuum energy

We have split the Hamiltonian such that the unperturbed vacuum energy

E_{0}^{(0)}
is the sum of the zero-point energies of the two atoms:

E_{0}^{(0)}=3\hbar\Omega
.
The first-order correction
E_{0}^{(1)}
is given by

E_{0}^{(1)}=\langle 0|\hat{H}_{\rm int}|0\rangle\;,

(20)

where
|0\rangle
denotes the state annihilated by all

\hat{a}_{\alpha,i}
.
This term vanishes, as can be seen by noting that it equals
the classical Coulomb interaction energy with the charge densities replaced
by
their vacuum expectation values:

\langle 0|\hat{\rho}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})|0\rangle=q\,\Big[\Big(\frac{m\Omega}{\pi\hbar}\Big)^{3/2}\,e^{-\frac{m\Omega}{\hbar}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha})^{2}}-\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}\big)\Big]\;.

(21)

The spherical symmetry of the vacuum charge densities about each
nucleus implies
E_{0}^{(1)}=0
.

The interaction Hamiltonian can be written equivalently in terms of the
polarization by integrating by parts:

\hat{H}_{\rm int}=\int d^{3}x\int d^{3}x^{\prime}\,\hat{P}_{A}^{i}(\mathbf{x})\,F^{ij}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\,\hat{P}_{B}^{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\;,

(22)

with
F^{ij}(\mathbf{R})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\frac{3R^{i}R^{j}}{|\mathbf{R}|^{5}}-\frac{\delta^{ij}}{|\mathbf{R}|^{3}}\right)
, where

\langle 0|\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}|0\rangle=0
. Note that we assume
no correlations between
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{A}
and
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{B}
.

Moving to the second-order term,
E_{0}^{(2)}
:

E_{0}^{(2)}=-\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{|\langle 0|\hat{H}_{\rm int}|n\rangle|^{2}}{E_{n}^{(0)}-E_{0}^{(0)}}\;.

(23)

Using a multipole expansion of the polarization operator
\hat{\mathbf{P}}
:

\hat{\mathbf{P}}=\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{(2)}+\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{(4)}+\ldots\;,

(24)

where we follow the convention of using
2^{2k}
to label the
corresponding terms (order
k=0
means monopole,
k=1
dipole, etc.),
we obtain an infinite number of second-order contributions by
inserting the multipole expansion in
\hat{H}_{\rm int}
. The simplest one
arises from the dipolar approximation, where

\hat{\mathbf{P}}\simeq\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{(2)}_{\alpha}

with
\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{(2)}_{\alpha}=\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{\alpha}\,\delta^{3}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha})
and

\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{\alpha}=q\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}
.

To second order, only
contributions from excited states of the form

|n\rangle=|i\rangle_{A}|j\rangle_{B}\;,\quad i,j=1,2,3\quad\text{(dipole directions)}\;,

(25)

survive, leading to

E_{0}^{(2)}=E_{\text{London}}=-\frac{3}{4}\hbar\,\frac{\alpha^{2}\Omega}{R^{6}}\;,

(26)

where the static polarizability is

\alpha=\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi m\Omega^{2}}\;.

(27)

This is London’s renowned result, displaying the characteristic
R^{-6}

dependence.

In what follows we will often rewrite the energy denominators that appear
in
stationary perturbation theory using integral representations that
introduce an

auxiliary
time variable. By this device, including higher-order
terms
becomes more tractable. However, note that this is a bookkeeping device:
the
Hamiltonian remains time independent and no external driving is implied.
Retardation enters explicitly only through the electromagnetic-field correlators at finite separation in
Sec.
4
.

3.4
Introducing a time-dependent formulation for higher orders

Let us see how the previous result may be reformulated in a time-dependent
fashion. Since
\langle 0|\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{x}})|0\rangle=0
implies
\langle 0|\hat{H}_{\rm int}|0\rangle=0
,
we can write
E_{0}^{(2)}
as

E^{(2)}_{0}=-\langle 0|\hat{H}_{\rm int}\,\frac{1}{:\!\hat{H}_{0}\!:}\,\hat{H}_{\rm int}|0\rangle\;,

(28)

with
:\!\hat{H}_{0}\!:\equiv\hat{H}_{0}-\langle 0|\hat{H}_{0}|0\rangle
.

The key step is to use the integral representation

\frac{1}{:\!\hat{H}_{0}\!:}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\int^{\infty}_{0}d\tau\,e^{-\tau:\hat{H}_{0}:/\hbar}\;,

(29)

which allows us to write

E^{(2)}_{0}=-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int^{\infty}_{0}d\tau\,\langle 0|\hat{H}_{\rm int}\,e^{-\tau:\hat{H}_{0}:/\hbar}\,\hat{H}_{\rm int}|0\rangle\;.

(30)

This may be rewritten equivalently as

\displaystyle E^{(2)}_{0}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{T\to\infty}\left\{\frac{1}{\hbar T}\,\int^{T}_{0}d\tau_{1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}d\tau_{2}\;\langle 0|\hat{H}_{\rm int}\,e^{-(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}):\hat{H}_{0}:/\hbar}\,\hat{H}_{\rm int}|0\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{T\to\infty}\left\{\frac{1}{2\hbar T}\,\int^{T}_{-T}d\tau_{1}\int_{-T}^{\tau_{1}}d\tau_{2}\;\langle 0|e^{\tau_{1}:\hat{H}_{0}:/\hbar}\hat{H}_{\rm int}e^{-\tau_{1}:\hat{H}_{0}:/\hbar}\,e^{\tau_{2}:\hat{H}_{0}:/\hbar}\hat{H}_{\rm int}e^{-\tau_{2}:\hat{H}_{0}:/\hbar}|0\rangle\right\}\;.

(31)

Using the interaction representation in imaginary time,

\hat{\mathcal{O}}(\tau)\equiv e^{\tau:\hat{H}_{0}:/\hbar}\hat{\mathcal{O}}e^{-\tau:\hat{H}_{0}:/\hbar}
, and the usual time
ordering

\mathrm{T}
:

E^{(2)}_{0}=-\lim_{T\to\infty}\left\{\frac{1}{2\hbar T}\,\int^{T}_{-T}d\tau_{1}\int^{T}_{-T}d\tau_{2}\,\langle 0|\mathrm{T}\big[\hat{H}_{\rm int}(\tau_{1})\hat{H}_{\rm int}(\tau_{2})\big]|0\rangle\right\}\;.

(32)

By an entirely analogous approach, the
n
th-order term (see the Appendix)
can be written as

E^{(n)}_{0}=(-1)^{n-1}\lim_{T\to\infty}\left\{\frac{1}{T\hbar^{n-1}n!}\,\int^{T}_{-T}d\tau_{1}\ldots\int^{T}_{-T}d\tau_{n}\,\langle 0|\mathrm{T}\big[\hat{H}_{\rm int}(\tau_{1})\ldots\hat{H}_{\rm int}(\tau_{n})\big]|0\rangle\right\}_{\rm c}\;,

(33)

where the subscript “c” indicates that only the
connected contribution should be considered.

The sum of all orders can be rendered as

\displaystyle E_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\,E^{(n)}_{0}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\hbar}{T}\left[\langle 0|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\,\mathrm{T}\Big(\frac{1}{\hbar}\int^{T}_{-T}d\tau\,\hat{H}_{\rm int}(\tau)\Big)^{n}|0\rangle\right]_{\rm c}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\hbar}{T}\left[\langle 0|\mathrm{T}\Big(e^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int^{T}_{-T}d\tau\,\hat{H}_{\rm int}(\tau)}\Big)|0\rangle\right]_{\rm c}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\hbar}{T}\log{\mathcal{Z}}\;,

(34)

where

{\mathcal{Z}}=\langle 0|\mathrm{T}\Big(e^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int^{T}_{-T}d\tau\,\hat{H}_{\rm int}(\tau)}\Big)|0\rangle\;,

(35)

and the logarithm extracts the connected piece.

Wick’s theorem
[
14
]
allows one to evaluate
{\mathcal{Z}}
to all orders,
based on knowledge of the contractions:

\langle 0|\mathrm{T}[{\hat{r}}_{\alpha}^{i}(\tau){\hat{r}}_{\beta}^{j}(\tau^{\prime})]|0\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\delta^{ij}\,\frac{\hbar}{2m\Omega}e^{-\Omega|\tau-\tau^{\prime}|}\;.

(36)

The resulting energy, including all terms, can be written as

\displaystyle E_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{2}\int\frac{d\nu}{2\pi}\left\{2\log\Big[1-\Big(\frac{\alpha\Omega^{2}}{(\nu^{2}+\Omega^{2})R^{3}}\Big)^{2}\Big]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\log\Big[1-\Big(\frac{2\alpha\Omega^{2}}{(\nu^{2}+\Omega^{2})R^{3}}\Big)^{2}\Big]\right\}\;.

(37)

One can verify that London’s result [Eq. (
26
)] emerges when

\alpha/R^{3}\ll 1
, or equivalently,
q^{2}/R\ll m\Omega^{2}R^{2}
.
This condition states that the Coulomb interaction between an electron and
the other nucleus is much smaller than the energy binding it to its own.

As explained in Ref.
[
10
]
, for small values of
R
,
thresholds appear for the emergence of an imaginary part in the energy:

R_{1}\equiv\Big(\frac{q^{2}}{2\pi m\Omega^{2}}\Big)^{1/3}\;,\quad R_{2}\equiv\Big(\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi m\Omega^{2}}\Big)^{1/3}\;.

(38)

This imaginary part signals a
nonvanishing probability of vacuum decay: when the two atoms are
sufficiently
close to each other, the true vacuum should be closer to that of two
electrons in a molecule.

Here we have obtained this result in the context of the instantaneous
approximation, which is appropriate for describing this
short-distance phenomenon.

4
Including retardation

Units and notation.
Throughout this section we keep the
speed of light
c
explicit in the discussion of scales, but we will set

c=1
in intermediate momentum-space steps so that the photon dispersion
reads
\omega_{k}=k
and the Fourier variable
k\equiv|\bm{k}|
may
be interpreted as an angular frequency. Restoring
c
is then achieved by
replacing
k\to c\,k
(and likewise
k^{\prime}\to c\,k^{\prime}
) and, equivalently, by
using the dimensionless combination
r\equiv\Omega R/c
when presenting
results as functions of the separation
R
.

At this point, assuming we want to use the dipole approximation while
keeping retardation effects, it is convenient to use the
Power-Zienau-Woolley
transformation,
[
11
,
12
]
whereby we add to
L
a total derivative:
L\to L_{F}=L+\frac{dF}{dt}

with
F(t)=-\int d^{3}x\,{\mathbf{P}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\cdot{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}(t,{\mathbf{x}})
.

The resulting Lagrangian is

L_{F}=\frac{m}{2}\sum_{\alpha}\big(\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}-\Omega^{2}{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}\big)+\int d^{3}x\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big(\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}^{2}-{\mathbf{B}}^{2}\big)+{\mathbf{J}}_{M}\cdot{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}-{\mathbf{P}}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}\Big]-U_{\rm Coul}\;.

(39)

where
{\mathbf{J}}_{M}={\mathbf{J}}-{\mathbf{J}}_{P}
, the latter
being the polarization current:
{\mathbf{J}}_{P}=\dot{\mathbf{P}}
. It is
straightforward to show that

{\mathbf{J}}_{M}(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\nabla\times{\mathbf{M}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\;,\quad{\mathbf{M}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})=q\sum_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}du\,u\,{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\times\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\,\delta^{3}\big({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\alpha}-u{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}(t)\big)\;.

(40)

After integrating by parts,

L_{F}=\frac{m}{2}\sum_{\alpha}\big(\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}-\Omega^{2}{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}\big)+\int d^{3}x\Big[\frac{1}{2}\big(\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}^{2}-{\mathbf{B}}^{2}\big)+{\mathbf{M}}\cdot{\mathbf{B}}-{\mathbf{P}}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}\Big]-U_{\rm Coul}\;.

(41)

Note that we could replace
{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathbf{P}}
by

{\mathbf{M}}_{\perp}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{\perp}
, respectively,
since they are multiplied by solenoidal fields.

The coupling between the EM field and the charges is contained in the
expression

L_{I}=\int d^{3}x\,({\mathbf{M}}_{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{B}}+{\mathbf{P}}_{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{E}}_{\perp})\;.

(42)

For a neutral system, the Coulomb energy may be written equivalently as

U_{\rm Coul}=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x\,|{\mathbf{P}}_{\parallel}|^{2}\;.

(43)

Neglecting contributions from
{\mathbf{M}}
(setting
{\mathbf{M}}=0
),
the resulting approximate Lagrangian becomes

L=\frac{m}{2}\sum_{\alpha}\big(\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}-\Omega^{2}{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x\Big[\big(\dot{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}-{\mathbf{P}}_{\perp}\big)^{2}-{\mathbf{B}}^{2}\Big]\;,

(44)

where we completed a square
and used the property that

\int d^{3}x\,|{\mathbf{P}}|^{2}
contains only self-energy terms.

Using
{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{\perp}
(
=-{\mathbf{E}}_{\perp}
) to denote the
conjugate
momentum to
{\mathbf{A}}_{\perp}
, we get for the classical Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{{\mathbf{p}}_{\alpha}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{m\Omega^{2}{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}^{2}}{2}\Big)+\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x\Big[{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{\perp}^{2}+{\mathbf{B}}^{2}+{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{P}}_{\perp}\Big]\;.

(45)

The Hamiltonian thus has the structure

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\rm atom}+\hat{H}_{\rm EM}+\hat{H}_{I}\;,

(46)

with the interaction
\hat{H}_{I}=-\int d^{3}x\,\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\perp}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{\perp}
.

The quantum Hamiltonian for this system can be written as

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\rm atom}+\hat{H}_{\rm EM}+\hat{H}^{\prime}\;,

(47)

where
\hat{H}_{\rm atom}
describes the two harmonic oscillators,

\hat{H}_{\rm EM}
is the free electromagnetic field Hamiltonian, and

\hat{H}^{\prime}
contains the atom-field coupling.

Now consider the description of the system states, which are
conveniently constructed as tensor products between states of each atom and
the electromagnetic field. The term
\hat{H}_{0}=\hat{H}_{\rm atom}+\hat{H}_{\rm EM}
contains no
interaction and its eigenstates are known. In particular, the vacuum
consists of both atoms in the ground state together with the
electromagnetic
field vacuum (absence of photons).

From now on we consider
\hat{H}_{0}
normal-ordered, so the
energy of the ground state
|0\rangle=|0\rangle_{\text{EM}}|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}
is zero. This convention simplifies the notation and does
not
affect the interaction potential between atoms, since that interaction only
involves energy differences.

The first nontrivial order yielding a distance-dependent energy
is fourth order in charge. Discarding terms independent of the distance
between atoms, we have

E^{(4)}_{0}=-\sum_{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\neq 0}\frac{\langle 0|\hat{H}^{\prime}|n_{1}\rangle\langle n_{1}|\hat{H}^{\prime}|n_{2}\rangle\langle n_{2}|\hat{H}^{\prime}|n_{3}\rangle\langle n_{3}|\hat{H}^{\prime}|0\rangle}{\left(E^{(0)}_{n_{1}}-E^{(0)}_{0}\right)\left(E^{(0)}_{n_{2}}-E^{(0)}_{0}\right)\left(E^{(0)}_{n_{3}}-E^{(0)}_{0}\right)}\;.

(48)

For example, one set of intermediate states has the form

\displaystyle|n_{1}\rangle=|k^{i},\lambda\rangle_{\rm EM}|i\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}\,,

\displaystyle\Delta E^{(0)}_{n_{1}}=\hbar\left(\Omega+k\right)\;,

\displaystyle|n_{2}\rangle=|k^{i},\lambda;k^{\prime j},\lambda^{\prime}\rangle_{\rm EM}|0\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}\,,

\displaystyle\Delta E^{(0)}_{n_{2}}=\hbar(k+k^{\prime})\;,

\displaystyle|n_{3}\rangle=|k^{\prime j},\lambda^{\prime}\rangle_{\rm EM}|0\rangle_{A}|j\rangle_{B}\,,

\displaystyle\Delta E^{(0)}_{n_{3}}=\hbar(\Omega+k^{\prime})\;.

The matrix elements take the form

\langle 0|\hat{H}^{\prime}|k^{i},\lambda\rangle_{\rm EM}|i\rangle_{A}|0\rangle_{B}=-\frac{q\hbar}{\sqrt{2m\Omega}}\frac{ik}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{3}2k}}\varepsilon^{i}_{k\lambda}e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{A}}\;.

(49)

Summing over all free indices, we use the property

\sum_{\lambda}\varepsilon^{i}_{k\lambda}\varepsilon^{j}_{k\lambda}=\delta^{ij}-\frac{k^{i}k^{j}}{k^{2}}\;.

(50)

Adding all 12 contributions, the interaction energy is obtained by
performing
the integral
[
13
]

\displaystyle E^{(4)}_{0}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar}{2}{\left(\frac{q^{2}}{2m\Omega}\right)}^{2}\int\frac{d\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int\frac{d\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\frac{(1+(\hat{k}\cdot\hat{k}^{\prime})^{2})\,kk^{\prime}\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}e^{i\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{R}}}{(\Omega+k)(\Omega+k^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{1}{\Omega(\Omega+k^{\prime})}+\frac{1}{(k+k^{\prime})(\Omega+k)}+\frac{1}{(k+k^{\prime})(\Omega+k^{\prime})}+\frac{1}{(\Omega+k)(\Omega+k^{\prime})}\right]\;.

(51)

Let us now see how Eq. (
48
) can be simplified by following
the same approach as for the instantaneous approximation, now with

\hat{H}^{\prime}(\tau)=-\hat{d}^{i}_{A}(\tau)\hat{E}^{i}(\bm{R}_{A},\tau)-\hat{d}^{j}_{B}(\tau)\hat{E}^{j}(\bm{R}_{B},\tau)\;.

(52)

Since
\hat{H}^{\prime}
is a sum of two terms, by distribution one would have to
calculate 16 expectation values and then sum them. Some vanish and others
give rise to self-energies; only 6 of those terms depend on the distance
between atoms. Each term is a product of four dipole moment and electric
field
operators. Wick’s theorem allows us to separate the expectation value into
a
sum of products of contractions. These
contractions involve two operators acting in the same space. The 6
expectation values that depend on the interatomic distance have 2
possible contractions. In total, there are 12 terms contributing to the
interaction, all equal. An example is

\langle\hat{d}^{i_{1}}_{A}(\tau_{1})\hat{d}^{i_{2}}_{A}(\tau_{2})\rangle\langle\hat{d}^{i_{3}}_{B}(\tau_{3})\hat{d}^{i_{4}}_{B}(\tau_{4})\rangle\langle\hat{E}^{i_{1}}(\bm{R}_{A},\tau_{1})\hat{E}^{i_{3}}(\bm{R}_{B},\tau_{3})\rangle\langle\hat{E}^{i_{2}}(\bm{R}_{A},\tau_{2})\hat{E}^{i_{4}}(\bm{R}_{B},\tau_{4})\rangle\;.

(53)

From now on,
\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}\rangle=\langle 0|\mathrm{T}[\hat{\mathcal{O}}]|0\rangle
denotes the time-ordered vacuum
expectation value. Using the definition of the dipole moment operator

\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{\alpha}=q\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{\alpha}

[cf. Eq. (
16
)] and its time evolution, we obtain

M^{ij}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})=\langle\hat{d}^{i}_{A}(\tau)\hat{d}^{j}_{A}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle=\delta^{ij}\frac{q^{2}\hbar}{2m\Omega}e^{-\Omega|\tau-\tau^{\prime}|}=\hbar\delta^{ij}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{d\nu}{2\pi}\,\tilde{M}(\nu)e^{i\nu(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}\;,

(54)

with

\tilde{M}(\nu)=\frac{q^{2}}{m}\frac{1}{\nu^{2}+\Omega^{2}}\;.

(55)

On the other hand, according to Maxwell’s equations, the electric field can
be
calculated from the four-potential:

\hat{E}^{i}(x)=\partial^{0}\hat{A}^{i}(x)-\partial^{i}\hat{A}^{0}(x)\;,

(56)

where
x
is the four-vector
(t,\bm{x})
. This allows calculation
of
the contraction between two electric field operators from the photon
propagator.
Although the interaction was introduced in the Coulomb gauge, it is
convenient here to
evaluate the gauge-invariant correlator

I^{ij}=\langle\hat{E}^{i}\hat{E}^{j}\rangle
in a covariant (Feynman) gauge;
the final result for
I^{ij}
is gauge independent.
In the imaginary-time scheme, the propagator takes the form

\langle\hat{A}^{\mu}(x)\hat{A}^{\nu}(x^{\prime})\rangle=\hbar\delta^{\mu\nu}\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,\frac{e^{ik\cdot(x-x^{\prime})}}{k^{2}}\;.

(57)

In this imaginary-time scheme (Wick rotation
t\to-i\tau
), the Minkowski
metric
g^{\mu\nu}
is replaced by the Euclidean metric
\delta^{\mu\nu}
.
We write the Euclidean four-momentum as
k=(\nu,\bm{k})
, with

k^{2}=\nu^{2}+\bm{k}^{2}
. Fourier-transforming the derivatives in

\hat{E}^{i}=\partial^{0}\hat{A}^{i}-\partial^{i}\hat{A}^{0}
then yields, after some
algebra

I^{ij}(x-x^{\prime})=\langle\hat{E}^{i}(x)\hat{E}^{j}(x^{\prime})\rangle=\hbar\delta^{4}(x-x^{\prime})+\hbar\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,\frac{e^{-ik\cdot(x-x^{\prime})}}{k^{2}}\left(-\delta^{ij}\bm{k}^{2}+k^{i}k^{j}\right)\;.

(58)

We can discard the delta since the positions of the atoms never coincide.
This
integral can also be obtained by summing different derivatives with respect
to

x
of the photon propagator:

I^{ij}(x-x^{\prime})=\hbar\left(\delta^{ij}\nabla^{2}-\partial^{i}\partial^{j}\right)\int\frac{d^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,\frac{e^{-ik\cdot(x-x^{\prime})}}{k^{2}}\;.

(59)

It is possible to perform the spatial integral to obtain the
propagator in terms of a single integral:

I^{ij}(x-x^{\prime})=\hbar\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{d\nu}{2\pi}\,\tilde{I}^{ij}(|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}|,\nu)e^{i\nu(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}\;,

(60)

with

\tilde{I}^{ij}(|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}|,\nu)=\left(\delta^{ij}\nabla^{2}-\partial^{i}\partial^{j}\right)\frac{e^{-|\nu||\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}|}}{4\pi|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}|}\;.

(61)

Putting all this together, the fourth-order energy correction becomes

E_{I}=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\frac{1}{\hbar^{3}}\frac{12}{4!}\left(\prod^{4}_{s=1}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\tau_{s}\right)M^{i_{1}i_{2}}(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})M^{i_{3}i_{4}}(\tau_{3}-\tau_{4})I^{i_{1}i_{3}}(\bm{R},\tau_{1}-\tau_{3})I^{i_{2}i_{4}}(\bm{R},\tau_{2}-\tau_{4})\;.

(62)

After manipulations (returning to the original time variables and canceling
normalization factors), we obtain

E_{I}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\hbar^{3}}\left(\prod^{3}_{s=1}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\tau_{s}\right)M(\tau_{1})M(\tau_{3})I^{ij}(\bm{R},\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})I^{ij}(\bm{R},\tau_{2}+\tau_{3})\;.

(63)

Using that
\tilde{M}(\tau)=\tilde{M}(-\tau)
and

I^{ij}(\bm{R},-\tau)=I^{ij}(\bm{R},\tau)
, together with
the convolution theorem, we arrive at

E_{I}=-\frac{\hbar}{2}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{d\nu}{2\pi}\left(\tilde{M}(\nu)\tilde{I}^{ij}(\bm{R},\nu)\right)^{2}\;.

(64)

Summing over
i,j
we obtain

E_{I}=-\frac{\hbar\Omega}{\pi}\left(\frac{q^{2}\Omega}{4\pi m}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{r^{3}}\int^{\infty}_{0}d\nu\,\frac{\nu^{4}+2\nu^{3}+5\nu^{2}+6\nu+3}{(\nu^{2}+r^{2})^{2}}\,e^{-2\nu}\;,

(65)

where
r\equiv\Omega R/c
(we used
c=1
in intermediate steps). This
result is equivalent to that obtained
in Ref.
[
10
]
. Figure
1
shows

-E_{I}\cdot r^{6}
, divided by
A^{2}
with
A=q^{2}\Omega/4\pi m
, as a
function
of
r
on a log-log scale.
Figure
2
shows the numerical derivative of this curve,
revealing the exponent throughout the entire range of distances.

Figure 1:
Numerical evaluation of Eq. (
65
) shown on a
log–log scale. The thick curve plots the dimensionless combination

-E_{I}(r)\,r^{6}/A^{2}
versus
r\equiv\Omega R/c
, where
A\equiv q^{2}\Omega/(4\pi m)
. Thin dashed lines show the expected behavior if

E_{I}
followed pure power laws: constant for
E_{I}\propto r^{-6}

(London regime, short distances) and
\propto r^{-1}
for

E_{I}\propto r^{-7}
(Casimir–Polder regime, large distances).

Figure 2:
Logarithmic slope extracted from Fig.
1
:

\mathrm{d}\log[-E_{I}(r)\,r^{6}]/\mathrm{d}\log r
. The curve interpolates
between the London exponent
-6
for
r\ll 1
and the Casimir–Polder
exponent
-7
for
r\gg 1
.

Both asymptotic behaviors can be determined analytically. For
distances such that
r\ll 1
, the exponential decay ensures that
\nu

remains of order unity, so
r\nu\ll 1
, yielding

E_{I}\approx-3\hbar\left(\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi m}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{R^{6}}\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{d\nu}{2\pi}\,\frac{1}{(\nu^{2}+\Omega^{2})^{2}}=-\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi m\Omega}\right)^{2}\frac{\hbar}{\Omega R^{6}}\;.

(66)

This result is identical to London’s equation (
26
).

The integral in Eq. (
65
) can be evaluated exactly:

E_{I}=-\frac{\hbar\Omega}{2\pi}\left(\frac{q^{2}\Omega}{4\pi m}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{r^{3}}\left[r(6-r^{2})+(3-7r^{2}+r^{4})f(2r)+2r(3-3r^{2}+r^{4})g(2r)\right]\;,

(67)

where

\displaystyle f(r)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ci}(r)\sin{(r)}-\big(\mathrm{Si}(r)-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)\cos{(r)}\;,

(68)

\displaystyle g(r)

\displaystyle=-\big[\mathrm{Ci}(r)\cos{(r)}+\big(\mathrm{Si}(r)-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)\sin{(r)}\big]\;,

(69)

and
\mathrm{Ci}(r)
and
\mathrm{Si}(r)
are the cosine and sine
integrals,
respectively. For
r\gg 1
, the energy approaches

E_{I}\approx-\frac{23\hbar c}{4\pi}\,\alpha^{2}\,\frac{1}{R^{7}}\;.

(70)

Using the polarizability
\alpha=q^{2}/(4\pi m\Omega^{2})

[Eq. (
27
)], we can identify the factor that
corrects the London result:

E_{I}\approx-\frac{23\hbar c}{4\pi}\,\frac{\alpha^{2}}{R^{7}}=E_{\text{London}}\times\frac{23}{3\pi}\,\frac{c}{\Omega R}\;.

(71)

Although the results obtained in this work are
identical to those of Casimir and Polder, the approaches considered differ.
Casimir and Polder considered the retarded potential of
an atom that emits radiation and showed that, when retardation is
negligible, the London result is recovered. Our starting point was a model
with electrostatic and instantaneous interaction. Although we have
introduced an imaginary-time dependence, the perturbations employed were
time-independent. This highlights the static and instantaneous character of
the interaction, which is not obvious at first sight.

5
Conclusions

We have presented a derivation of the van der Waals interaction energy
between two atoms, using first standard stationary perturbation theory as a
starting point, and then showing how a time-dependent formalism provides a
much simpler tool for evaluating higher orders.

In the instantaneous approximation, we showed that when the
atoms are too close, an instability phenomenon—already predicted
in Ref.
[
10
]
in the context of a path integral
calculation—is captured by our treatment. It should be clear, however,
that this instability signals the breakdown of our description; a
different framework (for instance, molecular orbitals) is needed in that
regime.

For the long-distance case, where retardation effects are essential,
we demonstrated how the time-dependent treatment yields a much
simpler way to evaluate the interaction energy.
Moreover, it makes it possible to identify the precise limit
required to recover the London approximation. The key step is to assume
that
the electric field propagator reduces to a delta function in time. This
approximation can be consistently applied at any stage of the calculation,
always yielding the same result. To understand why, note that
the harmonic oscillator propagator depends only on its energy, while the
electric field propagator depends only on the interatomic distance.
Effectively, the interaction becomes instantaneous
when the particles are sufficiently close, in which case we are essentially
calculating the Fourier transform of a delta function. From an operator
perspective, this requires the replacement

I^{ij}(x-x^{\prime})\to\tilde{I}^{ij}(\bm{R},0)\,\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\;,

(72)

where

\tilde{I}^{ij}(\bm{R},0)=\left(\delta^{ij}\nabla^{2}-\partial^{i}\partial^{j}\right)\frac{1}{4\pi R}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\frac{\delta^{ij}}{R^{3}}-\frac{3R^{i}R^{j}}{R^{5}}\right)-\delta^{ij}\delta^{(3)}(\bm{R})\;,

(73)

and thus

\tilde{I}^{ij}(\bm{R},0)\tilde{I}^{ji}(\bm{R},0)=\frac{6}{(4\pi)^{2}R^{6}}\;.

(74)

Appendix A
Vacuum energy in Rayleigh–Schrödinger perturbation theory

We summarize here some relevant results on stationary perturbation theory,
following the resolvent approach of Refs.
[
15
,
16
]
,
as applied to the present context.
One starts by introducing a parameter
\lambda
and considering the family
of
self-adjoint Hamiltonians

\hat{H}(\lambda)=\hat{H}_{0}+\lambda\hat{H}_{\rm int}\;,

(75)

such that
\hat{H}=\hat{H}(1)
, where
\hat{H}_{0}
has a nondegenerate
isolated vacuum with zero energy.
We denote by
E(\lambda)
the eigenvalue of
\hat{H}(\lambda)
that is
analytic
near
\lambda=0
and satisfies
E(0)=0
. We also introduce the projectors

\hat{P}\equiv|0\rangle\langle 0|\;,\quad\hat{Q}\equiv\hat{I}-\hat{P}\;,

(76)

where
\hat{I}
denotes the identity operator.

Under these assumptions, there exists a positively oriented contour

\Gamma
enclosing
E(\lambda)
(and no other point of the spectrum), and
one can write

E(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}z\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\big(z-\hat{H}(\lambda)\big)^{-1}\right]dz\;.

(77)

For
z\in\Gamma
and
|\lambda|
small, we have the Neumann series

(z-\hat{H}(\lambda))^{-1}=(z-\hat{H}_{0})^{-1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[\lambda\hat{H}_{\rm int}(z-\hat{H}_{0})^{-1}\right]^{n}\;.

(78)

We can then perform a Laurent expansion around
z=0
:

(z-\hat{H}_{0})^{-1}=\frac{1}{z}\hat{P}-\sum_{k\geq 0}z^{k}\,\hat{S}^{k+1}\;,

(79)

where
\hat{S}^{0}\equiv-\hat{P}
and
\hat{S}^{k}\equiv\hat{Q}\,\hat{H}_{0}^{-k}\,\hat{Q}
for
k\geq 1
. The proof follows from noting that
on
\mathrm{Ran}(\hat{P})
one has
(z-\hat{H}_{0})^{-1}=z^{-1}\hat{P}
, while on
\mathrm{Ran}(\hat{Q})
:

(z-\hat{H}_{0})^{-1}=-\hat{H}_{0}^{-1}(1-z\hat{H}_{0}^{-1})^{-1}=-\sum_{k\geq 0}z^{k}\,\hat{Q}\hat{H}_{0}^{-(k+1)}\hat{Q}\;,

(80)

where
\hat{H}_{0}^{-1}
is understood as the bounded inverse of
\hat{H}_{0}
on

\mathrm{Ran}(\hat{Q})
(which exists because
0
is isolated).

When inserted into Eq. (
77
), this yields a
power series
E(\lambda)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda^{n}E^{(n)}
, where
E^{(n)}

is
determined by extracting from
Eq. (
77
) the coefficient of
\lambda^{n}
and then
taking the residue at
z=0
. Using Eq. (
79
),
each factor
(z-\hat{H}_{0})^{-1}
contributes either
(1/z)\hat{P}
or a
regular
term
-z^{k}\hat{S}^{k+1}
. The coefficient of
z^{-1}

arises from choosing exactly
one

(1/z)\hat{P}
overall, while the
remaining

(z-\hat{H}_{0})^{-1}
factors contribute
regular powers whose total power of
z
cancels the extra
z
in
Eq. (
77
).

A convenient way to organize the resulting combinatorics is to record the
regular contributions via the operators
\hat{S}^{k}
.
The outcome is a closed formula for

n\geq 1
, giving the
n
th-order vacuum-energy coefficient
as

E^{(n)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{n-1}\geq 0\\
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n-1}=n-1\end{subarray}}\langle 0|\,\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{k_{1}}\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{k_{2}}\cdots\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{k_{n-1}}\hat{H}_{\rm int}\,|0\rangle\;,

(81)

with
\hat{S}^{0}=-\hat{P}
and
\hat{S}^{k\geq 1}=\hat{Q}\hat{H}_{0}^{-k}\hat{Q}
.

Equation (
81
) produces,
through the presence of
\hat{S}^{0}=-\hat{P}
, all the
“subtraction” terms that appear in the usual stationary
Rayleigh–Schrödinger expressions (except for cyclic ones).

Using
\hat{S}^{1}=\hat{Q}\hat{H}_{0}^{-1}\hat{Q}
and
\hat{S}^{0}=-\hat{P}
, subtraction terms first arise at fourth order.
With
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=3
, one finds

E^{(4)}=\langle 0|\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{1}\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{1}\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{1}\hat{H}_{\rm int}|0\rangle-\langle 0|\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{1}\hat{H}_{\rm int}|0\rangle\,\langle 0|\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{2}\hat{H}_{\rm int}|0\rangle\;,

(82)

where the second term arises from the contribution with the vacuum
projector
in the
middle
,

e.g.
,
\langle 0|\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{1}\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{0}\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{2}\hat{H}_{\rm int}|0\rangle=-\langle 0|\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{1}\hat{H}_{\rm int}|0\rangle\langle 0|\hat{H}_{\rm int}\hat{S}^{2}\hat{H}_{\rm int}|0\rangle
. This is precisely the familiar Rayleigh–Schrödinger
subtraction
structure at fourth order.

The role of the subtraction terms is, in the general case, to make
the correction at each order
connected
,
i.e.
, without any
sub-cycle (from vacuum to vacuum) like the one being subtracted
in the second term of Eq. (
82
).
