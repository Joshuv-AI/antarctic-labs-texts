Parity of k k -differentials in genus zero and one Thanks: Research of D.C. is supported in part by the National Science Foundation under Grant DMS-2301030 and by Simons Travel Support for Mathematicians.
Dawei Chen, Evan Chen, Kenny Lau, Ken Ono, Jujian Zhang

Abstract
. Here we completely determine the spin parity of k k -differentials with prescribed zero and pole orders on Riemann surfaces of genus zero and one. This result was previously obtained conditionally by the first author and Quentin Gendron assuming the truth of a number-theoretic hypothesis [ 4 , Conjecture A.10] . We prove this hypothesis by reformulating it in terms of Jacobi symbols, reducing the proof to a combinatorial identity and standard facts about Jacobi symbols. The proof was obtained by AxiomProver and the system formalized the proof of the combinatorial identity in Lean/Mathlib (see the Appendix).

1.
Introduction and Statement of Results

A (meromorphic)
k
-differential
\xi
is a section of the

k
th
power of the canonical bundle on a Riemann surface
X
of genus
g
.
For a tuple of integers
\mu=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})
where

m_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}=k(2g-2),

we let
\Omega^{k}\mathcal{M}_{g}(\mu)
be the moduli space of (primitive)
k
-differentials

(X,\xi)
with zero and pole orders given by
\mu
.
A
k
-differential induces a flat metric with conical singularities on
X
,
where the cone angles are multiples of
2\pi/k
, determined by the orders at the zeros and poles.
From this perspective, the study of
k
-differentials of a prescribed
type plays an important role in surface dynamics, moduli theory, and combinatorial enumeration.
We refer the reader to
[
18
,
17
,
6
,
2
]
for an introduction to this fascinating subject.

Although
\Omega^{k}\mathcal{M}_{g}(\mu)
is a complex orbifold, it can be disconnected for special
\mu
.
The classification of connected components of
\Omega^{k}\mathcal{M}_{g}(\mu)
has
been completed for holomorphic differentials (
k=1
and
m_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
,
[
10
]
);
meromorphic differentials (
k=1
and some
m_{i}<0
,
[
3
]
);
quadratic differentials of finite area (
k=2
and
m_{i}\geq-1
for all
i
,
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
5
]
);
and quadratic differentials of infinite area (
k=2
and some
m_{i}<-1
,
[
4
]
).
However, for
k\geq 3
, the classification of connected components of

\Omega^{k}\mathcal{M}_{g}(\mu)
remains largely unknown.

In the above results, besides the (easy-to-understand) hyperelliptic structure
and certain ad hoc structures occurring in low genus (e.g., the rotation number in genus one),
the only other known invariant that can help distinguish connected components of

\Omega^{k}\mathcal{M}_{g}(\mu)
is the spin parity.

For
k=1
, let
\omega
be a differential one-form whose zero and pole orders are even.
Then the half-canonical divisor
{\rm div}(\omega)/2
defines a theta-characteristic, whose spin parity

\dim H^{0}\bigl(X,{\rm div}(\omega)/2\bigr)\pmod{2}

is deformation invariant (see
[
1
,
14
]
).
This parity coincides with the Arf invariant defined by the flat surface
structure of
(X,\omega)
(see
[
9
]
and
[
10
, Section 3]
).

Given a
k
-differential
(X,\xi)
parameterized by
\Omega^{k}\mathcal{M}_{g}(\mu)
with
k>1
,
there exists a canonical cyclic cover
\pi\colon\widehat{X}\to X
of degree
k

such that
\pi^{*}\xi=\widehat{\omega}^{k}
,
where
\widehat{\omega}
is a differential one-form on
\widehat{X}
whose zero
and pole orders are uniquely determined by
\mu
(see
[
2
, Section 2.1]
).
If the zero and pole orders of
\widehat{\omega}
are even,
we say that
\Omega^{k}\mathcal{M}_{g}(\mu)
is of parity type.
Consequently, we use the spin parity of
(\widehat{X},\widehat{\omega})
to
define the spin parity of
(X,\xi)
in
\Omega^{k}\mathcal{M}_{g}(\mu)
.

It is thus natural to ask how to determine the spin parity for
k
-differentials of parity type.
In contrast to the case
k=1
, when
k
is even,
all
k
-differentials in
\Omega^{k}\mathcal{M}_{g}(\mu)
of parity type have the same spin parity,
which can be determined explicitly from
\mu
(see
[
12
, Theorem 1.2]
for

k=2
and
[
4
, Section 5.2]
for general even
k
). However, this is not the case when
k
is odd.
Indeed, even if
X
has low genus,
the domain
\widehat{X}
of the canonical cyclic cover can have high genus,
which makes the computation of spin parity nontrivial.
Moreover, if
k
is odd, then
\Omega^{k}\mathcal{M}_{g}(\mu)
is of parity type if
and only if all entries of
\mu
are even (see
[
4
, Proposition 5.1]
).

In this context, for odd
k
,
the spin parity of
k
-differentials in genus zero and one was studied explicitly in
[
4
, Appendix]
.
After a series of delicate geometric arguments and based on numerical evidence,
the entire study eventually reduced to a number-theoretic conjecture (see also

[
4
, Conjecture A.8 and Remark A.9]
for another equivalent form of the conjecture).

Conjecture 1.1

(
[
4
, Conjecture A.10]
)
.

For odd
k
and
\gcd(n,k)=\gcd(n+1,k)=1
,
let
N_{k}(n)
be the number of pairs of positive integers
(b_{1},b_{2})
such that
b_{1},b_{2}\leq(k-1)/2
,

b_{1}+b_{2}\geq(k+1)/2
, and
b_{2}\equiv nb_{1}\pmod{k}
. Then we have

N_{k}(n)\equiv\left\lfloor\frac{k+1}{4}\right\rfloor\pmod{2}.

In this paper, we prove this conjecture.

Theorem 1.2
.

Conjecture
1.1
is true.

Consequently, the results in
[
4
, Appendix]
that were originally stated
conditionally on Conjecture
1.1
become unconditional,
thereby completely determining the spin parity of
k
-differentials in genus zero and one for all
k
.

In order to state these results, we recall the
q
-adic valuation
\nu_{q}(m)

for a prime
q
and an integer
m
,
which is given by the largest exponent
\nu
such that
q^{\nu}
divides
m
.
Additionally, for odd
k
, consider the prime factorization

k=p_{1}^{h_{1}}\cdots p_{s}^{h_{s}}q_{1}^{\ell_{1}}\cdots q_{t}^{\ell_{t}}
,
where each
p_{i}
is an odd prime such that
\lfloor(p_{i}+1)/4\rfloor
is even
and each
q_{i}
is an odd prime such that
\lfloor(q_{i}+1)/4\rfloor
is odd.
For the collection of primes
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{t}\}
in the factorization of
k
,
define

\nu_{\mathcal{Q}}(m)\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\min\{\nu_{q_{i}}(m),\nu_{q_{i}}(k)\}.

Finally, for
\mu=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})
, we define

(1)

n_{k}(\mu)\coloneq\#\left\{i:\nu_{\mathcal{Q}}(m_{i})\not\equiv\nu_{\mathcal{Q}}(k)\pmod{2}\right\}

(see (
6
) for an equivalent definition using Jacobi symbols).
We also denote by
2\mu
the tuple
(2m_{1},\ldots,2m_{n})
.

Since we prove Conjecture
1.1
here, the following two theorems from
[
4
]
are now unconditional.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
4
, Theorem A.16]
)
.

For genus zero and odd
k
, the spin parity of
\Omega^{k}\mathcal{M}_{0}(2\mu)
is
determined by
n_{k}(\mu)\pmod{2}
.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
4
, Theorem A.21]
)
.

For genus one and odd
k
, the spin parity of the connected component

\Omega^{k}\mathcal{M}_{1}(2\mu)^{d}
of rotation number
d
is determined by
n_{k}(\mu)+d+1\pmod{2}
.

In Section

.

1.1

2.
Proof of Conjecture
1.1

The proof of Conjecture
1.1
is based on the observation that

\lfloor\frac{k+1}{4}\rfloor\pmod{2}
is periodic modulo
8
,
and can be reformulated in terms of the Jacobi symbol
\Big(\frac{2}{k}\Big).

2.1.
Preliminaries about Jacobi symbols

We recall three standard formulas (for example, see
[
8
,
15
]
) for the number-theoretic Jacobi symbol
\big(\frac{a}{k}\big)
(where
k
is odd and
\gcd(a,k)=1
).

Lemma 2.1

(Eisenstein
[
15
, p. 131]
)
.

Let
a
and
k
be odd positive integers with
\gcd(a,k)=1
. If
m\coloneq(k-1)/2,
then

\left(\frac{a}{k}\right)=(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lfloor\frac{ai}{k}\right\rfloor}.

Remark 1
.

Eisenstein’s lemma is often stated in the following “counting” form. Let

m=(k-1)/2
, and for
1\leq i\leq m
let
r_{i}\in\{1,2,\dots,k-1\}
denote the least
positive residue of
ai\pmod{k}
. Then

\left(\frac{a}{k}\right)=(-1)^{\,\#\{\,1\leq i\leq m:\ r_{i}>m\,\}}.

Equivalently, writing the even residue set as

E_{k}=\{2,4,\dots,k-1\}=\{2i:1\leq i\leq m\}
, one has

\left(\frac{a}{k}\right)=(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lfloor\frac{2ai}{k}\right\rfloor},

since
ai=q_{i}k+r_{i}
implies

\left\lfloor\frac{2ai}{k}\right\rfloor=2q_{i}+\mathbf{1}_{r_{i}>m}
, so the parity of

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big\lfloor\tfrac{2ai}{k}\big\rfloor
is exactly the number of residues

r_{i}
lying in the “upper half”
\{m+1,\dots,k-1\}
. We use the floor-sum
formulation stated in Lemma
2.1
, which is an equivalent form of Eisenstein’s lemma
for odd
a
and
k
.

The next lemma gives another formulation for these Jacobi symbols,
concerning the location of least residues.

Lemma 2.2

(Gauss–Schering
[
8
]
)
.

Let
a
and
k
be positive integers with
k
odd and
\gcd(a,k)=1
.
Let
r_{k}(s)
denote the least nonnegative residue of
s
modulo
k
and let

H_{k}=\{\,j:1\leq j\leq(k-1)/2\,\}\quad\text{ and }\quad W_{a,k}=\{\,r_{k}(aj):j\in H_{k}\,\}.

If we define

m(a,k)\coloneq\big|\{\,w\in W_{a,k}:w\notin H_{k}\,\}\big|,

then we have

\Big(\frac{a}{k}\Big)=(-1)^{m(a,k)}.

Finally, we recall the simple well-known closed formula for
\Big(\frac{2}{k}\Big).

Lemma 2.3

(Supplementary law for
\big(\frac{2}{k}\big)

[
15
, p. 131]
)
.

Let
k
be an odd positive integer. Then

\Big(\frac{2}{k}\Big)=(-1)^{(k^{2}-1)/8}=\begin{cases}+1,&k\equiv\pm 1\pmod{8},\\
-1,&k\equiv\pm 3\pmod{8}.\end{cases}

Lemmas
2.1
,
2.2
, and
2.3

reduce the conjecture to the study of
a combinatorial sum
F_{k}(a),
which we now define.
If
k\geq 3
is odd integer, then let
m\coloneq(k-1)/2
. For any integer
a
, we define the function

(2)

F_{k}(a)\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lfloor\frac{ai+m}{k}\right\rfloor.

The lemmas above imply the following claim.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
k\geq 3
be odd.
For any integer
a
with
\gcd(a,k)=1
, we have

F_{k}(a)\equiv\begin{cases}0\pmod{2}&{\text{if $a$ is odd}},\\
\lfloor\frac{k+1}{4}\rfloor\pmod{2}&{\text{if $a$ is even}}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
k\geq 3
be odd and set
m=(k-1)/2
.
For each
1\leq i\leq m
, write

ai=kq_{i}+r_{i},\qquad q_{i}=\left\lfloor\frac{ai}{k}\right\rfloor,\qquad 1\leq r_{i}\leq k-1,

where
r_{i}=r_{k}(ai)
is the least positive residue (note
r_{i}\neq 0
since
\gcd(a,k)=1
and
1\leq i<k
).
Then we have

\left\lfloor\frac{ai+m}{k}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{kq_{i}+r_{i}+m}{k}\right\rfloor=q_{i}+\left\lfloor\frac{r_{i}+m}{k}\right\rfloor=q_{i}+\mathbf{1}_{\{r_{i}>m\}},

because
0<r_{i}<k
and
r_{i}+m\geq k
if and only if
r_{i}\geq m+1
. Summing (
2
) over
i=1,\dots,m
gives

(3)

F_{k}(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lfloor\frac{ai}{k}\right\rfloor+\#\{\,1\leq i\leq m:\ r_{k}(ai)>m\,\}.

If we let

S_{k}(a)\coloneq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lfloor\frac{ai}{k}\right\rfloor,

then the second term in (
3
) is exactly
m(a,k)
from
Lemma
2.2
(it counts those residues
r_{k}(ai)
which lie outside
H_{k}=\{1,\dots,m\}
).
Hence, we have

(4)

F_{k}(a)=S_{k}(a)+m(a,k).

Case where
a
is odd.

By Lemma
2.1
,
\big(\frac{a}{k}\big)=(-1)^{S_{k}(a)}
, and by Lemma
2.2
,

\big(\frac{a}{k}\big)=(-1)^{m(a,k)}
.
Therefore, we have
S_{k}(a)\equiv m(a,k)\pmod{2}
, and from (
4
) we obtain

F_{k}(a)\equiv S_{k}(a)+m(a,k)\equiv 0\pmod{2}.

Case where
a
is even.

Then
a+k
is odd and
\big(\frac{a+k}{k}\big)=\big(\frac{a}{k}\big)
.
Applying Lemma
2.1
to
a+k
yields

\Big(\frac{a}{k}\Big)=\Big(\frac{a+k}{k}\Big)=(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lfloor\frac{(a+k)i}{k}\right\rfloor}.

But for each
i
we have

\left\lfloor\frac{(a+k)i}{k}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{ai}{k}+i\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{ai}{k}\right\rfloor+i,

and so we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\lfloor\frac{(a+k)i}{k}\right\rfloor=S_{k}(a)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}i.

On the other hand, Lemma
2.2
gives
\big(\frac{a}{k}\big)=(-1)^{m(a,k)}
.
Therefore, we have

S_{k}(a)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}i\equiv m(a,k)\pmod{2}.

Using (
4
), we conclude

F_{k}(a)=S_{k}(a)+m(a,k)\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{m}i\pmod{2}.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{m}i=m(m+1)/2=(k^{2}-1)/8
, we have

(5)

F_{k}(a)\equiv\frac{k^{2}-1}{8}\pmod{2}.

Finally, because
k
is odd,
k\equiv 1,3,5,7\pmod{8}
.
A direct check gives

\frac{k^{2}-1}{8}\equiv\begin{cases}0&(k\equiv 1,7\!\!\!\pmod{8}),\\
1&(k\equiv 3,5\!\!\!\pmod{8})\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\lfloor\frac{k+1}{4}\right\rfloor\equiv\begin{cases}0&(k\equiv 1,7\!\!\!\pmod{8}),\\
1&(k\equiv 3,5\!\!\!\pmod{8})\end{cases}

modulo
2
.
Thus
\frac{k^{2}-1}{8}\equiv\left\lfloor\frac{k+1}{4}\right\rfloor\pmod{2}
, and (
5
) implies

F_{k}(a)\equiv\left\lfloor\frac{k+1}{4}\right\rfloor\pmod{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Thanks to Lemma
2.4
, Theorem
1.2
follows from the following combinatorial identity.

Lemma 2.5

(Formula for
N_{k}(n)
)
.

If
k
is odd,
m=(k-1)/2
, and
n
satisfies
\gcd(n,k)=1
, then
we have

N_{k}(n)=F_{k}(n+1)-F_{k}(n).

Proof.

Fix
b\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and write

nb=qk+r,\qquad 1\leq r\leq k-1,

(which is possible since
\gcd(n,k)=1
).
Then we have

\left\lfloor\frac{(n+1)b+m}{k}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{nb+m}{k}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{r+m+b}{k}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{r+m}{k}\right\rfloor.

Set
x\coloneq r+m
. Since
1\leq r\leq k-1
and
m=(k-1)/2
, we have

m+1\leq x\leq(k-1)+m=\frac{3k-3}{2},

(with equality possible at the right endpoint), and also
1\leq b\leq m<k
.
In particular, since
x\leq\frac{3k-3}{2}
and
b\leq m=\frac{k-1}{2}
, we have

x+b\leq\frac{3k-3}{2}+\frac{k-1}{2}=2k-2<2k,

so
\left\lfloor\frac{x+b}{k}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{x}{k}\right\rfloor\in\{0,1\}
,
and this difference equals
1
exactly when
x<k\leq x+b
.
Hence, we have

x<x+b<x+k,

and so

\left\lfloor\frac{x+b}{k}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{x}{k}\right\rfloor\in\{0,1\}.

Moreover, this difference equals
1
if and only if
x<k\leq x+b
.
Since
x=r+m
, the condition
x<k
is equivalent to
r\leq m
, and the condition

k\leq x+b
is equivalent to

r\geq k-(m+b)=m+1-b.

Therefore, we have

\left\lfloor\frac{(n+1)b+m}{k}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{nb+m}{k}\right\rfloor=\mathbf{1}_{\{\,m+1-b\leq r\leq m\,\}}.

Now, the congruence condition
b_{2}\equiv nb\pmod{k}
with
1\leq b_{2}\leq m
is solvable
if and only if
r\leq m
, in which case the solution is unique and given by
b_{2}=r
.
Under this identification, the inequality
b+b_{2}\geq m+1
becomes

b+r\geq m+1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r\geq m+1-b.

Thus, for each fixed
b\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, the indicator that there exists a (unique)
b_{2}

making
(b,b_{2})
contribute to
N_{k}(n)
is exactly

\left\lfloor\frac{(n+1)b+m}{k}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{nb+m}{k}\right\rfloor.

Summing over
b=1,\dots,m
gives the desired conclusion

N_{k}(n)=\sum_{b=1}^{m}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{(n+1)b+m}{k}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{nb+m}{k}\right\rfloor\right)=F_{k}(n+1)-F_{k}(n).\qed

Remark 2
.

Lemma
2.5
also turns out to be true even
without the condition
\gcd(n,k)=1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Fix an odd integer
k
and set
m=(k-1)/2
. Let
n
satisfy

\gcd(n,k)=\gcd(n+1,k)=1
as in Conjecture
1.1
.
By Lemma
2.5
, we have

N_{k}(n)=F_{k}(n+1)-F_{k}(n).

Hence, it follows that

N_{k}(n)\equiv F_{k}(n+1)+F_{k}(n)\pmod{2}.

Exactly one of
n
and
n+1
is even; let
e\in\{n,n+1\}
be the even one
and let
o\in\{n,n+1\}
be the odd one. By assumption,

\gcd(e,k)=\gcd(o,k)=1
, so Lemma
2.4
applies to both
e
and
o
.
Since
o
is odd, Lemma
2.4
gives
F_{k}(o)\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, and therefore

N_{k}(n)\equiv F_{k}(e)\pmod{2}.

Since
e
is even, Lemma
2.4
also gives

F_{k}(e)\equiv\left\lfloor\frac{k+1}{4}\right\rfloor\pmod{2}.

Combining the last two congruences yields

N_{k}(n)\equiv\left\lfloor\frac{k+1}{4}\right\rfloor\pmod{2},

which is exactly Conjecture
1.1
, and Theorem
1.2
holds.
∎

Remark 3
.

After observing the relationship with Jacobi symbols,
we can also provide a more concise description for
n_{k}(\mu)
defined in (
1
).
Let
d_{i}=\gcd(k,m_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
\mu=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})
. Then we have

(6)

n_{k}(\mu)=\#\left\{i\ :\ \Big(\frac{2}{d_{i}}\Big)\neq\Big(\frac{2}{k}\Big)\right\}.

To see this, observe that for each prime
q_{i}
in the factorization of
k
and any integer
m
,

\min\{\nu_{q_{i}}(m),\nu_{q_{i}}(k)\}=\nu_{q_{i}}(\gcd(m,k)).

Therefore, for
d=\gcd(m,k)
, it follows that

\nu_{\mathcal{Q}}(m)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\nu_{q_{i}}(d).

Additionally, since
\Big(\frac{2}{p_{i}}\Big)=1
and
\Big(\frac{2}{q_{i}}\Big)=-1
, we have

\Big(\frac{2}{d}\Big)=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\Big(\frac{2}{q_{i}}\Big)^{\nu_{q_{i}}(d)}=(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{t}\nu_{q_{i}}(d)}=(-1)^{\nu_{\mathcal{Q}}(m)}.

Similarly,

\Big(\frac{2}{k}\Big)=(-1)^{\nu_{\mathcal{Q}}(k)}.

Hence, the condition

\nu_{\mathcal{Q}}(m_{i})\not\equiv\nu_{\mathcal{Q}}(k)\pmod{2}
in the original definition of
n_{k}(\mu)

is equivalent to

\Big(\frac{2}{d_{i}}\Big)\neq\Big(\frac{2}{k}\Big).

Further commentary

We offer further commentary and perspective for these results.

(1)
Scope of Automation.

This test case involved research mathematics where the solution path was not self-contained.
The system identified classical lemmas that enabled the Jacobi-sum reformulation,
and it formally verified the combinatorial identity required to complete the proof.

(2)
Scope of Formalization.

The reader will notice that we formalized the combinatorial core
(i.e. Lemma
2.5
) but not the number theoretic reduction
(i.e. Lemma
2.4
) in Lean.
This decision was driven by the dependence of Lemma
2.4

on Lemma
2.1
(Eisenstein) and Lemma
2.2
(Gauss-Schering).
These two lemmas are already well-established in the literature,
but would take additional work to formalize in Lean.
We chose not to demand that the system reprove these standard facts from scratch,
as the goal of this experiment was to test the discovery of novel proofs, not library building.

(3)
Implications.

We view this test case as a proof-of-principle for a research workflow:
the mathematician poses a conjecture, and the AI can assist with the retrieval,
reformulation, and formal verification of the components.
