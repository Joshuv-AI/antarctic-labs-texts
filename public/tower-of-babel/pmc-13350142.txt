First proctological application of a noncontact nitrous oxide cryotherapy device treating nonmalignant intra- and perianal HPV-related lesions
Authors: H Ruymbeke, J Geldof, T Holvoet, M Surmont, D De Looze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimAnal human papillomavirus (HPV) infection can cause benign and precancerous intra- and perianal lesions. Multiple treatments, including liquid nitrogen cryotherapy, are available with varying efficacy, safety and logistic issues. Little consensus exists considering best clinical practice. This study aims to assess efficacy, safety, and practical applicability of a handheld, noncontact cryotherapy device using nitrous oxide to treat nonmalignant HPV-related intra- and perianal lesions.MethodsA single-center exploratory, open-label proof of concept case series was performed followed by a prospective single-arm interventional study with standardized treatment protocol, conducted at the proctology clinic of the Ghent University Hospital (Belgium). Consecutive patients presenting with benign, intra-, and/or perianal HPV-related lesions were offered noncontact cryotherapy treatment. Primary endpoint was clinical remission, within 4 months of treatment. Secondary endpoints included 1 year recurrence rate and treatment-emergent adverse events. Practical applicability of the device was evaluated and technical properties were adjusted to maximize applicability in everyday ambulatory proctology practice.ResultsIn total, 73 patients were treated. Clinical remission within 4 months was seen in 47/63 (74.6%). No major adverse events were noted and treatment was well tolerated. Compliance to the study protocol follow-up schedule was rather low in this patient population.ConclusionsThis study is the first to describe this noncontact nitrous oxide cryotherapy device for treatment of intra- and perianal nonmalignant HPV-related lesions. Efficacy rates were high and treatment was well tolerated.Keywords: Cryotherapy, HPV, Anal, Anal cancer, Human papillomavirus

Introduction

Currently, there is no gold standard for treatment of nonmalignant intra- and perianal human papillomavirus (HPV)-related lesions. Treatment is directed to the macroscopic or pathologic lesions caused by HPV with the aim of removing all visible lesions, defined as clinical remission [1–4].

Several treatment options are available for HPV-related anogenital disease, with the treatment of benign anogenital warts (condyloma acuminata, CA) being the most widely studied. Different techniques have varying efficacy and safety and many of them are considered first-line treatments. All methods struggle with inadequate remission, tolerance issues, and high recurrence rates [5–7]. Topical patient-administered medical therapies (imiquimod, podophyllotoxin, sinecatechins) are licensed for perianal lesions only. Destructive techniques (cryotherapy, electrocautery, infrared coagulation, carbon dioxide (CO2) laser, etc.) can be performed for both intra- and perianal lesions and are considered more effective, but often have significant side effects, especially pain. Moreover they frequently carry logistic limitations and application often needs local or general anesthesia [7–10].

Cryotherapy using liquid nitrogen (N2) is considered to be an acceptable and safe first-line therapy for CA and it is defined as one of the recommended treatment options for intra-anal lesions by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. However, data on intra-anal HPV-related lesions such as low and high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (LSIL/HSIL) are sparse, treatment options more limited, and little consensus exists in guidance for best clinical practice [7, 10, 11]. Cryotherapy has shown some efficacy for treatment of anal HSIL, a precancerous growth, in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-positive men who have sex with men (MSM) in one recent study [12]. However, data are scarce and standardization of treatment is needed [6, 7].

The aim of this study was to evaluate efficacy, safety, and practical applicability of CryoPen® for cryotherapy treatment of intra- and perianal nonmalignant HPV-related lesions. CryoPen® is a handheld, noncontact cryotherapy device that has proven efficacy in many skin conditions and is generally considered to be safe [13, 14]. It allows for the application of a very precise flow of nitrous oxide (N20) directly on the lesion, suitable for treating sensitive areas [13]. CryoPen® is available throughout Europe, the Medical Device Single Audit Programme (MDSAP) region with the exception of Brazil, and is FDA approved and available in the USA, Canada, and Japan under the name CryoProbe®. This is the first report on the use of CryoPen® in an adapted configuration for a proctological indication.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

First, a proof of concept open-label, pilot case series was performed. Over the course of 1 year (April 2021–April 2022), consecutively presenting adult patients with nonmalignant intra- and perianal HPV-related lesions at the proctology unit of the Ghent University Hospital (Belgium) were offered open-label cryotherapy treatment with the use of CryoPen® (H&O Equipments NV/SA, Ath, Belgium) or standard of care according to internal guidelines (outside of this study). The lesions were diagnosed during proctological examination including anal inspection, digital anorectal examination, anoscopy with the use of a magnifying camera (IMAGE1 S™ camera system, KARL STORZ SE & Co. KG—Tuttlingen) after acetic-acid staining, and were considered nonmalignant on the basis of clinical appearance and/or anal cytology results.

In a second phase, a prospective single-arm interventional study with standardized treatment protocol and follow-up schedule was conducted. Again consecutively presenting adult patients with nonmalignant intra- and perianal HPV-related lesions were invited to participate. Eligible patients were aged ≥ 18 years and diagnosed with anal HPV-related lesions (see above). Patients were excluded if they were pregnant, had been treated for anal HPV-related lesions in the last month, had signs of anal malignancy or severe coagulation disorders, or if they had already been included in the pilot case series. Patients were asked to provide written informed consent and subsequently followed a standardized treatment and follow-up regimen between July 2022 and December 2023. Anal swabs were collected prior to treatment to confirm HPV-related lesion for cytology and HPV genotyping. Study treatment was administered at first visit by a nonblinded proctologist and the therapeutic effect was assessed by a nonblinded proctologist. Data were collected in a standardized way with evaluation of the following outcomes: clinical remission on the short-term (4 months) and recurrence rates on the long-term (1 year), risk factors for treatment failure/recurrence, and safety outcomes.

Given the exploratory nature of this study, no formal sample size calculation was performed. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University hospital of Ghent (reference number ONZ-2022-0106).

Study treatment

CryoPen® is a noncontact, handheld cryotherapy device that applies airflow of N2O gas directly on lesions to freeze them, resulting in cell necrosis. This gas flow reaches subzero temperatures by means of the Thomson-Joule effect, an adiabatic and irreversible process that describes the temperature change of a real gas when expanding. This effect causes the N20 to cool down to its boiling point −88.48 °C [15]. The gas is pressurized to 49–54 bar in N2O cartridges that allow release of a high velocity airflow through a small application catheter, leading to in-depth treatment: a 5 s freeze reaches a depth of 1.5 mm, whereas a 30 s freeze reaches a depth of 4.5 mm.

The specific CryoPen® used in this study was a CryoPen® B+ with small 23 g N2O cartridges at room temperature (Fig. 1). Short (5.3 cm) and long (16 cm) catheters were developed for perianal and intra-anal use, respectively. This handheld device, using N2O instead of N2, avoids initial difficulties of cryotherapy with procuring gas, storing, transporting, and refilling gas tanks. The small internal diameter catheters allow us to accurately apply the cryotherapy to the desired location without freezing healthy tissue surrounding the lesion to reduce pain sensation.

The CryoPen B+® device with replaceable cartridges

In our patient cohort, an office-based cryotherapy session was applied on every visible nonmalignant HPV-related lesion using the CryoPen® B+ device at every patient visit. One treatment session included a freeze on the HPV lesion that may last 5–30 s, followed by a thaw of 30 s and a second freeze. If the patient reports pain, freezing was temporarily stopped and then continued after a thaw period. All patients could be treated properly without need for early termination.

In the pilot case series, treatment intervals were not previously defined. In the prospective cohort follow-up visits were standardized with an interval of 3 weeks. During every follow-up visit, treatment response was assessed and every remaining HPV lesion was treated immediately, until clinical remission was achieved, with a maximum of 6 sessions (= 4 months).

Efficacy endpoints

Treatment response was assessed during follow-up visits with proctological examination including anoscopy with the use of the magnifying camera. In the pilot case series, short-term data were collected on the basis of patients medical records. Clinical remission, meaning the absence of any visible HPV-related lesion, within 4 months after start of treatment or after a maximum of 6 treatment sessions, was the primary endpoint in the prospective cohort (Fig. 2). If remission was not observed by then, it was considered as treatment failure. If patients achieved remission, they were followed every 4 months until 1 year after start of treatment to investigate recurrence rate, defined as having at least one new HPV lesion during follow-up.

Safety endpoints

In the pilot case series treatment-emergent adverse events were collected on the basis of analysis of the medical records. In the prospective cohort patients were asked to complete questionnaires after every treatment session. Adverse events such as anal bleeding, anal discharge, local swelling, and incontinence were systematically investigated with questionnaires. Starting from the day of the procedure (day 1) until 1 week after treatment, daily pain scores using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS, ranging 0–10) were collected. Other new symptoms or clinical findings occurring after treatment were collected and reported as well after every treatment session.

Practical applicability

During the proof-of-concept phase new technical insights were gained and adjustments were made to the prototype device to optimize applicability of CryoPen® specifically for this indication. The device was easy to handle and apply and had a short learning curve. The procedure does not require anesthesia or skin disinfection. To achieve adequate gas flow, the device must be held as vertically as possible with the tip directed downward and the catheter tip as close as possible to the lesion without making physical contact. Simultaneous aspiration of water vapor during treatment ensures adequate visualization. The catheters are single-use and surrounded by a reusable stainless steel shaft. In our experience, the catheter with diameter of 100 µm appeared to be most suitable. A reusable 45° angled PEEK applicator and stainless steel guidance tube housing a 16 cm long PEEK tube with a small internal diameter was developed for intra-anal use, allowing treatment of intra-anal lesions through a conventional anoscope without loss of flow (Fig. 3). This led to the development of a new prototype (Fig. 4).

Data collection and statistical analysis

Patient demographics were collected and in the prospective cohort questionnaires were completed to assess risk factors for treatment failure. Data were collected in a Microsoft Excel® electronic case report form and exported to SPSS version 29 for statistical analyses. Descriptive data are presented for every patient. Categorical variables are shown as frequencies and percentages, continuous variables as median and interquartile ranges (IQR).

Results

Demographics

In total, 73 patients were included, of whom 47 patients participated in the pilot case series. Six of them (12.8%) were lost to follow-up during treatment. More than half of them were immunocompromised. All people living with HIV in this study were on antiretroviral treatment. Intra-anal lesions were present in 78.7%. Anal cytology results were available for 39 patients and showed HSIL in 6 of them (15.4%) (Table 1).

A total of 26 patients were enrolled in the prospective cohort, of whom 4 (15.4%) were lost to follow-up during the treatment phase (within 4 months) and 9 (34.6%) during the 1-year follow-up period. The majority of participants in the prospective cohort were MSM with a history of receptive anal intercourse. More than two-thirds had > 10 previous sexual partners and underwent previous HPV treatment. Half of all patients were immunocompromised. Intra-anal lesions were present in 88.4%. High-risk HPV genotypes were found in the majority of the obtained swabs (Table 1). The lesion size varied significantly between patients.

Efficacy

In the pilot case series, complete clinical remission was observed in 35/41 patients (85.4%), after a median of 2 treatment sessions per patient (IQR 1–4), and 30 patients (73.2%) achieved remission within 4 months of treatment.

In the prospective cohort, complete clinical remission within 4 months or after a maximum of 6 treatment sessions was observed in 17/22 (77.3%) patients after a median of 3 treatment sessions (IQR 2–4). Of these 17 patients, 5 were lost to follow-up during the 1 year follow-up. Half of the 12 patients who completed the 1 year follow-up showed recurrence of visible HPV lesions.

Safety

In the pilot case series, data on safety and adverse events could be collected from 46 patients receiving 149 treatment sessions. In 30/46 patients (65.2%), no adverse events were reported. Mild self-limiting anal discomfort/pain was often reported, and minor anal blood loss or discharge rarely (Table 2). Only one serious hemorrhoidal bleed was reported, requiring local hemostasis with electrocoagulation. None of the reported adverse events led to treatment discontinuation.

For the 26 patients enrolled in the prospective cohort, safety data could be collected on 81 treatment sessions in 25 patients. One treatment session had to be postponed due to a nonhealed laceration after previous treatment and one patient reported transient (< 1 week) nocturnal unintended loss of feces while flatulating after one treatment session. Otherwise, no severe adverse events were reported. Seven patients (28.0%) reported no pain at all (NRS = 0) after any treatment session. Median NRS pain score for the 7 days following treatment was 1 (IQR 0–3) on day 1 and score 0 on days 2–7. Although patients occasionally reported high NRS scores, no one stopped treatment due to intolerance. Self-limiting anal bleeding without the need for intervention was reported after 25/81 (30.9%) treatment sessions in 11 patients. Anal discharge was reported similarly. Mild new anal swelling was frequently reported and other local symptoms such as itching or pressure and difficulty in passing stools were also reported in low rates (< 5%) (Table 2).

Discussion

This is the first study to describe practical application of CryoPen® for treatment of nonmalignant intra- and perianal HPV-related lesions. The device was optimized during the pilot phase, which led to an easy-to-use technique that can be administered during routine proctological consultations. Efficacy rates were high and treatment was well tolerated.

Remission rates within 4 months of treatment in both the pilot case series and prospective cohort were high and comparable in both groups (73.2% and 77.3%). Studies using N2 (instead of N2O) via spray, cryoprobe, or cotton swab show clearance rates for HPV-related anogenital disease in immunocompromised patients ranging between 27% and 89% and recurrence rates between 21% and 39% [5, 6, 16]. In both immunocompetent and immunocompromised patients, cryotherapy using N2 had shown equivalent or superior clearance rates when compared with other treatment options such as podophyllin, trichloroacetic acid, or imiquimod, whereas in immunocompetent patients a systematic review and meta-analysis concluded CO2 laser and electrosurgery appeared slightly less effective than cryotherapy in clearing CA. Previous studies also mentioned that most CA cleared with fewer than three cryotherapy treatment sessions, comparable to our results [6, 7, 17].

As previously stated, treatment with cryotherapy is generally well tolerated without systemic side effects and the local side effects are mostly mild and self-limiting [6]. This is definitively the case for CryoPen®, possibly due to the precise flow applied with this device. A high number of patients in our study did not report any pain. Other treatment-emergent safety outcomes were also reassuring. No one stopped treatment due to adverse events. After 230 treatment sessions, only 1 bleeding occurred that needed intervention by means of coagulation, 1 session had to be postponed due to nonhealed laceration, and 1 patient reported transient mild incontinence. Otherwise, no severe events occurred.

Our population was diverse, including high-risk and low-risk patients for developing anal cancer, with variable lesion localization and size. More than half of all participants were immunocompromised and had intra-anal lesions. Histological data were not available and anal swab results not consistently available, however, the primary focus was on nonmalignant lesions, whether it was typical CA in low-risk patients or preneoplastic lesions in high-risk patients. No anal cancers were reported during 1-year follow-up. Beyond its utility in managing CA and LSIL, cryotherapy may also play a role in treating HSIL and thereby preventing anal cancer in high-risk groups, such as MSM with HIV and solid organ transplant recipients. The ANCHOR study demonstrated that treating HSIL in people living with HIV significantly reduced the incidence of anal cancer. Despite this promising outcome, the optimal treatment modality for HSIL remains uncertain and continues to be a topic of debate and investigation [2]. Siegenbeek van Heukelom was the first to report treatment with cryotherapy (via N2 spray gun) in HSIL in MSM with HIV, with 60% partial or complete treatment response, comparable to success rates of other available HSIL treatment modalities [12]. In this context, CryoPen® emerges as a potentially valuable treatment option. This aligns with the recommendations of the International Anal Neoplasia Society, which emphasize innovative approaches to managing anal cancer risk [18]. Recent international guidelines also recommend the use of high-resolution anoscopy (HRA) in screening for anal cancer precursors in high-risk patients with abnormal primary screening test results [4, 18, 19]. However, as in most other centers, HRA was not available in our center. The use of the magnifying camera tries to overcome the low sensitivity of standard anoscopy for diagnosing HSIL and allows for adequate visualization during treatment as well.

This study has some limitations. This study lacks a control group, which makes it difficult to directly compare efficacy of this treatment to other treatment options. As the study was funded by H&0 Equipments, we cannot exclude funding bias. However, this company was not involved in the design, data collection, nor data processing. Drop-out rates were high during follow-up. The intensive treatment and follow-up regimen might have been an obstacle. In clinical practice and in future research, patients should therefore be informed that multiple treatment sessions are often necessary to achieve remission and encouraged to adhere to follow-up visits. The small sample size also precluded us to identify possible risk factors for treatment failure and recurrence.

Future research should aim for a larger sample with complete data to enhance reliability and validity, comparing CryoPen® with other available (cryotherapy) treatment options.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe the treatment effects of cryotherapy by the use of CryoPen® for this indication. We believe this easy-to-use, handheld device carries numerous potential benefits by applying a very precise flow, suitable for the sensitive anal region and applicable in the anal canal, without the need for anesthesia. As cryotherapy, similar to all provider-administered procedures, is operator-dependent, we aimed to describe, optimize, and standardize practical applicability of CryoPen® for this indication. Treatment sessions with two freeze–thaw cycles at a 3-week interval were very well tolerated. Further research in larger patient groups with randomized controlled and long-term data, including cost-effectiveness data, on treating intra-anal nonmalignant HPV-related lesions, should be encouraged. Additionally, studies incorporating histological analysis, particularly in high-risk groups for anal cancer such as MSM living with HIV, are warranted.
