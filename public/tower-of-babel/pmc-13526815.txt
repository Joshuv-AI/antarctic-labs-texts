East-west divergence and metabolic differentiation in celery domestication
Authors: Chenhao Wang, Mengyi Zhang, Qinglong Xu, Xiangyang Du, Jianeng Che, Kuangtian Xu, Lei Ge, Xiao Li, Zhongyuan Hu, Jinghua Yang, Xinsheng Wu, Mingfang Zhang, Xiaolong Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCelery (Apium graveolens) is a globally cultivated vegetable renowned for its health-promoting and nutraceutical benefits. Exploring its genomic and metabolic diversity can help to facilitate breeding selection. Here, by integrating genomic and metabolomic analyses of 305 globally representative accessions, we reveal pronounced east–west population structure that parallels geographical distribution and varietal differentiation, with three major variety groups showing distinct domestication trajectories. We identify key loci associated with domestication-related traits, including petiole morphology and bolting time. Metabolite profiling uncovers divergence among populations: var. dulce, predominantly consumed raw in Western culinary traditions, exhibits marked reductions in bitter coumarins and alkaloids—a metabolic signature consistent with fresh-eating preferences. Metabolite GWAS map 335,692 loci for 1552 metabolites. We develop genomic prediction models for metabolite abundance to aid breeding. Collectively, our study reveals the genomic and metabolic landscape of celery domestication, and provides hypotheses regarding how cultural practices, alongside regional environmental factors, may have jointly shaped current diversity.Subject terms: Plant breeding, Metabolomics, Agricultural genetics, Evolutionary genetics

Introduction

Celery (Apium graveolens) is valued not only as a widely consumed vegetable but also as a rich source of health-promoting metabolites. Its three major variety groups are distributed across Asia, Europe, North America, and Africa, with distinct morphologies and geographical preferences: var. rapaceum (celeriac), characterized by swollen, fleshy hypocotyl, and strong flavored leaves, is particularly popular in Europe; var. dulce, distinguished by solid and succulent petioles, mild flavor suitable for fresh-eating, is the most common variety cultivated globally; var. secalinum, featuring slender petioles along with pungent, edible petioles and leaves, is primarily cultivated in Southeast Asia1–3.

The geographic distribution differences among these celery varieties may support two independent domestication processes, giving rise to distinct variety groups adapted to different culinary preferences: one in the Western region (var. dulce and var. rapaceum) and another in the Eastern region (var. secalinum). Western culinary traditions emphasize the natural texture and inherent flavors of ingredients, leading to a preference for raw consumption of Western celery, which has become a staple in salad culture; in contrast, Eastern culinary philosophy focuses on the integration and enhancement of complex flavors through cooking techniques such as boiling, which has resulted in Eastern celery becoming the predominant cultivated variety in East and Southeast Asia, with its rich aromatic properties better suited to intricate seasoning requirements1,4. This east‑west divergence in celery may reflect a broader pattern of cuisine‑guided domestication. Archeological evidence points to an early cultural split between East Asia, where pottery enabled boiling and steaming, and West Asia, where ovens promoted roasting and raw consumption5. This material‑cultural divide set the stage for divergent culinary trajectories, as further illustrated by wheat—selected for high gluten content in European bread‑making versus low‑gluten varieties for East Asian steamed dumplings and noodles5,6—and by foxtail millet, where repeated selection for viscous texture occurred in East Asia7. Together, these cases suggest that human culinary preferences could have long acted as evolutionary forces influencing domesticated plants.

Beyond its culinary appeal, celery is also a classic “food-as-medicine” nutraceutical crop, rich in bioactive compounds that bridge the boundary between diet and therapy8,9. Historically, celery seeds were used for diuresis and treating ailments10,11. Today, its diverse nutraceutical ingredients—including flavonoids, phenolic acids, and unsaturated fatty acids—are recognized for their cardiovascular and anticancer benefits. Among them, apigenin, a major flavonoid, has been shown to lower blood pressure and inhibit tumor progression12,13 and inhibit tumor progression14,15, underscoring celery’s importance as both a dietary and medicinal resource.

Celery exhibits global distribution and notable phenotypic diversity. Previous research has revealed that genomic variations have influenced the divergence between celery varieties such as var. dulce and var. rapaceum16, highlighting distinct genomic profiles among different cultivars. However, a comprehensive understanding of celery evolution remains limited due to the lack of a high-quality genome for the economically important East Asian variety (var. secalinum). Although some metabolites have been characterized in var. dulce17,18, much of the celery metabolome—especially across diverse germplasm—remains unexplored. The evolutionary relationships among celery varieties and the mechanisms underlying the differentiation of morphological, flavor, and nutritional traits associated with domestication remain to be elucidated.

Here, we integrate genomic and metabolomic analyses to examine the cultural and biological forces associated with celery diversification. We generate high-quality genomes for var. rapaceum and var. secalinum, construct a genome-wide variation map for 305 accessions, and profile 1552 annotated metabolites. We identify key loci associated with five agronomic traits and 335,692 loci linked to metabolite accumulation. We further develop genomic selection models capable of predicting the relative abundance of 13 metabolite categories, thereby facilitating flavor enhancement and biofortification of nutraceutical ingredients in celery improvement.

Results

Genomic features and divergence of celery varieties

To analyze genomic differences, we first assembled the genomes of var. rapaceum (CY120) and var. secalinum (CY20) using PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) sequencing combined with high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) data (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 1). The final genome assemblies are 3.3 Gb with contig N50 of 232 Mb for var. secalinum, 3.4 Gb with contig N50 of 102 Mb for var. rapaceum (Supplementary Tables 1, 2). The genome sizes of two assemblies were highly comparable to the estimation profiles derived from our k-mer analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1), which provided a robust and sequence-based genome size baseline. The long terminal repeat (LTR)19 assembly index (LAI) of var. secalinum and var. rapaceum are 21.4 and 20.7, reaching the gold level (Supplementary Data 2), demonstrating the high contiguity of the genomes. In addition, the coverage rates of the second-generation reads have reached 99.96% and 99.86% for var. secalinum and var. rapaceum, respectively. The assembly of var. secalinum and var. rapaceum identified about 2.65 Gb and 2.92 Gb repeat sequences, respectively, accounting for 79.89% and 84.36% of the entire genome (Supplementary Tables 3, 4). Protein-coding gene annotation and BUSCO20 evaluation (99.20% for CY20 and 99.10% for CY120) indicated high gene completeness and accuracy (Supplementary Fig. 2). A total of 36,059 and 37,544 protein-coding genes were predicted in the var. secalinum and var. rapaceum, respectively (Supplementary Data 2 and Supplementary Tables 5–8). Collectively, these results demonstrated high-quality genome assemblies for both celery varieties studied.

To investigate the genomic evolution of celery varieties, comparative genomic analysis of three celery varieties using var. rapaceum as reference identified 277,757 and 310,777 InDels (2–49 bp) in var. secalinum and var. dulce, respectively. A total of 21,506 and 18,385 large structural variants (SVs ≥ 50 bp) of five types were identified in the var. secalinum and var. dulce, respectively (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Tables 9–12). This intriguing contrast—characterized by an accumulation of short InDels alongside a reduction in large SVs in var. dulce relative to var. secalinum—potentially reflects distinct evolutionary selection pressures. Large structural variations (≥50 bp) face much stronger purifying selection during intensive directional selection due to their higher likelihood of disrupting critical gene functions or chromosomal stability, leading to their efficient purging in the highly domesticated Western celery (var. dulce). Conversely, short InDels behave as near-neutral variants and persist or accumulate across the genome with fewer evolutionary constraints. A total of 103 identical gene absences occurred within SV intervals of var. secalinum and var. dulce; the enrichment revealed these genes significantly regulate the cell tip growth process and hydrolase activity (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Whole-genome duplications (WGDs) significantly influenced angiosperm genome evolution21. Analysis of collinear homologous genes across three celery varieties revealed distinct Ks (the ratio of the number of substitutions per synonymous site) peaks at ~0.55 and ~1.05, indicating that the celery population underwent the two WGDs events (Supplementary Fig. 4). The peak of the proximal Ks originated from recent tandem repeats22, Interestingly, we found that the tandem repeat genes identified from the three varieties exhibit distinct functional enrichments. Specifically, the tandem repeat genes of var. rapaceum were primarily enriched in protein methylation-related functions, while genes of var. dulce showed strong enrichment for glucosyltransferase activity, and tandem repeat genes of var. secalinum were significantly enriched in multiple protein modifications (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data 3, 4).

Genome-wide polymorphism defines four genetic populations of celery

To explore the genetic variations of celery, we collected 226 celery accessions and 79 published celery resequencing data, forming a diverse global celery genome database (Supplementary Data 5). Approximately 5.2 T of 226 celery accessions resequencing data was obtained, with an average depth of 11.07 ± 0.88×, covered 95.64 ± 1.05% of the var. secalinum reference genome (Supplementary Data 6). Resequencing resulted in 114,536,134 SNPs and 11,868,992 InDels.

We next investigated the genetic structure of celery populations from 2 to 6 clusters (K) based on a 2,023,070 SNPs dataset (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5). Clustering analysis indicated the support for K = 4 populations (Supplementary Fig. 6). In order to better elucidate the intraspecific relationships of celery, principal component analysis (PCA) and phylogenetic reconstruction also support this result (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Based on the above analysis, we identified four celery genetic populations (G1-G4), which largely correspond to morphological and geographical distribution differences. G1 is known to have a main geographical origin from the Mediterranean region. It is characterized by a compact and short plant type, showing the highest nucleotide diversity (π = 2.56 × 10−3) and the smallest LD decay distance (~2.2 Kb, r2 = 0.23, Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). These data indicate that, among the four groups, G1 retains the highest genetic diversity and most closely approximates the ancestral state, although it is not necessarily the direct progenitor of the group G4 (Fig. 2a). The G2 group is represented primarily by var. rapaceum, commonly known as celeriac, which is cultivated mainly in Europe and the Mediterranean region and is characterized by a distinctly swollen hypocotyl. The G3 group largely consists of var. secalinum, traditionally grown in East Asia and distinguished by its slender petioles and pronounced volatile aroma. The G4 group is dominated by var. dulce, which exhibits juicy petioles with a mild flavor, making it suitable for fresh consumption, and is primarily cultivated in Western regions. Based on their geographical distribution, G3 is referred to as Eastern celery, while G2 and G4 are classified as Western celery (Fig. 2d). Genetically, G4 shows the lowest nucleotide diversity (π = 1.33 × 10−3) and the highest LD decay distance (~6.3 Kb, r2 = 0.27), and strong divergence from the other three groups (pairwise Fst > 0.25, Supplementary Fig. 7b, c), reflecting its domestication status.

Domestication and spread of celery

To investigate the demographic history of the celery population, Effective population size (Ne) inference using SMC++23 revealed that four populations experienced a shared bottleneck (Supplementary Fig. 8), followed by recent expansion. We observed that the minimum Ne of the four groups is between 0.8 and 1.6 kilo annum (ka). Carrots24 and Persian walnut25 also experienced a population bottleneck during the same time period. To estimate the timing of more recent demographic separations among the four groups, we analyzed the folded SNP site frequency spectrum. The deepest splits were observed for the G2, G3, and G4 populations compared with the G1, with estimates of divergence ranging from 2000 to 2500 years ago. Subsequent divergence times between G2 and G3/G4 were estimated at 1200–1600 years ago, followed by the divergence time between G3 and G4 about 800–1100 years ago (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 8). These estimated divergence windows partially overlap with key historical records of celery dispersal. The prevailing view holds that celery originated in the Mediterranean region and subsequently spread3. Historical records suggest its introduction into China during the late Eastern Han Dynasty (~1800 years ago) via the Silk Road3. Concurrently, literary evidence indicates that G2 was utilized as a food source in France and Italy by the 16th century, before spreading across Europe3,26. Similarly, G4 is documented to have emerged in Italy around the 16th century and was later introduced from France to South Carolina and Michigan, in approximately the 17th century3.

With the exception of G3, celery accessions from other groups are grown to a notable extent along the Mediterranean coast (Fig. 2c). In contrast, G3 is geographically concentrated in Southeast Asia, particularly with extensive cultivation in China. This stark geographical separation suggests that local adaptation, potentially influenced by divergent climatic conditions, may have played a crucial role in the formation and selection of the G3. The ecological niche modeling projections indicate that during the Late Holocene (LH; 4.2–0.2 ka)27, the optimal niche (suitability > 0.6) for G1 was located along the Mediterranean coast, while that for G3 was in East Asia (Fig. 2d). This geographical and climatic divergence implies that celery domestication was not occurred in an environmental vacuum; rather, historical regional climates, particularly precipitation patterns, established the adaptive baseline for the G3 (Supplementary Fig. 9), upon which subsequent selection may have operated.

GWAS-based identification of genetic loci underlying yield and quality traits

The domestication and selection have influenced morphological changes in cultivated crops28. Relative to G1, which most closely approximates the ancestral state, G2, G3, and G4 exhibit significant morphological alterations. To investigate genes associated with the formation of morphological diversity across different celery populations, we conducted phenotypic observation and statistical analysis on 226 celery accessions over two consecutive years (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Data 7). Subsequently, we performed a GWAS to analyze associations with key agronomic traits (Supplementary Data 8). These traits included 4 yield-related components (petiole expansion, solid vs. hollow petioles, bolting time, and presence/absence of hypocotyl enlargement).

The petiole is one of the edible parts of celery, especially var. dulce. Phenotypic analysis confirms that Western celery (G4) exhibits significantly thicker and heavier petioles—traits desirable for fresh consumption (Supplementary Fig. 10). The expansion of petioles will increase their weight, which directly determines celery yield, making their increase a key breeding objective. One significant signal was associated strongly with petiole expansion on Chr 10 by GWAS (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 8), which contained a nitrate transporter gene, AgNPF (Ags10G01523). NPF is widely involved in the absorption and utilization of nitrogen by plants, promoting plant growth and thereby increasing yield29. A nonsynonymous SNP in its exon (G>A) resulted in an aspartic acid to asparagine substitution. Petiole expansion in G/G genotype (mean 121.21 g) is significantly lower than in A/A genotype (mean 142.65 g, Fig. 3b). Approximately 56.1% (n = 57) of G4 accessions carry the A/A genotype, correlating with higher petiole weight (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Data 7). Furthermore, Fst analysis of G1 vs. G4 and G1 vs. G3 revealed stronger differentiation at the AgNPF locus in the Western celery (G4) than in the Eastern celery (G3) (Supplementary Fig. 11). Because G1 is not a direct progenitor of G4 (Fig. 2a), this differentiation cannot be attributed solely to domestication-driven selection, but it is consistent with selection for thicker petioles during the improvement of fresh-eating Western celery (G4).

Another important agronomic trait in celery breeding is delayed bolting. Once vigorous reproductive growth begins, the plant prioritizes nutrient allocation to fruits and seeds, leading to an almost complete halt in vegetative growth24. Consequently, breeding delayed bolting varieties is a critical strategy for enhancing celery yield. Using GWAS, we discovered a gene involved in encoding a polycomb group protein AgSWN (Ags04G01450) in the Chr 4 signal region (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Data 8), which plays a key role in establishing FLC repression during vernalization in Arabidopsis30,31. A nonsynonymous SNP (A>T) exists in its exon, bolting time in the A/A genotype (mean 163 days) is significantly lower than in the T/T genotype (mean 187 days) (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Data 7). In addition, phenotypic analysis revealed that the G4 bolting later than other populations. This is corroborated by its genotype, in which approximately 89.5% (n = 57) of G4 individuals carry the late-bolting T/T genotype (Fig. 3f). Fst analysis between G1 vs. G4 and G1 vs. G3 revealed that the AgWSN locus exhibited stronger differentiation signals in Western celery (G4) than in Eastern celery (G3) (Supplementary Fig. 11). Because G1 is not a direct progenitor of G4 (Fig. 2a), these differences cannot be attributed solely to domestication-driven selection; nevertheless, they are consistent with stronger differentiation at bolting-related loci in Western celery (G4), in line with selection to prolong vegetative growth and promote leaf-stalk thickening. Moreover, Fst analysis of G2 vs. G4—two groups with pronounced differences in bolting time—identified multiple genes related to bolting within the differentiated regions (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Data 9).

In celery, hollowing compromises yield and commercial value severely. Previous study has indicated that the hollow stem trait in celery is associated with the presence/absence (PAV) of the NAC on Chr 4, where the presence of NAC is associated with the hollow phenotype and its absence with the solid phenotype16. Our GWAS analysis for this trait also detected this signal on Chr 4, along with an additional signal on Chr 5 (Supplementary Fig. 12a and Supplementary Data 8). Interestingly, we identified exactly 11 “exceptional” individuals (CY18, CY19, CY20, CY21, CY66, CY82, CY89, CY96, CY194, CY263, and CY264) that exhibited a clear hollow phenotype despite completely lacking this Chr 4 NAC locus (Supplementary Fig. 12b and Supplementary Data 7). A retrospective curation of public raw data32 also confirmed similar phenotypic discrepancies in traditional accessions (e.g., R18Q227 and R18Q243) (Supplementary Fig. 12b), ruling out potential phenotypic scoring artifacts.

To resolve the genetic basis supporting these exceptional lines under the classic 3:1 Mendelian segregation framework32, we performed a subpopulation conditional GWAS exclusively on the individuals lacking the Chr 4 NAC locus (13 hollow vs. 104 solid individuals). Upon removing the confounding effect of the major NAC locus, the initially observed peak on Chr 5 completely faded, and only a few scattered, minor-effect genomic signals emerged (Supplementary Fig. 12c and Supplementary Data 8). This reveals that the Chr 5 signal in the global population was a false-positive artifact driven by population substructure. These results suggest that the hollowing trait in these exceptional lines is likely governed by independent novel mutations or subtle modifier loci. Future mapping utilizing larger, specialized segregation populations derived from these specific lines will be essential to precisely capture these minor genetic influences.

Apart from the petioles, celery also has the enlarged hypocotyl represented by G2 as its edible part. We identified a GWAS signal located approximately 1.61 Mb in Chr 8 that is strongly associated with hypocotyl enlargement (Supplementary Fig. 13a and Supplementary Data 8), and this interval overlaps with the selection intervals of G2 and G4 (Supplementary Fig. 13). A SNP (C>A) located upstream of Ags08G00056 encoding the Auxin-binding protein is significantly associated with hypocotyl enlargement. The nucleotide polymorphism of the AgABP region in G2 and G4 also exhibited differences (Supplementary Fig. 13c). Further analysis of the promoter sequence revealed that a 13-bp deletion was present in approximately 74% of non-enlarged hypocotyl individuals, whereas this deletion was absent in approximately 75% of the enlarged group (Supplementary Fig. 13d). The transcriptome data indicate that this gene is expressed only in the enlarged individuals (Supplementary Fig. 13e). Therefore, we propose that AgABP is a candidate gene involved in hypocotyl enlargement.

Additionally, petiole color variation (green, white, and purple) influences consumer preference. Through comparative GWAS analyses of three contrasting groups (white vs. non-white, green vs. non-green, and purple vs. non-purple), an overlapping quantitative trait locus (QTL) interval spanning on Chr 4 was identified (Supplementary Data 8). This interval exhibits a significant association with petiole color variation.

Metabolomic panorama discloses the chemical basis of population divergence

The differences among the celery population are not only reflected in morphology, but also in metabolic flavor. Usually, G4 is characterized by crisp and juicy with a milder taste, while G3 has a strong taste with some bitterness. The content of flavor compounds such as flavonoids in celery leaves is higher than that in petioles and roots18,33. To comprehensively explore natural metabolic variation across the celery population, we performed extensive targeted metabolomics technology detection34 using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) on mature leaves from 203 germplasms (G1 = 49, G2 = 36, G3 = 47, G4 = 71), quantifying the relative metabolite levels. We identified 1552 annotated and non-redundant metabolites spanning diverse categories (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data 10). These annotated metabolites comprised 171 amino acids and derivatives, 270 flavonoids, 167 alkaloids, 203 phenolic acids, 118 lignans and coumarins, 74 organic acids, 150 lipids, 62 nucleotides and derivatives, 83 terpenoids, 21 quinones, 2 steroids, 5 tannins, and 226 other metabolites. Approximately 69.3% of the metabolites had a coefficient of variation (CV) greater than 50%, indicating abundant variation in metabolites of the celery population (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Data 10).

To further compare the differences in metabolic composition among the celery populations, we performed PCA on the metabolite dataset. Metabolic composition analysis revealed that the celery subgroups formed three distinct clusters, indicating significant metabolic differentiation among them (Fig. 4c). Subsequently, we used Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) to identify differential metabolites among the four populations (Supplementary Data 11). G4 exhibited the most pronounced differences compared to the other groups. Compared to G1, G4 showed the highest number of differential metabolites, with 354 up-regulated and 120 down-regulated metabolites (Fig. 4d). The up-regulated metabolites were primarily enriched in phenolic acids, flavonoids, and lipids, while the down-regulated metabolites were enriched in coumarins and alkaloids (Supplementary Fig. 14). Approximately 70% of differential alkaloids and 69% of coumarins were reduced in Western celery (G4) relative to the G1, whereas only about 30% alkaloids show reduction in Eastern celery (G3). These results indicate that the alkaloid content in Western celery (G4) is substantially lower than that in other celery groups. The downregulation of bitter-tasting compounds, alkaloids, is consistent with the reduced bitterness and improved palatability expected in the fresh-eating varieties. The differential metabolites were partitioned into several modules in G1 and G4. Phenolic acids, flavonoids, and lipids constituted three main clusters (Fig. 4e). Members within these clusters showed correlated expression patterns. Interestingly, approximately 54.2% of detected phenolic acid showed differential abundance across four groups, PCA results further suggested these compounds may serve as characteristic biomarkers for distinguishing celery populations (Supplementary Fig. 15). The pattern of increased flavonoids and decreased coumarin and alkaloid in the G4 resembles the differential patterns reported during domestication in other crops such as potato35, citrus36, and maize37; such comparisons indicate that these metabolites may carry domestication-associated signatures in celery, although they reflect plausibility rather than causality in celery.

Population divergence in key flavor-related metabolic pathways

Based on the significant metabolic differences among different celery varieties, this study focuses on the variations in metabolite content within the biosynthesis pathways of flavonoids, phenolic acids, and coumarins, as well as the genomic imprints of metabolic selection. These three classes of compounds are all derived from the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. L-phenylalanine is converted into cinnamic acid under the catalysis of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL). Subsequently, trans-cinnamic acid is transformed into p-coumaric acid and caffeic acid by cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H) and p-coumarate 3-hydroxylase (C3H, CYP98A), respectively. The contents of these two metabolites were higher in the G3 and G4 populations (Fig. 5a). Further modifications by methyltransferases and hydroxylases lead to the formation of new phenolic acid compounds. Genes associated with phenolic acid biosynthesis were identified through selective sweep analyses in G1 vs. G3 and G1 vs. G4 (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Data 12). These genes, along with their upstream 2 kb regions, exhibited variations in nucleotide diversity, and expression levels of key genes were significantly different (Fig. 5c). Cinnamoyl-CoA serves as the precursor for flavonoid synthesis, where it is converted into naringenin by chalcone synthase (CHS) and chalcone isomerase (CHI), entering the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway. The relative contents of the initial flavonoid precursors—apigenin, eriodictyol, and hesperetin—showed significant differences among the different populations (Fig. 5a). Similarly, key genes involved in the synthesis of these compounds were also identified in the selective sweep analyses of G1 vs. G3 and G1 vs. G4, with significant differences observed in both nucleotide polymorphism and gene expression levels (Fig. 5d).

Celery is a significant natural source of furanocoumarins. Furanocoumarins are photoactivated secondary metabolites known to cause dermatitis upon skin contact36. In the established coumarin biosynthesis pathway, umbelliferon serves as the initial structure, which is subsequently converted into the fundamental furanocoumarin skeleton by the enzyme 6’-prenyltransferase (6’PT). The relative contents of these two metabolites varied significantly among the four celery populations examined, with the highest levels observed in G1 and the lowest in G4 (Supplementary Fig. 16a). The 6’PT gene in the G4 population exhibited low nucleotide diversity and reduced expression levels compared to other groups (Supplementary Fig. 16b).

The patterns of metabolite abundance, together with the differentiation signals detected in biosynthetic genes, suggest that the flavonoid, phenolic acid, and coumarin pathways may have been targets of selection during celery diversification. The reduced coumarin content and downregulation of biosynthetic genes in G4 relative to G1 reflect a coordinated shift in phenotype and transcription; although G1 is not a direct progenitor, this shift is consistent with selection for improved palatability during the improvement of Western celery, rather than being attributable solely to domestication.

Metabolite GWAS identifies genetic loci for key nutraceutical metabolites

To explore the genetic variation of metabolic traits in celery leaves, we conducted a GWAS analysis on 1552 detected metabolic contents using 34,004,698 SNPs by a linear mixed model. A total of 335,692 non-redundant significant genetic loci were detected from 1552 metabolites, with an average of 216 significant loci per metabolite (Fig. 6a). We identified 322 potential metabolism-associated hotspots (significant signal number > 126 within the interval), and constructed a metabolism-gene association network to integrate all hotspot information (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Data 13). Among these 322 hotspots, 195 were identified as potential association hotspots for individual metabolite categories (hotspots enriched exclusively within specific metabolic classes). The hotspot (Hot136) on Chr 7 is associated with the accumulation of the terpenoid compound (quinovic acid). This interval contains the cytochrome P450 gene Ags07G03131 (Fig. 6c). Multiple SNPs in its sequence showed significant associations with variation in quinovic acid content (Fig. 6d). Given the well-established role of CYP450 enzymes in the biosynthesis and modification of terpenoid compounds38, Ags07G03131 is proposed as a potential candidate gene regulating the natural variation of quinovic acid. Conversely, 127 were identified as potential association hotspots for multiple metabolites, suggesting their involvement in the regulation of different metabolites. Two hotspots (Hot241, Hot242) located in Chr 11 are significantly associated with four types of metabolites: flavonoids, terpenoids, coumarins, and lignans. Interestingly, within this hotspot range, we identified a gene cluster involved in BAHD acyltransferase (Ags11G00931), oxidoreductase (Ags11G00935, Ags11G00937), and dehydrogenase (Ags11G00940) (Fig. 6a). Acyl transfer by the gene cluster affected acylation modifications of four metabolite classes39. Additionally, this study identified QTLs involved in the regulation of key metabolites. For example, Two independent QTLs, located on Chr 9 and 10, were found to be associated with the alkaloid biosynthesis pathway, were involved in regulating the accumulation of caffeine (Supplementary Fig. 17a). QTLs significantly associated with the content of the coumarin compound (5,8-dihydroxypsoralen) and phenolic acid compounds (4-hydroxybenzoic acid, 4-O-p-coumaroylquinic acid, 3-(3-hydroxyphenyl)-propionic acid) were identified (Supplementary Fig. 17b, c and Supplementary Data 14). These findings provide important clues for elucidating the genetic regulatory mechanisms of these metabolites.

In the traditional celery breeding process, breeders prioritize observable morphological traits but lack direct assessment of nutritional and flavor-related characteristics. The detection of these contents is time-consuming and laborious, which limits the breeding of nutrient-rich celery. To achieve precise breeding of celery and promote the breeding of flavor and nutrition content traits that are difficult to detect, we constructed genomic selection (GS) models for 13 categories (Supplementary Data 10) of metabolites in celery, providing a reference for the future selection and breeding of flavor and nutrient traits in celery. To evaluate the accuracy of GS prediction, we adopted three different methods to understand the predictive ability of metabolites and applicability of the models: Ridge regression for the best linear unbiased prediction (rrBLUP)40, Bayesian Generalized Linear Regression (BGLR)41, and Support Vector Regression (SVR) machine learning methods42. Besides, in order to reduce the error caused by the selection of the training dataset, tenfold cross-validation was adopted. The results show that the rrBLUP model has better accuracy (Supplementary Table 13). The prediction accuracy for all 13 metabolite categories reached above 0.5 (Fig. 6e), demonstrating the validity of metabolome data and the high predictive ability of the model. The prediction accuracy of lipids reached the highest level, and its predicted values showed a strong correlation with the observed values (Fig. 6f). The predicted values of metabolites in the remaining categories also showed a good correlation with the observed values (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Our mGWAS results provide a comprehensive explanation of the significant associated hotspots in celery metabolism. A gene cluster that plays a significant role in multiple metabolites synthesis pathways has been identified. Besides, the GS model constructed based on the metabolomics data of this study can effectively analyze the common metabolic substances related to the breeding of celery. These results help to elucidate the metabolic network of celery flavor and provide a reference for exploring its nutritional and medicinal values.

Flavonoids are abundant among the numerous metabolites detected and are important physiologically active components of celery. Flavonoids have C6-C3-C6 as the basic skeleton, and the phenylpropane metabolic pathway is the key pathway for the synthesis of flavonoids36,43. Among all detected flavonoid metabolites, approximately 36.7% flavonoid showed differences among the four populations. XP-CLR analysis revealed key biosynthetic genes in the phenylpropanoid pathway under selection: FLS (flavonol synthase, which catalyzes flavonol production) and F3H (flavanone 3-hydroxylase, catalytic generation of dihydroflavonol), FNSI (involved in the conversion of naringin to apigenin) (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Data 12).

Apigenin is a typical flavonoid compound and a key starting skeleton for flavonoids, flavanols, and other compounds. Its bioactive properties include contributing to vasodilation and blood pressure reduction12,44. Significant variation in apigenin content was observed among the four celery groups (Supplementary Fig. 19a). Nucleotide diversity analysis revealed greater conservation in the AgFNSI promoter region (2 kb upstream) in G3 and G4 compared to G1 and G2 (Supplementary Fig. 19b). Two haplotypes showed significant association with apigenin accumulation, with differential AgFNSI expression detected between haplotypes (Supplementary Fig. 19c, d). Subsequent dual-luciferase assays confirmed higher promoter activity for hap2 relative to hap1 (Supplementary Fig. 19e). Collectively, the cumulative difference in apigenin accumulation in celery is regulated by differences in AgFNSI promoter activity.

In addition, we identified a GWAS signal on Chr 10 significantly associated with the accumulation of homoeriodictyol, a flavonoid compound that has the function of masking bitterness45. This genomic interval contains a tandem repeat cluster comprising three O-methyltransferase genes (Fig. 7a): AgOMT1 (Ags09G03431), AgOMT2 (Ags09G03430), and AgOMT3 (Ags09G03429). There are two haplotypes in the AgOMT1 promoter region that are significantly associated with homoeriodictyol content. The relative content of homoeriodictyol in hap1 was significantly lower than that in hap2 (Fig. 7b). Hap2 exhibited characteristics of domestication selection in the four populations, with the highest frequency in G4 (Supplementary Fig. 20). RT-qPCR of three OMT in two different haplotypes showed that AgOMT3 was not expressed in both haplotypes, while the expression level of AgOMT1 was higher than AgOMT2, and the expression level of hap1 was lower than that of hap2 (Fig. 7c). To verify the functions of two OMT genes, in vitro enzyme assay of the recombinant proteins encoded by AgOMT1 and AgOMT2 were conducted, and it was demonstrated that both of these recombinant proteins could catalyze the production, homoeriodictyol, when eriodictyol was used as the substrate (Fig. 7d). To further explore the differences in the accumulation of homoeriodictyol, the promoter activity analysis of two haplotype in tobacco provided further evidence that hap2 had higher activity than hap1 (Fig. 7e). These results identified the key gene OMT for the synthesis of homoeriodictyol in celery, and the cumulative difference in homoeriodictyol is caused by the difference in the activity of the OMT promoter.

Discussion

The complementary transition of wild plants into diversified cultivars represents a multi-dimensional evolutionary process orchestrated by both ecological constraints and anthropogenic dynamics46,47. While classical crop domestication models often emphasize primary selected signals associated with yield or simple morphological changes, our evolutionary, genomic, and metabolomic dissection of celery varieties reveals a far more nuanced evolutionary canvas (Fig. 8). The recent domestication may not have relied solely on de novo mutations, but rather on the directional selection and rapid fixation of polymorphisms already present in ancestral populations48. This sorting of ancestral variation during recent history was not driven by human selection in an environmental vacuum49. Our ecological niche modeling demonstrates that during the Late Holocene, historical regional climates, particularly regional precipitation gradients, had established an adaptive baseline for the Eastern celery (G3).

Summary of agronomic and metabolic trait differences among celery populations.

Celery was first cultivated and domesticated in the Mediterranean region, where two cultivated varieties, var. rapaceum and var. dulce, were gradually developed. Subsequently, its ancestral population spread eastward along the Silk Road and underwent independent domestication in East Asia, resulting in the var. secalinum. Node time represents the inferred differentiation time of the celery population (ya: years ago). Morphological and metabolic divergence: (1) Yield-related traits: candidate genes underlying two key traits, bolting time and petiole expansion, have been identified. (2) Flavor evolution: flavor attenuation, particularly reduced bitterness, characterizes var. dulce. This phenotype correlates with a metabolic shift: decreased alkaloids and coumarins, and increased phenolic acids and lipids. (3) Genetic mechanism of bitterness reduction: elevated accumulation of the “bitter blocker” homoeriodictyol contributes to perceived bitterness reduction in var. dulce. Subsequent genetic analyses elucidate the regulatory mechanisms controlling its biosynthesis and accumulation.

It is inherently challenging to disentangle such deep ancestral natural selection from recent anthropogenic preferences, especially in the absence of a direct, monophyletic extant wild progenitor46. Importantly, given the currently available samples, the group G1 and Western celery (G4) are not subject to strict phylogenetic control; G1 may not be the direct monophyletic ancestor of G4, and profound ancestral divergence may exist between them (Fig. 2a). Nevertheless, while these genetic differences cannot be wholly attributed to domestication, the observed selection signals are suggestive of domestication‑associated processes acting on pre‑existing variation. Natural selection typically favors the maintenance or upregulation of chemical defense compounds, such as bitter coumarins and volatile alkaloids, to maximize ecological fitness and deter herbivores in natural habitats50. In stark contrast, our metabolomic data reveal a target-specific, coordinated reduction of these precise defense pathways exclusively in the fresh consumed Western celery (G4).

This drastic deviation from the natural state may be related to different anthropogenic preferences influenced by Eastern and Western culinary cultures. Western salad-oriented culinary cultures prize “raw” or lightly treated vegetables that preserve their “natural taste”51,52. Because volatile and bitter compounds are preserved when consumed raw, Western breeding efforts faced immense selective pressure to sacrifice vital survival compounds in favor of human palatability—a definitive hallmark of the “domestication syndrome” actively overriding wild constraints50. Conversely, Eastern cuisines traditionally emphasize “fully cooked” and “deep flavor absorption,” where bitter compounds like alkaloids easily degrade under high-temperature cooking, rendering intensive selection against bitterness less urgent. This parallels the well-documented adaptive selection in major staples, where European wheat was favored for its high gluten content tailored for baking, while East Asian introductions favored low-gluten variants adapted to traditional steamed foods5,53. Consistent with this culinary divide, our metabolomic analysis revealed substantially lower alkaloid levels in Western celery than in Eastern celery, likely because alkaloids impart bitterness that poses a barrier to fresh consumption54,55, whereas they are largely degraded by boiling in Eastern cooking practices56,57. Additionally, AgOMT, a gene involved in the synthesis of the bitterness‑masking compound homoeriodictyol45, shows a significantly higher frequency of the elevated‑expression haplotype in Western celery (G4) than in other groups (Supplementary Fig. 20). Beyond these metabolic adaptations, selection also targeted morphological traits: genes regulating petiole expansion (AgNPF) and delaying bolting (AgSWN) exhibit pronounced artificial selection signals in Western celery, confirming intensive selection pressure for petiole hypertrophy and yield (Supplementary Fig. 11). Together, these findings are consistent with the role of cultural preferences in guiding the fixation of specific genetic variations, influencing varieties adapted to distinct culinary demands.

In addition to this broad east–west cultural divide, human selection also operated on a much finer spatial scale within the same cultural domains. This micro-spatial diversification is most strikingly evidenced within the Mediterranean region, where Western celery (G4, selected for succulent petioles) and celeriac (G2, selected for expanded hypocotyls) emerged within overlapping geographical boundaries. Their divergent evolutionary trajectories cannot be simplistically explained by macro-cultural contrasts. Instead, this co-occurrence illustrates the capacity of localized human demands to drive multi-directional crop morphology through “organ-specific selection.” Much like the intra-cultural diversification observed in Brassica oleracea58,59, which yielded cabbages, cauliflowers, and kohlrabi from a shared genetic domain through tissue-specific remodeling, distinct market demands within the same broader society drove targeted directional selection on celery tissues, effectively partitioning the species into highly specialized morphotypes.

Beyond texture and form, the “food-as-medicine” philosophy linking diet, health, and environment is deeply embedded within celery’s evolutionary history. Originating as a wild medicinal herb—frequently placed in the tombs of ancient Egyptian pharaohs—celery’s domestication trajectory mirrored the dual human pursuit of both nourishment and therapy3. Our metabolomic data reveal extensive remodeling of biosynthetic pathways for flavonoids and phenolic acids (Fig. 5)—compounds with well-documented antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. AgFNSI, a key gene in the biosynthesis of apigenin—a major antihypertensive compound abundant in Apiaceae—exhibits strong differentiation signals in G4, with its high-expression haplotype being more frequent in this cultivated group, suggesting that metabolic remodeling may be linked to the maintenance of celery’s medicinal value.

In summary, our work filled the gap in high-quality genomic information for the var. secalinum of celery, identified new loci associated with bolting time and petiole enlargement, and constructed a systematic metabolomic atlas. The identified loci for yield, flavor, and nutraceutical metabolites offer a genomic framework for precision breeding, paving the way for future celery varieties that unite sensory appeal with nutritional value. When contextualized within a broader cultural framework, our findings offer insights into how culinary preferences may have potentially influenced plant genomes over time.

Methods

Plant materials

A total of 305 samples were collected (156 from the National Germplasm Resources Administration of the United States, 45 from Ningbo Weimeng Germplasm Company, 25 from agricultural markets, and 79 published reference sequencing files) (Supplementary Data 5). We cultivated a collection of 226 samples in Henan and Zhejiang provinces, China, in 2022 and 2023. A total of 203 celery samples (G1 = 48, G2 = 37, G3 = 47, G4 = 71) were used for metabolite detection. Celery seedlings were planted at the fifth fully expanded leaf stage. Phenotypic assessments were conducted about 120 days after planting (DAP); petiole weight, color, hollow/solid, hypocotyl expansion, and sample collection for metabolomic analysis were performed concurrently. Bolting time was recorded as the number of days from planting to visible flower stalk emergence. For each line, leaves and petioles from three individual plants were sampled and stored at −80 °C.

Genome sequencing and assembly

Fresh young leaves of var. secalinum and var. rapaceum (CY20 and CY120) were extracted for total DNA using the standard cetyltrimethylammonium bromide method60, and the quality and quantity of DNA were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and Qubit fluorescence meter, respectively. Fresh roots, petioles, and leaves were extracted for RNA using Trizol reagent, and an RNA seq library was constructed.

We obtained 88.9 Gb (~30×) and 95.0 G (~31×) data on the PacBio Sequel II platform, 371.4 Gb (~112×) and 324.4 Gb (~99×) paired-end sequencing data, 394.9 Gb (~120×) and 333.8 Gb (~100×) Hi-C sequencing data, and 21.5 Gb and 20.8 Gb transcriptome data on Illumina NovaSeq 6000, respectively (Supplementary Data 1). In order to obtain genome size, heterozygosity, and repeat content, Illumina short reads used Jellyfish (v2.2.0)61 generate k-mer frequency distribution, and then, based on the k-mer frequency distribution data and species ploidy, accurate genomic feature evaluation results were obtained using GenomeScope2.062.

Sequencing of genomic DNA was performed on two celeries using the Sequel II system (PacBio) in HiFi mode. The offline Subreads were analyzed using the CCS module of SMRT-link (v9.0.0.92188) to generate high-quality CCS sequences for subsequent genome assembly. High-quality CCS sequences assembled two celery samples from scratch using hifiasam (v0.2.0)63 default parameters. Hi-C data was first mapped to the genome assembly using Juicer (v1.6)64 with default parameter, next the chromosome-level assembly was generated using 3D-DNA (v180922)65. To check quality and improve chromosome-scale assembly, juicebox (v1.6)66 assembly tools were used to manually review and improve candidate assemblies.

We used three methods to evaluate the assembly quality of the genome. First, based on the eudicots_odb10 database, BUSCO with default parameters was used to evaluate the assembly and genomic patterns of two celery genomes. The Complete BUSCO genes of the two celery genomes were 1600 (99.1%) and 1601 (99.2%), respectively (Supplementary Data 2). Second, Illumina short reads were mapped to the assembled genome by using bwa (v0.7.9a)67, and the accuracy of genome assembly was further evaluated. The average depth and alignment rate of reads covering the two celery genomes were 111.84x and 93.59x, 99.96% and 99.86%, respectively. Finally, the quality of genome assembly was evaluated using the LTR assembly index (LAI). The LAI indices of the two genomes, var. secalinum and var. rapaceum (Supplementary Data 2), both reached the gold level (21.36, 20.71).

Genome annotation

Duplicate sequences were identified in two celery genomes by combining homologous annotation and de novo annotation methods. Using RepeatMasker and RepeatProteinMask68 against the RepBase database69 of repetitive sequences, we predicted sequences that are similar to known repeats. In order to process de novo annotation, first use RepeatModeler70 to build De Novo repeat sequence library and use RepeatMasker for prediction. The results showed that the two celery genomes had 79.89% and 84.36% of repetitive sequences, respectively.

The protein-coding genes of two celery genomes were identified by integrating de novo prediction, homology-based prediction, and annotation strategies based on transcriptional evidence. Use Augustus (v3.3.2)71, GlimmerHMM (v3.0.4)72, and SNAP73 to de novo identify gene structures in genome sequences. To conduct homology based on gene prediction, blastp74 (E value cutoff of 1e-5 and a minimum query coverage of 25%) was used to search for protein sequences of five published genomes (A. thaliana, D. carota, C. sativum, A. graveolens var. dulce, O. sativa). GeneWise (v2.4.1)75 was used to identify protein-coding regions based on sequence alignment. Perform de novo assembly of RNA seq data using Trinity (v2.10.0)76, and then use PASApipe (v2.4.1)77 to predict gene models based on transcriptional alignment. Based on the above prediction results, use GETA to integrate the gene sets predicted by various strategies into a non-redundant and more complete gene set.

Whole-genome synteny and assessment of WGD

To compare whole-genome synteny across three genomes (including the A. graveolens var. dulce genome from Song et al.78), we performed pairwise genome alignments using minimap2 (v2.28-r1209, https://github.com/lh3/minimap2)79. Structural variants (SVs ≥ 50 bp) and small variants (SNPs and InDels ranging from 2 to 49 bp) were subsequently identified using SyRI (v1.7.1, https://github.com/schneebergerlab/syri)80. The genomic locations of the identified SNPs and InDels were annotated with ANNOVAR81.

Mcscanx82 was applied to identify homologous blocks. The homologous blocks and dot plots at the chromosome scale of the three celery varieties were constructed using the Python version of Mcscanx (https://github.com/tanghaibao/jcvi/wiki/). Synonymous substitution rate (Ks) distribution was used to predict WGD events in species. WGDI83 was used to calculate the Ks density of paralogous and homologous gene pairs, and each Ks distribution peak represented a duplication event, and the curve was multi-peak-fitted by Gaussian approximation function. To further determine the pattern of duplication events, DupGen_finder (v1.12)84 (taking A. sinensis85 as an outgroup of three celery genomes) was used to analyze five different types of duplication genes, and the results of strict patterns were selected for subsequent enrichment analysis. Eggnog-mapper (v2.1.10, https://github.com/jhcepas/eggnog-mapper/)86 was used as the gene function annotation of the three celery protein sequences, and the GO and KEGG annotation results were used as the R package AnnotationForge input file to construct the annotation Library (org.db) of the three celery genomes. The R packages enrichplot (v1.20.3) and clusterprofiler (v4.8.3)87 were used for enrichment analysis of genes of different duplication types.

Variant calling, validation, and annotation

FASTP (v0.23.4)88 was used to filter 305 raw sequencing data of celery, remove the linker information and sequences with base Phred quality lower than 20, obtain clean data, and then the clean data was mapped back to the var. secalinum reference genome by bwa-mem289 with default parameters. The haplotypecaller pattern and joint calling method were used to detect population SNPs and indels using GATK. According to the suggestions of GATK, various parameters for filtering the original SNPs and indels were determined. The expression of SNP filtering was set as “QD < 2.0, QUAL < 30.0, SOR > 3.0, FS > 60.0, MQ < 40.0, MQRankSum < −10.0, ReadPosRankSum < −8.0”, and the expression of indel filtering was set as “QD < 2.0, QUAL < 30.0, SOR > 3.0, FS > 100.0, MQRankSum < −10.0, ReadPosRankSum < −8.0”, the number of SNPs and short INDELs after the initial filtering step was 114,536,134 and 11,868,992, respectively. The SNPs and INDELs were annotated using ANNOVAR81 and the existing genome annotation files.

Population structure and phylogenetic analyses

The 34,004,698 core SNPs set (--maf 0.05 --geno 0.2) was selected using Plink (v1.90). We used ADMIXTURE (v1.23)90 to infer population genetic structure. Prior to analysis, SNPs in high linkage disequilibrium (LD) were pruned by removing variants with an r² > 0.2 within 50-kb windows sliding in 10-kb steps, resulting in a final set of 2,023,070 SNPs for the analysis. The selection of K was determined using 5-fold cross-validation (CV), with K values (the number of assumed populations) ranging from 2 to 6, take K = 4 as the best value is based on two observations: (1) Statistical evidence: While the cross-validation error continued to decrease slightly from K = 4 to K = 5, the rate of this decrease slowed considerably, indicating that the marginal improvement in model fit had begun to plateau. This suggests that the K = 4 model captures the major genetic clusters effectively while maintaining lower complexity, which is often preferred to avoid over-interpretation of subtle substructure. (2) Biological coherence: More importantly, the population structure inferred under K = 4 demonstrates a stronger and more interpretable alignment with the topology of our phylogenetic tree. Principal component analysis was performed on 305 samples by Plink (v1.90). The PCA results were visualized using the ggolot2 R package.

Phylogenetic trees were constructed using fourfold degenerate synonymous sites in SNPs. First, SnpEff91 (https://pcingola.github.io/SnpEff/) was used to annotate the core SNP set, and then to extract the fourfold degenerate synonymous sites and transformed into Phy format files. Finally, iqtree2 (with parameter “-m TVM+F+ASC+R8 -st DNA -bb 1000 -bnni -alrt 1000”)92 was used to construct a phylogenetic tree, carrot as an outgroup. The tree was visualized using the ITOL.

Genetic diversity, Fst and LD analysis

The nucleotide polymorphism (π) and fixation index (Fst) were calculated for samples with low admixture (ancestry coefficient > 0.7049) through vcftools93 (v0.1.16, with parameter -window-size 500 kb -window-step 50 kb). To compare LD patterns between different groups, PopLDdecade (v3.40, with parameters “--maxdist 300 --maf 0.05 --miss 0.1”)94 was used to calculate the average of the squared correlation coefficient (r2) of paired markers.

Demographic history inference

SMC++ (v1.15.2)23 was used to obtain estimates of effective population size history and differentiation time at the population level. In order to reduce the confusion caused by gene flow, the samples with low admixture (ancestry coefficient > 0.70) were used in this analysis, and refer to the SMC++ workflow in Coe et al.24.

First, RepeatMasker (v3.2.9) was used to identify the reference genome duplication sites to build a mask file, and the vcf2smc command in SMC++, combined with the mask information, was used to convert the VCF file to SMC format. The genotype of a single sample from each population as distinguished lineages for allele selection, and randomly selected genotype data from 20 samples per population for SMC++ analysis. The population size history was evaluated using the estimate command. The mutation rate per generation was set at mu = 2.4 × 10−8, the mutation rate of celery was used as determined based on the evolutionary rate between lettuce and celery78,95, and the mature generation of species was set at 1 year. The split command (“-cores 8 –timepoints 100 10000”) was used to evaluate the divergence time between populations. The estimation uncertainty of population size and differentiation time was determined using a bootstrap method, in which twenty replicate samples of input genomic data for each different population were sampled in a 5 Mb block24. Finally, use the plots command to draw the results.

Ecological niche modeling

We analyzed different geographical records from all identified G1 and G3 accessions, respectively. We obtained raster files of 19 bioclimatic variables for Pleistocene: late Holocene (LH, 4.2–0.3 ka, v1.0) paleoclimate data from PaleoClim (http://www.paleoclim.org/)27, with a resolution of 2.5 min. ENMeval (v0.3.1)96 R package was used to determine all combinations of defined settings and perform cross-validation on model evaluation. For the G1 ecological type, the setting of LQH_1 is used to measure the variable importance of LH, and for the G3 ecological type, the setting of LQP_1.5 is used to measure the variable importance of LH. Then, in MaxEnt (v3.4.4)97, habitat suitability predictions were generated based on ENMeval results with 10 sub-sample runs and 20 random test percentages of parameters.

Selective sweep analysis

In order to identify genomic regions that have undergone selection during domestication, XP-CLR (https://github.com/hardingnj/xpclr)98 with parameters “--maxsnps 500 --size 100000 --step 10000” was used to compare XP-CLR scores among four populations. The top 5% of regions were assigned to candidate selective regions, and genes in these regions were considered candidate genes.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from mature leaves of four celery groups at 120 days after planting using a Trizol kit (Invitrogen, USA). Then, RNA from each sample was taken for sequencing using the DNBSEQ-T7 platform. Three biological replicates were performed for each sample. The transcriptome data of petiole hollowing and hypocotyl enlargement traits were obtained from PRJCA022437. Transcriptome analysis was performed using the following workflow. FASTP was used to remove adapter information and obtain clean data. Then, Clean reads were aligned to the var. secalinum reference genome using HISAT299, and FeatureCounts100 was employed to quantify raw read counts per. Finally, Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2101 in R, with statistical significance defined by a false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 and an absolute log₂(fold change) ≥1.

Metabolite profiling

A liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization-tandem mass spectrometry system was used for the relative quantification of widely targeted metabolites in celery leaf samples. Grinder (MM 400, Retsch) was used to grind freeze-dried fresh celery leaves at 30 Hz for 1.5 min. Weigh 100 mg of sample powder and dissolve it in 70% methanol. Vortex vigorously 5–6 times to ensure thorough mixing, store overnight at 4 °C. Centrifuge the extract at 2000×g for 5 min, extract the supernatant, filter the sample through a 0.22 μm membrane, and store it in an injection bottle for LC-MS/MS analysis.

High-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC ExionLC™ AD, QTRAP 4500) was used to detect extracts from each sample. The sample was separated using an Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm * 100 mm), with water (A) and acetonitrile (B) as mobile phases, both containing 0.1% formic acid. Set the flow rate to 0.35 mL/min, the column temperature is set to 40 °C, and the injection volume is 2 μL. The elution gradient is set as follows: at 0.00 min, the proportion of phase B is 5%. Within 9.00 min, the proportion of phase B increases linearly to 95% and remains at 95% for 1 min. From 10.00 to 11.10 min, the proportion of phase B decreases to 5% and reaches equilibrium at 5% for 14 min.

The mass spectrometry conditions are set as follows: the temperature of the electric spray ion source (ESI) is 550 °C; the ion spray voltage (IS) is 5500 V in the positive ion mode, and −4500 V in the negative ion mode; the ion source gas I (GSI), gas II (GSII), and curtain gas (CUR) are set to 50, 60, and 25 psi respectively, and the metabolites are quantified using the predetermined multi reaction monitoring method34.

Finally, a total of 1552 metabolites were detected from 203 celery samples (for each line, the leaves of three biological replicates are mixed together for metabolic substance detection), and the detected metabolites were annotated using real standard identification of metabolic characteristics, metabolic database such as PubChem, and those catalogued in a textbook102. The relative signal intensity of metabolites is normalized by referencing the control metabolites, and then log2 transformed to further normalize and improve the normality of the data.

Genome-wide association studies and mGWAS signal hotspots

A total of 34,004,698 high-quality SNPs (with missing data in genotype files estimated using Beagle (v5.0)103 at default settings) were used for GWAS analysis. The mixed linear model program in GEMMA104 was used for GWAS analysis, with population structure and SNP genotype matrix as fixed factors, and kinship evidence as random effects. The five detected agronomic traits and all metabolic data were used as phenotypic input for GWAS. The genome-wide significance threshold was determined using a uniform threshold of 0.05/n, where n is the number of valid SNPs. The effective number of independent SNPs was calculated using the Genetic Type 1 Error Calculator (GEC)105 at 12,468,828. A total of 335,692 non-redundant significant genetic loci were detected from 1552 metabolites. The genome was divided into 3286 non-overlapping 1-Mb intervals, and the total mGWAS signals were mapped onto these intervals. Signals within the interval showed a significant uneven distribution, deviating from the random distribution of the entire genome. Subsequently, by comparing the expected distribution and empirical distribution of significant signals, we identified genomic intervals within 99th of the expected distribution (significant signal numbers >126 within the interval) as potential metabolic association hotspots. Visualize the distribution patterns of different metabolites and metabolite-gene associations using the “Fruchterman Reingold” layout algorithm in Gephi (v0.10.1).

Metware Cloud, a free online platform for data analysis (https://cloud.metware.cn), was used for the visualization of the results. We used a comprehensive method to select differential metabolites between populations: Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) ≥ 0.8, Fold Change > 1.5 or Fold Change < 0.67. We used t-test to detect the p-value between the two groups and FDR test to screen for q-value ≤ 0.05. The results were visualized in ggplot2107.

The genomic prediction dataset was constructed using genome-wide SNP markers (mGWAS results p-value < 1.0 × 10−3 37). To ensure positive metabolite content values, both observed and predicted values were shifted by the detection‑standard mean of log₂(1 × 10⁵). The Pearson correlation coefficient between predicted values and observed values was reported as the prediction accuracy, and three methods were used to predict phenotypes. Ridge Regression Optimal Linear Unbiased Prediction (rrBLUP)40, Bayesian Generalized Linear Regression (BGLR)41, and the machine learning method SVR42 were used for optimal linear unbiased prediction. The BGLR method is executed using the R package BGLR with parameters “model =BRR, nTer =1000, burnIn =50, thin =5”; The rrBLUP method is executed using the R package rrBLUP with parameters “method = REML, SE = TRUE”; The SVR method is implemented in Python using the sklearn module (“kernel=rbf, C = 1.0, gamma=scale, epsilon=0.01”), and the results are plotted using the ggplot2107. We systematically compared the performance differences among the three prediction methods using paired t-tests. To account for multiple comparisons, p-values were further adjusted using the Bonferroni method.

Gene cloning and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from celery leaves and petioles using Trizol reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) and using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan), to reverse transcribe RNA according to the manual to construct a cDNA library. Design primers to amplify the complete CDS of candidate genes in cDNA. Use the StepOnePlus real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, ThermoFisher, USA) to set up reactions according to the standard steps of SYBR Green supermax (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan), with three biological replicates and three technical replicates for each treatment. Using TUB-B108 as an internal reference, further analyze the relative expression values using the 2−ΔΔCt algorithm.

Recombinant protein purification

Connect the candidate gene CDSs (without stop codon) to the Pet32a vector, then propagate the target vector using Escherichia coli competent state DH5α, and introduce the target vector into E. coli competent state BL21 (DE3) pLysS for expression by the heat shock method. Cultivate the bacteria in Luria Bertani (LB) medium (containing 0.1 g/L ampicillin) at 37 °C until OD600 reaches 0.6, and then induce recombinant protein synthesis at 16 °C for 24 h using 1 mM isopropyl-b-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Collect the bacterial cells and resuspend them in 1x PBS. After freezing at 80 °C for 24 h, cells were lysed using an ultrasonic instrument, and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 8000×g. The recombinant protein was purified using the HisTALON gravity column purification kit (Takara) and eluted with storage buffer (0.1 M Tris HCl, pH 6.5, containing 2 mM DTT and 10% glycerol) on a PD-10 column. The purified recombinant protein was detected by SDS-PAGE and stored at 80 °C for further analysis.

Enzyme activity validation assays

For in vitro enzyme activity validation of OMT protein: Enzyme activity reaction system: 200 µL Tris HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 8.0), 1 mM SAM, 200 μM eriodictyol standard, 25 μL recombinant protein; the reaction system with empty vector (Pet32a) protein was used as a control. After 2 h of reaction in a 37 °C water bath, an equal volume of ethyl acetate was added for extraction. The process was repeated once, and the supernatant was combined. After rotary evaporation, the mixture was resuspended in 200 μL of methanol and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane for HPLC analysis.

Dual-luciferase transactivation assays

Amplified candidate gene promoters of different haplotypes (2 kb upstream of the translation start codon) and cloned them into the pGreenII 0800-LUC vector containing LUC and Renilla luciferase (REN) reporter genes. Import the vector into the Agrobacterium GV3101 strain containing plasmid pSoup-p19. The dual luciferase reporter kit (Vazyme) was used to quantify LUC and REN activity (primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 14).
