Rethinking probiotics: breaking the Lactobacillus-Bifidobacterium duopoly in the management of dysbiosis and allergies
Authors: Claudia Cappella, Chiara Redaelli, Melissa Balzarotti, Pasquale Scarcia, Gennaro Agrimi, Maurizio Bettiga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver the past two decades, gut microbiota has emerged as a central regulator of host metabolism and immune homeostasis, leading to extensive clinical and translational exploration of probiotic interventions. However, despite increasing recognition of microbiota complexity, clinical and translational research has remained disproportionately focused on a limited set of taxa, primarily Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium. Meta-analyses published between 2012 and 2025 report modest benefits by these taxa and substantial heterogeneity across dysbiosis-associated and allergic conditions. This indicates limited and context-dependent efficacy, along with scarce and inconsistent impact on objective immunological endpoints. Here, we examine the prevailing probiotic paradigm in terms of ecological relevance and functional targeting, arguing that historical, technological, and regulatory biases have contributed to a misalignment between microbial selection and dominant metabolic and immunological functions in the adult gut ecosystem. Using dysbiosis and allergic disease as illustrative case studies, we integrate clinical evidence and mechanistic insights to evaluate emerging microbiota-based strategies, including next-generation probiotics, live biotherapeutic products, and non-viable microbial preparations. We suggest that future interventions should move beyond empirical taxonomic supplementation toward function-driven, context-aware approaches grounded in defined microbial activities and biologically informative clinical endpoints, enabling more precise and durable modulation of host-microbiota interactions.Keywords: Probiotics, dysbiosis, allergic disease, Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium , next-generation probiotics, microbiota-immune interactions

Introduction

Until the beginning of this millennium, intestinal microbiota occupied a marginal position in biomedical research, largely confined to its role in digestion and fermentation. Over the past two decades, omics-driven investigations have profoundly reshaped this view, redefining gut microbiota as a central orchestrator of host physiology rather than a passive microbial community. 1 Alterations in its composition and function are now implicated in a wide range of conditions, including digestive, metabolic, and neurological disorders, 2 as well as immune-mediated diseases and allergies. 3

At the same time, modulation of gut microbiota has emerged as a therapeutic strategy, most prominently through the use of probiotics, defined as “live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host”. 4 Despite the expanding recognition of microbiota complexity, both clinical research and commercial development of probiotics have remained focused on a limited set of taxa, primarily Lactobacillus (now reclassified into multiple genera) and Bifidobacterium. This dominance reflects their long-standing presence in fermented foods, favorable safety classifications, and technological suitability for industrial production, rather than their prevalence or functional dominance within the adult gut ecosystem. 5

Notably, Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium together account for less than 2%–3% of total microbial biomass in the healthy adult intestine 6 and represent only a limited fraction of its overall metabolic capacity. 7

This observation does not imply that low-abundance taxa are intrinsically functionally irrelevant. Rather, it highlights a limited alignment between ecological role and therapeutic targeting, whereby organisms selected primarily for historical, technological, and regulatory reasons have become disproportionately represented in clinical interventions, despite contributing only a limited subset of the metabolic and immunological functions dominant in the adult gut ecosystem.

This discrepancy raises a fundamental question: to what extent can interventions based on low-abundance, often transient colonizers meaningfully remodel a complex microbial ecosystem or induce sustained immunological effects?

Consistent with this concern, recent meta-analyzes reveal that the clinical efficacy of traditional probiotics is frequently modest, heterogeneous, and highly strain-dependent. 8 Benefits are often confined to specific patient subgroups or symptomatic outcomes, with limited and inconsistent effects on objective immunological biomarkers, 8-10 as observed also in allergic and dysbiosis-associated conditions. 11

These findings do not negate the clinical utility of traditional probiotics in well-defined contexts, but they suggest the limitations of an empirical, largely non-personalized paradigm in complex immune-mediated disorders. Increasingly, mechanistic and ecological studies suggest that many host-microbe interactions relevant to immune tolerance, epithelial integrity, and metabolic homeostasis are mediated by obligate anaerobes and specialized commensals that fall outside the classical probiotic repertoire. Their functions include short-chain-fatty-acid (SCFA) production, bile-acid transformation, and regulatory T-cell (Treg) induction. 12

The concept of next-generation probiotics (NGPs) has emerged to describe microbial candidates selected on the basis of defined biological functions rather than historical taxonomic classification, reflecting a shift towards function-driven, mechanism-informed intervention strategies. This category typically includes obligate anaerobes (e.g. Akkermansia, Faecalibacterium), spore-forming bacteria, and rationally designed microbial consortia. These candidates may be developed as live biotherapeutic products (LBPs), a regulatory category for therapeutic applications, distinct from the broader conceptual definition of NGPs, or as non-viable microbial preparations, such as postbiotics (defined as preparations of inanimate microorganisms and/or their components that confer a health benefit on the host, consistent with recent consensus definitions) 13 and parabiotics (inactivated microbial cells retaining biological activity, a term variably defined in the literature). Importantly, the designation is descriptive rather than comparative: it reflects differences in ecological niche, functional targets and development pathways, without implying superior clinical efficacy, which must be demonstrated on a case-by-case basis. 12

NGPs are selected for defined biological functions, including mucin degradation, bile-acid metabolism, and SCFA signaling. They increasingly follow drug-like development and regulatory pathways as LBPs, which introduces additional challenges related to chemistry, manufacturing and control, stability, safety assessment, engraftment potential, and antimicrobial-resistance risk (Food and Drug Administration - FDA 2016/2018; European Medicine Agency - EMA guidance). 8-10 , 12

To understand why taxonomic identity alone is an unreliable predictor of clinical efficacy, it is essential to consider the mechanistic basis of host-microbe interactions. The biological effects of probiotics and commensals are mediated by host-sensed microbial ligands, including metabolites and structural components. Two major signaling axes are particularly relevant. The first involves SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, which act through G-protein-coupled receptors, such as GPR41, GPR43, and GPR109A, and via epigenetic mechanisms. 14 The second comprises microbe-associated molecular patterns, including lipoteichoic acid, flagellin, and muramyl dipeptide, which are sensed by pattern-recognition receptors, such as TLR2, TLR5, and NOD2. 15 Together, these pathways modulate epithelial barrier integrity, immune tone, and inflammatory set-points, although their direction and magnitude are strongly context-dependent and influenced by microbial niche, diet, formulation, dose, and host genotype. This context dependency likely contributes to the modest, variable, and strain-dependent clinical effects observed with conventional probiotics.

Against this background, dysbiosis and allergic disease represent particularly informative case studies. Both conditions are characterized by early-life microbial perturbations, impaired epithelial barrier function, and skewed immune responses; yet both have shown only limited and inconsistent responsiveness to conventional probiotic supplementation. This convergence underscores the need to reassess not only which microorganisms are administered, but why they are selected and how their functional properties are expected to translate into clinically meaningful outcomes.

Aim of this review

This review critically examines the limitations of the prevailing probiotic paradigm centered on Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, drawing on evidence from recent meta-analyzes of randomized controlled trials. We propose that the modest and heterogeneous effects observed across studies reflect a potential mismatch between ecological function and therapeutic targeting, explaining the limited consistency of clinical outcomes observed with traditional probiotic interventions.

In the context of dysbiosis and, especially allergy, this review advances the argument that the empirical and relatively non-personalized use of legacy probiotic strains is insufficient in addressing the underlying biological complexity of these conditions. Instead, we propose that future microbiota-based interventions should be grounded in a more mechanistic understanding of microbial functionality and host-microbe interactions, and by converging mechanistic insights pointing to specific microbial ligands and functions. 2

Materials and methods

Evidence mapping: search strategy and selection criteria

This study adopts a structured evidence-mapping approach designed to provide a representative overview of clinical evidence across conditions, rather than a comprehensive systematic synthesis. This approach was chosen to enable comparison across heterogeneous indications, while minimizing redundancy from overlapping meta-analyzes.

Between February and July 2025, we conducted structured PubMed/MEDLINE searches to identify published meta-analyzes of randomized controlled trials evaluating “traditional” probiotics, primarily Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, in allergy-related and dysbiosis-associated conditions. Searches combined condition-specific keywords with the PubMeb filter “meta-analysis”. The time frame was restricted to 2012–2025 to minimize overlap with pre-2012 data and to maximize methodological comparability across analyzes. Further details on search terms are provided in Supplementary Materials (Table S1).

Records were screened at the title and abstract level, and full texts were consulted where needed to confirm eligibility and extract key methodological features. To avoid double counting of overlapping datasets, a single representative meta-analysis was selected per clinical condition. When multiple candidates were available, selection followed a predefined hierarchical set of pragmatic criteria: (i) recency or size of the included dataset; (ii) transparent eligibility criteria and risk-of-bias assessment; (iii) adequate study power, defined as inclusion of at least five randomized trials or a minimum of 500 participants; and (iv) reporting of pooled effect estimates and heterogeneity metrics.

When overlap among primary studies across meta-analyzes was suspected, we qualitatively assessed study lists and prioritized the most recent or most comprehensive synthesis. Consistent with this design, formal quantitative de-duplication was not undertaken.

Outcomes were required to align with the scope of this review, including clinical symptoms or quality-of-life measures, and objective biomarkers when available.

Importantly, selections were made irrespective of the direction or magnitude of the pooled effect and were intended to be representative rather than exhaustive.

This structured evidence-mapping approach does not aim to provide an exhaustive or quantitatively de-duplicated synthesis and may therefore be subject to selection bias and overlap between included meta-analyzes. Instead, it is designed to capture representative trends across heterogeneous conditions.

Bibliometric snapshot (2019–2024)

To provide contextual insight into current research priorities in the probiotic field, a bibliometric snapshot was generated by repeating the PubMed searches using genus-level terms for selected representative taxa (e.g., Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Akkermansia, Faecalibacterium) and restricting results to the period 2019–2024. Record counts were retrieved in July 2025.

This bibliometric snapshot provides an indicative overview and should be interpreted with some caution. In particular, recent taxonomic reclassification of the former Lactobacillus genus into multiple new genera (e.g. Lacticaseibacillus, Lactiplantibacillus), may influence publication counts due to differences in indexing and terminology over time. In addition, the use of combined search terms (e.g. “probiotics” and genus names) may favor historically established taxa and underrepresent recently reclassified or emerging genera. However, many recent publications continue to reference both former and updated nomenclature, resulting in substantial overlap across search results. Exploratory sensitivity checks using updated genus names yielded comparable trends, suggesting that both potential underestimation (due to missing new genus names) and overestimation (due to legacy terminology) are unlikely to substantially affect the overall conclusions.

Biological pathways underlying microbiota-mediated modulation of dysbiosis and allergic disease

Intestinal dysbiosis and epithelial barrier dysfunction

Dysbiosis refers to alterations in both the composition and the function of gut microbiota. It is characterized by reduced microbial diversity and altered community composition, loss of symbiotic taxa, overgrowth of pathobionts, and shifts in metabolic profiles. 23 , 24 Such alterations can compromise epithelial tight-junction architecture, increase intestinal permeability (“leaky gut”), and trigger pro-inflammatory signaling cascades. 25

Microbiota-based interventions are expected to influence dysbiosis primarily through functional mechanisms rather than simple taxonomic replacement. These include ecological competition and cross-feeding interactions that promote microbial diversity, enhanced production of SCFAs and other immunoregulatory metabolites such as indole-3-lactate, and reinforcement of mucus layers and tight-junction expression, thereby limiting bacterial translocation as demonstrated in preclinical models. Specifically, strict anaerobes, such as F. prausnitzii and Roseburia spp., contribute substantially to butyrate production; whereas Butyricicoccus pullicaecorum and Odoribacter splanchnicus have been shown to exert barrier-protective effects via extracellular vesicles, in both in vitro and in vivo models. 26 , 27

Collectively, these mechanisms illustrate how microbiota-mediated effects are primarily driven by specific functional traits and metabolite production, rather than taxonomic identity alone.

Microbiota-immune crosstalk: innate sensing and inflammatory tone

In allergic disease, dysbiosis skews immune responses toward a Th2-polarized profile and is associated with contraction of Treg pools, thereby amplifying IgE-mediated inflammation. 28 , 29 Epidemiological evidence supports a critical role of early-life microbiota composition in immune imprinting. Cohort studies indicate that, within the first 1000 days of life, a reduced abundance of bifidobacteria, lactobacilli, and SCFA-producing taxa correlates with an increased risk of sensitization and pediatric atopic disease. 30 , 31

From a mechanistic perspective, microbiota-based interventions are expected to influence innate immune sensing and inflammatory tone through complementary pathways. These include the production of SCFAs, which signal via GPR43 and GPR41 to promote Treg differentiation and restrain Th2 responses, as demonstrated in preclinical models and emerging evidence in humans, and induction of immunoregulatory cytokines, such as IL-10 and TGF-β, by selected microbial strains.

In addition to live microorganisms, non-viable microbial derivatives also contribute to immune modulation. Parabiotics and extracellular vesicles derived from commensals, including pasteurized A. muciniphila and vesicles from O. splanchnicus, have been shown to expand Treg populations and attenuate inflammatory responses in preclinical models, supporting immune regulation in the absence of bacterial engraftment. 32 , 33

Adaptive modulation: Th1/Th2 balance and Treg induction

At the adaptive immune level, microbiota-driven modulation of Th1/Th2 balance, together with the size and stability of the Treg compartment, represents a central determinant of immune tolerance and long-term control of allergic inflammation. 3 Specific microbial functions shape this balance by promoting IL-10 and TGF-β signaling and by supporting Foxp3⁺ Treg differentiation, thereby constraining pathogenic Th2 polarization.

In particular, spore-forming Clostridia belonging to clusters IV and XIVa induce the expansion of colonic Foxp3⁺ Tregs and enhance local TGF-β production, resulting in attenuated allergic inflammation in experimental models. 34 , 35 Similarly, polysaccharide A (PSA) derived from Bacteroides fragilis promotes Treg differentiation through antigen-presenting cell (APC)-mediated mechanisms and limits Th2-driven immune responses in preclinical models. 36

Enhancement of epithelial barrier integrity

Preservation of epithelial barrier integrity is a critical determinant of immune tolerance, as it limits the translocation of dietary and microbial antigens that can trigger or amplify allergic inflammation. 37 In dysbiosis-associated conditions, disruption of tight-junction architecture and thinning of the mucus layer increase epithelial permeability and facilitate inappropriate immune activation.

Several commensal microorganisms exert direct barrier-strengthening effects that are partly independent of their immunomodulatory actions. These include the upregulation of tight-junction proteins, modulation of claudin expression, and reinforcement of the mucus layer. Both live and pasteurized preparations of A. muciniphila, as well as its outer-membrane protein Amuc_1100, have been shown to upregulate claudin-5 and claudin-8 while downregulating claudin-2, thereby improving barrier function in preclinical models and, to some extent, in humans. 38

Similarly, F. prausnitzii contributes to barrier integrity through enhanced butyrate availability and restoration of tight-junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin, leading to reduced intestinal permeability in models of colitis and stress-induced barrier dysfunction. 39 Additional barrier-protective effects have been attributed to microbial extracellular vesicles, such as those released by O. splanchnicus, which modulate epithelial signaling pathways and dampen inflammasome activation, further limiting barrier disruption. 27

Specific microbial traits, including mucin utilization, modulation of tight-junction proteins, and vesicle-mediated signaling, can directly reinforce epithelial barrier function; whereas SCFAs have an indirect supportive role. Together, these mechanisms provide a biological rationale for prioritizing functionally defined targets in the development of next-generation microbiota-based interventions.

Immunoregulatory metabolites beyond SCFAs

While SCFAs represent a well-established link between gut microbiota, epithelial integrity, and immune regulation, a broader array of microbial metabolites further shapes mucosal immune responses. Tryptophan-derived indoles activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor and enhance epithelial-barrier gene expression; whereas secondary bile acids such as deoxycholic acid influence dendritic-cell tolerogenicity and antigen presentation. 36 , 40 In addition, structurally defined microbial glycans such as PSA from B. fragilis promote Treg expansion and limit Th2-polarized immune responses through defined host-microbe signaling pathways in preclinical models.

Collectively, these findings indicate that immunomodulatory activity can be mediated by discrete microbial molecules rather than by microbial presence per se. This underscores the importance of evaluating probiotic and next-generation candidates not only based on taxonomic classification alone, but also for their capacity to generate, or be co-delivered with, functionally relevant metabolites that reinforce epithelial integrity and calibrate mucosal immunity.

The gut-lung axis

Growing evidence supports the existence of a functional gut-lung axis. Accordingly, the composition and metabolic activity of intestinal microbiota influence immune responses in the respiratory mucosa. 41 , 42 This bidirectional crosstalk is mediated by a combination of circulating microbial metabolites, immune-cell trafficking, and shared inflammatory signaling pathways.

Multiple microbiota-derived mediators have been implicated in this process. SCFAs generated in the gut can enter systemic circulation and modulate pulmonary immune tone, attenuating bronchial hyper-responsiveness, stabilizing mast-cell activity, and promoting expansion of Tregs in the lungs, as evidenced by preclinical studies. 43-45

APCs conditioned in the intestinal environment can migrate to distal mucosal sites including the lungs, transferring tolerogenic signals and shaping local immune responses. In particular, dendritic cells educated by microbial metabolites and epithelial-derived cues in the gut have been shown to promote regulatory phenotypes upon trafficking, reinforcing oral tolerance and cross-mucosal immune regulation. 46

Together, these mechanisms provide a biological framework linking intestinal dysbiosis to respiratory allergic disease and support the rationale for microbiota-targeted interventions aimed at modulating systemic immune set-points. Rather than acting exclusively at the site of administration, probiotic, postbiotic, and next-generation strategies may exert clinically relevant effects through distal immune modulation along the gut-lung axis.

Although the gut-lung axis reflects bidirectional immune communication, most interventional evidence to date derives from oral modulation of the intestinal microbiota rather than direct nasal administration.

Taken together, these mechanistic insights highlight how consistent clinical effects are likely to benefit from targeting defined biological functions and host-microbe interactions.

The dominance of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium: origins and limitations

Historical, technological, and regulatory origins

The predominance of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium in probiotic research and commercial applications reflects historical precedent, technological feasibility, and regulatory facilitation. 5 , 47 In many cases, strain selection preceded detailed mechanistic understanding, establishing development trajectories that have persisted to this day.

Historically, lactobacilli were among the first microorganisms associated with health-promoting effects, following early twentieth-century observations linking fermented dairy consumption with longevity. Subsequent isolation of bifidobacteria from human milk further reinforced their perceived beneficial status, particularly in early life. 5 , 47 These associations established a durable conceptual link between these genera, food fermentation, and host health.

Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species are highly suitable for industrial-scale production. They tolerate moderately acidic conditions, grow efficiently on relatively inexpensive culture media, withstand downstream processing such as freeze-drying with limited loss of viability, and remain stable during storage and distribution. 48 These characteristics have made them attractive candidates for large-scale manufacturing and global commercialization.

Regulatory frameworks have further reinforced this dominance. In both the European Union and the United States, many Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species benefit from Qualified Presumption of Safety (QPS) or Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) status, substantially reducing the evidentiary burden required for market entry 47 , 49 . By contrast, microorganisms outside this historical repertoire are typically classified as novel, requiring extensive genomic characterization, toxicological evaluation, and antimicrobial-resistance profiling. While essential for protecting public health, these requirements introduce substantial time and cost constraints that may discourage investment in less-established taxa. 49

The combined effect of historical familiarity, manufacturing convenience, and regulatory acceleration has generated a self-reinforcing cycle. Early approvals facilitated widespread commercial use; commercial availability shaped clinical research priorities; and research output, in turn, validated continued focus on the same genera. 7 As reflected in recent bibliometric analyzes, this cycle has contributed to the sustained dominance of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium in the literature, even though mechanistic insights increasingly implicate a broader range of functionally specialized commensals. Hence, the taxonomic focus of traditional probiotics reflects historical and practical drivers rather than optimal functional targeting.

Clinical and scientific limitations of traditional probiotics

The therapeutic effects of traditional probiotics based on Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium remain modest and inconsistent across dysbiosis-related and allergic conditions. As summarized in Table 1, meta-analyzes of randomized controlled trials report small effect sizes, substantial inter-study heterogeneity, and limited reproducibility across clinical endpoints. 11 , 17 , 21 These patterns do not reflect an absence of biological activity, but rather a mismatch between the complexity of host-microbiota interactions and the resolution of current clinical approaches.

Many clinical studies rely on historically selected or commercially available strains rather than on predefined functional criteria, resulting in interventions that may not adequately target the dominant pathophysiological drivers of a given condition. 5 This limitation is compounded by the frequent extrapolation of strain-level effects across heterogeneous populations, despite growing evidence that baseline microbiota composition, host genetics, and environmental exposure strongly modulate probiotic responsiveness.

A second limitation concerns study design and outcome selection. Clinical trials often prioritize short-term symptom scores or composite endpoints; while mechanistically informative biomarkers, such as changes in barrier integrity, immune-cell phenotypes or metabolite profiles, are assessed inconsistently or not at all. As a result, biologically meaningful effects may remain undetected, misattributed or diluted when averaged across populations with divergent baseline states. 7

Addressing the above limitations will likely require a shift from empirical, taxonomy-based supplementation towards function-driven, context-aware, and mechanistically anchored microbiota-based interventions.

Heterogeneous efficacy

The modest benefits provided by Lactobacillus/Bifidobacterium-based probiotics across multiple dysbiosis-related conditions (Table 1) are accompanied by substantial inter-study heterogeneity, reflecting variation between strains, dosing regimens, treatment duration, and delivery matrices.

This pattern is well illustrated by irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), one of the most extensively studied indications. A large meta-analysis including 73 randomized controlled trials (n = 8682) reported a modest reduction in the risk of persistent symptoms (RR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.71–0.87), but with high statistical heterogeneity (I² = 72%), indicating limited comparability across trials. 50 Similar trends have been reported for other clinical indications, where small pooled effects coexist with inconsistent outcomes and weak reproducibility.

Beyond methodological variability, strong inter-individual differences in baseline microbiota composition and host context further contribute to heterogeneous responses. Overall, pooled estimates may mask substantial variability in treatment response, limiting their usefulness for guiding clinically meaningful interventions. 8

Allergy focus

In allergic disease, the clinical impact of traditional probiotics appear to be strongly context-dependent, with effects varying according to the timing of intervention (e.g. early-life prevention versus treatment of established disease), host immune status, and baseline microbiota composition. 51 Importantly, results are more encouraging for preventive approaches; this likely reflects the role of early-life microbiota-host interactions in shaping immune maturation and tolerance, which may be less modifiable in established disease. Meta-analyzes indicate that prophylactic administration of Lactobacillus/Bifidobacterium-based formulations during pregnancy and early infancy, a critical windows of immune development, is associated with a 20% reduced risk of infant eczema based on pooled estimates. 11 , 52

By contrast, meta-analyzes examining asthma, allergic rhinitis, and food allergy report no significant or reproducible clinical benefit, alongside marked inter-study heterogeneity. 18 , 22 Where modest pooled effects are reported, they are often restricted to symptom scores and fail to translate into durable immunological changes or slower disease progression.

Together, these findings suggest that traditional probiotics exert their most reliable effects when administered in early life, likely reflecting their influence on immune imprinting rather than their capacity to remodel entrenched allergic inflammation. This distinction between preventive and therapeutic contexts indicates a fundamental limitation of the current paradigm and underscores the need for more targeted, mechanism-driven strategies in allergic disease.

Dysbiosis disorders

Across dysbiosis-associated conditions, traditional Lactobacillus/Bifidobacterium-based probiotics show variable and generally modest efficacy, with outcomes that differ markedly by disease context. Importantly, conditions grouped under the umbrella term “dysbiosis” encompass distinct microbial configurations and pathophysiological drivers, limiting the applicability of a uniform probiotic strategy.

For example, probiotics are associated with small but reproducible improvements in overall symptoms of IBS, albeit with substantial heterogeneity. Instead, evidence in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is more restricted. Clinical benefits have been demonstrated for a few multi-strain formulations in ulcerative colitis, particularly for relapse prevention; whereas no consistent efficacy has been observed in Crohn’s disease. 53

In obesity-related dysbiosis, pooled analyzes indicate minimal effects on body weight and metabolic parameters, with modest average reductions that are often confined to specific subgroups defined by age, baseline metabolic status or intervention duration. 17

Together, these examples highlight how heterogeneous clinical responses reflect underlying biological diversity across dysbiosis-associated disorders, further supporting a shift towards functionally targeted strategies.

Safety profile

GRAS probiotics based on Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium have a long history of use in foods and dietary supplements. Meta-analyzes and post-marketing surveillance data indicate a low incidence of serious adverse events in the general population, supporting their favorable safety profile when used in otherwise healthy individuals. 47

However, rare cases of bacteraemia, fungaemia, and sepsis associated with probiotic use have been reported, primarily in severely immunocompromised patients, preterm infants, and critically ill individuals. 54 , 55 Additional concerns include horizontal gene transfer of antimicrobial-resistance determinants and the unpredictable behavior of live microorganisms in altered host environments, particularly when administered at high doses or for prolonged periods.

These considerations are consistent with the need for more nuanced risk stratification and for safety assessment frameworks that extend beyond taxonomic identity alone. While traditional probiotics remain appropriate for broad use in low-risk populations, the development of NGPs and postbiotic formulations calls for improved safety predictability based on mechanism of action, controlled functionality and, in some cases, reduced reliance on live microbial replication. These considerations support functionally defined and better-controlled microbiota-based interventions, including NGPs and postbiotic formulations.

NGPs and emerging strategies

The limitations of traditional probiotic approaches discussed above have caused a progressive shift towards function-driven, mechanism-informed, microbiota-based strategies. Increasingly, attention has moved from empirically selected, food-grade probiotics toward NGPs and related strategies that prioritize defined biological functions, ecological relevance, and mechanistic plausibility. 12

A central feature of this emerging paradigm is the decoupling of microbial taxonomic identity from presumed clinical benefit. Rather than assuming functionality based on genus-level familiarity or historical use, NGP candidates are identified on the basis of specific functional traits that are depleted or disrupted in disease states. These include reinforcement of epithelial-barrier integrity, modulation of mucosal immune responses, production of immunoregulatory metabolites, and participation in defined microbial networks within the gut ecosystem. 2

This transition aims for approaches better aligned with the complexity of host-microbiota interactions, prioritizing measurable biological activity and context-dependent efficacy over microbial presence.

From taxonomic supplementation to function-driven design

Traditional probiotic development has relied mainly on a model, in which live microorganisms are administered under the implicit and rarely tested assumption that viability and colonization are sufficient to confer clinical benefit. Accumulating evidence instead indicates that functional outcomes depend on multiple additional factors, including metabolic capacity, ecological compatibility with the resident microbiota, and host-specific variables. 2 , 8

Function-driven design seeks to address these limitations by selecting candidates around predefined biological targets rather than taxonomic labels. Examples include strains capable of modulating tight-junction protein expression, producing specific immunoregulatory metabolites, interacting with host pattern-recognition receptors or contributing to mucosal barrier maintenance. Within this framework, efficacy is evaluated using mechanistically informative endpoints, such as changes in epithelial permeability, immune-cell phenotypes, and metabolite profiles, rather than relying exclusively on symptom-based clinical scores. 12 This approach facilitates more informative early-phase clinical studies and improves the interpretability of trial outcomes. It also aligns biological mechanisms with clinical outcomes, addressing a key limitation of traditional probiotic strategies.

LBPs and regulatory evolution

The shift toward function-driven microbiota interventions has been accompanied, and in part driven, by changes in regulatory and developmental pathways. Many NGP candidates are now being developed as LBPs, a category that distinguishes medicinal microbiota-based interventions from food-grade probiotics and dietary supplements. Candidate strains undergo extensive genomic characterization, definition of mechanism of action, controlled manufacturing, and formal clinical evaluation within indication-specific contexts. 7 , 47

Although these requirements slow the development process, they offer important advantages in terms of standardization, reproducibility, and regulatory clarity. Importantly, LBP safety and efficacy is evaluated in defined patient populations, rather than inferred from historical use. This distinction is particularly relevant for obligate anaerobes and specialized commensals that lack QPS or GRAS status but may have promising functional effects. 12 , 49 Hence, the LBP framework exemplifies a shift towards more standardized, mechanism-based, and indication-specific microbiota interventions.

Postbiotics and parabiotics as controlled alternatives

Besides live NGPs, increasing attention has been afforded to non-viable microbial preparations, including postbiotics and parabiotics. These preparations retain the defined biological activity but reduce some of the inherent variability and safety concerns associated with live bacteria. Defined microbial metabolites, structural components, and extracellular vesicles have been shown to modulate epithelial and immune signaling pathways in preclinical and early clinical studies. 7 , 13

From a translational perspective, postbiotic and parabiotic strategies offer several practical advantages, such as standardized composition, precisely controlled dosing, and easier isolation of underlying mechanisms from confounding ecological effects. While they do not recapitulate the full complexity of live microbial interactions, these approaches may be particularly suitable for vulnerable patients or clinical contexts requiring predictable and tightly regulated biological effects. 13 Furthermore, they illustrate how functionally defined, non-viable microbial products can overcome some of the limitations associated with live microorganism-based interventions.

Conceptual implications for clinical development

NGPs, LBPs, and postbiotic strategies have direct implications for clinical trial design, endpoint selection, and regulatory evaluation. Rather than asking whether a given strain exerts a generalized benefit, these approaches focus on how, where, in whom, and by which measurable mechanisms specific microbial functions can be therapeutically leveraged. 2

Importantly, this paradigm does not invalidate the contribution of traditional probiotics; instead, it contextualizes their limitations and offers a pathway toward more precise and predictable microbiota-based interventions. Next-generation approaches represent a rational extension of existing strategies, informed by the mechanistic, clinical, and regulatory lessons of the past two decades.

Repurposing approved probiotics (bridge strategies)

While NGPs and related microbiota-based strategies offer obvious conceptual advantages, their translation into clinical practice is subjected to regulatory, manufacturing, and developmental constraints. 12 Accordingly, probiotics with established safety profiles may serve as transitional or “bridge” strategies within legacy frameworks, provided that they are reconsidered through a functional and mechanistic lens and deployed in clearly defined clinical contexts.

Several non-conventional or “borderline” probiotics, such as Saccharomyces boulardii, Escherichia coli Nissle 1917, and selected spore-forming bacteria, occupy an intermediate position between traditional food-grade strains and emerging LPBs. These organisms differ from classical Lactobacillus/Bifidobacterium probiotics in terms of ecological behavior, mechanistic specificity, and clinical application, and act beyond generic symptom modulation. 7 , 47

The following sections examine these approved probiotics as transient, bridge strategies, highlighting both their mechanistic potential and the structural constraints that limit their broader applicability.

Saccharomyces boulardii CNCM I-746

S. boulardii is one of the most extensively studied non-bacterial probiotics and a case in point in repurposing within the traditional probiotic framework. Originally isolated as a yeast with anti-diarrheal properties, S. boulardii differs from bacterial probiotics in terms of taxonomy, ecological behavior, and mechanism of action, explaining its distinct clinical profile. 56

Mechanistically, S. boulardii affects the secretion of proteases that degrade bacterial toxins, inhibit pathogen adhesion, modulate host signaling pathways involved in inflammation, and reinforce epithelial-barrier integrity via tight-junction proteins and cytoskeletal organization. 56 , 57 In addition, S. boulardii influences immune responses by regulating dendritic-cell activation and cytokine production, thereby attenuating excessive inflammatory signaling in the gut mucosa.

Strong clinical evidence favors the use of S. boulardii in the prevention and treatment of antibiotic-associated diarrhea and in the prevention of recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection, as suggested by modest but reproducible benefits. 58 , 59 Evidence for other indications, including IBD and allergic conditions, remains limited or inconsistent, underscoring context-specific efficacy.

Nevertheless, S. boulardii showcases also the intrinsic limitations of repurposing approved probiotics. As a transient, non-colonizing organism, its effect depends on continuous administration and does not result in durable integration within the gut microbiota. Moreover, although generally safe, rare cases of fungaemia have been reported in critically ill or immunocompromised patients, highlighting the importance of careful patient selection and risk stratification. 55

S. boulardii exemplifies how a well-characterized, approved probiotic can function as a bridge strategy when guided by mechanistic understanding and indication-specific evidence. At the same time, its reliance on transient activity and narrow clinical applicability highlights why such repurposing approaches cannot fully substitute for the development of NGPs and more targeted microbiota-based interventions.

Escherichia coli Nissle 1917

E. coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) represents one of the earliest examples of a medicinally developed probiotic and occupies a distinct position within the spectrum of approved microbiota-based interventions. Isolated during the First World War and subsequently developed for clinical use, EcN differs substantially from classical food-grade probiotics in its ecological behavior, genetic background, and interaction with the host, aligning it closely with modern LBP concepts. 60 , 61

Mechanistically, EcN functions beyond generic microbiota supplementation. Preclinical studies demonstrate that EcN can upregulate tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and protect against chemically induced mucosal permeability, leading to direct reinforcement of epithelial barrier integrity. 62 In vitro and organoid studies further suggest that EcN can inhibit pathogenic bacterial growth and enhance host defense pathways. 63

Clinically, the most robust benefit of EcN pertains to ulcerative colitis, where randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews indicate comparable performance to standard mesalazine therapy in maintaining remission in selected patients. 60 , 64 Data for other indications, including Crohn’s disease and functional gastrointestinal disorders, remain inconsistent or limited.

Despite its generally favorable safety profile, EcN suffers from limited ecological integration within the resident gut microbiota, transient persistence, and sustained administration to ensure clinical efficacy. Moreover, genomic analyzes highlight the presence of loci associated with potentially genotoxic metabolites, suggesting that even well-established probiotic strains require careful genetic characterization and risk assessment, particularly in vulnerable patients. 65

EcN 1917 exemplifies how a well-characterized, approved probiotic can achieve clinically meaningful effects in specific settings, when aligned with appropriate endpoints and patient populations. Nevertheless, its restricted scope of efficacy and mechanistic constraints, highlight the current limitations of bridge strategies.

Bacillus clausii (mix O/C, N/R, SIN, T)

B. clausii is a spore-forming Gram-positive bacterium employed for decades in the prevention of antibiotic-associated diarrhea. Its clinical benefits are largely attributable to the resilience of its spores, which enable survival during gastric transit and functional activity in a disrupted microbiota. 66 Upon germination, B. clausii secrete antimicrobial compounds, including subtilisin and the antibiotic clausin, which are effective against Staphylococcus aureus and Clostridioides difficile.

In addition, B. clausii exhibits immunomodulatory properties. In vitro studies have reported increased secretory IgA production and enhanced IL-10 expression in Peyer’s patch-derived immune cells following exposure to B. clausii. 67 In an ovalbumin-induced murine asthma model, oral administration of B. clausii reduced bronchoalveolar eosinophilia and normalized IL-4/IL-5 expression, accompanied by expansion of colonic Treg populations via HIF-1α downregulation. 68

Clinically, B. clausii presents a favorable safety profile and is effective against acute pediatric diarrhea. 69 Translational reviews have also highlighted its potential barrier-protective and immunomodulatory properties; 70 however, robust randomized controlled trials in allergic or chronic dysbiosis-related conditions remain limited.

Bacillus clausii illustrates how spore-forming probiotics with defined antimicrobial and immunomodulatory activities can be repurposed within existing regulatory frameworks. However, the transient nature of their activity and the breadth rather than specificity of their functional effects point to key limitations of optimization within the legacy paradigm.

Lacticaseibacillus (formerly Lactobacillus) casei Shirota

L. casei Shirota is a well-characterized food-grade probiotic that exemplifies how microorganisms traditionally associated with fermented products can be reinterpreted through a functional lens. Administered for decades as part of the fermented milk drink Yakult®, the strain has been extensively studied in relation to immune modulation.

L. casei Shirota interacts with lamina propria immune cells, stimulating dendritic cells and promoting IL-12 and IL-10 signaling, thereby partially rebalancing the Th1/Th2 axis toward an IL-10-biased, immunoregulatory immune profile. 71 Notably, these effects are retained following heat inactivation or lysis, highlighting a substantial contribution of cell wall components and postbiotic metabolites. 72

Clinically, these immunomodulatory properties have been explored in several settings. In a double-blind trial involving Japanese cedar-pollen allergy sufferers, eight weeks of daily intake reduced symptom scores compared with placebo and modulated local cytokine profiles in the nasal mucosa. 73 Moreover, L. casei Shirota produces abundant lactate, which can indirectly support epithelial and immune homeostasis by serving as a substrate for endogenous butyrate-producing taxa and contributing to barrier integrity and local immune regulation. 74

Overall, L. casei Shirota illustrates how a well-characterized food-grade strain can exert reproducible immunomodulatory effects through defined host-microbe and metabolite-mediated pathways. However, its transient colonization, reliance on continuous intake, and primary association with symptom modulation rather than durable ecosystem restructuring underscore the intrinsic constraints of such approaches and reinforce the limitation of the traditional probiotic paradigm.

Rational optimization within the limits of the legacy paradigm

Despite a substantial body of preclinical evidence, already approved probiotics have rarely translated into formal therapeutic indications for dysbiosis and even less so for allergic disease. This gap reflects both the limited number of dedicated randomized controlled trials, particularly in allergy-focused settings, and the intrinsic constraints of strain selection within the traditional probiotic repertoire 5 .

Rather than fundamentally redesigning probiotic interventions around defined biological functions, efforts to maximize the functional impact of existing, approved probiotic strains have focused on rational optimization strategies. These include multi-strain formulations that combine complementary mechanisms, such as barrier reinforcement, SCFA production, and Treg modulation, to create beneficial synergies for the microbiota and the immune system 75 .

Strain selection, fermentation conditions, delivery matrices, and co-administration of targeted prebiotics in symbiotic formulations can influence strain fitness, engraftment potential, and the capacity to generate immunoregulatory metabolites. Co-culture systems or sequential administration approaches in preclinical or controlled settings, suggest that such strategies enhance butyrate production and accelerate microbiota recovery following antibiotic perturbation. 76 However, in chronic or established disease contexts, evidence remains limited and less consistent, highlighting the specificity of any potential clinical benefit.

While these optimization strategies may broaden the functional scope of already available products, they do not fundamentally overcome the limitations of the legacy probiotic paradigm. As such, rigorous adherence to the International Scientific Association of Probiotics and Prebiotics definitions and usage criteria remains essential to ensure reproducibility, mechanistic clarity, and safety; but optimization alone is unlikely to deliver durable ecosystem-level modulation in complex chronic conditions. 47

Collectively, these considerations position rational strain combinations and optimized delivery modes as valuable transitional approaches, albeit not definitive solutions, particularly in complex chronic conditions. Nevertheless, they provide a conceptual bridge toward next-generation strategies explicitly designed around defined biological functions and ecological principles.

Emerging NGP candidates

The limitations of repurposing and optimization strategies outlined above have fostered growing interest in NGP candidates selected based on defined biological functions rather than historical taxonomic use. These approaches target microbial activities that are depleted or dysregulated in dysbiosis-associated and allergic diseases, including epithelial-barrier reinforcement, immune-tolerance induction, and metabolite-mediated host signaling. 12 However, despite strong mechanistic rationale, most NGP candidates remain at an early stage of clinical translation, with limited evidence supporting efficacy in disease-specific contexts in humans.

Many next-generation candidates belong to obligate anaerobic or specialized commensal taxa that play central roles in gut ecosystem function, but have been inaccessible to clinical development. Advances in anaerobic cultivation, genomic characterization, and controlled manufacturing have begun to overcome these barriers, enabling the systematic evaluation of candidate strains. 7

In NGPs, candidate selection is guided by mechanistic evidence linking specific microbial functions to host phenotypes; while clinical evaluation increasingly emphasizes function-aligned endpoints rather than generic symptom modulation.

Akkermansia muciniphila

A. muciniphila is an obligate anaerobic, Gram-negative, mucin-degrading commensal that has emerged as a leading NGP candidate based on its central role in mucosal homeostasis and metabolic regulation. Reduced abundance of A. muciniphila has been consistently associated with obesity and metabolic disorders and, more recently, with inflammatory and allergic conditions. 12

A. muciniphila promotes epithelial-barrier integrity through multiple complementary pathways. One includes the stimulation of mucin turnover and activation of host signaling pathways via surface-associated protein Amuc_1100, which engages TLR2 and enhances barrier function. Another involves production of SCFAs, particularly acetate and propionate, which support epithelial function and immune regulation through GPR43/41-mediated signaling. 77

The first evidence of clinical relevance in humans derives from a phase I/II randomized controlled trial in overweight and obese individuals, in which daily supplementation with pasteurized A. muciniphila (10¹⁰ cells/day for three months) improved lipid profiles, markers of intestinal permeability, and insulin sensitivity, with a reduction in in the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) score of −0.27 (95% CI −0.44 to −0.10) compared with placebo. 78 Notably, pasteurization preserved immunomodulatory activity, while improving safety and stability, challenging the assumption that viability is required for therapeutic efficacy.

Accordingly, pasteurized A. muciniphila has been authorized as a novel food in Europe, reflecting a regulatory pathway that bridges LBP and postbiotic concepts (Regulation (EU) 2015/2283; EFSA 2021). Therefore, defined microbial components with known mechanisms can be leveraged to deliver predictable, function-specific effects.

Preclinical studies suggest that A. muciniphila may exert protective effects in models of food allergy and airway hypersensitivity via combined barrier-enhancing and immunoregulatory mechanisms. 79 , 80 However, despite strong biological plausibility, clinical efficacy in allergic disease has not been demonstrated and awaits confirmation in well-designed, indication-specific trials. This gap between mechanistic promise and demonstrated clinical efficacy is a recurring challenge across next-generation candidates.

Faecalibacterium prausnitzii

F. prausnitzii is a Gram-positive, obligate anaerobic commensal that has emerged as a prominent NGP candidate based on its anti-inflammatory and barrier-supporting functional profile. A decrease in F. prausnitzii has been consistently reported across multiple dysbiosis-associated conditions, including IBD, metabolic disorders, and allergic disease. 7

Functionally, F. prausnitzii contributes to epithelial and immune regulation through several complementary mechanisms. Through butyrate production, it supports colonocyte energy metabolism, reinforces epithelial-barrier integrity, and exerts anti-inflammatory effects via both GPCR-mediated signaling and histone deacetylase inhibition. 81 , 82 In addition to SCFAs, F. prausnitzii secretes anti-inflammatory microbial factors that attenuate NF-κB activation and promote regulatory immune responses, indicating that its biological activity extends beyond metabolite production alone. 7

F. prausnitzii and its derived products can reduce intestinal inflammation, restore barrier function, and modulate immune tone in experimental models. These effects are consistent with observational data linking low abundance of F. prausnitzii to increased intestinal permeability and heightened inflammatory responses in humans, providing strong mechanistic plausibility for therapeutic relevance in dysbiosis-driven diseases.

Despite this compelling functional profile, translation into clinical application has been constrained by substantial technological and ecological challenges. F. prausnitzii is extremely oxygen-sensitive, complicating large-scale cultivation, formulation, and delivery, 83 and stable engraftment following administration can be challenging. As a result, efforts have increasingly sought to exploit its functional outputs, including defined consortia, stabilized/inactivated preparation or purified effector molecules and cell-free products. 84

In the context of allergic disease, F. prausnitzii is of particular interest due to its capacity to promote regulatory immune pathways and to counteract Th2-skewed inflammation through butyrate-dependent and independent mechanisms. However, as with other next-generation candidates, its promise currently rests on strong mechanistic rationale rather than demonstrated clinical efficacy, underscoring the need for innovative delivery strategies and well-designed, indication-specific clinical trials.

Clostridium clusters IV and XIVa

Spore-forming Clostridia belonging primarily to clusters IV and XIVa represent a functionally defined group of NGP candidates with a key role in immune tolerance and mucosal homeostasis. Selected members of these obligate anaerobic taxa induce regulatory immune pathways and promote intestinal immune tolerance, positioning them as drivers of microbiota health. 2 , 7

Specific Clostridia species promote the differentiation and expansion of colonic Foxp3⁺ Tregs through induction of TGF-β and IL-10 signaling pathways. Seminal studies in germ-free mice have shown that colonization with defined mixtures of spore-forming Clostridia is sufficient to drive Treg accumulation and suppress inflammatory responses in the intestinal mucosa, indicating a causal role for these taxa in shaping the regulatory immune tone. 34 These effects are mediated in part by microbial metabolites, including SCFAs, but involve also additional, uncharacterized signals that contribute to immune conditioning beyond simple metabolite delivery.

In preclinical models, rationally designed Clostridia consortia have been shown to attenuate allergic inflammation and reduce disease severity by restoring immune tolerance, rather than broadly suppressing immune activation. 85 This property makes them archetypal examples of function-driven, microbial community-based therapeutics.

Nevertheless, their clinical translation is hindered by manufacturing complexity, regulatory uncertainty, and the need for tightly controlled, indication-specific trials. Extreme oxygen sensitivity, strain-to-strain variability, and complex manufacturing complicate their development as LPBs. Moreover, safety considerations require extensive genomic characterization and regulatory oversight. As a result, efficacy in allergic disease has yet to be demonstrated in well-powered randomized controlled trials.

Collectively, the promise of spore-forming Clostridia clusters lies not in incremental optimization of existing probiotics, but in their capacity to re-establish regulatory immune networks that are disrupted in chronic inflammatory and allergic disease.

Bacteroides fragilis (PSA⁺ strain)

B. fragilis is a Gram-negative, obligate anaerobic commensal. Unlike many next-generation candidates whose relevance derives primarily from metabolic output, B. fragilis is notable for its capacity to directly shape host immune responses via capsular PSA.

PSA signals through TLR2 on APCs, promoting IL-10 production and the differentiation of Foxp3⁺ Tregs. This pathway inhibits excessive Th2-driven inflammation, while preserving protective immune functions, highlighting a highly specific mode of host-microbe communication that extends beyond generic pattern-recognition signaling. 36

Preclinical studies demonstrate that PSA-producing B. fragilis can restore immune balance and protect against inflammatory and allergic phenotypes in murine models of colitis and allergic airway inflammation. 28 , 86 Hence, the targeting of PSA-mediated pathways could ameliorate diseases characterized by impaired immune tolerance. Furthermore, PSA showcases how systemic immunomodulatory effects can be achieved without widespread ecosystem restructuring. 28

Despite this compelling functional profile, translation into clinical application presents significant challenges. B. fragilis comprises both commensal and opportunistic pathogenic lineages, raising legitimate safety and regulatory concerns. Moreover, immunological effects attributed to PSA are strain-specific and cannot be generalized, necessitating precise characterization and control of candidate strains. As a result, clinical data evaluating live B. fragilis or PSA-based interventions in humans remain extremely limited. 7 , 87

These constraints have prompted growing interest in alternative strategies that decouple immunoregulatory function from live bacterial administration, including the use of purified PSA, engineered delivery systems or defined microbial consortia that reproduce PSA-dependent signaling within safer and more controllable delivery systems. In this sense, B. fragilis exemplifies how NGP development is increasingly organized around specific organisms, highlighting both the promise and the complexity of function-driven microbiota therapeutics.

Emerging but context-dependent candidates

Additional taxa, including metabolically specialized SCFA producers and context-dependent commensals such as Prevotella copri, have been proposed as exploratory targets rather than immediate therapeutic candidates for microbiota-based intervention. While these organisms can display enriched functional capacities in specific ecological settings and immunometabolic activity in preclinical models, their effects are highly strain- and context-specific, with associations that vary across host populations, dietary patterns, and disease states. High genomic and functional heterogeneity, together with limited and sometimes conflicting human evidence, precludes their prioritization as therapeutic candidates. 2 , 7

Collectively, next-generation candidates highlight how successful translation will depend on rigorous safety characterization, scalable manufacturing, and high-quality, indication-specific clinical evidence. At present, even highly mechanistic, function-specific candidates face substantial translational barriers despite strong biological plausibility. These considerations frame the broader translational challenges discussed below, including the alignment of clinical trial design with mechanism-based endpoints.

Translational challenges and future directions

Building on the constraints highlighted in the preceding sections, the translation of NGPs into routine practice remains limited not only by safety approval, manufacturing feasibility, product stability, and regulatory fragmentation, but also by deeper conceptual and methodological challenges. Chief among these is the persistent tendency to evaluate microbiota-based interventions in terms of taxonomic identity rather than biological function. As a result, many current translational strategies continue to apply trial designs, endpoints, and evaluation frameworks that are poorly aligned with the mechanistic complexity of microbiota-host interactions.

Safety, toxicity, and history of use

For any microbiota-based therapeutic candidate, an adequate safety profile represents a prerequisite for clinical development. 47 However, NGPs cannot rely on long histories of dietary exposure, such as those afforded to many Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species, which enjoy GRAS or QPS status. Many emerging candidates fall outside these traditional categories and, therefore, require safety to be established through dedicated, mechanism-informed evaluation rather than inferred from taxonomic familiarity. 49

A substantial proportion of NGP candidates belong to taxa that include opportunistic pathogens or exhibit pronounced strain-level functional divergence, necessitating high-resolution genomic characterization and phenotypic screening. Examples include A. muciniphila, F. prausnitzii, and selected Clostridia, with documented strain-to-strain variation in metabolic capacity and immunomodulatory properties. 7 Prior to clinical deployment, such organisms require extensive assessment of potential toxic metabolites (e.g. p-cresol, biogenic amines, D-lactate), systemic translocation under barrier-compromised conditions, and horizontal transfer of antibiotic-resistance determinants.

These requirements impose substantially more stringent toxicological and safety standards than those historically applied to legacy probiotics. 8 B. fragilis exemplifies this challenge: while PSA-producing, non-toxigenic strains exhibit potent immunomodulatory properties; other lineages within the species encode enterotoxins associated with diarrhea and IBD. 36 Consequently, comprehensive genomic characterization, virulence, and antimicrobial resistance screening, pharmacokinetic evaluation, and rigorous phase I studies should be regarded as integral components of NGP development. This shift reflects a broader transition from safety inferred by historical exposure to safety established by design, and introduces uncertainty in defining acceptable risk thresholds for commensal-derived interventions.

Technological hurdles: anaerobiosis, production, and stability

A major barrier to the clinical translation of NGPs arises from a fundamental tension between ecological relevance and manufacturability. Many high-value NGP candidates are strict anaerobes, whose biological functions depend on extreme oxygen sensitivity, rendering large-scale production, formulation, and long-term stability technically demanding. 88

Exposure to oxygen during standard fermentation, processing or storage can rapidly compromise viability and functional integrity, complicating downstream steps such as lyophilisation, encapsulation, and shelf-life maintenance. These constraints are particularly pronounced for F. prausnitzii, defined Clostridia consortia, and other specialized commensals.

A range of technological strategies can mitigate these limitations, including anaerobic closed-system manufacturing, controlled-atmosphere processing, microencapsulation, co-formulation with oxygen-scavenging or metabolically complementary strains, and the use of inactivated cells or purified microbial products. While such approaches can improve stability and delivery, they often introduce additional complexity, costs, and regulatory uncertainty, limiting their immediate scalability.

Collectively, NGP development is constrained not simply by engineering challenges, but by the intrinsic difficulty of preserving ecologically and functionally specialized organisms outside their native niche. This reinforces the need for parallel investment in alternative delivery formats and microbiota therapeutics that decouple biological function from strict dependence on live-cell viability. Hence, simple adaptation of existing manufacturing pipelines may be insufficient, and fundamentally new production paradigms may be required for functionally specialized microbiota-based therapeutics. Regulatory fragmentation and classification gaps

Translational research on NGPs is further limited by regulatory fragmentation and, more fundamentally, by the absence of a harmonized legal definition of what constitutes a “probiotic” in the context of function-driven microbiota therapeutics. Existing regulatory frameworks were developed around traditional food-grade strains and struggle to accommodate emerging formats, such as defined microbial consortia, obligate anaerobes, and biologically active inactivated preparations. 47

The European Food and safety Agency (EFSA) applies the QPS approach, whereby novel taxa must undergo extensive assessment, including full genomic characterization, toxicological evaluation, and antimicrobial-resistance profiling. 49 By contrast, the US FDA adopts a more case-by-case model, distinguishing between GRAS status for food applications and the LBP pathway for therapeutic claims. However, dedicated guidelines for multi-strain NGPs, consortia or postbiotic formulations are limited, particularly as candidates progress toward clinical development and regulatory approval, where clear classification, and evidentiary frameworks become increasingly important. 89

Importantly, these discrepancies reflect not only jurisdictional differences but a deeper misalignment between regulatory categories and the emerging function-first logic. Many next-generation candidates cannot be readily classified as foods, supplements or conventional biologics, creating uncertainty for developers and regulators alike.

This lack of harmonization delays clinical translation, increases development costs, and complicates cross-jurisdictional marketing. Addressing these gaps will require regulatory frameworks that recognize microbiota-based interventions as modular, function-defined therapeutics rather than products defined primarily by taxonomic identity or historical use. At present, regulatory uncertainty does not merely slow development but may actively shape which candidates are prioritized, favouring those that fit existing frameworks over those with potentially greater functional relevance.

Clinical heterogeneity and the need for functional biomarkers

Clinical responses to NGPs display substantial inter-individual variability, reflecting differences in baseline microbiota configuration, immune tone, and environmental exposure. However, a major contributor to this heterogeneity is the continued reliance on symptomendpoints that are poorly aligned with targeted biological functions. 90 , 91

A central limitation of current trial design is the lack of validated predictive and pharmacodynamic biomarkers capable of capturing target engagement and mechanistic activity. Emerging longitudinal multi-omics studies are beginning to address this gap by integrating functional read-outs, including fecal metabolomics, secondary bile acid profiles, and mucosal transcriptomic signatures, as tools for patient stratification and response monitoring. 92 The Sensi-Biome study provides proof of concept that stratification based on bowel-pattern phenotypes can improve clinical outcomes and link increased abundance of SCFA-producing taxa to symptom reduction.

These observations point again to the fact that function-aligned endpoints and biomarker-guided stratification are prerequisites for rational evaluation of NGPs. Future trials will need to combine conventional clinical outcomes with multi-omics and system-level biomarkers, supported by integrative analytical frameworks, to enable biologically informed cohort selection and more reliable estimation of therapeutic efficacy.

Future progress will depend not only on integrating multi-omics and clinical data, but on redefining how efficacy is conceptualized, moving from symptom-based evaluation toward demonstrable modulation of disease-relevant pathways.

Discussion

The evidence reviewed here suggests a fundamental mismatch between the ecological and functional complexity of host-microbiota interactions in dysbiosis and allergic disease and the largely empirical framework that has historically guided probiotic development. While traditional Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains offer modest benefits in selected contexts, their narrow functional specificity, transient engraftment, and variable clinical effects constrain their relevance under chronic, heterogeneous conditions. It remains unclear to what extent this mismatch can be fully resolved through function-driven strategies alone, given the inherent variability and context-dependence of microbiota-host interactions.

Incremental optimization strategies, including multi-strain formulations, synbiotics, and improved delivery systems, can enhance performance at the margins but do not resolve the structural limitations of the legacy probiotic paradigm. In contrast, NGPs have been proposed as a conceptual shift toward function-driven, precision microbiota interventions that target defined biological processes, such as epithelial barrier integrity, immune tolerance induction, and metabolite-mediated host signaling. At present, their clinical translation remains contingent on demonstrating reproducible context-independent effects in well-defined patient populations.

Emerging candidates, including A. muciniphila, F. prausnitzii, spore-forming Clostridia consortia, and PSA-producing B. fragilis showcase this approach. However, their translation into practice is held back by safety-by-design, manufacturing feasibility, regulatory classification, and clinical evaluation. While these candidates illustrate mechanistic plausibility, their clinical relevance remains to be established by early-phase or context-specific studies.

Progress in this field will depend not only on advances in regulatory frameworks and trial design, but also on a more critical evaluation of whether current conceptual models adequately capture the causal pathways linking microbiota function to clinical outcomes. Together, these advances are likely to be important in pushing microbiota-based therapeutics toward durable, evidence-based interventions capable of delivering meaningful benefit in dysbiosis and allergic disease.
