RNF186 controls glucose metabolism in metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease
Authors: Jiang Du, Qizhang Du, Yuxuan Zhang, Jingwen Gao, Chiheng Wang, Beilin Kou, Yan Jiang, Dunyong Zhao, Juntang Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsRNF186, which encodes a ring-finger domain-containing E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase, has previously been implicated in the regulation of lipid metabolic disorders associated with metabolic dysfunction-related fatty liver disease (MAFLD). However, the precise mechanism by which RNF186 influences glucose metabolism in the context of MAFLD remains unclear. In this study, we aimed to elucidate the role of RNF186 in the regulation of glucose metabolism, with a particular focus on skeletal muscle.MethodsIn vitro, we treated skeletal myocytes and hepatocytes with high glucose concentrations to study the expression of RNF186 and its effects on glucose uptake and insulin signaling. In vivo, we developed a MAFLD model through long-term high-fat feeding and assessed the impact of RNF186 deficiency on glucose metabolism in skeletal muscle, liver and adipose tissue using Western blotting, quantitative PCR (qPCR), and immunofluorescence.ResultsOur findings demonstrate that RNF186 is regulated by glucose concentration in skeletal muscle cells and hepatocytes and is sensitive to insulin in a high-glucose environment. The deletion of RNF186 increases glucose metabolism and alleviates insulin signaling disruption in the MAFLD model, affecting skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tissue. Furthermore, in skeletal muscle, RNF186 deficiency reduces the ER stress-mediated unfolded protein response (UPR) by preventing the ubiquitination of ATF6, leading to increased transcription of GLUT4. Additionally, RNF186 deficiency promotes the membrane translocation of GLUT4 via the AKT/TBC1D4 signaling pathway. In contrast, overexpression of RNF186 decreases AKT signaling and GLUT4 expression, resulting in exacerbated disruption of glucose metabolism in MAFLD.ConclusionsRNF186 regulates glucose metabolism across multiple tissues in MAFLD, notably by playing a dual role in modulating the transcription and translocation of GLUT4 in skeletal muscle. These findings suggest that targeting the expression of RNF186 could be a potential therapeutic strategy for treating MAFLD and related metabolic disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01507-6.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, RNF186, Glucose metabolism, Skeletal muscle, GLUT4

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), previously known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), affects approximately 38% of adults worldwide, and its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood [1]. MAFLD can progress to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), which may subsequently develop into liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma [2]. Despite significant advancements in the understanding of the pathogenesis of MAFLD and the development of drug targets in recent decades, only one drug has been approved by the FDA for the treatment of adult patients with MAFLD [3]. MAFLD is often associated with glucose metabolism disorders in multiple organs [4]. Therefore, analyzing the underlying causes of these abnormalities is essential for the effective treatment of this disease.

Insulin is produced and released by pancreatic beta cells, which precisely regulate glucose uptake from the bloodstream into the liver, adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle [5, 6]. This regulation is essential for mediating systemic glucose metabolism across various tissues. In addition to its role in maintaining glucose homeostasis, insulin also influences a range of biological processes, including glycogen synthesis, lipid metabolism, gene transcription, and protein synthesis and degradation [5]. The AKT pathway is essential for the regulation of insulin-mediated glucose metabolism. AKT facilitates glycogen synthesis by phosphorylating GSK3β and modulating the expression of genes related to gluconeogenesis through the phosphorylation of FoxO1 [7, 8]. In MAFLD, insulin resistance and abnormalities in insulin-related signaling occur across multiple organs, resulting in widespread disruptions in glucose metabolism.

RNF186 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase localized to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where it plays a role in regulating ER stress. In mouse primary hepatocytes, overexpression of RNF186 increases the protein levels of ER stress sensors and modulates insulin sensitivity [9]. Conversely, specific knockout of RNF186 in mouse hepatocytes leads to reduced ER stress, resulting in improvements in the metabolic abnormalities associated with MAFLD [10]. Our recent studies revealed that RNF186 is significantly upregulated in the livers of MAFLD model mice and regulates glucose and lipid metabolism via the AMPK–mTOR signaling pathway [11]. Furthermore, our findings demonstrate that RNF186 mediates lipophagy by ubiquitinating cytoplasmic HMGB1, thereby influencing the progression of MAFLD [12]. These data suggest that RNF186 is involved in glucose metabolism in the liver in the context of MAFLD.

In addition to liver tissue, skeletal muscle and adipose tissue play significant roles in glucose metabolism in MAFLD [13]. Insulin resistance in skeletal muscle is one of the earliest indicators of MAFLD development [14]. The prolonged consumption of an HFD leads to an increase in ectopic lipids within skeletal muscle, which contributes to insulin resistance [15]. This resistance impairs insulin signaling, resulting in decreased insulin-stimulated glucose transport and muscle glycogen synthesis. When glucose cannot be properly stored as glycogen in muscle, it is redirected to the liver, where it promotes lipid accumulation and contributes to the pathogenesis of MAFLD [16]. GLUT4, the primary glucose transporter in muscle and adipose tissues, is regulated by AKT signaling and ER stress, making it a critical component of glucose metabolism [17, 18]. However, the mechanisms by which RNF186 contributes to systemic glucose metabolism disorders, particularly in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue in MAFLD, and the specific roles of ER stress and GLUT4 remain unclear.

In this study, we investigated the regulatory role of RNF186 in glucose metabolism associated with MAFLD in skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tissue. We focused specifically on the mechanism through which RNF186 regulates GLUT4 expression and its membrane transport in skeletal muscle. Consequently, RNF186 may represent a potential therapeutic target for addressing glucose metabolism disorders in individuals with MAFLD.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

Human HepG2 cells and mouse C2C12 myoblasts were obtained from Procell (China) and cultured in DMEM (Hyclone) containing 4.5 g/L glucose, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). After 16 h, the medium was replaced with starvation medium containing 2.5, 7.5, 15, or 25 mM glucose, supplemented with 0.5% (v/v) FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), until further treatment. 2-Deoxyglucose (2-DG) (Sigma #D8375) was dissolved in DMSO and added to the starvation medium at the specified concentration. Cells were harvested after 16 h of treatment. All cell lines were maintained in a cell culture incubator at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere and saturated humidity.

Primary myoblast isolation

Skeletal muscle from the forelimbs and hindlimbs of mice was isolated separately. Primary myoblasts were cultured in Ham F-10 medium (Procell) at 37 °C with 5% CO2, supplemented with 10% bovine serum (HyClone) and 5 ng/ml fibroblast growth factor.

Animals and treatment

Male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were obtained from Charles River (Beijing, China), while ob/ob mice (4 weeks old) were sourced from Skobes Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-hour light-dark cycle. To model MAFLD, the mice were fed a HFD (P23300, Trophic, Jiangsu, China), which comprised 19.4% protein, 60% fat, and 20.6% carbohydrates. Mice fed a NCD (LAD3001M, Trophic, Jiangsu, China) served as the control group. RNF186 knockout (KO) mice were generated by Cyagen Biosciences Inc. using CRISPR-Cas9 technology and were included in prior studies [19, 20]. All mice were sacrificed after 20 weeks of feeding on either the NCD or HFD, and serum and biochemical analyses were conducted. RNF186 expression was measured in the ob/ob mice after 12 weeks, using lean mice as controls. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinxiang Medical University (No. XXLL-20220059), ensuring compliance with animal welfare and ethical standards.

siRNA transfection

To investigate the roles of RNF186, specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting RNF186 was synthesized by Han Heng Biotechnology. The siRNA sequences used were as follows: si-human-RNF186 (5′-CGAUGGGUGCUCACCUUCA-3′) and si-mouse-RNF186 (5′-GAAGAACAGGACACUGUAA-3′). siRNA transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Invitrogen™) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h, the cells were harvested for subsequent experiments.

Glucose uptake analysis

Glucose uptake in C2C12 and HepG2 cells was assessed using the 2-NBDG method (Sigma). Briefly, C2C12 and HepG2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured in H-DMEM for 12 h. The medium was then replaced with starvation medium containing either 5.5 mM or 25 mM glucose for 24 h. To evaluate the effect of RNF186 on insulin sensitivity, the cells were treated with or without insulin (100 nM) for 30 min. Subsequently, 2-NBDG (100 µM) was added, and the cells were incubated for 1 h. Following this incubation, the cells were collected and examine them under a microscope (Leica). Simultaneously, prepare the cells for flow cytometry analysis. Use the group without 2-NBDG as a negative control and set appropriate gates. For fluorescence detection, use the FITC channel to measure the fluorescence signals of the cells. The percentage of 2-NBDG-positive cells is determined based on the fluorescence intensity. The y-axis represents the proportion of 2-NBDG-positive cells relative to the total number of cells.

Histological analysis

Liver, skeletal muscle, and gWAT sections were embedded in paraffin and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to visualize lipid accumulation patterns and assess the inflammatory status of the tissues. Additionally, the paraffin-embedded liver sections were subjected to periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining to evaluate glycogen content.

Blood glucose, insulin, and glycogen determination

Fasting blood glucose and serum insulin levels were measured using a blood glucose meter and an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Millipore, USA), respectively. Glycogen levels in the liver and skeletal muscle were assessed using 100 mg of liver tissue, following the protocol outlined in the glycogen determination kit (BC0345, Solarbio).

Immunofluorescence staining

Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h and then embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound. Cultured cells were also fixed with 4% PFA. Both the tissue sections and cultured cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h. After blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with diluted primary antibodies against RNF186 (orb1617, Biorbyt), ATF6 (66563-1-lg, Proteintech), GLUT4 (66846-1-lg, Proteintech), and ACTN4 (A9096-1-AP, Proteintech). Following washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The secondary antibodies used were fluorescein-conjugated: CoraLite488-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (#SA00013-2, Proteintech), CoraLite594-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (#SA00013-4, Proteintech), and CoraLite594-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (#SA00013-3, Proteintech). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Nikon C2).

qPCR and western blot analysis

For qPCR assays, total RNA was isolated from tissue samples or cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (#W9514, Tiangen). Two micrograms of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using the Reverse Transcription Kit (#PIA279, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR experiments were conducted using a CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, C1000) with SYBR Green real-time PCR master mix (G891, abm). The mRNA expression levels of the target genes were normalized to Gapdh or actin expression. The primer pairs used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

For Western blot analyses, total protein samples were isolated from tissue or cell samples by treating them with RIPA lysis buffer (#01408, CWBio) containing protease inhibitor (#01392, CWBio) and phosphatase inhibitor (#01385, CWBio). Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (#BCA02, Dingguo). Proteins were electrophoresed through polyacrylamide gels and then transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in PBS for 1 h and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Afterward, they were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (#abs20040, Absin). Antibodies were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence reagent. Protein expression levels were quantified using Image Lab software and normalized to β-tubulin, GAPDH and β-actin levels, which served as loading controls.

Antibodies

Primary antibodies specific for the following proteins were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology: AKT (#4691), p-AKT (Ser473) (#4060), p-GSK3β (#9322), GSK3β (#12456), Fasn (#3180), GCK (#3782), SCD1 (#2794), IRS-1 (#2382), p-IRS-1 (#2388), PI3K (#4292), p-PI3K (#4288), FoxO1 (#2880), p-FoxO1 (#9461), JNK (#9252), p-JNK (#4668), GS (#3886), Ubiqutin (#3936), and HKII (#2867), p-eIF2α (#3398), eIF2α (#9722). The primary antibody for RNF186 (orb1617) was obtained from Biorbyt. Antibodies for IRE1 (DF7709), p-IRE1(AF7150), PERK (AF5304), p-PERK (AF4499), AMPKα (AF6423) and p-AMPKα (AF3423) were sourced from Affinity. Additional primary antibodies, including β-tubulin (11224-1-A), β-actin (66009-1-Ig), GAPDH (10494-1-AP), Flag (20543–1-AP), ATF6 (66563-1-lg), GLUT4 (66846-1-lg), ACTN4 (A9096-1-AP), CHOP (15204-1-AP), XBP1 (83959-5-RR), Bip (11587-1-AP), and PPARγ (16643-1-AP) were obtained from Proteintech. All primary antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:1000. Secondary antibodies utilized in this study included goat anti-rabbit IgG (#abs20040) and goat anti-mouse IgG (#abs20039), both obtained from Absin. Secondary antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:3000.

Mouse adeno-associated virus 8 (AAV8) construction and injection

The AAV8 delivery system was employed to overexpress RNF186 in mouse liver and skeletal muscle. The RNF186 sequence (lacking a stop codon) was chemically synthesized and cloned into the pAAV-CAG-GFP vector, replacing the GFP-encoding gene. The pAAV-CAG-GFP vector served as a control. The AAV8 vector was produced by transfecting three plasmids into 293 A cells: the pAAV containing RNF186-T2A-GFP, a pAAV2/8-trans plasmid that provides the AAV rep and cap genes, and a pAd helper plasmid. The titers of the vector genomes were quantified using qPCR with vector-specific primers. Mice were then injected with 100 µL of the virus, containing 2 × 1011 AAV8 vector genomes, via the tail vein.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and ubiquitination analysis

In this study, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays were performed to investigate protein-protein interactions. 293T cells were co-transfected with the specified plasmids, followed by lysis in cold immunoprecipitation buffer. The resulting cell lysates were subjected to immunoprecipitation using appropriate antibodies to capture the target proteins. After washing the immunoprecipitated complexes with NaCl wash buffer to remove non-specifically bound proteins, the proteins were eluted from the beads using SDS loading buffer. The eluted proteins were then analyzed by immunoblotting to assess the presence and interactions of the proteins of interest.

To analyze ubiquitination, 293T cells were co-transfected with the specified plasmids and lysed in cold IP lysis buffer containing 1% SDS. The lysates were diluted tenfold with IP lysis buffer, centrifuged, and then immunoprecipitated using anti-DYKDDDDK magnetic beads, which specifically capture proteins tagged with the DYKDDDDK epitope. The immunoprecipitates were subsequently boiled to release the proteins from the beads and subjected to immunoblot analysis to evaluate ubiquitination levels.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted independently and repeated at least three times. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software). The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. If the data were not normally distributed, the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test was employed for comparing multiple groups. Conversely, if the data were found to be normally distributed, the Brown-Forsythe test was used to assess homogeneity of variance. For pairwise comparisons, a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was employed. To assess differences between groups, one-way ANOVA was applied, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with the following categorizations for P values: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.005, ***P < 0.001. Data are presented as the arithmetic mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

RNF186 in skeletal muscle cells and hepatocytes is modulated by glucose metabolism

Our previous research indicated that the deletion of RNF186 can improve the dysregulation of glucose and lipid metabolism associated with MAFLD [11, 12]. The liver, skeletal muscle, and adipose tissue are key organs involved in the disturbances in glucose metabolism under these conditions. In the present study, we found that RNF186 is expressed not only in the kidneys and intestines but also prominently in the skeletal muscle and liver, with comparatively low expression levels in gonadal white adipose tissue (gWAT) and the brain (Fig. 1A). Additionally, we observed that under high-glucose conditions, both the protein and RNA levels of RNF186 increased in mouse C2C12 cells and human HepG2 cells (Fig. 1B, C and Fig. S1A, B). Conversely, treatment with 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG), which inhibits glucose metabolism, resulted in a decrease in RNF186 expression (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1C). Furthermore, we found that RNF186 expression in mouse primary myoblasts also responded to varying glucose concentrations (Fig. 1E).

To further investigate the relationship between RNF186 expression levels and cellular glucose metabolism in vivo, we used qPCR to measure RNF186 expression in mice fed either a normal chow diet (NCD) or an HFD to induce the MAFLD model. Our results indicated that RNF186 expression was elevated in the liver and skeletal muscle of MAFLD model mice, whereas no significant changes were detected in the gWAT or kidney (Fig. 1F). Additionally, we assessed the protein expression of RNF186 in the skeletal muscle of MAFLD model mice and detected elevated levels compared with those in controls fed a normal diet (Fig. 1G and Fig. S1D). Moreover, RNF186 expression in the skeletal muscle tissue of ob/ob mice was significantly greater than that observed in lean mice (Fig. 1G and Fig. S1D). Moreover, RNF186 expression in the skeletal muscle tissue of ob/ob mice was significantly greater than that observed in lean mice.

RNF186 is sensitive to insulin stimulation in a high-glucose environment

MAFLD is commonly associated with hyperglycemia and elevated serum insulin levels [21]. To better replicate this characteristic in vitro, we treated mouse C2C12 cells and human HepG2 cells with insulin at varying glucose concentrations. Our findings indicate that insulin stimulation does not increase RNF186 expression in muscle cells or hepatocytes treated with 5.5 mM (low) glucose. In contrast, insulin stimulation significantly increased RNF186 expression in cells treated with 25 mM (high) glucose (Fig. 2A and Fig. S2A). Additionally, we employed 2-NBDG staining to assess the glucose uptake capacity of skeletal muscle cells and hepatocytes. The results indicated that insulin stimulation significantly increased glucose uptake. Notably, the absence of RNF186 led to markedly greater glucose uptake capacity in C2C12 and HepG2 cells in response to high-glucose treatment compared to that observed in response to low-glucose treatment (Fig. 2B, C and Fig. S2B, C).

AKT serves as a critical downstream effector of insulin signaling, mediating various metabolic processes that are essential for maintaining glucose homeostasis. We found that under low-glucose conditions, there were no significant differences in the activation of phosphorylated AKT and GSK3β between normal C2C12 and HepG2 cells and those with RNF186 knockdown. However, in cells treated with high glucose, compared with normal cells, RNF186 knockdown resulted in more pronounced stimulation of AKT and GSK3β phosphorylation (Fig. 2D, E and Fig. S2D, E). These findings suggest that RNF186 plays a regulatory role in the insulin-mediated AKT signaling pathway in a high-glucose environment.

Loss of RNF186 increases hepatic glycogen synthesis and activates the insulin-mediated AKT signaling pathway in MAFLD model mice

In diseases associated with hyperglycemia, the ability to clear glucose from the bloodstream declines as insulin resistance progressively increases. This reduction in glucose clearance efficiency leads to elevated blood glucose levels, which poses a significant challenge to glucose metabolism [22]. Our previous research demonstrated that the global loss of RNF186 protects mice from HFD-induced MAFLD and associated metabolic dysfunction [11, 12]. In this study, we found that 20 weeks of HFD feeding significantly elevated fasting blood glucose and insulin levels in mice compared to those on an NCD. Notably, the deletion of RNF186 was shown to significantly mitigate these increases induced by HFD feeding (Fig. 3A, B).

In both wild-type (WT) and RNF186 knockout (KO) mice, minimal changes in hepatic lipid accumulation and glycogen content were detected when the mice were fed an NCD. However, after 20 weeks on an HFD, the glycogen content in the livers of RNF186 KO mice was significantly greater than that in the control group, and lipid accumulation was reduced (Fig. 3C). Consistent with expectations, the mRNA levels of the gluconeogenesis-related genes G6pc and Pgc1-α were reduced in RNF186-KO mice compared with control mice (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, we observed that canonical insulin signaling was significantly elevated in the livers of RNF186 KO mice, as indicated by increased phosphorylation of AKT at S473, AMPK at T172, and GSK3β at S9, along with decreased phosphorylation of IRS1 at S633/639 (Fig. 3E). These findings suggest that the loss of RNF186 increases hepatic glycogen synthesis, inhibits gluconeogenesis, and activates the insulin-mediated AKT signaling pathway in the liver.

MAFLD leads to glucose intolerance and insulin resistance in various organs, including gWAT [10]. Additionally, MAFLD and metabolic dysregulation can arise from an imbalance between fat uptake, synthesis, and catabolism in WAT [23]. Histological analysis revealed an increase in the number of lipid droplets and acidophilic adipocytes in the gWAT of RNF186 KO mice (Fig. 3F). We also identified changes in key regulatory factors involved in lipogenesis and insulin signaling. In the gWAT of RNF186 KO mice, the expressions of proteins that regulate lipogenesis, such as SCD1 and Fasn, were significantly downregulated, whereas the expressions of insulin-stimulated phosphorylated proteins, including AKT and GSK3β, were markedly upregulated (Fig. 3G). These findings suggest that RNF186 depletion influences glucose metabolism by increasing AKT signaling in gWAT.

RNF186 deficiency increases glycogen synthesis, inhibits gluconeogenesis, reduces ER stress, and increases AKT signaling in skeletal muscle

Skeletal muscle is the primary tissue responsible for glucose metabolism in the body, and insulin resistance in skeletal muscle plays a significant role in the development of MAFLD [24]. We therefore examined the glycogen content in skeletal muscle tissue. The results indicated that glycogen levels were elevated in RNF186 KO mice compared with those in control mice (Fig. 4A, B). Furthermore, compared with those in control mice, the mRNA levels of the gluconeogenesis-related genes pepck, G6pc and Pgc1-α were reduced in RNF186-KO mice (Fig. 4C).

In addition, we observed significantly increased activity of the classical insulin signaling pathway in the skeletal muscles of RNF186 KO mice, as evidenced by increased phosphorylation levels of AKT, AMPK, and GSK3β, along with decreased phosphorylation of IRS1 (Fig. 4D, E). Considering the important role of RNF186 in ER stress, we examined its status in skeletal muscle. We found that the expressions of phosphorylated IRE1, PERK, and eIF2α were significantly downregulated in the skeletal muscle of the KO MAFLD model mice, suggesting that the loss of RNF186 reduces ER stress in skeletal muscle (Fig. 4F, G). These findings indicate that the global loss of RNF186 enhances glycogen synthesis, inhibits gluconeogenesis, reduces ER stress, and increases AKT signaling in skeletal muscle.

Loss of RNF186 inhibits the ATF6-mediated UPR and promotes GLUT4 expression in skeletal muscle by preventing the ubiquitination of ATF6

GLUT4 is an essential protein responsible for transporting glucose into cells, particularly in muscle tissue. It plays a vital role in maintaining glucose homeostasis and is regulated by insulin [25]. We examined the effect of RNF186 on Glut4 transcription levels in the C2C12 muscle cell line. Our results indicated that under both low-glucose and high-glucose conditions, knockdown of RNF186 led to an increase in Glut4 mRNA expression (Fig. 5A). These findings suggest that the absence of RNF186 increases Glut4 transcription in muscle tissue.

Various biological processes, including ER stress, can influence the expression of glucose transporters and impact glucose metabolism [26]. Previous studies have shown that overexpression of the ER stress-related factor ATF6 is sufficient to inhibit Glut4 gene expression [18]. We screened several potential proteins that might influence Glut4 transcription and detected a decrease in the expression of the ATF6 protein in RNF186 KO mice with HFD-induced MAFLD (Fig. 5B, C). Additionally, immunofluorescence experiments demonstrated that the absence of RNF186 significantly reduced ATF6 expression (Fig. 5D). RNF186 is localized to the ER, where it forms a complex with ER stress sensors and ubiquitinates the ER stress sensor ATF6, thereby promoting the ER stress-mediated UPR [27]. We hypothesize that in the context of skeletal muscle glucose metabolism, RNF186 may ubiquitinate ATF6, thereby influencing the ATF6-mediated UPR and subsequently regulating GLUT4 expression. To investigate this hypothesis, we performed co-immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting experiments, which revealed that RNF186 interacts with ATF6 in muscle cells (Fig. 5E, F). Furthermore, ubiquitination assays confirmed that RNF186 ubiquitinates ATF6 (Fig. 5F). In the skeletal muscle of RNF186 KO mice, the RNA and protein levels of genes related to the ATF6-mediated UPR, such as Bip, Chop, Erdj4 and Xbp1, were significantly decreased (Fig. 5G, H). These findings suggest that RNF186 ubiquitinates ATF6 to mediate the UPR, thereby regulating GLUT4 expression in skeletal muscle.

RNF186 deficiency increases the translocation of GLUT4 in skeletal muscle

The binding of insulin to its receptors on the surface of muscle cells triggers a signaling cascade that facilitates the transport of GLUT4-containing vesicles to the cell membrane. This process increases the uptake of glucose from the bloodstream into cells, effectively lowering blood glucose levels (Fig. 6A) [28]. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that, consistent with previous findings, GLUT4 expression in skeletal muscle was increased in RNF186 KO mice (Fig. 6B). In C2C12 cells, immunofluorescence staining revealed significant localization of GLUT4 to the cell membrane following RNF186 knockdown (Fig. 6C). We subsequently investigated the expression of several downstream proteins in the AKT signaling pathway in skeletal muscle. Our results indicated that, compared with those in WT mice, the phosphorylation of FoxO1 and JNK were increased in RNF186 KO mice when fed a HFD. Notably, the phosphorylation level of TBC1D4, which facilitates GLUT4 translocation, was significantly increased. We also observed elevated protein levels of the glucose-handling proteins HKII and GS (Fig. 6D, E). These findings suggest that the absence of RNF186 promotes GLUT4 translocation and increases glucose uptake in skeletal muscle cells.

Overexpression of RNF186 inhibits glycogen synthesis, promotes ER stress, and suppresses the AKT–GLUT4 signaling pathway in skeletal muscle

To further validate the role of RNF186 in regulating glucose metabolism, we established an RNF186 overexpression mouse model by transfecting mice with adeno-associated virus 8 (AAV8). qPCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that the mRNA and protein levels of RNF186 were significantly elevated in the liver and skeletal muscle following the injection of AAV-RNF186 viral particles (Fig. S3A, B). Furthermore, IF staining confirmed that RNF186 overexpression was significantly greater after injection of the AAV-RNF186 virus than after injection of AAV-Con (Fig. S3C). Serological experiments indicated that overexpression of RNF186 increased fasting blood glucose and insulin levels in mice that were fed an HFD for 20 weeks (Fig. 7A, B). Furthermore, the overexpression of RNF186 significantly reduced hepatic glycogen synthesis while promoting gluconeogenesis, as demonstrated by the analysis of the expressions of related genes (pepck, G6pc and Pgc1-α) through qPCR (Fig. S4A, B). These findings indicate that RNF186 overexpression inhibits hepatic glycogen synthesis and increases gluconeogenesis, ultimately leading to an increased burden on liver glucose metabolism.

We subsequently measured the glycogen content in skeletal muscle tissue. The results indicated that compared with control mice, glycogen levels were significantly lower and gluconeogenesis was increased in RNF186-overexpressing mice (Fig. 7C, D). Furthermore, we found that RNF186 overexpression promoted the expression of ATF6 while inhibiting the expression of GLUT4, as demonstrated by immunofluorescence and Western blot analyses (Fig. 7E, F, I). Additionally, we assessed the phosphorylation levels of key proteins downstream of insulin signaling. The results revealed that the phosphorylation levels of AKT and GSK3β were decreased in mice with RNF186 overexpression, and the phosphorylation levels of FoxO1 and JNK also decreased. Moreover, the expressions of glucose transport and handling-related proteins, including HKII, GS, and phosphorylated TBC1D4, were downregulated (Fig. 7G).

Furthermore, we examined the expressions of ER stress-related molecules in the skeletal muscle of RNF186-overexpressing mice (Fig. 7H). Interestingly, we found that the phosphorylation levels of IRE1, PERK, ATF6 and eIF2α were elevated (Fig. 7I). These findings suggest that the RNF186-ATF6 interaction may specifically influence GLUT4-mediated glucose metabolism in skeletal muscle. Collectively, these data suggest that in skeletal muscle, RNF186 overexpression inhibits glycogen synthesis, promotes gluconeogenesis, increases the expression of ER stress genes, and suppresses the AKT-GLUT4 signaling pathway.

Discussion

Global RNF186 gene KO mice can grow to adulthood and maintain reproductive capacity. Although these mice are relatively small in size, there are no significant defects in their organs. However, under certain stress conditions, such as DSS-induced inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), RNF186 exhibits regulatory functions [19, 29]. In the context of metabolic diseases, RNF186 can regulate hepatic lipid metabolism in MAFLD through ER stress and lipophagy [9–11, 28]. Glucose metabolism dysfunction is another important characteristic of MAFLD and involves abnormalities in liver, muscle, and adipose tissues [30, 31]. In this study, we utilized RNF186 KO mice to comprehensively demonstrate the regulatory role of RNF186 in glucose metabolism dysregulation in MAFLD and to explore its potential mechanisms.

Insulin regulates glucose metabolism in tissues such as the muscle and liver through the IRS-1/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [32–34]. In hepatocytes and skeletal muscle cells, we found that RNF186 responds to glucose concentrations and is more sensitive to insulin in high-glucose environments. We utilized glucose concentrations of 5.5 mM and 25 mM to represent low- and high-glucose conditions, respectively, and treated the cells with 100 nM insulin for 30 min to activate the insulin signaling pathway. Although these concentrations exceed physiological levels, 5 mM and 25 mM glucose are commonly used to study changes in gene expression patterns [35]. Treatment with 100 nM insulin has been shown to activate the AKT and FoxO1 signaling pathways in cells, while 10 nM insulin is insufficient to activate this pathway [36, 37]. Since cells are removed from the complex in vivo microenvironment, supraphysiological concentrations of compounds may be necessary to activate certain signaling pathways. To address the limitations of these in vitro concentrations, we used HFD-induced MAFLD model mice to investigate the regulatory role of RNF186 in insulin-mediated AKT signaling in the liver, skeletal muscle, and adipose tissue. Using global RNF186 KO mice, this study demonstrated that the absence of RNF186 promotes activity of the IRS1/PI3K/AKT/GSK3β and AMPKα signaling pathways in the liver and skeletal muscle and increases the activity of the AKT/GSK3β pathway in gWAT. Furthermore, loss of RNF186 promotes the phosphorylation of FoxO1 in skeletal muscle tissue. In its phosphorylated state, FoxO1 is translocated to the cytoplasm, where it is degraded, leading to the downregulation of gluconeogenesis-related genes such as pepck and G6pc [38, 39]. Conversely, overexpression of RNF186 significantly inhibits the phosphorylation of FoxO1, thereby promoting gluconeogenesis. These data also provide potential mechanistic insights into how RNF186 and FoxO1 regulate gluconeogenesis.

RNF186 is localized to the ER and may regulate ER stress in various tissues. We found that RNF186 can regulate both the expression and the translocation of GLUT4. Mechanistically, RNF186 can bind to and ubiquitinate the ER-associated protein ATF6, thereby regulating downstream UPR signaling and influencing GLUT4 expression [18, 27]. Moreover, the absence of RNF186 activates the AKT signaling pathway, promoting an increase in the phosphorylation level of TBC1D4. Increased phosphorylation of TBC1D4 leads to increased expression of GLUT4 on the cell membrane, as well as upregulation of glucose-handling proteins such as HKII and GS [40]. We also focused on ACTN4, which has been reported to bind with GLUT4 during its transport to the cell membrane via vesicles, thereby assisting in GLUT4 translocation [41, 42]. Our research revealed that after the overexpression of RNF186, the binding between ACTN4 and GLUT4 significantly decreased. This reduction may provide an alternative explanation for how RNF186 deficiency stimulates GLUT4 translocation (Fig. S5). These findings suggest that there may be additional mechanisms underlying the regulation of GLUT4 by RNF186, which require further investigation.

The use of RNF186 KO mice to elucidate the molecular mechanisms regulating glucose metabolism has limitations, as it does not eliminate potential interorgan interactions. In the liver, hepatocyte-specific knockout of RNF186 has a limited effect on the regulation of gluconeogenic genes. However, the significant changes in gluconeogenic gene expression observed in global RNF186 KO mice suggest that the regulation of gluconeogenesis in these mice is at least partially dependent on interorgan interactions. Given that RNF186 is expressed primarily in the intestine, kidneys, liver, and muscle and is expressed at relatively low levels in adipose tissue, we speculate that changes in insulin signaling in muscle tissue are related to the absence of the RNF186 gene and the interactions between liver and muscle tissues. Conversely, changes in insulin signaling in WAT seem to rely more strongly on interorgan interactions. These findings indicate that RNF186 not only regulates insulin signaling in skeletal muscle and liver tissues but also influences the functions of liver and adipose tissue through interorgan interactions. Mutations at the genomic level in RNF186 are associated with increased susceptibility to diseases such as IBD [29, 43]. These findings suggest that single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within the RNF186 gene may confer susceptibility to various diseases under different environmental stimuli. Considering that glucose metabolism in MAFLD involves complex interactions among multiple tissues, future research could focus on how polymorphisms in the RNF186 gene influence individual susceptibility to metabolic diseases. The use of RNF186 KO mice allows us to better simulate the long-term effects of this gene knockout on the development of HFD-induced MAFLD. However, at the molecular mechanism level, systemic KO does not clearly distinguish whether the observed phenotypes are due to autonomous gene regulation or interorgan interactions.

Previous reports have confirmed the high expression of RNF186 in liver samples from patients with MAFLD [12]. However, we did not perform similar experiments using skeletal muscle cells from MAFLD patients, as the difficulty in obtaining these samples is one reason for the lack of translational value of this research. Future clinical studies should focus on the potential association between RNF186 expression levels in skeletal muscle cells and the glucose metabolism status of MAFLD patients. By analyzing the expression of RNF186 in the liver and skeletal muscle of patients at different stages of MAFLD, we may uncover its potential role as a biomarker in disease progression. Furthermore, given the important role of RNF186 in glucose and fatty acid metabolism in MAFLD, future research could explore the development of small-molecule drugs, gene editing technologies, or RNA interference targeting RNF186 to improve abnormalities in glucose and lipid metabolism, thereby alleviating the pathological state of MAFLD.

In summary, our study provides insights into how RNF186 deficiency improves multiorgan glucose metabolism disruptions in MAFLD model mice. Notably, the absence of RNF186 increases glucose metabolism in the skeletal muscle of MAFLD model mice, promoting the expression and membrane translocation of GLUT4 (Fig. S6). These findings offer valuable evidence for a deeper understanding of how RNF186 regulates glucose metabolism in the context of MAFLD.
