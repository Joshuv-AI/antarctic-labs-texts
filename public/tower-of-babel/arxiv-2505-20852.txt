Optimal control of a two-dimensional elliptic equation with exponential nonlinearity and Dirac measure data
Vu Huu Nhu

Abstract
This work addresses an optimal control problem for a semilinear elliptic equation in two-dimensional space, characterized by an exponential nonlinearity and a singular source term. The source is modeled as a finite linear combination of Dirac measures concentrated at a fixed set of distinct points. The control variable is a finite-dimensional vector whose components represent the masses assigned to these point sources. Due to the interplay between the exponential nonlinearity and the singular measure data, the state equation is generally ill-posed and admits a unique very weak solution only when the largest component of the control vector does not surpass a certain critical threshold. Consequently, the control-to-state operator might be continuously differentiable only on an open subset of the control space. To derive first-order optimality conditions for the original problem, we introduce a family of regularized problems by imposing box constraints on the control variables. These constraints are chosen such that the admissible control sets of the regularized problems lie entirely within the open subset where the control-to-state operator is smooth. By analyzing the optimality systems associated with the regularized problems and passing to the limit, we obtain necessary optimality conditions for the original, unregularized problem.

Introduction

The study of elliptic partial differential equations with exponential nonlinearities and singular data is a rich area at the intersection of analysis, geometry, and mathematical physics. One important example is the Liouville-type equation that arises naturally in the context of prescribing Gaussian curvature, conformal metrics, and mean field models; see, e.g.
[
12
,
3
,
25
]
.
In
[
12
]
, Chen and Lin examined a generalized mean field equation on a compact Riemann surface, incorporating exponential nonlinearities and singular source terms represented by the Dirac delta function.

The formulation of the control problem in a measure space is motivated by the fact that the resulting optimal controls naturally exhibit sparsity, in the sense that they are supported on small subsets. This feature is especially advantageous in applications such as the optimal placement of sensors or actuators; see, e.g.,
[
10
,
15
]
, as well as in the context of inverse problems involving the identification of Dirac impulses; see, e.g.,
[
22
]
. Although similar sparsity-promoting effects can be achieved by incorporating
L^{1}
-norm control costs, such formulations generally do not guarantee the existence of minimizers due to the lack of compactness in
L^{1}
-spaces. This limitation is effectively addressed by adopting a measure-space framework, where the control cost is defined in terms of measures.

For optimal control problems governed by elliptic PDEs within the measure-space framework, we refer to
[
10
,
11
,
15
,
18
]
. In
[
11
]
, Casas and Kunisch derived second-order necessary and sufficient optimality conditions, as well as results on the stability of solutions, for problems involving semilinear equations where the nonlinearity satisfies a polynomial growth condition. The work
[
10
]
by Casas et al. investigates first-order optimality conditions and the approximation of control problems governed by linear state equations, showing that optimal control measures can be effectively approximated by linear combinations of Dirac measures.

More recently, in
[
26
]
, a semilinear elliptic optimal control problem was considered, where the nonlinearity in the state equation is similar to that investigated in
[
11
]
, and the forcing term is modeled as a linear combination of Dirac measures. In this work, the author established both first-order and second-order (necessary and sufficient) optimality conditions, along with error estimates for discrete approximations obtained via finite element methods.

In this paper, we study an optimal control problem governed by a two-dimensional elliptic partial differential equation with an exponential nonlinearity. The source term in the state equation consists of the sum of an
L^{p}
-function with
p>1
, and a measure represented as a linear combination of Dirac measures concentrated at fixed, distinct points.
The presence of the exponential nonlinearity combined with the singular measure data makes the analysis of the state equation particularly challenging. In general, the existence of a weak solution to the state equation requires certain smallness conditions on the positive masses of the control, as large values can lead to blow-up or loss of well-posedness.
As a result, the control-to-state mapping is defined only on a subset of the control space and is continuously differentiable only on another open subset thereof; see
Theorem

4.1
below.

The aim of this paper is to derive optimality conditions for the considered optimal control problem. To this end, we adopt a regularization approach by introducing a family of regularized problems in which box constraints on the controls are additionally imposed. The upper bounds of these constraints are chosen to lie within the open subset on which the control-to-state mapping is continuously differentiable.
This allows us to obtain the corresponding optimality systems for the regularized problems by using standard arguments.
By subsequently passing to the limit in these systems, we obtain the desired optimality conditions for the original problem.
Our approach is similar in spirit to that used in
[
4
,
28
,
23
,
14
,
24
]
, where non-smooth state equations are approximated by smooth approximations.

The paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
, we present the problem setting and state the main results.
Section

3
is devoted to auxiliary estimates for solutions to linear elliptic equations with Dirac measure data, as well as to the Gâteaux differentiability of the Nemytskii operator associated with the exponential nonlinearity. These results are applied in
Section

4
to establish the continuous differentiability of the control-to-state mapping on an open subset of the control space. The proofs of the main results are provided in
Section

5
. The paper concludes with an appendix containing estimates for Green’s function on the two-dimensional unit ball and a proof of the openness of certain subsets in the measure space and in a Sobolev space.

2
Problem setting and main results

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma
,
and let
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
\ldots
,
x_{k}
be fixed and distinct points in
\Omega
.
In this paper, we study optimal control problems in which the associated state equation is given by the following Dirichlet problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta y+(e^{y}-1)&=f_{0}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
y&=0&&\text{on }\Gamma\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

for
f_{0}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
with
p>1
,
\eta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\delta_{x_{i}}
denoting the Dirac measure concentrated at
x_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq k
.
We say that a function

for all

is a (very) weak solution to (
1
) if there holds

\left\{\begin{aligned} &e^{y}\in L^{1}(\Omega),\\
&\int_{\Omega}-\Delta\phi y+(e^{y}-1)\phi dx=\int_{\Omega}f_{0}\phi dx+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{i}\phi(x_{i})\quad\text{for all }\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega).\end{aligned}\right.

By the results of Vazquez
[
30
]
(see also
(
7
, Thm. 4.17)
, and
[
5
]
for the three-dimensional case), we obtain the following proposition concerning the existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
1
). Although the work of Vazquez
[
30
]
considers equation (
1
) on the entire space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the conclusions–and the structure of the proof–remain valid for bounded domains in two dimensions.

Proposition 1

The equation (
1
) admits a weak solution
y
if and only if
\bm{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{k})
satisfies

\bm{\eta}_{\max}\leq 4\pi.

(2)

Moreover, (
1
) has at most one solution.

We consider the optimal control problem

\min\left\{J(y,\bm{\eta})\,\middle|\,(y,\bm{\eta})=(y,\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{k})\in L^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\,\text{satisfies \eqref{eq:P-state}}\right\},

(P)

where objective functional is given by

J(y,\bm{\eta})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-y_{d}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\eta_{i}|

(3)

with
\kappa>0
and
y_{d}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

We first establish the existence of minimizers for the problem (
P
).

Theorem 2.1

There exists at least one global minimizer
(\bar{y},\bar{\bm{\eta}})
of (
P
). Moreover, any minimizer
(\tilde{y},\tilde{\bm{\eta}})
of (
P
) satisfies

\bm{\tilde{\eta}}_{\max}\leq 4\pi.

(4)

Proof.

Let
y_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be a unique solution to (
1
) corresponding to
\eta_{i}:=0
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.
Since
(y_{0},\bm{0})\in L^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
is an admissible point of (
P
), there holds

\alpha:=\inf\left\{J(y,\bm{\eta})\,\middle|\,(y,\bm{\eta})\,\text{satisfies \eqref{eq:P-state}}\right\}\leq J(y_{0},\bm{0}).

(5)

Let
\{(y_{n},\bm{\eta}_{n})\}
with
\bm{\eta}_{n}=(\eta_{1}^{n},\eta_{2}^{n},\ldots,\eta_{k}^{n})
,
n\geq 1
, be a minimizing sequence, that is,
(y_{n},\bm{\eta}_{n})
satisfies (
1
) and

\alpha=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}J(y_{n},\bm{\eta}_{n}).

(6)

Since

is a weak solution to (
1
), we deduce from (
2
) in

1
that

\eta_{i}^{n}\leq 4\pi\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq k,n\geq 1.

In view of (
5
), (
6
), and the definition of
J
, there exists an integer

such that

\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\eta_{i}^{n}|\leq J(y_{0},\bm{0})+1\quad\text{for all}\quad n\geq n_{0},

which implies the existence of subsequence
\{{n_{m}}\}
such that

\eta_{i}^{n_{m}}\to\eta_{i}^{*}
as
m\to\infty

for some
\eta_{i}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
. Obviously, there holds

\eta_{i}^{*}\leq 4\pi
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.
Combining this with
Proposition

1
shows that
(
1
) admits a unique solution

y^{*}
in
W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)
for all

, associated with

\bm{\eta}^{*}:=(\eta_{1}^{*},\eta_{2}^{*},\ldots,\eta_{k}^{*})
.

We now prove that
(y^{*},\bm{\eta}^{*})
is a global minimizer of (
P
). To this end, we employ the stability property of (
1

51

Lemma

5
below.
Thanks to (

y_{n_{m}}\to y^{*}\quad\text{in }W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)\quad\text{as }m\to\infty

for all

. From this and the continuous embedding

There thus holds

Finally, as a direct consequence of

, (
4

of (

Auxiliary results on elliptic equations involving measures and the exponential Nemytskii operator

We begin this section by deriving estimates for solutions to Poisson’s equation with a source being a measure concentrated at a set of finitely many points.
This result generalizes the one in
(
8

.

Proposition 2

Assume that
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
\omega_{i}>0
for all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
.
Let
y\in W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)
,
1\leq q<2
, be a unique solution to

-\Delta y=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}\,\text{in }\Omega,\quad y=0\,\text{on }\Gamma,

(9)

and let

be constant such that balls
B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},r_{0})\subset\Omega
,
i=1,2,\ldots,k,
are pairwise disjoint.
Then, for any
\alpha\in(0,4\pi)
, there hold

\int_{\Omega}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)|y(x)|}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}]dx\leq\pi(\frac{2R}{r_{0}})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}\\
\begin{aligned} &\times[(R^{2}-kr_{0}^{2})+2r_{0}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(2-(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}})^{-1}]\end{aligned}

(10)

and

\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{j},r_{0})}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)|y(x)|}{\omega_{j}}]dx\leq\frac{4\pi^{2}r_{0}^{2}}{\alpha}(\frac{2R}{r_{0}})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\omega_{j}}}

(11)

for all
1\leq j\leq k
with
R:=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{diam}\Omega
.

Proof.

By using a linear shift if necessary, we assume that
\Omega\subset B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,R)=:B_{R}
.
Let
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{r_{0}}{2})
be arbitrary but fixed, and let
y_{i}^{\epsilon}
and
\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}
,

, be unique solutions to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta y_{i}^{\epsilon}&=\phi_{\epsilon}(\cdot-x_{i})&&\text{in }\Omega,\\

\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}&=0&&\text{on }\partial B_{R},\end{aligned}\right.

(12)

respectively,
where
\{\phi_{\epsilon}\}_{0<\epsilon<r_{0}/2}
is a family of mollifiers satisfying

0\leq\phi_{\epsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad\mathrm{supp}\phi_{\epsilon}\subset\overline{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,\epsilon)},\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi_{\epsilon}dx=1.

A weak form of the maximum principle; see, e.g.
(
7

a.e. in
\Omega
and
\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}\geq 0
a.e. in
B_{R}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.
Again, using the maximum principle implies that

0\leq y_{i}^{\epsilon}\leq\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}\quad\text{a.e. in }\Omega.

(13)

On the other hand, since
\phi_{\epsilon}(\cdot-x_{i})
converges weakly-star to
\delta_{x_{i}}
in
\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
as
\epsilon\to 0
,
y_{i}^{\epsilon}
strongly converges to
y_{i}
in
W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)
that uniquely solves

-\Delta y_{i}=\delta_{x_{i}}\,\text{in }\Omega,\quad y_{i}=0\,\text{on }\Gamma

for all
1\leq i\leq k
;
see, e.g.
(
11
, Thm. 2.1)
.
Setting now
{y}^{\epsilon}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}y_{i}^{\epsilon}
and
\tilde{y}^{\epsilon}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}
thus yields

\left\{\begin{aligned} &{y}^{\epsilon}\to y\quad\text{in }W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)\quad\text{and a.e. in }\Omega,\\
&\tilde{y}^{\epsilon}\geq{y}^{\epsilon}\geq 0\quad\text{a.e. in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(14)

where the estimates in (
14
) is due to (
13
).

Fixing
\alpha\in(0,4\pi)
and

, we now estimate
\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}|\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)|]
and
\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\omega_{j}}|\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)|]
,
1\leq i\leq k
. To this end, we first employ the Green representation formula; see, e.g.
(

16

\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)=\int_{B_{R}}G(\frac{x}{R},\frac{x^{\prime}}{R})\phi_{\epsilon}(x^{\prime}-x_{i})dx^{\prime}=\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},\epsilon)}G(\frac{x}{R},\frac{x^{\prime}}{R})\phi_{\epsilon}(x^{\prime}-x_{i})dx^{\prime}

\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\omega_{j}}|\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)|]\leq(\frac{2R}{r_{0}-\epsilon})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\omega_{j}}}

\bullet

we arrive at

\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}|\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)|]\leq\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},\epsilon)}\exp[(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}\ln(\frac{2R}{|x-x^{\prime}|})]\phi_{\epsilon}(x^{\prime}-x_{i})dx^{\prime}\\
\begin{aligned} &=\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},\epsilon)}(\frac{2R}{|x-x^{\prime}|})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}\phi_{\epsilon}(x^{\prime}-x_{i})dx^{\prime}.\end{aligned}

Integrating over
{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},r_{0})}
gives

\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},r_{0})}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}|\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)|]dx\\
\leq\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},r_{0})}dx\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},\epsilon)}(\frac{2R}{|x-x^{\prime}|})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}\phi_{\epsilon}(x^{\prime}-x_{i})dx^{\prime}.

A method of integration by substitution then yields

\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},r_{0})}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}|\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)|]dx\\
\begin{aligned} &\leq\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,r_{0}+\epsilon)}d\eta\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,\epsilon)}(\frac{2R}{|\eta|})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}\phi_{\epsilon}(\psi)d\psi\\
&=\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,r_{0}+\epsilon)}(\frac{2R}{|\eta|})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}d\eta=2\pi\int_{0}^{r_{0}+\epsilon}(\frac{2R}{r})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}rdr\\
&=2\pi(2R)^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}\times\frac{1}{2-{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}}(r_{0}+\epsilon)^{2-{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}}.\end{aligned}

In conclusion, we have

\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},r_{0})}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}|\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)|]dx\leq\frac{2\pi(\epsilon+r_{0})^{2}}{2-{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}}(\frac{2R}{\epsilon+r_{0}})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}

(19)

for all
1\leq i\leq k
.
Similarly, there holds for all
1\leq i\leq k
that

\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},r_{0})}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\omega_{i}}|\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)|]dx\leq\frac{2\pi(\epsilon+r_{0})^{2}}{\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}}(\frac{2R}{\epsilon+r_{0}})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})}.

(20)

We have shown the estimates (
17
)–(
20
), which, together with (
13
), Fatou’s lemma, the limit in (
14
), and the definition of
y^{\epsilon}
, will be employed to prove the desired estimates.
Indeed for (
10
), by definition of
y^{\epsilon}
, there holds

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}{y}^{\epsilon}(x)]dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)]dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)]dx,

where the last inequality follows from (
13
) and the fact that
\Omega\subset B_{R}
.
Setting
B_{0}:=\cup_{i=1}^{k}B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},r_{0})
, we can rewrite the right-hand side of the previous estimate as

\int_{B_{R}\backslash B_{0}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)]dx\\
+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{l},r_{0})}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)]dx=:A_{1}^{\epsilon}+A_{2}^{\epsilon}.

For
A_{1}^{\epsilon}
, we deduce from (
17
) that

\displaystyle A_{1}^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{R}\backslash B_{0}}dx\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\frac{2R}{r_{0}-\epsilon})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}=\pi(\frac{2R}{r_{0}-\epsilon})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}[R^{2}-kr_{0}^{2}].

For
A_{2}^{\epsilon}
, in view of (
17
) and (
19
), there holds

\displaystyle A_{2}^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{l},r_{0})}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{l}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}\tilde{y}_{l}^{\epsilon}(x)]\times\prod_{i\neq l}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)]dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=1}^{k}(\frac{2R}{r_{0}-\epsilon})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\sum_{i\neq l}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{l},r_{0})}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{l}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}\tilde{y}_{l}^{\epsilon}(x)]dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=1}^{k}(\frac{2R}{r_{0}-\epsilon})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\sum_{i\neq l}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}\times\frac{2\pi(\epsilon+r_{0})^{2}}{2-{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{l}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}}\times(\frac{2R}{\epsilon+r_{0}})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{l}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}

\displaystyle=2\pi(r_{0}+\epsilon)^{2}(\frac{2R}{r_{0}-\epsilon})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{l=1}^{k}(\frac{r_{0}-\epsilon}{r_{0}+\epsilon})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{l}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}[{2-{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{l}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}}]^{-1}.

Consequently, one has

\int_{\Omega}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}{y}^{\epsilon}(x)]dx\leq\int_{B_{R}}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}{y}^{\epsilon}(x)]dx\\
\begin{aligned} &\leq\pi(\frac{2R}{r_{0}-\epsilon})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}[R^{2}-kr_{0}^{2}]+2\pi(r_{0}+\epsilon)^{2}(\frac{2R}{r_{0}-\epsilon})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}\\
&\qquad\times\sum_{l=1}^{k}(\frac{r_{0}-\epsilon}{r_{0}+\epsilon})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{l}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}[{2-{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{l}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}}]^{-1}.\end{aligned}

This, along with (
14
), the nonnegativity of
y^{\epsilon}
, and Fatou’s lemma, yields (
10
).

It remains to prove (
11
). For that purpose, by definition of
y^{\epsilon}
, there holds

\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{j},r_{0})}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)}{\omega_{j}}{y}^{\epsilon}(x)]dx=\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{j},r_{0})}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\omega_{j}}{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)]dx\\
\begin{aligned} &\leq\int_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{j},r_{0})}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{j}}{\omega_{j}}\tilde{y}_{j}^{\epsilon}(x)]\times\prod_{i\neq j}\exp[\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)\omega_{i}}{\omega_{j}}\tilde{y}_{i}^{\epsilon}(x)]dx\\
&\leq(\frac{2R}{r_{0}-\epsilon})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\omega_{j}}}\times\frac{2\pi(\epsilon+r_{0})^{2}}{\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}}\times(\frac{2R}{\epsilon+r_{0}})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})},\end{aligned}

in view of (
14
), (
18
) and (
20
). From this, along with (
14
), the nonnegativity of
y^{\epsilon}
, and Fatou’s lemma, we have (
11
).
∎

We now state a direct consequence of
Proposition

2
, which will contribute significantly to demonstrating the differentiability of the control-to-state operator investigated in
Section

4
.

Corollary 1

Let
g\in L^{p}(\Omega)
be arbitrary, but fixed with
p>1
. Assume that
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
satisfying
\bm{\omega}_{\max}<4\pi
.
Let
y
be the unique weak solution to

-\Delta y=g+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}\,\text{in }\Omega,\quad y=0\,\text{on }\Gamma.

(21)

Then, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
\bm{\omega}
satisfying following statements:

(a)

If
\bm{\omega}_{\max}\leq 0
, then there holds

\left\|e^{y}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq e^{C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}.

(22)

(b)

If
\bm{\omega}_{\max}\in(0,4\pi)
, then there holds

\left\|e^{y}\right\|_{L^{1+\tau}(\Omega)}\leq\pi^{\frac{1}{1+\tau}}e^{C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}(\frac{2R}{r_{0}})^{2k}[R^{2}+\frac{k(1+\tau)\bm{\omega}_{\max}r_{0}^{2}}{4\pi-(1+\tau)\bm{\omega}_{\max}}]^{\frac{1}{1+\tau}}

(23)

for all
\tau\in[0,\frac{4\pi}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}-1)
, where
R
and
r_{0}
are the constants defined in
Proposition

2
.

Proof.

Let
y_{0}
and
y_{1}
be the unique solutions to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta y_{0}&=g&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
y_{0}&=0&&\text{on }\Gamma\end{aligned}\right.\quad\text{and}\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta y_{1}&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
y_{1}&=0&&\text{on }\Gamma,\end{aligned}\right.

respectively. By the linearity, one has
y=y_{0}+y_{1}
. Since
g\in L^{p}(\Omega)
with
p>1=\frac{N}{2}
, there hold
y_{0}\in C(\overline{\Omega})
and

\left\|y_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|g\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

(24)

for some constant
C>0
; see, e.g.
(
29
, Thm. 4.7)
.

We proceed to estimate
e^{y_{1}}
, and consequently
e^{y}
, by considering the following two cases.

\bullet
Case 1:
\bm{\omega}_{\max}\leq 0
.

In this situation, we deduce from the maximum principle that
y_{1}\leq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
. This, together with (
24
), thus yields (
22
).

\bullet
Case 2:
\bm{\omega}_{\max}\in(0,4\pi)
.

We also derive from the maximum principle that

y_{1}(x)\leq z(x)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
,
where
z
uniquely solves

-\Delta z=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}^{+}\delta_{x_{i}}\quad\text{in }\Omega,\quad z=0\quad\text{on }\Gamma.

Let
\tau\in\left[0,\frac{4\pi}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}-1\right)
be arbitrary.
Setting now
\alpha:=4\pi-(1+\tau)\bm{\omega}_{\max}\in(0,4\pi)
gives
\frac{(4\pi-\alpha)}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}=1+\tau
.
Applying now
Proposition

2
to
z
yields

\int_{\Omega}e^{(1+\tau)|z|}dx\\
\begin{aligned} &\leq\pi(\frac{2R}{r_{0}})^{(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\omega_{i}^{+}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}}}[(R^{2}-kr_{0}^{2})+2r_{0}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(2-(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}^{+}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}})^{-1}]\\
&\leq\pi(\frac{2R}{r_{0}})^{k(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})}[R^{2}+kr_{0}^{2}(\frac{4\pi}{\alpha}-1)]\\
&=\pi(\frac{2R}{r_{0}})^{\frac{k(1+\tau)\bm{\omega}_{\max}}{2\pi}}[R^{2}+\frac{k(1+\tau)\bm{\omega}_{\max}r_{0}^{2}}{4\pi-(1+\tau)\bm{\omega}_{\max}}],\end{aligned}

where we have just used the estimates
\omega_{i}^{+}\leq\bm{\omega}_{\max}<4\pi
,
1\leq i\leq k
, and
(2-(2-\frac{\alpha}{2\pi})\frac{\omega_{i}^{+}}{\bm{\omega}_{\max}})^{-1}\leq\frac{2\pi}{\alpha}

to obtain the last inequality, and employed the equation
\alpha=4\pi-(1+\tau)\bm{\omega}_{\max}
to derive the last identity.
Consequently, there holds

\int_{\Omega}e^{(1+\tau)y_{1}}dx\leq\pi(\frac{2R}{r_{0}})^{2k(1+\tau)}[R^{2}+\frac{k(1+\tau)\bm{\omega}_{\max}r_{0}^{2}}{4\pi-(1+\tau)\bm{\omega}_{\max}}].

Combining this with (
24
) yields (
23
).
∎

To investigate the differentiability of the control-to-state mapping on a certain open set in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we now present the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the linearized state equation. The following result, concerning a linear elliptic equation with measure data and a zeroth-order coefficient in

L^{r}(\Omega)
for some
r>1
, can be derived using the same argument as in
(
2
, Thm. 4)
, where the authors treated Robin boundary value problems for linear elliptic PDEs with measure data.

Lemma 1

Assume that
y\in L^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying
e^{y}\in L^{r}(\Omega)
with
r>1
. Then, for any
\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
h\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique
z\in W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)
for all
1\leq q<2
such that

-\Delta z+e^{y}z=h+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}\,\text{in }\Omega\quad z=0\,\text{on }\Gamma.

(25)

Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
y
,
h
, and
\bm{\eta}
satisfying

\left\|z\right\|_{W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq C[\left\|h\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\eta_{i}|].

(26)

Proof.

Let
r^{\prime}
be the conjugate number of
r
, i.e.,
r^{\prime}:=\frac{r}{r-1}
and let
q\in[1,2)
be close to
2
, such that
r^{\prime}<\frac{2q}{2-q}
. The choice of
q
ensures that
W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)
is compactly embedded in
L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)
, due to the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem; see, e.g.
(
1
, Thm. 6.2)
.
We consider the mapping

\displaystyle T:L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\to W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\mapsto T(v):=z_{v},

where
z_{v}
is the unique solution to

-\Delta z_{v}+e^{y}v=h+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}\,\text{in }\Omega\quad z_{v}=0\,\text{on }\Gamma.

(27)

We first show that
T
is continuous as a mapping from
L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)
to itself. Indeed, for any
v_{1},v_{2}\in L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)
, by setting
z_{i}:=T(v_{i})
and then subtracting the equations for
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
, there holds

-\Delta(z_{1}-z_{2})=-e^{y}(v_{1}-v_{2})\quad\text{in }\Omega,\quad z_{1}-z_{2}=0\quad\text{on }\Gamma.

Obviously, the right-hand side of this equation belongs to
L^{1}(\Omega)
. Using the a priori estimate for Poisson’s equation with
L^{1}
-data yields

\left\|z_{1}-z_{2}\right\|_{W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|e^{y}(v_{1}-v_{2})\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|e^{y}\right\|_{L^{r}(\Omega)}\left\|v_{1}-v_{2}\right\|_{L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)};

see
[
9
]
, where we have exploited the Hölder inequality to derive the last estimate. From this and the continuous imbedding
W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)
, the continuity of
T
then follows.

Since
W^{1,q}_{0}
is compactly embedded in
L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)
,
T:L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)\to L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)
is compact.

We now show that the following set

K:=\{z\in L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)\,|\,\exists t\in(0,1):z=T(tz)\}

is bounded. To this end, for any
z\in K
, there holds

-\Delta z+te^{y}z=h+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}\,\text{in }\Omega\quad z=0\,\text{on }\Gamma

(28)

for some
t\in[0,1]
. By
(
7
, Prop. 4.B.3)
for
f:=te^{y}z
, one has

\left\|te^{y}z\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}te^{y}z\mathrm{sign}(z)dx\leq\left\|h\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\eta_{i}|.

The a priori estimate of solutions to (
28
); see, e.g.
(
7
, Thm. 4.B.1)
, gives

\left\|z\right\|_{W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq C[\left\|te^{y}z\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\left\|h\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\eta_{i}|]\leq C[\left\|h\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\eta_{i}|],

which, along with
W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega)
, thus shows the boundedness of
K
.

We now apply the Leray–Schauder principle to mapping
T
and deduce the existence of solutions to (
25
).
On the other hand, the uniqueness of solutions to (
25
) follows from
(
7
, Cor. 4.B.1)
.
Finally, by
(
7
, Prop. 4.B.3)
, there holds

\displaystyle\left\|e^{y}z\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}e^{y}z\mathrm{sign}zdx\leq\left\|h\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\eta_{i}|.

From this, we can conclude from
(
7
, Thm. 4.B.1)
that

\displaystyle\left\|z\right\|_{W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C[\left\|e^{y}z\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\left\|h\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\eta_{i}|]\leq C[\left\|h\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\eta_{i}|],

which gives (
26
).
∎

With the aim of studying the Fréchet differentiability of the control-to-state operator, considered in
Section

4
, we need to introduce some open sets, depending on
\bm{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
\bm{b}_{\max}\in(0,4\pi)
, and Banach spaces as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\bm{b}}

\displaystyle:=\{\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\,|\,\omega_{i}<b_{i}\,\forall 1\leq i\leq k\},

(29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\bm{b}}

\displaystyle:=\{\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)\,|\,\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{k})\in\mathcal{O}_{\bm{b}}\},

(30)

\displaystyle V_{\bm{b}}

\displaystyle:=\{y\in W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)\,|\,-\Delta y\in\mathcal{D}_{\bm{b}}+L^{r_{\bm{b}}}(\Omega)\},

(31)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle:=\{\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)\,|\,\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\},

(32)

\displaystyle W_{r_{\bm{b}}}

\displaystyle:=\{y\in W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)\,|\,-\Delta y\in\mathcal{D}+L^{r_{\bm{b}}}(\Omega)\}

(33)

with

r_{\bm{b}}:=\min\{\frac{4\pi}{{\bm{b_{\max}}}},p\}>1.

(34)

Obviously,
\mathcal{D}
is a closed finite-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
, while
W_{r_{\bm{b}}}
is a Banach space when endowed with the norm

\left\|y\right\|_{W_{r_{\bm{b}}}}:=\left\|y\right\|_{W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)}+\left\|\Delta y\right\|_{\mathcal{D}+L^{r_{\bm{b}}}(\Omega)}.

Here, for any
y\in W_{r_{\bm{b}}}
, the
(\mathcal{D}+L^{r_{\bm{b}}}(\Omega))
-norm of
\Delta y
is given by

\left\|\Delta y\right\|_{\mathcal{D}+L^{r_{\bm{b}}}(\Omega)}=\left\|\mu\right\|_{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}+\left\|h\right\|_{L^{r_{\bm{b}}}(\Omega)},

where,
(\mu,h)\in(\mathcal{D},L^{r_{\bm{b}}}(\Omega))
is the unique pair satisfying
-\Delta y=\mu+h
.
We also observe from
Lemma

2
below that the space
W_{r_{\bm{b}}}
is continuously embedded in
W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)
for all
1\leq q<\frac{N}{N-1}=2
.
Furthermore, by
Lemma

9
,

\mathcal{D}_{\bm{b}}
and

V_{\bm{b}}
are, respectively, open subsets of
\mathcal{D}
and
W_{r_{\bm{b}}}
.

The continuity of Nemytskii’s operator associated with the exponential function as a mapping defined on
V_{\bm{b}}
is established below and plays a crucial role in displaying the Fréchet differentiability of the control-to-state mapping.

Proposition 3

Assume that
\bm{b}_{\max}\in(0,4\pi)
and let
y_{*}\in V_{\bm{b}}
be arbitrary.
Then, there exist positive constants
\rho_{*}=\rho_{*}(y_{*})
and
\epsilon_{*}=\epsilon_{*}(y_{*})
such that

e^{y}\in{L^{r_{\bm{b}}+\epsilon_{*}}(\Omega)}\quad\text{for all }y\in W_{r_{\bm{b}}}\quad\text{such that }\left\|y-y_{*}\right\|_{W_{r_{\bm{b}}}}<\rho_{*}.

(35)

Furthermore, there holds

\left\|e^{y}-e^{y_{*}}\right\|_{L^{r_{\bm{b}}+\epsilon_{*}}(\Omega)}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad\left\|y-y_{*}\right\|_{W_{r_{\bm{b}}}}\to 0.

(36)

Proof.

By fixing now
y_{*}\in V_{\bm{b}}
, there exists a unique pair
(\bm{\omega}^{*},g_{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times L^{r_{\bm{b}}}(\Omega)
with
\bm{\omega}^{*}=(\omega^{*}_{1},\omega_{2}^{*},\ldots,\omega_{k}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\omega_{i}^{*}<b_{i}\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq k\quad\text{and}\quad-\Delta y_{*}=g_{*}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}^{*}\delta_{x_{i}}\quad\text{in }\Omega.

Similarly, for any
y\in W_{r_{\bm{b}}}
, by definition, there exists a unique pair
(\bm{\omega},g)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times L^{r_{\bm{b}}}(\Omega)
satisfying

-\Delta y=g+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
in
\Omega
.

By definition, there thus holds

\left\|y-y_{*}\right\|_{W_{r_{\bm{b}}}}=\left\|y-y_{*}\right\|_{W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)}+\left\|g-g_{*}\right\|_{L^{r_{\bm{b}}}(\Omega)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\omega_{i}-\omega_{i}^{*}|.

Fix
\delta_{*}>0
such that
0<\bm{\omega}_{\max}+\delta_{*}<\bm{b}_{\max}

and choose

\epsilon_{*}:=\frac{1}{2}[\frac{4\pi}{\bm{\omega}^{*}_{\max}+\delta_{*}}-\frac{4\pi}{\bm{b}_{\max}}]>0.

Easily, (
34
) implies

2\epsilon_{*}+r_{\bm{b}}\leq\frac{4\pi}{\bm{\omega}^{*}_{\max}+\delta_{*}}.
Assume now that
\left\|y-y_{*}\right\|_{W_{r_{\bm{b}}}}<\frac{\delta_{*}}{2}
. This gives

\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\omega_{i}-\omega_{i}^{*}|<\frac{\delta_{*}}{2},

and consequently,

\omega_{i}<\frac{\delta_{*}}{2}+\omega_{i}^{*}\leq\frac{\delta_{*}}{2}+\bm{\omega}^{*}_{\max}\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq k.

We therefore have

(2\epsilon_{*}+r_{\bm{b}})\bm{\omega}_{\max}\leq\frac{4\pi}{\bm{\omega}^{*}_{\max}+\delta_{*}}(\frac{\delta_{*}}{2}+\bm{\omega}^{*}_{\max})<4\pi

and thus

y_{0}&=0&&\text{on }\Gamma\end{aligned}\right.\quad\text{and}\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\tilde{y}&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{i}^{+}\delta_{x_{i}}&&\text{in }\Omega\\
\tilde{y}&=0&&\text{on }\Gamma,\end{aligned}\right.

respectively. Since

The following theorem on the continuous differentiability of the control-to-state mapping

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{D}+L^{r_{\bm{b}}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle(y,\bm{\eta})

\displaystyle\mapsto-\Delta y+e^{y}-1-f_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{i}\delta_{x_{i}},

), respectively, while the space

By

3

over

H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
There thus exists a subsequence
\{\bar{\varphi}^{n_{m}}\}
that converges to some
\tilde{\varphi}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
weakly in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and weakly-star in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Letting
m\to\infty
in the equation (
72b
) for
\bar{\varphi}^{n_{m}}
then yields

-\Delta\tilde{\varphi}+e^{\bar{y}}\tilde{\varphi}=\bar{y}-y_{d}\,\text{in }\Omega,\quad\tilde{\varphi}=0\,\text{on }\Gamma,

where we have used the limits (
78
) and (
76
). From this and the uniqueness of solutions to (
8b
), one has
\tilde{\varphi}=\bar{\varphi}
.
Consequently, the subsequence
\{\bar{\varphi}^{n_{m}}\}
converges weakly in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and weakly-star in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
to
\bar{\varphi}
.
The uniqueness of
\bar{\varphi}
implies that
the whole sequence
\{\bar{\varphi}^{n}\}
converges to
\bar{\varphi}
weakly in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and weakly-star in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

We now show that the convergence of
\{\bar{\varphi}^{n}\}
is indeed in the strong topology of
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. For this purpose, subtracting the equations for
\bar{\varphi}^{n}
and for
\bar{\varphi}
yields

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta(\bar{\varphi}^{n}-\bar{\varphi})+e^{\bar{y}^{n}}(\bar{\varphi}^{n}-\bar{\varphi})&=\bar{y}^{n}-\bar{y}-(e^{\bar{y}^{n}}-e^{\bar{y}})\bar{\varphi}&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
(\bar{\varphi}^{n}-\bar{\varphi})&=0&&\text{on }\Gamma.\end{aligned}\right.

Testing this equation by
(\bar{\varphi}^{n}-\bar{\varphi})
yields

\left\|\nabla(\bar{\varphi}^{n}-\bar{\varphi})\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}[\bar{y}^{n}-\bar{y}-(e^{\bar{y}^{n}}-e^{\bar{y}})\bar{\varphi}](\bar{\varphi}^{n}-\bar{\varphi})dx.

The right-hand side of the last estimate tends to
0
, since
\bar{y}^{n}-\bar{y}-(e^{\bar{y}^{n}}-e^{\bar{y}})\bar{\varphi}\to 0
strongly in
L^{1}(\Omega)
(see (
76
) and (
78
)) and
\{\bar{\varphi}^{n}\}
weakly-star converges to
\bar{\varphi}
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Therefore,
\{\bar{\varphi}^{n}\}
converges to
\bar{\varphi}
strongly in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

The final step is to prove (
8c
) and the continuity around
x_{i}
of
\bar{\varphi}
. Assume now that
\bar{\eta}_{i}<4\pi
. We shall show that
\bar{\varphi}
is continuous in a neighborhood of
x_{i}
. Indeed, by assertion
(b)
in
Proposition

4
, there exist constants
\rho>0
and
\tau>0
such that

e^{\bar{y}}\mid_{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},\rho)}\in L^{1+\tau}(B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},\rho)).

On the other hand, it is easy to see that

-\Delta\bar{\varphi}=-e^{\bar{y}}\bar{\varphi}+\bar{y}-y_{d}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},\rho)).

From this and the interior Hölder regularity of solutions to Poisson’s equation; see, e.g.,
(
17
, Thm. 8.24)
, we have the continuity around
x_{i}
of

\bar{\varphi}
. This, together with the limit
\bar{\varphi}^{n}\to\bar{\varphi}
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, implies that

\bar{\varphi}^{n}(x)\to\bar{\varphi}(x)\quad\text{for all }x\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(x_{i},\rho_{1})

for some
\rho_{1}>0
.
From this, together with (
72c
) and the definitions of
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}^{\epsilon}
in (
70
), we obtain (
8c
) by applying a standard argument.
∎

6
Conclusions

We have investigated an optimal control problem governed by a semilinear elliptic equation with exponential nonlinearity, where the source term includes a linear combination of Dirac measures concentrated at a finite set of distinct points.
Since the state equation admits a unique solution only when the masses of the Dirac measures do not exceed the threshold value
4\pi
, the control-to-state operator is continuously differentiable only on an open subset of the control space.
To derive first-order optimality conditions, we considered a family of regularized optimal control problems. These regularizations incorporated suitable control constraints, ensuring that the admissible controls remained within the domain of differentiability of the control-to-state operator. By passing to the limit in these regularized problems, we obtained an optimality system for the original problem.

Estimates of Green’s function on the unit ball

Lemma 8

Let
G
be the Green function of the Laplace operator
-\Delta
on the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then, there holds

0\leq G(x,y)\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln\left(\frac{2}{|x-x^{\prime}|}\right)\quad\text{for all }x,x^{\prime}\in{B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,1)},\quad x\neq x^{\prime}.

(80)

Proof.

We have for all
x,x^{\prime}\in B_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,1)
with
x\neq x^{\prime}
that

G(x,x^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln|x^{\prime}|&&\text{if }x=0,\\
&-\frac{1}{2\pi}[\ln{|x-x^{\prime}|}-\ln(|\frac{x}{|x|}-|x|x^{\prime}|)]&&\text{if }x\neq 0;\end{aligned}\right.

see, e.g.
(
17
, Form. (2.23), Chap. 2)
and
(
16
, Form. (41) & (6), Chap. 2)
.
The nonnegativity of
G
follows from
(
17
, Form. (2.24), Chap. 2)
with noting that the Green function defined in
[
17
]
is associated with the
\Delta
operator.
However, we now state the detailed argument showing both two estimates in (
80
). To this end, we first consider the case
x=0
and have

0\leq G(0,x^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln|x^{\prime}|=\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln\frac{2}{|0-x^{\prime}|},

which shows (
80
).
For
x\neq 0
, we have

\displaystyle|\frac{x}{|x|}-|x|x^{\prime}|^{2}

\displaystyle=1-2x\cdot x^{\prime}+|x|^{2}|x^{\prime}|^{2}=|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}+1-|x|^{2}-|x^{\prime}|^{2}+|x|^{2}|x^{\prime}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}+(1-|x^{\prime}|^{2})(1-|x|^{2}),

which thus gives

|x-x^{\prime}|\leq|\frac{x}{|x|}-|x|x^{\prime}|\leq 2

for all

∎
