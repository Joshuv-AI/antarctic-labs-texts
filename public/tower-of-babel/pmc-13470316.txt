Germline BRCA1 pathogenic variants and T cell dysfunction
Authors: J Kay, MA Harris, LE Lara Gonzalez, CT van Geelen, KA Clarke, B Virassamy, F Caramia, J-M Pang, MMR O’Malley, ML Hun, R Salgado, H Thorne, J Visvader, PJ Neeson, PK Darcy, S Loi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGermline BRCA1 (gBRCA1) pathogenic variants (PVs) are the most common cause of inherited breast cancer. Preclinical studies have shown that homozygous loss of BRCA1 in T cells impairs T cell-mediated antitumour immunity. However, the consequences of heterozygous gBRCA1 on human T cell function are unknown.Patients and methodsWe collected peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy donors and treatment-naïve patients diagnosed with early-stage breast cancer with or without a gBRCA1 PV. T cells derived from PBMC were activated in vitro and analysed for phenotypic and functional differences. To investigate the impact of gBRCA1 PV on tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), we carried out T cell receptor (TCR) sequencing and whole exome sequencing on triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) tumours from gBRCA1 carriers and wild-type (WT) patients.ResultsPatients with gBRCA1 PV had significantly reduced circulating T cell counts compared with WT individuals. BRCA1 protein was highly expressed in healthy donor activated T cells, but significantly reduced expression was observed in gBRCA1 patients compared with WT. Activated T cells from gBRCA1 carriers displayed a distinct transcriptional profile with significantly impaired proliferation and decreased effector molecule production. TCR sequencing of TILs extracted from TNBC tumours from gBRCA1 carriers revealed significantly more hyperexpanded T cell clones than in WT patients, with hyperexpansion correlating with increased tumour mutation burden (R2 = 0.86), particularly frameshift mutations (R2 = 0.72).ConclusionsCirculating T cells from gBRCA1 carriers exhibit intrinsic T cell dysfunction. This phenotype may be clinically relevant in the context of antitumour immunity, cancer development, progression and response to immunotherapy treatment.Key words: BRCA1, breast cancer, immunity, T cell dysfunction, T cell receptor, tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common form of cancer worldwide, with incidence projected to reach 3 million cases annually by 2040.1, 2, 3 Triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) is one of the most aggressive subtypes of breast cancer, with patients experiencing a particularly poor prognosis, in part due to a lack of targeted therapies. However, recent advances in breast tumour immunology and immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy have significantly improved the outcomes and quality of life of patients with TNBC,4, 5, 6, 7, 8 with ICB now standard of care for patients with early and metastatic TNBC. However, further research to better understand the complexities of the breast tumour immune microenvironment (TME), particularly the role of host factors,9,10 may result in more accurate personalised treatment strategies.

One of the most common germline variants in the population is found in the breast cancer susceptibility gene 1 (BRCA1).11 Germline variants in BRCA1 are associated with a cumulative risk of >70% of developing breast cancer by the age of 80 years.12 Furthermore, germline BRCA1 (gBRCA1) mutation or pathogenic variant (PV) carriers have a younger age of cancer onset compared with non-carriers, with the majority developing TNBC.13

The role of BRCA1 variants in breast cancer pathogenesis is well established,11 but their impact on the immune system is relatively unexplored. Specifically, little is known about how gBRCA1 variants may impact systemic immunity or non-malignant cells in the breast TME, particularly tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). TILs are a known predictor and prognostic factor in TNBC.14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Thus, a better understanding of how host factors, such as gBRCA1 variants, directly impact immune cell function could have clinical implications for immunotherapy and more accurate personalised care in the clinic.

Preclinical murine studies have found that homozygous loss of BRCA1 in T cells completely inhibited T cell development.19 Subsequent in vivo studies have reported that homozygous deletion of BRCA1 post-T cell development results in mature CD8+ T cells with impaired proliferative capacity and reduced cytokine production.20 Samstein et al.21 recently reported that a cohort of somatic and/or gBRCA1 carrier patients have poorer responses to ICB compared with BRCA2 (gBRCA2) carriers, potentially owing to the differences in their respective TMEs. Furthermore, paradoxically, higher levels of lymphocytes have been reported both in breast normal and tumour tissue from gBRCA1 carriers compared with non-carriers.22,23 These carriers also have increased sensitivity to radiation24, 25, 26 and reduced CD8+ T cells in the periphery,20 strongly indicating a functional role of BRCA1 in these lymphocytes.

Given that proliferating cells (such as activated T cells) depend on homologous recombination for DNA repair during rapid expansion,27 BRCA1 haploinsufficiency may render these cells more susceptible to replication stress and dysfunction, potentially impairing antitumour immunity in the setting of chronic antigen exposure. In this study, we explored the impact of heterozygous gBRCA1 PV on circulating and tumour-infiltrating T cells in a cohort of human carriers.

Methods

Blood sample collection

Healthy female donor blood (buffy coats; median age 27 years; range 21-38 years) samples were requested from the Australian Red Cross Lifeblood (ethics approval number 01/14). Blood from female BRCA1/2 pathogenic-variant carriers (with and without breast cancer) and non-carrier breast cancer patients (all aged <50 years and matched by age at time of collection) were collected before treatment and processed at the Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre following written and verbal consent according to ethics approval number SEGMENT 13/123, or obtained from the Kathleen Cuningham Foundation Consortium for Research into Familial Breast Cancer biobank (ethics approval number 97/27).

Peripheral blood mononuclear cell isolation and in vitro culture

Blood from healthy donors (HD) and patients was processed to isolate peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) containing T cells. In sterile conditions, up to 25 ml of blood was carefully overlaid on 15 ml of Ficoll-Paque (Cytiva). Samples were centrifuged at 500 × g for 20 min at 24°C, with level 1 deceleration, to gently separate the layers of the blood. The PBMC layer was collected and washed twice in sterile 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and spun down at 400 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Cell numbers were quantified using the Alinity HQ cell counter (Abbott) and resuspended in in-house Freeze Mix [90% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, EMD Millipore)]. Cells were divided into 1 ml aliquots containing 6 × 106 cells and frozen overnight at −80°C in a CoolCell (Sigma-Aldrich), before long-term storage in liquid nitrogen.

The frozen PBMCs were thawed and resuspended in culture media [RPMI (Gibco), 10% heat-inactivated human serum (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1× penicillin/streptomycin] containing 50 U/ml human IL-2 (Peprotech) to rest overnight. The cells were then resuspended in media containing T cell receptor (TCR) stimulation solution (25 μl/ml ImmunoCult T cell Activator, STEMCELL Technologies; and 50 U/ml human IL-2, Peprotech) and incubated at 37°C (18% O2, 5% CO2). Cells were counted using Trypan Blue (ThermoFisher) exclusion and re-cultured at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/ml in fresh culturing media, supplemented with 50 U/ml human IL-2 (Peprotech) every 2 to 3 days. Cells were re-stimulated with T cell activator every 7 days, for a maximum of six stimulations. Cells were harvested at multiple time points according to the experiments carried out. All steps were carried out in sterile conditions.

T cell phenotyping by flow cytometry

Up to 1 million PBMCs were washed with 1× PBS and stained with viability dye (Zombie Red Fixable Viability, BioLegend, #77475) for 10 min in the dark at room temperature. Cells were spun down at 300 × g for 3 min, and supernatant was discarded, followed by 10 min incubation with human Fc-block (1:20; BD) at room temperature. Cells were spun down, and supernatant was discarded. Cells were then stained with surface antibodies for 20 min in the dark at 37°C before being washed with fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) wash buffer (2% FBS, 1 mM EDTA in 1× PBS). Cells were spun and supernatants were discarded. Cells were fixed and permeabilised with Fix/Perm kit (ThermoFisher) as outlined in the supplier protocol. Cells were stained with intracellular antibodies for 20 min at room temperature, in the dark before washing twice with 1× Permeabilization buffer (ThermoFisher), followed by resuspension in 1% paraformaldehyde (PFA, ThermoFisher). Compensation controls were prepared for each antibody using UltraComp beads (BD). Data were acquired on the FACSymphony A5 (BD) flow cytometer and analysed using FlowJo v10.8.1. (BD). Antibodies used: Zombie Red (1:800, BioLegend #77475), CD45-APCH7 (1:100, BD #560178), CD3-BUV496 (1:100, BD #741206), CD4-BUV395 (1:100, BD #563550), CD8-BUV805 (1:100, BD #612889), TIM3-BB515 (1:100, BD #565568), PD1-BV421 (1:50, BioLegend #329920), LAG3-AF700 (1:100, BioLegend #369344), Ki67-PeCY7 (1:100, BioLegend #350526), CTLA4-APC (1:100, BioLegend #349908).

CD4+ and CD8+ T cell purification

In sterile conditions, human PBMCs were thawed and washed in culturing media (RPMI (Gibco), 10% FBS, 1× penicillin/streptomycin). Cells were spun down at 400 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Pellet was resuspended in magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) buffer [0.5% FBS, 2 mM EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich) in 1× PBS] at a concentration of 12 500 cells/μl. Magnetic beads anti-CD8 (Miltenyi) (10 μl/1 × 107 cells) were added to the cell solution and incubated on ice for 20 min. Cell suspension was washed with MACS buffer and resuspended in 1 ml MACS buffer. Cell sample was then applied to a prewashed and precooled LS column (Miltenyi) placed on a magnet (Miltenyi). Flowthrough was collected before washing the column thrice with MACS buffer. The column was removed from the magnet, and bound CD8+ cells were eluted with MACS buffer. Isolated CD8+ cells were resuspended in culturing media and counted before culturing. Anti-CD4 magnetic beads (Miltenyi) (10 μl/1 × 107 cells) were added to the flowthrough and incubated on ice for 20 min for magnetic isolation. All further steps were identical to those for the CD8+ purification. Purity (>90%) was confirmed using flow cytometry; antibodies/dyes used: FixYellow dye (Life Technologies, #L34968A), CD4-APC (1:100, BD, #340443), CD8-PeCy7 (1:100, BD, #335787).

Immunoblotting

Washed cell pellets were lysed in 20-50 μl of 1× Laemmli buffer (5.5% SDS, 28% glycerol, 170 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4) at 90°C for 10 to 20 min for immunoblotting. Protein levels were quantified using the DC protein quantification assay (BioRad); 1× electrophoresis sample buffer [ESB, 312 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 10% SDS, 45% glycerol, 1% bromophenol blue, 5% β-mercaptoethanol] was added to each sample, and concentrations were equalised using 1× Laemmli buffer. Samples were loaded on to a 4% to 15% gradient TGX pre-cast acrylamide gel (BioRad) and run at 150 V for ∼60 min. Proteins were transferred to a PVDF synthetic nitrocellulose membrane (BioRad) overnight at 30 V in transfer buffer [1× Transfer Buffer (BioRad), 10% pure ethanol]. Membranes were blocked using 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 0.1% TBST wash buffer [1× TBS (Tris-buffered saline), 0.1% Tween20 (Sigma)] for ∼60 min. Primary antibodies were diluted as outlined below in 5% BSA blocking buffer and added to the membrane to blot overnight at 4°C on a roller. Membranes were washed with TBST 3 times for 5 min. Secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase enzyme (Cell signalling, Ref. 7074/7076) was diluted 1/3000 in TBST wash buffer and added to the membrane to blot at room temperature on a roller for ∼60 min. Membranes were washed with TBST 3 times for 5 min. ECL reagent (PerkinElmer) was added to the membranes for 1 min before imaging with the ChemiDoc scanner (BioRad). Band intensity was quantified using ImageLab (BioRad) software; background values were subtracted, and resulting values were normalised to their respective quantified β-actin bands. Primary antibodies used: anti-human BRCA1 (Rabbit), Cell signalling, Ref 9010, 1/1000 dilution; anti-human BRCA2 (Rabbit), Cell signalling, Ref 10741, 1/1000 dilution; anti-human beta-actin (Mouse), MP Biochemicals, Ref 691001, 1/10000 dilution.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction

A minimum of 250 000 cells were pelleted for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and were lysed in TRIzol (ThermoFisher). Chloroform was added to the lysates in a 10:2 ratio of TRIzol:chloroform and vigorously shaken. Samples were incubated at room temperature for 3 min and centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The top aqueous layer containing the RNA was transferred to clean tubes. The RNA was then purified from the samples using the RNA Mini Kit (ZYMO). RNA concentration was measured using the Nanodrop2000 (ThermoFisher). cDNA was synthesised with 1 μg of RNA, using the Invitrogen SuperScript VILO cDNA synthesis kit (ThermoFisher) and diluted 1/5 with nuclease-free water. Two μl of cDNA sample was added to 8 μl of TaqMan master mix [5 μl TaqMan master mix buffer (ThermoFisher), 0.5 μl TaqMan primer (ThermoFisher), 2.5 μl nuclease-free water] in a 96-well Fast Optics PCR plate (ThermoFisher). qPCR was carried out for BRCA1 (Hs01556193_m1), BRCA2 (Hs00609073_m1) and 18S (Hs03003631_g1). Amplification was quantified using the Step-One Real-Time PCR machine (ThermoFisher). mRNA analysis was carried out using the ΔΔCq method.

Bulk RNA sequencing and analysis

PBMCs were stimulated in vitro for 3 weeks according to the in vitro culture protocol. Matched baseline and stimulated PBMCs from each individual were stained according to the T cell phenotyping flow cytometry with the following antibodies and sorted for CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells with the Fusion5 sorter (BD) into neat foetal calf serum: Zombie Red (1:800, BioLegend #77475), CD45-APCH7 (1:100, BD #560178), CD3-BUV496 (1:100, BD #741206), CD4-BUV395 (1:100, BD #563550), CD8-BUV805 (1:100, BD #612889). Each sample was bulk 3' sequenced and analysed in RStudio v2023.12.0 + 369 using the limma package.28 Raw RNA reads were normalised with the voom function, and differentially expressed genes were obtained with limma. Multiple testing correction was carried out using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false-discovery rate (FDR). GSEA pathways were obtained using the fgsea package in R.29 Plot visualisation and figures were made with ggplot2.30

Cytokine bead array

Supernatants were collected from the culture of PBMCs at 17 and 38 days after stimulation with T cell activator (25 μl/ml Immunocult, StemCell Technologies), following stimulation every 7 days. Concentrations of secreted cytokines were quantified using the human cytokine bead array flex sets specific to each antibody (BD, IFN-γ lot #558269, TNF lot #558273, GZMB lot #6356627). Protocol carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions in a 96-well V-bottom plate. A standard curve was constructed in the range 2500 pg/ml to 0 pg/ml for each cytokine. Samples were acquired with the Symphony A3 (BD), and concentrations were quantified using FCAP Array software v3 (BD Biosciences).

TIL quantification from Formalin-Fixed Paraffin-Embedded slides

TILs were quantified from standard formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) slides by an accredited pathologist according to the international TIL working group guidelines (2014).31

TCR sequencing of TILs

FFPE slices of treatment-naïve, triple negative breast tumours from young (<50 years old) patients with or without germline BRCA1 variants were collected following written and verbal consent according to ‘SEGMENT’ #13/123 ethics or obtained from the Kathleen Cuningham Foundation Consortium for Research into Familial Breast Cancer biobank (#97/27). The slices were deparaffinised by vortexing in pure xylene until all paraffin was dissolved. The samples were then spun at 16 000 × g for 2 min, and xylene was discarded, keeping the pellet. Samples were washed with pure ethanol twice, and DNA was extracted using a QIAamp FFPE Tissue Kit for DNA extraction (Qiagen, #56404). Sequencing was carried out using the Lymphotrack kit (Invivoscribe). TCR sequencing analysis was carried out using the MiXCR software suite (version 3.0.10) to profile productive TCR rearrangements from sequencing data. Raw sequencing reads were processed using the MiXCR pipeline with the following parameters: adapter sequences were trimmed (--adapters adapters-present), only productive receptor rearrangements were retained (--only-productive), and cross-chain receptor sequences (XCR) were specifically targeted (--receptor-type xcr). Additionally, sequencing data were generated using gene-specific primers, indicated by setting the 5' primers (-germline V-segment specific) and 3′ primers (--adapters adapters-present, -L xcr, -p xcr). Reads aligning to primer regions corresponding to V-gene (-L, -g) and J-segments were extracted, with alignment boundaries set according to the primer design (--receptor-type xcr, using V-region primers as forward primers and J-region primers as reverse primers).

Downstream analyses were carried out using R version 4.4.2. Immunoglobulin and TCR clonotype data derived from MiXCR were imported into R using the immunarch package. Known antigen-specific TCR sequences were annotated by comparing CDR3 amino acid sequences with the VDJdb and McPAS-TCR databases, both filtered for human TRB chain sequences. Clonotypes identified in these databases were removed before further analyses to retain novel clonotypes. Clonotype frequency data were categorized into five groups based on adjusted frequency: hyper-expanded (>1%), large (0.1%-1%), medium (0.01%-0.1%), small (0.001%-0.01%), and rare (≤0.001%). Relative proportions and absolute counts of clonotypes in each frequency category were calculated. Clonotype diversity was assessed using measures of richness, evenness, and Simpson’s diversity index calculated using the vegan package. Statistical comparisons between experimental groups (WT vs gBRCA1) were carried out using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with FDR-adjusted P values for multiple comparisons.

Whole exome sequencing and analysis

DNA was extracted from FFPE slides using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, #56404). Annotated somatic variants in Mutation Annotation Format (MAF) files were imported using maftools.

Somatic variant calling and filtering

Somatic single-nucleotide variants and small insertions/deletions (indels) were identified from matched tumour-normal whole exome sequencing data using GATK Mutect2 (v4.1.9.0) following GATK Best Practices. Raw paired-end reads were quality-trimmed and adapter-filtered using fastp (v0.23.4), aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome with BWA-MEM (v0.7.13), and processed with coordinate sorting, duplicate marking, and base quality score recalibration using GATK. Tumour whole exome sequencing was generated at a target depth of ∼120×, matched germline samples at ∼60×, and the median global sequencing depth across tumour samples was 124×. Mutect2 was run in matched tumour-normal mode, incorporating a germline resource and a panel of normals to suppress recurrent technical artefacts. To mitigate FFPE-associated sequencing artefacts, F1R2 read orientation data were collected during variant calling and used to train an orientation bias model with LearnReadOrientationModel. The resulting orientation priors were supplied to FilterMutectCalls (--ob-priors), together with sample-specific contamination estimates generated using CalculateContamination with matched normal pileups. Only variants passing FilterMutectCalls (PASS) were retained for downstream analyses and annotation.

PASS variants were annotated using GATK Funcotator and ANNOVAR and converted to MAF. Driver mutations were identified based on their presence as somatic variants in the Cancer Gene Census database, classification as pathogenic or likely pathogenic in ClinVar (20190305 version), and being reported as a top gene in the Achilles database. For downstream analyses, we restricted variants to protein-altering coding events (missense, nonsense, frameshift and in-frame indels) and applied additional inclusion thresholds requiring a minimum of three alternate reads in the tumour (t_alt_count ≥ 3), a tumour variant allele fraction ≥2%, and a minimum tumour sequencing depth of 10 reads (DP ≥ 10). These thresholds preserve sensitivity for subclonal variants while maintaining specificity through upstream Mutect2 filtering.

Tumour purity and ploidy were estimated using FACETS (v0.6.2, median purity 0.47; median ploidy 3.1), consistent with substantial heterogeneity and copy-number variation. In this setting, low VAFs may reflect true subclonal events diluted by normal contamination and aneuploidy, supporting permissive post-calling thresholds after stringent caller-level filtering. To assess the impact of FFPE-related artefacts, we carried out a sensitivity analysis excluding C>T/G>A substitutions, which showed minimal effect on correlation results. Tumour mutation burden (TMB) and frameshift mutation burden (FSB) were calculated based on the total number of variants per megabase of captured sequence. Correlation analyses between hyper-expanded T cell clonotype counts and TMB, FSB, and frameshift deletions were conducted using Pearson’s correlation test.

Results

Reduced circulating T cell counts in gBRCA1 variant carriers

Given that BRCA1 has a key role in cell survival,32 we investigated whether gBRCA1 carriers have reduced circulating lymphocytes compared with non-carriers with BRCA1-WT genotypes. We collected whole blood from 110 individuals with or without gBRCA1 or gBRCA2 variants and analysed their blood counts by BRCA status (group stratification in Figure 1A). Lymphocyte counts within the WT and gBRCA1 groups were similar between HD and breast cancer patients (Supplementary Figure S1A and B, available at, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601). Point mutations compared with larger mutations within the gBRCA1 group were also not significantly different (Supplementary Figure S1C, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601). However, compared with all WT participants, we observed a significant reduction in the concentration of circulating lymphocytes in whole blood of gBRCA1 carriers (P = 0.049) but not gBRCA2 carriers (P = 0.64; Figure 1B). Multivariate analysis showed that neither age at blood donation nor the presence of breast cancer were significantly correlated with lymphocyte concentrations; only the germline group (Figure 1C). Further analysis of circulating lymphocytes by flow cytometry confirmed their phenotype as T cells, with overall CD3+, CD4+ and CD8+ T cell counts by blood volume significantly decreased in gBRCA1 carriers compared with WT (Figure 1D-F). CD4/CD8 ratio as well as proportions of T cells within live lymphocytes were not significantly different between any of the groups (Supplementary Figure S1D and E, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601).

Significantly reduced BRCA1 expression in activated gBRCA1 carrier T cells

Reduced circulating lymphocytes in gBRCA1 carriers may reflect a role for BRCA1 expression in supporting T cell homeostasis, though its exact role remains to be determined. This would correlate with previous studies showing T cell development cannot occur in homozygous BRCA1 knockout mice.19 Therefore, we next wanted to determine whether BRCA1 is expressed in human T cells and, importantly, whether germline variants impact expression levels and whether this could lead to an altered phenotype. To investigate this, we designed an in vitro T cell expansion and phenotyping protocol (Figure 2A).

BRCA1 protein and mRNA expression levels were undetectable in resting T cells from PBMCs of WT HD but significantly increased after TCR stimulation, returning to baseline by day 10 (Figure 2B and Supplementary Figure S2A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601). Interestingly, when stimulation was repeated weekly, BRCA1 expression increased over time (Figure 2C and Supplementary Figure S2B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601). This expression was similar in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure 2D).

We then compared the expression levels of BRCA1 in activated PBMCs from gBRCA1 carriers and WT patients (all breast cancer patients in both groups). The expression of BRCA1 protein from gBRCA1 carriers was significantly reduced to half that of WT (Figure 2E and F). mRNA levels were also reduced to 0.8-fold that of WT in gBRCA1 PBMCs (Figure 2G). BRCA1/2 expression at day 2 and 4 post-TCR stimulation was comparable to that observed in common breast cancer cell lines (Supplementary Figure S2C, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601).

Activated T cells from gBRCA1 carriers are functionally impaired

Given the expansive role of BRCA1 as a transcription factor as well as a DNA damage repair protein,32 we wanted to explore further any differences between activated T cells from gBRCA1 and WT patients.

At the gene expression level, we observed no notable differences in the transcriptome of CD4+ nor CD8+ T cells between the three groups at baseline (no stimulation). However, after 3 weeks of repeated stimulation, at day 17 (D17), there were significant differences in the transcriptome according to BRCA status between the WT (breast cancer and HD) and the gBRCA1 breast cancer patients (Supplementary Figure S3A and B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601). To reduce variability, we focused solely on breast cancer patients and compared gBRCA1 and WT directly. We identified 105 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between breast cancer WT and gBRCA1 CD4+ T cells (FDR < 0.1 to account for low number of patients; Figure 3A and Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601). Notable genes upregulated in gBRCA1 CD4+ T cells were ATF5 (cellular stress), TNFRSF4 (activation marker OX40), HLA (antigen presentation) and CTLA4 (activation, differentiation and immunosuppression; Figure 3A). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using common hallmark and gene ontology pathways showed 40 dysregulated pathways between activated CD4+ T cells from WT and gBRCA1 breast cancer patients (Figure 3B and Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601). We observed a significant downregulation of cell cycle pathways including G2M checkpoints and E2F targets in CD4+ T cells from gBRCA1 carriers compared with WT (Figure 3B). To investigate DNA repair pathways, we carried out an adjusted analysis accounting for proliferation markers (MKI67, TOP2A, PCNA, MCM2, TYMS, CCNB1, STMN1, and BIRC5) and detected a significant upregulation of DNA repair pathways across 3 databases (Figure 3C). In the CD8+ analysis, we observed 75 DEGs in gBRCA1 breast cancer patients compared with WT (FDR < 0.1) post-activation (Figure 3D and Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601). There were, however, many hallmark and gene ontology gene sets (157) that were significantly deregulated (Figure 3E and Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601). This again included multiple cell cycle and proliferation pathways, such as apoptosis and p53 signaling, which were downregulated in CD8+ T cells from gBRCA1 carriers compared with WT (Figure 3E). Interestingly, DNA repair pathways were, however, not altered in the gBRCA1 CD8+ T cells after accounting for cell cycle changes (Figure 3F).

Phenotypic analysis of activated T cells from gBRCA1 carriers compared with WT by flow cytometry correlates with the transcriptomic analysis, displaying a significant fold reduction in percentage of Ki67+ CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in gBRCA1 carriers (Figure 3G and H), as well as decreased fold secretion of granzyme B (GZMB) (Figure 3I) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) after 38 days of chronic stimulation (Figure 3J). This may suggest that alterations in cell cycle regulation as a result of BRCA1 variants can lead to functional consequences.

To establish whether these differences are broad or T cell specific, we compared dysregulated pathways between CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. The majority of dysregulated pathways were unique to either CD4+ T cells or CD8+ T cells, with only 10 pathways in common (Figure 3K). These overlapping pathways were related to interferon signaling and antigen presentation, suggesting that BRCA1 haploinsufficiency also results in a shared global T cell stress, along with lineage-specific differences.

Tumour TCR hyperexpansion correlates with frameshift mutation burden in primary TNBC

Our data so far suggest that heterozygous germline mutations in BRCA1 significantly reduce expression levels in activated human T cells, suggesting the WT BRCA1 allele cannot fully compensate for the loss of the mutant allele. Moreover, the loss of BRCA1 correlates with a dysfunctional phenotype upon in vitro activation. These findings may reflect an impaired ability of heterozygous BRCA1-variant T cells to sustain activation over time, consistent with premature dysfunction or exhaustion after repeated stimulation. We next investigated whether there were differences in the TCR repertoire from gBRCA1 patients with breast cancer compared with non-carriers. To investigate this, we collected pretreatment FFPE breast tumour samples from 18 patients diagnosed with early-stage TNBC with or without gBRCA1 variants (clinical information in Supplementary Table S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601) and carried out TCR sequencing and whole exome sequencing to investigate whether neoantigen load, particularly frameshift mutations, might drive clonal expansion of intratumoral T cells (top mutations in each patient shown in Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101601).

Overall, we observed a significant difference in TIL clonality between gBRCA1 carriers and WT. Small and rare clones were negligible (average of <2 clones), with large and hyperexpanded clones being the most prevalent across both groups. We observed significantly increased expansion in gBRCA1 TIL compared with WT for hyperexpanded frequencies (Figure 4A), even though the TIL counts, measured from tumour H&E, were similar between gBRCA1 and WT tumours (Figure 4B). By plotting the average cumulative frequency of the top 450 clonotypes, we observed that the top 70 clonotypes accounted for 80% of the cumulative TCR frequency in the gBRCA1 tumors compared with ∼65% for the WT (Figure 4C). This indicates that the top clones in gBRCA1 tumours dominate the TCR repertoire, suggesting clonal expansion of a limited number of T cells. There was no correlation between TIL quantity and hyperexpansion of TCRs (Figure 4D) nor TMB (Figure 4E) within the gBRCA1 cohort. However, we observed a significant negative correlation between TIL and frameshift mutation burden (FSB, Figure 4F). Despite this, we observed a strong and significant correlation of increased FSB and TMB with TCR hyperexpanded clones in the gBRCA1 cohort (Figure 4G and H), with no correlation by age (Figure 4I).

Discussion

The impact of homozygous loss of BRCA1 in mouse T cell development was first described over two decades ago.19 However, to our knowledge, no studies have reported on the direct impact that heterozygous germline BRCA1 variants may have on human T cell phenotype and function. Here, we characterised differences in the phenotype and function of T cells from heterozygous human BRCA1 germline carriers.

We found that peripheral T cells from gBRCA1 carriers show reduced concentrations in whole blood and express less BRCA1 post-activation compared with BRCA1-WT (Figures 1 and 2). Reduced circulating lymphocytes in gBRCA1 carriers is consistent with the literature.20 Reduced numbers of peripheral T cells could have important implications for tumour surveillance, infection clearance and chronic immune conditions. As of time of writing, two clinical trials are currently investigating the effects of gBRCA1 variants on systemic immunity (NCT04499534 and NCT04367675), with results pending.

Increased expression of GZMB and IFN-γ is associated with improved prognosis in TNBC.33,34 Our data suggest that antigen exposure can lead to dysregulated proliferation within gBRCA1 T cells, leading to increased DNA repair pathway activation but reduced homologous recombination, potentially leading to increased DNA damage over time (Figure 3). This correlates with reduced Ki67 expressing cells and secretion of GZMB and IFN-γ (Figure 3). This supports a model of BRCA1 haploinsufficiency predisposing T cells to exhaustion under sustained stimulation.

In breast cancer, antitumour immune response depends on activation and expansion of intratumoral T cells recognising tumour antigens.35 TCR clonality was significantly hyperexpanded in gBRCA1-associated tumours, compared with BRCA1-WT, and we observed a strong positive correlation between FSB and hyperexpansion in gBRCA1 carriers (Figure 4). This finding is consistent with the literature, where indel mutations have been shown to be highly immunogenic in cancer36 and suggests a response to neoantigens derived from frameshift mutations. However, notably, this hyperexpansion was not associated with increased TIL quantity by H&E, suggesting a dissociation between antigen recognition and effective immune infiltration.

Given our findings, we propose that while T cells in gBRCA1 carriers can recognise neoantigens (likely derived from frameshift mutations) and expand, intrinsic defects in BRCA1 function may impair their ability to sustain successful effector function or persist in the tumour microenvironment, potentially due to replication stress or genomic damage (as we observed significant downregulation of cell cycle pathways but increased DNA repair genes in gBRCA1 T cells; Figure 3). Although tumour-intrinsic mechanisms such as HLA loss, neoantigen load or microenvironmental factors are likely to also contribute, our findings support a model of BRCA1 haploinsufficiency-intrinsic T cell dysfunction. This concept is consistent with previous studies reporting altered immunosurveillance in precancerous mammary tissue from gBRCA1 carriers.37

We acknowledge that our results remain hypothesis-generating and require future validation owing to the limited size of the dataset, as gBRCA1 carriers are a rare population. Moreover, we did not fully characterise the mechanism for how BRCA1 may be critical for T cell function. Future directions should also aim to investigate differences between BRCA1 and BRCA2 germline variants in the TME, particularly relating to proliferation stress and exhaustion.

In conclusion, we identify phenotypic differences in systemic and tumour-infiltrating T cells in human gBRCA1 variant carriers, including impaired effector function and skewed clonal expansion associated with frameshift mutation burden. These findings support a role for BRCA1 haploinsufficiency in shaping antitumour immunity and provide a framework for exploring immune dysfunction in other inherited cancer syndromes. Our results may also inform future studies evaluating immunotherapy strategies in BRCA1-associated TNBC.
