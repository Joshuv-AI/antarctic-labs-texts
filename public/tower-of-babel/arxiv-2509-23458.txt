Arnold Filtser Bar-Ilan University

Abstract
Given a weighted digraph G = ( V , E , w ) G=(V,E,w) , a stochastic embedding into DAGs is a distribution 𝒟 \mathcal{D} over pairs of DAGs ( D 1 , D 2 ) (D_{1},D_{2}) such that for every u , v u,v : (1) the reachability is preserved: u ↝ G v u\rightsquigarrow_{G}v (i.e., v v is reachable from u u in G G ) implies that u ↝ D 1 v u\rightsquigarrow_{D_{1}}v or u ↝ D 2 v u\rightsquigarrow_{D_{2}}v (but not both), and (2) distances are dominated: d G ​ ( u , v ) ≤ min ⁡ { d D 1 ​ ( u , v ) , d D 2 ​ ( u , v ) } d_{G}(u,v)\leq\min\{d_{D_{1}}(u,v),d_{D_{2}}(u,v)\} . The stochastic embedding 𝒟 \mathcal{D} has expected distortion t t if for every u , v ∈ V u,v\in V , 𝔼 ( D 1 , D 2 ) ∼ 𝒟 [ d D 1 ( u , v ) ⋅ 𝟙 [ u ↝ D 1 v ] + d D 2 ( u , v ) ⋅ 𝟙 [ u ↝ D 2 v ] ] ≤ t ⋅ d G ( u , v ) . \mathbb{E}_{(D_{1},D_{2})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[d_{D_{1}}(u,v)\cdot\mathds{1}[u\rightsquigarrow_{D_{1}}v]+d_{D_{2}}(u,v)\cdot\mathds{1}[u\rightsquigarrow_{D_{2}}v]\right]\leq t\cdot d_{G}(u,v)\penalty\ . Further, we can sample DAGs from this distribution in O ~ ​ ( m ) \tilde{O}(m) time.

Introduction

Metric embedding is a powerful algorithmic paradigm with numerous applications.
The basic idea is to take a complicated metric space and embed its points into a simpler metric space, while approximately preserving the original structure.
Then, an algorithmic problem can be solved in the simpler embedded space and then pulled back to the original space.
Here we focus on low-distortion metric embeddings, where the goal is to approximately preserve pairwise distances between points.
1
1

1

Classically, we say that a metric embedding
f:X\rightarrow Y
, between metric spaces
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
, has distortion
t
if
\forall x,y\in X
,
d_{X}(x,y)\leq d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))\leq t\cdot d_{X}(x,y)
.

There are a variety of celebrated results of this form; a partial list includes:
embedding general metric spaces into Euclidean space
[

,
ABN11
,
BFT24

[

embedding the shortest path metric of topologically restricted (undirected) graphs (e.g., planar, minor-free) into
\ell_{1}

[

,

,

,

,

,

embedding low-treewidth graphs (with additive distortion)
[

,

]

[

[

There is a natural tension between the “simplicity” of the target metric space and the distortion that one can achieve.
A prominent example is the shortest path metric of trees. While tree metrics are very simple (indeed, many NP-complete problems are tractable on trees), embedding a simple metric space such as the shortest path metric of the
n
-cycle
C_{n}
requires distortion
\Omega(n)

[
RR98
,
Gup01
]
.
Fortunately, there is a way to circumvent this barrier: stochastic metric embeddings (introduced by
[
AKPW95
]

Here, instead of embedding points from
X
to
Y
with a worst-case guarantee, there is a distribution over metric embeddings into metric spaces in some metric family
\mathcal{F}
(e.g., tree metrics), such that every pairwise distance is preserved only in expectation.
2
2

2

Formally, a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over metric embeddings
f:X\rightarrow Y
, for
(Y,d_{Y})\in\mathcal{F}

t

\forall x,y\in X
it is dominating (i.e.,
\forall f\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})

d_{X}(x,y)\leq d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))

\mathbb{E}_{f\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))\right]\leq t\cdot d_{X}(x,y)

For a digraph

First, observe that it is impossible to get finite expected distortion by sampling a single embedding into a DAG.
Indeed, if
u

u\rightsquigarrow_{D}v
and

\infty
.
It therefore makes sense to embed a digraph into a distribution over pairs of DAGs
(D_{1},D_{2})
such that for every pair
u,v
with
u\rightsquigarrow_{G}v
, it holds that either
u\rightsquigarrow_{D_{1}}v
or
u\rightsquigarrow_{D_{2}}v
.
Next, observe that we cannot hope that the DAGs in the support of the embedding will be sparse. Indeed, consider the complete bipartite digraph
\overrightarrow{K_{n,n}}
(see
footnote

4
); one has to use
\Omega(n^{2})
edges.

The third observation is that it is completely hopeless to embed into subgraphs.
Indeed, consider the directed
n
-cycle
C_{n}
, where there are directed edges
v_{1}\rightarrow v_{2}\rightarrow v_{3}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow v_{n}\rightarrow v_{1}
.
Then any DAG subgraph can satisfy reachability only for one of the pairs
(v_{1},v_{n}),(v_{2},v_{1}),\dots,(v_{n},v_{n-1})
.

Definition 1

(Stochastic Embedding into DAGs)
.

Given a weighted digraph
G=(V,E,w)
, a stochastic embedding into DAGs is a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over pairs
(D_{1},D_{2})
such that
D_{1},D_{2}
are DAGs over
V
, and for every
u,v\in V
:

•

Dominating distances:
d_{G}(u,v)\leq\min\{d_{D_{1}}(u,v),d_{D_{2}}(u,v)\}

•

Reachability preservation: If
u\rightsquigarrow_{G}v
, then either
u\rightsquigarrow_{D_{1}}v
or
u\rightsquigarrow_{D_{2}}v
(but not both).

We say that
\mathcal{D}
has expected distortion
t
if

\forall u,v\in V\quad\mathbb{E}_{(D_{1},D_{2})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[d_{D_{1}}(u,v)\cdot\mathds{1}[u\rightsquigarrow_{D_{1}}v]+d_{D_{2}}(u,v)\cdot\mathds{1}[u\rightsquigarrow_{D_{2}}v]\right]\leq t\cdot d_{G}(u,v)\penalty\ .

The sparsity of
\mathcal{D}
is
\max_{D\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D})}\{|E(D)|\}
, the maximum number of edges in any of the DAGs in the support of
\mathcal{D}
.

Assadi, Hoppenworth, and Wein
[
AHW25
]
showed that every digraph with polynomial
aspect ratio

\Phi=\frac{\max\left\{d(u,v)\mid u\rightsquigarrow_{G}v\right\}}{\min\left\{d(u,v)\mid u\rightsquigarrow_{G}v\right\}}

G
.

stochastically embeds into DAGs with expected distortion
\tilde{O}(\log^{3}n)
and sparsity
\tilde{O}(m+n)

1.1
DAG Cover

Roughly speaking, in a stochastic embedding into trees we construct a collection of embeddings into trees such that the distortion of every pair is small on average. In contrast, in a tree cover we embed into a collection of trees such that the minimum distortion of every pair is small (while all the trees in the cover are dominating).
Tree covers were introduced by Arya
et al.
[
ADM+95
]
in the context of Euclidean spaces and have been extensively studied ever since.
Every
n
-point metric admits a tree cover with distortion
2k-1
and
O(n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{k}}}\log n)
trees
[
TZ05
]

O(\log n)
with
O(\log n)
trees.
Tree covers have also been studied in the context of doubling metrics
[

,
BFN22
,
FGN24

CCL+24a
,
CCL+25
]
, for planar and minor-free graphs
[
BFN22
,
CCL+23
,
CCL+24b
]
, into spanning trees
[
GKR04
,
ACE+20
]
. There are also Ramsey trees (where every vertex has a tree in the cover approximating its shortest path tree)
[

,

Definition 2

(DAG Cover)
.

Given a weighted digraph

Technical Ideas

In this section we present a simplified overview of the techniques and ideas in this paper, as well as in previous work.
Brief and oversimplified summary:
Bartal
[
Bar96
]
constructed stochastic tree embeddings with expected distortion
O(\log^{2}n)
using LDDs, and later
[
Bar98
]
improved this to expected distortion
\tilde{O}(\log n)
using the so-called Seymour trick.
Bernstein
et al.
[
BNW25
]
introduced and constructed directed LDDs with loss parameter
O(\log^{2}n)
.
Later, Bringmann
et al.
[
BFHL25
]
improved the loss parameter to
\tilde{O}(\log n)
using the Seymour trick.
Assadi
et al.
[
AHW25
]
constructed a DAG cover by hierarchically using the LDDs of
[
BFHL25
]
.
Embedding digraphs into DAGs is much more delicate than embedding graphs into trees, and there are considerable challenges.

[
AHW25
]
constructed a stochastic embedding into DAGs with expected distortion
\tilde{O}(\log^{3}n)
. The analysis is similar in spirit to
[
Bar96
]
: they lose a
\tilde{O}(\log n)
factor across
O(\log n)
different scales, and another
\log n
factor due to technical reasons.

In this paper we introduce a simpler framework to analyze the expected distortion. Using this framework one can show that the stochastic embedding of
[
AHW25
]
actually has expected distortion
\tilde{O}(\log^{2}n)
.
We then show that one can apply the Seymour trick on top of the entire hierarchy of directed LDDs (and not only per scale).
Finally, this more sensitive approach requires a delicate analysis of the ball carving process w.r.t. a path (previously, it was analyzed only w.r.t. an edge).
Eventually, we obtain the desired
\tilde{O}(\log n)
expected distortion.

2.1
The Origins: Undirected Graphs

Low Diameter Decomposition (LDD) for Undirected Graphs.

Given an undirected graph
G=(V,E,w)
, a
(\beta,\Delta)
-LDD
(low diameter decomposition) is a distribution over partitions
\mathcal{C}
of
V
such that every cluster
C
in every partition
\mathcal{C}
has diameter
9
9

9

The diameter of a cluster
C
is
\max_{u,v}d_{G}(u,v)
— that is, the maximum pairwise distance in the original graph. This is often called weak diameter (strong diameter is
\max_{u,v}d_{G[C]}(u,v)
). We will use the same diameter definition for both graphs and digraphs.

at most
\Delta
, and for every pair
u,v\in V
, the probability that
u,v
belong to different clusters is at most
\beta\cdot\frac{d_{G}(u,v)}{\Delta}
.

\beta
is called the
loss parameter
(sometimes called Lipschitz parameter).
LDDs are extensively studied, and every
n
-vertex graph admits LDDs with loss parameter
\beta=O(\log n)
(regardless of
\Delta
), which is also tight
[
Bar96
]
.
A classic way to construct LDDs is by ball carving.
Denote by
A=V
the set of active vertices. Initially, all vertices are active.
Iteratively, while
A\neq\emptyset
we construct clusters as follows:
Take an arbitrary center
x\in A
, sample a radius
R\sim\mathsf{Exp}(\lambda)
using an exponential distribution with parameter
\lambda=\Theta(\frac{\log n}{\Delta})
, and carve a cluster
C=B_{G[A]}(x,R)
containing all active vertices at distance at most
R
from
x
in the induced graph
G[A]
. We then update
A\leftarrow A\setminus C
and continue until all vertices are clustered.
One can show that, as we carved at most
n
balls, w.h.p. all the sampled radii were at most
\frac{\Delta}{2}
, and thus all clusters indeed have diameter at most
\Delta
.
10
10

10

In fact,
[
Bar96
]
, as well as this paper, use a truncated exponential distribution: sampling radii conditioned on being at most
\frac{\Delta}{2}
. The analysis is based on the fact that the difference between the two distributions is small.

On the other hand, using the memoryless property of the exponential distribution, once one of
u,v
joined a cluster, the probability that the other will not join is bounded by
1-e^{-\lambda\cdot d_{G}(u,v)}=O(\log n)\cdot\frac{d_{G}(u,v)}{\Delta}
, and thus we obtain an LDD.

Stochastic Tree Embeddings with Expected Distortion
O(\log^{2}n)

[
Bar96
]
.

LDDs are a basic routine in many algorithms.
In particular, they are used in the construction of stochastic tree embeddings.
The framework is as follows:
let
\Phi=\mathrm{poly}(n)
be the maximum distance in
G
(the minimum distance is
1
).
Construct a laminar collection of partitions
\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{i},\dots
where every cluster in
\mathcal{C}_{i}
has diameter at most
\Phi\cdot 2^{-i}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{i}
refines
\mathcal{C}_{i-1}
.
This is done by taking each cluster
C\in\mathcal{C}_{i-1}

If we pay

The improvement idea is to show that it is possible to pay only

The basic intuition is as follows:
Suppose that, “miraculously”, we are guaranteed that no matter how we choose the radii, every carved ball will contain about

\approx\frac{m}{\mu}
.

Note that if the pair

is separated, the distance between them in the tree will be

.
Assume by induction on the number of edges

that the expected distortion is

If both

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d_{T}(s,t)\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\text{cut}\right]\cdot O(\Delta)+\Pr\left[\text{no cut}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[d_{T}(s,t)\mid s,t\text{ belong to a cluster with }\approx\mu\text{ edges}\right]

\displaystyle\leq O(\log\frac{m}{\mu})\cdot\frac{d_{G}(s,t)}{\Delta}\cdot O(\Delta)+O(\log\mu)\cdot d_{G}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\left(O(\log\frac{m}{\mu})+O(\log\mu)\right)\cdot d_{G}(s,t)=O(\log m)\cdot d_{G}(s,t)\penalty\ ,

(2)

and thus the induction holds.
Removing the “miraculous” assumption (that all carved balls have size about
\mu

Roughly, this is done in

We now take a closer look at the argument, from a potential function perspective.
Consider an edge

\tilde{O}(\log m)\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\Delta}
, which matches its initial potential.
Suppose that we grow an out ball

such that we are guaranteed that the number of edges in the ball will be
\approx\mu
(regardless of the sampled
R

Accordingly, we sample

using an exponential distribution with parameter
\lambda_{\mu}=\frac{1}{\Delta}\cdot\tilde{O}(\log\frac{m}{\mu})
.
We think of

Suppose that
u

, while
v

Using memorylessness, the probability that

will not join

\tilde{O}(\log\frac{m}{\mu})\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\Delta}
.
Morally,

\tilde{O}(\log\frac{m}{\mu})\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\Delta}
of its potential.
If
e
was fortunate enough and was not cut, it will now belong to a cluster of size
\approx\mu
, and appropriately has a remaining potential of
O(\log\mu)\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\Delta}
.
If
e
now belongs to a cluster with diameter greater than
\Delta
, we will continue to carve balls from this cluster, and argue inductively that the overall probability of being cut is

\tilde{O}(\log\frac{m}{\mu})\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\Delta}+\tilde{O}(\log\mu)\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\Delta}=\tilde{O}(\log m)\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\Delta}
.
However, it might be the case that

does not belong to any SCC, or that the SCC containing

. In this case, the LDD algorithm will stop processing

\tilde{O}(\log\mu)\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\Delta}

Laminar Topological Order with Expected Distortion
\tilde{O}(\log n)
over edges.

Next we will create a laminar topological order with a more local view.
That is, given a SCC of diameter
\Delta
, we will follow
[
BFHL25
]
and carve balls using a distribution proportional to the local density (
\lambda_{\mu}
), while attempting to construct clusters with diameter
\leq\frac{\Delta}{2}

As a result, we will delete some edges, and get SCCs

On each SCC

Continuing in this manner, we will eventually get a DAG, and thus a laminar topological order.

Fix an edge

be the diameter of the cluster where it was added to the cut set

We argue that

That is, the expected diameter of the cluster where

\tilde{O}(\log m)\cdot w(e)

Suppose that we carve a ball
B=B_{G}^{+}(x,R)
such that we are guaranteed that the number of edges in the ball will be
\approx\mu
(regardless of the sampled
R
). Accordingly, we sample

using parameter
\lambda_{\mu}=\frac{1}{\Delta}\cdot\tilde{O}(\log\frac{m}{\mu})
.
We do it gradually as before. Suppose that
u\in B
. The probability that

(that is, the edge

\tilde{O}(\log\frac{m}{\mu})\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\Delta}
.
If the edge

is not cut, then either

\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{e}]=\tilde{O}(\log\mu)\cdot w(e)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{e}]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[e\text{ is cut}\right]\cdot\Delta+\Pr\left[e\text{ is not cut}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{e}\mid e\text{ is not cut}]

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{O}(\log\frac{m}{\mu})\cdot\frac{w(e)}{\Delta}\cdot\Delta+\tilde{O}(\log\mu)\cdot w(e)=\tilde{O}(\log m)\cdot w(e)\penalty\ .

Thus, instead of paying loss
\tilde{O}(\log n)
in every scale, we are now paying

over all scales!
Can we utilize this new gain to improve the expected distortion? Is it possible to plug it into

5
to prove that

A priori, no.
The issue is that the triangle inequality is not sufficient to generalize the analysis from an edge to a path.
If miraculously it so happens that all the balls that we carve are guaranteed to have density
\approx\mu

Unfortunately, this is not the case.
That is, different portions of the shortest

This ball contains the subpath

Analyzing w.r.t. a Path.

As it turns out, the way to proceed with the analysis is to consider the effects of the carving on a path as a hole (and not only w.r.t. individual edges), which is considerably more delicate.
Assume by induction that given a SCC

-

3.1
DAGs and Laminar Topological Order

A
DAG
(directed acyclic graph) is a digraph not containing any directed cycle. In particular, the set of SCCs is the set of singleton vertices.
Given a DAG
D=(V,E,w)
, a
topological order
is a total order
<_{\rm TO}
over
V
such that for every edge
(u,v)\in E
,
u<_{\rm TO}v
.
Note that this total order is not necessarily unique.
Given a digraph
G
, one can construct a topological order over the SCCs. That is, for two SCCs
C_{1},C_{2}
, if there is a path from a vertex in
C_{1}
to a vertex in
C_{2}
, then
C_{1}
must appear in the topological order before
C_{2}
.
Given a digraph
G
, which is not necessarily a DAG, it is often useful to still order its vertices in a topological order w.r.t. some DAG contained in
G
.

A key notion in previous work on stochastic embedding into trees is the laminar partition: a collection of partitions of
V
,
\mathcal{P}_{0},\mathcal{P}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{P}_{i},\dots
, such that all the clusters in
\mathcal{P}_{i}
have diameter at most
2^{i}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{i}
refines
\mathcal{P}_{i+1}
.
The directed analog we introduce here is the notion of laminar topological order. This is essentially a laminar partition (implicitly) equipped with an order. Here all the clusters are strongly connected, and the final order over the singleton clusters is a topological order w.r.t. some DAG contained in
G
.

Definition 3

(Laminar Topological Order)
.

Given a digraph
G=(V,E,w)
, a laminar topological order of
G
is a pair
(<_{D},\mathcal{P})
where
<_{D}
is a total order over
V
, and
\mathcal{P}\subseteq P(V)
is a set of clusters with the following properties:

1.

Singleton clusters: For every vertex
v\in V
,
\{v\}\in\mathcal{P}
.

2.

Hierarchy: For every two clusters
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
, either the clusters
are disjoint (
C_{1}\cap C_{2}=\emptyset
), or one contains the other
(
C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}
or
C_{2}\subseteq C_{1}
).

3.

Continuity: Every cluster
C\in\mathcal{P}
is contiguous w.r.t.
<_{D}
. That is, for every
u,v\in C
, for every
x
such that
u<_{D}x<_{D}v
, it holds that
x\in C
.

4.

Connectivity: For every cluster
C\in\mathcal{P}
,
G[C]
is strongly connected.

Given a laminar topological order
(<_{D},\mathcal{P})
, and a cluster

, we denote
\Delta_{C}=\mathrm{diam}(C,G)=\max_{u,v\in C}d_{G}(u,v)
.
Additionally, we denote by

and

the first and last vertices in the cluster (w.r.t. the order
<_{D}
).
Given a pair of vertices

.
If there is no such cluster then
\mathrm{Parent}_{\mathcal{P}}(u,v)=\emptyset
.
When the laminar topological order is clear from the context, we use the notation

. If

.

The
Digraph-Partition

This algorithm is the basis for the creation of our laminar topological order, which leads to the creation of the DAGs.
The algorithm is similar to the cutting procedure from
[
BFHL25
]
(which in turn was used to create directed low-diameter decompositions).
The algorithm receives as input a weighted digraph
G=(V,E,w)

\Delta>0

In our implementation,
\Delta
is the weak diameter of
G
(in some graph not given to the algorithm), while

.
Nevertheless, we will think of them as general parameters, with the guarantee

Likewise, the algorithm will only be executed on strongly connected digraphs. However, for the same considerations, we will not assume that during the analysis.

We denote by
{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}A}\subseteq V
the set of
active vertices

{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}S}\subseteq E

Initially, all the vertices are active
A\leftarrow V

.
We say that an

if both its endpoints are active.
The algorithm repeatedly carves a ball
B_{\ell,j}
of (a random) radius

There are
L
different

In each phase, as long as the algorithm can find centers fulfilling the density requirement, it carves balls around these centers. We call each such carving an

of the algorithm.
Once no appropriate center is found, the algorithm moves to the next phase.
After the
L

S
all the edges between
\mathcal{R}
vertices of weight at least

It is guaranteed (see

(while there is no guarantee on the diameter of other SCCs in

The heart of the algorithm is the random choice of the radii of the carved balls.
The algorithm uses the following parameters (depending only on
m

\Delta

•

{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}L}=\lceil\log\log(m+1)\rceil

{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}r_{0}}:=\frac{\Delta}{2^{L+4}}

{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\mu_{\ell}}:=2^{2^{L-\ell}}

•

{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\lambda_{\ell}}=\frac{1}{r_{\ell}-r_{\ell-1}}\cdot\ln(\frac{2\mu_{\ell}}{\delta})

8
Conclusion and Open Problems

The main contribution of this paper is a stochastic embedding into DAGs with expected distortion
\tilde{O}(\log n)
and sparsity
\tilde{O}(m)
.
The main underlying tool is our (implicit) construction of directed LDDs (low-diameter decompositions).
The best-known loss factor
\beta
for directed LDDs is
O(\log n\log\log n)

[
BFHL25
,
Li25
]
, whereas there is a lower bound of
\Omega(\log n)

[
Bar96
]
.
Thus, by constructing a stochastic embedding into DAGs based on directed LDDs, one cannot hope to improve the result in this paper by more than an
O(\log\log n)
factor.
However, this does not rule out other approaches.
Several open questions follow:

1.

Settling the best possible expected distortion. At present, we cannot even rule out the possibility of a stochastic embedding into DAGs with sparsity
\tilde{O}(m)
and constant expected distortion.
Such a result, if possible, will not follow the steps of this paper (and
[
AHW25
]
) using directed LDDs.
On the other hand, it is not clear how to generalize the classic expander-based lower bounds for stochastic tree embeddings to the richer framework of DAGs.
Settling the best possible expected distortion is the main open problem.

2.

General trade-off for DAG covers. We constructed a DAG cover with
O(\log n)
DAGs and distortion
\tilde{O}(\log n)
.
In contrast, for trees we have a full trade-off: one can get a tree cover with
O(n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{k}}}\log n)
trees and distortion
2k-1

[
TZ05
]
.
Can we get a similar trade-off for DAG covers?

3.

Ramsey-type DAG cover. Given a graph
G
, one can find a subset
M
of
n^{1-\frac{1}{k}}
vertices that embeds into a single tree with (worst-case) distortion
O(k)

[
MN07
]
.
It would be nice to find something similar for DAGs.
For starters, given a digraph, is it always possible to find a subset of
\frac{n}{2}
vertices that embed into
2
DAGs
(D_{1},D_{2})
with sparsity
\tilde{O}(m)
and worst-case distortion (naturally defined)
\tilde{O}(\log n)
?

This is a very interesting question. A general trade-off for the Ramsey-type setting will also likely imply a general trade-off for DAG covers. However, it is not clear how to approach this problem using LDDs.
Indeed, Ramsey-type embeddings of graphs into trees are based on LDDs, where one ensures that a certain set
M
of vertices is “padded” at all scales; that is, they are never too close to the boundary of a cluster. This condition is sufficient to ensure low distortion for this set of vertices.
We can hope to obtain a similar phenomenon for our laminar topological order. However, it would not imply low worst-case distortion for these vertices. Indeed, suppose that both
s
and
t
are “padded” at all scales.
It might be that some vertices on the shortest
s
–
t
path
\pi
are badly cut (while being in an SCC not containing
s,t
). This could inflict significant distortion on the pair
(s,t)
.

4.

Remove aspect-ratio dependence. In our sparsity, we have a polylogarithmic dependence on the aspect ratio. It is likely redundant, and it would be nice to remove it.

5.

Improve directed LDDs. The current upper bound for the loss factor is
O(\log n\log\log n)

[
BFHL25
,
Li25
]
, while the lower bound is
\Omega(\log n)

[
Bar96
]
. It would be nice to close this gap.
Note that a similar gap also appears in stochastic and Ramsey-type embeddings into spanning trees
[
AN19
,
ACE+20
]
, and it seems to come from the same source.
All these papers with
O(\log n\log\log n)
upper bounds are based on the Seymour trick
[
Sey95
]
,
while papers with
O(\log n)
upper bounds
[
FRT04
,
Bar04
,
MN07
]
rely on more sophisticated ideas,
which are not clearly applicable to (previously, spanning trees, and now) directed LDDs.

6.

Steiner points. This question was previously raised by
[
AHW25
]
.
In this paper (as well as in
[
AHW25
]
) we constructed DAGs over the vertex set
V
.
It is interesting to see whether allowing embedding into DAGs with Steiner points, that is, vertices not in
V
, could be helpful, leading to better upper bounds.
