Assessing the role of community pharmacists in early skin cancer detection: a real-world study
Authors: Morena Pappalardo, Marta Alcalde, Maria Estrada, Joan Francesc Mir, Jordi Casas, Cristina Rodriguez, Sònia Martinez, Josep Malvehy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Identification of at-risk population is essential for skin cancer screening. Community pharmacists (CPs) play an important role in the healthcare system by participating in disease screening strategies in Spain. However, their role in skin cancer screening, in collaboration with general physicians and dermatologists, has not been addressed. To assess the role of CPs in identification of population with risk factors for early skin cancer detection. 184 CPs from 104 pharmacies (4.34% of the total number of pharmacies in the province of Barcelona), 49 primary care physicians (PCPs), and referral dermatologists across basic healthcare areas (BHAs) of the province of Barcelona. Volunteers attending community pharmacies were recruited by CPs. A skin cancer risk factor questionnaire was administered to each participant. Upper limbs were examined and pigmented lesions larger than 2 mm were noted. Participants were classified as at risk if at least one skin cancer risk factor was present. All participants were referred to PCPs for a Total Body Skin Examination (TBSE) and a skin cancer risk factor questionnaire re-administered, to the concordance with the pharmaceutical one. Finally, patients with suspected skin cancer or risk factors that required a dermatological evaluation were referred to dermatology specialists. 340 participants were prospectively recruited in the study, 240 were assessed by PCPs, and 74 of them were finally referred to dermatologists for diagnostic confirmation. Among referred patients, 15 malignant lesions were confirmed: one malignant melanoma, one squamous cell carcinoma, and 13 basal cell carcinomas. Additionally, seven actinic keratosis were confirmed. CPs can be involved in skin cancer screening programmes, in coordination with PCPs and dermatologists. They were able to identify skin cancer risk factors that enabled the diagnosis of a significant number of occult skin cancers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54469-1.

Introduction

Skin cancer is the most common cancer in the European population1 and it is classified as malignant melanoma (MM), the deadliest type of skin cancer, and non-melanoma skin cancers, including Basal-Cell Carcinoma (BCC), Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC) and other less frequent ones (i.e. Merkel cell carcinoma, Kaposi sarcoma, dermatofibrosarcoma, cutaneous lymphomas, etc.).

The incidence of skin cancer is increasing every year2,3. In Catalonia, found in Northeastern Spain, an incidence of melanoma of approximately 14 cases over 100.000 people was estimated in 2017, with a mean annual rise of about 3.5%4.

Primary care physicians (PCPs) and community pharmacists (CPs) play a crucial role in health promotion5, in the adherence to treatments and in lifestyle improvement6. On the one hand, CPs, thanks to their proximity to the population they have already been involved in colorectal cancer7, HIV8 and HPV screenings and vaccination campaigns9. In 2019, Mir et al. described the role of CPs in the early detection of skin cancer risk factors10. On the other hand, PCPs who are gatekeepers of the Spanish Healthcare system, claim to dedicate a significant amount of time to skin cancer detection during consultations11, since abnormal skin lesions are a frequent complaint. Our study involves the participation of CPs in the initial triage of skin cancer risk factors, enabling referral of selected patients to PCPs for further clinical assessment.

There is strong evidence that cancer screening can prevent at least 60% of cancers12,13. Early diagnosis of skin cancer improves prognosis and cost-effectiveness. However, lack of patient awareness, long waiting times for consultations and challenges in providing access to dermatological diagnosis and treatment contribute to delays in diagnosis and subsequent increase of morbidity, health costs and mortality.

Risk factors for skin cancer are well-known. Family or personal history of skin cancer, chronic sun damage and the presence of a high number of melanocytic nevi are the main risk factors and can be identified through a questionnaire and a total body skin examination (TBSE). In previous studies, it was shown that the melanocytic nevi count on the arms is predictive of the total number of melanocytic nevi in the whole body14–16. Based on this assumption, we propose an intervention in which CPs were trained in the identification of risk factors and in the examination and counting of pigmented lesions on the arms using a standardised questionnaire; PCPs were responsible for TBSE and detection of risk factors by the same questionnaire and, when needed, for referral to the teledermatology service for the diagnosis confirmation17.

We thus describe, for the first time, a model for the identification of patients at risk of skin cancer, within a structured skin cancer early detection pathway, based on a multidisciplinary collaboration. Hereby we assess the impact of this model on referrals times and capability to triage patients at risk of skin cancer.

Methods

Training of CPs and PCPs

In September 2022, 184 CPs from 104 pharmacies (accounting for the 53.61% of the total number of pharmacies belonging to the 27 participating BHAs and for the 4.34% of the 2398 CPs in Barcelona, encompassing a population of 642,166 inhabitants) were trained in a 3-hour session in: identification of suspicious skin lesions; identification of risk factors for skin cancer; identification of lesions of chronic sun damage on exposed skin areas (face, scalp and arms); and counting pigmented lesions on upper limbs through a customized dermatologic ruler (see supplementary materials).

Forty-nine PCPs from the primary care centres were trained with a short introductory course on dermoscopy to screen tumours (i.e. 3-point dermoscopy check list, basic criteria for benign and malignant skin tumours). In addition, CPs, PCPs and administrative staff were trained with a 2-hour introductory course on the entire study circuit and provided with instructions on the use and entry of data into the digital platform Farmaserveis18, a freely accessible tool for pharmaceutical care services and projects open to pharmacists in Catalonia.

Design

The study was initially approved by the ERB (Registration number 19/188-P on date 30/10/2019), with amendment approved on 03/06/2022 following a temporary suspension due to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Inclusion criteria were: being a legal resident in Spain and in possession of a valid national healthcare service card (TSI), registered with a PCP from a primary care centre participating in the study, aged 45 and over. This age threshold was based on previous studies and campaigns10,19,20, in which a higher incidence of skin cancer was observed in this age group.

Exclusion criteria were: a health problem that could interfere with skin examination or inability to respond to the questionnaire.

Participants were recruited from October 2022 to December 2023, following study reactivation, by the CPs. All participants signed an informed consent at the community pharmacy.

As a first step, each participant was administered a validated questionnaire of skin cancer risk factors through the aforementioned digital platform and underwent arms examination, including a count of pigmented lesions, performed by CPs. These examinations were performed in a private consultation area of the community pharmacy.

The questionnaire was based on four principal skin cancer risk factors (see Table 1) shown in previous studies including metanalyses21: (1) personal history of skin cancer; (2) familial (first-degree) history of melanoma; (3) more than 20 pigmented lesions with a diameter bigger than 2 mm in both arms16; and (4) presence of chronic sun damage. If at least one skin cancer risk factor was detected, participants were classified as at risk by the platform. More specifically, the algorithm was developed to classify the participants into 3 different skin cancer risk scores: R1 for participants with no risk factors; R2 for participants with at least one skin cancer risk factor already under dermatological follow-up; and R3 for participants with at least one skin cancer risk factor and no dermatological follow-up.

Questionnaire of skin cancer risk factors

The pharmaceutical questionnaire was divided into three different sections: sociodemographic data, skin cancer risk factors and photoprotection behaviours (see supplementary materials). Additionally, questions on the use of photosensitising medications, evaluation of the skin Fitzpatrick phototype, occurrence of severe sunburns before the age of 18 and participants’ awareness of the presence of suspicious moles were included. This risk factor section was shared with the PCP questionnaire (see supplementary materials).

The photoprotection habits section included four questions on the application of sunscreen, SPF and frequency, and reasons for not practising sun protection.

Skin examination

CPs used a ruler (Customised R-150D Model, Dopan, Barcelona, Spain) for the counting of the pigmented lesions larger than 2 mm in diameter on the arms. A handheld dermoscope (DermLite GL-GLA1253, Aliso Viejo, CA, United States) was provided to each participating PCP. PCPs, after training, were requested to the dermoscope for the triage of suspicious lesions.

Statistical analysis

We performed concordance analysis to compare the pharmacist- and physician-administered questionnaires. For each question, the bivariate frequency table, Cohen’s Kappa, McNemar p-value, odds-ratio and effect size were calculated. For the pigmented lesions count, we performed a bootstrapped distribution of differences in mean, since they did not follow a normal distribution (see supplementary materials). The statistical analysis was implemented in RStudio, version 4.4.1, using a significance level of 5%.

Results

Pharmacy-based recruitment

The study was initially planned to start at early 2020, but it was delayed due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The study recruitment eventually started on October 1st, 2022, the pharmacy-based recruitment ended on December 2023, and the primary care visits ended on June 30th, 2024. Approximately 450 individuals were invited to participate in the study and 340 accepted. Among them, 257 females (75.59%) and 83 males (24.41%) provided signed informed consent at the pharmacy. The mean age was 65 years old (range 46–93 years in female and up to 92 years in males). One hundred participants did not attend the primary care appointment, among them 73 (73%) females and 27 (27%) males, with a mean age of 69 years old in female (range from 48 to 91 years) and 74 years old in male (range from 53 to 94 years). Among them, 72 (21.18%) answered the questionnaire, but did not attend the PCP visit and 28 (8.24%) did not want to continue with the study after the questionnaire. Of the 166 participants not referred to dermatological consultancy, 61 (25.42%) were referred to their family physician for a follow-up of a present lesion, 14 (5.83%) received a diagnosis and treatment by the same participating PCP, 85 (35.42%) did not need any further follow-up and 6 (2.5%) did not attend the visit to the PCP for unknown reasons. Recruitment by CPs was higher during the warmer months of the year, as shown in Fig. 2B, while during winter season, CPs reported lower willingness amongst participants to undergo examination of both arms.

Intervention and referral times

In the present study, we observed favourable referral and visit times, which reflect good coordination between professionals. The mean referral from the CPs to the PCPs was 39.85 days.

In fact, for the CPs, the mean duration of the visit was approximately 15 min, consistently with a previous study10. For physicians, the mean time to complete the questionnaire was 8.27 min (mean = 8.27; SD = 4.10; median = 7.00; IQR = 5.00) and the mean duration for TBSE was 13.85 min (mean = 13.85; SD = 6.91; median = 12.00; IQR = 5.00). The mean duration of the complete medical visit, including the questionnaire and the TBSE, was approximately 22 min.

Mean referral time from PCPs to the dermatology was 23.28 days. Although most of the referrals were completed by teledermatology services in a few days, the mean time was increased due to a few primary care centres encountering logistic problems and those that did not dispose of an established teledermatology protocol at the beginning of the study.

Analysis of concordance between pharmaceutical and medical questionnaire

Study of concordance was conducted to compare the pharmaceutical and the medical questionnaires and then to evaluate the level of agreement between the medical classification and the dermatological confirmation.

Interestingly, responses regarding family history of melanoma showed a substantial difference between the two questionnaires. In fact, 184 participants denied any family history and 15 participants reported a positive family history in both the pharmaceutical and the medical questionnaire, but the remaining 28, contradicted themselves in the two questionnaires (see Table 1).

R1–R2–R3 scores comparison: This score classification was initially designed to identify individuals at risk and to include a reference group of individuals as not being at risk. A summary of risk score assessment comparison is shown in a Sankey diagram (see Fig. 3). Despite we found differences in some answers affecting risk classification, the statistical analysis showed statistically significant agreement, with Cohen’s kappa of 0.653 (p < 0.001), McNemar’s chi-squared 10.256 (p < 0.001), odds ratio 2.2, Positive Predictive Value (PPV) 0.25, Negative Predictive Value (NPV) 0.853, sensitivity 0.231 and specificity 0.866.

These data show that there is good agreement between the pharmaceutical and medical risk assessment, although the odds ratio indicates a mild difference in the assessment of individuals without risk factors. As previously observed, CPs showed a mild tendency to overestimate the risk assessment. This was mostly due to the presence of chronic sun damage in most of the participants, and the difficulty in distinguishing between chronic sun-damage and benign lesions.

Three out of 12 participants classified as R1 by pharmacists were confirmed as not at risk and the rest showed at least one skin cancer risk factor assessed by the PCP. Of the 176 participants classifieds as R3 by CPs, 27 were classified as R1 by the PCP, and 6 patients were referred to the dermatologist for a suspicious lesion.

Pigmented lesion counting: There was a significant difference between the median number of lesions counted by the pharmacists and the physicians on the upper arms. CPs counted, on average, between 17 and 24 more lesions per patient than the PCPs, with 95% confidence intervals.

This was expected, since CPs were trained to count pigmented lesions while PCPs only counted pigmented melanocytic lesions, excluding solar lentigos. If the median is greater, it is very likely that the mean is also greater. Therefore, we quantified the confidence interval for the differences in means (see Supplementary Materials). Among 240 patients, 197 (82.08%) were confirmed to show chronic sun damage. According to the pharmaceutical evaluation, these participants generally demonstrated an overcount of pigmented lesions on the arms. The mean in the pigmented lesions counting was 33 (range = 0 to 223) by the CPs and 11 (range = 0 to 101) by the PCP.

Regarding the rest of the questions, surprisingly there was only one question showing concordance: “Do you think you have a suspicious mole?”. The other questions showed a significant discordance between the pharmaceutical and the medical questionnaire (see Table 2).

Of the 74 participants referred to dermatology, one had been classified as R1 (no risk factors) by the CPs. This participant was ultimately found to have a malignant lesion. The remaining referred R1 participants required no further dermatologic follow-up.

Among those classified as R3 by CPs, 27 were reclassified as R1 by PCPs. Six of these R3 participants were nevertheless referred to dermatology and one of them was referred for a suspected melanoma.

Regarding diagnostic yield, dermatology provided a definitive outcome in the following cases: 54 patients received a diagnosis of non-malignant lesion, and 20 lesions were classified as precancerous or in need of further analysis for diagnosis confirmation. Among the 20 patients with malignant lesions, one squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) (Fig. 4), one invasive cutaneous melanoma (Fig. 5), and thirteen basal cell carcinomas (BCC) (see Fig. 6) were confirmed. Additionally, seven actinic keratosis (AK) were confirmed. These results indicate that the incidence of skin cancer in the studied population was higher than expected.

Photoprotection habits

Photoprotection habits have been extensively promoted by CPs in Barcelona. The pharmaceutical questionnaire differs from the PCP questionnaire with regard the section on photoprotection habits.

A previous study conducted by the COFB in 201310 showed that only the 35% of the participants was using photoprotection with SPF 50+, in another study conducted in 2016 the 57% declared to use SPF 50+, in 2018 the 69.66% declared to use SPF50 + and according to our results the 70.59% of participants use SPF50+.

According to the photoprotection habits asked at the pharmacy, at the question “which SPF (solar factor protection) do you use?” 240 (70.59%) participants reported to use SPF 50+, 36 (10.59%) SPF 30, 3 (0.88%) SPF 15, 8 (2.35%) apply SPF < 15 and 46 (13.53%) didn’t know which SPF they use.

One hundred thirty participants (38.24%) reapply SPF when they are exposed to the sun and 199 participants (58.53%) do not reapply SPF, showing that there is still a need for education in terms of photoprotection habits.

Discussion

Barriers for skin cancer screening are multiple. Generally, primary care services can be busy and the capacity to offer skin cancer screening with TBSE and the cost limit their implementation and effectiveness.

In 2021, a pilot study by Mendonça et al.21 on teledermatology involving community pharmacies was implemented in Seville (Spain). In this study, the pharmacists were trained on dermoscopy and they could offer a paid service for skin analysis directly from the pharmacy. The present study differs in the organisation of the care pathway, as teledermatology is initiated through primary care physicians rather than directly from the pharmacy, and is integrated within the public healthcare system.

Most previously published initiatives were mainly focussed on the assessment of already suspicious skin lesions through CP-performed dermoscopy and teledermatology-based referral systems21,23,24. In these models, patients frequently sought the service because they already perceived the presence of a concerning lesion, and the CP primarily acted as an intermediary for image acquisition or teleconsultation with dermatology specialists. Similarly, the population-based Australian experience by Whiteman et al.22 evaluated the impact of melanoma screening strategies on melanoma diagnoses and biopsy rates at a broader population level.

In contrast, our model was primarily structured around the proactive identification of individuals at increased skin cancer risk within the community pharmacy setting, including patients who were often unaware of their own vulnerability or who did not initially report suspicious lesions. Therefore, the objective of our intervention was not exclusively lesion-oriented assessment, but rather risk-based screening integrated into routine pharmaceutical care.

Another relevant difference is the referral pathway. In many international pharmacy-based teledermatology models, suspicious lesions are directly referred to dermatology services21,23,24. However, our circuit was specifically designed according to the organisational structure of our Catalan public healthcare system, where PCPs act as the central gatekeepers, before eventual referral to dermatology specialists. Considering the high prevalence of skin cancer-related consultations, this orchestrated approach may represent a more sustainable and system-adapted model by combining community pharmacy-based early risk detection, Primary Care diagnostic screening and specialist confirmation only when clinically necessary.

Finally, unlike some previously published services based on voluntary or paid teledermatology consultations21,23,24, our study explored the integration of community pharmacy screening into a publicly coordinated healthcare pathway involving CPs, PCPs and dermatologists.

In fact, in our model, for the first time, CPs coordinate with primary care to implement the identification of risk factors for skin cancer in the general population, allowing the finding of a high number of skin cancers in a sample of 340 individuals in short interventions. Our pilot study shows that the interprofessional coordination can result in good waiting times; in fact, we could demonstrate that despite the winter season, the influenza waves and the influenza awareness immunisation campaigns that overloaded some of the participating primary care centres, pre-screened patients were referred in 39.85 days (median).

Despite the good pharmacists’ predisposition and participation, unfortunately there was a limited recruitment, mostly due to the winter season and consequently there could have been a bigger portion of the pharmacy users who could have been included in the study. However, CPs showed a positive impact in detecting populations at risk that would never go to PCPs. Additionally, we effectively can acknowledge a possible selection bias, since the recruitment resulted in a limited number of people, which is not representative of the population belonging to the different BHA.

Considering that our strategy pivots the role of CPs in skin cancer structured early detection pathway, we can confirm that they can make population aware of sun exposure risks, they can promote health education for correct photoprotection habits, and they can promote periodical skin check-ups. The trust and the high level of confidence of the population in CPs25,26 is a crucial factor for the implementation of these kinds of programmes. Pharmacy pre-screening enabled a quicker TBSE during primary care consultations. Our data showed that there is an increasing awareness in the use of photoprotection thanks to the impact of the pharmaceutical counselling. However, we encountered a considerable number of people who do not re-apply sunscreen correctly, showing that there is still a need for a better education of the population. Another need that we encountered during the study is the access to the patient’s clinical history by the CPs, for a more effective and impactful counselling. This could help in a faster and reliable detection of risk factors, especially for those people not remembering which medications they take or for those cases in which patients’ responses are influenced by the healthcare professional administering the test and the level of trust. In our study, we found that responses varied in this regard, when there should be no variability. According to our results, we could encounter two different types of questions that could probably give a possible information bias. It is noteworthy that, in general, patients tended to place more confidence in the CP and to respond slightly more agreeably to them than to the PCPs. We could deduce that, given that the CPs were the patients’ usual pharmacists, which make participants more likely to provide positive or open questions in that setting. In contrast, responses to PCPs tended to be more conservative, for example where they asked about their awareness or concern about having skin cancer. However, access to patients’ EHR by CPs would reduce reliance on patient-reported personal and familial health histories.

The intervention of CPs showed that their collaboration can improve the detection of population with risk factors and occult skin cancer. Our model demonstrates high specificity (0.866) and a high negative predictive value (0.853), indicating a strong ability to correctly identify individuals who are not at risk. Although sensitivity is comparatively low (0.231), the positive predictive value (0.25) indicates that the model identifies a subgroup with a higher concentration of true high-risk individuals. However, the low sensitivity indicates that our model tends to minimise false positives at the cost of reduced detection of true positive cases.

In 2016 (5530 individuals)10, in 2017 (4819 individuals)19,20 and in 2018 (2579 individuals)19,20 three different campaigns and studies were performed by COFB to check for skin cancer risk factors among the population. We compared the prevalence of skin cancer risk factors in the three studies, and we could observe that there is an increasing prevalence of risk factors from 2016 to date (see supplementary materials). With this comparison we want to highlight the importance of an early risk factors detection when carrying out skin cancer screening programs.

Discrepancies found in the detection of risk factors between CPs and PCPs, especially those regarding the family history of melanoma, highlight the complexity of the skin cancer screening strategy, and the need to improve the risk factor questionnaire to make it optimal for clinical practice. In future strategies, the limitations of the quality of the information reported by the patient should be considered or aided by the CPs access to the patient’s clinical history. Discrepancies found in the pigmented lesions counting, highlight the difficulties in their detection when chronic solar lesions are present. In fact, most people with an overestimation of pigmented lesions, had chronic sun damages. In those people, PCPs could better distinguish between the two types of lesions, and thus the make a more precise pigmented lesions counting, with respect to the CPs. As outlined in the concordance section, the risk algorithm would benefit from some further refinement, particularly for questions that demonstrated discrepancies in risk classification. Specifically, self-reported family history of melanoma emerged as a potential source of misclassification, as some participants were uncertain, unable to recall, or did not accurately recognise a confirmed diagnosis of melanoma among relatives. In addition, discrepancies were identified in the reporting of pigmented lesion counts: CPs tended to overestimate lesion numbers, particularly in individuals exhibiting signs of chronic sun damage. These findings will be carefully considered and incorporated into future iterations of the algorithm to improve its accuracy and reliability.

In conclusion, a structured use of time and the necessary training could translate into early detection of various types of dermatological cancers, which constitutes an interesting support for these professionals.

This study has some other limitations: it was only open to people with a valid national healthcare service card (TSI) with access to a participating primary care centres, the limited sample size due to the difficulties in the recruitment, the lack of specific funding for participating CPs and PCPs and the lack of teledermatology pathways in some centres.
