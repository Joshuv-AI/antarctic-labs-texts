Aligning private incentives with societal goals in smallholder agriculture
Authors: Gashaw T. Abate, Kaleab Baye, Tanguy Bernard, Jordan Chamberlin, Ruth Hill, Rachid Moussadek, Lamine Senghor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Small-scale farmers in low-income countries are often encouraged to adopt new inputs and practices that generate significant societal benefits, such as improved nutrition, food safety, and environmental sustainability. The limited observability of these attributes, combined with small production scale and misaligned private incentives, leads to adoption levels below the social optimum. Addressing this issue often requires combining instruments identified by different scientific disciplines, spanning across multiple policy sectors, an exercise that is rarely articulated jointly. This paper aims to clarify this, through a simple conceptual framework that explains how farmers decide whether to adopt socially desirable inputs and practices, given the returns and costs they face, their access to financial instruments, and the information and preferences that shape their decisions. It identifies the relevant policy channels, and asks whether the social returns justify the public cost of delivering them. We apply it to three case studies: aflatoxin mitigation, biofortified crop varieties, and conservation agriculture. For each, we identify which parameters bind, which instruments can move them, and how emerging technologies may further contribute. The agenda that follows moves from seeking single-instrument fixes toward identifying the right combination of existing and emerging policy-actionable tools.

Small-scale farmers are frequently trained or encouraged to adopt new inputs and practices, hereafter innovations, that generate private benefits for themselves, such as higher yields or increased resistance to pests and drought, as well as benefits to others. These external benefits may, for instance, relate to improved consumer nutrition, enhanced food safety, better processing quality, or environmental sustainability. In low-income countries, the value of these benefits is rarely reflected in market prices, such that small-scale farmers’ adoption of socially beneficial innovations tends to occur at socially suboptimal levels, even when other barriers to adoption are overcome (1–4).

Why, then, are farmers in low-income countries not rewarded for the external benefits they generate when adopting these innovations? A key constraint lies in the combination of unobservable socially desirable attributes and limited scale. Smallholder production and marketing typically occur at levels that make it difficult to signal or verify the presence of desirable qualities in the marketplace.

This paper outlines these challenges using a simple conceptual framework that connects various strands of research along five key dimensions. They are the expected private and external returns from adopting an innovation that generates socially desirable outcomes, the effective cost of its adoption (often inflated by frictions on credit, insurance, and information markets), the cost of verifying the presence of the desired attribute and the conditions under which verification becomes relevant, the scale of production or size of transaction over which verification is spread, and a reservation premium that captures risk aversion, learning costs and behavioral resistance to switching to unfamiliar inputs or practices. We discuss how integrating insights across these dimensions could help improve our understanding of uptake constraints of innovations by smallholder farmers, and identify underexplored opportunities where targeted research could contribute to alleviate adoption barriers.

We illustrate this approach by examining innovations designed to encourage smallholders to contribute to socially desirable outcomes in the areas of food safety, nutrition, and environmental sustainability. For each, we examine innovations recognized as effective in generating social benefits while being accessible to small-scale farmers in Africa, but whose adoption remains limited compared to the social optimum. Using the proposed framework, we draw on past and ongoing research (and further discuss possibly new research areas) to discuss how small-scale farmers could be better encouraged in adopting these innovations in a way that produces the targeted social benefits.

Our goal in this exercise is fourfold. First, we argue that many aspects of innovation adoption that are traditionally examined as informational problems within value chains, particularly those related to quality signaling for attributes that are costly to verify (5, 6), should also be understood through the lens of public goods provision (7). This reframing highlights the essential role of public or socially oriented financing to address this challenge, whether through the development of technological solutions, investment in public goods or spending on subsidies of some form. Second, our framework indicates that there are multiple ways to increase the incentives farmers face, and that cost-effective strategies typically require combining instruments across several policy channels. Third, we emphasize that the private returns to many current innovations are scale-dependent. It is therefore crucial to explicitly account for the incentives smallholder producers face when assessing the viability and potential uptake of these socially desirable innovations. Finally, we use this framework to ask, for each instrument, whether the societal returns justify the public cost of delivering them through market-based and/or nonmarket interventions.

A Simple Framework

Farmers produce goods with multiple attributes, some of which may not be directly observable to the naked eye. Others in society may wish to compensate producers for supplying these unobservable attributes. They may be consumers or a public agency interested in food safety and nutritious products, local citizens concerned with the environmental impact of production, or global citizens concerned with greenhouse-gas emissions and resource use. From the producer’s perspective, the decision to supply such an attribute depends on the marginal production cost it entails, which must be offset by an equivalent incentive, whether a price premium or a public transfer. In some cases, that cost is effectively zero, because the attribute is bundled with other characteristics that farmers already value. Farmers then supply the attribute regardless of compensation, though they may still receive a premium reflecting the buyer’s utility gain. When the attribute is both unobservable and costly to supply, however, incentivizing its provision becomes significantly more complex.

The central challenge lies in verifying that the farmer has actually supplied the attribute, the classical asymmetric-information market failure of Akerlof (5). When the attribute is strongly correlated with observable features of the product, verification is cheap and the producer can be rewarded through a market premium aligned with the buyer’s valuation. As inspection costs rise, for instance when a laboratory test is needed, or when production practices must be measured and reported, the feasible compensation, namely the buyer’s valuation net of inspection costs, declines, potentially to zero. As a general rule, any positive verification cost leads to an underprovision of the unobservable attribute (8).

This underprovision is particularly acute in smallholder agriculture in low-income countries. Because smallholder transactions are typically small, the largely fixed costs of testing and verification translate into a disproportionately high per-unit cost, lowering feasible compensation, so that the supply of these attributes is limited.* In these contexts, the policy problem of inducing smallholder adoption of innovations that benefit others has two distinct dimensions. First, is the value the attribute creates for others, once we subtract the cost of verifying that the farmer is actually producing it, large enough to warrant any policy intervention? Second, conditional on the answer being yes, how is the corresponding compensation delivered to the farmer, and at what public cost? The framework that follows addresses both. We start with the parameters that enter the adoption condition, and turn to the planner criterion thereafter.

Let I denote the fixed (per-transaction) cost of verifying whether a farmer has adopted an innovation that generates the desired attribute, and let Q denote the quantity of product per transaction. The per-unit cost of verification is then I/Q≥0 and decreases with transaction size. The total per-unit return to the farmer, RS, has two components: RS≡RP+RE. The private return RP accrues to the farmer regardless of verification, since she or he is simultaneously the producer and the beneficiary of the attribute. The external return RE is the per-unit amount the farmer actually receives from others in society (consumers, neighbors, or a public agency) for the benefit she or he creates, conditional on verification. RE is the amount in hand, not the gross social value of the benefit, and is by construction positive only when the attribute is verified and a mechanism for conveying the payment is in place.

RE>0 can arise through two distinct mechanisms, which differ in their welfare properties and in the instruments best suited to deliver compensation to the farmer. In the first, the attribute generating RE is a credence good, one whose quality the buyer cannot observe even after consumption, so that the market fails to reward it not because no consumer values it but because no consumer can verify it. Aflatoxin safety is the clearest example: Evidence suggests that many consumers would pay for uncontaminated grain if they could identify it, but cannot, so the willingness to pay that exists in principle is not transmitted through prices. This is an asymmetric-information market failure of the Akerlof (5) kind, and the natural remedy is verification technology that makes the attribute observable, after which a market premium can do the work. In the second, the attribute generates a benefit that accrues to people outside the transaction entirely (carbon sequestration, watershed regulation), so that no individual buyer has an incentive to pay for it even with full information. This is a genuine externality in the public-goods sense of Samuelson (7), and market-based remedies are insufficient by construction: The compensation must flow through a public transfer or regulation that internalizes the social value. The distinction matters for policy design: Where RE reflects nonobservability, reducing I/Q is the priority lever, since it unlocks a latent market premium that already exists on the demand side; where RE reflects a genuine externality, demand-side instruments cannot substitute for public financing, regardless of how cheap verification becomes.

In the market-premium form, with competitive markets and small intermediation margins, the premium almost equals consumers’ willingness to pay.† In the public-transfer form, by contrast, the amount is set by the public valuation of the external benefit. The two channels coincide when consumers’ willingness to pay reflects the social value of the attribute and markets pass it through without friction. They diverge when part of the benefit is a public good that no single consumer has an incentive to pay for, or when limited pass-through leaves the farmer with significantly less than consumers would pay. Because adoption is forward-looking, both RP and RE enter the farmer’s decision in expectation. We write E[·] for these expectations, which are shaped by information, extension, training, and past experience, and are themselves policy-responsive.

On the cost side, smallholders rarely face only the cost of an input or a practice; credit, insurance, and information frictions can substantially inflate what adoption actually costs them. We let C denote this effective per-unit cost. Formally, C≡c(1+λ), where c is the marginal production cost relative to the farmer’s best alternative use of resources and λ≥0 is a shadow-cost factor summarizing these frictions. The benchmark λ=0 is frictionless; λ>0 arises whenever adoption requires an upfront investment that the farmer can finance only at a premium over the frictionless cost of capital.‡ Hereafter we write C, with λ implicit.

The farmer adopts when the expected per-unit return, net of adoption costs, clears a reservation premium π¯. This premium varies across farmers and depends on risk aversion, time preference, education, prior experience, and attitudes toward change. Formally, π¯ captures the heterogeneity across farmers that λ on the cost side does not absorb. Innovations with steep learning curves, whose benefits are delayed, uncertain, or hard to observe directly, tend to face a higher reservation premium than their discounted value would suggest, as illustrated for instance by studies on adoption of agricultural technologies (10, 11) and demand for preventive health goods (12).

The framework therefore accommodates the standard adoption barriers of risk, uncertainty, and lagged returns. Risk and risk aversion enter through π¯; when insurance markets are missing or weak, the resulting precautionary demand for liquidity additionally raises λ, so the risk premium (π¯) and the shadow price of capital (λ) remain conceptually distinct. Uncertainty about returns enters through E[·], which can diverge from objective returns when farmers lack information or have not observed the innovation. Lagged returns enter through λ on the front-loaded cost when capital markets are weak, and through π¯ when farmers discount delayed benefits more steeply than time preference alone would imply.

The farmer adopts when one of two sufficient conditions holds:[1]ERP−C≥π¯,[2]ERP+RE−C−I/Q≥π¯. Under condition Eq. 1, the expected private return alone covers the effective cost and clears the reservation premium, so adoption requires neither verification nor external reward. Under condition Eq. 2, the farmer must additionally be rewarded for the benefit she or he creates for others in society, net of the per-unit verification cost I/Q. Nonadoption arises only when both fail.

To move an innovation from below the orange line back into the adoption region (as illustrated in Fig. 1C), one or more of the following policy levers can be deployed: i) a reduction in the effective cost C moves both dots to the left, acting on the cost of the input or practice (through agricultural R&D, extension, or producer subsidies), or on the credit, insurance, and information frictions that inflate that cost (through support to financial services); ii) an increase in the expected private return E[RP] raises both dots by the same amount, through agricultural R&D, demonstration plots, farmer field schools, peer learning, and information on traits and health effects; iii) an increase in the expected external return E[RE] raises the orange dot only, widening the vertical gap between the two dots, working through demand-side measures that raise the market price (consumer awareness, verified consumer subsidies, regulation) or through policy-financed conditional payments to producers; and iv) a reduction in I/Q shifts the orange line downward, through lower I (testing R&D, testing subsidies, certification support) or larger Q (cooperatives, block farming, specialization).

Although Fig. 1C does not depict it as a separate arrow, the reservation premium π¯ is also responsive to policy where adoption requires unlearning established routines or contending with unfamiliarity costs: training, repeated exposure, and participatory breeding compress π¯ and shift both adoption frontiers downward. This channel is central in the conservation agriculture and biofortification cases discussed below.

The optimal combination of levers depends on the complementarities between them, and several patterns of interaction recur. Enabling complementarities arise when one lever only delivers if another is functional. Specialization raises Q, but requires an insurance market to be viable. Affordable testing technology lowers I/Q, but only to unlock a premium that must exist on the demand side to have value. Bundled instruments shift more than one parameter at once. Training raises E[RP] and compresses π¯ simultaneously. Cooperative action raises Q and eases financial frictions through shared credit, but members must verify each other’s compliance in supplying the unobservable attribute; coordination failures and free-riding often confine such arrangements to lower-quality products (13–15). Sequencing matters when the order of interventions changes their joint return. Early public investment in E[RP] and in π¯-reducing exposure can move an innovation from a state where verification is needed to one where the private return alone supports adoption, after which markets carry the load with minimal further subsidy.

The optimal policy package exploits one or more of these complementarities rather than optimizing each lever in isolation. But every lever carries a cost. Conditions in Eqs. 1 and 2 only describe when an additional farmer will adopt; they are silent on whether society has reason to support that adoption. When a policy moves a farmer across one of the two conditions, society should support it only when the marginal social benefit exceeds the social cost of the instrument used.§ Social benefits derived from each attributable adopter against the corresponding sum of the programme’s marginal costs. The two coincide when the social benefit per adopter is uniform; where they differ, the policy should target the highest-benefit adopters first and stop where the marginal benefit equals the marginal cost. Formally,[3]VS−Cpolicy≥0, where VS is the marginal social benefit per unit of adoption (health gains from reduced aflatoxin contamination, including improved vaccine response and immune function; averted micronutrient deficiency; sequestered carbon; or other externalities), and Cpolicy aggregates the social cost of the instrument (subsidy outlays plus deadweight loss, the cost of inspection and enforcement infrastructure, and training costs).

Condition Eq. 3 is not an additional constraint the farmer solves; it is the criterion the planner applies when choosing whether, and how, to move the farmer’s two conditions. Different instruments shift the balance between VS and Cpolicy in different ways. Subsidizing inspection to raise RS makes private adoption more likely but adds inspection costs to Cpolicy; reducing C directly through producer subsidies, or delivering the compensation innovation outside a market through public provision, shifts the balance again. The net effect depends on the instrument’s administrative cost and on the strength of the public-good rationale at stake. When the orange dot sits well below the orange line, single-instrument interventions rarely suffice, and packages that combine movements on C, E[RE] and I/Q must be assessed jointly against their combined public cost. Three Illustrative Case Studies

To show how the framework applies across different innovations, we use conditions Eqs. 1 and 2 and the planner criterion Eq. 3 to analyze three innovations promoted among small-scale farmers in low-income countries: aflatoxin mitigation through biological control, biofortified crop varieties, and conservation agriculture. All three generate societal benefits that are typically not rewarded in the markets where smallholder farmers sell their outputs. They differ, however, in the split between private and external components, in the cost of verification, and in the policy instruments best suited to support their adoption.

Food Safety: Promoting Aflatoxin-Free Production.

The issue.

Foodborne disease-related deaths are estimated to be the highest per capita in Africa, causing 137,000 deaths and 91 million illnesses annually (16). Visually undetectable food safety problems are widespread in the region. Reported examples include Salmonella and other pathogens in foods at prevalences up to 44% in Burkina Faso (17), high coliform counts in Kenya’s informal milk chains (18), pesticide residues exceeding safety limits in tomatoes and vegetables in Burkina Faso and Uganda (19, 20), antibiotic residues in animal products (21), and heavy metals and pathogens in wastewater-irrigated vegetables in Accra (22).

Aflatoxin contamination is among the most serious of these threats globally, with documented health, developmental, trade, and economic consequences (23), particularly in sub-Saharan Africa (24). Climate conditions and dependence on susceptible staple crops like maize and groundnuts drive persistently high levels of human and animal exposure across the region (25–28). Acute exposure can be fatal (29, 30); chronic exposure is linked to child stunting, cancer, and weakened immunity (31–35).

Aflatoxin-producing fungi infect crops before, during, and after harvest, and can produce aflatoxins at every stage when conditions are conducive (36, 37). Several practices reduce aflatoxin content at different points in the value chain (37–42), but in isolation they rarely keep contamination below safe thresholds, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions (43). A generally more effective approach is biological control, notably Aflasafe, which targets Aspergillus flavus (the fungus responsible for aflatoxin production) and reduces aflatoxin levels in maize and groundnut across diverse farming systems (44). Yet despite Aflasafe’s growing availability since its introduction in 2014, smallholder uptake in Africa remains limited.

Private and external benefits.

Most of Aflasafe’s benefit flows to consumers along the value chain: RE is large in health terms but unrewarded in domestic markets where contamination cannot be observed, which is an asymmetric-information market failure of the Akerlof (5) kind rather than a textbook externality. A modest RP arises from own-consumption when farmers are aware of the health risk and preferentially allocate treated grain to their household (45). The distinction matters: Strategies that work through RE require verification, while strategies that work through RP do not.

Position in Fig. 1.

In most domestic smallholder markets, Aflasafe sits in Fig. 1B situation: The orange dot lies below the orange line, and adoption requires the kinds of policy levers illustrated in Fig. 1C. E[RP] is low because own-consumption is a minority share of output, awareness of the health risk is uneven, and preventive-health goods typically attract low willingness to pay relative to their long-run benefit (12). E[RE] is essentially zero in most domestic markets for lack of testing-based premia. Where such premia do exist, they sit almost entirely within export-oriented contracts, such as the AgResults aggregation programme in Nigeria (46) and groundnut cooperatives in Senegal (47). I/Q is high because aflatoxin testing is sample-based: The cost is essentially the same whether the consignment is 50 kg or 5 tonnes, so per-unit verification falls only when Q is large, a condition rarely met in domestic smallholder marketing. C is high because Aflasafe, applied at 10 kg per hectare, costs roughly USD 20 per hectare, substantial against a baseline where grain smallholders often spend very little on purchased inputs under retained-seed, low-fertilizer practices. Aflasafe, like other inputs, is also exposed to risks linked to application timing and storage conditions; financial frictions on the front-loaded cost are therefore substantial.

Levers and complementarities.

No single lever unlocks adoption. On I/Q, recent technology offers two consecutive advances tailored to smallholder value chains. The portable competitive ELISA (cELISA) kit released by ICRISAT in 2016 provides ±10 ppb results in under 15 min at under USD 2 per test, without specialized laboratory personnel. An AI-based diagnostic tool developed with PureScan in 2022 delivers ±1 ppb results within seconds at approximately USD 50 per testing unit, with near-zero marginal cost test because it uses no consumables.¶ These devices collapse I/Q toward zero independently of Q, undoing the historical dependence of food-safety verification on cooperative aggregation, an approach whose track record for promoting nonvisible quality attributes has been weak outside strictly enforced export contracts (48).# Where aggregation remains necessary, allowing farmers to coordinate testing at flexible scales (in pairs rather than at full cooperative level, for instance) limits the free-riding that has confined traditional schemes to lower-quality products. Public support for reliable testing and certification infrastructure is the natural complement (49).

On C, public funds can subsidize Aflasafe directly where market rewards are thin. Full subsidies are expensive, but two targeting strategies reduce Cpolicy at equal social benefit: geographic targeting using time- and location-specific data to predict contamination-susceptible areas (50), and nonflat subsidy schedules in which higher levels of subsidies are naturally targeted at production that farmers dedicate to market sales (51). Field experiments in Kenya and Ghana show that combining incentives, subsidies, information, and market rewards raises adoption of aflatoxin-control technology (52, 53). Because Aflasafe is exposed to the risks noted earlier, direct subsidies work best when paired with training delivered through specialized extension services (which raise E[RP] and compress π¯) and with support to financial services (savings, credit, insurance) that ease the underlying frictions. This is an enabling complementarity: The subsidy lowers what the farmer pays for Aflasafe, while insurance compresses the downside risk of a failed season, and each is required for the other to be fully effective. A complementary investment is public R&D on lowering the production cost of Aflasafe itself.

On the demand side, consumer willingness to pay for safer food is positive but limited (54), and responsive to targeted information campaigns (55, 56).‖ Even when demand is established, the premium must be passed through every stage of the value chain, which typically requires coordination across many actors (57). Where domestic markets are too thin for pass-through and VS exceeds the combined cost of inspection and enforcement, the Pigouvian alternative** is regulation of food-safety standards, as in the EU and US. It shifts the cost burden to the public budget, but passes the planner’s criterion Eq. 3 whenever private willingness to pay is inelastic to unobserved quality. Its direct effect is to raise all consumer prices by the added cost of Aflasafe treatment per unit of product, internalizing the health benefit economy-wide.

Nutrition: Encouraging the Adoption of Biofortified Crop Varieties.

The issue.

While global trends in micronutrient deficiencies have generally declined between 1990 and 2019, sub-Saharan Africa continues to bear a significant burden, particularly for iodine, vitamin A, folate, zinc, and iron deficiencies. These are driven by inadequate dietary diversity, poor sanitation, frequent infections, and limited coverage of supplementation programmes. The health consequences are severe, including adverse pregnancy outcomes, congenital malformations, impaired cognitive development, increased perinatal, infant and child mortality, and reduced resistance to infections (58).

Supplementation, food fortification, and biofortified crop varieties can address deficiencies when nutrition requirements cannot be met through dietary intake alone. Biofortification increases the concentration of particular micronutrients in staple crops through agronomic, transgenic, or plant-breeding approaches and has proven feasible and cost-effective (59–61). Yet while more than 284 biofortified varieties of 12 crops have been released in 22 African countries over the past two decades, smallholder adoption remains suboptimal (3, 62–64).

Private and external benefits.

The allocation between RP and RE depends on whether the nutrient trait is visible to the consumer. The distinction separates a credence-good problem (invisible trait, of the same kind as aflatoxin above) from a more standard information-and-preference problem (visible trait). Orange-fleshed sweet potato, orange maize, and yellow cassava signal their micronutrient content through their color: Informed households can internalize the benefit directly, which raises RP, while uninformed or neophobic households perceive an unfamiliar color and may display a negative willingness to pay (65–67). Invisible traits, such as zinc in rice or iron in beans, deliver no RP absent information, and can be rewarded through RE only where the trait is verified at the point of purchase or where targeted public provisioning internalizes the health gain directly.

Position in Fig. 1.

The two subcases map to different positions in Fig. 1. For biofortified crops with visible traits, I/Q is effectively zero, the orange line collapses onto the blue line, and the binding question is whether E[RP] clears it. Two mechanisms can keep it below: A knowledge gap about the nutrient benefit depresses the subjective return below the objective one and enters through E[RP], and neophobia and status-quo attachment to familiar varieties elevate π¯ independently of beliefs, in the same family as the present-bias discount that preventive-health goods typically face (12). For well-informed, neophobia-free households the private return alone can sustain adoption but either channel, a knowledge gap or neophobia, can pull the innovation below the line yielding to Fig. 1A situation. Since I/Q is already at its floor, reducing it is not a lever.

For biofortified crops with invisible traits, the case corresponds to Fig. 1B situation. Laboratory reference technologies, X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectroscopy for minerals and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) for vitamins, are accurate but costly and largely inaccessible in rural areas, keeping I/Q high. A sufficient reduction in I/Q, through portable near-infrared spectroscopy (68–70), smartphone-based colorimetric and paper-based sensors (71), or hyperspectral remote sensing (72), brings the orange dot above the shifted orange line, provided a verified premium survives pass-through along the value chain.

Levers and complementarities.

For biofortified crops with visible traits, the appropriate lever depends on which of the two channels binds. Where a knowledge gap is the binding constraint, nutrition information, cooking demonstrations, household tasting, and school-feeding programmes shift E[RP] toward the measured return (73–75). Ex ante integration of the micronutrient trait into agronomically superior mega-varieties avoids the yield trade-off that would push adoption back below the blue line (76, 77); expected yields, taste, and cooking characteristics must be at least equivalent to conventional varieties to support widespread adoption (55, 78). Where neophobia binds, π¯ is the relevant parameter, and repeated exposure through public provisioning, school feeding, or time-bound consumer subsidies reduces the unfamiliarity premium more effectively than information alone. Sensory and sociocultural attributes also contribute to π¯, from cooking ease and storage characteristics to culturally relevant color and product preferences, and are best addressed through participatory breeding oriented to localized preferences (64).

For biofortified crops with invisible traits, cheap portable testing makes the RE route viable. Once I/Q is small, demand-side pass-through of consumer willingness to pay via certification becomes the natural channel; where pass-through fails, conditional producer payments or time-bound consumer subsidies substitute, and nonmarket approaches can leverage hyperspectral remote sensing to geographically target producer subsidies to areas growing nutrient-dense crops (72). The criterion Eq. 3 favors time-bound instruments over permanent ones here: The welfare case rests on building familiarity with novel products in a fixed target population, after which private markets can sustain adoption. This is the sequencing pattern in action. Early public investment in E[RP] and in π¯-reducing exposure, alongside affordable testing, moves an innovation from a state where verification is needed to one where the private return alone supports adoption; market pass-through then takes over.

Environment: Promoting Adoption of Conservation Agriculture.

The issue.

Agriculture in low-income countries, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, is a significant source of greenhouse-gas (GHG) emissions, stemming from both direct farm activities and land-use changes. Key contributors include methane from livestock enteric fermentation and irrigated rice production, nitrous oxide from inefficient fertilizer use and poor manure management, and emissions from widespread residue burning (79, 80). Indirect emissions result from land conversion, especially deforestation and grassland clearance for cropping, which depletes soil organic carbon (SOC) and above-ground biomass. These impacts are worsened by soil-degrading practices such as intensive tillage, residue removal, and inadequate nutrient cycling, which accelerate carbon loss and limit long-term sequestration potential (81).

In response, the 2015 COP21 summit in Paris intro duced the voluntary “4 per 1000 Initiative: Soils for Food Security and Climate,” promoting regenerative farming practices to enhance soil health and climate resilience. One recommended practice is conservation agriculture (CA), which combines crop diversification (cereal-legume rotations), permanent soil cover (crop residues or cover crops), and minimal soil disturbance (no or minimum tillage). Recent studies indicate that CA can increase SOC, particularly in dry regions like Africa, where yield effects are neutral or positive (82). Yet adoption by small-scale farmers remains low, covering only about 1.25% of cultivated land (83).††

Increasing net soil carbon storage limits CO2 emissions, captures atmospheric CO2, and enhances soil quality. Compensating smallholders for these benefits is challenging, however, because monitoring adoption, tracking changes over time, and verifying the integrated application of all three CA practices are all difficult.

Private and external benefits.

CA differs from the first two cases in that a substantial share of its benefit is genuinely private. Soil-moisture retention, reduced tilling costs, and improved water-use efficiency feed into RP, with the caveat that these benefits materialize with a lag of several years (86). A less widely noted but economically important component of RP is the reduction in exposure to sunk land-preparation costs under rainfall uncertainty: Under conventional tillage, farmers invest labor and draft power before the rains arrive and lose that investment when rains fail, whereas under no-till, seeding follows the first useful rainfall, so the sunk-cost exposure of a failed rainy season is much reduced. These short-run benefits are offset by specific adoption costs: Potential short-term yield reductions, increased weed pressure requiring additional labor or chemical control, loss of crop residues for livestock feed, and limited access to direct-seeding equipment (83, 86–88). The external return RE comes from carbon sequestration, reduced runoff, and watershed regulation, has public-good characteristics that intensify under climate stress (89), and is captured only through Payments for Ecosystem Services (PES) or verified carbon-offset contracts.

In the short run, CA sits in Fig. 1B situation: Both dots lie below their respective lines, and no single-lever intervention unlocks adoption. In the long run, CA may approach Fig. 1A situation as learning accumulates and the private soil-related benefits begin to materialize. On the adoption side, three parameters bind. λ is large because costs are front-loaded in labor and learning while benefits are delayed (the sunk-cost reduction noted above partly offsets this on the rainfall-risk dimension). π¯ is elevated because CA is a radical change in cultivation practice that requires unlearning established routines; status-quo bias and attachment to familiar methods mean that many farmers demand a larger net premium before switching than the standard time-preference assumption alone would imply. E[RP] is depressed because farmers discount uncertain soil benefits they have not themselves observed, a belief problem rather than a preference one. On the verification side, I/Q is high under conventional monitoring but can be reduced sharply by remote sensing combining Sentinel-1 radar and Sentinel-2 optical imagery to distinguish conservation from conventional tillage (90). Any measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) system must be paired with context-specific measurement of the social benefit, since SOC gains depend strongly on climate, soil type, and residue management. Reported outcomes range from gains that peak and decline over a 17-y horizon in Spain (91) to sustained accrual in semiarid Morocco (92, 93), humid Brazil (94) and the Canadian Prairies (95); spatially disaggregated evidence must guide the design of policy interventions such as PES (96).

Levers and complementarities.

The private-return route dominates in the medium run. Training programmes are the clearest example of a bundled instrument in this case: They raise E[RP] by making delayed soil benefits salient and credible, and compress π¯ by lowering the learning and unlearning costs that make a switch to an unfamiliar practice unattractive. Recent evidence from Niger shows that training and public awareness campaigns meaningfully increase take-up of soil-conservation practices (97). Combined with support to financial services (savings, credit, insurance) that compresses λ on the front-loaded cost, and with complementary investments in labor-saving technologies such as mechanized seeders or chemical weed management that bring C itself down, these measures can lift CA over the blue line for a substantial fraction of farmers without any reliance on testing. In areas of increasing rainfall variability, the private incentives to adopt CA rise naturally, because water-retention benefits become more valuable in the short run.

For the RE component, PES are the natural vehicle. Performance-based precedents now exist in programmes such as payments for forest conservation in Uganda (98) and payments to curb crop-residue burning in India (99); voluntary carbon-market financing can partly substitute for public outlays where verification and enforcement are credible. The distinctive feature of remote-sensing MRV is that it collapses I/Q independently of Q, so the adoption calculus for CA does not depend on waiting for farm consolidation, even though medium-scale farms are emerging alongside the smallholder sector in parts of sub-Saharan Africa (100) and Q may therefore be rising naturally for a subset of producers. Cluster and block-farming arrangements, where viable, deliver much of the scale benefit without any change in underlying farm structure, albeit typically with strong state support (101). The planner’s criterion Eq. 3 disciplines the rest: Publicly financed PES is justified when VS, priced at the social cost of carbon or the local value of averted watershed degradation, exceeds the combined cost of satellite MRV and conditional transfers.

Toward an Integrated Research-Policy Agenda

The framework developed above organizes policy around a small set of levers acting on the parameters of the farmer’s adoption condition: the effective cost of adoption, the expected private and external returns, the per-unit cost of verifying quality, and the reservation premium that captures risk, learning costs, and behavioral resistance to unfamiliar inputs or practices. It pairs this adoption condition with a planner criterion, under which society should support an instrument only when the social benefit of the additional adoption it induces exceeds the social cost of the instrument itself. This pairing gives innovations as different as aflatoxin mitigation, biofortified varieties, and conservation agriculture a shared analytical framework, connects strands of research usually pursued in isolation, and makes the trade-offs between instruments comparable across settings. Three implications follow for the research-policy agenda.

First, the public case for intervention requires measuring both the social benefit of adoption and the social cost of delivering it. The benefit side calls for cross-disciplinary research to quantify health gains from reduced aflatoxin contamination, averted micronutrient deficiency from biofortification, and sequestered carbon or averted watershed degradation from conservation agriculture. The cost side has often been left implicit. It includes public outlays on subsidies, the distortionary consequences of those subsidies, the infrastructure for inspection and enforcement, and the training and extension systems that sustain adoption. Universal testing and information campaigns have real administrative and distributional costs that earlier case-study literatures have rarely priced out.

Second, single-instrument interventions rarely unlock adoption where an innovation sits far from the adoption threshold. As many instruments act on several parameters at once, a well-designed package exploits complementarities that no single instrument can deliver. Training raises the expected private return and reduces the premium farmers demand for switching to an unfamiliar practice. Bundled credit and insurance compress the effective cost and the risk premium on a new input simultaneously. Nonlinear subsidies target public spending on the portion of output where the social benefit is greatest. The research frontier is not whether to train, whether to subsidize or whether to test, but which combinations of these instruments dominate in each setting. SI Appendix develops these portfolio predictions formally under a budget constraint.

Third, the opportunity space is not fixed. Early-stage public investment in information, in repeated exposure to novel products, and an affordable verification can shift innovations from situations where only the verification route is viable to situations where the private return alone sustains adoption, after which markets can carry the load with minimal further subsidy. This is the logic of the biofortification sequencing: time-bound consumer subsidies and repeated exposure through school feeding or public provisioning build familiarity with novel products, with the welfare case resting on the transition being self-limiting rather than permanent. Verification technologies are evolving in parallel: AI-based rapid testing for aflatoxin and remote-sensing monitoring for conservation agriculture both lower the per-unit cost of verification without requiring transaction size to rise, reframing the long-standing aggregation agenda as transitional rather than foundational.
