Interesting Case of Lung Fibrosis in a Patient With Vascular Ehlers Danlos Syndrome
Authors: Alicia Su Huey Kwan, Viral Nanda, Taleea Younas, Harshana Bandara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 39‐year‐old man with recurrent spontaneous (including bilateral) pneumothoraces and haemoptysis had unusual thoracic computed tomography (CT) abnormalities. In the absence of typical phenotypic features, these radiological findings prompted genetic testing, which confirmed a pathogenic COL3A1 mutation and established the diagnosis of vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome. Recognizing these radiological patterns prevents misdiagnosis and enables crucial, potentially life‐saving vascular management.

A 39‐year‐old man presented with a long‐standing history of recurrent spontaneous (including bilateral pneumothoraces as per Figure 1 in 2014) pneumothoraces and haemoptysis dating back to 2011. He had previously undergone a right sublobar wedge resection in 2011 for cavitating lung lesions and bilateral video‐assisted thoracoscopic pleurodesis (left in 2015, right in 2020). Histopathology demonstrated perivascular cuffing, haemosiderin‐laden macrophages and fibrosis, without evidence of vasculitis or malignancy. Congo red, Ziehl–Neelsen and Grocott stains were negative. Despite this, he received empirical anti‐tuberculosis therapy for 6 months.

Chest Xray in 2014 showing bilateral pneumothoraces (Lung border is marked with black arrow heads).

Chest CT performed in 2020 following right‐sided pleurodesis showed calcified and ossified dendriform parenchymal bands with areas of cystic degeneration (Figure 2a,b), together with marked mosaic attenuation with regions of decreased attenuation and constricted pulmonary vessels, consistent with air trapping (Figure 2c). Similar pleural and lung parenchymal abnormalities, including pulmonary ossification and fibrous nodules, have been described in vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome [1, 2]. These findings raised suspicion of an underlying connective tissue disorder. Genetic testing confirmed a pathogenic COL3A1 mutation, establishing the diagnosis of vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome.

CT chest in 2020 showing calcified/ossified dendriform parenchymal bands (arrow heads) with associated cystic (arrows) degeneration of the pulmonary parenchyma (2a and 2b). CT chest showing marked mosaic attenuation with areas of decreased attenuation (asterisk) containing constricted pulmonary vessels, likely reflecting physiological air trapping (2c).

The patient is currently under observational approach in the interstitial lung disease department with surveillance lung function tests and the CT scans and he remains stable without progression of the symptoms or any further pneumothorax.
