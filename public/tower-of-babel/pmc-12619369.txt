Modulating effects of microbiota on synbiotic intervention outcomes for microbiota-derived trimethylamine, trimethylamine N-oxide and indoxyl sulfate in healthy young medical students: insights from a 12-week randomized clinical trial
Authors: Mariusz Kaczmarczyk, Karolina Kędzierska-Kapuza, Karolina Skonieczna-Żydecka, Anna Surówka, Sylwester Drożdżal, Kacper Lechowicz, Mariusz Buszman, Urszula Szkudlarek, Danuta Cembrowska-Lech, Konrad Podsiadło, Emilia Samborowska, Igor Łoniewski, Kazimierz Ciechanowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMicrobiota-derived metabolites, trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) and indoxyl sulfate (IS), have been implicated in cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic diseases. Synbiotic interventions are a promising strategy to modulate these metabolites, but their efficacy may vary depending on host-microbial characteristics. This study investigated whether a multi-strain synbiotic could reduce serum concentrations of trimethylamine (TMA), TMAO, and IS in healthy young adults, and whether baseline characteristics of the gut microbiota influence individual responses to the intervention.MethodsIn a 12-week, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial, 38 healthy young medical students received either a synbiotic or placebo. Serum levels of TMA, TMAO, and IS were measured at baseline, 6 weeks, and week 12, two hours after consuming two eggs. Gut microbiota composition and function were assessed using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing and predicted through metagenomic profiling (PICRUSt2). Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was applied to identify groups of co-occurring bacterial taxa (ASVs) and functional orthologous groups - KEGG Orthologs (KOs).ResultsThe synbiotic intervention did not produce significant changes in TMA, TMAO, or IS levels across the entire study population. There were no significant changes in alpha diversity or microbiota composition during the intervention. However, baseline microbiota-related factors influenced individual responses to synbiotic therapy. Two taxonomic WGCNA modules, containing Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae, were associated with greater reductions in IS levels in participants receiving synbiotics. Also, a module containing Lachnospirales and Oscillospirales showed a potential modulatory effect on TMA levels. A KO module enriched in genes involved in bacterial secretion systems, sulfur metabolism, and methanogenesis pathways - including K14083 (mttB) and K14084 (mttC), both implicated in the conversion of TMA to methane - was significantly associated with reductions in TMA.ConclusionsIn this randomized, placebo-controlled trial in healthy young adults, the synbiotic did not produce a significant arm-wide effect on post-challenge serum TMA, TMAO, or indoxyl sulfate over 12 weeks. Exploratory moderation analyses suggest that baseline gut-microbiota features, taxonomic and functional, may modulate individual responses, particularly for IS and TMA, supporting a precision-nutrition framework. The translational significance of this study stems from the observation that primary prevention, which is particularly important in metabolic diseases, should be individualised based on the function of the microbiota.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07345-x.

Background

The gut microbial community exhibits a substantial metabolic capacity, allowing it to transform various nutrients into metabolites [1]. However, this metabolic potential of the intestinal microbial community can also lead to harmful effects on the host. Two microbiota-derived metabolites, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) and indoxyl sulfate (IS) have recently gained much attention, because of its potential adverse effects. Increased TMAO levels have been linked to cardiovascular disease [2], chronic kidney disease [3], type 2 diabetes mellitus [4], hypertension [5], cancer [6] and all-cause mortality [7]. More recently, TMAO has been implicated in sex–specific disease mechanisms. For example, women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) in the absence of hyperandrogenism exhibit higher fasting TMAO concentrations, which are positively associated with insulin resistance [8]. A narrative review by Annunziata et al. [9] positions TMAO as a central contributor to the elevated cardiovascular risk observed in PCOS. Long recognised as one of the uremic toxins [10], IS has been associated with a progression of chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, and disorders of the CNS [10, 11]. However, growing evidence suggests its pathological effects extend well beyond chronic kidney disease. Experimental data indicate that IS induces pro-inflammatory cytokine release via NF–κB activation and promotes senescence in renal tubular epithelium [12]. Human studies further demonstrate that IS levels rise in obesity-related inflammation [13] and contribute to the metabolic dysfunction observed in PCOS. Specifically, IS and indole–3-acetic acid are independent predictors of insulin resistance in women with PCOS [14]. Animal models also show that IS activates NADPH oxidase in adipose tissue, thereby driving chronic low–grade inflammation [15]. Collectively, these findings establish IS as a key effector in metabolic and vascular dysfunction.

Dietary nutrients containing choline or betaine are catabolized by intestinal bacteria to form trimethylamine (TMA), which is then absorbed and oxidized to TMAO by the hepatic flavin monooxygenase, FMO3 [16]. The gut microbiota is obligatory for TMA formation, and this was demonstrated in both animal [16, 17] and human [2] studies. IS, although similarly to TMAO synthesised in the host pathway (by the hepatic sequential action of the cytochrome oxidases CYP2E1 and SULT1A1), it requires metaorganismal pathway involving gut microbiome-dependent fermentation of the essential amino acid tryptophan into indole via tryptophanase [18].

Given the significant role of the nutrient precursors and gut microbiota in the production of TMAO and IS, appropriately designed dietary, probiotic or combined interventions may serve as therapeutic strategies for lowering levels of these compounds and in consequence associated harmful health conditions [10, 16, 19]. Interventional studies involving prebiotics, probiotics or synbiotics targeting lowering TMA and TMAO have been conducted in both animals [20–22] and humans [23–27], with more compelling results in the former. Similarly, the interventions involving prebiotics, probiotics or synbiotics targeting lowering IS have been conducted [24, 28–32].

Since the gut microbiota has been shown to be linked to the production of TMA, TMAO, and IS, the majority of human studies have concentrated on populations suffering from diseases or conditions that can be associated with gut dysbiosis. While TMAO-targeting interventions were carried out in both diseased (e.g. metabolic syndrome [23], CKD [24, 25], myocardial infarction [27]), and healthy subjects [26, 33, 34], the RCTs that targeted IS were nearly exclusively carried out in patients with CKD [28–32, 35–44]. Interestingly, the only study that combined CKD patients with healthy subjects found that the synbiotic was effective in lowering IS in the CKD, but not in the healthy group [41]. Moreover, only a few of these studies incorporated gut microbiota analysis, which was typically limited to taxonomic profiling. Notably, most of these studies focused on IS [30, 32, 35, 39, 40].

Clinically, the seven–strain synbiotic employed in our investigation, comprising Bifidobacterium lactis W51/W52, Lactobacillus acidophilus W22, Lacticaseibacillus paracasei W20, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum W21, Ligilactobacillus salivarius W24, and Lactococcus lactis W19, delivered alongside oligofructose–enriched inulin, has demonstrated efficacy in two randomized controlled trials. In women with PCOS, this formulation promoted weight loss, BMI reduction, and significant decrease in serum testosterone, LH, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), LPS-binding protein, and lipid profile (total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides) [45, 46]. These results confirm the potential of this synbiotic in the treatment of hormonal and metabolic disturbances, and its effect on LPS suggests a reduction in dysbiosis and/or improvement in intestinal barrier function. In light of the evidence presented, we tested the hypothesis that the synbiotic described above can mitigate postprandial TMA, TMAO and IS production. Our research is based on the assumption that supplementation with strains lacking the cutC and tnaA genes, but capable of reducing the abundance or metabolic capacity of TMA- and indole-producing taxa, will lead to a measurable reduction in plasma concentrations of TMA, TMAO, and IS. This strategy represents a precise, evidence-based approach to disrupting two well-characterized microbial pathways that underlie a range of chronic diseases.

Methods

Aim

The aim of the study was to evaluate whether a synbiotic-based intervention capable of altering the gut microbiota could affect the synthesis of TMA, TMAO and IS in healthy individuals, potentially contributing to the primary prevention of related chronic diseases. Next, we assessed the impact of the intervention on the gut microbiota community and examined the relationship between its composition, function and the effectiveness of the intervention. The modulatory effect of baseline gut characteristics has been shown in obese patients, where the pre-intervention levels of Butyricoccus, Akkermansia, and Anaerostipes were shown to predict body mass index reduction following inulin supplementation [47]. In the double-blind randomised controlled trial, we aimed to investigate the effect of the multi-strain synbiotic formulation on serum TMA, TMAO and IS concentrations in a group of young, healthy individuals following a dietary challenge, which served as a source of choline and tryptophan. Additionally, we sought to determine whether microbiota-related characteristics could influence individual responses to this intervention.

Participants

This study was approved by the local bioethics committee at Pomeranian Medical University in Szczecin (Resolution no. KB-0012/59/17; date: 27.03.2017). Written informed consent was obtained from all individuals. A total of 38 health medical students were enrolled between 2017 and 2018, including 18 males (47.4%). Participants were randomly assigned to receive either a synbiotic (SYN, N = 20) or a placebo (PLA, N = 18). Inclusion criteria were: age 20–35 years, self-reported general good health, and willingness to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria included: the presence of chronic illnesses, use of chronic medications, recent antibiotic use, or failing to adhere to the study protocol. Study design and synbiotic treatment

The study was of double blind, randomized, placebo controlled design. Equal randomization was accomplished using a randomly selected pre-prepared table of random permutations. The capsule and its content looked identical in both groups. The smell and taste of the study substances were also identical. Only a nurse not directly involved in the trial was able to break the treatment codes in the event of an emergency.

Dietary challenge

A simple dietary - chicken egg - challenge according to Tang et al. [2] was administered to all participants. At baseline and each follow-up participants were given two large hard-boiled eggs including yolk (containing approximately 250 mg of total choline and 80 mg of tryptophan each) to be eaten within a 10-minute period.

Synbiotic intervention

Participants used two capsules of synbiotic twice daily. The study used a combination of lyophilized strains of Bifidobacterium lactis W51, Bifidobacterium lactis W52, Lactobacillus acidophilus W22, Lacticaseibacillus paracasei W20, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum W21, Ligilactobacillus salivarius W24, Lactococcus lactis W19, and oligofructose enriched inulin P7 (trade name: Sanprobi Super Formula; manufacturer: Sanprobi sp. z o. o. sp. k., Szczecin, Poland; lyophilisate: Winclove Probiotics, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). The total bacteria content in the capsule was 0.2 × 109 CFU (colony forming unit)/AFU (active fluorescence units (method PN-ISO 19344)) - the daily dose was 0.8 × 109 CFU/AFU. The excipients were maize starch, maltodextrins, potassium chloride, magnesium sulfate, amylase, vanilla flavour, manganese sulfate. Placebo capsules contained cellulose powder, potato starch and magnesium stearate. Adherence to the intervention was assessed by capsule counts at each study visit, with compliance expressed as the percentage of capsules consumed relative to those dispensed. Compliance ranged from 11% to 100%, with a median of 88% at the second visit and 86% at the third visit. Because 16 S rRNA sequencing at the genus level was employed, fecal recovery of the specific probiotic strain was not possible.

Sampling

Before starting the intervention, and at 6 and 12 weeks into the trial, blood was collected two hours after the dietary challenge. At the same time points, participants provided stool samples collected using a specialized device. They were instructed to store the stool immediately at -20 °C in their home freezer, and the samples were delivered to the lab within 24 h of collection. Upon arrival, they were stored at -80 °C until analysis. Blood was drawn from the elbow vein using a coagulant tube, then centrifuged to extract serum, which was stored at -80 °C until analysis.

Quantification of TMA, TMAO and IS

Analyses were performed at the Institute of Biochemistry and Biophysics Polish Academy of Science using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry method (LC-MS/MS). The instrumentation consisted of a Waters Acquity Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography coupled with Waters TQ-S triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer. The method was described before [48]. Chromatographic separation was performed using a Waters HILIC column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm x 50 mm). Mobile phase A was Mili-Q water with addition of 1 mL of 25% NH4OH per 1000mL of water, and mobile phase B was 1 mL of formic acid in 1000 mL of acetonitrile. The mass spectrometer operated in multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM) negative electrospray ionization (ESI-) mode for indoxyl sulfate and in MRM) positive electrospray ionization (ESI+) mode for other analytes. Sample preparation was performed as follows: 10 µL of plasma sample was mixed with 100 µL of acetone containing internal standards. After the mixture was vortexed, centrifuged, and 7 µL of aliquot was injected. The assay employed isotope-labelled internal standards added to each sample prior to extraction: D9-trimethylamine N-oxide (internal standard for TMAO), trimethylamine-13C3,15N hydrochloride (internal standard for TMA), and D4-indoxyl sulfate (internal standard for IS). The calibration curve ranges were 0.02–20 µg / mL for TMAO, 0.1–120 µg / mL for TMA, and 0.1–50 µg / mL for indoxyl sulfate. The mean coefficients of determination (R²) for calibration curves generated from six calibrators were consistently ≥ 0.99.

Analytical sensitivity and quality control

The lower limits of detection (LOD) were approximately 0.01 µg / mL for TMAO, 0.05 µg / mL for TMA, and 0.05 µg / mL for IS, while the limits of quantification (LOQ) were defined as the lowest calibrator concentrations meeting the acceptance criterion of < 20% relative standard deviation (RSD). Quality control (QC) procedures included analysis of pooled plasma control samples within each analytical batch. For these QC samples, batch acceptability required the measured concentration to fall within ± 2 standard deviations of the established QC mean.

Dietary habits

A survey method was used to analyse dietary habits of the study participants [49]. The KomPAN method (Questionnaire of Dietary Behaviors and Beliefs) developed by the Committee for Science of Human Nutrition of the Polish Academy of Sciences to assess dietary habits, dietary beliefs and lifestyle in people aged 16–65 years was used. Based on the answers covering the following aspects: Eating behaviour, Frequency of food intake, Dietary beliefs and Lifestyle and personal information a healthy (HEI) and unhealthy dietary indexes (UHEI) were constructed and used in further analyses as study covariates.

Sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total DNA was extracted, and the V1-V2 hypervariable regions of the 16 S rRNA sequence were amplified using PCR. The resulting amplicons were sequenced on an Illumina platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) at the Institute of Clinical Molecular Biology at Kiel University (Germany) using their in-house protocol 2 × 250 bp paired-end sequencing protocol). An initial quality assessment was conducted through the application of FastQC (version 0.12.1) and MultiQC (version 1.23) tools. Following this, the sequences obtained were processed using the LotuS2 pipeline [50]. Quality filtering and dereplication of reads were implemented in sdm for miSeq Illumina platform, except with an increased minimum sequence length of 210 bp instead of 200 bp. In LotuS2, the DADA2 (Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm 2) algorithm [51] was used to cluster sequences into ASVs. Chimera were detected and removed on the basis of both reference-based and de novo chimera checking algorithms, using the RDP reference database (rdp_gold.fa) in UCHIME3 [52]. ASVs were by default aligned against the phiX genome using Minimap2 [53], and ASVs that produce significant matches against the phiX genome were subsequently removed. Additionally, the host contamination was also removed by mapping the reads against the reference human genome (Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.dna.toplevel.fa) (release 2024-02-13). Using the options for LULU (-lulu) [54] sequence clusters were curated and refined. Out of 2,964,258 and 2,963,951 (pair 1 and pair 2, respectively) reads, 1,803,751 and 2,367,529 (pair 1 and pair 2, respectively) reads were classified as “high quality” and “medium quality”, which clustered into 4320 ASVs. However, of these, 739 ASV were removed with LULU, and after filtering 3581 ASVs (2036520 reads) were remaining in the matrix. ASVs were aligned with Lambda v3.0.0 [55] to SILVA 138.1 SSU database [56], to obtain taxonomic assignments for ASVs using the LotuS2 LCA (least common ancestor) algorithm with default parameters. ASVs that were assigned to categories such as Eukaryota, Archaea, mitochondria, and chloroplasts were included in the dataset. Unassigned taxonomic levels were assigned to the last known taxonomic level and sequentially numbered. In order to minimize the inflation of rare species in the community analysis, we removed ASVs that were not present more than two times in at least 10% of the samples. The filtered ASV matrix was used as input to PICRUSt2 [57] with default settings to infer KEGG Ortholog (KO) abundances and MetaCyc pathway profiles. To minimize over-interpretation from distant phylogenetic placement, we applied an NSTI quality filter and excluded ASVs with NSTI >0.15 a priori; among retained ASVs, the cohort-level NSTI was 0.06 ± 0.02 (mean ± SD), supporting close reference genomes and acceptable prediction reliability.

Alpha diversity metrics were derived from amplicon sequence variant (ASV) data, with a count summary based on 3,581 ASVs. Descriptive statistics of the ASV counts included a median of 23,550, mean of 23,408, first quartile of 17,250, third quartile of 29,186, minimum of 7,587, and maximum of 45,194. Prior to the calculation of alpha diversity metrics, ASV data were rarefied to the minimum count of 7,587 using the rtk R package (version 0.2.6.1). Subsequently, the get.mean.diversity function from the same package was employed to calculate metrics such as richness, Shannon index, Simpson index, and evenness. For the taxonomic data, unclassified instances from the class level downward were first replaced with the highest classified taxonomic rank using a custom R script. The data were then either rarefied to a depth of 7,587 or transformed into relative abundances using total sum scaling. Taxa identified solely within the bacterial kingdom, chloroplasts, and mitochondria were excluded from the analysis. The refined data were subsequently aggregated at multiple taxonomic levels: family, order, class, and phylum. Similarly, MetaCyc pathway data derived from PICRUSt2 underwent processing through either rarefaction to a depth of 608,610 or transformation to relative abundances using the same total sum scaling approach.

Baseline beta-diversity was calculated at both the taxonomic (genus-level) and functional (pathway-level) data using two dissimilarity metrics. Firstly, the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was calculated on rarefied data, taxa (excluding those not present in at least 10% of the samples), or pathways using the vegdist function from the vegan R package (version 2.6-8). Secondly, the Aitchison distance, a measure suitable for compositional data, was computed using relative abundance data. For this, Euclidean distances were derived from centered log-ratio (CLR) transformed data, performed using the clr function from the compositions R package (version 2.0–8). For each beta-diversity metric, distances to the centroid for each individual were computed using the betadisper function from the vegan package. This analysis provides a measure of each sample’s variability around the group centroid in multivariate space, offering insights into the within-group dispersion patterns.

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

The Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was used to identify clusters of highly correlated features. This analysis was conducted using the WGCNA R package (version 1.73). The initial data preprocessing included filtering and transformation of ASV and KO functional levels. For the ASV data, a 10% presence threshold across samples at all time points was used, reducing ASVs from 3,581 to 2,120. After selecting baseline samples, we further filtered the data to keep ASVs occurring in at least 20% of samples with counts > 10, reducing the number to 306 ASVs. Similarly, the KO we used a prevalence filtering, in which 5,371 KOs were reduced to 4,837 KOs, maintaining those present with a count > 10 in at least 10% of the baseline samples. The prevalence-filtered compositional data were transformed using the centered log ratio (CLR) transformation provided by the aldex.clr function from the ALDEx2 R package (version 1.36.0), incorporating a Dirichlet distribution with 2,048 Monte Carlo samples. The final data for WGCNA were created by averaging over Monte Carlo instances. Outlier detection and initial clustering were conducted using hierarchical clustering (using the hclust function from the flashClust R package, version 1.01-2), which also assisted in identifying network outliers. For network construction, we utilized the pickSoftThreshold function to determine the optimal soft-thresholding power, which was set to 6 for ASVs and 18 for KOs. This selection was based on the analysis of the scale-free topology fit index (signed R2) and the mean connectivity, as a function of various soft-thresholding powers (Additional file 1, Supplementary Fig. 1). The adjacency matrix for the weighted network was then transformed into a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM), and modules were identified using hierarchical clustering, with the minimum module size set to 10 for ASVs and 30 for KOs. Module eigengenes (MEs), representing the first principal component of each module, were calculated to summarize the profiles within each module. Modules with more than 75% similarity in their MEs were merged to refine the module configuration. The eigengene-based connectivity (kME) was used in the selection of key hub features in each module. This was done by correlating the feature abundance with the module eigengenes for each module. Features with high kME values indicate strong module membership. ASVs with a kME of 0.75 or higher were considered hub features, while for KOs, a higher threshold of 0.9 was used to determine hub status. To evaluate the reliability of the identified WGCNA modules, we performed a bootstrap resampling stability analysis. In 500 iterations (80% of samples each), WGCNA was re-run and for each module we assessed the Jaccard overlap of membership, defined as the ratio of the intersection to the union of features assigned to the module in the original and resampled data. Modules with a median Jaccard index > 0.5 were considered stable. For visualization and further analysis, network data were exported to Cytoscape (version 3.10.3) using the export NetworkToCytoscape function, enabling detailed network exploration and visualization of the feature relationships.

For further analysis and interpretation of the functional implications of the identified modules, we utilized the pathview package (version 1.44.0) in R to map the KOs from the modules to corresponding metabolic pathways in the KEGG database. This step enabled us to visualize and assess the biological pathways associated with the WGCNA modules. Additionally, the list of KO members within these modules was used for enrichment analysis to determine which components of the pathways were overrepresented in the WGCNA clusters. Enrichment analysis was conducted using the enrichKEGG function from the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.12.6).

Statistical analysis

Mixed-effects linear models (using the lme4 R package, version 1.1–35.5) were used to analyze the trajectories of TMA, TMAO, IS. These models included fixed effects for intervention group, time, and their interaction, as well as baseline microbiome-related modifiers (alpha diversity, beta diversity, genera, and features from ASV- and KO-based WGCNA modules). All models were adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. To explore how microbial features associated with a modulatory effect on target microbiota-derived metabolites evolved over time, we applied the same modeling framework. For visualization of linear model predictions, we used predictor effect plots constructed with the effects R library (version 4.2-2). Significance testing for the mixed-effects models was conducted using the likelihood ratio test (LRT) to compare nested models (with and without the interaction terms). To investigate baseline associations between microbial features and metabolite levels, we applied general linear models (lm function, stats package), also adjusting for age, sex, and BMI. Effect sizes were quantified using correlation coefficients. Fisher’s exact test or Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare SYN and PLA groups. To account for the multiple comparisons we applied FDR (False Discovery Rate) adjustment using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Endpoints and statistical hierarchy

The primary endpoint was the intervention group × time interaction for each microbiota-derived metabolite (TMA, TMAO, IS) over 12 weeks following the standardised egg-challenge. Primary inference was based on linear mixed-effects models with participant-level random intercepts and fixed effects for group (SYN vs. PLA), time (baseline, midpoint, endpoint), and their interaction, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and Healthy Eating Index/Unhealthy Eating Index. Model fit was evaluated with likelihood-ratio tests comparing models with vs. without the interaction term. Metabolites were analyzed on a log scale. To control multiplicity across the three primary metabolites, false discovery rate (Benjamini-Hochberg) control was applied. Secondary endpoints comprised sensitivity analyses using time as a continuous variable (weeks) in the mixed-effects framework, and moderation analyses testing whether baseline microbiome features modified treatment effects via three-way interactions (group × time × feature). Moderators were prespecified feature class: diversity metrics (alpha and beta diversity), selected genera, and features from ASV- and KO-based WGCNA modules. For these classes, FDR correction was applied within each feature class.

Response to synbiotic intervention

To investigate the effect of synbiotic intervention on TMA, TMAO, and IS levels over a 12-week period, we utilized linear mixed-effects models with time treated as a categorical variable (to account for potential non-linear relationships between time and metabolite levels, Fig. 2). The results indicate that the synbiotic intervention did not alter the trajectories of TMA (LRT P = 0.818, covariate-adjusted P (Padj) = 0.731, FDR P (Q) = 0.731, Fig. 2A), TMAO (LRT P = 0.078, Padj=0.072, Q = 0.216, Fig. 2B), and IS (LRT P = 0.204, Padj=0.207, Q = 0.311, Fig. 2C) over time.

Modulatory effect of gut microbiota

Although initial analyses showed no significant effect of the synbiotic intervention on the trajectories of TMA, TMAO, and IS levels over time, we examined whether the baseline microbiota composition might modify the intervention response. To this end, firstly, we evaluated various baseline biodiversity metrics (Additional file 1, Supplementary Fig. 2A-C). We found no significant differences in alpha diversity metrics between the PLA and SYN groups at baseline, including richness (P = 0.805,Padj=0.615), Shannon index (P = 0.646, Padj=0.412), Simpson index (P = 0.571, Padj=0.311), and evenness (P = 0.547, Padj=0.338). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in genus-level Bray-Curtis (P = 0.070, Padj=0.064) and Aitchison distances to centroid (P = 0.173, Padj=0.104), or MetaCyc Bray-Curtis (P = 0.211, Padj=0.133) and Aitchison distances to centroid (P = 0.374, Padj=0.478). Regarding the effect of time on alpha diversity across the study, only richness showed a trend towards significance (P = 0.016, Padj=0.016, Q = 0.066), while Shannon (P = 0.404, Padj=0.413, Q = 0.827), evenness (P = 0.680, Padj=0.674, Q = 0.895), and Simpson index (P = 0.874, Padj=0.895, Q = 0.895) demonstrated no significant changes. Baseline-to-midpoint and baseline-to-endpoint within-subject beta diversity (Bray-Curtis) did not differ significantly between both arms (Padj=0.146, Padj=0.055, for baseline-to-midpoint, and baseline-to-endpoint, respectively, Additional file 1, Supplementary Fig. 2D-E).

However, these findings do not exclude the possibility that individuals with distinct levels of diversity might respond differently to the intervention over time. Modulatory effects of a baseline alpha-diversity and beta-diversity (as distances to centroids) were examined and the results are presented in Additional file 1, Supplementary Fig. 3 (alpha-diversity) and Additional file 1, Supplementary Fig. 4 (beta-diversity). Significant interactions between diversity metrics, the intervention, and time were observed only for IS and only in models where time was treated as a factor (thus accounting for the potential non-linear relationships - evenness: LRT P = 0.069, Padj=0.067, Q = 0.536 (Additional file 1, Supplementary Fig. 3A), richness: LRT P = 0.0065, Padj=0.0052, Q = 0.124 (Additional file, Supplementary Fig. 3B).

To evaluate whether baseline beta-diversity distances modified the effect of the intervention on the levels of TMA, TMAO, and indoxyl sulfate, the distances from the centroid were included as interaction terms in linear mixed-effects models using both continuous and factor-based time models. A significant interaction was observed for Aitchison genus-level beta-diversity and log-transformed indoxyl sulfate when time was treated as a factor (Padj=0.020, Q = 0.354, Additional file 1, Supplementary Fig. 4A), suggesting that individuals with differing baseline microbiota composition responded differently to the intervention over time. In contrast, no significant interactions were found after FDR correction for models assessing Bray-Curtis distances and pathway-level beta-diversity for all microbiota-derived metabolites, though some results approached marginal significance, such as Bray-Curtis genus-level for log-indoxyl sulfate (Padj=0.055, Q = 0.354, Additional file 1, Supplementary Fig. 4B). Full results (all microbiota-derived metabolites, time continuous or discrete) are presented in Additional file 2, Supplementary Table 1.

Modulatory effect of specific taxa

While overall community diversity and composition provide insights into the general stability and responsiveness of the microbiome, examining the influence of individual genera may offer a more precise understanding of which microbial groups are driving these effects. To gain a more targeted perspective on the microbiome’s role in shaping metabolite modulation in response to the intervention, we conducted further analysis to determine whether certain genera could act as key modifiers of treatment response. In this analysis, we applied linear models as before to evaluate potential interactions. Several genera emerged as potentially important modifiers (Additional file 2, Supplementary Table 2 [time as continuous variable] and Additional file 2, Supplementary Table 3 [time as factor]). For instance, we found that the Oscillospirales order showed a statistically significant interaction with log-transformed TMA (LRT P = 0.00052, Padj=0.0008, Q = 0.102, Fig. 3A), suggesting a possible role for this taxon in modulating the effect of the probiotic on TMA metabolism. Similarly, the genus NK4A214 group demonstrated a significant interaction with Indoxyl sulfate (LRT P = 0.0014, Padj=0.0011, Q = 0.095, Fig. 3B), which may point to its involvement in the response to the probiotic in the context of sulphur metabolism. While genera such as Erysipelatoclostridium (LRT P = 0.006, Padj = 0.0064, Q = 0.165), Faecalibacterium (LRT P = 0.0069, Padj=0.009, Q = 0.194), UCG-005 (LRT P = 0.0027, Padj=0.0015, Q = 0.095), and Veillonella (LRT P = 0.0035, Padj=0.0028, Q = 0.123) also showed suggestive interactions with Indoxyl sulfate, their adjusted FDR P values were above the threshold for statistical significance (Q > 0.1), indicating that while these genera may play a role, further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these associations.

Modulatory effect of baseline taxonomic and functional clusters

In addition to analyses at the genus taxonomic level, we also investigated more granular taxa (e.g., ASV level) and functional data (KEGG orthologs, KOs) to further refine our understanding of microbiome-mediated effects. However, given the high dimensionality of these datasets, we applied Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) to construct modules based on ASVs and functional profiles, which allowed us to condense the data into biologically meaningful clusters of co-expressed features. For each module, we calculated module eigengenes - representative profiles that capture the overall pattern of ASVs or functions within the module. These module eigengenes were then used as potential modifiers of response to therapy in our subsequent analysis, enabling us to examine more complex, functionally interconnected effects of the microbiome on the host response.

Using WGCNA, we identified a total of 6 ASV modules comprising 306 ASVs and 20 KO modules comprising 4,837 KOs. These modules represent clusters of co-abundant ASVs and functionally related KEGG orthologs, respectively, and provide a condensed, modular perspective on the microbiome’s structure and functional capacity. Dendrograms showing the hierarchical clustering of ASVs and KOs, along with module colours obtained from adaptive branch pruning, are displayed in Additional file 1, Supplementary Fig. 5. Among the ASV modules, the blue module (Jaccard overlap stability index 0.65, 57 ASVs, with 9 hub ASVs all belonging to the phylum Firmicutes, class Clostridia, order Lachnospirales, family Lachnospiraceae, and genera Blautia, Anaerostipes, [Eubacterium] hallii group, Agathobacter, and Roseburia) emerged as a significant potential modifier of the response to therapy with respect to indoxyl sulfate. This module showed strong associations in both models, where time was treated as a continuous variable (LRT P = 0.014, Padj=0.004, Q = 0.025) and as a factor (LRT P = 0.001, Padj=0.0002, Q = 0.0009, Fig. 3C). Similarly, the turquoise module (Jaccard overlap stability index 0.38, 117 ASVs, with 3 hub ASVs belonging to the phylum Firmicutes, class Clostridia, order Oscillospirales, family Ruminococcaceae, genus Faecalibacterium) exhibited a significant association when time was treated as a factor (LRT P = 0.004, Padj=0.008, Q = 0.024 Fig. 3D). For TMA, the green module (Jaccard overlap stability index 0.52, 32 ASVs, with 8 hub ASVs) included members belonging to the phylum Firmicutes, class Clostridia, and orders Lachnospirales (family Lachnospiraceae) and Oscillospirales (family Ruminococcaceae). This module was initially associated with time when treated as a factor (LRT P = 0.047), but the association did not remain significant after adjustment (Padj=0.156). All results are summarized in the Additionalfile 2, Supplementary Table 4 (time as a continuous variable) and Additional file 2, Supplementary Table 5 (time as a categorical variable).

Further analysis of the KO modules identified several functionally relevant clusters that appear to influence the response to the synbiotic intervention, particularly in relation to the modulation of TMA and indoxyl sulfate. For TMA modulation, three key modules emerged: purple (Jaccard overlap stability index 0.83, 78 KOs, 41 hub KOs), dark red (Jaccard overlap stability index 0.83, 33 KOs, 13 hub KOs), and light cyan (Jaccard overlap stability index 0.78, 44 KOs, 15 hub KOs). These modules demonstrated consistent associations in both modelling approaches, whether time was treated as a continuous variable or as a categorical factor. However, only the purple module remained robust after correction for multiple testing. The purple module (Fig. 4A) exhibited consistent associations with TMA levels (time as a continuous variable: LRT P = 0.003, Padj = 0.005, Q = 0.097; time as a categorical variable: LRT P = 0.003, Padj = 0.005, Q = 0.096). This suggests a potentially significant role for the KOs in this module in modulating TMA metabolism. To help prioritize relevant KOs among the 78 present in the purple module we performed enrichment analysis (Fig. 4B). The results indicated that the purple module was enriched with KOs belonging to the bacterial secretion system (type III, type IV, type VI, K03198/K03223/K04056/K04059/K11912, P = 5.88e-06, Q = 0.0003), sulphur metabolism (K11180/K11181/K15553/K15555, P = 7.74e-04, Q = 0.013), cysteine and methionine metabolism (K16843/K16844/K16845/K16846, P = 1.02e-03, Q = 0.013) and methane metabolism (K05299/K14083/K14084/K16176, P = 4.83e-03, Q 0.032). Discussion

We carried out a 12-week prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled trial in order to examine the effects of multi-strain synbiotic on the serum levels of trimethylamine (TMA), its hepatic oxidation product trimethylamine‑N‑oxide (TMAO), and the tryptophan‑derived indoxyl sulfate (IS) in young, presumably healthy individuals who had undergone a dietary challenge with two hard-boiled eggs as a source of choline and tryptophan. The choice of sampling at exactly 2 h post-consumption was intentional and biologically justified. This timing intercepts the rapid, microbiota-driven surge in plasma TMA, occurring prior to hepatic conversion into TMAO, a process that typically peaks 6–12 h later, and precedes the slower increase in IS [58, 59]. Thus, our 2-hour sampling provides a biologically meaningful functional baseline reflecting early microbial metabolism, allowing robust within-person comparisons of metabolic capacity across repeated visits. The modulatory impact of microbiota-related features (both taxonomic and functional) on the response was also investigated. While no significant overall effect of synbiotic supplementation on group-level metabolite concentrations was observed, several baseline gut microbiome features demonstrated the potential to modulate individual responses, aligning well with a precision-nutrition paradigm. These findings may have important practical implications for the personalized management of probiotic or synbiotic interventions. To our knowledge, aside from research on animals [20–22], only one RCT has combined a dietary challenge (phosphatidylcholine challenge test) with a probiotic intervention in humans [26]. Studies on animals that used dietary challenges involving choline showed that probiotics had a beneficial impact on reducing TMA or TMAO. For example, Xu et al. [22] discovered that supplementing mice with Akkermansia muciniphila resulted in a significant decrease in TMA and TMAO levels in serum, cecum, and urine. Contrary to these animal studies, we did not observe an overall attenuation effect of the multi-strain synbiotic on serum TMA, TMAO, or IS levels following a dietary challenge. The healthy and unhealthy diet index did not differ significantly between the two groups, indicating that their overall diet quality was similar. Our results align with previous studies demonstrating limited arm-wide probiotic or synbiotic effects on TMA and TMAO in healthy individuals [23–26, 28–31, 33, 34, 38–40, 44], reinforcing the notion that baseline metabolic capacity and health status strongly influence intervention outcomes. Importantly, this neutrality does not imply therapeutic futility. Rather, it most likely reflects a simply no visible effect in already healthy participants whose baseline metabolite values lie in the lower tail of the population distribution. By contrast, two recent single‑strain probiotic RCTs in patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease reported 9–21% reductions in TMAO [27, 62], illustrating the context‑dependence of efficacy.

Three TMAO-targeting RCTs have been conducted in healthy individuals [26, 33, 34]. Notably, among these three studies, Chen et al. [26] analyzed gut microbiota by sequencing the V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene, while our trial used V1-V2. However, no modulatory effects of alpha-diversity, beta-diversity, or taxonomic profiles on probiotic supplementation were investigated. Interestingly, the only two studies that demonstrated an arm-wide effect of probiotic (single-strain) supplementation on TMAO (TMA was not measured) were carried out in patients with cardiovascular disease, a condition for which TMA and TMAO have long been recognised as risk factors [2]. Specifically, Moludi et al. [27] demonstrated a beneficial effect of Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG in a group of patients with myocardial infarction who underwent coronary angioplasty, whereas Spasova et al. [60] has recently showed a positive effect of Lactobacillus plantarum GLP3 on TMAO levels in patients with a history of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

Similarly, we found no overall (an arm-wide) effect of the synbiotic intervention on IS serum level in healthy young individuals. It should be emphasized that, to our knowledge, almost all IS targeting RCTs investigating the gut microbiota-based interventions were carried out in the chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients, where elevated IS is a well-characterized uremic toxin and dietary management frequently involves protein restriction. Interestingly, only Cosola et al. [41] reported IS reduction only in CKD patients but not in healthy controls (age between 35 and 60, BMI between 18.5 and 29.9), reinforcing the context-specific nature of microbial modulation. The results of RCTs are equivocal. Some studies reported a reduction in IS following gut microbiota-targeted interventions [32, 35–37, 41–43], while others did not [24, 28–31, 38–40, 44]. Interestingly, not all studies reported an arm-wide effect. No dietary challenge involving tryptophan has been used in any of the RCTs examining the effectiveness of microbiota-targeted intervention in reducing IS levels. Our use of a tryptophan-rich dietary challenge was novel in this context and aimed at uncovering subtle but biologically meaningful microbiota-host interactions under controlled perturbation conditions.

Notably, despite the well-established link between the gut microbiome and IS production, only a few RCTs have analysed gut microbiota [30, 35, 39, 40]. In these cases, the focus was typically on analysing changes in diversity metrics and taxonomic profiles as a potential mechanism of the intervention [35, 40], with the emphasis on the indole producing bacteria [39] or end-point differences between the interventions [30]. Only McFarlane et al. [30] examined the predicted functional potential and its changes during the intervention. To our knowledge, so far gut microbiota has not been considered as a modulator in synbiotic (probiotic, prebiotic) based RCTs targeting TMA, TMAO or IS. Biruete et al. [40] identified distinct taxonomic profiles in the baseline microbiota that were associated with the type of phosphate binders used in CKD patients. However, these baseline differences in gut microbiota were not accounted for in the analysis of the intervention response, nor was their potential influence on the lack of an overall (arm-wide) IS-reducing effect considered [40].

The outcome of probiotic/synbiotic intervention may depend on a special combination of microbiome-related features (e.g. taxonomy, functional potential) and host-related characteristics (e.g. age, health status, genetic background, gene expression), as well as probiotic-related features (for instance dose, frequency, species and genetic diversity [61–63]. In line with this hypothesis, Rodriguez et al. [47] found that the response to prebiotic inulin in mice is determined by the baseline (pre-intervention) gut microbiota characteristics. Recently, Wastyk et al. [64] demonstrated that, even though a probiotic intervention did not have arm-wide effects in metabolic syndrome parameters, it was possible to divide probiotic arm into responders and non-responders. Although, responders and non-reponders did not differ with respect to baseline microbiota, within donor Bray-Curtis distances from baseline to endpoint differ significantly between responders and non-responders.

Andrikopoulos et al. [65] found a significant but weak association between microbiota composition and serum TMAO levels in large cohorts of obese individuals with or without type 2 diabetes (T2D). Over sixty species, the majority of which belonged to the Firmicutes phylum, were associated with TMAO levels in individuals without T2D [65]. Recently, Lin et al. [18] identified low and high IS-producing phenotypes in healthy individuals challenged with an oral dose of tryptophan. These two phenotypes were significantly associated with differences in gut microbiome composition, features and functions [18]. Although there were no differences in alpha-diversity and beta-diversity between the two phenotypes, at the species level, the relative abundance of Bacteroides salyersiae, Prevotella sp. CAG:5226 and Selenomonadales bacterium were markedly increased in the high-IS producers, whereas Prevotella copri, Prevotella sp., and Phocaeicola coprophilus were dominant in the low-IS producers [18]. These findings suggest that gut microbiota taxonomic signatures may serve as potential modulators of individual responses to interventions aimed at lowering TMAO or IS levels.

The Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) method [66] was employed for both the ASV level and functional (KEGG Orthologs, KOs) level. By creating groupings (modules) of strongly associated features, WGCNA helps reduce the high dimensionality of microbiome data [67]. One module (green) comprising 32 ASVs belonging to the phylum Firmicutes (class Clostridia, and orders Lachnospirales - family Lachnospiraceae and Oscillospirales - family Ruminococcaceae) displayed a modulatory potential at lowering TMA (a precursor of TMAO), however the association was not statistically robust. Two other ASV based WGCNA modules modulated the response of indoxyl sulfate. Both modules included hub ASVs from the Firmicutes phylum and their members were distributed among order Lachnospirales, family Lachnospiraceae, and genera Blautia, Anaerostipes, [Eubacterium] hallii group, Agathobacter, and Roseburia) and order Oscillospirales, family Ruminococcaceae, genus Faecalibacterium. This suggests that the baseline abundance of Oscillospirales (order), and families Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae could have an impact on how indoxyl sulfate level changes in response to synbiotic intervention. There is partial overlap between the taxa identified through univariate analyses and those represented in the WGCNA modules. Specifically, members of the ASV-based blue module, which consists entirely of ASVs from the order Lachnospirales, were found to be associated with IS response (P = 0.041, Padj=0.048, Q = 0.326, see Additional file 2, Supplementary Table 3). Faecalibacterium is another example of a taxon that appears in both analyses - it is a member of the turquoise module (Fig. 3D) and also emerged in the univariate analysis (P = 0.007, Padj=0.009, Q = 0.194, see Additional file 2, Supplementary Table 3). We would like to clarify that in our WGCNA analysis, we focused primarily on hub ASVs, which represent the most central and interconnected features within each module. It is possible that additional overlap between analyses exists among the non-hub ASVs, but WGCNA is specifically designed to reduce data dimensionality and highlight the most structurally and functionally relevant features within microbial networks.

However, whether this is just a true casual modulatory relationship or merely a statistical association remains unclear and warrants further investigations. Only about 1% of bacteria present in gut is able to convert choline to TMA, and some taxonomic signatures (e.g. members of the phylum Firmicutes and Proteobacteria) have been associated with increased TMA and TMAO levels [68]. However, based on findings from large cohorts of unhealthy people, gut microbiota does not play a major role in determining circulating TMAO levels, accounting for only 2% of the explained variance [65]. Moreover, certain observations suggest that gut microbiota and its function is a weak predictor of diet-induced TMAO also in healthy subjects [69]. In this context, an important mechanistic question arises: does the presence of an association between baseline metabolite levels and specific microbial taxa imply that these taxa have the capacity to modulate responses to dietary or probiotic interventions. To address this, we conducted baseline association analyses at the genus level, adjusting for age, sex, and BMI, and also leveraged ASV-based and KO-based WGCNA approaches. Several genera showed nominal associations with the microbiota-derived metabolites; however, none remained statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Notably, Oscillospirales (order), identified in this study as a taxonomic modulator of the intervention response, was positively associated with baseline TMA levels. Similarly, while ASV- and KO-based WGCNA modules did not demonstrate significant correlations with baseline metabolite levels, the KO-based green module showed a nominally significant negative association with indoxyl sulfate. Taken together, these results suggest that baseline associations with microbiota-derived metabolites, although potentially informative, did not provide insights into the modulatory features of the response. This view is supported by Diener et al. [70] who observed that while some bacterial genera were associated with baseline BMI, none were independently associated with weight loss outcomes. Similarly, at the functional level, only a very small fraction of the 2,975 gut bacterial gene clusters were associated with both baseline BMI and weight loss (0.1%). Along these lines, the availability of repeated gut microbiome measurements offered a unique opportunity to explore how microbial features associated with the modulation of TMA, TMAO, and IS changed over time. This longitudinal perspective allowed us to investigate potential microbial markers of interaction effects and provided insights that may inform future mechanistic studies. Trajectories of a few taxa were different in the synbiotic and placebo groups (e.g. Blautia, Agathobacter), suggesting that the intervention may have influenced their abundance dynamics in a manner relevant to host metabolite responses. These findings suggest that the synbiotic intervention may selectively influence the abundance of certain gut bacteria previously implicated in TMA or IS modulation, although these effects appear to be modest and taxon-specific.

One of the key novel findings of our study stems from the predicted functional potential inferred from the taxonomic profile, specifically through KEGG Orthologs. To perform this inference, we selected PICRUSt2 as it remains the most comprehensively validated 16 S-based functional inference tool to date. In benchmarking against shotgun metagenomes across seven human and environmental datasets, PICRUSt2 achieved a median Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.79 for KEGG ortholog profiles, double the accuracy of the PICRUSt and superior to Tax4Fun2 [71]. A subsequent update to its reference genome database (PICRUSt2–SC/MPGA) expanded archaeal genome coverage from 406 to over 1,002 genomes [72], thereby improving KEGG ortholog detection for methanogenic clades encoding mttB and mttC. These enhancements are reflected in our dataset, where the mean NSTI (Nearest Sequenced Taxon Index) was 0.06 ± 0.02, well below the 0.15 threshold beyond which prediction reliability significantly declines. Furthermore, all ASVs with NSTI >0.15 were excluded a priori, minimizing the risk of over–interpretation. With respect to Archaea, our functional analysis focused on a single, genomically well–characterized order, Methanomassiliicoccales, whose members encode a highly conserved methylamine methyltransferase system (mttB/mttC) and represent the only known gut microorganisms that consume TMA via methanogenesis rather than producing it [73, 74]. Since these genes are effectively lineage-specific, accurate taxonomic assignment based on 16 S sequences provides a strong proxy for their presence.

At the functional (KO) level, three key modules emerged as modulators of TMA response to the intervention, but only the purple module was robust to multiple testing correction. This module was significantly enriched in genes involved in methane metabolism, a pathway directly linked to the microbial conversion of TMA to methane. In addition, it was enriched for genes associated with the bacterial secretion system (types III, IV, and VI) and various metabolic pathways, as well as other metabolic functions, including sulphur metabolism (energy metabolism), as well as amino acid metabolism, particularly pathways related to cysteine, methionine, and lysine metabolism. Three KOs (K14083, K14084, and K16176) from the KEGG methane metabolism pathway belong to the methanogenesis module (M00563), which is involved in the conversion of methylamine, dimethylamine, and trimethylamine (TMA) into methane [75]. Since two of these KOs - mttB (trimethylamine corrinoid protein co-methyltransferase, K14083) and mttC (trimethylamine corrinoid protein, K14084) convert methylamine to methane [76], it raises the hypothesis that the balance between microbial TMA biosynthesis (from dietary precursors) and its degradation (e.g., conversion to methane) may influence the efficacy of synbiotic intervention. As a result, endogenous methanogenic capacity may likely modulate the competition between TMA-producing bacteria and TMA-consuming archaea. When evaluating Endpoint–Baseline differences in trimethylamine (TMA), individuals with low baseline levels of K14083 (up to − 2 on the CLR-transformed scale; 58.6% of the cohort) exhibited a protective effect from the synbiotic intervention: none of the individuals in the SYN group (20.7%) showed a significant increase in TMA following the choline-rich challenge. Although an upward trend in TMA was observed in the SYN group at higher baseline K14083 values, the magnitude of this increase remained lower than that observed in the PLA group. This suggests that all individuals receiving the synbiotic could be considered “benefiters” - either fully protected from TMA elevation or exhibiting a reduced increase compared to the placebo group. These findings underscore the value of baseline microbiome (functional) stratification: it can reveal individualized benefits that are masked in group-level averages, thereby transforming what may appear as a null overall effect into a clinically meaningful, stratified response. Together with animal work showing baseline microbiota control of prebiotic efficacy [47], and recent human data demonstrating responder / non‑responder clustering after probiotic intake [64], these findings reinforce the concept that personalised microbiome context is a prerequisite for successful synbiotic intervention. Furthermore, the clinical relevance of microbiota-derived metabolites should not be overlooked, even in ostensibly healthy (self-reported) populations. Reducing levels of microbiota-derived metabolites such as TMA may serve as a form of primary prophylaxis, particularly given the established link between TMA and its pro-atherogenic derivative, TMAO.

One limitation of this functional-based approach is that cutC and cutD, the two critical enzymes enabling bacterial conversion of choline into TMA [77], are not included in the PICRUSt2 reference database. As a result, we were unable to assess the abundance or activity of these TMA-synthesis genes in our dataset, and thus could not directly explore the potential interplay between TMA-producing and TMA-degrading microbial functions. It should also be mentioned that the small intestine absorbs all free choline in a typical diet, and the intestinal microbiota in the colon only converts it to TMA when there is an excess of choline [77]. Zeisel et al. [78] discovered that, under normal dietary conditions, the choline urinary excretion of methylamines, including TMA, was 1 mmol/day. After ingesting 27 mmol of choline chloride, this rose to 17 mmol/day. In our study we did not measure TMA urinary excretion, and it is unknown if the 250 mg (2.5 mmol) of choline found in two large eggs [79] causes the gut microbiota to produce TMA at a significantly higher rate. In the MetaCardis cohort a diet was shown to account for 5% of TMAO variance, and the best predictor of serum TMAO was serum metabolomics (excluding TMAO, and its precursor TMA). Microbiome functions displayed a weak predictive potential. It is unclear, nevertheless, if the same predictive patterns apply to populations free of metabolic disorders because there are currently insufficient studies with comparable sample sizes in healthy individuals.

Together with methanogenesis, the module also contained KOs from the bacterial secretion system (types III - needle proteins - YscO, YscX and ATPase-associated protein - YscL, IV - surface/pill protein, VirB3, and VI - regulatory protein PpkA), several metabolic pathways, sulphur metabolism (SsuACB, and 1.8.1.22 - trithionate sulphite, sulphide), and amino acid metabolism - specifically, the metabolism of cysteine/methionine (1.1.1.310, 1.1.1.338, 4.4.1.24 - L-cysteate - puryvate), and lysine (PylB, PylC and PylD - L-Lysine - Pyrrolysine) (Kanehisa and Sato, 2020). It is unclear whether the KOs found in the purple WGCNA module and mapped to the sulphur metabolism, cysteine/methionine metabolism, and methane metabolism interface are functionally connected to TMA metabolism in a way that could affect the efficacy of synbiotic therapy. Similarly, even though the bacterial secretion system has been implicated in processes that may impact the efficacy of probiotic therapy, such as adhesion and colonization processes [80], further investigation is needed.

Several methodological features of this study inevitably limit the scope of its conclusions. First, to standardize substrate availability for gut-derived toxin formation we adopted the acute two-egg choline challenge described by [2]. While this protocol is practical and pertinent given that most adults consume less choline than recommended [68], we collected only a single post-prandial blood sample two hours after ingestion and did not take additional serial or fasting samples on the same day. Consequently, we captured an early snapshot of the choline-TMA-TMAO axis rather than a full kinetic profile, and we could not verify the magnitude of the choline rise or identify participants who failed to mount any post-prandial response (non-responders). This design choice may have obscured treatment effects in individuals whose baseline microbiota inherently produces little or no TMA from choline. Second, the challenge contained approximately 160 mg of tryptophan, an order of magnitude below the 2 g recently used to characterize inter-individual variability in indoxyl sulfate production [18]. In conjunction with the low baseline IS concentrations typical of healthy adults (e.g. 0.7 mg L⁻¹ versus 20–30 mg L⁻¹ in chronic kidney disease), the modest tryptophan load may have limited our ability to detect decreases in IS attributable to the synbiotic. Third, the study was powered and interpreted at the cohort level even though emerging evidence, corroborated by our own mixed-effects modelling, indicates that microbiome-defined responder and non-responder phenotypes strongly influence toxin trajectories. Stratification by baseline microbial modules was therefore exploratory and requires confirmation in larger trials that prospectively incorporate this precision-nutrition framework. These associations should be interpreted with caution, as causality cannot be inferred and reverse causation (metabolites influencing microbiota composition) remains a plausible alternative explanation. Fourth, functional characterization of the gut microbiota relied on 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing with prediction of Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) orthologues in PICRUSt2. Although PICRUSt2 performs well in benchmarking studies and its latest release expands archaeal reference genome coverage, functional inference from 16 S data remains indirect, less sensitive to subtle health-related changes and limited to lineage-associated genes. In practical terms, this approach cannot directly identify or quantify key enzymes such as cutC/cutD (bacterial choline TMA-lyase system) or tnaA (tryptophanase for indole/IS), nor can it resolve species- or strain-level variability within Lachnospiraceae or Oscillospiraceae that may differentially encode these pathways, or detect extra-chromosomal or strain-specific gene flux. Similarly, our conclusions regarding methanogenic archaeal modules (mttB/mttC) and other metabolic signatures should be viewed as hypothesis-generating. To minimize over-interpretation, we applied an NSTI quality filter (exclude ASVs with NSTI >0.15) and report a cohort-level NSTI of 0.06 ± 0.02 for retained ASVs, supporting acceptable prediction reliability. Nevertheless, the results remain inference-based. Definitive validation will require shotgun metagenomics and/or metatranscriptomics to directly quantify cutC/cutD, tnaA, mttB/mttC (and related pathways). Fifth, we did not measure host determinants that modulate TMA oxidation, such as FMO-3 genotype, sex-hormone status or liver function, and thus cannot exclude differential hepatic conversion of TMA to TMAO as an additional source of variability. Finally, the absence of cardiometabolic abnormalities in the cohort precluded evaluation of downstream clinical markers. Nonetheless, strict exclusion of chronic illness, any continuous pharmacotherapy, and any antibiotic exposure before enrolment minimised confounding by recent microbial perturbation.

Conclusions

Our study conducted in healthy young medical students did not reveal the significant overall (arm-wide) effect of the synbiotic intervention on serum TMA, TMAO and IS level. However, we demonstrated that synbiotic intervention effectiveness may depend on the specific pre-intervention microbiota-related characteristics, especially functional such as KO modules enriched for methylamine methyltransferase genes (e.g., mttB/K14083), whichmight be an important factor determining the extent of beneficial response with regard to reduction of these toxic microbiota-derived metabolites. While the average metabolic impact in healthy individuals may be modest, our findings highlight the importance of microbiome-based stratification to identify responders and guide personalized synbiotic interventions. Notably, by attenuating the levels of dietary-derived toxins, targeted synbiotic strategies may contribute to primary prevention in populations at risk for metabolic and cardiovascular disease - even before clinical symptoms emerge. The translational significance of this study stems from the observation that primary prevention, which is particularly important in metabolic diseases, should be individualised based on the function of the microbiota. The microbiome can be regarded as a gene reservoir, and the bacterial and metabolic pathways associated with it may provide a basis for searching for probiotics and postbiotics, as well as for appropriate individualised dietary interventions and lifestyle changes.
