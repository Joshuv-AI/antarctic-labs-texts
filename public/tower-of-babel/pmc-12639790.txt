The impact of miR-221-3p modification by the m6A demethylase ALKBH5 on the inflammatory response in spinal tuberculosis and its diagnostic significance
Authors: Mengqi Zhu, Jiong Wang, XingYu Duan, Lijun Feng, Linan Wang, NingKui Niu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe interplay between N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification and microRNAs (miRNAs) in the pathogenesis of spinal tuberculosis (STB) remains incompletely understood. This study aimed to investigate the regulatory mechanism involving the m6A demethylase ALKBH5 and specific miRNAs in STB.MethodsmiRNA sequencing was conducted to identify differentially expressed miRNAs in STB tissues. The expression levels of miR-221-3p and ALKBH5 were validated via qRT-PCR in tissue samples and peripheral blood from 50 STB patients and healthy controls. The diagnostic potential of these biomarkers was assessed using ROC curve analysis. The expression of ALKBH5 and miR-221-3p was assessed by qRT-PCR, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and immunofluorescence. ALKBH5-mediated m6A modification of pri-miR-221-3p was analyzed by methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) Functional effects on inflammatory responses were evaluated by measuring TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels following functional experiments, combined with ELISA assays.ResultsmiR-221-3p was significantly downregulated in STB tissues, peripheral blood, and BCG-infected macrophages, indicating its potential as a diagnostic marker. In contrast, ALKBH5 expression was upregulated. Functionally, knockdown of miR-221-3p increased the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and exacerbated inflammation, whereas its overexpression had the opposite effect. Mechanistically, ALKBH5 was found to directly demethylate pri-miR-221-3p, as confirmed by MeRIP, thereby impairing its maturation. Silencing ALKBH5 restored miR-221-3p expression and reduced cytokine secretion.ConclusionmiR-221-3p may serve as a potential diagnostic biomarker for STB. ALKBH5 upregulation promotes STB progression by inhibiting miR-221-3p maturation via m6A demethylation, which enhances the inflammatory response. Targeting the ALKBH5/miR-221-3p axis may offer a novel therapeutic approach for STB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06473-w.Keywords: Spinal tuberculosis, miR-221-3p, ALKBH5, m6A methylation

Background

Spinal tuberculosis (STB) accounts for approximately 50% of all cases of bone and joint tuberculosis, making it one of the most prevalent forms of spinal infectious diseases [1]. The disease is characterized by an insidious onset and nonspecific early clinical manifestations [2]. Due to these features, STB often presents challenges in early diagnosis and timely intervention, which may result in severe complications such as spinal deformity and neurological impairment [3]. Therefore, identifying novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets and elucidating the underlying pathogenic mechanisms are of critical importance for improving early detection rates and optimizing clinical management strategies for STB.

In recent years, microRNAs (miRNAs), a class of non-coding RNAs, have emerged as a central focus in molecular biology research [4–6]. Accumulating evidence indicates that miRNAs play important roles in the pathogenesis, immune evasion, and individual susceptibility to tuberculosis [7, 8]. miRNAs constitute a highly conserved family of non-coding RNAs, typically comprising 18 to 25 nucleotides in length [9, 10]. Despite their short sequences, miRNAs exert critical regulatory functions in diverse biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and metabolic regulation [11]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that dysregulation mediated by non-coding RNAs is prevalent across a wide range of major diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disorders, autoimmune conditions, and infectious diseases [12–17]. Notably, miR-221-3p, a key member of the miRNA family, has been shown to be significantly upregulated in inflammatory conditions such as skeletal muscle inflammation and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [18, 19]. These findings suggest that alterations in miR-221-3p expression are closely associated with the initiation and progression of inflammatory responses, indicating its pivotal regulatory role in inflammatory disease pathology. Nevertheless, the functional contribution of miR-221-3p to spinal tuberculosis, a distinct form of the disease, remains largely unexplored.

In the field of RNA epigenetics, the RNA demethylase ALKBH5 has emerged as a key molecule of significant interest in life sciences in recent years [20]. Studies have demonstrated that ALKBH5 plays an essential role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and modulating disease progression by regulating N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification and influencing fundamental biological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [21]. Accumulating evidence further indicates that ALKBH5 participates in the biogenesis and functional regulation of miRNAs [22]. However, despite these advances in understanding ALKBH5 role in miRNA-mediated immune regulation, its specific functional mechanisms in spinal tuberculosis remain largely unexplored. This study focuses on the ALKBH5–miR-221-3p regulatory axis and systematically examines its role and molecular mechanisms in the inflammatory response of spinal tuberculosis, thereby providing new perspectives on the pathogenesis of this disease.

Materials and methods

Patient specimens

A total of 50 vertebral tissue samples were obtained from patients with spinal tuberculosis (STB) who underwent surgical treatment and were assigned to the experimental group. The control group consisted of 50 patients undergoing surgery due to lumbar intervertebral disc degeneration. In addition, peripheral blood samples were collected from all 50 patients in each group. Both groups were strictly matched for age, gender, and ethnicity to minimize the influence of confounding variables. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningxia Medical University (Ethics Number: KYLL-2022-0133). Inclusion criteria for the experimental group were as follows: confirmed diagnosis of spinal tuberculosis based on comprehensive evaluations including imaging, laboratory tests, and histopathological analysis; presence of surgical indications and completion of surgery within our department. Exclusion criteria encompassed concurrent tuberculosis at other anatomical sites, other infectious diseases, neoplastic disorders, traumatic injuries, immunodeficiency conditions, and metabolic diseases. For the control group, inclusion criteria included a clinical diagnosis of lumbar degenerative disease with surgical indications, and no history of pulmonary or extrapulmonary tuberculosis. The exclusion criteria for the control group were identical to those of the experimental group.

Cell culture and induction of differentiation

THP-1 cells (human acute monocytic leukemia cell line) were maintained in RPMI-1640 complete medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol, under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂, humidified atmosphere). When the cell confluence reached 80–90%, viable cells were quantified using an automated cell counter and seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁶ cells per well in 6-well culture plates, with each well containing 2 mL of fresh complete medium. Following cell attachment, differentiation was induced by the addition of phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) at a final concentration of 75 ng/mL. The cells were then incubated under standard culture conditions for 48 h to induce macrophage differentiation, after which they were used for subsequent experimental procedures.

Transfection and infection

Small interfering RNA targeting ALKBH5 (siRNA-ALKBH5) was designed and synthesized by Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd. Following the manufacturer’s instructions for the transfection reagent, 10 µL of siRNA-ALKBH5, 10 µL of RNA transfection reagent, and 200 µL of serum-free RPMI-1640 medium were combined in a sterile centrifuge tube and gently mixed by pipetting 3–5 times. The mixture was incubated at room temperature in a laminar flow biosafety cabinet for 20 min to allow complex formation. Subsequently, the transfection mixture was evenly distributed into each well, and the culture plate was gently agitated to ensure homogeneous liquid distribution. The plate was then incubated under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂) for 24 h prior to downstream analysis. The transfection procedures for miR-221-3p mimic and miR-221-3p inhibitor (1.2 µg/mL), both obtained from Shanghai Hanheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., were performed as previously described for siRNA transfection. Briefly, the transfection mixture was added uniformly to each well, and the culture plate was gently agitated to ensure even distribution of the solution. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ for 24 h prior to further experimental treatments. Following transfection, THP-1 cells were differentiated into macrophage-like cells by treatment with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) at a concentration of 75 ng/mL for 24 h. Subsequently, Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) was introduced into the 6-well plates to establish an in vitro cellular model mimicking Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) infection, after which the designated assays were carried out.

Cell viability assay

The cytotoxic effect of Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) on THP-1-derived macrophages was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Wuhan Selleck Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). Following PMA-induced differentiation, macrophages were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well, with a final volume of 100 µL per well. After cell attachment, various experimental groups with different BCG concentrations and infection durations were established. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plate was gently agitated to ensure homogenous mixing. The cells were then incubated under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂) for the designated time period. Upon completion of the incubation, the reaction was terminated, and the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded tissue samples were serially sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. After baking in an oven at 60 °C for 2 h, the sections were dewaxed in xylene (15 min per change) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 85%, 75%). Antigen retrieval was performed via heat induction using 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Sections were heated to boiling in a microwave oven at high power, followed by maintenance at medium-low power for 10 min, and then allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the sections in 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol at room temperature for 15 min. To minimize non-specific binding, sections were incubated with a blocking solution containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 30 min. Following blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with the primary antibody (anti-ALKBH5, Affinity; dilution 1:200). The next day, sections were washed three times in PBS (5 min per wash) and subsequently incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgG, Beyotime; dilution 1:500) at 37 °C for 1 h. After additional washing, immunoreactivity was visualized using a diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen kit (ZSGB-BIO, China), with staining duration monitored under a microscope (typically 1–3 min). Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min, differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol, and blued in tap water. Finally, sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. All slides were examined and imaged using an upright light microscope (Leica, Germany).

Western blotting (WB)

Total protein was extracted from samples using a total protein extraction kit and quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Affinity, China). Aliquots of 30 µg protein were loaded onto 10% or 15% SDS-PAGE gels and separated by electrophoresis. Following electrophoretic separation, proteins were transferred onto methanol-activated polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% freshly prepared skim milk in TBST at room temperature for 2 h to prevent nonspecific binding. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific for ALKBH5 (1:1000, Affinity, China) and β-actin (1:1000, Affinity, China), the latter serving as an internal loading control. After three washes with 1% TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000, Affinity, China) at room temperature for 2 h. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The ECL reagent was prepared by mixing solutions A and B in a 1:1 ratio and applied to the membrane. The immunoreactive bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence imaging system, and the relative band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cell and tissue samples using the Total RNA Extraction Kit (Saiwei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and concentration were assessed prior to use. Reverse transcription was performed using the SweScript One-Step RT-PCR Kit (Saiwei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) to synthesize cDNA. Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using the miRNA qPCR Assay Kit with SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Saiwei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) in a final reaction volume of 20 µL, consisting of 10 µL SYBR Premix Ex Taq II, 0.8 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 2 µL cDNA template, and 6.4 µL RNase-free water. Amplification was carried out under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s; followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was performed at the end of each run: 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 95 °C for 15 s. All samples were analyzed in triplicate technical replicates. GAPDH or U6 was used as the endogenous control. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method, with the control group set as the calibrator. All qPCR primers were synthesized by Saiwei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and their specific sequences are provided in Table 1 of the main text.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Treated cells were seeded onto sterile coverslips pre-placed in 24-well plates. Following experimental treatment, the culture medium was aspirated and cells were gently rinsed three times with ice-cold PBS. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 15 min. After fixation, cells were washed three times with PBS (5 min per wash). For intracellular antigen detection, permeabilization was performed using PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature. The cells were subsequently washed three additional times with PBS. Blocking was carried out at room temperature for 1 h using a blocking solution of 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS. After removal of the blocking solution, primary antibodies—anti-ALKBH5, anti-TNF-α, anti-IL-1β, and anti-IL-6 (Affinity; dilution 1:200)—were diluted in blocking buffer and applied directly to the cells. The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. On the following day, coverslips were carefully removed and washed three times with PBS (5 min each). Fluorescent secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, Beyotime; dilution 1:500) were added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h in the dark. After incubation, the coverslips were washed three times with PBS in the dark (5 min per wash). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), and coverslips were mounted upside down onto glass slides using mounting medium and sealed with nail polish to prevent drying. Prepared slides were stored at 4 °C in the dark until imaging. All images were acquired using a Leica confocal laser scanning microscope under consistent settings across all samples within the same experiment to ensure comparability for semi-quantitative analysis.

m6A detection and MeRIP experiment

Total RNA was extracted from THP-1 cells using the Magna RIP™ RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation kit (Millipore, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal quantities of magnetic beads were incubated overnight at 4 °C with either rabbit anti-m6A monoclonal antibody (experimental group) or rabbit IgG (negative control group) under continuous agitation to form antibody-bead complexes. The following day, these complexes were introduced into an RNA fragmentation system supplemented with RIP immunoprecipitation buffer and incubated at 4 °C with gentle rotation for 12 h to selectively enrich m6A-modified RNA fragments. Upon completion of the immunoprecipitation, proteins were digested with proteinase K, and the RNA was subsequently purified using phenol: chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) extraction. The enrichment of pri-miR-221-3p was then assessed by reverse transcription qPCR, serving as an indicator of m6A modification levels.

10 Enzyme-Linked immunosorbent Assay(ELISA)

Supernatants from cell cultures under various treatment conditions were collected and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. After aliquoting, the samples were stored at -80 °C until analysis. Prior to the assay, frozen samples were thawed slowly on ice. A series of standard solutions (0–1000 pg/mL) were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions to generate a standard curve. Then, 100 µL of each standard or appropriately diluted sample was added to the antibody-precoated 96-well plate. The plate was covered and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. After discarding the liquid, each well was washed with 350 µL of wash buffer, allowed to stand for 30 s, and then aspirated; this washing procedure was repeated four times. After blotting dry, 100 µL of biotinylated detection antibody was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. Following another wash step, 100 µL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated streptavidin was added, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After the final wash, 100 µL of TMB substrate solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 15–20 min for color development. When a clear blue color gradient appeared in the high-concentration standard wells, the reaction was terminated by adding 50 µL of stop solution per well. The absorbance of each well was immediately measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The actual concentration of cytokines in the samples was calculated based on the regression equation of the standard curve, with all results expressed in pg/mL. Each sample was assayed in triplicate to ensure the reliability of the experimental data.

11 statistical analysis

Experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.1.0 software. For comparisons between two independent groups, independent-sample t-tests were employed; for comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was applied to evaluate the association between peripheral blood miR-221-3p expression levels and clinical count data, while curve regression analysis was used to model the dose-response relationship. The diagnostic performance of miR-221-3p for STB was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) calculated as a measure of discriminatory accuracy. An AUC value ≥ 0.6 was predefined as indicative of potential diagnostic utility. In this study, all constructed ROC curves were based on a multivariate prediction model that incorporated the expression level of miR-221-3p together with key clinical parameters—including imaging findings, T-SPOT results, acid-fast staining, pathological examination, and bacteriological testing—as predictive variables. This integrated model was developed to assess the combined diagnostic performance for clinically relevant identification of spinal tuberculosis. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Screening and validation of differentially expressed MiRNAs in spinal tuberculosis patients’ tissues

Under the initial screening criteria, a total of 33 downregulated and 49 upregulated miRNAs were identified in vertebral tissues from patients with spinal tuberculosis (STB) (Fig. 1A, B). The differential expression profiles were visualized using a volcano plot and confirmed through cluster analysis (Fig. 1A, B). Among the 10 differentially expressed miRNAs, miR-221-3p was selected for further validation by qRT-PCR (Fig. 1C). Expression levels of miR-221-3p in both tissue and peripheral blood samples were found to be significantly lower in STB patients compared to controls, with consistent and stable expression patterns observed (Fig. 1D, E). ROC curve analysis revealed that miR-221-3p had an AUC value of 0.871 in peripheral blood samples, indicating strong diagnostic potential (an AUC > 0.6 is considered diagnostically relevant) (Fig. 1F). These findings suggest that miR-221-3p holds promise as a potential biomarker for the detection of spinal tuberculosis.

Diagnostic value of miR-221-3p in combination with clinical parameters and its correlation with quantitative clinical data in STB patients

The diagnostic performance of miR-221-3p in peripheral blood was evaluated across multiple clinical diagnostic modalities in STB patients, including imaging diagnosis, T-SPOT results, acid-fast staining, pathological findings, and bacteriological tests using ROC curve analysis. The AUC values for miR-221-3p were 0.709, 0.687, 0.722, 0.793, and 0.765 respectively (AUC > 0.6 is considered diagnostically relevant) (Fig. 2A–E). The ROC analysis demonstrates that miR-221-3p exhibits strong performance in differentiating STB from lumbar intervertebral disc degeneration; however, its discriminatory capacity is reduced when distinguishing STB from pyogenic spondylitis. These findings indicate that miR-221-3p is more closely associated with macrophage activation in the context of infection and inflammation, rather than serving as a disease-specific biomarker exclusively for STB. (Fig. 1F, G). Curve regression analysis revealed that miR-221-3p expression levels in peripheral blood were significantly correlated with CRP and ESR levels (P < 0.01), but showed no significant association with disease duration, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, or lymphocyte count (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2H–M). These findings suggest that miR-221-3p may serve as a potential biomarker for integrating imaging, immunological, microbiological, and peripheral blood-based diagnostic approaches in STB (Table S2, Table S3).

Determination of optimal BCG infection conditions in macrophages: concentration and time gradients

Following PMA-induced differentiation of THP-1 cells into macrophages, BCG infection was performed under varying concentrations and time points to simulate the infection state of MTB. Cytotoxicity was assessed using the CCK-8 assay across different BCG concentrations and infection durations (Fig. 3A, B). Western blotting was employed to detect the expression level of ALKBH5 (Fig. 3C, D), while ELISA was used to quantify the secretion of inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α (Fig. 3E–J). Experimental results demonstrated that BCG at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 15 for 48 h exhibited significant cytotoxic effects on macrophages. Furthermore, the expression levels of ALKBH5 and the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α increased progressively with higher BCG concentrations and longer infection times. Based on these findings, an MOI of 10 and an infection duration of 24 h were selected as the optimal experimental conditions for subsequent studies.

Involvement of ALKBH5 and miR-221-3p in the regulation of inflammatory response in STB

Immunohistochemical and qRT-PCR analyses of vertebral tissue from patients with lumbar degenerative changes and spinal tuberculosis, as well as peripheral blood samples from both spinal tuberculosis patients and healthy controls, revealed that ALKBH5 expression levels in the vertebral tissue and peripheral blood of spinal tuberculosis patients were significantly elevated compared to those in both the disease control group and the healthy control group(Fig. 4A, B). Furthermore, an STB cell model was established by infecting THP-1-derived macrophages with BCG. qRT-PCR and immunofluorescence analyses confirmed a significant increase in ALKBH5 expression under these experimental conditions (Fig. 4C, D). ELISA results demonstrated that the levels of inflammatory cytokines were markedly elevated following ALKBH5 knockdown (Fig. 4E–G). Similarly, miR-221-3p knockdown led to increased inflammatory factor expression, whereas miR-221-3p mimic treatment resulted in their downregulation (Fig. 4H–J).

ALKBH5 regulates miR-221-3p processing in STB via an m6A-Dependent mechanism

Accumulating evidence indicates that m6A modification plays a critical role in the post-transcriptional regulation and biogenesis of miRNAs. Therefore, we investigated whether ALKBH5 regulates miR-221-3p in the context of spinal tuberculosis. First, we confirmed the knockdown efficiency of ALKBH5 (Fig. 5A, B). Subsequent qRT-PCR analysis revealed that miR-221-3p was upregulated, while the expression of its primary transcript (pri-miR-221-3p) was reduced in ALKBH5-knockdown cells (Fig. 5C). m6A-qPCR analysis demonstrated that pri-miR-221-3p underwent substantial m6A methylation, and ALKBH5 knockdown further increased the methylation level of pri-miR-221-3p (Fig. 5D, E). Moreover, MeRIP-qPCR results showed that ALKBH5 knockdown enhanced m6A modification levels in both miR-221-3p and pri-miR-221-3p (Fig. 5F, G). Collectively, these findings indicate that ALKBH5 knockdown facilitates the recognition of pri-miR-221-3p and promotes its maturation through an m6A-dependent mechanism.

ALKBH5 mediates miR-221-3p to exacerbate inflammatory factor release in spinal tuberculosis

To further validate whether ALKBH5 modulates the inflammatory response after BCG infection through miR-221-3p, we performed a rescue experiment. siRNA targeting ALKBH5 and a miR-221-3p inhibitor were co-transfected into BCG-infected THP-1-derived macrophages, followed by a 24-hour co-culture period. ELISA and immunofluorescence analyses demonstrated that the miR-221-3p inhibitor partially reversed the anti-inflammatory effects induced by ALKBH5 knockdown (Fig. 6A–I). These findings suggest that ALKBH5 exacerbates the inflammatory response in spinal tuberculosis by suppressing miR-221-3p expression.

Discussion

The critical role of m6A methylation in regulating inflammatory and immune responses is well established in contemporary biological research. In this study, we investigated the regulatory mechanism by which ALKBH5 modulates the inflammatory response in spinal tuberculosis through miR-221-3p. Our results indicate that ALKBH5 mediates m6A demethylation of pri-miR-221-3p, impairing its maturation and thereby promoting the progression of spinal tuberculosis.It is noteworthy that our data demonstrate a limited capacity of miR-221-3p to distinguish between STB and suppurative spondylitis. This finding suggests that miR-221-3p may function as a robust biomarker associated with inflammatory or infectious processes, rather than serving as a specific and exclusive marker for STB. The diagnostic utility of miR-221-3p is therefore more appropriately realized within a multimodal biomarker panel, in conjunction with other more disease-specific indicators, to improve overall diagnostic accuracy.

In recent years, significant progress has been made in miRNA-related research, with an increasing number of biological functions mediated by miRNAs being identified [23–25]. miRNAs are extensively involved in the fine-tuned regulation of inflammatory responses, tumorigenesis and progression, as well as a variety of other biological processes [26, 27]. Furthermore, small interfering RNA plays a critical regulatory role in the pathogenesis and progression of various diseases [28–30].In the context of tuberculosis, miRNAs serve as key regulators of post-transcriptional immune responses by modulating the expression of genes associated with both innate and adaptive immunity [31]. During Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection, miRNAs play crucial roles in the activation of macrophage signaling pathways, regulation of cytokine secretion, control of autophagy and apoptosis, and modulation of key cytokines such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [32, 33]. For instance, miR-20a-3p has been shown to inhibit the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines by targeting the IKKβ/NF-κB pathway, thereby promoting the survival of Mycobacterium tuberculosis [34]. miR-342-3p regulates the switch between apoptosis and necrosis during M. tuberculosis infection via modulation of the SOCS6 axis, consequently shaping host anti-tuberculosis immunity [35]. In our study, miR-221-3p was found to be significantly downregulated in both clinical tissues from spinal tuberculosis patients and in established cell models. This observation suggests its potential as a diagnostic biomarker for spinal tuberculosis.Our study found that miR-221-3p exerts an anti-inflammatory effect in the STB context, which seems to be contradictory to its pro-inflammatory effect reported in some chronic inflammatory diseases. We speculate that this functional difference may stem from the specificity of disease types and inflammatory microenvironments. The function of miRNAs is highly dependent on the environment. Previous studies focused on skeletal muscle inflammation and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, which belong to chronic, non-infectious inflammatory environments. While this study focuses on tuberculosis, a specific immune inflammatory response driven by intracellular bacterial infection. These two completely different etiologies and immune microenvironments are very likely to determine that miR-221-3p plays opposite roles.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation is a critical RNA modification widely present in eukaryotic tissues and cells, playing essential roles in diverse biological processes [36]. Characterized by its dynamic and reversible nature, m6A has emerged as a central mechanism in the regulation of gene expression [37]. Accumulating evidence indicates that m6A modification influences fundamental cellular processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis through its effects on RNA splicing, translation initiation, nuclear-cytoplasmic transport, intracellular localization, and degradation [38]. Among the family of RNA demethylases, FTO and ALKBH5 are often termed “molecular erasers” for their ability to remove m6A methylation marks [39]. Among them, ALKBH5 serves as a key regulator of m6A modification and has been implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple diseases [40]. A study by Zhang et al. employing SNPscan technology revealed that ALKBH5 mRNA expression was significantly downregulated in patients with pulmonary tuberculosis but markedly upregulated in those with drug-resistant tuberculosis, based on genotyping analysis of over 400 individuals [41]. These findings suggest that ALKBH5 may participate in miRNA biogenesis during disease initiation and progression. Existing studies have demonstrated that m6A can interact with RNA-binding proteins to modulate miRNA-mediated mRNA splicing and translational regulation [42]. Furthermore, m6A methylation has been shown to influence miRNA biogenesis by regulating the processing of primary miRNA (pri-miRNA) or the splicing of precursor miRNA (pre-miRNA) [43]. For instance, miR-193a-3p suppresses ALKBH5 expression through direct targeting, while ALKBH5 negatively regulates the m6A modification of miR-193a-3p, thereby affecting its primary processing. This feedback regulatory loop promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) cells and facilitates tumor development and metastasis in vivo [22]. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrates that ALKBH5 knockdown reduces m6A methylation levels on pri-miR-221-3p, impairs its maturation, and consequently exacerbates inflammatory responses in spinal tuberculosis. However, the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying this regulatory axis warrant further investigation. In summary, both previous studies and our current findings demonstrate that the m6A demethylase ALKBH5 significantly influences disease initiation and progression by modulating miRNA maturation efficiency and remodeling the post-transcriptional landscape [44]. In this study, we establish a mechanistic link between ALKBH5-mediated m6A modification of pri-miR-221-3p and the inflammatory pathogenesis of spinal tuberculosis. Nevertheless, certain limitations exist in this study. Although our in vitro experiments clearly validated the regulatory effects of ALKBH5 on miR-221-3p expression and its functional mechanisms in BCG-infected THP-1 cells, the in vivo relevance of this regulatory pathway remains to be determined, representing a key focus for future investigations. Moreover, further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to assess the potential of miR-221-3p as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker. This study utilized BCG-infected macrophages to simulate the early innate immune response triggered by Mtb infection. We must clearly point out a key limitation of this model: BCG indeed lacks certain virulence factors present in the wild-type Mtb. The absence of these factors may lead to weakened later pathogenic capabilities such as escape from phagosomes and induction of cell necrosis. Therefore, the results of this study mainly reveal the miRNA regulatory mechanism of the host during the early infection and immune initiation stage of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. However, this may not fully represent all situations in the immune pathological process under the complete virulence effect of Mtb. Nevertheless, the core focus of our study lies in elucidating the basic regulatory role of miR-221-3p in the inflammatory response mediated by macrophages, and the BCG model provides a robust and highly relevant research platform for this purpose. Future studies will need to further verify these findings in an Mtb strain infection model with complete virulence.Despite these limitations, in light of the growing recognition of RNA post-transcriptional modifications in physiological regulation and disease pathogenesis, our findings highlight the critical regulatory role of m6A methylation in BCG-infected THP-1 cells. Our experimental data provide preliminary proof-of-concept evidence supporting the notion that targeting ALKBH5 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for spinal tuberculosis. We remain optimistic that specific ALKBH5 inhibitors will eventually enter clinical development, offering novel therapeutic options for patients suffering from this debilitating disease.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that miR-221-3p is consistently downregulated in spinal tuberculosis tissues, peripheral blood, and cellular models, showcasing its potential as a biomarker for integrated diagnostic approaches, including imaging, T-SPOT results, acid-fast staining, bacteriological testing, and peripheral blood analysis.Furthermore, ALKBH5 reduces miR-221-3p expression by modulating its m6A modification level, thereby enhancing the secretion of inflammatory factors in macrophages and ultimately promoting disease progression. Our findings demonstrate that ALKBH5-mediated m6A modification of miR-221-3p plays a crucial regulatory role in the pathogenesis of spinal tuberculosis and represents a promising therapeutic target. This work provides a molecular foundation for novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies against spinal tuberculosis, paving the way for future clinical translation with significant scientific and clinical implications.
