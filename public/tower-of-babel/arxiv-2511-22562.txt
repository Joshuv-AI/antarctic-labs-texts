Making an oriented graph acyclic using inversions of bounded or prescribed size
Jørgen Bang-Jensen, Frédéric Havet, Florian Hörsch, Clément Rambaud, Amadeus Reinald, Caroline Silva

Abstract
Given an oriented graph D D , the inversion of a subset X X of vertices consists in reversing the orientation of all arcs with both endpoints in X X . When the subset X X is of size p p (resp. at most p p ), this operation is called an ( = p ) (=p) -inversion (resp. ( ⩽ p ) (\leqslant p) -inversion ). Then, an oriented graph is ( = p ) (=p) -invertible if it can be made acyclic by a sequence of p p -inversions. We observe that, for n = | V ⁡ ( D ) | n=|V(D)| , deciding whether D D is ( = n − 1 ) (=n-1) -invertible is equivalent to deciding whether D D is acyclically pushable, and thus NP-complete. In all other cases, when p ≠ n − 1 p\neq n-1 , we construct a polynomial-time algorithm to decide ( = p ) (=p) -invertibility. We then consider the ( = p ) (=p) -inversion number , inv = p ⁡ ( D ) \inv^{=p}(D) (resp. ( ⩽ p ) (\leqslant p) -inversion number , inv ⩽ p ⁡ ( D ) \inv^{\leqslant p}(D) ), defined as the minimum number of ( = p ) (=p) -inversions (resp. ( ⩽ p ) (\leqslant p) -inversions) rendering D D acyclic. We show that every ( = p ) (=p) -invertible digraph D D satisfies inv = p ⁡ ( D ) ⩽ | A ⁡ ( D ) | \inv^{=p}(D)\leqslant|A(D)| for every integer p ⩾ 2 p\geqslant 2 . When p p is even, we bound inv = p \inv^{=p} by a (linear) function of the feedback arc set number, and rule out the existence of any bounding function for odd p p . Finally, we study the complexity of deciding whether the ( = p ) (=p) -inversion number, or the ( ⩽ p ) (\leqslant p) -inversion number, of a given oriented graph is at most a given integer k k . For any fixed positive integer p ⩾ 2 p\geqslant 2 , when k k is part of the input, we show that both problems are NP-hard even in tournaments. In general oriented graphs, we prove W ⁡ [ 1 ] W[1] -hardness for both problems when parameterized by p p , even for k = 1 k=1 . In contrast, we exhibit polynomial kernels in p + k p+k for both problems in tournaments. Keywords: inversion ; orientation; NP-hardness; acyclicity ; reconfiguration.

Introduction

Problems concerned with rendering a digraph acyclic through a small number of operations are among the most studied in digraph theory.
A prominent example are
feedback arc sets
, which for a digraph
D
, are the subsets of arcs whose deletion makes
D
acyclic.
The corresponding parameter is the
feedback-arc-set number

\fas(D)
of
D
, defined as the minimum size of a feedback arc set.
Algorithmically, the computation of
\fas(D)
is the
Feedback Arc Set
problem, already appearing in Karp’s seminal list of 21 NP-hard problems
[
20
]
.
A well-studied restriction is
Feedback Arc Set
on tournaments, which was shown to be NP-complete by Alon
[
2
]
and Charbit, Thomassé, and Yeo
[
10
]
.
Nevertheless, this restriction admits a polynomial-time approximation scheme, due to Kenyon and Schudy
[
21
]
, and linear kernels of size
(2+\varepsilon)k
for every
\varepsilon>0
, as shown by Bessy et al.
[
7
]
.
Asymptotic considerations of
\fas
were first brought up by Erdös and Moon
[
12
]
, who looked at estimating
\fas(n)
, the maximum value of
\fas
over digraphs of order
n
.
This question was eventually settled thanks to results by Spencer
[
34
,
35
]
and de la Vega
[
14
]
, achieving to show
\inv^{\leqslant 2}(n)=\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{2}-\Theta(n^{3/2})
.
In this paper, we investigate analogous questions when looking to render a digraph acyclic through
inversions of bounded size
, which are operations generalizing arc deletions.

Observe that if
F
is a minimum feedback arc set in a digraph
D=(V,A)
, then we may obtain an acyclic digraph from
D
by either removing the arcs of
F
, or by reversing each of them: replacing each
uv\in F
with
vu
.
In this sense,
Feedback Arc Set
can be rephrased as a problem about arc reversals.
Lifting this, Belkhechine et al.
[
6
]
introduced inversions, allowing for the “reversal” of any induced subdigraph.
Formally, if
X
is a set of vertices of
D
, the
inversion
of
X
consists in reversing the orientation of all arcs with both endvertices in
X
.
Then, the
inversion number

\inv(D)
of a digraph
D
, is the minimum number of inversions needed to turn
D
acyclic.
Observe that questions about
\inv(D)
are only of interest for oriented graphs — digraphs forbidding digons (cycles of length two).
Indeed, if a digon
uv,vu
exists in
D
, it will be preserved by any inversion, meaning
\inv(D)=+\infty
.
On the other hand, if
uv
is not part of a digon, then inverting
\{u,v\}
corresponds to the reversal of
uv
. In particular, a digraph can be rendered acyclic by a sequence of inversions if and only if it is oriented.

Following the well-established literature on feedback arc sets, inversion number has been studied both algorithmically and asymptotically.
On the algorithmic side, the authors of
[
6
]
proved that determining whether a given tournament
T
satisfies
\inv(T)\leqslant k
is in XP, that is, it is polynomial-time solvable for every fixed
k
.
This was improved upon by Alon et al.
[
1
]
, who showed that the problem is fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) with respects to
k
, meaning it admits an
f(k)n^{O(1)}
algorithm.
In contrast, Bang-Jensen et al.
[
4
]
proved that given oriented graph
D
, deciding
\inv(D)\leqslant k
is NP-complete for
k\in\{1,2\}
. This was generalized to all
k
by Alon et al.
[
1
]
.
On the asymptotic side, the main problem has been to estimate
\inv(n)
, the maximum inversion number over all oriented graphs of order
n
.
Independently, Aubian et al.
[
3
]
and Alon et al.
[
1
]
proved
n-2\sqrt{n\log n}\leqslant\inv(n)\leqslant n-\lceil\log(n+1)\rceil
.

In this paper, we will be interested in finer variants of inversions by bounding (or prescribing) the size of the inverted sets.
Inversions of bounded size were recently introduced by Yuster
[
36
]
as
(\leqslant p)
-inversions
, which are inversions over sets of size at most
p
.
These operations range from arc reversals when
p=2
, allowing us to express feedback arc sets, to general inversions when
p
exceeds the order
n
.
Given a digraph
D
, the parameter of interest is the
(\leqslant p)
-inversion number
,
\inv^{\leqslant p}(D)
, defined as the minimum number of
(\leqslant p)
-inversions rendering
D
acyclic.
Yuster investigated the asymptotic behaviour of
\inv^{\leqslant p}(n)
, defined as the maximum of
(\leqslant p)
-inversion numbers over all oriented graphs of order
n
.
Observe that
\inv^{\leqslant 2}(D)=\fas(D)
, meaning all results on
\fas
apply for
p=2
.
Asymptotically, this means we already know that
\inv^{\leqslant 2}(n)=\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{2}-\Theta(n^{3/2})
by
[
14
]
.
Yuster
[
36
]
proved

\frac{1}{12}n^{2}-o(n^{2})\leqslant\inv^{\leqslant 3}(n)\leqslant\frac{257}{2592}n^{2}+o(n^{2})

and conjectured

\inv^{\leqslant 3}(n)=\frac{1}{12}n^{2}+o(n^{2})
.
He also proved that there exists a positive integer
p_{0}
such that,
for every integer
p
with
p\geqslant p_{0}
, there exist
\delta_{p},\varepsilon_{p}>0
such that

\left(\frac{1}{2p(p-1)}+\delta_{p}\right)n^{2}-o(n^{2})\leqslant\inv^{\leqslant p}(n)\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{2\lfloor p^{2}/2\rfloor}-\varepsilon_{p}\right)n^{2}+o(n^{2})

as
n
tends to
+\infty
.
Still, the computational aspects of
\inv^{\leqslant p}
remained largely open until now.

Overview of the results

In this paper, we consider both inversions of bounded size, and those of prescribed size, for which we initiate the study. An inversion of prescribed size, specifically a
(=p)
-inversion
, is the inversion of a set size exactly
p
.
Then, the
(=p)
-inversion number

\inv^{=p}(D)
of an oriented graph
D
as the minimum number of
(=p)
-inversions rendering
D
acyclic.
In
Section

3
, we address the question of
(=p)
-invertibility, that is, deciding whether
\inv^{=p}(D)<\infty
.
Then,

deals with asymptotic bounds on

1.1.1
Inversions of prescribed size

Section

3
addresses arguably the first natural question arising when considering
(=p)
-inversions: given an oriented graph, is it
(=p)
-invertible
at all? That is, can it be made acyclic using (any number of)
(=p)
-inversions?
Clearly, this is not the case for oriented graphs of order at most
p
that are not already acyclic. But this is not the only obstacle: for every odd
p
, there exist oriented graphs of arbitrarily large order which are not
(=p)
-invertible.
A simple invariant witnessing this when
p
is odd, is given by the parity of the out-degrees of the vertices of a tournament, which is preserved under
(=p)
-inversions.
Hence, to be
(=p)
-invertible, a tournament of order
n
must have exactly as many vertices of even out-degree as
TT_{n}
, the transitive tournament of order
n
, that is
\lceil n/2\rceil
.

We exhibit a dichotomy on the complexity of
(=p)
-invertibility: given an
n
-vertex oriented graph
D
and a positive integer
p
, deciding whether
D
is
(=p)
-invertible is polynomial, except if
p=n-1
, in which case this problem is NP-hard.
First, in Subsection
3.1
, we show that
(n-1)
-Invertibility
is equivalent to decide whether a given digraph
D
is acyclically pushable. It is thus NP-complete as shown by Klostermeyer
[
24
]
.

Next, the remainder of Section
3
is devoted to prove the polynomial-time solvability when
p\neq n-1
.
First, in Subsection
3.2
, we characterize the pairs of tournaments on the same vertex set that can be transformed from one to the other using
(=p)
-inversions.
This is done by using the notion of
forward arcs
, which for a (ordered) tournament
T
on
[n]
, are the arcs
ij
with
i<j
. We denote by
F(T)
the set of forwards arcs in
T
and by
F(T,i)
the set of forwards arcs in
T
incident with
i
and by
F^{+}(T,i)
the set of forwards arcs in
T
with tail
i
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n,p
be positive integers with
n\geqslant p+2
,
and let
T_{1},T_{2}
be two tournaments with vertex set
[n]
.
There exists a family
X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}\subseteq[n]
such that
T_{2}=\Inv(T_{1};X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell})
if and only if
one of the following occurs

•

p=0\mod 4
and
|F(T_{1})|=|F(T_{2})|\mod 2
,

•

p=1\mod 4
,
|F(T_{1},i)|=|F(T_{2},i)|\mod 2
for every
i\in[n-1]
and
|F(T_{1})|=|F(T_{2})|
.

•

p=2\mod 4
, or

•

p=3\mod 4
, and
|F(T_{1},i)|=|F(T_{2},i)|\mod 2
for every
i\in[n-1]
.

This immediately yields the following algorithmic result.

Corollary 1.2
.

Given an integer
p\geqslant 2
and two tournaments
T_{1},T_{2}
with vertex set
[n]
,
where
n\geqslant p+2
, one can decide in linear time whether there exists a
(=p)
-family
\mathcal{X}
of subsets of
[n]
such that
\Inv(T_{1};\mathcal{X})=T_{2}
.

Based on
Theorem

1.1
,
we prove the following characterisation of
n
-vertex tournaments which are
(=p)
-invertible.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
p\geqslant 2
and let
n\geqslant p+2
. Let
T
be a tournament with vertex set
[n]
.
The tournament
T
is
(=p)
-invertible if and only if

p
is even or
|\{i\in[n]\mid d^{+}(i)\text{ even}\}|=\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
.

Then, in Subsection
3.3
, we prove the following algorithmic theorem for general oriented graphs.

Theorem 1.4
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that, given a positive integer
p
and an oriented graph
D
of order distinct from
p+1
, decides whether
D
is
(=p)
-invertible.

The dichotomy between the even and odd cases for
p
, outlined in Theorem
1.3
for tournaments, extends to oriented graphs.
Indeed, while there exist non-
(=p)
-invertible oriented graphs of every order at least 3 when
p
is odd, every oriented graph of order at least
p+2
is
(=p)
-invertible when
p
is even.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
p
be a positive even integer.
Every oriented graph of order at least
p+2
is
(=p)
-invertible.

1.1.2
Asymptotic bounds

Once we know that an oriented graph
D
is
(=p)
-invertible, we can ask the question of how many
(=p)
-inversions are needed to render it acyclic, that is its
(=p)
-inversion number
, denoted by
\inv^{=p}(D)
, is the minimum number of
(=p)
-inversions rendering
D
acyclic, or
+\infty
if
D
is not
(=p)
-invertible.
Clearly,
\inv^{=p}(D)\geqslant\inv^{\leqslant p}(D)
and
\inv^{=2}(D)=\inv^{\leqslant 2}(D)=\fas(D)
.
For every integer
n
, we let
\inv^{=p}(n)
be the maximum of the
(=p)
-inversion number over all the
(=p)
-invertible oriented graphs of order
n
.

Our next contribution, in Section
4
, is to provide upper bounds on the
(=p)
-inversion number.
We first prove in Subsection
4.1
an upper bound in terms of the size of the oriented graph.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
D
be an oriented graph and let
p
be an integer.
If
D
is
(=p)
-invertible, then
\inv^{=p}(D)\leqslant|A(D)|
.

We then restrict our study to the case of
even
p
. Then, recall that all the oriented graphs of order at least
p+2
are
(=p)
-invertible by Theorem
1.5
.
We establish a collection of incomparable upper bounds on the
(=p)
-inversion number as a linear function of the feedback-arc-set number and a corrective factor depending on the order.
The smaller the linear function, the larger the corrective factor.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
p\geqslant 4
be an even integer and
D
an oriented graph of order
n
. Then

\inv^{=p}(D)\leqslant\left\{\begin{array}[]{lrlll}(i)&(2p-2)\fas(D)&+\bigO(1)&\text{if $n\geqslant p+2$}&\text{\rm(Theorem~\ref{thm:ub-fas}),}\\
(ii)&2\fas(D)&+\bigO(n)&\text{if $n\geqslant p+2$}&\text{\rm(Theorem~\ref{thm:bound-2fas}),}\\
(iii)&\fas(D)&+\bigO(n^{1+1/k})&\text{if $n\geqslant p+2k+2$}&\text{\rm(Theorem~\ref{thm:bound-fas}),}\\
(iv)&\frac{1}{\lceil p/2\rceil\cdot\lfloor p/2\rfloor}\fas(D)&+o(n^{2})&&\text{\rm(Theorem~\ref{thm:upper-opt}).}\end{array}\right.

Observe that the bound (iv) in this theorem is asymtotically tight. Indeed, if
D

is a bipartite oriented graph, then each
(=p)
-inversion reverses at most
\lceil p/2\rceil\cdot\lfloor p/2\rfloor
arcs, and so
\inv^{=p}(D)\geqslant\frac{1}{\lceil p/2\rceil\cdot\lfloor p/2\rfloor}\fas(D)
.

The upper bound
(i)
of
Theorem

1.7
has several consequences.
The first one is that
\inv^{=p}(n)
and
\inv^{\leqslant p}(n)
are close to each other when
p
is even.

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
p
be an even positive integer. Then

\inv^{=p}(n)=\inv^{\leqslant p}(n)+\Theta(n)
.

Together with Yuster’s result, this implies

\displaystyle\inv^{=p}(n)\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}-\varepsilon_{p}\right)n^{2}+o(n^{2})
for some positive
\varepsilon_{p}
when
p
is even and at least
4
.

The second consequence of
(i)
is that, for any fixed even positive integer
p
,

\inv^{=p}
and
\inv^{\leqslant p}
are (linearly) tied.

Corollary 1.9
.

Let
p\geqslant 4
be an even integer, and let
D
be an oriented graph with at least
p+2
vertices and at least one directed cycle. Then,

\inv^{\leqslant p}(D)\leqslant\inv^{=p}(D)\leqslant(4p-4)\binom{p}{2}\inv^{\leqslant p}(D).

A third consequence is the following.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
p\geqslant 4
be an even integer and let
D
be an oriented graph of order
n\geqslant p+2
. Then,

\inv^{=p}(D)\leqslant\frac{|A(D)|}{p-1}+2p^{2}n.

This improves on Theorem
1.6
for oriented graphs with a superlinear number of arcs.

In Subsection
4.3
, we investigate whether analogous results to the ones obtained for even values of

hold when
p
is odd.
It turns out that this is not the case for the upper bound (i) of Theorem
1.7
: in Theorem
4.15
, we prove that the
(=p)
-inversion number cannot be upper bounded by any function of the feedback arc-set number.
This implies that, unlike in the case where
p
is even,
\inv^{=p}
and
\inv^{\leqslant p}
are not tied when
p
is odd.
This further accentuates the dichotomy between even and odd values of

.
The existence of upper bounds on the
(=p)
-inversion number analogous to the bounds (ii) to (iv) of Theorem
1.7
is left as open problems.

Notations and definitions

Digraph notation not given below is consistent with
[

]
.
For an oriented graph
D
, let

be an ordering of the vertices of

. An arc

is

(according to

) if

and

(according to

) if

.
It is well-known that an oriented graph is acyclic if and only if it admits an

(sometimes called topological ordering), that is, an ordering of its vertices with no backward arcs.

An
(=p)
-set
(resp. a
(\leqslant p)
-set
) is a set of cardinality exactly

(resp. at most
p
).
Let
D
be an oriented graph and

be a family of subsets of

.
We say that
\cal X
is a
(=p)
-family
(resp.
(\leqslant p)
-family
) if all members of
\cal X
are
(=p)
-sets (resp.
(\leqslant p)
-sets).
We denote by

the oriented graph obtained after inverting all sets of
\cal X
one after another. Observe that this is independent of the order in which we invert those sets:
\Inv(D;{\cal X})
is obtained from

by reversing exactly those arcs for which an odd number of members of
{\cal X}
contain both endvertices.
If
{\cal X}=\{X\}
for a set

, then we write
\Inv(D,X)
for
\Inv(D;{\cal X})
.
A

of an oriented graph

of subsets of

such that

is acyclic.

3.2
, we give our characterization for
(=p)
-invertibility for tournaments in all cases, that is, we prove
Theorem

1.3
. As a side product, we also prove
Theorem

1.1
. Finally, in Section
3.3
, we use this result to obtain
Theorem

1.4
, the corresponding algorithmic result in arbitrary oriented graphs, and
Theorem

1.5
.

3.1
NP-completeness of
(n-1)
-Invertibility

Let
D
be an oriented graph and
X\subseteq V(D)
. We define
\Push(D,X)
to be the oriented graph obtained from
D
by reversing the orientation of all arcs with exactly one endvertex in
X
. We say
the vertices of
X
are
pushed
and that
\Push(D,X)
is the result of
pushing
X
in
D
.
This operation has been studied in many papers
[
15
,
25
,
24
,
23
,
22
,
31
,
17
,
19
,
18
,
33
,
16
]
.
An oriented graph is
acyclically pushable
if there exists a set
X
of vertices such that
\Push(D,X)
is acyclic. Observe that
\Push(D,\{x\})
is obtained from
\Inv(D,V(D)\setminus\{x\})
by reversing every arc.
Thus an oriented graph on
n
vertices is acyclically pushable if and only if it is
(=n-1)
-invertible.
Klostermeyer
[
24
]
proved that deciding whether a given oriented graph is
acyclically pushable is NP-complete, and
Huang, McGillivray and Yeo
[
18
]
showed it remains NP-complete when restricted to bipartite graphs.
This directly implies the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

(n-1)
-Invertibility
is NP-complete, even for oriented bipartite graphs.

3.2
Characterization of
(=p)
-invertible tournaments

In this section, we prove
Theorems

1.1
and
1.3
.
The proof splits into four cases, depending on the value of
p
modulo
4
.

Let
p,n
be integers with
p\geqslant 2
and
n\geqslant p+2
.
When we want to go from a tournament with vertex set
[n]
to another tournament with the same vertex set using
(=p)
-inversions, several obstructions appear, depending on the value of
p
modulo
4
.
When
p
is odd, for any vertex
v
,
d^{+}(v)\bmod 2
is invariant under
(=p)
-inversions.
Moreover, when
\binom{p}{2}
is even (that is when
p=0\bmod 4
or
p=1\bmod 4
), the parity of the number of backward arcs with respect to the natural ordering (the arcs
ji
with
i<j
) is also invariant
under
(=p)
-inversions.
We will actually show that these two invariants are essentially the only ones, which gives a characterization
of tournaments reachable from any given tournament via
(=p)
-inversions.
To prove that, we will use an algebraic representation of the problem.

We shall do all operations in the two-element field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. For convenience, we index the vectors of the vector space

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
by the
(=2)
-sets of
[n]
.
Thus the coordinates of a vector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
are named as
\mathbf{u}_{\{i,j\}}
for every pair of distinct integers
i,j
in
[n]
.

We start by defining a representation of (labelled)
n
-vertex tournaments by
\binom{n}{2}
-dimensional vectors over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
Let
T
be a tournament with vertex set
[n]
.
We define
\mathbf{a}_{T}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
by

(\mathbf{a}_{T})_{\{i,j\}}=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if $ji\in A(T)$,}\\
0&\textrm{if $ij\in A(T)$,}\end{cases}

for every
i,j\in[n]
with
i<j
.
Let
X\subseteq[n]
, and let
\mathbf{i}_{X}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
be defined by

(\mathbf{i}_{X})_{\{i,j\}}=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if $i,j\in X$,}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for every
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
.
The point of these definitions is the following observation.

Observation 3.2
.

For every tournament
T
on
[n]
and for every
X\subseteq[n]
,

\mathbf{a}_{\Inv(T;X)}=\mathbf{a}_{T}+\mathbf{i}_{X}.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
will consist (for
p
and
n
fixed) in defining a linear mapping
\pi\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}

for some
t>0
such that for all tournaments
T^{\prime},T
on
[n]
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{there exists an $(=p)$-family $\mathcal{X}\subseteq 2^{[n]}$ such that $\Inv(T;\mathcal{X})=T^{\prime}$}\\
\text{if and only if $\pi(\mathbf{a}_{T})=\pi(\mathbf{a}_{T^{\prime}})$.}\end{array}

(1)

Informally,
\pi
corresponds here to the most general quantity which is invariant under
(=p)
-inversions.

Equation (
1
) can be reformulated as follows.
For every
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
, with
\mathbf{0}
being the null element of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}
:

\mathbf{u}\in\spn\{\mathbf{i}_{X}\mid X\subseteq[n],|X|=p\}
if and only if
\pi(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}
.

(2)

We construct the linear
\pi
according to the value of
p
modulo
4
, in order to satisfy (
2
).
Let us begin by showing a few lemmas routinely used across all cases.

3.2.1
Preliminaries

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
D
be the orientation of a graph
G
on
n
vertices and let
p
be an integer with
n-2\geqslant p\geqslant 2
.
Let
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
be any four vertices of
G
. Then using four
(=p)
-inversions, we can reverse the orientation of the edges in
\{x_{0}x_{1},x_{1}x_{2},x_{2}x_{3},x_{3}x_{0}\}\cap E(G)
and none other.

Proof.

Let
X
be a set of
p-2
vertices of
V(G)\setminus\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}
. Observe that such a set exists as
n\geqslant p+2
.
Let us invert the sets
X\cup\{x_{0},x_{1}\}
,
X\cup\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
,
X\cup\{x_{2},x_{3}\}
, and
X\cup\{x_{3},x_{0}\}
.
Doing so, all arcs are reversed an even number of times except those with underlying edge in
\{x_{0}x_{1},x_{1}x_{2},x_{2}x_{3},x_{3}x_{0}\}
.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
D
be an orientation of a graph
G
on
n
vertices and and let
p
be an even integer with
n-2\geqslant p\geqslant 2
.
With
2p-2

(=p)
-inversions, we can reverse the orientation of a given pair of adjacent edges of
G
, but no other edges.

Proof.

Let
uv_{1}
and
uv_{2}
be two adjacent edges of
G
and
X
a set of
p-2
vertices in
V(G)\setminus\{u,v_{1},v_{2}\}
. Observe that such a set exists as
n\geqslant p+2
.
Invert first
X\cup\{u,v_{1}\}
and
X\cup\{u,v_{2}\}
.
This reverses the orientation of the edges
uv_{1}
,
uv_{2}
, and those of the complete bipartite graph with bipartition
(\{v_{1},v_{2}\},X)
.
Since
p
is even, the edge set of this bipartite graph can be decomposed into
(p-2)/2

4
-cycles of
G
. Hence, they can be reversed one after another by Proposition
3.3
using four inversions each.
Doing so, only the arcs
uv_{1}
and
uv_{2}
remain reversed, and the total number of inversions is
4(p-2)/2+2=2p-2
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
D
be an oriented graph on
n
vertices and and let
p
be an even integer with
n-2\geqslant p\geqslant 2
.
With
4p-4

(=p)
-inversions, we can reverse a given pair of non-adjacent arcs and no others.

Proof.

To reverse two non-adjacent arcs
u_{1}v_{1}
and
u_{2}v_{2}
,
one can use Proposition
3.4
to reverse
u_{1}v_{1}
along with
u_{1}u_{2}
and then
u_{1}u_{2}
with
u_{2}v_{2}
.
This can be done with
2(2p-2)=4p-4

(=p)
-inversions.
∎

3.2.2
Case
p=2\mod 4
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and let
p
be a positive integer such that
n\geqslant p+2

and
p=2\mod 4
.
We take
t=0
, and so
for every
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
,
\pi(\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}
, here taking the convention that
\mathbf{0}
denotes the unique empty vector over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
Concretely, (
1
) means that it is always possible to transform an
n
-vertex tournament into any other using
(=p)
-inversions.
To prove (
2
), it is enough to show that
\dim(\spn\{\mathbf{i}_{X}\mid X\subseteq[n],|X|=p\})=\binom{n}{2}
.
For every
a,b\in[n]
with
a<b
,
let
\mathbf{v}_{ab}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}

be defined by, for every
i,j\in[n]
with
i<j
,

(\mathbf{v}_{ab})_{\{i,j\}}=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if $(a,b)=(i,j)$,}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Clearly, the
\mathbf{v}_{ab}
for
a,b\in[n]
with
a<b
form a family of linearly independent vectors of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
.
We will show that they all belong to
\spn\{\mathbf{i}_{X}\mid X\subseteq[n],|X|=p\}
.
Let
a,b\in[n]
with
a<b

and let
U\subseteq[n]
be a
(p+2)
-set which contains
a,b
.
We claim that

\mathbf{v}_{ab}=\sum_{X\subseteq U;\,|X|=p;\,a,b\in X}\mathbf{i}_{X}.

Indeed, for every
i,j\in U
with
i<j
,

|\{X\subseteq U\mid|X|=p;a,b,i,j\in X\}|=\begin{cases}\binom{p+2-4}{p-4}&\textrm{if $i,j,a,b$ are pairwise distinct,}\\
\binom{p+2-3}{p-3}&\textrm{if $|\{i,j\}\cap\{a,b\}|=1$,}\\
\binom{p+2-2}{p-2}&\textrm{if $\{i,j\}=\{a,b\}$,}\end{cases}

which is odd if and only if
\{i,j\}=\{a,b\}
since
p=2\mod 4
.
This proves that
\mathbf{v}_{ab}=\sum_{X\subseteq U;\,|X|=p;\,a,b\in X}\mathbf{i}_{X}
,
and so (
2
) holds.
This proves the cases
p=2\mod 4
in
Theorem

1.1

and in
Theorem

1.3
.

3.2.3
Case
p=0\mod 4
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and let
p
be a positive integer such that
n\geqslant p+2

and
p=0\mod 4
.
Let
t=1
, and let
\pi
be defined as follows.
For every
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
, let

\pi(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{i,j\in[n],i<j}\mathbf{u}_{\{i,j\}}.

Less formally,
\pi
is defined so that for any
T
,
\pi(\mathbf{a}_{T})
is the parity of the number of backward arcs (since the operations are in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
).

First, since
p=0\mod 4
,
\binom{p}{2}
is even, and so
\pi(\mathbf{i}_{X})=0
for every
(=p)
-set
X\subseteq[n]
, meaning
\spn\{\mathbf{i}_{X}\mid X\subseteq[n],|X|=p\}\subseteq\ker(\pi)
.
Hence, since
\pi
is clearly surjective, and by the rank theorem, to prove (
2
), it is now enough to show that
\dim(\spn\{\mathbf{i}_{X}\mid X\subseteq[n],|X|=p\})\geqslant\binom{n}{2}-1
.
For every
a,b\in[n]
with
a,b
and
(a,b)\neq(1,2)
, let
\mathbf{w}_{ab}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be the vector defined by

(\mathbf{w}_{ab})_{\{i,j\}}=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if $(i,j)=(a,b)$ or $(i,j)=(1,2)$,}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for every
i,j\in[n]
with
i<j
.
By Propositions
3.4
and
3.5
,

\mathbf{w}_{ab}\in\spn\{\mathbf{i}_{X}\mid X\subseteq[n],|X|=p\}
.
For every
(\alpha_{ab})_{a,b\in[n],\,a<b,\,(a,b)\neq(1,2)}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}-1}
,
if

\sum_{a,b\in[n],a<b,(a,b)\neq(1,2)}\alpha_{ab}\mathbf{w}_{ab}=\mathbf{0},

then for every
a,b\in[n],a<b,(a,b)\neq(1,2)
, the coordinate corresponding to
ab
gives
\alpha_{ab}=0
.
It follows that
\alpha_{ab}=0
for every
a,b\in[n]
with
a<b
and
(a,b)\neq(1,2)
.
Hence the vectors
(\mathbf{w}_{ab})_{a,b\in[n],a<b,(a,b)\neq(1,2)}
are linearly independent and belong to
\spn\{\mathbf{i}_{X}\mid X\subseteq[n],|X|=p\}
.
This proves
\dim(\spn\{\mathbf{i}_{X}\mid X\subseteq[n],|X|=p\})\geqslant\binom{n}{2}-1
and so (
2
),
and concludes the proof of the case
p=0\mod 4
in
Theorem

1.1
.

Theorem 3.6

(Case
p=0\mod 4
in
Theorem

1.3
)
.

Let
p
be an integer such that
p=0\mod 4
. Every tournament of order at least
p+2
is
(=p)
-invertible.

Proof.

Let
n
be an integer such that
n\geqslant p+2
.
It is enough to show that there are two acyclic tournaments
T_{0},T_{1}
on
[n]
such that

\pi(\mathbf{a}_{T_{\varepsilon}})=\varepsilon
for each
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
We take
A(T_{0})=\{ij\mid 1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n\}

and
A(T_{1})=\{ij\mid 1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n\}\setminus\{12\}\cup\{21\}
.
∎

3.2.4
Case
p=3\mod 4
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and let
p
be a positive integer such that
n\geqslant p+2

and
p=3\mod 4
.
Let
\pi
be defined as follows.
For every
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
, let
\pi(\mathbf{u})
be the vector of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n-1}
defined as:

\pi(\mathbf{u})=\left(\textstyle\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}}\mathbf{u}_{\{i,j\}}\right)_{i\in[n-1]}.

Less formally,
\pi
is such that for any tournament
T
,
\pi(\mathbf{a}_{T})_{i}
is the parity of the number of backward arcs incident to
i
(as tail or head).
Hence

\displaystyle\pi(\mathbf{a}_{T})_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|N^{+}_{T}(i)\cap[i-1]|+|N^{-}_{T}(i)\cap\{i+1,\dots,n\}|\mod 2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|N^{+}_{T}(i)\cap[i-1]|+n-i-|N^{+}_{T}(i)\cap\{i+1,\dots,n\}|\mod 2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n+i+d^{+}_{T}(i)\mod 2

for every
i\in[n-1]
, which is indeed invariant under

(=p)
-inversions since
p
is odd.
From the algebraic point of view, this means that for every
(=p)
-set
X\subseteq[n]
,
for every
i\in[n-1]
,
\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}}(\mathbf{i}_{X})_{\{i,j\}}=0
.
Therefore,
\pi(\mathbf{i}_{X})=\mathbf{0}
for every
X\subseteq[n]
with
|X|=p
.
Thus, by the rank theorem, to prove (
2
), it is now enough to show that

1.

\pi
is surjective, and

2.

\dim(\spn\{\mathbf{i}_{X}\mid X\subseteq[n],|X|=p\})\geqslant\binom{n}{2}-n+1=\binom{n-1}{2}
.

First we show that
\pi
is surjective.
For every
a\in[n]\setminus\{1\}
,
let
\mathbf{y}_{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
be defined by

(\mathbf{y}_{a})_{\{i,j\}}=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if $(i,j)=(1,a)$,}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for every
i,j\in[n]
with
i<j
.
Then the matrix whose columns are
\pi(\mathbf{y}_{a})
for
a\in[n]\setminus\{1\}

is, up to a permutation of the columns, the identity matrix.
We conclude that
\pi
is surjective.

We now show that
\dim(\spn\{\mathbf{i}_{X}\mid X\subseteq[n],|X|=p\})\geqslant\binom{n-1}{2}
.
Let
a,b\in[n]\setminus\{1\}
with
a<b
.
Let
\mathbf{z}_{ab}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
be defined
by

(\mathbf{z}_{ab})_{\{i,j\}}=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if $(i,j)\in\{(1,2),(2,a),(a,b),(b,1)\}$,}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for every
i,j\in[n]
with
i<j
.
First, if
a>2
, then
\{a,b\}\cap\{1,2\}=\emptyset
, and so

\mathbf{z}_{ab}\in\spn\{\mathbf{i}_{X}\mid X\subseteq[n],|X|=p\}
by Proposition
3.3
.
Suppose now that
a=2
.
Let
U\subseteq[n]
be a set of size
p+2
containing
1,2,b
. Observe that such a set exists as
n\geqslant p+2
.
We claim that

\mathbf{z}_{ab}=\sum_{X\subseteq U;|X|=p;1,2,b\in X}\mathbf{i}_{X}.

Indeed, for every
i,j\in U
with
i<j
,

|\{X\subseteq U\mid|X|=p;1,2,b,i,j\in X\}|=\begin{cases}\binom{p+2-5}{p-5}&\textrm{if $\{i,j\}\cap\{1,2,b\}=\emptyset$,}\\
\binom{p+2-4}{p-4}&\textrm{if $|\{i,j\}\cap\{1,2,b\}|=1$,}\\
\binom{p+2-3}{p-3}&\textrm{if $\{i,j\}\subseteq\{1,2,b\}$,}\end{cases}

which is odd if and only if
\{i,j\}\subseteq\{1,2,b\}
since
p=3\mod 4
.
It remains to show that
\mathbf{z}_{ab}
for
a,b\in[n]\setminus\{1\}
with
a<b

are linearly independent.
Suppose that
\alpha_{ab}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
for
a,b\in[n]\setminus\{1\},a<b

are such that

\sum_{a,b\in[n]\setminus\{1\},a<b}\alpha_{ab}\mathbf{z}_{ab}=\mathbf{0}.

First, for every
a,b\in[n]\setminus\{1,2\}
with
a<b
,
the coordinate corresponding to
\{a,b\}
implies that
\alpha_{ab}=0
.
Hence,
\sum_{b\in[n]\setminus\{1,2\}}\alpha_{2b}\mathbf{z}_{2b}=\mathbf{0}
.
Now, for every
b\in[n]\setminus\{1,2\}
,
the coordinate corresponding to
\{2,b\}
implies that
\alpha_{2b}=0
.
This proves that the vectors
\mathbf{z}_{ab}
for
a,b\in[n]\setminus\{1\}
with
a<b

are linearly independent,
and concludes the proof of (
2
).
This shows the case
p=3\mod 4
in
Theorem

1.1

A vertex in a digraph
D
is
out-even
(resp.
out-odd
) if its out-degree in
D
is even (resp. odd).

Theorem 3.7

(Case
p=3\mod 4
in
Theorem

1.3
)
.

Let
p
be a positive integer with
p=3\mod 4
.
For every tournament
T
on
n\geqslant p+2
vertices,

T
is
(=p)
-invertible if and only if it has
\lceil n/2\rceil
out-even vertices.

Proof.

For every
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
,
let
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be defined by

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})_{i}

\displaystyle=\pi(\mathbf{u})_{i}+n+i\mod 2,

for every
i\in[n-1]
, and

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})_{n}

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{2}+\sum_{i\in[n-1]}\pi(\mathbf{u})_{i}\mod 2.

Observe that for every tournament
T
on
[n]
,
for every
i\in[n]
,
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{a}_{T})_{i}=d^{+}_{T}(i)\mod 2
. Indeed, for every
i\in[n-1]
,

\displaystyle d^{+}_{T}(i)

\displaystyle=|\{j\in[i-1]\mid ij\in A(T)\}|+|\{j\in\{i+1,\dots,n\}\mid ij\in A(T)\}|

\displaystyle=|\{j\in[i-1]\mid ij\in A(T)\}|+(n-i)-|\{j\in\{i+1,\dots,n\}\mid ji\in A(T)\}|

\displaystyle=n-1+|\{j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}\mid\text{$ij\in A(T)$ and $j<i$, or $ji\in A(T)$ and $j>i$}\}|

\displaystyle=n+i+\pi(\mathbf{a}_{T})_{i}\mod 2.

For
i=n
, the equality
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{a}_{T})_{i}=d^{+}_{T}(i)\mod 2
then follows
from the fact that
\sum_{i\in[n]}|\{j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}\mid\text{$ij\in A(T)$ and $j<i$, or $j>i$ and $ji\in A(T)$}\}|=\binom{n}{2}

and
d^{+}_{T}(n)=|\{j\in[n-1]\mid\text{$ij\in A(T)$ and $j<i$, or $ji\in A(T)$ and $j>i$}\}|
.
This proves that, for every
i\in[n]
,

\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{a}_{T})_{i}=d^{+}_{T}(i)\mod 2
, and so
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{a}_{T})_{i}=0
if vertex
i
is out-even and
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{a}_{T})_{i}=1
if vertex
i
is out-odd.
Moreover, for every
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\binom{n}{2}}
,

\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{u})=\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})
if and only if
\pi(\mathbf{u})=\pi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})
.
Hence, it remains to show that for every
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n-1}
,
there exists an acyclic tournament
T
on
[n]
such that
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{a}_{T})=\mathbf{v}

if and only if the number of zeros in
\mathbf{v}
is
\lceil n/2\rceil
.

First, for every transitive tournament
T
,
the number of vertices of even out-degree is
\lceil n/2\rceil
,
and so the number of zeros in
\mathbf{a}_{T}
is
\lceil n/2\rceil
.
Conversely,
for every
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n-1}
with
\lceil n/2\rceil
zeros,
let
\sigma
be a permutation of
[n]

such that
for every
i\in[n]
,

\mathbf{v}_{\sigma(i)}=1
if and only if
n+i
is odd.
Then,
T=([n],\{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)\mid 1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n\})
is an acyclic tournament
with
\pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{a}_{T})=\mathbf{v}
.
This proves the corollary.
∎

3.2.5
Case
p=1\mod 4

t(K_{0})<\frac{1}{2}(u(K_{0})-\ell(K_{0}))

\ell(K_{0})=u(K_{0})\mod{2}
, we obtain that
t(K_{0})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}(u(K_{0})-\ell(K_{0}))-1
. We now define
t^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
by
t^{\prime}(K_{0})=t(K_{0})+1
and
t^{\prime}(K)=t(K)
for all
K\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{K_{0}\}
. As
t(K_{0})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}(u(K_{0})-\ell(K_{0}))-1
, we obtain that
0\leqslant t^{\prime}(K)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}(u(K)-\ell(K))
holds for all
K\in\mathcal{K}
. Finally, as
\sum_{K\in\mathcal{K}}\ell(K)+2t(K)\leqslant s-2
, we obtain that
\sum_{K\in\mathcal{K}}\ell(K)+2t(K)<\sum_{K\in\mathcal{K}}\ell(K)+2t^{\prime}(K)\leqslant s
. This contradicts the choice of
t
.
∎

In the following, we take any
t
given by the claim above.
For every
K\in\mathcal{K}
, we clearly have
\ell(K)+2t(K)\geqslant\ell(K)\geqslant 0
and
\ell(K)+2t(K)\leqslant u(K)\leqslant|V(K)|
. Further, we have
\ell(K)+2t(K)=\ell(K)=|V_{1}\cap V(K)|+|E(K)|\mod{2}
. It follows by Proposition
3.12
that there exists an orientation
\overrightarrow{K}
of
K
with
\gamma_{\overrightarrow{K}}(V_{1}\cap V(K),V_{2}\cap V(K))=\ell(K)+2t(K)
. Now let
\overrightarrow{G}
be the orientation of
G
in which every
e\in E(K)
has the orientation it has in
\overrightarrow{K}
for every
e\in E(K)
and every
K\in\mathcal{K}
. We obtain that
\gamma_{\overrightarrow{G}}(V_{1},V_{2})=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{K}}\gamma_{\overrightarrow{K}}(V_{1}\cap V(K),V_{2}\cap V(K))=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{K}}\ell(K)+2t(K)=s
.
∎

We are now ready to give the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

If
p\geqslant n
, then
D
is
(=p)
-invertible if and only if it is acyclic, which can be checked in polynomial time. Now suppose
p\leqslant n-2
.
If
p
is even, then the statement directly follows from Theorem
1.5
.
Now suppose that
p
is odd.
Let
G
be the complement of

,

V_{1}=\{v\in V(D)\mid d_{D}^{+}(v)\text{ is even}\}
and
V_{2}=V(D)\setminus V_{1}
.
Using Lemma

, in polynomial time, one can decide whether

\overrightarrow{G}
with
\gamma_{\overrightarrow{G}}(V_{1},V_{2})=\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
.
If no such orientation, exists, we obtain from Lemma

that

(=p)
-invertible.
Otherwise, we obtain from Lemma

(=p)

∎

4
Bounds on the
(=p)
-inversion number

Once we know whether an oriented graph
D
is
(=p)
-invertible or not, two different questions arise.
The first one is the following.
If
D
is not
(=p)
-invertible, how close to an acyclic oriented graph can it be made ?
Of course, for this question, we need a measure of closeness to acyclicity.
A natural one is the feedback-arc-set number.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
p
be an odd integer such that
p\geqslant 3
.
Every oriented graph of order
n\geqslant p+2
can be transformed
using
(=p)
-inversions in an oriented graph with feedback-arc-set number at most
\frac{1}{2}\lceil n/2\rceil
.

Proof.

Let
D
be an oriented graph of order
n
.
Let
T
be a tournament of order
n
such that
D
is an oriented subgraph of
T
.
Since an arc-reversal changes the parity of the out-degree of exactly two vertices, one could reverse a set
R
of at most
\frac{1}{2}\lceil n/2\rceil
arcs in
T
to obtain a tournament
T^{\prime}
having exactly
\lceil n/2\rceil
out-even vertices.
Now, by Corollaries
3.7
and
3.8
,
T^{\prime}
is
(=p)
-invertible, so there is family
\cal X
of
(=p)
-sets such that

\Inv(T^{\prime};{\cal X})
is acyclic. Then
R
is a feedback arc-set of
\Inv(T;{\cal X})
, and
R\cap A(D)
a feedback arc-set of
\Inv(D;{\cal X})
.
∎

The above proposition is tight. Indeed, for some odd positive integer
k
, consider a
k
-diregular tournament
T
, that is
d_{T}^{-}(v)=d_{T}^{+}(v)=k
holds for every vertex
v\in V(T)
.
Its number of vertices is
n=2k+1
.
Let
T^{\prime}
be a tournament obtained from
T
using
(=p)
-inversions for some odd
p
.
Since
T
has no out-even vertices, neither does
T^{\prime}
.
Now a transitive tournament on
n
vertices has
\lceil n/2\rceil
out-even vertices, and an arc-reversal changes the parity of the out-degree of two vertices.
Thus
\fas(T^{\prime})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\lceil n/2\rceil
.

The second question is the following.
If
D
is
(=p)
-invertible, what is the
(=p)
-inversion number of
D
?
We begin in
Section

4.1
by bounding the
(=p)
-inversion number by the number of arcs.
Then, in
Section

4.2
, we give bounds for even
p
, in terms of the feedback arc set number and the number of vertices.

4.1
First bound in terms of the size

The algebraic point of view used in Subsection
3.2
, and in particular Equation (
2
), has an immediate consequence on the number of
(=p)
-inversions to go from an oriented graph to another (when it is possible):

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G
be a graph,
and let
\overrightarrow{G}_{1},\overrightarrow{G}_{2}
be two orientations of
G
.
For every family
X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}\subseteq V(G)
such that
\overrightarrow{G}_{1}=\Inv(\overrightarrow{G}_{2};X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell})
,
there exists
X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}}\in\{X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}\}
such that
\overrightarrow{G}_{1}=\Inv(\overrightarrow{G}_{2};X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}})
and

\ell^{\prime}\leqslant|E(G)|.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E(G)}
be defined by

\mathbf{a}_{e}=\begin{cases}0&\textrm{if $e$ has the same orientation in $\overrightarrow{G}_{1}$ and $\overrightarrow{G}_{2}$}\\
1&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{cases}

for every
e\in E(G)
.
Moreover, for every
X\subseteq V(G)
, let
\mathbf{i}_{X}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E(G)}
be defined
by

(\mathbf{i}_{X})_{uv}=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{if $u,v\in X$}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{cases}

for every
uv\in E(G)
.
Then we have, for every
X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}\subseteq V(G)
,

\mathbf{a}=\sum_{j\in[\ell]}\mathbf{i}_{X_{j}}\Leftrightarrow\overrightarrow{G}_{1}=\Inv(\overrightarrow{G}_{2};X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}).

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}\subseteq V(G)

such that
\overrightarrow{G}_{1}=\Inv(\overrightarrow{G}_{2};X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell})
.
Consider
X_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}}\in\{X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}\}
such that
\overrightarrow{G}_{1}=\Inv(\overrightarrow{G}_{2};X^{\prime}_{1},\dots,X^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}})
and
\ell^{\prime}
is minimal.
We claim that
\ell^{\prime}\leqslant|E(G)|
.
Suppose for contradiction that
\ell^{\prime}>|E(G)|
.
Since
\ell^{\prime}>|E(G)|=\dim\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E(G)}
, the family
(\mathbf{i}_{X_{j}})_{j}
is dependent over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{E(G)}
, so
there exists
J\subseteq[\ell^{\prime}]
nonempty such that
\sum_{j\in J}\mathbf{i}_{X^{\prime}_{j}}=\mathbf{0}
.
It follows that

\mathbf{a}=\sum_{j\in[\ell^{\prime}]\setminus J}\mathbf{i}_{X^{\prime}_{j}},

and so
\overrightarrow{G}_{1}=\Inv(\overrightarrow{G}_{2};(X^{\prime}_{j})_{j\in[\ell^{\prime}]\setminus J})
, contradicting the minimality of
\ell^{\prime}
.
∎

This theorem directly implies Theorem
1.6
stating that

\inv^{=p}(D)\leqslant|A(D)|
for every
(=p)
-invertible oriented graph
D
.

4.2.1
Bound in terms of the feedback-arc-set number and its consequences

The aim of this subsection is to prove the upper bound (i) of Theorem
1.7
, which is more precisely restated in Theorem
4.3
, and its consequences.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
p\geqslant 4
be an even integer and let
D
be an oriented graph with at least
p+2
vertices. Then,

\inv^{=p}(D)\leqslant(2p-2)(\fas(D)+1)

Proof.

Let
D_{1}
be the acyclic oriented graph obtained by reversing every arc of a minimum feedback arc set of
D
and let
D_{2}
be an acyclic oriented graph obtained from
D_{1}
by reversing exactly one arc.
Then
D
disagrees with one of
D_{1}
,
D_{2}
in a set
E^{\prime}
of arcs such that
|E^{\prime}|
is even and
|E^{\prime}|\leqslant\fas(D)+1
.

By Propositions
3.4
and
3.5
,
one can reverse the arcs of
|E^{\prime}|
two by two using at most
(4p-4)\frac{|E^{\prime}|}{2}

(=p)
-inversions. Thus
\inv^{=p}(D)\leqslant(2p-2)(\fas(D)+1)
.
∎

A consequence of Theorem
4.3
is that, when
p
is even,

\inv^{=p}(n)
and
\inv^{\leqslant p}(n)
are close to each other.

See
1.8

Proof.

Let
n
be an integer with
n\geqslant p+2
.
The lower bound
\inv^{=p}(n)\geqslant\inv^{\leqslant p}(n)
is clear.
For the upper bound, consider
a tournament
T
on
n
vertices,
and let
A
be a set of
p
vertices in
T
.
Consider a decycling
(\leqslant p)
-family
\mathcal{X}
of
T-A
of size at most
\inv^{\leqslant p}(|V(T-A)|)\leqslant\inv^{\leqslant p}(n)
.
For every
X\in\mathcal{X}
, consider the
(=p)
-set obtained from
X
by adding
p-|X|
vertices of
A
,
and let
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
be the family of all these sets.
Observe that
\Inv(T,\mathcal{X}^{\prime})-A=\Inv(T-A,\mathcal{X})
, which is acyclic.
Hence,
\fas(\Inv(T,\mathcal{X}^{\prime}))\leqslant\binom{p}{2}+(n-p)p
.
It follows from Theorem
4.3
that
\inv^{=p}(\Inv(T;\mathcal{X}^{\prime}))\leqslant(2p-2)\left(\binom{p}{2}+(n-p)p+1\right)=\bigO(n)
,
and so
\inv^{=p}(T)\leqslant|\mathcal{X}^{\prime}|+\inv^{=p}(\Inv(T;\mathcal{X}^{\prime}))\leqslant\inv^{\leqslant p}(n)+\bigO(n)
.
∎

Another consequence of Theorem
4.3
is that, for a fixed even positive integer
p
and an oriented graph
D
on at least
p+2
vertices,

\inv^{=p}(D)
and
\inv^{\leqslant p}(D)
are linearly tied.

See
1.9

Proof.

The first inequality clearly holds. Moreover, if
\fas(D)=0
, then the second inequality also trivially satisfied. We may hence suppose that
\fas(D)\geqslant 1
. Note that
\inv^{\leqslant p}(D)\geqslant\frac{\fas({D})}{\binom{p}{2}}
, and using this we argue as follows.

\displaystyle\inv^{\leqslant p}(D)(4p-4)\binom{p}{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant(4p-4)\binom{p}{2}\frac{\fas({D})}{\binom{p}{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant(4p-4)\fas(D)

\displaystyle\geqslant(4p-4)\frac{\fas(D)+1}{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\inv^{=p}(D)

\displaystyle\textrm{by Theorem~\ref{thm:ub-fas}}.\qed

A third consequence of Theorem
4.3
is the following bound which, for even
p
and dense digraphs, is better than the one of Theorem
1.6
.

See
1.10

This theorem is an immediate consequence of Theorem
4.3
and the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
p
be an integer greater than
1
, and let
D
be an oriented graph of order
n
.
In at most
|A(D)|/(p-1)

(=p)
-inversions, we can transform
D
into an oriented graph
D^{\prime}
with
\fas(D^{\prime})\leqslant(p-2)n-\frac{3}{4}p^{2}+\frac{7p}{4}
.

Proof.

Let
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
be an ordering of the vertices of
D
and set
D=D_{1}
. For
k=1,\dots,n-p
, we do the following.

Let
B_{k}
be the set of backwards arcs with head
v_{k}
in
D_{k}
.
Let
u_{1},\dots,u_{t}
be the tails of the arcs in
B_{k}
. (Note that
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{t}\}\subseteq\{v_{k+1},\dots,v_{n}\}
.)
Set
s_{k}=\lfloor t/(p-1)\rfloor
, and for
i=1,\dots,s_{k}
, let
X_{i}=\{v_{k}\}\cup\{u_{j}\mid(i-1)(p-1)+1\leqslant j\leqslant i(p-1)\}
.
We then invert all the
X_{i}
(which are all
p
-sets) to obtain
D_{k+1}
.

Observe that for each
k\in[n-p]
, at step
k
, we reversed all backward arcs with head
v_{k}
except possibly
t-s_{k}(p-1)\leqslant p-2
. Moreover
v_{k}
is in no inversions in further steps.
So in
D^{\prime}
, there are at most
p-2
backward arcs with head
v_{k}
.
Thus in
D^{\prime}
, there are at most
(p-2)(n-p)
backward arcs with head in
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n-p}\}
.

Now consider a median order
(w_{n-p+1},\dots,w_{n})
of
D_{n-p+1}\langle\{v_{n-p+1},\dots,v_{n}\}\rangle
. It has at most
\frac{1}{2}\binom{p}{2}
backward arcs.
Thus, considering the number of backward arcs in
D^{\prime}
for the ordering
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n-p},w_{n-p+1},\dots,w_{n})
,
we obtain:

\displaystyle\fas(D^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leqslant(p-2)(n-p)+\frac{1}{2}\binom{p}{2}

\displaystyle=(p-2)n-\frac{3}{4}p^{2}+\frac{7}{4}p.

The number of inversions made to go from
D
to
D^{\prime}
is

\sum_{k=1}^{n-p}s_{k}\leqslant 1+\sum_{k=1}^{n-p}|B_{k}|/(p-1)
.
Now each arc in
B_{k}
is incident to
v_{k}
and a vertex of
\{v_{k+1},\dots,v_{n}\}
, so
\sum_{k=1}^{n-p}|B_{k}|\leqslant|A(D)|
.
So the number of inversions is at most
|A(D)|/(p-1)
.
∎

4.2.2
Improved bounds for dense oriented graphs

The aim of this subsection is to prove the upper bounds (ii) and (iii) of Theorem
1.7
.

Let us first prove the upper bound (ii). We need some preliminary result. The first one an easy and well known proposition. Its proof follows directly from Exercise 10.1 of Lovasz’ book
[
30
]
which states that every graph with more than
3(n-1)/2
edges has two vertices joined by three independent paths.

Proposition 4.5
.

A graph of order
n\geqslant 1
with no even cycles has at most
\big\lfloor\frac{3(n-1)}{2}\big\rfloor
edges.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
p\geqslant 3
and
\ell\geqslant 2
be two integers, let
D
be an orientation of a graph
G
of order at least
p+2
, let
x_{0},\dots,x_{2\ell-1}
be
2\ell
distinct vertices of
G
and let
C=(x_{0},\dots,x_{2\ell-1},x_{0})
be a cycle (not necessarily contained in
G
).
With
4\ell

(=p)
-inversions, we can reverse the orientation of the edges in
E(C)\cap E(G)
but no other edges.

Proof.

For each
i\in[\ell]
, let
C_{i}
be the cycle
(x_{i},x_{i+1},x_{i+\ell+1},x_{i+\ell},x_{i})
with indices modulo
2\ell
.
By Proposition
3.3
, one can reverse the orientation of the edges of each
E(C_{i})\cap E(G)
but no others using four
(=p)
-inversions. Doing so for each
i
, every edge of type
x_{i+\ell}x_{i}
has its orientation reversed twice and every edge of
E(C)\cap E(G)
has its orientation reversed exactly once.
Thus, those
4\ell
inversions reverse the orientation of the edges in
E(C)\cap E(G)
but no other edges.
∎

We are now ready to prove the following more precise version of the upper bound (ii) of Theorem
1.7
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
p
be an even integer greater than
2
,
k\geqslant 1
an integer and
D
an oriented graph of order
n\geqslant p+2
.
Then
\inv^{=p}(D)\leqslant 2\fas(D)+2pn
.

Proof.

Let
D_{1}
be the acyclic oriented graph obtained by reversing every arc of a minimum feedback arc set of
D
and let
D_{2}
be an acyclic oriented graph obtained from
D_{1}
by reversing exactly one arc.
Then there exists an acyclic oriented graph
D^{*}\in\{D_{1},D_{2}\}
that disagrees with
D
in a set
F^{\prime}
of arcs such that
|F^{\prime}|
is even and
|F^{\prime}|\leqslant\fas(D)+1
.

Let
F^{\prime}_{1}
be a maximal subset of
F^{\prime}
which is forms the union of arc-disjoint even cycles.
By Proposition
4.5
,
|F^{\prime}\setminus F^{\prime}_{1}|\leqslant\lfloor\frac{3(n-1)}{2}\rfloor
.
Let
P_{2}
be a maximum set of disjoint pairs of adjacent arcs in
F^{\prime}\setminus F^{\prime}_{1}
, let
F^{\prime}_{2}
be the set of arcs in pairs of
P_{2}
, and
F^{\prime}_{3}=F^{\prime}\setminus(F^{\prime}_{1}\cup F^{\prime}_{2})
.
By our choice of
P_{2}
,
F^{\prime}_{3}
is a matching so
|F^{\prime}_{3}|\leqslant n/2
. Moreover
|F^{\prime}_{3}|
is even because
F^{\prime}
was even and
F^{\prime}_{1}
and
F^{\prime}_{2}
are also even by definition.
We first reverse the arcs of
F^{\prime}_{1}
using
4\ell

(=p)
-inversions per
2\ell
-cycle by Proposition
4.6
,
which gives
2|F^{\prime}_{1}|
inversions.
We then reverse the arcs of
F^{\prime}_{2}
using
2p-2

(=p)
-inversions per pair of adjacent edges by Proposition
3.4
, which can be averaged as
p-1

(=p)
-inversions per arc.
Finally, we reverse the arcs of
F^{\prime}_{3}
using
4p-4

(=p)
-inversions per pair of non-adjacent arcs by Proposition
3.5
, so in average
2p-2

(=p)
-inversions per arc.
Hence
D
can be transformed into the acyclic oriented graph
D^{*}
using

2|F^{\prime}_{1}|+(p-1)|F^{\prime}_{2}|+(2p-2)|F^{\prime}_{3}|

(=p)
-inversions.
Since
|F^{\prime}_{2}\cup F^{\prime}_{3}|\leqslant\frac{3(n-1)}{2}
, and
|F^{\prime}_{3}|\leqslant n/2
, we get

\displaystyle\inv^{=p}(D)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2|F^{\prime}_{1}|+(p-1)|F^{\prime}_{2}|+(2p-2)|F^{\prime}_{3}|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2|F^{\prime}|+(p-3)|F^{\prime}_{2}|+(2p-4)|F^{\prime}_{3}|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\fas(D)+2+(p-3)|F^{\prime}_{2}\cup F^{\prime}_{3}|+(p-1)|F^{\prime}_{3}|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\fas(D)+2+(p-3)\frac{3(n-1)}{2}+(p-1)\frac{n}{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\fas(D)+(2p-5)n-\frac{3}{2}p+\frac{13}{2}.

∎

be a positive integer, and let

be a graph with

.
There exists an integer
\ell
with
\ell\leqslant\frac{|E(G)|}{\lceil p/2\rceil\cdot\lfloor p/2\rfloor}
,
and a family

of

-sets such that

The proof relies on the Kővári-Sós-Turán Theorem.

Theorem 4.12

(Kővári, Sós, and Turán
[
26
]
)
.

Let

be positive integers with

,
and let

be a graph on
n

If
|E(G)|>4s^{\frac{1}{t}}n^{2-\frac{1}{t}}
, then

is a subgraph of

.

Proof of
Theorem

4.11
.

Let
s=2\lfloor p/2\rfloor
,
t=2\lceil p/2\rceil
,

\varepsilon_{p}=\frac{1}{t}
, and
\alpha_{p}=4s^{\frac{1}{t}}
.
We prove the result by induction on
|E(G)|
, the result holding trivially if
|E(G)|\leqslant\alpha_{p}n^{2-\frac{1}{t}}
.

Now assume
|E(G)|>\alpha_{p}n^{2-\frac{1}{t}}
.
By Theorem
4.12
, there is a subgraph
H
of
G
isomorphic to
K_{s,t}
.
Let
(B,C)
be the bipartition of

with
|B|=s
and
|C|=t
.
Let
(B_{1},B_{2})
(resp.
(A_{1},A_{2})
) be an arbitrary partition of

(resp.

) into two sets of size
\lfloor p/2\rfloor
(resp.
\lceil p/2\rceil
),
and let
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})=(B_{1}\cup C_{1},B_{1}\cup C_{2},B_{2}\cup c_{1},B_{2}\cup C_{2})

Observe that
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4}
all have size

,
and
E(G)\Delta\binom{X_{1}}{2}\Delta\binom{X_{2}}{2}\Delta\binom{X_{3}}{2}\Delta\binom{X_{4}}{2}=E(G)\setminus E(H)
.
Then, by the induction hypothesis applied to
E(G)\setminus E(H)
,
there exists
X_{5},\dots,X_{\ell}\subseteq[n]
with
\ell-4\leqslant\frac{|E(G)|-st}{\lfloor p/2\rfloor\lceil p/2\rceil}

such that

\left|E(G)\Delta\binom{X_{1}}{2}\Delta\dots\Delta\binom{X_{\ell}}{2}\right|=\left|(E(G)\setminus E(H))\Delta\binom{X_{5}}{2}\Delta\dots\Delta\binom{X_{\ell}}{2}\right|\leqslant\alpha_{p}\cdot n^{2-\varepsilon_{p}}.

Since

, we deduce that
\ell\leqslant\frac{|E(G)|}{\lfloor p/2\rfloor\lceil p/2\rceil}
,
and so

is as claimed.
∎

Theorem 4.13
.

Let

A natural question is whether the results established in the previous subsection for even values of

Theorem 4.15
.

Let

For every positive integer

(so

Note that for any even

or

yields that

(=p)
-invertible.
Towards a contradiction,

Then, consider the first such

Thus

.
∎

Since

5
Complexity of computing the
(=p)
-inversion and
(\leqslant p)
-inversion numbers

5.1
NP-completeness of
Tournament
(=p)
-Inversion
and
Tournament
(\leqslant p)
-Inversion

Given a tournament
T
and an integer
k
, recall that deciding whether
T
has a feedback arc set of size at most
k
is NP-complete
[
2
,
10
]
.
This means that
Tournament
(=p)
-Inversion
and
Tournament
(\leqslant p)
-Inversion
are NP-complete when
p=2
.
We show this generalizes to any
p
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Tournament
(=p)
-Inversion
and
Tournament
(\leqslant p)
-Inversion
are NP-complete, for any fixed integer
p
greater than
2
.

Our reduction will be from the hypergraph edge-colourability problem.
A
hypergraph

\mathcal{H}
consists of a vertex set
V(\mathcal{H})
and a multiset
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H})
of subsets of
V(\mathcal{H})
, called the
hyperedges
of
\mathcal{H}
. For some nonnegative integer
k
, we say that
\mathcal{H}
is
k
-uniform
if
|e|=k
holds for every
e\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H})
and
k
-regular
if every
v\in V(\mathcal{H})
is contained in exactly
k
hyperedges.
For some positive integer
k
, a
k
-edge-colouring
of a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
is a mapping
\phi\colon E(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow[k]
. If
\phi(e)\neq\phi(e^{\prime})
holds for each pair of distinct hyperedges
e,e^{\prime}
with
e\cap e^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
, then we say that
\phi
is a
proper

k
-edge-colouring. We say that a hypergraph is
k
-edge-colourable
if it admits a proper
k
-edge-colouring.

A hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
is
p
-special
if it satisfies the following four properties:

(S1)

\mathcal{H}
is
p
-uniform;

(S2)

\mathcal{H}
is
3
-regular;

(S3)

|e\cap e^{\prime}|\leqslant 1
for all distinct hyperedges
e,e^{\prime}\in E(\mathcal{H})
;

(S4)

for all triples
(v,v^{\prime},v^{\prime\prime})
of distinct vertices, if each of
\{v,v^{\prime}\},\{v,v^{\prime\prime}\}
, and
\{v^{\prime},v^{\prime\prime}\}
is contained in a hyperedge of
\mathcal{H}
, then
\{v,v^{\prime},v^{\prime\prime}\}
is contained in a hyperedge of
\mathcal{H}
.

We denote by
p
-Special Hypergraph 3-Edge-Colourability
, in short
(p,3)
-SHEC, the problem of deciding whether a given
p
-special hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
is 3-edge-colourable.

The hardness of
(p,3)
-SHEC will be crucial for our reduction proving Theorem
5.1
. Note that the problem
(2,3)
-SHEC is exactly the problem of deciding whether a cubic, triangle-free graph is 3-edge-colourable. This problem is well known to be NP-hard. (See for example Theorem 4 in
[
9
]
.)

Proposition 5.2
.

(2,3)
-SHEC is NP-hard.

In the following, we give a slightly technical proof for the fact that this hardness result generalizes for arbitrary
p\geqslant 3
.

Lemma 5.3
.

(p,3)
-SHEC is NP-hard for every
p\geqslant 2
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.2
, the statement holds for
p=2
. Now consider an integer
p\geqslant 3
. Inductively, we may suppose that
(p-1,3)
-SHEC is NP-hard. We will show the hardness of
(p,3)
-SHEC by a reduction from
(p-1,3)
-SHEC.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a
(p-1)
-special hypergraph. Let us create a particular hypergraph
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. For every
v\in V(\mathcal{H})
and every
(i,j)\in[p]^{2}
, we let
V(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
contain a vertex
v_{i,j}
.
Next, for every
e\in E(\mathcal{H})
and every
(i,j)\in[p]^{2}
, we let
V(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
contain a vertex
x_{i,j}^{e}
. For every
e\in E(\mathcal{H})
and every
(i,j)\in[p]^{2}
, we let
E(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
contain a hyperedge
e_{i,j}
which consists of
\{v_{i,j}\mid v\in e\}
and
x_{i,j}^{e}
.
Further, for every
e\in E(\mathcal{H})
and every
i\in[p]
, we let
E(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
contain a hyperedge
f_{e,i}
consisting of
\{x_{i,j}^{e}\mid j\in[p]\}
. Finally, for every
e\in E(\mathcal{H})
and every
j\in[p]
, we let
E(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
contain a hyperedge
f^{\prime}_{e,j}
consisting of
\{x_{i,j}^{e}\mid i\in[p]\}
. This finishes the description of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
.
Observe that
|V(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})|=p^{2}(|V(\mathcal{H})|+|E(\mathcal{H})|)
and, as
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is 3-regular,
|E(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})|\leqslant 3|V(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})|
.
Hence the size of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is polynomial in the size of
\mathcal{H}
.

Claim 5.3.1
.

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is
p
-special.

Proof of the claim.

We need to prove that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
satisfies (S1), (S2), (S3) and (S4).

It follows immediately from the fact that
\mathcal{H}
is
(p-1)
-uniform and by construction that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
satisfies (S1).

Similarly, as
\mathcal{H}
is 3-regular, is easy to see that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is 3-regular, so
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
satisfies (S2).

We next prove (S3). Let
g
,

be distinct hyperedges in

. First suppose that
g=f_{e,i}
for some
e\in E(\mathcal{H})
and some
i\in[p]
. If
g^{\prime}=f_{e^{\prime},j}
for some

and

, we obtain that
(e,i)\neq(e^{\prime},j)
by assumption. It follows by construction that
g\cap g^{\prime}=\emptyset
. Next suppose that
g^{\prime}=f^{\prime}_{e^{\prime},j}
for some

and some

. We obtain by construction that
g\cap g^{\prime}=\{x_{i,j}^{e}\}
if

and
g\cap g^{\prime}=\emptyset
, otherwise. Now suppose that
g^{\prime}=e^{\prime}_{i^{\prime},j}
for some

and
(i^{\prime},j)\in[p]^{2}
. It follows by construction that

if

, and
g\cap g^{\prime}=\emptyset
, otherwise. The case of
g=f^{\prime}_{e,j}
for some

and some

can be handled similarly. We may hence suppose that
g=e_{i,j}
for some

and
(i,j)\in[p]^{2}
. Using similar arguments, we may suppose that

. As
\mathcal{H}
is

-special, we have

. As

for some

. Similarly, we have that either

for some
