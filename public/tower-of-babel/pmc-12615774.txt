Boosting the brightness of aggregation-caused quenching chromophore-based covalent organic frameworks via energy level matching strategy
Authors: Liang Zhang, Weihang Geng, Xinwen Ou, Shan He, Xiao-Hang He, Wenlang Li, Yantian Jiao, Ziyi Cheng, Ziyi Jin, Jun Qian, Ben Zhong Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost chromophores exhibit an aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ) effect, resulting in minimal or weak emission when incorporated into covalent organic frameworks (COFs). Consequently, the development of luminescent COFs, particularly those utilizing ACQ chromophores, presents both a compelling and formidable challenge. Herein, we report the construction of highly emissive ACQ chromophore-based COFs by an energy level matching strategy. This approach effectively modulates the electronic structures within the COFs, thereby suppressing intralayer charge transfer to ACQ chromophores, such as the imine linkage and dibenzo[g,p]chrysene unit, while simultaneously inhibiting interlayer charge transfer and non-radiative decay pathways. As a result, the undesired ACQ effect is mitigated, enabling the realization of highly emissive COFs. Notably, COF-BT-PhDBC demonstrates high brightness with a solid-state photoluminescence quantum yield of 14.7%, standing among the best ACQ chromophore-based COFs. Furthermore, COF-BT-PhDBC has been successfully utilized for in vivo three-photon fluorescence imaging of murine brain vasculature, achieving an impressive imaging depth of approximately 800 µm.Subject terms: Metal-organic frameworks, Two-dimensional materials, Supramolecular polymers

Introduction

The optical properties of two-dimensional materials are determined by the combined effects of both intralayer and interlayer charge transfer between chromophores upon light excitation1–5. The challenge is that such charge transfer is highly sensitive to the chemical functionality, connectivity, and stacking modes of these chromophores, thus frustrating attempts to optimize the intralayer and interlayer charge transfer properties6–8. Two-dimensional covalent organic frameworks (2D COFs) have emerged as a promising category of organic porous materials9–15. However, the exploration of luminescent 2D COFs, especially aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ) chromophore-based 2D COF, remains limited16,17. The achievement of desirable bright fluorescence COF materials requires careful consideration of two important factors. Firstly, it is crucial to ensure that intralayer charge transfer predominantly occurs within the luminescent motifs, thereby minimizing the involvement of quenching chromophores and imine linkages in the emission process. Secondly, as most chromophores exhibit the ACQ phenomenon due to strong interlayer π-π interactions, such ACQ effect should be effectively mitigated. Although several luminescent COFs have been reported, these COFs are constructed using the aggregation-induced emission (AIE) motifs18–23. However, the potential for promoting the fluorescence of ACQ chromophore-based COFs was left largely unexplored. Developing effective strategies for the design and synthesis of fluorescent ACQ chromophore-based COFs remains challenge.

In this contribution, we report the construction of highly emissive ACQ chromophore-based 2D COFs by integrating the strong electron-withdrawing motif, benzothiadiazide (BT), into the COF skeleton, to fine-tune the HOMO-LUMO energy levels between amine and aldehyde units. This approach allows for precise tuning of the HOMO-LUMO energy levels between amine and aldehyde units, resulting in the orderly alignment of the ACQ chromophores, dibenzo[g,p]chrysene (DBC), in an inclined stacking configuration. Within the COF intralayer, this energy level matching strategy effectively confines charge transfer predominantly to the BT core, thereby preventing charge transfer to ACQ chromophores, such as the DBC unit and imine linkage. Simultaneously, between the COF interlayers, the strong electron-withdrawing nature and twisted conformation of the BT unit efficiently suppress undesirable interlayer charge transfer, thus mitigating the adverse ACQ effect and enhancing fluorescence generation (Fig. 1). Specifically, a typical ACQ chromophore, M-PhDBC-NH2, was linked with two different aldehyde monomers, tetrakis(4-formyl-phenyl)ethene (M-TPE-CHO), and 4,4’,4”,4”‘-((benzo[c][1,2,5]thiadiazole-4,7-diylbis(4,1-phenyl-ene)) bis-(azanetriyl))tetrabenzaldehyde (M-BT-CHO), to construct two COFs, COF-TPE-PhDBC, and COF-BT-PhDBC, respectively. Interestingly, despite the known high brightness of M-TPE-CHO as an AIE motif, the resulting COF, COF-TPE-PhDBC, exhibited negligible fluorescence. In contrast, COF-BT-PhDBC, constructed using the M-BT-CHO motif, displayed high brightness. As a positive control, we also synthesized another AIE COF, COF-BT-PhTPE, by connecting two AIEgens, M-BT-CHO and M-PhTPE-NH2. Notably, the ACQ + AIE motif-based COF, COF-BT-PhDBC, exhibited a higher solid-state photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) value, 14.7%, approximately 1.5-fold higher than that of the AIE + AIE motif-based COF, COF-BT-PhTPE, with PLQY value of 9.8%.

Illustration of the fabrication of high-emissive ACQ chromophore-based COFs.

This was achieved by integrating the strong electron-withdrawing and donating benzothiadiazide motif into the COF skeleton to form an inclined stacking 2D COF.

To investigate the underlying mechanism, we conducted density functional theory (DFT) and time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations to elucidate HOMO-LUMO energy levels, and the intralayer as well as interlayer charge transfers in these COFs. The results indicate that the HOMO-LUMO alignment between the ACQ unit (M-PhDBC) and various aldehyde units (M-TPE and M-BT) is crucial. Specifically, the alignment where the HOMO and LUMO of the aldehyde unit are enveloped within those of the amine unit is essential for the inhibition of photoinduced electron transfer, thereby enhancing the fluorescence of the COF. Additionally, intralayer electron redistribution analysis showed that electrons preferentially localize on the BT core in both COF-BT-PhTPE and COF-BT-PhDBC upon photoexcitation. This localized excitation effectively prevents ACQ chromophores from participating in the luminescence process. Notably, COF-BT-PhDBC exhibited a higher intralayer charge transfer value (0.49479 e) compared to COF-BT-PhTPE (0.49253 e), attributed to the enhanced electron-donating capability of the DBC unit. Interlayer electron redistribution analysis revealed an obvious interlayer charge transfer value (0.05418 e) in COF-TPE-PhDBC, leading to fluorescence quenching. In contrast, COF-BT-PhTPE and COF-BT-PhDBC showed substantially reduced interlayer charge transfer values, with COF-BT-PhDBC displaying the lowest (0.01323 e). Intramolecular motion increases non-radiative decay processes, reducing emission; thus, comprehensive reorganization energy calculations were performed to evaluate this behavior in these COFs. Among them, COF-BT-PhDBC exhibited the lowest reorganization energy, indicating successful inhibition of non-radiative decay processes. These results collectively demonstrate that integrating the BT unit into the COF skeleton effectively fine-tunes the HOMO-LUMO energy levels between molecular building blocks to enhance intralayer charge transfer, confining it within luminescent motifs, while inhibiting interlayer charge transfer and preventing the undesired ACQ effect, thus leading to highly emissive 2D COFs. Additionally, COF-BT-PhDBC exhibits excellent three-photon fluorescence (3PF) capabilities upon near-infrared (NIR-II) light irradiation. The 3PF efficacy of COF-BT-PhDBC was demonstrated through in vivo 3PF imaging of brain vasculature in mice, achieving an imaging depth of approximately 800 µm. This work provides valuable insights into the design of emissive 3PF COFs and presents a promising direction for advancing in vivo bioimaging techniques utilizing COF materials.

Results and discussion

Construction and characterization

The synthesis of COF-TPE-PhDBC and COF-BT-PhDBC was accomplished through the co-condensation reaction of M-PhDBC with M-TPE and M-BT, respectively. COF-BT-PhTPE was obtained by linking M-BT-CHO and M-PhTPENH2 under the same conditions (Fig. 2a, and Supplementary Figs. 1 to 9). Then, the high crystallinity of these COFs was proven by the sharp powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns collected using both laboratory (Fig. 2b–d) and 2D small-angle X-ray scattering beam (Supplementary Figs. 10 to 11, Supplementary Tables 1–4). The investigation of imine linkage formation in these 2D COFs was carried out through a series of spectroscopic analyses. The Fourier transform infrared spectrum exhibited a distinctive peak at approximately 1610 cm-1, indicative of the presence of imine bonds (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 12). Furthermore, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy revealed a characteristic imine binding signal at 398.2 eV (Supplementary Figs. 13 to 15), while solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (ssNMR) spectroscopy indicated imine signals in the range of 150 to 160 ppm (Fig. 2f). Collectively, these spectroscopic results provide compelling evidence for the successful formation of imine linkages within the 2D COFs. The morphology and purity of the COF crystals were confirmed by scanning electron microscopy images and dynamic light scattering (Supplementary Figs. 16–17), demonstrating their uniform size and purity. The permanent porosity of these 2D COFs was assessed using N2 isotherm measurements at 77 K, from which the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller surface areas were derived, ranging from 700 to 2060 m2 g−1 (Fig. 2g, S18).

Although COF-BT-PhTPE and COF-BT-PhDBC share similar chemical structures, their PXRD patterns demonstrate distinct variations (Figs. 3a–c). To elucidate their precise structures, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was employed, which allows the acquisition of HRTEM images and electron diffraction (ED) patterns for both COF-BT-PhTPE and COF-BT-PhDBC. The obtained results indicate a favorable correspondence between the HRTEM images and PXRD patterns of COF-BT-PhTPE when compared to simulated patterns based on an eclipsed stacking COF structure (Fig. 3b, d–g). This agreement can be attributed to the effective docking function of the TPE motif 24. In contrast, noticeable deviations are observed between the HRTEM images and PXRD patterns of COF-BT-PhDBC in comparison to those simulated using the Same eclipsed stacking mode. These discrepancies may arise from the increased planarity of the DBC motif, which results in interlayer shifting and the formation of an inclined stacking structure25,26. To quantitatively evaluate the interlayer offset, a series of inclined stacking modes with varying degrees of offset were systematically generated (Fig. 3a–c). Interestingly, only the PXRD patterns derived from the 75° inclined stacking mode demonstrated a satisfactory agreement with the experimental PXRD patterns of COF-BT-PhDBC (Fig. 3c). Moreover, the 75° inclined stacking structure exhibited consistency with the HRTEM images of COF-BT-PhDBC (Fig. 3h–k). The pore size distribution results further supported the 75° inclined stacking structure, as the pore size closely aligned with experimental data (Supplementary Fig. 18). Collectively, these results unambiguously reveal the presence of an inclined stacking structure in COF-BT-PhDBC.

Photophysical properties

Subsequently, a series of photophysical characterizations was undertaken to explore the optical properties of the monomers and their corresponding COFs. M-PhDBC demonstrated the typical ACQ phenomenon, wherein it exhibited intense fluorescence at low concentrations but experienced fluorescence quenching upon aggregation in the solid state (Supplementary Fig. 19). In contrast, both M-BT and M-PhTPE-NH2 displayed AIE characteristics, wherein the emission intensity increased gradually with an increasing H2O ratio (Supplementary Figs. 20–21). The UV-vis absorption spectra of these COFs revealed a noticeable redshift in COF-BT-PhDBC compared to COF-TPE-PhDBC and COF-BT-PhTPE (Fig. 4a). Specifically, ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy showed a lower valence band and band gap in COF-BT-PhDBC, compared to COF-TPE-PhDBC and COF-BT-PhTPE (Supplementary Figs. 22 to 27). Consistent results were obtained from interlayer DFT calculations, which also indicated a lower HOMO-LUMO energy gap for COF-BT-PhDBC (Supplementary Fig. 28). Subsequently, steady-state and time-resolved PL spectra were acquired to investigate the fluorescence properties of these COFs, where COF-TPE-PhDBC exhibited weak emission with a PLQY value of only 0.5% (Fig. 4b, c). In contrast, COF-BT-PhDBC displayed longer lifetime and enhanced brightness with a solid-state PLQY of 14.7%, 1.5-fold higher than that of COF-BT-PhTPE (9.8%) (Fig. 4c–e, Supplementary Table 5). Interestingly, power-dependent experiments revealed a bright fluorescence and a correlation coefficient of 2.7, close to 3, in COF-BT-PhDBC under NIR-II region light irradiation (Fig. 4f), suggesting COF-BT-PhDBC exhibits excellent 3PF property, and have the potential to serve as 3PF fluorescent probes for in vivo imaging.

Then, DFT calculations were conducted to investigate the electronic structure properties of these aldehyde and amine units (Fig. 4g). The results indicate that the alignment of HOMO-LUMO energy levels between the ACQ unit (M-PhDBC-NH2) and the various aldehyde units (M-TPE-CHO and M-BT-CHO) is critical. As shown in Fig. 4h, in the case of COF-TPE-PhDBC, the HOMO level of the M-TPE-CHO units is higher than that of the amine ACQ unit (M-PhDBC-NH2), facilitating electron transfer from TPECHO to the M-PhDBC-NH2. In contrast, in COF-BT-PhTPE and COF-BT-PhDBC, the HOMO level of the M-BT-CHO units exceeds that of both M-PhDBC-NH2 and M-PhTPE-NH2, while the LUMO level remains lower (Fig. 4I, j). This HOMO-LUMO energy matching effectively inhibits photoinduced electron transfer, resulting in an enhancement of the COF’s fluorescence.

To gain deeper insights into the excited-state kinetics of these COFs, we conducted transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy measurements (Fig. 5). All three COFs exhibit characteristic TA features, including ground-state bleaching (GSB) in the 450–550 nm region and excited-state absorption (ESA) in the 550–750 nm region (Fig. 5a–f). Given that COF materials can simultaneously generate singlet states and charge-separated states upon photoexcitation27, the ESA band between 550-750 nm may be considered as a superposition of singlet and charge-separated state features. Notably, COF-BT-PhTPE and COF-BT-PhDBC display an additional ESA feature centered at ~540 nm after 1 ns, which persists with negligible decay within the 7 ns window. Such long-lived species in TA spectra are typically associated with triplet states28, thus suggesting that the ~540 nm ESA signal originates from triplet excitations in these COFs (Fig. 5b, c). In contrast, COF-TPE-PhDBC does not exhibit a comparable triplet ESA feature, which is most likely due to its rapid non-radiative relaxation pathways that suppress intersystem crossing (ISC).

To quantitatively analyze the kinetics, we extracted decay traces at ~700 nm (Fig. 5g–i). Although these traces reflect a convolution of multiple excited-state kinetics, we applied a three-exponential fitting approach and used the weighted average lifetime to represent the ensemble decay behavior, which is a standard practice in such systems29,30. The radiation rates (kr) of COF-TPE-PhDBC, COF-BT-PhTPE, and COF-BT-PhDBC were determined to be 0.013 ns−1, 0.149 ns−1, and 0.121 ns−1, respectively, whereas the non-radiation rates (knr) were 2.587 ns−1, 1.374 ns−1, and 0.701 ns−1, respectively (Supplementary Table 6). Clearly, the highest PLQY (14.7%) of COF-BT-PhDBC arises from the combination of a relatively large kr and a small knr. Although COF-BT-PhTPE also possesses a relatively large kr, its PLQY (9.8%) is compromised by the presence of a higher knr. In contrast, the very low PLQY of COF-TPE-PhDBC (0.5%) originates from both a small kr and a large knr, consistent with the absence of observable triplet ESA features in its TA spectrum (Fig. 5a).

Theoretical calculation

Then, TD-DFT and DFT calculations were conducted to investigate the electronic structure properties of these COFs. Within the intralayer, distinct electron localization was observed in the ACQ chromophores, DBC, and the imine linkage of COF-TPE-PhDBC (Fig. 6a). This observation suggests the involvement of ACQ chromophores in the luminescence process of COF-TPE-PhDBC, resulting in the quenching of their emission. Conversely, the electron distribution primarily resides on the BT core of COF-BT-PhTPE and COF-BT-PhDBC upon photoexcitation, thereby preventing the engagement of ACQ chromophores and fluorescence quencher imine linkage in their luminescence processes, thus would be favorable for luminescence (Fig. 6a). To quantitatively evaluate the charge-transfer behavior within the intralayer of these COFs, we further calculated the extent of charge transfer from the electron-donating motif (DBC or TPE) to the electron-withdrawing motif (imine linkage or BT). The results demonstrated facile electron transfer to the imine linkages, with high charge transfer amounts of 0.07668 e for COF-TPE-PhDBC (Fig. 6b). In contrast, charge transfer to the BT motif occurred more readily than in the imine linkages, with COF-BT-PhDBC exhibiting a larger charge transfer amount (0.49479 e) than COF-BT-PhTPE (0.49253 e), owing to the good electron-donating capability of the DBC unit (Fig. 6b).

Intralayer electron excitation analysis of COF-TPE-PhDBC, COF-BT-PhTPE, and COF-BT-PhDBC.

a Electron (red)-hole (blue) distributions of COF-TPE-PhDBC, COF-BT-PhTPE, and COF-BT-PhDBC. b quantitatively assess the intralayer charge-transfer amounts of COF-TPE-PhDBC, COF-BT-PhTPE, and COF-BT-PhDBC.

Regarding the interlayer interactions, the electron redistribution analysis further revealed a strong interlayer charge transfer amount (0.05418 e) in COF-TPE-PhDBC, resulting in fluorescence quenching (Fig. 7a). In contrast, both COF-BT-PhTPE and COF-BT-PhDBC exhibited the sharply reduced interlayer charge transfer amounts, 0.03760 e, and 0.01323 e, respectively, with COF-BT-PhDBC displaying the lowest value (Fig. 7b). Additionally, comprehensive reorganization energy calculations were employed to assess the intramolecular motion behavior of these COFs. Among them, COF-TPE-PhDBC exhibited a high reorganization energy, reaching 10111 cm−1, indicating the presence of strong intramolecular motion-induced non-radiative decay upon light excitation (Fig. 7c). In contrast, both COF-BT-PhTPE and COF-BT-PhDBC displayed a sharply reduced reorganization energy, with COF-BT-PhDBC exhibiting the lowest reorganization energy (3076 cm−1), suggesting successful inhibition of non-radiative decay processes within the skeleton of COF-BT-PhDBC (Fig. 7c). Moreover, the non-radiative decay was closely related to the normal modes in the low-frequency region contributed by the variations of dihedral angles31. The decreased contribution from the variations of dihedral angles to the total reorganization energy of COF-BT-PhDBC (33.16%) and COF-BT-PhTPE (33.59%) compared to that of COF-TPE-PhDBC (61.2%) further confirmed that the enhanced restriction of intramolecular motions (RIM)32–34 effect of COF-BT-PhDBC and COF-BT-PhTPE should be conducive to enhancing their AIE effect, resulting in a strong radiative decay and a high PLQY. These results collectively demonstrate that the integration of the BT unit into the COF skeleton effectively regulates the charge transfer pathways (preventing undesired ACQ effects) and excited-state decay processes (enhancing desired AIE effects). Especially, by inducing the intralayer charge transfer to the AIE chromophores while prohibiting it to the ACQ chromophores and imine linkages, confining it within the luminescent motifs as well as inhibiting interlayer charge transfer, the undesired ACQ effects were considerably prevented, thus leading to the formation of highly emissive 2D COFs. Furthermore, the crystallinity and porosity of COF materials may also influence their fluorescence properties. Enhanced porosity and crystallinity are associated with improved fluorescence performance5.

3PF imaging of the brain vasculature in mice

While previous studies have investigated the use of small molecules in the realm of 3PF bioimaging35–40, COF materials with 3PA capabilities have not been reported, and the potential application of COF materials for 3PF bioimaging remains unexplored. Considering the excellent 3PF properties and nanoscale particle size of COF-BT-PhDBC, we hypothesized that COF-BT-PhDBC could serve as a fluorescent probe for in vivo 3PF bioimaging. To test this hypothesis, we conducted imaging of blood vessels in a mouse brain using an upright scanning scope equipped with a 1600 nm fs laser. Initially, three-dimensional reconstructions of high-resolution images within depths ranging from 0 to 800 μm clearly illustrated the distribution of blood vessels in brain tissue (Fig. 8a, Supplementary Fig. 29). Additionally, we analyzed capillary diameters at various depths by quantifying the full width at half-maximum of the fitting curves. At a depth of 100 μm, we observed capillaries as narrow as 5.1 μm (Fig. 8b). Similarly, at a depth of 300 μm, a consistent capillary width of 5.2 μm was observed, indicating a uniform morphological profile of the capillaries within the tissue up to 300 μm (Fig. 8c). Notably, even at a depth of 800 μm, the structure of blood vessels remained distinguishable (Fig. 8d, Supplementary Fig. 29). These results collectively demonstrate the multi-functionality of COF-BT-PhDBC, including deep tissue penetration, high resolution, and excellent contrast for in vivo 3PF bioimaging.

Discussion

In summary, we report the construction of highly emissive ACQ chromophore-based 2D COFs by an energy level matching strategy. This approach not only ensures the intralayer charge transfer predominantly occurs within the luminescent motifs but also effectively inhibits intralayer charge transfer to ACQ chromophores and fluorescence quencher imine linkages. Additionally, it also facilitates an inclined stacking mode to mitigate the undesired ACQ effect from the interlayer interactions, thus promoting the formation of highly emissive 2D COFs. Notably, one member, COF-BT-PhDBC, exhibits high brightness with a PLQY value of 14.7% in solid-state. It has also been successfully utilized for in vivo 3PF imaging of the brain vasculature in mice, achieving an imaging depth of approximately 800 µm. This research provides valuable guidance for the design of emissive ACQ chromophore-based COFs.

Methods

Chemicals

The amine and aldehyde monomers were sourced from Shanghai Kylpharm Co., Ltd. Puromycin. Organic solvents, including mesitylene, n-butanol, dimethylacetamide, acetic acid, and acetone, were acquired from Adamas-beta®. All chemicals were used as received, without additional purification.

Animals

C57BL/6 mice, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing approximately 20 g, were procured from SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for use in this study. The animals were housed under standardized conditions at the Laboratory Animal Center of Zhejiang University, in specific pathogen-free environments. They were maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle, at a room temperature of 25 °C, and with humidity controlled at 50.0 ± 5.0%. Mice had ad libitum access to food and water, and no sex bias was applied in the selection of subjects. All experimental procedures were conducted following the approval of the Animal Use and Care Committee at Zhejiang University (ZJU20240156).

Construction of COF-TPE-PhDBC, COF-BT-PhTPE, and COF-BT-PhDBC

A Pyrex tube was charged with 0.09 mmol of tetra-topic amine building blocks and 0.09 mmol of tetra-topic aldehyde building blocks. To this mixture, 1.5 mL of mesitylene and 1.5 mL of dimethylacetamide were added, followed by the addition of 600 µL of acetic acid (6 M). The mixture was rapidly frozen at 77 K using a liquid nitrogen bath, evacuated to an internal pressure of 50 mTorr, and flame-sealed to maintain a length of 15 cm. Following a natural warm-up to room temperature, the sealed tube was transferred to an oven and heated at 120 °C for 3 days. Then, COF samples were isolated by filtration, washed with dimethylacetamide and acetone, and subjected to sequential Soxhlet extraction with dimethylacetamide and acetone, each for 48 hours. Subsequently, the COF samples were heated to 120 °C and maintained at this temperature for 24 hours to ensure complete activation.

In vivo three-photon fluorescent imaging of mouse brain blood vessels

In vivo three-photon fluorescence imaging of cerebral vasculature in mice was conducted using a customized scanning microscopy system (Ultima 2p plus, Bruker, USA), equipped with a noncollinear optical parametric amplifier (NOPA-VISIR, Spectra-Physics, USA) driven by a regenerative amplifier (Spirit-70, Spectra-Physics, USA). A C57 mouse, subjected to craniotomy, was anesthetized and administered an intravenous injection of 150 μL COF-BT-PhDBC (1 mg/ml). The excitation light was directed through a water-immersion microscope objective (XLPLN25XWMP-SP, Olympus, Japan). Signal detection during intensity imaging was achieved using a GaAsP photomultiplier tube (H7422-40, Hamamatsu, Japan). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Use and Care Committee at Zhejiang University (ZJU20240156) and adhered to the National Institutes of Health Guidelines.

Transient absorption (TA) measurements

Femtosecond pump-probe transient absorption (TA) measurements were conducted using a regenerative amplified Ti: sapphire laser system (Coherent; 800 nm, 70 fs, 6 mJ/pulse, 1 kHz repetition rate, 100 μW). The 800 nm laser pulse was divided into two components by a beam splitter. The higher-energy component was frequency-doubled using a BBO crystal to produce a 400 nm pulse, serving as the excitation beam. The lower-energy component was used to generate a white light continuum (WLC) by exciting a crystal, which acted as the probe beam. Temporal delay between the excitation and probe pulses was controlled via a motorized delay stage. For sample preparation, the powder crystal was placed directly onto a quartz substrate and ground into a solid thin film for TA measurements.

N2 adsorption analysis of COF-TPE-PhDBC, COF-BT-PhTPE, and COF-BT-PhDBC

Nitrogen adsorption isotherms were measured using an iPore 1000 automatic volumetric analyzer (PhysiChem Instruments LTD) at 77 K in a liquid nitrogen bath. Ultra-high purity grade nitrogen was employed for the adsorption experiments. The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area was determined by plotting (x/v(1-x)) against (x), where (x = P/P0) (with (P0 = 1) bar), and (v) denotes the volume of nitrogen adsorbed per gram of COF at standard temperature and pressure (STP).
