How strong is the administrative backbone of ethical governance? A descriptive cross-sectional study of health research ethics committees in Tanzania
Authors: Lazaro Amon Solomon Haule, Muhsin Aboud, Doreen Mloka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundHealth research ethics committees (HRECs) provide the administrative backbone of ethical governance by safeguarding the rights, safety and well-being of research participants and ensuring the ethical conduct of health research. Effective ethical oversight depends not only on rigorous protocol review but also on robust governance, competent personnel, adequate infrastructure, sustainable institutional support and efficient administrative systems. Despite their critical role, evidence on the administrative functionality of HRECs in Tanzania remains limited.ObjectiveTo assess the administrative functionality of HRECs in mainland Tanzania, with emphasis on governance, operational procedures, human resource capacity, training, infrastructure, documentation systems and institutional support.DesignQuantitative descriptive cross-sectional study.SettingAll active registered HRECs in clinical, research and training institutions in mainland Tanzania.Participants10 administrative representatives responsible for HREC secretariat functions, one from each of the 10 active registered HRECs.MethodsA census approach included all 10 active registered HRECs in mainland Tanzania, with one administrative representative purposively selected from each committee. Data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire and a document review checklist adapted from the Research Ethics Committee Assessment Toolkit. The assessment covered governance, committee membership, independence, training, decision-making procedures, administrative operations, financing, documentation practices and infrastructure. Document reviews verified standard operating procedures, meeting minutes, protocol-tracking systems, approval records and archival practices. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics.ResultsAll 10 HRECs participated. Administrative functionality was the highest for administrative operations (94%) and decision-making procedures (93%), followed by membership standards (86%). Compliance was lower for committee independence (75%) and training (50%). Key gaps included limited structured training, inadequate institutional financing, insufficient dedicated administrative staff, weak transparency mechanisms and inconsistent administrative and digital infrastructure. Electronic protocol-tracking systems were available in 60% of HRECs.ConclusionsTanzanian HRECs have established a solid administrative foundation for ethical governance. However, persistent gaps in training, committee independence, financing, staffing, infrastructure and digital systems may compromise the effectiveness, transparency and sustainability of ethical oversight. Strengthening administrative standards, institutional investment, continuous professional development and digital infrastructure is essential to reinforce the administrative backbone of ethical governance and improve research ethics oversight in Tanzania and other low-income and middle-income countries.Keywords: ETHICS (see Medical Ethics), MEDICAL ETHICS, Health, Health & safety, Health policy

Introduction

Strengthening the backbone of ethical governance is essential to ensuring that health research involving human participants is conducted responsibly and with respect for human rights.1 2 Effective ethical review depends not only on sound ethical judgement but also on the administrative systems that support these processes. Health research ethics committees (HRECs), also known as institutional review boards (IRBs), play a central role in safeguarding the rights, dignity and welfare of participants by ensuring that research proposals comply with established ethical and regulatory standards.3 Beyond their traditional role of reviewing research protocols, contemporary HRECs are increasingly recognised as institutional governance mechanisms responsible for promoting transparency, accountability, quality assurance and public trust in health research systems.

Effective ethical review depends not only on sound ethical judgement but also on the administrative systems that support these processes. Administrative functionality, including adequate staffing, standardised procedures, documentation systems, communication mechanisms, infrastructure and sustainable financing, is fundamental to ensuring that ethical review processes are timely, consistent and transparent. Weaknesses in these supporting systems may contribute to delayed reviews, inconsistent decision-making, inadequate monitoring of approved research and reduced institutional accountability.4 5

Ethics review processes are guided by internationally recognised frameworks, including the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki,6 the Council for International Organisations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) International Ethical Guidelines (2016)7 and the WHO Operational Guidelines for Ethics Committees That Review Biomedical Research.8 9 These frameworks emphasise not only ethical principles and scientific validity but also the importance of institutional capacity, administrative stability, independence and clear operational procedures.

Globally, research ethics oversight systems have evolved differently according to available resources, regulatory maturity and institutional capacity. In high-income settings, including the USA, the UK and several European countries, HRECs/IRBs are often supported by dedicated administrative offices, professional ethics administrators, stable institutional funding, electronic review management platforms and established quality improvement mechanisms.10 11 These systems allow committees to manage increasing research complexity, monitor compliance and maintain transparent governance structures.

In contrast, many low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), including those in sub-Saharan Africa, continue to strengthen their research ethics review systems while facing persistent challenges related to limited administrative staffing, inadequate infrastructure, insufficient training and professional development opportunities, financial constraints and inconsistent implementation of governance and independence safeguards. Although considerable progress has been achieved through national reforms and international capacity-strengthening initiatives, these resource and governance constraints continue to influence the efficiency, consistency and sustainability of ethics review processes.12 13 Although substantial progress has been achieved in establishing institutional and national ethics governance frameworks, operational capacity remains an important determinant of effective ethical oversight.

Across Africa, HRECs have expanded considerably in response to increasing health research activity, international collaborations and the growing complexity of research involving human participants. However, studies from sub-Saharan Africa have reported persistent challenges, including limited administrative support, inadequate resources, inconsistent application of standard operating procedures (SOPs), insufficient continuing education and dependence on external funding mechanisms.13 These challenges highlight that the effectiveness of ethics review systems depends not only on the existence of formal policies but also on the institutional capacity to implement and sustain them.

The WHO has emphasised the importance of strengthening research ethics governance systems in LMICs by improving operational capacity, ensuring appropriate oversight structures and supporting sustainable mechanisms for ethics review.8 Therefore, assessing the administrative functionality of HRECs provides important evidence for understanding whether existing governance structures are sufficiently supported to meet current and future demands of health research oversight.

In Tanzania, HRECs operate under the oversight of the National Institute for Medical Research (NIMR) through the National Health Research Ethics Committee (NatHREC).14 Establishment of an institutional ethics committee is a mandatory requirement for institutions conducting health research involving human participants. The Tanzanian ethics review system has expanded alongside increasing national and international health research activities, including clinical trials, implementation research and collaborative research initiatives.

Despite this progress, empirical evidence regarding the administrative functionality of Tanzanian HRECs remains limited. Existing literature has primarily focused on ethical review principles, regulatory frameworks and general research governance.15 16 At the same time, less attention has been given to the administrative systems that enable committees to function effectively. Although previous reports have highlighted challenges such as delays in protocol review, limited documentation systems, increasing administrative workload and resource constraints, these issues have not been systematically assessed across all active registered HRECs in mainland Tanzania. Understanding administrative functionality is particularly important because weaknesses in operational systems may affect not only efficiency but also the consistency, independence and credibility of ethical review decisions. For example, inadequate staffing may limit timely protocol processing, insufficient training may affect the quality of reviews and unstable funding mechanisms may raise concerns about sustainability and perceived independence.

This study therefore aimed to assess the administrative functionality of HREC secretariats across all active registered committees in mainland Tanzania. Specifically, the study evaluated governance structures, membership-related policies, independence safeguards, training requirements, decision-making procedures, administrative operations, institutional support, financing mechanisms and infrastructure capacity. By providing a comprehensive national-level assessment, this study contributes evidence to the broader global discussion on strengthening research ethics governance in resource-constrained settings. The findings provide insights relevant not only to Tanzania but also to other LMIC contexts seeking to develop sustainable, transparent and effective ethics review systems.

Methodology

Study design and rationale

This study employed a quantitative descriptive cross-sectional survey design17 to assess the administrative functionality of HREC secretariats across mainland Tanzania. A cross-sectional approach was appropriate18 for generating a single-time-point overview of administrative capacities, practices and structural challenges among the 10 active registered HRECs. Descriptive studies focus on identifying patterns and distributions within defined populations rather than testing hypotheses19 aligning with the study’s objective to generate foundational, policy-relevant data on HREC secretariat operations. This framework was particularly suitable given the limited baseline information on ethics administration in Tanzania, ensuring methodological simplicity20 while producing actionable evidence for future policy and capacity-building interventions targeting the administrative backbone of ethical review systems.

Study settings

The study was conducted in mainland Tanzanian institutions that host one of the 10 active registered HRECs recognised by NatHREC. These academic, clinical and research institutions are legally mandated to review and approve health-related research, forming the core of the national system for ethics oversight. Focusing on these settings ensured alignment with national regulatory structures and provided an accurate basis for assessing administrative functionality, procedural consistency and existing capacity gaps within HREC secretariats.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study because its primary focus was the administrative functionality of HREC secretariats. The study participants were HREC administrators and secretariat personnel responsible for coordinating committee operations, protocol management, documentation and communication. As the study evaluated institutional administrative processes rather than patient care or public-facing services, patient and public involvement was not considered applicable. However, future studies evaluating the overall performance and impact of HRECs may benefit from incorporating the perspectives of researchers, research participants and members of the public to provide a more comprehensive understanding of research ethics governance.

Study population, sampling approach and recruitment

The study population consisted of administrators and secretaries responsible for the daily operations of HRECs, including protocol management, coordination of meetings, record keeping and communication with researchers and committee members.2 Their roles positioned them as the most relevant informants on administrative practices and operational challenges within the ethics review system.

Given the limited number of active registered HRECs in mainland Tanzania (n=10) during the study period, a census sampling strategy was employed to achieve complete national coverage of eligible committees.16 21 One administrative respondent represented each HREC. Where multiple individuals were involved in HREC administration, the respondent was purposively selected based on their primary responsibility for secretariat coordination, protocol management, record keeping and routine communication with researchers and committee members.

This approach was considered appropriate because the study aimed to assess administrative functionality rather than individual perceptions of ethical review. The selected respondents were therefore expected to possess the most comprehensive institutional knowledge regarding administrative systems, resources and operational procedures,22 providing a comprehensive picture of administrative functionality across Tanzanian HRECs.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Institutions were eligible for inclusion if they (1) hosted an active HREC registered and recognised by NatHREC, (2) were located in mainland Tanzania and (3) had formally designated personnel responsible for HREC administrative activities. Participants were eligible if they were administrators or secretariat personnel directly involved in the day-to-day operations of HRECs and willing to provide informed consent.

Institutions were excluded if their HRECs were inactive, undergoing restructuring, not formally registered or lacked designated administrative personnel. Eligible staff members who were unavailable during the data collection period or declined participation were also excluded.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data collection tools

Data were collected using a modified version of the Research Ethics Committee Assessment Toolkit (RECAT) developed by the African Bioethics Consortium.23 The tool was adapted to assess the administrative functionality and operational capacity of HREC secretariats within the Tanzanian context. The assessment instrument consisted of two complementary components:

Semi-structured questionnaire

The semi-questionnaire assessed seven domains of HREC administrative functionality: (i) governance and policy framework, including institutional mandates, registration requirements and availability of standard operating procedures; (ii) membership composition, including professional diversity, gender representation, inclusion of non-scientific members and independent representation; (iii) committee independence, including conflict-of-interest management procedures and safeguards against investigator participation in conflicted reviews; (iv) training and capacity development, including requirements for initial ethics training and continuing professional development; (v) decision-making procedures, including review mechanisms, voting or consensus procedures and expedited review processes; (vi) administrative operations, including submission management, meeting documentation, communication procedures and record archiving; and (vii) infrastructure and institutional resources, including staffing, office facilities, information and communication technology (ICT) resources and financing mechanisms (online supplemental file 2). Response options included binary responses (yes/no), categorical responses and open-ended responses where additional clarification was required.

Semi-structured document review checklist

A semi-structured document review checklist was used to verify reported administrative practices by examining relevant institutional records, including SOPs, committee policies and guidelines, meeting minutes, protocol submission and tracking records, ethics approval letters and training records (online supplemental file 2). The document review was conducted by the principal researcher using a standardised checklist adapted from RECAT. To enhance consistency and minimise subjective interpretation, all documents were reviewed systematically using predefined criteria and operational definitions specified in the checklist. Where clarification was required, the researcher cross-referenced information across multiple institutional documents to ensure completeness and accuracy of data extraction.

Data collection procedures

Data collection was conducted through face-to-face visits to the participating institutions. Following written informed consent, the principal researcher administered the structured questionnaire to HREC administrators or secretaries and subsequently conducted a document review using a standardised checklist. The document review involved examining relevant institutional records to verify self-reported administrative practices. The integration of questionnaire responses with documentary evidence facilitated methodological triangulation, enhancing the credibility, completeness and validity of the study findings. The modified questionnaire and structured document review checklist are provided as supplementary materials (online supplemental file 2).

Validity and reliability

The study ensured content validity by using a recognised assessment toolkit (RECAT),23 tailored for African ethics committees. Face validity was established through expert review by two senior bioethics scholars. Pretesting at one non-sampled HREC site informed revisions to improve clarity, structure and contextual appropriateness. Although statistical reliability measures (eg, Cronbach’s alpha)24 were not calculated owing to the study’s descriptive nature, informal checks for internal consistency were conducted during data entry and analysis. Data collection was conducted in person, following participant consent.

Data management and analysis

Completed questionnaires and document review forms were checked for completeness and internal consistency. Each questionnaire was assigned a unique identifier for traceability while maintaining anonymity. Data were entered into a customised Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, with double data entry performed independently by two researchers to minimise errors; discrepancies were resolved by verifying against the original forms.

Data cleaning involved applying logical checks and pre-established coding rules to address incomplete or inconsistent entries.25 Closed-ended responses were numerically coded to support quantitative analysis. Descriptive statistics, frequency counts and percentages for categorical variables and measures of central tendency for numerical variables were used to summarise administrative characteristics such as staffing, documentation practices and availability of SOPs.

Graphical outputs, including tables and charts, were generated in Microsoft Excel to illustrate key trends and institutional comparisons, consistent with best practices for descriptive survey analysis.26 The analysis remained strictly descriptive, aimed at identifying patterns and administrative gaps rather than testing hypotheses or examining causal relationships.

Findings

Overview of key findings

This study assessed the administrative functionality of HRECs across mainland Tanzania using structured questionnaires completed by HREC administrative representatives and complementary document reviews.

Generally, Tanzanian HRECs demonstrated strong administrative foundations, with high compliance observed in administrative operations (94%) and decision-making procedures (93%). These findings indicate that most committees have established formal procedures, documentation systems and review mechanisms that support routine ethical oversight. However, important gaps were identified in areas critical for sustainable ethics governance, including structured training opportunities (50%), implementation of independence safeguards (75%), sustainable financing mechanisms, dedicated administrative staffing, transparency practices and infrastructure availability. Although electronic submission tracking systems were available in 60% of HRECs, differences in digital capacity and administrative resources suggest variation in institutional support and operational readiness among committees.

The overall mean compliance across all standards was 80%, suggesting that while administrative and procedural structures are generally strong, improvements are needed in training and independence safeguards (figure 1).

Demographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 10 respondents, one from each participating institution, were interviewed. Most were female (70%). The median age was 41 (IQR: 38–49) years. Half of the participants (50%) were aged 35–40 years, followed by those aged 46–53 (30%) and 41–45 (20%). Most respondents held a master’s degree (60%). Other qualifications included bachelor’s degrees in administration or human resource management (20%), a diploma (10%) and a PhD (10%). Participants represented diverse professional backgrounds, with administrators (20%) and public health specialists (20%) comprising the largest groups. Additional disciplines, bioethics, counselling, epidemiology, human resource management, pharmacy and secretariat services, were each represented by 10%. REC experience varied: half (50%) had served for 1–5 years, 30% for 6–10 and 20% for 11–15 (table 1).

Administrative capacity determinants

These findings relate to governance, policies, procedures, human resources, workflows, oversight, accountability and management practices.

Governance frameworks, policies and standard operating procedures

All institutions (100%) reported having a written policy mandating ethical review of human research. Oversight practices differed, with 70% conducting audits of approved studies, 20% not conducting audits and 10% uncertain. All institutions had an appointed official responsible for REC oversight. Regarding registration, 70% required RECs to be registered with an official body. The NIMR was the primary registration authority, while 30% reported dual registration with NIMR and the Office for Human Research Protections (OHRP). However, only 30% of RECs provided a formal registration number, primarily those registered with OHRP. REC establishment years ranged from 2002 to 2018, with peaks in 2002, 2005, 2014, and 2018 (each 20%). Smaller proportions were established in 2009 and 2011 (each 10%). Appointment authorities varied, most commonly the REC chair (30%) or the dean of research (30%). Other appointing bodies included the deputy vice chancellor (DVC) academic, institutional board, provost and senate research committee (each 10%) (table 2).

Membership composition and representation

Standard I assessed seven criteria related to REC membership: procedures for selecting chairs and members, term limits, professional diversity, gender representation, inclusion of non-scientific members, inclusion of independent (non-affiliated) members and quorum requirements.

Policy availability was generally high. All RECs (100%) had documented term limits, professional diversity requirements and quorum rules. Selection procedures were documented in 90% of RECs. Gender diversity policies were present in 70%, whereas policies requiring non-scientific and independent members were available in 80%. Compliance varied across RECs: 80% met four of the seven criteria, 10% met five and 10% met three. The median score was 4 out of 7, indicating partial but uneven adherence to membership standards (table 3).

Committee independence and conflict of interest safeguards

Standard II evaluated four elements of REC independence: required attendance of non-affiliated members, restrictions on investigator participation in discussions, presence of a conflict-of-interest (COI) definition and procedures for COI management.

All RECs (100%) had written COI definitions and management procedures. However, structural safeguards were inconsistently implemented. 60% required a non-affiliated member during deliberations, and only 40% restricted investigator participation when their own protocols were reviewed. Compliance scores ranged from 2 to 4. 40% of RECs met two criteria, 20% met three and 40% met all four. The median score was 3 out of 4, reflecting moderate but variable adherence to independence requirements (table 4).

Human resources and training capacity

Training-related policies (standard III) demonstrated the lowest overall compliance. Half of the RECs (50%) had policies requiring initial ethics training, and an equal proportion had provisions for refresher training. Overall, 50% lacked any training-related policies, whereas the remaining 50% had both requirements in place. The median score was 1 out of 2, indicating limited institutional emphasis on structured training for REC members (table 5). Administrative staffing was limited: 40% of RECs had no full-time staff, whereas another 40% had only one full-time staff member (figure 2).

Decision-making procedures

Standard IV compliance was generally strong. While 80% of RECs had policies governing the use of expedited review procedures, all RECs (100%) had documented decision-making processes, such as voting or consensus, and referenced widely recognised ethical guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki, the Belmont Report and CIOMS guidelines. Overall, 80% of RECs met all three criteria, and 20% met two. The median score was 3 out of 3, reflecting high-quality documentation and adherence to decision-making standards (online supplemental table S6).

Administrative operations

Compliance with administrative operations (standard V) was high. All RECs (100%) had policies on confidentiality, submission requirements, circulation of documents before meetings, approval of minutes and archiving procedures. Most (90%) had documented processes for minute-taking, communicating decisions and monitoring safety for studies involving more-than-minimal risk. Policies on post-approval monitoring were available in 80%. Overall, 80% of RECs met all nine criteria, 10% met six and another 10% met seven. The median score was 9 out of 9, indicating strong administrative structuring (online supplemental table S7).

Institutional support and financing

Most HRECs relied on submission fees (70%), while only 20% received institutional budget allocations. Additionally, 30% lacked institutional support and primarily managed student-led, self-funded protocols (figure 3).

Transparency, accountability and oversight

Although most HRECs reported that policies and procedures were accessible to researchers (80%), fewer committees had mechanisms for public accountability, including dedicated websites (50%), publicly available annual reports (20%) or formal performance assessments (40%) (online supplemental figure S4). These findings suggest that transparency mechanisms remain an area requiring further development.

Infrastructural capacity determinants

These findings relate to physical, digital and technological resources supporting HREC operations.

Administrative resources and institutional infrastructure

Most RECs (90%) had confidential meeting space and secured storage facilities, although only 60% had dedicated office space for administrative staff (online supplemental figure S5). Access to essential office resources varied: 80% had an official email address, 70% had internet access and printers and 60% had computers, telephones, photocopiers and basic office supplies such as stationery and ink (online supplemental table S8).

Information and communication technology capacity

Internet access was available in 70% of RECs, though connectivity challenges were common. Availability of computers (60%), printers (70%), telephones (60%), photocopiers (60%) and basic office supplies (60%) was inconsistent. Official email addresses were available in 80% of HRECs (online supplemental table S8).

Submission tracking and data management systems

Six HRECs (60%) had tracking systems, mostly electronic (83%), with 100% up-to-date usage. Systems captured submission details, reviewer assignments, review timelines, decisions and investigator correspondence, though reviewer assignment was inconsistently documented in 17% of cases. All 6 (100%) with tracking systems recorded all the required fields (online supplemental table S9).

Administrative operations

Meeting documentation

All HRECs (100%) reported routinely documenting committee meetings, and 90% used standardised templates for recording minutes. However, variation was observed in the completeness of documented information. Although meeting dates, attendance, applications reviewed and decisions were commonly recorded, documentation of ethical deliberations and justification for decisions was less consistently reported. This finding suggests that while administrative documentation processes are established, strengthening documentation of ethical reasoning may improve transparency, accountability and institutional learning (online supplemental table S10).

Communication with researchers

All RECs (100%) reported regularly sending letters or emails to investigators after protocol review meetings. Most (70%) communicated decisions within 1 week, whereas 30% did so within 1–2 weeks. The majority (80%) used a standard approval letter template. The consistency of content in approval letters was moderate. 75% of RECs routinely included essential elements such as the approval decision, expiration date, requirements for submitting amendments, reporting adverse events and protocol deviations, use of approved and stamped consent/data collection forms and submission of closeout reports (online supplemental table S11).

Discussion

Principal findings and global context

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the administrative functionality of all active, registered HRECs in mainland Tanzania. The findings indicate that Tanzanian HRECs have established strong administrative foundations that support ethical oversight, particularly in governance policies, decision-making processes and routine administrative operations. However, important challenges persist in areas that are increasingly recognised as critical to effective ethics governance globally, including committee independence, sustainable financing, continuous professional development, dedicated administrative staffing, transparency and digital infrastructure.

Globally, RECs have evolved beyond their traditional role of reviewing individual research protocols to become integral institutional governance bodies responsible for ensuring accountability, promoting quality assurance, enhancing transparency and fostering public trust in research.27 28 In high-income countries, including the USA, the UK and several European nations, ethics review systems are typically supported by dedicated administrative offices, professionally trained ethics personnel, sustainable funding mechanisms, electronic protocol management platforms and well-established quality improvement systems.29

In contrast, many RECs in LMICs operate in resource-constrained settings, where limitations in staffing, infrastructure, training opportunities and sustainable financing continue to impede optimal performance.30 31 The findings from Tanzania reflect this broader LMIC context while demonstrating notable progress in the establishment of formal administrative and governance structures that strengthen ethical oversight of health research.

Administrative capacity determinants

Governance frameworks and procedural organisation

Tanzanian HRECs demonstrated strong performance in governance frameworks, institutional policies and SOPs. All participating committees reported having institutional policies requiring ethical review of research involving human participants, and the majority maintained documented SOPs to guide committee operations. These findings suggest that Tanzania has established a relatively mature formal governance framework for institutional ethics review. Similar progress has been reported across several African countries, where national research ethics systems have strengthened in response to increasing health research activity and international collaborations.13 32 However, the existence of governance policies and SOPs alone does not guarantee their effective implementation. Evidence from Africa and other LMICs indicates that substantial gaps often exist between formal regulatory requirements and actual practice, largely because of inadequate resources, inconsistent monitoring and limited administrative capacity. Consequently, well-developed governance frameworks must be accompanied by sufficient institutional support to ensure their effective operationalisation.

In high-income countries, ethics review systems are similarly anchored in comprehensive governance frameworks; however, these are typically supported by dedicated administrative offices, professionally trained ethics personnel, routine quality assurance programmes, electronic protocol management systems and robust regulatory oversight mechanisms.33 34 Furthermore, ethics committees in these settings commonly complement SOPs with structured deliberative processes, ethics consultation services, performance monitoring and periodic quality audits. Evaluations of IRBs and RECs in the USA and other high-income settings have shown that despite well-established governance structures, variations in review quality and procedural implementation persist, underscoring the need for continuous quality assessment and improvement beyond the existence of formal procedures.35

The Tanzanian experience reflects a broader challenge faced by many LMICs: building the institutional capacity needed to translate well-established governance policies into consistently implemented administrative practices. Strengthening committee deliberative processes, enhancing quality assurance mechanisms and investing in administrative capacity will be essential to ensuring that governance frameworks effectively support high-quality, transparent and accountable ethical oversight of health research.

Committee independence and conflict-of-interest management

Although Tanzanian HRECs demonstrated strong compliance with COI policies, the implementation of committee independence safeguards was less consistent. All participating committees reported having documented definitions of COI and established procedures for their management. However, only 60% required the participation of non-affiliated members during protocol deliberations, whereas just 40% prohibited investigators from participating in the review of their research protocols. These findings highlight an important distinction between the existence of formal independence policies and their effective implementation. Robust ethics governance requires not only documented procedures but also institutional mechanisms that safeguard committees from undue influence arising from organisational affiliations, financial interests or investigator involvement. Independence is widely recognised as a cornerstone of credible and trustworthy ethical review, ensuring that decisions are based solely on ethical and scientific considerations rather than institutional or personal interests. Similar challenges have been reported across LMICs, where institutional hierarchies, limited pools of independent members and resource constraints can compromise committee autonomy and the effective implementation of COI safeguards.13 31

In contrast, ethics review systems in many high-income countries typically require the formal inclusion of independent or community members, comprehensive COI disclosure and management procedures and clear institutional separation between investigators and ethics committee decision-making processes.36 37 Strengthening independence safeguards in Tanzania may therefore require clearer national standards, transparent appointment mechanisms, protected committee roles and routine monitoring of compliance with independence requirements. These measures are reinforced through national regulations, accreditation standards and periodic quality assurance reviews to promote transparency, accountability and public trust. The findings suggest that strengthening committee independence in Tanzania will require more than the existence of written policies. Clearer national guidance on committee independence, transparent and merit-based appointment processes, greater representation of independent and community members, protected decision-making authority and routine monitoring of compliance with independence standards are essential to enhancing the credibility and effectiveness of ethical oversight. Such measures would further align Tanzanian HRECs with international best practices while reinforcing public confidence in the ethical governance of health research.

Training, expertise and professional development

Training emerged as the weakest domain assessed in this study, with only half of the participating HRECs reporting formal requirements for both initial orientation and ongoing refresher training. This finding is consistent with evidence from other African countries and LMICs, where ethics committee members often undertake protocol reviews with limited opportunities for structured continuing professional development.31 32 As health research continues to evolve, ethics committees are increasingly expected to review complex studies involving genomics, artificial intelligence, digital health technologies, big data, biobanking and adaptive clinical trial designs. These emerging fields require specialised knowledge and continuous updating of ethical and regulatory competencies.

In contrast, ethics review systems in many high-income countries are supported by well-established training infrastructures, including structured induction programmes, continuing education, professional certification, regulatory updates and access to national and international ethics networks.38 These mechanisms enable committee members and administrative staff to remain current with evolving ethical standards, regulatory requirements and scientific advances. Nevertheless, even well-resourced systems continue to face challenges in maintaining expertise in rapidly developing areas of research, highlighting the universal need for continuous professional development. The findings of this study underscore the importance of institutionalising competency-based training programmes for both HREC members and secretariat personnel in Tanzania. Regular induction and refresher training, complemented by mentorship, peer learning and participation in regional and international ethics networks, would strengthen the quality and consistency of ethical review. Such investments would also enhance committees’ capacity to respond effectively to emerging ethical challenges and align national ethics oversight with evolving international standards and best practices.

Financing, institutional support and accountability

Most Tanzanian HRECs relied primarily on protocol submission fees to finance their operations, whereas only a few received dedicated budgetary allocations from their host institutions. Reliance on submission fees raises important concerns regarding financial sustainability, as ethics review committees require predictable and adequate funding to support administrative operations, maintain qualified personnel, manage records, implement digital systems and undertake continuous quality improvement activities. Similar financing challenges have been reported across other LMICs, where RECs often operate with limited institutional investment and depend heavily on externally generated revenue or project-based support.39 40

In contrast, ethics review systems in many high-income countries are supported by more stable institutional or government funding, enabling committees to maintain dedicated administrative infrastructure, employ professional staff, invest in information management systems and conduct routine monitoring and quality assurance activities without relying primarily on individual protocol submissions.41 Such financing arrangements strengthen institutional independence, operational efficiency and the long-term sustainability of ethics oversight systems.

The findings suggest that diversifying funding sources and increasing institutional investment in Tanzanian HRECs would enhance their financial sustainability, operational capacity and administrative effectiveness. Equally important is strengthening accountability and transparency mechanisms. The limited availability of publicly accessible annual reports, performance indicators and quality improvement activities observed in this study indicates that accountability systems remain underdeveloped. Regular public reporting of committee performance, operational metrics and quality improvement initiatives would enhance transparency and strengthen confidence among researchers, research participants, institutions, regulators, funders and the wider public. Such measures would reinforce the credibility, accountability and independence of Tanzania’s health research ethics governance system while aligning it with international best practices.

Infrastructural capacity determinants

Physical infrastructure and administrative resources

This study identified considerable variation in the administrative infrastructure available to Tanzanian HRECs. While most committees reported having confidential meeting spaces and secure facilities for document storage, fewer had dedicated office space, adequate ICT resources and comprehensive administrative support. Similar infrastructural constraints have been documented in other LMICs, where limited physical and technological resources hinder confidentiality, efficient records management, communication and overall administrative performance.42 43 The observed differences in infrastructure are likely attributable to variations in institutional characteristics, including research intensity, organisational priorities, financial resources and the historical development of ethics review systems. Institutions with greater research activity typically require more robust administrative capacity to manage increasing numbers of protocol submissions, amendments, continuing reviews, protocol deviations and post-approval monitoring responsibilities. Consequently, infrastructure needs should be aligned with the volume and complexity of research activities undertaken within each institution.

In contrast, RECs in Europe, the USA and other high-income countries generally operate within well-established administrative structures supported by dedicated office space, professional administrative personnel, electronic information management systems and organisational resources that facilitate efficient protocol review and regulatory oversight.37 44 Such infrastructure promotes operational continuity, secure document management, timely communication and consistent implementation of ethical review processes, thereby enhancing both efficiency and accountability.

The findings of this study highlight the importance of investing in physical infrastructure and administrative resources to strengthen the operational capacity of Tanzanian HRECs. Expanding dedicated office facilities, improving ICT infrastructure, implementing secure electronic document management systems and providing adequate administrative support would enhance committee efficiency and reinforce the quality and reliability of ethical oversight. Although this study provides a comprehensive assessment of the administrative functionality of all active registered HRECs in mainland Tanzania, it did not evaluate annual protocol volumes or other workload indicators. Future research should incorporate measures such as the number of protocols reviewed annually, review turnaround times, frequency of protocol amendments, continuing review activities and post-approval monitoring responsibilities. These indicators would provide a more comprehensive understanding of committee workload, staffing requirements, infrastructure needs and resource allocation, thereby informing evidence-based planning for strengthening research ethics governance.

Digital systems and submission tracking

This study found that 60% of Tanzanian HRECs had adopted electronic systems for protocol submission and tracking, representing an important step towards modernising ethics review administration. The adoption of digital platforms has the potential to improve transparency, streamline administrative workflows, strengthen records management, facilitate communication among committee members and enable more effective monitoring of review timelines and committee performance. Electronic systems also support audit trails, secure document storage and timely reporting, all of which contribute to more efficient and accountable ethics oversight. Despite these advances, considerable variation in digital infrastructure remains across Tanzanian HRECs. Similar challenges have been reported in many LMICs, where financial constraints, limited information technology infrastructure and insufficient technical expertise continue to necessitate partial reliance on paper-based review processes. These limitations can contribute to administrative delays, inconsistent record management, reduced monitoring capacity and inefficiencies throughout the ethics review process.45 46

In contrast, research ethics review systems in many high-income countries have largely transitioned to comprehensive electronic ethics management platforms that facilitate protocol submission, reviewer assignment, communication, document archiving, monitoring of review timelines and post-approval oversight.46 47 These integrated digital systems improve operational efficiency, enhance regulatory compliance, support quality assurance activities and provide valuable data for monitoring committee performance and institutional accountability. The findings of this study suggest that expanding digital infrastructure across Tanzanian HRECs should be a strategic priority. Investment in secure electronic ethics management systems, reliable information technology infrastructure and technical support would improve administrative efficiency, strengthen record-keeping and monitoring, enhance transparency and increase preparedness for the ethical oversight of increasingly complex and data-intensive health research. Such investments would further align Tanzania’s ethics review system with national regulatory expectations and international best practices.

Documentation practices and communication with investigators

Tanzanian HRECs demonstrated strong administrative documentation practices, with all committees reporting routine preparation of meeting minutes and communication of review decisions to investigators. Communication systems were generally functional; however, variation was observed in the content and structure of approval letters, particularly regarding the inclusion of post-approval requirements, monitoring expectations and conditions associated with ethical approval.

Although documentation practices were largely established, the findings suggest that records often emphasise administrative decisions and procedural outcomes rather than the ethical reasoning and deliberative processes that inform committee decisions. This pattern has also been reported in other LMIC settings, where analyses of REC documentation have shown that meeting records frequently focus on decisions, regulatory compliance and administrative actions while providing limited documentation of substantive ethical deliberations and justification for decisions.48 49

In contrast, RECs in high-income settings increasingly emphasise comprehensive documentation practices that capture not only review outcomes but also the ethical considerations, rationale and deliberative processes underlying committee decisions.48 49 50 Such approaches enhance transparency, promote institutional learning, facilitate quality improvement and support accountability in ethics review processes. This is consistent with international guidance that emphasises the importance of evaluating how ethical considerations are identified, discussed and applied during research review.9 50 Strengthening documentation standards among Tanzanian HRECs to systematically capture key ethical issues considered, the rationale for decisions, conditions for approval and post-approval monitoring requirements would enhance transparency and support continuous quality improvement. Furthermore, the adoption of standardised communication templates for approval letters and investigator correspondence could improve consistency, clarify researchers’ ongoing ethical obligations and strengthen compliance with post-approval oversight requirements.

Synthesis

Tanzanian HRECs demonstrate strong procedural foundations, particularly in governance structures, administrative processes and documentation practices. However, persistent administrative, financial and infrastructural limitations constrain the depth, consistency and overall effectiveness of ethical review processes. Compared with other LMIC settings, Tanzanian HRECs show considerable progress in establishing formal ethics governance systems; nevertheless, important gaps remain in areas such as member training and professional development, committee independence, gender representation, sustainable financing, digital capacity and operational support.

In contrast, RECs in high-income countries generally benefit from more established institutional support systems, including stronger independence safeguards, structured capacity-building programmes, dedicated administrative personnel, stable financing mechanisms and integrated digital platforms. These differences highlight that effective ethical governance depends not only on the existence of formal policies and procedures but also on the availability of adequate institutional capacity, professional expertise and sustainable operational resources. Generally, the findings reinforce global evidence that high-quality ethics oversight requires a multidimensional approach that integrates governance frameworks with functional administrative systems, continuous professional development, infrastructure investment, accountability mechanisms and institutional commitment. Strengthening these determinants is essential for enhancing the quality, transparency, efficiency and sustainability of health research ethics review in Tanzania and ensuring that HRECs are adequately prepared to address increasingly complex research landscapes.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It employed a census design that included all active and registered HRECs in mainland Tanzania, ensuring comprehensive national coverage. The use of validated RECAT-based instruments and triangulation of data from a structured questionnaire and document review enhanced the reliability and credibility of the findings. Face-to-face data collection further improved data quality by clarifying and verifying responses. Despite these strengths, some limitations should be acknowledged. The study captured only the perspectives of HREC administrators or secretariat representatives and did not include those of HREC members, researchers, institutional leaders or regulators. In addition, operational indicators such as protocol workload and review turnaround times were not assessed. Nevertheless, the findings provide valuable baseline evidence to inform efforts to strengthen HREC governance and administrative capacity in Tanzania and other LMICs.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Tanzanian HRECs have established essential administrative and review structures that support organised and timely ethical oversight of health research involving human participants. These foundations represent important progress in strengthening research ethics governance and safeguarding the rights, safety and welfare of research participants. However, the findings also reveal persistent administrative and infrastructural gaps, particularly related to member training and professional development, committee independence, gender and community representation, sustainable financing and the standardisation of documentation, communication and operational infrastructure. These limitations may affect the consistency, transparency, efficiency and long-term sustainability of ethical review processes. Addressing these challenges requires a comprehensive strengthening approach that combines investment in institutional capacity, continuous competency development, enhanced safeguards for independence, improved infrastructure and robust accountability mechanisms. Strengthening HREC administrative functionality will not only improve the quality and reliability of ethical review but also reinforce public confidence in research governance and support the conduct of high-quality, ethically responsible health research in Tanzania and other LMICs.

Recommendations

National regulatory and oversight authorities

National regulatory and oversight bodies including national health research ethics authorities and relevant government ministries should establish and enforce minimum administrative standards for HRECs. These standards should define essential requirements related to staffing, documentation practices, infrastructure, digital systems and records management to promote consistency, efficiency and quality across ethics review committees. Clear national guidance on committee independence should also be developed, accompanied by routine monitoring and evaluation mechanisms to safeguard HRECs from undue institutional, financial or external influence. Furthermore, national authorities should facilitate coordinated and sustainable capacity-building initiatives for HRECs including the development of standardised training curricula, competency-based orientation programmes and continuing professional development opportunities for committee members and secretariat personnel. Such initiatives would strengthen ethical review expertise and improve preparedness for emerging challenges in health research oversight.

Institutional authorities

Institutions hosting HRECs including universities, research institutions and healthcare facilities should provide dedicated financial, human resource and infrastructural support to ensure the effective, independent and sustainable functioning of ethics review committees. Investment in appropriate administrative and digital infrastructure including secure records management systems, electronic ethics review platforms and reliable information technology support is essential for improving operational efficiency, transparency, data security and accountability. Host institutions should also strengthen internal governance policies that protect HREC autonomy by ensuring clear separation between ethics review responsibilities and institutional interests related to research promotion, funding or academic performance. Adequate administrative staffing and institutional recognition of HREC functions should be prioritised to support effective oversight and maintain public confidence in the research ethics system.

Health research ethics committees

HRECs should strengthen their internal systems by adopting standardised documentation and communication procedures including harmonised application forms, review checklists, decision templates and approval letters to enhance consistency, transparency and accountability in ethics review processes. Committees should also prioritise regular training, mentorship and peer-learning opportunities for members and secretariat staff, with emphasis on ethical principles, regulatory requirements, emerging research methodologies and effective administrative practices.

In addition, HRECs should actively promote diversity and inclusiveness in committee composition by improving gender balance and strengthening representation of community members and other relevant stakeholders. Greater diversity within committees can enhance contextual understanding, promote socially responsive ethical decision-making and strengthen the legitimacy and public trust of research ethics oversight.
