Dual optical frequency comb using a multi-functional PIC based on fully integrated injection locked gain-switched lasers
Authors: Alejandro Rosado, M. Deseada Gutierrez-Pascual, Gaurav Jain, Frank Smyth, Prince M. Anandarajah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The authors demonstrate and characterise a versatile photonic integrated circuit (PIC) that could serve as a mutually injection locked optical frequency comb (OFC), a 4-channel optical coherent transmitter or a dual optical frequency comb (DOFC). This architecture offers flexibility in terms of its configuration so that it could be adopted to serve the requirements of specific applications. In this paper, we focus on the DOFC configuration, showcasing the ability of this PIC to generate a dual comb with a flexible free spectral range (FSRs) using fully integrated components. Such integration significantly reduces the size of the interrogator compared to other architectures enabling compact, lightweight designs suitable for space-constrained applications. The downconverted radiofrequency (RF) comb generated by heterodyning, on a photodiode, the two phase correlated OFCs exhibits an extremely high amplitude stability with less than 0.4 dB fluctuations over 30 minutes. In the best-case scenario, the resulting RF-DOFC portrays more than 50 lines with a carrier-to-noise ratio (CNR) exceeding 20 dB . The results obtained, with an adaptable PIC, show that such a fully integrated dual comb device, offering a small form factor, displays immense potential to be employed in photonic sensing applications such as spectroscopy, distributed sensing and fibre Bragg grating sensing.

Introduction

An optical frequency comb (OFC) is a source that generates an equally spaced array of phase-correlated optical tones with a precise value of the free spectral range (FSR)1. In the temporal domain, the comb corresponds to a train of highly coherent and short optical pulses. These inherent characteristics make OFCs an attractive and versatile asset for high-end applications like optical communications2, metrology and atomic clocks3,4, microwave photonics5 and infrared spectroscopy6,7.

OFCs have been commonly applied in photonic sensing8–10, however, in recent years, dual optical frequency combs (DOFCs)6,7,11–16 have become a popular approach to overcome many of the constraints of OFC spectroscopy. The latter is achieved mainly through the simplification of the receiver, offering higher precision, shorter acquisition times and the use of low-bandwidth receivers17. To realise the DOFC configuration, two OFCs with slightly different repetition rates are detected on a photodiode, to generate a multi-heterodyne downconverted RF comb spectrum. This low frequency signal contains all the information of the original optical (i.e.; multi-THz) signal allowing for the processing of the sensing information using low-cost, low-frequency off-the-shelf electronic components. Different architectures have been employed for the implementation of dual-comb spectroscopy (DCS), such as electro-optical modulators18, mode-locking19 and gain-switching20, generating OFCs with features that are typical of each of these techniques. Amongst them, an optically injected gain switched laser (OI-GSL) has been successfully implemented in multiple and novel applications, including gas sensing21,22, ranging23 and fibre Bragg grating (FBG) sensing24. One of the positive attributes associated with the OI-GSL technique is the high degree of flexibility including central wavelength and FSR tunability albeit with a trade-off on the spectral bandwidth. While the above-mentioned demonstrations showcase the benefits of using DCS, they utilise fibre-based components such as circulators, polarization controllers and isolators, which lead to instabilities arising from polarization, temperature or mechanically induced changes in the fibre. This in turn can cause fluctuations of the resulting RF comb, thus limiting the achievable resolution and accuracy of the measurement. In addition, the use of discrete devices, employed for the generation of the OFCs, results in a large footprint, high cost and power-hungry components/system. However, with the advent of PICs, the realisation of DOFC generators in compact, integrated platforms is creating renewed interest and paving the way for their use in industrial environments. As PIC technology matures, economies of scale drive down costs per chip, making each new generation more affordable. This contrasts with traditional bulk-optic designs that remain relatively expensive to manufacture. In the past, some preliminary realisations were reported on DOFC PICs that were based on the OI-GSL architecture. In25, the authors demonstrated a PIC capable of producing two individual combs, without demonstrating the simultaneous generation of a DOFC nor showing a spectrum of the detected RF beat signal. More recently, the authors in26 reported on a dual-comb PIC where the OFCs were generated using two gain-switched semiconductor ring lasers. However, optical injection into the two GS lasers was achieved with the aid of an external tunable laser. While these two works represent significant advancements in the field, they only partially validate the feasibility of the implementation of a flexible all-integrated DOFC PIC. Hence, a fully integrated dual-comb PIC based on optically-injected GSLs remains unreported, leaving a critical gap yet to be addressed.

In this paper, we present a novel and versatile PIC, based on optically injected GS lasers27, whose architecture can be reconfigured to deliver versatile functionalities, ranging from a multichannel optical transmitter, a millimetre wave generator and an OFC/DOFC source. Here, we demonstrate for the first time, to the best of our knowledge, a monolithically integrated dual-comb generator based on the OI-GSL architecture, in which all the lasers (the two secondary and the primary laser) are integrated in the same PIC. We also report on a comprehensive optical and electrical characterisation of each individual OFC as well as the combined dual-comb. Finally, a frequency and power stability analysis of the electrical beat spectrum is carried out, highlighting the excellent stability of the PIC required to achieve a high-resolution measurement in a field implementation.

PIC design and fabrication

Fig. 1(a) Schematic and (b) block diagram of the DOFC PIC based on the OI-GSL architecture.

(a) Schematic and (b) block diagram of the DOFC PIC based on the OI-GSL architecture.

A schematic and a block diagram of the fabricated InP PIC used in this demonstration are shown in Fig. 1a and b, respectively. The part of the device used as a DOFC comprises two single-mode secondary lasers (SLs) that are gain-switched and subsequently mutually injection-locked28 by a wavelength tunable single mode primary laser (PL). The PIC is designed to allow flexibility in the injection in such a way that the PL can be either on-chip (internal) or off-chip (external, connected through input O2 in Fig. 1. The internal PL is implemented as a multi-section distributed Bragg reflector (DBR) laser, contained within the red rectangle in Fig. 1, and two distributed feedback (DFB) lasers act as the SLs, within the purple rectangle in Fig. 1. The DBR PL consists of 4-sections: gain, phase, and two DBR sections (front and back)29. This configuration enables fine wavelength tuning and adequate spectral alignment for the simultaneous injection-locking of the two SLs. An optical waveguide, denoted as O1, located at the rear DBR section, is used to tap a portion of the light from the PL and channel it towards the PIC output for characterisation and monitoring of the PL, as shown in Fig. 1.

The SLs are custom-designed for high-speed direct modulation/gain switching through RF ground-signal-ground (GSG) inputs. Wavelength tunability of the SLs, to achieve partial spectral overlap of both GSLs, is facilitated by two integrated resistance-based thermal heaters (TH) attached to the two DFBs. Additionally, each SL is preceded by a short gain section that functions as a variable optical attenuator (VOA), for controlling the PL’s optical power injected into the SLs.

The outputs of the two SLs are combined using a multimode interference (MMI) coupler, designed to split the incoming signal and couple it into an array of four single mode injection-locked lasers for selective demultiplexing of the desired comb tones, contained within the green rectangle in Fig. 1. It is important to note that this part of the PIC is left unused in this work and is described for completeness. All optical inputs/outputs to/from the PIC are mode-matched to single mode fibres through spot-size converters (SSC) for the purpose of fibre coupling. The optical outputs of the PIC, marked by black rectangles in Fig. 1 are denoted as O1 to O6 (output 1-6). The PIC was fabricated by Fraunhofer HHI in a dedicated wafer as part of the JePPIX InPulse Pilot line, leveraging their well-established generic InP platform. The fabrication platform is based on the 3-inch, semi-insulating InP substrate wafer technology, offering high-speed modulation capabilities. The active photonic components are realised using InGaAsP/InGaAsP multi-quantum well (MQW) structures optimised for 1.55 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\upmu$$\end{document}m operation. Two types of MQW stacks are used: one optimised for optical gain (used in lasers, amplifiers and VOAs), and another tailored for refractive index variation (used in phase shifters, tunable DBRs, etc). The passive waveguides are fabricated as deeply-etched ridge structures ensuring low propagation loss and tight mode confinement. The monolithic integration of the passive and active elements is done through butt-joints, supporting the integration of a wide variety of photonic components in a single chip, suitable for PICs as presented in this work.

The PIC was packaged in a 50-pin Kovar optical butterfly box by Fraunhofer HHI. The package includes a thermo-electric cooler (TEC), and a thermistor for temperature control of the PIC at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${25}^\circ$$\end{document}C. The RF signals are supplied through high-speed pins in the package, and an internal RF interposer board is used to route the RF signals between the package and the high-speed ground-signal-ground bond pads in the PIC interface. The substrate for the RF interposer is Rogers 3010 and the RF transmission lines are designed as 50 Ohm ground-backed coplanar waveguides (GBCPW) for speeds > 20 GHz. The PIC is coupled with an 8-channel polarization-maintaining fiber array unit (FAU), actively optical aligned. The DC biasing of the sections is done through a high-channel count PIC controller developed by Pilot Photonics.

For the optical and electrical characterisation of the resulting OFCs, the light from the output of the PIC (O2 in Fig. 1) was passed through an optical isolator (Iso) to prevent undesired optical feedback into the PIC, amplified using an Erbium-doped fibre amplifier (EDFA, Amionics, AEDFA-IL-20-B-FA) and split by means of a 1:2 passive optical splitter (50:50). The upper branch of the splitter was used for recording and analysis of the optical spectrum (APEX Technologies, AP2083A, resolution of 0.04 pm). The lower branch was passed into a 20 GHz photodetector (Thorlabs, DXM20AF) for analysis of the individual combs or a 150 MHz photodetector (Thorlabs PDA10D) for dual-comb detection. The detected signal was then characterised with the aid of an electrical spectrum analyser (ESA) with a nominal bandwidth of 6 GHz (Rodhe & Schwarz FSL).

The quality of each independent OFC was quantified in terms of its carrier-to-noise ratio (CNR), defined as the mean value of the signal-to-noise ratio for tones within 10 dB from the highest tone, and its 10-dB spectral width (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta f_{10}$$\end{document}), defined as the maximum frequency separation between two tones again within a 10-dB spectral envelop. The product of both quantities (with units of dB\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\cdot$$\end{document}GHz) was used as the figure of merit (FoM) for the quality of the OFC in the optical domain. For the electrical downconverted RF comb, the electrical CNR (e-CNR) and spectral width (e-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta f_{10}$$\end{document}) are defined in a similar way to their optical counterparts. The spectral flatness of the RF comb is defined as the ratio between the geometric mean and the arithmetic mean of the amplitude of all the tones of the RF comb within the first 10 dB from the peak, providing a figure of merit for the amplitude uniformity of RF comb.

Further to the OFC characterisation, an in-depth analysis of the down-converted RF spectra obtained by detecting the DOFC was conducted. In this scenario, the two OFCs are optically coupled (via the internal coupler within the PIC), passed through the optical isolator and then externally filtered using a bandwidth tunable optical bandpass filter (OBPF, EXFO XTM-50). The function of the OBPF was to suppress the injected tone as well as all higher wavelength lines. Without filtering, the heterodyne beat between tones located symmetrically around the injected wavelength would generate the same RF frequency, thus introducing an uncertainty22 as well as significant amplitude fluctuations. Conclusions

A versatile and highly-reconfigurable PIC platform has been presented here, addressing and characterizing its capabilities as a fully-functional and integrated DOFC generator. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of a fully-monolithically integrated PIC comprising a wavelength tunable primary laser and two optically injected gain-switched semiconductor lasers that are used for dual-comb generation. The integration significantly reduces the size of the interrogator, by more than 40 times in comparison to other discrete-based architectures, and thereby enables compact, lightweight designs suitable for space-constrained applications. It is also important to note reduction in the power consumption by a factor of 5. Moreover, PIC-based designs enhance resistance to shock, vibration, and EMC interference, making them ideal for aerospace and harsh industrial environments where compliance with standards is required. A systematic analysis of the optimum operation conditions for the PIC exhibits that a mutually coherent dual-OFC can be generated at different frequency ranges, including 200 MHz and 2 GHz, but not limited to these values. The provided examples of OFC generation at these two frequency ranges achieve performance metrics comparable to discrete-component systems, as evidenced by their similar values of the FoM. This exceptional performance stems directly from the maturity in the fabrication processes in HHI. By employing a common on-chip laser to injection lock both OFCs, the down-converted RF combs exhibit unparalleled quality, as their electrical CNR exceeds 30 dB in both ranges, with a remarkably flat spectral shape. Furthermore, the obtained results showcase the excellent performance of this PIC-based dual-comb source in terms of wavelength and power fluctuations. Specifically, the averaged value of the linewidth is 30 Hz, a value constrained by the minimum attainable spectral resolution. In addition, the power fluctuations remain extremely low (below 0.5 dB) without averaging, especially when compared with similar architectures based on discrete components22. By leveraging its high coherence, low fluctuations and overall performance, these excellent results clearly show that such a PIC based DOFC could be employed in various photonic sensing applications, such as gas sensing or FBG interrogation.

Methods

OFC generation

As mentioned in the text, the dual-comb generator is based on an integrated injection-locked gain-switched comb laser assembly followed by an all-active 1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 4 demultiplexer PIC, that can be used for many different applications. The main subsystem in the dual-comb generator consists of the two SLs, marked in blue in Fig. 2 and the PL, marked in red in Fig. 2. These three laser sources are constituted by different sections. The SLs have three different sections that can be actively controlled by current. The first section of these two SL is the DFB section that can be driven in either CW or GS operation to produce a CW signal or optical pulses. The second section of these two SLs is the integrated semiconductor optical amplifier (SOA), biased for higher optical power. And the third section that is constituted by a heater (TH), allowing some degree of wavelength tunability. The internal PL is implemented as a multi-section distributed Bragg reflector (DBR) laser and has six different sections. Firstly, a gain (GN) section acts as the main source of light; and the back and the front reflectors (BR and FR), primary source of optical feedback. These reflectors, when biased, also allow the coarse wavelength tuning of the laser. Apart from these elements, there is a phase (PH) section for further fine tuning of the laser, and an integrated SOA and a VOA, allowing for optimum optical power injection from the PL to both SLs. The overall temperature of the PIC is controlled by a temperature-controller (Thorlabs, TED200)

For the gain-switching of these devices, we have used a combination of DC current for each SL that are provided through the XDAC section, presented in Fig. 2. The RF signals are generated by two different RF sources (Rodhe & Schwarz SMB100A and RIGOL DSG830) and then amplified (by more than 20 dB) by means of two 12-GHz bandwidth electrical amplifiers (OPTILAB, MD12). Additionally, for the experiments at 200 MHz, the outputs of these two amplifiers are connected to two SRD (TEKBOX Comb generator, TBCG2) and then attenuated by fixed RF attenuators.
