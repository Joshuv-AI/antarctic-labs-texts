Hypercube C*-Algebras and an Application to Magic Isometries
Björn Schäfer

Abstract
. We study C*-algebras generated by two partitions of unity with orthogonality relations governed by hypercubes Q n Q_{n} for n ∈ ℕ ∖ { 0 } n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\} . These “hypercube C*-algebras” are special cases of bipartite graph C*-algebras which have been investigated by the author in a previous work. We prove that the hypercube C*-algebras C ∗ ​ ( Q n ) C^{\ast}(Q_{n}) are subhomogeneous and obtain an explicit description as algebra of continuous functions from a standard simplex into a finite-dimensional matrix algebra with suitable boundary conditions. Thus, we generalize Pedersen’s description of the universal unital C*-algebra C ∗ ​ ( p , q ) C^{\ast}(p,q) of two projections. We use our results to prove that any 2 × 4 2\times 4 “magic isometry” matrix can be filled up to a 4 × 4 4\times 4 “magic unitary” matrix. This answers a question from Banica, Skalski and Sołtan.

Introduction

Consider the universal unital C*-algebra
C^{*}(p,q)
generated by two projections
p
and
q
. It has been first noted by Pedersen
[
13
]
that this C*-algebra can be described very explicitly as algebra of continuous functions from the interval
[0,1]
into the
2\times 2
-matrices with suitable boundary conditions. More precisely, one has

(1)

\displaystyle C^{\ast}(p,q)\cong\{f\in C([0,1],M_{2})\mid f(0),f(1)\text{ are diagonal}\},

and the isomorphism is given by

\displaystyle p\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}_{t\in[0,1]},\quad q\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}t&\sqrt{t(1-t)}\\
\sqrt{t(1-t)}&1-t\end{pmatrix}_{t\in[0,1]}.

One can prove this theorem by different methods: For instance it follows from Halmos’ description of two projections in generic position
[
9
]
, or it can be proved using the Mackey machine
[
14
]
. More generally, Roch and Silbermann studied Banach algebras generated by two idempotents in
[
15
]
and recovered the same result by very different methods. In fact, they proved that any unital Banach algebra
A
generated by two idempotents satisfies the
standard identity
of order
4
which means that for any four elements
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in A
the identity

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{4}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)x_{\sigma(1)}x_{\sigma(2)}x_{\sigma(3)}x_{\sigma(4)}=0

is true where
S_{4}
is the symmetric group on four elements and

is the sign of the permutation
\sigma
. By general results, see e.g.
[
12
]
, it follows that for every maximal ideal

of
A
the quotient
A/I
is isomorphic to some matrix algebra
M_{\ell}
with
\ell\leq 2
. This enables a detailed description of
A
, and one recovers Pedersen’s result in the case where

is the universal C*-algebra generated by two projections.

In the preceding work
[
16
]
the author investigated a class of C*-algebras which generalizes the algebra

Definition 1.1
.

Given a bipartite graph

with disjoint vertex sets
U
and

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in U}p_{u}

These C*-algebras occur naturally when one studies the hypergraph C*-algebras of Trieb, Weber and Zenner

is nuclear. The author proved in

It is an easy observation that a bipartite graph C*-algebra

is the complete bipartite graph with two vertices on one side and three vertices on the other side, see

As an application, we use our findings to analyze
2\times 4
magic isometries. Let us first recall their definition from e.g.
[

Definition 1.3
.

Let
m\leq n
and let

be a unital
C^{\ast}
-algebra. An

-matrix

with entries from
A
is called a

if the entries
P_{ij}
are projections which form a partition of unity along rows and are pairwise orthogonal along columns, i.e.

\displaystyle(P_{ij})_{j=1,\dots,n}

\displaystyle\text{ is a partition of unity for each fixed }i\leq m,

\displaystyle(P_{ij})_{i=1,\dots,m}

\displaystyle\text{ is a family of pairwise orthogonal projections for each fixed }j\leq n.

Here, with a partition of unity we mean a family of projections in a unital
C^{\ast}
-algebra that sum up to the unit.

If
m=n
and the projections
P_{ij}
form a partition of unity along both rows and columns, then we call
P
a
magic unitary
.

The following problem was first raised by Banica, Skalski and Sołtan in
[
6

, see also Weber’s introductory article
[
20

.

Problem 1.4
.

Can every
m\times n
-magic isometry be filled up to a magic unitary matrix, i.e. given a magic isometry

P=(P_{ij})_{i\leq m,j\leq n}\in M_{m,n}(A),

where
A=C^{\ast}(P_{ij})
and
m\leq n
, can one find another
C^{\ast}
-algebra
B
and a magic unitary

U=(U_{ij})_{i,j\leq n}\in M_{n}(B)

such that
A
embeds into
B
via
P_{ij}\mapsto U_{ij}
for
i\leq m,j\leq n
?

It turns out that the universal
C^{\ast}
-algebra generated by the entries of a

-magic isometry is the bipartite graph algebra

associated to the three-dimensional cube. On the other hand, a useful matrix model for the universal
C^{\ast}
-algebra generated by the entries of
4\times 4
-magic unitary,

, has been described by Banica and Collins in
[

]
. As already suggested by Banica, Skalski and Sołtan we can use their matrix model and combine it with our description of

to solve the above problem in the affirmative for
m=2
and
n=4
.

Proposition C

(Proposition
7.6
)
.

Every
2\times 4
-magic isometry can be filled up to a
4\times 4
-magic unitary matrix in the sense of Problem
1.4
.

2.1.
Bipartite graph C*-algebras

Bipartite graph C*-algebras have been introduced by the author in a previous paper. We recall their definition shortly and refer to
[

Definition 2.1
.

A bipartite graph
G
consists of two vertex sets
U
and
V
together with an edge set
E\subset\{\{u,v\}\mid u\in U,v\in V\}
. We write
G=(U,V,E)

For two vertices in a bipartite graph

let us write

if

and let
\mathcal{N}(u):=\{v\in V\mid v\sim u\}
(
\mathcal{N}(v):=\{u\in U\mid v\sim u\}
) be the neighbors of
u
(
v
). For vertices

or edges

we also write
x\in G
or
e\in G
, respectively, and say that
x
or
e
are contained in
G
.

A path
\mu
in a bipartite graph
G
is a finite sequence of vertices
x_{1}\dots x_{n}
such that
x_{i}\sim x_{i+1}
holds for all
i<n
. For every
i<n
, we say that
x_{i}
is contained in
\mu
. We set
s(\mu)=x_{1}
and

. For two paths

and

with

we write
\mu\nu
for the combined path
x_{1}\dots x_{m}y_{1}\dots y_{n}
.

Subgraphs and induced subgraphs of a bipartite graph are defined in the natural way, for more details see
[
16
, Definition 2.3]
. Examples of bipartite graphs include the complete bipartite graphs
K_{m,n}
for
m,n\in\mathbb{N}

Let us next recall the definition of bipartite graph C*-algebras.

Definition 2.2

(
[
16
, Definition 2.4]
)
.

Given a bipartite graph
G=(U,V,E)
let

be the universal

-algebra generated by a family of projections
(p_{x})_{x\in U\cup V}
subject to the following relations:

(GP1)

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in U}p_{u}

\displaystyle=1=\sum_{v\in V}p_{v},

(GP2)

\displaystyle p_{u}p_{v}

\displaystyle=0\;\text{ if }\{u,v\}\not\in E.

The following dense subset of

is immediate.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
16
, Proposition 2.6]
)

Let
G
be a bipartite graph and for every path

Hypercubes

In this section we give a precise definition of the hypercubes

The following notation will be crucial throughout this paper.

Notation 2.4

For every number

such that

the

\displaystyle i_{n-1}\dots i_{1}i_{0}\in\{0,1\}^{n}\quad\text{ such that }\quad i=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}i_{k}2^{k}.

\displaystyle\mathrm{par}_{k}(i)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}i_{\ell}\mod 2.

Note that

is the parity of the number

Further, for every

be the number obtained by flipping the

-th binary digit of

\displaystyle i\#k=\begin{cases}i+2^{k}&\text{if }i_{k}=0,\\

Note that we will be using square brackets
[

]
both to denote digits of a binary number

The

\displaystyle U_{n}=\{i<2^{n}\mid\mathrm{par}_{n-1}(i)=0\},\qquad V_{n}=\{j<2^{n}\mid\mathrm{par}_{n-1}(j)=1\}

\displaystyle E_{n}=\{\{i,j\}\mid i\in U_{n},\;j\in V_{n},\;i\#k=j\text{ for some }k<n\}.

We denote the edge

and

3.
Hypercube C*-algebras are subhomogeneous

In this section we prove that every hypercube C*-algebra
C^{\ast}(Q_{n})
is subhomogeneous, and more particularly, for every irreducible representation
\rho
of
C^{\ast}(Q_{n})
and projection
p_{x}
we have that
\rho(p_{x})
is either zero or a rank-one projection. We prove this by analyzing paths in the hypercube with the aim of showing

\displaystyle p_{\mu}p_{\nu}=p_{\nu}p_{\mu}

for any two loops
\mu
and
\nu
in
Q_{n}
.

To do that, let us describe a path
\mu
in
Q_{n}
in another way. Given
\mu=x_{1}\dots x_{m+1}
with vertices
x_{1},\dots,x_{m+1}\in U_{n}\cup V_{n}
one can choose indices
k_{1},\dots,k_{m}<n
such that

\displaystyle x_{i+1}=x_{i}\#k_{i}

holds for all
i\leq m
. In operational terms, the index
k_{i}
records in which direction the path
\mu
continues at the vertex
x_{i}
. It is possible to recover the whole path
\mu
from the starting vertex
x_{1}
and the sequence
k_{1}\dots k_{m}
. Therefore, we may use the following notation.

Notation 3.1
.

Given a vertex
x\in U_{n}\cup V_{n}
and a sequence of indices
k_{1},\dots,k_{m}<n
we write

\displaystyle[x;\,k_{1}\dots k_{m}]

for the path
\mu=x_{1}\dots x_{m+1}
given by

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=x,

and

\displaystyle x_{i+1}

\displaystyle=x_{i}\#k_{i}\quad\text{for }i\leq m.

Proposition 3.2
.

(1)

For every path
\mu
in
Q_{n}
there is a unique vertex
x\in U_{n}\cup V_{n}
and a unique sequence
k_{1}\dots k_{m}
of indices with
k_{i}<n
such that

\displaystyle\mu=[x;\,k_{1}\dots k_{m}].

(2)

The path
[x;\,k_{1}\dots k_{m}]
is a loop if and only if for every
\ell<n
the set
\{i\leq m\mid k_{i}=\ell\}
has an even number of elements.

Proof.

The first statement follows from the definition of edges in
Q_{n}
. The second statement is true since the
\ell
-th binary digit of the endpoint of

is equal to

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mu=[x;\,k_{1}\dots k_{m}]
and
\nu=[x;\,\ell_{1}\dots\ell_{m^{\prime}}]
be two loops in

starting at

. Then
p_{\mu}p_{\nu}=p_{\nu}p_{\mu}
.

Proof.

Evidently, we have

\displaystyle p_{\mu}p_{\nu}

\displaystyle=p_{[x;\,k_{1}\dots k_{m}\ell_{1}\dots\ell_{m^{\prime}}]}

as well as

\displaystyle p_{\nu}p_{\mu}

\displaystyle=p_{[x;\,\ell_{1}\dots\ell_{m^{\prime}}k_{1}\dots k_{m}]}.

From the previous lemma we obtain immediately

\displaystyle p_{\mu}p_{\nu}=\pm p_{\nu}p_{\mu}.

It only remains to check that the sign on the right-hand side of this identity is positive. To see this, note that the path
[x;\,k_{1}\dots k_{m}]
is a loop. Therefore, every
\ell<n
features an even number of times in the sequence

\displaystyle p_{[x;\,k_{1}\dots k_{m}\ell_{1}\dots\ell_{m^{\prime}}]}=p_{[x;\,\ell_{1}k_{1}\dots k_{m}\ell_{2}\dots\ell_{m^{\prime}}]}.

Repeating this argument successively for the indices
\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{m^{\prime}}
yields the claim.

Lemma 4.6

Let

. However, two different edge weightings can define unitarily equivalent representations. Therefore, we introduce below a particular class of edge weightings

The definition below is a straight generalization of the edge weightings of

from Example

Definition 4.7
.

For every tuple
\mathbf{t}=[t_{0},\dots,t_{n-1}]\in\Delta_{n-1}
let an edge weighting

of

be given by

\displaystyle c_{\mathbf{t}}(ij)=(-1)^{\mathrm{par}_{k}(i)}\sqrt{t_{k}}\qquad\text{ for all }ij\in E_{n},

.

The representation
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
in the previous definition is well-defined, i.e. the edge weighting

is admissible for all
\mathbf{t}\in\Delta_{n-1}
and its induced subgraph

satisfies

.

Proof.

Let us first check

. Since
\mathbf{t}=[t_{0},\dots,t_{n-1}]\in\Delta_{n-1}
satisfies
\sum_{k}t_{k}=1
, there exists at least one index
\ell
with

. Consequently, for every

one has

if one chooses

. Thus,
i
is contained in the subgraph
Q_{n}(c_{\mathbf{t}})
, and it follows that
U_{n}(c_{\mathbf{t}})=U_{n}
.

It remains to check that

satisfies the conditions (
3
) and (
4
) from Definition
4.1
. One immediately observes for any fixed

and

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in U_{n}}|c_{\mathbf{t}}(ij)|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}t_{k}=1=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}t_{k}=\sum_{j\in V_{n}}|c_{\mathbf{t}}(ij)|^{2}.

On the other hand, for distinct

recall from Proposition

that there are either no or exactly two common neighbors

Then it is

\displaystyle c(i_{1}j_{1})

\displaystyle\overline{c(i_{1}j_{2})}+c(i_{2}j_{1})\overline{c(i_{2}j_{2})}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\mathrm{par}_{\ell}(i_{1})+\mathrm{par}_{k}(i_{1})}\sqrt{t_{\ell}}\sqrt{t_{k}}+(-1)^{\mathrm{par}_{k}(i_{2})+\mathrm{par}_{\ell}(i_{2})}\sqrt{t_{k}}\sqrt{t_{\ell}}.

One checks that the two terms in this sum have opposite signs, and therefore the sum vanishes. Indeed, the binary representation of

and

\mathrm{par}_{k}(i_{1})=\mathrm{par}_{k}(i_{2})
.
Thus, (

∎

Lemma 4.11

For

\displaystyle=(y\#0)^{(+\ell)}=(\tilde{i}\#0)^{(+\ell)}\#k=i\#k,

5.
Irreducible representations of hypercube C*-algebras

Building on the previous sections, we show that any irreducible representation of the hypercube algebra
C^{\ast}(Q_{n})
is unitarily equivalent to a subrepresentation of the representation
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
from Definition
4.7
for some point
\mathbf{t}=[t_{0},\dots,t_{n-1}]\in\Delta_{n-1}
. We call a representation
\rho_{1}
a subrepresentation of a representation
\rho
if there is a third representation
\rho_{2}
such that
\rho=\rho_{1}\oplus\rho_{2}
holds.

Let us briefly outline the proof strategy. We know from the previous sections that every irreducible representation
\rho
is unitarily equivalent to a representation
\rho_{c}
induced by an admissible edge weighting
c:E_{n}\to\mathbb{C}
. Further, since
\rho_{c}
is irreducible, the induced subgraph
Q_{n}(c)\subset Q_{n}
is a single sub-hypercube of
Q_{n}
. It will turn out that it suffices to prove
\rho_{c}\cong\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
for suitable
\mathbf{t}\in\Delta_{n-1}
in the case where
Q_{n}(c)=Q_{n}
. In this case the edge weighting
c
is nowhere-vanishing, and we prove the statement in two steps:

•

First, in Lemma
5.1
we show that any nowhere-vanishing admissible weighting
c
satisfies
|c(ij)|=\sqrt{t_{k}}
for all
ij\in E_{n}
with
i=j\#k
for a suitable point
\mathbf{t}=[t_{0},\dots,t_{n-1}]\in\Delta_{n-1}
.

•

Then, in Lemma
5.2
we prove that such a representation
\rho_{c}
is unitarily equivalent to
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
. For that we use Lemma
4.6
(2) which says that
\rho_{c}\cong\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
holds if and only if there are
\lambda_{x}\in S^{1}
for
x\in U_{n}(c)\cup V_{n}(c)
such that

\displaystyle c(ij)=\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}c_{\mathbf{t}}(ij)\qquad\text{ for all }ij\in E_{n}(c).

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that

is a nowhere-vanishing admissible edge weighting of the hypercube
Q_{n}
, i.e.

for all edges

.

where

Proof.

Since

is nowhere-vanishing, we have

. Let us set

\displaystyle t_{k}=|c(0[0\#k])|^{2}

for all

. Note hat

It remains to show

∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Let

be an irreducible representation of the hypercube algebra

. Further, since
\rho

of
Q_{n}
.

First assume
Q_{n}(c)=Q_{n}
. Then
c
is nowhere-vanishing, and by Lemma
5.2
there is a
\mathbf{t}\in\Delta_{n-1}
such that
\rho_{c}
is unitarily equivalent to
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
.

If
Q_{n}(c)\subsetneq Q_{n}
, then by Lemma
2.9
(1) there is some
\ell<n
such that
Q_{n}(c)\subset Q_{n}^{(+\ell)}
or
Q_{n}(c)\subset Q_{n}^{(-\ell)}
, where
Q_{n}^{(\pm\ell)}
is the sub-hypercube induced by all vertices
x\in U_{n}\cup V_{n}
with
[x]_{\ell}=0
or
[x]_{\ell}=1
depending on the sign
\pm
. Let us assume
Q_{n}\subset Q_{n}^{(+\ell)}
. In this case,
\rho_{c}
factors through the surjective map
\pi^{(+\ell)}:C^{\ast}(Q_{n})\to C^{\ast}(Q_{n-1})
from Notation
4.10
since
\rho_{c}
vanishes on all
p_{x}
with
[x]_{\ell}=0
. Thus, there is an irreducible representation
\sigma:C^{\ast}(Q_{n-1})\to B(\mathcal{H})
with

\displaystyle\rho_{c}=\sigma\circ\pi^{(+\ell)}.

By the induction hypothesis there is some
\mathbf{s}\in\Delta_{n-2}
such that
\sigma
is unitarily equivalent to a subrepresentation of
\rho_{\mathbf{s}}
. Consequently,
\rho_{c}
is unitarily equivalent to a subrepresentation of
\rho_{\mathbf{s}}\circ\pi^{(+\ell)}
. However, this is again a subrepresentation of
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
for
\mathbf{t}=[s_{0},\dots,s_{\ell-1},0,s_{\ell},\dots,s_{n-2}]\in\Delta_{n-1}
by Lemma
4.11
.
∎

6.
Explicit description of hypercube C*-algebras

Finally, we are ready to describe the hypercube C*-algebras
C^{\ast}(Q_{n})
explicitly as algebra of continuous functions on the simplex
\Delta_{n-1}
. First, let us introduce a useful notation.

Notation 6.1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{t}=[t_{0},\dots,t_{n-1}]\in\Delta_{n-1}
let

Q_{n}(\mathbf{t})\subset Q_{n}

be the subgraph obtained by removing all edges
ij\in E_{n}^{(k)}
with
t_{k}=0
, where
E_{n}^{(k)}=\{ij\in E_{n}\mid i=j\#k\}
as in Notation
2.8
. Evidently,
Q_{n}(\mathbf{t})
partitions
Q_{n}
into a disjoint union of sub-hypercubes, where
Q_{n}(\mathbf{t})
consists of
2^{\alpha}
-sub-hypercubes of size
2^{n-1-\alpha}
if
\mathbf{t}
has
\alpha
zeros (see the proof of Lemma
2.9
(1)).

We say that a matrix
A\in M_{U_{n}}
is in
\mathbf{t}
-block diagonal form
if it satisfies

\displaystyle A_{i_{1}i_{2}}=0\quad\text{whenever }i_{1},i_{2}\text{ are in distinct connected components of }Q_{n}(\mathbf{t}).

In this case every block of
A
corresponds to a connected component of
Q_{n}(\mathbf{t})
.

Example 6.2
.

As an example consider the square
Q_{2}
as sketched in Figure
4.5
. For
\mathbf{t}=[1,0]=[t,\bar{t}]\in\Delta_{1}
the graph
Q_{2}(\mathbf{t})
consists of the two horizontal edges. Both connected components contain exactly one vertex from
U_{2}
and thus a matrix
A\in M_{U_{2}}\cong M_{2}
is in
\mathbf{t}
-block diagonal form if and only if it is diagonal.

Lemma 6.3
.

For every
\mathbf{t}=[t_{0},\dots,t_{n-1}]\in\Delta_{n-1}
and
x\in U_{n}\cup V_{n}
the projection
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}(p_{x})\in M_{U_{n}}
is in
\mathbf{t}
-block diagonal form where
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}:C^{\ast}(Q_{n})\to M_{U_{n}}
is the representation from Definition
4.7
.

Proof.

For
i\in U_{n}
we have
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}(p_{i})=E_{ii}
, which is clearly in
\mathbf{t}
-block diagonal form. For
j\in V_{n}
recall that
\rho(p_{j})
is the projection onto the span of the vector
\psi_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{U_{n}}
given by

\displaystyle[\psi_{j}]_{i}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\mathrm{par}_{k}(i)}\sqrt{t_{k}},&\text{ if }j=i\#k\text{ for some }k<n,\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

If
i,i^{\prime}\in U_{n}
are in different connected components of
Q_{n}(\mathbf{t})
, then there are two possibilities: If
j
is not a common neighbor of
i
and
i^{\prime}
in
Q_{n}
, then
[\psi_{j}]_{i}=0
or
[\psi_{j}]_{i^{\prime}}=0
. In any event it follows that the
(i,i^{\prime})
-th entry of
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}(p_{j})
vanishes. If, on the other hand,
j
is a common neighbor of
i
and
i^{\prime}
, then there are
\ell,k<n
with
j=i\#\ell
and
j=i^{\prime}\#k
. Since
i
and
i^{\prime}
are in different connected components of
Q_{n}(\mathbf{t})
, we must have
t_{k}=0
or
t_{\ell}=0
. Again this entails that the
(i,i^{\prime})
-th entry of
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}(p_{j})
vanishes.
Thus,
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}(p_{j})
is in
\mathbf{t}
-block diagonal form.
∎

Theorem 6.4
.

The C*-algebra
C^{\ast}(Q_{n})
is isomorphic to the algebra of continuous functions
f:\Delta_{n-1}\to M_{U_{n}}\cong M_{2^{n-1}}
such that
f(\mathbf{t})
is in
\mathbf{t}
-block diagonal form for all
\mathbf{t}\in\Delta_{n-1}
.

Proof.

For every
\mathbf{t}\in\Delta_{n-1}
and
x\in U_{n}\cup V_{n}
let
p_{x}^{\mathbf{t}}:=\rho_{\mathbf{t}}(p_{x})\in M_{U_{n}}
. One readily checks that the map
\Delta_{n-1}\to M_{U_{n}}
given by
\mathbf{t}\mapsto p_{x}^{\mathbf{t}}
is continuous for all
x\in U_{n}\cup V_{n}
. Thus, the universal property of
C^{\ast}(Q_{n})
yields a
\ast
-homomorphism

\displaystyle\varphi:C^{\ast}(Q_{n})\to C(\Delta_{n-1},M_{U_{n}}),\quad p_{x}\mapsto(p_{x}^{\mathbf{t}})_{\mathbf{t}\in\Delta_{n-1}}.

It remains to show that
\varphi
is injective and that its image consists of all continuous functions
f:\Delta_{n-1}\to M_{U_{n}}
such that
f(\mathbf{t})
is in
\mathbf{t}
-block diagonal form for all
\mathbf{t}\in\Delta_{n-1}
.

To show injectivity, it suffices to note that every irreducible representation
\rho
of
C^{\ast}(Q_{n})
factors through
\varphi
. Indeed, by Theorem
5.3
every irreducible representation is unitarily equivalent to a subrepresentation of some
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
for
\mathbf{t}\in\Delta_{n-1}
.

For surjectivity, first observe that for every
\mathbf{t}\in\Delta_{n-1}
and
x\in U_{n}\cup V_{n}
the projection
p_{x}^{\mathbf{t}}
is in
\mathbf{t}
-block diagonal form by Lemma
6.3
.
On the other hand, whenever
i,i^{\prime}\in U_{n}
are in the same connected component of
Q_{n}(\mathbf{t})
, then there is a path
i=i_{1}j_{1}i_{2}j_{2}\dots j_{m-1}i_{m}=i^{\prime}
in
Q_{n}(\mathbf{t})
that connects
i
and
i^{\prime}
. Then one has

\displaystyle p_{i_{1}}^{\mathbf{t}}p_{j_{1}}^{\mathbf{t}}p_{i_{2}}^{\mathbf{t}}p_{j_{2}}^{\mathbf{t}}\dots p_{j_{m-1}}^{\mathbf{t}}p_{i_{m}}^{\mathbf{t}}\neq 0,

and the matrix on the left-hand side has a single non-zero entry at the
(i,i^{\prime})
-th position. Thus,

\displaystyle\varphi(\Delta_{n-1})|_{\mathbf{t}}:=\{\varphi(x)|_{\mathbf{t}}\mid x\in C^{\ast}(Q_{n})\}\subset M_{U_{n}}

is the algebra of matrices in
\mathbf{t}
-block diagonal form. Now, the Stone-Weierstrass theorem for C*-algebras implies that
\varphi
is surjective, see e.g.
[
8
, Theorem 11.1.8]
.
∎

7.
Application to magic isometries

In this final section we apply our findings about the
C^{\ast}
-algebra of the cube
Q_{3}
in order to solve a problem about magic unitaries. We discussed this problem and its background from
[
6
, Section 5]
in the introduction. Recall the notions of an
n\times m
-magic isometry and an
n\times n
-magic unitary from Definition
1.3
.

The general question is whether a given
n\times m
-magic isometry can be completed to an
n\times n
-magic unitary by adding further projections. In this section we investigate the particular case of a
2\times 4
-magic isometry and its completion to a
4\times 4
-magic unitary. Thus, the problem we solve is the following.

Problem 7.1
.

Can every
2\times 4
-magic isometry
P=(P_{ij})_{i=1,2;j=1,2,3,4}\subset M_{2,4}(A)
with entries in a
C^{\ast}
-algebra
A=C^{\ast}(P_{ij})
be filled up to a
4\times 4
-magic unitary
U=(U_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{4}\subset M_{4}(B)
with entries in another
C^{\ast}
-algebra
B
in the sense that there is an embedding
A\hookrightarrow B
with
P_{ij}\mapsto U_{ij}
for
i=1,2
and
j=1,2,3,4
?

The following is a simple observation which connects
2\times 4
-magic isometries to the hypercube
C^{\ast}
-algebra
C^{\ast}(Q_{3})
.

Proposition 7.2
.

The universal
C^{\ast}
-algebra generated by the entries
p_{ij}
with
i=1,2
and
j=1,2,3,4
of a
2\times 4
-magic isometry is isomorphic to the hypercube C*-algebra
C^{\ast}(Q_{3})
via

\displaystyle p_{11}\mapsto p_{000},

\displaystyle p_{21}\mapsto p_{111},

\displaystyle p_{12}\mapsto p_{011},

\displaystyle p_{22}\mapsto p_{100},

\displaystyle p_{13}\mapsto p_{110},

\displaystyle p_{23}\mapsto p_{001},

\displaystyle p_{14}\mapsto p_{101},

\displaystyle p_{24}\mapsto p_{010},

where the
p_{x}
on the right-hand side are the canonical generators of
C^{\ast}(Q_{3})
as in Example
4.9
.

In what follows we will identify the two C*-algebras from the previous proposition and denote the canonical generators of
C^{\ast}(Q_{3})
by
p_{ij}
with
i=1,2
and
j=1,2,3,4
.

Recall that the universal C*-algebra generated by the entries of a
4\times 4
-magic unitary
u=(u_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{4}
is known as
C(S_{4}^{+})
, the underlying C*-algebra of the quantum permutation group on four points introduced by Wang in
[
19
]
. This C*-algebra is well-understood, see e.g.
[
4
,
5
,
2
,
1
,
10
,
11
]
.

As suggested in
[
6
]
, we will make use of the following faithful representation of
C(S_{4}^{+})
which is due to Banica and Collins, see also
[
4
]
.

Theorem 7.3

(
[
3
, Theorem 4.1]
)
.

Let

\displaystyle c_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\;c_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\;c_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\;c_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

be the Pauli matrices, and for every matrix
X\in M_{2}
let
\Pi(X)\in M_{4}
be the projection onto
\mathrm{span}(X)\subset M_{2}
after identifying the Hilbert spaces
M_{2}
and
\mathbb{C}^{4}
.

The
\ast
-homomorphism
\Phi:C(S_{4}^{+})\to C(\mathrm{SU}(2),M_{4})
given by

\displaystyle\Phi:u_{ij}\mapsto(\Pi(c_{i}xc_{j}))_{x\in\mathrm{SU}(2)}=:(\sigma_{x}(u_{ij}))_{x\in\mathrm{SU}(2)}\quad\text{ for }i,j\leq 4,

defines a faithful representation of
C(S_{4}^{+})
.

We can reformulate Problem
7.1
in terms of universal C*-algebras as follows.

Lemma 7.4
.

Problem
7.1
has an affirmative answer if the following condition (
\ast
) holds: Every irreducible representation of
C^{\ast}(Q_{3})
factors through the unique
\ast
-homomorphism
\varphi:C^{\ast}(Q_{3})\to C(S_{4}^{+})
with

\displaystyle\varphi:p_{ij}\mapsto u_{ij}\quad\text{ for all }i\leq 2,j\leq 4,

where we identify the generators of
C^{\ast}(Q_{3})
with the
p_{ij}
as in Proposition
7.2
.

This means that whenever
\rho:C^{\ast}(Q_{3})\to B(\mathcal{H})
is an irreducible representation, then there exists a representation
\sigma:C(S_{4}^{+})\to B(\mathcal{H})
such that

\displaystyle\rho=\sigma\circ\varphi.

Note that the existence and uniqueness of the map
\varphi
follows from the universal property of
C(S_{4}^{+})
.

Proof.

Let
(P_{ij})_{i=1,2;j=1,2,3,4}\subset M_{2,4}(A)
be a magic isometry where
A=C^{\ast}(P_{ij})
. The elements
P_{ij}
satisfy the relations (
GP1
) and (
GP2
); therefore there is a unique (surjective)
\ast
-homomorphism
\pi:C^{\ast}(Q_{3})\to A
with
\pi(p_{ij})=P_{ij}
for
i=1,2
and
j=1,2,3,4
. Let
I:=\mathrm{ker}(\pi)
be the kernel of
\pi
, and let
J\subset C(S_{4}^{+})
be the closed ideal generated by
\varphi(I)
with quotient map
\pi_{J}:C(S_{4}^{+})\to C(S_{4}^{+})/J
. Since
\pi_{J}\circ\varphi
vanishes on
I
, it factors through
\pi
as in the diagram below.
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C^{\ast}(Q_{3})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C(S_{4}^{+})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C(S_{4}^{+})/J}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{J}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}

Evidently, the magic isometry
(P_{ij})_{i\leq 2;j\leq 4}
can be filled up to the magic unitary
(\pi_{J}(u_{ij}))_{i,j\leq 4}
if the map
\psi
is injective.

To prove that this is true, it suffices to show that every irreducible representation
\rho^{\prime}
of
A
factors through
\psi
. But that is implied by (
*
) as follows. If
\rho^{\prime}:A\to B(\mathcal{H})
is irreducible, then the same goes for
\rho:=\rho^{\prime}\circ\pi_{I}
. By (

) that representation factors as
\rho=\sigma\circ\varphi
for a suitable irreducible representation
\sigma:C(S_{4}^{+})\to B(\mathcal{H})

\rho

and therefore factors through

as

. This means that
\rho^{\prime}
factors through

as
\rho^{\prime}=\sigma^{\prime}\circ\psi
.
∎

To prove that condition (
\ast
) in Lemma
7.4
is true, we combine our knowledge about
C^{\ast}(Q_{3})
with the faithful representations of
C(S_{4}^{+})
due to Banica and Collins mentioned above.
Indeed, all we need is a technical computation.

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
\mathbf{t}=[a^{2},b^{2},c^{2}]\in\Delta_{2}
with
a,b,c\geq 0
be given and set

\displaystyle x:=\begin{pmatrix}is&t+iu\\
-t+iu&-is\end{pmatrix}\in M_{2},

where

\displaystyle s:=\frac{a}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{c+1}},

\displaystyle t:=\frac{b}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{c+1}},

\displaystyle u:=\sqrt{\frac{c+1}{2}}.

Then
x\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
and
\sigma_{x}\circ\varphi
is unitarily equivalent to
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
. Here
\sigma_{x}
is the representation of
C^{\ast}(S_{4}^{+})
from the previous theorem and
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
is the representation of
C^{\ast}(Q_{3})
induced by the weighting
c_{\mathbf{t}}
from Definition
4.7
.

Proof.

For
x\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
it suffices to check that
s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2}=1
. Indeed,

\displaystyle s^{2}+t^{2}+u^{2}=\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2(c+1)}+\frac{c+1}{2}=\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}+(c+1)^{2}}{2(c+1)}=\frac{1+2c+1}{2(c+1)}=1.

Next, recall that
\Pi(c_{i}xc_{j})
is the projection onto
\mathrm{span}(c_{i}xc_{j})
after identifying
M_{2}
with
\mathbb{C}^{4}
. The obtained representation
\sigma_{x}
is unitarily equivalent to
\sigma_{x}^{\prime}
given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}^{\prime}:u_{ij}\mapsto\Pi(x^{\ast}c_{i}xc_{j}),

for
x^{\ast}
is a unitary transformation.
Indeed, if
X\in M_{4}
is any unitary matrix, then the map
\mathbb{C}^{4}\cong M_{2}\ni A\mapsto XA\in M_{2}\cong\mathbb{C}^{4}
is a unitary transformation.
For the identification of
M_{2}
and
\mathbb{C}^{4}
we may use the Pauli matrices and identify

\displaystyle x_{1}c_{1}+x_{2}c_{2}+x_{3}c_{3}+x_{4}c_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
x_{3}\\
x_{4}\end{pmatrix}.

Then a calculation confirms

\displaystyle x^{\ast}c_{1}xc_{4}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle x^{\ast}c_{1}xc_{3}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\\
0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle x^{\ast}c_{1}xc_{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle x^{\ast}c_{1}xc_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle x^{\ast}c_{2}xc_{4}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}a\\
ib\\
-ic\\
0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle x^{\ast}c_{2}xc_{3}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}b\\
-ia\\
0\\
ic\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle x^{\ast}c_{2}xc_{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}c\\
0\\
ia\\
-ib\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle x^{\ast}c_{2}xc_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
c\\
b\\
a\end{pmatrix},

using

\displaystyle c=2u^{2}-1=u^{2}-s^{2}-t^{2},

\displaystyle b=2tu,

\displaystyle a=2su.

By applying the unitary transformation
U=\mathrm{diag}(1,-i,i,-i)
the vectors in the second row are transformed into

\displaystyle\varphi_{24}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}a\\

0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\varphi_{23}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}b\\
-a\\
0\\

\displaystyle\varphi_{22}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}c\\

-a\\

\displaystyle\varphi_{21}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-ic\\

Thus, a comparison with the vectors
\psi_{j}
for

from Example
4.9
immediately yields that
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
and
\sigma_{x}
are unitarily equivalent.
∎

Proposition 7.6
.

The statement from Problem
7.1
is true.

Proof.

By Lemma
7.4
it suffices to show that every irreducible representation
\rho:C^{\ast}(Q_{3})\to B(\mathcal{H})
factors through
\varphi
.
However, by Theorem
5.3
every irreducible representation of
C^{\ast}(Q_{3})
is unitarily equivalent to a subrepresentation of some
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
with
\mathbf{t}\in\Delta_{2}
. Further, by Lemma
7.5
every
\rho_{\mathbf{t}}
factors through
\varphi
.
∎
