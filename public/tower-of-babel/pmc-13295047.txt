Astrocyte-Targeted Biomarkers for Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide-Related Neurovascular Disorders: Insights from Calcium and Metabolic Profiling
Authors: Thipjutha Phatruengdet, Borwonwong Niraso, Isara Phiwchai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Advancing our understanding of astrocyte-mediated neurochemical communication and the role that calcium plays in astrocyte function may ultimately enable the development of strategies for diagnosing, treating, and preventing conditions that involve astrocyte dysfunction, including calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)-based neurovascular disorders. We investigated the utility of intracellular calcium concentration and metabolic changes as potential indicators of astrocyte dysfunction. We used CGRP to induce cellular dysfunction in CTX-TNA2 astrocytes and examined the relationship between astrocyte dysfunction and intracellular calcium levels using inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry. In addition, we compared the metabolic and neurochemical profiles of untreated (control) astrocytes with those of CGRP- and CaCl2-treated astrocytes. Higher intracellular calcium concentrations were observed in the CGRP-treated astrocytes. Glucose metabolites exhibited chemical shifts in the control and CaCl2-treated astrocytes but were less evident in CGRP-treated astrocytes, suggesting potential alterations in glucose metabolism. The CGRP-treated astrocytes also had significantly lower levels of creatine (Cr), the glutamate–glutamine complex (Glx), and myo-inositol than the control astrocytes. The Cr and myo-inositol levels were significantly higher in the CaCl2-treated astrocytes than in the control astrocytes. Our findings suggest that monitoring the intracellular calcium concentration and metabolic profile can be a valuable approach for detecting early astrocyte dysfunction. The metabolic and neurochemical changes induced by CGRP highlight the potential of astrocyte-targeted strategies for mitigating CGRP-related neurovascular disorders.

Introduction

Calcium, the fifth most abundant element in the human body, plays crucial roles in the signal transduction pathways that control cell survival, proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. In the central nervous system, it regulates neuronal and astrocytic functions, including neurotransmitter release, synaptic plasticity, membrane excitability, gene transcription, and cell proliferation and death. − The influx of calcium through voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs) following an action potential triggers neurotransmitter release via SNARE-mediated vesicle fusion, while calcium entry through ligand-gated receptors and release from intracellular stores modulate synaptic strength through long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD). Tight regulation of intracellular calcium levels by coordinated influx, storage, and efflux mechanisms is essential for normal neuronal function and synaptic integrity. , Neural communication is partly governed by the regulation of calcium movement across neuronal membranes, and intracellular calcium signaling within neurons influences neurotransmission. Previous studies have shown that impaired calcium homeostasis disrupts numerous cellular processes and is a principal pathological mechanism underlying neurodegenerative and neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, epilepsy, and migraines. ,, Disruption of calcium signaling between the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, and progressive neuronal degeneration in Alzheimer’s disease. Sustained elevations in intracellular calcium also promotes tau hyperphosphorylation, a key factor contributing to synaptic structural deterioration and impaired neuronal communication. Moreover, calcium directly influences amyloid-β (Aβ) aggregation, as disturbances in calcium homeostasis facilitate the formation of neurotoxic Aβ oligomers. Dysfunction of calcium channels leads to elevated intracellular calcium levels in neurons and excessive glutamate release, thereby triggering cortical spreading depression (CSD) through the activity of presynaptic P/Q-type calcium channels, a key mechanism in migraine initiation.

Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) is a multifunctional neuropeptide that has been shown to promote vasodilation, pain transmission, and neuroinflammation. ,− It serves as a potent vasodilator and neurotransmitter in the peripheral and central nervous systems and reportedly affords protection against several cardiovascular diseases. , CGRP triggers an increase in intracellular calcium levels in astrocytes by promoting the release of calcium from internal stores, mainly the endoplasmic reticulum, through the stimulation of inositol trisphosphate receptors (IP3R), while also influencing the activity of ion channels in the plasma membrane. These changes in calcium signaling induced by CGRP play a crucial role in the release of gliotransmitters, such as glutamate, ATP, and d-serine, thus influencing synaptic transmission and neuronal excitability in the adjacent neural microenvironment. ,, CGRP has a proven role in migraines, and selective CGRP antagonists and anti-CGRP antibodies are now in clinical use for migraine treatment. CGRP binds to specific receptors and is widely distributed in the body, particularly in small-diameter dorsal root ganglion neurons, trigeminal ganglion neurons, and nerve fibers controlling the spinal cord and brainstem dorsal horn.

Astrocytes, a subtype of glial cells in the brain and spinal cord, support essential physiological functions, including the maintenance of neurotransmitter pools, cellular health and metabolism (by providing trophic support), synapse formation and plasticity, myelin sheath formation, injury healing, and immune surveillance. , In addition, they are active players in brain energy delivery, production, utilization, and storage. Astrocytes respond to neurotransmitters by increasing the intracellular calcium level, which triggers the release of gliotransmitters, including ATP and glutamate. These gliotransmitters can modulate synaptic transmission and contribute to the regulation of neuronal excitability. Astrocytes rely on tightly regulated intracellular calcium signaling to mediate neurovascular coupling, gliotransmission, and responses to injury.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is a noninvasive and nonselective analytical technique that enables the investigation of molecular composition and structure, forming the foundation of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) methodologies developed using whole-body magnets. Building upon this principle, magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) allows the in vivo quantification of multiple brain metabolites across different regions, providing critical insights into cellular energetics, neurotransmitter metabolism, and ionic homeostasis in the central nervous system. Implementable on standard clinical MRI systems, MRS enables the detection of key metabolites, including N-acetylaspartate (NAA) as a marker of neuronal integrity, Cr reflecting cellular energy metabolism, choline-containing compounds (Cho) associated with membrane turnover, myo-inositol as an astrocytic marker, Glx and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) representing excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission, respectively, as well as lactate (Lac) as an indicator of anaerobic metabolism. −

Calcium homeostasis is a fundamental determinant of neuronal and glial integrity and is essential for the stable operation of neural circuits. Converging evidence indicates that CGRP critically regulates astrocytic calcium signaling by inducing sustained elevations in intracellular calcium, which are tightly coupled to the activity-dependent release of gliotransmitters, including ATP and glutamate. Through these astrocyte-mediated calcium-dependent mechanisms, CGRP exerts a significant modulatory influence on synaptic transmission and neurotransmitter release dynamics. The aims of this study were to advance our understanding of astrocyte-mediated neurochemical communication and to potentially identify new links between CGRP signaling and brain homeostasis and neuropathology. Hence, the findings of this study have implications for the development of targeted therapies designed to modulate astrocyte function, restore neural balance, and prevent CGRP-related neurovascular dysfunction.

Experimental Methods

Cell culture

CTX-TNA2 astrocytes were purchased from Biomedia (Thailand). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (ATTC, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The CTX-TNA2 astrocyte was used as an in vitro model because it has been widely employed in studies of astrocyte physiology, intracellular calcium signaling, neurochemical metabolism, and mitochondrial function. This model is therefore suitable for investigating CGRP-induced astrocyte dysfunction. ,

Measurement of calcium cytotoxicity

To measure calcium-induced cytotoxicity, the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay was used. This colorimetric assay evaluates cell viability based on metabolic activity. Viable cells with active metabolism convert MTT into formazan, whereas dead cells lose this ability and therefore produce no detectable signal. CTX-TNA2 astrocytes were seeded into a 24-well plate (5 × 104 cells/well) and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 48 h. Then, 1–1,000 μM CaCl2 was added to the wells, and the astrocytes were incubated 24 and 48 h. The culture medium was discarded, and the astrocytes were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and incubated with 200 μL of 5 mg/mL MTT (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) solution at 37 °C for 4 h. Thereafter, the MTT solution was removed, the astrocytes were washed twice with PBS, and the intracellular formazan crystals were dissolved with 500 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 37 °C for 15 min. The absorbance of the formazan solution was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA). Cytotoxicity is expressed as the percentage of cell viability in the treated astrocytes relative to the untreated astrocytes.

Measurement of CGRP cytotoxicity

To measure the cytotoxicity of CGRP (Merch Millipore, Germany) by using MTT assay, CTX-TNA2 astrocytes were seeded into a 24-well plate (5 × 104 cells/well) and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 48 h. Then, 10–2,000 ng/mL CGRP was added to the wells, and the astrocytes were incubated for 30, 60, or 90 min. The culture medium was discarded, and the astrocytes were washed twice with PBS. Thereafter, 200 μL of 5 mg/mL MTT solution was added, and the astrocytes were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The MTT solution was then removed, and the astrocytes were washed twice with PBS. The formazan crystals were dissolved with 500 μL of DMSO at 37 °C for 15 min. The absorbance of the formazan solution was measured as described above. Cytotoxicity is expressed as the percentage of cell viability in the treated astrocytes relative to the untreated astrocytes

Quantification of oxidative stress

To quantify oxidative stress, the 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) assay was used. This assay measures intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. DCFH-DA is oxidized to a fluorescent compound, with the fluorescence intensity being directly proportional to the amount of ROS present and is detected using a microplate reader. CTX-TNA2 astrocytes were seeded into a 6-well plate (1 × 105 cells/well) and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 48 h. Then, 10–1,000 ng/mL CGRP was added to the wells, and the astrocytes were incubated. The culture medium was discarded, and the astrocytes were washed twice with PBS. Thereafter, 0.10 μM DCFH-DA (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added, and the astrocytes were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. The green fluorescence intensity was measured at 535 nm using a microplate reader.

Quantification of intracellular calcium

Intracellular calcium content was quantified using inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) with minor modifications from a previously described method. , This technique is widely used for trace elemental analysis due to its low detection limits, rapid analytical performance, high sensitivity, and ability to simultaneously analyze multiple elements. CTX-TNA2 astrocytes were seeded into a 6-well plate (1 × 105 cells/well) at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 48 h. Then, 10 μM or 100 CaCl2 and 100 ng/mL or 1,000 ng/mL CGRP were added to the wells. After different incubation periods, the culture medium was discarded, and the astrocytes were washed twice with PBS. The astrocytes were detached by trypsinization and washed twice with PBS. Then, they were lysed with a mixed acid solution (1:1 ratio of HCl to HNO3) at 60 °C for 30 min. Finally, the intracellular calcium content was measured using ICP-OES (PerkinElmer, USA).

Determination of metabolic profiles via nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy

CTX-TNA2 astrocytes were seeded into 75 cm2 cell culture flasks (5 × 105 cells/flask) and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 48 h. Then, CGRP and CaCl2 were added to the flasks. After different incubation periods, the culture medium was discarded, and the astrocytes were washed twice with PBS. The astrocytes were detached by trypsinization, washed twice with PBS, transferred to conical tubes at −20 °C, and dehydrated by lyophilization. They were then mixed with 200 μL of deuterium oxide (D2O; CIL, USA) and transferred into 5 mm NMR tubes. Metabolic profiling was performed using a 500 MHz NMR spectrometer (Bruker NEO, Switzerland). For NMR analysis, the spectra presented in the figures represent representative spectra obtained from independent experiments. Each experiment was repeated at least twice to ensure reproducibility.

Determination of neurochemical profiles via magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS)

CTX-TNA2 astrocytes were seeded into a 6-well plate (1 × 105 cells/well) and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 48 h. Then, CGRP and CaCl2 were added to the wells. After different incubation periods, the culture medium was discarded, and the astrocytes were washed twice with PBS. The astrocytes were then detached by trypsinization and washed twice with PBS. Next, the astrocytes were dispersed in agarose gel. Neurochemical profiles were obtained via MRS using a 3.0 T Philips Ingenia magnetic resonance imaging system (Philips, Netherlands) with a 32-channel phase array head coil. For neurochemical profile analysis using MRS, metabolite ratios were calculated from the acquired spectra. The values shown in the figures represent representative results from independent experiments. Each experiment was repeated at least twice to confirm reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 10 was used to perform statistical analyses. Student’s t test was used for comparisons of two experimental groups (control group vs treated group). One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple was used for comparisons test to compare each treatment group with the control group. Two-way ANOVA to assess the effects of treatment and time on cell survival, followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The data are expressed as mean ± SEM, and when p-value was <0.05, the result was considered statistically significant.

Results

When we treated CTX-TNA2 astrocytes with different concentrations of CaCl2, no significant reduction in cell viability was observed following CaCl2 treatment across the tested concentrations, as shown in Figure a. We then proceeded to evaluate the effect of CGRP on the viability of CTX-TNA2 astrocytes by incubating cells with 10–2,000 ng/mL CGRP for 30, 60, or 90 min. As shown in Figure b, CGRP treatment was associated with changes in astrocyte viability across different concentrations and incubation times. Although a general decrease in viability was observed at higher concentrations and longer exposure times, some variability was present at lower concentrations. Therefore, these results suggest that CGRP may influence astrocyte viability under the tested conditions. The reduced viability observed at the lowest CGRP concentration at 60 min may reflect experimental variability or transient cellular responses during early exposure. Regarding the capacity of CGRP to induce ROS, CGRP exposure induced oxidative stress responses in CTX-TNA2 astrocytes (Figure c). We used ICP-OES to measure the intracellular calcium level, and the results showed that the astrocytes treated with CaCl2 exhibited slightly lower calcium levels than the control astrocytes (Figure d). The ICP-OES analysis also revealed that the astrocytes treated with CGRP had higher intracellular calcium levels than the control astrocytes (Figure e).

Discussion

In this study, we examined intracellular calcium concentration and metabolic changes as potential indicators of astrocyte dysfunction. Identifying such indicators could lead to the development of strategies for diagnosing, treating, and preventing conditions that involve astrocyte dysfunction, including CGRP-associated neurovascular disorders. The ultimate aim is to restore neural homeostasis.

We used CGRP to induce cellular dysfunction in CTX-TNA2 astrocytes. An increase in the concentration of CGRP can trigger neuroinflammation, which is a key mechanism in the pathophysiology of neurovascular disorders. Previous studies have shown that oxidative stress can cause significant increases in CGRP levels. , Although oxidative stress was observed following CGRP treatment, the fluorescence intensity did not show a clear dose-dependent pattern across the tested concentration range, suggesting that the oxidative response may reach a plateau at relatively low CGRP concentrations. In terms of its effects on astrocytes, CGRP can trigger signaling cascades that negatively impact membrane permeability and calcium-buffering mechanisms, which can disrupt calcium homeostasis and result in elevated calcium concentrations. Elevated intracellular calcium has been associated with astrocytic dysfunction and impaired neuroprotective capacity. Our results are aligned with these previous findings. Another limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size used for some experimental groups. In particular, the CaCl2-treated astrocytes were analyzed with a limited number of biological replicates (n = 2). Although the observed metabolic trends were consistent, the small sample size may reduce the statistical power for detecting subtle differences in metabolic ratios. Future studies with larger sample sizes will help confirm and strengthen the metabolic findings reported here.

Glucose is the main source of energy in the brain, and a decrease in the energy supply is a causative factor in neurological diseases. ,, Inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction can be induced in low-glucose conditions, and migraines may result from irregularities in brain glucose metabolism, according to recent studies. ,,, When we analyzed the metabolic profiles of control cells and those treated with CaCl2 or CGRP, the NMR spectra suggested potential alterations in glucose-related metabolites following CGRP treatment. However, quantitative metabolite analysis will be required to confirm these observations. Glucose metabolites exhibited chemical shifts in the control and CaCl2-treated astrocytes but were less evident in CGRP-treated astrocytes, suggesting potential alterations in glucose metabolism. This metabolic disturbance may reflect alterations in cellular energy metabolism, potentially involving mitochondrial or glycolytic pathways.

Our analysis of the neurochemical profiles of untreated and CGRP-treated astrocytes identified Cr as a metabolic marker associated with cellular energy status. CaCl2-treated astrocytes exhibited higher Cr levels compared with control astrocytes, suggesting a metabolic profile consistent with preserved energy homeostasis. In contrast, CGRP-treated astrocytes showed reduced Cr levels relative to controls, which may reflect altered energy metabolism, as inferred from changes in neurochemical signatures. This is in line with the findings of previous MRS studies showing that CGRP causes abnormal energy metabolism and mitochondrial failure. ,, In addition, CGRP-induced changes in metabolite ratios are reportedly related to inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction. ,,

The significantly lower Cr, Glx, and myo-inositol levels in the CGRP-treated astrocytes underscore the metabolic vulnerability induced by CGRP. Lower Cr levels may reflect compromised energy metabolism, as Cr plays a critical role in maintaining cellular ATP homeostasis. Lower Glx levels suggest impaired neurotransmitter cycling or glutamate–glutamine shuttling between neurons and astrocytes. Lower levels of myo-inositol, a marker of glial integrity, further indicate astrocytic dysfunction. In contrast, the CaCl2-treated astrocytes exhibited higher levels of Cr and myo-inositol than the control astrocytes, which may reflect the occurrence of a compensatory response to calcium-induced stress that promoted energy buffering and osmoregulatory adjustments. This provides further evidence that intracellular calcium homeostasis is important for normal biochemical and metabolic functions and is in accordance with the finding that migraine headaches are related to calcium ion levels in hypercalcemic patients. An increased risk of migraines has also been shown to be associated with elevated blood calcium levels. Taken together, these findings suggest that calcium dysregulation and altered metabolic profiles may serve as potential indicators of astrocyte dysfunction.

Despite the promising findings, the present study quantified intracellular calcium using ICP-OES, which provides total intracellular calcium levels but does not allow real-time monitoring of calcium dynamics or subcellular localization such as mitochondrial calcium. Future studies using fluorescent indicator or live-cell calcium imaging and mitochondrial-specific assays will further clarify the role of calcium signaling in CGRP-induced astrocyte dysfunction.

Conclusions

Our findings show that CGRP-induced dysfunction in CTX-TNA2 astrocytes is associated with elevated intracellular calcium levels and distinct metabolic alterations. CGRP-treated astrocytes exhibited lower levels of Cr, Glx, and myo-inositol, along with potential alterations in glucose metabolism, whereas CaCl2 treatment increased Cr and myo-inositol levels. These findings suggest that monitoring intracellular calcium levels together with metabolic and neurochemical profiles may provide a useful approach for detecting astrocyte dysfunction. Future studies using complementary functional assays and in vivo models will be required to clarify the mechanistic link between CGRP-induced calcium dysregulation and astrocytic metabolic alterations.
