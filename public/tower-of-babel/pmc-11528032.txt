SPP1+ macrophages and FAP+ fibroblasts promote the progression of pMMR gastric cancer
Authors: Zhixiong Su, Yufang He, Lijie You, Jingbo Chen, Guifeng Zhang, Zhenhua Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunotherapy has become a primary and secondary treatment for gastric cancer (GC) patients with mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR), and is used in both perioperative and advanced stages. The tumor immune microenvironment (TiME) is crucial for immunotherapy efficacy, yet the impact of MMR status on TiME remains understudied. We employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to analyze 33 fresh tissue samples from 25 patients, which included 10 normal tissues, 6 dMMR tumor tissues, and 17 pMMR tumor tissues, aiming to characterize the cellular and molecular components of the TiME. The proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) group displayed a significantly higher prevalence of a specific GC cell type, termed GC2, characterized by increased hypoxia, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and angiogenic activities compared to the dMMR group. GC2 cells overexpressed BEX3 and GPC3, and they significantly correlated with poorer survival. The pMMR group also showed increased infiltration of SPP1 + macrophages and FAP + fibroblasts, exhibiting strong hypoxic and pro-angiogenic features. Furthermore, a higher proportion of E2 endothelial cells, involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and showing heightened VEGF pathway, HIF pathway, and angiogenesis activity, were identified in pMMR patients. Intercellular communication analyses revealed that GC2 cells, SPP1 + macrophages, FAP + fibroblasts, and E2 endothelial cells interact through VEGF, SPP1, and MIF signals, forming a TiME characterized by hypoxia, pro-angiogenesis, and ECM remodeling. This study uncovered TiME heterogeneity among GC patients with different MMR states, highlighting that the pMMR TiME is distinguished by hypoxia, pro-angiogenesis, and ECM remodeling, driven by the presence of GC2 cells, SPP1 + macrophages, FAP + fibroblasts, and E2 endothelial cells. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide1. Despite the decline in incidence, projections show that the burden of GC is expected to increase 62% by 20402. Systemic chemotherapy continues to be the mainstay of treatment for metastatic GC, offering a median overall survival (OS) of approximately 12 months for patients undergoing conventional chemotherapy regimens3. Recent advancements in clinical trials have underscored the superior prognostic benefits of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in both the perioperative and advanced stages of gastric cancer4–6. Notably, the CheckMate-6496,7 and KEYNOTE-8118,9 trials have illuminated the significant role of ICIs in enhancing survival outcomes for patients afflicted with advanced GC. Nonetheless, the determinants that account for the variability in the efficacy of ICIs among diverse patient populations remain subjects of ongoing investigation.

The integrity of post-replicative DNA mismatch repair (MMR), facilitated by protein complexes including MutL homolog 1 (MLH1), MutS protein homolog 2 (MSH2), MutS homolog 6 (MSH6), and PMS1 homolog 2 (PMS2), is paramount for maintaining the fidelity of DNA replication10. This process is instrumental in identifying and rectifying single nucleotide mismatches that may otherwise evade the proofreading mechanisms during replication11. While MMR deficiency (dMMR) does not directly induce cellular proliferation, tumors with impaired MMR are characterized by an accumulation of mutations, a phenomenon that is linked to rapid disease progression yet paradoxically correlates with a more favorable prognosis in cancer12,13. A meta-analysis pooling data from four pivotal phase III trials (CLASSIC, ARTIST, MAGIC, and ITACA-S) has revealed that resectable GC patients with high microsatellite instability (MSI-H)/dMMR, when managed exclusively with surgery, fared better in terms of prognosis compared to those who also received adjuvant chemotherapy post-surgery14. The PROSECCO Study has furthered our understanding by demonstrating that among GC patients treated with the FLOT chemotherapy regimen, those with dMMR exhibit superior disease-free survival (DFS) and OS rates in comparison to their pMMR counterparts15. In addition, the absence of MMR proteins can lead to DNA replication errors, culminating in the buildup of mutations and the potential emergence of neoantigens16. These neoantigens may serve as targets for immune cells, potentially enhancing the infiltration of immune cells and bolstering the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs in dMMR tumors17. Consequently, MMR status has emerged as a pivotal biomarker for predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy in GC patients. The outcomes of KEYNOTE-05918, 06119, and 0625 trials have collectively indicated that pembrolizumab, either as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy, confers improved OS and objective response rates (ORR) in advanced dMMR GC patients. The latest iteration of the NCCN Guidelines for Gastric Cancer (Version 2.2023) encapsulates the following recommendations: for patients with locoregional disease, the consideration of neoadjuvant or perioperative administration of ICIs is advised if the tumor presents with dMMR; the preferred treatment strategies for unresectable locally advanced, recurrent, or metastatic dMMR GC patients include ICIs as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy20. However, the intrinsic factors that contribute to the heterogeneity of outcomes among patients with varying MMR statuses remain largely enigmatic. Current research has predominantly concentrated on examining the disparity in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) relative to MMR status21–23, while the broader exploration of the entire tumor immune microenvironment has been somewhat overlooked. Hence,

there is an urgent necessity to comprehensively analyze the overall tumor immune microenvironment (TiME) among patients with varying MMR statuses in order to unveil additional potential influencing factors.

The advent of single-cell genomic techniques has marked a significant paradigm shift, enabling the detailed dissection of human tumors at the single-cell level and offering profound insights into the underlying cancer biology24–26. In this study, we have meticulously analyzed 121,624 cells derived from 33 single-cell samples of 25 patients, constructing single-cell transcriptome maps for both dMMR and pMMR gastric cancer patients. Through this atlas, we have delineated subpopulation annotation, functional analysis, differentiation trajectory analysis, and intercellular interaction analysis for distinct cellular subsets. Our systematic investigation of the interplay between FAP + fibroblasts and SPP1 + macrophages, as revealed by the single-cell atlas, proffers an unprecedented understanding of the heterogeneity within the TiME, particularly across different MMR states.

Results

Single-cell transcriptional landscape of dMMR and pMMR GC

After the screening process, we successfully extracted single-cell sequencing data from 33 samples derived from 25 patients. Our dataset encompassed 10 samples of normal tissue (NT), alongside 6 primary tumor (PT) tissue samples from patients with deficient mismatch repair (dMMR), and 17 PT tissue samples from patients with proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) (Fig. 1A). The clinical information of these samples is presented in Supplementary Table 3. Specifically, among the patients included in this study, there were a total of 3 stage I cases (two dMMR and one pMMR), 6 stage II cases (two dMMR and four pMMR), 12 stage III cases (two dMMR and ten pMMR), and 2 stage IV cases (both pMMR). It is noteworthy that all patients enrolled in the study had recently been diagnosed with GC and had not yet received any treatment at the time of tissue collection. After quality control, a comprehensive dataset of 121,624 cells was assembled for analysis, comprising 31,198 cells from the NT group, 17,619 cells from the dMMR group, and 72,807 cells from the pMMR group. The exact cell count for each sample is detailed in Supplementary Table 3. Through reducing dimensionality as well as unsupervised cell clustering techniques, we identified various cell types including Epithelial cells (25,476; marked by EPCAM, MUC1, CLDN4), Endothelial cells (6,320; marked by MCAM, VWF, PECAM1), Fibroblasts (12,226; marked by DCN, COL1A1, COL2A2), NK/T cells (36,584; marked by CD3D, CD3E, NKG7), B cells (26,714; marked by CD79A, DERL3, IGKC), Myeloid cells (11,473; marked by CD68, CD14, CXCL8), and unknown cells (2,831) (Fig. 1B-D; SFig. 1). Owing to differences in dissociation efficiency among the cell types, immune cells constituted approximately 61.4% of the total analyzed samples. This finding is consistent with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from other cancer studies reported in the literature27,28.

Malignant epithelial cell heterogeneity

We screened a cohort of 25,476 epithelial cells (ECs), discerning them into five quintessential clusters through dimensionality reduction and unsupervised clustering techniques (Fig. 2A). Employing established malignancy scoring criteria27, we differentiated non-malignant and malignant ECs. Clusters 0 and 4, with elevated scores indicative of malignancy, were aggregated as malignant ECs, totaling 12,288 cells, and were contrasted with clusters 1–3, comprising 13,188 non-malignant ECs, predominantly isolated from normal tissue samples (Fig. 2B-D, p < 0.05; SFig. 2A, B). The distribution of these ECs revealed a stark contrast, with non-malignant ECs constituting approximately 77% of the cells in normal tissues, in stark contrast to the cancerous tissues where malignant ECs were more prevalent (SFig. 2C). Further gene expression profiling identified a significant upregulation of tumor-associated genes in malignant ECs, including CLDN4, CLDN7, TFF3, and REG4 (all p < 0.001; SFig. 2D). Conversely, non-malignant ECs exhibited heightened expression of genes pivotal for gastric mucus secretion and digestive enzyme function, namely PGC and LIPF (all p < 0.001; SFig. 2D).

To elucidate the heterogeneity within malignant ECs in both dMMR and pMMR cohorts, we executed an unsupervised clustering analysis on 10,122 ECs from PT samples. This stratification yielded three distinct GC cell subsets, GC0 to GC2, with a notable predominance of GC2 cells in the pMMR group (Fig. 2E). Gene ontology-biological process (GO-BP) analysis of differentially expressed genes among these subsets exposed a divergence in their functional enrichment. GC0 cells were particularly enriched in pathways associated with oxidative phosphorylation, xenobiotic metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism, indicative of a metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 2F). GC1 cells showed significant enrichment in TNFα signaling via NF-κB, IFNγ response, and IL2-STAT5 signaling, pathways critical for inflammation and cell proliferation (Fig. 2G). Conversely, GC2 cells demonstrated a marked enrichment in glycolysis, hypoxia, malignancy-associated pathways such as mTOR and KRAS, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and unfolded protein response, suggesting a role in aggressive tumor behavior (Fig. 2H). Additionally, expression analysis of cancer stem cell (CSC) markers CD44 and CXCR4 indicated a significant upregulation specifically within GC1, which also exhibited higher proliferation and migration scores compared to GC0 and GC2, underscoring their CSC-like characteristics (Fig. 2I; all p < 0.05; SFig. 3B, C). The GC0 subset, characterized by high metabolic activity, was the most abundant in all PT samples, suggesting a foundational role in GC. Interestingly, the pMMR group exhibited a significant increase in the proportion of GC2 cells, which displayed heightened activity in glycolysis, hypoxia, EMT, and angiogenesis pathways. This suggests that GC2 cells may play a pivotal role in tumor progression and metastasis, particularly within the pMMR GC patients.

We then applied pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle 2 to uncover the dynamics and heterogeneity of malignant ECs. GC1, characterized by CSC-like properties, was found at the onset of development (Fig. 2J, K). Furthermore, density maps, depicting the cell distribution of dMMR and pMMR with the pseudotime, demonstrated that towards the end of the differentiation process, there was an increasing trend in pMMR patients for GC2 cells, whereas a decreasing trend was observed in dMMR patients (Fig. 2L). Additionally, the analysis of the proportion of cells at different stages revealed a significant increase in the proportion of GC2 in stage III within the pMMR group (SFig. 3D). These findings imply a propensity of malignant cells from pMMR patients to differentiate into GC2 cells endowed with hypoxic, EMT, and angiogenic traits. Finally, the Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves demonstrated that the marker genes of GC2, specifically BEX3 and GPC3, were correlated with poorer overall survival outcomes (all p < 0.05; Fig. 2M). Additionally, the results of differential expression analysis revealed a significant upregulation in the expressions of BEX3 and GPC3 in pMMR tissues in ACRG cohort and GSE13911 (all p < 0.05; Fig. 2N, O).

Heterogeneity of NK/T cells infiltration in dMMR and pMMR patients

In alignment with prior findings29, our study noted a higher proportion of NK/T cells among dMMR patients compared to pMMR patients (Fig. 1D). To delve deeper into the diversity of NK/T cell subsets, we classified them into seven distinct subtypes, characterized by specific markers: Cytotoxic CD8 (8433), Naive CD8 (6081), Resident CD8 (5868), Proliferation CD4 (1819), Naive CD4 (6435), Treg (4181), and an unclassified cell group (439) (Fig. 3A, B). The defining markers for these subtypes were illustrated in Fig. 3C. Notably, echoing the observations by Li et al.26, we found no evidence of exhausted CD8 + T cells within the TiME of gastric cancer.

Figure 3D shows significant heterogeneity in the infiltration patterns across NT, dMMR, and pMMR samples. Relative to PT tissues, NT tissues showed an increased presence of resident CD8 + T cells and cytotoxic CD8 + T cells, with a concomitant decrease in naive CD8 + T cells. This suggests that PT tissue undergoes varying levels of immune cell infiltration and may face constraints in immune activation. dMMR/MSI-H tumors are known for their high mutational burden and neoantigen load, factors that can enhance immune cell recognition and infiltration30. However, the active immunoreactive state, coupled with elevated expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-1/PD-L1, and negative feedback from the immune system, can promote the differentiation and proliferation of Tregs31. In our study, we found the dMMR group displayed a higher infiltration of Proliferation CD4 and Treg cells compared to the pMMR group (Fig. 4D). Moreover, the results of the expression levels of immunosuppressive receptors on cytotoxic CD8 + T cells showed that the dMMR groups had significantly upregulation of PDCD1, CTLA4, LAG3, and HAVCR2 (Fig. 3E).

Additionally, the presence of Tregs within the TiME can impede the development of host anti-tumor immunity by dampening the responses of effector T cells specific to the tumor32. Upon analyzing the activity of anti-tumor signaling pathways in cytotoxic CD8 + T cells, we noted a significant reduction in the activity of IL-2 STAT5 signaling, INF gamma response, and TNF-α signaling in the dMMR group (Fig. 3F-H). Cellular communication analysis further revealed that Tregs in the dMMR group exert a more potent inhibitory effect on TILs via the MIF-(CD74 + CXCR4) signaling axis compared to those in the pMMR group (Fig. 3I). Collectively, these findings suggest that while dMMR tumors may exhibit high immunogenicity leading to increased TIL infiltration, the activity of immunoeffector cells might be compromised due to elevated Treg infiltration and increased expression of immunosuppressive receptors.

Heterogeneity of myeloid cells in dMMR and pMMR patients

Myeloid cells play an indispensable role in both the innate and adaptive immune responses. With this in mind, we conducted a detailed analysis of myeloid cell subtypes within the dMMR and pMMR groups. We categorized 11,473 myeloid cells into six distinct types: Monocytes (3,098), SPP1 + Macrophages (3,068), SLC40A1 + Macrophages (3,245), Conventional Dendritic Cells (cDCs) (1,128), Activated Dendritic Cells (242), and Proliferating Cells (692), and observed a significant increase in SPP1 + macrophages in the pMMR group (Fig. 4A; SFig. 4A, B). We observed a significant increase in SPP1 + macrophages in the pMMR group, contrasting with the proportions found in both NT and dMMR groups. This distinction implies unique differentiation patterns within the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) that are influenced by the state of MMR (Fig. 4A).

Subsequently, we employed pseudotime trajectory analysis to delineate the differentiation pathways of MPS. The cells were observed to transition sequentially from monocytes to SPP1 + and SLC40A1 + macrophages, each stage marked by distinct immunomodulatory functions(Fig. 4B; SFig. 4C). Notably, the peak presence of SPP1 + macrophages in the pMMR group was at the terminus of the pseudotime trajectory, whereas in the dMMR group, they were prominent during the intermediate stages of cell maturation (Fig. 4C). Functional assessments of SPP1 + and SLC40A1 + macrophages revealed that the latter displayed heightened pro-inflammatory activity, whereas SPP1 + macrophages were implicated in hypoxia-related pathways, including hypoxia response and dampening of immune system processes (Fig. 4D, E). In congruence with Su et al.'s observations in colorectal cancer33, our study found that SPP1 + macrophages had elevated expression of IL1A and IL1B ligands and were markedly induced under anoxic conditions to express genes linked with angiogenesis, such as VCAN (Fig. 4F). These results imply that stressors within the TiME, like hypoxia, might drive the differentiation of MPS into SPP1 + macrophages, potentially promoting tumor progression in pMMR contexts.

FAP + fibroblasts facilitate the malignant progression in pMMR patients

Fibroblasts are the primary constituents of the TiME, with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) playing diverse roles in cancer progression34. We classified 12,226 fibroblasts into 3 subpopulations: CXCL14 + fibroblasts (5,610), ACTA2 + fibroblasts (2,186), and FAP + fibroblasts (4,430) (Fig. 5A; SFig. 5A, B). The cell proportion diagram indicates that the dMMR group has a higher proportion of ACTA2 + fibroblasts than other groups, while the pMMR group has a significantly increased proportion of FAP + fibroblasts than other groups (Fig. 5A). The results of immunofluorescence in GC patient tissues demonstrated that pMMR patients have a higher number of FAP + fibroblasts in the TiME stroma (Fig. 5B).

By GO-BP analysis, we observed the involvement of CXCL14 + fibroblasts in extracellular matrix organization and negative regulation of cell migration and motility (Fig. 5C). It constituted the predominant population in NT, suggesting that CXCL14 + fibroblasts likely represent the normal fibroblast phenotype. The ACTA2 + fibroblasts subset was primarily implicated in wound healing, cell-substrate adhesion, and muscle tissue development (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, it exhibited high expression levels of genes associated with wound repair, including MYL9, TAGLN, and ACTA2 (SFig. 5 A, B). These findings indicate that ACTA2 + fibroblasts may represent myofibroblasts associated with carcinoma (mCAF).

FAP + fibroblast, which is associated with malignant progression of GC, might represent a distinct subtype differentiated under cancer-related pressures that promote proliferation, metabolism, and angiogenesis35. In our study, FAP + fibroblast was mainly involved in cytoplasmic translation, response to decreased oxygen levels, cell growth, negative regulation of external stimulus, and glycolytic process (Fig. 5C). It demonstrated elevated expression levels of various growth factor-encoding genes within FAP + fibroblasts, such as VEGF and IGF family genes, as well as CXCL12, which can promote GC cell invasion and EMT (Fig. 5D). Further GSCA analysis based on the TCGA-STAD dataset revealed a positive correlation between the expression of FAP + fibroblast-related genes and poor OS and progression-free survival (PFS) (Fig. 5E). Moreover, the pathway enrichment analysis also demonstrated significant enrichment for EMT pathways along with upregulation of cancer-related pathways(SFig. 5C).

To explore the differentiation among different fibroblast subtypes, we conducted a pseudotime analysis, which revealed that fibroblasts underwent differentiation from CXCL14 + fibroblast into ACTA2 + fibroblast and FAP + fibroblast, respectively (SFig. 4D). To validate their differentiation potential, CytoTRACE analysis was performed, demonstrating that CXCL14 + fibroblasts exhibited higher differentiation potential, while FAP + fibroblasts displayed the lowest (Fig. 5F, G; SFig. 5E). Pseudo-time differential gene analysis and pathway enrichment analysis indicated a gradual shift in the biological function of fibroblasts during differentiation, from matrix formation to response to malignant tumor microenvironment stressors such as hypoxia and angiogenesis. (Fig. 5H). These suggest an important role for FAP + fibroblasts in the progression of gastric cancer.

E2 endothelial cells promote angiogenesis and invasion

Endothelial cells in the tumor microenvironment are involved in processes such as vascular survival, immune response regulation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune evasion36. We screened 4,712 endothelial cells from peritumoral tissue (PT) samples and categorized them into three subpopulations through dimensionality reduction clustering: E0 (3,649 cells; VWF, CLU, C7), E1 (544 cells; TPT1, FTH1, SOD2), and E2 (519 cells; TMSB4X, IGFBP5, STC1) (Fig. 6A, D).

(E) Heatmap and network map showed that VEGF signaling interactions send from fibroblasts and GC to ECs. (F) K-M survival curves depicted the expression of E2 marker genes (IGFBP5, TMSB4X, and STC1) were associated with poor prognosis in the TCGA-STAD cohort.

Among the identified endothelial subtypes, the E0 subset was predominant across all cohorts, with GO-BP analysis indicating a significant association with translation-related pathways (Fig. 6A, B). This finding implies that E0 may correspond to normal endothelial cells. In contrast, the E1 subset was notably more abundant in the dMMR cohort and exhibited positive immunomodulatory functions. Additionally, the E1 subset displayed attenuated activity in angiogenesis, VEGF, and other related pathways, suggesting that E1 may represent a unique endothelial subset with immune-enhancing properties (Fig. 6A-C). The E2 subset was found to be increasing in the pMMR group, with its primary functions linked to extracellular matrix organization. Notably, the E2 subset demonstrated heightened scores in the mTOR signaling pathway, as well as in IGF1, angiogenesis, VEGF, and HIF pathways, indicative of a role in promoting gastric cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and migration (Fig. 6A-C).

Further investigation into cell-to-cell communication patterns revealed substantial interactions within the VEGF signaling pathway between fibroblasts, particularly FAP + fibroblasts, GC2 cells, and E2 cells. This suggests that FAP + fibroblasts and GC2 cells may modulate the behavior of E2 cells via VEGF signaling, thus facilitating the malignant progression of gastric cancer (Fig. 6E). Intriguingly, a higher prevalence of FAP + fibroblasts and GC2 cells was observed in the pMMR group, suggesting that the TiME in pMMR exhibits more aggressive biological behaviors, including enhanced angiogenesis, proliferation, and invasion. Additionally, a prognostic analysis utilizing TCGA-STAD data for E2-specific marker genes was conducted. The outcomes underscored a positive correlation between the expression of TMSB4X, IGFBP5, and STC1 marker genes and poor patient prognosis (all p < 0.05; Fig. 6F).

Heterogeneity of intercellular communication in d/pMMR

Quantifying the crosstalk between and within different cell types in the tumor microenvironment is pivotal for devising interventional strategies against tumor progression. In this study, we stratified all cells into dMMR and pMMR cohorts to discern the differences in cellular interactions across various MMR states. Overall, the dMMR group exhibited a lower number of interactions compared to the pMMR group (2637 vs. 3275), yet displayed a marginally higher interaction strength (0.148 vs. 0.140) (Fig. 7A). Analysis of the intercellular communication network, encompassing both the number and intensity of interactions, indicated that FAP + fibroblasts and SPP1 + macrophages within the pMMR group had a significantly greater impact on other cells than their counterparts in the dMMR group (Fig. 7B). These observations were corroborated by consistent patterns in the heatmaps (Fig. 7C, D). Furthermore, an analysis of cellular proportions across different clinical stages revealed an increase in the proportion of FAP + fibroblasts and SPP1 + macrophages with tumor stage progression in pMMR patients, a trend not observed in dMMR patients (SFig. 6 A, B).

Additionally, a comparative analysis of interaction frequency between pathways and ligands revealed heightened activity in VEGI, OSM, CD70, CCL, and GDF pathways in the dMMR group (SFig. 7 A). In contrast, the pMMR group showed increased activity in VEGF, WNT, IGF pathways, and other ligand-associated pathways (SFig. 7 A). Word cloud maps highlighted a predominant enrichment of the VEGFA ligand in the pMMR group, suggesting elevated VEGF signaling pathway activity (SFig. 7B). Comparative analysis of VEGF signaling pathway intensity across different groups indicated that endothelial cells predominantly served as ligand cells for this pathway. Notably, FAP + fibroblast cells and SPP1 + macrophages within the pMMR group exhibited significantly higher signals for E2 as VEGF ligands (Fig. 7E).

Given the substantial differences in cellular interactions between FAP + fibroblasts and SPP1 + macrophages (Fig. 7C), we compared their interactions with other cells within the TiME. FAP + fibroblasts in the pMMR group showed enhanced interaction with GC cells through the MK signaling pathway, particularly via MDK-NCL, MDK-SDC1, MDK-SDC4, and MDK-(ITGAB + ITGB1) interactions, as well as increased interaction with endothelial cells via VEGF and MK signaling pathways (Fig. 7F). These findings suggest that FAP + fibroblasts are implicated in both the malignant progression of GC and angiogenesis within the pMMR TiME. Moreover, compared to the dMMR group, FAP + fibroblasts demonstrated intensified interaction with immune cells through the MIF signaling pathway and CXCL12-CXCR4 ligand binding, indicating a potential immunosuppressive role mediated by MIF and CXCL signaling pathways in the pMMR group (SFig. 7C). In the pMMR group, SPP1 + macrophages showed enhanced SPP1 and MIF signaling interactions with GC cells, as well as increased VEGF signaling with endothelial cells, suggesting a role in promoting gastric cancer progression and angiogenesis (Fig. 7G). Additionally, both SPP1 + macrophages and FAP + fibroblasts in the pMMR group showed significantly heightened activity in the SPP1 signaling pathway, indicating their potential involvement in promoting GC progression and metastasis through interactions within this pathway (Fig. 7G). Recent studies have shown collaborative interactions between SPP1 + and FAP + cells in the immune microenvironments of liver and colorectal cancers, leading to ECM remodeling and the formation of a dense tumor microenvironment that can result in immune rejection32,35. Consistently, we observed enhanced ligand interactions related to ECM remodeling (e.g., FN1-CD44, COL1A1-CD44, and COL1A2-CD44) between FAP + fibroblasts and SPP1 + macrophages in the pMMR group, potentially contributing to the diminished efficacy of immunotherapy in this subgroup (SFig. 7D).

Discussion

The MMR status is a pivotal determinant of the responsiveness to immunotherapy in gastrointestinal tumors37. The influence of MMR status on the TiME has long been acknowledged as a significant factor in the efficacy of ICIs38. However, our current understanding of the TiME landscape alterations in GC due to MMR status is still limited. There is an urgent need to comprehensively understand the heterogeneity of the TiME in GC across different MMR states. In this study, we utilized scRNA-seq to delineate the TiME landscape associated with different MMR states and analyzed distinct cell subsets to explore potential intervention strategies.

By identifying distinct subpopulations of malignant ECs and their respective roles, we have determined that cancer cells exhibit a high degree of heterogeneity with variations in MMR status. Our findings revealed a significantly increased proportion of the GC2 subtype within the pMMR group, which was characterized by heightened hypoxia, EMT, and angiogenesis-related pathway activity. Chida et al., through transcriptomic analysis of 36 patients with gastrointestinal tumors treated with PD-1 blockers, demonstrated that nonresponders to ICIs exhibited an immunosuppressive TiME associated with EMT, angiogenesis, hypoxia, and enrichment of various metabolic-related signaling pathways39. Furthermore, elevated expression levels of glypican-3 (GPC3) were observed in both subsets of GC2 cells and pMMR patients. GPC3 has been identified as a marker for multiple tumors and can influence the TiME; thus, making it an excellent target for ICIs40. Yu et al. developed GPC3-specific NK cells capable of efficiently and specifically eliminating GPC3 + hepatocellular carcinoma both in vivo and in vitro41. Additionally, Phase I clinical trials evaluating the safety and efficacy of the GPC3 peptide vaccine (UMIN Clinical Trials Registry: 000001395) have shown promising results in inducing an immune response42. These findings suggest that the enrichment of GC2, which over-expresses GPC3, may contribute to the low response rate to immunotherapy among pMMR patients. Therefore, it indicates that combination anti-GPC3 treatment may potentially augment ICIs efficacy among this patient population.

NK and T cells are crucial effector cells responsible for tumor eradication. Consistent with previous studies, we initially observed a higher proportion of NK/T cell infiltration in the dMMR group, which could be attributed to the impact of tumor neoantigens in the dMMR state43. Interestingly, further subpopulation analysis revealed an elevated level of Treg infiltration within the dMMR group. dMMR/microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) tumors typically exhibit increased neoantigen expression, activated CD8 cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and heightened infiltration of activated Th1 cells. Simultaneously, there was significant over-expression of immune checkpoint-associated proteins within the tumor microenvironment of dMMR/MSI-H patients, elucidating why tumor cells can persist in such a highly immunogenic TiME31,44. Therefore, we compared immune checkpoint expression on cytotoxic CD8 + T cells between the dMMR and pMMR groups and found significantly higher expression levels in the former than in the latter. Additionally, the formation of dense extracellular matrix and abnormal vascular structures in tumor tissues can trap T cells at the tumor periphery, limiting their migration and infiltration45. In patients with proficient mismatch repair (pMMR), the interaction of SPP1 + macrophages, FAP + fibroblasts, GC2, and E2 endothelial cells constitutes a tumor immune microenvironment (TiME) characterized by pro-angiogenesis, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and hypoxia, which may contribute to the limited efficacy of immunotherapy. Of course, further confirmation through single-cell spatial transcriptomics is required.

By analyzing the proportions of different subtypes of myeloid cells in various MMR states, we observed a significant increase in the infiltration proportion of SPP1 + macrophages in the pMMR group. In recent years, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) have been extensively investigated across diverse malignancies owing to advancements in single-cell sequencing technology46. Our study revealed that SPP1 + macrophages within the GC TiME exhibit upregulated hypoxia-related pathways and promote angiogenesis through activation of the VEGF signaling. Furthermore, two recent studies conducted by Qi et al.33 and Liu et al.47, focusing on liver cancer and colorectal cancer respectively, demonstrated that FAP + fibroblast and SPP1 + macrophage contribute to ECM remodeling and orchestrate the formation of a connective tissue hyperplasia microenvironment, thereby impeding lymphocyte infiltration into tumor cores and diminishing PD-L1 treatment efficacy. Consistently with these findings, our results indicated that compared to the dMMR group, both SPP1 + macrophage and FAP + fibroblast displayed enhanced interactions with ECM remodeling-related ligands within the TiME of pMMR GC patients; moreover, both cell types exhibited characteristics associated with hypoxia. These suggest potential synergistic interaction between SPP1 + macrophages and FAP + fibroblasts in promoting connective tissue hyperplasia within the TiME of pMMR patients, ultimately leading to immune rejection. However, these results need to be further described in the future through single-cell spatial transcriptomics to elucidate the positional relationship between FAP + fibroblasts and SPP1 + macrophages in the TiME to draw more detailed conclusions.

CAFs, as a pivotal component in the TiME, exhibited distinctive markers, particularly demonstrating elevated levels of FAP as a membrane-anchored enzyme48. Substantial evidence suggested that FAP exerts its influence on tumor growth through multiple mechanisms, encompassing proliferation, facilitation of invasion and angiogenesis, promotion of stem cells, and suppression of immunity49. In the current study, we observed a significant increase in infiltration levels of FAP + fibroblasts in the pMMR group; moreover, it was closely associated with enhanced angiogenesis and tumor progression. GSCA analysis revealed a positive correlation between FAP + fibroblast infiltration and poor prognosis. Additionally, FAP can remodel the ECM by modulating factors such as MMP-9 and MMP-2 while also secreting growth factors including VEGF, EGF, TGF-β, and IL-11 along with chemokines and cytokines50. Similarly, in the GC’s TiME, FAP + fibroblasts exhibited elevated expression levels for various growth factors and cytokines. Additionally, the analysis conducted on endothelial cells demonstrated that endothelial cell subset E2 possesses an ECM remodeling function, and there exists a significant VEGF signaling pathway connecting E2 with FAP + fibroblasts. Interestingly, the proportion of E2 subgroup was remarkably low among dMMR patients. These findings suggest that FAP + fibroblasts are crucial in angiogenesis and ECM remodeling in gastric cancer patients with pMMR status.

In conclusion, this investigation has yielded novel perspectives on the heterogeneity of the TiME in GC patients, stratified by MMR status, at the single-cell resolution. The study underscored the significant differences in the TiME among patients with distinct MMR statuses. Our comprehensive analysis revealed that, in contrast to the dMMR cohort, the pMMR group demonstrated heightened overall hypoxia, EMT, ECM remodeling, and a pro-angiogenic phenotype. This phenotype was attributed to an increased infiltration of GC2 cells, SPP1 + macrophages, FAP + fibroblasts, and E2 endothelial cells. Notably, a synergistic interaction between FAP + fibroblasts and SPP1 + macrophages was observed, potentially augmenting ECM remodeling in GC patients with pMMR status and contributing to immunotherapy resistance. The insights garnered from this study offer a valuable compass for future research initiatives. Furthermore, additional inquiry through an array of experimental approaches and single-cell spatial transcriptomics is essential to elucidate the intricate dynamics of intercellular interactions and the underlying regulatory pathways.

Conclusion

Through comparative analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data from gastric cancer patients with different MMR statuses, we found that patients in the pMMR group had a higher presence of GC2 cells, FAP + fibroblasts, SPP1 + macrophages, and E2 endothelial cells in their TiME compared to those in the dMMR group. Moreover, these cells interact with each other through signaling pathways such as VEGF, ECM, and hypoxia, collectively forming a unique TiME that promotes the progression of gastric cancer in pMMR patients.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

In this study, we obtained single-cell sequencing data and clinicopathological features of gastric cancer patients from GSE18390435. After excluding samples with unknown MMR status, a total of 33 samples were included in this current analysis. All samples were collectively sourced from 25 individuals, comprising 2 healthy patients and 23 GC patients. Among the 23 GC patients, 8 had both cancerous and adjacent non-cancerous tissues sampled, while 15 patients provided cancerous tissue only. Additionally, bulk-RNA sequencing data and clinical information for the TCGA-STAD cohort were obtained from TCGA, while data for the ACRG cohort (GSE66229) and GSE13911 were acquired from GEO database.

Data processing

For single-cell sequencing data, following previous studies’ protocols25,51,52, we initially filtered cells based on quality control criteria (nFeature > 50 and percent.mt < 20). Subsequently, batch effects were removed using the “harmony” method. The R software package “Seurat 4.3.0.1” was employed for normalization, clustering analysis, differential gene expression analysis, and visualization tasks. Differential genes between subpopulations were identified using the “FindAllMarkers” function; cell markers provided by Cellmarker2.0 (http://bio-bigdata.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellMarker/index.html)53 as well as previously published articles aided in annotating different subpopulations (Supplementary Table 1)25,54.

Regarding bilk-RNA sequencing data, FPKM format data for STAD was obtained from TCGA for subsequent analyses. log2(x + 1) transformation was applied to datasets sourced from GEO databases.

Distinguish between nonmalignant and malignant epithelial cells

As described in the previous article25,27, the top 50 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with an adjusted p value < 0.01 in tumor tissues from TCGA data were selected to calculate malignant scores. Similarly, the top 50 DEGs with an adjusted p value < 0.01 in normal tissues were chosen to calculate non-malignant scores. This was achieved using the ‘AddModuleScore’ function within the Seurat R package. Subsequently, the final scores were calculated by deducting the non-malignant score from the malignant score. Finally, a comprehensive differentiation between non-malignant and malignant cells was achieved by integrating the final score with the location of the epithelial cell source.

Analysis of single-cell trajectories

The “monocle 2.22.0” (R package) was utilized for the construction of single-cell tracks. The criteria used for gene selection in the analysis were as follows: (1) expression in more than 4 cells, (2) average expression level exceeding 0.5, and (3) q value of differentially expressed gene analysis below 0.01. Additionally, “cytoTRACE “(R package) was employed to infer differentiation levels and assist in validating the single-cell tracks51.

Functional analysis

First, we utilized the ‘FindMarkers’ function in “Seurat” (R package) to identify differentially expressed genes between cell subpopulations (LogFC ≥ 0.5, p < 0.05). Subsequently, the Gene Ontology Biology Process (GO-BP) functional enrichment analysis of distinct subgroups was conducted using the “org.Hs.eg.db” package in R. Furthermore, for evaluating specific pathway activity in individual cells, we obtained the corresponding gene set from the msigdbr website (Supplementary Table 2), and subsequently employed “AUCell” R package to assign a score reflecting its level of activity.

Intercellular communications analysis

The R package “CellChat 1.6.1” is utilized for the analysis of intercellular communication. CellChat constructs the cell communication database, cellchatDB, by integrating the KEGG database and relevant literature55. This comprehensive database comprises three main components: (1) Secreted Signaling, (2) ECM-Receptor, and (3) Cell-Cell Contact. In addition to directly interacting proteins, CellChat also considers soluble agonists, antagonists, and other interaction modulators. In current study, we employed “Secreted Signaling” to compare cellular interactions across different cell types and MMR states.

GSCA analysis

GSCA (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA/) is a comprehensive database designed for the purpose of searching, investigating and exploring gene sets that are associated with mRNA expression, mutation, immune infiltration and drug resistance in cancer analysis56. It has the capability to perform GSVA analysis on specific gene sets which enables estimation of the level of integration of gene set expression in each TCGA sample. Additionally, it can calculate the effect of gene set GSVA score on prognosis as well as correlation with cancer-related pathway activity.

Immunofluorescence staining

The GC tissue microarray were purchased from Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co.,Ltd (Shanghai, China) ( ID: HStmA180Su19; Ethics No.SHYJS-CP-1910016). Firstly, GC tissue microarray was cleaned with Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS). It was then treated with a permeabilization solution consisting of 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS to facilitate access to intracellular antigens. After permeabilization, the slides were blocked with a 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) solution to minimize non-specific binding, followed by an incubation period with primary antibodies at 4 °C to allow specific binding to occur. The antibodies utilized for immunodetection included anti-FAP (catalog number BMS168, sourced from eBiosciencce, 1:500).Once the primary antibody incubation was complete, the slides underwent a washing process to remove any unbound antibodies. Subsequently, they were incubated with secondary antibodies at ambient temperature for a duration of 2 h to bind to the primary antibodies and enable fluorescence-based detection. The resulting immunofluorescence was visualized using Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope.

Statistical methods

The R4.1.0 software was utilized to compare the proportions of different cell subsets between dMMR and pMMR samples using a two-tailed Mann-Whitney test and an unpaired t-test. Prior to conducting the t-test, a normality test was performed. In cases where data had unequal standard deviations, rankings were compared using the Mann-Whitney test. Bar charts and error bars represent the mean and standard error of the mean, respectively.
