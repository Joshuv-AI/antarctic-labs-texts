Successful intracardiac repair of unrepaired tetralogy of fallot in a 51-year-old woman: a case report
Authors: Airi Kageyama, Keiichi Itatani, Yosuke Nakai, Jiryo Haibara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnrepaired tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) diagnosed in adulthood is extremely rare, and preoperative assessment of right and left ventricular functional reserve is essential for determining surgical indications and strategy.Case presentationA 51-year-old woman, who had been followed up a ventricular septal defect (VSD) since childhood, was diagnosed with TOF and with right ventricular failure due to progressive fatigue and peripheral edema. Cardiac imaging revealed overriding aorta, a membranous VSD with a pouch, hypoplastic pulmonary valve annulus, and right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) obstruction caused by anomalous muscular bundles, suggesting TOF anatomy. 4D flow MRI (Time resolved 3D cine phase contrast MRI) demonstrated accelerated systolic flow across the RVOT with minimal pulmonary regurgitation. Surgical repair consisted of a bioprosthetic pulmonary valve replacement with RVOT reconstruction using a bovine pericardial patch, VSD patch closure, and tricuspid valvuloplasty. The postoperative course was uneventful, and RV pressure was significantly reduced. She was discharged on postoperative day 13 and remained asymptomatic at 4 months.ConclusionsEven in adults with unrepaired TOF, favorable outcomes can be achieved when surgical indications are carefully evaluated and procedures are tailored to the individual anatomy and hemodynamics.

Introduction

Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is the most common cyanotic congenital heart disease, and early intracardiac repair during infancy is well established [1]. Consequently, encountering unrepaired TOF in adulthood has become rare. Adult patients typically present with chronic right ventricular overload and progressive right heart failure. A comprehensive preoperative assessment is essential for surgical planning. Here, we report a rare case of a 51-year-old woman with unrepaired TOF requiring complete repair, highlighting preoperative hemodynamic assessment including 4D flow MRI (Time resolved 3D cine phase contrast MRI).

Case presentation

A 51-year-old woman with a history of chronic hepatitis B and bladder cancer had been diagnosed with a ventricular septal defect (VSD) in childhood but remained asymptomatic without medical follow-up. She had no history of cyanosis or heart failure symptoms during pregnancy or adulthood. At around age 50, she developed gradually progressive fatigue and lower-limb edema, prompting evaluation at a local clinic, where right ventricular (RV) failure caused by unrepaired tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) was diagnosed. She was referred to us for surgical management.

On admission, she had bilateral leg edema but no cyanosis, and her oxygen saturation on room air was ≥ 95%. A grade IV/VI holosystolic murmur was audible along the left sternal border. Chest radiography showed a cardiothoracic ratio of 50% without pulmonary congestion or evidence of pulmonary oligemia, and the cardiac silhouette demonstrated a boot-shaped configuration. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm at 74 bpm without axis deviation or clear evidence of right ventricular hypertrophy. The preoperative hemoglobin and hematocrit levels were 14.6 g/dL and 42.4%, respectively.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 54%, LV end-diastolic and end-systolic diameters of 42.1 mm and 30.4 mm, respectively, and mild aortic regurgitation. RV function was preserved with an RV fractional area change of 32.5% and a tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) of 23 mm, which is within the reported normal range. Mild tricuspid regurgitation was present, with a transtricuspid pressure gradient of 51.8 mmHg. A 10-mm membranous VSD was identified with a restrictive jet, and the shunt direction was left-to-right, but the shunt volume appeared small.

Contrast-enhanced cardiac CT revealed a hypoplastic pulmonary valve annulus measuring 15 mm and RV outflow tract (RVOT) stenosis caused by prominent anomalous muscle bundles. The aorta was overriding the interventricular septum, and the membranous VSD formed a pouch on the RV side (Fig. 1). Cardiac catheterization showed marked RV hypertension with an RV systolic pressure of 93 mmHg and an RV–pulmonary artery gradient of 50 mmHg. Pulmonary vascular resistance was normal at 1.98 Wood units, with a Qp/Qs ratio of 1.05. RV end-diastolic pressure was 7 mmHg, and the RV/LV systolic pressure ratio was 0.57, based on directly measured right and left ventricular systolic pressures (93 mmHg and 163 mmHg, respectively), while peripheral systole and mean pulmonary arterial pressure was 35 and 20 mmHg, respectively. RV angiography confirmed significant RVOT stenosis (Fig. 2). No major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs) were identified.

4D flow MRI demonstrated a right ventricular end-diastolic/end-systolic volume index of 48.1/25.8 mL/m², which was almost the lower limit of the normal range for right ventricular diastolic volumes (48–112 ml/m2)in healthy adults [2, 3], with a right ventricular ejection fraction of 46.3%, suggesting mildly impaired and reduced right ventricular function compared with reported healthy adults’ value (51–71) [2].

Markedly accelerated flow was observed from the right ventricular outflow tract to the main pulmonary artery. Pulmonary blood flow was measured at 3.50 L/min with a regurgitant fraction of only 0.9%, and antegrade flow during end-diastole was noted. (Fig. 3). Late gadolinium enhancement imaging was not performed, as the primary objective of cardiac MRI in this case was the assessment of intracardiac flow dynamics using 4D flow MRI rather than myocardial tissue characterization. However, flow energy loss was high in RVOT during systole (peak 6.08 mW, average 15.52 mW) compared with that in the reported healthy cases [3], indicating long-term prominent RV pressure load increase caused deterioration of the RV function in the current case.

Given the presence of symptomatic right-sided heart failure, significant RVOT obstruction, and a hypoplastic pulmonary annulus, surgical repair was indicated. Under cardiopulmonary bypass and cardioplegic arrest, a right atriotomy was performed. The membranous VSD (10 mm) was exposed by incising the pouch, and patch closure was performed using a 0.4-mm ePTFE patch. Tricuspid valve repair was performed using an edge-to-edge technique at the commissures between the septal and anterior leaflets and between the anterior and posterior leaflets to eliminate regurgitation. The RVOT and main pulmonary artery were opened longitudinally. Hypertrophied and fibrotic RV free-wall muscle was resected, and the infundibular septum was incised on both the ventriculo-infundibular fold (VIF) and septal sides, taking care to preserve the moderator band and conduction tissue. The native pulmonary valve leaflets were excised, and a 27-mm Epic bioprosthetic valve was implanted in the supra-annular position. RVOT reconstruction was completed using a bovine pericardial patch over the RVOT incision and the anterior aspect of the prosthetic valve (Fig. 4). Total operative time was 4 h 14 min, with cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamp times of 120 min and 93 min, respectively.

The patient was extubated on the day of surgery and transferred out of the ICU on postoperative day 1. Echocardiography performed on postoperative day 7 revealed no residual defects, a left ventricular ejection fraction of 67.1%, mild tricuspid regurgitation, and a reduced transtricuspid gradient of 20.6 mmHg. Postoperative CT was performed to evaluate the morphology of the reconstructed RVOT and main PA, and position of the prosthetic pulmonary valve, confirming an enlarged RVOT lumen (Fig. 5).She was discharged home on postoperative day 13 and remained free of heart failure symptoms at 4-month follow-up.

Discussion

Tetralogy of Fallot is the most common form of cyanotic congenital heart disease [4], and early surgical repair during infancy has become standard, yielding excellent outcomes. As a result, encountering unrepaired TOF in adulthood is rare. The natural survival of unoperated TOF is extremely poor, with a reported 40-year survival of only 3% [5]. Nevertheless, exceptional reports have described unrepaired or late-repaired TOF in elderly patients [6, 7]. Adult patients with unrepaired TOF are typically exposed to decades of cyanosis and pressure overload, resulting in marked RV hypertrophy and endocardial fibrosis [8].

In the present case, the patient exhibited symptoms of right-sided heart failure, including lower-extremity edema and palpitations. Surgical correction was necessary to reduce the right ventricular afterload, specifically the severely hypoplastic pulmonary annulus and RVOT obstruction. Although pressure drop and/or RV pressure in catheter might be overestimated when systemic pressure was over 160mmHg, but it’s probable that RV overload could be induced under stress in this patient, and that is why hemodynamic assessment with 4D flow MRI would be useful to examine the hemodynamic and anatomical detail. In this case, RV volume and function indicated that restricted RV volume resulted in reduced contraction. Pulmonary valve replacement and RVOT reconstruction were therefore essential components of the operative strategy. Following relief of RV afterload, a substantial increase in left-to-right shunting through VSD was anticipated. Accordingly, complete VSD closure was deemed necessary, regardless of its preoperative size or shunt magnitude, to avoid residual postoperative shunting.

Although the preoperative Qp/Qs was modest at 1.05, VSD closure and tricuspid valvuloplasty were performed because the VSD and tricuspid regurgitation could increase right ventricular preload postoperatively. As a result, the final operative plan encompassed the essential elements of complete TOF repair even though the aim itself was to release RVOT obstruction to improve RV failure.

Previous reports have indicated that perioperative mortality in adults undergoing initial TOF repair approaches 6%, significantly higher than that in infants, which remains below 1% [9]. This underscores the need for rigorous assessment of operative tolerance, given the marked hemodynamic changes that follow TOF correction. Postoperatively, increased pulmonary blood flow may lead to elevated pulmonary vascular resistance and augmented left ventricular volume load. Patients with pre-existing left ventricular dysfunction may develop left-sided heart failure, whereas those with severely impaired right ventricular function may experience inadequate right ventricular output despite relief of obstruction.

In this patient, preoperative echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular diastolic function and maintained right ventricular systolic performance. Cardiac catheterization revealed no elevation in end-diastolic pressures, suggesting adequate biventricular reserve. In the present case, 4D flow MRI provided additional hemodynamic insights beyond those obtained by conventional imaging modalities. Specifically, it enabled detailed visualization of accelerated systolic flow across the RVOT obstructed with the abnormal muscle structures and quantification of pulmonary blood flow with regurgitant fraction in addition to the preload increase. Interestingly, despite the long-standing nature of the disease, the patient did not exhibit significant polycythemia, and the shunt direction remained left-to-right, suggesting relatively preserved pulmonary blood flow. This hemodynamic profile is atypical for long-standing unrepaired TOF and underscores the importance of detailed flow assessment using 4D flow MRI. Therefore, 4D flow MRI may represent a useful adjunctive tool for preoperative evaluation and surgical planning in selected adult congenital heart disease patients.

Conclusion

Adults with unrepaired TOF may present with heart failure symptoms after long-standing RV pressure overload. Comprehensive preoperative assessment and tailored surgical intervention can lead to excellent outcomes.
