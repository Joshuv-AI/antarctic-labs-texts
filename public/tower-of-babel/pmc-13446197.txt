Associations Between Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy, Cognitive Impairment, and Depressive Symptoms
Authors: Nikita Nukala, Ryan T. Muir, Andrew E. Beaudin, Cheryl R. McCreary, Myrlene Gee, Krista Nelles, Feryal Saad, Janina Valencia, Glen C. Jickling, Dylan X. Guan, Zahinoor Ismail, Richard M. Camicioli, Eric E. Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesCerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is associated with intracerebral hemorrhage, cognitive decline, and dementia. We examined (1) associations between CAA, cognitive impairment, and depressive symptoms; (2) whether depressive symptoms mediate the relationship between CAA and cognition; and (3) whether CAA neuroimaging markers predict depressive symptom severity.MethodsRecruitment occurred through memory and stroke prevention clinics across 2 sites. Cross-sectional data from probable CAA and controls were analyzed. Neuropsychological tests were grouped into episodic memory, executive function, and processing speed domains. Scores ≥5 on the Geriatric Depression Scale: Short Form (GDS-15) defined “possible depression.” Regression and mediation analyses examined associations among CAA status, cognition, and depressive symptoms, and in CAA, associations between neuroimaging biomarkers and GDS-15.ResultsIn 85 CAA (mean age 73.5; 35.3% female) and 83 controls (mean age 68.8; 62.7% female), CAA status was associated with poorer performance across cognitive domains. Higher GDS-15 scores were associated with poorer performance across domains. CAA participants had higher odds of “possible depression” (odds ratio 15.71; 95% CI 4.26–80.05, p < 0.001) and scored 2.71 times higher on the GDS-15 than controls (95% CI 2.10–3.51, p < 0.001). In mediation, “possible depression” accounted for significant proportions of the effect of CAA on episodic memory (11%; β = −0.14, 95% CI −0.30 to −0.03, p = 0.014) and executive function (9%; β = −0.14, 95% CI −0.30 to −0.02, p = 0.016), but not processing speed (2%; β = −0.04, 95% CI −0.18 to 0.08, p = 0.56). In 81 CAA participants, higher GDS-15 scores were associated with lower mean cortical thickness (count ratio [CR] 1.33 per SD decrease in thickness, 95% CI 1.09–1.62, p = 0.005), presence of cortical superficial siderosis (CR 2.04, 95% CI 1.35–3.09, p < 0.001), and higher CAA small vessel disease total score (CR 1.16, 95% CI 1.00–1.35, p = 0.047).DiscussionCAA participants exhibited greater depressive symptoms and poorer cognition than controls. Depressive symptoms mediated a small portion of the association between CAA and cognition. Cerebral cortex damage may underlie some depressive symptoms. Interventions targeting CAA-related neurodegeneration and treatment of depressive symptoms may support cognitive health in CAA.

Introduction

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is a cerebral small vessel disease characterized by the deposition of β-amyloid in the walls of cerebral vessels.1 In a community-based autopsy study, CAA was linked to an increased risk of dementia and cognitive impairment, along with antemortem impairments in global cognition, perceptual speed, episodic memory, and semantic memory.2 Similarly, participants with CAA have been found to perform worse than age-adjusted norms on tests of episodic memory, executive function, and processing speed.3

CAA can be clinically diagnosed using established MRI markers.4 Characteristic neuroimaging biomarkers of CAA, such as white matter lesions, have been linked to impaired cognitive performance.5 Other CAA-related markers, including white matter hyperintensities (WMH), disseminated cortical superficial siderosis (cSS), and higher small vessel disease (SVD) scores, are similarly associated with an increased risk of developing dementia following an intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH).6 Together, these disease-related MRI features highlight the impact of CAA-related vascular injury on cognitive outcomes.

Beyond cognitive and structural consequences, CAA has also been linked to depression. In a study conducted by our research group exploring the neuropsychiatric profiles of participants with CAA, depression/dysphoria was the most frequently reported symptom on the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire.7 In addition, survivors of CAA-related ICH are more likely to experience depression, both before and after hemorrhage, compared with those with ICH because of hypertensive arteriopathy.8 Studies examining depression in CAA have primarily used informant reports or have focused on certain populations of participants with CAA, such as survivors of ICH or specific CAA subtypes.7,9-11 Kaushik et al.11 used the self-reported Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale and identified that 25% of participants with CAA had symptoms of depression or anxiety, highlighting the need to further explore depressive symptoms across the broader CAA population to inform appropriate screening and interventions.

Depression is also independently associated with cognitive impairment. Individuals with depression have poorer performance on tests of episodic memory relative to healthy controls.12 In addition, deficits in various elements of executive function, such as working memory, planning, and mental flexibility are also present in those with major depression.13 Impairments in processing speed are similarly associated with depressive symptoms.13

Given the potential heightened risk of depression in CAA, understanding how depressive symptoms relate to both cognitive impairment and CAA pathology is critical. Early identification and treatment of depressive symptoms might help support cognitive function in this population. Furthermore, clarifying the neurobiologic changes associated with depressive symptoms in CAA could help identify patients most at risk of cognitive impairment and who may benefit from targeted screening and interventions. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to examine how depressive symptoms may influence the relationship between CAA and cognitive function. A secondary objective was to determine whether CAA-mediated brain damage is associated with increased depressive symptoms.

Methods

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study was approved by the Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board of the University of Calgary (REB15-0601) and the Health Research Ethics Board of the University of Alberta (Pro00067006). All participants provided written informed consent.

Study Participants

Participants ≥55 years of age were recruited between 2011 and 2020 for the Functional Assessment of Vascular Reactivity study, a 2-center Canadian prospective longitudinal cohort study.14 An age of ≥55 years was selected as the Boston Criteria version 1.5 were used at study inception. Recruitment occurred in Calgary and Edmonton. For the current study, baseline data from CAA and cognitively normal control participants were included. Participants with probable CAA were identified from either stroke prevention, memory, or geriatric clinics using the Boston Criteria version 2.0.4 Cognitively normal controls between age 60 and 85 years were recruited using community advertising, had no history of stroke or other CNS diseases, and Montreal Cognitive Assessment ≥26. Participants were excluded if they were not fluent in English or French, had ongoing alcohol or drug use affecting cognition, or were unable to undergo a MRI scan.

Assessments

Clinical Assessment

Sociodemographic information and medical history were collected from participants using standardized case report forms.

Neuropsychological Assessment Battery

A 1.25-hour neuropsychological test battery was conducted at the baseline visit to assess episodic memory, executive function, and information processing speed. In November of 2016, several of the neuropsychological tests were replaced with alternate tests to harmonize assessments with a national study of cognitive disorders.15

Individual testing scores were converted to z scores using published normative data from test manuals,16,17 adjusting for age, sex, and education and were then averaged into overall domain z scores.3

For the episodic memory domain in the first (pre-2016) study iteration, an average z score of the Rey-Osterreith Complex Figure delayed recall18 and California Verbal Learning Test II delayed recall19 was taken. In the second (post-2016) iteration, scores were based on an average of the Brief Visuospatial Memory Test Revised delayed recall20 and the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test delayed recall.18 Executive function domain scores were the average of the z scores on the Trail Making B17 and the Controlled Oral Word Association Test-FAS21 for the first iteration, and average of the Trail Making B22 and the phonemic and category fluency in the Delis-Kaplan Executive Function System23 in the second iteration. Finally, across both study iterations, the processing speed domain was quantified as the average z scores on the Trail Making A22 and the digit symbol coding test of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (third or fourth edition).24

Geriatric Depression Scale: Short Form

The self-reported 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale: Short Form (GDS-15) was used to assess the presence of depressive symptoms in participants.25 Each item on the scale is answered as “yes” or “no,” scored as either a 1 or 0, with higher scores indicating more depressive symptoms. Although the 30-item Geriatric Depression Scale26 was introduced in the second study iteration, only questions corresponding to the Short Form version were included for this analysis, allowing for the pooling of data across both study iterations. The Geriatric Depression Scale total scores were consequently calculated out of a maximum of 15 points, and GDS-15 total scores ≥5 were used as a threshold to identify participants with symptoms suggestive of depression.26,27 Those with total scores ≥5 were therefore categorized into a group referred to as “possible depression.”

MRI Acquisition and Processing

A 3.0T General Electric Discovery MR750 MRI scanner was used at the Calgary site, and a 3.0T Siemens Prisma scanner was used at the Edmonton site. Images were reviewed by an experienced neurologist (E.E.S.) and evaluated for CAA based on Boston Criteria 2.0.4 T1-weighted images were processed with FreeSurfer (v6.0)28 for cortical thickness. FreeSurfer output was reviewed and adjusted manually, as necessary. Mean cortical thickness, in millimeters, was computed as the average of the right and left hemispheres and subsequently converted into z scores. In cases where there were large, destructive hemorrhagic lesions, we used the unaffected hemisphere only. Several markers of interest were defined in accordance with the standards for reporting vascular changes on neuroimaging criteria29 and identified using T2*-weighted gradient recalled echo images, including enlarged perivascular spaces (PVS) grades in the centrum semiovale, presence of any cSS, and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs). Enlarged PVS grades were calculated using the Wardlaw rating scale (range: 0 [no PVS], 1 [mild; 1–10 PVS], 2 [moderate; 11–20 PVS], 3 [frequent; 21–40 PVS] or 4 [severe; >40 PVS]).30 WMH volumes were quantified by trained readers from FLAIR images using a previously published semiautomated seed-based 3D region growing algorithm (Cerebra-Lesion-Extractor v1.1.2, CIPAC, University of Calgary).14,31 CAA-related SVD burden scores were quantified using an ordinal scale constructed from various disease-related MRI markers, including lobar CMBs (2–4 = 1 point, ≥5 = 2 points), cSS (focal = 1 point; disseminated = 2 points), moderate to severe enlarged PVS within the centrum semiovale (grades 3–4, i.e., >20 = 1 point), and moderate WMH burden (rated using the Fazekas scale, with Fazekas subcortical score ≥2 or periventricular score of 3 = 1 point).32,33

Statistical Analyses

Participant characteristics between patients with CAA and cognitively normal controls were compared using independent samples t tests, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, or χ2 tests. GDS-15 total scores were compared across CAA symptom presentations using Kruskal-Wallis tests.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to explore the association between CAA status and “possible depression” (i.e., GDS-15 ≥ 5), adjusting for age, sex, and years of education. To further investigate the relationship between depressive symptoms and CAA, Poisson regression was used to examine the association between GDS-15 total scores, treated as a count variable, and CAA status. The exponentiated coefficients from the Poisson regression represent the multiplicative change in the expected GDS-15 score, which we term count ratios (CRs), associated with being in the CAA group compared to the control group, after controlling for age, sex, and years of education. Multivariable linear regressions were used to determine the association between “possible depression” and the 3 cognitive domains, with all models adjusting for age, sex, and years of education. The possibility of modification by sex was explored using interaction terms, and if the interaction term was not significant, it was removed from the model. Causal mediation analyses using 1,000 simulations were performed to determine the proportion of the effect of CAA on cognition mediated by possible depression, controlling for age, sex, and years of education. Additional mediation analyses were conducted to determine the extent to which the effect of CAA on cognition was mediated by GDS-15 total scores, with GDS-15 modelled as a count variable, controlling for age, sex, and years of education.

For secondary analyses, negative binomial regression models were used to examine associations between selected MRI markers of CAA and the GDS-15 total score (which was treated as a count variable). All models were adjusted for age, sex, and years of education. As with Poisson regression, the exponentiated beta coefficients can be interpreted as CRs.

Results were considered statistically significant with a 2-sided p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were completed using R version 4.4.1,34 with the tidyverse,35 mediation,36 and MASS packages.37

Data Availability

Deidentified study data are available on reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Results

Demographic and Cognitive Data

The initial sample consisted of 182 participants. Neuropsychological testing data were missing for some participants across cognitive domains: episodic memory (n = 3), executive function (n = 1), and processing speed (n = 2). Several participants were missing GDS-15 total scores (n = 8). A complete case analysis was conducted, resulting in a final sample of 168 participants (85 CAA; 83 cognitively normal controls) for the primary analyses.

Baseline participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. The participants with CAA presented with CAA-related inflammation (n = 2; studied in remission after steroid treatment), cognitive impairment (n = 27), stroke caused by lobar ICH (n = 33), and transient focal neurologic episodes (n = 23). Compared with controls, participants with CAA were older, had a higher proportion of men, and had fewer years of education (all p < 0.001). A higher proportion of CAA participants had a history of hypertension than controls (p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in proportions of hypercholesterolemia (p = 0.35), diabetes (p = 0.46), and current (p = 0.72), or past smoking history (p = 0.08) between the groups. Among participants with available data (CAA: n = 85; controls: n = 77), a self-reported history of depression was present in 14 (16.5%) CAA participants and 30 (41.1%) controls (p = 0.004). Median GDS-15 score was higher for the CAA group than for controls (Figure 1). Within the CAA group, 20 participants (11.9%) met criteria for “possible depression” (GDS-15 ≥ 5), compared with 3 (1.8%) participants in the control group (p < 0.001). Mean z scores across all 3 cognitive domains (Table 1) indicate that the CAA group were significantly impaired compared with controls (p < 0.001 for all domains). Median GDS-15 scores were similar for CAA patients with ICH (median 2, interquartile range [IQR] 1–5), transient focal neurologic episodes (TFNE) (median 2, IQR 0.5–6), and cognitive impairment or inflammation (median 2, IQR 1–3), with no significant difference between CAA symptom presentation groups (p = 0.91). Eighteen (21.2%) participants in the CAA group and 20 participants in the control group (24.1%). reported use of antidepressant medication.

Associations Between CAA and Depression

In analyses adjusted for age, sex, and education, patients with CAA had higher odds of having “possible depression” compared with controls (odds ratio 15.71; 95% CI 4.26–80.05, p < 0.001). Patients with CAA had 2.71 times more depressive symptoms on the GDS-15 than controls (CR 2.71, 95% CI 2.10–3.51, p < 0.001).

Associations Between “Possible Depression” and Cognitive Performance Across Domains

“Possible depression” status was significantly associated with poorer performance in episodic memory (β = −1.03, 95% CI −1.53 to −0.53, p < 0.001), executive function (β = −1.11, 95% CI −1.65 to −0.58, p < 0.001), and processing speed (β = −0.73, 95% CI −1.24 to −0.21, p = 0.006), after adjusting for age, sex, and years of education. Interactions between “possible depression” and sex were not significant.

Mediation of the Association of CAA With Cognitive Function by Depression

In mediation analyses (Figure 2), CAA status was associated with poorer cognition after controlling for age, sex, and years of education. When accounting for mediating variables, 89%, 91%, and 97% of the total effects of CAA on episodic memory, executive function, and processing speed, respectively, were attributable to direct effects of CAA, independent of depression, although there were significant indirect (mediation) effects of “possible depression” for the associations between CAA and both episodic memory and executive function. For episodic memory, the indirect effect was (β = −0.14, 95% CI −0.30 to −0.03, p = 0.014) and accounted for approximately 11% of the total effect. Similarly, for executive function, the indirect effect (β = −0.14, 95% CI −0.30 to −0.02, p = 0.016) accounted for 9% of the total effect. However, “possible depression” did not mediate the relationship between CAA and processing speed (β = −0.04, 95% CI −0.18 to 0.08, p = 0.56), contributing only 2% to the total effect.

Additional mediation analyses (Figure 3) were conducted using GDS-15 total scores as a count variable modeled with Poisson regression, rather than the categorical “possible depression” status. In these models, the direct effect of CAA accounted for 87%, 93%, and 92% of the total effect for episodic memory, executive function, and processing speed, respectively. For episodic memory, the indirect effect mediated by count of depressive symptoms was significant (β = −0.17, 95% CI −0.35 to −0.03, p = 0.01), representing approximately 12% of the total effect. For executive function, the indirect effect mediated by count of depressive symptoms was not statistically significant (β = −0.10, 95% CI −0.25 to 0.02, p = 0.11), accounting for approximately 6% of the total effect. Finally, for processing speed the indirect effect of processing speed was also not significant (β = −0.11, 95% CI −0.28 to 0.02, p = 0.10), accounting for 7% of the total effect.

Associations Between Depressive Symptoms and CAA-Related MRI Markers

Of 93 participants meeting the modified Boston criteria for probable CAA,4 4 were excluded for missing GDS-15 data, 1 for missing MRI, and 7 for processing errors from large destructive hemorrhages, leaving 81 participants for analysis. In this sample, the mean age was 73.7 years (SD = 7.36), 37% were female (n = 30), and mean years of education was 14.2 (SD = 3.14). The median GDS-15 score for CAA participants was 2 (IQR 0–4). Participants presented with CAA-related inflammation (n = 2), cognitive impairment (n = 26), stroke caused by lobar ICH (n = 31), and transient focal neurologic episodes (n = 22). Median GDS-15 scores were again similar for patients across CAA presentation types: ICH (median 2, IQR 1–5), TFNE (median 2, IQR 1–6), and cognitive impairment or inflammation (median 2, IQR 1–3.25), with no significant difference between CAA symptom presentations (p = 0.91). Sixteen (17.2%) participants reported the use of an antidepressant medication.

In negative binomial regression with GDS-15 total scores as the outcome and adjusted for age, sex, and years of education (Table 2), higher GDS-15 total scores were significantly associated with lower mean cortical thickness (CR 1.33 per SD decrease in thickness, 95% CI 1.09–1.62, p = 0.005), any cSS (CR 2.04, 95% CI 1.35–3.09, p < 0.001), and CAA SVD total score (CR 1.16, 95% CI 1.00–1.35, p = 0.047). Higher GDS-15 scores were, however, not significantly associated with centrum semiovale PVS grade (CR 0.95 per 1 point increase, 95% CI 0.76–1.19, p = 0.67), CMB count (CR 1.00 per additional CMB, 95% CI 0.997–1.001, p = 0.28), or WMH volume (CR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.02, p = 0.06).

Discussion

Participants with CAA were more likely to have “possible depression” (GDS-15 ≥5) compared with cognitively normal controls, with CAA status being associated with higher GDS-15 total scores. Compared with cognitively normal controls, those with CAA had poorer test performances across episodic memory, executive function, and processing speed cognitive domains. We found that depressive symptoms, as measured by either the GDS-15 total score or by “possible depression,” partly mediated the relationship between CAA and cognitive impairment. Mediation analyses conducted using GDS-15 scores produced comparable findings, with indirect effects accounting for 12% of the total effect on episodic memory (compared with 11% using “possible depression” status), 6% on executive function (compared with 9%), and 7% on processing speed (compared with 2%). There are several plausible interpretations of these data. First, these findings may be consistent with the hypothesized causal model whereby CAA affects cognition by causing depressive symptoms. However, other explanations include reverse or bidirectional relationships. Future longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the complex relationships between cognition and depressive symptoms. Notwithstanding this, these results raise the possibility that treating depressive symptoms could improve cognitive function in patients with CAA, although with modest effects. In addition, greater depressive symptoms in CAA were associated with lower mean cortical thickness, the presence of cSS, and higher CAA SVD scores. Interventions that target cortical neurodegeneration could potentially improve depressive symptoms in patients with CAA. Overall, a broader understanding of depression in CAA is essential for improving overall mental health and guiding appropriate clinical care.

Cerebral small vessel disease is associated with a higher prevalence of depression,38 and depressive symptoms are the most common neuropsychiatric symptom among individuals with CAA.7 Our finding that participants with CAA were more likely to meet the threshold for “possible depression” and to have elevated GDS-15 scores is consistent with previous work. In line with previous studies that used informant reports or examined depression in specific subtypes of CAA,7,9,10,39 our results further support the association between CAA and an increased risk of depressive symptoms. Given that depression can contribute to cognitive deficits in areas such as memory, executive function, and processing speed,13 assessing its impact in individuals with CAA is essential for deepening our understanding of cognitive function in this patient population.

Previously, using a study cohort that partly overlaps with the current one, we found that CAA was associated with impaired performance on tests of episodic memory, executive function, and processing speed.3 This study replicates these findings in a larger cohort. Importantly, the relationship between depression in patients with CAA and cognitive impairment has been underexplored. CAA is a leading cause of lobar ICH,40 and ICH itself is associated with deficits in information processing speed, executive function, and memory.41 Survivors of CAA-related ICH exhibited higher levels of depressive symptoms both before and after ICH, in comparison with survivors of ICH because of hypertensive arteriopathy.8 Furthermore, CAA is related to Alzheimer disease pathology,2 a condition similarly linked to elevated depressive symptoms42 and cognitive decline.2,43 This overlapping risk profile makes it critical to examine whether addressing depression in patients with CAA could potentially mitigate cognitive impairments and improve overall clinical outcomes. Although the cognitive deficits experienced by many patients with CAA may be more directly attributable to other CAA-directed mechanisms, such as ischemic damage to cerebral white matter,44 our findings suggest that depressive symptoms may be an important contributor.

To explore potential neurobiologic mechanisms underlying these depressive symptoms, we examined their relationship with MRI markers of CAA. We found that greater depressive symptoms in CAA were associated with reduced cortical thickness, the presence of cSS, and higher CAA SVD scores, supporting the notion that depressive symptoms may partly reflect underlying neurodegeneration and cerebrovascular pathology in CAA. Previous studies have demonstrated that CAA-related imaging markers, including white matter lesions and decreased brain volume, are associated with cognitive impairment.5,45 Similarly, post-ICH dementia has been linked to MRI features such as increased WMH, disseminated cSS, and higher SVD scores.6 Our findings suggest that cortical and vascular brain damage may contribute to the depressive symptoms observed in individuals with CAA, pointing to a potential mechanism through which CAA-related brain changes contribute to both depressive symptoms and cognitive deficits.

Key strengths of this study are the inclusion of cognitively normal controls as a comparison group, a well-phenotyped cohort of CAA participants, the use of mediation analysis to assess the effect of depression on cognition in CAA, and the inclusion of neuroimaging markers of CAA. A limitation is the cross-sectional study design, which restricts our ability to examine how depressive symptoms relate to changes in cognition over time in participants with CAA and precludes us from ascertaining the potential duration that depressive symptoms were present, which could have predated study enrollment and itself may be a useful variable to evaluate in future longitudinal analyses. In addition, the observed associations could also reflect alternative explanations, such as depressive symptoms developing in response to cognitive decline or overall disease burden. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the potential causal effects of depressive symptoms as they interact with cognitive changes over time.

It is also possible that there are unmeasured confounding variables influencing the mediation analysis, despite controlling for age, sex, and years of education. Although we failed to find evidence that the association between depressive symptoms and cognition differed in men and women, there was limited power for these analyses and this should be revisited in future, larger studies. In addition, some neuropsychological tests were replaced with alternative measures across the 2 study iterations. Although replacements were chosen based on their validated psychometric properties and conceptual overlap, differences between test versions may have affected the results.

Although we employed the GDS-15, a validated and widely used screening tool for depressive symptoms in older adults,46 we did not include structured diagnostic interviews such as the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-547 to confirm diagnoses of major depressive disorder. This is notable, as the GDS-15 has been shown to overestimate the prevalence of depression in cognitively impaired populations,48 raising the possibility that participants categorized as having “possible depression” may not meet formal diagnostic criteria for depression. Future studies would benefit from incorporating these diagnostic tools to improve the validity of depression classifications. Use of antidepressant medications may have also influenced GDS-15 scores, potentially leading to underestimation of depressive symptoms in treated individuals or a misclassification of symptom status, particularly if medications were prescribed for nondepressive indications. Although controls generally had lower depressive symptom scores than participants with CAA, a substantial proportion were taking antidepressants and reported a history of depression at some point in time, suggesting that some may have been receiving treatment for depression or other indications.

It is possible that the control group may not be fully representative of the general population as they were not selected at random. Volunteer controls may underrepresent individuals with current or severe depressive symptoms, which could affect prevalence estimates and comparisons between groups. Notably, although a substantial portion of controls self-reported a history of depression, their current depressive symptom levels were lower than in the CAA group. This discrepancy may reflect differences between symptoms across the lifespan, as well as a potential selection bias in volunteer controls. This should be considered when interpreting the observed differences in depressive symptoms and cognition.

Furthermore, the absence of additional measures that may influence depressive symptoms, such as fatigue, anxiety, apathy, or medication usage beyond antidepressants, represents a limitation. Future studies incorporating these variables will be important to more fully understand the relationships among CAA, depressive symptoms, and cognition.

Clinicians should routinely screen for depressive symptoms in patients with CAA using validated tools like the GDS-15.46 Proactively screening for and addressing mental health concerns in this population is crucial, as depression is known to adversely impact a range of health outcomes, including stroke recovery49 and overall quality of life.50 It may also speak to the need for increased interdisciplinary care models incorporating close collaborations with geriatric psychiatry in clinics caring for patients with CAA. Furthermore, recognizing that those CAA patients with low cortical thickness, cSS, and a high burden of small vessel disease may have an increased risk of depressive symptoms may inform targeted screening and have utility to identify the highest risk CAA patients where depressive symptoms may be contributing to cognitive impairment. Timely identification and appropriate intervention to address depressive symptoms in patients with CAA may provide improvements to cognitive function, promote patient well-being and quality of life, and the mitigation of other health complications.
