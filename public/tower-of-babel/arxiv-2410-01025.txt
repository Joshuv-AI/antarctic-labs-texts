Abstract
. We consider the two-dimensional two-component plasma, or Coulomb gas, consisting of N N positive and N N negative charges with logarithmic interaction. We introduce a suitable regularization of the interaction by smearing the charges over a small length scale λ \lambda , which allows us to give meaning to the system in the continuum at any temperature. We provide an expansion of the free energy for each inverse temperature β ≥ 2 \beta\geq 2 , as N → ∞ N\to\infty and λ → 0 \lambda\to 0 . Doing so allows us to show that, for β ≥ 2 \beta\geq 2 , the charges (for the most part) pair into neutral dipoles of very small size as λ → 0 \lambda\to 0 . This complements the prior work of Leblé, Zeitouni, and the second author, which proved that this does not happen for β < 2 \beta<2 , thereby implying a transition at β = 2 \beta=2 from free charges to dipole pairs. Moreover, we obtain an estimate on the size of linear statistics. The description in terms of dipoles is made via a decomposition into nearest-neighbor graphs of the point configurations, à la Gunson–Panta. This is combined with new energy estimates obtained via an electric reformulation of the interaction energy and a ball-growth method, which are expressed in terms of the nearest-neighbor graph distances only. In this way, the model is compared to a reduced nearest-neighbor interaction model, by showing the relative smallness of the dipole-dipole interactions.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Setting of the problem

In the 1970s, Kosterlitz and Thouless
[
20
,
19
]
and independently Berezinsky
[
5
]
predicted a completely new type of phase transition without long-range order in two-dimensional systems, now called the Berezinsky-Kosterlitz-Thouless (BKT) transition. This celebrated transition (see
[
6
]
for a review) was predicted to happen in a whole range of models which exhibit quantized vortices in a neutral ensemble, more specifically the XY or “rotator” spin model, models of dislocations and superfluids, and it has important consequences for condensed matter physics.

The transition in the XY model is probably the one that has attracted the most attention in the mathematical physics community.
In this model unit spins are sampled on a lattice, constituting a
\mathbb{U}(1)
analogue of the Ising model. The correlation function between distant spins decays exponentially above the BKT transition temperature and as a power law below
[
29
,
12
,
27
,
28
,
8
]
.

This transition is explained by the formation of topological vortices, which are points around which the spin field has a nonzero degree or winding number. Below the transition temperature, vortices are bound into dipole pairs (i.e., pairs of vortices with opposite winding numbers), while above the transition temperature, vortices are like “free particles”.

In the original papers, as well as in subsequent research, it is expected that in the XY model (or its simplified variant, the Villain model) the energy of the system can be split into a vortex-gas energy and a spin-wave contribution, corresponding to the fluctuations around the vortex configurations
[
20
,
19
,
18
]
.
This statement turns out to be delicate to prove rigorously, and this has attracted the attention of researchers, even recently
[
16
,
15
]
.

Once the spin-wave contribution can be separated, the model reduces to a (2D) gas of positive and negative charges with logarithmic interaction, which can also be called a two-component plasma, or (two-component) Coulomb gas. The Coulomb gas is thus a fundamental model on which to understand the BKT transition, as seen in the original paper of Kosterlitz
[
19
]
.

The lattice (two-component) Coulomb gas was studied in the seminal work of Fröhlich-Spencer
[
12
]
via the sine-Gordon representation and expansions into multipole ensembles, allowing them to analyze the decay rate of correlation functions, thus giving the first proof of the BKT transition (see also
[
29
,
27
,
28
]
).

The Coulomb gas may as well be studied in the continuum rather than on a lattice, and is expected to exhibit the same transition between a situation with free vortices and a situation with vortices of opposite sign bound in dipole pairs. There is a subtlety, however, due to the fact that this “dipole transition” should happen at inverse temperature
\beta=2
in the units we use, while the KT transition between exponentially and algebraically decaying correlations is expected to happen at
\beta=4
in this setting. Also, it is a little delicate to directly compare the situation of the Coulomb gas in the continuum where one takes a fixed number
N
of charges of each sign, corresponding to a canonical ensemble, and the situation of the XY model, corresponding to a grand-canonical ensemble where the number of vortices is not prescribed.

Here we will focus on the continuum Coulomb gas or “two-component plasma” in the canonical case and we will provide a proof of the “dipole transition” based simply on the analysis of dipole pairs via large deviations techniques that allow us to weigh their energy and entropy costs, in some sense very close to the arguments and computations found in the original papers
[
19
,
20
]
and also in the seminal paper
[
17
]
.

It was predicted in
[
12
,
4
,
14
]
that such a system also has an infinite sequence of transitions at

\beta_{p}:=4-\frac{2}{p},\quad\text{where $p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}$},

which corresponds to the divergence (at small scale) of the free energy of multipoles of size
2p
(i.e. sets of
p
dipoles clustered together).
This was mostly analyzed by sine-Gordon transformation, using field theory techniques and Mayer expansions
[
12
,
4
,
14
,
10
]
. Analyzing these multipole transitions, and obtaining refined asymptotic expansions via cluster expansions, is the object of our subsequent paper
[
7
]
.

1.2.
Model

We first consider the continuum Coulomb gas, defined as an ensemble with configurations
(X_{N},Y_{N})
(with
X_{N}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\Lambda^{N}
and
Y_{N}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})\in\Lambda^{N}
) of
N
positive and
N
negative particles (or vortices with degrees
+1
or
-1
) in the blown-up cube
\Lambda=[0,\sqrt{N}]^{2}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, having energy

(1.1)

\mathsf{F}(X_{N},Y_{N})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl(-\log|x_{i}-x_{j}|-\log|y_{i}-y_{j}|\bigr)+2\sum_{i,j}\log|x_{i}-y_{j}|\right),

and
consider the (canonical) ensemble

(1.2)

\frac{1}{Z_{N,\beta}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathsf{F}(X_{N},Y_{N})\right)\mathrm{d}X_{N}\mathrm{d}Y_{N},

with
\mathrm{d}X_{N}
and
\mathrm{d}Y_{N}
the Lebesgue measures on
\Lambda^{N}
.
This model was studied in particular in
[
17
,
9
]
, and more recently in
[
22
]
.
The integral defining
Z_{N,\beta}
diverges as soon as
\beta\geq 2
, since the Boltzmann weight of very short dipoles is not integrable, which corresponds to the dipole transition.
The ensemble (
1.2
) was thus studied only in the regime
\beta<2
in the aforementioned works
[
17
,
9
,
22
]
.
The latest results of
[
22
]
, building on important insights from
[
17
]
and techniques developed for the study of the one-component Coulomb gas in
[
35
,
32
,
23
,
24
]
, show an expansion of
\log Z_{N,\beta}
as
N\to\infty
, as well as a large deviations principle on point processes, which characterize a situation with free interacting particles, with competition between the attraction of opposite charges and the entropic repulsion. This corresponds to temperatures above the critical temperature.

In order to study such a system for
\beta\geq 2
, a truncation of the interaction is needed, as already recognized in
[
20
,
19
]
and analyzed in
[
12
]
(see also the discussion in
[
21
]
).
For brevity, let us always denote

\mathsf{g}(x)=-\log|x|,

and we will abuse notation by considering
\mathsf{g}
as a function on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or on
\mathbb{R}
depending on the context.

Truncating the interaction involves introducing a small length scale
\lambda
and
renormalizing
the divergent part of the free energy as
\lambda\to 0
. A natural proposed way is to truncate the energy at a distance
\lambda
and consider

(1.3)

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\bigl(\mathsf{g}(x_{i}-x_{j})\wedge\mathsf{g}(\lambda)+\mathsf{g}(y_{i}-y_{j})\wedge\mathsf{g}(\lambda)-2\bigl(\mathsf{g}(x_{i}-y_{j})\wedge\mathsf{g}(\lambda)\bigr)\bigr)-N\mathsf{g}(\lambda),

where
\wedge
denotes the minimum of two numbers.

We use a variant convenient for our techniques: instead of truncating
\mathsf{g}
we consider charges smeared on discs of radius
\lambda
, with
\lambda
small, interacting otherwise in the normal Coulomb fashion: letting
\delta_{z}^{(\lambda)}
denote the uniform measure of mass
1
supported on
B(z,\lambda)
, we let

\kappa:=\iint\mathsf{g}(x-y)\delta_{0}^{(1)}(x)\delta_{0}^{(1)}(y),

and observe, by scaling, that

(1.4)

\iint\mathsf{g}(x-y)\delta_{0}^{(\lambda)}(x)\delta_{0}^{(\lambda)}(y)=\mathsf{g}(\lambda)+\kappa.

We then consider the energy

(1.5)

\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}(X_{N},Y_{N})=\frac{1}{2}\iint\mathsf{g}(x-y)\mathrm{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\delta_{x_{i}}^{(\lambda)}-\delta_{y_{i}}^{(\lambda)}\bigr)\right)(x)\mathrm{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\delta_{x_{i}}^{(\lambda)}-\delta_{y_{i}}^{(\lambda)}\bigr)\right)(y)-N(\mathsf{g}(\lambda)+\kappa).

Here, compared to (
1.1
) we have reinserted the self-interaction terms which are no longer infinite but equal to
\mathsf{g}(\lambda)+\kappa
,
and then subtracted them off.

We will denote by

(1.6)

\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(z)=\iint\mathsf{g}(x-y)\delta_{0}^{(\lambda)}(x)\delta_{z}^{(\lambda)}(y),

the effective interaction between two points at distance
|z|
. Since the convolution
\mathsf{g}*\delta_{0}^{(\lambda)}
is harmonic outside of
B(0,\lambda)
, it follows from the mean-value theorem (or Newton’s theorem) that

\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(z)=\int\mathsf{g}*\delta^{(\lambda)}_{0}\delta_{z}^{(\lambda)}=\mathsf{g}(z)\quad\text{for }|z|\geq 2\lambda,

so
\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}
coincides with
\mathsf{g}
at large enough distance.

Thus
\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}
is obtained from (
1.3
) by replacing the truncated kernel by
\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}
, including in the self-interaction term, and subtracting
N\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(0)=N(\mathsf{g}(\lambda)+\kappa)
instead of
N\mathsf{g}(\lambda)
. If all pairwise distances are at least
2\lambda
, then
\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}=\mathsf{F}
. In particular,
\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}\to\mathsf{F}
as
\lambda\to 0
for configurations of distinct points. The kernel
\mathsf{g}_{1}
and the constant
\kappa
depend on the smearing profile, as does
\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}
below when
\beta>2
.
Finally, we could in principle use charges smeared on circles instead of discs as in previous works
[
30
,
23
,
24
]
. It does make the initial computations simpler but the potential generated by a circle is too singular for our needs here.

We thus work with (
1.5
) and study

(1.7)

\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\mathsf{Z}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}(X_{N},Y_{N})\right)\mathrm{d}X_{N}\mathrm{d}Y_{N}

in the limit where
\lambda
tends to zero, where

\mathsf{Z}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}=\int_{\Lambda^{N}\times\Lambda^{N}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}(X_{N},Y_{N})\right)\mathrm{d}X_{N}\mathrm{d}Y_{N}.

When
\beta<2
one can set
\lambda=0
and recover the model studied in
[
22
]
, but when
\beta\geq 2
one expects
\log\mathsf{Z}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}
to diverge as
\lambda\to 0
.
The picture that emerges from the literature, mostly based on the sine-Gordon representation,
is well-described in
[
21
]
:
for
\beta>2
, the divergence of the system as
\lambda\to 0
corresponds to the pairing of short dipoles (of length scale
\lambda
), and the transition at
\beta=2
is followed as
\beta
increases by a sequence of transitions corresponding to the formation of a subdominant proportion of multipoles (quadrupoles, sextupoles, etc.) as the temperature is decreased and the entropic repulsion becomes less strong
[
12
]
. This is due to the weakly attractive nature of the dipole-dipole interaction. When
\beta
reaches
4
, in the grand canonical setting (when the number of particles is not fixed) the system is expected to collapse under the attraction of the dipoles, as first shown in
[
13
]
via a Euclidean Field Theory approach. However, we will see that it is not the case in the canonical setting here.

Our main goal here is to analyze (
1.5
)–(
1.7
) via a simple and direct approach based solely on energy and entropy, precisely via electrostatic estimates for the energy. We obtain below a precise free energy expansion as
N\to\infty
and
\lambda\to 0
, and use it to prove that configurations mostly form isolated neutral dipoles for all
\beta\in[2,+\infty)
(this shows that the multipoles, though present, concern only a vanishing fraction of the particles). Combined with the description of
[
22
]
, it constitutes a proof of the dipole transition, and we hope this point of view will also inform the understanding of the BKT transition. We also address the important question of the fluctuations of the (two-component) Coulomb gas.

Note that the two-component Coulomb gas or plasma is quite different from the one-component Coulomb gas or plasma, which consists only of positively charged particles repelling each other and confined by an external potential, or, in the jellium case, by a uniform negative background charge. The one-component Coulomb gas never diverges nor forms dipole pairs, but rather the particle density converges macroscopically to an equilibrium measure limit (dictated by the external potential) while at the microscopic scale the particles arrange themselves in more and more ordered point patterns as temperature decreases. In fact the system is expected to crystallize at zero temperature, at least in low dimensions. There has been much progress on the one-component plasma in recent years, including free energy expansion
[
26
,
23
,
2
,
36
]
, local laws for the distribution of points down to the microscale
[
25
,
2
]
, variational characterization of the limiting point processes
[
23
]
, and CLTs for the fluctuations of linear statistics
[
1
,
31
,
3
,
24
,
36
]
. We refer to
[
37
]
for a comprehensive description.

The main points in common with
[

,

,

,

,
2

]
but also with
[
22
]
will be the general philosophy of electrostatic energy and large deviations techniques, as well as the

of the energy that we present just below.

1.3.
Main results

The electric formulation mentioned above consists in reexpressing the energy in terms of the
electric potential

h_{\lambda}
generated by the configuration
(X_{N},Y_{N})
and defined as a function
over all

\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

(1.8)

h_{\lambda}[X_{N},Y_{N}]:=\mathsf{g}*\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\delta_{x_{i}}^{(\lambda)}-\delta_{y_{i}}^{(\lambda)}\bigr)\right),

where
*
denotes the convolution.
In the sequel, we will most often drop the
[X_{N},Y_{N}]
dependence in the notation.

Note that by definition of
\mathsf{g}
,
h_{\lambda}
satisfies the Poisson equation

(1.9)

-\Delta h_{\lambda}[X_{N},Y_{N}]=2\pi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\delta_{x_{i}}^{(\lambda)}-\delta_{y_{i}}^{(\lambda)}\bigr)\right).

A direct insertion into (
1.5
) and integration by parts using (
1.9
) yields the following electric rewriting of the energy

(1.10)

\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}(X_{N},Y_{N})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|\nabla h_{\lambda}[X_{N},Y_{N}]\right|^{2}-N(\mathsf{g}(\lambda)+\kappa).

Neutrality gives
h_{\lambda}(x)=O(|x|^{-1})
and
\nabla h_{\lambda}(x)=O(|x|^{-2})
at infinity. A smooth cutoff tending to
1
justifies the integration by parts.

Before stating our main result, let us introduce some more notation.
For
\beta>2
, let us define the probability measure

\mathrm{d}\mu_{\beta}(r):=\frac{2\pi}{\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}}\exp\Bigr(\beta\mathsf{g}_{1}(r)\Bigr)\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}(r)r\mathrm{d}r,

where
\mathsf{g}_{1}
was defined in (
1.6
) and
\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}
is given by

(1.11)

\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}:=\begin{cases}2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\Bigr(\beta\mathsf{g}_{1}(r)\Bigr)r\mathrm{d}r&\text{if $\beta>2$}\\
2\pi&\text{if $\beta=2$}.\end{cases}

For
\beta>2
, we have
0<\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}<\infty
, since
\mathsf{g}_{1}
is bounded on
[0,2]
and
e^{\beta\mathsf{g}_{1}(r)}r=r^{1-\beta}
for
r\geq 2
. This constant is related to our precise way of smearing the Dirac charges and corresponds to the interaction of overlapping disc charges.

We will denote by
(z_{1},\dots,z_{2N})=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N},y_{1},\dots,y_{N})
the collection of all points (positive or negative) and set
d_{i}=1
if
i\in[1,N]
and
d_{i}=-1
if
i\in[N+1,2N]
.
We exclude configurations with coinciding points or equal distances between distinct unordered pairs. This is a null set by Fubini. We denote by
\phi_{1}(i)
the index of the nearest neighbor to
z_{i}
.
A generic configuration can be quite complicated. In particular, it is not obvious how to extract its dipoles.
In this paper, dipoles are defined via the nearest neighbor graph of a configuration, following important ideas of
[
17
]
. This definition ignores, in the first pass, the sign of the particle, by considering only for each point
z_{i}
, its first nearest neighbor
z_{\phi_{1}(i)}
of arbitrary sign, and its scaled, truncated nearest neighbor distance
\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})
defined in (
2.2
).
Other definitions are possible, and we will pursue another approach in forthcoming work.

The nearest-neighbor graph is a (directed) graph on
\{1,\dots,2N\}
with an edge from
i
to
j=\phi_{1}(i)
, i.e. if
z_{j}
is the nearest neighbor to
z_{i}
. It is a general fact (see
[
11
]
) that the connected components of such nearest-neighbor graphs are all formed of a 2-cycle with trees possibly attached to them. Note that points in a 2-cycle are characterized by the fact that
\phi_{1}\circ\phi_{1}(i)=i
.

A nearest-neighbor graph with Gunson–Panta variables

We will say that a 2-cycle is isolated if it has no tree attached to it, i.e. forms a whole connected component of the graph. We will say that such a 2-cycle is an isolated neutral 2-cycle if in addition it connects two points of opposite sign, i.e., it connects
i
and
j
with
d_{i}d_{j}=-1
. We also call such 2-cycles “isolated dipoles.” The length of the dipole
\{i,j\}
is
|z_{i}-z_{j}|
.

Our first theorem provides a free energy expansion and a concentration on dipole configurations.

1.4.
Method

In order to prove Theorem
1

we need to obtain sharp upper and lower bounds on the energy of a configuration in terms of its dipoles, and good corresponding “volume estimates”.
This amounts to comparing the original model to a reduced nearest neighbor model
and the main work is to prove that the two models are close. Their closeness then allows us to show that the Gibbs measure concentrates on dipole configurations, control their sizes and control linear statistics.

Combining the description in terms of the nearest neighbor graph à la Gunson–Panta with the electric formulation (
1.10
) turns out to be an efficient way of obtaining energy lower bounds. This is done by a
ball-growth method
, which consists in expanding the smeared charges
\delta_{z_{i}}^{(\lambda)}
into a charge
\delta_{z_{i}}^{(\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i}))}
of the same mass but supported in the disc
B(z_{i},\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i}))
. Every disc whose radius grows stays disjoint from the other discs, so Newton’s theorem preserves the pairwise interactions. This, however, misses a term of order 1 in the interaction energy of each dipole.
In order to avoid this loss, we push the method further by examining second nearest neighbor distances
\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{i})
and grow each pair to a common radius determined by these distances.
In the works on the one-component plasma
[
24
,
2
,
36
]
, see also
[
37
, Chap. 4]
, we were able to take advantage of the fact that when all the charges are positive, the interaction of disc charges decreases when the radii are increased. This is no longer the case in a situation with different charge signs, and so instead of using monotonicity, we proceed to a direct estimation of the change in the interaction when the discs are increased. We then obtain an estimate which bounds from below the energy
in terms of nearest neighbor interactions only
.
This part contains the most delicate estimates as we need to control the contributions of the dipole-dipole interactions and the errors they create, something not handled in
[
17
,
22
]
. We provide more precise estimates than in those papers via new ideas.

An important feature of this dipole decomposition lower bound is that it is amenable to integration in phase-space with the method of Gunson–Panta, as revisited in
[
22
]
, of separating the integral over types of nearest neighbor graphs.
For the matching lower bound on the free energy, we bound the energy from above by a sum of local Neumann energies, one for each dipole.
Together with the constrained upper bounds, this yields concentration on dipole configurations, as in (
1.15
).

The bound on fluctuations of Theorem
2
is obtained by using the electric formulation of the energy which allows us to show that the energy directly controls the fluctuations, and combining it with the ball-growth method which is this time used by inflating the balls to an intermediate length scale
\theta
much larger than
\lambda
and much smaller than
1
.

Plan of the paper:
Section
2
is devoted to the proof of the energy lower bound in terms of nearest neighbor interactions with dipolar interaction errors, via the ball-growth method. In Section
3
, this lower bound is inserted into the Gibbs measure to produce, via a suitable decomposition of the phase-space and large deviation estimates, the free energy upper bound.
Section
4
provides a matching lower bound by explicit construction of configurations and estimates of their free energy. Finally, Section
5
provides the proof of Theorem
2
.

Lemma 2.1

(Bounded in-degree of nearest-neighbor graphs)
.

Let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{2N}
be distinct points of the plane such that distinct unordered pairs of points are at distinct distances. Then each point
z_{j}
is the first nearest neighbor of at most six points and the second nearest neighbor of at most twelve points. Moreover, if we choose one vertex from each isolated pair of the nearest-neighbor graph, then at most six chosen vertices have
z_{j}
as second nearest neighbor.

Proof.

Divide the plane into six half-open cones of angle
\pi/3
with apex
z_{j}
. Let
z
and
w
be two other points of the configuration in one cone, with
|z-z_{j}|\leq|w-z_{j}|
. The angle at
z_{j}
between them is less than

, so the cosine rule gives

|z-w|^{2}<|z-z_{j}|^{2}+|w-z_{j}|^{2}-|z-z_{j}||w-z_{j}|\leq|w-z_{j}|^{2}\,.

Hence
z
is closer to
w
than
z_{j}
is. As a consequence, each cone contains at most one point whose first nearest neighbor is
z_{j}
. It also contains at most two points whose second nearest neighbor is
z_{j}
, since otherwise the farthest of three such points would have two points closer than

. This proves the first two bounds.

Now let

and
w
be two chosen vertices in one cone, with

, and assume that both have
z_{j}
as second nearest neighbor. The partner of

, so it is closer to
w
than
z_{j}
. The point

is also closer to
w
than

, and it is not the partner of

, since we chose one vertex from each pair. This contradicts the fact that

is the second nearest neighbor of

. Hence each cone contains at most one chosen vertex whose second nearest neighbor is
z_{j}
.

2.12
). By symmetry, the sum reduces to
i=j
or mutual nearest neighbors, which we denote by
i\sim j
. For a mutual-neighbor pair whose enlarged discs intersect, (
2.12
) becomes

|z_{i}-z_{j}|\leq 2\tau_{i}=2\bigl(\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{i})\wedge\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{\phi_{1}(i)})\bigr).

Terms with both radii unchanged or with disjoint enlarged discs vanish. We may therefore restore all mutual-neighbor pairs to the sum. Dropping
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla h_{\vec{\tau}}|^{2}\geq 0
in (
2.11
) and using (
1.6
) and (
1.4
), we obtain

(2.13)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla h_{\lambda}|^{2}\\
\geq 2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\left(\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(0)-\mathsf{g}(\tau_{i})-\kappa\right)+2\pi\sum_{i\neq j:j\sim i}d_{i}d_{j}\Bigl(\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(z_{i}-z_{j})-\iint\mathsf{g}(x-y)\delta_{z_{i}}^{(\tau_{i})}(x)\delta_{z_{j}}^{(\tau_{j})}(y)\Bigr).

We examine the contribution of the
2
-cycles. If
i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
and
j=\phi_{1}(i)
, then
\tau_{i}=\tau_{j}
by definition and thus by (
2.6
) the contribution of the parenthesis in (
2.13
) is

2\pi d_{i}d_{j}\Bigl(\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(z_{i}-z_{j})-\mathsf{g}(\tau_{i})-\mathsf{g}_{1}(\frac{z_{i}-z_{j}}{\tau_{i}})\Bigr).

This term appears twice due to the two edges between the vertices of the cycle and to the equality
\tau_{i}=\tau_{j}
.

If
i\sim j
, then, considering
z_{k}
a “second nearest neighbor” to
z_{i}
, i.e. achieving the min in (
2.3
), by the triangle inequality we have

\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{j})\leq\frac{1}{4}|z_{i}-z_{j}|+\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{i}).

Reversing the roles of
i
and
j
, we obtain, for
i\sim j
,

(2.14)

|\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{i})-\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{j})|\leq\frac{1}{4}|z_{i}-z_{j}|\leq\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i}).

Writing
d=|z_{i}-z_{j}|
, (
2.14
) gives

\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})\leq\tau_{i},\qquad\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{i})\leq\tau_{i}+d/4\leq 2\tau_{i},\qquad d\leq 4\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i}).

By (
2.5
),

E_{i}:=\kappa-\mathsf{g}_{1}\left(\frac{z_{i}-z_{j}}{\tau_{i}}\right),\qquad|E_{i}|\leq\frac{d^{2}}{2\tau_{i}^{2}}\leq 32\left(\frac{\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})}{\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{i})}\right)^{2}.

Inserting these facts into (
2.13
), we obtain that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla h_{\lambda}|^{2}\geq 4\pi N\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(0)-2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{2N}(\mathsf{g}(\tau_{i})+\kappa)+2\pi\sum_{i\neq j:j\sim i}d_{i}d_{j}\Bigg(\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(z_{i}-z_{j})-\mathsf{g}(\tau_{i})-\kappa+E_{i}\Bigg)\,.

Since
d_{i}d_{j}=\pm 1
, we have
d_{i}d_{j}E_{i}\geq-C(\frac{\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})}{\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{i})})^{2}
. Regrouping over the components gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla h_{\lambda}|^{2}\geq 4\pi N\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(0)\\
+2\pi\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Bigg(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\Bigl[d_{i}d_{\phi_{1}(i)}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(z_{i}-z_{\phi_{1}(i)})-d_{i}\left(d_{i}+d_{\phi_{1}(i)}\right)(\mathsf{g}(\tau_{i})+\kappa)-C\Bigl(\frac{\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})}{\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{i})}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]\\
-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}\backslash\mathcal{C}_{k}}\bigl(\mathsf{g}(\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{i}))+\kappa\bigr)\Bigg).

In view of the rewriting of
\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}
(
1.10
) we obtain the result.

∎

3.
Free energy upper bound

This section is devoted to the proof of the free energy upper bound.
This will be based on the energy lower bound of Corollary
2.3
which reduces the interaction to nearest neighbor terms, together with a quadratic error depending on second nearest neighbor distances. The main difficulty is to partition the phase-space efficiently to integrate the exponential of this reduced energy. This is based on refinements of the Gunson–Panta change of variables and approach
[
17
]
.
We will start by proving upper bounds for the simpler nearest neighbor model
\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{nn}}
, then for simpler models with errors, and build up to the upper bound for the full model including the second nearest neighbor errors.
Throughout this section,
N\geq 2
and the estimates as
\lambda\to 0
are uniform in
N
.

3.1.
Preliminaries: Dirichlet integrals, Gaussian decoupling and an optimization

We start by presenting some tools needed to implement the integrations.

Dirichlet integrals

We first recall a result on computing “multiple Dirichlet integrals of type 1” (see
[
38
, p. 258]
).

Lemma 3.1

(Dirichlet integrals)
.

Given an integer
k\geq 1
,
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}>0
and
s>0
, let

I_{k,s}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}):=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{k}}\mathbf{1}_{0<t_{1}+\ldots+t_{k}<s}\,t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}\ldots t_{k}^{\alpha_{k}-1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}t_{k}.

We have

(3.1)

I_{k,s}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})=s^{\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{k}}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{1})\ldots\Gamma(\alpha_{k})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{k})}\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{k}}.

For
k_{0}\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, if the
k
-tuple
(\alpha_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
is defined by
\alpha_{i}=\alpha>0
for
1\leq i\leq k_{0}
and
\alpha_{i}=1
for
k_{0}+1\leq i\leq k
, then

(3.2)

\log I_{k,s}=(\alpha k_{0}+(k-k_{0}))\left(\log s-\log(\alpha k_{0}+k-k_{0})\right)+k+O_{\alpha}(k_{0}+\log(k+1)).

Moreover,

I_{k,s}\leq\left(\frac{s}{\alpha k_{0}+k-k_{0}}\right)^{\alpha k_{0}+k-k_{0}}e^{k}C_{\alpha}^{k_{0}}.

Proof.

The identity

\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{k}}\mathbf{1}_{0<t_{1}+\ldots+t_{k}<1}\ t_{1}^{\alpha_{1}-1}\ldots t_{k}^{\alpha_{k}-1}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}t_{k}=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{1})\ldots\Gamma(\alpha_{k})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{k})}\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{k}}

follows by successive applications of Euler’s beta integral.
It follows by the change of variables
t_{i}=su_{i}
that (
3.1
) holds.

For the particular choice of
\alpha_{i}
’s stated in the lemma, we thus find that

I_{k,s}=s^{\alpha k_{0}+k-k_{0}}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)^{k_{0}}}{\Gamma(\alpha k_{0}+k-k_{0})}\frac{1}{\alpha k_{0}+k-k_{0}}.

Applying Stirling’s formula, we obtain

\log I_{k,s}=(\alpha k_{0}+k-k_{0})\log s+k_{0}\log\Gamma(\alpha)\\
-(\alpha k_{0}+k-k_{0})\log(\alpha k_{0}+k-k_{0})+(\alpha k_{0}+k-k_{0})+O_{\alpha}(\log(k+1)).

The relation (
3.2
) follows. We deduce the upper bound from
\Gamma(x+1)\geq(x/e)^{x}
for
x>0
.∎

Gunson–Panta change of variables

The Gunson–Panta method relies on considering the nearest neighbor graph of a configuration and partitioning the phase-space accordingly. We use the notation introduced already in Section
2
, but we can apply it more generally to
p
points, and not necessarily an even number of points.
For
p\geq 2
distinct points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}\in\Lambda
with unique nearest neighbors, let
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{K}
denote the connected components of their nearest-neighbor graph, and denote by
m_{k}
and
m_{k}^{\prime}
the indices of the two vertices of the 2-cycle
\mathcal{C}_{k}
. On each graph, choose
m_{k}^{\prime}=\max\mathcal{C}_{k}
and set

\Omega_{\gamma}:=\{Z_{p}\in\Lambda^{p}:\gamma_{p}(Z_{p})=\gamma\}.

We then define the Gunson–Panta change of variables via the map

(3.3)

\Phi^{GP}_{p}(z_{1},\dots,z_{p})=(u_{1},\dots,u_{p}),

where

u_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}z_{i}-z_{\phi_{1}(i)}&\text{if }i\in\mathcal{I}_{k},i\neq m_{k}^{\prime}\\
z_{i}&\text{if }i=m_{k}^{\prime}.\end{array}\right.

Deleting the outgoing edge of each
m_{k}^{\prime}
leaves a rooted tree in each component. Ordering parents before children makes the map triangular with identity diagonal blocks. Hence
\Phi_{p}^{GP}
is invertible on
\Omega_{\gamma}
and
|\det D\Phi_{p}^{GP}|=1
.

Gaussian decoupling of packing constraints

After the Gunson–Panta change of variables, the integrands are products of radial weights in the variables
u_{j}
, but the domain of integration is not a product. The discs
B(z_{i},|z_{i}-z_{\phi_{1}(i)}|/2)
are disjoint and contained in
[-\sqrt{N},2\sqrt{N}]^{2}
, which has area
9N
. Hence the variables
u_{i}
other than the reference points
u_{m_{k}^{\prime}}=z_{m_{k}^{\prime}}
satisfy the packing constraint

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}\setminus\{m_{k}^{\prime}\}}|u_{i}|^{2}\leq\frac{36N}{\pi}\,.

Lemma
3.1
computes such constrained integrals exactly for power weights. For the other weights, we decouple the constraint as follows.

Lemma 3.2

(Gaussian decoupling)
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer and let
s,a>0
. For all
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have

(3.4)

\mathbf{1}_{\sum_{j=1}^{k}|u_{j}|^{2}\leq s}\leq e^{a(s-\sum_{j=1}^{k}|u_{j}|^{2})}\,.

Consequently, for all measurable functions
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
, we have

\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{k}}\mathbf{1}_{\sum_{j=1}^{k}|u_{j}|^{2}\leq s}\prod_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}(u_{j})\mathrm{d}u_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}u_{k}\leq e^{as}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}w_{j}(u)e^{-a|u|^{2}}\mathrm{d}u\,.

Proof.

The right side of (
3.4
) is positive, and it is at least
1
when
\sum_{j}|u_{j}|^{2}\leq s
. This proves (
3.4
). Integrating it against
\prod_{j}w_{j}(u_{j})
and using Fubini’s theorem gives the second bound.
∎

In the proof of Proposition
3.6
, the parameter
a
is of order
1
, and its value affects only the constants. The weight
e^{2\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)}
behaves differently. It equals
|u|^{-2}
for
|u|\geq 2\lambda
, so its integral over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
diverges logarithmically. After the Gaussian cutoff, its mass is
\pi\log(1/(a\lambda^{2}))+O(1)
, which depends on
a
at leading order. This weight appears for
2
-cycles at
\beta=2
and for tree edges at
\beta=4
. The next lemma collects its integrals.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
0<\lambda\leq e^{-1}
and
\ell=|\log\lambda|
. For
a>0
and
R\geq 0
, let

J_{\lambda}(a;R):=\int_{|u|\geq R}e^{2\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)-a|u|^{2}}\mathrm{d}u\qquad\text{and}\qquad J_{\lambda}(a):=J_{\lambda}(a;0)\,.

There is a universal constant
C>0
such that the following hold, where every
O(1)
term is bounded by
C
in absolute value.

(1)

If
a\lambda^{2}\leq 1/4
, then

(3.5)

J_{\lambda}(a)=\pi\log\frac{1}{a\lambda^{2}}+O(1)\,.

(2)

We have

(3.6)

J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)=2\pi\Bigl(\ell+\frac{1}{2}\log\ell\Bigr)+O(1)\quad\text{and}\quad\log J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)\leq\log(2\pi\ell)+C\frac{1+\log\ell}{\ell}\,.

If
2\lambda\leq R\leq 1
, then

(3.7)

J_{\lambda}(1/\ell;R)=2\pi\Bigl(\log\frac{1}{R}+\frac{1}{2}\log\ell\Bigr)+O(1)\,.

(3)

For every
C_{1}>0
, there is
C_{2}>0
such that, for every
\rho\geq\lambda
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\int_{|u|\leq 4\rho}e^{2\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)}\Bigl(e^{C_{1}((|u|/4)\vee\lambda)^{2}/\rho^{2}}-1\Bigr)\mathrm{d}u\leq C_{2}\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\int_{|u|\leq 4\rho}e^{2\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)}\Bigl(\frac{(|u|/4)\vee\lambda}{\rho}\Bigr)^{4}\mathrm{d}u\leq C_{2}\,.

Proof.

By (
2.6
),
\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)\leq\mathsf{g}(|u|\vee\lambda)+C\leq\ell+C
on
B(0,2\lambda)
, so the core satisfies

(3.9)

0\leq\int_{|u|<2\lambda}e^{2\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)}\mathrm{d}u\leq 4\pi e^{2C}.

By Newton’s theorem,
\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)=-\log|u|
for
|u|\geq 2\lambda
. Let
E_{1}(x):=\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-t}t^{-1}\mathrm{d}t
. For
0<x\leq 1
, the bounds
|e^{-t}-1|\leq t
and
\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-t}t^{-1}\mathrm{d}t\leq e^{-1}
give

(3.10)

\Bigl|E_{1}(x)-\log\frac{1}{x}\Bigr|\leq 1.

(1) In polar coordinates and with
t=ar^{2}
, the part
|u|\geq 2\lambda
of
J_{\lambda}(a)
equals

2\pi\int_{2\lambda}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-ar^{2}}}{r}\mathrm{d}r=\pi E_{1}(4a\lambda^{2}).

Since
4a\lambda^{2}\leq 1
, (
3.10
) and (
3.9
) give (
3.5
).

(2) Since
\lambda^{2}/\ell\leq e^{-2}<1/4
, item (1) applies with
a=1/\ell
and gives
J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)=\pi\log(\ell/\lambda^{2})+O(1)=2\pi\ell+\pi\log\ell+O(1)
. Hence
J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)\leq 2\pi\ell\,(1+(\log\ell+C)/(2\ell))
, and
\log(1+x)\leq x
gives the bound on
\log J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)
. Since
R\geq 2\lambda
, the integrand of
J_{\lambda}(1/\ell;R)
is
|u|^{-2}e^{-|u|^{2}/\ell}
, so
J_{\lambda}(1/\ell;R)=\pi E_{1}(R^{2}/\ell)
. Since
R^{2}/\ell\leq 1
, (
3.10
) gives (
3.7
).

(3) For
|u|\leq 4\rho
, we have
(|u|/4)\vee\lambda\leq\rho
, and
e^{C_{1}x^{2}}-1\leq(e^{C_{1}}-1)x^{2}
for
0\leq x\leq 1
, by convexity. It therefore suffices to bound
\int_{|u|\leq 4\rho}e^{2\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)}((|u|/4)\vee\lambda)^{2}\rho^{-2}\mathrm{d}u
, which also dominates the second integral. On the core, the factor
((|u|/4)\vee\lambda)^{2}\rho^{-2}
is at most
1
, and (
3.9
) applies. For
|u|\geq 2\lambda
, we have
(|u|/4)\vee\lambda\leq|u|/2
, so the annulus contributes at most

\int_{2\lambda\leq|u|\leq 4\rho}\frac{1}{|u|^{2}}\frac{|u|^{2}}{4\rho^{2}}\mathrm{d}u\leq\frac{\pi(4\rho)^{2}}{4\rho^{2}}=4\pi.

∎

An elementary optimization

In the proofs below, the entropy of the graphs and the energy combine into expressions of the form
\delta(A+B\log(1/\delta))
, where
0<\delta\leq 1
is a proportion of vertices or of pairs. For every real number
A
and every
B>0
, we have

(3.11)

\sup_{0<\delta\leq 1}\delta\bigl(A+B\log(1/\delta)\bigr)\leq Be^{A/B-1}\,.

Indeed, the function
\delta\mapsto\delta(A+B\log(1/\delta))
is concave on
(0,\infty)
, and its derivative
A-B+B\log(1/\delta)
vanishes at
\delta=e^{A/B-1}
. Hence its maximum on
(0,\infty)
is its value at this point, which is
Be^{A/B-1}
.

3.2.
Upper bound for a nearest neighbor model

Corollary
2.3
tells us that, up to an error term involving ratios of nearest and second-nearest neighbor distances, one can bound
\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}
from below by the nearest-neighbor energy
\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{nn}}(Z_{2N})
defined after it.
We examine in the next two lemmas the nearest neighbor model studied in
[
17
]
and the reduced dipole model. The proofs will be a model for what follows.

Lemma 3.4

(Upper bounds for the nearest-neighbor model on a fixed graph)
.

Let
\beta>2
and
\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}
be the constant defined in (
1.11
). Let
p\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
K\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
and
\gamma\in D_{2p,K}
. The bounds below hold uniformly in
N\geq 2
for
0<\lambda<\lambda_{\beta}
, where
\lambda_{\beta}\in(0,e^{-2}]
depends only on
\beta
.

\bullet
For
\beta\in(2,4)
, we have

\log\int_{\Omega_{\gamma}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{nn}}(Z_{2p})\right)\mathrm{d}Z_{2p}\\
\leq K\log N+(2-\beta)K\log\lambda+K\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}+\Bigl(1-\frac{\beta}{4}\Bigr)(2p-2K)\log\frac{N}{p-K}+C_{\beta}(p-K).

\bullet
For
\beta=4
, we have

\log\int_{\Omega_{\gamma}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{nn}}(Z_{2p})\right)\mathrm{d}Z_{2p}\leq K\log N\\
+(2-\beta)K\log\lambda+K\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}+(2p-2K)\log\Bigl(|\log\lambda|+\log\frac{N}{p-K}\Bigr)+C_{\beta}(p-K).

\bullet
For
\beta>4
, we have

\log\int_{\Omega_{\gamma}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{nn}}(Z_{2p})\right)\mathrm{d}Z_{2p}\leq K\log N\\
+(2-\beta)K\log\lambda+K\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}+(2p-2K)\Bigl(2-\frac{\beta}{2}\Bigr)\log\lambda+C_{\beta}(p-K).

Terms proportional to
p-K
are zero when
p=K
.

Proof.

Step 1: change of variables.

Let
\gamma\in D_{2p,K}
. As in Section 2, let us denote by
\mathcal{I}_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
, the connected components of
\gamma
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}:=\{m_{k},m_{k}^{\prime}\}\subset\mathcal{I}_{k}
the
2
-cycle in each connected component.

Performing the Gunson–Panta change of variables (
3.3
) in the integral of the statement, we may write

(3.12)

\int_{\Omega_{\gamma}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{nn}}(Z_{2p})\right)\mathrm{d}Z_{2p}=\int_{\Phi^{GP}_{2p}(\Omega_{\gamma})}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\Bigg[\exp(\beta\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{m_{k}}))\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}\backslash\mathcal{C}_{k}\end{subarray}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{2}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{i})\Bigr)\Bigg]\mathrm{d}U_{2p}.

Indeed, if
\{m_{k},m_{k}^{\prime}\}
is a
2
-cycle, the interaction
\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{m_{k}})
is counted twice, which explains why this term appears in the above integral with a factor
\beta
instead of
\beta/2
. This turns out to be crucial:
since
\beta>2
, the integral of the
2
-cycle factor
\exp(\beta\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{m_{k}}))
diverges as
\lambda\to 0
. On the other hand, for a tree edge, that is, for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{k}
, the weight
\exp(\frac{\beta}{2}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{i}))
is locally integrable uniformly in
\lambda
if
\beta<4
, while its integral near the origin diverges as
\lambda\to 0
if
\beta\geq 4
.

Step 2: separation of variables.

The domain of integration in (
3.12
) is a complicated subset of
\mathbb{R}^{4p}
but one can approximate it by only keeping the volume constraint:

\Phi^{GP}_{2p}(\Omega_{\gamma})\subset B:=\Bigl\{U_{2p}\in\mathbb{R}^{4p}:\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k},i\neq m_{k}^{\prime}}|u_{i}|^{2}\leq\frac{36N}{\pi},\forall k\in\{1,\ldots,K\},u_{m_{k}^{\prime}}\in\Lambda\Bigr\}.

This takes advantage of the fact that the balls
B(z_{i},\frac{|z_{i}-z_{\phi_{1}(i)}|}{2})
are disjoint balls in
[-\sqrt{N},2\sqrt{N}]^{2}
.

Splitting the variables into two categories (those corresponding to
i=m_{k}^{\prime}
, i.e. the reference points, and the others) and integrating a reference point in each connected component in the box
\Lambda
of volume
N
, we find the bound below. In its last integral, we relabel the tree-edge variables
u_{i}
with
i\in\bigcup_{k}(\mathcal{I}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{k})
as
u_{1},\ldots,u_{2p-2K}
.

(3.13)

\int_{B}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\Bigg[\exp(\beta\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{m_{k}}))\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}\backslash\mathcal{C}_{k}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{2}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{i})\Bigr)\Bigg]\mathrm{d}U_{2p}\\
\leq\Bigl(\int_{B_{1}}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\beta\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{m_{k}}))\mathrm{d}u_{m_{k}}\Bigr)N^{K}\times\int_{B_{2}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{2}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{1})\Bigr)\ldots\exp\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{2}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{2p-2K})\Bigr)\mathrm{d}U_{2p-2K},

where

B_{1}:=\Bigl\{U_{K}\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{K}:|U_{K}|^{2}\leq\frac{36N}{\pi}\Bigr\},

B_{2}:=\Bigl\{U_{2p-2K}\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{2p-2K}:|U_{2p-2K}|^{2}\leq\frac{36N}{\pi}\Bigr\}.

Step 3: integration of the
2
-cycle distances.

Performing a polar change of coordinates and using (
2.6
), we get

(3.14)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\exp(\beta\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u))\mathrm{d}u=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(\beta\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(r))r\mathrm{d}r=2\pi\lambda^{2-\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(\beta\mathsf{g}_{1}(r))r\mathrm{d}r=\lambda^{2-\beta}\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}>0

by definition of
\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}
, see (
1.11
). Hence, removing the restriction to the set
B_{1}
, we obtain

\log\int_{B_{1}}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\beta\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{m_{k}}))\mathrm{d}u_{m_{k}}\leq K\log(\lambda^{2-\beta}\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}).

Step 4: Integration of the other distances and conclusion.

For
p=K
, the
B_{2}
integral is
1
. We assume
p>K
in this step.

\bullet
The case
\beta\in(2,4)
. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,2p-2K\}
, the unregularized weight
\exp(\frac{\beta}{2}\mathsf{g}(u_{i}))
is locally integrable, and therefore the cutoff
\lambda
can be removed. Using that
\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}\leq\mathsf{g}+C
from (
2.6
) we find

\int_{B_{2}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{2}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{1})\Bigr)\cdots\exp\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{2}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{2p-2K})\Bigr)\mathrm{d}U_{2p-2K}\\
\leq C_{\beta}^{2p-2K}\int_{B_{2}}\frac{1}{|u_{1}|^{\frac{\beta}{2}}}\cdots\frac{1}{|u_{2p-2K}|^{\frac{\beta}{2}}}\mathrm{d}U_{2p-2K}.

By performing again a polar change of coordinates and the change of variables
r_{i}^{\prime}=r_{i}^{2}
, we can rewrite this as an integral over a simplex of
\mathbb{R}^{2p-2K}
:

(3.15)

\int_{B_{2}}\frac{1}{|u_{1}|^{\frac{\beta}{2}}}\cdots\frac{1}{|u_{2p-2K}|^{\frac{\beta}{2}}}\mathrm{d}U_{2p-2K}=\pi^{2p-2K}\int_{B_{2}^{\prime}}{r_{1}^{-\frac{\beta}{4}}}\ldots{r_{2p-2K}^{-\frac{\beta}{4}}}\mathrm{d}R_{2p-2K},

where

B_{2}^{\prime}:=\left\{R_{2p-2K}\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2p-2K}:r_{1}+\ldots+r_{2p-2K}\leq\frac{36N}{\pi}\right\}.

Since
\beta\in(2,4)
, we can set
\alpha=1-\frac{\beta}{4}>0
and
k=k_{0}=2p-2K
and insert (
3.2
) with
s=\frac{36N}{\pi}
into (
3.15
) to obtain

(3.16)

\log\int_{B_{2}}\frac{1}{|u_{1}|^{\frac{\beta}{2}}}\cdots\frac{1}{|u_{2p-2K}|^{\frac{\beta}{2}}}\mathrm{d}U_{2p-2K}\\
\leq\Bigl(1-\frac{\beta}{4}\Bigr)(2p-2K)\log N-\Bigl(1-\frac{\beta}{4}\Bigr)(2p-2K)\log\Bigl(\Bigl(1-\frac{\beta}{4}\Bigr)(2p-2K)\Bigr)+C_{\beta}(p-K)\\
=\Bigl(1-\frac{\beta}{4}\Bigr)(2p-2K)\log\frac{N}{p-K}+C_{\beta}(p-K).

\bullet
The case
\beta=4
. Here the tree weight is
e^{2\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}}
. We apply Lemma
3.2
to the
2p-2K
tree variables, with
s=36N/\pi
and
a=(2p-2K)/(sL)
, where
L=\ell+\log(N/(p-K))
. Since
2p-2K\leq 2N
and
L\geq\ell\geq 1
, we have
a\leq\pi/18
and
as\leq 2p-2K
. Hence
a\lambda^{2}\leq 1/4
, and the decoupling costs at most
e^{2p-2K}
. Since
\log(1/(a\lambda^{2}))=2\ell+\log(sL/(2p-2K))\leq 2L+\log L+C
, the estimate (
3.5
) gives
\log J_{\lambda}(a)\leq\log L+C
. Hence

\log\int_{B_{2}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{2}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{1})\Bigr)\cdots\exp\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{2}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{2p-2K})\Bigr)\mathrm{d}U_{2p-2K}\leq(2p-2K)\log\Bigl(\ell+\log\frac{N}{p-K}\Bigr)\\
+C_{\beta}(p-K).

\bullet
The case
\beta>4
. Arguing as in (
3.14
), one can check that

\log\int_{B_{2}}\exp\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{2}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{1})\Bigr)\cdots\exp\Bigl(\frac{\beta}{2}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{2p-2K})\Bigr)\mathrm{d}U_{2p-2K}\leq(2p-2K)\Bigl(2-\frac{\beta}{2}\Bigr)\log\lambda+C_{\beta}(p-K).

Inserting the estimates of Steps 3 and 4 into (
3.13
) proves the result.

∎

We now expand the partition function of the reduced dipole model by summing over the neutral
2
-cycles.

Lemma 3.5

(Free energy of the reduced dipole model)
.

Let
\beta\geq 2
. There exists
\lambda_{\beta}\in(0,e^{-2}]
, depending only on
\beta
, with the following property. Let
N\geq 2
and
0<\lambda<\lambda_{\beta}
, let

\tilde{\mathsf{F}}_{\lambda}(Z_{2N}):=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(z_{i}-z_{\phi_{1}(i)})\mathbf{1}_{\phi_{1}\circ\phi_{1}(i)=i,d_{i}d_{\phi_{1}(i)}=-1}

be the energy of the neutral
2
-cycles, and let

\mathsf{K}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}:=\int_{\Lambda^{2N}}\exp(-\beta\tilde{\mathsf{F}}_{\lambda}(Z_{2N}))\mathrm{d}Z_{2N}.

Let

(3.17)

\bar{\omega}_{\lambda}:=\begin{cases}|\log\lambda|^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}&\text{if $\beta=2$},\\
\lambda^{\min\{(\beta-2)/2,1\}}&\text{if $\beta>2$}.\end{cases}

Then
0<\mathsf{K}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}<\infty
and

\log\mathsf{K}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}=2N\log N+\begin{cases}N\bigl((2-\beta)\log\lambda+\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}-1\bigr)+O_{\beta}(N\bar{\omega}_{\lambda}+\log(N+1))&\text{if $\beta>2$},\\
N\bigl(\log(2\pi|\log\lambda|)-1\bigr)+O(N\bar{\omega}_{\lambda}+\log(N+1))&\text{if $\beta=2$}.\end{cases}

Note that the expansion of
\log\mathsf{K}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}
is the same as (
1.13
) up to the error terms.

Proof.

We obtain the upper bound by summing over the neutral
2
-cycles, and the lower bound by restricting to configurations of
N
short neutral pairs. First assume
\beta=2
.
For a configuration, let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of pairs
\{x_{i},y_{j}\}
which are mutual nearest neighbors. Since nearest neighbors are unique, these pairs are disjoint, so
\mathcal{M}
is a partial matching between positive and negative particles. There are

\binom{N}{p}^{2}p!=\frac{(N!)^{2}}{p!\,(N-p)!^{2}}

such matchings with
|\mathcal{M}|=p
.

We partition the configurations according to this matching, and we write
u_{j}
for the relative position of the two charges of the
j
-th pair of
\mathcal{M}
. These two charges are mutual nearest neighbors, so the discs of radius
|u_{j}|/2
centered at the
2p
matched charges are disjoint. Since
|u_{j}|\leq\sqrt{2N}
, these discs lie in
[-\sqrt{N},2\sqrt{N}]^{2}
, which gives the packing constraint
\sum_{j=1}^{p}|u_{j}|^{2}\leq CN
. By Lemma
3.2
with
a=1/\ell
, we may therefore insert
e^{-\sum_{j}|u_{j}|^{2}/\ell}
at cost
e^{CN/\ell}
. We then integrate one charge of each pair and the
2N-2p
remaining particles over
\Lambda
, which gives
N^{2N-p}
, and we extend each integral over
u_{j}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Summing over the matchings yields

\mathsf{K}_{N,2}^{\lambda}\leq e^{CN/\ell}\sum_{p=0}^{N}\frac{(N!)^{2}}{p!(N-p)!^{2}}N^{2N-p}{J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)}^{p}\leq N!N^{N}{J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)}^{N}e^{CN/\ell+2N/\sqrt{J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)}}.

Indeed, setting
d=N-p
and using
N!/(N-d)!\leq N^{d}
, the sum divided by
N!N^{N}{J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)}^{N}
is at most

\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{(N/\sqrt{J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)})^{2d}}{(d!)^{2}}\leq\left(\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\frac{(N/\sqrt{J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)})^{d}}{d!}\right)^{2}=e^{2N/\sqrt{J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)}}.

By (
3.6
), we have
J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)\geq\pi\ell
for
\lambda
small, so the last factor is at most
e^{CN\ell^{-1/2}}
. The bound on
\log J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)
in (
3.6
) completes the upper bound.

For the lower bound, let
t=\ell^{-1/2}
. Restrict positive particles to boundary distance at least
t
and mutual distances at least
4t
, and dipole lengths to
2\lambda\leq|u|\leq t^{2}
. At each placement of a positive particle, the boundary strip excludes area at most
C\sqrt{N}\,t
, and the previous particles exclude at most
CNt^{2}
. For
\lambda
small enough, successive placement therefore gives volume at least

N^{N}e^{-C(\sqrt{N}\,t+Nt^{2})}\geq N^{N}e^{-CNt}.

Particles in different pairs are farther apart than the partners within each pair. Thus all partners are mutual nearest neighbors, and the
N!
assignments of negative labels give disjoint configuration sets. Each pair contributes
2\pi(\ell-\log\ell-\log 2)
. Since
\log\ell/\ell=O(\ell^{-1/2})
and
\mathcal{Z}_{2}=2\pi
, the upper and lower bounds and Stirling’s formula prove the expansion at
\beta=2
, with error
O(N\ell^{-1/2}+\log(N+1))
.

We now assume
\beta>2
. Recall from (
3.14
) that
\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}\lambda^{2-\beta}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\beta\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)}\mathrm{d}u
. The same matching bound, without the Gaussian factor, gives

\mathsf{K}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}\leq\sum_{p=0}^{N}\frac{(N!)^{2}}{p!(N-p)!^{2}}N^{2N-p}(\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}\lambda^{2-\beta})^{p}\leq N!N^{N}(\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}\lambda^{2-\beta})^{N}e^{2N/\sqrt{\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}\lambda^{2-\beta}}}.

For the lower bound, take
t=\lambda^{(\beta-2)/\beta}
. Impose the same restrictions on the positive particles, and allow dipole lengths
|u|\leq t
. The preceding placement estimate gives volume at least

N^{N}e^{-C(Nt^{2}+\sqrt{N}\,t)}\geq N^{N}e^{-CNt^{2}-C}.

The last inequality uses
\sqrt{N}\,t\leq Nt^{2}+1/4
. Points in different pairs are at distance at least
2t
, so the partners are again mutual nearest neighbors and the label assignments are disjoint. For
\lambda
small enough,
t\geq 2\lambda
, and the omitted radial tail is
2\pi t^{2-\beta}/(\beta-2)
. Hence each pair contributes

\int_{|u|\leq t}e^{\beta\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)}\mathrm{d}u\geq\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}\lambda^{2-\beta}\left(1-C_{\beta}(\lambda/t)^{\beta-2}\right).

Multiplying these bounds over the pairs and summing over the label assignments gives

\mathsf{K}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}\geq N!N^{N}(\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}\lambda^{2-\beta})^{N}\exp\left[-C_{\beta}N\bigl(t^{2}+(\lambda/t)^{\beta-2}\bigr)-C\right].

Here
t^{2}=(\lambda/t)^{\beta-2}=\lambda^{2(\beta-2)/\beta}
. Since

\frac{2(\beta-2)}{\beta}\geq\min\{(\beta-2)/2,1\},\qquad(\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}\lambda^{2-\beta})^{-1/2}=O_{\beta}(\lambda^{(\beta-2)/2}),

the errors in both bounds are
O_{\beta}(N\bar{\omega}_{\lambda}+1)
. Stirling’s formula concludes the proof.
∎

3.3.
Upper bound on the free energy of a nearest-neighbor model with marked points

We now study a new nearest-neighbor model which will be useful for evaluating the contributions of the dipole-dipole interaction or quadrupole errors that appear in (
2.8
). We consider an integral over
p
variables
z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}
living on
\Lambda^{p}
, where the nearest neighbor interaction energy is counted only for a small subset of
k
points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
.
The estimates track the combinatorial cost of placing both endpoints of a
2
-cycle in the marked subset. This cost is useful when
k/p
is small.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\beta>0
,
0<\lambda\leq e^{-1}
and
0\leq k\leq p\leq N
be such that
k,p
are integers and
p\geq\max(2,N/2)
. Let us consider the energy over
\Lambda^{p}
defined by

(3.18)

F_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{p}):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i}),

where
\mathsf{r}_{1}
is defined as in (
2.2
).
Let
\delta=0
when
\beta\neq 4
and
\delta\in\{0,1\}
when
\beta=4
. Then, we have

\log\int_{\Lambda^{p}}\exp(-\beta F_{k}(Z_{p}))\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(1+\log\frac{\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})}{\lambda}\Bigr)^{\delta}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{p}\leq p\log N+C_{\beta}k+\frac{k}{2}\nu_{\beta,k}\,,

where

(3.19)

\nu_{\beta,k}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\ \beta\in(0,2)\\
\log\Bigl(|\log\lambda|\frac{k}{N}\vee 1\Bigr)&\text{if }\ \beta=2\\
\log\Bigl(\lambda^{2-\beta}\frac{k}{N}\vee 1\Bigr)&\text{if }\ \beta\in(2,4)\\
\log\Bigl(\lambda^{-2}\frac{k}{N}\vee|\log\lambda|^{2(1+\delta)}\Bigr)&\text{if $\beta=4$ }\\
\log\Bigl(\lambda^{2-\beta}\frac{k}{N}\vee\lambda^{4-\beta}\Bigr)&\text{if }\ \beta>4.\end{cases}

Every occurrence of
\nu_{4,k}
uses the current value of
\delta
.

Proof.

For
k=0
, the integral equals
N^{p}
. We assume
k\geq 1
below.
For brevity, we let
r(z_{i})=\frac{1}{4}\min_{j\neq i}|z_{i}-z_{j}|
and note that
\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})=r(z_{i})\vee\lambda
by (
2.2
).

Step 1: counting graphs.

We define the directed graph
\gamma_{p}^{\prime}:=(V,E)
where the set of vertices
V
is given by
\{1,\ldots,p\}
and there is an edge
\vec{ij}\in E
when
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
\phi_{1}(i)=j
. The graph
\gamma_{p}^{\prime}
is a subgraph of the nearest-neighbor graph
\gamma_{p}
. We claim that the nontrivial connected components (i.e., not reduced to a point) of
\gamma_{p}^{\prime}
are as follows: either they contain a
2
-cycle within
\{1,\ldots,k\}
and no point in
V\setminus\{1,\ldots,k\}
,
or they do not and they contain a unique point in
V\setminus\{1,\ldots,k\}
. In the latter case the connected component is a tree and its root is in
V\setminus\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

Let us prove the above claim. We can observe that
V\setminus\{1,\ldots,k\}
is exactly the set of vertices of
\gamma_{p}^{\prime}
with no outgoing edge. Let
U
be a nontrivial connected component of
\gamma_{p}^{\prime}
. Since
U
is contained in a connected component of
\gamma_{p}
, in view of the structure of
\gamma_{p}
, either it contains a
2
-cycle with trees attached to each vertex of the
2
-cycle or it is a tree. In the first case, there is no vertex with
0
out-degree and therefore no vertex in
V\setminus\{1,\ldots,k\}
. In the second case, the root of
U
is the only vertex of
U
with
0
out-degree and it is therefore the only vertex in
V\setminus\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

Figure 2.
Nearest-neighbor graph and restricted nearest-neighbor graph

Figure
2
shows the points from
\{1,\ldots,k\}
in black and the points from
V\setminus\{1,\ldots,k\}
in blue. The solid lines represent the connected components of
\gamma_{p}^{\prime}
, and the dashed lines represent the edges in
\gamma_{p}
that are not part of
\gamma_{p}^{\prime}
.

We denote by
D(k_{0},k_{1})
the set of such graphs with
k_{0}

2
-cycles and
k_{1}
points in
V\setminus\{1,\ldots,k\}
that do not form a trivial connected component. The number of nontrivial connected components is
k_{0}+k_{1}
. Each cycle uses two marked vertices and each rooted tree uses at least one, so
2k_{0}+k_{1}\leq k
. The measurable cells
\Lambda^{p}\cap\{\gamma_{p}^{\prime}=\gamma\}
partition
\Lambda^{p}
outside the excluded null set.

Let us enumerate the graphs
\gamma
in
D(k_{0},k_{1})
. Let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{k_{0}+k_{1}}
be the nontrivial connected components of such a
\gamma
.

\bullet
Since connected components are unordered, we need to divide by
(k_{0}+k_{1})!
.

\bullet
Among these connected components we choose
k_{0}
of them to be those that have a
2
-cycle. There are
\binom{k_{0}+k_{1}}{k_{0}}
choices.

\bullet
For these
k_{0}
connected components, we choose the

2
-cycles. There are

\frac{k!}{(k-2k_{0})!2^{k_{0}}}

choices, corresponding to ordered collections of
k_{0}
disjoint unordered pairs chosen from
k
points.

\bullet
On the
k_{1}
trees, we need to fix the label of the root of the tree, which belongs to
\{k+1,\ldots,p\}
. There are at most
p^{k_{1}}
choices.

\bullet
To each of the remaining
k-2k_{0}
points not in a
2
-cycle we need to assign a nearest-neighbor. Since there are
k_{1}
points of
V\setminus\{1,\ldots,k\}
in the graph, there are at most
k+k_{1}
choices. Therefore, the total number of choices for these points is bounded by
(k+k_{1})^{k-2k_{0}}\leq k^{k-2k_{0}}C^{k}
.

Combining these and using Stirling’s formula, we obtain the upper bound

\log|D(k_{0},k_{1})|\leq\log\frac{k!p^{k_{1}}k^{k-2k_{0}}C^{k}(k_{0}+k_{1})!}{(k-2k_{0})!2^{k_{0}}(k_{0}+k_{1})!k_{0}!k_{1}!}\\
\leq Ck-k_{0}\log k_{0}-k_{1}\log k_{1}+k\log k-(k-2k_{0})\log(k-2k_{0})+(k-2k_{0})\log k+k_{1}\log p.

Step 2: integration.

Fix
\gamma\in D(k_{0},k_{1})
and let
m=k+k_{1}
be the number of vertices in its nontrivial connected components. We write

I(\gamma):=\int_{\Lambda^{p}\cap\{\gamma_{p}^{\prime}=\gamma\}}e^{-\beta F_{k}(Z_{p})}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1+\log\frac{\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})}{\lambda}\right)^{\delta}\mathrm{d}Z_{p}.

We first integrate the
p-m
vertices in trivial components. Conditional on the other coordinates, the distances
r(z_{i})=|z_{i}-z_{\phi_{1}(i)}|/4
,
i\leq k
, are fixed, and each remaining vertex avoids all the balls
B(z_{i},4r(z_{i}))
. The balls
B(z_{i},2r(z_{i}))
,
i\leq k
, are disjoint. Moreover, since
r(z_{i})\leq\sqrt{2N}/4
, each intersection with
\Lambda
contains a quarter of a disc of radius
\sqrt{2}r(z_{i})
. The available volume is therefore at most
N-c\sum_{i=1}^{k}r(z_{i})^{2}
.

If
k\leq p/4
, then
p-m\geq p-2k\geq N/4
, so this integration contributes at most

N^{p-m}\exp\left(-c\sum_{i=1}^{k}r(z_{i})^{2}\right).

If
k>p/4
, packing gives
\sum_{i=1}^{k}r(z_{i})^{2}\leq CN
. By Lemma
3.2
, we insert the same exponential at cost
e^{CN}\leq e^{Ck}
, and integrate the trivial components in volume
N^{p-m}
.

In each nontrivial component, we retain the root or the larger-indexed vertex of its
2
-cycle, and replace every other coordinate by its position relative to its parent. As for (
3.3
), this is an invertible map with determinant
1
. This leaves
k_{0}+k_{1}
reference points,
k_{0}
cycle variables and
k-2k_{0}
tree-edge variables. We integrate the reference points in
\Lambda
and extend each integral over a variable
u_{j}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Polar coordinates, followed by
r=|u|/4
, give the annular integrals

\int_{\lambda}^{1}r^{1-\beta}\left(1+\log\frac{r}{\lambda}\right)^{2\delta}\mathrm{d}r,\qquad\int_{\lambda}^{1}r^{1-\beta/2}\left(1+\log\frac{r}{\lambda}\right)^{\delta}\mathrm{d}r.

The cores contribute
O_{\beta}(\lambda^{2-\beta})
and
O_{\beta}(\lambda^{2-\beta/2})
. The Gaussian tails contribute
O_{\beta}(\ell^{2\delta})
and
O_{\beta}(\ell^{\delta})
. Evaluating the annular integrals gives

I(\gamma)\leq C_{\beta}^{k}N^{p-k+k_{0}}\exp\left(k\psi_{\beta}(k_{0}/k)\right),

where

\psi_{\beta}(x):=\begin{cases}0,&0<\beta<2,\\
x\log\ell,&\beta=2,\\
(2-\beta)x\log\lambda,&2<\beta<4,\\
-2x\log\lambda+(1+\delta)(1-2x)\log\ell,&\beta=4,\\
(2-\beta)x\log\lambda+(2-\beta/2)(1-2x)\log\lambda,&\beta>4.\end{cases}

In particular, at
\beta=4
each tree edge contributes
C\ell^{1+\delta}
, whereas each cycle contributes
C\lambda^{-2}
.

Step 3: optimization.

The count in Step 1 gives

|D(k_{0},k_{1})|\leq\frac{C^{k}k^{k}p^{k_{1}}}{k_{0}!k_{1}!}.

Using
n!\geq(n/e)^{n}
,
p\leq N
and
0\log 0=0
, we find

\displaystyle\log|D(k_{0},k_{1})|+\log I(\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq p\log N+C_{\beta}k-(k-k_{0}-k_{1})\log(N/k)+k\psi_{\beta}(k_{0}/k)

\displaystyle\leq p\log N+C_{\beta}k+k_{0}\log(k/N)+k\psi_{\beta}(k_{0}/k),

where the last inequality uses
2k_{0}+k_{1}\leq k
. Summing over
k_{0},k_{1}
costs at most
2\log(k+1)\leq Ck
. Hence the logarithm of the integral in the statement is bounded by

p\log N+C_{\beta}k+k\max_{0\leq x\leq 1/2}\left\{x\log(k/N)+\psi_{\beta}(x)\right\}.

The function being maximized is affine. Its maximum is attained at
0
or
1/2
and equals
\nu_{\beta,k}/2
, which proves the result.
∎

We now prove a variant of Proposition
3.6
allowing two species of points appearing with distinct exponents in the integral.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\beta>4
. Let
0<\lambda\leq e^{-1}
and let
k,k^{\prime},p
be nonnegative integers with
k+k^{\prime}\leq p\leq N
and
p\geq\max(2,N/2)
. Consider

F_{k,k^{\prime}}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=k+1}^{k+k^{\prime}}\log\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})

and recall the definition of
F_{k}
from (
3.18
). Then, for
\delta\in\{0,1\}
, we have

(3.20)

\log\int_{\Lambda^{p}}\exp(-4F_{k}(Z_{p})-\beta F_{k,k^{\prime}}(Z_{p}))\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1+\log\frac{\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})}{\lambda}\right)^{\delta}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{p}\\
\leq p\log N+C_{\beta}(k+k^{\prime})+\frac{k}{2}\nu_{4,k}+\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}\nu_{\beta,k^{\prime}},

where
\nu_{\beta,k}
is as in (
3.19
), with the current
\delta
in
\nu_{4,k}
.

Proof.

If one marked group is empty, the result follows from Proposition
3.6
. We assume
k,k^{\prime}>0
and set
t=k+k^{\prime}
. Let
I
denote the integral in the statement.

Step 1: counting graphs.

We use
\gamma_{p}^{\prime}
with marked vertices
\{1,\ldots,t\}
. Let
k_{0,1},k_{0,2},k_{0,3}
count cycles in the first group, in the second group, and between the groups, respectively. Set
k_{0}=k_{0,1}+k_{0,2}+k_{0,3}
, and let
k_{1}
count the nontrivial tree components. Then

2k_{0,1}+k_{0,3}\leq k,\qquad 2k_{0,2}+k_{0,3}\leq k^{\prime},\qquad 2k_{0}+k_{1}\leq t.

The first two inequalities count the vertices in each group. The third counts two vertices per cycle and at least one marked vertex per rooted tree.
Disregarding the two species, Step 1 of Proposition
3.6
bounds the number of graphs with these counts by

\frac{C^{t}t^{t}p^{k_{1}}}{k_{0}!k_{1}!}.

Step 2: integration.

We repeat the Gaussian integration from Step 2 of Proposition
3.6
, with
t
marked vertices. A cycle within the first group contributes
C\lambda^{-2}
, and one within the second contributes
C_{\beta}\lambda^{2-\beta}
. For a mixed cycle, dropping the Gaussian and scaling
r=\lambda s
bounds the radial integral by

C\lambda^{-\beta/2}\left(1+\int_{1}^{\infty}s^{-1-\beta/2}(1+\log s)^{\delta}\mathrm{d}s\right)\leq C_{\beta}\lambda^{-\beta/2}.

Each tree edge from the first group contributes
C\ell^{1+\delta}
, and one from the second contributes
C_{\beta}\lambda^{2-\beta/2}
. Thus, for a fixed graph
\gamma
, its integral is at most

C_{\beta}^{t}N^{p-t+k_{0}}\lambda^{-2k_{0,1}+(2-\beta)k_{0,2}-(\beta/2)k_{0,3}}\ell^{(1+\delta)(k-2k_{0,1}-k_{0,3})}\lambda^{(2-\beta/2)(k^{\prime}-2k_{0,2}-k_{0,3})}\\
=C_{\beta}^{t}N^{p-t+k_{0}}\lambda^{-2k_{0}+(2-\beta/2)k^{\prime}}\ell^{(1+\delta)(k-2k_{0,1}-k_{0,3})}.

Step 3: optimization.

Combining the preceding estimates as in Step 3 of Proposition
3.6
, and summing over the counts, gives

\log I\leq p\log N+C_{\beta}t+k(1+\delta)\log\ell+(2-\beta/2)k^{\prime}\log\lambda\\
+\max\left\{k_{0,1}A+k_{0,2}B+\frac{k_{0,3}}{2}(A+B)\right\},

+\left(2-\frac{\beta}{2}\right)\log\lambda\mathbf{1}_{\beta>4}\Bigg)+C_{\beta}(N-K).

Set
\delta=(N-K)/N
and
\alpha_{0}=M_{0}+2
. By (
3.32
),

-\frac{p}{N}\log\frac{p}{N}-\frac{K-p}{N}\log\frac{K-p}{N}\leq\alpha_{0}\delta\log(1/\delta)+C\delta.

Combining (
3.33
), (
3.35
), (
3.36
) and (
3.37
), we obtain, for
p\geq N/2
,

(3.38)

\log|D_{2N,K,p,\widehat{p}_{0}}|+Q(K,p,\widehat{p}_{0})\leq 2N\log N+N\bigl((2-\beta)\log\lambda+\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}-1\bigr)\\
+N\chi_{\beta}(\delta)-c_{\beta}\frac{(p-\widehat{p}_{0})^{2}}{N}+C_{\beta}N\omega_{\lambda}+C_{\beta}\log(N+1),

where
\chi_{\beta}(0)=0
and, for
0<\delta\leq 1
,

(3.39)

\chi_{\beta}(\delta):=\begin{cases}\delta\bigl[-(\beta-2)\ell+(\alpha_{0}+4-\beta/2)\log(1/\delta)+C_{\beta}\bigr],&2<\beta<4,\\
\delta\bigl[-2\ell+2\log\ell+(\alpha_{0}+4)\log(1/\delta)+C_{\beta}\bigr],&\beta=4,\\
\delta\bigl[-2\ell+(\alpha_{0}+2)\log(1/\delta)+C_{\beta}\bigr],&\beta>4.\end{cases}

For
\beta=4
, we used
\ell+\log(1/\delta)\leq\ell/\delta
, which follows from
\ell\geq 1
. For
p<N/2
, we use (
3.23
) if
2\leq m<N/2
and (
3.21
) if
m\geq N/2
. For
m\leq 1
, direct integration gives the same crude bound. This gives (
3.38
) with
C_{\beta}N
in place of
C_{\beta}N\omega_{\lambda}
, after absorbing the quadratic term into this error.

Step 3: optimization.
Applying (
3.11
) to (
3.39
), we obtain

\sup_{0\leq\delta\leq 1}\chi_{\beta}(\delta)\leq C_{\beta}\begin{cases}\lambda^{\frac{2(\beta-2)}{12-\beta+2M_{0}}},&2<\beta<4,\\
(\lambda\ell)^{\frac{2}{M_{0}+6}},&\beta=4,\\
\lambda^{\frac{2}{M_{0}+4}},&\beta>4.\end{cases}

By (
1.12
), the right side is
C_{\beta}\omega_{\lambda}
. Applying the same estimate to
\chi_{\beta}(\delta)+\delta^{2}
, using
\delta^{2}\leq\delta
, gives
\chi_{\beta}(\delta)\leq-\delta^{2}+C_{\beta}\omega_{\lambda}
. Since

1-\frac{\widehat{p}_{0}}{N}\leq(1+\alpha_{0})\delta+\frac{p-\widehat{p}_{0}}{N},

we deduce from (
3.38
) that, for
p\geq N/2
,

(3.40)

\log|D_{2N,K,p,\widehat{p}_{0}}|+Q(K,p,\widehat{p}_{0})\leq 2N\log N+N\bigl((2-\beta)\log\lambda+\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}-1\bigr)\\
-c_{\beta}\frac{(N-\widehat{p}_{0})^{2}}{N}+C_{\beta}N\omega_{\lambda}+C_{\beta}\log(N+1).

For
p<N/2
, we have
\delta\geq[2(1+\alpha_{0})]^{-1}
. In this range
\chi_{\beta}(\delta)\to-\infty
uniformly as
\lambda\to 0
, so (
3.40
) also follows from the crude estimate above.

Summing over
K,p,\widehat{p}_{0}
costs at most
3\log(N+1)
, proving (
3.29
). On
\mathcal{A}(p_{0})
, the marked count satisfies

\widehat{p}_{0}\leq p_{0}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad-c_{\beta}\frac{(N-\widehat{p}_{0})^{2}}{N}\leq-c_{\beta}\frac{(N-p_{0})^{2}}{N}.

Summing (
3.40
) over the classes compatible with
\mathcal{A}(p_{0})
proves (
3.30
). Dropping the nonnegative quadratic correction from the exponent in (
3.34
) gives

(3.41)

\log\int_{\Lambda^{2N}}e^{-\beta G(Z_{2N})}\mathrm{d}Z_{2N}\leq 2N\log N+N\bigl((2-\beta)\log\lambda+\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}-1\bigr)\\
+O_{\beta}(N\omega_{\lambda})+C_{\beta}\log(N+1),

where

G:=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(z_{i}-z_{\phi_{1}(i)})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I^{\mathrm{pair}}\backslash I^{\mathrm{dip}}\\
\phi_{2}(i)\in I^{\mathrm{pair}},\phi_{2}(\phi_{1}(i))\in I^{\mathrm{pair}}\end{subarray}}\left(\log\frac{\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{i})\wedge\mathsf{r}_{2}(z_{\phi_{1}(i)})}{\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})}-C\right).

Step 4: control on the size of dipoles.

We use the separation (
3.35
). In the proof of Proposition
3.8
, the quadratic correction in each radial integral is bounded. Requiring
|u_{j}|>R
for a pair
j\notin S
therefore bounds its radial factor by

C_{\beta}\int_{|u|\geq R}e^{\beta\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)}\mathrm{d}u=C_{\beta}\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}\lambda^{2-\beta}\mu_{\beta}([R/\lambda,+\infty)).

The bound (
3.24
) without a length restriction is at least
\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}\lambda^{2-\beta}
. Thus each prescribed long pair
j\notin S
contributes a factor
C_{\beta}\mu_{\beta}([R/\lambda,+\infty))
to
\mathcal{R}_{A,S}
in (
3.27
). The bounds (
3.28
) therefore apply uniformly to every prescribed set of long pairs. This also covers the case
p<N/2
, because the proof of Proposition
3.8
establishes (
3.21
) and (
3.23
) for (
3.28
). For
m\leq 1
, direct pair integration gives the same tail factor.

For
m\geq 2
, every isolated neutral pair lies in
I\setminus I^{\prime}
and receives the quadratic correction in
F^{(1)}
. On
\{D_{R}\geq n\}
, at least
n
such pairs are long. Summing over their choices costs at most
\binom{N}{n}
. The graph sum is otherwise the same as in Steps 2 and 3, so

\log\int_{\Lambda^{2N}\cap\{D_{R}\geq n\}}e^{-\beta\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}Z_{2N}\leq 2N\log N+N\bigl((2-\beta)\log\lambda+\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}-1\bigr)\\
+\log\binom{N}{n}+n\log\bigl(C_{\beta}\mu_{\beta}([R/\lambda,+\infty))\bigr)+C_{\beta}N\omega_{\lambda}+C_{\beta}\log(N+1).

Let
x_{0}=n/N
and
\alpha=\mu_{\beta}([R/\lambda,+\infty))
. If
x_{0}\geq C_{\beta}^{\prime}\alpha
and
C_{\beta}^{\prime}
is large enough, then

\log\binom{N}{n}+n\log(C_{\beta}\alpha)\leq-Nx_{0}\log\frac{x_{0}}{eC_{\beta}\alpha}\leq-\frac{1}{2}Nx_{0}\log\frac{x_{0}}{\alpha}.

This proves (
3.31
).
∎

3.5.
Upper bound at the critical temperature

At
\beta=2
, the Gaussian-regularized mass
J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)
of a neutral pair is of order
\ell
by (
3.6
), and the quadratic correction of Proposition
2.2
changes the mass of a pair by
O(1)
only, by (
3.8
). Hence, unlike in the proof of Proposition
3.9
, we do not need to single out the twice-isolated pairs, and we treat all isolated pairs alike.

Proposition 3.10
.

There exist constants
\lambda_{2}\in(0,e^{-2}]
and
C>0
with the following property. Let
N\geq 2
be an integer, let
0<\lambda<\lambda_{2}
and let
2\lambda\leq R\leq 1
. Let
I^{\mathrm{dip}}
be the set of particles in isolated dipoles, and let
D_{R}
be the number of isolated dipoles of length larger than
R
. Then

(3.42)

\log\int_{\Lambda^{2N}}\exp\Bigl(-2\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}+N-\frac{1}{2}|I^{\mathrm{dip}}|+D_{R}\Bigr)\mathrm{d}Z_{2N}\leq 2N\log N+N\bigl(\log(2\pi|\log\lambda|)-1\bigr)\\
+CN\left(|\log\lambda|^{-\frac{1}{5}}+\frac{\log(1/R)}{|\log\lambda|}\right)+C\log(N+1)\,.

Since
N-\frac{1}{2}|I^{\mathrm{dip}}|\geq 0
and
D_{R}\geq 0
, taking
R=1
bounds
\log\mathsf{Z}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}
at
\beta=2
. The first of these terms yields (
1.15
) and the second yields (
1.17
), as shown in the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proof.

We bound the integral on each cell of the nearest-neighbor graph, and then sum over the graphs.

Step 1: energy on a fixed graph.

Fix an unmarked nearest-neighbor graph
\gamma
with
K
components, as in Section
2
, and write
\{\gamma_{2N}=\gamma\}
for the set of configurations whose unmarked nearest-neighbor graph is
\gamma
. Let
m
be the number of isolated pairs of
\gamma
, and let
p_{0}
be the number of its isolated dipoles. The graph has
2(N-K)
tree vertices, and each of the
K-m
other components contains at least one of them, so
K-m\leq 2(N-K)
.

In each isolated pair, let
w_{j}
be the endpoint with the larger index, and write the other endpoint as
w_{j}+u_{j}
. We call retained points the points
w_{j}
and all points of the other components. Let

\rho_{j}:=\left(\frac{1}{4}\min_{y\neq w_{j}}|w_{j}-y|\right)\vee\lambda\,,

where the minimum runs over the retained points
y
. For both endpoints
z
of the pair, we have
\mathsf{r}_{1}(z)=(|u_{j}|/4)\vee\lambda
and

|u_{j}|\leq 4\rho_{j}\,,\qquad\mathsf{r}_{1}(z)\leq\rho_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho_{j}\leq 3\mathsf{r}_{2}(z)\,.

The first two bounds follow from the fact that
w_{j}+u_{j}
is the nearest neighbor of
w_{j}
. For the third bound, let
v
be the second nearest neighbor of
z
, so that
|z-w_{j}|\leq|z-v|
. Either
v
is retained, or
v
is the non-retained endpoint of another isolated pair, whose retained endpoint lies within distance
|z-v|
of
v
. In both cases, a retained point other than
w_{j}
lies within distance
3|z-v|
of
w_{j}
. Hence
\rho_{j}\leq(3|z-v|/4)\vee\lambda\leq 3\mathsf{r}_{2}(z)
.

We insert these bounds into Proposition
2.2
, using (
2.6
). The error of an isolated dipole is at most
C(\mathsf{r}_{1}(w_{j})/\rho_{j})^{2}
. The correction of a same-sign isolated pair is at least
2\log(\rho_{j}/\mathsf{r}_{1}(w_{j}))-C
. The other components cost at most
C(N-K)
, as in the proof of Corollary
2.3
. Since
(N-K)+(m-p_{0})\leq N-p_{0}
, we obtain

\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}\geq\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{nn}}-C\sum_{\text{isolated dipoles}}\Bigl(\frac{\mathsf{r}_{1}(w_{j})}{\rho_{j}}\Bigr)^{2}+2\sum_{\text{same-sign isolated pairs}}\log\frac{\rho_{j}}{\mathsf{r}_{1}(w_{j})}-C(N-p_{0})\,.

Step 2: integration on a fixed graph.

We use the Gunson–Panta variables (
3.3
). As in Step 2 of the proof of Lemma
3.4
, the cycle and tree variables satisfy the packing constraint
\sum_{i}|u_{i}|^{2}\leq 36N/\pi
. We bound its indicator by the product of the indicators of the same constraint on the cycle variables and on the tree variables. The bound (
3.4
) of Lemma
3.2
, with
s=36N/\pi
and
a=1/\ell
, replaces the first indicator by the factor
\exp(-\sum_{\text{cycles}}|u_{j}|^{2}/\ell)
, at cost
e^{CN/\ell}
. The tilt
e^{N-\frac{1}{2}|I^{\mathrm{dip}}|}=e^{N-p_{0}}
is absorbed into
e^{C(N-p_{0})}
.

We first integrate the variables
u_{j}
of the isolated pairs, with the retained points fixed. Then
\rho_{j}
is fixed and
|u_{j}|\leq 4\rho_{j}
. By Step 1, an isolated dipole carries the weight

e^{2\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{j})-|u_{j}|^{2}/\ell}e^{C(\mathsf{r}_{1}(w_{j})/\rho_{j})^{2}}\bigl(1+(e-1)\mathbf{1}_{|u_{j}|>R}\bigr)\,,

where the last factor comes from
e^{D_{R}}
. With
x=\mathsf{r}_{1}(w_{j})/\rho_{j}
, we write
e^{Cx^{2}}=1+(e^{Cx^{2}}-1)
. In the second term, we bound
e^{-|u_{j}|^{2}/\ell}
by
1
and the last factor by
e
. Since
\mathsf{r}_{1}(w_{j})=(|u_{j}|/4)\vee\lambda
, the bound (
3.8
) then shows that the integral of this weight over
|u_{j}|\leq 4\rho_{j}
is at most

T_{R}:=J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)+(e-1)J_{\lambda}(1/\ell;R)+C\,.

A same-sign isolated pair carries the weight
e^{2\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u_{j})}(\mathsf{r}_{1}(w_{j})/\rho_{j})^{4}
, up to a constant factor, and its integral is at most
C
by (
3.8
).

Each of the
K-m
other cycles contributes
J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)
, and the
K
reference points contribute
N^{K}
. The tree weight is
e^{\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)}\leq C|u|^{-1}
, by (
2.6
). Lemma
3.1
with
\alpha=1/2
, as in (
3.16
), bounds the integral over the
2(N-K)
tree variables by
(CN/(N-K))^{N-K}
, which we read as
1
when
K=N
. Collecting these factors, we obtain

(3.43)

\int_{\Lambda^{2N}\cap\{\gamma_{2N}=\gamma\}}e^{-2\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}+N-p_{0}+D_{R}}\mathrm{d}Z_{2N}\\
\leq e^{C(N-p_{0})+CN/\ell}N^{K}T_{R}^{p_{0}}J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)^{K-m}\Bigl(\frac{CN}{N-K}\Bigr)^{N-K}\,.

Step 3: counting and optimization.

We now sum over the graphs with given
K
,
m
and
p_{0}
. To build such a graph, we choose the
p_{0}
isolated dipoles and the
2(N-K)
tree vertices. We pair the other
2(K-p_{0})
vertices into
m-p_{0}
same-sign isolated pairs and
K-m
other cycles, and we assign a parent to each tree vertex. Hence the number of such graphs is at most

\frac{(N!)^{2}}{((N-p_{0})!)^{2}p_{0}!}\cdot\frac{(2N-2p_{0})!}{2^{K-p_{0}}(2N-2K)!(m-p_{0})!(K-m)!}\cdot(2N)^{2(N-K)}\,.

By (
3.6
), we have
J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)\geq\pi\ell
for
\lambda
small enough. Let
\delta=(N-K)/N
and
\zeta=(m-p_{0})/N
. We multiply the count by the right side of (
3.43
), and we use, for every integer
n\geq 0
, the bounds
(2n)!\leq 4^{n}(n!)^{2}
and
n!\geq(n/e)^{n}
, together with
N!/p_{0}!\leq N^{N-p_{0}}
and
K-m\leq 2(N-K)
. This bounds the product by

N!N^{N}J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)^{N}\Bigl(\frac{T_{R}}{J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)}\Bigr)^{p_{0}}e^{CN/\ell}\\
\times\exp\Bigl(N\delta\bigl(5\log(1/\delta)-\log\ell+C\bigr)+N\zeta\bigl(\log(1/\zeta)-\log\ell+C\bigr)\Bigr)\,.

The terms
-\log\ell
account for the
(N-K)+(m-p_{0})
factors
J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)\geq\pi\ell
that are missing compared with
J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)^{N}
. The term
5\delta\log(1/\delta)
collects
2\delta\log(1/\delta)
from
(2N)^{2(N-K)}/(2N-2K)!
,
\delta\log(1/\delta)
from the tree integral, and
2\delta\log(1/\delta)
from
N^{K-m}/(K-m)!
.

By (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), we have

\log\frac{T_{R}}{J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)}\leq\frac{C(1+J_{\lambda}(1/\ell;R))}{J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)}\leq C\frac{1+\log\ell+\log(1/R)}{\ell}\,.

We apply (
3.11
) with
(A,B)=(C-\log\ell,5)
and with
(A,B)=(C-\log\ell,1)
. It bounds the exponent in the second line by
CN\ell^{-1/5}+CN/\ell
. Finally, we use
\log N!\leq N\log N-N+C\log(N+1)
, the bound on
\log J_{\lambda}(1/\ell)
in (
3.6
) and
(1+\log\ell)/\ell\leq C\ell^{-1/5}
, and we sum over the at most
(N+1)^{3}
triples
(K,m,p_{0})
. This proves (
3.42
).
∎

4.
Free energy lower bound and proof of Theorem
1

We now derive a lower bound on the partition function matching (
3.29
) and, at
\beta=2
, (
3.42
). For that, we use a method inspired by previous work on the one-component plasma and on Ginzburg-Landau, starting in
[
33
,
35
]
, which allows us, thanks to the electric formulation of the energy, to compute the interaction additively in terms of electric potentials defined in disjoint subregions of the space.

Proposition 4.1
.

The following estimates hold as
\lambda\to 0
, uniformly in
N\geq 2
.
For
\beta\in(2,+\infty)
, with
\varepsilon_{\lambda}
as in subsection
1.5
, we have

(4.1)

\log\mathsf{Z}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}\geq 2N\log N+N(2-\beta)\log\lambda+N(\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}-1)+O_{\beta}(N\varepsilon_{\lambda}).

For
\beta=2
, we have

(4.2)

\log\mathsf{Z}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}\geq 2N\log N+N\log|\log\lambda|+N(\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}-1)-\frac{CN}{|\log\lambda|^{1/5}}.

Proof.

Step 1: bounding the energy from above.

We restrict
X_{N}
to

H:=\left\{X_{N}\in\Lambda^{N}:\begin{array}[]{l}|x_{i}-x_{j}|\geq 8\lambda\text{ for }i\neq j,\\
\mathrm{dist}(x_{i},\partial\Lambda)\geq 4\lambda\text{ for }1\leq i\leq N\end{array}\right\},

and
y_{i}\in B(x_{i},\frac{1}{2}\varrho(x_{i}))
, where

\varrho(x_{i}):=\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}\min_{j\neq i}|x_{j}-x_{i}|,\mathrm{dist}(x_{i},\partial\Lambda),1\right\}.

For
\lambda
small, we have
\varrho(x_{i})\geq 4\lambda
. The balls
B(x_{i},\varrho(x_{i}))
are disjoint and contained in
\Lambda
, and both smeared charges of the
i
-th pair are supported in
B(x_{i},\frac{3}{4}\varrho(x_{i}))
.
For such configurations let us bound the energy from above. First we recall (
1.10
).

We then let, for each
i
,
\psi_{i}
solve

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta\psi_{i}=2\pi(\delta_{x_{i}}^{(\lambda)}-\delta_{y_{i}}^{(\lambda)})&\text{in}\ B(x_{i},\varrho(x_{i}))\\
\frac{\partial\psi_{i}}{\partial\nu}=0&\text{on}\ \partial B(x_{i},\varrho(x_{i}))\end{array}\right.,

where
\nu
is the outer unit normal to the ball.

The Neumann problem is compatible because the source has total mass zero.

We then define a global “electric field”
E
by pasting together the electric fields defined over these disjoint balls:

E:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{B(x_{i},\varrho(x_{i}))}\nabla\psi_{i}.

Thanks to the crucial choice of zero Neumann boundary conditions on the boundary of the disjoint balls, this vector field satisfies

(4.3)

-\mathrm{div}\,E=2\pi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\delta_{x_{i}}^{(\lambda)}-\delta_{y_{i}}^{(\lambda)}\bigr)\right)=-\Delta h_{\lambda}\quad\text{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{2},

where
h_{\lambda}
is the electric potential of the configuration as in (
1.8
).
We now bound the energy from above by the
L^{2}
norm of
E
, using that
\nabla h_{\lambda}
is the
L^{2}
projection of
E
onto gradients. Expanding the square, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|E|^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla h_{\lambda}|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|E-\nabla h_{\lambda}|^{2}+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(E-\nabla h_{\lambda})\cdot\nabla h_{\lambda}\,.

Both fields lie in
L^{2}
. Since
E
has compact support and neutrality gives
h_{\lambda}=O(|x|^{-1})
and
\nabla h_{\lambda}=O(|x|^{-2})
, integration by parts with a cutoff at infinity makes the last term vanish by (
4.3
). It thus follows that

(4.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla h_{\lambda}|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{B(x_{i},\varrho(x_{i}))}|\nabla\psi_{i}|^{2},

and thus we can reduce the computation to a sum over the disjoint balls.
We next bound the right-hand side.
First we let
v_{i}:=\psi_{i}-(\mathsf{g}*\delta_{x_{i}}^{(\lambda)}-\mathsf{g}*\delta_{y_{i}}^{(\lambda)})
. It solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta v_{i}=0&\text{in}\ B(x_{i},\varrho(x_{i}))\\
\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial\nu}=\left(\frac{(x-x_{i})}{|x-x_{i}|^{2}}-\frac{(x-y_{i})}{|x-y_{i}|^{2}}\right)\cdot\nu&\text{on}\ \partial B(x_{i},\varrho(x_{i}))\end{array}\right.

and thus by elliptic regularity estimates we have

(4.5)

\|\nabla v_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(B(x_{i},\frac{3}{4}\varrho(x_{i})))}\leq C\frac{|x_{i}-y_{i}|}{\varrho(x_{i})^{2}}.

Now, using (
4.5
), (
1.4
) and (
1.6
) and decomposing
\psi_{i}
as above, we find

\displaystyle\int_{B(x_{i},\varrho(x_{i}))}|\nabla\psi_{i}|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{B(x_{i},\varrho(x_{i}))}\psi_{i}\left(\delta_{x_{i}}^{(\lambda)}-\delta_{y_{i}}^{(\lambda)}\right)

\displaystyle=2\pi\left(2(\mathsf{g}(\lambda)+\kappa)-\int\mathsf{g}*\delta_{x_{i}}^{(\lambda)}\delta_{y_{i}}^{(\lambda)}-\int\mathsf{g}*\delta_{y_{i}}^{(\lambda)}\delta_{x_{i}}^{(\lambda)}\right)+2\pi\int v_{i}\left(\delta_{x_{i}}^{(\lambda)}-\delta_{y_{i}}^{(\lambda)}\right)

\displaystyle=4\pi(\mathsf{g}(\lambda)+\kappa)-4\pi\iint\mathsf{g}(x-y)\delta_{x_{i}}^{(\lambda)}(x)\delta_{y_{i}}^{(\lambda)}(y)+O\Bigr(\frac{|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{2}}{\varrho(x_{i})^{2}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=4\pi(\mathsf{g}(\lambda)+\kappa)-4\pi\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(x_{i}-y_{i})+O\Bigr(\frac{|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{2}}{\varrho(x_{i})^{2}}\Bigr).

Inserting this into (
4.4
) and (
1.10
), we deduce that

(4.6)

\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}(X_{N},Y_{N})\leq-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(x_{i}-y_{i})+O\Bigr(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{2}}{\varrho(x_{i})^{2}}\Bigr).

In all cases, since we have built the configurations so that
|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\varrho(x_{i})
, the error term is bounded by
O(N)
.

For
\beta=2
, set
t=\ell^{-1/5}
. Restrict the
x_{i}
’s to boundary distance at least
t
and mutual distances at least
4t
. Successive placement gives volume at least
N^{N}e^{-CNt}
. Restrict each dipole length to
2\lambda\leq|x_{i}-y_{i}|\leq t^{2}
. For
\lambda
small, the disjoint balls
B(x_{i},t)
contain both smeared charges. The preceding Neumann construction gives an energy error at most
CNt^{2}
. The
N!
assignments of negative labels are disjoint; hence

\mathsf{Z}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}\geq N!N^{N}e^{-CNt-CNt^{2}}\left[2\pi\left(\ell-\tfrac{2}{5}\log\ell-\log 2\right)\right]^{N}.

Using
N!\geq(N/e)^{N}
proves (
4.2
). We now assume
\beta>2
.

Step 2: bounding the free energy.

For
r\geq 4\lambda
, define

5.
Energetic control on linear statistics

In this section, we use our ball-growth method introduced in Section
2
to derive an energetic control on the fluctuations of linear statistics, similar to
[
24
, Prop. 2.5]
for the one-component plasma. We prove Theorem
2
, giving a fluctuation bound at scale
\sqrt{N}\,\omega_{\lambda}^{1/4}
for Lipschitz test-functions. Let us emphasize that linear statistics are in fact expected to fluctuate much less, specifically at a rate
o_{N}(\sqrt{N})
for small but fixed
\lambda
. Proving such a rigidity statement would require more involved techniques.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Step 1: the electric energy bounds the fluctuations.
As in the one-component case
[
34
,
24
]
or in
[
22
]
, the fluctuations are well bounded by the electric energy
\int|\nabla h_{\vec{\alpha}}|^{2}
, where
h_{\vec{\alpha}}
is as in (
2.1
), for every
\vec{\alpha}\in(0,\infty)^{2N}
. We recall the elementary argument.

Let
\xi
be a compactly supported Lipschitz test-function from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\tilde{\xi}
be
\xi-\xi(0)
multiplied by a cutoff equal to
1
on
\Lambda
, supported in
[-\sqrt{N},2\sqrt{N}]^{2}
and with gradient bounded by
C/\sqrt{N}
. Then
\mathrm{Fluct}_{N}(\tilde{\xi})=\mathrm{Fluct}_{N}(\xi)
and

\|\nabla\tilde{\xi}\|_{L^{\infty}}+N^{-1/2}\|\nabla\tilde{\xi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C\|\nabla\xi\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Neutrality gives
\nabla h_{\vec{\alpha}}\in L^{2}
. We apply Green’s formula to mollifications of
\tilde{\xi}
and pass to the limit in
W^{1,2}
. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\tilde{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\left(2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{2N}d_{i}\delta_{z_{i}}^{(\alpha_{i})}\right)\right|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\tilde{\xi}\Delta h_{\vec{\alpha}}\right|\leq\|\nabla\tilde{\xi}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla h_{\vec{\alpha}}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

On the other hand, by definition of the smeared charges, we may write

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\tilde{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\left(2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{2N}d_{i}(\delta_{z_{i}}-\delta_{z_{i}}^{(\alpha_{i})})\right)\right|\leq C\|\nabla\tilde{\xi}\|_{L^{\infty}}\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\alpha_{i}.

Combining the two relations, we deduce that

\left|\mathrm{Fluct}_{N}(\xi)\right|\leq C\|\nabla\xi\|_{L^{\infty}}\left(\sqrt{N}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla h_{\vec{\alpha}}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\alpha_{i}\right),

hence

\left|\mathrm{Fluct}_{N}(\xi)\right|^{2}\leq C\|\nabla\xi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\left(N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla h_{\vec{\alpha}}|^{2}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\alpha_{i}\right)^{2}\right).

If
\xi(x)=\xi_{0}(x/\sqrt{N})
, then
\|\nabla\xi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{\|\nabla\xi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\sqrt{N}}
. It follows that

\left|\mathrm{Fluct}_{N}(\xi)\right|^{2}\leq C\|\nabla\xi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla h_{\vec{\alpha}}|^{2}+\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\alpha_{i}\right)^{2}\right).

+\log\int_{\Lambda^{2N-2m}\cap\{\gamma_{2N-2m}=\gamma^{2}\}}e^{-\beta F^{(2)}}\prod_{i\in I^{c}}\mathrm{d}z_{i}+C_{\beta}(N-K).

For a pair in
I\setminus I^{\prime}
, the extra radial contribution is controlled by

\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}\lambda^{2-\beta}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{\beta\mathsf{g}_{\lambda}(u)}\left(\left(\frac{(|u|/4)\vee\lambda}{\theta}\right)^{2}\wedge 1\right)\mathrm{d}u\leq C_{\beta}\varphi_{\beta}(\theta).

This follows by splitting at
2\lambda
and
4\theta
. For
0\leq s\leq 1
,

e^{2ts}\leq 1+(e^{2t}-1)s.

The unperturbed quadratic factor is bounded on
|u|\leq 4\rho
. Hence the tilt adds at most
C_{\beta,t}\varphi_{\beta}(\theta)
to the normalized radial factor, bounded by

(1+C_{\beta,t}\varphi_{\beta}(\theta))(1+C_{\beta}\varphi_{\beta}(\rho)).

For each marked same-sign pair, the tilt is at most
e^{2t}
, absorbed by increasing
C_{0}
. Thus (
3.28
) and the entropy comparison above (
3.36
) give, for
p\geq N/2
,

\log\int_{\Lambda^{2m}\cap\{\gamma_{2m}=\gamma^{1}\}}\exp\left(t\sum_{i\in I}\left(\left(\frac{\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})}{\theta}\right)^{2}\wedge 1\right)-\beta F^{(1)}\right)\prod_{i\in I}\mathrm{d}z_{i}\\
+p\log p-\widehat{p}_{0}\log\widehat{p}_{0}-(p-\widehat{p}_{0})\log(p-\widehat{p}_{0})+C_{0}(p-\widehat{p}_{0})\\
\leq m\bigl(\log N+(2-\beta)\log\lambda+\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}\bigr)+O_{\beta,t}\bigl(N(\varphi_{\beta}(\theta)+\omega_{\lambda})\bigr)-c_{\beta,t}N\left(\frac{p-\widehat{p}_{0}}{N}\right)^{2}+C_{\beta,t}\log(N+1).

For
p<N/2
, the tilt is at most
e^{2tm}
, so the crude bound suffices as in Step 3 of the upper-bound proof. Taking
0<\lambda<\lambda_{\beta,t}
for a threshold depending only on
\beta,t
, combining with (
3.37
) and summing over the marked graphs yields

\log\int_{\Lambda^{2N}}e^{tA_{3}-\beta\mathsf{F}_{\lambda}}\mathrm{d}Z_{2N}\leq 2N\log N+N\bigl((2-\beta)\log\lambda+\log\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}-1\bigr)\\
+O_{\beta,t}\bigl(N(\varphi_{\beta}(\theta)+\omega_{\lambda})\bigr)+C_{\beta,t}\log(N+1).

Subtracting the lower bound of Proposition
4.1
gives

(5.4)

\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}}[e^{tA_{3}}]\leq C_{\beta,t}N\bigl(\varphi_{\beta}(\theta)+\omega_{\lambda}\bigr)+C_{\beta,t}\log(N+1),

with

as in (

Step 5: control on the radii.
Set

R_{0}=\begin{cases}\omega_{\lambda}^{1/4}&\text{if $\beta=2$},\\
\lambda\omega_{\lambda}^{-1/(2(\beta-2))}&\text{if $\beta>2$},\end{cases}\qquad\mathcal{A}_{M}=\{N-\frac{1}{2}|I^{\mathrm{dip}}|+D_{R_{0}}\leq MN\sqrt{\omega_{\lambda}}\}.

For
\lambda
small, we have
2\lambda\leq R_{0}\leq\omega_{\lambda}^{1/4}
. Indeed, for
\beta>2
, the upper bound amounts to
\lambda\leq\omega_{\lambda}^{1/4+1/(2(\beta-2))}
, and the right side is at least
\lambda^{1/40}
by (
1.12
), since
\ell\geq 1
. For
\beta>2
, we also have
\alpha=(\lambda/R_{0})^{\beta-2}=\omega_{\lambda}^{1/2}
, and we use (
1.15
) and (
1.16
). For
\beta=2
, we use (
1.15
) and (
1.17
), since
\log(1/R_{0})/\ell=\log\ell/(20\ell)=o(\omega_{\lambda})
. A union bound and summation over the counts give, for
M
large,

(5.5)

\mathbb{P}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}(\mathcal{A}_{M}^{c})\leq C_{\beta}(N+1)^{C_{\beta}}e^{-c_{\beta}MN\omega_{\lambda}}.

On
\mathcal{A}_{M}
, at most
2MN\sqrt{\omega_{\lambda}}
particles lie outside isolated dipoles of length at most
R_{0}
. The balls
B(z_{i},|z_{i}-z_{\phi_{1}(i)}|/2)
are disjoint in
[-\sqrt{N},2\sqrt{N}]^{2}
, so
\sum_{i}\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})^{2}\leq CN+2N\lambda^{2}\leq C^{\prime}N
. Thus

\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})\leq 2NR_{0}+\left(2MN\sqrt{\omega_{\lambda}}\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq C(1+\sqrt{M})N\omega_{\lambda}^{1/4}.

It follows that

(5.6)

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2N}\mathsf{r}_{1}(z_{i})\right)^{2}\leq C(1+M)N^{2}\sqrt{\omega_{\lambda}}.

Step 6: conclusion.
For
\beta>2
, fix
t=1
in (
5.4
) and choose

so that
12\pi c_{\beta}\leq\beta
and
12\pi c_{\beta}C\leq 1
, with

from Step 2. Hölder’s inequality with three factors and

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}}[e^{sX}]\leq\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}}[e^{rX}]\bigr)^{s/r},\qquad 0\leq s\leq r,\quad r>0,

give the following bound from (
5.2
), (
5.3
) and (
5.4
). For

5.1

\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}}\left[\exp\left(c_{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla h_{\vec{\alpha}}|^{2}\right)\right]\leq C_{\beta}N(\varphi_{\beta}(\theta)+\omega_{\lambda})+C_{\beta}\log(N+1).

Therefore, there exists an event
\mathcal{B}_{M}
such that for all

large enough,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla h_{\vec{\alpha}}|^{2}\leq C_{\beta}MN(\varphi_{\beta}(\theta)+\omega_{\lambda})\quad\text{on }\mathcal{B}_{M},

\mathbb{P}_{N,\beta}^{\lambda}(\mathcal{B}_{M}^{c})\leq C_{\beta}(N+1)^{C_{\beta}}e^{-c_{\beta}MN\omega_{\lambda}}.

Combining the last displays and (

(5.7)

|\mathrm{Fluct}_{N}(\xi)|^{2}\leq C_{\beta}M\|\nabla\xi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}N({\theta}^{2}+\omega_{\lambda}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\varphi_{\beta}(\theta))\quad\text{on the event $\mathcal{A}_{M}\cap\mathcal{B}_{M}$.}

Optimizing over

\theta=\begin{cases}\ell^{-1/2}&\text{if $\beta=2$}\\
\lambda^{\frac{(\beta-2)}{\beta}}&\text{if $\beta\in(2,4)$}\\
\lambda^{1/2}\ell^{1/4}&\text{if $\beta=4$}\\
\lambda^{1/2}&\text{if $\beta\in(4,\infty)$}.\end{cases}

We reduce

\min\{1,C_{\beta}(N+1)^{C_{\beta}}e^{-c_{\beta}MN\omega_{\lambda}}\}\leq C_{\beta}^{\prime}e^{-c_{\beta}^{\prime}M\sqrt{N}\,\omega_{\lambda}}

absorbs the polynomial prefactor. Replacing the event parameter
