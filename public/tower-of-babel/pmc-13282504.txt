When benign mimics malignancy: a call for surgical restraint and clinical nuance in endometriosis-associated ovarian masses
Authors: Farr Nezhat, Pegah Rashidian
Published: 2026

Few scenarios in gynecologic surgery test clinical judgment more than the evaluation of an adnexal mass suspicious for malignancy. Nowhere is this challenge more pronounced than in patients with endometriosis, a disease that is at once common, heterogeneous, and biologically complex. Affecting approximately 10% of reproductive-age women, endometriosis frequently involves the ovary in the form of endometriomas (1). Although overwhelmingly benign, these lesions exist within a spectrum that includes a small but real risk of malignant transformation, most notably into endometrioid and clear cell carcinomas. It is precisely this duality (benign prevalence coupled with malignant potential) that places clinicians in a persistent state of diagnostic tension (1).

The study by Parpex et al. (2) brings this tension into sharp focus through its examination of polypoid endometrioma, a rare and underrecognized entity that can closely mimic ovarian malignancy. By combining a detailed case presentation with a systematic review of 22 previously reported cases, the investigators illuminate a consistent and sobering pattern: when confronted with atypical imaging features, clinicians overwhelmingly err on the side of aggressive surgical management. In all reported cases, surgery was performed for presumed malignancy, and nearly half of the patients underwent extensive procedures, including hysterectomy, despite ultimately benign histopathology (2).

This is not merely a reflection of individual decision-making, but rather an expression of a broader and deeply ingrained surgical instinct — to act decisively in the face of potential cancer. That instinct is understandable and, in many contexts, lifesaving. However, as the present study underscores, it also may predispose to what can be termed an “overtreatment bias,” particularly in diseases such as endometriosis that blur the conventional boundaries between benign and malignant pathology (2).

Modern imaging systems, including structured approaches, such as ovarian-adnexal reporting and data system (O-RADS), have significantly improved our ability to stratify risk. Yet, as demonstrated in the index case, even a lesion categorized as O-RADS 4, suggesting intermediate risk and justifying surgical exploration, ultimately may prove benign (3). This is not a failure of the system, but rather a reflection of the biological reality that endometriosis often exhibits features that overlap with malignancy. Chronic inflammation, increased cellular proliferation, resistance to apoptosis, and invasive behavior all contribute to imaging findings, such as mural nodules, vascularity, and contrast enhancement. These “cancer-like” characteristics are intrinsic to the disease itself and not necessarily indicative of malignant transformation (1).

The consequence is a diagnostic gray zone in which imaging, biomarkers, and clinical presentation converge but do not definitively resolve uncertainty. Within this space, the threshold for aggressive intervention often lowers, particularly when the perceived risk of missing a malignancy outweighs the consequences of overtreatment (4). Yet, this calculus deserves closer scrutiny. In reproductive-age women, the removal of ovaries or the uterus carries profound and irreversible implications for fertility, endocrine function, and long-term health. Even beyond reproductive considerations, the psychological and physical burdens of extensive surgery are substantial (4).

What emerges from the work of Parpex et al. (2) is not simply the identification of a relatively uncommon entity, but a broader challenge to our current decision-making paradigm. It invites us to reconsider whether the default response to uncertainty always should be maximal intervention, or whether a more calibrated, patient-centered approach is feasible and preferable (2).

Such an approach begins with recognizing that not all uncertainty mandates the same response. Clinical decision-making should extend beyond algorithmic classification systems to incorporate individual patient factors, including age, reproductive goals, symptomatology, prior medical and surgical history, and personal risk tolerance. Disease-specific features, such as lesion size, morphology, bilaterality, and interval change, must be interpreted within this broader clinical context (4). The wide age range observed in reported cases of polypoid endometrioma, from young reproductive-age women to postmenopausal patients, further reinforces that management strategies cannot be uniform (2).

Equally important is an appreciation of the limitations of the existing literature. As highlighted by the investigators, many reported cases lack detailed patient-level data, including prior treatments, hormonal exposure, and longitudinal imaging. This absence of granularity limits our ability to identify risk factors for atypical presentations or to predict which lesions are most likely to mimic malignancy. The suggestion that interventions, such as ethanol sclerotherapy, may induce polypoid transformation through inflammatory and reparative mechanisms is particularly intriguing, raising the possibility that some of these “atypical” lesions may, in part, be iatrogenic (2). This hypothesis, although speculative, underscores the need for more rigorous and systematic data collection.

If preoperative assessment is inherently limited, then intraoperative decision-making assumes even greater importance. Decades ago, laparoscopic evaluations of large series of adnexal masses demonstrated that direct visualization, combined with frozen section analysis, offers the highest diagnostic accuracy for distinguishing benign from malignant disease compared with preoperative imaging and serum CA-125 (5). This principle remains highly relevant in contemporary practice. Rather than committing to definitive and potentially radical surgery based solely on preoperative suspicion, a staged approach, beginning with diagnostic laparoscopy and proceeding according to intraoperative findings, may provide a more balanced strategy (5).

Frozen section analysis, when available, serves as a critical adjunct in this process, allowing real-time histopathologic assessment to guide the extent of surgery. Although not infallible, it significantly reduces the likelihood of overtreatment and undertreatment. Importantly, this approach aligns with the broader movement toward precision surgery, tailoring intervention not only to disease characteristics but also to patient priorities (5).

At the same time, the risk of undertreatment should not be overlooked. Endometriosis is not a benign condition in the experiential sense; it frequently is associated with chronic pain, abnormal bleeding, ovarian dysfunction, and diminished quality of life. In the index case, the presence of multiple uterine leiomyomas, a small contralateral ovarian endometrioma, and endometriosis of the posterior cul-de-sac suggests a multifactorial source of symptoms. The patient had already undergone six months of medical therapy with relugolix–estradiol–norethisterone acetate without adequate relief (2). In such scenarios, a more comprehensive surgical approach addressing all contributing pathologies may be warranted. Thus, the challenge is not simply to avoid overtreatment, but to achieve appropriate treatment, neither excessive nor insufficient.

Ultimately, the significance of this study lies in its ability to reframe a familiar clinical dilemma (2). The question is not whether we should operate on suspicious adnexal masses — clearly, many warrant surgical evaluation — but rather how we operate, and how much we do at surgery. It is a question of proportion, judgment, and restraint.

As our diagnostic tools continue to evolve, they undoubtedly will enhance our ability to stratify risk. Yet, they are unlikely to eliminate uncertainty altogether. In this enduring gray zone, clinical judgment remains paramount. The study by Parpex et al. (2) serves as a timely reminder that in the management of endometriosis-associated ovarian masses, caution must be balanced with preservation, and decisiveness with nuance.

A shift toward individualized, staged, and patient-centered care, anchored in intraoperative assessment and informed by a deep understanding of disease biology, offers a path forward. In doing so, we may better align our surgical decisions with the fundamental principle that has long guided medicine: first, do no harm.
