Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and management of renal involvement in Fabry disease
Authors: Wenkai Guo, Jingru Bi, Pengcheng Ji, Yuansheng Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFabry disease is an X-linked hereditary lysosomal storage disease caused by variants in the GLA gene. These variants result in reduced or absent α-galactosidase A (α-Gal A) enzyme activity, leading to the progressive accumulation of enzyme metabolism substrates in multiple organs. This accumulation ultimately causes systemic clinical manifestations involving multiple organ systems. Renal involvement is a common clinical manifestation in Fabry disease and an important determinant of morbidity and disease progression. Early identification and active intervention of renal involvement in Fabry disease can effectively slow the progression of renal function deterioration and may significantly reduce the incidence of secondary cerebrovascular and cardiovascular events in advanced stages of Fabry disease nephropathy. This review summarizes the latest research advances on renal involvement in Fabry disease, covering its epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, diagnostic indicators, differential diagnosis and treatment strategies, in order to deepen the understanding of renal involvement in Fabry disease and reduce missed diagnosis and misdiagnosis.Keywords: Fabry disease, renal involvement, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, treatment

Introduction

Fabry disease is a rare X-linked hereditary lysosomal storage disorder. It results from variants in the GLA gene, which encodes the lysosomal hydrolase alpha-galactosidase A (α-Gal A) located at Xq22.1 [1]. The variants cause reduced or complete loss of α-Gal A enzyme activity, leading to impaired cleavage of α-galactose residues from neutral glycosphingolipids. This results in abnormal glycosphingolipid metabolism and progressive accumulation of the enzyme’s substrates-globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) and its deacetylated derivative, globotriaosylsphingosine (Lyso-Gb3)-within the lysosomes of cells throughout multiple organs and tissues. This accumulation induces multi-organ lesions and corresponding clinical manifestations [2–5], such as acroparesthesia, angiokeratomas, anhidrosis or hypohidrosis, digestive system involvement, and ocular/auditory involvement, which typically first present in children or adolescents. As patients age, progressive multi-organ damage occurs and may eventually lead to life-threatening renal, cardiac, and cerebrovascular complications, potentially resulting in premature death. Clinically, Fabry disease is commonly described as having classical and later-onset phenotypes [6,7]. For clarity, we distinguish variant category (genotype/variant association) from clinical phenotype: GLA variants are often referred to as classical-associated or later-onset–associated based on the phenotype they most consistently produce in affected male hemizygotes, whereas ‘classical’ vs ‘later-onset’ refers to the patient’s clinical presentation. Because male hemizygotes typically show a more uniform and often more severe disease course, this classification has traditionally been anchored in male phenotypes. In males, the classical phenotype is usually characterized by absent or severely reduced α-galactosidase A activity (often <1%), onset in childhood or adolescence, and progressive multi-organ involvement (e.g., neuropathic pain, angiokeratomas and corneal changes) and severe prognosis. In contrast, later-onset Fabry disease in males is generally characterized by residual α-Gal A activity, presents in adulthood, and may be organ-predominant-most commonly involving the heart and/or kidneys-with a milder prognosis. Female heterozygotes carrying either classical- or later-onset–associated GLA variants exhibit a highly variable clinical spectrum, in part due to skewed X-chromosome inactivation, ranging from asymptomatic to severe organ involvement; nevertheless, males are generally affected earlier and more severely.

Clinically, progressive renal involvement represents one of the most common manifestations in both classical and later-onset Fabry disease, particularly among male patients. Progressive accumulation of enzyme metabolic substrates occurs in all renal cell types, leading to renal manifestations such as proteinuria and reduced glomerular filtration rate. These manifestations may gradually progress to chronic kidney diseases (CKD) and ultimately end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [8–10], which became one of the common causes of mortality in Fabry disease prior to the availability of dialysis, kidney transplantation, and enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) [11–13]. Enhanced recognition of renal involvement in Fabry disease, coupled with early diagnosis and proactive intervention, can improve renal outcomes and is likely associated with more favorable cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes.

This review focuses on recent advances in renal involvement in Fabry disease and provides a comprehensive overview in the form of a state-of-the-art narrative review. It summarizes the epidemiology, pathogenesis, clinical and pathological features, diagnostic and differential diagnostic considerations, and therapeutic strategies of Fabry nephropathy, with the aim of improving clinicians’ understanding of renal involvement in Fabry disease, facilitating earlier recognition, and reducing missed or delayed diagnoses. To enhance the transparency of the review, we searched PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science from database inception to November 2025, with a furthertargeted update to include literature published through April 2026, using keywords related to ‘Fabry disease’, ‘Fabry nephropathy’, ‘kidney involvement’, ‘renal manifestations’, ‘diagnosis’, ‘pathology’, ‘enzyme replacement therapy’, ‘migalastat’, ‘substrate reduction therapy’, and ‘gene therapy’, in combination with both controlled vocabulary and free-text terms. Eligible literature included randomized and non-randomized clinical studies, registry and cohort studies, cross-sectional screening studies, pathology and biomarker studies, expert consensus statements/guidelines, and representative mechanistic investigations relevant to renal involvement in Fabry disease. Publications with limited relevance to renal involvement, duplicate reports, or insufficient information were excluded. Given the substantial heterogeneity of the available evidence in terms of study design, outcome measures, and follow-up duration, we did not apply a formal systematic review process or quantitative risk-of-bias assessment tools. Instead, the evidence was synthesized narratively, and the principal types of evidence supporting key statements were indicated whenever possible.

Epidemiology of renal involvement in Fabry disease

Clinically, the onset and progression of renal involvement in Fabry disease are highly heterogeneous and are influenced by the variant category (classical vs late-onset), residual α-Gal A activity, sex, and clinical phenotype. In natural-history studies of untreated classical male hemizygotes, proteinuria tends to occur earlier and increases with age: Branton et al. reported that approximately 50% develop proteinuria by age 35, rising to ∼90% by age 50 [13]. In female heterozygotes, due in part to skewed X-chromosome inactivation, the clinical phenotype is more variable; renal involvement generally occurs later with a wider spectrum of severity, and FOS analyses also highlight this marked heterogeneity [14]. A cross-sectional analysis of data from the Fabry Registry showed that both males and females may exhibit proteinuria even in earlier CKD stages, with substantial differences across CKD strata, supporting the need for genotype/phenotype-informed stratification and longitudinal monitoring of proteinuria (or albuminuria) together with eGFR to assess renal risk [15]. Our group further found that classical male patients may develop proteinuria around age 20 and kidney dysfunction by approximately age 25. With advancing age, over 50% of patients manifest proteinuria and kidney dysfunction of varying severity by ages 25 and 40, respectively [16]. A National Institutes of Health (NIH) study of 105 classical male patients revealed that all patients surviving to age 55 developed evidence of renal involvement [13].

In epidemiological studies of renal involvement in Fabry disease, dialysis and kidney transplant populations have historically been among the most extensively screened high-risk groups. However, beyond patients receiving kidney replacement therapy for end-stage renal disease, screening is also highly relevant in non-dialysis CKD populations, as earlier recognition at this stage is more likely to influence treatment timing and renal prognosis. Although screening studies in non-dialysis-dependent CKD (NDD-CKD) remain relatively limited, available evidence suggests that detection rates in this population may be at least comparable to those observed in ESRD patients receiving kidney replacement therapy. Battaglia et al. noted in a 2021 review that previous Fabry disease screening efforts had largely focused on dialysis and kidney transplant recipients, in whom renal damage is often already advanced, thereby underscoring the greater clinical value of moving screening upstream to the non-dialysis CKD stage. In that review, the reported prevalence of Fabry disease in previously published NDD-CKD cohorts ranged from 0% to 0.95%, although marked heterogeneity existed across studies in terms of inclusion criteria, screening methods, and the inclusion of female patients [17]. In a Japanese male CKD cohort, Nagata et al. found a Fabry disease prevalence of 0.48% (2/419) in patients with CKD stages 1–5, compared with 0.06% (1/1,703) in patients with CKD stage 5 on dialysis, suggesting that screening during the non-dialysis phase may facilitate earlier diagnosis [18]. Similarly, the Australian aCQuiRE study screened 2,992 individuals across a broad CKD spectrum, including stages 1–5 and kidney replacement therapy (dialysis/transplant), and identified 6 confirmed cases (0.20%), 3 of which were newly diagnosed, further supporting the clinical value of screening across a wider CKD population [19]. More recently, a multicenter Italian study published by Battaglia et al. in 2025 suggested that the prevalence of Fabry disease in NDD-CKD populations may have been underestimated because of overly restrictive prior screening criteria and methodological limitations, further supporting more targeted high-risk screening in non-dialysis CKD patients [20].

This article reviews studies on Fabry disease screening in dialysis and kidney transplant populations. The findings reveal considerable variation in Fabry disease prevalence, ranging from approximately 0.02% to 2.82% and 0.12% to 2.64% in these cohorts respectively, as summarized in Table 1. The dialysis- and transplant-based studies included in this review were largely cross-sectional screening studies conducted across different countries and centers, with substantial heterogeneity in study populations, screening approaches, and case-confirmation pathways; therefore, the reported prevalence ranges should be interpreted with caution. Since not all ESRD patients undergo renal biopsy or genetic testing prior to dialysis or kidney transplantation, the prevalence of Fabry disease may be severely underestimated. Despite the currently low detection rate of Fabry disease among ESRD patients, active screening in high-risk populations (including ESRD) remains clinically significant for timely diagnosis and intervention. Furthermore, such screening facilitates early identification of potentially affected family members within pedigrees.

From a clinical practice perspective, Fabry disease screening is better implemented as a targeted strategy based on high-risk features rather than as broad, indiscriminate screening of the general nephrology population. The main groups who merit screening include patients with CKD stages 1–5 of unknown etiology, particularly those with persistent albuminuria/proteinuria, progressive decline in eGFR, FSGS-like presentations or relevant pathological clues, and those with signs suggestive of multisystem involvement; patients with ESRD of unknown cause, as well as those receiving dialysis or living with a kidney transplant; and family members of index cases. The timing of screening should also be appropriately shifted earlier within high-risk populations. In other words, for non-dialysis CKD patients with the above suggestive features, screening should be considered at the initial evaluation of unexplained CKD or once suggestive extrarenal manifestations become apparent, rather than being deferred until the patient requires kidney replacement therapy [17]. In addition to unexplained proteinuria, CKD progression, and ESRD of unknown cause, clinical red flags for Fabry disease include acroparesthesia, hypohidrosis or anhidrosis, angiokeratomas, corneal verticillata, gastrointestinal symptoms, tinnitus or hearing loss, unexplained left ventricular hypertrophy, early-onset stroke, and a relevant family history. Among supportive clues, the urinary Maltese cross sign and elevated urinary Gb3 may raise suspicion for Fabry nephropathy, but neither is sufficient to establish the diagnosis on its own [3,21]. Overall, the current evidence more strongly supports targeted screening in high-risk populations, combined with family cascade screening, rather than nonselective broad screening in populations with low prevalence or without suggestive clinical features [22–25] (Table 1).

Pathogenesis of renal involvement in Fabry disease

Fabry disease primarily results from GLA gene variants that cause reduced or absent α-Gal A enzyme activity, leading to overload deposition of enzyme metabolic substrates (Gb3/Lyso-Gb3). While Gb3 storage is a histopathological hallmark of Fabry disease, available translational and experimental studies suggest that Lyso-Gb3 is a bioactive mediator that plays a central role in promoting downstream pathogenic mechanisms [65]. However, clinical data suggest that residual α-Gal A activity and substrate burden (Gb3/Lyso-Gb3, particularly Lyso-Gb3) are not linearly or consistently associated with overall clinical severity, the extent of renal involvement, or the rate of progression; moreover, even after disease-specific therapy, a subset of patients may continue to exhibit persistent proteinuria and/or progressive decline in renal function, reflecting ongoing or irreversible injury [65–68]. These observations imply that substrate accumulation is likely an initiating event, but is insufficient to fully account for the phenotypic heterogeneity and long-term renal outcomes in Fabry disease. Multiple downstream pathways-including pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic signaling, oxidative stress and endothelial/microvascular dysfunction, mitochondrial and autophagy-lysosomal imbalance, and missense variant–related endoplasmic reticulum stress/unfolded protein response (UPR)-may interact and amplify one another on the background of substrate burden, collectively driving multi-organ pathology, including renal involvement (see Figure 1).

Accumulation of Gb3/Lyso-Gb3 in Fabry disease triggers a cascade of pathological mechanisms, including pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic responses, oxidative stress, vascular dysfunction, mitochondrial dysfunction, autophagy-lysosomal dysfunction, and endoplasmic reticulum stress. These processes collaboratively contribute to the initiation and progression of renal involvement in Fabry disease.

Inflammatory and fibrotic responses

In vitro and translational studies suggest that Fabry disease-related glycosphingolipids (Gb3 and Lyso-Gb3), particularly the bioactive Lyso-Gb3, activates the intracellular Notch1 signaling pathway, recruiting transcription of nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) and upregulating chemotactic/adhesion factors to promote inflammatory responses [69–71]. Notch1 activation further induces expression of its canonical target hairy and enhancer of split 1 (HES1) and extracellular matrix (ECM)-encoding genes (e.g., fibronectin, type IV collagen). HES1 expression drives podocyte dedifferentiation, resulting in albuminuria, while ECM component overexpression promotes renal fibrosis. Additionally, Gb3 and Lyso-Gb3 induce transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) secretion. As a key mediator of glomerulosclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, TGF-β1 can induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition. It also promotes the expression of ECM and macrophage migration inhibitory factor receptor CD74 and activated fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF-2) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which jointly drive chronic inflammation and fibrosis [72–76]. In addition, TGF-β1 and VEGF can also activate caspase-dependent apoptotic pathways [77].

Oxidative stress

Accumulation of Fabry-related glycosphingolipids (Gb3 and Lyso-Gb3), beyond serving as storage markers, can promote reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and disturb redox homeostasis, thereby acting as a key amplification link between substrate accumulation, endothelial dysfunction, and the progression of inflammation/fibrosis. Studies have shown that multiple tissues in Fabry disease exhibit increased DNA/protein oxidative damage, impaired antioxidant defenses (e.g., abnormalities in glutathione-related systems), and elevated lipid peroxidation products (such as malondialdehyde), accompanied by upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators including IL-6 and TNF-α [65,72,78–84]. At the endothelial level, experimental data suggest that substrate-related oxidative stress can induce endothelial activation, upregulate adhesion molecules, and promote leukocyte adhesion [80]. Moreover, oxidative stress can disrupt eNOS/iNOS signaling, reduce NO bioavailability, and promote eNOS uncoupling, thereby aggravating microvascular dysfunction and establishing a positive feedback loop with vascular injury [65,79,85–87].

Vascular dysfunction

Fabry disease-related glycosphingolipids accumulation stimulates the proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells, resulting in intima-media thickening and vascular wall remodeling [65]. Gb3 also silenced transcription factor activity by inhibiting extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)/activating protein-1 (AP-1) pathway and up-regulating inhibitor-1. It reduces the activity of the intermediate-conductance Ca2+-activated K+ (KCa3.1) channel, which maintains vascular endothelium-dependent relaxation, resulting in vascular dysfunction, thrombosis and atherosclerosis [88,89]. Vascular dysfunction exhibits intimate crosstalk with oxidative stress: substrates induce endothelial adhesion molecule expression and disrupt endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS)/inducible NOS (iNOS) signaling. This reduces NO bioavailability and causes eNOS uncoupling, exacerbating oxidative stress [65,79,85]. In addition, thrombospondin-1 (inhibiting angiogenesis) [90] and von Willebrand factor (promoting platelet adhesion/thrombosis) [91] are also overexpressed in the vascular endothelium of Fabry disease patients, which together mediate vascular lesions.

Mitochondrial dysfunction

Gb3 and Lyso-Gb3 accumulation impairs mitochondrial respiratory chain enzyme activity and compromises oxidative phosphorylation, reducing ATP production and directly contributing to multi-organ dysfunction [4,92–95]. Dysfunctional mitochondria produce reactive oxygen species and deplete ATP, further exacerbating lysosomal impairment. Importantly, renal involvement in Fabry disease shows segmental vulnerability: podocytes and distal tubular/loop of Henle-associated epithelial cells often exhibit more pronounced injury, whereas proximal tubules may appear relatively less affected in some histopathological observations. Emerging evidence suggests that the accumulation of Fabry-related glycosphingolipids (Gb3 and Lyso-Gb3) can further disrupt mitochondrial structure, metabolism, and turnover in renal cells, manifesting as abnormal mitochondrial morphology and dysregulated energy metabolism, which may be particularly relevant in podocytes and distal nephron segments [96–99].

Autophagy-lysosomal dysfunction

In Fabry disease, pathological glycosphingolipid accumulates in the lysosomes of cells and cannot be degraded, disrupting the intracellular homeostasis and negatively inhibiting the autophagy pathway. Autophagy-lysosomal dysfunction also contributes to renal involvement [100,101]: accumulation of autophagic vacuoles, increased autophagosomes, and upregulation of autophagy marker microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3-II (LC3-II) have been observed in both patient renal cells and GLA-knockdown podocytes. Reduced activity of the autophagy-negative regulatory AKT-mTOR pathway triggers excessive autophagy activation, indicating autophagy-lysosomal dysfunction is a pathogenic mechanism distinct from Gb3 accumulation.

Endoplasmic reticulum stress

Missense variants represent the predominant variant type in the GLA gene. As the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) serves as the essential membranous organelle for protein synthesis, folding, and secretion in eukaryotic cells, missense variants generate misfolded proteins that accumulate chronically within the ER lumen. This triggers endoplasmic reticulum stress and activates the unfolded protein response (UPR) [102]. Under physiological conditions, the UPR, which integrates complex signaling networks, counteracts endoplasmic reticulum stress by dynamically modulating ER folding/degradation capacity through three sensors (protein kinase R-like ER kinase, inositol-requiring enzyme 1, and activating transcription factor 6). However, under severe or chronic endoplasmic reticulum stress, the UPR fails to resolve stress and restore ER homeostasis. Unresolved ER stress may promote cytokine production/release through UPR-associated pro-inflammatory signaling pathways (such as IRE1α-TRAF2/JNK and PERK-related NF-κB signaling) and may concomitantly activate pro-apoptotic/cell-death programs, ultimately resulting in irreversible cellular damage [103–108].

Clinical manifestations of renal involvement in Fabry disease

Clinically, renal involvement in Fabry disease is better described as a continuum rather than as three rigid stages. Early manifestations may include glomerular hyperfiltration and/or microalbuminuria, and some patients may have enlarged kidneys. With disease progression, overt proteinuria may develop-typically mild to moderate and usually non-nephrotic range-along with a gradual decline in eGFR. As podocyte injury and damage to other glomerular and microvascular-related cell types accumulate, glomerulosclerosis emerges as a downstream pathological change (which may be focal segmental and/or global) and is often accompanied by tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis; these alterations are closely associated with long-term renal prognosis. Importantly, this ‘classical natural history’ largely reflects untreated classical male hemizygotes, whereas renal involvement in female heterozygotes and late-onset patients is more heterogeneous in age at onset, severity, and rate of progression, underscoring the need for genotype/phenotype-informed stratification and longitudinal follow-up [97]. In Fabry disease, all types of cells in the kidney can be affected, especially glomerular podocytes, which play a key role in the development of proteinuria and are closely related to the progression of renal involvement [109].

Clinically, renal involvement is common in Fabry disease; however, the age at onset, severity, and rate of progression are highly heterogeneous and are influenced by the GLA variant category (classical vs. late-onset), residual α-Gal A activity, and sex. In natural history studies, classically affected hemizygous males tend to develop renal involvement earlier, typically presenting with albuminuria/proteinuria and declining glomerular filtration rate, and may progress to CKD and ultimately ESRD [110–112]. Due to skewed X-chromosome inactivation, heterozygous females exhibit wide phenotypic variability-from asymptomatic presentations to severe manifestations resembling male hemizygotes. Typically demonstrating milder disease than males, some female patients never develop overt proteinuria, maintain relatively stable renal function, and show significantly lower ESRD incidence [113]. Importantly, late-onset Fabry disease can also involve the kidneys: in patients carrying the late-onset N215S variant, typical Fabry-related renal histopathological changes may be detectable even early in the disease course, and CKD may still progress over time during follow-up, underscoring the need for risk stratification and longitudinal monitoring of renal involvement in late-onset phenotypes [114].

Urinary abnormalities

Albuminuria is the most common early clinical manifestations of renal involvement in Fabry disease, and is also independent risk factors for renal progression [115,116]. Previous data from the Fabry Outcome Survey demonstrated proteinuria prevalence of 44%–54% in males and 33%–41% in females with renal involvement [117]. Analysis of 1,262 adult Fabry disease patients in the international Fabry Registry further revealed that among CKD Stage 1 patients, approximately 43% of males and 26% of females developed proteinuria (>300 mg/day), with prevalence increasing with advancing renal impairment [15]. Our research group previously reported that classical type male patients may develop proteinuria around age 20, with over 50% exhibiting proteinuria of varying severity by age 25 [16].Because albuminuria often reflects established glomerular barrier injury, including podocyte injury, it provides a more precise indicator of renal involvement severity. Clinically, nephrotic-range proteinuria (>3.5 g/day) is uncommon in Fabry disease. Large cross-sectional data from the Fabry Registry showed that nephrotic-range proteinuria occurred only in a small minority of patients with Fabry disease (11/306 [3.6%] in females and 22/300 [7.3%] in males) [15]. When nephrotic syndrome or an abrupt onset of heavy proteinuria is present, a coexisting glomerular disease should be strongly suspected, and kidney biopsy should be considered to clarify the underlying pathology and to rule out other potential renal diseases unrelated to Fabry disease.

In addition to proteinuria, podocyte shedding in urine (podocytouria) is also an important manifestation. Detection of shed podocytes-via indirect immunofluorescence targeting specific podocyte biomarkers (e.g., WT-1, synaptopodin, nephrin) in urinary sediment-serves as a sensitive biomarker for early renal involvement in Fabry disease [118]. Additionally, the urine of Fabry disease patients may contain ovoid fat bodies (renal tubular epithelial cells containing lipid inclusion bodies) exhibiting lamellated structure that display Maltese cross patterns under polarized light microscopy [119]. Some patients develop impaired urinary concentrating capacity due to tubular dysfunction, manifesting as polyuria and nocturia, which may also be the earliest functional manifestations of renal involvement [9]. In addition, glycosphingolipid deposition in proximal tubular cells may cause features of incomplete Fanconi syndrome, including proximal renal tubular acidosis.

Kidney dysfunction

If patients with renal involvement of Fabry disease, especially classical male patients, do not receive early treatment, their glomerular filtration rate will gradually decrease, progressive renal dysfunction will occur, and may gradually progress to ESRD between ages 30–50, and in severe cases, dialysis or kidney transplantation will be required [13,15,120]. Our cohort study demonstrated that classical males develop elevated serum creatinine (indicating kidney dysfunction) around age 25, with >50% manifesting impairment by age 40. Furthermore, 15.94% (37/232) of Fabry disease patients in our cohort underwent hemodialysis or kidney transplantation, with dialysis initiation at a mean age of 37.10 ± 5.03 years [16]. Notably, there is also a study that reported that ESRD patients with Fabry disease can be treated with renal replacement therapy in the early stage of the disease (about 10–20 years old), suggesting that Fabry disease may also be one of the causes of renal failure in young patients [121]. ESRD further increase cardiovascular or cerebrovascular event risk, and represents a primary contributor to premature mortality [122].

Hypertension

Hypertension is generally uncommon in Fabry disease, and the blood pressure of patients may even be lower than that of the general population, mainly due to the effect of autonomic nervous dysfunction on cardiovascular function caused by the deposition of enzyme metabolic substrates, and the mechanism is still unclear [123]. However, hypertension prevalence increases with advancing renal involvement [9,15]. In the Fabry Outcome Survey study, approximately 57% of male patients with Fabry disease and approximately 47% of female patients developed poorly controlled hypertension, which worsened with increasing CKD stage [124]. A study involving 105 Fabry disease patients reported hypertension in 30% of cases, with over half developing hypertension after the onset of CKD [13].

Renal sinus or parapelvic cyst

Compared with control populations, renal sinus or parapelvic cysts are detected significantly more frequently in patients with Fabry disease [125,126]. In particular, in an Italian multicenter study including 173 patients with Fabry disease, Pisani et al. reported that the prevalence of renal sinus or parapelvic cysts on routine renal ultrasonography was 28.9% in Fabry patients, significantly higher than the 1.1% observed in controls (p < .001); with more targeted ultrasonographic assessment, the detection rate increased further to 43.3% [126]. Accordingly, renal sinus or parapelvic cysts may serve as an imaging clue suggestive of Fabry disease, particularly in patients with unexplained kidney disease accompanied by other Fabry-related manifestations or a suspicious family history. Their pathogenesis, however, remains incompletely understood.

Renal biopsy pathological findings

Gb3 deposition in the glomeruli can occur as early as fetal development in Fabry disease.[127,128]. Characteristic pathological changes may be observed via renal biopsy in pediatric/adolescent patients before clinical or laboratory manifestations of renal involvement emerge [127,129,130].Gb3 deposition in the glomeruli can occur as early as fetal development in Fabry disease.

Light microscopy reveals diffuse vacuolization of glomerular podocytes and distal tubular epithelial cells (it should be noted that this ‘vacuolization’ is largely attributable to dissolution of lipid/glycosphingolipid deposits during routine paraffin embedding and staining procedures). Deposits in mesangial cells, glomerular endothelial/parietal cells, proximal tubules, and peritubular capillary/arteriolar endothelium are often sparse and may escape detection by routine light microscopy [8,13]. Renal arteries and arterioles exhibit Gb3 deposition within smooth muscle cells and degenerative changes indicating smooth muscle cell death, which can subsequently contribute to progressive glomerulosclerosis (focal segmental and/or global), accompanied by capillary wall thickening, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and arterio/arteriolosclerosis, ultimately leading to ischemic and degenerative renal changes [131]. Importantly, the tubulointerstitial compartment constitutes the major volume of the kidney. Although tubulointerstitial involvement may be clinically underestimated because early manifestations can be subtle (e.g., mild impairment of urinary concentrating ability), histological evidence indicates that tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis may already be present early in the disease course. With progression, these lesions often parallel sustained declines in renal function and are closely associated with renal fibrosis and kidney disease progression. Moreover, several studies suggest that expansion of interstitial fibrosis is a key indicator of renal progression and may even be more informative than assessment of glomerular injury alone. Therefore, tubulointerstitial damage should be considered an important prognostically relevant component when evaluating renal involvement in Fabry disease [97,98].

Electron microscopy identifies pathognomonic osmiophilic myelin-like inclusions (‘myeloid’ or ‘zebra’ bodies) in all renal cell types, most prominently podocytes, with a diameter of about 1–3 µm, mainly due to the accumulation and gradual enlargement of the enzymatic metabolic substrate in the cellular secondary lysosome. Among them, the myeloid bodies show concentric circles (onion skin like) structure with alternating light and dark, while the zebra bodies show the appearance of layered stripes (zebra stripes), which is the characteristic pathological sign of Fabry disease and highly suggests the diagnosis of this disease [132]. However, it is still necessary to pay attention to the differential diagnosis of drug-induced phospholipid storage diseases, which may also have myelin inclusions, and other lysosomal storage diseases related to lipid deposition (see the differential diagnosis section for details) [133].

To improve comparability across centers, among patients, and over longitudinal follow-up, the International Study Group of Fabry Nephropathy (ISGFN), led in part by Fogo and colleagues, proposed a consensus renal histology scoring/recording framework for Fabry disease [134]. This framework emphasizes that, in addition to conventional descriptive light and electron microscopic findings, Fabry-specific Gb3 storage lesions-particularly podocyte involvement, which is more sensitively detected using semithin sections and/or electron microscopy-should be recorded in a semi-quantitative manner. It also recommends systematic documentation of chronic/irreversible lesions closely related to disease progression, including focal segmental and/or global glomerulosclerosis, mesangial expansion, tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis, and vascular wall thickening/sclerosis. This framework complements descriptive LM/EM assessment and facilitates longitudinal comparisons of treatment response and disease course. In addition to semi-quantitative scoring, recent quantitative morphometric studies have further highlighted podocytes as a key target cell type in Fabry nephropathy. Using unbiased stereology, Najafian et al. quantified podocyte Gb3 inclusion burden (e.g., inclusion volume fraction/volume density) and assessed ultrastructural indices such as foot process alterations, suggesting that podocyte substrate storage and injury correlate with clinical phenotypes such as proteinuria, thereby supporting a pathological continuum of ‘podocyte injury-proteinuria-progression’ [127]. Tøndel et al. reported in pediatric/adolescent cohorts that characteristic podocyte involvement and ultrastructural changes may be present on kidney biopsy even when albuminuria is minimal or not yet apparent; moreover, foot process effacement/podocyte injury can precede overt proteinuria, indicating that ultrastructural and quantitative metrics may enable earlier detection and risk stratification of renal involvement [128,130].

Although the diagnosis of Fabry disease is primarily established by enzymatic testing and genetic analysis, kidney biopsy remains valuable for confirming renal involvement, assessing disease burden and chronicity, and identifying concomitant kidney diseases. In untreated patients, biopsy may be considered to: (1) confirm renal involvement when the clinical presentation is atypical or the available evidence is insufficient; (2) provide histopathologic support for assessing the pathogenicity of a GLA variant of uncertain significance (VUS); and (3) evaluate potential coexisting glomerular disease in patients with unexplained CKD and/or proteinuria. In treated patients, if renal function continues to decline and/or proteinuria progresses despite disease-specific therapy, kidney biopsy may be considered to evaluate ongoing Fabry-related renal injury, exclude concomitant renal disease, and assess possible suboptimal therapeutic response, including evaluation of lesion activity, thereby informing subsequent treatment optimization and renal protective strategies [135].

Diagnosis of renal involvement in Fabry disease

Early renal involvement in Fabry disease often lacks significant clinical manifestations. Therefore, regardless of whether there are renal symptoms, patients with Fabry disease should be diagnosed and treated early to delay kidney dysfunction and avoid progression to ESRD. However, in patients who have developed significant proteinuria or a significantly reduced glomerular filtration rate (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73m²), renal function may continue to deteriorate even with specific treatment [3,136]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for biomarkers that can early identify and predict the occurrence and development of renal involvement in Fabry disease. Currently, neither pediatric nor adult Fabry disease has highly sensitive, early-stage, noninvasive renal biomarkers. In addition, its renal manifestations (such as proteinuria) are similar to other glomerular diseases and lack specificity, and Fabry disease may coexist with other renal diseases (such as IgA nephropathy, minimal change nephropathy, etc.), so early diagnosis of renal involvement in Fabry disease is still challenging [137].

At present, the diagnosis of renal involvement in Fabry disease needs to meet two conditions: confirmed Fabry disease (via genetic/enzymatic testing) and the presence of evidence of renal involvement (supported by specific biomarkers and pathological features). Common indicators of renal involvement, including elevated serum creatinine and cystatin C levels, increased monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) levels, reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and proteinuria [138–140], as well as imaging findings such as renal cysts [125], lack disease specificity. It should also be noted that the levels of evidence supporting currently available diagnostic and assessment markers are not uniform. Among them, Lyso-Gb3 and characteristic renal biopsy findings are supported by relatively stronger clinical evidence, whereas several urinary and omics-based markers remain largely exploratory and require validation in larger, prospective studies. Differential diagnosis of renal involvement in Fabry disease

Clinically, renal involvement in Fabry disease needs to be differentiated from various causes of renal disease, mainly based on pathological characteristics (especially the morphology of inclusion bodies under electron microscopy) and clinical background. The key points include the following three categories.

Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)

During the course of Fabry nephropathy, secondary FSGS-like segmental sclerotic lesions may occur. These lesions are largely attributable to reparative processes following injury to podocytes and other glomerular cells, and therefore are not pathologically equivalent to primary FSGS. Clinically, when ‘FSGS-like lesions with proteinuria’ constitute the predominant presentation-particularly in the presence of unexplained decline in renal function or clues suggestive of a lysosomal storage disorder-Fabry disease should be considered and a renal biopsy performed to establish the diagnosis. From a pathological perspective, in addition to routine light microscopy and immunofluorescence, electron microscopy typically demonstrates characteristic lamellated (‘zebra body’/myelin-like) inclusions within podocytes and other renal cells in Fabry disease, whereas such diagnostic inclusions are generally absent in isolated primary FSGS [8,153,154]. Moreover, in suspected Fabry nephropathy, toluidine blue staining of resin-embedded semithin sections can serve as a valuable adjunct to more clearly demonstrate intracellular storage-related changes (Heptinstall’s Pathology of the Kidney, 7th ed.).

Drug-induced phospholipidosis

Numerous cationic amphiphilic drugs (such as antimalarial drugs: chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine; antiarrhythmic drugs: amiodarone; antidepressants: chlorphentamide, imipramine, sertraline and clomipramine; antibiotics: gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin, etc.) may induce phospholipid accumulation. These drugs form non-degradable drug-lipid complexes via electrostatic/hydrophobic interactions, accumulating as lamellated or crystalline acid phosphatase-positive inclusions within lysosomes. This blocks phospholipid catabolism, causing lysosomal overload and iatrogenic lipid storage. Consequently, myelin-like inclusions similar to Fabry disease can appear in various types of renal cells [133,155–158]. For example, chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine-induced phospholipidosis shows lamellar inclusions (some curvilinear) in podocytes and other renal cells, closely mimicking Fabry disease ultrastructural findings (Table 3) [159,160].

Other lysosomal storage disorders with lipid accumulation

Several other lipid storage disorders may also manifest with characteristic inclusions in renal and other tissue cells. The ultrastructural morphology and main cellular distribution of these inclusions are the key points for identification, as shown in Table 3 [11,157,159–179]. For example, fingerprinted, curvilinear, or granular osmiophilic deposits can be seen in Batten’s disease [179], irregularly shaped cytoplasmic vacuoles containing comma-shaped curvilinear Farber or banana-shaped bodies can be seen in Farber’s disease [170], and crumpled tissue paper-like lipid deposits can be seen in Gaucher’s disease [176]. These disorders exhibit structurally distinct inclusion bodies compared to Fabry disease. In addition, although myelin-like inclusions with structural similarities to Fabry disease can also appear in Niemann-Pick disease, they are generally not significantly deposited in mesangial and arterial cells. There are few clinical storage diseases that present extensive and prominent deposition of myelin-like inclusions in all cell types of the kidney as seen in Fabry disease. Although ultrastructural features provide crucial diagnostic clues, morphology and cellular distribution alone cannot definitively differentiate Fabry disease from other lipidoses. Final diagnosis requires integration of characteristic clinical manifestations (such as acroparesthesia, angiokeratomas and corneal vortex opacity), α-Gal A enzyme activity test and GLA gene variant analysis.

Treatment of renal involvement in Fabry disease

Clinically, the long-term goal of the treatment of renal involvement in Fabry disease is to delay the progressive decline of renal function and minimize irreversible renal damage. Given the marked heterogeneity across GLA variant categories (classical versus late-onset), clinical phenotypes, and sex, there is currently no universally accepted official international guideline that defines a single ‘when-to-start’ threshold for disease-specific therapy; treatment decisions are therefore more commonly based on expert consensus and individualized assessment [181–183]. In general, treatment initiation may be triggered either by objective evidence of organ involvement or progression (e.g., persistent proteinuria, a declining eGFR trajectory, or biopsy/imaging findings consistent with Fabry nephropathy) or by an early-intervention window suggested by Fabry-related symptoms and high-risk genotypes-particularly in children and adolescents, in whom neuropathic pain may precede overt abnormalities in renal indices. Patients with classical male hemizygosity and those carrying high-risk variants indicative of severe deficiency (e.g., truncating mutations) typically warrant earlier evaluation for treatment initiation. In addition, renal supportive treatment, such as control of proteinuria and daily management of CKD, is also needed throughout the treatment process. For patients progressing to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), dialysis or kidney transplantation is required to sustain renal function (summarized in Table 4).

Disease-specific treatments

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT)

ERT is currently considered to be the most effective measure to treat Fabry disease, control disease progression and delay organ dysfunction. Exogenous intravenous infusion of recombinant human α-Gal A can supplement the reduced activity or complete deficiency of α-Gal A in patients and reduce the accumulation of enzyme metabolic substrates in multiple organs and tissues of the whole body. Currently, three recombinant human α-Gal A preparations have been approved for clinical use: agalsidase alfa (Replagal®, 0.2 mg/kg every other week; available in Europe and other regions but not approved in the United States), agalsidase beta (Fabrazyme®, 1.0 mg/kg every other week), and pegunigalsidase alfa (PRX-102/Elfabrio®).

With respect to agalsidase alfa and agalsidase beta, At present, clinical studies have reported that initiation of agalsidase infusion therapy in patients with Fabry nephropathy can effectively stabilize renal function and prevent progressive deterioration in Fabry nephropathy [109,116,188–190]. Long-term follow-up evidence suggests that both ERT formulations may provide clinical benefits over extended periods; however, treatment effects are closely related to the timing of initiation and the extent of baseline organ damage. For agalsidase alfa, long-term cohort/registry analyses from the Fabry Outcome Survey (FOS) indicate a slower annual decline in eGFR or relative renal stability over 5–10 years, particularly in patients with preserved baseline kidney function [191,192]. For agalsidase beta, registry-based long-term analyses suggest delayed progression of clinically relevant outcomes, including renal and cardio-/cerebrovascular events, and underscore proteinuria level and baseline renal function as important determinants of long-term renal prognosis [186,193]. Although ERT is effective, the clinical benefit is greatest when therapy is initiated early, before irreversible organ injury occurs. In contrast, delayed initiation-after extensive and irreversible pathological changes have developed in the kidney and other target organs-may result in only modest improvements in organ function and symptoms [186,194]. Overall, the evidence supporting agalsidase alfa and agalsidase beta in Fabry-related renal involvement is relatively robust, comprising both randomized clinical trials and long-term registry and cohort studies.

In addition, anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) should be considered during ERT. A subset of patients-particularly males with the classic phenotype-develop anti-agalsidase IgG after agalsidase alfa or beta, and cross-reactivity between the two formulations is common. ADAs have been linked to infusion-associated reactions and may attenuate biochemical response, including less reduction in Gb3/Lyso-Gb3, likely by reducing effective enzyme activity [195,196]. Although data on a direct impact of ADAs on long-term renal outcomes are mixed, ADA testing and longitudinal monitoring of Lyso-Gb3, proteinuria, and renal function may be informative in patients with suboptimal response or prominent infusion reactions [195,197]. Given that suboptimal ERT response in Fabry disease may result from multiple factors-including ADA formation, insufficient drug exposure, poor adherence, irreversible organ damage, or coexisting kidney disease-clinical assessment should integrate ADA status, longitudinal Lyso-Gb3 trends, proteinuria, and changes in eGFR. To enhance the clinical applicability of the ERT section, the key evaluation points, major follow-up indicators, and subsequent management pathway for suspected suboptimal ERT response are summarized in Table S1 of the supplementary material.

In addition to agalsidase α/β, another new ERT drug has also been used in clinical practice. Pegunigalsidase-α (PRX-102/Elfabrio) is a recombinant α-Gal A expressed in plant cell culture and chemically modified with polyethylene glycol (PEG) to form a structurally stable recombinant α-Gal A. PEG attachment can mask the effect of this molecule on the immune system. It prolongs the plasma half-life of the drug and reduces the immunogenicity, thereby enhancing the efficacy of the drug [198]. This agent was authorized in the European Union and approved in the United States in May 2023 for the treatment of adults with Fabry disease; however, long-term real-world experience remains limited. Clinical trials demonstrate PRX-102 significantly preserves renal function with efficacy comparable to conventional agalsidase α/β [199,200], and its potential therapeutic effect and prospect are highly anticipated. Nevertheless, additional long-term follow-up and real-world studies are needed to further clarify its benefits and safety across patient subgroups with different baseline risk profiles. At present, the evidence supporting PRX-102 is derived mainly from early-phase clinical studies and head-to-head randomized trials, whereas long-term real-world data on renal outcomes remain relatively limited. Further long-term follow-up and real-world studies are still needed to better define its efficacy and safety across patients with different baseline risk profiles.

Enzyme-enhanced therapy

Although ERT has become the first choice for the specific treatment of Fabry disease, its long-term application is prone to produce anti-agalsidase antibodies and the treatment cost is high. Meanwhile, the intravenous infusion cannot fully reach all target organs, which limits its clinical application [190,201]. In addition to ERT, another oral small-molecule agent, the pharmacological chaperone 1-deoxy-galactonojirimycin (DGJ or migalastat), has emerged as an additional specific treatment option for Fabry disease. By reversibly binding to the active site of α-Gal A enzyme, DGJ can promote the proper folding of the protein with a specific variant and improve the stability of the protein structure, enable it to restore the appropriate spatial conformation, enhance the enzyme activity, and enable the enzyme protein to successfully escape from the ER, further mature and be transported to the lysosome to play the catalytic function [202–207]. Current studies have confirmed that DGJ can effectively increase α-Gal A enzyme activity and reduce the accumulation of enzyme metabolic substrates, which has a good effect on improving multiple organ involvement in patients with Fabry disease, and can eliminate the lifelong management burden of intravenous infusion therapy and the immunogenicity related to enzyme replacement therapy. In addition, DGJ can cross the blood-brain barrier, which also plays an important role in improving nervous system involvement [205,208–210]. In addition, migalastat has been reported to have the potential to cross the blood-brain barrier, and biochemical improvements have been observed in brain tissue in some studies [205,208–210]. However, based on tissue-distribution data and PBPK modeling, its exposure in brain tissue may be relatively limited (with predicted brain concentrations in most individuals unlikely to consistently reach pharmacologically meaningful binding levels). Therefore, convincing evidence for a clinically meaningful benefit on CNS involvement remains lacking. Available longitudinal neuroimaging follow-up generally suggests overall stability of cerebral small-vessel lesion burden, and larger, longer-term studies are still needed for confirmation [211–213].

The prerequisite for clinical use of the drug is that the patient has an suitable GLA gene variant and because DGJ is mainly metabolized by the kidney, kidney dysfunction will lead to accumulation of DGJ metabolites in the body, which will inhibit endogenous α-Gal A enzyme activity. Therefore, DGJ is not recommended for Fabry disease patients with eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 [214]. At present, it is not clear the internal relationship between GLA gene variant and the treatment response of migalastat, and the specific application of the drug in Fabry disease patients with GLA gene variant sites. Our previous study found that DGJ may have better intervention effects on variant sites with slight changes in protein structure and function than those with severe changes, which is expected to be applied to the selection of personalized treatment strategies for patients with Fabry disease [207].

Current evidence supporting migalastat is derived mainly from clinical trials, extension studies, and some real-world studies in patients with amenable variants; therefore, its efficacy should be interpreted in the context of genotype eligibility.

Substrate reduction therapy (SRT)

SRT utilizes glucosylceramide synthase inhibitors to suppress glycosphingolipid biosynthesis, reducing pathological substrate accumulation and ameliorating multi-organ manifestations. The main oral glucosylceramide synthase inhibitors currently under clinical investigation/evaluation in Fabry disease include Lucerastat and Venglustat. Their mechanism of action is independent of ‘migalastat amenability’; however, existing studies have largely focused on classical phenotypes (particularly males), and additional evidence is needed to define clinical benefits across different phenotypes and sexes. Available studies indicate that Lucerastat and Venglustat can reduce Fabry-related substrate burden with an overall favorable safety profile [215–218].

Moreover, a historical control/propensity score-matched analysis by Germain DP and colleagues, based on data from classical male patients, suggested that Venglustat was associated with a greater reduction in plasma Gb3 compared with placebo at 6 months and compared with agalsidase beta at 24–36 months, with a sustained downward trend over 3 years. Nevertheless, these findings primarily reflect improvements in biomarkers, and the impact on hard renal endpoints and overall clinical outcomes still requires confirmation in further prospective studies [219]. At present, the evidence for substrate reduction therapy is focused mainly on improvements in biomarkers, whereas data on hard renal endpoints and long-term clinical benefit remain limited; further prospective studies are needed to confirm its efficacy.

Gene therapy

Gene therapy corrects enzymatic deficiencies by delivering functional GLA genes via viral/non-viral vectors. At present, a variety of viral vectors can be used for the treatment of Fabry disease, including retrovirus, lentivirus, adenovirus and adeno-associated virus (AAV) [220,221]. In addition, hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell-mediated gene therapy, mRNA therapy and gene editing therapy are becoming more and more mature, whichmay provide a potentially durable disease-modifying therapeutic strategy in the future [180,222,223]. Gene therapy is still at an experimental stage. Current evidence is derived mainly from early clinical development and preliminary studies, suggesting potential benefits in improving enzyme activity and reducing substrate burden; however, its long-term efficacy and safety still require confirmation through ongoing follow-up.

Symptomatic and supportive treatments

Symptomatic and supportive treatments of Fabry nephropathy focuses on: proteinuria control (key target), blood pressure/lipid/sodium regulation, avoidance of nephrotoxic agents (notably non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs; substitute analgesics like carbamazepine or gabapentin) and renal replacement therapy (dialysis/ kidney transplantation) for ESRD. Given ERT’s limited efficacy in reducing proteinuria, a critical risk factor, ACEI/ARB drugs are recommended (barring contraindications) to reduce proteinuria and slow renal decline. It should be noted that the main purpose of ACEI/ARB is to reduce proteinuria (not all patients with hypertension) [116,190,224]. Given the antiproteinuric and cardiorenal protective effects of SGLT2 inhibitors and finerenone in the broader CKD population, both agents may be considered in selected cases as part of an individualized renoprotective strategy for Fabry disease [225,226]. In a recent multicenter prospective study, the addition of dapagliflozin 10 mg/day for 12 months to stable ERT or migalastat plus RAS inhibition in Fabry patients with albuminuric CKD significantly reduced 24-h proteinuria (p < .001), while eGFR remained stable (63.50 [SD 25.13] vs 64.31 [SD 26.40] mL/min/1.73m², p = .65), suggesting that SGLT2 inhibitors may represent a useful adjunctive renoprotective option [227]. By contrast, direct clinical evidence for finerenone in Fabry disease remains limited, and at present it is better regarded as an individualized adjunctive option extrapolated from the broader CKD literature. Overall, both SGLT2 inhibitors and finerenone may be considered on top of disease-specific therapy and RAS blockade, but their long-term efficacy in Fabry disease still requires further validation. In addition, physical activity and structured exercise interventions may be considered as part of supportive management in Fabry disease. A recent review highlighted that exercise intolerance is common and often underrecognized in Fabry disease, with a substantial impact on quality of life, while limited pilot data suggest that regular exercise training may improve fatigue, muscle strength, and endurance. Therefore, after individualized cardiovascular and exercise risk assessment, regular physical activity together with a personalized, progressively advanced exercise program implemented under medical supervision may be considered an adjunct to the comprehensive management of Fabry disease; however, its long-term renal benefits and safety still require further validation in future studies [228].

Dialysis

Dialysis can be considered for Fabry disease patients with ESRD, both hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis can be used, and there is no guideline to support which dialysis method is better. Studies have reported that patients with Fabry disease have worse outcomes than other ESRD patients, and their survival rate is also lower than that of non-Fabry disease patients. A United States dialysis study showed a 3-year survival rate of 63% in dialysis patients with Fabry disease compared with 74% in a non-diabetic cohort; In another case-control study, Fabry disease dialysis patients had significantly better survival than diabetic dialysis patients (53%). The difference in survival between Fabry disease and non-diabetic dialysis patients may be attributed to cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events in Fabry disease dialysis patients. Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular complications and cachexia are also the main causes of death in dialysis patients with Fabry disease [26,27,229].

Although dialysis can partially correct renal dysfunction in patients with Fabry disease, the persistence of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular involvement associated with Fabry disease may lead to a higher mortality rate in patients with Fabry disease on dialysis [230]. This also suggests the necessity of ERT for dialysis patients with Fabry disease, despite the fact that their renal function has been irreversibly damaged. At present, a number of studies have confirmed that ERT for Fabry disease patients treated with dialysis is also beneficial for further stabilizing renal function and improving the involvement of extra-renal organs such as cardiovascular and cerebrovascular [231–233].

Agalsidase infusion can be administered during routine dialysis sessions for Fabry disease patients on dialysis without significant loss of the enzyme during the procedure or compromising its biological activity and pharmacokinetics [234,235]. However, some studies have also suggested that the infusion of agalsidase during dialysis may theoretically cause some of the enzyme to adsorb to the filter, so it is recommended that the infusion of agalsidase be performed about 15 min after the start of dialysis. By this time, the surface of the filter membrane will be covered by plasma proteins such as fibrinogen or albumin, thereby reducing the possibility of enzyme adsorption to the filter membrane and the pipeline system [234,236]. For ESRD patients with Fabry disease who must receive dialysis, infusion of agalsidase during dialysis can save patients’ treatment time, and the infusion during dialysis does not compromise the safety or tolerability of the therapy [230]. Meanwhile, it can effectively improve the involvement of other organs except kidney in dialysis patients [234,237].

Kidney transplantation

Compared to dialysis, kidney transplantation more effectively ameliorates clinical symptoms in Fabry disease patients with ESRD and provides functional α-Gal A via the graft, enhancing the degradation capacity for enzyme substrates. Consequently, kidney transplantation combined with ERT is currently regarded as the optimal therapeutic strategy for Fabry disease patients progressing to ESRD [238–240].

Historically, kidney transplantation in Fabry-associated ESRD was controversial due to complications such as infection and sepsis, alongside high graft failure rates [241–243]. Since the 1980s, with advances in immunosuppression and medical management, both graft and patient outcomes have significantly improved. Multiple studies demonstrate that graft survival rates (both short- and long-term) in Fabry ESRD transplant recipients are comparable to those of ESRD patients with other etiologies. For instance, an ERA-EDTA registry analysis of 33 patients over 3 years showed similar graft survival (72% vs 69%) and patient survival (84% vs 87%, compared to non-Fabry patients <55 years) [26]. A USRDS study of 93 patients reported equivalent 1-year (91%) and 5-year (76%) graft survival, and 5-year patient survival (83%) compared to controls [244]. Long-term studies (e.g., Inderbitzin et al.: 66% 10-year graft survival, 76% patient survival in 10 patients [245]; Ersözlü et al.: 22% 25-year graft survival, 25% patient survival in 17 patients [246] further confirm durable transplant outcomes in Fabry patients. Mirroring outcomes in other ESRD populations, post-transplant survival surpasses that of patients remaining on dialysis [26,244–246]. Therefore, kidney transplantation represents a preferred therapeutic option for Fabry patients with ESRD. However, due to frequent late-stage involvement of critical organs like the heart in Fabry disease, long-term patient survival post-transplant may remain lower than in non-Fabry transplant recipients. This disparity is primarily attributable to Fabry disease’s inherent multisystem complications rather than the effect of transplantation. Additionally, a large study of 424 Fabry kidney transplant recipients (median follow-up 3–11.5 years) reported a Fabry recurrence rate of 11.1% in grafts, with all-cause graft failure (including death with a functioning graft and graft loss), death-censored graft failure, and rejection rates of 32.5%, 14.5%, and 20.2% respectively [247].

Like dialysis, kidney transplantation is not a complete replacement for ERT because there is no cross-correction for enzyme defects between cells in the body’s organs. In renal transplant recipients with Fabry disease, multi-organ involvement other than the kidney still exists and progresses. Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular complications are also the main reasons for high morbidity and mortality in renal transplant recipients. Most renal transplant recipients still need ERT to prevent the progression of other organs involved. Current evidence from multiple studies confirms that transplant recipients receiving ERT demonstrate significantly better improvement in multiorgan (cardiac, cerebral, renal) function compared to those not receiving ERT. Benefits include amelioration of declining creatinine clearance and progression of proteinuria, attenuation of left ventricular hypertrophy, and reduction in cerebrovascular events [233,248,249] (Table 4).

Conclusion

Renal involvement is a common clinical manifestation in patients with classical and late-onset Fabry disease. Clinically, over half of male and some female patients may have renal involvement, which can gradually progress to ESRD. Its pathological basis can start from the deposition of enzyme metabolic substrates in the fetal period, accompanied by secondary inflammatory and fibrotic cascades. Diagnosis and assessment rely on characteristic lamellated inclusions observed in renal biopsy and biomarkers such as lyso-Gb3, necessitating clinical differentiation from drug-induced phospholipidoses and other lipid storage-related lysosomal storage disorders.

ERT is a specific treatment for Fabry disease including renal involvement. Early initiation of ERT can effectively stabilize renal function and delay systemic multi-organ damage. Additionally, migalastat is an approved disease-specific option for patients with amenable GLA variants, whereas substrate reduction therapy and gene therapy remain investigational or under clinical evaluation.Fabry disease patients with renal involvement should be treated with specific treatment, combined with symptomatic and supportive treatment, such as reducing urinary protein excretion. Dialysis or kidney transplantation should be performed if necessary. Dialysis can effectively maintain renal function in Fabry disease ESRD patients, and agalsidase can be given at the same time during dialysis. Kidney transplantation provides graft and patient survival similar to other renal disease patients, and can be used as the preferred treatment for ESRD patients with Fabry disease. Because Fabry disease is a systemic lysosomal enzyme deficiency, renal replacement therapy (kidney transplantation or dialysis) can only replace renal excretory function and cannot provide sufficient α-Gal A activity to cells in other organs-such as the heart, vasculature, and nervous system-to correct intracellular substrate accumulation. There is no dependable ‘cross-correction’ between organs to compensate for the generalized cellular enzyme defect; therefore, renal replacement therapy cannot substitute for disease-specific therapy. In patients who have progressed to ESRD, continuation and/or initiation of ERT (or other disease-specific therapies) should still be considered after comprehensive evaluation to slow the progression of extra-renal involvement.
