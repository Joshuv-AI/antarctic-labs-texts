Graphene aerogel for supercapacitors: influence of hydrothermal reduction parameters on electrochemical performance
Authors: Khaled Abdou Ahmed Abdou Elsehsah, Zulkarnain Ahmad Noorden, Norhafezaidi Mat Saman, Noor Azlinda Ahmad, Mohd Faizal Hasan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the present work, a hydrothermal reduction method is used to further enhance and optimize the pristine graphene aerogels (GAs) for supercapacitors. Three key variables (GO concentration, hydrothermal reduction temperature, and time) were optimized through the application of Taguchi design of experiments to the synthesis process. A total of nine experimental runs were conducted and repeated three times to confirm reproducibility. Electrochemical performances of the electrodes were evaluated by cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic discharge–charge (GCD), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The optimized sample (5 mg/mL GO at 180 °C for 16 h) was further analyzed using morphological studies, including FIB-FESEM, Raman, XPS, and BET. The as-obtained 3D reduced graphene oxide network exhibited a maximum specific capacitance of 182.33 F/g at the current density of 0.2 A/g, corresponding to an energy density of 6.33 Wh/kg at a power density of 108.5 W/kg in a 6 M KOH electrolyte. This study systematically investigates the influence of hydrothermal reduction parameters on the energy density of graphene aerogel-based supercapacitors and pinpoints the best synthesis conditions. This also highlights the potential of pure GA as an electrode material, indicating opportunities for further enhancement through ongoing research and development.

Introduction

As portable electronics, electric vehicles (EVs), and renewable energy sources become ubiquitous, the need for effective energy storage systems only increases. To address this incredible energy transition, the need for researchers and engineers to develop next-generation energy storage devices with robust performance, safety, and scalability to meet the needs of contemporary applications has never been more critical than now. Although conventional batteries achieve high energy density, they do not work well at high charge/discharge rates because of the low power densities and short cycle life. On the other hand, supercapacitors (SCs) are becoming more and more popular because of their special high-power density, rapid charge/discharge, and long cyclability properties1. These characteristics make SCs an attractive replacement or supplement to batteries for many applications that require energy.

An electrochemical capacitor, more commonly known as a supercapacitor, is unique in charge storage compared to batteries. Instead of faradaic redox reactions (like seen in pseudocapacitors or hybrid capacitors), energy storage in SCs mainly occurs by means of electrostatic interactions between charged species at the interface between an electrode and an electrolyte. This property enables SCs to be charged and discharged within seconds, providing over one million stable cycles without noticeable capacity degradation. Additionally, their inherent non-flammability of the aqueous electrolytes, wide range of operating temperatures, and negligible environmental impact enhance their attractiveness for use in developing applications like hybrid vehicles and grid storage systems2,3.

There are three main groups of SCs according to their charge storage mechanism, namely, Electric Double-Layer Capacitors (EDLCs), surface pseudo capacitance, and hybrid capacitors4,5. Energy is stored in EDLCs by physical adsorption of ions at the electrode surface, leading to high reversibility and an immense cycle life. In contrast, pseudocapacitors based on fast and reversible redox reactions have higher energy density but commonly have low cycling stability. Hybrid capacitors target the middle ground between EDLCs and batteries by combining both faradaic and non-faradaic charge storage mechanisms4,6. Of these, EDLCs are the most promising for applications where power needs to be delivered quickly and maintained over long periods. Charge storage in electrostatics involves the accumulation of ions on the microscale distance at the interface of the electrode–electrolyte to create an electric double layer, allowing for fast charge and discharge while avoiding faradaic reactions or chemical changes in the electrode material. This pushes the devices to such high levels that degradation and, thereby, efficiency losses over many charge–discharge cycles are at a minimal level4,5.

Though electrode and electrolyte materials are both essential to SC performance, recent development progress indicates the superior influence of electrode material design on enhanced energy density, power delivery, and cycling stability. Research on supercapacitors has concentrated on the development of high-surface-area, highly conductive, and structurally stable electrode materials7,8. Over the past few years, the design of electrodes has shifted towards the abundance of renewable, environmentally sustainable materials, especially carbon-based materials. Common carbon materials for the EDLC electrodes are activated carbon (AC), Carbon nanotubes (CNTs), Carbon ions, and Graphene9–12. The structure and electronic properties of these materials are different for each of them. Activated carbon is a typical low-cost adsorbent with a high surface area but poor conductivity and uneven pore distribution. The CNTs are better in conductivity and mechanical strength, whereas the graphene, which is two-dimensional with sp2-hybridized carbon atoms, has excellent electrical conductivity, thermal stability, and the highest theoretical surface area of about 2630 m2/g9. Graphene is one of the most promising carbon-based materials with high charge transport properties and tunable surface chemistry, which are essential features for superior EDLC.

Although the intrinsic properties of graphene are quite impressive, versatile, and interesting, their real implementation in SCs is hindered by the restacking of pristine 2D graphene sheets, which stems from strong van der Waals forces, resulting in a low effective surface area and reduced ion accessibility as well13,14. In order to avoid these constraints, researchers have explored three-dimensional (3D) graphene-based architectures, especially graphene aerogels (GAs). There are many ways that can be used to synthesize graphene aerogel, including hydrothermal reduction and freezing, and each of them has its own benefits, such as an improved hierarchical porous structure15,16. Graphene aerogels have many advantages over isolating individual graphene sheets. The open and 3D porous framework ensures real-time contact with electrolyte, increases the ion diffusion pathway, and maintains mechanical integrity during charge/discharge processes. The continuous graphene network also facilitates rapid electron transport and maintains a large part of the area that is electrochemically active14. GAs possess significant elasticity and compressibility as well, enabling their usage in flexible and wearable electronics13.

Nonetheless, the performance of GAs in EDLCs can be improved by structural and compositional variations17–20. Heteroatom doping has been one of the most efficient strategies investigated. When atoms such as nitrogen, sulfur, or phosphorus are introduced into the graphene lattice through doping, they can alter the orbital arrangements and interact strongly with the graphene surface to change electronic structure and surface chemistry21–23. These modifications can create new active sites, increase surface wettability, and increase conductivity to improve the electrochemical double-layer capacity and rate capability21. Among the heteroatom doping strategies, nitrogen doping, however, has been more effective and achieved better levels of performance. Due to its relatively similar atomic size and, more importantly, a higher electronegativity, nitrogen can be well incorporated into the carbon lattice with low lattice distortion24–26. Different functionalities of nitrogen, including pyridinic, pyrrolic, and graphitic nitrogen, have been demonstrated to improve pseudocapacitive performance, stabilize redox-active sites, and accelerate electron transfer during electrochemical processes26–28. The second benefit of the N-doped status is the increased electron-donating ability of graphene, which gives high conductivity and facilitates electrolyte ion interaction with the electrode surface.

Recent investigations on pure GAs emerge as attractive supercapacitor materials owing to their lightweight, high surface areas, and good conductivity. In one of the works, hierarchically porous graphene aerogel (HP-GA) was synthesized via a template method using MnO229. The aerogel provides a high specific capacitance of 284 F/g at a current density of 1 A/g and an excellent energy density of 10 Wh/kg at a power density of 250 W/kg in aqueous KOH electrolytes. Such superior performance resulted from its hierarchical porous structure and abundant oxygen functional groups that facilitated ion transport and raised wettability, besides the absence of any exogenous dopants. In the same manner, without the aid of a carbohydrate-based polymer, another research group prepared a pure GA and obtained the specific capacitance of approximately 95 F/g at 10 mV/s30.​ Pure GA also showed a good electrochemical performance with the capacity retention of about 85% for 5000 cycles, although this was lower than that of DD aerogels (fructose- or sucrose-assisted GAs), which still have very limited capacitance. That’s additional proof that even unlaced rib twists can result in consistent execution with a number of the fraction cut down from restacking issues, and this was explored in a similar study of the reduction process with vitamin C for graphene aerogel. From the findings of the study, it was shown that the pristine graphene aerogel was reported to exhibit a surface area of around 893.9 m2/g and high-rate performance; it showed specific capacitance of 176 F/g at a current density of about 20 A/g​31. Its capacitance was shown to have retained 99·9% even after 10,000 cycles, revealing an exceptional stability of the aerogel, and though the growth of heteroatom doping has helped further improve empirical performance, this study focuses on pure GA. However, the information on nitrogen and other heteroatom-doped elements is covered here to specify how the field is transforming and the need for improvements and optimization of parameters, which is not standardized across the studies till now.

Experimental investigations that are being undertaken in the study are capable of overcoming these limitations by developing a comparative experiment design, which would explore the performance of GA that is synthesized by hydrothermal reduction followed by Freeze-drying. The process is known to be dependent on several factors, such as duration, temperature, and concentration of GO, and here the study used the Taguchi method to help in optimizing the process, where it systematically changed the synthesis parameters, which helped in finding key components that would ensure improved performance. Based on the double approach of structural and composition tuning, this work aims to clarify the individual as well as the synergistic effects of GA morphology on the performance in supercapacitor application, and the insights available would help in designing more advanced graphene-based electrode materials with improved electrochemical performance for next-generation storage devices.

Experiments

Chemicals and materials

The aerogels were synthesized using different concentrations of GO solution as the precursor. The GO was purchased from Graphenea (Spain). The freeze-drying process was done using a SCANVAC dryer purchased from Chemopharm Group (Malaysia). Hydrothermal reduction was conducted in Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclaves of 50 mL purchased from Evergreen Engineering & Resources (Malaysia). Electrochemical evaluation was performed using a Gamry Interface 1000 as the potentiostat, connected to a computer with software for data acquisition and analysis.

Material synthesis

GAs were synthesized by a simple one-pot hydrothermal reduction followed by freeze drying. Initial concentrations of 2, 5, and 8 mg/mL of aqueous GO suspensions were used. 35 mL of GO solution was subsequently transferred to each 50 mL Teflon-lined autoclave. These sealed autoclaves were then placed in a vacuum oven for hydrothermal treatments at various temperatures and treatment durations, as dictated by an experimental matrix designed using the Taguchi method from Design of Experiments (DoE). The self-assembly of GO sheets into a three-dimensional hydrogel structure is facilitated by a hydrothermal process, which results in partial reduction and cross-linking of the GO nanosheets. This assembly was mediated by π–π stacking and hydrogen bond interactions of functionalities on neighboring sheets, and after this, the hydrogels were taken out of the autoclaves and rinsed with deionized water for 3 days to remove residual ions and byproducts. The neutrality of the water that was used in the process was maintained by controlling the pH to 7.

All hydrogel samples were put into the lyophilizer and freeze-dried under vacuum for 48 h to obtain light and highly porous graphene. The obtained samples were then labeled GA-1 to GA-9, corresponding to the different hydrothermal conditions.

Structure characterization

Four significant structural and morphological characterization techniques were employed (FIB-FESEM, Raman spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, and BET surface area analysis) for material characterization. Raman spectroscopy was used to get valuable information about the molecular structure and chemical components of the optimized sample. FIB-FESEM was used to analyse the microstructure and pore distribution within the optimized sample. High-resolution imaging yielded information about the structure of the aerogel matrix, porosity, and surface texture.

The optimized graphene aerogel sample was characterized by XPS to obtain chemical composition, identification of different elements by the characteristic binding energy of their electrons, and bonding states information, in particular, sp2 and sp3 hybridized carbon structure and oxygen-containing groups. Based on the C1s spectra, the reduction level of graphene oxide to graphene, as well as the restoration of the conjugated carbon network, was evaluated. Collectively, these characterization techniques provided insights into the surface chemistry, elemental composition, and structural evolution of the optimized sample.

BET surface area measurements were conducted using nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms at 77 K, and the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method was used to evaluate the pore size distribution of the optimized sample. These results were useful in assessing the specific surface area and porosity, both of which are important variables affecting electrochemical performance.

Electrochemical characterization and measurements

The electrochemical performance of GA samples was investigated in a two-electrode system. The three-electrode test cell is essential for assessing the electrochemical behaviour of materials. However, a 2-electrode system is preferable for practical applications, as it can replicate the actual performance of an SC and hence reveal the material’s true energy storage capabilities. It is essential to note that this study employed a two-electrode system, with results initially calculated for the whole cell, and for comparison and reporting purposes, the values obtained were converted to specific capacitance per single electrode and then multiplied by four. To fabricate the electrode, the process involved cutting the GAs into small pieces, which were then placed on a 4 cm × 4 cm nickel foam sheet, serving as the current collector. These samples were then pressed for 10 min at 10 MPa to ensure good adhesion of the material to the Ni foam. After this, the sheet was cut into four circular electrodes with a diameter of approximately 2 cm. The absence of conductive additive or binder in the fabrication of these aerogel electrodes would accurately reflect the intrinsic characteristics of the aerogels. Each electrode contained approximately 10 mg of active material. In this research, a cellulose membrane served as the separator, and a 6 M KOH aqueous solution was used as the electrolyte.

CV, GCD, and EIS tests were performed on the Gamry interface 1000. For the CV, the potential window was from 0 to 1 V at various scan rates ranging from 5 to 50 mV/s, while GCD tests were conducted at multiple current densities ranging from 0.2 to 2 A/g to test specific capacitance and rate performance. Results

Taguchi analysis

The method applies the “larger-is-better” criterion for calculating signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios, which is calculated based on the equation described in section "Taguchi method", enabling the identification of factor-level combinations that enhance energy density as an output while minimizing variability caused by uncontrollable noise factors. The Taguchi results were quite revealing in terms of the relative effects GO concentration, reduction temperature, and reduction duration had on the energy density of pure GAs. Based on the S/N ratio analysis using the “larger-is-better” criterion, the hydrothermal reduction temperature had the highest effect among the three factors, followed by hydrothermal reduction duration, while GO concentration had the lowest impact. In fact, the reduction temperature exhibited the highest delta value of 0.47, thus confirming that it is the primary factor influencing the material’s capacitive behavior and must be precisely optimized to attain enhanced SC performance, as illustrated by the S/N ratio plot shown in Fig. 1. From this, it is clear how each experimental factor affects the overall response, with the vertical axis representing the mean S/N ratio, which is a measure reflecting both performance and consistency, with higher values showing improved robustness and desirable outcomes. When it comes to the GO concentration, the curve shows only a modest variation, with a peak at a concentration of 5 mg/mL, followed by a decrease at both lower and higher concentrations (2 and 8 mg/mL). This limited change demonstrates that the concentration exerts a very minor influence on the response variable. At the same time, for the reduction temperature, we observe a sharp increase from 160 °C to 180 °C, before leveling off at 200 °C, which highlights that temperature is a critical factor, as even a small increase impacts system performance. On the other hand, for the reduction duration, the increase was steady from 8 to 16 h, and the slope is less steep compared to the temperature. This observation is consistent with the Taguchi mean response table, as shown in Table 3, which confirms that the hydrothermal reduction temperature is the most critical synthesis parameter and exhibits a steep upward trend from level 1 to level 3. This indicates that energy density increases continuously with higher reduction temperature. This behavior can be attributed to the progressive reduction of GO sheets, leading to improved graphitization, which enhances electrical conductivity and promotes the development of a more favorable porous aerogel structure. This suggests that although higher temperatures contribute to improved performance consistency through increased signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios, excessively high temperatures can cause the aerogel to partially collapse, lose porosity, and reduce its accessible surface area, thereby decreasing material capacitance even if reproducibility improves. The Taguchi analysis also further revealed that the reduction time or duration operates as the second most important parameter that affects the GA performance. Plots of the mean response, shown in Fig. 2, and the corresponding S/N ratio for energy density revealed a positive correlation as the hydrothermal reduction time increased. This is because the GA layers reinforce structurally, and the aerogel surface becomes smoother after prolonged exposure to high temperatures, which improves its electrochemical performance. Extended heat treatment at the maximum reduction period of Level 3, lasting 16 h, results in a noticeable improvement in reduction kinetics and enhanced structural integrity.Fig. 2Main effects plot for means.

Main effects plot for means.

The influence of GO concentration on energy density exhibited a clear optimum at 5 mg/mL, though both lower and higher concentrations also provided helpful insights. At the lowest concentration (2 mg/mL), the performance was consistently the poorest, which can be attributed to an insufficient number of GO sheets during the reduction process to establish a mechanically stable and conductive aerogel framework. By contrast, the intermediate level (5 mg/mL) offered the highest energy density with the most stable and balanced compromise between network connectivity and porosity. At the highest concentration (8 mg/mL), the performance was in some cases close to that of 5 mg/mL, indicating that a denser aerogel can still sustain relatively high capacitance. However, the average performance was slightly lower, most likely due to partial sheet restacking and structural compaction, which reduce pore volume and ion-accessible sites. This behavior indicates that though the higher precursor concentrations can still result in good-performance aerogels, the intermediate concentration of 5 mg/mL has the most controllable balance of structural stability, connected porosity, and ion transport paths, resulting in the best overall EDLC performance.

The optimal synthesis conditions were identified as a GO concentration of 5 mg/mL, a reduction temperature of 180 °C, and a reduction duration of 16 h, based on S/N analysis and mean response plots taken together. These parameters correspond to experimental run number 5, represented by the sample labelled GA-5. These parameters consistently gave the highest energy densities across all experimental trials, indicating the usefulness of the Taguchi DOE in systematically tuning various synthesis parameters to achieve improved electrochemical performance.

The XPS elemental analysis also showed a high increase in the carbon content of GA-5, 83.28 at.% carbon and 16.72 at.% oxygen, respectively. Conversely, the GO precursor solution from Graphenea exhibited a different carbon content, ranging from 49 to 56 at.% carbon and 41 to 50 at.% oxygen. The obvious increase in carbon and decrease in oxygen contents indicate a significant deoxygenation caused by hydrothermal treatment. Nevertheless, the elimination of several oxygen groups suggests a partial recovery of the sp2 carbon structure. These results indicate that GO was reduced to rGO in GA-5. Graphitic domains are partly restored, and the structure is changed compared to the GO precursor. In terms of electrochemistry, such a chemical evolution is of significant interest. The large number of π-bonded carbon domains contributes to the electronic conductivity, thereby facilitating effective electron transfer at the electrode. On the other hand, the remaining oxygen groups enhance the surface wettability and facilitate electrolyte penetration into the aerogel, which should enable rapid ion migration within the macroporous aerogel network. This balance between conductivity and ion accessibility directly explains why it was found from electrochemical tests that GA-5 provided higher specific capacitance and energy density than less optimized samples.

Electrochemical analysis

CV analysis

The CV profiles of the GA-5 SC at four different scan rates of 5, 10, 20, and 50 mV/s are illustrated in Fig. 8, which provides information about ion percolation characteristics, capacitance characteristics, and rate performance of GA-5 in a symmetric two-electrode cell configuration. At the lowest scan rate of 5 mV/s, the current densities range from approximately − 0.2 A/g to + 0.2 A/g, and the CV curve is almost rectangular, which is the best profile for ideal EDLC behavior, indicating efficient EDL formation and highly reversible ion adsorption and desorption occurring at the interface between the electrode and electrolyte. Moreover, the symmetry of the anodic and cathodic currents further suggests a high reversibility of CD process with minimal energy loss. When the scan rate increases to 10 mV/s, a corresponding increase in the current response is observed, with maximum current densities of approximately ± 0.5 A/g. The CV curve remains nearly rectangular, although some distortion is visible, indicating the development of kinetic limitations. This deviation from the quasi-rectangular shape at higher scan rates is attributed to increased internal resistance and restrictions on ion diffusion within the porous network of the aerogel. The current density rises to ~ + 1 and − 1 A/g at 20 mV/s, and the CV curve deviates more from the ideal rectangular shape, which is consistent with increasing resistive effects and ion access limitation at the higher sweep rates. However, in the presence of these distortions, the GA-5 electrode still displays fast ion transport and high charge storage, as indicated by the retained integrated area beneath the CV profiles.Fig. 8CV curves.

CV curves.

At the highest scan rate of 50 mV/s, GA-5 exhibits the largest current response, with current densities reaching approximately − 2.1 to + 2.1 A/g, which actually makes the CV curve transform from an ideal rectangular profile to an elliptical or leaf-like shape. At high scan rates, these characteristic distortions are common for porous carbon electrodes and are caused by the limited time for ions to diffuse into the internal pores of the electrode framework. This restrictive ionic transport leads to an access limitation of active sites, which eventually results in a deviation from ideal EDLC behavior. Nonetheless, although distortion of the CV profile is apparent at higher scan rates, the continuously increasing enclosed CV area is a reflection of the higher current response related to the faster potential sweeps. While the calculated specific capacitance is decreased due to incomplete ion penetration into the porous network, the maintenance of a significant CV area even at high scan rates establishes GA-5's good rate capability and efficient CD kinetics. Moreover, retaining a quasi-rectangular profile even at 50 mV/s further highlights the mechanical stability and robustness of the interconnected 3D porous structure of the GA-5, which facilitates rapid ion diffusion and reduces internal resistance.

The remarkable capacitive behavior observed is ascribed to the unique structural features of the GA. Not only does the 3D porous network resulting from hydrothermal reduction and freeze-drying hinder the restacking issue of graphene sheets, but it also offers a large surface area that is accessible for the electrolyte. Overall, these CV results indicate that the optimized GA-5 exhibits exceptional rate capability and electrochemical stability across a broad range of scan rates, establishing it as a promising candidate for high-performance SC applications. Discussion

The electrochemical performance of the synthesized GAs samples generally agrees with the performance trends observed in earlier studies. The CV profiles for the GA-5 sample showed a near-rectangular shape within a scan rate range of 5–10 mV/s, typical of ideal EDLC behavior. In fact, this behavior is higher than that of the pure GA reported in some prior research studies, which obtained a specific capacitance of approximately 95 F/g at 10 mV/s. By the same token, though minor deviations from ideal capacitive behavior were apparent at high scan rates (20 and 50 mV/s) due to diffusion limitations, the GA-5 retained a highly capacitive response, confirming the structural robustness and favorable ion transport pathways of the 3D aerogel framework.

The GCD results further highlight the superior electrochemical properties of GA-5. At a low constant current of 5 mA, the specific capacitance reached 182.3 F/g, outperforming previously reported undoped GAs (~ 85.6 F/g) and even exceeding vitamin C-reduced GAs (176 F/g)34,35.

Although direct comparison with literature values must be made cautiously due to differences in current densities and electrode fabrication methods, the capacitance values achieved here strongly suggest improved electrochemical surface accessibility and more efficient ion transport within the optimized aerogel. At higher constant currents, specific capacitances of 165.96 F/g (10 mA) and 144.6 F/g (20 mA) were still achieved, significantly surpassing the normal capacitance drops typically seen with most undoped graphene aerogels, which often fall short of the ion diffusion limitation at charge–discharge rates.

The good conductivity of GA-5 was substantiated by the EIS and GCD results, with an ESR of 7.96 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document} at 5 mA, a moderate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{ct}$$\end{document} of 8.7 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document}, and a low \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{s}$$\end{document} of 0.18 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document}. The GA-5 supercapacitor achieved low resistance levels that exceeded those of former aerogels with the same supercapacitor fabrication method​. A near-vertical line in low-frequency regions indicates capacitive behavior with efficient ion diffusion, which is essential for stable charge storage. The high \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{ct}$$\end{document} is a concern, but there have been prior studies that have proved that capacitance is not governed solely by charge transfer resistance, and a morphology-driven increase in an accessible surface area is capable of raising capacitance, and at the same time, intrapore and contact resistances also can inflate the semicircle in Nyquist plots36. In a study on pseudocapacitive carbon-oxide systems, it was found that the transition-metal-oxide systems often had higher specific capacitance coupled with increased ESR, reflecting a trade-off between energy and power density37. In the present work, the high capacitance of GA-5 can be attributed to its abundant accessible surface area, while the observed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{ct}$$\end{document} likely arises from electrode–electrolyte wetting or diffusion barriers manifesting at EIS timescales.

The Taguchi experiments provided an extremely valuable insight into the synthetic parameters that are most important to the electrochemical behavior. As shown by the analysis, the reduction temperature turned out to be the most important parameter, as higher reduction temperatures correspond to a more complete reduction of the GO sheets, leading to a better electrical conductivity and, consequently, a better capacitance. According to the S/N ratio, 200 °C was determined as the statistically optimal condition, which confirmed that increasing thermal reduction will accelerate deoxygenation and structural ordering. Nonetheless, according to the average response value, 180 °C (combined with 5 mg/mL of GO and a time of 16 h) was established as the practical optimum, which also coincided with that of the best experimental sample GA-5. This divergence between the S/N ratio prediction (200 °C) and the mean response (180 °C) highlights the distinction between statistically predicted robustness and experimentally validated performance, underscoring the importance of balancing conductivity and porosity in optimizing pure GAs. On the other hand, higher temperature promotes conduction but could, however, cause partial pore collapse and densify the active material, thus limiting ion penetration. Therefore, 180 °C was concluded to be the best point between conductivity and porosity.

In addition, it is necessary to acknowledge certain restrictions in the process of the hydrothermal reduction method applied, where one of them is that it usually results in incomplete reduction of oxygen-containing groups, and we’ve observed this phenomenon in our study as well, as confirmed by Raman and XPS analysis—there was a partial recovery of the carbon network. The incomplete removal of oxygen functionalities would decrease the electrical conductivity, which further limits performance ability. For that, other strategies or supplemental methods could be used, as heteroatom-doped materials (those materials prepared by adding elements such as N and B during the reduction of GO), which have been shown to enhance the aforementioned properties38. Post-treatment, such as thermal annealing, plasma treatment, and chemical reduction, can also enhance the relative ratio of oxygen reduction and overall structural quality. Addressing these problems stresses the demand for a hybrid approach that can produce much higher quality and better-performing rGO aerogel.

From the findings, it can be summarized that the GA-5 SC either satisfies or exceeds the performance thresholds reported on pristine GAs that has been explored in the literature and this is a result of improvement hierarchical porosity, as well as mechanical robustness, low internal resistance, and high electrochemical activity, which shows and proves that with an orderly control on structure as well as optimization of synthesis conditions, the issue of restacking can be circumvented, realizing the actual potential of pure graphene aerogels towards high-performance EDLC material. Conclusion

The study was focused on the optimization of the key synthesis parameters or conditions of the hydrothermal reduction method for pristine GAs to fabricate a high-performance EDLC electrode. This approach eventually helps to circumvent issues like graphene restacking and low ion accessibility in the two-dimensional materials. Thus, 3D GAs were prepared via a hydrothermal reduction and freeze-drying method to achieve high electrical conductivity, structural integrity, and good ion transport properties. In order to investigate this, the synthesis process was optimized in a systematic manner using a Taguchi statistical approach, which used varying hydrothermal reduction temperature, duration, and GO concentration to identify the effect and the best combination governing energy storage performance as an output.

A total of nine samples were prepared and characterized using CV, GCD, and EIS, with each experiment being conducted three times for each condition to verify the accuracy of the results and reproducibility based on a 95% confidence interval. The 3D structure of the optimal sample (GA-5) exhibits super electrochemical performance and high specific capacitances, which are several times higher than those values of the reported undoped GAs, reaching up to 182.33 F/g at a current density of 0.2 A/g, correspondingly delivering an energy density of 6.33 Wh/kg and power density of 108.5 W/kg. GA-5 had a low ESR value (7.96 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document} ) and a high rate and stability at high scan rates, indicating that it was an ideal electrode material. It was concluded, by means of Taguchi analysis, that the hydrothermal reduction temperature is the most significant factor on the energy density as output, followed by reduction time and GO concentration, with optimal conditions being for 5 mg/mL concentration of GO, 180 °C reaction temperature, and 16 h in duration, respectively.

Notably, an outstanding specific capacitance and cycling stability that are higher than common values of pure and undoped graphene aerogels from previous literature were achieved in the case of GA-5, confirming the effectiveness of the synthesis/optimization strategy when compared to the literature for pure aerogels. The present findings are potentially expandable, and the optimized GA framework developed here could serve as a platform used for the introduction of heteroatom doping (N, S, B) or hybrid post-treatment (thermal, plasma, or chemical) to induce pseudocapacitance behavior and enhance cycling stability. Further study can also be made on the hybrid electrolytes and flexible substrates to further extend the applications of GAs in wearables and next-generation energy storage systems. Critically, the optimization strategies presented here are not limited to pristine GAs but are also directly transferable to advanced doping and alternative synthesis routes, making sure that future work can build on the methodology and insights presented here to achieve further improvements in both material quality and device performance.
