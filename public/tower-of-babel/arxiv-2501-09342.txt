Monochromatic graph decompositions and monochromatic piercing inspired by anti-Ramsey colorings
Yair Caro, Zsolt Tuza

Abstract
Anti-Ramsey theory was initated in 1975 by Erdős, Simonovits and Sós, inspiring hundreds of publications since then. Given a graph G G , the problem is to determine the smallest integer k k such that every edge coloring of the complete graph K n K_{n} of order n n with at least k k colors forces a copy of G G with all edges of distinct colors. This minimum is denoted by Ar ⁡ ( n , G ) \mathrm{Ar}(n,G) . The current paper is the third item of a trilogy in which we consider a far-reaching generalization of the anti-Ramsey paradigm. Two kinds of functions are studied. For any graph G G and family ℱ \mathcal{F} of graphs, introduce: • If K 2 ∈ ℱ K_{2}\in\mathcal{F} , let f ⁡ ( n , G | ℱ ) f(n,G|\mathcal{F}) be the smallest integer k k such that every edge coloring of K n K_{n} with at least k k colors forces a copy of G G in which all color classes are members of ℱ \mathcal{F} . • If K 2 ∉ ℱ K_{2}\notin\mathcal{F} , let g ⁡ ( n , G | ℱ ) g(n,G|\mathcal{F}) be the largest integer k k for which there exists an edge coloring of K n K_{n} using exactly k k colors, such that every copy of G G contains an induced color class which is a member of ℱ \mathcal{F} . Clearly the case ℱ = { K 2 } \mathcal{F}=\{K_{2}\} is the classical anti-Ramsey (aka rainbow) paradigm. In Part 1 of the trilogy we concentrated mainly on hereditary families and developed some of the necessary tools to allow lower and upper bounds in this more general setting. In many cases sharp asymptotic results were obtained. In Part 2 of the trilogy we concentrated on families (not necessarily hereditary) related to popular edge-coloring types with parity constraints. Here in Part 3 we focus on the most general situation, regardless of any hereditary consideration or parity constraints. We show the close relation between the functions f f and g g , and develop further useful tools. We supply dozens of examples (mostly with asymptotically sharp bounds) concerning this panoramic view, as well as raise many open problems for future research. To get a glimpse of the spirit and varieties of results proved here, we give two illustrative examples of the sharp asymptotics obtained. Then ex ⁡ ( n , K ⌈ p / ( q − 1 ) ⌉ ) + ⌈ p / ( q − 1 ) ⌉ − 1 ≤ g ⁡ ( n , K p | ⟨ K q ⟩ ) ≤ g ⁡ ( n , K p | ⟨ H ⟩ ) ≤ ( 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ​ ex ​ ( n , K ⌈ p / ( q − 1 ) ⌉ ) \mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/(q-1)\right\rceil})+\left\lceil p/(q-1)\right\rceil-1\leq g(n,K_{p}|\langle K_{q}\rangle)\leq g(n,K_{p}|\langle H\rangle)\leq(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/(q-1)\right\rceil}) . Introduction

1.1
Anti-Ramsey paradigm and recent developments — a short overview

Anti-Ramsey theory was initated in 1975 by Erdős, Simonovits and Sós
[
12
]
, inspiring hundreds of publications since then.
The main problem is:
Given a graph
G
, determine the smallest integer
\mathrm{Ar}(n,G)

such that every edge coloring of the complete graph
K_{n}
with
at least that many colors forces a copy of
G
with all edges
getting distinct colors.
The main result of
[
12
]
is:
Let
\chi_{e}(G)=\min\{\chi(G-e):e\in E(G)\}
.
If
\chi_{e}(G)=k\geq 3
then

\mathrm{Ar}(n,G)=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k})
, where
\mathrm{ex}(n,G)
is
the famous Turán number.
The proof relies heavily on the Erdős–Stone–Simonovits theorem

[
13
,
11
]
that if
\chi(G)=k\geq 3
then

\mathrm{ex}(n,G)=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k})
.
Decades later it was finally proved that, for all
n\geq k\geq 3
,
\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{k+1})=\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k})+2

[
21
,
23
]
.
The subject is still very active today, an extensive survey with
lots of results and references is given in
[
14
]
.
For the various directions in anti-Ramsey theory we explicitly mention
here the papers

[
2
,
3
,
4
,
8
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
20
,
22
,
24
]
;
some of them are old and some are recent, the list is by no means complete.

Recently the present authors initiated the study of the following more general “rainbow problem”
[
6
]
.
Let
G
be a graph, and
\mathcal{F}
any given family of graphs.
For every integer
n\geq|G|
, let
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
denote the smallest integer
k
such that any edge coloring of
K_{n}
with at least
k
colors forces a copy of
G
in which each color class induces a member of
\mathcal{F}
.
Observe that in anti-Ramsey problems each color class is a single edge; i.e.,
\mathcal{F}=\{K_{2}\}
.

A major result in
[
6
]
is:
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a hereditary family and let

\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}}}(G):=\min\{\chi(G-D):D\subset G,\,D\in\mathcal{F})\}\,.

If
\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}}}(G)=k\geq 3
then
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k})
.
Again, a heavy use of the Erdős–Stone–Simonovits theorem is inevitable, together with new tools of independent interest, e.g. the Independent Transversal Lemma in directed graphs of bounded outdegree.

Also, it has been proved that if
G
is stable with respect to

\mathcal{F}
, which means
\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}}}(G)=\chi(G)
, and if

\chi(G)=k\geq 3
also holds, then
(regardless of whether or not
\mathcal{F}
is hereditary),

f(n,G|\mathcal{F})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k})
.
Many examples of interesting and natural hereditary families and
the implied results concerning
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
or
f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F})

are given in
[
6
]
.

Already in
[
6
]
we mentioned that certain lower bounds obtained, as well as the case where
\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}}}(G)=\chi(G)
, remain true even if
\mathcal{F}
is not hereditary but contains
K_{2}
.

1.2
Generalized anti-Ramsey monochromatic decomposition and piercing

The main purpose of this paper is to consider the following:

Assume that a family
\mathcal{F}
is given.
For every graph
G
, we define two functions on the domain of
all integers
n\geq|G|
.
The first one,
f
, was already the subject of the first
[
6
]

and second
[
7
]
part of the current “trilogy”.
The other function,
g
, termed
piercing
, will be studied
in Section
9
of the present paper.
A main distinction is made depending on whether
the single edge
K_{2}
is, or is not a member of
\mathcal{F}
.

\mathcal{F}
contains
K_{2}
:

\bullet

Define
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
as the smallest integer
m
such that in

every
edge coloring of
K_{n}
using at least
m
colors
there exists

a copy of
G
in which
all
color classes are members of
\mathcal{F}
.

This
f
is well defined, satisfying
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\leq\binom{n}{2}
,
as the conditions hold for any
G
in the rainbow
K_{n}
.

\mathcal{F}
does not contain
K_{2}
:

\bullet

Define
g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
as the largest integer
m
such that

there exists
a coloring of
K_{n}
with
m
colors, where

every
copy of
G
contains a color class
which is a member of
\mathcal{F}
.
If no such coloring exists, then
g(n,G|\mathcal{F}):=0
.

A color class in which a subgraph of the considered copy of
G

occurs, will be called a
piercing color class
.
For distinct copies of
G
, the piercing color classes are
allowed to be distinct.
If
\mathcal{F}
consists of just one graph, we call this case

exact piercing
.

In the next subsection we will give a more detailed description
of the structure of the paper.
Briefly saying here, our main goal is to develop the general
approach to anti-Ramsey theory via detailed analysis of the functions

f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
and
g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.
For this purpose we introduce new tools, also
using classical theorems from extremal graph theory and
incorporating results about packing graphs.
Doing so, we mostly study the
case
G=K_{p}
with respect to numerous families
\mathcal{F}
.
The power of our approach is demonstrated
by a wide range of applications.

1.3
Structure of paper and sample results

Here we sketch the contents of the subsequent sections,
and mention some representative results or their
main message, not always quoting all their conditions in
full detail.
For their strongest forms please see the corresponding theorems.

Section
2
describes some basic inequalities and
coloring patterns that are used along the paper.
Among the simple relations we mention the following important
principle of complementarity:

\bullet
If
K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
and
G\notin\mathcal{F}
, then

g(n,G|\overline{\mathcal{F}})=f(n,G|\mathcal{F})-1
.
(Proposition
2.2
)

This allows deriving results on one of

f
and
g
, and obtaining an immediate result on the other function.
It is highly beneficial, as the methods developed for estimating
the two functions are in general much different from each other.
We apply this relation several times along Section
9
,
and a general consequence (Proposition
10.17
) is
also derived from it in the concluding section.
However, the reader may discover that the complementarity
principle can be applied in many more situations where we
do not mention it one by one along the text.

In Section
3
we collect some important results
by which efficient upper bounds on
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
can be obtained.
Among them are the Erdős–Rado theorem, the Erdős–Stone–Simonovits
theorem, the Rainbow Cut Lemma that we developed in
[
6
]
,
and the Canonical Cut Lemma proved here as Theorem
3.4
,
that will be a major tool in the present paper.

Section
4
concerns numerous families
for which
f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F})
has quadratic growth order.
Among those
\mathcal{F}
are the following families:
all regular graphs;
all graphs except the complete graphs

K_{r}
with
r\geq q
for any fixed
q\geq 3
if
p\geq 2q-1
;
all graphs except the starts
K_{1,r}
with
r\geq q
for any fixed
q\geq 2
if
p\geq 2q+1
;

K_{2}
together with all graphs containing at least one cycle;
all graphs of maximum degree at most
\Delta
for any fixed
\Delta
;
families with connectivity constraints; and more.
From the many tight asymptotic results we mention two examples:

\bullet
If
p
and
r
are integers with either
p\geq 4
and
r=1

or
p\geq 2r
and
r\geq 2
, and
\mathcal{F}=\{mK_{2}\mid 1\leq m\leq r\}
,
then
f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{p-r})
.
The asymptotic formula remains the same if
\mathcal{F}
is supplemented
with all triangle-free graphs. (Theorem
4.10
)

\bullet
If
\mathcal{F}
is the family of all graphs on at most
k
vertices
(
k\geq 2
), and

p\geq k+2
, then
f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{p-k+1})
.
(Theorem
4.11
)

Section
5
concerns a certain kind of stability.
As a very general result beyond
K_{p}
,
we prove a sufficient condition for the
existence of graphs which are chromatically stable with respect
to a family
\mathcal{F}
and make
f
grow proportionally to
n^{2}

(more precisely
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\sim\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r})
for a given
r\geq 3
).

\bullet
If the family
\mathcal{F}
contains
K_{2}
, and

\max\{e(D):D\in\mathcal{F},\,|V(D)|=n\}<n^{2}/k^{2}
holds
for every
n
where
k\geq 3
is a fixed integer, then
there exist infinitely many graphs
G
such that

\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}}}(G)=\chi(G)=k
and all those
G
have

f(n,G|\mathcal{F})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k})
. (Theorem
5.1
)

Section
6
concerns the behaviour of
f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F})

where
\mathcal{F}
is obtained from the class of all graphs
by omitting just one graph
H
.
These densest nontrivial families will be major tools to handle
instances of exact piercing in Section
9
.
There is a surprising difference if the deleted graph
H

is a star or any other graph:

\bullet
If
\mathcal{F}
is obtained by excluding the star
K_{1,t}
, then

f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/t\right\rceil})
if
p\geq 2t+1
,
and
o(n^{2})
if
t+1\leq p\leq 2t
. (Theorem
6.1
)

\bullet
If
\mathcal{F}
is obtained by excluding a non-star
G\neq K_{p}
of
order at most
p
, then
f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F})=1
for all

n>n_{0}(p)
. (Theorem
6.2
)

Section
7
concerns the asymptotic sharp
determination of
f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F})
where the members of
\mathcal{F}
are
the graphs whose number of edges belongs to a prescribed
integer sequence
S
.
Among the considered cases are various arithmetic progressions,
and also the much sparser sequence of all squares.

\bullet
If
p\geq 5
and
\mathcal{F}
is generated by
S=\{k^{2}\mid k\geq 1\}
,
or
p\geq 4k-1
and
S=\{m\mid m\equiv 1

f(n,G|\mathcal{F})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil})
.
(Theorems
7.1
and

)

Section
8
concerns the existence of families
\mathcal{F}

for which
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\leq h(n)
where
h(n)
is a
prescribed function that ranges from a constant function to

\bullet
If
\mathcal{F}
is obtained by excluding all graphs

with

\mathrm{Ar}(n,H)\leq h(n)
where
h(n)
is a positive integer function,
then
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\leq h(n)
if

, and
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})=1

for all
n>n_{0}(G)
if
G\in\mathcal{F}
. (Proposition
8.3
)

Section
9
constitutes a major contribution of this
paper concerning the notion of piercing and the function
g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.
Before entering into details, let us emphasize that the
complementary relation between
f
and
g
allows to derive
asymptotic results for numerous families in either direction.

After a short part in which we prove subquadratic growth under
certain conditions, a subsection is devoted to
g(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F})

where
\mathcal{F}=\langle H\rangle
is the family of all graphs containing a
specified small graph
H
.
This track is done in parallel to anti-Ramsey type results given in

[
4
]
and
[
15
]
.
Its aim is to show also the extra efforts needed to obtain exact

\bullet
We have
g(n,K_{4}-e|\langle K_{3}\rangle)=1

g(n,K_{4}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)=n

all

,
whereas

We quote the following ones here:

\bullet

)

\bullet

1.4
Standard definitions and some special notation

Most of our notation is standard.
For a graph
G
we denote by
|G|
the number of vertices and
by
e(G)
the number of edges.
The degree of vertex
v
is denoted by
d_{G}(v)
, and is
abbreviated as
d(v)
when
G
is understood.
Also, as usual,
\delta(G)
and
\Delta(G)
stand for the
minimum degree and maximum degree of
G
, respectively.
The path, the cycle, and the complete graph on
k
vertices is
denoted by
P_{k}
,
C_{k}
, and
K_{k}
, respectively.
Further,
K_{a,b}
denotes the complete bipartite graph with

a
and
b
vertices in its vertex classes.

A standard notation in extremal graph theory is

\mathrm{ex}(n,F)
for the Turán number of graph
F
, defined as the
maximum number of edges in a graph of order
n
that does not
contain any subgraph isomorphic to
F
.
In case of
F=K_{m+1}
the unique extremal graph—the Turán graph—is denoted by
T_{n,m}
.
It is the complete
m
-partite graph in which the sizes of
any two vertex classes differ by at most 1.

For the sake of easier reading we also collect here some
non-standard notation that will be important later,
although they will be defined in the sequel.
We consider the following huge families of graphs:

•

\mathfrak{F}
— all
finite graphs without isolated vertices;

•

\mathfrak{F}(\neg F)
— omitting the single given graph
F

from
\mathfrak{F}
;

•

\langle F\rangle
— all graphs containing the given
graph
F
as a subgraph;

•

\mathcal{F}\langle S\rangle
— all graphs
H
with
e(H)\in S
, where
S

is a given set of positive integers.

The families
\langle F\rangle
of graphs generated by a
single graph
F
are of great
interest in the context of function
g
.
A part of our study we will concentrate on
G=K_{p}
and
F=K_{q}
.
For this purpose we introduce the notation

g(n,p,q):=g(n,K_{p}|\langle K_{q}\rangle)

where
p>q\geq 3
will naturally be assumed.

Beside the anti-Ramsey function
\mathrm{Ar}(n,G)
we shall also apply its
local version
\mathrm{Lr}(n,G)
in the sequel.
It is defined as the smallest integer
k
such that
every edge coloring of
K_{n}
with at least
k
colors
contains a properly edge-colored copy of
G
.
This function was introduced and studied in

[
6
,
7
]
.

2
Some basic inequalities and coloring types

We begin with some simple inequalities.

Proposition 2.1
.

(Monotonicity.)

(i)

If
K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}\subset\mathcal{F}_{2}
, then
for every
G
we have
f(n,G|\mathcal{F}_{1})\geq f(n,G|\mathcal{F}_{2})
.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{F}
is hereditary,
and
H\subset G
, then

f(n,H|\mathcal{F})\leq f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.

(iii)

If
\mathcal{F}_{1}\subset\mathcal{F}_{2}
and
K_{2}\notin\mathcal{F}_{2}
, then
for every
G
we have
g(n,G|\mathcal{F}_{1})\leq g(n,G|\mathcal{F}_{2})
.

(iv)

If
H\subset G
and
Q\subset H
, then

g(n,H|\langle Q\rangle)\leq g(n,G|\langle Q\rangle)
.
More generally, if
H\subset G
, then

g(n,H|\mathcal{F})\leq g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
holds for every
\mathcal{F}

(
K_{2}\notin\mathcal{F}
) closed under supergraphs; i.e.,
if
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
F\subset F^{\prime}
, then also
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
.

(v)

If
Q_{1}\subset Q_{2}\subset G
, then

g(n,G|\langle Q_{1}\rangle)\geq g(n,G|\langle Q_{2}\rangle)
.

Proof.

(i)
Since every member of
\mathcal{F}_{1}
also belongs to
\mathcal{F}_{2}
,
a copy of
G
having an
\mathcal{F}_{1}
-decomposition by its color
classes satisfies the same requirement for
\mathcal{F}_{2}
as well.

(ii)
If
G^{\prime}\subset K_{n}
is a copy of
G
valid for
\mathcal{F}
,
then any subgraph
H^{\prime}\subset G^{\prime}
isomorphic to
H
inherits a
coloring in which the monochromatic classes are subsets of
those in
G^{\prime}
, hence are members of
\mathcal{F}
because

\mathcal{F}
is hereditary.

(iii)
Using
g(n,G|\mathcal{F}_{1})
colors, every copy of
G
contains
a color class that belongs to
\mathcal{F}_{1}
, and by assumption this
subgraph is also a member of
\mathcal{F}_{2}
.

(iv)
The inequality trivially holds if
g(n,H|\mathcal{F})=0
.
If
g(n,H|\mathcal{F})=k>0
, let
\psi
be an edge
k
-coloring of
K_{n}
that
pierces every copy of
H
.
Any
G^{\prime}\cong G
in
K_{n}
contains a
H^{\prime}\cong H
.
Let
c
be the color that pierces
H^{\prime}
under
\psi
with a
subgraph
F\in\mathcal{F}
.
Then the
c
-colored subgraph
F^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
contains
F
, hence

F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
and therefore also
G^{\prime}
is pierced.

(iv)
If
\psi
is an edge coloring of
K_{n}
that pierces
every copy of
G
, for
\langle Q_{2}\rangle
, then it is feasible also
for
\langle Q_{1}\rangle
, because any monochromatic supergraph of
Q_{2}

contains
Q_{1}
as a subgraph, hence is a member of
\langle Q_{1}\rangle
.
So,
\langle Q_{1}\rangle
admits at least as many colors as
\langle Q_{2}\rangle
does.
∎

There is also a complementary relation between the
functions
f
and
g
.
Then, for any family
\mathcal{F}
of graphs with
K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
, define its
complementary family
\overline{\mathcal{F}}:=\mathfrak{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}
,
obrained from the universe
\mathfrak{F}
of all finite graphs
without isolated vertices by removing all members of
\mathcal{F}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

(Family complementation.)

(i)

For every
\mathcal{F}
with
K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

g(n,G|\overline{\mathcal{F}})<f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.

(ii)

If
K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
and
G\notin\mathcal{F}
, then

g(n,G|\overline{\mathcal{F}})=f(n,G|\mathcal{F})-1
.

Proof.

Let
\psi
be any edge coloring of
K_{n}
using
k
colors, where

k\geq f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.
By the definition of
f
, there exists a copy
G^{\prime}
of
G

such that every color class of
G^{\prime}
belongs to
\mathcal{F}
.
Hence, this
G^{\prime}
does not contain any color class from

\overline{\mathcal{F}}
, violating the conditions on
g
, therefore
implying that
g(n,G|\overline{\mathcal{F}})=k
is impossible.
Since this impossibility holds for all
k\geq f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
, the
claimed inequality
(i)
follows.

For the proof of
(ii)
, let us denote
k:=f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.
By the definition of
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
, there exists an edge
coloring
\psi
of
K_{n}
using
k-1
colors, in which
every copy of
G
induces a color class not belonging to
\mathcal{F}
.
It follows that every copy of
G
contains a color class from

\overline{\mathcal{F}}
, thus
g(n,G|\overline{\mathcal{F}})\geq k-1
.
Applying
(i)
we obtain
g(n,G|\overline{\mathcal{F}})=k-1
,
hence proving
(ii)
.
∎

Before proceeding with the next section, the following two simple
remarks should be taken into account for the rest of the paper.

Remark 2.3
.

An important warning is that if
G\in\mathcal{F}
—that is, if

\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}}}(G)=1
holds—one cannot conclude
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})=1
.
The fundamental class of counterexamples is of the form

\mathcal{F}=\{G,K_{2}\}
, assuming that
G
contains a cycle.
Define the edge coloring
\psi
of
K_{n}
as
\psi(v_{i},v_{j})=j-1

for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
(see the
LEX
pattern in
Definition
2.5
below).
It implies
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\geq n
because the vertex of highest
index in a cycle is incident with a star color class, other than

K_{2}
, induced by
\psi
in
G
.

Remark 2.4
.

(i)

A necessary condition for
g(n,G|\mathcal{F})\geq 1
is to have a subgraph

F\subseteq G
with
F\in\mathcal{F}
.

(ii)

A sufficient condition for
g(n,G|\mathcal{F})\geq 1
is to have a subgraph

F\subseteq G
with
\langle F\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, where
\langle F\rangle

denotes the family of graphs containing
F
as a subgraph.

2.1
Some fundamental types of colorings of
K_{n}

Typically, lower bounds on the considered functions are obtained
by explicit constructions, which use standard coloring patterns
or their combinations on the edges of
K_{n}
.

Definition 2.5
.

(Homogeneous patterns.) The following three patterns are the standard homogeneous ones.

•

Monochromatic: all edges of
K_{n}
have the same color.

•

Rainbow: each edge of
K_{n}
has its private color.

•

LEX
: the vertices are in linear order
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}
,
and each edge
v_{i}v_{j}
with
1\leq i<j\leq n
has color
j-1
.

Compound patterns.

The general approach to lower-bound constructions with
fast-growing (superlinear) numbers of colors is to take a
large rainbow-colored graph
H\subset K_{n}
and use one or
relatively few additional colors in its complement
\overline{H}
.

In cases where the maximum number of colors is
o(n^{2})
,
usually the structure of
H
is not explicitly known, and
we take a monochromatic complement.
In situations with quadratic growth, it is convenient to let
H

be a Turán graph extremal for a certain
K_{q}
; that is, the
complete
(q-1)
-partite graph
T_{n,q-1}
where each of the
vertex classes
V_{1},\dots,V_{q-1}
has
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{q-1}\right\rfloor

or
\left\lceil\frac{n}{q-1}\right\rceil
vertices.
Then one has the freedom to choose an edge coloring inside each
V_{i}
.

The original anti-Ramsey problem was solved asymptotically in Erdős, Simonovits and Sós’s paper
[
12
]
by taking monochromatic complement.
In our general theory, however, in many situations it is
useful—and sometimes substantial, unavoidable—to
apply
LEX
inside some or all of the
V_{i}
.
So, the four general schemes for constructions yielding quadratic growth are:

•

RTEM
(n,q-1)
: rainbow
T_{n,q-1}
+ monochromatic
K_{|V_{i}|}

using the same color inside all
V_{i}
;

•

RTDM
(n,q-1)
: rainbow
T_{n,q-1}
+ monochromatic
K_{|V_{i}|}

inside all
V_{i}
, whose colors are mutually distinct;

•

RTEL
(n,q-1)
: rainbow
T_{n,q-1}
+
LEX
inside each
V_{i}
,
using the same colors for
LEX
in every vertex class.

•

RTDL
(n,q-1)
: rainbow
T_{n,q-1}
+
LEX
inside each
V_{i}
,
each of those
V_{i}
having its private
LEX
colors.

Hence, to make it easy to remember: RT = rainbow Turán,
M = monochromatic parts, L =
LEX
-colored parts,
E = equally colored, D = distinct colors are used.
We include the number
n
of vertices in the notation, and the
second parameter denotes the number of parts (vertex classes)
of the rainbow Turán graph involved.

3
Tools for asymptotic upper bounds

Here we describe a method that will be applied many times in
proofs of asymptotic upper bounds.
We take the following three major ingredients.

Theorem 3.1
.

(Erdős–Rado Canonical Theorem
[
9
]
.)
For every
t\geq 2
there exists an integer
n_{0}(t)
such
that, for every
n\geq n_{0}(t)
, in every edge coloring of

K_{n}
, there exists a complete subgraph
K_{t}

which is either monochromatic or rainbow or
LEX
-colored.

Theorem 3.2
.

(Erdős–Stone–Simonovits Theorem
[
13
,
11
]
.)
For every
a\geq 2
and every
r\geq 3
, the Turán number of
the complete
r
-partite graph
K_{a,\dots,a}
satisfies

\mathrm{ex}(K_{a,\dots,a})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r})=\frac{r-2}{2r-2}n^{2}+o(n^{2})
as
n\to\infty
.

As an immediate consequence, the same asymptotic holds for the Turán number
of any
r
-chromatic graph (
r\geq 3
),
but we won’t need this seemingly more general fact.

Theorem 3.3
.

(Rainbow Cut Lemma
[
6
]
.)
For any integers
b\geq 2
and
r\geq 2

there exists an
a=a(b,r)
with the following property.
If
\psi
is an edge coloring of
K_{ar}
,
and
K\cong K_{a,\dots,a}
is a rainbow spanning subgraph of

K_{ar}
under
\psi
, then
K
contains a (rainbow)
subgraph
K^{\prime}\cong K_{b,\dots,b}
of order
br
such that
the colors of edges inside the vertex classes of
K^{\prime}

in
K_{ar}
do not occur in
K
.

Combining the above, the following result can be derived.
For
r\geq 3
it is a strong tool to prove asymptotically tight
estimates; and if
r=2
, then the involved Turán function
is zero and the obtained bound is the subquadratic
h(n)
,
yielding an arbitrarily large “clean” complete bipartite
rainbow subgraph derived from as few as
o(n^{2})
colors.

Theorem 3.4
.

(Canonical Cut Lemma.)
For any integers
r\geq 2
and
t\geq 2
there exists a
function
h(n)=h(n,r,t)
of subquadratic growth rate

h(n)=o(n^{2})
with the following property.
In every edge coloring
\psi
of
K_{n}
with more than

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r})+h(n)
colors, there occurs a complete

r
-partite graph
K\cong K_{t,\dots,t}
, say with vertex
classes
V_{1},\dots,V_{r}
, such that

(i)

K
is rainbow-colored,

(ii)

for
i=1,\dots,r
each
V_{i}
induces a
K_{t}

which is either monochromatic or rainbow or
LEX
-colored,

(iii)

no color of
K
is repeated inside any
V_{i}
.

Proof.

Given
t
, we choose
b=n_{0}(t)
according to Theorem
3.1
,
and then take
a=a(b,r)
satisfying the requirements of
Theorem
3.3
.
For fixed
r
and
t
also
a
is fixed, hence by Theorem
3.2

the difference
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{a,\dots,a})-\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r})
can be
expressed as a subquadratic function
h(n,r,t)
.
Then more than
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{r})+h(n,r,t)
colors always imply the
presence of a rainbow
K_{a,\dots,a}
;
it can be cleaned to a rainbow
K^{\prime}\cong K_{b,\dots,b}

with the additional restriction that no color of
any
e\in E(K^{\prime})
appears in
\binom{V(K^{\prime})}{2}\setminus E(K^{\prime})
;
and then each vertex class of
K_{b,\dots,b}
contains a
complete subgraph of order
t
, which is either monochromatic
or rainbow or
LEX
-colored.
∎

With reference to part
(iii)
of this theorem, we will use the following terminology.

Definition 3.5
.

(Clean multipartite graph.) A rainbow complete multipartite graph
K\subset K_{n}
with
vertex classes
V_{1},\dots,V_{r}
is said to be clean if
none of the
V_{i}
induces any edge whose color occurs in
K
.

In many situations we shall ensure further that the vertex classes
have homogeneous colorings (monochromatic, rainbow, or
LEX
);
but this will not be a requirement for being “clean”.

4
Quadratic growth: The role of exclusions and structural requirements

Here we present families
\mathcal{F}
for which there exist graphs

G
with
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\geq cn^{2}
for some
c>0
.
In most cases we shall achieve much more, namely
tight asymptotic bounds.
The general principle in obtaining effective lower bounds is
to apply some of the composite patterns

RTEM
(n,m)
,
RTDM
(n,m)
,
RTEL
(n,m)
,
RTDL
(n,m)

introduced in Section
2.1
.
For the readers’ convenience we recall with emphasis that the
core of these constructions is a rainbow-colored
complete
m
-partite Turán graph
R\cong T_{n,m}
, and none of
the
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{m+1})\sim\frac{m-1}{2m}n^{2}
colors of
R
is
repeated inside any vertex class
V_{i}
;
i.e., the color set of
K_{n}-R
is disjoint from that of
R
.

The fewest and the most colors occur in
RTEM
(n,m)
and

RTDL
(n,m)
, respectively; however, the difference is just

n-m-1
, which is negligible in comparison to
\Theta(n^{2})

as
n
gets large.
The distinctive properties of the four variants—as expressed
with the suffixes
EM
,
DM
,
EL
,
DL
—become
relevant when particular families
\mathcal{F}
are considered.

4.1
Three case studies

In this subsection we collect three simple and very natural graph classes.

(a)
\mathcal{F}
= {regular graphs}.

Clearly,
K_{5}
can be decomposed into two copies of
C_{5}
;
but it is regular, hence
\chi(K_{5}|\mathcal{F})=1
.
However,
f(n,K_{5}|\mathcal{F})
is a quadratic function of
n
,
as demonstrated by the
RTDL
(n,2)
coloring.
Then, in one side there must be at least three vertices of
K_{5}

that form
K_{3}
(or
K_{4}
, or the entire
K_{5}
), colored by

LEX
which forbids monochromatic regular graphs except
K_{2}
. Hence
f(n,K_{5}|\mathcal{F})\geq\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{3})+n-2=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{4}(n^{2}+4n-8)\right\rfloor
.

On the other hand,
\mathrm{Lr}(n,K_{5})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{3})
where
the function
\mathrm{Lr}
requires proper edge coloring;
i.e., all monochromatic edge classes are
1-regular, so that
f(n,K_{5}|\mathcal{F})\leq\mathrm{Lr}(n,K_{5})
holds.
As a consequence, the asymptotics of

f(n,K_{5}|\mathcal{F})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{3})
is determined.

Exactly the same proof gives
f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F})>\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\lceil p/2\rceil})
using either of the

RTEL
(n,\lceil p/2\rceil-1)
or

RTDL
(n,\lceil p/2\rceil-1)
coloring pattern.
Then Theorem 5.2 in
[
6
]
, stating

\mathrm{Lr}(n,K_{p})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\lceil p/2\rceil})
,
gives a matching asymptotic upper bound.
Clearly, although
G\in\mathcal{F}
, the fact
\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}}}(G)=1
is useless,
rather we applied the property of
LEX
that it allows only stars as monochromatic classes; and for
K_{p}
we computed the order
\lceil p/2\rceil
that can make use of this property.

We will show that this can be done in a wider setting, where,
given
\mathcal{F}
, a certain large enough induced subgraph of
G

is anti-
LEX
with regard to
\mathcal{F}
.

Before some steps towards the solution of this problem, let us
give two further case studies as negative examples;
i.e., graph classes that do admit quadratic growth.

(b) Omitting large complete graphs.

Let
t\geq 3
be a fixed integer, and let

\mathcal{F}
be obtained from the family of all graphs by
omitting just the complete graphs
K_{r}
for all
r\geq t
.
We now take the
RTDM
(n,2)
coloring of
K_{n}
.
This yields a quadratic lower bound for every complete
graph
K_{p}
of order
p\geq 2t-1
.
Indeed, since
p\geq 2t-1
, any copy of
K_{p}
has at least
t

vertices in one of the monochromatic classes (and its color is
distinct from all the other color classes), hence forces a
color class
K_{r}
,
r\geq t
, which is not a member of
\mathcal{F}
.

(c) Omitting large stars.

Let
t\geq 2
be a fixed integer, and let

\mathcal{F}
be obtained from the family of all graphs by
omitting just the stars
K_{1,r}
for all
r\geq t
.
Take now the edge coloring
RTDL
(n,2)
of
K_{n}
.
This coloring yields a quadratic lower bound for every complete
graph
K_{p}
of order
p\geq 2t+1
.
Indeed, any copy of
K_{p}
has at least
t+1
vertices in
one of the
LEX
-colored classes, and its edges
are split there into monochromatic stars whose colors are
distinct from all the other color classes.
The maximum degree among those stars is at least

\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil-1\geq t
, hence at vertex
t+1
in the
LEX
order there is a color class
K_{1,t}
which is not in
\mathcal{F}
.

4.2
Cycles

Let
\mathcal{F}
be any family of graphs each containing a cycle.
As already explained above, in order to allow decomposition for
all graphs, we set
\mathcal{F}^{*}:=\mathcal{F}\cup\{K_{2}\}
.

Remark 4.1
.

If
G
is acyclic, then the only decomposition into members
of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
is to make
G
rainbow (i.e., all color classes are

K_{2}
), and then
f(n,G|\mathcal{F}^{*})=\mathrm{Ar}(n,G)
.

For this reason we will assume here that
G
is not acyclic.
Note that the addition of
K_{2}
is necessary even if
G
is
decomposable into members of
\mathcal{F}
, because a rainbow
K_{n}

does not admit any decomposition other than with classes
K_{2}
.

It follows from the above discussion that, for
p\geq 5
and

G=K_{p}
, the
RTEL
(n,2)
or
RTDL
(n,2)
coloring of
K_{n}

with its more than
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{3})
colors
forces
f(n,G|\mathcal{F}^{*})
to be quadratic, because any
K_{r}
with

r\geq 3
in
LEX
induces a color class
K_{1,r-1}
,
which is not a member of the current
\mathcal{F}^{*}
.

This example can be raised to a more general level.
For any non-forest graph
G
let us define
\mu(G)
as the
smallest integer
k
such that every induced subgraph of
G

on
k
vertices contains a cycle; and if
G
is acyclic,
then let
\mu(G)=\infty
.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
G
contains a cycle, then
\mu(G)\leq 2\alpha(G)+1
.

Proof.

Suppose there is an induced subgraph
H
of
G
on

2\alpha(G)+1
vertices containing no cycle.
Then
H
is acyclic, hence bipartite, and
\alpha(G)\geq\lceil\,|H|/2\rceil=\alpha(G)+1
, a contradiction.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose
m\geq 2
and
\lceil\,|G|/m\rceil\geq\mu(G)
.
Then
f(n,G|\mathcal{F}^{*})\geq\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{m+1})+n-m+1
.

Proof.

Consider the
RTDL
(n,m)
coloring built upon the rainbow Turán graph
T_{n,m}
with
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{m+1})
edges,
using
n-m
additional colors.
In any copy
G^{\prime}
of
G
, there is a class of
T_{n,m}
containing
a subgraph
H\subset G^{\prime}
with at least
\mu(G)
vertices.
By definition, this
H
contains a cycle, say
C
,
which is
LEX
-colored allowing only stars as color classes.
Consider the vertex
v\in V(C)
of highest index under
LEX
.
The edges incident with
v
in
H
form a monochromatic star
other than
K_{2}
(as it contains at least two edges of
C
),
and no other edge of
H
—neither of
G
—has this color.
Thus, the subgraph induced by this color class in
G
is
not a member of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
, and consequently no copy of
G
is

\mathcal{F}^{*}
-colored.
∎

We have to note that the role of cycles is substantial.
This fact is demonstrated also by the next observation, which
was first proved in
[
3
]
, using induction.
We include here a short argument that applies the well known
BFS algorithm.

Proposition 4.4
.

Every forest
T

can be packed in
LEX
so that
T
is rainbow.

Proof.

We may assume that
T
is connected (otherwise extending
T

to a spanning tree does not make the task of rainbow
embedding easier).
Fix a root of
T
and place it as the lowest vertex of the

LEX
order.
Then pack the vertices step by step upward using Breadth First
Search.
Then at each step every new vertex has only one lower neighbor,
and the rest are upper neighbors.
Hence,
T
is embedded as a rainbow subgraph.
∎

It is important to note that this proposition does not imply

f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
to be non-quadratic for every family
\mathcal{F}

of trees.
Transparent counterexamples are the stars and the paths,
as demonstrated by rainbow Turán graphs combined with
monochromatic and
LEX
colorings, respectively.
Tight asymptotics on these will be presented later.

4.3
Large stars

Next we consider two families
\mathcal{F}
defined in terms of stars
parametrized with the fixed integer
r\geq 2
:

•

\mathcal{F}(r)
— the graphs without
K_{1,r}
as a subgraph
(i.e.,
\Delta\leq r-1
);

•

\mathcal{F}^{*}(r)
— the graphs without
K_{1,r}
as an induced
subgraph.

The family
\mathcal{F}(r)
is hereditary, and
\mathcal{F}^{*}(r)
is induced-hereditary.

The following observation will be useful.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
|G|\geq r\alpha(G)+1
, then every induced subgraph
of
G
on
r\alpha(G)+1
vertices is non-
r
-colorable.

Proof.

One of the basic facts in graph coloring is that the product

\chi\alpha
is an upper bound on the order of the graph
in question.
In the current case if
H\subset G
is of order

r\alpha(G)+1
, then
\chi(G)\geq\chi(H)\geq\lceil\,|H|/\alpha(H)\rceil=r+1
.
∎

Since all
(r-1)
-degenerate graphs are
r
-colorable, it
follows that every non-
r
-colorable graph
G
must contain an
induced subgraph with minimum degree at least
r
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose
m\geq 2
and

\lceil\,|G|/m\rceil\geq r\alpha(G)+1
.
Then, for any fixed
r\geq 2
, both
f(n,G|\mathcal{F}(r))
and

f(n,G|\mathcal{F}^{*}(r))
exceed
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{m+1})
.

Proof.

Consider the
RTDL
(n,m)
pattern.
Some vertex class of its rainbow core
T_{n,m}

contains at least
r\alpha(G)+1
vertices, hence by the
preceding lemma a non-
r
-colorable
H\subset G
occurs.
Consequently a graph
H^{*}\subset G
with minimum degree
at least
r
is present in that class.
Let
v
be the top vertex of
H^{*}
in the corresponding
LEX
order.
Then
v
has at least
r
neighbors from
H^{*}
in lower indices
of
LEX
and hence induces a monochromatic
K_{1,r^{\prime}}
edge
class with
r^{\prime}\geq r
in
G
, which is not a member of
\mathcal{F}(r)
.
∎

This result shows that already with the partial information

|G|>r\alpha(G)
a quadratic growth of
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
follows.
However, for an asymptotically tight formula one needs
to know more details about
G
.
A result of this kind is Theorem 5.6 in
[
6
]
.

4.4
Connectivity

Requiring all members of
\mathcal{F}

to be connected is a very strong condition with substantial
impact on the behavior of
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.
We demonstrate this fact with two results.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be obtained from the family of all connected graphs
by deleting all complete graphs
K_{r}
with
r\geq 3
.
Then, for every
k\geq 4
we have

f(n,K_{k}|\mathcal{F})=\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k-1})+2
.

Proof.

To prove that
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k-1})+2
is a lower bound
on
f(n,K_{k}|\mathcal{F})
, we take the
RTEM
(n,k-2)
coloring.
Denote by

the vertex classes of its rainbow

R\cong T_{n,k-2}
, and by
c
the color assigned to the edges of
K_{n}-R
.
Then any copy of
K_{k}
either has at least two vertices in each
of two classes
V_{i},V_{j}
, or has at least three vertices in a
class
V_{i}
.
In the former case the color class

induces a disconnected

, which is not a member of
\mathcal{F}
; and in the
latter case it induces a

with

, which is
not in
\mathcal{F}
either.

For the matching upper bound we observe that the inequality

f(n,K_{k}|\mathcal{F})\leq\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{k})
holds due to the definitions,
and it is known that
\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{k})=\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k-1})+2
.
This was proved for large

in
[
12
]
, and for all

n\geq k
in
[
23
]
.
∎

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the family of all connected bipartite graphs.
If
\chi(G)=k\geq 4
and
G
contains an induced
forest
T
such that
\chi(G-T)=k-1
, then

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k-1})+2\leq f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\leq(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k-1})
.

Proof.

The lower bound is obtained in the same way as above:
we take the
RTEM
(n,k-2)
coloring with rainbow

R\cong T_{n,k-2}
and additional color
c
.
Then clearly
\chi(G)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k-2}\chi(G_{i})
.
Since
\chi(G)=k
, and
R
has only
k-2
vertex classes,
any copy of
G
in
K_{n}
either contains
edges from at least two classes of
R
, making the

c
-edge-class of
G
disconnected, or contains a
non-bipartite
c
-colored subgraph in one class of
R
.
Hence, in either case, color

induces a subgraph of
G

which is not a member of
\mathcal{F}
.

For the asymptotic upper bound we apply Theorem
3.4
and
find a clean
(k-1)
-partite grapk
K\cong K_{t,\dots,t}
,
where
t=|G|
(in fact a very generous choice for

)
and the edge coloring inside each vertex class is

LEX
.

Inside the first vertex class of

we select a

which is either monochromatic or rainbow, hence a member
of
\mathcal{F}
in either case.
This is trivial to do if the class is monochromatic or rainbow;
and can also be done via Proposition
4.4
if it is

-colored.
And then

is extendable to an
\mathcal{F}
-decomposed copy of

,
using a proper number of vertices from each further class of

to form the vertex classes of an
\mathcal{F}
-colored
copy of

.
∎

4.5
Disconnectedness

The notion considered here is complementary to the one in the
previous subsection, but again quadratic growth will be observed.
Let us recall for
p\geq 5
that
\mathrm{Lr}(n,K_{p})\sim\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\lceil p/2\rceil})
, which means the family

\mathcal{M}:=\{mK_{2}:m\geq 1\}
of matchings in the terminology
of the
f
function.
It turns out that quadratic growth order remains the same if

\mathcal{M}
is supplemented with all disconnected graphs, but on the
other hand the same asymptotics remain valid if just
K_{2}
is
taken together with a single suitably chosen disconnected graph.
Note that the family
\mathcal{F}^{*}
below contains
\mathcal{M}
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
p,s
be integers where
p\geq 5
and
2\leq s\leq p-2
.
Further, let
\mathcal{F}
be the family of all disconnected graphs
without isolated vertices, and set
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\mathcal{F}\cup\{K_{2}\}
.
Then both
f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F}^{*})
and
f(n,K_{p}|\{K_{2},K_{s}\cup K_{p-s}\})

are at least
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil})+\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil

and at most
(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\lceil p/2\rceil})
.

Proof.

Since both
K_{2}
and
K_{s}\cup K_{p-s}
are members of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
, by
Proposition
2.1

(i)
it suffices to prove the lower
bound for
\mathcal{F}^{*}
and the upper bound for
\{K_{2},K_{s}\cup K_{p-s}\}
.

For
\mathcal{F}^{*}
we can take the
RTDM
(n,\lceil p/2\rceil-1)
coloring.
Then any copy of
K_{p}
contains at least three vertices in
some vertex class of the rainbow
T_{n,\lceil p/2\rceil-1}
,
inducing a color which defines a connected (complete) subgraph
in
K_{p}
and therefore not a member of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
.
Hence, more than
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil})+\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil-1
colors
are needed to force the presence of a suitable copy of
K_{p}
.

For
\{K_{2},K_{s}\cup K_{p-s}\}
we apply Theorem
3.4
.
If more than
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\lceil p/2\rceil})+h(n)
colors are
used, we find a clean rainbow
K_{p,\dots,p}
with
r=\lceil p/2\rceil

homogeneously colored vertex classes
V_{1},\dots,V_{r}
of size
p
each.
If at least one class
V_{i}
is rainbow, we take it as
K_{p}
.
If at least two classes
V_{i},V_{j}
are monochromatic in the same
color, we take
s
vertices from
V_{i}
and
p-s
vertices from

V_{j}
; those
p
vertices induce a required copy of
K_{p}
,
in which one color class is
K_{s}\cup K_{p-s}
and the
other edges have their private colors.
In any other case we can take a rainbow matching of size

\lceil p/2\rceil
whose edges are inside mutually distinct
vertex classes.
Since
K_{p,\dots,p}
is rainbow and clean, this yields a rainbow
copy of
K_{p}
.
∎

The other side of the coin is when only matchings of relatively
small sizes are allowed, nothing else.
The result for this sparse small family
\mathcal{F}^{*}_{r}
gives a smooth
transition from the local anti-Ramsey number
\mathrm{Lr}(n,K_{p})\sim\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\lceil p/2\rceil})
to the (standard) anti-Ramsey number

\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{p})\sim\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{p-1})
.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer, and let
\mathcal{F}^{*}_{r}=\{mK_{2}\mid 1\leq m\leq r\}
be the family of matchings of size at most
r
.
If
r\geq 2
and
p>2r
, or
r=1
and
p\geq 4
, then

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{p-r})+2\leq f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F}^{*}_{r})\leq(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{p-r})\,.

The same bounds are valid if
\mathcal{F}^{*}_{r}
is supplemented with all
non-matching triangle-free graphs (connected and disconnected
as well).

Proof.

For the lower bound we now take the
RTEM
(n,p-r-1)
color pattern.
In this case a complete subgraph satisfying the conditions of
g

cannot meet a vertex class of the rainbow core
T_{n,p-r-1}
in
more than two vertices, because a monochromatic
K_{s}
with
s>2

is not allowed in
\mathcal{F}^{*}_{r}
, nor in any triangle-free graph.
Hence, the largest complete subgraph having its color partition
with members of
\mathcal{F}^{*}_{r}
and/or non-matching
triangle-free graphs can have at
most
p-1
vertices, because it can meet only
r
classes of the
rainbow
T_{n,p-r-1}
in two vertices, and contain only one
vertex from each of the other
p-2r-1
classes.
Hence, we need at least
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{p-r})+2
colors in order to
guarantee a
K_{p}
subgraph compatible with
\mathcal{F}^{*}_{r}
for sure.

For the upper bound we apply Theorem
3.4
for the
particular case of
K\cong K_{2,\dots,2}
with
p-r
vertex classes.
As it has been proved, to obtain a clean rainbow
K
it suffices to have
no more than
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{p-r})+h(n)
colors, where
h(n)=o(n^{2})
.
Keeping
r
vertex classes of
K
, and selecting one vertex from
each of its other
p-2r
vertex classes, an
\mathcal{F}^{*}_{r}
-colored
copy of
K_{p}
is obtained, regardless of the color distribution on
the edges contained in the
r
selected vertex classes of
K
.
∎

We conclude this section with a finite mixture of connected and
disconnected graphs, mentioned already at the end of
Section
5
.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
k\geq 2
be any integer and
\mathcal{F}(k)
the family of
all graphs on at most
k
vertices.
Then for every
p\geq k+2
we have
f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F}(k))=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{p-k+1})
.

Proof.

For a lower bound we take the
RTEM
(n,p-k)
coloring of
K_{n}
.
In a complete subgraph the edges from the big color class can
span at most
k
vertices.
Even when they belong to the largest vertex class of the rainbow

T_{p-k}\subset K_{n}
, only one additional vertex can be
taken from each of the other
p-k-1
vertex classes.
Hence, no
K_{p}
compatible with
\mathcal{F}(k)
exists.

For an upper bound we apply Theorem
3.4
.
In any edge coloring of
K_{n}
with more than
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{p-k+1})+h(n)
colors
we find a clean rainbow
K=K_{k,1,\dots,1}\cong K_{p}-K_{k}
.
No matter how the big vertex class is edge-colored, it is
compatible with
\mathcal{F}(k)
, because its colors do not appear in
K
.
∎

5
Stability concerning chromatic number

In
[
6
]
, a graph was defined to be stable with respect
to a hereditary family

\mathcal{F}
, if
\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}}}(G)=\chi(G)
; that is, if
\chi(G-D)=\chi(G)
holds whenever
D\subset G
and
D\in\mathcal{F}
.
This notion allows us to prove that any (not necessarily
hereditary)
\mathcal{F}
allows infinitely
many graphs
G
to have
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
with quadratic growth,
unless
\mathcal{F}
contains graphs that are very dense.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
k\geq 3
be any fixed integer, and
\mathcal{F}
any family
of graphs that contains
K_{2}
.
If there is a threshold
n_{0}
such that

\max\{e(D):D\in\mathcal{F},\,|V(D)|=n\}<n^{2}/k^{2}
holds
for every
n>n_{0}
, then there exist infinitely many graphs

G
with
\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}}}(G)=\chi(G)=k
, and all those
G
have

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k})+2\leq f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\leq(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k})
.

Proof.

We prove that the complete
k
-partite graphs
K=K_{p,\dots,p}

satisfy the conditions for all
p
sufficiently large.
Consider any
D\in\mathcal{F}
.
Each edge in any copy of
D
in
K
is contained in
p^{k-2}

cliques
K_{k}
of
K
.
By assumption, if
kp>n_{0}
then any
D\in\mathcal{F}
of order at most

kp
can have fewer than
(kp)^{2}\!/k^{2}=p^{2}
edges.
Hence, removing all the edges of any
D
, only fewer than
p^{k}

copies of
K_{k}
can be destroyed.
Consequently
K_{k}\subset K-D
and
\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}}}(K)=k
are valid.

Concerning the inequalities on
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
, for the lower bound
we can take the
RTEM
(n,k-1)
pattern.
Recall that here the rainbow
T_{n,k-1}
has a
monochromatic complement.
Since the large color class of
E(K_{n})
can accommodate only one
color class of
G
, say
F\in\mathcal{F}
, the remaining
k
-chromatic

G-F
should be part of the
(k-1)
-chromatic rainbow
T_{n,k-1}
, which is impossible.
On the other hand, since
K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
and
\chi(G)=k
, we have

f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\leq\mathrm{Ar}(n,G)\leq\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{|G|,|G|,\dots,|G|})+1\leq(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{k})

by the Erdős–Stone–Simonovits
theorem, hence proving the claimed upper bound.
∎

An interesting example is the family
\mathcal{F}
of stars.
It is hereditary, hence the methods developed in
[
6
]

perfectly handle it.
Moreover it is sparse, with
e(D)=|V(D)|-1
.
On the other hand it is worth observing that the condition

\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}}}(G)=\chi(G)=k
holds if and only if
G
is a

k
-chromatic graph which is not vertex-critical.
Further, with respect to rainbow coloring the condition
“vertex-critical” is replaced with “edge-critical”.

Example 5.2
.

A simple natural case is provided by the collection of all graphs
on
k
vertices (
k\geq 3
),
to which we shall return in Section
4.5
.
Also with respect to this family, there exist infinitely many
graphs satisfying the stability property described in
Theorem
5.1
.

The following very rich class of families may be viewed as a
generic case for which the principle of stability applies.

Example 5.3
.

Let
H
be any bipartite graph.
Consider the vamily
\mathcal{F}
of all graphs not containing
H

as a subgraph.
Since
H
is bipartite, we have
\mathrm{ex}(n,H)=o(n^{2})
, hence the
conditions of Theorem
5.1
hold true for every
k
and
every
n>n_{0}(H,k)
.

6
Excluding just one graph: Complement of exact piercing

In this section we consider the most flexible cases possible,
namely where the color classes can induce anything but just one
excluded graph.
These results, when combined with the complementary property
(Proposition
2.2

(ii)
), determine exact piercing

g(n,K_{p}|G)
tightly if
G
is not a star, and asymptotically
if
G
is a star
K_{1,t}
, for
t\geq 2
and
p\geq 2t+1
.

Formally, for a graph
H\neq K_{2}
, denote by
\mathfrak{F}(\neg H)
the
family of all finite graphs without isolates, except
H
.
It turns out that there is a substantial distinction whether

H
is a star or a non-star graph.

Theorem 6.1
.

(Excluding one star.) Let
p>t\geq 2
be any integers.

(i)

If
p\geq 2t+1
, then

f(n,K_{p}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg K_{1,t}))=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/t\right\rceil})
.

(ii)

If
t+1\leq p\leq 2t
, then

f(n,K_{p}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg K_{1,t}))\leq O(n^{2-1/t})
.
Moreover, for all
n\geq p\geq t+1
,

f(n,K_{p}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg K_{1,t}))\geq(p-t-1)(n-p+t+1)+\binom{p-t-1}{2})+1
.

Proof.

(i)
The lower bound
f(n,K_{p}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg K_{1,t}))\geq\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/t\right\rceil})+n-\left\lceil p/t\right\rceil+1

is obtained from the composite color pattern
RTDL
(n,\left\lceil p/t\right\rceil-1)
.
Indeed, among any
p
vertices at least
t+1
belong to the
same vertex class of
T_{n,\left\lceil p/t\right\rceil-1}
, and then
LEX
induces a forbidden monochromatic class
K_{1,t}
.

The upper bound

f(n,K_{p}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg K_{1,t}))=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/t\right\rceil})
,
that matches the lower bound in its asymptotics,
follows by Theorem
3.4
.
In any coloring with that many colors, we can find a clean
complete
\left\lceil p/t\right\rceil
-partite rainbow subgraph in which all
vertex classes have size
t
, hence leaving no room for a
monochromatic
K_{1,t}
.

(ii)
By the same method, applying Theorem
3.4
, we find a
clean rainbow
K_{t,t}
subgraph; to do so, we do not need
more than
O(n^{2-1/t})
colors.
Then take a whole vertex class of
t
vertices, and supplement it
with
p-t\leq t
vertices from the other class.
In the obtained
K_{p}
subgraph none of the color classes can be
a star
K_{1,t}
.

The claimed lower bound can be verified by taking a monochromatic

K_{p-t-1}
, and assigning a private color to each edge of

K_{n}-K_{p-t-1}
.
Then from any
p
vertices, at least
t+1
belong to
K_{p-t-1}
,
and that color class is a complete graph, not
K_{1,t}
.
Every other color class is a single edge.
∎

One may observe that in the range
t+1\leq p\leq 2t-2

it suffices to find a rainbow
K_{p-t,t}
and the strength
of Theorem
3.4
is not needed;
the result then follows just by the fact

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{t-2,t})=o(n^{2})
.

Theorem 6.2
.

(Excluding one non-star graph.) If
G
is not a star, and

(i)

either
G
is non-complete and
p\geq|G|
,

(ii)

or
G
is complete and
p>|G|
,

then
f(n,K_{p}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg G))=1
holds for every

n
sufficiently large with respect to
p
.

Proof.

Consider any edge coloring of
K_{n}
.
According to Theorem
3.1
, for every
n\geq n_{0}(p)
,
there occurs a
K_{p}
which is either monochromatic or rainbow
or
LEX
-colored.
Under the current assumptions, none of these patterns can induce
a color class isomorphic to
G
.
(If
G
is complete, then
p>|G|
, hence if the copy of
K_{p}

is monochromatic, then
G
is only a proper subgraph of
that color class.)
∎

7
Quadratic growth via integer sequences

Here we consider some rather peculiar classes, for which our
methods still allow to prove tight asymptotic results,
despite that those classes are very far from being hereditary.
The common principle in forming those classes is that we take
a finite or infinite sequence
S
and consider the family

\mathcal{F}\langle S\rangle
of all graphs whose number of edges is in
S
.

In the lower-bound constructions of this section it will suffice
to consider the
RTDL
(n,m)
colorings with suitably chosen
m
.

7.1
Graphs with square numbers of edges

Let us denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sq}}
the family of all graphs having a square
number of edges,

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sq}}=\{G\mid e(G)=k^{2},\ k\geq 1\}
.

Theorem 7.1
.

For every
p\geq 5
,

f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sq}})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil})
.

Proof.

For the lower bound

f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sq}})\geq\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil})+n-\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil+2
,
we take the
RTDL
(n,\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil-1)
coloring.
The number of colors is
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil})+n-\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil+1
.
From any
p
vertices, at least three belong to the same
vertex class of the rainbow
T_{n,\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil-1}
,
and then a color class
P_{3}
with just two edges
occurs, which is not allowed in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sq}}
.
(The color of that
P_{3}
does not occur in any other vertex class.)

We derive the upper bound
f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sq}})\leq(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil})
on applying Theorem
3.4
.
With the claimed number of colors, we can guarantee a complete

\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil
-partite rainbow subgraph
K
with
p
vertices
in every partite set, and each of those sets induces a
monochromatic or rainbow or
LEX
-colored
K_{p}
.

If a rainbow vertex class arises, then in the
K_{p}
induced
by it, every color class is a
K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sq}}
.
If at most one part is monochromatic, or if the monochromatic
parts have mutually distinct colors, then we select one edge
from each partite set of
K
, first from the monochromatic ones, and
then from the
LEX
parts, in a way that the selected matching
is rainbow.
This is possible because each
LEX
part offers
p-1
colors
for choice.

Finally, if there are two monochromatic parts
V_{i},V_{j}
of the
same color, let us write
p
in the form
p=2q-1+\epsilon
,
where
q=\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil
(hence
\epsilon=0
if
p
is odd, and

\epsilon=1
if
p
is even).
We compose
K_{p}\subset K_{n}
with
q
vertices from
V_{i}
,

q-1
vertices from
V_{j}
, and
\epsilon
vertices from a
third vertex class.
Then, in this
K_{p}
, each edge connecting distinct parts of
K

forms a color class
K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sq}}
, and the other edges form a
monochromatic
K_{q}\cup K_{q-1}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sq}}
with

\binom{q}{2}+\binom{q-1}{2}=(q-1)^{2}
edges.
Note that if
\epsilon=1
, then the last vertex can be selected
because the number of parts in
K
is
\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil\geq 3
,
as
p\geq 5
.
∎

7.2
Arithmetic progressions

Let
k\geq 2
be any integer, and consider the family
\mathcal{F}_{1,k}

of all graphs
G
with
e(G)\equiv 1
(mod
k
).
In particular,
\mathcal{F}_{1,2}
consists of all graphs whose number
of edges is odd.

Theorem 7.2
.

For every
p\geq 2k-1
if
k
is odd, and
p\geq 4k-1
if
k
is even,

f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F}_{1,k})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil})
.

Proof.

Also here, the lower bound is obtained from the

RTDL
(n,\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil-1)
coloring.
Then every
K_{p}\subset K_{n}
induces a monochromatic

P_{3}\notin\mathcal{F}_{1,k}
as a color class.
As a matter of fact, this construction works for all
p\geq 5
,
independently of the value of
k
.

For the upper bound we choose an integer
t
such that

p\geq t\geq\left\lceil(p+1)/2\right\rceil
and
\binom{t}{2}\equiv 1
(mod
k
).
Under the assumptions put on
p
, such a
t
always exists:

•

If
k
is odd, let
t\equiv k-1
(mod
k
).
Then
t(t-1)/2\equiv(k-1)(k-2)/2=(k^{2}-3k+2)/2=k(k-3)/2+1\equiv 1
(mod
k
),
because
(k-3)/2
is an integer for
k
odd.

•

If
k
is even, let
t\equiv 2k-1
(mod
2k
).
Then
t(t-1)/2\equiv(2k-1)(2k-2)/2=(4k^{2}-6k+2)/2=2k^{2}-3k+1\equiv 1
(mod
k
).

Observe that the interval

Now, if the number of colors in

-partite, clean, rainbow subgraph

∎

In case of

numbers of edges, it can be proved that the general asymptotic

Theorem 7.3
.

For every

f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F}_{1,2})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil})

Proof.

Our general Theorem

Then

subgraph without a 2-edge

Turning to the asymptotic upper bound, for

refer to the preceding proof.
Namely, in this case the value

.
But this method does not work for

Instead, for

If a rainbow part occurs, we supplement it with one vertex

∎

8
Towards subquadratic growth

Certainly, if
\mathcal{F}
is the family of all graphs, then we have

f(n,G|\mathcal{F})=1
for all
G
and all
n\geq|G|
.
Further, if
K_{2}
is not a member of
F
, then
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})

doesn’t exists, as shown by the rainbow coloring of
K_{n}
.
On the other hand, we have seen many classes of families
\mathcal{F}

for which
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
is a quadratic function of
n
.
In this context the results of this short section give
contributions to the following natural problem.

Problem 8.1
.

Characterize the families
\mathcal{F}
minimal under inclusion,
for which
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
is subquadratic for every
G
.

Based on observations from erlier sections, we can
propose the following approach.

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be obtained from the family of all graphs by
omitting all forests except stars.
Equivalently,
\mathcal{F}
is the union of

•

the family of all graphs containing a cycle, and

•

the family of all stars.

Then the following holds.

(i)

If
G
is acyclic, then
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})=O(n)
.

(ii)

Otherwise
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})=1
for all
n

large enough.

Proof.

If
G
is an acyclic graph, then, as
K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
, we can
apply the general inequality chain

f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\leq\mathrm{Ar}(n,G)\leq\mathrm{ex}(n,G)+1=O(n)

valid for all graphs except the very last step, which is
now applicable under the assumption that
G
is a forest,
hence its Turán number is linear in
n
.
Otherwise, if
G
contains a cycle, we assume that
n
is large
and apply Theorem
3.1
.
Then any edge coloring of
K_{n}
contains a
K_{|G|}
which is
either monochromatic or rainbow or
LEX
-colored.
Monochromatic
G
is in
\mathcal{F}
since
G
has a cycle.
In rainbow
G
each color class is
K_{2}
, a member of
\mathcal{F}
.
In
LEX
-colored
G
all color classes are stars, which are
members of
\mathcal{F}
.
∎

A further result offers a possibility to verify growth rates
in the entire range between constant and quadratic:

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
h(n)
be a positive integer function.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be obtained from the family of all graphs by
omitting all
H
such that
\mathrm{Ar}(n,H)\leq h(n)

and
H
is not a star.

(i)

If
G\notin\mathcal{F}
, then

f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\leq h(n)
.

(ii)

If
G\in\mathcal{F}
, then
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})=1

for all large
n
.

Proof.

If
G\notin\mathcal{F}
then
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\leq\mathrm{Ar}(n,G)\leq h(n)

holds by definition.
So, assume
G\in\mathcal{F}
.
According to Theorem
3.1
, for
n
large enough,
with any number of colors in
K_{n}
there is either a
monochromatic copy of
G
which is a member of
\mathcal{F}
,
or rainbow copy of
G
whose color classes are single edges
hence members of
\mathcal{F}
, or
LEX
-colored whose classes
decompose
G
into stars which are members of
\mathcal{F}
.
∎

9
The piercing number

In this section we study the function
g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.
Recall that
K_{2}\notin\mathcal{F}
is assumed in the definition of
g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.
As a preliminary observation, we state the following simple inequality.

Proposition 9.1
.

For every graph
G
and family
\mathcal{F}
, we have
g(n,G|\mathcal{F})<\mathrm{Ar}(n,G)
.

Proof.

In every coloring with at least
\mathrm{Ar}(n,G)
there occurs a
rainbow copy of
G
.
Since
K_{2}\notin\mathcal{F}
, this copy violates the conditins on
g
.
∎

9.1
Non-quadratic piercing

The next result presents a class of instances which
does not admit quadratic growth.

Theorem 9.2
.

Let
H
be a connected bipartite graph of order

|H|=t+1\geq 4
.
If
2t\geq p\geq t+1
, then

g(n,K_{p}|\langle H\rangle)=O(n^{2-\epsilon})
for some

\epsilon=\epsilon(H)>0
.
In particular,
g(n,K_{p}|\langle P_{q}\rangle)=o(n^{2})
and

g(n,K_{p}|\langle K_{1,q-1}\rangle)=o(n^{2})
whenever

p\leq 2q-2
.

Proof.

Assume first that
H
is 2-edge-connected.
In this case we apply the fact that

\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{t,t})\leq\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{t,t})\leq O(n^{2-1/t})
,
hence
\epsilon(H)=1/t
is a proper choice.
If an edge coloring uses more than that many colors, a
rainbow
K^{\prime}\cong K_{t,t}
occurs, say with vertex classes
A,B
.
Let then
K\cong K_{p}
,
V(K)\subset V(K^{\prime})
, such that the
edge set of
H
can be packed inside
E(K)\cap E(K^{\prime})
.
(That is, the
p
vertices of
K
are suitably distributed
between
A
and
B
.)
This can be done, since
|H|=t+1\leq p\leq 2t
.

There is not enough space for a copy of
H
inside
A
or
B
,
hence a monochromatic copy of
H
should contain vertices
in both parts of the rainbow
K^{\prime}
.
But 2-edge-connectivity requires at least two edges between

A
and
B
, thus no monochromatic
H
can occur.

Assume now that
H
is connected but not 2-edge-connected, i.e.,
it contains a bridge.
Using Theorem
3.4
we find a clean copy
K^{\prime}
of

K_{t,t}
; and choose
K\cong K_{p}
as above.
If
H^{\prime}\cong H
and
H^{\prime}\subset K
, both sides of
K^{\prime}
must contain
vertices of
H^{\prime}
.
Now, since
H
is connected, there is an edge

e\in E(K^{\prime})\cap E(H^{\prime})
, and the color of
e
is not repeated
inside
K
because
K^{\prime}
is rainbow and clean.
Consequently,
K
does not contain any monochromatic subgraph
from
\langle H\rangle
, and the upper bound follows.
∎

We note that the assumptions of the theorem cannot be improved.
If
p>2t
is allowed, then even dropping the requirement that

H
is bipartite, we shall see that already the densest case

H=K_{t+1}
yields quadratic growth for
g(n,K_{p}|\langle H\rangle)

(and then of course every subgraph of
K_{t+1}
does the same).
Also, connectivity is substantial: If
t=2s-1
and
H=sK_{2}
,
let
p=t+2=2s+1
.
Then
g(n,K_{p}|\langle H\rangle)>\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{3})=\left\lfloor n^{2}\!/4\right\rfloor
.

9.2
Piercing by small graphs

In the following, we determine the exact values for some further
families
\langle F\rangle
generated by small graphs
F
.

The following example shows that the upper bound
in Proposition
9.1
is tight.

Proposition 9.3
.

For every
n
we have
g(n,K_{3}|P_{3})=g(n,K_{3}|\langle P_{3}\rangle)=n-1
.

Proof.

Since
g(n,K_{3}|P_{3})\leq g(n,K_{3}|\langle P_{3}\rangle)
, it suffices to prove

g(n,K_{3}|P_{3})\geq n-1
and
g(n,K_{3}|\langle P_{3}\rangle)\leq n-1
.
The required lower bound is shown by the
LEX
-colored
K_{n}
,
where any three vertices induce a
K_{3}

in which the two color classes are
K_{2}
and
P_{3}
.
On the other hand, in the rainbow
K_{3}
all color classes are
single edges
K_{2}\notin\langle P_{3}\rangle
.
Thus, the upper bound follows from the fact
\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{3})=n
,
that was proved in
[
12
]
.
∎

Theorem 9.4
.

For every
n\geq 5
,
g(n,K_{4}|\langle 2K_{2}\rangle)=g(n,K_{4}|\langle P_{4}\rangle)=n+1
; and
g(4,K_{4}|\langle 2K_{2}\rangle)=5
,
g(4,K_{4}|\langle P_{4}\rangle)=4
.

Proof.

The smallest cases
g(4,K_{4}|\langle 2K_{2}\rangle)=5
,
g(4,K_{4}|\langle P_{4}\rangle)=4

are obviously valid, as shown by a monochromatic
2K_{2}
or
P_{4}

supplemented with its rainbow complementary graph using new colors.
For larger
n
, note that
2K_{2}\subset P_{4}
implies

g(4,K_{4}|\langle 2K_{2}\rangle)\geq g(4,K_{4}|\langle P_{4}\rangle)
.
Then a general construction showing the lower bound
n+1
for
both of them is demonstrated for every
n\geq 5

by the coloring of
K_{n}
where a Hamiltonian cycle
C_{n}
is
rainbow-colored and its complement is monochromatic in a new color.
In the big monochromatic edge class any four vertices contain

P_{4}\in\langle 2K_{2}\rangle
as a subgraph,
hence every copy of
K_{4}
is pierced by
P_{4}
in
K_{n}
.

In the rest of the proof, for simplicity let us abbreviate

g(n,K_{4}|\langle 2K_{2}\rangle)
as
g(n)
.
For the upper bound
g(n)\leq n+1
we apply induction on
n
.
We we clearly have
g(4)\leq 5
because the rainbow
K_{4}
with
6 colors is not pierced.
So, let
n>4
be any larger integer, assuming that the theorem
has been proved for
n-1
.

Consider an extremal coloring of
K_{n}
.
From
[
7
]
we recall the notion of critical colors:
A color
c
is critical at a vertex
v
if all edges of color
c

are incident with
v
.
It means that the color class in question is either a star
centered at
v
,
or a single edge, which is then critical at its both ends.
The theorem follows by the simple induction
g(n)\leq g(n-1)+1

if there is a vertex incident with at most one critical color.

We observe that each vertex has exactly two incident critical colors.
Suppose for a contradiction that
v_{0}
has at least three;
say, the edges
v_{0}v_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
have color
i
critical.
In this case consider the copy of
K_{4}
induced by
\{v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
.
The colors
1,2,3
do not occur inside
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
because
they are critical at
v_{0}
, and there is not enough space
inside
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
to accommodate a
2K_{2}
in another color.
Hence, the coloring does not satisfy the requirements for
g
.

In fact the above argument gives more:
The two critical color classes at each
v
are single edges,
because it does not matter if
v_{0}v_{2}
and/or
v_{0}v_{3}
has
color 1, still there is no room for a monochromatic
2K_{2}

inside
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
.
So, each critical color is an edge, being critical at its both ends.
Thus, the number of critical edges equals
n
, and those edges form a graph in which each component is a cycle.

If one of those cycles is a triangle, say
C=v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}
,
then we select any other
v_{0}
and observe that

\{v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
violates the conditions on
g

because the colors incident with
v_{0}
are distinct from
the ones connecting
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
to each other, and
their common
v_{0}
does not make
2K_{2}
possible.
Hence, each cycle has length at least 4.

We prove that all non-critical edges have the same color,
consequently there are just
n+1
colors altogether.
Let
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
be any three consecutive vertices along a cycle
(hence
v_{1}v_{2}
and
v_{2}v_{3}
are two adjacent critial edges),
and let
v_{0}
be any fourth vertex.
Now the only way to satisfy the conditions for
g

on
\{v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
is that the two edges

v_{0}v_{2}
and
v_{1}v_{3}
have the same color, because the two
critical colors are not incident with
v_{0}
.
It means that all non-critical edges incident with any one
vertex (in the present example
v_{2}
) have the same color.
But then this color is the same for all vertices, because
the non-critical edges form a connected graph
(of diameter 2) whenever
n
exceeds 4.
∎

Theorem 9.5
.

For every
n
,
g(n,K_{4}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)=n
, and
g(n,K_{4}-e|\langle K_{3}\rangle)=1
.

Proof.

We begin with the simpler case,
K_{4}-e
.
Of course, the monochromatic
K_{n}
satisfies the conditions,
hence
g(n,K_{4}-e|\langle K_{3}\rangle)\geq 1
.
Consider any edge coloring
\psi
of
K_{n}
with more than one color.
Then there exist three vertices
x,y,z
such that

\psi(xy)\neq\psi(yz)
.
Take a fourth vertex
w
.
The color of
wy
differs from at least one of
\psi(xy)
and

\psi(yz)
, say
\psi(wy)\neq\psi(yz)
.
Now the copy of
K_{4}-e
whose edges are
wy,wz,xy,xz,yz
contains
no monochromatic triangle, consequently
g(n,K_{4}-e|\langle K_{3}\rangle)<2
.

A rainbow star
K_{1,{n-1}}
supplemented with a monochromatic

K_{n-1}
in a new color on its leaves is a piercing because
every
K_{4}\subset K_{n}
has at least three vertices in the
monochromatic part.
This yields
g(n,K_{4}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)\geq n
for every
n\geq 4
.

For the proof of the upper bound
g(n,K_{4}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)\leq n
, we apply
induction on
n
.
The inequality is clearly true for
n=4
because there can occur
at most three further colors beside the monochromatic
K_{3}

needed for piercing.
We may also artificially extend the bound for smaller values of
n
,
admitting
g(n,K_{4}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)=\binom{n}{2}
for
n=1,2,3

(i.e.,
g(1)=0
,
g(2)=1
,
g(3)=3
).

Let
n>4
and consider any edge coloring
\psi
of
K_{n}
with
n

or more colors.
Since
\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{3})=n
, we can find a rainbow
K_{3}
, say with the
three vertices
x,y,z
where the colors are
\psi(xy)=a
,

\psi(yz)=b
,
\psi(zx)=c
.
Supplementing this
K_{3}
with any further vertex
v
there must
occur a monochromatic triangle.
Hence, the other
n-3
vertices can be partitioned into three sets

A,B,C
where
A=\{v\mid\psi(vx)=\psi(vy)=a\}
and
B,C

are defined analogously with respect to colors
b
and
c
.

We assume without loss of generality that
A\neq\varnothing
; one or both
of
B
and
C
may be empty.
However, any two nonempty sets from
A,B,C
are joined only by
edges whose colors are from
a,b,c
.
Indeed, if
v\in A
,
w\in B
, and
\psi(vw)=d\notin\{a,b,c\}

held, then
vxzw
would be a rainbow 4-cycle (having colors

a,c,b,d
in this order), leading to the contradiction that the

K_{4}
induced by these four vertices contains no monochromatic
K_{3}
.

The fact that no colors other than
a,b,c
can occur on edges of
types
A
–
B
,
A
–
C
and
B
–
C
, has the particular
implication that if
|A|=1
then

g(n,K_{4}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)\leq g(n-1,K_{4}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)+1
,
because removing the single vertex of
A
we can destroy only one
color, namely that of the edge from
A
to
z
.

Consider now the set
A
, and assume
|A|>1
.
If it has a vertex
v
such that
\psi(vz)=d
is a new color,
then let
w
be any vertex in
A-v
.
We look at the
K\cong K_{4}
induced by
\{x,z,v,w\}
.
Removing
v
or
w
from
K
, there remain the distinct colors

a,c
, hence
K
is not pierced in this way.
If piercing is achieved by removing
z
, then we must have

\psi(vw)=a
; and if it is by the removal of
x
, then
color
d
remains present, hence the piercing color is then
forced to be
\psi(vw)=d
.
Consequently, a new color
d
between
A
and
K
incident with a
vertex
v
forces
v
to be incident with only the colors

a,b,c,d
in the entire
K_{n}
.
Thus,
g(n,K_{4}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)\leq g(n-1,K_{4}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)+1\leq n
follows
by induction applied to
K_{n}-v
.

Otherwise, if all edges between
A
and
K
are in the colors

a,b,c
, consider the subgraph induced by
A
.
Its coloring is supposed to be a piercing, hence at most

g(|A|)\leq|A|
colors occur inside
A
, due to the
induction hypothesis.
Moreover only the colors
a,b,c
connect
A
with
B\cup C\cup K
.
Applying a large induction jump now from
K_{n}-A
to
K_{n}
,
we obtain
g(n,K_{4}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)\leq g(n-|A|,K_{4}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)+|A|\leq n
.
∎

Replacing
K_{3}
with
P_{3}
, the growth order jumps from linear
to
n\sqrt{n}
; here we can derive estimates which are tight
apart from a multiplicative constant.

Theorem 9.6
.

g(n,K_{4}|\langle P_{3}\rangle)=\Theta(n^{3/2})
, and also the smaller
class
\langle\{K_{3},P_{4}\}\rangle
generates the same growth order

g(n,K_{4}|\langle\{K_{3},P_{4}\}\rangle)=\Theta(n^{3/2})
.

Proof.

Since
\langle P_{3}\rangle
is obtained from
\langle\{K_{3},P_{4}\}\rangle
by
supplementing the latter with
P_{3}
and
K_{1,3}
,
we have
g(n,K_{4}|\langle P_{3}\rangle)\geq g(n,K_{4}|\langle\{K_{3},P_{4}\}\rangle)
.
Then the upper bound
O(n^{3/2})
is easily obtained by

g(n,K_{4}|\langle P_{3}\rangle)<\mathrm{Lr}(n,K_{4})\leq n\left\lceil\sqrt{2n}\,\right\rceil
, applying the
corresponding estimate from
[
7
]
.
For the lower bound we take
C_{4}
-free bipartite graphs
B_{n}

whose numbers of edges grow with
n^{3/2}
.
(For instance, if
q
is a prime power, then the point-line
incidence bipartite graph on
2(q^{2}+q+1)
vertices is

(q+1)
-regular and
C_{4}
-free.)
Let
\psi
be the edge coloring of
K_{n}
with rainbow
B_{n}

and its monochromatic complement in a new color.
If four vertices are distributed as
3+1
or
4+0
between the
two classes of
B_{n}
, then the corresponding copy of
K_{4}

contains a monochromatic
K_{3}\in\langle P_{3}\rangle
.
And if the distribution is
2+2
, then the induced subgraph of

B_{n}
cannot be
K_{2,2}=C_{4}
, hence an edge from the big
color class must occur, which is extendible to a monochromatic

P_{4}\in\langle P_{3}\rangle
.
This implies
g(n,K_{4}|\langle\{K_{3},P_{4}\}\rangle)\geq e(B_{n})+1
, completing the
proof of the theorem.
∎

9.3
The method of graph packing

The method introduced in this subsection is based on the
following notion.

Definition 9.7
.

(Packing graphs.) Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be two graphs on
p
vertices.
We say that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
pack (in
K_{p}
) if there exist
bijections
\eta_{i}:V(G_{i})\to V(K_{p})
for
i=1,2
such that
the induced mappings on the edge sets satisfy

\eta_{1}(E(G_{1}))\cap\eta_{2}(E(G_{2}))=\varnothing
.

Notation.

For a graph
F
we denote by
\langle F\rangle
the family of all graphs
that contain
F
as a subgraph.
Analogously, for a collection
\{F_{1},\dots,F_{k}\}
of graphs,

\langle F_{1},\dots,F_{k}\rangle
denotes the family of those graphs which
contain at least one
F_{i}
as a subgraph (
1\leq i\leq k
).

Proposition 9.8
.

(Packing Lemma.) Let
p
be an integer, and let
H
and
Q
be given graphs,
such that
|H|,|Q|\leq p
.
If all
H
-free graphs of order
p

(also those having some isolated vertices) pack with
Q

in
K_{p}
, then
g(n,K_{p}|\langle Q\rangle)\geq\mathrm{ex}(n,H)+1
.
More generally, if
\mathcal{H}
is a family of graphs, such that
all graphs of order
p
not containing any members of
\mathcal{H}
pack
with
Q
in
K_{p}
, then
g(n,K_{p}|\langle Q\rangle)\geq\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{H})+1
.

Proof.

We take a rainbow extremal graph for
H
,
with
\mathrm{ex}(n,H)
edges and that many distinct colors, and
make its complementary graph monochromatic in a new color
c
.
Consider any
K\subset K_{n}
,
K\cong K_{p}
, and let

F_{c}\subset K
be its subgraph whose edges have color
c
,
with
V(F_{c})=V(K)
.
The complementary subgraph
K-F_{c}
formed by the rainbow edges is

H
-free, hence it packs with
Q
in
K_{p}
.
Thus,
Q\subset F_{c}
, and consequently the color class
F_{c}
in

K
is a member of
\langle Q\rangle
, satisfying the conditions for
g
.
The situation in the more general case of
\mathcal{H}
is similar:
we can take a rainbow extremal graph for
\mathcal{H}
with a
monochromatic complement in a new color
c
,
and observe that in any copy of
K_{p}

the ranbow subgraph contains no member of
\mathcal{H}
, hence
a copy of
Q
occurs in the
c
-colored subgraph of
K
.
∎

We will apply the following classic result of this area.

Theorem 9.9
.

(Bollobás and Eldridge
[
5
]
)

Suppose that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are graphs with
n
vertices,

\Delta(G_{1}),\Delta(G_{2})<n-1
,
e(G_{1})+e(G_{2})\leq 2n-3
,
and
(G_{1},G_{2})
is none of the following pairs:

(2K_{2},K_{1}\cup K_{3})
,
(2K_{1}\cup K_{3},K_{2}\cup K_{3})
,

(3K_{2},2K_{1}\cup K_{4})
,
(3K_{1}\cup K_{3},2K_{3})
,

(2K_{2}\cup K_{3},3K_{1}\cup K_{4})
,
(4K_{1}\cup K_{4},K_{2}\cup 2K_{3})
,

(5K_{1}\cup K_{4},3K_{3})
.
Then
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
pack.

An application of this theorem will be given in
Proposition
9.11
below.

Proposition 9.10
.

(Basic lower-bound construction.) For a given
p
and sufficiently large
n
, let
R
be a

(2p-2)
-regular graph with girth
g>2p
.
Let
\psi
be the edge coloring of
K_{n}
composed from rainbow

R
and its monochromatic complement assigned with a new color
c
.
Then every
K\subset K_{n}
,
K\cong K_{2p}
, contains a
monochromatic
pK_{2}
(in color
c
).
As a consequence,
g(n,K_{2p}|\langle pK_{2}\rangle)\geq(p-1)n+1
holds
for every
n\geq n_{0}(p)
.

Proof.

Since the girth of
R
exceeds
2p
, any
K
(copy of
K_{2p}
)
induces an acyclic graph
R_{K}
with edge set
E(R)\cap E(K)
,
that has at most
2p-1
edges.
Further,
\Delta(K_{R})\leq 2p-2
as
K_{R}\subset R
.
Now we have
e(K_{R})+e(pK_{2})\leq 2p-1+p=3p-1\leq 2(2p)-3=4p-3
for
p\geq 2
, and of course

\Delta(pK_{2})=1
.
Thus, none of the forbidden cases Theorem
9.9
occurs,
so a monochromatic
pK_{2}
packs with
K_{R}
.
This proves the lower bound on
g(n,K_{2p}|\langle pK_{2}\rangle)

because
(2p-2)
-regular graphs of girth larger than
2p

exist whenever
n
is large enough with respect to
p
,
by the theorem of Erdős and Sachs
[
10
]
.
∎

The lower-bound construction is applicable not only for matchings
but also for many more graphs.

Theorem 9.11
.

Let
H
be a graph with
p
vertices (isolates allowed) and
at most
p-2
edges.
If
n
is sufficiently large,
n>n_{0}(p)
, then

g(n,K_{p}|\langle H\rangle)>(p-2)n/2
.

Proof.

Similarly to the previous proof, we take a rainbow graph
R
of
order
n
, which is
(p-2)
-regular if
pn
is even, or one
vertex has degree
p-3
and all the others have degree
p-2

if
pn
is odd; and the girth of
R
is larger than
p
.
If
n
is large enough with respect to
p
, such a graph
R
exists.
Make the complementary graph
\overline{R}
monochromatic in a
new color.
Then any
p
vertices induce a forest in
R
, which packs with

H
due to Theorem
9.9
.
Indeed, in the list of forbidden pairs of Theorem
9.9
,
precisely the seven graphs
2K_{2}
,
2K_{1}\cup K_{3}
,
3K_{2}
,

3K_{1}\cup K_{3}
,
2K_{2}\cup K_{3}
,
4K_{1}\cup K_{4}
,

5K_{1}\cup K_{4}
are sparse enough to play the role of
H
,
but each of these has a pair that contains a
K_{3}
, hence
cannot arise from
R
.
Consequently, in every copy of
K_{p}
, the subgraph induced by the
big color class is a member of
\langle H\rangle
, and so the conditions
for
g(n,K_{p}|\langle H\rangle)
are satisfied.
∎

One may also observe that the lower-bound construction on

g(n,K_{4}|\langle 2K_{2}\rangle)
is a rather particular case of the above, putting
p=4
.

Next, we prove that the lower bound is tight apart from a
muliplicative constant of 2.

Proposition 9.12
.

For every
p
and
n
we have

g(n,K_{2p}|\langle pK_{2}\rangle)<(2p-2)n
.

Proof.

The assertion is clearly valid for
n=2p
, because there is
no room for
2p(2p-2)
colors in
K_{2p}
.
For larger
n
we prove the inequality
g(n,K_{2p}|\langle pK_{2}\rangle)\leq g(n-1,K_{2p}|\langle pK_{2}\rangle)+2p-2
,
hence applying induction.

Let
\psi
be any coloring of
K_{2p}
in
K_{n}
with

g(n,K_{2p}|\langle pK_{2}\rangle)
colors.
There can be no vertex incident with
2p-1
critical colors
(or more), because a rainbow star
of that many critical edges would not admit
a
K_{2p}
supergraph containing a monochromatic
pK_{2}
.
Hence, removing any one vertex we destroy at most
2p-2
colors,
and consequently obtain the required recursion.
∎

9.4
Blockers and anti-packers

In this subsection we further elaborate the use of graph packing as a tool in investigating
g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.

Definition 9.13
.

(Blocker.)
For a given
p\geq 3
and a graph
G
on at most
p
vertices,
a graph
H
is called
(G,p)
-blocker if, for any placement of
any copy of
G
and any copy of
H
in
K_{p}
,

|E(G)\cap E(H)|\geq 2
holds.
The graph
H
is a minimal
(G,p)
-blocker if no subgraph of
H

is a
(G,p)
-blocker.
Define
B(G,p)
as the family of all minimal
(G,p)
-blockers.

Definition 9.14
.

(Anti-packer.)
For a given
p\geq 3
and a graph
G
on at most
p
vertices,
a graph
H
is called
(G,p)
-anti-packer if there is no packing
of
H
and
G
in
K_{p}
;
that is, for any placement of
any copy of
G
and any copy of
H
in
K_{p}
,

|E(G)\cap E(H)|\geq 1
holds.
The graph
H
is a minimal
(G,p)
-anti-packer if no subgraph of
H

is a
(G,p)
-anti-packer.
Define
A(G,p)
as the family of all minimal
(G,p)
-anti-packers.

Surprisingly, sometimes anti-packers alone are strong enough to
determine the asymptotic behavior of
g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)
.
This fact is shown in the next result.

Theorem 9.15
.

Let
G
be a graph of order at most
p
.

(i)

If there exists a bipartite anti-packer

H\in A(G,p)
, then
g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)=O(n^{2-\epsilon})

for some
\epsilon=\epsilon(H)>0
.

(ii)

If the minimum chromatic number of anti-packers

H\in A(G,p)
is
q\geq 3
, then

g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)=(1+o(1)\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{q})
.

Proof.

We begin with the upper bound, concerning which
(i)
and
(ii)

can be handled essentially together.
Let
H
be an anti-packer of minimum chromatic number,
q\geq 2
.
We are going to apply Theorem
3.4
and its notation,
here writing
r:=q
.
Let
\psi
be any edge coloring of
K_{n}
, using more than

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{q})+h(n)
colors, where
h(n)=o(n^{2})
, if
q\geq 3
.
If
q=2
, we assume that
\psi
uses

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{s,s})+1
colors, for a sufficiently large
s
.
This means
O(n^{2-\epsilon})
colors with
\epsilon=\epsilon(s)>0

if
q=2
, and a quadratic number of colors if
q\geq 3
.

Since
\chi(H)=q
, there exists a clean rainbow complete

q
-partite graph
Q\subset K_{n}
,
Q\cong K_{t,\dots,t}
,
with vertex classes
V_{1},\dots,V_{q}
such that
H\subset Q
holds.
We fix the position of
H
in
Q
, and extend
H
to a copy

K\cong K_{p}
with
V(H)\subset V(K)\subset V(Q)
.
We claim that this
K
violates the conditions on
g
, namely

K
does not contain
G
as a monochromatic subgraph.
Indeed,
G
must share an edge
e
with

H
, for otherwise
G
would pack with
H
,
contradicting the assumption that
H
is an anti-packer.
Further, it is impossible for a monochromatic
G
to have more
than one edge in
Q
, because the edges of
Q
have mutually
distinct colors.
But then
G-e
has all its edges inside the color classes
V_{i}

of
Q
, whose colors
cannot be the same as
\psi(e)
, because
Q
is clean.
This contradicts the assumption that
G
is monochromatic.

For the other side of
(ii)
we take the
RTEM
(n,q-1)
coloring
of
K_{n}
, whose core is a rainbow
R\cong T_{n,q-1}
.
Recall that
R
is a
(q-1)
-partite graph.
Consider any copy
K\subset K_{n}
of
K_{p}
, and
let
H
be its intersection
with
R
, where
V(H)=V(K)
and
E(H)=E(K)\cap E(R)
.
Since
\chi(H)\leq\chi(R)=q-1
and the minimum chromatic,
number in
A(G,p)
is
q
, it follows that
H
packs with
G

inside
K
.
But the complement of
R
is monochromatic in
RTEM
(n,q-1)
.
Thus, every copy of
K_{p}
contains a monochromatic copy of
G
,
implying
g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)>|E(R)|=\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{q})
.
∎

Corollary 9.16
.

If
G
is a connected graph of order at least
p/2+1
, or
disconnected but has a component on at least
p/2+1
vertices,
then
g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)=o(n^{2})
.

Proof.

The complete bipartite graph
K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil,\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor}
is an
anti-packer for
G
, because
p/2+1>(p+1)/2\geq\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil
,
therefore even the larger vertex class is not big enough for
accommodating
G
.
∎

In Theorem
9.15
the chromatic number was the
relevant parameter.
In general, the anti-Ramsey number is useful from both sides, as shown next.

Proposition 9.17
.

\mathrm{Ar}(n,A(G,p))\leq g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)<\mathrm{Ar}(n,B(G,p))
.

Proof.

Let
\psi
be any edge coloring of
K_{n}
using at least

\mathrm{Ar}(n,B(G,p))
colors.
By definition, there is a rainbow copy
H^{\prime}\subset K_{n}
of some

H\in B(G,p)
under
\psi
, say inside the complete subgraph

K^{\prime}\subset K_{n}
,
K^{\prime}\cong K_{p}
.
In order to satisfy the conditions of the
g
function, this
K^{\prime}

should contain a monochromatic copy
G^{\prime}
of some member of
\langle G\rangle
.
However, since
H
is a
(G,p)
-blocker, it blocks not only
G

but also all members of
\langle G\rangle
.
Hence, if a required
G^{\prime}
existed, we would then have

|E(G^{\prime})\cap E(H^{\prime})|\geq 2
, yielding at least two distinct
colors in
G^{\prime}
, a contradiction.
This implies
g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)<\mathrm{Ar}(n,B(G,p))
.

To prove the lower bound
g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)\geq\mathrm{Ar}(n,A(G,p))
,
choose an edge coloring of
K_{n}
with
\mathrm{Ar}(n,A(G,p))-1

colors, without any rainbow subgraphs
H\in A(G,p)
.
Select just one edge from each color class, and make the rest
monochromatic with one extra color.
In this way an edge coloring
\psi
of
K_{n}
with
\mathrm{Ar}(n,A(G,p))

colors is obtained, in which any
K_{p}
contains from the
original colors only a subgraph that packs with
G
.
Thus, the big monochromatic class in any
K_{p}\subset K_{n}
is a
member of
\langle G\rangle
, and the requirements of function
g
are
satisfied.
∎

Example 9.18
.

If
p=4
and
G=K_{3}
, then
A(G,4)=\{2K_{2}\}
and

B(G,p)=\{C_{4}\}
.
In this case
\mathrm{Ar}(n,A(G,p))=2
,
g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)=n
,

\mathrm{Ar}(n,B(G,p))=\left\lfloor 4n/3\right\rfloor
.

This example already indicates that
\mathrm{Ar}(n,A(G,p))
and

\mathrm{Ar}(n,B(G,p))
are not very close to each other.
Nevertheless, they may serve as useful estimates on

g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)
from at least one side.
We begin with a case whose
g
values are farthest from the
anti-Ramsey numbers of blockers.
Let us recall that
K_{2}\notin\mathcal{F}
is assumed in the definition of

g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
, therefore the notation
g(n,K_{2}|K_{2})
is meaningless.

Theorem 9.19
.

Apart from the single exception that
g(4,2K_{2}|2K_{2})=3
,
all graphs

have
g(n,G|G)=1
for all

.

Proof.

Using more than three colors in
K_{4}
would yield a 2-colored

, which is not allowed by the conditions on

.
On the other hand, the proper edge 3-coloring of
K_{4}
paints
each
2K_{2}
monochromatic.

Assume
G\neq 2K_{2}
, and suppose that an edge coloring
\psi

of
K_{n}
makes all copies of
G
monochromatic.
If
G
is not a matching, we choose a
P\subset G
,
P\cong P_{3}
;
let
E(P)=\{e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}\}
.
Identifying an edge
e_{1}
of
K_{n}
with
e^{\prime}
in suitably
chosen copies of
G
, the image
e_{2}
of
e^{\prime\prime}
must get

\psi(e_{2})=\psi(e_{1})
.
Next, identifying
e^{\prime}
with any of those

, any incident edge

e_{3}
in the role of

must get
\psi(e_{3})=\psi(e_{2})
.
Thus, the entire
K_{n}
is monochromatic under
\psi
.

The situation is similar if

is a matching other than
2K_{2}
,
or
G=2K_{2}
and

.
In that case all edges disjoint from
e_{1}
must get

\psi(e_{2})=\psi(e_{1})
, hence inducing a

entirely painted with
\psi(e_{1})
.
But now

holds, therefore any edge
e_{3}
connecting

with the big monochromatic

is disjoint from an edge
of this

, and is extendible to a copy of

.
It follows that also the connecting edges must have the same

uses only one color.
∎

Applying blockers, we can obtain a linear or subquadratic upper bound on

g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)
under some very natural simple assumptions.

Theorem 9.20
.

(Minimum and maximum degree.)
Let
G
be a graph with
p\geq 3
vertices, and let
n\to\infty
.

(i)

If
G
has no isolated vertices, i.e.,
\delta(G)\geq 1
,
then
g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)=O(n)
.

(ii)

If
G
has isolated vertices, but

\Delta(G)\geq p/2
, then
g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)=O(n^{2-\epsilon})
for some
\epsilon=\epsilon(G)>0
.

Proof.

Let us begin with the proof of
(ii)
, as it is much simpler.
As in Corollary
9.16
, we see
that
K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil,\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor}
is an anti-packer of
G

whenever
\Delta(G)\geq p/2
, because
a maximum-degree vertex has too many neighbors to fit all in its
vertex class inside
K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil,\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor}
.
Thus, Theorem
9.15

(i)
can be applied for the current
(ii)
.

Turning now to
(i)
, suppose first
\delta(G)\geq 2
.
Then
H=K_{1,p+1-\delta(G)}
is a blocker for
G
, as any
placement of
G
and
H
in
K_{p}
gives
|E(G)\cap E(H)|\geq 2
.
Hence, by Proposition
9.17
we obtain

g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)<\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{1,p+1-\delta(G)})
,
and the right-hand side is known to be linear in
n
.

Suppose next that
\delta(G)=1
holds and
G
is not a star.
Assume that the number of colors is at least
\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{1,s})
,
where
s
is chosen to be large enough to force a homogeneously
colored
K_{p}
on the leaves of a rainbow
K_{1,s}
,
as guaranteed by Theorem
3.1
.
If the homogeneous

is either rainbow or
LEX

we are done as we can take just those

vertices and no
monochromatic copy of

occurs in that copy of
K_{p}
.
Else, if

is monochromatic, then the rainbow star

attached to

can be reduced to

by dropping a vertex from

and eliminating
the shared color.
In this way the edges of

incident with the vertex mapped to
the center of the star have colors different from the colors of

no monochromatic

can arise in the modified copy of

.
Since the required

depends only on

, and

is linear in

Finally, assume

.
In this last case

is a blocker of

,
again known to be bounded above by

.
This completes the proof.
∎

9.5
Piercing by complete and nearly complete graphs

Next, we list some blockers for complete subgraphs.

Proposition 9.21
.

(i)

The graph
H(t):=C_{3}\cup tK_{2}

(ii)

The matching

(iii)

(v)

Generalizing

By definition, each of the graphs claimed to be blockers have

under the given conditions.
Each of them is a blocker, because

•

∎

The anti-Ramsey numbers of these graphs have been studied in

]

Theorem 9.22
.

Assume

(i)

For every fixed

(ii)

(iii)

If

Proof.

The upper bound of

follows from the quoted formula on

, by applying Proposition

with

.

The lower bound of

is obtained by taking a

Part

∎

Remark 9.23
.

The principle behind the lower bound in Theorem
9.22

(ii)
can be
extended from

to non-complete graphs

of order
q=p-t

in a slightly stronger form, as follows.
Choose an integer
s

, color the edges incident
with

specified vertices to obtain a rainbow

,
and use

.
In this way we obtain

\textstyle g(n,K_{p}|\langle G\rangle)\geq sn-\binom{s+1}{2}+g(n,K_{p-s}|\langle G\rangle)

.

A strong upper bound can be proved for

also in
the cases where a non-complete graph

differs from

only
with few edges.
For this purpose, we first prove the following auxiliary result.

Lemma 9.24
.

For any integer
k\geq 3
, every edge coloring of
K_{n}
with
at least
k
colors contains a rainbow subgraph
Q
with

e(Q)=k
and
|Q|\leq 2k-2
.
Moreover, the upper bound

is tight.

Proof.

There are two ways to construct a graph with
k
edges and
more than

vertices, namely

and
P_{3}\cup(k-2)K_{2}
.
Given a coloring
\psi
of

with at least
k

let

be a rainbow subgraph
with

edges and as few vertices as possible.
Then

because otherwise inserting
an edge between two edges of the
rainbow matching of size

and deleting the edge of the same
color from
kK_{2}
, the number of vertices would decrease.
(If the color of the inserted edge does not appear in

, we can
delete any third edge distinct from the two connected ones.)
Also,

because in a similar way inserting
an edge from the center of

to an edge of

and deleting the edge of the same color from

Thus,

.

Tightness is shown by
K_{2k}
where
\psi
consists of a rainbow
perfect matching and its monochromatic complement in a new color.
∎

Now we are in a position to prove the following upper bound.

Theorem 9.25
.

Let
t\geq 1
and
p\geq 2t+2
be integers, and let

H
be a graph with at most
p
vertices.
If
e(K_{p})-e(H)\leq t

We shall apply the principle that any graph

with

edges and at most

vertices is a blocker of
any graph with
p
vertices and at least

To prove the theorem, suppose for a contradiction
that there exists an edge coloring
\psi

of
K_{n}
with at least

colors, satisfying the requirements
for

.
On applying Lemma

we find a rainbow subgraph

with

edges and

.
Choose a subgraph

such that

,
and assume that a

with

is monochromatic.
There are at most

, therefore

But

∎

Returning to

:
In the range
p\leq 2t+1
, or equivalently
p\geq 2q-1
,
the behavior of

is substantially different.
We put the result in a very general form, to include all
connected graphs.

Theorem 9.26
.

Let
p\geq 2q-1
, and let
H
be any connected graph of order

.
Then

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil\frac{p}{q-1}\right\rceil})+\left\lceil\frac{p}{q-1}\right\rceil-1\leq g(n,p,q)\leq g(n,K_{p}|\langle H\rangle)\leq(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil\frac{p}{q-1}\right\rceil})\,.

In particular, the same asymptotic formula

(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/(q-1)\right\rceil})
applies for paths and
cycles, determining
g(n,K_{p}|\langle P_{q}\rangle)
and
g(n,K_{p}|\langle C_{q}\rangle)
.

Proof.

We take the
RTDM
coloring with the
k
-partite rainbow Turán graph and
k
monochromatic classes with distinct colors,
where
k=\left\lceil p/(q-1)\right\rceil-1
.
Then, since
(q-1)k<p
, among any
p
vertices, at least
q

of them are from the same vertex class, hence they induce
a monochromatic copy of a member of
\langle K_{q}\rangle
which is a
subfamily of
\langle H\rangle
.
This implies the validity of the lower bound.

The asymptotic upper bound is derived from Theorem
3.4
.
With reference to its notation, more than

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/(q-1)\right\rceil})+h(n)
colors (

)
guarantee the presence of a clean rainbow
K_{\left\lceil p/(q-1)\right\rceil}
-partite
Turán graph with

vertices in each of its classes.
Keeping
p
of those

vertices, we obtain a

which does not contain any monochromatic connected subgraph
of order

, hence violating the conditions on the

∎

Apart from the completely settled
g(n,4,3)=n
, the case of

p=2q-2
is more complicated.
To handle it, we prove a result on the following anti-Ramsey number.

Theorem 9.27
.

For every
k\geq 2
and
n>n_{0}(k)
,

kn-(k+2)(k-1)/2\leq\mathrm{Ar}(n,\{C_{2k+1}\cup K_{2},C_{2k+2}\})\leq kn\,.

Proof.

For the lower bound we take a complete monochromatic subgraph

K\cong K_{n-k}
and make its complement
K_{n}-K
rainbow with

k(n-k)+\binom{k}{2}=kn-\binom{k+1}{2}
new colors.
If a rainbow cycle does not use an edge from the big color class,
then the number of its vertices in
V(K_{n})\setminus V(K)
is not
smaller than that in
V(K)
, which means length at most
2k
.
If a rainbow cycle uses an edge from the big color class, then
length
2k+1
is possible, but such a cycle covers the entire

V(K_{n})\setminus V(K)
, therefore an additional
K_{2}
would
repeat the color of
K
.
Likewise, any cycle longer than
2k+1
contains at least two
edges of the monochromatic
K
, hence cannot be rainbow.

For the upper bound let
\psi
be any edge coloring of
K_{n}
with
at least
kn
colors, that contains no rainbow
C_{2k+1}\cup K_{2}

and no rainbow
C_{2k+2}
.
We first observe that
kn
exceeds

\mathrm{Ar}(n,C_{2k+1})=(\frac{2k-1}{2}+\frac{1}{2k})\,n+O(1)

if
n
is large.
Thus, a rainbow cycle
C\cong C_{2k+1}
surely is present.
Denote
X:=V(C)
and
Y:=V(K_{n})\setminus V(C)
.
Inside
Y
every edge has its color from

\psi(C)
, otherwise a rainbow
C_{2k+1}\cup K_{2}
would exist.

Consider any vertex
y\in Y
.
At most
k
colors different from those in
\psi(C)
can occur
on the edges connecting
y
with
X
, otherwise there would be
two of them to two consecutive vertices of
C
, and so
C

would be extendable to a rainbow
C_{2k+2}
, a contradiction.
Consequently the number of colors used by
\psi
is at most

k\cdot|Y|+\binom{|X|}{2}=k(n-2k-1)+\binom{2k+1}{2}=kn
.
∎

This result is strong enough for the determination of

with high precision.

Theorem 9.28
.

If
p=2q-2
and

(p-q)n-\binom{p-q+1}{2}+1\leq g(n,p,q)\leq\mathrm{Ar}(n,\{C_{2p-2q+1}\cup K_{2},C_{2p-2q+2}\})\leq(p-q)n\,.

For simplification, let us denote

.
Then the lower bound is a consequence of Theorem

(ii)
, which is valid for all

.
For the upper bound we observe by Proposition

–
(v)
that both

and

are
minimal blockers for

.
Hence, Proposition

and Proposition

(iii)
imply

.
The explicit upper bound is then obtained from Theorem

.
∎

The obtained growth orders concerning
g(n,p,q)=g(n,K_{p}|\langle K_{q}\rangle)

can be summarized as follows.

Corollary 9.29
.

(Dichotomy.)
The function
g(n,p,q)
is linear in

if

, and
quadratic otherwise.

Using the principle of family complementaion as given in
Proposition

, tight results can be obtained also
for the function

.
We present two results of this kind as a closing of the current
subsection.

Theorem 9.30
.

Let
p\geq 2t+3
(
t\geq 1
),
and let
\mathcal{F}
be the family of all graphs not containing
K_{p-t}
.
Then, for
n>n_{0}(p)
sufficiently large,

f(n,K_{p}|\mathcal{F})=g(n,p,p-t)+1=t(n-t)+\binom{t}{2}+2
.

Proof.

The complement
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
of
\mathcal{F}
is the family

\langle K_{p-t}\rangle
, and of course
K_{p}\notin\mathcal{F}
.
Thus, the formula follows by Proposition
2.2

(ii)
and
Theorem

.
∎

A further interesting application of the complementarity
principle between the functions
f
and
g

is the problem considered in Theorem 5.10 of
[
6
]
.
Before stating the new formula, let us quote that result first.
Recall the definition
\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}}}(G):=\min_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\chi(G-F)
.
Let now
{\mathcal{F}^{*}}
denote the family of all triangle-free graphs.
Concerning the behavior of the
f
function with respect to

{\mathcal{F}^{*}}
it was proved in
[
6
]
that

\displaystyle f(n,K_{p}|{\mathcal{F}^{*}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}^{*}}}(K_{p})})

(1)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(1+o_{n}(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{(1/2+o_{p}(1))\sqrt{p\log p}}).

The rightmost expression was derived from the currently known
strongest upper bound on the chromatic number of triangle-free
graphs of given order, which is very hard to determine exactly.
We now prove the following simple formula.

Theorem 9.31
.

For the family
{\mathcal{F}^{*}}
of
K_{3}
-free graphs we have

f(n,K_{p}|{\mathcal{F}^{*}})=g(n,K_{p}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)+1=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil})
for every
p\geq 5
.

First proof.

Since the hereditary family
{\mathcal{F}^{*}}
contains all triangle-free
graphs, and
K_{3}\notin{\mathcal{F}^{*}}
, the complement

\overline{{\mathcal{F}^{*}}}
is the upward-closed family

\langle K_{3}\rangle
of all graphs containing a triangle.
Thus, according to Proposition
2.2
we have

f(n,K_{p}|{\mathcal{F}^{*}})=g(n,K_{p}|\langle K_{3}\rangle)+1
.
In this way asymptotic equality is implied by Theorem

9.26
above, putting
q=3
.

Second proof.

Despite that triangle-free graphs can have arbitrarily large
chromatic number, we prove that

\chi_{{}_{\mathcal{F}^{*}}}(K_{p})=\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil

(2)

holds for every
p
.
Let
G
be any graph of order
p
such that
\overline{G}
is
triangle-free.
Suppose for a condtradiction that
\chi(G)=k<p/2
.
Let
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}
be the color classes in a proper vertex
coloring with the minimum number of colors.
Since
2k<p
, some
V_{i}
has size greater than 3.
Thus,
K_{3}\subset K_{|V_{i}|}\subset\overline{G}
, a contradiction.
Consequently, Equality (
1
) implies the theorem
via (
2
).
∎

Theorem 9.32
.

If

and

-free graphs contains

Moreover, it is the complementary family of

.
Thus, we can apply Theorem

together with

for all

We have already seen by the smallest nontrivial example

that the growth-order
dichotomy of piercing
K_{p}
by complete subgraphs does
not remain valid in general for
g(n,K_{4}|\langle H\rangle)
.
In this section we take a closer look at this situation.

We begin with a little Ramsey-type lemma and its consequence on
cycle–tree packing.

Lemma 9.33
.

Let
a\geq b\geq 2
be integers.
In every red-blue edge coloring of
K_{a,b}
there is a
red
2K_{2}
or a blue
K_{1,a}
.

Proof.

If any two edges of a graph meet, then they form a star or a
triangle.
Hence, if the 2-coloring of
K_{a,b}
does not contain a red
2K_{2}
,
then the blue subgraph is obtained by deleting a star from it.
Consequently, at least
b-1\geq 1
vertices have blue degree
a
.
∎

In the present context the following implication of this lemma
will be important, because the condition
e(G_{1})+e(G_{2})\leq 2p-3

of Theorem
9.9
is not satisfied in the case under consideration.

Proposition 9.34
.

Let
T
be a tree or forest of order
p\geq 3
.
If
\Delta(T)\leq p-3
, then
T
and
C_{p}
pack in
K_{p}
.

Proof.

If
p=3
, then
T=3K_{1}
, and if
p=4
, then the largest
possible
T
is
2K_{2}
.
The complements of these graphs are
C_{3}
and
C_{4}
,
respectively, verifying the assertion for such small
p
.

If
p\geq 5
, we may restrict attention to trees, because if
T

has more than one component, we may connect two leaves of
distinct components by inserting a new edge,
without violating the maximum-degree condition.
Viewing
T
as a bipartite graph, let
A
and
B
be its vertex
classes; say,
|A|=a\geq|B|=b
.
We have
b\geq 2
because
T\neq K_{1,p-1}
.

Assume
T
embedded in
K_{a,b}
, all inside
K_{p}
.
Color the edges of
T
blue, and the edges of
K_{a,b}
red.
If there exist two red edges
e,e^{\prime}
, supplement them with
e
–
e^{\prime}

paths
P_{a}
and
P_{b}
of
K_{p}
inside
A
and
B
, respectively.
In this way a
C_{p}
is obtained, which is edge-disjoint from
T
.
Hence, it suffices to prove that such red
e,e^{\prime}
exist.

If there is no blue
K_{1,a}
, then there are red
e,e^{\prime}
by the
preceding lemma.
On the other hand, if there is a blue
K_{1,a}
, then we have

a\leq\Delta(T)\leq p-3
, hence
b\geq 3
.
More than one blue
K_{1,a}
centered in
B
would imply the
contradiction
K_{a,2}\subset T
.
Thus, omitting the vertex of degree
a
from
B
, we obtain a
subforest of
T
with more than one vertex in each vertex class,
without blue
K_{1,a}
, consequently containing a red
2K_{2}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 9.35
.

Piercing by the
4
-cycle for large enough

(i)

g(n,K_{p}|\langle C_{4}\rangle)=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/3\right\rceil})

(ii)

g(n,K_{p}|\langle C_{4}\rangle)=\Theta(n^{3/2})

(iii)

.

Proof.

Assume throughout the proof that

is sufficiently large
with respect to

.

(i)
We know that the

C_{4}
is not smaller than
that for
K_{4}
.
Hence we only need to prove

.
Using more than
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/3\right\rceil})+h(n)
colors, where

, by Theorem
3.4
we can find a clean
rainbow
K\cong K_{3,\dots,3}
with
\left\lceil p/3\right\rceil
vertex classes.
Inside

, none of the

subgraphs contains a monochromatic

.

(ii)
The upper bound follows by the fact

,
because extending a rainbow

to any coloring of
K_{6}
,
at most
2K_{3}
plus an edge can occur as monochromatic.
For a lower bound growing with

, we take

for

,

Make the edges of the complement

monochromatic
in a new color.
We prove that every

subgraph of this

has a

C_{4}
in the large color class.

Let
X,Y
be the vertex classes of

.
The vertex distribution of
K
between them is either

5+0
or
4+1
or

.
The first two obviously contain a monochromatic

, hence
also a
C_{4}
.
For the case of

, denote by

and

the

in the two classes of

Temporarily restricting attention to the edges of

, now

and

have either just one common neighbor

There can be at most two edges from

to

(one from

and one from

If both

are edges, then

are non-edges, so that

is a monochromatic

in

.
The situation is similar if both

are edges.
Finally, if

and

are edges of

is a

.

(iii)
The

spanned by a rainbow

cannot contain a

C_{4}
; this implies that
\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{1,3})-1
is
an upper bound on

The simple construction yielding the same value as lower bound
is the rainbow matching with

Clearly, any

in the big color class.
∎

Concerning longer cycles

, the following result determines
the exact value for all large

Theorem 9.36
.

For every fixed
p\geq 5
we have
g(n,K_{p}|\langle C_{p}\rangle)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{p-3}{2}\,n\right\rfloor+1

for all
n\geq 2p-5
.
Moreover, the equality
g(n,K_{p}|\langle C_{p}\rangle)=\left\lfloor\frac{p-3}{2}\,n\right\rfloor+1

is valid for every sufficiently large

.

Proof.

Observe that the star
K_{1,p-1}
is a minimal blocker for
C_{p}

in
K_{p}
, therefore
g(n,K_{p}|\langle C_{p}\rangle)\leq\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{1,{p-1}})-1
holds by Proposition
9.17
.
Jiang
[
16
]
and Montellano-Ballesteros
[
20
]
proved
the formula
\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{1,k})=\left\lfloor\frac{(k-2)n}{2}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{n}{n-k+2}\right\rfloor+\epsilon
,
where
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
.
If
n/2>k-2
, then the second term is equal to 1.
Thus, plugging in
k=p-1
, for all
n\geq 2p-5
we obtain that

\left\lfloor\frac{p-3}{2}\,n\right\rfloor+1

is an upper bound.

For the matching lower bound we can take a rainbow graph

G_{n}\subset K_{n}
of girth bigger than
p
,
with
e(G_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{p-3}{2}\,n\right\rfloor
and

\Delta(G_{n})=p-3
, and assign a new color
c
to its complement.
With reference to
[
10
]
we know that such graphs exist
for every

if
n
is even or
p
is odd; and a slight
modification of the proof implies the same for odd

also.

Any set

of

vertices induces a

, of maximum degree at most

in
G_{n}
.
According to Proposition
9.34
,

and

pack
inside

, which means that a monochromatic

in color

exists in every copy of

.
Hence, the number

\left\lfloor\frac{p-3}{2}\,n\right\rfloor+1
of colors is
a lower bound on

.
∎

This result determines
g(n,K_{p}|\langle C_{q}\rangle)
for

, whereas
the range

was settled asymptotically in
Theorem
9.26
, showing that it is essentially the
same as

and

.
For the remaining range

we prove relation with the
Turán numbers
\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{s,t})
of complete bipartite graphs,
where
p-q<s\leq t<q
.
The well known estimates are
\Omega(n^{2-\frac{2}{s-1}})\leq\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{s,t})\leq O(n^{2-\frac{1}{s}})
, and tightness of the
upper bound is proved for some small values of

.
In this way, improvements in the Turán-type problems on bipartite
graphs may also improve the numerical bounds in the next result.

Theorem 9.37
.

Let
p,q\geq 4
be integers, with
q<p\leq 2q-2
.

(i)

For cycles, there exist constants
c_{1}=c_{1}(p,q)>0

and
c_{2}=c_{2}(p,q)>0
such that

c_{1}n^{2-\frac{2}{p-q+1}}\leq g(n,K_{p}|\langle C_{q}\rangle)<\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{p-q+1,q-1})\leq c_{2}n^{2-\frac{1}{p-q+1}}
.

(ii)

For paths, there exists a constant
c=c(p,q)>0

such that

g(n,K_{p}|\langle P_{q}\rangle)\geq cn^{2-\frac{2}{p-q+2}}
.

Proof.

We relate the
g
-values in question to Turán numbers of complete
bipartite graphs.
First, observe that
K_{p-q+1,q-1}
is a blocker for
C_{q}
in
K_{p}

in the given range of
p
with respect to
q
, because

p-q+1\leq q-1
holds, hence any copy of
C_{q}
inside

V(K_{p-q+1,q-1})
contains vertices in both partite sets of

K_{p-q+1,q-1}
.
Thus, the upper bound given in
(i)
follows by
Proposition
9.17
.

For the lower bound in
(i)
we consider the family

\mathcal{K}=\{K_{p-q,q-2},K_{p-q+1,q-3},\dots,K_{\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor-1,\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil-1}\}\,.

We claim that
g(n,K_{p}|\langle C_{q}\rangle)>\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{K})
.
From this, the standard lower-bound proof on

yields
the stated growth order from below.
As a matter of fact, instead of
\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{K})
one can also take
the bipartite Turán number of
\mathcal{K}

of edges in a subgraph

So, let

be either an extremal bipartite

Consider the edge coloring

where

.
Let us denote by

.

Consider any

in

.
Assume that

has
a

and

,

.
(To achieve this, rename

Further, restricting

Remark 9.38
.

The proof of Theorem

provides a method by which

9.7
Piercing by stars

As stars constitute another fundamental class of graphs, here we
consider the piercing problem for the family
\langle K_{1,q}\rangle

.
Let us recall from Theorem

Below we give more precise estimates for the entire range of

.

Theorem 9.40
.

The
g

\langle K_{1,q}\rangle
admits the following estimates.

(i)

If
p=q+1
, then
g(n,K_{p}|\langle K_{1,q}\rangle)
is

n+\binom{p-2}{2}-1\leq g(n,K_{p}|\langle K_{1,p-1}\rangle)<\mathrm{Ar}(n,C_{p})=(\frac{p-2}{2}+\frac{1}{p-1})\,n+O(1)\,.

(ii)

g(n,K_{p}|\langle K_{1,q}\rangle)<\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{q-1,p-q+1})=O(n^{2-\frac{1}{p-q+1}})
,

(iii)

If

being

(iv)

If

.

Proof.

(i)
The lower bound is obtained by modifying the

Inside
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{p-1}\}
we take a rainbow
K_{p-1}
, and
for every

with

we define a private color
assigned to all edges connecting

with vertices of smaller index.

The upper bound follows by Proposition
9.17
, observing
that

is a blocker for

in

.

(ii)
Also here, we apply Proposition
9.17
.
Generalizing the above example of

that was taken for

, observe that every spanning subgraph

with

Hence, we can take

(iii)
Here we apply Theorem
3.3
with the parameters

and

10
Concluding remarks and open problems

This present work is the third and last part of our trilogy on
some generalizations of anti-Ramsey theory.
The starting point was that in a rainbow edge-colored graph
every color class is a single edge.
Anti-Ramsey theorems ask for a tight lower bound on the number of
colors that guarantees, in every allowed coloring, the presence
of a subgraph of given type in which every color class consists
of just one edge.
Our general approach is to generalize the “single edge”
color class to specified conditions on the color classes
of the required subgraph.

The first part of the trilogy,
[
6
]
, introduced the
general problem and developed the theory for conditions
expressible in terms of hereditary graph properties.
The second part,
[
7
]
, defined seven natural classes of
subproblems, some of them expressible inside the “graph classes”
scenario, and some others related to popular types of graph coloring.
This current third part extends the panorama to graph properties
which are not supposed to be hereditary anymore.
We developed some useful methods for this general approach, and
established asymptotically tight results in several cases.
We also introduced a new anti-Ramsey-type invariant, showed that it is
strongly related to the former one by family complementation,
and derived many further tight asymptotic results.

Almost all formulas derived here deal with the functions

f(n,H|\mathcal{F})
and
g(n,H|\mathcal{F})
in the case of
H=K_{p}
, the
complete graph of given order.
In the first and second parts of the trilogy, many results are
given for
f(n,H|\mathcal{F})
with non-complete
H
, but mainly with
particularly defined
\mathcal{F}
(a considerable number of them, though).
A very natural direction of future research opens:

Problem 10.1
.

(i)

Develop the general theory to the involvement of graphs
H
other than complete graphs.

(ii)

Develop the theory to the involvement of families
\mathcal{H}
of graphs richer than a single graph
H
.

Moreover, so far the edge colorings of complete graphs have been considered.
However, Turán-type extremal theory has already been investigated
in the literature for the restricted universe of bipartite graphs
and also for the generalizations to hypergraphs, and more.
These directions offer a wide field of potential research in
anti-Ramsey theory, too.

Problem 10.2
.

Develop the theory for

•

bipartite graphs;

•

directed graphs;

•

signed graphs;

•

uniform hypergraphs.

10.1
General problems on growth order and function relations

Let us begin this subsection with some simple questions
concerning the behavior of function

.

Problem 10.3
.

Determine a condition which is necessary and sufficient for

.
(Cf. Remark

.)

Problem 10.4
.

Characterize the pairs

for which

,
or a little further

for

Problem 10.5

Determine a condition which is necessary and sufficient for

(Cf. Proposition

.)

It is worth emphasizing that the tight relation between

g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
and
f(n,G|\overline{\mathcal{F}})
allows translation of
results from
f
to
g
and also the other way round.
Some examples of this kind have been given in Section
9
,
but many more can be derived using this tool.

For the further discussion let us recall that
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})
is
defined if and only if
K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
,
and
g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
is defined if and only if
K_{2}\notin\mathcal{F}
.
So, given any
\mathcal{F}
, assuming that
\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})
is defined
for
\phi\in\{f,g\}
, it is uniquely determined whether

\phi=f
or
\phi=g
.
In the sequel
\phi
stands for any of
f
and
g
, provided that

\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})
is defined.

The most general problem concerning
\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})

is the following.

Problem 10.6
.

Determine the families
\Phi_{f}
and
\Phi_{g}
of functions

\eta=\eta(n)
for which there exists a graph
G

and a family
\mathcal{F}
of graphs such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})/\eta(n)=1

as
n\to\infty
.

Of course, the trivial identically zero function
P_{0}

(i.e.,
P_{0}(n)=0
for all
n
)
is a member of
\Phi_{g}\setminus\Phi_{f}
.
But we do not know which other functions are in the symmetric
difference of
\Phi_{f}
and
\Phi_{g}
.

Problem 10.7
.

Determine
\Phi_{f}\setminus\Phi_{g}
and
\Phi_{g}\setminus\Phi_{f}
.

From now on we assume without further mention that
\phi

determines a function different from
P_{0}
.

It is not known whether every
\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})
is proportional to
a real power of
n
at least in the asymptotic sense.

Problem 10.8
.

Prove or disprove that for every graph
G
and family
\mathcal{F}
of
graphs there exist constants
c=c(G,\mathcal{F})
and
d=d(G,\mathcal{F})

such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})}{c\cdot n^{d}}=1\,.

(3)

Motivated by this problem, we introduce the following notion.

Definition 10.9
.

(Feasible pair.)
We call a pair
(c,d)
of real numbers
c>0
and
0\leq d\leq 2

feasible if there exists a graph
G
and a family
\mathcal{F}
of
graphs for which
(\ref{eq:limit})
holds.
In that case we say that
G
and
\mathcal{F}
determine
(c,d)
.

More directly, we raise:

Conjecture 10.10
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is finite, and
\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})\to\infty
, then
G
and

\mathcal{F}
determine a feasible pair
(c,d)
for every
G
.

Currently we expect that the assumption
|\mathcal{F}|<\infty
is
superfluous in the conjecture.

Problem 10.11
.

Characterize the feasible pairs
(c,d)
for
\phi\in\{f,g\}
.

Translating to this language the results available in the anti-Ramsey literature, many examples of feasible pairs can be obtained.
In particular, since
\mathrm{Ar}(n,G)
is expressible in terms of
function
f
, and under some conditions the former has the
same asymptotics as
\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
with a suitably constructed

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, every Turán-type theorem exhibiting an explicit asymptotic
formula provides us with a feasible pair.

In the subquadratic world—which means
d^{\prime}<2
in terms of
feasible pairs
(c,d)
, or bipartite forbidden graphs in Turán-type
extremal theory—the following problem turned out relevant,
see Theorem
9.37
and its subsequent remark,
keeping also Theorem
9.15
in mind:

Problem 10.12
.

Investigate the role of edge connectivity number in the context
of
g(n,G|H)
for graphs
H
which admit a bipartite anti-packer
inside
G
.

Consider the largest possible value of
d
in a feasible pair,
d=2
.
In the non-degenerate case the Turán function depends only
on the chromatic number, therefore it
may even hold that for
d=2
all feasible
(c,d)
can be written
in the form
(\frac{r-2}{2r-2},2)
for some integer
r\geq 3
.
This question is open so far in general, nevertheless
Theorem
9.15
specifies a large class of instances for
which it is proved that other pairs
(c,d)
with
d=2
cannot exist.

The other extreme,
d=0
represents
\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})=O(1)
.
In this case we can prove that (
3
) is satisfied.

Theorem 10.13
.

If
\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})=O(1)
, then there is a constant
c
and a
threshold
n_{0}=n_{0}(G,\mathcal{F})
such that
\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})=c

for all
n>n_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
p:=|G|
.
Assuming
\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})=O(1)
, there exists a universal
upper bound say
k
on
\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.
Then also the isomorphism types of edge colorings of
K_{p}
with
at most
k
distinguished colors (not allowing the renumbering
of colors) is bounded, say it is at most
m(k)
.

We let
n^{\prime}=m(k)\cdot p+2k
.
With this choice, the following properties are valid in any
edge coloring
\psi
of
K_{n}
for any
n>n^{\prime}
:

1.

If more than
k
colors are used, then there exists a
vertex
w
such that
K_{n}-w
still contains at least
k
colors.

2.

If at most
k
colors are used, then there exists a
vertex
w
such that
K_{n}-w
still contains the same set of
color patterns induced by its copies of
K_{p}
as
K_{n}
does.
And, in particular, all colors of
\psi
are present
also in
K_{n}-w
.

Proof of 1.:

Assume that the number of colors used is
k+t
for some
t\geq 0
.
If a color class is a single edge or a star, mark the two ends
of that color or the center of the corresponding star with
the color.
There are at most
2k+2t
marks on the total of
n
vertices,
thus a vertex
w
marked with at most

\left\lfloor\frac{2k+2t}{n}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2k+2t}{2k+p}\right\rfloor\leq 1+\left\lfloor\frac{t-p/2}{k+1}\right\rfloor\leq t

exists.
Removing this
w
does not decrease the number of colors to
less than
k
.

Proof of 2.:

If
k^{\prime}
is the number of colors, then in the current case we have

k^{\prime}\leq k
and
m(k^{\prime})\leq m(k)
.
For each of the
m(k^{\prime})
possible coloring patterns
\pi
of
K_{p}
,
select a
p
-element subset
X_{\pi}
in which
\psi
induces
\pi
,
if
\pi
occurs in
K_{n}
.
We set
X:=\bigcup_{\pi}X_{\pi}
.
By the choice of
n
we have
n>|X|
.
Pick any vertex
w\in V(K_{n})\setminus X
.
By the choice of
X
, all
patterns incident with
w
have their copies in
K_{w}
.

If
\phi=f
, let
\psi
be any edge coloring of
K_{n}
using

k^{\prime}\geq f(n-1,G|\mathcal{F})
colors for
K_{n}
.
If
k^{\prime}\geq k
, a copy of
G
required for
f
exists by the
choice of
k
.
If
f(n-1,G|\mathcal{F})\leq k^{\prime}<k
, we remove
w
as guaranteed by
2.

Since at least
f(n-1,G|\mathcal{F})
colors remain, a required copy of

G
is present; and as this property holds also for

k^{\prime}=f(n-1,G|\mathcal{F})
, we obtain
f(n,G|\mathcal{F})\leq f(n-1,G|\mathcal{F})
.

If
\phi=g
, consider an edge coloring
\psi
of
K_{n}
with

k^{\prime}:=g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
colors, such that
\psi
realizes the
requirements of
g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.
Then of course
k^{\prime}\leq k
, by the choice of
k
.
Applying
2.
, we obtain a coloring of
K_{n}-w\cong K_{n-1}
,
still with
k^{\prime}
colors, in which all copies of
G
contain a
monochromatic copy of some
F\in\mathcal{F}
.
This implies
g(n-1,G|\mathcal{F})\geq g(n,G|\mathcal{F})
.

All the above imply that
(k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
with
k_{n}:=\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})

is an ultimately non-increasing sequence of nonnegative integers.
Thus, it is a constant for all
n>n_{0}
, and (
3
) is valid.
∎

Problem 10.14
.

Characterize the pairs
(G,\mathcal{F})
for which

\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})=O(1)
as
n\to\infty
.

Problem 10.15
.

Given an integer
c>0
, characterize the pairs
(G,\mathcal{F})

for which
\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})=c
for all
n
sufficiently large.
In particular, is
(k,0)
a feasible pair for every positive
integer
k
?

Perhaps the second part of this problem is not too hard if
k
is
very small.

Let us formulate a more explicit version of Problem
10.7
.

Problem 10.16
.

Are the feasible pairs the same for
f
and for
g
?

It seems plausible to guess that the answer is affirmative.
A partial evidence
is the following fact for
d>0
, by which this problem
seems to be much easier than the complete characterization of
feasible pairs.

Proposition 10.17
.

Assume that
\phi(n,G|\mathcal{F})\to\infty
as
n
gets large, and

(G,\mathcal{F})
determines the feasible pair
(c,d)
.
If
K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
and
G\notin\mathcal{F}
, or if
K_{2}\notin\mathcal{F}
and

G\in\mathcal{F}
, then
(c,d)
is a feasible pair also for

\{f,g\}\setminus\{\phi\}
.

Proof.

This follows by Proposition
2.2
, taking the family
complementary to
\mathcal{F}
.
∎

Let us recall Proposition
2.1
, which implies that
if
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
are any three nested families,
such that
(G,\mathcal{F})
and
(G,\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime})
determine the same pair

(c,d)
, then also
(G,\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
determines the same pair.
This fact motivates the following natural problem.

Problem 10.18
.

Given a graph
G
and the real numbers
c,d
with
c>0
and

0\leq d\leq 2
, find the inclusionwise minimal and maximal
families
\mathcal{F}
such that
G
and
\mathcal{F}
determine
(c,d)
.

Although not minimal or maximal, nevertheless many “small” and
“large” families can be observed in the three parts of our trilogy.
In this way the interested reader can state various “sandwich
theorems”, mostly in the quadratic scenario.
Here we give only one example.

Example 10.19
.

Let
G=K_{p}
,
p\geq 5
, and let
\phi=f
.
Search for families
\mathcal{F}
such that
(G,\mathcal{F})
generate

(c,d)=(\frac{r-2}{2r-2},2)
with
r=\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil
.
It is equivalent to the asymptotics

f(n,G|\mathcal{F})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil})
.

The extremely dense family
\mathfrak{F}(\neg P_{3})
obtained by the omission
of the single graph
P_{3}\cong K_{1,2}
satisfies the conditions,
undoubtedly it is a very transparent maximal family.

From the other side, there are sparse families with the same

f(n,G|\mathcal{F})=(1+o(1))\,\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/2\right\rceil})
.
Some examples are:

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sq}}
(the graphs with square number of edges),

\mathcal{F}_{1,k}
(the graphs whose number of edges is congruent to

1

modulo

k
,
or
\{mK_{2}\mid m\geq 1\}
(matchings), the latter being the
defining family of the local anti-Ramsey numbers
\mathrm{Lr}(n,G)
.
These families seem to be rather unrelated, so perhaps it is
rather hard to characterize the minimal classes generating
this partiular
(c,d)
.

Recall further that
\mathfrak{F}(\neg K_{1,t})
leads to the analogous function

\mathrm{ex}(n,K_{\left\lceil p/t\right\rceil})
, which means among other things that in case of

p/t=p^{\prime}/t^{\prime}
we arrive at the same asymptotics for

f(n,K_{p}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg K_{1,t}))
and
f(n,K_{p^{\prime}}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg K_{1,t^{\prime}}))
.
This is an additional indication that the problem of minimality
for
(c,d)
is really hard.

Note that this “sandwich” phenomenon is brand new in anti-Ramsey theory.
This possibility emerged only in the context of the functions
f

and
g
, because the classical concept of
\mathrm{Ar}(n,G)
is defined
purely in terms of
\mathcal{F}=\{K_{2}\}
.

10.2
Miscellaneous open problems

Concerning
f(n,K_{p}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg P_{3}))
the smallest possible instance is

f(n,K_{3}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg P_{3}))=n\,.

The upper bound is implied by

f(n,K_{3}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg P_{3}))\leq\mathrm{Ar}(n,K_{3})=n

is implied by the fact that any three vertices in the
LEX
pattern (using

a monochromatic
P_{3}
as a color class, hence not feasible
for
\mathfrak{F}(\neg P_{3})

However, already the second ssmallest case is far from being solved.

Problem 10.20
.

Prove or disprove that
f(n,K_{4}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg P_{3}))

n

So far our best lower bound on
f(n,K_{4}|\mathfrak{F}(\neg P_{3}))
is
2n-2
,
obtained by a rainbow star
K_{1,n-1}
together with the

LEX

K_{n-1}
subgraph induced by its leaves.
Our currently best upper bound grows with
O(n^{3/2})
,
derived from the presence of a clean
K_{2,2}
via Theorem
3.4
.
In fact the local anti-Ramsey function
\mathrm{Lr}(n,K_{4})=\Theta(n^{3/2})

is an upper bound on

, but the
lower-bound construction on
\mathrm{Lr}
is not applicable here.
We note that
2n-2
is the right value for
n=4
, because
a monochromatic
P_{3}\subset K_{4}
and four private colors
on the edges of
K_{4}-P_{3}
yield a 5-coloring
that does not satisfy the requirements on
