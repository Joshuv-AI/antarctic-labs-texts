Age‐Period‐Cohort Analysis of the Global Death Burden of Main Diseases Attributable to High Temperature in the Past 30 Years: Based on the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Zhikang Wang, Yifang Liu, Lve Deng, Ke Li, Jiawei Ren, Xuran Shi, Qi Wang, Junan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh temperatures pose a serious threat to human health. Quantifying the main disease death burden attributable to high temperatures can provide scientific evidence for policy development. Using Global Burden of Disease 2021 data, we identified the death burden attributable to high temperatures across all causes and determined the three leading causes. For each, we used age‐standardized mortality rates attributable to high temperature (HA‐ASMR) and estimated temporal trends via age‐period‐cohort modeling at global, regional, and national levels (1990–2021). Ischemic heart disease, stroke, and lower respiratory infections were the top three causes of deaths attributable to high temperature in 2021. Low‐middle Socio‐demographic Index (SDI) regions had the highest HA‐ASMR for ischemic heart disease (3.49, 95%UI: 0.96–6.57) and stroke (2.55, 95%UI: 0.77–5.01), whereas lower respiratory infections showed the highest HA‐ASMR in low SDI regions (2.52, 95%UI: 0.85–4.35). Age‐period‐cohort analysis generated net drift values for HA‐ASMR in high SDI regions, with values of 0.99% (95%CI: 0.60%–1.39%) for ischemic heart disease, 1.19% (95%CI: 0.99%–1.40%) for stroke, and 2.36% (95%CI: 2.01%–2.70%) for lower respiratory infections. Longitudinal age curves showed elevated mortality risks attributable to high temperature in populations over 60 years. Global period and cohort rate ratios for high‐temperature‐attributable ischemic heart disease mortality increased over time. Global ischemic heart disease and stroke deaths attributable to high temperature continued rising with sharply elevated risks in older adults, while lower respiratory infection deaths attributable to high temperature among youth in high‐income countries are increasing. Therefore, adaptation strategies must be specific to disease and age, varying by regional characteristics.

Introduction

The Sixth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) explicitly states that global warming is driving an evolutionary pattern of increased frequency and intensity in extreme high‐temperature events (IPCC, 2023). Report data indicate that the global average temperature rise by the end of this century is projected to reach 1.4°C–4.4°C compared to pre‐industrial levels, posing significant threats to both natural ecosystems and human societies. High‐temperature exposure impacts human health through multiple pathophysiological mechanisms (Ebi et al., 2021): it directly disrupts thermoregulation while increasing metabolic load on cardiovascular and urinary systems (Zhang et al., 2024), and may induce multi‐organ failure with potentially fatal outcomes (Wang et al., 2024). Multiple studies demonstrate that high temperature serves as a risk factor for various diseases (Achebak et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2024; X. Song et al., 2021). The Global Burden of Disease study (GBD) estimated that high temperatures caused approximately 310,000 deaths in 2019, accounting for 0.54% of global mortality (J. Song et al., 2021). Quantifying the diseases mortality burden attributable to high temperature is essential for improving public health risk awareness and providing evidence for climate‐adaptive public health policies (Vicedo‐Cabrera et al., 2021).

Current global burden of disease studies have several key limitations in assessing disease burden attributable to high temperatures. Previous analyses have often focused on single diseases or general descriptions of such burden, with limited attention to the leading diseases contributing the highest mortality burden attributable to high temperatures. Moreover, temporal trend analyses have rarely examined age, period, and cohort patterns in mortality attributable to high temperatures. Therefore, the objectives of this study are to characterize the overall burden of mortality attributable to high temperature across all causes, and then to further explore age, period, and cohort trends in such mortality for the three leading causes of death burden attributable to high temperature at the global, regional, and national levels.

Methods

Data Source

This study constituted a secondary analysis based on GBD2021 data. Coordinated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation at the University of Washington, GBD 2021 estimates health metrics for 371 diseases or injuries and 88 risk factors across 204 countries or territories from 1990 to 2021 through epidemiological data and modeling analysis. These model‐derived epidemiological estimates are built on multiple data sources and standardized statistical frameworks (GBD 2021 Causes of Death Collaborators, 2024; GBD 2021 Diseases and Injuries Collaborators, 2024; GBD 2021 Risk Factors Collaborators, 2024), with the overall aim to assess global disease burden patterns.

Previous studies have detailed the GBD2021 methodology (GBD 2021 Causes of Death Collaborators, 2024; GBD 2021 Diseases and Injuries Collaborators, 2024; GBD 2021 Risk Factors Collaborators, 2024). High temperature exposure is defined as daily mean temperature above the theoretical minimum risk exposure level (TMREL), the temperature associated with the lowest mortality risk. Importantly, TMREL is not a globally uniform value, and it varies across geographic locations and time periods. The disease burden attributable to high temperature estimation process involves three steps: first, constructing cause‐specific exposure–response curves for temperature and mortality; second, using daily temperature exposure, the TMREL, and the corresponding exposure–response functions to calculate excess risk; and third, converting this excess risk into a population attributable fraction and multiplying it by the GBD estimated death burden for the corresponding cause (GBD 2021 Risk Factors Collaborators, 2024). Consequently, the resulting annual burden estimates inherently reflect geographic and temporal variation in temperature exposure and the non‐linear nature of temperature–mortality relationships. GBD2021 calculates the Socio‐demographic Index (SDI) for each region, a composite health indicator reflecting socioeconomic status through fertility rates, educational attainment, and income levels (GBD, 2021). SDI values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater socioeconomic development. Regions and countries were stratified into SDI quintiles (GBD 2021 Diseases and Injuries Collaborators, 2024): High SDI (0.81–1), High‐middle SDI (0.71–0.81), Middle SDI (0.62–0.71), Low‐middle SDI (0.47–0.62), and Low SDI (0–0.47).

Disease Inclusion Criteria

This study focused on diseases with the highest mortality burden attributable to high temperature. The included diseases were ranked by the scale of global deaths burden attributable to high temperature, and the top three diseases were included as the core analysis objects: ischemic heart disease, stroke, and lower respiratory infection.

Study Population and Data Extraction

GBD2021 provides epidemiologic estimates of 88 risk factors and their health outcomes using data from 54,561 different sources. This secondary analysis focused on the global death burden of the main diseases attributed to high temperatures. It incorporates populations experiencing mortality from specific causes attributable to high temperature (explicitly stratified by demographic characteristics as defined in the data extraction section) across global, regional, and national epidemiological contexts.

We restricted the GBD2021 database search to 5‐year age groups (0–4 to 95+ years), covering 20 age groups with sex‐specific (male, female) and combined population data. We extracted both all‐cause and cause‐specific mortality estimates attributable to high temperature, accompanied by 95% uncertainty intervals (UI), across global, regional, and national levels during 1990–2021. The outcome metrics include mortality counts and rates, specifically the age‐standardized mortality rate (ASMR) for the total population.

Age‐Period‐Cohort Analysis

This study employed an age‐period‐cohort (APC) model to analyze temporal trends in mortality. The APC model decomposes variations in incidence or mortality rates into age effects, period effects, and birth cohort effects. However, a fundamental challenge with the traditional APC model arises from the exact linear dependency among the three time scales (cohort = period − age), which leads to non‐identifiability of the model parameters. Conventional approaches typically impose arbitrary constraints to achieve identifiability, but these constraints rely on unverifiable assumptions.

This study adopted an estimable functions approach grounded in generalized linear model theory (Rosenberg & Anderson, 2011). This method focuses on linear combinations of APC parameters that are invariant to underlying non‐identifiable transformations. By partitioning the effects into linear trends and orthogonal nonlinear components, the model separates the part uniquely determined by the data from the inherently ambiguous part. Although the individual slope parameters for age, period, and cohort cannot be separately identified, specific linear combinations—such as the net drift—are independent coefficients in the model and can be uniquely estimated from the data. The nonlinear components themselves are fully identifiable, as they describe local fluctuations orthogonal to the linear trends.

Based on these estimable functions, we derived several key measures: the net drift, representing the overall log‐linear annual percentage change in age‐standardized rates; local drifts, representing the annual percentage change for each age group. The net drift captures the overall temporal trend, and the local drifts represent the age‐specific temporal trends. The longitudinal age curve, describing age‐specific mortality rates for a reference cohort after adjusting for period and cohort effects; period rate ratios, comparing mortality across different periods relative to a reference period; and cohort rate ratios, comparing mortality across different birth cohorts relative to a reference cohort. These functions have been implemented in a publicly available web tool (Rosenberg et al., 2014). By focusing on these identifiable linear combinations, this approach enables robust inference on temporal patterns despite the inherent non‐identifiability of the model.

The data input into the APC model need to comply with specific conditions (Rosenberg et al., 2014). The age and period intervals must be equal. If a 5‐year age group is used, a 5‐year calendar period has to be used. In addition, we need to set the reference age, period, and cohort. The reference point setting does not affect the results (Rosenberg et al., 2014). In this study, we chose the initial period as the reference and set the age group “0–4” as the reference age, the year “1992–1996” as the reference period, and the reference cohort as the year “1987–1996.” Due to the lack of data on deaths from 0 to 14 years of age in ischemic heart disease attributable to high temperature, the reference age was set to “15–19” years, and the reference cohort was changed to “1972–1981” accordingly.

Statistical Analysis

The 95%UI for GBD 2021 indicators were derived via Monte Carlo sampling. Based on 1,000 independent repeated estimations, the 95% UI was determined using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting distribution (GBD, 2021 Causes of Death Collaborators, 2024). Differently, the 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to quantify the statistical uncertainty of effect sizes generated by age‐period‐cohort analysis. This study uses a two‐sided test with α = 0.05 as the level for statistical significance. A change or effect is considered statistically significant if the 95%CI of the effect size does not contain the corresponding null value (the null value is 0 for annual percentage change, and 1 for risk ratio). All rates are expressed per 100,000 population. Data processing, analysis, and visualization were performed by R (version 4.3.3).

Results

Global Burden of All‐Cause and Specific‐Cause Deaths Attributable to High Temperatures From 1990 to 2021

Globally, the age‐standardized mortality rate attributable to high temperatures (HA‐ASMR) remained stable from 1990 to 2021, decreasing slightly from 5.42 (95%UI: 3.56–7.65) in 1990 to 5.41 (95%UI: 3.51–7.56) in 2021. Table 1 shows the global burden of death from all relevant diseases attributable to high temperatures. In 2021, the three leading causes of mortality burden attributable to high temperatures were ischemic heart disease, stroke, and lower respiratory infections, substantially surpassing other diseases or injuries. Specifically, the HA‐ASMR for ischemic heart disease increased from 0.90 (95%UI: −0.03–2.46) in 1990 to 1.34 (95%UI: 0.20–3.07) in 2021 globally. The HA‐ASMR for stroke increased from 0.86 (95%UI: 0.00–2.18) in 1990 to 0.94 (95%UI: 0.12–2.25) in 2021 globally. In contrast, the HA‐ASMR for lower respiratory infections decreased from 1.48 (95%UI: 0.36–2.68) in 1990 to 0.91 (95%UI: 0.14–1.65) in 2021 globally.

The Burden of and Trends in Deaths From Ischemic Heart Disease, Stroke, and Lower Respiratory Infections Attributable to High Temperatures at the Regional and National Levels

Among the five SDI quintiles, low‐middle SDI regions bore the heaviest burden for stroke and ischemic heart disease attributable to high temperatures in 2021. Between 1990 and 2021, the HA‐ASMR for ischemic heart disease in low‐middle SDI regions increased from 2.43 (95%UI: 0.28–4.71) to 3.49 (95%UI: 0.96–6.57), while the HA‐ASMR for stroke in this region decreased from 2.61 (95%UI: 0.87–4.88) to 2.55 (95%UI: 0.77–5.01). Low SDI regions had the highest HA‐ASMR for lower respiratory infections in 2021, with a numerical decrease from 4.00 (95%UI: 1.81–6.74) in 1990 to 2.52 (95%UI: 0.85–4.35) in 2021. High SDI regions bore relatively lower mortality burdens across all three diseases (Figure 2).

Stroke mortality attributable to high temperatures showed an upward trend in high SDI (Net drift = 0.99%, 95%CI: 0.60%–1.39%) and high‐middle SDI regions (Net drift = 0.47%, 95%CI: 0.07%–0.87%). Ischemic heart disease mortality attributable to high temperatures increased across all SDI quintiles. Lower respiratory infections mortality attributable to high temperatures exhibited an increasing trend in high SDI regions (Net drift = 2.36%, 95%CI: 2.01%–2.70%) (Table 2). Overall mortality risk was higher in males, though females born before the 1972–1981 birth cohort generally showed higher cohort rate ratios (RR) (Table 2, Figure 2).

Figure 2 presents the APC model results for ischemic heart disease attributable to high temperatures across global and SDI quintile regions. Globally, local drift values were positive across all age groups. All SDI regions showed positive local drift values across most age groups, except for the high‐SDI region, where negative local drift values emerged after the 65–69 age group (Figure 2a). The longitudinal age curve demonstrated similar patterns globally and across SDI regions, with mortality risks increasing after older adulthood (>60 years) (Figure 2b). Both period RR and cohort RR showed numerical overall increases over time globally, with analogous numerical changes observed across SDI quintile regions (Figures 2c and 2d). Tables S1–S27 in Supporting Information S1 display APC analysis results for stroke and lower respiratory infections attributable to high temperatures across global and SDI quintile regions (Tables S5, S6, S8, S9, Table S11, S12, S14, S15 in Supporting Information S1).

Globally, lower respiratory infections mortality attributable to high temperatures showed negative local drift values in children and adolescents aged 0–19 years and positive local drift values in other age groups. Notably, high‐SDI regions exhibited sustained local drift growth after 15–19 years, peaking at 30–34 years (local drift = 5.37%, 95%CI: 4.40%–6.36%). Discussion

This study quantified the global disease burdens attributable to high temperatures using GBD 2021 data, with a focused investigation on the spatiotemporal trends of mortality rates for the top three diseases with the heaviest attribution burden. The findings revealed a dramatic increase in global disease death count attributable to high temperatures from 1990 to 2021, with ischemic heart disease, stroke, and lower respiratory infections identified as the primary causes of death. Through APC analysis, the study further explored the effects of age, period, and cohort on mortality variations in these three diseases, providing deeper insights into the underlying drivers of mortality burden changes attributable to high temperatures.

The major findings of the present study were cross‐validated by numerous population‐based surveillance studies independent of the GBD database, which fully confirm the robustness of our results. Ischemic heart disease, stroke, and lower respiratory infections are the top three causes of mortality attributable to high temperatures globally. This pattern is consistent with findings from large‐scale epidemiological studies and systematic reviews, which have repeatedly shown that cardiovascular and respiratory diseases are the primary contributors to death burden attributable to high temperature (Chen et al., 2018; Gai et al., 2025; Munzel et al., 2025). Second, the regional heterogeneity observed in our study, including the concentrated cardiovascular burden in low‐middle SDI regions and statistically significant annual mortality growth in high SDI regions, is also supported by multi‐center studies in East Asia and city‐level projection analyses (Li et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2022). Together, these findings suggest that the geographic distribution and temporal evolution of mortality attributable to high temperatures are shaped by substantial regional differences in vulnerability and adaptation capacity, underscoring the need for context‐specific prevention strategies. Third, our analyses identified that older adults constitute the primary high‐risk population, the finding supported by field‐based surveillance studies (Chen et al., 2018; Yin et al., 2018). Given the reduced thermoregulatory capacity and higher prevalence of chronic diseases among older individuals, targeted high temperatures health interventions for this group remain a public health priority.

The associations between high temperature exposure and mortality from ischemic heart disease, stroke, and lower respiratory infections identified in our study are underpinned by well‐characterized pathophysiological pathways. Under high temperature conditions, dehydration induced by the body's thermoregulatory sweating and high temperatures dissipation responses results in hemoconcentration, a hypercoagulable state, and electrolyte imbalances, alongside sympathetic nervous system activation. These physiological alterations collectively elevate the risks of thrombosis, arrhythmia, and myocardial ischemic events, consequently increasing mortality from ischemic heart disease and stroke (Bouchama et al., 2022; Gostimirovic et al., 2020). In parallel, dehydration impairs the respiratory mucosal barrier and inhibits local immune function, heightening susceptibility to lower respiratory infections. When combined with high temperature‐induced systemic inflammation and cardiopulmonary injury, these effects drive the progression to severe infection and raise the corresponding mortality risk (Bouchama et al., 2022). Furthermore, the enhanced survival and transmission of respiratory pathogens in hot and humid environments (Shang et al., 2025), together with the exacerbated vulnerability of older adults and those with underlying chronic diseases, further magnify these adverse health impacts of high temperature exposure (Cheng et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2025).

The study revealed an approximate 80% increase in global deaths attributable to high temperatures, while the ASMR remained stable, suggesting population growth and aging rather than elevated age‐standardized risks may be primary drivers. Furthermore, ischemic heart disease, stroke, and lower respiratory infections constitute the top three diseases with the heaviest mortality burden attributable to high temperatures, indicating that the public health sector should take targeted measures against these diseases, such as health education for vulnerable groups and strengthening the control of related risk factors. Notably, cardiovascular diseases have surpassed lower respiratory infections as the leading cause of disease mortality attributable to high temperatures. Meta‐analyses confirm significant associations between heatwaves and cardiovascular mortality risks, with risk escalation correlating with heatwave intensity (Cheng et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022). The findings underscore emerging challenges for chronic disease management under global warming, necessitating proactive intervention measures to prevent cardiovascular disease onset and progression.

Our findings indicate that the burden of mortality attributable to high temperatures from the top three diseases was predominantly borne by low‐middle SDI regions. This disparity stems from multiple factors: geographically, these regions are primarily located around the Arabian Peninsula and Saharan Africa, characterized by arid climates with frequent extreme high temperatures and droughts; additionally, with limited financial resources, these low‐income countries have inadequate high temperatures response strategies and tend to be more vulnerable than the richer areas (Schiermeier, 2018). For example, local residents generally have poorer health status and longer outdoor high temperature exposure time. The International Labor Organization estimates that more than 1 billion workers are exposed to high temperature events (Tord et al., 2019). The 2023 report of the Lancet Countdown on health and climate change highlights that high‐temperatures‐induced labor productivity losses disproportionately affect low‐middle HDI countries, amplifying global inequalities (Romanello et al., 2023). Notably, against the global trend of a declining burden of deaths from lower respiratory infections attributable to high temperatures, high SDI regions bucked the trend, which may be in part related to factors such as antibiotic resistance (Haenni et al., 2022), increasing population aging (Hua et al., 2024), and the urban heat island effect (Ho et al., 2023).

We observed higher mortality risk among males than females for the top three diseases, though females born before the 1972–1981 birth cohort showed elevated cohort risks. Physiological differences and evolving societal roles may significantly influence high‐temperatures‐related morbidity mechanisms. Hormonal fluctuations during menstrual cycles and menopause affect female high temperatures tolerance, while higher body fat percentage compared to males may impair thermoregulatory capacity (He et al., 2024). Conversely, male occupational concentration in high‐intensity outdoor jobs frequently involves high temperatures exposure environments with inadequate high temperatures protection measures. The heightened mortality risk among before 1972–1981 birth cohorts of females suggests insufficient economic, healthcare, and social support systems for this generation. Concurrently, mortality risks surge dramatically in the elderly population (>60 years), a pattern corroborated by multiple studies (Ebi et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022; Silveira et al., 2023; Xi et al., 2024). This phenomenon may correlate with age‐specific physiological factors, such as diminished thermoregulatory capacity, reduced high temperatures acclimatization potential, and pre‐existing chronic conditions (Ebi et al., 2021; Xi et al., 2024).

Continuous increases in period RR and cohort RR across most regions and countries may reflect exacerbated disease burdens through the cumulative effects of climate warming. China shows marked success in lower respiratory infection control but faces rising burdens of ischemic heart disease and stroke, potentially linked to accelerated population aging and lifestyle transformations (Chang et al., 2017; Guo et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2023). While ischemic heart disease management improves in the United States, worsening respiratory infections among younger populations partially reveal healthcare coverage disparities (Donohue et al., 2022; Renna et al., 2021). High‐burden nations like Iraq and Sudan, often plagued by conflict or ecological fragility, may require climate‐health integration into humanitarian aid frameworks.

The strengths of the current study include, primarily, a summary analysis of the burden of cause‐specific deaths attributable to high temperatures, which identifies the focus of high temperatures prevention and treatment efforts; subsequently, the temporal changes in the burden of deaths were divided into age, period, and cohort components through the APC model to capture in‐depth their temporal trends. This study has several limitations. First, although seasonal variation may influence short‐term temperature–mortality associations, its impact on the long‐term trends observed in this study is limited. Because GBD annual burden estimates are derived from daily temperature exposures and mortality data, seasonal fluctuations and their health effects are inherently incorporated into the annual attributable mortality rates analyzed in this study. Second, constrained by APC model data requirements, our analysis focused on the top 30 countries by ASMR, resulting in incomplete global coverage of mortality trends. Third, our core data are derived from GBD 2021, which only provides aggregated data at the national level. On one hand, this results in a lack of matched fine‐scale data to support spatiotemporal analysis at the microclimate level; on the other hand, it prevents us from conducting further sensitivity analyses that explore additional non‐linear terms. However, the original GBD framework has already incorporated the nonlinear relationship between temperature and mortality through a nonlinear exposure–response function. Future studies could integrate fine‐scale meteorological and health surveillance data to explore the impact of microclimatic conditions on mortality attributable to high temperature. Meanwhile, more comprehensive data sources or adaptable models could be adopted to enable deeper global investigations, and targeted studies on vulnerable populations are warranted to generate more precise evidence for policy making.

Conclusions

For ischemic heart disease and stroke, prevention efforts should prioritize older adults given their disproportionately elevated mortality risks under high temperature. For lower respiratory infections, regions with high socioeconomic development need targeted interventions for younger populations. Disparities across socioeconomic status, sex, and age in attributable mortality further underscore that adaptation strategies cannot be uniform but must account for these multidimensional inequalities and local epidemiological profiles.
