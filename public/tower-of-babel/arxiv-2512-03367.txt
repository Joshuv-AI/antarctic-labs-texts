Pairs of eventually constant maps and nilpotent pairs
Weixi Chen, Mee Seong Im, Mikhail Khovanov, Catherine Lillja, Nicolas Rugo

Abstract
. Tom Leinster gave a bijective correspondence between the set of operators on a finite-dimensional vector space V V and the set of pairs consisting of a nilpotent operator and a vector in V V . Over a finite field this bijection implies that the probability that an operator be nilpotent is the reciprocal of the number of vectors in V V . We generalize this correspondence to pairs of operators between pairs of vector spaces and determine the probability that a random pair of operators be nilpotent. We also determine the set-theoretical counterpart of this construction and compute the number of eventually constant pairs of maps between two finite sets, closely related to the number of spanning trees in a complete bipartite graph.

1.
Introduction

The nilpotent cone is foundational and prevalent in geometric and combinatorial representation theory.
It provides key geometric and algebraic structures for the study of representations of Hecke algebras and related algebras
[
4
]
.

Let
X
be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathcal{N}(X)\subset\mathsf{End}_{\mathbf{k}}(X)
be the set of nilpotent operators on
X
. Tom Leinster proved in
[
13
]
that there is a bijection between
\mathcal{N}(X)\times X
and
\mathsf{End}_{\mathbf{k}}(X)
. Working over a finite field with
q
elements, the result specializes to an older theorem of Fine and Herstein
[
8
, Theorem 1]
, which says that the number of
n\times n
nilpotent matrices is
q^{n(n-1)}=|X|^{n-1}
. Equivalently, the probability that a random operator be nilpotent is
q^{-n}
. A useful feature of Leinster’s argument is that it requires very little calculation, and exposes a deeper structural pattern.
The set-theoretical analogue of nilpotent matrix counting reduces to the count of trees on
n
labeled vertices, given by the celebrated Cayley’s formula
n^{n-2}
; see
[
13
]
and references there.

In
[
8
, Theorem 2]
, Fine and Herstein determine the number of nilpotent matrices over rings of the form
\mathbb{Z}/a\mathbb{Z}
, where
a
is any positive integer. The mod
a
count follows quite simply from the mod
p
count, for
p
a prime number, and this is the crux of the argument there.

A linear operator on a vector space describes a representation of a quiver (oriented graph) with a single vertex and single loop at the vertex, shown in Figure

, equivalent to the nilpotency of

.

Our main result is Theorem
3.17
, which determines the number of nilpotent pairs
(f,g)
. Corollary

rephrases it: the probability that a random pair of operators

be nilpotent is

.

The analogue of Leinster’s bijection

is given earlier, by Theorem

, which uses the notion of a

The set-theoretical counterpart of a nilpotent pair is given by a pair of finite sets and maps

The count of nilpotent matrices over the Boolean semiring is explained in Section

In Section

2.
Eventually constant pairs of maps of sets

2.1.
Background on graph theory

We consider unoriented graphs without loops or multiple edges.
A simple path is a sequence of vertices in a graph, where each consecutive pair of vertices is connected by an edge, and no vertices, except possibly the endpoints, are repeated.
If there is a repetition, a simple path is called a cycle. A tree is an unoriented graph in which every pair of distinct vertices is connected by exactly one simple path; equivalently, a tree is a connected graph with no cycles.

A rooted tree is a tree together with a choice of vertex, which we call the root. A spanning tree inside a connected graph
\Gamma
is a subgraph
T\subseteq\Gamma
such that
T
is a tree, and maximal with respect to the property of being a tree inside
\Gamma
. This is equivalent to saying that
T
is a tree that contains all vertices of
\Gamma
.

A graph is called bipartite or bicolorable if it is possible to color the vertices in two distinct colors, such as red and black, such that no two vertices of the same color are adjacent, i.e., connected by an edge. Recall that a graph is bipartite if and only if its cycles are all of even length
[
2
, Theorem 5.3]
or
[
5
]
. Denote the complete bipartite graph by
K(m,n)
.

2.2.
The enumeration of eventually constant pairs of functions

We say that a map
f:X\longrightarrow X
of a finite set
X
is
eventually constant
if
|\mathsf{im}(f^{k})|=1
for some
k\geq 0
, i.e., iterating
f
results in a map that takes
X
into a single element of
X
.

Given sets
X,Y
of cardinalities
m,n
, respectively, a pair
(f,g)
of maps
f:X\to Y
,
g:Y\to X
is called an
eventually constant
pair if the composite
gf:X\longrightarrow X
is eventually constant. Equivalently,
fg:Y\longrightarrow Y
is eventually constant.
Denote the set of eventually constant pairs by
P(m,n)
. The cardinality
|P(n,m)|
is divisible by
mn
since the eventually constant condition gives us unique “final” elements
x_{0},y_{0}
of
X,Y
, respectively, with
f(x_{0})=y_{0}
,
g(y_{0})=x_{0}
and
(gf)^{k}(x)=x_{0},(fg)^{k}(y_{0})=y_{0}
for large
k
.

Counting eventually constant pairs is a distributed version of Cayley’s formula on counting trees.

Lemma 2.1

(Cayley’s formula)
.

The number of unrooted trees with vertex set
X
is
m^{m-2}
.

Proof.

We refer the reader to
[
12
, page 16]
, and see
[
13
]
for the connection to eventually constant maps.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

There are
m^{n-1}n^{m-1}
spanning trees in the complete bipartite graph
K(m,n)
.

Proof.

Originally proved in
[
7
]
, also see
[
1
]
and
[
15
,
6
]
.
∎

The following theorem generalizes Cayley’s formula:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be finite sets of cardinality
m
and
n
, respectively. Then there are
m^{n-1}n^{m-1}(m+n-1)
eventually constant pairs of maps between
X
and
Y
.

Proof.

Pairs of maps
(f,g)
between
X
and
Y

are in a bijection with bipartite oriented graphs on the vertex set pair
(X,Y)
, such that there is one oriented edge out of each vertex. The graph associated to
(f,g)
has an oriented edge from
x\in X
to
f(x)\in Y
for each
x
and an oriented edge from
y\in Y
to
g(y)\in X
for each
y
. Let
G(f,g)
be the graph associated with
(f,g)
.

An oriented cycle in a bipartite oriented graph has an even number of vertices, alternating between vertices in
X
and
Y
.
The graph
G(f,g)
for an eventually constant pair
(f,g)
has an (oriented) 2-cycle
x_{0}\longrightarrow f(x_{0})=y_{0}\longrightarrow g(y_{0})=x_{0}
. It has no other oriented
2k
-cycles for any
k\geq 1
.

Vice versa, if
G(f,g)
has an oriented 2-cycle and no other oriented cycles then
(f,g)
is an eventually constant pair. Consequently, there is a bijection between eventually constant pairs
(f,g)
and oriented bipartite graphs
G
on vertex set
(X,Y)
with one edge out of each vertex such that
G
has a unique 2-cycle and no other oriented cycles.

Given a spanning tree
T
in a complete (unoriented) graph
K(m,n)=K(X,Y)
, pick an edge
e
of
T
. This edge connect vertices
x_{0}\in X
and
y_{0}\in Y
. To the data
(T,e)
associate an oriented bipartite graph
G(T,e)
on vertex set
(X,Y)
as follows. Deleting edge
e
from
T
while keeping vertices
x_{0},y_{0}
gives a disjoint union of 2 trees
T_{x},T_{y}
with
x_{0}\in T_{x},y_{0}\in T_{y}
(so that
T\setminus\{e\}=T_{x}\sqcup T_{y}
).

Orient each edge of
T_{x}
towards
x_{0}
and each edge of
T_{y}
towards
y_{0}
.
Define the graph
G(T,e)
to contain all of these oriented edges and two oriented edges
x_{0}\longrightarrow y_{0}
and
y_{0}\longrightarrow x_{0}
.
Thus, we’re orienting all edges of
T
except
e
and converting
e
to a pair of oriented edges between
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
.

Oriented graph
G(T,e)
satisfies all properties above and determines a pair of eventually constant maps
(f,g)
. Vice versa, the graph
G(f,g)
of an eventually constant pair determines a tree
T
by taking the 2-cycle
x_{0}\longrightarrow y_{0}\longrightarrow x_{0}
in
G(f,g)
, converting it to an unoriented edge
e
of
T
and adding all other edges of
G(f,g)
without their orientations. We obtain the following result.

Lemma 2.4
.

The above correspondence is a bijection between eventually constant pairs of maps
(f,g)
and spanning trees in
K(m,n)
together with a choice of a edge in the tree.

Any spanning tree in
K(m,n)
contains
(m+n-1)
edges, and there are
m^{n-1}n^{m-1}
trees in
K(m,n)
, see Lemma
2.2
. This completes the proof of Theorem
2.3
.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

The probability that a pair
(f,g)
of maps
f:X\to Y,\;g:Y\to X
is eventually constant is
\frac{m+n-1}{mn}
.

Proof.

There are
m^{n}n^{m}
pairs of maps between
X
and
Y
.
∎

3.
Pairs of nilpotent maps and a balanced vector

Let
V,W
be pairs of vector spaces of dimension
m
and
n
, respectively, over any field
\mathbf{k}
. Consider pairs of linear maps
(f,g)
, with
f\in\mathsf{Hom}(V,W)
and
g\in\mathsf{Hom}(W,V)
. Denote the set (or cone) of nilpotent operators on

\mathcal{N}(V)\subseteq\mathsf{End}(V)

In this section, we generalize Leinster’s main result
[
13
, Theorem 5]
.

3.1.
Linear algebra for pairs of vector spaces

We begin by recalling some linear algebra. Firstly, a complement of a subspace
X
in a vector space
V
is a subspace
X^{\prime}
with
X\oplus X^{\prime}=V
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There is a canonical bijection between
\mathsf{Hom}(V,W)
and complements of
W
in
V\oplus W
.

Proof.

A linear map
h:V\longrightarrow W
determines the complement that consists of vectors
(v,h(v))\in V\oplus W
,
v\in V
.
(See
[
13
, Lemma 1]
.)
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
f:V\to W
and
g:W\to V
are linear maps. Then
gf
and
fg
are automorphisms of
V
and
W
, respectively, if and only if both
f
and
g
are isomorphisms.

Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
are generalizations of standard facts about linear operators to the case of pairs of maps. The first is a generalization of the Fitting decomposition.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
V,W
be finite-dimensional vector spaces. An ordered pair
(f,g)
of linear maps, where
f\in\mathsf{Hom}(V,W)
and
g\in\mathsf{Hom}(W,V)
,
is uniquely characterized by the following data. Take subspaces, which we write as
V_{N},V_{I}
, such that
V=V_{I}\oplus V_{N}
, and subspaces
W_{N},W_{I}
of
W
with
W=W_{I}\oplus W_{N}
. These are subject to the condition that
(gf)|_{V_{I}}
and
(fg)|_{W_{I}}
are automorphisms, and
(gf)|_{V_{N}}\in\mathcal{N}(V_{N}),\;(fg)|_{W_{N}}\in\mathcal{N}(W_{N})
.

Proof.

We can take
V_{I}=\bigcap_{i\geq 0}\mathsf{im}((gf)^{i})
,
V_{N}=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}\mathsf{ker}((gf)^{i})
.
Likewise, take
W_{I}=\bigcap_{i\geq 0}\mathsf{im}((fg)^{i})
and
W_{N}=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}\mathsf{ker}((fg)^{i})
. The fact that
V=V_{I}\oplus V_{N}
and
W=W_{I}\oplus W_{N}
is the statement of Fitting’s lemma, see
[
11
, pages 113–114]
.

It is immediate that,
in the above subspace decompositions,
f
and
g
have the following block-diagonal form

f=\begin{pmatrix}S_{1}&0\\
0&B_{1}\end{pmatrix},\quad\quad g=\begin{pmatrix}S_{2}&0\\
0&B_{2}\end{pmatrix},

where
S_{i}
are invertible, and
B_{i}
compose to nilpotent operators.
Maps
S_{1}:=f|_{V_{I}}:V_{I}\to W_{I}
and
S_{2}:=g|_{W_{I}}:W_{I}\to V_{I}
are isomorphisms, while
(gf)|_{V_{N}}=B_{2}B_{1}
and
(fg)|_{W_{N}}=B_{1}B_{2}
are nilpotent operators.
∎

The following statement is clear.

Lemma 3.4
.

There is a natural bijection between ordered bases of finite-dimensional vector space
X
of dimension
\ell
and isomorphisms
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\simeq}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbf{k}^{\ell}
.

Corollary 3.5
.

There is a bijection between pairs of ordered bases of finite-dimensional vector spaces
V
and
W
with
\dim V=\dim W
, and pairs of isomorphisms
(f,g)\in\mathsf{Hom}(V,W)\times\mathsf{Hom}(W,V)
.

T_{\circ}v_{u}=gf^{\prime}v_{u}=gw_{u}
, so
T_{\circ}[T_{\circ}v_{u}]=T_{\circ}[gw_{u}]
. Because
f^{\prime}=f
on
V^{\prime}
, in particular
f^{\prime}(gw_{u})=f(gw_{u})
, we know
T_{\circ}[T_{\circ}v_{u}]=T_{\circ}[gw_{u}]=T[gw_{u}]
. Also,
f
and
f^{\prime}
agreeing on
V^{\prime}
implies
f^{\prime}(Tv_{u})=f(Tv_{u})
, so
T_{\circ}[Tv_{u}]=T[Tv_{u}]
. Hence
T_{\circ}[T_{\circ}v_{u}]\oplus T_{\circ}[Tv_{u}]=T[Tv_{u}]\oplus T[gw_{u}]
. Adding
v_{u}
to
T_{\circ}[T_{\circ}v_{u}]
and
T[Tv_{u}]
respectively, we have
V^{\prime\prime}=V^{\prime\prime}_{\circ}
by the choice of them at the beginning of the proof. Hence
V^{\prime}=V_{\circ}^{\prime}
.

Thus the bijection is established, completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

Corollary 3.16
.

There is a bijection between the set of quadruples
(f,g,v_{b},w_{b})
with
(f,g)
nilpotent and
v_{b},w_{b}
balanced and the disjoint union of the following two sets: (1) the set of quadruples
(f,g,v_{u},w_{u})
with
(f,g)
nilpotent and
v_{u},w_{u}
unbalanced, (2) the set of triples
(f,g,w_{b})
where
(f,g)
is nilpotent and
w_{b}\in W
is balanced.

Proof.

Use the previous lemma and consider two cases:
v_{b}\not=0
and
v_{b}=0
.
∎

Theorem 3.17
.

There is a bijection

(21)

\mathsf{Hom}(V,W)\times\mathsf{Hom}(W,V)\times(V\cup_{0}W)\cong\mathcal{N}(V,W)\times V\times W.

The number of nilpotent pairs
(f,g)
is

(22)

\mathcal{N}_{m,n}:=|\mathcal{N}(V,W)|=q^{2mn-m-n}(q^{m}+q^{n}-1).

In (
3.17
),
V\cup_{0}W
denotes the union of
V
and
W
along their
0
vectors. This union is naturally a subset of
V\oplus W
.
Expression (
22
) is a closed formula, c.f. (
4
), see also Lemma
3.8
for the notation.
It would also be interesting to relate the count in Theorem
2.3
to that in (
22
).

Proof.

The count of nilpotent pairs in (
22
) follows from the bijection in (
21
) by taking cardinalities of both sides. The cardinality of the LHS is
q^{2nm}(q^{n}+q^{m}-1)
and that of the RHS is
\mathcal{N}_{n,m}q^{n}q^{m}
.

To establish bijection (
21
) we
introduce some notations:

•

\{f,g\}
denotes the set of nilpotent pairs
(f,g)
,

•

\{v\}
denotes the set of vectors
v\in V
,

•

\{f,g,v\}
denotes the set of triples consisting of nilpotent pair and a vector
v\in V
,

•

\{f,g,v_{b}\}
, respectively
\{f,g,v_{u}\}
denotes the set of triples as above together with a choice of a balanced vector
v_{b}\in V
, respectively unbalanced vector
v_{u}\in V
,

•

\{f,g,v_{b},w_{u}\}
denotes the set of quadruples consisting of a nilpotent pair, a balanced
v_{b}\in V
, and an unbalanced
w_{u}\in W
,

•

[f,g]
denotes the set of all pairs of maps
f\in\mathsf{Hom}(V,W),g\in\mathsf{Hom}(W,V)
.

We omit a full list of such notations.

From Theorem
3.6
we have bijections

(23)

\{f,g,v_{b}\}\sim[f,g]\sim\{f,g,w_{b}\}.

Corollary
3.16
gives a bijection

(24)

\{f,g,v_{b},w_{b}\}\sim\{f,g,v_{u},w_{u}\}\sqcup[f,g].

Let us separate
v\in V
into balanced

\{f,g,v\}\sim\{f,g,v_{b}\}\sqcup\{f,g,v_{u}\}\sim[f,g]\sqcup\{f,g,v_{u}\},

(26)

\{f,g,v_{b},w\}\sim[f,g]\times\{w\}\sim[f,g]\times W,\ \ \{f,g,v,w_{b}\}\sim[f,g]\times\{v\}\sim[f,g]\times V.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\{f,g,v,w\}\sqcup[f,g]

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\{f,g,v_{b},w_{b}\}\sqcup\{f,g,v_{b},w_{u}\}\sqcup\{f,g,v_{u},w_{b}\}\sqcup(\{f,g,v_{u},w_{u}\}\sqcup[f,g])

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\left(\{f,g,v_{b},w_{b}\}\sqcup\{f,g,v_{b},w_{u}\}\right)\sqcup\left(\{f,g,v_{u},w_{b}\}\sqcup\{f,g,v_{b},w_{b}\}\right)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\{f,g,v_{b}\}\times W\sqcup\{f,g,w_{b}\}\times V

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle[f,g]\times(V\sqcup W).

In the second step bijection (

(27)

\{f,g,v,w\}\sqcup[f,g]\sim[f,g]\times(V\sqcup W).

Modifying this bijection to remove a copy of the set

from both sides gives a bijection

\{f,g,v,w\}\sim[f,g]\times(V\cup_{0}W)

for (

∎

4.
Nilpotent endomorphisms over the Boolean semiring

The notion of a nilpotent operator makes sense for
linear operators on semimodules over semirings. Consider the Boolean semiring

\mathbb{B}=\{0,1:1+1=1\}
. The number of idempotents in the matrix semiring
M_{n}(\mathbb{B})
was obtained by Butler
[
3
]
. We give a count of nilpotent matrices in
M_{n}(\mathbb{B})
, denoting the set of such matrices by
\mathcal{N}_{n}(\mathbb{B})
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A=(A_{ij})\in\mathcal{N}_{n}(\mathbb{B})
. Then

A_{ii}=0
for all
i
, and if
A_{ij}=1
, then
A_{ji}=0
. A Boolean matrix is nilpotent iff there is no oriented cycle of
1
’s among its entries, i.e., there is no sequence
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}
such that
A_{i_{1},i_{2}}=A_{i_{2},i_{3}}=\ldots=A_{i_{k},i_{1}}=1
.

Proof.

A
1
on a diagonal in
A
remains
1
in any power of
A
. An oriented
k
-cycle of
1
’s create
1
on the diagonal in
A^{k}
. If
A
has no oriented cycles of
1
’s (including 1-cycles, i.e., diagonal entries
1
) then
A^{n}=0
necessarily.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

The set
\mathcal{N}_{n}(\mathbb{B})
is in a bijection with the set of directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) on
n
ordered vertices.

Proof.

Indeed, to a direct acyclic graph
G
on vertices
1,2,\dots,n
assign the matrix
A
with
A_{ij}=1
iff there is an oriented edge from vertex
j
to vertex
i
, otherwise
A_{ij}=0
. This is a bijection between DAGs and nilpotent Boolean matrices in view of the above lemma.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
a_{n}=|\mathcal{N}_{n}(\mathbb{B})|
be the number of nilpotent Boolean matrices of size
n
. These numbers satisfy the recurrence relation

(29)

a_{0}=1,\ \ \ a_{n}=\displaystyle{\sum_{k=1}^{n}}(-1)^{k-1}\binom{n}{k}2^{k(n-k)}a_{n-k}\quad\mbox{ for }n\geq 1.

Proof.

See
[
14
]
for the references that the number of DAGs on
n
labeled vertices satisfies relation (
29
).
∎

Matrices in
M_{n}(\mathbb{B})
are in a bijection with endomorphisms of the free
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule
\mathbb{B}^{n}
, and the count of nilpotent matrices is given above. One can ask a natural question about counting nilpotent endomorphisms of a general finitely-generated projective semimodule
P
instead of the free semimodule
\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Isomorphism classes of such semimodules are in a bijection with isomorphism classes of finite topological spaces
X
, see
[
10
, Section 3.2]
, for instance. Semimodule
P
associated to
X
as above has its elements the open subsets of
X
. Zero element of
P
is the empty subset of
X
and the sum in
P
is given by the union of sets. Counting nilpotent endomorphisms of
P
is a combinatorial problem that generalizes the count of labeled DAGs, and it may be interesting for at least some classes of finite topological spaces. The linear counterpart of this problem might be counting nilpotent endomorphisms of a finite-dimensional module
M
over a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-algebra.
