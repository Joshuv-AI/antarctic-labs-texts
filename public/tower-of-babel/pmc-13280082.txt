Real-World Ritlecitinib Treatment of Severe Alopecia Areata in the United States: Patient Characteristics and Physician Satisfaction According to a Secondary Database Analysis
Authors: Samantha K Kurosky, Ashley S Cha-Silva, Jenny Austin, Arash Mostaghimi, Chesahna Kindred, Genevieve Gauthier, Mojgan Sadrarhami, Peter Anderson, Benjamin Ungar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRitlecitinib, an oral, selective dual inhibitor of Janus kinase (JAK) 3 and tyrosine kinase expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma (TEC) family kinases, is approved for treating severe alopecia areata (AA) in patients aged ≥ 12 years in the USA. This study describes characteristics, treatment satisfaction, and success among adults initiating ritlecitinib.MethodsA sample of US dermatologists treating adults who received ritlecitinib for severe AA were surveyed between November 2023 and June 2024. Demographics, clinical characteristics, treatment history, satisfaction, and health-related quality of life were analyzed overall, among recent initiators, and stratified by treatment duration.ResultsA total of 123 adults were included (mean age 37.1 years; 57% male; 79% white; 88% commercial insurance). Mean disease duration was 2.1 years. At ritlecitinib initiation (n = 93), mean scalp hair loss was 71% and 73% had worsening AA. The proportion of patients with hair regrowth increased with longer treatment duration, 49% among those with < 3 months of treatment (n = 18/37) to 82% with > 6 months (n = 28/34). Physicians reported many recent initiators (n = 26) felt moderate/severe embarrassment (84%), frustration (73%), and anxiety (65%) due to their AA. Among patients with available data (n = 102), ritlecitinib was the first AA treatment for 46%. Treatment goals (n = 123) included reducing hair loss (94%), improving quality-of-life (41%), and achieving long-term control (32%). Reasons for selecting ritlecitinib (n = 113) included its ability to reduce scalp hair loss (38%), cost–benefit ratio (28%), and safety profile (25%). Satisfaction with AA control was 54%, 68%, and 85% after < 3 (n = 20/37), 3–6 (n = 27/40), and > 6 months (n = 29/34), respectively, of ritlecitinib treatment.ConclusionsMany patients experienced psychological burden due to their AA around the start of treatment. Ritlecitinib was chosen for its efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness. Hair regrowth, disease stability, and treatment satisfaction increased with longer ritlecitinib treatment duration suggesting sustained treatment is important for achieving treatment goals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01773-3.Keywords: Alopecia areata, Real-world data, Ritlecitinib

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Ritlecitinib, a dual JAK3/TEC kinase inhibitor, is approved for the treatment of severe alopecia areata (AA) in patients ≥ 12 years of age, but data on its use in real-world clinical practice and its impact on patients’ health-related quality of life (HRQOL) remain limited.Understanding patient characteristics; treatment patterns, goals, and satisfaction; and the impact on HRQOL can help guide dermatologists in optimizing AA management and aligning treatment expectations for their patients. What was learned from the study? Ritlecitinib was used as first line AA treatment in nearly half of patients and was associated with improvements in hair regrowth, disease stability, and satisfaction the longer the patient was on treatment, emphasizing the value of sustained treatment in achieving treatment goals.Physicians selected ritlecitinib primarily for its efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness.Many patients experience psychosocial burdens around the time of initiating treatment, highlighting the unmet need for effective treatments for patients with severe AA.

Introduction

Alopecia areata (AA) is an autoimmune disease characterized by patchy or complete nonscarring hair loss on the scalp, with or without additional loss of facial and/or body hair [1]. The clinical presentation of AA is unpredictable and can vary by the extent and location of hair loss including scalp, eyebrows, eyelashes, facial hair, and body hair; in severe cases it can progress to alopecia totalis (complete loss of scalp hair) or alopecia universalis (complete loss of scalp, face, and body hair) [2]. The global prevalence of AA is estimated to range from 0.58% to 2% [3–6]. In the USA, a large, cross-sectional survey found the self-reported point prevalence and lifetime prevalence to be 1.1% and 2.5%, respectively [4]. AA has an unpredictable disease course; some patients will spontaneously recover while many will experience periods of relapse and remission and some will progress to more severe forms of AA, which can be more difficult to treat [2, 7, 8].

In addition to hair loss, AA negatively impacts patients’ health-related quality of life (HRQOL), which is an important consideration when determining the severity of a patient’s AA [9]. Psychosocial and emotional impacts of AA are consistently observed, with patients reporting mental health issues including feeling sad/depressed and embarrassed/ashamed along with avoidance of social situations and impacts on work productivity and major life decisions [10–14]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 37 studies found that the prevalence of depressive disorders and anxiety disorders were higher among patients with AA compared with the general population and that 7–17% of patients had depressive or anxiety disorders that required care or medication [15]. This study found that, among patients with AA, the prevalence and odds ratio (OR) of depressive symptoms (37%; OR, 2.70) and anxiety symptoms (34%; OR, 3.07) were higher than that of depressive disorders (9%; OR, 1.38) and anxiety disorders (7–17%; OR, 1.51–1.69).

Traditional treatments for AA have included topical, intralesional, and systemic corticosteroids; immunosuppressants; phototherapy; and topical calcineurin inhibitors [8, 16]. Newer, US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved therapies are also available, including ritlecitinib, an oral, selective dual inhibitor of Janus kinase (JAK) 3 and tyrosine kinase expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma (TEC) family kinases [8, 17]. Although there are, at the time of writing, no US treatment guidelines for AA, guidelines and consensus statements from other regions include JAK inhibitors as efficacious treatment options for adult patients with AA [18–21]. Ritlecitinib demonstrated efficacy and safety in the pivotal ALLEGRO phase 2b/3 study (NCT3732807) in adults and adolescents, and in June 2023, ritlecitinib 50 mg once-daily (QD) was FDA-approved for the treatment of severe alopecia areata in adults and adolescents 12 years and older [17].

As approved kinase inhibitor treatments for AA are relatively new, limited data exist describing how dermatologists are integrating them into real-world clinical practice, or the impact of such treatments on patients. The objective of this study was to describe patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and HRQOL along with treatment history, satisfaction, and success among adult patients with severe AA who were treated with ritlecitinib in the USA. Analyses were stratified by ritlecitinib treatment duration and by recent ritlecitinib initiation (within 2 months of the survey).

Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

A secondary database analysis was performed using data from the Adelphi Real World AA II US Disease Specific Programme™ (DSP)—a cross-sectional survey of US dermatologists and their consulting patients with AA in routine clinical practice. Patients aged ≥ 18 years who were diagnosed with physician-assessed severe or very severe AA (referred to hereafter as “severe”) in the USA and receiving ritlecitinib at the time of the survey were included in the analysis (Fig. S1). The overall severity of AA was assessed by survey respondents’ answers to the question “What is/was your overall assessment of the severity of AA symptoms in this patient based on your own definition of the terms mild, moderate, severe, and very severe?”

The DSP was conducted between November 2023 and June 2024 and collected de-identified information from patient medical records and the most recent consultation (Fig. S1). A convenience sample of dermatologists in the USA who were actively involved in managing the cases of at least seven adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) with AA monthly were recruited to participate. Eligible dermatologists were asked to complete a physician survey and patient record forms (PRFs) for the next seven consecutively consulting adult patients with AA (one patient with mild, three patients with moderate, and three patients with severe disease, as evaluated subjectively by each physician). US dermatologists were consulted in the development of the survey to ensure that questions were phrased to align with terminology that would resonate with most dermatologists. PRFs were completed at a single point in time but had an element of retrospective data collection as physicians were able to use patients’ medical records when completing the PRFs. Data collection was performed in compliance with the European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association code of conduct and Adelphi Real World standard operating procedures and are submitted for review to the Pearl Institutional Review Board.

Outcomes

Outcomes included baseline patient demographics, clinical characteristics, treatment characteristics, treatment history, physician-reported HRQOL and psychological burden, physician-reported treatment goals, physician-reported satisfaction with treatment, and physician perception of treatment success. HRQOL and psychological burden included the impact of AA on patients’ daily lives and feelings about their disease and its management as reported to their physician (e.g., avoidance of social interactions, impairment in ability to conduct normal daily activities, impairment in ability to work/study, frustration, stress, annoyance, anxiety/worry, and depression/low mood). To assess patients’ quality of life close to the time of treatment decision-making, but before the effect of treatment could have reasonably occurred, psychosocial burden was analyzed among the subgroup of patients who initiated ritlecitinib within 2 months of the survey (ie., “recent initiators”). A 2-month window for the survey was selected to proxy baseline burden to align with routine clinical practice, in which follow-up visits to assess AA treatment effectiveness typically occur 3–4 months following treatment initiation.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported for each objective, for the overall cohort sample (patients receiving ritlecitinib) and each subgroup. Subgroups included ritlecitinib treatment duration (< 3 months, 3–6 months, > 6 months), and recent initiators.

Ethical Approval

A complete description of the methods of the survey has been previously published and validated [22–24]. Permission was obtained from the owner of the Adelphi Real World AA II US DSP to use the database for this study. Using a check box, patients provided informed consent for use of their anonymized and aggregated data for research and publication in scientific journals. Data were collected in such a way that patients and physicians could not be identified directly; all data were aggregated and de-identified before receipt. Data collection was undertaken in line with European Pharmaceutical Marketing Research Association guidelines and as such it does not require ethics committee approval. Each survey was performed in full accordance with relevant legislation at the time of data collection, including the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act legislation [25].

Results

Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

The analytic cohort included 123 adults with a mean (SD) age of 37.1 (11.5) years, with 57% being male and 79% White (Table 1). Overall, 88% had commercial insurance, and 78% were working full- or part-time. Of these 123 patients, 26 were recent initiators with a mean (SD) age of 34 (11.9) years with 35% being male, 85% being White, 96% having commercial insurance, and 73% working full- or part-time (Table 1).

Psychosocial Burden among Recent Ritlecitinib Initiators

Among the subset of patients who recently initiated ritlecitinib (n = 26), dermatologists assessed patient HRQOL and psychological burden prior to initiating ritlecitinib. Dermatologists reported that prior to starting ritlecitinib, 69% of patients learned to manage their AA; however, HRQOL impacts were still experienced including that AA is a major problem in everyday life and causes considerable problems with day-to-day activities (69%), as well as causing considerable emotional upset (65%), and the patient has become disheartened about achieving better control due to poor performance of available treatments and lack of new treatment options (38%; Fig. 2A). Dermatologists also reported that, prior to initiating ritlecitinib, 85%, 73%, and 65% of patients had moderate/severe embarrassment, frustration, and anxiety, respectively, due to their AA, and the majority had moderate/severe fear of persistence of the condition, stress, avoidance of social interactions, and annoyance (81%, 73%, 69%, and 65%, respectively; Fig. 2B).

Treatment History and Treatment Selection Factors

Among patients with known ritlecitinib treatment duration (n = 111/123 [90%]), measured from the start of ritlecitinib to the date of the survey, mean (SD) ritlecitinib treatment duration was 4.8 (3.2) months and the proportion of patients with a ritlecitinib treatment duration of < 3 months, 3–6 months, and > 6 months was 33% (n = 37/111), 41% (n = 46/111), and 25% (n = 28/111), respectively (Table 4). Among patients in the overall cohort (N = 123), 84% were receiving ritlecitinib monotherapy, and 16% were receiving concomitant therapies, which included intralesional corticosteroids, topical corticosteroids, oral corticosteroids, or other treatment (Table 4). Among those with data on the number of previous lines of AA treatment received (n = 102/123 [83%]), patients had a mean (SD) of 1.8 (0.9) lines of treatments, and ritlecitinib was the first treatment for 46% (n = 47/102) of these patients (Table 4). Among patients (n = 60/123 [49%]) with data for AA therapies received before initiating ritlecitinib, 33% (n = 20/60) received topical corticosteroid, 30% (n = 18/60) intralesional corticosteroid (e.g., triamcinolone), 18% (n = 11/60) oral corticosteroid, 18% (n = 11/60) another JAK inhibitor (oral or topical), 15% (n = 9/60) systemic immunomodulator, 7% (n = 4/60) intravenous injected corticosteroid, 5% (n = 3/60) topical immunomodulator, and 12% (n = 7/60) other treatment (Table 4).

Among all patients (N = 123), the most common physician-reported treatment goals for adult patients with AA included reducing hair loss (94%), improving quality of life (41%), and achieving long-term control of their AA (32%) (Fig. S3A). Additional treatment goals included reduced eyebrow hair loss (28%), reduced psychosocial impact (28%), reduced eyelash hair loss (18%), minimized adverse events (17%), reduced impact on the patient’s ability to do what they enjoy (12%), reduced scalp itch/irritation (12%), reduced impact on patient’s ability to work/study (10%), and reduced nail damage (10%).

Among patients with physician-reported reasons for selecting ritlecitinib, reported among 113 (92%) of 123 patients, the most common factors were its ability to reduce scalp hair loss (38%), reasonable cost–benefit ratio (28%), its safety for long-term use (25%), and its good overall safety profile (25%) (Fig. S3B). Additional factors included ease of access/lack of administrative barriers (18%), lack of required intensive monitoring (15%), reimbursement for the patient (14%), improvement in scalp hair coverage (12%), reduced patchiness of scalp hair regrowth (12%), and improved quality of life (11%).

Physicians also reported on their satisfaction with their patients’ AA control. Among patients with available data, satisfaction increased with the duration of ritlecitinib treatment and was 54% (n = 20/37), 68% (n = 27/40), and 85% (n = 29/34) after < 3, 3–6, and > 6 months, respectively (Fig. 3A). The proportion of patients for whom physicians considered ritlecitinib treatment to be success also increased with treatment duration with 19% (n = 7/37), 30% (n = 12/40), and 68% (n = 23/34) at < 3 months, 3–6 months, and > 6 months, respectively (Fig. 3B). In the > 6-month group, physicians felt it was still too early to tell whether ritlecitinib was a successful treatment for 29% (n = 10/34) of patients.

Discussion

This real-world, cross-sectional survey of dermatologists treating patients with severe AA in the USA demonstrates the impact of ritlecitinib treatment on patients’ AA, including improvements in scalp hair regrowth, how ritlecitinib is being integrated into real-world clinical practice, and physician satisfaction. Overall, patients who received ritlecitinib had improvements in scalp hair regrowth from the time of ritlecitinib initiation to the time of survey with a reduction in mean percentage of SHL and an increase in the proportion of patients reported as having improving hair-regrowth status. The proportion of patients with hair regrowth, the mean percentage of scalp hair regrowth, and quality of hair regrowth all increased with longer treatment duration. It should be noted that it is plausible that those patients who improve are more likely to continue treatment, while those who do not are more likely to discontinue early. Disease status and disease severity also improved with increased ritlecitinib treatment duration, as did physician-reported satisfaction with the current control of their patient’s AA and the proportion of physicians who considered the ritlecitinib treatment regimen to be a success.

Improvements in hair regrowth with ritlecitinib treatment were also observed in the ritlecitinib clinical trials. In the ALLEGRO-2b/3 study, there was a higher proportion of female patients, a slightly lower proportion of White patients, and slightly higher extent of scalp hair loss at baseline compared with the present survey. In ALLEGRO-2b/3, the proportion of patients with response based on Severity of Alopecia Tool (SALT) score ≤ 10 and ≤ 20 was significantly higher among patients receiving ritlecitinib versus placebo at Week 24 and continued to increase through Week 48 [17]. Similar results were demonstrated for the adolescent population in ALLEGRO-2b/3 [26]. In addition, in the ongoing long-term extension study, ALLEGRO-LT (NCT04006457), which included patients rolled over from ALLEGRO-2b/3 as well as de novo patients, the proportion of patients with SALT score ≤ 10 and ≤ 20 increased from Month 12 to Month 24 among patients who rolled over from ALLEGRO-2b/3 [27] and was maintained from Month 12 to 24 among de novo patients [28]. Our survey demonstrated that patients receiving ritlecitinib in real-world practice also experience improvements in scalp hair regrowth over time. In addition to assessing scalp hair regrowth, the ALLEGRO-2b/3 study also demonstrated increasing rates of eyebrow and eyelash response through Week 48 among patients with abnormal eyebrows or eyelashes at baseline but did not examine hair regrowth in other body regions [17]. The survey data collected in the present study now provide insight into hair regrowth over a range of body regions and demonstrated that the proportion of patients with facial hair (beard or mustache) regrowth over time increases with duration of ritlecitinib treatment. Overall, the present study expands our understanding of the efficacy of ritlecitinib from the clinical trials and demonstrates the real-world impact ritlecitinib can have for patients with AA, highlighting the importance of sustained treatment, although longer-term follow-up data will be needed to further understand the impact of ritlecitinib treatment in real-world clinical practice.

The present survey also demonstrated how AA impacted HRQOL among patients who recently initiated ritlecitinib. Physicians reported that a majority of patients had learned to manage their AA prior to initiating ritlecitinib, but for many it was still a major problem in everyday life and caused considerable psychosocial burden. Physicians reported that the majority of their patients felt embarrassed, frustrated, stressed, annoyed, and anxious/worried; feared persistence of the condition; and avoided social interactions prior to initiating ritlecitinib. These findings support previous studies on the psychosocial burden among patients with AA. In an online survey of 216 patients in the USA, 85% of respondents said that coping with AA was a daily challenge, and 85% reported making different life decisions regarding relationships, career, or education due to their AA [11]. In a survey of 26 patients with AA in Finland, 23% reported a psychiatric diagnosis of anxiety, which was often received after the diagnosis of AA [29]. In the study in Finland, the impact of AA on HRQOL was correlated with the extent of hair loss; however, a US survey of 259 patients found that impacts of AA on quality of life were not correlated with the degree of SHL, but rather with patient’s perception of disease severity [12]. Overall, AA has been consistently demonstrated to negatively impact patient quality of life, which is an important component of assessing the severity of disease [9].

In the present study, the majority of patients were receiving ritlecitinib monotherapy, which aligns with current treatment guidelines [18, 19]. Our survey did not explicitly capture whether AA treatments were prescribed or received outside of the survey dermatologist’s clinic. However, dermatologists completed the patient forms using information available in patients’ medical records. Therefore, prior treatments prescribed by other healthcare professionals or at other clinics may have been included if they were documented in the medical record. Just under half of patients received ritlecitinib as their first line of treatment, and the most common treatments prior to ritlecitinib were topical and intralesional corticosteroids [18]. This could suggest that patients may not have had a satisfactory response to these treatments and their AA may have progressed prior to initiating ritlecitinib.

Physician-reported treatment goals and reasons for choosing ritlecitinib reflected the psychosocial burden of AA with the most common goals, including improving quality of life and reducing psychological impact, in addition to goals that address the clinical characteristics of AA (reducing scalp hair loss, achieving long-term control, and reducing eyebrow hair loss). Similar treatment goals were reported in a survey of dermatologists and patients with AA in Europe [30]. Reducing scalp hair loss was the most common treatment goal in the European survey (92% of patients) followed by improving quality of life (49%), reducing psychological impact (38%), and achieving long-term/chronic control of the condition (29%) [30]. The most common physician-reported reason for choosing the current therapy in the European survey was reducing scalp hair loss. In the present study, reducing scalp hair loss was the most common physician-reported reason for choosing ritlecitinib, and this decision is supported by clinical trials and other studies in adults and adolescents that demonstrated the efficacy of ritlecitinib in reducing scalp hair loss [17, 26–28]. Other common physician-reported reasons for choosing ritlecitinib in the present study included the good overall safety profile and safe for long-term use. The safety profile of ritlecitinib has been demonstrated in the ALLEGRO clinical trial program [17, 26–28, 31], of which an integrated safety analysis demonstrated that ritlecitinib is well-tolerated with an acceptable safety profile up to ~5 years [32].

Physicians provided data on consecutively consulting patients meeting the eligibility criteria, thus reducing the chance of selection bias; however, generalizability of the study results to the wider patient population may be impacted in a few ways beyond the limited sample size. First, participants from the AA DSP did not constitute a true random sample, and participation was influenced by the willingness to complete the record forms. Second, the patient sample may have been more severely affected by their disease and/or treatment due to their consulting nature and patients who consulted more frequently were more likely to be included in the patient sample. Third, study data are self-reported by participants, as no independent verification was possible due to the nature of the DSP methodology. Fourth, as the study used data from real-world clinical practice, not all outcomes relevant to these patients were able to be captured, and some data may be missing for the selected outcomes. Fifth, a minority of patients were not receiving ritlecitinib monotherapy, which may be a confounding factor. Sixth, as patients’ SHL and disease severity were reported only for the time periods at treatment initiation and at the time of survey, data were not available on whether patients had responded and subsequently relapsed between treatment initiation and the survey. And finally, as patients currently on treatment may be more likely to feel satisfied with their therapy (i.e., did not discontinue due to lack of efficacy, safety issues, or other unsatisfactory events), satisfaction results cannot be generalized.

Conclusions

Despite many patients managing the impacts of their severe AA, in this study dermatologists reported substantial negative emotional and daily-life effects among patients due to their AA before initiating ritlecitinib. Ritlecitinib was chosen by dermatologists for its efficacy, safety profile, and cost–benefit ratio and used in nearly half of patients as first-line treatment for severe AA. As ritlecitinib treatment duration increased, disease stability, hair regrowth, physician satisfaction, and perceived treatment success also increased. While selection bias should be considered, results emphasize the importance of sustained treatment for the management of patient expectations and achieving AA treatment goals.
