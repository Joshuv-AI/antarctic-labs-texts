Therapeutic consequences of a diagnostic broncho-alveolar lavage in patients in the Intensive Care Unit due to COVID-19: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hartger I. Welling, Thom J. Wilbers, Jorrit B. A. Welling, C. Tji Gan, Franz M. N. H. Schramel, Hazra S. Moeniralam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhen patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) on the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) were not improving or clinically deteriorated, bronchoscopy with broncho-alveolar lavage (BAL) was performed to rule out potentially treatable bacterial, viral and fungal superinfections. The purpose of this study was to investigate the therapeutic consequences of a diagnostic BAL in ICU patients with COVID-19.MethodsWe performed a retrospective analysis in a cohort of patients with COVID-19 on the ICU of the St. Antonius Hospital Nieuwegein in the Netherlands. We compared the administration of antibiotics, antifungal therapy and prednisolone on the day of the BAL and three days later. We hypothesized that a diagnostic BAL did not result in significant changes in therapeutic management.ResultsIn 73 ICU-patients with COVID-19 who underwent a BAL, the results led to changes in medication prescription in 62% (p < 0.01) of patients. In these patients, the antibiotic management changed in 36% (p < 0.01), the antifungal management in 21% (p < 0.01) and the prednisolone management in 34% (p < 0.01) of the patients.ConclusionsIn ICU-patients who did not improve and therefore underwent a BAL, there was a significant change in antibiotics, antifungal or prednisolone prescriptions in 62% of the patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04525-3.

Introduction

Approximately 10 to 20% of patients with Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) progressed to severe respiratory failure during the early 2020 pandemic, requiring advanced respiratory support, such as nasal high flow oxygen therapy or (non)-invasive mechanical ventilation [1].

Respiratory failure in COVID-19 was primarily observed in a subgroup of patients who entered a hyper-inflammatory phase, also known as cytokine storm [2, 3]. In some cases, this resulted in the development of Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) or the formation of Organizing Pneumonia [4–7].

In a selection of patients with COVID-19 who required advanced respiratory support, clinical deterioration or stagnation of recovery was observed. Potentially caused by bacterial, viral and fungal co-and super-infection, which was associated with an increased morbidity and mortality in mechanically ventilated COVID-19 patients [8–10].

Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) can be used to diagnose potentially treatable co-occurring bacterial, fungal or viral infections [11, 12].

Performing a BAL is an invasive procedure with potential complications such as respiratory deterioration or pulmonary bleeding [13]. Additionally, healthcare workers performing the BAL may be exposed to high concentrations of the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), increasing their risk of infection [14]. Earlier studies investigating the added value of performing a BAL in COVID-19 patients demonstrated relevant therapeutic consequences. However, most of these studies included only a small number of patients [14–17]. The goal of this study was to investigate the therapeutic consequences of a diagnostic BAL in patients with COVID-19 in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU).

Methods

We performed a retrospective analysis in patients admitted to the ICU of the St. Antonius Hospital Nieuwegein, which is a large secondary teaching hospital in the Netherlands, from March 2020 to April 2022. The Medical Ethics Committee United (MEC-U), Nieuwegein, The Netherlands, reviewed the study (Institutional Review Board reference number W22.174) and confirmed that it does not fall under the scope of the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO). Therefore, formal ethical approval was not required according to national regulations. Informed consent was not required for this retrospective study, as it did not fall under the WMO. The study involved the use of existing data only and imposed no additional interventions or behavioral rules on participants.

Aim

The purpose of this study was to evaluate whether performing a BAL in ICU patients with COVID-19 leads to changes in clinical management, reflected by changes in administration of antibiotic, antifungal and/or corticosteroid therapy. The primary outcome was defined as the difference in the administration of antibiotics, antifungal agents, and corticosteroids on the day of, and 3 days after the BAL. The BAL result was considered positive if a bacterial, fungal, or viral pathogen other than SARS-CoV-2 was isolated in the bronchoscopic sampling. A positive galactomannan antigen result in BAL-derived material was recorded as a positive fungal culture, even when no specific fungal specimen could be isolated. The cutoff point of 3 days was chosen because it typically took at least 24 to 48 h for the microbiological results to be available and discussed in a multidisciplinary meeting. We hypothesized that a diagnostic BAL had no added value in ICU patients with COVID-19, as it leads to few changes in medical management. Secondary outcome consisted of in-hospital survival. Survival outcomes were compared between patients who underwent BAL and those who did not. In addition, survival was analyzed according to the type of pathogen identified in the BAL fluid.

Study population

We included patients who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test or patients with a strong clinical or radiological suspicion of COVID-19. Patients were excluded if they were admitted to the ICU for less than 24 h or if they did not receive dexamethasone treatment at admission to the ICU. Patients who did not receive dexamethasone were excluded because these patients were often admitted to the ICU for other reasons and had an additional SARS-CoV-2 infection without clinical COVID-19. Patients with incomplete data due to transfer to and from other hospitals were also excluded.

Data collection

In addition to the above outcome measures, the following variables were obtained: demographic information, dates of admission/discharge/death, PCR test results, presence of comorbidities, and the type of respiratory support provided. Data were collected from the Electronic Patient record system (EPIC) and processed in the online data management system REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) [18]. Data were anonymized and not traceable to individual patients.

Standard ICU procedure

COVID-19 patients admitted to the ICU because of respiratory failure, received dexamethasone 6 milligram for maximally 10 days or until hospital discharge, Interleukine-6 inhibitor, preferably tocilizumab, 8 milligram/kilogram with a maximum of 400 milligram, or sarilumab 400 milligram fixed dose. High prophylactic, 2 times a day a fixed dose 5700IE low molecular weight heparin (weight or renal function adjusted), and prophylactic antibiotics (cefuroxime for 4 days and selective digestive decontamination (SDD) throughout the entire ICU stay). Sputum baseline cultures, urinary antigen tests for Legionella pneumophila and Streptococcus pneumoniae, and a viral throat swab were obtained to rule out other pathogens. The viral throat swab was analyzed using a multiplex polymerase chain reaction array detecting Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2, Influenza A virus, Influenza B virus, Respiratory Syncytial Virus, Parainfluenza viruses, Human Metapneumovirus, Rhinoviruses, and Adenoviruses. These procedures were performed to further clarify the etiological pathogen. Suspected or proved by culture bacterial superinfections were treated with targeted antibiotics. The indication for BAL was based on the treating physician’s clinical assessment, including the severity and progression of respiratory symptoms, findings on radiological imaging suggestive of atypical or progressive pulmonary involvement, and clinical deterioration despite appropriate empirical antibiotic therapy. Decisions were made on an individual patient basis, integrating these clinical and diagnostic factors. The volume of saline instilled during BAL varied depending on the bronchoscopist’s judgment and the patient’s respiratory stability, typically using multiple aliquots to achieve an adequate sample. Most procedures involved the use of 20 cc of saline per segment. In principle, lavage was directed toward segments corresponding to radiological abnormalities. In cases with extensive pulmonary involvement BAL was generally performed in multiple affected regions rather than being limited to a single site.

Patient characteristics

Patient characteristics were described in detail, including demographic data and the presence of key comorbidities. Immunocompromised status was defined as primary (congenital) immunodeficiencies, prolonged or severe neutropenia, advanced HIV infection (CD4 < 200/µl), allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, solid organ transplantation, or significant immunosuppressive medication. Chronic kidney insufficiency was defined as eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m² for ≥ 3 months and/or albumin creatinine ratio ≥ 3 mg/mmol. Chronic respiratory insufficiency was diagnosed with FEV1/FVC < 0.7, assessed by FEV1, dyspnoea, and exacerbation frequency. Chronic cardiovascular insufficiency was characterised by symptoms and signs of heart failure with evidence of systolic (LVEF < 40%) or diastolic dysfunction on echocardiography. These definitions are based on international guidelines and adapted for Dutch intensive care practice in the National Intensive Care Evaluation (NICE) database [19].

Medication management change post-BAL

The selection of antibiotics and antifungal agents analyzed before and 3 days after the BAL are listed in Appendix 1. A selection was made of antibiotics, excluding vancomycin, cefuroxime, and SDD, as these antibiotics were frequently given prophylactically or primarily for indications other than bacterial respiratory infections, such as central-line associated bloodstream infections.

Statistical analysis

Differences in patient characteristics between groups were analyzed using an unpaired t-test for continuous data with a normal distribution, and a Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. For binary outcome data, differences between groups were analyzed using the Chi-square test, and differences within a group were analyzed using the McNemar test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or as median [interquartile range (IQR)] for non-normally distributed data. Normal distribution was assessed using Q-Q plots and histograms. Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) software, IBM (New York, USA), version 26. Outcomes were considered statistically significant at a p-value < 0.05.

Results

A total of 622 patients admitted to the ICU with COVID-19 were identified. Of these, 135 patients were excluded from analysis because they were admitted to the ICU for less than 24 h or did not receive dexamethasone for COVID-19 related respiratory failure. Of the remaining 487 patients, 73 (15%) underwent a BAL. Among the 73 patients who underwent BAL, 28 (38%) underwent two or more procedures during their hospital stay. Among those who underwent a BAL, 41 patients (56%) had a positive microbiological culture.

The majority of the included patients were male (69%), the mean age was 63 ± 12 years, and an average Body Mass Index (BMI) of 29 ± 6. Patients who underwent a BAL had a significantly higher BMI of 31.4 ± 2.4 (p < 0.01), were significantly more often mechanically ventilated (61.3%, p < 0.01) compared to patients who did not underwent a BAL (Table 1). The median ICU length of stay for COVID-19 patients was 6 days. Patients who underwent a BAL had a median ICU stay of 26 days until discharge or death, significantly longer than patients who did not underwent a BAL (20 days, p < 0.01) (Table 1). The interval between hospital admission and performance of the BAL was 13 days (median) [IQR 9–19]. Adverse events associated with BAL occurred in 8 patients (11%). Desaturation was observed in 6 patients (9%), while hypertension accompanied by discomfort occurred in 2 patients (3%).

Of the 309 patients with a positive SARS-CoV-2 PCR, 37 patients (12.0%) underwent BAL. Among the 178 patients without PCR confirmation of SARS-CoV-2 infection, in whom the diagnosis of COVID-19 was established based on clinical presentation and radiological findings, 36 patients (20.2%) underwent BAL. The median time from hospital admission to BAL was 13 [IQR 8–16] days in the PCR-confirmed group and 15 [IQR 10–22] days in the clinically and radiologically diagnosed group. BAL yielded a positive microbiological result in 22 patients (59.5%) with PCR-confirmed COVID-19 and in 19 patients (52.8%) in the clinically and radiologically diagnosed group.

Changes in medication management post-BAL

In patients who underwent a BAL, medication management was significantly adjusted in 62% (p < 0.01). After undergoing a BAL, antibiotic (36%, p < 0.01), antifungal (21%, p < 0.01), and prednisolone (34%, p < 0.01) prescriptions were significantly modified (Table 2). The microbiological analysis of the BAL identified at least one new pathogen (bacterial, fungal, or viral) in 41 patients (56%). This included 16 cultures of bacterial pathogens, 20 cultures of fungal pathogens, and 16 cultures of viral pathogens (Table 3). Of the 16 positive bacterial BAL cultures, the same bacterium was identified in standard sputum cultures in 13 cases that were obtained within 48 h of the BAL procedure. In patients with an isolated positive galactomannan antigen test in BAL, no fungi were identified in standard sputum cultures obtained within 48 h of the BAL procedure.

Survival

Among 487 ICU patients with COVID-19, 73 (15%) underwent BAL. Mortality was significantly higher in the BAL group compared with the non-BAL group (62% vs. 18%; p < 0.01). Within the BAL group, fungal pathogens were identified in 20 patients with 85% mortality, bacterial pathogens in 16 patients with 56% mortality, and viral pathogens in 16 patients with 81% mortality (Table 4).

Discussion

A BAL in respiratory deteriorating COVID-19 patients in the ICU, led in 62% of the patients to a change in medication regimen concerning antibiotics, antifungal medication, and prednisone within three days. This suggests that BAL outcomes play a crucial role in the short-term decision-making process for the treatment of these critically ill patients.

The findings of our study are largely consistent with previously published research on the utility of BAL in ICU patients with COVID-19. For instance, in a study by Baron et al., BAL led to changes in the medication regimen in 71% of cases in ICU patients with COVID-19 [14]. Similarly, Taton et al. reported medication changes in 60% of patients after BAL [16]. In our study, antibiotic therapy was altered in 36% of cases post-BAL, which aligns with Mehta et al., who reported a 32% change rate [17].

In this study, bacterial pathogens identified in BAL cultures (n = 16) largely corresponded with those isolated from standard bacterial sputum (n = 13) cultures obtained in the days surrounding the BAL procedure. This concordance suggests that, in many cases, BAL did not yield additional bacterial diagnostic information beyond that provided by conventional sputum cultures, raising questions about the incremental value of BAL for bacterial pathogen detection in this clinical context. Our decision to classify an isolated positive galactomannan antigen test as evidence of fungal infection may have influenced the study outcomes. Galactomannan testing in BAL has a sensitivity of approximately 0.88 and a specificity of around 0.81, indicating a substantial but imperfect diagnostic performance [20]. In our cohort, isolated galactomannan positivity was not accompanied by the identification of fungal pathogens on culture, raising uncertainty as to whether true fungal superinfection was present. Given the less-than-perfect specificity of the assay, false-positive results cannot be excluded, and reliance on galactomannan positivity alone may have led to an overestimation of fungal infections. Therefore, the clinical relevance of isolated galactomannan positivity in this context should be interpreted with caution.

An interesting observation is that patients with a COVID-19 diagnosis confirmed by PCR were significantly less likely to undergo BAL compared to those diagnosed based on clinical or radiological criteria. This difference may reflect greater diagnostic uncertainty in the latter group, prompting clinicians to perform BAL to identify an alternative or additional pathogen. However, our data showed that among patients without positive SARS-CoV-2 PCR results (n = 36), the median interval between hospital admission and BAL was 15 days, compared to 13 days in those with positive PCR (n = 37). These findings suggest that although BAL was performed relatively more frequent in patients without PCR-confirmed COVID-19, it was not used as an early diagnostic tool to resolve initial uncertainty, but rather as a later, problem-oriented investigation during a prolonged or atypical disease course.

The diagnostic microbiological yield of BAL in this study (56%) is comparable to previously published data. Taton et al. reported a yield of 42%, Ora et al. 46%, and Bruyneel et al. 60% [15, 16, 21]. However, Baron et al. reported a higher yield (86%), which could be explained by differences in infection pressure between France and the Netherlands and by the inclusion of SARS-CoV-2 as a countable pathogen in the BAL by Baron et al. [14]. Immunocompromised patients at admission underwent BAL significantly more frequently in our study, possibly due to the higher a priori risk of developing superinfections in this group, prompting clinicians to utilize additional diagnostics.

A relevant consideration in our study was the rationale for performing BAL, an invasive and potentially risky procedure, in critically ill COVID-19 patients. After the COVID-19 pandemic, we questioned whether BAL provided meaningful clinical information, as our initial hypothesis was that its added value might be limited. However, the study was designed to evaluate the actual effectiveness of BAL in this population. Interestingly, the results suggest that BAL may indeed have contributed valuable diagnostic and therapeutic insights, despite our initial skepticism. This highlights the importance of carefully weighing the risks and benefits of invasive procedures in critically ill patients, particularly when evidence is limited and clinical uncertainty persists.

BAL in mechanically ventilated ICU patients is generally safe, but complications may occur, mainly temporary worsening of oxygenation and hemodynamic instability. Patients with hypoxic respiratory failure are at higher risk of experiencing further deterioration of oxygenation during bronchoscopy [22]. Reported adverse events in our cohort were 11%, similar to previous studies [23–25]. Although BAL is considered the golden standard for detecting pulmonary infections, less invasive approaches for obtaining microbiological diagnoses exist, including blood cultures and sputum cultures acquired via bronchial suction. Future research could explore the diagnostic yield of sputum cultures or tracheal aspirates compared to BAL.

Patients who underwent BAL appeared to represent the most vulnerable subgroup, as they failed to improve despite ongoing therapy, which may partly explain the higher observed mortality in this group. Mortality was markedly higher among patients undergoing BAL, likely reflecting greater underlying disease severity and selection bias. Within the BAL cohort, fungal and viral infections were associated with particularly poor outcomes, consistent with previous reports in critically ill COVID-19 patients [26–28]. These findings underscore the prognostic impact of secondary infections and the importance of early recognition and management. However, interpretation is limited by the observational study design and small subgroup sizes. Nonetheless, our results may encourage clinicians to consider performing BAL at an earlier stage in selected patients to facilitate timely diagnosis and targeted treatment.

A strength of this study is the relatively large number of included patients, especially in the Netherlands, and the structured recording of the diagnostic yield of BAL and the timing of medication administration. A limitation of this study is that it only examined changes in the medication regimen before and after BAL. This does not conclusively demonstrate that the BAL results were the determining factor for the change in medication. Future studies should focus on documenting the underlying rationale for modifications in medication strategy, preferably supported by an antibiotic stewardship team. In our hospital, an intensivist, microbiologist, pharmacist and pulmonologist.

Another limitation is the lack of comparison between the therapeutic value of BAL and other diagnostic methods, such as CT scans. In this critically ill patient group, extensive diagnostics are often performed in a short period, making it difficult to determine which diagnostic method led to the change in treatment strategy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, performing BAL in ICU patients with COVID-19 significantly more often leads to changes within three days in the medication regimen, particularly in the use of antibiotics, antifungal therapy, and prednisone.
