Ginzburg-Landau minimizers with high topological degrees in an annulus
Amandine Aftalion, Rémy Rodiac

Abstract
. Motivated by recent experiments on fermionic rings, we study the asymptotic behaviour of minimizers of the Ginzburg-Landau (GL) energy in an annulus with a Dirichlet data which depends on the GL parameter on the outer boundary. We show that there is a critical degree of order | ln ⁡ ε | |\ln{\varepsilon}| under which the ground state displays a giant vortex and above which minimizers exhibit a combination of a giant vortex and vortices which tend to the outer boundary as the GL parameter tends to zero. Our analysis relies on the construction of suitable upper and lower bounds, on the extension to a slightly bigger annulus and on the minimization of the mean-field energy appearing in the lower bound. In order to be able to derive the minimum of this energy we use the symmetry of the domain and criticality with respect to inner variations.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

Persistent currents in annular geometries have attracted a lot of attention both for fundamental and experimental reasons. Moreover, they are promising for quantum technologies. Indeed, quantum gases in annuli provide
unique opportunities to explore quantum many body physics
[
39
]
. Long lived supercurrents have been observed and studied in superfluids (starting with Helium
[
20
]
), weakly interacting Bose-Einstein condensates
[
31
,
36
]
and very recently in fermionic superfluid rings
[
12
,
13
]
. These fermionic rings are in a regime of molecular condensate described by the Gross-Pitaevskii equation.

The geometry of the annulus can also be the source of mathematical questions and, for example, the issue of understanding the behaviour of minimizers of the Ginzburg-Landau (GL) energy in an annulus has been stated as an open problem in the seminal book by Bethuel-Brezis-Hélein “Ginzburg-Landau vortices”, cf.
[
10
, Problem 3, Chap. XI]
. More precisely, if a boundary data with degree
d_{\text{out}}
on the outer boundary and with degree
d_{\text{inn}}
on the inner boundary is prescribed, the
issue is to describe minimizers of the GL energy, the location of their zeroes around which the winding-number does not vanish, and the renormalized energy. Some mathematical works allow to give partial answers as we will describe below.

In this article, in addition to the difficulty of dealing with an annular geometry we consider a high exterior degree, as
{\varepsilon}
, a small parameter, tends to zero. The situation is the following: the Dirichlet data is prescribed only on the outer boundary and its degree grows as
{\varepsilon}
tends to zero. Our motivation comes from very recent experiments on fermionic rings, namely the production of persistent currents in annular geometries
[
12
,
13
]
. The ring is realized thanks to a tight confinement. Experimentally, a phase of
2\pi d
is imprinted, which can be modelled mathematically by a prescribed boundary Dirichlet data
e^{id\theta}
. The specificity of the experiment is that for intermediate values of
d
, vortices are nucleated in the bulk, while for lower values of
d
, only the giant vortex is present. An experimental question raised by
[
12
,
13
]
is to understand the critical degree where vortices start to appear in the bulk. In the experiments,
{\varepsilon}
is not so small that
|\ln{\varepsilon}|
is large, so that nucleation occurs for a degree 8. Mathematically, it is easier to deal with the case where
{\varepsilon}
is small. It is quite surprising that so recent experiments can be described by a simple mathematical formulation where the mechanism of creation of vortices is due to an interaction between the geometry of the domain and the high degree of the Dirichlet boundary data on the exterior boundary. We prove in this paper that there is a critical degree of order
|\ln{\varepsilon}|
under which the minimizer has a giant vortex and above which vortices also appear in the bulk and go to the outer boundary as
{\varepsilon}
tends to zero.

1.2.
Main results

Motivated by recent experiments in physics
[
13
]
, we consider a circular annulus
A=B_{R_{2}}(0)\setminus\overline{B}_{R_{1}}(0)
with
0<R_{1}<R_{2}
. For small
{\varepsilon}
, we are interested in the behaviour of minimizers of

E_{\varepsilon}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{A}(1-|u|^{2})^{2}

(1.1)

in a space of maps with degree
d_{\varepsilon}
on the outer boundary. A first natural choice of minimization space could be
\{v\in H^{1}(A,\mathbb{C});|v|=1\text{ on }\partial A\text{ and }\deg(v,\partial B_{R_{2}})=d_{\varepsilon}\}
. However existence and non-existence of minimizers of the GL energy in such a space were studied e.g. in
[
7
,
8
,
17
,
19
,
22
,
33
]
and it was shown that existence is not always guaranteed. It depends on the “size” of annular domains, or more precisely their capacity
[
8
]
, as do the uniqueness and symmetry of minimizers
[
19
,
22
]
. This is why we work with the minimization space:

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}=\{v\in H^{1}(A,\mathbb{C});v=e^{id_{\varepsilon}\theta}\text{ on }\partial B_{R_{2}},\ |v|=1\text{ on }\partial B_{R_{1}}\}

where the boundary data has a topological degree
d_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
The direct method in the calculus of variations can be used to prove the existence of minimizers of
E_{\varepsilon}
in
{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}
. Furthermore, we can check that such a minimizer satisfies the following Euler-Lagrange equations:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}-\Delta u_{\varepsilon}&=&\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}(1-|u_{\varepsilon}|^{2})&\text{ in }A\\
u_{\varepsilon}&=&e^{id_{\varepsilon}\theta}&\text{ on }\partial B_{R_{2}}\\
|u_{\varepsilon}|&=&1\text{ and }u_{\varepsilon}\wedge\partial_{\nu}u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{ on }\partial B_{R_{1}},\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

where
\nu
denotes the unit outward normal to
\partial A
and
a\wedge b=\frac{1}{2}(\bar{a}b-a\bar{b})
for
a,b
in
\mathbb{C}
.

We aim at describing the location of vortices of minimizers of
E_{\varepsilon}
in
\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
in
A
in the limit
{\varepsilon}\to 0
with the degree
d_{\varepsilon}
going to
+\infty
. In order to describe this situation we use the current vector and the Jacobian vorticity measure of a family of minimizers
(u_{\varepsilon})_{{\varepsilon}>0}
. They are defined by

j_{\varepsilon}:=u_{\varepsilon}\wedge\nabla u_{\varepsilon}=(u_{\varepsilon}\wedge\partial_{x}u_{\varepsilon},u_{\varepsilon}\wedge\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}),\quad\mu_{\varepsilon}:=2\partial_{x}u_{\varepsilon}\wedge\partial_{y}u_{\varepsilon}={\rm curl\,}j_{\varepsilon}.

(1.3)

Our first result shows that there exists a critical degree under which, for
{\varepsilon}
small enough, minimizers of
E_{\varepsilon}
in
{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}
are vortex-less and resemble a giant vortex of degree
d_{\varepsilon}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
be a family of minimizers of
E_{\varepsilon}
in
{{\mathcal{I}}}_{\varepsilon}
. Let

\alpha_{c}:=\frac{1}{2\ln\left(R_{2}/R_{1}\right)}.

(1.4)

We recall that
\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)
.

1)

Assume that
d_{\varepsilon}<\alpha_{c}|\ln{\varepsilon}|
. Then

\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{e^{id_{\varepsilon}\theta}}\rightarrow 1,\text{ in }{\mathcal{C}}^{0}_{\text{loc}}(\overline{B}_{R_{2}}\setminus\overline{B}_{R_{1}}).

(1.5)

2)

Assume that
d_{\varepsilon}/|\ln{\varepsilon}|\to\alpha
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
with
\alpha\leq\alpha_{c}
. Then
\lim_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}\frac{E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})}{|\ln{\varepsilon}|^{2}}=\pi\alpha^{2}\ln\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}
,

\frac{j_{\varepsilon}}{|\ln{\varepsilon}|}\rightharpoonup\frac{\alpha\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}}{r}\text{ in }L^{2}(A)\text{ and }\frac{\mu_{\varepsilon}}{|\ln{\varepsilon}|}\rightharpoonup 0\text{ in }({\mathcal{C}}^{0,\gamma}_{c}(\overline{B}_{R_{2}}\setminus\overline{B}_{R_{1}}))^{*}\text{ for all }\gamma\in(0,1).

We point out that
\alpha_{c}
is large for a small capacity.
Our second theorem states that, when
d_{\varepsilon}=\alpha|\ln{\varepsilon}|
with
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
then vortices appear in the domain and they are close to the exterior boundary.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
be a family of minimizers of
E_{\varepsilon}
in
{{\mathcal{I}}}_{\varepsilon}
. Assume that
d_{\varepsilon}/|\ln{\varepsilon}|\to\alpha
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Recall that
\alpha_{c}
is defined in (
1.4
). If
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
then :

\displaystyle\lim_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}\frac{E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})}{|\ln{\varepsilon}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\pi(\alpha-\frac{1}{4\ln\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}}),

\displaystyle\frac{j_{\varepsilon}}{|\ln{\varepsilon}|}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\frac{\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}}{2\ln(R_{2}/R_{1})r}\text{ in }L^{2}(A)\text{ and }

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{\varepsilon}}{|\ln{\varepsilon}|}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{2\ln\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}}\right)\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\partial B_{R_{2}}}\text{ in }({\mathcal{C}}^{0,\gamma}_{c}(\overline{B}_{R_{2}}\setminus\overline{B}_{R_{1}}))^{*}\text{ for all }\gamma\in(0,1).

This result states that, when
\alpha>\alpha_{c}
minimizers look like a combination of a giant vortex of degree of order
(1/2\ln(R_{2}/R_{1}))|\ln{\varepsilon}|
plus a sum of approximately
d_{\varepsilon}-1/2\ln(R_{2}/R_{1})|\ln{\varepsilon}|
vortices located near
\partial B_{R_{2}}
. However, although these vortices converge to the boundary
\partial B_{R_{2}}
they are at a distance much bigger than
{\varepsilon}
of it. Hence they do not cost twice as much as inner vortices as this is the case for vortices at a distance less than
{\varepsilon}
from
\partial B_{R_{2}}
.

We observe that in the case of rotating superfluids, the situation is similar but different: at low velocity, there is a giant vortex and as the velocity is increased, vortices are nucleated in the bulk
[
20
,
31
]
but they do not approach the outer boundary. Indeed the trapping potential allows an equilibrium position for the circle of vortices inside the condensate
[
2
]
. This takes place at a critical rotational velocity of order
|\ln{\varepsilon}|+\omega\ln|\ln{\varepsilon}|
.

There are two difficulties emerging in our problem which are the annular geometry and the large number of vortices. The proof of Theorem
1.1
makes use of a splitting of the energy obtained by writing minimizers as
u_{\varepsilon}=\eta_{\varepsilon}e^{id_{\varepsilon}\theta}v
where
\eta_{\varepsilon}
is the minimizer of the energy among real-valued functions in
H^{1}((R_{1},R_{2}))
satisfying
\eta(R_{1})=\eta(R_{2})=1
. This decomposition, originally due to
[
32
]
, allows to write
E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})=E_{\varepsilon}(\eta_{\varepsilon}e^{id_{\varepsilon}\theta})+G_{\varepsilon}(v)
where
G_{\varepsilon}(v)
is a weighted GL energy with an interaction term whose sign is not controlled. In order to prove that, if
u_{\varepsilon}
minimizes
E_{\varepsilon}
, then
G_{\varepsilon}(v)=0
and
v=1
we use a weighted Jacobian estimate due to Jerrard
[
28
]
in the same way it was employed in
[
3
]
to prove uniqueness of the real-valued minimizer in a Bose-Einstein condensate under low-rotation.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
consists in constructing good test functions to obtain an upper bound on the value of the minimum of the energy and to derive a corresponding lower bound. The upper bound is built with a giant vortex of degree of order
\beta|\ln{\varepsilon}|
and about
(\alpha-\beta)|\ln{\varepsilon}|
vortices located on a circle close to the outer boundary and optimizing on
\beta
. For the lower bound, we use the vortex-balls construction of Sandier
[
44
]
and Jerrard
[
27
]
, and the Jacobian estimates of Jerrard-Soner
[
30
]
. A key tool to understand the concentration phenomena close to the boundary is to extend the wave function in a slightly larger annulus before applying the estimates.

Another approach to tackle this problem could be to use the conformal transform between an annulus and a finite cylinder. This has been analyzed by Fetter et al
[
20
,
23
]
using image vortices: vortices located on a circle inside the annulus are mapped to vortices on a line in a cylinder. For the
finite cylinder, the optimal wave function is constructed for each vortex, by taking into
account a series of image vortices. This changes the decay of the wave function at
infinity from algebraic to exponential, see also
[
4
]
.
We have not used this conformal transform here but it is likely that this approach and image vortices could be used to better understand the interaction with the boundary and the energy expansion. At least extending the wave function to compute a proper
interaction term seems important.

In the case of large degree, that is
d_{\varepsilon}\gg|\ln{\varepsilon}|
, at leading order, vortices accumulate close to the outer boundary:

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
be a family of minimizers of
E_{\varepsilon}
in
{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}
. Assume that there exists
\delta>0
such that
|\ln{\varepsilon}|\ll|d_{\varepsilon}|\ll{\varepsilon}^{\delta-1}
. Then,

j_{\varepsilon}/d_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0
in
L^{2}(A)
. Furthermore if we let
a>0
and if we define

\tilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}:=\begin{cases}\mu_{\varepsilon}&\text{ in }A,\\
0&\text{ in }B_{R_{2}+a}\setminus B_{R_{2}},\end{cases}

then
\tilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}/d_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\partial B_{R_{2}}}
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(A_{a})
.

We observe that the convergence obtained in this proposition is much weaker than in the previous theorems. But it roughly indicates that the vorticity is concentrated near the boundary. Although it does not appear at first order it is possible that there is still a giant vortex of lower order on the inside boundary.

1.3.
Main ideas

In their pioneering work
[
10
]
, Bethuel-Brezis-Hélein studied the emergence of vortices for the Ginzburg-Landau (GL) energy
(
1.1
) in a smooth star-shaped domain
{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with a fixed boundary data
u=g\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\partial{\Omega},\mathbb{S}^{1})
having a non zero topological degree
\deg(g,\partial{\Omega})=d\neq 0
. They obtained in
[
10
]
that for small values of the parameter
{\varepsilon}
, minimizers of the energy have exactly
d
vortices of degree one whose limiting locations minimize a renormalized energy. We also refer to
[
50
]
,
[
14
]
for an extension of these results to simply-connected domains. We point out that all the limiting vortices are inside the domain
{\Omega}
.

Generalizing this result to the framework of annuli and a large degree is the aim of this paper.
The main tool to handle this problem is the vortex-balls construction independently devised by Jerrard
[
27
]
and Sandier
[
44
]
. Thanks to this vortex-balls construction, the behaviour of minimizers with large GL energy can be understood. For example, this is the case of the GL energy with exterior magnetic field
h_{\text{ex}}
of order
|\ln{\varepsilon}|
, many vortices may appear inside the domain and their location is related to the obstacle problem, see
[
46
]
and references therein. Actually the vortex-balls construction allows to treat also the case of non simply connected domain as observed e.g. in
[
30
]
,
[
5
]
and also to treat vacuum manifolds
\mathcal{N}
which are different from
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, see e.g.
[
34
]
,
[
35
]
. The renormalized energy for non simply connected domains was derived in
[
15
]
and
[
43
]
. In particular the results of
[
34
,
35
,
43
]
, allow to obtain results analogous to the one of Bethuel-Brezis-Hélein
[
10
]
for multiply connected domains. But the extra difficulty in our paper is the large degree.

In
[
47
]
, Sandier-Soret considered the limit of minimizers of the Bethuel-Brezis-Hélein renormalized energy, and proved that the singularities of harmonic maps
minimizing the renomalized energy go to the boundary when their number becomes large, that is when the degree
d
tends to
+\infty
. This corresponds to studying firstly the limit
{\varepsilon}\to 0
to get the renormalized energy and then to let the number of points or the degree
d
tend to
+\infty
. They found that the empirical probability measures
\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{x_{i}}
always converge to a measure supported on the boundary of the domain. Since in our case, the degree is large and depends on
{\varepsilon}
, this is a double limit and we cannot apply their proof as such. Their limiting measure only contains an interaction term while ours encompasses a mass term as well, since in our case the number of vortices is not fixed a priori and is an unknown of the problem.

The GL energy in a regime allowing for a diverging number of vortices was studied by Jerrard-Soner
[
30
]
. More precisely they studied the
\Gamma
-limit of
E_{\varepsilon}/|\ln{\varepsilon}|^{2}
. We observe that this is exactly the energy scaling of Theorem
1.2
. However their results cannot be applied directly to our setting because they did not consider any boundary data. There also exist other results about solutions to the GL equations with a diverging number of vortices. For example, the asymptotic behaviour of critical points of
E_{\varepsilon}
was studied by Sandier-Serfaty in
[
45
]
and
[
46
, Chapter 7]
. They obtained that if the limit of the vorticity measures
\mu_{\varepsilon}/\sqrt{E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})}
belongs to
L^{p}(\Omega)
, where
\Omega
is a smooth simply connected domain, for some
p>1
then necessarily
\mu=0
. This means that if many vortices appear, they have to concentrate on singular sets (with respect to the Lebesgue measure). This was later extended in
[
42
]
where it is shown that actually, limiting vorticity measures can only concentrate on a union of (possibly intersecting) smooth curves. Thus we expect a priori that the limit of
\mu_{\varepsilon}/\sqrt{E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})}
in the sense of measures will be supported on curves possibly included in the boundary. We obtain a more precise result by the construction of an upper bound and a lower bound. More specifically, we obtain that when vortices appear, the curve on which they concentrate is the outer boundary. In fact, to understand this phenomenon of concentration, we need to extend the boundary data slightly away from the outer boundary. We also point out that it is crucial for us to work with a circular annulus since it implies that the curves where vortices concentrate can only be circles and this reduces the problem to a 1D problem.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we provide upper bounds for the value of the minimum of the energy
E_{\varepsilon}
in
{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}
. The main upper bound is obtained by constructing test functions which are made of a giant vortex of degree
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\leq d_{\varepsilon}
and
d_{\varepsilon}-\gamma_{\varepsilon}
vortices near the boundary. We optimize this upper bound with respect to the number of vortices and find the critical value such that for
d_{\varepsilon}\geq\left(1/2\ln(R_{2}/R_{1})\right)|\ln{\varepsilon}|
then it is preferable to have vortices near
\partial B_{R_{2}}
. In Section
3
we recall the vortex-balls construction of Jerrard
[
27
]
and Sandier
[
44
]
and the Jacobian estimates of Jerrard-Soner
[
29
]
. These tools are tailored to describe vortices in the interior of the domain. In order to capture the possible convergence of vortices to the boundary we need to extend minimizers to a slightly bigger annulus. The most difficult case is the case where
d_{\varepsilon}
is of order
|\ln{\varepsilon}|
and is treated in Section
4
where we prove Theorem
1.2
and obtain the second item of Theorem
1.1
. Section
5
is devoted to the proof of the first item of Theorem
1.1
, namely we show the behaviour of the minimizer when
d_{\varepsilon}<\alpha_{c}|\ln{\varepsilon}|
and
{\varepsilon}
is small enough. On the contrary many vortices appear near the boundary
\partial B_{R_{2}}
when
d_{\varepsilon}\gg|\ln{\varepsilon}|
and this is the object of Section
6
.

2.
Upper bounds

In this section we construct two upper bounds for
\min_{v\in{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}}E_{\varepsilon}(v)
. Each of these upper bounds will be optimal for some regimes of
d_{\varepsilon}
. The first one is a giant vortex and the second one with a circle of vortices inside the bulk.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
u_{\varepsilon}
be a minimizer of
E_{\varepsilon}
in
{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}
then

E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq\pi d_{\varepsilon}^{2}\ln\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}\left(1-\frac{{\varepsilon}^{2}d_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{8\ln\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}}\left(\frac{1}{R_{1}^{4}}-\frac{1}{R_{2}^{4}}\right)+O({\varepsilon}^{4}d_{\varepsilon}^{4})\right)\leq\pi d_{\varepsilon}^{2}\ln\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}.

Proof.

A first upper bound can be obtained by taking as a test function
v_{\varepsilon}=e^{id_{\varepsilon}\theta}
and observing that
E_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})=\pi d_{\varepsilon}^{2}\ln\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}
. A more refined one is to restrict the minimization of
E_{\varepsilon}
to functions of the type
\eta e^{id_{\varepsilon}\theta}
where
\eta
is real valued,
\eta(R_{1})=\eta(R_{2})=1
. Then the energy is

I_{\varepsilon}(\eta)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}(\nabla\eta)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}\frac{d_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{r^{2}}\eta^{2}+\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{A}(1-\eta^{2})^{2}.

(2.1)

Note that this can be rewritten as

\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}(\nabla\eta)^{2}+\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{A}\left(1-\frac{d_{\varepsilon}^{2}{\varepsilon}^{2}}{r^{2}}-\eta^{2}\right)^{2}+\int_{A}\left(-\frac{{\varepsilon}^{2}d_{\varepsilon}^{4}}{4r^{4}}+\frac{d_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{2r^{2}}\right).

(2.2)

The last term is a constant term which provides the leading order term of the estimate.
We can use as a test function
\eta^{2}=1-d_{\varepsilon}^{2}{\varepsilon}^{2}/r^{2}
matched to 1 at
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
on a size of order
d_{\varepsilon}^{2}{\varepsilon}^{2}
, which leads the estimate, the leading order term coming from the second term.
∎

Thanks to the formulation (
2.2
), proceeding as in
[
1
]
, we can obtain that the minimizer
\eta_{\varepsilon}
among real functions is uniformly close to 1 with an error of order
{\varepsilon}^{2}d_{\varepsilon}^{2}
. We see that as soon as
d_{\varepsilon}\ll 1/{\varepsilon}
, then minimizing among real functions or taking 1 is equivalent at leading order.

A more refined upper bound consists in putting a giant vortex of degree
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
and
N_{\varepsilon}
vortices on a circle close to the outer boundary:

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume that
d_{\varepsilon}\gg 1
and let
u_{\varepsilon}
be a minimizer of
E_{\varepsilon}
in
{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}
. For any
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
in
\mathbb{N}
with
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\leq d_{\varepsilon}
and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\gg 1
, if
N_{\varepsilon}:=d_{\varepsilon}-\gamma_{\varepsilon}
satisfies that
1\ll N_{\varepsilon}\ll{\varepsilon}^{-1}
, then there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq\pi\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\ln\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}+\pi N_{\varepsilon}(|\ln\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}N_{\varepsilon}}|+C)+\pi\gamma_{\varepsilon}N_{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{1-\frac{\pi}{2N_{\varepsilon}}}+O(d_{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}+d_{\varepsilon}^{3}{\varepsilon}^{2}).

(2.3)

The idea to construct this upper bound is to take test-functions of the form
v_{\varepsilon}=e^{i\gamma_{\varepsilon}\theta}w_{\varepsilon}
with
w_{\varepsilon}
which is equal to one in most of the annulus except near the boundary
\partial B_{R_{2}}
. Furthermore
w_{\varepsilon}
has
N_{\varepsilon}=d_{\varepsilon}-\gamma_{\varepsilon}
vortices of degree 1 equi-distributed on a circle of radius
R_{2}(1-\frac{\pi}{2N_{\varepsilon}})>R_{1}
. The first term in the upper bound is the energy of the giant vortex, the next term is the energy of the vortex balls and the last term is the interaction energy between the giant vortex and the individual vortex balls.

We start by showing a decoupling lemma for functions of the form

\tilde{j}_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\tilde{j}\text{ in }L^{2}(A_{a})\quad\text{ and }\tilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\tilde{\mu}\text{ in }({\mathcal{C}}_{c}^{0,\gamma}(A_{a}))^{*}\text{ for any }\gamma\in(0,1).

Furthermore,
{\rm curl\,}\tilde{j}=\tilde{\mu}
in
A_{a}
,
\dive\tilde{j}=0
in
A
and
{\rm supp\,}\tilde{\mu}\subset\overline{B_{R_{2}}}\setminus\overline{B_{R_{1}}}
. We have

\liminf_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}\frac{E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})}{|\ln{\varepsilon}|^{2}}\geq\frac{\|{\rm curl\,}\tilde{j}\|}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}|\tilde{j}|^{2}

(4.1)

Here
\|\mu\|
denotes the total variation of the measure
\mu
, i.e.,
\|\mu\|=|\mu|(A_{a})
.

Proof.

We follow the proof of
[
46
, Theorem 7.1, Item 1)]
. We first observe that, when
d_{\varepsilon}/|\ln{\varepsilon}|\to\alpha
, then the GL energy of minimizers is of order
|\ln{\varepsilon}|^{2}
thanks to Lemma
2.1
. We can write that

E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},A)=E_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon},A_{a})-\pi d_{\varepsilon}^{2}\ln\frac{R_{2}+a}{R_{2}}.

(4.2)

We apply the vortex-balls construction of Theorem
3.1
to
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}
to find a finite collection of balls
B_{i}^{\varepsilon}=B(x_{i,{\varepsilon}},r_{i,{\varepsilon}})
for

such that

E_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon},A_{a})\geq\pi D_{\varepsilon}\left(\ln\frac{r_{\varepsilon}}{D_{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}}-C\right)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{A_{a}\setminus\left(\cup B_{i}^{\varepsilon}\cap A_{a}^{\varepsilon}\right)}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2},

where

r_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}r_{i,{\varepsilon}},\quad A_{a}^{\varepsilon}=\{x\in A_{a};{\rm dist\,}(x,\partial A_{a})>{\varepsilon}\},\quad D_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}|n_{i,{\varepsilon}}|,

with
n_{i,{\varepsilon}}=\deg(u_{\varepsilon}/|u_{\varepsilon}|,\partial B_{i}^{\varepsilon})
if
B_{i}^{\varepsilon}\subset A_{a}^{\varepsilon}
and
n_{i,{\varepsilon}}=0
otherwise and

is a universal constant. We can choose
r_{\varepsilon}
which goes to zero but with
|\ln r_{\varepsilon}|\ll|\ln{\varepsilon}|
since, from the energy upper bound,
r_{\varepsilon}
can be chosen so that
1>r_{\varepsilon}>C{\varepsilon}^{\delta/2}
for any
\delta\in(0,1)
. From Theorem
3.1
we also have

|D_{\varepsilon}|\leq C\frac{E_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon},A_{a})}{\delta|\ln{\varepsilon}|},\quad\forall\delta\in(0,1).

(4.3)

In particular we find that
|D_{\varepsilon}|\leq C|\ln{\varepsilon}|
and we obtain the lower bound

E_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon},A_{a})\geq\pi D_{\varepsilon}|\ln{\varepsilon}|-o(|\ln{\varepsilon}|^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\int_{A_{a}\setminus\left(\cup B_{i}^{\varepsilon}\cap A_{a}^{\varepsilon}\right)}|\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}.

(4.4)

Now we use that
|\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\geq|\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\wedge\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}=|\tilde{j}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}
in
A_{a}
.

We have that
\frac{\tilde{j}_{\varepsilon}}{|\ln{\varepsilon}|}
is bounded in
L^{2}(A_{a})
, thus, up to a subsequence it converges weakly in
L^{2}
towards some
\tilde{j}\in L^{2}(A_{a})
. We then take a sequence
{\varepsilon}_{n}\to 0
and we set
\mathcal{A}_{N}=\bigcup_{n\geq N}V_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}
where

V_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}=\left(\bigcup_{i\in I_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}}B_{i}^{{\varepsilon}_{n}}\right)\cap\{x\in A_{a};{\rm dist\,}(x,\partial A_{a})>{\varepsilon}_{n}\}.

Up to a subsequence, we have that
|\mathcal{A}_{N}|\to 0
as

. Indeed, recall that
r_{{\varepsilon}}=\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}r_{i,{\varepsilon}}
and observe that
\sum_{i\in I_{\varepsilon}}|r_{i,{\varepsilon}}|^{2}\leq r_{\varepsilon}^{2}
. Now, since
r_{\varepsilon}\to 0
, up to a subsequence we have that the series
\sum_{n\geq 1}r_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}^{2}\to 0
converges and this implies that
|\mathcal{A}_{N}|\to 0
as
N\to+\infty
.
But for every
N
fixed, we see that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\int_{A_{a}\setminus V_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}}\frac{|\tilde{j}_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}}{|\ln{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\int_{A_{a}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{N}}\frac{|\tilde{j}_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}}{|\ln{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}}\geq\int_{A_{a}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{N}}|\tilde{j}|^{2}.

We can then pass to the limit

to find

\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\int_{A_{a}\setminus V_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}}\frac{|\tilde{j}_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}|^{2}}{|\ln{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}}\geq\int_{A_{a}}|\tilde{j}|^{2}.

(4.5)

Now, since
j_{\varepsilon}\cdot\tau=j_{\varepsilon}\cdot\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}=\frac{d_{\varepsilon}}{r}
on
\partial B_{R_{2}}
, a computation in the sense of distribution shows that

{\rm curl\,}\tilde{j}_{\varepsilon}=\begin{cases}{\rm curl\,}j_{\varepsilon}&\text{ in }A\\
0&\text{ in }B_{R_{2}+a}\setminus\overline{B}_{R_{2}}\end{cases}=2\partial_{x}\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\wedge\partial_{y}\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}.

From (
4.3
) we deduce that
\left(2\pi\sum_{i\in I_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}}n_{i,{\varepsilon}_{n}}\delta_{x_{i,{\varepsilon}_{n}}}\right)/|\ln{{\varepsilon}_{n}}|
is a bounded sequence of measures and thus, up to a subsequence, it converges to some limit. From the Jacobian estimate of Theorem
3.2
this limit is also the limit of
\tilde{\mu}_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}/|\ln{\varepsilon}_{n}|={\rm curl\,}\tilde{j}_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}
. Since
\tilde{\mu}_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}/|\ln{\varepsilon}_{n}|\rightharpoonup{\rm curl\,}\tilde{j}
in the sense of distributions we find that

\frac{2\pi\sum_{i\in I_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}}d_{i,{\varepsilon}_{n}}\delta_{x_{i,{\varepsilon}_{n}}}}{|\ln{{\varepsilon}_{n}}|}\rightharpoonup\tilde{\mu}:={\rm curl\,}\tilde{j}\text{ in }\mathcal{M}(A_{a}).

Hence,

\liminf_{{\varepsilon}_{n}\to 0}\frac{\pi|D_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}||\ln{{\varepsilon}_{n}}|}{|\ln{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}}\geq\frac{\|\tilde{\mu}\|(A_{a})}{2}.

(4.6)

Note that Theorem
3.3
implies that
\tilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}
converges to
\tilde{\mu}
in
({\mathcal{C}}_{c}^{0,\gamma}(A_{a}))^{*}
for any
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

By using (
4.4
),(
4.5
) and (
4.6
) we find that

\liminf_{{\varepsilon}_{n}\to 0}\frac{E_{{\varepsilon}_{n}}(\tilde{u}_{{\varepsilon}_{n}},A_{a})}{|\ln{\varepsilon}_{n}|^{2}}\geq\frac{\|\tilde{\mu}\|(A_{a})}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{A_{a}}|\tilde{j}|^{2}

(4.7)

with
{\rm curl\,}\tilde{j}=\tilde{\mu}
in
A_{a}
and
{\rm supp\,}\tilde{\mu}\subset\overline{B}_{R_{2}}\setminus\overline{B}_{R_{1}}
. This last property comes from the fact that
{\rm curl\,}\tilde{j}_{\varepsilon}=0
in
B_{R_{2}+a}\setminus\overline{B}_{R_{2}}
for every
{\varepsilon}
. Now we obtain (
4.1
) by using (
4.7
), (
4.2
) and by observing that
\int_{A_{a}\setminus A}|\tilde{j}|^{2}=\pi\alpha^{2}\ln(\frac{R_{2}+a}{R_{2}})
.
∎

4.2.
Minimization of the function
F

The result of the previous section leads us to study the minimization of the functional

F^{a}(j)=\frac{\|{\rm curl\,}j\|(A_{a})}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}|j|^{2}

(4.8)

defined on

\mathcal{J}^{a}:=\Bigl\{j\in L^{2}(A_{a});\dive(j)=0\text{ in }A,{\rm curl\,}j\in{\mathcal{M}}(A_{a}),\\
{\rm supp\,}{\rm curl\,}j\subset\overline{B}_{R_{2}}\setminus B_{R_{1}},j=\frac{\alpha}{r}\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}\text{ in }B_{R_{2}+a}\setminus B_{R_{2}}\Bigr\}.

(4.9)

Actually, for
j\in{\mathcal{J}}^{a}
we rewrite
F^{a}(j)
as

F^{a}(j)=\frac{\|{\rm curl\,}j\|(A_{a})}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{A_{a}}|j|^{2}-\pi\alpha^{2}\ln\frac{R_{2}+a}{R_{2}}.

(4.10)

A first remark is that
\mathcal{J}^{a}
is a vectorial space and
F^{a}
is strictly convex on
{\mathcal{J}}^{a}
. We can also check that, since the total variation and the
L^{2}
norm are weakly lower semi-continuous for the corresponding topologies,
F^{a}
is weakly lower semi-continuous. An application of the direct method in the calculus of variations yields that
F^{a}
admits a unique minimizer in
\mathcal{J}^{a}
. We are able to describe more precisely this minimizer.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
j_{0}^{a}
be the unique minimizer of
F^{a}
in
{\mathcal{J}}^{a}
then
j_{0}^{a}=f^{a}(r)\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}
for some
f^{a}\in L^{2}((R_{1},R_{2}+a))
and
\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)
.

Proof.

Let
\text{\boldmath$e_{r}$}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
. We consider the action of the group of rotations on elements of
\mathcal{J}^{a}
defined by
(R,j)\in SO(2)\times\mathcal{J}^{a}\mapsto Rj(R^{-1}\cdot)
. The subset
{\mathcal{J}}^{a}\cap\{j\in L^{2}(A_{a});j=g(r)\text{\boldmath$e_{r}$}+f(r)\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}\}
is the set of invariants of
{\mathcal{J}}^{a}
by this action. It can be checked that the functional
F^{a}
is invariant by this action. Furthermore we can write
F^{a}=F_{0}^{a}+F_{1}^{a}
with

F_{0}^{a}(j)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A_{a}}|j|^{2},\quad F_{1}^{a}(j)=\frac{\|{\rm curl\,}j\|(A_{a})}{2}.

Since
F_{0}^{a}
is
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
on
\mathcal{J}^{a}
and
F_{1}^{a}
is a convex, proper, lower semi-continuous function which is invariant by the action of
SO(2)
, we can apply Proposition 3 and Theorem 4 in
[
49
]
to deduce that the principle of symmetric criticality of Palais holds true in our situation. Hence we obtain that the minimizer of
F^{a}
in
{\mathcal{J}}^{a}\cap\{j\in L^{2}(A_{a});j=g(r)e_{r}+f(r)\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}\}
is a critical point of
F^{a}
in
{\mathcal{J}}^{a}
. But by strict convexity, this critical point is the unique minimizer of
F^{a}
in
{\mathcal{J}}^{a}
.
However from the condition
\dive j_{0}^{a}=\frac{1}{r}(rg(r))^{\prime}=0
in
A
, we find that
g(r)=C/r
for some constant
C
in
A
. But
{\rm curl\,}\frac{C}{r}\text{\boldmath$e_{r}$}=0
. Hence we can take
C=0
, since having
C\neq 0
only increases the energy
F^{a}
.
∎

The rest of this section is devoted to find the function
f^{a}
such that
j_{0}^{a}=f^{a}(r)\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}
in
A
. We aim to show

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
j_{0}^{a}
be the unique minimizer of
F^{a}
in
{\mathcal{J}}^{a}
then
j_{0}^{a}=f^{a}(r)\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}
in
A
with
|f^{a}(r)|=\frac{\lambda}{r}
for some constant
\lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
{\rm curl\,}j_{0}^{a}
can be supported only on some circles in
\overline{A}
.

Thanks to Proposition
4.2
, the problem of describing the minimizer
j_{0}^{a}
of
F^{a}
in
{\mathcal{J}}^{a}
reduces to a 1D problem. This is due to fact that we are working in a symmetric domain with rotationally invariant boundary condition. We will obtain Proposition
4.3
by proving that
j_{0}^{a}=\nabla^{\perp}h^{a}
with

which is stationary harmonic in
A
. Here by “stationary harmonic” we mean that it is a critical point of the Dirichlet energy for inner variations or that the stress-energy tensor is divergence-free, cf. e.g.
[
24

(r^{2}|h^{\prime}|^{2})^{\prime}=0

First to each
j\in{\mathcal{J}}^{a}
of the form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\Delta h&=&\mu&\text{ in }A_{a}\\
\partial_{\nu}h&=&\frac{\alpha}{R_{2}+a}&\text{ on }\partial B_{R_{2}+a}\\

(4.11)

where

. Indeed, if

can be written

then we can write

for

radial and
h=\int_{R_{1}}^{r}f(s)ds
. We then see that

(4.12)

We next show that we can rewrite the energy

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}-\Delta_{x}G(x,y)&=&\delta_{y}&\text{ in }A_{a},\\

This Green function satisfies the usual following properties.

Let

3)

Actually we have that

Proof.

1)

The symmetry of the Green function is classical and the proof of Theorem 13 p.35 in

2)

If we write

The third points is proved thanks to the explicit formula of the Green function given in Appendix

Proposition 4.5

Let

H^{a}(\mu)=\frac{\|\mu\|(A_{a})}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\iint_{A_{a}\times A_{a}}G(x,y)d\mu(x)d\mu(y)\\
-\frac{\alpha}{2(R_{2}+a)}\Bigl(\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}+a}}\int_{A_{a}}G(x,y)d\mu(x)d\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)+\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}+a}}\int_{A_{a}}G(x,y)d\mu(y)d\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)\Bigr)\\
+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2(R_{2}+a)}\int_{\partial B_{R_{2}+a}}\int_{B_{R_{2}+a}}G(x,y)d\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)d\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)-\pi\alpha^{2}\ln\frac{R_{2}+a}{R_{2}}.

(4.14)

Proof.

Assume first that
\mu\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(A_{a})

We can actually see that (

(4.17)

Now, for

\pi\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{4\ln\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}}\right)&\text{ if }\alpha>\frac{1}{2\ln(R_{2}/R_{1})}.\end{cases}

But thanks to the upper bounds in Lemma
2.1
and Proposition
2.4
we also have that

\liminf_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}\frac{E_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})}{|\ln{\varepsilon}|^{2}}\leq\begin{cases}\pi\alpha^{2}\ln\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}&\text{ if }\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2\ln(R_{2}/R_{1})}\\
\pi\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{4\ln\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}}\right)&\text{ if }\alpha>\frac{1}{2\ln(R_{2}/R_{1})}.\end{cases}.

This entails, by the strict convexity of
j\in{\mathcal{J}}^{a}\mapsto F^{a}(j)
that
\tilde{j}=j_{0}^{a}
and we know explicitly
j_{0}^{a}
according to the value of
\alpha
. In particular if
\alpha\geq 1/(2\ln(R_{2}/R_{1})
then
j_{0}^{a}=\frac{1}{2\ln(R_{2}/R_{1})r}\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}
in
A
and
\tilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}/|\ln{\varepsilon}|\rightharpoonup{\rm curl\,}j_{0}^{a}=(\alpha-\frac{1}{2\ln(R_{2}/R_{1})})\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\partial B_{R_{2}}}
in
\mathcal{M}(A_{a})
. Hence we obtain that
\tilde{\mu}_{\varepsilon}/|\ln{\varepsilon}|\rightharpoonup\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\partial B_{R_{2}}}
in
{\mathcal{M}}(\overline{B}_{R_{2}}\setminus\overline{B}_{R_{1}})
. This completes the proof of Item 2) Theorem
1.1
and of Theorem
1.2
.
∎

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

This lemma can be proved by using the techniques in

In Ginzburg-Landau theory, to prove uniqueness of minimizers in some special situations it is customary to use a splitting of the energy which takes advantage of the fact that we work with complex-valued function. This was originally devised in

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

be in
{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}
, thanks to Lemma
5.1
, for
{\varepsilon}>0
small enough,

does not vanish in

for some

and

E_{\varepsilon}(u)=E_{\varepsilon}(\eta_{\varepsilon}e^{id_{\varepsilon}\theta})+G_{\varepsilon}(v),

where

G_{\varepsilon}(v)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}\eta_{\varepsilon}^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}+\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{A}\eta_{\varepsilon}^{4}(1-|v|^{2})^{2}+d_{\varepsilon}\int_{A}\frac{\eta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{r}\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}\cdot(v\wedge\nabla v).

Proof.

We let
w_{\varepsilon}:=\eta_{\varepsilon}e^{id_{\varepsilon}\theta}
. We drop the subscript
{\varepsilon}
for simplicity, a computation shows that

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(wv)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}\left(|v|^{2}|\nabla w|^{2}+|w|^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}+2v\nabla w\cdot w\nabla v\right)+\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{A}(1-|wv|^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}\left(|v|^{2}\left[|\nabla\eta|^{2}+\eta^{2}\frac{d_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{r^{2}}\right]+\eta^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{A}\eta\eta^{\prime}v\cdot\partial_{r}v+\int_{A}\frac{d_{\varepsilon}\eta^{2}}{r^{2}}v\wedge\partial_{\theta}v+\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{A}(1-|wv|^{2})^{2}.

Thus

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(wv)-E_{\varepsilon}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}(|v|^{2}-1)(|\nabla\eta|^{2}+\eta^{2}\frac{d_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{r^{2}})+\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}\eta^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{A}\eta\eta^{\prime}v\cdot\partial_{r}v+\int_{A}\frac{d_{\varepsilon}\eta^{2}}{r^{2}}v\wedge\partial_{\theta}v

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{A}[(1-|wv|^{2})^{2})-(1-|w|^{2})^{2}].

We can check that
(1-|wv|^{2})^{2})-(1-|w|^{2})^{2}=2|w|^{2}(1-|v|^{2})-|w|^{4}(1-|v|^{4})
.
Now we use the equation satisfied by
\eta_{\varepsilon}
. It reads

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}-\Delta\eta+\frac{d_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{r^{2}}\eta&=&\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\eta(1-\eta^{2})&\text{ in }A,\\
\eta&=&1&\text{ on }\partial A.\end{array}\right.

(5.3)

We multiply (
5.3
) by
\eta(1-|v|)
, which vanishes on
\partial A
, and integrate by parts to find that

\int_{A}|\nabla\eta|^{2}(|v|^{2}-1)+2\int_{A}\eta\nabla\eta\cdot v\nabla v+\int_{A}\eta^{2}(|v|^{2}-1)\frac{d_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{r^{2}}\\
=\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{A}\eta^{2}(1-\eta^{2})(|v|^{2}-1).

(5.4)

By using (
5.4
) we obtain that

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(wv)-E_{\varepsilon}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{A}\eta^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}+\frac{1}{2{\varepsilon}^{2}}\eta^{2}(1-\eta^{2})(|v|^{2}-1)+\frac{1}{2{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{A}\eta^{2}(1-|v|^{2})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\int_{A}\eta^{4}(1-|v|^{4})+\int_{A}\frac{d_{\varepsilon}\eta^{2}}{r}\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}\cdot(v\wedge\nabla v).

To conclude we observe that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2{\varepsilon}^{2}}\eta^{2}(1-\eta^{2})(|v|^{2}-1)+\frac{1}{2{\varepsilon}^{2}}\eta^{2}(1-|v|^{2})-\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\eta^{4}(1-|v|^{4})

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\eta^{4}}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}(|v|^{2}-1)-\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\eta^{4}(1-|v|^{4})

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta^{4}}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}(1-2|v|^{2}+|v|^{4})=\frac{\eta^{4}}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}(1-|v|^{2})^{2}.

∎

Now our goal is to show that if

is a minimizer of

in

then

is small (like
{\varepsilon}^{\beta}
for some

) in

. With this information and with the help of a gradient bound on

of the type

converges uniformly to

as at the beginning of Section
4

and

by

We define

\xi_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{R_{1}}^{r}\frac{\eta_{\varepsilon}^{2}(s)}{s}ds\quad\text{ for }r\in(R_{1},R_{2})

(5.5)

so that

in

to

(5.6)

We can rewrite the term whose sign is not controlled in

thanks to this function

and an integration by parts:

\displaystyle\int_{A}\frac{\eta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{r}\text{\boldmath$e_{\theta}$}\cdot v\wedge\nabla v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\tilde{A}}}\eta^{2}|\nabla v|^{2}-d_{\varepsilon}\int_{{\tilde{A}}}\xi_{\varepsilon}\mu_{v}+\frac{1}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}\eta^{4}(1-|v|^{2})^{2}

Now we take a non-vanishing smooth function

and

\chi_{\varepsilon}\equiv 1\text{ in }B_{R_{2}+a-\frac{2a_{\varepsilon}}{d_{\varepsilon}}}\setminus\overline{B}_{R_{1}+\frac{2a_{\varepsilon}}{d_{\varepsilon}}},\quad\chi_{\varepsilon}\equiv 0\text{ in }B_{R_{1}+\frac{2a_{\varepsilon}}{d_{\varepsilon}}}\setminus\overline{B}_{R_{1}},\\

We decompose

where

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\tilde{A}}}\chi_{\varepsilon}\left[\frac{\eta_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{2}|\nabla v|^{2}+\frac{\eta_{\varepsilon}^{4}}{4{\varepsilon}^{2}}(|v|^{2}-1)^{2}\right],

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle B

(5.7)

Lemma 5.3
.

(

\displaystyle(1-C{\varepsilon}^{\beta})|A_{2}|

\tilde{{\varepsilon}}={\varepsilon}(1+O((d_{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon})^{2})

We prove in this Appendix that we can give an explicit expression of the Green function (

]

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
G
be the Green function in
A_{a}
defined in (
4.13
). Then for all

we have
G(x,y)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln|x-y|+\varphi(x,y)

\begin{array}[]{rccll}A_{0}&=&-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\frac{\rho}{R_{1}},&B_{0}=&\frac{-1}{2\pi},\\
A_{m}&=&\frac{1}{2\pi m}\frac{\left(\frac{R_{1}}{\rho}\right)^{m}-\left(\frac{\rho}{R_{1}\tilde{R}_{2}^{2}}\right)^{m}}{R_{1}^{m}+R_{1}^{-m}}\cos(m\psi),&B_{m}=&\frac{1}{2\pi m}\frac{\left(\frac{R_{1}}{\rho}\right)^{m}+\left(\frac{R_{1}\rho}{\tilde{R}_{2}^{2}}\right)^{m}}{R_{1}^{m}+\tilde{R}_{2}^{2m}R_{1}^{-m}}\tilde{R}_{2}^{2m}\cos(m\psi),\\
C_{m}&=&\frac{1}{2\pi m}\frac{\left(\frac{R_{1}}{\rho}\right)^{m}-\left(\frac{\rho}{R_{1}\tilde{R}_{2}^{2}}\right)^{m}}{R_{1}^{m}+R_{1}^{-m}}\sin(m\psi),&D_{m}=&\frac{1}{2\pi m}\frac{\left(\frac{R_{1}}{\rho}\right)^{m}+\left(\frac{R_{1}\rho}{\tilde{R}_{2}^{2}}\right)^{m}}{R_{1}^{m}+\tilde{R}_{2}^{2m}R_{1}^{-m}}\tilde{R}_{2}^{2m}\sin(m\psi),\end{array}

where we defined
\tilde{R}_{2}:=R_{2}+a
. In particular
\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\overline{A}_{a}\times A_{a})
and
\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(A_{a}\times\overline{A}_{a})
.

Proof.

Since
\tilde{\varphi}
is harmonic in
A_{a}
we can write it as

\tilde{\varphi}(r,\theta,\rho,\psi)=A_{0}+B_{0}\ln r+\sum_{m=1}^{+\infty}\Bigl[(r^{m}A_{m}+r^{-m}B_{m})\cos(m\theta)\\
+(r^{m}C_{m}+r^{-m}D_{m})\sin(m\theta)\Bigr].

The boundary condition satisfied by
G
on
\partial B_{R_{1}}
implies that
\varphi(x,y)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln|x-y|
for every
x\in\partial B_{R_{1}}
and every
y\in A_{a}
. By using that
\ln|z|=\text{Re}(\ln z)
for any
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}_{-}
we can expand

\displaystyle-\ln|R_{1}e^{i\theta}-\rho e^{i\psi}|

\displaystyle=-\ln\rho-\text{Re}\ln\left(1-\frac{R_{1}}{\rho}e^{i(\theta-\varphi)}\right)

\displaystyle=-\ln\rho+\text{Re}\sum_{m=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{m}\left(\frac{R_{1}}{\rho}\right)^{m}e^{im(\theta-\psi)}

\displaystyle=-\ln\rho+\sum_{m=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{m}\left(\frac{R_{1}}{\rho}\right)^{m}\cos(m(\theta-\psi)).

We thus find a first relation between the coefficients
A_{m},B_{m},C_{m},D_{m}
which can be written as

\displaystyle A_{0}+B_{0}\ln R_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln\rho

\displaystyle R_{1}^{m}A_{m}+R_{1}^{-m}B_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi m}\left(\frac{R_{1}}{\rho}\right)^{m}\cos(m\psi)

\displaystyle R_{1}^{m}C_{m}+R_{1}^{-m}D_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi m}\left(\frac{R_{1}}{\rho}\right)^{m}\sin(m\psi).

Now on
\partial B_{R_{2}+a}
we must have
\partial_{r}\tilde{\varphi}(r,\theta,\rho\psi)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\partial_{r}\ln|re^{i\theta}-\rho e^{i\varphi}|
. To simplify notation we set
\tilde{R}_{2}=R_{2}+a
. We can check that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\partial_{r}\ln|re^{i\theta}-\rho e^{i\varphi}|=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{r}+\sum_{m=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\rho^{m}}{r^{m+1}}\cos(m(\theta-\psi))\right).

This leads to a second relation between the coefficients.

\displaystyle B_{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{2}^{m-1}A_{m}-mB_{m}\tilde{R}_{2}^{-m-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho^{m}}{2\pi\tilde{R}_{2}^{m+1}}\cos(m\psi)

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{2}^{m-1}C_{m}-mD_{m}\tilde{R}_{2}^{-m-1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\rho^{m}}{2\pi\tilde{R}_{2}^{m+1}}\sin(m\psi).

To find the coefficients we thus have to solve the linear systems

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}R_{1}^{m}A_{m}+R_{1}^{-m}B_{m}&=\frac{1}{2\pi m}\left(\frac{R_{1}}{\rho}\right)^{m}\cos(m\psi)\\
m\tilde{R}_{2}^{m-1}-m\tilde{R}_{2}^{-m-1}&=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\rho^{m}}{\tilde{R}_{2}^{m+1}}\cos(m\psi)\end{array}\right.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}R_{1}^{m}C_{m}+R_{1}^{-m}D_{m}&=\frac{1}{2\pi m}\left(\frac{R_{1}}{\rho}\right)^{m}\sin(m\psi)\\
m\tilde{R}_{2}^{m-1}-m\tilde{R}_{2}^{-m-1}&=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\rho^{m}}{\tilde{R}_{2}^{m+1}}\sin(m\psi).\end{array}\right.

This leads to the desired formulas for
A_{m},B_{m},C_{m},D_{m}
. We point out that other derivations of Green functions in an annulus can be found with the help of Jacobi
\theta
functions, see e.g. in
[
20
,
23
]
.

The fact that
\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\overline{A}_{a}\times A_{a})
and
\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(A_{a}\times\overline{A}_{a})
follows from the theorems of differentiation of series.
∎

Appendix B: Convergence of quadratic expressions

In this Appendix we study the convergence of quadratic expressions of measures under weak convergence, see also
[
48
, Lemma 5.3]
and
[
38
, Lemma 6.3]
,

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an open set. Let
K:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be such that
K\in{\mathcal{C}}((\Omega\times\Omega)\setminus{\mathcal{D}})
, where
{\mathcal{D}}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N};x=y\}
, and such that
K\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times\Omega)
. Let
(\mu_{n})_{n}\subset\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
be a sequence of Radon measures such that
\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup\mu
in the sense of measures. Assume furthermore that
\mu
is non-atomic and that
|\mu_{n}|\rightharpoonup|\mu|
in the sense of measures. Then

K(\cdot,\cdot)\mu_{n}\otimes\mu_{n}\rightharpoonup K(\cdot,\cdot)\mu\otimes\mu\quad\text{ in }{\mathcal{M}}(\Omega\times\Omega).

(6.1)

Proof.

By density in
{\mathcal{C}}_{c}(\Omega\times\Omega)
of functions of the form
(x,y)\mapsto\sum_{k,l}f_{k}(x)g_{l}(y)
for
f_{k},g_{l}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{c}(\Omega)
, we obtain that
|\mu_{n}|\otimes|\mu_{n}|\rightharpoonup|\mu|\otimes|\mu|
in
{\mathcal{M}}(\Omega\times\Omega)
. We also have that

\left|K(\cdot,\cdot)\mu_{n}\otimes\mu_{n}\right|\leq\|K\|_{L^{\infty}}|\mu_{n}|\otimes|\mu_{n}|
. Hence we see that
K(\cdot,\cdot)\mu_{n}\otimes\mu_{n}
is uniformly bounded and, up to a subsequence, it converges to a measure
m
in the sense of measures. We can also apply Lemma
6.3
below to obtain that
m
is absolutely continuous with respect to
|\mu|\otimes|\mu|
.
Now we observe that, since
K\in{\mathcal{C}}((\Omega\times\Omega)\setminus{\mathcal{D}})
, for every
\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}_{c}(\Omega\times\Omega)
vanishing on
{\mathcal{D}}
we have that

\displaystyle\iint_{{\Omega}\times{\Omega}}\varphi(x,y)K(x,y)d\mu_{n}(x)d\mu_{n}(y)\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\iint_{{\Omega}\times{\Omega}}\varphi(x,y)K(x,y)d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

Let
\eta\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be such that
\eta\equiv 1
in a neighbourhood of
{\mathcal{D}}
. Then for all
\varphi\in{\mathcal{C}}_{c}(\Omega\times\Omega)
, and for all
\kappa>0

\displaystyle\iint_{{\Omega}\times{\Omega}}\varphi(x,y)K(x,y)d\mu_{n}(x)d\mu_{n}(y)=\iint_{{\Omega}\times{\Omega}}\varphi(x,y)K(x,y)\eta\left(\frac{|x-y|}{\kappa}\right)d\mu_{n}(x)d\mu_{n}(y)

\displaystyle\quad+\iint_{{\Omega}\times{\Omega}}\varphi(x,y)K(x,y)\left[1-\eta\left(\frac{|x-y|}{\kappa}\right)\right]d\mu_{n}(x)d\mu_{n}(y)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}\iint_{{\Omega}\times{\Omega}}\varphi(x,y)K(x,y)\eta\left(\frac{|x-y|}{\kappa}\right)dm(x,y)

\displaystyle\quad+\iint_{{\Omega}\times{\Omega}}\varphi(x,y)K(x,y)\left[1-\eta\left(\frac{|x-y|}{\kappa}\right)\right]d\mu(x)d\mu(y)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\kappa\to 0]{}\iint_{{\mathcal{D}}}\varphi(x,y)K(x,y)dm(x,y)

\displaystyle\quad+\iint_{{\Omega}\times{\Omega}}\varphi(x,y)K(x,y)d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

In the last step we have used the dominated convergence theorem. Now we observe that since
|\mu|
is non-atomic then
|\mu|\otimes|\mu|
does not charge the diagonal
{\mathcal{D}}
, see e.g.
[
16
]
p.566. Indeed , since
|\mu|(y)=0
for all
y\in\Omega
, Fubini’s theorem shows that

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}\textbf{1}_{x=y}d|\mu|(x)d|\mu|(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}d|\mu|(y)\int_{\Omega}\textbf{1}_{\{x=y\}}d|\mu|(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|\mu|(y)d|\mu|(y)=0.

But since
m
is absolutely continuous with respect to
|\mu|\otimes|\mu|
, we also have that
m
does not charge
{\mathcal{D}}
and then

\iint_{{\mathcal{D}}}\varphi(x,y)K(x,y)dm(x,y)=0
. This proves that (
6.1
) holds.
∎

In the above proof we have used the following result, whose proof can be found in
[
48
, Lemma 5.1]

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\Omega
be an open set of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Let
(\nu_{n})_{n}\subset{\mathcal{M}}(\Omega)
and
(\sigma_{n})_{n}\subset{\mathcal{M}}({\Omega})^{+}
be such that there exists
C>0
with
|\nu_{n}|\leq C\sigma_{n}
for all

\nu_{n}\rightharpoonup\nu

\sigma_{n}\rightharpoonup\sigma

\sigma
-measurable function

\nu=f\sigma
and
|f|\leq C
for
\sigma

{\Omega}
