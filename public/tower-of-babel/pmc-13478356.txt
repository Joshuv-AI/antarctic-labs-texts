The Association Between School Physical Education Practices and School Level Socioeconomic Status
Authors: Sam Biver, Ben D Kern, Hans van der Mars, Enbal Shacham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundYouth physical activity in the United States remains low, with disparities by socioeconomic status (SES). Physical education (PE) is an effective structural intervention, yet implementation is below recommendations. This study examines whether elementary school SES is associated with the implementation of recommended PE practices.MethodsElementary school data from School Health Map were merged with National Center for Education Statistics data to fit four mixed‐effects logistic models with SES and locale as fixed effects and state as a random intercept. Outcomes were four recommended PE practices.ResultsSchools with lower SES had higher odds of meeting recommended weekly instructional minutes compared to high‐SES schools. However, low‐SES schools also had higher odds of allowing students to be withheld from PE for disciplinary or academic reasons and lower odds of employing qualified PE teachers. State‐level factors appeared to influence physical education minutes, class size, and teacher qualifications.Implications for School Health Policy, Practice, and EquityAddressing disparities in PE practices will require coordinated state, district, and school‐level efforts that go beyond weekly instructional time to prevent withholding PE, ensure manageable class sizes, and support qualified teachers.ConclusionsLow‐SES schools met PE minutes more often, but other equity gaps remained.Keywords: elementary education, physical education access, physical education class size, physical education quantity, state policy

There is strong evidence that physical activity (PA) enhances child and adolescent health, development, and well‐being [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends that children ages 6–17 years engage in 60 min of moderate to vigorous PA daily [6]. Healthy People 2030, a US national initiative that sets public health objectives each decade, include an objective to increase youth PA to improve the health of the nation [7]. Despite national objectives supporting efforts to increase youth PA, PA levels among US youth remain suboptimal and seem to be declining. In the 2020 school year, 23.6% of children aged 6–13 met the current PA guidelines, which decreased from 25.9% in the 2016 school year [7, 8].

Schools are an ideal setting for promoting youth PA because they provide consistent, structured access to a large and diverse population of students [9, 10]. Specifically, physical education has been shown to be one of the most effective strategies to increase child and adolescent PA [11]. In addition to increasing youth PA, physical education has been shown to improve child and adolescent health and development in several domains, including physical and cognitive wellbeing, and social/emotional self‐regulation [12]. As a result of this evidence, the Healthy People 2030 initiative also has an objective to increase the proportion of US students who attend physical education classes daily [7].

Although federal agencies and national organizations have promoted daily physical education consistently, implementation remains inconsistent because school‐level practices are controlled by state and district policies [10, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Kern et al. found large variations in physical education implementation between states, suggesting that students' access to physical education differs depending on the state where they attend school [17, 18, 19]. This variation likely reflects differences not only in whether policies exist, but also in how clearly and strongly physical education requirements are written [13, 14, 15, 20]. Moreover, limited or lack of accountability and funding may further weaken implementation, as only 25% of codified state physical education policies include accountability language and only one state explicitly funds physical education implementation [21].

Against this policy backdrop, it is important to consider the specific policy components that influence physical education implementation in the US. The Society of Health and Physical Educators of America (SHAPE America) has set national recommendations for state‐level physical education policies, including class frequency and duration, class size, teacher qualifications, and restrictions on substitutions, waivers, exemptions, and withholding physical education [13, 22, 23, 24]. Research on these policy recommendations is disproportionately focused on physical education time and PA achievement, leaving a gap in understanding equity in access [13, 20]. To address this gap, this study examines whether elementary school‐level socioeconomic status (SES) is associated with the implementation of recommended physical education practices including instructional minutes, class size, teacher qualifications, and withholding physical education for disciplinary or academic reasons.

Several core dimensions of physical education implementation remain misaligned with national recommendations. For example, most K‐12 schools provide less physical education than recommended and often have class sizes that exceed those in other subject areas [17, 25, 26]. Large class sizes further compromise implementation, as physical education leaders have identified them as a major barrier to delivering quality physical education [27]. Teacher qualifications also matter, as schools with certified physical education teachers report higher‐quality physical education and higher levels of PA than schools without specialists [28, 29, 30]. Finally, allowing students to be withheld from physical education for disciplinary or academic reasons undermines implementation by reducing students' access to instructional time [31].

These implementation gaps are especially important given persistent disparities in youth PA, with females and non‐White youth consistently reporting lower levels of PA [32]. Although these disparities are often described by individual characteristics, students' opportunities for PA are also shaped by the schools they attend, including the socioeconomic context of those schools. In the United States, school‐level SES is commonly measured by the percentage of students eligible for free or reduced‐price meals, a federal program available to students from households with incomes at or below 185% of the poverty threshold [33], and has been associated with lower levels of PA‐related determinants, including fitness knowledge and sedentary behaviors [34]. Similarly, lower school‐level SES is associated with fewer opportunities for PA [30, 35, 36, 37]. Students in high‐SES elementary schools had on average 4.4 more minutes per day of PA compared to peers in low‐SES schools [30]. Although lower school‐level SES is associated with fewer PA opportunities and lower PA levels, far less is known about whether these school characteristics are associated with implementation of recommended physical education practices.

As stated previously, research on the association between school‐level SES and physical education implementation in the United States is limited and reflects mixed results. Although a 2014 state‐level analysis found that states with greater sociodemographic disadvantage had higher odds of enacting physical education laws consistent with instructional minute recommendations, that analysis was conducted at the state level and did not account for within‐state variation in school SES [38]. These findings underscore the need for school‐level analysis, as variation in local socioeconomic conditions likely contributes to differences in physical education implementation that are obscured in state‐level estimates. At the school level, however, findings remain mixed. For example, one California study found that physical education instructional time policies were associated with an increase in physical education class minutes in high‐income elementary schools and a decrease in mean class size in low‐income middle schools [39]. This pattern is notable in light of broader evidence that lower‐SES schools tend to have larger general class sizes [40, 41]. Another study found that higher‐SES elementary schools were more likely to employ certified physical education teachers but were less likely to meet state‐mandated physical education time requirements [30]. In contrast, other studies reported no association between SES‐related factors and physical education implementation [25, 32]. A study using 2014 data found no association between school‐level SES and total number of physical education practices; however, it did identify low‐SES schools were less likely to prohibit exemptions from physical education [42]. Taken together, these studies provide inconsistent evidence and suggest that the relationship between school‐level SES and physical education implementation remains poorly understood.

Research on the association between school locale (urban, suburban, town, and rural) and PA opportunities, including physical education implementation in the United States is also limited and offers mixed results. For example, schools in rural settings provide more minutes for physical education and recess than suburban and urban schools [43]. Similarly, rural schools report better adherence to physical education and PA practice guidelines than suburban or urban schools [15]. In contrast, no association was found in a national study between locale and the total number of physical education practices overall, although rural schools were more likely than urban schools to require teacher certification, licensure, or endorsement [42].

Given the limited and mixed evidence on the relationships of school‐level SES and locale with physical education implementation in the United States, additional school‐level research that accounts for state‐level factors is needed to better understand contextual influences on implementation. In addition, examining these relationships by grade‐span is necessary, as the organizational context, practices, policies, and recommendations of physical education differ across elementary, middle, and high school settings.

Elementary school physical education serves as a critical foundation for lifelong health and physical literacy [44]. During these formative years, physical education has been shown to help children develop essential motor skills and improve physical fitness [45]. Therefore, it is reasonable to focus efforts on understanding factors that influence the quantity and quality of physical education in elementary schools.

Thus, the objective of this study was to determine whether elementary school‐level SES is associated with school‐level implementation of national physical education recommendations, while accounting for school locale and state‐level clustering. Specifically, is school‐level SES associated with meeting recommended physical education instructional minutes and class size, employing certified and endorsed physical education teachers, and allowing students to be withheld from physical education for disciplinary or academic reasons?

We hypothesized that elementary schools with lower school‐level SES would be less likely to meet recommendations for weekly physical education minutes and class size. Although prior findings are mixed, including null SES differences in physical education instructional time in smaller regional samples [25], or greater physical education policy enactment in more disadvantaged states [38], evidence that low‐SES elementary schools have fewer physical education‐supportive practices and less access to certified physical education teachers [30] led us to expect corresponding shortfalls in weekly physical education minutes and class size.

Consistent with previous findings [30, 42], we further hypothesized that lower‐SES schools would be less likely to employ certified and endorsed physical education teachers. Despite mixed findings in prior research [42], we expected lower‐SES schools to be more likely to allow students to be withheld from physical education for disciplinary or academic reasons.

Methods

Participants

This cross‐sectional analysis included elementary school (K‐5) data from the School Health Map [46]. The School Health Map project is an open‐access, interactive dashboard where accurate and up‐to‐date data on state‐level implementation of physical education and PA policies are available [46]. The School Health Map survey data were collected between April 2022 and February 2024 from a representative sample of physical education teachers (n = 7039) in all 50 US states and the District of Columbia using the valid and reliable US Physical Education and Physical Activity Policy (US‐PEPAP) questionnaire [17, 19].

School‐level SES and locale data were obtained from the most recent National Center for Education Statistics (NCES) listing and merged with School Health Map data. School‐level SES was measured as the percentage of students eligible for either free or reduced‐price lunch, and locale was categorized as urban, suburban, township, or rural, consistent with NCES criteria [33]. The final dataset included only School Health Map observations with matched SES indicators from NCES and is limited to teachers who reported teaching elementary school (K‐5), resulting in 2510 observations from 41 states. Washington D.C. and the following states were not included in the sample: Alaska, Delaware, Georgia, Illinois, Massachusetts, Montana, Oklahoma, Tennessee, and West Virginia because SES and locale data were not reported by NCES for 2024, the matched year of the US‐PEPAP survey.

Procedures

A few SES observations from NCES exceeded 100% (n = 6); several surveys were started but had no data (n = 48) and responses with values of physical education minutes more than 601 per week were deemed outliers (n = 2), as this threshold exceeds 2 h of physical education per instructional day in a standard 5‐day school week, which is incompatible with typical US elementary school physical education schedules and likely reflects reporting error. All were removed, resulting in 2454 observations.

The percentage of students eligible for free and reduced lunch was categorized into equal tertiles based on the sample distribution, creating three approximately equal‐sized groups for relative comparison: low‐SES > 61%, moderate‐SES 33%–61%, and high‐SES < 33%. The following variables from the School Health Map dataset were included in the analysis: physical education minutes per week, physical education class size (number of students per class), if schools withhold physical education as punishment or for academic reasons, and if teachers reported being licensed/certified (or having an endorsement) to teach physical education. Each variable was dichotomized to “yes” or “no” consistent with national recommendations: at least 150 min of physical education per week, class sizes of 25 students or fewer, no withholding of physical education for disciplinary or academic reasons, and teachers who were licensed/certified and endorsed to teach physical education [22, 24].

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics for each variable were calculated and stratified by SES category (see Table 1). A total of 331 (13.49%) observations had at least one missing value. To handle missing values in the dataset we used multiple imputations using chained equations (MICE) with 20 imputations and 20 iterations per imputation [47]. The imputation approach was customized to reflect the multilevel structure of the data (schools nested within states); specifically, a two‐level binary imputation method (21.bin) for the four newly created dichotomous variables. The predictor matrix was modified to include fixed effects for SES, locale, and a random intercept for state, which was designated as the cluster variable. This approach allowed us to account for the nested structure of schools within states while preserving variability across imputations.

Results

The total sample includes 2454 elementary school teachers nested within 41 states. Of the sample, 17% of schools met the recommendation for physical education minutes per week, 68% met the recommendation for class size, 17% allowed physical education to be withheld as punishment or for academic reasons, and 95% of teachers reported being licensed to teach with an endorsement in physical education. Table 1 includes a summary of school locale and the percentage of schools meeting select national recommendations, stratified by SES category.

The proportion of elementary schools meeting the recommended number of physical education minutes per week was highest in the low‐SES category, with a stepwise decline observed in the moderate and high‐SES categories. In contrast, adherence to physical education class size recommendations was most prevalent among high‐SES schools, with progressively lower adherence in the moderate and low‐SES groups. Variables related to withholding physical education and teacher qualifications showed that low‐SES schools had the highest percentage of teachers reporting that physical education was withheld as punishment or for academic reasons, and the lowest percentage of teachers who were licensed and endorsed to teach physical education.

Table 2 includes the results from the four mixed effect regression models and Figure 1 displays the estimated probabilities based on marginal means to show the probability of implementing physical education practices by SES category from all four models. In Model 1, schools with moderate‐SES had 49% greater odds (OR = 1.49, CI = 1.08–2.07, p = 0.016) and schools with low‐SES had 44% greater odds (OR = 1.44, CI = 1.03–2.01, p = 0.032) of meeting the recommended number of physical education minutes per week compared to the high‐SES reference group. Additionally, elementary schools located in rural areas had 53% greater odds (OR = 1.53, CI = 1.09–2.16, p = 0.015) of meeting physical education minute recommendations compared to schools in urban settings. The average intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICC) for Model 1 across the imputed datasets was 0.265, with a range of 0.256–0.271.

Similarly, the association with the school locale was not statistically significant. The mean ICC for Model 2 across the imputed datasets was 0.378 with a range of 0.361–0.389.

The results of model 3 indicate elementary schools in the low‐SES category had 87% greater odds (OR = 1.87, CI = 1.39–2.51, p < 0.001) of allowing physical education to be withheld as punishment or for academic reasons compared to schools in the high‐SES category, see Model 3. Additionally, schools in towns had 53% greater odds (OR = 1.53, CI = 1.04–2.23, p = 0.029) and rural areas had 72% greater odds (OR = 1.72, CI = 1.26–2.37, p < 0.001) of allowing physical education to be withheld compared to schools in urban settings. Discussion

While physical education serves as a structural intervention across elementary schools throughout the United States, the national recommendations to improve potential health outcomes are not followed consistently [13, 22, 23, 24]. Overall, SES is an important correlate of physical education implementation, although the direction varies by implementation practice. Contrary to our hypothesis, low‐SES schools were more likely than high‐SES schools to meet the recommendation for weekly physical education minutes. At the same time, low‐SES schools were more likely to allow students to be withheld from physical education for disciplinary or academic reasons and were less likely to employ qualified physical education teachers. Although SES was not significantly associated with recommended class size in adjusted models, this finding warrants further discussion given the descriptive pattern and the substantial state‐level variance observed.

After accounting for SES and state‐level clustering, rural schools had higher odds than urban schools of meeting recommended weekly physical education minutes, and schools in towns and rural areas had higher odds of withholding physical education. These findings are broadly consistent with prior literature suggesting that locale may be associated with some physical education practices [15, 43], although the pattern has not been consistent across outcomes [42].

Contrary to our hypothesis, moderate‐ and low‐SES schools had higher odds than high‐SES schools of meeting the recommended weekly physical education minutes. While prior school‐level findings have been mixed [25, 30, 39], state‐level analyses have shown that more disadvantaged states are more likely to enact specific physical education time laws [38]. Although we did not directly examine state policy, the relatively high ICC for weekly minutes suggests that state policy contexts may help explain this pattern. Future research should be focused on school‐level physical education practices to specific state policy characteristics to test this directly.

Surprisingly, no significant association was found between SES and adherence to recommended class size. This finding is inconsistent with prior research that suggests schools in lower SES communities often have larger class sizes [30, 39]. However, the ICC for Model 2 was notably high, which suggests a large portion of the variance is attributable to state‐level factors. Additionally, Table 1 showed a stepwise SES pattern in recommended physical education class size, with adherence highest in high‐SES schools and lowest in low‐SES schools. Kern et al. identified a positive correlation between average weekly physical education minutes and class size, indicating that schools providing more weekly physical education may also have larger classes [17]. This highlights the need to explore state‐specific physical education policies and their effects on school‐levelimplementation, particularly in ways that could unintentionally compromise class size, and thus the quality of physical education delivery.

Consistent with prior literature, low‐SES elementary schools were less likely to have licensed teachers endorsed to teach physical education compared to high‐SES schools [30, 42]. Previously, school principals and elementary school teachers identified a lack of both financial resources and physical education specialists as key barriers to implementing evidence‐based physical education [48]. Additionally, higher SES schools were associated with greater teacher capacity, teacher qualifications, experience, and opportunities for professional development [49].

Lower‐SES schools were more likely to allow physical education to be withheld as punishment or for academic reasons, and this practice was also more prevalent in town and rural schools compared to urban settings. Given the lower ICC for this outcome, withholding is shaped more by local school or district norms than by state‐level factors. Further examination of how local disciplinary practices and academic pressures contribute to these patterns is warranted.

Given the hierarchical structure of the data, with schools nested within states, we used mixed‐effects models to account for state‐level variation in physical education practices. The higher ICC values for the models examining physical education minutes, class size, and teacher qualifications reflect a stronger influence of state‐level factors on these practices. In contrast, the lower ICC for withholding physical education reflects that this practice is more influenced by local school or district factors. Taken together, variation in physical education implementation likely reflects both school‐level socioeconomic conditions and broader state‐level factors such as policy.

One factor to consider is public school funding mechanisms. Many states continue to be criticized for underfunding public schools with the highest need [50, 51], siphoning funding that instead has been allocated to private/public charter schools and/or school voucher programs [52]. While federal contributions to K‐12 education have been relatively consistent, disparities between and within states, and between districts, often result in inequitable resources [41]. Although these school funding studies are not specific to physical education, they are foundational in identifying the structural inequities that likely influence the capacity of schools to implement quality physical education programs [41, 52]. Previously, there was no association between state‐level K‐12 educational funding variables and the presence and strength of state physical education laws [38]. However, only aggregated state‐level data and policies were considered, and no district or school‐level policy and practices were assessed [38]. As new policies are being implemented across states and local districts, tax bases are likely affected. Future studies will need to account for these impacts on education, and in particular physical education. Further, investigating mandated increased physical education minutes at the expense of manageable class sizes needs to be studied and the context in which this occurs. Understanding these relationships will help inform advocacy efforts toward more equitable and effective K‐12 physical education policy design and program implementation.

Implications for School Health Policy, Practice, and Equity

Low‐SES elementary schools were more likely to meet weekly physical education minute recommendations, but this greater adherence did not extend to other supports for equitable access and quality, including manageable class sizes, qualified teachers, and protections against withholding physical education. The substantial variation across states further suggests that school‐level differences in implementation should be interpreted within broader state contexts rather than as isolated school‐level decisions. At the state level, policies should pair time mandates with support for qualified physical education teachers and safeguards for manageable class sizes. At the district level, accountability systems should monitor and reduce withholding practices. At the school level, physical education should be treated as a core component of education rather than a discretionary one. Aligning these efforts is essential to ensure that all students have consistent access to physical education that supports both learning and long‐term health.

Limitations

The cross‐sectional design of this study limits the ability to draw causal inferences from the results. Additionally, SES and locale data from NCES is not available for all US states, limiting the sample size included in this analysis. Furthermore, we were unable to take into consideration potential random effects of school districts due to limited responses from individual districts.

Conclusions

School‐level SES is associated with elementary physical education practices in complex ways that appear to be shaped by state‐level factors. Low‐SES schools were more likely than high‐SES schools to meet recommended weekly physical education minutes, but they were also less likely to employ qualified physical education teachers, more likely to withhold physical education for disciplinary or academic reasons, and showed a descriptive pattern of lower adherence to recommended class size. The substantial state‐level variance observed for minutes, class size, and teacher qualifications underscores the need for further research linking specific state policies to school‐level implementation, particularly in ways that support equitable access to high‐quality physical education.
