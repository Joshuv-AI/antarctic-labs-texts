Footwork recognition and trajectory tracking of football goalie using Multi-Sensor technology
Authors: Guoxi Ye, Lin Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In football penalty kicks, due to limited reaction speed and time requirements of the task, the goalkeeper must predict the direction of the ball’s flight before the opponent’s football touches. This article mainly focuses on the action recognition and tracking of penalty kick technology for football players on the football field. A system that integrates multiple sensors to solve the high requirements, privacy violations, and expensive equipment costs of scenarios and testing environments is proposed based on video image technology. The system collects 1,440 penalty action data from 24 subjects at the 11 m standard penalty spot using a 12 channel IMU embedded in the insole, and establishes a gait dataset. The trajectory analysis section extracts 30 segments from the competition video and manually marks 100 real motion trajectories for verification. The algorithm takes complementary filtering to fuse acceleration and angular velocity, combined with dual-mode convolutional neural network to achieve gait recognition, and completes trajectory tracking with Kalman filter as the core. The experimental results showed that the proposed algorithm achieved an area under the target tracking curve (AUC) of 91.15% in normal scenarios. In the case where members of the same team block the target, the maximum AUC was 67.73%, which was higher than that of comparative algorithms such as Multiple Instance Learning, Online AdaBoost, and Kernel Correlation Filter. This indicates that the proposed method is more accurate in target tracking than pure visual or single sensor schemes and has good robustness. In addition, the error between the estimated distance and the actual distance did not exceed 6%. The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed system breaks through the limitations of traditional video analysis in field and privacy, which can provide reliable data support for predicting penalty direction and optimizing actions in goalkeeper training.

Introduction

With the rapid development of Internet of Things technology and computer image processing technology, athlete data analysis and training no longer rely on traditional data recording and coach experience [1, 2]. Researchers apply multi-sensor technology and video image technology to various sports scenes to identify players’ technical actions or track players [3]. Building an intelligent data analysis and training system for football players has become a popular research direction. Accurately tracking athlete trajectories is one of the core technological challenges. Zhang et al. [4] proposed a computer vision technique to track athletes’ action trajectories. Based on kernel correlation filtering tracking algorithm and related depth information, motion performance could be improved and reliable body data analysis could be provided. The results indicated that the proposed algorithm could reduce trajectory prediction errors and improve operational efficiency. Zhang et al. [5] designed a basketball flight trajectory tracking method based on video signal filtering. This method took adaptive median filtering algorithm to filter the video signal, Harris corner detection algorithm to enhance the image, and particle swarm optimization algorithm to optimize the basketball flight trajectory tracking results obtained by Kalman filtering algorithm. The experimental results showed that this method could accurately track the flight path of the basketball, with a true rate of 97%. Wang et al. [6] analyzed the feature extraction of basketball game videos and constructed a basketball sports video object tracking algorithm using an improved grey neural network algorithm. The experimental test results showed that this method could accurately recognize basketball poses, providing a new method for basketball pose recognition. Li et al. [7] discussed the image recognition and visual target tracking method based on the Internet of Things to help athletes train. This method used edge computing to track moving targets, used the moving target recognition technology based on neural network for image recognition, and used the error back propagation algorithm to promote target recognition in track and field training images. The results indicated that the occlusion problem could be effectively solved while ensuring real-time tracking performance. The above-mentioned video based basketball and athletics research has made significant progress in tracking the trajectories of athletes or spheres, revealing the potential of visual tracking technology in controllable scenes. However, visual tracking technology is highly dependent on environmental lighting and unobstructed scenes. The football penalty environment is more complex, involving rapid dynamic interaction between the goalkeeper and the shooter, frequent limb occlusion, and the goalkeeper’s unique save gait. Therefore, directly transferring the above vision based basketball tracking scheme to the analysis scenario of football goalkeepers is prone to failure and carries privacy compliance risks. Therefore, a new high-precision trajectory tracking method for football penalty kick technology analysis needs to be proposed.

In the field of football penalty kick analysis, multi-modal collection and deep mining of action data are key to improving referee accuracy. Kalpana et al. [8] utilized wearable IoT devices to collect gait data and proposed a gait recognition method based on sparse gate loop units and devil fear feedforward networks. Compared with existing models, this method achieved higher accuracy and computational efficiency, with an AUC of 0.988 for real-time data. Guilherme De Sousa et al. [9] selected relevant body orientation variables for penalty analysis and extracted them from video data using OpenPose technology. They used OSPAF that had been previously validated by experts. The average confidence score of OpenPose measurement was 0.80 ± 0.14. The OpenPose retest reliability value was 0.976 ± 0.03. They used logistic regression to investigate the relationship between OpenPose survey variables (free throw player: shoulder, buttocks, and non-kicking direction; goalkeeper: left and right feet, prediction), observable behavior (OSPAF variable), and free throw strategy (free throw player: goalkeeper dependent or independent; goalkeeper: shooter dependent or independent). There was no significant correlation between the selected body angle measured by OpenPose and the shooter’s strategy regarding penalty takers. Zhou et al. [10] proposed a soccer penalty trajectory prediction method based on RBF. They utilized the simplification ability of the RBF network model to establish a flight trajectory prediction model, combined with a parallel filtering controller to integrate all the above information. Based on known visual probability calculations, they estimated the flight trajectory state of the football and calculated its error covariance as the input value for filtering control, thus obtaining data for all variances and means. The experimental results showed that the football trajectory of this method had a fitting degree of 12 mm, and the maximum standard deviation of the landing point distance was only 0.0412 m. Therefore, this prediction method could obtain more accurate prediction data. Niu [11] tracked different rotation states based on the calculated velocity value trajectory. After non-linear change processing, the sampling distribution distance was set to obtain the coordinates of the rotating balls during the motion process. This completed the motion trajectories of different rotating balls in football, and analyzed the four forces acting on the balls during the rotation process. The proposed method could obtain the motion trajectories of different rotating balls in football and complete the force analysis. However, the proposed penalty kick technique for motion data processing was not comprehensive.

In summary, significant progress has been made in tracking athletes’ movement trajectories and analyzing football penalty kick techniques in existing research. However, it mostly relies on video image technology and is susceptible to factors such as environmental lighting and occlusion scenes, making it difficult to continuously capture the dynamic characteristics of athletes. In particular, there is a lack of reliable analysis methods for the gait and movement trajectory of goalkeepers under complex field conditions. In response to the above issues, this study constructs a multi-sensor intelligent insole system, integrates accelerometer and angular velocity data, and combines complementary filtering and dual-mode convolutional neural network (DMCNN) to achieve high-precision recognition and trajectory tracking of goalkeeper gait in challenging scenarios such as occlusion and lighting changes. This study effectively fills the research gap in dynamic feature mining and anti-interference ability of traditional methods, providing more stable and privacy friendly data support for penalty direction prediction. This study assumes that multi-sensor fusion is more accurate in reproducing the true trajectory of goalkeepers than a single sensor. The main innovation of this study lies in proposing a fusion framework that integrates multi-sensor intelligent insoles, complementary filtering, and DMCNN. This framework helps overcome the limitations of occlusion, lighting, privacy, and cost faced by traditional video analysis methods in football penalty scenes, and effectively suppresses integral drift by integrating acceleration and angular velocity data, achieving effective recognition and tracking of goalkeeper gait and motion trajectory.

This study is divided into four sections. The first section introduces the data and trajectory analysis method of free throw techniques for football players based on multi-sensor technology, and collects data on goalkeeper footwork recognition. The second section conducts experiments on the proposed system performance to verify its feasibility. The third section is to compare and discuss the experimental results with previous research findings. The last section is to summarize and discuss the article, and point out the insufficient temporary storage and future prospects of the article.

This process roughly includes the following five steps: selecting appropriate sensors and testing environments for different sports scenarios, obtaining information on the sports status and environmental changes of football players, and establishing corresponding datasets. When football players move on the field, the intelligent insole embedded with multiple sensors will touch the soles of their feet. Therefore, the movement state of the sensor at a specific time depends on the changes in the foot movement state of the football player. The movement state of football players’ feet can be divided into two types. One is the contact state between the sole and the ground, and the other is the non-contact state between the sole and the ground. When a football player’s sole touches the ground, although the speed value is 0 at this time, the acceleration value is not 0. Then, during the integration process, it will cause error accumulation, resulting in a certain drift. Therefore, it is necessary to determine a stable period and judge the switching of two motion states through acceleration. When the player is in contact with the ground, the acceleration value is 0 or small enough. It is considered to be in a stable period. If it is in a stable period at some time, the constraint with Z axis height of 0 is executed again to ensure that the start and end of the motion are in the same plane. After obtaining the displacement of football players at the current moment, people can describe the movement track of football players in a period of time. Data collection for goalie footwork identification

(1) Football Footwork Recognition Based on K-NearestNeighbor.

This experiment recruits 24 football players as participants. Each participant wears smart insoles equipped with embedded sensors located in the metatarsal area, arch, and heel area of the foot to collect three-axis acceleration and three-axis angular velocity data. There are a total of 12 IMUs on both insoles, using Bosch BMI160 chip with a sampling rate of 200 Hz. Each person repeats 60 penalty kicks (20 each on the left, center, and right), for a total of 1,440 action samples. The experiment is conducted on a standard 11-a-side indoor court, with the penalty spot 11 m away from the goal. The shooters are randomly placed in order, and the goalkeeper only makes a save gait without any hand movements. Sensor saturation frames and BLE packet loss frames are removed during data cleaning. This experiment first selects K-NearestNeighbor (KNN), a relatively simple algorithm, to identify and classify five basketball steps. This algorithm is actually used in this experiment to judge which category is closest to a football step action and which category is selected. First, a distance function is taken to calculate the distance between football footwork data samples. Common distance functions include Manhattan distance, Hamming distance, and European distance. Because the basketball footwork data in this experiment is a continuous variable, Euclidean distance is selected for calculation.

Secondly, the K value is particularly important for classifying football footwork.

(2) Football Footwork Recognition Based on SVM.

In this experiment, SVM, a binary classification method, is also used to identify and classify five football footwork methods. This algorithm maps the basketball footwork training set data to a high-dimensional area through nonlinear mapping. A support vector corresponding to the hyperplane is defined to separate the two types of basketball footwork data.

(3) Football Footwork Recognition Based on CNN.

This experiment also selects CNN, a deep learning method, to recognize and classify five football steps, and the role and working principle of convolution layer and pooling layer in feature extraction and selection. In addition to these two parts, CNN also has two important components: full connection layer and softmax. The CNN architecture for soccer footwork recognition in this experiment consists of an input layer, a first convolutional layer, a pooling layer, a second convolutional layer, and a softmax layer. In this experiment, the filter size of the first convolutional layer is set to 60. This filter extracts low-level features from five soccer footwork data. Secondly, the convolution kernel size of the maximum pooling layer is set to 20, and the convolution stride is set to 2. The convolution kernel size selects features from five football stride data. Thirdly, the output of the pooling layer is used as the input of the second convolutional layer, and the filter size is set to 6. This filter extracts advanced features from five types of soccer footwork data. After obtaining the advanced features of five types of soccer footwork data, these advanced features are mapped to the soccer footwork data label space through a fully connected layer with 1,000 neurons and a tanh function nonlinear mapping.

(4) Football Footwork Recognition Based on DMCNN.

A single network model may show good recognition performance when facing small sample data. To prevent over-fitting, the method of increasing the number of samples is usually used. However, this method will reduce the computational efficiency. To solve this problem, this paper attempts to fuse multiple networks on the basis of CNN, and proposes a dual model convolution neural network to recognize five football steps. Firstly, unlike the method of identifying soccer steps by inputting acceleration data and angular velocity data into the same CNN, DMCNN inputs the acceleration data of soccer steps into the CNN as the base classifier, and then inputs the angular velocity data of soccer steps into the CNN as the base classifier. For these two CNNs, in this experiment, the size of the convolution kernel for the first convolutional layer is set to 60, the size of the convolution kernel for the max pooling layer is set to 20, and the stride is determined to be 2. The size of the convolution kernel for the second convolutional layer is set to 6. Secondly, the above two CNNs use a full connection layer containing 1,000 neurons to synthesize the output of the second convolution layer, that is, advanced football footwork features, and output “highly purified” football footwork action features, which together constitute a new set of feature vectors. Thirdly, to reduce the dimension of the feature vectors of basketball footwork samples, the principal component analysis method is adopted and n-components are set to 200. Therefore, the first 200 components contain important feature information. Then, through a full connective layer containing 200 neurons to synthesize the above features, the football footwork features required by the final experiment are obtained. Finally, the softmax layer outputs the probability distribution of five football footwork movements after classification.

In summary, the KNN algorithm, as a benchmark method, has simple implementation and fast response to small sample features, but the Euclidean distance is ineffective in high-dimensional IMU sequences. The performance of SVM algorithm is superior to KNN, which proves its effectiveness in handling moderately complex classification tasks. However, its performance limit is limited by the quality of manual feature engineering and kernel function selection. CNN has demonstrated strong automatic feature learning capabilities and achieved better performance than traditional KNN and SVM methods, but there is overfitting in small sample sizes, and its performance improvement mainly depends on a single data modality. Based on the above analysis, this article ultimately adopts DMCNN as the core step recognition algorithm. Its advantage lies in the ability to input acceleration and angular velocity data separately into independent CNNs for feature extraction, which is more discriminative than the mixed input features of a single CNN. By setting the n-component to 200 through PCA, the dimensionality of the fused features is significantly reduced, redundant information is removed, and the generalization ability of the model is enhanced [26, 27].

Discussion

This study proposes a soccer player footwork recognition and trajectory tracking system based on intelligent insole multi-sensor fusion. The experimental results show that the proposed method performs well in trajectory tracking tasks, with an AUC of up to 91.15%. Compared with video image-based technologies such as OpenPose adopted by Guilherme et al. [9], this method breaks through the sensitive limitations of lighting, viewing angle, and occlusion, and can stably collect data in complex arena environments. In the field of trajectory prediction, although this method does not directly predict the ball path like the RBF network proposed by Zhou et al. in [10], it achieves high robustness pose calculation of the player body through Kalman filtering and complementary filtering, with displacement error controlled within 6%, laying the foundation for subsequent global analysis combined with ball trajectory. References [8–10] mainly focus on the trajectory of shooting players or balls, or rely on external video devices. And this study provides an effective new path to solve the inherent defects of visual technology in capturing key movements of goalkeepers (occlusion, light sensitivity) through an innovative multimodal insole sensor solution. This approach echoes Ren et al.‘s multimodal method in fencing footwork training across sports domains [27], and both demonstrate that multimodal fusion can significantly improve action recognition accuracy (94.43% vs. Ren et al. 93.3% in this study), while avoiding modal interference through feature separation. However, this study further reveals the irreplaceability of sensor modes in anti occlusion scenarios. When players on the same team obstruct, pure visual tracking methods are prone to failure due to their reliance on appearance continuity, such as the flower pollination algorithm used by Ong et al. in athlete tracking [28]. However, the IMU data stream of this system can still maintain 67.73% AUC, providing a new paradigm for motion analysis in complex adversarial scenes. The research results are similar to those of Wu et al. [29]. Wu et al. also identified tennis players’ hitting movements through wearable devices and sensors, producing reliable action classification results. Overall, compared with the aforementioned methods, the multi-sensor intelligent insole system proposed in this study breaks away from the passive analysis mode that relies on external environmental perception and shifts towards an active data collection and analysis paradigm based on wearable sensors, starting from the athlete’s body. This transformation firmly fixes the “anchor” of data analysis from the easily disturbed external environment to the biological trajectory of athletes themselves, thereby continuously and stably obtaining high-quality athlete body data.

The advantage of this research system is that it combines multi-sensor intelligent insoles with complementary filtering algorithms to achieve high-precision and robust gait recognition and trajectory tracking for football goalkeepers. In practical application scenarios, the system proposed in this study has broad application potential. For goalkeepers, the system can quantify their save footwork and displacement efficiency, providing data support for targeted physical training, thereby improving the success rate of saves. For other football players, the system can be extended to gait analysis and performance improvement, helping them optimize running routes and movement efficiency. In addition, the system can also be combined with existing video analysis systems to provide more comprehensive athlete data support for coaches and referees, especially in complex competition environments such as night games or crowded audience scenes. However, the calibration accuracy and wearing comfort of insole sensors may affect data quality, which may lead to data drift or loss during long-term use. The real-time performance of the system still needs to be improved, which may not be able to meet certain application scenarios that require extremely high real-time performance. Therefore, in future research, adaptive calibration modules should be further introduced, such as dynamically resetting integration errors based on gait events, to monitor environmental changes in real time and dynamically adjust data collection parameters, providing coaches with reliable longitudinal performance analysis. At the same time, optimize the material and structural design of the insole to improve wearing comfort. In addition, by optimizing low latency transmission protocols and exploring end-to-end inference techniques, processing latency can be reduced to millisecond level, laying the foundation for real-time feedback systems (such as instantly alerting for footwork errors during training) or referee systems (quickly providing data references when fouls occur).

Conclusions

This study proposes a gait recognition and trajectory tracking system for soccer goalkeepers based on multi-sensor intelligent insoles. The system achieves high-precision gait recognition and trajectory tracking by integrating accelerometer and angular velocity data, using complementary filtering algorithm and DMCNN model. The experimental results show that the accuracy of the DMCNN model reaches 94.43%, and the F1 score reaches 95.56%. The AUC of the proposed system in trajectory tracking tasks reaches 91.15%, and the displacement error is controlled within 6%. These quantitative performance indicators demonstrate that the system has high stability and robustness in complex competition environments. This study proposes an innovative multi-sensor intelligent insole system that breaks through the limitations of traditional video image technology on occlusion, lighting, and privacy, providing a more reliable and convenient way to obtain motion information.
