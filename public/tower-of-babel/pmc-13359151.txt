The Amber of This Moment: A Parable of Training Traditions
Authors: E. Steve Roach
Published: 2026

Here we are, … trapped in the amber of this moment. There is no why.Kurt Vonnegut, Slaughterhouse‐Five

Here we are, … trapped in the amber of this moment. There is no why.

It was your pride and joy, that 8‐year‐old convertible. It idled like a purring cat but roared like a lion when challenged. Its quaint turquoise‐and‐white color scheme made it more suited for a tropical movie set than for the school parking lot. It represented the prospect of worldly adventure and independence and suggested a stature and maturity not yet earned. It was cool, and by extension, so were you.

Over time, the car became a mobile museum to the fading memories of youth. Its classic design could never be replaced by the computer‐generated sameness of the newest models. Its energy was always a step ahead of the more fuel‐efficient present. It came to represent simpler, idyllic times, an icon of an era that may never have actually existed. The notion of trading it for a newer model was an unthinkable betrayal.

Life moved on even though you did not. The aging car became a bit sluggish and picked up a few dings and leaks. There seemed to be less and less of interest on the car's old AM radio. Knowledgeable mechanics and spare parts became difficult to find, and it stranded you a few times while driving to work. Its lack of fuel efficiency became increasingly painful as gasoline prices soared. Its lack of air conditioning in summer and drafty ragtop in winter promoted nearly year‐round discomfort. Your grandchildren were not allowed to ride with you because the car lacked seat belts and airbags. But tradition and nostalgia cannot be easily erased. There were still frequent requests for the car to participate in parades, anniversary celebrations, and weddings, increasingly without you as the driver.

The unfortunate thing about traditions is that no matter how desperately you cling to them, they still sometimes leave you behind. The solution to the above dilemma would be to find a way to acknowledge the past while taking better advantage of modern fuel efficiency, comfort features, and safety measures. Perhaps you could give the classic car a place of honor in the garage next to its modern cousin, try to retrofit some of its more antiquated features, or donate it to a museum where you can visit whenever you like, and others can enjoy it as well.

I am a neurologist, and I still see lots of adult patients and help run a neurology department. I can hardly be accused of bias against adult neurology training. We should find ways to embrace our ties to neurology. But how can we in good conscience maintain decades‐old training requirements for child neurologists that allocate so much time to adult neurology and general pediatrics and leave so little time for future child neurologists to learn about the remarkable developments in the field? How many adult neurology residency directors would happily embrace an equivalent 5‐year adult neurology residency that required 2 preliminary years of internal medicine and a full year of child neurology? Adult neurologists constitute a strong majority of the Residency Review Committee, but the committee's child neurologists should be acknowledged as the experts when determining the child neurology training requirements.

Time spent in training is an investment in one's future, and we must periodically assess the return on this investment. Published surveys of US child neurology residency program directors strongly suggest that some assets in our current residency training investment portfolio offer a lot less value than others [1, 2]. We have been discussing most of these same training issues for at least a couple of decades [3]. With the pending 10‐year review of the residency requirements, it is time to take a serious look at what constitutes optimal training for child neurologists and what is best for the children these future neurologists will be entrusted to help. It is time to muster the courage to acknowledge our current training shortcomings and to make meaningful changes.
