MR arthrographic examination of the association with glenohumeral instability of posterior labroligamentous i̇nsertion types and posterior labrum anatomical variants
Authors: Rodi Ertogrul, Hayri Ogul, Yusuf Yahsi, Zakir Sakci, Yusuf Sulek, Mecit Kantarci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe objective of this study is to evaluate the relationship between posterior labroligamentous morphological variants and abnormal position of the humeral head in the glenoid cavity using MR arthrography (MRA). Additionally, the study seeks to investigate whether these variations act as biomechanical risk factors for subclinical instability.MethodsThe study’s sample population comprised 190 patients who did not exhibit posterior labral tears or clinical instability. The posterior labrum morphology and capsulolabral insertion types were assessed using MRA, and the posterior subluxation index (PSI) of the humeral head in the glenoid cavity was quantitatively measured. A statistical analysis was conducted to examine the associations between capsulolabral variations and posterior humeral head positioning.ResultsThe prevalence of labral hypoplasia was observed to be 29% among the patient population, while aplasia was identified in 2% of cases. Sublabral clefts were identified in 52% of cases. A statistically significant increase in posterior displacement of the humeral head was observed in cases of labral hypoplasia and sublabral clefts, particularly in cases of deep clefts (p < 0.001). Posterior capsular insertion type was also identified as an independent predictor of the posterior subluxation index (PSI).ConclusionsPosterior labral variations, specifically hypoplasia and deep sublabral clefts, have been observed to be associated with increased posterior humeral head translation, even in the absence of clinical instability. These structures may serve as biomechanical risk factors contributing to subclinical posterior shoulder instability.Level of evidenceLevel IV; Case Series Using Large Database.Clinical trial numberProtocol code: E-10840098-202.3.02-2631, date of approval: 21/04/2025Keywords: Posterior labrum, Hypoplasia, Labral variants, MR arthrography, Glenohumeral instability

Introduction

The shoulder joint, despite its wide range of motion afforded by the shallow glenoid depth and lack of bony congruity, relies heavily on its complex capsulolabral structure for stability, making it one of the most instability-prone joints in the body. Although anterior shoulder instability is more prevalent, posterior shoulder instability is noteworthy due to its diagnostic challenges and clinical outcomes, despite accounting for only 5–10% of all instability cases [1–4]. Morphological features such as glenoid retroversion, glenoid dysplasia, and posterior capsule enlargement have been described in the literature in relation to posterior shoulder instability [1, 3, 5, 6]. Nevertheless, the impacts on humeral head positioning of posterior labrum variations, and their role in susceptibility to instability, remains to be fully elucidated [3, 7–12].

Although congenital abnormalities of the posterior labrum – such as hypoplasia, aplasia, and sublabral clefts – are frequently documented in the literature as normal variants, the impact of these formations on joint biomechanics remains to be clearly defined [7, 8, 10, 13, 14]. It has been demonstrated by preceding studies that posterior labral tears, specifically, are linked to posterior translation by disrupting the centering of the humeral head on the glenoid cavity [15–18]. However, the effect of structural variations of the posterior labrum on this positioning has not been systematically investigated [7, 9, 10, 13]. Consequently, the question of whether these variations predispose to posterior instability by increasing the tendency of the humeral head to shift posteriorly remains significant and unanswered.

Magnetic resonance arthrography (MRA) is widely accepted as the gold standard method for evaluating the labrum-capsular complex of the shoulder joint [7, 12, 19]. The injection of contrast media into the joint allows for the distension of the capsulolabral structures, enabling more detailed visualisation of posterior labrum variations and defects [10, 13, 20, 21]. Despite the evidence that MRA is capable of achieving high levels of both sensitivity and specificity in the diagnosis of posterior labral tears, there is a paucity of studies that quantitatively examine the relationship between structural variations such as hypoplasia, aplasia, and sublabral cleft and humeral head positioning [12, 22, 23]. The extant literature on the subject is largely confined to morphological descriptions or case series, with a paucity of systematic research addressing the biomechanical effects of these variations [9, 10, 21]. This lacuna in knowledge creates a significant void in understanding the potential clinical significance of posterior labrum variations and whether they predispose to posterior instability.

The hypothesis that structural labral abnormalities, such as hypoplasia, aplasia, and sublabral cleft, may increase the tendency for posterior positioning of the humeral head and create a predisposition to posterior instability in these individuals is being tested. The objective of this study is to present new evidence that posterior labrum variations may not only be anatomical variants but also clinically significant biomechanical factors.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

A total of 275 consecutive patients who underwent MRA were prospectively and retrospectively evaluated in this ambispective study. The primary indications for MRA encompass the diagnosis and classification of rotator cuff rupture, anterior and posterior labrum tear, superior labrum anterior to posterior (SLAP) lesion, capsular and ligamentous damage, biceps tendon damage, and unexplained shoulder pain. Inclusion criteria were patients aged 18 years or older who underwent MRA and had no evidence of posterior labral tear or clinical glenohumeral instability on imaging or clinical evaluation. Exclusion criteria included technically inadequate images (massive contrast extravasation, insufficient joint distension, inadequate contrast effect, inability to properly position the patient, and patient motion artifact), the presence of posterior labral tear, clinical instability, and full-thickness tears of the subscapularis or infraspinatus tendons, as these may cause humeral head imbalance and potentially confound the posterior translation measurements. Seventy-five of the 275 patients were excluded for various reasons, and a further 10 patients were excluded due to full-thickness subscapularis or infraspinatus tendon tears (Fig. 1). The remaining 190 patients’ MRA results were incorporated into the study. Ethical committee approval (No. E-10840098-202.3.02-2631) was obtained from our institution for the study. Informed consent forms were obtained from each patient included in the study.

Injection procedure and MRA imaging protocol

Each patient included in the study was first sent to the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) unit for conventional MRI. Thereafter, they were escorted to the injection room for MRA. The injections were administered by a single radiologist under ultrasound guidance [24]. Local anesthesia was not utilized during the injection procedure. The injections were performed under sterile conditions using the posterior trace, with the patient in a sitting or semi-prone position. For each shoulder, 10–18 cc of gadolinium chelate diluted with serum physiologic at a ratio of 1/200 was injected according to joint capacity. Once adequate distension had been attained, patients were subsequently referred to the MRI unit for MRA. The scans were performed on a 3T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Vida, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) using 20-channel shoulder coils. During the imaging procedure, patients were positioned supine with the shoulder and arm in a neutral position. For MRA, all shoulder joints were imaged in three planes, including at least one sequence sensitive to fluid and at least one volumetric sequence. Although all patients initially underwent conventional MRI prior to MRA, quantitative measurements of humeral head positioning were performed exclusively on MRA images. This preference was based on two main reasons: first, MRA sequences included at least one volumetric acquisition with a wider field of view, which allowed optimal visualization of the scapular body and reliable identification of the Friedman line; second, MRA provided superior delineation of posterior capsulolabral structures, enabling more precise characterization of labral morphological variants compared with conventional MRI.

Image analysis

Axial plane MRA images with optimal distension and positioning were evaluated by two radiologists, one with 8 years of experience in musculoskeletal radiology and the other with 20 years of experience in the same field. In cases where a diagnosis proved challenging, the ultimate determination was arrived at through consensus among the two radiologists involved. The posterior labrum examinations were then used to determine the labrum’s morphological classification, which was divided into three categories: aplasia, hypoplasia, and normal labrum. In the absence of the labrum at the posterior glenoid margin, it was classified as aplastic labrum. The posterior labrum was located behind the line tangent to the anterior corners of the glenoid cartilage, it was classified as labral hypoplasia (Fig. 2). A posterior labrum with normal size, morphology, and signal characteristics was classified as a normal labrum. The posterior glenolabral junction types were examined in three categories, as indicated by the attachment pattern of the posterior labrum to the glenoid and cartilage: normal attachment, shallow sublabral recess, and deep sublabral recess. In the absence of a space between the posterior labrum and the adjacent cartilage, this was deemed to be a normal attachment, or tight attachment. Well-defined indentations with smooth margins in the form of a thin sulcus were observed immediately below the posterior labrum in the labrocartilaginous junction. These indentations were considered sublabral cleft in patients without evidence of posterior instability or labral tear. The distinction between a sublabral cleft and a chronic posterior labral tear was based on established MRA criteria: sublabral clefts were identified by smooth margins, regular contour, and absence of intrasubstance signal abnormality or labral fragmentation, whereas posterior labral tears were characterized by irregular margins, intrasubstance signal changes, or frank disruption of the labral tissue [22] The categorization of posterior sublabral clefts is determined by the termination point of the cleft relative to the line tangent to the anterior corners of the glenoid bone cortex. If the termination point is anterior to the tangent line, the cleft is described as “shallow.” If the termination point is posterior to the tangent line, the cleft is classified as “deep.” (Fig. 3) The insertion of the posterior joint capsule at the glenoid bone was classified into three types [21]. Accordingly, type 1 insertion was defined as attachment to the glenolabral junction level of the posterior labrum. Type 2 insertion was defined as attachment to the glenoid neck behind the glenolabral junction. Type 3 insertion was defined as attachment to the glenoid body behind the glenoid bone neck.

All axial MRA images were obtained perpendicular to the coronal plane of the scapula to ensure standardized measurement conditions. The posterior subluxation index (PSI) was calculated to quantify the position of the humeral head within the glenoid cavity, as previously described by Walch et al. and subsequently validated by Domos et al. and Beeler et al. [5, 25, 26]. Measurements were performed on axial plane MRA images at the midglenoid level (3–9 o’clock position). A reference line (Friedman line) was drawn from the midpoint of the glenoid fossa to the medial border of the scapular body, passing through the center of the glenoid and parallel to the long axis of the scapula [25]. A second line perpendicular to the Friedman line was drawn through the center of the glenoid, dividing the humeral head into anterior and posterior hemispheres. A best-fit circle was applied to the humeral head to determine its diameter. The PSI was calculated as the ratio of the distance from the perpendicular line to the outermost posterior margin of the humeral head circle, divided by the total diameter of the humeral head, expressed as a percentage (Fig. 4). A PSI greater than 55% indicates posterior subluxation of the humeral head [5, 25, 26]. Glenoid version was assessed on the same axial MRA images using the Friedman line method [25]. The glenoid version angle was measured between the line connecting the anterior and posterior margins of the glenoid articular surface and the line perpendicular to the Friedman line (neutral version reference). Retroversion was assigned a negative value, anteversion a positive value, and neutral version was recorded as zero.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as number (percentage) for categorical variables. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using histogram plots and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. As data were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were used. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and comparisons among more than two groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by pairwise comparisons when appropriate. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate associations between continuous and ordinal variables. To identify independent predictors of posterior humeral head translation, multivariate linear regression analysis was performed using PSI as the dependent variable. Variables entered into the model included age, gender, posterior labrum type, sublabral cleft type, posterior capsular insertion type, and glenoid retroversion angle. All analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 27.0, Armonk, NY, USA). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Of the 190 patients, 113 (59.5%) were male and 77 (40.5%) were female, and 100 shoulders (52.6%) were on the right side. Univariate analysis demonstrated that PSI and glenoid retroversion were not significantly associated with gender (p = 0.183 and p = 0.551, respectively) or shoulder side (p = 0.936 and p = 0.129, respectively). Significant differences in both PSI and retroversion values were observed according to sublabral cleft type (p < 0.001 for both) (Figs. 8 and 9) and posterior labrum type (p < 0.001 for both) (Fig. 10). Posterior capsular insertion type showed a significant association with PSI (p = 0.014), whereas no significant relationship was found with retroversion (p = 0.417) (Table 3). Spearman correlation analysis revealed that PSI was negatively correlated with posterior labrum type (r = − 0.516, p < 0.001) and positively correlated with sublabral cleft type (r = 0.567, p < 0.001) and posterior capsular insertion (r = 0.211, p = 0.003). Additionally, glenoid retroversion showed a positive correlation with labrum type (r = 0.399, p < 0.001) and a negative correlation with cleft type (r = − 0.429, p < 0.001), while no significant correlation was observed with capsular insertion (p = 0.189) (Table 4). Multivariate linear regression analysis identified posterior labrum type (β = −0.222, p < 0.001), sublabral cleft type (β = 0.270, p < 0.001), posterior capsular insertion type (β = 0.121, p = 0.009), and glenoid retroversion angle (β = −0.473, p < 0.001) as independent predictors of PSI. Age and gender were not found to be significant predictors (Table 5).

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrated a statistically significant association between posterior labral morphological variants and increased posterior subluxation index (PSI) in cases of labral hypoplasia, aplasia, or sublabral cleft. Despite the investigation of the relationship between posterior labrum tears and humeral head position in the literature, the effect of posterior labrum variations in this direction has not been systematically demonstrated [3, 12, 27, 28]. In this regard, our study makes a significant contribution to the existing literature by demonstrating that posterior labrum variations may not only be considered as morphological differences but also as structures that can affect glenohumeral joint biomechanics. Posterior humeral head positioning was quantified using the posterior subluxation index (PSI), calculated as the ratio of the posterior part of the humeral head to the total humeral head diameter, as previously described by Walch et al. and subsequently validated by Domos et al. and Beeler et al. [5, 25, 26]. This standardized approach normalizes for individual differences in humeral head size, thereby allowing reliable comparisons across patients regardless of anatomical variation. In our cohort, PSI did not differ significantly between male and female patients (p = 0.183) or between shoulder sides (p = 0.936). A particularly noteworthy finding of the present study is the significant role of glenoid retroversion as an independent predictor of PSI (β = −0.473, p < 0.001), representing the strongest predictor identified in the multivariate regression model. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that glenoid retroversion is an important factor contributing to posterior humeral head decentering [5, 6, 25]. Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed a positive relationship between retroversion and posterior labrum type (r = 0.399, p < 0.001), suggesting that increasing glenoid retroversion is associated with more severe labral morphological abnormalities. The negative correlation between retroversion and sublabral cleft type (r = − 0.429, p < 0.001) may indicate that retroversion and sublabral cleft formation represent partially independent pathomechanical pathways contributing to posterior glenohumeral instability — one primarily osseous and the other soft-tissue based. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating glenoid version assessment when evaluating posterior humeral head positioning.

Posterior glenoid labrum variations and sublabral clefts have long been considered anatomical variants, and their clinical significance has often been a subject of debate. Formations such as sublabral clefts and hypoplastic labrum structures have been considered normal variations, as they are often seen in asymptomatic individuals. The potential effects of these structures on glenohumeral joint biomechanics have not been sufficiently investigated [29]. However, recent studies on structural risk factors associated with posterior shoulder instability and posterior glide have begun to prioritize the role of these variations. However, the majority of studies on this subject are based on limited case series, and the quantitative counterparts and biomechanical effects of these variations remain unclear [13, 30, 31].

The findings of our study suggest that these structures, which are generally considered benign in the literature [7, 19, 31], may be potential structures that could affect glenohumeral joint biomechanics. The quantitative differentiation of these variations based on posterior displacement measurements may enable more careful recognition and reporting of these structures in imaging-based assessments [32]. The detection of these structures in conjunction with increased posterior displacement points to a potential biomechanical effect consistent with the subclinical instability mechanisms described in the literature [1, 11, 29].

Despite the plethora of studies in the extant literature investigating the relationship between posterior labral tears and posterior shoulder instability, the preponderance of these studies have focused exclusively on patient groups clinically diagnosed with instability [1, 11]. Consequently, the relationship between posterior humeral head position and posterior capsulolabral variations has been largely overlooked in individuals without a diagnosis of instability. However, the present study observed a significant increase in posterior placement in individuals with structural variations, such as hypoplasia and sublabral cleft. These findings were statistically supported. In this regard, our study suggests that posterior labral variations may not only be anatomical variants but also potential biomechanical risk factors that could lead to subclinical instability, thus offering a unique contribution to the current literature on this subject [6, 29, 31].

Magnetic resonance arthrography has been documented in the extant literature as the imaging modality with the highest sensitivity and specificity for accurately detecting anatomical variations and pathological changes in posterior labrum-capsular structures [29, 30, 33]. It has been documented that MRA exhibits a marked superiority over conventional MRI in the comprehensive anatomical evaluation of passive stabilizers, including the glenoid labrum, capsule, and posterior band of the inferior glenohumeral ligament [19, 29]. In instances where posterior labral variations may be misinterpreted as tears, arthrographic imaging using contrast medium facilitates precise identification of these structures, thereby averting potential erroneous surgical indications [8, 12, 21]. Recent studies have highlighted MRA not only for pathology detection but also as a diagnostic and prognostic instrument that allows for quantitative measurements in posterior positioning assessments [17]. Beyond labral morphology, the type of posterior capsular insertion was also identified as an independent predictor of PSI in the multivariate regression model (β = 0.121, p = 0.009). This finding suggests that a more lateral capsular attachment — specifically Type 2 and Type 3 insertions — may contribute to increased posterior humeral head translation by reducing the effective restraining force of the posterior capsule on the glenohumeral joint. Although the capsular insertion type has been described and classified in previous studies [21, 31], its independent biomechanical contribution to posterior humeral head positioning has not been previously demonstrated in a quantitative manner. This represents a novel finding of the present study and warrants further investigation in future biomechanical and clinical studies.

The present study has several limitations. First, as the study was based solely on MRA findings without direct correlation with clinical symptoms, the relationship between the identified labral variants and clinical instability could not be fully established. Second, the limited number of patients with posterior labral aplasia (n = 4) reduces the statistical power of analyses involving this subgroup, and findings related to this variant should be interpreted with caution. Third, the absence of physical examination findings and functional scoring data precluded evaluation of the correlation between labral variants and clinical instability. Fourth, glenoid retroversion was measured on two-dimensional axial MRA images using the Friedman line method; three-dimensional CT-based measurements may provide greater precision in future studies. Fifth, formal interrater reliability analysis was not performed, and future studies should include such metrics to further validate the reproducibility of the labral classification system employed.

In summary, while the effects of posterior glenoid labrum variations on glenohumeral joint biomechanics have long been a topic of debate, the present study has revealed that these variations create significant changes in the humeral head position and may potentially be structures that could pave the way for subclinical instability. Structures such as posterior labral hypoplasia and deep sublabral clefts should be regarded as variations that necessitate consideration from both a morphological and a functional perspective. The identification of these variations can offer significant clinical insights that are beneficial for both radiologists and shoulder surgeons. In this context, the accurate identification of anatomical variations and a better understanding of their biomechanical effects will contribute to developing a more comprehensive view of the glenohumeral instability spectrum.
