Induced pluripotent stem cell-derived dendritic cells potentiate the efficacy of interleukin-12-expressing oncolytic HSV-1
Authors: Satoru Taguchi, Hiroshi Fukuhara, Songjie Cai, Xiao-Kang Li, Akihiro Naito, Shigenori Kakutani, Yuta Takeshima, Jimpei Miyakawa, Haruki Kume, Tomoki Todo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oncolytic herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), including marketing-approved G47Δ, elicits antitumor immunity via facilitating antigen presentation during immune-mediated clearance of infected tumor cells. Dendritic cell (DC) therapy should therefore enhance oncolytic HSV-1 efficacy, but its clinical application is hindered by the need to generate autologous DCs. Induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technologies offer a scalable and standardized source of DCs. Here, we investigate the therapeutic potential of combining iPSC-derived DCs (iPSDCs) with an interleukin-12-expressing oncolytic HSV-1, T-mfIL12. iPSDCs, generated from a murine embryonic fibroblast-derived iPSC line, possess characteristics and functions comparable to bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs), including costimulatory molecule expression, antigen uptake capacity, and the ability to stimulate CD8+ and CD4+ T cells isolated from ovalbumin-specific transgenic mice both in vitro and in vivo. In a syngeneic murine bladder cancer model, iPSDCs markedly enhanced the efficacy of intratumorally inoculated T-mfIL12, eliciting augmented immune responses including intratumoral T cell infiltration, tumor-responsive T cell activation, and in situ maturation of DCs, similarly to BMDCs. The results indicate that iPSDCs can serve as an effective and practical alternative to conventional DCs in oncolytic virus immunotherapy. The combination of T-mfIL12 with iPSDCs offers a promising and clinically applicable cancer treatment strategy.

Introduction

Oncolytic virus therapy has emerged as a promising therapeutic modality for a wide range of malignancies.1,2,3 Talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC), a second-generation oncolytic herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), has received regulatory approval from both the United States Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency for the treatment of inoperable melanoma.1,2 G47Δ (teserpaturev), a third-generation oncolytic HSV-1, contains triple mutations in the HSV-1 genome (γ34.5, ICP6, and α47), enabling a strong antitumor activity via efficient replication and cancer-specific immune induction while sparing normal tissues.3 Multiple clinical trials, including a phase 2 study in patients with glioblastoma (UMIN000015995), have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of G47Δ, which resulted in its approval for malignant glioma as Japan’s first oncolytic virus therapy product (Delytact) in 2021.4,5,6 It has been shown that G47Δ elicits tumor-specific immunity, likely via facilitated processing of destroyed tumor cells by antigen-presenting cells during immune-mediated virus elimination, a major therapeutic mechanism for a long-term efficacy.3,5,6

Dendritic cell (DC)-based vaccination is a well-established strategy in cancer immunotherapy, and its combination with oncolytic virus therapy has shown considerable promise.7,8 Previous studies have demonstrated an enhancing effect on the efficacy when combining DC immunotherapy with oncolytic virus therapy, because oncolytic virus-infected tumor cells may serve as a source of tumor-associated antigens for DCs.9,10,11 Despite its therapeutic potential, the clinical implementation of DC-based immunotherapy remains limited by the need to derive autologous DCs from each individual patient, a time-consuming and labor-intensive process.12,13 Recent advances in induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology, however, have made it possible to generate unlimited numbers of functional DCs.12,13,14 The present study aims to evaluate the therapeutic potential of combining iPSC-derived dendritic cells (iPSDCs) with oncolytic virus therapy. To enhance this approach further, we employed a G47Δ-derived oncolytic HSV-1 expressing fusion-type interleukin-12 (IL-12),15 a cytokine that should augment DC function and thereby promote greater antitumor efficacy.

In this study, we generated iPSDCs from a murine embryonic fibroblast-derived iPSC line. We demonstrate that iPSDCs possess characteristics and functions comparable to bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs) and can effectively stimulate T cells isolated from ovalbumin (OVA)-specific OT-I and OT-II transgenic mice,16,17 both in vitro and in vivo. Using a syngeneic subcutaneous MB49 (murine bladder cancer) model, we evaluate the therapeutic efficacies of immature iPSDCs and BMDCs, in combination with an IL-12-expressing oncolytic HSV-1, T-mfIL12, and demonstrate that iPSDCs can enhance the antitumor efficacy of intratumorally inoculated T-mfIL12 via augmentation of immune responses, similarly to BMDCs.

Efficacy of T-mfIL12 combined with immature BMDCs

Using a syngeneic subcutaneous tumor model with MB49 murine bladder cancer, we first evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of combining immature BMDCs with an IL-12-expressing oncolytic HSV-1 (T-mfIL12). Efficacy of T-mfIL12 combined with immature iPSDCs

To assess whether iPSDCs can function similarly to BMDCs in vivo, reflecting our findings with iPSDCs in vitro, we assessed the efficacy of combining T-mfIL12 with iPSDCs in the subcutaneous MB49 tumor model. Whereas T-mfIL12 alone was more efficacious than mock, BMDCs alone, or iPSDCs alone (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.05, respectively), both BMDCs and iPSDCs caused a significant enhancement of tumor growth suppression when combined with T-mfIL12 (p < 0.001 for both groups versus T-mfIL12 alone; Figure 3B). The extent of growth suppression was comparable between the two combination groups.

Discussion

The results demonstrate that immature iPSDCs possess functional and phenotypic characteristics comparable to those of BMDCs, including their ability to induce antigen-specific T cell responses and promote intratumoral immune cell infiltration. In contrast to previously investigated iPSC-derived regulatory DCs for inhibiting rejection of organ transplants,14,18 immature iPSDCs are necessary for cancer immunotherapy to allow maturation in response to exposure to tumor antigens. Notably, the combination of T-mfIL12 with iPSDCs exhibited potent antitumor effects, equivalent to those achieved with BMDCs, both in terms of tumor suppression and immunological activation. Furthermore, T-mfIL12 promoted the in situ maturation of DCs within the tumor microenvironment, evidenced by positive conversion of CD86. These findings suggest that iPSDCs represent a promising and scalable alternative to BMDCs in DC-based cancer immunotherapy, particularly when combined with oncolytic virus therapy.

It has been demonstrated that co-administration of immature BMDCs enhances the efficacy of G47Δ, the backbone virus of T-mfIL12, suggesting that virus-infected tumor cells can serve as a source of antigens for DC activation.9 Local administration of G47Δ has been shown to induce systemic antitumor immunity efficiently, both preclinically and clinically,1,3,5,6,19 providing a rationale for DCs to work effectively with G47Δ. Similar findings have been reported using oncolytic adenoviruses in combination with DCs.10,11 In this study, we not only show that immature BMDCs enhance the efficacy of T-01, the control virus compatible with G47Δ, but also show that such augmentation by immature BMDCs occurs with the immune-stimulating virus T-mfIL12. Furthermore, the functions of immature BMDCs can be replaced and reproduced by immature iPSDCs, and the augmentation of efficacies reflected the enhanced stimulation of tumor-specific immune responses.

IL-12 is a heterodimeric cytokine known to potently stimulate natural killer (NK) cells and CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes.20,21 It is increasingly recognized as a master regulator of antitumor immunity that can powerfully boost antitumor immunity.22 However, systemic administration of IL-12 in humans induces severe immune-related adverse effects including death,23 prompting investigation of localized IL-12 deliveries, including the use of viruses.24 Our strategy of combining IL-12-expressing oncolytic HSV-1 with immature DCs aims to maximize the immunologic functions of IL-12 for antitumor effects while ensuring safety. While virus-mediated destruction of tumor cells may initiate the processes by DCs and provide tumor-associated antigens, the local expression of IL-12 is likely to facilitate the functions of DCs, resulting in enhanced efficacy. In support, we show that the augmentation of efficacy by immature BMDCs is significantly higher when combined with T-mfIL12 than with T-01. Underlying immunological mechanisms are shown by robust infiltration of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells and the highest tumor-specific IFN-γ secretion by splenocytes with the T-mfIL12 combination. The human IL-12 version of T-mfIL12, termed T-hIL12, is currently undergoing a phase 1/2 clinical trial in patients with progressive malignant melanoma (jRCT2033190086).

It was not long after the discovery of iPSCs25 that DCs could be generated from iPSCs with minorly modified procedures from embryonic stem cells.26 Several other groups have also examined the use of iPSDCs in cancer immunotherapy,13,27,28 such as generating iPSDCs expressing tumor-associated antigens13,27 and combining iPSDCs with local radiotherapy.28 Several challenges have surrounded the clinical application of iPSC-based therapies, including scalability, manufacturing, and regulatory considerations, while they are being addressed by recent advances.29 The construction of iPSC banks containing common HLA haplotypes is currently underway,12,29 which may overcome one of the major limitations of conventional DC-based therapies—the labor-intensive and individualized preparation of autologous DCs for each patient. Because of the scalability of iPSDC production and their functional potencies compatible with BMDCs, iPSDCs are practical alternative to BMDCs. Particularly, the combination of iPSDCs with oncolytic virus therapy represents a promising strategy for clinical application.

This study has several methodological limitations. The limited availability of murine bladder cancer cell lines, with MB49 being practically the only widely used syngeneic model, precluded investigation in additional tumor models. Although short-term antitumor immune activation is demonstrated, long-term immune memory (e.g., tumor rechallenge) and detailed characterization of T cell subsets (e.g., memory T cells) are yet to be assessed. The safety profile of iPSCs, including tumorigenic potential and its possible mitigation by irradiation,30 also needs future evaluation. In addition, the use of male-derived DCs (BMDCs and iPSDCs) introduces the potential for immune recognition mediated by H-Y minor histocompatibility antigens in male-to-female transfer. However, DC-only controls did not show significant differences compared with mock controls, suggesting that such nonspecific immune activation is limited. Further studies are warranted to address these limitations.

Materials and methods

Generation of immature BMDCs

Bone marrow (BM) cells were harvested from the femurs and tibias of male C57BL/6 mice (Shizuoka Laboratory Animal Center, Japan), as described previously.14,31 To generate immature BMDCs, the BM cells were cultured for 5 days in 24-well culture plates (Greiner Bio-One Japan, Japan) containing RPMI-1640 medium (Life Technologies, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal calf serum (FCS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 10 ng/mL granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), 10 ng/mL interleukin-4 (IL-4; PeproTech, UK), and 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME; Wako, Japan).

Generation of immature iPSDCs

The male-derived murine embryonic fibroblast-derived iPSC line 2A-4F-100, generated using the 2A plasmid vector pCX-OKS-2A at the National Institute of Radiological Sciences (Chiba, Japan),32 was maintained and differentiated into iPSDCs as previously described.14 The differentiation process consisted of the following three steps, modified from a previously published protocol.26 In step 1, the iPSCs were suspended in α-MEM supplemented with 20% FCS and seeded onto murine BM stromal cell (OP9) layers in 100-mm dishes. On day 7, the cells were treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 0.25% trypsin/EDTA for 10 min, recovered in medium containing FCS, and subjected to step 2 of the culture or stored frozen for future use. In step 2, cells harvested from the step 1 culture were suspended in α-MEM supplemented with 20% FCS, GM-CSF (10 ng/mL), and 2-ME (50 mM) and plated in 100-mm dishes. The cells containing both iPSCs and OP9 cells recovered from one dish of the step 1 culture were divided into three dishes. The next day, the same culture medium with GM-CSF and 2-ME was added. Thereafter, 2–3 days after the passage, floating cells were recovered by pipetting and subjected to the step 3 culture or stocked frozen. In step 3, the cells were transferred to 2 × 106 cells/100-mm bacterial Petri dishes without feeder cells and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FCS, GM-CSF, and 2-ME. On day 2 after beginning step 3, the cells were transferred to 24-well hydroplates cultured in RPMI-1640/10% FCS supplemented with GM-CSF (20 ng/mL), IL-4 (10 ng/mL), and 2-ME.14 Cells were harvested on day 5 of step 3 as immature iPSDCs. For certain functional analyses, cells were further stimulated with IFN-γ (10 ng/mL) for additional 2 days to generate mature iPSDCs.14

Viruses

An IL-12-expressing virus based on G47Δ, designated T-mfIL12, was used in this study.15 Briefly, T-mfIL12 was engineered by inserting the murine fusion-type IL-12 gene into the ICP6-deleted locus of the G47Δ backbone, utilizing bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) technology.15,33 As a control, a virus termed T-01 was generated by inserting an empty cassette into the same locus.

Cells

The murine bladder cancer cell line MB49, originally induced by 7,12-dimethylbenz[a]anthracene,34 was kindly provided by Dr. Timothy L. Ratliff (Purdue University, USA; RRID: CVCL_7076). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FCS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin and maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Syngeneic subcutaneous bladder cancer model

Six-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Nippon SLC (Shizuoka, Japan; RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664) and housed in the animal care facility of our institution for 1 week prior to experimentation. Mice were housed in groups of five or fewer per cage under specific pathogen-free conditions in a restricted-access area, maintained at a temperature of 20°C ± 1°C and relative humidity of 50% ± 10%, with ad libitum access to food and tap water. For tumor implantation, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), followed by subcutaneous inoculation of 2 × 105 MB49 cells suspended in RPMI-1640 medium (total volume: 50 μL) into the right flank of each mouse.35 All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval no. P-16-008).

Microscopic analysis of immature iPSDCs

Cytospin preparations were generated using a Shandon Cytospin 4 cytocentrifuge (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The cytospin specimens were stained with May-Grünwald-Giemsa stain (Merck, Germany), and microscopic images were acquired using a DP70 digital camera system (Olympus, Japan).

Flow cytometry analysis of immature iPSDCs

Cells were harvested, resuspended in PBS, and incubated for 20 min at 4°C with optimal concentrations of various fluorescent monoclonal antibodies. The following anti-mouse monoclonal antibodies conjugated with fluorescence isothiocyanate (FITC), allophycocyanin (APC), or phycoerythrin (PE)-Cy7 were used for staining: CD11b (clone M1/70; BioLegend, cat. #101216, RRID: AB_312799), CD11c (clone N148; BioLegend, cat. #117310, RRID: AB_313779), CD40 (clone 3/23; BioLegend, cat. #124608, RRID: AB_1134096), CD80 (clone 16-10A1; BioLegend, cat. #104706, RRID: AB_313127), CD86 (clone GL-1; BioLegend, cat. #105005, RRID: AB_313148), and IA/IE (clone M5/114.15.2; BioLegend, cat. #107606, RRID: AB_313321). Matched isotype IgGs, as recommended by the manufacturer, were used as negative controls. For T cell proliferation analysis, APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD4 (clone RM4-5; BioLegend, cat. #100516, RRID: AB_312719) and PE/Cy7-conjugated anti-mouse CD8 (clone 53-6.7; BioLegend, cat. #100722, RRID: AB_312761) monoclonal antibodies were used. Stained cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (Gallios, Beckman Coulter, USA, or Attune, Life Technologies-ABI, USA), and data were processed using FlowJo software version 10.1 (FlowJo, RRID: SCR_008520).

Antigen uptake assay of immature iPSDCs

Immature iPSDCs were seeded into 24-well cell culture plates at 1 × 106 cells per well and cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FCS. For the antigen uptake assay, cells were incubated with either 20 mg/mL OVA-FITC (O23020, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 3 h at 37°C or 4°C, or 1 mg/mL Dextran-FITC (D-1845, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 13 min at 37°C or 4°C. Following incubation, cells were collected, washed with PBS, and stained with PE/Cy7-anti-CD11b (BioLegend, cat. #101216, RRID: AB_312799) and APC-anti-CD11c (BioLegend, cat. #117310, RRID: AB_313779) antibodies. Stained cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry.

MLR of immature iPSDCs

Transgenic mice C57BL/6-Tg(TCR-OT-I)Cbn (OT-I, H-2Kb) and C57BL/6-Tg(TCR-OT-II)Cbn (OT-II, H-2Kb) were kindly provided by Dr. N. Ishii (Graduate School of Medicine, Tohoku University, Japan; RRID: IMSR_JAX:003831) and Dr. S. Nakae (The Institute of Medical Science, The University of Tokyo, Japan; RRID: IMSR_JAX:004194), respectively. OT-I mice carry CD8+ T cells expressing a T cell receptor specific for the OVA peptide 257–264 presented on MHC class I,16 and OT-II mice carry CD4+ T cells expressing a T cell receptor specific for the OVA peptide 323–339 presented on MHC class II.17 Splenic T cells were isolated from OT-I and OT-II mice using a nylon wool column (Wako, Japan), labeled with CellTrace carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) or CellTrace Violet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and used as responder cells. Immature iPSDCs or BMDCs were used as stimulator cells (1 × 104 per well), pre-treated with OVA, and stimulated with IFN-γ for 2 days. These stimulator cells were co-cultured with responder T cells (2 × 105 per well) in 96-well round-bottom culture plates (Greiner Bio-One Japan, Japan) for 3 days. After the co-culture period, cells were harvested and stained with PE/Cy7-anti-CD8 (BioLegend, cat. #100722, RRID: AB_312761) and APC-anti-CD4 (BioLegend, cat. #100516, RRID: AB_312719) antibodies, followed by flow cytometric analysis.

PLNA of immature iPSDCs

Male C57BL/6 mice received adoptive transfer of CellTrace Violet-labeled T cells (3 × 106 cells in 0.5 mL per mouse) isolated from the spleens of OT-I or OT-II transgenic mice via intravenous injection into the tail vein. After 16 h, immature iPSDCs or BMDCs (a positive control), pulsed with OVA for ≥18 h, were injected into the hind footpads (1.5 × 105 cells in 30 μL PBS per footpad). The same volume of PBS was used as a negative control. Three days after footpad injection, the popliteal lymph nodes were harvested. Lymphocytes were stained with PE/Cy7-anti-CD8 and APC-anti-CD4 antibodies and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Combination of T-mfIL12 with immature BMDCs in the subcutaneous MB49 tumor model

When subcutaneous tumors reached approximately 5 mm in diameter, typically 7 days after implantation, mice were randomly assigned to one of six treatment groups by a biotechnician who was blinded to the treatment groups: mock, BMDC, T-01, T-01+BMDC, T-mfIL12, and T-mfIL12+BMDC. On day 0, tumors were intratumorally injected with 4 × 104 plaque-forming units (PFU) of either T-mfIL12 or T-01, suspended in 20 μL of PBS containing 10% glycerol. The optimal viral dose was determined by a preliminary dose-finding study that compared three doses of T-mfIL12 (4 × 104, 2 × 105, and 1 × 106 PFU) (Figure S2). On day 2, 1 × 106 immature BMDCs were injected intratumorally in 50 μL of PBS, followed by a second intratumoral inoculation with the virus (4 × 104 PFU) on day 5. The timing of BMDC and virus administration was based on a previous study by others evaluating the combination of G47Δ (the parental virus of T-mfIL12) and immature BMDCs.9 Tumor growth was monitored every 3 days by measuring tumor volume (length × width × height). Mice were euthanized when the maximum tumor dimension reached 21 mm, in accordance with the protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Immunohistochemistry

Subcutaneous MB49 tumors were excised on day 15 and snap-frozen in embedding medium (Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound; Sakura, USA). Tumors were sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm and fixed with cold acetone for 10 min and formaldehyde for 2 min. Sections were incubated with primary mouse monoclonal antibodies against CD8α (clone 53-6.7; Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #14-0081-82, RRID: AB_467087) and CD4 (clone RM4-5; Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #14-0042-82, RRID: AB_467067) for 1 h at room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked for 30 min. Sections were then incubated with a peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Rat IgG [H + L]; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, cat. #712-035-153, RRID: AB_2340639) for 30 min at 37°C. Immunostaining was visualized using the diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (ImmPACT DAB peroxidase substrate; Vector Laboratories, USA). Tissue sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and examined under a light microscope (KEYENCE, Japan).

The timing for evaluation of excised tumors and splenocytes was set at day 15 based on a previous study assessing the combination of immature BMDCs with G47Δ.9

IFN-γ ELISpot assay

Splenocytes were isolated from the spleens of mice in each treatment group on day 15 (n = 3 per group).9 Cells were plated in triplicates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 50 μL of RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FCS, 2% penicillin-streptomycin, and 50 μM 2-ME, using a MultiScreen 96-well plate (Millipore, USA) pre-coated with anti-mouse IFN-γ monoclonal antibodies from the Mouse IFN-γ ELISpot Kit (AN18; Mabtech AB, cat. #3321-2A). To stimulate cytokine production, ethanol-fixed MB49 cells (2 × 103 cells in 2 μL PBS per well) or phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, 40 ng/mL) plus ionomycin (4 μg/mL) (5 μL per well) as a positive control was added. Ethanol fixation was performed based on previous studies demonstrating that treatment with 30% ethanol (v/v) for 1 h at room temperature effectively inactivates tumor cells, including MB49.36,37 Plates were incubated for 48 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere and then washed five times with PBS. Wells were incubated with biotinylated anti-IFN-γ antibodies (R4-6A2; Mabtech AB, cat. #3321-2A) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by a 1-h incubation with the streptavidin-alkaline phosphatase conjugate. Cytokine-producing cells were visualized using the 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-phosphate (BCIP)/nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) substrate for 15 min. IFN-γ spot numbers were analyzed using a fully automated video imaging analysis system (KS ELISpot Compact, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Combination of T-mfIL12 with immature iPSDCs in the subcutaneous MB49 tumor model

To evaluate the efficacy of T-mfIL12 in combination with immature iPSDCs, a set of experiments identical to that with immature BMDCs was performed, which included measurement of tumor volume, immunohistochemistry, and IFN-γ ELISpot assays. Mice were divided into the following six treatment groups: mock, BMDC, iPSDC, T-mfIL12, T-mfIL12+BMDC, and T-mfIL12+iPSDC. In addition, immunohistochemical analysis of subcutaneous tumors was conducted to assess the status of DCs using surface markers CD11c (a marker for DC presence) and CD86 (a marker for DC maturation). The procedure followed the same protocol described earlier. For immunostaining, anti-mouse CD11c (clone N148; Abcam, cat. #ab33483, RRID: AB_726084) and anti-mouse CD86 (clone GL-1; Abcam, cat. #ab119857, RRID: AB_10902800) monoclonal antibodies were used.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons between treatment groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference test unless stated otherwise. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro version 17.0.0 (SAS Institute, RRID: SCR_022199).
