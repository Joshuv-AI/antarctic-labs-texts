Clinical Outcomes in a Longitudinal Virtual Dermatology Care Model for Eczema and Psoriasis
Authors: Lana Salloum, Eliza Dewey, Ursula Biba, Rachel Day, Arash Mostaghimi
Published: 2026

Introduction

Eczema and psoriasis impose substantial physical and psychosocial burdens requiring ongoing dermatological care [1,2]. Access to care remains limited, leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment escalation [3,4]. These barriers disproportionately affect patients with chronic, relapsing disease [5]. Virtual care models have been proposed to improve access to dermatological care [6]. This study evaluated the impact of a high-access virtual chronic care model on quality of life (QoL) using the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) in patients with eczema and psoriasis.

Methods

This prospective observational analysis evaluated a virtual chronic care model for longitudinal management of eczema and psoriasis. Patients received rapid, multimodal access to care, including synchronous video or audio visits and asynchronous messaging along with education, monitoring, and treatment adjustments.

At intake, patients completed standardized assessments, including medical and treatment history, DLQI, and disease severity. Individualized care plans included topical therapies, phototherapy, systemic treatments, and lifestyle support. DLQI was assessed and categorized as mild (0–5), moderate (6–10), or severe (11–30) and analyzed by domain: Symptoms and Feelings, Daily Impact, and Disease Burden. Clinically meaningful improvement was defined as a ≥4-point reduction (MCID). This project was conducted as a Quality Improvement Initiative at Zest Health and was not formally reviewed by an Institutional Review Board.

Findings

Among 329 patients (mean age 38.3 ± 12.7 years; 62% female), diagnoses included eczema (44.7%) and psoriasis (50.2%) (Table 1). Baseline QoL impairment was substantial (mean DLQI 11.60 ± 7.79), with 51.4% classified as severe.

Mean DLQI improved by 39.6% by the third encounter (7.19 ± 7.15; mean 87.9 ± 74.6 days) and by 65.5% by the final encounter (3.99 ± 5.68; mean 144.6 ± 154.9 days). The proportion of patients with mild impact increased from 26% to 75%, while severe impairment declined from 51% to 12%. Improvements were comparable between patients with eczema and psoriasis.

Longitudinal analysis demonstrated progressive reductions in DLQI over the first 200 days, with parallel improvements across all domains (48–63%) (Figure 1). By the second encounter, 84.2% of patients demonstrated improvement in at least one DLQI domain. MCID occurred in 35.6% of patients at the second encounter, 59.9% at the third, and 66.3% by the final encounter.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates rapid and sustained QoL improvement among patients with eczema and psoriasis managed through a high-access virtual chronic care model. Early gains were observed within 12 weeks, with 35.6% of patients achieving a clinically meaningful improvement, followed by continued longitudinal reductions in DLQI. Overall, DLQI decreased by 65.5% at last observation, accompanied by a marked shift from severe to mild disease burden.

Consistent improvements across DLQI domains and between eczema and psoriasis suggest this model may be broadly applicable. These findings highlight the potential impact of continuous monitoring, improved access, and dynamic treatment adjustments for patients with chronic inflammatory skin disease.

Interpretation is limited by selection and attrition bias, and future prospective studies would be valuable. Overall, these results support the role of longitudinal, technology-enabled care models in improving dermatological access and outcomes, with value-based payment frameworks critical for scalability and sustainability.
