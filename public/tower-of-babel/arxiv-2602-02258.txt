Alignment-Aware Model Adaptation via Feedback-Guided Optimization
Gaurav Bhatt, Aditya Chinchure, Jiawei Zhou, Leonid Sigal

Abstract
Fine-tuning is the primary mechanism for adapting foundation models to downstream tasks; however, standard approaches largely optimize task objectives in isolation and do not account for secondary yet critical alignment objectives (e.g., safety and hallucination avoidance). As a result, downstream fine-tuning can degrade alignment and fail to correct pre-existing misaligned behavior. We propose an alignment-aware fine-tuning framework that integrates feedback from an external alignment signal through policy-gradient–based regularization. Our method introduces an adaptive gating mechanism that dynamically balances supervised and alignment-driven gradients on a per-sample basis, prioritizing uncertain or misaligned cases while allowing well-aligned examples to follow standard supervised updates. The framework further learns abstention behavior for fully misaligned inputs, incorporating conservative responses directly into the fine-tuned model. Experiments on general and domain-specific instruction-tuning benchmarks demonstrate consistent reductions in harmful and hallucinated outputs without sacrificing downstream task performance. Additional analyses show robustness to adversarial fine-tuning, prompt-based attacks, and unsafe initializations, establishing adaptively gated alignment optimization as an effective approach for alignment-preserving and alignment-recovering model adaptation.

Introduction

Fine-tuning is the dominant paradigm for adapting foundation models, including LLMs and vision–language models
(
Howard & Ruder 2018
;
Devlin et al. 2019
;
Dai et al. 2023b
)
. However, common approaches such as supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement-based methods (e.g., RLHF, PPO, DPO, GRPO) optimize primarily for task performance and lack explicit mechanisms to enforce alignment properties
(
Wei et al. 2021
;
Rafailov et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2025
)
. As a result, critical behaviors such as safety, hallucination avoidance, uncertainty awareness, and robustness to misuse may degrade during adaptation, undermining reliability in real-world deployments
(
Kang et al. 2025
;
Qi et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2023a
)
.

Several studies have demonstrated that LLM fine-tuning can introduce unintended artifacts that degrade key alignment properties. For example, fine-tuning on harmful or unsafe instructions can weaken model’s resistance to unsafe queries
(
Peng et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024c
;
Guan et al. 2025
;
Rosati et al. 2024
;
Yi et al. 2025a
;
Huang et al. 2024a
;
Dai et al. 2023a
;
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Qi et al. 2025
)
. More importantly, even benign fine-tuning (performed without malicious intent or harmful data) can still measurably erode safety behaviors
(
Qi et al. 2023
;
Betley et al. 2025
;
He et al. 2024
)
. Recent works also highlight a separate but related issue: hallucination. LLMs are known to produce confident but incorrect statements, particularly on queries that fall outside their training distribution
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Kalai & Vempala 2024
;
Xu et al. 2024b
;
Shuster et al. 2021
)
. Fine-tuning can intensify this tendency, making models more likely to generate fabricated or ungrounded content when encountering unfamiliar or underspecified inputs
(
Kang et al. 2025
;
Wei et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025
)
.

Many alignment methods rely on curated auxiliary datasets to enforce alignment during fine-tuning, either by filtering unsafe samples or by jointly optimizing task and alignment objectives
(
O’Brien et al. 2025
;
Ham et al. 2025
;
Guan et al. 2025
;
Huang et al. 2024a
;
Dai et al. 2023a
)
. While effective, these approaches depend heavily on the availability and coverage of high-quality alignment data, which is costly to collect and often infeasible for open-ended properties such as hallucination, uncertainty awareness, or faithfulness. Moreover, data-driven alignment typically requires careful trade-off tuning between competing objectives, frequently leading to degraded alignment or task performance.

Recent works have explored
structured feedback
signals derived from large language models acting as external evaluator, where model outputs are evaluated according to explicitly defined criteria rather than relying on direct human annotations
(
Lambert et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Wen et al. 2025
;
Mu et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2025
)
.
Such feedback-based supervision reduces the need for large labeled datasets and enables scalable alignment across properties that are difficult to annotate exhaustively, including safety, uncertainty awareness, and faithfulness
(
Kang et al. 2025
;
Xu et al. 2024a
;
Wei et al. 2025
)
.
Crucially, structured feedback makes abstract alignment properties operational by rendering them observable to the optimization process through explicit scores, judgments, or directives, even in open-ended or underspecified settings.
This flexibility allows a single feedback model to provide multiple forms of guidance, such as scalar alignment scores, categorical assessments, or abstention instructions in case of misalignment, making structured feedback a natural foundation for feedback-guided, alignment-aware model adaptation.

Unlike prior approaches that rely on separate alignment stages or auxiliary datasets

Related Work

Fine-tuning.

Fine-tuning is the primary approach for adapting foundation models across modalities, including language, vision, and vision–language models
(
Devlin et al. 2019
;
Radford et al. 2018
;
Dong et al. 2024
;
Bhatt et al. 2024
;
Dai et al. 2023b
)
. To reduce the cost of full-model adaptation, parameter-efficient fine-tuning updates only a small subset of parameters
(
Hu et al. 2022
;
Lester et al. 2021
;
Zaken et al. 2021
)
. In practice, models are optimized via supervised or reinforcement-based objectives (e.g., RLHF, PPO, DPO, GRPO)
(
Wei et al. 2021
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Rafailov et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2025
)
. Although in-context learning provides a test-time alternative, fine-tuning remains preferred due to stronger performance and no inference-time overhead
(
Addlesee et al. 2023
;
Mosbach et al. 2023
)
.

Safety Alignment.

Existing safety-alignment methods fall into three categories: pre-training, fine-tuning, and post-training. Pre-training approaches either filter harmful data or modify training dynamics to improve robustness
(
Ham et al. 2025
;
O’Brien et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Huang et al. 2024b
)
. At the fine-tuning stage, both harmful and benign adaptation have been shown to degrade safety behaviors
(
Qi et al. 2023
;
He et al. 2024
;
Guan et al. 2025
;
Qi et al. 2025
)
, with preference-based objectives such as DPO being particularly vulnerable to shallow alignment and token-level bypasses (e.g., prefilling)
(
Qi et al. 2025
;
Andriushchenko et al. 2024
)
. To mitigate this, prior work incorporates safety data, alternates task and safety objectives, constrains gradients, or relies on safe reference policies during optimization
(
Bianchi et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2024a
;
Yi et al. 2025a
;
Li et al. 2025
)
. Post-training methods enforce safety at inference time via decoding-time interventions or output distribution control
(
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Yi et al. 2025b
)
. Despite these efforts, aligned models remain vulnerable to jailbreak attacks at inference, highlighting the limitations of static or surface-level alignment mechanisms
(
Andriushchenko et al. 2024
;
Qi et al. 2025
)
.

In contrast,
AWARE
neither relies on auxiliary safety datasets (e.g.,
SafeInstruct

(
Bianchi et al. 2023
)
,
LISA

(
Huang et al. 2024a
)
) nor on gradient-level constraints (e.g., Safe Gradient Surgery
(
Yi et al. 2025a
)
,
SA-LoRA

(
Li et al. 2025
)
), and does not assume access to a pre-aligned reference policy as required by preference-based methods such as
DPO

(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
and its variants
DPO-C

(
Qi et al. 2025
)
. Instead,
AWARE
injects alignemnt feedback directly into downstream fine-tuning and adaptively balances task and alignment objectives on a per-sample basis, enabling alignment correction even from unsafe initializations.

Hallucinations in LLMs.

Hallucination refers to the generation of fluent but factually incorrect or fabricated responses by large language models
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
, often arising from out-of-knowledge queries, ambiguous inputs, or limitations in factual representation and retrieval
(
Ji et al. 2023b
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Kalai & Vempala 2024
)
. Mitigation strategies fall into two broad classes: decoding-time interventions, which modify the output distribution at inference (e.g., contrastive decoding, self-consistency, calibrated decoding)
(
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Kadavath et al. 2022
)
, and fine-tuning–based methods that promote faithful generation during adaptation
(
Wei et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Khadangi et al. 2025
)
. Recent fine-tuning approaches emphasize learning abstention from out-of-knowledge data, either by replacing such samples with explicit refusals
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
or by using conservative reward signals, including structured rewards (like ternary rewards) optimized via GRPO, to discourage hallucination under uncertainty
(
Wei et al. 2025
;
Kang et al. 2025
)
.

Notably,
TruthRL

(
Wei et al. 2025
)
(GRPO-based) and
UFT

(
Kang et al. 2025
)
(RLHF-based) optimize structured (e.g., ternary) rewards primarily through policy-gradient objectives. In contrast,
AWARE
jointly combines supervised fine-tuning with policy-gradient–based alignment feedback, enabling more stable task learning while selectively enforcing alignment under uncertainty.

Reward-augmented likelihood training.

A line of work augments maximum likelihood estimation with sequence-level rewards to mitigate exposure bias and better align training with task-level metrics, including reward-augmented maximum likelihood (RAML;
(
Norouzi et al. 2016
)
), minimum risk training
(
Shen et al. 2016
)
, and reinforcement-learning–based sequence training
(
Ranzato et al. 2016
;
Paulus et al. 2018
)
. While effective in some settings, these approaches typically combine NLL with reward terms using fixed weights and implicitly assume well-specified, low-variance reward signals. Prior analyses have shown that such reinforcement-based objectives can be brittle in practice, exhibiting high variance, sensitivity to reward scaling, and limited gains over likelihood-based training when rewards are noisy or miscalibrated
(
Choshen et al. 2019
)
.

In contrast,
AWARE
incorporates alignment feedback as an on-policy regularizer and adaptively modulates its influence on a per-sample basis using reward distribution statistics, preserving stable supervised updates for confidently aligned inputs while remaining robust to underspecified or noisy rewards.

3
Method

3.1
Problem Formulation

We consider a fine-tuning task defined by a dataset of labeled samples
(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}
.
In this work, we focus on instruction-tuning of large language models, where
x
denotes an instruction and
y
denotes the corresponding ground-truth response.
Let
\mathcal{A}\in\{\text{safety},\,\text{faithfulness},\,\text{privacy},\,\ldots\}
denote a class of alignment properties of interest, and let
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}(x,y,\hat{y})\in[0,1]
be a sample-level alignment function that measures how well a model-generated response
\hat{y}
aligns with property
\mathcal{A}
for a given input–output pair
(x,y)
.

Our objective is to minimize the downstream task loss (
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
) of a model
\pi_{\theta}
while constraining its expected misalignment under the target alignment property.
This leads to the following constrained optimization problem:

\min_{\theta}\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[1-\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}(x,y,\hat{y})\Big]\leq\varepsilon.

(1)

We relax the constraint in Eq.
1
using a Lagrangian formulation, yielding the following unconstrained objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[-\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)+\lambda\big(1-\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}(x,y,\hat{y})\big)\Big],

(2)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is the Lagrange multiplier controlling the strength of alignment regularization.

The alignment term in Eq.
2
optimizes an explicit alignment metric
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}
, which is typically non-differentiable, sparse, and accessible only via black-box evaluation. Directly optimizing such metric-level objectives is known to be challenging in sequence modeling and reinforcement learning due to high-variance gradients and reward misspecification
(
Ranzato et al. 2016
;
Norouzi et al. 2016
;
Paulus et al. 2018
)
. We therefore treat alignment as a constrained expectation rather than a surrogate loss, and later introduce an adaptive optimization strategy that stabilizes training by modulating the alignment signal based on policy-induced uncertainty.

3.2
Alignment Function

We define a alignment function
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}(x,y,\hat{y})\in[0,1]
that assigns a scalar alignment score to a model-generated response, where higher values indicate stronger adherence to the target alignment property
\mathcal{A}
. The function is instantiated as an external LLM-based model that evaluates responses in a judge-like manner. Unlike parametric reward models used in RLHF or preference-based objectives such as DPO, the verifier is treated as a black box: its parameters are fixed, its outputs may be sparse or discrete, and gradients cannot be backpropagated through it. Alignment feedback is therefore incorporated via policy-gradient. In addition to scalar scores, the verifier can emit structured signals such as safety or harmfulness flags and context-dependent
creative abstention
instructions (
e.g.
, generating natural refusal responses) (see Figures
7
and
8
). This enables richer alignment supervision without modifying the optimization objective.

3.3
Feedback-guided optimization

The gradient of the downstream task objective in Eq.
1
can be estimated as

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=\underset{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\big].

(3)

Substituting the alignment function into the Lagrangian objective in Eq.
2
results in an optimization problem that combines a differentiable task loss with an alignment term whose gradients cannot be obtained by direct backpropagation, since
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}(x,y,\hat{y})
operates as a black-box.
To optimize the alignment objective, we therefore resort to the log-derivative trick introduced in the
REINFORCE
algorithm
(
Williams 1992
)
.
Specifically, we treat the model policy
\pi_{\theta}
as the sampling distribution and compute gradients through the log-probability of the generated response.

Alignment signal.

We use the alignment score as an
alignment reward
, where higher values indicate better adherence to the target property
\mathcal{A}
.
Formally, let
u(x,y,\hat{y})\triangleq\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}(x,y,\hat{y})\in[0,1]
denote the alignment signal for a sampled response
\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
. (When convenient, we may also refer to the misalignment as
1-u(x,y,\hat{y})
.)
We define the alignment objective as maximizing expected alignment reward,

\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta)\triangleq\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\big[u(x,y,\hat{y})\big].

Then its gradient admits the standard policy-gradient form

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta)=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[u(x,y,\hat{y})\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\hat{y}\mid x)\Big].

(4)

Equivalently, if we write an alignment
loss

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta)\triangleq-\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta)
, then

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta)=-\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[u(x,y,\hat{y})\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\hat{y}\mid x)\Big].

Update Rule.

Given a minibatch of size
B
and
k
sampled responses per input, the stochastic gradient of the Lagrangian objective can be approximated as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta,\lambda)\approx\frac{1}{B}\sum_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{B}}\Bigg[-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\;-

(5)

\displaystyle\lambda\cdot\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{A}_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\hat{y}_{i}\mid x)\Bigg],

where
\lambda
is the scaling coefficient, and
\hat{A}_{i}
denotes the normalized alignment
advantage
associated with the
i
-th sampled response and are computed as

\hat{A}_{i}=\frac{u_{i}-\bar{u}}{\mathrm{std}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})+\varepsilon},

(6)

with
u_{i}
denoting the alignment score for sample
i
and
\bar{u}
its sample mean. This per-input normalization stabilizes the policy-gradient updates and ensures that parameter updates are driven by relative deviations from the local alignment baseline rather than absolute reward magnitudes.

Unnormalized sequence log-probabilities bias policy-gradient updates toward longer responses, favoring verbosity over alignment quality. Accordingly, in Eqn.
5
we use length-normalized log-probabilities; for a sampled response
\hat{y}=(y^{t}_{1},\dots,y^{t}_{T})
, we compute the mean per-token log-likelihood

\log\pi_{\theta}(\hat{y}\mid x)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(y^{t}_{j}\mid x,y^{t}_{<j}).

(7)

3.4
Conflicting Objectives Problem.

The update in Eq.
5
combines two learning signals that can be
inherently conflicting. The alignment term reinforces responses that satisfy
the target property
\mathcal{A}
(e.g., safety), independent of task
correctness, while the supervised term maximizes the likelihood of the
ground-truth response
y
regardless of its alignment. This mismatch induces
gradient conflict: a safe refusal may receive high alignment scores yet
directly contradict the supervised objective that minimizes

-\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
. The conflict is most pronounced in adversarial
fine-tuning settings, where ground-truth responses
y
are harmful—the
supervised gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
pushes the model
toward unsafe behavior, while alignment gradients pull it toward refusal.
Naively combining these gradients can lead to conflict,
weakening the alignment signal precisely when it is most critical. This
observation motivates our adaptive gating mechanism, which dynamically
modulates the relative influence of each objective to prevent either from
dominating under conflict (shown in Figure
1
).

Adaptive Soft-Gating.

Given an input
x
and its corresponding set of
k
sampled responses

\{\hat{y}_{1},\ldots,\hat{y}_{k}\}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
, we denote the associated alignment scores by

u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
.
We first compute per-input summary statistics over the sampled responses,

\displaystyle\mu_{\theta}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}u_{j},\quad\sigma_{\theta}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(u_{j}-\mu_{x})^{2}},

(8)

which capture the average alignment level and its variability, respectively. We then define an adaptive soft-gating function
\beta_{x}\in[0,1]
as a monotonic
function of the computed statistics:

\displaystyle\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}(x)

\displaystyle=g\Big(\mu_{\theta}(x),\sigma_{\theta}(x)\Big)

(9)

\displaystyle g(\mu,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\gamma e^{-\sigma}\Big)\Big(1-\mu^{\rho}\Big),

where
\gamma>1
increases sensitivity to high-variance (uncertain) cases, and
\rho>1
downweights high-mean responses (already well-aligned).
\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}(x)
quantifies misalignment strength for input
x
: low mean or high variance in sampled responses yield large
\beta
(strong regularization), while confident, well-aligned samples yield small
\beta
. Critically,
\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}(x)
is policy-dependent, evolving as
\pi_{\theta}
improves. As alignment rewards concentrate during training (higher mean, lower variance),
\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}(x)
decreases automatically, regularization is strong under uncertainty and vanishes at convergence.

Align-Plots
(Figure
2
) visualize this behavior via
(\mu_{x},\sigma_{x},\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}(x))
mappings: high-
\mu_{x}
/low-
\sigma_{x}
samples (well-aligned) receive low
\beta
; low-
\mu_{x}
(misaligned) trigger abstention; high-
\sigma_{x}
(uncertain) indicate intermittent violations of
\mathcal{A}
. This diagnostic enables principled threshold selection and real-time monitoring of alignment dynamics.

Abstention for Fully Misaligned Samples.

For inputs identified as fully misaligned as indicated by persistently low alignment rewards, we enforce abstention using pseudo-labels that express refusal or uncertainty (e.g., “I cannot comply” or “I do not know”). This conservative behavior is especially important in high-risk settings such as medical QA, where avoiding confident errors is critical. Moreover, abstention is triggered in low-reward regimes, mitigating under-specified and high-variance policy-gradient updates that arise when optimizing sparse, non-differentiable alignment objectives.

We further adopt
creative abstention
, where the feedback model generates context-specific refusal instructions (Figure
8
), improving robustness to shallow fine-tuning
(
Qi et al. 2025
)
and prefilling attacks
(
Andriushchenko et al. 2024
)
.
Align-Plots
provide a principled mechanism for identifying fully misaligned regions (e.g.,
\mu_{x}<0.3
,
\sigma_{x}<0.15
in Figure
2
). Crucially, abstention is learned during training rather than imposed at decoding, enabling conservative behavior to generalize at inference time.

Objective.

We incorporate the adaptive soft-gating coefficient
\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}
to interpolate between the supervised fine-tuning objective and the alignment-driven policy-gradient loss:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta,\lambda)\approx\frac{1}{B}\sum_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{B}}\Bigg[-(1-\beta_{\pi_{\theta}})\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\;-

(10)

\displaystyle\lambda\cdot\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}\cdot\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{A}_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\hat{y}_{i}\mid x)\Bigg],

This formulation yields an adaptive training objective in which gradient updates are modulated on a per-sample basis. Inputs exhibiting higher alignment uncertainty or disagreement receive stronger alignment regularization, while samples that are already stable and well aligned are predominantly optimized using the supervised fine-tuning objective.

4
Experiments

Finetuning tasks.

We adopt general instruction tuning as the downstream fine-tuning task and evaluate our method on two benchmark datasets. The first is
Alpaca

(
Taori et al. 2023
)
, which contains a diverse set of instruction-following examples spanning open-ended questions, sentence completion, mathematical reasoning, and multiple-choice tasks. The second dataset is
BioInstruct

(
Tran et al. 2024
)
, which focuses on the biomedical domain and includes question answering, information extraction, text generation, and related tasks.

We evaluate responses using
BERTScore

(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
, which measures semantic similarity to reference answers but does not assess factual correctness. To address this, we additionally use a GPT-based judge (
Judge Score
,
JS
) that evaluates factuality and faithfulness by verifying the correctness of factual claims, conditioned on whether the query is fact-based or open-ended.

Safety Alignment Finetuning.

We evaluate safety alignment under an adversarial fine-tuning setting, following prior work
(
Huang et al. 2024a
;
Lyu et al. 2024
)
, in which the fine-tuning data are intentionally poisoned with unsafe examples. Consistent with
Huang et al. 2024a
, we construct fine-tuning datasets using
n=5000
samples drawn from the corresponding base datasets (
Alpaca
and
Bio-instruct
), and inject a fraction
ur
of unsafe or harmful samples sourced from the
BeaverTails
dataset
(
Ji et al. 2023a
)
.
To evaluate safety alignment in this setting, we assess the fine-tuned models on the
HEx-PHI
benchmark
(
Qi et al. 2023
)
and report the proportion of harmful responses, referred to as the harmful score (
HS
), which is also commonly termed the attack success rate (lower the better). We use
GPT-4o-mini
as an automated judge to determine whether a generated response is safe or unsafe, following
(
Qi et al. 2025
;
Huang et al. 2024a
)
.

Hallucination Alignment Finetuning.

We follow similar hallucination setup from prior works
(
Kang et al. 2025
;
Wei et al. 2025
)
and use out-of-knowledge (OOK) samples from the
SLAQ
dataset
(
Lauscher et al. 2025
)
, specifically the
LongQ
–
LongA
split. We evaluate hallucinations using an LLM-based verifier that assigns faithfulness scores to non-refusal responses, while explicitly detecting refusal responses. Each output is categorized as
truthful
,
hallucinated

5
Conclusion

We introduce
AWARE
, an alignment-aware fine-tuning framework that integrates external alignment feedback directly into optimization through adaptive, per-sample regularization. By dynamically balancing task learning and alignment enforcement and by learning abstention for fully misaligned inputs,
AWARE
resolves gradient conflicts that limit prior methods. Experiments across general and biomedical domains show consistent improvements in safety and hallucination robustness while preserving downstream performance, demonstrating that adaptive feedback-guided optimization is an effective approach for alignment-preserving fine-tuning.

Appendix A
Theorems and proofs

We analyze the asymptotic behavior of
AWARE
and show that, once the policy becomes
confidently aligned, the optimization dynamics reduce to standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
on an augmented dataset that explicitly encodes abstention targets for fully misaligned inputs.
Importantly, our analysis accounts for the
abstention override
, under which alignment
regularization is disabled and supervision is replaced by an abstention pseudo-label.

A.1
Setup

Let
u(x,y,\hat{y})=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}}(x,y,\hat{y})\in[0,1]
denote the alignment reward
provided by an external alignment model, where larger values indicate better adherence to the
target alignment property
\mathcal{A}
. For a fixed input
x
and
\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
, define the policy-induced moments

\displaystyle\mu_{\theta}(x)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\big[u(x,y,\hat{y})\big],

(14)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\theta}(x)

\displaystyle\triangleq\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\big(u(x,y,\hat{y})\big)}.

(15)

The adaptive gate is defined as

\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}(x)\triangleq\mathrm{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\Big((1-\gamma e^{-\sigma_{\theta}(x)})(1-\mu_{\theta}(x)^{\rho})\Big),

(16)

with
\rho>1
and
\gamma>0
. As in the algorithm, gradients are stopped through

\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}(x)
.

Abstention override.

Let
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
denote the region of
fully misaligned
inputs,
identified in practice using thresholding on
(\mu_{\theta}(x),\sigma_{\theta}(x))
(e.g.,
via
Align-Plots
). For
x\in\mathcal{R}
, we apply

\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}(x)\leftarrow 0,\qquad y\leftarrow y^{\text{abs}}(x),

(17)

where
y^{\text{abs}}(x)
is an abstention pseudo-label (e.g., refusal for safety or “I do not
know” for faithfulness). Thus, on
x\in\mathcal{R}
, updates reduce to pure supervised learning
on abstention targets.

A.2
Consistency of the Empirical Gate

In practice,
\mu_{\theta}(x)
and
\sigma_{\theta}(x)
are estimated using
k
Monte Carlo samples

\hat{y}_{1},\ldots,\hat{y}_{k}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
:

\hat{\mu}_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}u(x,y,\hat{y}_{i}),\qquad\hat{\sigma}_{k}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big(u(x,y,\hat{y}_{i})-\hat{\mu}_{k}(x)\big)^{2}}.

Lemma A.1

(Consistency of empirical moments)
.

For fixed
(\theta,x)
and bounded rewards
u\in[0,1]
, as
k\to\infty
,

\hat{\mu}_{k}(x)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mu_{\theta}(x),\qquad\hat{\sigma}_{k}(x)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\sigma_{\theta}(x),\qquad\hat{\beta}_{k}(x)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}(x).

Proof.

Since
u\in[0,1]
, both
\mathbb{E}[u]
and
\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]
exist. Almost sure convergence of

\hat{\mu}_{k}(x)
and
\hat{\sigma}_{k}(x)
follows from the strong law of large numbers applied to
first and second moments. The mapping in Eq. (
16
) is continuous, and clipping
preserves limits; hence
\hat{\beta}_{k}(x)\to\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}(x)
almost surely by the continuous
mapping theorem.
∎

A.3
Vanishing of the Gate under Confident Alignment

We next show that confident alignment implies collapse of reward variance and vanishing of the
adaptive gate.

Lemma A.2

(Variance collapse)
.

Let
U\in[0,1]
. If
\mathbb{E}[U]\to 1
, then
\mathrm{Var}(U)\to 0
.

Proof.

Since
U^{2}\leq U
almost surely,

\mathrm{Var}(U)=\mathbb{E}[U^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[U]^{2}\leq\mu-\mu^{2}=\mu(1-\mu)
, which vanishes as

\mu\to 1
.
∎

A.4

Define the effective supervision target

y,&x\notin\mathcal{R},\end{cases}

AWARE update.

\widehat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sft}}^{\mathrm{abs}}(\theta_{t})

adaptively interpolates between alignment-driven updates and

Appendix C
Alignment

C.1
Safety

To enforce safety during alignment-aware fine-tuning, we employ a structured LLM-based safety verifier that provides response-level supervision under strict behavioral constraints. As shown in Figure
7
, the verifier operates in three sequential phases. First, it performs a binary assessment of the user input to determine whether the request itself is harmful or benign. This input-level decision ensures that downstream safety evaluation is conditioned on the intent of the query rather than solely on the model’s response. Second, the verifier independently scores each of the
k
candidate responses using a continuous safety scale with hard upper bounds, assigning low scores to any response containing actionable or enabling harmful content. These strict caps prevent partial compliance or disclaimer-based responses from receiving high safety scores. Finally, when the input is deemed unsafe, the verifier generates a single creative refusal instruction that adheres to predefined constraints, ensuring that refusals remain non-actionable, concise, and helpful without relying on templated language. The verifier returns a structured JSON output containing the input safety flag, per-response safety scores, and the refusal instruction, enabling seamless integration into downstream optimization. The concrete verifier behavior, scoring guidelines, and output schema are specified through explicit LLM instructions, as illustrated in Figure
8
. This design enforces safety as a fine-grained, response-level signal while remaining robust to ambiguous inputs and avoiding over-refusal on benign queries.

C.2
Hallucination

To measure and mitigate hallucination during alignment-aware fine-tuning, we employ a strict LLM-based hallucination verifier that assigns response-level factuality scores under explicitly defined evaluation rules. As illustrated in Figure
9
, the verifier operates as a two-branch decision process conditioned on the type of user query. It first classifies the query as either factual question answering or open-ended generation. This distinction is crucial, as hallucination is only well-defined in the presence of verifiable factual content, whereas open-ended queries admit multiple valid responses.

For open-ended queries, the verifier scores each response independently based on relevance, coherence, and faithfulness to the instruction, without direct comparison to the ground-truth answer. This avoids penalizing legitimate stylistic or creative variation and ensures that the ground truth is not treated as a unique gold standard. For factual queries, the verifier follows a stricter evaluation protocol by extracting the
Core Factual Mechanism
(CFM) from the ground-truth answer, representing the essential definition, relationship, equation, or set of facts required for correctness. Each response is then evaluated for explicit inclusion of this mechanism. Responses that omit the CFM are automatically capped at low scores, while those containing incorrect facts, fabricated claims, or contradictory mechanisms receive progressively harsher penalties. These hard caps ensure that confident but incorrect answers, partial explanations, or superficially plausible fabrications are not rewarded.

The verifier outputs a structured JSON object containing per-response hallucination scores, enabling seamless integration into downstream optimization. The concrete scoring rules, penalties, and output schema are implemented through explicit LLM instructions, as shown in Figure
10
. By separating open-ended evaluation from mechanism-level factual verification, this design provides a robust and fine-grained signal for controlling hallucination while avoiding over-penalization of benign or creative responses.

C.2.1
Hallucination metrics

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote the evaluation set.
For each input
x_{i}
, the model generates a response
\hat{y}_{i}
.
We use a refusal detector
\mathbb{I}_{\text{ref}}(\hat{y}_{i})
that returns
1
if
\hat{y}_{i}
is an abstention and
0
otherwise.
For non-refusal responses, an LLM judge assigns a scalar faithfulness score
s_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.

A response is considered
truthful
if its judge score exceeds a fixed threshold
\tau
.
We define the Truthfulness score (
T
) as

\textbf{T}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\!\left[s_{i}\geq\tau\right]\cdot\big(1-\mathbb{I}_{\text{ref}}(\hat{y}_{i})\big),

(21)

where
\mathbb{I}[\cdot]
denotes the indicator function.

Similarly, the Hallucination Score (
HS
) is defined as the fraction of non-refusal responses whose judge score falls below the threshold:

\textbf{HS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}\!\left[s_{i}<\tau\right]\cdot\big(1-\mathbb{I}_{\text{ref}}(\hat{y}_{i})\big).

(22)

Finally, we define the abstention rate (
AR
) as

\textbf{AR}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}_{\text{ref}}(\hat{y}_{i}).

(23)

By construction, each response belongs to exactly one of the three mutually exclusive categories—truthful, hallucinated, or abstained—and therefore the metrics satisfy the identity

\textbf{T}+\textbf{HS}+\textbf{AR}=1.

(24)

This decomposition provides a complete and interpretable characterization of model behavior under hallucination evaluation.

D.6
Align-plots for analyzing safety behavior of base models

Figure
13
illustrates the safety alignment dynamics of the base pretrained models before any fine-tuning. Each point corresponds to a model response characterized by the mean and variability of verifier rewards, revealing how alignment behavior is distributed inherently in the pretrained representations. Well-aligned responses form tight clusters with high mean reward and low variance, indicating stable and consistently safe behavior, whereas misaligned responses exhibit lower rewards and greater uncertainty. This separation suggests that pretrained models already encode latent alignment structure, which can be exploited during fine-tuning by selectively amplifying alignment-driven updates rather than uniformly enforcing safety constraints.

D.7
Effect of
\gamma
and
\rho
in adaptive soft-gating.

Figures
14
and
15
analyze the sensitivity of the adaptive soft-gating coefficient
\beta_{\pi_{\theta}}
to the hyperparameters
\gamma
and
\rho
, which control variance sensitivity and mean suppression, respectively.
As shown in Figure
14
, increasing
\gamma
(from
\gamma=1
to
\gamma=5
with fixed
\rho=2
) progressively amplifies the influence of reward variance: partially misaligned samples with high uncertainty receive larger
\beta
, while well-aligned, low-variance samples are more sharply downweighted. Small
\gamma
yields smoother transitions but under-penalizes uncertain regions, whereas large
\gamma
produces a clearer separation between well-aligned and uncertain samples at the cost of increased aggressiveness.
Figure
15
fixes
\gamma=3
and varies
\rho
, revealing that larger
\rho
increasingly suppresses
\beta
for high-mean (well-aligned) samples. While low
\rho
assigns moderate alignment weight even to already aligned responses, higher
\rho
concentrates regularization almost exclusively on low-mean regions, sharpening the boundary between aligned and misaligned samples.
Overall, these ablations demonstrate that
\gamma
primarily controls sensitivity to uncertainty (variance), whereas
\rho
governs how quickly alignment regularization is relaxed as mean alignment improves. In practice, intermediate values (e.g.,
\gamma=3
,
\rho=2
) strike a favorable balance, yielding stable separation between well-aligned, uncertain, and fully misaligned regimes without over-regularizing confident samples.

D.8
Limitations

AWARE
relies on external alignment models to provide structured, on-policy feedback, and its effectiveness is therefore influenced by the quality, coverage, and bias of these models. While our experiments demonstrate robustness to moderately underspecified alignment models, severely miscalibrated or adversarial feedback may still affect performance. As an on-policy method,
AWARE
adapts alignment behavior based on responses generated by the current policy, which improves stability and avoids reliance on static or offline preference signals, but also incurs additional computational cost due to response sampling and alignment-model evaluation. This overhead may limit scalability in resource-constrained settings. Improving the efficiency of on-policy alignment and strengthening robustness to noisy or adversarial feedback are important directions for future work.
