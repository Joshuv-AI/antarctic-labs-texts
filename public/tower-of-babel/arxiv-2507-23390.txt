FMIP: Multimodal F low Matching for M ixed I nteger Linear P rogramming
Hongpei Li, Hui Yuan, Han Zhang, Dongdong Ge, Mengdi Wang, Yinyu Ye

Abstract
Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) is a cornerstone of mathematical optimization, enabling the modeling of complex decision-making problems involving both integer and continuous variables. Despite its versatility, most MILP problems are NP-complete, making them challenging to solve in practice. Existing graph neural network (GNN)-based heuristics aim to reduce problem scale by predicting only the solutions on integer variables for a given instance, struggling to capture the intricate interplay between continuous and integer variables and lack sufficient representational power. To address these limitations, we propose FMIP, a novel multimodal flow-matching framework that models the joint distribution over integer and continuous variables in the mixed solution space of MILP. To enable more accurate and scalable heuristics, FMIP integrates a guidance mechanism to guide solution sampling under both objective function optimization and constraint satisfaction. We evaluate FMIP on seven standard MILP benchmarks. Our experiments show that FMIP improves solution quality by 50.04% on average over existing GNN-based predictive baselines. These results highlight FMIP’s potential as a powerful new approach for developing learning based MILP solution strategy.

Introduction

Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) is a cornerstone of mathematical optimization. It provides a robust framework for modeling complex decision-making problems that involve both discrete and continuous variables subject to linear constraints. MILP is particularly valuable because it can simultaneously capture integer decisions, such as facility locations or binary choices, and continuous quantities like resource allocation. For instance, MILP frequently models facility location problems
KRATICA20142118
and resource allocation scenarios
(
HE2015441
)
, with applications also extending to areas such as Combinatorial Optimization (CO)
(
DellaCroce2014
)
, Energy Systems Optimization
(
miehling2023energyoptimisationusingmixed
)
, and Supply Chain Network Design
(
IVANOV2022107976
)
. By integrating integer and continuous components, MILP offers the flexibility to represent a wide range of real-world scenarios.

Solving MILP problems is a fundamental challenge in both computer science and operations research. The inherent complexity of MILP—where most instances are NP-complete
(
milpisnpc
)
—makes them notoriously difficult to solve in practice. This NP-completeness means that the computational effort required to solve these problems can increase exponentially with problem size, often necessitating the use of heuristics and approximation methods in practice. The difficulty of solving MILP problems has motivated extensive research into more efficient algorithms, including those based on deep learning. Existing deep learning-based methods for MILP predominantly focus on learning to predict variables or an initial solution for a given instance, typically in a supervised learning manner. To guarantee feasibility and improve solution optimality, various downstream solvers are employed to refine the predicted solutions from the deep learning models in a first stage
(
nair2020solving
;
han2023gnnguidedpredictandsearchframeworkmixedinteger
;
liu2025apollomilpalternatingpredictioncorrectionneural
;
chen2025datadrivenmixedintegeroptimization
)
. However, current frameworks commonly employ graph neural networks (GNNs) for prediction, and crucially, often predict only integer variables while ignoring the continuous variables and leaving them for downstream solvers to determine
(
nair2020solving
;
liu2025apollomilpalternatingpredictioncorrectionneural
)
. Due to the limited representational capacity of GNNs
(
chen2023representingmixedintegerlinearprograms
)
for MILP and the strong coupling between variables, particularly between integer and continuous types, these previous works struggle to significantly improve quality of estimated solution, thus hindering the exploration of superior solutions.

Multimodal flow matching has recently gained remarkable success in jointly modeling both discrete and continuous data
(
multiflow
;
tfg
)
. Leveraging this capability, guidance mechanisms can be used to steer the generation process. Motivated by the strengths of multimodal flow matching, particularly its ability to address limitations found in GNN-based predictive methods for MILP, we propose a novel framework named FMIP, standing for Multimodal
F
low Matching for
M
ixed
I
nteger Linear

FMIP leverages the strengths of multimodal flow matching to jointly capture the coupling between integer and continuous variables and control the generation process through a guidance mechanism incorporating objective function and constraint satisfaction. Similar to previous works, downstream solvers use the generated results of multimodal flow matching to efficiently explore solutions. FMIP is compatible with all existing solvers designed for GNN-based predictive models, such as Neural Diving

Related Work

Machine learning techniques for solving MILP can be broadly categorized into two approaches: (i)
Inside the solver
, which directly modifies or enhances components of the MILP solving algorithm—particularly within the Branch-and-Bound framework
(
gasse2019exact
;
parsonson2022reinforcementlearningbranchandboundoptimisation
)
and Large Neighborhood Search
(
liu2024mixedinteger
)
; and (ii)
Outside the solver
, which employs ML models to predict high-quality solutions or variable assignments, without altering the core solver architecture
(
han2023gnnguidedpredictandsearchframeworkmixedinteger
;
10603216
;
liu2025apollomilpalternatingpredictioncorrectionneural
;
chen2025datadrivenmixedintegeroptimization
)
. The former approach requires integrating ML methods into the internal logic of the MILP solver. In contrast, the latter—which is the focus of our work—leverages predictive models to predict candidate solutions to guide downstream solvers and algorithms. This approach is generally easier to integrate with existing solvers, as it avoids changes to the underlying algorithm. Compared to other existing methods in this category, our approach is based on multimodal flow matching. This allows us to better model the distribution of high-quality solutions and, through a guidance mechanism, provides the model with instance-wise feedback to improve prediction quality, thereby further enhancing the quality of solutions found by downstream solvers. E
.

Preliminaries

In this section, we review the definition of MILP problems and the basics of flow matching.

3.1
Definition of MILP

Let the decision variables be denoted by
\boldsymbol{x}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{I}},\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{C}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{q}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-q}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{I}}
represents the
q
integer variables and
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{C}}
represents the
n-q
continuous variables.
Then, an MILP instance can be defined as:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}

(1)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle A\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{b}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{lb}\leq\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{ub}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\in\{0,1,\dots,K\}^{q}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-q}

Here,
\boldsymbol{lb},\boldsymbol{ub},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the vectors representing the lower bounds, upper bounds for the decision variables, and the objective function coefficients, respectively.
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
denote the coefficient matrix and the right-hand-side vector of the linear constraints. Furthermore,
K
is a scalar integer defining the maximum feasible value of each integer variable (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\{0,1,\dots,K\}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}
).
An MILP instance with
m
linear constraints and
n
variables can thus be represented by the tuple
\boldsymbol{M}=(A,\boldsymbol{b,lb,ub,w})
. A solution
\boldsymbol{x}
is considered feasible if all constraints are satisfied.

In this paper, we focus on MILP problems with bounded integer variables, as most real-world applications exhibit this characteristic. Specially, when
K=1
such that
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\in\{0,1\}^{q}
, the problem specializes to Mixed Integer Binary Programming (MIBP), which is the most commonly and extensively studied subclass of MILP
(
han2023gnnguidedpredictandsearchframeworkmixedinteger
;
pmlr-v235-huang24f
;
liu2025apollomilpalternatingpredictioncorrectionneural
)
. Our proposed method can be extended to address MILPs with unbounded integer variables by incorporating techniques in prior work
(
nair2020solving
)
.

3.2
Flow Matching

Flow Matching (FM)
(
lipman2022flow
;
albergo2022building
;
liu2022flow
)
presents a powerful and intuitive framework for generative modeling. It operates by learning a time-dependent vector field that deterministically transforms samples from a simple noise distribution
p_{0}
(e.g., a Gaussian) into samples from a target data distribution.

Given a dataset of samples from the target distribution
q
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the objective is to learn a model that generates new samples from
q
, which we also denote as
p_{1}
. FM achieves this by defining a probability path
(p_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
that interpolates between
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
. Each
p_{t}
is a distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the path is typically induced by an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE)
\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}{dt}=v_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\sim p_{1}
and
v_{t}
is the vector field to learn. The core idea of FM is to directly regress this vector field
v_{t}
using a simple objective, making it an attractive alternative to other generative paradigms. The framework defines both forward flow (from noise to data) and backward flow (from data to noise), and is flexible to model both continuous and discrete data. In what follows, we review the continuous flow matching (CFM) and the discrete flow matching (DFM).

CFM and DFM
In CFM, the noise distribution is typically chosen as a standard Gaussian. The backward flow from data to noise is defined by a conditional distribution
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(t\boldsymbol{x}_{1},(1-t)^{2}I)
, where
\boldsymbol{x}
denotes the continuous data vector. In DFM, the noise distribution is the uniform categorical distribution. The backward flow can be defined for each component
i
as
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}^{(i)}\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{1}^{(i)}\sim\operatorname{Cat}(t\delta\{\boldsymbol{y}_{t}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}^{(i)}\}+(1-t)/K\})
, where
\boldsymbol{y}
denotes the discrete data vector,
K
is the number of categories,
\delta(y,y^{\prime})
is the Kronecker delta, which is 1 when
y=y^{\prime}
and 0 otherwise and
\operatorname{Cat}
denotes a Categorical distribution. With these definitions, the conditional velocity field
\bm{\nu}_{t\mid 1}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{1})
in CFM and conditional rate matrix
R_{t\mid 1}(\cdot,\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{1}^{(i)})
in DFM have closed form expressions:

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}_{t\mid 1}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right)=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}{1-t},\qquad R_{t\mid 1}\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}^{(i)},j\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{1}^{(i)}\right)=\frac{\delta\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{1}^{(i)},j\right)}{1-t}(1-\delta\{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{y}_{t}^{(i)}\}),

(2)

where
j=1,2,\cdots,K
represents a category index and
i=1,2,\cdots,N
is the component index of the data vector.

The forward flow can be defined as the following, in which the velocity
\bm{\nu}_{t\mid 1}
and the rate matrix
R_{t\mid 1}
will be replaced by model estimates during the sampling process.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{t+\Delta t}=\boldsymbol{x}_{t}+\bm{\nu}_{t\mid 1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\Delta t\quad\text{(for CFM)},

(3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{t+\Delta t}^{(i)}\sim\operatorname{Cat}\left(\delta(\boldsymbol{y}_{t+\Delta t}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{y}_{t}^{(i)})+R_{t\mid 1}(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{y}_{t+\Delta t}^{(i)}\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{1}^{(i)})\Delta t\right)\quad\text{(for DFM, per component }i).

Here, the second line for DFM describes the transition probability for the
i
-th component
\boldsymbol{y}^{(i)}
from state
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}^{(i)}
to state
\boldsymbol{y}_{t+\Delta t}^{(i)}
for a small time step
\Delta t
. Note that the argument of
\operatorname{Cat}
is interpreted as providing probabilities proportional to the likelihood of transitioning to each possible category
\boldsymbol{y}_{t+\Delta t}^{(i)}
.

4
FMIP: Multimodal Flow Matching for MILP

In this section, we present the FMIP, a framework for solving MILP based on multimodal flow models. The pipeline of FMIP includes the construction of a graph representation for MILP instance and solution, the training process of a graph-based multimodal flow model, the (guided) inference procedure, and the integration with downstream solvers. Illustration in Figure
1
provides an overview of the FMIP pipeline.

4.1
Graph Representation

Graph-based formulations have emerged as a powerful way to represent Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) instances for machine learning models, particularly graph neural networks. Building on the pioneering work by
(
gasse2019exact
)
which modeled the Branch and Bound search state using a bipartite graph, subsequent studies
(
digmilp
;
chen2023representingmixedintegerlinearprograms
;
geng2023deep
;
wang2023g2milp
)
have adapted this concept to represent the static MILP instance structure itself. A widely adopted approach is the bipartite graph representation, where the vertex set is partitioned into two distinct sets:
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}
representing the
n
decision variables and
C=\{con_{1},con_{2},\dots,con_{m}\}
representing the
m
constraints. Edges in this structure typically connect variables to the constraints in which they appear. While effective, these bipartite graphs primarily capture the variable-constraint relationships, motivating the exploration of richer representations to encode more detailed problem structure.

Representation of MILP as a Graph

Building on previous work, we’ve adapted the bipartite graph structure into a tripartite graph. This new structure more naturally accommodates our multimodal setting by explicitly distinguishing three types of nodes: Integer Variables (Ivar), Continuous Variables (Cvar), and Constraints (con).Edges, denoted as
e_{ij}
, are drawn between the variable nodes (both Ivar and Cvar) and the constraint nodes to capture their algebraic relationships within the MILP formulation.The edge weights
e_{ij}

correspond to the coefficients of variables in their associated constraints. The features of nodes
and edges of a problem graph
\boldsymbol{G}=(\mathcal{V}_{\text{Ivar}},\mathcal{V}_{\text{Cvar}},\mathcal{V}_{\text{con}},\mathcal{E})
are:

•

Ivar/Cvar Nodes
(
\mathcal{V}_{\text{Ivar}}/\mathcal{V}_{\text{Cvar}}
): The feature vector for variable
v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{\text{Ivar}}\cup\mathcal{V}_{\text{Cvar}}
is defined as:

\boldsymbol{h}_{v_{i}}=\left[\underbrace{\boldsymbol{w}_{i}}_{\text{objective coefficient}},\,\underbrace{\boldsymbol{lb}_{i}}_{\text{lower bound}},\,\underbrace{\boldsymbol{ub}_{i}}_{\text{upper bound}},\,\underbrace{\boldsymbol{hlb}_{i}}_{\text{if }lb_{i}>-\infty},\,\underbrace{\boldsymbol{hub}_{i}}_{\text{if }ub_{i}<+\infty}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{5},

where
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{lb},\boldsymbol{ub}
are defined in Eq.
1
.

•

Constraint Nodes
(
\mathcal{V}_{\text{con}}
): The feature for constraint
con_{j}
is scalar
b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, representing its right-hand side constant.

•

Edges
(
\mathcal{E}
): We construct a sparse bipartite graph based on the nonzero entries of the constraint matrix
A
. Specifically, an edge is added between variable node
j
and constraint node
i
if and only if
A[i,j]>0
. The edge feature is set as the corresponding coefficient, i.e., the edge from
j
to
i
carries weight
A[i,j]
.

Remark:
It’s important to note that our tripartite structure is not fundamentally different from the previous bipartite structure. The reason for this modification is to make the model easier to understand and implement in a multimodal context. Our ablation study, as shown in Table
5
, proved this fact.

Graph Representation used in Flow Matching
To represent the time-dependent state in the solution generation process within our flow matching framework, we define the solution graph at time-step
t\in[0,1]
as the tuple
\boldsymbol{G}_{t}=\left(\boldsymbol{G},\,\boldsymbol{d}_{t},\,\boldsymbol{c}_{t}\right)
, where
\boldsymbol{d}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{c}_{t}
are the solution values for the integer and continuous variables at time-step

\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=(\boldsymbol{d}_{t},\boldsymbol{c}_{t})
as the vector of solution values for all variables,
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{(i)}
denotes the state of the
i
-th variable. Here,
\boldsymbol{G}
is the static tripartite problem graph defined previously. When processing with a graph neural network, this state information
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
is incorporated into the features of the corresponding variable nodes in
\boldsymbol{G}
. Specifically, for each variable node
v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{\text{Ivar}}\cup\mathcal{V}_{\text{Cvar}}

\boldsymbol{h}_{v_{i}}
from the problem graph is augmented with its current value from the solution vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
, forming the time-dependent feature vector
\boldsymbol{h}_{v_{i},t}=[\boldsymbol{h}_{v_{i}},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{(i)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{6}

4.2
Multimodal Flow Matching in Mixed Solution Space

To overcome the limitation of existing methods where deep-learning models only predict integer variables, we utilize the multimodal flow model (Multiflow)
(
multiflow
)

(
flowmatching
)
, to generate both integer and continuous variables. Multiflow constructs a probability flow
p_{t}(\boldsymbol{G}_{t})\text{ for }t\in[0,1]
, where
p_{0}(\boldsymbol{G}_{0})=p_{\text{noise}}\text{ and }p_{1}(\boldsymbol{G}_{1})=p_{\text{data}}
, where
p_{\text{noise}}
and
p_{\text{data}}
are the distribution of random noise and clean data, respectively.

p_{\text{data}}
denotes the distribution of training set and
\boldsymbol{G}_{1}=(\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\boldsymbol{c}_{1})
denotes a piece of training data containing a MILP instance and its solution. Then a conditional flow can be defined as
p_{t\mid 1}(\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{G}_{1})
, which is a probability density over the continuous variables and a probability mass function over the discrete variables. According to our state
\boldsymbol{G}_{t}
, this flow can be factorized as follows:

p_{t\mid 1}(\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{G}_{1})=p_{t\mid 1}(\boldsymbol{d}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\boldsymbol{G})p_{t\mid 1}(\boldsymbol{c}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}).

Multimodal Training Loss
With a model predicting the values of continuous variables by
\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\theta}_{1}(\cdot)
and predicting the logits of integer variables by
\operatorname{log}p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\mid\cdot)
for any state
\boldsymbol{G}_{t}
, our training loss combines terms for both continuous and integer variables following the multimodal flow matching framework
(
multiflow
)
:

\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,\boldsymbol{G}_{1}}\left[\frac{\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\theta}_{1}(\boldsymbol{G}_{t})-\boldsymbol{c}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{1-t}-\omega\operatorname{log}p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{G}_{t})\right]\text{with}t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1),\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\sim p_{\text{data}},

(4)

where
\omega
is a hyperparameter balancing the two terms,
\theta
denotes the model parameters.

Sampling Process
At time
t
during the sampling phase, the neural network takes the noised graph
\boldsymbol{G}_{t}
as input and outputs the distribution of integer variables
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{G}_{t})
and denoised continuous variables
\hat{\textbf{c}}^{\theta}_{1}(\boldsymbol{G}_{t})
. By parameterizing the velocity and rate matrix described in Eq.
2
with
\hat{\textbf{c}}^{\theta}_{1}
and
p_{\theta}
, we obtain the following estimated velocity field and rate matrix:

\nu^{\theta}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\right)=\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\theta}_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\right)-\boldsymbol{c}_{t}}{1-t},\qquad R^{\theta}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{G}_{t},j\right)=\frac{p_{\theta}\left(\boldsymbol{d}_{1}=j\mid\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\right)}{1-t}(1-\delta\{\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\boldsymbol{d}_{t}\}).

Then the update equations described in Eq.
3
are used to sample the generative model, utilizing
\nu^{\theta}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\right)
and
R^{\theta}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{G}_{t},j\right)
. During the sampling process, uniform and cosine discretizations on
\Delta t
are the most common practice in diffusion models
(
shi2025simplifiedgeneralizedmaskeddiffusion
)
, which can be applied naturally in flow matching. We choose the cosine discretization, which iterates more steps in the low noise region, contributing to better generations
(
nichol2021improved
;
yu2023magvit
;
shi2025simplifiedgeneralizedmaskeddiffusion
)
.

Compared to existing methods, our multi-flow framework offers two key advantages. First, it leverages both discrete and continuous information during inference, allowing for a more comprehensive representation of the solution space. Second, it is a generative model, which enables greater flexibility through the incorporation of guidance mechanisms during sampling. This stands in contrast to traditional GNN-based predictors, which are typically limited to one-shot inference. The guidance mechanism is further detailed in Section
5
.

4.3
Integration with Downstream Solvers

After the training and sampling phases, we obtain a set of candidate solutions, each comprising a sampled integer variable assignment
\boldsymbol{d}_{1}
and generated continuous variable values
\boldsymbol{c}_{1}
. The generated results can then further refined by downstream solvers to efficiently search for better solutions. FMIP is compatible with the downstream search methods previously designed for GNN-based predictive models such as
(
han2023gnnguidedpredictandsearchframeworkmixedinteger
)
. Specifically, we pass the generated integer variable distribution to these downstream solvers as input. Downstream solvers then perform search or solve a subproblem based on this distribution, where higher quality distributions can better guide downstream solvers. We selected four representative methods to use as downstream solvers for FMIP: Neural Diving
(
nair2020solving
)
, Predict&Search
(
han2023gnnguidedpredictandsearchframeworkmixedinteger
)
, PMVB
(
chen2025datadrivenmixedintegeroptimization
)
, and Apollo-MILP
(
liu2025apollomilpalternatingpredictioncorrectionneural
)
. Our experimental results are shown in Table
1
, where FMIP achieved state-of-the-art (SOTA) results, improving performance by over 50% on average compared to GNN-based predictive models when used with the same downstream solvers.

4.4
Architecture of FMIP

Our neural network architecture builds on the Bipartite Graph Convolutional Network (Bi-GCN) from
nair2021solvingmixedintegerprograms
, and extends it to a tripartite graph tailored for MILP problems. This graph includes three types of nodes: integer variables, continuous variables, and constraints. We use separate parameter sets to encode each node type, allowing the model to capture their distinct roles within the MILP formulation. To enhance representation learning and training stability, we incorporate residual links in the message-passing layers. These links help preserve node features across layers and improve gradient flow, especially in deeper networks. For implementation details, see Appendix
B
. Section
6
includes an ablation study demonstrating the benefits of the residual architecture.

5
Guidance Mechanism

Though the multimodal generative modeling of MILP solutions can help improve solution quality by taking account the relationship between continuous and discrete variables, further integrating a guidance mechanism in the sampling process will better uncover the potential of flow matching in solving MILP. In this section, we introduce the guidance design in FMIP.

In MILP, there are two major metrics to judge the quality of a solution, objective value and constraint satisfaction. Objective value measures the distance between current solution and optimal solution hyperplane defined by objective coefficient
w
in Eq.
1
, and constraint satisfaction measures the distance between current solution and feasible solution region. In this section, we introduce our guidance mechanism inside FMIP, which incorporate both objective value and constraint satisfaction.

Target of Guidance
Motivated by the analysis above, we propose following target function to apply guidance:

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}+\gamma\sum_{j\in m}\left(A_{[i,*]}\boldsymbol{x}-b_{j}\right)_{+}^{2},

where
\gamma
is a hyperparameter to balance the two terms,
A_{[i,*]}
is the
i
-th row of the coefficient matrix
A
, and
(x)_{+}=\max(0,x)
. The first term is
the objective value of the MILP problem, and the second term is the penalty for violating the constraints.

Following the guidance strategy in
tfg
, we introduce the guided step for sampling MILP solutions. Given a target function value
f^{*}
, the guided flow
p_{t}(\boldsymbol{c}_{t}\mid f^{*}),p_{t}(\boldsymbol{d}_{t}\mid f^{*})
is defined based on the Fokker-Planck equation
\partial_{t}p_{t}=-\nabla\cdot(\nu_{t}p_{t})
and the Kolmogorov equation
\partial_{t}p_{t}=p_{t}R_{t}
. Specifically, the guided velocity is given by
v_{t}(\boldsymbol{c}_{t}\mid f)=\mathbb{E}_{p_{1\mid t}(\boldsymbol{c}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{c}_{t},f^{*})}[v_{t\mid 1}(\boldsymbol{c}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{c}_{1})]
, and the guided rate matrix is defined as
R_{t}(\boldsymbol{d}_{t}^{(i)},j\mid f^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{p_{1\mid t}(\boldsymbol{d}_{1}^{(i)}\mid\boldsymbol{d}_{t}^{(i)},f^{*})}[R_{t\mid 1}(\boldsymbol{d}_{t}^{(i)},j\mid\boldsymbol{d}_{1}^{(i)})]
, where
j=1,2,\cdots,K
represents a category index and
i=1,2,\cdots,N
is the component index of the data vector.

Guidance on Continuous Variables
Following the most common approach for adding continuous guidance, we employ a gradient ascent strategy to modify the continuous variables at each step. Specifically, we simulate the following iterative process with a small step size
\rho_{t}
:

\boldsymbol{c}_{t+\Delta t}=\text{Project}_{\boldsymbol{lb},\boldsymbol{ub}}\left(\boldsymbol{c}_{t}+\rho_{t}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{c}_{t}}\log f(\boldsymbol{d}_{1|t},\boldsymbol{c}_{1|t}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{d}_{1|t}
is a sample drawn from
p_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{G}_{t})
and
\boldsymbol{c}_{1|t}=\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\theta}_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\right)
.
\text{Project}_{\boldsymbol{lb},\boldsymbol{ub}}(\cdot)
is a projection function that constrains the continuous variables within their specified bounds, thereby ensuring that the gradient ascent updates respect the feasible region constraints while effectively guiding the continuous variables toward optimal values.

Guidance on Integer Variables
We employ a simple but effective sampling-based approach motivated by
(
tfg
)
to guide the discrete variables. This method uses a sampled batch of integer variables to estimate the expected target function value and derive an approximate guided rate matrix. This approximation method is proved to converge to the true rate matrix
(
tfg
)
. Specifically, we sample
R
integer variable assignments
\boldsymbol{d}_{1,r}
from the distribution
p_{\theta}\left(\boldsymbol{d}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{G}_{t}\right)
for
r=1,\dots,R
, and evaluate the target function
f(\boldsymbol{d}_{1,r},\boldsymbol{c}_{1|t})
for each sample, using the generated continuous values
\boldsymbol{c}_{1\mid t}
. Then we calculate the approximate guided rate matrix
\hat{R}
as follows:

\hat{R}(\boldsymbol{d}_{t}^{(i)},\cdot)\leftarrow\sum_{r=1}^{R}f(\boldsymbol{d}_{1\mid t,r},\boldsymbol{c}_{1\mid t})R_{t\mid 1}(\boldsymbol{d}_{t}^{(i)},\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{d}_{1\mid t,r}^{(i)})/\sum_{r=1}^{R}f(\boldsymbol{d}_{1\mid t,r},\boldsymbol{c}_{1\mid t}).

The discrete update rule described in Eq.
3
then utilizes this approximate rate matrix
\hat{R}
to determine the next state
\boldsymbol{d}_{t+\Delta t}
. After this update, we apply a projection step
\boldsymbol{d}_{t+\Delta t}=\text{Project}_{\boldsymbol{lb},\boldsymbol{ub}}(\boldsymbol{d}_{t+\Delta t})
, where
\text{Project}_{\boldsymbol{lb},\boldsymbol{ub}}(\cdot)
is a projection function to the valid integer values within the bounds
\boldsymbol{lb}
and
\boldsymbol{ub}
.

Experiment Settings

Benchmarks
We collect a comprehensive set of MILP benchmarks from previous representative work, containing seven MILP datasets. The first five of these are all CO problems containing only binary variables: Combinatorial Auctions (CA), Minimum Set Covering (SC), Maximal Independent Set (MIS), Generalized Independent Set (GIS)
(
colombi2017generalized
)
, and Fixed-Charge Multi-Commodity Network Flow (FCMNF)
(
hewitt2010combining
)
. SC, CA, and MIS are the most common benchmarks for evaluating ML+Solver methods, as adopted in
(
gasse2019exact
)
. GIS and FCMNF are from
(
khalil2022mip
)
and are harder than these three. The last two benchmarks, Item Placement (IP) and Load Balancing/Workload Appointment (LB), are the two most challenging datasets and have both binary and continuous variables, coming from real-world problem families used in the NeurIPS ML4CO 2021 competition
(
ml4co
)
.

Training and Inference
Training labels for FMIP and GNN were generated by solving the MILP instances using COPT
(
copt
)
, an outperforming MILP solver
(
DBLP:journals/mpc/GleixnerHGABBCJ21
)
, with a time limit of 3600 seconds per instance and 12 threads. The training loss for the GNN baseline is binary cross entropy for the prediction of binary variables, and the training loss for FMIP is described in Eq.
4
. Inference parameters were tuned on the validation set and are detailed in Appendix
C

Baselines
Previous approaches typically employ GNN-based predictive model to predict the distribution of optimal solutions
(
nair2020solving
;
han2023gnnguidedpredictandsearchframeworkmixedinteger
)
. This approach, referred to as GNN in the following sections, serves as one of the primary baselines in our study. We evaluate FMIP against four representative methods introduced earlier: Neural Diving (ND), Predict&Search (PS), PMVB (PM), and Apollo-MILP (Apollo), each incorporating different decoding strategies. Additionally, we include Gurobi
(
gurobi
)
as a strong classical baseline, given its state-of-the-art performance in solving mixed-integer linear programs (MILPs). For a fair comparison, both the GNN-based method and our FMIP utilize the same GCN architecture, which consists of 12 layers with residual connections. The effectiveness of this deeper structure and the inclusion of residual connections is demonstrated in our ablation study, as shown in Figure

Metrics
Following the definitions used in
(
han2023gnnguidedpredictandsearchframeworkmixedinteger
;
liu2025apollomilpalternatingpredictioncorrectionneural
)
, we define the best-known solution (BKS) as the best objective value obtained across all methods evaluated, including Gurobi. We then define the absolute primal gap
GAP
as the difference between the objective value found by a method (OBJ) and the BKS, which can be expressed as
\text{GAP}=|\text{OBJ}-\text{BKS}|
. Within the time limit, a lower absolute primal gap indicates stronger performance. Improvement is measured using the
GAP

\text{Imp}\;=\;\frac{\text{GAP}^{\text{GNN}}-\text{GAP}^{\text{FMIP}}}{\text{GAP}^{\text{GNN}}}\times 100\%,

(5)

where
\mathrm{GAP}^{\mathrm{GNN}}
and
\mathrm{GAP}^{\mathrm{FMIP}}
denote the absolute primal gap achieved by the GNN baseline and FMIP, respectively.

Downstream Solver Setting
We evaluate the methods with all selected decoding strategies on each test instance and record the best objective value (OBJ) within the same time limit. For ND, PS, and PMVB, we set the time limit to 600 seconds, and we set the time limit to 1000 seconds for Apollo, as it requires several iterations. For Gurobi, we set the time limit to 3600 seconds. All other parameters for these heuristic methods were tuned on the validation set and are detailed in Appendix
C
. For a fair comparison, we use the same parameters for the downstream solvers when evaluating both GNN and FMIP.

We provide three ablation studies that demonstrate the benefits of key designs in FMIP, including the multimodal flow matching, the guidance mechanism and the residual architecture in GCN.

Extra time cost brought by model inference
Due to the iterative nature of the Flow Matching for Integer Programs (FMIP) method, which requires multiple steps to converge to a final prediction, it inherently incurs a higher inference time compared to single-pass GNN evaluations. However, as evidenced by our results in Table

This minor overhead is often negligible within the broader context of the MILP solving phase, which can span hundreds or even thousands of seconds for complex instances. Therefore, the increased inference time of FMIP can be considered an acceptable trade-off for its potential performance benefits in MILP solving.

Tripartite Graph vs. Bipartite Graph

To verify the point that our tripartite structure has similar performance with bipartite structure, stated in Remark
4.1
, we conducted an ablation study comparing the tripartite graph with a standard bipartite graph formulation. In the bipartite setup, we utilized a GCN and applied different output layers for integer and continuous variable nodes. This design ensures that both approaches ultimately differentiate how they process information for different variable types, with the key distinction being whether this differentiation occurs at the graph structure or the output layer level.

As detailed in Table
5
, the results of our ablation study show marginal performance differences between the tripartite and bipartite graph formulations, with all metrics exhibiting less than a 1% difference. This confirms that the graph structure itself is not the primary driver of our method’s performance. Instead, our core contribution lies in the guided generative framework, for which the tripartite graph serves as an intuitive and effective representation.

7
Limitations and Future Work

Despite demonstrating superior performance to traditional Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) instances, our method still leverages GNNs as encoding modules. This presents a potential area for improvement, as GNNs inherently face limitations in fully capturing the complex, discrete, and combinatorial nature of MILP problems. A promising avenue for future work involves re-framing MILP instances into a sequence-based formulation and employing powerful sequence models (e.g., Transformers or advanced Recurrent Neural Networks) to encode their intricate information more effectively. This could potentially unlock a more comprehensive understanding of the problem structure, leading to enhanced solution strategies.

Another notable limitation of our current approach lies in the arbitrary selection of the initial point within the Flow Matching mechanism. The use of uniform random noise as an initialization often places the starting point far from the optimal solution. This substantial distance can significantly impede the learning process and reduce the efficiency of convergence. Future research will focus on exploring more sophisticated and strategically informed methods for selecting the initial point. Investigating techniques such as problem-specific heuristics, approximate solutions, or learned initialization strategies could substantially improve the efficacy and robustness of the Flow Matching mechanism, thereby accelerating the discovery of optimal or near-optimal solutions for MILP instances.

Appendix B
Graph Neural Networks

The input to our neural network is a tuple

(\boldsymbol{G}_{t},t),

where
\boldsymbol{G}_{t}
represents the solution graph as described in Section
4.1
. This graph encodes the relationships between variables and constraints, effectively capturing the structure of the MILP problem. The scalars
t
and
t
are temporal parameters associated with the integer and continuous variables, respectively, with both
t\in[0,1]
. These time parameters are introduced to enable a dynamic, progressive refinement of the solution space during the training process, aligning with the flow matching paradigm where time plays a critical role in guiding the evolution of the generated solution. We’ll details our neural networks structure in the following subsection.

B.1
TripartiteGCNConv Layer

We adopt the following mathematical notations for the following parts:
\text{Linear}_{\cdot}(\cdot)
denotes a learnable affine transformation (linear layer),
\text{LN}_{\cdot}(\cdot)
represents layer normalization, and
\text{GELU}(\cdot)
indicates Gaussian Error Linear Unit activation. The element-wise sigmoid function is written as
\sigma(\cdot)
, while
\odot
signifies element-wise multiplication. Vector concatenation is denoted by
[\cdot\ ;\ \cdot]
, and
\mathbb{I}_{\text{res}}\in\{0,1\}
serves as an indicator variable for residual connection usage.

The output feature
h_{v}^{\prime}
of a target node
v
is computed as:

h_{v}^{\prime}=\text{LN}_{\text{out}}\left(\text{Linear}_{o2}\left(\text{GELU}\left(\text{Linear}_{o1}\left([\text{LN}_{\text{PC}}(g_{v}\odot a_{1}+(1-g_{v})\odot a_{2})\ ;\ h_{v}]\right)\right)\right)\right)+\mathbb{I}_{\text{res}}\cdot h_{v},

where the aggregated messages from source node types
k=1,2
are defined as:

a_{k}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(v)}\text{Linear}_{\text{final}}\left(\text{GELU}\left(\text{LN}_{\text{PN}}\left(\text{Linear}_{t}(h_{v})+\text{Linear}_{s_{k}}(h_{u}^{(k)})+\text{Linear}_{e}(e_{uv}^{(k)})\right)\right)\right),

and the gating vector is computed by:

g_{v}=\sigma\left(\text{Linear}_{fg}([a_{1};a_{2}])\right)
.

B.2
Model Structure

Overview
We propose a graph-based neural architecture tailored for mixed-variable optimization problems. The model captures the interactions among integer variables, continuous variables, and constraints using iterative message passing with temporal conditioning.

Initial Embeddings
We define three types of nodes:

•

Integer variables (
\mathcal{V}_{\text{Ivar}}
):
n_{\text{Ivar}}
nodes, with features
\mathbf{X}_{\text{Ivar}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{Ivar}}\times d_{\text{Ivar}}}

•

Continuous variables (
\mathcal{V}_{\text{Cvar}}
):
n_{\text{Cvar}}
nodes, with features
\mathbf{X}_{\text{Cvar}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{Cvar}}\times d_{\text{Cvar}}}

•

Constraints (
\mathcal{C}_{\text{con}}
):
n_{\text{con}}
nodes, with features
\mathbf{X}_{\text{con}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{con}}\times d_{\text{con}}}

Initial node embeddings are computed via type-specific MLPs:

\mathbf{h}_{\text{Ivar}}^{(0)}=\text{MLP}_{\text{Ivar}}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{Ivar}}),\quad\mathbf{h}_{\text{Cvar}}^{(0)}=\text{MLP}_{\text{Cvar}}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{Cvar}}),\quad\mathbf{h}_{\text{con}}^{(0)}=\text{MLP}_{\text{con}}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{con}}),

where each embedding lies in
\mathbb{R}^{\cdot\times h}
with
h
denoting the shared hidden dimension.

The time embedding
\mathbf{e}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
is initialized using positional encoding
[
81
]
. This embedding is broadcasted and added to the variable node features at every iteration.

Message Passing Layers
For each layer
\ell=1,\dots,L
, we update the node representations via a message passing scheme over a tripartite graph. The relevant edge sets are defined as:

•

\mathcal{E}_{\text{Ivar2con}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{\text{Ivar}}\times\mathcal{C}_{\text{con}}
: integer-to-constraint edges

•

\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cvar2con}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{\text{Cvar}}\times\mathcal{C}_{\text{con}}
: continuous-to-constraint edges

•

Reverse edges
\mathcal{E}_{\text{con2Ivar}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{con2Cvar}}
: constraint-to-variable edges for backward message flow

The node updates at layer
\ell
are:

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\text{MLP}_{t}^{(\ell)}\big(\mathbf{e}_{t}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{\text{con}}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{h}_{\text{con}}^{(\ell-1)}+\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell)}+\text{MLP}_{\text{con}}^{(\ell)}\big(\text{TriConv}\big(\mathbf{h}_{\text{Ivar}}^{(\ell-1)},\mathbf{h}_{\text{Cvar}}^{(\ell-1)},\mathbf{h}_{\text{con}}^{(\ell-1)},\mathcal{E}_{\text{Ivar2con}},\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cvar2con}}\big)\big),

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{\ast}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{h}_{\ast}^{(\ell-1)}+\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell)}+\text{MLP}_{\ast}^{(\ell)}\big(\text{BiConv}_{\ast}(\mathbf{h}_{\text{con}}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{h}_{\ast}^{(\ell-1)},\mathcal{E}_{\text{con2}\ast})\big),\quad\ast\in\{\text{Ivar},\text{Cvar}\}.

TriConv
is a tripartite message aggregation module that aggregates signals from both integer and continuous variable nodes into constraint nodes.
BiConv
has the same architecture as
TriConv
, but applies bipartite message passing from constraints back to one variable type.

Output Layer
After
L
layers of message passing, we apply type-specific MLP heads to predict outputs from the final node embeddings:

\mathbf{o}_{\text{Ivar}}=\text{MLP}_{\text{out}}^{\text{Ivar}}(\mathbf{h}_{\text{Ivar}}^{(L)}),\quad\mathbf{o}_{\text{Cvar}}=\text{MLP}_{\text{out}}^{\text{Cvar}}(\mathbf{h}_{\text{Cvar}}^{(L)}),

where
\mathbf{o}_{\text{Ivar}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{Ivar}}\times d_{\text{out}}^{\text{Ivar}}}
and
\mathbf{o}_{\text{Cvar}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{Cvar}}\times d_{\text{out}}^{\text{Cvar}}}
are the prediction outputs for integer and continuous variables, respectively.

Appendix D
Downstream Solver Algorithm

In this section, we introduction detailed algorithms employed in our experiments, including Neural Diving, Predict&Search, PMVB and Apollo-MILP.

D.1
Neural Diving

For a new instance, the neural network first generates multiple partial variable assignments based on predicted probability distributions. Let
\mathcal{V}
denote the set of variables. The algorithm fixes a subset
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{V}
where:

|\mathcal{S}|=\alpha\cdot|\mathcal{V}|\quad(\alpha\in(0,1))

and generates
K
parallel sub-MIPs through
\{\mathcal{S}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
assignments. Through a selective prediction mechanism, it decides which variables to fix and samples their specific values, forming various assignment combinations that only involve subsets of variables. Subsequently, each partial assignment is transformed into a smaller sub-MIP problem by fixing these assigned variables. These sub-MIP problems are then solved in parallel using traditional solvers to generate multiple complete feasible solutions. Finally, the solution with the optimal objective function value is selected as the final result. This process combines the predictive capabilities of neural networks with the efficiency of traditional solvers, achieving an end-to-end workflow from partial assignments to optimal solutions.

D.2
Predict&Search

In the predict phase, a graph neural network estimates binary variable assignments
\hat{x}_{j}\in\{0,1\}
. During the search phase, the algorithm constructs a trust region based on the predictions, restricting the original problem to a feasible solution space within the neighborhood of the predicted solutions. The trust region is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}=\{x_{j}\mid\hat{x}_{j}\leq k_{0}\}\quad\text{(variables near 0)}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}=\{x_{j}\mid\hat{x}_{j}\geq 1-k_{1}\}\quad\text{(variables near 1)}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}_{0}}\mathbb{I}(x_{j}^{*}=1)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}_{1}}\mathbb{I}(x_{j}^{*}=0)\leq\Delta\cdot(|\mathcal{T}_{0}|+|\mathcal{T}_{1}|)

where
k_{0},k_{1}\in(0,1)
control selection thresholds and
\delta
defines permissible deviation. Specifically, by adding constraints (e.g., limiting the number of variables with scores close to 0 or 1 and controlling deviations from predicted values), the problem is transformed into a smaller subproblem, which is then solved using traditional solvers to efficiently search for high-quality feasible solutions. Compared to methods that directly fix variables, the trust region strategy retains flexibility to avoid infeasibility while improving solution quality through localized search.

D.3
PMVB

The PMVB (Probabilistic Multi-Variable Cardinality Branching) method leverages predictions from a graph neural network to construct statistically grounded branching constraints. Specifically, the binary variables are partitioned into two disjoint sets based on their predicted probabilities:

\mathcal{U}=\{j:\hat{y}_{j}\geq\tau\},\quad\mathcal{L}=\{j:\hat{y}_{j}\leq 1-\tau\}

where
\tau\in(0.5,1]
is a confidence threshold indicating how certain the model is about a variable being 1 or 0, respectively. Using the principles of risk pooling and concentration inequalities, the algorithm constructs two soft constraints that serve as branching hyperplanes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{U}}:\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}}y_{j}\geq\left\lceil(1-\delta)\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}[\hat{y}_{j}]-\gamma\right\rceil,\quad\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{L}}:\sum_{j\in\mathcal{L}}y_{j}\leq\left\lfloor\delta\sum_{j\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}[\hat{y}_{j}]+\gamma\right\rfloor

where
\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\ln(2/\delta)}{2}}
for
\mathcal{S}\in\{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{L}\}
, and
\delta
is a tunable confidence parameter.

These hyperplanes probabilistically constrain the variable assignments, forming a high-confidence trust region that filters out unlikely configurations while preserving feasible and high-quality candidates. By intersecting the feasible region with both
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{U}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{L}}
, the algorithm defines a subproblem
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{U}},\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{L}}}
that is both reduced in size and refined in quality.

This subproblem is then passed to a traditional MILP solver for efficient resolution. Compared to methods that directly fix variables, PMVB offers greater robustness by retaining feasible flexibility while introducing statistically grounded directional guidance. As a result, it effectively balances confidence-driven pruning with solution diversity, improving both computational efficiency and solution quality in challenging problem instances.

D.4
Apollo-MILP

The algorithm begins by employing a graph neural network to predict values for currently unfixed variables, yielding a predicted solution. Subsequently, the Predict&Search method is applied to solve subproblems derived from this prediction, generating a reference solution. By comparing the predicted and reference solutions, variables with identical values in both solutions are fixed, thereby constructing a reduced problem. At each iteration
t\in\{1,...,K\}
, the algorithm applies parameters
[k_{0}^{(t)},k_{1}^{(t)},\Delta^{(t)}]
in Predict&Search algorithm,
where
K
controls total iterations. This process iteratively repeats, progressively identifying high-quality partial solutions and expanding the subset of fixed variables to reduce problem dimensionality.

Throughout the iterations, Apollo-MILP alternates between prediction and correction steps, continuously refining the predicted solution and reducing the complexity of the MILP problem. This approach ensures optimality while significantly enhancing both solution quality and computational efficiency.
