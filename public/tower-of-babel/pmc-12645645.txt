Screening programmes for early detection of chronic kidney disease in the USA and other English-speaking countries (Canada, Australia and UK): a systematic literature review

Published: 2025

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a growing health concern, affecting approximately 10% of the global population.1 In the USA, more than one in seven adults is estimated to have CKD. Early diagnosis is important since CKD is usually a progressive condition that is associated with an increased risk of morbidity, and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality.2 However, up to 9 in 10 adults with CKD are unaware of their condition,3 indicating the need for increased efforts to identify affected individuals. An inverse association has been identified between estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) levels and the risk of death from cardiovascular disease (CVD), especially due to heart failure and valvular disease, as well as deaths from infections and other causes.2

Clinical practice guidelines from Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) define CKD as the presence of abnormalities of kidney structure or function for at least 3 months, with associated adverse health outcomes.4 The diagnosis of CKD is indicated by either a reduction in glomerular filtration rate to <60 mL/min per 1.73 m2, and/or albuminuria (albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) ≥30 mg/g or ≥3 mg/mmol). The most common underlying cause of CKD is diabetes (type 2 and type 1), accounting for 30–50% of cases in the USA and worldwide.3 5 Hypertension is the second most common cause of CKD. Additional risk factors for CKD include CVD, obesity, smoking, a family history of CKD, inherited kidney disorders, previous kidney damage, advanced age, adverse pregnancy outcomes, autoimmune disease and environmental risk factors such as dietary salt intake and pollution.36,8 Furthermore, the risk of CKD is slightly increased in women and certain racial groups (non-Hispanic black people).3 6

Early detection of CKD provides an important opportunity for intervention aimed at delaying progression and other adverse outcomes. The identification of early-stage kidney disease involves two primary strategies: (1) early detection programmes, which aim to reduce the number of patients diagnosed with CKD at an advanced stage and (2) screening, that is, the application of tests within a general or at-risk population to pinpoint individuals with a disease who may not yet exhibit symptoms.9 10 However, at present, there is no consensus on the optimal approach to screening for CKD.11 A recent scoping review provided a global overview of early CKD screening programmes in the general population and identified wide variations in the methods used to identify CKD worldwide in addition to an overall lack of policies to guide the early identification of people with CKD.10 Despite the lack of clinical consensus, recent systematic literature reviews (SLRs) found that screening for CKD in patients with diabetes or hypertension is cost-effective in all groups including ethnic groups identified to be at high risk.12 13 Annual screening has also been shown to be cost-effective for individuals with hypertension aged 30–70 years.14

The KDIGO guidelines advise screening of high-risk groups, including individuals with diabetes, hypertension and CVD. The guidelines recommend that assessment should include evaluation of urinary ACR (UACR) and eGFR and emphasise that both assessments are required for determination of CKD severity and risk of progression and development of complications.4 The timing of repeat testing should be based on patient characteristics such as the risk of CKD progression. The KDIGO guidelines also recommend that screening and preventive interventions for CKD should not be restricted to nephrologists but should largely be performed in primary care settings, in addition to specialist settings such as endocrinology, cardiology and oncology. The American Society of Nephrology goes further than KDIGO and strongly recommends regular screening for CKD, regardless of the presence of risk factors. In contrast, the American College of Physicians advises against screening for CKD in asymptomatic adults without risk factors.15 For people with diabetes, the latest guidelines from the American Diabetes Association advise screening for CKD at least annually in all people with type 2 diabetes and in those with type 1 diabetes with a duration of at least 5 years.16 In the presence of concurrent CKD, monitoring of UACR and eGFR should be performed one to four times annually, with the frequency depending on the CKD stage.

In the USA, the availability and types of screening programmes that are being currently offered to high-risk populations are not well documented. The aim of this SLR was to assess the current literature on early CKD screening programmes in the USA that target high-risk patients, such as those with diabetes or hypertension, and high-risk racial or ethnic groups. The main focus was on CKD screening practices in the USA; CKD screening programmes in Canada, the UK and Australia were also included. This SLR also examined the prevalence of CKD among the screened populations, in cases where screening programmes were not described.

Methods

This SLR was conducted using a standardised methodical, thorough and transparent approach following guidance presented in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.17 The protocol followed the PRISMA Protocol guidelines18 and defined all processes and methodologies used to conduct this SLR. This SLR was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews to avoid duplication and reduce potential reporting bias (registration number: CRD42023492433).

Inclusion criteria

The SLR methodology involved a rigorous and objective search and selection process following prespecified inclusion/exclusion criteria. Relevant evidence was identified to meet the review objectives and prespecified population, intervention, comparator, outcome and study design types eligibility criteria. Eligibility criteria are summarised in table 1. The main target was the USA; studies from Canada, the UK and Australia were also included, and studies from Western European countries were considered if there were insufficient data from the target countries. Studies published in English from 1 January 2018 to 17 October 2023 were eligible for inclusion. There were no restrictions on participant age.

Searches, screening and data extraction

Systematic searches were conducted in Embase and MEDLINE (both via Ovid) using a prespecified search strategy. A detailed search strategy, including the number of articles, is provided in supplementary materials (online supplemental tables S1 and S2), and a summary is provided in table 2. Deduplication of records from the database searches was conducted in EndNote. Searches were developed by one reviewer and checked by a second reviewer. One reviewer screened the references at title/abstract, and a second reviewer checked 20% of the references. Full-text screening was conducted in the same way. Disagreements were adjudicated by a third senior researcher. Data extraction was conducted by one reviewer, with a second reviewer validating every data point for accuracy. Quality assessment was conducted by a single reviewer and checked by a second reviewer. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. Reasons for exclusion were provided and cross-checked between the two reviewers, with a third senior reviewer consulted for discrepancies (online supplemental table S3). Full-text articles were obtained for studies that met the criteria for inclusion. A single reviewer re-screened the full texts of publications selected against the same inclusion and exclusion criteria. Reference lists from relevant systematic reviews and papers selected for inclusion in the SLR were also screened by a single researcher to identify any additional relevant publications. Full-text publications (where outcome data were available) were assessed for quality. Study protocols and congress abstracts were not quality assessed. For randomised controlled trials (RCTs), quality/risk of bias (ROB) was assessed using version 2 of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials (online supplemental tables S4–6).19 For observational longitudinal or prospective studies and non-randomised trials, quality/ROB was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.20

Weighted mean prevalences of CKD testing were calculated for the pooled population in the included studies. Population weights were calculated by dividing the sample size of each study by the sum of patients across all studies. Population weights for each study were multiplied by the prevalence of screening in each study, and then the weighted prevalence across all studies was summed. This approach takes account of different population sizes to enable data to be compared between different studies. The rate of screening was reported as a percentage.

Results

In total, 5542 records were identified from the database searches. A summary of studies included at each stage of the screening process and reasons for exclusion are shown in the PRISMA diagram (figure 1). A total of 23 publications were included in this review, representing 21 unique studies. Details of the studies, including the tests used for diagnosis of CKD, are presented in online supplemental table S7. Of the 23 included publications, 14 reported on US screening programmes, five publications21,25 were from Canada, three publications were from Australia,26,28 and one publication29 was from the Netherlands. No screening programmes were found in the UK or the rest of Western Europe.

Patient characteristics

A pooled total of 30 643 162 participants was reported in these studies. The median proportion of male participants in the included studies was 44.5% (range 47.4%30–100%31). The mean age of participants ranged from 12 years22 to 68 years.31 The presence of cardiac diseases was reported in nine studies2527 28 31,36 and ranged from 12%25 to 45%.32 Further details of patient characteristics are shown in online supplemental table S8.

Study designs

The majority of included studies were observational (17 studies, 19 publications). Of these, nine were retrospective,2527 32,34 37 seven were cross-sectional22 24 28 30 36 41 42 and three were prospective in design.21 23 26 Two cross-sectional publications22 24 and one prospective observational study23 were linked to the same FINISHED study conducted in an indigenous population in Canada. The remaining four studies were RCTs.29 31 35 43 A total of 13 studies2528 30,35 37 39 40 42 43 reported CKD screening in high-risk populations with diabetes or hypertension, with five studies28 30 34 39 40 reporting exclusively in populations with diabetes and three studies31 35 42 in populations with hypertension. Four out of 21 studies21,2426 27 were conducted in indigenous populations, three studies36 38 41 were conducted in underserved areas (defined as areas with limited access to primary healthcare services) and one study29 was conducted in older patients (45–80 years of age).

Early screening programmes and interventions

Of the 21 included studies, 16 reported early CKD screening programmes in high-risk populations. Of these, 10 studies were conducted in the USA,3031 33 35 36 38,41 43 of which seven included patients with diabetes and/or hypertension and three were in underserved communities.36 38 41 Three studies reported screening programmes in Canada21,25 and two in Australia.26 27 A further study was identified from Western Europe, which reported a screening programme in the Netherlands.29 Eight of the 16 early screening programmes were delivered in community outreach programmes, and the remaining eight were conducted in primary care. Of the studies that reported the testing procedure, four studies27 30 33 40 reported testing in 1 year and one study each reported testing frequency of 6 months25 and 3 months,35 respectively. Review of the screening programmes identified in the SLR indicated several clinical practice approaches that could be considered for incorporation into future CKD screening initiatives. A descriptive summary of practices is shown in online supplemental table S9.

Criteria for CKD diagnosis

Of the 21 studies included, CKD identification with both UACR and eGFR testing was used in 14 studies (47.6%).21,2426 Seven (33.3%) studies used a single test (UACR or eGFR).2535 36 38,40 42 Serum creatinine was frequently used in the calculation of eGFR. Rate of CKD screening in high-risk populations

The prevalence of CKD screening in the pooled population in the included studies (n=24 930 715) ranged from 8.6%23 to 100%.36 The weighted mean prevalences of CKD screening in patients with diabetes or hypertension were 33% and 12%, respectively (figure 3A). For the pooled population of 24 608 Indigenous persons or underserved communities, the weighted mean prevalence of CKD screening was 91%. The weighted mean prevalences for any type of CKD screening were markedly different in primary care and community outreach settings: 22% (n=30 705 837) and 93% (n=26 640), respectively (figure 3B).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first SLR to evaluate the types of early CKD screening programmes for patients with diabetes and/or hypertension from an international perspective, including the USA, Canada, Australia and the Netherlands. The findings of this SLR suggest a low prevalence of screening of high-risk patients with diabetes and hypertension, particularly in primary care. The weighted mean prevalences of CKD screening in high-risk patients with diabetes or hypertension were alarmingly low at 33% and 12%, respectively. Furthermore, the rates of complete screening in these patients are even lower than these figures suggest given that less than half of the screened individuals (47.6%) received both UACR and eGFR testing to identify the presence of CKD. When participants were pooled across the 21 included studies, only a minority (weighted mean 21%) received guideline-recommended4 16 screening that included both ACR and eGFR testing. In addition, approximately one-third of studies showed incomplete screening for CKD, with the use of only one test. Accordingly, inclusion of follow-up testing was infrequent or not reported in most of the studies reviewed. Together, these findings indicate a marked lack of uptake of practice recommendations for CKD screening, and where screening is offered, there is not a consistent approach to screening. Although the KDIGO guidelines advise that prevention and screening for CKD should largely be conducted in primary care,4 11 the findings of this SLR indicate substantial underuse of primary care facilities for the early detection of CKD. Further evidence of insufficient screening for CKD has been shown in a recent study of electronic health record data across 33 healthcare systems in the USA.44 This study evaluated the rates of follow-up testing in more than 1 million individuals who had received a urine dipstick test in 2021. Among the ~138 306 patients (13%) who received abnormal results, only 6.7% received follow-up testing with ACR or protein-creatinine ratio during the following year. Follow-up testing was slightly higher, although still suboptimal, among patients with diabetes (16.6%) than for those without diabetes (3.8%).

In view of the availability of evidence-based interventions to slow the development of CKD and reduce the occurrence of complications, measures aimed at improving screening practices have the potential to significantly improve patient care and delay the progression of CKD in these high-risk populations.11 In the present SLR, the high rates of screening observed in community settings largely reflect the targeting of indigenous populations21,2426 27 or underserved areas36 38 41 by outreach programmes in Canada and Australia. The approaches used in these programmes can provide valuable insights for the future development of successful screening programmes. The community-based screening programmes appeared to be successful in reaching at-risk individuals by bringing screening programmes to the communities, where there are often challenges in obtaining routine healthcare. These included community outreach programmes in the FINISHED programme and the Kidney Check Programme, which focused on indigenous communities in rural Manitoba, Canada; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)-hosted community screening programmes in the USA; Kidney Mentoring and Assessment Program for Students in Atlanta, the Kidney Disease Screening and Awareness Programme in the USA; and National Kidney Foundation of Illinois KidneyMobile screenings.33 36 38 41 One Canadian study used community pharmacies as a point-of-care screening programme for patients with at least one CKD risk factor (hypertension, CVD, diabetes, family history of kidney disease, age >55 years or high-risk ethnicity).25 The prevalence of CKD in the population was only reported in 11 of the 21 studies, therefore, it is difficult to draw conclusions from their findings. The wide range of reported prevalences in these studies is likely to reflect differences in the populations studied and differences in the screening tests used and the diagnostic criteria for CKD. A previous scoping review of global CKD screening and detection programmes also reported wide variations in the reported CKD prevalence due to differences in methods used to identify CKD.10

Implications for practice

Since there remains a need to improve the early identification of CKD screening, future CKD screening initiatives could benefit from the incorporation of successful elements of the screening programmes identified in the present SLR. Measures that could be considered include the use of targeted education and awareness programmes for healthcare professionals to increase the use of eGFR and UACR testing in high-risk individuals, in addition to improving rates of follow-up assessment.4 16 In addition, the efficiency of primary care-based screening programmes could be enhanced by using electronic medical records to identify high-risk patients with diabetes and/or hypertension, and linking automated CKD screening test requests. Where appropriate, offering home-based screening could improve access for patients who have difficulties in accessing existing healthcare provision. In addition, access to CKD screening, treatments and educational initiatives could be further improved by establishing outreach programmes at accessible locations, such as pharmacies and community centres. Implementation of some or all of these initiatives has the potential to increase the early detection of CKD, increase access to appropriate management, ensure timely referrals for specialist assessment and ultimately improve health outcomes for high-risk individuals.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this SLR review is the use of a standardised, comprehensive and transparent approach to identify and quality-appraise included studies. In addition, this SLR was conducted according to the PRISMA guidelines and included a thorough and comprehensive literature search to identify relevant studies, and two independent researchers completed the study selection. This SLR also has some limitations. First, while Embase and MEDLINE are extensive databases, it is possible that some relevant publications were not indexed in these sources and were omitted. Restricting the search period to 5 years could also have resulted in relevant articles being missed. Second, there was heterogeneity in the approaches to screening in the included studies, including the design and delivery of interventions, CKD identification criteria and outcomes assessed. It was not possible to evaluate these differences; for example, there was insufficient information on the use of different eGFR equations in the diagnosis of CKD across the included studies. Third, the sample was limited to studies published within a defined 5-year period and only included English-language studies indexed in the MEDLINE and Embase databases. A further limitation is that the findings from the reported CKD screening programmes cannot be generalised to all primary care practices or community settings, due to specific settings, limited sample sizes, short follow-up periods and potential selection bias. Nonetheless, it may be possible to identify best practices based on experiences reported in the identified studies that could be applied to other settings.

Conclusions

The findings of this SLR indicate a lack of a consistent approach to screening in the USA (and other included countries), and an alarmingly low prevalence of CKD screening of high-risk patients, which was particularly marked in primary care. Contrary to KDIGO guidelines, the majority of high-risk patients studied received incomplete screening. Lack of adherence to KDIGO guidelines on CKD screening may result in delays in CKD diagnosis and missed opportunities for therapy. Initiatives aimed at improving the early detection of CKD and ensuring timely access to appropriate management are urgently needed to improve health outcomes for individuals at risk from the development and progression of CKD.
