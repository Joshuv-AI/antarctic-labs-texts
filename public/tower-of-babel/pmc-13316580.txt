Drosophila jump muscle myofibrils: A new tool for investigating activation and relaxation
Authors: Axel Fenwick, Bernadette M. Glasheen, Anthony Cammarato, Douglas M. Swank
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drosophila models have proven invaluable for studying skeletal and cardiac muscle diseases. While permeabilized indirect flight muscle (IFM) and jump muscle fibers from Drosophila yield insightful mechanical data, these preparations cannot resolve the kinetics of activation and relaxation because calcium diffusion into the fiber core is rate limited at this scale. In contrast, myofibrils have a diameter of only 1–3 μm, making them ideal for measuring physiologically relevant activation and relaxation rates. However, previous attempts using IFMs failed to produce myofibrils that generated measurable active force, likely due to the muscle’s inherently low force output. Therefore, instead of using IFMs as the source, we developed a method to isolate myofibrils from the Drosophila jump muscle. By applying brief, low-amplitude sonication to permeabilized jump muscles, we isolated myofibrils that produced 19.8 ± 10.5 mN/mm2 net active tension, the first active force measurements from an insect myofibril, with an activation rate of 8.2 ± 4.0 s-1. Jump muscle myofibrils exhibited the typical biphasic relaxation seen in vertebrates: an initial slow, linear phase lasting 75.6 ± 21.1 ms, followed by a fast exponential decay with a rate constant of 19.7 ± 9.6 s−1. We also characterized myofibrils from jump muscles transgenically expressing a Drosophila larval body wall myosin isoform (EMB), which we previously reported displays slower actin-binding and detachment kinetics. EMB expression caused a 1.6-fold increase in active tension and a 38% slower activation rate compared with controls but did not change relaxation parameters. These characterizations and comparisons highlight the scientific value of expressing customizable sarcomeric proteins in transgenic Drosophila jump muscle myofibrils to elucidate their contributions to muscle activation and relaxation.

Significance

For the first time, to our knowledge, mechanically testable myofibrils have been prepared from Drosophila. Previous attempts to isolate myofibrils that could be activated from Drosophila indirect flight muscle (IFM) have been unsuccessful likely because of IFM’s intrinsically low force producing capabilities. The jump muscle myofibril preparation now enables us to bring the full power of the Drosophila genetic toolkit to discover fundamental mechanisms and physiological properties and to answer disease state questions pertaining to sarcomere activation and relaxation.

Introduction

Drosophila is an excellent model organism for investigating sarcomeric protein structure-function relationships, muscle and heart diseases, and the mechanical and physiological properties of muscle. The Drosophila experimental toolkit includes one of the widest arrays of genetic techniques of any model organism, and the mechanisms by which many muscle contractile proteins are expressed make Drosophila ideal for studying the role of specific proteins in muscle function. For example, Drosophila myosin heavy chain (MHC) is encoded by a single gene (Mhc) that produces multiple myosin isoforms through alternative splicing of five exons and the inclusion or exclusion of exon 181,2,3 (Figure 1). The development of Drosophila membrane permeabilized fiber preparations, from IFM and jump muscle, coupled with these genetic advantages, has provided key insights into fundamental muscle mechanics.5,6 For instance, Drosophila fibers have been used to study the molecular basis of the classic muscle force-velocity curve7 and to model muscle and heart diseases such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.8,9,10,11 Permeabilized fibers allow precise control of calcium concentration and enable a variety of mechanical measurements. However, they have limitations. Activation and relaxation rates in fiber bundles are limited by the slow diffusion of calcium into and out of the preparation,12 likely masking other kinetic steps in these processes. Another consideration is that permeabilized fibers require high levels of MgATP and an MgATP-regeneration system to prevent development of a rigor core, especially in fast fiber types.13 Intact (live) muscle preparations would avoid some of these disadvantages, but no viable intact Drosophila muscle preparation has been developed. This is largely due to anatomical constraints that make it difficult to mount the fiber on a muscle mechanics apparatus without damaging the muscle membrane (IFMs lack tendons entirely, and the jump muscles have only a single tendon on the ventral end).

An alternative approach to overcome these limitations in Drosophila muscle is to isolate single myofibrils. Single myofibrils have a small cross-sectional area (1–3 μm2), allowing Ca2+ to move rapidly throughout the myofibril so that sarcomere activation and relaxation kinetics can be resolved independent of diffusion. Despite these advantages, all previous attempts to prepare Drosophila myofibrils have achieved limited success. Researchers have isolated IFM myofibrils and measured passive tension,14,15 but to our knowledge, no one has reported active force generation from IFM myofibrils. This may be due to the inherently low isometric tension generation of Drosophila IFM, ∼1.5 mN/mm2.5,16 The extremely high contraction frequency of Drosophila IFM, ∼200 Hz at 20°C,17 necessitates lower force production.13 We reasoned that isolating myofibrils from the jump muscle might prove more fruitful, since jump muscle fibers produce much higher tension, about 40 mN/mm2.6 After attempting various preparation methods, we successfully isolated single myofibrils from permeabilized Drosophila jump muscle that could be fully activated. Similar to vertebrate myofibrils, switching from relaxing to activating concentrations of Ca2+ (pCa 8 to pCa 4) produced an isometric contraction with a rapid, single-exponential rise to a steady force plateau. Subsequent relaxation (upon Ca2+ removal) was found to occur in two phases: an initial slow, almost linear decline in force followed by a fast mono-exponential decay.

As an initial demonstration of the jump muscle myofibril system’s future utility, we measured the force-generating properties of myofibrils that transgenically express either the native jump muscle myosin isoform (pWMhc2)4 or a slower myosin found in embryonic and larval body wall muscles (EMB).18 In Drosophila, these two isoforms differ only at four regions in the S-1 head (encoded by alternative exons 3, 7, 9, and 11; see Figure 1). Prior biochemical and mechanical studies have shown that these isoforms have different cross-bridge kinetics. From these studies comparing the EMB, IFM, and jump muscle myosin isoforms, we estimate that EMB’s detachment rate from actin is ∼10-fold slower than that of the native jump muscle myosin, while its attachment (binding) rate is only ∼30% slower than that of the jump muscle isoform. These estimates are derived from ATPase, motility, and optical trapping assays comparing the EMB, jump, and IFM myosin isoforms,19,20,21 which provided us with relative duty ratios and on-times for myosin isoforms binding to actin. IFM sinusoidal assays, combined with a six-state cross-bridge model, when expressing the EMB, IFM, and jump muscle myosin isoforms,22 provided us with apparent attachment (2πb) and detachment rates (2πc). Mechanics from jump muscles expressing the EMB myosin isoform also supported these relative attachment and detachment rates.6

Armed with a detailed knowledge of these two isoforms, we were intrigued to see if the differences at the cross-bridge level would manifest changes in myofibril force-generating properties, especially given the renewed inquisition into modulators of activation and relaxation mechanisms, such as the involvement of different myosin structural states.23,24,25 We found that EMB increased active tension 1.6-fold and slowed activation rate by 38% compared with the native jump muscle myosin, but that it did not change relaxation parameters. These results using Drosophila genetic techniques combined with our new myofibril model highlight the great potential for novel insights into fundamental muscle properties, basic structure-function relationships of muscle proteins, and determining disease mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Fly lines

We employed three Drosophila lines: w1118, pWMhc2, and EMB. w1118 (bearing the white eye mutation), which was used as a wild-type control because it provides the common genetic background for many of our transgenic lines. pWMhc2 is a transgenic control line that expresses a genomic Mhc transgene in the Mhc10 background, allowing for a transgenic copy of the jump muscle MHC isoform to be expressed in the jump muscles of these flies.4 The EMB flies contain a transgenic copy of the myosin isoform normally expressed in embryonic and larval body wall muscles in the Mhc10 background.18 Therefore, the jump muscle’s native MHC is replaced with the EMB isoform. The expression of these specific myosin isoforms in these fly lines has been previously verified using rtPCR and protein gels.5,18,21 Flies were raised at room temperature (∼21°C) on standard cornmeal molasses Drosophila food.

Jump muscle preparation

Jump muscles were isolated from 2- to 3-day-old female flies of each genotype (w1118, pWMhc2, and EMB). Muscle dissection and chemical permeabilization followed a published protocol5,6 and are illustrated in Figure 2A. Briefly, an isolated thorax was placed into ice-cold dissection solution (pCa 8.0) containing 5 mM EGTA, 12 mM MgATP, 1 mM free Mg2+, 0.25 mM inorganic phosphate, 20 mM N,N-bis(2-hydroxyethyl)-2-aminoethanesulfonic acid (BES, pH 7.0), 30 mM creatine phosphate, 300 U/mL creatine phosphokinase, 200 mM ionic strength-adjusted with sodium methane sulfonate (pH 7.0), 1 mM DTT, 50% glycerol, and 0.5% Triton X-100. The thorax was cut in half longitudinally, the middle leg was removed, and the top of the thorax (dorsal cuticle) was trimmed away to free the jump muscle. The dissected muscles were left in skinning solution for 1 h and then transferred to storage solution (identical to dissection solution minus Triton X-100) and stored at −20°C until used for myofibril isolation (Figure 2B).Figure 2Drosophila myofibril isolation. Myofibril mechanics

Myofibrils were isolated from stored muscles as described in the results (see “myofibril isolation” below). For mechanical experiments, 5–10 μL of the myofibril suspension was added to a 500-μL bubble of relaxing solution (pCa 8; 0.115 mM CaCl2, 10 mM EGTA, 5 mM MgATP, 1 mM free Mg2+, 100 mM BES, 10 mM creatine phosphate, 175 mM ionic strength-adjusted with potassium propionate, 10 mM DTT, protease inhibitors [cOmplete-EDTA free, Roche], pH 7.0) in the 17°C temperature-controlled chamber of the myofibril apparatus. The preparation was left undisturbed for at least 5 min to allow myofibrils to settle onto the chamber bottom. We then gently replaced the solution bubble with fresh relaxing solution to remove excess debris. Individual myofibrils were mounted between a piezoelectric length controller and a cantilever force probe (0.031 N/m) and stretched to a sarcomere length of 3.6 μm. The diameter of the myofibril was measured at three points along its length at 64× magnification. The average diameter was used to estimate cross-sectional area by assuming a circular cross-section. Rapid solution changes were achieved by using a fast step motor (Warner Instruments) to move between relaxing and activation solution (same as relaxing, but with 10.31 mM CaCl2, pCa 4.0) continuously emanating from a double-barreled pipette. Data were collected and analyzed with custom LabView software (National Instruments).

Active force was calculated as the difference between the baseline tension in relaxing solution and the peak tension in activating solution just prior to the onset of relaxation. Tension values were obtained by normalizing force to the myofibril’s cross-sectional area. The rate constant of tension development (kACT) was determined by fitting the force rise during activation to a single-exponential function (Levenberg-Marquardt nonlinear least squares). The fast phase of relaxation was analyzed similarly: the final, rapid exponential force decay was fit to obtain a rate constant kREL. The duration of the initial slow relaxation phase (tLIN) was measured from the start of solution exchange (digital trigger signal from the switch to relaxing solution) to the onset of the fast exponential drop in force, detected with an automated linear-fit residual algorithm. The rate of force decline during this slow relaxation phase (kLIN) was calculated as the slope of a linear fit to the force trace during tLIN, normalized to the maximum tension.

Results

Myofibril isolation

We explored several methods to isolate single myofibrils from jump muscle. Our initial approach followed a protocol used for vertebrate myofibrils26 that applied mechanical homogenization for 10 seconds at 7,500 RPM (Omni PDH 700D) to jump muscles suspended in relaxing solution. This treatment yielded only muscle fragments with no clearly discernible sarcomeres at 64× magnification and no apparent myofibrils. We also tried mechanical homogenization with the muscle in storage solution with the idea that the presence of glycerol might reduce damage to the muscle, but this did not improve the results.

Next, we attempted a gentler manual disruption using a micro-pestle in a microcentrifuge tube. Individual jump muscles were suspended in relaxing solution and ground by hand with 3, 15, or 20 rotations of the pestle. This approach showed some promise: we observed recognizable pieces of muscle and some long, thread-like structures that could be either very thin muscle fiber segments or individual myofibrils. A few of these threads showed visible sarcomere striations. We mounted several candidate myofibrils (threads with diameters in the expected 1–3 μm range) on the apparatus, but none produced measurable force upon activation.

Somewhat surprisingly, the only successful method for isolating Drosophila myofibrils we found was the application of brief ultrasonication. We suspended two to three permeabilized jump muscles in 200 μL of storage solution (which contains 50% [w/v] glycerol) in a 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tube. We then applied a single 5-second pulse of low-power ultrasound using a sonic dismembrator at 10% amplitude, ∼1 kJ delivered at 40 kHz (Fisher Scientific Model 150). This very short, mild sonication released a large quantity of single myofibrils into the solution. The isolated myofibrils had clearly defined sarcomeres with sharp Z-discs and, in many cases, visible M-lines, suggesting preserved sarcomeric structure (Figure 2C). Some small bundles of myofibrils, larger tissue fragments, and cellular debris were also present, but not in amounts that impeded locating or mounting myofibrils.

Wild-type jump muscle myofibril characteristics

This is the first report, to our knowledge, of insect myofibrils activating. Isolated Drosophila jump muscle myofibrils responded to rapid changes in Ca2+ concentration in a manner analogous to vertebrate myofibrils. Upon switching from relaxing solution (pCa 8) to activating solution (pCa 4), the myofibrils produced a quick force rise to a plateau and then, upon return to pCa 8, relaxed in a biphasic fashion (Figure 3A). For reasons not yet clear, roughly half of the tested myofibrils failed to generate any active tension, even though they appeared structurally intact (with well-defined striations visible at 64× magnification). In vertebrate myofibril preparations, a subset of myofibrils can similarly fail to activate, though not at this frequency. EMB and pWMhc2 jump muscle myofibril characteristics

To explore Drosophila’s genetic utility, we compared myofibril mechanics between flies expressing different MHC isoforms. Specifically, we examined jump muscle myofibrils from the pWMhc2 line, which expresses the native jump muscle myosin 4 versus the EMB line, which expresses the slower embryonic body wall muscle myosin isoform (EMB) in the jump muscle.18 The EMB isoform is normally present in embryonic/larval muscles and differs from the native isoform in four alternative exon encoded S-1 domains. This comparison allows us to assess how a slower myosin isoform influences contractile parameters, while other sarcomeric proteins remain constant.

Myofibrils from EMB flies had similar passive tension to those from pWMhc2 controls (Table 1). However, EMB myofibrils produced significantly more active tension than pWMhc2, approximately 1.6-fold higher on average (Table 1; Figure 3). Interestingly, the rate of activation was substantially slower in EMB myofibrils: kACT was about 38% lower than in pWMhc2 controls (Table 1; Figure 4). Despite these differences in tension and activation speed, the relaxation parameters were essentially unchanged between these myosin isoforms. Neither the duration of the slow relaxation phase (tLIN) nor the rates of slow (kLIN) or fast (kREL) relaxation showed statistically significant differences between EMB and pWMhc2 myofibrils (Table 1; Figure 5). Thus, substituting the native jump muscle myosin with the slower EMB myosin had a pronounced effect on force production and activation kinetics, but no detectable effect on relaxation kinetics.

Verification of myofibril preparation using vertebrate cardiac muscle

Because sonication can potentially impact protein structure, especially at high power or long duration, and because this is (to our knowledge) the first use of sonication to isolate myofibrils, we wanted to verify that our ultrasonication protocol does not artifactually alter myofibril mechanics. To do so, we applied our Drosophila sonication protocol to mouse left ventricular myocardium and successfully isolated single cardiac myofibrils. However, the yield was very low, on the order of fewer than 100 myofibrils per milligram of tissue, which is orders of magnitude below the yield from standard mechanical homogenization of the same tissue. The yield (number of myofibrils) was also far lower than that following Drosophila jump muscle sonication. We are unclear as to whether optimizing the sonication parameters (energy, duration, etc.) could improve the yield for vertebrate muscle, or whether inherent structural differences between vertebrate and insect muscle (e.g., fibrillar collagens) make vertebrate myofibrils harder to liberate with this gentler protocol.

We compared the mechanics of mouse cardiac myofibrils isolated by sonication to those isolated by the conventional mechanical homogenization protocol that we have previously employed.26 The two sets of cardiac myofibrils showed no significant differences in any measured mechanical parameter: resting tension, active tension, kACT, tLIN, kLIN, and kREL were all statistically indistinguishable (Figure 6). We conclude that the mild ultrasonication protocol did not measurably damage cardiac myofibrils or alter their mechanical behavior. Discussion

Development of a novel myofibril isolation procedure

We have developed the first method, to our knowledge, to isolate and activate single myofibrils from an insect muscle. This novel Drosophila jump muscle myofibril preparation enables direct measurements of activation and relaxation kinetics, filling a crucial gap in the Drosophila muscle toolkit. Our advance is especially valuable for Drosophila research, as no preparation method has previously enabled measurements of sarcomere activation and relaxation rates of Drosophila muscles; thus, our preparation expands the repertoire of mechanical assays that can be combined with Drosophila’s powerful genetics.

Several observations indicate that our sonication-based method yields high-quality myofibrils that retain normal function. First, using mouse myocardium, we found no differences in mechanical properties between myofibrils isolated by our ultrasonication protocol and those isolated by standard homogenization. The sonicated cardiac myofibrils had the same active tension, activation rate, and relaxation behavior as control myofibrils (Figure 6), though with lower yield. Thus, any consequences of sonication (if present at all) are equal to traditional methods.

Second, Drosophila myofibrils expressing the EMB myosin isoform produced greater tension than those expressing native jump muscle myosin. Our previous work with permeabilized fibers showed that replacing native myosin with EMB increased isometric tension ∼1.4-fold in jump muscle fibers.6 Here, we observed a similar effect at the single myofibril level: EMB myofibrils produced ∼1.6-fold higher tension than pWMhc2 controls. One known reason for EMB’s higher force is its slower cross-bridge cycling, which confers a higher duty ratio (a greater fraction of myosin heads strongly bound to actin at any given time). A higher duty ratio directly translates to more force per myofibril,6,19 at least partially explaining the increased tension in EMB preparations.

Third, the kinetic parameters we measured in wild-type (w1118) Drosophila myofibrils are faster than those we measured from mouse cardiac myofibrils, which is what one would expect when comparing myofibrils from a very fast muscle type, Drosophila jump muscle,6 with mouse cardiac myofibrils expressing alpha myosin. Activation rate of jump muscle myofibrils is 4-fold faster, linear duration is 2-fold shorter and 2-fold faster in rate, and the exponential relaxation rate is 3-fold faster than in mouse cardiac myofibrils (Table 1 compared with Figure 6). This parallels our earlier findings at the fiber level: Drosophila jump muscle fibers have velocity and kinetic rates similar to those of mouse fast-twitch fibers, whose kinetics are faster than mouse cardiac preparations.6 These cross-species similarities underscore the physiological relevance of the Drosophila jump muscle myofibril and support its utility as a model for muscle function.

Previous attempts at myofibril isolation from Drosophila muscles yielded only passive mechanical measurements, not active contractions.14,15 A likely explanation is that earlier efforts focused on the IFM, which is inherently challenging for calcium-activated isometric force measurements. Drosophila IFM fibers produce only ∼1.5 mN/mm2 of isometric tension,16 so we estimate that a single IFM myofibril (∼1 μm diameter; cross-sectional area ∼0.8 μm2) should only generate a little over 1 nN of force. This is below the detection limit of current myofibril force transducers (our apparatus noise floor is ∼5 nN). In contrast, jump muscle fibers produce ∼40 mN/mm2,6 so a single jump muscle myofibril should produce tens of nanonewtons of force, well within detectable range. This difference in force output is a primary reason we succeeded in measuring active myofibril mechanics from Drosophila jump muscle while prior IFM-based attempts were unsuccessful.

Another distinction from the jump muscle is that Drosophila IFM is an asynchronous, stretch-activated muscle, meaning it relies on stretch activation to achieve maximal force output.5,27,28 Stretch activation amplifies IFM tension several-fold above the calcium-activated tension alone.29 The jump muscle, in contrast, is a synchronous muscle possessing only a small amount of stretch activation, the same as mammalian skeletal muscle.6 The jump muscle relies on calcium-mediated activation, which is reflected in the similar activation and relaxation kinetics we observed between Drosophila jump muscle and vertebrate muscles.

Our ultrasonication method may prove useful for isolating myofibrils from other muscle types that do not work with conventional methods or even engineered muscle tissues that resist traditional homogenization. In our hands, mechanical homogenization completely failed to release any myofibrils from Drosophila jump muscle, instead destroying the tissue, whereas ultrasonication was highly effective. We have not yet tested ultrasonication on other muscle types beyond Drosophila jump muscle and mouse ventricular muscle.

Potential insights into activation and relaxation mechanisms

This is the first time, to our knowledge, that anyone has directly measured how different native myosin isoforms affect muscle activation and relaxation kinetics. By exchanging only the MHC in the Drosophila jump muscle, any changes in activation or relaxation can be attributed to differences in cross-bridge properties. Introducing the EMB myosin isoform did not significantly perturb the muscle’s ultrastructure or the expression of other muscle proteins.6,18

We found that slowing the myosin attachment and detachment kinetics (via the EMB isoform) affected activation but not relaxation. Previous studies have shown that EMB myosin’s detachment rate from actin is roughly ten times slower than that of the native jump muscle myosin, and its attachment rate is moderately (∼30%) slower.6,19,20,21,22 In our myofibrils, EMB caused a significant slowing of the activation rate (∼38% decrease in kACT), which aligns closely with the difference in attachment kinetics between EMB and native myosin. This supports the idea that the myosin kinetics are a key determinant of how fast a muscle can activate. Besides the myosin kinetics, a difference in myosin recruitment rate, such as the transition from the SRX to DRX state, for EMB compared with the control could also be affecting myofibril activation rate. The correlation suggests that attachment rate (cross-bridge formation) is more critical to activation rate than detachment kinetics.

Studies in cardiac muscle myofibrils support the hypothesis that myosin kinetics are rate limiting for activation rate. For instance, troponin C’s Ca2+ switching is ∼20-fold faster than force development, suggesting something downstream (likely cross-bridge binding) is rate limiting.30,31,32,33 Likewise, interventions that enhance thin-filament activation, such as adding bepridil to increase troponin Ca2+ affinity in cardiac myofibrils, do not speed up activation, again implying that cross-bridge kinetics set the pace.34 In contrast, previous studies of relaxation have suggested that either or both the linear and exponential phase rates are mostly set by cross-bridge kinetics and not thin filament inactivation kinetics.34,35,36 Recent experiments suggest potential involvement of myosin (perhaps including the IHM) in one or both relaxation phases. One possibility is that sarcomeres may remain isometric during the first phase and the fast phase starts with a sudden give (lengthening) of a few sarcomeres that initiates a cascade of redistribution of sarcomere lengths resulting in the second phase.37,38,39 If this is true, then why are there large differences, for example, between atrial and ventricular myofibril exponential relaxation rates?34 What differences in protein isoforms would cause sarcomeres from different muscle types to have different rates of sarcomere length redistributions? Leveraging the advantages of our new Drosophila myofibril system will help provide answers.

Conclusion

Our results with EMB myosin compared with the jump muscle isoform are only a preview of the muscle physiology, protein structure and function, and disease mechanisms we can test with the powerful combination of Drosophila genetics and myofibrils. We plan to more thoroughly explore the role of myosin kinetics in future studies by employing additional native myosin isoforms, custom-made myosins, and other contractile protein isoforms to increase our understanding of sarcomere activation and relaxation.

Limitations

One limitation of our study is the difficulty in measuring kLIN. The slow linear phase of relaxation is brief and relatively small, and it directly follows the rapid solution change, which generates significant noise. While the duration of this phase can be delineated by the time between the trigger signal and the start of the fast phase, fitting a slope to it is inherently more error prone, especially in fast muscle types where the duration is short and the solution exchange noise constitutes a proportionally larger fraction of the signal. This difficulty is not unique to our preparation, and relaxation rate parameters in other models consistently carry higher relative error than activation or tension parameters (e.g., compare variance in kLIN versus kACT in34,40). Additionally, the known sensitivity of the linear phase to mechanical perturbations41 likely compounds this measurement difficulty. Therefore, for this study, we do not draw any firm conclusions based on differences in kLIN between genotypes and instead focus our interpretation on parameters where the level of variation and sample sizes provided adequate statistical power.

Additional limitations include an approximate 50% success rate of activating jump muscle myofibrils, that the data were collected exclusively from female flies, and that we do not expect this technique to work for Drosophila IFM due to the IFM’s very low isometric-force-generating ability.
