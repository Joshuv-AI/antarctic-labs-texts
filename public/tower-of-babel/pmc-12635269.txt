Effectiveness of transcranial direct current stimulation on emotional instability and impulsivity in atypical depression and bipolar II disorder
Authors: Sara Javanbakhti, Mehdi Nobakht, Sepideh Amin Afshari, Samira Amin Afshari, Elmira Hajaghaie, Masoud Sheikhi, Arya Haddadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtypical depressive disorder and bipolar disorder often present with symptoms like emotional instability and impulsive behaviors. Various treatments, both pharmaceutical and non-pharmacological, are employed to manage these conditions. Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a modern treatment option that has shown promise with minimal side effects. This study aims to compare the effectiveness of tDCS on emotional instability and impulsivity in individuals with atypical depression and bipolar type II. This study utilized a semi-experimental, pre-test-post-test design with a control group. The participants were patients diagnosed with atypical and bipolar depression, all of whom were referred from the neuropsychiatric clinic at Sina Hospital in Hamedan, Iran. Two hundred instability and impulsivity questionnaires were distributed among the patients using a purposeful sampling method. From these, participants were randomly assigned into four groups of 20 individuals each. The research tools included transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), delivered in ten consecutive sessions (30 min each, 2 mA) with the anode at F3 and the cathode at F4; the sham condition used the same montage with current discontinued after 30 s to ensure blinding., the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ) by Eysenck and Sybil, and Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS). Data analysis using covariance and multivariate covariance analysis (MANCOVA) revealed a significant difference in emotional instability and impulsivity scores between the experimental and control groups for individuals with atypical depression and bipolar type II. Direct current electrical brain stimulation was found to reduce emotional instability in these groups, leading to improved emotional stability and reduced impulsivity in people with atypical depression and bipolar disorder. The results of this study confirm the potential of direct current electrical brain stimulation in reducing emotional instability and impulsivity in individuals with atypical depression. The emergence of tDCS as a promising non-pharmacological treatment option for managing these symptoms in people with both atypical depression and bipolar disorder type II is reassuring. This method modulates the prefrontal cortex’s activity, a brain region crucial for planning, decision-making, and emotional regulation. The significant potential of tDCS as a non-pharmacological intervention for atypical depression and bipolar disorder type II is a positive sign for the future. Introduction

Mood disorders are among the most common psychiatric conditions, and depression is a major subtype with a lifetime prevalence of about 15%1,2. Atypical depression, the most prevalent outpatient form, differs from typical depression by symptoms such as hypersomnia, increased appetite, leaden paralysis, and sensitivity to rejection3. Bipolar disorder is another chronic mood disorder, affecting about 2% of the population4,5. It is divided into Bipolar I, with full manic episodes, and Bipolar II, with hypomania. Both atypical depression and bipolar II are associated with cognitive dysfunction, emotional instability, and impulsive behaviors6. Emotional instability is a biological feature of bipolar disorder. It encompasses traits such as irritability, restlessness, aggressiveness, and impulsiveness7. Emotional instability involves frequent shifts in emotions, moving from positive feelings like happiness to negative ones such as sadness. It can show up in various forms, including impulsive actions, self-destructive behaviors, anxiety, depression, dissatisfaction, and anger8,9. People experiencing emotional instability often struggle with irrational thoughts, find it challenging to control their impulses, and may have difficulty effectively handling situations9. Impulsive behaviors are defined as acting without thinking and inappropriately responding to stimuli that often have adverse consequences10. Impulsive behaviors, often referred to as risky behaviors, encompass a variety of actions characterized by minimal forethought. These actions are typically immature, lack focus on specific tasks, and occur without proper planning, posing a significant risk11. Today, to manage emotional instability and impulsivity in individuals with atypical depression and bipolar type II, medications are commonly used. However, due to the potential side effects of these drugs, non-pharmacological approaches are also being explored as alternatives12.

Seemingly, emotional instability and impulsivity in individuals with atypical depression and bipolar type II disorder may be effectively treated using direct current brain stimulation. Various methods are available, both invasive and non-invasive, to stimulate the brain13. One non-invasive option is transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), which involves using a mild electric current to influence the activities of the cerebral cortex. This technique modulates the activity of specific brain regions, enhancing or diminishing particular brain functions13. tDCS is a neurotherapeutic approach that involves the application of a mild direct current to cortical areas, aimed at facilitating or inhibiting neural spontaneity14. In the past decade, direct electrical stimulation of the brain has emerged as a non-invasive, safe, and cost-effective technique aimed at altering the excitability of the cerebral cortex. This method achieves its effects by modifying the resting potential of the nerve cells within the cerebral cortex, and it has been the subject of extensive research.

The significance of addressing mood disorders, including atypical depression, has resulted in the creation and advancement of numerous treatment approaches. However, these methods encounter significant obstacles, such as the high incidence of disease recurrence, with rates exceeding 80% following recovery, as well as challenges related to treatment resistance15. Bipolar disorder can have severe consequences if not addressed promptly and is recognized as the sixth leading cause of disability globally. Consequently, mental health professionals and researchers need to explore non-pharmacological and psychological interventions to assist individuals experiencing depression and bipolar disorder. Accordingly, the primary objective of the present study was to examine whether transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) reduces emotional instability and impulsivity in individuals with atypical depression and bipolar type II.

Methods

The current research utilized a quasi-experimental methodology, incorporating a pre-test and post-test design along with follow-up assessments involving a control group. The statistical population consisted of patients diagnosed with atypical and bipolar type II depression who were referred from the Neuropsychiatric Clinic of Sina Hospital in Hamedan, Iran. These patients were evaluated through interviews conducted by a psychiatrist and diagnosed according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Fifth Edition (DSM-5). All patients were in hypomanic or high episodes at the time of the study and had no prior history of drug use, ensuring that their responses were not influenced by medication history. The questionnaire assessing emotional instability and impulsivity was administered, leading to the selection of 80 individuals who scored 11.5 or lower on the emotional instability assessment and 72 or lower on the impulsivity assessment. The sample size was determined using a priori power analysis in G*Power 3.1.9.7, assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), α = 0.05, and statistical power (1 − β) = 0.80 for a repeated measures ANOVA with four groups. This analysis suggested that a minimum of 20 participants per group (total N = 80) would be sufficient to detect significant effects.

These 80 patients were then randomly assigned to four groups (two intervention groups and two control groups), each comprising 20 participants. Simple randomization was performed using a computerized random number generator by an independent researcher not involved in the assessment or intervention phases, to ensure allocation concealment. Group allocation was done after participant eligibility was confirmed, based on established inclusion and exclusion criteria.

To qualify for the study, participants had to meet the following criteria: be aged between 18 and 50, have no other psychiatric disorders, and possess no metal objects in their body or head, including intracranial implants. Additionally, consent from both the participant and their family was required. Participants could not be taking anticonvulsant or antipsychotic medications and should have no history of epilepsy, seizures, concussions, or brain surgery. Exclusion criteria included missing more than three intervention sessions, insufficient cooperation, or an inability to comprehend related concepts. Ethical standards, such as scientific integrity, respect for authors’ intellectual rights, confidentiality, and obtaining informed consent from all participants, were rigorously upheld throughout the research process. At the end of the study, all participants in both the experimental and control groups resumed standard pharmacological treatment, managed and monitored by a psychiatrist, to ensure continued care and to address any potential symptoms or relapses. The study received approval from the Research and Ethics Council of Hamedan Azad University, documented under letter number IR.IAU.H.REC.1402.80.

Research tools

Barratt impulsiveness scale (BIS-11)

Developed by Barratt et al. in 1997, the BIS-11 is a widely used self-report questionnaire designed to measure impulsivity. It consists of 30 items rated on a four-point Likert scale: 1 (never/rarely), 2 (sometimes), 3 (often), and 4 (most of the time/always). Notably, 11 of these items are reverse-scored to enhance response accuracy. The total score can range from 20 to 120, indicating varying levels of impulsivity16. The Persian version has been validated, showing adequate reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81) and test-retest reliability = 0.7717.

Eysenck personality questionnaire (EPQ)

Created by Eysenck and Sybil in 1973, the EPQ is intended for individuals aged 16 and older. Comprising 90 statements, it assesses key personality traits such as introversion, extroversion, neuroticism, psychoticism, and deception. Based on the theory of personality dimensions, it is widely used in research with established reliability and validity18. Studies across cultures, including Iran, reveal a 99% similarity in neuroticism and extroversion. The Persian version demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.68–0.85) and acceptable test-retest reliability (0.71–0.90)19.

tDCS direct current electrical brain stimulation method

In the present study, direct current brain electrical stimulation was performed using the protocol of Liu et al.20. Participants received direct current stimulation for ten consecutive days, with each session lasting 30 min. The anode electrode was placed at F3 and the cathode at F4, delivering a current intensity of 2 mA. The tDCS was administered using the Neurostim-2 device (manufactured by Medinateb Company, Iran). The device delivers constant current stimulation with adjustable parameters, and includes safety features such as electrode impedance monitoring and sham stimulation mode.

Procedures

Participants were interviewed to provide key information about the conditions, duration, and safety of the intervention. To participate in the study, they completed an informed consent form, ensuring their understanding and agreement regarding confidentiality and the non-disclosure of personal information. The study outlined how the tDCS device operates and discussed its potential side effects, as well as details about the testing procedure. Prior to beginning the intervention, participants filled out two questionnaires assessing emotional instability and impulsivity.

The Intervention group received individual treatment with the device for ten consecutive days, with each session lasting 30 min. The anode electrode was placed at the F3 location, while the cathode was positioned at F4, both operating at an intensity of 2 mA. In contrast, the control group had electrodes placed in the same positions for the same duration, but the electric current was interrupted after 30 s. This duration was intended to prevent participants from being aware of whether the current was actually applied.

Research hypotheses

This study proposed three hypotheses. First, there would be a difference in the effectiveness of tDCS on emotional instability and impulsivity between individuals with atypical depression and those with bipolar type II. Second, tDCS would significantly affect emotional instability and impulsivity in individuals with atypical depression. Finally, it was hypothesized that tDCS would also influence emotional instability and impulsivity in individuals with bipolar type II.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA), analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), and Bonferroni tests to compare differences in impulsivity and emotional instability between groups over time. Assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variances, and homogeneity of regression slopes were checked using Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Levene’s test, and ANOVA interaction tests, respectively. Pre-test scores were included as covariates in the analyses, ensuring that group differences at baseline were adjusted for and that the post-test comparisons more accurately reflected the effects of the intervention.

Results

In the atypical depression group, the sample was evenly split between men and women, with each group making up 50% (20 men and 20 women). In the bipolar type II group, 47% of the participants were men (19 individuals), while 52% were women (21 individuals). In the atypical depression group, most participants (≈ 48%) were aged 33–39 years, while in the bipolar II group the largest proportion (≈ 43%) also fell in this age range. Other age categories were less represented.

The findings indicate that the experimental group’s average scores for impulsivity and emotional instability were assessed through pre-test and post-test evaluations. In the atypical depression group, impulsivity scores significantly decreased from pre-test to post-test (F = 880.81, p < 0.001, η² = 0.61, 95% CI [− 9.8, − 6.5]), and emotional instability also declined (F = 550.34, p < 0.001, η² = 0.39, 95% CI [− 4.2, − 2.8]; see Table 4). In the bipolar type II group, impulsivity scores were reduced as well (F = 60.34, p < 0.001, η² = 0.63, 95% CI [− 13.7, − 7.3]), along with emotional instability (F = 304.74, p < 0.001, η² = 0.89, 95% CI [− 5.9, − 4.5]; see Table 5).

Before conducting the comparative analyses, assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variances, and homogeneity of regression slopes were examined. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test confirmed normal distribution of scores (p > 0.05), Levene’s test indicated equality of variances, and ANOVA showed that the assumption of homogeneity of regression slopes was satisfied. These results justified the use of ANCOVA and MANCOVA for hypothesis testing.

The analysis of the regression slope assumption indicates that the condition of homogeneity of interactive effects is satisfied in both groups, as the F-value for interactions is not significant at the p < 0.05 threshold. Consequently, the assumption of homogeneity of interactive effects holds true. Both multivariate and univariate covariance analyses are employed to test the hypotheses, adhering to the fundamental assumptions of covariance analysis, including the homogeneity of interaction effects.

According to Table 3, the Bonferroni post hoc tests were conducted to examine pairwise differences between groups on emotional instability and impulsivity scores, following the significant ANCOVA results.

For emotional instability, the depression test group showed significantly higher scores compared to the depression control and bipolar control groups (adjusted p < 0.01), while the difference between the depression test and bipolar test groups was not statistically significant. Additionally, the difference between the depression control and bipolar test groups was marginally significant (p = 0.03), and a significant difference was found between bipolar test and bipolar control groups (p < 0.01).

Furthermore, the depression control group scored significantly higher than the bipolar test group, and the bipolar test group scored significantly lower than the bipolar control group (all adjusted p < 0.01).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) in reducing emotional instability and impulsivity in individuals diagnosed with atypical depression and bipolar type II disorder. The results confirmed the efficacy of tDCS in mitigating these symptoms in both groups, with notable differences in its impact on each disorder. Our findings show that tDCS significantly reduced emotional instability and impulsivity in individuals with atypical depression. These results are in line with prior research by Fregni et al.21, Hu et al.22, Chang et al.23, and He et al.24, who observed similar improvements following tDCS interventions. Atypical depression, characterized by distinct symptoms like hypersomnia, overeating, and a sensation of heaviness in the limbs, differs from classical depression in its symptomatology and response to treatment3. Emotional instability and impulsivity are prominent features of atypical depression, and while pharmacological treatments remain common, their side effects have driven the search for alternative therapeutic options such as tDCS12.

tDCS applies low-intensity electrical stimulation to the prefrontal cortex, a brain region responsible for higher cognitive functions, including emotion regulation, planning, and decision-making. In individuals with atypical depression, prefrontal cortical activity is often diminished, which can exacerbate emotional dysregulation and impulsive behaviors25. By enhancing neuronal excitability in this region, tDCS strengthens brain-behavior connections in both cognitive and emotional domains, leading to more stable emotional responses and improved impulse control.

Several studies have explored potential neurobiological pathways specifically linked to the alleviation of mood symptoms following tDCS. For example, tDCS applied over the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) has been shown to modulate fronto-limbic connectivity, leading to enhanced top-down regulation of emotion-related brain regions such as the amygdala26. Neuroimaging evidence suggests that increased prefrontal activation and reduced amygdala hyperactivity after repeated tDCS sessions are associated with significant reductions in mood lability and impulsive behaviors in individuals with mood disorders27. Additionally, changes in cortical excitability patterns, reflected in normalized resting-state EEG alpha asymmetry, have been correlated with improvements in emotional regulation and decreased symptom severity. These findings support the notion that the therapeutic effects of tDCS on mood symptoms are mediated through targeted modulation of neural circuits implicated in affective regulation, rather than through generalized biochemical changes alone.

The study also showed that tDCS effectively reduced emotional instability and impulsivity in individuals with bipolar type II disorder, consistent with previous findings such as Feeser et al. (2014), which reported modulation of emotional responses through stimulation of the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) — a key region for emotion regulation28. Bipolar type II involves severe mood variability, irritability, impulsivity, and poor decision-making, linked to frontal cortex dysregulation29, especially in the DLPFC30. By increasing neuronal excitability in this region, tDCS can enhance emotional control and reduce impulsivity31. Electrode placement at F3 and F4 specifically targets this area, helping restore its function and strengthening top-down regulation of subcortical structures such as the amygdala32,33.

When comparing groups, symptom reduction was greater in atypical depression, possibly reflecting the higher clinical complexity of bipolar type II34,35. Emotional variability in bipolar disorder may limit treatment consistency, while symptoms in atypical depression are more stable. tDCS also modulates neurotransmitters involved in mood regulation, including serotonin and dopamine36, which may contribute to its effects. However, differences in neurobiology, including hypomanic episodes, may alter responsiveness. Given the scarcity of direct comparative evidence, these interpretations are exploratory and require further study.

Ongoing research is investigating optimal parameters and alternative electrode placements. For instance, Mehrsafar et al. (2020) found that stimulation at Fp1 and CZ/OZ improved mood after a single session, underscoring the potential of tDCS as a non-invasive treatment for emotional instability and impulsivity37.

Conclusion

The tDCS presents a promising non-drug treatment option for managing emotional instability and impulsivity in individuals with atypical depression and bipolar II disorder. Clinically, tDCS can influence the activity of the prefrontal cortex, a crucial area for planning, decision-making, and emotion regulation. For those with bipolar disorder, tDCS holds potential benefits in reducing emotional instability and impulsivity. However, the simultaneous use of psychiatric medications may impact the effectiveness of this treatment. Future research should aim to enhance tDCS treatment protocols to boost its therapeutic benefits. Studies need to explore the long-term effects of tDCS and the optimal duration and frequency of sessions. Additionally, examining its effectiveness when combined with other treatments is crucial. Investigating the underlying mechanisms of tDCS in various patient groups will aid in personalizing treatment plans. Overall, tDCS shows great promise as a non-drug intervention for atypical depression and bipolar II disorder. Further research and clinical application are essential to fully understand and maximize its advantages, ultimately improving patient outcomes and quality of life.

Limitations

The research is subject to several limitations, including a small sample size resulting from the clinical characteristics of the statistical population. Additionally, the absence of a follow-up phase can be attributed to time constraints. Furthermore, the reliance on a self-report measurement tool introduces the potential for bias in the responses provided in the questionnaire. Another limitation of this study is the absence of clinical severity assessments, such as the Clinical Global Impression Scale, at the time of data collection. Mood disorders like Bipolar II can have rapid symptom changes that affect impulsivity and emotional instability scores. Without controlling for clinical severity at both time points, the findings may be less precise. Future research should include these measures and adjust analyses accordingly to improve validity.

Based on the research findings and identified limitations, the following recommendations are made for future researchers: It is advisable to conduct further studies with a larger sample size and to incorporate follow-up measures. Additionally, it is recommended that the impact of this treatment be explored in conjunction with pharmacological therapy or psychotherapy.
