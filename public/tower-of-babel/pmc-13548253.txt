Association between air pollution, multimorbidity and polypharmacy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hanbin Zhang, Abiola Rodemade, Siyu Wang, Xinyi Zhang, Shawn Y Lee, Dimitris Evangelopoulos, Maoyi Tian, Zixuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo comprehensively examine the existing literature investigating the association between air pollution exposure and multimorbidity and/or polypharmacy.DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis.Data sourcesPubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE and the Cochrane Library.Eligibility criteriaObservational studies assessed the association between air pollution and the risk of multimorbidity and/or polypharmacy among the general population were searched. Only full-text original human studies written in English up to 1 April 2026 were included.Data extraction and synthesisData was extracted using a standardised data extraction template and analysed using Stata SE V.19. Estimates were pooled using a random effects model, with the I2 statistic used to estimate heterogeneity of results (PROSPERO registration: CRD42024613048).ResultsIn total, 22 studies met the inclusion criteria in our systematic review and 21 of 22 were included for meta-analysis. After excluding incompatible effect estimates (increment other than per 5 µg/m3, 10 µg/m3 or IQR increase) and studies using overlapping datasets, four, three, two and four were included in the main meta-analysis, respectively, with a pooled adjusted risk ratio (aRR) of 1.15 (95% CI 1.08 to 1.23; I2=93.0%) per 5 µg/m³ particulate matter (PM)2.5 increase, a pooled aRR of 1.27 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.47; I2=99.5%) per 5 µg/m³ PM10 increase, a pooled aRR of 1.07 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.09; I2=0.0%) per 5 µg/m³ PM1 increase and a pooled aRR of 1.12 (95% CI 1.00 to 1.26; I2=96.6%) per 5 µg/m³ nitrogen dioxide (NO2) increase. There was no evidence of associations between other air pollutants (nitrogen oxides (N=3), black carbon (N=1), sulphur dioxide (N=3), ozone (N=4), carbon monoxide (N=3)) and multimorbidity. No eligible studies on polypharmacy, indoor air pollution, older adults, American and Oceanian populations were identified.ConclusionsLong-term exposure to ambient PM2.5 and NO2 may elevate the risk of multimorbidity. Future research should prioritise household air pollution, rural areas, older populations as a target group, continents other than Europe and Asia and polypharmacy.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Epidemiological evidence is well established regarding the effects of air pollution exposure on individual categories of adverse health outcomes, such as cardiovascular, respiratory and neurological conditions; however, evidence relating to multimorbidity and/or its accumulate medication use for multiple conditions (eg, polypharmacy) remains limited.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

We present the first systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the association between air pollution and the risk of multimorbidity and/or polypharmacy. Our findings suggest that particulate matter (PM)2.5 and PM10 exposure are associated with a 15% and a 27% increased risk of multimorbidity, respectively, although substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies.

We present the first systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the association between air pollution and the risk of multimorbidity and/or polypharmacy. Our findings suggest that particulate matter (PM)2.5 and PM10 exposure are associated with a 15% and a 27% increased risk of multimorbidity, respectively, although substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Our investigation supports the need for continued policies aimed at reducing ambient air pollution to lessen the burden of multimorbidity. It also highlights key research gaps, particularly in relation to polypharmacy, household air‐pollution measurements, rural settings and older populations.

Introduction

Ambient air pollution, primarily driven by fossil fuel combustion, consists of pollutants such as particulate matter (PM2.5, PM10), nitrogen oxides (nitrogen dioxide (NO2), NOx) and ozone (O3).1 Household air pollution (HAP), largely resulting from the burning of solid fuels, is a significant contributor to disease burden, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).2 Ambient and HAP altogether contribute to an estimated 8.1 million premature deaths annually and are linked to a wide range of chronic diseases such as ischaemic heart disease (IHD) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).3

Substantial synthesised evidence has supported the effects of air pollution exposure to a single category of adverse health outcomes, such as cardiovascular,4 respiratory,5 neurological6 and metabolic diseases.7 However, how air pollution is linked to multimorbidity (the coexistence of two or more long-term conditions in an individual8) remains largely unexplored. Chronic inhalation of pollutants induces systemic inflammation and oxidative stress that produces simultaneous biological effects across multiple organ systems.9 These shared mechanisms in theory can concurrently promote endothelial dysfunction and accelerated atherosclerosis in the cardiovascular system,910 intensify airway inflammation in the respiratory system,1112 disrupt metabolic and immune regulation,13 activate the autonomic nervous system and neuroendocrine stress.14 Moreover, a single disease triggered by air pollution may progress into multimorbidity. For example, air pollution can affect lung infection via a few mechanisms including disturbing macrophage from functioning11 and then lead to a response in the systemic blood system via either activation of the endothelium or activation of atherosclerotic plaque.15 The infection in the lung can also further instigate neurological disorders via inflammatory responses and hypoxia-induced damage through the ‘lung-brain axis’.16 Meanwhile, current literature suggests that multimorbidity is a primary driver of polypharmacy (the regular concomitant use of five or more medications17), yet studies have not explicitly examined how air pollution-induced multimorbidity contributes to increased medication burden. Given the rising global prevalence of multimorbidity18 and the significant challenges posed by polypharmacy,19 including adverse drug interactions, medication errors and increased healthcare costs,20 understanding this link is also of critical importance.

This study primarily aimed to systematically review and synthesise evidence on the association between long-term exposure to air pollution and the risk of multimorbidity and examine the differential effects of specific pollutants (eg, PM2.5, NO2) on multimorbidity. The secondary aim was to determine whether air pollution is associated with increased medication use (polypharmacy), given its role as a downstream consequence of multimorbidity.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE and the Cochrane Library, up to 1 April 2026, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines21 and checklist (online supplemental appendix 1). Observational studies (cohort or case−control) that identified the association between air pollution and multimorbidity and/or polypharmacy were identified using keywords ((“Air Pollution” OR “Particulate Matter” OR “Gaseous Pollutants” OR “Volatile Organic Compounds” OR “Black Carbon”)] AND [(“Multimorbidity”) OR (“Polypharmacy”)). Detailed search string and search strategies for each keyword above in each database are provided in online supplemental appendix 2, online supplemental tables 1-4. Only original human research in full-text and English writing was considered for review inclusion. Studies with irrelevant topic (other than identifying the association between air pollution and multimorbidity/polypharmacy) or in other study types or languages were excluded. The Population, Exposure, Comparator and Outcomes framework is shown in table 1, and the study protocol could be found on International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews database (online supplemental protocol file, PROSPERO: CRD42024613048, https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42024613048).

Data extraction

All identified studies were screened by individual reviewers (AR and SW) to assess eligibility, based on titles, abstracts and full-text contents. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and with adjudication by senior researchers (HZ and ZW). Data were extracted using a standardised data extraction template including study characteristics (eg, author, year of publication, location, data source, sample size, study design, participants baseline information), exposure details (eg, pollutant type, measurement method, exposure resolution), outcome ascertainment for multimorbidity and/or polypharmacy, outcome metrics including ORs, HRs, risk ratios (RRs) and their corresponding 95% CIs, confounding factors and methods used to address confounding, statistical analysis, etc. Where key information is missing, for example, events of newly developed multimorbidity, we filled in the missing data by directly contacting the author team via email.22 For multimorbidity, any outcome ascertainment by the original study was accepted as long as they were reported in the original studies with confirmation, for example, International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, clinical testing, self-reports and surveys, physician or hospital diagnoses.

Quality assessment

Two investigators (HZ and SW) independently assessed all the quality of included studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS)23 for methodological quality and the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies-of Interventions (ROBINS-I)24 and the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies-of Exposure (ROBINS-E)25 tools for risk of bias. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, where necessary, adjudication by a senior reviewer (ZW). Inter-rater reliability was assessed using a two-way absolute-agreement single-measure intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for NOS numerical scores and Cohen’s kappa for ROBINS categorical judgements. The NOS evaluates studies based on three domains: selection, comparability and outcomes. Only studies with good quality (a score of at least one in each major section and a total score of 6 or above) in NOS and with overall bias other than ‘critical risk’ in ROBINS-I and ROBINS-E were included in our meta-analysis (online supplemental appendix 3).

Statistical analysis

Adjusted estimates were pooled using a random-effect model26 with the corresponding 95% CI for each outcome and each air pollutant, where applicable, in the meta-analyses.26 Per 5 µg/m3 was set to be the standard exposure increment for meta-analyses considering the exposure heterogeneity across different study locations. Only studies reporting estimates of association (and CIs) due to exposure increment per IQR increase and per 10 µg/m3 increase, but not other exposure increments, were converted to estimates of association per 5 µg/m3 using established methods,27 considering exposure heterogeneity between high-income countries and LMICs. This standardisation follows the continuous-increment reporting used in the original studies and assumes a log-linear exposure-response relationship over the relevant exposure range. Among the reported outcome metrics, RR was considered as the reference statistical estimate. OR and HR were considered to approximate RR when the outcome was rare (incidence of outcome <10% in the study population); otherwise, converted to RR according to Zhang and Yu (1998)28 and VanderWeele (2020).29

Meta-analysis was conducted using the most appropriate independent estimate from each non-overlapping study population. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on region (continent, ie, Europe and Asia in this study) and, where available, patterns of multimorbidity. If two or more articles had overlapping datasets, the article with the largest dataset or longest follow-up period was retained for the main meta-analysis to ensure robustness and minimise redundancy. Sensitivity analyses were performed by replacing these studies with alternative studies from the same data source. A post hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding the study with children’s outcomes.30 Heterogeneity among included studies was assessed using I2, where a value of 0% is considered as no observed heterogeneity and a larger value indicates increasing heterogeneity.31 Given that fewer than ten independent studies were available in each meta-analysis, we did not formally assess publication bias or conduct meta-regression to detect sources of heterogeneity in accordance with Cochrane recommendations.32 As an exploratory sensitivity analysis, we assessed funnel plot asymmetry for PM2.5 with the highest number included for meta-analysis using visual inspection, Egger’s test33 and the trim-and-fill method,34 while recognising that some estimates were derived from overlapping datasets.

All analyses were conducted using Stata SE V.19.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the conduct of this study. No ethical approval was required for this study as this is based on aggregated-level data.

Results

Study selection

We identified a total of 22 791 records on 1 April 2026. After removing duplicates and screening by title and abstract, 80 studies were identified as eligible for full-text screening. Inter-rater reliability was assessed before adjudication. For NOS numerical scores, agreement was low (ICC=0.24, 95% CI −0.08 to 0.55; p=0.08). Although exact agreement was low, 92.3% of NOS scores differed by no more than one point. For ROBINS categorical judgements, squared-weighted Cohen’s kappa was −0.15 (p=0.41) and unweighted kappa was −0.06 (p=0.72), indicating no evidence of agreement beyond chance before adjudication. All disagreements were resolved through discussion and adjudication. Among these, 22 studies were eligible for inclusion in the systematic review.223035–54 21 studies were included at the meta-analysis stage; Cui et al (2024)38 was excluded due to self-reported health outcomes, and lack of description on follow-up, which received a low methodological quality from NOS quality assessment38 (figure 1 and online supplemental appendix 3, online supplemental table S5). It is noted that seven studies were not presented in meta-analyses due to non-comparable analysis method, including Mulat et al,44 which reported HAP measurements but there is no evidence that HAP was used to estimate health effect, Xia et al (2024),48 which adopted multipollutant model and employed exposure increment being per SD increase in air pollution to assess health effects, Chen et al (2024),36 reported exposure increment being per one-factor increase in air pollution without definition on one-factor, Cao et al (2025),50 defined exposure increment as comparing the second, third and fourth quartiles with the first quartile as the reference group, Chen et al (2023),37 Shi et al (2023),46 and He et al (2024),54 all adopted fuel type as proxy for HAP using the same dataset.

MCC, Multiple Chronic Conditions.

All studies included in the review were published after 2010 and employed a cohort study design. Of them, 10 studies investigated European populations from the UK,3639424347–4951 Denmark35 and France,30 11 focused on Chinese,223738404145465052–54 and one was conducted in an African population in Ethiopia.44 Among these, several studies used the same data sources: eight from UK Biobank3639424347–4951 and eight from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) data source.223741454652–54 UK Biobank is a large prospective UK cohort which includes about 500 000 adults with longitudinal health-record data. Summary of results

Particulate matter2.5

In total, 17 studies investigated PM2.5, with one study44 reported HAP levels but not used the measurements to associate with the risk of multimorbidity. 16 studies assessed the association between long-term ambient PM2.5 exposure and the risk of multimorbidity.22303639–434547–53 13 of them were included in the meta-analyses.223039–4345474951–53 Four studies were included in the main analysis,30404345 with a pooled adjusted RR (aRR) of 1.15 (95% CI 1.08 to 1.23) per 5 µg/m³ PM2.5 increase and high between-study heterogeneity (I2=93.0%) (figure 2). When stratified by continents: no evidence supported the increased risk of multimorbidity in European (aRR=1.35, 95% CI 0.89 to 2.06; I2=76.4%), while 8% higher risk was found in Asian (aRR=1.08, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.10; I2=59.0%) population after long-term ambient PM2.5 exposure. Sensitivity analyses using alternative studies from the UK Biobank3942434749 and CHARLS cohorts22415253 yielded similar results to the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of the main findings (online supplemental appendix 5, online supplemental figure S1-S9); similar result was found in the sensitivity analysis on children outcome exclusion (online supplemental appendix 5, online supplemental figure S10).

Forest plot of meta-analysis for PM2.5 exposure and multimorbidity. DL, DerSimonian–Laird random-effects method; PM, particulate matter; RR, risk ratio.

In an exploratory analysis including all 13 eligible estimates, visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested possible asymmetry (online supplemental appendix 5, online supplemental figure S11), with seven estimates falling outside the 95% CI,39404245495153 including two estimates from studies included in the main analysis.4051 However, Egger’s test did not indicate statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (β=1.18, SE=1.76, z=0.67, p=0.50). Similarly, the trim-and-fill method did not materially alter the pooled effect estimate, which remained unchanged at 0.28 (95% CI 0.10 to 0.47). These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the analysis included overlapping estimates and a limited number of independent datasets.

Particulate matter10

Six studies estimated the association between PM10 exposure and the risk of multimorbidity using both European (British) and Asian (Chinese) population.394047495253 Three were included in the meta-analysis404953—strong evidence supported the association (aRR=1.27, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.47) per 5 µg/m³ PM10 increase404953 (figure 3). Substantial heterogeneity was identified (I2=99.5%). Sensitivity analysis overall supports the association between PM10 and multimorbidity (online supplemental appendix 5, online supplemental figure S12-14).

Particulate matter1

Two studies estimated the association between PM1 exposure and the risk of multimorbidity using Asian population—both were included in meta-analysis with strong evidence supported the association (aRR=1.07, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.09, I2=0%) per 5 µg/m³ PM1 increase4052 (online supplemental appendix 5, online supplemental figure S15).

Nitrogen dioxide

10 studies identified the association between NO2 exposure and the risk of multimorbidity which were all conducted using European population.3035394347–4951–53 Four were included in the main meta-analysis after removal of overlapping datasets.30354353 Weak evidence supported a modest increase in the risk of multimorbidity after NO2 exposure, with a pooled aRR of 1.12 (95% CI 1.00 to 1.26) per 5 µg/m³ NO2 increase and high between-study heterogeneity (I2=96.6%) (online supplemental appendix 5, online supplemental figure S16). Findings in the sensitivity analyses support the robustness of the observed direction of effect3943474953 (online supplemental appendix 5, online supplemental figure S17-S21); similar result was found in the sensitivity analysis on children outcome exclusion30 (online supplemental appendix 5, online supplemental figure S22).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis explored the association between long-term ambient air pollution exposure and the risk of multimorbidity. Risk of polypharmacy was not available due to insufficient data reported. We found evidence that particles, PM2.5, PM10 and PM1 exposure were associated with a 15% (95% CI 1.08 to 1.23; I2=93.0%), a 27% (95% CI 1.11 to 1.47; I2=99.5%) and a 7% (95% CI 1.05 to 1.09; I2=0.0%) increased risk of multimorbidity, respectively, although substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies for PM2.5 and PM10. In contrast, only limited evidence was identified for NO2, and no evidence was found for other air pollutants.

Our finding suggests that particles, particularly fine PM2.5, may be a potent driver of multimorbidity. This agrees with previous literature that particles are associated with more morbidity health outcomes than NO2.55 The epigenetic changes caused by PM2.5 can exacerbate metabolic dysfunction by altering gene expression in insulin signalling pathways56 and promote insulin resistance, obesity and dyslipidaemia, ultimately increasing the risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity.13 This evidence aligns with findings from our study, where seven of the 13 included studies specifically investigated the risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity. Among these, Luo et al (2023),42 also analysed all phases of cardiometabolic multimorbidity progression which indicate the vulnerability of cardiovascular and metabolic systems to air pollution.42

All included studies were in Europe or Asia, apart from Mulat et al (2024),44 being the only African study. No study focused on population from America or Oceania, although evidence on the adverse cardiovascular impacts of air pollution was established early through well-conducted studies in the USA,57–59 and more recent large-scale cohort investigations have been conducted.60 Evidence from rural areas is also under-represented, with only one study focused on rural areas,30 where resources can be limited to support health data collection or necessary parameters for air pollution estimation via approaches such as land use regression models. Moreover, studies looked at multimorbidity beyond the middle-aged population were under-represented, with two of our included studies3044 looked at multimorbidity among children. It is noted that multiple studies in this systematic review relied on existing cohort dataset (eg, UK Biobank) which included older population in their general population group, yet no specific study focused on older population only as a target group, despite multimorbidity increases with age61 and an overall prevalence of 66.1% (IQR 54.4–76.6) among older adults in higher income country.62

In our review, two studies specifically investigated the association between air pollution exposure and progression of multimorbidity from mental illness, including mood disorder42 and neuropsychiatric disorder.51 Both studies reported strong evidence to support the association from baseline to mental illness, but overall weak or no evidence for the progression from mental illness to multimorbidity. Previous systematic review between air pollution and depression also reported a significant elevated risk, equivalent to 1.05 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.09) per 5 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5.63 More studies are needed to consider chronic mental illness into multimorbidity when assessing the impact of air pollution on health.

Our findings on the elevated multimorbidity risk (aRR=1.15, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.23 per 5 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5) were slightly higher than previous meta-analyses focusing on the association between PM2.5 and a single disease outcome under the same exposure increment, for example, elevated risk for depression 1.05 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.09),63 COPD 1.09 (95% CI 1.06 to 1.11),64 type 2 diabetes 1.05 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.08),65 hypertension 1.07 (95% CI 1.04 to 1.09),66 coronary heart disease 1.10 (95% CI 1.04 to 1.16),66 chronic kidney disease 1.05 (95% CI 1.00 to 1.10),67 asthma 1.07 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.13).68 This may suggest that PM2.5 has a larger health effect on other organ systems in susceptible population once it triggered response in one body system, while we note the population of each study may be different.

We intended to further disentangle the association between air pollution and multimorbidity by analysing different multimorbidity patterns, separately assessing the effects on (1) healthy population (from general population to multimorbidity) and (2) population with chronic disease (from patients’ underlying diseases to multimorbidity via different progressions). These exercises were unsuccessful due to insufficient data, where multiple recent studies used the same data source (eg, UK Biobank, CHARLS) to answer similar research questions—concerns over intensive analysis on open-access database have been highlighted in discussions within the science community.6970 It should also be noted that outcome ascertainment varied across studies (eg, by ICD codes, clinical testing, self-reports and surveys, physician and hospital diagnoses), which may contribute to the high heterogeneity observed. More studies conducted in diverse cohort populations with more precise and consistent outcome ascertainment are requested to detect the true effect.

Our included studies reported significant association between HAP fuel proxy and multimorbidity, but mixed evidence on sources or purposes of solid fuel burning, demonstrate the need for more detailed exposure assessment. This is important as women and children in LMICs, and poor communities are disproportionately exposed to higher concentrations of air pollutants, and increasingly vulnerable to multimorbidity.71

No study was available for assessing the association between polypharmacy induced from air pollution. Yet individuals with multimorbidity often face polypharmacy, which can impose additional burdens on health systems and personal well-being.72 Although there is little literature explicitly mentioning polypharmacy in this context, the assumption can be rightly made that individuals with air pollution-related multimorbidity, specifically cardiometabolic conditions, will use multiple medications for treatment. Given that polypharmacy could increase rate of hospitalisation, mortality73 and contributes to 0.3% of the total global health expenditure (US$42 billion),74 more research is needed to explore strategies to reduce the burden at both the individual level—through improved clinical management—and on a larger economic and social scale. In particular, future studies would require sufficiently large sample sizes (eg, large population-based datasets), robust exposure assessment and longitudinal designs to capture medication use patterns to ensure adequate statistical power.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis that assessed the association between air pollution exposure and the risk of multimorbidity and/or polypharmacy. Second, selection bias was kept minimal with a comprehensive search strategy using four different databases and a relatively broad search string. The individual article screening and data extraction ensured only high-quality, relevant studies were selected based on validated tools such as NOS, ROBINS-I and ROBINS-E for methodology quality and risk of bias assessments.

However, this study also has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, although all studies adjusted for key confounders such as body mass index, age and sex, the extent of confounder adjustment varied. Some studies accounted for additional factors such as socio-demographic status, lifestyle factors (eg, physical activity, smoking and alcohol consumption) and urban versus rural residence, while others did not. This inconsistency may have introduced residual confounding, potentially biased the results and contributed to variability. Second, methodological differences in the included studies may therefore affect the accuracy of the pooled RRs as well as the between-study heterogeneity, for example, lack of standardisation of exposure increments relevant to measure of association. Third, we found the lack of consensus in measuring and defining multimorbidity, which agrees with previous literature,75 further complicates the synthesis of findings. For example, some of our reviewed studies focused on cardiometabolic conditions only39 while others included a broader spectrum of chronic diseases.41 Since multimorbidity seems to be a relatively ‘qualitative’ measure of disease, and contains different patterns, we acknowledge that a meta-analysis to pool these together may not be as informative. Future studies are needed to focus on air pollution and different multimorbidity patterns for more clinical insights. Given the substantial heterogeneity inherent in multimorbidity, future reviews and meta-analyses should move beyond multimorbidity in aggregate to focus on specific pollutant-multimorbidity combinations, with the support of more diverse available datasets. Lastly, we did not observe associations between other pollutants (NOx, BC, SO2, O3, CO, NO) and multimorbidity, likely due to limited number of available studies and insufficient statistical power. Further research examining a wider spectrum of air pollutants, particularly gaseous pollutants, is warranted to better characterise their associations with multimorbidity.

Conclusion

Our systematic review and meta-analysis found elevated risk of multimorbidity after air pollution exposure to PM2.5 and PM10, but not other pollutants. These findings highlight the critical need for continued efforts to mitigate ambient air pollution in order to reduce the burden of multimorbidity. Future research should prioritise expanding the geographic scope of studies focusing on continents other than Europe and Asia, alternative air pollutants other than PM2.5 and NO2, rural areas, older population groups, HAP measurements and polypharmacy.
