Moving Least Squares without Quasi-Uniformity: A Stochastic Approach
Shir Tapiro Moshe, Yariv Aizenbud, Barak Sober

Abstract
Local Polynomial Regression (LPR) and Moving Least Squares (MLS) are closely related nonparametric estimation methods, developed independently in statistics and approximation theory. While statistical LPR analysis focuses on overcoming sampling noise under probabilistic assumptions, the deterministic MLS theory studies smoothness properties and convergence rates with respect to the fill-distance (a resolution parameter). Despite this similarity, the deterministic assumptions underlying MLS fail to hold under random sampling. We begin by quantifying the probabilistic behavior of the fill-distance h n h_{n} and separation δ n \delta_{n} of an i.i.d. random sample. That is, for a distribution satisfying a mild regularity condition, h n ∝ n − 1 / d log 1 / d ( n ) h_{n}\propto n^{-1/d}\log^{1/d}(n) and δ n ∝ n − 2 / d \delta_{n}\propto n^{-2/d} . We then prove that, for MLS of degree k − 1 k\!-\!1 , the approximation error associated with a differential operator Q Q of order | m | ≤ k − 1 |m|\leq k-1 decays as h n k − | m | h_{n}^{\,k-|m|} up to logarithmic factors, establishing stochastic analogues of the classical MLS estimates. Additionally, We show that the MLS approximant is smooth with high probability. Finally, we apply the stochastic MLS theory to manifold estimation. Assuming that the sampled Manifold is k k -times smooth, we show that the Hausdorff distance between the true manifold and its MLS reconstruction decays as h n k h_{n}^{k} , extending the deterministic Manifold-MLS guarantees to random samples. This work provides the first unified stochastic analysis of MLS, demonstrating that—despite the failure of deterministic sampling assumptions—the classical convergence and smoothness properties persist under natural probabilistic models.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background

Local Polynomial Regression (LPR) and Moving Least Squares (MLS) are two closely related nonparametric methods, both relying on weighted local polynomial fitting. While they emerged independently—LPR from statistics and MLS from numerical analysis—their mathematical formulations are essentially equivalent. This parallel development has led to two complementary traditions: one focused on statistical inference under random sampling, the other on numerical approximation under deterministic sampling.

In statistics, LPR was introduced as a framework for estimating functions and their derivatives from noisy observations. The method constructs a local polynomial approximation around a target point, with nearby data weighted more heavily than distant ones. Its development has been driven by classical statistical concerns such as asymptotic consistency, the bias–variance trade-off, and optimality in stochastic settings. Early work by Stone
9
established the global optimal rates of convergence for nonparametric estimators, showing how the smoothness of the underlying function and the dimension of the data determine achievable accuracy. Subsequent contributions, including those of Ruppert and Wand
7
, provided refined asymptotic analyses of bias and variance, emphasizing the role of bandwidth selection and boundary effects. Within this framework, LPR was first studied for point estimation and later extended to interval estimation, consolidating its role as a central tool in nonparametric statistics.

In parallel, MLS was developed in computational mathematics as a meshfree method for function approximation
3
. Like LPR, it employs weighted polynomial fitting, but its construction yields smooth shape functions with local support that enable continuous reconstruction across a domain. This property makes MLS especially suitable for geometric applications, such as surface reconstruction
4
, solving partial differential equations
5
, and manifold approximation
8
. The deterministic analyses of MLS rely on geometric sampling conditions, notably the notions of
fill distance
(sometimes referred to as the
covering radius
), the largest gap between sample points and the domain, and
separation
, the minimal distance between two distinct points. In this setting, the two quantities are comparable up to a constant factor, which provides a convenient framework for proving convergence and stability. Within this setting, Levin
3
demonstrated convergence rates for smooth functions, while Mirzaei
5
extended the theory to derivatives. In particular, Mirzaei showed that if one applies a differential operator of order
m

to both the MLS approximation and the true function, the difference between them decays like the fill distance raised to the power

k-m
, where

k
is the smoothness order of the function. These results underscore the strength of MLS in preserving smoothness and geometric structure under deterministic sampling assumptions.
More recently, Krieg and Sonnleitner
2
relaxed the reliance on fill distance by showing that random points on convex domains are asymptotically optimal for Sobolev approximations. This suggests that the large gaps characteristic of stochastic sampling do not prevent optimal average convergence rates.

Although LPR and MLS arose from distinct motivations—statistical inference and numerical approximation, respectively—their equivalence suggests a unified perspective. On the one hand, the global approximation framework and geometric intuition of MLS offer a natural way to extend LPR to data supported on manifolds. On the other hand, the stochastic theory of LPR provides the probabilistic tools needed to analyze MLS when data are generated randomly. Yet a fundamental obstacle arises: the deterministic assumptions underlying MLS, such as fill distance and separation, do not hold under stochastic sampling. This prevents a direct transfer of results from one framework to the other.

The main contribution of this work is to bridge this gap. The deterministic theory of MLS relies on a geometric relation between the fill distance and the separation, but these assumptions are not satisfied when the data are drawn stochastically. Within this stochastic framework, we reestablish the central approximation results of Mirzaei, thereby extending his deterministic convergence results to random samples. In particular, we show that the rate of convergence for the error between derivatives of the MLS approximation and derivatives of the true function coincides with the optimal rate established by Stone
9
in statistics. Building on this, we further extend the theory of manifold approximation by MLS—originally formulated under deterministic assumptions—to the stochastic regime, demonstrating that the same convergence rates persist.

4
Bridging the gap between deterministic and stochastic MLS

This section lays the probabilistic foundations required to rigorously compare deterministic and stochastic MLS frameworks. We begin by introducing two central definitions: the interior cone condition, which imposes a geometric regularity on the domain, and a family of distributions with well-behaved sampling properties. Under these assumptions, we establish that the convergence rate of the fill distance differs from that of the separation distance—a key distinction from the deterministic setting. We then derive properties of how the number of sample points in local neighborhoods grows with

n

A set

\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
and a radius

(6)

is contained in
\Omega
.

Remark 2
.

If the domain has no boundary, then the interior cone condition is automatically satisfied, and all proofs in the article remain valid . However, if the domain has a boundary, the interior cone condition becomes necessary. The interior cone condition (see Definition
3

\theta

(1-\rho_{\delta})^{1/n}=\exp\left(\frac{1}{n}\log(1-\rho_{\delta})\right)
. Applying the inequality
e^{-x}\geq 1-x
for
x\geq 0
, where
x=\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\rho_{\delta}}\right)
, we have:

(1-\rho_{\delta})^{1/n}\geq 1-\frac{\log(1/(1-\rho_{\delta}))}{n}.

(25)

Rearranging this inequality gives:

1-(1-\rho_{\delta})^{1/n}\leq\frac{\log(1/(1-\rho_{\delta}))}{n}.

(26)

Substituting this into (
24
), we define the constant
K_{\delta}
as:

K_{\delta}:=\left(\frac{\log(1/(1-\rho_{\delta}))}{c^{\prime}}\right)^{1/d}.

(27)

It follows that for all
n\geq 1
:

\varepsilon(n)\leq K_{\delta}\,n^{-2/d}.

(28)

Consequently, substituting this bound back into (
22
), we obtain:

P\left(\delta_{n}<K_{\delta}\,n^{-2/d}\right)\leq\rho_{\delta},\quad\forall n\geq 1.

(29)

By the definition of order in probability for lower bounds, this strictly implies:

\delta_{n}=\Omega_{P}(n^{-2/d}).

(30)

∎

Corollary 1
.

Since
h_{n}=O_{P}(n^{-\frac{1}{d}}\ln^{\frac{1}{d}}(n))
and
\delta_{n}=\Omega_{P}(n^{-\frac{2}{d}})
we get that the quasi-uniform property does not hold (see Definition
2
).

Corollary
1
poses a challenge, as the quasi-uniform property is a common assumption, particularly in the deterministic MLS method. Consequently, the proofs by Levin
3
, Davoud
5
, and Wendland
11
cannot be directly applied to a stochastically generated data set, necessitating modifications to their arguments.
Our proposed solution is to prove Theorem
2
, assuming that the stochastic data is well behaved distribution, as defined in Definition
4
.

As a preliminary step, we first establish a fundamental lemma that captures a key asymptotic property of the sample size as
n\rightarrow\infty
, which underpins each of these results.

Lemma 3
.

Let
D_{n}
be a ball of radius
R_{n}=C_{D}\cdot\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{1/d}
, centered at some point
x\in\Omega
and let

N_{D_{n}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i}\in D_{n}\}}

denote the number of sample points lying in
D_{n}
. Then, there exist finite constants
0<\gamma_{1}\leq\gamma_{2}
such that for some constant
c>0
,

P\left(\gamma_{1}\log n\ \leq\ N_{D_{n}}\ \leq\ \gamma_{2}\log n\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c}},

that is,

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\left(\gamma_{1}\log n\ \leq\ N_{D_{n}}\ \leq\ \gamma_{2}\log n\right)=1.

Proof.

Let
N_{D_{n}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i}\in D_{n}\}}
. Each indicator variable
\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i}\in D_{n}\}}
is Bernoulli with success probability
p_{n}=P(x_{i}\in D_{n})
. Then
N_{D_{n}}\sim\text{Bin}(n,p_{n})
, and:

\mathbb{E}N_{D_{n}}=np_{n}=n\int_{D_{n}}p(x)dx.

Since
D_{n}
is a ball of radius
R_{n}=C_{D}\cdot\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{1/d}
, its volume is

\operatorname{Vol}(D_{n})\propto R_{n}^{d}\propto\frac{\log n}{n}.

Because
p(x)
is bounded below by a constant
\underline{c}>0
on its support, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}N_{D_{n}}\geq n\cdot\int_{D_{n}}\underline{c}\,dx=\underline{c}\cdot n\cdot\operatorname{Vol}(D_{n})\geq c_{1}\cdot\log n

(31)

for some constant
c_{1}>0
. Likewise, since
p(x)\leq\bar{c}
, we also get:

\mathbb{E}N_{D_{n}}\leq\bar{c}\cdot n\cdot\operatorname{Vol}(D_{n})\leq c_{2}\cdot\log n.

(32)

We now apply the Chernoff bound: If
X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)
with
\mu=\mathbb{E}X
, then for any
0<\delta<1
,

P\left(|X-\mu|\geq\delta\mu\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}\mu}{3}\right).

Applying this to
N_{D_{n}}
with
\mu=\mathbb{E}N_{D_{n}}
, we obtain:

P\left(\left|N_{D_{n}}-\mathbb{E}N_{D_{n}}\right|\geq\delta\mathbb{E}N_{D_{n}}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-c\log n\right)=\frac{2}{n^{c}},

for some constant
c>0
. Thus,

P\left((1-\delta)\mathbb{E}N_{D_{n}}\leq N_{D_{n}}\leq(1+\delta)\mathbb{E}N_{D_{n}}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c}}.

(33)

Denote
\gamma_{1}=(1-\delta)c_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}=(1+\delta)c_{2}
, and use the inequalities in (
31
),(
32
) we obtain that

P\left(\gamma_{1}\log n\ \leq\ N_{D_{n}}\ \leq\ \gamma_{2}\log n\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c}},

(34)

which implies that

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\left(\gamma_{1}\log n\ \leq\ N_{D_{n}}\ \leq\ \gamma_{2}\log n\right)=1.

∎

We denote by
\mathscr{E}_{I}
the event

\mathscr{E}_{I}:=\{\#I_{B(\hat{x},sh_{n})}\in[\gamma_{1}\log n,\gamma_{2}\log n]\}.

(35)

We wish to emphasize an important point. As the total number of samples increases, the number of samples contained in any fixed ball tends to infinity. Consequently, the quasi-uniformity property fails. This is precisely the obstruction that prevents a direct application of the techniques developed in
3
,
5
,
11
, and
8
.
In the stochastic setting, however, this phenomenon is not only unavoidable but also advantageous, since it is natural for the quality of the approximation to improve as the number of samples grows. By adapting the proof techniques to accommodate this behavior, together with our results, the difficulty is resolved, and the conclusions obtained in the deterministic framework can be retained.

Proof of Theorem

We first establish Lemma

, which is a refined version of Theorem
2
. In this lemma, the analysis is carried out for individual partial derivatives of fixed order, represented by multi-index derivatives. This allows us to treat each derivative separately and obtain precise bounds. We then derive Theorem
2
as a direct consequence by applying the lemma to general linear differential operators with constant coefficients.

Lemma 4
.

Let
X_{n}
be a sample set of size
n
drawn from a nicely behaving distribution
(
Definition
4
)
on
\Omega
, which satisfies the interior cone condition
(
Definition

)
.
Let
F_{n}
be the corresponding sampled values of some function

, and let
s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(x)
be the value of the
(k-1)
degree MLS function at a point

, then for any
\hat{x}\in\Omega

P\left(\left|\partial^{\alpha_{0}}s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})-\partial^{\alpha_{0}}f(\hat{x})\right|\leq\mathcal{K}\cdot h_{n}^{k-|\alpha_{0}|}\cdot\log(n)\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c}}-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}},

(36)

i.e.,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\left|\partial^{\alpha_{0}}s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})-\partial^{\alpha_{0}}f(\hat{x})\right|\leq\mathcal{K}\cdot h_{n}^{k-|\alpha_{0}|}\cdot\log(n)\right)=1,

(37)

where
\mathcal{K}
is a positive constant depends on
\varepsilon
,
|\alpha_{0}|\leq k-1
is some multi-index, and
c,c^{\prime},c_{\mathcal{Q}}
are some positive constants.

Proof.

Given a set of scattered data points
X_{n}=\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the neighborhood index set around a fixed but arbitrary point
\hat{x}\in\Omega
:

I_{B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)}=\left\{i:x_{i}\in B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)\right\},

where
B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x-\hat{x}\|\leq sh_{n}\right\}
. In the following, we work under the assumption that event
\mathscr{E}_{I}
hold, i.e., by (
34
), the expected number of samples in the index set

I_{B(\hat{x},sh_{n})}
is contained in
[\gamma_{1}\log n,\gamma_{2}\log n]
with probability at least
1-\frac{2}{n^{c}}
.

In
3
it was shown that the MLS approximation is given by

s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(x)=\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)}}a_{i}^{*}(x)f\left(x_{i}\right),

where the coefficients
a_{i}^{*}(x)
are called

, and obtained by minimizing the quadratic form

a_{i}^{*}(x)=\arg\min_{a_{i}(x)}\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)}}a_{i}(x)^{2}\frac{1}{\theta_{h}\left(x_{i}-x\right)},

under the constraints

\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)}}a_{i}(x)p\left(x_{i}\right)=p(x),\quad\forall p\in\Pi_{k-1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right),

and
\theta_{h}(x_{i}-x)
is a weight function satisfies the properties in (
1
).
Let
A
denote the number of multi-indices
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
with nonnegative entries such that
|\alpha|\leq k
.
It is shown in
5
that the shape functions can be expressed in the following form:

a_{i}^{*}(x)=\theta_{h}(x_{i}-x)\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k-1}\eta_{\alpha}(x)\frac{\left(x_{i}-x\right)^{\alpha}}{h_{n}^{|\alpha|}},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n.

(38)

where
\eta_{\alpha}(x)
are the components of
\eta(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{A}
, the solution of the positive definite system

\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)\eta(x)=\boldsymbol{p}(x),

\boldsymbol{p}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{A}
is the vector whose components are
p_{\alpha}(x)
,

p_{\alpha}(x)=\left\{\frac{(x-\hat{x})^{\alpha}}{h_{n}^{|\alpha|}}\right\}_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq k-1},

and
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{A\times A}
is a matrix with entries
\mathscr{A}_{n\alpha\beta}(x)
where,

\mathscr{A}_{n\alpha\beta}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{h}(x_{i}-x)p_{\alpha}(x)\cdot p_{\beta}(x),\quad\alpha,\beta=1,\ldots,A,

which equals,

\mathscr{A}_{n\alpha\beta}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{h}(x_{i}-x)\frac{(x_{i}-x)^{\alpha}}{h_{n}^{|\alpha|}}\cdot\frac{(x_{i}-x)^{\beta}}{h_{n}^{|\beta|}},\quad\alpha,\beta=1,\ldots,A,

(39)

with
\eta(x),\boldsymbol{p}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{A}
.
To illustrate the approximation of a function by the stochastic MLS operator, we follow Levin’s approach
3
. Given an arbitrary
\hat{x}\in\Omega
, we define the MLS error as

E_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})=s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})-f(\hat{x}).

By adding and subtracting a polynomial
p(x)
expressed in the monomial basis scaled by
h_{n}
and centered at
\hat{x}
, we get

E_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})=\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)}}a_{i}^{*}(\hat{x})\left(f\left(x_{i}\right)-p\left(x_{i}\right)\right)+p(\hat{x})-f(\hat{x}).

(40)

Denote by
p_{f}
the taylor polynomial of
f(x)
around
\hat{x}
. We have:

\max_{x\in B(\hat{x},sh_{n})}|f(x)-p_{f}(x)|\leq C|f|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}\left(B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)\right)}h_{n}^{k},

where the seminorm,
|f|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}\left(B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)\right)}
, is defined by

|f|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}\left(B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)\right)}\triangleq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\alpha|=k\\
x\in B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)\end{subarray}}\left|\partial^{\alpha}f(x)\right|,

with
\alpha=\left(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}\right)
denoting a multi-index of order
|\alpha|=k
, and

\partial^{\alpha}f(x)=\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\partial_{x_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots\partial_{x_{d}}^{\alpha_{d}}f(x).

Using
p_{f}
as the polynomial in Equation
40
we get

\left|E_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})\right|\leq C|f|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}\left(B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)\right)}h_{n}^{k}.

Since the focus is on measuring the error in the derivatives, we obtain

\partial^{\alpha}E_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})=\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(\hat{x},h_{n}\right)}}\left(\partial^{\alpha}a_{i}^{*}(\hat{x})\right)\left(f\left(x_{i}\right)-p_{f}\left(x_{i}\right)\right)+\partial^{\alpha}(p_{f}(x)-f(x)).

Note that in the current case,
\partial^{\alpha}
is scalar-valued as
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Then, for a fixed but arbitrary
\hat{x}\in\Omega
we get

\left|\partial^{\alpha}E_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})\right|\leq\max_{x\in B(\hat{x},sh_{n})}|f(x)-p_{f}(x)|\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(\hat{x},sh_{n}\right)}}\left|\partial^{\alpha}a_{i}^{*}(\hat{x})\right|+\left|\partial^{\alpha}p_{f}(x)-\partial^{\alpha}f(x)\right|.

(41)

Since
p_{f}
is the Taylor expansion of
f(x)
at
\hat{x}
and
\|\hat{x}-x\|\leq sh_{n}
we get that

\left|\partial^{\alpha}p_{f}(x)-\partial^{\alpha}f(x)\right|\leq c_{1}|f|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}(B(\hat{x},sh_{n}))}h_{n}^{k-|\alpha|}.

(42)

Given the event
\mathscr{E}_{I}
hold, we showed in Lemma
9
that for all
x\in B(\hat{x},sh_{n})
,

P\left(\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(x,sh_{n}\right)}}\left|\partial^{\alpha}a_{i}^{*}(x)\right|\leq C_{\alpha}^{\prime}\cdot\log(n)\cdot h_{n}^{-|\alpha|}\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}},

(43)

where
C^{\prime}_{\alpha}
depends on
\varepsilon
.
Hence the probability of the event in (
43
) intersects with
\mathscr{E}_{I}
is bounded by,

P\left(\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(x,sh_{n}\right)}}\left|\partial^{\alpha}a_{i}^{*}(x)\right|\leq C_{\alpha}^{\prime}\cdot\log(n)\cdot h_{n}^{-|\alpha|}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c}}-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}}.

(44)

Substituting the bounds from Equations (
42

43

41

\max_{x\in B(\hat{x},h_{n})}|f(x)-p_{f}(x)|
is bounded by

up to a positive constant (Taylor remainder estimate), we evaluate at
\hat{x}
for a specific
\alpha_{0}
, and we obtain that for some positive constant
\mathcal{K}
depends on

P\left(\left|\partial^{\alpha_{0}}s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})-\partial^{\alpha_{0}}f(\hat{x})\right|\leq\mathcal{K}\cdot h_{n}^{k-|\alpha_{0}|}\cdot\log(n)\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c}}-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}}.

That is,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\left|\partial^{\alpha_{0}}s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})-\partial^{\alpha_{0}}f(\hat{x})\right|\leq\mathcal{K}\cdot h_{n}^{k-|\alpha_{0}|}\cdot\log(n)\right)=1,

as desired.

Proof of
Theorem
2
.

Let
Q
be a linear differential operator of order
m
, which can be written as

Q=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}q_{\alpha}\,\partial^{\alpha},

where the coefficients
q_{\alpha}
are real constants. Let

M_{Q}:=\{\alpha:\,|\alpha|\leq m,\ q_{\alpha}\neq 0\},

which is a finite index set.
Define the vector
\mathscr{Q}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{\#(M_{Q})}
by

(\mathscr{Q}_{n})_{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha!\,q_{\alpha}}{h_{n}^{|\alpha|}},\qquad\alpha\in M_{Q}.

With this notation, the operator
Q
can be expressed as

Q=\mathscr{Q}_{n}^{\prime}\,\partial^{\alpha}.

By Lemma
4
, for each fixed
\alpha\in M_{Q}
,

P\!\left(\left|\partial^{\alpha}s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})-\partial^{\alpha}f(\hat{x})\right|\geq\mathcal{K}\,h_{n}^{k-|\alpha|}\log n\right)\leq\frac{2}{n^{c}}+\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}+\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}}.

Applying the union bound over the finite set
M_{Q}
, we obtain

\displaystyle P\!\left(\max_{\alpha\in M_{Q}}\left|\partial^{\alpha}s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})-\partial^{\alpha}f(\hat{x})\right|\geq\mathcal{K}\,h_{n}^{k-m}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{\alpha\in M_{Q}}P\!\left(\left|\partial^{\alpha}s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})-\partial^{\alpha}f(\hat{x})\right|\geq\mathcal{K}\,h_{n}^{k-|\alpha|}\log n\right)\leq\frac{2}{n^{c}}+\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}+\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}},

since
\#(M_{Q})
is finite.
Consequently,

P\!\left(\left|Qs^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})-Qf(\hat{x})\right|\geq\mathcal{K}\,h_{n}^{k-m}\log n\right)\leq\frac{2}{n^{c}}+\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}+\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}}

i.e.,

P\!\left(\left|Qs^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(\hat{x})-Qf(\hat{x})\right|\geq\mathcal{K}\,h_{n}^{k-m}\log n\right)\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\;0,

which proves the theorem.
∎

Proof.

Let
B\left(\hat{x},h_{n}\right)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x-\hat{x}\|\leq h_{n}\right\}

is the fill distance as defined in Theorem

Thus, the MLS function can be written as

s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(x)=\sum_{\alpha\in A}\hat{b}_{n\alpha}(x)p_{\alpha}(x),

(47)

where
A
denotes the collection of all

\alpha
of nonnegative integers such that

. The expression
\hat{b}_{n\alpha}(x)
is the
\alpha

\hat{b}_{n}(x)=\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)\mathscr{X}_{n}^{\top}(x)F_{n},

(48)

where
\mathscr{X}_{n}(x)

are defined as follows:
Using our basis defined in Equation (

\tilde{\mathscr{X}}_{n}(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\sqrt{\theta_{1}}p_{\alpha_{0}}(X_{1})&\sqrt{\theta_{1}}p_{\alpha_{1}}(X_{1})&\cdots&\sqrt{\theta_{1}}p_{\alpha_{|A|}}(X_{1})\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
\sqrt{\theta_{n}}p_{\alpha_{0}}(X_{n})&\sqrt{\theta_{n}}p_{\alpha_{1}}(X_{n})&\cdots&\sqrt{\theta_{n}}p_{\alpha_{|A|}}(X_{n})\end{array}\right),

which leads to the solution

\hat{b}_{n}(x)=(\tilde{\mathscr{X}}_{n}(x)^{\top}\tilde{\mathscr{X}}_{n}(x))^{-1}\tilde{\mathscr{X}}_{n}(x)^{\top}F_{n}(x).

Next, we normalize the design matrix by rescaling it:

\mathscr{X}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{N_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\tilde{\mathscr{X}}_{n}(x),

\mathscr{X}_{ni\alpha}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{\theta_{h}(x_{i}-x)}}{N_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\frac{\left(x_{i}-x\right)^{\alpha}}{h_{n}^{|\alpha|}},&i\in I_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}\text{ and }\alpha\in A,\\

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\cdot N_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}^{2}\cdot\mathscr{X}_{n}(x)^{\top}\mathscr{X}_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=N_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}\cdot\mathscr{X}_{n}(x)^{\top}\mathscr{X}_{n}(x).

\mathscr{A}_{n\alpha\beta}(x)=\frac{1}{N_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\sum_{I_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\theta_{h}(x_{i}-x)\frac{\left(x_{i}-x\right)^{\alpha}\left(x_{i}-x\right)^{\beta}}{h_{n}^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}}.

\mathscr{X}_{n}(x)

) expressed in the polynomial basis.
Define the normalized weight function by

w(x_{i}-x)=\frac{\theta_{h}(x_{i}-x)}{1/h_{n}\int_{B(0,sh_{n})}\theta_{h}(u-x)du}=\frac{\Phi(x_{i}/h_{n}-x/h_{n})}{1/h_{n}\int_{B(0,sh_{n})}\Phi(u/h_{n}-x/h_{n})du},

using change of variables with
\left[t=\frac{u-x}{h_{n}},u-x=th_{n},du=h_{n}dt\right]
we obtain,

=\frac{\Phi(t_{i})}{1/h_{n}\int_{t\in B(0,s)}\Phi(t)h_{n}dt}=\frac{\Phi(t_{i})}{\int_{t\in B(0,s)}\Phi(t)dt}.

where
t_{i}=\frac{x_{i}-x}{h_{n}}
.
Therefore,

w(x_{i}-x)=\frac{\Phi(t_{i})}{\int_{t\in B(0,s)}\Phi(t)dt},

(50)

so that
w(x)
is a distribution over the ball
B(0,sh)
.
Hence, the normalized empirical matrix is given by

\mathscr{A}_{n\alpha\beta}^{w}(x)=N_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}\sum_{I_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}w(x_{i}-x)\mathscr{X}_{ni\alpha}(x)\mathscr{X}_{ni\beta}(x).

Hence,

\mathscr{A}^{w}_{n}=\mathscr{A}_{n}\cdot\frac{1}{\int_{t\in B(0,s)}\Phi(t)dt}.

(51)

Define the
A\times A
matrix
\mathscr{A}^{w}
by

\mathscr{A}^{w}_{\alpha\beta}={\int_{|z-x|\leq s}(z-x)^{\alpha}(z-x)^{\beta}w(z-x)dz},

Then
\operatorname{det}\mathscr{A}^{w}_{\alpha\beta}>0
. Notably, it is essential to recognize that
\mathscr{A}^{w}
is the weighted covariance matrix of the transform and that the matrices
\mathscr{A}(x)
and
\mathscr{A}^{w}_{n}(x)
both serve as covariance matrices of the basis functions, with
\mathscr{A}^{w}_{n}(x)
being a scaled version of
\mathscr{A}(x)
that approximates the theoretical weighted covariance matrix
\mathscr{A}^{w}
. The primary difference lies in the normalization factors,
N_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}
and
\frac{1}{\int_{t\in B(0,s)}\Phi(t-t_{i})dt}
, making them equivalent up to a multiplicative constant.

By matrix Bernstein inequality, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

P\left(\left|\mathscr{A}_{na\beta}^{w}(x)-\mathscr{A}^{w}_{\alpha\beta}\right|\leq\varepsilon\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I},\,\forall x\in\Omega\text{ and }\alpha,\beta\in A\right)\geq 1-2e^{-c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}\log(n)}=1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}},

where
c^{\prime}
is a positive constant. So if we denote
C^{\Phi}=\int_{t\in B(0,s)}\Phi(t)dt
, then by Equation (
51
)

P\left(\left|\mathscr{A}_{na\beta}(x)-C^{\Phi}\cdot\mathscr{A}^{w}_{\alpha\beta}\right|\leq\varepsilon\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I},\,\forall x\in\Omega\text{ and }\alpha,\beta\in A\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}.

(52)

Now, we claim that for all
|\alpha|\leq k
, there exists a constant
C_{\alpha}
such that

\left|\partial^{\alpha}w(x_{i}-x)\right|\leq C_{\alpha}h_{n}^{-|\alpha|},\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

(53)

Since
\Phi(x)
is a compactly supported and
C^{k}
function, all of its derivatives up to order
k
are continuous and bounded (see Lemma
6
).
Combining Equations (
52
) and (
53
) together implies that

P(|\partial^{\alpha_{0}}\mathscr{A}_{n}({x})-C_{\alpha}h_{n}^{-|\alpha_{0}|}|\leq\varepsilon\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I}\,\,\forall\,\,x\in\Omega\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\alpha,\beta\in A)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}.

(54)

∎

Next, we bound the derivatives of the weight function used in the MLS formulation. This is necessary for controlling the growth of the entries in
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
and related expressions.

Lemma 6
.

For
w(x_{i}-x)
a weighted function as defined in (
50
), we have

\left|\partial^{\alpha}w(x_{i}-x)\right|\leq C_{\alpha}h_{n}^{-|\alpha|},\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

Proof.

Applying the chain rule to
w(x)
, we differentiate:

\partial^{\alpha}w(x_{i}-x)=\partial^{\alpha}\left(\frac{\Phi(x_{i}/h_{n}-x/h_{n})}{\int_{t\in B(0,s)}\Phi(x_{i}/h_{n}-u/h_{n})du}\right).

Since the denominator is independent of
x
, differentiation only affects the numerator. Using the chain rule,

\partial^{\alpha}\Phi\left(\frac{x}{h_{n}}\right)=h_{n}^{-|\alpha|}\left(\partial^{\alpha}\Phi\right)\left(\frac{x}{h_{n}}\right).

Thus,

\partial^{\alpha}w(x_{i}-x)=\frac{h_{n}^{-|\alpha|}\left(\partial^{\alpha}\Phi\right)(x_{i}/h_{n}-x/h_{n})}{\int_{t\in B(0,s)}\Phi(x_{i}/h_{n}-u/h_{n})du}.

Since
\Phi
and its derivatives are bounded, there exists a constant
C_{\alpha}^{\prime}
such that

\left|\left(\partial^{\alpha}\Phi\right)(x_{i}/h_{n}-x/h_{n})\right|\leq C_{\alpha}^{\prime}.

Therefore,

\left|\partial^{\alpha}w(x_{i}-x)\right|\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}^{\prime}h_{n}^{-|\alpha|}}{\int_{t\in B(0,s)}\Phi(x_{i}/h_{n}-u/h_{n})du}.

The denominator is independent of
x
and is just a normalization factor ensuring
w(x_{i}-x)
integrates to 1 . Then if we denote
C_{\alpha}=\frac{C^{\prime}_{\alpha}}{\int_{t\in B(0,s)}\Phi(x_{i}/h_{n}-u/h_{n})du}
we obtain

\left|\partial^{\alpha}w(x_{i}-x)\right|\leq C_{\alpha}h_{n}^{-|\alpha|}.

∎

To ensure the stability of the MLS solution, we need a lower bound on the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
, which guarantees invertibility with high probability.

Lemma 7
.

Given the event
\mathscr{E}_{I}
, there exists a constant
C_{\lambda}>0
, such that for every
0<\varepsilon<C_{\lambda}

P\left(\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)\right)\geq C_{\lambda}-\varepsilon\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I},\,\,\forall x\in\Omega\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}},

where
c_{\mathcal{Q}}
is a real positive constant and
\lambda_{\text{min }}\left(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)\right)
is the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
. In particular,
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
is positive definite and full rank for all sufficiently small
h_{n}
.

Proof.

We want to prove that
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
is uniformly positive definite, i.e., its smallest eigenvalue is bounded away from zero independently of

n
, given
1
,
49
and under Lemma
3
.

Let
p=\left(p_{\alpha}\right)_{\alpha\in A}
be a unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{A}
, i.e.,
\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}^{2}=1
. Define the polynomial:

P(z)=\sum_{\alpha\in A}p_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}

Let us define the rescaled coordinates:

t_{i}:=\frac{x_{i}-x}{h_{n}},\quad\text{ so that }\quad x_{i}=x+h_{n}t_{i},

Now define the empirical quadratic form:

\mathcal{Q}_{n}(p):=p^{\top}\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)p=\frac{1}{N_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\theta_{h}\left(x_{i}-x\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\frac{\left(x_{i}-x\right)^{\alpha}}{h_{n}^{|\alpha|}}\right)^{2}.

Substituting
t_{i}=\frac{x_{i}-x}{h_{n}}
, and using
\theta_{h}\left(x_{i}-x\right)=\Phi\left(t_{i}\right)
, we get:

\mathcal{Q}_{n}(p)=\frac{1}{N_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\Phi\left(t_{i}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}t_{i}^{\alpha}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{N_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\Phi\left(t_{i}\right)\cdot\tilde{P}\left(t_{i}\right)^{2},

where
\tilde{P}(t)=\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}t^{\alpha}
.
By Lemma
3
, the samples
\left\{t_{i}\right\}_{i\in I_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}
inside the rescaled ball
B(0,s)
become dense as
n\rightarrow\infty
with high probability, i.e., for any fixed polynomial
p
, the empirical quadratic form

\mathcal{Q}_{n}(p)=\frac{1}{N_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(x,h_{n}\right)}}\Phi\left(t_{i}\right)\cdot\tilde{P}\left(t_{i}\right)^{2},

satisfies with high probability

\mathcal{Q}_{n}(p)\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}(p):=\int_{B(0,s)}\Phi(t)\cdot\tilde{P}(t)^{2}dt.

More precisely, by Hoffding’s inequality, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

P\left(\left|\mathcal{Q}_{n}(p)-\mathcal{Q}(p)\right|\leq\varepsilon\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I}\right)\geq 1-2e^{-c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}\log n}=1-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}},

where
c_{\mathcal{Q}}
is a positive real constant.
Now we show that
\mathcal{Q}(p)
is strictly positive.
Suppose, for contradiction, that
\inf_{p}\mathcal{Q}(p)=0
. Then there is a sequence of polynomials
P_{m}(t)=

\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}^{(m)}t^{\alpha}
with
\left\|p^{(m)}\right\|=1
, such that
Q\left(P_{m}\right)\rightarrow 0
, i.e.,

\int_{B(0,s)}\Phi(t)\cdot P_{m}(t)^{2}dt\rightarrow 0.

Since
\Phi(t)>0
on
B(0,s)
, this implies
P_{m}\rightarrow P
in
L^{2}(B(0,s))
, and by compactness (unit sphere is compact in finite-dimensional space), there is a limit point
P\neq 0
such that

\int_{B(0,s)}P(t)^{2}dt=0,

which implies
P\equiv 0
, contradicting
\|p\|=1
. Hence, there exists a constant
C_{\lambda}
such that

\mathcal{Q}(p)\geq C_{\lambda}>0\quad\text{ for all unit }p.

Now, recall that by definition:

\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)\right)=\inf_{\|p\|=1}\mathcal{Q}_{n}(p).

Therefore, by combining the deterministic positivity of
\mathcal{Q}(p)
with the stochastic convergence of
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(p)
to
\mathcal{Q}_{p}
we conclude that for every
0<\varepsilon<C_{\lambda}
,

P\left(\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)\right)\geq C_{\lambda}-\varepsilon\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I},\,\,\forall x\in\Omega\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}},

which means the minimum eigenvalue of
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
is bounded away from zero with high probability.
∎

Having established the invertibility of
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
and bounds on its derivatives, we now derive corresponding bounds for the derivatives of its inverse. This result relies on the two previous Lemmas
5
and
7
.

Lemma 8
.

Given the event
\mathscr{E}_{I}
hold, and under the assumptions of Lemma
5
and Lemma
7
, then for all
0<\varepsilon<C_{\lambda}
, and
\forall x\in\Omega\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\alpha,\beta\in A,

P\left(\left|\partial^{\alpha_{0}}\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)\right|\leq C_{\alpha_{0}}h_{n}^{-\left|\alpha_{0}\right|}\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}},

(55)

where
C_{\alpha_{0}}
is a positive constant depends on
\varepsilon
but independent of
h_{n}
.

Proof.

We consider the matrix identity

\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)=I.

Differentiating both sides with respect to a multi-index
\alpha_{0}
and evaluating at a point
x\in\Omega
, we obtain

\partial^{\alpha_{0}}\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)=-\sum_{\beta<\alpha_{0}}\binom{\alpha_{0}}{\beta}\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)\left(\partial^{\alpha_{0}-\beta}\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)\right)\left(\partial^{\beta}\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)\right).

We now prove the desire result by induction on
\left|\alpha_{0}\right|
.

Starting with the base case:
\left(\left|\alpha_{0}\right|=1\right)
.
Let
\alpha_{0}=e_{j}
, the unit multi-index in the
j
th direction.
Then,

\partial^{e_{j}}\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)=-\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)\left(\partial^{e_{j}}\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)\right)\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x).

From Lemma
5
we have,

P\left(|\partial^{e_{j}}\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)-C_{\alpha}h_{n}^{-1}|\leq\varepsilon\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}},

and from Lemma
7
, we know that given the event
\mathscr{E}_{I}
, the matrix
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
is a positive definite with probability at least
1-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}}
, hence:

P\left(\left\|\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{C_{\lambda}-\varepsilon}\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}}.

(56)

Using the standard bound
|M|\leq C\|M\|_{2}
for matrices, we conclude:

P\left(|\partial^{e_{j}}\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)|\leq C_{e_{j}}h_{n}^{-1}\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}},

where
C_{e_{j}}h_{n}^{-1}=\frac{C_{\alpha}h_{n}^{-1}+\varepsilon}{(C_{\lambda}-\varepsilon)^{2}}
.
This establishes the base case.
Next, the inductive step is
assume that for all multi-indices
\beta<\alpha_{0}
, we have for all
x\in\Omega

P\left(|\partial^{\beta}\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)|\leq C_{\beta}h_{n}^{-|\beta|}\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}}.

(57)

Now we prove the inductive step. From the derivative identity we obtain:

\left|\partial^{\alpha_{0}}\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)\right|\leq\sum_{\beta<\alpha_{0}}\binom{\alpha_{0}}{\beta}\left\|\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)\right\|_{2}\cdot\left|\partial^{\alpha_{0}-\beta}\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)\right|\cdot\left|\partial^{\beta}\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)\right|.

(58)

Using the induction hypothesis (
57
) together with Equations (
56
),(
58
), we obtain that,

P\left(|\partial^{\alpha_{0}}\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}({x})|\leq C_{\alpha_{0}}h_{n}^{-|\alpha_{0}|}\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}},

where
C_{\alpha_{0}}(\varepsilon)\sim\frac{1}{\left(C_{\lambda}-\varepsilon\right)^{k+1}}
. This completes the inductive argument, as desired.

∎

Finally, we use the preceding bounds to control the derivatives of the shape functions
a_{i}^{*}(x)
themselves. This establishes a uniform stability estimate essential for proving convergence in the MLS approximation.
The following Lemma is the stochastic version of Theorem 3.11 in
5
.

Lemma 9
.

Given that the event
\mathscr{E}_{I}
holds, then for all
0<\varepsilon<C_{\lambda}

the MLS shape functions
a^{*}_{i}
of (
38
) have the following stability condition for
|\alpha|\leq k-1
and for all
x\in\Omega
,

P\left(\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(x,sh_{n}\right)}}\left|\partial^{\alpha}a_{i}^{*}(x)\right|\leq C_{\alpha}^{\prime}\cdot\log(n)\cdot h_{n}^{-|\alpha|}\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}},

(59)

where
C^{\prime}_{\alpha}\sim\frac{1}{\left(C_{\lambda}-\varepsilon\right)^{|\alpha|+1}}
is independent of
x
, and provided that all assumptions of Lemma
6
are satisfied.

Proof.

Recall the stochastic MLS shape functions (
38
) are given by

a_{i}^{*}(x)=\theta_{h}(x_{i}-x)\sum_{|\beta|\leq k-1}\eta_{\beta}(x)\cdot\frac{\left(x_{i}-x\right)^{\beta}}{h_{n}^{|\beta|}}.

(60)

We start with bounding the derivatives of
\eta(x)=\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)^{-1}\boldsymbol{p}(x)
.

\partial^{\alpha}\eta(x)=\sum_{\zeta\leq\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\zeta}\partial^{\alpha-\zeta}\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)\cdot\partial^{\zeta}\boldsymbol{p}(x)\quad\forall\alpha\text{ with }|\alpha|\leqslant k-1.

Now, by the definition of the scaled polynomial basis, we have:

\partial^{\zeta}\boldsymbol{p}(x)=\zeta!\cdot h_{n}^{-|\zeta|}\cdot e_{\zeta},

where
e_{\zeta}
is the canonical basis vector in multi-index ordering (i.e., the standard basis vector with a 1 in the position corresponding to multi-index
\zeta
, and zeros elsewhere).
By Lemma
8
, with probability of at least
1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}}
, the inverse matrix satisfies

\left|\partial^{\gamma}\mathscr{A}_{n}^{-1}(x)\right|\leq C_{\gamma}h_{n}^{-|\gamma|},

where
C^{\prime}_{\gamma}\sim\frac{1}{\left(C_{\lambda}-\varepsilon\right)^{|\gamma|+1}}

Hence, with the same probability, for a constant matrix
C_{\alpha,\zeta}
:

\left|\partial^{\alpha}\eta(x)\right|\leq\sum_{\zeta\leq\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\zeta}{C_{\alpha,\zeta}h_{n}^{-|\alpha-\zeta|}}\cdot e_{\zeta}h_{n}^{-|\zeta|}\leq C_{\alpha}h_{n}^{-|\alpha|},

(61)

where the vector
C_{\alpha}
is a bound for
\sum_{\zeta\leqslant\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\zeta}C_{\alpha,\zeta}e_{\zeta}
.
Taking the derivatives of both sides in Equation
60
and using the product rule, we have,

\partial^{\alpha}a_{i}^{*}(x)=\sum_{\zeta\leq\alpha}\binom{\alpha}{\zeta}\partial^{\alpha-\zeta}\theta_{h}(x_{i}-x)\sum_{|\beta|\leq k-1}\partial^{\zeta}\eta_{\beta}(x)\cdot\frac{\left(x_{i}-x\right)^{\beta}}{h_{n}^{|\beta|}}.

By Lemma
6
, the weight functions satisfy:

\left|\partial^{\alpha}\theta_{h}(x_{i}-x)\right|\leq C_{\alpha,\theta}h_{n}^{-|\alpha|},

and since
x_{i}\in B\left(x,sh_{n}\right)
, we have
\left|\left(x_{i}-x\right)^{\beta}\right|\leq\left(sh_{n}\right)^{|\beta|}
. Applying the latter in addition to the previous bounds, we get that with probability of at least
1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}}
,

\left|\partial^{\alpha}a_{i}^{*}(x)\right|\leqslant\sum_{\zeta\leqslant\alpha}\left\{\binom{\alpha}{\zeta}C_{\alpha,\zeta}h_{n}^{|\zeta|-|\alpha|}\sum_{|\beta|\leqslant k-1}C_{\alpha}h_{n}^{-|\zeta|}h_{n}^{-|\beta|}h_{n}^{|\beta|}\right\}\leqslant C_{\alpha}h_{n}^{-|\alpha|}.

Apply Lemma
3
yields that for all
x\in\Omega
and for all
0<\varepsilon<C_{\lambda}
,

P\left(\sum_{i\in I_{B\left(x,sh_{n}\right)}}\left|\partial^{\alpha}a_{i}^{*}(x)\right|\leq C_{\alpha}^{\prime}\cdot\log(n)\cdot h_{n}^{-|\alpha|}\Big|\mathscr{E}_{I}\right)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{c^{\prime}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{2}{n^{c_{\mathcal{Q}}\varepsilon^{2}}},

(62)

where
C^{\prime}_{\alpha}\sim\frac{1}{\left(C_{\lambda}-\varepsilon\right)^{|\alpha|+1}}
, as desired.
∎

6
Smoothness of the Approximation

In this section, we show that the MLS approximation
s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(x)
inherits smoothness from the weight function and the local sample configuration.
The structural assumptions on the point cloud ensure that, with high probability, each local neighborhood contains sufficiently many well-distributed points.
This guarantees that the local least-squares matrix
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
is full-rank and smoothly invertible.
Together with the weight function
\theta_{h}\in\mathcal{C}^{k}
, this implies that the shape functions
a_{i}^{*}(x)
inherit the same smoothness.
Consequently, the MLS approximation
s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(x)
, expressed as a linear combination of the sampled values
f(x_{i})
with these smooth weights, belongs to
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\Omega)
once the theorem is applied.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
\theta_{h}\in\mathcal{C}^{k}
. We show that, with probability one as
n\to\infty
, the MLS approximation
s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(x)
belongs to
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\Omega)
.

Set
\varepsilon=\frac{C_{\lambda}}{10}
, then by Lemma
7
, for every fixed
x_{0}\in\Omega
,

P\!\big(\lambda_{\min}(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x_{0}))\geq\frac{9C_{\lambda}}{10}\big)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{\tilde{c}_{\lambda}}},

where
\tilde{c}_{\lambda}=c_{\mathcal{Q}}\cdot(\frac{C_{\lambda}}{10})^{2}
.
To show that
s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(x)
is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
on all of
\Omega
, we need the matrix
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
to be uniformly invertible (positive definite) and smooth across the domain.
Lemma
7
only gives a pointwise probabilistic lower bound on
\lambda_{\min}(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x_{0}))
.
To extend this to all
x\in\Omega
, we apply Weyl’s eigenvalue perturbation inequality for Hermitian matrices, which allows us to control how the minimal eigenvalue changes with
x
as follows

|\lambda_{\min}(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x_{0}))-\lambda_{\min}(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x))|\leq\|\mathscr{A}_{n}(x_{0})-\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)\|_{op}\quad

Since all matrix norms on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are equivalent, there exists a constant
C_{\text{norm}}>0
such that

\|B\|_{op}\leq C_{\text{norm}}\|B\|_{F,1},

where
\|B\|_{F,1}=\sum_{i,j}|B_{ij}|
is the entrywise Frobenius 1-norm.
Recall (
39
) then
\mathscr{A}_{n,ij}(x)
is given by,

\mathscr{A}_{n,ij}(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\theta_{h}\left(x_{t}-x\right)\frac{\left(x_{t}-x\right)^{\alpha_{i}}}{h_{n}^{\left|\alpha_{i}\right|}}\frac{\left(x_{t}-x\right)^{\alpha_{j}}}{h_{n}^{\left|\alpha_{j}\right|}},\quad i,j=1,\ldots,A.

Each entry
\mathscr{A}_{n,ij}(x)
of
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
as a function of
x
, since it is built from smooth weights
\theta_{h}
and polynomial basis functions. Therefore, each
\mathscr{A}_{n,ij}(x)
is locally Lipschitz, and on the compact domain
\Omega
we can choose a uniform Lipschitz constant
C_{\text{Lip}}
such that

\sum_{i,j}|\mathscr{A}_{n,ij}(x_{0})-\mathscr{A}_{n,ij}(x)|\leq C_{\text{Lip}}\cdot d\cdot\|x_{0}-x\|.

Combining the above inequalities, we obtain a Lipschitz bound for the minimal eigenvalue:

|\lambda_{\min}(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x_{0}))-\lambda_{\min}(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x))|\leq C_{\text{norm}}\cdot C_{\text{Lip}}\cdot d\cdot\|x_{0}-x\|.

Let

\delta_{0}=\frac{C_{\lambda}}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{C_{\text{norm}}\cdot C_{\text{Lip}}\cdot d}.

Then, for all
x\in B(x_{0},\delta_{0})
, assuming that the event
\left\{\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathscr{A}_{n}\left(x_{0}\right)\right)\geq\frac{9C_{\lambda}}{10}\right\}
holds,

|\lambda_{\min}(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x_{0}))-\lambda_{\min}(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x))|<\frac{C_{\lambda}}{2}\quad\implies\quad\lambda_{\min}(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x))>\frac{C_{\lambda}}{2}.

Hence, for each fixed
x_{0}
,

P\!\Big(\lambda_{\min}(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x))\geq\tfrac{C_{\lambda}}{2}\text{ for all }x\in B(x_{0},\delta_{0})\Big)\geq 1-\frac{2}{n^{\tilde{c}_{\lambda}}}.

Now, since
\Omega
is compact, it can be covered by finitely many balls
B(y_{j},\delta_{0})
,
j=1,\dots,M
where
\delta_{0}>0
is independent of
n
,
y_{i}
are arbitrary points in
\Omega
and
M
is the minimal number of balls of radius
\delta_{0}
required to cover
\Omega
(see (
9
)). We consider, for each
j=1,\ldots,M
, the events

E_{j}=\left\{\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)\right)\geq\frac{C_{\lambda}}{2}\text{ for all }x\in B\left(y_{j},\delta_{0}\right)\right\}.

Since each of these events
E_{j}
occurs with probability at least
1-\frac{2}{n^{\tilde{c}_{\lambda}}}
, the union bound yields

P\left(E_{1}\cap\cdots\cap E_{M}\right)\geq 1-M\cdot\frac{2}{n^{\tilde{c}_{\lambda}}},

i.e.,

P\!\Big(\forall j=1,\dots,M:\lambda_{\min}(\mathscr{A}_{n}(x))\geq\tfrac{C_{\lambda}}{2}\text{ for all }x\in B(y_{j},\delta_{0})\Big)\geq 1-M\cdot\frac{2}{n^{\tilde{c}_{\lambda}}}.

Therefore, with probability tending to one, the matrix
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
is uniformly positive definite on
\Omega
.
Now, recall that the shape functions are defined as (
38
)

a_{i}^{*}(x)=\theta_{h}(x_{i}-x)\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k-1}\eta_{\alpha}(x)\frac{(x_{i}-x)^{\alpha}}{h_{n}^{|\alpha|}}.

Since the entries of
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)
are
\mathcal{C}^{k}
, the entries of
\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)^{-1}
are also
\mathcal{C}^{k}
, and the coefficients
\eta_{\alpha}(x)
, which are the components of
\eta(x)=\mathscr{A}_{n}(x)^{-1}\boldsymbol{p}(x)
inherit this smoothness. Combining this with the smoothness of the weight
\theta_{h}
, each shape function
a_{i}^{*}(x)
is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
, and therefore the MLS approximation
s^{\text{MLS}}_{F_{n},X_{n}}(x)\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(\Omega)
with probability one as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Manifold Estimation

Section
2
addresses the problem of function estimation. A natural question that arises is whether this framework can be extended to the setting of manifolds. In this section, we review the work of Sober and Levin
8
, who consider deterministic sampling assumptions consistent with the function estimation works mentioned above. Their results show that approximation error rate matches the rate obtained in the function approximation case, as established in
3
. We summarize the key components of their analysis and show that, in a similar manner, replacing the deterministic sampling assumptions with stochastic ones preserves the same convergence rate as in Equation
37
.

The manifold MLS (MMLS) computation as presented in
8
, involves two key steps. First, we establish a local coordinate system over which the manifold is locally represented as the graph of a function. Second, we compute a weighted least-squares estimate to approximate the manifold as a function over this local coordinate system. In details, assume that
\mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(k\geq 2)
is a closed (i.e., compact and boundary-less) submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, where
\mathcal{C}^{k}
means that the manifold is smooth, with a structure that allows for
k
continuous derivatives in the usual topology, giving it a well-defined notion of local smoothness. Let
R=\left\{r_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{M}
be a sample set with respect to the domain
\mathcal{M}
.
Then, Given a point
r
near
\mathcal{M}
the MMLS projection
of
r
is defined through two sequential steps, following a projective approximation approach, where points in the neighborhood of the manifold are considered and projected onto a nearby local manifold, providing the projection of
r
.

1.

Find a local
d
-dimensional affine subspace
H(r)
centered at a point
q(r)\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, which serves as an approximation to the sampled data near
r
. Specifically, let
H=q+\operatorname{Span}\left\{e_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
, where
\left\{e_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
is an orthonormal set in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. This affine subspace will act as a local coordinate system. In other words,
we wish to Find a
d
-dimensional affine space
H(r)
, and a point
q(r)
on
H(r)
, such that

(q(r),H(r))=\underset{q\in\mathbb{R}^{D},(H-q)\in Gr(d,D)}{\operatorname{argmin}}J_{1}(q,H\mid r),

(63)

where

J_{1}(q,H\mid r)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d\left(r_{i}-q,H\right)^{2}\theta_{1}\left(\left\|r_{i}-q\right\|\right),

(64)

under the constraints

(a)

r-q\perp H

(b)

q\in B_{\mu}(r)

(c)

\#\left(R\cap B_{h}(q)\right)\neq 0,

where
\theta_{1}(t)
is a fast decaying radial weight function,
Gr(d,D)
is the Grassmannian, i.e., the set of
d
-dimensional sub-spaces in an
D
-dimensional vector space,
d\left(r_{i},H\right)
is the Euclidean distance between the point
r_{i}
and the affine sub-space
H,\mu
is some fixed number,
B_{\eta}(x)
is an open ball of radius
\eta
around
x
, and
h
is the fill distance.

2.

Define the projection of
r
using a local polynomial approximant
\pi:H\simeq\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
of
\mathcal{M}
over the new coordinate system. Explicitly, we define
x_{i}=P_{H}(r_{i}-q)
as the orthogonal projection of
r_{i}-q
onto
H\in Gr(d,D)
, and denote the corresponding samples of a function
\varphi
by
\varphi(x_{i})=r_{i}
. The approximation in the local coordinates is performed by finding a polynomial
\pi^{*}(y)\in\Pi_{k-1}^{d}
which minimizes

J_{2}(p(x)\mid q,H)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|p(x_{i})-r_{i}\|^{2}\,\theta_{2}(x_{i}),

(65)

where
\theta_{2}(t)
is a fast-decaying radial weight function consistent across scales, i.e., it is normalized to maintain its relative decay profile across different local scales (e.g., bandwidths).
That is,

p^{*}_{r}(x\mid r)=\underset{\pi\in\Pi_{k-1}^{d}}{\operatorname{argmin}}J_{2}(\pi(x)\mid q(r),H(r)).

(66)

The Manifold-MLS projection is defined as

\mathcal{P}_{k}^{h}(r)=p^{*}_{r}(0\mid r)

(67)

Using this procedure, the approximating manifold is defined as

\mathcal{M}^{h}=\left\{\mathcal{P}_{k}^{h}(p)\mid\forall p\in\mathcal{M}\right\}.

(68)

Lastly, it is shown that when provided with a finite set of samples of
\mathcal{M}
with some fill distance h, the MMLS method yields an approximation
\mathcal{M}^{h}
that converges to
\mathcal{M}
with a provable rate.

We now state and prove this result in the context of stochastic sampling, employing the notations
h_{n}
and
\mathcal{M}^{h_{n}}
.

Theorem

(Theorem
4
)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{M}^{h_{n}}\in\mathcal{C}^{k}
. Then, there exists a constant
s>0
, independent of
h_{n}
, such that the MMLS approximation using a radial weight function
\theta
with finite support of size
sh_{n}
satisfies, for sufficiently small
h_{n}
, the error bound

\left\|\mathcal{M}^{h_{n}}-\mathcal{M}\right\|_{\text{Hausdorff}}=O_{P}(\log nh_{n}^{k}),

where
\mathcal{M}^{h_{n}}
denotes the MMLS approximation of degree
k-1
.

Here, the Hausdorff distance between two sets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
is defined as

\|\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B}\|_{\text{Hausdorff}}=\max\left\{\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}d(a,\mathcal{B}),\sup_{b\in\mathcal{B}}d(b,\mathcal{A})\right\},

where
d(p,\mathcal{N})
denotes the Euclidean distance from the point
p
to the set
\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

Let
r\in\mathcal{M}^{h_{n}}
. Denote by
(q^{*}(r),H^{*}(r))
the minimizers of the cost function in equation
63
(step 1 in the algorithm), where
H^{*}(r)
represents the affine subspace, i.e., the local approximation of the tangent space, and
q^{*}(r)
represents the origin of the local coordinate system used for the approximation.
Now since
H^{*}(r)
is a first-order (tangent) approximation of the manifold, the deviations from
H^{*}(r)
scale quadratically with the neighborhood size, i.e.

d(r_{i},H^{*}(r))=O(h_{n}^{2}).

(69)

This result yields that
H^{*}(r)
approximates the sample set
\left\{r_{i}\right\}
up to the order of
O\left(h_{n}^{2}\right)
in an
O(h_{n})
neighborhood of
q^{*}(r)
.
Consequently, the objective
J_{1}
evaluated at the tangent space is an infinite sum of second-order terms:

J_{1}(q,H\mid r)\geq J_{1}(q^{*}(r),H^{*}(r)\mid r)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d\left(r_{i}-q^{*}(r),H^{*}(r)\right)^{2}\theta_{1}\left(\left\|r_{i}-q^{*}(r)\right\|\right).

Letting

T_{i,n}:=d(r_{i}-q^{*},H^{*})^{2}\,\theta_{1}(\|r_{i}-q^{*}\|),\quad i=1,\dots,n,

we observe from Equation
69
that each term is
O(h_{n}^{4})
, so that
\sup_{i}T_{i,n}=O(h_{n}^{4})
. Denoting by

M_{n}:=\#\{i:T_{i,n}\text{ contributes to the sum}\},

the number of non-negligible terms, then Lemma
3
implies
M_{n}\sim\log n
with probability at least
1-\frac{2}{n^{c}}
. It then follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i,n}\leq M_{n}\cdot\sup_{i}T_{i,n}=O_{P}(\log n)\cdot O(h_{n}^{4})=O_{P}(\log n\,h_{n}^{4}),

and consequently,

J_{1}(q,H\mid r)\geq J_{1}(q^{*}(r),H^{*}(r)\mid r)=O_{P}(\log n\,h_{n}^{4}).

Now, according to Theorem
2
, as the projection
\mathcal{P}^{h}_{k}\in\mathcal{M}^{h_{n}}
is merely a local polynomial approximation of
\mathcal{M}
, we achieve
that
\mathcal{P}^{h}_{k}
is
O_{P}(\log nh_{n}^{k})
away from the manifold
\mathcal{M}
.
Accordingly, for all
r\in\mathcal{M},d\left(r,\mathcal{M}^{h_{n}}\right)\leq

O_{P}\left(\log nh_{n}^{k}\right)
. Furthermore, for each
s\in{\mathcal{M}}^{h_{n}}
there exists a point
r\in\mathcal{M}
such that
s=

\mathcal{P}_{k}^{h}(r)
which is
O_{P}\left(\log nh_{n}^{k}\right)
away from
\mathcal{M}
. Thus, for all
s\in\mathcal{M}^{h_{n}},d(s,\mathcal{M})\leq O_{P}\left(\log nh_{n}^{k}\right)
as well, and the theorem follows.

8.2
Failure of the deterministic proof in stochastic setup

In Wendland’s work
11
(page 42), there is a review of Levin’s work, which proves that
\|s^{\text{MLS}}_{f,X}(x)-f\|_{\Omega,\infty}<L\cdot h^{k}
. The technique used there involves bounding the error by controlling the shape functions through certain manipulations. In order to achieve this, it is necessary to bound the number of samples in
I(x)
by a constant. This is done by comparing the volume of the union of the small balls with the volume of the large ball, leading to the following bound:

\#I(x)\operatorname{vol}(B(0,1))\delta_{n}^{d}\leq\operatorname{vol}(B(0,1))(\delta_{n}+h_{n})^{d}

(70)

Using the quasi-uniformity property (which holds in the deterministic setup) finally leads to

\#I(x)\leq\left(1+\frac{h_{n}}{\delta_{n}}\right)^{d}\leq\left(1+c_{qu}\right)^{d}.

(71)

This implies that the number of indices in
I(x)
remains bounded as

tends to infinity, which contradicts Lemma
3
, as is the case in stochastic settings. Mirzaei used this result in his paper to prove his theory. He relies on it in several of his lemmas, and therefore his Theorem 3.12—which is the main result we address in our paper (Theorem
2
)—cannot be reproduced in the stochastic setting. We re-proved the claims that depend on this result without relying on the quasi-uniformity property, and together with the remaining claims that do not depend on it, we constructed the entire proof.
