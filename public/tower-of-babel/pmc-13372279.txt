Ventricle Width and Cerebrospinal Fluid Diversion Rate after Open Fetal Spina Bifida Repair: A 4-Year Follow-Up
Authors: Julia Wawrla-Zepf, Ladina Rüegg, Martin Meuli, Luca Mazzone, Ueli Moehrlen, Sandra Fernandes Dias, Jenny C. Kienzler, Nicole Ochsenbein-Kölble, Ladina Vonzun, on behalf of SPINA BIFIDA STUDY GROUP ZURICH
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe objective of this study was to assess the postnatal cerebrospinal fluid diversion (CSFD) rates and associated prenatal risk factors (RFs) in patients with open fetal spina bifida (fSB) repair over a 4-year follow-up.MethodsIn 185 patients, CSFD rate was determined at 1 year and incremental risk (IR) for CSFD at 2 and 4 years. Characteristics of children without CSFD were compared to those with CSFD, and multiple regression analysis evaluated RF.ResultsSixty-four children (35%) needed CSFD within the first year. These children had larger head circumference (HC) at screening (p = 0.001) and before delivery (p < 0.001) and larger ventricles at screening (Vp screening) and before delivery (Vp delivery) (each p < 0.001). Multiple regression identified only Vp screening, Vp delivery, and their delta as independent RF for CSFD. Vp values were higher in children needing CSFD within the first year compared with years 2–4 (p < 0.001 and p = 0.01), and larger Vp was associated with earlier CSFD in the first year (p = 0.02). IR for CSFD declined over follow-up.ConclusionThe 1-year CSFD rate after open fSB repair was 35%, with decreasing IR through year four. Larger Vp at screening, before delivery, and their delta were independent RF for CSFD, while HC was not.

Introduction

Prenatal fetal spina bifida (fSB) repair has become a standard treatment option for selected cases, since results of the Management of Myelomeningocele Study (MOMS) showed a reduced need for cerebrospinal fluid diversion (CSFD) (40% vs. 82%, p < 0.001) and better motor outcomes in case of prenatal compared to postnatal repair [1]. The reduced need for CSFD after prenatal surgery is thought to be the result of normalized hydrostatic back pressure with consequent reversal of hindbrain herniation and restoration of normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation consequent to fetal repair [2]. Several previous studies tried to assess prognostic factors associated with the need for CSFD after prenatal fSB repair [3–6]. Factors that were found to be associated with postnatal CSFD after fSB repair are the maximal width of the posterior horn of the lateral ventricle (Vp) at screening (Vp screening) and/or before delivery (Vp delivery), fetal Vp-to-hemisphere ratio, and gestational age (GA) at fetal surgery [3, 4, 6].

A systematic review and meta-analysis that included 9 studies, investigating prenatal predictors for CSFD after fSB repair. They found that GA at surgery ≥25 weeks, myeloschisis, Vp screening ≥15 mm, Vp delivery, and preoperative lesion level at T12-L2 were significantly associated with the need for CSFD [7]. Results of a previous study from our center that analyzed the value of prenatal sonographic head circumference (HC) and Vp measurements to predict CSFD within the first year in a cohort of 30 children with open fSB showed that Vp screening, Vp delivery, and their delta were independent risk factors (RFs) for CSFD (p = 0.003). Furthermore, all children with Vp screening >15 mm received a CSFD [8]. Clearly, Vp and its association with the need for postnatal CSFD is one of the key elements when counseling patients considering fetal SB repair. This study was conducted to identify additional RFs for CSFD in a larger, contemporary cohort. Secondary goal of this study was to extend the follow-up duration up to 4 years and assess incremental risk (IR) for CSFD at 2 and 4 years.

Methods

Between December 2010 and January 2023, 200 women and their fetuses consecutively received open fSB repair at the Zurich Center for Fetal Diagnosis and Therapy. The MOMS inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied with some modifications (shown in online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552456) [9, 10]. Women with missing data regarding CSFD placement were excluded, as well as the ones with withdrawn informed consent.

All women referred to our center for evaluation of fSB underwent a standardized obstetric ultrasound examination performed by one of our senior consultants specialized in prenatal ultrasound. The examination was performed using a GE Voluson E8, E10 or Expert 22 with a transabdominal transducer. The detailed examination of the defect included scanning the fetal spine in both sagittal and axial planes. If the lesion showed a sac filled with CSF, meninges, and the spinal cord that passed through to form the neural placode on the surface of the sac, it was defined as a myelomeningocele. In case the spinal cord was flattened without a sac, the lesion was defined as a myeloschisis. For this study, other lesion types that were seen more rarely were summarized as “other”. To determine the level of the lesion, T12 was identified (most caudal vertebrae with a corresponding rib) and then counted down to the highest bony lesion level. Measurements of the fetal HC and Vp were performed in the same way as previously reported [8]: HC was measured on the axial transthalamic plane at the level where the continuous midline echo is broken by the cavum septi pellucidi in the anterior third [11, 12]. According to the ISUOG guidelines, fetal Vp was measured at level of the glomus of the choroid plexus, perpendicular to the ventricular cavity, positioning the calipers inside the echoes generated by the lateral walls [12, 13]. To depict the third ventricle, the area between the thalami was again scanned in an axial plane [14, 15]. If it appeared as a single echogenic line, it was classified as normal. The cutoff for third ventricle dilatation was >3 mm, corresponding to a sonographic visualization and measurability of the third ventricle. The open defect was repaired as previously reported [16]: first, the zona epithelia-serosa was incised at the border to the skin. Then the tissue of the zona epithelia-serosa was meticulously dissected from the neural placode. In case in which the filum terminale or other pathological attachments and adhesions were present and caused the spinal cord to be tethered, they were severed. Then the open pia (if possible) and dura mater were dissected and tubularized, completely enclosing the exposed non-neurulated part of the spinal cord in a watertight manner. To protect the spinal cord, it was then covered first with a muscle layer, followed by a skin layer, which formed a protective outer covering and ensured a watertight closure. By the time, the neural placode was not tubularized anymore. Also, the dura was not any longer closed separately but together with the muscle flap as it has shown to provide a more stable closure.

After surgery, fetuses underwent sonographic scans at least once per week until delivery, allowing follow-up measurement of Vp and HC. Following delivery, neonates received standard baseline postnatal diagnostic evaluation. After discharge, follow-up appointments at our center were scheduled at 3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 months and thereafter annually up to 18 years of age.

At our center, the indication for CSFD was made when at least two of the following criteria were met: accelerated increase of HC (indicated by linear percentile crossing, advancing toward or above the 90–97th centile), bulging fontanelle, sunset sign (stuck downward deviation of the eyes, revealing the sclera between the iris and the upper eyelid), persistent CSF leakage from repair site, neurological symptoms from Chiari II malformation (e.g., sleep apnea with increasing oxygen desaturations), presence of papilledema on ophthalmologic examination, disproportional increase in ventricular width in imaging studies (e.g., ventricular index ≥97th centile ± 4 standard deviation or fronto-occipital-horn ratio >0.33) compared to increase in HC, combination of ventriculomegaly and syringomyelia. When only one criterion was present, we assessed for other signs suggestive of increased intracranial pressure, such as increasing sleepiness, reduced daytime activity, or recurrent vomiting. For children with slightly enlarged ventricles and/or HC growing toward or above the 90–97th centile (disproportional to body height and weight), but with no papilledema or symptoms of increased intracranial pressure, we tended to adopt a more conservative strategy, aiming to observe whether the CSF dynamics would achieve an equilibrium between production and reabsorption or at least wait for the child to be 6 months old, at which point an endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) could be performed. Whenever the above-mentioned criteria were fulfilled, a ventriculoperitoneal shunt was inserted in children <6 months of age. For children ≥6 months of age, an ETV was the preferred approach, whenever an obstructive cause for the hydrocephalus was diagnosed (e.g., aqueduct stenosis, Chiari malformation with obstruction of the foramen magnum through the cerebellar tonsils). In the past 5 years, due to growing evidence of success, ETVs have been increasingly offered to children with communicating hydrocephalus that developed an insidious CSF disorder over months [17, 18]. In these particular cases, the advantages (no foreign body implants) and risks (insufficient CSF drainage with necessity of later implantation of a ventriculoperitoneal shunt) of ETV were extensively discussed with the parents and a joined decision was made.

To assess differences between children without CSFD and those with CSFD, the cohort was divided into two groups accordingly. Baseline and perioperative characteristics, as well as Vp and HC measurements, were evaluated and compared between the two groups. An additional subgroup analysis was performed in children who required CSFD within the first year of life, to determine the timing of CSFD based on their Vp screening (<10.0 mm, 10.0–15.0 mm, or >15.0 mm), as well as the type of CSFD procedure performed (ventriculoperitoneal shunt vs. ETV).

Data collection was conducted in the following way: at the very beginning of our program, a data registry was created to record all pertinent data in a prospective and comprehensive way. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap® electronic data capture tools hosted at the University Hospital of Zurich [19, 20]. The following additional baseline, as well as peri- and postoperative characteristics were retrieved from our RedCap® database: maternal age, fetal sex, GA at screening, surgery and delivery, fetal HC at screening and before delivery, third ventricle dilatation at screening and its size, type and level of lesion, talipes, Vp screening, Vp delivery, and birth weight.

Written informed consent was obtained from all women included in this study. The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and in accordance with the approval of the Local Ethics Commission Zurich (KEK-ZH, Nr. 2021–01101).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29.0). Categorical data were analyzed using the Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate, whereas the Mann-Whitney U test, independent t test, or Kruskal-Wallis test were used for the analysis of continuous data. Normal distribution was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. For significant parameters in the univariate statistics, a multivariate logistic regression was performed to evaluate RFs for CSFD. To avoid multicollinearity between continuous Vp measurements itself, as well as between continuous and categorical representations of Vp, separate logistic regression models were fitted. Model 1A included continuous screening parameters (Vp screening and HC at screening), model 2A included continuous parameters measured before delivery (Vp delivery and HC before delivery), and model 3 included the change in Vp and HC from screening to before delivery. On the other hand, model 1B included categorical Vp variables at screening (<10.0 mm/10.0–15.0 mm/>15.0 mm) and model 2B included categorical Vp variables before delivery (<10.0 mm/10.0–15.0 mm/>15.0 mm). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for Vp and HC to assess how well they could discriminate between children who did and did not require CSFD. Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation in case of normal distribution of data or median with interquartile range in case data were not distributed normally, whereas categorical data are presented as number (N) with percentage (%). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Vp screening measurements were significantly lower in children without CSFD compared to children with CSFD (9.9 ± 2.2 mm vs. 13.1 ± 3.3 mm, p < 0.001) and children without CSFD had significantly more Vp screening measurements of <10.0 mm and significantly fewer Vp screening measurements of 10.0–15.0 mm or >15.0 mm compared to children with CSFD (70 (58%) vs. 12 (19%), p < 0.001; 49 (41%) vs. 37 (58%), p = 0.03, and 2 (2%) vs. 15 (23%), p < 0.001).

Similarly, Vp delivery measurements were significantly lower in children without CSFD compared to children with CSFD (13.9 ± 4.2 mm vs. 20.6 (16.7–26.0) mm, p < 0.001) and children without CSFD had significantly more Vp delivery measurements of <10.0 mm and significantly fewer Vp delivery measurements of >15.0 mm compared to children with CSFD (19 (16%) vs. 2 (3%), p = 0.01, and 36 (31%) vs. 51 (81%), p < 0.001). The delta Vp (Vp change from screening to last evaluation before delivery) was significantly lower in children without CSFD compared to children with CSFD (3.6 (1.8–5.8) mm vs. 8.0 (4.2–15.0) mm, p < 0.001).

The subgroup analysis of children who required a CSFD within the first year showed a significant difference in the timing of CSFD based on their Vp screening: Vp screening <10.0 mm at 5.6 ± 3.3 months, Vp screening 10.0–15.0 mm at 3.6 (0.6–6.6) months, Vp screening >15.0 mm at 0.0 (0.0–3.6) months (p = 0.02). Furthermore, the type of CSFD significantly differed according to the age of placement. Ventriculoperitoneal shunts were placed at a median age of 2.4 (0.0–4.8) months, whereas ETVs were performed at a mean age of 7.0 ± 3.1 months (p < 0.001).

HC at Screening (HC Screening) and before Delivery (HC Delivery)

Measurements of HC screening were significantly lower in children without CSFD compared to children with CSFD (195.1 ± 18.0 mm vs. 204.5 ± 20.1 mm, p = 0.001). The same was observed for HC delivery measurements (322.0 (307.1–332.7) mm vs. 336.1 (313.2–362.4) mm, p < 0.001). The delta HC (HC change from screening to last evaluation before delivery) was lower in children without CSFD compared to children with CSFD but did not reach significance (125.1 (91.5–143.7) mm vs. 133.1 (102.2–155.2) mm, p = 0.06). For Vp screening, ROC analysis showed an AUC of 0.791 (95% CI 0.72–0.86, p < 0.001); for HC screening, an AUC of 0.635 (95% CI 0.55–0.72, p = 0.003); and for HC delivery, an AUC of 0.664 (95% CI 0.57–0.76, p < 0.001). When Vp delivery was used to predict CSFD requirement, a cutoff value of 10 mm had a sensitivity of 97%, specificity of 84%, positive LHR of 6.0, and negative LHR of 0.04.

On the other hand, when a Vp screening cutoff of 15 mm was used, CSFD was predicted with a sensitivity of 25%, a specificity of 3%, a positive LHR of 0.3, and negative LHR of 30.3. The same cutoff of 15 mm for Vp delivery allowed CSFD prediction with a sensitivity of 83%, specificity of 31%, a positive LHR of 0.5, and negative LHR of 0.4.

Long-Term Follow-Up

Since not all children of our cohort of 185 had reached 2 and 4 years at the time point of data analysis, we did not calculate the absolute, but rather the IR for CSFD. For the analysis at 2 years, we saw the following: 167 of 185 had reached the 2 years, but complete data was only available in 152 cases. Eight additional children required CSFD during year 2. Therefore, the lower end of the IR was 7% (8 CSFD in 121 children at risk), whereas the upper end of the IR was 19% (23 CSFD assuming all children with missing data would have received CSFD in 121 children at risk). For the analysis at 4 years, 127 had reached this age at the time of analysis, but data were missing in 9 cases. Therefore, a full data set was available in only 118 cases. Two additional children received CSFD between year 2 to year 4. The lower end of the IR was therefore 2% (2 CSFD in 98 children at risk), whereas the upper end of the IR was 11% (11 CSFD (when assuming all children with missing data would have received CSFD in 98 children at risk). Overall, the risk for CSFD decreased over time.

Discussion

This study showed a 1-year CSFD rate of 35% in children with previous fSB repair. Vp and HC at screening and before delivery were significantly smaller in children without CSFD compared to children with CSFD. In the following, CSFD rates and associated RFs are discussed.

Within our cohort, the CSFD rate at 1 year was 35% and therefore lower compared to our previously reported data [8]. The MOMS [1] follow-up data, reported by Tulipan et al. [21], showed a higher CSFD rate of 44%, including follow-ups of 1 year, whereas Agrawal et al. [4] reported a similar CSFD rate of 32% in a cohort of 25 children having had fSB repair and >1-year follow-up. Longer follow-up data was presented by Houtrow et al. [22], who analyzed outcomes of the MOMS cohort at school age (5.9–10.3 years) and reported a CSFD rate of 49%, showing that additional children required CSFD over a longer follow-up time. Since not all children from our cohort had reached 2 and 4 years, and some were lost to follow-up, we were unfortunately not able to calculate the absolute CSFD rate at these ages but only the IR. The IR showed to decrease from 7–19% at 2 years to 2–11% at 4 years, which is important to know when counseling parents and patients at follow-up visits. Future follow-up studies of our cohort will allow calculations of absolute risk for CSFD at older ages and will therefore allow comparison with the MOMS cohort [22]. Yet, and most importantly, the CSFD rates reported here remain significantly lower compared to CSFD rates after postnatal spina bifida repair, which lay around 80–85% [1, 4, 22].

Regarding RFs for CSFD, results of this study confirmed that Vp screening and Vp delivery are associated with postnatal CSFD after fSB repair, consistent with our previous analysis of the first 30 children who had fSB repair at our center [8]. In this current cohort, the CSFD rate at 1 year of age for Vp screening <10.0 mm was 15%, for Vp screening 10.0–15.0 mm was 43%, and for Vp screening >15.0 mm was 88%. When comparing our CSFD rates with those of the MOMS cohort at 1 year of age, calculated using the revised CSFD criteria that were similar, though not identical to ours, we found that CSFD rates in our cohort were lower across all three Vp screening groups: Vp screening <10 mm (15% vs. 20%), Vp screening 10.0–15.0 mm (43% vs. 52%), and Vp screening >15.0 mm (88% vs. 90%). This suggests that our center followed a more conservative approach to CSFD management, which is also reflected in our CSFD criteria. As mentioned in the methods section, at our center, at least two criteria had to be present to require CSFD, whereas this was not the case for the MOMS cohort [21], neither in their original nor in their revised CSFD criteria. Furthermore, in particular cases where Vp and HC increased in the first year of life, but children remained clinically a-/oligosymptomatic, our center followed a more conservative “watch and wait” approach. This strategy aimed to assess whether CSF dynamics would achieve an equilibrium between production and reabsorption, or to defer intervention until the child reached 6 months of age, when an ETV could be performed. Thus, CSFD rates in general need to be interpreted carefully, since different centers follow different criteria/management for CSFD after fSB repair.

While Vp screening of ≥15 mm was already a well-established risked factor for CSFD at the time, this study was conducted, less was known about the influence of Vp delivery and HC as RFs for CSFD [6, 8, 21]. Results of this study showed that Vp delivery represented an additional independent risk factor for CSFD, with similar ability like Vp screening, to discriminate between children who required CSFD within the first year of life and the ones who did not (AUC = 0.819 vs. 0.791). HC measurements, on the other hand, did not prove to be independent RFs for CSFD, although HC screening and HC delivery were significantly larger in children with CSFD. ROC curve calculations showed that using a cutoff value of 10 mm compared to 15 mm had a higher sensitivity and specificity for both Vp screening and Vp delivery, with a Vp delivery cutoff of 10 mm providing the best diagnostic value for CSFD prediction (sensitivity of 97% and specificity of 84%).

Nevertheless, the value of Vp delivery is limited to reassessing the risk of CSFD shortly before delivery and does not aid in optimizing patient counseling prior to surgery. At the time of counseling, Vp screening remains the available predictor for CSFD. Results of this study, namely, the fact that 85% of children with Vp screening <10 mm did not need CSFD, whereas in 88% of children with Vp screening >15 mm CSFD had to be performed, can help counseling parents. Additionally, our results indicate that Vp screening is not only associated with the risk for CSFD but also directive for its timing. Among children who required CSFD, those with Vp screening >15.0 mm received it approximately 6 months earlier compared to those with Vp screening <10.0 mm. This is understandable and further supports parental counseling, as later CSFD is generally preferable [23, 24].

Furthermore, delta Vp (Vp change from screening to last evaluation before delivery) should be considered, since it was approximately doubled in children with CSFD. One needs to keep in mind that also other reasons, like an underlying aqueductal stenosis, which is not treated by the fetal surgery, can lead to a further/stronger increase of Vp after surgery until delivery. Discussing the potential presence of such additional pathologies, which may further influence CSFD, should therefore be part of patient counseling. Interestingly, other previously reported RFs, such as higher GA at surgery, myeloschisis, or lesions level above L3, did not show any significant association with CSFD in our cohort, and its value to predict CSFD should therefore be reassessed.

Overall counseling of women with Vp screening >15 mm needs special care and parents should be informed about the increased risk for an early postnatal CSFD and its potential impacts. Additionally, all women, regardless of their Vp screening value, should be informed about the diagnostic value of postoperative follow-up Vp measurements until delivery, as well as the potential impact of additional pathologies on the need for CSFD. Finally, counseling about CSFD risks is of course only one of the elements when talking to parents expecting a child with SB, since fSB repair has additional benefits such as improved motor function persisting at least into school age [22, 25].

Strengths and Limitations

The study’s main strengths were its large cohort, its single-center study design that allowed for a standardized management with a consistent surgical team, and its application of previously defined CSFD criteria. Furthermore, the almost 100% follow-up rate at 1 year can be seen as a strength, whereas the loss to follow-up at 2 and 4 years, as well as the generally smaller group size due to fewer children having reached the age of 2 and 4 years, are limitations of this study. The division of the cohort for subgroup analysis furthermore resulted in smaller groups, thus limiting the power of results. The study’s retrospective design represents a further limitation.

Conclusion

This study showed a 1-year CSFD rate of 35% following fSB repair, with decreasing IR for CSFD over the next 3 years. Overall, CSFD rates after fSB repair were substantially lower than the ones reported after postnatal repair. When CSFD was required, it was mostly performed within the first year, and Vp screening was a predictor of timing. Vp screening, Vp delivery, and their delta were all independent RFs for CSFD. Notably, 88% of children with Vp screening >15 mm required CSFD, compared with 85% of those with Vp screening <10 mm who did not. Apart from Vp, no additional independent RFs could be identified.
