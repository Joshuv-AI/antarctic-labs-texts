The politicization of academic collaborations threatens to harm international science
Authors: Marc W. Cadotte
Published: 2026

The goal of science is the generation of knowledge to advance human understanding of the world and our place in it, while also helping to solve global problems. This ideal transcends nationality, ethnicity, and religion—or at least it should.

The International Space Station, seen here in a photo taken by a SpaceX Dragon astronaut, is a prime example of an important, successful international collaboration that found a way to put politics aside. Image credit: Science Source/NASA.

Academic research over the past couple of decades has witnessed an unprecedented opening of geopolitical and disciplinary borders to permit the flow of people, ideas, tools, and data to address intellectual endeavors and global crises (1). However, it’s clear that more recent trends seriously undermine that spirit of openness.

In a series of academic-led protests and boycotts triggered by international disputes, collaborations are ending, cooperation is harder, and research is once again retreating behind borders. While often understandable, these moves are counterproductive and can only hamper human progress in the long run. Institutions and nations must resist the urge to reflexively limit collaborations due to geopolitical events. Doing so confers serious and lasting consequences by limiting the flow of ideas and stemming advances in science.

Damage Done

Clearly, there’s pressure on academics to show solidarity with some nations and to distance themselves from the actions of others. This is only likely to increase as conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East continue and the United States pursues policies that create rifts with allies. Some would argue that the international standing of the United States remains at a low point and could yet fall further. It’s important that scientists and others remain cognizant of global conflict, while not necessarily allowing these politics to influence their research.

Restricting international academic connection and collaboration is not a new phenomenon. The 7th-century scholar Xuanzang had to defy the travel ban imposed by the Chinese Emperor Taizong to journey west through India to study Sanskrit texts (2). We still feel the reverberations of restrictions on collaboration from the Cold War era, with sustained differences in priorities and philosophies, as well as mistrust and restriction on collaboration with former Soviet bloc countries and China (3). Today, we see countries and institutions place restrictions on the numbers of incoming Chinese students and researchers—for example, Chinese students and researchers excluded from US F and J visas if they have been affiliated with military organizations (4). Further, recently, the Texas governor has imposed heightened restrictions on university researchers’ abilities to collaborate with institutions in China (5); this has caused widespread impacts on academic collaboration and cooperation. I’ve personally experienced this with US-based professors withdrawing from a grant panel in Hong Kong at the last minute.

There is a reason that totalitarian governments, eager to avoid dissent, purge academics. Research collaboration, academic exchanges, and the pursuit of knowledge should continue to be a beacon during difficult times. If institutions impose restrictions, we limit these opportunities.

In Canada, all partnership grant applications to the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council require Sensitive Technology Research Areas reviews that flag and limit collaboration with researchers associated with certain Chinese and Russian institutions. These measures sometimes do have merit, but it’s important to recognize that the lack of cooperation during the Cold War resulted in technology inequalities that continue to fuel mistrust. While we could assume that governments restrict collaboration between nations for legitimate national security and economic reasons (6), there’s little accounting of the negative impacts of these restrictions.

Despite concerns, we can all recognize the authority of nations to restrict activities between adversarial countries, but we are now seeing restrictions on academic engagement and collaboration that are different and difficult to navigate. Universities, research institutions, and professional societies, not just governments, are levying curbs on collaborations. This patchwork of collaboration restrictions in response to multiple geopolitical events has created confusion. And the trend threatens to restrict the flow of ideas and data in an era that can ill-afford slowing down research.

The multiple crises of Russia’s attack on Ukraine and the hostilities in Gaza, the West Bank, and Lebanon, along with the ongoing military action in Iran, all clearly entail humanitarian crises that deserve international attention. There have been calls for restrictions on academic collaborations with institutions in Russia and Israel by organizations and subnational institutions, predominantly in the West, by universities, research institutes, and professional societies. This has resulted in a plethora of policies and restrictions that create conflicts for academics, including inconsistent publishing standards and reduced academic freedoms.

Troubling Trends

Such policies have real consequences. Reading the statements from universities and professional societies that have moved to limit connections with Russian or Israeli academic institutions reveals two primary motives for these actions. The first is to fill the void from the lack of sufficient action from national governments or intergovernmental entities. For example, the University of South-Eastern Norway terminated agreements with universities in Israel because, in part, “Israeli authorities have chosen to ignore the statements of the International Court of Justice and not made any moves to improve the humanitarian situation” (7). Similarly, the University of Helsinki suspended exchange activities with Israeli universities because “no international sanctions have been imposed as part of the Gaza conflict” (8).

The second reason that institutions appear to be restricting ties is to express a moral stand. CERN, the European Organization for Nuclear Research, ended ties with Russia and Belarus because of the “ongoing invasion of Ukraine” (9). German research institutions suspended collaborations with Russia due to the war (10). And while an editorial in the Journal of Radiological Protection (11) notes that collaboration with Russian scientists is critical to understanding the societal consequences of radioactive materials, they announced an end to work with Russian scientists until Russia withdraws from Ukraine.

As moral positions, opposing the outrageous killing of innocent people in Ukraine, Gaza, Lebanon, and Iran is completely justifiable. But such policies have unfortunate by-products: They detract from academic freedom and publishing ethics, and they can negatively affect scientists outside of Russia and Israel who depend on these collaborations for materials, funds, or information in order to conduct their research (12). And universities often must grapple with how to be consistent and avoid cherry picking only certain conflicts and certain countries. Some institutions might, for example, have taken a moral position against the Russian invasion of Ukraine, but neglected to offer up any position on Russia’s illegal attack on Syrian citizens in 2017.

Restrictions with Real Consequences

I was recently involved in a large global collaboration on biodiversity in cities where this issue came to the fore (13). A colleague based in Russia had been involved in an ongoing project since 2018. But when it was time to publish a paper that relied on their contribution, several collaborators from European countries declared that their institutional policies forbade them to publish with this researcher. They cited slightly different types of restrictions, from cut-off dates for starts of collaborations, that no co-authored article can have affiliation with a Russian institution (but a private address and email were acceptable), or no Russian involvement period. Ultimately, the Russian colleague agreed to use a private address to appease these policies. It’s clear that these policies have personal and ethical repercussions for collaborators from restricted institutions, whether this entails difficulty publishing, starting new collaboration, and/or ultimately meeting the requirements for promotion.

This episode highlights three important implications that authors need to be aware of and navigate. First, different institutional policies could vary in key components like those mentioned above. This means that for large collaborative projects, lead authors need to navigate a potentially complex policy landscape, subject to change over time based on political events. Second, hiding author affiliation (e.g., using a private address) does not conform to ethical publishing standards, and journals might have affiliation guidelines that will be contravened by hiding an affiliation. For example, Science requires that authors state “the academic https://www.science.org/content/page/science-journals-editorial-policies, corporate, government, industry, and/or other relevant institutional affiliations where the work was performed.” It is important to acknowledge that authors should have the right to remain anonymous, to protect them from political repercussions (as is the case at some journals; see, e.g., https://publicationethics.org/guidance/cope-position/handling-requests-publish-articles-anonymously), but should generally not be allowed to skirt various institutional policies and restrictions. Finally, removing an author’s affiliation can personally affect them negatively by impacting their ability to get due credit from their institution, hence impacting tenure and promotion.

And it’s unlikely that these policies are actually swaying the civil and military leaderships of Russia and Israel. More likely, the impact is simply on research and training and on researchers who themselves might not be supportive of the actions of their governments.

Restrictive collaboration policies can also clash with the core principles of academic freedom. The Canadian Association of University Teachers supports the position of neutrality to international conflict; this policy has been adopted by many Canadian universities (14), in part because Canadian faculty enjoy substantial freedom to voice their opinions and to disagree with and disobey their employers. This unfettered freedom to voice one’s opinions in academia is in direct conflict with policies that limit international collaboration. It’s not even clear how Canadian and other Western universities would handle such disobedience, given the rights of academic freedom.

Lasting Consequences

Restrictions and boycotts go against a long history of institutions supporting science-led diplomacy via collaborations among researchers hailing from adversarial nations, such as US–Russian collaboration on the International Space Station (15). In this tradition, a 2022 editorial in the Asian Journal of Psychiatry called for more collaboration with researchers in Ukraine and Russia to gain insights into the mental health of child and adolescent war refugees (16). Bilateral Arctic research between Russia and Japan continues despite government sanctions because of the importance of understanding and protecting the Arctic (17). These collaborations not only provide important insights into global problems, but they also serve to create important ties that can fuel dialogue and diplomacy (15) and that will help normalize relations when conflicts end or regimes fall.

Who researchers choose to collaborate with should be free from institutional restrictions, so long as they adhere to national security protections stipulated in governmental law and policy. If they elect to not collaborate with a researcher based on that researcher’s ethnicity or nationality, they should offer up a rational argument for that choice. Ultimately, the outcome is best for everyone when it’s international- or national-level agencies, such as funders or science ministries, who set (hopefully harmonized) cooperation policies, while taking into account researchers’ own ethical and moral principles. Institutions creating a hodgepodge of policies results in confusion and conflicts that can be challenging and unnecessary.

Academics often help guide thought and debate on difficult or sensitive issues. There is a reason that totalitarian governments, eager to avoid dissent, purge academics. Research collaboration, academic exchanges, and the pursuit of knowledge should continue to be a beacon during difficult times. If institutions impose restrictions, we limit these opportunities.
