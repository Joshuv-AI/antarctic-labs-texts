Chronic Myelomonocytic Leukemia: History, Pathobiology, Diagnostic Controversies, and Evolving Classification
Authors: Sanam Loghavi, Robert P. Hasserjian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewChronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) is a clonal myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm characterized by sustained monocytosis, recurrent gene mutations, and a risk of transformation to acute myeloid leukemia (AML). This review examines the historical evolution of CMML as a diagnostic entity, its genetic landscape, fundamental controversies in definition, including the contentious boundaries of oligomonocytic CMML (OM-CMML), and the spectrum of associated phenomena, such as plasmacytoid dendritic cell proliferations, systemic mastocytosis, extramedullary disease, and autoimmune manifestations.Recent FindingsSince the publication of the most recent myeloid classification systems, genomic studies in large CMML cohorts have clarified that cases with borderline monocyte counts are biologically heterogeneous: a subset with bi-allelic TET2 inactivation or TET2 + SRSF2 co-mutation bears the greatest similarity to true CMML, while cases harboring SF3B1, bi-allelic TP53 inactivation (biTP53), or del(5q) may be better classified as myelodysplastic syndrome/neoplasm (MDS), irrespective of the presence of borderline monocytosis. Molecular evolution from clonal hematopoiesis through OM-CMML to overt CMML, and ultimately to acute myeloid leukemia (AML), follows a defined clonal trajectory characterized by the stepwise acquisition of spliceosome, epigenetic, and signaling mutations. Plasmacytoid dendritic cell proliferations, particularly blastic forms, arising in association with or following CMML, share clonal origin and represent a distinct spectrum of monocyte/plasmacytoid dendritic cell lineage dysregulation. In future classification systems, better biologic homogeneity of CMML may be achieved by incorporating specific molecular signatures in the diagnostic criteria and by excluding cases with biTP53 or AML-defining alterations.SummaryCMML is defined by its combination of proliferative (monocytic) and dysplastic features, but exhibits considerable genetic heterogeneity. Optimal diagnosis and distinction from related entities require integration of morphology, immunophenotype, and comprehensive genomic profiling.

Definition of CMML: History and Current Concepts

Chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) is a clonal hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell disorder defined by sustained peripheral blood (PB) monocytosis, often with bone marrow (BM) morphologic dysplasia, and an inherent risk of transformation to acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in approximately 15–20% of patients over three to five years [1, 2].

The French-American-British (FAB) Cooperative Group first recognized CMML in 1982 [3] as a form of myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), defined by an increase in peripheral blood monocytes (absolute monocyte count (AMC) > 1.0 × 109/L), morphologic dysplasia, increased bone marrow monocytes, and fewer than 5% circulating blasts. In 2001, the 3rd edition WHO Classification (WHO3) [4] assigned CMML to the MDS/myeloproliferative neoplasm (MDS/MPN) overlap category, retaining the AMC > 1.0 × 109/L threshold. The 2008 4th edition WHO Classification (WHO4) [5] introduced the requirement for relative monocytosis (comprising at least 10% of circulating leukocytes), as well as myelodysplastic (MD-) and myeloproliferative (MP-) CMML subtypes, defined by a presenting white blood cell count (WBC) of < 13 or ≥ 13 × 109/L, respectively. These two CMML subtypes remain in use today and reflect clinically important differences in proliferative biology, mutational pattern, and prognosis.

While the initial description of CMML in the FAB classification limited blasts to 5%, subsequent WHO Classifications allowed cases with increased blast counts; as in MDS, higher blast counts confer a higher risk of transformation to AML and shortened survival in CMML. Blasts in CMML include myeloid and monocytic blasts, as well as promonocytes (cells with cytologic features intermediate between monoblasts and monocytes) [6]. Thus, blasts and promonocytes together are considered to represent “blast equivalents” in risk-stratifying CMML (Fig. 1). The WHO4 Classification introduced a two-tier system of subclassifying CMML based on the percentage of blast equivalents: CMML-1 (< 10% in the BM and < 5% in the PB) and CMML-2 (10–19% in the BM or 5–19% in the PB, or blasts with Auer rods). The revised 4th edition WHO Classification (WHO4R) [7] introduced a third tier, CMML-0, defined by blast equivalents < 5% in the BM and < 2% in the PB, in an attempt to capture the lowest-risk morphologic stratum. However, subsequent studies have contested the clinical utility of this three-tiered approach: large-cohort analyses, including those incorporating molecularly annotated patients, have demonstrated that CMML-0 adds limited independent prognostic information beyond what is already captured by the molecular risk score, WBC count, and MD versus MP subtype designation [8]. For these reasons, the two most recent classifications, the WHO 5th Classification (WHO5) [9] and the International Consensus Classification (ICC) [10], eliminated CMML-0 as a distinct subtype, consolidating the three-tier system of the WHO4R back to two tiers: CMML-1 and CMML-2.

The WHO5 and the ICC [10] made an additional revision to the original CMML diagnostic criteria: both lowered the AMC threshold that defines CMML from 1.0 to ≥ 0.5 × 109/L, while maintaining the requirement for relative monocytosis (monocytes at least 10% of blood leukocytes). This change was based on evidence from several studies that myeloid neoplasms with relative monocytosis and absolute monocytes 0.5 - <1.0 × 109/L had similar biologic and genetic features to traditional CMML defined by absolute monocytes ≥ 1.0 × 109/L [11–14]. However, these newly added ‘oligomonocytic’ CMML (OM-CMML) cases required cytogenetic or molecular evidence of clonality. The ICC also introduced a requirement for certain bone marrow features to diagnose CMML, which had not been included in the WHO5 or in any prior formal WHO CMML definitions: bone marrow hypercellularity with granulocytic proliferation, often with increased bone marrow monocytes.

Morphology and Immunophenotype

Morphologically, CMML is characterized by bone marrow cellularity that is usually increased for age, with greater cellularity in the MP- than in the MD-subtype [15]. About two-thirds of patients have an elevated myeloid: erythroid ratio [11]. BM monocytes are often, but not always, increased in the aspirate smear (median 10–12% of all cells) and are also increased by flow cytometry [11, 16]. The increased BM monocytes may be difficult to distinguish from hypogranular dysplastic granulocytic forms, and cytochemical staining (such as non-specific esterase) may aid in their identification [17]. In bone marrow biopsies, monocytes are usually increased, as highlighted by immunostains such as CD68 (PGM1) or CD14 [18, 19]. Dysplastic features are usually seen in megakaryocytes, which are usually small and hypolobated, resembling those of MDS rather than MPN [17]; large, MPN-type megakaryocytes should raise the differential diagnosis of an MPN, particularly if accompanied by JAK2, CALR, or MPL mutation. Dysgranulopoiesis and/or dyserythropoiesis are also commonly seen, and ring sideroblasts are relatively frequent: over 15% ring sideroblasts are reported in 19–35% of CMML cases [11, 20].

Abnormal flow cytometric monocyte subset partitioning, in which classical (Mo1) monocytes (CD14++CD16−) are increased and constitute > 94% of total PB monocytes, was established as a high-specificity diagnostic signature of CMML by Sélimoglu-Buet et al. [21], with sensitivity 90.6% and specificity 95.1% in the original French cohort. The assay has since been adopted widely in European centers for routine diagnostic use. However, real-world sensitivity has ranged from 73 to 75% in some institutional series using different gating strategies, underscoring the importance of technical standardization and some difficulties in applying monocyte partitioning in the setting of inflammatory conditions [22].

The slan (6-sulfo LacNAc)+ non-classical (Mo3) monocyte fraction has emerged as a complementary flow cytometry assay. slan+ non-classical monocytes ≤ 1.7% of total monocytes support the diagnosis of CMML in the setting of active inflammatory or autoimmune disease, where Mo3 suppression may produce false-positive Mo1 > 94% results [23].

Genetic Features

90–95% of CMML patients harbor at least one somatic mutation detectable by standard next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels [1, 2]. The molecular landscape of CMML is defined by a characteristic combination of epigenetic and spliceosome driver mutations, namely TET2 (~ 60%), SRSF2 (~ 50%), and ASXL1 (~ 40%), often superimposed on activating signaling pathway mutation as the disease progresses [2]. TET2 encodes a dioxygenase that catalyzes the conversion of 5-methylcytosine to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, a key step in active DNA demethylation. Loss-of-function mutations are predominantly frameshift or nonsense variants and are distributed throughout the coding sequence. Bi-allelic TET2 inactivation (biTET2) correlates most strongly with the CMML disease phenotype [24, 25] and the highest BM monocyte burden [26]. biTET2 is functionally defined as either the presence of two independent mutations in trans, or a single mutation with concurrent TET2 locus (4q24) loss, either by way of deletion or copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity (cn-LOH), thus resulting in inactivation of TET2 function. The presence of a TET2 variant allele fraction (VAF) of ≥ 55% is also considered to represent presumptive evidence of cn-LOH. SRSF2 encodes a serine-arginine-rich splicing factor whose mutations, predominantly affecting proline 95 (P95H/L/R), alter splice-site recognition and cooperate with TET2 loss to promote a monocytic differentiation bias through dysregulated cytokine signaling and innate immune activation [2]. ASXL1 truncating mutations dysregulate Polycomb-mediated chromatin repression and confer an independent adverse prognosis in CMML [27, 28]. Activating mutations affecting the RAS/MAPK pathway occur in approximately 30% of CMML patients overall and promote cytokine-independent proliferation and leukocytosis [18]. These mutations, including NRAS, KRAS, CBL, and PTPN11 , are enriched in MP-CMML (~ 54% vs. ~ 25% in MD-CMML). BRAF mutations are rare in CMML and their identification should prompt evaluation for a concurrent histiocytic neoplasm such as Langerhans cell histiocytosis or Erdheim-Chester disease. JAK2 V617F mutation, most commonly associated with “pure” MPN, is present in 5–10% of cases and preferentially co-occurs with TET2 mutations in MP-CMML. JAK2 mutation is associated with increased frequency of BM fibrosis in CMML [29].

Other genes mutated in CMML include DNMT3A (~ 5–8%), which is enriched in cases lacking TET2 co-mutation and confers adverse prognosis [30]. Mutations in the splicing gene SF3B1 are far less common (~ 5–8%) than the aforementioned SRSF2 mutations [31] and are most common in OM-CMML; as discussed later, SF3B1 mutations in the setting of OM-CMML may indicate MDS biology rather than true CMML [26, 32]. RUNX1 mutations (~ 10–15%) are associated with a higher risk of progression to AML. TP53 alterations are present in 3–5% of CMML cases and define the highest-risk molecular subgroup, with a short overall survival similar to that of TP53- mutated MDS and AML [32, 33].

Cytogenetic abnormalities are identified in approximately 20–30% of CMML patients. The most frequent aberrations are trisomy 8, monosomy 7/del(7q), del(20q), complex karyotype, and − Y [34]. None are CMML-specific; their primary value lies not in disease classification but in risk stratification through the CMML-specific prognostic scoring system (CPSS) [35] and its molecular derivative (CPSS-Mol) [28]. The more recent BLAST and BLAST-Mol models integrate molecular and cytogenetic data with clinical variables for improved survival prediction across CMML subtypes [36].

Controversies in CMML Definition

OM-CMML vs. MDS: The Case for Refined Criteria

The formal incorporation of OM-CMML, defined by AMC 0.5–<1.0 × 109/L with relative monocytosis, into the WHO5 and ICC classifications effectively loosened the criteria for CMML, which now encompasses cases that would previously have been classified as MDS. This change generated a diagnostic controversy: does the OM-CMML category, as currently defined, represent a biologically coherent entity equivalent to overt CMML, or does it constitute a heterogeneous admixture of true CMML and MDS with low-level monocytosis? The answer, emerging from several large genomic studies, appears to be both, and the distinction may be driven primarily by molecular profile.

Geyer et al. first introduced the concept of OM-CMML in 2017, demonstrating that cases with AMC 0.5–<1.0 × 109/L that met other CMML criteria showed morphologic, immunophenotypic, and mutational profiles resembling overt CMML [11]. Importantly, bone marrow (BM) morphological features were analyzed in the study, revealing that only 32% of OM-CMML cases showed a granulocytic proliferation (increased M: E ratio), compared with 68% of overt CMML cases. Calvo et al. subsequently confirmed that OM-CMML exhibited clinical and genomic profiles similar to those of overt CMML, with Mo1 immunophenotype achieving 84.9% sensitivity and 92.1% specificity when OM-CMML was considered a CMML subtype [12]. Calvo et al. further demonstrated that 29.3% of OM-CMML patients progressed to dysplastic CMML over a median 53-month follow-up period, suggesting that OM-CMML and MD-CMML form a continuum [13]. A Mayo Clinic/MD Anderson Cancer Center collaborative analysis of 849 patients [14] also found that OM-CMML (n = 57, 7% of all CMML) was clinically and molecularly similar to MD-CMML, with comparable leukemic transformation rates, overall survival, and mutational profiles, differing primarily by numerically higher TET2 mutation frequency (70% vs. 55%, p = 0.031) and more frequent isolated TET2 mutations (12% vs. 6%, p = 0.048) compared to overt CMML. Notably, 33% of evaluable OM-CMML patients had progressed to MD-CMML at last follow-up, and no patient progressed directly from OM-CMML to MP-CMML, supporting the concept of sequential disease evolution from OM-CMML to MD-CMML to MP-CMML (Fig. 2).

However, several subsequent studies have provided compelling arguments that OM-CMML is more heterogeneous than initially thought, and that the current WHO5 and ICC OM-CMML criteria should be refined. Montalban-Bravo et al. [26] demonstrated in 911 patients that six genomic clusters within OM-CMML range have fundamentally different biological identities: the biTET2/SRSF2 cluster, defined by biTET2 alterations or TET2 + SRSF2 co-mutation (33% of OM-CMML cases), showed the highest BM monocyte burden, most frequent Mo1 > 94%, and the highest risk of progression to overt CMML, arguing that this group constitutes “true CMML”. Supporting this hypothesis, Quang et al. [37] identified biTET2 as the dominant predictor of progression from MDS to CMML in a prospectively followed cohort of MDS patients. Conversely, the SF3B1 cluster (13%) and TP53-complex (mostly corresponding to biTP53 alteration) cluster (11%) showed low Mo1 > 94% frequency, low BM monocyte counts, and biologic features more akin to MDS. In a multimodal molecular clustering analysis by Lachowiez et al. [38], OM-CMML cases with SF3B1-mutated, DNMT3A-mutated, or del(5q) genomic profiles segregated with MDS, not CMML. In another study, Xicoy et al. showed that CMML with an SF3B1 mutation had lower PB monocyte counts compared with other CMML subtypes. These cases resembled MDS with an SF3B1 mutation more than CMML in terms of their blood counts and outcomes: patients had longer overall survival (median of 6.75 years versus 3.17 years in other CMML cases) and a lower risk of progression to AML [20, 28]. In a large series from the Munich Leukemia Laboratory (MLL), Baumgartner et al. [39] demonstrated that applying the ICC morphologic criteria resulted in fewer cases classified as OM-CMML compared to the WHO5 criteria based on blood counts and dysplasia, and that combining morphology with molecular profiling yielded a more homogeneous OM-CMML group. Baloda et al. validated this finding in a separate cohort of OM-CMML patients [40].

These data collectively support a practical refinement of the OM-CMML criteria: molecular profiling should be considered for cases with AMC in the 0.5–<1.0 × 109/L range. Cases with biTET2 or TET2 + SRSF2 are biologically akin to CMML and should be classified as such. However, cases with SF3B1, bi-allelic TP53, or isolated del(5q) may be more appropriately classified as MDS, even in the setting of relative monocytosis (≥ 10%).

Refining CMML Criteria: Integrating Monocyte Partitioning, BM Histology, and Genetics

Although WHO5 and ICC show substantial overlap in their CMML diagnostic approaches, they differ in how they incorporate BM morphology, cytopenia requirement, and the decision-directing role of monocyte subset partitioning by flow cytometry. These differences generate meaningful diagnostic discordance in clinical practice, particularly in the OM-CMML range, as highlighted by Baumgartner et al. and Baloda et al. [39, 40].

The diagnostic criteria for CMML for WHO5 and ICC are compared in Table 1. WHO5 requires either dysplasia, abnormal monocyte partitioning, or a clonal abnormality to diagnose CMML when the AMC ≥ 1.0 × 109/L, and mandates the presence of both clonality and dysplasia for cases with AMC 0.5–<1.0 × 109/L. The ICC has a similar approach but additionally requires some cytopenia and abnormal bone marrow morphology (hypercellularity with granulocytic proliferation, with or without increased bone marrow monocytes) for all CMML. Like WHO5, the ICC requires clonality (with a minimal mutation VAF of 10%) for cases with AMC 0.5–<1.0 × 109/L and requires the CMML-type bone marrow morphology, but not dysplasia. The ICC also applied a broader definition of “abnormal monocyte immunophenotype”, which could be evidenced by either abnormal Mo1 partitioning or other immunophenotypic aberrancy (such as CD56 or CD2 expression).

Aside from a sustained AMC ≥ 0.5 × 109/L and sustained relative monocytosis of ≥ 10% (optimally for at least 3 months), CMML should have < 20% blasts and absence of BCR::ABL1 and other tyrosine kinase (TK) fusions defining myeloid/lymphoid neoplasm with TK fusions. Although not explicitly stated in WHO5 or ICC criteria, exclusion of AML-defining genetic alterations (NPM1, KMT2A rearrangements, core binding factor gene fusions, etc.) is supported by accumulating evidence that such cases are biologically and prognostically akin to AML regardless of blast count [38, 41–43].

For cases with sustained AMC ≥ 1.0 × 109/L, the ICC and WHO5 general diagnostic criteria appear appropriate and correspond well to historical defined CMML cases. However, for OM-CMML cases with monocytes 0.5 – <1.0 × 109/L, emerging data suggest that additional CMML-specific criteria should be applied to avoid overclassifying MDS cases (which may have mild monocytosis) as CMML. As mandated by the ICC, the OM-CMML cases should demonstrate both clonality and cytopenia and future classifications could consider assignment to CMML if supported by an additional feature: (i) CMML-type morphology: increased myeloid-to-erythroid ratio (> 2:1) and/or increased BM monocytes; (ii) abnormal monocyte subset partitioning by flow cytometry (defined as Mo1 > 94% or slan + ≤ 1.7% in cases with a high inflammatory profile as suggested by the presence of an Mo1 monocyte bulge) or (iii) a CMML molecular signature, defined as biTET2 (≥ 2 mutations, or single mutation with 4q24 loss, or VAF ≥ 55%), TET2 + SRSF2 co-mutation, or TET2 + ASXL1 co-mutation. This last criterion would represent a major conceptual advance, as it would elevate specific molecular signatures to the status of qualifying diagnostic determinants. The use of flow cytometry monocyte partitioning to aid in distinguishing MDS from CMML is supported by a recent study by Sélimoglu-Buet et al., which demonstrated that 35% of patients with MDS and Mo1 ≥ 94% eventually progressed to overt CMML [44].

Finally, the presence of bi-allelic TP53 alteration identifies a highly aggressive myeloid disease phenotype that transcends conventional MDS-MDS/MPN-AML group boundaries, with morphology, blast percentage, and therapy-related status having only minimal effect on patient trajectory. Cases with bi-allelic TP53 alterations, even if meeting diagnostic criteria for CMML, would be more appropriately classified with other TP53-mutated myeloid neoplasms. The justification for this proposal is grounded in a convergence of molecular, prognostic, and therapeutic evidence demonstrating that multihit TP53 alterations confer a disease trajectory so distinct from canonical CMML that retaining these cases within the CMML diagnostic category risks biologic misclassification and potentially inappropriate therapeutic approach. Evidence to support excluding these cases from CMML is a median overall survival of only 10 months for biTP53-mutated CMML in one series [33] and a median OS of only 1.6 years for the biTP53 cluster in another recent nine-class CMML molecular taxonomy [32].

Disease Evolution

Clonal Hematopoiesis and the Pre-CMML Spectrum

CMML does not arise de novo in most patients but rather evolves from a defined clonal precursor landscape; clonal hematopoiesis (CH) precedes the majority of myeloid neoplasms, including CMML [45]. In CMML, the canonical early driver mutations are TET2 and SRSF2, which promote monocytic differentiation bias in the bone marrow and establish the epigenetic and spliceosome mutation backbone upon which signaling mutations subsequently accumulate. (Fig. 2)

Clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS), defined as CH with accompanying unexplained cytopenia but without diagnostic morphologic criteria for a myeloid neoplasm, is a precursor state that confers a risk for developing myeloid neoplasia. Clonal monocytosis of undetermined significance (CMUS) and clonal cytopenic monocytosis of undetermined significance (CCMUS) specifically describe clonal monocytoses that do not meet diagnostic criteria for CMML. Dunn et al. [46] quantified the population-level burden of CMUS/CCMUS in UK Biobank (n = 431,531) and Danish (n = 625,328) cohorts, demonstrating that clonal monocytoses confer a substantial myeloid neoplasm risk (higher than CCUS without monocytosis) that warrants active surveillance.

A sequential transition from CMUS/CCMUS to OM-CMML and then to overt CMML is supported by longitudinal data. In the Dunn et al. study, CCMUS specifically was associated with a high risk of subsequent progression to CMML, while CCUS and CMUS with lower monocyte counts (0.5-<1.0 × 109/L) had a lower risk of progression to CMML [40, 46]. Quang et al. [37] identified biTET2 as the strongest predictor of MDS to CMML progression in a prospectively followed cohort, with a hazard ratio substantially exceeding that of any other individual mutation. In studies of patients with established OM-CMML, predictors of progression to overt CMML included the presence of more than three mutated genes, an ASXL1 mutation, or biTET2 or TET2 + SRSF2 co-mutations. Peripheral blood monocyte percentage > 20% [13, 37] and increased BM monocytes also correlated with subsequent progression to overt CMML [26].

Molecular Evolution of CMML: MD-CMML to MP-CMML

The transition from MD-CMML to MP-CMML (Fig. 3) represents a clinically significant disease evolution, typically associated with splenomegaly, constitutional symptoms, greater mutational complexity, and worse prognosis.

The clonal architecture of this MD-to-MP evolution is incompletely characterized at the single-cell resolution required to fully map the acquisition of driver and passenger mutations. However, it is well known that the founding clone, almost invariably harboring TET2 and/or SRSF2 mutations, persists across both phenotypes, whereas signaling mutations arise as subclonal events that confer a proliferative advantage and drive phenotypic shift. In a large Mayo-GFM-Austrian cohort study, clonal RAS pathway mutations [47] (predominantly NRAS G12D) emerge as defining molecular features of MP-CMML, occurring at a significantly higher frequency (46% vs. 25%) and with higher VAFs than in MD-CMML. These mutations appear to arise early in the evolution of MP-CMML rather than being late, subclonal acquisitions, and their presence correlates directly with aggressive disease features, including leukocytosis, peripheral blood blasts, and AML transformation risk.

Mechanistically, RAS pathway mutations drive transcriptional upregulation of the mitotic checkpoint kinase PLK1 through an epigenetic axis involving KMT2A, which deposits activating H3K4me1 marks at the PLK1 promoter in a RAS mutation-specific manner. This axis is distinct from MP-CMML driven by JAK2 mutations and from subclonal RAS pathway mutations that are seen in some MD-CMML, underscoring its specificity to MP-CMML biology. Pharmacologic PLK1 inhibition with volasertib demonstrated selective efficacy in RAS-mutant patient-derived xenografts, reducing monocytic infiltrates and restoring hematopoiesis [47], thus establishing a rationale for biomarker-driven therapeutic targeting in this relatively treatment-refractory disease.

CMML to AML: Mechanisms and Molecular Drivers of Leukemic Transformation

Approximately 15–20% of CMML patients undergo leukemic transformation to AML over three to five years of follow-up [1]. This transformation is heralded by increasing blast counts (such as progression from CMML-1 to CMML-2) and is typically accompanied by the acquisition of additional somatic mutations and progressive cytopenias. Secondary AML arising from CMML carries a significantly worse prognosis than de novo AML [2].

Mutations commonly acquired at the time of AML transformation include RUNX1, RAS pathway genes (particularly NRAS and KRAS), FLT3, and additional ASXL1 alterations [48]. AML-defining genetic alterations, such as NPM1 mutations (Fig. 4), are associated with aggressive biology in CMML, supporting the inclusion of these cases within AML rather than CMML [49, 50]. A clinically important and underappreciated caveat pertains to cases of previously undiagnosed CMML that acquire NPM1 mutation and progress to AML. In the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) 2022 risk classification, NPM1-mutated AML (without FLT3 ITD) is designated as favorable risk [51]. However, NPM1-mutated AML arising from an antecedent CMML (or harboring biTET2 genetic signature, suggesting possible antecedent CMML) appears to be biologically distinct from de novo NPM1-mutated AML, and some recent studies suggest it has a less favorable prognosis [52]. Practically speaking, the persistence or emergence of CMML-characteristic features (such as a mature monocytic proliferation and significant background dysplasia) in a remission BM together with a molecular profile showing persistence of canonical CMML drivers (biTET2, TET2 + SRSF2 or TET2 + ASXL1 co-mutation patterns), could be considered suggestive of a secondary AML that has evolved from an underlying CMML. These findings could indicate more aggressive behavior, even if the patient presented with an apparently genetically favorable NPM1-mutated AML. However, further study is needed to elucidate the biologic behavior of this subset of NPM1-mutated AML.

CMML- Associated Phenomena

Blastic Plasmacytoid Dendritic Cell Neoplasm (BPDCN) and Mature pDC Proliferations

BPDCN is an aggressive hematologic malignancy derived from plasmacytoid dendritic cell (pDC) precursors characterized by skin involvement, BM infiltration, and frequent secondary leukemic dissemination. Its association with CMML represents a phenomenon of convergent lineage dysregulation of biological interest. CMML is the most common antecedent myeloid neoplasm in patients who develop secondary BPDCN, and mature pDC expansions have also been documented in CMML BM specimens, particularly in MP-CMML, suggesting a shared monocyte/dendritic cell progenitor dysregulation.

BPDCN characteristically consists of a proliferation of medium-sized monotonous blasts with irregular nuclei, fine chromatin, and scant agranular cytoplasm, cells with so-called “plasmacytoid” morphology. It frequently involves the skin, but can involve other organs such as lymph nodes, spleen, and BM. Bone marrow involvement shows interstitial or diffuse infiltration by blastic cells. The core diagnostic triad of markers for BPDCN includes expression of CD4, CD56, and CD123 (IL-3Rα) on cells that are otherwise negative for lineage-defining antigens. Additional pDC markers include HLA-DR, BDCA-2 (CD303), BDCA-4 (CD304/Neuropilin-1), TCL1, TCF4 [53, 54] along with variable expression of TdT. BCL2 [55]and IRF8 [56] are also frequently expressed. CMML and BPDCN may arise together from a shared TET2-mutated progenitor cell founder clone, while maintaining divergent downstream evolutionary trajectories [57].

Approximately 20% of CMML patients demonstrate clonal expansion of mature pDCs forming irregular nodular islands in the BM (Fig. 5). Critically, this accumulation of pDCs is biologically and genetically distinct from BPDCN. CMML-associated pDCs are morphologically mature, typically lack CD56 (common in BPDCN) and TdT expression, have a low proliferation index, and do not harbor the MYC, RB1, or IKAROS family alterations characteristic of BPDCN. Whole-exome sequencing of sorted CMML pDCs confirmed the absence of BPDCN-associated genetic alterations, reinforcing that this represents a reactive clonal skewing of myeloid differentiation rather than a neoplastic pDC precursor proliferation. Despite this, these mature pDC proliferations are integral members of the leukemic clone, sharing identical somatic mutations (particularly in TET2 and RAS pathway genes) with the CMML monocytes at concordant VAFs. Clinically, pDC proliferations confer a higher cumulative incidence of AML transformation in CMML [58, 59]. Their presence also correlates with an expansion of regulatory T cells, suggesting an immunosuppressive microenvironment that may facilitate disease progression [60].

Systemic Mastocytosis with Associated CMML

Systemic mastocytosis (SM) occurring concurrently with CMML (SM-CMML) is a common variant of “SM with an associated hematologic/myeloid neoplasm” (SM-AHN/AMN) [9, 10]. The mast cell component is driven by the KIT D816V mutation in nearly all cases, while the CMML component harbors the characteristic CMML-type mutations. In most cases, these mutations arise in separate or partially overlapping clones, with the KIT D816V mutation typically occurring as a subclone and with TET2 as the founding mutation in the ancestral clone, as demonstrated by clonal hierarchy analysis [45].

The diagnostic approach to SM-AHN/AMN in the setting of CMML requires concurrent recognition and characterization of both components: BM mast cell aggregates (typically with aberrant CD25 and/or CD2 expression and the KIT D816V mutation) and the aforementioned diagnostic criteria for CMML. KIT D816V genotyping should be systematically pursued in all CMML patients presenting with unexplained organomegaly, urticaria pigmentosa, or elevated tryptase levels, which may potentially reveal an associated SM [61].

The distinction between SM-CMML and myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with eosinophilia and tyrosine kinase gene fusions (MLN-TK-Eo) is diagnostically critical, given the overlapping morphologic and immunophenotypic features. In SM-CMML, mast cells form characteristic dense, compact clusters (> 15 mast cells) with aberrant CD25 and/or CD2 expression, arising within the context of a KIT D816V-driven SM clone superimposed on the CMML molecular background. However, MLN-TK-Eo, most commonly encompassing neoplasms driven by rearrangements of PDGFRA, PDGFRB, FGFR1, JAK2, and FLT3 and, less commonly, other TKs, can also demonstrate significant mast cell proliferation, and the pattern of mast cell distribution may vary by fusion type. For example, PDGFRA-rearranged cases (most commonly FIP1L1::PDGFRA) characteristically show loose mast cell aggregates rather than compact aggregates of true SM [62], while PDGFRB-rearranged cases may show increased dispersed mast cell infiltrates and additionally often show monocytosis, potentially simulating SM-CMML [63]. Mast cells in MLN-TK-Eo cases may also express CD25, further compounding the mimicry of SM and potentially leading to an erroneous diagnosis of SM-CMML if confirmatory molecular genetic testing is not systematically performed. Given that PDGFRA and PDGFRB rearrangements confer sensitivity to tyrosine kinase inhibitors, a therapeutic opportunity entirely distinct from the management of SM-CMML, the clinical stakes of misclassification are substantial. It is therefore imperative that all cases of apparent SM-CMML, particularly those with peripheral eosinophilia and absent KIT mutation, undergo a systematic and comprehensive molecular workup, including FISH, RT-PCR, and/or RNA-based fusion panels for PDGFRA, PDGFRB, and if appropriate, for other TK fusions, before a definitive diagnosis of SM-CMML is rendered.

Spectrum of Extramedullary CMML

Extramedullary disease is a recognized and clinically consequential manifestation of CMML, occurring most prominently in the MP subtype [1]. Splenomegaly, the most common extramedullary manifestation, is present in 20–30% of MP-CMML patients at diagnosis and reflects extramedullary myeloid hematopoiesis driven by monocytic and granulocytic progenitors trafficking from the BM. Hepatomegaly and lymphadenopathy, though less common, occur in the context of hepatic sinusoidal infiltration and nodal monocytic infiltration, respectively. Thus, the latter may mimic histiocytic neoplasms on biopsy. Extramedullary CMML also may present as periorbital, gingival, and serosal (pleural and peritoneal) monocytic infiltrations. Thus, CMML should be included in the differential diagnosis of any unexplained monocytic infiltrate in the skin or serosal surfaces of a middle-aged to elderly patient. Extramedullary disease in CMML is associated with an inferior prognosis and can herald the development of myeloid sarcoma (equivalent to AML progression), in which blasts and promonocytes rather than mature cells form an extramedullary mass [64].

Cutaneous manifestations of CMML are histologically diverse and represent one of the most distinctive extramedullary manifestations of CMML. They can precede, coincide with, or follow the diagnosis of CMML. A retrospective analysis of 42 cases by the French Study Group of Cutaneous Lymphomas identified four distinct histologic categories, highlighting the strong association of CMML with proliferations of dendritic cell lineage: (1) myelomonocytic tumors (CD68+/MPO+, dendritic marker-negative); (2) mature pDC tumors (CD123+/TCL1+/CD303+, CD56−); (3) BPDCN, with the classic CD4+/CD56+/CD123 + blast phenotype; and (4) a novel category of blastic indeterminate dendritic cell tumors co-expressing monocytic and dendritic markers (CD1a+/S100+). Clonal identity is typically shared between the CMML and these cutaneous proliferations, and there is a striking predominance of KRAS mutations in CMML cases that present with extramedullary disease [65–67].

Autoimmune and Inflammatory Manifestations of CMML

Systemic inflammatory and autoimmune phenomena are underrecognized but clinically important complications of CMML, occurring in 15–20% of patients and occasionally dominating the initial presentation [68]. The mechanistic basis likely involves constitutive activation of innate immune signaling in clonal monocytes, including dysregulation of TLR pathways and increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β), driven in part by the TET2 and SRSF2 mutations that define the CMML molecular backbone.

The spectrum is broad, ranging from well-defined autoimmune entities such as rheumatoid arthritis, polymyalgia rheumatica, systemic vasculitis, Sweet’s syndrome, other neutrophilic dermatoses, immune thrombocytopenia, and serositis, to undefined inflammatory syndromes such as seronegative inflammatory arthritis and lupus-like presentations that defy clear categorization. Importantly, these manifestations may precede the hematologic diagnosis by months to years; thus, CMML should be considered in elderly patients with unexplained or refractory inflammatory arthritis or neutrophilic dermatosis in the context of cytopenias and monocytosis.

The potential therapeutic relevance of monocyte-driven inflammation in CMML extends beyond supportive care. JAK1/2 inhibition (ruxolitinib) may attenuate the inflammatory cytokine milieu and alleviate systemic symptoms in CMML patients with prominent inflammatory manifestations, though prospective data are limited.

Conclusions and Future Directions

CMML is a molecularly heterogeneous MDS/MPN overlap neoplasm defined by sustained monocytosis and associated with a canonical TET2–SRSF2–ASXL1 mutational triad. Its classification has evolved substantially since its initial description by the FAB; the most recent classifications, WHO5 and ICC, while representing a major advance over prior classifications, have introduced new diagnostic complexities, particularly around the boundaries between CMML and MDS with borderline monocytosis.

Data now emerging from large molecularly annotated cohorts provide a compelling framework for refining OM-CMML criteria around molecular biology rather than arbitrary monocyte thresholds alone. By incorporating specific molecular signatures as diagnostic criteria, employing flow cytometry-based monocyte partitioning, and recognizing the bone marrow morphologic pattern, greater precision can be achieved in defining CMML as a biologically distinct disease. This is a critical issue for patient management, since CMML has unique prognostic models, treatment approaches, and therapeutic response criteria. Additionally, clinical trials may specify inclusion criteria for CMML versus MDS patients, and thus, increased accuracy in identifying these groups can improve the development of disease-specific therapies.

Disease evolution from CH/CMUS through OM-CMML to MD-CMML, MP-CMML, and AML follows well-defined mutational trajectories. The associated phenomena of BPDCN, SM, extramedullary disease, and autoimmune manifestations are not merely clinical complications or coincidental occurrences but rather reflect biologically relevant aspects of CMML’s clonal architecture and monocyte lineage dysregulation.

For the practicing hematopathologist, the imperatives are clear: accurate diagnosis requires considering morphology, flow cytometric monocyte partitioning, and comprehensive molecular profiling. Recognition of CMML-associated phenomena is also critical to direct appropriate clinical interventions. Finally, continued comprehensive molecular interrogation of CMML and related myeloid proliferations is needed to support the current classification, refine future classifications, and reveal patterns of disease evolution.
