Quality and performance of machine learning versus logistic regression for predicting IVIG resistance in Kawasaki disease: a PROBAST+AI systematic comparison
Authors: Jiaying Zhang, Difan Wang, Jinfeng Dong, Ying He, Ying Liu, Tingjiao You, Jing Li, Lizhi Li, Xiaodan Wu, Qiuyu Tang, Shurong Ma, Panpan Liu, Haitao Lv, Hongbiao Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to systematically compare the predictive performance and methodological quality of logistic regression (LR) and machine learning (ML) models for intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) resistance in Kawasaki disease (KD) using the PROBAST + AI framework.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science to identify studies on prediction models for IVIG resistance in KD published between January 1, 2006, and July 31, 2025. We assessed methodological rigour, risk of bias, and applicability using PROBAST + AI. A meta-analysis was performed using random-effects models with logit-transformed area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values. Subgroup, sensitivity, and publication bias analyses were additionally conducted.ResultsWe identified 52 eligible studies (40 LR and 12 ML). In external validation, pooled AUCs were similar between ML and LR models (0.76 [95% CI 0.64–0.86] vs. 0.75 [95% CI 0.68–0.81]). In internal validation, ML showed a slightly higher pooled AUC than LR (0.86 [95% CI 0.78–0.92] vs. 0.76 [95% CI 0.72–0.79]), although no statistically significant differences were observed. All studies were judged to be at high risk of bias, mainly due to retrospective single-centre designs, inadequate handling of missing data and continuous predictors, and poor reporting of calibration and clinical utility. No study reported sample size calculations.ConclusionsGiven the limited external validation and substantial heterogeneity across studies, ML does not consistently outperform LR in predicting IVIG resistance in KD. Future studies should prioritise rigorous external validation and adherence to TRIPOD + AI and PROBAST + AI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-026-02893-2.Keywords: Kawasaki disease, IVIG resistance, Machine learning, Logistic regression, Systematic review, PROBAST + AI

Introduction

Kawasaki disease (KD) is an acute systemic vasculitis and the leading cause of acquired heart disease in children in developed countries [1]. Although high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) reduces coronary complications [2], 10–20% of patients develop IVIG resistance, which markedly increases the risk of coronary artery abnormalities (CAAs) [3]. Previous studies have shown that, in patients at high risk of IVIG resistance, corticosteroids combined with initial IVIG therapy are more effective than IVIG alone in preventing CAAs [4]. Therefore, early identification of IVIG-resistant patients may help guide individualized treatment and reduce coronary complications.

To address this clinical need, various prediction models for IVIG resistance have been developed. Early predictive efforts mainly relied on traditional regression-based scores, such as the Kobayashi [5], Egami [6], and Sano scores [7]. However, their performance varies across populations, limiting external generalisability [8], largely due to heterogeneity in patient characteristics and clinical settings [9]. Consequently, prediction models often require recalibration or adjustment before implementation in different populations [9, 10].

With advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and the increasing availability of electronic health record (EHR) data, machine learning (ML) has been increasingly applied to prediction modelling [11]. However, whether ML outperforms logistic regression (LR) remains uncertain. Although some studies favour ML [12, 13], others have reported no clear advantage over LR when using structured clinical data [14, 15]. ML may better capture nonlinear relationships, whereas LR remains advantageous for its interpretability and clinical feasibility [14].

The Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool for Artificial Intelligence (PROBAST + AI) [16] is an updated tool for assessing methodological quality, risk of bias, and applicability of prediction models using regression or artificial intelligence methods. This structured framework facilitates the identification of high-quality prediction models and supports their reliable application in clinical practice. Using this framework, we systematically compared ML and LR models to inform future prediction model research in KD.

Methods

This study followed the CHARMS (Checklist for Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modelling Studies) checklist for structured appraisal and data extraction [17]. Methodological quality, risk of bias, and applicability were assessed using PROBAST + AI [16], while model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Study objectives, search strategy, and eligibility criteria were prespecified according to the PICOTS (Population, Index model, Comparator model, Outcome, Timing, Setting) framework (Supplementary Table S1) [16]. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261303670), and study selection followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [18].

Literature search

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase to identify English-language studies on prediction models for IVIG resistance in KD published between January 2006 and July 2025. Both controlled vocabulary terms, including MeSH terms in PubMed and EMTREE terms in Embase, and free-text terms related to Kawasaki disease, IVIG resistance, and prediction models or model performance were used with Boolean operators. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened. Conference abstracts and grey literature, including preprints and unpublished studies, were excluded because they may not have undergone peer review and often provide incomplete methodological information. Detailed search strategies are provided in Appendix A of the Supplementary Materials.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) developed using KD populations; (2) incorporated at least two predictors; and (3) clearly defined IVIG resistance as the outcome, and reported model development and/or validation performance.

Studies were excluded if they: (1) were not original research articles; (2) did not involve prediction model development or validation; (3) defined outcomes beyond IVIG resistance or within specific clinical contexts; (4) were based on a single biomarker only; or (5) did not report outcomes of interest.

Data extraction and PROBAST + AI assessment

ML- and LR-based prediction models were evaluated from two complementary perspectives: predictive performance (discrimination, calibration, and DCA) and key study characteristics, including first author and year of publication, geographic region, study design, modelling algorithm, study setting, reporting guideline adherence, number and type of predictors, sample size, and model evaluation strategy. Two reviewers (JZ and DW) independently performed title/abstract screening, full-text eligibility assessment, and data extraction, with disagreements resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (HH). Extracted data were additionally verified by JD and YH.

Model quality, risk of bias, and applicability were assessed using PROBAST + AI, with detailed assessment procedures described in Appendix B and Supplementary Table S2.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the results. Penalised LR methods, including least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), ridge, and elastic net regression, were classified as LR rather than ML models [14]. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used as the primary performance metric and prioritised according to validation level: external validation (evaluation in an independent dataset), internal validation (including bootstrap resampling, cross-validation, random training–test splits, and temporal splits), and apparent performance (evaluation using the same dataset as model development) [19].

The analyses comprised two aspects. First, cross-study comparisons were performed to evaluate the overall predictive performance of ML and LR models across different studies. Second, head-to-head comparisons were conducted for studies reporting both ML and LR models within the same dataset, in which average AUC differences were calculated.

Quantitative synthesis was limited by heterogeneity in AUC reporting. AUCs were categorised by reporting completeness as: (A) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs); (B) with numbers of positive and negative cases allowing standard error estimation [20]; and (C) insufficiently reported for quantitative synthesis, including one study lacking both CIs and sample size information, and eleven models without reported AUC values, which were excluded from quantitative synthesis but retained for narrative analysis when applicable.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5). Logit-transformed AUC values were used to improve variance stabilisation and approximate normality before pooling. Random-effects models were applied because substantial heterogeneity was anticipated across studies, and heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic. Differences between ML and LR models were evaluated using subgroup Q tests with regression coefficients and 95% CIs. Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted according to region, predictor number, and missing-data handling strategy, while leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed to assess robustness. Funnel plots and Egger’s regression tests were used to assess funnel plot asymmetry and possible small-study effects.

Results

A total of 924 records were identified through database searching. After removing 435 duplicates, 41 studies were included following screening and full-text assessment. An additional 11 studies identified through manual searching were also included. In total, 52 studies were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1; Table 1, and Supplementary Table S3).

General study characteristics

Among the 52 included studies, 12 (23.1%) applied ML methods [3, 21–31], whereas 40 (76.9%) used LR approaches [5–7, 32–68]. Most studies were conducted in East Asia, and the number of published ML and LR models increased after 2015 (Fig. 2A-B).

Most studies employed retrospective cohort designs. Only eight LR studies adopted a prospective design [35, 38, 43, 46, 47, 53, 55, 62], involving either prospective model development or prospective validation, and no prospective ML studies were identified. Adherence to TRIPOD + AI (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis for Artificial Intelligence) was reported in only one study [27], and one study reported adherence to STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) [67].

Overview of algorithms

Thirty-seven studies applied standard logistic regression, whereas three used penalised LR with LASSO regularisation. Among ML studies, gradient boosting–based algorithms were most common, followed by random forest (RF) (Supplementary Table S4).

Methods for handling missing data

Complete-case analysis was the most common missing-data handling strategy, used in 50.0% of ML studies [3, 21, 22, 24, 26, 30] and 50.0% of LR studies [5, 33, 36, 38, 41–43, 45, 48, 49, 51, 54, 55, 59, 60, 62, 64, 65, 67, 68]. Only twelve studies explicitly reported missing-data handling methods, of which seven used multiple imputation [28, 29, 40, 47, 52, 58, 61]. However, only one study provided detailed information on the proportion of missing data and imputation procedures [47].

Simple imputation methods, including mean, median, or mode substitution, were reported in three ML studies [23, 27, 31], while random forest–based imputation was used in one ML study and one LR study [25, 46]. In addition, 35.0% of LR studies (14/40) did not report missing-data handling methods [6, 7, 32, 34, 35, 37, 39, 44, 50, 53, 56, 57, 63, 66].

Sample size adequacy

Notably, no study performed a formal sample size calculation. Only one ML study used learning-curve analysis [3], assessing whether model performance stabilized as the sample size increased.

Predictive variables

Only 17 studies appropriately handled continuous predictors and categorical predictors during model development [3, 21, 22, 24, 25, 27–31, 40, 47, 52, 53, 58, 60, 61], whereas 35/52 studies dichotomised continuous variables.

ML models used 3–82 predictors (mean = 18), with 41.7% including ≥ 15 predictors, whereas LR models used 2–10 predictors (mean = 5), most using ≤ 5 predictors. C-reactive protein (CRP) was the most frequently used predictor in ML models, while neutrophil percentage (NEU%) and albumin (ALB) were most common in LR models (Fig. 2C–D, Supplementary Table S3).

Model development, validation, and performance

All twelve ML studies reported internal validation, most commonly using cross-validation [3, 23, 25, 27, 28, 31], while random data splitting was frequently used [21, 22, 24, 26]. Among LR studies, nine reported internal validation, mainly through random splitting [40, 53, 61], with additional methods including cross-validation [42, 58], time-series splitting [52, 66], and bootstrap resampling [60, 67] (Supplementary Table S4).

Class imbalance handling was uncommon. Five ML studies applied over-sampling techniques, including the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), SMOTE for Nominal and Continuous features (SMOTE-NC), and Random Over-Sampling Examples (ROSE) [23, 27–29, 31], but none reported evaluation on uncorrected datasets, post-correction class distributions, or recalibration results.

AUC was the most commonly reported discrimination metric. Only one ML model reported a calibration curve during internal validation [25]. In contrast, among LR models, 12 reported Hosmer–Lemeshow tests [5, 6, 33, 36–38, 45, 48, 49, 54, 57, 59] and 3 reported calibration curves at apparent performance [5, 47, 59]; 4 reported Hosmer–Lemeshow tests [40, 53, 58, 61] and 6 reported calibration curves during internal validation [52, 53, 58, 60, 61, 67]; and two models reported both the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and calibration curves during external validation [5, 53]. Assessment of clinical utility was limited, with DCA reported for only two ML models during internal validation [21, 25] and for seven LR models (apparent or internal validation) [52, 53, 58–61, 67], and none during external validation.

Comparison between performance of LR and ML

As shown in Fig. 3A–C, pooled AUCs in external validation were similar for ML (0.76, 95% CI 0.64–0.86) and LR (0.75, 95% CI 0.68–0.81), with no significant difference (QM = 0.10; p = 0.75); ML models showed no detectable heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), likely reflecting the limited number of externally validated studies, whereas LR models exhibited moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 65.95%).

In internal validation, ML showed a slightly higher pooled AUC than LR (0.86 [95% CI 0.78–0.92] vs. 0.76 [95% CI 0.72–0.79]), but the difference was not statistically significant (QM = 3.62; p = 0.057). ML models demonstrated considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 96.87%), suggesting substantial variation in performance across studies, whereas LR models showed moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 43.65%). Apparent performance was reported only for LR models (pooled AUC 0.78 [95% CI 0.75–0.81], I² = 80.2%), with no ML data available.

Furthermore, Supplementary Table S5 was added to transparently report the predictive performance of all models included in the head-to-head comparison studies. As shown in Fig. 3D, among the seven head-to-head comparison studies, LR models demonstrated better average performance than ML models in five studies (ΔAUC range: 0.0094–0.1095) [21, 25, 26, 28, 58], whereas ML models performed better in two studies (ΔAUC range: 0.0008–0.094) [3, 22]. Notably, all studies were judged to have a high risk of bias.

However, one apparent-performance study reported an AUC but did not provide the corresponding 95% confidence interval or sample size information and was therefore excluded from the meta-analysis [62]. In addition, four apparent-performance models from four studies [7, 32, 34, 39] and seven externally validated models from five studies [35, 41, 48, 50, 63] did not report AUC values and were therefore excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis (Supplementary Table S6).

Publication bias assessment

Substantial heterogeneity was observed in the apparent-performance (I² = 80.2%) and internal validation analyses (I² = 94.8%), and Egger’s regression test indicated significant funnel plot asymmetry, suggesting possible small-study effects (p = 0.0002 and p = 0.012, respectively) (Supplementary Figs. 1–2). Meanwhile, the external validation analysis showed moderate heterogeneity (I² = 58.6%) and no significant funnel plot asymmetry or evidence of small-study effects (p = 0.240) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted according to geographic region, number of predictors, and missing-data handling strategy. In apparent-performance analyses, pooled AUCs were slightly higher in Chinese than non-Chinese studies (0.803 vs. 0.761), although substantial heterogeneity persisted across subgroups (Supplementary Table S7). Models with ≤ 5 and > 5 predictors showed similar pooled AUCs (0.781 vs. 0.785). Studies using complete-case analysis showed slightly higher pooled AUCs than studies with other or unclear missing-data handling strategies (0.795 vs. 0.758).

For ML models with internal validation, pooled AUCs were higher in Chinese studies than in non-Chinese studies (0.888 vs. 0.808), in models with > 5 predictors than in those with ≤ 5 predictors (0.901 vs. 0.789), and in studies using non-complete-case approaches than in those using complete-case analysis (0.912 vs. 0.802). However, substantial heterogeneity persisted across all subgroups (Supplementary Table S8).

To further explore heterogeneity, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses showed that pooled AUC estimates remained stable after sequential exclusion of individual studies. For apparent-performance analyses of LR models, pooled AUCs ranged from 0.777 to 0.786, with persistent heterogeneity (I² range, 76.4%–81.3%). For internal validation analyses, pooled AUCs ranged from 0.805 to 0.829; exclusion of the study by Gahao Chen et al. [27] and Gang Luo et al. [31] reduced I² to 91.3% and 92.3%, respectively. For external validation analyses, pooled AUCs ranged from 0.727 to 0.764, and exclusion of Kobayashi et al. [5] reduced I² from 58.6% to 37.2%. In ML models with internal validation, pooled AUCs ranged from 0.836 to 0.875, whereas heterogeneity remained high in all analyses (I² range, 94.6%–97.4%).

Discussion

Comparison between ML and LR models

Performance comparison between ML and LR models

Recent advances in AI and the increasing availability of EHR data have driven the growing use of ML in clinical prediction modelling; however, its performance advantage over LR remains debated. In the context of acute kidney injury (AKI) prediction, Song et al. found no significant difference in AUC between ML and LR [15]. Similarly, in major chronic disease risk prediction, Nusinovici et al. evaluated 6762 adults in a prospective cohort and reported that ML methods did not confer meaningful performance gains compared with LR [69]. LR remains advantageous when predictors are well-defined and data dimensionality is low, whereas ML may be better suited to complex or high-dimensional data [70]. Notably, in our study, only 1/12 ML studies underwent external validation [3], and most were developed in single-centre retrospective cohorts, limiting the generalisability and reliability of model comparisons. Therefore, the current evidence remains insufficient to demonstrate a stable and consistent advantage of ML models over LR models.

In addition, funnel plot asymmetry and statistically significant Egger’s regression tests were observed in the apparent-performance and internal-validation analyses, suggesting possible small-study effects or selective reporting of better-performing models. Together with the persistently high heterogeneity across studies, these findings indicate that apparent-performance and internal-validation results should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered substitutes for rigorous external validation. In contrast, although the number of external validation studies remained limited, the lower heterogeneity and absence of statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry suggest that external-validation estimates may provide a more reliable approximation of model transportability than apparent-performance or internal-validation estimates.

Furthermore, substantial heterogeneity existed across studies in terms of population characteristics, predictor selection, and modelling strategies, which may have affected the reliability of performance comparisons and the generalisability of the conclusions. Future studies should therefore place greater emphasis on rigorous external validation across different regions, populations, and clinical settings to improve the robustness, transportability, and real-world applicability of prediction models.

Seven studies conducted head-to-head comparisons using the same datasets. Among these studies, LR models demonstrated better average performance than ML models in five studies [21, 25, 26, 28, 58], whereas ML models performed better in two studies [3, 22]. Notably, all of these studies were judged to be at high risk of bias. This variability may reflect methodological limitations across studies, as well as the greater modelling flexibility of ML algorithms, warranting further investigation.

Practical trade-offs between LR and ML models

LR models are generally easier to interpret and implement, making them suitable for low-dimensional structured clinical data [14]. In contrast, ML models can capture nonlinear effects and complex interactions but often require larger sample sizes, feature engineering, hyperparameter tuning, robust internal validation, and post-development calibration [69, 71]. ML models may also be more difficult to implement and maintain in routine clinical workflows unless they are embedded within electronic health record systems with appropriate monitoring and updating procedures [72]. Therefore, modest AUC improvements alone may not justify the additional complexity of ML models unless accompanied by better calibration, clinical utility, and implementation feasibility.

Methodological limitations revealed by PROBAST + AI

The PROBAST + AI assessment identified substantial methodological limitations and high risk of bias in both ML and LR models, mainly related to participant selection and analysis domains. Key methodological shortcomings are summarized below.

Study design and participant representativeness

Most bias identified by PROBAST + AI originated from the Participants and Data Sources domains, mainly due to single-centre retrospective designs that limited representativeness and generalisability.

Dichotomization of predictors

ML models generally included more predictors, likely reflecting advances in AI methods and increased availability of EHR data. Notably, the remaining 35/52 studies dichotomised continuous predictors. Dichotomization of continuous variables has been recognised as a major methodological concern because it may lead to information loss, unreasonable risk estimation, increased risk of bias, and impaired predictive performance [73–75]. In addition, the cut-off values used for categorization are often somewhat arbitrary and highly cohort-dependent [76]. The same variable may therefore be categorised using different thresholds across studies, and individuals with similar risks but located on opposite sides of a cutoff value may be assigned to different risk categories, whereas individuals with substantial underlying differences may be assigned to the same category [77]. Moreover, arbitrary cut-off values are unlikely to capture non-linear associations between predictors and IVIG resistance, potentially obscuring clinically meaningful effects. As emphasised by Ma et al., categorising continuous predictors or assuming simple linear relationships is not recommended, as misspecification of functional forms can further compromise predictive performance [77].

Handling and reporting of missing data

Complete-case analysis remained the predominant approach, which can lead to underestimated variability, biased parameter estimates, overly narrow standard errors, and additional information loss, particularly when the proportion of missing data is substantial or the effective sample size is limited [78].

At present, multiple imputation is recommended as the preferred strategy for handling missing data [78]. However, transparency in reporting its implementation remains limited. Key methodological details, including the extent of missingness, number of imputations performed, imputation models used, and pooling procedures, were frequently omitted, thereby hindering reproducibility and the critical appraisal of model validity.

Sample size and risk of overfitting

Notably, none of the included studies reported a formal sample size calculation. Inadequate sample size increases the risk of overfitting and bias [79], particularly for ML models, which require larger datasets due to their flexibility [80]. Previous evidence similarly shows that only a small proportion of prediction model studies provide sample size justification, and many fail to meet minimum requirements for limiting overfitting and accurately estimating outcome risk [71, 81].

Traditionally, events-per-variable rules have been used to guide sample size decisions [82]. However, contemporary methodological guidance emphasises that sample size determination should rely on formal, context-specific calculations that incorporate outcome prevalence, the number of candidate predictor parameters, and anticipated model performance (e.g., Cox–Snell R2 or the C statistic), rather than on empirical rules of thumb [83].

Class imbalance and recalibration

Because IVIG resistance occurs in well under 50% of patients [28], outcome data are inherently imbalanced. Only five studies applied imbalance-handling strategies, and none reported post-correction recalibration or event rates. Class imbalance, where the outcome of interest is substantially underrepresented, can cause models to favour the majority class, thereby inflating overall performance metrics while reducing sensitivity for identifying minority cases [84]. Notably, Carriero et al. found that several widely used imbalance-correction techniques systematically overestimated risk, leading to miscalibration that could not be fully corrected even after recalibration [85].

Validation, calibration, and clinical utility

Most studies reported only apparent performance without adequate internal or external validation. For internal validation, bootstrap resampling and cross-validation are generally regarded as the preferred approaches [79]. Many models relied on the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, which is sensitive to sample size and provides no information on the direction or magnitude of miscalibration, rendering it insufficient as a sole measure of calibration [86]. By contrast, calibration plots are the recommended method for assessing calibration [87]. In external validation, calibration should additionally be evaluated using calibration intercepts, slopes, and observed-to-expected ratios, as models with good development performance may still show systematic miscalibration in new population [10].

Assessment of clinical utility was also limited, with few studies conducting DCA, leaving the net benefit and practical value of most models uncertain. Furthermore, assessments of implementation feasibility were generally insufficient.

Future methodological recommendations

Future research should emphasise methodological rigour, clinical generalisability, and robust external validation to improve the reliability and applicability of prediction models. Large multicentre prospective studies with formal sample size assessment, appropriate handling of missing data, and proper modelling of continuous predictors are needed. Model evaluation should include calibration and DCA in addition to discrimination, with external validation and region-specific recalibration performed when necessary. Despite advances in ML and increased use of EHR data, predictive performance remains limited by data heterogeneity, highlighting the need for biologically relevant biomarkers. Adherence to TRIPOD + AI [88] and PROBAST + AI [16] is essential to improve transparency, reproducibility, and clinical applicability.

Limitations

This study was limited to English-language publications and was affected by substantial heterogeneity across studies in terms of region, predictor number, and missing-data handling strategy, which may limit the robustness of performance comparisons. In addition, calibration metrics and DCA were frequently underreported, restricting assessment of clinical applicability. Furthermore, most included studies primarily relied on internal validation or apparent performance evaluation, with limited external validation across different regions and populations. The limited number of externally validated ML studies may also have reduced the statistical power of the meta-analysis. Therefore, the non-significant differences between ML and LR models should be interpreted cautiously, and the current evidence remains insufficient to demonstrate a stable and consistent advantage of ML models over LR models.

Conclusion

Particularly given the limited external validation and substantial heterogeneity across studies, ML does not consistently outperform LR in predicting IVIG resistance in KD. Existing models are limited by methodological weaknesses, including inappropriate dichotomisation of continuous predictors, poor handling of missing data, inadequate assessment of calibration and DCA, and limited external validation. Future studies should adopt multicentre prospective designs and adhere to TRIPOD + AI and PROBAST + AI to improve model robustness and clinical applicability.
