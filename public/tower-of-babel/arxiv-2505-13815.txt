Dimension-independent convergence rates of randomized nets using median-of-means
Zexin Pan

Abstract
Recent advances in quasi-Monte Carlo integration demonstrate that the median of linearly scrambled digital net estimators achieves near-optimal convergence rates for high-dimensional integrals without requiring a priori knowledge of the integrand’s smoothness. Building on this framework, we prove that the median estimator attains dimension-independent convergence, a property known as strong tractability in complexity theory, under tractability conditions characterized by low effective dimensionality. Using a probabilistic, integrand-specific error criterion, our analysis establishes both faster and dimension-independent convergence under weaker assumptions than previously possible in the worst-case setting.

Introduction

The quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) method is a powerful alternative to conventional Monte Carlo (MC) techniques for high-dimensional integration. While both methods approximate integrals by averaging
n
function evaluations, QMC replaces MC’s pseudorandom sampling with low-discrepancy point sets engineered to uniformly explore the sampling space. This structured approach allows QMC to avoid the exponential cost growth with dimension of classical quadrature rules while achieving faster error convergence than MC. These advantages have made QMC particularly valuable in fields like computational finance
[
19
,
27
]
and physics
[
23
]
, where high-dimensional integrals are pervasive.

Building on these strengths, we focus on randomized quasi-Monte Carlo (RQMC) methods based on linearly scrambled base-2 digital nets. Digital nets are a widely studied QMC construction that achieves low-discrepancy sampling through deterministic point sets
[
4
]
. The random linear scrambling
[
28
]
, a computationally efficient alternative to Owen’s scrambling
[
31
]
, preserves the structure of digital nets while introducing controlled randomness. This randomization enhances the root mean squared error convergence rate to nearly
O(n^{-3/2})
under smoothness conditions and enables statistical inference. However, traditional approaches that estimate integrals by averaging independent QMC replicates face a critical limitation: outliers inherent to linearly scrambled digital nets can prevent these methods from attaining faster convergence rates, even when the integrand satisfies stronger smoothness assumptions
[
34
]
.

To address this limitation, we adopt the median trick, a methodological innovation that has inspired many recent advances in high-dimensional integration
[
3
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
44
]
. The median of linearly scrambled digital net estimators was first analyzed in
[
34
]
, which demonstrated a convergence rate of
O(n^{-c\log(n)})
for one-dimensional analytic functions on
[0,1]
, where
c<3\log(2)/\pi^{2}\approx 0.21
. Subsequent work in
[
35
]
extended this result to higher dimensions. For finitely differentiable integrands,
[
36
]
further showed that the median estimator achieves near-optimal asymptotic convergence rates across diverse function spaces, requiring neither a priori smoothness parameters nor pre-optimized digital nets.

A critical gap in these prior studies is the role of dimensionality. We focus on the regime where the dimension grows unboundedly while the function norm remains bounded—a common scenario in path integral approximations
[
24
]
. Our analysis demonstrates that the median estimator attains dimension-independent convergence, a property termed strong tractability in information-based complexity
[
30
]
, under assumptions on the decay of the relative variation
\gamma_{u}
of each ANOVA component (formally defined in Section
3
). This notion of relative variation aligns with the effective dimension framework introduced in
[
2
]
, which quantifies how high-dimensional integrands can concentrate their variation in low-order interactions. The connection to weighted function spaces
[
38
]
is discussed in Remark
1
, further bridging our tractability conditions to classical QMC theory.

Before presenting our results, we compare our contributions to the tractability analysis of median digital nets in
[
12
]
. A key distinction lies in the error criterion: their analysis employs the worst-case integration error over the unit ball in a weighted Sobolev space, whereas we demonstrate that median estimators achieve near-optimal convergence rates on a fixed integrand with high probability. Our probabilistic approach is justified by the exponential decay in the likelihood of large errors as the number of replicates increases, aligning with the
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximation framework studied in
[
21
,
22
]
.

Within this framework, we prove, informally stated, the following result:

Theorem 1
.

Given
\eta>0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in C^{(k,\dots,k)}([0,1]^{s})
, it holds with probability
1-2^{-r}
that the median estimator
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
from Subsection
2.3
satisfies

\left|\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\right|\leqslant C_{\eta,k,f}n^{-k-1/2+\eta}.

Here,
C^{(k,\dots,k)}([0,1]^{s})
denotes functions with continuous dominating mixed partial derivatives of order
k
(see Subsection
2.4
). The constant
C_{\eta,k,f}
is independent of
s
under the random linear scrambling if the relative variation
\gamma_{u}
satisfies the tractability conditions in Theorem
5
.

These nearly
O(n^{-k-1/2})
integrand-specific error rates complement the nearly
O(n^{-k})
worst-case rates established in
[
12
]
. Furthermore, Theorem
5
requires strictly weaker assumptions than those assumed in
[
12
]
for the random linear scrambling, demonstrating that dimension-independent convergence is more easily achieved under our error criterion.

As a final remark, we note that while several algorithms match lower complexity bounds for integration in specific spaces—such as higher-order digital nets
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
, Frolov lattice rules
[
8
,
20
,
42
]
and Smolyak algorithms
[
9
,
37
,
39
]
—our method offers distinct practical advantages. It requires no smoothness parameter specification, yet automatically matches theoretical lower bounds up to an arbitrarily small
\eta>0
in the convergence rate, making it both efficient and immune to parameter misspecification. Combined with its straightforward implementation and the dimension-independent convergence established in this paper, the median estimator presents a highly practical and robust alternative for applied problems. Furthermore, recent work has successfully extended median RQMC to
L^{2}
-approximation
[
32
,
33
]
, demonstrating its broader utility beyond numerical integration.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section
2
reviews essential background on digital nets and establishes notation. Subsection
2.3
in particular details how the median trick enables probabilistic error guarantees and accelerates convergence rates. Section
3
introduces ANOVA decomposition and formally defines the relative variation
\gamma_{u}
, a key measure of effective dimensionality. Section
4
provides a foundational analysis of the median estimator’s error bounds and tractability under the completely random design, a simplified randomization scheme that isolates core theoretical insights. Section
5
generalizes these results to a broader class of randomization schemes, with the random linear scrambling serving as a key example. Section
6
empirically validates our theoretical findings through simulations on high-dimensional integrands. Finally, Section
7
concludes the paper with a discussion of future research directions.

2
Background and notation

Let
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
denote the natural numbers and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. For
s
-dimensional indices, we define
\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}=\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
to exclude the zero vector. We define
\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant\ell}=\{0,1,\dots,\ell\}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{<\ell}=\{0,1,\dots,\ell-1\}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. The dimension of the integration domain is denoted by
s
, and we write
1{:}s=\{1,2,\dots,s\}
for
s\in\mathbb{N}
. For a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\real^{s}
and a subset
u\subseteq 1{:}s
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{u}
denotes the subvector of
\boldsymbol{x}
indexed by
u
, while
\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}
denotes the subvector indexed by
1{:}s\setminus u
. The indicator function
\boldsymbol{1}\{\mathcal{A}\}
equals
1
if event
\mathcal{A}
occurs and
0
otherwise. For
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{s})\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
,
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}
. The cardinality of a set
K
is
|K|
. For positive sequences
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
,
a_{n}=O(b_{n})
denotes asymptotic dominance:
a_{n}\leqslant Cb_{n}
for some constant
C<\infty
and all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We assume the integrand
f:[0,1]^{s}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous, denoted as
f\in C([0,1]^{s})
, with
\|f\|_{\infty}=\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{s}}|f(\boldsymbol{x})|
. The
L^{p}
-norm of
f
is
\|f\|_{L^{p}}=\big(\int_{[0,1]^{s}}|f(\boldsymbol{x})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\big)^{1/p}
, and
L^{p}([0,1]^{s})
denotes the space of functions with finite
L^{p}
-norm.

Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) methods approximate

\mu=\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\quad\text{by}\quad\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})

for points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in[0,1]^{s}
. In this paper, the points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
are constructed by base-2 digital nets defined in the next subsection.

2.1
Digital net construction and randomization

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{<2^{m}}
, we express
i
in its binary form
i=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}i_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}
, encoded as the column vector
\vec{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
. Analogously, for
a\in[0,1)
, we approximate its infinite binary expansion
a=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}a_{\ell}2^{-\ell}
by truncating to
E\in\mathbb{N}
digits, yielding
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{E})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
. For numbers with dual representations (such as dyadic rationals), we select the finite expansion ending in zeros.

A base-2 digital net in
[0,1]^{s}
is determined by
s
matrices
C_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
. The deterministic points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=(x_{i1},\dots,x_{is})
are constructed via:

\displaystyle\vec{x}_{ij}=C_{j}\vec{i}\ \ \mathsf{mod}\ 2\text{ for }i\in\mathbb{Z}_{<2^{m}},j\in 1{:}s,

where
\vec{x}_{ij}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
maps to
x_{ij}\in[0,1)
by appending zeros to the truncated
E
-digit representation. Classical constructions typically assume
E\leqslant m
.

To introduce randomness, we modify the construction as:

\displaystyle\vec{x}_{ij}=C_{j}\vec{i}+\vec{D}_{j}\ \ \mathsf{mod}\ 2,

(1)

where
C_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
and
\vec{D}_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
are random with precision
E\geqslant m
. The vector
\vec{D}_{j}
is called the
digital shift
and comprises independent Bernoulli
(0.5)
entries. A common way to randomize
C_{j}
is the
random linear scrambling

[
28
]
:

C_{j}=M_{j}\mathcal{C}_{j}\ \ \mathsf{mod}\ 2,

where
M_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
is a random lower-triangular matrix (diagonal entries fixed at
1
, subdiagonal entries independently drawn from Bernoulli
(0.5)
), and
\mathcal{C}_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
is a predetermined generating matrix optimized to improve the discrepancy of resulting points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
(see
[
4
, Chapter 4.4]
for details).

While effective, the reliance of random linear scrambling on carefully designed generating matrices complicates theoretical analysis. We address this in Section
5
. For initial insights, Section
4
adopts the
complete random design

[
36
]
, where all entries of
C_{j}
are drawn independently from Bernoulli
(0.5)
. This approach retains the asymptotic convergence rate of random linear scrambling while circumventing the need for pre-optimized generator matrices.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}[E]
denote points generated by equation (
1
) with
E
-bit precision. The QMC estimator becomes:

\hat{\mu}_{E}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\quad\text{for $\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}[E]$}.

(2)

We conveniently assume
E=\infty
and focus our theoretical analysis on
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
. In practice,
E
is constrained by computational precision. Lemma 1 of
[
35
]
shows
|\hat{\mu}_{E}-\hat{\mu}_{\infty}|\leqslant\omega_{f}(\sqrt{s}2^{-E})
, where
\omega_{f}
is the modulus of continuity of
f
. Thus,
\hat{\mu}_{E}
closely approximates
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
when
\omega_{f}(\sqrt{s}2^{-E})\ll|\hat{\mu}_{E}-\mu|
.

2.2
Walsh functions and error analysis for base-2 digital nets

Walsh functions form the natural orthonormal basis for analyzing base-2 digital nets. For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
x\in[0,1)
, the univariate
Walsh function
\mathrm{wal}_{k}(x)
is defined as

\mathrm{wal}_{k}(x)=(-1)^{\vec{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{x}},

where
\vec{k}\in\{0,1\}^{\infty}
and
\vec{x}\in\{0,1\}^{\infty}
are the binary expansions of
k
and
x
, respectively. Since
\vec{k}
has finitely many nonzero digits, finite-precision truncations of
\vec{x}
suffice for computation.

For
s
-dimensional functions, the multivariate Walsh function
\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}:[0,1)^{s}\to\{-1,1\}
is the tensor product of univariate Walsh functions:

\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\mathrm{wal}_{k_{j}}(x_{j})=(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{k}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{x}_{j}},

where
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{s})\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
. These functions constitute a complete orthonormal basis for
L^{2}([0,1]^{s})

[
4
]
, enabling the Walsh series decomposition:

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}}\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})=\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}.

(3)

The equality holds in the
L^{2}
-sense. Using this decomposition,
[
36
, Theorem 1]
establishes a key error structure for QMC estimators:

Lemma 1
.

For
f\in C([0,1]^{s})
, the error of
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
defined by equation (
2
) satisfies

\hat{\mu}_{\infty}-\mu=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}}Z(\boldsymbol{k})S(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}),

(4)

where

Z(\boldsymbol{k})=\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{k}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}C_{j}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \mathsf{mod}\ 2\Big\}\quad\text{and}\quad S(\boldsymbol{k})=(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{k}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{D}_{j}}.

Here,
Z(\boldsymbol{k})
is an indicator for failure to integrate the Walsh mode
\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
exactly, and
S(\boldsymbol{k})
encodes the random sign induced by the digital shifts
\vec{D}_{j}
.

2.3
Probabilistic error guarantee of the median estimator

Given
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider
2r-1
independently generated replicates of

\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
ordered from the smallest to the largest:

\hat{\mu}^{(1)}_{\infty}\leqslant\dots\leqslant\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}\leqslant\dots\leqslant\hat{\mu}^{(2r-1)}_{\infty}.

A key property of the sample median
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
is the following probabilistic error bound:

Lemma 2
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}
and
\mathcal{A}
be the event
\{Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\text{ for some }\boldsymbol{k}\in K\}
. If
\Pr(\mathcal{A})\leqslant\delta<1/8
, then

\Pr(|\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\mu|^{2}>\varepsilon_{K}/\delta)\leqslant(8\delta)^{r}\quad\text{for}\quad\varepsilon_{K}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\setminus K}\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}.

Proof.

First note that the random signs
S(\boldsymbol{k})
in equation (
4
) are zero-mean and pairwise-independent for distinct
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}
(see
[
34
, Lemma 4]
for the one-dimensional case). Since
\mathcal{A}
is independent of
S(\boldsymbol{k})
, we have
\mathbb{E}(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}\!\mid\!\mathcal{A}^{c})=\mu
and

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}\mid\mathcal{A}^{c})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\setminus K}\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\mid\mathcal{A}^{c})|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon_{K}}{\Pr(\mathcal{A}^{c})}.

Applying Chebyshev’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\Pr(|\hat{\mu}_{\infty}-\mu|^{2}>\varepsilon_{K}/\delta)\leqslant

\displaystyle\Pr(\mathcal{A})+\Pr(\mathcal{A}^{c})\Pr(|\hat{\mu}_{\infty}-\mu|^{2}>\varepsilon_{K}/\delta\mid\mathcal{A}^{c})\leqslant 2\delta.

Finally,
|\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\mu|>\varepsilon_{K}/\delta
only if the event
|\hat{\mu}_{\infty}-\mu|^{2}>\varepsilon_{K}/\delta
occurs for at least
r
out of the
2r-1
independent replicates. A union bound then implies

\Pr(|\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\mu|^{2}>\varepsilon_{K}/\delta)\leqslant{2r-1\choose r}(2\delta)^{r}\leqslant 2^{2r-1}(2\delta)^{r}\leqslant(8\delta)^{r}.\qed

This lemma justifies the use of the sample median
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
as an estimator for
\mu
. For

f\in C([0,1]^{s})
, the mean squared error satisfies

\mathbb{E}|\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\mu|^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon_{K}/\delta+(8\delta)^{r}\|f\|^{2}_{\infty}.

Because the second term decays exponentially in
r
, it can be made negligible relative to the first term by choosing
r
sufficiently large.

The main challenge is therefore to select a set
K
so that
\varepsilon_{K}
converges at the desired rate. We address this by constructing
K
adaptively based on the decay rate of the Walsh coefficients
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})
, which encodes the integrand’s smoothness and effective dimensionality. Notably, the median estimator
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
does not require explicit knowledge of
K
, making it applicable without prior information.

2.4
Walsh coefficients and function smoothness

To analyze the decay of Walsh coefficients, we introduce the following notation. For
k=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}a_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}
, let
\kappa=\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}\mid a_{\ell}=1\}
denote the positions of nonzero bits in the binary expansion of
k
. We use
k
and
\kappa
interchangeably because each uniquely determines the other. In this framework,
|\kappa|
equals the number of nonzero bits of
k
, and the Walsh function simplifies to:

{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa}}(x)=\prod_{\ell\in\kappa}(-1)^{\vec{x}(\ell)},

where
\vec{x}(\ell)
denotes the
\ell
-th digit of
x
in its binary expansion.

For a finite subset
\kappa\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, we define

•

\lceil\kappa\rceil_{q}
: The
q
-th largest element of
\kappa
(or
0
if
|\kappa|<q
).

•

\lceil\kappa\rceil_{1{:}q}
: The set of the
q
largest elements of
\kappa
(or
\varnothing
if
q=0
).

The following lemma connects the Walsh coefficients
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})
to the smoothness of
f
. For
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s})\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
, we define the mixed partial derivative:

f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}=f^{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s})}=\frac{\partial^{\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}}f}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{s}^{\alpha_{s}}},

with the convention
f^{(\boldsymbol{0})}=f
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
and
f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\in C([0,1]^{s})
. If
|\kappa_{j}|\geqslant\alpha_{j}
for all
j\in 1{:}s
, then

\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})=(-1)^{\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}}\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}(\boldsymbol{x})\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa_{j}\setminus\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{1{:}\alpha_{j}}}}(x_{j})W_{\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{1{:}\alpha_{j}}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},

(5)

where
W_{\kappa}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
for
\kappa\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is defined recursively by
W_{\varnothing}(x)=1
and

W_{\kappa}(x)=\int_{[0,1]}(-1)^{\vec{x}(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor)}W_{\kappa\setminus\lfloor\kappa\rfloor}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

with
\lfloor\kappa\rfloor
denoting the smallest element of
\kappa
. Furthermore, for nonempty
\kappa
,
W_{\kappa}(x)
is continuous, nonnegative, periodic with period
2^{-\lfloor\kappa\rfloor+1}
and

\int_{[0,1]}W_{\kappa}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\prod_{\ell\in\kappa}2^{-\ell-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\max_{x\in[0,1]}W_{\kappa}(x)=2\prod_{\ell\in\kappa}2^{-\ell-1}.

(6)

Proof.

Equation (
5
) comes from
[
41
, Theorem 2.5]
, while properties of
W_{\kappa}(x)
are proven in
[
41
, Section 3]
.
∎

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we say
f\in C^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}([0,1]^{s})
if the mixed partial derivative
f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}
exists and is continuous on
[0,1]^{s}
for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}_{\leqslant\alpha}
. In this work, we assume
f\in C^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}([0,1]^{s})
in order to directly apply Lemma
3
. This regularity condition can be relaxed to the existence of weak derivatives
f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\in L^{1}([0,1]^{s})
satisfying the integral identity in equation (
5
), as discussed in
[
36
, Remark 1]
.

3
ANOVA decomposition and effective dimensionality

To characterize the effective dimensionality of integrands
f\in L^{2}([0,1]^{s})
, we employ the ANOVA decomposition
[
25
]
:

f=\sum_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}f_{u},

where each component
f_{u}
depends only on
\boldsymbol{x}_{u}
and satisfies

\int_{0}^{1}f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}=0\quad\forall j\in u.

This decomposition is unique in the
L^{2}
-sense, with
f_{\varnothing}=\mu
. For
f\in C([0,1]^{s})
, we enforce
f_{u}\in C([0,1]^{s})
via the recursive definition:

f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{[0,1]^{s-|u|}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\subseteq u\\
v\neq u\end{subarray}}f_{v}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(7)

This construction preserves smoothness:
f_{u}\in C^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}([0,1]^{s})
for all
u\subseteq 1{:}s
if
f\in C^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}([0,1]^{s})
. Here,
f_{u}
captures the variation due to interactions among variables
\boldsymbol{x}_{u}
. By an abuse of notation, we write
f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})
, where the latter
f_{u}:[0,1]^{|u|}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
|u|
-dimensional function.

Let
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})=\{j\in 1{:}s\mid k_{j}\neq 0\}
denote the support of
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{s})
. From the Walsh decomposition (
3
), we know that

f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}\\
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})=u\end{subarray}}\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(8)

because
\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})
depends only on
\{x_{j}\mid j\in\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})\}
and

\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}=\Big(\int_{0}^{1}{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa_{j}}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}\Big)\prod_{j^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})\setminus\{j\}}{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa_{j^{\prime}}}}(x_{j^{\prime}})=0

if
j\in\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})
. Thus,
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})=\hat{f}_{u}(\boldsymbol{k})
if
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})=u
.

To quantify the variation of ANOVA components
f_{u}
, we adopt the fractional Vitali variation with
\mathfrak{p}=2
from
[
5
]
, which generalizes the usual Sobolev norms. For a function
f:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and a subcube
J=\prod_{j=1}^{d}[a_{j},b_{j})
, we define the alternating sum over
J
:

\Delta(f,J)=\sum_{v\subseteq 1{:}d}(-1)^{d-|v|}f\Big((\boldsymbol{a}_{v},\boldsymbol{b}_{v^{c}})\Big),

where
(\boldsymbol{a}_{v},\boldsymbol{b}_{v^{c}})
denotes the vertex of
J
with
x_{j}=a_{j}
if
j\in v
and
x_{j}=b_{j}
otherwise. For
\lambda\in(0,1]
, the fractional Vitali variation of order
\lambda
is:

V^{(d)}_{\lambda}(f)=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\sum_{J\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Vol}(J)\Bigl|\frac{\Delta(f,J)}{\mathrm{Vol}(J)^{\lambda}}\Bigr|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

(9)

where the supremum is over all partitions
\mathcal{P}
of
[0,1)^{d}
into subcubes and
\mathrm{Vol}(J)
is the volume of subcube
J
. When
d=0
and
f\in\mathbb{R}
,
V^{(0)}_{\lambda}(f)=|f|
by convention.

For
f
with weak mixed derivative
f^{(1,\dots,1)}
, we have the inequality

V^{(d)}_{1}(f)\leqslant\|f^{(1,\dots,1)}\|_{L^{2}},

(10)

with equality attained when
f\in C^{(1,\dots,1)}([0,1]^{s})

[
5
, equation (3.4)]
.
More generally,
[
26
]
shows
V^{(d)}_{\lambda}(f)\leqslant\|f^{(1,\dots,1)}\|_{L^{p}}
for
p=2/(3-2\lambda),\lambda\in[1/2,1]
. Discontinuous
f
can also have finite
V^{(d)}_{\lambda}(f)
(see
[
36
, Section 4.1]
for an example).

Next, for
v\subseteq 1{:}d
, we define the partial integral operator
I_{v}:L^{1}([0,1]^{d})\to L^{1}([0,1]^{|v|})
as:

I_{v}(f)(\boldsymbol{x}_{v})=\int_{[0,1]^{d-|v|}}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{v^{c}};\boldsymbol{x}_{v})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{v^{c}},

where
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{v^{c}};\boldsymbol{x}_{v})
denotes
f
with fixed
\boldsymbol{x}_{v}
. Let
D_{v^{c}}
be the class of functions:

D_{v^{c}}=\Big\{\rho_{v^{c}}\Big|\rho_{v^{c}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{j\in v^{c}}\rho_{j}(x_{j}),\rho_{j}\in C([0,1]),\rho_{j}\geqslant 0,\|\rho_{j}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1\Big\}.

(11)

The partial fractional Vitali variation of order
\lambda
with respect to
v\subseteq 1{:}d
is:

V^{v}_{\lambda}(f)=\sup_{\rho_{v^{c}}\in D_{v^{c}}}V^{(|v|)}_{\lambda}\Big(I_{v}(f\rho_{v^{c}})\Big).

(12)

By convention,
V^{1{:}d}_{\lambda}(f)=V^{(d)}_{\lambda}(f)
. From
[
36
, Section 3.2]
,

\Bigl(V^{v}_{\lambda}(f)\Bigl)^{2}\leqslant\int_{[0,1]^{d-|v|}}\Bigl(V^{(|v|)}_{\lambda}\Bigl(f(\text{ }\cdot\text{ };\boldsymbol{x}_{v^{c}})\Bigr)\Bigr)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{v^{c}},

which, together with equation (
10
), implies

V^{v}_{1}(f)\leqslant\|f^{(\boldsymbol{1}_{v},\boldsymbol{0}_{v^{c}})}\|_{L^{2}}

(13)

if the weak derivative
f^{(\boldsymbol{1}_{v},\boldsymbol{0}_{v^{c}})}
exists.
Here
(\boldsymbol{1}_{v},\boldsymbol{0}_{v^{c}})
denotes the vector whose
j
-th entry equals
1
if
j\in v
and equals
0
otherwise.

The following lemma connects the Walsh coefficients
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})=\hat{f}_{u}(\boldsymbol{k})
(where
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})=u
) to the fractional Vitali variation of
f_{u}
:

Lemma 4
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\lambda\in(0,1]
. Suppose
V^{v}_{\lambda}(f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u})}_{u})<\infty
for nonempty
u\subseteq 1{:}s
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}=(\alpha_{j},j\in u)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant\alpha}^{|u|}
and a subset
v\subseteq\{j\mid\alpha_{j}=\alpha\}
. Then for
\boldsymbol{k}_{u}=(k_{j},j\in u)\in\mathbb{N}^{|u|}
satisfying
|\kappa_{j}|=\alpha+1
for
j\in v
and
|\kappa_{j}|=\alpha_{j}
for
j\in u\setminus v
,

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\in B(\boldsymbol{k}_{u},v)}|\hat{f}_{u}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})|^{2}\leqslant 4^{|u|}\Big(V^{v}_{\lambda}(f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u})}_{u})\Big)^{2}\prod_{j\in u}4^{(1-\lambda)\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}}\prod_{\ell^{\prime}\in\kappa_{j}}4^{-\ell^{\prime}-1},

where

B(\boldsymbol{k}_{u},v)=\{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\mid\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})=u,\ \lceil\kappa^{\prime}_{j}\rceil_{1:(\alpha+1)}=\kappa_{j}\ \forall j\in v,\ \kappa^{\prime}_{j}=\kappa_{j}\ \forall j\in u\setminus v\}.

(14)

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.
∎

Motivated by Lemma
4
, we define for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\lambda\in(0,1]
and nonempty
u\subseteq 1{:}s
:

\|f\|_{u,\alpha,\lambda}=\sup_{v\subseteq u}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant\alpha}^{|u|}\\
\alpha_{j}=\alpha\ \forall j\in v,\\
\alpha_{j}>0\ \forall j\in u\setminus v\end{subarray}}V^{v}_{\lambda}(f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u})}_{u}),

(15)

where
f_{u}
is the ANOVA component of
f
defined in equation (
7
). Notice that we add the restriction
\alpha_{j}>0
for
j\in u\setminus v
because
k_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
implies
\alpha_{j}=|\kappa_{j}|>0
in the statement of Lemma
4
. In particular,
\|f\|_{u,0,\lambda}=V^{u}_{\lambda}(f_{u})
. When
\lambda=1
and the weak mixed derivative
f^{(\alpha+1,\dots,\alpha+1)}
exists, equation (
13
) implies the bound

\|f\|_{u,\alpha,1}\leqslant\sup_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\in\{1:(\alpha+1)\}^{|u|}}\|f_{u}^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u})}\|_{L^{2}}.

We further set
\|f\|_{u,\alpha,\lambda}=0
if
u=\varnothing
, and define the global variation norm:

\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}=\sup_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}\|f\|_{u,\alpha,\lambda}.

By Lemma
4
,
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}=0
implies
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})=0
for all
\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, forcing
f
to be a constant. For non-constant
f
, the
relative variation
of ANOVA component
f_{u}
is:

\gamma_{u}=\gamma_{u}(f,\alpha,\lambda)=\frac{\|f\|_{u,\alpha,\lambda}}{\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}},

(16)

which satisfies
\sup_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}\gamma_{u}=1
and
\gamma_{\varnothing}=0
. The relative variation
\gamma_{u}
depends on
f,\alpha
and
\lambda
, but we suppress this dependence in the notation as these variables are fixed throughout our theoretical analysis. Section
6
later exemplifies how
\gamma_{u}
changes under different choices of
\alpha
.

The decay of
\gamma_{u}
encodes effective dimensionality, with
\gamma_{u}=1
for all nonempty
u\subseteq 1{:}s
representing the worst dimensionality. As shown in Theorem
5
, dimension-independent convergence rates emerge under suitable decay conditions on
\gamma_{u}
.

Remark 1
.

For positive weights
\{\tilde{\gamma}_{u},u\subseteq 1{:}s\}
, we can define the weighted norm:

\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda,\tilde{\gamma}_{u}}=\sup_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}\tilde{\gamma}_{u}^{-1}\|f\|_{u,\alpha,\lambda}.

All subsequent results hold equivalently with
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda,\tilde{\gamma}_{u}}
replacing
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}
and
\tilde{\gamma}_{u}
replacing
\gamma_{u}
. However, since
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}
and
\gamma_{u}
are intrinsic to
f
, while
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda,\tilde{\gamma}_{u}}
requires external weights
\tilde{\gamma}_{u}
unused by our algorithm, we adopt the unweighted framework for simplicity.

Notably,
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}
equals the infimum of
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda,\tilde{\gamma}_{u}}
over all weights
\{\tilde{\gamma}_{u},u\subseteq 1{:}s\}
with
\sup_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}\tilde{\gamma}_{u}=1
. Thus, the median RQMC method implicitly selects optimal weights tailored to
f
, avoiding explicit weight specification.

4
Tractability under the completely random design

In this section, we show the median RQMC estimator
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
under the completely random design achieves near
O(2^{-(\alpha+\lambda+1/2)m})
convergence rates given
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}<\infty
, with no dimension-dependence if further
\gamma_{u}
satisfies equation (
25
).

First, we define

\|\kappa\|=\sum_{\ell\in\kappa}\ell
for
\kappa\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, with
\|\varnothing\|=0
.
Motivated by Lemma
4
, we choose
K
in Lemma
2
for given
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\lambda\in(0,1]
to be:

K=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}K_{u}(T_{u,m})

(17)

with thresholds
T_{u,m}
and

K_{u}(T)=\bigcup_{v\subseteq u}\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{k}_{u}\in\mathcal{K}_{u,v}(T)}B(\boldsymbol{k}_{u},v)

for
B(\boldsymbol{k}_{u},v)
defined as in equation (
14
) and

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{u,v}(T)=\Big\{(k_{j},j\in u)\in\mathbb{N}^{|u|}\Big|\begin{matrix}|\kappa_{j}|=\alpha+1\ \forall j\in v,\ |\kappa_{j}|\leqslant\alpha\ \forall j\in u\setminus v,\\
(\lambda-1)\sum_{j\in u}\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}+\sum_{j\in u}\|\kappa_{j}\|\leqslant T\end{matrix}\Big\}.

We note that
K_{u}(T)
implicitly depends on
\alpha
and
\lambda
.

The next lemma quantifies how
T
controls the size of
|\hat{f}_{u}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}
for
\boldsymbol{k}\notin K_{u}(T)
.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose
\|f\|_{u,\alpha,\lambda}<\infty
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\lambda\in(0,1]
and nonempty
u\subseteq 1{:}s
. Then for
T\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta\in(0,1)
,

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus K_{u}(T)}|\hat{f}_{u}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leqslant\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{u,\alpha,\lambda}}{4^{\theta T}}C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta}^{|u|},

where

C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta}=\frac{4^{-\alpha}}{\alpha!(4^{(\alpha+\lambda)(1-\theta)}-1)(4^{1-\theta}-1)^{\alpha}}+\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=0}^{\alpha}\frac{4^{-\alpha^{\prime}+1}}{(\alpha^{\prime})!(4^{1-\theta}-1)^{\alpha^{\prime}}}.

(18)

Proof.

See Appendix
B
.
∎

While larger
T
implies a tighter bound on
|\hat{f}_{u}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}
with
\boldsymbol{k}\notin K_{u}(T)
, it also increases the probability that
Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1
for some
\boldsymbol{k}\in K_{u}(T)
. The next lemma provides a bound on the cardinality of
K_{u}(T)
.

Lemma 6
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\lambda\in(0,1],T\in\mathbb{R}
and nonempty
u\subseteq 1{:}s
,

|K_{u}(T)|\leqslant 2^{T/(\alpha+\lambda)}\Big(A_{\alpha,\lambda}\max\big(T/(\alpha+\lambda),1\big)+B_{\alpha,\lambda}\Big)^{|u|},

where

A_{\alpha,\lambda}=\frac{1}{\alpha!(2^{1/(\alpha+\lambda)}-1)^{\alpha}}\quad\text{and}\quad B_{\alpha,\lambda}=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{(\alpha^{\prime})!(2^{1/(\alpha+\lambda)}-1)^{\alpha^{\prime}}},

(19)

with the sum from
1
to
\alpha
interpreted as
0
when
\alpha=0
.

Proof.

See Appendix
C
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gamma_{u}
defined by equation (
16
), let

\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u}}(m)=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}_{u}(A_{\alpha,\lambda}m+B_{\alpha,\lambda})^{|u|}.

(20)

Then for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\Pr\Big(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\text{ for some }\boldsymbol{k}\in\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}K_{u}(T_{u,m})\Big)\leqslant\delta

under the completely random design, where

T_{u,m}=(\alpha+\lambda)m-(\alpha+\lambda)\Big(\log_{2}\big(\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u}}(m)\big)-\log_{2}(\delta)-\frac{\log_{2}(\gamma_{u})}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}\Big).

(21)

Proof.

Because
\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u}}(m)\geqslant 1,\delta<1
and
\sup_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}\gamma_{u}=1
, we have
T_{u,m}/(\alpha+\lambda)\leqslant m
for all nonempty
u\subseteq 1{:}s
. Lemma
6
then implies

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}|K_{u}(T_{u,m})|\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}2^{T_{u,m}/(\alpha+\lambda)}(A_{\alpha,\lambda}m+B_{\alpha,\lambda})^{|u|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta 2^{m}}{\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u}}(m)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}_{u}(A_{\alpha,\lambda}m+B_{\alpha,\lambda})^{|u|}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\delta 2^{m}.

Under the completely random design,
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)=2^{-m}
for any
\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, so our conclusion follows by taking a union bound.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the following error bounds on
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
:

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}<\infty
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\lambda\in(0,1]
. Then for
m\in\mathbb{N},r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\theta\in(0,1)
, the completely random design ensures

\Pr\Big(|\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\mu|^{2}>\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u},\theta}\|f\|^{2}_{\alpha,\lambda}\frac{(\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u}}(m))^{2\theta(\alpha+\lambda)}}{2^{(2\theta(\alpha+\lambda)+1)m}}\Big)\leqslant 2^{-r},

(22)

where
\gamma_{u}
is given by equation (
16
),
\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u}}(m)
is given by equation (
20
) and

\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u},\theta}=16^{2\theta(\alpha+\lambda)+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\frac{1+2(1-\theta)(\alpha+\lambda)}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}_{u}C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta}^{|u|},

with
C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta}
given by equation (
18
).

Proof.

Let
\delta=1/16
in Lemma
2
and Corollary
1
. By Lemma
2
and
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)=2^{-m}
for
\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, it suffices to show

2^{-m+4}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\setminus K}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leqslant\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u},\theta}\|f\|^{2}_{\alpha,\lambda}\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u}}(m)^{2\theta(\alpha+\lambda)}}{2^{(2\theta(\alpha+\lambda)+1)m}}

for
K
defined by equation (
17
) with
T_{u,m}
given by equation (
21
). From equation (
8
) and Lemma
5
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\setminus K}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus K_{u}(T_{u,m})}|\hat{f}_{u}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{u,\alpha,\lambda}}{4^{\theta T_{u,m}}}C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta}^{|u|}.

(23)

Our conclusion follows by plugging in
\|f\|^{2}_{u,\alpha,\lambda}=\gamma^{2}_{u}\|f\|^{2}_{\alpha,\lambda}
and equation (
21
).
∎

Remark 2
.

Given a failure probability tolerance
\bar{\delta}>0
, we can set
r=\lceil\log_{2}(\bar{\delta}^{-1})\rceil
to ensure
2^{-r}\leqslant\bar{\delta}
. For this choice of
r
,
the median estimator
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
asymptotically achieves a convergence rate
O(N^{-\alpha-\lambda-1/2+\eta})
for any
\eta>0
, where
N=r2^{m}
is the total number of function evaluations.

To justify this, observe that
\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u}}(m)^{2(\alpha+\lambda)}=O(2^{\eta m})
asymptotically for any
\eta>0
because
\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u}}(m)
is a polynomial of
m
. Setting
\theta=1-\eta/(2(\alpha+\lambda))
, we derive for all
f
with
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}<\infty
that

\Pr\Big(|\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\mu|^{2}>C_{\eta}\|f\|^{2}_{\alpha,\lambda}2^{-(2\alpha+2\lambda+1-2\eta)m}\Big)\leqslant 2^{-r}\leqslant\bar{\delta},

(24)

where
C_{\eta}
is a constant depending on
\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u}
and
\eta
. This convergence rate is nearly optimal, as it aligns with the lower bound established in
[
1
]
for one-dimensional
f
with
\lambda
-Hölder continuous
f^{(\alpha)}
, a subclass of functions with finite
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}
.

Remark 3
.

In settings where
s\to\infty
while
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}\leqslant 1
, dimension-independent error bounds hold if there exist weights
\{\gamma_{j},j\in\mathbb{N}\}
satisfying

\gamma_{u}\leqslant\prod_{j\in u}\gamma_{j}\quad\forall u\subseteq\mathbb{N}\quad\text{ and }\quad C_{\infty}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}_{j}<\infty.

(25)

To see this, we first bound

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u},\theta}}{16^{2\theta(\alpha+\lambda)+1}}\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}_{u}C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta}^{|u|}\leqslant\prod_{j=1}^{s}(1+\gamma^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}_{j}C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta})\leqslant\exp(C_{\infty}C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta}),

where we have used
1+x\leqslant\exp(x)
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u}}(m)=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\gamma^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}_{u}(A_{\alpha,\lambda}m+B_{\alpha,\lambda})^{|u|}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{s}\Big(1+\gamma^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}_{j}(A_{\alpha,\lambda}m+B_{\alpha,\lambda})\Big),

which can be further bounded by
C^{\prime}_{\eta}2^{\eta m}
for any
\eta>0
with a constant
C^{\prime}_{\eta}
independent of
s

[
17
, Lemma 3]
. Putting the above bounds into equation (
22
), we conclude that equation (
24
) holds with a constant
C_{\eta}
independent of
s
. Notably, equation (
25
) resembles the tractability conditions assumed by
[
12
, Corollary 3.13]
for polynomial lattices to achieve near
O(2^{-(\alpha+\lambda+1/2)m})
convergence rates.

5
Tractability under more general randomization

While the completely random design offers theoretical advantages in near-optimal convergence rates and tractability, the random linear scrambling often outperforms it in practical settings due to tighter error bounds on low-dimensional ANOVA components. For example,
[
18
]
designs the generating matrices
\mathcal{C}_{j}
to explicitly optimize the discrepancy of all two-dimensional projections of the resulting digital nets. In this section, we extend the analysis of Section
4
to a broader class of randomization schemes, with the random linear scrambling as a key example.

To state our assumption, we first define, for
d\in\mathbb{N}
and finite subset
\kappa\subseteq\mathbb{N}
:

\|\kappa\|_{(d)}=\begin{cases}\|\kappa\|,&\text{ if }|\kappa|\leqslant d\\
\|\lceil\kappa\rceil_{1{:}d}\|,&\text{ if }|\kappa|>d\end{cases}.

Assumption 1
.

Under the randomization scheme for
C_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
,
j\in 1{:}s
, there exists
d\in\mathbb{N}
and a set of nonnegative integers
\tilde{t}_{u}
for each nonempty
u\subseteq 1{:}s
such that for
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}
with
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})=u
,

\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)\leqslant 2^{-m+\tilde{t}_{u}}\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\sum_{j\in u}\|\kappa_{j}\|_{(d)}>d(m-\tilde{t}_{u})\Big\}.

From
[
12
, Lemma 2.17]
, the random linear scrambling satisfies the above assumption with parameters
d=1
and
\tilde{t}_{u}=t_{u}+|u|-1
, where
t_{u}
denotes the projection-dependent
t
-value defined in
[
12
, Section 2]
. We frame this assumption to align with broader applications in higher-order digital nets
[
6
]
, where the parameter
d
(the order of digital nets) may exceed
1
. This formulation ensures compatibility with both classical
d=1
constructions and advanced higher-order constructions.

We first consider the
d\geqslant\alpha+\lambda
case. The analysis is easy thanks to the good properties of our randomization.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}<\infty
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\lambda\in(0,1]
. Then for
m\in\mathbb{N},r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\theta\in(0,1)
, a randomization scheme satisfying Assumption
1
with
d\geqslant\alpha+\lambda
ensures

\Pr\Big(|\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\mu|^{2}>\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u},\tilde{t}_{u},\theta}\|f\|^{2}_{\alpha,\lambda}2^{-(2\theta(\alpha+\lambda)+1)m}\Big)\leqslant 2^{-r},

where
\gamma_{u}
is given by equation (
16
),
\tilde{t}_{u}
is given in Assumption
1
and

\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u},\tilde{t}_{u},\theta}=16\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\gamma^{2}_{u}C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta}^{|u|}2^{\tilde{t}_{u}(2\theta(\alpha+\lambda)+1)}

with
C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta}
given by equation (
18
).

Proof.

Let
K
be defined as in equation (
17
) with
T_{u,m}=(\alpha+\lambda)(m-\tilde{t}_{u})
. For any
\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}=(k^{\prime}_{1},\dots,k^{\prime}_{s})\in B(\boldsymbol{k}_{u},v)
with
\boldsymbol{k}_{u}=(k_{j},j\in u)\in\mathcal{K}_{u,v}(T_{u,m})
,
\|\kappa^{\prime}_{j}\|_{(d)}=\|\kappa_{j}\|
for
j\in u\setminus v
because
|\kappa^{\prime}_{j}|=|\kappa_{j}|\leqslant d
and

\|\kappa^{\prime}_{j}\|_{(d)}\leqslant\frac{d}{\alpha+\lambda}\Big((\lambda-1)\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}+\|\kappa_{j}\|\Big)

for
j\in v
because
\lceil\kappa^{\prime}_{j}\rceil_{1{:}(\alpha+1)}=\kappa_{j}
. Hence,

\sum_{j\in u}\|\kappa^{\prime}_{j}\|_{(d)}\leqslant\frac{d}{\alpha+\lambda}\Big((\lambda-1)\sum_{j\in u}\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{\alpha+1}+\sum_{j\in u}\|\kappa_{j}\|\Big)\leqslant\frac{d}{\alpha+\lambda}T_{u,m}=d(m-\tilde{t}_{u}).

It follows from Assumption
1
that
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)=0
for all
\boldsymbol{k}\in K
and we can apply Lemma
2
with
\delta=1/16
to get

\Pr\Big(|\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\mu|^{2}>16\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\setminus K}\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\Big)\leqslant 2^{-r}.

Assumption
1
also implies
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)\leqslant 2^{-m+\tilde{t}_{u}}
if
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})=u
. An argument similar to equation (
23
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\setminus K}\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}2^{-m+\tilde{t}_{u}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus K_{u}(T_{u,m})}|\hat{f}_{u}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

Remark 4

Analogous to Corollary

Lemma 7

For

Proof.

Corollary 2
.

For

and
\gamma_{u}

\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,d,\theta^{\prime},\gamma_{u}}(m)=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\

Then for any

\Pr\Big(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\text{ for some }\boldsymbol{k}\in\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\

Let

\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{k})=u

Remark 6
.

Consider again settings where
s\to\infty
while
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}\leqslant 1
. We assume equation (
25

\{\gamma_{j},j\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Setting
\theta^{\prime}=1-\eta/d
for
\eta>0

for a constant

\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
attains dimension-independent error bounds if
\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,d,\gamma_{u},\tilde{t}_{u},\theta}

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,d,\gamma_{u},\tilde{t}_{u},\theta}}{16^{2\theta(\alpha+\lambda-d)+1}}\leqslant

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\exp\Big(C_{\alpha,\lambda,\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\gamma^{\frac{1+2d}{\alpha+\lambda+1/2}}_{j}2^{\tilde{t}_{j}(2(\alpha+\lambda)+1)}\Big).

\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,d,\gamma_{u},\tilde{t}_{u},\theta}

Remark 7
.

Viewing
\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,\gamma_{u},\tilde{t}_{u},\theta}
from Theorem
3

\Gamma_{\alpha,\lambda,d,\gamma_{u},\tilde{t}_{u},\theta}

When

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{\Gamma(m)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\

Remark 9
.

For base-2 Niederreiter sequences, substituting the bound (

6
Numerical experiments

This section tests our theoretical results on integrands with varying dimensions and decay rates of
\gamma_{u}
. We also reference
[
36
]
, which provides further experiments demonstrating near-optimal convergence rates of median RQMC and comparisons to higher-order scrambled digital nets.

The test integrands have the common form

where
s=10,100,1000
controls the dimension of integration domain,
\gamma=2,3,4
controls the decay rate of
\gamma_{u}
and
c_{\alpha}
for
\alpha=0,1
is a normalizing constant to ensure
\|f\|_{\alpha,1}=1
. To determine
c_{\alpha}
, we start by computing the ANOVA component given by equation (
7
):

f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{c^{|u|}_{\alpha}}{(\prod_{j\in u}j)^{\gamma}}\prod_{j\in u}(x_{j}\exp(x_{j})-1).

(33)

For
\lambda=1
and smooth function
f
, the Vitali variation defined in equation (
9
) can be viewed as a Riemann sum for

\Big(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\Big(f^{(1,\dots,1)}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\Big)^{1/2}.

Hence,
V^{(|u|)}_{1}(f_{u})=\|f^{(1,\dots,1)}_{u}\|_{L^{2}}
. As discussed in Section
3
,
\|f\|_{u,\alpha,\lambda}=V^{u}_{\lambda}(f_{u})
when
\alpha=0
. Therefore,

\|f\|_{u,0,1}=\|f^{(1,\dots,1)}_{u}\|_{L^{2}}=\frac{c^{|u|}_{0}}{(\prod_{j\in u}j)^{\gamma}}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}(x+1)^{2}\exp(2x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{|u|/2}

and we can make
\|f_{s,\gamma,0}\|_{0,1}=1
by setting

c_{0}=\Big(\int_{0}^{1}(x+1)^{2}\exp(2x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{-1/2}.

Next, when
\alpha=1
, it follows from the definition (
15
) that

\|f\|_{u,1,1}=\sup_{v\subseteq u}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant 1}^{|u|}\\
\alpha_{j}=1\ \forall j\in v,\\
\alpha_{j}>0\ \forall j\in u\setminus v\end{subarray}}V^{v}_{1}(f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u})}_{u})=\sup_{v\subseteq u}V^{v}_{1}(f^{(1,\dots,1)}_{u}).

Let

g=f^{(1,\dots,1)}_{u}.

For each
v\subseteq u
, because
((x+1)\exp(x))^{\prime}=(x+2)\exp(x)
,

V^{(|v|)}_{1}\Bigl(g(\text{ }\cdot\text{ };\boldsymbol{x}_{v^{c}})\Bigr)=\frac{c^{|u|}_{1}}{(\prod_{j\in u}j)^{\gamma}}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}(x+2)^{2}\exp(2x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{|v|/2}\prod_{j\in u\setminus v}(x_{j}+1)\exp(x_{j}).

Therefore, by equation (
12
),

\displaystyle\Big(V^{v}_{1}(g)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{c^{2|u|}_{1}}{(\prod_{j\in u}j)^{2\gamma}}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}(x+2)^{2}\exp(2x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{|v|}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}(x+1)^{2}\exp(2x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{|u|-|v|}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|f^{(2,\dots,2)}_{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

while
V^{u}_{1}(g)=\|g^{(1,\dots,1)}\|_{L^{2}}=\|f^{(2,\dots,2)}_{u}\|_{L^{2}}
. Therefore,

\|f\|_{u,1,1}=\|f^{(2,\dots,2)}_{u}\|_{L^{2}}=\frac{c^{|u|}_{1}}{(\prod_{j\in u}j)^{\gamma}}\Big(\int_{0}^{1}(x+2)^{2}\exp(2x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{|u|/2}

and we can make
\|f_{s,\gamma,1}\|_{1,1}=1

7
Discussion

Our current analysis presumes fixed constants
\alpha
and
\lambda
for which
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}<\infty
. In practice, integrands are expected to satisfy
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}<\infty
across a spectrum of
\alpha
and
\lambda
values, as demonstrated by
f_{s,\gamma,\alpha}
in Section
6
. Crucially, since median RQMC methods do not explicitly incorporate smoothness information, the resulting error bound achieves the minimum over all viable
\alpha
and
\lambda
. A more nuanced analysis should consequently incorporate variable smoothness parameters and derive error bounds under the optimal ones. Developing a framework for such an analysis is an open problem we leave for future research.

Another unresolved challenge is how to select
d
for our randomization. As noted in Remark
7
,
\tilde{t}_{u}
generally scales with
d
. Theorem
3
further implies that increasing
d
beyond
\alpha+\lambda
does not enhance the convergence rate, suggesting the upper bound
d\leqslant\lceil\alpha+\lambda\rceil
. This constraint, however, offers little insights for high-dimensional integration of smooth integrands, where dimensionality—rather than smoothness—governs the convergence rate. A critical open question is how to rigorously define an effective smoothness criterion that jointly incorporates dimensionality and traditional smoothness parameters. Addressing this could yield a principled framework for selecting
d
, thereby optimizing integration performance.

A natural extension of our analysis is to integrands with discontinuities in their derivatives or function values. As discussed in Subsection
2.4
, the requirement
f\in C^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}([0,1]^{s})
can be relaxed provided that the weak derivatives satisfy equation (
5
). Notably, the fractional Vitali variation framework inherently accommodates discontinuities. By developing a theory for non-smooth integrands, our results could be extended to real-world applications in finance and related fields. For instance,
[
16
]
showcases an infinite-dimensional integration problem where all ANOVA components are smooth despite discontinuities in the integrand’s derivatives, suggesting that non-smoothness may not fundamentally compromise convergence rates in high-dimensional settings.
