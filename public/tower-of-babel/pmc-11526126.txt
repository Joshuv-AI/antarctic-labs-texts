Single-cell transcriptomics of rectal organoids from individuals with perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease reveals patient-specific signatures
Authors: Shanta Murthy, Murugadas Anbazhagan, Sushma Chowdary Maddipatla, Vasantha L. Kolachala, Anne Dodd, Ranjit Pelia, David J. Cutler, Jason D. Matthews, Subra Kugathasan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease (CD) is a severe gastrointestinal disorder causing extensive mucosal damage with limited treatment options. Severe manifestations of the disease appear at higher rates in non-Europeans but the genetic and cellular mechanisms driving the disease phenotypes remain poorly understood. Herein, we tested whether pathologic determinants in the epithelial stem cell compartment could be detected at the transcript level in rectal organoids derived from a diverse patient population. Rectal organoid and mucosal cells from endoscopic biopsies of each patient having perianal fistulizing CD or no disease controls were prepared for and sequenced at the single cell level. After cell type annotations based on expressed marker genes, samples were analyzed by principal components, for differential transcript expression, cell type proportions, and pathway enrichment. After QC, we produced 77,044 rectal organoid cells (n = 13 patients; 8 CD, 5 controls) with high quality sequences that identified 10 distinct epithelial subtypes, that we compared to 141,367 mucosal epithelial cells (n = 29 patients; 18 CD, 11 controls). Consistent with mucosal epithelial cells, rectal organoids prominently displayed disease signatures represented by the stem and transit amplifying regions of the rectal crypt, including alterations in transcriptional signatures of metabolic, epigenetic, and proliferating pathways. Organoids also retained their gender- and ancestral-specific gene expression signatures. However, they lacked many of the inflammatory signatures observed in epithelial cells from diseased mucosa. Perianal CD patient derived rectal organoids reflect gene expression signatures related to disease, gender, and ancestry, suggesting they harbor inherent properties amenable to further patient-specific, disease-related experimentation.

Introduction

Crohn’s disease (CD), one of the main forms of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), can occur anywhere along the gastrointestinal tract, but rectal and perianal manifestations of CD are generally more severe, affecting individuals from non-European ancestries at twice the rate1–3. Moreover, perianal fistulizing CD responds poorly to currently available biologicals commonly used for treating luminal CD, and thus new strategies are needed for mitigating these disease trajectories. The limited success in targeting the immune system to treat perianal fistulizing CD indicates there might be other pathogenic cell behaviors in the mucosa that play a negative role in disease. Multiple studies have indicated that epithelial cells are involved in the pathobiology of CD4–7. More specifically, we have shown in a genetically diverse patient population with severe perianal fistulizing CD that transcription and cell composition changes are occurring in rectal epithelial cells8. Thus, having an in vitro system with the patients’ epithelial cells harboring measurable disease signatures that can be leveraged as endpoints for drug testing would be instrumental in finding tailored interventions to different types of CD manifestations.

Mucosal barrier function requires regulated proliferation and differentiation of the epithelium; thus, mucosal healing is also unlikely to be as effective when pathogenic changes are taking place in epithelial stem or transit amplifying cells at the base of the crypt. We hypothesized that innate factors in the patients’ epithelial progenitor cells facilitate conditions for perianal fistulizing CD. Since the inflammatory milieu of the mucosa during CD makes it difficult to assess cause and effect, rectal organoids grown in the absence of signals to the mucosa gives an independent assessment of epithelial cell behavior.

To this end, we tested whether pathogenic transcriptomic signatures are retained at the single cell level in patient derived rectal organoids and examined how well these changes corresponded with mucosal epithelial cell signatures from the same patients of a genetically diverse cohort in different stages of perianal fistulizing CD.

Results

Single cell profiling of rectal organoids from patients with perianal CD

To begin the assessment on the ability of patient derived rectal organoids to harbor disease signatures, they were first established from crypts dissected from freshly obtained mucosal biopsies. Patient organoids expanded in Intesticult™ media typically ranged from 250 to 550 µm at the time of experimentation, demonstrating a rounded morphology across the samples when viewed microscopically (Fig. 1A). The organoids displayed a mix of spherical and oblong structures, but no associations were discernable between disease phenotype and organoid structures, regardless of whether the corresponding patient had CD, severe disease including perianal CD with inflamed mucosa, or a non-IBD control. After 3–4 weeks of growth in culture, gene expression in the organoids was analyzed by scRNA-seq and used to assess the subtypes and abundance of epithelial cells present in the culture (Fig. 1B, C). The expression levels of genes from single organoid cells were normalized, scaled, clustered, and shown as UMAP color coded to show epithelial cell subtypes (left) and lineage groups (right) in Fig. 1C. At the single cell level, 10 epithelial subtypes were identified in organoids, corresponding to four basic epithelial cell lineages: undifferentiated/stem, early absorptive, early secretory and a fourth group comprising lineages including a microfold-like cluster (Fig. 1C). Expression levels of marker genes used to identify the clusters are visualized in the dot plot in Fig. 1D. Thus, as expected the rectal organoids expressed features typical for the base of an intestinal crypt9.

Perianal fistulizing CD patients have mucosal epithelial cells with changes in WNT signaling and disruptions to their metabolic, epigenetic, and proliferative pathways

Next, we examined the abundance of epithelial cell subtypes amongst the phenotypic and genotypic groups. Cells from each patients’ organoid sample are shown in Fig. 2A color coded by their epithelial subtypes. The relative abundance of these epithelial subtypes for each patient organoid sample are quantified in Fig. 2B along with a comparison across the phenotypes is shown in Fig. 2C. The proportions of absorptive progenitors were significantly different across the ancestral and gender groups (Fig. 2C) but had similar levels of abundance between disease phenotypes. The proportions of the proliferating G2-M/TA subtypes were significantly different between genders (P < 0.05*, 0.01**, Wilcoxon and Anova). Thus, while rectal organoids did not show significant epithelial compositional changes amongst the CD groups, surprisingly they did reflect differences between the individuals’ gender and ancestry.

Pathway analysis of biological activity using transcriptional data from the organoids of different CD phenotypes indicated significant changes across multiple reactome pathways shown in Fig. 2D and Supplemental Table 1 (FDR significance with P adjust < 0.05). For example, metabolic pathways in organoids from the inflamed group revealed decreased fatty acid and cholesterol metabolic activity, among other metabolic changes. Organoid samples established from inflamed patient mucosa had increased expression of epigenetic pathway genes involving chromatin modification, and an enrichment for Rho GTPase. They also revealed a reduced enrichment of genes linked to pathways involved in proliferation as compared to organoids derived from patients with inactive perianal fistulizing CD or to non-IBD controls. No remarkable signals were noted for inflammatory pathways in the organoids although the inflamed group did show enrichment for NLRP3 related activity.

To further investigate properties of the rectal organoids, we compared them with sequenced mucosal cells from the base of the crypt. To this end, most of the organoids in this study were established from a set of biopsies that were collected at the same mucosal site during a patient’s endoscopy procedure and then processed for scRNA-seq, thus allowing for the patient’s mucosal epithelial cells to be compared to their cultured organoids. The epithelial compartment from the mucosal samples of these patients (141,367 mucosal epithelial cells; n = 29 patients; 18 CD, 11 controls) comprised 18 epithelial cell subtypes (Fig. 3A), ranging from stem cells to fully differentiated goblet, colonocytes, and tuft cells. As expected, integration of mucosal epithelial and organoid cells by rPCA and shown by UMAP conveyed that organoids recapitulated most of the cell types in the lower crypt region (Fig. 3B) but did not produce cells that clustered with fully differentiated cell subtypes including goblet and tuft cells. Consistent with this, crypt axis scoring that used marker genes to define regions of the crypt axis, showed organoids aligned mostly with transcriptional profiles corresponding to the base region of the crypt (Fig. 3C). Mapping the mucosal epithelial and organoid cells to their corresponding epithelial lineages as shown in Fig. 3D revealed that rectal organoids grown with Intesticult™ media produced a high abundance of undifferentiated (stem-like) cells, whereas mucosal epithelial cells consisted of higher proportions of differentiated absorptive cell subtypes.

To look at transcriptomic differences under these conditions that might account for the lack of differentiated cell types in rectal organoids, we compared the gene expression levels of several cell-fate regulating transcription factors expressed in organoids and mucosal epithelial cells (Fig. 3E). Organoid transcriptomes lacked signatures for mature secretory (e.g. co-expression of ATOH1 and SPINK4) or absorptive lineages but were like the undifferentiated cell types of the mucosal epithelium showing robust expression for HES1 and the stem marker OLFM4, along with the proliferative marker MKI67. Real time PCR confirmed that rectal organoids expressed comparable levels of HES1 to that of rectal mucosa, but unlike secretory cells of the mucosa, the organoids expressed only minute levels of ATOH1 and SPINK4 (Fig. 3F). We also noticed remarkable differences in the transcription density of unique genes expressed by cells in vivo versus those grown in vitro, with cells in vitro expressing twice the number of unique genes per cell (Fig. 3G). The distinguishing bimodal pattern in unique gene expression that is shown by all but one mucosal sample, is absent from about half of the inflamed mucosal samples (Fig. 3G; IF, inflamed; NI, non-inflamed). However, when analyzed similarly by cell subtypes, the stem cells from the inflamed mucosa show a bimodal pattern, whereas the non-inflamed and control cells are unimodal. Thus, with more unique genes expressed per cell than mucosal epithelial cells, the organoids appeared to have ectopic gene expression that might be associated with imbalances in the WNT/NOTCH pathways and the constitutive HES1 expression keeping organoids in an undifferentiated state.

We continued with a supervised assessment of WNT and NOTCH signaling activity based on known pathway genes that are differentially expressed across organoid phenotypic groups, and between organoid and mucosa cells (Fig. 4, P < 0.05). Inflamed rectal mucosa from perianal CD patients had higher levels of WNT and NOTCH signaling compared to the non-inflamed perianal CD and controls. Higher levels of expressed WNT and NOTCH genes were also associated with organoids obtained from inflamed perianal CD patients, showing increased expression of FBXW11, CTNNB1, CHD8 and PPP2R5A. Inflamed mucosal samples had increased levels of RAC1 expression, however RAC1 expression was highest in the organoids from non-inflamed controls.

Rectal organoids replicate metabolic and epigenetic transcriptional signatures associated with undifferentiated epithelial cells from the base of the crypt

To confirm that the signatures detected in organoids were representative of mucosal signatures of disease, we performed pathway analysis on mucosal single cell RNA sequencing samples of undifferentiated epithelial cell populations (base of crypt) that is shown in Fig. 5A and Supplemental Table 2 (P < 0.05 and Log2FC > 0.25 using MAST algorithm). Consistent with our previous study8, active inflammatory disease in the mucosa had a clear impact on the epithelium, affecting transcript levels of genes related to pathways across the spectrum of cellular behavior (Fig. 5A). For instance, in the mucosal epithelial cells, inflammation was associated with changes in transcriptional signatures associated with metabolism and chromatin modification pathways. There were also notable differences in WNT and NOTCH pathways across the organoid disease groups.

To assess differences in pathway activity based on transcription profiles between the mucosa epithelial cells and the corresponding patient derived organoids, we calculated pathway enrichment scores shown in Fig. 5B and Supplemental Table 3. The mucosal epithelial cells were enriched for genes associated with TLR and chromatin modification pathways, while the organoids were enriched for IL-1, antigen processing, and TCR signaling. In some instances, there was partial enrichment of a transcript profile associated with pathways when comparing profiles between organoids and mucosa, as in the case for antigen presentation, C-lectin, and cell proliferating pathways scores. This phenomenon is reflected in the bar graph as enrichment in both organoid and mucosa (Fig. 5, red and blue) for the same pathway because different genes in the pathway were significantly enriched in organoids but other genes in the pathway were enriched for mucosa.

Organoid transcription patterns are strongly associated with patient genetics, but not inflammation

We also analyzed psuedobulked organoid single cell data by PCA without integrated mucosal epithelial cells to look for additional similarities and differences across patient organoid samples that we did not detect in our supervised analysis. Results from the unsupervised approach shown in Fig. 6A and Supplemental Table 5 revealed that ancestry and gender contributed the most to variance amongst the patient organoid samples, while patient age and disease status were both significantly correlated with patient organoid gene expression (Spearmen P < 0.05*, 0.01**, 0.001***). There were no associations found with inflammation in the gene expression profiles of the patient organoid samples.

To find additional associations and biological implications of the gene expression levels driving variance in each principal component, we entered the top genes from each component (listed in Supplemental Table 5) into the STRING network10 and the analytical outputs making connections within the gene group are shown in Fig. 6B. Considering each PC individually, PC1 is defined by gene expression related to patient genetics that spans several STRING networks involving genes KRT20, DUOX2 and DDX3Y, for example. In PC2, the expression levels of TMSB4Y, PRKY and KDM5D, each related to gender-specific biology, are the primary drivers of the component’s variance. In PC3, the age-related variance correlated with the expression patterns of several homeobox genes, including HOXD9-11 and -13 (Fig. 6B). In PC5, the disease-related gene expression driving variance are MMP7, CXCL5, and CXCL14 levels, along with associated STRING networks involving HLA-DQB1, HLA-DRB1, and HLA-DRB5 (Fig. 6B).

Lastly, we also performed PCA on individual epithelial cell subtypes in the organoid cultures for transcriptional signals that might give further mechanistic insight. Examining the 10 epithelial subtypes found in organoids revealed that gene expression in the absorptive progenitor cells drove most of the observed variance for gender and ancestry, whereas no other significant variance patterns were present in the other subtypes, shown for example are Microfold-like cells (Fig. 6 C and Supplemental Table 4).

Discussion

The overarching goal of this study was to test the ability of rectal organoids derived from a genetically diverse group of individuals to retain and reveal disease signatures that would give new insight into rectal epithelial cell behavior during perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease. To do so, we directly examined the mucosal epithelial cells in the rectum near the fistula site of patients with perianal fistulizing CD and used their biopsy derived organoids for comparative analysis and testing for pathogenic signatures. While multiple studies have reported dysfunction in epithelial cells during CD4–7, only a few studies have focused on perianal fistulizing CD8,11–13, and even fewer employing rectal organoids or examining non-Europeans. Our organoid study on a genetically diverse group with perianal fistulizing CD presented here shows that epithelial cells in mucosa near the fistula site are likely undergoing metabolic and epigenetic changes that we could detect in the patients’ biopsy derived organoids. Thus, patient derived rectal organoids do appear to maintain some aspects of disease under the conditions used here.

However, many of the inflammatory signatures and TLR pathways observed in the mucosa based on transcriptomic data were not preserved in the organoids in this study, indicating that other stimuli, including the immune cells and microbiome, might be needed to drive these epithelial cell pathways. At the outset of the study, we hypothesized that a pathogenic event(s) in the epithelial cells at the base of the crypt are contributing to the immune response. On the contrary, we found that most of the differences in metabolic signatures in epithelial transcriptomes were residing in organoids established from inflamed mucosa with active disease, suggesting that the inflammatory milieu from which they were harvested had promoted changes in the mucosal epithelium and could still be detected in the organoids three passages after being established. We made a similar observation in bulk RNA-seq data obtained from ileal organoids derived from inflamed CD patients with B1, B2 or B3 disease14. We think that if the pathologic changes were inherent to the patient’s epithelial cells, we would see these signals in organoids from the patients in all phases of perianal fistulizing disease activity that would set them apart from normal epithelial cells, including mucosa with inactive perianal disease (non-inflamed), but we did not. The differences we observed were primarily related to ancestry and gender, and with respect to disease, it was the organoids from inflamed samples that showed significant changes in epithelial cell gene expression, indicating the presence of carry-over effects, potentially epigenetic ones, from the inflammatory milieu. Based on the transcriptomic and ectopic gene expression profiles exemplified by the organoids, it is likely that at least some of the changes associated with organoids from inflamed mucosa are epigenetic modifications that were originally promoted by mucosal inflammation and maintained after three passages in vitro. The other differences between organoids and mucosa epithelial cells are likely related to the absence of an appropriate crypt-axis and mesenchymal cells to exquisitely regulate the spatial and temporal growth and differentiation of the organoid cells in culture.

The culturing conditions of organoids unquestionably influence their ability to retain patient specific characteristics ex vivo, and there are also cellular changes caused by growth conditions that are not patient specific15. To date, unlike iPSC cultures16, most systems propagating epithelial cells from intestinal crypts as organoids rely on culturing conditions that will promote either a more stem-like cell population, or enrich for one or more, but usually not all, differentiated lineage subtypes17. However, while most of the epithelial subtypes can be generated to some extent in vitro through removal or supplementation of factors like IL-22 in the media18,19, there are currently no established media preparations and/or growth conditions used widely and commercially available that can generate the spatio–temporal axis necessary to promote a compositional and functional subcellular profile of intestinal epithelial subtypes in vitro that fully replicates the behavior of mucosa. Recently, murine assembloid systems made by combining mesenchymal and organoid cells together, have shown promise in generating the full repertoire and proportions of mucosal epithelial subtypes in vitro17.

While considerable effort has been made in defining standards for intestinal organoid growth20, these metrics have not been widely established, leading to challenges in comparative analysis across reports from different laboratories using different culture media formulations. Based on our hypothesis focusing on cells in the lower crypt region, we chose Intesticult™ media since it grows these cell subtypes well and is a widely used and quality-controlled media formulation. Our analysis shows, however, that there are still modifications to the transcriptomes of the epithelial cells in organoids grown in this media and are thus not precise replicas of their mucosal stem cell counterparts. For example, epithelial cells from the organoids expressed more genes per cell than epithelial cells in the mucosa, suggesting there might be modifications in chromatin accessibility facilitating aberrant gene expression in vitro. Nonetheless, based on the overall gene expression profiles of the organoids, our results here indicate that rectal organoids grown in Intesticult™ media do provide sufficient representation of cells comprising the lower crypt and importantly, retained several of the CD signatures.

We expected the rectal organoids to have more transcriptional changes associated with the patients’ clinical phenotype. However, we were surprised to find that ancestry and gender had more robust associations with gene expression than the CD phenotype. Primary human rectal epithelial cells grown as organoids that showed these patterns of ancestral and gender transcriptional associations have not been reported elsewhere. There are differences in gene expression behavior detected between African Americans and Europeans in the reprogramming efficiency of their primary fibroblasts21, but clearly more investigation is needed and with more cell types, to delineate any biological significance to these differences.

Thus, the effect these differences in ancestral and gender associated gene expression in the epithelium might have on CD should be considered in future studies. Our present study suggests that a person’s response to mucosal injury and inflammation might be related to the metabolic activities in their epithelial cells. Furthermore, the differences in gene expression observed amongst gender and ancestral samples in absorptive progenitor cells suggest these subtypes might play a unique protective role in facilitating the maintenance of homeostasis in some individuals or indicate the onset/presence of disease. While the transcriptional associations appear to be related more to genetic factors than disease status, the complex physiological imbalances taking place during CD might be related to these subtle cellular differences. One of the limitations of our study using bioinformatic assessments of these cells was not testing or interrogating the predicted pathways mechanistically to determine how each person’s cells respond to perturbation while further deciphering the implications of the predicted STRING networks, particularly the ones defined by PC5 that is most associated with disease phenotype. Future experimental work should leverage and target the patterns of gene expression captured herein and focus on the associated metabolic and chromatin dynamics and regulatory elements that are influenced the most by inflammation. Taken together, the work here gives a rich source of sequence data for further hypothesis building and substantiates the utility of patient derived rectal organoids as an individualized testing platform for identifying epithelial-specific factor(s).

Methods

Biospecimen collection and organoid culturing

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Rectal mucosal biopsies were obtained from patients with perianal fistulizing CD and from patients that did not have inflammatory bowel disease (controls) under a protocol approved by Emory University Institutional Review Boards (IRB) (Supplemental Table 6). Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s). Organoid cultures were established from patient biopsies as previously described14 and maintained in Matrigel (Corning) with Intesticult™ Human Organoid Growth Media (Stem Cell Technologies). Thus, from the two biopsies obtained from the same rectal location, one biopsy was used for mucosal single cell RNA-sequencing directly, the other patient biopsy taken from the same mucosal site, was used to grow organoids. Single cell experiments on the patient derived organoids were performed at passage 3.

Single cell RNA sequencing

Mucosal biopsies not used for organoid derivation were immediately processed after endoscopy, minced with scissors and enzymatically digested on ice using Bacillus licheniformis protease, before finally filtering and determining cell viability and numbers. For organoids, a single cell suspension of filtered epithelial cells was obtained after 30–40 min of TrypLE™ digestion. Cells were encapsulated with a 10× Genomics Chromium, and after RT-PCR and cDNA amplification, libraries were prepared using 10× Genomics 3ʹ single-cell gene expression chemistry and sequenced on Illumina’s Novaseq6000 at the Yerkes Primate Research Center Genomics Core at Emory University.

Data quality control

Raw fastq files were processed using 10× CellRanger software (v7.0.1) and the human transcriptome GRCh38 as the reference to generate gene expression count matrices. Filtered count matrices were processed using Seurat (v5.0.0). We excluded cells with greater than 50% mitochondrial transcripts, epithelial cells comprising fewer than 750 or greater than 7500 features. Cells from organoids were similarly filtered to exclude cells with features below 750 as well as cells with greater than 50% mitochondrial transcripts. We also removed mitochondrial and ribosomal features from our analysis of mucosal epithelial and organoid cells. These filtering parameters were derived following an initial integration of count matrices and by examining the distribution of features, UMI counts and mitochondrial content per cell type and compartment. Integration of count matrices before QC involves log-normalization, identifying the top 2000 variable features and selecting integrated features list which are used for scaling and running PCA analysis for 30 PCs for each sample. These samples are integrated by reciprocal PCA method followed by scaling, PCA using the first 30 significant components, UMAP and clustering with 0.5 resolution on the integrated assay. Identities of the resultant clusters are assigned based on cell markers (Supplemental Table 7) and their cell cycle phases.

Unsupervised clustering

Principal component (PC) plots were used to evaluate the transcriptomic variation across samples. Cells were pseudobulked by summation of transcripts per gene per sample and PCA was plotted using the plotPCA() function from DESeq2. Correlation between patient metadata and the first 10 principal components were obtained using the top 500 variable genes with prcomp(). Spearman test was used to assess correlation significance between patient factors and principal components.

Cell type compositional analysis

Cell type proportional differences between PCA and phenotypic grouping of samples were assessed for significance using Wilcoxon and ANOVA tests.

Pathway analysis

Differentially expressed genes per cell type were determined by MAST22. The pathways involving differentially expressed genes were determined by the enrichR package version 3.2 using the REACTOME database. Pathways were filtered by the number of differentially expressed genes (filtered by P < 0.05 and magnitude of log2FC > 0.25) identified in selected pathways and the frequency of cell types enriched for the pathway.

Crypt-axis score

SELENOP, CEACAM7, PLAC8, CEACAM1, TSPAN1, CEACAM5, CEACAM6, IFI27, DHRS9, KRT20, RHOC, CD177, PKIB, HPGD, and LYPD8 were used with AddModuleScore() function to assess their aggregated expression and the score distribution across cell clusters23.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR

Total RNA from organoids and tissue biopsies were extracted using a Qiagen micro-RNA kit and cDNA prepared with a High-capacity RT kit (ThermoFisher). Gene expression analysis was performed using a 7500 fast real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) system (Applied Biosystems). Taqman gene expression primers for SPINK4, EPCAM, ATOH1, HES1, and SPDEF, were obtained from ThermoFisher. Data were expressed using the 2−ddCt method with Ct limit of 40.

Statistical analysis

Statistical tests were used to assess significant associations and differences between categories within the organoids and epithelial mucosa single-cell datasets. Spearman correlation was conducted to identify association of patient metadata with principal components demonstrating transcriptomic variation within the organoid data. Wilcoxon and ANOVA were used to identify significant proportional differences when comparing gender, ancestry, or disease phenotypic groups using a significance of P < 0.05. Significant REACTOME pathway enrichment with P < 0.05 were used for further analyses. Pathways were determined by significant (P < 0.05, log2FC > 0.25) differentially expressed genes tested using the MAST algorithm.
