Clinical and Microbiological Characteristics of Pulmonary Aspergillosis in Elderly vs Non-Elderly Patients: A tNGS-Based Retrospective Study
Authors: Miao Yu, Qianqian Zhao, Jingwen Huang, Jiaxin Tian, Xuefei Liu, Simin Xie, Chao Sun, Yijie Li, Wanjie Xu, Fengfei Qian, Xiaohua Zhong, Chuanhua Nie, Xiaoli Zhao, Wenxin Wu, Min Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElderly patients are more susceptible to pulmonary aspergillosis(PA) and exhibit worse clinical outcomes. This study aims to compare the clinical characteristics, pathogen distribution, co-infection patterns, and outcomes of elderly and non-elderly patients with PA diagnosed via targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS).MethodsThis retrospective study included 107 patients with tNGS-confirmed pulmonary aspergillosis admitted to Ruijin Hospital between March 2023 and January 2025. Demographic, clinical, radiological, and laboratory data were extracted from electronic medical records to compare the clinical and microbiological profiles between the elderly (≥65 years) and non-elderly (<65 years) cohorts.ResultsA total of 107 patients including elderly (n=64) and non-elderly (n=43) were included retrospectively. The prevalence of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis was significantly higher in the elderly group compared to the non-elderly group (93.8% vs 74.4%). Similarly, the elderly group had significantly higher proportions of overall co-infection (79.7% vs 51.2%), bacterial co-infection (68.8% vs 39.5%), and atypical imaging features such as patchy infiltrates/consolidation (89.1% vs 62.8%) (all P < 0.05). Furthermore, the elderly exhibited significantly lower lymphocyte and albumin levels, and higher D-dimer levels (P < 0.05). Mortality was also significantly higher among elderly patients (18.8% vs 4.7%; P = 0.042).ConclusionWithin our study scope, pulmonary aspergillosis in the elderly is characterized by higher mortality, atypical imaging, prevalent bacterial co-infections, and specific laboratory deviations—most notably, diminished lymphocytes and albumin alongside elevated D-dimer and LDH levels. Furthermore, tNGS serves as a valuable adjunctive tool to identify complex mixed pathogens and assist in clinical management.Keywords: pulmonary aspergillosis, targeted next-generation sequencing, tNGS, elderly, co-infection

Introduction

Aspergillus species are ubiquitous environmental molds that primarily infect humans via spore inhalation.1 While typically harmless in immunocompetent individuals, they can cause a wide spectrum of diseases in susceptible hosts. Pulmonary aspergillosis (PA) is the most common manifestation, encompassing clinical entities ranging from hypersensitivity-driven allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) and saprophytic pulmonary aspergilloma to life-threatening invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA).2,3

The specific clinical presentation and severity of PA depend heavily on the host’s immune status and underlying structural lung diseases. Amidst global population aging, the elderly have emerged as a highly susceptible cohort to pulmonary aspergillosis.4,5 Recent epidemiological data from a large-scale national cohort reveal a continuous annual increase in aspergillosis prevalence, with older adults (≥65 years) exhibiting a nearly five-fold higher prevalence compared to younger demographics.6 Their vulnerability is primarily driven by immunosenescence—a physiological decline in immune surveillance—coupled with a high prevalence of cardiopulmonary comorbidities and recurrent corticosteroid or antibiotic use, which severely compromise both local airway barriers and systemic defenses.5 Because these pre-existing conditions often obscure the atypical clinical and radiological manifestations of PA in older adults, diagnosis is frequently delayed. This diagnostic challenge, combined with reduced physiological reserve, ultimately leads to delayed antifungal therapy, an elevated risk of severe co-infections, and significantly higher mortality.7

Given the complex clinical picture and high mortality in this adult population, rapid and accurate diagnosis is critical. However, diagnosis of pulmonary aspergillosis is often challenging due to the limitations of conventional methods.2 Sputum Aspergillus culture, the standard approach, suffers from low sensitivity, long turnaround times, and the inability to reliably distinguish between active infection and benign colonization or environmental contamination.8 Non-culture-based methods, such as the galactomannan (GM) antigen assay and polymerase chain reaction (PCR), offer improved diagnostic speed. However, the GM assay’s sensitivity is notably reduced in non-neutropenic patients, and it is known to produce false-positive results due to cross-reactivity with other fungi, certain foods, or even some intravenous antibiotic preparations.9 While PCR assays demonstrate high sensitivity, their clinical utility has been hampered by a lack of standardization in methodologies across different laboratories, which can lead to variable results.9 Critically, all of these targeted methods are hypothesis-driven; they only detect Aspergillus if it is specifically sought. This inherent limitation means they are unable to identify unexpected bacterial, viral, or other fungal co-pathogens that can mimic or complicate the clinical picture, potentially delaying a comprehensive diagnosis.

To overcome these diagnostic bottlenecks and meet the urgent clinical needs of vulnerable adult populations, targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) has emerged as a transformative, culture-independent modality. By employing pre-designed primer panels, tNGS enables the simultaneous and rapid detection of a broad spectrum of pathogens, offering enhanced specificity, cost-effectiveness, and minimal sample requirements.10,11 Recent studies have validated the high concordance of tNGS with clinical diagnoses in lower respiratory tract infections, demonstrating its unique value in decoding complex co-infection landscapes.12–14

Although previous studies highlight that elderly PA patients suffer from more comorbidities and worse outcomes, their reliance on conventional diagnostics overlooks the critical landscape of polymicrobial co-infections and atypical imaging manifestations.7,15 Given the elderly’s heightened vulnerability to secondary pathogens, the absence of tNGS-based studies exploring these complex co-infection dynamics represents a major knowledge gap. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to utilize tNGS specifically to uncover these complex co-infection dynamics in geriatric PA. We hypothesized that elderly PA patients exhibit a distinctly vulnerable clinical phenotype driven by more severe mixed infections. Accordingly, this study aims to compare the clinical characteristics, tNGS-derived pathogen distributions, co-infection patterns, and prognostic outcomes between elderly and non-elderly patients, ultimately guiding tailored clinical management.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

This retrospective study was conducted at the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine, Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, from March 2023 to February 2025. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration (No.2024–393), with the requirement for informed consent waived due to the retrospective design. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) completion of tNGS using bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), serum, or sputum; and (3) clinical suspicion of pulmonary infection (defined by a combination of clinical symptoms such as fever or cough, and suggestive radiological abnormalities). Patients were excluded if: (1) tNGS results were negative for Aspergillus; (2) pulmonary aspergillosis was clinically ruled out (eg, Aspergillus presence was attributed to colonization or environmental contamination); (3) had prior confirmed or relapsing pulmonary aspergillosis; or (4) essential clinical data were incomplete. Diagnoses were established according to the 2016 Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines.9 Specifically, establishing a proven diagnosis necessitated definitive histopathological documentation of tissue invasion or a positive culture from a normally sterile site. Conversely, a probable clinical diagnosis was ascertained through a diagnostic composite comprising recognized host predisposing factors, compatible clinico-radiological manifestations, and corroborative mycological evidence. Two experienced clinicians independently validated the diagnoses by reviewing medical histories, symptoms, imaging, laboratory parameters, and tNGS results; discrepancies were resolved by a third senior physician to reach a consensus. Notably, raw tNGS results were carefully screened to exclude non-pathogenic colonizers and contaminants to accurately determine the co-infection profiles. Ultimately, 107 eligible patients were included and stratified by age into non-elderly (<65 years, n=43) and elderly (≥65 years, n=64) groups (Figure 1). Clinical, laboratory, and radiological data were systematically extracted from electronic medical records.

Patient enrollment flowchart.

Sample Collection and Nucleic Acid Extraction

To ensure data reliability within this retrospective framework, all clinical specimens were collected, handled, and stored following unified standard operating procedures (SOPs) implemented by our hospital’s clinical laboratory. Briefly, respiratory specimens were rapidly transported to the laboratory via a cold chain and processed for nucleic acid extraction, including 72 bronchoalveolar lavage fluid samples, 25 sputum samples, and 10 other specimen types. Nucleic acids were extracted and purified using the TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) and the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Dusseldorf, Germany). Sequencing libraries were prepared with the Pathogeno One 400 Plus Kit (Pathogeno, Guangdong, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Library quality was assessed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis and a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, California, USA). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, CA, USA).

Library Preparation and Sequencing

Raw data from the Illumina MiniSeq platform were processed through an automated quality-control pipeline. Low-quality reads and adapter sequences were removed using fastp (v0.19.5). The remaining reads were aligned to a microbial reference database with Kraken2 (v2.1.2), to the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD; https://card.mcmaster.ca) with RGI (v5.2.0), and to the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB; http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/) with BLASTn (v2.9.0+). Because one or more primer pairs were used for targeted gene amplification, read counts were normalized by the number of primer pairs. Pathogens with >1 read were considered for further analysis.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Venn diagrams were plotted using the “ggvenn” package. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), according to data distribution, while categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and Baseline Clinical Characteristics

A total of 107 patients (64 males; mean age 64.9 ± 14.0 years; mean BMI 21.5 ± 3.7 kg/m 2) were included. Five patients reported occupational exposure to damp or chemical environments. The cohort primarily comprised IPA (85.9%), aspergilloma (11.2%), and ABPA (6.5%), with four patients exhibiting overlapping features. Subtype distribution varied significantly by age: IPA was notably more prevalent in the elderly (≥65 years) (93.8% vs 74.4%, P = 0.011), while aspergilloma was more frequent in the non-elderly (20.9% vs 4.7%, P = 0.022). Common presentations included cough, sputum production, and moist rales on auscultation. Notably, fever was significantly more common in the elderly group (59.4% vs 34.9%, P = 0.022).

A vast majority of patients (92.5%) presented with chronic comorbidities, among which underlying structural or chronic pulmonary diseases were the most prevalent, affecting 57.9% of the cohort (predominantly COPD, bronchiectasis, lung cancer, and asthma). Previous tuberculosis was significantly more common in the non-elderly (9.3% vs 0.0%, P = 0.049). Other systemic comorbidities (eg, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes) showed no significant age-related differences. Additionally, 20.6% of patients had a history of systemic immunosuppressive therapy, including corticosteroids or chemotherapy (Table 1).

Distribution of Pathogens and Co-Infection Patterns

Among the 107 pulmonary aspergillus patients, Aspergillus fumigatus was the most commonly detected species (60.7%), followed by A. flavus (26.2%), A. niger (9.3%), A. terreus (6.5%), and A. nidulans (2.8%). Other less common species were identified in 5 cases (4.7%), including A. tubingensis, A. oryzae, A. lentulus, and A. tabacinus. Co-infection with two or more Aspergillus species was observed in 11 patients (10.3%), with the most frequent combination being A. flavus and A. fumigatus. The distribution of Aspergillus species stratified by age group is illustrated in Figure 2. Overall, the species distribution profiles were highly similar between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences observed in the relative percentages of any individual Aspergillus species.

Species distribution of Aspergillus stratified by age group. The stacked bar chart illustrates the relative percentages of different Aspergillus species identified in the elderly (≥65 years) and non-elderly (<65 years) cohorts.

Among the 107 patients included, co-infections with other pathogens were identified by tNGS and further confirmed by clinical evaluation. A total of 73 patients (68.2%) had mixed infections of varying types, with Aspergillus–bacterial co-infection being the most frequent pattern. The overall co-infection rate was notably higher in the elderly patients compared to the non-elderly cohort (79.7% vs 51.2%, P =0.003). When evaluating the detection rates of each pathogen category, bacterial co-infection was the most prominent (Figure 3A). Elderly patients exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of bacterial co-infections compared to non-elderly patients (68.8% vs 39.5%, P = 0.005). The detection rates for viruses and other fungi were 28.1% vs 25.6% (P = 0.827) and 18.8% vs 7% (P = 0.098), respectively (Figure 3B). The most commonly detected bacterial co-pathogens were Pseudomonas aeruginosa (n=18) and Acinetobacter baumannii(n=14). Viral co-pathogens included SARS-CoV-2, cytomegalovirus (CMV), and influenza A virus. Other fungal co-infections primarily involved Pneumocystis jirovecii, Candida, and Cryptococcus(Figure 3C–3E). Chest Imaging Characteristics

The chest imaging manifestations exhibited significant heterogeneity, including halo sign, cavitary lesions, pulmonary nodules, air crescent sign, aspergilloma formation, and gloved finger sign (Figure 4A–4F). Overall, patchy infiltrates or consolidation constituted the predominant pattern, observed in 84 patients (78.5%). Regarding anatomical distribution, the right upper lobe was the most frequently involved site (76 cases, 71.0%), followed by the right lower lobe (70 cases, 65.4%), left upper lobe (67 cases, 62.6%), right middle lobe (67 cases, 62.6%), and left lower lobe (64 cases, 59.8%). Multilobar involvement (≥2 lobes) was detected in the majority of the cohort (79 cases, 73.8%).

Representative imaging features in patients with Aspergillus infection. (A) Halo sign; (B) Cavitary lesions; (C) Pulmonary nodules; (D) Air crescent sign; (E) Aspergilloma formation; (F) Gloved finger sign.

Age-stratified analysis revealed distinct radiological profiles. Patchy infiltrates or consolidation were significantly more prevalent in elderly patients compared to the non-elderly cohort (89.1% vs 62.8%, P = 0.002). Furthermore, elderly patients were significantly more prone to exhibit pleural effusion (25.0% vs 9.3%, P = 0.046). Conversely, classic radiological hallmarks of pulmonary aspergillosis were significantly more frequent in non-elderly patients. Specifically, typical features such as pulmonary nodules (41.9% vs 21.9%, P = 0.033) and the air crescent sign (25.6% vs 6.2%, P = 0.009) were notably more common in the younger cohort (Figure 5).

Comparison of Clinical Characteristics in Elderly and Non-Elderly Patients

Clinical and laboratory characteristics were further compared between non-elderly and elderly patients. Elderly patients demonstrated significantly lower eosinophil counts (0.05 vs 0.12, 109/L, P = 0.009) and lymphocyte counts (1.12 vs 1.52, 109/L, P = 0.002) compared with younger patients. In contrast, markers of systemic inflammation and coagulation were elevated among elderly individuals, including D-dimer (0.95 vs 0.35 mg/L, P = 0.004) and lactate dehydrogenase (218 vs 188 U/L, P = 0.050). Nutritional status also appeared worse in elderly patients, as reflected by significantly lower serum albumin levels (32.0 vs 36.0 g/L, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Treatment and Prognosis

All 92 patients with invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) received antifungal therapy, primarily including voriconazole, isavuconazole, amphotericin B, posaconazole, itraconazole, and caspofungin. Among them, 78 patients (84.8%) showed improvement or were cured, while 14 patients (15.2%) died. Subgroup analysis based on age revealed a significantly higher mortality rate in elderly patients compared to non-elderly patients (12[18.8%] vs 2[4.7%], P = 0.042). The deceased were predominantly those with comorbid cardiopulmonary diseases (such as COPD and coronary heart disease), malignancies (including lung cancer, lymphoma, leukemia), or immune deficiencies. Causes of death mainly included disseminated infection (Aspergillus-induced septicemia and intracranial infection), organ failure (complicated by renal failure, heart failure), mixed infections (Pneumocystis jirovecii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, SARS-CoV-2, CMV, etc), and massive hemoptysis. Seven patients with ABPA improved after treatment with oral corticosteroids or inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) combined with antifungal therapy. Among 12 patients with pulmonary aspergilloma, 4 improved following combined antifungal and surgical treatment, and 8 improved after antifungal therapy alone.

Discussion

Over recent decades, the incidence of pulmonary aspergillosis has progressively increased, driven largely by the expanding population of immunocompromised hosts, the widespread use of broad-spectrum antibiotics and corticosteroids, and demographic aging.2,16 However, Aspergillus infections frequently present with atypical clinical and radiological features, posing significant diagnostic challenges that often lead to delayed therapeutic intervention and disproportionately high mortality.17,18 The present study provides a comprehensive comparative analysis of the clinical, radiological, microbiological, and prognostic profiles of pulmonary aspergillosis stratified by age. Our findings highlight profound age-related dichotomies in disease presentation—particularly regarding clinical subtypes, imaging hallmarks, and co-infection patterns—underscoring the critical need for tailored diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in the elderly population.

Our cohort demonstrated a distinct epidemiological divergence in the clinical subtypes of aspergillosis across age groups. IPA was overwhelmingly predominant in the elderly (93.8%), whereas pulmonary aspergilloma was significantly more frequent in the non-elderly cohort. This disparity is intimately linked to the underlying structural pulmonary conditions of the hosts. In our study, a history of previous tuberculosis was exclusively observed in the non-elderly group, providing the preexisting cavitary architectures necessary for Aspergillus colonization and subsequent aspergilloma formation. Conversely, elderly patients frequently harbored chronic debilitating respiratory conditions, most notably COPD. In these individuals, impaired mucociliary clearance, diminished local mucosal immunity, and frequent requirement for systemic corticosteroids collaboratively compromise airway defense mechanisms. While intact phagocytic and epithelial cells efficiently eradicate Aspergillus spores in healthy hosts, the structural disruption and local immunosuppression inherent to chronic lung diseases facilitate fungal invasion, thereby driving the high prevalence of IPA among the elderly.19–21

Laboratory parameters further elucidated the disparate systemic pathophysiological states between the two cohorts. Elderly patients exhibited significantly lower lymphocyte and eosinophil counts, alongside diminished serum albumin levels, reflecting a dual burden of immunosenescence and malnutrition. This compromised baseline immunity undoubtedly contributes to impaired host defense against fungal pathogens. Additionally, the significantly elevated D-dimer and LDH levels in the elderly cohort are indicative of a heightened systemic inflammatory state coupled with coagulation activation. These biomarkers suggest that Aspergillus infection in older adults is less effectively localized, rapidly triggering systemic stress, diffuse endothelial injury, and coagulation cascade activation, which complicate the clinical course. Importantly, the observed laboratory deviations in the elderly (eg, lower albumin and lymphocytes) are fundamentally driven by age-related physiological decline. Rather than solely being a direct consequence of the fungal infection, this baseline frailty constitutes a vulnerable clinical phenotype that dictates their higher mortality.

Furthermore, our study demonstrated significant age-related differences in chest imaging profiles. Non-elderly patients frequently exhibited classic, highly specific hallmarks of pulmonary aspergillosis, such as cavitary lesions and the air crescent sign. In stark contrast, these typical features were absent in the majority of elderly patients, who predominantly presented with non-specific patchy infiltrates, massive consolidation, and pleural effusions. We hypothesize that this discrepancy is closely associated with age-dependent variations in immune responsiveness. While immunocompetent younger individuals mount robust local defenses that compartmentalize the fungal infection (leading to classic nodules or cavitations), profound immunosenescence in the elderly—particularly impaired neutrophil recruitment and diminished reactive oxygen species production22,23—fails to contain the initial invasion. This blunted local containment frequently results in non-specific, diffuse inflammatory exudation on imaging, a phenomenon consistent with recent studies showing that elderly and non-neutropenic hosts often lack typical radiological hallmarks of aspergillosis.7,24

Given the inherent limitations of conventional culture-based diagnostics and the atypical clinical-radiological presentation in the elderly, non-culture-based molecular diagnostics are indispensable. While traditional biomarkers such as GM assays and fungal-specific PCR provide critical adjunctive evidence, they often fall short in identifying complex polymicrobial infections. In this context, tNGS has emerged as a transformative diagnostic modality. By utilizing a curated microbial reference database, tNGS offers a cost-effective, rapid, and broad-spectrum pathogen detection platform.25 Unlike traditional diagnostics that often underestimate polymicrobial burdens, our tNGS-based approach uniquely highlights a significantly higher prevalence of complex bacterial co-infections in the elderly, distinguishing our findings from previous conventional reports. In our cohort, tNGS illuminated the complex microbiological landscape of pulmonary aspergillosis, revealing an overall co-infection rate of 68.2%. Notably, elderly patients exhibited a significantly higher propensity for polymicrobial infections, particularly Aspergillus-bacterial co-infections. Pathogens such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii were frequently co-detected. The high prevalence of P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii in the elderly is closely linked to their structural lung diseases (eg, COPD) and frequent healthcare contacts, which predispose them to these opportunistic pathogens. Additionally, substantial concurrent viral infections (eg, SARS-CoV-2, CMV, and influenza) were identified. Respiratory viruses and pathogenic bacteria can disrupt the bronchial mucosal barrier, impair ciliary function, and induce local hypoxia and immunosuppression, thereby creating a highly permissive microenvironment for opportunistic Aspergillus invasion. The synergistic pathogenicity of these mixed infections exacerbates tissue damage and complicates therapeutic decision-making. Therefore, the early application of tNGS in elderly patients presenting with non-specific pulmonary infiltrates is of great importance to unravel these covert co-infections and guide precise antimicrobial stewardship.

Consequently, the combination of compromised immunity, complex co-infections, and underlying comorbidities leads to significantly poorer clinical outcomes in the elderly. Our subgroup analysis identified age as a critical prognostic determinant, with the mortality rate in elderly patients being nearly four times higher than that in the non-elderly cohort (18.8% vs 4.7%). This disparity aligns with previous comparative studies demonstrating higher mortality and atypical presentations in geriatric pulmonary aspergillosis.4,7 The poorer prognosis in older adults is multifactorial. Beyond the immediate severity of age-related immunosenescence and polymicrobial co-infections, elderly patients often possess limited cardiopulmonary reserves due to severe comorbidities (eg, COPD, cardiovascular diseases, and underlying malignancies). Furthermore, age-related declines in hepatic and renal clearance narrow the therapeutic window for systemic triazoles and polyenes, increasing the risk of drug-induced toxicity and limiting optimal antifungal escalation, underscoring the urgent need for individualized therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) for the elderly.5 Causes of death in this vulnerable demographic were predominantly driven by disseminated infection, refractory mixed infections, and multi-organ failure.

The translational value of this study lies in optimizing real-world clinical decision-making. Given the high polymicrobial co-infection rates in the elderly, integrating tNGS significantly improves diagnostic efficiency by rapidly identifying both Aspergillus and secondary pathogens. This rapid profiling empowers clinicians to transition from prolonged empirical therapy to targeted, individualized antimicrobial management much earlier in the disease course.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design and relatively small sample size—constrained by the low clinical incidence of pulmonary aspergillosis—may introduce selection bias and limit the geographic generalizability of our findings. Second, reliance on retrospective data limited clinical granularity. Specifically, we were unable to definitively distinguish between community- and hospital-acquired co-infections, fully adjust for critical confounders (eg, exact ICU duration and prior broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure), or obtain precise time-to-event data for robust Kaplan-Meier survival analyses. Additionally, the long-term functional recovery and sequential radiological evolution of surviving patients were not prospectively tracked. Third, the clinical application of tNGS presents inherent methodological challenges. Its variable sensitivity and specificity, coupled with the risk of amplifying environmental contaminants or colonizers, may introduce potential diagnostic bias.26 Although we rigorously mitigated this by cross-referencing tNGS findings with clinical symptoms and radiological data, minor unavoidable variations in real-world specimen handling and a lack of cross-regional technical standardization remain. Therefore, future large-scale, multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate our findings, establish consensus diagnostic thresholds for tNGS, and evaluate the impact of tNGS-guided interventions on long-term survival.

Conclusion

Within our study scope, pulmonary aspergillosis in the elderly is characterized by higher mortality, atypical imaging, prevalent bacterial co-infections, and specific laboratory deviations—most notably, diminished lymphocytes and albumin alongside elevated D-dimer and LDH levels. Furthermore, tNGS serves as a valuable adjunctive tool to identify complex mixed pathogens and assist in clinical management.
