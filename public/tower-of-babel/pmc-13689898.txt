Leveraging Data‐Driven and Fundamental Insights for Electrolyte Innovation in Lithium Metal Batteries
Authors: Minh Van Duong, Mitchell Kaiser, Xia Cao, Trung Thien Nguyen, Au Nguyen, Phung My Loan Le, Jun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe rise of data science and decades of accumulated battery research have paved the way for the general use of computing in research. Here, the largest experimental coulombic efficiency (CE) dataset curated from over 100 scientific articles under strict selection criteria was analyzed to extract insights that would have been difficult to achieve through traditional scientific methods alone. By splitting the dataset according to CE measured using the Aurbach or cycle methods, normalizing electrolyte formulations by mass, and utilizing advanced data analytics coupled with physically informed feature engineering, we discovered that higher sF, lower sC, and sO values, influenced by fluorination degree, carbon chain length, and oxygen amount, respectively, contribute to improved CE. Surprisingly, boiling becomes a governing factor when all other features are identical, with lower boiling corresponding to higher CE. By leveraging insights gained and the model's accurate CE prediction (R 2 = 0.85), a new electrolyte enabled a high CE of 99.57% in Li||Cu cells, excellent oxidative stability of ∼4.8 V versus Li+/Li, and 80% of capacity retention after more than 700 cycles in Li||NMC811 cells. The versatile machine learning workflow and the crafted features presented in this work can inspire the design of liquid electrolytes for other battery chemistries.

Introduction

Established on the foundational investigation acknowledged by the Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2019, lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs) have transformed every facet of human life [1, 2, 3]. With the expansion of the electric vehicle (EV) industry, the demand for higher energy density and longer‐lasting batteries that are also safe and economically viable has become urgent [4, 5]. Lithium‐metal batteries (LMBs) have re‐emerged as the holy grail of batteries owing to the high specific capacity of Li metal [6, 7]. However, the benefits of LMBs are eclipsed by fundamental issues posed by the inherently high chemical reactivity of Li metal and the notorious formation of spiky structures [8], which eventually causes catastrophic failure [9, 10]. Central to the performance and safety of LMBs is electrolyte, a key component that facilitates Li+ ion mobility between the cathode and anode [11, 12]. Despite its critical role, the discovery and optimization of new electrolytes has remained challenging due to the vast chemical space and the complex dynamics of molecular structures that must be balanced to achieve optimal physical and electrochemical properties.

Over the past decade, both academia and industry have increasingly sought computing approaches to speed up the discovery of new battery materials [13, 14, 15]. With the rise of advanced algorithms, computing power, and large‐scale datasets, machine learning (ML) has quickly penetrated every facet of battery research [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. In the realm of electrolyte research, ML accelerates the electrolyte molecule screening process by utilizing an AI emulator, enabling the prediction of the thermal [21] and electronic properties [22] of molecules at a faster speed. Moreover, the growth of experimental datasets and the emergence of effective data‐driven models facilitate understanding of the underlying interplay between molecular structure and properties, thus informing researchers of promising molecules that endow high CE, high oxidative stability, and good ionic conductivity [23, 24, 25]. Despite such potential, a lack of high‐quality, standardized experimental datasets is the primary roadblock deterring battery scientists from applying ML to their research [26].

This study aims to integrate fundamental knowledge with robust data analytics, data‐driven modelling, and material characterization to elucidate how electrolytes underpin high CE and, ultimately, to design a new electrolyte to improve the CE and long‐cycling performance of LMB. It is recognized that although CE alone may not be universally indicative of a battery's electrochemical performance, it is regarded as one of the most powerful indicators in the early stage of electrolyte development. It is also worth noting that the Li||Cu CE measurements and Li||NMC811 full‐cell cycling were performed under different cell configurations and electrochemical conditions (Table S1). Beyond that, the CE was adopted for the following reason. The so‐called cycle and modified Aurbach methods are well established for measuring CE in Li||Cu cells [27], resulting in a wealth of untapped CE data in the literature. Engineered features, combined with advanced data analytics, were employed to interrogate the dataset and elucidate the relationship between electrolyte composition and CE under two distinct measurement conditions. Finally, a new electrolyte was developed and evaluated against a state‐of‐the‐art localized high‐concentration electrolyte.

Data‐Driven Approach for Electrolyte Development

A holistic approach to discovering novel electrolytes that integrates experimental data and a data‐driven model is depicted in Figure 1a. In this approach, a large amount of CE data was curated from literature. Data cleaning in the following step is essential to ensure good quality of data inputs, with irrelevant information removed. In this study, we place a greater emphasis on rigorous data mining and preprocessing, addressing data quality and representation as central challenges in electrolyte modeling, rather than introducing new model architectures or algorithms [24, 28]. Under strict selection criteria (Table S2), a total of 640 electrolyte formulations comprising 126 distinct molecules spanning salts, solvents, additives, and diluents were compiled from 103 sources, including 102 peer‐reviewed articles and one published patent highly relevant to LMB research. To ensure data consistency, only CE data measured using Cu∥Li coin‐cell configurations with polymeric separators and conducted within the temperature range of 21.5°C–30°C and current density range of 0.25–1 mA cm− 2 were retained. Notably, several previous studies [23, 24, 28] have typically treated CE values as equivalent across measurement protocols, despite evidence that identical electrolytes produce different CE values depending on whether the cycle or modified Aurbach (Aurbach for short) method is employed [27]. This limitation can obscure protocol‐dependent relationships between electrolyte composition and CE, preventing models from capturing underlying patterns and consequently reducing their predictive performance. To address this dependence, we partitioned the dataset into two subdatasets corresponding to cycling‐ and Aurbach‐derived CE measurements and treated them separately. To encode electrolyte, eight features were engineered to capture salt concentration, anion type, carbon chain length, boiling point, and chemical bond type, which are listed in Table 1. Some of these features were intentionally designed to represent conceptual frameworks established within the battery research community. For instance, sO was developed to capture solvating power governed by oxygen as a strongly coordinating species [29]. sF and aF were designed to reflect the formation of fluorine‐derived interphase components [30]. Lithium‐ion concentration [31] (Li) and carbon chains [32] (sC) are known to influence electrolyte properties and, consequently, battery performance. Before feature calculation, one of the key steps is to normalize the mass of each electrolyte formulation to a total of 100 g, ensuring consistency across formulations. Building on these crafted features, an ML model was constructed. If the constructed model does not show good predictive performance, we iterate back to earlier steps to refine features and fine‐tune the model. Eventually, the goal is to extract relevant insights that guide the identification of promising electrolytes.

Electrolyte and Molecular Trends for High CE

Over the past decade, electrolyte research for LMBs has shifted from conventional electrolytes to high‐concentration electrolytes (HCEs), and recently LHCEs, in a race to the goal of 99.99% (Figure S4) [33, 34, 35]. The advantages of LHCEs are illustrated in Figure 2d,h, where LHCEs formulated with ether solvents occupy a broad region of high CE. When it comes to solvents, fluorinated, non‐cyclic, or a combination of these two tends to yield high CE (Figure 2a,e). Such patterns are consistent with some previous studies [36, 37, 38, 39] which identified linear fluorinated molecules as promising candidates for LMB electrolytes. That is partly because during SEI formation, the degradation of the fluorinated electrolytes often produces a LiF‐rich interphase, which facilitates ionic transport [40]. Additionally, a few electrolytes belonging to noncyclic or fluorinated groups displayed unexpectedly low CE. Upon careful examination of the data, these cases involved the use of unsuitable Li salts (e.g., LiClO4) or suboptimal salt‐to‐solvent ratios [41], both of which can undermine the advantageous properties of fluorinated linear molecules. Comparing formulations with the same Li salt across all solvents (Figure 2c,g) provides a detailed view of the impact of solvent groups on CE performance. C─O bonds corresponding to ether groups dominate the high CE region. This pattern is largely attributed to the advantages of LHCEs, which are commonly prepared using ether‐based solvents and diluents [34]. Conversely, carbonate electrolytes are in the low CE region, which is owing to the inherent instability of C═O bonds on Li metal [42]. With Li salt, the high CE territory is dominated by LiFSI, while LiPF6 is mostly present in the low CE region (Figure 2b,f). LiPF6 has been widely used in commercial LIBs, but less so for LMBs due to its limited ability to passivate metallic Li surfaces [43]. Another benefit of LiFSI is its high solubility in ether‐based solvents, making it an ideal salt for making HCE and LHCE [44]. At high salt concentration, the presence of abundant Li+–FSI− contact‐ion pairs (CIP) enhances anion participation in the Li+ solvation shell and promotes a robust SEI formation [41]. Interestingly, the use of salt mixtures, represented by green scattering points, can improve CE from the low (<95%) to the medium region (95%–99%), but this strategy struggles to achieve high CE (>99%). Furthermore, analysis of ionic conductivity data (Figure S7) revealed no correlation between conductivity and logarithmic CEs under either measurement method.

When isolated features are viewed in a monotonic relationship with logarithmic CE (i.e., LCE), a fairly high positive correlation between sF and LCE was observed (Figures S8–S10). On the other hand, sO, sC, pi, and boiling features show a negative correlation with LCE. The level of correlation can vary based on LCE, LCEi, and LCEs, but the general trend is preserved across all methods. Among the features studied, sO exhibits the strongest correlation with CE, while Li and aF show little to no correlation. The high correlation of sO can be explained by the important role of oxygen in determining the solvating power of the solvent, which alters the solvation shell of Li+ ion [29]. Therefore, reducing sO by reducing the number of oxygen in a solvent molecule creates a weakly solvating electrolyte. Such an electrolyte has stronger interactions between lithium ions and the salt anion, resulting in a stable anion‐derived SEI and higher CE [45]. Similarly, sC shows a negative correlation with CE. When data are visualized specifically on two classes, C═O groups (carbonate, ketone, and ester) and C─O groups (ether) (Figures S14–S17), the negative impact of π bonds on CE is primarily associated with the C═O class. No correlation between π bonds and CE is observed within the C─O class. Another interesting insight emerges from the boiling feature, which exhibits a negative correlation with CE. Moreover, Figure 3a reveals that a large area of high CE is present with low‐boiling electrolytes. To the best of our knowledge, this boiling insight has not been explicitly reported. This trend is consistent with the sC and sO discussed above, since raising sC or sO by increasing the number of carbons or oxygens generally raises boiling point through stronger London dispersion forces and, in the case of oxygen, additionally dipole‐dipole interactions.

Three ML models were trained on the cycle and Aurbach datasets to predict LCEi, LCEs, and LCE. Figure 3c shows that the model trained on LCEi as the target output delivers the poorest performance in terms of coefficient of determination (R 2) and mean absolute error (MAE). This is likely because the initial CE is more impacted by non‐electrolyte factors such as the nature of electrode materials, which are not originally encoded as training features. Meanwhile, models trained using LCEs from the cycle and LCE from the Aurbach method achieved higher accuracy, indicating that LCEs and LCE might be less affected by these non‐electrolyte factors [46]. Feature importance analysis revealed that sO, boiling, sF, and sC are the most influential features governing CE performance. In the correlation analysis plots (Figures S8–S10), we observed that sO also exhibits the strongest correlation with CE, as indicated by its highest Spearman coefficients. To further validate the feature‐importance ranking, we performed a SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis on both trained random‐forest models (Figures S19–S24). The SHAP‐based ranking confirms that sO, sF, and boiling are the three leading contributors in the cycle method, in close agreement with the Gini‐based ranking in Figure 3b. Notably, the SHAP interaction value between boiling and sC is only ∼9% of the boiling main effect, demonstrating that boiling carries explanatory power independently of the other compositional descriptors. Based on these feature importance analyses, sF, sC, and boiling features were selected for experimental validation and in‐depth study of their roles in determining CE performance.

Investigation of sC, sF, and Boiling Features

An LHCE system using LiFSI salt and DME solvent with a diluent varied to explore these three most important features was chosen for two reasons (Table S5). First, LHCEs are well recognized for their stability against lithium metal. Second, the diluents used here do not dissolve LiFSI salt. Thus, the influence of salt solubility is negligible, and any observable effects can be attributed to the chosen features. From the CE results obtained using the Aurbach method (Figure 4a–c), increasing sF leads to higher CE, whereas increasing sC reduces CE. When all other features remain the same values, the boiling feature becomes the determining factor, with lower boiling corresponding to higher CE. Moreover, when two electrolytes share all identical features, their CE values remain comparable, as observed for the boil_2 and boil_3 electrolytes. These results are consistent with the above‐mentioned correlation patterns derived from analyzing the CE datasets. CE acquired by the cycle method provided additional information on cyclability (Figure 4d–l). Namely, electrolytes with higher sF, lower sC, and lower boiling showed longer cyclability despite the average CE values remaining nearly unchanged. From Figure 5a–c, the cyclability observed in symmetrical cells shows a similar pattern to the CE values obtained from the Aurbach method, where higher sC and boiling features result in poorer cyclability and lower CE.

In terms of impedance (Figure S30), larger sC raises R b resistance while R ct remains mostly unchanged. A higher sF, on the one hand, increases R b, which at first seems inconsistent with the polarization data. On the other hand, R ct​ decreases significantly when moving from nonfluorinated to fluorinated diluents. Because the reduction in R ct outweighs the growth in R b, fluorinated electrolytes exhibit lower overall resistance, which helps explain the polarization pattern observed in the sF trend. It is worth emphasizing that merely relying on polarization and impedance data to interpret CE behavior may not be sufficient. This limitation is more evident in the boiling feature, where both R b and R ct fluctuate across diluents, making it difficult to establish a clear correlation with polarization, impedance, and CE. Therefore, an examination of solvation structure via Raman spectroscopy, discussed in the following section, can provide additional insights into how these features govern CE behavior.

In Raman spectra, the appearance of the aggregate (AGG) band and disappearance of free FSI− peaks suggest the formation of localized regions of highly concentrated salt. In the sF trend, Raman blue shift of the AGG band at around 750 cm−1 suggests stronger anion–anion correlations in fluorinated electrolytes compared to their nonfluorinated counterparts (Figure 5d). Stronger correlations promote the participation of FSI− in the solvation sheath, facilitating the formation of an anion‐derived SEI, which is beneficial to achieve high CE [47]. It is believed that the C─F bonds in fluorinated molecules lower the oxygen donor number, weakening Li+–solvent coordination and shifting the solvation shell toward FSI−‐dominated coordination. Additionally, C─F cleavage at the anode generates LiF that suppresses continuous solvent decomposition [48, 49]. In the case of sF_2 and sF_3, the AGG peak shows no clear difference between them, likely because a one‐fluorine difference between the two diluents used to make the electrolytes produces a minimal fluorination effect. In the sC trend, Raman spectra show that the anion–anion interaction remains unchanged, as evidenced by the absence of a noticeable AGG band shift across the trend (Figure 5e). Simultaneously, a red shift of the Li+‐DME coordination band was observed, indicating a weaker interaction between Li+ and solvent when reducing carbon chain length. Shorter carbon chains reduce the steric and electronic donor strength of the solvent, generating less voluminous organic SEI decomposition products [23]. In the boiling trend, Figure 5f reveals that boil_1 with lower boiling shows an increased Li+–FSI− association with a blue shift of the AGG band compared to boil_2 and boil_3. Both boil_2 and boil_3, with comparable boiling points, exhibit similar coordination behavior in their Raman spectra. This pattern of stronger anion–anion interaction and higher CE is similar to that observed in the sF trend, where higher sF led to both enhanced Li+–FSI− association and improved CE, which is consistent with the literature [50]. Importantly, variations in CE across all feature trends cannot be attributed to corrosion behavior (Figure S29), oxidative stability (Figure S31), or to differences in Li deposition morphology on Cu foils (Figure S32), as none of these showed significant differences.

A New Electrolyte Demonstrated for LMB Applications

Building on insights gained from the analysis of CE data and supporting CE values predicted by the model shown in Figure S33, a new S45 electrolyte was tested. The results show that S45 delivered 99.57% CE in Li||Cu cells and long cyclability in Li||NMC811 cells (Figure 6a–c). It enabled 80% capacity retention over 700 cycles and 250 cycles when cycled at 4.4 and 4.5 V, respectively. Meanwhile, the M47 benchmark electrolyte enabled 80% capacity retention only after 340 and 200 cycles at 4.4 and 4.5 V, respectively. In addition, S45 exhibited enhanced oxidative stability up to 4.8 V compared to 4.6 V for M47 (Figure 6d). This higher stability is consistent with a thinner accumulated SEI and thicker metallic Li leftover when cycled at 4.5 V in S45 (Figures S34 and S35), as severe electrolyte depletion in M47 causes the SEI to accumulate more rapidly, consuming more metallic Li. In the 1 9F NMR spectra (Figure 6e), S45 shows a downfield shift, implying reduced electron cloud density around FSI−. Conversely, 7Li NMR spectra (Figure 6f) show an upfield shift relative to M47, suggesting an increased electron cloud density around Li+. This observation leads to the hypothesis that more FSI− anions participate in the Li+ solvation shell, resulting in a more anion‐coordinated Li+ environment. Raman spectra (Figure 6g) support this hypothesis, showing that S45 exhibits a blue shift of the AGG band and a red shift of the Li+–solvent coordination band, indicating weakened Li+–solvent interactions. This more aggregated ionic microstructure also explains the lower ionic conductivity and higher viscosity of S45 (Figure 6h), owing to fewer free Li+ and FSI− charge carriers and reduced ion mobility.

To understand the SEI composition, the surface chemistry of the Li metal anode retrieved from Li||NMC811 full cells after 20 cycles was analyzed by XPS (Figure 7). In both electrolytes, F 1s spectrum shows C─F (∼688 eV) and Li─F (∼684.5 eV), where LiF peak accounts for a significant amount of the total F 1s signal, indicating that anion has substantially been reduced to form LiF‐rich SEI layer. This anion‐derived SEI can also be seen in the N 1s spectrum, where the N─SOx (∼400 eV) and reduced LixN (∼398 eV) peaks are present. This implies a contribution of sulfur‐ and nitrogen‐containing FSI‐derived decomposition products to the SEI, which can be attributed to the more aggregated structure with more FSI− anion present in the solvation shell. Furthermore, the Li 1s spectrum confirms the presence of LiF at ∼55 eV, and the LiF peak in the S45 electrolyte appears more pronounced than in the M47. Overall, the high CE, oxidative stability, and long cycling performance of S45 are consistent with a Li+ solvation shell dominated by FSI− anions, which promotes the formation of an anion‐derived and LiF‐rich SEI layer.

Conclusions

By integrating data mining with an emphasis on data quality, data preprocessing, and effective feature engineering, we uncovered insights into functional solvent groups and salt type, providing battery scientists with guidance on electrolyte design to achieve high CE. To capture the complexity of the electrolyte system, eight features encoding information on the degree of fluorination, carbon chain length, oxygen amount, boiling point, anion type, and salt concentration were successfully crafted. Analyzing features reveals that increasing sF, decreasing sC and sO, all lead to improved CE performance. One of the reasons underlying these behaviors lies in the solvation structure of Li+, where stronger anion–anion correlations and/or weaker Li+–solvent interactions may hold the key to enhancing CE performance. Moreover, when these features are fixed, boiling becomes the governing factor, where lower boiling produces higher CE. Finally, a new electrolyte demonstrated high CE of 99.57%, great oxidative stability of 4.8 V, and over 700 cycles at 80% capacity retention in Li‖NMC811 cells. This work highlights the importance of adopting data‐driven insights to accelerate electrolyte discovery, a process that traditionally demands substantial resources and human effort. More importantly, the established methodology is broadly applicable, enabling researchers to design electrolytes not only for LMBs but also for other battery chemistries.

Electrolyte and Cell Preparation

The entire process was done in an argon‐filled glovebox (MBraun, O2 < 10 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm). All electrolyte formulations are listed in the Table S5. NMC811 cathodes (4.0 mAh cm−2) were prepared with a weight ratio of 96% NMC811, 2% Super P carbon, and 2% polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder, cast onto an Al foil. The thick Li (450 µm), moderate Li (100 µm), and thin Li (50 µm, coated on Cu foil) were purchased from China Energy Lithium Co. The electrochemical measurements were performed in CR2032‐format coin cells (MTI Co.) using a polyethylene (20 µm) separator purchased from Asahi Hi‐Pore Co. With CE determination using the modified Aurbach method, Li||Cu cells were assembled with thick Li foil (diameter: 15.6 mm) and Cu foil (diameter: 19 mm). When CE is evaluated over long cyclability, a moderate Li foil (diameter: 15 mm) and a Cu foil (diameter: 16 mm) were coupled. Symmetrical Li||Li cells were assembled with two pieces of moderate Li foil (diameter: 15 mm). Li||NMC811 cells were assembled with a thin Li anode (diameter: 15.8 mm) and NMC811 cathode (diameter: 12.7 mm). An aluminum (Al)‐clad positive case with an additional piece of Al foil (diameter: 19.0 mm) was placed between the NMC811 electrode and the Al‐clad case. For Li||Cu and Li||NMC811 cells, 60 and 75 µL of electrolyte were used, respectively.

Electrochemical Tests

Ionic conductivities were measured using Bio‐Logic MCS 10 in a range of 20°C–60°C. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was scanned from OCV to 4.8 V at 0.05 mV s−1. Li||Al cells with a carbon‐coated aluminum (diameter: 14 mm) cathode and moderate Li (diameter: 15 mm) anode were assembled for this test. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was measured in a frequency range of 0.2 MHz to 20 mHz at 10 mV amplitude. EIS was measured after 4 mAh cm−2 Li was deposited onto Cu foil. Both LSV and EIS were performed on a SP300 Bio‐Logic instrument. Symmetrical cells were used to evaluate the polarization of electrolytes, where 4 mAh cm−2 Li was plated and stripped at a current of 0.5 mA cm−2. Galvanostatic charge/discharge tests were performed at 25°C. Cells were initially cycled at 0.1C for two cycles, followed by 0.1C charge and 0.33C discharge for subsequent cycles (1C = 200 mAh g−1), in a cutoff range of 2.8–4.4 V. A Chronoamperometry (CA) experiment to measure corrosion current was conducted in Li||Cu cells using an Arbin instrument. The voltage was applied from OCV to 15 mV versus Li+/Li and then held at 15 mV for 60 h while the response current was recorded.

CE Measurements

CEs were measured at 25°C using Neware battery cycler (A211‐BTS‐4S‐1U). For long‐term CE evaluation, plating and stripping processes were implemented at 0.5 mA cm−2 where Li is plated until 1 mAh cm−2 and then stripped until 1 V (Figure S1a). In the context of this study, this is called the cycle method. For average CE determination, modified Aurbach (or Aurbach for short) method was used as follows: (1) plating 4 (or 5) mAh cm−2 Li on Cu foil and stripping until 1 V, (2) plating 4 mAh cm−2 (Q T) and stripping 0.5 (or 1) mAh cm−2 (Q c) in the following cycle, (3) plating and stripping at the same amount of Q c 10 times before stripping until the remaining Li reservoir is fully stripped to 1 V. A stripped capacity (Q s) is measured in this cycle. A current of 0.5 mA cm−2 was applied throughout all steps. The entire process was schematically described in Figure S1b. The average CE was calculated using the following equation: CE = (nQ c + Q s)/(nQ c + Q T). For the measurement using the Aurbach method, three cells were assembled to acquire the average CE.

Characterizations

All samples were prepared in an argon‐filled glovebox. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) was collected on a 11.74T magnet (7Li 194.3 MHz, 19F 470.47 MHz) on a Bruker Avance III console equipped with a BBFO iProbe. Samples were loaded into coaxial tubes, where the deuterated acetonitrile lock solvent was filled in the outer tube, and the electrolyte was filled in the insert. The chemical shift in ppm is referenced to the expected room temperature chemical shift of the residual proton for the deuterated lock solvent. 7Li and 19F spectra were measured with a 60 and 1 s delay, and 3 and 64 scans, respectively. Raman spectra were obtained using a Renishaw inVia Confocal Raman Microscope, equipped with a 532 nm laser, a 50x objective, and a 1200 L/mm grating. Each spectrum was measured with five 30‐s accumulations and 10% of the maximal laser power used, covering a range from 700 to 900 cm−1. A polished and tightly closed quartz cuvette with a path length of 1 mm was filled with the liquid electrolyte. Collected raw spectra were processed using the asymmetric least squares (ALS) smoothing method to subtract the baseline, followed by a normalization process. Top‐view SEM images were captured on a ThermoFisher Scientific SEM at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Cross‐sectional SEM images were captured using a JEOL SEM at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. XPS was performed on the Li‐metal electrode obtained from Li||NMC811 cells after 20 cycles. Cells were disassembled in an argon‐filled glovebox, and lithium electrodes were rinsed with anhydrous DME and then dried under vacuum. Spectra were acquired with a monochromatic Al Kα source (1486.6 eV) on a Kratos Axis Supra+ spectrometer. X‐ray spot size was on the order of 700 × 300 µm. Pressure in the analytical chamber during spectral acquisition was below 5 × 10−9 Torr. Pass energy for the high‐resolution spectra was 20 eV. Charge correction was performed by referencing the C─C/C─H component of the C 1s spectrum to 285.0 eV. High‐resolution F 1s, N 1s, and Li 1s spectra were fitted in the Kratos ESCApe software using Shirley background subtraction and Gaussian–Lorentzian peak shapes.

Construction of the Dataset

Advanced data extraction tools and large language models were leveraged to accelerate the extraction process, but most of the time, data was manually extracted, as such tools can not handle data extraction from complicated plots (Note SA). During data collection, a checklist (Table S2) was used to exclude articles that did not meet the data requirements. The entire dataset was split into two subdatasets based on the cycle and Aurbach methods. In the Aurbach method, the average CE, denoted as CE, was extracted. In the cycle method, initial and stabilized CE, denoted as CEi and CEs, respectively, were extracted from the cycling plot. A typical example of extraction was demonstrated in Figure S2.

Machine Learning

Eight features were engineered for insight extraction and model training, summarized in Table 1. To facilitate calculations of moles of molecules, the total mass of electrolytes is prenormalized to be 100 g. The electrolyte moles of Li+, fluorine, oxygen, carbon, and pi bond were calculated based on the assumption of linear mixing where the bulk moles are the summation of moles of each component. Their mathematical representation is as n bulk = ∑i ni​. Where n bulk is the total moles of electrolyte, n i is the moles of the i‐th species. For the boiling feature, it is computed as the mole‐weighted geometric mean of the normal boiling points of the liquid components only, using commercial values of each pure component as Boilingbulk = ∏I (T b,i) xi . Where T b,i is the normal boiling point of liquid component i in °C and x i is its mole fraction. Before model training, CE was logarithmically transformed using LCE = −log10(1 – CE/100). After the transformation, CE distribution spreads across the length scale, making it easier to capture patterns from data visualization (Figure S2). To predict CE, a random forest (RF) model was constructed by calling the RandomForestRegressor function from scikit‐learn. All model parameters are listed in Table S6. Input data were standardized to the range [0, 1] before training. 80% and 20% of the data were used for training and testing, respectively.
