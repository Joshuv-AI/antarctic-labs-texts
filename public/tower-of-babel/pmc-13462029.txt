Comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of MEX3C in human tumors
Authors: Xuezhong Zhang, Wenqiang Chen, Yan Wang, Chengjie Guo, Meilin Gao, Tingting Chu, Xuebin Zhang, Tonggang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMEX3C, an RNA-binding protein implicated in cancer progression, was analyzed for its expression, genetic alterations, prognostic value, immune associations, and pathway enrichment across various cancers. Using TCGA and GTEx datasets, MEX3C expression was found elevated in multiple cancers, including BRCA, HNSC, and LUSC, while reduced in ACC and TGCT. High MEX3C expression correlated with poor overall survival (OS) in LIHC and MESO, and disease-specific survival (DSS) in PAAD and MESO, highlighting its prognostic potential. Genetic analysis revealed frequent “deep deletions” and missense mutations, particularly in PAAD and STAD. Strong associations between MEX3C and RNA modification-related genes (e.g., YTHDF3, DNMT3A) suggest its regulation via RNA modifications. Immune infiltration analysis demonstrated MEX3C’s correlation with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and immune checkpoints such as CD276 and VEGFA, indicating its role in immune modulation. Additionally, MEX3C expression showed cancer-type-specific correlations with tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI), linking it to genomic instability. Enrichment analysis identified pathways such as RNA transport, spliceosome function, and endocytosis as significantly associated with MEX3C-related genes. These findings establish MEX3C as a pivotal biomarker and potential therapeutic target, warranting further investigation into its mechanistic roles in cancer progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05367-5.Keywords: MEX3C, Pan-cancer analysis, Prognostic biomarker, Tumor microenvironment, Immune infiltration

Introduction

Recent advances in bioinformatics, high-throughput sequencing, and multi-omics integration have substantially reshaped cancer research by enabling systematic investigation of the molecular mechanisms underlying tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic response across diverse cancer types [1–7]. Pan-cancer analysis, in particular, provides a broader and more integrated perspective than single-cancer studies by identifying both shared and tumor-specific molecular alterations across malignancies. By integrating large-scale transcriptomic, genomic, epigenomic, proteomic, and clinical datasets from resources such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project, and other public databases, pan-cancer studies can uncover common oncogenic mechanisms, classify molecular subtypes, identify universal biomarkers, and reveal therapeutic targets with potential relevance across multiple tumor types. For example, a pan-cancer analysis of interleukin-17 family members demonstrated their associations with prognosis, immune infiltration, tumor microenvironment characteristics, immune checkpoint inhibitor-related indicators, and drug sensitivity, supporting the value of pan-cancer approaches in biomarker discovery and immunotherapy stratification [8]. Therefore, pan-cancer research represents an important strategy for understanding tumor heterogeneity while also identifying conserved mechanisms that may be exploited for precision oncology.

The tumor microenvironment (TME), especially the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), is now recognized as a key determinant of tumor progression, immune escape, metastatic potential, and response to immunotherapy. The TIME is composed of diverse immune and stromal components, including T cells, B cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), endothelial cells, immune checkpoint molecules, chemokines, cytokines, and extracellular matrix components. Recent integrative studies have shown that molecular subtyping based on immune-, metabolism-, and regulated cell death-related signatures can effectively distinguish patient groups with different prognoses, immune infiltration patterns, and therapeutic sensitivities [9–12]. For example, disulfidptosis-related gene-based subtyping in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) identified distinct molecular subtypes with different survival outcomes and immune profiles, while a low-density lipoprotein-related gene signature based on single-cell and bulk transcriptomic analyses revealed close associations among metabolic regulation, cellular communication within the TME, immune surveillance, and clinical prognosis [13, 14]. In parallel, newly developed cloud-based genomic and multi-omics platforms have improved the accessibility and efficiency of large-scale genome-wide association studies, Mendelian randomization, drug target validation, and multi-omics integration, further supporting the application of computational approaches in precision cancer research [15].

The human MEX3 family, a conserved group of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), comprises four members, MEX3A-D, which encode phosphorylated proteins with distinct expression patterns [16–18]. Each MEX3 protein contains two K homology (KH) RNA-binding domains and a C-terminal RING finger domain with putative ubiquitin E3 ligase activity [17, 19]. Through these structural features, MEX3 proteins are involved in post-transcriptional regulation, including RNA stability, localization, translation, and protein ubiquitination. Among these family members, MEX3C is located on chromosome 18q21.2 and has attracted increasing attention because of its potential involvement in tumor biology. Dysregulated MEX3C expression has been reported in several malignancies, including BLCA [20], CESC [21], LUAD [22], LIHC [23, 24], OS [25] and OV [26]. Altered MEX3C expression has also been associated with poor prognosis, aggressive clinicopathological features, and tumor-promoting phenotypes in specific cancer contexts. However, despite these findings, the expression pattern, prognostic significance, genomic alteration landscape, association with RNA modification regulators, and immunological relevance of MEX3C across human cancers remain insufficiently characterized.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis to investigate the biological and clinical significance of MEX3C. We systematically evaluated MEX3C expression, associations with tumor stage and patient survival, genetic alterations, RNA modification-related genes, immune cell infiltration, immune checkpoint and immune modulator genes, tumor mutational burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI), and functional enrichment profiles across multiple cancer types. Given the emerging importance of pan-cancer research and TME-based molecular subtyping in precision oncology, this study aimed to clarify whether MEX3C may serve as a pan-cancer biomarker linked to tumor progression, immune regulation, and potential therapeutic vulnerability.

Materials and methods

Gene expression analysis

RNA-seq data for ENSG00000176624 (MEX3C), expressed as transcripts per million (TPM), were obtained from the UCSC Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/) for TCGA and GTEx datasets, which were uniformly processed using the Toil pipeline [27]. MEX3C expression was evaluated across multiple sample types, including tumour and normal tissues. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.1 and the ggplot2 package version 3.4.4.

Expression data from the TCGA_GTEx dataset were analysed using a threshold of |log2 fold change (log2FC)| ≥ 1, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.01. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used for unpaired comparisons, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied where appropriate. Boxplots were generated using ggplot2. Violin plots showing MEX3C expression across different cancer stages were generated using GEPIA2 (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn) [28]. Protein expression analysis was conducted using the UALCAN portal (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis-prot.html), an interactive platform for cancer omics analysis, based on data from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) [29].

Survival prognosis analysis

The TCGA Pan-Cancer dataset was retrieved from UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/) [27]. MEX3C expression profiles and corresponding survival information were extracted. Samples with follow-up durations of less than 30 days were excluded from the analysis. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed using the survival package version 3.3.1 in R. Heatmaps and forest plots were generated using ggplot2 version 3.4.4 to visualize the results. Survival differences were assessed using the log-rank test, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Genetic alteration analysis

Genetic alteration data, including mutation frequency, alteration type, site-specific mutations, and copy number alterations (CNAs), were obtained from cBioPortal (http://www.cbioportal.org/) using TCGA datasets across multiple cancer types [30]. Overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) were compared between tumors with and without MEX3C alterations using the “Comparison” module in cBioPortal.

RNA modification, immune checkpoints, and immune moderator genes of MEX3C

A standardized pan-cancer dataset was downloaded from UCSC Xena [27]. This dataset included expression data for ENSG00000176624 (MEX3C) and 44 marker genes associated with RNA modifications, including m1A, m5C, and m6A [31]. Expression data for 150 immune moderator genes involved in five immune-related pathways, namely chemokine, receptor, MHC, immunoinhibitory, and immunostimulatory pathways, as well as 60 immune checkpoint genes, were also obtained [32]. Log2-transformed expression values were analyzed using the corrplot package version 0.90 and psych package version 2.1.6 in R. Heatmaps illustrating the correlations between MEX3C expression and RNA modification-, immune checkpoint-, and immune moderator-related genes were generated using ggplot2 version 3.4.4.

Immune infiltration analysis

The associations between MEX3C expression and immune cell infiltration were assessed using TIMER2 [33]. Heatmaps and scatter plots were used to visualize these correlations. Immune infiltration levels were estimated using multiple computational algorithms, including TIMER, EPIC, XCELL, MCPCOUNTER, CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, and CIBERSORT.

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI)

MEX3C expression data were extracted from the TCGA Pan-Cancer dataset via UCSC Xena. Tumour mutational burden (TMB) data were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal and processed using MuTect2 [34]. TMB scores were calculated using the maftools package (version 2.8.05). Microsatellite instability (MSI) scores were obtained from a prior study [35]. Correlations between MEX3C expression and TMB or MSI were analyzed in R.

MEX3C-related gene enrichment analysis

Proteins interacting with MEX3C were identified using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/), with a minimum required interaction score of 0.150 and a maximum of to 50 interactors [36]. The top 100 genes correlated with MEX3C in TCGA datasets were identified using GEPIA2 [28]. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess relationships between MEX3C and selected genes, with a dot plot of mean log2TPM values generated. Heatmaps were produced using the “Gene_Corr” module in TIMER2. Partial correlation coefficients and corresponding P values were calculated using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis adjusted for tumour purity. Functional enrichment analyses, including KEGG [37] and Gene Ontology (GO) [38], were performed using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.4.4). Circular charts illustrating the results were created with ggplot2 (version 3.4.4). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25.

Results

Gene expression analysis data

MEX3C expression was evaluated using TCGA and GTEx datasets obtained from the UCSC database by comparing tumour tissues with their corresponding normal counterparts. Comprehensive statistical analyses were performed, and the results summarizing MEX3C expression across different cancer types are provided in Supplementary Table 1. As shown in Fig. 1A, MEX3C expression was significantly upregulated in multiple cancer types, including BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, DLBC, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LAML, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, PAAD, PRAD, READ, SKCM, STAD, THCA, and THYM. In contrast, MEX3C expression was significantly downregulated in ACC and TGCT.

Protein expression analysis based on the CPTAC dataset showed that MEX3C protein levels were increased in BRCA, HNSC, and LUSC compared with those in normal tissues (Fig. 1B). These findings suggest that MEX3C may be involved in cancer progression across diverse tumor types.

Survival prognosis analysis

The prognostic significance of MEX3C expression was assessed by stratifying patients into high- and low-expression groups based on TCGA data. Detailed associations between MEX3C expression and overall survival (OS) across different cancer types are provided in Supplementary Table 2. As shown in Fig. 2A, high MEX3C expression was significantly associated with poorer OS in LIHC (P = 0.0008) and MESO (P = 0.0023). In contrast, low MEX3C expression was associated with worse OS in KIRC (P = 0.0269).

Similarly, the association between MEX3C expression and disease-specific survival (DSS) is summarized in Supplementary Table 3. High MEX3C expression was associated with poorer DSS in LIHC (P = 0.0014), MESO (P = 0.0071), and PAAD (P = 0.0435), whereas reduced MEX3C expression was associated with worse DSS in COAD (P = 0.0441) (Fig. 2B). These findings indicate that MEX3C may serve as a prognostic biomarker for OS and DSS in multiple cancer types.

Genetic alteration analysis

The frequency and types of genetic alterations in the MEX3C gene across different cancer types were examined using TCGA data. As shown in Fig. 3A, the highest alteration frequency was observed in PAAD (9.24%), where deep deletion was the predominant alteration type. This was followed by STAD (5.23%), ESCA (4.95%), HNSC (3.25%), and COAD (3.2%). In other cancers, including CHOL, SKCM, and KIRC, mutation was the predominant alteration type, whereas amplification was the only alteration detected in DLBC and PCPG. MESO exhibited deep deletion exclusively.

RNA modification

Accumulating evidence indicates that RNA modifications play critical roles in cancer progression, including angiogenesis [27, 28]. Detailed correlation coefficients and P values for the associations between MEX3C and RNA modification-related genes across different tumour types are summarized in Supplementary Table 4. As shown in Fig. 4, MEX3C expression was positively correlated with multiple RNA modification-related genes, particularly those involved in m1A, m5C, and m6A modifications across most tumor types.

RNA Modification Analysis of MEX3C Across Different Tumors. A strong positive correlation exists between MEX3C and several RNA modification-related genes, particularly those involved in m1A, m5C, and m6A modifications

MEX3C showed significant positive associations with several enzymes involved in RNA modification. For m1A modification, these included YTHDF3, YTHDC1, YTHDF1, YTHDF2, and ALKBH1. For m5C modification, the associated genes included DNMT3A, TRDMT1, NSUN3, NSUN4, and TET2. For m6A modification, positive associations were observed with WTAP, RBM15, METTL14, CBLL1, ZC3H13, YTHDC1, YTHDF3, HNRNPA2B1, YTHDF1, YTHDF2, YTHDC2, and FMR1. These findings suggest a potential link between MEX3C expression and RNA modification pathways in cancer.

To further validate the associations of MEX3C with RNA modification enzymes, including YTHDF3 and DNMT3A, and the immune checkpoint molecule CD276, we downloaded LIHC, PAAD, and READ datasets from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC) database and performed correlation analyses. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1A, MEX3C expression was significantly and positively correlated with CD276 (R = 0.320), YTHDF3 (R = 0.637), and DNMT3A (R = 0.536) in LIHC. Similarly, in PAAD, MEX3C expression showed significant positive correlations with CD276 (R = 0.209), YTHDF3 (R = 0.233), and DNMT3A (R = 0.378) (Supplementary Fig. 1B). In READ, MEX3C expression was also significantly and positively associated with CD276 (R = 0.281), YTHDF3 (R = 0.453), and DNMT3A (R = 0.740) (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Together, these validation analyses based on independent ICGC datasets further support the positive associations between MEX3C and RNA modification- and immune checkpoint-related genes across multiple cancer types.

Immune infiltration analysis

Tumor-infiltrating immune cells are key components of the tumour microenvironment (TME) and play important roles in tumour development and progression. Using multiple computational algorithms, including TIMER, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, TIDE, xCell, MCP-counter, quanTIseq, and EPIC, we assessed the relationship between immune cell infiltration and MEX3C expression across TCGA cancer types. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, MEX3C expression was positively correlated with cancer-associated fibroblast infiltration in ESCA, HNSC, HNSC-HPV−, and PAAD. These results highlight the complex interactions between MEX3C and the TME across different cancer types.

Enrichment analysis of MEX3C-related genes

To investigate the functional role of MEX3C in cancer, we identified MEX3C-binding proteins and genes significantly correlated with MEX3C expression. Using the GEPIA2 platform, the top 100 genes most strongly correlated with MEX3C expression were retrieved. A heatmap showing the top six correlated genes, namely BAG5, BRAP, FAM104A, NSUN4, PIAS2, and RNF138, across different cancer types is presented in Fig. 9A. Significant positive correlations were also observed between MEX3C and genes including FAM104A, ISG20L2, MED26, ODF2, and TTLL5 (P < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 9B. Protein-protein interaction analysis using the STRING database identified 50 proteins that interact with MEX3C (Fig. 9C). To further explore the functional implications of these interactions, Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed. GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in terms including “nucleobase-containing compound catabolic process”, “RNA localization”, “ribonucleoprotein granule”, and “ATP hydrolysis activity”, suggesting that these biological processes may contribute to MEX3C-mediated tumorigenesis. KEGG pathway analysis identified several pathways associated with MEX3C, including RNA transport, spliceosome, Huntington disease, and endocytosis pathways (Fig. 9D and E). Together, these results provide insight into the multifaceted role of MEX3C in cancer biology, emphasizing its potential involvement in genomic instability, molecular interactions, and key cellular pathways related to tumour progression.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a systematic pan-cancer analysis of MEX3C and identified its potential clinical and biological relevance across multiple tumor types. Our results showed that MEX3C expression varied substantially among cancers and was significantly associated with tumor stage, survival outcomes, genetic alterations, RNA modification-related regulators, immune infiltration, immune checkpoints, immune modulators, TMB, MSI, and cancer-related pathways. Together, these findings suggest that MEX3C may participate in tumor progression through complex and context-dependent mechanisms involving post-transcriptional regulation, genomic instability, RNA modification, and remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment.

A major strength of the present study is its pan-cancer design. Compared with analyses restricted to a single tumor type, pan-cancer research enables the identification of molecular events that are conserved across cancers as well as those that are highly context dependent. This broader perspective is particularly important for RNA-binding proteins such as MEX3C, whose functions may differ according to tissue origin, tumor lineage, genetic background, and immune context. Previous pan-cancer studies have demonstrated that integrated analyses across multiple tumor types can reveal clinically meaningful biomarkers associated with prognosis, TME features, immune checkpoint inhibitor response, and drug sensitivity [8]. Consistent with this concept, our study shows that MEX3C is not uniformly associated with a single biological phenotype across all cancers. Instead, MEX3C appears to have tumor-specific associations with prognosis, immune infiltration, genomic instability, and immune regulatory pathways. These findings support the importance of pan-cancer approaches for identifying both universal and cancer-specific mechanisms relevant to biomarker development and therapeutic targeting.

We first observed differential expression of MEX3C across cancer types. Elevated MEX3C expression was detected in several tumors, including BRCA, CESC, COAD, and LIHC, suggesting that MEX3C may contribute to malignant progression in these cancers. Moreover, MEX3C expression was significantly correlated with tumor stage in KIRC, LIHC, and THCA, indicating a possible association between increased MEX3C expression and advanced disease. These observations are consistent with previous evidence that RNA-binding proteins can regulate oncogenic transcripts and influence cancer-related processes such as proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and therapy resistance [39, 40]. Given the ability of MEX3C to bind RNA and potentially regulate ubiquitination, aberrant MEX3C expression may affect multiple levels of gene expression control in cancer cells [41]. However, the direction and magnitude of its effect appear to be cancer specific, as reduced MEX3C expression was also observed in tumors such as ACC and TGCT. These findings highlight the need to interpret MEX3C function within specific tissue and molecular contexts.

Survival analyses further supported the prognostic relevance of MEX3C. High MEX3C expression was associated with poorer overall survival and disease-specific survival in LIHC, MESO, and PAAD, suggesting a potential oncogenic role in these cancers. In contrast, lower MEX3C expression was associated with worse outcomes in KIRC and COAD, indicating that MEX3C may also have protective or tumor-suppressive associations in certain malignancies. This dual prognostic pattern is consistent with the context-dependent roles of many RNA-binding proteins, which may act as oncogenes or tumor suppressors depending on tumor type, molecular subtype, genetic background, and the surrounding microenvironment [42]. Therefore, MEX3C should not be considered a uniformly oncogenic or tumor-suppressive factor across all cancers. Instead, its biological function likely depends on the downstream transcripts, protein partners, and signaling pathways active in each tumor context.

We also found that although MEX3C expression was higher in KIRC tissues than in normal tissues, lower MEX3C expression was associated with poorer overall survival, and MEX3C expression gradually decreased with increasing tumor stage. This pattern suggests that MEX3C may not act as a conventional oncogene in KIRC, but may instead exert a context-dependent tumor-suppressive role. In contrast, in cancer types such as LIHC and MESO, high MEX3C expression was generally associated with unfavorable prognosis, indicating a more oncogene-like function. These divergent roles across cancer types may partly reflect differences in genetic background, particularly the unique VHL mutation landscape of KIRC. More than 80% of KIRC cases harbor VHL inactivation, leading to constitutive activation of hypoxia-inducible factors, especially HIF-2α, which is a central driver of renal carcinogenesis [43]. As an RNA-binding protein, MEX3C may potentially regulate the stability or translational efficiency of mRNAs encoded by HIF downstream target genes, such as VEGF, PDGFB, GLUT1, and CA9 [22, 44]. In VHL-mutant KIRC cells, preserved MEX3C expression may help restrain excessive HIF pathway activation, thereby delaying tumor progression [45]. By contrast, VHL mutations are rare in LIHC, where HIF signaling is more commonly induced transiently by the hypoxic tumor microenvironment [46]. In this setting, the potential protective effect associated with MEX3C expression may be absent. Instead, MEX3C may promote tumor progression through alternative mechanisms, such as stabilizing pro-proliferative transcripts, including c-Myc or β-catenin. Previous studies have shown that MEX3C can directly bind to the 3′untranslated region of c-Myc mRNA and regulate its expression, while c-Myc is a well-established oncogenic driver in hepatocellular carcinoma [24, 47]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the biological role of MEX3C should be interpreted in a cancer-type-specific manner, with careful consideration of genetic background, especially VHL mutation status, as well as tumor microenvironmental features. Future studies using VHL-deficient and VHL-restored KIRC cell models, together with stromal or immune cell co-culture systems, are needed to directly validate whether MEX3C regulates HIF-dependent transcripts and thereby influences KIRC progression and microenvironmental remodeling.

Genomic alteration analysis revealed a heterogeneous alteration pattern of MEX3C across cancer types. PAAD showed the highest alteration frequency, predominantly characterized by deep deletion. Other tumors, including STAD, ESCA, and HNSC, also exhibited notable alteration frequencies, mainly involving deep deletion. These findings suggest that loss of MEX3C may be biologically relevant in certain cancers and may affect tumor behavior by impairing RNA regulatory or ubiquitination-related functions [48]. In contrast, mutations were the dominant alteration type in CHOL, SKCM, and KIRC, whereas amplification was observed mainly in DLBC and PCPG. The presence of distinct alteration types across cancers supports a context-dependent role of MEX3C in tumor biology. In particular, missense mutations such as R336H/S in UCEC may alter RNA-binding capacity, protein stability, or interactions with regulatory partners. Functional studies are needed to determine whether these alterations are passenger events or drivers of tumor progression.

RNA modifications, including m1A, m5C, and m6A, are important post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms that influence RNA stability, splicing, translation, and degradation, thereby contributing to cancer progression [49, 50]. Our analysis demonstrated broad correlations between MEX3C expression and RNA modification-related genes across multiple tumor types. MEX3C was positively associated with several m1A-related regulators, including YTHDF3, YTHDC1, and ALKBH1, as well as m5C-related enzymes such as DNMT3A and NSUN4. MEX3C also showed correlations with m6A-related regulators, including WTAP, RBM15, and METTL14. These associations suggest that MEX3C may be involved in RNA modification-mediated regulatory networks. As an RNA-binding protein, MEX3C may preferentially interact with modified transcripts or cooperate with RNA modification enzymes to regulate transcript fate. Conversely, RNA modifications may also influence MEX3C expression or activity. Although the present results are correlative, they provide a rationale for future mechanistic studies to determine whether MEX3C directly participates in m1A-, m5C-, or m6A-dependent RNA regulation in cancer.

The tumor immune microenvironment has a major influence on cancer progression, immune escape, and response to immune checkpoint inhibitors [51]. In our study, MEX3C expression was significantly associated with immune cell infiltration, particularly CAF infiltration in ESCA, HNSC, and PAAD. CAFs are critical stromal components that promote extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenesis, immune suppression, and resistance to therapy [52–54]. The positive association between MEX3C and CAF infiltration suggests that MEX3C may be involved in stromal remodeling or tumor-stromal communication. This finding is particularly relevant in light of recent molecular subtyping studies showing that tumor-intrinsic molecular programs can stratify patients into groups with distinct immune and stromal features. For instance, disulfidptosis-related molecular subtyping in LUAD identified subtypes with different immune profiles and demonstrated enrichment of disulfidptosis-related genes in myeloid cells [13]. Similarly, LDL-related gene analysis using single-cell transcriptomics showed that metabolic gene programs were linked to cell-cell communication, immune surveillance, prognosis, and drug sensitivity in LUAD [14]. These studies support the concept that gene-specific regulatory networks can shape the TME and influence clinical outcome. Our findings extend this concept to MEX3C and suggest that MEX3C may contribute to TME heterogeneity across cancer types.

When considering immunotherapy, the TME should not be viewed as a passive background but rather as an active regulator of therapeutic efficacy and resistance. Immune checkpoint blockade depends on a coordinated antitumor immune response involving antigen presentation, dendritic cell activation, cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, cytokine signaling, and the balance between effector and suppressive immune populations. However, this response can be weakened by immunosuppressive components of the TME, including CAFs, tumor-associated macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, regulatory T cells, abnormal vasculature, hypoxia, extracellular matrix deposition, and suppressive cytokines. These components can limit immune cell trafficking, impair antigen presentation, induce T-cell exhaustion, and promote immune escape. Recent studies further emphasize that immune-related biomarkers and machine learning-based models can help identify tumor-specific immune signatures relevant to prognosis and immune-targeted treatment. For example, a machine learning-based study in KIRC identified key genes associated with immune infiltration, immune checkpoint expression, prognosis, and potential immune-targeted treatment [55]. In HNSC, single-cell and multi-omics analyses identified PAK2 as a key regulator associated with programmed cell death, cancer stemness, and the immune microenvironment, further supporting the value of integrated computational approaches for dissecting tumor-immune interactions [56]. These findings are consistent with our observation that MEX3C is linked to immune infiltration and immune regulatory molecules across multiple cancers.

Immune checkpoints and immune modulator genes play pivotal roles in shaping the immune response within the TME [57]. We also found significant associations between MEX3C and immune checkpoint or immune regulatory genes. MEX3C was positively correlated with CD276 and VEGFA in multiple cancers. CD276 is an immune checkpoint molecule involved in tumor immune evasion, whereas VEGFA promotes angiogenesis and can contribute to immunosuppressive TME formation [58]. The correlation between MEX3C and VEGFA, particularly in LIHC, suggests that MEX3C may be linked to angiogenesis-associated immune suppression in specific tumors. In addition, MEX3C showed positive correlations with immune stimulatory genes such as HMGB1, ENTPD1, TLR4, TNFSF4, BTN3A1, and BTN3A2. HMGB1 can act as a damage-associated molecular pattern and activate immune signaling through TLR4, whereas ENTPD1 and TNFSF4 are involved in T-cell regulation [59]. These findings suggest that MEX3C may participate in both immunosuppressive and immune-activating pathways. Such a dual association may reflect a complex immune state in which localized immune activation coexists with immune escape mechanisms.

Cytokines and chemokines are also central mediators of TME–immunotherapy interactions. Pro-inflammatory cytokines can promote dendritic cell maturation, antigen presentation, T-cell priming, and effector immune responses, whereas immunosuppressive cytokines can promote T-cell dysfunction, macrophage polarization, CAF activation, and tumor immune escape. Chemokines regulate the recruitment and spatial distribution of immune cells, thereby shaping whether tumors display an immune-inflamed, immune-excluded, or immune-desert phenotype [57]. In the present study, the positive correlation between MEX3C and CX3CL1 suggests a potential role in chemokine-mediated immune cell recruitment, whereas negative correlations with CXCR5 in several tumors may indicate effects on B-cell or follicular helper T-cell trafficking. MEX3C was also associated with TAPBP, a key factor in MHC class I antigen presentation, implying a possible link between MEX3C and tumor immune recognition. In addition, positive correlations with immunoinhibitory molecules such as TGFBR1, KDR, CD160, and IL10RB, together with correlations with immunostimulatory genes such as NT5E, IL6R, TNFSF4, and MICB, further support the view that MEX3C may regulate immune balance in a tumor-specific manner.

These findings have potential implications for immunotherapy. Tumors with high MEX3C expression and strong associations with CAFs, VEGFA, CD276, TGFBR1, or IL10RB may possess an immunosuppressive microenvironment that limits the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade. In such tumors, MEX3C may be explored as part of a broader biomarker panel for identifying immune-excluded or immune-suppressed phenotypes. Conversely, the association of MEX3C with immune stimulatory molecules and antigen-presentation-related genes suggests that MEX3C may also reflect immune-active states in certain tumor types. Therefore, MEX3C may not simply indicate immune suppression or activation; rather, it may mark a complex immune regulatory network whose biological meaning depends on tumor type, immune subtype, and cytokine context. Recent therapeutic studies also support the importance of remodeling the TME to improve immunotherapy response. For example, antibody–drug conjugates have been proposed as immuno-oncology agents capable of inducing immunogenic cell death or pyroptosis, thereby promoting dendritic cell activation, T-cell infiltration, and conversion of immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors [60]. These observations suggest that targeting tumor-intrinsic pathways together with TME-modulating strategies may help overcome immune resistance.

Although our analysis identified significant correlations between MEX3C expression, CAF infiltration, and immune checkpoint-related genes, these associations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of direct causality. Given that MEX3C is an intracellular RNA-binding protein and ubiquitin E3 ligase, it is unlikely to directly recruit CAFs or directly induce surface checkpoint expression. Instead, MEX3C may influence the tumor microenvironment indirectly through tumor-cell-intrinsic regulatory programs. First, as an RNA-binding protein, MEX3C may regulate the stability or translation of transcripts encoding secreted cytokines, chemokines, angiogenic factors, or upstream regulators of these molecules. Previous evidence that MEX3C can bind target transcripts and regulate mRNA decay, including immune-relevant transcripts such as HLA-A, supports this possibility [61]. Such post-transcriptional regulation may affect TGF-β-, IL-6-, CCL2-, CXCL12-, or VEGFA-related signaling, thereby contributing to CAF activation, extracellular matrix remodeling, angiogenesis, and immune suppression. Second, the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of MEX3C may promote TME remodeling by degrading intracellular repressors of stromal-activating pathways. For example, MEX3C has been reported to induce ubiquitination-dependent degradation of RUNX3, a tumor suppressor functionally linked to TGF-β-related regulatory programs [22]. Third, the observed correlations between MEX3C and checkpoint-related genes such as CD276 and VEGFA may reflect indirect regulation through oncogenic pathways, including mTORC1- and NF-κB/VEGFA-associated signaling. Therefore, MEX3C may bridge intracellular RNA/protein regulation and extracellular TME phenotypes through post-transcriptional control of secreted mediators, ubiquitin-mediated degradation of stromal pathway repressors, and indirect modulation of immune checkpoint-associated signaling. However, these mechanisms remain speculative and require validation using RNA immunoprecipitation or CLIP-seq, RNA stability assays, ubiquitination assays, cytokine profiling, and tumor cell-CAF co-culture experiments.

TMB and MSI are widely used indicators of genomic instability and potential immunotherapy responsiveness [62, 63]. We observed both positive and negative correlations between MEX3C expression and TMB across different cancer types. Positive correlations in GBM, COAD, LAML, and STAD suggest that high MEX3C expression may be associated with increased mutational burden or genomic instability in these tumors. In contrast, negative correlations in BRCA, ESCA, KIRP, KIRC, and LIHC may indicate that MEX3C is linked to lower mutation accumulation or different DNA repair contexts. Similarly, MEX3C expression showed significant correlations with MSI in several cancers. Positive correlations in COAD and STAD may suggest a relationship between MEX3C and mismatch repair deficiency-associated molecular features, whereas negative correlations in LGG, PRAD, HNSC, and DLBC may reflect alternative mechanisms of genomic regulation. These findings support a nuanced role of MEX3C in genomic instability and immune responsiveness and suggest that its value as a biomarker may depend on tumor type and molecular subtype.

Functional enrichment analysis provided additional insight into the potential biological roles of MEX3C. Genes correlated with MEX3C, including BAG5, BRAP, FAM104A, NSUN4, PIAS2, and RNF138, are involved in processes such as protein stability, RNA metabolism, DNA repair, and cellular stress responses [64, 65]. Protein-protein interaction analysis also identified MEX3C-associated proteins involved in RNA metabolism, cellular trafficking, and immune regulation. GO enrichment analysis revealed significant terms related to nucleobase-containing compound catabolic processes, RNA localization, ribonucleoprotein granules, and ATP hydrolysis activity [66]. KEGG pathway analysis further indicated enrichment in RNA transport, spliceosome, Huntington disease, and endocytosis pathways. These pathways are closely related to post-transcriptional regulation, protein homeostasis, and cellular adaptation under stress. Given that MEX3C contains both RNA-binding KH domains and a RING domain, these enrichment results are biologically plausible and suggest that MEX3C may influence cancer progression through RNA processing, transcript localization, protein turnover, and energy-dependent cellular processes.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, most analyses were based on public bulk transcriptomic and genomic datasets, and therefore the findings are primarily correlative. Second, although immune infiltration algorithms provide useful estimates of TME composition, they cannot fully replace spatial, single-cell, or experimental validation. Third, the regulatory relationship between MEX3C and RNA modification genes, immune modulators, cytokine-related pathways, TMB, or MSI requires further mechanistic investigation. Recent studies integrating pan-cancer analysis, single-cell sequencing, molecular subtyping, machine learning, and multi-omics platforms have demonstrated the value of combining computational prediction with experimental validation for identifying clinically relevant tumor subtypes and therapeutic targets. Therefore, future work should integrate single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, in vitro and in vivo functional assays, and clinical cohorts receiving immunotherapy to clarify the causal role of MEX3C in tumor progression and immune regulation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our pan-cancer analysis suggests that MEX3C is a clinically relevant gene with context-dependent roles across human cancers. MEX3C expression is associated with tumor stage, prognosis, genetic alterations, RNA modification regulators, immune infiltration, immune checkpoints, immune modulators, TMB, MSI, and cancer-related pathways. These findings indicate that MEX3C may contribute to tumor progression through post-transcriptional regulation, RNA modification-related networks, genomic instability, and remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. Importantly, the associations between MEX3C and immune cells, cytokine-related molecules, CAFs, VEGFA, CD276, TMB, and MSI suggest that MEX3C may have potential relevance for immunotherapy stratification in selected tumor contexts. Although further experimental and clinical validation is required, MEX3C may represent a promising biomarker and potential therapeutic target, particularly in tumors in which its expression is linked to poor prognosis and immune microenvironment alterations.
