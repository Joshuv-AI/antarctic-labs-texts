Comprehensive geriatric assessment and optimisation in advanced chronic kidney disease: a systematic review and narrative synthesis examining clinical outcomes and decision-making
Authors: John A Holland, Judith SL Partridge, Antonia J Cronin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults with advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) frequently experience frailty, multimorbidity, cognitive impairment and functional decline, yet these geriatric syndromes are not routinely evaluated in nephrology services. Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) can support personalised treatment decisions and optimise outcomes, but its role in CKD stages 4–5 remains unclear. This systematic review examined the impact of CGA on clinical outcomes, decision-making, and implementation within nephrology services.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. MEDLINE and Embase were searched from inception to November 2025, alongside clinical trials registries. Studies were eligible if they included adults aged ≥ 60 years with CKD stages 4–5 (predialysis, dialysis-dependent, and/or undergoing kidney transplant evaluation) and evaluated CGA or a nephrology-tailored multidomain geriatric assessment incorporating ≥ 3 geriatric domains. Outcomes of interest included clinical outcomes, shared decision-making, and/or implementation features. Risk of bias was assessed using ROBINS-I and CASP tools. Narrative synthesis was undertaken due to study heterogeneity.ResultsOf 6,978 records identified, 5,923 were screened after duplicate removal; 39 full texts were assessed and six studies met inclusion criteria. CGA consistently identified high burdens of frailty, cognitive impairment, and functional limitation. CGA domains were strongly associated with mortality, hospitalisation, dialysis initiation, and transplant listing outcomes. Across conservative care, dialysis, and transplant pathways, CGA informed shared decision-making, treatment planning, and advance care planning. Implementation was feasible but heterogeneous, with variation in CGA models, fidelity, and workforce requirements.ConclusionCGA meaningfully informs prognostic assessment and shared decision-making in older adults with advanced CKD, particularly in conservative kidney management (CKM) and transplant pathways. However, evidence for improving clinical outcomes remains limited, underscoring the need for rigorous trials and implementation frameworks to establish CGA as a core component of kidney care.Keywords: Frailty, Multiple long-term health conditions, Cognitive impairment, Transplantation, Renal replacement therapy, Dialysis, Equitable access, Comprehensive geriatric assessment, Older adults

Introduction

The prevalence of advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) increases in the context of an ageing global population [1]. CKD affects almost 40% of people over the age of 65 in the United States of America (USA) and the United Kingdom (UK) [2, 3]. End-stage kidney disease (ESKD), also known as CKD stage 5, is diagnosed when an individual’s estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) falls below 15 ml/min/1.73 m2. At this stage treatment with kidney replacement therapy (KRT)- including dialysis therapies (haemo- or peritoneal dialysis) - and kidney transplantation (KT) - is usually required to prolong the lives of affected patients [4]. Individuals who do not wish to pursue KRT may opt for supportive care, an approach which integrates principles of palliative care medicine into conservative CKD management strategies [5]. As the population ages it is anticipated that demand for KRT, KT and supportive care will inevitably increase.

Older adults with advanced CKD frequently live with geriatric syndromes that are not captured by traditional nephrology metrics such as estimated glomerular filtration rate or biochemical markers alone. Frailty, multimorbidity, cognitive impairment, and functional dependence are highly prevalent in this population and are independently associated with adverse outcomes, including mortality, hospitalisation, reduced tolerance of dialysis, and lower likelihood of access to kidney transplantation [6–8]. Importantly, these vulnerabilities also influence patients’ values, goals, and preferences, shaping how risks and benefits of treatment options are perceived and weighed.

Frailty, characterised by reduced physiological reserve and vulnerability to stressors, is markedly over-represented among older adults with CKD compared with age-matched populations without kidney disease [9]. Across predialysis, dialysis, and transplant cohorts, frailty has been consistently associated with poorer survival, increased healthcare utilisation, and worse post-transplant outcomes [9, 10]. Similarly, cognitive impairment is common in advanced CKD and may affect decisional capacity, adherence, and postoperative recovery [11–15]. Multimorbidity further compounds risk, introducing treatment burden and increasing the likelihood that kidney-focused interventions interact with other long-term conditions in ways that may undermine overall benefit[6, 16].

Despite the recognised impact of these geriatric syndromes, their assessment in routine nephrology practice remains inconsistent. While single-domain tools; such as frailty screens or brief cognitive tests, are increasingly used, these approaches do not provide an integrated understanding of how medical, functional, psychological, and social factors interact to influence prognosis and treatment decision-making. As a result, clinicians may struggle to move beyond risk identification toward structured, person-centred care planning.

Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) offers a multidimensional framework for addressing this gap. CGA is a structured, multidisciplinary process that evaluates medical conditions, functional status, cognition, mood, nutrition, social circumstances, and polypharmacy, with the explicit aim of generating a coordinated management plan aligned with patient goals. A 2018 umbrella review of CGA, emphasised that CGA is characterised not only by multidomain assessment but also by interdisciplinary team working, structured care planning, and linkage to management action [17]. CGA is traditionally geriatrician-led, although elements may be delivered collaboratively across disciplines provided core principles and integration mechanisms are maintained [18, 19]. In non-renal populations, CGA has demonstrated benefits across a range of outcomes, including reduced hospitalisation, improved functional trajectories, enhanced shared decision-making, and more appropriate use of healthcare resources [20, 21]. In perioperative and oncological settings, CGA-guided optimisation has been shown to influence treatment selection, mitigate complications, and support more nuanced risk-benefit discussions[21–23].

In nephrology, interest in CGA has grown in response to the increasing complexity of older patients approaching dialysis or transplant evaluation. Emerging studies have applied CGA or nephrology-adapted multidomain geriatric assessments across predialysis clinics, dialysis populations, conservative care pathways, and transplant assessment programmes [22–28]. These studies suggest that CGA can uncover clinically relevant vulnerabilities, inform prognostic conversations, and influence treatment planning. Narrative work and expert editorials emphasise the conceptual rationale for CGA [29]. However, the evidence base remains fragmented, with substantial heterogeneity in CGA models, outcome measures, and implementation strategies. To date, the impact of CGA on clinical outcomes and shared decision-making in advanced CKD has not been systematically synthesised.

The aim of this systematic review and narrative synthesis is therefore twofold. First, to evaluate the existing evidence on the influence of CGA on clinical outcomes and decision-making among older adults with advanced CKD, including those managed conservatively, receiving dialysis, or undergoing kidney transplant evaluation. Second, to examine how CGA has been implemented within nephrology services, identifying key contextual factors, models of delivery, and areas of variability. By consolidating the available evidence, this review seeks to clarify the current role of CGA in advanced CKD and to inform future research, service design, and policy development in geriatric nephrology. Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search of MEDLINE and Embase was conducted from inception to 3rd November 2025 using a prespecified search strategy (see Appendix 1). Trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov and ISRCTN) were also searched for unpublished or ongoing studies. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were hand-searched to identify additional citations.

Selection process

Two reviewers (JAH and JSLP) independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility. Full-text articles were then assessed in duplicate. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (AJC). Screening results are shown in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). A total of 5923 records were screened; 39 full texts were assessed; and six studies met inclusion criteria. Reasons for exclusion are detailed in the PRISMA figure.

Data extraction

A standardised data extraction form was used to collect information on study characteristics, population, sample size, CGA components, comparator (if applicable), clinical outcomes, decision-making outcomes, implementation features, main findings, authors’ conclusions, and quality assessment results. Extraction was performed independently by JH and JP, with disagreements resolved by consensus.

Quality/Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias in quantitative non-randomised studies was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool, which evaluates potential bias across seven domains: confounding, selection, intervention classification, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting (Appendix 1) [30].

Qualitative studies examining decision-making were appraised using the CASP Qualitative Checklist, which assesses validity, rigour, and relevance across domains such as clarity of aims, appropriateness of methodology, recruitment, data collection, reflexivity, ethics, and analytic depth. It supports transparent judgement of credibility using ‘Yes/No/Can’t tell’ responses. (Appendix 2) [31].

The overall certainty of evidence was summarised narratively using adapted GRADE principles, which rate confidence in effect estimates as high, moderate, low, or very low based on study limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and reporting bias [32].

Synthesis methods

In view of the heterogeneity in study designs, populations, CGA models, and outcomes, a narrative synthesis was undertaken in accordance with the pre-specified review questions outlined in the Introduction and registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251238256).

Findings were organised thematically to address:

The impact of CGA on clinical outcomes,

The impact of CGA on clinical outcomes,

The impact of CGA on shared decision-making and treatment planning, and

The impact of CGA on shared decision-making and treatment planning, and

Quantitative data were summarised descriptively, and qualitative findings were synthesised thematically. No meta-analysis was possible due to heterogeneity.

Study selection

The database search identified 6978 records, of which 5923 remained after removal of duplicates. Following title and abstract screening, 39 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, six studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis. The most common reasons for exclusion were: use of single-domain geriatric measures only (e.g., frailty alone), lack of outcome or decision-making data, non-CGA MDT assessments, protocol-only publications, and studies involving CKD stages < 4 without separate analysis. The study selection process is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics and population

The six included studies comprised five observational studies (Hall et al.; [33] Ohta et al.; [34] van Loon et al.; [35] Campbell et al.; [36], Patel et al. [37]) and one qualitative study (Berkhout-Byrne et al. [38]), published between 2016 and 2025 across Europe and Asia. Sample sizes ranged from 16 to 646 participants, and study settings varied across predialysis CKD clinics, dialysis units, transplant evaluation pathways, and conservative kidney management (CKM) programmes.

Across the studies, the populations predominantly consisted of older adults with CKD stages 4–5 or ESKD, including patients undergoing dialysis (Hall et al.; [33] Ohta et al.; [34] van Loon et al. [35]), individuals being evaluated for kidney transplantation (Campbell et al. [36], Patel et al. [37]), and older adults attending multidisciplinary renal-geriatric services (Hall et al.; [33] Berkhout-Byrne et al. [38]). Mean participant ages ranged from 70 to 87 years. Frailty, multimorbidity, and cognitive impairment were common across all study populations, aligning with known geriatric complexity in advanced CKD.

CGA components and CGA models used

All six studies employed a multidomain geriatric assessment, though CGA models varied substantially in structure and delivery. Core domains assessed across studies included: functional status (ADL/IADL), physical performance and frailty, cognition, mood, comorbidity, nutrition, social context, and polypharmacy.

Three studies (Hall et al.; [33] Campbell et al.; [36] Patel et al. [37]) employed geriatrician-led CGA delivered within nephrology or transplant evaluation clinics, consistent with classical CGA models. In contrast, Ohta et al.; [34] van Loon et al.; [35] and Berkhout-Byrne et al. [38] utilised nephrology-led or nephrology-integrated multidomain geriatric assessments without direct geriatrician leadership. These latter models retained core CGA domains but varied in the degree of interdisciplinary integration and formal optimisation planning. Berkhout-Byrne et al. described a nephrology-tailored geriatric assessment integrated into routine renal consultations [38]. Van Loon utilised a multidomain geriatric battery within the GOLD prospective cohort, capturing frailty, disability, comorbidity, cognitive status, and psychosocial health [35].

CGA delivery settings included: geriatric medicine clinics (Campbell et al.; [36] Patel et al. [37]), integrated nephrology–geriatric pathways (Hall et al.; [33] Berkhout-Byrne et al. [38]), and home or outpatient CKM settings (Ohta et al. [34]).

Although all studies captured core CGA domains, fidelity, depth of assessment, standardisation, and the extent of optimisation varied substantially. These structural and leadership differences in CGA delivery provide important context for interpreting subsequent findings relating to clinical outcomes and shared decision-making.

Impact of CGA on clinical outcomes

Four studies evaluated clinical outcomes. In the GOLD cohort, van Loon demonstrated that multidomain geriatric impairments- including frailty, disability, and cognitive decline- were independently associated with mortality, hospitalisation, and dialysis initiation, underscoring the prognostic relevance of CGA domains in CKD stage 5/ESKD [35]. Hall and colleagues reported similar incidence of onward referral to specialist geriatric medicine services following CGA for their CGA-4-CKD and Renal Silver initiative populations (6 and 9% respectively), though the study was not sufficiently powered to demonstrate statistical association between CGA findings and increased clinical care needs[33].

Two studies examined the impact of CGA on transplant listing outcomes. Campbell found that CGA conducted during transplant evaluation influenced transplant listing decisions, with frailty and functional impairment predicting non-listing or deferral [36]. Patel reinforced this evidence in a larger retrospective cohort, showing that global CGA ratings (Excellent, Good, Fair, Poor) strongly predicted listing, deferral, or declination decisions during the transplant multidisciplinary meeting. Poor CGA ratings were associated with markedly lower likelihood of listing, while Excellent/Good ratings correlated with favourable decisions [37].

Available evidence suggests that CGA domains meaningfully predict clinical trajectories in advanced CKD; however, interventional evidence of CGA-led optimisation improving outcomes currently remains limited to observational data.

Impact of CGA on shared decision-making and treatment planning

Five studies demonstrated that CGA directly influenced SDM conversations or treatment planning. In Hall’s integrated geriatric-nephrology clinic, CGA informed recommendations regarding dialysis versus conservative kidney management, shaped ACP discussions, and identified unmet needs affecting treatment tolerance [33]. Ohta reported that CGA findings- especially frailty and cognitive impairment-guided decisions to pursue CKM rather than dialysis, with CGA supporting repeated ACP conversations and aligning treatment with patient preferences [34]. Berkhout-Byrne found that nephrology-tailored CGA influenced nephrologists’ clinical reasoning, enhanced patient-clinician communication, and enabled more comprehensive SDM[38].

In the transplant pathway, Campbell showed that CGA findings informed decisions around transplant listing, optimisation, or deferral, with geriatric deficits triggering targeted prehabilitation or influencing the decision not to list [36]. Patel et al. found that CGA ratings substantially shaped transplant multidisciplinary meeting decisions, with frailty, impaired cognition, reduced mobility, and low global CGA scores prompting decisions to defer or decline listing [37].

Implementation of CGA within nephrology pathways

Four studies provided implementation insights. Delivery logistics varied substantially across studies. Geriatrician-led models were typically delivered within co-located outpatient clinics or transplant evaluation pathways, often requiring dedicated consultation time and formal presentation of findings at multidisciplinary meetings. In contrast, nephrology-led multidomain geriatric assessments were integrated within routine renal consultations or conservative kidney management clinics, relying on renal clinicians trained in geriatric assessment principles. Research-based geriatric assessment batteries, such as the GOLD cohort, were administered by trained assessors but were not embedded within routine optimisation pathways. Geriatrician-led models were described as resource-intensive and contingent on specialist workforce availability.

Hall et al. evaluated two related service models within an academic nephrology setting. The “CGA-4-CKD” model involved a geriatrician co-located within a nephrology clinic providing structured comprehensive geriatric assessment to older adults with advanced CKD referred for geriatric input. The “Renal Silver” initiative represented a broader multidisciplinary programme integrating geriatric principles into renal care, with referral pathways for geriatric assessment embedded within routine nephrology practice. Both models targeted older adults with advanced CKD and high geriatric complexity, aiming to inform treatment planning, dialysis versus conservative care decisions, and advance care planning [33].

Berkhout-Byrne described enablers (interprofessional relationships, structured tools) and barriers (workflow integration, training gaps) in embedding nephrology-tailored CGA within routine care [38]. Ohta demonstrated successful incorporation of CGA into CKM pathways, particularly for guiding decision-making around supportive care options and end-of-life planning [31].

Patel offered additional insights into CGA implementation within transplant pathways. A geriatrician-delivered CGA was routinely integrated into pretransplant evaluation, with structured CGA summaries presented at the transplant multidisciplinary meeting. This model facilitated real-time incorporation of geriatric findings into listing decisions but also highlighted potential limitations, including dependence on geriatric workforce capacity and variability in how transplant teams interpreted CGA ratings [37].

Few studies formally evaluated patient-reported acceptability or burden of CGA delivery. Where described, acceptability was typically inferred from integration into clinical workflow, qualitative interviews, or sustained service uptake rather than structured patient experience measures.

Validated tools used across CGA models

Across the six included studies, a wide range of validated tools were used to assess frailty, cognition, physical function, comorbidity, nutrition, mood, and social vulnerability. Although all models undertook multidomain assessment consistent with CGA principles, the choice of tools varied considerably, reflecting differences in clinical setting, purpose, and disciplinary leadership.

Frailty assessment was most commonly performed using components of the Fried frailty phenotype, as applied in the GOLD cohort (van Loon et al. [35]), Campbell’s pretransplant CGA [36], and Patel’s pretransplant CGA [37]. Hall also incorporated frailty elements- such as grip strength, weight loss, fatigue, and mobility- within the geriatric clinic assessment [33], while Ohta used the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) to classify global frailty severity [34]. Berkhout-Byrne assessed frailty informally within their nephrology-tailored geriatric assessment, without specifying a single validated frailty instrument [38].

Cognitive screening was included in every study but again using varying tools. Hall et al. employed a mini-cog test to screen for impairment [33]. Campbell used the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) embedded within their CGA model [36]. Patel used the St Louis University Mental Status Examination (SLUMS), enabling more sensitive detection of mild cognitive impairment compared to MMSE [37]. Ohta employed the DASC-21, a validated multidomain tool capturing both cognitive and functional deficits. The GOLD cohort included extensive structured cognitive testing as part of its geriatric battery (MMSE, Clock drawing test, fluency test, enhanced cued recall test.) [35], while Berkhout-Byrne incorporated cognition routinely into nephrology-tailored CGA, although specific constituent instruments were not reported as part of their work [38].

Physical function was assessed using validated performance measures in all studies: gait speed, Timed Up and Go (TUG), and handgrip strength in Campbell et al. [36] and Patel et al.; [37] ADL/IADL functional dependence in Hall et al., [33] van Loon et al., [35] and Berkhout-Byrne et al.; [38] and dependency descriptors within Ohta’s CKM model [34].

Mood was commonly assessed using the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) (van Loon [35]; Campbell [36]; Patel [34]; Hall [30]), while comorbidity was captured through structured indices such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) (Campbell [36], Patel [37], van Loon [35]). Nutritional assessment varied: Patel et al. included BMI and albumin [37]; while van Loon et al. and Campbell et al. incorporated weight loss within frailty assessment [35, 36].

All studies addressed social circumstances, including living arrangements, caregiver support, and social vulnerability, although none used a formal social network scale [33–38]. Polypharmacy was routinely assessed within geriatrician-led CGA models [33, 34, 36, 37].

Quality/Risk of bias reporting

Across the five quantitative studies, risk of bias was rated as moderate to moderate-serious using the ROBINS-I framework, largely reflecting the observational design of included cohorts, potential residual confounding, limited adjustment for prognostic variables, and absence of randomised comparators.

For clarity, “geriatrician-led CGA” refers to models consistent with consensus definitions of comprehensive geriatric assessment involving geriatrician leadership and structured interdisciplinary care planning. “Nephrology-led multidomain geriatric assessment” refers to structured assessment across ≥ 3 geriatric domains without formal geriatrician leadership.

Discussion

This systematic review identified six studies examining the role of CGA in older adults with advanced CKD. Despite methodological heterogeneity, through narrative synthesis three consistent findings emerged.

First, CGA reliably detected frailty, cognitive concerns, functional limitations, multimorbidity, and psychosocial vulnerabilities that were not identified in routine nephrology and pre-transplant evaluation practice. These multidomain deficits were associated with important clinical outcomes, including mortality, hospitalisation, dialysis initiation, and transplant listing outcomes. Transplant studies demonstrated that higher CGA performance predicted successful listing and transplantation, while deficits in function or cognition predicted non-listing, deferral, and poorer survival [33–37].

Second, CGA had a measurable impact on SDM and treatment planning across CKD care pathways. In both transplant and CKM contexts, CGA provided essential information that shaped decisions regarding dialysis versus conservative care, suitability for transplantation, and the initiation of advance care planning. Qualitative evidence indicated that CGA broadened clinicians’ understanding of patient needs, supported more patient-centred discussions, and facilitated alignment of treatment plans with patient priorities [33–38].

Finally, implementation of CGA within nephrology services was shown to be feasible, though variably executed. Geriatrician-led models demonstrated strong clinical value but required significant workforce and time investment [33, 35, 36]. Nephrology-tailored models were pragmatic but less standardised [34, 37]. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of CGA to enhance risk stratification, support personalised decision-making, and inform resource allocation for older adults with advanced CKD. However, further work is required to embed CGA sustainably within existing nephrology pathways while maintaining fidelity to established CGA principles, alongside ongoing audit, governance, and research to demonstrate clinical and cost-effectiveness.

The CGA intervention varied considerably across studies, reflecting differences in workforce, setting, and purpose. Some studies delivered high-fidelity geriatrician-led CGA, incorporating all core domains and elements of optimisation [33, 36, 37], whereas others used nephrology-tailored or research-derived multidomain batteries [34, 38]. This heterogeneity underscores the absence of a unified renal CGA framework and echoes concerns raised in broader implementation literature regarding definitional drift with impact on fidelity to CGA models as they have evolved in the literature. Several excluded but relevant studies illustrate this point: some applied multidomain assessment without associated optimisation, MDT review, or care planning- features essential to the CGA model as established in the original geriatric medicine literature [39–41]. This illustrates the importance of distinguishing screening (e.g., frailty measures) from true CGA, which includes coordinated intervention/optimisation.

In geriatric oncology, several ‘CGA’ trials have shown limited impact on outcomes when assessment findings were not reliably translated into implemented optimisation plans, or when CGA occurred after treatment decisions were effectively fixed. In contrast, benefit is more consistently seen when CGA is embedded before/at the time of decision-making and paired with actionable recommendations and mechanisms to deliver and implement them- highlighting that assessment alone is insufficient without structured intervention and decision integration [42–44].

Insights from the wider CGA literature support this point. The 2021 UK Centre for Perioperative Care- British Geriatrics Society (CPOC-BGS) guidelines on managing frail surgical patients emphasise that CGA is effective only when delivered with fidelity to multidomain assessment, interdisciplinary collaboration, structured communication, and linked optimisation pathways. These guidelines also outline metrics for evaluating CGA service performance which so far have rarely been applied in nephrology research to date [45]. While classical CGA models are geriatrician-led, successful adaptation into specialty settings requires both preservation of core principles and appropriate geriatric medicine oversight. Evidence from the mixed-methods HoW-CGA study suggests that geriatrician leadership, interdisciplinary collaboration, and embedded optimisation pathways are central to realising the clinical impact of CGA, and that dissemination of assessment toolkits as a surrogate for CGA itself is ineffective [19]. However, sustainable implementation in high-volume specialties such as nephrology may necessitate transdisciplinary delivery models, whereby trained renal clinicians apply CGA principles within structured frameworks, with clear referral pathways to specialist geriatric medicine services when complex vulnerability is identified.

Such insights are directly relevant to nephrology, where the truncation of CGA may occur given lack of specialist training in CGA delivery or workforce pressures, but risk undermining demonstration of the effectiveness of CGA. Furthermore, it is important that geriatric syndromes are accurately identified and properly diagnosed. For example, cognitive impairment can only be diagnosed where cognitive deficits are accompanied by functional impairment accounting for premorbid educational level, a nuance sometimes overlooked in renal studies attempting to classify CI with cognitive testing alone [35, 38]. These considerations highlight the need for careful attention to fidelity, training, and conceptual clarity when implementing CGA in CKD pathways.

A growing body of ongoing research aims to address the limitations seen in existing CGA studies. DIALOGICA, a large Dutch prospective study, is evaluating dialysis versus conservative management outcomes using a structured nephrogeriatric assessment tailored to patients with CKD 4–5. Its focus on functional outcomes, decisional regret, health-related quality of life and mortality aligns with gaps highlighted by this review [28].

The GOAL trial, an Australian cluster-randomised CGA intervention study, uses descriptive observational work in those with frailty to testing CGA as a service-level complex intervention. Its embedded process evaluation [45] and prespecified statistical analysis plan [46] represent a methodological advance, illustrating how fidelity, mechanisms of action, and contextual factors must be understood to interpret the impact of a multicomponent intervention such as CGA [47, 48]. These features contrast with earlier studies that lacked the modelling and mechanistic evaluation now expected for complex interventions.

A Chinese RCT led by Chang and colleagues extends this paradigm earlier in the pathway of care or earlier in the trajectory of disease, testing CGA-driven personalised interventions in frail CKD 3–5 patients, aiming to demonstrate feasibility of early optimisation before dialysis or transplant decisions arise [48]. The ongoing Czech CoGeriaTx study is examining whether CGA-led optimisation improves readiness for listing or post-transplant outcomes [49].

Thematically, these studies are aligned with many of the priorities identified in the 2025 Delphi study undertaken by the US Kidney Disease and Aging Research Collaborative (KDARC) group, which highlighted SDM, QoL, frailty, and personalised care as central research priorities in geriatric nephrology [50].

Across included studies and supported by wider literature, several barriers and enablers to CGA implementation were identified [34–36, 51, 52]. In nephrology services, clinicians report persistent workforce shortages, lack of protected time, limited geriatric assessment training, and uncertain pathway ownership- findings captured in a recent national UK survey study assessing attitudes, beliefs, and practices around CGA among renal physicians and transplant surgeons [53]. The POLDER CGA implementation study similarly found that while clinicians perceive nephrology-tailored CGA to be highly relevant and feasible, practical challenges such as patient burden, health literacy, and time constraints impede routine delivery [24]. These findings mirror experiences in perioperative CGA services, where investment in training, workforce development, and service redesign has been essential for successful adoption [54, 55].

Conversely, strong interdisciplinary collaboration culture, MDT integration, and supportive leadership consistently enabled effective CGA delivery [24, 35, 36, 38]. Nurse-led or shared-assessment models, as demonstrated in the POLDER group implementation study, may help mitigate workforce pressures, promote transdisciplinary working and support scalability but will require adequate upskilling of staff responsible for CGA delivery [24]. Transformative approaches- such as embedding CGA competencies into trainee curricula, adopting perioperative CGA style collaborative frameworks, and utilising implementation science guidance (e.g., MRC, CFIR)- will be crucial for establishing sustainable CGA services [48, 56].

Interpretation of the findings in these studies must be considered in light of methodological limitations. All quantitative studies included in this review were observational and demonstrated moderate to moderate-serious risk of bias, primarily due to confounding, small sample sizes, and heterogeneity in CGA delivery models. While consistent associations between multidomain geriatric impairment and adverse outcomes strengthen biological plausibility, evidence that CGA-led optimisation improves hard clinical endpoints remains insufficient and existing studies demonstrate moderate to moderate-to-serious risk of bias. These limitations restrict causal inference regarding the effectiveness of CGA-led optimisation and necessitate cautious interpretation of associations between CGA findings and clinical outcomes. reflect the early developmental stage of nephrology-specific CGA research rather than absence of signal, and highlight the need for well-designed complex intervention trials incorporating appropriate control groups, fidelity monitoring, and longer follow-up.

Importantly, co-design with patients and frontline clinicians is likely to be central to sustainable CGA implementation. Ensuring that CGA models reflect patient priorities, minimise assessment burden, and integrate meaningfully into pathways of care will be critical to translating study findings into routine clinical practice. Notably, patient acceptability was formally assessed in only one of the six included studies, despite being a key dimension of implementation evaluation [38]. Clinician acceptability was similarly underreported and was typically inferred from sustained programme activity rather than systematically measured. Although perioperative CGA literature suggests high levels of patient and surgical stakeholder engagement [20, 21, 23], the longitudinal nature of nephrology care relationships may introduce additional complexities that warrant explicit assessment of both patient and clinician perspectives in future CGA research. Future nephrology-specific CGA studies should incorporate structured evaluation of acceptability, including patient-reported experience measures, decision quality assessments, and qualitative exploration of perceived value and burden. Embedding formal process evaluations within CGA trials, aligned with updated MRC guidance for the development and evaluation of complex interventions, would enable assessment of fidelity, mechanisms of action, contextual influences, and stakeholder acceptability [48].

Ultimately it is possible that effective CGA delivery could help address health inequity in the management of older kidney patients with advanced CKD by providing a structured assessment of frailty, cognition, and functional capacity and reducing reliance on subjective age-based impressions. Fidelity to CGA principles and robust optimisation processes are central to ensuring CGA enhances access to treatment options through meaningful intervention and valuable shared decision-making conversations [51, 57]. Given the consistent evidence that multidomain geriatric vulnerabilities strongly predict outcomes in CKD 4–5 [9–17, 58], and that CGA meaningfully influences treatment decisions [33–38], future research must move beyond describing risk to modifying it. CGA and optimisation strategies must now be evaluated in pragmatic, nephrology-facing trials enrolling older adults with advanced CKD and using core outcome sets that capture treatment-decision quality, goal attainment, quality of life, frailty trajectory, resource allocation and healthcare utilisation.

Within kidney transplantation, a transplant-specific CGA model has the potential to be transformative for older potential transplant candidates. There is now an urgent need for RCT-level evaluation examining whether CGA-guided optimisation improves key clinical outcomes, including waitlisting rates, perioperative length of stay, graft function at 12–18 months and beyond, mortality, and days alive and out of hospital.

There is a compelling rationale also for shifting CGA upstream into predialysis CKD clinics, using easily applied validated frailty screens to identify patients who would benefit from full CGA and triggering onward specialist referral. Early characterisation of vulnerability may prevent crisis-driven dialysis starts, improve ACP quality, and support more balanced discussions around transplant, dialysis, and conservative management.

Drawing on translational lessons from perioperative medicine for older people, where CGA is now embedded within many surgical pathways, the renal academic community should prioritise rigorous modelling, co-design, and early-phase evaluation of CGA across CKD, dialysis, and transplant settings.

Future research on CGA in renal populations may be better served by accelerated, agile, yet methodologically robust approaches to evaluation. The Older Kidney Patient Optimisation Pre-transplant (OK-POP) study is a single-centre, patient-prompted and codesigned randomised controlled feasibility trial which will evaluate a kidney transplant-specific CGA intervention embedded within pre-transplant care pathways. This study aligns with MRC guidance to incorporate structured evaluation of clinical processes and feasibility endpoints, with a focus on patient and clinician experience and acceptability, as well as implementation logistics [59]. Systematic reviews and consensus work on rapid research and appraisal demonstrate that timely, iterative, and implementation-focused methodologies can generate trustworthy, actionable evidence- provided rigour, transparency, and standards such as Standards for Rapid Evaluation and Appraisal Methods (STREAM) are explicitly maintained- supporting faster translation of CGA models into clinical practice [60, 61].

Early, holistic, multidimensional intervention aligns closely with the NHS England Fit for the Future: 10-Year Health Plan, which emphasises prevention, proactive care, and personalised support for people living with long-term conditions [62]. It is also consistent with UKKA and Kidney Research UK research priorities, which highlight frailty, symptom burden, decision-making, and equity of access as critical areas for innovation in older adults with kidney disease [63, 64]. Internationally, organisations such as Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) and the European Renal Association–European Dialysis and Transplantation Association (ERA-EDTA) have increasingly emphasised a multidisciplinary, person-centred model of kidney care, an approach inherent in CGA principles [65, 66].

Conclusion

This systematic review and narrative synthesis examined the role of CGA in older adults with advanced CKD both predialysis and on dialysis.

First, CGA consistently identified frailty, functional impairment, cognitive impairment, and multimorbidity that were strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including mortality, hospitalisation, dialysis initiation, and transplant listing decisions. However, evidence that CGA-led optimisation improves clinical outcomes remains limited and predominantly observational.

Second, CGA meaningfully influenced shared decision-making and treatment planning across conservative kidney management, dialysis, and transplant pathways, supporting clearer risk communication, alignment of care with patient priorities, and more individualised recommendations regarding modality choice and transplant candidacy.

Finally, CGA implementation within nephrology services was feasible but heterogeneous, with variation in fidelity, workforce models, and integration into clinical pathways. Geriatrician-led models demonstrated high clinical utility but were resource-intensive, while nephrology-tailored approaches offered scalability with less standardisation. Robust trials and implementation frameworks are now required to establish CGA as a core component of personalised, equitable kidney care.[67]
