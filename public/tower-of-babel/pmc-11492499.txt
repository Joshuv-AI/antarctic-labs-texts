Central artery pulse pressure, not central arterial stiffness impact on all-cause mortality in patients with viral pneumonia infection
Authors: Lin Jin, Jianxiong Chen, Lingheng Wu, Mengjiao Zhang, Xiaobo Tang, Cuiqin Shen, Jiali Sun, Lianfang Du, Xifu Wang, Zhaojun Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesCOVID-19 viral pneumonia can result in increased arterial stiffness, along with cardiac and systemic inflammatory responses. This study aimed to investigate the association between arterial stiffness, inflammation severity, and all-cause mortality in patients with COVID-19.MethodsIn this study, anthropometric data, pneumonia infection severity, and blood tests were analyzed. Arterial stiffness was assessed using the non-invasive assessment indices, including arterial velocity pulse index (AVI) and central arterial pulse pressure (CAPP). Infection volumes and percentages for the whole lungs, most lobes, and most segments were extracted from CT images using artificial intelligence-based quantitative analysis software. The relationship between arterial stiffness, central hemodynamics, and all-cause mortality was investigated.ResultsIn multivariable Cox regression analysis, high CAPP was significantly associated with all-cause mortality (hazard ratio: 0.263, 95% CI, 0.073–0.945, p = 0.041). Whole lung infection percentages were independently associated with high CAPP, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.662 and a specificity of 89.09%.ConclusionsHigh CAPP, but not high AVI, demonstrated independent prognostic value for all-cause mortality in patients due to COVID-19 pneumonia infection. Evaluating this parameter could help in risk assessment and improve diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in viral pneumonia infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10091-y.

Introduction

On May 5th, 2023, the World Health Organization announced that the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic no longer constitutes a “public health emergency of international concern”. However, evidence suggests that viral infection, represented by COVID-19 may lead to arterial stiffness, systemic inflammation, and cardiac response through direct viral attack, hypoxia-induced injury, or immunological dysregulation [1, 2]. Furthermore, vascular impairment in COVID-19 may be associated with clinical symptoms and could influence further risk stratification of patients [3–5]. COVID-19 may cause damage to the vascular system, including endothelial dysfunction and large artery stiffening, which may persist beyond the recovery phase [6, 7]. These vascular impairments may be related to the degree of inflammation in coronavirus infection [8].

Central artery pulse pressure (CAPP) and arterial velocity pulse index (AVI) are non-invasive and repeatable technique for measuring arterial stiffness. CAPP indicates the burden on the heart and coronary and cerebral vessels and is demonstrated to be a better predictor of clinical outcomes in cardiovascular disease (CVD) than brachial blood pressure in clinical studies [9, 10]. The principal cause of increased pulse pressure (PP) is increased stiffness of the elastic arteries as a result of degeneration and hyperplasia of the arterial wall [11]. As arterial stiffness increases, the reflected wave amplitude rises, which raises left ventricular (LV) and aortic pressure in late systole. This leads to higher LV afterload and myocardial oxygen demand, while lowering myocardial perfusion pressure. As a result, this creates an imbalance between the heart’s oxygen supply and demand [12]. AVI is widely used as a strong predictor of cardiovascular risk [13]. An increased AVI indicates increased arterial stiffness from the aorta to the brachial artery, as well as higher resistance in the peripheral arteries [14]. As the aorta stiffens, its ability to buffer pressure diminishes, leading to an increased pulsatile arterial load and elevated central blood pressure [15].

In infectious diseases, aortic stiffness may have a significant impact on patient clinical outcomes beyond the total volume and site of infectious lesions [16, 17]. If fibrosis, commonly seen in post-infection lung complications, extends to the vascular wall tissue and replaces the elastic components of the large artery walls, it may lead to long-term complications [8, 18].

Chest computed tomography (CT) imaging has emerged as a crucial tool in identifying infections, assessing severe cases, and monitoring disease progression [19, 20]. The most frequently observed CT findings in COVID-19 pneumonia include ground-glass opacities, consolidation, air bronchogram, crazy paving pattern, nodular opacities, and pleural effusion [21]. However, the reliability of the semiquantitative CT score can be influenced by the criteria employed for scoring and the radiologists’ skill level. Artificial intelligence (AI) diagnostic systems and quantitative analysis based on CT images can assist physicians in quickly generating preliminary and differential diagnoses, assessing disease severity, and predicting clinical prognosis [22, 23]. Previous studies have shown that deep learning-based segmentation systems demonstrate a high level of accuracy in automatically identifying lesion regions and calculating lesion percentage metrics [24].

Therefore, the objective of this retrospective study was to investigate the association between elevated CAPP or AVI levels and the severity of infection, as well as all-cause mortality, in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia infection.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study enrolled patients who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 through polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing of nasal swabs or other respiratory samples within the first five days of hospital admission. The recruitment took place at our medical facility between December 2022 and January 2023.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) moderate and severe cases according to the diagnosis and treatment protocol for COVID-19 (Trial Version 10) issued by the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China. Mild cases presented with mild clinical symptoms (fever, cough, sore throat, etc.). Moderate cases exhibited clinical symptoms such as fever>3 days, cough, dyspnea and tachypnea, with radiological findings of pneumonia, while SpO2 ≥ 93% in room air and respiratory rate<30 breaths/min. Severe cases in adults met any of the following criteria that could not be attributed to any cause other than COVID-19: (1) respiratory rate ≥ 30 breaths/min; (2) oxygen saturation < 93% in room air; (3) arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) ≤ 300 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa); (4) progressive worsening of clinical symptoms with lung imaging showing 24–48 h significant lesion progression > 50% within 24–48 h. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of lung surgery or thoracic deformity; (2) the quality of CT images did not meet the quantitative analysis requirements; and (3) no artery stiffness measurement was performed after symptom onset.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital. All procedures performed in the studies were done in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Physical examination and biochemical tests

A questionnaire was used to collect information on participants’ age, acute and chronic illnesses, and medical history, particularly on the use of medications for hypertension and diabetes. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Weight and height measurements were obtained following a standardized protocol, allowing for the calculation of body mass index (BMI, kg/m²). BMI was computed by dividing body weight (in kilograms) by the square of height (in meters). After a 5-minute rest, systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP, respectively) were obtained using an automated blood pressure (BP) monitor (PASESA AVE-2000 Pro, DAIWA Healthcare, Shenzhen, China) by experienced technicians. Routine laboratory analyses were conducted on white blood cell (WBC) counts, leucocytes and lymphocytes percentage, C-reactive protein, and alkaline phosphatase.

Measurement of artery stiffness

Central systolic blood pressure (CSBP), central arterial pulse pressure (CAPP), and arterial stiffness indices (AVI, API) were measured non-invasively using a brachial oscillometric blood pressure cuff (PASESA AVE-2000Pro, DAIWA Healthcare, Shenzhen, China). Before measurements, participants were instructed to rest for at least five minutes and to refrain from consuming caffeine, tea, alcohol, or smoking for at least 24 h prior to the examination. While seated, a cuff was placed around one upper arm [13]. For analysis, the average of three measurements was used, with a minimum interval of two minutes between each measurement. High CAPP was defined as ≥ 50 mm Hg [25].

Chest CT scan protocol and AI-based quantitative data acquisition

All patients underwent chest CT scans using a 64-row scanner (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) in the supine position after deep inspiration. The scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage of 120 kV, automatic tube current modulation, matrix size of 512 × 512, slice thickness of 5 mm, and spacing of 5 mm. Volumetric data with a reconstructed slice thickness of 0.5–1 mm were obtained for post-processing.

Quantitative analysis of infection volumes and percentages in the whole lung, most lobes, and segments was performed using the AI Intelligent Assistant Analysis System (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China). Each lobe was automatically segmented by identifying the bilateral lung contours and interlobular fissures (left oblique fissure, right horizontal and oblique fissures), with pneumonia lesions being automatically detected and distinguished by a deep learning (DL)-based method. This AI method employs the “VB-Net” neural network to segment COVID-19 infection regions in CT scans [26], and previous study has shown that DL-based segmentation systems are highly accurate in automatically delineating lesion regions [27].

The software used an improved 3D convolutional neural network (VB-Net) to segment the lung CT images, and used the human-in-the-loop (HITL) strategy to iteratively update the model. Specifically, the first segmentation network based on manually contoured CT data was used as an initial model, and then the initial model was applied to the next batch of infection areas, followed by manual correction. In this way of human–machine interaction, the training dataset was iteratively increased, and the final VB-Net model was built [20]. The software finally realised the automatic extraction of the lungs (five lung lobes and 18 lung segments) and the lesion area. Parameters were then calculated including: whole infection volume and infectious components (-750 to -300 HU) in the whole lung, most lobes, and segments (cm³); the percentage of infection volume in the whole lung, most lobes, and segments (%); and the number of infected lobes or segments.

Inter-rater agreement for AI intelligent assessments

Two radiologists, with 5 and 17 years of experience, recorded the lesion locations visible on the CT images. Inter-reviewer agreement on whole lung and predominant lobe infection percentage assessments was evaluated using Bland-Altman plots, as well as quantitatively by calculating inter-reviewer correlations and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 30-day mortality, as recorded in medical records. Secondary outcomes included the need for mechanical ventilation—whether non-invasive ventilation, high-flow nasal cannula, or invasive mechanical ventilation—and/or acute respiratory failure [28].

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median values and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, and as absolute frequencies and percentages for categorical variables.

To compare baseline demographic and clinical characteristics among the different groups, ANOVA was used for continuous variables, and Chi-square test was applied for categorical variables. Due to skewness, WBC counts weas log-transformed to achieve normality.

The CAPP continuous outcome was modeled in linear regression models, then modeled in binary logistic models. Explanatory variables included age, BMI, whole lung, most lobe and most segment infection percentage. Each outcome was fit against each explanatory variable in initial univariable regression models, then step-wise model selection was employed to find the significant multivariable model at α = 0.15. This significance level was used because this somewhat looser criterion may reveal borderline significant findings that can direct future research [29, 30]. To assess the ability of infection parameters in identifying high CAPP, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were utilized.

By stratifying by CAPP and AVI index, Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves were used to determine the incidence rate of major and secondary outcomes. The multivariate Cox proportional-hazard regression model included variables that were clinically relevant or which had a univariate relationship with outcome [31]. The variables included in the final model were carefully chosen based on the number of events available. The final model adjusted for age, BMI, high CAPP, high AVI, whole infection percentage, the most lobs and segments infection percentage. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 28.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Characteristics of viral pneumonia patients

The sample size for this study was determined by data availability, resulting in a total of 106 participants. These participants were categorized as follows: 51 with severe COVID-19 viral pneumonia, 39 with moderate, and 16 with mild cases. The basic characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. Significant differences were observed in age and gender among the three groups, while no statistical differences were found in other demographic characteristics, medical history, or hospital stay duration. However, patients with severe infections had higher WBC counts, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios, and elevated CRP levels compared to those with mild infections (all p < 0.05).

Multivariable logistic analysis

Biologically plausible variables and variables showing significant association (p < 0.05) with CAPP in linear regression were included in the regression model (at α = 0.15) to model the probability of a positive correlation between our explanatory variables and high CAPP. In clinical practice, the whole lung infection is an important biological variable in COVID-19 [27]. The result revealed that whole lung infection percentage had a significant correlation with high CAPP in COVID-19 pneumonia patients [OR (95% confidence interval (CI)): 1.092(1.012,1.178)] (Table 3).

Diagnostic value of the most lobe infection percentage index for predicting high CAPP

The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for whole lung infection percentage in predicting high CAPP was 0.662 (95% CI: 0.561–0.752, p = 0.003) in COVID-19 pneumonia patients, with an optimal cut-off point of 21.0% for whole lung infection percentage (sensitivity: 38.30%, specificity: 89.09%) (Fig. 2).

Central artery pulse pressure and all-cause mortality

During a median follow-up of 15 days (interquartile range: 10–30 days), 18 (16.98%) patients died from any cause. In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis, CAPP ≥ 50 mm Hg associated with death risk [hazard ratios (HR), 0.263 (95% CI, 0.073–0.945), p = 0.041] (Table 4). Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Fig. 3) illustrated that patients with high CAPP had significantly higher all-cause mortality compared to those with normal CAPP (log-rank p < 0.05). In contrast, high AVI was not significantly associated with all-cause mortality after adjustments.

Inter-rater agreement for AI assessment

A strong inter-rater agreement between reviewers for the AI assessment of whole lung and most lobe infection percentages were observed, with ICC values of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.89–0.98, p < 0.05) and 0.96 (95% CI: 0.91–0.98, p < 0.05), respectively, between the two reviewers (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate the association between arterial stiffness index and pneumonia, as quantitatively assessed through CT scans, along with all-cause mortality in individuals with COVID-19 pneumonia infection. A key finding of our research was that high CAPP levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality. Furthermore, the infection percentage of whole lung was found to be independently associated with elevated levels of CAPP, a marker indicative of central arterial stiffness.

CAPP, which depends on the stiffness of large arteries and pulse wave reflection, provides a better indication of the loading conditions for heart, brain, and other organs [12]. The elastic properties of conduit arteries vary along the arterial tree, with proximal arteries being more elastic and distal arteries becoming stiffer. The typical stiffness gradient between central and peripheral arteries can be diminished or even reversed with aging or disease [32]. Therefore, using brachial pressure as a measure of central pressure is often inaccurate, and arterial stiffness is just one of several factors influencing CAPP. In our study, a high CAPP (≥ 50 mm Hg) was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality.

The evaluation of vascular damage involves both functional and morphological assessments [33]. AVI, a non-invasive physiological marker used to identify abnormal vascular function, can be more sensitive than morphological assessments, particularly in the acute phase that cause vascular damage [34, 35]. Moreover, both endothelial dysfunction and increased arterial stiffness have been linked to reduced survival during the acute phase of COVID-19 [36]. The current study similarly found that upregulation of AVI and CAPP, indicative of endothelial dysfunction, was associated with increasing disease severity.

Additionally, it is noteworthy that the COVID-19-related cytokine storm has been hypothesized as a consequence of aging, leading to the term “inflamm-aging” [37]. Both arterial stiffness and the severity of COVID-19, along with associated inflammation, have a direct, bidirectional correlation, with each accelerating the other [38].

Previous studies have shown that the quantitative analysis indicators of the severity and distribution in pneumonia lesions correlates with negative prognoses in COVID-19 patients [39, 40]. In our study, regression results indicated that, after adjusting for age, which is a significant risk factor for poor outcomes in COVID-19 patients, the whole infectious percentage was related to high CAPP. Moreover, it demonstrated high specificity in predicting elevated CAPP levels. Combined alterations in cardio-pulmonary function may result from a variety of mechanisms, including the hypothesis of diminished hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction [41]. Significantly, diminished hypoxic vasoconstriction has been associated with increased cardiac output and post-capillary pulmonary hypertension, ultimately resulting in lung stiffness and promoting a deleterious cycle between the heart and lungs.

One reason for the low sensitivity but high specificity in predicting high CAPP levels in our study may be the numerous factors that influence CAPP increases, with COVID-19 infection being only one of them. The elasticity of the arteries, particularly in the large arteries, significantly influences blood pressure. The primary indicator of the elastic state of the vessel wall is the degree of distensibility under force. This arterial distensibility is directly proportional to volume accretion and inversely proportional to the increase in transmural pressure. In clinical practice, this relationship can be expressed as arterial distensibility being equal to cardiac stroke volume divided by PP. When the stroke volume remains constant and arterial elasticity decreases, the average arterial pressure remains relatively unchanged. This is due to the fact that systolic and diastolic pressures change in opposite directions, while PP increases. However, in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia, a higher proportion of whole lung infection percentage correlated with an increased likelihood of high CAPP. This finding suggests that the severity of COVID-19 pneumonia significantly impacted CAPP, indicating that high CAPP may be a specific manifestation associated with the disease.

The strengths of this study are as follows: Firstly, this study, for the first time, elucidates the association between arterial stiffness index and pneumonia, as AI quantitatively assessed through CT scans, and its correlation with all-cause mortality in individuals suffering from COVID-19 pneumonia infections. Secondly, inflammation of COVID-19 pneumonia had a direct correlation with CAPP and increased risk of all-cause mortality. Thirdly, this study provided a new methodology for non-invasive assessment of CAPP using a brachial oscillometric blood pressure cuff.

Despite these significant findings, our study has several limitations. Firstly, the small sample size and the study’s confinement to a single center may limit the generalizability of the results. Therefore, future studies may require multi-center validation to strengthen the findings. Secondly, there were variations in age within the cohort’s distribution of COVID-19 severity, which highlights the need for further investigation in larger and more diverse populations. To reduce the risk of bias, we adjusted for age and used endpoint events to ensure the reliability of the analysis. However, the predictive value of central BP in predicting cardiovascular events deserves future confirmation in long-term, large-scale therapeutic trials.

Conclusion

High CAPP, but not high AVI, demonstrated independent prognostic value for all-cause mortality in patients with COVID-19 viral pneumonia infection. Assessment of this parameter could help in risk estimation and improve diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in infection diseases.
