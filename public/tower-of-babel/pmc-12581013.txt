How many monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities are optimal for recovery from fatigue among shift-working nurses? A 1-month sleep log observational study to test anchor nighttime sleep in Japan
Authors: Tomohide Kubo, Hiroki Ikeda, Shuhei Izawa, Yuki Nishimura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven that nighttime sleep has more recuperative power than daytime sleep, ensuring the former is important to recovery from work, especially for shift-working nurses. In this 1-month sleep log study, we preliminarily examined how the number of monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities relates to fatigue-related outcomes among shift-working nurses.Methods526 nurses were required to record their daily sleep duration immediately after waking from sleep for 1 month. We analysed the associations among the four sleep groups (ie, ≤ 12, 13–19, 20–24 and≥24 nighttime-sleep opportunities per month) and fatigue-related outcomes. A one-way analysis of covariance or generalised linear mixed model (factor: sleep group) was conducted using R (version 4.2.2.) to test the association.ResultsSignificant differences among the four sleep groups were obtained using the excessive fatigue symptom inventory (EFSI), Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI) and the numbers of near-miss incidents. Moreover, significant linear trends were observed in the EFSI and PSQI scores.ConclusionsShift-working nurses who experienced 12 or fewer monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities showed worse fatigue-related outcomes than those who had sufficient monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities. Therefore, more attention should be paid to ensuring monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities when creating a shift schedule.Keywords: Occupational Medicine, Mental Health, Accident Prevention

Introduction

Nighttime-sleep opportunities could be a significantly powerful recovery opportunity for shift workers engaged in any type of shift work.1 However, shift-working nurses cannot get sufficient sleep at night due to their work schedule and, therefore, have greater potential health risks than daytime workers.2 Studies regarding shift-work strategies to guard against fatigue and sleepiness have focused on shift duration, shift system and direction of rotation.3 Collectively, attention has been paid to the management of work, but available knowledge about the management of off-job time is lacking. Notably, little is known about the optimal number of monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities for fatigue recovery among shift-working nurses. Knauth and Rutenfranz’s nine recommendations, which have been considered the ‘gold standard’ to improve shift-work conditions, did not directly address the importance of nighttime sleep.4

Minors and Waterhouse proposed the ‘anchor sleep’ hypothesis, which concerns a guidepost for the body clock.5 According to the hypothesis, half of the sleep time individuals needed was taken during the fixed core time, and the remaining sleep was taken during other periods. Consequently, the circadian rhythm could be maintained. Of course, there are other potential factors to maintain/disrupt the circadian rhythms, such as light exposure6 and social rhythms.7 Moreover, given the hypothesis mainly targets day-to-day variations (ie, relatively acute effects) in circadian rhythms, chronic effects are not considered. Despite these limitations, the hypothesis inspired our research question of how many nighttime-sleep opportunities per month shift-working nurses should have to recover from work. If the empirical data were obtained to solve the research question, the results could be informative and innovative in the creation of a healthy shift schedule for shift-working nurses. As mentioned, previous studies focused on the management of shift schedules. However, there are many types of shift schedules and work and sleep vary even when shift-working nurses work under the same shift schedule. Therefore, shift-working strategy is very diverse and complicated. Meanwhile, focusing on nighttime sleep is very simple, and there is no need to consider the difference in shift type. In this 1-month observational study, we used a sleep log to preliminarily examine the association between the number of monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities and fatigue-related outcomes among shift-working nurses.

Methods

In total, 1201 nurses were recruited through an online market research company, MyVoice Communications, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) currently working as a nurse; (2) currently working a shift-work schedule or daytime schedule and (3) currently not being treated for any diseases. Of the eligible 726 nurses, 526 (mean age=36.8, SD=8.4 and female=442) completed the survey. The Institutional Review Board of the National Institute of Occupational Safety and Health, Japan approved this study (H3030). Power estimation was conducted using G*power (ver. 3.1.9.7_143). Given that the PSQI used in this study is the golden standard to measure sleep quality, sample size was calculated based on the PSQI difference between shift types.8 Expecting a small effect on sleep quality (ie, effect size was set as 0.25) and power of 0.80 with two covariates, the power analyses showed that a minimum of 179 participants were needed. However, the sleep log is self-reported, and the participants were required to record their sleep for 1 month every day. Given those risk factors regarding data loss, we estimated that 40% of respondents could complete the sleep log without data loss. Therefore, we recruited approximately 700 participants based on the eligible criteria.

Participants were asked to record their daily sleep duration on a paper-based sleep log in 30-min intervals for 1 month. Additionally, they were instructed to record their sleep duration immediately after waking from sleep throughout the period of this study.

The excessive fatigue symptom inventory (EFSI) was developed to evaluate the potential risks of overwork-related death by using the prodromes from workers’ compensation cases.9 The EFSI is a questionnaire for evaluating excessive fatigue. On this scale, respondents were instructed to rate 26 symptoms of excessive fatigue on a 4-point scale (1=never to 4=frequently).

The Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI) is a validated scale to measure sleep disturbance.10 The PSQI consists of 19 items with scores ranging from 0 to 21, with a higher score indicating worse sleep quality.

In terms of occupational safety, near-miss incidents were counted in this study. A near-miss incident is defined as an event or circumstance that could have resulted or did result in unnecessary harm to a patient.11 The number of near-miss incidents was measured using a single question: ‘How many times have you had a near-miss incident in the previous 1 month?’

The EFSI score, PSQI score and the number of near-miss incidents were retrospectively answered after the participants completed their 1-month sleep log.

Based on the sleep log, nighttime-sleep opportunities were operationally defined as (1) sleep duration of 4 hours or more and (2) sleep between 22:00 and 8:00. Then, the numbers of total sleep opportunities (ie, regardless of time) and nighttime-sleep opportunities per month were, respectively, calculated. The nurses were divided into four groups based on the quartile distribution of the number of monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities (ie, ≤ 12, 13–19, 20–24 and≥24). The EFSI and PSQI scores were analysed using a one-way analysis of covariance (factor: sleep group). In contrast, the data on near misses were analysed using a generalised linear mixed model because those data assume Poisson’s distribution of error. Then, the sleep group was set as a fixed effect, and the participant was set as a random effect. Shift schedules and the number of total sleep opportunities per month were adjusted as covariates. Statistical analysis was conducted using R version 4.2.2.

Patient and public involvement

We did not involve patients or members of the public directly in designing, conducting and interpreting the results of the study or writing the manuscript.

Discussion

The primary findings suggest that fatigue-related outcomes were significantly related to the monthly number of nighttime-sleep opportunities. Notably, nurses who had fewer monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities showed more fatigue-related outcomes. This tendency was more obvious in nurses who had 12 or fewer nighttime-sleep opportunities per month. The dose–response relationships between nighttime-sleep opportunities and fatigue observed in this study were consistent with previous research, suggesting that sleep plays an essential role in recovering from work.12 In particular, the preliminary findings highlight that ensuring nighttime-sleep opportunities could be important for healthy and safe work among shift-working nurses.

In this research area, attention has been paid to the management of work schedules, such as shift rotation, shift length and shift type.13 Meanwhile, empirical data regarding the management of the off-duty period have been lacking.14 However, issues of quick returns (ie,<11-hour shift interval between shifts) are becoming critical. Of them, it has been reported that increased quick returns are prospectively linked to increased occupational accidents after 2 years among nurses.15 Also, an interventional study showed the positive influence of extending shift intervals from 31 to 55 hours after two consecutive night shifts on fatigue and stress among shift-working nurses.16 Therefore, ensuring shift intervals between shifts is becoming the norm in shift-work strategy in this area. However, the strategies only focused on the length of the recovery period but did not consider the circadian rhythm aspect of the recovery period. The well-known fact that nighttime sleep is of better quality than daytime sleep is based on polysomnography research.17 Therefore, providing nighttime-sleep opportunities is linked to accounting for circadian rhythm in addition to caring about protecting shift intervals. In other words, it could be a more effective way to manage shift-work schedules than only ensuring shift intervals. In fact, our preliminary data support the importance of nighttime-sleep opportunities for shift-working nurses. At the same time, it suggests novel insights that making a schedule plan for shift work while caring about the monthly number of nighttime-sleep opportunities could be an effective way to prevent severe fatigue, sleepiness and occupational accidents among shift-working nurses. However, the association observed here could be mediated by factors, such as job stress, depression and burnout. Therefore, future investigations are required to consider the effects of mediators.18

Some limitations should be noted. First, given that the monthly number of nighttime-sleep opportunities was recorded using a self-reported assessment, it should be objectively assessed in future research. Particularly, the sleep log method used in this study could not measure the indicators of sleep quality, such as slow wave sleep and rapid eye movement sleep. Studies have reported a significant mean total sleep time difference between sleep log and actigraphy.19 Therefore, wearable devices, such as ring-type devices,20 would be useful to objectively measure daily sleep during long periods. Second, our data were collected following a 1-month observational method, making it difficult to address the causal links. Therefore, a longer observation period with a larger sample is needed to test our preliminary data. Consequently, it is very informative to reveal specific criteria regarding the monthly number of nighttime-sleep opportunities needed to recover from fatigue due to work among shift-working nurses. Namely, the research question may be better called the ‘anchor nighttime-sleep hypothesis’, inspired by the anchor sleep hypothesis.5 Third, this study focused on a relatively short-term effect (ie, fatigue, sleep disturbance and occupational accident) on shift-working nurses. Even more important is examining the causal links between monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities and long-term effects, such as sick leave and disease among shift-working nurses. Of course, the causal links should be considered coupled with other potential factors to maintain/disturb circadian rhythms. Finally, the definition and question methodology regarding near-miss incident should be noted. As far as we know, there was no validated questionnaire to measure the near-miss incident. In our country, the term ‘near miss’ is spreading, especially in hospitals, but there is potential to misunderstand the term in other countries. A validated questionnaire is needed to equally measure the incident regardless of country differences.

Concluding remarks

Our primary findings suggest that shift-working nurses who had fewer monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities showed worse fatigue-related outcomes than those who had sufficient monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities. In particular, 12 or fewer monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities were associated with deteriorated fatigue-related outcomes, even when the type of shift schedules (ie, two- or three-shift system) is adjusted. The criteria shown here could provide a new insight into healthy and safe shift schedules among shift-working nurses. However, a longer observation period with a larger sample is required in future work. Collectively, the message from this study is that more attention should be paid to ensuring sufficient monthly nighttime-sleep opportunities in the creation of a shift schedule.
