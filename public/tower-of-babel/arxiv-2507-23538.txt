Quantum-enhanced dark matter detection using Schrödinger cat states
Pan Zheng, Yanyan Cai, Bin Xu, Shengcheng Wen, Libo Zhang, Zhongchu Ni, Jiasheng Mai, Yanjie Zeng, Lin Lin, Ling Hu, Xiaowei Deng, Song Liu, Jing Shu, Yuan Xu, Dapeng Yu

Abstract
Quantum metrology enables sensitive dark matter detection, particularly using nonclassical states, such as Schrödinger cat states featuring sub-Planck interference structures in microwave cavities. Here, we report the first experimental application of four-component Schrödinger cat states within a high-quality superconducting microwave cavity to detect dark photons, a potential dark matter candidate. We demonstrate an 8.1-fold enhancement in the signal photon rate and constrain the dark photon kinetic mixing angle to an unprecedented ϵ < 7.32 × 10 − 16 \displaystyle\epsilon<7.32\times 10^{-16} near 6.44 GHz (26.6 \upmu \displaystyle\upmu eV). By employing a parametric sideband drive to actively tune the cavity frequency, we achieve dark photon searches and background subtraction across multiple frequency bins, yielding a sensitivity at the 10 − 16 \displaystyle 10^{-16} level within a 100 kHz bandwidth. Our Schrödinger’s cat-assisted detection (SCaD) scheme demonstrates a substantial improvement over previous results, promising potential implications in quantum-enhanced searches for new physics.

II
Dark photon detection scheme using cat states

The wave-like bosonic DPDM manifests as a coherent oscillating microwave field that weakly couples to Standard Model photons in a cavity via the kinetic mixing parameter
\displaystyle\epsilon
. Under the resonance condition, the DPDM field acts as a displacement operator
\displaystyle\hat{D}(\beta)=\exp(\beta a^{\dagger}-\beta^{*}a)
within the DM coherence time
\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{DM}}
, where
\displaystyle\beta
is proportional to
\displaystyle\epsilon
and the signal integration time
\displaystyle\tau
, and
\displaystyle a^{\dagger}
,
\displaystyle a
are the cavity ladder operators. The weak coupling leads to a small displacement (
\displaystyle|\beta|\ll 1
), which converts a vacuum state into a superposition
\displaystyle|\psi\rangle\propto|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{2})
. By measuring the single-photon excitation probability
\displaystyle P_{1}=|\langle 1|D(\beta)|0\rangle|^{2}\approx|\beta|^{2}
, we can extract
\displaystyle\beta
and thus constrain
\displaystyle\epsilon
.

To enhance the DM-induced signal, the cavity can be prepared in nonclassical states, such as Schrödinger cat states
Schrodinger1935
;
Schleich1991
;
Gerry1997
.
While conventional two-component cat states – superpositions of phase-opposed coherent states
\displaystyle|\phi_{\pm}\rangle\propto|\alpha\rangle\pm|-\alpha\rangle
– have shown advantages in quantum metrology and information processing
Munro2002
;
Vlastakis2013
;
Ofek2016
;
Grimm2020
;
Reglade2024
;
Pan2025
, their susceptibility to single-photon loss introduces a critical limitation.
Specifically, both the DM-induced displacement and single-photon loss lead to parity flipping between even (
\displaystyle|\phi_{+}\rangle
) and odd (
\displaystyle|\phi_{-}\rangle
) cat states, making it difficult to distinguish the DM-induced signals from relaxation errors in background noise.

To address this challenge, we propose to use a four-component cat state, also known as a compass state
Zurek2001
, defined as

|\phi_{0}(\alpha)\rangle\propto|\alpha\rangle+|-\alpha\rangle+|i\alpha\rangle+|-i\alpha\rangle

(1)

for DM detection. This state exhibits fourfold rotational symmetry and ultrafine interference structures in phase space, making it extremely sensitive to small displacements induced by the DM field, as illustrated in Fig.
1
(a). The photon number occupations of this state follow a Poisson distribution restricted to
\displaystyle n=0\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
, referred to as “doubly even” parity. Figure
1
(b) depicts the photon number distributions of the compass state
\displaystyle|\phi_{0}(\alpha)\rangle
, which evolves into a superposition state
\displaystyle|\psi\rangle\propto|\phi_{0}(\alpha)\rangle+\beta\alpha^{*}|\phi_{1}(\alpha)\rangle-\beta^{*}\alpha|\phi_{3}(\alpha)\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{2})
under a weak DM-induced displacement (
\displaystyle|\beta|\ll 1
). Here,
\displaystyle|\phi_{1/3}(\alpha)\rangle\propto|\alpha\rangle-|-\alpha\rangle\mp i|i\alpha\rangle\pm i|-i\alpha\rangle
represents the left- and right-odd parity cat states with photon numbers restricted to
\displaystyle n=1\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
and
\displaystyle n=3\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
subspaces, respectively.
We project the evolved cat state onto the “left-odd” parity state
\displaystyle|\phi_{1}(\alpha)\rangle
, with the projection probability given by

P_{\alpha}(\beta)=\left|\langle\phi_{1}(\alpha)|\hat{D}(\beta)|\phi_{0}(\alpha)\rangle\right|^{2}\approx|\beta|^{2}|\alpha|^{2},

(2)

thereby demonstrating a signal enhancement by a factor of
\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}
compared to the vacuum-state detection scheme. This approach provides an inherent error discrimination since single-photon loss converts
\displaystyle|\phi_{0}(\alpha)\rangle
to
\displaystyle|\phi_{3}(\alpha)\rangle
rather than
\displaystyle|\phi_{1}(\alpha)\rangle
. This property enables robust separation of the DM-induced signals from the single-photon-loss errors in the background noise through generalized modulo-4 photon-number parity measurements.

It is noted that although the
\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}
-enhancement of the compass state comes with a reduction factor of
\displaystyle 1/|\alpha|^{2}
in its coherence lifetime, the signal rate remains unaffected. This is because the coherence time is primarily limited by the DM bandwidth rather than the lifetime of the compass states due to the long coherent lifetime of the cavity. This mechanism is similar to that of the Fock-state-based DM detection scheme
Agrawal2024
.

V
Dark photon search with frequency tuning

Although the cat-state-based DP detection scheme achieves an unprecedented sensitivity with on-resonance DP signals, the sensitivity to off-resonant signals drops quickly due to the finite bandwidth of the DM lineshape. To address this challenge, we propose dynamically in-situ tuning the cavity frequency and performing DP search experiments across various frequency bins to subtract the background noise.

The cavity frequency tuning is achieved by using a detuned parametric sideband drive
Zeytinoglu2015
;
Rosenblum2018
, with the Hamiltonian described as

H_{d}=\frac{\Omega}{2}a^{\dagger}|g\rangle\langle f|e^{i\Delta t}+\mathrm{h.c.}.

(6)

Here,
\displaystyle\Omega
denotes the drive strength, and
\displaystyle\Delta
represents the frequency detuning between the drive frequency
\displaystyle\omega_{d}
and the resonant frequency
\displaystyle\omega_{fg}-\omega_{c}
, where
\displaystyle\omega_{fg}
is the frequency difference between the qubit ground state
\displaystyle|g\rangle
and the second excited state
\displaystyle|f\rangle
. As illustrated in Fig.
4
(a), this drive couples the levels
\displaystyle|n,f\rangle
to
\displaystyle|n+1,g\rangle
, with
\displaystyle n
representing the photon number in the cavity.

For a sufficiently detuned sideband drive (
\displaystyle\Delta\gg\Omega
), the drive Hamiltonian can be approximated as a time-independent interaction
\displaystyle H_{d}^{\mathrm{eff}}={\Omega^{2}}/{(4\Delta)}\,a^{\dagger}a
with the qubit remaining in the ground state, resulting in a drive-induced frequency shift
\displaystyle{\Omega^{2}}/{(4\Delta)}
to the cavity frequency
\displaystyle\omega_{c}
. As a demonstration in our experiment, the cavity
\displaystyle|0\rangle\rightarrow|1\rangle
transition frequency is measured and plotted as a function of
\displaystyle\Delta
in Fig.
4

Based on the cavity frequency tuning within a bandwidth of about 100 kHz, we conduct independent DP search experiments at each frequency bin using the vacuum state as the initial probe state, with the experimental sequence shown in Fig.
4
(c). The tuning step between adjacent frequency bins is set to approximately 6 kHz, comparable to the DM linewidth, to ensure that the signal appears in one bin. We first characterize the detector efficiency and false positive probability across 16 frequency bins within a 100 kHz bandwidth, with the results shown in Fig.

\displaystyle 10^{-4}

We then perform the DP search experiments using an integration time
\displaystyle\tau\sim T_{1}^{c}
at each frequency bin to extract the accumulated excitation populations using the hidden Markov analysis. As the DM signal cannot be distinguished from background noise in a single bin, we subtract the latter using its weighted average and variance across different bins (see Supplement). This background correction allows us to tighten the upper limits on the DM signal within the 100 kHz frequency range by removing the noise contribution from the total measurement. Figure

\displaystyle 10^{-16}

VI
Conclusions and outlook

In this work, we present a novel quantum-enhanced approach to detect bosonic DPDM using four-component Schrödinger cat states in a high-quality superconducting microwave cavity. This approach is of great advantage because of the inherent error discrimination of the DM-induced displacements from single-photon-loss errors in the background noise. We experimentally demonstrate an 8.1-fold enhancement in the signal photon rate over vacuum states and set a constraint on the DP kinetic mixing angle
\displaystyle\epsilon<7.32\times 10^{-16}
. Further enhancement is anticipated using cat states with mean photon numbers exceeding 100
Vlastakis2013
;
milul2023
. Furthermore, we employ a parametric sideband drive to actively tune the cavity frequency, enabling DP search across different frequency bins and subtraction of incoherent background noise. This method achieves a sensitivity on the order of
\displaystyle 10^{-16}
within a 100 kHz bandwidth near 6.5 GHz. When integrated with frequency-tunable qubits
Chen:2022quj
;
zhao2025
;
kang2025
;
Braggio2025
and sensor networks that exploit quantum coherence
Chen:2023swh
;
Shu:2024nmc
, the demonstrated protocol offers a scalable route towards probing DM with significantly improved sensitivity in the microwave regime.

I
Theoretical concept of the detection scheme using cat states

A coherent state
\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle
is an eigenstate of the annihilation operator
\displaystyle a
with eigenvalue
\displaystyle\alpha
, as expressed by
\displaystyle a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle
. This state can be prepared by applying a displacement operator
\displaystyle\hat{D}(\alpha)=e^{\alpha a^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}a}
to the vacuum state, yielding
\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle=\hat{D}(\alpha)|0\rangle
. The coherent state exhibits a Poisson distribution in the Fock basis, parameterized by
\displaystyle\alpha
:

|\alpha\rangle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle.

(S1)

The product of two displacement operators yields an identity:

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\beta)\hat{D}(\alpha)=e^{(\beta\alpha^{*}-\beta^{*}\alpha)/2}\hat{D}(\alpha+\beta)
, which allows us to compute the inner product of two coherent states:
\displaystyle\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}(|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}-2\beta^{*}\alpha)}
.

A two-comoponent Schrödinger cat state is a superposition of two coherent states with opposite parameters:
\displaystyle|\phi_{\pm}\rangle=\mathcal{N}_{\pm}(|\alpha\rangle\pm|-\alpha\rangle)
, where
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\pm}={1}/{\sqrt{2(1\pm e^{-|\alpha|^{2}})}}\approx{1}/{\sqrt{2}}
is the normalization constant. Cat states contain only even (odd) Fock states:

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\phi_{+}\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=2\mathcal{N}_{+}e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{2n}}{\sqrt{(2n)!}}|2n\rangle,

(S2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\phi_{-}\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=2\mathcal{N}_{-}e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{2n+1}}{\sqrt{(2n+1)!}}|2n+1\rangle.

(S3)

Thus, these states have definite parity:
\displaystyle\hat{P}|\phi_{\pm}\rangle=\pm|\phi_{\pm}\rangle
with
\displaystyle\hat{P}=e^{i\pi a^{\dagger}a}
. Applying the annihilation operator changes the parity of a cat state:
\displaystyle a|\phi_{\pm}\rangle\propto|\phi_{\mp}\rangle
.

A compass state (four-component cat state) is a superposition of four coherent states:

|\phi_{0}\rangle=\mathcal{N}_{4,0}(|\alpha\rangle+|i\alpha\rangle+|-\alpha\rangle+|-i\alpha\rangle),

(S4)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{4,0}
denotes the normalization constant. The other three compass states can be obtained by successive applcation of the annihilation operator:

\displaystyle\displaystyle a|\phi_{0}\rangle\propto|\phi_{3}\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{4,3}(|\alpha\rangle+i|i\alpha\rangle-|-\alpha\rangle-i|-i\alpha\rangle),

\displaystyle\displaystyle a|\phi_{3}\rangle\propto|\phi_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{4,2}(|\alpha\rangle-|i\alpha\rangle+|-\alpha\rangle-|-i\alpha\rangle),

\displaystyle\displaystyle a|\phi_{2}\rangle\propto|\phi_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}_{4,1}(|\alpha\rangle-i|i\alpha\rangle-|-\alpha\rangle+i|-i\alpha\rangle),

and finally, applying
\displaystyle a
to
\displaystyle|\phi_{1}\rangle
returns to
\displaystyle|\phi_{0}\rangle
, such that
\displaystyle a|\phi_{1}\rangle\propto|\phi_{0}\rangle
with all
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{4,j}\approx 1/2
for
\displaystyle\alpha\gg 1
.

The photon number distribution of compass states exhibits definite modulo 4 properties:

|\phi_{j}\rangle=4\mathcal{N}_{4,j}e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{4n+j}}{\sqrt{(4n+j)!}}|4n+j\rangle,

(S5)

thereby identifying them as eigenstates of the operator
\displaystyle\hat{P}_{4}=e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}a^{\dagger}a}
with
\displaystyle\hat{P}_{4}|\phi_{j}\rangle=e^{ij\pi/2}|\phi_{j}\rangle
.

In general, an
\displaystyle M
-component cat state is a superposition of
\displaystyle M
coherent states with parameters equidistantly placed on a circle of radius
\displaystyle|\alpha|
:

|\phi_{M,j}\rangle=\mathcal{N}_{M,j}\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}e^{-ij\varphi_{k}}|e^{i\varphi_{k}}\alpha\rangle,

(S6)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{M,j}\approx M^{-1/2}
, and
\displaystyle\varphi_{k}=2\pi k/M
. These states are eigenstates of the operator
\displaystyle\hat{P}_{M}=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{M}a^{\dagger}a}
. In the Fock basis, they are expressed as:

|\phi_{M,j}\rangle=M\mathcal{N}_{M,j}e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{Mn+j}}{\sqrt{(Mn+j)!}}|Mn+j\rangle.

(S7)

The time evolution of the aforementioned states, in the zero-temperature limit, can be described by the master equation in the standard Lindblad form:

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[H,\rho]+\frac{\kappa}{2}[2a\rho a^{\dagger}-a^{\dagger}a\rho-\rho a^{\dagger}a],

(S8)

where
\displaystyle\kappa
denotes the single-photon-loss rate, equivalent to the inverse of the single-photon lifetime:
\displaystyle\kappa={1}/{T_{1}^{c}}={\omega}/{Q}
. Solving the master equation reveals that the amplitude of a coherent state decays as follows:
\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle\rightarrow|\alpha(t)\rangle=|\alpha e^{-\kappa t}\rangle
.

For cat states, the density matrix of the
\displaystyle j
-th cat state
\displaystyle\rho_{M,j}=|\phi_{M,j}\rangle\langle\phi_{M,j}|
during the time evolution is given by:

\rho_{M,j}(t)=\mathcal{N}_{M,j}^{2}\sum_{k,l}e^{-ij(\varphi_{k}-\varphi_{l})-|\alpha|^{2}(1-e^{-\kappa t})(1-e^{i(\varphi_{k}-\varphi_{l})})}|e^{i\varphi_{k}-\kappa t/2}\alpha\rangle\langle e^{i\varphi_{l}-\kappa t/2}\alpha|.

(S9)

The transition probability between different cat states is expressed as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{j\rightarrow l}(t)=\text{Tr}[\rho_{j}(t)\rho_{l}(0)]=M\mathcal{N}_{M,j}^{2}\mathcal{N}_{M,l}^{2}\sum_{k}e^{-i(j-l)\varphi_{k}-|\alpha|^{2}(1-e^{-\kappa t})(1-e^{i\varphi_{k}})}.

(S10)

Taking
\displaystyle j=l
and expanding to the first order in time, we obtain:
\displaystyle P_{jj}(t)\approx 1-|\alpha|^{2}\kappa t
, which indicates that the cat state has a reduced lifetime of
\displaystyle\tau_{\alpha}={T_{1}^{c}}/{|\alpha|^{2}}
.

It is convenient to use the Wigner function
\displaystyle W(z)={2}\text{Tr}[\hat{P}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(z)\rho\hat{D}(z)]/{\pi}
to visualize coherent states and their superpositions. For a state displaced by
\displaystyle\beta
, the density matrix undergoes a transformation:
\displaystyle\rho\rightarrow\hat{D}(\beta)\rho\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\beta)
, and the corresponding Wigner function shifts by
\displaystyle\beta
with
\displaystyle W^{\prime}(z)=W(z-\beta)
in phase space

The Wigner function for the vacuum state
\displaystyle\ket{0}
is a standard Gaussian wave packet centered at the origin of the complex plane with
\displaystyle W(z)=\frac{2}{\pi}e^{-2|z|^{2}}
. For a coherent state
\displaystyle\ket{\alpha}=\hat{D}(\alpha)\ket{0}
, the Wigner function is also a Gaussian wave packet, but centered at
\displaystyle z=\alpha
with
\displaystyle W(z)=\frac{2}{\pi}e^{-2|z-\alpha|^{2}}
. Thus, the sensitivity for measuring the amplitude of weak displacements using the vacuum state (or coherent states) is limited by the standard quantum limit (SQL) due to the unit Gaussian width of the wave packet.

Now, consider the cat states
\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{\pm}(\alpha)}
with
\displaystyle\alpha
real, positive, and large enough to approximate the normalization factors as
\displaystyle 1/\sqrt{2}
. In this limit, the Wigner function is given by:

W(z)=\frac{2}{\pi}\left\{e^{-2|z-\alpha|^{2}}+e^{-2|z+\alpha|^{2}}\pm 2e^{-2|z|^{2}}\cos[4\alpha\text{Im}z]\right\},

(S11)

which comprises two Gaussian wave packets centered at
\displaystyle z=\pm\alpha
, accompanied by an interference term between them, as shown in Fig.
S1
(a). This interference fringe is an important nonclassical resource for improving the measurement sensitivity of small displacements along the imaginary axis.

Here, we compare the signal strength for the same displacement
\displaystyle\beta
between the vacuum state, a single-photon Fock state, and a cat state with opposite parity:

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\braket{1|\hat{D}(\beta)|0}|^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\approx\left|\langle 1|\beta a^{\dagger}|0\rangle\right|^{2}=|\beta|^{2},

(S12)

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\braket{\phi_{\mp}|\hat{D}(\beta)|\phi_{\pm}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\approx\left|\langle\phi_{\mp}|\beta a^{\dagger}-\beta^{*}a|\phi_{\pm}\rangle\right|^{2}=4|\alpha|^{2}(\text{Im}\beta)^{2}.

(S13)

The response to the imaginary component of the displacement is enhanced by an additional factor of
\displaystyle 4|\alpha|^{2}
, facilitating signal detection.

Figure S1
:
Cavity Wigner functions
of (a) cat state, and (b) compass state for
\displaystyle\alpha=\sqrt{10}
.

A limitation of employing cat states is that the enhancement is restricted to the imaginary part of
\displaystyle\beta
, and decoherence effects may eventually compromise the signal, as both the dark matter (DM) signal and background noise can alter the parity. These challenges can be mitigated by using a compass state. As shown in Fig.
S1
(b), the Wigner function of a compass state consists of four Gaussian wave packets centered at
\displaystyle z=i^{k}\alpha
, with interference fringes emerging in both directions at the center for small
\displaystyle\beta
. Using
\displaystyle|\phi_{0}\rangle
as the initial state and
\displaystyle|\phi_{1}\rangle
as the signal indicator, the signal probability can be expressed as:

\left|\langle\phi_{1}|\hat{D}(\beta)|\phi_{0}\rangle\right|^{2}\approx\left|\langle\phi_{1}|\beta a^{\dagger}|\phi_{0}\rangle\right|^{2}=|\alpha|^{2}|\beta|^{2},

(S14)

which exhibits an enhancement factor of
\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}
for displacement
\displaystyle\beta
with an arbitrary phase. The decoherence effect primarily induces transitions from
\displaystyle|\phi_{0}\rangle
to
\displaystyle|\phi_{3}\rangle
, rather than affecting
\displaystyle|\phi_{1}\rangle
, as is evident from the transition probability from
\displaystyle|\phi_{0}\rangle
to
\displaystyle|\phi_{1}\rangle
:

P_{0\rightarrow 1}(t)=\text{Tr}[\rho_{0}(t)\cdot\rho_{1}(0)]=\frac{1}{6}\left(|\alpha|^{2}\kappa t\right)^{3}+O\left(t^{4}\right),

(S15)

which is two orders of magnitude smaller than
\displaystyle P_{0\rightarrow 3}\approx|\alpha|^{2}\kappa t
for small times (
\displaystyle t\ll 1/|\alpha|^{2}\kappa
).

II
Dark matter signal

In the interaction basis, the dark photon (DP)
\displaystyle A^{\prime\mu}
with mass
\displaystyle m_{A^{\prime}}
couples to the Standard Model photon
\displaystyle A^{\mu}
through the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}_{A^{\prime}}\supset\epsilon m^{2}_{A^{\prime}}A^{\prime\mu}A_{\mu},

(S16)

which defines an effective current
\displaystyle J_{\text{eff}}^{A^{\prime}\,\mu}=\epsilon m^{2}_{A^{\prime}}A^{\prime\mu}
.
Here,
\displaystyle\epsilon
denotes the kinetic mixing coefficient.

The electromagnetic fields within a cavity of volume
\displaystyle V
are quantized as bound states. In the Coulomb gauge, the vector potential is parameterized as

\vec{A}=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_{c,n}}}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\vec{\epsilon}_{n}(\vec{x}\,)e^{i\omega_{c,n}\,t}+h.c.,

(S17)

where the summation extends over different modes labeled by
\displaystyle n
. Here,
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{n}
(
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}
) represents the annihilation (creation) operator for a mode characterized by eigenfrequency
\displaystyle\omega_{c,n}
and wave function
\displaystyle\vec{\epsilon}_{n}(\vec{r})
. The wave functions must satisfy the orthogonality condition:
\displaystyle\int_{\text{V}}\vec{\epsilon}_{m}^{\ *}\,\vec{\epsilon}_{n}\,dV=\delta_{mn}
. For the analysis focusing on the
\displaystyle\rm{TM}_{010}
mode of the cylindrical cavity considered in the main text, the mode label
\displaystyle n
is omitted in the subsequent discussions.

To characterize the cavity signal induced by the DM field, one can either model the DM field by a density matrix diagonal in momentum space
Derevianko:2016vpm
, or consider it as a superposition of
\displaystyle N\gg 1
classical freely propagating plane waves
Foster:2017hbq

\vec{A^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\vec{A^{\prime}}_{i}(t,\vec{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{A^{\prime}}_{i,0}\vec{\epsilon}_{i}\cos(\omega_{i}t-\vec{k}_{i}\cdot\vec{x}+\varphi_{i})

(S18)

where
\displaystyle{A^{\prime}}_{i,0}
denotes the effective magnitude of gauge potential vector, uniform across all particles and related to the local DM density in the galaxy by
\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}={N}m_{\mathrm{DM}}^{2}{A^{\prime}}_{i,0}^{2}/2=0.4\text{ GeV/cm}^{3}
. Here,
\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{DM}}=m_{A^{\prime}}
represents the mass of the DP particle,
\displaystyle\vec{\epsilon}_{i}
is the isotropically distributed polarization vector,
\displaystyle\omega_{i}=m_{\mathrm{DM}}(1+v_{i}^{2}/2)
is the Compton frequency,
\displaystyle\vec{k_{i}}=m_{\mathrm{DM}}\vec{v}_{i}
is the galactic wave vector associated with the apparatus motion through the DM halo, and
\displaystyle\varphi_{i}
is a random phase distributed uniformly from 0 to
\displaystyle 2\pi
. The speed of the DM field follows the Maxwellian distribution according to the standard halo model of cold DM

f_{\mathrm{DM}}(v)=\frac{v}{\sqrt{\pi}v_{vir}v_{g}}e^{-(v+v_{g})^{2}/v_{vir}^{2}}(e^{4vv_{g}/v_{vir}^{2}}-1),

(S19)

where
\displaystyle v_{vir}\approx 220
km/s is the virial velocity and
\displaystyle v_{g}\approx 232
km/s is the speed of the Sun relative to the halo rest frame. The energy distribution is then given by

f_{\mathrm{DM}}(\omega_{\mathrm{DM}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2({\omega_{\mathrm{DM}}}{m_{\mathrm{DM}}}-m_{\mathrm{DM}}^{2})}}f_{\mathrm{DM}}\left[\sqrt{2\left(\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{DM}}}{m_{\mathrm{DM}}}-1\right)}\right].

(S20)

Note that the DM line profile is strictly zero for
\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{DM}}<m_{\mathrm{DM}}
, and the maximum
\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{DM}}(\omega_{m})\approx 10^{6}/m_{\mathrm{DM}}
occurs at
\displaystyle\omega_{m}=m_{\mathrm{DM}}(1+v_{m}^{2}/2)\approx(1+3\times 10^{-7})m_{\mathrm{DM}}
for
\displaystyle v_{m}\approx 237
km/s.

The interaction Hamiltonian between the DP background and the cavity is given by:

\displaystyle\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{int}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\text{V}}\,\vec{A}\cdot\vec{J}_{\rm eff}\,dV=\epsilon m^{2}_{\mathrm{DM}}\sum_{i}\int_{\text{V}}\,\vec{A}\cdot\vec{A}^{\prime}_{i}\,dV

(S21)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon m^{2}_{\mathrm{DM}}}{\sqrt{2\omega_{c}}}\sum_{i}\,A^{\prime}_{i,0}\sqrt{V_{\rm eff,i}}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\,e^{i(\omega_{c}-\omega_{i})\,t-i\varphi_{i}}+h.c..

(S22)

where the rotating wave approximation is applied and
\displaystyle V_{\rm eff,i}
is the effective volume defined as

V_{\rm eff,i}=\left|\int_{V}\vec{\epsilon}\cdot\vec{\epsilon}_{i}e^{-i\vec{k}_{i}\cdot\vec{x}}dV\right|^{2}.

(S23)

In the presence of a DP field
\displaystyle\vec{A^{\prime}}_{i}(t,\vec{x})=A^{\prime}_{i,0}\vec{\epsilon}_{i}\cos(\omega_{i}t-\vec{k}_{i}\cdot\vec{x}+\varphi_{i})
, the Hamiltonian can be interpreted as a coherent drive with detuning
\displaystyle\delta_{i}=\omega_{i}-\omega_{c}
and amplitude
\displaystyle\Omega_{i}=\epsilon m^{2}_{\mathrm{DM}}A^{\prime}_{i,0}\sqrt{V_{\rm eff,i}/{2\omega_{c}}}
.

Under resonance condition
\displaystyle\delta_{i}=0
, the DM field acts as a displacement operator, expressed as
\displaystyle e^{-iH_{i}\tau}=\hat{D}(\beta)
with
\displaystyle\beta=-i\Omega_{i}e^{i\varphi_{i}}\tau
. In the off-resonant case, the time evolution of the cavity state is given by

|\psi_{i}(t)\rangle=\hat{D}(z_{i})e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}K_{i}(\tau)d\tau}e^{-ia^{\dagger}a\omega_{c}t}|\psi(0)\rangle,

(S24)

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle z_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\Omega_{i}e^{-i\omega_{c}t-i\varphi_{i}}({1-e^{-i\delta_{i}t}})/{\delta_{i}},

(S25)

\displaystyle\displaystyle K_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Omega_{i}^{2}({1-\cos{\delta_{i}t}})/{\delta_{i}}.

(S26)

For a cavity initialized in the ground state
\displaystyle|\psi(0)\rangle=|0\rangle
and assuming
\displaystyle\Omega_{i}\ll 1
, the state evolves to
\displaystyle|\psi_{i}(t)\rangle\approx c_{0,i}|0\rangle+c_{1,i}|1\rangle
, where
\displaystyle c_{0,i}\approx 1
and
\displaystyle c_{1,i}\approx e^{-i(\omega_{i}+\omega_{c})t/2-i\varphi_{i}}\Omega_{i}\frac{\sin(\delta_{i}t/2)}{\delta_{i}/2}
. The total effect of the DP background drives the cavity from the vacuum state to
\displaystyle|\psi(t)\rangle\approx c_{0}|0\rangle+c_{1}|1\rangle
with
\displaystyle c_{0}\approx 1
and
\displaystyle c_{1}=\sum_{i}c_{1,i}
.

The ensemble average of an observable
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
is defined as

\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle=\prod_{i,j,k}\int d^{3}\vec{v}_{i}f_{\mathrm{DM}}(\vec{v}_{i})\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi_{j}}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{2}\hat{k}}{4\pi}\mathcal{O}.

(S27)

Then the effective volume of a cylindrical cavity can be estimated as
\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}=\langle V_{\rm eff,i}\rangle\approx 0.23V
and the excitation probability of the cavity state can be calculated as

p_{1}(t)=\langle|c_{1}|^{2}\rangle\equiv N\Omega^{2}g(t),

(S28)

where
\displaystyle N\Omega^{2}=\epsilon^{2}m^{2}_{\mathrm{DM}}\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}V_{\rm eff}/{\omega_{c}}
and

g(t)=\int d\omega f_{\mathrm{DM}}(\omega)\left(\frac{\sin((\omega-\omega_{c})t/2)}{(\omega-\omega_{c})/2}\right)^{2}.

(S29)

Here, we take
\displaystyle\omega_{c}=\omega_{m}=(1+3\times 10^{-7})m_{\mathrm{DM}}
, where
\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{DM}}
reaches its maximum
\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{DM}}(\omega_{m})\approx 0.98\times 10^{6}/m_{\mathrm{DM}}
.
When
\displaystyle(\omega-\omega_{m})t\ll 1
, we can approximate
\displaystyle\sin((\omega-\omega_{m})t/2)\approx(\omega-\omega_{m})t/2
, leading to
\displaystyle g(t)\approx t^{2}
, which indicates coherent signal buildup within the DM coherence time.

Conversely, for sufficiently long integration time, we can approximate

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sin((\omega-\omega_{m})t/2)}{(\omega-\omega_{m})/2}\right)^{2}\approx 2\pi t\delta(\omega-\omega_{m})
,
resulting in
\displaystyle g(t)\propto\tau_{\mathrm{DM}}t
, which indicates coherence loss due to the dispersion caused by variations in the velocities of its constituent DM particles.
The DM coherence time is then given by

\tau_{\mathrm{DM}}=2\pi f_{\mathrm{DM}}(\omega_{m})\approx\frac{2\pi\times 10^{6}}{m_{\mathrm{DM}}}

As depicted in Fig.
S2
, the function
\displaystyle g(t)
exhibits a piecewise behavior, with a transition occurring around
\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{DM}}
:

g(t)\simeq\begin{cases}t^{2}&\text{if }0<t<\tau_{\mathrm{DM}},\\
\tau_{\mathrm{DM}}t&\text{if }t>\tau_{\mathrm{DM}}.\end{cases}

(S30)

Similarly, when initializing the cavity with the compass state
\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{0}(\alpha)}
, we can obtain that the state transition probability to
\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{1}(\alpha)}
is given by

p_{0\rightarrow 1}(t)=N\Omega^{2}|\alpha|^{2}g(t),

(S31)

which exhibits an enhancement factor of
\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}
compared to Eq. (
S28
).

Parity measurement calibration

Parity measurements are essential for preparing and characterizing cat states, as well as for repeated quantum non-demolition (QND) measurements of the final cavity state after integrating the DM signal. This process employs the Ramsey interferometry method, which includes a qubit
\displaystyle\pi/2
pulse, a delay time
\displaystyle t
, another qubit
\displaystyle-\pi/2
pulse, and a final qubit state projection. The two
\displaystyle\pi/2
pulses act as beam-splitters for the qubit states, while the free evolution during the delay time entangles the qubit and the cavity states. This process corresponds to a sinusoidal photon number filter
Deng2024
operation
\displaystyle P_{S}(\theta)\approx\sum_{n}{\cos{\frac{n\theta}{2}}|n\rangle\langle n|}
, which transforms arbitrary input cavity state
\displaystyle|\psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle
to output state
\displaystyle|\psi_{\mathrm{out}}\rangle\propto P_{S}(\theta)|\psi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle
with
\displaystyle\theta=\chi t
. The delay time is set to
\displaystyle\pi/\chi
for mod-2 parity measurements and
\displaystyle\pi/2\chi
for mod-4 parity measurements, corresponding to
\displaystyle P_{S}(\pi)
and
\displaystyle P_{S}(\theta/2)
, respectively. These delay times are experimentally optimized by performing parity measurements on a known cavity state with a certain parity.

Ideal parity measurements retrieve only the parity information without disturbing the cavity states. However, in practice, these measurements may introduce additional relaxation errors in the cavity states. The QND nature of the parity measurements can be assessed by measuring the cavity coherence times with interleaved repeated parity measurements
Sun2014
. The decoherence of cat states is a combination of the bare energy relaxation
\displaystyle\kappa_{s}
and a demolition probability
\displaystyle p_{d}
. The demolition probability can be determined by fitting the measured coherence times against the repetition interval between parity measurements, as listed in Table

Cavity drive pulse calibration

A coherent drive pulse applied to the cavity is used to generate a coherent state
\displaystyle|\beta\rangle
, which is useful for both cat state preparation and DM detector calibration. Calibrating the drive pulse is essential to determine the injected photon numbers
\displaystyle|\beta|^{2}
.

The calibration procedure is illustrated in Fig.
S5
(a). The cavity is first displaced to a coherent state
\displaystyle|\beta\rangle
by a drive pulse with a fixed duration and nominal amplitude
\displaystyle A
. The qubit spectrum is then measured using the photon-number-selective
\displaystyle\pi
pulse followed by qubit readout. The measured qubit excited probability
\displaystyle P_{e}
at the detuned frequency
\displaystyle\omega-\omega_{q}=-n\chi
corresponds to the photon number population
\displaystyle P_{n}
, which follows a Poisson distribution:

P_{n}=|\langle n|\beta\rangle|^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{-|\beta|^{2}}\frac{|\beta|^{2n}}{n!}.

(S34)

The photon number
\displaystyle|\beta|^{2}

\displaystyle-\chi

\displaystyle P_{1}/P_{0}

\displaystyle|1\rangle

\displaystyle\pi

Measurement error is assessed using readout signals at frequencies far from those corresponding to possible excited photon number components, such as at detuned frequencies
\displaystyle-3\chi

\displaystyle+2\chi

\displaystyle+6\chi

\displaystyle\pi

Kim2023
;
Cai2023

\displaystyle P_{0}
and
\displaystyle P_{1}
increase gradually as the duration is shortened, with the
\displaystyle y

After subtracting the two error contributions, the corrected
\displaystyle P_{1}/P_{0}

\displaystyle|\beta|^{2}

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle|e\rangle

\displaystyle|g\rangle

\displaystyle|g\rangle

\displaystyle|e\rangle

\displaystyle|\phi_{+}\rangle
or
\displaystyle|\phi_{-}\rangle

\displaystyle\pi/2

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\phi_{+}\rangle\otimes|g\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rightarrow|\phi_{0}\rangle\otimes|g\rangle+|\phi_{2}\rangle\otimes|e\rangle,

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\phi_{-}\rangle\otimes|e\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rightarrow|\phi_{1}\rangle\otimes|e\rangle+|\phi_{3}\rangle\otimes|g\rangle.

\displaystyle|\phi_{0}\rangle
involves first generating
\displaystyle|\phi_{+}\rangle
using a Ramsey sequence with wait time
\displaystyle t=\pi/\chi
and then projecting onto target compass state using another Ramsey sequence with wait time
\displaystyle t=\pi/2\chi

VIII
Hidden Markov model

In the ideal scenario, repeated parity measurements on the cavity would alter the states of the cavity and qubit based solely on their current states.
However, these states are susceptible to errors and may change randomly during free evolution and measurements.
Consequently, observed random sequences may not necessarily indicate that the evolved cavity is in the even-parity states
\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{0}}
or
\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{2}}
.
To account for potential measurement errors and assess the probability of the evolved cavity state, we adopt a three-layer Hidden Markov Model (HMM) approach, as presented in
Dixit2021
;
Agrawal2024
;
zhao2025
.

The cavity and qubit states are treated as hidden variables, with their evolution described by a transition matrix
\displaystyle T
that captures all possible changes. We assume that the cavity state switches between four compass states,
\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{j}}\in\{\ket{\phi_{0}},\ket{\phi_{1}},\ket{\phi_{2}},\ket{\phi_{3}}\}
. The transition probability due to single-photon loss from
\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{j}}
to
\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{j-1}}
is
\displaystyle P_{j,j-1}=1-e^{-|\alpha|^{2}t_{m}/T_{1}^{c}}
, while that due to spontaneous heating from
\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{j}}
to
\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{j+1}}
is
\displaystyle P_{j,j+1}=\bar{n}_{c}(1-e^{-|\alpha|^{2}t_{m}/T_{1}^{c}})
. Here, the interval between parity measurements
\displaystyle t_{m}=1.9\,\mu
s is primarily limited by the readout time (
\displaystyle 1.2\,\mu
s) and the time required to actively reset the readout resonator
McClure2016
to the ground state (
\displaystyle 0.3\,\mu
s). The coherence time of the compass state
\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{j}(\alpha)}
is approximately as
\displaystyle T_{1}^{c}/|\alpha|^{2}
with
\displaystyle T_{1}^{c}
representing the cavity single-photon lifetime.
\displaystyle\bar{n}_{c}
denotes the steady-state thermal population in the cavity. The high-order multi-photon processes (either relaxation or heating) are neglected, giving
\displaystyle P_{j,j\pm 2}=0
. The probability of no errors is then given by
\displaystyle P_{jj}=1-P_{j,j-1}-P_{j,j+1}
.

Next, we assume that the qubit state switches between the ground state
\displaystyle\ket{g}
and the first excited state
\displaystyle\ket{e}
, neglecting higher-order excitations. The qubit can relax, undergo spontaneous heating, or experience dephasing, with probabilities
\displaystyle P^{\downarrow}_{eg}=1-e^{-t_{m}/T_{1}^{q}}
,
\displaystyle P^{\uparrow}_{ge}=\bar{n}_{q}(1-e^{-t_{m}/T_{1}^{q}})
and
\displaystyle P^{\phi}=1-e^{-t_{p}/T_{2}^{q}}
, respectively. Here,
\displaystyle t_{p}
is the wait time in the parity measurement. Qubit errors are characterized by
\displaystyle P_{ge}=P^{\uparrow}_{ge}+P^{\phi}
and
\displaystyle P_{eg}=P^{\downarrow}_{eg}+P^{\phi}
. The no-error probabilities are given by
\displaystyle P_{gg}=1-P_{ge}
and
\displaystyle P_{ee}=1-P_{eg}
. Here,
\displaystyle T_{1}^{q}
and
\displaystyle T_{2}^{q}
represent the qubit’s energy relaxation and coherence lifetimes, respectively, and
\displaystyle\bar{n}_{q}
denotes the qubit’s steady-state thermal population.

\displaystyle t_{p}=\pi/2\chi

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{0}}

\displaystyle t_{p}
.

The cavity frequency tunability in this work is achieved using a detuned parametric sideband drive
\displaystyle H_{d}=\frac{\Omega}{2}a^{\dagger}|g\rangle\langle f|e^{i\Delta t}+\mathrm{h.c.}

\displaystyle|n,g\rangle

\displaystyle|n-1,f\rangle

\displaystyle\Delta\approx 0

\displaystyle|n-1,f\rangle\leftrightarrow|n,g\rangle

\displaystyle\Omega/2\pi=0.51

\displaystyle\Omega^{2}/(4\Delta)

In the DP search experiment with tuning cavity frequency, the tuning step is set to
\displaystyle\Delta\omega\approx 6
kHz to match the DM linewidth, ensuring that the DM signal appears at most in one of 16 frequency bins. Assuming that the background noise remains unchanged within the 16 bins, we perform a constant fit. This introduces an attenuation factor
\displaystyle\eta_{\mathrm{fit}}
to the potential signal. Any signal entering the fit function will be reduced by a factor of
\displaystyle 1/16
, resulting in
\displaystyle\eta_{\mathrm{fit}}=93.75\%
.

The background occupation at each frequency
\displaystyle\omega_{i}
is defined as

n_{i}=\frac{N_{\text{mea},i}}{\eta_{i}N_{\text{trial},i}},

(S44)

where
\displaystyle N_{\text{mea},i}
is the number of excitations measured,
\displaystyle N_{\text{trial},i}
is the number of trials, and
\displaystyle\eta_{i}
is the detection efficiency. The background is estimated using a weighted average and variance:

\bar{n}=\frac{\sum_{i}N_{\text{trial},i}n_{i}}{\sum_{i}N_{\text{trial},i}},\quad\sigma_{n}^{2}=\frac{\sum_{i}N_{\text{trial},i}(n_{i}-\bar{n})^{2}}{\sum_{i}N_{\text{trial},i}}.

(S45)

and plotted in Fig.
S13
. The signal rate is defined as

p_{i}=\frac{n_{i}-\bar{n}}{n_{\text{ref},i}}\quad\mathrm{with}\quad n_{\text{ref},i}=\eta_{\text{fit}}n_{\text{sig}}(m_{\rm{DM}},f_{i},\epsilon=1),

(S46)

where the reference signal rate
\displaystyle n_{\text{ref},i}
accounts for the attenuation factor
\displaystyle\eta_{\text{fit}}
. The signal power
\displaystyle n_{\text{sig}}
is given by

n_{\text{sig}}=\epsilon^{2}\rho_{\mathrm{DM}}V_{\text{eff}}m_{\mathrm{DM}}T^{1}_{c,i}\mathcal{F}(m_{\mathrm{DM}},\omega_{i}),

(S47)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(m_{\mathrm{DM}},\omega_{i})
is the normalized frequency spectrum of the DPDM, and we neglect the decoherence uncertainty by choosing the integration time to be
\displaystyle T^{1}_{c,i}
.

Using error propagation, the variance of the signal rate is calculated as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{p_{i}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sigma_{n_{i}}}{n_{\text{ref},i}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{n_{i}-\bar{n}}{\sigma_{n_{i}}}\right)^{2}\left[\left(\frac{\sigma_{T^{1}_{c,i}}}{T^{1}_{c,i}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\sigma_{V_{\text{eff}}}}{V_{\text{eff}}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sigma_{n_{i}}}{n_{\text{ref},i}}\right)^{2}.

(S48)

which is dominated by the statistical uncertainty from the background estimation.

Since the reference signal
\displaystyle n_{\text{ref},i}
is defined for
\displaystyle\epsilon=1
, the probability function can be expressed in terms of
\displaystyle\epsilon

(S49)

where
Const
is a normalization factor ensuring
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}P(p_{i}|\epsilon)d\epsilon^{2}=1

\displaystyle 90\%
