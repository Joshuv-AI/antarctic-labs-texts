GPATCH11 ortholog Sap34 regulates pre-mRNA splicing by interacting with early spliceosomal complexes in Schizosaccharomyces pombe
Authors: Ingrid Cipakova, Laura Olivia Karika, Lucia Hronska, Tomas Selicky, Bogdan Iaparov, Miloslav Karhanek, Lenka Kohutova, Peter Barath, Lubos Cipak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPre-mRNA splicing is an essential step in gene expression regulation. It is mediated by the spliceosome, a large ribonucleoprotein complex that undergoes dynamic structural and compositional rearrangements during each splicing cycle. Although the mechanisms of splicing and the roles of main spliceosomal components are well defined, the identities and functions of transiently associated spliceosomal proteins remain incompletely understood. Here, we investigated the molecular function of the poorly characterized G-patch domain–containing protein SPAC6F6.19 (herein Sap34, for spliceosome-associated protein of 34 kDa) in Schizosaccharomyces pombe, an ortholog of human GPATCH11. Using affinity purification and a yeast two-hybrid assay, we analyzed its interactome and identified its interaction partners. In addition, long-read sequencing was employed to assess Sap34-dependent changes in splicing efficiency. We found that Sap34 forms a complex with components of the U2 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein (snRNP) and the U4/U6 × U5 tri-snRNP, which are required for early spliceosome assembly and activation. Furthermore, we defined the interaction specificity of Sap34 with splicing proteins, demonstrating the importance of its C-terminal region for binding to Sap61 and Ini1, and of its G-patch domain for interaction with ATP-dependent RNA helicase Prp43, suggesting that G-patch domain of Sap34 may contribute to the regulation of Prp43 activity in early spliceosomes. Notably, we showed that deletion of sap34 leads to a global reduction in splicing efficiency, predominantly associated with increased intron retention. Together, these findings identify Sap34 as a previously unrecognized and important G-patch domain–containing protein regulating the early steps of pre-mRNA splicing in fission yeast.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57150-9.Keywords: G-patch protein, Sap34, SPAC6F6.19, Splicing factor, Pre-mRNA splicing, Fission yeastSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cell biology, Genetics, Molecular biology

Introduction

Pre-mRNA splicing plays a central role in controlling gene expression in eukaryotic cells. It is carried out by the spliceosome, a large and dynamic ribonucleoprotein complex comprising more than 170 proteins. Some of these proteins form stable complexes with small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), giving rise to small nuclear ribonucleoprotein particles (snRNPs). In contrast, others associate with the spliceosome only transiently to coordinate and regulate its function1,2.

Throughout the splicing cycle, the spliceosome undergoes dynamic rearrangements during assembly, activation, catalytic progression, and disassembly. The assembly occurs de novo on each intron and is accompanied by extensive RNA–protein remodeling. The process begins with the U1 snRNP recognizing the 5′ splice site and the U2 snRNP recognizing the branch site3. It has been proposed that a network of auxiliary splicing factors, which can vary depending on splice-site strength and intron architecture, facilitates the recruitment and stabilization of U1 and U2 snRNPs, thereby preventing incorrect splice-site selection4. Following this, the early spliceosome assembly proceeds through the formation of the E complex, in which U1 snRNP binds the 5′ splice site and splicing factor 1 (SF1/BBP) recognizes the branch point sequence. Then, the U2 auxiliary factor heterodimer U2AF65/U2AF35 stabilizes recognition of the polypyrimidine tract and the 3′ splice site5–8. Subsequent recruitment of the U2 snRNP, mediated by the SF3A and SF3B subcomplexes, displaces SF1 and results in formation of the A complex, thereby establishing intron definition and licensing incorporation of the U4/U6 × U5 tri-snRNP2,9,10. The binding of the U4/U6 × U5 tri-snRNP then converts the A complex into the pre-B complex, followed by extensive conformational and compositional rearrangements that activate the spliceosome and give rise to catalytically competent complexes, in which the two transesterification reactions of splicing occur2,11–16. In the later stages of splicing, the spliceosome promotes exon ligation and intron lariat release, after which individual snRNPs are recycled for subsequent rounds of splicing1,2,17–22.

Although the core architecture of snRNPs and the overall pathway of spliceosome assembly and remodeling are conserved from yeast to humans, early spliceosomal complexes in yeasts are generally more transient. Recent single‑molecule spectroscopy studies have shown that this behavior reflects intrinsic properties of the yeast snRNPs, most notably the more dynamic association of U1 with the 5′ splice site, rather than differences in splice‑site strength, as S. cerevisiae introns contain highly conserved splicing signals23,24. These properties favor rapid spliceosome progression and shift regulatory strategies toward dynamic remodeling and stringent splicing quality control rather than prolonged stabilization of early assembly intermediates. Consistent with this, the intron lariat spliceosome (ILS) complex is particularly abundant in the fission yeast S. pombe, reflecting efficient catalytic progression coupled with slower and tightly regulated post-catalytic disassembly and recycling25–27.

To ensure the fidelity of pre-mRNA splicing, cells have developed regulatory mechanisms that coordinate spliceosome dynamics and safeguard accurate splice-site selection. Among the proteins that participate in this regulation are G-patch domain–containing proteins, which function primarily as cofactors of spliceosomal RNA helicases. Although these proteins share several common features, they display substantial diversity in size, domain architecture, and subcellular localization. Through specific protein–protein and protein–RNA interactions, these proteins play important roles in RNA metabolism. By stimulating the ATPase and helicase activities of their partner helicases, they facilitate specific structural rearrangements within the spliceosome at defined stages of the splicing cycle. This process plays a crucial role in maintaining splicing accuracy and efficiency28–31. Recently, it has been shown that aberrant splicing intermediates generated during early spliceosome transitions are eliminated by quality-control mechanisms that monitor spliceosome progression and direct defective substrates to discard pathways. Central to this surveillance mechanism is the G-patch domain–containing protein GPATCH1, which recruits the helicase DHX35 to stalled or improperly assembled spliceosomes, thereby facilitating the recognition and removal of defective pre-mRNAs and preventing erroneous intermediates from progressing through the catalytic cycle32. Similarly, in S. pombe, the G-patch domain–containing protein Gpl1 was found to cooperate with the putative DEAD/DEAH-box helicase Gih35 (ortholog of human DHX35) to promote efficient recognition and discard of aberrant splicing intermediates, highlighting the evolutionary conservation of G-patch–helicase–mediated splicing quality control mechanisms33–37. Another well-studied G-patch protein involved in pre-mRNA splicing in S. pombe is Ntr1. This conserved spliceosomal factor modulates the activity of the DEAH-box RNA helicase Prp43. It contains an N‑terminal G‑patch motif that directly interacts with Prp43 and stimulates its ATPase and helicase activities, thereby coupling its function to the disassembly of post‑spliceosomal complexes and the release of excised intron lariats21,27,38,39. In addition to activating Prp43, Ntr1 functions together with Ntr2 as part of the NTR complex, targeting Prp43 to specific substrates and regulating its activity. This prevents premature disassembly of correctly assembled spliceosomes while facilitating the discard of defective or stalled intermediates37,40,41. Collectively, accumulating evidence indicates that many spliceosomal regulators, including G-patch domain–containing proteins, associate with the spliceosome transiently and at specific stages of the splicing cycle. As a result, they are often underrepresented in conventional biochemical purification methods, and their regulatory roles in pre-mRNA splicing remain concealed.

In this study, we investigated the molecular function of the poorly characterized protein Sap34 in the fission yeast S. pombe. A detailed analysis of the Sap34 interactome revealed that this G-patch domain–containing protein associates with splicing factors characteristic of early spliceosomal complexes, suggesting a role in the initial stages of spliceosome assembly and regulation. Functional analyses of pre-mRNA splicing demonstrated that deletion of sap34 leads to a global reduction in splicing efficiency, mainly evidenced by increased intron retention. Together, our findings define Sap34 as a previously unrecognized regulator of pre-mRNA splicing in fission yeast and highlight its importance in controlling the early stages of the splicing cycle.

Materials and methods

Strains, plasmids, and media

The genotypes of strains, plasmids, and primers used in this study are listed in Table S1. S. pombe strains carrying gene deletions or expressing TAP-tagged proteins were generated as described previously42,43. S. pombe cells were grown in complete YE + 5S medium at 32 °C (5 g/L yeast extract, 3% (w/v) glucose, 0.1 g/L L-leucine, 0.1 g/L L-lysine, 0.1 g/L L-histidine, 0.1 g/L uracil, and 0.15 g/L adenine). During selection, nourseothricin or geneticin was added to the YE + 5S medium at 100 µg/mL or 150 µg/mL, respectively. EMM-N medium used for sporulation of S. pombe cells consisted of 3 g/L potassium hydrogen phthalate, 2.2 g/L Na2HPO4, 2% (w/v) glucose, 0.15 g/L adenine, 0.1 g/L uracil, 0.1 g/L L-histidine, 0.1 g/L L-lysine, and 0.1 g/L L-leucine, supplemented with salts, vitamins, and minerals44. Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain PJ69-4 A was grown on YPAD medium at 30 °C (10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L peptone; 2% (w/v) glucose, and 0.08 g/L adenine). Synthetic dropout medium lacking leucine and tryptophan (SD-L,W) was used to select co-transformants. This medium was prepared using yeast nitrogen base without amino acids (6.7 g/L) and ammonium sulfate (5 g/L), supplemented with a complete amino acid mixture lacking leucine and tryptophan, and 2% (w/v) glucose as the carbon source. To detect protein–protein interactions, histidine (SD-L,W,H) or adenine (SD-L,W,A) was additionally omitted as required45. For solid media, 2% (w/v) agar was included.

Western blotting

Schizosaccharomyces pombe cells expressing Sap34-TAP, Cwf28-TAP, and Pxr1-TAP were grown at 32 °C to an OD600 of 0.7–0.8. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (4,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) and resuspended in ice-cold IPP150 buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8; 150 mM NaCl; 10% glycerol; 0.1% NP-40) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and Complete protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Protein extracts were prepared by vortexing cells with glass beads (Ø 0.4–0.6 mm; 2 × 5 min at 2,850 rpm, 4 °C, with 1 min cooling on ice between cycles). Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation (20,000 × g, 5 min, 4 °C), and proteins were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE before transfer onto PVDF membranes (0.45 μm pore size). Membranes were blocked in 5% (w/v) non-fat dry milk prepared in PBS-T (phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20) and incubated with a peroxidase–anti-peroxidase (PAP) antibody (Dako) diluted 1:30,000 in 2.5% (w/v) non-fat dry milk in PBS-T. Signals were visualized using Pierce ECL Plus Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Scientific) and detected on Amersham Hyperfilm™ ECL (GE Healthcare).

Tandem affinity purification

Cells expressing Sap34-TAP protein were grown in 16 L of YE + 5 S medium at 32 °C to mid-log phase (OD600=0.7–0.8) and harvested by centrifugation (4,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). Cell pellets were frozen in liquid nitrogen. Yeast cell powder (40 g) was generated using a SPEX SamplePrep 6770 Freezer/Mill (SPEX SamplePrep). Proteins were extracted in IPP150 buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and Complete protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails, using a 1 g of yeast cell powder per 3 mL of IPP150 buffer ratio. The protein extract was clarified by centrifugation (40,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C) and subjected to tandem affinity purification as described previously43,46. Briefly, 0.5 mL of IgG Sepharose 6 Fast Flow beads (GE Healthcare) were equilibrated in IPP150 buffer, incubated with protein extract for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation, and subsequently washed with 20 bead volumes of IPP150 buffer. Beads were then equilibrated with five bead volumes of TEV cleavage buffer (TCB: 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8; 150 mM NaCl; 10% glycerol; 0.1% NP-40; 0.5 mM EDTA; 1 mM DTT). On-bead cleavage was performed in 2 mL of TCB supplemented with 400 U of Turbo TEV protease (MoBiTec GmbH) for 2 h at 16 °C. The resulting eluate was supplemented with 6 µL of 1 M CaCl₂ and diluted with 6 mL of Calmodulin Binding Buffer 1 (CBB1: 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8; 150 mM NaCl; 10% glycerol; 0.1% NP-40; 1 mM imidazole; 1 mM magnesium acetate; 2 mM CaCl₂; 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol). Subsequently, 0.15 mL of Calmodulin Sepharose 4B beads (GE Healthcare) equilibrated in CBB1 were added, and binding was allowed to proceed for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Beads were washed with 10 bead volumes of CBB1 followed by five bead volumes of Calmodulin Binding Buffer 2 (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8; 150 mM NaCl; 1 mM magnesium acetate; 2 mM CaCl₂; 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol). Bound proteins were eluted stepwise using one bead volume of elution buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8; 150 mM NaCl; 1 mM magnesium acetate; 2 mM EGTA; 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol). Eluted fractions were analyzed by 8% SDS–PAGE and visualized by silver staining to check the elution profile47. Peak fractions were pooled and subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis.

Mass spectrometry analysis

The samples were reduced with 5 mM DTT, then alkylated with 15 mM iodoacetamide, and the reaction was quenched with an additional 5 mM DTT. Parallel digestions with trypsin, GluC, and chymotrypsin were performed overnight (16 h) in the presence of phosphatase inhibitors at 37 °C, 37 °C, and 25 °C, respectively. Digestions were further processed and measured separately. Peptides were purified by microtip C18 SPE. The separation of peptides was performed by nano-HPLC Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The samples were loaded onto a trap column (PepMap100 C18, 300 μm × 5 mm, 5-µm particle size; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated by an EASY-Spray C18 analytical column with integrated nanospray emitter (PepMap RLSC C18, 75 μm × 500 mm, 2-µm particle size, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a 60-min gradient (3–43% B) at a flow rate of 250 nL/min. The two mobile phases used were: 0.1% formic acid (v/v) and 80% acetonitrile (v/v) with 0.1% formic acid. Eluted peptides were sprayed directly into the Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific). The emitter potential was set to 2.3 kV, the transfer capillary temperature to 250 °C, and the S-lens RF level to 60%. Spectral datasets were collected in a data-dependent mode using the Top15 strategy to select precursor ions for fragmentation48. Precursors were measured in the mass range 300–1700 m/z with a resolution of 120,000, and fragmented by the HCD mechanism with a normalized collision energy of 25. MS/MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 15,000. Each sample was measured in technical duplicates. The resulting datasets were processed together using MaxQuant version 1.6.17.0 with the built-in Andromeda search engine49. The searched database consisted of S. pombe proteomes (UniProt, downloaded 24.1.2025, isoforms included and PomBase, downloaded 23.1.2025) and a predicted N-terminally extended Sap34 amino acid sequence (with TAP tag) based on the newly defined sequence of the sap34 allele. The cleavage specificities of proteases were set as follows: trypsin – C-terminal of R and K also if P follows; GluC – C-terminal of E and D; chymotrypsin – C-terminal of F, Y, L, W, and M. The number of tolerated missed cleavages was 2 for trypsin, 4 for GluC, and 3 for chymotrypsin. Carbamidomethylation (C) was set as a permanent modification, and oxidation (M), acetylation (protein N-terminus), and phosphorylation (STY) were set as variable modifications. The precursor ion mass tolerance was set to 20 ppm in the first search and to 4.5 ppm in the main search upon recalibration, and the fragment tolerance was 20 ppm. The target-decoy database strategy was used to control for false identifications, and the false discovery rate of 1% was imposed at both the peptide-spectrum-match and protein group levels. Identified protein groups and Sap34 peptides are listed in Table S2. Peptide coverage across the Sap34 protein was visualized using Protein Sequence Coverage Map software50.

Functional analysis of Sap34 interactome

To explore functional interactions among proteins comprising the Sap34 complex, Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis was performed using the STRING database51. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network for the Sap34 complex was generated using STRING v12.0 (http://string-db.org), applying a high-confidence minimum interaction score threshold of 0.700.

Yeast two-hybrid assay

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) constructs were generated using plasmids from the Matchmaker GAL4 Two-Hybrid System (Clontech). Sequences coding proteins or their domains were cloned in-frame either into the pAS2-1 vector to produce fusions with the GAL4 DNA-binding domain (BD), which carries the TRP1 selection marker, or into the pGADT7 vector to generate fusions with the GAL4 activation domain (AD), which carries the LEU2 selection marker (Table S1). The Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain PJ69-4 A was co-transformed with the corresponding BD and AD plasmid constructs using the lithium acetate method as previously described52. Co-transformants were selected on SD-L,W medium. Colonies growing on SD-L,W medium were replica-plated onto SD medium lacking leucine, tryptophan, and histidine (SD-L,W,H), or onto SD medium lacking leucine, tryptophan, and adenine (SD-L,W,A) to assess reporter gene activation. Plates were incubated at 30 °C for 3–4 days.

Quantitative β-galactosidase assay

β-galactosidase activity was quantified using o-nitrophenyl-β-d-galactopyranoside (ONPG) as the substrate. Briefly, 20 mL overnight cultures were grown in SD-L,W medium in 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. Cultures were diluted into 20–25 mL of YPD to an initial OD600 of 0.18–0.25 and incubated at 30 °C with shaking for 3–5 h until mid-log phase (OD600=0.5–0.8). ONPG was prepared fresh at 4 mg/mL in Z buffer (60 mM Na2HPO4, 40 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM MgSO4). Cells (1.5 mL) were harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 60 s, washed once with 1.5 mL of Z buffer, and resuspended in 0.3 mL of Z buffer. Cell suspensions were subjected to 7 freeze–thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen and a 37 °C water bath to ensure cell lysis. For each sample, 0.05 mL of lysate was combined with 0.35 mL of Z buffer supplemented with β-mercaptoethanol (final concentration, 39 mM), and the reaction was initiated by adding 0.08 mL of ONPG solution. After incubation at 30 °C until a yellow color developed, the reactions were terminated with 0.2 mL of 1 M Na2CO3, and the elapsed time was recorded. Samples were centrifuged to remove debris (12,000 × g, 3 min), and supernatants were transferred to cuvettes for measurement of OD420 relative to a blank. β-galactosidase activity was calculated as: β-galactosidase units = 1,000 × OD420/(t × V × OD600), where t is the reaction time (min), V is 0.1 mL multiplied by the concentration factor (5), and OD600 corresponds to the absorbance of 1 mL of culture at 600 nm. Six independent biological replicates were analyzed for each co-transformant.

Prediction of protein structures and interactions using AlphaFold

The newly characterized amino acid sequence of Sap34 was used as input for AlphaFold2 to generate its three-dimensional monomeric structure. Subsequently, AlphaFold-Multimer was applied to model the interactions between Sap34 and its partner proteins. All predicted structures were accompanied by confidence metrics, including per-residue pLDDT scores and predicted aligned errors, which were used to assess both the accuracy of the monomeric model and the reliability of the predicted inter-protein interfaces53,54.

Random spore analysis

Spore viability was assessed by random spore analysis. Diploid wild-type (sap34+/sap34+) and heterozygous diploid deletion (sap34+/sap34Δ) strains of S. pombe were induced to sporulate on EMM-N medium at 25 °C for 3 days. Sporulated cultures were treated with 0.4% glucuronidase to digest remaining vegetative cells (25 °C, 10 h). Approximately 5000 spores from each strain were plated onto YE + 5S agar plates and incubated at 32 °C for 30 h. After incubation, 600 spores/colonies were scored per strain. Microcolonies, defined as colonies containing up to four cells, and larger colonies containing more than four cells, were recorded. Spore viability was calculated as the percentage of colonies containing more than four cells. For each strain, three independent biological replicates (n = 3) were analyzed.

Testing the sensitivity of sap34Δ mutant to temperature, EGTA and LiCl

The sensitivity of sap34Δ mutant cells to temperature, EGTA and LiCl was tested using a standard dilution spot test. Briefly, serial dilutions of exponentially growing wild-type and sap34Δ mutant cells were spotted onto solid YE + 5S plates and incubated at 20 °C, 25 °C, 30–35 °C, and on solid YE + 5S plates supplemented with 5 mM or 7.5 mM EGTA, or 7.5 mM LiCl, and incubated at 32 °C. Plates were imaged after 3–6 days of growth.

Sample preparation, total RNA extraction, and mRNA enrichment

Overnight cultures of S. pombe cells were diluted in fresh YE + 5S medium to an OD600 of 0.2 and grown at 32 °C with gentle shaking (∼150 rpm) until the OD600 reached 0.6–0.7. Cultures were cooled on ice for 5 min, after which 3 × 10^8 cells were harvested by centrifugation and washed with 1 mL of ice-cold DEPC-treated water (2,000 × g, 1 min, 4 °C). Cell pellets were stored at -80 °C until RNA isolation.

For total RNA extraction, frozen cell pellets were thawed on ice, resuspended in 0.1 mL of ice-cold TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 1 mM EDTA), and disrupted by vortexing with glass beads (Ø 0.4–0.6 mm; 3 × 2 min at 2,850 rpm, with 1 min cooling on ice between cycles). Total RNA was isolated using the GeneJET RNA Purification Kit (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Residual genomic DNA was removed using the RapidOut DNA Removal Kit (Thermo Scientific). Subsequently, 20 µg of total RNA was used to isolate mRNA using the NEBNext High Input Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Synthesis of double-stranded DNA and sequencing

For full-length cDNA synthesis, 125 ng of mRNA was used as input and processed according to the Ligation Sequencing Kit V14 PCR-free Direct cDNA Sequencing Protocol (Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), Oxford, UK; SQK-LSK114), optimized for full-length cDNA and double-stranded DNA synthesis from RNA. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using Maxima H Minus Reverse Transcriptase (250 U, 42 °C, 90 min; Thermo Scientific), followed by enzyme inactivation at 85 °C for 5 min. Reverse transcription was primed with a VN primer (/5phos/ACTTGCCTGTCGCTCTATCTTCTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTTV-3´) and a strand switching primer (5´-TTTCTGTTGGTGCTGATATTGCTmGmGmG-3´). RNA was removed by treatment with RNase Cocktail Enzyme Mix (20 U, 37 °C, 10 min; Thermo Scientific). Second-strand cDNA synthesis was carried out using LongAmp Taq 2×Master Mix (6.25 U; New England Biolabs) with the PR2 primer (/5Phos/TTTCTGTTGGTGCTGATATTGC-3′), using the following conditions: denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 50 °C for 1 min, and extension at 65 °C for 15 min. To ensure efficient adapter ligation and sequencing, double-stranded DNA was repaired using the NEBNext FFPE DNA Repair Mix, and DNA ends were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra II End Repair/dA-Tailing Module. Sequencing adapters from the Ligation Sequencing Kit (SQK-LSK114) were ligated to the DNA using NEBNext Quick Ligase according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentrations of the prepared samples were measured using the Qubit dsDNA HS 1X solution (Thermo Scientific) and NanoDrop (MicroDigital). Samples (50–90 ng) were sequenced on R10.4.1 Flow cells and MinION Mk1B device using the Ligation sequencing kit (ONT). Sequencing was stopped after reaching 4–5 Gb of sequenced bases, corresponding to 4–6 million reads with a mean length N50 of approximately 1300 bases.

Processing and analysis of sequencing data

Run setup and data acquisition were performed with MinKNOW software version 25.09.16 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies), and reads were basecalled with MinKNOW/Dorado version 7.11.2 in super‑accurate mode55,56. Reads were trimmed using fastplong (v0.2.2)57 with the following parameters: -q 10 --trimming_extension 0 -s TTTTTTTTCCTGTACTTCGTTCAGTTACGTATTGCT -e GCAATACGTAACTGAACGAAGTACAGG. Trimmed reads were aligned to the S. pombe reference genome obtained from PomBase58,59 using Minimap2 (v2.28)60, with splice junctions provided from the genome annotation. The following additional alignment parameters were applied: -r 500,8k --no-long-join --junc-bonus 13 --secondary = no. The resulting alignments were sorted and indexed using SAMtools (v1.21)61. Sorted BAM files were used to reconstruct the transcriptome with StringTie (v3.0.3)62. Gene-level expression as well as individual intron inclusion and exclusion counts were quantified using IsoQuant (v3.7.0)63 in quantification-only mode (--no-model-construction).

Each intron was assigned to a putative aberrant splicing event (ASE) category based on coordinate overlap with other introns within the same gene. If an intron shared its start coordinate with another intron, the event was classified as an alternative 3′ splice site (A3), whereas sharing the end coordinate indicated an alternative 5′ splice site (A5). If both types of overlaps were present, one intron sharing the same start coordinate and another sharing the same end coordinate, the event was classified as a skipped exon (SE). If an intron contained another intron entirely within its coordinates but did not share either splice site with it, the event was classified as undefined. All remaining introns without coordinate overlap with other introns in the same gene were classified as intron retention (IR). Notably, this classification reflects only the geometric relationships between introns and does not imply that A3, A5, or SE events cannot result in intron-retention–like transcript isoforms.

Aberrant Splicing Frequency (ASF) was calculated as the fraction of exclusion counts (i.e., reads supporting A3, A5, SE, or IR category of ASE) divided by the total number of reads spanning the intronic region. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on an ASF value for each intron. For differential aberrant splicing frequency (ΔASF) analysis, introns were filtered using a stringent coverage criterion (mean read count ≥ 100). ΔASF was quantified using a modified beta-binomial likelihood-ratio test derived from the IsoTools (v2.0) implementation64, in which the alternative model included group-specific means but a shared dispersion parameter. The beta-binomial likelihood was parameterized using the mean ASF µ and a shared concentration parameter φ, with α = µφ and β = (1 – µ)φ. This formulation enforces a common overdispersion across conditions while allowing condition-specific mean ASF under the alternative model. The null model assumes a shared mean ASF across conditions, whereas the alternative model allows distinct means for each condition with a common φ. Gradients for optimization were obtained via the chain rule from the beta-binomial log-likelihood with respect to (α,β), yielding ∂l/∂µ = φ(∂l/∂α – ∂l/∂β) and ∂l/∂φ = µ(∂l/∂α) + (1 – µ) (∂l/∂β), with φ optimized on the log scale. The likelihood ratio statistic asymptotically follows a χ² distribution (df = 1).

Introns were considered to have significant ΔASF if they passed Benjamini–Hochberg multiple testing correction (FDR < 0.05) and showed an absolute change in aberrant splicing frequency (|ΔASF| ≥ 0.05). To evaluate the global effect on intron retention frequency (IRF), all introns classified as IR in ASE categories, with a mean read count ≥ 10, were analyzed using a beta-binomial mixed-effects model: IRF~sap34Δ + (1 ∣ geneid/intronid) implemented in glmmTMB (v1.1.13)65. Gene expression fold changes were estimated using DESeq2 (v1.48.1)66. For functional enrichment analysis, the same beta-binomial test used for ΔASF analysis was applied to all introns with a mean read count ≥ 10 across samples. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) annotations obtained from PomBase58,59. GO terms with identical gene sets were collapsed by retaining a single representative term. To obtain gene-level statistics from intron-level ASF results, Sidák-corrected p-values were calculated for each gene as pvalue=1 – (1 – pmin)^m, where pmin is the smallest p-value among all introns within the gene and m is the number of introns in that gene. The ranking metric was defined as –log10 (pvalue), and enrichment was evaluated using clusterProfiler (v4.16.0)67 with the fgsea algorithm68, testing for enrichment in the positively ranked portion of the gene list.

Figures were generated using the R packages ggplot2 (v3.5.2)69 and ggseqlogo (v0.2.2)70. Data processing was performed in Python (pandas v2.3.0)71 and R (dplyr v1.1.4)72.

Analysis of splicing defects by semiquantitative RT-PCR

cDNA was prepared from 1 µg of total RNA using the Lunascript RT SuperMix Kit (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To validate the intron retention defects, semiquantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) was performed to compare wild-type and sap34Δ mutant strains. Genes selected for validation (cdc2, lsm1, mde6, mnh1, red5, and shg1) were chosen from those exhibiting intron retention defects in the sap34Δ mutant. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was included as a size control to define the migration position of PCR products corresponding to unspliced pre-mRNA. Actin (act1) served as an internal control to monitor cDNA quality and PCR amplification efficiency. Conditions for RT-PCR are listed in Table S1. PCR amplicons representing spliced mRNA and unspliced pre-mRNA were resolved by agarose gel electrophoresis (2.5% in 1×TAE buffer) and quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.53q). Gel images were converted to grayscale, and bands corresponding to spliced and unspliced isoforms were selected using a rectangular selection tool. Signal intensities were quantified by measuring the integrated density (area under the peak) for each band. For each gene, the total transcript signal was calculated as the sum of the intensities of the spliced mRNA and unspliced pre-mRNA bands. Splicing efficiency was defined as the ratio of spliced mRNA intensity to total transcript intensity. All analyses were performed using three independent biological replicates.

Results

Characterization of the sap34 allele

The allele SPAC6F6.19, hereafter referred to as sap34, was originally identified in a screen for novel transcripts in S. pombe using high-throughput sequencing of complementary DNAs73. It was annotated as a 555-nt sequence (genomic location: chromosome I, coordinates 2,759,709–2,759,155) and predicted to encode a 122-amino-acid G-patch type RNA-binding protein58,59. However, our expression analysis of C-terminally affinity-tagged G-patch domain–containing proteins in S. pombe indicated that, unlike other G-patch domain–containing proteins, the molecular weight of the protein expressed from the sap34 allele differed from the predicted value. Inspection of the upstream sequence of this allele suggested the presence of additional exons and an intron that may be part of the pre-mRNA transcript.

To define the correct sequence of the sap34 allele, we used a set of primers complementary to sequences 741 nt upstream and 158 nt downstream of the originally annotated SPAC6F6.19 allele (Table S1). We found that the PCR fragment of sap34 amplified from genomic DNA (gDNA) was 336 bp longer compared to the PCR fragment amplified from complementary DNA (cDNA; representing spliced mRNA) (1455 bp in lane 2 vs. 1119 bp in lane 6; Fig. 1A). Restriction digest analysis using BspTI, an enzyme that cuts within the known intron of the sap34 allele (Fig. 1B), confirmed the presence of this intron in the sap34 fragment amplified from gDNA, as it was cleaved into 1087 bp and 368 bp products. As expected, the PCR fragment amplified from cDNA was resistant to BspTI digestion (lane 4 vs. lane 8; Fig. 1A). Furthermore, restriction analysis of the PCR fragment amplified from cDNA using DraI, an enzyme that cuts within the novel intron and exon sequence of the sap34 allele (Fig. 1B), resulted in 572 bp and 547 bp products (lane 7; Fig. 1A). In contrast, DraI digestion of the PCR fragment amplified from gDNA yielded 758 bp, 376 bp, and 321 bp cleavage products (lane 3; Fig. 1A). These results suggest that the sap34 allele contains an additional intron. Finally, sequencing of the sap34 allele revealed that Sap34 protein is encoded by a 1203-nt gene containing two introns of 150 nt and 186 nt (Fig. 1C).

Sap34 is expressed as a 288-amino acid protein featuring a conserved N-terminal G-patch domain

To determine whether the size of the Sap34 protein matches that predicted from the newly defined sap34 allele (33.50 kDa; 288 aa), we fused a TAP tag to its C-terminus. Western blot analysis of the Sap34-TAP protein revealed a band above 50 kDa, consistent with the expected size of Sap34 fused to the TAP tag (57.57 kDa) (lane 3; Fig. 2A). To analyze the amino acid sequence of Sap34 in detail, we purified the Sap34-TAP fusion protein along with its interacting partners using tandem affinity purification43,46. Mass spectrometry analysis did not detect peptides starting at the originally annotated N-terminal sequence of Sap34 (MELEKKGIEL). Instead, we identified peptides spanning this region, including EIMELEKKGIEL, KLEIMELEK, KLEIMELEKK, LEIMELEK, LEIMELEKK, RKLEIMEL, and RKLEIMELEKKGIEL. Importantly, using the amino acid sequence derived from the newly defined sap34 allele, we detected the unique peptide MMESSESEVEDYMSMK, which establishes the novel N-terminus of the Sap34 protein (Fig. 2B; Table S2; Fig. S1). Notably, we also identified phosphorylation of residues T20 and S59 of Sap34 protein (Table S2; Fig. 2B), which explains its slightly slower electrophoretic mobility on SDS-PAGE (Fig. 2A). Together, these data establish the correct amino acid sequence of Sap34 and demonstrate that this protein is expressed as a 288-amino-acid protein (Fig. 2B).

To further characterize Sap34 protein, we compared its amino acid sequence with that of its predicted orthologs, namely CMG1 from S. cerevisiae, GPATCH11 from H. sapiens, GPT11 from M. musculus, GPATCH11 from B. taurus, and GPATCH11 from M. abdita. Sequence alignment revealed a high degree of conservation between these proteins and enabled the identification of the G-patch and DUF4187 domains of Sap34 (Fig. 2C; Fig. S2). To gain additional insight into the structural features of Sap34, we predicted its three-dimensional structure using AlphaFold. The resulting model suggests that Sap34 adopts a fold characteristic of other G‑patch domain–containing proteins, with the G-patch motif prominently exposed and thus well‑positioned for potential RNA or protein interactions (Fig. 2D).

Sap34 copurifies with proteins of the early U2 and U4/U5 × U6 snRNP spliceosomal complexes

To identify proteins that associate with Sap34 in vivo, we next analyzed the components of its protein complex (Fig. 3A). Mass spectrometry analysis of proteins co-purifying with Sap34-TAP revealed several distinct functional groups. The first group comprised the majority of proteins constituting the U2 snRNP complex, including Ini1, Lea1, Lsm2–8, Msl1, Prp10, Prp12, Sap10, Sap14, Sap49, Sap61, Sap62, Sap114, Sap145, Smb1, Smd1–3, Sme1, Smf1, and Smg1. The only proteins known to be components of the U2 snRNP complex in S. pombe that did not copurify with Sap34 were Cwf29, Uap2, and SPBC11C11.01. The second group consisted of proteins of the U4/U6 × U5 tri-snRNP complex, including Brr2, Cwf10, Lsm2–8, Prp1, Prp3, Prp31, Rna4, Smb1, Smd1–3, Sme1, Smf1, Smg1, Snu13, Snu66, and Spp42 (S. cerevisiae PRP8 and human PRPF8). Proteins known to be part of the U4/U6 × U5 tri-snRNP complex in S. pombe but not detected in Sap34 interactome included Dim1, Prp18, Prp38, Snp27, Snu23, Ubp10, and SPAC22E12.02. The final group comprised additional splicing factors with established roles in pre-mRNA splicing, including Cip1, Cip2, Nop58, Pdc2, Prp19, Prp43, Saf2, Spf30, and Wbp4 (Fig. 3B; Table S2). Notably, our mass spectrometry analysis identified phosphorylation of several spliceosomal proteins when they were complexed with Sap34. Specifically, phosphorylation was detected at residues S194 of Lea1; S352 and S358 of Sap114; S360 of Sap61; and S51 and T117 of Prp10 (Table S2).

To gain better insight into the molecular function of Sap34, we used STRING to analyze the functional relationships among proteins in its interactome. After excluding ribosomal proteins as common contaminants, we analyzed the remaining proteins to identify functional enrichments. Among the most enriched Gene Ontology (GO) biological‑process terms, we identified mRNA splicing, via spliceosome (GO:0000398, FDR = 5.09E-34), mRNA processing (GO:0006397, FDR = 2.49E-30), mRNA metabolic process (GO:0016071, FDR = 2.49E-30), mRNA cis splicing, via spliceosome (GO:0045292, FDR = 6.50E-29), RNA processing (GO:0006396, FDR = 1.92E-19), gene expression (GO:0010467, FDR = 1.02E-19), and RNP complex biogenesis (GO:0022613, FDR = 3.04E-18), among others. Regarding GO cellular component terms, we found significant enrichment for terms related to spliceosomal complex (GO:0005681; FDR = 1.39E-40), ribonucleoprotein complex (GO:1990904; FDR = 2.30E-27), spliceosomal snRNP complex (GO:0097525; FDR = 1.12E-39), U2 snRNP (GO:0005686; FDR = 6.61E-23), U2-type spliceosomal complex (GO:0005684; FDR = 2.90E-20), U4/U6 × U5 tri-snRNP complex (GO:0046540; FDR = 2.30E-27), and precatalytic spliceosome (GO:0071011; FDR = 6.15E-18), among others (Fig. 3C; Table S3).

In summary, the dominance of proteins forming U2 snRNP and U4/U6 × U5 tri‑snRNP complexes, together with other splicing proteins, in the Sap34 interactome strongly suggests that Sap34 functions as a previously unrecognized component of the early splicing machinery (Fig. 3D).

Sap34 interacts with the spliceosomal proteins Sap61 and Ini1, and helicase Prp43

To determine which co-purified proteins can potentially interact with Sap34, we performed a yeast two-hybrid analysis, testing interactions between Sap34 and proteins from the U2 snRNP complex, including Ini1 (RDS3, S. cerevisiae; PHF5A, human), Lea1 (LEA1, S. cerevisiae; SNRPA1, human), Prp10 (HSH155, S. cerevisiae; SF3B1, human), Prp12 (RSE1, S. cerevisiae; SF3B3, human), Sap10 (YSF3, S. cerevisiae; SF3B5, human), Sap14 (SF3B2, human), Sap49 (HSH49, S. cerevisiae; SF3B4, human), Sap61 (PRP9, S. cerevisiae; SF3A3, human), Sap62 (PRP11, S. cerevisiae; SF3A2, human), Sap114 (PRP21, S. cerevisiae; SF3A1, human), the U4/U6 × U5 tri‑snRNP complex (Cwf10 (SNU114, S. cerevisiae; EFTUD2, human) and Brr2 (BRR2, S. cerevisiae; SNRNP200, human)), and ubiquitin-protein ligase E4 Prp19 (PRP19, S. cerevisiae; PRPF19, human) and ATP-dependent RNA helicase Prp43 (PRP43, S. cerevisiae; DHX15, human).

Analysis of co-transformants capable of growth on SD-L,W,A medium revealed that Sap34 interacts with the U2 snRNP-associated protein Sap61, as indicated by strong β-galactosidase activity (53.92 ± 6.00 units). We also detected weaker but reproducible interactions between Sap34 protein and the U2 snRNP-associated RING finger-like protein Ini1 (2.93 ± 0.24 β-galactosidase units), as well as the spliceosomal ATP-dependent RNA helicase Prp43 (3.76 ± 0.11 β-galactosidase units) (Fig. 4A and B).

The ability of Sap34 to interact with Sap61, Ini1, and Prp43 prompted us to map the domains of Sap34 responsible for these interactions. We generated a series of constructs spanning different domains and regions of Sap34 (Fig. 4C) and assessed their binding to Sap61, Ini1, and Prp43. Growth of co-transformants expressing either the C-terminal region or the C-terminal region plus the G-patch domain of Sap34, together with Sap61 or Ini1, on selective SD-L,W,H medium indicates that the C-terminal region of Sap34 mediates interactions with Sap61 and Ini1. In contrast, interaction with the Prp43 helicase required the G-patch domain of Sap34, mirroring the interaction mechanism previously reported for other G-patch domain–containing proteins40,41,75. No growth of co-transformants expressing either the N-terminal region or the C-terminal region plus the DUF4187 domain of Sap34 and Prp43 helicase was observed on selective SD-L,W,H medium (Fig. 4D).

To further explore the structural basis of the interactions identified in the Y2H assay, we conducted AlphaFold-based structural predictions of the corresponding protein dimers. The models support our experimental findings, revealing domain-specific interaction patterns. Specifically, the AlphaFold models show that the C-terminal region of Sap34 can form interfaces with Sap61 and Ini1, consistent with the observed growth of co-transformants expressing these domains on selective SD-L,W,H medium. Conversely, the interaction of Sap34 with the Prp43 helicase is predicted to be mediated specifically by the G-patch domain of Sap34, in agreement with both the Y2H results and previously reported interactions for other G-patch domain–containing proteins29–31,40,41,75. No stable interaction interfaces were predicted between the N- or the C-terminal region of Sap34 with Prp43 (Fig. 4E, Fig. S3), consistent with the lack of growth of the corresponding co-transformants on selective medium (Fig. 4D). Overall, these findings support Sap34 as a novel spliceosome-associated protein and indicate that its interactions with Sap61, Ini1, and Prp43 are domain-specific.

Sap34 is required for efficient pre-mRNA splicing, with its loss resulting in intron retention

As an initial step toward analyzing the potential role of Sap34 in pre-mRNA splicing, we deleted the newly defined allele of sap34 in a diploid S. pombe strain by replacing it with a dominant drug-resistance cassette. Analysis of colony formation revealed that spores derived from a heterozygous diploid sap34 deletion mutant (sap34+/sap34Δ) exhibited a viability of 87.2 ± 3.5%, which was comparable to the viability observed for spores derived from wild-type diploid cells (88.2 ± 3.8%). Patching the haploid colonies onto selective plates revealed that 47% of those derived from the sap34+/sap34Δ mutant were resistant to nourseothricin. The deletion of the sap34 allele in haploid mutants was further verified by colony PCR. All tested haploid mutants were positive for the deletion, producing PCR amplicons for the upstream (lanes 5, 8, and 11) and the downstream (lanes 6, 9, and 12) check regions. As a control, colony PCR of wild-type cells confirmed the presence of the sap34 allele (1293 bp; lane 4), whereas no PCR amplicon for the sap34 allele was detected in the tested haploid sap34Δ mutants (lanes 7, 10, and 13) (Fig. S4). Together, these results revealed that the sap34 allele is non-essential for cell viability.

To characterize the phenotype of the generated sap34Δ mutant, we assessed its sensitivity to various temperatures and chemical agents. The sap34Δ mutant was neither cold-sensitive nor temperature-sensitive, but displayed sensitivity to EGTA and LiCl (Fig. S5). This finding is consistent with a previous study, in which deletion of the originally annotated SPAC6F6.19 allele sensitized cells to several stress-inducing compounds, including EGTA, potassium chloride, SDS, and lithium salts76.

To determine whether Sap34 is required for pre-mRNA splicing, we compared splicing efficiency between wild-type and sap34Δ mutant cells using long-read sequencing. We identified 9797 out of 12,685 (77%) PomBase-annotated transcripts, including both protein-coding and RNA-coding genes, and 4728 out of 5306 (89%) annotated introns in our dataset, indicating broad sequencing coverage. Comparing the difference in splicing efficiency between sap34Δ mutant and wild-type cells revealed a marked global impairment of pre-mRNA splicing, as evidenced by altered splicing frequency (ΔASF) and increased intron retention frequency (model ΔIRF = 0.0126, red line) in the mutant (Fig. 5A and B). However, we would like to note that the observed effects of sap34 deletion on splicing may be partially underestimated due to strain background, NMD-mediated degradation of aberrantly spliced transcripts, or the use of poly(A)-selected RNA-seq, as aberrantly spliced transcripts can be rapidly degraded or lost during RNA processing, potentially masking additional effects of Sap34 on splicing efficiency. In total, more than 130 genes in the sap34Δ mutant exhibited a significant altered splicing frequency defect (FDR < 0.05) compared with wild-type cells (Table S4). Notably, none of these genes showed significant changes in overall expression levels. To assess whether defects in splicing are associated with intron length or A/T content, we analyzed these features but detected no significant differences between affected and unaffected introns (Fig. 5C). We further examined whether a defect in splicing might correlate with specific intron sequence elements by analyzing the 5′ splice site (5′SS) and 3′ splice site (3′SS), but found no significant deviations from consensus sequences between introns with increased or decreased retention frequency in the sap34Δ mutant (Fig. 5D).

To validate the long-read sequencing results, we selected several representative candidates (cdc2, lsm1, mde6, mnh1, red5, and shg1), including genes that both met and did not meet stringent statistical criteria for intron retention (≥ 100 reads, FDR < 0.05). Semi-quantitative RT–PCR analysis confirmed reduced splicing efficiency of these genes in sap34Δ mutant, as indicated by decreased ratios of spliced to total mRNA (Fig. 5E). These results strongly support the importance of Sap34 in controlling pre-mRNA splicing.

Notably, among the genes whose splicing was affected in sap34Δ mutant, several encode functionally important proteins, such as protein kinases Cdc2 and Cmk1, kinetochore assembly protein Wip1, subunit of Mis6–Sim4 complex Fta3, or proteins involved or implicated in regulating pre-mRNA splicing, such as Srp2, Lsm6, Tam10, Smd1, Smg1, and Mnh1, and many others (Table S4). To explore the functional relationships among dysregulated genes, we performed enrichment analysis employing GSEA. This revealed that deletion of sap34 affects genes involved in specific biological processes, including mitotic cytokinesis, actin cortical patch organization, and assembly of the actomyosin apparatus involved in cytokinesis, among others; molecular functions, such as rRNA binding, actin binding, cytoskeletal protein binding, and actin filament binding, among others; and cellular components, including the cytosolic ribosome, large ribosomal subunit, and actin or cortical actin cytoskeleton, among others (Fig. 5F; Table S5). The enrichment of these categories suggests that Sap34 plays a role in coordinating the splicing of genes essential for cytoskeletal organization and ribosome function, linking its splicing regulatory activity to key cellular processes.

Discussion

Pre-mRNA splicing is carried out by the spliceosome and numerous associated proteins, many of which interact transiently with the spliceosome to regulate its assembly, activation, catalytic progression, or disassembly. Consequently, the functions of these proteins remain poorly understood. This is particularly true for G-patch domain–containing proteins, which primarily associate with the spliceosome via partner RNA helicases28–31.

In this study, we demonstrate that the previously poorly characterized Sap34 protein is a novel spliceosome-associated factor in S. pombe that functions during the early stages of spliceosome assembly and activation. We first defined the sequence of the sap34 allele and corrected the misannotation of the SPAC6F6.19 locus in existing databases. Sequence alignment of Sap34 protein revealed a conserved G-patch motif within its N-terminal region and confirmed that it represents the predicted fission yeast ortholog of human GPATCH1158. GPATCH11, also known as coiled-coil domain–containing protein 75 (CCDC75) or centromere protein Y (CENP-Y), belongs to the G-patch domain–containing protein family. This protein has been implicated in RNA processing, including pre-mRNA splicing and transcriptional regulation, and may also contribute to synaptic plasticity and the nuclear stress response77.

Our analysis of the Sap34 interactome revealed its association with the majority of U2 snRNP components, including Ini1, Lea1, Lsm2–8, Msl1, Prp10, Prp12, Sap10, Sap14, Sap49, Sap61, Sap62, Sap114, Sap145, and Sm proteins. The U2-associated proteins Lea1 and Msl1 are known to function during early spliceosome assembly, where they play critical roles in prespliceosome formation78,79. Together with Sm proteins and U2 snRNA, they assemble into the inactive 12 S U2 snRNP80. Subsequently, the SF3B complex, comprising, in S. pombe, Prp10, Prp12, Sap14, Sap145, Sap49, and Sap1058, stabilizes interactions with the pre-mRNA flanking the branch site, a step essential for U2 snRNP recruitment and retention9,81,82. SF3B proteins then recruit the SF3A complex, represented in S. pombe by Sap61, Sap62, and Sap11458, facilitating the conversion of the inactive U2 snRNP into its active form, which is required for branch site recognition and stabilization of pre-mRNA interactions81,83–85. Importantly, human SF3b14b (Ini1 in S. pombe) contributes to anchoring and stabilization of the U2 snRNP at the branch point sequence, a critical step for accurate splice site recognition81,86–89. The observed association of Sap34 with the U2-associated proteins Sap61 and Ini1 suggests that Sap34 may play a key role in regulating SF3A–SF3B interactions during early spliceosome assembly. However, further studies will be required to determine whether and how Sap34 modulates the spliceosomal functions of Sap61 or Ini1, and to elucidate its potential impact on the assembly, stability, and dynamics of the SF3A and SF3B complexes.

In addition, several components of the U4/U6 × U5 tri-snRNP, along with other key splicing factors, were identified in the Sap34 complex. These included proteins implicated in spliceosome activation and progression through the first and second catalytic steps, such as Cwf10, Prp1, Prp19, Spf30, and Spp42. Prp1 functions early during spliceosome activation, facilitating the transition to a catalytically competent spliceosome90, whereas Prp19 and its associated factors, including Cwf10 and Spf30, are core components of the NineTeen Complex. This complex stabilizes U5 and U6 snRNP interactions following U4 release, promoting the RNA–RNA and RNA–protein rearrangements required for the first transesterification reaction91–94. Spp42, the S. pombe homolog of human PRPF8, is known to reside at the catalytic center of the spliceosome and to play an essential role in coordinating both step 1 branch formation and step 2 exon ligation95–97.

Notably, the RNA helicases Brr2 and Prp43 were also identified in the Sap34 interactome. Brr2 catalyzes U4/U6 unwinding, a critical step for spliceosome activation and progression to a catalytically competent state98–101. Prp43, in addition to its well-established role in ILS complex disassembly19,27,39–41, has been shown to contribute to splicing fidelity by monitoring spliceosome conformation and targeting suboptimal intermediates for discard32,102–104. Although the ILS complex is likely the primary spliceosome assembly in which Prp43 functions in S. pombe, components of the NTR complex responsible for ILS disassembly, including Cwf23, Ntr1, and Ntr2, were not detected in the Sap34 complex. Their absence indicates that Sap34 associates with spliceosomal complexes in pre-catalytic or early-activated states rather than with the post-catalytic ILS complex. In the ILS complex, Ntr1 and Ntr2 form a heterodimer that recruits and activates Prp43 to mediate spliceosome disassembly following catalysis38,40,41. By contrast, the absence of Ntr1/Ntr2 in the Sap34 interactome suggests that Prp43 is not operating in its disassembly capacity. Instead, these data support a model in which Prp43 participates in an earlier, NTR‑independent function associated with spliceosomal proofreading36,37,102,103. The presence of Prp43 without its established NTR cofactors therefore points to a distinct functional state of Sap34‑associated Prp43, one more consistent with quality‑control or surveillance activities during spliceosome assembly rather than the terminal disassembly phase characteristic of the ILS complex. Importantly, the presence of Prp43 in the Sap34 complex suggests that Sap34 may facilitate recruitment of this helicase via its conserved G-patch domain into early spliceosomal complexes, consistent with the established role of G-patch motifs in mediating interactions with DEAH-box helicases28–31. This interaction may contribute to proofreading mechanisms during early spliceosome assembly. Collectively, these findings indicate that Sap34 associates with spliceosomal components spanning both early and catalytic stages, supporting its potential role as a novel regulatory factor that coordinates spliceosome function.

Our finding that Sap34 is an important component of early and catalytic spliceosomal complexes is strongly supported by the observation that its deletion significantly affects pre-mRNA splicing, leading to an increased frequency of intron retention. Among the affected transcripts are genes encoding factors involved in the regulation of early stages of spliceosome function, including srp2, lsm6, smd1, smg1, and mnh1. Srp2 is an essential serine/arginine-rich (SR) protein that binds purine-rich exonic splicing enhancers (ESEs) to promote splice site recognition105. Lsm6 is a known component of the nuclear Lsm2–Lsm8 complex, which stabilizes U6 snRNA and is required for efficient spliceosome function106. Smd1 and Smg1 are core spliceosomal components implicated in 5′ splice site recognition and early spliceosome assembly107. Finally, Mnh1, a subunit of the exon junction complex, has been shown to functionally interact with the MTREC complex, where, together with the essential component Red5, it contributes to the regulation of meiotic mRNA expression and splicing108. Taken together, these findings raise the possibility that the splicing defects observed upon Sap34 loss may arise through multiple, non-mutually exclusive mechanisms. On the one hand, Sap34 may directly contribute to spliceosome function by its physical interactions with U2 and U4/U6 × U5 tri-snRNP complexes or Prp43 helicase, thereby affecting splice site recognition, catalytic progression, or proofreading mechanisms. On the other hand, the widespread intron retention phenotype could reflect an indirect consequence of Sap34 dysfunction, whereby impaired splicing of other regulatory factors further exacerbates defects in spliceosome assembly and activity. Future studies will therefore be required to discriminate between these possibilities and to determine the extent to which Sap34 acts as a direct regulator of pre-mRNA splicing versus an upstream modulator of the splicing machinery through its effects on other splicing factors.

Functional enrichment analysis of genes affected in the sap34Δ mutant further revealed that Sap34 alters splicing of transcripts involved in distinct but interconnected cellular processes. These genes are enriched in biological processes such as mitotic cytokinesis, actin cortical patch organization, and actomyosin apparatus assembly, as well as molecular functions including rRNA binding, actin binding, and cytoskeletal protein interactions. In addition, the affected genes are associated with cellular components such as the cytosolic ribosome, large ribosomal subunit, and the actin or cortical actin cytoskeleton.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that the G-patch domain–containing protein Sap34 is associated with early U2 and U4/U6 × U5 spliceosomal complexes, where it may interact with Sap61, Ini1, and Prp43, potentially regulating their functions. The importance of Sap34 for efficient pre-mRNA splicing is further supported by the observation that its loss leads to global splicing defects, predominantly characterized by increased intron retention. These defects affect genes involved in pre-mRNA splicing as well as those encoding proteins that coordinate cytoskeletal organization and ribosome function, highlighting a broader role for Sap34 in linking RNA processing to fundamental aspects of cell division and cellular architecture. Collectively, these results provide a foundation for future biochemical and structural studies aimed at elucidating the role of Sap34 in pre-mRNA splicing in the fission yeast S. pombe, and are expected to facilitate and accelerate investigations of its orthologs in higher eukaryotes.

Amino acid sequences are colored according to their predicted secondary structures (red: alpha-helix, blue: beta-strand). The first line in each block shows conservation indices for positions with a conservation index above 4. The last two lines show consensus amino acid sequence (Consensus_aa) and consensus predicted secondary structures (Consensus_ss). Consensus predicted secondary structure symbols: alpha-helix: h; beta strand: e. Conserved amino acids are in bold and uppercase letters; aliphatic (I, V, L): l; aromatic (Y, H, W, F): @; hydrophobic (W, F, Y, M, L, I, V, A, C, T, H): h; alcohol (S, T): o; polar residues (D, E, H, K, N, Q, R, S, T): p; tiny (A, G, C, S): t; small (A, G, C, S, V, N, D, T, P): s; bulky residues (E, F, I, K, L, M, Q, R, W, Y): b; positively charged (K, R, H): +; negatively charged (D, E): -; charged (D, E, K, R, H): c.

Supplementary Material 3: Effect of sap34 deletion on cell viability. (A) Spore viability of diploid wild-type strain (sap34⁺/sap34+) and heterozygous diploid deletion mutants (sap34⁺/sap34Δ) was assessed by random spore analysis. Approximately 5,000 spores were plated on YE + 5S plates, incubated for 30 h at 32 °C, and 600 spores/colonies were scored. Microcolonies (up to four spores) and colonies containing more than four spores were recorded. Spore viability, expressed as the percentage of colonies containing more than four cells, represents mean ± S.D. from three independent biological replicates (n = 3). (B) Verification of sap34 deletion in haploid sap34Δ mutants by colony PCR. Primers used are listed in Table S1. M, DNA size marker.

Supplementary Material 4: Sensitivity of wild-type and sap34Δ mutant cells to temperature and selected chemical agents. Five-fold serial dilution of wild-type and sap34Δ mutant cells was spotted onto YE + 5S media and incubated at the indicated temperature, or onto YE + 5S media supplemented with 5 mM and 7.5 mM EGTA, or 7.5 mM LiCl, and incubated for 3–6 days.
