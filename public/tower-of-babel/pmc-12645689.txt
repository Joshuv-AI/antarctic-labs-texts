Increased plasma concentrations of acyl-coenzyme A binding protein (ACBP) predict future lung cancer development in smokers at risk of cardiovascular disease
Authors: Marine Fidelle, Hui Chen, Léa Montégut, David Boulate, Pamela Abdayem, Magalie Martineau, Tatiana Kuznetsova, Carlijn M van der Aalst, Harry J de Koning, Laurence Zitvogel, Guido Kroemer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcyl-coenzyme A binding protein (ACBP), encoded by the diazepam binding inhibitor (DBI) gene, is a tissue stress hormone the circulating concentrations of which increase with age, obesity and cardiometabolic risk factors. Elevated plasma ACBP/DBI levels have recently been associated with future cardiovascular and cancer risk, particularly lung cancer (LC), independent of smoking status.MethodsTo validate ACBP/DBI as a biomarker of LC risk, we analyzed plasma samples from four independent cohorts within the PREVALUNG EU consortium: healthy volunteers, the FLEMENGHO cohort of smokers at cardiovascular risk, the ROBINSCA cohort of smokers at risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) who later developed LC, and the PREVALUNG cohort of smokers with manifest CVD. ACBP/DBI concentrations were quantified using the Olink proximity extension assay, benchmarked against ELISA and Somascan platforms.ResultsAcross cohorts, individuals who subsequently developed LC exhibited higher baseline ACBP/DBI levels than cancer-free controls. In smokers at cardiovascular risk but without manifest CVD, ACBP/DBI discriminated future LC cases with an AUC-ROC of 0.68 (unadjusted) and 0.73 (adjusted for age, sex, BMI and smoking). Elevated ACBP/DBI levels predicted LC occurrence over more than a decade of follow-up. In contrast, among smokers with established CVD, ACBP/DBI levels were uniformly high regardless of cancer outcome.ConclusionsThese findings independently validate ACBP/DBI as a circulating biomarker of future LC development in at-risk individuals. Clinically, ACBP/DBI quantification could refine risk-adapted lung cancer screening strategies, guiding the frequency of low-dose CT scans and identifying candidates for preventive interventions, including potential ACBP/DBI-neutralizing therapies.

Main text

Acyl-coenzyme A binding protein (ACBP) encoded by the diazepam binding inhibitor (DBI) gene, also called endozepine (a contraction of "endogenous benzodiazepine"), is a tissue hormone produced by multiple cell types and organs [1, 2]. ACBP/DBI plasma concentrations increase with age, body mass index (BMI) and cardiovascular risk factors including arterial hypertension, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia and dyslipidemia (i.e., free fatty acid, triglycerides, total cholesterol, low density lipoprotein and reduced levels of high density lipoprotein) [3–5]. Independently from its association with age and BMI, in apparently healthy individuals, high ACBP/DBI levels (quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, ELISA) predict future cardiovascular disease (CVD) [6], as well as future cancer diagnosis [7].

Although described across different cancer types, this disease-predictive effect was particularly strong for lung cancer (LC), independently of smoking status [7]. Accordingly, mice subjected to chronic antibody-mediated neutralization of ACBP/DBI became relatively resistant to carcinogen-induced and intravenously injected lung cancer [7]. These observations, along with the fact that ACBP/DBI neutralization can improve cancer immunosurveillance and immunotherapy outcome in mice [7, 8] lend support to the hypothesis that elevated ACBP/DBI constitutes an actionable biomarker of future LC development in patients. However, this hypothesis required confirmation in an independent cohort of patients.

To corroborate ACBP/DBI as a risk factor of future LC diagnosis, we turned to a compendium of patient cohorts that are currently investigated by the PREVALUNG EU consortium, which is identifying LC-relevant predictive and diagnostic biomarkers (Table 1). These cohorts involved (i) a group of healthy volunteers (HV) that remained CVD and cancer-free; (ii) the FLEMENGHO cohort [9] of heavy smokers at risk of developing CVD that either remained free of LC or developed LC during 16 years of follow-up; (iii) the ROBINSCA cohort [10] of patients at risk of CVD who all developed LC after blood sampling within 8 years; and (iv) the PREVALUNG cohort (NCT03976804) [11] composed of smokers with manifest CVD, who were subjected to low-density thoracic scans and surgical removal of suspicious nodules leading to LC diagnosis or not over a follow-up period of up to 3 years (Table 1).

ACBP/DBI plasma levels were measured in these cohorts at the time of enrollment using the means of the latest version of the Olink proximity extension assay, which yields data that correlate with those obtained by conventional ELISA (Fig. 1A), as well as with Somascan technology [12]. Notably, in the cohorts that we investigated, ACBP/DBI levels were consistently higher in individuals that developed LC in the future than in HV. This applies to the FLEMENGHO, ROBINSCA and PREVALUNG cohorts (Fig. 1B). Moreover, within the FLEMENGHO cohort, ACBP/DBI plasma concentrations were higher among future LC patients than among controls not developing LC. This contrasts with the PREVALUNG cohort of patients with manifest CVD, in which individuals developing LC and those failing to do so exhibited similarly high ACBP/DBI levels (Fig. 1B). Thus, it appears that measuring ACBP/DBI plasma levels among smokers at risk of developing CVD (but without manifest CVD at the time of blood sampling) can help identify future LC patients with an area under the curve of the receiver operation characteristics curve (AUC-ROC) of 0.68 (p=0.002), if unadjusted, and 0.73 (p=0.05) when adjusted for confounding factors, such as age, gender, body-mass index (BMI) and smoking status (Fig. 1C, Table 1). In this segment of the at-risk population, high ACBP/DBI levels indicate future LC diagnosis with a consistent difference in the slope (risk over time) for a period of at least 12 years (Fig. 1D), even when adjusted for confounding factors (Fig. 1E).

Altogether, our results validate the hypothesis that, among apparently healthy individuals, elevated circulating ACBP/DBI constitutes a biomarker of future LC development. In this context, it appears intriguing - at least at the speculative level - that long-term treatments with benzodiazepines, which act on the same GABA receptor as ACBP/DBI, increase the risk of lung cancer development and reduces the efficacy of PD-1 blockade against non-small cell lung cancer in patients [13]. This observation supports the idea that overstimulation of the ACBP/DBI system favors lung carcinogenesis and tumor progression by immunosuppression.

Potentially, ACBP/DBI plasma concentrations could be used to guide the frequency of low-dose CT scans for the early detection of lung cancer, thereby maximizing the effectiveness of this screening strategy while minimizing costs and radiation exposure It remains to be determined whether additional clinical or biological parameters should be integrated with ACBP/DBI plasma levels to provide optimal guidance for such screening protocols. Furthermore, pending IND approval by regulatory authorities, future clinical studies should investigate whether neutralizing ACBP/DBI with appropriate monoclonal antibodies [14] could enhance immunosurveillance and thus prevent or delay lung carcinogenesis, particularly in those individuals with supranormal ACBP/DBI levels.
