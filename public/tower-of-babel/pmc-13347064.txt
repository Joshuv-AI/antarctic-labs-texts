The influences of mandible-first versus maxilla-first sequencing on postoperative occlusion
Authors: Kasper Stokbro, Jeroen Liebregts, Frank Baan, Bryan Bell, Torben Thygesen, Tong Xi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to evaluate the influences of surgical sequencing on the postoperative occlusion in bimaxillary osteotomies. Patients who underwent bimaxillary surgery without maxillary segmentation, based on a virtual surgical plan were enrolled. The primary outcome variable was occlusal shift, defined as the difference between the surgical accuracy of the mandible and maxilla. The primary predictor variable was the sequence of surgery, with secondary predictors including inferior maxillary repositioning, counterclockwise rotation and gender. 145 patients were enrolled, 68% female, mean age 28 years. Mandible-first procedures resulted in occlusion shift within 0.5 mm of the planned position along all axes, whereas maxilla-first procedures were associated with a 0.5 mm posterior mandibular position (p = 0.05). Inferior maxillary repositioning and gender had larger greater influence on the final occlusion compared to surgical sequencing. Male patients exhibited vertical discrepancies < 0.6 mm, while females showed tendency to develop anterior open bite of 1.4–1.6 mm (p < 0.01). Rotation of the occlusal plane had no significant effect on postoperative occlusion. While both sequencing offers clinically acceptable occlusal accuracy, postoperative occlusal shift is more strongly correlated with vertical repositioning and gender-specific factors than the surgical sequence itself.Keywords: Orthognathic surgical procedures, Malocclusion, Le fort osteotomy, Sagittal split ramus osteotomy, Computer-assisted surgerySubject terms: Anatomy, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Achieving a stable Class I occlusion is one of key goals in orthognathic surgery1. Small occlusal discrepancies can influence the patient’s perception of surgical outcome more than equivalent skeletal discrepancies, i.e. a malocclusion of 1 mm is often considered as more problematic than a 1-mm malposition of the maxilla or mandible2. Therefore, the surgical accuracy of the maxilla or mandible should also be interpreted according to its influence on the occlusion. Despite the high quality of the current literature evaluating surgical accuracy on the skeletal level3–6, there are very few studies that have investigated whether postoperative occlusion corresponds to the planned occlusion7. Studies on postoperative occlusion were primarily performed on two-dimensional (2D) lateral cephalograms or plaster casts models8. These 2D measurements are more prone to errors, limiting the measurement accuracy9, and do not fully exploit the information from the three-dimensional (3D) analysis of surgical accuracy.

In clinical practice, the postoperative occlusion is traditionally evaluated qualitatively based on how easily it can be corrected orthodontically. Success is often categorized along a spectrum from “perfect” or “within acceptable limits” when the occlusal discrepancy is tolerable, or as “unacceptable” when it cannot be corrected orthodontically and requires a reoperation. These categories are important to define the goal of postoperative orthodontic treatment and the desired outcome for the patient. However, these categories are less sensitive to differences between groups and large variations may still exist within the range from “perfect” to “within acceptable limits”. This low sensitivity makes it challenging to measure the impact of different surgical techniques and patient-related factors on postoperative occlusion. To address this, a new quantitative method is presented for measuring how close the postoperative occlusion corresponds to the planned occlusion measured, expressed as the intermaxillary relationship.

The choice of operating on the mandible or maxilla first to achieve the planned occlusion remains debated2,10–14. Until now, the lack of accuracy methodology to evaluate occlusion has limited clinical evidence to support either approach in the theoretical discussion. Therefore, it is relevant to apply the proposed method to assess whether the theoretical advantages of operating on the mandible or maxilla first, in terms of occlusion, can be confirmed by clinical data.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of surgical sequencing on postoperative occlusion in patients who underwent bimaxillary procedures and the influence of covariate factors such as inferior maxillary repositioning, rotation of the maxillary occlusal plane and gender. The null hypothesis was that no difference would exist between the maxilla-first and mandible-first sequencing.

Materials and methods

A retrospective observational study was performed using the combined clinical data from three previously published studies15–17. This cohort was also used in a study on maxillary surgical accuracy18. The cohorts consisted of consecutive patients treated with orthognathic surgery at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Radboud University Nijmegen Medical Center (Nijmegen, the Netherlands) or at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Odense University Hospital (Odense, Denmark). The inclusion criterion was bimaxillary surgery without maxillary segmentation. Exclusion criteria were absence of the virtual surgical plan in the dataset or the presence of palatal splits. In Nijmegen, the study cohort was approved by the Institutional Review Board (CMO Arnhem-Nijmegen, #181/2005) and informed consent was obtained from all patients. In Odense, the study was evaluated by The Regional Committees on Health Research Ethics for Southern Denmark and exempt from ethical approval due to the retrospective and non-experimental nature of the study (20212000-167). Participants and data were treated in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Variables

The primary outcome variable was the occlusal shift, defined as the difference between the surgical accuracy of the mandible and that of the maxilla. Surgical accuracy was calculated as the difference between the planned and achieved surgical repositioning. When both jaws were placed at an equal distance from the planned position, the occlusion was considered accurate, and the occlusal shift was close to zero (Fig. 1). The primary predictor variable was surgical sequencing (maxilla-first or mandible-first approach). Secondary predictors were the virtually planned reposition, direction of maxillary repositioning (inferior or superior), and planned maxillary rotation (clockwise or counterclockwise). Other clinical variables of interest were patient age and gender.

Virtual surgical planning and orthognathic surgery

CBCT scans were obtained 4–6 weeks before surgery and 1 week after surgery. In Odense, using a NewTom 3G CBCT scanner (NewTom, Verona, Italy) with standard settings (field of view: 20 cm diameter/20 cm height), and in Nijmegen, using an i-CAT scanner (3D Imaging System, Imaging Sciences International Inc, Hatfield, PA, USA) in “Extended Field” modus (field of view: 16 cm diameter/22 cm height). All patients were scanned with the mandible in centric relation to the fossa by relaxing the muscles and maintaining the jaw position at first occlusal contact during the scan. VSP was performed using either Maxilim-software (Medicim NV, Mechelen, Belgium) or Dolphin 3D Surgery-software (Dolphin Imaging and Management, Chatsworth, CA). The occlusion was set manually on plaster casts and digitized using laser surface scanners. During VSP, condylar segments were rotated around the condylar hinge point, set at the most lateral part of the condylar head, without further repositioning in the fossa.

Orthognathic surgery was performed using occlusal splints to position the mandible and maxilla according to the VSP. No active orthodontics was carried out following the preoperative CBCT to ensure the optimal fit of the occlusal splint, which was checked just before the osteotomy. Vertical height was controlled with calipers, measuring from a bone-anchored nasion reference pin (Nijmegen) or from the right medial canthal ligament (Odense). Mandibular fixation was achieved with three bicortical screws (Biomet 2.0, Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA) (Odense) or one miniplate fixated with four monocortical screws (Champy 2.0, KLS Martin, Tuttlingen, Germany) (Nijmegen). Maxillary fixation was achieved using four mini-plates, 1.5 KLS Martin (KLS Martin, Tuttlingen, Germany) in Nijmegen and 2.0 Biomet (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA) in Odense. Local bone grafting was performed, when necessary, no extraoral bone grafts were used. Postoperatively, all patients followed the standard protocol with light intermaxillary elastics during the first week.

In Nijmegen, all surgeries from 2010 to 2012 followed a mandible-first sequence, and those from 2013 to 2014 followed a maxilla-first sequence. In Odense, the mandible-first sequence was always used.

Outcome measurements

In the virtual surgical plan (VSP), the relationship between the mandible and maxilla in VSP should correspond to the final occlusion that the surgeon is seeking to obtain. In this study, the occlusion is measured indirectly as the intermaxillary relationship at the level of dentition. The ideal occlusion is obtained when the mandible and maxilla are both placed in the planned position (Fig. 1A). If the mandible is placed posterior to the plan while the maxilla is correctly positioned, a horizontal overbite will be present (Fig. 1B); but if both jaws are positioned equally posterior to the planned position, the ideal occlusion still can be obtained (Fig. 1C). Thus, the occlusion can be measured indirectly as the difference in surgical accuracy between the maxilla and mandible. This indirect method quantifies how closely the mandible–maxilla relationship corresponds to the planned occlusion, but it does not indicate the exact alignment of the teeth nor how easily orthodontic correction can be achieved postoperatively.

Jaw repositioning was measured once by a researcher at each center using validated software, the OrthoGnathicAnalyser (Nijmegen)19 and a semi-automatic algorithm using 3D Slicer (Odense)20. Both systems have a reproducibility of 95% within 0.3 mm. To evaluate reliability of measurements, 10 patients had performed repeated measurements following a wash-out period, blinded to the initial measurements19.

Linear measurements of the repositioned maxilla and mandible were calculated from the midline at the edge of the upper central incisors (UCI). Surgical accuracy for each jaw was calculated by subtracting the obtained movement of the jaw from the planned movement of the corresponding jaw. The occlusal shift was calculated by subtracting the surgical accuracy of the mandible from that of the maxilla. All measurements expressed the mandibular position relative to the maxilla. Positive values corresponded with right (mediolateral axis), anterior (anteroposterior axis), and superior (superoinferior axis). Negative values indicated that the mandible was positioned to the left, posterior and inferior to the maxillary position.

Rotation was measured in degrees for both the mandible and maxilla. A positive yaw indicated a mandibular rotation to the right relative to the maxilla. A positive pitch corresponded to a counterclockwise rotation of the mandible. A positive roll rotated the left side of the mandible inferiorly.

Statistics

Data were analyzed using STATA 16.0 (STATA Corp Lt, College Station, TX, USA). The descriptive variables were analyzed by chi-square test and ANOVA test to justify pooling for hypothesis testing. Reliability was evaluated through repeated measurements. Relative reliability was evaluated using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), specifically a two-way random-effects model based on a single-measure, absolute-agreement definition. Absolute reliability was evaluated using the Standard Error of Measurement (SEM), which provides a value to distinguish between true surgical discrepancies and inherent measurement noise.

A linear mixed-model regression was used on the pooled cohort to evaluate the influence of potential confounders on the primary outcome variable (occlusal shift). Significant associations identified in the mixed-model regression were further analyzed with two-sample t-tests. Two-sample t-tests were used to evaluate the cohort differences in surgical sequencing. Multiple spatial measurements along three axes for each patient were treated as clusters to allow for fixed and random effects. The level of statistical significance in all tests was set at p ≤ 0.05. The level of clinical significance for occlusal differences were set at ≥ 1 mm for linear measurements and ≥ 2 degrees for rotational measurements.

Results

The total cohort enrolled 145 participants, 68% females, mean age 28 years (Table 1). All maxilla-first procedures were performed at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial surgery in Nijmegen. The VSP included only a few mandibular setbacks and was primarily aimed at mandibular advancement, often bimaxillary advancement. No significant differences were observed between cohorts regarding age, gender or incidence of inferior maxillary repositioning. The only significant difference was in the frequency of counterclockwise rotation, where the Nijmegen mandible-first cohort underwent CCWR in 55% of the patients compared with 33% in the two remaining cohorts (p = 0.033). Due to the relative homogeneous distribution between the mandible-first procedures in Nijmegen and Odense, these cohorts were combined for further analysis.

However, all measurement errors remained well within clinically acceptable limits for all linear and rotational dimensions.

Inferior maxillary repositioning

Comparing surgical sequencing with vertical direction of the maxillary reposition showed that the largest occlusal shift was present in the maxilla-first procedures with maxillary impaction (Table 5). In maxillary impaction, the maxilla-first procedures positioned the mandible 0.9 mm posterior to the maxilla, compared with the mandible-first procedures where the mandible was positioned 0.2 mm posterior to the maxilla (p = 0.05). Overall, the mandible-first approach resulted in an occlusion closer to the planned position along all axes but within the clinical acceptable limit of 1 mm.

Gender

Comparing the surgical sequencing between male and female patients showed a significant correlation in the vertical direction (Table 7). Male patients were close to the planned occlusion, whereas female patients showed more vertical distance between the incisors, with anterior open bite of 1.4 mm in mandible-first sequencing and 1.6 mm in maxilla-first cases. In maxilla-first procedures, there was also a sagittal difference with the mandible being more posteriorly positioned relative to the maxilla. This sagittal difference was slightly larger in females than in males.

Discussion

This study used a new quantitative method to evaluate the postoperative occlusion in orthognathic surgery by measuring the difference in surgical accuracy between the maxilla and mandible. By quantifying the occlusion as a deviation in the intermaxillary relationship at the level of the dentition, this approach allowed evaluation of surgical sequencing and patient-related factors that have not been addressed by conventional analyses of surgical accuracy.

The results demonstrated that mandible-first sequencing placed the jaws closer to the planned occlusion compared to maxilla-first procedure in the vertical and sagittal directions. However, the overall mean difference in occlusion between the groups was less than 0.5 mm. Patients undergoing maxilla-first surgery combined with superior maxillary repositioning had a larger horizontal overbite postoperatively, 0.9 mm more than planned. Regardless of the surgical sequence, maxillary pitch rotation had minimal effect on the occlusion, although clockwise rotation was associated with a tendency toward anterior open bite. Gender-related factors showed a correlation with vertical inaccuracy as female patients showed a higher tendency of anterior open bite postoperatively.

The advantages of operating on the mandible first to obtain an occlusion closer to the planned position have been the topic of debate2,10–14. Theoretically, operating on the mandible first should provide an occlusion closer to the planned position because any error in condylar seating should be compensated by positioning the maxilla according to the mandibular position2. Therefore, operating on the mandible first would maintain the planned occlusion even if both jaws end up being displaced posteriorly than planned (Fig. 1C). If the maxilla is operated on first, and the condyles move posteriorly after seating, the mandible will subsequently move posteriorly relative to the maxilla, resulting in a sagittal shift in the occlusion and a larger overbite than planned (Fig. 1B).

This theoretical advantage of operating on the mandible first is supported by our clinical data, but the effect size was below the clinically relevant threshold of 1 mm. After seating the condyles in maxilla-first cases, there may be a 0.5 mm posterior movement affecting the occlusion. This finding is in line with our previous study in which maxilla was also positioned more than 1 mm posterior in mandible-first procedures compared with maxilla-first procedures18.

Rotation of the occlusal plane did not significantly influence the postoperative occlusion, although vertical differences just reached the clinically relevant threshold of 1.0 mm. The observed 1.0 mm anterior open bite following clockwise rotation in maxilla-first sequencing was unexpected, as maxilla-first procedures are generally believed to be more accurate in achieving the planned clockwise pitch rotation than mandible-first procedures14. The splint design and need for condylar autorotation during large clockwise pitch rotations in mandible-first procedures would favor maxilla-first procedures, especially in cases with inferior maxillary repositioning2. However, this study did not have enough participants to evaluate the interaction between vertical maxillary repositioning and occlusal plane rotation2,14. The mixed model regression analysis also indicated that the occlusion was more strongly affected by inferior maxillary repositioning and gender than by occlusal plane rotation.

Inferior maxillary repositioning had the largest influence on occlusion according to the mixed model regression analysis. Maxillary vertical extrusion may result in more clockwise autorotation of the mandible, thereby increasing the occlusal shift. When comparing surgical sequencing within each vertical movement type (inferior vs. superior repositioning), the differences in occlusion were small and remained below the predefined clinical relevance threshold of 1.0 mm. The largest occlusal difference, 0.9 mm, occurred in the sagittal direction when a maxilla-first approach was combined with superior maxillary repositioning. This finding is particularly interesting when correlated to a previous study18, which showed that under similar surgical conditions, the maxilla was positioned 1.5 mm anterior to its planned position in maxilla-first cases, whereas in mandible-first cases, the maxilla was positioned 1.5–2.0 mm posteriorly. These results suggest a correlation between the anterior displacement of the maxilla and the posterior occlusion as observed in this study. In maxillary impaction, posterior bony interference could restrict the superior maxillary movement, causing the maxilla to shift anteriorly. While this could explain the anterior maxillary position, it would not affect the positioning of mandible, as the mandible would still be aligned according to the maxillary position. The occlusion in mandible-first procedures should have been more impacted by such bony interference during maxillary superior repositioning, but this was not observed in this study.

Correlation between gender-related factors and occlusion

The correlation between gender and occlusion was unexpected as previous study demonstrated no gender-related effects on the surgical accuracy of maxillary repositioning18. The mixed model regression analysis reported that female patients displayed larger vertical occlusal discrepancies, in terms of more anterior open bite tendency. This correlation could theoretically be explained by smaller condylar head in females21–23, which makes condylar seating more difficult, with a higher risk of edema in the joints, or a lighter seating may have been carried out in female patients to reduce the risk of postoperative temporomandibular joint disorders4. Furthermore, female condyles are more prone to postoperative edema and resorption, though this usually takes months to develop and should not be visible on the 1-week postoperative scan24–26. The increased muscle tension in male patients may better retain the optimal occlusion in the surgical splint despite postoperative swelling and edema. An additional theoretical explanation may be related to compliance during the postoperative scan, where patients are asked to bite down lightly until primary occlusal contact is achieved. Female patients may be more hesitant to bite down due to pain on movement. Finally, pre-existing anterior open bite is more common in females, which may also have contributed to the observed differences. Further studies on temporomandibular joint function and postoperative occlusion are needed to further explain these findings.

Strengths, limitations, and future perspectives

Direct measurement of postoperative occlusion on CBCT scan is not yet possible, so this study used an indirect approach. A limitation of this method is that it relies on highly accurate measurements of the maxillary and mandibular skeletal movements. Potential measurement errors in both the maxilla and mandible could add up and affect the measurement accuracy on the occlusal level. Thus, accurate occlusion measurement requires protocols with high repeatability, such as the semi-automatic methods used in this study19,20. Conventional methods rely on identifying corresponding landmarks in both the preoperative and postoperative scans. The manual landmark identification errors showed large variances, with standard deviations of up to 0.9 mm27, often including outliers exceeding 2 mm28,29. The semi-automatic measurement method applied here has a variance in repeatability of less than 0.3 mm19–30. This method is acceptable for measuring occlusion and is preferable for clinical use.

Occlusal inaccuracy was measured at the central incisors, while molar malocclusions measured as rotational discrepancy, such as roll (unilateral) and pitch (bilateral) for vertical inaccuracies. This focus on the incisal point, rather than a centered volumetric centroid, was selected because the intermaxillary relationship of the incisors is the primary determinant of both functional and aesthetic success. Transverse incisal deviations are more clinically visible than molar discrepancies. Furthermore, the sagittal and vertical incisor positions stabilize the anterior bite for the orthodontic setup, which carries greater clinical weight than similar isolated molar inaccuracies. Future studies are needed to correlate this 1 mm clinical threshold with patient-reported outcomes and long-term functional stability.

This study evaluated occlusion only as an indirect measure based of discrepancy between the planned intermaxillary relationship and the obtained intermaxillary skeletal positioning. Therefore, the outcome only represented the surgical accuracy of the intermaxillary relationship and did not encompass conventional direct measurements of the occlusion, such as dental intercuspation, overjet, overbite, or the need for postoperative orthodontic adjustments. Furthermore, the short period between the surgery and the postoperative scan could also potentially have influenced the occlusal accuracy, especially in the vertical dimension. Therefore, future studies should use intraoral scans and surface-to-surface mapping to evaluate these aspects of postoperative occlusion at repeated measurements as swelling subsides. The clinical value of the occlusal measurement presented here is that it will enable surgeons to compare different surgical procedures and help optimize surgical techniques to obtain the planned occlusion.

Future studies on orthognathic surgery should incorporate assessment of occlusion as an outcome measure to better understand its role in surgical accuracy and patient-related outcome measures, particularly when evaluating the outcome of emerging technological aids such as patient-specific plates. Hopefully, the presented method in this study for measuring the occlusion along with established protocols in measurements for the temporomandibular joints will help correlate findings between the temporomandibular joint and the occlusion26,31. The relationship between the postoperative changes in temporomandibular joint and occlusion remained unclear and should be the topic of future studies in orthognathic surgery.

Conclusion

The influence of surgical sequencing on the occlusal shift was small and both sequencing procedures were well below the clinical threshold of 1 mm. However, male patients had vertical occlusal discrepancies of less than 0.6 mm, whereas female patients showed a tendency to develop anterior open bite of 1.4–1.6 mm. These results suggest that while surgical sequencing played a role in achieving the planned occlusion, patient-related factors, particularly gender and vertical repositioning, are more strongly associated with postoperative occlusal outcomes.
