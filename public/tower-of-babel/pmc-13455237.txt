Depression at follow-up and associated social determinants among isiZulu-speaking South Africans: evidence from the National Income Dynamics Study
Authors: Thembelihle Qiniso Luthuli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression is a major public health concern globally, with a disproportionate burden in low- and middle-income countries. While the prevalence of depression has been widely documented in South Africa, less is known about depression at follow-up among individuals without depression at baseline and the social determinants associated with it in population-based cohorts. Evidence on depression at follow-up is essential for informing prevention and early intervention strategies.MethodsThis study used longitudinal data from the National Income Dynamics Study (NIDS). Baseline depression status was assessed at Wave 1 using the CES-D-10, and depression at Wave 5 was assessed among adults who were not depressed at baseline and were observed again at follow-up. Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine associations between selected social determinants of health and depression at follow-up. Multiple imputation was applied to address missing data in selected covariates. Missingness was generally low across most variables, with higher levels observed for income expectations and age category. The analysis reflects cumulative risk of depression status at follow-up rather than the timing of first onset across intermediate waves.ResultsAt baseline, 28.3% (95% CI: 26.2–30.3) of participants met criteria for depression. Among adults who were not depressed at baseline, 14.0% (95% CI: 12.7–15.3) met criteria for depression at Wave 5. Depression at follow-up was independently associated with selected social and economic factors, including employment (aOR 0.55, 95% CI 0.37–0.83) and high income expectations (aOR 0.50, 95% CI 0.36–0.70), while several other demographic and behavioural factors were not associated after adjustment.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates a substantial risk of depression at follow-up among isiZulu-speaking South Africans and highlights the socially patterned nature of depression at follow-up. Longitudinal evidence on depression at follow-up among baseline non-cases provides critical insights beyond prevalence estimates and underscores the importance of addressing social determinants as part of comprehensive mental health prevention strategies.

Introduction

Depression is a leading contributor to the global burden of disease and a major public health concern worldwide [1]. It is associated with substantial disability, reduced quality of life, and increased risk of morbidity and mortality [2, 3]. In low- and middle-income countries, including South Africa, depression occurs within contexts characterised by persistent socioeconomic inequality, labour-market instability, and constrained access to mental health services [4–6]. These structural conditions heighten vulnerability to poor mental health outcomes and pose significant challenges for prevention and early intervention efforts.

Public health research has consistently demonstrated that depression is socially patterned, with a higher risk observed among individuals exposed to adverse social and economic conditions [7–9]. The Social Determinants of Health framework emphasises the role of structural, socioeconomic, and contextual factors, such as education, employment, income security, social relationships, and living environments, in shaping mental health outcomes [10, 11]. Empirical studies from diverse settings have linked these determinants to depression prevalence; however, much of the existing evidence is based on cross-sectional designs, limiting understanding of the temporal processes through which social disadvantage contributes to the onset of depression [7–9].

Distinguishing between prevalence and incidence is critical for public health planning. While prevalence describes the burden of depression at a given point in time, incidence reflects the emergence of new cases and provides additional insight into population mental health dynamics. Although depression is widely studied, population-level evidence on temporal changes in incidence is scarce, and recent syntheses of “trends over time” have been largely limited to prevalence, as eligible incidence studies were not identified [12]. The present study contributes a longitudinal perspective by examining depression at follow-up among individuals without depression at baseline within a historically disadvantaged subpopulation in South Africa, complementing national-level evidence by describing patterns of depression at follow-up and associated social determinants within this group.

Nationally representative panel datasets provide an opportunity to address these gaps by examining depression over time within a longitudinal framework while accounting for a range of social determinants. The National Income Dynamics Study (NIDS), a South African national household panel survey, enables the examination of mental health trajectories over time within a population-based cohort [13, 14]. Leveraging such data allows for a clearer understanding of how social, economic, behavioural, and health-related factors are associated with the risk of depression over time, beyond static measures of burden.

The aim of this study was to examine the risk of depression at follow-up and its association with selected social determinants of health among isiZulu-speaking adults in South Africa using longitudinal data from the NIDS. Baseline prevalence estimates are presented to contextualise the analysis of depression at follow-up and to describe the underlying burden of depression in the study population.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study used longitudinal secondary data from the NIDS, a nationally representative household panel survey conducted in South Africa [15]. NIDS follows individuals over time to collect information on demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related outcomes. Baseline data were drawn from Wave 1 (2008), and follow-up data were drawn from Wave 5 (2017). NIDS employs a multistage, stratified sampling design to ensure national representativeness of the South African population [15]. Detailed survey design and data collection procedures have been described elsewhere [13, 14].

Study population

The analytic sample included individuals aged 15 years and older who participated in both Wave 1 and Wave 5 of NIDS and had non-missing data for depression status at both waves. The age threshold of 15 years reflects the NIDS survey design, in which individuals aged 15 years and older complete the adult questionnaire. Depression was assessed using the CES-D-10, a widely used screening instrument in population-based studies and was applied here as implemented in the NIDS dataset. IsiZulu-speaking participants were identified based on self-reported home language. For the analysis, participants who met criteria for depression at baseline (Wave 1) were excluded to define a population at risk of depression at follow-up.

Measures

Depression

Depression was assessed using the 10-item Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), administered at both Wave 1 and Wave 5 [16]. Responses were scored according to standard guidelines, and a validated cut-off was applied to classify participants as depressed or not depressed, consistent with prior validation studies in South Africa [17]. Baseline depression status was used to estimate prevalence, while depression at follow-up was defined as meeting criteria for depression at Wave 5 among participants who were not depressed at Wave 1. This outcome reflects depression status at follow-up rather than the timing of first onset across intermediate waves.

Social determinants of health

Selected social determinants of health were examined based on their relevance in the literature and availability in NIDS. These included demographic characteristics (such as age and sex), socioeconomic factors (such as educational level, employment status, and income expectations), social factors (such as marital status and trust in neighbours), health-related factors (perceived health status), and behavioural factors (exercise frequency). Income expectations were measured by comparing respondents’ current self-reported household position on the six-step ladder with the step they expected to occupy in two years’ time. Respondents who expected to move to a higher step were classified as having “high expectations”, while those who expected to remain at the same step or move to a lower step were classified as having “low or no expectations”. This measure reflects perceived future socioeconomic position rather than actual income.

Statistical analysis

Baseline prevalence of depression was estimated at Wave 1 and presented with 95% confidence intervals to describe the burden of depression in the study population. Depression at follow-up was assessed among participants who were not depressed at baseline and were followed up at Wave 5. Logistic regression models were fitted to examine associations between selected social determinants of health and depression at follow-up. Results are presented as odds ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 18. A fully adjusted multivariable model was specified a priori, and all selected covariates were included simultaneously based on their theoretical relevance and evidence from the literature.

Missing data

Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation under the assumption that data were missing at random [18]. Multiple imputed datasets were generated using covariates included in the regression analysis, such as educational level, employment status, perceived health status, income expectations, and other baseline variables. Estimates were combined using Rubin’s rules. Missingness was generally low across most variables, with higher levels observed for income expectations and age category. Depression status was not imputed. The use of multiple imputation was intended to reduce bias and improve efficiency relative to complete-case analysis. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted using complete-case data.

Ethical considerations

The National Income Dynamics Study received ethical approval from the relevant institutional review bodies, and all participants provided informed consent at the time of data collection. The present study involved secondary analysis of de-identified data and was therefore exempt from additional ethical approval.

Results

Study sample

A total of 1,925 adults participated in both Wave 1 and Wave 5 of the National Income Dynamics Study and had non-missing data on depression status at both waves. At baseline (Wave 1), 544 participants (28.3%) met criteria for depression and were excluded from the analysis. The remaining 1,381 participants constituted the population at risk of depression at follow-up during the study period.

Social determinants associated with depression at follow-up

Multivariable analysis

In the multivariable logistic regression model, several baseline social determinants remained independently associated with depression at follow-up after adjustment for covariates as reflected by adjusted odds ratios (aORs) (Table 3). Higher odds of depression at follow-up were observed among middle-aged adults compared with younger adults. When considering socioeconomic factors, employed adults had 45% lower odds of depression at follow-up than those who were not economically active. In addition, those reporting high income expectations had 50% lower odds of depression at follow-up. Very good perceived health status was also associated with lower odds of depression at follow-up. Those who reported their health as very good had 39% lower odds of depression at follow-up than those who reported their health as excellent.

Discussion

This study examined depression at follow-up among adults who were not depressed at baseline and its association with selected social determinants of health using longitudinal data from the NIDS. Approximately one in seven adults who were not depressed at baseline met criteria for depression at follow-up, highlighting a substantial burden of depression at follow-up in this population. In multivariable analysis, depression at follow-up was associated with age, employment status, income expectations, and self-rated health, while several other demographic, social, and behavioural factors did not remain independently associated after adjustment. Together, these findings support the interpretation that depression at follow-up is socially patterned and shaped by differential exposure to socioeconomic conditions over time, consistent with social determinants of health frameworks [10, 11, 19].

The analysis reflects the cumulative risk of depression status at Wave 5 rather than the timing of first onset across intermediate waves. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted within a two-wave framework capturing depression status at follow-up over an extended period.

Interpretation of key associations

The elevated odds of depression at follow-up observed among middle-aged adults may reflect cumulative exposure to socioeconomic stressors over the life course, consistent with life-course perspectives on mental health risk and social causation frameworks [20, 21]. In the South African context, midlife often coincides with economic precarity, caregiving obligations, and labour-market instability, which may further contribute to vulnerability to poor mental health outcomes at follow-up [22].

In contrast, being employed was associated with lower odds of depression at follow-up, aligning with evidence that employment may confer both material security and psychosocial benefits that protect mental health. Meta-analytic and longitudinal studies have consistently shown that unemployment is associated with a higher risk of depression, partly through income loss, reduced social participation, and loss of role identity [23, 24]. These findings support social causation explanations, whereby adverse socioeconomic conditions increase the risk of mental ill-health [8].

High income expectations were also strongly associated with lower odds of depression at follow-up, suggesting that perceived future economic security may play an important role in shaping mental health trajectories, independent of current employment status. Income expectations, reflecting perceived future socioeconomic position, may influence mental health through pathways related to perceived control and optimism, which are particularly salient in contexts of structural inequality and constrained opportunity [25, 26]. This finding highlights the importance of considering subjective dimensions of socioeconomic position alongside objective indicators.

Very good perceived health status was associated with lower odds of depression at follow-up, reinforcing the close relationship between perceived health and mental well-being. Self-rated health is widely recognised as a multidimensional measure that captures physical functioning, psychological resilience, and underlying morbidity, and has been shown to predict mental health outcomes longitudinally [27, 28]. The pattern observed in this study suggests that perceived health status may capture early subjective experiences of vulnerability that precede poor mental health outcomes at follow-up.

The absence of significant associations for several other social and behavioural factors after adjustment highlights heterogeneity in the social patterning of depression at follow-up and suggests that not all commonly cited determinants operate uniformly over time. This highlights the value of longitudinal analyses in distinguishing factors associated with depression at follow-up from those related to prevalence or persistence [7, 22].

Comparison with previous studies

By focusing on depression at follow-up among individuals who were not depressed at baseline, this study contributes to a limited body of longitudinal evidence. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis identified substantial heterogeneity in depression prevalence trends over time but found no population-based studies reporting changes in depression incidence [12]. This finding highlights the scarcity of incidence-focused research. The present study contributes to this broader evidence base by providing a longitudinal assessment of depression at follow-up and its association with social determinants within a population-based cohort. The observed protective associations of employment and positive economic expectations align with prior research linking socioeconomic stability to lower depression risk, while extending this evidence by examining depression at follow-up rather than prevalence alone [8, 23].

By focusing on depression at follow-up within a defined longitudinal sub-sample, the present study provides a more granular view of depression risk in South Africa than analyses that consider the population as a whole. This distinction is important, as determinants of depression at follow-up among individuals initially free of depression may differ from those associated with prevalent or chronic depressive symptoms.

Public health implications

These findings have important implications for public health planning and mental health prevention strategies in South Africa. The substantial proportion of participants meeting the criteria for depression at follow-up highlights the need for early identification and preventive interventions, particularly among population groups exposed to socioeconomic vulnerability during mid-adulthood. Prevention strategies that address upstream social determinants, including employment opportunities and economic security, may play a critical role in reducing the risk of poor mental health outcomes over time [10, 11].

The associations observed between employment and income expectations suggest the potential mental health benefits of policies and interventions that enhance labour-market participation and improve perceptions of future economic stability. Integrating mental health considerations into broader social and economic policy frameworks may therefore be essential for reducing the burden of depression at follow-up in contexts characterised by persistent inequality and labour-market instability [8, 9, 29].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the use of nationally representative longitudinal data from the NIDS, which enables the examination of depression at follow-up within a population-based cohort [13, 14]. The application of multivariable modelling and multiple imputation strengthens the robustness of the findings by addressing confounding and missing data [18, 30, 31].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Depression was assessed using the CES-D-10, a screening instrument rather than a clinical diagnostic tool, which may result in some misclassification of depressive status, despite its validation in the isiZulu-speaking South African population [17]. Attrition between survey waves may have introduced selection bias; however, loss to follow-up in the analytic sample was minimal (0.58%) and was not associated with baseline characteristics, suggesting a low risk of attrition bias. Additionally, the use of Wave 1 and Wave 5 only limits the ability to examine the timing and duration of depressive episodes and does not allow identification of the first onset across intermediate waves. Sensitivity analyses comparing multiple imputation and complete-case models yielded broadly consistent results. Finally, although a range of social determinants was examined, residual confounding cannot be ruled out.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a substantial risk of depression at follow-up in the isiZulu-speaking population in South Africa and highlights the role of selected social and economic factors in shaping depression at follow-up. By focusing on depression at follow-up, the findings provide longitudinal evidence that strengthens the understanding of how social determinants influence mental health trajectories. These results underscore the importance of addressing socioeconomic conditions as part of comprehensive, prevention-oriented mental health strategies in South Africa.
