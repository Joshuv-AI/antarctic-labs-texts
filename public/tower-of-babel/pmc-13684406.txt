Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and tricyclic antidepressants differentially affect heart failure risk in patients with kidney failure
Authors: Yun Seob Jung, Byoung Geun Han, Yoonjong Bae, Dong Hui Shin, Tae Sic Lee, Sang Won Hwang, Semi Hwang, Seung-Kuy Cha, Jun Young Lee, Jinhee Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlthough selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are the treatment of choice for dialysis patients, tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) are still frequently prescribed in clinical practice. No studies have compared the cardiovascular outcomes of patients with kidney failure (KF) and depression prescribed SSRIs and those of patients with KF and depression prescribed TCAs.MethodsData of patients who were diagnosed with depression after being diagnosed with KF between January 2003 and December 2022 were extracted from the Korean National Health Institute Database System. The primary outcome was incident heart failure. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality and other cardiovascular outcomes.ResultsWe extracted the data of 3096 patients who were prescribed a single type of antidepressant that was continued throughout the follow-up period. Among these patients, 2169 were prescribed TCAs (mean age, 59.3 years; 57.4% were male), and 927 were prescribed SSRIs (mean age, 60.5 years; 48.8% were male) after KF was diagnosed. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 844 patients remained in each group, and no significant difference in all-cause mortality was observed between patients prescribed TCAs and those prescribed SSRIs. However, the risk of incident heart failure associated with TCA use was higher than that associated with SSRI use [adjusted hazard ratio (HR) 1.21, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.07–1.36]. This result was consistent with those indicated by the unmatched multivariate adjusted analysis and landmark analysis.ConclusionsAmong patients who were diagnosed with depression after being diagnosed with KF, SSRI use was associated with a lower risk of incident heart failure than TCA use. Individualized antidepressant treatment strategies should be considered for patients at high risk.

INTRODUCTION

Depression is one of the most common psychiatric comorbidities in patients with kidney failure (KF) [1, 2]. Although its reported prevalence varies depending on the diagnostic method, its conservative estimate is ∼20%–40%, even when diagnosed using structured clinical interviews rather than self-reported questionnaires [2, 3]. Depression is independently associated with poor quality of life, high hospitalization rates, and increased risks of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality among patients with KF [4, 5]. Despite its high prevalence and adverse clinical consequences, depression is under-recognized and undertreated among these patients [5, 6].

Although antidepressant therapy is commonly prescribed to patients with depression and KF, the cardiovascular safety of specific antidepressant classes is uncertain in this unique high-risk group [7]. Furthermore, the management of depression in patients on dialysis is complicated by diagnostic challenges, such as the overlap of symptoms between uremia and depression, which often delay recognition, as well as a lack of clear guidance regarding pharmacotherapy. Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) are among the first-line treatment agents for depression; however, they are known to cause cardiotoxic events, including conduction abnormalities, arrhythmias, and orthostatic hypotension. Moreover, despite guideline preferences for selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), real-world prescription patterns for patients with KF vary substantially based on the provider, which may contribute to the continued use of TCAs in clinical practice [6]. SSRIs are generally considered safer than TCAs in terms of cardiac adverse effects [8]. However, direct comparisons of cardiovascular risks associated with TCAs and SSRIs among patients on dialysis remain limited.

Patients with KF are particularly vulnerable to heart failure caused by chronic volume overload, left ventricular hypertrophy, and vascular calcification. Therefore, understanding whether certain antidepressants exacerbate cardiovascular risks is clinically important. In this nationwide cohort study, we compared the incidence of heart failure in patients on dialysis who used TCAs with that of those who used SSRIs. We hypothesized that the risk of heart failure associated with SSRI use was lower than that associated with TCA use.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source and study population

This study utilized data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database of South Korea. The NHIS is a mandatory national health insurance program operated by the Korean government that provides healthcare coverage for ∼97% of the population. The NHIS database has been widely validated and used in numerous studies. Data were obtained through the Korean National Health Insurance Sharing Service (http://nhiss.nhis.or.kr) with appropriate approval (NHIS-2024-1-520). Details of the diagnostic and medication codes of patients included in this study are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Based on these codes, patients were categorized into the SSRI group or TCA group.

Using the NHIS database, we identified patients diagnosed with KF between 1 January 2003 and 31 December 2022. Among patients with KF, we identified those newly diagnosed with depression between 2005 and 2017 using ICD-10 codes. Patients with a history of depression before KF diagnosis were excluded. We additionally excluded patients who experienced cardiovascular outcomes or cancer within 1 year after index date and those who had received antidepressant prescriptions before the diagnosis of depression. The index date was defined as the initiation date of SSRI or TCA treatment after the diagnosis of depression in patients with KF. To ensure sustained exposure, patients prescribed antidepressants for fewer than 90 days were excluded. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in Fig. 1.

To compare outcomes of the SSRI and TCA groups, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) using the nearest neighbor method with a caliper width of 0.05 times the standard deviation. Propensity scores were calculated using all available baseline covariates, including age, sex, residential area, year of KF diagnosis, comorbidities (such as peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, rheumatic disease, peptic ulcer disease, mild liver disease, hemiplegia, severe liver disease, HIV, hypertension, diabetes, and atrial fibrillation), and concomitant medications (such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, statins, and aspirin) (Supplementary Fig. 1). After PSM, hazard ratios (HRs) of study outcomes were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yonsei University Wonju College of Medicine (approval number CR324309). Informed consent was waived because anonymized and de-identified data were analyzed.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the occurrence of heart failure. Secondary outcomes included myocardial infarction, stroke, arrhythmia, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality.

Statistical analysis

We compared baseline characteristics of the SSRI and TCA groups before and after PSM. Continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t test, and categorical variables were examined using the chi-square test. The results of continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. To address potential immortal time bias, a landmark analysis was performed. Differences in the cumulative incidence of the matched groups were assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves with log-rank tests. After PSM, HRs of study outcomes were estimated using multivariate Cox proportional hazards models. Although covariate balance after matching was generally acceptable, additional adjustment for clinically important covariates, including age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, residential area, and CCI scores, was performed to improve robustness of the estimates. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0; Windows; http://cran.r-project.org). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 646 765 patients diagnosed with KF between 20 032 and 2022 were screened. After applying exclusion criteria, 3096 patients were included in the study cohort, including 927 SSRI users and 2169 TCA users (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics of unmatched and matched patients are presented in Table 1. Before PSM, the TCA group had a younger mean age, higher CCI score, higher prevalence of male patients, earlier diagnosis of KF, higher prevalence of peripheral vascular disease, diabetes, and hypertension, and higher prescription rates of renin-angiotensin system blockers, beta blockers, aspirin, and calcium channel blockers. After PSM, all covariates were well-balanced between the TCA and SSRI groups. The standardized mean difference for all covariates after matching was <0.1, indicating adequate covariate balance between the matched groups (Supplementary Fig. 1). Comparison of clinical outcomes of the TCA and SSRI groups

The matched cohort included 542 incident cases of heart failure in the TCA group (112.8 per 1000 person-years) and 491 incident cases of heart failure in the SSRI group (94.4 per 1000 person-years) (Fig. 2). In the univariate Cox regression analysis, the risk of incident heart failure associated with TCA use was significantly higher than that associated with SSRI use [HR, 1.21; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.07–1.37; P < 0.01]. This association remained consistent in the multivariate Cox regression model after adjusting for age, sex, residential area, CCI score, hypertension, diabetes, and atrial fibrillation (HR, 1.21; 95% CI, 1.07–1.36; P < 0.01) (Table 2).

In the matched cohort, no significant differences in all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, MACE, myocardial infarction, stroke, or arrhythmia between the TCA and SSRI groups were observed (Table 2). In contrast, in the multivariate adjusted unmatched cohort, the risks of heart failure, MACE, and myocardial infarction associated with TCA use were higher than those associated with SSRI use (Supplementary Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis

The sensitivity analysis included 6-, 12-, and 18-month landmark approaches and extended follow-up to 60 months after matching. In the matched analyses (Supplementary Table 4), TCA use was consistently associated with a higher risk of heart failure compared with SSRI use across all landmark time points and during extended follow-up. Similar findings were observed after additional multivariable adjustment (Supplementary Table 5). No statistically significant differences in all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, MACE, stroke, or arrhythmia were observed in either the matched or additionally adjusted analyses. The risk of myocardial infarction was significantly higher only during the 60-month follow-up analysis in both analytic approaches (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). In the subgroup analysis, the association between TCA use and heart failure was more pronounced among women, individuals younger than 65 years of age, patients with higher CCI scores, patients diagnosed with KF in 2010 or later, and patients with diabetes. Interaction tests indicated significant effect modifications by age, CCI score, and diabetes status (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

This nationwide Korean cohort study showed that baseline TCA use was independently associated with a 21% higher risk of incident heart failure compared with that associated with SSRI use, even after adjusting for a comprehensive range of potential confounders including demographics, socioeconomic factors, comorbidities, and concomitant medications. The results remained robust across multiple analytic methods, including the multivariate adjusted analysis, landmark analysis, and PSM analysis. To our knowledge, this is the first population-based study to directly compare the cardiovascular safety of SSRIs and that of TCAs among patients on dialysis without pre-existing cardiovascular disease. Our findings suggest that SSRIs may offer important long-term safety benefits in clinical contexts in which heart failure is a critical consideration. Additionally, studies of related populations have found evidence of greater cardiovascular risks with TCA use. Depression in KF has been linked not only to cardiovascular disease but also to severe psychiatric sequelae, underscoring the importance of selecting antidepressant therapies that are psychiatrically and medically safe [6, 7, 9]. The initiation of TCAs as first-line therapy has been associated with a higher risk of coronary artery disease compared with that associated with SSRI initiation [10, 11]. Across multiple populations, TCA use, compared with no antidepressant use, has been associated with increased cardiovascular events. Among older adult patients with cardiovascular disease, TCA use was associated with a high risk of coronary heart failure [12]. Furthermore, among initially healthy postmenopausal women, TCA use was associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events [13]. Similarly, among community-dwelling adults without known cardiovascular disease, TCA use was associated with an increased risk of incident cardiovascular disease [14]. Collectively, these findings indicate that TCAs may confer a less favorable cardiovascular profile and underscore the importance of evaluating their comparative safety for patients on dialysis. TCAs inhibit the fast cardiac sodium channel, prolong phase 0 depolarization, and widen QRS intervals, thereby increasing the risks of ventricular arrhythmias and conduction abnormalities [15]. Additionally, TCAs exert anticholinergic and α₁-adrenergic blockade effects that cause tachycardia, hypotension, and reduced myocardial perfusion, resulting in impaired left ventricular function and contributing to heart failure [16].

According to previous studies, SSRIs have conflicting relationships with cardiovascular events [14, 17–19]. Nevertheless, the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort study demonstrated a strong association between serum serotonin levels and cardiovascular outcomes; therefore, serotonergic modulation by SSRIs may be relevant to cardiovascular risks among patients on dialysis [20]. These findings may be explained by the ability of SSRIs to restore endothelial cell damage, improve vascular function, reduce arterial stiffness, and attenuate inflammation in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and depression [21–23]. Additionally, SSRIs alone may lead to a reduced cardiovascular disease incidence by addressing depression and improving patient adherence to medication. Notably, SSRI class agents with higher QT-prolonging potential (e.g. citalopram/escitalopram) have been associated with a higher 1-year risk of sudden cardiac death among patients on hemodialysis [17]. Therefore, careful agent selection and avoidance of potentiating drug interactions are warranted.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the choice of antidepressant class may have prognostic implications for patients on dialysis who are at exceptionally high risk for cardiovascular disease. Because of the complex interaction between depression, autonomic imbalance, and cardiovascular risk in this population, individualized treatment strategies are necessary. Future studies should elucidate the mechanisms that underlie antidepressant-related cardiac effects in advanced CKD, including dose dependency and differential effects across SSRI subclasses.

This study had some limitations. First, because it was an observational retrospective analysis, we cannot fully rule out residual confounding despite the use of PSM and multivariate adjustment. Second, claims-based data lacked key clinical variables such as depression severity, serum albumin, B-type natriuretic peptide level, physical activity, and echocardiogram findings of patients that may influence cardiovascular outcomes.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that SSRI use by Korean patients on dialysis with CKD and depression was associated with a lower risk of heart failure than that associated with TCA use. Despite the study limitations, we found that SSRI use tended to decrease the overall risk of heart failure. These findings highlight the need for large-scale prospective studies with better control of potential confounding factors to confirm the effects of SSRIs on the risk of heart failure.
