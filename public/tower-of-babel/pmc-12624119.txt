Long-term anthropogenic disturbances alter plant species composition in the Southern Atlantic forest
Authors: Fernanda Ribeiro da Silva, André L. Giles, André L. de Gasper, Alexander C. Vibrans, Marcio Baldissera Cure, Catarina C. Jakovac
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
It is well known that anthropogenic impacts reshape species composition and cause biodiversity loss, altering tree diversity patterns in natural forests. Here, we use a robust database encompassing three forest formations in the subtropical Atlantic Forest to determine how environmental conditions and anthropogenic impacts affect diversity patterns. We compare alpha and beta-diversity indexes using generalized dissimilarity models (GDM) within and between forest formations and two successional stages (intermediate and advanced). We found that beta-diversity across all forest formations and successional stages was mainly explained by species richness (53.3%), followed by environmental factors (26.9%), including precipitation seasonality and mean annual temperature, geographic distance (7.6%), and anthropogenic factors (7.5%). In the Araucaria forests, beta-diversity was also explained by grazing and precipitation seasonality, while in the Evergreen Forests by fire frequency, elevation and annual range of temperature. Differences in driver importance across forest formations might be related to ecological differences and distinct histories of anthropogenic impacts. By understanding the unique biodiversity patterns and human impacts across different forest types, our findings offer key insights for developing effective conservation strategies to ensure the long-term protection and resilience of these ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24131-3.

Introduction

Anthropogenic impacts reshape species composition and biodiversity patterns in tropical forests1–3. Atlantic forest, one of the ‘hottest’ biodiversity hotspots4 has suffered greater impacts since the European invasion in the 16th century5,6. Several types of disturbance are related to anthropization, such as forest loss, species invasion, over-exploitation, landscape fragmentation, forest isolation, forest regrowth and climate change7,8. Currently, forest loss and land use change are the main anthropogenic factors affecting Atlantic Forest fragments9,10. Such activities have reduced the original cover of the Atlantic Forest domain to a scattered small amount of forest fragments inserted in landscapes that might be less or more degraded6,11,12. This had led to impoverished biological communities, decreasing habitat quality and species diversity13, and thus changed species composition14. To what extent anthropogenic factors drive the local and regional variation in the floristic composition of actual forest fragments remains to be revealed.

Anthropogenic impacts directly affects species diversity by preventing or facilitating the colonization and establishment of plant species, affecting diversity patterns at multiple spatial scales, i.e. from local (alpha-diversity) to regional assemblages (beta-diversity1,15. Anthropization of landscapes can lead to two concurrent processes: biotic homogenization and differentiation. Biotic homogenization happens when a subset of species are favored and proliferate in human-modified landscapes (usually generalist species), while other species are eliminated (usually forest specialists) leading to an increasing similarity among plant communities across landscapes (with a decrease in beta-diversity)15–17. Biotic differentiation happens when different sets of species are favored by anthropogenic impacts in different landscapes, usually because of seed dispersal limitation and/or by compositional heterogeneity of forest fragments, resulting in greater differences between assemblages across landscapes (with an increase in beta-diversity)1,18. There are evidences for either biotic homogenization or differentiation of tropical forests in response to human impacts, without a consensus yet on which process would prevail in which conditions19. To identify whether biotic homogenization or differentiation is actually happening, biological communities should be followed over time but most studies use space for time substitution approaches comparing across landscapes with varying levels of degradation20,21. This is because long-term monitoring is especially rare in tropical regions. Indications of such processes, however, can be inferred by comparing biological communities across landscapes subjected to varying degradation levels [e.g.20,22] and over successional stages of forest regrowth. Secondary forests are currently major components of landscapes in the Atlantic Forest, where primary and well-conserved forest fragments are rare6,11. These secondary forests might play a major role in species diversity within and across landscapes. Studies have shown that species alpha diversity increases with the advancement of successional stages, because a smaller set of generalist species colonizes open areas and are gradually replaced by a larger group of forest-specialist species23,24.

Successional trajectories, however, are highly variable across space due to the interaction of drivers such as landscape composition, land use history, edaphic and climatic conditions25, with patches showing very different species composition even at early stages of succession23,26. Therefore, if a small set of early successional species is favoured in anthropogenic landscapes, beta diversity might decrease over succession, but if dispersal limitation prevails and the floristic composition of early patches is very variable across landscapes, beta-diversity might be reduced over succession.

Besides anthropogenic impacts, environmental site characteristics including climate and edaphic conditions also drive compositional variation across landscapes2,16,27. For example, in tropical forests, alpha and beta diversities tend to increase with water availability (e.g. high annual rainfall and low rainfall seasonality), temperature and soil nutrient availability (e.g. cation exchange capacity)28–30. Such factors, therefore, must always be taken into account when evaluating diversity patterns at regional scales.

Notably, the Southern Brazilian Atlantic Forest is a unique type of rainforest with distinct climatic and edaphic conditions compared to those found in the northern and central Atlantic Forest31. In general, these forests are situated in transitional zones between tropical and temperate climates, typically characterized by year-round rainfall without a pronounced dry season. These climatic patterns support three major forest formations: Evergreen Forest, Seasonal Forest and Araucaria Forest. Historical processes of anthropization (e.g. replacement of forests by agricultural lands and timber extraction) has fragmented these forests transforming them into small and often degraded forest fragments32. Currently, less than 5.7% of the original forest cover in the Santa Catarina Atlantic forest is formally protected, being 1,6% of the Protected Areas aimed for integral protection and 4,1% for sustainable use (see Brazilian conservation units panel, available in Microsoft Power BI). Such reduced amount of protected areas do not cover enough regions necessary for the effective conservation of tree species33. Although subjected to similar anthropization processes as other Atlantic Forest regions, the distinct environmental drivers of the Southern Brazilian Atlantic Forest formations might shape different patterns of biodiversity. These specific environmental conditions might interact with anthropogenic impacts to shape plant species diversity differently in the Southern compared to the Northern Atlantic Forest formations. Although Lingner et al.34 investigated the impacts of anthropogenic factors on forest attributes in Evergreen southern Atlantic Forest, efforts to understand these patterns have mainly focused on other regions35,36.

Here, we use a robust dataset to identify patterns of species diversity and unveil how anthropogenicand environmental factors affect tree communities in the Southern Brazilian Atlantic forest. Specifically, we aim to evaluate how environmental and anthropogenic factors affect the alpha- and beta- diversities of forest fragments across different successional stages and forest formations in the Southern Atlantic Forest. To achieve this aim, we first characterized alpha- and beta-diversities across the three forest formations (Evergreen Forest, Seasonal Forest and Araucaria Forest) and successional stages (intermediate and advanced) in our dataset. Given the known taxonomic and environmental variation across forest types37–39, we expected higher floristic dissimilarity among forest formations than among forest remnants within formations. Then, we tested how environmental and anthropogenic impacts affect forest beta-diversity within and between forest formations. We hypothesized that (i) alpha and beta-diversities will decrease with anthropogenic impacts and (ii) will increase with successional stages within all forest formations, and that (iii) floristic dissimilarity between forest formations will be more affected by environmental conditions (climate and soils) than by anthropogenic impacts.

Results

Alpha diversity variation across forest formations

Alpha-diversity, measured by the three hill series, varied significantly across forest formations (Fig S4). The Evergreen Forests presented the highest alpha-diversity compared to the Araucaria Forests and the Seasonal Forest, which did differ between each other between successional stages (Fig. 1; ANOVA, p < 0.001). Forests in advanced successional stages had higher alpha-diversity than intermediate successional stages, but the alpha-diversity in the two successional stages did not differ at the Araucaria and Seasonal Forests (Fig. 1).

The analyses across formations showed that species richness (i.e. hill0) increased with mean annual temperature, amount of forest in a 1000 m² buffer, longitude, soil clay content, annual range of air temperature, and decreased with fire frequency, latitude, and the successional stage (in intermediate stage) (R²=0.52; p < 0.001) (Table 1, Supplementary Material).

Beta-diversity variation across forest formations

Beta-diversity varied across forest formations. When calculated the Bray-curtis index, the Evergreen and Araucaria forests had higher beta-diversity than the Seasonal forests. In addition, different from expected, beta-diversity was higher in intermediate successional stages than in advanced successional stages. The NMDS based on Bray-Curtis index shows that the floristic composition is significantly different among the three formations (ANOSIM - R = 0.124, p < 0.001). SUs of AF and EF were more dispersed in the NMDS space, suggesting greater dissimilarity (i.e. beta-diversity) within this forest, compared with SF, which SUs were closer together, indicating lower beta-diversity (AF = 0.76 ± 0.13; EF = 0.76 ± 0.12, SF = 0.64 ± 0.12; mean ± standard deviation) (Fig. 2). The three groups show some overlap, suggesting similar floristic composition on some SUs classified as different forest formations, which is probably driven by the SUs located at ecotone zones between adjacent formations. (Supplementary Figure S2).

Intermediate and advanced successional stages were grouped together by forest formation in the NMDS and therefore do not show a trend towards homogenization across forest formations related to successional stage. Moreover, when we analyse the dissimilarity between SUs within each forest formation separately, pairs of advanced successional forests are less dissimilar between each other than intermediate successional forests, for all three forest formations. This suggests that forest patches at later successional stages show lower dissimilarity than those at earlier stages.

Considering only the forests at intermediate successional stage, SF had the lowest mean dissimilarity (0.64 ± 0.14) and EF had slightly higher dissimilarity than AF (AF = 0.76 ± 0.15; EF = 0.77 ± 0.15). Considering only the forests in advanced successional stages, SF showed lower dissimilarity (mean = 0.59 ± 0.14) compared to AF (0.73 ± 0.14) and EF (0.74 ± 0.13), which showed similar values (Fig. 2).

When we decomposed the beta-diversity within each forest formation, we found a greater contribution of species turnover (i.e. substitution of species) than nestedness (i.e. a subgroup of a species-rich group) components in all three forest formations. However, in SF nestedness was greater than in the other formations, demonstrating more shared species among communities in SF. Furthermore, unlike EF and AF, for SF, beta-diversity in advanced forests was lower (Supplementary Fig S3).

Drivers of diversity: environmental and anthropogenic factors

Considering all forest types and both successional stages together, most of the variation was explained in order of importance by: species richness, precipitation seasonality, geographic proximity, mean annual temperature, fire frequency, cattle grazing, amount of surrounding forest, and soil clay content (Table 1).

The species dissimilarity within intermediate and advanced successional forests was explained by similar factors, with a similar relative contribution of anthropogenic factors (10.4% and 7.73% respectively) (Table 1). For intermediate successional forests, species dissimilarity was affected, in order of importance, by species richness, precipitation seasonality, geographic proximity, landscape fire frequency, euclidean distance between forest patches (i.e. isolation) in a 750 m buffer, grazing, and amount of forest in a 1000 m buffer around SUs.

For advanced successional forests, species turnover was significantly affected by species richness, precipitation seasonality, geographic proximity, elevation, fire frequency, mean annual temperature, cattle grazing and the amount of surrounding forest.

Within EF, when combining intermediate and advanced forest stages, beta-diversity was affected by species richness, landscape fire frequency and geographical proximity. At the advanced successional EF, annual range of air temperature, elevation, temperature seasonality and geographical proximity significantly explained species turnover. For intermediate successional EF, landscape fire frequency, species richness and geographic proximity significantly affected beta-diversity.

Within AF, when combining intermediate and advanced successional stages, species dissimilarity was explained by species richness (i.e. hill0), precipitation seasonality, and geographical proximity. In advanced successional AF, species richness, geographical proximity, and grazing explained beta-diversity. In intermediate successional stage AF, only precipitation seasonality and species richness were important to explain beta-diversity (Table 1).

Within SF, at combined intermediate and advanced successional stages, only species richness and geographic proximity explained dissimilarity. For advanced and intermediate successional SF, species dissimilarity was explained only by geographic distance (Table 1).

Discussion

We presented an overview of alpha and beta tree species diversity and their main drivers within three Atlantic Forest formations across the state of Santa Catarina in Southern Brazil. Alpha-diversity is distinct among forest formations, with greater values in landscapes within the Evergreen Forest formations, which suffered lower anthropogenic impacts, and lower values in the Araucaria Forest and Seasonal Forest. Beyond bioclimatic factors, anthropogenic impacts affect the beta-diversity of tree species in the Southern Atlantic Forest. Contrary to our expectations, anthropization led to greater floristic similarity in advanced-succession remnants than in intermediate-succession remnants across all forest formations. Our results show that conservation strategies for tree diversity in the Southern Atlantic Forest must include specific anthropogenic interventions related to how people use the land. For example, conserving Araucaria forest fragments requires reducing the impact of cattle grazing. In Evergreen forest fragments, it is crucial to control fires. These strategies consider the main stressor and ecological characteristic of each forest formation.

Evergreen Forest exhibit the highest species richness and the greatest species turnover (i.e., beta diversity) among the forest formations. This pattern may be explained by the facts that this is the best conserved formation in the Southern Atlantic Forest40, and it covers broader environmental conditions (ranging from coastal to montane areas) under less restrictive environmental conditions compared to the other two formations40. Indeed, we found that mean annual temperature and elevation (associated to lower temperatures), in addition to species richness and geographic distance which also affect beta-diversity. Beta-diversity in EF was also affected by frequency of landscape fires, even though fire occurs more frequently in AF (Fig S5, Supplementary Material), suggesting that this forest formation is more sensitive to this type of anthropogenic disturbance. In fact, landscape fire frequency might promote the spread of generalist species less sensitive or tolerant to fires, which are usually characterized by having broad environmental niches and large population sizes41. Additionally, most generalist species can withstand disturbances and environmental constraints typically found in secondary forests42. Despite the fact that EF forest remnants are better conserved compared to AF and SF, land use impacts and fire frequency can promote the expansion of generalist species in forest remnants at different successional stages. On the other hand, EF harbors a high number of rare species43, a characteristic that may contribute to increasing beta-diversity. These facts, combined with broader environmental variation, may lead to greater compositional dissimilarity and consequently higher species turnover.

Our results indicate that the average species richness in remnants of Araucaria Forest (AF) is lower than in Evergreen Forest (EF) and comparable to Seasonal Forest (SF). The reduced diversity observed in AF, compared to EF, can be attributed to the combined effects of colder temperatures and higher altitudes31, which limit species occurrence along with a legacy of land use degradation32. Despite these constraints, AF exhibits beta-diversity similar to EF, with low beta-nestedness, showing similarly high species turnover. Two potential mechanisms may explain this pattern, both of which could be operating concurrently: (1) the broader geographic range of AF and its associated environmental heterogeneity44 may drive high beta-diversity through species turnover across fragments; and (2) degradation may reduce local species richness, resulting in regional-scale species turnover (i.e., biotic differentiation) that corroborated with our model showing beta-diversity significantly affected by species richness. Additionally, geographic distance, precipitation seasonality, and cattle grazing alter species composition, highlighting the environmental influence and impact of cattle ranching on forest remnants in AF45 (Fig S6 Supplementary Material).

We observed low beta-diversity within the Seasonal Forest (SF), particularly in patches at advanced successional stages. Compared to other forest formations, SF exhibited greater beta-nestedness (i.e. when communities are subgroup of species from a richest group), indicating that its plant communities tend to be subsets of neighboring communities. Seasonal Forest has been regarded as a more recently established vegetation type in Santa Catarina, developing after the grasslands and Araucaria Forests46. Additionally, Gasper et al.47 proposed that SF form a transitional zone with the Araucaria Forests. These historical and ecological factors may contribute to its lower species diversity and higher number of shared species compared to other forest types. On the other hand, SF not only occupies a smaller area compared to EF and AF, but also experiences higher levels of fragmentation and historical land-use degradation, making it the most threatened forest type among these formations48. Despite these pressures, alpha-diversity in SF was similar to that in AF, suggesting potential local biodiversity loss in both forest types.

Contrary to our expectations, floristic similarity reduced with successional stage. Processes of biotic differentiation led by dispersal limitation and context-dependent human impacts may explain the higher similarity among intermediate than among advanced forests. Although intermediate successional stages might have a higher contribution of generalist species, the identity of those species might differ across landscapes (due to different local species pools) corroborating previous studies in the region21. In addition, variations in the type, intensity and extent of the anthropogenic impacts might contribute to the variation in species composition across forest fragments, as found in studies in other tropical forests23,49,50.This result suggests that the great extent of forest fragments at intermediate successional stages in the Atlantic Forest landscapes doesn’t necessarily mean biotic homogenization, although being associated with an reduced alpha-diversity.

Moreover, the greater similarity among advanced successional forests, compared to intermediate ones, may reflect the intense historical exploitation of the Atlantic Forest. Even forests in advanced successional stages have been subjected to anthropogenic impacts, which have affected their ecological structure51. This pattern was further supported by the GDM analysis, in which we considered only forests at advanced stages across all formations, where in addition to environmental variables, anthropogenic factors such as cattle grazing, fire frequency in the surrounding landscape, and the amount of nearby forest cover were identified as significant drivers of community dissimilarity. Therefore, anthropogenic impacts are reducing the beta diversity of forest fragments at advanced successional stages.

It is important to acknowledge certain limitations that may help explain the absence of a clear pattern of floristic homogenization across formations. First, we lack definitive information on the age of the study sites. Second, the successional trajectories may have been interrupted by anthropogenic disturbances not captured by the variables included in our analysis. Third, potential inaccuracies in our classification of successional stages may have influenced the results. Additionally, we did not evaluate composition or species geographic distribution into species composition (i.e. rare and generalist species)19. Such factors may have confounded the identification of overarching ecological trends.

Anthropogenic factors influence beta diversity when all forest formations are considered together, but their effect is less pronounced within each formation individually. This suggests that the presence and nature of anthropogenic impacts vary more among forest formations than within them, which may lead to different ecological responses and conservation needs.

In the Seasonal Forest (SF), beta diversity was not affected by anthropogenic factors. A plausible explanation is the greater historical degradation of this forest type, which may have already filtered out tree species through repeated anthropogenic disturbances. Current characteristics of forest remnants in this formation corroborate the idea that a biotic simplification has happened in SF, such as low: alpha diversity values, high floristic similarity and greater proportion of shared species between forest remnants of SF compared to the other formations. Indeed, previous studies have shown that most regenerating species in these formation belong to early successional stages (i.e., are pioneer species)52. Moreover, a recent study in SF provides evidence of regional homogenization, associated with the dominance of widespread generalist species53. Moreover, this region of the Santa Catarina State has the lower forest cover and smaller forest fragment sizes54 due to higher agricultural suitability due to higher soil fertility and less complex topography. Taken together, these findings may indicate that biotic homogenization may have already happened in SF.

Implications for conservation strategies and actions

AF and EF exhibit high species turnover (i.e., beta diversity) across spatial and environmental gradients compared to SF. These forest formations are known to host a rich assemblage of rare and threatened species33, which play a crucial role in maintaining ecosystem functions55,56. Moreover, networks of small habitat patches might support a greater number of species than large contiguous patches, even when considering only species of conservation concern57. Therefore, effective conservation strategies for forests characterized by high beta diversity should prioritize the establishment of networks of protected areas that capture this variation. Rather than focusing solely on large individual parks, conservation efforts should be emphasized creating multiple spatially distinct reserves to better preserve biodiversity at larger scales and ecosystem functionality58. However, a recent study showed that larger fragments support more complex communities and sustain key ecological processes that smaller patches cannot maintain in fragmented landscapes59.

While we advocate for the conservation of all classes of small forest patches within the Atlantic Forest region, our results highlight that within-site species richness (i.e., alpha diversity) varies along spatial gradients. This suggests that conservation efforts could be more effective if focused on sites with high species diversity, and especially on key sites of critical conservation value, rather than on species-poor areas18. In this context, studies on species distributions are essential for setting conservation priorities. Furthermore, understanding the ecology of rare and threatened species targeted by restoration initiatives can greatly enhance biodiversity recovery efforts. Identifying the main drivers of biodiversity loss can guide the development of more effective mitigation strategies. For example, cattle grazing alters species composition in the AF, highlighting the need to engage rural landowners in forest conservation efforts. This stakeholder group is crucial, as most forested areas are privately owned60. Increasing awareness about the ecological and economic benefits of native forests—such as ecosystem services provided by forests and sustainable use of natural resources can strengthen conservation initiatives in human-modified landscapes.

To effectively monitor and manage tree assemblages in the EF affected by landscape fires, fire prevention measures must be implemented. Controlling fire spread is essential to minimize its impact on species composition and maintain ecosystem stability. Strategies such as firebreaks, controlled burns, and community-based fire prevention programs can help mitigate damage and contribute to forest conservation.

The Atlantic Forest represents the refuge for Brazil’s rich biodiversity in the south of Brazil, yet it remains highly threatened by human activities. In the face of escalating challenges such as climate and land use changes, this study provides crucial insights into the patterns of tree diversity across the Southern Atlantic Forest. Our findings highlight the importance of considering biodiversity variation among different forest formations and the role of anthropogenic impacts in shaping these patterns. These results can help guiding the development of targeted and effective conservation strategies that promote the long-term resilience and survival of these ecosystems.

Methods

This study encompasses the forest formations located in the Santa Catarina State at the Southern region of the Atlantic Forest Domain. Three forest formations were analyzed: Evergreen forest, Araucaria Forest and Seasonal Forest. The Evergreen Forest (EF) originally covered 29,309 km² in Santa Catarina state61, accounting for approximately 31% of the state area. According to Vibrans et al.62, only 16,821 km² of forest fragments remain, most of which classified as at intermediate stages of succession, representing 40% of the original forest cover63. This forest formation is located at the eastern part of the state, ranging along an altitudinal gradient from the coastal plains at sea level until the mountain ranges at approximately 1,500 m a.s.l38. The Araucaria Forest (AF) originally covered an area of 42,851 km² in Santa Catarina, which is 44.94% of the state’s territory32. Currently, the AF covers 22% of its original area64, depicting the significant alteration of its original coverage. This forest is located between regions from 500 to 1,500 m a.s.l. at the altiplano (or highlands)64. The Seasonal Semi-deciduous Forest (SF), is located in the western part of the state, mainly in the Uruguay river basin, in an altitudinal range between 150 and 800 m (exceptionally 900 m61. SFs were severely affected by human occupation in the Santa Catarina state since the second half of the 20th century65, being the most deforested and degraded among the three forest formations analyzed here. Currently, only around 16.3% of its original area remains, with 52% of the fragments being smaller than 50 hectares, indicating the extensive fragmentation of those forests54.

Data were compiled from the Floristic and Forest Inventory of Santa Catarina State (hereafter FlorestaSC) sampled between 2007 and 2010 (see fieldwork details in62. It includes the three forest formations present in the Santa Catarina State. The sampling design for was based on a 10 km × 10 km grid covering the entire state, except for the SF, where a finer 5 km × 5 km grid was used in order to guarantee sampling the few remaining forest fragments in this relatively small forest formation62. Each sample unit (SU) had included a total area of 0.4 ha, distributed in four subplots of 20 × 50 m arranged in a cross, aligned along the cardinal points. Only trees with a diameter > 10 cm were considered in our analyses. Because the SU were systematically located, some were not entirely covered by forests. We excluded samples units with an area lower than or equal to 0.35 ha, as well as those located at early successional stages. The FlorestaSC’ database includes 418 sampled units, where the number of sampling units for each forest formation is proportional to the total area of each formation66. Thus, in our study, we analyzed 331 sampling units, 60 in SF (35 in intermediate successional stage and 25 in advanced successional stage), 129 in EF (50 in intermediate successional stage and 79 in advanced successional stage), and 142 in AF (63 in intermediate successional stage and 79 in advanced successional stage) (Fig. 3).

We used the successional stages classification as defined by FlorestaSC botanists, including a quantitative and qualitative assessment, taking into account the anthropogenic impacts to which these forests are subjected and their structural attributes, as the number of plant strata, the presence of large trees, the presence of lianas and epiphytes and the diversity of plants. In this way, intermediate successional forests present species richness between 10 and 30 species, with climax species still lacking in significant numbers; multiple vegetation layers and where large, shade-tolerant species are absent; and advanced successional forests were marked by greater species richness (> 30 species), the presence of large-diameter trees and epiphytes, resembling the old-growth stage, although showing signs of human impact. All plots are located in landscapes partially or totally affected by human activities62.

Data analyses

Alpha diversity Estimation and variation across forest formations

In order to estimate species alpha-diversity, we calculated the Hill’s diversity index for each forest formation (SF, EF, AF) considering all selected plots. Hill’s diversity index is recognized for providing the true diversity of plant communities67. When q = 0, all relative abundances are raised to zero (equal weight for rare species, corresponding to average species richness or number of species within an SU); when q = 1, abundances are as shown and comparable to the exponential of Shannon entropy index; while q = 2, abundances are squared, giving greater weight to the most abundant species, corresponding to the inverse of Simpson’s index (the probability that two randomly selected individuals belong to the same species).

To assess differences in Hill numbers between forest types and successional stages, we performed a two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc tests. Statistical differences between groups were summarized using letters, with groups sharing the same letter showing no significant differences (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1).

Beta-diversity Estimation and variation across forest formations

We defined beta-diversity as the variation in species composition among SUs. Beta diversity was quantified using the Baselga (2017)68 framework, which partitions dissimilarity based on species abundances, based on the Bray-Curtis index, into two components: (1) beta turnover - or substitution of species, representing species replacement between assemblages; and (2) beta nestedness – occurring when assemblages represent a subgroup of richer communities. We used the Betapart package69 in the R, version 4.3.2.

In addition, to investigate how similar in species composition are the three forest formations and the two successional stages, we performed a NMDS (Non-Metric Multidimensional Scaling) ordination based on the Bray-curtis dissimilarity, which accounts for the relative abundances of individual species. To test floristic separation, the variation of NMDS scores between forest formations was tested using a non-parametric multivariate analysis (ANOSIM).

Drivers of diversity: environmental and anthropogenic factors

We considered climate, soil, and anthropogenic predictors to understand their influence on beta- and alpha-diversity among the three forest formations. Previously, we tested the Pearson correlation between numerical predictors to eliminate strong potential covariates (Pearson > 0.7) (Fig S1 – Supplementar material).

Environmental factors: climate and soil

Environmental factors were selected based on their ecological relevance to our inquiries. Climate data were extracted from the Chelsa database70. The climatic predictors selected were the historical mean of: mean annual air temperature - Bio1 (°C), temperature seasonality- Bio4 (°C), Bio5: mean daily maximum air temperature of the warmest month (°C), Bio7: annual range of air temperature (°C), Bio12: annual precipitation amount (kg m-²), Bio13: precipitation amount of the wettest month (kg m-²), Bio14: precipitation amount of the driest month (kg m-²), Bio15: precipitation seasonality (kg m-²). After eliminating highly correlated factors and considering the relevance of predictors for our analyses, we used five climatic factors in further analyses: mean annual air temperature, temperature seasonality, mean daily maximum air temperature of the warmest month, annaul range of air temperature and precipitation seasonality .

Edaphic variables were extracted from the SoilGrids database (soilgrids.org), at 250 m resolution and averaged over the first 30 cm of the soil depth. Soil conditions used in further analyses were: soil pH, bulk density (g cm -³ - soil dry mass over soil volume), volumetric carbon concentration in the soil (C proportion of sand (> 0.05 mm), silt 0.002 –0.05 mm) and clay (< 0.002 mm).

Anthropogenic factors

Anthropogenic impacts were described at two scales. At the landscape scale, we extracted landscape descriptors of forest cover and fragmentation when the field inventories took place, and the land-use history during 26 years before it.

Elevation also was considered as a predictor in our analyses.

At the plot scale, we used the occurrence of different descriptors of anthropogenic disturbance that were visually identified during the field expeditions (data from FlorestaSC). Below we describe the methods for acquiring each metric.

Landscape descriptors of forest cover and fragmentation - For each SU, we set three concentric circles (buffers) with different radii sizes: 750 m, 1000 m, and 1500 m, considering the sampling unit as the central point of each landscape. For each buffer size, we calculated the (a) amount of forest vegetation (expressed in hectares), which represents the forest cover and the quantity of possible species habitats in the landscape71; and the (b) patch isolation (expressed in meters) measured as the mean distance between the edges of forest patches, which is related to the reduction of habitat quality in the fragment. Forest cover was extracted from the land use and land cover product of the Mapbiomas 7th collection for the year 2010, which was just before the forest inventories were done. The two landscape metrics were acquired using the “raster” and “landscapemetrics” packages, from the R software72,73 .

The land-use history around each SU was characterized by three metrics: land-use duration, number of land-use changes and fire frequency during the period between 1988 and 2011. For that, we applied a buffer of 1000 m radius around each SU and extracted the following metrics: (i) Previous land-use duration was characterized as the average of the total number of years each pixel was classified as non-forest. This metric represents how long the surroundings of the SU have been used for alternative land uses, as longer land use duration leads to stronger anthropogenic impacts on forest ecosystems74. (ii) Number of land-use changes is the frequency that a land cover class changed to another along the time series. It was calculated within the 1.000 m buffer as the average number of changes in the pixels within the buffer. (iii) Fire frequency was estimated as the average number of years each pixel was detected as burned along the time series within a buffer of 5.000 m. We did not consider the number of times a pixel was burned within a year, hence recording only one fire event per pixel per year, which represents the number of years a pixel has been burned over the time series. We used a buffer of 5.000 m because the source data was the MODIS fire product that has a resolution of 500 m.

Local scale anthropogenic disturbances were identified within each sampling unit by visual interpretation of the presence or absence (binary information) of nine types of disturbance as identified in the field by the FlorestaSC teams: roads crossing, yerba mate exploitation, fire scars, garbage dumping, presence of exotic species, grazing, hunting traces, vegetation suppression and selective logging. We explored this information in two ways: (a) using an anthropogenic index calculated as the sum of the presence of all disturbances dividing by 9 to each SUs, and (b) we selected the three anthropogenic impacts that were most robust and relevant for this study to be used separately in the models: cattle grazing, clear-cutting and roads.

Statistical analyses

Evaluating the drivers of diversity

To describe how the alpha diversity differs between forest formations and successional stages we performed ANOVA tests on the hill number series (hill0, hill1, hill2). To evaluate how species richness (hill0) is affected by environmental and anthropogenic factors, we performed linear regression analyses. Before this analysis we verified multicollinearity removing predictors with high correlation (VIF > 5).

To investigate the role of geographical distance, environmental and anthropogenic factors on species dissimilarity (beta-diversity) we used two Generalized Dissimilarity Model (GDM). First we built a GDM for all SUs across forest formations, in order to evaluate how the predictors explain the beta diversity across the entire Santa Catarina state. Then, we built a GDM for SUs within each forest formation and successional stages separately in order to understand which and how the predictors explain the beta diversity within each forest formation (EF, AF and SF).

The GDM explains pairwise dissimilarities between sampling units by adjusting a linear model with geographic distances and other drivers of interest employing a maximum likelihood and I-splines to model and forecast species turnover75. This model uses Pairwise dissimilarities calculated using the Bray-Curtis index, which accounts for species abundances through the Bray-Curtis index to test the influence of predictors on species turnover components76. The predictors measured on different scales were transformed during model fitting to ensure that the adjusted distances between pairs of locations for different predictors can be meaningfully compared and integrated. GDM was fitted using maximum likelihood and I-splines to capture nonlinear species–environment relationships. Predictor significance was assessed through Monte Carlo permutation tests, following Ferrier et al.75, and a backward elimination procedure was applied to retain only the significant variables and most parsimonious models. Specifically, we used the gdm.varImp function from the R package gdm, which compares the deviance explained by the observed model with that of models fitted to permuted data. We ran 999 permutations and considered predictors significant at α = 0.05.
