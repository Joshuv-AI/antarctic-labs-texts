A User‐Centric Comprehension Assessment of Prescription Drug Package Inserts in China: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Weiwei Zhang, Li Du, Shu Tan, He Zhao, Jing Chen, Wenya Wang, Jing Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDrug package inserts (DPIs) are crucial to ensure patients use drugs appropriately. Currently, only standard DPIs are available in China. Users' comprehension of standard DPIs and patient‐centered DPIs, and the factors influencing their comprehension, remain poorly understood.AimOur objective was to evaluate and compare users' comprehension of standard DPIs and patient‐centered DPIs. Additionally, we aimed to explore the association between DPI types and users' comprehension and other influencing factors.MethodsWe selected two standard DPIs and developed two corresponding patient‐centered DPIs for examination in 258 participants. Participants were divided into four groups (standard DPI A and B groups, patient DPI A and B groups) and interviewed using a structured questionnaire to test their comprehension of DPIs. We scored the participants' responses and recorded the time taken during the interviews. Regression models were employed to analyze the association between DPI types and comprehension.ResultsParticipants in the patient DPI groups scored significantly higher than those in the standard DPI groups [9.47 (±4.56) versus 6 (±4.02) in the DPI A group; 11.94 (±2.99) versus 9.25 (±2.70) in the DPI B group, p < 0.001]. Participants in the patient DPI groups spent less time during the interviews compared to those in the standard DPI groups (543 [±274.74] s versus 776.11 [±264.03] s in the DPI A group and 457.25 [±213.01] s versus 729.55 [±278.14] s in the DPI B group, p < 0.001). Patient‐centered DPIs were positively associated with good comprehension (OR: 80.97; 95% CI: 9.77~670.76; p < 0.001) and negatively associated with time consumption (Coef.: −230.20; 95% CI: −299.13, −161.27; p < 0.001). Education level, age, and income were also identified as potential contributors.ConclusionsComparative analysis showed patient‐centered DPI design significantly improves medication comprehension, supporting its use for effective information delivery.

Introduction

Drug package inserts (DPIs) serve as essential legal documents containing technical and scientific information about medicines and medication use guide [1]. Users' comprehension of DPIs directly impact the degree of using medications correctly. Poorly understanding of DPIs may result in medication misuse [2] and lead to medication errors and causing drug‐related hospitalizations [3, 4, 5, 6].

Most of DPIs usually use complex language and lots of medical terms to meet legal requirements, which are not patient friendly. To address this issue, some countries and organizations have implemented several strategies to enhance the quality and readability of DPIs. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) mandates that medications placed in the market must be accompanied by patient package inserts with clear and simple text [7]. All written medication information should be tested with the consumer legibility mandatorily in the European Union [7]. Similarly, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has established standards for drug labeling content, formats, and sequences, requiring select prescription medications to be distributed with patient package inserts [8, 9]. In Australia, DPIs are regulated by Usability Guidelines to ensure legislative compliance and readability [10].

Despite these regulatory efforts, studies have shown persistent challenges in patients' understanding of package inserts [11, 12, 13]. In many developing countries, such as China, this issue is particularly pronounced because only standard DPIs are available. Although the China Food and Drug Administration revised the DPIs of 32 over‐the‐counter drugs in 2018 [14] to help users understand DPIs, there is still a lack of patient‐centered package inserts to assist patients in fully comprehending the contents of the medication instructions. Thoroughly comprehending the DPIs is crucial for the correct use of drugs, especially for prescription medications, as inappropriate use of prescription drugs may lead to more serious adverse events.

Recent research evaluating over‐the‐counter (OTC) DPIs in China revealed unsatisfactory readability based on internal and international guidelines [15]. However, no studies have examined users' comprehension of DPIs in China to date. Furthermore, research on DPI comprehension tests in prescription drugs remains limited, despite their frequent use by patients without comprehensive education from healthcare providers, particularly in developing countries.

Our study aims to assess users' comprehension of current standard DPIs versus newly designed patient‐centered DPIs and further examine the correlation between patient‐centered DPI implementation and comprehension enhancement. Additionally, we seek to explore other potential factors influencing users' comprehension of DPIs.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study employed a cross‐sectional design. Participants were eligible if they were 18 years or older and could read and express themselves without difficulty. Exclusion criteria included: (a) individuals who had participated in similar DPI testing within the past 6 months; (b) those experiencing difficulty in reading or expression; (c) individuals who had undergone any medical or medication training program other than basic medical first aid; (d) those with cognitive impairments such as Alzheimer's Disease; (e) participants with immediate family members among the research team; (f) individuals who did not consent to be recorded during the interview process. We calculated the sample size based on the recommendations from the European commission guideline on the readability of the labeling and package leaflet of medication products for human use, which recommended 23 ~ 25 people to be interviewed for each package leaflet [16]. Since we planned to test 4 DPIs (2 standard DPIs and 2 patient DPIs), we set the sample size at 260 individuals. We enrolled 260 participants from December 2022 to March 2023, but two individuals were subsequently excluded because of their medical backgrounds. Thus, 258 participants were included in the study. All participants provided informed consent upon enrollment. Demographic characteristics of participants were collected. We also investigated the health literacy level of all participants through European Health literacy Survey Questionnaire (HLS‐EU‐Q47) [17] and classified their literacy level as excellent, sufficient, problematic and inadequate according to the average score they got.

Participants were randomly allocated to either the standard DPI group (S group) or the patient DPI group (P group) with an electronic randomization tool based on the educational level of the participants. According to the randomization results, each participant was assigned a unique identification number and ultimately 128 participants were allocated in S group and 130 participants were allocated in P group. To mitigate the bias due to product itself, two prescription drugs (Drug A: XINSUMIN and Drug B: AIBEIXI) of different therapeutic areas were selected for the DPI development. Each group was further divided into two DPI subgroups, resulting in four subgroups in total (SA, SB, PA, PB). The flow chart is showed in Supporting Information S1. The two standard DPIs (Supporting Information S2 and S3) used for testing were authentic prescription drug DPIs with only the drug name altered, while the corresponding patient‐centered DPIs were newly developed in accordance with European Medication Agency guidelines [7] with considerations of public feedback from previous survey. Compared with standard DPIs, the main improvement measures for patient‐centered DPIs include: (a) simplifying the information of each DPI into six parts according to the patient's needs and medication habit; (b) minimizing the use of long sentences and instead present them in short sentences; (c) applying bullet points to make information presentation more organized; (d) using partition and block display of drug information; (e) highlighting the titles of each section and important safety information in the DPIs with different colors; (f) using easy to understand patient friendly language to describe the content of the DPIs and avoid using too many professional terms; (g) reducing textual descriptions and displaying usage and dosage information in the form of a table for the “usage and dosage” section; (h) providing the dosage and minimum packaging quantity corresponding to different body weights to avoid patients or their caregivers calculating medication dosage by themselves. Before formal study, we recruited 8 participants to conduct pre‐testing on the patient instructions formed. These 8 participants included 4 males and 4 females and the average age was 58 (±27.38). Among them, 1 had a bachelor's degree, 2 had an associate's degree, 1 had a high school diploma, 2 had a junior high school diploma, 1 had an elementary school diploma, and 1 had an unknown educational background. During pre‐testing, we found some participants answered questions based on their common sense but not according to the DPIs. Therefore, we modified the expression of questions of the questionnaire to make them only be answerable based on DPI contents. Additionally, we refined the procedures for the subsequent formal comprehension tests based on the observations of participants' responses from the pre‐testing process. For instance, in the pre‐testing, some participants were not confident enough or dared not answer the questions, especially those with lower levels of education. Therefore, we felt it necessary to make a brief introduction of the study and encourage all participants to actively answer all the test questions in the subsequent formal tests. Moreover, some participants in pre‐testing were able to provide correct answers based on intuition or from incorrect paragraphs. As a result, we thought we should emphasize that participants must answer the test questions directly according to the DPIs provided in the formal tests. In summary, the pre‐testing allowed us to optimize the testing DPIs and refine the protocol for the formal test, thereby ensuring the acquisition of objective feedback of participants and facilitating the seamless progression of the formal testing phase that follows. The patient‐centered DPIs were finally formatted according to the style of the drug packaging instructions, resulting in the final draft of the patient‐centered DPIs (Supporting Information S4 and S5) for testing.

Comprehension Assessment

We designed a survey questionnaire based on the content of DPIs, including 15 questions related to indications, side effects, usage, interactions, overdose, pediatric dosage, duration, contraindications, ingredients, storage, and safety to assess the comprehension of DPI A (XINSUMIN) and DPI B (AIBEIXI) (Supporting Information S6). Questionnaire reliability and validity was assessed before participant interviews. All participants in the four groups were one on one interviewed by researchers (WWZ, HJM, JKX, XW, KS, YY, FL, and WZ) to answer the 15 questionnaire questions after completing DPI reading. To ensure methodological rigor, all researchers completed standardized training before data collection. The training encompassed (1) research protocol familiarization, (2) standardized administration procedures (including interview techniques and scoring rubrics), and (3) certification in data recording methods. Required materials, including DPIs, validated questionnaires, digital recording systems, and standardized assessment forms, were systematically prepared. Responses were scored based on predetermined criteria (0 points for incorrect, 1 point for correct), and audio recordings for all interviews were made for quality check. Comprehension was defined as the proportion of correct answers to the 15 questions, with a total possible score of 15 points. A correct answer rate exceeding 80% (12 points) was considered indicative of “good comprehension.” Additionally, since time consumption during reading is also a factor reflecting individual comprehension, we calculated the total time each participant spent on reading and completing the questionnaires during the interview as another indicator of DPI comprehension evaluation. This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tsinghua Changgung Hospital (IRB Approval No. 22217‐4‐02).

Statistical Analysis

Data were coded and analyzed using STATA (version 15, StataCorp) and SPSS (version 27, IBM). Descriptive statistics including means, standard deviations (SD), medians and frequencies were calculated for all variables. The data were analyzed using two‐sided tests, with a p value less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. For quantitative data, values following a normal distribution are presented as mean (SD) and compared between groups using t‐tests. For data that does not adhere to a normal distribution, the median (interquartile range) (M[IQR]) is used, with group comparisons conducted using the Mann‐Whitney U test. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages, and group comparisons are performed using the chi‐square test. A binary logistic regression model was constructed to analyze the independent effects of factors such as age, gender, education level, average household income, health literacy level, and DPI types on the comprehensibility of the DPIs. The regression coefficient β was calculated, and the odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were used to assess the magnitude of the correlation. Multivariate general linear models (GLMs) were employed to investigate the association between DPI types and total interview time.

Comprehension Assessment

Figures 1 and 2 depict the comprehension scores and total interviewing time for the four groups. Regarding to groups reading DPI A, participants in the standard DPI group (SA) scored an average of 6 (±4.02) points and took 776.11 (±264.03) seconds to complete the questionnaire, while those in the patient DPI group (PA) scored 9.47 (±4.56) points and spent 543 (±274.74) seconds on the test. There was a statistically significant difference between the SA and PA groups (p < 0.001). Similar results were observed for groups reading DPI B, with participants in the standard DPI group (SB) scoring 9.25 (±2.70) points and taking 729.55 (±278.14) seconds, while those in the patient DPI group (PB) scored higher [11.94 (±2.99 points)] and spent less time (457.25 [±213.01] seconds) on the interview, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, we assessed the participants' comprehension of patient‐centered DPIs and standard DPIs using a questionnaire‐based test among a Chinese population. Our findings revealed that participants who read patient‐centered DPIs achieved higher comprehension scores and spent less time compared to those reading standard DPIs. Regression analysis further confirmed the association between patient‐centered DPIs and better comprehension scores, as well as reduced time consumption during the test. Education levels emerged as another significant factor influencing comprehension in our study. Additionally, education levels, advanced age (≥ 65) and high family monthly income (≥ $1367/month) were found to impact the time participants spent on reading and answering questions of DPIs during interview process.

Previous research has underscored the importance of DPI comprehension in ensuring patients' comprehension and proper medication usage [18]. Standard DPIs, typically authored by pharmaceutical companies and regulated by national authorities, often present challenges due to their complex medical terminology and dense information [18, 19]. Recognizing this issue, global health organizations like the World Health Organization (WHO) have advocated for the inclusion of patient‐centered DPIs alongside standard ones [20] and have released guidelines to enhance the comprehension of pharmaceutical product packaging [21]. Regulatory authorities such as the FDA and EMA have also established standards for drug label readability, emphasizing patient‐centered approaches in content, format, layout, and language [7, 8, 9]. Indeed, studies have shown that patient‐centered DPIs or PILs exhibit superior comprehension compared to traditional ones [22, 23, 24]. Our findings align with prior research. Participants reading patient‐centered DPIs had better comprehension and consumed less time in reading and answering questions during interview process than those reading standard DPIs.

Human factors such as age, educational level, and prior medication experience may impact DPI comprehension [25]. In this study, we analyzed the association of DPI comprehension and DPI forms and other factors including age, gender, education level, residence, income, and literacy level. Our result demonstrated that patient‐centered DPIs positively associated with good comprehension (OR = 80.97; 95% CI: 9.77, 670.76; p < 0.001) which defined as comprehension scores no less than 12 points. This result was in accordance with other studies [19, 26, 27]. Consistent with previous research, our study revealed that higher education levels were associated with better comprehension scores [25, 28]. Participants graduated from middle school, high school and college were more likely to get better comprehension scores when compared to those whose education level was primary school. One unexpected result was the association of good comprehension and health literacy level. In our study, the correlation between good comprehension and health literacy level were not significant in our study, which was not in accordance with other study [29]. We thought it may result from we exclude participants who had medical ground and the participants' literacy levels were relatively homogenous. Another possible reason was the sample size was not big enough to detect the association, suggesting a nuanced relationship that warrants further investigation.

Reading speed, alongside word recognition, serves as a fundamental aspect of reading comprehension [30, 31]. Our study assessed participants' time consumption in the interview process including reading DPIs and completing the questionnaires as another indicator to assess participants' comprehension. Participants reading patient‐centered DPIs consumed less time compared to participants reading standard DPIs [543 (±274.74) seconds versus 776.11 (±264.03) seconds in DPI A group; 457.25 (±213.01) seconds versus 729.55 (±278.14) seconds in DPI B group]. The result further corroborated the superiority of patient‐centered DPIs in terms of comprehension. The GLM regression results demonstrated that participants reading patient‐centered DPIs negatively associated with time consuming with a significant statistic difference (Coef. = − 230.20; 95% CI: −299.13, − 161.27; p < 0.001). Moreover, education level emerged as another significant factor associated with time consuming in a negative way. The higher the education levels of participants were, the less time they spent on the DPI comprehension tests. This association between education level and understanding of medical documents was identified in other studies [32, 33]. Advanced age (65 and above) and high income (more than $1367) were also associated with time consumption in comprehension tests. Old adults usually have multiple diseases and take many medications and their sensory and cognitive functions are decreased compared to young people, therefore they tend to spend more time reading DPIs. People with high incomes may pay more attention to their health in contrast to people with limited resources, which leads to them spending longer time reading DPIs and answering related questions.

DPIs are the mostly used materials for conducting essential medication and health‐related information. Assessing the users' comprehension of DPIs is paramount for understanding the acceptance of DPIs and further helping us improve them. While some studies have employed tools like the Flesch Reading Ease scores or Flesch‐Kincaid Grade Level to assess readability [34, 35], our study utilized one‐to‐one interviews, offering a nuanced comprehension test of DPIs from the users' perspective. In our study, we first used the one‐to‐one interview method to assess and compare users' comprehension of standard DPIs and patient‐centered DPIs in a Chinese population. Moreover, we firstly utilized prescription DPIs and their corresponding patient‐centered counterparts to assess user comprehension as far as we know. The results were meaningful for further promoting the application of patient‐centered DPIs in China and other developing countries. Notably, our research found that DPI types, education levels, advanced age and high income associated with user's DPI comprehension, highlighting the need for corresponding improvements to enhance DPI comprehensibility.

There are some limitations of our study. First, the sample lacked representation from various geographic and socioeconomic backgrounds in China. We plan to include a more diverse range of populations, including rural and underprivileged populations, in our future studies. Secondly, we selected two DPIs as examples to test users' comprehension which may not reflect users' responses to all DPIs. We will utilize more DPIs to test users' comprehension in the future research. Thirdly, this study employed a cross‐sectional design, which precluded the detection of clinical outcomes such as whether a better understanding of the information leads to correct medication usage and improved health. We will follow‐up tracking and hope to design prospective research to investigate the effects of various types of medication instructions on patients' medication‐taking behaviors, adherence to medication regimens, and therapeutic outcomes in the future.

Conclusion

DPIs serve as indispensable resources for patients in navigating medication therapy and understanding diseases. Our findings underscore the superiority of patient‐centered DPIs in comprehension compared to standard DPIs, suggesting the efficacy of patient‐centered approaches in conveying medication information. Additionally, education levels, age, and income emerged as factors influencing DPI comprehension. To optimize medication communication and promote patient understanding, concerted efforts are needed to revamp existing standard DPIs and enhance their effectiveness in conveying crucial health information. Such initiatives are essential to advancing patient‐centered care and improving health outcomes.
