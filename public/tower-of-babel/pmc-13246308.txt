Customizing native T1 mapping: The effects of compressed sensing, deep learning-based denoising, and high-resolution on measurement of native myocardial T1
Authors: Alessio Perazzolo, Camilla V Vita, Vincenzo Scialò, Mohamed Gamal, Elisa Bruno, Tzu Cheng Chao, Jacinta Browne, Burak Demirel, Spencer Waddle, Tim Leiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundQuantitative native T1 mapping is a key component in cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) for myocardial tissue characterization; however, further improvements in acquisition efficiency and robustness are needed to optimize clinical applicability. Undersampling techniques in k-space, such as compressed sensing (CS) and deep learning-based (DL) denoising reconstructions, have improved morphological and cine-imaging, but their impact on quantitative relaxation times remains underexplored. This study evaluated image quality and native T1 quantification across ten combinations with varying CS acceleration levels, spatial resolutions, and application of DL-denoising reconstruction.MethodsIn this prospective single-center study, 48 healthy volunteers underwent native T1 mapping. After quality review, 41 subjects were included. Blurring, aliasing, susceptibility artifacts, and overall image quality (IQ) were rated by three blinded readers using a 4-point Likert scale. Quantitative analysis was performed on a per-segment basis using custom software, yielding mean native T1 values for each AHA segment. Nine cases were reanalyzed by three additional readers to assess interobserver variability. Test–retest and phantom experiments were performed to assess reproducibility and to cover pathological T1 ranges, respectively. The reference T1-mapping protocol was obtained with CS3, with spatial resolution of 2.0×2.0×10 mm3. Additional acquisitions were obtained with CS3, 4, 5, and spatial resolutions of 2.0×2.0×10 mm3, 1.8×1.8×10 mm3, and 1.6×1.6×10 mm3 for CS3 and 2.0×2.0×10 mm3 for CS4 and 5.ResultsSignificant differences in IQ, blurring, and aliasing were observed among acquisition protocols (p<0.05), but not in susceptibility artifacts (p=0.66). Higher CS levels slightly reduced IQ and increased aliasing and blurring. DL-denoising and higher spatial resolution methods improved sharpness without changing overall scores. Segment-wise T1 quantitative analysis revealed only minor differences, even in the presence of high-acceleration factors or increased spatial resolution. In most segments, two one-sided testing confirmed equivalence with low bias (−21 to +22 ms). Scan–rescan experiments confirmed repeatability in healthy volunteers, while phantom experiments extended repeatability and inter-method stability to pathological T1 ranges.ConclusionNative T1 values remained stable across various CS acceleration factors, changes in spatial resolution, and application of denoising, with clinically negligible bias for tissue characterization. These findings, within a framework where total acquisition time is primarily determined by the number of inversion time (TI) images, support the use of CS to improve temporal resolution within each TI image, benefiting patients with arrhythmias or elevated heart rates, and also support the use of denoising and higher spatial resolution to improve image details. Overall, these results can enable patient-tailored T1 mapping protocols, improving the efficiency and diagnostic utility of quantitative CMR.Keywords: Native T1 mapping, MOLLI, Compressed sensing, Denoising, Deep learning reconstruction, Spatial resolution

Introduction

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) has increasingly become a cornerstone in the diagnostic and prognostic evaluation of cardiac diseases [1], [2]. In addition to being the reference standard for morphological and functional assessment, CMR plays a key role in myocardial tissue characterization, where quantitative parametric mapping techniques are increasingly valuable tools. T1 mapping, the focus of this study, provides quantitative information for myocardial tissue characterization, including native T1 and extracellular volume fraction (ECV); the latter requires the combination of native T1, post-contrast T1, and the patient’s hematocrit to quantify the extracellular space [3], [4].

While CMR is increasingly valuable for evaluating cardiovascular function, one major limitation of this modality is acquisition speed. Acceleration techniques have been developed that shorten CMR acquisition duration, among them compressive sensitivity encoding (CS) and deep learning-based (DL) denoising reconstructions, which aim to reduce scan duration while maintaining high image quality.

CS is a signal processing framework which operates on the principle that magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) signal inherently contains redundant information; this property can be leveraged to reconstruct high-quality images from incoherently undersampled k-space data. The CS framework relies on three core principles as follows: sparsity, incoherent sampling, and nonlinear iterative reconstruction. In MRI, CS is implemented by acquiring fewer phase-encoding steps in a variable-density and pseudo-random pattern, which introduces incoherent aliasing artifacts. They are mitigated through iterative reconstruction algorithms that enforce both data fidelity and sparsity constraints, enabling a significant reduction in scan time without a proportional loss in image quality [5]. However, when undersampling is too aggressive or regularization parameters are not well calibrated, blurring of fine structures and spatial smoothing may occur [5]. When combined with conventional parallel imaging or simultaneous multi-slice (SMS) methods, such as sensitivity encoding (SENSE) or generalized autocalibrating partially parallel acquisitions (GRAPPA), CS offers a synergistic acceleration strategy, further reducing scan time, particularly in applications requiring volumetric coverage and short imaging times [6], [7], [8].

DL denoising is an increasingly widely available method on modern MRI equipment to directly reduce residual noise in the image reconstruction pipeline. Unlike traditional post-processing filters, DL-denoising employs convolutional neural networks (CNNs) during the reconstruction phase and operates on undersampled multi-coil data, enforcing data consistency while suppressing noise-related components [9], [10]. Importantly, by integrating the CNN-based denoising step early in the reconstruction process and leveraging raw k-space information together with coil sensitivity profiles, DL-based reconstruction preserves fine anatomical details while selectively reducing noise, rather than applying uniform spatial smoothing to the final magnitude images. This approach enhances image quality, particularly in low-signal-to-noise (SNR) sequences or with aggressive acceleration, and can be used to mitigate the risk of over-smoothing anatomical structures [9].

These techniques have shown great utility across various body regions. In musculoskeletal MRI, combined approaches integrating CS with DL-denoising have reduced scan times by up to 75% while maintaining diagnostic quality [11], [12]. In neuroimaging, artificial intelligence-assisted compressed sensing (AI-CS) has been shown to cut acquisition times for three-dimensional sequences by over 60% without compromising diagnostic confidence [13]. In liver MRI, studies have primarily evaluated CS acceleration alone, demonstrating faster imaging with comparable or superior image quality to conventional techniques, reducing motion artifacts and maintaining lesion detectability [14], [15].

Notably, in CMR, CS, and denoising techniques have proven effective in both morpho-functional assessments and tissue characterization. CS has enabled single-breath-hold cine steady-state free precession (SSFP) imaging for rapid quantification of left ventricular function, volumes, and mass [16], [17]. In tissue characterization, high-resolution late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging with CS has demonstrated improved clinical performance [18]. However, while accelerated imaging techniques have been explored in several CMR applications [19], [20], there is a lack of literature investigating their impact on quantitative CMR mapping.

In addition to CS acceleration and DL-denoising, spatial resolution represents another potentially critical factor influencing T1 mapping performance, as variations in spatial resolution may influence image sharpness, SNR, and the fidelity of T1 quantification, especially in thin myocardial segments or at higher heart rates [21]. The Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (SCMR) recommends maintaining adequate spatial resolution to minimize partial volume effects and ensure accurate delineation of myocardial borders, particularly at the endocardial interface. While no strict cut-offs are provided, commonly used in-plane resolutions range between 1.6×1.6 mm and 2.0×2.0 mm [22]. The effect of spatial resolution on native T1 mapping has received limited attention in the literature, and its interaction with acceleration and denoising strategies remains poorly understood.

This study addresses a current gap in the literature by systematically evaluating the agreement across multiple T1 mapping protocols that differ in CS acceleration, spatial resolution, and the application of DL-denoising. We performed both qualitative and quantitative assessments of native T1 maps acquired using ten distinct acquisition and reconstruction strategies. This comprehensive evaluation was designed to elucidate their individual and synergistic impact on image quality and T1 quantification in healthy volunteers.

Material and methods

Study design and CMR protocol

The study was structured as a cross-sectional prospective single-center investigation with approval for all study procedures from the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB 18–009727). From April 14, 2023 to September 17, 2024, healthy volunteers provided informed consent and underwent multiple T1 mapping scans acquired with varying CS acceleration levels and spatial resolutions using a 3T Philips MRI scanner (Philips Ingenia Elition X, Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands). The imaging protocol consisted of cine-SSFP sequences of conventional single-slice (left ventricular [LV] two-chamber, LV three-chamber, and four-chamber) and volumetric multi-slice (short-axis) views. Modified Look-Locker Inversion Recovery (MOLLI) T1 mapping sequences were performed on three standard short-axis slices (basal, mid-ventricular, and apical) using different CS acceleration levels (CS3, 4, and 5) and varying spatial resolutions (2.0×2.0×10 mm3, 1.8×1.8×10 mm3, and 1.6×1.6×10 mm3 for CS3 and 2.0×2.0×10 mm3 for CS4 and CS5). These acceleration and spatial resolution settings were selected a priori as a pragmatic compromise between increased scan acceleration and the anticipated need for sufficient k-space data to ensure robust myocardial T1 estimation. All MOLLI T1-mapping acquisitions were performed with a reconstructed field of view of 300×300 mm², a reconstruction matrix of 256×256 and a flip angle of 20°. Receiver bandwidth was approximately 1.08 kHz/pixel across all protocols. ECG synchronization was performed using prospective cardiac gating, with one end-expiratory breath-hold per slice. Additional technical parameters are listed in Table 1. Each of the five acquisition settings (three CS levels with three spatial resolutions for CS3) was reconstructed using two distinct reconstruction pipelines, both performed fully inline on the scanner at the time of acquisition: the conventional CS and the DL-denoising reconstructions. Both reconstructions were performed using standard vendor implementations as follows: conventional CS relied on iterative enforcement of wavelet-domain sparsity, while DL-denoising integrated a CNN-based denoising step within the reconstruction pipeline. This resulted in a total of 10 distinct T1 mapping methods (Fig. 1).

MOLLI acquisition on a Philips scanner optimizes the number of Inversion Time (TI) images according to the duration of the cardiac cycle, ensuring that the maximal TI reaches at least 4000 ms. For each dataset, native T1 maps and fitting error maps or reliability maps were reconstructed directly from the scanner.

To assess test–retest reproducibility, an additional dedicated imaging session was performed during the first half of December 2025 in a separate subset of eight healthy volunteers. Each participant underwent a scanning session following the same protocol described above and using the same scanner, during which the full T1 mapping protocol was acquired twice.

Healthy volunteers were screened through medical history and clinical interview to exclude any known cardiovascular disease or major cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, active smoking, dyslipidemia, and impaired renal function.

Data collection

Qualitative assessment

Overall Image Quality (IQ), presence of blurring, aliasing, and susceptibility artifacts were assessed using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = excellent image quality, 2 = good image quality, 3 = fair image quality, and 4 = poor image quality; Supplementary Figure 1). Blurring was used as a qualitative surrogate of perceived image sharpness. Acquired T1 images and native T1 maps were assessed independently by three CMR-trained readers (A.P., C.V.V., and V.S.), all radiology fellows with 3 to 4 years of experience in cardiac imaging. Investigators were blinded to the acquisition or the reconstruction methods and assessed the images and the corresponding maps in a random order.

Post-processing of native T1 maps

Post-processing was performed by a single investigator (A.P.). To assess interobserver variability, a subset of nine randomly selected cases was independently reanalyzed by three additional investigators (C.V.V., V.S., and M.G.), all radiology fellows with 3 to 4 years of experience in cardiac imaging.

All T1 mapping datasets were post-processed using a custom-developed software tool that allows manual myocardial segmentation through delineation of endocardial and epicardial contours, avoiding inclusion of blood pool, trabeculations, and epicardial fat. This provides the mean and standard deviation (SD) values of native T1 for each segment according to the American Heart Association (AHA) 17-segment model, with the exception of the LV apex [23].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using BlueSky Statistics version 10.3.4 (BlueSky Statistics LLC, Chicago, Illinois, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test at multiple levels: globally for qualitative analyses and at the per-segment level for native T1 values. Normally distributed data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data are reported as median values with percentage distributions. The dataset acquired using CS factor 3 at the lowest spatial resolution (2.0×2.0×10 mm3) was considered the reference standard, as it reflects current routine clinical practice.

Qualitative assessment

The Friedman test was used to assess systematic differences among the ten myocardial T1 mapping methods, based on the average qualitative assessments from three independent readers. For qualitative parameters showing a statistically significant overall effect at the Friedman test, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. These comparisons were conducted between each method and the reference standard, as well as between the two reconstruction approaches (conventional CS and DL-denoising) for each acquisition.

Quantitative assessment

Interobserver variability was assessed using the two-way random-effect intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC2), based on segmental mean T1 values, across the ten different mapping protocols.

For the analysis of T1 values, mean and SD per segment were compared across the ten methods. For mean T1 values, overall agreement and pairwise comparisons against the reference method were assessed using ICC2. In addition, Bland–Altman analysis and equivalence testing through the two one-sided Tests (TOST) procedure were applied to quantify bias/Limit of Agreement (LoA) and equivalence of each method with respect to the reference standard. For the TOST analysis, an equivalence margin of ±30 ms was predefined. This threshold was selected based on established evidence showing that clinically meaningful differences in native T1 values exceed 30 ms in several myocardial pathologies. Specifically, diseases such as Anderson–Fabry disease typically result in T1 reductions greater than 30 ms, while conditions such as diffuse myocardial fibrosis and cardiac amyloidosis are associated with similar or greater T1 increases [3], [24], [25], [26], [27]. Therefore, the chosen margin was considered conservative and clinically relevant, representing the minimum difference likely to reflect a true physiological abnormality rather than measurement variability. Importantly, an equivalence margin of ±30 ms corresponds to a total equivalence range of 60 ms, which remains well within the normal physiological variation observed in healthy individuals scanned at 3T using Philips MRI systems (mean T1 = 1057 ms; range approximately 1011–1103 ms) [28], [29]. This further supports the appropriateness of the selected threshold in the context of both clinical interpretability and scanner-dependent physiological fluctuations.

For SD values, only the Bland–Altman analysis was performed to assess the agreement in measurement precision of each method with respect to the reference standard.

Qualitative image quality scores were not used as an exclusion criterion for quantitative analysis; accordingly, all scans were retained to ensure a comprehensive and unbiased comparison across acquisition and reconstruction strategies.

Test–retest reproducibility in the scan–rescan subset was assessed on a segment-wise basis using the coefficient of variation (CV), the repeatability coefficient (RC), and the LoA width. These metrics were derived from paired mean native T1 values obtained from repeated acquisitions for each method.

Results

Study population and enrollment

From the 48 consented participants, 7 were excluded due to image-degrading artifacts: arrhythmia-related artifacts (n=2), respiratory motion artifacts (n=1), and severe plane misalignment (n=4). These artifacts were not attributable to the imaging sequences or reconstruction methods themselves, but rather to subject-related factors or technical issues during acquisition; when present in any acquisition, the entire dataset was excluded to ensure fair and internally consistent comparisons across mapping methods.

Among the 41 included participants, the mean age was 38 years (SD=11.5), 28 were female (68.3%; 28/41), and the mean weight was 77.5 kg (SD=18.0).

Qualitative results

Shapiro-Wilk tests confirmed non-normal distribution for qualitative scores. Significant differences emerged in IQ, blurring, and aliasing among methods (Friedman, p<0.05), but not in susceptibility artifacts (p=0.66).

As shown in Fig. 2, consistent qualitative trends emerged across mapping methods. Increasing levels of CS acceleration were associated with a mild but progressive degradation in overall IQ, accompanied by increased aliasing and perceptible blurring, as confirmed by post hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests compared with the reference standard (Holm-corrected p<0.05). In contrast, when comparing the two reconstruction approaches within the same protocol, DL denoising significantly improved image sharpness across most protocols, as reflected by lower blurring scores (Holm-corrected p<0.05 for all protocols except CS3 1.6×1.6×10 and CS4 2×2×10). Overall image quality showed a trend toward improvement with DL-denoising; conversely, DL-denoising was associated with a modest increase in perceived aliasing severity across most acquisition protocols; however, after correction for multiple comparisons, none of these differences reached statistical significance (Holm-corrected p>0.05 for all protocols).

Agreement declined progressively with higher acceleration levels and increasing spatial resolution for both conventional CS and DL-denoising reconstructions, with the most pronounced discrepancies again localized to segments prone to reduced measurement reliability, such as the basal lateral (segments 4–6) and apical regions (segments 13–16).

When applied to the reference acquisition, DL-denoising reconstruction demonstrated the highest agreement across all segments (ICC 0.75–0.95), showing also the narrowest LoA widths (50–117 ms). However, when directly compared with conventional CS reconstruction across the other methods, DL-denoising did not reveal a consistent trend toward improved agreement: while certain segments exhibited enhanced concordance, others showed no clear benefit or even a slight reduction.

Methods with high acceleration tended to show more positive biases, while those with DL-denoising, especially in high-resolution acquisitions, showed more negative biases. The CS3 high-resolution methods reconstructed with conventional CS reconstruction had the smallest segment-wise deviations, with biases often close to zero.

The vast majority of comparisons across methods and AHA segments supported statistical equivalence (Fig. 9) with the TOST test. Only a few isolated exceptions were observed: specifically, non-equivalence was detected in AHA 5 segment for the high-resolution method CS3 1.6×1.6×10 (bias: +17 ms), and in AHA segments 4 and 5 for the accelerated method CS4 2×2×10 (biases: +19 ms and +22 ms, respectively). SD T1 values per segment

Bland–Altman analysis was performed to evaluate the agreement in SD of native T1 values between each mapping method and the reference standard at the individual AHA segment level, serving as a surrogate for measurement precision (Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Figures 4 and 5). Overall, the LoA demonstrated considerable segment-dependent variability, generally remaining below 250 ms for most segment-method combinations, reflecting some differences in precision. Broader LoA widths exceeding 200 ms were primarily observed in myocardial segments known for reduced measurement reliability, including the basal lateral (particularly segment 5), midventricular lateral (segment 11), and certain apical segments (segments 13–16).

Among DL-denoising reconstructions, DL CS3 2×2×10 consistently demonstrated narrower LoA widths across nearly all segments; however, the impact of DL-denoising on SD agreement was not uniform and remained strongly segment- and method-dependent.

Conversely, methods using higher spatial resolution or increased acceleration factors generally exhibited broader LoA intervals.

Segment-wise bias in the SD of native T1 values revealed generally positive biases across nearly all evaluated myocardial segments, indicating a consistent but mild reduction in precision relative to the reference standard.

Additional analysis

A quantitative sharpness analysis, although showing overall similar values, confirmed the same trend for blurring: CS acceleration increased blurring, whereas higher spatial resolution and DL-denoising reduced it (Supplementary file; Section 1).

Quantitative analysis of the reliability maps showed consistent reliability estimates across all evaluated methods, despite methodological differences (Supplementary file; Section 2).

An analysis of LV blood-pool native T1 measurements demonstrated moderate-to-good inter-method agreement, with wide LoA consistent with the known intrinsic limitations of blood-pool T1 assessment (Supplementary file; Section 3).

Scan-rescan reproducibility

In the dedicated scan–rescan subset (n=8; mean age 38±9.7 years; 3 females [37.5%]; mean weight 74.2±16.9 kg), segment-wise test–retest reproducibility was overall good across all acquisition and reconstruction strategies. Across all methods and segments, CV were low, with median values around 1.5% (IQR: 1.2%–2.0%) and an overall range of approximately 0.5%–3.6%. RC were similarly consistent, with median values around 51 ms (IQR: 42–68 ms) and an overall range of approximately 16–122 ms. No consistent pattern indicating systematically higher repeatability for any single protocol was observed. As expected, repeatability was segment-dependent, with higher stability consistently observed in septal segments. Detailed segment-wise CV and RC results for each method are provided in the Supplementary Figures 9–10 in Section 4.

To facilitate direct comparison with the inter-method analyses, we additionally calculated the LoA width for repeated measurements obtained with the same protocol. The LoA width remained below 150 ms for most segment–method combinations, with a median value of 103 ms (IQR: 84–137 ms), (Supplementary Figure 11 in Section 4).

Phantom results

To further isolate sequence- and reconstruction-related sources of variability under controlled conditions, a dedicated phantom experiment was performed. As detailed in Supplementary file Section 6, all T1 mapping methods demonstrated high agreement with the reference standard (LoA mostly <20 ms), excellent test–retest reproducibility (CV generally <0.3%, RC mostly <10 ms), and high measurement precision (intra-ROI SD generally <40 ms) across a wide range of Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR)-traceable T1 values, including those extending into clinically relevant pathological ranges at 3T.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study evaluated the impact of CS acceleration, DL-denoising, and spatial resolution on the qualitative and quantitative performance of native myocardial T1 mapping. Higher CS acceleration led to a slight degradation in IQ, while higher spatial resolution did not impact overall scores; these findings are consistent with prior literature reporting increased blurring and aliasing artifacts with CS acceleration [5], [6]. DL-denoising consistently improved image fidelity. Quantitatively, native T1 values remained mostly stable across different acquisition and reconstruction settings. Segment-wise analysis revealed only minor, clinically negligible differences, even in the presence of high-acceleration factors or increased spatial resolution; notably, these findings indicate that, within the range of acquisition and reconstruction parameters evaluated in our study, variations in perceived image quality had a limited impact on quantitative robustness. Evaluation of T1 value precision revealed a progressive yet mild reduction in precision compared to the reference standard acquisition protocol.

These findings were further supported by a complementary phantom experiment, which, owing to its controlled conditions and absence of physiological motion and susceptibility effects, demonstrated even higher inter-method agreement and more stable measurement precision, including across T1 samples spanning pathological values. Scan–rescan experiments in both healthy volunteer and phantom settings further confirmed the overall repeatability of the evaluated protocols. Notably, the observed inter-method deviations were only slightly greater than the intrinsic variability associated with repeating the same acquisition, as reflected by comparable median LoA widths (153 ms [IQR: 127–178 ms] for inter-method comparisons and 103 ms [IQR: 84–137 ms] for scan–rescan reproducibility).

Clinical and technical implications of advanced acquisition strategies

Taken together, these findings indicate that the core quantitative assessment of native T1 remains reliable, supporting the implementation of patient-tailored acquisition strategies for native myocardial T1 mapping.

Regional variability of native T1 measurements: superior stability in the septum

Our results confirm the superior stability of native T1 mapping in the septal region, even when assessed using ten different mapping protocols. It is well established that septal segments demonstrate higher measurement reliability and reproducibility compared to other myocardial segments, which are more susceptible to motion artifacts and magnetic susceptibility effects [30], [31], [32]. Clinically, when a global assessment of myocardial T1 relaxation is required, current recommendations from the SCMR support placing an ROI in the mid-ventricular septum on short-axis T1 maps; conversely, in focal disease, additional ROIs in visually abnormal regions remain clinically informative despite the lower reproducibility of non-septal segments [28], [33].

In this context, regional variability likely contributes to the isolated non-equivalence observed for CS4 but not CS5 at the same nominal spatial resolution, a finding that does not follow an expected direct relationship between CS acceleration and measurement deviation. While no definitive mechanistic explanation can be provided, this behavior likely reflects the complex interplay of segment-specific factors, including motion, through-plane effects, and local field inhomogeneity, where multiple competing sources of bias may act in opposite directions and partially offset each other when comparisons are made against a reference standard. Larger, targeted reproducibility-focused studies allowing controlled evaluation of individual acquisition parameters at the segmental level will be required to further characterize how acceleration-related effects interact with regional myocardial measurement repeatability.

Improving temporal resolution through faster acquisition windows

One of the main advantages of CS is the reduction of the acquisition window for each image, specifically, for each TI in MOLLI-based T1 mapping, as shown in Table 1 and Fig. 10. This acceleration enhances temporal resolution and reduces susceptibility to cardiac and respiratory motion within each TI image, particularly in non-cooperative patients or those with elevated heart rates or arrhythmias.

This is followed by a three-heartbeat recovery period and a second inversion pulse, allowing the acquisition of three additional images with slightly shifted TIs. In such contexts, shortening the acquisition window per image through low-resolution high-acceleration protocols may theoretically enhance measurement consistency within each heartbeat and ultimately improve the robustness and reliability of native T1 mapping. Importantly, the benefits of CS acceleration primarily address motion occurring within individual images, rather than inter-image misregistration caused by respiratory drift or inconsistent breath-holding between successive inversion times. Nonetheless, by reducing motion-related artifacts at the level of each TI image, CS may still contribute to improved overall image quality and more stable T1 curve fitting, even in the presence of residual inter-image misalignment.

These potential advantages, however, remain to be formally tested, and future studies specifically designed to assess the impact of improved temporal resolution in such clinical scenarios are needed.

Expanding coverage and reducing exam time with simultaneous multi-slice (SMS)

In conventional MOLLI sequences, images are acquired during diastole within a narrowly defined cardiac phase, but the fixed number of acquisitions and recovery periods limits temporal flexibility and prevents direct exam time shortening through CS alone; although CS reduces the acquisition window of individual TI images, the total number of images, and therefore the number of cardiac cycles required for T1 estimation, remains unchanged. Consequently, reductions in scan time or expansion of spatial coverage require integration with complementary strategies such as SMS, which enables concurrent multi-slice acquisition and improves overall acquisition efficiency by simultaneously exciting and acquiring multiple slices.

Recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of SMS in myocardial T1 mapping protocols [34], [35]. When combined with CS, SMS has the potential to further reduce total scan time and/or increase volumetric coverage by enabling a more aggressive implementation of SMS, facilitated by the reduced k-space data burden associated with CS-based undersampling. This is particularly advantageous for comprehensive cardiac evaluations where multi-slice coverage from base to apex is desired.

Although the application of SMS-CS strategies in myocardial T1 mapping remains under investigation, evidence from myocardial perfusion imaging already highlights the advantages of this combined approach. McElroy et al. demonstrated that integrating SMS-bSSFP with CS enabled high-resolution first-pass perfusion imaging with significantly reduced acquisition time and extended anatomical coverage [36], [37]. Similar benefits have been reported in neuroimaging, where SMS-CS combinations allowed whole-brain acquisitions in under 20 s without compromising image quality [38]. These findings suggest that SMS-CS integration could likewise improve efficiency and coverage in myocardial T1 mapping, pending validation by dedicated studies.

Enhancing spatial resolution, sharpness, and signal-to-noise ratio: towards synthetic and virtual LGE

Another key implication of our findings is the feasibility of achieving high-resolution imaging without compromising image quality, with the most notable improvements observed when high-resolution acquisitions are combined with DL-denoising.

Such improvements are crucial for the accurate delineation of myocardial tissue features and become even more relevant in the context of emerging synthetic and virtual LGE techniques. These approaches aim to generate LGE-like contrast using either enhanced post-contrast T1 maps or native T1 maps, the latter typically combined with cine imaging [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]. Synthetic and virtual LGE allow for retrospective adjustment of myocardial nulling, which enhances both reproducibility and overall image quality. Moreover, virtual techniques offer the added advantage of potentially eliminating the need for contrast agent administration, making them especially appealing.

Future directions: integration of customizable acquisition strategies

As discussed, CS, SMS, DL-denoising, and high spatial resolution acquisition techniques each offer significant potential to enhance CMR efficiency and image quality, particularly in terms of temporal resolution, volumetric coverage, and spatial detail.

However, it is through their integrated application that native T1 mapping may reach a new level of clinical utility. By simultaneously optimizing scan time, anatomical coverage, and image quality, these combined strategies pave the way for next-generation T1 mapping protocols tailored to individual patients, specific clinical scenarios, and pathologies. One of the most promising advantages of this approach is the realization of whole-heart T1 mapping, capable of providing comprehensive quantitative tissue characterization across the entire myocardium.

Limitations

This study has three main limitations. First, although we addressed the potential influence of pathological T1 ranges through a complementary phantom experiment, this approach cannot fully replicate the complex physiological conditions present in patients with cardiac pathology; therefore, further validation in patient populations remains warranted to confirm the generalizability of these findings under real-world clinical conditions. Second, although multiple levels of CS acceleration, spatial resolution, and DL-denoising were tested, the study was restricted to a single vendor and scanner platform, limiting cross-platform generalizability. Third, the use of a single T1 mapping scheme (MOLLI) precludes conclusions about the behavior of alternative mapping techniques such as SASHA or SAPPHIRE under similar reconstruction strategies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that native myocardial T1 values remain stable across a wide range of acquisition and reconstruction strategies, including varying levels of CS acceleration, spatial resolution, and DL-denoising. The observed differences were minimal and clinically negligible, confirming the robustness of native T1 mapping under accelerated and denoised conditions. These results support the development of patient-tailored T1 mapping protocols, which can optimize scan efficiency while preserving quantitative accuracy, thereby broadening the clinical utility of myocardial T1 mapping in CMR.
