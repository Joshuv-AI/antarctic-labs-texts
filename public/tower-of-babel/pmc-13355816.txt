Geomorphic characteristics and basin evolution of the river basin in southern India: A morphometric perspective
Authors: Seeththa Sankar Narayanan M, Stephen Pitchaimani V, Gobinath R, Manoj Kumar Sahu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis paper aims to assess the morphometric characteristics of the Gundar River Basin in southern Tamil Nadu, thereby enabling us to understand the region’s hydrologic regime and geomorphic evolution. The morphometric analysis was conducted by extracting linear, area, and relief characteristics from the DEM generated from the SRTM dataset using GIS software. The morphometric analysis has revealed a dendritic drainage pattern, which is typical in lithologically homogeneous regions with minimal structural influence. The morphometric analysis has shown a high degree of integration and maturity in the fluvial geomorphic processes in the region. The shape and drainage characteristics have revealed an elongated configuration with moderate runoff and infiltration responses. The relief characteristics have revealed a subdued topography with long-term denudation processes in a stable environment. The morphometric analysis would enable us to understand the hydrologic regime and geomorphic evolution in the region, which would serve as a scientific tool in groundwater exploration, soil conservation, and land use in semi-arid coastal regions.Subject areas: Earth sciences, River geochemistry, Applied sciences

Introduction

Morphometric analysis is a quantitative approach used to evaluate the configuration, shape, and dimensions of the Earth’s surface, with particular significance on drainage basins and associated landforms. It provides understanding of the structural organization, hydrological behavior, and geomorphic evolution of river basins and has become an essential analytical tool in hydrology and geomorphology. The objective assessment of basin form and drainage organization, morphometric analysis supports informed decision-making related to water resource management, flood control, erosion mitigation, and sustainable development.1,2,3 The technique offers a quantitative framework for interpreting hydrological responses, sediment dynamics, and fluvial landscape development across diverse environmental settings, particularly in climatically sensitive regions.

The integration of geographic information systems (GISs) and remote sensing technologies has enhanced the precision and efficiency of morphometric studies.4 Advances in DEM-based analysis, satellite imagery interpretation, and spatial modeling now allow for accurate extraction and reproducible evaluation of morphometric parameters at the basin scale.5,6,7,8,9 These geospatial tools facilitate comprehensive terrain evaluation and enable consistent comparison of drainage characteristics across regions, making morphometric analysis particularly valuable in semi-arid environments where hydrological variability is pronounced.

The Gundar River Basin, located in the Kadaladi region of southern Tamil Nadu, is a hydrologically significant yet relatively understudied watershed within a semi-arid coastal transitional zone. The basin is influenced by diverse geological formations, gentle regional slopes, and seasonally variable climatic conditions, which together control its drainage behavior and water availability. Increasing water demand, expanding agricultural activity, and localized urbanization have intensified the need for systematic basin-scale evaluation and effective resource management.10 The Gundar Basin lacks a comprehensive geospatial morphometric appraisal, limiting scientific understanding of its drainage organization, hydrological sensitivity, and geomorphic maturity.

A well-made interpretation of basin morphometry requires the consideration of broader lithological and tectono-geomorphic contexts.11 Southern India is characterized by long-term passive-margin tectonics, with neotectonic adjustments and lithological contrasts that influence the development of basin geometry and drainage patterns.12,13 Morphometric and geomorphic indices are frequently employed within tectonic geomorphology. Parameters such as the hypsometric integral (HI), stream length gradient index (SL), valley floor width-to-height ratio (Vf), mountain front sinuosity (Smf), knickpoint analysis, and lineament mapping do not constitute direct evidence of tectonic activity. These indices primarily quantify the geomorphic response of landscapes such as erosion, relief development, drainage adjustment, and surface dissection to potential tectonic forcing, rather than directly measuring tectonic uplift rates, fault displacement, or crustal deformation.

Morphometric parameters primarily provide relative indicators of basin evolution and drainage organization rather than definitive measures of absolute geomorphic maturity. A comprehensive assessment of geomorphic maturity and evolutionary stage requires additional analyses, such as hypsometric evaluation, longitudinal profile assessment, and sedimentological investigations. The interpretation of geomorphic signatures derived from morphometric analysis is inherently non-unique, as similar expressions may arise from multiple controlling factors, including lithological contrasts, climatic variability, base level fluctuations, and differential erosion. Moreover, geomorphic landforms often represent time-lagged responses to past tectonic events, implying that present-day basin morphology may reflect inherited or relict conditions rather than ongoing deformation. In low-relief or slowly deforming regions, these indices may yield subdued or ambiguous signals, potentially resulting in false positives or underestimation of subtle structural influences. Consequently, morphometric parameters are best regarded as relative indicators of landscape evolution rather than direct measures of tectonic activity. Within this framework, the Gundar Basin occupies gently sloping fluvial plains adjacent to the Eastern Ghats structural domain, where lithology, base-level stability, and rainfall seasonality interact to control drainage arrangements and hydrological responses. Understanding these controls is essential for interpreting the emergence of dendritic drainage patterns and evaluating the degree of structural influence on channel development.

Morphometric parameters derived from linear, areal, and relief analyses provide an understanding of the basin behavior. Linear parameters such as stream order, SL, and bifurcation ratio reveal drainage hierarchy and structural controls. Areal parameters, including drainage density, stream frequency, circularity ratio, and elongation ratio, reflect basin shape, runoff efficiency, and flood susceptibility.14 Relief parameters such as basin relief, relief ratio, and ruggedness index explain the topographic variability, erosion potential, and landscape evolution.15,16 Comparative studies from southern India explain the diagnostic value of these parameters in assessing basin maturity, tectonic influence, and hydrological performance, underscoring the need to situate the Gundar Basin within a broader regional morphometric context.

A detailed assessment of these morphometric dimensions allows the identification of hydrological vulnerabilities such as flood-prone zones, erosion-sensitive areas, and regions good for groundwater recharge.17 These understandings derived from morphometric analysis contribute directly to soil and water conservation planning, land-use regulation, and sustainable basin management.18,19

The present study employs a geospatially driven morphometric framework to quantify the linear, areal, and relief characteristics of the Gundar River Basin, and to interpret their implications for basin evolution and hydrological functioning. The present study focuses on quantitative morphometric characterization and hence the interpretations are limited to basin-scale geomorphic attributes. By integrating structural context, climatic considerations, and regional comparative evidence, the study addresses a knowledge gap and provides a scientifically strong baseline for future hydrological modeling, environmental planning, and sustainable development initiatives in southern Tamil Nadu.

Results and discussion

Linear aspect

The morphometric analysis of the Gundar River Basin in the Kadaladi region provides a clear understanding of the drainage configuration and structural characteristics of the basin. Stream order analysis indicates that the basin shows a dendritic drainage pattern (Figure 1), which is typically associated with lithologically homogeneous terrain and minimal structural control. Such a drainage organization reflects the natural development of channels under uniform geological conditions.

Stream order map of the Kadaladi region

The drainage network comprises a total of 61 streams distributed across three stream orders, including 33 first-order, 18 s-order, and 10 third-order streams (Table 1). This hierarchical distribution conforms to Horton’s law of stream numbers, demonstrating a systematic decrease in stream frequency with increasing order and indicating a well-organized fluvial network. The dominance of lower-to intermediate-order streams, with development up to the third order, reflects the low-relief coastal geomorphic setting of the basin, where limited vertical gradients restrict the evolution of higher-order channels rather than indicating any under-development or data limitation.

Linear aspect of the drainage basin of Kadaladi taluk

The total SL is a key linear morphometric parameter that reflects erosional processes and runoff characteristics within the basin. The analysis shows a progressive decline in total SL with increasing stream order. First-order streams contribute the maximum total length of 106.41 km, followed by 46.74 km for second-order streams and 28.99 km for third-order streams. This dominance of lower-order streams highlights their role in collecting surface runoff and initiating sediment transport. The mean SL further reflects basin adjustment to relief and slope conditions, with values of 3.22 km, 2.60 km, and 2.90 km for first-, second-, and third-order streams, respectively, indicating a generally consistent fluvial development pattern.

SL ratios between successive orders are 1.24 between the first and second orders and 0.90 between the second and third orders. These variations suggest localized geomorphological adjustments influenced by minor lithological and topographic irregularities rather than strong structural controls. Such variability is common in naturally evolving drainage systems and reflects differential erosion across the basin.

The bifurcation ratio serves as an important indicator of drainage branching and structural influence. The bifurcation ratios are 1.83 between the first and second orders and 1.80 between the second and third orders, yielding a mean bifurcation ratio of 1.82. This relatively low mean value is characteristic of dendritic drainage basins and indicates limited tectonic disturbance and minimal structural interference in channel development. The high spatial correspondence between the extracted drainage network and Survey of India topographic maps (91.6%, Kappa = 0.87), together with the low mean bifurcation ratio, provides strong quantitative evidence that the Gundar Basin drainage system has evolved under uniform lithological conditions with minimal structural or tectonic disturbance. While the Kappa statistic reflects rasterized spatial agreement, the percentage match represents the primary indicator of drainage extraction reliability, reinforcing confidence in the morphometric interpretations.

Overall, the linear morphometric parameters of the Gundar River Basin indicate a well-integrated and mature drainage system, shaped mainly by regional slope and lithological uniformity. The systematic organization of stream orders, dominance of lower-order channels, and low bifurcation ratios collectively suggest stable geomorphic conditions governing basin evolution, with limited influence from structural or tectonic forces.

Areal aspect

The areal morphometric analysis of the Gundar River Basin in the Kadaladi region provides an understanding of the basin’s spatial extent, drainage texture, and surface configuration. The basin covers an area of 593.03 km2 and is bounded by a perimeter of 91.04 km, reflecting its broad spatial extent and influence on hydrological responses and sediment transport dynamics (Table 2). The drainage density, defined as the total length of streams per unit area, is 0.31 km/km2, indicating a coarse drainage texture. Such low drainage density is typically linked to permeable subsurface conditions, gentle terrain, and limited surface runoff; however, these inferences are drawn from morphometric analysis rather than direct hydrological observations.

The stream frequency of the basin is 0.10, indicating low to moderate stream frequency and relatively sparse drainage development. Geomorphology of the Kadaladi taluk

The texture ratio, calculated as the ratio of the number of streams to the basin perimeter, is 0.67, indicating a moderately dissected drainage surface. This value reflects a balance between erosional and depositional processes governed by rainfall intensity, lithological composition, and slope gradient. The moderate texture ratio suggests that the basin surface has attained relative geomorphic stability without excessive erosion.

Geometric indices derived from basin morphology indicate an elongated basin configuration. The basin length is measured as 35 km, and the elongation ratio of 0.78 signifies a moderately elongated to sub-circular basin shape. Such basin geometry may influence runoff response by increasing flow travel time under similar rainfall conditions; however, it does not directly predict flood magnitude or timing without supporting discharge data.

The circularity ratio of 0.90 further indicates deviation from a perfectly circular form, supporting the elongated nature of the basin platform. Elongated basins are associated with mature drainage systems developed over uniform lithological conditions, where structural control is minimal, and channel evolution is governed primarily by slope and runoff processes. The form factor ratio of 0.48 confirms the longitudinally stretched geometry of the basin and reflects the distribution of flow over an extended drainage area. This basin shape indicates a tendency for reduced peak flows under similar hydro-meteorological conditions, while still being influenced by rainfall intensity, land use, and the storage characteristics of the basin.

Overall, the areal morphometric characteristics of the Gundar River Basin show a developed geomorphic system with low drainage density, sparse drainage texture, and elongated basin geometry. These attributes influence runoff distribution, infiltration behavior, and erosion dynamics, while providing a potential recharge-favorable morphometric setting that should be validated through hydrological and hydrogeological observations.

Relief aspect

The relief aspect of the Gundar River Basin represents the vertical differentiation of the terrain and plays an important role in controlling runoff generation, erosion, and geomorphic evolution. The basin exhibits a basin relief of 29 m, representing the elevation difference between the highest and lowest points within the watershed (Table 3). This vertical gradient indicates a gently undulating topography, suggesting that long-term erosional processes have smoothed the landscape and contributed to terrain stabilization. The relief ratio, calculated as the ratio of basin relief to basin length, is 0.0008, reflecting a gentle longitudinal slope and a low propensity for rapid vertical incision. Such a low relief ratio implies reduced runoff velocity and limited erosional intensity, supporting infiltration and groundwater recharge over surface flow. This topographic configuration supports hydrological stability and moderated sediment transport across the basin.

The ruggedness index, recalculated using the standard formulation (Ri = basin relief × drainage density), yields a very low value of approximately 0.01, indicating extremely subdued terrain roughness. This low ruggedness replaces the earlier interpretation of “moderate ruggedness” and is geomorphically consistent with the basin’s low relief, gentle slopes, and coastal plain setting. The dominance of gently sloping Pediplain and buried pediment surfaces, as depicted in the geomorphology map shown in Figure 2, further supports the interpretation of a smooth and weakly dissected surface shaped by long-term denudational processes rather than active tectonic forcing.

The dissection index (DI) of 1.00 indicates complete vertical dissection of the available relief, rather than implying geomorphic maturity or stability, and reflects the extent of valley incision relative to the basin’s limited elevation range. Although the drainage network has fully incised the available relief, the very low basin relief, gentle slopes, and very low ruggedness index demonstrate that incision occurs within a subdued topographic framework rather than a tectonically active or unstable landscape.

Slope characteristics derived from the DEM (Figure 4) further confirm that gentle gradients dominate the basin, with steeper slopes confined to localized segments. Aspect analysis (Figure 5) reveals diverse slope orientations that influence microclimatic conditions, surface moisture distribution, and erosion dynamics. Overall, the relief morphometric parameters of the Gundar River Basin indicate a geomorphically subdued landscape, characterized by low relief, gentle slopes, complete incision within a limited elevation range, and very low ruggedness, reflecting a drainage system operating under the absence of strong morphometric anomalies rather than pronounced structural or tectonic control.

Discussion

The morphometric characteristics of the Gundar River Basin offer essential insight into the geomorphic organization, hydrological tendencies, and landscape evolution of this coastal watershed. The dendritic drainage pattern, coupled with the presence of 61 mapped streams, reflects a terrain largely governed by lithological homogeneity and minimal structural control. Such a drainage organization is characteristic of stable cratonic terrains in peninsular India and shows close correspondence with patterns documented in the Sunai River Basin of Odisha20 and the Shimsha River Basin of Karnataka.20 These regional analogs are presented here as corroborative evidence supporting the cratonic geomorphic setting of the Gundar Basin rather than as primary justification for its evolutionary interpretation. The dendritic configuration, therefore, indicates a fluvial system shaped predominantly by surface gradients and long-term denudational processes rather than by active tectonic deformation. Such interpretations are based on geomorphic expressions of drainage organization and do not represent direct evidence of tectonic activity, but rather reflect the long-term surface response to stable regional boundary conditions.

The linear morphometric indices indicate a well-integrated drainage network developed under limited structural influence. The mean bifurcation ratio of 1.82 falls within the established natural range for basins lacking strong tectonic or lithological controls and is comparable to values reported for the Kalyani River Basin.21 The systematic reduction in stream number and SL with increasing order conforms to Hortonian laws and reinforces the interpretation of an organized drainage hierarchy. It is acknowledged, however, that drainage order and derived linear indices are threshold-dependent, and the application of lower flow-accumulation thresholds could potentially yield higher-order streams. In low-relief coastal terrains such as the Gundar Basin, such sensitivity may reduce geomorphic reliability if not carefully constrained.

Areal morphometric indices provide insight into basin geometry and potential hydrological behavior. The drainage density of 0.31 km/km2 and stream frequency of 0.10 indicate a coarse drainage texture, generally associated with subdued slopes, permeable surface materials, and moderated runoff generation. Comparable values have been reported from the Sarayan River Basin of the Ganga Plain,17 where low drainage density reflects gentle gradients and favorable infiltration conditions. These values suggest potential hydrological tendencies rather than exceptionally high infiltration or extremely low runoff, and must be interpreted cautiously. Direct hydrological validation through infiltration tests, soil moisture measurements, groundwater-level monitoring, or subsurface flow analysis was not undertaken in this study, creating an associative gap between morphometric indicators and actual hydrological response.

The geometric configuration of the basin also influences its hydrological response characteristics. The elongation ratio of 0.78 and form factor of 0.48 identify the basin as moderately elongated, a configuration commonly associated with delayed peak discharge and reduced flash-flood susceptibility, as observed in the Karanja River Basin.22 However, no historical flood records, discharge measurements, or flow-duration data were used in this analysis. Morphometric parameters alone cannot capture seasonal variability, monsoon-driven runoff fluctuations, or anthropogenic influences such as land-use change and channel modification. Consequently, the confirmation of flood response and runoff behavior requires systematic flow monitoring and physically based hydrological modeling.

Relief morphometric characteristics indicate a subdued terrain with limited elevation variability. The basin relief of 29 m and the relief ratio of 0.0008 confirm a gentle longitudinal gradient, suggesting relatively low fluvial energy and a dominance of lateral erosional processes. The ruggedness index of 8.91 indicates moderate surface irregularity, while the DI of 1.00 indicates complete vertical dissection of the available relief. The DI reflects the extent of valley incision rather than landscape stability or geomorphic maturity. Similar morphometric conditions are reported from the Ghaghara River Basin,23 where low relief combined with high DI values reflects incision operating within low-gradient alluvial settings.

Although the DI indicates a complete vertical incision, the low basin relief, gentle slopes, and recalculated low ruggedness index suggest that incision occurs within a subdued topographic framework rather than within a tectonically active or unstable landscape. Complete dissection does not necessarily imply geomorphic stability or maturity and must be interpreted cautiously, particularly in low-relief basins where incision is constrained by minimal elevation differences. The recalculated low ruggedness index resolves earlier inconsistencies between relief parameters and confirms that the basin is characterized by gentle terrain and geomorphic stability typical of coastal plain environments. Accordingly, relief-based geomorphic indices discussed here are interpreted as expressions of erosional adjustment within a stable morphoclimatic setting rather than indicators of active tectonic forcing.

Taken together, the morphometric indices place the Gundar River Basin within a geomorphic context characteristic of mature, low-relief coastal watersheds in southeastern India. The overall organization of the drainage network, elongated basin geometry, and low drainage density collectively suggest long-term evolution under tectonically stable conditions. This interpretation aligns with findings from stable cratonic basins such as the Lower Chambal Basin,24 where dendritic drainage and low bifurcation ratios reflect minimal neotectonic influence. The spatial continuity of pediplain and pediment units suggests advanced landscape denudation, consistent with the morphometrically inferred mature stage of basin evolution. It is important to note that no HI or hypsometric curve analysis was conducted, nor were slope area relationships or longitudinal channel profiles evaluated. Accordingly, geomorphic maturity in this study is interpreted only in a relative morphometric sense, based on integrated linear, areal, and relief parameters rather than process-based evolutionary metrics. This limitation further reinforces that the interpretations derived from this study represent relative geomorphic behavior rather than direct or quantitative evidence of tectonic activity.

The morphometric analysis also highlights key methodological limitations shaping interpretative certainty. Hydrological interpretations presented in this study are inferred exclusively from morphometric parameters and do not incorporate rainfall variability, soil permeability, land-use characteristics, or groundwater-level data. Consequently, these interpretations should be viewed as indicative tendencies rather than validated hydrological responses. Seasonal monsoonal variability characteristic of the Tamil Nadu coast may significantly influence runoff dynamics, sediment transport, and channel adjustment. Human interventions, including irrigation infrastructure, groundwater abstraction, agricultural expansion, and settlement growth, may further modify drainage behavior beyond what morphometric indices alone can capture.

Comparative assessment with other Indian river basins shows that the morphometric values of the Gundar Basin fall well within published ranges for mature, cratonic watersheds, including the Reth,12 Kali,25 and the Sai River Basins.6 These similarities indicate that the morphometric structure of the Gundar Basin reflects regional geomorphic controls rather than localized anomalies. Nevertheless, tectono-geomorphic linkages remain subdued compared to basins affected by active neotectonic processes, such as the Chambal or Ghaghara systems.

Overall, the morphometric characteristics indicate that the Gundar River Basin has undergone long-term surface adjustment to its lithological and climatic setting, with drainage evolution reflecting gradual denudation under stable boundary conditions. Relief-based interpretations presented here are derived entirely from DEM analysis and lack direct field verification. The integration of basin shape, drainage organization, and relief attributes depicts a fluvial system approaching geomorphic equilibrium, though all interpretations must be understood within the acknowledged analytical and methodological constraints.

The morphometric analysis of the Gundar River Basin provides a comprehensive understanding of its geomorphic organization, hydrological behavior, and landscape evolution. The basin is characterized by a well-organized dendritic drainage pattern, reflecting a largely homogeneous lithological framework with minimal structural disturbance. The extracted drainage network, comprising 61 streams, demonstrates systematic hierarchical organization, with third-order channels representing the dominant perennial drainage framework rather than a limitation imposed by basin size. The progressive reduction in SL with increasing order indicates an efficiently integrated fluvial system, suggesting relative geomorphic adjustment under current topographic and climatic conditions rather than an absolute evolutionary stage.

The areal and relief morphometric parameters collectively indicate a stable morphometric configuration characterized by low drainage density (0.31 km/km2), moderate surface dissection, and an elongated basin geometry, as reflected by an elongation ratio of 0.78 and complementary shape indices. The stream frequency value of 0.10 indicates a relatively sparse to moderately developed drainage network, reflecting limited channel density rather than an exceptionally low degree of drainage development. This elongated basin configuration is commonly associated with delayed runoff response under similar physiographic settings; however, direct hydrological behavior such as flood response or peak discharge decline cannot be conclusively inferred without event-based flow data. The mean bifurcation ratio of 1.82 further suggests limited structural control on drainage alignment and a drainage evolution broadly governed by natural slope and surface conditions. Although the morphometric organization appears orderly, tectonic interpretations cannot be conclusively drawn in the absence of dedicated tectonic geomorphic indices.

Relief analysis indicates a basin relief of 29 m, a relief ratio of 0.0008, a DI of 1.00, and a ruggedness index of 8.91, suggesting a geomorphologically subdued topographic landscape. The Di represents the ratio of relative relief to absolute relief and reflects the degree of vertical incision rather than geomorphic stability or maturity. These parameters reflect subdued topographic gradients where erosional and depositional processes have attained relative equilibrium, promoting infiltration-dominated hydrological pathways and moderated sediment transport. In the absence of HI analysis, longitudinal channel profiling, and slope-area relationship assessment, the geomorphic maturity of the Gundar Basin is interpreted here as a relative morphometric condition inferred from drainage integration, basin geometry, and relief attributes rather than an absolute evolutionary stage. The combined influence of linear, areal, and relief aspects underscores the basin’s suitability for sustainable water-resource utilization, agricultural planning, and erosion management.

Methodologically, the study demonstrates the effectiveness of GIS-based morphometric analysis using DEM-derived drainage extraction. For validation, both the extracted drainage network and the Survey of India reference drainage were rasterized at the DEM resolution using a binary presence-absence scheme before comparison. Percentage spatial agreement was employed as the primary validation metric, while the Kappa coefficient was computed to quantify agreement beyond chance and should be interpreted as a supplementary indicator rather than a definitive measure of accuracy. This approach ensures proportional interpretation of methodological strength without over-reliance on a single statistical index.

Despite these strengths, the study is subject to certain limitations. It primarily relies on DEM-based morphometric indices and does not explicitly incorporate seasonal hydrological variability, land-use dynamics, or anthropogenic modifications that may influence basin behavior. While relief-based morphometric indices indicate relative terrain organization and drainage integration, the absence of direct field-based geomorphic measurements does not undermine the morphometric conclusions but represents a clear scope limitation of the present investigation. Field-based infiltration measurements, seasonal soil-moisture monitoring, and groundwater-level fluctuation analysis are therefore recommended to strengthen hydrogeomorphic interpretation in future studies. Additionally, uncertainties related to DEM resolution and the lack of extensive subsurface and soil data remain inherent constraints.

Future research should adopt a more integrative framework to refine basin-scale interpretations. Long-term (≥10 years) discharge monitoring at the basin outlet is recommended to establish hydrological response characteristics, alongside rainfall-runoff and flood simulations using physically based models such as SWAT. The integration of time-series land-use change analysis and climatic variability will further enhance understanding of drainage evolution and basin response. Advanced morphometric investigations incorporating hypsometric curve and integral analysis, longitudinal channel profile assessment, slope-area relationships, and multi-threshold drainage extraction techniques are also suggested to strengthen geomorphic inference. The direct assessment of infiltration, recharge, and flood response requires integrated hydrological and hydrogeological investigations beyond the scope of the present morphometric analysis. Such interdisciplinary approaches will improve the applicability of morphometric findings and support informed, sustainable management of the Gundar River Basin’s natural resources under evolving environmental conditions.

Limitations of the study

The present study provides a comprehensive morphometric assessment of the Gundar River Basin using widely accepted geospatial techniques and publicly available elevation datasets. The analysis is based on the shuttle radar topography mission (SRTM) digital elevation model at 30 m spatial resolution, which is suitable for basin-scale geomorphological and hydrological investigations. However, the spatial resolution of the DEM may limit the representation of very fine-scale topographic features such as micro-drainage channels and minor terrain variations. The drainage network extraction in this study was performed using a flow-accumulation threshold optimized through multiple trials and validated against Survey of India topographic maps and recent Sentinel-2 satellite imagery. While this approach ensures a reliable representation of the regional drainage system, future studies that integrate higher-resolution elevation datasets could improve the representation of smaller tributary channels and localized terrain features.

The present analysis primarily focuses on quantitative morphometric parameters derived from elevation data to understand the basin’s structural organization and hydrological behavior. Incorporating additional datasets such as detailed lithological variations, long-term hydrological observations, and high-resolution terrain models into future investigations could further refine the interpretation of geomorphological processes and watershed dynamics. Despite these considerations, the applied methodology provides a strong, reproducible framework for morphometric evaluation and contributes valuable insights into the drainage characteristics and geomorphological evolution of the Gundar River Basin.

Experimental model and study area details

The study area, focused on the Gundar Basin shown in the figure below, Location map of the Gundar Basin of the Kadaladi taluk, is located within the Kadaladi taluk of Ramanathapuram District, Tamil Nadu, and India. It covers an area of 612 km2, delineated by latitudes 9.2273920 N and longitudes 78.4725950 E. The Kadaladi region is bordered by the Tiruppullani Block to the north, the Vaippar to the south, and the Bay of Bengal to the east, providing a diverse geological and hydrological setting for morphometric analysis. The area’s geology is primarily composed of sedimentary formations, with sand, silt, and clay accounting for nearly 70% of the total lithology. The remaining 30% comprises alluvial, eolian, and coastal deposits, with a small section in the west exposing Archaean rocks such as garnetiferous biotite gneiss, quartzite, and charnockites. Eolian deposits, appearing as red sands along the beach, are interspersed with swampy black clay patches. The region is characterised by four major soil types: sand, sand and silt (dominant), clay, and red dunal sand.26 The alluvial deposits show alternating layers of clay and sand with a thickness ranging between 15 and 25 m. Freshwater lenses are found at depths of 6–7 m, providing good-quality groundwater, while shallow dug wells (3–6 m) are commonly preferred for extraction.27

The region experiences a tropical climate, with intense heat from March to September, where maximum temperatures can reach 39°C. The average annual temperature is 28.7°C, with humidity levels averaging 79%. Rainfall is influenced by both the southwest and northeast monsoons, to the annual average rainfall of 769.6 mm near the coast.28 The study area shows a dendritic drainage pattern, with the Gundar river basin as the primary hydrological feature. The Gundar River which flows through the Kadaladi Block, drains into the Bay of Bengal. Surface runoff is well channelled into subsurface formations, playing an important role in groundwater recharge. The region is a combination of flat plains and coastal terrains, marked by sand dunes running parallel to the coastline for approximately 10 km, as shown in Figure 2. These dunes are interspersed with patches of black clay and swampy areas. The coastal zone shows relatively good-quality water, with saline intrusion limited to deeper aquifers. The Gundar Basin, with its distinct geological, climatic, and hydrological characteristics, presents a suitable environment for morphometric analysis. The dendritic drainage pattern, sedimentary formations, and diverse soil types influence the hydrological response of the basin. The region’s vulnerability to saline intrusion and dependence on monsoonal rainfall make it an acute area for studying water resource management and geomorphological evolution.

Methods for calculating morphometric parameters

The primary elevation dataset used was the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) DEM at 30 m spatial resolution, downloaded from the USGS Earth Explorer platform (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/). The dataset corresponds to SRTM Version 3 (void-filled), which provides enhanced accuracy and eliminates elevation gaps through improved interpolation techniques.3 The DEM has a vertical accuracy of ±16 m,30 and this has now been clearly reported to address reviewer concerns. All spatial data were projected to the WGS 1984 UTM Zone 44N coordinate system, confirming consistency across all spatial measurements.

Before hydrological modeling, the DEM underwent a sequence of hydrological preprocessing steps, which included sink filling to remove artificial depressions, followed by the creation of Flow Direction and Flow Accumulation rasters. These steps ensure hydrologically correct flow pathways and were implemented using ArcGIS 10.8, following the protocols of Jenson & Domingue (1988).31 The DEM was additionally evaluated for vertical inconsistencies, voids, and projection errors to ensure accurate relief computation and slope modeling.

Drainage network extraction was performed using a flow accumulation–based approach. A threshold value of 1,000 cells, corresponding to approximately 0.9 km2, was applied to define stream initiation points. This response level was chosen after testing a range of accumulation values to achieve a consistent and continuous drainage network. Smaller values tended to produce short-lived channels influenced by minor local relief, while larger values failed to capture many true first-order streams. The selected threshold value effectively minimised noise-induced channel artifacts while preserving geomorphologically meaningful drainage features. The extracted drainage network was validated against Survey of India (SOI) 1:50,000 topographic maps and recent Sentinel-2 satellite imagery, confirming that the chosen value represents surface drainage patterns and channel continuity. The final drainage network was converted into a polyline format and classified using Strahler 196432 hierarchical ordering scheme, wherein stream order increases only when streams of equal order converge Horton 1945.

Basin delineation was performed using the hydrologically corrected flow direction raster, with the pour point manually assigned at the pixel with maximum flow accumulation located near the terminal outlet. The ArcGIS Watershed tool was then used to delineate the entire basin boundary. Sub-basins were described based on major tributary junctions, applying topographic segmentation.10,33 These steps strengthen methodological clarity and address reviewer-identified gaps in the basin delineation process.34

Morphometric parameters in this study were comprehensively classified into three major categories: linear, areal, and relief aspects. These parameters provide a detailed understanding of the drainage network’s structural organisation, the basin’s geometric form, and the terrain’s vertical variability. Linear morphometric variables, including stream order, stream number, stream length, mean stream length, bifurcation ratio, and stream length ratio, were calculated to describe the branching pattern and hierarchical arrangement of the drainage network. Stream ordering followed Strahler’s (1964) system, wherein first-order streams are the smallest unbranched channels, and higher-order streams are formed through the combination of streams of equal order. Stream lengths for each order were measured from the extracted drainage network. The bifurcation ratio, defined as the ratio of the number of streams of a given order to those of the next higher order, gives an understanding of the drainage system’s structural control. Mean stream length was obtained by dividing the total length of streams in an order by the number of streams, while the stream length ratio expresses the relative change in mean stream length between consecutive orders.35,36 Areal morphometric parameters characterise the geometry, shape, and drainage texture of the basin. These include drainage density, stream frequency, form factor, circularity ratio, elongation ratio, and texture ratio. Drainage density, calculated as the ratio of total stream length to basin area, reflects the efficiency of runoff and the degree of terrain dissection. Stream frequency shows the number of streams per unit area, while the form factor describes the shape of the basin based on the relationship between basin area and basin length. Circularity ratio evaluates how closely the basin shape resembles a circle, and the elongation ratio quantifies deviations of the basin shape from circularity. Texture ratio, derived from the number of first-order streams per unit perimeter, provides information on the drainage texture and surface roughness of the basin.2 Collectively, these areal parameters help reveal the hydrological behavior and geomorphological evolution of the watershed.

Relief morphometric parameters measure the vertical characteristics and erosional potential of the basin. Basin relief, defined as the difference between maximum and minimum elevations, measures the overall elevation range of the watershed. Relief ratio expresses the relative steepness of the basin by normalising relief against basin length. Ruggedness number, computed as the product of basin relief and drainage density, integrates vertical and linear characteristics to represent the ruggedness of the terrain and its influence on sediment transport. The dissection index, calculated as the ratio of actual basin relief to the theoretical maximum possible relief, indicates the degree of landscape dissection and erosional development.5,37 These relief parameters provide a clear understanding of the geomorphic maturity and erosional state of the basin.

Quantification and statistical anlaysis

All morphometric parameters were calculated using ArcGIS 10.8 and independently cross-verified through spreadsheet-based computations to ensure numerical accuracy. Validation of the extracted drainage network and basin boundary was performed through a raster-based accuracy assessment. Vector drainage networks derived from DEM analysis and SOI maps were rasterised at the DEM spatial resolution and reclassified into binary categories representing drainage and non-drainage cells. A cell-by-cell comparison was then conducted to quantify spatial correspondence between datasets. This assessment revealed a spatial agreement of 91.6% between the extracted drainage network and SOI drainage, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.87, indicating strong agreement beyond chance and directly supporting the reliability of the drainage extraction process and subsequent structural interpretations. Basin boundary validation indicated a positional deviation of less than 3%, confirming strong watershed delineation.
