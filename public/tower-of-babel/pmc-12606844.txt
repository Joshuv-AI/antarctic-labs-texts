Upregulation of miR-151a-5p in high DFI sperm induces DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction by targeting INPP4B and VAMP1
Authors: Xiaoting Lin, Jiaming Guo, Xibo Wang, Xi Xiao, Jie Sun, Wen Ding, Yun Peng, Hongli Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the potential the role of miR-151a-5p in sperm dysfunction and its association with DNA fragmentation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and male infertility.MethodsSperm samples with high DNA fragmentation were collected, and miR-151a-5p expression was measured using quantitative polymerase chain reaction. GC-2 cells were transfected with miR-151a-5p mimics to assess its effects on DNA damage, apoptosis, mitochondrial function, and reactive oxygen species levels. RNA sequencing, RNA pull-down, and bioinformatics analyses were used to identify the direct target genes of miR-151a-5p. Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed the binding of miR-151a-5p to the 3’ untranslated regions of INPP4B and VAMP1. Knockdown of these genes was performed to validate their roles in miR-151a-5p-induced effects. In vivo experiments were conducted by injecting miR-151a-5p mimics into mouse zygotes to examine the impact on embryo development.ResultsmiR-151a-5p was significantly upregulated in sperm with high DNA fragmentation and negatively correlated with sperm motility and viability. Overexpression of miR-151a-5p in GC-2 cells was associated with increased DNA damage, apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and elevated reactive oxygen species. RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analyses suggested INPP4B and VAMP1 as direct targets of miR-151a-5p. Dual-luciferase assays confirmed that miR-151a-5p binds to their 3’ untranslated regions, inhibiting their expression at both the messenger RNA and protein levels. Knockdown of INPP4B or VAMP1 partially reproduced the cellular changes observed with miR-151a-5p overexpression. In vivo, microinjection of miR-151a-5p mimics into mouse zygotes exerted only mild effects on embryo development, indicating a possible contribution of miR-151a-5p primarily through sperm dysfunction.ConclusionsmiR-151a-5p may contributes to sperm dysfunction by targeting INPP4B and VAMP1, linking DNA fragmentation with mitochondrial and genomic instability. This study offers additional insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying male infertility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12958-025-01474-0.

Introduction

Male infertility has emerged as a growing global health concern. According to data from the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 10% − 15% of couples experience difficulties in conceiving, with male factors contributing to nearly 50% of these cases [1, 2]. While traditional semen analysis parameters—such as sperm count, motility, and morphology—remains the standard clinical tool, these parameters often fail to capture underlying defects in sperm function [3]. Notably, about 15% of infertile men present with normal semen profiles [4], suggesting that conventional semen analysis is insufficient for identifying cases of “subtle infertility,” where patients present with normozoospermia yet fail to achieve conception. In recent years, the sperm DNA fragmentation index (DFI) has been recognized as a sensitive biomarker of genomic integrity [5–9], strongly associated with reduced fertilization efficiency, impaired embryonic development, and increased miscarriage risk [10]. These findings suggest that sperm DNA fragmentation is not merely a molecular abnormality but a clinically relevant factor affecting reproductive outcomes.

Sperm DNA integrity is vulnerable to both intrinsic and extrinsic stressors. Among these, oxidative stress plays a pivotal role. Spermatozoa possess limited cytoplasmic antioxidant defenses, making them highly susceptible to reactive oxygen species (ROS) [9]. Mitochondria are both the primary source and major target of ROS in sperm cells, and mitochondrial dysfunction is closely associated with excessive ROS accumulation, increased DNA fragmentation, and impaired motility [11, 12]. Disruption of mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production impairs flagellar motility, energy supply, and redox balance, ultimately compromising sperm vitality and fertilization capacity [13, 14]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular pathways that mediate mitochondrial stress and DNA damage in sperm is critical for improving the diagnosis and treatment of male infertility, particularly in cases of unexplained high sperm DFI.

Among the molecular regulators involved in sperm homeostasis, microRNAs (miRNAs)—a subclass of small non-coding RNAs—have garnered increasing attention due to their roles in post-transcriptional gene regulation. miRNAs typically bind to the 3’ untranslated regions (3’UTRs) of target mRNAs, leading to translational repression or mRNA degradation. In the male reproductive system, miRNAs are enriched and dynamically expressed during spermatogenesis and sperm maturation, where they regulate genes involved in sperm production, maturation, and DNA repair [15, 16]. Aberrant expression of specific miRNAs has been closely associated with male infertility, including asthenozoospermia, oligozoospermia, and teratozoospermia. Increasing evidence suggests that dysregulated miRNAs contribute to impaired mitochondrial function, oxidative stress responses, and DNA damage, thereby influencing both sperm quality and reproductive outcomes [17–24]. These studies underscore the potential role of miRNAs not only as biomarkers of sperm quality but also as mediators of sperm function.

miR-151a-5p is a mitochondria-related microRNA that previously implicated in oxidative stress and apoptosis in somatic cells [25–27]. Previous studies have reported that miR-151a-5p downregulates mitochondrial transcripts such as cytb, impairs mitochondrial respiration, and reduces ATP levels in sperm from patients with asthenozoospermia [28, 29]. In our previous small non-coding RNAs (sncRNAs) sequencing study, we identified miR-151a-5p as one of the most significantly upregulated miRNAs in sperm with high DFI, suggesting a potential role in the pathogenesis of DNA damage and sperm dysfunction [30, 31]. However, its functional role and molecular mechanisms in this context remain unclear.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the potential role of miR-151a-5p in sperm with elevated DFI. By combining clinical semen analysis with in vitro experiments using GC-1 spermatogonia and GC-2 spermatocytes, we investigated whether miR-151a-5p directly induces DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction. Transcriptomic profiling, RNA pull-down assays, and bioinformatic predictions were employed to identify downstream targets, and dual-luciferase assays were used to validate binding interactions. INPP4B and VAMP1 emerged as candidate effectors. We further examined whether their silencing could partially reproduce the effects of miR-151a-5p overexpression, thereby verifying their involvement in maintaining DNA integrity and mitochondrial homeostasis. Beyond these findings, we also explored the paternal role of miR-151a-5p and found that its detrimental impact on fertility may be primarily mediated by impairing sperm function, rather than by directly disrupting intrinsic embryonic developmental programs.

Our findings offer additional mechanistic clues regarding how miR-151a-5p may contribute to sperm quality decline through disruption of mitochondrial function and genomic stability. This study adds to the current understanding of the molecular basis of elevated DFI and highlights miRNA-mediated regulation as a potential contributor to male reproductive health. Moreover, miR-151a-5p and its downstream effectors may serve as potential biomarkers and exploratory targets for early screening or intervention strategies in male infertility, suggesting possible clinical relevance.

Materials and methods

Human sperm sample collection

A total of 300 semen samples were collected from male outpatients at the Reproductive Medicine Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University between January 2023 and December 2023. All samples were fresh ejaculates and were cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen following collection. Based on DFI values, samples were categorized into three groups: the DFI-L group (DFI < 15%), the DFI-M group (15% ≤ DFI ≤ 30%), and the DFI-H group (DFI > 30%). Before sample collection, all participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) Han Chinese males aged ≤ 40 years; (2) 3–5 days of sexual abstinence before semen analysis; (3) normal endocrine profiles, including follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), inhibin B, and testosterone; and (4) absence of systemic or reproductive disorders known to affect semen quality, specifically: (i) azoospermia or severe oligoasthenozoospermia; (ii) teratozoospermia; (iii) disorders of sexual development; (iv) idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism; (v) karyotypic abnormalities or Y-chromosome microdeletions; and (vi) known fertility-impairing conditions such as varicocele, orchitis, or genital tract infections. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Naval Medical University (No. chrec-2021-03) and conducted by the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all semen donors before participation.

Animals and cell lines

Eight-week-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade ICR male and female mice were purchased from Shanghai BK Laboratory Animal Center, in compliance with the requirements of Microbiological and Parasitological Standards for Laboratory Animals (GB 14922 − 2022). All animals were housed in the SPF-grade animal facility at the Naval Medical University Laboratory Animal Center under controlled conditions: temperature (21 ± 1) °C, appropriate humidity, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Animals were fed standard chow and had ad libitum access to food and water. The cell lines used in this study included GC-1、GC-2, and HEK293T cells, which were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and maintained in our laboratory under standardized conditions. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) analysis and confirmed to be free of contamination. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Sperm chromatin structure assay

After routine semen analysis, sperm DFI was assessed using the sperm chromatin structure assay (SCSA). Briefly, semen samples were subjected to acid treatment to induce DNA denaturation, followed by staining with propidium iodide (PI). PI binds to denatured single-stranded DNA emitting red fluorescence, while intact double-stranded DNA emits green fluorescence. Fluorescence signals were acquired by flow cytometry, and DFI values were calculated. According to established reference thresholds, samples were categorized into three groups: Low DFI group (DFI < 15%), Medium DFI group (15% ≤ DFI ≤ 30%), and High-DFI group (DFI > 30%). All procedures were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions to ensure data accuracy and reproducibility.

Semen analysis and sperm purification

Semen parameters were evaluated following the WHO Laboratory Manual for the Examination and Processing of Human Semen (6th edition), including assessment of appearance, viscosity, liquefaction time, pH, sperm concentration, total count, and motility patterns via light microscopy. For sperm isolation, semen was centrifuged and washed with PBS, then lysed on ice. After confirming lysis microscopically, the pellet was collected for RNA extraction or stored at − 80 °C. Small RNA was extracted using RNAiso for Small RNA, following the manufacturer’s protocol [32].

Reverse transcription and RT-qPCR

Reverse transcription was performed using a PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions, with customized stem-loop primers used for miRNA synthesis instead of the kit’s Oligo dT and Random 6-mer primers. The 20 µL reaction system included 4 µL of 5× buffer, 1 µL of RT enzyme mix, 1 µL of stem-loop primer, 1 µL of total RNA, and RNase-free water. The RT conditions were 37 °C for 15 min, 85 °C for 5 s, and hold at 4 °C. Real-time Quantitative PCR Assay (RT-qPCR) was carried out using SYBR Green Master Mix in a 20 µL reaction containing 10 µL of Supermix, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 1 µL of cDNA, and nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling program was: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 70 °C for 20 s. Melting curve analysis was performed to confirm amplification specificity. All primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Cell transfection

miR-151a-5p mimics, inhibitors, and their respective negative controls were synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting INPP4B and VAMP1, along with a non-specific control siRNA, were obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The sequences used are provided in Supplementary Table 2. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Comet assay

DNA double-strand breaks were assessed using the neutral comet assay, which provides higher specificity for DSBs than the alkaline assay and avoids confounding signals from single-strand breaks. This choice enabled us to directly evaluate persistent genomic instability relevant to high DFI sperm. Treated cells were washed twice with PBS and adjusted to a concentration of ~ 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL. Cell suspensions were mixed with low-melting-point agarose at 37 °C and layered onto slides pre-coated with 1% normal-melting-point agarose. After solidification at 4 °C, slides were immersed in ice-cold neutral lysis buffer for 1–2 h to remove cellular membranes and proteins. Slides were then incubated in neutral electrophoresis buffer at room temperature for 20–30 min to allow DNA relaxation, followed by electrophoresis at 25 V and 300 mA for 20–30 min. After electrophoresis, DNA was stained with SYBR Green or propidium iodide (PI) in the dark for 5–10 min and rinsed gently. Fluorescence microscopy was used to visualize comet structures, and DNA damage was quantified using comet analysis software (e.g., CASP or Comet Assay IV) by measuring parameters such as tail length and percentage of DNA in the tail. This method has been validated in male infertility studies, where the neutral comet assay effectively detected sperm DNA damage (Evenson et al., 2000; Zini et al., 2008) [33, 34].

Western blot assay

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After incubation on ice and sonication, lysates were centrifuged at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected for protein quantification using the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were denatured at 100 °C, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system. The primary antibodies used in this study included: anti-β-actin (AC026, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), anti-Bax (A19684, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), anti-Bcl-2 (A19693, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), anti-γ-H2AX (sc-101696, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), anti-8-OHdG (251640, Abbiotec, USA), anti-phospho-P53 (Ser15, #12571, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-P21 (ab109199, Abcam, UK), anti-INPP4B (#4039, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-VAMP1 (ab151712, Abcam, UK).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The predicted miR-151a-5p binding sites within the 3′UTRs of INPP4B and VAMP1 were analyzed using RNAhybrid. Wild-type and mutant 3′UTR fragments were synthesized and cloned into the pmirGLO luciferase reporter vector (Promega, USA), and all constructs were validated by Sanger sequencing. Luciferase reporter plasmids were co-transfected with miR-151a-5p mimics or miR-NC into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Relative luciferase activity was measured using a dual-luciferase assay kit (Yeasen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq)

Total RNA was used to construct cDNA libraries following the protocol of the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit. Indexed libraries were clustered using the Kit v3-cBot-HS on the cBot Cluster Generation System and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform by LC Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China), generating 125 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were quality-checked using NGSQC Toolkit v2.3.3. The reference genome and gene annotation files were obtained from the Ensembl database. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome, and transcript assemblies were merged using Cuffmerge to generate a unified reference annotation for quantification and functional analysis.

RNA pull-­down assay

The RNA pull-down assay was performed using the GenSeq® RNA Pull-Down Kit (GenSeq Inc., China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Biotin-labeled probes specific to miR-151a-5p and a corresponding negative control were synthesized by CLOUD-SEQ (Shanghai, China). The probe sequences were: Positive probe (5′–3′): Biotin-CCCTCGAGGAGCTCACAGTCTAGTGGG; Negative control probe (5′–3′): Biotin-CCCACTAGACTGTGAGCTCCTCGAGGG. Probes were incubated with streptavidin-coated magnetic beads at room temperature for 30 min. HUVECs (1 × 10⁷cells) were lysed on ice, and the supernatant was incubated with the probe-conjugated beads at 4 °C overnight. After thorough washing, RNA–protein complexes were eluted using elution buffer, and total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The purified RNA was subsequently used for RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) to identify RNAs interacting with miR-151a-5p.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experimental replicates unless otherwise specified. Between-group comparisons employed Student’s t-test, whereas multi-group analyses utilized one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc correction. In correlation analyses, Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was applied. Statistical computations were predominantly performed in GraphPad Prism (v9.0.1), supplemented by FlowJo (v10.8.2) for specialized analytical workflows. A p-value < 0.05 defined the statistical significance threshold throughout all evaluations.

Results

Upregulation of miR-151a-5p in high DFI sperm is associated with sperm quality

In our previous small RNA sequencing analysis of sperm with elevated DFI, miR-151a-5p emerged as one of the most significantly dysregulated sncRNAs. Initial profiling revealed a consistent upregulation of miR-151a-5p in high-DFI samples, raising the possibility that it may be involved in maintaining sperm DNA integrity and functional homeostasis. Previous studies have also shown that mitochondria-related miR-151a-5p plays an important role in asthenozoospermia [28]. To validate these findings and further explore the association between miR-151a-5p expression and sperm quality, we conducted a series of expression and correlation analyses using clinical semen samples.

We first stratified fresh semen samples into three groups based on DFI levels: DFI-L (< 15%), DFI-M (15–30%), and DFI-H (> 30%). Quantitative analysis showed that miR-151a-5p expression was significantly elevated in the DFI-H group, with a progressive increase observed across the three groups (Fig. 1a). These data suggest that miR-151a-5p may be involved in the molecular regulation specific to high-DFI sperm populations. To further assess its functional relevance, we performed Spearman correlation analysis between miR-151a-5p expression and various semen quality parameters. Notably, miR-151a-5p expression exhibited a significant negative correlation with progressive motility (PR; R = − 0.441, p < 0.001) and overall motility (R = − 0.422, p < 0.001), while showing no statistically significant correlation with age, total sperm count (Sperm.Num), and sperm concentration (Sperm.Conc) (Fig. 1b).

To compare the evaluative performance of miR-151a-5p with DFI in predicting sperm quality, we analyzed semen parameters across the three DFI groups. As expected, higher DFI was associated with reduced PR and motility, while total sperm count and sperm concentration showed less consistent variation (Fig. 1c). We further divided the samples based on the median expression of miR-151a-5p into high and low-expression groups (n = 129 per group). Strikingly, the high miR-151a-5p group showed more pronounced reductions in sperm concentration, PR, and motility (Fig. 1d). Collectively, these findings indicate that miR-151a-5p is more sensitive and specific than DFI in reflecting functional abnormalities in sperm motility and viability. Its expression not only parallels DFI progression but also more distinctly differentiates sperm populations with impaired function. These results support the potential utility of miR-151a-5p as a molecular indicator for sperm quality assessment and provide new insight into its role in the pathophysiology of male infertility.

Upregulation of miR-151a-5p induces DNA damage and apoptosis

As observed in clinical samples, miR-151a-5p is significantly upregulated in sperm with elevated DFI, and its expression shows a negative correlation with key parameters of sperm quality, including progressive and total motility. These findings suggest that miR-151a-5p may function as a negative regulator of sperm function. To further investigate whether miR-151a-5p contributes to reduced sperm quality by promoting DNA damage and apoptosis, we established an in vitro model by transfecting GC-1 and GC-2 spermatogenic cells with synthetic miR-151a-5p mimics.

To assess the evolutionary conservation and functional relevance of miR-151a-5p, we first aligned its seed sequence across different species. The comparison revealed a high degree of conservation among Homo sapiens, Mus musculus, and Rattus norvegicus (Fig. 2a), suggesting that this miRNA may play a preserved biological role in mammals. We then transfected mouse-derived GC-1 (spermatogonia) and GC-2 (spermatocyte-like) cells with synthetic miR-151a-5p mimics to artificially elevate its expression. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis confirmed a marked increase in miR-151a-5p expression following transfection (Fig. 2b), indicating successful model construction. To determine whether increased miR-151a-5p expression results in DNA damage, we employed the single-cell gel electrophoresis assay (comet assay), a sensitive method for detecting strand breaks at the single-cell level [35]. Cells transfected with miR-151a-5p mimics exhibited significantly increased tail DNA percentages and tail moment values compared to controls (Fig. 2c-d), indicating enhanced DNA fragmentation. To further assess DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), γ-H2AX immunofluorescence staining was performed, a highly sensitive method for detecting DSBs [36]. Upon DNA damage, histone H2AX becomes phosphorylated at serine 139 by kinases such as ATM and ATR, resulting in the formation of γ-H2AX. These phosphorylated foci accumulate at sites of DNA damage and are readily visualized as discrete nuclear signals [36]. In our study, cells treated with miR-151a-5p exhibited a marked increase in γ-H2AX nuclear foci, indicating elevated levels of DNA damage (Fig. 2e). These results were further supported by Western blot analysis, which showed elevated γ-H2AX protein levels in the mimic-treated groups (Fig. 2f), confirming activation of DNA damage signaling pathways at the molecular level.

We next evaluated oxidative DNA damage using 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) immunofluorescence staining. A marked increase in 8-OHdG-positive signals was detected in cells transfected with miR-151a-5p mimics (Fig. 2g), indicating that miR-151a-5p overexpression exacerbates oxidative stress-induced DNA lesions. These results are in line with our clinical observations in sperm with high DFI. To assess whether this DNA damage led to downstream apoptosis, we conducted Annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) double staining followed by flow cytometry. The analysis revealed a significant increase in both early and late apoptotic populations in the miR-151a-5p mimic group in both GC-1 and GC-2 cell lines, resulting in an overall higher apoptotic rate compared to the negative control (Fig. 2h). This indicates that miR-151a-5p overexpression not only induces DNA fragmentation but also activates apoptotic signaling cascades.

In summary, these findings demonstrate that miR-151a-5p overexpression induces both strand breaks and oxidative DNA damage in GC-1 and GC-2 cells, leading to programmed cell death. These results suggest that miR-151a-5p may contribute to sperm dysfunction under conditions of elevated DFI by modulating pathways associated with genomic instability and apoptosis.

Upregulation of miR-151a-5p impairs mitochondrial function and activates apoptotic signaling

In the previous section, we demonstrated that overexpression of miR-151a-5p in GC-1 and GC-2 cells leads to pronounced DNA damage and increased apoptosis. Previous studies have also reported that elevated miR-151a-5p levels in asthenozoospermic sperm are associated with reduced ATP synthesis. Considering the central role of mitochondria in energy metabolism, ROS generation, and regulation of apoptosis, we next investigated whether the observed cellular phenotypes were mediated by mitochondrial dysfunction triggered by miR-151a-5p. In subsequent experiments, we primarily chose to use GC-2 spermatocyte cells rather than employing both GC-1 and GC-2 cell lines. This decision was based on two key considerations. First, GC-2 cells exhibit biological characteristics that more closely resemble the critical stages of spermatogenesis, making them more suitable for investigating mechanisms related to mature sperm function [37]. Secondly, the GC-2 cell line exhibits well-defined biological characteristics related to meiosis, DNA repair, and mitochondrial function [38], offering more direct experimental insights to address our research questions and aligning more closely with the objectives of this study. Therefore, we focused more specifically on the functional alterations in GC-2 cells and their role in the underlying mechanisms.

To assess mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), we employed the JC-1 staining method. Under normal conditions, JC-1 accumulates in mitochondria with intact membrane potential, forming red fluorescent aggregates. When MMP collapses, JC-1 remains in its monomeric form, emitting green fluorescence. In cells transfected with miR-151a-5p mimics, we observed a marked reduction in red fluorescence and a corresponding increase in green fluorescence, a pattern consistent with that induced by the known mitochondrial uncoupler CCCP (Fig. 3a). These results indicate that miR-151a-5p overexpression induces significant mitochondrial membrane depolarization and functional impairment.

We then assessed intracellular ROS levels to evaluate oxidative stress status. Compared to the negative control, ROS production was significantly elevated in miR-151a-5p-overexpressing cells (Fig. 3b), suggesting that mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation may be disrupted, leading to excessive ROS accumulation. To further validate the physiological consequence of mitochondrial impairment, cellular ATP levels were measured. miR-151a-5p overexpression resulted in a significant decrease in ATP production (Fig. 3c), indicating compromised mitochondrial bioenergetic function. Collectively, these results suggest that miR-151a-5p disrupts mitochondrial integrity by depolarizing the membrane potential, enhancing oxidative stress, and suppressing energy metabolism.

Given the co-occurrence of DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction, we next investigated the activation of downstream apoptotic signaling pathways. Western blot analysis revealed that both phosphorylated p53 (p-p53) and its downstream effector p21 were upregulated in GC-1 and GC-2 cells following miR-151a-5p mimic treatment (Fig. 3d), indicating activation of the DNA damage response and p53-dependent apoptosis. To determine whether mitochondrial alterations also contributed to apoptotic signaling, we further examined the expression of key apoptosis-related proteins. In miR-151a-5p-overexpressing GC-2 cells, we observed increased levels of pro-apoptotic Bax, CytC, and p-p53, along with decreased expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. Moreover, p21 was also significantly upregulated (Fig. 3e), further supporting activation of the intrinsic (mitochondrial) apoptotic pathway.

In summary, miR-151a-5p overexpression disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis by depolarizing MMP, suppressing ATP production, and promoting ROS accumulation, thereby triggering mitochondrial-dependent apoptotic signaling. These findings suggest that under conditions of elevated DFI, miR-151a-5p may impair sperm quality and reproductive potential by compromising mitochondrial function. This mechanistic evidence reinforces the notion that miR-151a-5p plays a pivotal role in male reproductive damage associated with high sperm DNA fragmentation.

miR-151a-5p May mediate mitochondrial dysfunction and sperm DNA damage by targeting INPP4B and VAMP1

Having confirmed that miR-151a-5p overexpression induces both DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction, we hypothesized that these biological effects are likely mediated through downstream target genes regulated by this miRNA. To explore the potential underlying mechanisms, we employed an integrative multi-omics strategy combining RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq), miRNA pull-down assays, and bioinformatic prediction to identify key functional targets or pathways modulated by miR-151a-5p.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) based on RNA-Seq data revealed that the oxidative phosphorylation pathway was the most significantly enriched and top-ranked downregulated pathway in the miR-151a-5p mimic group (Fig. 4a). This suggests that miR-151a-5p may interfere with mitochondrial oxidative metabolism by targeting crucial genes in this pathway, resulting in reduced ATP synthesis, increased ROS accumulation, and ultimately, a decline in sperm quality. These findings further support our previous observations regarding the detrimental role of miR-151a-5p in mitochondrial function.

To pinpoint potential direct targets, we cross-referenced three independent datasets: (1) predicted miR-151a-5p target genes from the miRWalk database, (2) significantly downregulated mRNAs identified via RNA-Seq after miR-151a-5p overexpression, and (3) transcripts pulled down in miR-151a-5p RNA-binding assays. Venn diagram analysis revealed 11 high-confidence candidate genes that overlapped across all three datasets (Fig. 4b). The RNA-Seq volcano plot illustrated the overall distribution of differentially expressed genes, with several genes known to be involved in mitochondrial function and apoptosis regulation (Fig. 4c) [39, 40]. Hierarchical clustering analysis of the 11 candidate genes showed clear expression differences between the miR-151a-5p mimic and control groups (Fig. 4d) [41, 42]. Literature review indicated that many of these genes are involved in energy metabolism and apoptosis regulation and are predominantly expressed in reproductive and neural tissues. To validate these findings, we performed RT-qPCR in GC-2 cells for all 11 candidates. Among them, INPP4B and VAMP1 exhibited the most pronounced downregulation upon miR-151a-5p overexpression (Fig. 4e), strongly implicating them as direct targets. To assess clinical relevance, we next examined their expression levels in human sperm samples stratified by DFI. Both INPP4B and VAMP1 expression levels were significantly reduced in samples with higher DFI, with the lowest levels observed in the DFI-H group (Fig. 4f). This inverse correlation between miR-151a-5p expression and its potential targets further supports the hypothesis that miR-151a-5p may contribute to sperm dysfunction by downregulating VAMP1 and INPP4B.

Building upon these human data, we further investigated whether this regulatory axis also functions during spermatogenesis. Using a mouse model of elevated DFI induced by low-dose electromagnetic radiation, we isolated spermatogonia, spermatocytes, and spermatids from both control and DFI groups. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that miR-151a-5p expression progressively increased from spermatogonia to spermatids, while INPP4B and VAMP1 levels were significantly suppressed in the DFI group, particularly at the spermatocyte and spermatid stages (Fig. 4g). These findings indicate that miR-151a-5p may interact with INPP4B and VAMP1 at early stages of spermatogenesis, thereby impairing DNA integrity and mitochondrial function before sperm maturation.

In summary, our results suggest that miR-151a-5p may directly suppress the expression of VAMP1 and INPP4B, thereby disrupting critical biological processes such as mitochondrial energy metabolism, membrane integrity, and DNA repair. This mechanism may underlie the observed decline in sperm quality under high DFI conditions. These findings provide novel mechanistic insight and identify VAMP1 and INPP4B as potential molecular targets involved in miR-151a-5p-mediated sperm damage and DNA fragmentation.

miR-151a-5p directly targets the 3′-UTRs of INPP4B and VAMP1 to suppress their expression

In the previous section, we demonstrated that overexpression of miR-151a-5p significantly downregulates the expression of INPP4B and VAMP1 and predicted that these genes contain conserved miR-151a-5p binding sites within their 3′ untranslated regions (3′-UTRs). To further validate whether INPP4B and VAMP1 are direct targets of miR-151a-5p, we first performed sequence alignment using miRNA target prediction tools including miRanda, RNAhybrid, and TargetScan. The results revealed evolutionarily conserved binding motifs for miR-151a-5p within the 3′-UTRs of both genes, showing canonical seed sequence complementarity (Fig. 5a). These data suggest that miR-151a-5p may exert post-transcriptional regulatory effects via direct binding to these regions.

To experimentally assess this possibility, we conducted gene knockdown assays by transfecting siRNAs targeting INPP4B or VAMP1 into GC-2 cells. RT-qPCR analysis showed that mRNA levels of both genes were significantly decreased after siRNA transfection (Fig. 5b), and this was further supported by Western blot results demonstrating a parallel reduction in protein expression (Fig. 5c–d). These findings provide initial functional evidence that INPP4B and VAMP1 are indeed susceptible to miR-151a-5p-mediated regulation at both the transcript and protein levels. To confirm whether this regulation occurs via direct interaction with the 3′-UTR, we constructed dual-luciferase reporter plasmids containing either the wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) 3′-UTR sequences of INPP4B and VAMP1. These constructs were co-transfected into HEK293T cells along with either miR-151a-5p mimics or negative control mimics. As expected, miR-151a-5p significantly reduced luciferase activity from the wild-type 3′-UTR reporters. However, this suppressive effect was markedly attenuated in the mutant constructs lacking predicted miR-151a-5p binding sites (Fig. 5e–f). These results strongly support that miR-151a-5p directly binds to the 3′-UTRs of INPP4B and VAMP1 to suppress their expression.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that miR-151a-5p exerts direct negative regulation on INPP4B and VAMP1 by binding to their 3′-UTR regions. This interaction may disrupt key cellular processes such as mitochondrial metabolism and apoptotic signaling. Notably, both INPP4B and VAMP1 are functionally involved in critical biological pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation, vesicle trafficking, and DNA damage repair. Through repression of these targets, miR-151a-5p may act as a molecular bridge linking miRNA upregulation to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and germ cell apoptosis. This regulatory axis provides new insight into how miR-151a-5p contributes to sperm mitochondrial impairment and DNA fragmentation.

miR-151a-5p targets INPP4B and VAMP1 to induce DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and promote apoptosis

Following confirmation that miR-151a-5p directly targets and suppresses the expression of INPP4B and VAMP1, we next investigated whether these two genes serve as key functional mediators in miR-151a-5p-induced phenotypes, including DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction. To evaluate this, we designed a two-tiered approach: (1) single-gene knockdown of INPP4B or VAMP1; and (2) combined knockdown in the presence of miR-151a-5p inhibition, to determine whether the protective effects of miR-151a-5p suppression depend on these targets.

We first assessed DNA damage using the comet assay. Knockdown of either INPP4B or VAMP1 alone significantly increased DNA strand breaks, as reflected by increased tail moment values. Notably, when INPP4B or VAMP1 was silenced in the context of miR-151a-5p inhibition, the DNA-protective effect of miR-151a-5p suppression was completely abolished, with tail moments returning to high levels (Fig. 6a–b). These findings indicate that loss of either gene is sufficient to override the benefit of miR-151a-5p inhibition and reintroduce DNA fragmentation. This was further supported by γ-H2AX immunofluorescence staining, which showed significantly increased nuclear γ-H2AX foci in the co-inhibition groups (Fig. 6c), suggesting impaired DNA repair capacity. To further evaluate mitochondrial health, JC-1 staining coupled with flow cytometry was used to assess MMP. Cells in the si-INPP4B, si-VAMP1, and combined inhibition groups exhibited decreased red fluorescence (JC-1 aggregates) and increased green fluorescence (JC-1 monomers), indicating MMP depolarization (Fig. 6d). Notably, the VAMP1 suppression group showed a more pronounced shift, suggesting a potentially stronger role in maintaining mitochondrial polarization. We next measured intracellular ATP content to assess mitochondrial energy metabolism. ATP levels were significantly reduced in both single-gene and co-inhibition groups (Fig. 6e), indicating impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics regardless of miR-151a-5p status. Finally, Annexin V/PI flow cytometry analysis revealed that combined knockdown of INPP4B or VAMP1 significantly increased both early and late apoptotic populations in GC-2 cells (Fig. 6f), confirming their pro-survival roles.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that INPP4B and VAMP1 are not only direct targets of miR-151a-5p but also act as essential downstream effectors responsible for regulating DNA repair, mitochondrial integrity, energy production, and cell survival. Their loss mirrors the detrimental effects of miR-151a-5p overexpression and nullifies the beneficial impact of its inhibition. This highlights the miR-151a-5p–INPP4B/VAMP1 axis as a central mechanism in high DFI-associated sperm dysfunction and suggests that restoring their function could be a promising molecular strategy for targeted therapeutic intervention.

Effects of miR-151a-5p on early embryonic development

Although our previous findings demonstrated that miR-151a-5p significantly impairs sperm DNA stability and mitochondrial function by targeting INPP4B and VAMP1, its potential influence on embryonic development remains unclear. To investigate whether miR-151a-5p also plays a role in regulating early embryogenesis, we microinjected synthetic miR-151a-5p mimics into mouse zygotes at the 2-pronuclear (2PN) stage and monitored their developmental progression up to the blastocyst stage.

As shown in Table 1, under comparable fertilization conditions, the miR-151a-5p injected group exhibited slightly lower cleavage and developmental rates at the 2-cell, 4-cell, morula, and blastocyst stages compared with the miR-NC group. While the differences in cleavage rates from the 2-cell to morula stages were minimal, the most noticeable reduction occurred at the blastocyst stage (74.7% vs. 83.3%).

Discussion

With the delay in paternal age and changing reproductive patterns, male infertility has become an increasing concern, and the underlying molecular mechanisms are increasingly becoming a focal point of research. Among the numerous pathogenic factors, sperm DFI and mitochondrial dysfunction are considered important contributors to sperm function and fertilization capacity. DFI is a crucial indicator of sperm DNA integrity and is closely linked to sperm motility, fertilization success, and embryo developmental potential. Elevated DFI levels are commonly associated with reduced sperm motility, fertilization failure, impaired embryo development, and adverse reproductive outcomes such as miscarriage [43–45]. Although current ART can partially address the challenges of conception associated with high DFI sperm, such as through Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection (ICSI), which bypasses the natural selection barrier during sperm and oocyte, sperm DNA damage may not be fully repaired. This may still result in a reduced developmental potential of the embryo post-implantation [5, 46]. Li et al. demonstrated that in IVF patients with DFI exceeding 30%, the miscarriage rate was significantly higher compared with the low DFI group [5]. Bungum et al. found that when DFI exceeded 30%, the pregnancy rate following intrauterine insemination (IUI) significantly decreased to 3%, highlighting the more pronounced negative impact of high DFI on IUI outcomes [47]. High DFI sperm also appears to negatively influence the success rate of in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer (IVF-ET), particularly in patients with recurrent implantation failure (RIF) and recurrent spontaneous abortion (RSA), where the effect is even more pronounced [44, 48]. Agarwal A et al. proposed that sperm DNA damage may impair sperm chromatin integrity, thereby disrupting the regulation of embryonic gene expression and leading to arrested embryo development [49]. Recent studies have shown that sperm DNA damage may explain, at the molecular level, the association between between high DFI sperm and miscarriage or embryo developmental failure, by inducing apoptosis and disrupting DNA repair pathways during embryonic development. Although techniques such as ICSI can assist with conception in cases of poor sperm quality, the miscarriage rate remains significantly higher when high DFI sperm are used in ICSI-assisted fertilization. The accumulation of ROS and the imbalance of energy metabolism are also well-recognized mechanisms contributing to sperm damage. In sperm cells, ROS may act as signaling molecules in certain physiological pathways. However, excessive ROS production and the resulting oxidative stress have been reported to be closely related to the pathogenesis of male infertility [50]. Oxidative stress can lead to lipid peroxidation, protein modifications, and DNA damage, subsequently disrupting normal gene expression and epigenetic patterns [51]. Nevertheless, the role of sncRNAs in regulating these processes has not been fully explored.

Several studies have highlighted that miRNAs play important roles in sperm production, maturation, and stress responses. For example, miR-34c, let-7a, and miR-19b regulate germ cell proliferation and differentiation, while miR-23a and miR-100 are implicated in oxidative stress responses and mitochondrial dysfunction [17–22]. Mitochondria-associated miR-574 has been shown to reduce ATP production by targeting ND5 in aged mice, contributing to decreased sperm motility [23]. miR-125a-5p was reported to induce mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA damage in sperm of aged males and disrupt embryonic progression, particularly during the morula-to-blastocyst transition [24]. These findings collectively emphasize that dysregulated miRNAs are not only biomarkers but also active mediators of sperm dysfunction, particularly in the context of oxidative stress and aging.

Notably, our study suggests that miR-151a-5p acts as an upstream factor in regulating sperm genomic and mitochondrial homeostasis, particularly playing a significant role in the context of high DFI. In our previous study, we conducted high-throughput sncRNA sequencing of sperm with high DFI and identified miR-151a-5p as a significantly differentially expressed sncRNA. It appeared capable of distinguishing between high and low DFI sperm, suggesting that it may serve as a candidate biomarker [30]. Subsequently, miR-151a-5p was found to be significantly upregulated in clinical semen samples, and its expression was negatively correlated with sperm motility, showing preliminary specificity and sensitivity for evaluating sperm quality [31]. We then expanded the sample size for further confirmation, and the results were consistent with expectations. In the DFI-H group, miR-151a-5p expression was significantly elevated (Fig. 1a), and its expression level negatively correlated with key sperm motility parameters (such as progressive motility and sperm vitality) (Fig. 1b). These findings are consistent with previous studies that have highlighted the role of microRNAs in the decline of sperm function related to stress and aging [23, 24, 52]. We also compared the performance of miR-151a-5p and DFI in evaluating sperm quality. Interestingly, the high expression group of miR-151a-5p not only exhibited a clearer downward trend in sperm concentration, progressive motility, and motility parameters, but the differences were also more significant than those observed with DFI (Fig. 1c-d). These observations suggest that miR-151a-5p levels may complement or provide additional value beyond DFI in assessing sperm quality, although further validation in larger and independent cohorts is required before clinical application.

In the GC-1 and GC-2 cell models, we further observed that miR-151a-5p overexpression leads to significant DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), increased γ-H2AX, and elevated levels of the oxidative damage marker 8-OHdG (Fig. 2), suggesting a potential role in promoting genomic instability. Zhou et al. previously found that elevated miR-151a-5p levels result in reduced sperm ATP production [28], which is consistent with our findings. In addition to decreased ATP synthesis, we observed that miR-151a-5p also induces mitochondrial membrane depolarization and ROS accumulation (Fig. 3), highlighting its critical role in disrupting cellular redox homeostasis and energy metabolism.

INPP4B and VAMP1 are crucial genes involved in regulating cellular functions, particularly in controlling mitochondrial function and DNA damage repair. INPP4B (inositol polyphosphate-4-phosphatase type II) participates in phosphoinositide metabolism and has been shown to play a role in signaling pathways that regulate cell survival and apoptosis. It especially influences the cell’s sensitivity to oxidative stress by modulating the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. Several studies have indicated that dysregulation of INPP4B is associated with various cellular stress responses induced by oxidative stress, supporting its potential role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and protecting DNA from damage [53, 54]. In addition, INPP4B has been reported to play a role in regulating cell migration, apoptosis, and autophagy, processes that are critical for spermatogenesis and sperm function [55, 56]. The tissue and cell type expression data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) indicate that INPP4B exhibits testis-specific expression in late-stage spermatids. Tissue RNA-seq data clustering shows that it is primarily a key gene involved in sperm flagellum assembly. Ceyhan et al. have shown that Inpp4b expression is highest in post-meiotic germ cells in mice and males, and that it plays a role in maintaining male germ cell differentiation and protecting testicular function from the harmful effects of ageing and high-fat diets [41]. They constructed Inpp4b knockout mice and found that the knockout of Inpp4b led to a significant increase in germ cell apoptosis and elevated DNA damage. This finding is consistent with our current study, where elevated miR-151a-5p targets INPP4B, thereby affecting DNA repair and stress responses. Its downregulation reduces the cell’s ability to repair DNA damage, subsequently increasing DFI levels. This could represent one possible mechanism contributing to the fertilization difficulties observed in cases of high DFI sperm, although further in vivo validation is required to confirm this pathway. Consistent with these findings, our in vivo experiments further support a regulatory role of miR-151a-5p during spermatogenesis. In a mouse model of elevated DFI induced by low-dose electromagnetic radiation, qRT-PCR analysis across germ cell populations revealed a progressive increase in miR-151a-5p expression, accompanied by stage-dependent suppression of INPP4B and VAMP1. Notably, the most pronounced downregulation was observed in spermatocytes and spermatids, suggesting that the deleterious effects of miR-151a-5p likely originate prior to sperm maturation.

These observations provide two important insights. First, they indicate that miR-151a-5p may compromise germ cell genomic integrity and mitochondrial homeostasis during meiotic and post-meiotic stages, rather than acting exclusively in mature sperm. This is biologically plausible given that spermatocytes are undergoing active chromatin remodeling and DNA repair, processes that may be especially vulnerable to dysregulated miRNA activity. Second, the early suppression of INPP4B and VAMP1 implies that the miR-151a-5p axis may interfere with both DNA repair signaling and mitochondrial dynamics in a cumulative manner across spermatogenesis. Such stage-specific disruption could amplify the burden of DNA fragmentation, explaining the strong correlation between miR-151a-5p and high DFI in clinical samples.

VAMP1 (Vesicle-Associated Membrane Protein 1) is a vesicle-associated membrane protein. Tissue and cell type expression data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) show that VAMP1 is predominantly expressed in the meiotic spermatocytes of the testes. As a member of the vesicle-associated membrane protein family, VAMP1 is involved in intracellular vesicular trafficking and mitochondrial membrane fusion. It has been implicated in regulating mitochondrial morphology and functional stability by influencing the processes of mitochondrial fusion and fission, thereby maintaining normal mitochondrial function. Liu et al. reported a specific type of extracellular vesicle that encapsulates mitochondria, which they termed “mitophers” [57]. They proposed that mitopherogenesis, the generation of these mitophers, is the main mechanism regulating mitochondrial quantity in sperm, and it plays a key role in modulating sperm motility and fertility [57]. This process is crucial for maintaining male gamete motility and reproductive potential. In our study, we first observed that miR-151a-5p targets the vesicle-associated membrane protein VAMP1, which may contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction in sperm. Downregulation of VAMP1 may impair mitochondrial dynamics, thereby affecting energy metabolism and triggering sperm cell apoptosis. Therefore, the miR-151a-5p–INPP4B/VAMP1 axis may represent a regulatory pathway involved in oxidative stress, apoptosis, and energy metabolism, although further validation in vivo is required.

It is noteworthy that, although miR-151a-5p has a significant impact on sperm function, its effect on early embryonic development appears to be minimal. Microinjection of miR-151a-5p mimics into mouse 2PN zygotes did not significantly alter the embryo cleavage rate, and the development rates at the morula and blastocyst stages showed only a slight decrease. Similarly, direct knockdown of INPP4B or VAMP1 in embryos led to only mild developmental delays. These findings suggest that miR-151a-5p primarily damages sperm function at the pre-fertilization stage, such as by reducing vitality and motility, which in turn lowers the energy capacity for fertilization and reduces natural fertilization ability, rather than directly interfering with the intrinsic genetic regulatory mechanisms of embryonic development. This observation supports the possible use of miR-151a-5p as a biomarker for assessing sperm quality rather than as a predictor of embryonic developmental potential. Therefore, this study has significant clinical implications. High DFI sperm are closely associated with IVF failure, recurrent miscarriage, and an increased risk of birth defects, while traditional semen analysis often fails to reveal underlying molecular damage. The upregulation of miR-151a-5p in high DFI sperm, potentially through the regulation of DNA repair and mitochondrial homeostasis-related genes, offers a possible molecular explanation for these reproductive disorders. Moreover, intervention of the miR-151a-5p regulatory pathway may serve as a new therapeutic strategy to improve sperm quality, for example by restoring INPP4B or VAMP1 function or by modulating miR-151a-5p activity, although such strategies remain speculative and require extensive further validation.

To visually summarize the proposed mechanism, we have constructed a schematic diagram of the miR-151a-5p–INPP4B/VAMP1 axis (Fig. 8). The diagram illustrates how various adverse factors, such as aging, smoking, alcohol consumption, environmental pollution, and radiation, can lead to elevated DFI, which in turn induces increased expression of miR-151a-5p. After being processed by Dicer/TRBP, this miRNA enters the the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) complex and targets the 3′-UTR of INPP4B and VAMP1, resulting in post-transcriptional repression and mRNA degradation. Ultimately, this leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA damage, inducing sperm dysfunction, reduced motility, and contributing to male infertility.

Certainly, this study also has some limitations. Although the in vitro experiments provided mechanistic insights, further validation in vivo models is needed to confirm its tissue-specific roles. In particular, while our data strongly support that miR-151a-5p directly targets INPP4B and VAMP1 in vitro, direct in vivo evidence in germ cells is still lacking. Future studies employing conditional knock-in mouse models that disrupt the 3′-UTR binding sites of INPP4B and VAMP1 will be essential to confirm the causal role of this regulatory axis during spermatogenesis. Additionally, miR-151a-5p may regulate other downstream genes, and future research using transcriptomics and proteomics is needed to comprehensively explore its regulatory network. Despite the limited impact on embryonic development, long-term follow-up studies are necessary to clarify its effects on offspring health and epigenetic profiles.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of miR-151a-5p in high DFI sperm, integrating clinical observations with functional assays. We show that miR-151a-5p is associated with reduced expression of INPP4B and VAMP1, which may contribute to sperm dysfunction by impairing DNA stability and mitochondrial integrity, thereby potentially reducing motility and fertilization capacity. These findings suggest a possible mechanism underlying high DFI-associated male infertility. Expression profiling further indicates that INPP4B and VAMP1 are important in later spermatogenesis, particularly in maintaining structural stability and energy metabolism. Thus, miR-151a-5p may exert its effects primarily during sperm maturation, indirectly influencing fertility outcomes without directly interfering with embryonic development. Furthermore, in assessing sperm quality, miR-151a-5p appears to provide complementary value to DFI, supporting its potential as a molecular biomarker. Overall, our findings offer additional mechanistic clues into the molecular pathways linking high DFI to male infertility and point to possible avenues for early screening and exploratory intervention strategies, pending further validation.
