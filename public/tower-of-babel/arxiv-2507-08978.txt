Characterizing Security and Privacy Teaching Standards for Schools in the United States
Katherine Limes 1 , Nathan Malkin 2 , and Kelsey R. Fulton 1

Abstract
Increasingly, students begin learning aspects of security and privacy during their primary and secondary education (grades K-12 in the United States). Individual U.S. states and some national organizations publish teaching standards—guidance that outlines expectations for what students should learn—which often form the basis for course curricula. However, research has not yet examined what is covered by these standards and whether the topics align with what the broader security and privacy community thinks students should know. To shed light on these questions, we started by collecting computer science teaching standards from all U.S. states and eight national organizations. After manually examining a total of 11,954 standards, we labeled 3,778 of them as being related to security and privacy, further classifying these into 103 topics. Topics ranged from technical subjects like encryption, network security, and embedded systems to social subjects such as laws, ethics, and appropriate online behavior. Subsequently, we interviewed 11 security and privacy professionals to examine how the teaching standards align with their expectations. We found that, while the specific topics they mentioned mostly overlapped with those of existing standards, professionals placed a greater emphasis on threat modeling and security mindset.

I
Introduction

Education about security and privacy (S&P) is critical, whether the goal is to create future security professionals, train developers to prioritize security and privacy, or simply equip users to protect themselves online.
Increasingly, such education starts before university:
many primary and secondary schools have added classes on computer science and technology,
which often cover S&P topics.
But what exactly are they teaching?
What skills and topics are deemed most important, and which are emphasized less?
How up-to-date is the curriculum?
If a security or privacy professional looked at it, would they agree with what is being taught?

These questions are difficult to answer;
the United States, for example, has over 120,000 schools
[
1
]
,
and the majority of school districts offer at least some computer science education
[
2
]
.
Moreover, teachers often have substantial discretion in what they teach. However, their freedom is not total.
Often, their classes are meant to prepare students for standardized tests (like Advanced Placement and International Baccalaureate exams), so they must teach the topics on the tests.
Besides this, curriculum development is often guided by state teaching standards.

Teaching standards

(see Figure
1
)
are guidelines for the curriculum of a given course.
They dictate the minimum knowledge that a student should have on a particular topic at the end of the year.
Teachers can and do go beyond the requirements, but the standards provide a baseline for what topics should be covered.
They therefore offer an interesting vantage point for understanding what states want their students to learn.
They are also a potentially powerful mechanism of effecting change:
rather than targeting individual curricula at thousands of schools, standards can be improved, and the changes will propagate to individual classrooms.

To date, no research has systematically examined teaching standards for their security and privacy content.
Our research aims to fill this gap.
To do this, we formulated the following research questions:

RQ1

What security and privacy topics are covered by K-12 computer science teaching standards in the U.S.?

RQ2

How are K-12 security and privacy teaching standards presented (e.g., organization, presence of examples)?

RQ3

How do these standards differ between states and grade levels?

RQ4

How do the standards compare with the expectations of security and privacy experts?

To answer our research questions,
we first analyzed computer science teaching standards
from all U.S. states and eight national organizations, yielding 11,954 standards. Through manual analysis, we labeled 3,778 of them as being related to security and privacy. These standards spanned 103 security and privacy topics ranging from technical subjects, like encryption and network security, to social subjects such as laws, ethics, and appropriate online behavior. Standards often lacked examples of what to teach, and, when they had examples, these ranged from detailed clarification statements to short lists of key terms. When categorizing standards, standards covering security were explicitly labeled as such far more often than those covering privacy, making privacy standards harder to find. We found that topic coverage varied heavily by state and grade level, with the most common topics not being taught in every state, and a focus on fundamental security and privacy topics at the elementary level and technical topics at the high school level.

We then interviewed 11 security and privacy professionals to better understand how the standards align with expert expectations.
While the specific topics they mentioned mostly overlapped with existing standards, professionals placed a greater emphasis on security mindset and non-technical skills over technical topics, arguing that those would stay relevant longer.
Participants also expressed some concerns about the vagueness of standards, the utility of specific technical details, and the need to keep standards updated.

Based on our results,
we recommend clearer separation of standards between S&P fundamentals (important for everyone) and more specialized content for future computing professionals,
as well as greater investment in teachers and resources for them.

II
Background

This section provides background on K-12 teaching standards and discusses related work on teaching computer science (CS) and security and privacy (S&P) in K-12 schools.

II-A

Teaching standards

Teaching standards describe what a student should know or be able to do at the conclusion of a given grade level. For core subjects (e.g., social studies, math, science), states write their own standards when legislation is passed to indicate that they should. The state then puts together a committee of educators and professionals in the space to draft the standards, releases the draft for public comment, and revises as appropriate. For core subjects, standards are required to be implemented and followed. However, in CS and S&P this is rarely the case. As of 2024, only 11 states allowed computer science to count towards the number of course credits required to graduate, and only five of those required some computer science course to be taken before graduating
[
3
]
. Only one state, North Dakota, required students to learn security and privacy before graduating
[
3
]
.

Anatomy of a standard.

Every standard has text, which describes the material to be covered. Additionally, states often provide an identifier, a category and sometimes a subcategory, and a grade level to which the standard applies. Some standards will include examples: additional detail about the intent of the standard or how it should be taught. We provide an example standard in Figure
1
.

III
Method

The goal of our research is to understand the various current S&P teaching standards for K-12 students
and how these align with what security and privacy professionals deem important.
In this section, we describe the two parts of our study: our collection and analysis of S&P standards and our interviews with subject-matter experts.

III-A

Standards analysis

Only Georgia explicitly outlines a security and privacy course in its CS standards.
Students in other states also learn S&P topics, but in different classes.
We reasoned that computer science and technology classes were most likely to contain this content and therefore focused our analysis on them.
While other classes (e.g., social studies) may also cover relevant subjects, our manual analysis methodology precluded us from examining all school subjects in this initial exploratory study.
Future work can build on our findings and perhaps use more automated approaches to expand the coverage.

Collecting CS standards.

We started by searching for computer science standards from each state, yielding standards for 47 states. We then confirmed that we had found all state standards by comparing against a list of states with known computer science standards
[
3
]
. Our search identified three more states with standards than the report indicated.
1
1

1

Nebraska and South Dakota released standards after the report was published. Minnesota’s standards are intended to be integrated into other core topics and are not intended for a standalone CS class. We included Minnesota regardless because it provided examples of how CS content might be covered outside of CS courses.

In addition to states, we also searched for standards developed by well-known CS teaching organizations. We identified CS standards from the Computer Science Teacher’s Association (CSTA)
[
33
]
and the International Society for Technology Educators (ISTE)
[
34
]
, which had been adopted in 10 and one state, respectively. We also identified security and privacy standards developed by the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), which hade not been adopted directly, but were a set of S&P-specific standards
[
35
]
.In addition to organizations, we considered exams and certifications that high school students may complete as additional sources of CS education. To this end, we analyzed the topic lists from both CS Advanced Placement exams (APCSA
[
36
]
and APCSP
[
37
]
), both CS International Baccalaureate exams (IBCS and IBCSD
[
38
]
), and the IC3 GS6 Digital Literacy certification
[
39
]
.

Identifying security and privacy standards.

Once we had collected all computer science standards, we wanted to select only those related to S&P.
We started by using open coding
[
40
]
to label the topics covered in
all
CS standards from four states.
Each team member individually sorted the labels into S&P-related and not S&P-related;
the research team then met to arrive at a consensus.
The labels that were agreed to be S&P-related formed our initial codebook.
In categorizing topics as (not) security-relevant,
we excluded those we considered to be too fundamental to be directly relevant to security
(e.g., the definition and applications of algorithms, control features like loops and conditionals).
However, we erred on the side of including topics like troubleshooting, debugging, and networking,
which represent starting points for developing security skills.

Two researchers then read and labeled
all
CS standards from the remaining states and organizations.
(If a standard was not security- or privacy-related, it was labeled as such.)
They met after coding each state to resolve disagreements and update the codebook as needed.
IRR was calculated for all states using Kupper-Hafner agreement, which is used to accurately calculate IRR in situations where more than one code can be assigned to an item
[
41
]
, as was the case for standards, which may cover multiple topics.
The average IRR was 0.578
but full agreement was reached on all labels after discussion.

Labeling security and privacy standards.

As described above, we labeled each standard with the topic it covered.
We also collected several other pieces of information.
Since states and organizations almost always categorized their standards, we identified whether the categories explicitly referenced security and/or privacy
(e.g., “Safety, Privacy, and Security”), as this illuminates what a teacher might find if they searched for these key terms.

Standards are often very general;
e.g.,

the student will evaluate current and emerging programming security practices
(Virginia).
Consequently, teachers may lack sufficient security and privacy background to effectively implement them
[
31
]
.
We therefore identified standards that contained
any type of additional detail outside of the standard text.

Finally, not all S&P content is relevant to everyone: some deeply technical topics (e.g., modes of encryption) might only be useful to future technology professionals. Therefore, we divided topics into those that would benefit everyone and those that would benefit only future professionals. To do this, the research team classified every label in the codebook as being beneficial to general internet/tech users, or primarily to future technology professionals, discussing each code and resolving any disagreements.

III-B

Interviews

After identifying and categorizing the standards, we interviewed S&P professionals to understand how the standards aligned with their expectations.

Protocol.

Between January 2025 and May 2025, we conducted 11 semi-structured interviews via Zoom. Each session lasted about one hour and was attended by two team members, one who asked questions and one who took notes.

The interviews started with a brief discussion of the subject’s professional background.
Next, we asked participants to describe the security and privacy topics they would cover in both a high school (HS) CS course and a more general course targeted towards all HS students. We also asked them to roughly prioritize the topics they suggested
and assess their longevity,
since standards may change infrequently.

In the second phase, we provided the participants with a link to an interactive web interface that contained all of the standards we collected and labeled.
(See Figure
4
for screenshot.
2
2

2

We will include a link to the interface upon acceptance.
)
The interface displayed the state where the standard originated, the categories covered by the standard, the standard text, and any examples included in the standard.
Users could filter the standards by grade (elementary, middle, and high school; the latter selected by default) and topic category.
All standards were presented in a randomized order.
Participants were encouraged to select a single category to further filter the standards. They were then asked to evaluate the filtered standards for longevity, target audience, topic coverage, and overall utility. Participants repeated this process for at least two categories, sometimes more. The full interview guide can be found in our supplementary materials.
3
3

3

Supplementary materials can be found at
https://osf.io/fgks3/?view_only=247f8466c22741af9bba98f5631819a2

Prior to the interviews, we piloted our study with four security and privacy professionals.
Based on early feedback,
we adapted the visualization tool to reduce the number of standards initially displayed to make it less overwhelming. We also adapted the interview protocol to be less repetitive and to better align with our research questions.
We made minor tweaks after subsequent pilots.

Recruitment.

For our interviews, we recruited professionals who worked (or had worked) in the field of security or privacy.
To reach them, we used snowball sampling
and leveraged personal connections,
asking personal contacts within the security and privacy field to send our recruitment message via email or make posts on social media (LinkedIn, Bluesky, and Mastodon) on our behalf.
Interested participants were directed to fill out a screening survey that asked them about their background and for their contact information.
4
4

4

A copy of the screening survey can be found in the supplementary materials.

All qualified participants were contacted for the interview.
We interviewed participants until we reached thematic saturation,
resulting in a total of 11 participants.

Analysis.

Once the interviews were complete, transcripts were generated using Zoom’s automatic transcription service. Two team members manually verified and corrected the transcripts and then analyzed them using
iterative open coding
[
40
]
. The two team
members independently coded each interview one at a time,
developing the codebook incrementally and resolving disagreements after every transcript.
The codebook was updated after each interview to reflect the new themes that emerged.
Since each transcript was labeled by both researchers and disagreements were discussed and resolved after every transcript, we did not calculate IRR.

Ethics.

We received approval from our institution’s ethics review board. Participants were asked for consent before both the screening survey and interview. Our interview consent form asked permission to record audio.
Participants were not compensated for the study.

III-C

Limitations

As discussed above, one limitation of our study is that it analyzed all CS standards
but not standards from other subjects.
As with all interview studies, our research suffers from limitations of generalizability due to our small sample size,
though we aimed to recruit a diverse interview population with varying career paths.
Participants only had a limited time to examine the standards, so they were not able to read through all of them.
To partially mitigate this, we randomized the standards in our interface to ensure that not all participants saw the same exact standards.
Finally,
our study does not shed light on all the possible ways standards could be improved.
In particular, we leave for future work questions about the usability and understandability of standards, which are best answered by teachers.

IV-A

S&P topics covered by CS standards (
RQ1
)

Standards covered 103 security- and privacy-relevant topics, most of which were technical, like encryption, and some of which were social, like equity, accessibility, and diversity. All topics and their definitions can be found in Table
I
.

States teach dangers of tracking—and benefits.

Unsurprisingly, almost all states
9
9

9

For brevity, we will refer to states and organizations collectively as “states.”

had standards about keeping students and their data safe. This was often framed in terms of practicing helpful behaviors and preventing harmful behaviors, though most standards did not specify which behaviors to teach. Some specified areas of focus, like online safety and settings management.

Around two-thirds of states had standards that instruct students to be careful with their online reputations and that information shared online can be permanent. This was sometimes framed as a way of protecting student privacy:
Explain the connection between the longevity of data on the internet, personal online identity and personal privacy
(Maryland). Elsewhere, it was framed as a way of protecting a student’s reputation:
Students will demonstrate an understanding of the importance of creating and maintaining a positive online identity and the permanence and future impact of their online and offline decisions when using digital technology
(South Dakota).

Almost 75% of states had standards covering the presence and scale of online tracking. Some framed this as a privacy concern:
Explain the privacy concerns related to the collection and generation of data through implicit and explicit processes
(Indiana). Others presented it as a process for students to understand:
Describe ways web advertising collects personal information
(Alabama).
Only three states had standards that covered tracking prevention.

While around 75% of states had standards stating that some information should be kept private, almost 40% of states had standards that indicated that there were benefits to sharing information online, such as:
Discuss trade-offs such as privacy, safety, and convenience associated with the collection and large scale analysis of information about individuals (e.g., social media, online shopping, how grocery/dept stores collect and use personal data)
(Idaho).
The Advanced Placement curriculum appeared to go even further:
PII and other information placed online can be used to enhance a user’s online experiences
(APCSP).

Almost all states cover ethics and accessibility.

We coded societal topics such as diversity, ethics, and accessibility as security-relevant and found that they were commonly found among CS standards.
Ethics was covered in more than 90% of states, with 85% covering usability and accessibility, around 75% covering equitable access to technology (e.g.,
Evaluate the impact of equity, access, and influence on the distribution of computing resources in a global society
, Wisconsin), and around 70% covering bias and diversity.
Many states had standards that asked students to take a computational artifact and make it less biased or more equitable. Standards mentioned ethics in many contexts, from defenses (see below), to laws and regulations (e.g.,
Evaluate the evolving legal and ethical tradeoffs that shape computing practices
, South Carolina), to student behavior when using technology (e.g.,
Students will practice positive, safe, legal, and ethical behavior when using technology
, South Dakota).

Encryption is painted in broad strokes.

Roughly two thirds of states had standards about encryption, though most detailed it only in broad terms:

Implement an encryption, digital signature, or authentication method
(Florida).
Specific examples tended to be historical (e.g., Caesar, Bacon’s, or Vigenère cipher).
While ten states had standards that mentioned public-key encryption, only one named a specific cryptosystem (RSA).
In comparison, seven states had standards that focused on steganography.

Standards cover a breadth of defenses and attacks.

States’ standards indicated that students should be able to choose defenses to address different scenarios. A little more than half of states had standards covering recommended protections for a specific scenario (e.g.,
Recommend security measures to address various scenarios based on factors such as efficiency, feasibility, and ethical impacts
, Illinois), and a little over 40% of the states had standards covering how to evaluate the benefits and drawbacks of different defenses (
Explain tradeoffs when selecting and implementing cybersecurity recommendations
, CSTA). A few states had standards that mentioned specific defenses, most of which related to authentication (e.g., 2FA, biometrics), though these were mostly a list of examples in a standard that was primarily about recommending or evaluating defenses. When evaluating defenses, state standards had text encouraging students to consider feasibility, efficiency, and usability. Of these, usability was the most popular, occurring in 40% of states.

States’ standards also covered social engineering, malware, ransomware, viruses, spyware, and worms. These topics sometimes occurred in the same standard as a list of example attacks to teach. DDoS also appeared in these lists, but other networking security topics did not. Malware (around 57% of states), viruses (around 44% of states), and social engineering (around 42% of states) were the most common attacks discussed, but it was equally common to discuss attacks without specifying specific ones to teach (around 53% of states):
Research and describe common attacks on hardware, software, and networks
(Arkansas). Attackers were rarely discussed, most often in the context of white hat versus black hat hacking.

IV-B

Organization of S&P standards (RQ2)

In this section, we detail our analysis of the presence of examples, and how standards were organized.

Examples ranged from missing to detailed.

As discussed in Section
II-C
,
we noted whether standards had examples or clarifying statements,
since these provide essential guidance to teachers,
especially those who may lack prior S&P background.
Of the 3,778 S&P standards we identified, 1,379— from 28 different states—contained examples.
For instance, the standard

Distinguish between private vs. public information
(CISA) had an accompanying “
Clarification statement: At this level, student discussions should focus on grade-appropriate examples of privacy and what is OK to share about themselves and how that relates to confidentiality.
”

The format of examples varied, although they tended to be consistent within states. Examples fell into three major patterns.
Seven states included clarifying statements to explain what should be taught, though they did not always label them as such or include them on all standards.
Five states provided specific examples of how a topic should be taught;
e.g., one from California specified an activity: “
Students could devise a plan for sending data to represent a picture, and devise a plan for interpreting the image when pieces of the data are missing.
”
California was also the only state to include definitions of terms and a statement of intent.

The most common type of example (17 states) was a list of terms that fell under a given topic. For example,
Explain principles of network security and techniques that protect stored and transmitted data (e.g., encryption, cryptography, and authentication)
, West Virginia. Some states only had one or two standards which contained the list of terms.

S&P standards were not always labeled as such.

While most state standards were grouped under categories (e.g., Networks & the Internet, Algorithms & Programming, Impacts of Computing), only 27 state standards had categories explicitly labeled as security and/or privacy, despite 44 states having standards that covered this topic. Of the 3,778 S&P-related standards we identified, only 1,627 were categorized by their state as related to S&P (13.6% of all standards, 43.1% of all standards we labeled as S&P).
For example, we categorized tracking as security and privacy, but states generally categorized it as “Impacts of Computing” or “Digital Citizenship.” When states did not have a security category, security standards often fell under “Networks & the Internet.” States generally labeled encryption, attacks & attackers, defenses, and organizational security as security.

Secure coding was sometimes mixed in with other topics.

States were divided on whether or not to label software security as security or place it with other programming standards. For example, Alaska labeled
Compare ways software developers protect devices and information from unauthorized access
as Cybersecurity and
Explain security issues that might lead to compromised computer programs
as Program Development. Generally, specific actions that programmers should take (such as secure coding, bounds checking, and input validation) were not categorized as security and instead fell under program development. The only topic related to secure coding that was categorized as S&P more than half of the time was software security (e.g.,
Utilize a software lifecycle process that considers security to plan and develop programs for all types of users
, Maryland), at around 53% of standards categorized as S&P.

States labeled security more often than privacy.

Topics referencing privacy were often not categorized as such. Only three states had any standards that were categorized as privacy. Privacy-relevant topics such as keeping information private, the importance of curating your online presence, and the social implications of privacy were only categorized as S&P 42%, 26%, and 17% of the time, respectively. This may make it difficult for teachers creating a unit about privacy to easily find all relevant standards.

Variance between states

Topic coverage between states varied widely.

Even the most popular topics were not universal.

Variance between grade-levels

The subjects that standards focused on varied with grade level.

Fundamental security and privacy topics prioritized in elementary schools.

Acceptable use and general safety were most commonly found in elementary level standards. For example, passwords and logins were most often taught at the elementary level, and dropped off sharply as students grew older. Acceptable use and appropriate behavior online were also emphasized at the elementary school level, but the drop-off in later grades was less sharp: high school students were still expected to
demonstrate and advocate for positive, safe, legal, and ethical habits when using technology and when interacting with others online
(West Virginia).

Secure transmission and networking were present most often at the middle school level.

Web security (e.g.,
Identify existing cybersecurity concerns with the Internet and systems it uses
, Alaska) and
secure data transmission (e.g.,

Apply multiple methods of encryption to model the secure transmission of information
, Mississippi)
were covered most often in middle school standards. Foundational versions of technical topics that were covered at the high school level were also sometimes mentioned here. For example, standards covering how to identify defenses were covered in middle school standards. Then, at the high school level, students were expected to recommend defenses for different scenarios and explain their reasoning.

Technical topics and critical thinking were covered most often in high school standards.

Secure coding, embedded systems, and real-world security and privacy were all covered most frequently at the high school level. Attacks and attackers was also present most often in high-school-level standards, with the exception of social engineering, which was covered more often in middle school standards.
Debate and discussion were considered most suitable for high school students. For example, laws and regulations were covered in at least 15 states at every grade level, but the tradeoffs associated with regulation were covered in 18 states at the high school level, two states at the middle school level, and one state at the elementary level.

Social and societal topics were covered at all levels.

On the social side, intellectual property & credit and usability & accessibility were covered at all grade levels. Other topics were covered more heavily at one grade level and then covered again, less frequently, in other grade levels. Diversity and inclusion was present most often at the elementary-school level. Ethics was covered at all levels, but most frequently in high school. In middle school, standards covered identifying bias, and in high school, they covered reducing bias and understanding and improving equity.

V-A

Topics S&P professionals would cover

In order not to bias the participants,
prior to showing them the standards, we asked what topics they would cover in a HS course. Our participants divided the content they suggested teaching into topics for students who might go on to become CS professionals and topics for students who would go on to have other professions.

V-A
1
Topics for everyone

When thinking about a more general HS course, participants mentioned topics such as phishing awareness and understanding how to be safe online.

Focus on online security and privacy.

Participants advocated for teaching security and privacy specifically in the online context.
Many participants felt that it was important for students to learn about the risks of social media, including the platforms’ data collection and monetization: “
They need to understand… that [their] information is being collected. How do [they] think Facebook, Instagram, TikTok is making any money?
” (P1). Participants also highlighted the importance of teaching students about the broader data collection and web tracking ecosystem.

Highlight consequences of S&P decisions.

Participants also highlighted the importance of teaching students the security and privacy implications of their actions online. Some stressed that online life is still real life, advocating for the importance of “
being mindful that it is still a human world with a different form of communication… there’s still humans on the other side of this, for better and for worse
” (P3). Privacy implications were a specific area of concern, as was caution around what students shared online.

Critical thinking is essential.

Participants placed a general emphasis on critical thinking, mentioning topics such as phishing awareness and a general suspicion of anything online. P8 explained that they would prioritize “
learning how to… think critically or… be skeptical, question emails, even if they’re coming from someone that you think might be real or might be a legitimate person.
” Participants later listed this as a top priority.

All students should be exposed to some technical topics.

Participants mentioned the importance of covering some technical topics for all students, such as good authentication practices, considering security and privacy when making consumer decisions, and keeping software and apps updated, describing these as basic life skills. One participant even suggested that all students be taught how to perform their own secure network setup.

V-A
2
Topics for future CS professionals

When thinking about a CS course, participants brought up topics ranging from technical subjects to a focus on getting students to see security and privacy as a developer’s responsibility.

Prioritize the security mindset.

When thinking about future developers, participants emphasized the importance of instilling a security and privacy mindset. For a CS course, P10 said, “
I would look at… what do you do in terms of responsible coding?… What are the factors there that you should be thinking about from a cybersecurity perspective?
”
Participants also highlighted the value of getting CS students to think about the tradeoffs between functionality and security & privacy. As P3 explained, “
I think a great place to start [is] I could do more if I had more information from this user… Where do you draw that line between: I don’t want to ask for too much, and I want it to be a unique tailored experience.
”

Spark interest with technical topics.

Participants highlighted the importance of teaching technical security and privacy topics as a way to get students interested in the area.
Hacking and penetration testing were brought up as a topic to teach, both for their utility and as a way to get students interested in the topic.
P6 advised covering “
the sexiest part of cybersecurity… red teaming.
” To better prepare students for their future learning and careers, participants also emphasized the importance of teaching fundamentals, such as networking, since “
if [students] don’t understand the network, it’s going to be hard for them to understand the threats
” (P5).

Focus on people and non-technical skills.

In addition to technical topics, participants wanted future CS professionals to be taught about how to integrate people into their considerations of security and privacy. P8 explained, “
I think a lot of times what’s left out is the end user part. … It doesn’t matter how much technology you put in place. As long as you have end users on your system, there will always be a way for attackers to get in.
” In addition to an emphasis on end users, participants also highlighted the importance of covering non-technical skills, such as communication, teamwork, and project management.

V-A
3
S&P mindset is more important than technologies

When asked which topics they would cover if they only had time for one or two, participants indicated that cultivating a security and privacy mindset in students was more important than covering technical topics. Threat modeling was the most common priority, for both future technology professionals and general internet/tech users, but suspicion, critical thinking, and phishing awareness were also commonly mentioned. Participants prioritized a security and privacy mindset because “
cybersecurity isn’t something that happens once, right? It’s something that has to be a continual piece of the way you’re thinking about things.
” (P10). For participants who prioritized technical topics, their first choices included authentication and keeping software updated.

V-A
4
Alignment between standards and participants’ suggestions

Almost all of the topics mentioned by participants were covered in some capacity by the standards we analyzed. This is true both for topics intended for future professionals and topics intended for everyone.
The standards coverage on topics intended for everyone aligned well with the topics participants prioritized, including social media (18 states), tracking and data collection (33 states), being cautious of sharing information online (30 states), and phishing and social engineering (19 states) (e.g.,
Identify phishing emails, insecure websites (e.g., not https), and risky links when on the Internet; build a positive digital footprint (e.g., blog, website, social media)
, Georgia). Topics advocated for by participants but covered less frequently were keeping software updated (e.g.,
Recognize the importance of and effectively perform software and security patches/updates
, North Dakota) and critical thinking, which were only covered by 1–4 states.

The topics mentioned by participants for technical students covered most often in the standards were hacking (19 states) and testing (35 states). However, topics heavily prioritized by participants, such as threat modeling and considering end-users (e.g.,
Implement software security preventative maintenance techniques, such as installing service packs and patches and training users about malicious software prevention technologies
, Georgia) were only covered by 1–3 states.
The topics mentioned and prioritized by participants but omitted from standards were more philosophical, like security being a developer’s responsibility or how to get end user buy-in for security.

V-A
5
Longevity of suggested topics

Since standards have a long turnover rate, we asked professionals to estimate the longevity of the topics they suggested. Participants generally tended to believe that core concepts in security and privacy will not change in the near future but the specific technologies and methods used will.

Technology changes rapidly, but concepts do not.

About half of our participants believed that specific technologies would change rapidly, but core concepts would move more slowly. When discussing the longevity of phishing awareness and suspicion, P2 said, “
Cyber-criminals trying to steal your information and get into your system, that’ll never change… How they do it will change.
” Some participants listed specific technologies they thought would change in the near future, such as authentication approaches and social media structures. However, even these participants emphasized that understanding how to manage settings on social media would still remain important.

AI will change security and privacy priorities.

One of the key reasons participants believed that the technology and methods used in security and privacy will change is the increased prevalence of AI. Almost all participants said that AI will change the way security and privacy is done. A few participants mentioned specific ways AI may impact key aspects of security and privacy, such as bias in AI becoming more important, AI-written code making validation more important, and automation reducing or removing certain problems. However, participants also emphasized that while AI may change how security and privacy is done, it will never remove the need for human involvement in the process. As P3 illustrated when referring to idea of AI fully generating applications: “
I still have to ask it, and I still have to validate it. And so I think we focus on some of those things that we know are less dynamic as the core.
”

Anything involving a human-in-the loop will remain relevant.

When participants brought up things that would not change, they sometimes talked about humans. A few brought up phishing and some thought that threat modeling was likely to remain important. P6 explained their reasoning: “
As long as there’s a human element in existence, there’s going to be social engineering, and there’s going to be a need for skepticism.
”

V-B

Experts’ exploration of teaching standards

Once participants had explained what they thought should be taught, they were asked to explore the set of standards we collected. They were asked to comment on the content (was there anything they expected to see but did not?), the utility, and the longevity of the standards.

V-B
1
Standards’ content

When asked whether they expected to see something in the presented standards,
most of the participants did not find any topics missing from the standards they viewed, and a few found that the standards’ coverage exceeded their expectations.

Standards could be strengthened with new technologies and approaches.

When evaluating the standards, some participants noted that the standards did not include some technologies and approaches that would have been nice to have. Examples included a discussion of how policy impacts technology, a mention of quantum when discussing encryption, more coverage of specific tools that an attacker could use, and discussion of how to recover from attacks when defenses have failed. Participants did not say these should be strictly required, instead identifying them as topics that would be nice to include in the standards to make them as comprehensive as possible.

Standards should focus on people and non-technical skills.

Participants expected to see standards in every category (technical and societal) have a focus on people and non-technical skills, and the standards were successful at this to varying degrees. Some participants identified a lack of content focused on social engineering, communication skills, and end users—all topics prioritized by participants when asked what they would cover. One place where the standards met the expectations of participants was their coverage of critical thinking, a topic they emphasized as essential earlier in the interview. When assessing organizational security standards, P3 commented, “
I do like that a number of these seem to be getting into the more critical thinking… and kind of the organizational security.
”

Concerns about the lack of specifics.

A few participants were concerned about the format of the teaching standards. Some participants expected that the teaching standards would contain concrete specifics of what to teach, since teachers using these standards are unlikely to have a security and privacy background. P2 shared, “
My assumption was there would be more to it than this… So if… by the time we’re finished, we want people to know how to keep information safe and know the appropriate behavior. Then there would be guidelines… they would break it down and explain what they consider to be the safe, appropriate behavior. And if that’s not with it, then I don’t know how you would do it.
”
One participant
expressed concern about the very textual nature of the standards, instead advocating for a more visual depiction.

V-B
2
Standards’ usefulness

Since utility is fairly subjective, participants were asked to evaluate standards for their intended audience (i.e., who would benefit from a class that utilized this standard) and their overall “usefulness.”

All students would benefit from some
S&P education.

Some of our participants emphasized that security and privacy is beneficial to all students, and one even thought security and privacy should be a core topic in high school. While there was less agreement about what should be specifically covered, participants thought that all students should be exposed to security and privacy at some point during their high school education because all students “
need to be a security person… no matter what field they’re going into, because [in] every field you’re going to have stuff that you want to protect… You’re going to have information that you don’t want getting out there
” (P8).

Fundamental concepts are more useful than details.

When looking at what specifically to cover, participants highlighted that fundamental concepts were more useful than technical details, aligning with their concerns about longevity. Some participants indicated that some of the standards in their selected topic area (encryption and networking) would be useful for everyone to learn, but a detailed technical understanding would not be necessary. When discussing the utility of networking standards, P4 said, “
I don’t necessarily need to know the details of how IP addresses are routed, and things like that, to understand that things are not just falling out of the sky… You know that there are backbone, infrastructure things that have to be there. Understanding that… I think, is valuable to all students and that’s where I would put emphasis.
”

Technical standards have varying degrees of utility.

Participants thought that specific technical standards varied in their utility. Some participants named specific professions that would benefit from the technical details of various topics like encryption, attacks and attackers, networking, and programming. In the case of encryption, P11 said it was for “
students who want to go into application development, or like a more technical trajectory or a business trajectory.
” P11 also mentioned law and journalism as careers where encryption might be useful. Some participants believed that encryption is only useful for technical professions.

Some participants highlighted specific technical standards that would not be useful for anyone to learn, since they covered what they viewed as outdated or distant future technical topics, such as steganography and quantum computing, respectively. When looking at encryption, three participants indicated that steganography was not useful, though it only appeared in 11 standards.

Social- and societal-centered standards are useful for everyone.

A few participants indicated that social and societal concepts (safe and appropriate behavior, communication skills, critical thinking, laws and policies, and social engineering) were particularly useful, describing them as something that all students would benefit from learning. P6 summarized, “
Safe and appropriate behavior, content and information, organizational security… I think those are very appropriate for for all futures.
”

Participants were split on the utility of ethics and law standards.

Within the societal standards, participants were split on the benefits of ethics and law. On the topic of ethics, one participant thought it would be useful for everyone, one participant mentioned it would be useful for legal and academic careers specifically, and P9 said, “
Debate issues of ethics related to real world computing technologies? That’s pretty cool… I don’t know how much that would come into play in a coding class.
”

V-B
3
Standards’ longevity

Since standards are used for 5–10 years before being updated, understanding the longevity of the content is important. Participants highlighted aspects of both the standard content and formatting that may impact the longevity.

The nature of security and privacy makes good longevity difficult.

A few participants noted that promoting good longevity in security and privacy standards inherently goes against the ever-changing nature of security and privacy because “
what to teach or focus on changes weekly, not yearly [or] monthly
” (P9). In fact, P10 described this as the “
biggest challenge for educators
” because it is not clear “
how you keep pace with evolving technology, and specifically to cyber, the evolving threat landscape.
”

People- and mindset-focused standards have better longevity than technical standards.

When looking at the specifics of the content, most participants highlighted that mindset and people-focused standards had better longevity than technical standards because they focus more on concepts rather than technical details. Similar to the topics they prioritized earlier in the interview, participants described standards covering topics such as threat modeling, social engineering, policies, law and ethics, safe and appropriate behavior, real-world impacts, and equity, accessibility and diversity as having better longevity because “
at the core of these equity, accessibility, and diversity standards is empathy… That has longevity beyond any technology or technological concept
” (P11).

Conversely, standards covering technical specifics, such as encryption and networking, were thought to have shorter longevity by half of our participants, since technology changes rapidly in the security and privacy space, mirroring their longevity concerns about teaching specific technologies in the classroom, as discussed in Section
V-A5
.

Broader standards have better longevity than specific standards.

Focusing more on how the standards are written rather than the content, half of our participants mentioned concerns about the broadness or specificity of the standards. Standards that were written broadly and focused more on core concepts rather than specifics were thought to have better longevity because “
there’s no guarantee that specific things are going to continue to be relevant
” (P6), echoing the focus of participants on concepts rather than technologies in Section
V-A5
.

Standards need to be updated regularly.

A few participants said that standards should be changed regularly because “
what’s relevant today isn’t going to be relevant tomorrow, and it wasn’t relevant yesterday
” (P6). One participant emphasized the need for regular change without providing a timeline, while another specifically stated that “
tools would be 1 year [and] concepts 3 years
” (P6). P4 served on a standards committee which determined that all standards should change every five years.

VI
Discussion

As an initial exploration of S&P teaching standards in the United States, our results shed light on how states would like these topics to be taught to K-12 students.

State curricula overlap, but no topics are universal.

State S&P standards covered a large range of topics—103, according to our categorization.
At a high level, there was a considerable degree of agreement about what should be taught.
For example, 37 out of 45 states had standards that discussed different types of attacks and attackers,
and 40 out of 45 had standards covering various defenses.
Yet, it is notable that no topics were covered by all states,
and even the most popular topics were covered by only two thirds of states and organizations.
This leads us to conclude that there is no complete consensus about what should be taught in security and privacy.
On one hand, this is not surprising:
some variation between states is always expected,
and prioritization of S&P topics is notoriously difficult even for experts
[
43
]
.
On the other hand, security threats are the same regardless of the state where one grows up,
and the fact that different students are receiving different preparation suggests that some students are also obtaining better—or worse—outcomes.
Future research should examine student outcomes by state
and investigate the possibility of more empirically driven curricula.

Standards have good longevity, but AI may change things.

Bureaucracies move slowly, and the education field is no exception.
Standards take time to be approved and are updated only infrequently.
As such, there is a risk that standards, especially in a fast-moving field like S&P, become out of date by the time they reach students.
Fortunately, our research suggests that this does not seem to be the case currently.
Security experts concurred that today’s standards cover relevant and useful topics.
When they discussed what they think schools
should
teach,
we were heartened to find these topics largely covered by existing standards.
Furthermore, the experts believed that the most important topics, such as threat modeling and the security mindset, would remain relevant regardless of future technological advances.
However, our participants emphasized that many S&P topics
may
become out of date,
especially due to recent advances in AI.
As such, they agreed that it is critical for standards to be frequently reviewed and revised.
With this in mind, standards designers may wish to delineate those standards they believe are teaching fundamental skills from those that are more closely tied to specific technologies.

Vagueness is strategic but may create difficulties for teachers.

One aspect of standards noted by both researchers and participants is their vagueness.
Our interviewees, especially one who had experience with standards development, highlighted this as a double-edged sword.
The strategic ambiguity of a standard like

Compare ways to protect devices, software, and data
(Alabama)
means that a teacher can still illustrate this topic with different defenses as technology evolves.
However, doing so also increases demands on the teacher:
they must know enough about the subject to be able to turn the vague guideline into a concrete lesson plan,
then keep it up-to-date with changing technology.
Standards could help by including more examples—something our analysis found was lacking.
States and national organizations could also provide sample curricula and modules.
Another solution is to recruit more teachers with relevant S&P background and to provide training for those who do not already have it.

Teachers are central.

Our analysis illustrates the crucial role of teachers,
who are tasked with interpreting the often-vague standards and turning them into detailed curricula and lesson plans.
Are the standards too vague or just right?
It all depends on the teacher.
We also acknowledge teachers as a missing element of this study.
We see their perspective as critical,
but we felt that incorporating an additional set of interviews with the existing components of this work would dilute the results of each.
We therefore leave this for future work,
noting, however, that our team includes a researcher with significant high school teaching experience,
which has shaped our approach at all stages of research.

Standards need a clearer audience and goals.

Our participants highlighted a persistent point of confusion with the standards:
who are they for?
The interviewee with standards-writing experience confirmed that this was also a question their group had struggled with.
Students in high school CS classes might become security professionals, or work in software more generally, or end up as neither of those.
Which of these paths should the course be preparing them for?
A similar dilemma faces those developing standards for CS in general, as well as other topics.
However, our participants felt that this question was especially acute in S&P.
Topics like secure coding are important for future developers and appropriate even in an introductory CS class.
However, staying safe online is even more fundamental.
Given limited classroom time, having a clear vision for the educational goals of the class (and associated standards) can help with prioritization.
This is something current standards seem to be lacking,
even accounting for the fact that they cover earlier grade levels.
Future standards would do well to more clearly articulate their goals.
One approach might be to divide standards into mandatory courses and elective courses, so that future tech professionals can learn the security and privacy topics they need while all students get a necessary background in security and privacy.

Security and privacy are for everyone, but K-12 CS classes are not.

Interviewees emphasized that S&P education is important for everyone, and some even said it should be a core topic.
But the security and privacy material covered by the standards we analyzed is not reaching the majority of students.
Code.org estimates that only 6.4% of high school students are enrolled in a CS course in any given year,
with only 60% of American high schools offering elective CS courses—and even fewer in low-income districts
[
3
]
.
Since most of the US population will not go to college, K-12 remains the best place to inculcate critical security and privacy skills.
But, with school schedules already packed and teachers already overloaded, incorporating S&P will require substantial resources—and a clearly articulated argument for allocating them.
A more secure and private future requires effort from all stakeholders:
parents, educators, students, practitioners, and scholars will all need to contribute.
