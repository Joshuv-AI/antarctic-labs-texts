The middle frontal gyrus as a neural mediator between family relationships and internet addiction among adolescents with internet gaming disorder: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yibo Zhang, Yanyan Li, Huijie Zhang, Tao Zhao, Yange Li, Jie Wu, Ruiqi Wang, Qiyan Lv, Yan Lang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have shown that family relationships are closely related to internet gaming disorder (IGD) in adolescents. There are also neurological mechanisms that correlate spontaneous brain activity with adolescent family relationships and IGD. Therefore, this study explores the mediating role of spontaneous brain activity between family relationships and IGD in adolescents.MethodsThis study used a cross-sectional design. It recruited 52 IGD adolescents as the IGD group and 51 normal adolescents as the healthy control (HC) group. The study included adolescents aged 12–18 years, with a male-to-female ratio of 45:7 in the IGD group and 41:10 in the HC group. The diagnosis of IGD was established based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria. The Young Internet Addiction Scale (YIAS) was used to assess the severity of internet addiction among adolescents, and the Chinese version of the Family Environment Scale (FES-CV) was used to evaluate family relationships. Resting-state functional MRI was utilized to assess adolescents’ spontaneous brain activity, specifically measuring the mean amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (mALFF) and functional connectivity (FC). MRI data were collected using a Siemens Magnetom Prisma 3.0T MRI scanner. The specific scan sequence and parameters are as follows: BOLD sequence, repetition time (TR) = 1000 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, flip angle = 70°, slice thickness = 2.2 mm, number of slices = 52, matrix size = 64 × 64, voxel size = 3 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm, scan duration = 360 s. The steps of data preprocessing included data format conversion, exclusion of time points, slice timing correction, head motion correction, spatial normalization, removal of linear drift, regression of covariates, band-pass filtering, and data cleaning. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 22.0. Gaussian random field (GRF) correction was applied in the DPABI toolbox, with voxel-level p < 0.005 and cluster-level p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. Pearson correlation was employed to analyze the relationships between various scales and between these scales and brain spontaneous activity. Mediation analysis was performed using the SPSS Process program.Results(1) Compared to the HC group, the IGD group had significantly higher YIAS scores (t = 21.571, P < 0.05). For the FES-CV scores, the IGD group scored higher in conflict (t = 4.228, P < 0.05) and lower in cohesion (t=-3.768, P < 0.05), with statistically significant differences. (2) Pearson correlation analysis revealed a negative correlation between cohesion in family relationships and middle frontal gyrus (MFG) (r = − 0.443, p < 0.01), a negative correlation between cohesion and internet addiction (r = − 0.474, p < 0.01), and a positive correlation between internet addiction and MFG (r = 0.557, p < 0.01) in the IGD group. The MFG mediated the relationship between family relationships and internet addiction, with a mediation effect value of -0.183 (95% CI=-0.403 ~ -0.037), accounting for 38.77% of the total effect (-0.183/-0.472). (3) In the IGD group, the FC value from the MFG to the right insula was positively correlated with conflict in family relationships (r = 0.349, P < 0.05).ConclusionFamily relationships are related to internet addiction in adolescents, and the MFG mediates this relationship. Additionally, the FC value from the MFG to the right insula in the IGD group is positively correlated with conflict. This suggests that the MFG may serve as a neurobiological marker through which family relationships influence IGD development in adolescents.Keywords: Internet gaming disorder, Family relationships, Middle frontal gyrus, Adolescents, Mediating effect

Introduction

With the widespread use of the Internet, internet gaming has become one of the most popular recreational activities among adolescents, leading to the increasing prevalence of internet gaming disorder (IGD). IGD is defined as excessive or uncontrolled immersion in internet games, ignoring long-term negative consequences, resulting in impairments in interpersonal relationships, social functioning, and both physical and mental health. Recent epidemiological studies have found that the overall prevalence of IGD among adolescents is 8.8% [1], with the prevalence among Asians being the highest among the four continents, reaching 9.9% [2]. Therefore, exploring factors influencing the development of IGD in adolescents is of great significance.

Family relationships and IGD

Family relationships play a significant role in adolescent development, family relationships refer to the cohesion, emotional expression, and conflict among family members, particularly between parents and adolescents [3]. During adolescence, the growth environment undergoes changes, and adolescents gradually detach from their families to become independent [4]. Adolescents with poor family relationships tend to turn to online gaming to avoid negative emotions [5]. Adolescents with IGD perceive their family relationships as more negative [6], often characterized by low cohesion, limited emotional expression, and high conflict [7, 8]. Research has shown that poor family relationships are associated with an increased prevalence of IGD [9, 10], while positive family relationships act as protective factors against IGD [11, 12]. A lack of familial care [13], high conflict [12], and single-parent households [14]are identified as risk factors for IGD, in family environments characterized by childhood psychological abuse, adolescents may seek support in the virtual world and gradually develop IGD [15].

Spontaneous brain activity and IGD

Spontaneous brain activity can be detected through resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI). Research on brain spontaneous activity in IGD is developing rapidly, providing a better understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying IGD and making it possible to differentiate IGD from normal populations. Previous studies have indicated that individuals with IGD exhibit changes in brain function and structure; however, most studies have focused on adults, and the findings cannot be directly applied to adolescents. Adolescence is a critical period for cognitive and behavioral brain development, and studies of brain spontaneous activity reveal that the prefrontal cortex develops later, showing a linear increase during adolescence [16]. In adolescents with IGD, brain spontaneous activity is primarily characterized by abnormalities in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) [17], which is activated when engaging in internet gaming, leading to increased impulsivity [18], low control [19, 20], impaired cognitive abilities [21], and underdeveloped frontal-parietal networks (FPN) [22]. Therefore, there are differences in brain spontaneous activity between adults and adolescents, with adolescents being a high-risk group for IGD [23].

Family relationships and spontaneous brain activity

Advancements in neuroimaging techniques have allowed for the exploration of the neural mechanisms linking family relationships and IGD in adolescents. Studies have shown that family relationships can affect the development of brain function and structure in adolescents [24]. Family relationships play an essential role in cognitive control mediated by the adolescent prefrontal cortex [25]. High cohesion in family relationships can promote the development of cognitive control functions in the adolescent brain [26] and improve cognitive flexibility in children [27]. Growing up in a supportive family environment has been associated with stronger executive function (EF) in children [28]. Conversely, exposure to stress-related family environments may adversely impact prefrontal cortex development [29], high conflict in family relationships leads to a longitudinal increase in the activation of the left prefrontal cortex in adolescents [30]. Aggressive family relationships have been shown to result in poor development of the adolescent prefrontal cortex [31]. Additionally, some studies have found that the family relationships of adolescents with IGD are related to reward sensitivity mediated by the prefrontal-striatal circuitry [11]. Family support has been found to significantly buffer the negative predictive effect of anxiety on EF in adolescents [32].

In summary, both family relationships and brain spontaneous activity play vital roles in the development of IGD, but the neurobiological mechanisms underlying the relationship between family relationships and IGD remain poorly understood. A widely recognized framework for explaining the development and maintenance of IGD is the I-PACE model [33, 34]. This model integrates psychological and neurobiological factors, positing that those individual predispositions—such as personality traits, motivation, and psychopathology—interact with affective states (e.g., stress, anxiety, depression) and cognitive processes (e.g., reward sensitivity, biased expectancies) to influence behavioral execution. Notably, deficits in executive functioning, including impaired inhibitory control or planning ability, can exacerbate maladaptive behavior, forming a feedback loop that sustains addictive patterns. Overall, the I-PACE model provides a comprehensive theoretical framework for investigating the neural and behavioral mechanisms underlying IGD. Based on the current research, this study focuses on adolescent and employs rs-fMRI to assess spontaneous brain activity, specifically mean amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (mALFF) and functional connectivity (FC). The study will analyze the correlations between the scales and mALFF, and further explore the mediating role of brain spontaneous activity in the relationship between family relationships and IGD. This research aims to provide better preventive strategies and interventions for adolescent IGD.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

This study was a cross-sectional investigation. 1.IGD Group: Adolescents diagnosed with IGD who visited the outpatient psychiatry department at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University from September 2021 to April 2023 were selected for the IGD group, which consisted of 45 boys and 7 girls. Inclusion criteria: (1) Diagnosed with IGD based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria by two psychiatrists; (2) Aged 12–18 years, Han ethnicity, right-handed; (3) A total score of ≥ 40 on the Young Internet Addiction Scale (YIAS); (4) Daily gaming time ≥ 4 h or weekly gaming time ≥ 30 h. Exclusion criteria: (1) History of severe brain injury or brain organic diseases such as encephalitis or epilepsy; (2) Intellectual disability with an IQ < 80; (3) MRI contraindications, such as metal implants, phobias, or inability to cooperate with the scan; (4) Use of any psychiatric medications or other treatments (e.g., physical therapy, psychotherapy) in the past month; (5) Previous or current diagnosis of other mental disorders (such as depression, anxiety disorder, schizophrenia, etc.). During recruitment, 90 adolescents met the inclusion and exclusion criteria; however, 35 declined to participate, and 3 did not complete all assessments, therefore the sample comprised 52 IGD adolescents. 2.Healthy Control Group (HC): Adolescents recruited from the community between September 2021 and April 2023 were included in the HC group, consisting of 41 boys and 10 girls. Inclusion criteria: (1) No diagnosis of IGD according to DSM-5; (2) Aged 12–18 years, Han ethnicity, right-handed; (3) YIAS score < 40; (4) Daily gaming time < 4 h and weekly gaming time < 30 h. Exclusion criteria were the same as for the IGD group. During recruitment, 52 adolescents met the inclusion and exclusion criteria; however, 1 did not complete all assessments, resulting in a final sample of 51 adolescents in the HC group. This study complied with the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki. It was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Approval No. 2022-KY-0438-002). Prior to participation, all subjects and their families were informed of the study’s purpose, significance, and procedures, and provided written informed consent.

General sociodemographic data

General sociodemographic data was collected from participants, including gender, age, years of education, and daily or weekly gaming time, along with psychological assessments and rs-fMRI data acquisition. The psychological assessments were completed by the participants themselves.

Psychological assessment

Young internet addiction scale

The Young Internet Addiction Scale (YIAS), developed by Young, was used to assess the severity of internet addiction in adolescents [35]. This scale consists of 20 items rated on a 5-point scale, with scores ranging from “almost never” (1) to “always” (5). The total score ranges from 20 to 100, with a score ≥ 40 indicating internet addiction. Higher scores indicate more severe addiction. Previous studies have shown that the YIAS has good reliability and validity in Chinese adolescents [10, 36]. In this study, the YIAS had a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.81.

Chinese version of the family environment scale

The Chinese Version of the Family Environment Scale (FES-CV) was used to assess the family environment of adolescents. The scale includes 90 items scored as “yes” or “no,” and comprises 10 subscales: cohesion, emotional expression, conflict, independence, achievement orientation, intellectual-cultural orientation, recreation, moral-religious emphasis, organization, and control [37]. This study focused on the subscales of cohesion, emotional expression, and conflict, as they are the most relevant to family relationships [3]. Higher scores on cohesion and emotional expression indicate better family relationships, while higher scores on conflict indicate worse family relationships. The FES-CV has demonstrated good reliability, validity, and internal consistency [37]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the FES-CV was 0.71.

MRI data acquisition

MRI data were collected using a Siemens Magnetom Prisma 3.0T MRI scanner with a 64-channel head coil. Participants were instructed to remain still during the scan. First, a routine MRI head scan sequence was performed to exclude individuals with obvious structural brain abnormalities. This was followed by a rs-fMRI scan using a single-shot echo planar imaging technique. The specific scan sequence and parameters are as follows: BOLD sequence, repetition time (TR) = 1000 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, flip angle = 70°, slice thickness = 2.2 mm, number of slices = 52, matrix size = 64 × 64, voxel size = 3 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm, scan duration = 360 s.

fMRI data preprocessing

Data preprocessing was performed using the DPABI toolbox on the Matlab platform. The steps included data format conversion, exclusion of time points, slice timing correction, head motion correction, spatial normalization, removal of linear drift, regression of covariates, band-pass filtering, and data cleaning. No participants were excluded due to excessive head motion.

Amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation calculation

The amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) reflects the level of spontaneous brain activity in each voxel during the resting state, as measured by the BOLD signal in the 0.01–0.08 Hz range. The mean ALFF value for each voxel is computed within this frequency band, and the mALFF value is calculated as the ratio of the ALFF value for each voxel to the average ALFF value across the whole brain. mALFF values were then detrended and filtered to improve sensitivity to spontaneous brain activity.

Functional connectivity analysis

Regions of interest (ROIs) were selected based on brain areas of interest, with a spherical ROI of 3 mm radius centered on the coordinates of the selected regions. FC was computed between the selected ROIs and other brain regions. Fisher’s Z transformation was applied to the FC values to improve normality, and 6 mm Gaussian kernel smoothing was used for full-width half-maximum smoothing. FC values for each ROI were extracted using the DPABI toolbox, with FC representing the strength of activity between different brain regions.

Statistical analysis

mALFF and FC analysis

Independent sample t-tests were conducted on mALFF values of brain regions between the IGD and HC groups using SPM8 software, with age, gender, and frame-wise displacement (FD) as covariates. The mALFF differences in brain regions were identified, and FC analysis was performed on selected regions using independent sample t-tests. Gaussian random field (GRF) correction was applied in the DPABI toolbox, with voxel-level p < 0.005 and cluster-level p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance, and the minimum cluster size set to 30 voxels.

General data and mediation analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0. Normality tests for all data were conducted using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical data were expressed as n (%), and continuous data with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For the comparison of quantitative data in general information (such as age and years of education), the independent sample t-test was used, and the chi-square test was used for the comparison of qualitative data (such as gender). Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships between variables. Mediation analysis was conducted using the PROCESS plugin in SPSS, with Model 4 selected [38]. Using the mALFF value of the differential brain regions in the IGD group as the mediator variable, family relationships of the IGD group as the independent variable, and YIAS scores of the IGD group as the dependent variable, with gender and age considered as covariates due to their potential impact on IGD, the mediation model was constructed. Bootstrap methods with 5000 samples were used for mediation testing, and a 95% confidence interval (CI) was used. Mediation effects were considered significant if the CI did not contain 0. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Discussion

This study focused on adolescents with IGD to explore the impact of family relationships on IGD. First, the study found cohesion in family relationships was negatively correlated with internet addiction. Second, the MFG was identified as a mediator between family relationships and internet addiction. Lastly, FC analysis using MFG as the ROI revealed that the FC value between MFG and the right insula was positively correlated with conflict in the IGD group.

Impact of family relationships on IGD

The results indicate a negative correlation between cohesion and internet addiction, consistent with previous studies [5, 39]. Cohesion, as defined in the FES-CV, refers to the “emotional bonding among family members and the feeling of closeness expressed through a sense of belonging and acceptance within the family“ [40]. Families with low cohesion tend to increase adolescents’ escape motivation, as these adolescents may turn to online gaming for social interaction in order to escape dysfunctional family environments [5]. In contrast, families with higher cohesion can promote adaptive emotional regulation strategies through communication and sharing [41], providing a sense of belonging and a safe outlet for tension. This, in turn, helps regulate external stressors, such as peer problems, reducing the risk of IGD [42] and anxiety levels in IGD adolescents [43]. In terms of treatment, cohesion plays an important role in IGD management. Han et al. conducted family therapy for IGD adolescents and found that after three weeks, improvements in family cohesion were associated with increased activation in the caudate nucleus in response to emotional stimuli, and were negatively correlated with online gaming time [44]. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) has also been shown to reduce IGD severity and improve family cohesion to the greatest extent [45]. However, longitudinal studies suggest that high family cohesion does not necessarily reduce the risk of IGD, indicating that other factors, in addition to cohesion, should be considered in IGD treatment [43].

Mediating role of MFG between family relationships and internet addiction

This study found that the MFG plays a mediating role in the pathway from family relationships to internet addiction in adolescents diagnosed with IGD. The MFG, which plays a key role in emotional regulation, shows significant activation when processing negative emotions [46]. As part of the DLPFC, the MFG is a crucial node in the cognitive control network. During adolescence, cognitive control capabilities are still developing, and the neuroplasticity of adolescent cognitive functions, along with social influences on the nervous system [47], leads to developmental changes in the prefrontal cortex thickness, which are related to both cognition and emotions [48]. Therefore, adolescents’ family relationships can influence the development of brain functions and structures.

These findings can be interpreted within the framework of the I-PACE model of internet gaming disorder [33, 34]. According to the model, addictive behaviors result from the interaction of individual predispositions (Person), affective states (Affect), cognitive processes (Cognition), and executive functioning (Execution). The MFG, as a key region involved in cognitive control and executive functions, corresponds to the “Execution” component of the model. Low family cohesion may affect adolescents’ affective states and cognitive processes, which in turn engage the MFG to regulate behavioral responses. This mechanism provides a neurobiological explanation for how family relationships impact adolescents’ tendencies toward internet addiction through alterations in cognitive control and executive functioning.

According to the cognitive-behavioral model of IGD [49], adolescents from families with low cohesion tend to have higher reward sensitivity and lower cognitive control, leading to stronger desires for online gaming and an inability to control these urges. This aligns with the I-PACE model, as the interaction of affective and cognitive factors, mediated by executive function regions such as the MFG, contributes to maladaptive gaming behavior. Therefore, our study proposes a neurobiological mechanism linking family relationships to internet addiction. Previous studies have demonstrated that family therapy can improve family cohesion in IGD adolescents and reduce DLPFC activation [44], while CBT can enhance cognitive control [50].

Integrating the I-PACE model provides a comprehensive theoretical framework that links familial, cognitive, and neural mechanisms in adolescent internet addiction.

FC analysis of MFG

Using MFG as an ROI for FC analysis, the results showed abnormal FC in brain areas predominantly within the FPN. The DLPFC is a core node of FPN [51] and participates in feedback learning during adolescence [52]. Family relationships have also been found to be associated with FPN [53], suggesting that the abnormal FC in the FPN of IGD adolescents may be due to impaired cognitive function and emotional regulation abilities resulting from poor family relationships. FPN abnormalities have also been observed in substance addiction and are associated with cognitive control [54]. Additionally, our study revealed a positive correlation between the FC value of MFG to the right insula and conflict in the IGD group. The insula is a key region for emotional processing [55] and one of the critical brain areas for addiction behaviors [56]. Both MFG and the insula are key nodes in significant brain networks that play a role in emotional responses [57], and they are also involved in the prefrontal-limbic circuitry that regulates emotions [55]. The abnormal FC between MFG and the insula may be explained by the fact that, during adolescence, changes in the adolescent’s perception of the external environment occur while the parents’ parenting styles may not have adapted. As a result, IGD adolescents have poorer emotional processing capabilities and are less willing to face negative emotions with family members. Family disagreements, especially when adolescents react angrily to strict parenting styles, can lead to conflicts [5], resulting in the formation of family discord.

Limitations and future research

There are several limitations in this study that need to be addressed in future research: First, the cross-sectional design of the study, while providing a theoretical basis for the mediation model, does not allow for direct conclusions about causal relationships between the variables. Future research should consider adopting longitudinal cohort studies to explore the associations between IGD, family relationships, and spontaneous brain activity. Second, the severity of IGD in this study was generally mild, and the sample was not homogeneous. Adolescents with more severe IGD may have poorer self-awareness and may not seek treatment, limiting the study’s generalizability. Future research should include a broader range of IGD severity. Third, our sample included a higher proportion of boys than girls, which aligns with epidemiological findings suggesting that boys are more vulnerable to developing IGD [58]. Although gender was statistically controlled for in our analyses, future research should further investigate potential gender-specific neural mechanisms in the MFG. Fourth, the sample size of this study is relatively small, which may reduce the ability of statistical analysis. Future studies suggest expanding the sample size to further verify the findings of this study and enhance its universal applicability. Finally, this study relied on self-reported questionnaires from adolescents, which may introduce potential biases, as responses could be influenced by emotional or situational factors during the evaluation. Additionally, this study did not collect information on socioeconomic status, parenting styles, or parental education. These variables may have a potential impact on IGD. These key social factors should be incorporated in future research designs to enhance the breadth of research results and the rigor of controlling confounding variables.

Conclusion

This study is the first to reveal the mediating role of the MFG in the relationship between family relationships and internet addiction in adolescent, demonstrating the interdisciplinary design integrating psychological and neuroimaging methods. The findings suggest that improving family relationships (such as increasing cohesion) may reduce the risk of IGD by modulating MFG function, offering novel insights for neurofeedback interventions targeting adolescent IGD. For example, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) could potentially decrease the risk of IGD. Although the sample consisted of Chinese adolescents, previous research indicates that the psychological and behavioral mechanisms related to IGD exhibit cross-cultural invariance [59], therefore, the results of this study may be generalizable to other populations with similar family cultural backgrounds.

In summary, this study explored the correlation between family relationships and IGD in adolescents, and the results showed that family relationships were related to internet addiction, MFG played an intermediary role in family relationships and internet addiction, and the FC value of MFG to the right insula in IGD group was positively correlated with the conflict. This suggests that the MFG may serve as a neurobiological marker through which family relationships influence IGD development in adolescents.
