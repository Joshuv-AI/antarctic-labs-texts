Whole-genome sequencing to explore genomic profile, pathogenicity, virulence motifs, and pangenome of clinical MRSA isolates
Authors: Abdul Kuddus, Md Rafiul Islam, Anwar Hossain, Kenneth A Bongulto, Kozo Watanabe, Md Abdul Muhit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultidrug-resistant (MDR) and Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) are major opportunistic pathogens, particularly in healthcare settings. Despite the importance of such strains as a priority nosocomial pathogen, genomic data on Bangladeshi isolates remain limited. This study presents whole genome sequence (WGS) analysis of three MDR-MRSA strains (SA-18, SA-36, and SA-41) isolated from wound-infected patients in a tertiary care hospital in Bangladesh. Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) identified SA-18 and SA-36 as sequence type ST6, and SA-41 as ST1930. All isolates carried the mecA gene and SCCmec type IVa were classified as MRSA. A total of 22 antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) were identified, including blaZ, erm(C), norA, and sdrM. Pathogenicity prediction classified all isolates as human pathogens, with SA-41 showing the highest pathogenic potential (98%). Virulence analysis revealed 75 virulence factor genes (VFGs), such as hla, spa, icaA-D, and PVL. Mobile genetic elements (MGEs), plasmids, and an intact 50.9 kb prophage region were detected, with the ebp gene identified within the prophage in SA-41. Pangenome analysis revealed 3764 gene clusters, indicating an open and diverse pangenome. The findings of the present study highlight the importance of WGS in antimicrobial stewardship programs by rapidly identifying resistant genes and spread of multidrug-resistant MRSA strains in Bangladesh which will allow better infection control, rational use of antibiotics and developing effective public health policies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32897-9.Keywords: MRSA, WGS, Virulence factors, ARGs, SCCmec, MGEsSubject terms: Genetics, Microbiology

Introduction

Clinical and epidemiological impact of Staphylococcus aureus is attributed to its potential to induce a wide range of infections, ranging from moderate dermatological conditions to severe life-threatening conditions such as bacteremia, necrotizing pneumonia, and endocarditis1. The growing prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) S. aureus, especially methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), poses a significant threat to global health2. The effects of such resistance and infections on human health and hospitalized environments have prompted extensive research on this organism in recent years3. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) has emerged as one of the powerful tools for understanding the genetic diversity, virulence factors, and antimicrobial resistance mechanisms of S. aureus, thereby aiding in epidemiological surveillance and infection control strategies4.

Globally, infections caused by S. aureus result in high morbidity and mortality. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, S. aureus was responsible for more than 119,000 deaths worldwide in 2019, with significant contributions from low- and middle-income countries5. In Bangladesh, limited studies have been conducted to quantify S. aureus related mortality, but reports indicate an alarming rise in MRSA cases, particularly in hospital settings6. The increasing resistance to commonly used antibiotics, including beta-lactams and glycopeptides, has complicated treatment outcomes and heightened the urgency for molecular epidemiological investigations7.

Notwithstanding the global focus on S. aureus genomics, comprehensive WGS studies of S. aureus strains prevalent in Bangladesh are limited. The majority of current research in Bangladesh has been on phenotypic antibiotic resistance patterns, with minimal genotypic characterizations8. The lack of extensive genomic data restricts our understanding of local strain diversity, antibiotic resistance genes, and transmission patterns, which are essential for efficient infection control strategies.

Given the rising incidence of S. aureus-associated infections and the limited genomic data available in Bangladesh, this study aims to bridge the knowledge gap by conducting whole genome sequencing of S. aureus isolates from hospitalized patients. As a part of our ongoing research on the phenotypic profiling of clinical isolates of S. aureus8 collected from the hospitalized patients, three cefoxitin-resistant MRSA wound isolates (SA-18, SA-36 and SA-41) among 60 clinical samples were chosen for whole-genome sequencing based on their diversified phenotypic profiling and the genomic DNA purity. The genomic study of these samples will elucidate antibiotic resistance mechanisms, virulence factors, and genetic linkages among isolates. This will be crucial in directing public health initiatives and antimicrobial stewardship programs to address the escalating problem of antibiotic-resistant S. aureus infections in Bangladesh and worldwide.

Genome characterization of the isolated MRSA

There were 225, 385, and 79 contigs generated from the genomes of S. aureus SA-18, SA-36, and SA-41 isolates, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). Reference-guided scaffolding was performed using two S. aureus reference genomes (GenBank accession: NC_007795 and NZ_JH691956) to improve assembly contiguity. This approach reduced the number of contigs to 2, 3, and 2 for isolates SA-18, SA-36, and SA-41, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1). Genomic annotation revealed that the taxonomy of all three genomes to be, cellular organisms > Bacteria > Terrabacteria group > Bacillota > Bacilli > Bacillales > Staphylococcaceae > Staphylococcus > Staphylococcus aureus. The isolate SA-18 genome has 2,956 protein-coding sequences (CDS), 44 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and 3 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes and the isolate SA-36 genome has 2,870 CDS, 7 tRNA genes, and 2 rRNA genes. Whereas, the isolate SA-41 genome exhibited 2,691 CDS, 35 tRNA genes, and 3 rRNA genes. The highest number of CDS and hypothetical CDS were detected in S. aureus SA-18. On the other hand, the lowest number of hypothetical CDS was predicted in S. aureus SA-41 (See supplementary Table 1).

Comparative genomic analysis of isolates SA-18, SA-36, and SA-41 revealed an average genome size of approximately 2.87 mega base pairs (Mbp), indicating a compact genomic architecture (see Supplementary Table 1). Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) revealed that strains SA-18 and SA-36 belonged to ST6, whereas SA-41 belonged to ST1930 (Table 3). All the alleles exhibited 100% for both sequence identity and sequence coverage (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Resistome composition and localization

The majority of the ARGs detected across all S. aureus strains were observed in chromosome sequences. Genes coding for efflux pumps include norA, norC, arlR, arlS, mepR, mgrA, sepA, and sdrM genes. A single-nucleotide polymorphism was detected in both parC (S80F) and gyrA (S84L) genes, which resulted in antibiotic target alteration. There were only 2 ARG types detected in plasmid sequences, the beta-lactam resistance gene blaZ and macrolide resistance gene erm(C). The genomic context and distribution of ARGs were depicted within the sequenced S. aureus isolates, revealing 22 different ARGs were detected in all three isolates (Fig. 1). Comparative analysis of the ARGs revealed that sequenced isolates SA-18, SA-36, and SA-41 harbored 20, 16, and 19 ARGs, respectively (Supplementary Table 2). All three isolates shared a conserved set of core ARGs associated mainly with β-lactam, macrolide, and multidrug efflux resistance, while each strain exhibited a small number of unique resistance determinants (Supplementary Table 2). Each of S. aureus SA-18 and SA-41 had 2 exclusive ARGs namely fusC, FosB; & kdpD, GlpT, respectively, which were found to be absent in the other isolate, whereas the isolate SA-36 showed no exclusive ARGs. Strains SA-41 and SA-18 shared 2 ARGs (gyrA, parC), and SA-18 and SA-36 shared only 1 ARG (Erm). The distribution of ARGs among SA-18, SA-36, and SA-41 showed that the ARGs are scattered throughout the chromosome (Fig. 1c–e). Some ARGs are situated adjacent to mobile genetic elements (MGEs), indicating the possibility of genetic mobility and the dissemination of resistance traits. These genomic discoveries enhance the phenotypic antibiogram outcomes, especially for SA-18, which demonstrates the most extensive resistance profile.

Pathogenicity of the bacterial strains and virulence determinants in MRSA isolates

Pathogenicity determination of the three S. aureus strains predicted the isolates to be human pathogens. Among the three isolates, the SA-41 strain was found to be more pathogenic (98%) than the other two strains (Table 4). Virulence gene composition varies across all S. aureus strains. Across the three isolates, 75 virulence factor genes spanning adherence, enzyme production, immune evasion, secretion systems, and toxin families were detected (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Table 3). Numerous fundamental virulence factors such as adhesion-related genes (e.g., clf, ica) and immune evasion genes (e.g., spa, cap) were preserved across all isolates, but toxin-associated genes (including hla, sea) exhibited variability among strains.

The comparative analysis revealed clear distribution patterns among the three isolates. SA-18 harbored the most extensive repertoire with high copy numbers distributed across all five major functional categories: adherence, enzymes, immune evasion, secretion systems, and toxins (Fig. 2a). In contrast, SA-36 displayed a moderately diverse profile with reduced adherence- and secretion-related genes, indicating a more specialized or attenuated pathogenic capability and SA-41 showed the lowest diversity, particularly within toxin and adherence gene classes. These trends indicate meaningful inter-strain variability in virulence potential. Hierarchical clustering of VF genes further supports the presence of functionally co-regulated gene modules. Mobile genetic elements and their cargo genes

A thorough investigation of MGEs was performed in all sequenced isolates of S. aureus and found a total of 211, 200, and 219 MGEs were identified in sequenced isolates of SA-18, SA-36, and SA-41 (Table 5). All three strains of S. aureus exhibited similar characteristics of MGEs except in their numbers of each MGE type. There were no prophages detected in SA-18 and SA-36. However, a 50.9 kb intact prophage region was detected in SA-41. Probing into the prophage region, there were no ARGs detected. Only the ebp gene, which is an adherence-related gene, was detected within the prophage region (Fig. 3). Various genes encoding MGEs were detected across the sequenced S. aureus isolates, and their distribution is presented in (Supplementary Tables 4–8).

Discussion

Staphylococcus aureus is a prominent etiological agent of infections linked with both healthcare settings and the community. The investigation of this pathogen at a molecular level through comparative genome analysis strengthens our understanding of pathogenesis and evolution9. This study represents one of the few comprehensive WGS and genetic characterizations of multidrug- and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) isolates from wound-infected patients in a Bangladeshi clinical setting. By elucidating the genomic architecture of these resistant strains, the findings provide a foundational reference for future research aimed at identifying novel therapeutic targets and developing effective antimicrobial agents to combat this highly virulent pathogen.

Biomedical tests and genome identification through KmerFinder and LINbase revealed that the three isolates are S. aureus. MLST showed that isolates SA-18 and SA-36 belong to ST6, while strain SA-41 belongs to ST1930 type. All alleles had 100% identity and coverage. Isolates were predicted as MRSA SCCmec Type IVa (2B), revealing that mecA gene was detected in all three strains (see Supplementary Fig. 2). Several VFGs and ARGs were detected in all three identified ST types10. As part of the normal flora of healthy humans, these isolates seem to gain an advantage by acquiring a simple and less burdensome version of SCCmec (type IVa) to survive occasional exposure to the antibiotics typically11 prescribed in outpatient clinics, like in this study.

Subsequently, all S. aureus strains in this study were identified as MDR strains since they were resistant to more than two classes of antibiotics. Genome analysis revealed that in SA-18 there were 225 contigs, an estimated genome length of 2.98 Mbp, and an average G + C content of 32.57% which provides data about amino acid and codon usage with their density, and therefore, this may give us a better understanding of the comprehension of bacterial evolution12. The assembled genome of the isolate SA-36 contained 385 contigs, an estimated genome length of 2,836,278 bp, and an average G + C content of 32.55%. The isolate SA-41 genome is comprised of 79 contigs, an estimated genome length of 2,789,449 bp with an average G + C content of 32.69%.

Over the past decade, the prevalence of MRSA has consistently increased. mecA gene confers methicillin resistance13. The epidemiological investigation into MRSA facilitates the identification of risk factors linked to MRSA infections and distinguishes among different MRSA strains14. Molecular epidemiology studies based on genomic data identified that strains SA-18 and SA-36 were classified under ST6, while strain SA-41 was classified under ST1930. Research has shown that MRSA strains with SCCmec types I, II, or III are primarily found in HA-MRSA (Hospital-acquired MRSA), while SCCmec types IV or V are typical of CA-MRSA (Community-acquired MRSA)15,16. In hospitals, multidrug-resistant SCCmec type III was progressively supplanted by multidrug-susceptible SCCmec type IV (ST22) strains17. The distribution of SCCmec types is reported to vary by region worldwide. For instance, SCCmec types I to III are the most frequent nosocomial MRSA strains in the world18. SCCmec type III strains are predominantly found in isolates from Saudi Arabia and various Asian countries, whereas SCCmec type II strains are primarily observed in Japan and Korea, with a notable absence in Saudi Arabia, the Philippines, and other regions. SCCmec type III strains are rare in Saudi Arabia, the Philippines, Korea, and Japan12.

The detection of resistance determinants uncovered a total of 22 resistance genes associated with resistance to antiseptics and different antibiotic classes. The methicillin resistance in the MRSA isolates in this study is contributed by mecA, although the mecA homologue, mecC, was not identified. Although SCCmec IV isolates in this investigation were viable pandemic clones, they had significant susceptibility, demonstrating resistance solely to beta-lactams, fluoroquinolones, and MLSB antibiotics, aligning with other research findings18,19. The blaZ gene encodes staphylococcal β-lactamase, which was carried by transposon Tn552 or Tn552-like elements found on a large plasmid. This enzyme can exhibit either non-inducible or inducible characteristics in response to antibiotics20. It was noted that this marker ARG was investigated in all three MDR & MRSA sequenced isolates.

The crucial step in the development of S. aureus pathogenicity is adhesion to host tissues, a key factor in bacterial virulence21. Bacteria utilize several adherence factors to facilitate adhesion to host cells and extracellular matrix elements22. Among them, crucial VF gene ica, which is harbored by all strains found to be the principal component of biofilm formation in S. aureus23, and play a crucial role in the initial stage of bacterial cell adherence to surfaces and intercellular adhesion for the cells to aggregate21. S. aureus produces the essential coagulase enzyme24, also called staphylococcal coagulase, encoded by coa. These are essentially called coagulase-positive S. aureus and are known to be the most pathogenic species of S. aureus25. This highly pathogenic VFG was found in sequenced isolates of SA-18 and SA-41. By protecting the bacterium from the host immune cells and promoting the development of localized infections and abscesses, coagulase plays a part in immune evasion26. esaB is only found in isolate SA-18. These proteins play a role in the interactions between hosts and pathogens as well as immune evasion27.

Superantigens like hla, that harbored by three strains, can cause the cell membrane to develop tiny pores, which causes the K+ ion to be released quickly. It also causes the production of IL-1, IL-6, and IL-828. Gamma-hemolysin and Panton-Valentine (PV) leukocidin are two of the bicomponent toxins produced by S. aureus29. PVL is a bacteriophage-encoded heterodimeric toxin thought to cause immune cell necrosis by specifically interacting with host membranes29. MRSA strains produce PVL, encoded by two genes, lukS-PV, and lukF-PV, which have been shown to play a role in the pathogenicity of S. aureus by provoking necrosis30. PVL toxin genes LukS-PV and LukS-PV were particularly detected in sequenced isolates of SA-36, whereas gamma hemolysin types hlgA and hlgC were detected in all three sequenced isolates, and hlgB was detected in isolates SA-18 and SA-41. It was found that 97% of S. aureus strains generate delta-hemolysin encoded by Hld50–70. % of staphylococci that lack coagulase are thought to be produced this toxin31.

Pangenome investigation of S. aureus demonstrates a dynamic equilibrium between a conserved core genome, vital for fundamental cellular processes, and a highly changeable accessory genome comprising mobile genetic components, VFs, and ARGs. Research indicates that S. aureus possesses an open pangenome, perpetually assimilating new genes via horizontal gene transfer, especially in antibiotic-resistant (MRSA) and hypervirulent variants32,33. Comparative studies on S. aureus indicated that with the analysis of more strains, the core genome percentage diminishes from roughly 60% to 48%, signifying continuous genetic diversification34,35. Prior investigations corroborate this pattern, since S. aureus pangenome analyses revealed the incorporation of phage-associated genes, host adaptation factors, and resistance determinants36.

MGEs play a central role in shaping the genetic diversity, virulence, and antimicrobial resistance of S. aureus37. In our sequenced isolates, MGEs encoded genes associated with integration, excision, recombination, repair, and replication processes that facilitate genomic rearrangements and horizontal gene transfer (HGT)38,39. Bacteriophages, identified as a major MGE class, are particularly important due to their ability to integrate into the S. aureus genome as prophages, leading to lysogenic conversion and the acquisition of virulence factors such as staphylokinase (Sak), staphylococcal complement inhibitor (SCIN), and chemotaxis inhibitory protein (CHIPS)40. These phages may also carry genes encoding toxins, including enterotoxins and Panton–Valentine leukocidin (PVL), contributing to cytolytic activity37. MGEs such as plasmids and transposons are known to disseminate antimicrobial resistance (AMR) determinants, including mecA, gyrA, and gyrB, which provide significant survival advantages under antibiotic pressure41. Genes involved in DNA repair and excision, such as uvrA, uvrB, and pcrA, maintain genomic integrity by removing damaged DNA segments42, while recombination-related genes (sbcC, sbcD) contribute to capsule regulation, another key virulence trait43. Defense-related MGEs, such as hsdR, may also function as restriction-modification systems, limiting foreign DNA entry and supporting bacterial persistence in hostile environments44. Overall, the diversity and functional repertoire of MGEs in S. aureus underscores their evolutionary significance in pathogenicity, adaptability, and antibiotic resistance.

Conclusion

This study presents one of the first comprehensive whole-genome sequencing investigations of multidrug-resistant methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MDR-MRSA) clinical isolates from Bangladesh. The three sequenced strains (SA-18, SA-36, SA-41) possessed numerous antibiotic resistance genes, various virulence factors including coagulase positive strains, and a broad spectrum of mobile genetic elements, underscoring their pathogenic potential and adaptability. The existence of SCCmec type IVa in all isolates, along with a broad and varied pangenome, highlights their capacity to acquire and propagate resistance and virulence characteristics. Considering the increasing prevalence of MDR-MRSA in Bangladesh, it is recommended to strengthen genomic surveillance of S. aureus to monitor the emergence and dissemination of high-risk clones. This should include the integration of WGS-based pathogen tracking into hospital infection control programs for the early detection of outbreaks and primary detection tool to detect resistance. Additionally, implementing rational antimicrobial stewardship is essential to reduce selective pressure and slow the spread of resistance. Continued research into mobile genetic elements is also necessary to identify potential targets for novel therapeutics or preventive strategies. However, the findings of this study are based on only three isolates derived from wound infections from a single hospital in Dhaka city, and therefore careful interpretation regarding their nationwide transmission patterns are required. Therefore, the conclusions drawn here may reflect only the genomic features of the MRSA present in this specific study site and time period.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

In the previous study, we reported the cultural, biochemical, and molecular characterization of sixty clinical S. aureus isolates (61% from wound, 34% from urine, and 5% from other sources). These isolates were obtained from the Department of Microbiology and Immunology Laboratory at Bangabandhu Sheikh Mujib Medical University, Bangladesh, during the period from June 2021 to September 2021 which has been described earlier in the previous published report8. The present study focuses on the whole genome sequencing of three MRSA strains, namely SA-18, SA-36, and SA-41 (S. aureus). In this study, three MDR isolates from wound sources were selected, which were initially part of the sixty 60 samples study. The stock cultures of bacteria from earlier work were then stored at 4 °C for future use. Ten different antibiotics, Ciprofloxacin (CIP) (5 mg), Vancomycin (VA) (30 mg), Chloramphenicol (C) (30 mg), Linezolid (LNZ) (10 mg), Tetracycline (TE) (30 mg), Azithromycin (AZM) (15 mg), Cefixime (CFM) (5 mg), Kanamycin (K) (30 mg), Imipenem (IPM) (10 mg), Amoxicillin (AMX) (30 mg) were used to determine the antibiograms of the S. aureus strains (Table 1). MRSA was confirmed phenotypically through the cefoxitin (CEF) susceptibility test in accordance with CLSI guidelines (M100, 30th Ed. 2020), as resistance to cefoxitin serves as a confirmatory indicator for MRSA. All three isolates (SA-18, SA-36, SA-41) exhibited phenotypic resistance to cefoxitin and were consequently classified as MRSA prior to whole-genome sequencing. The results of the antibiotic susceptibility tests, along with the sources of the clinical samples and the justification for multidrug resistance (MDR) based on biochemical characterization, are presented in (Tables 1 and 2).

Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) and Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB) (TM media Ltd., India) were used to isolate and purify the S. aureus strains. The bacterial isolates were revived from the stock culture of the laboratory on MHA. For broth preparation, 21 g of MHB powder was dissolved in 1 L of distilled water, mixed thoroughly, and sterilized by autoclaving at 120 °C for 15 min, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Five microliters of broth was poured into each falcon tube and sterilized in the autoclave. Distinguished colonies from each of the isolates were inoculated into MHB and incubated for about 6 h by transferring suitable amount (500µL) of inoculated samples in falcon tube. After that, the clinical isolates were aseptically inoculated into the tubes. The falcon tubes with samples were then shaken at 180 rpm in a shaker incubator at 37 °C for 20 h to facilitate bacterial growth. 500 µL of each sample were then taken out and centrifuged in a centrifuged machine using an appropriate Eppendorf tube on two separate occasions. White pellets formed at the base of centrifuged tubes, which were thereafter sealed meticulously with parafilm and kept.

DNA isolation, quality assessment, and sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from the bacterial suspension using Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kits (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. The extracted DNA’s purity and concentration were assessed employing a NanoDropTM spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA). The purified DNA was preserved at -20 ℃ for further treatment (Table 6).

WGS of the three isolates was conducted under the Illumina platform using the NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system at the Genome Research Laboratory, Bangladesh Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (BCSIR). The generated FASTQ files underwent quality assessment using FastQC (v0.11)45. Additionally, the adapter sequences and low-quality ends for each read were trimmed employing Trimmomatic (v0.39)46, applying a criterion that required a minimum average quality score of 20 and a minimum read length of 50 bp (Table 7). Following the trimming process, the high-quality reads underwent de novo assembly utilizing SPAdes v3.15.4 (Species Prediction and Diversity Estimation)47 employing a k-mer range from 21 to 121. During the assembly process, the contigs for each bacterial sample underwent screening, leading to the removal of those contigs that were less than 500 bps, had low coverage scores, and/or showed weak association with the S. aureus species. The draft contigs undergo recording, mapping, and scaffolding according to NCBI reference complete genome sequences of S. aureus strains NCTC 8325 (Accession no. NC_007795.1) and LCT-SA112 (Accession no. NZ_JH691956.1) by multi-CSAR, utilizing multi-CSAR, a web server designed for scaffolding contigs with multiple reference genomes48. The scaffold’s completeness was assessed utilizing CheckM49. Scaffolded contigs were searched for the identification of the isolates by Nucleotide BLAST (BLASTn)50 and the KmerFinder tool of the Center for Genomic Epidemiology51. SCCmec Finder tool of the Center for Genomic Epidemiology, employed to detect Staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec) elements in sequenced S. aureus isolates.

Genome annotation, genomic organization mapping, and In-silico characterization of genome assemblies

The assembled sequences for the three isolates were annotated using Prokka52 and Patric server53. In-silico epidemiologic specifications of genome assemblies were conducted using SCCmecFinder-1.2 for the identification of SCCmec types54, MLST 1.8 for Multilocus Sequence Type55. The graphical maps of the circular genomes were generated through the Proksee server (previously CGviewer)56 which is an expert system for genome assembly, annotation, and visualization. Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) were investigated through ResFinder tool57,58 of Center for Genomic Epidemiology. The obtained result was further verified by analyzing the ARGs through CARD (Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database)59 and PATRIC53 tools.

Investigation of pathogenicity and virulence factor genes (VFGs)

The pathogenicity prediction of the bacterial isolate towards humans was performed using the PathogenFinder (v1.1) web tool developed by the Center for Genomic Epidemiology60. This tool uses a deep neural model which is an ensemble of 4 neural networks, hence the prediction of the model is the mean of the 4 predictions. The mean of these predictions determines the capacity of human pathogenicity from the queried bacterial genome. The virulence factor genes (VFGs) were investigated using Victors, a novel, manually curated, web-based integrative knowledge base and analysis resource for VFs of pathogens responsible for infectious diseases in humans and animals61. Afterwards, the VFGs were also investigated with PATRIC_VF53 and VFDB (Virulence Factor Database)62.

Mobile genetic elements (MGE) and associated genomic features in sequenced strains

MGEs are DNA segments that code for enzymes and other proteins that facilitate the transfer of DNA inside genomes or between bacterial cells. Mobile genetic elements (MGEs) from the complete genomes of the isolates were analyzed using the MGEFinder tool63 tool and mobileOG-db. After the MGEs investigation, integrons were identified from the sequences utilizing the IntegronFinder64 tool of the Center for Genomic Epidemiology. Phage integration within the bacterial genomes was investigated through PHASTEST (PHAge Search Tool with Enhanced Sequence Translation)65 webserver, VirSorter66, Prophage Hunter67 and Phigaro68 tool.

Pan-genome profiling of sequenced isolates

The genome sequences underwent re-annotation via Prokka v1.14.652, and with GFF3 files serving as input for pan-genome analysis conducted using Roary v3.13.069 and BPGA70.
