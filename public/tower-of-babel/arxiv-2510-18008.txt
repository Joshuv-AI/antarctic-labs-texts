Abstract
We consider the problem of non-coherent over-the-air computation (AirComp), where n n devices carry high-dimensional data vectors 𝐱 i ∈ ℝ d \mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} of sparsity ∥ 𝐱 i ∥ 0 ≤ k \lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}\rVert_{0}\leq k whose sum has to be computed at a receiver. Previous results on non-coherent AirComp require more than d d channel uses to compute functions of 𝐱 i \mathbf{x}_{i} , where the extra redundancy is used to combat non-coherent signal aggregation. However, if the data vectors are sparse, sparsity can be exploited to offer significantly cheaper communication. In this paper, we propose to use random transforms to transmit lower-dimensional projections 𝐬 i ∈ ℝ T \mathbf{s}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T} of the data vectors. These projected vectors are communicated to the receiver using a majority vote (MV)-AirComp scheme, which estimates the bit-vector corresponding to the signs of the aggregated projections, i.e., 𝐲 = sign ​ ( ∑ i 𝐬 i ) \mathbf{y}=\text{sign}(\sum_{i}\mathbf{s}_{i}) . By leveraging 1-bit compressed sensing (1bCS) at the receiver, the real-valued and high-dimensional aggregate ∑ i 𝐱 i \sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i} can be recovered from 𝐲 \mathbf{y} . We prove analytically that the proposed MVCS scheme estimates the aggregated data vector ∑ i 𝐱 i \sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i} with ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -norm error ϵ \epsilon in T = 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ​ n ​ log ⁡ ( d ) / ϵ 2 ) T=\mathcal{O}(kn\log(d)/\epsilon^{2}) channel uses. Moreover, we specify algorithms that leverage MVCS for histogram estimation and distributed machine learning. Finally, we provide numerical evaluations that reveal the advantage of MVCS compared to the state-of-the-art.

I
Introduction

AirComp
(
AirComp
) is a wireless communication method that leverages signal superposition to communicate mathematical functions. Compared to current communication systems,
AirComp
is poised to reduce latency or spectrum use by a factor proportional to the number of simultaneously transmitting devices. Therefore,
AirComp
has the potential to offer cheap and efficient collection of distributed data in dense wireless networks. Wireless data collection has numerous current and upcoming applications, such as federated analytics, sensor networks, and distributed control systems
[
1
]
. This is also increasingly relevant for distributed machine learning, as the growing size of
ML
(
ML
) models necessitates training across many servers
[
2
]
. However, while
AirComp
is a promising technology for these applications, it is also associated with significant technical challenges that, so far, make it difficult to implement it in practice.

One of the main challenges of
AirComp
is the lack of reliable error correction. Consider that
n
devices each carry a message
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\forall i\in\{1,...,n\}
and that we are interested in recreating some function
f(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{n})
of these messages at an
AP
(
AP
). With traditional orthogonal communication, these
n
messages can be communicated almost perfectly to the
AP
by leveraging forward error correcting codes and retransmissions triggered by cyclic redundancy check. The desired function
f(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{n})
can then be computed at the
AP
with very high levels of reliability. However, in
AirComp
, instead of transmitting these messages separately, all
n
devices transmit over the same radio resources, and the superimposed signal is used to estimate
f(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{n})
. Since the
AP
never sees individual signals from the devices, error correction and detection are challenging
[
3
]
. In the current state-of-the-art, an
AirComp
receiver does not know if the function is computed correctly. Instead, such systems generally provide estimates
\hat{f}
of the desired function, with some residual function estimation error
\epsilon=\lVert\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{n})-f(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{n})\rVert_{2}
.

For a system designer considering
AirComp
, the estimation error
\epsilon
can be viewed as a price to pay for the promised latency reduction and spectral efficiency gain. Ideally, this should not be a binary choice, but
AirComp
should be tunable to trade off communication efficiency and
\epsilon
, similar to the role of the code rate in digital communications.

To achieve such a tradeoff several authors have considered the use of
CS
(
CS
)
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. The basic idea of compressed sensing is to project the high-dimensional messages
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to lower-dimensional vectors
\mathbf{s}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
by applying a linear transform
\mathbf{s}_{i}\triangleq\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}_{i}
at the user devices before transmission. Given that the messages
\mathbf{x}_{i}
satisfy sparsity constraints
\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{0}\leq k,\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and the matrix
\mathbf{M}
fulfills the restricted isometry property
[
7
]
, the messages
\mathbf{x}_{i}
can be exactly recreated from
\mathbf{s}_{i}
at the receiver. In the context of
AirComp
, the receiver will not have access to the individual vectors
\mathbf{s}_{i}
but rather a noisy estimate of
f(\mathbf{s}_{1},...,\mathbf{s}_{n})
. Still, the desired function
f(\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{n})
can be recreated from
\hat{f}(\mathbf{s}_{1},...,\mathbf{s}_{n})
, with an estimation error that depends on the dimension
T

[
6
]
. As such, the parameter
T
can be tuned to provide the desired tradeoff.

While
CS
has gotten prior attention in the context of
AirComp
, the state-of-the-art papers make significant simplifications in the channel model, either by limiting the distortions to
AWGN
(
AWGN
)
[
4
]
or by assuming perfect channel inversion at the transmitter-side
[
8
,
5
,
6
]
. Both of these simplifications ignore the need for transmitter side phase correction, which is known to be challenging in
practice, since it requires synchronization of the devices’ oscillators
[
9
,
10
,
3
]
. In this paper, we bridge this gap by developing
CS
-based
AirComp
that does not require phase correction at the transmitters. Our main idea is to combine the recently proposed
MV
(
MV
)
AirComp

[
10
]
scheme with
1bCS
(
1bCS
)
[
11
]
.

In
MV
-
AirComp
, the
n
vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are communicated in
2d
orthogonal radio resources, with the goal to compute the majority vote, defined as
\mathbf{f}=\text{sign}(\sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i})
. For each element
x_{i}[t]
(where
t\in\{1,...,d\}
) of the vector, the devices decide to communicate in one of two orthogonal resources, depending on the sign of
x_{i}[t]
. By comparing the received power over the two resources, the
AP
can determine the majority vote, effectively collecting one bit of information from
n
devices. This way, by leveraging
2d
orthogonal radio resources, all
n
devices can communicate
d
bits of information, leading to a spectral efficiency of
n/2
bits per channel use
1
1

1

The outcome of the majority vote only consists of
d
bits of information, but
nd
bits are used to compute it.
. However, noise in the wireless channel can result in bit errors with
MV
-
AirComp
, which in turn can lead to large estimation error for
\mathbf{f}
. To combat these errors, we employ
1bCS
.
1bCS
is a dimensionality reduction method that recreates a real-valued vector
\mathbf{x}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, using a compressed bit-vector
\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{T}
, where
T<d

[
11
]
. It allows us to reduce the number of channel uses needed significantly below
d
in the regime where
n\ll d
and
k\ll d
. This is the regime of interest for many federated learning and analytics applications. As we discuss later in Section
II
,
1bCS
is robust to errors under basic assumptions. The combination of these ideas allows the proposed
MVCS
(
MVCS
) scheme to efficiently and reliably compute real-valued functions without posing any demands on phase synchronization.

The contributions of our paper can be summarized as follows:

•

We propose, as far as we are aware, the first non-coherent
AirComp
scheme with compressed sensing.

•

We prove that
MVCS
can achieve any desired estimation error
\epsilon
in
T=\mathcal{O}\left(kn\log(d)/\epsilon^{2}\right)
channel uses, where
d
is the dimension of the desired function
\sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\lVert\sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\rVert_{0}\leq kn
.

•

We demonstrate that
MVCS
can be leveraged for private histogram estimation, without having access to individual user item.

•

We provide numerical results for training deep neural networks on the MNIST problem over wireless channels that incorporate path loss, fading, and
AWGN
. The numerical results suggest that
MVCS
can outperform state-of-the-art schemes in distributed machine learning.

Background

II-A

1-bit Compressed Sensing

In the original
1bCS
framework, the goal is to estimate a high-dimensional sparse target vector
\mathbf{x}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
using lower-dimensional and quantized 1-bit measurements of
\mathbf{x}_{*}
. In particular, the measurement process is modeled as a matrix-vector multiplication
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}_{*}
, where
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
is called the measurement matrix
2
2

2

In compressed sensing (not the 1-bit version), exact target reconstruction is possible given that the measurement matrix satisfies the restricted isometry property, which holds for many random matrix ensembles
[
12
]
. However,
1bCS
is more restrictive, since certain discrete distributions, such as Bernoulli matrices, will yield indistinguishable measurements
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
for distinct pairs of target vectors
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
. Even so, there exists some random matrix constructions, such as the gaussian matrix, that yield exact reconstruction in
1bCS
with high probability
[
13
]
.
. A common choice is to let the elements of
\mathbf{M}
be i.i.d. standard Gaussian
RV
. After compression, the measurement
\mathbf{s}
is quantized down to a single bit of information per element
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
as

b[t]=\sign\left(s[t]\right)\triangleq\begin{cases}+1,&\text{if }s[t]\geq 0\\
-1,&\text{if }s[t]<0,\end{cases}

(1)

where the choice for the tie-breaker of
s[t]=0
is arbitrary. Such a measure yields two types of compression. Firstly, a reduction in dimension since
\mathbf{M}
can be constructed such that
T\ll d
and secondly a quantization gain down to 1 bit per element. However, note that the quantized measure
\mathbf{b}
does not preserve any information about the magnitude of
\mathbf{s}
. As such, it is not possible to recreate the norm of
\mathbf{x}_{*}
from
\mathbf{b}
but only the relative magnitudes of its elements. Therefore, it is common practice to assume that
\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}=1
and force the estimate
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be a unit
\ell_{2}
-norm vector as well. Given this setup, the goal of
1bCS
is to develop an algorithm
\mathcal{A}\left(\mathbf{b};\mathbf{M}\right)\rightarrow\hat{\mathbf{x}}
that can guarantee

\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\rVert_{2}\leq\epsilon

(2)

with some probability
p
. There are many published algorithms of this sort
[
11
,
14
,
15
,
13
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
, with variants in the system model, reconstruction performance, and/or computational complexity. The extension of the system model to noisy measurements is of particular importance to our work
[
13
,
16
,
17
,
20
]
. A simple way to model noisy measurements is to assign a random probability that each bit is flipped in (
1
) but there is also a more general model introduced in
[
13
]
. In particular, they consider that the quantized bits are drawn independently at random and that their expected value is given by

\theta\left(\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle\right)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[b[t]|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right],

(3)

where
\mathbf{m}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
is row
t
of the measurement matrix,
\langle.,.\rangle
is the inner product, and
\theta
is some function. A key result in
1bCS
is that this function
\theta
does not need to be known to estimate
\mathbf{x}_{*}

[
13
]
. Given that
\mathbf{M}
is a random Gaussian matrix, the only required assumptions on
\theta
are that
-1\leq\theta(\cdot)\leq 1
and

\mathbb{E}\left[\theta(g)g\right]\triangleq\lambda_{g}>0,

(4)

where
g
is a standard Gaussian variable. This assumption ensures that the 1-bit measurements
\mathbf{b}
are positively correlated with the measurements
\mathbf{s}
. Generally, the error
\epsilon
in (
2
) is inversely proportional to
\lambda_{g}
, indicating that a higher correlation yields better reconstruction results.

Note that (
4
) comes from the compressed sensing literature, and not from the
AirComp
literature. In such setups, there is only one original vector
\mathbf{x}_{*}
. Therefore,
[
13
]
can guarantee that
\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle
is standard Gaussian by normalizing
\mathbf{x}_{*}
to have unit norm. In the
AirComp
case, we are interested in recreating
\mathbf{f}\coloneq\sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}
. We can select the norm of each
\mathbf{x}_{i}
by performing pre-processing at the sensor devices, but
\lVert\sum_{i}\mathbf{f}\rVert
cannot be controlled. Therefore, in this paper, we define
\lambda
as follows.

\mathbb{E}\left[\theta(\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{f}\rangle)\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{f}\rangle\right]\triangleq\lambda.

(5)

The
1bCS
method still works under the same requirement on
\theta
;
\lambda
must be greater than 0.

II-B

Passive Algorithm

Most
1bCS
algorithms are iterative algorithms based on linear programming
[
15
]
, convex programming
[
13
]
, first-order algorithms
[
20
]
, matching pursuit
[
14
]
, or variants of gradient descent
[
11
,
17
,
18
]
. However, in
[
16
]
, the authors proposed a passive algorithm that estimates
\mathbf{x}_{*}
in one shot with a closed-form solution. In addition to being computationally efficient, the passive algorithm has the advantage that it does not impose an exact sparsity
\lVert\hat{\mathbf{x}}\rVert_{0}
. This is important in our application because the receiver only knows an upper bound on the sparsity of the target, while the exact value of
\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{0}
is unknown. For a complete description of the passive algorithm, we refer to
[
16
]
, but we also give a brief exposition here for completeness and to unify notation.

The passive algorithm leverages the soft-thresholding operator, defined as follows.

Definition 1

(Soft-Thresholding Operator)
.

The soft-thresholding operator with argument
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and threshold
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is defined as

P_{\gamma}(\alpha)\triangleq\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }|\alpha|\leq\gamma\\
\sign\left(\alpha\right)\left(|\alpha|-\gamma\right),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(6)

where
\sign(\cdot)
was defined in (
1
). We extend the operator to vector-valued arguments
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

P_{\gamma}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\triangleq\left[P_{\gamma}(\alpha[1]),\,P_{\gamma}(\alpha[2]),\,\dots,\,P_{\gamma}(\alpha[d])\right]^{H},

(7)

where
(\cdot)^{H}
is the transpose, i.e.,
P_{\gamma}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})
is a column vector.

Given this definition, the closed-form estimate is computed as

\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{0},&\text{if }\lVert\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{M}^{H}\mathbf{b}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\gamma,\\
\frac{1}{\lVert P_{\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{M}^{H}\mathbf{b}\right)\rVert_{2}}P_{\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{M}^{H}\mathbf{b}\right)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(8)

where
\mathbf{M}
is the measurement matrix.
With this algorithm, the probability of estimating
\mathbf{x}
within a given error margin
\epsilon
increases exponentially in
T
, as specified in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Assume

\gamma=2c\sqrt{\frac{\delta+\log(d)}{T}}

(9)

for some constant
c
. Consider
\delta>0
to be a hyperparameter that can be chosen freely. Additionaly, consider that the noise process defined in (
3
) satisfies
\lambda_{g}>0
. Let
\mathbf{x}_{*}
be sparse and
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
be selected according to (
8
). Then, with a probability of at least
1-e^{1-\delta}
, we have

\lVert\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}\leq\epsilon\triangleq\frac{3\gamma}{\lambda_{g}}\sqrt{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{0}}.

(10)

Proof.

See
[
16
]
.
∎

In other words, in order to guarantee that our estimate
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
is within an
\ell_{2}
-norm ball of radius
\epsilon
to the target vector, we need the measurement’s dimension
T
to be in the order of

T=O\left(\frac{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{0}}{(\lambda_{g}\epsilon)^{2}}\left(\log(d)+\delta\right)\right).

(11)

Remark 1
.

Recently,
[
20
]
proposed a new
1bCS
algorithm that provides a similar guarantee for
T=O\left(\frac{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{0}}{\epsilon}\log(d)\right)
, a factor
\epsilon
improvement compared to
[
16
]
’s algorithm. However,
[
20
]
has two downsides compared to
[
16
]
. Firstly, the computational cost is significantly higher, since many iterations can be required to converge. Secondly, in every iteration, the algorithm forces the vector to have an exact sparsity, which might be unknown at the receiver. If the computational capability at the receiver is sufficient and
\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{0}
is known,
[
20
]
’s algorithm can be used in place of the passive algorithm without major changes to
MVCS
.

II-C

Over-the-Air Computation

The core idea of
AirComp
is to leverage the electromagnetic superposition of simultaneously transmitted signals to compute a function
[
9
]
. This superposition is generally modeled using the
W-MAC
(
W-MAC
)
[
9
]
, which is defined as follows.

Definition 2

(W-MAC)
.

For any channel use
t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, the
W-MAC
is a map from
\mathbb{C}^{n}
to
\mathbb{C}
defined to be

\left(a_{1}[t],\ldots,a_{n}[t]\right)\rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}h_{i}[t]a_{i}[t]+z[t]\triangleq y[t],

(12)

where
l_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes large-scale fading coefficients,
h_{i}[t]\in\mathbb{C}
denotes small-scale fading coefficients,
a_{i}[t]\in\mathbb{C}
denotes the transmitted I/Q symbols, and
z[t]\in\mathbb{C}
denotes
AWGN
. When applicable,
t
is omitted for brevity.

From Definition
2
it is clear that the
W-MAC
naturally computes a sum of the transmitted messages. However, due to the phase rotations
\angle h_{i}[t]
, the superposition can be destructive. Moreover, there is an issue of heterogeneous signal attenuation, which adds real-valued weights
l_{i}|h_{i}[t]|
to the message amplitudes. As wireless researchers, we are used to dealing with these types of distortions, but compared to orthogonal communication schemes, it is significantly more challenging to compensate for the attenuation and phase rotation in
AirComp
. In particular:

1.

Channel Estimation
: It is not straightforward to attach a preamble to an
AirComp
message for channel estimation. Consider that a known preamble is attached to the beginning of each message
a_{i}
. When the receiver samples
y[t]
from (
12
), the non-coherent addition causes the received pilot to differ significantly from the transmitted ones, making the channel estimation problem challenging. Instead, orthogonal resources could periodically be allocated to estimate channels, but then the communication efficiency benefits of
AirComp
are substantially reduced
[
21
]
.

2.

Phase and Frequency tracking:
To avoid phase rotations of the received symbols, it is standard practice to compensate for frequency and phase offsets by aligning the receiver’s local oscillator with the incoming signal. In schemes such as IEEE 802.11a, this is achieved by sending dedicated pilot tones that act as a reference for a phase-locked loop at the receiver
[
22
, Section 27.3.11.10]
. However, in
AirComp
the signals are superimposed, so the receiver’s local oscillator cannot compensate for the non-coherent superposition of the
n
waves at the passband. Instead, coherent superposition requires that the
LO
of all
n
transmitters are synchronized. To put this into perspective, if the carrier waves are in the GHz range, the
n
transmitters need to be synchronized well within the carrier period
T_{c}=1/f_{c}\leq 1\text{ns}
. The 5G standard, at best, guarantees
260\text{ns}
time synchronization
[
23
]
.

For these reasons, there is a significant number of articles on non-coherent
AirComp
schemes
[
9
,
24
,
25
,
10
,
26
]
. Some schemes consider that the magnitude of the channel information is available, i.e., the devices can correct for
l_{i}|h_{i}[t]|
but are unable to synchronize phase
[
9
,
24
,
25
]
. Others operate with even more stringent assumptions, where neither magnitude nor phase can be corrected at the devices
[
10
,
26
]
. The system proposed in this paper assumes no device-side knowledge of
|h_{i}[t]|
nor ability to synchronize phase. However, we assume that large-scale fading
l_{i}
is known by the network, to facilitate power control.

III-A

System Model and Assumptions

Consider a wireless network with
n
user devices, each carrying a message
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}\rVert_{0}\leq k
and of unit
\ell_{2}
-norm
\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}=1
3
3

3

Both these assumptions can be made true by applying top-

. The goal of the network is to recover the aggregate
\mathbf{f}\triangleq\sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}
at an
AP
, by communicating over the

. To enable

, we consider that every device (including the

) share a common matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
. The matrix is generated by independent sampling from a standard Gaussian distribution. Once sampled,
\mathbf{M}
remains static. This matrix can be large but, in practice, it can be generated on all devices independently as long as they agree on a random seed.

The devices communicate I/Q symbols
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{2T}
over the

, where power control is performed to satisfy the average power constraint

\mathbb{E}_{a_{i}[t]}\left[|a_{i}[t]|^{2}|x_{i}[t]\right]\leq\overline{P},\,\forall\,i

(13)

where
\overline{P}
is common to all devices. Throughout the paper, all expectations on the transmitted symbols

are conditioned on
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, since we do not wish to impose any known distribution on the transmitted messages. We may omit the explicit expectation for brevity.

We model the large-scale fading as

l_{i}=r_{i}^{-\frac{\beta}{2}},

(14)

where
r_{i}
is the distance from the
AP
to
UE
(
UE
)
i
and
\beta
is the path loss exponent. We assume that the large-scale fading remains constant for the duration of at least
2T
transmissions and that all
l_{i}
are known by the
AP
. For the fast-fading channel coefficients, we consider Rayleigh fading, i.e.,
h_{i}[t]\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{h}^{2}\right)
for all
t
. Further, we assume independence between devices
\mathbb{E}[h_{i}[t]h_{j}[t]]=0\,\forall i\neq j
and channel uses
\mathbb{E}[h_{i}[t]h_{i}[s]]=0\,\forall t\neq s
, and we consider that there is no
CSI
(
CSI
) available.

III-B

Transmitter Baseband

Before transmission begins, all devices generate a measure
\mathbf{s}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{i}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}_{i}.

(15)

To transmit these measures, the
AP
allocates
2T

n
devices, which yields two channel uses for each element of
\mathbf{s}_{i}
. These two channel uses are leveraged to communicate negative and positive measures, respectively. Device
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
will only activate one of the two resources for each index
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, depending on the sign of
s_{i}[t]
. This way, positive and negative values are encoded in the power of their respective radio resources, rather than in the phase of the IQ symbols
[
10
]
, which is usually how it is implemented in coherent over-the-air computation. We define that channel uses
\{1,\ldots,T\}
and
\{T+1,\ldots,2T\}
are used to communicate negative and positive values, respectively. For index
t
, device
i
will transmit

\displaystyle a_{i}[t]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{i}|s_{i}[t]|}u\left(-s_{i}[t]\right),

(16)

\displaystyle a_{i}[t+T]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{i}|s_{i}[t]|}u\left(s_{i}[t]\right),

(17)

where
u(\cdot)
is the Heaviside step function and
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is a power control factor. The power control factor is used to align the received power of the devices and to satisfy the power constraint (
13
). The selection of
p_{i}
is outlined in the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Let the measurement matrix
\mathbf{M}
be a standard Gaussian matrix, let
l_{\min}\triangleq\min_{i}l_{i}
, and let
\mathbf{x}_{i}
be a
k
-sparse vector with unit
\ell_{2}
-norm. Then, the power control factor

p_{i}=\sqrt{2\pi}\frac{\overline{P}l_{\min}^{2}}{l_{i}^{2}}

(18)

satisfies the average power constraint

\mathbb{E}_{a_{i}}\left[|a_{i}[t]|^{2}\right]\leq\overline{P},\,\forall\,i,

(19)

and aligns the received powers as

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[|l_{i}h_{i}[t]a_{i}[t]|^{2}\right]=\\
&\mathbb{E}_{h_{i}[t],s_{i}[t]}\left[|l_{i}h_{i}[t]\sqrt{p_{i}s_{i}[t]}u(s_{i}[t])|^{2}\right]=\overline{P}\sigma_{h}^{2}l_{\min}^{2}\,\forall\,i.\end{split}

(20)

Proof.

The average transmit power is given by

\mathbb{E}_{a_{i}}\left[|a_{i}[t]|^{2}\right]=p_{i}\mathbb{E}_{s_{i}[t]}\left[|s_{i}[t]|u(s_{i}[t])\right]\underset{(a)}{=}\frac{p_{i}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{s_{i}}\left[|s_{i}[t]|\right],

(21)

where
(a)
holds since
\mathbb{E}_{s_{i}}[u(s_{i}[t])]=1/2
(
s_{i}[t]
is symmetric around zero) and
|s_{i}[t]|
is uncorrelated to
u(s_{i}[t])
. Without loss of generality, consider that the
k
non-zero elements of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
are the first
k
elements
x_{i}[j]
where
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. The measure
\mathbf{s}_{i}
can then be expressed as

\mathbf{s}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{k}m_{1}[j]x_{i}[j]\\
\sum_{j=1}^{k}m_{2}[j]x_{i}[j]\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j=1}^{k}m_{T}[j]x_{i}[j]\\
\end{pmatrix},

(22)

where
m_{t}[j]
is element
j
of row
t
of
\mathbf{M}
. Since the elements of
\mathbf{M}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian
RV
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[|s_{i}[t]|\right]&=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}m_{t}[j]x_{i}[j]\right|\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|G\right|\right]\\
&\underset{(a)}{=}\frac{\sqrt{2}\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\underset{(b)}{=}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}},\end{split}

(23)

where
G
is a zero-mean Gaussian
RV
with variance
\sigma_{g}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Equality
(a)
holds because
|G|
follows the half-normal distribution and equality
(b)
holds since we assume that all messages
\mathbf{x}_{i}
have unit
\ell_{2}
-norms. Combining (
23
) with (
21
) and (
18
) yields

\mathbb{E}_{a_{i}}\left[|a_{i}[t]|^{2}\right]=\frac{\overline{P}l_{\min}^{2}}{l_{i}^{2}}\underset{(a)}{\leq}\overline{P}.

(24)

Note that power constraint
(a)
in (
24
) is only active for the device furthest from the
AP
and that all other devices will on average communicate with less power to achieve alignment. The equality
\mathbb{E}\left[|l_{i}h_{i}[t]a_{i}[t]|^{2}\right]=\overline{P}\sigma_{h}^{2}l_{\min}^{2}
follows by combining (
24
) with the left-hand side of (
20
).
∎

Theorem 2
.

Consider that the user devices transmit
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{2T}
based on (

) and (

) with the power control scheme from Proposition
1
. Further consider that the receiver samples
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{2T}

according to (

\theta\left(\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{f}\rangle\right)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[b[t]|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right],

(27)

where
\mathbf{f}\triangleq\sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}

\theta(\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{f}\rangle)=\frac{E^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{f}\rangle}{E^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{i}\rangle\right|+2\sigma_{z}^{2}},\,

(28)

(29)

\sigma_{z}^{2}
is the variance of the

Proof.

We begin by noting that, given
\mathbf{M}

\begin{split}\sigma_{+}^{2}&=E^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}\sum_{i}\left|\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{i}\rangle\right|u\left(\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{i}\rangle\right)+\sigma_{z}^{2}\,\,\,\text{and}\\
\sigma_{-}^{2}&=E^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}\sum_{i}\left|\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{i}\rangle\right|u\left(-\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{i}\rangle\right)+\sigma_{z}^{2}\end{split}

(30)

respectively. Their powers

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[b[t]|\mathbf{m}_{t}\right]&=\Pr\left(D\geq 0\right)-\Pr\left(D<0\right)=1-2F_{D}(0)\\
&=\frac{\sigma_{+}^{2}-\sigma_{-}^{2}}{\sigma_{+}^{2}+\sigma_{-}^{2}}=\frac{E^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{f}\rangle}{E^{2}\sigma_{h}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{i}\rangle\right|+2\sigma_{z}^{2}}.\end{split}

\mathbb{E}\left[\theta(\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{f}\rangle)\langle\mathbf{m}_{t},\mathbf{f}\rangle\right]>0

∎

Consider the communication scheme where the user devices transmit

local iterations in each communication round and by communicating model updates rather than gradients. If
E=1
, the model corresponds to distributed
SGD
, and if
E>1
it corresponds to
FedAvg
.

The considered network setup is the same as in Section
II
, i.e.,
n
devices are communicating with one
AP
over the W-MAC from Definition
2
with Rayleigh fast-fading and large-scale fading according to (
83
) and (
14
). We maintain the communication and power control schemes proposed in Section
III
but add pre- and post-processing steps that frame the communication problem within the
ML
application.

The goal of the
ML
application is to converge on a machine learning model, parameterized by
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that minimizes some loss function
l(\cdot)
. To find
\mathbf{w}
, there is a nested iterative process that finds increasingly better model parameters until the system either runs out of resources or decides that the model has a sufficiently small loss. In this process, the
AP
has the role of coordinating the devices, who are the real work-horses in the training process. The devices are both responsible for running the optimization algorithms, and they are providing the datasets
\mathcal{D}_{i}
that are used to train
\mathbf{w}
.

Before training begins, the
AP
decides the structure of the
ML
model, selects the learning rate
\mu
, and randomly initiates the model parameters
\mathbf{w}^{(0)}
. Communication round
l
is initiated by the
AP
, which broadcasts the model parameters
\mathbf{w}^{(l)}
to all
n
devices. Upon receiving the model parameters, the devices run an internal loop for
E\in\mathbb{N}
iterations to train the model with learning rate
\mu
as

\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)}[e]=\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)}[e-1]-\mu\nabla_{i}^{(l)}[e-1],

(40)

for all
e\in\{1,...,E\}
, where
\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)}[0]\triangleq\mathbf{w}^{(l)}
and
\nabla_{i}^{(l)}[e]
is the gradient of the loss function
L(\cdot)
with respect to
\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)}
. The gradient is computed as

\nabla_{i}^{(l)}[e]=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\nabla L\left(\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)}[e];\mathcal{D}_{i}[k]\right),

(41)

where
\mathcal{D}_{i}[k]
refers to the
k
th datasample of
\mathcal{D}_{i}
, and
\mathcal{K}
is a set of
K
randomly chosen unique integers from
\{1,..,|\mathcal{D}_{i}|\}
, i.e.,
K
is the batch size of the mini-batch gradient descent algorithm. After running
E
iterations of the training algorithm, device
i
has access to a local model
\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)}[E]
. At that point, it computes the model update as

\Delta\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)}=\mathbf{w}^{(l)}-\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)}[E]+\mathbf{\Delta}_{i}^{(l-1)},

(42)

where
\mathbf{\Delta}_{i}^{(l-1)}
is the accumulated sparsification error of the previous communication round, computed in (
45
). For the first communication round,
\mathbf{\Delta}_{i}^{(-1)}=\mathbf{0}
. Once training is complete, the devices sparsify the model updates with the
r
Top-
k
algorithm
[
28
]
which does two rounds of sparsification. Firstly, all entries of
\Delta\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)}
are set to zero, except for the
r\leq d
elements of greatest magnitude, i.e.,

\text{top}_{r}(\Delta\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)})\triangleq\begin{cases}\Delta w_{i}^{(l)}[j]&\text{if }j\in\{\pi(1),...,\pi(r)\},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(43)

where
j\in\{1,...,d\}
and
\pi
is a permutation of
\{1,...,d\}
such that
|\Delta w_{i}^{(l)}[\pi(i)]|\geq|\Delta w_{i}^{(l)}[\pi(i+1)]|
for all
i\in\{1,...,d-1\}
. Secondly,
k\leq r
random elements of
\text{top}_{r}(\Delta\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)})
are kept and the remaining elements are dropped, resulting in a
k
-sparse vector
\text{k-top}_{r}(\Delta\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)})
. To align with the notation in Section
III
, we denote this vector as

\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(l)}\triangleq\text{k-top}_{r}(\Delta\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)}).

(44)

The information which is lost during sparsification is stored in the memory of the devices, according to the error accumulation algorithm
[
29
]
,

\mathbf{\Delta}_{i}^{(l)}=\Delta\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l)}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(l)}.

(45)

This accumulated error
\mathbf{\Delta}_{i}^{(l)}
is used in the following communication round to compute
\Delta\mathbf{w}_{i}^{(l+1)}
.

At this point, we have
n
devices carrying
k
-sparse vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(l)}
and we are interested in recreating the sum of the sparsified model updates, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}_{*}^{(l)}\triangleq\sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(l)}
. This aligns with the setup for
MVCS
. The transmitter and receiver basebands from Sections
III-B
and

at the

in
2T

\mathbf{w}^{(l+1)}=\mathbf{w}^{(l)}-\eta^{(l)}\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{(l)},

(46)

where

IV-A

Step Size

In a traditional
FedAvg
setup, the model updates are communicated separately and then combined in the processor of the receiver. This way, the norm of the aggregated model update is available, which is advantageous for convergence. In particular, as training starts from a randomly initialized model,
\mathbf{w}^{(0)}
is generally far from the optimal point, which results in large updates. As the weights approach the optimum, the model update norms decline. Having this information at the receiver helps to select the step size since it is beneficial to take large steps when the weights are far from the optimum. This information is unavailable with the proposed
MV
scheme since all norm information is lost during communication.

With this in mind, the step size schedule for
\eta^{(l)}
becomes critical when training with
MV
. In this work, we have chosen to leverage an exponential schedule, which aligns with the norms of our numerical results. Specifically,

\eta^{(l)}=\eta_{s}+(\eta_{f}-\eta_{s})\cdot(1-e^{-\eta_{d}l}),

(47)

where
\eta_{s}
,
\eta_{f}
, and
\eta_{d}
are the starting step size, final step size, and step size decay, respectively. In our numerical results, we select these hyperparameters based on previous training results. In particular, we train a network with an oracle to fit these three parameters. Then, when we train another network with
MVCS
, the step size follows (
47
) with the fitted parameters. In practice, the choice of
\eta_{s}
,
\eta_{f}
, and
\eta_{d}
can be based on prior experience of training other models.

V
MVCS Applied to Histogram Estimation

In this section, we illustrate how
MVCS
can be applied to exact histogram estimation. In our earlier work
[
30
]
, we considered the problem of estimating the histogram support. With
MVCS
, we extend this work to estimate the full histogram. We consider the set-up where
n
user devices hold a message
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, which are one-hot vectors, and the non-zero element of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
indicates the location of device
i
’s item. Given this formulation, the histogram can be obtained by computing
\mathbf{x}_{*}=\sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}
. The goal of the
AP
is to obtain the histogram in a secure way, i.e. directly estimating
\sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}
without obtaining knowledge of any individual
\mathbf{x}_{i}
.

Note that, as mentioned in Remark
2
, the
MVCS
algorithm yields a unit-norm estimate
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\approx\mathbf{x}_{*}/\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}
.
To retrieve the full histogram
\mathbf{x}_{*}
, additional post-processing steps are required. Since
\mathbf{x}_{*}
is a sum of one-hot vectors,
each element of
\mathbf{x}_{*}/\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}
is an integer multiple of
1/\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}
. Furthermore, since the sum of all elements in
\mathbf{x}_{*}
is equal to
n
, the sum of the elements in the normalized histogram will be
n/\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}
. Thus, the full histogram can be recovered from its normalized version by first dividing it by the sum of its elements, and then multiplying it by
n
,

\mathbf{x}_{*}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{*}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}}\div\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{x_{*}[j]}{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}}\right)\times n=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{*}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}}\div\frac{n}{\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}}\times n,

(48)

where
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
represents the index of
\mathbf{x}_{*}
.

If the
MVCS
estimate
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
is close to
\mathbf{x}_{*}/\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}
, we can approximately recover the full histogram
\mathbf{x}_{*}
by the process similar to (
48
). Since the original histogram has at most
n
non-zero elements, we will keep the
n
largest elements and define the resulting vector as
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{th}
.
Then we define the estimated full histogram
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{f}
as follows, where we first divide
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{th}
by the sum of its elements and multiply
n
.

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{f}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{th}\frac{n}{\sum_{j}\hat{x}_{th}[j]}.

(49)

From (
35
), we observe that
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
has non-integer elements, as do
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{th}
and
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{f}
. For
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
close enough to
\mathbf{x}_{*}/\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}
, we can retrieve the exact histogram by rounding each element of
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{f}
to the nearest integer. A sufficient condition for exact histogram estimation by rounding is

\left\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{f}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{th}\frac{n}{\sum_{j}\hat{x}_{th}[j]}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}

(50)

Under this condition, each element of
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{f}-\mathbf{x}_{*}
will have an absolute value less than or equal to
1/2
, which leads the rounded version of
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{f}
to be equal to
\mathbf{x}_{*}
. However, note that this condition represents a loose bound on exact histogram estimation and it is possible to recover the exact histogram even when the square error exceeds 1/4. For example, consider the case where
\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{0}=n
,
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{f}
and
\mathbf{x}_{*}
share the same support, and

\max_{j}|\hat{x}_{f}[j]-x_{*}[j]|=\nu<1/2
. Then,
\lVert\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{f}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\nu^{2}n
, while still yielding the exact histogram by rounding. The bound
\lVert\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{f}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\nu^{2}n
is significantly looser than (
50
) for large
n
.

As mentioned in Theorem
3
, our
MVCS
protocol can guarantee
\left\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{*}/\|\mathbf{x}_{*}\|_{2}\right\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
with high probability. We first bound
\|\mathbf{x}_{*}\|_{2}
using the fact that it’s a sum of one-hot vectors, and show that

can guarantee
\left\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{th}-\mathbf{x}_{*}/\|\mathbf{x}_{*}\|_{2}\right\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
as well for
\epsilon
that is small enough.

\sqrt{n}\leq\lVert\mathbf{x}_{*}\rVert_{2}\leq n

Proof.

For the upper bound,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}_{*}\|_{2}

VII
Conclusion

We proposed a non-coherent
AirComp
method for reliable function computation. The proposed approach leverages the state-of-the-art
MV
communication scheme, which uses orthogonal radio resources to indicate the sign of local real-valued vectors. On its own,
MV
-
AirComp
experiences frequent bit errors due to the random phase offsets between the superimposed signals. To correct these errors, we leverage methods from
1bCS
, which serves a similar purpose to a forward error correcting code, offering a receiver-side correction mechanism. Theoretically, we proved that the proposed
MVCS
scheme achieves arbitrarily low errors in
T=\mathcal{O}(kn\log(d))
channel uses, where
n
is the number of devices,
k
is the sparsity of the local vectors, and
d
is the dimension of the vector. We also provided results for two example applications of
MVCS
: histogram estimation, and distributed
ML
. For histogram estimation,
MVCS
can securely retrieve the histogram in
T=\mathcal{O}(n^{4}\log(d))
channel uses, and in practice, it is achievable with lower complexity. For distributed
ML
, we compared our scheme to a state-of-the-art
AirComp
scheme and found that
MVCS
can reach higher classification accuracy under fixed radio resource constraints.

Both within histogram estimation and distributed
ML
, the proposed scheme opens up new research directions. In histogram estimation, we suspect that
MVCS
can be improved further since it is not designed to exploit the discrete nature of vectors
\sum_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}
. We suspect that sketching
[
31
]
or quantified group testing
[
33
]
could be combined with
AirComp
to design a more efficient method, which remains as future work. In distributed
ML
, the loss of norm information from
1bCS
harms convergence, as described in Section
IV-A
. Recent work on
1bCS
with norm estimation could potentially relieve this problem
[
34
]
.
