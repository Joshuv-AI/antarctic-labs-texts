Nanoscale Geometrical Patterning for Junctionless Thermoelectrics
Authors: Sergio Gonzalez-Munoz, Peng Xiao, Charalambos Evangeli, Elisa Castanon, Stuart Finch, Matthew Hamer, Johanna Zultak, Olga Kazakova, Kimberley Siret, Stefania Skorda, Athanassios G. Kontos, Edward McCann, Roman Gorbachev, Oleg Kolosov, Alexandros El Sachat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Typical thermoelectric phenomena require a junction between two dissimilar or chemically/electrically modified materials with different Seebeck coefficients, where temperature gradients generate a voltage (Seebeck effect) and applied electrical currents induce localized heating/cooling (Peltier effect). Here we show that periodic geometrical patterning alone is sufficient to generate spatially extended thermoelectric responses, such as the Seebeck coefficient, governed by a characteristic thermoelectric relaxation length, without requiring compositional modification or heterojunctions. Specifically, we found that the Seebeck coefficient, the key parameter governing thermoelectric performance, can be engineered over arbitrarily shaped, large-area regions of a uniform two-dimensional (2D) material through geometrical patterning. The modification of the Seebeck coefficient extends exponentially from the geometric discontinuity with a characteristic “decay length”, d TE ∼ 0.4 μm. By constructing nanopatterns of voids with pitch smaller than d TE, we effectively achieve a thermoelectric effect without material “junctions”, providing alternative routes to manipulate the directionality of thermoelectric response in diverse scaled 2D devices for nanoelectronics, photodetectors, sensors and energy applications.

Thermoelectric (TE) phenomena are of great importance for low-grade (small temperature difference) thermal energy recovery and solid-state cooling that does not require any moving parts. As of today, significant improvements in TE efficiency are still needed for thermoelectric technology to compete with conventional cooling systems or energy recovery methods such as vapor-compression refrigeration and heat engines. The efficiency of TE materials primarily depends on the Seebeck coefficient as well as on their electrical and thermal conductivities. Two dimensional (2D) materials, that consist of tightly covalently bound atomically thin layers linked in multilayer stacks via weak van der Waals (vdW) forces, allow one to manipulate thermal transport and electrical conductivity via heterostructuring and defect engineering. At the same time, present methods for Seebeck coefficient modification in 2D materials primarily rely on varying the composition, introducing dopants, forming material composites, , or creating nanomeshes. Recently, this research took an unexpected turn thanks to the discovery of the Geometric Thermoelectricity (GTE) phenomenon, indicating that the Seebeck coefficient in 2D materials is changed locally in the vicinity of nanoscale constrictions in a compositionally uniform material. GTE has already inspired works on single material “thermocouples”, , metal nanowires, − and local cooling in nanodevices. , Furthermore, as we have recently demonstrated in suspended 2D materials, nanopatterning also enables the modification of transport properties of thermal carriers and it can be used to confine and guide heat flow in prescribed directions.

Here, we uncover the impact of nanopatterning on local thermal and thermoelectric properties of 2D materials, and we demonstrate that GTE can be used to modify the Seebeck coefficient across a large arbitrary-shaped area of 2D material rather than at a single point of nanoscale constriction. We find that the local Seebeck coefficient modification is directly linked to the distance from the discontinuity in the 2D material, without any discernible dependence on the shape or symmetry of the discontinuity, and we were able to rule out the possibility that the GTE effect is linked to local doping at the boundary of the devices. The modification of the Seebeck coefficient extends up to several hundred nanometers from the discontinuity, an order of magnitude larger than the modification of thermal transport properties or the size of the used single-hole (void) pattern. Such nonlocality of the GTE effect allows us to modify the Seebeck coefficient in the 2D material over an arbitrary large area via dedicated patterning, resulting in, effectively, a material with drastically different TE properties, made solely via geometrical means. Additionally, by combining experimental measurements with analytical and finite element modeling, we show that geometric modulation induces a spatial redistribution of the thermoelectric response, giving rise to extended thermoelectric landscapes with a modified Seebeck coefficient characterized by a finite thermoelectric relaxation length.

Results and Discussion

Exploring the Origin of GTEImaging Thermal and Thermoelectric Transport in Nanoscale Patterns

Here we study the origin of GTE by combining scanning thermal microscopy (SThM) − and scanning thermal gate microscopy (STGM) experiments, supported by analytical and finite element analysis (FEA) simulations. To this aim, we first measure nanoscale thermal and thermoelectric transport in devices based on nanopatterned SnSe2 flakes of different thicknesses, namely Sample 1 (30 nm) and Sample 2 (100 nm) as described in Methods and Note SN1 of the Supporting Information (SI). Sample 1 was used to optimize the FIB processing conditions by testing different ion beam currents for patterning, while Sample 2 was used for detailed thermal and thermoelectric transport measurements. To minimize ion-beam-induced damage and Ga implantation effects, the FIB processing was carried out using a low Ga+ ion beam current of 2 pA, which has previously been shown to cause only limited structural modifications in 2D materials under otherwise identical processing conditions. Additionally, Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) and X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) measurements performed on representative FIB-patterned regions showed no evidence of chemical modifications of the SnSe2 surface, as shown in Figure S3 of the SI.

AFM topography analysis of this sample is shown in Figure S2a,b of the SI. Figure b,c show representative SEM images of nanopatterns consisting of line cuts and arrays of holes with different “pitch”, shape and order fabricated on the SnSe2 surface. Figure d shows a schematic illustration of the experimental setup used to spatially resolve the local thermoelectric and thermal properties of the studied patterns with sub-nW and sub-mK heat flux and temperature sensitivities, and sub-50 nm spatial resolution. The measurements were performed by heating the scanning probe tip and simultaneously recording topography, thermal and thermovoltage signals in a vacuum environment. For local Seebeck measurements, the tip was heated by applying an AC voltage, resulting in the modulation of the temperature of the tip at double AC voltage frequency (second harmonic), while the global voltage drop (V TE) over the device was measured at the second AC frequency harmonic during tip 2D scanning (STGM mode). In such measurements, the local temperature rise by the probe modifies the potential difference across the two electrical terminals located at different x positions on the right and left of the sample, and a 2D nanoscale map of the TE response is recorded. Thermal, thermovoltage and topography maps of a single hole (void) patterned in the SnSe2 flake are shown in Figures e,f, respectively. Regions exhibiting a low thermal signal in the SThM map correspond to areas of higher thermal conductance. Based on Figure e, the lateral resolution of SThM for resolving local variations in thermal properties is estimated to be below 50 nm. In addition, from the thermal contrast around the boundary of the void, we observe that the disruption length of the local thermal transport (and hence, the rise of the local temperature produced by the heated tip) is on the order of 100 nm. This is in stark contrast with the 2D map of the thermovoltage generated by the tip, V TE (x, y) (see Figure f), that shows two opposite polarity thermovoltage responses as “wings” to the right and to the left of the void, covering an area on the order of hundreds of nanometers, much larger than the thermal transport disruption length observed in Figure e. This becomes even clearer from the thermal and thermovoltage profiles of Figures g,h (see also Figure S4d in the SI), where we observe that the modulation of the local thermal conductance (and hence the temperature of the probe apex contacting the sample) is much more localized compared to the TE response (note the different scale in 2D maps in Figure f vs Figure e and in profiles Figure h vs Figure g). As a result, the broad spatial extent of the thermoelectric response beyond the void cannot be attributed to thermal transport, indicating the presence of an additional, nonthermal mechanism.

Equation allows us to interpret the STGM thermovoltage map in Figure f as a gradient of the Seebeck coefficient dS(x)dx in the x-direction from the left to right electrodes of the device. This was further examined using finite-element simulations to estimate the point-spread function (PSF), or “kernel,” of the SThM thermal and the STGM thermoelectric responses, supported by test experimental SThM measurements, as the tip scans across the modified region (see Note SN4, Figure S16 of the SI). The red and blue areas in the STGM response of Figure f indicate positive and negative gradients in the Seebeck coefficient whereas white areas indicate an absence of this gradient and, hence, a uniform Seebeck coefficient. Essentially, we can see that, while the Seebeck coefficient is altered in the vicinity of the hole, significantly, this alteration extends to a large distance away from the hole of up to 800 nm, much larger than the length scale of the variation of the thermal transport, which is confined to a few tens of nanometers. As the Seebeck coefficient far from the void equals that of the unperturbed flake, and following the original model linking the GTE effect to the dependence of the charge carriers mean free path (MFP) near the constriction, it is reasonable to assume that the Seebeck coefficient vanishes at the perimeter of the void (maximal disruption) and recovers exponentially to the unperturbed value S 0 over a characteristic distance d TE 3 S(x)=S0[1−e−|x−xp|dTE] for x – x p > 0, where x p is the position of the perimeter and d TE is the characteristic length scale of the Seebeck coefficient recovery.

Quantitative Analysis of the Local Thermoelectric Response

To quantitatively interpret the experimental data of Figure , we conduct FEA simulations. The SThM tip was modeled as a localized heat source producing a temperature rise of ΔT within an approximately 50 nm-diameter area. The backside of the SiO2 layer resting on the Si substrate was assumed to be at ambient temperature considering the high thermal conductivity of Si. We use (eq ) to calculate the local Seebeck coefficient in the vicinity of the void (Figure i) and use it in the FEA modeling to explore and analyze the TE trends of our systems. As discussed above, we assume that the Seebeck coefficient gradually recovers from zero at the perimeter of the void, as the underlying boundary scattering mechanism of the Seebeck coefficient suppression is expected to be similar for both the nanoconstriction studied in ref . and the nanoscale voids investigated here. This suggests that, at a distance, l, from the constriction, the Seebeck coefficient approaches zero with a first-order dependence on l/l 0, where l 0 is the mean free path of the charge carriers. The Seebeck coefficient then reaches the characteristic negative value of n-doped SnSe2, while the void itself is assumed to contain no thermoelectric material. The spatial scale of the decrease is defined by the decay length d TE, yielding a simulated thermovoltage map for the 100 nm diameter single hole that reproduces the experiment: an opposite-polarity signal around the hole and nearly zero elsewhere (excluding regions near the Au electrodes). By varying d TE, we found a characteristic length in the exponent in (eq ) of approximately 400 nm, extracted from the simulated map (Figure j), that provides the best agreement with the experimental thermovoltage map shown in Figure f. A detailed fit of the thermovoltage profile extracted along a single void can be found in Figure S17a,b of the SI. By integrating the thermovoltage map along the x-direction using (eq ), we directly obtain the spatial distribution of the normalized local Seebeck coefficient from thermovoltage profiles across the center of the void, as shown in Figures k,l.

From the above analysis, it is clear that the thermovoltage modification (and hence the nonzero gradient of the Seebeck coefficient) occurs over a much wider area, when compared with the thermal measurements, where the local temperature rise around the hole is confined to a few tens of nm from the void boundary (see Figure e). Our simulations therefore indicate that another mechanism is responsible for the large scale (several hundreds of nanometers) variation of the thermovoltage response. It is possible to consider that this length scale reflects the dynamics of heat transfer between phononic (ph) (STGM generated local heating) and the electronic e (responsible for the thermovoltage and TE effects) subsystems with the characteristic length d TE being the product of the e-ph heat exchange time, τ c , and the Fermi velocity, v F , of the charge carriers in the 2D material. , With the characteristic ph-e heat transfer time (on the order of 1–2 ps), we consider an heuristic estimate of the heat transfer length as d TE = v F τ c , where v F characterizes ballistic electronic transport in the in-plane direction. Our analytical model (see analysis in Note SN3 of the SI) gives τ c ∼ 1.4 ps, v F ∼ 2 × 105ms –1 and d TE ∼ 280nm. The latter is close to our experimental value and significantly larger than a few tens of nm compared to the thermal transport modification.

Effect of Multi- Pattern Geometry on Local Seebeck Coefficient

Thermovoltage Modification with Gradient Patterns

Here, we increase the size of the patterned area (increasing the number of voids) while keeping the pitch constant. Figure a–c display the thermal and thermovoltage maps and line profiles of the patterned areas. The thermal map shows a clear contrast between the holes and the SnSe2 intact surface indicating a highly localized thermal transport disruption within a few tens of nanometers. At the same time, the thermovoltage signal, with positive (red areas) and negative (blue areas) gradients on the left and right side of each pattern, is extended over several hundreds of nm. The magnitude change of these gradients is proportional to the size of the patterned area while the maximum thermovoltage value that is measured is around 3 mV (103 differential amplifier gain was used). From the thermovoltage line scans of each pattern in Figure c we observe that the amplitude of the thermovoltage response inside the patterns slightly increases with the increase of the number of holes. However, the most notable difference arises in the peak-to-peak TE output, which increases by increasing the size of the patterned area.

Moreover, the positive and negative thermovoltage gradients observed at the opposing sides of the patterned rectangles and inverse polarity thermovoltages at the proximity holes, fully match the simulations and further support the initial assumption of exponential “decay” of the Seebeck coefficient described in (eq ). Due to the change in the gradient’s amplitude and length, it is determined that the number of holes predominantly affects the TE behavior of the material at the short edge, but less within the patterned rectangles. Note that a “tilted” thermovoltage transition is observed in the areas between such side gradients, i.e., inside the rectangles. This feature is more pronounced in the smaller patterns, indicating that the side gradients have a major effect in the thermovoltage response inside the rectangles. To clarify the thermovoltage sign convention, we have added a source-drain thermovoltage polar switching analysis (see Figure S8 of the SI), which compares STGM thermovoltage measurements with reversed source–drain assignments. Reversing the source and drain results in a corresponding inversion of the thermovoltage polarity while preserving the spatial signal distribution. The extracted line profiles of Figure S8c confirm this behavior, validating the sign convention used throughout the manuscript.

Finally, the simulated thermovoltage output and the Seebeck coefficient for each pattern are shown in Figures d–f and Figures S12, S13a–f, emulating the experimental sample. Here, the same increase in the peak-to-peak amplitude and steep side thermovoltage gradients are observed as the number of constrictions increases. Although such nanopatterning yields a lower Seebeck coefficient than a full p–n thermoelectric junction with opposing Seebeck coefficients, it enables thermoelectric responses to be directed along predefined pathways over variable length scales. These findings also highlight the effect of the gradients between the holes, suggesting that an optimized nanopatterning can be employed to create custom sized areas within a single material with controlled Seebeck coefficient directionality as we see in the next section.

Thermovoltage Dependence on the Varying Pitch and Temperature

Here we study the influence of sample temperature and pitch size on local thermal and thermoelectric transport by creating periodic arrays of holes of the same diameter and different pitches (400–1000 nm). Topography and thermovoltage maps of these patterns are shown in Figure a,b, respectively. Overlapping the thermovoltage and topography data (Figure c), we observe that each adjacent hole generates a surrounding small thermovoltage gradient inverse polarity, which modifies the response of neighboring hole with the TE response influenced, in this case, by the pitch dimensions. As in case of the patterns with a varying number of holes, this creates an undulating shape on the extracted horizontal thermovoltage 1D profiles, following a potential difference between the electrodes. Essentially, it is clear that patterning with different pitches offers a means to control the local TE behavior. As clearly observed in Figure c, the peak-to-peak intensity of the thermovoltage increases with increasing pitch distance. If the two holes are close to each other, the opposite polarity of the thermovoltage generated by adjacent holes suppresses the local variation in the Seebeck coefficient. Such nanopatterns, with characteristic sizes smaller than the average MFP of thermal carriers in SnSe2, suggest that it is possible to create a continuous area of a material with arbitrary size or shape with a significantly modified Seebeck coefficient.

We also compared the STGM measurements at room (300 K) and low (203 K) temperatures on the sample using the same 1000 nm pitch. At low temperatures, the thermovoltage around the holes and on the right and left sides of the pattern increased by a factor of 3 compared to thermovoltage measured at room temperature (see Figure e–g). With changes of the local temperature rise due to the STGM tip heating on the order of 100 K, one would expect an approximately two times increase of the thermovoltage due to the local increase of the temperature under the tip if the Seebeck coefficient does not change. This result suggests an approximately 50% increase of the Seebeck coefficient at 203 K compared to 300 K, which is typical for semiconductors. Figure h shows a comparison of the peak-to-peak scale of the thermovoltage for room and low temperature. Besides showing a larger thermovoltage difference in all the patterns, a steeper fitted slope is observed in the low temperature data, indicating a stronger influence of the larger pitch patterns on changes in the Seebeck coefficient at lower temperatures of the sample.

Can We Create Continuous Material with New TE Properties Based on the GTE Effect?

Besides changing the material temperature, pitch and constriction number, other factors such as geometrical disorder were also tested here to extract whether they can influence the local TE response in SnSe2. For that, ordered and disordered 100 nm diameter holes are patterned on the same sample and studied with AFM and STGM. As seen in Figures b,e, the disorder produces a response of a similar order of magnitude, with an approximate 60% increase, possibly due to a different width of the patterned area. As in the previous patterns, the side gradients are clearly visible on both ordered and random hole distributed STGM thermovoltage maps and the corresponding profiles (Figures c,f). A detailed fit of the thermovoltage profile extracted along the ordered patterned area can be found in Figure S17c,d of the SI. Despite the amplitude of the side gradients being smaller for the ordered structure, no other major differences were observed between the two configurations. We note that the absence of experimentally observed thermovoltage gradients between the constriction formed by two neighboring holes and the perimeter of each hole further supports our assumption that the Seebeck coefficient is zero along the perimeter of the voids. This implies that the design and nanopatterning of 2D channels consisting of different shape and varying disorder structures at multiple length scales could potentially open a new route to achieve directional thermoelectric transport control over large distances, and that it is the distance between the voids vs d TE that is a major factor in modifying the Seebeck coefficient. The void spacing can vary over a wide range, from very small distances up to several hundreds of nanometers, while still effectively creating regions with a modified Seebeck coefficient. This provides an additional degree of freedom to tailor thermoelectric transport in the desired direction, enabling spatial control over the thermoelectric response. It must be noted, however, that for the extended linear voids (lines), we found that the experimental data can be explained only if the electrical conductivity within each slit is zero (see Figure S10 of the SI). This implies that, even when there is a gradient in the Seebeck coefficient that generates a thermoelectric potential, such potential will be suppressed if there is not a sufficiently short conductive path between the electrodes and the area with the gradient. This indicates that localized voids, rather than extended cuts, are the preferred geometry for modifying the material’s TE properties.

Consequently, our observations are explained by a geometry-induced redistribution of the thermoelectric response, whereby patterning generates spatially extended thermoelectric landscapes with significantly modified (reduced) Seebeck coefficient governed by a characteristic thermoelectric relaxation length. In this framework, geometry acts as a design parameter controlling the spatial thermoelectric response. This interpretation differs from previous studies on nanoporous or nanomesh materials, where the enhancement of the thermopower was attributed to energy-filtering effects, in which pore-edge potential barriers tend to filter out low-energy carriers, thereby increasing the Seebeck coefficient. Edge effects, with a depletion region on the scale of a few nanometers, appear to be inconsistent with the spatial extent of the thermovoltage response observed here.

Testing the Effects of Geometrical Asymmetry and Carrier Polarity

To directly attribute the origin of geometric TE modification to the length scale of patterns and to exclude the potential influence of asymmetry of the patterns on the GTE Seebeck coefficient modification, we studied TE effects in 25 nm thick hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN)encapsulated monolayer graphene heterostructures with geometrically symmetric and asymmetric junctions (see Figure S7). More details on the fabrication, morphological and chemical characterization of these junctions can be found in Notes SN1 and SN2 of the SI. These structures also allowed a change in the polarity of the carriers using back-gating, to elucidate the possibility of edge doping on the TE response,with Figure a,b showing the topography and thermal signal and Figure c showing the thermovoltage map. The SThM contrast vs SiO2/Si substrate indicates the higher thermal conductance of graphene and h-BN, typical for such encapsulated structures. While the Seebeck coefficient is modified around the constriction, as would be expected from the previous studies, we found no effect of the symmetry of the constriction observed in three geometries tapered-flat, flat–flat, flat-tapered, which rules out the effect of geometric asymmetry on the sign of the GTE effect.

Then, by using back gating, we were able to reliably invert in such a sample the carrier polarity in the encapsulated graphene layer. If there were any edge doping at the perimeter of the voids, the changing of the polarity of the carriers would not change the sign of the GTE effect, as such doping would produce the same sign of the TE response. At the same time, Figure c and Figure S7c show STGM maps of the n- and p-type back-gated structures, with their corresponding profiles extracted in Figure d. The central symmetric constriction (area 2) shows a clear thermovoltage polarity inversion for both doping configurations, with the opposing polarity, allowing us to rule out edge doping in the system, as that would not lead to the inversion of the contrast. At the asymmetric areas 1 and 3, such an inversion is also observed, while slightly shifted away from the constriction center due to the different widths at each constriction side. Additional measurements on the encapsulated graphene, demonstrating the reversal of the GTE response in the vicinity of the junction upon inversion of the doping, are provided in Figure S7 of the SI.

Conclusions

In summary, we linked the GTE phenomenon to geometrical factors and demonstrated that such an effect is present in devices based on encapsulated monolayer graphene and 2D semiconductors such as SnSe2. By combining analytical and finite element modeling, state-of-the art nanofabrication processes and advanced scanning probe microscopy techniques (SThM/STGM), we demonstrated that it is possible to create, in an otherwise uniform 2D semiconductor material, arbitrary shaped nanoscale to microscale arrays of varying thermoelectric Seebeck coefficient. Such areas offer the advantage of directing the thermoelectric response along predefined pathways on the material surface. In addition, we linked the observed Seebeck coefficient modification to the product of the electron–phonon heat exchange time, τ, and the Fermi velocity, v F, of the charge carriers, providing an order-of-magnitude estimate in agreement with the experimental data. Additionally, we investigated the dimensional effects of geometric modifications and compared the normalized TE response across different geometries through a combination of experimental measurements and finite element modeling.

The ability to create large and arbitrary shaped areas of multilayer 2D material with tailored TE properties, opens the way for much broader application of the GTE effect in nanoscale and microscale cooling, heat manipulation and TE energy generation. For example, GTE could enable effective TE junctions, thus providing a route toward an all-2D thermoelectric module in which areas with different Seebeck can be designed to direct thermoelectric currents toward selected electrodes. However, it should be noted that symmetric patterned structures alone do not produce a net thermovoltage under a uniform macroscopic temperature gradient because the thermoelectric contributions from symmetry-related regions cancel. A finite thermoelectric output requires the combination of GTE-patterned and unpatterned regions through asymmetric patterning, asymmetric placement relative to the electrodes, or a nonuniform temperature distribution. Nevertheless, such devices would exploit geometry as an active design parameter, allowing thermoelectric junctions, current collectors and energy conversion pathways to be defined lithographically within a single material platform. This capability could facilitate the realization of thermoelectric circuits with spatially programmable function e.g., localized energy harvesting. Realizing these opportunities will require, however, engineering approaches that tailor the thermoelectric response while preserving both the material’s thermal and electrical transport performance.

Finally, our approach may enable the exploration of thermoelectric phenomena in conventional semiconductor materials and in broader applications beyond the scope of this work. For example, our findings could enable large area sensing arrays for infrared, terahertz and thermal imaging by simply patterning a uniform 2D material into the desired geometry. They also open pathways toward nanoscale temperature sensors, streamlined on-chip cooling without junctions between dissimilar materials, and electrically decoupled thermal energy harvesting (e.g., from RF and near-field communications). Combining with bandgap engineering strategies and careful selection of materials with appropriate thermal and electrical transport characteristics, the proposed material engineering approach could provide a promising route to realize a wide variety of electronic devices based on thin film semiconductors for nanoscale electronics.

Methods

Sample Fabrication

We studied 30 (Sample 1) and 100 nm (Sample 2) thick SnSe2 on SiO2/Si substrate that were processed by a focused ion beam (FIB) to drill various holes and/or line patterns, namely sets of 100 nm diameter holes and 100 nm wide slits (see Figure S1 of the SI). The crystalline SnSe2 flakes were prepared via mechanical exfoliation from bulk centimeter-scale SnSe2 single crystals (HQ Graphene). Thin flakes were exfoliated using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamps and transferred onto dual electrodes prefabricated on substrates. The substrates comprised 300 nm silicon dioxide (SiO2) thermally grown on 500 μm-thick silicon wafers. Dual electrodes were defined by laser lithography, followed by electron beam evaporation of a 5 nm titanium (Ti) adhesion layer and a 95 nm gold (Au) conductive layer. After transfer, nanopatterning was performed using a Ga+ focused ion beam (FIB) milling with a Zeiss Crossbeam system.

The h-BN-encapsulated graphene heterostructures have been assembled using a PMMA transfer method and patterned using electron beam lithography with subsequent CHF6 reactive ion etching step. PMMA was chosen as carrying material due to its optical compatibility with h-BN, otherwise hard to visualize. After treating the 90 nm thick SiO2/Silicon substrate, the first h-BN flake was directly deposited on its surface, shielding from charged surface states and adding flatness to the ensemble. Then, a new thin 3 nm layer of h-BN was exfoliated and used to pick-up the monolayer graphene from its original substrate. The last step was to deposit such two layers on top of the original h-BN flake, completely covering the monolayer graphene with the 2D insulator (see Figure S5a–c of the SI). The constrictions were created through electron beam lithography following a double half bow tie/rectangular junction and a center narrow strip transition (Figure S5d,e of the SI). More details on sample and device fabrication, as well as FIB patterning, are provided in Note SN1 of the SI.

Materials, Device Characterization and Modeling

The dimensions and chemical composition of the fabricated patterns were characterized by Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), as presented in Figures S1–S4 of the SI. The SThM and STGM measurements performed using a scanning probe microscope (SPM) (NT-MDT Spectrum Instruments Smena) operating in high vacuum environment (∼10–6 Torr) and doped Si resistive scanning thermal probe (Bruker Anasys Instruments Therma-Lever AN2–200) that served as one of the legs of the modified Wheatstone bridge (see Figure S5 of the SI). This probe incorporated a resistive heater close to its apex, which acted as the main heat source as well as the thermometer over the sample. To power the probe, its electrodes were connected to the bridge, which, in the SThM configuration, was excited with 3 V DC and 4 V peak-to-peak AC at a frequency of 91 kHz from a Keithley 3390 arbitrary waveform generator, with bridge output preamplified by the low noise (1nV Hz-1/2) instrumental amplifier (AD8429, Analog Devices). The bridge was initially balanced at the low AC and zero DC voltage and ambient temperature and then energized with AC and DC voltages to produce approximately 1 mW power to the probe to heat the apex approximately to 100 K above the sample temperature via constant Joule heating. More details on the characterization of the samples and devices are provided in Note SN2 of the SI. The FEA modeling was performed in COMSOL Multiphysics environment using heat transport and thermoelectric moduli with typical modeling parameters specified in Table ST2 of the SI. The analytical model used to estimate the cooling rate and characteristic length is described in detail in Note SN3 of the SI.
