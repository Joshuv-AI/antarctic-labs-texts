Background

Malignant pericardial effusion is a rare but potentially fatal manifestation of cancer, frequently associated with advanced malignancies. Lung adenocarcinoma is the most commonly implicated primary tumor overall, while breast cancer is more frequently reported in female cohorts, with pericardial involvement occurring in up to 15% of cases with metastatic spread [1, 2]. However, cardiac tamponade as an initial presentation is rare and may be easily overlooked, particularly in the absence of hypotension or classic cancer symptoms.

In modern clinical practice, differentiating malignant from infectious or cardiogenic effusions remains challenging. Subacute normotensive tamponade—often linked with malignancy—presents subtly with dyspnea, fatigue, or persistent tachycardia, without dramatic hemodynamic compromise [1, 3]. This can delay diagnosis, especially when early imaging (for example, chest radiograph or CTPA) appears non-revealing of any tumor.

Pericardial fluid cytology is superior to pleural fluid cytology for diagnosing malignancy, with sensitivities as high as 80% [4, 5]. Echocardiography is essential for detecting tamponade physiology, while positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET–CT) provides added value by identifying metabolically active lesions undetectable by conventional (computed tomography) CT [6, 7]. As this case illustrates, early cardiology involvement and integration of advanced imaging were crucial for uncovering the underlying malignancy and expediting oncologic referral.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old Malaysian woman of Chinese ethnicity, presented with a 3-week history of productive cough, low-grade fever, and exertional dyspnea. She reported no weight loss, night sweats, or contact with tuberculosis (TB) patients. She was initially prescribed oral augmentin by her general practitioner, but her symptoms showed minimal improvement. She had no known underlying medical illness and prior malignancy, no significant family history of cancer, and no history of occupational or environmental exposure. She was a lifelong non-smoker and denied alcohol use. Her medication history prior to admission was unremarkable.

Upon admission, she was alert and hemodynamically stable. Her pulse rate was 118 beats per minute, blood pressure 110/70 mmHg, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute, and SpO₂ 93% on room air (improving to 97% with 3 L/min of supplemental oxygen). Physical examination revealed bilateral crepitations on chest auscultation. Cardiovascular examination demonstrated tachycardia with a regular rhythm, normal S1 and S2, without murmurs or pericardial rub, and no evidence of elevated jugular venous pressure, peripheral edema, or muffled heart sounds. Electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia.

Initial blood investigations showed neutrophilic leukocytosis, elevated C-reactive protein, and mild microcytic anemia with reduced mean corpuscular volume (MCV) and mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH). She was admitted to the general ward with an initial working diagnosis of community-acquired pneumonia with right parapneumonic effusion. De novo decompensated heart failure was also considered due to the cardiomegaly and dyspnea. She was commenced on intravenous piperacillin–tazobactam.

A pigtail catheter was inserted for drainage of the right pleural effusion, yielding straw-colored exudative fluid. However, pleural fluid cytology was negative for malignancy. Despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy, the patient remained persistently tachycardic and tachypneic, raising concern for alternative diagnoses.

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) (Fig. 2) was performed to exclude pulmonary embolism. It confirmed subsegmental emboli in the left lung and incidentally revealed a large circumferential pericardial effusion with contrast reflux into the inferior vena cava and hepatic veins, in addition to bilateral pleural effusions. Notably, no pulmonary mass was visualized on CTPA.

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography revealing circumferential pericardial effusion, most prominent adjacent to the right atrium (blue arrow)

Given these findings, the patient was concurrently initiated on subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin at a dose of 1 mg/kg twice daily for treatment of the subsegmental pulmonary embolism and an urgent cardiology consult was obtained. Bedside transthoracic echocardiography (Fig. 3) demonstrated classic features of tamponade physiology—diastolic collapse of the right atrium and right ventricle, along with respiratory variation in mitral inflow—despite blood pressure remain normotension.

Transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrating a large pericardial effusion (blue arrow) with diastolic collapse of the right ventricle (red arrow), consistent with tamponade physiology

Emergency transthoracic echocardiogram guided pericardiocentesis was performed, via subxiphoid approach under local anesthesia, yielding 600 mL of hemorrhagic pericardial fluid. Tuberculosis workup of the pericardial sample was negative. However, pericardial fluid cytological analysis (Table 2) revealed malignant adenocarcinoma cells, representing a critical turning point in the diagnostic process. Smears demonstrated numerous three-dimensional clusters and tight aggregates of dysplastic cells exhibiting marked nuclear pleomorphism, coarse to stippled chromatin, and occasional prominent nucleoli. The background contained abundant lymphocytes, with scattered neutrophils, macrophages, and mesothelial cells in a hemorrhagic milieu. Occasional mitotic figures were identified, with no evidence of epithelioid granulomas or microorganisms. Overall, the findings were consistent with pericardial fluid positive for malignant cells, compatible with adenocarcinoma. The unexpected malignant cytology redirected the clinical focus toward an underlying occult malignancy and prompted oncologic workup.

A whole-body positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET–CT) scan (Fig. 4) revealed a hypermetabolic mass in the right upper lobe of the lung with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 8.5, suggestive of a primary lung adenocarcinoma. In addition, there were widespread hypermetabolic lesions involving the pleura, pericardium, bones, adrenal glands, and subcutaneous tissues, consistent with extensive metastasis, suggestive of advanced malignancy. The full timeline of her clinical progression from symptom onset to diagnosis is illustrated in Table 3. The patient was subsequently referred for respiratory and oncology evaluation for staging and management. A tissue diagnosis was planned; however, her clinical course was complicated by hospital-acquired pneumonia leading to septic shock, and she succumbed prior to definitive histopathological confirmation.

Discussion

Malignant pericardial effusion is an uncommon initial manifestation of lung cancer, although pericardial involvement is reported in up to 15% of advanced cases [8, 9]. Cardiac tamponade as the presenting feature is particularly rare and may be underrecognized, especially in the absence of classical signs such as hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and elevated jugular venous pressure. In our patient, persistent tachycardia without overt hemodynamic instability prompted further investigation, ultimately leading to the unexpected diagnosis of subacute, normotensive cardiac tamponade.

In tuberculosis-endemic regions such as Malaysia, a chronic cough with fever frequently prompts workup for Mycobacterium tuberculosis and also initiation of empirical antibiotic therapy targeting common bacterial pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae or Haemophilus influenzae. Accordingly, this patient was started on piperacillin–tazobactam for presumed community-acquired pneumonia. However, in the absence of classical clinical or radiological features suggestive of pulmonary tuberculosis and with poor response to antibiotics, consideration of other differential diagnoses such as malignancy should be made.

Heart failure was also considered in this case due to the presence of dyspnea and radiographic cardiomegaly. However, the discovery of an exudative pleural effusion and hemorrhagic pericardial fluid made a cardiogenic origin less likely. Hemorrhagic pericardial effusion warrants a broad differential that includes malignancy, tuberculosis, uremia, and connective tissue disorders [10, 11]. Pleural fluid cytology may be limited by sampling variability and lower cellular yield, particularly in early or patchy pleural involvement, which can result in false-negative findings [12, 13]. In contrast, pericardial fluid analysis may provide more direct evidence of malignant involvement when the pericardium is affected, thereby playing a complementary role in establishing the diagnosis in patients with concurrent pleuropericardial disease [14].

Subacute tamponade due to malignancy often presents insidiously and without hypotension due to compensatory mechanisms [15]. This can delay diagnosis unless clinicians maintain a high index of suspicion. In this case, echocardiographic evidence of tamponade physiology and timely pericardiocentesis were crucial to prevent impending hemodynamic compromise as well as uncovering the underlying malignancy.

Although cardiac tamponade due to lung malignancy has been previously described in the literature, it most often occurs in patients with an established cancer diagnosis or during the course of oncologic treatment [8, 16]. In contrast, this case is notable for subacute, normotensive cardiac tamponade without classical constitutional symptoms as the initial manifestation of occult metastatic lung adenocarcinoma, particularly in a female patient, in whom breast cancer is the more commonly associated with malignant pericardial effusion [17, 18].

The diagnosis was established prior to any identifiable lung mass on conventional contrasted CT, underscoring the diagnostic value of echocardiography and pericardial fluid cytology in such atypical presentations. CT, including CTPA, while valuable for detecting pulmonary embolism, is limited in identifying small or metabolically active tumors, whereas PET–CT was more instrumental in detecting a hypermetabolic right upper lobe lesion and widespread metastatic disease, thereby facilitating definitive diagnosis and oncologic referral [19, 20]. The concurrent subsegmental pulmonary embolism likely reflected a paraneoplastic hypercoagulable state, a well-documented phenomenon in advanced malignancy [21, 22]. Early involvement of a multidisciplinary team, including cardiology, radiology, respiratory and oncology, was essential in expediting diagnosis and treatment planning.

Conclusions

This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of malignant pericardial effusion presenting as subacute, normotensive cardiac tamponade in a patient without a known history of malignancy. In patients with persistent tachycardia and unexplained effusions, early echocardiographic assessment and pericardial fluid analysis are essential to identify the etiology including occult malignancy. This atypical presentation in a female patient further underscores its clinical rarity. Although histopathological confirmation of the primary tumor was not obtained, which represents the main limitation of this case, the combination of cytological findings and PET–CT imaging strongly supported the diagnosis of metastatic disease. Early recognition of pericardial involvement may facilitate timely diagnosis and guide appropriate management.
