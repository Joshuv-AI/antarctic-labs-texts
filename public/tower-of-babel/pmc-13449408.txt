Prevalence, symptom phenotype, and multidomain associated factors of fatigue among Chinese college students: a nationwide cross-sectional study
Authors: Guoshu He, Xu Zhang, Guihua Fu, Xiaomin Bao, Wen Huang, Jingjing Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFatigue is a highly prevalent but understudied health complaint among college students. This nationwide study aimed to estimate the prevalence, severity distribution, and lifestyle determinants of fatigue among Chinese college students using a validated multidimensional instrument.MethodsA geographically stratified cross-sectional survey was conducted across 30 Chinese provinces. Full-time college students completed an anonymous online questionnaire including the Brief Fatigue Inventory (BFI), sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, and self-reported disease history. Hierarchical multiple linear regression was used to identify independent predictors of BFI Total Score.ResultsA total of 3,014 valid responses were analyzed (63.9% female). The overall mean BFI Total Score was 4.06 ± 2.01. Using established cutoffs, 49.0% experienced clinically meaningful fatigue (moderate or severe). The most prevalent co-occurring symptoms were difficulty concentrating (60.3%) and low mood (56.6%), indicating a predominantly cognitive-affective fatigue phenotype. Sleep duration was the strongest modifiable lifestyle predictor; sleeping fewer than 5 h per night was associated with a BFI increase of 0.90 points relative to the 7–8 h reference (β = 0.90, p < 0.001). Breakfast skipping (β = 0.20, p = 0.008) and late-night eating (β = 0.51, p < 0.001) were also significant independent predictors after full adjustment. A history of anxiety disorder was the single strongest predictor overall (β = 1.22, p < 0.001). The full model explained 13.3% of the variance in BFI Total Score (R²=0.133).ConclusionClinically meaningful fatigue affects nearly half of Chinese college students and presents a predominantly cognitive-affective phenotype. Anxiety disorder history and sleep deprivation are the leading determinants. Integrating psychological screening into fatigue consultations and implementing institutional sleep-protective policies are evidence-based priorities for campus health systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28038-0.Keywords: Fatigue, College students, Brief Fatigue Inventory, Sleep deprivation, Anxiety, Lifestyle factors, Campus health

Introduction

Globally, approximately 235 million students are enrolled in tertiary education, a figure projected to exceed 600 million by 2040 as higher education access expands across developing regions [1]. This rapid expansion has elevated student health as a public health priority. Large-scale surveys document a substantial mental health burden in this population: the WHO World Mental Health International College Student (WMH-ICS) project, conducted across 19 countries and over 14,000 students, estimated that 20.3% of college students met 12-month criteria for a common mental disorder [2]. More recent surveys following the COVID-19 pandemic report substantially higher rates, with pooled prevalence of depression and anxiety exceeding 30–40% in multi-country samples [3, 4]. The long-term consequences extend beyond wellbeing: psychological distress in college students is associated with academic underperformance, reduced workforce readiness, and increased risk of chronic mental illness in adulthood [5, 6].

Among the health complaints reported by college students, fatigue is both highly prevalent and insufficiently studied as an independent endpoint. Physiologically, fatigue encompasses progressive reduction in the capacity to sustain effort as well as persistently diminished energy at rest [7]. In young adults without serious medical illness, fatigue most commonly presents as a central syndrome. It is characterized by cognitive slowing, motivational loss, and emotional exhaustion. This presentation is distinct from the peripheral muscular fatigue of athletes and from cancer-related fatigue in oncology [8, 9]. As a transdiagnostic symptom spanning depressive, anxiety, and functional somatic presentations, fatigue may precede diagnosable mental disorders and function as a sensitive early-warning signal [5, 6]. Despite its relevance, fatigue appears to have been less frequently examined as a standalone primary outcome in college student research, compared with depression and anxiety.

Within this global landscape, China presents a particularly salient context. With over 47.6 million students enrolled in higher education in 2023, China operates the world’s largest tertiary education system [10]. The academic environment is highly competitive. Pressure begins with the gaokao college entrance examination and continues through postgraduate preparation and employment competition [11]. These structural demands may amplify fatigue burden beyond what is observed in lower-pressure educational settings. Studies conducted during and after the COVID-19 pandemic document elevated rates of psychological distress among Chinese college students [12, 13], yet population-level data on fatigue specifically, including its prevalence, severity distribution, and modifiable determinants, remain limited. Most prior Chinese studies focused on single institutions, specific disciplines, or the pandemic period [14].

Evidence from general young adult and college student populations identifies several modifiable lifestyle factors as key determinants of fatigue. Chronic sleep restriction produces cumulative neurobehavioral impairment in a dose-response manner, with most studies finding that fewer than 7 h of sleep per night is associated with significantly elevated fatigue [15, 16]. Irregular dietary behaviors, including skipping breakfast and late-night eating, disrupt circadian entrainment and metabolic homeostasis through chrono-nutritional mechanisms that extend beyond simple nutritional adequacy [17]. Among Chinese college students, the rising use of food delivery platforms has been associated with increasing prevalence of irregular meal timing and nutrient-poor dietary patterns [18]. Physical inactivity and smoking are further associated with both fatigue and psychological distress in campus populations [5]. Anxiety and depressive disorders, in turn, are well-established correlates of persistent fatigue, operating through shared neuroendocrine and sleep-disruption pathways [8].

Despite this background evidence, large-scale geographically diverse studies systematically characterizing fatigue prevalence and correlates using a validated multidimensional fatigue instrument in Chinese college students remain limited. We used the Brief Fatigue Inventory (BFI) to assess fatigue. The BFI is a validated nine-item instrument that captures both fatigue severity and functional interference. The BFI was originally developed and validated in oncology settings [9]; however, subsequent validation studies have confirmed the BFI’s reliability and structural validity in non-oncology clinical populations, including patients with chronic non-malignant pain [19] and community-dwelling adults without cancer [20], supporting its applicability beyond the original oncology context. The survey was conducted through a geographically stratified nationwide design across 30 provinces. Our objectives were: (1) to estimate the prevalence and severity distribution of fatigue; (2) to describe co-occurring symptoms; (3) to examine associations between lifestyle factors and fatigue severity; and (4) to identify factors independently associated with fatigue burden. The findings are intended to inform evidence-based clinical and public health strategies for fatigue identification and management in campus settings.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a nationwide multi-center cross-sectional design to investigate the prevalence of fatigue and its associated lifestyle risk factors among full-time college students in China. Data collection was conducted through an anonymous online self-administered questionnaire distributed via WeChat and a mobile survey platform, with sampling control and sample size validation (Text S1).

The minimum required sample size was calculated using the cross-sectional prevalence formula.

. With a 95% confidence level , a conservative estimated fatigue prevalence of 50% (p = 0.5), and a 2% margin of error (d = 0.02), the theoretical minimum sample size was 2,401. To account for incomplete submissions under the chain‑referral design (where the denominator of link viewers is unknown), the target sample size was increased by a 15% completion‑rate buffer to 2,825, based on prior online survey experience. A geographically stratified convenience sampling method was adopted, with stratification by region (eastern, central, western, and northeastern China) across 30 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities in mainland China (Table S1).

To guarantee the validity of responses and exclude low-effort or careless submissions, we implemented rigorous quality control measures. As illustrated in the flow chart, a total of 3,243 questionnaires were initially distributed and returned via the WeChat platform. First, 203 questionnaires with a completion time of less than 180 s were excluded, resulting in 3,040 eligible responses. Subsequently, an additional 26 questionnaires with missing data exceeding 20% of the core items were further excluded. After these two rounds of screening, a final sample of 3,014 valid responses was retained for analysis, covering students from 30 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities across mainland China (Fig. 1).

Survey instruments

Sociodemographic and lifestyle questionnaire

The data on fatigue and its potential determinants were collected using a self-developed questionnaire specifically designed for this study. Before formal large-scale administration, the questionnaire was pilot-tested among 30 college students to evaluate its clarity and acceptability. Internal consistency was examined using Cronbach’s α coefficient; a value greater than 0.70 was considered indicative of acceptable reliability. Three items were revised after pilot testing, one COQ item rephrased to remove a double-barreled construction; one fatigue frequency item clarified to specify the 6-month recall period; one lifestyle item restructured from open-text to multiple-choice format. No items were added or deleted; overall questionnaire structure, scoring, and domain allocation remained unchanged. After minor revisions based on the pilot results, the finalized questionnaire was distributed to the study participants. The full English version of the complete questionnaire (including all items and response options) is provided as Supplementary File 1. In the final sample (n = 3,014), the 9-item Cognitive Overload Questionnaire (COQ) component of the self-developed questionnaire demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.951; mean inter-item r = 0.682). Subscale alphas were: Emotional Load α = 0.893, Physical Load α = 0.890. and Cognitive Load α = 0.910. All corrected item-total correlations ranged from 0.770 to 0.851, and no item deletion improved the overall alpha, confirming that all items contributed meaningfully (Table S3).

Brief fatigue inventory (BFI)

Fatigue severity and functional interference were assessed using the validated Brief Fatigue Inventory (BFI). The BFI comprises 9 items rated on an 11-point numerical scale ranging from 0 (no fatigue/no interference) to 10 (worst imaginable fatigue/complete interference). It includes a 3-item severity subscale and a 6-item interference subscale. The total BFI score was calculated as the mean of all 9 items. According to established cutoff values, participants were classified into mild fatigue (BFI < 4), moderate fatigue (4 ≤ BFI ≤ 6), and severe fatigue (BFI > 6).

Psychometric properties of the BFI in the present sample

To verify the measurement validity of the BFI in the present non-oncology college student sample, we conducted reliability analyses using the Reliability Analysis procedure in IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0) and an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using the Factor Analysis procedure (principal axis factoring, direct oblimin oblique rotation, factors fixed at 2). Internal consistency for the full 9-item BFI was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.952; mean inter-item r = 0.692). The Severity subscale (Items 1–3) yielded α = 0.923 (mean inter-item r = 0.809); the Interference subscale (Items 4–9) yielded α = 0.940 (mean inter-item r = 0.726). All corrected item-total correlations exceeded 0.69 (range: 0.693–0.889), confirming adequate item discrimination. EFA yielded two factors explaining 82.0% of total item variance (Factor 1: eigenvalue = 6.553, 72.8%; Factor 2: eigenvalue = 0.829, 9.2%). After oblique rotation, severity items (1–3) loaded exclusively on Factor 1 (loadings: 0.916–0.953) and interference items (4–9) loaded exclusively on Factor 2 (loadings: 0.803–0.915), confirming the two-factor structure in this non-cancer population. AVE was computed manually from factor loadings: Severity AVE = 0.873 (√AVE = 0.934), Interference AVE = 0.773 (√AVE = 0.879). Both √AVE values exceeded the inter-subscale correlation (r = 0.783), supporting discriminant validity per the Fornell-Larcker criterion. These results confirm that the BFI retains acceptable reliability and construct validity in this college student population, while acknowledging that Item 6 (interference with walking ability) showed the lowest item-total correlation (r = 0.693) and may be less sensitive in this physically healthy demographic (Table S2).

Statistical analysis

Most statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0). Continuous variables were described as mean±standard deviation (SD) and median [interquartile range (IQR)]; categorical variables were presented as frequencies and proportions. Differences in mean BFI Total Scores across sociodemographic subgroups were assessed using independent samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to quantify the linear associations between ordinal lifestyle variables (sleep duration, physical activity frequency, smoking status, skipping breakfast, late-night eating) and BFI Total Score. Differences in the prevalence of perceived attributional factors across BFI severity groups (mild, moderate, severe) were evaluated using chi-square tests.

To identify independent predictors of BFI Total Score, a three-step hierarchical multiple linear regression was conducted. Regression analyses were restricted to four-year undergraduate students (Years 1–4; n = 2,873). Fifth-year (n = 62), master’s (n = 64), and doctoral (n = 15) students were excluded from regression models due to small sample sizes and substantially different BFI profiles from the main undergraduate sample; their descriptive data appear in Table 1. Missing data were minimal. For multi-item scales, within-scale mean imputation was applied when item-level missingness < 20%. Single-item categorical predictors were handled by listwise deletion. The final analytic sample for regression (n = 2,873) had complete data on all predictors. In Model 1, sociodemographic variables (sex and academic year) were entered. In Model 2, lifestyle factors (sleep duration, skipping breakfast, physical activity frequency, and late-night eating) were additionally included. In Model 3, prior disease history variables were further added as a block. Model fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R²) and adjusted R², and incremental explanatory variance (ΔR²) was reported for each model step. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess multicollinearity among predictors. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout. Comparison of OLS standard errors with heteroscedasticity-consistent HC3 standard errors for all Model 3 predictors. HC3 standard errors were computed using the statsmodels package in Python 3.12. Although smoking status was associated with higher BFI scores in univariate analysis (Table 4), it was not entered into hierarchical regression model. This decision was pre-specified based on two considerations: (1) the low prevalence of current smokers in this sample (n = 185, 6.1%) risked yielding an unstable coefficient with wide confidence intervals in a multivariable model; and (2) the primary analytical objective was to characterize modifiable dietary and sleep-related lifestyle determinants. Smoking status should be examined in future studies with larger proportions of smokers.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study sample

A total of 3,014 valid responses were analyzed. The majority of participants were female (n = 1,926; 63.9%), with males comprising 36.1% of the sample (n = 1,088). With respect to academic year, Freshmen (Year 1) accounted for the largest proportion (45.2%; n = 1,362), followed by Sophomores (26.5%; n = 800), Juniors (16.2%; n = 489), Seniors (7.4%; n = 222), fifth-year students (2.1%; n = 62), Master’s students (2.1%; n = 64), and Doctoral students (0.5%; n = 15). Regarding academic discipline, medical students constituted the most prevalent subgroup (29.7%), followed by engineering (26.0%), natural sciences (17.1%), arts and humanities (15.2%), fine arts (3.4%), and others (8.7%). The majority of participants were single (76.6%), with 20.4% in a relationship and 3.0% reporting uncertain relationship status. Full sociodemographic characteristics and corresponding BFI scores are presented in Table 1.

Statistically significant differences in mean BFI Total Scores were observed across academic year groups (p < 0.001) and major/discipline categories (p < 0.001). Sophomore students recorded the highest mean BFI score among undergraduate levels (4.35 ± 1.98), while Doctoral students showed the highest overall score (4.55 ± 2.71). Students in the fine arts major had the highest mean BFI among all discipline groups (4.42 ± 1.87). In contrast, sex (p = 0.624) and relationship status (p = 0.097) were not significantly associated with BFI Total Score.

Fatigue severity distribution

The overall mean BFI Total Score was 4.06 ± 2.01, with a median of 3.9 [IQR: 2.4–5.4] (Table 2; Fig. 2). The Severity Subscale (Items 1–3) yielded a mean of 4.62 ± 2.20, while the Interference Subscale (Items 4–9) had a mean of 3.78 ± 2.08, indicating that participants’ subjective perception of fatigue intensity was, on average, modestly higher than the degree of functional interference reported. Among individual items, Item 3 (worst fatigue in the past 24 h) recorded the highest mean score (5.18 ± 2.61), whereas Item 6 (interference with walking ability) had the lowest (2.96 ± 2.20), suggesting that the impact of fatigue on physical mobility was relatively limited compared to its emotional and cognitive consequences.

Distribution of BFI total scores

Based on BFI Total Score cutoff classifications, 1,536 participants (51.0%) were categorized as experiencing mild fatigue (BFI < 4), 953 (31.6%) as moderate fatigue (BFI 4–6), and 525 (17.4%) as severe fatigue (BFI > 6). Collectively, these figures indicate that approximately 49.0% of the surveyed student population experienced clinically meaningful fatigue (moderate or severe), underscoring the high burden of fatigue in this demographic.

Fatigue frequency and co-occurring symptoms

With regard to fatigue frequency over the preceding six months, 12.5% of participants reported experiencing fatigue almost every day, 11.7% reported it 3–5 times per week, and 16.0% reported it 1–2 times per week. The largest subgroup (47.0%) endorsed occasional fatigue, while 12.9% reported never experiencing fatigue. Taken together, approximately 87.1% of respondents reported fatigue at some frequency, with 24.2% experiencing it three or more times per week (Table 3).

Co-occurring symptoms and fatigue frequency distribution (n = 3,014)

Among co-occurring symptoms assessed via multi-select reporting (Fig. 3), difficulty concentrating was the most prevalent, endorsed by 60.3% of participants (n = 1,818), followed by low mood (56.6%; n = 1,705), dizziness (37.9%; n = 1,141), insomnia (32.2%; n = 972), and reduced appetite (32.0%; n = 963). Somatic symptoms were less commonly reported: muscle aches were endorsed by 19.3% of participants, thirst by 19.0%, dyspnea by 8.1%, and chest pain by 6.8%. The predominance of neurocognitive and affective co-occurring symptoms—relative to somatic and cardiorespiratory ones—points to a distinctive symptom cluster in college-student fatigue that may reflect psychosocial and cognitive etiologies.

Associations between lifestyle factors and BFI scores

Table 4 presents univariate associations between lifestyle factors and BFI Total Score. Sleep duration demonstrated the strongest negative correlation with fatigue severity (r = − 0.195, p < 0.001). Participants sleeping fewer than 5 h per night recorded the highest mean BFI (5.09 ± 2.31), compared to those sleeping 5–7 h (4.38 ± 1.99), 7–8 h (3.61 ± 1.88), and more than 8 h (3.58 ± 2.13), reflecting a clear dose-response gradient in which longer sleep duration was associated with lower fatigue (Fig. 4).

BFI Score by sleep duration

Late-night eating was associated with significantly higher BFI scores (Yes: 4.52 ± 2.15 vs. No: 3.97 ± 1.98; r = 0.132, p < 0.001), as was skipping breakfast (Yes: 4.36 ± 2.04 vs. No: 3.89 ± 1.98; r = 0.114, p < 0.001). Current smokers reported notably higher BFI scores than non-smokers (4.83 ± 2.42 vs. 4.01 ± 1.97; r = 0.108, p < 0.001). Physical activity frequency showed an inverse association with BFI (r = − 0.097, p < 0.001), with participants who never exercised recording the highest mean score (4.54 ± 2.23) and those exercising five or more times per week the lowest (3.74 ± 2.06).

Perceived attributional factors by fatigue severity

Table 5 displays the proportions of participants attributing their fatigue to various factors, stratified by BFI severity group. Academic pressure was the most commonly cited attributional factor across all severity levels, with endorsement rising significantly from 70.2% in the mild group to 77.9% in the moderate group and 85.0% in the severe group (p < 0.001). Sleep insufficiency was the second most frequently cited factor overall (68.6%, 74.5%, and 75.8% across mild, moderate, and severe groups respectively; p < 0.001). Emotional problems (anxiety/depression) showed the steepest gradient across severity levels, increasing from 43.5% (mild) to 60.4% (moderate) and 75.8% (severe; p < 0.001), suggesting a particularly strong association between psychological distress and higher fatigue burden.

This model explained 1.7% of the variance in BFI Total Score (R²= 0.017).

The addition of lifestyle factors in Model 2 substantially improved model fit, with an incremental R² of 0.065 (ΔR²= 0.065, total R²= 0.082). Shorter sleep duration emerged as a strong predictor: sleeping less than 5 h per night (vs. 7–8 h; β = 1.047; 95% CI: 0.606–1.489; p < 0.001) and sleeping 5–7 h (vs. 7–8 h; β = 0.740; 95% CI: 0.589–0.891; p < 0.001) were both significantly associated with higher fatigue scores, whereas sleeping more than 8 h showed no significant association (β=−0.176; p = 0.265). Late-night eating was also a significant independent predictor (β = 0.536; 95% CI: 0.353–0.718; p < 0.001), followed by skipping breakfast (β = 0.262; 95% CI: 0.109–0.414; p < 0.001). Regarding physical activity, exercising 1–2 times per week (vs. never; β=−0.314; p = 0.003) and exercising 5 or more times per week (vs. never; β=−0.399; p = 0.015) were significant negative predictors; exercising 3–4 times per week showed a borderline association (β=−0.254; p = 0.050). Sex remained non-significant in this model (β=−0.078; p = 0.321). Sophomore year (β = 0.491; p < 0.001) and Junior/Senior year (β = 0.387; p < 0.001) remained significant.

In Model 3, prior disease history was further incorporated (total R²= 0.133; ΔR²= 0.051). Among disease-related variables, a self-reported history of anxiety disorder was the strongest predictor (β = 1.218; 95% CI: 0.969–1.466; p < 0.001), followed by heart disease (β = 0.896; 95% CI: 0.326–1.467; p = 0.002), gastrointestinal disease (β = 0.643; 95% CI: 0.349–0.937; p < 0.001), and asthma (β = 0.652; 95% CI: 0.013–1.291; p = 0.046). A history of other diseases was negatively associated with BFI Total Score (β=−0.328; 95% CI: -0.560 to -0.095; p = 0.006). In contrast, histories of hypertension (p = 0.621), diabetes mellitus (p = 0.652), hyperthyroidism (p = 0.244), hypothyroidism (p = 0.289), and tuberculosis (p = 0.529) were not independently associated with BFI Total Score after adjustment. The sequential inclusion of lifestyle factors and disease history collectively accounted for approximately 12% additional variance beyond sociodemographic factors alone (ΔR²=0.065 + 0.051 = 0.116), highlighting the multidimensional nature of fatigue determinants in the college student population.

Discussion

This nationwide study documents a high and clinically meaningful burden of fatigue among Chinese college students. Nearly half the surveyed population (49.0%) experienced moderate or severe fatigue, and 87.1% reported fatigue at some frequency over the preceding six months. The co-occurring symptom profile was dominated by difficulty concentrating (60.3%) and low mood (56.6%). This pattern indicates a predominantly cognitive-affective fatigue phenotype, consistent with central rather than peripheral etiology. Hierarchical regression identified anxiety disorder history as the single strongest independent predictor (β = 1.218), followed by sleep duration and dietary timing behaviors. Modifiable lifestyle and clinical factors together explained 11.6% additional variance beyond sociodemographic characteristics alone (total R²= 0.133). Sex was not significantly associated with BFI Total Score in any analytic model. These findings are discussed below in relation to existing evidence, with implications for clinical practice and campus public health policy.

Viewed through the lens of the Self-Regulatory Resource Depletion (SRRD) framework [21], the multidomain risk factor profile identified here reflects a coherent psychophysiological cascade rather than a collection of independent predictors. Students with high trait anxiety, consistent with the anxiety disorder history identified as the dominant predictor in Model 3 (β = 1.218), expend disproportionate prefrontal regulatory resources on ruminative suppression and anticipatory worry, accelerating cognitive resource depletion resources on ruminative suppression and anticipatory worry, accelerating cognitive resource depletion throughout the day. Compensatory nocturnal behaviors, including late-night eating and delayed sleep onset, further curtail the overnight replenishment of these resources through circadian misalignment and sleep architecture disruption. The resulting resource-depletion state manifests clinically as the two cardinal co-occurring symptoms documented here: difficulty concentrating (60.3%) and low mood (56.6%), which together define the cognitive-affective fatigue phenotype. This cascade is also consistent with Cognitive Load Theory [22], which posits that when total intrinsic and extraneous cognitive demand exceeds available working memory capacity, processing efficiency deteriorates and perceived fatigue escalates. This integrative framework elevated the present findings from phenomenological description to mechanistic explanation: reducing anxiety-driven resource depletion is the highest-leverage intervention target, and restoring adequate overnight resource replenishment through sleep is the primary restorative mechanism. Dietary timing behaviors serve as a modifiable upstream determinant of sleep quality, operating through chrono-nutritional pathways that are accessible to behavioral intervention.

Prevalence in context

Population-based epidemiological surveys in the United Kingdom have documented that approximately 18.3% of community-dwelling adults aged 18–45 years report clinically significant fatigue using the Chalder Fatigue Questionnaire [23], and studies of academic burnout in European university students from Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands document that emotional exhaustion, the dimension most conceptually proximate to fatigue, is prevalent across Western tertiary education contexts [24]. While these findings use different instruments and populations, they collectively indicate that energy depletion in university and community settings is not a phenomenon unique to Chinese students. The finding that 49.0% of Chinese college students in our sample met the moderate-to-severe BFI threshold nonetheless represents a substantial burden relative to general adult community norms, and cross-national benchmarking using methodologically harmonized protocols remains a priority for future research. This difference likely reflects the interaction of global student health trends with China-specific structural factors. The gaokao-centered educational model sustains intense competitive pressure into and through university, while sociocultural norms around academic achievement may reduce the perceived legitimacy of fatigue as a concern warranting help-seeking. Analogous patterns have been documented in South Korea, Japan, and India [25], suggesting that the Chinese experience represents a high-pressure examination culture with particular structural fatigue determinants. Fatigue is a core manifestation of psychological distress among college students. From a public health perspective, the marked gap between the high prevalence of psychological distress and the notably low rates of professional help-seeking among Chinese college students underscores the inadequacy of passive service-delivery models. In the present sample, 91.6% of students with severe fatigue (BFI Total Score ≥ 7.00) did not seek medical care in the preceding six months. This figure consistent with internationally documented patterns of low treatment utilization among university students with mental health problems [26]. This within-sample finding provides a university-student-specific estimate of unmet need, complementing the broader population-level evidence that help-seeking for health concerns remains low in the Chinese adult context [27]. The substantial gap between objective fatigue burden and help-seeking behavior observed here underscores the case for proactive case-finding within routine campus health consultations, rather than reliance on self-referral.

The cognitive-affective phenotype: implications for assessment and surveillance

Difficulty concentrating and low mood ranked far above somatic complaints such as dyspnea (8.1%) and chest pain (6.8%). This predominance of cognitive and affective co-occurring symptoms defines a fatigue phenotype that is functionally distinct from that observed in medically ill populations. This central fatigue pattern directly impairs academic engagement, learning efficacy, and occupational competency development during critical period of human capital formation [2, 10]. At the population level, a large proportion of enrolled students operating with substantially reduced cognitive resources represents a systemic inefficiency, reducing the educational return on substantial social investment in tertiary education.

Existing student health surveillance systems in China and most other countries rely predominantly on self-referral to campus health or counseling services—a passive approach that systematically underdetects burden in populations with high stigma and low help-seeking [28, 29]. Integrating brief validated fatigue screening into routine annual student health examinations would provide population-level surveillance data while creating a non-stigmatizing entry point for identifying students at risk. Experience from universal mental health screening in school-age populations shows increased early identification and reduced treatment delay [29]. Adapting similar approaches to tertiary education is an evidence-consistent policy translation. Such adaptation should be calibrated to the cognitive-affective phenotype documented.

Sleep deprivation as a population-level exposure: from individual behavior to institutional determinants

Sleep duration was the strongest modifiable lifestyle predictor in both univariate and hierarchical regression. In Model 2, sleeping fewer than 5 h per night (vs.7–8 h reference) was associated with a BFI increase of 1.047 points, and sleeping 5–7 h was associated with an increase of 0.740 points. Sleeping more than 8 h was not significantly associated with BFI score (β=-0.176; p = 0.265). The dose-response gradient was clinically significant: mean BFI score decreased from 5.09 ± 2.31 in students sleeping fewer than 5 h per night to 3.58 ± 2.13 in those sleeping more than 8 h. This difference spans a full fatigue severity category. This gradient aligns with experimental deficits [15], and with population surveillance data consistently documenting insufficient sleep (fewer than 7 h) in 50–60% of college students across multiple countries [16, 30]. In the present sample, 56.1% of students (n = 1,689) reported sleeping seven hours or fewer per night, including 2.9% (n = 86) sleeping fewer than five hours. This figure is consistent with Chinese university surveys and indicates a population-level shortfall relative to the 7–9 h recommended for young adults aged 18–25 years [31]. Academic stress, despite being the leading perceived cause of fatigue (Table 5), was not entered into regression because the survey item captured post-hoc causal attribution rather than a validated exposure. Its effects likely operate through the measured mediators of sleep and anxiety, which were already included in the models.

This shortfall cannot be attributed solely to individual behavioral choices. Early mandatory class scheduling, concentrated examination periods, dormitory noise and light conditions, and the competitive social norm of late-night studying are institutional and environmental determinants of sleep that individual behavioral counseling can not fully overcome [32]. Evidence that delaying university start times substantially increases sleep duration and improves academic performance has accumulated sufficiently to inform policy action in several countries [33–36]. In the Chinese university context, scheduling reform that delays first-period classes represents a structural intervention with the potential to shift sleep duration for the entire student population. The present data quantify the population attributable burden of insufficient sleep and provide the empirical basis for advocating such upstream policy levers.

Critically, structural scheduling reform must be implemented alongside evidence-based cognitive-behavioral skill training to yield meaningfu sleep benefits. Providing sleep opportunity without addressing the anxiety-driven cognitive hyperarousal that delays sleep onset will produce suboptimal results. Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I), which directly targets sleep-interfering cognitions, conditioned hyperarousal, and behavioral patterns, has demonstrated efficacy in college student populations and can be delivered in group or fully digital formats at population scale [37]. Students with highest anxiety disorder burden are precisely those most likely to experience ruminative bedtime procrastination regardless of scheduling changes. An institutional intervention package that combines delayed class start times with universally accessible brief CBT-I, as has been piloted in several university settings [38], represents the minimum required dual-component approach for meaningfully shifting the population sleep duration distribution.

Dietary behavior as a circadian health issue: contextualizing findings in modern urban campus life

Breakfast skipping (β = 0.262, p < 0.001) and late-night eating (β = 0.536, p < 0.001) were significant independent predictors of fatigue after full adjustment, including for sleep duration. This confirms that these dietary timing effects on fatigue are independent of sleep quantity. Late-night food intake desynchronizes peripheral metabolic clocks from the central suprachiasmatic nucleus, impairing slow-wave sleep depth and the cortisol awakening response. Breakfast omission disrupts morning glycemic and monoaminergic restoration of prefrontal arousal tone [17]. These are well-characterized chrono-biological pathways with documented epidemiological consequences.

The public health context of these findings warrants attention. The rapid expansion of on-demand food delivery platforms in China has restructured dietary timing in the college environment: 24-hour delivery access structurally facilitates late-night consumption rather than requiring active effort or planning [18]. This shifts the analytical frame from individual dietary choice to commercial and environmental determinants of eating behavior. In the clinical and campus health context, these dietary behaviors are best addressed as behavioral counseling targets anchored to students’ own fatigue goals, rather than as prescriptive dietary regimens that ignore the structural realities of campus food environments.

The primacy of psychological distress: fatigue as an entry point for mental health identification

Anxiety disorder history was the single strongest predictor of BFI Total Score across all analytic models (β = 1.218, 95% CI: 0.969–1.466; p < 0.001). Its effect exceeded that of all lifestyle variables combined, positioning psychological distress as the primary pathogenic driver of severe fatigue in this population. This is consistent with the transdiagnostic literature characterizing fatigue as a shared mechanistic outcome of sustained sympathoadrenal and HPA-axis dysregulation, monoaminergic depletion, and sleep architecture disruption in anxiety and depressive disorders [8]. The steep cross-severity gradient of emotional symptom endorsement, rising from 43.5% in mild to 75.8% in severe fatigue (p < 0.001), establishes emotional distress as the most sensitive marker of fatigue escalation. This gradient is detectable within a brief clinical assessment encounter.

These findings have direct implications for service delivery. In China, university counseling services remain substantially under-resourced relative to demand: student-to-counselor ratios commonly exceed 1000:1, and utilization is limited by persistent stigma associated with direct mental health help-seeking [12, 28]. Campus general practice and student health clinics carry lower access thresholds and therefore represent strategically important settings for mental health identification. Somatic presentations including fatigue may serve as a culturally more acceptable entry point than explicit disclosure of depression or anxiety. Chinese psychiatric outpatients have been shown to report significantly more somatic symptoms than their Euro-Canadian counterparts [39], suggesting a cultural preference for somatic over psychological framing of distress. In the broader Chinese adult population, low mental health help-seeking rates have been documented even among those with diagnosed mental disorders [40]. While direct evidence from Chinese university students specifically is limited, our finding that only 8.4% of students with severe fatigue sought medical care is consistent with these documented barriers. Training campus health clinicians to use fatigue consultations as opportunities for psychological screening with validated instruments such as the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 [41, 42] does not require additional infrastructure. It requires a reorientation of the fatigue consultation toward population-level case-finding, applicable wherever campus general practice services exist.

A practical translation of these findings into clinical practice can be illustrated through a Motivational Interviewing (MI)- informed consultation script [43]. When a student presents with fatigue as the chief complaint, the clinician might open: “ You mentioned feeling exhausted and having trouble concentrating that sounds really frustrating. On a scale of 0 to 10, how much is this affecting your daily life? ” After the student responds, the clinician bridges: “ It sounds like this is significantly disrupting your studies. Sometimes when people feel this kind of persistent exhaustion, it can be connected to how they are managing stress or worry. Would it be okay if I asked you a few quick questions to see if anything like that might be contributing? ” This MI-consistent approach, eliciting permission, affirming experience, and naturalizing the transition to psychological inquiry, reduces stigma-related resistance and facilitates administration of validated screening tools (GAD-7, PHQ-9) within the same fatigue consultation, without requiring referral to a separate mental health service.

Internationally, integrated collaborative care models embedding psychological screening within primary care have demonstrated effectiveness in improving mental health identification rates and treatment uptake [44]. Adapting such models to the university campus setting, using fatigue as the sentinel symptom that activates a structured psychological assessment pathway, represents a resource-proportionate approach to bridging the mental health treatment gap in higher education. The present study’s quantification of the anxiety-fatigue association provides the empirical justification for prioritizing psychological assessment over organic investigation in the majority of fatigued college students.

Organic comorbidities: calibrating clinical investigation in a low-prevalence context

In Model 3, heart disease (β = 0.896; 95% CI: 0.326–1.467; p = 0.002), gastrointestinal disease (β = 0.643; 95% CI: 0.349–0.937; p < 0.001), and asthma (β = 0.652; 95% CI: 0.013–1.291; p = 0.046) were significant independent predictors of higher BFI scores. These associations are consistent with established pathophysiology: cardiac conditions contribute to fatigue through impaired exercise capacity and autonomic dysregulation; gastrointestinal disorders operate through gut-brain axis pathways including systemic low-grade inflammation and serotonin dysregulation [45]; and asthma-related nocturnal bronchospasm disrupts sleep architecture with consequent daytime fatigue.

Notably, thyroid disorders were not independently associated with fatigue severity after full adjustment: hyperthyroidism (β = 0.516; p = 0.244) and hypothyroidism (β=−0.580; p = 0.289) both failed to reach statistical significance [46]. This finding primarily reflects the small subgroup sizes (hyperthyroidism n = 20; hypothyroidism n = 13) and the low prevalence of thyroid disease in the college-aged population, rather than an absence of pathophysiological relationship. Similarly, the non-significant variation in chronic disease attribution across BFI severity groups (p = 0.139) is epidemiologically expected given the low overall chronic disease burden of this demographic.

Collectively, these findings support a calibrated clinical approach: behavioral and psychological interventions should be first-line management for the majority of fatigued college students, while targeted investigation for specific organic conditions, particularly heart disease, gastrointestinal disorders, and asthma, is warranted when clinical features suggest these diagnoses or when fatigue is refractory to psychosocial and lifestyle-focused management.

Toward a multilevel public health response: from campus to policy

The multidomain risk factor profile identified in this study maps onto a multilevel public health intervention framework. At the individual level, campus health clinicians can implement a tiered assessment protocol: lifestyle inquiry and simultaneous psychological screening (GAD-7, PHQ-9) at first contact; brief behavioral counseling for sleep regularization and dietary timing as first-line intervention; and targeted organic investigation for refractory presentations. At the institutional level, universities can implement structural changes including scheduling reform to protect sleep, expanded access to CBT-I formats, and integration of mental health screening into routine health examinations. These measures shift the determinant distribution for the entire student population rather than relying solely on individual help-seeking. At the policy level, the burden data from this study contribute to the evidence base for national student wellness standards specifying minimum campus mental health provision relative to enrollment size [12].

Globally, the findings align with an expanding consensus that student mental health requires systemic, policy-driven responses. The Lancet Commission on Global Mental Health and Sustainable Development has identified young people aged 14–24 as the group in whom the mental health treatment gap carries the greatest consequences for human capital development [47]. Student fatigue driven by the anxiety-sleep-dietary behavior cluster identified here represent a measurable, modifiable, and highly prevalent manifestation of this gap in the tertiary education context. Addressing it effectively requires multilevel action: clinical protocols that integrate psychological screening into fatigue consultations; institutional policies that protect sleep and create accessible, low-stigma mental health pathways; and national frameworks that treat student well-being as a systemic policy priority.

Strengths, limitations, and future research directions

This study’s principal strengths include its large, geographically diverse sample spanning 30 Chinese provinces, the use of a validated multidimensional fatigue instrument (BFI) with both severity and interference dimensions, rigorous response quality control, and a hierarchical regression design enabling systematic decomposition of sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical variance contributions. The finding that lifestyle and disease factors explained an additional 11.6% of BFI variance beyond sociodemographic characteristics (total R²= 0.133) underscores the necessity of multidomain assessment and provides the empirical basis for a risk-stratified clinical and public health response. Furthermore, the use of HC3 heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors confirmed the robustness of ordinary least squares (OLS) inference; the maximum difference in standard errors or coefficients across all predictors was only ± 0.003, indicating that heteroscedasticity did not meaningfully affect the conclusions (Table S5).

Several limitations constrain interpretation. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; in particular, the sleep–fatigue and anxiety–fatigue relationships are plausibly bidirectional, and all associations reflect statistical correlation rather than established temporal precedence. All measures were self-reported, introducing potential recall and social desirability bias; objective sleep assessment (actigraphy), dietary measurement, and biological markers were not available. Online WeChat-based recruitment, while enabling nationwide reach, may have underrepresented students with the most severe impairment or limited digital access. Beyond this, the sample exhibited compositional imbalances: freshmen (45.2%), medical students (29.7%), and female students (63.9%) were overrepresented, and three provinces accounted for 56.9% of the sample, indicating substantial provincial skew. Important contextual determinants including academic workload intensity, financial stress, social support quality, and residential environment were not captured. Academic major and geographic region were not included in regression models; future studies should employ multilevel modelling or cluster-robust standard errors to account for university-level clustering and intraclass correlations. Geographic and institutional heterogeneity may represent important sources of variation not captured in the present analysis. The BFI was originally developed and validated in oncology populations [9]; although it has been applied in non-cancer settings [19, 20], its severity cutoff thresholds have not been specifically calibrated for healthy young adult populations, and findings based on these thresholds should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The BFI measures fatigue as a two-factor construct; however, fatigue shares variance with anxiety and depressive symptoms, and the present study did not conduct bifactor modelling to formally separate fatigue-specific from general internalising distress. Future studies should apply higher-order confirmatory factor models to quantify this overlap. Furthermore, while the current sample of 3,014 satisfies the pre-specified statistical power requirements for the primary prevalence and regression objectives, it remains insufficient for robust province-level subgroup analysis. Future nationwide surveys should target more than 10,000 participants to permit reliable subnational disaggregation. Dietary timing behaviors (breakfast skipping and late-night eating) were operationalized as binary yes/no variables, which forfeits information about frequency, timing, and caloric content; this binary classification likely underestimates the true dose-response association between chronic dietary timing irregularity and fatigue severity, and future studies should employ validated chrono-nutritional instruments to more precisely quantify these effects. Physical activity was assessed with a single frequency item (moderate-intensity only), with no data on vigorous/light activity, sedentary behavior, or total volume. This simplified measure is subject to recall bias and lacks objective validation, likely obscuring dose-response relationships between activity patterns and fatigue. Future studies should use validated instruments. Although BFI demonstrated excellent overall internal consistency in the present sample (α = 0.952 ), item-level analysis revealed that Item 6 (interference with walking ability) exhibited a relative floor effect (mean = 2.96 ± 2.20) and the lowest corrected item-total correlation (r = 0.693); this item may be less sensitive in a physically healthy college population, and future instrument adaptations for this demographic should consider replacing it with an item assessing cognitive or motivational interference. The study did not collect qualitative data on how students understand, interpret, or evaluate their fatigue experiences; whether participants viewed fatigue as problematic, normative, or an acceptable academic cost cannot be determined from BFI scores alone. Future research should complement quantitative prevalence studies with phenomenological inquiry into student fatigue narratives.

Future research should address these limitations through longitudinal cohort designs that map fatigue trajectories across the academic year and establish temporal relationships among key predictors. Multi-country comparative studies, including institutions across East Asia, South Asia, and lower-income settings, would clarify the extent to which the determinant profile identified here reflects China-specific structural factors versus generalizable features of high-pressure educational environments. Randomized controlled trials testing tiered fatigue assessment and intervention models within campus general practice settings, with mental health and academic outcomes as endpoints, are needed to establish the clinical and economic effectiveness of the screening-and-referral approach supported by the present findings. Finally, the development of fatigue-specific surveillance indicators that can be integrated into national student health monitoring systems would enable longitudinal tracking of population-level fatigue burden and evaluation of policy impacts over time.

Conclusions

This nationwide study establishes that clinically meaningful fatigue is highly prevalent among Chinese college students, affecting nearly half the surveyed population. Fatigue presents a predominantly cognitive-affective phenotype reflecting psychosocial and lifestyle-driven central fatigue rather than organic somatic disease. The associated factors of fatigue follow a clear and actionable hierarchy: anxiety disorder history exerts the largest independent effect, followed by sleep duration operating across a dose-response gradient, and dietary timing behaviors mediated by circadian disruption rather than simple caloric mechanisms. The non-significant association between chronic disease attribution and fatigue severity appropriately reflects the low chronic disease prevalence of this demographic, reinforcing the case for prioritizing behavioral and psychological responses over organic investigation in most presentations. Situated within the global context of a growing mental health crisis in tertiary education, these findings position student fatigue as a measurable public health signal and an underutilized entry point for campus-based mental health identification. Addressing student fatigue effectively requires multilevel responses: clinical protocols that integrate psychological screening into fatigue consultations; institutional policies that protect sleep and create accessible, low-stigma mental health pathways; and national framework that treat student well-being as a systemic policy priority rather than an individual responsibility.
