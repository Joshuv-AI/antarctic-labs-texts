Methylenetetrahydrofolate Reductase C677T and A1298C Polymorphisms are Not Associated With Recurrent Aphthous Stomatitis: A Case-Control Study
Authors: Chiara Magrì, Alessia Vezzosi, Angela Vinella, Maria Addolorata Mariggiò, Domenico De Falco, Massimo Petruzzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesMethylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) polymorphisms have been investigated as potential genetic factors associated with recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS). This study investigated whether MTHFR C677T and A1298C polymorphisms, as well as circulating homocysteine, folate, and vitamin B12 levels, are associated with RAS.Materials and methodsIn this prospective case-control study, 55 patients with clinically diagnosed RAS and 55 age- and sex-matched controls were enrolled. MTHFR C677T and A1298C genotyping was performed by polymerase chain reaction on genomic DNA extracted from EDTA blood samples. Plasma homocysteine was measured by chemiluminescent immunoassay, while serum folate and vitamin B12 were assessed by electrochemiluminescence. In patients with RAS, number of ulcers, clinical subtype, episode frequency, healing time, and pain intensity were recorded. Associations were analysed using χ² tests and ANOVA, with statistical significance set at P < .05.ResultsNo significant differences were found between RAS and non-RAS groups in the distribution of MTHFR C677T or A1298C genotypes. Mean homocysteine, folate, and vitamin B12 levels, as well as their categorical distributions, were also comparable between groups (all P > .05). Within the RAS group, episode frequency was significantly associated with homocysteine and folate serological levels, whereas no significant associations were observed between MTHFR genotypes and the clinical variables assessed.ConclusionsIn this cohort, MTHFR C677T and A1298C polymorphisms were not associated with RAS occurrence. However, homocysteine and folate serological levels were associated with selected indicators of disease course, suggesting that metabolic factors may contribute to RAS phenotype in a subset of patients.Clinical relevanceAlthough MTHFR polymorphisms do not appear to be directly associated with RAS onset, assessment of homocysteine and folate serological levels may help identify patients with a more severe or recurrent clinical course and support a more individualized approach.Key words: Recurrent aphthous stomatitis, MTHFR, Homocysteine, Folate, Vitamin B12, Oral ulceration

Introduction

The 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) gene, located on chromosome 1, encodes an enzyme that plays a pivotal role in folate metabolism and is essential for DNA, RNA, and protein methylation.1,2 The MTHFR gene is known to harbour several rare mutations associated with varying degrees of enzymatic deficiency; among these, the most common allelic variants are C677T and A1298C.1,2 The MTHFR C677T polymorphism results from a cytosine to thymine substitution at nucleotide position 677, leading to the replacement of alanine with valine in the enzyme structure. This amino acid substitution is associated with a reduction in enzymatic activity of up to 50% in homozygous individuals, resulting in moderately elevated plasma homocysteine levels, particularly in the presence of inadequate folate intake.3 Heterozygous carriers typically exhibit mildly increased homocysteine concentrations compared with individuals without the mutation.2 The MTHFR A1298C polymorphism occurs in exon 7 and leads to the substitution of glutamate with alanine at codon 429, resulting in a modest reduction in enzyme activity.4 Although this variant is associated with decreased MTHFR function, its impact is generally less pronounced than that of the C677T polymorphism. Consequently, its effect on homocysteine levels is usually mild and becomes clinically relevant primarily in individuals who are compound heterozygotes for both mutations and have suboptimal nutritional status. The prevalence of the A1298C allelic variant is less extensively documented than that of C677T.5 Individuals who are heterozygous for both the C677T and A1298C polymorphisms, representing approximately 15% of the population, exhibit residual enzyme activity of approximately 50% to 60%, which is lower than that observed in single heterozygotes for the C677T variant. To date, no individuals homozygous for both mutations have been identified.5 Numerous studies have demonstrated an inverse relationship between plasma homocysteine concentrations and folate levels, suggesting that hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy) may serve as a reliable marker of inadequate folate intake or absorption. Folates play a crucial role in homocysteine metabolism by acting as methyl donors in the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine, a reaction catalysed by methionine synthase, which requires vitamin B12 as an essential cofactor.3,6 MTHFR polymorphisms have been associated with an increased risk of several systemic conditions, including breast cancer, gliomas, and gastric cancer.4

HHcy has also emerged as a risk factor for cardiovascular diseases and congenital anomalies, such as congenital heart defects, cleft lip and palate, and adverse pregnancy outcomes.3,7 Furthermore, a well-established association has been reported between the MTHFR C677T polymorphism and immune-mediated disorders, including Behçet’s disease, celiac disease, Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, and familial Mediterranean fever.8 Elevated homocysteine levels, often associated with reduced folate and vitamin B12 concentrations, have also been implicated in the pathogenesis of recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS). Folate deficiency may impair oral mucosal trophism, thereby increasing susceptibility to RAS.9,10

In this context, the combined assessment of MTHFR gene polymorphisms and related biochemical parameters may provide clinically relevant insights into individual susceptibility and disease expression. MTHFR gene polymorphisms may represent one of several factors potentially involved in the clinical expression of RAS.8

To date, only one study has reported a significant association between the MTHFR C677T polymorphism and RAS, specifically in relation to the number of oral ulcers.8 However, that study did not investigate other MTHFR allelic variants, nor did it include a concurrent assessment of serum folate and vitamin B12 levels.8 As a result, the potential role of combined genetic and metabolic profiling in RAS remains largely unexplored. The primary objective of the present study was to compare the frequencies of the MTHFR A1298C and C677T polymorphisms between individuals with RAS and a healthy non-RAS group. Secondary objectives were to evaluate serum levels of homocysteine, folic acid, and vitamin B12 in relation to MTHFR polymorphisms and to investigate potential correlations between these biochemical parameters, genetic variants, and the clinical characteristics of RAS with the aim of contributing to a more personalized and biologically informed approach to the management of this condition.

Materials and methods

Patient recruitment and ethics

This case–control study was conducted between November 2023 and November 2025 at the Oral Pathology and Medicine Unit of the University Hospital of Bari, Italy, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association (2004). The study was approved by the Independent Ethics Committee of the Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria Consorziale Policlinico di Bari (authorization number 7268). The study adhered to the STROBE guidelines for observational studies (Supplementary 1).

Consecutive patients attending for the first time with a clinical diagnosis of RAS, established by a specialist in Oral Pathology and Medicine (MP) based on a detailed medical history and clinical features as described by Porter et al,11 were enrolled in the study group. Additional inclusion criteria were the absence of other systemic diseases, no use of medications, supplements, or dietary supplements in the preceding month, provision of written informed consent, and adherence to scheduled follow-up visits. Patients without aphthous ulcers, as well as those who were pregnant, lactating, or noncompliant, were excluded from the study group. The control group consisted of individuals without RAS (non-RAS), comparable for sex and age. Control participants were randomly recruited among healthy patients scheduled for different dental services, such as third molar extraction for orthodontic reasons, patients undergoing routine oral hygiene procedures, patients with Class I dental caries, and healthy volunteer students. The sample size was calculated based on previously reported data on the prevalence of MTHFR gene polymorphisms in the general population and on the expected difference in their frequency between patients with RAS and healthy non-RAS group, as reported in genetic association studies on MTHFR C677T and A1298C polymorphisms,11, 12, 13 assuming a significance level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%.

MTHFR gene polymorphisms and biochemical analysis

For all subjects, clinical assessment and sample collection were performed at a subsequent follow-up visit when oral ulcers were present. Molecular analyses on venous blood samples were collected in EDTA-K3 tubes. Genomic DNA was extracted from 25 µL of venous blood using a commercial DNA extraction kit (Nuclear Laser Medicine Srl). The C677T and A1298C polymorphisms of the MTHFR gene were identified by polymerase chain reaction amplification of the target genomic regions using AA901 commercial kits (Nuclear Laser Medicine Srl).

Plasma homocysteine levels were measured using a direct chemiluminescent immunoassay (Atellica IM Analyzer, Siemens Healthineers). Homocysteine concentration values were classified as normal (5-15 µmol/L), slight increased (15-30 µmol/L), moderate increased (30-100 µmol/L), or high increased (>100 µmol/L).14

Serum vitamin B12 and folic acid levels were determined by electrochemiluminescence according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Roche Diagnostics). Vitamin B12 levels were categorized as normal (>300 pg/mL), borderline (200-300 pg/mL), or deficient (<200 pg/mL). Folic acid levels were classified as normal (>4 ng/mL), borderline (2-4 ng/mL), or deficient (<2 ng/mL).15

Clinical assessment of RAS

All enrolled RAS patients underwent a comprehensive extraoral and intraoral examination. Clinical data related to RAS were recorded, including: (1) number of aphthous ulcers (1, 2, 3, or ≥4); (2) RAS subtype (minor, major, or herpetiform); (3) frequency of RAS episodes (number of episodes per week); (4) healing time (expressed in days); and (5) pain intensity assessed using a visual analogue scale (VAS) ranging from 0 to 10.

Statistical analysis

The chi-square (χ²) test was used to assess Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium for genotype distributions. Associations between the MTHFR C677T and A1298C polymorphisms and categorical demographic or clinical variables were evaluated using the χ² test. Relationships between MTHFR polymorphisms and continuous clinical variables were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Correlations between clinical characteristics and biochemical parameters (homocysteine, folic acid, and vitamin B12 levels) were also analysed using appropriate χ² tests. Associations between MTHFR polymorphisms (C677T and A1298C) and biochemical markers were evaluated using ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Results

A total of 110 patients were enrolled: 55 patients received a diagnosis of RAS, while the remaining 55 healthy patients represented the non-RAS group. The mean age was 46 ± 20 years, male:female ratio was 40:70. In the RAS group, 36 patients were female, and the mean age was 41 ± 17 years. Expression of MTHFR C677T and MTHFR A1298C genes in RAS group and non-RAS group did not show any significant differences (as reported in Table 1).

In the comparison between the RAS group (n = 55) and the non-RAS group (n = 55), no statistically significant differences were observed in the main serological parameters assessed (as reported in Table 2). For homocysteine, mean concentrations were 13.31 ± 11.14 µmol/L in the RAS group and 15.67 ± 18.79 µmol/L in the non-RAS group, with no significant between-group difference (P = .21). The patient distribution across homocysteine level categories was also similar between groups (P = .20): most participants had normal values in both groups (47/55 in RAS vs 40/55 in non-RAS group), while mildly increased values were more frequent in the non-RAS group (12 vs 5). Vitamin B12 levels tended to be higher in the RAS group (474.55 ± 233.7 pg/mL) than in non-RAS group (350.84 ± 122.55 pg/mL), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .06). Likewise, the distribution of vitamin B12 serological level did not differ significantly between groups (P = .34). Folic acid levels were comparable between the RAS group (5.64 ± 3.21 ng/mL) and the non-RAS group (5.35 ± 3.40 ng/mL), with no significant difference (P = .31). Similarly, the distribution of folate serological level showed no statistically significant between-group differences (P = .68).

Among patients with RAS, mean homocysteine, vitamin B12, and folic acid levels did not differ significantly across MTHFR C677T genotypes (Table 3). Mean homocysteine concentrations were 9.40 ± 4.09 µmol/L in CC, 14.57 ± 12.85 µmol/L in CT, and 16.87 ± 12.14 µmol/L in TT carriers (P = .19), and the distribution across homocysteine categories (normal, mildly increased, moderately increased, markedly increased) was similar (P = .83). Vitamin B12 levels were likewise comparable among C677T genotypes (523.11 ± 266.91 pg/mL in CC; 467.06 ± 215.62 pg/mL in CT; 399.95 ± 187.92 pg/mL in TT; P = .55), with no significant differences in vitamin B12 serological level categories (P = .80). Folic acid concentrations also showed no significant variation across C677T genotypes (6.41 ± 3.15 ng/mL in CC; 5.64 ± 3.40 ng/mL in CT; 4.01 ± 1.72 ng/mL in TT; P = .23), and folate serological level categories were comparable (P = .08). A similar pattern was observed for MTHFR A1298C. Mean homocysteine levels were 16.27 ± 14.98 µmol/L in AA, 12.13 ± 5.84 µmol/L in AC, and 7.16 ± 4.40 µmol/L in CC carriers (P = .14), and homocysteine category distributions did not differ significantly (P = .72). Vitamin B12 levels did not vary significantly by A1298C genotype (427.35 ± 189.79 pg/mL in AA; 457.97 ± 203.64 pg/mL in AC; 329 ± 674.95 pg/mL in CC; P = .07), nor did vitamin B12 serological level categories (P = .86). Folic acid levels were also comparable across A1298C genotypes (5.33 ± 2.91 ng/mL in AA; 5.53 ± 3.55 ng/mL in AC; 6.99 ± 3.60 ng/mL in CC; P = .51), with no significant differences in folate serological level distribution (P = .77).

In the cohort of 55 patients with RAS, the distribution of homocysteine, folic acid, and vitamin B12 categories was evaluated in relation to clinical characteristics (Table 4). No significant associations were found with RAS dimension for homocysteine (P = .13), folic acid (P = .22), or vitamin B12 (P = .42). Likewise, the number of aphthous ulcers (one, two, three, four or more) was not significantly associated with homocysteine (P = .45), folic acid (P = .41), or vitamin B12 serological level (P = .20). In contrast, episode frequency was significantly associated with homocysteine (P = .01) and folic acid serological levels (P = .01), whereas no significant association was observed with vitamin B12 serological level (P = .18). Healing time showed no significant relationship with homocysteine (P = .16) or folic acid serological level (P = .46); however, a significant association was observed with vitamin B12 serological level (P = .008), indicating that vitamin B12 categories differed across healing-time groups. Finally, pain intensity (VAS, 0-10) was not significantly associated with folic acid (P = .29) or vitamin B12 serological level (P = .33). For homocysteine, the association with VAS approached significance but did not meet the conventional threshold (P = .06).

Discussion

In the present study, no statistically significant differences were observed between RAS patients and non-RAS group in the distribution of MTHFR C677T and A1298C genotypes, nor in mean blood levels (and categorical distributions) of homocysteine, folic acid, and vitamin B12. In our cohort, episode frequency was significantly associated with homocysteine and folate serological levels, suggesting that a more favourable metabolic profile (homocysteine within the normal range and nonreduced folate levels) was associated with a lower recurrence frequency. Conversely, no significant association was observed between vitamin B12 serological level and episode frequency. Interestingly, vitamin B12 serological level was significantly associated with healing time, with shorter healing times observed in patients with normal vitamin B12 levels. This finding is biologically plausible, given the role of vitamin B12 in DNA synthesis, cellular turnover, and mucosal repair, and may suggest that adequate vitamin B12 status contributes to a more favourable healing pattern in RAS lesions.16,17 The correlation between the MTHFR C677T polymorphisms and RAS was investigated by Kalkan et al.8 In their study they showed a significant difference in the frequency of the MTHFR C677T polymorphisms in the RAS patients compared to the non-RAS group.8 However, they did not evaluate the MTHFR A1298C polymorphisms nor the service levels of homocysteine, folic acid, and vitamin B12. They concluded that a significant statistical correlation existed between the allelic variants of the MTHFR C677T gene and the clinical characteristics of aphthae (size, number, frequency, and healing time).8 In our study, homocysteine levels in the MTHFR C677T study group tended to be higher in patients with the CT and TT genotypes than in those with the CC genotype; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Similarly, in the A1298C study group, homocysteine levels showed a decreasing trend from the AA to the AC and CC genotypes, but no statistically significant differences were observed. Moreover, unlike previous studies, we investigated not only the MTHFR C677T polymorphism but also the MTHFR A1298C polymorphism and the blood levels of vitamin B12, folic acid, and homocysteine.

Homocysteine assessment is of particular interest, as available evidence suggests that HHcy may exert a proinflammatory effect through the generation of reactive oxygen species and the activation of nuclear transcription factors involved in the expression of adhesion molecules, chemokines, and cytokines.18 HHcy-induced activation of NF-κB (a nuclear transcription factor) has been shown to increase the expression of intercellular adhesion molecule-1, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, vascular adhesion molecule-1, and E-selectin, which promote the interaction of inflammatory cells with the endothelium and lead to the development of atherosclerosis.19 In a primate study involving diet-induced HHcy, Lentz et al20 reported a significant decrease in endothelium-dependent vasodilation along with a reduction in the anticoagulant activity of thrombomodulin. Additionally, patients with homocystinuria have been found to have low levels of antithrombin III.21 Similarly, other studies on HHcy have reported a reduction in activated protein C and irreversible inhibition of thrombomodulin, promoting a procoagulant state.22 Moreover, HHcy appears to enhance lipoprotein binding to fibrin, suggesting a link between thrombosis and the development of atherosclerosis.23 The cumulative effect of these processes causes damage and loss of endothelial cells.24

These findings suggest that metabolic factors, rather than MTHFR polymorphisms alone, may contribute to the clinical phenotype of RAS in a subset of patients.16 In fact, patients with the mutation, whether homozygous or heterozygous, do not necessarily exhibit the disease phenotypically.25,26 Various factors can influence this, such as proper nutrition and an adequate intake of vitamin B12 and folic acid, which can reduce HHcy.17,27 Homocysteine is converted into methionine through two coenzymes, folic acid and vitamin B12.17 When these are deficient, the reaction does not occur or occurs only partially, leading to homocysteine accumulation in the blood.28,29

Therefore, the Mediterranean diet, rich in a wide range of nutrients, including folate and vitamin B12, may play a key protective role against a broad spectrum of oral diseases.30,31 In conclusion, this study expands on the findings of Kalkan et al,8 by evaluating both MTHFR polymorphisms and selected metabolic markers related to one-carbon metabolism, including homocysteine, folate, and vitamin B12. Given the number of statistical comparisons performed, the possibility of type I error inflation cannot be excluded. Accordingly, the statistically significant associations observed between selected serological markers and clinical features of RAS should be interpreted with caution and require confirmation in larger studies based on predefined hypotheses and appropriate correction for multiple testing. Indeed, from a clinical perspective, these findings do not support a direct role of MTHFR polymorphisms in RAS onset, but they suggest that selected metabolic markers, particularly homocysteine and folate serological level, may be relevant to disease course and deserve further investigation in larger studies. Unfortunately, patients with RAS are not always referred to an oral pathologist unless their condition is severe, even when seen by general practitioners or other specialists.32 Despite its impact on patients, this condition often receives limited clinical attention, and its medical management is frequently underestimated.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in this cohort, MTHFR C677T and A1298C polymorphisms were not associated with the occurrence of RAS. Similarly, homocysteine, folate, and vitamin B12 levels did not differ significantly between RAS and non-RAS groups. However, selected biochemical markers involved in one-carbon metabolism, particularly vitamin B12, homocysteine, and folate serological levels, were associated with certain indicators of disease course, suggesting that metabolic factors may influence the clinical expression of RAS in a subset of patients. These findings should be interpreted with caution in light of the limited sample size and require confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies.
