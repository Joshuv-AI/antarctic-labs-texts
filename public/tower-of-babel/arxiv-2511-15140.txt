Gravitational Bound State Perturbations Inside Black Holes and Isospectrality
Hassan Firouzjahi 1, Kazem Rezazadeh 1, Masoud Molaei 2

Abstract
We study the bound state solutions for the polar perturbations in the interior of the Schwarzschild black hole. It is shown that for a given value of the spherical harmonic index ℓ \ell , there are a total of ℓ − 1 \ell-1 bound states for polar perturbations. We show both analytically and numerically that the spectrum of ℓ − 2 \ell-2 of these perturbations coincides exactly with the spectrum of axial perturbations. Consequently, the isospectrality between the bound states of axial and polar perturbations in the interior of the black hole is preserved. Furthermore, the additional mode found in the spectrum of polar perturbations is the algebraically special mode, which also furnishes the ground state of polar perturbations. It is shown that the spectrum of the highly excited states is equally spaced, which, in the semi-classical approximation, yields the black hole area quantization Δ ​ A = 16 ​ π ​ l Pl 2 \Delta A=16\pi l_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2} .



Introduction:

The unperturbed black hole (BH) solutions of the Einstein field equation are mathematically simple and yet elegant
Chandrasekhar 1985
. However, the observable physical effects appear at the level of perturbations, such as in the detection of GWs emission during the merging of BHs and the ringdown phase as observed in LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA collaborations
Abbott et al. 2016
;
Abbott et al. 2017
;
Abbott et al. 2020
;
LIG 2025
. A key role in BH physics is played by the BH event horizon which separates the interior region of the BH from its exterior region. In particular, as the interior region is causally disconnected from the exterior region, it is assumed that the dynamical events happening in the interior region are inaccessible to the exterior observers and hence irrelevant for gravitational and cosmological observations. While this notion is practically viable, nonetheless, the interior of the BH is part of the whole manifold, and a consistent treatment of BH perturbations requires that the perturbations in the interior of the BH to be studied as well.

The question of bound state perturbations in the interior of the Schwarzschild BH was originally studied in
Firouzjahi 2018
and was revisited more recently in
Steinhauer et al. 2025
;
Firouzjahi 2025
. For other works
concerning the interior of BH see also
Fiziev 2006
;
Firouzjahi 2024
. The bound states are interpreted as the solutions with an imaginary spectrum in which the perturbations are regular at the center of the BH, while their time-dependent profile falls off exponentially on the event horizon. It is important to note that these perturbations are independent of perturbations in the exterior region and are defined intrinsically in the interior of BHs.
It is demonstrated in
Firouzjahi 2025
that scalar, vector, and axial tensor perturbations all admit bound state solutions. Specifically, it is shown in
Firouzjahi 2025
that for a given value of the angular index
\ell
and the spin
s
of the fields, there are a total of
\ell-s
bound state solutions.
Finally, it is shown in
Steinhauer et al. 2025
that the bound state solutions inside the BHs have the curious property that their total wave function has a non-zero amplitude on the event horizon. Correspondingly, these bound states are named in
Steinhauer et al. 2025
as overdamped quasibound states (OQBSs).

The quasi-normal modes (QNMs) perturbations in the exterior of BHs are vastly studied, for some reviews and an incomplete list of theoretical studies
see
Nollert 1999
;
Kokkotas and Schmidt 1999
;
Berti et al. 2009
;
Konoplya and Zhidenko 2011
;
Berti et al. 2025
;
Iyer and Will 1987
;
Motl and Neitzke 2003
;
Andersson and Howls 2004
;
Cardoso et al. 2003
;
Medved et al. 2004
;
Berti et al. 2006
;
Konoplya et al. 2019
;
Hod 2025
. The scalar, vector, and the axial tensor perturbations are governed by the Regge-Wheeler (RW) equation, while the corresponding equation for the polar tensor perturbations is the Zerilli equation
Regge and Wheeler 1957
;
Zerilli 1970
;
Chandrasekhar 1985
. The RW and Zerilli potentials have different algebraic forms, but there is a one-to-one transformation which links the polar perturbations to the axial perturbations
Chandrasekhar 1985
;
Chandrasekhar and
Detweiler 1975
;
Anderson and Price 1991
. Since this transformation is regular in the exterior region, an immediate conclusion is that the axial and polar perturbations have the same spectra of QNMs perturbations.

In this work, we extend upon the analysis of
Steinhauer et al. 2025
;
Firouzjahi 2025
and study the bound state perturbations for the polar perturbations in the interior of the Schwarzschild BH.
It is important to note that the bound state solutions considered here and in
Firouzjahi 2018
;
Steinhauer et al. 2025
;
Firouzjahi 2025
are different from the bound state solutions of the inverted potential proposed by Mashhoon and collaborators
Mashhoon 1982
;
Blome and Mashhoon 1981
;
Ferrari and
Mashhoon 1984a
;
Ferrari and
Mashhoon 1984b
;
Liu and Mashhoon 1996
. More specifically, the bound states in
Mashhoon 1982
;
Blome and Mashhoon 1981
;
Ferrari and
Mashhoon 1984a
;
Ferrari and
Mashhoon 1984b
;
Liu and Mashhoon 1996
are obtained from the complex transformation of the RW equation to bring the potential in the form of Poschl-Teller or the Eckart potentials which can be solved analytically. This method was further employed recently in
Völkel 2025
(see also
Hatsuda 2020
) in which the inverted RW potential is considered directly and a numerical method is used to determine the eigenvalues of energies.

Gravitational Perturbations Inside the BH:

The Schwarzschild metric in spherical coordinates is given as usual by,

\displaystyle\differential s^{2}=-\big(1-\frac{1}{r}\big)\differential{ t\, }^{2}+\frac{\differential r^{2}}{\big(1-\frac{1}{r}\big)}+r^{2}\differential\Omega^{2}\,,

(1)

in which
\differential\Omega^{2}
represents the angular part of the metric. We have rescaled the dimensionful coordinate by
r\rightarrow 2GMr
(with
M
and
G
being the BH mass and the Newton constant)
so the event horizon is located at
r=1

As usual, let us define the tortoise coordinate
dr_{*}=\differential r\big(1-\frac{1}{r}\big)^{-1}

and

We expand the perturbations in terms of the spherical harmonics and denote the remaining parts of the wave function by

\displaystyle\Big(4\lambda(\lambda+1){\mp}6i\omega\Big)Z^{\pm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[4\lambda(\lambda+1)+\frac{18(r^{2}-r)}{r^{3}(2\lambda r+3)}\Big]Z^{\mp}

\displaystyle{\pm}

\displaystyle 6\frac{\differential Z^{\mp}}{\differential r_{*}}\,,

Isospectrality from SUSY Analysis:
Here, using the supersymmetry (SUSY) method, we analytically confirm the existence of isospectrality between the polar and axial perturbations in the interior of BH and highlight
the special role which is played by the ASM mode. We comment that this method is formally parallel to corresponding analysis for the QNMs of the exterior region, see for example
Cardoso and Lemos 2001
;
Konoplya and Zhidenko 2011
;
Berti et al. 2009
. However, there are important differences between the two setups
which require independent investigations. First, we look for the bound state solutions which have different boundary conditions than QNMs in the exterior region. Second, the potentials
V^{\pm}
are singular at
r=0
so the regularity of transformations (
3
) and (
4
) is not manifest a priori
Steinhauer et al. 2025
. Here we present the main results while further technical details are provided in Appendix
B
. For a review of the SUSY method see
Gangopadhyaya et al. 2017
;
Cooper et al. 1995
.

Consider the superpotential,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(r)\equiv\frac{3\,(1-r)}{r^{2}(3+2\lambda r)}-\frac{2\lambda(\lambda+1)}{3}=-3f+i\omega_{\mathrm{sp}}\,,

(8)

in the domain
0\leq r\leq 1
. Equipped with this superpotential,
SUSY ladder operators are constructed as follows,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\pm}_{\ell}(r_{*})\equiv\pm\dfrac{d}{dr_{*}}+\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(r_{*})\,.

(9)

Using the above ladder operators, we construct the partner Schrodinger Hamiltonians,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(r_{*})\equiv\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\mp}(r_{*})\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\pm}(r_{*})=-\dfrac{\differential^{2}}{\differential r_{*}^{2}}+U_{\ell}^{\pm}(r_{*})\,,

(10)

with the partner potentials given as,

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274ptU_{\ell}^{\pm}(r_{*})\equiv\mathcal{W}_{\ell}^{2}(r_{*})\mp\dfrac{\differential\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(r_{*})}{\differential r_{*}}\,.

(11)

The Schrodinger-like equations associated to this system are,

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(r_{*})\phi^{\pm}_{\ell,n}=\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell,n}\phi^{\pm}_{\ell,n},\qquad n\geq 0,

(12)

With
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}
given in Eq. (
8
), one can easily show that the partner potentials
U^{\pm}_{\ell}
are related to the original axial and polar potentials via
U^{\pm}_{\ell}=V^{\pm}-\omega_{\mathrm{sp}}^{2}
. Correspondingly, the Schrodinger-like equation (
12
) is equivalent to the original Eq. (
2
) with
\big(\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell,n}\big)^{2}=\big(\omega^{\pm}_{\ell,n}\big)^{2}-\omega_{\mathrm{sp}}^{2}
. Note that
\omega^{\pm}_{\ell,n}
represents the spectrum of polar and axial perturbations at the level
n
.
Furthermore, the values of the superpotential at the end points of its domain
have opposite signs,
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(0)=+\infty
, and
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(1)<0
, so the SUSY is unbroken
Gangopadhyaya et al. 2017
.

Due to the forms of
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\pm}
and their relations to

\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}
, it can be shown that
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell,n}\geq 0
. Furthermore, if
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\pm}\phi^{\pm}_{\ell,0}=0
then
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}\phi^{\pm}_{\ell,0}=0
, which means
\phi^{\pm}_{\ell,0}
is an eigenvector of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}
with eigenvalue zero,
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell,0}=0
(ground state).
Correspondingly, the ground state eigenfunction can be written as
\phi^{\pm}_{\ell,0}(r_{*})=N^{\pm}\,e^{\mp\int_{x_{0}}^{r_{*}}\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(x)\differential x}
, where
N^{\pm}
are normalization constants.
From
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}
given in Eq. (
8
),
\phi^{+}_{\ell,0}
is obtained to be as in Eq. (
7
) for the ASM solution.
While
\phi^{+}_{\ell,0}(r)
is regular and normalizable
in the domain of the superpotential, the other ground state
\phi^{-}_{\ell,0}\sim\big(\phi^{+}_{\ell,0}\big)^{-1}
is singular at
r=0,1
and non-normalizable; this confirm that the SUSY is unbroken.

Since
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}
are SUSY partners, their eigenfunctions and eigenvalues for
n=1,2,\dots
, are related to each other by,

\displaystyle\phi^{\mp}_{\ell,n}(r_{*})=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell\,n\pm 1}}}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\pm}_{\ell}(r_{*})\,\phi^{\pm}_{\ell,n\pm 1}(r_{*}),

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{-}_{\ell\,n-1}=\mathcal{E}^{+}_{\ell\,n}.

(14)

Here the level number
n
for
\phi^{-}_{\ell,n}
is redefined such that the lowest normalizable sate of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{-}
is
\phi^{-}_{\ell,0}
.

As mentioned, the Schrodinger-like equation (
12
) is equivalent to the original equation (
2
). Consequently, the eigenfunctions of polar perturbations are given by
Z^{+}_{\ell,n}=\phi^{+}_{\ell,n}
. In particular, the ground state is given by
Z^{+}_{\ell,n=0}(r)=\phi^{+}_{\ell,0}(r)
, with
\phi^{+}_{\ell,0}(r)
as given in Eq. (
7
) and with the spectrum
\omega^{+}_{\ell,n=0}=\omega_{\mathrm{sp}}
. Furthermore, the eigenfunctions of axial perturbations are given by
Z^{-}_{\ell,n}=\phi^{-}_{\ell,n}
. Finally, from Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
) and the fact that there are only
\ell-2
axial bound states, we obtain,

\displaystyle Z^{\mp}_{\ell,n}(r_{*})=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{(\omega^{\pm}_{\ell\,n\pm 1})^{2}-\omega^{2}_{sp(\ell)}}}\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\pm}_{\ell}(r_{*})Z^{\pm}_{\ell,n\pm 1}(r_{*})\,,

(15)

with
\omega^{+}_{\ell,n}=\omega^{-}_{\ell,n-1}
for
n=1,2,\dots,\ell-2
.

The above analysis confirms that the isospectrality between polar and axial gravitational perturbations is preserved in the Schwarzschild interior. This isospectrality arises because
U^{\pm}_{\ell}
are partner potentials in SUSY sense and the singularity of the potentials at
r=0
does not destroy this property. In addition, the polar perturbations contain an extra mode that does not have any correspondence in axial modes. This is nothing but the ASM
Z^{+}_{\ell,n=0}=Z^{+}_{\ell,\mathrm{sp}}
with the eigenfrequency

\omega_{\mathrm{sp}}
.

Following the logic of

;

(here we have restored the scaling

).
Now consider the transition from the most excited state

to the second most excited state

. This yields
(2GM)\Delta\omega=1
, inducing a change in the mass of BH,
\Delta M=\hbar\Delta\omega=\hbar(2GM)^{-1}
. On the other hand, from the BH area formula

A=16\pi G^{2}M^{2}
we have
\Delta A=32\pi G^{2}M\Delta M
. Using our predictions for
\Delta M
, this yields the BH area quantization,

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\Delta A=32\pi G^{2}M\Delta M=16\pi l_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}\,,\quad\quad(l_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}\equiv\hbar G)\,.

(16)

In comparison, Maggiore

has used the transition between highly excited states of QNMs in the exterior region and obtained

\Delta A=8\pi l_{\mathrm{Pl}}^{2}
.
Indeed, parameterizing the BH area quantization via

Finally, it is helpful to examine the similarities and differences of our bound states with QNMs in the exterior region. In both cases, the isospectrality is valid and the polar perturbations have the additional ASM excitations. However, a key difference is that for a given value of
\ell

the number of bound states in the interior of the BH is finite but the number of QNMs is infinite. This is due to differences between the asymptotic shapes of the corresponding effective potentials in the interior and exterior regions. More specifically,
V^{-}_{\mathrm{eff}}
can be approximated by the Coulomb potential in far regions
Liu and Mashhoon 1996
. However, near the horizon in the interior
(r_{*}\rightarrow-\infty)
it falls off exponentially like
e^{r_{*}}

Firouzjahi 2018
(see Appendix
A
). Another difference is that ASM in our analysis is the ground state (

Conclusions:
In this work, we have studied the bound states of the polar perturbations in the interior of the Schwarzschild BH and examined various of its physical implications.
The bound states are solutions with imaginary spectrum

r=0

We have shown numerically that for a given value of

Appendix B
SUSY-Inspired Methods:

In this section, we briefly review SUSY approach for analyzing the isospectrality associated to a general one-dimensional
superpotential. For further reviews on SUSY approach see
Gangopadhyaya et al. 2017
;
Cooper et al. 1995
.

Consider a real function
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(x)
which is called the superpotential. Using
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(x)
, the conjugate ladder operators are defined on the physical Hilbert space
as follows,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)=\pm\dfrac{d}{dx}+\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(x).

(28)

It is straightforward to define the Hermitian operators,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)=\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\mp}(x)\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)=-\dfrac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+U_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)\,.

(29)

These operators have the familiar form of the Schrodinger Hamiltonian with the potentials given in term of the superpotential
\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(x)

as follows,

\displaystyle U_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)=\mathcal{W}_{\ell}^{2}(x)\mp\dfrac{d\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(x)}{dx}\,.

(30)

In this view
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
are called the partner Hamiltonians and the
U_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
are the partner potentials. These Hamiltonian possesses finite or infinite set of physical normalizable bound states which form the basis for the physical Hilbert spaces.

The physical normalizable and bounded
eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
are denoted by
\phi^{\pm}_{\ell n}(x)
and
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell n}
for
n=0,1,2,\dots
(this sequence
can be finite or infinite). It is straightforward to show that,

•

Due to the forms of
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
and their relations to
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
then
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell n}\geq 0
.

•

If
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)\phi^{\pm}_{\ell 0}(x)=0
then
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)\phi^{\pm}_{\ell 0}(x)=0
, which means that
\phi^{\pm}_{\ell 0}(x)
is eigenvector of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
with eigenvalue zero
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell 0}=0
(ground state). Furthermore, the ground state eigenfunction can be written as
\phi^{\pm}_{\ell 0}(x)=N^{\pm}\,e^{\mp\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\mathcal{W}_{\ell}(x)dx}
, in which
N^{\pm}
are the normalization constants.

It is not possible to simultaneously normalize the ground states
\phi^{\pm}_{\ell 0}(x)
due to the relation
\phi^{+}_{\ell 0}(x)\sim\frac{1}{\phi^{-}_{\ell 0}(x)}
. This puts strong restrictions on the system. In the case in which
\phi^{\pm}_{\ell 0}(x)
is normalizable and
\phi^{\mp}_{\ell 0}(x)
is not, the ground energy of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
is zero and it is said that the SUSY is unbroken. In the case in which none of
\phi^{\pm}_{\ell 0}(x)
are normalizable, neither of the two Hamiltonians have a zero-energy bound state, and it is said that the SUSY is spontaneously broken. It is possible to show that a superpotential that has different signs (the same sign) at the ends of the domain will generate a system with unbroken SUSY (spontaneously broken SUSY)
Gangopadhyaya et al. 2017
.

In the case of unbroken SUSY, we suppose the Hamiltonian that possess the normalizable ground state is
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{+}(x)
. As the two Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
originate from the same superpotential they share some common properties, like isospectrality.
This is defined in
Gangopadhyaya et al. 2017

as both Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
having the same set of eigenvalues, except possibly for the ground state of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{+}(x)
if this ground state exists.

By representing the eigenfunctions and the corresponding eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
by
\phi^{\pm}_{\ell n}(x)
and
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell n}
for
n=1,2,\dots
(note,
n\neq 0
), and using the definition of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
, one can show that,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\mp}(x)\left(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)\phi^{\pm}_{\ell n}(x)\right)=\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell n}\left(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)\phi^{\pm}_{\ell n}(x)\right)\,.

(31)

This shows that
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
transfers the eigenfunction
\phi^{\pm}_{\ell n}(x)
of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)
with the eigenvalue
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell n}
to the eigenfunction of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\mp}(x)
with the same eigenvalue
\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell n}
.

In the case that SUSY is not broken, it can be shown that
Gangopadhyaya et al. 2017
,

\displaystyle\phi^{\mp}_{\ell n}(x)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{E}^{\pm}_{\ell n\pm 1}}}\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)\phi^{\pm}_{\ell n\pm 1}(x),\quad\mathcal{E}^{-}_{\ell n-1}=\mathcal{E}^{+}_{\ell n},

(32)

for
n=1,2,3\dots
. The number of
n
for
\phi^{-}_{\ell n}(x)
is redefined such that the lowest normalizable sate of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{-}(x)
to be
\phi^{-}_{\ell 0}(x)
. Furthermore, the ground state of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{+}
satisfies
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{+}(x)\phi^{+}_{\ell 0}(x)=0
, and it does not connect to any state of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{-}
, this is why we have excluded
n=0
in Eq. (
31
).

In the case that SUSY is broken, none of the equations
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\ell}^{\pm}(x)\phi^{\pm}_{\ell 0}(x)=0
result in a normalizable state. This excludes the existence of zero-energy bound states, while we have a strict isospectrality between the two partner Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}^{\pm}
, meaning that
\mathcal{E}^{-}_{\ell n}=\mathcal{E}^{+}_{\ell n}
for all
n\geq 1
. As a result, in each case, the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of either of the partner Hamiltonians determine the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the other one.

The isospectrality between the spectra of two potentials
U_{\ell}^{+}
and
U_{\ell}^{-}
originates from the fact that these potentials are SUSY partners, and the singular behaviours of the potentials (at
r=0
in the case of BH) do not affect the isospectrality.
