Deletion of glycosyltransferase galE impairs the InlB anchoring and pathogenicity of Listeria monocytogenes
Authors: Xiaowei Fang, Mei Yuan, Minghao Zheng, Qian Guo, Yuting Yang, Yuying Yang, Xiongyan Liang, Jing Liu, Chun Fang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTListeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes) is a foodborne intracellular pathogen that causes serious disease in both humans and animals. InlB is the major internalin protein of L. monocytogenes, which anchors to the bacterial surface and mediates its invasion into various host cells. Recent studies have shown that galactosylation of the cell wall polymer wall teichoic acid (WTA) is essential for InlB anchoring on the cell surface of L. monocytogenes serotype 4b strains. Galactosylation of WTA is exerted by the coordinated action of several glycosyltransferases, including GalU, GalE, GtcA, GttA, and GttB. Among these glycosyltransferases, GttA and GttB are specific to serotype 4b strains, whereas GalE, GalU, and GtcA are conserved across all serotypes. The role of GalE in InlB anchoring and L. monocytogenes pathogenicity remains unclear. In this study, we deleted the galE gene, which is involved in galactosylation, from L. monocytogenes strain ScottA. We found that galE deletion reduced InlB anchoring, weakened bacterial adhesion and invasion of Caco-2 cells (human colorectal adenocarcinoma cells) and MGC803 cells (human gastric carcinoma cells), increased phagocytosis but decreased proliferation in RAW264.7 cells (mouse mononuclear macrophage leukaemia cells), and decreased bacteria load, mortality, and tissue damage in infected mice. Taken together, galE deletion significantly reduced the anchoring of InlB and weakened the pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes. This finding provides new insights into the correlation between cell wall modification and pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes.KEYWORDS: Listeria monocytogenes, glycosyltransferase, internalin B, pathogenicity

Introduction

Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes) is a widely distributed gram-positive bacterium that infects humans and animals. It causes severe symptoms such as enteritis, meningitis, and sepsis. It is one of the fatal foodborne pathogens worldwide [1]. As an intracellular pathogen, L. monocytogenes can break through the intestinal, placental, and blood-brain barriers, causing systemic infection [2,3]. InB is a key virulence factor for L. monocytogenes which invades various host cells via the hepatocyte growth factor receptor c-Met [4]. As a surface-anchoring virulence factor, InlB binds to the cell wall polymer wall teichoic acid (WTA) through non-covalent bonds via its C-terminal GW domain. [5]. WTAs are modified by various types of glycosylation, which determine the serotypes of L. monocytogenes [6]. WTAs are classified into type I WTAs and type II WTAs according to the presence or absence of GlcNAc residues in the repeating units of the WTAs. The GlcNAc residues are present on the I WTAs, whereas the GlcNAc motifs on the backbone of the II WTAs are modified by various glycosylations [7]. The GlcNAc residues in the main chain structure of type II WTAs can be modified with polysaccharides, including glucose (Glc), galactose (Gal), O-acetyl, and GlcNAc modification [8–10]. It has been found that WTAs are involved in the anchoring of several virulence proteins on the cell wall surface [3,11–13]. Rhamnosylation and galactosylation are crucial for the anchoring of InlB to the cell wall. In serotype 1/2a strains, deletion of the rhamnosylation-important gene cluster rmlACBD results in attenuated binding of InlB and Ami to the cell wall [11]. The absence of galactosylation modifications in WTA of the serotype 4b strain also resulted in the inability of InlB to properly anchor to WTA. A previous study showed that deletion of the gttA and gttB genes resulted in the absence of galactosylation modifications in the WTA of the serotype 4b strain, which led to a notably lower anchoring of surface proteins such as InlB [14]. They hypothesized that galactosylation modification of L. monocytogenes WTA is mainly accomplished by the involvement of glycosyltransferases encoded by the genes gttA, gttB, galU, galE, and gtcA [14]. GalU catalyzes the production of UDP-Glc [15]; GalE catalyzes the conversion of UDP-Glc to UDP-Gal; GttA then catalyzes the addition of Gal to intracellular membranes of the UndP lipid carrier molecule; GtcA converts UndP-Gal from the cytoplasm to the outside of the cytoplasmic membrane [16]; and GttB catalyzes the addition of Gal from UndP-Gal to the growing chain of WTAs. Previous studies have shown that the gttA, gttB, gtcA, or galU genes are closely associated with the pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes [14,17,18]. Deletion of gtcA in the serotype 4 b strain significantly weakened galactosylation modification and pathogenicity in infected mice [19]. gtcA deletion in serotype 4b strain also resulted in a significant reduction in the ability of the bacteria to adhere to and invade host cells and the pathogenicity of the mutant in mice [20]. Similar phenotypes were observed in Salmonella typhimurium and Streptococcus pneumoniae when the glycosyltransferase galU gene was deleted [21,22]. Genomic analysis revealed that gttA and gttB were specific to serotype 4b strains, whereas galE, galU and gtcA were conserved in all serotypes of L. monocytogenes. The role of galE in InlB anchoring and the pathogenicity of various serotypes of L. monocytogenes remains unclear. In this study, we aimed to elucidate the effect of galE on InlB anchoring and pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

L. monocytogenes and Escherichia coli (E. coli) were inoculated in brain heart infusion (BHI) medium and Luria-Bertani (LB) medium with continuous shaking at 180 rolls per minute (rpm), respectively. The strains containing the shuttle plasmid pKSV7 were cultured with 10 μg/mL chloramphenicol for L. monocytogenes and 100 μg/mL ampicillin for E. coli, and the strains containing the complementary plasmid pIMK2 were cultured with 50 μg/mL kanamycin. The L. monocytogenes strains ScottA, EGDe, HumLm4, HumLm6, and Lm850658-prfAM7 were kindly provided by professor Weihuan Fang from Zhejiang University as gifts.

Construction of deletion and complementary strains

The galE deletion strain ΔgalE was constructed by homologous recombination based on the reference strain ScottA (serotype 4b), and the complementary strain CΔgalE was produced based on strain ΔgalE with a recombinant complementary plasmid according to a previous report [23]. Primers used for producing mutant strains are listed in Table S1. Briefly, the upstream and downstream homologous arms of galE were amplified, fused, and ligated into pKSV7. The recombinant shuttle plasmid was transformed into the parent strain by electroporation. Recombinant strains were cultured at 42 °C with 10 μg/mL chloramphenicol to produce homologous recombinant strains. The recombinant plasmids were then eliminated to produce ΔgalE by serial passage at 28 °C in BHI medium without chloramphenicol. To produce the complementary strain CΔgalE, galE was cloned into plasmid pIMK2 and transformed into ΔgalE by electroporation.

Growth curve determination

Growth curve determination was performed according to a previously reported method [23]. L. monocytogenes strains were inoculated in BHI medium overnight for culturing. The bacteria were collected by centrifugation and then diluted 100 times with BHI medium. The diluted bacterial suspensions were added to a 96-well plate, with 200 μL in each well, and the OD600 nm value was determined using a microplate reader at 1-h interval.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed as previously described [24]. L. monocytogenes was cultured overnight, centrifuged to separate the bacterial cells and culture supernatant, and used to prepare samples of cell wall (CW) proteins and supernatant (SN) proteins. Bacterial cells were resuspended in 2% SDS and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After incubation, the supernatant was collected, centrifuged, and CW protein was collected. Trichloroacetic acid (100%) was added to the bacterial culture supernatant to a final concentration of 10%. The bacterial culture supernatant was incubated at 4 °C overnight to fully precipitate proteins. The next day, the precipitate was collected and centrifuged, and resuspended in 0.1 M NaOH, which is the SN protein.

CW and SN proteins were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE, and the target proteins on the gel were imprinted onto a PVDF membrane (Merck Millipore, Germany) using the wet transfer method. The membrane was incubated with 5% skim milk for 2 h and then incubated with a specific antibody (anti-InlB, 69 kDa; anti-InlA, 88 kDa; or anti-GAPDH, 36 kDa; Catalogue number of the ladders: C520010, Sangon Biotech) at 4 °C overnight. On the second day, the membrane was repeatedly cleaned with TBST and incubated with HRP-labelled goat anti-rabbit antibody for 1 h. The membranes were washed with TBST. A chemiluminescence imaging system (G-box, Syngene, UK) was used for development, and the ImageJ software was used to analyse the grey values.

Adhesion and invasion assay

Adhesion and invasion assays were conducted as previously described [23]. Caco-2 cells (human colorectal adenocarcinoma cells) or MGC803 cells (human gastric carcinoma cells) were incubated overnight in 24-well plates (two 24-well plates were needed: one for the adhesion assay and another for the invasion assay). The old cell culture medium was discarded, the cells in the 24-well plate were cleaned with sterile PBS, and 1 mL of cell culture medium was added to the plate. L. monocytogenes was inoculated overnight at 37 °C and cultured. The bacteria were collected by centrifugation and diluted with sterile PBS solution. Diluted bacterial solution was added to each well of the 24-well plate at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 and incubated in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37°C for 0.5 h for adhesion or 1h for invasion , respectively. Here, the number of bacteria is the initial infection amount, which is denoted as N0. After incubating for 0.5 h, wash one of the 24-well plates 3 times with PBS to remove unattached bacteria. Then, 1 mL of sterile ice water was added to each well to swell the cells and release bacteria. The bacteria were diluted and plate counting was performed. The bacterial count at this time point was recorded as N1. After incubation for 1 h, the 24-well plates were washed 3 times with PBS. DMEM containing 20 µg/mL gentamicin was added to each well and incubated for 1 h to remove bacteria attached to the cell membrane. After incubation, the cells were lysed, and plate counting was performed in the same manner. The bacterial count at this time point was recorded as N2. Adhesion rate: N1/N0 ×100%; invasion rate: N2/N0 ×100%.

Phagocytosis and proliferation assay

Phagocytosis and proliferation assays were performed as previous reports [24]. The RAW264.7 cells (mouse mononuclear macrophage leukaemia cells) were inoculated on two 24-well plates, one for the phagocytosis assay and the other for the proliferation assay. Phagocytosis assay: Took RAW264.7 cells cultured overnight, the old medium was discarded, and fresh medium was added. We added 106 CFU of bacteria to each well at a MOI of 10, four replicate wells for each bacterium, and counted the bacteria at the same time. The bacterial count at this time was the infection amount, which was denoted as N3. The 24-well plate was incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 1 h to allow RAW264.7 cells to phagocytose L. monocytogenes. After incubation, the medium was discarded, the wells were washed three times with PBS, and DMEM containing 20 μg/mL gentamicin was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to kill the extracellular bacteria. After incubation, each well was washed three times with PBS, and 1 mL of ice water was added to each well to swell and burst the cells and release intracellular bacteria. Finally, the bacteria were serially diluted, and the plates were used to count the bacteria. The bacterial count at this time point was the amount of phagocytosis, denoted as N4. Proliferation assay: The previous steps were the same as the phagocytosis assay, except that another 24-well plate was used to infect the same number of bacteria. After gentamicin treatment, the wells were washed with PBS, and fresh medium was added to each well. The 24-well plate was incubated in the incubator for another 4 h to allow the bacteria to proliferate in RAW264.7 cells. After incubation, the cells were treated with ice water to release bacteria. The plate counted the number of bacteria in each well to determine the bacterial count, which was the proliferation amount, denoted as N5. Phagocytosis rate = N4/N3 ×100%; proliferation fold = N5/N3.

Mouse infection assay

Mouse virulence tests were performed as described in our previous studies [23]. The 6-week-old female BALB/c mice were randomly divided into three groups with 12 mice in each group, which were adapted to the IVC experimental animal feeding system for 3 days and fasted for 24 h the day before the experiment.

The target strains were inoculated into BHI medium and incubated overnight in a shaker at 37 °C. The bacteria were collected by centrifugation, washed three times with PBS, and diluted with PBS. Mice were infected by intraperitoneal injection, 0.2 mL/mouse (about 106 CFU), and the actual infective dose was determined by the plate counting method. The mental state of the mice was observed and attention was paid to any other abnormal phenomena. Forty-eight hours after infection, the mice (seven per group) died from cervical dislocation. Liver, spleen, and brain tissues were extracted aseptically, placed in a mortar, and ground with 1 mL PBS. Then, the tissue homogenate was serially diluted and the actual infection dose was determined by plate counting. The remaining mice (five per group) were maintained, and the survival rate was calculated to draw a survival curve.

Histopathological analysis

Histopathological analysis was performed according to a previous report [25]. Freshly collected mouse livers, spleens, and brains were soaked in 10% formalin for 48 h. After the tissues were fixed, they were rinsed thoroughly with running water to remove any residual formaldehyde. The tissues were then dehydrated by soaking them in different concentrations of ethanol and xylene and then embedded in paraffin. The tissues were cut into 5-μm-thick sections and spread on glass slides, and stained with haematoxylin and eosin. The stained sections were mounted and observed under an optical microscope (Leica DM 2500).

All animal experiments comply with the relevant requirements of animal operation and animal welfare in the Regulations on the Administration of Experimental Animals of the People’s Republic of China, and strictly follow the Regulations on the Administration of Experimental Animals approved by the People’s Government of Hubei Province (Index Number: 011043145/2022-36,412). All animal assays were performed according to the ARRIVE guidelines for the reporting of animal (Table S2).

Data analysis

The mean and standard deviation of the three replicate tests were used to express the results. The experimental data were graphed using GraphPad Prism 5. Unpaired t-tests and Mann–Whitney tests were used for statistical analysis. p ≤ 0.05 and p ≤ 0.01 indicate significant and highly significant differences, respectively, and p > 0.05 indicates no significant differences.

Results

galE deletion did not affect the growth of L. monocytogenes

The growth of parent strain ScottA (serotype 4b), EGDe (serotype 1/2a), HumLm4 (Serotype 1/2c), HumLm6 (Serotype 1/2b), 850658-prfAM7 (Serotype 4a) and the deletion strain ΔgalE in brain-heart infusion (BHI) medium for 12 h was measured. The results showed that the parent strain and ΔgalE exhibited the same growth trend at 37 °C for 0–12 h (Figure 1 and Fig S1 a, b, c, d). The absence of galE had no significant effect on the growth rate of L. monocytogenes in the BHI medium.

Growth curves of ScottA, ΔgalE and CΔgalE in BHI medium.

galE deletion significantly reduces the anchoring of InlB on the cell wall of L. monocytogenes

In this study, we compared the distribution of InlB on the cell wall surface of ScottA, ΔgalE and CΔgalE strains by western blotting (InlA serves as the positive control for cell wall proteins). The results showed that InlB anchored to the surface of ΔgalE was remarkably reduced, and InlB secreted into the supernatant was appreciably increased. galE complementation restored the binding ability of InlB to the surface of the cell wall (Figure 2a,b).

Distribution of InlB after galE deletion. (a) Chemiluminescent development image. (b) Grayscale value analysis result. Statistical analyses were carried out by t-test: *p ≤0.05, **p ≤0.01, ns means not significant.

We also deleted the galE gene from other serotypes of L. monocytogenes and compared the distribution of InlB. The results showed that after deleting galE in L. monocytogenes EGDe, HumLm4, and Lm850658-prfAM7, the InlB anchored on the cell wall significantly decreased, and the InlB in the supernatant protein significantly increased (Fig S2 a, c, and d), which was consistent with the results in ScottA. After deleting galE in L. monocytogenes HumLm6, InlB anchored to the cell wall did not change significantly, and InlB in the supernatant protein significantly decreased (Fig S2 b). The results indicated that after deleting galE, the anchoring of InlB on the cell wall significantly decreased in L. monocytogenes of serotypes 1/2a, 1/2c, 4a, and 4b, whereas the anchoring of InlB on the cell wall did not change significantly in serotype 1/2b.

galE deletion substantially reduces the in vitro pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes

To determine whether GalE affected the in vitro pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes, Caco-2 cells and MGC803 cells were selected as the model cells in this study to detect the adhesion and invasion of ScottA, ΔgalE and CΔgalE, and RAW264.7 cells were selected to detect phagocytosis and proliferation. The results showed that the adhesion rates of ΔgalE to Caco-2 and MGC803 cells were significantly lower than those of ScottA (Figure 3a–c). The adhesion rate of CΔgalE to Caco-2 cells was not significantly different from that of ScottA cells. The results of the invasion assay showed that ΔgalE had significantly lower invasion rates in Caco-2 cells and MGC803 cells than ScottA and CΔgalE (Figure 3b–d). The phagocytosis assay showed that the phagocytosis rate of ΔgalE was significantly higher than that of ScottA and CΔgalE (Figure 4a). The results of the proliferation assay showed that the fold change in proliferation was significantly lower than that of ScottA and CΔgalE (Figure 4b). All in vitro assays demonstrated that galE deletion significantly reduced the pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes ScottA in vitro.

Adhesion and invasion of ScottA, ΔgalE and CΔgalE to caco-2 and MGC803 cells. (a) Adhesion to caco-2 cells. (b) Invasion to caco-2 cells. (c) Adhesion to MGC803 cells. (d) Invasion to MGC803 cells. The experiments were conducted in triplicate, and data were expressed as mean ± sd. Statistical analyses were carried out by t-test: *p ≤0.05, **p ≤0.01.

Phagocytosis and proliferation of ScottA, ΔgalE and CΔgalE in RAW264.7 cells. (a) Phagocytosis in RAW264.7 cells. (b) Proliferation in RAW264.7 cells. The experiments were conducted in triplicate, and data were expressed as mean ± sd. Statistical analyses were carried out by t-test: *p ≤0.05, **p ≤0.01.

galE deletion significantly reduces the in vivo pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes

We further investigated the role of GalE in a mouse infection model and compared the bacteria load in the liver, spleen, and brain of mice infected with ScottA, ΔgalE and CΔgalE for 48 h. The results showed that the colonization ability of mice infected by intraperitoneal injection with ΔgalE decreased substantially in the liver (Figure 5a), spleen (Figure 5b), and brain (Figure 5c). In addition, survival assays showed that all ScottA-infected mice died 72–96 hours post infection (h.p.i), and only approximately 20% of the final mice survived after infection with CΔgalE. In contrast, the survival rate of ΔgalE infected mice remained at 100% within 168 h.p.i (Figure 6).

Bacteria load in the (a) liver, (b) spleen and (c) brain of mice infected with ScottA, ΔgalE and CΔgalE. Statistical analyses were carried out by Mann-Whitney test: **p≤0.01.

Survival curves of mice infected with ScottA, ΔgalE and CΔgalE.

Pathological sections of the liver, spleen, and brain tissues of intraperitoneally injected mice were analysed. The results showed that compared with ΔgalE, mice infected with ScottA showed acute degeneration of hepatocytes and small foci of necrosis in the liver. Mice infected with ScottA showed multinucleated giant cells proliferating in the red medulla of the spleen. Necrosis of cerebellar Purkinje cells occurs in brain tissue. The histopathological signs of ScottA infection were more severe than those of ΔgalE, whereas the histopathological symptoms of CΔgalE infection were similar to those of ScottA infection (Figure 7). The above results indicated that the in vivo pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes ScottA was significantly reduced after galE deletion, and GalE played an important role in in vivo pathogenicity.

Histopathological changes in the liver, spleen and brain of mice after infection with ScottA, ΔgalE or CΔgalE, or without infection. The white arrows in the figure indicate the lesion area or cells.

Discussion

L. monocytogenes teichoic acid is an anionic sugar polymer on the surface of the cell wall that generally consists of ribitol-phosphate (RboP) in a linear backbone structure. Glycosylation modifications, such as rhamnose, GlcNAc, galactose, and glucose, are used to increase the structural diversity of the wall teichoic acid [5]. The wall teichoic acid structures with various types of glycosylation modifications are not only determinants of L. monocytogenes serotypes, but also play important roles in the regulation of cell morphology, maintenance of osmolality, antibiotic resistance, virulence, and pathogenesis of infections [14].

In this study, we deleted galE in L. monocytogenes serotypes 1/2a, 1/2b, 1/2c, 4a, and 4b (Table S1). We found that galE deletion did not affect the growth of L. monocytogenes in all serotypes in BHI medium (Figure 1 and Fig S1). It was hypothesized that the homology of the galE gene was consistent across serotypes, was not essential for the growth of L. monocytogenes, and did not influence the growth ability of bacteria.

Our study found that galE deletion in the serotype 4b strain ScottA resulted in a statistically significant decrease in the amount of InlB anchoring to the cell wall as compared to a statistically significant increase in the supernatant proteins (Figure 2). This indicates that galE is essential for anchoring the surface protein InlB in the 4b serotype strain ScottA. The anchoring of InlB in the bacterial cell wall was similarly affected by galE deletion in the non-4b serotype results (Fig S2). Both galactosylation and rhamnosylation modifications of L. monocytogenes WTAs are involved in the surface binding of the virulence factor InlB [11,26], with the galactosylation modification of serotype 4b WTAs being a key component in the biological function of InlB [5,11]. In serotype 4b L. monocytogenes, Sumrall et al. speculated that GalE serves as an isomerase that catalyzes the conversion of UDP-glucose to UDP-galactose during galactosylation and participates in the synthesis of WTA galactosylation [26]. We found that galE deletion in strain ScottA (serotype 4b) resulted in a significant decrease in the anchoring of InlB to the cell wall and a striking increase in the supernatant proteins. The galE gene was confirmed to be essential for the anchoring of InlB in serotype 4b strains and is an important player in the galactosylation modification of WTA in serotype 4b strains. It was also found in non-4b serotypes that galE deletion plays an influential role in the anchoring of InlB in the bacterial cell wall. In the structures of WTAs from strains EGDe (serotype 1/2a), HumLm6 (serotype 1/2b), and HumLm4 (serotype 1/2c), GlcNAc modifies the C-2 position of RboP or rhamnosylates the C-4 position of RboP. These types of WTAs are I WTAs and are not galactosylated [27,28]. Serotypes 1/2a, 1/2b, and 1/2c all contain rhamnose [8,27]. Although the rhamnosylation modification is involved in the surface conjugation of the virulence factor InlB, its glycosylation process is shared by specific rhamnosyl transferases, and GalE is not involved in the WTA rhamnosylation modification [28]. In serotype 4a strains, WTAs are predominantly bound to GlcNAc in the main chain structure, whereas serotype 4a strains also exhibit galactose modifications, but not specifically [8]. Strain 850658-prfAM7 (serotype 4a) resulted in a significant decrease in the amount of InlB anchoring in the cell wall and a significant increase in the supernatant proteins after galE deletion (Fig S2b). Therefore, we speculate that the galE gene serves more functions than catalysing the conversion of UDP-glucose to UDP-galactose and that it may play different roles in other serotypes to generate different substrates, which in turn have an impact on the anchoring of InlB to the cell wall. In addition, we suspect that the absence of galE may also lead to changes in the surface localization of other proteins, not just InlB. We analysed the overall profiles of both cell wall proteins and secreted proteins by SDS-PAGE. We found that there were significant changes in both surface and secreted fractions, not just restricted to InlB (Fig S3). This finding was similar to our previous report [24].

Cellular assays revealed that the invasion of ScottA to Caco-2 and MGC803 cells were significantly reduced after galE deletion (Figure 3). L. monocytogenes is induced to internalize into certain human cells through the interaction of the bacterial surface protein InlB with the host receptor tyrosine kinase Met [29]. We found that the amount of InlB anchored to the cell wall surface of ScottA was significantly reduced after galE deletion. We hypothesized that the degraded invasion of ScottA to Caco-2 and MGC803 cells after galE deletion was due to the inability of InlB to properly anchor to the bacterial cell wall. However, it is strange that the absence of galE also reduced the adhesion of ScottA to these two types of cells. Although both InlA and InlB could mediate the invasion into host cells independently, the role of InlB in adhesion is very limited. Mickey et al. found that InlB does not act as an adhesin, but can accelerate bacterial internalization after attachment [30]. Eric et al. and Filipe et al. found that InlB dissociation from the wall teichoic acid resulted in the invasion rather than adhesion of L. monocytogenes decreased significantly [11,14,26]. However, their evidence showed that InlB plays a role in adhesion to host cells. Claire et al. found that InlB could favour the persistence of L. monocytogenes in the gut of the host [31]. Liu et al. found that deletion of the inlB gene in strain F4244 resulted in the adhesion rate decreasing significantly and overexpression of InlB in strain F2365 and L. innocua ATCC33090 could increase their adhesion ability to epithelial cells Caco-2. This phenotype might be accounted for by the fact that secreted Listeria adhesion protein (LAP) is anchored by InlB [32]. In this study, we found that InlB is not the only protein affected by galE (Fig S3). The decreased adhesion might be attributed to the decrease of other adhesion proteins. We also examined phagocytosis and the multiplicity of proliferation of each strain of L. monocytogenes serotype 4b. The phagocytosis rate was much higher than that of 10403S and CΔgalE after galE deletion, but the multiplicity of proliferation was the lowest for ΔgalE (Figure 4). As an intracellular pathogen, L. monocytogenes could survive and proliferate in the cytoplasm of host cells. In this study, we found that the proliferation rate decreased significantly in the galE deletion strain (Figure 4b), which might be attributed to the changes of cell surface protein distribution as shown in Fig S3. Since L. monocytogenes needs the help of surface proteins LLO, PlcB, and ActA to escape from the phagosomes and disguise themselves before proliferation and migration to adjacent cells. Otherwise, they will be degraded by the phagolysosome upon the Listeria-contained phagosome fused with the lysosome, or by the autophagosome. Paricia et al. found that deleting galU or gtcA in the 4b strain resulted in cell-to-cell spread ability decreasing significantly, although the expressed ActA on the bacterial surface remained unchanged [19]. The cell-to-cell spread and autophagy escape ability not only affect the virulence but also affect the intracellular survival and growth of L. monocytogenes [33]. The above results showed that the absence of galE significantly affected the pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes serotype 4b.

The virulence assay showed that the bacteria load in the liver, spleen, and brain of mice was significantly reduced after galE deletion and the survival rate of mice was greatly improved. However, complementation with the galE gene restored the bacteria in organs to varying degrees, the survival rate of which was similar to that of ScottA-infected mice (Figure 5 and 6). The rhamnosylation modification in serotype 1/2a and galactosylation in serotype 4b have been found to be involved in the anchoring of virulence factors, which facilitates the expression of important virulence proteins, as well as the performance of their biological functions and maintenance of L. monocytogenes bioactivity. We found that galE deletion affected anchoring of the virulence protein InlB. InlB can bind to the hepatocyte growth factor receptor Met and the complement molecule Clq receptor in mice and is involved in adhesion to liver and spleen cells [34]. In serotype 4b strains, galE deletion reduced the ability of L. monocytogenes to colonize the liver, spleen, and brain and reduced the mortality rate of mice. This suggests that galE deletion reduces the virulence and pathogenicity of L. monocytogenes, which may be related to the inability of InlB to anchor properly to the bacterial surface. Among the EGDe, HumLm6, and HumLm4 strains, galE deletion decreased the colonization ability of HumLm4 (Fig S4c) and EGDe (Fig S4a) strains in the spleen. However, the colonization ability of the HumLm6 strain was unaffected in both the liver and spleen (Fig S4b). Based on the effect of GalE on the anchoring of different serotypes of InlB, we hypothesized that different substrates were produced by GalE in strains that were not modified with galactosylation of WTAs. Furthermore, different strains are affected by their substrates to different degrees, showing differences in virulence and pathogenicity.

In this study, we demonstrated that deletion of galE resulted in the inability of InlB of L. monocytogenes serotype 4b strains to anchor properly on the bacterial surface, reducing the ability of L. monocytogenes to adhere to epithelial cells, as well as its pathogenicity in mice. However, the degree of influence of galE deletion on InlB anchoring and L. monocytogenes pathogenicity in different serotype strains was different. The findings of this study expand our understanding of the key factors for pathogenicity formation in different serotypes of L. monocytogenes. Further elucidation of the function of galE and its mechanism in non-4b serotyped strains may provide possible targets for the prevention and control of listeriosis.
