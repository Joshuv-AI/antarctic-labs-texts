Profiling systematic uncertainties in Simulation-Based Inference with Factorizable Normalizing Flows
Davide Valsecchi, Mauro Donegà, Rainer Wallny

Abstract
Unbinned likelihood fits aim at maximizing the information one can extract from experimental data, yet their application in realistic statistical analyses is often hindered by the computational cost of profiling systematic uncertainties. Additionally, current machine learning-based inference methods are typically limited to estimating scalar parameters in a multidimensional space rather than full differential distributions. We propose a general framework for Simulation-Based Inference (SBI) that efficiently profiles nuisance parameters while measuring multivariate Distributions of Interest (DoI), defined as learnable invertible transformations of the feature space. We introduce Factorizable Normalizing Flows to model systematic variations as parametric deformations of a nominal density, preserving tractability without combinatorial explosion. Crucially, we develop an amortized training strategy that learns the conditional dependence of the DoI on nuisance parameters in a single optimization process, bypassing the need for repetitive training during the likelihood scan. This allows for the simultaneous extraction of the underlying distribution and the robust profiling of nuisances. The method is validated on a synthetic dataset emulating a high-energy physics measurement with multiple systematic sources, demonstrating its potential for unbinned, functional measurements in complex analyses.

Unbinned likelihood fits have long been recognized as a powerful statistical tool in High Energy Physics (HEP) data analysis, offering the potential to fully exploit the information contained in continuous distributions of observables. Unlike binned approaches, which discretize data into histograms and can suffer from information loss and binning artifacts, unbinned fits operate directly on the raw event data, enabling more precise parameter estimation and hypothesis testing. However, the practical implementation of complete unbinned likelihood fits in realistic LHC analyses has been hindered by the computational complexity of profiling systematic uncertainties.

Traditionally, uncertainties are handled by modelling their effect on the observables as a variation around a nominal template, often relying on constructing histograms for every “up” and “down” variation of the nuisance parameters. While effective and scalable to hundreds of parameters in binned fits for a single or a limited number of parameters of interest
[
16
,
14
]
, this strategy struggles in high-dimensional feature spaces where binning becomes intractable.

Recent advances in Simulation-Based Inference (SBI)
[
15
,
20
]
have introduced powerful techniques to approximate likelihoqod ratios using neural networks. However, incorporating systematic uncertainties remains a significant hurdle. Many current SBI strategies
[
5
,
1
,
6
]
require either retraining models for each systematic variation or training computationally expensive conditional density estimators that must explicitly learn the effect of every nuisance parameter over its full range. Moreover, most existing unbinned methods focus solely on parameter estimation (e.g. signal strengths or Wilson coefficients)
[
10
,
6
,
31
,
3
]
, leaving the more general problem of measuring a
differential distribution
, crucial for cross-section measurements and generator tuning, largely unaddressed.

In this work, we propose a framework that bridges this gap. We introduce a methodology for
unbinned profile likelihood fits
that leverages
Factorizable Normalizing Flows
to structurally decompose the probability density into nominal components and systematic deformations. By treating systematic uncertainties as learnable, invertible transformations of the feature space, we avoid the combinatorial explosion typical of template morphological interpolation. This approach enables the modeling of continuous, high-dimensional systematic effects with a complexity that scales linearly with the number of nuisance parameters, rather than exponentially.

Furthermore, we extend the target of the fit from scalar parameters to
Distributions of Interest
(DoI), learnable functions that map the reference simulation to the observed data. This allows for what can be interpreted as a "functional unbinned measurement", capable of correcting data-simulation mismodelling or measuring differential cross-sections without binning. Crucially, we present an
amortized optimization strategy
that learns the response of the likelihood to nuisance parameters globally, bypassing the need for repetitive training during the minimization process.

This paper is structured as follows: Section
2
details the mathematical formulation, modelling probability densities with Normalizing Flows and defining the DoI framework. Section
3
introduces Factorizable Normalizing Flows for systematic handling and the amortized profiling strategy. In Section
4
, we validate the method on a synthetic physics dataset with multiple systematic sources. Finally, Section
5
discusses the outlook and potential applications of this framework in real-world HEP analyses.

2
Formulation

We adopt a likelihood-based approach to data analysis, formulating the problem as the optimization of a probabilistic model to best describe the observed data density. In this section, we first describe the construction of the baseline probability density model using Normalizing Flows
[
22
]
and then introduce our novel extension: the functional likelihood fit targeting invertible transformations.

2.1
Probabilistic Model with Normalizing Flows

We model the probability density of the observed data events
x
. We assume the dataset consists of independent and identically distributed events belonging to a set of distinct physics processes or "flavours", indexed by
f\in\{1,\dots,F\}
. The total probability density is expressed as a mixture model:

p(x|\theta,\nu)=\sum_{f=1}^{F}\eta_{f}(\theta,\nu)\,p_{f}(x|\nu)

(1)

where
\eta_{f}
are the normalization factors (fractions or yields) which depend on the physics parameters of interest
\theta
and nuisance parameters
\nu
. The functions
p_{f}(x|\nu)
represent the per-flavour probability densities, which may also depend on systematic uncertainties parameterized by
\nu
.

To model the per-flavour densities
p_{f}(x|\nu)
, we employ Normalizing Flows (NFs)
[
22
]
, a class of generative models that provide a flexible and powerful framework for density estimation. NFs allow for the exact evaluation of the probability density
p_{f}(x|\nu)
by learning a mapping between the complex data distribution and a simple base distribution (e.g., a multivariate Gaussian). In our framework, these reference densities are pre-trained on simulated events and fixed during the statistical inference, serving as the static components of the mixture model.

In our formulation, we explicitly split the event feature space into two sets: variables
x
defining the kinematic phase space (e.g.
p_{T}
,
\eta
) and discriminative variables
y
(e.g. output of a classifier, reconstructed masses). We factorize the component densities autoregressively and for each flavour
f
as:

p_{f}(y,x|\nu)=p_{f}(y|x,\nu)\,p_{f}(x|\nu)

(2)

This factorization is implemented using conditional Normalizing Flows (CNFs). Specifically, we train a conditional flow to learn
p_{f}(y|x,\nu)
and a separate flow (conditional only on the flavour) to learn the kinematic density
p_{f}(x|\nu)
.

2.1.1
Conditional vs Joint Likelihood

The maximum likelihood fit can be performed in two distinct regimes using this architecture. The standard approach maximizes the
joint likelihood
over the full feature space
(x,y)
. The negative log-likelihood (NLL) is given by:

-\ln\mathcal{L}_{\text{joint}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln\left(\sum_{f}\eta_{f}\,p_{f}(y_{i}|x_{i})p_{f}(x_{i})\right)

(3)

Alternativey, one may wish to facilitate the fit by focusing solely on the information contained in
y
conditionally on
x
. This corresponds to the
conditional likelihood
:

-\ln\mathcal{L}_{\text{cond}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln p(y_{i}|x_{i})=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln\left(\sum_{f}w_{f}(x_{i})\,p_{f}(y_{i}|x_{i})\right)

(4)

where the event-wise posterior weights
w_{f}(x_{i})
are dynamically determined by the model as:

w_{f}(x)=\frac{\eta_{f}p_{f}(x)}{\sum_{k}\eta_{k}p_{k}(x)}=P(\text{flavour}=f|x)

(5)

This duality allows us to choose whether to model the systematic variations of the kinematic distributions explicitly in the fit (Joint) or to use the kinematics primarily as a condition for the discriminator (Conditional), providing flexibility in specific analysis scenarios. The conditional likelihood approach has the advantage of decoupling the modeling of
p(x)
from the fit, which can be beneficial when
p(x)
is complex or high-dimensional
[
15
]
. In fact, in that case the weight
w_{f}(x)
can be estimated using classifiers trained to distinguish between flavours in the kinematic space only, as proposed in
[
4
]
.
A combination of both approaches is also possible, where part of the kinematic space is modeled explicitly in the fit in order to constrain the
\nu
systematic variations, while other dimensions are treated conditionally in order to reduce the complexity of the density estimation.
This choice has also implications on the treatment of systematic uncertainties, as discussed in Section
3
.

Extended Likelihood

To account for the total expected number of events, we employ the extended likelihood formalism. The extended likelihood incorporates both the probability of observing the data given the model and the Poisson probability of observing
N
events when
\mu
are expected:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ext}}=\frac{\mu^{N}e^{-\mu}}{N!}\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(x_{i},y_{i})

(6)

where
\mu=\sum_{f}\eta_{f}
is the total expected yield. The corresponding negative log-likelihood is:

-\ln\mathcal{L}_{\text{ext}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln\left(\sum_{f}\eta_{f}\,p_{f}(y_{i}|x_{i})p_{f}(x_{i})\right)+\mu-N\ln\mu+\ln N!

(7)

The term
\ln N!
is constant for fixed
N
and can be omitted during optimization. This extended likelihood approach enables simultaneous fitting of both shape and normalization parameters, as is standard in HEP analyses.

2.2
Distributions of Interest
as fit targets

While the mixture model described above provides a robust baseline for standard parameter estimation (where
\eta_{f}
and
\nu
are optimized), we propose a paradigm shift: treating the
invertible transformation itself as the primary measurement target
.

Conventionally, unbinned likelihood fits optimize a set of scalar parameters
\theta
(such as signal strength or masses) given a fixed, pre-computed probability density model
[
15
,
6
]
. In this work, we extend the optimization domain to include a
family of invertible transformations

T_{\phi}(x)
, parameterized by neural networks. We call these
Distributions of Interest
(DoI), as they generalize the concept of measuring a single parameter to measuring a full distribution, defined implicitly by the transformation
T_{\phi}
.

In general this approach is transformative when the target of the fit is not a single parameter but a full distribution, as in unbinned cross-section measurements or simulation calibration tasks. In these scenarios, the goal is to measure how much the observed data distribution deviates from a reference model, without relying on predefined templates or histograms. Instead of assuming a static density
p(x|\theta)
, we model the observed data distribution by composing a reference density
p_{\text{ref}}(u)
(built from the mixture model in Eq.
1
) with a learnable diffeomorphism
u=T_{\phi}(x)
.
This allows the fit to optimize not only the normalization and standard shape parameters but also to learn the optimal "morphing" of the observable space itself.

Specifically, the likelihood function becomes a functional of the transformation
T_{\phi}
:

\mathcal{L}(T_{\phi},\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{\text{ref}}(T_{\phi}(x_{i})|\theta)\left|\det\nabla_{x}T_{\phi}(x_{i})\right|

(8)

This formulation is particularly powerful for handling systematic uncertainties, as the effect of nuisance parameters can be naturally modeled as a
continuous deformation of the feature space
, learned directly during the fit or profiled via an amortized training procedure. In the Sec.
5
we discuss how this approach generalizes to many different analysis scenarios in HEP, enabling a new general paradigm for unbinned likelihood fits.

The invertible transformation
T_{\phi_{f}}
needs to be implemented using architectures that ensure
invertibility and tractable computation
of the Jacobian determinant. This allows us to compute the transformed density
p_{f}(T_{\phi_{f}}(x)|\nu)
efficiently during the likelihood evaluation. Differently from optimal transport applications
[
1
,
5
]
, where the focus is on finding the minimal cost mapping between two fixed distributions, here the focus is on making it possible to compute the likelihood of the data given the transformed distribution. This will allow us to perform systematic uncertainty profiling in Section
3
.

The transformation
T_{\phi_{f}}
is therefore implemented as an invertible neural network, such as the ones used to build Normalizing Flow
[
22
]
. In particular,
T_{\phi_{f}}
has been implemented as a conditional Rational Quadratic Spline Transformation
[
18
]
, which morph the input space through a series of piecewise spline functions. This architecture allows for flexible and expressive transformations while ensuring invertibility and efficient computation of the Jacobian determinant. Alternative, simpler architectures are possible, such as affine coupling layers
[
22
,
27
]
, which provide a good trade-off between expressivity and computational efficiency. The choice of architecture for
T_{\phi_{f}}
can be tailored to the specific requirements of the analysis, such as the dimensionality of the feature space and the complexity of the expected shape variations.

The invertibility of the transformation is crucial to allow us to transform the reference density
p_{\text{ref}}
into the target density
p_{f}(x|\nu)
after the fit, a useful operation if
T_{\phi_{f}}
is used to correct for data-simulation mismodelling. It’s also important to notice that the transformation
T_{\phi_{f}}
is dependent on the flavour
f
, allowing for flavour-specific shape corrections. This is particularly relevant in HEP analyses, where different physics processes may exhibit distinct systematic effects that need to be modeled independently.

The formulation of
T_{\phi_{f}}
is general: in our specific toy model application, the transformation is applied in the feature space
y
, conditionally on the kinematic variables
x
and nuisance parameters
\nu
, for each flavour. The per-flavour density model is therefore expressed as:

p_{f}(y,x|\nu)=p_{f}(T_{\phi_{f}}(y|x,\nu))\,p_{f}(x|\nu)\,\cdot\left|\det\nabla_{y}T_{\phi_{f}}(y|x,\nu)\right|

(9)

In this formulation the transformation
T_{\phi_{f}}
is setup up to bring the
data space

y^{\prime}
to the
simulation space

y
. The inverse transformation would need to match the distortion map applied to the data
F(y|x,c)
:

T_{\phi_{f}}(y^{\prime}|x,\nu)=y\quad\Rightarrow\quad y^{\prime}=F(y|x,c)

(10)

This setup is fairly general in HEP application, and it does not limit the generality of the method. In fact, the transformation
T_{\phi_{f}}
can be applied in the full feature space
(x,y)
, or only in the kinematic space
x
, depending on the specific analysis requirements. The flexibility of our framework allows for various configurations of the transformation, enabling tailored solutions for different types of systematic uncertainties and measurement goals.

2.3
Maximum Likelihood optimization

The full likelihood is therefore defined as a function of the physics
Distribution of interest

T_{\phi_{f}}
and nuisance parameters
\nu
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ext}}(y,x|\theta,\nu,T_{\phi_{f}})=\frac{\mu^{N}e^{-\mu}}{N!}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{f}\eta_{f}(\theta,\nu)\,p_{f}(T_{\phi_{f}}(y_{i}|x_{i},\nu))\,p_{f}(x_{i}|\nu)\,\cdot\left|\det\nabla_{y}T_{\phi_{f}}(y_{i}|x_{i},\nu)\right|\right)

(11)

In this model, the inputs PDFs
p_{f}(x|\nu)
and
p_{f}(y|x,\nu)
are modeled using Normalizing Flows trained on simulated events, while the target transformation
T_{\phi_{f}}(y|x,\nu)
is implemented as an invertible neural network. The inputs PDFs are trained prior to the fit (and then frozen), while the transformation
T_{\phi_{f}}
and nuisance parameters
\nu
are optimized during the maximum likelihood fit.

The full likelihood fit is therefore corresponding to the optimization process:

\hat{T}_{\phi_{f}},\hat{\nu}=\arg\max_{\phi,\nu}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ext}}(y,x|\theta,\nu,T_{\phi_{f}})

(12)

The training is performed using gradient-based optimization methods, such as AdamW
[
25
]
, to minimize the negative log-likelihood. The gradients of the likelihood with respect to the parameters
\phi
and
\nu
are computed using automatic differentiation
[
28
]
, allowing for efficient optimization even in high-dimensional parameter spaces.
For more details about the training dynamics and strategies for handling nuisance parameters, we refer to Section
3.4
.

2.4
From Coupling Layers to Optimal Transport

The use of invertible transformations as fit targets naturally connects our framework to the theory of Optimal Transport (OT)
[
29
,
23
]
. In OT, the goal is to find the most efficient way to transform one probability distribution into another, minimizing a cost function that quantifies the "effort" required for the transformation. This is particularly relevant in HEP analyses, where systematic uncertainties and data-simulation mismodelling often manifest as shape distortions in observable distributions. In this context, OT provides a principled way to quantify and correct for these distortions and it has been investigated successfully in simulation calibration tasks on realistic LHC datasets
[
5
,
1
,
26
]
. Nevertheless, OT applications so far have focused on finding the optimal mapping between two fixed distributions, with the objective difficulty of incorporating directly systematic uncertainties and profiling nuisance parameters in a likelihood fit. This is because OT methods typically do not provide a likelihood function that can be optimized directly, but rather focus on the cost of the transformation itself.

Our approach instead focuses on learning invertible transformations that can be directly incorporated into a likelihood framework, enabling the profiling of nuisance parameters and the extraction of Distributions of Interest. However, the connection to OT remains valuable, as it provides insights into the nature of the transformations being learned and their relationship to the underlying distributions. In particular, the transformations
T_{\phi_{f}}
learned during the fit can be interpreted as approximations of optimal transport maps between the reference distribution and the observed data distribution, conditioned on the nuisance parameters. This perspective opens up avenues for further exploration, such as incorporating OT-inspired regularization terms into the likelihood optimization or leveraging OT metrics to assess the quality of the learned transformations.

A possible way out is to leverage the learnt invertible transformations to be able to generate a dataset distribution as
y^{\prime}\sim p(T_{\phi_{f}}(y|x,\nu))
and then use this to learn an OT-mapping between
p_{\text{data}}(y)
and
p(T_{\phi_{f}}(y|x,\nu))
using standard OT methods such as Conditional Flow-Matching
[
34
]
or Input Convex Neural Networks (ICNN)
[
2
,
21
]
. This would allow us to extract the optimal transport path between the data and the learnt distribution, providing additional insights into the nature of the systematic distortions present in the data. We leave a detailed exploration of this connection for future work.

3
Handling of systematic uncertainties

In HEP the effect of systematic uncertainties is traditionally modeled using
template variations
, where the impact of each uncertainty source is captured by a set of pre-defined shape variations (e.g.,
\pm 1\sigma
histograms)
[
14
,
16
]
.
This approach, while effective, can be limited in flexibility and scalability, especially when dealing with high-dimensional feature spaces or a large number of nuisance parameters. Nevertheless, the approach of parameterizing the effect of systematic uncertainties as shape variations with a quadratic dependency over the nuisance parameters remains a powerful and widely used technique in LHC analyses, allowing for efficient likelihood computation up to hundreds of nuisance parameters.

When dealing with unbinned likelihood fits using generative models, a direct extension of this approach is to model the effect of systematic uncertainties as
parametric deformations
of the learned probability density functions. This can be achieved by 1) making the generative model directly dependent on the nuisance parameters(parametric conditional densities) or 2) by introducing additional invertible transformations that capture the shape variations induced by each source of uncertainty. However, naively implementing this idea can lead to a combinatorial explosion in the number of parameters and transformations, making the optimization process intractable and the training costly.

For example, in the case of 1), trying to model the effect of
K
independent nuisance parameters would require learning a Normalizing Flow
p(y|x,K_{i}
) conditional on all nuisance parameters, which can be challenging due to the high dimensionality of the nuisance space and the complex interactions between different sources of uncertainty. In fact, in order to capture the full effect of each nuisance parameter, one would need to sample the full
K
-dimensional space during training, meaning that one would need to generate training samples for all combinations of nuisance parameters, leading to an exponential growth in the required training data and computational resources.

Learning additional invertible transformations independently for each nuisance parameter, the approach 2), may be more doable:
p(T_{\nu}(\nu)|x,\nu_{i})
with
T_{\nu}=T_{\nu_{1}}\circ T_{\nu_{2}}\circ T_{\nu_{3}}
. Nevertheless, in this case the order of the composition of the effects would matter, leading to ambiguities and potential biases in the modeling of the systematic uncertainties. Moreover, this would anyway require training with samples covering the full
K
-dimensional space of nuisances, leading again to scalability issues as the number of nuisance parameters increases.

The problem of including systematic uncertainties in unbinned likelihood fits is multifaceted:

•

Effect on inputs
: The method should accurately capture the effect of systematic uncertainties on the input probability density functions, ensuring that the learned transformations reflect the true impact of each source of uncertainty.

•

Profiling capability
: The framework should allow for efficient profiling of nuisance parameters during the likelihood fit, enabling robust uncertainty quantification of their effect on the Distributions of Interest.

•

Scalability
: The method should scale gracefully with the number of nuisance parameters, avoiding combinatorial explosions in the number of required transformations or training samples.

•

Training efficiency
: The training process should be efficient, allowing us to learn the effect of systematic uncertainties without requiring exhaustive sampling of the full nuisance space.

•

Interpretability
: The learned transformations should be interpretable, allowing us to understand how each nuisance parameter affects the shape of the distributions.

First, we need to define a flexible yet tractable parameterization of the effect of each nuisance parameter on the input PDFs distributions learnt from simulation; second, we need to devise efficient training strategies that allow us to learn these effects without requiring exhaustive sampling of the full nuisance space.

3.1
Factorizable Normalizing Flows for systematic uncertainties

To address the challenge of scalable systematic profiling, we introduce the
Factorizable Normalizing Flow
(FNF). The central design philosophy of the FNF is to structurally decouple the modeling of the complex, multidimensional physics distribution from the modeling of systematic variations. We achieve this by defining the probability density as a composition of two distinct components: a fixed, high-fidelity
nominal model

p_{\text{nom}}(y|x)
, trained on high-statistics nominal simulation, and a learnable
systematic transformation

T_{\nu}
, which captures how probability mass migrates under systematic perturbations.

Mathematically, we treat the systematic-aware density as the
pullback
of the nominal density by the diffeomorphism
T_{\nu}
. If
y
represents an observable in the "distorted" space (e.g., data affected by detector effects), the transformation
T_{\nu}(y|x,\nu)
acts to map this variable back to the "nominal" reference space
y_{\text{nom}}
. The likelihood is then evaluated by effectively "correcting" the event and scoring it under the nominal model, penalized by the volume change:

y_{\text{nom}}=T_{\nu}(y|x,\nu)\quad\Rightarrow\quad p(y|x,\nu)=p_{\text{nom}}(T_{\nu}(y|x,\nu)|x)\det|\nabla_{y}T_{\nu}(y|x,\nu)|

(13)

To ensure the likelihood remains tractable and efficient to evaluate, we implement
T_{\nu}
as an
autoregressive affine flow

[
27
,
22
]
. This guarantees a triangular Jacobian matrix, whose determinant can be computed comfortably in
O(D)
time for
D
dimensions. For each dimension
j
, the transformation reads:

y_{\text{nom},j}=y_{j}\cdot\exp\left(s_{j}(y_{<j},x,\nu)\right)+t_{j}(y_{<j},x,\nu)

(14)

where
s_{j}
and
t_{j}
are scale and translation functions.

The key innovation of the FNF lies in the functional form of these scale and shift functions. Standard Normalizing Flows model these as opaque, fully connected neural networks. Instead, we impose a strict factorization inspired by the Taylor expansion of systematic effects. We decompose the total shift and scale as a
sum of independent contributions
from each nuisance parameter
\nu_{k}
, enforcing a quadratic dependency on the magnitude of the systematic deviation:

\displaystyle s_{j}(y_{<j},x,\nu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\alpha_{j}^{(k)}(y_{<j},x)\,\nu_{k}+\beta_{j}^{(k)}(y_{<j},x)\,\nu_{k}^{2}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle t_{j}(y_{<j},x,\nu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\gamma_{j}^{(k)}(y_{<j},x)\,\nu_{k}+\delta_{j}^{(k)}(y_{<j},x)\,\nu_{k}^{2}\right)

(16)

This structure has profound implications. By enforcing additivity in the latent space of the affine transformation components, we prevent the "curse of dimensionality" typically associated with learning joint conditional distributions
p(y|x,\nu)
. The coefficients
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta
are capable of capturing arbitrarily complex kinematic dependencies, as they are output by deep neural networks
\Psi^{(k)}
conditioned on
y
and
x
. Specifically, we employ
Masked Multi-Layer Perceptrons
(Masked MLPs)
[
27
,
22
]
for
\Psi^{(k)}
, allowing a single network to output the autoregressive coefficients for all feature dimensions
j
in a single forward pass, while strictly respecting the causality constraint (dependence only on
y_{<j}
). This results in a model that is both expressive enough to capture deep correlations and structured enough to scale linearly with the number of systematic uncertainties.

This formulation has several key advantages:

•

Independence
: The effect of each nuisance parameter
\nu_{k}
is modeled independently, allowing for straightforward interpretation and control over individual systematic effects. Moreover this structure allows us to
train each variation independently
using only the
\pm 1\sigma
samples for each nuisance parameter, without needing to sample the full
K
-dimensional space.

•

Scalability
: The parameters for each systematic uncertainty are encapsulated in separate networks
\Psi^{(k)}
, preventing the combinatorial explosion associated with learning a joint conditional density.

•

Modularity
: New systematic uncertainties can be added by simply introducing additional networks
\Psi^{(k)}
without retraining the entire model, facilitating incremental updates as new sources of uncertainty are identified.

•

Interpretability
: The learned coefficients
\alpha^{(k)}
and
\gamma^{(k)}
directly represent the linear response of the feature distribution to the nuisance parameter
\nu_{k}
, while
\beta^{(k)}
and
\delta^{(k)}
capture non-linear (quadratic) effects.

•

Efficiency
: The joint transformation can be evaluated in a single forward pass by summing the outputs of the individual networks, and the diagonal Jacobian structure allows for fast density evaluation during the fit.

•

Flexibility
: The use of neural networks for the coefficients allows for complex, non-linear dependencies on the features
y
and kinematics
x
, enabling the modeling of intricate systematic effects.

•

Expressivity
: Despite the factorized structure, the overall transformation
T_{\nu}
remains highly expressive due to the non-linear nature of the neural networks and the autoregressive formulation.

By stacking multiple such layers, interconnected with permutation layers to mix information across feature dimensions, we construct a highly expressive transformation capable of modeling complex, non-linear systematic effects while retaining precise control over the dependence on nuisance parameters.
It is crucial to note that this approach effectively generalizes the traditional template variation method to a continuous and high-dimensional setting, allowing for smooth interpolation of systematic effects across the nuisance parameter space. This is particularly advantageous in scenarios where the impact of uncertainties is not well-captured by discrete variations, enabling more accurate and robust modeling of systematic uncertainties in unbinned likelihood fits. Moreover, thanks to this parametrization, we have the ability to train each nuisance variation independently using only the
\pm 1\sigma
samples for each nuisance parameter. This significantly reduces the computational burden, making this approach feasible even in analyses with a large number of systematic uncertainties.

\Psi^{(k)}
conditioned on the inputs.

3.1.1
Expansion around the nominal model

The complete probabilistic model
p_{f}(y|x,\nu)
for a given flavour
f
is constructed by composing this systematic-aware transformation
T_{\nu}
with a
nominal density model

p_{\text{nom},f}(y_{\text{nom}}|x)
. This nominal model describes the distribution of features in the absence of systematic variations (i.e., when
\nu=0
) and is typically learned from the nominal simulation sample using a conditional Normalizing Flow. Crucially,
p_{\text{nom},f}
does
not
depend on the nuisance parameters
\nu
. The dependence on systematics is entirely encapsulated within the transformation
T_{\nu}
, which maps the observed (displaced) features
y
back to the nominal space
y_{\text{nom}}
. Applying the change of variables formula, the likelihood for an observed event
(y,x)
becomes:

p_{f}(y|x,\nu)=p_{\text{nom},f}(T_{\nu}(y|x,\nu)|x)\cdot\left|\det\nabla_{y}T_{\nu}(y|x,\nu)\right|

(17)

This factorization allows for a modular workflow: the nominal model
p_{\text{nom},f}
can be trained once with high precision on the large nominal Monte Carlo dataset and then frozen. The lighter transformation network
T_{\nu}
is then trained to capture the systematic variations relative to this backbone.

In this context, it is useful to further parametrize the transformations inside
T_{\nu}
as a deviation from the identity map (corresponding to just the nominal model), especially when systematic effects are expected to be small. We define:

T_{\nu}(y|x,\nu)=T(y|x,\Delta\nu/\sigma_{\nu})

(18)

where
\Delta\nu
captures the distance of the nuisance parameter from the nominal value used to train the nominal model, and
\sigma_{\nu}
corresponds to the expected uncertainty on the parameter. The transformations in
15
are then re-expressed as:

\displaystyle s_{j}(y_{<j},x,\Delta\nu/\sigma_{\nu})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\alpha_{j}^{(k)}(y_{<j},x)\,\frac{\Delta\nu_{k}}{\sigma_{\nu_{k}}}+\beta_{j}^{(k)}(y_{<j},x)\,\left(\frac{\Delta\nu_{k}}{\sigma_{\nu_{k}}}\right)^{2}\ \right)

(19)

\displaystyle t_{j}(y_{<j},x,\Delta\nu/\sigma_{\nu})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\gamma_{j}^{(k)}(y_{<j},x)\,\frac{\Delta\nu_{k}}{\sigma_{\nu_{k}}}+\delta_{j}^{(k)}(y_{<j},x)\,\ \left(\frac{\Delta\nu_{k}}{\sigma_{\nu_{k}}}\right)^{2}\ \right)

(20)

This reparameterization centers the transformations around the nominal model, with
\Delta\nu=0
corresponding to the identity map. The linear and quadratic coefficients now directly quantify the sensitivity of the feature distribution to deviations in the nuisance parameters, normalized by their expected uncertainties.
This allows the neural network learning the coefficients to focus on modeling the relative impact of systematic variations, rather than needing to learn the full transformation from scratch. This can improve training stability and convergence, especially when systematic effects are small compared to the overall feature distribution.

3.1.2
Uncertainties on the input densities

Before performing the maximum likelihood fit on data, the Factorizable Normalizing Flow framework allows us to learn the prior dependency of the input probability densities on the systematic uncertainties. This is a crucial step: instead of relying on discrete template interpolations, we explicitly learn the continuous function
p(x,y|\nu)
that will serve as the building block of our likelihood function (Eq.
1
).

This is achieved in a two-stage training procedure. First, the nominal density
p_{\text{nom}}(y|x,f)
is learned on the high-statistics nominal simulation. Then, fixing the nominal model, the parameters of the systematic transformation
T_{\nu}
(Eq.
19
) are optimized to maximize the likelihood of simulated variation samples (e.g., datasets generated with
\nu_{k}=\pm 1\sigma
). Because the transformation
T_{\nu}
is differentiable and factorized, training on these discrete points effectively learns a smooth interpolation of the systematic effects across the entire nuisance parameter space.

To demonstrate this capability, we applied the FNF architecture to the toy dataset described in Sec.
4.1
, which features two distinct sources of systematic uncertainty. Figure

visualizes the learned effect of the first systematic uncertainty (
\nu_{1}

, in two different points

. The plot displays the
residual transformation
, the shift applied by
T_{\nu}
to map the distorted space back to the nominal space, as a function of the conditional variable
x
. The model successfully captures the non-trivial, phase-space dependent deformations induced by the systematic effect, providing the rigorous continuous parameterization needed for the subsequent profiling step.

3.2
Uncertainties on the Distribution of Interest

The same approach used to make the likelihood ingredients dependent on nuisance parameters can be used to model uncertainties on the Distribution of Interest itself. In fact, in all HEP analysis, the effect of systematic uncertainties is not limited to the inputs of the fit, but it is propagated to the final measurement as well, with a frequentist approach called
profiling
[
24
,
14
]
. In this procedure, the likelihood function
\mathcal{L}(\theta,\nu)
is maximized jointly over the parameters of interest
\theta
and the nuisance parameters
\nu
. The uncertainty on
\theta
is then determined by constructing the profile likelihood ratio
\lambda(\theta)=\mathcal{L}(\theta,\hat{\hat{\nu}}(\theta))/\mathcal{L}(\hat{\theta},\hat{\nu})
, where
\hat{\hat{\nu}}(\theta)
are the values of the nuisance parameters that maximize the likelihood for a fixed
\theta
. This ensures that the confidence interval for
\theta
includes the variations allowed by the systematic uncertainties constrained by their priors.

We now need to translate this procedure to our framework, where we have a Distribution of Interest, represented by the invertible transformation
T_{\phi_{f}}
, instead of the POI
\theta
. In this case, the profiling procedure involves optimizing the likelihood to find the optimal transformation
\hat{T}_{\phi_{f}}
that best matches the observed data, while simultaneously adjusting the nuisance parameters
\nu
to account for systematic uncertainties. This can be formulated as:

\hat{T}_{\phi_{f}},\hat{\nu}=\arg\max_{\phi,\nu}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ext}}(y,x|\theta,\nu,T_{\phi_{f}})

(21)

This requires extending the training dynamics to jointly optimize over
\phi_{f}
and
\nu
, ensuring that the learned transformation accurately captures the systematic variations in the Distribution of Interest.

Thanks to the formulation in Sec.
15
the systematic effects on the likelihood are directly propagated in the likelihood optimization in a tractable way. However the formulation in Eq.
21
is still quite challenging from a training perspective, as it requires jointly optimizing over a high-dimensional space of transformation parameters
\phi_{f}
and nuisance parameters
\nu
. In Section
3.4
we will discuss strategies to efficiently train this joint optimization problem. In practice we would need to scan over the nuisance parameter space during the fit, optimizing the transformation
T_{\phi_{f}}
for each nuisance configuration. This can be computationally intensive, especially when dealing with a large number of nuisance parameters or complex transformations.

3.2.1
Distribution of Interest uncertainty expansion

In order to make the optimization in Eq.
21
more tractable, we can further extend the factorization approach used for the inputs to also model the effect of systematic uncertainties on the Distribution of Interest itself. This involves introducing an additional transformation
T_{\psi_{f}}
that captures the systematic variations in the Distribution of Interest, parameterized similarly to
T_{\nu}
:

T_{\phi_{f}}(y|x,\Delta\nu)=T_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}}(y|x)\circ T_{\psi_{f}}(y|x,\Delta\nu)

(22)

where
T_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}}
is the nominal transformation corresponding to the best-fit values of the nuisance parameters
\hat{\nu}
, and
T_{\psi_{f}}
is a systematic-aware transformation parameterized as in Eq.
19
. This allows us to model the effect of systematic uncertainties on the Distribution of Interest in a modular way, separating the nominal behavior (no dependency on nuisance parameters), from the systematic variations. The joint optimization problem in Eq.
21
then becomes:

\hat{T}_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}},\hat{T}_{\psi_{f}},\hat{\nu}=\arg\max_{\phi^{\hat{\nu}},\psi,\nu}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ext}}(y,x|\nu,T_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}}\circ T_{\psi_{f}})

(23)

It is important to stress that
T_{\phi_{f}}(y|x,\nu)
corresponds to a new model, built as a deviation from a nominal model, so that if the
\Delta\nu
from the global minimum
\hat{\nu}
is small, the transformation
T_{\psi_{f}}
would be close to the identity map, making the optimization easier.

3.3
Profiling systematics in unbinned likelihood fits

Optimizing the nominal and systematic-aware transformations together is still challenging in practice: therefore we can adopt a two-step training procedure, as follows:

•

Step 1 - Global minimum
: First perform a likelihood optimization to learn the global best-fit transformation
\hat{T}_{\phi_{f}}
corresponding to the best-fit values of the nuisance parameters
\hat{\nu}
. This can be achieved by jointly optimizing
T_{\phi_{f}}
and
\nu
on the full dataset, effectively capturing both the nominal behavior for the DoI and the effect induced by the systematic variations
on the likelihood inputs
. This procedure corresponds to the first step of classical profiling, and it is needed to
center
the DoI transformation around the best-fit configuration of the nuisances.

\hat{T}_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}},\hat{\nu}=\arg\max_{\phi^{\hat{\nu}},\nu}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ext}}(y,x|\nu,T_{\phi_{f}}^{\nu})

(24)

•

Step 2 - Systematic-aware training
: Once the global best-fit transformation
\hat{T}_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}}
is obtained, we can decompose the full transformation model into a nominal component
T_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}}
and a systematic-aware component
T_{\psi_{f}}
as in Eq.
22
. The nominal component
T_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}}
is then fixed, and we optimize
T_{\psi_{f}}
and
\nu
on the full dataset to learn the effect of the systematic variations on the DoI. Only the parameters of the neural networks learning the residual corrections in Eq.
15
are updated during this step, while the parameters of the nominal transformation are frozen.

\hat{T}_{\psi_{f}},\hat{\nu}=\arg\max_{\psi,\nu}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ext}}(y,x|\nu,T_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}}\circ T_{\psi_{f}})

(25)

The Step 2 effectively corresponds to the profiling procedure: the nuisance values are scanned around their best-fit value
\hat{\nu}
and the full likelihood is optimized in each point of the nuisance parameter space. As in classical profiling, this allows us to determine the confidence interval for the DoI transformation that includes the variations allowed by the systematic uncertainties constrained by their priors. Once the likelihood contour in the nuisance parameter space is obtained, we can then extract the uncertainty on the DoI transformation by looking at the variations of
T_{\psi_{f}}
along the likelihood contour.

The Step 2 would be anyway incredibly costly, as it would require scanning over the nuisance parameter space and optimizing
T_{\psi_{f}}
for each configuration. Even if the effect can be learnt effectively with the transformation expansion, the procudure would be anyway computationally intensive, especially when dealing with a large number of nuisance parameters or complex transformations.

A possible way to mitigate this issue is to leverage the Factorizable Normalizing Flows formulation and learn the effect of single nuisances at a time. In fact, since the effect of each nuisance parameter is modeled independently in
T_{\psi_{f}}
, we can train each variation independently scanning each single nuisance. Although interesting, this procedure
would not capture the effect of correlations between different nuisance parameters
, making the approach useless for realistic HEP analysis.

3.3.1
Amortized profiling with Factorizable Normalizing Flows

Our work introduces another critical ingredient to allow realistic profiling of systematic uncertainties in unbinned likelihood fits: the use of an
amortized training procedure
to learn the effect of systematic uncertainties on the Distribution of Interest across
the full nuisance parameter space in a single training run
. This is achieved by leveraging the factorizable structure of the transformations in
T_{\psi_{f}}
, which allows us to model the effect of each nuisance parameter independently while still
capturing their combined effect on the DoI
.

This amortized training procedure involves moving the computational cost of the profiling procedure from the a-posteriori likelihood scan to the training phase. During training, we sample nuisance parameter configurations
\nu_{j}
from their prior distributions and optimize the likelihood only for
T_{\psi_{f}}^{\nu_{j}}
, while keeping the nominal transformation
T_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}}
fixed and evaluating the rest of the likelihood in the chosen nuisance point. By doing so, we effectively learn a mapping from the nuisance parameter space to the corresponding transformations of the DoI, allowing us to capture the effect of systematic uncertainties on the DoI across the entire nuisance space in
a single training run
.

The algorithm is detailed as follows:

1.

Train the best-fit model
\hat{T}_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}}
by optimizing the likelihood jointly over
T_{\phi_{f}}
and
\nu
on the full dataset (as described in
3.3
), effectively capturing both the nominal behavior for the DoI and the effect induced by the systematic variations on the likelihood inputs.

2.

Build a composition of a nominal component
T_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}}
and a systematic-aware component
T_{\psi_{f}}
, and fix the parameters of the nominal transformation to the global best-fit
\hat{T}_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}}
.

3.

During the training of
T_{\psi_{f}}
, sample nuisance parameter configurations
\nu_{j}
from their prior distributions and optimize the likelihood only for
T_{\psi_{f}}^{\nu_{j}}
, while keeping the nominal transformation
T_{\phi_{f}}^{\hat{\nu}}
fixed and evaluating the rest of the likelihood in the chosen nuisance point.

This amortized approach effectively transforms the computationally expensive profiling step into a one-time training cost. By leveraging the factorizable structure of FNF, we learn a global mapping from the nuisance parameters to the optimal DoI transformation, efficiently approximating the profile likelihood surface in a single run.

Once trained, the model yields the maximum-likelihood transformation
\hat{T}_{\phi_{f}}(\nu)
for
any
given configuration of nuisances instantaneously. Consequently, extracting the systematic uncertainty on the DoI becomes straightforward: instead of running multiple fits, we simply evaluate the pre-trained transformation along the likelihood contours determined by the global fit. This ensures robust frequentist coverage while maintaining full interpretability of the individual systematic components.

This capability represents a significant advancement over traditional methods, enabling accurate and robust profiling of systematic uncertainties in unbinned high-dimensional measurements.

3.3.2
Orthogonal decomposition of the uncertainty space

Interpreting the fit result in this framework follows the same conceptual path as standard profiling, with the difference that instead of looking at the likelihood contour in the nuisance parameter space, we can directly look at the variations of the DoI transformation
T_{\psi_{f}}
along the likelihood contour. This allows us to directly visualize the effect of systematic uncertainties on the DoI, providing insights into how different sources of uncertainty impact the measurement. However, when dealing with a large number of nuisance parameters, it can be challenging to interpret the combined effect of all uncertainties on the DoI, as the variations can be complex and intertwined. To address this issue, we can leverage the factorizable structure of the transformations in
T_{\psi_{f}}
to perform an orthogonal decomposition of the uncertainty space, allowing us to identify the principal components of the systematic variations and their corresponding impact on the DoI. This can be achieved by performing a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the learned transformations, effectively decomposing the uncertainty space into orthogonal modes that capture the most significant variations in the DoI due to systematic uncertainties. By analyzing these principal components, we can gain insights into which combinations of nuisance parameters have the most significant impact on the DoI, and how they interact with each other, providing a more interpretable representation of the systematic uncertainties in the measurement.

To disentangle the often complex and correlated interplay of systematic uncertainties, we employ an orthogonal decomposition of the learned uncertainty model.
The procedure consists of:

•

Hessian Analysis
: We compute the Hessian matrix of the negative log-likelihood with respect to
\nu
at the global minimum
\hat{\nu}
using automatic differentiation. This matrix captures the local curvature and correlations between nuisance parameters.

•

Eigen-decomposition
: The eigendecomposition of the Hessian yields orthogonal eigenvectors representing independent modes of uncertainty, with eigenvalues quantifying their constraining power.

•

Principal Mode Visualization
: We visualize the “principal systematic variations” by evaluating the trained map
T_{\psi_{f}}
along these eigenvector directions. This reveals the dominant shapes of uncertainty driving the analysis, decoupled from the original (potentially correlated) physics parametrization.

This procedure is showcased in Fig.
11
, where we visualize the principal systematic variations obtained from the orthogonal decomposition of the uncertainty space on the synthetic dataset. The plot displays the effect of the leading eigenvector on the DoI transformation, providing insights into the dominant mode of systematic variation and its impact on the measurement.

3.4
Training dynamics

The joint optimization in the Step 1 of the profiling procedure described in Sec.
3.3
can still be quite challenging in practice, as it involves optimizing over both the transformation parameters
\phi_{f}
and the nuisance parameters
\nu
simultaneously. This can lead to issues such as slow convergence, instability, and sensitivity to initialization, especially when dealing with a large number of nuisance parameters or complex transformations. To address these challenges, we can adopt several strategies to improve the training dynamics and ensure stable and efficient optimization:

•

Alternating optimization
: Instead of optimizing both
\phi_{f}
and
\nu
simultaneously, we can adopt an alternating optimization approach where we fix one set of parameters while optimizing the other. For example, we can fix
\nu
and optimize
\phi_{f}
for a few iterations, and then fix
\phi_{f}
and optimize
\nu
for a few iterations. This can help to stabilize the optimization process and prevent oscillations or divergence.

•

Learning rate scheduling
: We can use different learning rates for
\phi_{f}
and
\nu
, allowing us to control the optimization dynamics for each set of parameters independently. For example, we can use a higher learning rate for
\nu
to encourage faster convergence, while using a lower learning rate for
\phi_{f}
to ensure stable updates.

•

Regularization
: We can introduce regularization terms in the loss function to penalize large changes in
\nu
or
\phi_{f}
, encouraging smoother updates and preventing overfitting. For example, we can add a term that penalizes the L2 norm of the changes in
\nu
or
\phi_{f}
between iterations.

Moreover, if the DoI objective has a degenerate direction with respect to some nuisance parameters, the optimization can become ill-posed, leading to instability and poor convergence. This effect would be the same as in classical profiling, where some nuisances are not identifiable from the data due to lack of sensitivity.
To mitigate this issue, we can introduce additional constraints or priors on the nuisance parameters to regularize the optimization and ensure well-defined solutions. The flexibility of the framework allows to include in the likelihood additional components that can help in breaking these degeneracies, such as control regions or auxiliary measurements that provide independent constraints on the nuisance parameters. By incorporating these additional sources of information, we can improve the identifiability of the nuisance parameters and enhance the stability of the joint optimization.

By adopting these strategies, we can improve the training dynamics for the joint optimization in the profiling procedure, ensuring stable and efficient convergence to the best-fit parameters.

3.5
Comparison with related work

Several directions of research are exploring SBI methods for unbinned likelihood fits in HEP, with a particular focus on building multidimensional likelihood ratios using classifiers trained on simulated data
[
15
,
11
,
6
,
31
,
32
,
10
,
33
,
4
,
19
,
3
]
. These methods typically involve training a classifier to distinguish between different hypotheses (e.g., signal vs background) and then using the output of the classifier to construct a likelihood ratio that can be used for inference. A recent very comprehensive result is the ATLAS measurement of off-shell Higgs boson production in the
H^{*}\rightarrow ZZ\rightarrow 4l
decay channel
[
7
,
6
]
, exploiting for the first time a fully unbinned likelihood fit in multiple dimensions.
This analysis represents a significant milestone in the application of SBI methods for unbinned likelihood fits in HEP, demonstrating the potential of these techniques to improve the precision and robustness of measurements in complex analyses. However, the handling of the systematic uncertainties in this analysis required a significant computational effort, as the effect of each nuisance parameter was modeled as a vertical interpolation of the likelihood using additional classifiers trained on the
\pm 1\sigma
variations of each nuisance parameter. This approach, while effective, is computationally intensive and does not scale well with the number of nuisance parameters, as it requires training a separate classifier for each variation. Finally, the extraction of the final uncertainty on the POI relied only on the estimation of the Hessian matrix of the likelihood at the best-fit point, exploiting the automatic differentiation capabilities of the framework.

Another similar direction of research is the factorization of the effect of systematic uncertainties using parametric ansatz, as proposed in
[
31
,
9
]
. This approach involves modeling the effect of systematic variations on the likelihood ratio between two hypotheses using a parametric form, typically involving a linear expansion around a nominal configuration.

p(y|\theta,\nu)=p(y|\theta,\nu_{0})\cdot\frac{p(y|\nu)}{p(y|\nu_{0})}

(26)

where the effect of the nuisance parameters is then factorized in a linear expansion around a nominal configuration
(\nu_{0})
using a classifier written in the form:

\hat{f}_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(\hat{g}_{i}(x))}

(27)

with the log-odds function factorized as:

\hat{g}_{i}(x;\hat{\Delta}_{1},\hat{\Delta}_{2},\ldots)=\nu^{T}\hat{\Delta}_{1}(x)+\nu^{T}\hat{\Delta}_{2}(x)\nu+\cdots

(28)

The coefficient functions
\Delta(x)
are implemented as neural networks or decision trees that capture the kinematic dependence of the systematic effects.
While this method provides a way to capture systematic variations in the likelihood ratio, in an efficient and refinable way, it does not allow working with conditional densities where the systematic variation is not factorizable from the parameter of interest
\theta
.

Our approach expands the current landspace of SBI methods in two directions: first, we expand the target of the measurement to a multidimensional Distribution of Interest, represented by an invertible transformation, instead of a single POI
\theta
. This allows us to capture more complex and high-dimensional measurements, which are becoming increasingly common in HEP analyses. Second, we introduce a novel method for modeling systematic uncertainties that allows us to learn the effect of systematic variations on the likelihood across the entire nuisance parameter space in a single training run, leveraging the factorizable structure of the transformations. This amortized training procedure significantly reduces the computational cost of profiling systematic uncertainties, making it feasible to handle a large number of nuisance parameters in unbinned likelihood fits.

Targeting a Distribution of Interest instead of a single POI is also a direction explored in the literature, with methods such as morphing-based approaches
[
1
,
5
]
that aim to learn optimal mapping between data and simulation using Optimal Transport techniques
[
2
,
29
]
. Although these methods provide a powerful way to capture complex distributions with mathematical guarantees on the optimality of the mapping, they are not designed to handle systematic uncertainties in a way that allows for profiling, as they typically focus on learning a single mapping between data and simulation without explicitly modeling the dependence on nuisance parameters. Hence, including systematic variations in these approaches would require training separate morphing functions for each variation, which can be computationally expensive and may not capture the full complexity of the likelihood landscape, especially in high-dimensional feature spaces
[
5
]
. Using Normalizing Flows to learn the morphing
[
17
]
permits instead to compute the likelihood efficiently and implement the profiling procedure in a tractable way, while still allowing to extract an Optimal Transport mapping between data and simulation with a post-processing step, as discussed in Sec.
2.4
.

4.2
Likelihood model implementation

The likelihood model implementation follows the structure outlined in Section
2.1
. The joint likelihood of
x
and
y
is maximized to find the best-fit Distribution of Interest (DoI) parameters
\phi
and nuisance parameters
\nu
, in order to match the distorted data distribution. The effect of nuisance parameters on the DoI is modeled through the Factorizable Normalizing Flows (FNF) approach, which decomposes the transformation into a nominal component and a systematic-aware component, as described in Section
3.1.1
. An example of the residual transformation learned by the FNF to capture the effect of a systematic variation on the input feature distribution is shown in Figure
3
.

The nominal transformation
T_{\phi}^{\hat{\nu}}
is implemented as a series of invertible transformations parameterized by rational quadratic splines, while the systematic map
T_{\psi}(y|x,\nu)
is designed to incorporate the effects of nuisance parameters through a factorized architecture, as illustrated in Figure
2
. An important aspect of the implementation is that the transformation depends on both the kinematic variable and the event flavour, allowing for a more flexible modeling of the systematic effects. The events flavour is encoded as a one-hot vector and concatenated to the kinematic variable
x
before being passed to the neural networks that parameterize the transformations. When we evaluate the likelihood for a given data events where the flavour is not known, the transformation for all the classes is computed, and the full mixture model is evaluated as in Eq.
1
.

4.3
Training procedure

The training procedure follows the two-step approach outlined in Section
3.3
. In the first step, we perform a joint optimization of the DoI parameters
\phi
and nuisance parameters
\nu
to find the global best-fit transformation
\hat{T}_{\phi}^{\hat{\nu}}
with stochastic gradient descent. This step effectively captures both the nominal behavior for the DoI and the effect induced by the systematic variations on the likelihood inputs. The optimization is performed using a high learning rate for the nuisance parameters to encourage faster convergence, while using a lower learning rate for the DoI parameters to ensure stable updates (see Sec.
4.4.1
for the likelihood scans obtained after this step).

In the second step, we decompose the best-fit transformation into its nominal and systematic-aware components, and freeze the nominal transformation. We then employ the amortized training procedure to optimize the systematic-aware component
T_{\psi}
across the entire nuisance parameter space, as described in Section
3.4
. During this step, we sample nuisance parameter configurations from their 95% confidence intervals extracted from a likelihood scan on the global fit, and optimize the likelihood for each configuration, effectively learning the mapping from nuisance parameters to DoI transformations in a single training run. For each data sample in each training batch, a different nuisance parameter point is used. See Section
4.4.2
for the results obtained after this step.

5
Discussion and Outlook

The experimental results presented in this work demonstrate that Factorizable Normalizing Flows (FNF) effectively allow for the profiling of systematic uncertainties in unbinned likelihood fits. This provides a robust alternative to classifier-based interpolation or parametric ansatz methods discussed in Sec.
3.5
, which suffer from scalability issues as the number of nuisance parameters increases. By learning the response of the likelihood to systematic variations during a single training phase, we transform the typically expensive profiling procedure into an upfront optimization cost, yielding an inference model that can instantaneously evaluate the profile likelihood for any specific configuration of systematics.

A central contribution of this framework is the practical validation of the Distribution of Interest (DoI) paradigm. Unlike traditional analyses that reduce complex data to scalar parameters of interest (POIs), the DoI approach targets minimal sufficient statistics, in the form of invertible transformations, that map observed data directly to physical distributions. This enables a richer interpretation of the experimental results:

•

Multidimensional Interpretability
: The learned transformation captures correlations in high-dimensional feature spaces that are typically lost in binned analyses.

•

Data Preservation
: The result is not merely a fitted cross-section, but a reusable function that corrects data to simulation, (or detector-level to particle-level if used for unfolding), preserving the full information content of the unbinned dataset.

•

Decoupled Systematics
: The orthogonal decomposition of the uncertainty space (Fig.
11
) allows analysts to identify the "principal modes" of systematic variation. This separates nuisance parameters that significantly impact the physics measurement from those that form flat directions, simplifying the review and debugging of complex fit models.

This methodology introduces significant opportunities for the broader HEP community, particularly in the domain of
unfolding
. While recent contributions
[
11
,
33
,
13
,
8
,
12
]
have shown that precise multidimensional unfolding using generative machine learning is possible, they focus primarily on statistical precision in idealised settings, leaving the full incorporation of experimental systematic uncertainties as an open challenge. Our amortized profiling strategy targets this gap, enabling "systematic-aware unfolding" and providing the comprehensive uncertainty quantification required for realistic precision measurements.

Furthermore, the invertibility of the DoI transformation facilitates a new class of
reinterpretability studies
. Theoretical physicists can test alternative models against the experimental result by mapping their predictions through the learned transformation layers representing the principal systematic variations. This avoids the need for full detector simulation of new models, while still accounting for the dominant experimental uncertainties derived from the original analysis.

Finally, the capability to model high-dimensional conditional densities with accurate systematic profiling facilitates more comprehensive searches for new physics. By removing the need for manual binning and variable selection, this approach can expose subtle deviations in the tails of distributions or in the correlations between kinematic variables, maximizing the discovery potential of current and future LHC datasets.

6
Conclusion

We have developed a practical framework for unbinned likelihood fits that efficiently profiles systematic uncertainties while measuring distributions of interest in high-dimensional feature spaces. The method combines three core components: (1) Factorizable Normalizing Flows that structurally decompose systematic effects into independent, interpretable contributions; (2) Distributions of Interest that generalize unbinned measurements beyond scalar parameters; and (3) an amortized training strategy that learns the full response of the likelihood to nuisance parameters in a single pass, eliminating the need for expensive a-posteriori likelihood scans.

Validation on the synthetic dataset demonstrates the feasibility of this workflow on realistic scenarios. The approach is modular, is scalable to large numbers of nuisance parameters, and preserves full interpretability of individual systematic contributions.

This work provides a practical foundation for unbinned analyses with realistic systematic complexity, making feasible the simultaneous measurement of differential distributions and their systematic uncertainties in high-dimensional feature spaces, opening new possibilities for both precision measurements and searches for new physics at the LHC.

The kinematic variable

x_{\text{raw}}|c\sim\begin{cases}\mathcal{N}(-1.0,0.9^{2})&\text{if }c=0\\

(A.1)

to yield the observed

:

x=x_{\text{raw}}\cdot(1+s_{c}\nu_{x})

(A.2)

where
s_{0}=+1,s_{1}=-1
, and
\nu_{x}
represents the kinematic energy scale uncertainty (corresponding to the ‘variation_x‘ parameter in the code).

p(y_{\text{base}}|x,c)=\mathcal{N}(\mu(x,c),\Sigma(x))

The observed features

are obtained by applying a sequence of transformations to

.

A linear shear is applied which mixes the dimensions and adds an offset proportional to the mixed value to both dimensions:

y^{\prime}=y_{\text{base}}+\Delta y\cdot\mathbf{1}

where
\mathbf{1}=(1,1)^{T}

\Delta y=\begin{cases}0.2y_{0}-0.1y_{1}&\text{if }c=0\\

y^{\prime\prime}=R(\theta(x,c))y^{\prime}

(A.11)

3. Amplitude Modulation

Finally, a class-dependent radial scaling is applied:

y_{\text{final}}=y^{\prime\prime}\cdot\begin{cases}(1+0.03x)&\text{if }c=0\\
