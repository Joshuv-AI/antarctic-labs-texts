Dissipative Yao-Lee Spin-Orbital Model: Exact Solvability and 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{P}\mathcal{T} Symmetry Breaking
Zihao Qi, Yuan Xue

Abstract
Exactly solvable dissipative models provide an analytical tool for studying the relaxation dynamics in open quantum systems. In this work, we study an exactly solvable model based on an anisotropic variant of the Yao-Lee spin-orbital model, with dissipation acting in the spin sector. We map Liouvillian dynamics to fermions hopping in a doubled Hilbert space under a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, and demonstrate the model’s exact solvability. We analyze the strong and weak symmetries of the model, which protect an exponentially large manifold of non-equilibrium steady states, establishing the system as a physically feasible dissipative spin liquid. Furthermore, we analyze the transient dynamics in a translationally invariant sector and discover that the single-particle Liouvillian spectrum hosts an exceptional ring in momentum space. We map out a characteristic 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{P}\mathcal{T} symmetry breaking transition driven by the dissipation strength, which governs the crossover from oscillatory to decaying relaxation of physical observables. Our work provides a physically motivated, solvable setting for exploring the coexistence of dissipative spin liquid physics and non-Hermitian spectral topology.

III
Exactly solvable dissipative spin orbital model

We consider a spin–orbital model on the honeycomb lattice with two spin-
1/2
degrees of freedom per site: a “spin”
\sigma
and an “orbital” (pseudospin)
\tau

H=\frac{J}{4}\sum_{\braket{ij}_{\lambda}}(\tau_{i}^{\lambda}\tau_{j}^{\lambda})\left(\sigma^{x}_{i}\sigma_{j}^{x}+\sigma^{z}_{i}\sigma_{j}^{z}\right),

(6)

where
\braket{ij}_{\lambda}
(
\lambda=x,y,z
) denotes the
\lambda

i
and
j

\sigma

\tau

Our model is a variant of the Yao-Lee (YL) model

38

H_{\text{YL}}=\frac{J}{4}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle_{\lambda}}\big(\tau_{i}^{\lambda}\tau_{j}^{\lambda}\big)\big(\bm{\sigma}_{i}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{j}\big).

(7)

Compared to YL Hamiltonian (Eq. (
7
)),

6

\text{SU}(2)
symmetry of the
\sigma

\sigma_{i}^{y}\sigma_{j}^{y}

(1)

\exp{(i\theta\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{y})}
. This anisotropic choice could be realized by an easy-plane anisotropy in the spin sector and will be essential for the solvability of the Liouvillian.

On top of the Hamiltonian Eq. (
6
), we apply two jump operators on each site,
\{\sqrt{\gamma}\sigma_{i}^{x},\sqrt{\gamma}\sigma^{z}_{i}\}
. The local dephasing terms only act on the
\sigma

4

\mathcal{H}
acting on the doubled Hilbert space:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{4}\sum_{\braket{ij}_{\lambda}}\tau^{\lambda}_{i}\tau^{\lambda}_{j}\left(\sigma^{x}_{i}\sigma_{j}^{x}+\sigma^{z}_{i}\sigma_{j}^{z}\right)-\tilde{\tau}^{\lambda}_{i}\tilde{\tau}^{\lambda}_{j}\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{x}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}_{j}^{x}+\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\tilde{\sigma}_{j}^{z}\right)

Since above decomposition into Majoranas (Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=

\displaystyle\frac{J}{4}\sum_{\braket{ij}_{\lambda}}\left[id_{i}^{\lambda}d_{j}^{\lambda}(ic_{i}^{x}c_{j}^{x}+ic_{i}^{z}c_{j}^{z})-i\tilde{d}_{i}^{\lambda}\tilde{d}_{j}^{\lambda}(i\tilde{c}_{i}^{x}\tilde{c}_{j}^{x}+i\tilde{c}_{i}^{z}\tilde{c}_{j}^{z})\right]

We can further define bond operators:

\displaystyle c_{i}^{\alpha}\rightarrow\Lambda_{i}c_{i}^{\alpha}\,,\,\tilde{c}_{i}^{\alpha}\rightarrow\tilde{\Lambda}_{i}\tilde{c}_{i}^{\alpha}\,,

\displaystyle f_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(c_{i}^{x}+ic_{i}^{z}),~~\tilde{f}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{c}_{i}^{x}+i\tilde{c}_{i}^{z}),

which live on two layers in the doubled Hilbert space. In terms of these fermion operators, the Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=

\displaystyle\frac{iJ}{2}\sum_{\braket{ij}_{\lambda}}u_{ij}\left(f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}-f_{j}^{\dagger}f_{i}\right)-\tilde{u}_{ij}\left(\tilde{f}_{i}^{\dagger}\tilde{f}_{j}-\tilde{f}_{j}^{\dagger}\tilde{f}_{i}\right)

(16)

, and

IV
Symmetry Analysis and Steady States

In this section, we first analyze the strong and weak symmetries of the dissipative spin-orbital model, Eq (
8
). We then outline the criterion for choosing flux sectors that contain non-equilibrium steady states (NESS). From this, we show that there are exponentially many NESS in this model, which realizes a dissipative spin liquid.

IV.1
Strong and Weak Symmetry

In closed quantum systems, the Hamiltonian
H
is Hermitian and generates unitary time evolution. Symmetries are represented by unitary operators
U
that commute with the Hamiltonian,
[U,H]=0
. This commutation relation implies the existence of a conserved “charge”
Q
, namely the generator of this symmetry.
Q
also commutes with
H
, and its expectation value is therefore conserved in time:

i\frac{d}{dt}\left<Q\right>=\braket{[H,Q]}=0.

(17)

As a consequence, the Hilbert space decomposes into sectors labeled by eigenvalues of
Q
.
Under the evolution operator
U(t)=e^{-iHt}
, transitions between different sectors are forbidden, and an initial eigenstate
\ket{\psi_{0}}
with eigenvalue
q
under the charge operator
Q
will remain an eigenstate of
Q
with the same eigenvalue:

Q\left(U(t)\ket{\psi_{0}}\right)=U(t)Q\ket{\psi_{0}}=q\left(U(t)\ket{\psi_{0}}\right).

(18)

In open quantum systems, symmetries are defined with respect to the Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}
, and the above notion of symmetry can be generalized. There are two types of symmetries: strong and weak symmetry
10
;
3
. The interplay between these two types of symmetries, such as spontaneous strong-to-weak symmetry breaking, has drawn lots of recent interest
31
;
45
;
76
;
44
;
42
;
27
.

When an open quantum system has a
strong
symmetry, there exists an
operator

S
that commutes with both the Hamiltonian and the jump operators,

[S,H]=[S,L_{i}]=0,\,\forall\,i.

(19)

On the other hand, a
weak
symmetry is satisfied when there exists a
super-operator

\mathcal{S}
that commutes with the Liouvillian,

[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L}]=0,\,\mathcal{S}(\rho)=S(\rho)S^{\dagger}

(20)

for some operator
S
. Note that
[S,L_{i}]=[S,H]=0
implies
[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{L}]=0
, but not vice versa; a strong symmetry therefore necessarily implies a weak symmetry, but the converse is not true. While both types of symmetries allow for block-diagonalizing the Liouvillian, a strong symmetry has an associated conserved charge, while weak symmetries do not
10
.

One can also understand the difference between strong and weak symmetries in the bilayer picture, in which
\mathcal{L}
is mapped to a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
in the doubled space. Both types of symmetries leave
\mathcal{H}
invariant. However, weak symmetries must act jointly on
both
copies of the doubled Hilbert space to preserve
\mathcal{H}
, whereas strong symmetries only act on
either
copy independently
36
. Consequently, charges associated with strong symmetries are conserved on
each
layer independently. In contrast, weak symmetry only implies conservation of the
difference
between charges on the two layers, which do not translate to conserved observables of the original single-layer system
31
.

The dissipative spin-orbital model we study has an extensive number of strong and weak symmetries. Indeed, similar to Kitaev’s honeycomb model
38
, it is precisely the model’s extensive symmetries that allow for its exact solvability. To understand the strong symmetries, we first consider the dissipation-less limit,
\gamma=0
. The model reduces to a variant of the Yao-Lee model
93
, whose
\text{SU}(2)
symmetry is explicitly broken. In this (closed) system, the conserved fluxes are products of bond operators around each hexagon
93

W_{p}=\prod_{\left<jk\right>\in p}u_{jk},

(21)

where
p
denotes a hexagonal plaquette, and
\left<jk\right>
runs over each of its six bonds. The operators
W_{p}
are gauge-invariant under the local
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
transformation in Eq. (
14

[W_{p},H]=0\,\,\forall\,p

In this model, the

\displaystyle=u_{12}u_{23}u_{34}u_{45}u_{56}u_{16}

\displaystyle=(-id_{1}^{z}d_{1}^{x})(-id_{2}^{x}d_{2}^{y})(-id_{3}^{y}d_{3}^{z})(-id_{4}^{z}d_{4}^{x})(-id_{5}^{x}d_{5}^{y})(-id_{6}^{y}d_{6}^{z})

Similar to the hexagonal plaquettes

, our system hosts an additional, weak
\text{U}(1)
symmetry under the following super-operator:

\mathcal{S}_{\text{U}(1)}=\exp\left(i\theta\sum_{i}\left(\sigma_{i}^{y}-\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{y}\right)\right),

(27)

where
\theta\in(0,2\pi)
is a phase. The weak
U(1)
symmetry is inherited from the
U(1)
symmetry of the Yao-Lee variant
H
(Eq. (
6
)). It is straightforward to verify that
[\mathcal{S}_{\text{U}(1)},\mathcal{H}]=0
. Note that since
S=e^{i\theta\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{y}}
does not commute with the jump operators
\{L_{i}\}
, this is not a strong symmetry.

A direct consequence to this (weak)
U(1)
symmetry is conservation of the generator
\mathcal{Q}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{y}-\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{y}
. Interestingly, this can be translated into the conservation of the total number of fermions on the bilayer. Using the Majorana decomposition (Eq. (
9
)), we can write the local fermion number as:

n_{i}=f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{i}=\frac{1}{4}(c_{i}^{x}-ic_{i}^{z})(c_{i}^{x}+ic_{i}^{z})=\frac{1}{2}(1+\sigma_{i}^{y}),

(28)

while on the other layer

\tilde{n}_{i}=\mathbb{I}\otimes(n_{i})^{T}=\mathbb{I}\otimes\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{i}^{y}\right)^{T}=\frac{1}{2}(1-\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{y}).

(29)

Therefore, conservation of the weak
\text{U}(1)
charge becomes the weak conservation of the total fermion number,
\sum_{i}n_{i}+\tilde{n}_{i}
,. This conservation is precisely why there are no BCS-like pairing terms in the fermionic non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, Eq. (
16

IV.2
Steady States

Unlike closed (Hermitian) systems, where Lieb’s Theorem
48
dictates that the ground state lies in the sector with zero flux, there are no generic principles for choosing flux configurations in open quantum systems. Nevertheless, strong and weak symmetries still provide useful guidelines for counting non-equilibrium steady states (NESS) of the system.

Let us first analyze the steady states in the two limits,
\gamma=0
and
\gamma=\infty
. In the absence of dissipation, the dynamics is unitary under the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
6
). For a system with
N
sites and on-site Hilbert space dimension
4
(spin and orbital), the total Hilbert space dimension is
D=4^{N}
. If
H

is nondegenerate, there are
D
orthogonal energy eigenstates and therefore
D
linearly independent projectors
\{\ket{\psi_{n}}\!\bra{\psi_{n}}\}_{n=1}^{D}
that all commute with the Hamiltonian. The space of steady states is therefore the
D=4^{N}
-dimensional space of operators that are diagonal in the energy basis.

In the opposite limit
\gamma=\infty
, the bilayer Hamiltonian (Eq. (
8
)) reduces to:

\mathcal{H}_{\gamma=\infty}=i\gamma\sum_{i}\left(\sigma^{x}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{x}_{i}+\sigma^{z}_{i}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i}\right)-2iN\gamma.

(30)

Since the sites are decoupled in this limit, it is helpful to instead analyze the single-site Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}_{i}
:

\mathcal{L}_{i}(\cdot)=\gamma\left[\sigma_{i}^{x}(\cdot)\sigma_{i}^{x}+\sigma_{i}^{z}(\cdot)\sigma_{i}^{z}-2(\cdot)\right].

(31)

One can check that
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\sigma_{i}^{x})=-2\gamma\sigma_{i}^{x};\mathcal{L}_{i}(\sigma_{i}^{z})=-2\gamma\sigma_{i}^{z}
, while
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\sigma_{i}^{y})=-4\gamma\sigma_{i}^{y}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathbb{I}_{2})=0\times\mathbb{I}_{2}=0
. Therefore, the only zero mode (steady state) in the
\sigma
sector is identity, the maximally mixed state, while all traceless components decay. In this limit, steady states factorize as:

\rho_{\text{ss}}=\left(\frac{\mathbb{I}_{2}}{2}\right)^{\otimes N}\otimes\rho_{\tau},

(32)

where
\rho_{\tau}
is an
arbitrary
density matrix in the
\tau
sector. Since the
\tau
-sector Hilbert space dimension is
2^{N}
, the dimension of the space of steady states is therefore also
(2^{N})^{2}=4^{N}
.

Beyond the two limits, we need to analyze using symmetries. As shown in Ref.
10
, the number of NESS is at least the number of distinct eigenvalues of strong symmetries in the system. Indeed, the Liouvillian is block-diagonalized under left and right actions by strong symmetries, and steady states live in the “diagonal” blocks where the left and right labels coincide. In the bilayer representation, this enforces matched hexagonal fluxes,
W_{p}=\widetilde{W}_{p}
, for every plaquette
p
, so each steady sector is labeled by a common flux pattern across both layers. Note that each of the hexagonal plaquettes
W_{p}
can be
+1
or
-1
; the only requirement is a matched pattern on the two layers.

Next we analyze the weak symmetries. Following Ref.
10
, we will assume that each sector labeled by eigenvalues of the weak symmetries contains at most one NESS. This behavior is typical in local dissipative models
10
. Under this assumption, a sector can host an NESS only if it transforms trivially under all weak symmetries (i.e. all weak symmetries have eigenvalue 1). To see this, let
\mathcal{U}
be a weak symmetry acting as
\mathcal{U}\rho=V\rho V^{\dagger}
, where
V
is a unitary operator. If
\rho_{\text{ss}}
is the unique steady state that satisfies
\mathcal{L}\rho_{\text{ss}}=0
, then:

0=\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{L}(\rho_{ss}))=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}\rho_{ss}).

(33)

By uniqueness,
\mathcal{U}\rho_{ss}=u\rho_{ss}
for some eigenvalue
u
. Taking the trace on both sides yields
\Tr(V\rho_{ss}V^{\dagger})=\Tr(\rho_{ss})=u\Tr(\rho_{ss})\rightarrow u=1
. For our model, these constraints select weak-symmetry sectors with a uniform interlayer flux parity
V_{p}=-1
on every plaquette. Note that an eigenvalue of
1
under the weak symmetry in Eq. (
26
) implies
V_{p}=-1
due to the minus sign in the definition of
V_{p}
in Eq. (
25
). It also constraints steady states to sectors with zero relative U
(1)
charge,
\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{y}-\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{y}=0
. In terms of the fermionic variables, the requirement for zero relative U
(1)
translates to:

\sum_{i}n_{i}+\tilde{n}_{i}=N,

(34)

i.e. one fermion per site shared across the two layers on average.

We can count the number of NESS following the above analysis. On a honeycomb lattice with
N
sites and periodic boundary conditions, there are
E=\frac{3}{2}N
edges. Using Euler’s formula for planar graph
V+E-F=2
, where
V
,
E
,
F
denote the number of vertices, edges, and faces respectively, we see that there are
N/2
hexagonal plaquettes. The plaquette fluxes satisfy the global constraint
\prod_{p}W_{p}=1
, leaving
\frac{N}{2}-1
independent fluxes. In addition, there are two independent, non-contractible
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
Wilson loops. Hence the number of diagonal sectors under decomposition under the strong symmetries is
2^{N/2-1}\times 2^{2}=2^{N/2+1}
. Imposing the weak-symmetry constraints yields exactly one NESS per (weak symmetry) sector. Therefore, the number of NESS is
2^{N/2+1}
; this may reduce to
2^{N/2-1}
if global Wilson loops are pinned by boundary conditions. The resulting exponentially large number of NESS demonstrates that our model realizes a type of “dissipative spin liquid”, as discussed in Refs.
29
;
74
;
78
.

V
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
-symmetry breaking and Liouvillian exceptional rings

In the previous section, we analyzed strong and weak symmetries to pin down sectors that support non-equilibrium steady states (NESS), namely with
W_{p}=\widetilde{W}_{p},V_{p}=-1
. However, the transient dynamics outside those sectors remains rich. In fact, the NESS-supporting sectors constitute but an exponentially small fraction of all flux sectors.

In this section, we deliberately break away from sectors that govern long-time dynamics, and study relaxation restricted to a flux sector that does
not
contain NESS. We first analytically compute the single-particle spectrum of the Liouvillian by choosing a translation invariant flux configuration. This amounts to diagonalizing the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
in the bilayer picture. We discover that the exceptional points (EPs), at which eigenvalues and eigenstates coalesce, form a ring in momentum space. Eigen-modes inside and outside the ring are
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
preserving and broken, respectively. We discuss the qualitative consequences of
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetry breaking on observable relaxation dynamics.

In addition to unitary symmetries, open quantum systems may also exhibit an anti-unitary parity-time (
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
) symmetry. Intuitively, an open quantum system is
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetric if the combined action of a unitary super-operator
\mathcal{P}
and anti-unitary super-operator
\mathcal{T}
leaves the dynamics unchanged, up to a uniform shift in the Liouvillian spectrum.

Interestingly, our dissipative model is
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetric, according to the criteria by Prosen
67
. We will take
\mathcal{P}
to be

\mathcal{P}(\rho)=U\rho U^{\dagger},

(35)

where the unitary
U
is:

U=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{y}\right)\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\tau},

(36)

i.e.
U
is a parity operator on the
\sigma
channel and acts trivially on the
\tau
sector. On the other hand,
\mathcal{T}
is taken to be conjugation on operators and imaginary numbers:

\mathcal{T}(\rho)\mathcal{T}^{-1}=\rho^{*}=\rho^{T}\,\,;\,\,\mathcal{T}(i)\mathcal{T}^{-1}=-i,

(37)

where that we have implicitly used the fact that
\rho
is Hermitian. In Appendix.
A
, we detail the criteria for a Liouvillian to be
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetric
68
;
67
and prove that our model satisfies the conditions, for the above choice of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
. Therefore, under the mapping
i\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{H}
, the traceless part of the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, defined as
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}-c\mathbb{I}
with
c=\frac{\text{Tr}(\mathcal{H})}{\text{Tr}(\mathbb{I})}
, is
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetric in the usual sense for a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian.

Like other symmetries,
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry can be spontaneously broken. This can be diagnosed directly from the eigenvalue structure of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
. Consider an eigenstate
|\psi\rangle
of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}|\psi\rangle=E|\psi\rangle
.
If
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is
\mathcal{PT}
symmetric,
[\mathcal{PT},\mathcal{H}^{\prime}]=0
, then
\mathcal{PT}|\psi\rangle
is also an eigenstate of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
:

H^{\prime}(\mathcal{PT}|\psi\rangle)=\mathcal{PT}(H^{\prime}|\psi\rangle)=\mathcal{PT}(E|\psi\rangle)=E^{*}\mathcal{PT}|\psi\rangle),

(38)

where we used the anti-unitarity of
\mathcal{PT}
, which complex-conjugates eigenvalues, in the last step. Thus
\mathcal{PT}
maps an eigenstate with eigenvalue
E
to an eigenstate with eigenvalue
E^{*}
. If the eigenvalues are real,
E=E^{*}
, it implies
\mathcal{PT}|\psi\rangle\propto|\psi\rangle
, so
|\psi\rangle
is also an eigenstate of
\mathcal{PT}
and the mode
\ket{\psi}
is said to be
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
preserving. In contrast, if
E
is complex (
E\neq E^{*}
), then
|\psi\rangle
and
\mathcal{PT}|\psi\rangle
are linearly independent eigenstates with different eigenvalues, so
|\psi\rangle
cannot be an eigenstate of
\mathcal{PT}
;
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry is then spontaneously broken for that particular mode
6
;
57
;
7
.

To best leverage the solvability of our model, we choose a translation-invariant flux configuration, which allows for computing the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
in momentum space analytically. Specifically, we fix the intralayer plaquette fluxes to
W_{p}=\widetilde{W}_{p}=1
, and the interlayer plaquettes to
V_{p}=1
. Importantly, this sector does
not
host NESS; the NESS reside in sectors where
V_{p}=-1
. Nonetheless, the choice of this sector is ideal for studying the Liouvillian single-particle spectrum analytically.

In order to realize this flux configuration, we set the bond variables to:

u_{ij}=\tilde{u}_{ij}=v_{i}=1\,\,\forall\,i,j.

(39)

We then implement the Fourier transformation by introducing
f_{j\in A}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{j}}a_{\bm{k}}
and
f_{j\in B}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{j}}b_{\bm{k}}
for sublattices
A
and
B
respectively, with analogous expressions for the other fermion species
\tilde{f}
. In momentum space,
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
can be written as

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\Psi^{\dagger}(\bm{k})h(\bm{k})\Psi(\bm{k}),

(40)

where

h(\bm{k})=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&Ji\Delta(\bm{k})&4\gamma&0\\
-Ji\Delta(\bm{k})^{*}&0&0&4\gamma\\
-4\gamma&0&0&-Ji\Delta(\bm{k})\\
0&-4\gamma&Ji\Delta(\bm{k})^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}

(41)

is the Bloch Hamiltonian,
\Psi(\bm{k})=(a_{\bm{k}},\,b_{\bm{k}},\,\tilde{a}_{\bm{k}},\,\tilde{b}_{\bm{k}})^{T}
, and

\Delta(\bm{k})=e^{-ik_{x}}\left[1+2e^{3ik_{x}/2}\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}k_{y}\right)\right]

(42)

is the structure factor on a honeycomb lattice, where we have set the lattice spacing
a=1
.

Diagonalizing Eq. (
41
), we obtain two doubly degenerate bands,

E_{\pm}(\bm{k})=\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{J^{2}|\Delta(\bm{k})|^{2}-16\gamma^{2}}.

(43)

The eigenvalues
E_{\pm}(\bm{k})
are purely real when
J^{2}|\Delta(\bm{k})|^{2}>16\gamma^{2}
, corresponding to
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
preserving modes and purely imaginary Liouvillian eigenvalues
\lambda(\bm{k})
. When
J^{2}|\Delta(\bm{k})|^{2}<16\gamma^{2}
,
E_{\pm}(\bm{k})
are imaginary and the corresponding Liouvillian modes are decaying in time. The critical points
(k_{x}^{*},k_{y}^{*})
at which
E=0
define the boundary between these two qualitatively distinct regimes. At the critical points, the spectrum exhibits a singularity and eigenvalues coalesce in pair, rendering them exceptional points (EPs) of the model
90
;
22
;
40
;
8
;
91
;
61
. The location of EPs can be solved as

1+4\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}k_{y}^{*}\right)+4\cos\left(\frac{3}{2}k_{x}^{*}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}k_{y}^{*}\right)=16\frac{\gamma^{2}}{J^{2}}.

(44)

In Fig.
3
, we plot the real and imaginary parts of the energy gap,
E_{+}-E_{-}
, over the Brillouin zone for the dissipation strength
\gamma=0.4
, with
J=1
. The set of EPs in momentum space forms an “exceptional ring”, as shown in the yellow contours. The exceptional rings encircle the
\Gamma
point
(k_{x},k_{y})=(0,0)
. Inside the ring, the gap is
purely real
, indicating
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
preserving modes, while outside the rings it is
purely imaginary
, corresponding to
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
broken modes.

Note that the
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetry breaking threshold is different for modes labelled by different momenta. In particular, let
\Delta_{\text{max}}=\max_{\bm{k}}\Delta(\bm{k})=3
denote the maximum of the structure factor over the Brillouin zone.
For
\gamma>\gamma^{*}=\sqrt{J^{2}|\Delta_{\text{max}}(\bm{k})|^{2}/16}=3J/4
, all eigenvalues become entirely imaginary. The momentum-resolved
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetry breaking is similar to the exceptional rings emerging from interactions and disorders in a Kitaev spin liquid
90
. However, the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian in our model arises from an exact mapping from the Liouvillian, as opposed to an effective description incorporating effects of the interactions. Therefore, the dissipation strength
\gamma
can serve as a tuning parameter for the shape and size of the exceptional rings.

Two remarks are in order regarding the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{L}
. First, while we worked with the traceless part
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}-c\mathbb{I}
, all discussions above apply to the full Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
. Indeed, since
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
are related by a constant offset and share the same set of eigenvectors, the two Hamiltonians have the same
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
breaking threshold and therefore the same set of EPs.

Secondly, we emphasize that all results presented above are within a single flux sector
W_{p}=\widetilde{W}_{p}=V_{p}=1
, chosen for its analytical tractability. The full, many-body spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
is the union of eigenvalues in all of the (exponentially many) flux sectors. However, since the Liouvillian is block-diagonal under strong and weak symmetries, dynamics is restricted to the initial sector. Using this observation, we could probe the dynamical consequences of
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetry breaking.

To this end, we first need to prepare an initial density matrix
\rho_{0}
that lies in the flux sector of interest. This can be achieved by applying projectors that enforce the flux configuration. In particular, since
W_{p}
,
\widetilde{W}_{p}
, and
V_{p}
mutually commute, we can define projectors

\displaystyle P_{W}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\frac{1+W_{p}}{2}\cdot\frac{1+\widetilde{W}_{p}}{2};

(45)

\displaystyle P_{V}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\frac{1+V_{p}}{2},

(46)

which project onto the subspace in which all hexagonal fluxes on both layers and all verticle plaquettes satisfy
W_{p}=\widetilde{W}_{p}=1
,
V_{p}=1
. Then we can define the (properly normalized) initial density matrix as:

\rho_{0}=\frac{P_{W}P_{V}(\rho)}{\text{Tr}(P_{W}P_{V}(\rho))},

(47)

where
\rho
is any initial density matrix. It is easy to check that
W_{p}\rho_{0}=\rho_{0}W_{p}=V_{p}\rho_{0}=\rho_{0}
. In practice, one can choose
\rho
to be a simple translation-invariant state, such as the maximally mixed state
\rho=\mathbb{I}
or a pure state
\rho=\ket{\psi}\!\bra{\psi}
.

Since the initial density matrix is prepared in this sector, and both strong and weak fluxes are conserved quantities, the dynamics remains confined to the sector. Therefore, the Bloch Hamiltonian (Eq. (
41
)) governs the dynamics of observables and the exceptional ring structure has a tangible consequence on the relaxation dynamics. For example, consider the observable
M=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{y}
, the magnetization in the
y
-direction. Under Liouvillian dynamics, the expectation value of
M
is:

\braket{M(t)}=\text{Tr}(M\rho(t))=\text{Tr}(Me^{\mathcal{L}t}\rho_{0}).

(48)

This expectation value can also be computed in the bilayer picture. Recall that upon vectorization,
M\rightarrow M\otimes\mathbb{I}
,
\rho_{0}\rightarrow|\rho_{0}\rangle\!\rangle
, and
\mathcal{L}\rightarrow-i\mathcal{H}
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\braket{M(t)}

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle M\otimes\mathbb{I}|e^{-i\mathcal{H}t}|\rho_{0}\rangle\!\rangle.

(49)

In terms of complex fermions, the magnetization can be written as
M=\sum_{i}f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{i}-N,
i.e. it is the fermion occupation number on the original layer, up to a constant. Note that while the
total
fermion number is conserved, the number on each layer can change due to the hopping term in Eq. (
16
).

We can extract the qualitative behavior of this expectation value by computing in momentum space. Importantly, since both
\rho_{0}
and
M
are translation-invariant, only the uniform
(\bm{k}=\Gamma=(0,0))
mode of the Bloch Hamiltonian contributes to
\braket{M(t)}
. All other momenta average to zero when summed over the Brillouin zone. From the spectrum Eq. (
43
) and the fact that
\Delta(\bm{k}=(0,0))=3
, we see that for
\gamma<3J/4
, the eigenvalues of
h(\bm{k})
are entirely real, corresponding to oscillatory dynamics. On the other hand, for
\gamma>3J/4
,
E_{\pm}(\bm{k}=(0,0))
becomes imaginary, leading to decaying Liouvillian modes. Finally, reinstating the constant shift of Liouvillian spectrum (which we denote as
c_{L}=\frac{\text{Tr}(\mathcal{L})}{\text{Tr}(\mathbb{I})}
and manifests as a rescaling of the observables
83
), we expect the magnetization to scale as:

\braket{M(t)}\sim e^{-c_{L}t}\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\cos(\sqrt{9J^{2}-16\gamma^{2}}t),\gamma<\frac{3}{4}J;\\
e^{-\sqrt{16\gamma^{2}-9J^{2}}t},\gamma>\frac{3}{4}J,\end{array}\right.

(50)

There is a clear, qualitative difference in the dynamics. Specifically, up to a decaying envelope,
\braket{M(t)}
remains oscillatory throughout the
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
mixed phase and transitions to decaying when
\gamma>\gamma^{*}
. This could in principle be observed in experimental platforms where both the spin-orbital Hamiltonian and local spin dissipations could be engineered. To probe other points in momentum space, one could measure spatially modulated observables.

Our analysis, therefore, provides a qualitative description of the relaxation dynamics for any state prepared within this specific sector. This demonstrates that
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetry breaking is a genuine dynamical feature of the model, existing alongside the dissipative spin liquid physics (i.e., the NESS) found in other sectors.

VI
Summary and Discussion

In this work, we study a dissipative variant of the Yao-Lee spin-orbital model. By vectorizing density matrices, we map the Liouvillian to a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian acting on two copies of the original Hilbert space. We decompose the spin and orbital operators into Majoranas, followed by rewriting in terms of complex fermions. Since the Liouvillian is quadratic in the fermion operators, the model is exactly solvable. We analyze the model’s (extensive number of) strong and weak symmetries and demonstrate that it hosts an exponential large manifold of non-equilibrium steady states.

Furthermore, we break away from sectors that host NESS and analytically compute the Liouvillian eigenvalues in a translationally-invariant symmetry sector. We discover a three-stage behavior of the eigenvalues as the dissipation strength
\gamma
is increased: first, below a system-size dependent threshold
\gamma_{\text{PT}}
, all eigenvalues are imaginary, corresponding to a
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
preserving phase. For
\gamma_{\text{PT}}<\gamma<\gamma^{*}
, the spectrum contains both purely real and purely imaginary eigenvalues, and the system is in a
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
-mixed phase
83
. Finally, when the dissipation strength is larger than
\gamma^{*}
, all modes have real eigenvalues and decay with distinct rates, i.e. the system is
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
broken. We are able to obtain a qualitative (from oscillatory to decaying) crossover of observable dynamics, up to a decaying envelope.

We supplement our analysis with numerical calculations in both real and momentum space. In
k
-space, we discover that the exceptional points form an exceptional ring
90
. In real space, we calculate the Liouvillian eigenvalues and demonstrate that the scalings of
\gamma_{\text{PT}}
and
\gamma^{*}
with system size are in agreement with our analysis.

There are a few interesting directions for future exploration. First, in the long-time limit, the relaxation timescale is determined by the Liouvillian gap, which is the slowest non-zero decay rate and governs relaxation dynamics at long times in the system
56
. More precisely, since every eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
of
\mathcal{L}
has a non-positive real part
66
, we can order the Liouvillian spectrum by its real part as
0=|\Re\lambda_{0}|<|\Re\lambda_{1}|<\cdots<|\Re\lambda_{n}|
. The Liouvillian gap is defined as
56

g=|\Re\lambda_{1}|.

(51)

One can analyze “excitations” on top of the sectors that host steady states: in particular, the model hosts distinct excitations in the Liouvillian spectrum, corresponding to flipping a vertical plaquette
V_{p}
, flipping a hexagonal plaquette
W_{p}
, or creating/removing fermions. Note that since the fermion
number
, instead of parity
78
;
29
, is conserved, there is an extensive number of distinct excitations in the fermionic degree of freedom.

Another quantity that one could study in the long time limit is the purity of steady states, defined as:

P=\text{Tr}(\rho^{2}).

(52)

From Sec.
IV.2
, when we analyzed the nature of steady states for
\gamma=0
and
\gamma=\infty
, we have already seen a hint of this. Without dissipation, the system’s steady states are pure states; at very large dissipation,
\rho_{\text{ss}}
are fully mixed (i.e. identity) in the
\sigma
sector. Therefore, there might be a
purity transition
of the steady states as the dissipation strength increases
33
.

Finally, it is known from non-Hermitian physics that exceptional points in two dimensions and above have distinct topology compared to those in 1D
79
. While exceptional points in the Liouvillian spectrum have been studied in one-dimensional spin and fermionic systems, their nature has not been elucidated in a two-dimensional, dissipative setting. Our exactly solvable dissipative model, therefore, provides a playground for exploring the topology of Liouvillian exceptional points in a higher dimension.

Appendix A
Conditions for
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
Symmetric Liouvillian

On the super-operator level, a Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}
is
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetric if it satisfies
68
:

(\mathcal{{PT}})\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\mathcal{{PT}})^{-1}=-\mathcal{L}^{\prime\dagger},

(53)

under the combined action of two super-operator
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
, where

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}-\frac{\text{Tr}(\mathcal{L})}{\text{Tr}(\mathbb{I})}\mathbb{I}

(54)

is the traceless part of the Liouvillian.

The parity operation
\mathcal{P}
is unitary and squares to identity,
\mathcal{P}^{2}=I
. It acts on operators as

\mathcal{P}(\rho)=U\rho U^{\dagger},

(55)

where
U
is a unitary operator satisfying
U^{2}=1
. On the other hand,
\mathcal{T}
is
anti
-unitary, but also squares to identity
\mathcal{T}^{2}=I
. A common choice for
\mathcal{T}
is the complex conjugation (Eq.
37
), which we adopt in this work.

The above definition is generic on the super-operator level. On the operator level, it turns out that
\mathcal{L}
is
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetric if the Hamiltonian
H
and jump operators
\{L_{i}\}
satisfy the following conditions
67
:

1.

\{L_{m},L_{m}^{\dagger}\}=c_{m}\mathbb{I}
for some real
c_{m}
.

2.

UH^{*}U^{\dagger}=H
, where
H
is the Hamiltonian, and
U
is the unitary operator that implements parity as in Eq. (
55
).

3.

The set of jump operations
\{L_{i}\}
is
closed
under
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetry. In particular:

UL_{i}U^{\dagger}=-\sum_{j}V_{ij}L_{j}^{\dagger},

(56)

where
V
is a unitary matrix. Intuitively, the negative sign comes from the anticommutator in the Lindbladian, and this condition ensures that the jump terms
\sum_{i}L_{i}L_{i}^{\dagger}
in
\mathcal{L}
remain invariant under
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetry.

Our dissipative spin-orbital model satisfies all of the above conditions. The first condition is immediately met, as
\{\sigma_{i}^{x},(\sigma_{i}^{x})^{\dagger}\}=\{\sigma_{i}^{z},(\sigma_{i}^{z})^{\dagger}\}=2\mathbb{I}
for all sites
i
.
Under conjugation by the unitary
U
defined in Eq. (
36
), the Hamiltonian (Eq. (
6
)) (which is real,
H=H^{*}
) remains invariant. While conjugation by
U
flips the sign of individual
\sigma_{i}^{x}
and
\sigma_{i}^{z}
,
H
only contains two-body terms
\sigma_{i}^{x}\sigma_{j}^{x}
and
\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{j}^{z}
in the spin channel, and therefore condition (2) is satisfied. Finally, since
U\sigma_{i}^{z}U^{\dagger}=-\sigma_{i}^{z}
,
U\sigma_{i}^{x}U^{\dagger}=-\sigma_{i}^{x}
, and the jump operators are Hermitian,
L_{i}=L_{i}^{\dagger}
, condition (3) is satisfied, with
V=\mathbb{I}
. Therefore the Liouvillian in our model is indeed
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}
symmetric.
