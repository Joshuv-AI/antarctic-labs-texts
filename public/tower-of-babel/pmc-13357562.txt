Integrative transcriptomic and machine learning approach reveals key DDR genes and predictors of ami risk in the Bangladeshi population
Authors: Hasnat Zahin, Abdullah Al Saba, Rifat Hossain Ripon, A.H.M. Nurun Nabi, Tahirah Yasmin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute Myocardial Infarction (AMI) is a significant contributor to cardiovascular death, with rising prevalence in Bangladesh. Although DNA damage contributes to AMI pathogenesis, the role of DNA damage repair (DDR) genes remains poorly characterized. This study aims to identify key DDR genes associated with AMI, and develop a machine learning-based risk prediction model for the Bangladeshi population. Differentially expressed DDR genes in AMI were identified via bioinformatics analysis, with WGCNA revealing AMI-associated hub genes. These candidates were validated by qRT-PCR in Bangladeshi patients. A machine learning model using demographic and gene expression data was then trained using five classifiers. Differential expression and WGCNA identified Nibrin (NBN) and 8-Oxoguanine glycosylase (OGG1) as key dysregulated DDR genes. RT-qPCR confirmed a ~ 2.78-fold upregulation of OGG1 (p < 0.001) in AMI patients, while NBN expression was significantly higher in smokers (p < 0.05). Feature selection identified age, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, BMI, and OGG1 as critical AMI risk predictors. Logistic regression showed the best performance (accuracy 87.3%, AUC = 0.903). This study provides novel evidence linking DDR genes to AMI and presents a strong ML-based risk stratification model for the first time, integrating transcriptomic and clinical data from Bangladeshi patients to advance personalized AMI management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51826-y.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI), a common form of coronary artery disease (CAD), refers to the death of cells in the myocardium caused by the sudden occlusion of the coronary arteries. In Bangladesh, CAD affects approximately 4–6% of the population, while acute myocardial infarction (AMI) prevalence ranges from 0.3 to 1.0%1.Urban populations experience a higher AMI prevalence (8%) than rural areas (2%)2. Myocardial infarction is a multifactorial disease influenced by both modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors. Modifiable factors, such as smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, and an unhealthy diet, significantly contribute to MI risk. It can be addressed through lifestyle changes and medical interventions. Conversely, non-modifiable factors, including advanced age, gender, family history of early coronary artery disease, and specific ethnic backgrounds3(e.g., South Asians), are inherent and cannot be altered. While understanding the various modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors is essential for preventing and managing myocardial infarction, these factors do not operate separately. Many of them, such as smoking, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, have been linked to increased cellular stress and damage, triggering a cascade of molecular events that can compromise cellular integrity4–6. One critical mechanism that mediates the response to cellular stress is DNA damage repair (DDR). The ability of cells to repair DNA damage efficiently is vital in preventing the progression of myocardial infarction. Recent evidence suggests that dysfunctions in DDR pathways may exacerbate the detrimental effects of risk factors, contributing to the onset and progression of cardiovascular diseases7. Therefore, exploring the role of DDR in the context of these risk factors provides an essential understanding of how myocardial infarction develops and progresses. For instance, a study in the UK population analyzed the expression of 22 DDR genes in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). The result of this study showed differences in gene expression in pathways related to DNA double-strand break repair, nucleotide excision repair, and base excision repair8. One common oxidative lesion, 8-oxoguanine, is removed by 8-oxoguanine DNA glycosylase 1 (OGG1), which plays a key role in base excision repair. Impaired OGG1 function leads to the accumulation of damaged DNA, genomic instability, and cell death during AMI9. Another gene, NBN, encodes NBS1, a key protein involved in detecting DNA double-strand breaks and activating their repair. If NBN function is impaired, unresolved DNA breaks increase genomic instability and cell death, worsening heart injury10.

Despite the vital role of biomarkers in the diagnosis of acute myocardial infarction, existing markers such as cardiac troponins, creatine kinase-MB, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein etc11. exhibit limitations in specificity, sensitivity, and early detection. To address these issues, bioinformatics-based methodologies have emerged as effective instruments for discovering critical gene sets that influence disease pathogenesis. Through the examination of gene expression patterns and their interactions, these methodologies yield profound insights into the molecular processes behind acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and present novel opportunities for biomarker identification and risk classification. As DNA damage and repair are common during MI, DDR pathway genes could be potential biomarkers.

However, the specific DDR-related genes and their clinical significance remain poorly understood. This study, therefore, aimed to bridge this knowledge gap by using integrative bioinformatics and machine learning to identify key DDR-related genes associated with AMI and develop a risk stratification model using the expression pattern of DDR genes in the Bangladeshi population. Furthermore, this work investigated the influence of smoking on the expression of these identified key genes to have a deeper understanding of how smoking increases AMI risk in the Bangladeshi population. It is known that tobacco smoke contains numerous harmful compounds, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), nitrosamines, and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which contribute to DNA adduct formation, oxidative stress, and DNA strand breaks12. To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate both lifestyle habits and expression profiles of key DDR genes in the Bangladeshi population to develop a population-specific risk prediction model for myocardial infarction (MI).

Results

Data collection

A total of 57 myocardial infarction (MI) samples and 58 healthy controls were obtained from two NCBI-GEO datasets to identify prognostic biomarkers associated with DNA damage repair (DDR) pathways. The GSE66360 dataset is a microarray-based study where circulating endothelial cells were isolated from blood samples. In contrast, GSE166780 is an RNA sequencing dataset generated using high-throughput sequencing, with samples derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). The detailed information on these two datasets is presented in the supplementary Table 1.

Identification of the differentially expressed DDR genes and functional enrichment analysis

The first dataset, GSE66360, contained 2,312 upregulated genes and 2,839 downregulated genes, and the second dataset, GSE166780, included 1,626 upregulated and 2,832 downregulated genes based on the filtering criteria (|log2FC| > 0.5 and p-value < 0.05). In GSE66360, 14 DDR genes exhibited significant differential expression (Table 1), of which 11 genes demonstrated upregulation and three genes showed downregulation. In GSE166780, 14 DDR genes (Table 2) showed significant differential expression; 4 were upregulated, and 10 were downregulated. The overall distribution of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of both datasets is illustrated in the volcano plots (Fig. 1a and b). Three genes, NBN, OGG1, and RAD52, were found common in both datasets (Fig. 1c) when comparing differentially expressed DDR genes. Furthermore, GO terms of biological processes associated with the DNA damage response and repair were enriched for all the differentially expressed genes, including the regulation of double-strand break repair and nucleotide excision repair, etc. (Fig. 1d).

Construction of co-expression networks

To identify biologically relevant gene modules and explore their association with AMI, Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was applied. This method enables the detection of co-expressed gene clusters that may contribute to the molecular mechanisms underlying the disease. Following the dataset’s preprocessing, dataset GSE166780 was assessed for quality and sample clustering. Hierarchical clustering, illustrated in Fig. 2a, indicates no outliers in the data. The cluster dendrogram in Fig. 2b illustrates the hierarchical organization of genes based on co-expression patterns. The well-defined clustering structure indicates that genes within the same module likely share functional similarities, making these modules biologically significant. The optimal soft-thresholding power (β) for this WGCNA analysis was determined to be 18, as it balances the trade-off between achieving a scale-free topology and maintaining reasonable network connectivity in the Scale-Free Topology Fit Index plot (Fig. 2c). Meanwhile, the Mean Connectivity plot (Fig. 2d) showed a steep decline as power increased. At β = 18, the mean connectivity is sufficiently reduced to minimize excessive connections while still preserving the integrity of the network structure. Thus, β = 18 was chosen as the optimal value to ensure a biologically relevant network structure.

The Module-Trait Relationship Heat map (Fig. 2e) shows the correlation between gene co-expression modules (rows) and a disease-related trait (column). A total of 32 modules were identified. Among those 32 modules, nine modules (MEgrey, MEturquiso, MEblack, MEred, MEdarkgrey, MEsteelblue, MEdarkturquiso, MEdarkred, MEbrown) showed a significant correlation (> 0.5) with the trait, with a p-value < 0.05 (Fig. 2f). Notably, the MEturquoise module shows the strongest negative correlation with the disease trait (r = −0.77, p = 0.00513). Conversely, Module MEpink (r = 0.82, p = 0.00364) exhibited the highest positive correlations. A similar WGCNA analysis was performed for dataset GSE66360. However, no significant correlation was found between the gene modules and disease trait. The data, therefore, is not shown here.

Identification of differentially expressed DDR hub genes

Hub genes within the WGCNA modules were identified through their Module Membership (MM) values, which calculate the correlation of each gene with the module eigengene. Among the hub genes in the nine significant modules, module MEbrown contains eight DDR hub genes: MLH1, PMS2, CHK2, OGG1, ERCC4, POLD1, PARP1, and CDK7; Module MEred encompasses a single DDR hub gene, BRIP1 and MEturquoise contains four DDR hub genes: BARD1, NBN, POLD1, and ABL.

An intersection-based filtering method was applied to retain only those genes that were both differentially expressed and classified as hub genes within the network (Fig. 3a). This analysis revealed that OGG1 (shared between DEGs and MEbrown) and NBN (shared between DEGs and MEturquoise) were the key candidate genes demonstrating significant expression changes and functional importance in AMI. The first network, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network in Fig. 3b, reveals how NBN interacts with key DNA damage response proteins, including ATM, TP53BP1, and BRCA1, emphasizing its role in double-strand break repair. On the other hand, OGG1 is linked to base excision repair (BER) proteins like RAD9A, XRCC1, and APEX1, confirming its function in oxidative DNA damage repair. The denser connections around NBN indicate its involvement in multiple DNA repair pathways, while OGG1 appears more specialized in oxidative damage repair. The second network in Fig. 3c illustrates the regulatory interactions between NBN and OGG1 with microRNAs (miRNAs) and transcription factors (TFs). The presence of multiple miRNAs (blue squares), such as hsa-miR-145 and hsa-miR-410, suggests post-transcriptional regulation, while TFs (green diamonds) like SP1, STAT3, and EGR1 likely control their transcription.

Gene expression analysis in the Bangladeshi population

To validate the bioinformatics findings in our population, gene expression analysis was performed using RT-PCR. Initially, 70 acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patient samples and 60 healthy control samples were included in the study. However, after performing quality control (QC) by assessing melting curves and Ct value reliability, the final number of samples was reduced to 64 AMI patients and 56 healthy controls. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the validation cohort, including age, sex, and the prevalence of traditional risk factors such as hypertension and smoking, are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. The housekeeping gene used was GAPDH, and normalized gene expression was calculated using the ΔCt method. The relative gene expression change (fold change) was analyzed using the 2–ΔΔCt method, and results were plotted on a log₂ scale. Before calculating relative expression and fold change, Ct values were adjusted for potential confounders, including age, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, and family history of cardiovascular disease.

OGG1 and NBN gene expression analysis

The relative expression of OGG1 gene was significantly higher in the patients, with a p-value < 0.001 (Fig. 4a). The relative expression of the gene was approximately 2.78 higher in the patient group compared to the control. After the adjustment, the relative expression of the gene remained significantly higher in the patients’ group, with a p-value < 0.001(Fig. 4b).

For the NBN gene, the relative expression was not significantly higher in the patient’s group, with a p-value of > 0.05 (Fig. 4c). After the adjustment, the change was still not significant (Fig. 4d), with a p-value > 0.05.

Smoking’s effect on gene expression

There was no significant difference in the relative expression levels of NBN and OGG1 between the smoker patient group and the smoker control group (p > 0.05) (Fig. 5a and b). However, considering only patients, the relative expression of the NBN gene was significantly higher in smokers compared to non-smokers (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5c), while OGG1 did not show any significant difference between the two groups (Fig. 5d).

Co-expression analysis of the genes

The Spearman correlation analysis between NBN and OGG1 gene expression reveals a moderate positive correlation with a Spearman’s rho of 0.46 and a highly significant p-value < 0.001 (Fig. 5e). This suggests that as the expression of NBN increases, the expression of OGG1 also tends to rise and vice-versa.

Machine learning models

Feature selection

The original set of features included age, BMI, gender, hypertension, diabetes, family history of cardiovascular disease (CVD), and the relative expressions of OGG1 and NBN. Feature selection was performed using Elastic Net regularization, which identified age, BMI, smoking status, hypertension, family history of cardiovascular disease (CVD), and fold change of OGG1 as the most relevant predictors of acute myocardial infarction (AMI).

Model training

This selected feature set was used to train multiple machine-learning models for AMI classification. The dataset was split into 80% training and 20% testing subsets with 10-fold cross-validation, ensuring stratified sampling to maintain the class distribution. The classification performance of six machine learning models was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and AUC-ROC metrics. Logistic regression achieved the highest individual model accuracy at 87.3%, followed by Random Forest (RF) at 82.3%, naïve Bayes at 82.1%, K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) at 81.1%, and Support Vector Machine at 80.9%. The ensemble model, which combined the predictions from logistic regression, random forest, SVM, and naïve Bayes and KNN, exhibited the overall performance with an accuracy of 81.1%. Figure 6a shows the accuracy of all six models.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for logistic regression, random forest, and SVM models demonstrated strong discriminatory power, with high sensitivity indicating their ability to identify AMI cases (Fig. 6b to g) correctly.

The logistic Regression (LR) model was developed to predict the risk of AMI, with the resulting formula indicating the relative contributions of age, BMI, tobacco use, hypertension, family history of CVD, and fold change of OGG1, as quantified by their respective coefficients.

The risk formula of AMI according to the LR model = − 9.6336 + 0.1354* (Age) + 1.1176* (Tobacco) + 0.0556* (BMI) + 4.7597* (Hypertension) + 1.0382* (FHCVD) + 0.0127* (OGG1_FC) To evaluate the incremental contribution of OGG1 expression, model performance was compared using clinical variables alone versus combined clinical and molecular features across five machine learning algorithms (Supplementary Table 3). In logistic regression, inclusion of OGG1 improved accuracy from 81.8% to 87.3% and increased AUC from 0.893 to 0.903. Random forest and support vector machine models demonstrated modest increases in both accuracy and AUC following integration of OGG1 expression. For Naïve Bayes, accuracy improved (71.7% to 82.1%), whereas AUC remained unchanged. In contrast, KNN showed no improvement in performance metrics with the addition of OGG1. Overall, the integrated models demonstrated modest performance gains in selected algorithms, particularly logistic regression, while clinical variables accounted for the majority of predictive capacity.

Discussion

In this study, key DDR genes associated with acute myocardial infarction were studied in AMI patients from Bangladesh, a population that is underrepresented in cardiovascular genomics. These findings were further used to develop a risk prediction model for the Bangladeshi population by integrating both clinical and molecular data. While classical risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and smoking are well-established contributors to AMI, recent research has highlighted the emerging role of dysregulated DNA damage repair (DDR) mechanisms in cardiovascular pathophysiology. Despite the growing evidence of the role of DDR in myocardial infarction13,14, there is lack of research on DDR gene expression patterns in AMI patients, particularly in a developing country like Bangladesh. Most prior studies have focused on animal models while limited investigations have been carried out using human blood samples15,16. In particular, AMI patients from South Asian populations remain largely overlooked in DDR-related transcriptomic research. This study provides novel insights into the molecular mechanisms of AMI by focusing on the expression and co-expression network involvement of DDR genes. By conducting differential gene expression (DGE) analysis on two independent transcriptomic datasets: one microarray and one RNA-seq, a subset of 14 DDR genes, consistently altered in AMI, were identified, among which OGG1, NBN, and RAD52 were common across both platforms. This overlap strengthens the biological relevance of these genes and helps eliminate platform-specific artifacts. While previous studies17,18 focused on overall differential gene expression patterns in acute myocardial infarction patients, this study explicitly examines changes in the expression of DDR genes. Functional enrichment analyses revealed that these genes are involved in pathways associated with double-strand break repair, nucleotide excision repair, and recombinational repair, consistent with existing studies that demonstrate how DNA damage can trigger inflammatory responses, endothelial dysfunction, and cell death in the myocardium19,20. Additionally, to envisage the regulatory architecture of DDR gene expression, Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was performed, which identified 32 co-expression modules, nine of which were significantly correlated with AMI status. The differentially expressed DDR genes were then mapped onto these modules to determine which genes were both differentially expressed and functioned as hub genes. This combined strategy of integrating differential gene expression analysis with WGCNA-based hub gene identification is particularly powerful because it captures both the magnitude of gene expression changes and the functional importance of genes within co-regulated biological networks. This integrative approach identified 8-oxo guanine glycosylase 1 (OGG1) and nibrin (NBN) as key DDR genes that were not only significantly dysregulated in AMI but also centrally positioned in disease-relevant co-expression networks.

In addition to OGG1 and NBN, several other DDR-related hub genes such as PARP1, POLD1, CHK2, and BRIP1 were identified in AMI-associated modules, suggesting the involvement of multiple DNA repair pathways, including base excision repair (BER), mismatch repair, and homologous recombination, in the molecular pathology of AMI. Further interaction network analysis revealed that OGG1 was clustered with BER-related genes like XRCC1 and APEX1, while NBN formed a network with key double-strand break repair proteins such as RAD51, ATM, and BRCA1. Moreover, both OGG1 and NBN were found to be regulated by a complex network of microRNAs and transcription factors, indicating their sensitivity to both genetic and environmental influences. This analysis reinforces their importance as potential biomarkers and key regulators in the DDR response to AMI.

The bioinformatics findings were next validated in a cohort of AMI patients from Bangladesh through qRT-PCR. It was found that 8-oxo guanine glycosylase 1 (OGG1), a BER protein responsible for eliminating 8-oxoG, was significantly upregulated in patients, supporting its role as a biomarker of oxidative DNA damage. This strong upregulation in AMI patients supports the hypothesis that OGG1 is critical in responding to oxidative DNA damage during myocardial infarction21. The increased expression of OGG1 suggests an attempt to repair oxidative lesions induced by ischemic injury. In contrast, NBN, a core component of the MRE11–RAD50–NBS1 (MRN) complex essential for DNA double-strand break (DSB) recognition and homologous recombination repair10, showed a more complex result. At the systems level, WGCNA revealed that the MEturquoise module, containing NBN had the strongest negative correlation with AMI status, suggesting an overall suppression of DSB repair–related pathways in AMI patients independent of smoking history. This indicates that impairment of homologous recombination and DSB repair may represent a broader feature of AMI pathophysiology.

However, subgroup analysis revealed that NBN expression was significantly upregulated in AMI patients who smoked in this population. Rather than contradicting the overall module-level downregulation, this finding may reflect a compensatory response to chronic tobacco-induced genotoxic stress superimposed on a globally suppressed repair background. Tobacco smoke contains numerous DNA-damaging agents capable of inducing double-strand breaks4, and persistent exposure may selectively stimulate the expression of specific DDR genes such as NBN, even when the broader repair network is functionally compromised. This gene-environment interaction suggests that smoking may modulate DDR dynamics in AMI and needs further mechanistic investigation. Although OGG1 and NBN showed different overall differential expression patterns in the public transcriptomic dataset, correlation analysis in the Bangladeshi cohort revealed a positive association between their expression levels. This difference likely reflects the distinction between group-level differential expression and individual-level co-regulation. Coordinated activation of multiple DNA damage repair pathways in response to oxidative stress may drive positive correlation within individuals, even when average expression changes differ between cases and controls. Additionally, platform-related and population-specific factors may contribute to these differences. In the context of clinical application, a machine learning (ML)-based risk model was developed for predicting the severity and prognosis of AMI in the Bangladeshi population by integrating clinical variables (e.g., age, BMI, hypertension, family history, smoking) and fold change of gene expression data. Risk prediction models have long been used to assess myocardial infarction susceptibility based on clinical and biochemical parameters. Traditional models, such as the Framingham Risk Score22 and GRACE score23, primarily rely on demographic factors, lipid profiles, blood pressure, and lifestyle-related variables. While these models provide useful risk estimations, they often overlook the molecular alterations that precede clinical manifestations. Recent advances in ML have enabled the incorporation of high-dimensional datasets, including genetic and transcriptomic markers, to improve MI risk and severity prediction. However, in these datasets, data from South Asian populations are largely absent. In this study, to enhance model performance and interpretability, valuable features were selected using Elastic Net regularization thus eliminating redundant or weakly correlated variables and ensuring that only the most informative predictors contributed to AMI risk assessment. Using Elastic Net feature selection and multiple ML algorithms, the Logistic Regression model achieved high prediction accuracy, reaching 87% accuracy and an AUC of 0.903. Importantly, OGG1 expression was selected as a strong predictor alongside traditional risk factors, suggesting that incorporating molecular biomarkers can enhance AMI risk stratification. Although external validation was not possible due to the lack of an independent dataset containing all the features that this study included, 10-fold cross-validation was performed, and class imbalance was also addressed to strengthen the robustness and reliability of the models. Additionally, a risk formula was generated by Logistic regression, which would allow for the calculation of an individual’s risk of AMI severity. The resulting score would provide a quantitative measure of how the prognosis of individual AMI patients would be like which would lead to improved tailored intervention.

This study has several important implications. South Asian populations remain underrepresented in cardiovascular transcriptomic research. By generating population-specific molecular data and integrating gene expression features with established clinical predictors, this study contributes to understanding how oxidative DNA repair pathways relate to AMI within this demographic context.

The addition of OGG1 was associated with a modest increase in classification accuracy. To reduce potential confounding from systemic inflammatory conditions that could influence oxidative stress–related gene expression, individuals with known acute or chronic inflammatory diseases were excluded during sample collection. Nonetheless, OGG1 is not cardiomyocyte-specific and likely reflects broader systemic oxidative stress rather than myocardial-restricted injury. Therefore, while OGG1 was not alone a dominant independent discriminator in this dataset, its role in oxidative DNA repair provides mechanistic relevance linking cellular stress responses to cardiovascular pathology. The upregulation of NBN in smokers may reflect a compensatory response to this persistent DNA damage. Clinically, NBN could be explored as a molecular indicator of DNA damage burden in smokers and may help stratify risk or guide targeted interventions in high-risk individuals, particularly smokers with other comorbid conditions. The integration of molecular and clinical data into a machine learning-based risk prediction model demonstrates the utility of combining genomic and clinical information to improve AMI risk stratification and better treatment strategies. This approach not only enhances predictive accuracy but also bridges the gap between basic molecular research and clinical decision-making. Moreover, the study provides a foundation for future research into DDR-targeted therapies and highlights the need for more inclusive genomic studies to ensure the benefits of precision medicine to be widely distributed across diverse populations. However, this study has several limitations. The sample size for qRT-PCR validation (n = 120) and for machine learning model development was relatively modest, which may limit statistical power and generalizability. Although 10-fold cross-validation and class imbalance correction were applied to improve internal robustness, external validation could not be performed due to the absence of a publicly available dataset containing both qRT-PCR–based DDR gene expression measurements and the full set of clinical variables required to replicate the model. Most existing AMI transcriptomic datasets lack harmonized molecular and clinical data suitable for direct model validation. Future multi-center collaborations with standardized data collection protocols will be essential to validate and refine this integrative risk prediction model in larger and more diverse populations. Moreover, this study used a moderate differential expression threshold (|log2FC| > 0.5), which may include genes with smaller effect sizes. The results using a more stringent threshold (|log2FC| > 1.0) are provided in Supplementary Table 4 and 5. Under this criterion, OGG1 and NBN did not meet the fold-change cutoff, although they remained statistically significant and were consistently identified as hub genes in AMI-associated modules. This supports the interpretation that biological relevance in this context is better reflected by coordinated network behavior rather than fold-change magnitude alone. In this study network-based filtering and experimental validation were applied to reduce false positives, however, future studies with larger sample sizes and independent cohorts may further refine effect size thresholds and validate the identified candidate genes. In addition, while transcriptomic data provide valuable insights, gene expression does not always correlate with protein function. Thus, follow-up studies involving protein-level validation (e.g., Western blotting, ELISA, immunohistochemistry) are necessary. Finally, longitudinal sampling could further elucidate the temporal dynamics of DDR gene activation during different stages of AMI, from onset to recovery.

Conclusion

All in all, this study makes significant contributions to the field of AMI research. It provides the first comprehensive analysis of DDR gene expression in AMI patients from Bangladesh, identifies key hub genes and co-expression networks through in-silico analyses, highlights a gene-environment interaction with smoking, and develops a high-accuracy ML-based risk prediction model for the Bangladeshi population. These findings have important implications for understanding AMI pathogenesis, identifying potential therapeutic targets, and improving risk assessment strategies. Future research based on these findings, therefore, holds significant promise for advancing personalized medicine and developing more effective preventative and therapeutic approaches for AMI in Bangladesh.

Materials and methods

Data mining

Two datasets, GSE6636024(49 myocardial infarction patients and 50 healthy controls) and GSE16678025(8 acute myocardial infarction patients and 8 healthy controls), were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus26. The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) is a publicly available, open-access database managed by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), USA. Datasets were selected based on the following criteria: (1) the dataset must be originated from Homo sapiens, (2) RNA must be extracted from whole blood samples, and (3) the patient cohort must include individuals diagnosed with acute myocardial infarction (AMI). The search keywords were “Homo Sapiens”, “Acute Myocardial Infarction”, and “whole blood”.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis can identify genes that show significant differences in gene expression levels across two or more conditions, including distinct cell types, disease states, or experimental treatments. The analysis was performed separately for datasets GSE66360 and GSE166780. Since GSE66360 is a microarray dataset, the Bioconductor package limma27 version 3.62.2 was used to analyze this dataset to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). It is well-suited for dealing with microarray data and small sample sizes. Here, RMA (Robust Multi-array Average) normalization was applied to normalize data distribution across all the samples. After data preprocessing, a design matrix was created to define the samples and the control group. A linear model was fitted to each gene with the lmFit() function, and a contrast matrix was set up using the makeContrasts() function to specify the comparisons between the samples and control group. Differentially expressed genes were collected using the top table() function, and False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was used to adjust multiple tests. Similarly, for GSE166780, DEGs were determined using the DESeq2 package from Bioconductor version 2.1328. The DESeq2 package was used for the GSE166780 because this was an RNAseq dataset, and DESeq2 provides robust normalization and differential expression analysis for RNA-Seq count data. Low-expression genes were filtered out, keeping only those with at least 10 counts in at least two samples, ensuring that only meaningful genes with sufficient expression levels were retained. Variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) was applied as the normalization method to stabilize variance and make expression values more comparable across samples. For both datasets, DEGs were filtered using a cut-off criterion of absolute log fold changes (|log2FC|) > 0.5 and p-value < 0.05.

Genes associated with DNA Damage Repair (DDR) were retrieved from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database29,30. It is a reliable and comprehensive resource known for its curated, updated, and integrated gene pathway information. This DNA Damage Repair (DDR) related gene list was used to identify differentially expressed DDR genes in both datasets. Filtering of the genes was conducted using the dplyr package in R version 1.1.4, and the common differentially expressed DDR genes in both datasets were determined using the intersect function in R. For the visualization, the “ggplot2” package in R version 3.5.1 was used31.

Weighted gene co-expression network construction

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)32 is a systems biology technique used to reveal association patterns among genes in samples. In this study, this network analysis was performed on both datasets to identify groups of co-expressed gene modules associated with the disease status. Quality control was performed using the goodSamplesGenes() function, which ensured no missing values or low-quality samples were in the data. The hierarchical clustering function hclust() was applied to detect outlier samples, which were visualized using a dendrogram. Low-expression genes were removed by keeping only genes expressed in at least three samples. A soft-thresholding power of β = 18 was selected for network construction. Although the scale-free topology fit index (R²) exceeded the conventional 0.8 threshold at β = 15, the R² value continued to increase progressively through β = 17–18. Lower powers (β = 15–16) were not selected because the curve had not yet approached the plateau region, indicating that scale-free topology had not stabilized. At β = 18 (R² ≈ 0.87–0.88), the increase in R² began to level off, suggesting near-stable scale-free behavior. At this power, mean connectivity remained adequate (approximately 180–220), indicating that the network retained sufficient density. In contrast, higher powers yielded minimal additional improvement in R² while substantially reducing mean connectivity, increasing the risk of excessive network sparsification. Therefore, β = 18 was selected as the lowest power within the plateau region that balances stable scale-free topology with preservation of meaningful co-expression structure. The topological overlap matrix was computed using only positive correlations. Modules were defined with a minimum size of 30 genes, and those with highly similar expression profiles were merged using a height cutoff of 0.25. The maximum module size was limited to 5,000 genes, and modules were labeled with distinct colors rather than numeric identifiers.

Intramodular Hub Genes Identification

To identify modules significantly associated with MI, the module’s eigengenes values were correlated by Pearson’s correlation with the disease status. Significant modules were selected based on a p-value less than 0.05 and a correlation coefficient of more than 0.5. Additionally, hub genes within each module were identified by calculating each gene’s module membership (MM) which indicates the relationship between individual gene expression and the associated module eigengene. Genes with a high absolute module membership (|MM| > 0.8) were classified as hub genes, indicating a strong relationship with their corresponding modules. Finally, the previously identified DDR genes were filtered from these hub genes.

Identification of the prognostic DDR Genes related to AMI and their interaction with other proteins, miRNA, and transcription factors

Only those genes that were both differentially expressed and classified as hub genes were screened by applying R’s intersection function, thus narrowing down the list to genes that demonstrate both statistical significance and key roles within the network. This integrative approach is particularly promising because differential expression analysis identifies genes with large expression shifts in AMI, while WGCNA highlights genes that have been strongly co-expressed and functionally relevant. NetworkAnalyst 3.033 was used to construct and analyze the protein-protein interaction (PPI), gene-miRNA, and gene-transcription factor (TF) networks for the identified prognostic genes. The PPI network was built using literature-curated data from the IMEx Interactome database34 to identify key protein interactions. The gene-miRNA interaction network was generated using miRTarBase v9.035, which provides experimentally validated interactions to explore post-transcriptional regulation. The gene-TF regulatory network was constructed using TRRUST36, a curated database of human transcriptional regulatory networks, to understand transcriptional control mechanisms. To refine the networks, degree centrality analysis was performed to identify hub genes with the highest connectivity, which are more likely to be functionally significant. Additionally, network topology parameters, including betweenness, centrality, and clustering coefficient, were analyzed to assess the importance of individual genes within the regulatory framework.

Gene expression analysis in the Bangladeshi population

Study population and processing of blood samples

The study involved 70 patients diagnosed with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) who were admitted to the Cardiology Department of Dr. Sirajul Islam Medical College Hospital in Dhaka, Bangladesh. The patients were selected based on diagnostic criteria such as elevated troponin levels, angiogram findings, and ECG reports. In addition, 60 healthy control individuals were included in the study who had no previous history of cardiovascular disease or other chronic illnesses. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethical Approval Committee of the Faculty of Biological Sciences, University of Dhaka. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and were in line with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent. They filled out a questionnaire to gather their demographic information and health measurements.

Blood samples were collected from the patients immediately within 4 h of their admission into the hospital under strict aseptic conditions. During the transportation time, 4–8℃ temperature was maintained.

Validating the expression of DDR genes via qRT-PCR

RNA was extracted from the collected blood samples within 1 h of the blood being drawn from the patients to maximize the RNA output. RNA extraction was performed using the RNAsimple Total RNA Kit (TIANGEN BIOTECH (BEIJING) CO., LTD, catalog: 4992858) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA were evaluated using the NanoDrop OneC Micro volume UV–Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) and the extracted RNA was stored at −80℃. Afterward, cDNA was constructed from the extracted RNA using Takara PrimeScript™ 1 st strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (catalogue: 6110 A). The cDNA was diluted in nuclease-free water to minimize inhibition caused by excessive template amounts, which can affect qPCR performance for most genes. qRT-PCR was used to measure the expression of the DDR genes, along with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as the reference gene. The reactions were carried out on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using the Takara TB Green Premix Ex Taq (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Cat. No. RR420L) as the master mix. Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using the R programming language. The frequency and percentage of categorical variables were calculated. Numeric variables were represented by Mean (± SD) values. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check whether the data set of this study has a normal distribution. A linear regression model was employed to adjust the Ct values of the OGG1, NBN, and GAPDH genes for potential confounders, including age, hypertension, body mass index (BMI), diabetes, smoking status, and family history of cardiovascular disease (CVD). The Mann-Whitney U test was performed to assess whether there were significant differences in gene expression levels between groups. This non-parametric test was selected for its appropriateness in comparing independent groups without the presumption of a normal distribution.

Developing the machine learning model

Data preprocessing

Data on clinical parameters and gene expression were merged into a singular matrix. A Group variable was created to denote AMI patients (1) and controls (0). Gene expression data were represented as fold change values, calculated relative to the reference gene GAPDH, to reflect the magnitude of expression differences between target and reference genes. Missing values in essential gene expression columns were removed in order to maintain data integrity. Categorical variables (Hypertension, Tobacco, Diabetes, Family History of CVD, and Group) were transformed into factors to improve the machine learning model training.

Feature engineering, selection, and data splitting

Feature selection was performed with Elastic Net regularization, a variant of LASSO that combines L1 and L2 penalties to reduce overfitting in the presence of highly correlated features. The dataset was divided into 80% training and 20% testing subsets utilizing the caret package (version 7.1). The dependent variable was the group (0 = Control, 1 = AMI), while the independent variables consisted of the chosen attributes.

Training machine learning models

Multiple machine learning models were implemented for AMI classification, including Logistic Regression (LR)37, Random Forest (RF)38, Support Vector Machine (SVM)39, Naïve Bayes (NB)40 and K-nearest neighbor (KNN)41. The logistic Regression (LR) model was trained utilizing the glm() function with the binomial family, providing probability outputs for classification. A Random Forest (RF) model was developed with the randomForest() function, incorporating 500 decision trees to enhance generalization. The Support Vector Machine (SVM) model was trained utilizing the svm() function with a linear kernel, hence establishing strong classification boundaries. The probability=TRUE option was activated, permitting the model to produce probability estimates for each sample. The Naïve Bayes model was trained utilizing the naive_bayes() function with Laplace smoothing to avert zero-probability complications. The model computed conditional probabilities derived from feature distributions and classified test samples accordingly. Following training, predictions were produced for the test dataset, and probability scores were obtained from each model. To ensure robust evaluation and avoid overfitting, 10-fold cross-validation was performed during model training using the trainControl() function from the caret package, enabling internal validation and hyperparameter tuning where applicable. Additionally, the class distribution between AMI and control samples was examined to check for imbalance. The relatively balanced dataset (or specify the imbalance ratio if applicable) ensured that no resampling or weighting techniques were necessary (or specify the method used if any, e.g., SMOTE or class weights). An ensemble method was implemented to improve predictive accuracy by averaging the probability scores from logistic regression, random forest, support vector machine, and naive Bayes, capitalizing on the strengths of each model42. Model performance was assessed by calculating accuracy, sensitivity (recall), precision, specificity, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). ROC curves were plotted for all models utilizing the pROC package version 1.18.5.
