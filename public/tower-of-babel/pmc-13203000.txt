Gut microbial metabolic disorder in depression: insights from computational modeling and mediation analysis
Authors: Yuchen Zhang, Wenkai Lai, Meiling Wang, Shirong Lai, Qing Liu, Qi Luo, Zheng Chen, Da Zhao, Ziwei Wang, Fenglong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Depression is increasingly recognized as a disorder not only of the brain but also of systemic metabolic dysfunction, particularly involving the gut microbiota. Integrating multi-cohort gut microbiome data with constraint-based metabolic modeling, this study investigates how microbial metabolic fluxes mediate depressive symptoms. Significant alterations in microbial pathways, notably those related to amino acid metabolism and neurotransmitter precursors, were identified. Causal mediation analysis showed that gut microbial composition influenced depressive symptoms, significantly mediated by specific metabolites including butyrate, Cu2+, and tryptophan-derived compounds. This study, employing systems biology and mediation analysis, suggests that microbial metabolic activity mediates the gut-brain axis’s role in depression development and severity. Introduction

Depression is one of the most prevalent mental disorders, characterized primarily by a prolonged low mood, high prevalence, recurrence rates, and suicidal tendencies [1]. Despite its profound social and individual impacts, the pathogenesis of depression remains poorly understood and presents substantial challenges for diagnosis and treatment. The gut microbiota, which communicates with the central nervous system through multiple pathways, is increasingly recognized as a key factor in the development and progression of depression [2–4].

Research over the past decade has highlighted the critical role of the brain-gut-microbiota axis, with causal studies suggesting that gut dysbiosis contributes to depression and increases its risk [5, 6]. Studies increasingly link gut dysbiosis to depression, often observing an increase in pro-inflammatory (Eggerthella [7], Tannerellaceae [8]) and a decrease in anti-inflammatory bacteria (Sutterella [7], Akkermansiaceae [8]). In depression, the phyla Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes are commonly affected, often showing an increased Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio [9, 10]. Several species within the Bacteroides genus (e.g., B. thetaiotaomicron, B. fragilis, and B. uniformis) have been associated with increased depression risk [11, 12]. Specific components of the gut microbiota, such as several species within the Bacteroides genus (e.g., B. thetaiotaomicron, B. fragilis, and B. uniformis), have been consistently associated with altered profiles (typically lower abundance) in individuals with depression.

Changes in the composition of gut microbiota can lead to alterations in the microbial metabolome [13]. Transplantation of fecal microbiota from individuals with depression into germ-free mice can induce depressive-like behaviors in the recipients [9]. These mice predominantly exhibit disruptions in microbial genes and host metabolites, particularly those involved in carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism [9]. Van et al. have shown that retinol (vitamin A), 4-hydroxycoumarin, 2-aminooctanoate, 10-undecenoate, 1-palmitoyl-2-palmitoleoyl-GPC, and 1-linoleoyl-GPA are associated with depression [13], and hippurate is considered a biomarker for depression [14]. Skonieczna et al. found that short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), common microbial metabolites in the gut, are closely associated with depression, exhibiting significantly lower concentrations in the feces of depressed patients compared to healthy individuals [15].

Directly linking specific microbial features to depression is difficult due to microbiome data sparsity and inter-individual variability [16–19]. As metabolomic data more accurately reflect the gut microbiota-depression relationship, this study uses species abundance data and applies constraint-based reconstruction and analysis (COBRA) methods [20, 21] to predict gut microbial metabolic fluxes in both depression patients and healthy controls, analyzing microbiota and metabolic dysbiosis in depression. By examining species-specific contributions to dysregulated metabolic flux and conducting mediation analysis, we aim to elucidate the gut microbiota-depression connection (Fig. 1).

Species abundance processing

Microbial relative abundance data are inherently compositional, meaning that the proportions of all taxa within a sample sum to one. This closed-sum constraint introduces interdependencies among variables, violating the assumptions of many standard statistical techniques that require variable independence. To address this issue, we applied the centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation [26], which mitigates the compositional effect by projecting the data into an unconstrained Euclidean space.

The CLR transformation improves the interpretability of microbial abundance data(population size of a genus) and enables the application of conventional statistical and multivariate methods. It enhances the detection of biologically meaningful patterns and relationships by removing spurious correlations arising from the compositional structure.

To ensure the mathematical validity of the natural logarithm in the CLR formula, genera with zero relative abundance in a given sample were excluded prior to transformation. This preprocessing step ensures that the geometric mean and the individual ratios are calculated based on the taxa detected within each specific community profile.

The CLR transformation computes the natural logarithm of each species’ abundance relative to the geometric mean of all species in the sample, effectively normalizing the data for downstream statistical analysis.

Diversity and differential analysis

To assess differences in gut microbial diversity between individuals with depression and healthy controls, we computed multiple diversity indices, including Pielou’s evenness, Shannon diversity, and Simpson’s diversity. These indices capture various aspects of within-sample microbial diversity, such as richness and evenness. Statistical comparisons between the two groups were performed using the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test, which is robust to non-normal data distributions.

To further explore inter-sample compositional differences ( diversity), we conducted principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on species-level dissimilarities. This dimensionality reduction technique enabled the visualization of microbial community structure across samples. Group-level differences in diversity were quantitatively assessed using analysis of similarities (ANOSIM), a permutation-based method that evaluates whether the microbial composition differs significantly between predefined groups.

In addition, Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was applied to identify specific microbial taxa that were significantly enriched in either the depression or control group. LEfSe combines statistical significance with biological consistency and effect size estimation, offering a robust framework for discovering taxa that may serve as potential biomarkers or mechanistic contributors.

Community-scale metabolic modeling

Constructing a generic species model at the genus level

The AGORA2 database [27] comprises genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) for 7,302 microbial strains, detailing all known metabolic reactions specific to each strain, including reaction stoichiometry and directionality [28, 29]. These models are curated based on genomic sequences, functional annotations, and strain-specific biochemical and physiological data obtained from the literature [29].

To construct genus-level metabolic models, we aggregated the reactions of all strains belonging to a given genus into a unified model. Redundant reactions were removed during consolidation, and the genus-level biomass objective function was defined as the average biomass reaction across all strains. The biomass reaction represents cellular growth and includes the synthesis of essential macromolecules such as nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids.

These metabolic models can be visualized as complex biochemical networks, where nodes represent metabolites and edges correspond to enzymatic reactions that interconvert them. Such network representations allow for a systems-level understanding of microbial metabolic capabilities (Fig. 3).

To simulate the metabolic activity of the gut microbial communities, we employed Flux Balance Analysis (FBA) using the individualized models. The community biomass was defined as the objective function for optimization, representing the steady-state growth of the entire microbial population. Furthermore, to reflect a typical nutritional environment, simulations were conducted under constraints representing a Western diet as the boundary condition for exchange fluxes. These dietary constraints were derived from standardized Virtual Metabolic Human (VMH) database profiles, ensuring the physiological relevance of the predicted metabolic fluxes.

Constructing individualized gut microbiota metabolic models

To construct individualized gut microbiota metabolic models, the genus-level abundance profiles of the gut microbiota are first mapped to genus-level metabolic models, thereby obtaining the initial metabolic model for each genus present in a given sample. Subsequently, mgPipe [30] is used to integrate the metabolic models of all genera within each sample into a community-level metabolic model.

It estimates the steady-state distribution of metabolic fluxes by formulating the system as a linear programming problem, subject to mass balance and physicochemical constraints (Fig. 3). Statistical analysis

Differential and enrichment analysis

To evaluate differences between paired groups, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied [31]. For functional enrichment analysis, we employed the hypergeometric test [32] to assess the overrepresentation of specific metabolic features. To investigate the potential mediating role of microbial metabolites in the relationship between gut microbiota and depression, we conducted mediation analysis using the mediation package in R. All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.0).

Causal mediation analysis

To systematically explore the potential causal relationships between the gut microbiota, their metabolic activities, and depression, we performed causal mediation analysis (CMA). Specifically, we tested all possible combinations between significantly differential microbial genera (as independent variables, X) and significantly differential predicted metabolic fluxes (as mediators, M), with depression status as the dependent variable (Y).To ensure the directionality of the observed effects, we also conducted inverse mediation analysis, where the roles of X and Y were swapped. A relationship was considered a potential causal link only if the forward ACME (Average Causal Mediation Effect) was significant (P < 0.05) and the inverse mediation showed no significant effect .

Results

Balanced depressive-healthy cohort after sample matching

To mitigate the influence of potential confounding variables, sample matching was conducted, yielding 271 depression samples paired with matched healthy controls. In the original dataset, significant disparities were observed in age and BMI distributions between the depression and healthy groups. However, these differences were markedly reduced following the matching process (Fig. 2). Genus-level taxonomic mapping against the AGORA2 database demonstrated complete coverage for all genus-level species present in the depression samples. Additionally, over 60% of genus-level species from the healthy control group were successfully mapped (Figure S2). Regarding the number of species, an average of 23.47% of the genera in the healthy samples could be found in the AGORA2 database (out of a total of 290 genera). In the depression samples, an average of 42.23% of the genera could be found in the AGORA2 database (out of a total of 162 genera). This high degree of coverage indicates that the reconstructed community-scale metabolic model captures a broad and representative range of microbial taxa. As a result, it provides a robust foundation for downstream metabolic network analyses and offers a comprehensive view of the functional metabolic landscape associated with depression.

Microbial ecology disruption in depression

The microbiome diversity and composition between the depressive and healthy cohorts were compared (Fig. 4). The diversity indices, including observed ASVs, Shannon, Chao1, Simpson, and Pielou, were calculated (Fig. 4A). The depressive group exhibited significantly lower observed ASVs and Shannon indices (p < 2.22e-16), while the Chao1 index was marginally lower (p = 0.045). No significant differences were observed for the Simpson (p = 0.25) or Pielou (p = 0.39) indices. diversity analysis, based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, revealed a significant difference between the two groups (ANOSIM R = 0.008, p = 0.004; Fig. 4B). The principal coordinates analysis showed distinct clustering, with PCO1 and PCO2 explaining 26.78% and 15.11% of the variance, respectively.

The phylogenetic distribution of differentially abundant taxa was further analyzed (Fig. 4C). The depressive group was characterized by an increased abundance of genera such as Auritidibacter, Kocuria, and Fusobacterium, whereas the healthy group was enriched in Bosea and Lautropia. These findings suggest that the gut microbiome composition is significantly altered in the depressive cohort, with specific taxa potentially associated with disease status.

Metabolic reaction dysregulations in depression

By integrating species-level, reaction-level, and pathway-level abundance into a binary presence–absence (0–1) matrix (Fig. 5), we observed pronounced species-level sparsity across both depression and healthy cohorts. This sparsity suggests that microbial species may exhibit substantial individual variability, thereby limiting their generalizability as universal biomarkers for depression. In contrast, the distributions of metabolic reactions and subsystems demonstrated higher consistency across samples, implying that functional features may serve as more robust and broadly applicable indicators of depressive phenotypes, meriting further exploration.

The 0–1 matrices for Genus-level, Metabolic Reactions, and Subsystems (where 1 denotes presence and 0 denotes absence) show that the Genus-level matrix exhibits high sparsity in both the depressive and healthy samples, whereas the matrices for Metabolic Reactions and Subsystems do not present a sparsity problem

Changes in microbial community structure lead to alterations in microbial community function. diversity analysis based on reaction-level abundance revealed significant differences between groups in the Observed (P = 0.015) and Chao1 (P = 0.015) indices, as assessed by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Fig. 6A). However, diversity analysis did not detect significant dissimilarities in reaction composition between depression and control groups (ANOSIM R = − 0.0003, P = 0.493; Fig. 6B), indicating comparable overall functional diversity between the cohorts.

To pinpoint specific metabolic reactions associated with depression, we applied the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and identified 89 reactions with significant differential abundance (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 6C). The top 20 most significantly altered reactions (ranked by FDR) are visualized in Fig. 6D. Functional enrichment analysis of these reactions revealed significant overrepresentation in four metabolic subsystems: Fatty acid synthesis, Pentose and glucuronate interconversions, Thiamine metabolism, and Primary amine metabolism (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 6E), highlighting key pathways potentially involved in the gut microbiota’s role in depression.

Metabolic flux and species contribution differences

To investigate functional metabolic alterations, genus-level metabolic models were subjected to flux balance analysis (FBA), optimizing for the community biomass objective function. This approach allowed us to infer steady-state metabolic fluxes across microbial communities. A total of 21 metabolites exhibited significantly different fluxes between depression patients and healthy controls (FDR < 0.05, Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Fig. 7A). Of these, 13 metabolites were successfully annotated in the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), encompassing diverse chemical classes: 1 in Homogeneous transition metal compound, 2 in Non-metal oxoanionic compounds, 1 in Carboxylic acid and derivative, 1 in Fatty acyl, 1 in Imidazopyrimidine, 3 in Organonitrogen compounds, 1 in Organooxygen compound, 2 in Purine nucleotides, and 1 in Steroid or steroid derivative.

Interestingly, further decomposition of species-level contributions to these metabolites revealed that 6 microbial genus, despite showing no significant differences in relative abundance between groups, contributed significantly different flux levels (Fig. 7B). This suggests functional shifts in microbial metabolism independent of taxonomic abundance.

Notable among these metabolites were L-Dopa and Cu. Six genera demonstrated differential contributions to L-Dopa metabolism between the two cohorts, with four of these genera (highlighted in red in Fig. 7B) showing no significant change in abundance. These findings underscore the value of metabolic flux profiling in uncovering latent functional shifts not apparent at the taxonomic level.

For Cu metabolism, species such as Dorea and Coprococcus displayed significantly increased consumption rates in depression samples compared to healthy controls (Fig. 7C). At the phylum level, these shifts translated into discernible differences in Cu metabolic contributions, as illustrated in Fig. 7D. This functional divergence suggests potential microbial modulation of metal ion homeostasis in depressive states.

Microbiota-depression causal inference via mediation analysis

To elucidate the potential mechanistic pathways linking gut microbiota to depression, we tested all combinations of differentially abundant microbial genera and differentially abundant metabolites and calculated the Average Causal Mediated Effect (ACME) and the Average Direct Effect (ADE) values for the mediation analysis. A total of 9 significant mediation (ACME P value < 0.05) relationships were identified (Fig. 8A; all ). These relationships encompassed four bacterial genera: Coprococcus, Escherichia, Papillibacter, Ruminococcus, and involved eight metabolites, notably including Cu and L-Dopa.

These pathways suggest key metabolic intermediates that may mediate the relationship between gut microbiota and mental health. B. Proposed mechanisms by which gut microbiota influence depression. Reduced Cu2 + consumption by gut microbes leads to elevated Cu2 + levels in the bloodstream, which may enhance oxidative stress and elevate the risk of depression (left side). These results reveal distinct mechanistic routes through which microbial metabolic activity may influence depressive pathology. One proposed pathway involves the reduced microbial consumption of Cu in individuals with depression, resulting in elevated systemic Cu levels. Excess circulating Cu is known to exacerbate oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, thereby contributing to the onset or exacerbation of depressive symptoms.

Another key pathway centers on the neurotransmitter precursor L-Dopa. Under normal physiological conditions, L-Dopa crosses the blood-brain barrier and is converted to dopamine in the central nervous system. However, in depression-associated microbiota, microbial conversion of L-Dopa to dopamine occurs prematurely in the gut lumen, effectively decreasing its systemic availability for neural uptake and thereby impairing dopaminergic signaling in the brain (Fig. 8B). This disruption may underlie the observed neurochemical imbalances in depressive disorders.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive investigation into the intricate interplay between gut microbiota, metabolic reactions, and depression. While traditional microbial profiling revealed only modest differences in -diversity and -diversity between depressed and healthy individuals, metabolic reaction and metabolite-level analyses uncovered more consistent and biologically informative patterns. These findings suggest that gut microbiome-associated metabolic activity may represent a more robust and generalizable layer for biomarker discovery in depression.

Notably, our study identified novel microbial genera such as Auritidibacter and Kocuria associated with depression for the first time (Table S1). While Auritidibacter has previously been linked to otic infections [33, 34], and Kocuria remains underexplored, their emergence as potential microbiome markers of depression warrants further mechanistic and clinical investigation. In contrast, microbial taxa previously reported as enriched in depression, including Micrococcaceae and Burkholderiaceae [35, 36], were found to be more abundant in healthy individuals in our dataset, suggesting context-dependent microbial effects and underscoring the importance of population-specific validation.

Compared to species-level variability, metabolic features such as reaction abundance and metabolite fluxes demonstrated greater stability across individuals. We identified 89 significantly altered reactions in depression, enriched in four key metabolic subsystems—fatty acid synthesis, pentose and glucuronate interconversions, thiamine metabolism, and primary amine metabolism—highlighting critical metabolic signatures that may underpin depressive pathophysiology.

Among these, copper (Cu) metabolism emerged as a particularly compelling pathway. Copper is essential for the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase (SOD), which mitigates oxidative damage by converting superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide. In depression, increased systemic Cu levels and reduced Cu/Zn-SOD activity are associated with heightened oxidative stress and neuroinflammation [37–40]. Our results suggest that decreased microbial Cu consumption in depressed individuals may contribute to systemic Cu accumulation, thus amplifying oxidative stress and disrupting neural homeostasis (Fig. 8B). The brain’s heightened susceptibility to oxidative damage due to high oxygen consumption and limited antioxidant defenses further supports this mechanistic link [41]. Moreover, excess Cu has been implicated in synaptic dysfunction, memory impairment, and reduced expression of hippocampal proteins such as GluN2B and PSD95 [42], further connecting copper dysregulation to depressive symptoms.

Additionally, L-Dopa metabolism was found to be significantly influenced by gut microbiota. Unlike dopamine, L-Dopa can cross the blood-brain barrier and is a critical precursor for central dopamine synthesis. Our study identified six genera, particularly Enterococcus, as major contributors to L-Dopa metabolism, with significant differences in metabolic flux between depressed and healthy individuals. Enterococcus expresses tyrosine decarboxylase, which converts L-Dopa into dopamine in the gut lumen, thus limiting its availability for brain uptake [43–46]. This microbial interference in neurotransmitter precursor metabolism provides a plausible mechanism by which the microbiota can modulate central nervous system function.

Importantly, several taxa that showed no significant differences in relative abundance still exerted strong influences on host metabolic profiles, particularly with respect to Cu and L-Dopa metabolism. This highlights the importance of functional contributions over taxonomic presence in microbiome research and suggests that low-abundance taxa may exert disproportionately large metabolic effects. Research has shown that maintaining a consistent diet while transplanting the gut microbiota from healthy rats into depressive rats can alleviate depressive-like behaviors in the recipient rats [47]. However, there is currently no direct evidence demonstrating a causal role for the observed abnormality in the copper metabolism pathway in inducing depressive-like behavior, independent of the host’s diet.

A major limitation of this study is the reliance on the existing AGORA2 repository for community model construction. Although our mapped genera represented the majority of the total abundance, a significant number of species remained unmapped. The exclusion of these ‘missing’ taxa, which may possess unique metabolic niches or synergetic roles, could lead to an underestimation of certain metabolic fluxes. This highlights the inherent gap in current genome-scale metabolic model (GEM) databases. Future research should prioritize the reconstruction of specific GEMs for these uncharacterized or niche-specific species to enhance the granularity and representativeness of individualized community models.

Our constraint-based metabolic modeling revealed a notable finding: the depression group exhibited diminished microbial capacity for consumption. This functional result, which also highlights the critical distinction between Functional Drivers and Taxonomic Indicators, emerged despite certain methodological limitations. Specifically, in our Flux Balance Analysis (FBA) framework, the predicted microbial consumption fluxes—including that of —are fundamentally dependent on the defined medium composition (i.e., the dietary input). Although we applied a standardized ’Western diet’ as the boundary condition for all simulations (a common practice in population-level FBA), this approach assumes a uniform nutrient environment and does not account for the high inter-individual variability of intake or the overall nutritional profile in a real-world setting. Therefore, the quantitative magnitude of the reduced consumption predicted by the model may be highly sensitive to the specific dietary inputs used.Our analysis shows that Functional Contribution—such as the strong metabolic influence (e.g., on and metabolism) exerted by taxa with non-significant abundance changes—underscores that low-abundance but functionally critical keystone species or coordinated metabolic pathways drive the mechanistic link. Conversely, Taxonomic Indicators like the genera Auritidibacter and Kocuria were identified due to their statistically significant shifts in relative abundance (Fig. 4C/D). While these indicators may not be the sole drivers of the observed metabolic changes, their robust and detectable change makes them valuable as practical, easy-to-measure biomarkers for distinguishing the depressive cohort from healthy controls.

The gut bacterial metabolite, trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), is a small organic molecule that recent studies have linked to neurodegenerative diseases [48] and postoperative cognitive dysfunction [49]. The research by Sophie Meinitzer et al. indicated that, in individuals without carbohydrate malabsorption, serum TMAO concentration is positively correlated with the severity of depressive symptoms [50]. Furthermore, their results suggested a sex- and gastrointestinal function-specific association between TMAO and Zonulin with depressive symptoms [50]. Sowjanya Mudimela et al. proposed that the role of TMAO in the Central Nervous System (CNS) is complex. While it may serve a fundamental physiological role as an osmolyte involved in protein folding, altered levels of TMAO may also become a potential risk factor or biomarker for various neurological and neuropsychiatric disorders (including Alzheimer’s disease and depression) by regulating oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and blood-brain barrier integrity [51]. However, the exact mechanisms and causal relationship of TMAO in these diseases require further prospective and longitudinal studies for full elucidation.

We recognize that dietary habits are a critical and often confounding factor in microbiome-MDD research, given the likely bidirectional relationship where depression can alter dietary intake (e.g., changes in appetite, increased consumption of processed foods), and diet profoundly shapes the gut microbiota composition [52]. Because this study lacked rigorous dietary control—a common limitation in cross-sectional clinical designs—the observed microbial changes should be interpreted cautiously, as they could potentially be secondary to depression-related changes in dietary behavior rather than a direct consequence of the disease pathology itself.

Furthermore, a methodological limitation of the current study is the removal of zero-abundance genera before CLR transformation. While this avoids the potential bias introduced by arbitrary pseudocounts, it constitutes a distortion of the original abundance profiles and carries the risk of excluding low-abundance taxa that might be biologically relevant in specific individuals. Future research utilizing more sophisticated zero-handling techniques, such as Bayesian-multiplicative replacement or specialized compositional data imputations, will be necessary to further validate the robustness of the identified microbial signatures.

In conclusion, this study highlights the limitations of genus-level microbial analysis in isolation and emphasizes the value of integrating metabolic reaction networks and flux analysis to understand the microbiota-depression axis. Metabolic reactions and microbial-modulated metabolites, such as L-Dopa and Cu, represent promising targets for future diagnostics and therapeutics. Further longitudinal and interventional studies focusing on these pathways may pave the way for microbiome-informed strategies in managing depression.

Conclusion

In this study, constraint-based metabolic modeling was employed to investigate the functional interplay between the gut microbiome and depression. While microbial diversity and composition differed significantly between individuals with depression and healthy controls, the most striking findings emerged at the functional level. Genera such as Auritidibacter and Kocuria were newly associated with depression, suggesting novel microbial signatures of the disorder. Flux balance analysis revealed significant alterations in key metabolic pathways, including fatty acid biosynthesis, pentose and glucuronate interconversions, thiamine metabolism, and primary amine metabolism. Notably, the depression group demonstrated reduced microbial capacity for L-Dopa metabolism, potentially limiting dopamine biosynthesis in the brain. Additionally, diminished Cu consumption by gut bacteria in depression may contribute to systemic copper accumulation and heightened oxidative stress. These findings elucidate distinct microbiome-mediated metabolic disruptions in depression and offer promising avenues for the development of microbiota-targeted therapeutic interventions.
