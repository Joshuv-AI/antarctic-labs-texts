AdaFRUGAL: Adaptive Memory-Efficient Training with Dynamic Control
Quang-Hung Bui, Anh Son Ta

Abstract




1
Introduction

The proliferation of Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4
[
1
]
, LLaMA
[
2
]
, and BLOOM
[
17
]
has revolutionized Natural Language Processing (NLP). However, their immense scale brings a significant challenge: the exorbitant memory requirements for training. A substantial portion of this memory is not consumed by the model parameters themselves, but by optimizer states, such as the momentum and variance vectors required by popular optimizers like Adam
[
11
]
and AdamW
[
3
]
. For large models, these states can demand upwards of 64GB of GPU memory, creating a significant barrier to entry
[
4
,
12
]
.

To address this bottleneck, the FRUGAL framework
[
5
]
recently emerged as a highly effective solution. It introduces a novel "gradient splitting" mechanism that partitions parameters into a small, critical
state-full
subspace (updated with AdamW) and a large
state-free
subspace (updated with a memoryless optimizer like SignSGD
[
13
]
). This hybrid approach enables full-parameter training with performance competitive to AdamW at a fraction of the memory cost.

Despite its effectiveness, the original FRUGAL framework relies on two static hyperparameters: the state-full subspace ratio (
\rho
) and its update frequency (
T
). A fixed value for these may be suboptimal across the different phases of training. In this paper, we propose AdaFRUGAL, an adaptive extension that introduces dynamic control mechanisms to automate and optimize resource management. Instead of static values, AdaFRUGAL intelligently adjusts
\rho
and
T
based on training progress. Our contributions are threefold:

•

We introduce a dynamic decay policy for
\rho
, allowing the model to start with a larger state-full subspace for robust initial learning and progressively reduce it to conserve memory.

•

We propose a loss-aware adaptive schedule for
T
, reducing the frequency of expensive subspace updates as the model’s convergence slows, thereby saving training time.

2
Related Work and Background

2.1
Memory-Efficient Optimization Strategies

Research on reducing optimizer memory overhead has yielded several distinct strategies. One prominent family is Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT), which includes methods like LoRA
[
6
]
, Adapter Tuning
[
14
]
, and Prefix-Tuning
[
15
]
. These techniques freeze most model parameters and only train a small number of auxiliary parameters, which is highly efficient but not suitable for full-parameter training.

Another line of work enables full-parameter training while minimizing memory. GaLore
[
4
]
projects gradients into a low-rank subspace to store smaller optimizer states. Methods based on Block Coordinate Descent (BCD)
[
16
]
, such as BAdam
[
7
]
, update only a subset of parameters at a time. System-level optimizations like ZeRO
[
20
]
partition optimizer states across multiple GPUs. Orthogonal to these are techniques that directly modify the optimizer, such as 8-bit optimizers
[
19
]
and AdaFactor
[
18
]
, which can often be combined with other frameworks.

2.2
The FRUGAL Framework

The FRUGAL framework
[
5
]
, the foundation of our work, enables full-rank, full-parameter updates with minimal memory by employing
gradient splitting
. At each step, a parameter’s gradient is split into two components. A
state-full
component, projected onto a low-dimensional subspace, is updated via a stateful optimizer like AdamW. The remaining
state-free
component is updated via a memoryless optimizer like SignSGD
[
13
]
. This subspace is periodically redefined based on two static hyperparameters: its size, determined by the ratio
\rho
, and its update frequency,
T
. FRUGAL’s mechanism of resetting optimizer states upon subspace change avoids the staleness issue seen in prior work, ensuring training stability.

3
The AdaFRUGAL Framework

While the original FRUGAL framework provides a robust baseline for memory-efficient training, its reliance on static hyperparameters
\rho
and
T
limits its adaptability. The optimal values for these parameters can vary significantly not only between different tasks and models but also across different phases of a single training run. To address this limitation, we introduce AdaFRUGAL, which incorporates two dynamic control mechanisms to automate resource management and enhance training efficiency.

3.1
Dynamic State-Full Ratio Control (Dynamic
\rho
)

Motivation:
In the early stages of training, a model undergoes rapid and significant weight updates as it learns fundamental features from the data. During this period, a larger state-full subspace (higher
\rho
) is beneficial, as it allows the powerful AdamW optimizer to stabilize and accelerate convergence. However, as training progresses and the model converges, updates become smaller and more focused on fine-tuning. In this later phase, maintaining a large state-full subspace becomes memory-inefficient, as the simpler SignSGD optimizer is often sufficient to handle these smaller adjustments. A static
\rho
fails to capture this dynamic, leading to either insufficient adaptation early on or wasted memory later.

Mechanism:
To address this, we introduce a dynamic control policy for
\rho
based on a simple yet effective linear decay schedule. The state-full ratio at a given training step
k
, denoted as
\rho(k)
, is calculated as follows:

\rho(k)=\max\left(\rho_{\text{end}},\rho_{\text{start}}-(\rho_{\text{start}}-\rho_{\text{end}})\cdot\frac{k}{K_{\text{total\_steps}}}\right)

(1)

where
\rho_{\text{start}}
is the initial ratio,
\rho_{\text{end}}
is the final target ratio,
k
is the current training step, and
K_{\text{total\_steps}}
is the total number of training steps. This mechanism allows AdaFRUGAL to begin with a large, robust state-full subspace and gradually reduce its size, continuously optimizing the trade-off between performance and memory usage throughout the training process.

3.2
Adaptive Update Frequency Control (Dynamic
T
)

Motivation:
The frequency
T
at which the state-full subspace is redefined represents a trade-off between adaptability and computational cost. Frequent updates (small
T
) allow the framework to quickly adapt to changing gradient dynamics but incur significant computational overhead from re-projecting gradients and resetting optimizer states. Infrequent updates (large
T
) are more efficient but risk using a "stale" subspace that no longer captures the most important update directions. This trade-off is also phase-dependent: frequent updates are more critical early in training, while less frequent updates are sufficient once the model has stabilized.

Mechanism:
We propose a loss-aware adaptive schedule to dynamically adjust
T
. The core idea is to increase the update interval when the model’s convergence plateaus. Specifically, we monitor the validation loss periodically. Every
N_{\text{eval}}
steps, we calculate the relative change in validation loss,
\Delta L_{\text{rel}}
, over the last evaluation window as follows:

\Delta L_{\text{rel}}=\frac{|L_{\text{val}}(k-N_{\text{eval}})-L_{\text{val}}(k)|}{L_{\text{val}}(k-N_{\text{eval}})}

(2)

where
L_{\text{val}}(k)
is the validation loss at step
k
. If this relative change falls below a predefined stability threshold
\tau_{\text{low}}
, we deem the training to be stable and increase
T
:

T_{\text{new}}=\min(T_{\text{max}},T_{\text{current}}\cdot\gamma_{\text{increase}})

(3)

where
T_{\text{max}}
is a maximum allowed frequency and
\gamma_{\text{increase}}
is an increase factor. This mechanism allows AdaFRUGAL to reduce computational overhead automatically in later training stages without manual intervention.

3.3

By integrating both dynamic control mechanisms, we formulate the complete AdaFRUGAL algorithm, as detailed in Algorithm
1
. The algorithm dynamically computes the state-full ratio
\rho_{k}
and adjusts the update frequency
T_{k}
at each step, using them to periodically redefine the state-full projection
P_{k}
. The core gradient splitting and hybrid update steps from the original FRUGAL are then executed within this adaptive context.

4
Experimental Setup

To comprehensively evaluate the performance and resource efficiency of AdaFRUGAL, we conduct experiments across three distinct scenarios: large-scale pre-training, domain-adaptive continued pre-training, and fine-tuning. This section details the datasets, models, baselines, and implementation specifics for each scenario.

4.1
Tasks and Datasets

1) Pre-training on English (C4):
To assess performance in a standard English pre-training context, we use the Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus (C4)
[
8
]
. We train a LLaMA-130M model for 200,000 steps, allowing for a direct comparison with established baselines.

2) Pre-training on Vietnamese (VietVault):
To validate the robustness of our methods across different languages, we perform a large-scale pre-training task on Vietnamese. We use
VietVault

[
21
]
, a high-quality 80GB corpus curated from Common Crawl dumps prior to 2023. The dataset has undergone extensive cleaning, deduplication, and filtering for toxic content. Training on this corpus for 200,000 steps allows us to assess AdaFRUGAL’s performance in a non-English, large-scale setting.

3) Fine-tuning on GLUE:
To evaluate effectiveness on downstream tasks, we fine-tune the RoBERTa-Base model
[
9
]
on the General Language Understanding Evaluation (GLUE) benchmark
[
10
]
.

4.2
Baselines and Models

We compare AdaFRUGAL against several key baselines:

•

AdamW Full-Rank:
The standard, memory-intensive AdamW optimizer, which serves as the upper bound for performance.

•

FRUGAL (static):
The original FRUGAL framework with static, manually-tuned hyperparameters (
\rho=0.25
,
T=200
). This serves as our primary baseline to measure the improvements from our dynamic controls.

•

AdaFRUGAL Variants:
We evaluate our two proposed mechanisms both individually (
AdaFRUGAL-Dynamic-
\rho
and
AdaFRUGAL-Dynamic-
T
) and in combination (
AdaFRUGAL-Combined
) to perform a thorough ablation study.

For all pre-training tasks, we use a LLaMA-130M architecture. For the GLUE fine-tuning task, we use the RoBERTa-Base model.

4.3
Implementation Details

All experiments are conducted using PyTorch on systems equipped with NVIDIA RTX A6000 48GB GPUs. This choice of hardware reflects a common, resource-constrained environment where memory-efficient techniques like AdaFRUGAL are particularly impactful. For all FRUGAL-based methods, the state-full optimizer is AdamW (
\beta_{1}=0.9,\beta_{2}=0.999
) and the state-free optimizer is SignSGD. Block-wise projection is used as the default projection type.

For
AdaFRUGAL-Dynamic-
\rho
, we set
\rho_{\text{start}}=0.25
and
\rho_{\text{end}}=0.05
for the C4 and VietVault pre-training tasks. For
AdaFRUGAL-Dynamic-
T
, we use
T_{\text{start}}=100
,
T_{\text{max}}=800
,
N_{\text{eval}}=10,000
,
\gamma_{\text{increase}}=1.5
, and a stability threshold
\tau_{\text{low}}=0.008
. For the GLUE fine-tuning experiments, hyperparameters are adjusted to fit the shorter training duration. The learning rate schedules and other hyperparameters are kept consistent with the original FRUGAL paper to ensure a fair comparison. All reported performance metrics are averaged over multiple runs with different random seeds to ensure statistical significance.

5
Results and Discussion

In this section, we present a comprehensive empirical evaluation of AdaFRUGAL. We compare its performance and resource efficiency against a full-rank AdamW baseline, the original static FRUGAL, and other prominent memory-efficient methods. Our analysis spans three key scenarios: large-scale pre-training, domain-adaptive pre-training, and downstream fine-tuning.

5.1
Large-Scale Pre-training on C4

We first validate our methods in a standard large-scale pre-training setting on the C4 dataset with a LLaMA-130M model. This experiment serves to establish the performance of AdaFRUGAL’s dynamic mechanisms against both the original framework and other contemporary memory-efficient baselines. Table
1
presents the validation perplexity at multiple checkpoints throughout the 200,000 training steps, alongside the optimizer memory overhead for each method. The data clearly shows that the FRUGAL family of methods establishes a strong performance baseline, significantly outperforming other techniques like GaLore and BAdam, particularly in later training stages.

Building on this strong foundation, our adaptive mechanisms in AdaFRUGAL further refine the performance and efficiency trade-offs, each catering to different optimization priorities:

•

Computational Efficiency (AdaFRUGAL-Dynamic-
T
):
This variant demonstrates the best of both worlds. It not only achieves the best final perplexity (18.58) among all memory-efficient methods but also significantly reduces training time (as will be shown in Section
5.4
. This suggests that adaptively scheduling subspace updates can lead to a more stable and efficient convergence path, making it the ideal choice when performance and training speed are paramount.

•

Memory Efficiency (AdaFRUGAL-Dynamic-
\rho
):
This variant exemplifies a deliberate trade-off in favor of memory conservation. While its final perplexity is slightly higher at 18.85, this modest performance cost is exchanged for a substantial and dynamic reduction in memory footprint from 0.52G to 0.37G over time. This feature is critical for long-running jobs on resource-constrained hardware, where freeing up GPU memory is more valuable than achieving the absolute lowest perplexity.

•

Balanced Solution (AdaFRUGAL-Combined):
The combined approach provides a holistic profile, delivering comprehensive resource savings in both memory and time. Its final perplexity of 18.83, while not the absolute best, remains highly competitive and represents an excellent balance for environments where both memory and training time are critical constraints.

Large-Scale Pre-training on VietVault

To demonstrate the cross-lingual robustness of our framework, we replicated the pre-training experiment on the large-scale Vietnamese corpus, VietVault. The results are presented in Table

. As anticipated, the final perplexity scores are consistently higher than those on C4, which can be attributed to the linguistic differences and the inherent challenges of tokenizing Vietnamese text. Importantly, the relative performance trends and the complex dynamics between the optimizers remain remarkably consistent, underscoring the general applicability of our adaptive mechanisms.

The FRUGAL family of methods again significantly outperforms other memory-efficient baselines like GaLore and BAdam. Within our proposed methods, we observe the nuanced behaviors expected from our dynamic controls. In the early stages (up to 20k steps),
AdaFRUGAL-Dynamic-
T

\rho

AdaFRUGAL-Combined
method effectively balances these two dynamics, showcasing how the stability gains from Dynamic-
T
can partially offset the performance trade-off from Dynamic-
\rho
in the later stages.

Fine-tuning on GLUE Benchmark

To evaluate the downstream performance of our methods, we conducted extensive fine-tuning experiments with RoBERTa-Base on the GLUE benchmark. We focus on the high-performance (QV, Rank 8) setting to provide a direct comparison against strong PEFT baselines like LoRA. The results in Table

Our proposed adaptive methods build upon this success. Most notably,
Ada FRUGAL-Dynamic-

Nonetheless, all AdaFRUGAL variants remain highly competitive and outperform other memory-efficient baselines, solidifying the robustness of our approach.

5.5
Analysis of Hyperparameter Robustness

AdaFRUGAL’s adaptive mechanisms replace the static hyperparameters
\rho
and
T
with a new set of dynamic controls, such as
\rho_{\text{start}}
and
\tau_{\text{low}}
. A crucial aspect of their practical utility is their robustness across diverse tasks, which in turn reduces the need for extensive, task-specific manual tuning.

Our primary evidence for this robustness is the consistent, high-quality performance achieved across a wide range of tasks. First, in large-scale pre-training, the same set of adaptive hyperparameters (detailed in Section
4.3
) was used for both the English (C4) and Vietnamese (VietVault) corpora. As shown in Table
1
and Table
2
, the performance of AdaFRUGAL’s variants remains strong on these two linguistically distinct datasets without any domain-specific retuning.

This robustness is further and more strongly corroborated by the fine-tuning results on the GLUE benchmark. As detailed in Table
3
, the adaptive variants demonstrate consistently competitive performance across eight distinct downstream tasks, spanning sentiment analysis, paraphrase detection, and natural language inference. Crucially, while parameters related to training length (e.g., total steps for the
\rho
decay) were naturally adjusted for the shorter fine-tuning duration, the core adaptive logic and key thresholds proved effective without requiring a per-task grid search. If the adaptive hyperparameters were sensitive, one would expect the need to find different optimal values for each of the eight GLUE tasks. Their consistent success across this diverse suite of tasks is a powerful testament to their generalizability.

This robustness stems from the intuitive design of the parameters. The combined evidence from large-scale pre-training and multi-task fine-tuning validates the design of our automated mechanisms and confirms their effectiveness in reducing the overall tuning burden, a core objective of this work.

5.6
Analysis of Scalability

The practical impact of AdaFRUGAL hinges on the scalability of its resource efficiency benefits to the billion-parameter regimes where memory and computational constraints are most acute. Building on our empirical results and the underlying scaling laws of optimizer memory, we can analytically extrapolate this impact.

Scaling Benefits of Dynamic
\rho
(Memory Savings):

The memory-saving advantages of Dynamic
\rho
are expected to be particularly pronounced at larger scales. The optimizer state overhead for Blockwise projection in Transformer layers scales approximately with
\mathcal{O}(L\cdot p\cdot h^{2})
, where
L
is the number of layers,
p
is the state-full ratio, and
h
is the hidden size
[
5
]
. The quadratic dependence on
h
is the dominant factor in this scaling.

Our experiments on the LLaMA-130M model (
h=768
) show that dynamically reducing
\rho
from 0.25 to 0.05 yields an absolute memory saving of approximately 0.15 GB. For a 7B-scale model (e.g.,
L=32,h=4096
), the scaling factor for this overhead is approximately
(32/24)\times(4096/768)^{2}\approx 37.8
. Consequently, the extrapolated absolute saving grows to roughly 5.7 GB (
0.15\,\text{GB}\times 37.8
). This magnitude of memory reduction transitions from a mere optimization to a critical enabler, potentially fitting a model onto a hardware configuration that would otherwise be out of memory.

Scaling Benefits of Dynamic
T
(Computational Savings):

Similarly, the computational efficiency gains from Dynamic
T
are projected to increase with model scale. This is because the cost of each state-full subspace update, which Dynamic
T
adaptively curtails, grows polynomially with the model’s dimensions. By adaptively increasing
T
as the model stabilizes, AdaFRUGAL intelligently avoids these increasingly costly operations when they are less critical for convergence.

The
\sim
7-15% reduction in training time observed on the 130M model, depending on the static baseline chosen for comparison, is thus a conservative baseline. As the absolute cost of each subspace update becomes more significant in billion-parameter models, the wall-clock time saved by omitting these updates is poised to be substantially greater.

In summary, AdaFRUGAL’s dynamic control mechanisms are designed to yield super-linear resource savings with scale. They represent a strategic shift from being efficiency optimizations at smaller scales to enabling technologies at the frontier of LLM training.

5.7
Theoretical Considerations and Justification

While AdaFRUGAL’s dynamic control mechanisms are primarily data-driven, their design is grounded in the convergence properties of the original FRUGAL framework
[
5
]
. We provide a justification for why these adaptive policies are expected to preserve convergence while optimizing resource usage.

Convergence with Dynamic
\rho
:

The convergence analysis for static FRUGAL assumes a fixed probability
p
for a parameter to belong to the state-full subspace. Our dynamic schedule introduces a time-varying probability,
p(k)
, which changes slowly and deterministically. In stochastic optimization theory, convergence guarantees often hold for algorithms with slowly varying hyperparameters. Since our linear decay ensures that the change
p(k+1)-p(k)
is infinitesimal at each step (on the order of
1/K_{\text{total\_steps}}
), we hypothesize that the optimization trajectory can adapt smoothly. The core variance-reduction mechanism of FRUGUAL remains active at every step, albeit on a gradually shrinking subspace, suggesting that the established convergence properties are largely maintained.

Convergence with Dynamic
T
:

The loss-aware schedule for
T
is a data-driven heuristic, yet its design is grounded in the stability-efficiency trade-off inherent in subspace updates. The original FRUGAL convergence analysis does not mandate a fixed
T
, providing theoretical flexibility. However, this choice presents a practical dilemma: a very small
T
(frequent updates) can introduce instability from repeated optimizer state resets, whereas a large
T
risks using a stale subspace that no longer captures the most important gradient directions.

Our dynamic mechanism is designed to navigate this trade-off autonomously. It maintains a frequent update schedule (a small, fixed
T_{\text{start}}
) during the initial, volatile training phase where adaptability is crucial. The frequency
T
is only increased when the model’s convergence stabilizes (indicated by
\Delta L_{\text{rel}}<\tau_{\text{low}}
). In this more stable regime, the identified state-full subspace is likely to remain effective for longer, making infrequent updates both computationally efficient and theoretically sound. This approach ensures that we reduce computational overhead primarily when it is safest to do so, minimizing risk to the convergence path. While a formal analysis of such an adaptive schedule presents a non-trivial challenge, we propose it as a compelling direction for future theoretical work.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced AdaFRUGAL, an adaptive framework that significantly enhances the resource efficiency of the memory-saving FRUGAL optimizer for training Large Language Models. By replacing the static hyperparameters of the original framework with two novel dynamic control mechanisms—a dynamic decay policy for the state-full ratio (
\rho
) and a loss-aware adaptive schedule for the update frequency (
T
)—AdaFRUGAL automates the optimization of memory and computational overhead during training.

Our comprehensive experiments across large-scale pre-training, domain adaptive pre-training, and downstream fine-tuning tasks consistently demonstrate the value of our approach. We showed that the FRUGAL family of methods outperforms prominent baselines like GaLore and BAdam. More importantly, our proposed
AdaFRUGAL-Dynamic-
T
mechanism not only reduces training time by up to 15% but also consistently maintains or even slightly improves model performance across all scenarios, highlighting the benefits of adaptive stability. Meanwhile,
AdaFRUGAL-Dynamic-
\rho
provides a powerful tool for proactively managing GPU memory, offering a valuable trade-off for severely resource-constrained environments.

By intelligently adapting to the dynamics of the training process, AdaFRUGAL presents a more practical, autonomous, and efficient solution for training large models. It alleviates the need for manual hyperparameter tuning and provides a robust framework for researchers and practitioners to tackle large-scale NLP tasks with greater resource efficiency. Future work could explore more sophisticated, non-linear control policies for
\rho
and
T
, or investigate their synergy with orthogonal techniques like 8-bit quantization to achieve even greater resource efficiency.
