The mediating role of cognitive function in the association between tooth loss and depressive symptoms: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Huang Shi-jia, Hu Ze-long, Qian Yu-xin, Xin Ming-zhe, Teng Bin-hong, Jin Lei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression is a significant health concern among the aging population, and prior research suggests edentulism as a risk factor for depression. However, the specific mechanisms linking edentulism and depression are not fully understood.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the complex interrelationships between tooth loss, cognitive function, and depressive symptoms in middle-aged and older adults. We specifically sought to determine if cognitive function mediates the association between tooth loss and depression.MethodsThis study performed a secondary analysis of cross-sectional data from the 2018 wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), with a mean age of 60.8 years (47% women, 53% men). Depressive symptoms, the binary outcome variable, were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10). Tooth loss was measured by self-report and categorized as a binary variable (complete tooth loss vs. not). Cognitive function, encompassing orientation, memory, calculation, and visuospatial skills, served as the mediate variable. Causal mediation analysis was performed to estimate the mediating role of cognitive function.ResultsThe final analysis included 11,000 participants. Univariate analysis showed a significant association between tooth loss and depression, which remained significant across all three multivariate logistic regression models. Causal mediation analysis indicated that the average causal mediation effect (ACME) of global cognitive ability on the association between tooth loss and depression was 0.043 (95% CI: 0.037, 0.050). The average direct effect (ADE) of tooth loss on depression was 0.024 (95% CI: 0.001, 0.050). Global cognitive ability mediated 61.6% of the total effect (95% CI: 0.439, 0.987).ConclusionIn this nationally representative cohort, complete tooth loss was associated with a higher prevalence of depression, with cognitive impairment accounting for a substantial proportion of this association. Background

Depression has emerged as a growing global public health concern, posing significant threats to individuals' physical and mental well-being [1]. The study estimates that 332 million people worldwide suffered from depression in 2021, making depression the second leading cause of non-fatal health loss worldwide [2]. Particularly in the older people population, depression is closely linked to increased all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease mortality [3]. Furthermore, the functional and cognitive impairment associated with depression is more severe in older adults, placing a heavy burden on individuals and families [4]. Therefore, the early identification and management of depression among the older people population have become crucial.

Oral health status, especially tooth loss, is recognized as an important factor affecting the health of older adults [5]. Tooth loss is a multifactorial oral disease process and comorbidity that impacts both oral and overall health [6]. Epidemiological and clinical studies consistently indicate that tooth loss is a risk factor for numerous chronic diseases in older populations, including kidney diseases, higher blood pressure, and cognitive impairments [7–9]. A growing body of research has identified a positive association between tooth loss and depression [10]. For instance, a study estimated that the causal effect of losing ten or more teeth had an impact comparable to that observed in adults with major depressive disorder [11]. In addition to tooth loss, periodontal disease, as a common oral health problem, is also one of the main causes of tooth loss. Recent studies indicate that periodontal disease itself is not only associated with systemic inflammation and chronic diseases, but may also be linked to cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms [12, 13]. The relationship between oral health, cognitive function, and depressive symptoms is complex and increasingly recognized. Therefore, examining the relationship between oral indicators such as tooth loss and periodontal disease as a whole and depression and cognitive function helps to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the impact of oral health on overall health.

Cognitive decline and depressive symptoms often co-occur and share several mechanisms [14, 15]. Longitudinal studies have provided evidence for a bidirectional association between depressive symptoms and memory function, where higher depressive symptoms are associated with poorer memory and faster memory decline, and conversely, a steeper decline in memory is associated with an acceleration in depressive symptoms [4, 16]. Biologically, depression may negatively affect cognition through increased cortisol levels resulting from HPA axis dysregulation, leading to neurotoxicity and accelerated neurodegeneration [17–19].

The specific mechanisms linking tooth loss, cognitive function, and depression are still being explored. Therefore, our study is the first to investigate cognitive ability as a mediator in the relationship between tooth loss and depressive symptoms, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of these interconnected health issues in the aging population.

Methods

Study participants

For this study, we performed a secondary analysis of cross-sectional data from the 2018 wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). While CHARLS is a longitudinal survey, the 2018 wave provided the most recent and comprehensive assessment of several key variables central to our investigation, including dental status and cognitive function. Therefore, we used this latest available dataset for our analysis.

Our initial dataset included 19,816 respondents. We sequentially excluded participants with missing data on key variables. Specifically, 1,811 individuals lacking information on depressive symptoms were excluded, leaving 18,005 participants. Subsequently, 18 individuals with missing data on tooth loss were removed, resulting in 17,987 participants. A further 5,872 participants were excluded due to missing cognitive data, yielding a sample of 12,115. Finally, 1,115 individuals were excluded due to missing values in other covariates. Consequently, 11,000 participants were included in the final analysis. The inclusion flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram of data clean

The CHARLS project stands as China's first nationally representative, prospective, and longitudinal cohort study dedicated to middle-aged and older adults. It was designed following the models of established international aging surveys, such as the US Health and Retirement Study (HRS), the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA), the Survey of Health, Aging and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), and the Japanese Study of Aging and Retirement (JSTAR). In its 2011 baseline wave, a multistage probability sampling method recruited over 17,500 Chinese residents aged 45 and above, encompassing 150 counties/districts and 450 communities across 28 provinces. The survey collects comprehensive information on demographics, socioeconomic status, health conditions, family structure, and healthcare utilization, supporting extensive research on aging. The CHARLS data used in this study are publicly available one year after collection. Detailed sampling procedures and methodology have been extensively described in prior publications [20].

Outcome variable

For this study, depressive symptoms served as the binary outcome variable. We evaluated participants' depressive status using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), a well-established self-report instrument for measuring depressive symptomatology, frequently employed in middle- and low-income contexts. The CES-D-10 comprises 10 items that gauge participants' experiences over the preceding week, covering aspects such as annoyance, hopefulness, fear, loneliness, unhappiness, attention deficit, and sleep disorder. Each item is rated on a Likert scale: 0 for 'rarely or none of the time' (< 1 day), 1 for 'some or a little of the time' (1–2 days), 2 for 'occasionally or a moderate amount of the time' (3–4 days), and 3 for 'most or all of the time' (5–7 days). A cumulative score, ranging from 0 to 30, is then calculated. Consistent with prior research, a cutoff score of ≥ 10 on the CES-D-10 was used to identify depressive symptoms. Consequently, individuals scoring ≥ 10 were classified as having depressive symptoms, and those scoring < 10 were classified as not having them [21, 22].

Independent variable and mediate variable

In this study, tooth loss was considered as an independent variable and was defined based on previous studies [23]. It was measured using the question “Have you lost all your teeth?” with the answer being “yes” or “no.” “Yes” means the respondent is tooth loss. Cognitive function was evaluated based on an approach adapted from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) [23], and applied the Wave4 of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The assessment encompassed four domains: orientation, memory, calculation, and visuospatial skills. Orientation was tested by asking participants to report the current year, month, date, day of the week, and season, with each correct response awarded one point (maximum score: 5). Memory was measured using immediate and delayed recall of a 10-word list, and the total memory score was the sum of all correctly recalled words from both tasks (maximum score: 20). For calculation, participants performed a serial subtraction task, subtracting seven from 100 in five steps, with one point for each correct answer (maximum score: 5). Visuospatial ability was assessed by asking participants to replicate two overlapping pentagons, with one point given for an accurate drawing. These component scores were summed to produce a total cognitive score ranging from 0 to 31, with higher scores reflecting better cognitive performance [24].

Based on evidence from previous studies [11, 25, 26], we included the following factors as covariates in the analysis: sociodemographic variables (age, gender, marital status, education level—illiterate, primary school, junior high school, and high school or above—and place of residence), lifestyle behaviors (smoking status—never smoked, currently smoking—and drinking status—never drank, currently drinking), physical health indicators (presence of chronic disease, average daily sleep duration, and total metabolic equivalent of task), as well as the presence of insurance.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Differences in baseline characteristics between groups were assessed using the independent sample t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Logistic regression analysis

To investigate the cross-sectional association between tooth loss and depression, logistic regression analyses were performed. Three progressively adjusted models were constructed to account for potential confounders: Model 1 (Demographic factors): Adjusted for age, sex, education level, marital status, and residential area. Model 2 (Demographic + lifestyle factors): Further adjusted for chronic disease status, medical insurance, alcohol consumption, smoking status, exercise habits, sleep duration, and total metabolic equivalents (METs) based on Model 1. Model 3 (Demographic + lifestyle + cognitive factors): Additionally adjusted for total cognitive score, episodic memory, and executive function based on Model 2. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Mediation analysis

To explore whether cognitive function mediates the relationship between tooth loss and depression, causal mediation analysis was performed within a counterfactual framework. Given that depression is a binary outcome, this approach allows for nonparametric estimation of causal effects, accommodating models with exposure–mediator interactions and non-continuous mediators or outcomes. The mediation analysis estimated the average mediation effect (indirect effect), the average direct effect, and the proportion of the total effect mediated through cognitive function (including total cognitive score, episodic memory, and executive function). This method assumes no unmeasured confounding between exposure, mediator, and outcome.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted using multivariable logistic regression to assess the robustness of the association between tooth loss and depression across different population strata. Subgroups were stratified by sex, marital status, education level, physical activity, cognitive function, total METs, alcohol consumption, smoking status, chronic disease status, and sleep duration. Interaction effects were evaluated using the likelihood ratio test to determine whether the association varied significantly across subgroups.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

Table 1 lists the baseline characteristics of the 11,000 participants included in the analysis. The overall mean age of the participants was 60.79 years ± 9.48 years. Women accounted for 47% of the total sample, and men accounted for 53%. Most participants were married (89%) and reported regular exercise (94%). The cohort was divided into two groups based on depression status (0: no depression, 1: depression). The two groups showed significant differences in various characteristics, including education level, gender, marital status, exercise habits, memory, executive function, global cognitive ability, chronic disease status, alcohol consumption status, smoking status, place of residence, and sleep duration (p < 0.001). Specifically, patients with depression (group 1) tended to be older (mean age 61.15 ± 9.37 years vs. 60.66 ± 9.51 years in group 0, but the difference was not statistically significant, p = 0.067). Group 1 had a higher proportion of illiterates (31% vs. 18%), females (57% vs. 43%), singles (15% vs. 9.9%), and inactives (9.0% vs. 5.1%). Depressed patients also had lower mean scores for memory (3.86 ± 1.96 vs. 4.64 ± 1.99), executive function (7.76 ± 2.40 vs. 8.75 ± 2.06), and general cognitive ability (11.62 ± 3.68 vs. 13.39 ± 3.38), all with p values ​​less than 0.001. In addition, depressed patients had a higher prevalence of chronic diseases (88% vs. 77%), were more likely to not drink alcohol (54% vs. 44%), were more likely to not smoke (59% vs. 53%), and were more likely to live in rural areas (56% vs. 41%). They had significantly shorter sleep duration (5.69 ± 1.92 h vs. 6.41 ± 1.61 h, p < 0.001). There was a statistically significant difference in the proportion of participants with health insurance (p = 0.008), with 2.6% of the depressed group and 1.7% of the non-depressed group without health insurance. Total metabolic equivalents did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.777). Excluded participants, defined as those with missing data on key study variables, differed systematically from included participants. They were older, more likely to be male, less educated, less physically active, and more likely to reside in rural areas, while marital status distributions were broadly similar between the two groups (Supplemental Table 1).

Analysis of the association between tooth loss and depression

Univariate logistic regression analysis revealed that individuals with tooth loss had significantly higher odds of depression compared to those without tooth loss (OR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.28–1.64; Table 2). This association remained robust across all three multivariate models: Model 1 adjusted for sociodemographic variables (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.16–1.50), Model 2 further adjusted for lifestyle factors (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.13–1.47), and Model 3 additionally adjusted for health-related covariates (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.11–1.45; Table 2). Detailed results for other covariates included in Models 1, 2, and 3 are presented in Supplemental Tables 2–4.

Causal mediation analysis

To explore the role of global cognitive ability in the association between tooth loss and depression, we conducted a causal mediation analysis (Fig. 3). The average causal mediation effect (ACME) of global cognitive ability on the association between tooth loss and depression was 0.043 [95% CI: 0.037, 0.050]. This suggests that global cognitive ability played a significant mediating role in the association between tooth loss and depression. The average direct effect (ADE) of tooth loss on depression was 0.024 [95% CI: 0.001, 0.050]. In addition, the proportion of the total effect mediated by global cognitive ability was 0.616 [95% CI: 0.439, 0.987].

Discussion

This study found a significant association between tooth loss and depression in the older people population in China. At the outset, the key findings should be highlighted concisely: complete tooth loss was associated with a higher prevalence of depression, and cognitive function explained 61.6% of this association through mediation analysis. These results suggest that tooth loss may indirectly affect depression by affecting cognitive function.

Baseline characteristics showed that patients with depression and non-depressed patients had significant differences in multiple variables, including education level, gender, marital status, exercise habits, memory, executive function, global cognitive ability, chronic disease status, alcohol consumption, smoking status, place of residence, and sleep time (p < 0.001). Patients with depression tended to have lower scores in memory, executive function, and general cognitive ability, and had a higher prevalence of chronic diseases. These results are consistent with previous studies showing a link between poor oral health, cognitive decline, and depressive symptoms.

To better understand the complex mechanisms underpinning this mediation, recent network analyses offer valuable insights into the interrelationships between symptoms of depression and cognitive performance [27]. Studies have identified specific "central" and "bridge" symptoms that are pivotal in the co-occurrence of depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment. Our finding of global cognitive ability as a mediator is consistent with this perspective. Specifically, cognitive domains such as "Orientation" (C1) and "Linguistic skills" (C5) have been identified as key bridge symptoms connecting depressive symptoms and cognitive performance in older adults with multimorbidity. This suggests that poor oral health and subsequent tooth loss may contribute to systemic inflammation or neurological changes that impair brain regions responsible for spatiotemporal orientation. Furthermore, difficulties with chewing due to tooth loss can impact "Linguistic skills" and overall communication, potentially affecting executive function and leading to cognitive and social impairments [28, 29].

While this study provides valuable insights into the mediating role of cognitive function, it is important to note that our exploration of other potential pathways (such as functional impairment or social relationships) remains speculative, since these mechanisms were not directly measured in our dataset. For example, poor oral health, including tooth loss, is associated with functional impairment in older adults [30]. Another longitudinal study found that social relationships mediated the association between tooth loss and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) in participants with moderate or high psychological resilience [31]. The study also noted that complete tooth loss and having 1–9 teeth increased the risk of ADL and IADL impairment, respectively. This suggests that the impact of tooth loss on overall well-being is multifaceted, involving not only cognitive function but also social and physiological aspects.

Another study similarly found that chewing ability mediated the relationship between tooth loss and depression in Japanese people aged 65 years and older, with a mediating proportion of 21%, which was higher than 11.7% in this study [11]. These discrepancies may be explained not only by differences in participant age but also by methodological differences in how cognitive or functional mediators were assessed, as well as the extent of covariate adjustment across studies. Our study highlights that tooth loss is a fundamental aspect of oral health that deserves special attention in older adults. Although the causal relationship between tooth loss and depression needs to be further confirmed by randomized clinical trials, our findings highlight the importance of comprehensive interventions to address emotional well-being in older adults. Therefore, our results suggest that maintaining good oral health through regular dental care may provide additional benefits for improving mental health in older adults.

An important limitation of our study is the issue of selection bias. A large proportion of participants were excluded due to missing data on key variables, and excluded individuals were systematically older, more likely to be male, less educated, less physically active, and more likely to reside in rural areas (Supplemental table S1). These differences suggest that the final analytic sample may underrepresent more vulnerable subgroups, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Future work with improved data completeness will be important to confirm whether the observed associations hold across broader populations. Meanwhile, we acknowledge that nutritional status may affect cognition and depression, but we did not include this in our analyses.

In interpreting our mediation findings, it is important to acknowledge the key assumptions underlying causal mediation analysis. This approach relies on sequential ignorability, which requires that there be no unmeasured confounding between exposure, mediator, and outcome after adjustment for covariates. Although we controlled for a wide range of sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health-related variables, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Moreover, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to fully verify temporal ordering, which is another important consideration for the validity of causal inference.

Ideally, sensitivity analyses or robustness checks could be conducted to evaluate how violations of these assumptions might influence the mediation results. However, in our study the exposure variable tooth loss was measured as a binary indicator (“yes” vs. “no”). Existing sensitivity analysis methods for causal mediation in R (such as the medsens function in the mediation package) require continuous or at least multi-level exposures and are not applicable to dichotomous exposures. Consequently, formal sensitivity analyses could not be implemented in our case. Instead, we attempted to mitigate this limitation by conducting extensive subgroup analyses and by adjusting for a broad set of covariates to reduce the likelihood of unmeasured confounding. Future longitudinal studies using multiple waves of CHARLS will be valuable to further validate the temporal relationships and causal pathways suggested by our cross-sectional findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals the significant association between tooth loss and a higher prevalence of depression among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Crucially, our findings highlight the prominent mediating role of global cognitive ability in this relationship, explaining 61.6% of the total effect of tooth loss on depression. This suggests that the impact of oral health on mental well-being extends beyond direct physiological links, actively involving cognitive pathways. These results align with existing literature on the interconnectedness of oral health, cognitive function, and mental health. Clinically, these findings suggest that interventions for older adults with tooth loss should adopt a holistic approach, incorporating cognitive assessment and interventions, to mitigate the risk of depressive symptoms and promote overall well-being.
