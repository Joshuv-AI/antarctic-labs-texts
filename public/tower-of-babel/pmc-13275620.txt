Association between intraoperative carotid blood flow reductions and postoperative delirium in older patients undergoing surgery: a prospective observational study
Authors: Xingyang Liu, Chunyan Ye, Jinghan Wu, Yingqi Li, E Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePostoperative delirium (POD) is common among older patients undergoing surgery and is associated with impaired cerebral perfusion. In this study, we sought to investigate the relationship between intraoperative carotid artery blood flow changes and POD risk in older patients undergoing abdominal surgery.MethodsWe conducted a prospective observational analysis of patients older than 65 yr who underwent elective abdominal surgery in a single tertiary care hospital from September 2021 to May 2022. Common carotid artery (CCA) blood flow was continuously monitored intraoperatively using ultrasound vector flow imaging (VFI). We assessed the association between CCA flow changes and POD with multivariable logistic regression models. We explored additional models incorporating intraoperative mean arterial pressure (MAP) reductions to evaluate relative predictive performance. Postoperative delirium was assessed within 7 days postoperatively.ResultsAmong 210 patients analyzed (from 242 screened), POD occurred in 41 (19.5%). In the primary analysis, longer intraoperative reductions in CCA blood flow > 40% from baseline were independently associated with increased POD risk (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] per 10-min increment, 2.24; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.51 to 3.31; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001), which remained robust in sensitivity analyses (aOR, 1.78; 95% CI, 1.45 to 2.19; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001). In secondary analyses, prolonged CCA blood flow increases were associated with reduced POD (aOR, 0.58 per 10-min increment; 95% CI, 0.40 to 0.83; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001). Common carotid artery flow reductions showed limited concordance with MAP-based hypotension (Cohen’s κ ≤ 0.08). Models incorporating CCA flow showed better discrimination and calibration metrics for predicting POD than those based on MAP.ConclusionsIn older patients undergoing abdominal surgery, persistent intraoperative CCA blood flow reductions > 40% from baseline were associated with an increased risk of POD Intermittent CCA flow measurement with VFI may help identify patients not identifiable through blood pressure monitoring alone. Nevertheless, because the results derive from an observational design with interval-based sampling, the findings should be interpreted with caution and warrant prospective validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12630-026-03139-x.Keywords: carotid artery blood flow, intraoperative monitoring, older adults, postoperative delirium, vector flow imaging

Postoperative delirium (POD) is a common perioperative complication in older patients, with an incidence ranging from 5% to 50% depending on surgery type.1 It manifests as acute confusion, inattention, and altered consciousness2 and is associated with adverse outcomes such as prolonged hospitalization, cognitive decline, and increased 1-year mortality.3–5 Despite its clinical significance, the pathophysiology of POD remains poorly understood.

Intraoperative cerebral hypoperfusion is considered a key mechanism,6 yet direct cerebral perfusion monitoring during general anesthesia remains challenging. The carotid arterial system supplies approximately 82% of cerebral blood flow7 intraoperative changes in carotid flow may reflect cerebral perfusion status. Doppler ultrasound and more recently vector flow imaging (VFI) have emerged as practical, noninvasive modalities for real-time assessment of carotid blood flow dynamics intraoperatively.8,9

Prior research has identified various physiologic predictors of POD, such as intraoperative hypotension10 and cerebral oxygen saturation.11 Recent evidence indicates that patients undergoing abdominal surgery frequently experience significant reductions in carotid artery blood flow.12 Nevertheless, limited data exist concerning the association between fluctuations in carotid blood flow and POD occurrence. Furthermore, the clinical implications of both reduced and increased carotid blood flow during surgery have yet to be systematically explored.

In this prospective observational study, we enrolled older adults undergoing elective abdominal surgery under general anesthesia. Intermittent intraoperative monitoring of carotid blood flow was performed using VFI. Our primary aim was to determine whether intraoperative carotid flow reductions were associated with POD. The secondary aim was to investigate the relationship between increased or mean carotid blood flow and the occurrence of POD.

Methods

Study design and population

This single-centre prospective observational cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital Central South University (Changsha, China) on 9 March 2021 (ref. No.: 20210386) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100048599) on 11 July 2021. We enrolled older patients (aged 65–80 yr) who underwent elective gastrointestinal surgery at Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, between September 2021 and May 2022. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: 1) history of severe traumatic brain injury, intracranial infection, Alzheimer’s disease, other forms of dementia, or psychosis; 2) imaging-confirmed cerebral infarction; 3) severe preoperative hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or coronary artery disease (for detailed definitions, see Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM] eAppendix 1); 4) alcohol dependence or withdrawal syndrome; 5) severe vision or hearing impairment; 6) hospitalization within the previous 3 months or terminal-stage illness; and 7) presenting with delirium at admission. Data were collected from electronic medical charts, anesthesia records, surgical reports, and laboratory results throughout the entire hospital stay. We obtained written informed consent from all participants or their legal representatives.

Vector flow imaging measurement of carotid blood flow

The primary exposure variable was intraoperative common carotid artery (CCA) blood flow. Preoperative baseline carotid artery blood flow was measured bilaterally, whereas intraoperative carotid artery blood flow was measured unilaterally using ultrasound VFI (linear array transducer, 3–9 MHz, Mindray Resona 7 ultrasound machine, Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronic Co., Shenzhen, China). Vector flow imaging is an angle-independent technique that quantifies blood flow velocity and direction. This technology is increasingly available on modern high-end commercial ultrasound systems, although it is not yet standard on all machines. Preoperative baseline carotid artery blood flow was reported after patients entered the operating room. Following a 10-min rest period in the supine position, bilateral carotid artery blood flow was assessed. For each side, three consecutive flow measurements were obtained and averaged to determine the baseline carotid blood flow for the patient. Electronic Supplementary Material eTable 1 presents the intraobserver variability of carotid blood flow measurements. The mean (standard deviation [SD]) left-sided flow was 317.7 (67.5) mL·min−1, while the right-sided flow was 325.0 (66.9) mL·min−1. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the two sides was 0.724 (P < 0.001), indicating a strong linear relationship. Intraoperatively, carotid artery blood flow was recorded every 10 min from anesthesia induction until the end of surgery. All patients underwent consistent intraoperative measurement of the left CCA owing to operating room layout constraints.

Intraoperative carotid artery blood flow reduction was defined by the degree of reduction relative to baseline values. The thresholds used to define blood flow reduction were < 30%, 30–40%, 40–50%, and > 50% relative to baseline. Blood flow measurements at each time point were used to give an estimate of blood flow over a 10-min period. Intraoperative carotid artery blood flow reduction was quantified using the duration of blood flow reduction within different thresholds throughout the surgery. Mean intraoperative carotid blood flow was calculated by dividing the sum of all reported carotid blood flow values by the total intraoperative duration of mechanical ventilation.

Common carotid artery blood flow measurements adhered to a standardized protocol: The measurement site was 1.5–2 cm below the carotid bulb8 at a depth of approximately 2 cm. The arrow lifecycle was set to 25 msec, with arrow density maintained at 10%. The luminal diameter was measured as the distance between the inner layers of the parallel vessel walls. Electronic Supplementary Material eFig. 1 presents the specific illustration of carotid artery VFI measurement.

Anesthesia and perioperative management

Intraoperative monitoring included electrocardiography, noninvasive blood pressure monitoring, pulse oximetry, Bispectral Index™ (BIS™; Medtronic, Dublin, Ireland), end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) concentrations, anesthetic gas concentrations, and urine output. Invasive arterial pressure and central venous pressure were monitored when clinically indicated.

The same anesthesia protocol was applied to all patients. Anesthesia induction was achieved using midazolam, etomidate, propofol, sufentanil, and cisatracurium, with dosages adjusted according to patient weight and age. Anesthesia maintenance involved propofol, remifentanil, cisatracurium, and sevoflurane, with sevoflurane discontinued 30 min before surgery completion. Routine intraoperative management included controlling anesthesia depth, fluid administration, and transfusions. Anesthesia depth was targeted to maintain a BIS value between 40 and 60, managed by adjusting anesthetic agents and administering vasoactive drugs such as ephedrine, keeping blood pressure fluctuations within 20% of baseline values. Additionally, mean arterial pressure (MAP) was kept above 65 mm Hg, and heart rate was maintained above 50 min−1. Electronic Supplementary Material eAppendix 2 details the specific hemodynamic management strategies. Mechanical ventilation parameters included a tidal volume of 6–8 mL·kg−1 body weight and respiratory rate adjustments to keep EtCO2 concentrations between 30 and 35 mm Hg. Upon completion of surgery, patients were extubated and initially observed in the postanesthesia care unit. Stable individuals were then transferred to general wards, whereas those deemed high risk or clinically unstable were admitted to the intensive care unit for continued monitoring and management. Postoperative analgesia was provided with self-controlled intravenous analgesia, using a combination of sufentanil and dezoxine. Other perioperative managements were provided per routine.

Postoperative delirium

The primary outcome was the occurrence of POD within 7 days following surgery. Postoperative delirium was diagnosed using the 3-Minute Diagnostic Interview for Confusion Assessment Method-Defined Delirium (3D-CAM) administered twice daily—morning (08:00–10:00 a.m.) and afternoon (4:00–6:00 p.m.)—from postoperative day 1 to day 7.13 The 7-day diagnostic window was chosen in accordance with clinical practice and is consistent with definitions supported by the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions, Clinical Modification (ICD-9/10-CM)14 and prior studies.15 Delirium was further evaluated through review of clinical notes, nursing documentation, and psychiatric consultation records. Electronic Supplementary Material eTable 2 provides further details. To minimize assessment bias, delirium was evaluated under blinded conditions.

Statistical analysis

A predefined data analysis and statistical plan was submitted to the institutional review board and is publicly accessible at the Open Science Framework (10.17605/OSF.IO/YV5QH). Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean (SD) and compared using the independent samples t test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed using the Chi square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To examine the association between the duration of intraoperative reductions in CCA blood flow and the occurrence of POD, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed. Potential confounders were identified on the basis of a combination of prior studies and clinical reasoning.16,17 As previously described, CCA flow reductions were quantified as the cumulative duration within specific reduction ranges relative to baseline: < 30%, 30–40%, 40–50%, and > 50%. These variables were treated as continuous predictors in the regression model, with all patients contributing either zero or non-zero values for each range. All analyses were conducted using the full cohort (N = 210) with no loss of sample size for any predictor. To assess the linearity assumption of the logistic regression models, we used restricted cubic spline functions (with three knots) to model each continuous predictor. Likelihood ratio tests were used to compare spline-based models with linear models. None of the tested variables showed statistically significant nonlinearity and were therefore modeled as linear terms (ESM eTable 3). Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported after adjusting for relevant confounders, including American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status classification and blood transfusion (categorical variables), as well as preoperative Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores, intraoperative blood loss, and intraoperative dose of ephedrine (continuous variables). Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for all covariates, and all values were below 5, indicating no concerning multicollinearity. Electronic Supplementary Material eTable 4 presents detailed VIF results. We also used restricted cubic spline models (with knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) to visualize the relationship between the duration of CCA blood flow reduction and POD risk.18 To formally test whether the effect of CCA flow reduction > 50% differed significantly from that of 40–50%, we conducted a postestimation comparison of the regression coefficients. To verify the robustness of the effect estimates, sensitivity analyses were conducted using both nonparametric bootstrap resampling and the Huber–White robust variance estimator. We used receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to assess the discriminative ability of the logistic regression model. The area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% CI are reported.

To mitigate potential residual confounding, we performed additional sensitivity analyses by expanding the original multivariable model to include the covariates age, preoperative serum albumin concentration, and intraoperative hypotension, quantified as the AUC for MAP < 65 mm Hg. Although these variables did not reach statistical significance in univariate analysis, they have been consistently associated with POD in prior studies.16,17,19 The sensitivity analyses were intended to assess the robustness of the primary findings.

We conducted subgroup analyses on the basis of the following prespecified criteria: age (65–70 yr vs > 70 yr; 70 yr selected on the basis of clinical relevance and literature-based risk stratification), sex (male vs female), ASA Physical Status classification (II vs III, as assessed during preoperative evaluation), and duration of mechanical ventilation (≤ 240 vs > 240 min, with 240 min chosen on the basis of the median duration). Adjusted odds ratios and their corresponding 95% CIs were calculated for each subgroup analysis.

An exploratory analysis was conducted to evaluate the association between intraoperative systemic hypotension and POD. Several blood-pressure-based metrics were examined, including the AUC for MAP < 65 mm Hg and the cumulative duration of MAP reductions from baseline (< 30%, 30–40%, 40–50%, and > 50%). Concordance between each hypotension definition and carotid hypoperfusion was evaluated using the Cohen’s κ statistic. Subsequently, we performed multivariable logistic regression analyses using each MAP-based parameter as the primary exposure. All models were adjusted for prespecified confounders. Model performance was assessed using the area under the ROC curve (AUC-ROC), Akaike information criterion (AIC), the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and Brier scores. The Brier score ranges from 0 to 1, with lower values indicating better overall model performance in terms of both discrimination and calibration.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Electronic Supplementary Material eAppendix 3 provides the specific R packages and code. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and we considered a P value of < 0.05 statistically significant. To control for family-wise error rate due to multiple comparisons, we applied the Bonferroni correction to both P values and 95% CIs in the main analysis. Specifically, this correction was applied for at least six multiple comparisons involving different ranges of carotid blood flow changes.

Sample size calculation

Based on previous studies, we assumed the incidence of POD in older patients undergoing abdominal surgery to be approximately 15%.20 A preliminary pilot study conducted by our group indicated that patients who developed delirium experienced a mean duration in CCA blood flow reduction from baseline of 190 min, compared with 140 min among patients without delirium. Assuming an SD of 62 min, we calculated the required sample size to detect this 50 min difference with a power of 85% at a two-sided significance level of 0.05, using PASS 2025 software (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA). The calculated required sample size was 180 patients. To account for a potential dropout rate of approximately 15%, we planned to enroll 210 participants.

Results

Study population and patient characteristics

Of the 242 patients initially screened, 210 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis. Figure 1 illustrates the screening and inclusion process, and Table 1 summarizes baseline characteristics of the study population. The mean (SD) age of the cohort was 69 (4) yr, and 98 patients (46.7%) were female. Of these, 141 (67.1%) were classified as ASA Physical Status III. Surgery was performed laparoscopically in 176 (83.8%) patients, while the remaining 34 (16.2%) underwent open laparotomy procedures. Postoperative delirium occurred in 41 (19.5%) patients, whereas the remaining 169 (80.5%) patients did not develop delirium. As Table 1 summarizes, baseline carotid artery blood flow was similar between the two groups, but intraoperative reductions in carotid blood flow were significantly greater among patients who developed POD (P < 0.001). No patients in the cohort experienced stroke or persistent neurologic deficits during hospitalization.

Primary analysis

As Table 2 shows, after adjusting for ASA Physical Status classification, preoperative MoCA scores, intraoperative blood loss, blood transfusion, and cumulative ephedrine dose, both 40–50% and > 50% reductions from baseline were significantly associated with an increased risk of POD. Electronic Supplementary Material eTable 6 provides unadjusted odds ratios and corresponding 95% CIs. Building upon the primary findings, further analysis showed that, after adjustment for confounding variables, the duration of intraoperative carotid blood flow reduction > 40% from baseline was associated with an increased risk of POD (aOR, 2.24 per 10-min increment; 95% CI, 1.51 to 3.31; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001), as Table 3 (Model 1) shows. Secondary analysis

In the secondary analysis, a longer duration of intraoperative increases in carotid blood flow relative to baseline was associated with a lower risk of POD (Table 2). Specifically, for every 10-min increase in the duration of increased carotid blood flow was associated with an aOR of 0.58 (95% CI, 0.40 to 0.83; Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001). As Table 3 (Model 2) shows, additional covariates including age, preoperative serum albumin level, and intraoperative hypotension were incorporated into the regression model. As ESM eTables 15 and 16 present, concordance analyses showed no agreement between CCA flow and MAP reduction, except when MAP fell by > 40% and CCA flow by > 50%, where Cohen’s κ indicated only slight agreement (κ = 0.08; P = 0.01). Discussion

Our study found that a longer reduction in carotid blood flow during surgery, particularly a reduction greater than 40% from baseline, was associated with an increased risk of POD. Conversely, prolonged periods of increased carotid blood flow above baseline were associated with a reduced risk of delirium. These associations underscore the potential importance of intraoperative cerebral perfusion patterns in POD development.

Several previous studies have highlighted the importance of intraoperative hemodynamic fluctuations, including alterations in cerebral perfusion, as a key factor contributing to POD.21,22 By specifically examining carotid artery blood flow, our findings provide quantitative evidence regarding how the magnitude and duration of intraoperative perfusion changes may influence delirium risk. Supporting our results, a prior study reported significantly lower carotid artery blood flow velocities in patients who developed delirium following cardiac surgery compared with patients without delirium.23 Additionally, reduced CCA blood flow has been associated with cognitive impairment among older patients with heart failure,24 indicating a broader relationship between carotid artery perfusion and neurocognitive outcomes. Our study expands upon these findings by showing that intraoperative reductions in CCA blood flow may serve as a risk factor for POD among older patients undergoing noncardiac surgery.

The observed link between reduced carotid artery blood flow and POD may be attributed to impaired cerebral perfusion and subsequent disturbances in brain function. Cognition-related regions such as the medial temporal lobes and frontal-subcortical circuits are particularly vulnerable to hypoperfusion and oxygen deficiency.25 Even brief intraoperative reductions in cerebral blood flow can trigger neuronal stress and cognitive impairment.26 Additionally, hypoperfusion may increase oxidative stress via reactive oxygen species production,27 and activate microglia-driven neuroinflammation, further contributing to neuronal injury.28,29 Ageing amplifies these effects through neuronal loss, impaired vascular regulation, and blood–brain barrier disruption.30

Nonetheless, prior intraoperative monitoring studies report mixed associations with POD. Transcranial Doppler studies generally align with our findings and link reduced cerebral flow with delirium, although most evidence derives from cardiac surgery with limited data in noncardiac surgical cohorts.31,32 In contrast, cerebral oximetry has yielded inconsistent associations with delirium.33–35 In this context, our study used an alternative approach by measuring extracranial carotid inflow and identified an association between cerebral hypoperfusion and increased POD risk. Given the multifactorial nature of delirium, these observations should be interpreted with caution.

Additionally, longer durations of intraoperative increases in carotid blood flow relative to baseline were associated with a decreased risk of delirium in this study, suggesting a potential neuroprotective effect. Evidence on intraoperative cerebral hyperperfusion and delirium remains limited and inconsistent. One cohort study found no significant association using cerebral oxygen saturation monitoring,11 while another study of cardiac surgery linked a MAP above the upper limit of autoregulation to higher delirium risk, possibly due to hyperperfusion-related damage.36 In our cohort, only 3.8% of patients showed > 130% increases from baseline, which likely remained within autoregulatory limits and may have mitigated ischemic injury. Further studies are needed to clarify the neurologic effects of more substantial cerebral perfusion increases.

These findings underscore the importance of intraoperative cerebral perfusion monitoring. Although no cases of overt stroke or persistent neurologic deficits were observed in this cohort, the absence of such events should not be interpreted as evidence that intraoperative carotid hypoperfusion lacks clinical relevance. Longer-term follow-up studies are needed to evaluate potential subclinical or delayed neurologic consequences.

Nevertheless, real-time intraoperative monitoring of carotid perfusion remains challenging in routine practice. Carotid blood flow during surgery is affected by multiple factors, including anesthesia, ventilation settings, and surgical manipulation.12 Ultrasound technology provides dynamic, noninvasive bedside evaluation of carotid blood flow parameters for surgical patients. Additionally, during laparoscopic surgery, fluctuations in arterial CO2 partial pressure can lead to cerebral vasodilation.37,38 Simply measuring blood flow velocity may not accurately reflect the true perfusion status, and therefore, we have achieved more precise measurement of blood flow volume through the application of VFI technology.9 Vector flow imaging is an advanced ultrasound technique that captures velocity components from multiple angles to reconstruct actual blood flow vectors.39,40 Prior studies have reported that VFI offers acceptable accuracy for quantifying carotid blood flow, with in vivo measurement errors ranging from 10% to 20% when compared with the gold standard, phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging. Vector flow imaging has also shown superior consistency relative to conventional Doppler methods, particularly in complex flow environments such as near the carotid bifurcation.41 Vector flow imaging has been proven to have significant clinical value in flow measurement.

An additional finding was that, although most patients were not diagnosed with intraoperative hypotension using noninvasive arterial blood pressure measurement, a considerable proportion exhibited carotid hypoperfusion. This dissociation suggests impaired cerebrovascular regulation, particularly in older adults. Age-related factors such as reduced baseline cerebral blood flow, increased vascular resistance, and a narrowed autoregulatory range, as well as chronic hypertension elevating the lower autoregulatory threshold, may render the brain more vulnerable to hypoperfusion despite normal systemic pressures.42–44 General anesthesia further impairs cerebrovascular responsiveness by reducing sympathetic tone and baroreflex sensitivity.45,46 Other factors such as positive pressure ventilation and reduced venous return may also contribute.47 In our study, models incorporating carotid flow reduction showed better predictive performance for delirium than MAP-based models, underscoring the limitations of standard blood pressure monitoring and supporting the value of direct carotid flow assessment.

In addition to the primary findings, several baseline differences between the POD and non-POD groups warrant further consideration. One notable observation was that patients without POD received a higher cumulative dose of ephedrine during surgery. Nevertheless, the overall incidence of intraoperative hypotension did not significantly differ between groups (P = 0.30), suggesting this was not because of divergent blood pressure management. The discrepancy may be due to the modest sample size or individual variability in responsiveness to ephedrine. Moreover, cumulative ephedrine dose was significantly associated with POD in only a few adjusted models, suggesting that the role of vasopressor administration in the development of delirium should be interpreted with caution. This finding requires further validation in studies with larger sample sizes.

Although several prior studies have reported an association between intraoperative blood transfusion and POD,16,48,49 this relationship was not consistently observed across all models in our study. Transfusion remained statistically significant in only a subset of models, and the wide CIs observed in other models suggest limited precision. These findings indicate that transfusion may reflect downstream physiologic deterioration, such as blood loss or metabolic stress, rather than serve as an independent determinant of delirium risk.

In summary, our findings underscore the clinical value of individualized intraoperative management strategies aimed at preserving adequate carotid artery perfusion to mitigate the risk of POD among older patients undergoing surgery. Future studies are necessary to further validate these associations in broader surgical populations and to determine whether targeted interventions that maintain or enhance cerebral perfusion during surgery can improve other neurologic outcomes and long-term cognitive health.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-centre study, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings; additionally, the restricted setting may have constrained the diversity of patient characteristics and practice patterns, potentially introducing unrecognized or residual confounding. Second, only the left CCA was monitored owing to technical constraints. Although baseline concordance between sides was high, intraoperative asymmetry cannot be excluded. Third, although we averaged multiple measurements to reduce error, interobserver variability was not formally assessed. Fourth, carotid flow was reported every 10 min, potentially missing transient fluctuations. This intermittent sampling assumes a uniform risk within each interval, which may have contributed to the relatively large effect sizes observed (aOR, 1.78–2.24 per 10-min increment). While the association was robust across sensitivity analyses and thresholds, the magnitude of association should be interpreted with caution and not directly generalized as a clinical risk estimate. Future studies should adopt more frequent, real-time monitoring and evaluate active modulation strategies. Finally, while sample size estimation was based on group differences in a continuous variable, the final analysis used multivariable logistic regression for the binary outcome (POD). Although robustness was confirmed via bootstrap and robust variance estimation, this methodologic shift should be considered when interpreting results.

Conclusions

In older patients undergoing abdominal surgery, persistent intraoperative CCA blood flow reductions > 40% from baseline were associated with an increased risk of POD. Conversely, sustained increases in carotid blood flow were associated with a lower risk. These observed associations support the potential value of monitoring cerebral perfusion during surgery. Further research is required to confirm these findings in other surgical populations and to explore clinical approaches for optimizing cerebral perfusion management during surgery.
