Introduction

Dental trauma is a prevalent issue in pediatric dentistry, with significant potential for both immediate and long-term consequences on a child's oral health and development.1 Trauma to the anterior teeth, especially the maxillary incisors, is particularly common in children because of their position in the dental arch and their involvement in various physical activities.2 The long-term management of such injuries requires careful consideration, as the effects of dental trauma can extend beyond immediate physical damage to the tooth and surrounding tissues, often leading to esthetic, functional, and psychological challenges.3

Fractures involving the roots of teeth present a unique challenge in pediatric patients. These fractures can significantly compromise the prognosis of the affected tooth, often necessitating multidisciplinary approaches for optimal management.4 Such cases require not only endodontic and periodontal interventions but also long-term restorative and prosthetic planning. In pediatric patients, considerations related to growth and development further complicate treatment decisions, as definitive interventions, such as implants, may need to be delayed until the patient reaches skeletal maturity.5

Tooth extraction is often recommended for teeth that cannot be maintained in optimal health, function, or esthetics or for strategic purposes, such as during orthodontic treatment.6 Dental extraction triggers a series of biological changes that disrupt homeostasis and alter the structural configuration, resulting from the local inflammatory response to the surgical trauma, making bone resorption after tooth extraction an unavoidable outcome.6 Studies done by Araujo and Lindhe in 2005 on a canine model have shown significant dimensional changes in the alveolar ridge within the first 2–3 months after extraction, which occur more prominently on the buccal aspect.7 Alveolar ridge volume deficiencies can complicate future tooth replacement therapies, such as fixed dental prostheses, including implant-supported restorations.8

In order to achieve the most optimal prosthetic outcome, it is crucial for the alveolar ridge to have an appropriate height and width, especially in the anterior area of the esthetic zone.9 Bone augmentation procedures can be used as an alternative to correct a ridge defect, but, unfortunately, they can be associated with unpredictable outcomes. Depending on the age of the patient, a trauma or extraction site may need to be managed for 10 years or more before a child's facial growth is complete.10

Alveolar ridge preservation (ARP) therapy is often recommended to prevent significant alveolar ridge resorption following complete or partial tooth extraction. It can be utilized during immediate implant placement procedures or to minimize the need for additional ridge augmentation before or during delayed implant placement.11 The objective of ARP is to maintain the horizontal and vertical dimensions of the alveolar ridge using various techniques, such as bone grafts known as autografts, allografts, xenografts, or alloplastic materials; soft tissue grafts; guided bone regeneration (GBR) with resorbable or nonresorbable barriers; biologically active materials (growth factors); or combinations of the abovementioned techniques, with the purpose of minimizing the reduction in coronal alveolar bone height and width following tooth extraction.12

In recent years, autologous dentin grafts (ADG) have emerged as promising biomaterials in ARP. Extracted teeth can be processed into graftable particulate dentin, providing a biocompatible, osteoconductive, and patient-derived scaffold that avoids immune response and eliminates the need for donor-site morbidity.13,14 This technique has shown comparable outcomes to xenografts and allografts in ridge preservation, making it a valuable option in pediatric and adult patients.

Additionally, clinical hypnosis has gained attention as a noninvasive behavioral management tool to reduce anxiety and improve cooperation in children undergoing dental procedures, including those involving extractions and ARP.15 When implemented appropriately, hypnosis can help minimize the need for pharmacological sedation or general anesthesia, thereby enhancing both clinical efficiency and patient comfort in pediatric dental trauma management.

This case series presents the long-term management of two boys, aged 9 and 12, who sustained root fractures of the upper left central incisor (tooth 21) following traumatic falls due to multiple reasons. Despite initial root canal treatment and several attempts to restore the fractured tooth, the tooth eventually required extraction. The chosen treatment approach involved ARP with an ADG, a relatively novel technique aimed at maintaining the alveolar ridge for future implant placement. This case series also emphasizes the role of anxiety management in pediatric patients undergoing complex dental procedures. Dental anxiety is a significant barrier to effective treatment, especially in children, and can hinder cooperation during procedures. In these cases, clinical hypnosis was employed as a nonpharmacological technique to manage the patients' anxiety, allowing for a smoother and more comfortable surgical experience.

The report highlights the importance of interdisciplinary cooperation involving pediatric dentistry and periodontics in the management of complex dental trauma cases. Such collaboration ensures a comprehensive approach to treatment, addressing not only the immediate clinical needs but also the long-term functional and esthetic outcomes for the patients. Additionally, the case series emphasizes the significance of tailored anxiety management strategies in enhancing patient cooperation and improving treatment outcomes in pediatric dental trauma cases.

This case series serves as a valuable contribution to the growing body of literature on the use of ARP with ADG in young patients, demonstrating its efficacy in maintaining alveolar bone height and preparing for future restorative procedures. It also reinforces the need for personalized, patient-centered approaches in managing dental anxiety, particularly in young patients facing invasive dental treatments.

Case Description 1

AF, a 12-year-old boy, presented with a dislodged filling in his upper anterior tooth. His dental trauma occurred when he was 8 years old after a fall while playing on a scooter. Despite root canal treatment at a hospital, his condition worsened, with multiple episodes of restoration loss. He was medically healthy, with no known allergies or significant medical history. Upon examination, the presence of a fracture line on the distal aspect extending subgingivally was noted on tooth 21, complicated by a dislodged restoration with gutta-percha exposed at the canal orifice. Tooth 21 was mesiolabially rotated, with mild gingival overgrowth present on the mesial aspect of tooth 21. It was not tender to percussion or palpation, with no signs of abscess or fistula. Localized gingivitis with multiple plaque accumulations was noted on the posterior teeth, with the presence of calculus on the lingual surfaces of the lower teeth.

Pretreatment Examination

Extraoral: Symmetrical face, normal mouth opening, competent lips, and normal temporomandibular joint (TMJ) function.

Intraoral: Tooth 21 had a fractured distal aspect extending subgingivally, with exposed gutta-percha and food impaction, mild gingival overgrowth, no tenderness on percussion, localized gingivitis with plaque accumulation, and calculus on the posterior teeth.

Radiographic findings: Supracrestal coronal third root fracture with poor root canal obturation and sufficient alveolar bone height (Figs 1 and 2).

Treatment Plan

The treatment plan included:

Preventive measures: Oral hygiene instructions and full-mouth scaling, application of topical fluoride, and surveillance for early caries.

Surgical intervention: Extraction of tooth 21 followed by ARP using an ADG, in collaboration with an endodontist and a periodontist.

Anxiety management: The patient was highly anxious about the surgical procedure, and clinical hypnosis was employed during surgery to manage his anxiety.

Restorative management: Temporary glass ionomer cement (GIC) filling was placed to seal the canal before extraction, and a temporary immediate denture was provided to replace tooth 21 after extraction.

Surgical Procedure

On the day of surgery, AF underwent clinical hypnosis, guided by a storyline based on his favorite video game. Blood was drawn and processed for platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) to aid healing. The extraction was atraumatic, and the socket was filled with a combination of crushed ADG and PRF. The surgical area was covered with a collagen plug, and the flap was sutured.

Follow-up and Outcomes

The patient was reviewed at 1 week, 3 months, 1 year, and 2 years postoperatively. The clinical and radiographic findings showed successful maintenance of alveolar bone height with no complications. Plans for a future dental implant at the extraction site are in place once the patient reaches skeletal maturity.

Case Description 2

AL, a 9-year-old boy with no known medical illnesses or allergies and no history of hospitalization, presented with a broken upper anterior tooth. The patient reported a fall from a bicycle and sustained a complicated crown fracture of tooth 21 with a fracture at the mesial part of the root surface and uncomplicated crown fractures of teeth 11 and 31. Tooth 21 was restored by a dentist at a private clinic, but the filling became dislodged. The composite restoration was redone multiple times, but it repeatedly fractured or became dislodged.

Pretreatment Examination

Extraoral: Symmetrical face, normal mouth opening, competent lips, and normal temporomandibular joint (TMJ) function.

Intraoral: Tooth 21—Dislodged composite restoration up to the initial fracture line, movable temporary filling, positive tenderness to percussion (TTP), no mobility, swollen adjacent gingiva due to poor oral hygiene, no sinus tract, and tenderness on palpation at the upper left labial sulcus. Generalized plaque was noted on all tooth surfaces.

Radiographic findings: Periapical radiolucency indicating a periapical lesion, no evidence of a fracture at the mesial surface on two-thirds of the root surface, and sufficient alveolar bone height noted on the mesial and distal aspects (Figs 3 and 4).

Periapical radiograph of tooth 21 taken at the first visit. Irregular radiopacity is present inside the canal of tooth 21, indicating a void gutta-percha (GP) restoration, with loss of continuity of the lamina dura and a jagged root on the mesial aspect. Alveolar bone height is sufficient

Tooth 21 temporized with glass ionomer cement (GIC) to prevent bacterial infiltration into the canal

Diagnosis

Complicated crown fracture with a fracture at the mesial part of the root surface.

Uncomplicated crown fractures of teeth 11 and 31.

Gingivitis and early caries lesions.

Treatment Plan

The treatment plan included:

Preventive measures: Oral hygiene instructions, full-mouth scaling, application of topical fluoride, and surveillance for early caries.

Surgical intervention: Extraction of tooth 21 followed by ARP using an ADG, frenectomy, and crown lengthening of tooth 11, all in collaboration with a periodontist.

Anxiety management: The patient was highly anxious about the surgical procedure, and clinical hypnosis was employed during surgery to manage his anxiety.

Restorative management: A temporary immediate denture attached to teeth 11 and 22 using fiber-reinforced composite to replace tooth 21.

Surgical Procedure

On the day of surgery, AL underwent clinical hypnosis, guided by a storyline based on his favorite video game. Blood was drawn and processed for platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) to aid healing. The extraction was atraumatic, and the socket was filled with a combination of crushed ADG and PRF. The surgical area was covered with a collagen plug, and the flap was sutured (Figs 5 and 6).

Discussion

Pediatric dental trauma poses unique challenges because of the interplay between clinical, developmental, and psychological factors.1,2,4 The cases presented in this report highlight the innovative use of ADGs for ARP and clinical hypnosis for anxiety management, both of which address the immediate needs and long-term outcomes of young patients.

Tooth extraction is often associated with significant alveolar ridge resorption, with up to 50% of the ridge volume lost within the first 2–3 months postextraction, particularly on the buccal side.6,7 ARP is a critical intervention to mitigate this bone loss and prepare the site for future restorative procedures, such as implants.11,16

The use of autologous dentin as a grafting material has emerged as a promising alternative to xenografts and synthetic grafts. Dentin shares structural and compositional similarities with bone, comprising approximately 45% organic material (primarily type I collagen and bone morphogenetic proteins) and 55% inorganic hydroxyapatite, which contribute to its osteoinductive and osteoconductive properties.17,18 Studies by Kim et al. in 2020 demonstrated that ADG effectively promotes new bone formation while minimizing inflammatory responses, making it an ideal choice for ARP (Figs 9 to 17).19

In the cases presented, ADG was prepared using a Smart Dentin Grinder, ensuring sterilization and optimal particle size. The graft was processed to remove organic debris, further enhancing its biocompatibility. Long-term follow-up confirmed the efficacy of ADG in maintaining ridge dimensions, as evidenced by stable alveolar bone height at 1 year and 2 years postsurgery. These results align with findings by Solyom et al. in 2023, who noted that ADG significantly reduces the need for secondary bone augmentation procedures.20

Dental anxiety is a common barrier to effective treatment, particularly in pediatric patients. Hypnosis has been shown to be a reliable, nonpharmacological method for managing procedural anxiety, improving patient cooperation and comfort.15 By creating personalized narratives aligned with the interests of the patients, such as favorite video games, clinical hypnosis helped reduce anxiety during surgical procedures in both cases presented. Hypnosis enhances the child's ability to cope with dental procedures by promoting relaxation and a sense of control. This aligns with previous research emphasizing its efficacy in reducing procedural pain and anxiety in pediatric dental settings.15

Maintaining ridge dimensions is essential for future implant placement, particularly in the anterior esthetic zone. Research by Couso-Queiruga et al. in 2022 emphasizes that ARP reduces the need for ancillary bone augmentation, streamlining future interventions and improving outcomes.9 In pediatric patients, delaying implant placement until skeletal maturity is critical to avoid complications related to ongoing facial growth, further underscoring the importance of effective ARP strategies.6

This case series highlights the efficacy of ARP with ADG in maintaining alveolar ridge dimensions, making it a favorable option for young patients awaiting future implants.13,14 The use of clinical hypnosis was instrumental in managing the patients' anxiety, ensuring a smooth surgical process without pharmacological interventions.15 The successful collaboration between specialties ensured comprehensive care for the patients. This case series adds to the growing evidence supporting ADG as a biocompatible and cost-effective alternative for ARP. The successful outcomes observed reinforce the potential of ADG to minimize the need for synthetic or xenogeneic materials, making it a preferred choice for young patients.19,21 Additionally, the integration of clinical hypnosis highlights the importance of addressing psychological aspects of care, ensuring a holistic approach to pediatric dental trauma management.15

Future studies should focus on the long-term outcomes of implants placed in sites preserved with ADG and further explore the potential of hypnosis techniques for managing anxiety in diverse clinical settings.

Conclusion and Clinical Implications

This case series demonstrates the successful long-term management of dental trauma in pediatric patients using a multidisciplinary approach and innovative techniques such as ARP with ADG and clinical hypnosis. ADG should be considered an alternative biomaterial to xenografts for alveolar socket preservation. One of the special characteristics of dentin is its osteoinductive and osteoconductive properties, and it can act as a bone replacement material without causing inflammation. This approach not only ensured the immediate treatment of the fractured tooth but also prepared the site for future implant placement, preserving both function and esthetics.
