Cardiac Structure and Function and Pulmonary Congestion Across New York Heart Association Classes in Patients With Acute Heart Failure
Authors: Ali Hikmat Al‐Rubai, Filip Soeskov Davidovski, Caroline Espersen, Anton Stanchev, Laura Maria Adam, Ayat Khoraizat, Kristoffer Grundtvig Skaarup, Maria Dons, Jacob Christensen, Emil Durukan, Ema Rastoder, Morten Sengeløv, Katrine Feldballe Bernholm, Lisa Steen Duus, Caroline Løkke Bjerregaard, Zahra Juma, Mats Christian Højbjerg Lassen, Anne Marie Reimer Jensen, Adam Femerling Langhoff, Peter Godsk Jørgensen, Manan Pareek, Emil Wolsk, Morten Schou, Tor Biering‐Sørensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAcute heart failure (AHF) is a major global health challenge, yet the relationship between New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification and objective markers such as echocardiography, lung ultrasound (LUS), and biomarkers remains unclear.MethodsIn this prospective two‐center study, patients admitted with AHF (2022‐2024) underwent echocardiography, 8‐zone LUS, ECG, and biomarker sampling. Patients were stratified by NYHA class at enrollment, and associations with clinical, echocardiographic, and LUS measures were analyzed. Participants were invited for an identical follow‐up examination 1–3 months post‐discharge. The main outcome was a 180‐day composite of AHF readmission or all‐cause mortality evaluated across NYHA classes.ResultsAmong 564 patients (mean age 78.2 years; 56.3% male), 23% were classified as NYHA class I/II, 27% as NYHA class III, and 50% as NYHA class IV. Higher NYHA class at admission was associated with older age, greater comorbidity burden, lower left ventricular ejection fraction, impaired global longitudinal strain, higher E/e′ ratio, and B‐line count (all p <0.05). At follow‐up (n = 160), left ventricular systolic function, B‐line count, and left atrial size parameters were similar across baseline NYHA class. Atrial functional parameters (peak atrial longitudinal and contraction strain) remained lower with increasing NYHA class at admission (both p <0.05). NYHA class at admission was not associated with the 180‐days outcome.ConclusionIn this prospective study of hospitalized AHF patients, higher NYHA class at admission was associated with greater cardiac dysfunction, B‐line burden, and comorbidity burden, but these differences largely resolved at follow‐up. NYHA class at admission did not significantly predict the composite outcome.

Introduction

Acute heart failure (AHF) is a public health concern with >10 million people hospitalized due to AHF worldwide annually. [1, 2, 3] Despite advancements in treatment and improved survival, AHF remains associated with high mortality and poor quality of life, and a burden to the global healthcare system. [2] Previous studies display a very poor prognosis with a 5‐year mortality rate of around 50% and a 6‐month readmission rate of at least 30%. [1, 4, 5]

New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification has for decades served as the standard measure for assessing symptom burden, functional capacity and to estimate mortality in heart failure (HF) patients, guiding therapy in both research and clinical settings. [6] Although NYHA classification is widely and systematically applied, patients with AHF within the same NYHA class may experience heterogene.ous clinical outcomes. [7, 8]

The vast majority of patients hospitalized with AHF belong to NYHA classes III and IV. [9, 10] Patients in these groups have a higher risk of cardiovascular death, HF readmission, and all‐cause hospitalization compared with those belonging to lower NYHA classes. [9, 11, 12] However, even within the same NYHA class, outcomes differ markedly—for instance, while 30–60% of patients in NYHA class IV experience rehospitalization or death during follow‐up in clinical studies, a substantial proportion remain clinically stable. [13, 14] This variability highlights that the NYHA classification alone may not accurately capture the biological or hemodynamic differences among patients with similar symptom burdens. The objective of this study was to evaluate the association between admission NYHA class and objective multi‐modal measures in patients hospitalized with AHF, to investigate the relationship between NYHA class and 180‐day clinical outcomes, and to characterize the recovery trajectory of cardiac structure and function at mid‐term follow‐up.

Methods

Study Population

The Copenhagen Study of Acute Heart Failure (COSTA‐HF) is a prospective two‐center observational cohort study that enrolled adults hospitalized with AHF between September 2022 and July 2024. The study was conducted at the Departments of Cardiology at Copenhagen University Hospital—Herlev and Gentofte, Denmark.

The study design has been described in detail elsewhere. [15] In short, new admissions to the cardiology departments at both hospitals would be screened for eligibility after being seen by a cardiologist. Inclusion criteria were clinical signs and symptoms of AHF, such as venous congestion, dyspnea, fatigue, weight gain, and peripheral edema, were included. [15] Patients with a prior history of HF who were admitted with acute decompensation were also enrolled. Exclusion criteria included inability to provide informed consent and age under 18. Participants with missing NYHA class assignment were excluded from all analyses.

Eligible patients were examined by study investigators during the early admission period. Upon enrollment, patients were examined with transthoracic echocardiography, ECG, 8‐zone LUS, and blood samples were collected.

Echocardiography, LUS, and NYHA classification were performed by study investigators at enrollment, a median of 2 days after hospital admission, following transfer from the emergency department to the cardiology ward. Examinations were performed at the bedside using portable ultrasound systems rather than in a dedicated echocardiography laboratory.

All patients had to provide written informed consent prior to participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the second Declaration of Helsinki.

Ethics Declaration

The study was approved by the Regional Ethics Committee (VEK; H‐21000018) and the Danish Data Protection Agency (PRIVACY; P‐2022‐355).

Baseline Characteristics

Medical records were used to obtain clinical and demographic data, including prior medical history, comorbidities, and medications. Identification of the etiology of AHF relied on review of the cardiologist's notes together with the documented clinical assessment in the patient's medical record. NYHA classification, Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) score, and family history of cardiovascular disease were assessed by study staff during enrollment in the study.

Medical history was defined based on International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision codes adjudicated by manual medical record review. A history of ischemic heart disease (IHD) was defined as prior myocardial infarction, percutaneous coronary intervention, or coronary artery bypass grafting. Significant valvular disease was considered present in cases of stenosis or regurgitation of at least moderate severity, as documented by the treating physician. Cerebrovascular events included cerebral infarction, cerebral hemorrhage, and transient ischemic attack. Hypercholesterolemia was defined as low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) level ≥3.0 mmol/L at admission [16] or the use of lipid‐lowering therapy at enrollment. Hypertension was defined as baseline use of antihypertensive medication, where the treatment indication was documented as hypertension, while atrial fibrillation or flutter (AF) was defined as having ICD‐10 codes for AF and/or history of AF‐related procedures.

Laboratory results reported below the lower detection limit were replaced with the corresponding threshold value (e.g., values reported as <5.9 were assigned a value of 5.9) to allow for analysis. Baseline medication use was determined from the medication list available at admission.

Transthoracic Echocardiography

Trained sonographers examined the participants at inclusion using portable Vivid IQ 4D Ultrasound Systems (GE Healthcare, Horten, Norway) following a predefined protocol provided in Appendix 1 . The images were stored in a vault and analyzed by a single trained investigator using ViewPoint (GE Healthcare, Horten, Norway). Three cardiac cycles were recorded for each projection. The cardiac cycle with the most optimal image quality was selected for analyses. The investigator was blinded to clinical outcomes. Only imaging of sufficient quality was included in the analysis. All strain measurements are presented as absolute values. A detailed description of the measurement of echocardiographic parameters is provided in Appendix 2 .

Lung Ultrasound

The cardiac ultrasound probe was also used for LUS examination. A detailed description of how the images were acquired and analyzed is provided elsewhere. [17, 18] A standardized 8‐zone imaging protocol was used. [17] The protocol included 4 zones per hemithorax with the depth set to 15 cm, and each clip was recorded for 6 s (Appendix 3 ). In each zone, the frame with the greatest number of B‐lines was identified. The total B‐line count was reported as the sum of all B‐lines in each zone. Examinations with inadequate images in three or more zones were excluded. Patients were stratified by pulmonary congestion level: low (0–2 B‐lines), medium (3–7 B‐lines), and elevated (≥8 B‐lines). [19]

Follow‐up Visit

All alive participants were invited for a follow‐up assessment within 3 months following discharge, during which repeat TTE, LUS, ECG, blood pressure evaluation, and blood collection were performed. Changes in imaging from baseline admission to follow‐up were assessed. All follow‐up echocardiography examinations were performed using Vivid 9 4D Ultrasound Systems (GE Healthcare, Horten, Norway).

Outcome

The main outcome was a composite of readmission with AHF or all‐cause mortality within 180 days of enrollment. Rehospitalization with AHF was defined as readmission with symptoms and clinical signs consistent with AHF (the same definition used for eligibility screening upon inclusion) and an admission diagnosis of AHF adjudicated by the treating physician. Outcome data were obtained by manual medical record review.

Statistical Analysis

The study population was stratified into three groups according to the NYHA class at admission: NYHA class I/II, NYHA class III, and NYHA class IV. Baseline characteristics were presented as mean with standard deviation (SD) for Gaussian distributed continuous variables, median with interquartile range (IQR) for non‐Gaussian continuous variables, and counts with percentages for categorical variables, respectively. Gaussian distributed continuous variables were compared using ANOVA and linear‐trend ANOVA. Non‐Gaussian continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal‐Wallis test and the Jonckheere‐Terpstra test. Categorial variables were compared using the Cochran‐Armitage trend test.

The association between NYHA class and the composite outcome was assessed using Kaplan‐Meier time to event survival analyses and differences between the groups were evaluated using the log‐rank test. Patients in NYHA classes were furthermore stratified according to their N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) level at baseline. Associations between baseline clinical data and the 180‐day composite outcome were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression. To formally assess whether the prognostic impact of natriuretic peptides varied significantly across different levels of baseline symptom severity, a multiplicative interaction term between NYHA class and log‐transformed NT‐proBNP (NYHA_grouped * log(proBNP_numeric)) was included in the regression models. Multivariable adjustment was conducted across three distinct tiers: Model 1 evaluated univariable associations; Model 2 adjusted for baseline clinical covariates (sex, age, BMI, diabetes, eGFR, atrial fibrillation, dyslipidemia, and NT‐proBNP); and Model 3 further adjusted for markers of cardiac function and congestion (LVEF and B‐line burden). Model discriminative capacity was quantified using Harrell's Concordance index (C‐statistic).

For survival analyses stratified by NT‐proBNP level, patients were divided using the cohort‐specific median NT‐proBNP concentration in an exploratory analysis, rather than a validated clinical decision threshold.

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio version 4.4.3 (Posit Software, PBC).

Results

A total of 564 (3 were excluded due to missing NYHA class) patients were included (78.2 years ± 11.6; 56.4% male), of whom 263 (46%) were admitted due to acute decompensation of chronic heart failure (ADCHF) and 297 (53%) due to de novo HF. Baseline characteristics, clinical, and biochemical data are summarized in Table 1. Patients were enrolled after a median of 2 days (IQR: 1–3) after admission and hospitalized for a median of 4 days (IQR 2–7). At admission, 128 patients (23%) were classified as NYHA class I/II, 151 (27%) as NYHA class III, and 285 (51%) as NYHA class IV. Patients in higher NYHA classes tended to be older, were more likely to have diabetes, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, prior cerebrovascular events, and prior admissions with AHF, as seen in Table 1. Of the 227 (40%) who had a prior AHF admission, 44 belonged to NYHA class I/II, 54 were NYHA class III, and 134 were NYHA class IV (p for trend = 0.021), corresponding to 34%, 36%, and 45% of their respective NYHA class totals.

Echocardiography and LUS Findings

Trends for echocardiographic and LUS measures by NYHA classes are presented in Table 2. On echocardiography, patients with higher NYHA class had lower LVEF (NYHA class I/II: 39.0%, NYHA class III: 39.2%, and NYHA class IV: 35.7%) and lower global longitudinal strain (GLS) (NYHA class I/II: 11.06%, NYHA class III: 10.23%, and NYHA class IV: 9.41%, p for trend < 0.001) compared with those with lower NYHA classes. Higher NYHA class was associated with higher left atrial volume index (LAVi) (NYHA I/II: 16.0 mL/m2, NYHA III: 39.18 mL/m2, and NYHA IV: 40.98 mL/m2, p for trend = 0.023) and higher E/e’ (NYHA I/II: 14.95, NYHA III: 17.20, and NYHA IV: 17.40, p for trend < 0.001). All left atrial strain parameters decreased across NYHA classes (Table 2) whereas right ventricular function assessed by tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) did not differ across NYHA classes.

Follow‐up Echocardiographic Findings

Of the 564 enrolled patients, 160 (28.2%) attended follow‐up examinations (median 49 days after discharge [IQR 38–63 days]). A total of 184 patients had died before invitation for follow‐up examination. Patients who did not attend follow‐up were older, had more comorbidities, and showed more severe disease markers than those who attended (Table 3). Echocardiographic findings from the follow‐up assessment are summarized in Table 4. Of the 160 patients who attended follow‐up, 45 (28.3%) were classified as NYHA class I/II, 43 (27.0%) were NYHA class III, and 72 (45.3%) were NYHA class IV from the baseline examination. LV systolic function and LAVi at follow‐up were comparable across NYHA classes at baseline (Table 4). Meanwhile, left atrial strain measures differed at follow‐up according to baseline NYHA‐class, such that higher NYHA class at baseline was associated with lower PALS (20.2% vs.13.9% vs. 14.4%, p for trend = 0.026) and PACS at follow‐up (13.5% vs. 8.3%, p for trend = 0.007). Furthermore, while longitudinal right ventricular function did not differ (TAPSE, p = 0.26), global right ventricular function showed persistent impairment across worsening baseline NYHA classes (RV FAC: 46.2% vs 44.4% vs. 41.9%, p for trend = 0.040). LUS measurements at follow‐up displayed no significant difference.

Survival Analysis

Within the follow‐up period of 180 days, 185 (32.8%) met the composite outcome, of whom 97 patients (17.1%) had died, and 88 (15.5%) had at least one HF readmission. Mortality and readmission rates did not differ significantly across NYHA classes at baseline (Figure 1). We found no significant difference in the composite outcome of all‐cause mortality or HF readmission within 180 days across the NYHA classes (log‐rank p = 0.70) (Figure 1).

Kaplan‐Meier curve depicting the difference in outcome across NYHA classes within 180 days of enrollment.

Unadjusted Kaplan‐Meier survival estimates are shown for the different NYHA classes. The shaded areas represent 95% confidence interval. The number of patients at risk is displayed below the x‐axis.

A survival analysis was conducted to evaluate the prognostic impact of NYHA class, log‐transformed proBNP, and their interaction. Among patients in baseline NYHA classes I/II, a one‐unit increase in log(proBNP) was significantly associated with an increased hazard of the primary outcome (HR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.36–2.69, p < 0.001). While the baseline hazard was higher for advanced NYHA classes, a significant negative interaction was observed between NYHA class IV and log(proBNP) (Interaction HR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.44–0.97, p = 0.035).

Patients in NYHA classes I/II with NT‐proBNP above the cohort median (>575 pmol/L) were associated with a significantly higher risk of death/readmission at 180 days compared with levels ≤575 pmol/L (log‐rank p <0.001) (Figure 2). For NYHA class III patients, survival curves for NT‐proBNP groups overlapped substantially and no significant difference in outcomes was observed (log‐rank p = 0.32) (Figure 2). Among patients in NYHA class IV, NT‐proBNP > 575 pmol/L was again associated with worse outcomes, with significantly lower event‐free survival compared with patients with lower baseline NT‐proBNP (log‐rank p = 0.023) (Figure 2). Similarly, when stratifying by NYHA class, NT‐proBNP above the cohort median and B‐lines ≥ 8, significantly different event rates are observed for patients in NYHA class I/II and in NYHA class IV patients (Figure S1).

AHF vs. ADCHF

In a sensitivity analysis stratified by admission type, patients with de novo AHF (n = 297) and those with ADCHF (n = 263) showed broadly similar patterns of worsening cardiac structure and function with increasing NYHA class. Formal testing for a NYHA class × admission‐type interaction did not reach statistical significance for LVEF, GLS, left atrial volume index, E/e', or total B‐line count (all p ≥0.18), indicating that the association between NYHA class and imaging findings did not differ significantly between the two subgroups.

Discussion

In this prospective two‐center observational cohort study of 564 patients with AHF, higher NYHA class at baseline was associated with greater comorbidity burden, worse cardiac dysfunction, and more severe pulmonary congestion on LUS. Patients in higher NYHA classes more frequently presented with clinical congestion and peripheral edema, compared to lower classes. Comorbidities increased stepwise with higher NYHA class, consistent with prior reports. [11, 20] Echocardiography reflected increasing atrial remodeling and functional impairment (LAVi, LACS, and PALS) with worsening NYHA class. Measures of left ventricular filling pressure likewise showed consistent worsening with increasing NYHA class. LV systolic dysfunction (LVEF and GLS) was more prominent in higher NYHA classes.

NYHA classification remains an integrated component of therapeutic decision‐making and risk stratification in both clinical practice and trials. It is widely incorporated into international HF guidelines and used to determine eligibility for pharmacological therapies. Its ease of application makes it a practical and accessible tool for assessing functional symptom burden. Despite its inherent subjectivity and interobserver variability, NYHA class remains a useful indicator of functional status in AHF patients. [6, 21, 22] While other studies have found that higher NYHA class is associated with a higher rate of mortality/readmission, this association was not observed in our cohort, despite our findings of worse cardiac dysfunction with higher NYHA class. [12, 23, 24] Thus, our results do not refute the established prognostic role of NYHA class, but suggest that its prognostic discrimination was limited in this specific study population. This discrepancy may be explained by several factors. First, our study population was heterogeneous, encompassing patients with both de novo AHF and ADCHF across all heart failure phenotypes. Such diversity in patient characteristics may have attenuated class‐based differences in outcomes. Second, previous research has demonstrated substantial interobserver variability in NYHA classification, which may have contributed to misclassification and reduced discriminatory power. [25, 26] Finally, as our study was conducted in a contemporary cohort during a period of highly optimized heart failure management, the improved quality of care and medical therapy may have mitigated outcome differences traditionally observed between NYHA classes. Functional NYHA classification being a subjective tool may not fully capture disease severity and objective measures improve risk stratification of HF patients.

As previous studies have demonstrated, NT‐proBNP levels in these patients adds prognostic value. [27] Patients in NYHA class I/II and in NYHA class IV with NT‐proBNP levels above the median had significantly higher event rates. In NYHA class I/II patients, elevated NT‐proBNP identifies individuals with more advanced cardiac dysfunction or residual hemodynamic congestion not reflected by functional capacity alone thereby enhancing discrimination. High NT‐proBNP levels in NYHA class IV patients may reflect advanced decompensation. This significant interaction indicates that the relative prognostic impact of elevated proBNP is attenuated in patients with NYHA class IV compared to those in classes I/II. Although statistically significant, this remains modest discrimination by conventional standards, and this improvement should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating.

Patients in NYHA class III had similar event rates regardless of NT‐proBNP levels. Given the smaller size of this subgroup (n = 151) relative to NYHA class IV, this finding may reflect limited statistical power to detect an interaction effect rather than a biologically distinct or attenuated response to NT‐proBNP.

At follow‐up examination, the marked differences in cardiac structure and function observed across NYHA classes at baseline were no longer apparent among the subset of patients who attended follow‐up. This finding should be interpreted with caution as the most severely affected NYHA class IV patients were disproportionately lost to follow‐up because of death or inability to return for reassessment. Among the surviving patients who returned for follow‐up, echocardiographic profiles were similar, irrespective of baseline NYHA class. This normalization may be attributed to effective medical titration and recovery after the acute decompensation phase. However, as we observed the most severely ill NYHA IV patients were underrepresented at follow‐up due to mortality or due to their more advanced disease symptomatology rendering them unable to return for reassessment, which may have contributed to the attenuation of group differences. The uniform normalization of LAVI implies successful widespread mechanical decongestion. However, the persistent, stepwise depression of PALS and PACS alongside RV FAC indicates that advanced functional burden leaves a lasting imprint of subclinical impairment. This impairment persists even though the volume has returned to normal, as assessed by LAVi. This could be an early indicator of atrial cardiomyopathy among these patients.

Our findings are consistent with those of Lee et al. who reported that baseline pulmonary congestion on LUS was associated with higher NYHA class in patients hospitalized with AHF. Their study was a prospective two‐center study where four‐zone LUS was performed on patients hospitalized with AHF. In total, they enrolled 322 patients. [28] In contrast, the present study included 564 patients and employed an 8‐zone LUS assessment, allowing for a more comprehensive evaluation of pulmonary congestion. A meta‐analysis conducted by Caraballo C. et al. concluded that NYHA classification poorly stratified HF patients according to their functional impairment. [21] In the randomized controlled LUS‐HF trial, diuretic therapy was individualized based on LUS‐assessed pulmonary congestion. The study demonstrated that LUS‐guided management significantly reduced the risk of heart failure‐related rehospitalization after discharge compared with standard care. [29] Performing LUS in patients with AHF may help distinguish those with severe pulmonary congestion from those with less severe pulmonary congestion, thereby improving risk stratification and guiding more individualized therapeutic decisions and thereby potentially improve post‐discharge outcomes.

This study's findings demonstrate that, among patients hospitalized with AHF, NYHA class at admission reflects significant differences in underlying admission etiology as well as cardiac structure and function and pulmonary congestion, differences that are not fully captured by symptoms alone. These results highlight the clinical utility of supplementing functional classification with objective assessment of congestion. Such integration could help clinicians better identify patients with subclinical congestion or residual hemodynamic compromise, even in those with similar NYHA class. In practice, this could support more individualized diuretic titration, closer post‐discharge monitoring, and optimized resource allocation for high‐risk patients. Future clinical pathways may benefit from integrating a multimodal approach combining NYHA class, echocardiographic parameters, and LUS‐derived measures of pulmonary congestion.

Study Limitations

The study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The study population was heterogeneous, including a wide range of patients with both de novo AHF as well as ADCHF across all HF phenotypes. NYHA classification is inherently subjective and prone to interobserver variability, which may have led to misclassification bias. However, the distribution of NYHA classes in our cohort, particularly the high proportion of patients in NYHA IV, is broadly consistent with other AHF studies. [9, 10] Given the observational nature of the study, our findings are associative and do not establish definitive causation. Despite following a standardized imaging protocol, examinations were performed bedside in acutely ill patients, often yielding suboptimal imaging and missing data, which might have introduced selection bias when analyzing the images. Examinations were performed a median of two days after first hospital assessment in the emergency department. By this time, many patients had already initiated decongestive treatments, for example, diuretics, which may have altered the acute measures. A proportion of patients were classified as NYHA class I/II and lacked peripheral edema at time of study assessment. This may raise questions regarding the indication for their index admission and whether they truly represented AHF cases. However, this reflects the timing of evaluation rather than the absence of preceding congestion. Clinical assessment and imaging were performed after transfer to the cardiology department, after a median of 2 days from admission. By this time, most patients had received decongestive therapy. Decongestive therapy has shown to reduce peripheral edema substantially within the first few days of treatment. [30] Consequently, NYHA class and peripheral edema status at inclusion do not necessarily reflect the clinical severity of these patients, but rather the response to early in‐hospital‐treatment. Importantly, biomarker data support that these patients represented a decompensated population. The observed NT‐proBNP concentrations in NYHA class I/II patients were not low. The median was 359 pmol/L corresponding to approximately 3,000 pg/mL, which is consistent with values commonly described in other AHF cohorts. [31]

Given the distribution of LVEF in this cohort, we did not perform a formal subgroup analysis of the relationship between NYHA class, congestion, and prognosis stratified by HF phenotype, as further division of the HFpEF group in particular across three NYHA classes would yield insufficient numbers per stratum for reliable estimation. These findings may therefore have limited generalizability to patients with HFpEF, and future studies in larger, phenotype‐enriched cohorts are warranted to determine whether the relationship between NYHA class and cardiac imaging findings differs across HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF.

Additionally, the number of covariates included in the multivariable Cox models was limited. As patients were already stratified into three NYHA groups and some variables contained missing data, adjustment for additional factors resulted in a reduction in the number of events available for analysis. This increases the risk of model overfitting and reduces statistical power. Therefore, covariate selection was restricted to clinically relevant variables to balance confounding control with model robustness.

Furthermore, stratification by NT‐proBNP was performed using a cohort‐derived median value rather than an externally validated clinical threshold, which may limit the generalizability of these exploratory findings to other populations or clinical settings.

Lastly, the availability of echocardiographic and LUS data may have been influenced by patient‐related factors and image quality. Patients with more severe dyspnea may have had greater difficulty completing a comprehensive echocardiographic examination, while thoracic pain or tenderness and obesity may have limited the acquisition of images of sufficient quality for analysis. Consequently, variable‐specific missingness may have introduced selection bias, particularly among patients with more severe symptoms

Conclusion

In this prospective two‐center observational study of patients hospitalized with AHF, higher NYHA class at admission was associated with worse cardiac dysfunction and greater B‐line burden on LUS, as well as a higher burden of comorbidities. Among the subset of patients who survived and returned for follow‐up, differences in cardiac structure and function according to baseline NYHA class were no longer apparent. NYHA class alone did not significantly correlate with the composite outcome of mortality and readmission within 180 days. NT‐proBNP added prognostic value to the models.
