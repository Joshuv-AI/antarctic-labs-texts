Infection prevention and control in the CT suite: a qualitative study with Australian radiographers in intravenous contrast administration
Authors: Dania Abu Awwad, Suzanne Hill, Sarah Lewis, Yobelli Jimenez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWithin radiology departments, computed tomography (CT) has been identified as presenting a higher risk of infection compared to other imaging modalities. CT scanning often deploys contrast injectors to administer iodine contrast intravenously, which poses infection risks for patients and staff. The aim of this study was to explore the variations in practice and the enablers and barriers to infection prevention and control (IPC) practices in the CT suite in Australia.MethodsSemi-structured focus group discussions (FGD) were conducted over three sessions with thirteen registered radiographers, who had expressed interest in participating in FGDs after completing a survey on IPC knowledge and practice. The FGDs ran for one hour and questions focused on workplace surveillance, IPC education, risks associated with different components of contrast injectors, and variations between staff. FGDs were transcribed verbatim and coded using thematic analysis to identify key themes and concepts.ResultsThree key themes arose from the focus group discussions: ‘Communication and Education in Radiology’, ‘Safety in CT’, and ‘Injection Technology’. Participants described general IPC modules, most of which did not relate to the CT environment. In hospital settings, IPC policies were often considered broad, and participants described having no written policies relating to IPC and the CT contrast injector. IPC safety was impacted by other staff members, particularly staff from other departments who assist with connecting the CT injector but were often not familiar with CT equipment. CT injectors have connection points that must remain sterile and poor adherence to IPC practices will increase the risk of infections. The number of connection points are less in multi-use injectors compared to single-use injectors, hence, there was a perceived advantage with multi-use injectors. However, single-use injectors were used for infectious patients if available for easier cleaning.ConclusionThe lack of CT-specific IPC training and policies led to variations in practice between staff in the CT suite. Training and resources focused on radiology settings and scenarios are needed and would benefit both radiology staff and other wards that require radiology services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13728-z.Keywords: Infection control, Computed tomography, Radiographers, Contrast injectors, Radiology department

Background

Hospital acquired infections (HAI) are preventable common incidents in healthcare settings [1, 2]. Standard precautions must be applied as a minimum by healthcare workers (HCWs) to all patients to help reduce the risk of infections to both patients and staff [2]. Furthermore, needles, administration sets, tubing and connectors all hold an infection risk to patients and HCWs if not handled correctly [2]. A scoping review found that, within radiology departments, computed tomography (CT) imaging had the highest incidence of infection-related adverse outcomes for patients compared to other imaging modalities such as MRI [3]. Contaminated surfaces can include radiographic hardware such as gantries, X-ray tubes, operating consoles, and contrast injectors as these are shared equipment in healthcare settings,2 and CT scanning regularly uses intravenous (IV) access to administer iodine contrast using single-use or multi-use contrast injectors which carries risk of cross contamination [4].

A recent national survey on Australian CT radiographers and radiology nurses reported that participants had high knowledge of correct standard precaution practices in CT, but had lower practice scores [5, 6]. Part of the survey included an open-ended survey question on increased risk, and radiographers revealed that emergency imaging situations, complications during procedures such as patient bleeding at the IV site, and workload pressures were scenarios where they would likely not adhere to correct IPC practices [7]. A recent scoping review reported that most studies on IPC in radiology departments were single site surveys, consisted of microbial analysis related to a single adverse event, and were simulation or observation studies of one type of IPC practice [3]. There was a lack of qualitative studies and understanding of when and why correct IPC practices were not always adhered to as well as a paucity of research into recent technological changes such as multi-dose injectors [3].

Previous qualitative studies with nurses have explored facilitators and barriers to appropriate adherence to IPC practices, and as a result, solutions specific to ward nurses have been determined [8–10]. The aim of this current study is to explore in-depth the facilitators and barriers to correct IPC practices in the CT suite in Australia. The study also aims to explore variations in practices between radiographers and workplaces and understand how radiographers determine best practice in IPC related to contrast injection. The study focuses on common daily CT tasks, particularly around the IPC risks, contrast injectors, and intravenous contrast administration.

Methods

In 2022, radiographers were invited to complete a nation-wide survey on their knowledge, attitudes, and practice of IPC in CT [5, 6]. At the end of the survey, radiographers were invited to participate in a focus group discussion (FGD) and 21 radiographers volunteered for this research extension and listed their emails as contact points. Potential participants were then contacted by a member of the research team to attend a FGD and thirteen radiographers attended over three sessions. The remaining radiographers were either not available during the study period or did not respond. Participants were provided information on the study prior to attending a FGD and provided their consent to attend via email. Ethics approval was granted by the University of Sydney’s Human Research Ethics Committee for this study (Project number: 2022/493).

The FGDs were conducted via a videoconferencing platform over a two-week period in April and May 2023, allowing participants to join from any geographic location. Three sessions were organised based on participants’ availabilities and there were three, four, and six participants in each session respectively. A member of the research team hosted the discussion and was supported by a second researcher, asking questions or occasionally prompting participants for further detail. All members of the research team are registered imaging professionals and are able to understand the contexts shared by participants. The discussions were recorded, and after the FGDs, the audio data were transcribed verbatim by a researcher (DA) and checked by another (SH). The sessions were semi-structured and ran for one hour, allowing participants to comment on each other’s workplace training and habits. The questions were based on the themes that arose from the open-ended questions on an IPC survey for CT staff [7]. This included questions on workplace surveillance, IPC education, IPC breaches, risks associated with different components of contrast injectors, and variations between staff. The barriers and facilitators to good IPC practices were explored and the interview guide is included in Appendix 1.

During the data analysis process, participants were randomly assigned a code ranging from Participant 1 to Participant 13 and any identifying information that was provided, such as workplace names or brands was de-identified. After the three FGD were held, it was determined that there were no new themes arising between participants and groups, theoretical saturation had been reached, and no additional recruitment was necessary.

NVivo version 12 (QSR International, Melbourne, Australia) was used to code the transcripts, and the data was analysed using thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke [11]. This was achieved through authors’ individually (DA & YJ) undertaking iterative steps of data analysis, via data familiarisation, arranging data into codes and grouping the codes into content analysis and then themes. Coding and content analysis was also used to systematically examine and categorise the thematic content emerging from the transcribed data to determine common patterns and observations. Ongoing group discussions were used to debate discrepancies in codes and themes. Sub-themes were then compared and assessed for overarching themes and key concepts by all authors, ensuring that the data was reliable and consistent, prior to defining and naming them.

Results

Thirteen radiographers participated in the FGDs. Ten participants worked in public hospitals, one worked in a private hospital (Participant 5), and two worked in private practices (Participant 7 and Participant 13). The participants were from New South Wales (NSW) (n = 9; 6 regional/metro workplaces; 3 rural workplaces) and Queensland (n = 4; regional/metro). Only two participants from the same workplace and these two participants did not attend the same FGD. Participants had a variety of experience, including one chief radiographer, two CT section chiefs participating, and four recently CT-trained radiographers.

Overall, participant responses were categorised into three main themes of ‘Communication and Education in Radiology’, ‘Safety in CT’ and ‘Injection Technology’ (Fig. 1).

IPC education and communication in radiology

Participants discussed available policies at different levels within their department, such as the requirements they had to meet for health guidelines in their respective states. Radiographers named overarching state health guidelines, such as the Department of Health for NSW and standard Queensland Health guidelines, and this covered correct hand hygiene practices, precautions for different infection types, and fluid and IV connections. Updates to IPC policy were generally conveyed via the intranet or shared to department seniors to then cascade to relevant staff.

There were variations between participants regarding their CT-specific policies, primarily related to the contrast injector, contrast administration or interventional procedures like drainages and biopsies. Participants referenced broad hospital guidelines that were accessible online and then adapted this information to the CT environment. Radiographers reported that training on the safe use of contrast injectors and IV contrast administration rarely happened outside normal orientation for a new workplace. Participants said their workplaces would train students or new graduates but not already qualified new staff despite different injectors having different operating functions, and radiographers were expected to apply their current knowledge to different circumstances, leading to different behaviours and variations seen in radiology regarding IPC. Simultaneously, IPC for CT procedures was usually taught through in-house training and collegial sharing without written policy. Radiology colleagues were a common source of information, and multiple participants mentioned having informational posters regarding multiple infectious diseases. Participant 6 explained that known infectious patients would be scheduled for the end of the day whenever possible because of the cleaning requirements with the scanner, in which the CT scanner could not be used for an hour period afterwards. This mostly pertained to patients with coronavirus (known now through this paper as Covid-19). Departments that had multiple CT scanners also attempted to dedicate one scanner to infectious patients.

Participant 11: “For general infections, we do have some guidelines that are up on the wall about the different infections the patients come in with and what specifically to do regarding those infections.”

Participant 10: “During the height of Covid we had two [CT scanners], and so we would prioritize using one scanner for our Covid patients and the other scanner for other patients. … And then that also meant that we often were much more diligent with cleaning that scanner because we’d known what patients were in prior, and therefore what kind of cleaning was required. However, moving forward into kind of when Covid was no longer at its height, and we had to start using the scanners for other patients as well, and then that’s when the grey areas began. And that’s when corners were cut.”

Participants explained that they felt a greater sense of workplace and personal IPC action and education when Covid-19 became the dominant infection issue. However, IPC policies would frequently change in relation to Covid-19 precautions and processes, and that resulted in different practices among staff.

Participant 1: “The reaction to Covid changed the whole focus of infection control in the whole hospital. There were signs up … and there were spot checks in departments. So, nurses would walk around with a clipboard and sort of quietly look and observe the radiographers and sort of mark down what they’re doing.”

Participant 10: “As Covid was evolving and throughout the months, the policies kept changing. Hospital approaches kept changing, and it made it really hard to address our Covid patients in the correct manner. A lot of radiographers were confused with what the up-to-date processes were, because it was information overload and not everyone was up to date. There was no clear single source that they could refer to, be that a poster on the wall that was updated as per policy changes. So that would have been really helpful.”

Safety in CT

In terms of safety, participants described inter-departmental challenges such as patients being brought in from the wards or via the emergency department before radiographers could clean the room. This was an issue from emergency department staff because of the expected 4-hour turnaround time or Covid-19 patients that needed to be scanned earlier than scheduled, with minimal consultation with radiology staff. Another major issue of concern raised by participants was when radiology staff were not informed about a patient’s infectious status until after the scan was completed which impeded radiographers from actioning transmission-based precautions.

Participants reflected on the IPC practices of colleagues and could recall instances of poor IPC practices among various HCWs, including nurses, doctors, technical assistants, and fellow radiographers. The participants also acknowledged that there are many points of connections in CT injectors, and each pose an infection risk to the patient, and it was suggested that it was easy for radiographers to miss a step or ‘cut corners’. This was especially the case when the department was busy, there was a high workload, or increased workplace pressures. Variations in practice between occupational groups

Participants described going to their team leader, Nursing Unit Manager (NUM), Chief Radiographer, or colleagues for IPC questions. However, multiple participants explained that radiographers may have had different in-house training and experiences in CT and as a result, they have observed behaviours from different radiographers that are different from their own practice. This was also extended to nurses and doctors, who often would accompany ward patients to the radiology department for scans. Overall, variations were expressed as either from one practitioner to another or at the departmental level where radiographers varied from nurses.

Participant 7: “I think their practice was probably not the same as what I would have given to the patient.”

Participant 3: “I find where I work, there’s some practices that I myself do that I know other people don’t. And like I might see people doing something that I feel might be room to introduce risk. We’ve got people that have been trained at all different sites that come and work here over the years, and only recently starting to sort of get formalised policies and procedures in place, I guess. So, there’s always going to be different behaviours that people have.”

Professional roles and the variations in practices due to the presence of nurses in the CT department was evident. Nurses typically cannulated, set up the contrast injectors, and connected tubes to patients’ cannulas in public hospitals. After business hours, radiographers often resumed these roles, however, participants noted that not all sites allowed radiographers to cannulate. Participants believed that doctors tended to know the least about IPC in radiology and their involvement was limited to what radiographers were not permitted to do in CT, such as connecting contrast injectors to peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) and central venous catheters (CVCs). Radiography students were described as being apprehensive when setting up contrast injectors, but capable students were more likely to be allowed to have greater involvement in CT. However, some sites did not allow students to load up the injector. Regardless, participant 13 clarified that “whoever is connecting the injector to the patient always, always, always checks that it’s been primed”.

Participant 4: “Some sites nurses control it all. And I have worked at sites where nurses are in charge of the cannulation and loading the injector, and things like that. And then one of the last places I went to, halfway through the term, the nurses let go of that, and let the radiographers do it. … I think that opened up a lot of questions for the radiographers because they hadn’t been doing it for such a long time, because it was always the nurses. So it was up to them [nurses] to come in and go through things with us and let us know what their experience told us we should be doing.”

Injection technology

Participants were asked about their perceptions of safety and use of emerging contrast injector technologies in CT. Overall, single-use injectors were considered easier to clean but more likely to result in an IPC breach due to constant refilling and longer set-up between patients. Multiple participants from workplaces that had both multi-use and single-use injectors remarked that they would use the single-use injectors for Covid-19 patients as cleaning the components of the single-use injectors was simpler, and it was perceived as safer to discard all bottles and tubing used. A couple of participants mentioned covering the CT injector with plastic sleeves to keep the injector clean. Some participants explained that it was still safe to use the multi-use injector without having to change all the components and tubing of the system except what would normally be changed between patients.

Multiple participants described poorer IPC practices when new injectors were installed, particularly multi-use injectors, and this was due to the different workflow compared to single-use injectors and the difficulties adapting to new IPC processes. Safer IPC practice was equated with more experience as a CT radiographer, however, participants also commented that very experienced radiographers can become indifferent to maintaining proper IPC practices.

Participant 6: “A lot of radiographers are quite blasé about infection control with injecting, because there’s lots of points of contact … The more experienced ones just do it and not really care and a bit blasé. So there could be issues around that.”

Table 2 presents FGD points on multi-use and single-use injectors. In general, the perceived risk for both systems were deemed to be related to loading contrast from bottles and connecting the injector to the patient’s cannula. If an error was made or if the tubing needed to be reconnected to the patient, participants explained that the single use system can be thrown out and a new package opened and set up. For the multi-use system, participants expressed that as long as only the external connection tube was affected, the tube could be changed while the remaining injector set-up and day set remained in place. There were variations in the use of gloves or alcohol rub when setting up the CT injector:

Participant 9: “I was just trained when getting trained in CT to wear gloves. Hand hygiene and then wear gloves.”

Participant 10: “We also wear gloves and are encouraged to wear gloves, but they’re not sterile gloves. They’re the purple gloves that you use your normal hands to pick out. So, if you’re touching outside of the gloves, they’re not really sterile anymore. So it defeats the point fundamentally of the whole process, so it’s more so to look like you’re making an effort, I think.”

Participant 12: “When we had our multi-use injector installed, a rep came out, and they actually said that you just need to like use a spray, or like a alcohol rub between each motion of changing the bottle, and then the tubing. And there wasn’t actually a need for gloves and then it was also brought up that we aren’t using sterile gloves. We’re just using gloves that we’ve touched with our normal hands anyway. But the emphasis was on, we should be using [alcohol rub brand name] before we touch the glove. But yeah, it’s not a rule where we have to use gloves for using the injector, but it is highly advised that we use alcohol rub in between all the steps.”

Multi-use and single-use contrast injectors

Infection Risk

- Less opportunity for infection spread with multi-use injectors because of less connection points and set-up between patients.

- Contrast bottles are changed less frequently due to the use of larger bottles such as 500 ml contrast bottles.

- Single use injectors have more avenues where infection control can be breached per set-up.

- Single use injectors are easier to clean when used for infectious patients.

Participant 6: “There are so many points of potential infection control issues and contact when you’re filling a single barrel injector via a hockey stick or via a spike. Whereas you’ve got a multi-dose system, if it’s set up correctly to start off with, the points of infection control contact are actually a lot less. And it’s actually safer for the patient.”

Participant 12: “We got ours during the height of Covid, and we decided to use the single use injector for Covid patients just because it’s just easier to throw something away, especially if the patient was aerosolizing., I think that in terms of infection control, or just for Covid patients in particular, the single is better.”

Participant 6: “If we do an infectious patient, we don’t change the lines. We just wipe the machine down and stuff like that and keep the day set, but of course, change the patient line per infectious patient.”

Waste

- Participants discussed the reduced waste associated with multi-use injectors as less syringes and plastic packaging is required. However, if mistakes were made, there was a bigger associated cost to throw out a day set, and contrast compared to the single-use set-up.

Multiple participants admitted that contrast had been wasted with multi-use injectors when the day set needed to be discarded at the end of the day. Participant 5 explained that when they worked in a public hospital, the contrast bottles would be removed, the day set replaced, and the bottles placed back. They expressed concern over having the bottles exposed but that was the only perceived downside with the multi-use injector. Other contrast waste was mentioned for injections and procedures in CT when 5 ml of contrast might be required. Participant 13 explained that only a sterile needle would be used to draw out contrast and the rest of the contrast would be used for scans requiring an IV injection. The bottle would be wiped with an alcohol swab before being opened again.

Sterile considerations

Participants were asked about their knowledge and practices regarding components of the injectors that were sterile and how they ensured best IPC practice. The most common responses from the participants were the ‘tube ends’ and the ‘injector spikes’. Participants explained that any points of connection between the injector components and the patient should be sterile and essentially, the internal aspects of the tubes are sterile, along with the syringe tips and internal components of the cannula bungs. Simultaneously, participants acknowledged that “the products we use is sterile until opened. And it’s opened, we go to use them. We’re making things as clean as we possibly can, but they’re not technically sterile. If someone comes in with a cannula, I’m going clean that before I use it. But no way it can be called sterile because that’s been in them before they’ve come in the room” (Participant 4).

Participant 9: “Any point of contacts where the contrast could come exposed to air or where you could touch it when connecting lines- that has to be sterile.”

Participant 3: “I think the important thing is to just be aware of what you’re touching and know that once you touch something that that’s no longer sterile. And if that’s something that’s going to come into contact with the contrast or the tip of something that’s going to be joining something else that’s sterile, then you’ve compromised your sterile area. I think awareness the whole time of where you’re placing things, what you’re touching, is important.”

Discussion

This study explored IPC policies, barriers, and facilitators in radiology departments via FGDs with CT radiographers. Most participants reported that they were not aware of any written infection control policies specific to CT or radiology in their workplace. This finding is not unexpected, as there has been a reported lack of IPC protocols in radiology departments [12]. Participants from public hospital settings agreed they had guidelines but acknowledged that guidelines were broadly directed to the entire hospital environment. This is similar to the Australian guidelines for the prevention and control of infection in healthcare, which are generic guidelines, and radiology is only specifically mentioned in terms of scheduling infectious patients [1]. Of note, study participants were aware of the guidelines around scheduling infectious patients and acted upon them as part of their department policy despite it not being part of their written policy.

Although the recent pandemic related to Covid-19 was not a line of questioning in the FGDs, it was raised multiple times by participants as it was considered to impact CT practice on multiple levels. Recent literature on IPC in radiology departments have also focused on Covid-19 because of its pivotal role in raising awareness and compliance of IPC in radiology, especially CT which is important for respiratory imaging [3]. This aligns with participants’ experiences, with a clear increase in IPC messaging and practices with the rise of Covid-19 cases and the severity of its impact on Australians. FGD data revealed it was common for radiology departments to dedicate a specific scanner and contrast injector for infectious patients. IPC reminders in the form of posters and notifications via staff intranet sites were used in participants’ workplaces. This included updates on Covid-19 and is similar to a study among emergency nurses and doctors where workplace websites were the commonly accessed source for Covid-19 information [13]. Colleagues were also considered a common source of IPC information in our study, and this finding aligns well with studies on other disciplines [10, 13, 14], including that almost two-thirds of emergency doctors sought information from colleagues about Covid-19 [13]. The FGD further triangulates a recent national survey of Australian radiographers by our group, reporting that radiographers would seek information from colleagues on IPC resources, contrast injectors, and CT contrast administration [15].

All participants could describe recent IPC training, which was largely delivered via online modules. IPC modules undertaken by participants in public hospitals were described as broad and sometimes irrelevant, as they often covered scenarios not applicable to radiology departments. However, IPC modules undertaken by private practice radiographers were described to be more specific to radiology and CT overall. The disparity in training between public and private practice could be due to many reasons, including the presence or absence of dedicated IPC departments that need to target all wards and professions simultaneously, the removal of the need to report infection status in private settings, or the dedicated training programs private practices create for their staff.

CT training itself was described as “hands on”, and IPC was often included as part of in-house training, yet it was often reserved for trainees, where qualified staff often had to rely on their early training and experience alone, even if they changed workplaces. Overall, different training and expectations of IPC in workplaces has led to different practices being observed between colleagues and the frequent perception that colleagues did not exhibit best practice, although there was no evidence that the participants challenged what they perceived to be unsafe IPC practices. Our study highlights that regular, compulsory training for all staff is important to foster a better IPC-conscious team as workplace culture and colleagues are known to be influential towards staff adherence to IPC protocols [10, 14]. In fact, each public health organisation is expected to provide IPC training to staff and keep record of staff education and competence, however, our participants did not refer to such records [2].

Hand hygiene was a common topic for IPC training in radiology [3]. The importance of hand hygiene was emphasised by participants prior to setting up contrast injectors, and participants discussed whether hand rub was sufficient or if gloves were necessary. Participants agreed that touching sterile or pierced skin with gloves was still problematic because gloves used are not sterile, and dirty or contaminated gloves can also spread infections. Some participants were expected to wear gloves when setting up the CT injector and others stated that hand rub was suitable instead. Whichever method is followed when handling the CT injector, it remains important for hand hygiene to be used in between contact with clean or contaminated surfaces. The variations in practice in this study concur with other reported variations in hand hygiene use among healthcare professionals and found that most respondents self-reported high adherence to hand hygiene but reported lower rates for their colleagues [16, 17].

Other breaches or incidents of increased infection risk mentioned by participants included breaches related to cannulas, compromising sterile components of the contrast injectors, and insufficient cleaning between patients. This was more common in instances of high workload or having urgent patients. An Australian qualitative study among hospital nurses also found that it was challenging for staff to uphold proper hand hygiene practices in emergency situations or if there were practical constraints that limit a person’s ability to leave a patient or situation to clean prior to commencing a procedure [7, 10]. There is high patient turnover in radiology, and high workloads were also reported to negatively impact IPC practices in other professions as time pressures make it difficult for staff to maintain the required cleaning standard all the time [15, 18].

Another key example of increased infection risk, discussed by our participants, related to using contrast bottles for multiple patients, exacerbated by the recent contrast shortage experienced in 2022 and the need to ensure contrast was not wasted. In response to the contrast shortage, the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Radiologists (RANZCR) released a statement recognising that single-use contrast bottles may be used for multiple patients if local approval at the institutional level was granted, and strict IPC measures were implemented. RANZCR further suggested using smaller sized bottles to reduce contrast waste with single-use injectors [19]. Multi-use injectors can use larger bottles for multiple scans and patients, and they have been found to significantly reduce contrast waste compared to single-use injectors [20] and this was commented on by the study participants. For both types of contrast injectors, participants recognised several key components of the CT injectors that need to remain sterile, and improper practice can increase the risk of infection to patients. Syringes and tubes are regularly refilled or replaced, and every use is associated with contamination risks [21, 22]. Participants considered multi-use injectors as safer than single-use injectors because they required less handling of contrast tubing connection points. This was explained by Buerke et al., [21] where multi-use injectors had the advantage of less manipulation, hence, it was more likely that the contrast injector content will remain free of contamination. However, participants explained that during training to use the multi-use injectors, mistakes were common and contrast wastage was high until radiographers gained more confidence using these systems.

Limitations

It is difficult to quantify the extent of patient complications from poor IPC practices in CT, but the Australian IPC guidelines in healthcare highlight areas of risk, which include any use of cannulas and tubing for intravenous access [1]. Only radiographers from NSW and Queensland participated. Additionally, most participants were from public hospitals and more insight from radiographers working in private institutions could reveal more variations in practice. However, multiple participants described previous workplaces that they have worked at and the radiographers from private institutions shared similar experiences to participants in public hospitals. The participants had a variety of experiences and were from various locations yet similar themes were brought up in all groups and data saturation had been reached. Future research should target student and graduate radiographers to benchmark known IPC CT knowledge before entering the workforce. Additionally, further areas of CT could be explored, such as CT-guided procedures (e.g. steroid injections or biopsies), or the administration of oral and rectal contrast.

Conclusion

Infection control practices in CT present with a variety of challenges including high throughput of patients, different staff involved in handling contrast injectors, and variations in training. The suggested barriers and solutions in this study are important considerations for any radiology department as there are shared challenges in both Australia and abroad. Multiple infection risks mentioned by study participants are unique to CT, yet IPC policies and practices are often verbally taught or written policies were too broad for radiology departments. IPC training was also considered to be generic, and IPC specific CT training was limited to trainees and usually performed by colleagues, leading to inconsistent practices among staff observed. The lack of policies specific to CT and radiology needs to be addressed with increased attention on regular modality-specific training for radiographers and support staff at each institution to ensure consistency in IPC practices, which can also improve compliance [23]. Education and training should be tailored to local equipment rather than having generic instructions [23]. This requires a multi-disciplinary approach between managers in radiology, CT, and IPC teams to ensure that the uniqueness of CT workplaces are reflected in any infection control guidelines while ensuring patient safety.
