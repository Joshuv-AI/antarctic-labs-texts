The Impact of Cystic Fibrosis‐Related Diabetes on Barriers to Self‐Management
Authors: Rebecca J Vitale, Christine Ford, Stephanie S Filigno, Liam Sweeney, Carolyn Snell, Melissa S Putman, Shipra Rai, Kristin A Riekert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCystic fibrosis (CF) is a complex disease requiring adherence to an intensive medical regimen to maintain health. For those who develop CF‐related diabetes (CFRD, 20% adolescents, 30%–50% adults), daily self‐management is additionally complex and burdensome. This study aimed to examine how managing CFRD influences self‐efficacy, adherence, mental health, and executive functioning for people with CF.MethodsUsing data collected in a cross‐sectional study addressing barriers to daily care, we examined relationships between self‐efficacy (CF‐Medication Beliefs Questionnaire), adherence measures (composite medication possession ratio), barriers to daily care (Daily Care Check‐in), psychological outcomes (Patient Health Questionnaire‐8 and General Anxiety Disorder Scale‐7), and executive function (Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function), comparing those with and without CFRD.ResultsOf 405 participants enrolled, 131 (33%) had CFRD. Treatment complexity was higher for those with CFRD (p < 0.001), but adherence, barriers to daily care, anxiety, depression, and executive function did not differ by CFRD status (p > 0.05 for all). People with CFRD had higher self‐efficacy (p = 0.030) and lower concerns about privacy (p = 0.015) than those without CFRD.ConclusionsDespite higher treatment complexity, people with CFRD had similar adherence and higher self‐efficacy than those without CFRD, suggesting that, in our sample, managing CFRD may uniquely impact self‐efficacy, supporting adherence by way of adaptive coping. No differences were seen in mental health or executive function variables. Future research is needed to further explore relationships between executive function, glycemic outcomes, and diabetes management in samples representative of the population with CF and CFRD.Keywords: adherence, cystic fibrosis, diabetes, executive function, self‐efficacy

Introduction

Management of cystic fibrosis (CF) is a complex and time‐consuming daily process that often takes upwards of 2 h of time each day prior to the availability of elexacaftor‐tezacaftor‐ivacaftor (ETI) therapy [1]. Adherence is often suboptimal; Quittner et al. found that the mean composite medication possession ratio (cMPR) for all pulmonary medications was 48% [2]. Experiencing more barriers to adherence (such as forgetting, time management, and oppositional behaviors) is associated with lower medication adherence [3, 4]. CF‐related diabetes (CFRD) is a common extrapulmonary complication in people with CF (PWCF), affecting up to 20% of adolescents and 30%–50% of adults [5]. Treatment of CFRD includes monitoring blood glucose and nutritional intake, insulin administration by injection or pump, and vigilance against potentially life‐threatening hypoglycemia [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. which can add to the already burdensome CF regimen. In a 2019 study, people with CFRD on insulin had higher treatment burden as compared to those without CFRD [11]. Indeed, treatment complexity and perceived regimen burden are correlated [2, 12]. Organizing and executing management of both CF pulmonary manifestations and CFRD can carry an additional emotional, cognitive, and behavioral load for people with CFRD and may affect perceptions and management of the pulmonary aspects of CF. Despite the high prevalence and increased burden of CFRD, surprisingly little research has examined health beliefs, barriers to adherence, executive function, and pulmonary medication adherence of people with CFRD.

An important aspect of self‐management is self‐efficacy, which is the confidence that one can take action—in this case, managing CF and/or CFRD—despite barriers and competing demands [13, 14]. Indeed, a study using the CF‐Medication Beliefs Questionnaire (CF‐MBQ) found that general self‐efficacy and self‐efficacy in settings with a lack of privacy were both highly correlated with CF‐medication adherence as measured by medication possession ratio (MPR) [13]. This study did not examine the impact of CFRD on self‐efficacy or medication adherence. CFRD may decrease pulmonary medication adherence because the addition of insulin increases regimen complexity. Additionally, the unpredictable nature of glycemic variability may lower self‐efficacy. Hypoglycemia, for example, is less predictable in CFRD than in type 1 diabetes due to dysregulated pancreatic insulin secretion [15]. Relatedly, there has been little research on the barriers to CFRD self‐management beyond qualitative research, which has found that lack of concern about immediate and future health effects of CFRD, time constraints, and embarrassment treating CFRD in public inhibit self‐management, while integration of diabetes‐related tasks into existing routines supported self‐management [16, 17].

Executive function encompasses goal‐oriented behaviors such as planning, organization, and problem‐solving, which are important for managing complex chronic conditions [18]. There has been one study on the association between CF, CFRD, and executive functioning, which found PWCF had worse processing speed, memory, and cognitive flexibility on performance‐based tests compared to healthy volunteers. People with CFRD had more difficulty with attention switching than PWCF without CFRD [19], reflecting a potential increased risk of impaired cognitive functioning and consequently lower adherence.

While there is robust literature on the burden of CF management and challenges in CF adherence, little is known about how CFRD affects treatment complexity or related factors like self‐efficacy, barriers to adherence, and executive function. In this exploratory secondary analysis, we hypothesized that those with CFRD would have lower self‐efficacy, lower pulmonary medication adherence, more barriers to self‐management, higher rates of depression and anxiety, and higher rates of executive functioning challenges.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This is a secondary analysis of the Daily Care Check‐In (DCC) Validation study, a cross‐sectional study conducted at 13 pediatric and 9 adult CF Care Centers within the CF Foundation's Success with Therapies Research Consortium from March 2017 to March 2018 [4]. Eligibility criteria included being aged 13 and older, prescribed a chronic pulmonary medication, and English‐speaking/literate. Exclusion criteria included the enrollment of another family member, inability to complete the survey due to developmental delays, hospitalization during enrollment, declining to authorize pharmacy refill data, and participation in the pilot study used to draft the DCC.

Procedures

Potential participants were identified and approached by a research coordinator at each site. If the participant and/or their legal guardian agreed to learn more about the study, the research coordinator confirmed that they met the eligibility criteria, then described the study procedures. If interested and eligible, informed consent/assent and authorization to collect medication refill data from pharmacies to calculate an MPR were obtained. The research coordinator then administered demographic and psychosocial surveys for participants to complete at the study visit [4]. Each site's Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved study procedures or ceded review to Boston Children's Hospital IRB (Study Number IRB‐AR00023999‐9) as the IRB of Record. The study followed the standards of the US Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects.

Measures

Self‐Efficacy

Participants completed the CF‐MBQ, a measure of beliefs about medications intended to better understand health behaviors in PWCF. It includes multiple sub‐scales, including Self‐Efficacy: General (14‐item), and Self‐Efficacy: Lack of Privacy (5‐item) [13]. Each question is a 10‐point Likert scale from 1 to 10, with 1 representing “not at all” and 10 representing “extremely.” The Self‐Efficacy: General subscale measures an individual's beliefs about their ability to complete self‐care tasks to manage their disease, while the Self‐Efficacy: Lack of Privacy subscale assesses an individual's confidence in their ability to complete health‐related tasks when they are around other people.

Adherence

Adherence was measured with a cMPR. Participants identified pharmacies where they had filled medications for 12 months before study enrollment, and the pharmacies were queried regarding the timing of refills for guideline‐recommended pulmonary CF medications and CFTR modulators available at the time of the study (Kalydeco, Orkambi, and Symdeko). For each medication, an MPR was calculated by dividing the supply of drug dispensed to the participant divided by the number of days the medication had been prescribed. These were averaged to create a cMPR for each participant [4, 13]. Of note, diabetes‐related medications were not included in the cMPR.

Barriers to Treatment

The DCC, a measure developed and validated by the research team, was used to evaluate participants' barriers to adherence [4]. The DCC consists of 18 items and is comprised of two scores: the Occurrence Scale (score range: 0–18), which measures the number of perceived barriers, and the Interference Scale (score range: 0–90), which measures the perception of how much each barrier interferes with treatment adherence. Survey items asked about various barriers to treatment, including financial burden, insurance issues, and treatment beliefs. If participants answered “yes” to a barrier item (Occurrence), they were then asked how often the issue occurred (Interference), rated on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from “never” to “always” [4]. No DCC items are specific to CFRD management, but the instructions ask the person to consider all their treatments.

Treatment Complexity

Treatment complexity was assessed using the Treatment Complexity Score (TCS), a composite measure that quantifies the overall burden of prescribed therapies for PWCF. The scale includes pulmonary, nutritional, and modulator therapies, abstracted from the medical record [20]. Components are weighted on a scale of 1 to 3 and then summed them for a total score. Complex or time‐consuming behaviors, such as airway clearance and insulin use, receive a score of 3. For CFRD, the TCS captures insulin use as the primary diabetes‐specific treatment; non‐insulin therapies and glucose monitoring are not included in the scoring.

Depression and Anxiety Symptoms

The 8‐item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐8) was used to describe depression symptoms, which inquires participants to rate how often they have been bothered by a symptom in the past 2 weeks using a 4‐point Likert scale (0 = “not at all” to 3 = “nearly every day”) with scores ranging from 0 to 24 [21]. Anxiety symptoms were measured using the 7‐item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale, a 4‐point Likert scale measuring the severity of symptoms (0 = “not at all” to 3 = “nearly every day”) with scores ranging from 0 to 21 [22]. A score ≥ 10 on the PHQ‐8 and GAD‐7 is indicative of clinically elevated depression and anxiety respectively.

Executive Function

The Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function (BRIEF) scales are standardized self‐report rating scales designed to assess executive function skills in an individual's daily environment across different age groups. In this study, participants completed the 75‐item BRIEF‐A (adults ≥ 18 years) [23] or the 55‐item BRIEF‐2 (adolescents ages 13–17) [24]. Both measures report individual scales, which are summarized into a Global Executive Composite (GEC) t‐score (mean 50, standard deviation 10). Subscales are also organized into indices, which differ between the two versions of the measure. The BRIEF‐A includes the Behavioral Regulation Index (BRI) and the Metacognition Index (MI), while the BRIEF‐2 includes BRI, the Cognitive Regulation Index (CRI), the Emotional Regulation Index (ERI).

All self‐report measures have the possibility of social desirability bias, with participants being more likely to respond in a way that would make others view them positively. This was minimized by allowing participants to complete the survey remotely, outside of the medical care setting, and by reassuring participants during consent that the responses to the surveys would not be shared with their medical team or impact their medical care.

Statistical Analyses

Frequency counts, means, and standard deviations were used to summarize participant demographics, health characteristics, and patient‐reported outcomes. Chi‐square tests, Fisher's Exact tests, and Welch's t‐tests were used to compare characteristics and patient‐reported outcomes for participants with CFRD to those who did not have CFRD. Due to differences in subscales between the BRIEF‐A and the BRIEF‐2, differences in BRIEF scores were analyzed separately for adolescents and adults, which limits statistical power. Pairwise deletion was used to handle missing data, and participants without CFRD status were excluded from the analyses, resulting in a final sample of 398 participants. To assess whether missing data were systematically related to CFRD status or other characteristics, a series of chi‐square tests was conducted comparing missingness rates for the scales between participants with and without CFRD; these revealed that there was no association between missingness and CFRD status. Statistical significance was tested at α = 0.05, and all analyses were conducted using Statistical Analysis System (SAS) version 9.4. Given the exploratory nature of this study, adjustments for multiple comparisons were not conducted.

Results

Participants

Of the 405 participants enrolled in the study, 398 had CFRD status and were included in the analyses. Compared to those without CFRD, the 33% (n = 131) of participants who had CFRD were on average older and had a lower BMI percentile and FEV1 percent predicted. A greater proportion of those with CFRD had pancreatic insufficiency and had received a course of IV antibiotics in the past year than those who did not have CFRD.

Survey Results

Treatment complexity, self‐efficacy, and lack of privacy self‐efficacy differed by CFRD status (p < 0.001, p = 0.030, and p = 0.015, respectively), with those with CFRD having higher treatment complexity, general self‐efficacy, and lack of privacy self‐efficacy than those without CFRD (Table 2). There were no differences in pulmonary medication adherence (cMPR) or in the occurrence of barriers or the interference from barriers between those with and without CFRD (Table 2). Additionally, there were no differences in the percent of participants with significantly elevated depression or anxiety scores between the two groups (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study of adolescents and adults with CF, we found that people with CFRD had higher disease burden and treatment complexity, but there were no differences in pulmonary medication adherence, barriers to treatment adherence, depression or anxiety symptoms, or self‐reported executive functioning between those with and without CFRD. People with CFRD had higher self‐efficacy scores and less concerns over privacy than those without CFRD. This study is among the first to comprehensively evaluate adherence, executive functioning, and self‐efficacy in those with and without CFRD. These findings were unexpected and contrary to our hypotheses. Interestingly, this sample demonstrated better pulmonary medication adherence and mental health among people with CFRD than would be expected based on the literature to date [3, 25]. People with CFRD had significantly higher treatment complexity, which is consistent with existing qualitative literature [16], but even with this complexity, they were able to maintain similar pulmonary medication adherence. More complex regimens have been shown in the literature to be associated with lower adherence as well as increased barriers to care [3]. Interestingly, however, there is some evidence that PWCF may have greater adherence when more medications are prescribed [2]. In this study, the occurrence and interference scores from the DCC were similar between those with and without CFRD, supporting the idea that having CFRD was not associated with more barriers to overall CF self‐management. The adherence measure used in this study, the cMPR, only included pulmonary CF medications and did not address adherence to other medications, notably medications to treat CFRD. This was also similar regardless of CFRD diagnosis, further supporting that the addition of CFRD management does not lead to lower adherence to pulmonary treatments. In future studies exploring this relationship, it will be important to use measures that include adherence to diabetes medications to better capture the full scope of medication adherence.

In qualitative studies, people with CFRD have emphasized the challenges of managing CFRD in addition to their CF management [26], but this has not been well‐evaluated quantitatively. ETI therapy has decreased the burden of pulmonary CF management, but its impact on endocrine manifestations of CF, such as CFRD, is not as clear. It appears that, at least initially, insulin needs and A1c may decrease, but the duration of these effects and the impact on disease burden have not been studied [27, 28, 29]. As the burden of pulmonary CF decreases with ETI therapy, CFRD management has become more of a focus for some PWCF [30]. With the potential for higher rates of CFRD among PWCF, given the impact of ETI therapy, and greater risk of diabetes‐related complications with longer life expectancy, more research is needed to understand the burdens of disease management in people with CFRD. Our results revealed that people with CFRD had higher self‐efficacy than those without CFRD. We had hypothesized that people with CFRD would have lower self‐efficacy due to the greater burden of disease management seen in CFRD as compared to CF. Among PWCF, greater self‐efficacy is associated with greater adherence and better mental and social health [18, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35], but self‐efficacy among people with CFRD has not been well‐studied. Higher adherence to pulmonary medications was not seen among those with CFRD despite the higher self‐efficacy. This finding was unexpected and supports the need for further study of the relationships between self‐efficacy and adherence to pulmonary medications among people with CFRD. It is possible that this higher self‐efficacy is what allows people with CFRD to be similarly adherent to their pulmonary medications despite the added treatment complexity of diabetes management. Among individuals in this sample, CFRD may be associated with some degree of adaptive coping [36], which should be studied further. Within type 1 diabetes research, strengths‐based measures that assess the skills and beliefs that support resilience have been developed [37]. The evaluation of resilience among PWCF and people with CFRD, to be evaluated alongside other strengths‐based measures [38], may lead to more rigorous investigations in the future.

We did not see differences in anxiety or depression between those with CF and those with CFRD. This is different from what was seen by Hjelm et al., who found that individuals with CFRD have higher depression screening scores, however their study determined mental health diagnoses from diagnosis codes in the medical record, which may have led to different results [25]. There are associations between higher levels of self‐concept (which includes self‐efficacy) and lower anxiety and depression [39]. Further studies are needed to clarify these relationships, especially as the treatment burden of pulmonary disease may have decreased in the era of ETI therapy.

The relationship between CF, CFRD, and executive function has yet to be fully elucidated. In a recent study of 19 children and adolescents with CF, executive function scores (as measured by a parent‐report questionnaire) were similar to general population norms [40]. A key difference in examinations of executive function in PWCF appears to be related to whether executive function is measured by performance‐based tasks or self‐report [19, 41]. In our study, we did not find differences in executive function between those with and without CFRD. Mean BRIEF t‐scores were within 1 standard deviation of the general population normative sample, indicating overall executive function was broadly within expected limits across groups. Executive function data in this study were analyzed by sub‐groups, as adolescents completed the BRIEF‐2 and adults completed the BRIEF‐A, which reduced power to detect significant differences between those with and without CFRD. The discrepancies between our results and other studies may also relate to the poor correlations between self‐report and performance‐based executive function measures. Further investigation into executive function status among those with CFRD is warranted, especially to better understand how physiological differences in people with diabetes could impact executive function [42, 43, 44].

The timing of this study was prior to widespread access to and utilization of ETI therapy. ETI may decrease the burden of pulmonary self‐care activities for PWCF [45], which may influence some of these relationships. While ETI has reduced the number of pulmonary daily treatments for some people, treatment burden entails more than doing a treatment [46, 47]. Studies have not examined if the pulmonary treatment burden is lower for people with CFRD or if diabetes‐specific burden has changed. Since these data were collected, people with CFRD have increasingly begun to use continuous glucose monitors and automated insulin delivery systems [48], which are not included in the TCS and may affect treatment burden. Still, these findings could provide an important baseline for comparison for future studies.

A primary strength of this study is the inclusion of a large number of adolescents and adults with CF, which allowed for a comprehensive assessment of multiple psychosocial and clinical outcomes and novel comparisons. A notable limitation of the study is the inability to examine diabetes‐specific outcomes, specifically including blood sugar fluctuations and adherence to the diabetes regimen. Diabetes‐related medications, for which an MPR cannot be reliably calculated [49], were not included in the cMPR, which may limit the relevance of these findings to CFRD self‐management. While the DCC instructions say to consider all treatments, it is possible that participants defaulted to thinking of pulmonary treatments, which may limit its applicability to CFRD management. There are limitations in both self‐report and performance‐based EF measures, and neither is a true “gold standard” [50]. Although a performance‐based measure of EF would be informative, this was not feasible in a large, multi‐center observational study. Given that these data were collected in 2019, the unique impacts of the COVID‐19 pandemic were not represented in this sample. Because the study is exploratory, we did not correct for multiple comparisons or adjust for potential confounders such as time since diagnosis, disease severity, and treatment complexity. Future research should consider a priori hypotheses about confounders and be powered for robust multivariate modeling. As with all studies, selection bias may affect the generalizability of the findings, for example, individuals with EF challenges be less likely and those with higher self‐efficacy more likely to participate in research.

In conclusion, participants with CFRD were able to maintain similar pulmonary medication adherence despite higher treatment complexity, which could be related to their greater self‐efficacy. EF was similar between those with and without CFRD, which is contrary to some prior studies [19], but this may be impacted by differences in self‐report and performance‐based EF measures. Future studies are needed to investigate the relationships between CF, CFRD, and self‐efficacy, adherence (including adherence to insulin), and EF.
