Star formation powers optical line emission from the CGM
Authors: Huanian Zhang, Lizhi Xie, Zhijie Qu, Dennis Zaritsky, Luis C. Ho, Min Bao, Fabio Fontanot, Michaela Hirschmann, Chenxu Liu, Gabriella De Lucia, Enci Wang, Haoyan Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Using integral field spectroscopy, we explore the disk-halo interface, or the inner circumgalactic medium (CGM), of individual galaxies by constructing and analyzing emission-line maps for a large sample (72) of normal, low-redshift galaxies spanning three orders of magnitude in stellar mass and four orders in star formation rate (SFR). We find a steep turnover occurring at (1 to 2)Re in the Hα, [O II], and [O III] line emission radial profiles. Beyond this radius, the slope of the line emission radial profiles becomes shallower as the SFR of the central galaxy decreases, which might reflect the strength of the feedback processes. The line emission fluxes at a large radius [(5 to 10)Re or ∼(0.1 to 0.25)rvir] correlate with the galaxy’s SFR but not with its stellar mass. These findings suggest that ionizing photons escaping from star-forming regions in the central galaxy account for the observed emission-line fluxes from the inner CGM, with escape fractions inferred from the [O III] and [O II] ratio. Different state-of-the-art theoretical models do not agree on the predicted dependence of cool gas on the SFR of the central galaxies, highlighting the importance of CGM emission line measurements to distinguish between different subgrid models for star formation and feedback processes.

INTRODUCTION

The circumgalactic medium (CGM) of galaxies is the fundamental baryon reservoir for galaxies (1–5) and has been studied primarily in absorption along a limited number of sightlines taking advantage of the features it imprints on the spectra of bright background sources (3, 6, 7). The detection of the CGM in emission lines, while observationally more challenging, allows for a more complete characterization of its properties by constructing two-dimensional distribution maps. The first detection of Hα and [N II] λ6585 emission extending to projected radii of ∼100 kpc from low-redshift, normal galaxies (8) used massive stacks of Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) (9, 10) spectra and reached a surface brightness (SB) sensitivity of ∼10−20 erg cm−2 s−1 arc sec−2. Subsequent studies characterized the line-emitting, cool CGM within 50 kpc or 0.25 virial radius (rvir) in low-redshift, normal galaxies (11–18) as a function of radius, stellar mass, environment, interaction, and orientation angle. Using long exposures with integral field spectrographs (IFSs) on Multi Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE) (19), the average extended emission in multiple lines (Mg II λλ2796,2803 and [O II]λλ3727,3729) can be detected out to ∼25 to 45 kpc with a similar SB sensitivity of ∼10−20 erg cm−2 s−1 arc sec−2 by stacking a sample of ∼600 galaxies at z≈1 (20, 21), and bipolar outflows out to ∼10 kpc traced by Mg II λλ2796,2803 emission are detected by stacking a few tens of galaxies with a stellar mass >109.5 M⊙ (solar mass) at z≈1 (22). These studies primarily use the Mg II λλ2796,2803 emission line as a tracer. However, as a resonant line, it traces mostly the neutral gas. Consequently, a direct probe of the cool, ionized gas is still lacking.

Using long exposures with an IFS on an 8- or 10-m telescope, one can study the cool CGM in specific systems where one might have anticipated stronger than typical CGM emission. Examples of such work include the studies of a compact massive galaxy [galaxy stellar mass (M∗) ∼ 1010.3 M⊙] with a star formation rate (SFR) of ∼10 M⊙ year−1 at z=0.737 (23), a starburst sub-L∗ galaxy (M∗ ∼1010.05 M⊙) with an SFR of 10 to 20 M⊙ year−1 at z=0.702 (24) that is probed by a quasar sightline, a starburst sub-L∗ galaxy (M∗ ∼109.9 M⊙) with an SFR of 50 M⊙ year−1 at z=0.6942 (25), a massive galaxy (M∗ ∼1011.1 M⊙) at z=0.459 with an SFR of 245 M⊙ year−1 (26), a nearby starburst sub-L∗ galaxy (M∗ ∼1010.1 M⊙) with an SFR of 12.1 M⊙ year−1 at z=0.01911 (27), a very low-mass blue compact dwarf galaxy (M∗ ∼6 × 106 M⊙; SFR ∼0.7 M⊙ year−1) (28) in the local Universe, the nearby starburst M 82 (29), and a source-blind survey for emission from the CGM across a wide range of redshifts in one deep MUSE field (30). However, the missing piece was the ability to do similar studies on a large sample of individual, more representative galaxies across a wide range of stellar masses and SFRs.

Using the Mapping Nearby Galaxies at Apache Point Observatory (MaNGA) (31) integral field spectroscopic data, we map the cool CGM using multiple optical emission lines in a large sample of individual nearby galaxies at a sufficiently high spatial resolution. The MaNGA survey is a large integral field spectroscopic survey targeting ∼10,000 nearby galaxies with the hexagonal fiber-bundle integral field units (IFUs) (32). Among the full survey, there are 72 galaxies at 0.015<z<0.146 targeted with an IFU bundle with >91 fibers for which the field of view (FOV) is >5 times the half-light, or effective, radius (Re) of the galaxy. For these galaxies, we can probe the line emission originating from the inner CGM (≳5Re and within the MaNGA FOV; details in Materials and Methods). We construct the narrow-band images of the optical emission lines using velocity windows of 150 to 375 km s−1, depending on the stellar mass of the galaxy (details in Materials and Methods), across each MaNGA fiber bundle FOV. The stellar mass, SFR, and specific SFR (sSFR) of our MaNGA galaxy sample span approximately three to four orders of magnitude, with which we can probe how the line emission from the cool CGM depends on galaxy properties and can constrain its origin. We do not correct the emission-line fluxes for dust extinction. Reliable extinction measurements due to the CGM are challenging, and the effect is expected to be negligible (33, 34), which report circumgalactic dust extinction on the order of just 0.03 mag in the V-band. In the analysis of these data that we present below, we adopt a ΛCDM cosmology with parameters Ωm = 0.3, ΩΛ= 0.7, Ωk = 0, and h= 0.7 (35, 36).

The ratio O32 ≡ [O III]/[O II], which is commonly used to trace the gas ionization parameter (37), is presented in the right panel of Fig. 1 for those galaxies with both 3σ [O II] and [O III] detections for all radial bins. The mean O32 radial profiles for 10 (6) galaxies with an SFR above (below) 0.1 M⊙ year−1 are also presented. The galaxy with the highest SFR shows very distinct O32 features and is therefore not included in the stack. The mean stack is consistent with the median stack, except for the last radial bin for the high-SFR stack, with a mean (median) O32 value of 0.58±0.25 (0.24±0.25). We observe consistent radial trends in O32 for galaxies with an SFR above and below 0.1 M⊙ year−1. The galaxy with the highest SFR (SFR ∼3.9 M⊙ year−1; top dark blue line) is classified as having an active galactic nucleus (AGN) on the basis of the mid-infrared color selection in the MaNGA-AGN catalog (38). However, the line ratios presented in our Baldwin, Philips, and Terlevich (BPT) diagram (39) are not consistent with the AGN being the ionization source (for details, see Materials and Methods). It exhibits two notable turnover points in the O32 radial profile at ∼3Re and ∼10Re, respectively, with an O32 difference between neighboring radial measurements of 2.1±0.3 and 6.3±1.2, respectively. These inflection points might reflect transitions from the galaxy disk to the disk-halo interface, where ionization is influenced by escaping photons from the central star-forming regions, and then, at a larger radius, to a region dominated by high escaping ionizing photons from the central galaxy or possibly by heat shocks or cooling gas inflows (lower metallicity). We caution, however, that the interpretation of O32 is complicated by its additional dependence on metallicity (40). We refer to Materials and Methods for more details on the metallicity gradient measurements for individual galaxies, where it has been possible to measure the gas metallicity.

Our measurements of the O32 ratio can also be used to study the distribution of the ionizing radiation that is able to escape from the star-forming galaxies and ionize the CGM and intergalactic medium around them, referred to as the escape fraction. Studies using O32 to constrain the escape fraction suggest that the extreme O32 values measured in Green Pea galaxies at z=0.1 to 0.3 are due to a high escape fraction (41–44), although there exist additional complicating factors such as the galaxy stellar mass, the metallicity, the SFR and sSFR of the galaxy, the Re and the SFR surface density of the galaxy, etc. We measure similar values of O32 for some of the high-SFR galaxies in our sample. Adopting the best-fit scaling relation between the Lyman continuum (LyC) escape fraction and O32 from the recent studies (43, 44), with the caution that the scatter in the scaling relation is large (as high as 1 to 2 dex), and acknowledging the difficulties in measuring the escape fraction, we conclude that the inferred escape fractions are within the plausible limits of 1 to 2% (<1%) out to a radius of ∼5Re in general for the high-SFR (low-SFR) galaxies in our sample but as high as ∼35% for the galaxy with the highest SFR out to rp∼13 kpc (∼0.2 rvir).

Inner CGM across the entire sample

Among the 72 target galaxies, we find significant (>3σ) Hα emission detection out to a radius of at least 5Re [mean measurements within annuli of (4.5 to 5.5)Re] for 31 galaxies and bare to moderate (∼1 to 2σ) measurements for four additional ones. Among those 35 galaxies, 15 of them have significant (>3σ) detections, and four have bare to moderate (∼1 to 2σ) measurements out to 7.5Re [mean measurements within annuli of (7 to 8)Re]. Among the total of 13 galaxies that can be covered out to a radius of 10Re, six of them have significant (≳4σ) Hα emission out to a radius of 10Re [mean measurements within annuli of (9.5 to 10.5)Re], and one of them is poorly measured (∼1σ). The mean and median values of the Hα emission measurements within those annuli are consistent with each other. All measurements with significance less than 2σ are treated as the upper limits. The mean (median) stellar mass of the galaxies with Hα emission detection at 5Re is 109.82 (109.71) M⊙, which is slightly lower than those without Hα emission detections (mean, median: 109.97, 109.77 M⊙). The mean (median) SFR of the galaxies with Hα emission detection at 5Re is 10–1.21 (10–1.13) M⊙ year−1, which is more than one order of magnitude higher than those without Hα emission detections (mean, median: 10–2.21, 10–2.47 M⊙ year−1), which is also the case for the sSFR of the galaxies (mean, median: 10–11.03, 10–11.11 year−1 for detections and 10–12.21, 10–12.27 year−1 for nondetections). The mean (median) stellar metallicity of the galaxies (45) with Hα detection is 0.5 Z⊙ (solar metallicity) (0.7 Z⊙), which is lower than that for those without Hα detection (mean, median: 0.8 Z⊙, 0.9 Z⊙). Six among these 72 target galaxies are classified as AGNs according to the MaNGA-AGN catalog (38), denoted by cyan open cycles in Fig. 2. Four of them are selected purely by Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE) (46, 47) mid-infrared color cuts, and the remaining two are selected purely by radio emission detection. None of them has detected x-ray emission or the signature of broad emission lines.

Also, in Fig. 2, we include literature measurements for two galaxies for comparison. The Hα emission SB of a nearby low-mass galaxy (30) at z=0.1723 (M∗ ∼108.78±0.42 M⊙ and SFR ∼0.1 M⊙ year−1) is significantly detected at 5Re (Re∼1.34 kpc) (48), 7.5Re, and 10Re in the Hubble Deep Field South (49) using MUSE data. The consistency between the MaNGA and MUSE for galaxies with similar properties is reassuring. The Hβ SB (27) of a nearby starburst galaxy [IRAS 08339+6517 (50, 51), M∗ ∼1010.0±0.1 M⊙ and SFR ∼12.1±0.1 M⊙ year−1] at z=0.01911 is significantly detected at 5Re (Re∼1 kpc) (52), 7.5Re, and 10Re. To place this object on the plot, we adopt Hβ/Hα=0.3, a rough value consistent with theoretical expectations (53–55). Again, this object also falls on the general trend.

At z∼0.05, galaxies with SFR ∼1 M⊙ year−1 exhibit a typical Hα SB of ∼10–17.4 erg cm−2 s−1 arc sec−2 at 5Re, ∼10.0 kpc at z=0.05 estimated from the mean value of the entire sample. This corresponds to a LyC photon production rate of 1.9 × 1051 s−1 on the basis of a scaling relation between Hα luminosity and LyC photon production (56), which is derived from simulations of starburst galaxies (57) under the assumption of zero escape fraction into the intergalactic medium. Several mechanisms could produce these ionizing photons, including escaping radiation from central galaxies, the ultraviolet background (UVB) (58, 59), shock ionization (55, 60, 61), or other feedback-driven processes. The UVB alone, however, is insufficient: With ΓHI≈4×10−14 to 10×10−14 s−1 (59), it yields an Hα SB of only ≈10−19 erg s−1 cm−2 arc sec−2 at the disk-halo interface (62), close to our 2σ detection limit (≈10−19.2 erg s−1 cm−2 arc sec−2, estimated from the mean value of those 2σ detection limits). Given that the detected Hα emission (Fig. 2) far exceeds this limit, the UVB is not the dominant ionization source.

We also conclude that shock ionization is also unlikely to dominate for several reasons. First, the stacked O32 profile from our galaxy sample remains nearly constant at ∼0.4 from 0.2 Re to 5 Re, indicating a substantially uniform ionization parameter over this radial range. If fast radiative shocks dominate the inner CGM ionization, they would require nearly identical shock velocities and parameters across the entire range of 0.2Re to 5 Re to maintain such a constant O32. Second, a substantial fraction of galaxies shows a slightly decreasing O32 profile, which is inconsistent with the pure shock model predictions (60, 63). Third, our BPT diagrams (39) for two galaxies for which we have data covering the same radial range lie in the star-forming region (for details, see Materials and Methods). Last, shock models (60, 63) that produce O32 ∼0.4 predict BPT line ratios (e.g., elevated [N II]/Hα and [O III]/Hβ) that are inconsistent with our BPT diagnostics. Unfortunately, there are concerns with several of these arguments. First, O32 is sensitive to metallicity, and we are stacking across a wide range of galaxy masses. Second, the O32 profiles show some variance and become more difficult to measure at larger radii. Third, we have the radially sampled BPT diagrams only for two galaxies, and again, at large radii, the uncertainties are large and do allow for ratio values that place the region slightly outside the star formation region of the diagram. As such, contributions from shocks cannot be definitively ruled out. Nevertheless, in light of these arguments and the connections we identify next between the Hα emission at a large radius and the star formation properties of these galaxies, we conclude that the most likely source of the ionizing radiation in the inner CGM region is star formation in the central galaxy.

We independently estimate the ionizing photon output from the central star formation of the galaxies. Assuming solar metallicity, continuous star formation, and the Kroupa initial mass function (64), the production rate of ionizing photons can be estimated from the SFR using established scaling relations. For an SFR ∼1 M⊙ year−1 and assuming that 1 to 2% photons escape into the 5Re region, the resulting LyC photon rate is 1.4×1051 to 2.7×1051 s−1, which is consistent with that estimated from the observed Hα emission flux. Note that the mean stellar metallicity of our galaxy sample is lower than the adopted solar metallicity, suggesting that the production rate of ionizing photons calculated above is actually underestimated or that the assumed LyC escape fraction can be relaxed. Even in lower-SFR galaxies, the ionizing photon production estimated from SFR remains higher than that inferred from the observed Hα SB. Although uncertainties exist in estimating LyC photon production, because of assumptions about metallicity, rough estimation of the escape fraction, star formation history, and initial mass function, the simple calculations clearly demonstrate that the central galaxy’s star formation activity can produce a sufficient number of ionizing photons to fully account for the observed emission-line fluxes.

One caveat is that the different stellar mass and SFR estimations have an ∼0.1 to 0.2 dex scatter, especially for galaxies with low SFR. We adopt different measurements of the stellar mass and SFR to test the scaling relation, as expressed in Eq. 1. We extract the stellar mass (65–67) and SFR from the MPA-JHU catalog (68, 69). We also retrieve the stellar mass and SFR from the GSWLC (GALEX-SDSS-WISE LEGACY CATALOG) (70, 71) using the spectral energy distribution fitting technique. All model parameters are consistent within 1σ for the three different radii, except for the b1 estimate, where the deviation lies between 1.5 and 2.5σ for the different stellar mass and SFR estimates for the different radii. It further demonstrates that the strong correlation between the Hα emission SB and the galaxy’s SFR is an intrinsic property of the galaxies, regardless of the scatter of stellar mass and SFR estimations.

Implications from simulations

Cosmological simulations provide a useful tool to study the multiphase gas of the galaxy and to understand the subgrid physics by the comparison to observations. In Fig. 3, we present the cool gas (with a temperature of ∼104 K) surface density, Σ, at 5Re and 10Re as a function of M*, SFR, and sSFR for the GAlaxy Evolution and Assembly (GAEA) semianalytic model (72) and the IllustrisTNG (TNG) hydrodynamical simulation (73–79), where GAEA and TNG mock/model galaxies are selected to have a stellar mass and SFR within ±0.1 dex of each galaxy in our MaNGA galaxy sample. This does not provide a direct comparison to the observations (cool gas surface density versus emission line SB but, under the simplest assumptions, one might expect emission line SB ∝Σ2), given that converting from gas surface density to emission line SB involves understanding the ionization spectrum, thermal equilibrium, and recombination rates, and GAEA does not provide temperature distribution. However, combining the fact that GAEA and TNG galaxies share the same properties as our MaNGA galaxy sample and there exists strong correlation between Hα emission SB and SFR (sSFR) as shown in Fig. 2, it is expected that GAEA and TNG galaxies should share similar Hα emission SB. The consistency in terms of the sense and magnitude of the correlations between our observations and the GAEA model is excellent, which might reflect the notion that the underlying physics driving the CGM line emission is the amount of cool gas. In addition, Fig. 3 clearly demonstrates that the strength of the star formation process (and sSFR) is the main regulator for the amount of cool gas at the circumgalactic scale.

Correlation between the cool gas and the galaxy’s properties from simulations.

The cool gas surface density at 5Re (red dot) and 10Re (coral triangle) as a function of M∗, SFR, and sSFR for GAEA (top) and TNG (bottom) simulations. The M∗ and SFR of GAEA and TNG galaxies are already matched to our MaNGA galaxy sample within 0.1 dex.

In contrast, we find that the cool gas surface density at either 5Re or 10Re does not correlate with any physical properties of the galaxies selected from TNG, as shown in the lower panel of Fig. 3. These results are consistent with previous work, which found that the neutral hydrogen (HI) gas density of TNG galaxies at large radii (30 to 70 kpc) depends only weakly on SFR, while the central density varies markedly with SFR (80, 81). In the TNG simulation, SFR is mainly suppressed by kinetic feedback from AGNs, which pushes gas outward and yields more extended HI density profiles and gas deficiency in the central region (82, 83), with minimal reduction of the HI gas reservoir. Consequently, the central galaxies in the TNG simulation have a larger fraction of cool gas at large radii than their counterparts in the GAEA model, and their surface density is independent of SFR. However, we do not detect any optical emission from a possible cool CGM at large radii for quenched massive galaxies (M∗>1010.5 M⊙; sSFR <10−12 year−1). Furthermore, using the HI-MaNGA sample (84), we find that HI is detected in our sample for those galaxies with M∗<1010.5 M⊙ and sSFR >10−12 year−1, suggesting that massive quenched galaxies do not retain an HI reservoir, different from TNG simulations. One possibility, discussed in the recent literature (85–87), is that a high angular momentum supports a stable HI gas at the outskirt region and prevents the gas from falling into the galaxy disk, thereby keeping galaxies quenched. Our discovery of the tight correlation between the Hα emission SB at all radii and SFR or sSFR for both high-mass and low-mass galaxies, however, disfavors this scenario.

In summary, we present a mapping of the dominant baryonic component of galaxies. Although previous IFU observations have detected the CGM either in high-redshift (88–90) or lower-redshift extreme galaxies (91), this is a systematic unbiased study to probe the inner cool CGM for a large sample of normal, low-redshift galaxies with a wide range of stellar mass, SFR, and sSFR. Using the MaNGA IFS data, we find a steep turnover occurring at (1 to 2)Re in the detected Hα, [O II], and [O III] emission SB radial profiles and a shallower slope of the line emission radial profiles beyond this radius for low-SFR galaxies and identify strong correlations in Hα, [O II], and [O III] SB in the inner CGM with both the galaxy’s SFR and sSFR at all radii but not with its stellar mass. The ionizing photo escape fraction estimated for our MaNGA galaxy sample is ∼(1 to 2)% out to a radius of the inner CGM or ∼5Re and could reach as high as 35% for the galaxy with the highest SFR at a large radius of rp∼13 kpc. The star formation in the galaxies can provide enough escaping ionizing photons to fully account for the observed emission-line fluxes. We compare our results against the prediction of two state-of-the-art theoretical models of galaxy formation and show that they provide quite different predictions for the relations between Hα emission/gas density profiles and SFR. Our conclusions highlight the critical role these kinds of observations can play in a better understanding of the fundamental physical processes responsible for galaxy evolution.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

MaNGA survey and data sample

The MaNGA survey is an IFU spectroscopic program using the Sloan 2.5-m telescope (92), targeting ∼10,000 galaxies in the nearby universe (0.01≲z≲0.15), covering a wide range of stellar mass, SFR, and colors (93). The primary goal of the MaNGA survey is to seek the physical origin of the mechanisms that drive galaxy evolution by providing and analyzing the stellar population and the gaseous component across the face of each galaxy. The fiber bundles vary in diameter from 12″ (19 fibers) to 32″ (127 fibers), typically covering (1.5 to 2.5)× the effective radius of the target, with a spatial resolution of 2.5″. The typical total integration time of the target is roughly 3 hours. The wavelength coverage is from 3600 to 10350 Å with spectral resolving power R∼2000, corresponding to a velocity of 75 km s−1 at 5100 Å (94). The spectroscopic data cubes are resampled on a 0.5×0.5″ grid using the MaNGA’s DRP (Data Reduction Pipeline) (94).

There are only 219 galaxies in the entire MaNGA sample for which the IFU bundle samples out to a radius of at least 5Re of the galaxy. Furthermore, we require that the IFU bundle consists of at least 91 fibers to provide sufficiently spatially resolved measurements of the CGM. We are left with 75 target fields, including one galaxy of a galaxy pair. The galaxy’s stellar masses are provided in the MaNGA DRP catalog (94), and SFRs are provided in the MaNGA’s DAP (Data Analysis Pipeline) catalog (95, 96). The galaxy’s stellar metallicity is adopted from the MaNGA Pipe3D catalog (45). Two galaxies with a bright bulge or nuclear source and one galaxy that is part of a galaxy pair are excluded from the galaxy sample, which will be elaborated below. So, we are left with 72 galaxies. The stellar masses of the final galaxy sample span from 108.6 M⊙ to 1011 M⊙, and the SFRs of the final galaxy sample span from 10–3.5 M⊙ year−1 to 100.59 M⊙ year−1, both of which span approximately three to four orders of magnitude. The mean (median) redshift, stellar mass, SFR, sSFR, and stellar metallicity of this galaxy sample are 0.0528 (0.0384), 109.91 (109.74) M⊙, 0.016 (0.025) M⊙ year−1, 10–11.70 (10–12.11) year−1, and 0.66 (0.81) Z⊙, respectively. Our sample contains both star-forming and quiescent galaxies, and six of them are classified as AGNs according to the MaNGA-AGN catalog (38). However, we do not find any difference for the emission line measurements or the other galaxy properties among those galaxies, which are claimed to host AGNs and not host AGNs, except the galaxy with the highest SFR. Because the targets are not selected on the basis of any physical properties, except possibly SB because of the comparably shallow observational sensitivity, and it spreads a wide range of stellar mass and SFR, our sample can be considered as an unbiased sample for the large-scale CGM study.

Transition to the CGM

A key challenge in the study of the CGM is the identification of the disk-halo boundary. At what radius are we detecting emission from the CGM rather than the galaxy disk? In our previous stacking work (11, 12), we adopted a radial range defined in terms of the rvir values of 0.05<r/rvir<0.25. The outer radius was set to avoid contamination from neighboring galaxies, while the inner radius was set to avoid emission from the central galaxy. Neither of these boundaries is absolute or particularly well defined. In the best of cases, the boundary is defined by a clear change in the nature of the SB emission profile. For example, a break at ∼50 to 60 kpc for Milky Way–like galaxies in the stacked SB profile has been associated with contamination from nearby neighbors using simulations (11). This result helps define the outer limit of the range. In a recent study (27), there is a tell-tale break in the profile at ∼4.0 kpc (roughly twice the 90% stellar radius) between a galaxy disk’s exponential profile and the CGM power law profile for a starburst sub-L∗ galaxy (M* ∼1010.0±0.1 M⊙; SFR ∼12.1±0.1 M⊙ year−1) at z=0.01911 that is associated with the disk-halo boundary. In this case, the rvir of this galaxy is estimated to be 115 kpc (27), so this break occurs at r/rvir∼0.04, consistent with what was adopted as the inner boundary in our previous stacking analysis.

For a more direct, empirical definition of the disk-halo boundary, we translate our previous limit into units of Re values. The previous work, based on scaling relations between the galaxy’s stellar mass and rvir derived from the UniverseMachine simulation (97), suggests a mean value of 0.05 rvir = 5.7 kpc for our MaNGA galaxy sample. To be somewhat conservative, we set the boundary at approximately twice this value, which corresponds to ∼5Re (11.4 kpc). To verify, we also evaluate 5Re in UniverseMachine simulation (97) at z=0.05, and it is 12.1 kpc, which is consistent with that for our MaNGA galaxy sample. Thus, we adopt a radius of 5Re to represent the inner edge of the CGM. The effective radius might be a misleading indicator if a galaxy has a bright bulge or nuclear source. We identify two such galaxies among the 75 targets. These two galaxies sit systematically below the galaxy stellar mass and effective radius scaling relation (Fig. 4) (98, 99). In these cases, we do not trust the 5Re criterion and remove those two galaxies from our sample. In addition, there is one galaxy in our MaNGA sample that is part of a targeted galaxy pair, and the galaxy is not centered in the FOV. Therefore, we also remove this galaxy, and we are left with 72 galaxies.

A legitimate concern is that we have significantly underestimated the radius out to which disk HII regions produce emission lines. To provide some more intuition and support for our choice, 2R90 was adopted as the disk-halo boundary in a recent study (27), where R90 is the radius containing 90% of the optical flux. The concentration index R90/Re in the SDSS g-band can be obtained from the Pipe3D catalog. The mean (median) ratio is 2.78 (2.73), with a dispersion of 0.49 for the mean value. 2R90 is ∼5.6Re, roughly consistent with our choice of 5Re as the disk-halo boundary. Furthermore, the independent measurement of outer-disk star-forming clusters in a sample of nearby galaxies (100) shows that even among galaxies with extended, star-forming disks, e.g., M 83 and NGC 628, HII regions are found only within 2R90. There is no evidence for disk emission line sources outside of ∼5Re.

Last, we test these assumptions and results using a toy model for the radial profile of the Hα equivalent width (EW). We measure the radial profile of the Hα EW for all 72 galaxies. We were able to measure EW profiles (requiring at least three positive measurements to define a profile) for 38 systems. We will return to discuss the 34 galaxies for which we do not have profiles. For most of the 38 systems with profiles, the EW remains roughly constant within the galaxy disk and increases beyond it, driven primarily by a drop in the continuum emission, although there are some that drop at large radii. To mimic this range of behavior, we model the profile as the combination of a star-forming disk component that has constant EW out to Rd and decreases suddenly but smoothly beyond this radius.EWdisk(R)=Adisk/(1+e−k(R−Rd))and a CGM component that is simply proportional to the radius. Therefore, the asymptotic behavior of the model isEW(R)≈Adisk+ACGM (RRe)R≪RdACGM (RRe)R≫Rd(2)and we set k=10 and fit for Adisk, Rd, and ACGM. We fit the model for each galaxy, only using measured EW values >10−5 because lower values are unreliable.

We show examples of the fits, including a variety of behavior, in Fig. 5. Our two key findings are that there is a strong correlation between ACGM and sSFR (Spearmen rank correlation coefficient, ρs, of 0.771 and a P value of 1.52×10−8; Fig. 6) and that 〈Rd/Re〉=1.61±0.17. The first of the two results supports our previous claims of a connection between the CGM emission and the star formation of the central galaxy, and the second result supports our claim that the disk influence ends well before 5Re for most galaxies in the sample. Returning to the 34 galaxies for which we do not have EW profiles, the lack of a measured EW at any radius suggests that these galaxies have little if any CGM emission (at least to our sensitivity level). The mean sSFR for these galaxies is 10–12.29 year−1, placing them at the far left of the distribution in Fig. 6 and further supporting the evident correlation. These galaxies are also interesting in the context of the role of shock ionization. It is circumstantial evidence against shocks as the dominant source of ionizing photons for the inner CGM that none of these low-sSFR galaxies exhibits significant Hα emission at large radii.

Data analysis

To increase the signal-to-noise ratio (S/N ratio) sufficiently to detect the CGM, we bin the MaNGA data along both the spectral and spatial dimensions. In the spectral direction, we extend the approach we used with the SDSS spectral stacks (8). We measure the residual emission flux above continuum within a filter window centered on the relevant central wavelength at the corresponding rest frame for each spectrum in each MaNGA data cube. The velocity windows are ±150 km s−1 for galaxies with M∗≤109.5 M⊙, ±200 km s−1 for galaxies with 109.5<M∗/M⊙≤1010.3, ±275 km s−1 for galaxies with 1010.3<M∗/M⊙≤1011, and ±375 km s−1 for galaxies with M∗>1011 M⊙. In addition, the tests demonstrate that the results are not sensitive to the slight change of the velocity windows. The residual flux of the spectrum in each spaxel is determined by fitting and subtracting a third-order polynomial to a 300-Å-wide spectral section. We find that the estimation of the continuum from a third-order polynomial is far from satisfactory for quiescent galaxies because of strong stellar absorptions, especially within the galaxy disk. Thus, we estimate the continuum from a linear combination of simple stellar population models, as described further below. As done previously (8), we require the continuum level within the 300-Å spectral window for each spaxel spectrum to be less than 3 × 10−17 erg cm−2 s−1 Å−1 to limit the noise introduced by interloping strong emitters such as satellite galaxies or outer-disk emission line regions, such as the HII regions (100, 101). In the spatial dimension, we bin 2 × 2 spaxels to accumulate signal, which roughly matched the spaxel size to the observing seeing, but do not sacrifice too much spatial resolution. Note that we do not need to rebin 2 by 2 spaxels to construct the radial profile presented in Fig. 1 to retain the small-scale emission features.

We correct for the underlying stellar continuum within 3Re of each MaNGA data cube, including the stellar absorption lines that can affect the emission-line flux measurements, over the rest-frame wavelength range of 3700 to 8000 Å using the pPXF (penalized pixel-fitting) package (102–104) with a linear combination of stellar templates from the MILES library (105, 106). Beyond 3Re of each data cube, the S/N ratio in the continuum of the spectrum in each spaxel is insufficient to do this. Instead, we still use a third-order polynomial to estimate the continuum for each spectrum. We measure our emission-line SB within the annuli across the entire MaNGA FOV. For those galaxies with b/a (minor axis–to–major axis ratio) less than 0.75, we correct for the annuli by adopting the ellipse with the same b/a. An S/N ratio of ≳3 is generally required for reliable CGM detection, and this threshold is adopted in the above discussion. To estimate the emission-line SB or the line ratio uncertainties, we adopt the bootstrap method. We repeat the bootstrap resampling 1000 times and calculate the mean measurements of the resampled data for each iteration. From the distribution of measurements, we quote use the 16.5 and 83.5 percentiles as the uncertainty range.

The linear regression analyses in this study were performed within a Bayesian framework using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling for parameter estimation. In particular, we adopted the likelihood function for straight line fitting (107) to model the Hα SB (logfHα) dependence on either SFR or sSFR in the logarithmic scale. The likelihood function simultaneously accounts for measurement uncertainties in both the Hα SB and SFR (or sSFR). Crucially, the model also incorporates an intrinsic scatter term (σp), accounting for the astrophysical dispersion beyond measurement uncertainties. The intrinsic scatter was prescribed a fixed value of σp=0.2 dex for all fittings. The adopted priors are symmetric with respect to rotation [i.e., uniform for the inclination angle (θ) rather than the line slope; slope = tanθ] (107) and uniform for the intercept. The MCMC sampling was completed with 50 walkers and 5000 steps per chain, with convergence assessed and confirmed using the Gelman-Rubin diagnostic (108). Best-fit parameters and their uncertainties reported in Table 1 correspond to the median and the central 68% credible interval (the 16.5 and 83.5 percentiles, ±1σ) of the marginalized posterior distributions. The Python packages used in this work are all the newest version, including SciPy (109), NumPy (110), AstroPy (111), Matplotlib (112), Pandas (113), and emcee (114).

GAEA and TNG simulations

We use the latest version of GAEA model (72), with more details in https://sites.google.com/inaf.it/gaea/home, running on merger trees extracted from Millennium Simulation (115), which has a box size of 683 cMpc. In this work, we use about 1/50 of the volume. This version of the model implements an updated and well-calibrated treatment for supermassive black hole accretion and AGN feedback (116) and a state-of-the-art treatment of environmental effect (like tidal stripping and dynamical friction) (117), which predicts good agreement with observational measurements on quenched fraction up to z∼3 (118). In the semianalytic approach, each galaxy includes several discrete components (bulge, disc, and halo), where baryons are described by a multiphase gas (hot gas, cold gas, and stars). Hot gas is heated up to the virial temperature, which exceeds the temperature limit considered in this study for all resolved massive halos at z=0, and therefore is not accounted for in the measured cool gas surface density. Cold gas on the disk is assumed to have a temperature of ≤104 K and follow exponential distribution of Σg(r)=Mg2πrg2e−rrg. Here, Mg and rg are the cold gas mass and the scale length of the cold gas disk, respectively. TNG is a spatially resolved magnetohydrodynamical simulation (73–79), which is available at www.tng-project.org/data/. In this study, we use TNG100 from the TNG project. The side length of the simulation box is 106.5 Mpc. The baryon mass resolution is 1.4×106 M⊙, the gravitational softening length of the dark matter and stars is 0.7 kpc at z=0, and the minimum gas cell radius is 185 pc. We compute the effective temperature of each gas cell using the electron abundance and internal energy. The star-forming gas cells are excluded. Gas cells in TNG have a minimum temperature of 104 K; therefore, we select gas cells with 104≤Teff<104.25 K as the cool gas.

To compare to our MaNGA results, we measure the surface density of gas at 5Re and 10Re [within annuli of (4.5 to 5.5)Re and (9.5 to 10.5)Re, respectively] of the central galaxies in both the GAEA model and TNG simulation. For the GAEA galaxies, the comparison is not straightforward because those models only include cold (able to form stars for the molecular component) and hot (virial temperature) gas. To proceed, on the one hand, we assume that the gas our observations trace is more closely related to the cold gas and that this cold gas has an exponential density profile beyond the galaxy disk. On the other hand, we include the cool gas (with temperature of ∼10,000 K) estimated according to the scaling relation between the mass cooling rate and the stacked Hα emission flux (16). For the TNG simulation, we first identify the three axes of the distribution of stellar particles, then determine the stellar disk using the long and intermediate axes, and lastly project the cool gas cells with a temperature Teff<104.25 K onto the stellar disk. We then calculate the surface density of the cool gas by summing up all the gas cells in each annulus. To match our MaNGA galaxy sample, we select analogs from both GAEA and TNG that have a stellar mass and SFR within ±0.1 dex of each galaxy in our MaNGA galaxy sample. Furthermore, we select only central galaxies because our MaNGA galaxies are not interacting with other galaxies or otherwise disturbed by the ambient environment. This leaves us with 3481 galaxies from the GAEA simulation and 2588 galaxies from the TNG simulation. We then calculate the median cool gas surface density for each bin in M*, SFR, and sSFR.

Inner CGM of example galaxies

To provide examples of the power of IFS data, we select two individual galaxies for which we detect most or all of the principal optical emission lines ([O II]λλ3727,3729, Hβ, [O III]λ5008, Hα, and [N II]λ6585, all vacuum wavelengths). The first target we highlight is a starburst galaxy at z=0.04723 with M* = 108.57 M⊙ and SFR = 3.90 M⊙ year−1. The position angle of this target galaxy is 74°, and the minor axis–to–major axis ratio is 0.92. The Re is 0.86″, corresponding to ∼0.81 kpc at the distance of the target galaxy. The rvir of this galaxy is estimated to be ∼74 kpc according to the scaling relation between the stellar mass and the rvir derived from the UniverseMachine simulation (97). For this galaxy, although it is star-bursting, the Re is still a good measurement and consistent with identifying the location of the CGM in terms of fractional rvir values (5Re∼0.06 rvir).

With significant detections of all the optical emission lines (Fig. 9), we can use the emission line ratios to estimate the metallicity and ionization of the CGM. The line ratio of R23 [≡log(([O II]λ3727+[O III]λλ5008,4960)/Hβ)] is widely adopted as a metallicity diagnostic (119), as it involves emission lines of both the main ionization stages of oxygen, O+ and O+2, and so is less affected by the ionization parameter than alternatives (40, 120). We caution that the metallicity estimation using R23 as an indicator for the CGM at a larger radius might be biased because the R23 method is calibrated using HII regions rather than the CGM, and the ionization structure, temperature, and electron density will be quite different. Therefore, the metallicity estimates here are only used for qualitative purposes. Using the stacked spectra within annuli, we find that the metallicity within 8 kpc (10Re or 0.12 rvir) is quite homogeneous, with a value of 12 +log(O/H)∼8.0, consistent with the Pipe3D estimation at 2Re (45). We also measure a metallicity gradient, with the gas metallicity decreasing from 7.91−0.03+0.03 at 10 kpc (within the annulus with the radius between 9 and 11 kpc) to 7.78−0.06+0.08 at 15 kpc (within the annulus with the radius between 14 and 16 kpc).
