Association between a body shape index and diabetic kidney disease in patients with diabetes mellitus: a cross-sectional study from the 2005–2016 U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey
Authors: Qinqin Lv, Zhenwei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUncertainty persists regarding the associations between a body shape index (ABSI) and diabetic kidney disease (DKD), albuminuria, and low estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Data from 2,770 participants in the 2005–2016 U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were analyzed in this study. Multiple logistic regression models and subgroup analyses were utilized to evaluate the relationships between ABSI and DKD, albuminuria, and low eGFR. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) plot was applied to examine trends in these associations. In the fully adjusted model, each unit increase in ABSI was associated with a 33% higher risk of DKD (adjusted OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.09–1.61, p = 0.0043) and a 50% higher risk of albuminuria (adjusted OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.23–1.84, p < 0.0001). In contrast, no significant relationship was observed with low eGFR (adjusted OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.88–1.46, p = 0.3444). These relationships exhibited variation across age, sex, and comorbidity subgroups. Results based on standardized ABSI (z-score) were similar, confirming significant associations with DKD and albuminuria. RCS plots showed that ABSI was significantly and linearly associated with the risks of DKD (P-overall = 0.026, P-nonlinear = 0.577) and albuminuria (P-overall = 0.001, P-nonlinear = 0.658), indicating that the risk of DKD and albuminuria increased with higher ABSI levels. In contrast, no significant association was observed between ABSI and low eGFR (P-overall = 0.298, P-nonlinear = 0.238). Higher ABSI levels are associated with increased risks of DKD and albuminuria, particularly in specific subgroups.

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), a complication of diabetes mellitus, results from metabolic disturbances, hemodynamic alterations, inflammatory responses, and fibrotic processes triggered by impaired glucose metabolism [1]. End-stage renal disease (ESRD), a major consequence of chronic kidney disease (CKD), places a significant burden on healthcare systems and society. The key risk factors for DKD include hyperglycemia, hypertension, obesity, and activation of the renin-angiotensin system, which are primary targets for therapeutic intervention, with abdominal obesity being one of the most important contributors [2]. However, commonly used measures of obesity in current clinical and epidemiological research include body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC). However, both have notable limitations: BMI cannot distinguish between fat mass and lean mass, while WC alone does not account for individual height and body shape differences, making it an imprecise indicator of visceral fat [3,4]. These shortcomings reduce their sensitivity and accuracy in predicting metabolic and renal risks. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed several alternative indices—such as waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), lipid accumulation product (LAP), and visceral adiposity index (VAI)—to more accurately assess the relationship between obesity and diabetes-related complications [5]. Among these, a body shape index (ABSI), proposed by Krakauer et al. incorporates WC, height, and weight to better reflect central obesity and is considered useful in predicting health risks associated with visceral fat accumulation [6]. Previous studies have demonstrated associations between ABSI and aortic calcification, diabetes, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality [7,8]. However, findings on its relationship with DKD remain inconsistent. For example, one cross-sectional study in a Chinese diabetic population suggested a positive association between ABSI and DKD prevalence [9], while another study based on U.S. data failed to identify a significant link between ABSI and DKD [10]. This inconsistency highlights a gap in current evidence and underscores the need for further investigation into whether ABSI can serve as a reliable indicator of renal impairment in diabetic patients.

In addition, a large cohort study by Lee et al. involving 6,746 participants demonstrated that compared to BMI and WC, ABSI performed better in predicting 10-year cardiovascular disease mortality, showing greater risk discrimination and stability, suggesting that ABSI is a more reliable anthropometric risk indicator [11]. Moreover, Ikeue et al. found in a study of 188 overweight/obese patients that, compared to BMI, WC, and other indices, ABSI could more effectively identify obese individuals at high cardiovascular risk with metabolic abnormalities and muscle loss, independently of body weight [12]. Additionally, a cross-sectional study by Li et al. which included 3,140 individuals aged 40–80 from the general population, revealed that ABSI outperformed traditional indices such as BMI and WC in predicting the risk of abdominal aortic calcification [8]. ABSI demonstrated stronger independence and discriminatory power, allowing for stable and effective identification of high-risk individuals across diverse populations. These findings suggest that ABSI may be superior to BMI and WC in predicting the risk and prognosis of certain diseases. Therefore, exploring the associations between ABSI and DKD, albuminuria, and low estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) may not only further validate the utility of ABSI in renal risk assessment but also offer a novel tool and perspective for the early identification of high-risk individuals for kidney damage among patients with diabetes.

Given these findings, this study aimed to explore the associations between ABSI and DKD, as well as its potential links to albuminuria and low eGFR, using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). By evaluating the relationship between ABSI and early markers of renal impairment, we sought to assess the potential of ABSI as a noninvasive, accessible tool for early identification of high-risk individuals for kidney damage among patients with diabetes.

Materials and methods

Study population

Data from the NHANES database (2005–2016 cycles) were analyzed, initially including 60,936 participants [13]. After applying strict exclusion criteria, 58,166 individuals were removed, resulting in a final analytical sample of 2,770 participants (Figure 1). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 20 or > 80 years (n = 27,104); (2) a history of cancer (n = 3,098); (3) pregnancy (n = 644); (4) absence of diabetes (n = 26,502); (5) missing albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) data (n = 247); (6) missing eGFR data (n = 193); (7) missing data required for ABSI calculation (n = 230); and (8) missing information on smoking status, alcohol consumption, or educational attainment (n = 150). The age range of 20–80 years was selected for the following reasons: (1) individuals under 20 years of age are undergoing ongoing developmental changes, which may limit the interpretability of ABSI in this age group; (2) NHANES codes all individuals older than 80 as “80+”, lacking precise age data and therefore reducing the accuracy of age-related subgroup or continuous analyses. The NHANES protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Definition of ABSI

In this study, anthropometric measurements including height, weight, and WC were obtained from the NHANES dataset, following standardized protocols conducted at Mobile Examination Centers (MECs). All measurements were performed by professionally trained health technicians using calibrated instruments under uniform procedures to ensure data accuracy and reproducibility. Each technician completed a two-day standardized training course that included NHANES instructional videos, live demonstrations by expert anthropometrists, and supervised practice sessions on both children and adult volunteers. Ongoing supervision and periodic expert evaluations were conducted to maintain measurement quality. Standing height was measured using a stadiometer with participants barefoot, standing upright, and with their head aligned in the Frankfort horizontal plane. Weight was recorded using a digital scale with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. WC was measured using a non-elastic measuring tape at the horizontal midpoint between the right iliac crest and the lower margin of the 12th rib, after normal expiration, with the tape snug but not compressing the skin. All data were subject to post-collection quality control, including outlier screening based on sex- and age-specific percentiles. Implausible values were flagged or excluded without imputation. To facilitate interpretation, the ABSI values in this study were scaled by multiplying the original values by 100. The calculation formula is as follows [6]: ASBI=WaistlineBMI23×Height12×100

Diagnosis of DKD, albuminuria, and low eGFR

The eGFR is a key indicator of renal function, reflecting kidney filtration capacity based on specific blood biomarkers. In this study, eGFR was calculated using the 2021 CKD-EPI equation with serum creatinine (Scr). This updated equation enhances accuracy by excluding race as a factor and incorporating refined adjustments for age and sex [14]. Low eGFR was defined as an eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, while albuminuria was identified as an ACR of ≥ 30 mg/g. CKD was diagnosed when either eGFR was < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or ACR was ≥ 30 mg/g [15]. Diabetes was defined based on one or more of the following criteria: (1) a physician-diagnosed case of diabetes; (2) use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents; (3) a two-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) result ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (4) glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% [16]. Participants diagnosed with both diabetes and CKD were classified as having DKD.

Covariates

Relevant sociodemographic and lifestyle factors were included as covariates: age, sex, race/ethnicity (Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other Race), education level (less than high school, high school diploma/General Educational Development (GED), above high school), poverty income ratio (PIR) (categorized as low income (≤ 1.3), middle income (> 1.3 to 3.5) and high income (> 3.5)), BMI, drinking status, smoking status, hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), WC, total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and HbA1c. Hypertension was defined as a prior diagnosis of hypertension or current use of antihypertensive medication. Smoking status was categorized as never/former smokers (never smoked or those who had quit for more than one year) and current smokers (actively smoking, having smoked within the past 30 days, or consuming more than two cigarettes per day after quitting). Drinking status was classified as never drinkers (lifetime alcohol consumption of fewer than 12 drinks) and current drinkers (consuming at least 12 drinks per year or more than six drinks in the past 12 months). CVD was defined as a history of heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction, or stroke. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2).

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were summarized as means ± standard deviations (SDs), while categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and chi-square tests were used to compare differences across ABSI tertiles. Multivariate logistic regression was conducted to examine the association between ABSI and DKD, albuminuria, and low eGFR across three models: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for sex, age, and race), and Model 3 (adjusted for sex, age, race, education level, PIR, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, CVD, ALT, AST, TC, and HDL-C). Sensitivity analyses were performed by categorizing ABSI into tertiles to assess the robustness of findings and compare results with trend tests. To account for within-population variability, standardized ABSI (z-scores) were computed as (ABSI–mean)/standard deviation (SD), based on the distribution of ABSI, and were applied in subsequent sensitivity analyses. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) plot was used to explore potential non-linear relationships. Subgroup analyses were conducted using interaction tests across sex, age, ethnicity, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, CVD, ALT, AST, TC, and HDL-C to evaluate heterogeneity. Missing values for continuous variables were addressed using mean imputation. All statistical analyses were conducted using the Empower software package, with two-sided P-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Participants in the highest ABSI tertile had a significantly higher prevalence of DKD, albuminuria, and low eGFR than those in the lowest tertile (p < 0.05). Significant differences across ABSI tertiles were also observed in sex, ethnicity, education level, BMI, TC, HDL-C, and ALT (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Association between ABSI and the risk of DKD, albuminuria, and low-eGFR

Table 2 presented the associations between ABSI and the risks of DKD, albuminuria, and low eGFR. In the unadjusted model, ABSI was positively associated with an increased risk of DKD (OR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.51–2.13, p < 0.001), albuminuria (OR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.46–2.09, p < 0.001), and low eGFR (OR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.61–2.49, p < 0.001). After adjusting for confounders (age, sex, race, education level, PIR, alcohol consumption, smoking, hypertension, CVD, TC, HDL-C, ALT, and AST), ABSI remained significantly associated with DKD (adjusted OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.09–1.61, p = 0.004) and albuminuria (adjusted OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.23–1.84, p < 0.001). Each unit increase in ABSI was associated with a 33% higher risk of DKD and a 50% higher risk of albuminuria. However, the association between ABSI and low eGFR was no longer statistically significant (adjusted OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.88–1.46, p = 0.344).

In sensitivity analyses using standardized ABSI (z-score), the results remained consistent. One SD increase in ABSI was associated with a 14% increased risk of DKD (adjusted OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.05–1.24, p = 0.004) and a 20% increased risk of albuminuria (adjusted OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.10–1.31, p < 0.001). The association with low eGFR remained non-significant (adjusted OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.94–1.19, p = 0.344), further supporting the robustness of the observed relationships.

When participants were categorized into tertiles based on ABSI, those in the second tertile had an increased risk of DKD (adjusted OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.01–1.53, p = 0.044), while those in the highest tertile had significantly higher risks of both DKD (adjusted OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.01–1.66, p = 0.043) and albuminuria (adjusted OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.05–1.63, p = 0.015). However, the association between ABSI and albuminuria in the second tertile, as well as the association between ABSI and low eGFR across all tertiles, were not statistically significant. Trend analysis indicated that each tertile increase in ABSI corresponded to a 29% increase in DKD risk (adjusted OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.01–1.66, p = 0.043) and a 38% increase in albuminuria risk (adjusted OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.06–1.78, p = 0.015). In contrast, the increase in low eGFR risk per tertile increase in ABSI was not statistically significant (adjusted OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 0.79–1.55, p = 0.557).

Subgroup analysis of ABSI and the risk of DKD, albuminuria, and low eGFR

Subgroup analyses were performed to assess potential effect modifications in the association between ABSI and the risks of DKD, albuminuria, and low eGFR (Table 3). A significant association between ABSI and DKD was observed among males (adjusted OR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.05–1.93, p = 0.023), participants aged > 60 years (adjusted OR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.34–2.16, p < 0.001), those with a history of alcohol consumption (adjusted OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.07–1.78, p = 0.014), nonsmokers (adjusted OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.12–1.91, p = 0.005), hypertensive individuals (adjusted OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.14–1.75, p = 0.001), those without CVD (adjusted OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.07–1.62, p = 0.008), individuals with TC < 4.5 mmol/L (adjusted OR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.26–2.27, p < 0.001), and those with HDL-C > 1 mmol/L (adjusted OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.06–1.63, p = 0.013).

Similarly, the association between ABSI and albuminuria remained significant among participants aged > 60 years (adjusted OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.37–2.26, p < 0.001), those with a history of alcohol consumption (adjusted OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.07–1.78, p = 0.014), hypertensive individuals (adjusted OR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.22–1.91, p < 0.001), those without CVD (adjusted OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.21–1.86, p < 0.001), and those with HDL-C > 1 mmol/L (adjusted OR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.24–1.95, p < 0.001). Discussion

This study found a significant linear association between ABSI and the risk of DKD and albuminuria in the U.S. community population, while no significant association was observed between ABSI and reduced eGFR. Specifically, as ABSI increased, the risks of DKD and albuminuria rose accordingly. After adjusting for potential confounders, each unit increase in ABSI was associated with a 33% higher risk of DKD and a 50% higher risk of albuminuria, whereas the 13% increase in the risk of low eGFR did not reach statistical significance. In the tertile analysis, participants in the highest ABSI group had a 24% higher risk of DKD and a 38% higher risk of albuminuria compared to those in the lowest group, while the 5% increase in low eGFR risk was not statistically significant. Further trend analysis showed that each increase in ABSI category was significantly associated with a 29% rise in DKD risk and a 38% rise in albuminuria risk, while the association with low eGFR remained non-significant. To further validate these findings, we performed sensitivity analyses using standardized ABSI (z-scores). The results remained consistent, showing significant associations of standardized ABSI with increased risks of DKD and albuminuria, but not with low eGFR. Therefore, the findings clearly indicate that ABSI is associated with DKD and early kidney injury indicators such as albuminuria, but not with reduced eGFR.

The use of tertile-based stratification in this study serves to provide a practical and interpretable framework for assessing the relationship between ABSI levels and renal outcomes. In clinical and public health contexts, classifying ABSI into categories such as tertiles can help identify subgroups at elevated risk, support risk communication, and inform early intervention strategies, especially when standardized clinical cutoffs are not yet established. This approach enables clinicians and researchers to intuitively understand how increasing ABSI levels relate to health risks within a given population, facilitating comparison and decision-making in practice. Nonetheless, we recognize that ABSI is typically reported as a z-score standardized against a reference population, especially in epidemiological applications. To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we additionally performed sensitivity analyses using z-score–standardized ABSI values. The results remained consistent with those based on tertile groupings: z-score–based ABSI showed similar associations with DKD and albuminuria, and no significant association with low eGFR. These findings reinforce the reliability of the observed relationships and support the potential clinical utility of ABSI as a screening indicator.

In addition, stratified comparisons across ABSI tertiles revealed statistically significant differences in several classical CKD-related variables, including smoking status, CVD, albuminuria, reduced eGFR, DKD, BMI, and TC. These findings suggest that individuals with higher ABSI tend to carry a greater burden of metabolic and renal risk factors. Although some absolute differences may appear small, their statistical significance indicates meaningful variations in health status across different ABSI levels. It is important to note that absolute differences—such as prevalence rates or mean values—can sometimes provide more intuitive and clinically meaningful insights than adjusted ORs. In particular, in cross-sectional population studies, even moderate ORs accompanied by large absolute differences may highlight clear and actionable health disparities at the population level. Therefore, future research evaluating the predictive utility of anthropometric indicators such as ABSI should consider not only the influence of relative measures but also the predictive value of absolute differences in health metrics.

Notably, a seemingly paradoxical phenomenon was observed in this study: ABSI was inversely associated with BMI, and participants in the highest ABSI tertile appeared to have more favorable lipid profiles in certain indicators. This pattern may be partially explained by the structure of the ABSI formula, in which BMI appears in the denominator. As a result, for a given WC, a lower BMI can lead to a higher ABSI. In other words, individuals with elevated ABSI may have relatively low body weight but disproportionately large WC, representing a body shape characterized by “normal weight with central fat accumulation”. Such individuals may not appear obese based on total body mass but may still exhibit features of central obesity. Additionally, despite having high ABSI, these individuals may not yet show overt metabolic abnormalities such as dyslipidemia or insulin resistance. This could be due to the early stage of metabolic changes, relatively healthy lifestyle behaviors, or previous medical interventions. It is also possible that some belong to emerging phenotypic subgroups such as “metabolically healthy central obesity” or “normal-weight metabolically unhealthy”. These considerations suggest that ABSI may reflect a risk profile distinct from that captured by BMI, focusing more on fat distribution—particularly abdominal fat—rather than overall body mass. This highlights the importance of evaluating body shape and fat distribution when assessing metabolic and renal health risks, rather than relying solely on traditional weight-based metrics.

Interestingly, in the subgroup analysis, the association between ABSI and DKD appeared stronger among nonsmokers (OR = 1.46) than among smokers (OR = 1.20), which may superficially suggest a protective effect of smoking. However, this phenomenon is unlikely to reflect a true biological protective role of smoking. Instead, it may be attributable to confounding factors such as differences in baseline health status, fat distribution, or metabolic profiles between smokers and nonsmokers. Additionally, potential survivor bias may exist, as individuals with severe comorbidities are more likely to have quit smoking, while current smokers may represent a relatively healthier subset. These factors could attenuate the observed association in the smoking subgroup and should be considered when interpreting stratified results. Further studies are needed to investigate these potential interactions.

A prior study demonstrated that ABSI, along with height, BMI, and hip index, constitutes the so-called “allometric quartet”—a group of anthropometric indicators that are approximately statistically independent [17]. This finding is significant because traditional indicators such as BMI and WC are often highly correlated, with correlation coefficients approaching 0.9 in both NHANES and international populations. Such high collinearity can reduce their individual explanatory power when used together in statistical models. In contrast, ABSI was derived through an allometric adjustment of waist circumference, specifically designed to minimize its correlation with BMI and height. As a result, ABSI offers a more independent representation of body shape and fat distribution, particularly abdominal obesity—features that BMI may fail to capture on its own. Taking these methodological advantages into account, we selected ABSI as the main exposure variable in this study to provide additional insight into obesity-related risks. Furthermore, ABSI has been recognized as an independent predictor of all-cause mortality [11]. A cross-sectional study of U.S. patients with diabetes also reported a linear association between ABSI and an increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [6]. Additionally, research by Wu et al. found a positive correlation between ABSI levels and hypertension risk, with the strongest association among eight anthropometric indicators [18]. Similarly, a Japanese study on atherosclerosis in diabetes concluded that ABSI is independent of BMI in reflecting visceral obesity and serves as a significant marker of atherosclerosis in these patients [19]. Furthermore, a Mendelian randomization study by Kjaergaard et al. indicated that genetically predicted ABSI was associated with albuminuria [20]. Given these established associations between ABSI and CVD, it is plausible that ABSI may influence early diabetic renal injury through CVD-related pathways, such as hemodynamic stress, endothelial dysfunction, or vascular inflammation [21–23]. Although major CVD-related confounders (e.g. hypertension, CVD history) were adjusted for in our models, potential residual confounding from unmeasured cardiovascular factors—such as arterial stiffness or subclinical atherosclerosis—may still partly mediate the observed associations. Consistent with previous studies [9,24], this study found that higher ABSI levels were associated with increased prevalence of DKD and albuminuria. However, unlike prior studies that were either region-specific or limited in scope, our research utilized nationally representative data from the NHANES cohort, included a larger diabetic population, and conducted stratified and trend analyses, thereby offering more robust evidence to support the potential utility of ABSI in identifying early renal impairment among diabetic patients.

The relationship between ABSI and DKD or renal dysfunction may be attributed to its role as an indicator of visceral obesity. Visceral fat accumulation increases intrarenal pressure, leading to glomerular hyperfiltration. Additionally, obesity enhances sodium reabsorption, activates the sympathetic nervous system and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, and elevates blood pressure. Furthermore, adipose tissue secretes inflammatory cytokines (such as interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha), which promote albuminuria and exacerbate renal fibrosis [25]. To further support the above viewpoint, we conducted a comparative analysis using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate the predictive performance of ABSI and BMI for DKD, albuminuria, and low eGFR. The results showed that BMI had no statistically significant predictive value for any of the three renal outcomes (all P-values > 0.05), whereas ABSI demonstrated statistically significant associations with all three outcomes (all P-values < 0.05). However, the area under the curve (AUC) values for ABSI ranged from 0.5 to 0.6, indicating limited discriminative power despite statistical significance. Based on these findings, we decided not to include the ROC comparison results between BMI and ABSI in the main text, as doing so might lead to overinterpretation of ABSI’s predictive capability. We recommend that future studies with larger samples and retrospective or prospective clinical cohorts further validate these findings.

The association between ABSI and DKD was significant in males but not in females. Likewise, the relationship between ABSI and DKD or albuminuria was stronger in older adults (> 60 years) than in younger individuals. These findings align with prior studies, which suggest that men are more susceptible to obesity-related metabolic complications due to differences in fat distribution, hormonal influences, and insulin sensitivity [26–29]. While estrogen and progesterone promote fat synthesis and storage in females, leading to higher subcutaneous fat accumulation, testosterone in males facilitates visceral fat deposition, increasing metabolic and cardiovascular risks. The greater proportion of subcutaneous fat in women may confer protective effects on kidney function [1,30–33].

Age-related variations in the relationship between ABSI and DKD or albuminuria may stem from declining renal function and increased metabolic susceptibility in older individuals. Aging is linked to reduced renal reserve, heightened oxidative stress, and a greater prevalence of hypertension and diabetes, all of which may exacerbate the impact of obesity on kidney health [34,35]. In contrast, younger individuals may exhibit greater physiological resilience and compensatory mechanisms that mitigate the adverse effects of elevated ABSI on renal function. Additionally, lifestyle and behavioral differences across age groups may contribute to these variations [36–39].

An unexpected finding was the absence of a significant association between ABSI and low eGFR in the adjusted models. Given previous studies suggesting a strong link between central obesity and renal function decline [40–43], further research is needed. One possibility is that ABSI primarily reflects metabolic dysfunction and early-stage kidney impairment, such as albuminuria, rather than advanced kidney disease characterized by low eGFR. This discrepancy may indicate that the underlying mechanisms through which ABSI contributes to renal injury are more relevant to early pathological changes—such as glomerular hyperfiltration, endothelial dysfunction, or inflammatory damage—than to later-stage structural decline in filtration capacity [44,45]. ABSI may capture the metabolic and inflammatory burden of visceral adiposity that manifests initially as microalbuminuria, while measurable reductions in eGFR may arise at a more advanced phase due to multifactorial insults [19,46]. Therefore, future studies integrating renal imaging or pathological markers could help elucidate the structural and temporal dynamics of ABSI-associated kidney injury.

The above evidence suggests that ABSI holds potential clinical utility for the early identification of high-risk individuals with early kidney injury among patients with diabetes. Its calculation is based on routine anthropometric parameters—height, weight, and WC—making it simple, noninvasive, low-cost, and suitable for widespread implementation in routine physical examinations and primary healthcare settings [6]. As a body shape assessment tool that integrates WC relative to height and weight, ABSI is considered to more accurately reflect central obesity, thereby offering a preliminary estimation of metabolic burden without the need for imaging-based evaluation [6,47]. Since central obesity is closely associated with early diabetic kidney injury, such as albuminuria, ABSI may serve as a practical screening index for early DKD risk, facilitating the identification of at-risk individuals even before clinical symptoms emerge [48,49]. Moreover, ABSI has shown stable applicability across different sexes, age groups, and ethnic populations, demonstrating strong generalizability and supporting personalized management, early intervention, and longitudinal follow-up in diabetic patients. Future studies may further explore its integration into clinical risk prediction models to improve the efficiency and cost-effectiveness of chronic kidney disease screening.

In addition to these findings, this study offers several novel insights that contribute to the existing body of evidence. First, to our knowledge, this is one of the few studies to systematically examine the associations between ABSI and DKD, albuminuria, and low eGFR in a large, nationally representative sample of diabetic individuals in the United States. Previous studies on this topic were limited by regional populations or smaller sample sizes. Second, by conducting stratified analyses and trend assessments across ABSI tertiles, this study further clarified the dose–response relationship between ABSI and early renal injury markers, particularly albuminuria. Third, the use of rigorous multivariable models adjusting for a broad range of sociodemographic, metabolic, and cardiovascular factors enhanced the robustness of the observed associations, suggesting that ABSI may reflect additional risk pathways not captured by traditional obesity indices. These findings support the potential utility of ABSI as a noninvasive and practical tool for identifying individuals at higher risk for early-stage DKD in clinical and public health settings.

This study had several limitations. First, due to its cross-sectional design, it was not possible to establish causal relationships between ABSI and DKD, albuminuria, or low eGFR. Second, although prognostic analysis could enhance the clinical value of this study—particularly in evaluating the predictive ability of ABSI for the development of DKD—the primary objective was to explore the associations between ABSI and DKD, albuminuria, and low eGFR in patients with diabetes, rather than to predict future outcomes. Therefore, prognostic analysis was not included. In addition, the NHANES database lacks long-term follow-up information specific to diabetes (e.g. DKD progression or initiation of renal replacement therapy), which limits our ability to assess outcome trajectories or conduct survival analysis. Third, this study found that ABSI was significantly associated with DKD and albuminuria, but not with low eGFR. This differential association suggests that the observed relationship between ABSI and DKD may be primarily driven by albuminuria. The diagnosis of DKD in this study was based on a combination of albuminuria and reduced eGFR rather than biopsy-proven pathological criteria, which may lead to potential misclassification. Moreover, given the absence of data on renal biopsy or imaging-based etiology in NHANES, it was not possible to definitively exclude other causes of CKD such as hypertensive nephropathy or glomerular disease. While the use of a composite outcome like DKD introduces some complexity due to the heterogeneous nature of its components, we retained DKD in the analysis for several reasons: it remains a widely used clinical definition for diabetic kidney involvement, and including it enhances the applicability of our findings to real-world clinical settings. Additionally, separate analyses were conducted for albuminuria and low eGFR to provide more nuanced interpretation and confirm that the association with DKD largely reflects the relationship with albuminuria. Therefore, the observed associations between ABSI and DKD may have been partially influenced by non-diabetic CKD cases, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, although we adjusted for a wide range of covariates in the multivariable analysis, including major cardiovascular-related confounders such as hypertension and history of CVD, there may still be residual confounding from unmeasured variables. These could include arterial stiffness, levels of inflammatory biomarkers, or underlying vascular dysfunction, which may act as mediators or modifiers in the relationship between ABSI and DKD. Future studies should consider incorporating more comprehensive cardiovascular indicators and applying methods such as mediation analysis or structural equation modeling to further explore these potential pathways.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that ABSI is an important predictor of DKD and albuminuria, but not a predictor of low eGFR in the U.S. population. Future prospective studies should investigate the mechanisms linking ABSI to DKD and albuminuria, and evaluate its clinical utility across diverse populations.
