Electrocatalysis and Photoelectrocatalysis: Perspective from Physicochemical Mechanisms to Applications
Authors: Xing-Liang Xu, Liang Xu, Isao Watanabe, Lingbo Xiao, Xing’ao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrocatalytic and photoelectrocatalytic technologies, which enable the direct conversion of renewable electricity and solar energy into storable and high-value chemicals, have emerged as key research frontiers in addressing the global energy crisis and environmental challenges. This review aims to systematically elucidate recent advances in the field, insights from fundamental physical mechanisms to practical applications. First, we provide an in-depth analysis of the core physicochemical principles governing catalytic processes, including electronic structure, band engineering, interfacial charge transfer kinetics, and key reaction pathways. Second, we highlight the advanced characterization techniques for probing the dynamic behavior of catalysts, such as in situ electron microscopy, X-ray absorption spectroscopy, and vibrational spectroscopy. These “eyes” offer compelling evidence for unveiling the nature of active sites and their dynamic reconstruction processes. Based on these foundational concepts, this review further showcases the applications of rationally designed catalytic materials across multiple fields: clean energy synthesis, carbon and nitrogen resource recycling and conversion, deep purification of environmental pollutants, green chemical synthesis, as well as biosensing and environmental monitoring. Finally, we preconceive a new paradigm of “intelligent catalysis research and development”, which integrates high-throughput computation, artificial intelligence, and automated experimentation. We also emphasize that advancing multiscale theoretical simulations, in situ characterization of complex systems, and system-level integration and optimization are critical steps toward practical implementation. This work not only offers a solid theoretical framework and methodology for deepening the understanding of electrocatalytic and photoelectrocatalytic processes but also outlines a roadmap for the design of next-generation intelligent catalytic systems toward sustainable development.

Introduction

The overreliance on traditional fossil fuels has led to intensified global energy consumption and environmental pollution, creating an urgent need for sustainable technologies that can convert renewable resources into valuable chemicals and enable ecological remediation. Among various candidate technologies, electrocatalysis (EC) and photoelectrocatalysis (PEC) are regarded as the most promising approaches. , In terms of the underlying physicochemical principles, the distinction between the two technologies lies primarily in their energy input and charge-generation mechanisms. Pure EC systems are driven solely by an externally applied electrical potential and can be readily coupled with renewable electricity for sustainable operation. In contrast, PEC systems utilize semiconductor photoelectrodes to harvest solar energy, where light-generated electron–hole pairs and an applied bias synergistically promote charge separation for direct solar-to-fuel conversion. Fundamentally, PEC integrates the light-harvesting capability of semiconductors with the efficient interfacial charge-transfer and redox characteristics of EC, rather than representing a simple combination of the two. Accordingly, this review discusses EC and PEC in parallel while clearly distinguishing their respective regulatory mechanisms and application scenarios throughout.

The EC and PEC technologies exhibit substantial potential for a range of critical applications, including the generation of green hydrogen via water splitting, CO2 reduction to close the anthropogenic carbon cycle, utilizing ammonia (NH3) as a carbon-free energy carrier, remediating environmental pollutants, and enabling on-site chemical sensing. − The performance, selectivity, and long-term stability of EC/PEC systems are fundamentally governed by the electronic structure, surface chemical property, and dynamic behavior of the catalytic materials. , Therefore, the rational design of advanced catalysts is of paramount importance. Historically, catalyst development has largely relied on empirical approaches or trial-and-error methods. Such strategies often involve extended development cycles, substantial resource consumption, and difficulty in establishing universal design principles, resulting in a degree of contingencies for the discovery of new catalytic materials. However, with the rapid advancement of theoretical chemistry, materials science, and characterization techniques, a profound paradigm shift is underway in catalysis research, transforming from an experience-driven trial-and-error model to a mechanism-informed, data-driven paradigm for rational design.

This paradigm is built upon two interrelated factors. First, a profound understanding of the core physicochemical principles, such as electronic band structure, interfacial charge transfer kinetics, and the adsorption energetics of reaction intermediates, can provide the theoretical foundation for material design. − Computational approaches, particularly density functional theory (DFT), play an indispensable role in predicting activity-related electronic descriptors and adsorption energetics, as well as elucidating complex reaction mechanisms. For example, DFT-guided screening has enabled the identification of catalytically active sites through electronic-structure descriptors, thereby providing valuable insights into catalyst design and optimization. Second, the advanced in situ and operando characterization techniques offer essential insights into catalytic reaction mechanisms. Methods such as in situ transmission electron microscopy, X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), and Raman spectroscopy enable real-time, atomic-scale observation of catalysts under working electrochemical or photochemical conditions. These techniques can reveal dynamic processes such as surface reconstruction, evolution of active phases, and the formation mechanisms of key reaction intermediates. , The deep integration of theoretical calculations with advanced characterizations has established a closed loop of “computational predictionexperimental validationmechanism refinementnew theoretical guidance.” This synergistic approach has greatly accelerated the fundamental understanding of catalytic processes and significantly improved the success rate and precision of new material design.

Although numerous excellent reviews have focused on specific reactions (e.g., oxygen evolution reaction), particular material classes (e.g., single-atom catalysts), or individual techniques (e.g., elemental doping) in EC/PEC, − comprehensive reviews that systematically bridge fundamental theoretical research with diverse practical applications remain relatively scarce. This review aims to fill this gap by providing an integrated perspective on the development of electrocatalytic and photoelectrocatalytic fields. First, we specify the fundamental theoretical frameworks underpinning modern catalyst research and systematically outline advanced characterization tools that offer insights into these principles. This section forms the cornerstone for the rational design of high-performance catalysts. Based on these knowledge and tools, we elucidate how to achieve applications across several key domains: (1) clean energy molecule synthesis (e.g., H2, O2); (2) environmental remediation (pollutant degradation); (3) carbon and nitrogen resource recycling (e.g., carbon dioxide reduction reaction (CO2RR), NH3 synthesis); (4) green chemical engineering (H2O2 synthesis); and (5) biosensing and environmental intelligence monitoring. Finally, we discuss future trends in the EC/PEC field, with particular emphasis on the rapidly evolving paradigm of intelligent catalysis. By integrating high-throughput computation, machine learning, and automated experimentation to establish a data-driven “design-synthesis-testing-optimization” closed loop, it can enable unprecedented high-speed exploration of the vast chemical and materials space, offering a more powerful engine to address grand challenges in energy and environmental sectors. , Through a systematic exposition ranging from microscopic physical mechanisms to macroscopic practical applications, and from scientific principles to engineering integration, this review is expected not only to provide researchers with timely knowledge updates and cross-references in the field, but also to stimulate forward-looking discussions on the future direction of catalytic science, thereby accelerating the translation of EC/PEC technologies from the laboratory to broader real-world applications.

Fundamental Principles and Characterization Techniques

Fundamental Principles

Electrocatalytic and photoelectrocatalytic processes are fundamentally governed by heterogeneous charge transfer and interfacial chemical reactions at the solid–liquid interface under externally applied fields. However, the origin of charge carriers and the underlying regulatory mechanisms differ substantially between the two systems. In purely electrocatalytic systems, charge carriers are supplied directly through the external circuit, and catalytic performance is primarily determined by the intrinsic electronic structure of the catalyst, interfacial charge-transfer kinetics, and the adsorption behavior of reactants and intermediates at active sites. In contrast, charge carriers in purely photoelectrocatalytic systems are generated by light-induced electron–hole pairs within semiconductor photoelectrodes. In addition to the interfacial redox processes shared with EC, light harvesting, photogenerated charge-carrier separation and transport, and band-structure-dependent carrier dynamics constitute equally critical factors governing PEC performance. A comprehensive understanding of both the shared interfacial processes and the distinct physicochemical principles underlying EC and PEC is essential for advancing catalyst development from empirical trial-and-error optimization toward rational, mechanism-guided design.(a) Electronic Structure and Band Engineering: Electronic-structure modulation constitutes a common fundamental design principle for both EC and PEC systems, whereas band-structure engineering is a distinctive and indispensable strategy for optimizing PEC performance. All photoelectrochemical behaviors are root in the electronic structure. In PEC, the light absorption, charge carrier generation, and separation processes in semiconductor catalysts are directly governed by the band structure. When the energy of incident photons is greater than or equal to the band gap of the semiconductor material, electrons in the valence band are excited and undergo a transition to the conduction band, simultaneously leaving holes in the valence band and forming electron–hole pairs. − The band gap width and type can determine the spectral response range and light absorption efficiency. The photogenerated electrons and holes formed in the bulk need to migrate to the semiconductor-electrolyte interface to participate in redox reactions, a process that always competes with bulk and surface recombination. Key factors influencing the migration and recombination kinetics include the carrier mobility of the material, the density of defect states, and the built-in electric field (such as that in p–n junctions or heterojunctions). As demonstrated in the field of molecular EC, precise electronic structure modulation can significantly enhance reaction kinetics by promoting key intermediates and reducing energy barriers. Therefore, a primary objective of band engineering is to spatially separate electrons and holes and suppress their recombination by modifying the band structure.For interfacial reactions in EC and PEC, the relative position of the Fermi level of the catalyst within the electrolyte is crucial. In the electrochemical equilibrium state, the Fermi level of a semiconductor or metal aligns with the redox potential of the electrolyte. For reduction reactions, such as hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and CO2RR, the conduction band minimum or Fermi level of the catalyst must be higher (more negative on the energy diagram) than the thermodynamic potential of the reaction to provide sufficient driving force for electron transfer. Conversely, for oxidation reactions, such as oxygen evolution reaction (OER), the valence band maximum or Fermi level must be lower (more positive) than the reaction potential. For instance, doping-induced electronic modulation has been investigated to shift the d-band center and optimize intermediate adsorption, thereby enhancing reaction kinetics. Similarly, defect engineering in RuO2 alters its density of states and work function, directly impacting OER activity and stability. Therefore, optimizing the alignment between a catalyst’s electronic structure and the target reaction potential is fundamental to enhancing the reaction driving force. This can be achieved by tailoring the catalyst’s work function and band-edge positions through strategies such as doping, strain engineering, and interface engineering.(b) Interfacial Charge Transfer and Reaction Kinetics: Once charge carriers migrate to the interface, the rate which they participate in electrochemical reactions is governed by interfacial charge transfer kinetics. This process, typically slower than bulk migration, often constitutes the rate-determining step for the overall reaction. The equivalent circuit of the interfacial process can be resolved using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy. In this context, the magnitude of the charge transfer resistance directly reflects the ease of interfacial electrochemical reactions. Designing highly conductive substrates, constructing strong metal–support interactions, or introducing interfacial sites that promote coupled proton/electron transfer are all effective strategies for reducing charge transfer resistance. For a multistep electrochemical reaction, the slowest step is the rate-determining one, which can be identified through experimental techniques such as Tafel analysis, reaction order determination, and kinetic isotope effect studies. For instance, the rate-determining step involves OH– deprotonation or O–O bond formation can be verified by examining the effect of pH on the reaction rate in the oxygen evolution reaction. Identifying the rate-determining step is a prerequisite for designing targeted catalytic modification strategies.(c) Adsorption Energy and Reaction Pathway Theory: The nature of catalytic reaction is the process in which the reactant molecules are adsorbed, activated, transformed, and finally desorbed at the active sites on the catalyst surface. The adsorption energies of reactants, intermediates, and products on these active sites constitute the most fundamental microscopic descriptors linking the electronic structure of a catalyst to its macroscopic performance. DFT calculation plays a pivotal role in this regard and has become an indispensable tool for clarifying catalytic mechanisms and predicting performance from the atomic scale perspective. , It can precisely calculate the most stable adsorption configuration and the corresponding Gibbs free energy (ΔG) of the reaction intermediates that located at the specific active sites of the catalyst surface. Taking the hydrogen evolution reaction as an example: DFT calculations can be used to determine the Gibbs free energy of hydrogen adsorption (ΔG H*) at a specific active site. According to the Sabatier principle, an optimal catalyst should exhibit near-thermoneutral adsorption of H (ΔG H* ≈ 0). By computing ΔG H* for a wide range of different catalyst materials or active sites and correlating these values with experimentally measured exchange current densities, the classic “volcano curve” of catalytic activity can be constructed, enabling the rapid screening of potential high-performance HER catalysts. Theoretical calculations further reveal a linear scaling relationship between the d-band center position of transition metals and ΔG H*, establishing the d-band center as an effective electronic-structure descriptor for predicting HER activity. Tong et al. used DFT calculations to further validate the established adsorption patterns of H and OH* on the (111) facet of a high-entropy alloy: H* preferentially occupies hollow sites, while OH* favors atop sites. The calculations revealed a broad distribution of adsorption free energies for H* and OH* across different sites (ΔG H*: −0.612 to −0.044 eV; ΔG OH*: −1.174 to 0.349 eV), highlighting the energetic heterogeneity of surface sites for HEA catalysts. This heterogeneity can effectively accommodate the energy requirements of different elementary steps within a multistep reaction.

Beyond conventional electronic structure descriptors, emerging evidence suggests that surface spin polarization can serve as an independent lever to tune adsorption energetics. The electronic structure determines the potential and driving force of a system for participating in reactions, the interfacial kinetics governs the reaction rate, while the adsorption energy dictates the reaction selectivity and pathway at the microscopic level. These three interconnected elements collectively form the core physicochemical framework for understanding and designing EC/PEC catalysts. Integrating advanced theoretical calculations with experimental characterization to analyze specific catalytic systems within this framework is essential for achieving performance breakthroughs.

Characterization Techniques

The performance of a catalyst is a macroscopic manifestation of its microstructure. To gain a deep understanding of the intrinsic reaction mechanisms of catalysts and ultimately guide the design of high-performance catalysts, it is essential to employ a suite of characterization techniques that systematically elucidate the “structure-property-performance” relationships across multiple dimensions. Based on the probed physicochemical information, the existing characterization techniques can be systematically classified into three categories: morphology/structure analysis, composition/valence state analysis, and mechanism/dynamic process analysis [Figure ].(a) Morphology/Structure Analysis: Structural, morphological, and phase analysis techniques primarily address the fundamental questions of “what a catalyst is” and “what it looks like”. X-ray diffraction (XRD) serves as the cornerstone for identifying the bulk crystal structure, phase composition, crystallinity, and crystallite size of material system. By analyzing diffraction patterns, the crystalline phases of a catalyst can be determined, and phase transitions during synthesis or reaction can be monitored. As shown in Figure (a), XRD confirmed the successful synthesis of the RuO2/CeO2 heterojunction and revealed lattice strain effects, including the lattice contraction in RuO2 and the lattice expansion in CeO2 induced by the heterojunction. The electron microscopy provides direct visual evidence from the macroscopic to the atomic scale: scanning electron microscopy (SEM) clearly reveals the surface morphology, pore structure, particle size, and size distribution of catalysts, with resolution reaching the micrometer or nanometer scale; While the transmission electron microscopy (TEM), especially the high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), advances observation to the atomic level, directly revealing lattice fringes, specific crystal planes, various defects, and the dispersion of nanoparticles. The advanced in situ TEM techniques further enable real-time tracking of the dynamic structural evolution of catalysts under thermal, electrical, or chemical environments. As shown in the SEM image in Figure (b), the ZnWO4 catalyst exhibited straight and slender nanorod-like morphologies, which was further confirmed by TEM imaging [Figure (c)]. The HRTEM image revealed the lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.249 nm, corresponding to the (021) crystal plane of ZnWO4 [Figure (d)]. Briefly, these techniques establish structure as the fundamental carrier of performance. Well-defined phases and clear morphologies are prerequisites for evaluating catalyst synthesis, understanding active site exposure, and correlating catalyst stability with structural evolution.(b) Composition/Valence State Analysis: Techniques for analyzing elemental composition and chemical state are dedicated to revealing the chemical composition of catalysts and their precise chemical environment. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is a core method for analyzing the elemental composition, chemical valence states, and functional groups on the outermost surface of catalysts. By precisely measuring the shifts in inner-shell electron binding energies, we can effectively distinguish the valence states of metal ions and identify the types of heteroatom doping, thereby linking surface chemical states to catalytic activity. As shown in the XPS spectra in Figure (e), the OV/(OL+OV) ratio under electric field-assisted treatment increased by 1.25% compared to that under thermal treatment, demonstrating that the applied electric field significantly enhanced the oxygen vacancy OV content in the Cu/In2O3 catalyst. This also indirectly suggested that the catalyst exhibited higher CO2 hydrogenation activity and methanol selectivity exhibited under electric field assistance. XAS can reveal the electronic and geometric structural information on specific elements utilizing synchrotron radiation sources at the local scale. Its near-edge structure directly reflects the oxidation state and orbital occupancy of the absorbing atoms, while the extended fine structure provides precise details for the type, number, distance, and disorder of coordinating atoms. This makes XAS a decisive tool for analyzing the active center structures of single-atom and cluster catalysts. The spectra in Figure (f) displayed the normalized Co K-edge XAS spectra of Co2V2O7 (CVO) and Ni-CVO samples with varying Ni doping levels. Systematic changes in the pre-edge peak intensity confirmed that Ni doping induced Jahn–Teller distortion in the CoO6 octahedra, promoting a transition of Co from a low-spin to a high-spin state. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), often coupled with electron microscopy, enables semiquantitative analysis of elemental composition in microregions and elemental mapping. It visually demonstrates the spatial distribution uniformity of various components in the catalyst. As shown in the EDS elemental maps on the right side of Figure (a), mapping analysis verified the distribution of Pd and C elements in the carbon-encapsulated structure, further confirming the Pd@C core–shell configuration of the catalyst. These techniques collectively probe the electronic-structural nature of catalytic activity and are important to understanding doping, defects, interfacial effects, and the precise tuning of catalyst performance.(c) Mechanism/Dynamic Process Analysis: Vibrational spectroscopy and optical characterization techniques probe the vibrational modes of molecular bonds and the electronic transitions of materials through the interactions between light and matter. Raman spectroscopy can provide molecular vibration information about the catalyst bulk and surface-adsorbed species. The key advantage of in situ Raman and infrared spectroscopy lies in their ability to detect and identify the key reaction generated intermediates during electrocatalytic or photoelectrocatalytic process in real time and in a nondestructive manner. , Examples include adsorbed hydrogen in the hydrogen evolution reaction, carbon monoxide (CO) or carboxylate intermediates in CO2 reduction, and hydroperoxyl intermediates in the oxygen evolution reaction. These techniques provide the most direct experimental evidence for investigating reaction pathways. Figure (b) shows the Raman spectra of intermediates observed on an Au (111) electrode at a potential of −0.3 V in LiNO3 electrolytes under different concentrations. These spectra reveal how the electrolyte concentration influences the stability of the key NO2 intermediate by modulating the interfacial behavior of cations (Li+), thereby determining the activity and pathway of the nitrate reduction reaction (NO3RR). Ultraviolet–Visible (UV–vis) absorption spectroscopy is used to evaluate the light absorption range, optical band gap, and electronic transition characteristics of semiconductor photocatalysts. Analyzing the spectra allows for the determination of band gap type and magnitude, directly guiding the band engineering design of photocatalysts. Figure (c) presents UV–vis absorption spectra, illustrating that the introduction of carbon dots (CDs) significantly enhanced the light-harvesting capability of the catalyst. This provided an ample source of photogenerated electrons for the subsequent photoelectrocatalytic nitrate reduction reaction, serving as crucial evidence for explaining its markedly enhanced photoelectrocatalytic performance. Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy directly reflects the recombination rate of photogenerated charge carriers by detecting the intensity of radiative recombination luminescence after photoexcitation. It is an important tool for assessing the charge separation efficiency of photocatalysts and investigating defect states. Figure (d) shows the PL spectra of the relevant photoanodes, demonstrating that the CoWO x cocatalyst significantly reduced charge carrier recombination losses in the BiVO4 photoanode, which was a key factor in improving its photoelectrocatalytic performance for chloride ion oxidation in seawater. These techniques can be likened to “listening to the voice” and “observing the colors” for the material, serving as a vital bridge connecting macroscopic chemical reactions with microscopic molecular processes.

(b) Composition/Valence State Analysis: Techniques for analyzing elemental composition and chemical state are dedicated to revealing the chemical composition of catalysts and their precise chemical environment. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is a core method for analyzing the elemental composition, chemical valence states, and functional groups on the outermost surface of catalysts. By precisely measuring the shifts in inner-shell electron binding energies, we can effectively distinguish the valence states of metal ions and identify the types of heteroatom doping, thereby linking surface chemical states to catalytic activity. As shown in the XPS spectra in Figure (e), the OV/(OL+OV) ratio under electric field-assisted treatment increased by 1.25% compared to that under thermal treatment, demonstrating that the applied electric field significantly enhanced the oxygen vacancy OV content in the Cu/In2O3 catalyst. This also indirectly suggested that the catalyst exhibited higher CO2 hydrogenation activity and methanol selectivity exhibited under electric field assistance. XAS can reveal the electronic and geometric structural information on specific elements utilizing synchrotron radiation sources at the local scale. Its near-edge structure directly reflects the oxidation state and orbital occupancy of the absorbing atoms, while the extended fine structure provides precise details for the type, number, distance, and disorder of coordinating atoms. This makes XAS a decisive tool for analyzing the active center structures of single-atom and cluster catalysts. The spectra in Figure (f) displayed the normalized Co K-edge XAS spectra of Co2V2O7 (CVO) and Ni-CVO samples with varying Ni doping levels. Systematic changes in the pre-edge peak intensity confirmed that Ni doping induced Jahn–Teller distortion in the CoO6 octahedra, promoting a transition of Co from a low-spin to a high-spin state. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), often coupled with electron microscopy, enables semiquantitative analysis of elemental composition in microregions and elemental mapping. It visually demonstrates the spatial distribution uniformity of various components in the catalyst. As shown in the EDS elemental maps on the right side of Figure (a), mapping analysis verified the distribution of Pd and C elements in the carbon-encapsulated structure, further confirming the Pd@C core–shell configuration of the catalyst. These techniques collectively probe the electronic-structural nature of catalytic activity and are important to understanding doping, defects, interfacial effects, and the precise tuning of catalyst performance.

(c) Mechanism/Dynamic Process Analysis: Vibrational spectroscopy and optical characterization techniques probe the vibrational modes of molecular bonds and the electronic transitions of materials through the interactions between light and matter. Raman spectroscopy can provide molecular vibration information about the catalyst bulk and surface-adsorbed species. The key advantage of in situ Raman and infrared spectroscopy lies in their ability to detect and identify the key reaction generated intermediates during electrocatalytic or photoelectrocatalytic process in real time and in a nondestructive manner. , Examples include adsorbed hydrogen in the hydrogen evolution reaction, carbon monoxide (CO) or carboxylate intermediates in CO2 reduction, and hydroperoxyl intermediates in the oxygen evolution reaction. These techniques provide the most direct experimental evidence for investigating reaction pathways. Figure (b) shows the Raman spectra of intermediates observed on an Au (111) electrode at a potential of −0.3 V in LiNO3 electrolytes under different concentrations. These spectra reveal how the electrolyte concentration influences the stability of the key NO2 intermediate by modulating the interfacial behavior of cations (Li+), thereby determining the activity and pathway of the nitrate reduction reaction (NO3RR). Ultraviolet–Visible (UV–vis) absorption spectroscopy is used to evaluate the light absorption range, optical band gap, and electronic transition characteristics of semiconductor photocatalysts. Analyzing the spectra allows for the determination of band gap type and magnitude, directly guiding the band engineering design of photocatalysts. Figure (c) presents UV–vis absorption spectra, illustrating that the introduction of carbon dots (CDs) significantly enhanced the light-harvesting capability of the catalyst. This provided an ample source of photogenerated electrons for the subsequent photoelectrocatalytic nitrate reduction reaction, serving as crucial evidence for explaining its markedly enhanced photoelectrocatalytic performance. Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy directly reflects the recombination rate of photogenerated charge carriers by detecting the intensity of radiative recombination luminescence after photoexcitation. It is an important tool for assessing the charge separation efficiency of photocatalysts and investigating defect states. Figure (d) shows the PL spectra of the relevant photoanodes, demonstrating that the CoWO x cocatalyst significantly reduced charge carrier recombination losses in the BiVO4 photoanode, which was a key factor in improving its photoelectrocatalytic performance for chloride ion oxidation in seawater. These techniques can be likened to “listening to the voice” and “observing the colors” for the material, serving as a vital bridge connecting macroscopic chemical reactions with microscopic molecular processes.

Advanced electrochemical and operando characterization techniques are specifically designed to investigate catalytic processes in situ, dynamically, and with spatiotemporal resolution under simulated or actual reaction conditions. Their goal is to establish real-time correlations between “structure” and “performance.” Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) applies a small-amplitude AC perturbation and analyzes the frequency response of the system to construct equivalent circuit models. This allows for the separation and quantification of resistances associated with different kinetic steps, such as interfacial charge transfer and mass transport, thereby indirectly revealing the rate-determining step of the overall reaction. As shown in the EIS Nyquist plot in Figure (e), Ni-PW12-1D (one-dimensional) exhibited the smallest charge transfer resistance (R ct = 129.7 Ω) among the compared catalysts. This indicated that more efficient electron transport appeared at the electrode/electrolyte interface for this catalyst. Combined with the rate-determining step (*NO → *NOH) identified by DFT calculations, it can be inferred that this superior electron transport capability helped to supply ample electrons for the proton-coupled electron transfer processes, thereby promoting the overall electrochemical nitrate reduction to NH3 reaction. Scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM) employs an ultramicroelectrode as a scanning probe to quantitatively measure and image the distribution of local electrochemical activity on a sample surface at the micrometer scale. It can directly correlate specific morphological features, such as particle edges or crystal defects, with regions of high catalytic activity. The current frontier and pinnacle of characterization technology lies in the coupling of spectroscopic and microscopic techniques under operando conditions. This involves integrating customized electrochemical or photoelectrochemical cells with Raman spectroscopy, XAS, XPS, or even transmission electron microscopy, alongside a potentiostat and light source. While applying working potentials, illuminating the sample, and monitoring current or product generation rates in real time, this setup enables the simultaneous collection of information on catalyst structure, composition, chemical state, and molecular vibrations. This synchronized, multimodal characterization strategy can unequivocally reveal the dynamic generation of active sites, the transient appearance of reaction intermediates, and the real-time coupling between microstructure and macroscopic performance. In particular, this capability has been robustly demonstrated in the study of complex catalytic systems. This strategy can serve as a “dynamic eye” that unveils the inner workings of the “catalytic black box.”

In summary, modern EC and PEC research has established a multidimensional, integrated characterization system spanning from static identification to dynamic observation, and from macroscopic averaging to atomic-scale localization. Proficient mastery and integrated application of this comprehensive “toolbox” are key to gaining a deep understanding of catalytic mechanisms, overcoming performance bottlenecks, and ultimately achieving rational catalyst design. The future trajectory is profoundly evolving toward high-throughput methods, multimodal in situ coupling, and intelligent data analysis. The fundamental principles and characterization methodologies discussed above establish the physicochemical framework for understanding how distinct rate-determining factors govern specific catalytic applications. Building upon this foundation, the following sections systematically examine their roles in different EC and PEC processes.

Applications of EC and PEC

The physicochemical principles and characterization methodologies discussed above collectively establish the foundation for rational catalyst design across diverse application scenarios. Notably, different EC and PEC reactions are governed by distinct rate-determining physicochemical factors.

For water-splitting reactions, interfacial charge-transfer kinetics and the adsorption energetics of reaction intermediates serve as key activity descriptors. Specifically, efficient hydrogen evolution requires near-thermoneutral hydrogen adsorption free energy (ΔG H* ≈ 0), whereas oxygen evolution relies on optimized binding of *OH, *O, and *OOH intermediates to overcome intrinsic scaling relationships. These requirements make electronic-structure modulation and defect engineering particularly effective strategies for tuning catalytic activity.

In environmental pollutant degradation, the generation, migration, and utilization of reactive oxygen species represent critical mechanistic steps in both electrocatalytic oxidation and photoelectrocatalytic processes. Accordingly, regulating redox potentials, charge-separation efficiency, and visible-light harvesting capability plays a decisive role in improving catalytic performance.

For CO2 reduction and NH3 synthesis, the complex multielectron transfer processes and reaction networks involving multiple intermediates necessitate accurate identification of rate-determining steps and effective stabilization of key intermediates. Advanced in situ characterization techniques are therefore essential for revealing the dynamic evolution of active sites under operating conditions.

In H2O2 electrosynthesis, selective 2e– ORR and water oxidation pathways compete with thermodynamically favored four-electron processes, making the precise regulation of oxygen adsorption configurations and suppression of O–O bond cleavage central mechanistic challenges.

In biosensing and environmental monitoring, interfacial electron-transfer kinetics between analytes and electrode surfaces, together with molecular recognition and catalytic selectivity at active sites, critically influence sensitivity, selectivity, and detection limits.

The following sections systematically discuss how these mechanistic principles guide the rational design of catalytic materials for different application domains, thereby bridging fundamental physicochemical understanding with practical performance optimization.

Clean Energy Synthesis

In the field of clean energy conversion, purely electrocatalytic water splitting is considered a relatively mature technological route with greater potential for large-scale industrial deployment, owing to its ability to achieve high current densities and efficient energy conversion. In contrast, photoelectrocatalytic water splitting focuses on direct solar-to-fuel conversion, aiming to minimize external energy input and potentially enable bias-free self-driven operation. In recent years, both approaches have witnessed significant advances in material engineering and mechanistic understanding. Through the synergistic integration of multifaceted design strategies, substantial progress has been achieved in improving catalytic activity, enhancing operational stability, and minimizing reliance on noble-metal-based catalysts. −

In terms of material morphology engineering, ultrathin two-dimensional (2D) structures and three-dimensional (3D) porous architectures exhibit distinct advantages. In EC systems, Fe-based ultrathin catalytic films achieved a turnover frequency (TOF) of 1528 h–1 in a neutral environment with an ultralow loading of 12.3 nmol cm–2 [Figure (a) and (b)]. The Ni–Co2-O hollow nanosponge (HNS) exhibited a markedly enhanced OER catalytic performance benefiting from the porous architecture and compositional synergistic effect [Figure (c)–(e)]. In the field of PEC, dimensional regulation is equally crucial: a zero-dimensional CdSe quantum dots (QDs)/graphdiyne (GDY) system enhanced hole transport efficiency through π–π interactions with achieving a Faradaic efficiency (FE) of nearly 90% [Figure (f)]. This result demonstrated that π–π interactions can effectively facilitate hole transport at low-dimensional heterointerfaces and offered a feasible strategy for boosting the charge separation efficiency of PEC photoelectrodes; a 1D ZnO nanowire exhibited an increase of 27.4% in photocurrent under strain modulation [Figure (g) and (h)]; a 2D SiHJ/GDY/NiO x photoelectrode achieved a saturated photocurrent density of 39.1 mA cm–2 [Figure (i)].

Defect engineering serves as an “activity switch” for tuning catalytic performance, exhibits tremendous potential in dynamically regulating reaction pathways. In EC, rNiCo2O4 achieved directional control over surface redox behavior through the construction of oxygen vacancies, resulting in OER activity surpassing that of the noble metal catalyst RuO2. The CoAl-LDH/GDY superhydrophilic interface established a new benchmark for OER performance [Figure (b) and (c)]. In PEC, oxygen vacancy modulation in PbCrO4 enhanced the surface trap-state density, significantly increasing charge separation efficiency [Figure (d)–(g)]. The combined photoluminescence and surface photovoltage results demonstrate that rationally introduced oxygen vacancies can function as shallow charge-trapping centers, suppressing bulk carrier recombination and enhancing PEC charge-separation efficiency. This strategy represents a typical example of defect engineering for regulating photogenerated carrier dynamics. Meanwhile, the introduction of single-atom Pt sites into α-Fe2O3 effectively suppressed the formation of deep-level defects and improved carrier mobility [Figure (h) and (i)]. In addition to defect engineering, second-sphere coordination modulation has emerged as an effective strategy to enhance oxygen EC. Li et al. demonstrated that a Sc3+-hung CoIII corrole promoted O2 activation at the CoIII center, achieving a 1.0 V decrease in ORR overpotential in acetonitrile and a high electron transfer number of 3.87 in acidic media.

In the design of noble-metal-based catalytic materials, precise regulation becomes the key issue to balance catalytic performance and material cost. At present, noble-metal-based systems remain the only one capable of simultaneously achieving long-term stability and high catalytic activity under strongly acidic and complex reaction environments, making it critically important for the fine structural tuning of such materials. , For example, the Pt atomic cluster catalyst which was confined within the 3D porous MXene support achieved the efficient alkaline HER at a low overpotential (54 mV @ 10 mA cm–2), due to the enhanced metal–support interaction by the construction of Pt–Se bridging bonds. Rh2P@NC achieved exceptional HER performance across a wide pH range by regulating proton adsorption energy through phosphorus doping [Figures (a)–(c)]. In addition, Au nanoparticles (NPs) serving as cocatalysts not only enhanced the selectivity of oxygen evolution during photoelectrochemical water oxidation but also significantly accelerated the hydrogen evolution rate in methanol-assisted photocatalytic systems. In addition to composition, the nanoscale morphology of Pd nanoparticles also governed hydrogen absorption: Roncaglia et al. showed that particle shape (icosahedral, decahedral, cuboctahedral) and internal strain critically influenced the stability of hydrogen absorption sites.

Emerging catalytic mechanisms are continuously expanding the boundaries of water-splitting technologies. The FeN4–SC-NiN4 dual-atomic sites achieved bidirectional electronic modulation by sulfur doping, establishing a “killing two birds with one stone” dual-promoting mechanism [Figure (d) and (e)]. The bidirectional electronic modulation enabled by dual atomic sites alleviates the intrinsic scaling constraints of single-atom catalysts, in which a single active site typically cannot simultaneously optimize intermediate adsorption energetics for two different reactions, offering a promising strategy for designing high-performance bifunctional electrocatalysts. Meanwhile, metal–organic framework (MOF)-derived Cu–C/N heterojunctions exhibited outstanding OER performance across a wide pH range. By integrating the concept of high-entropy materials into porous crystalline frameworks, the constructed multimetal-variate covalent organic frameworks (e.g., Quint-MMV-COF) can serve as efficient solid-phase redox mediators, simultaneously enabling high-selectivity S–S coupling and H2 production in indirect EC. Taking the hydrazine oxidation reaction as a model reaction, they revealed that polycrystalline Co with abundant grain boundaries and a lower coordination environment can facilitate the adsorption of NH2 intermediates. Experimental conditions, including electrolyte composition, cell configuration, and testing protocols, vary among the reported studies. In summary, the four core material design strategies discussed above exhibit distinct functional roles and mechanistic features in electrocatalytic and photoelectrocatalytic water-splitting systems, collectively establishing a complementary hierarchical design framework. Morphological engineering serves as a universal structural optimization strategy that enhances active-site accessibility and mass-transport efficiency in both systems, while dimensional regulation in PEC systems further influences light harvesting and photogenerated carrier migration pathways. Heterojunction engineering represents a key band-structure modulation strategy for PEC systems, primarily aimed at promoting spatial charge separation and suppressing bulk and interfacial recombination. In EC systems, by contrast, heterostructures mainly improve catalytic performance through strong metal–support interactions and interfacial electronic redistribution. Defect engineering provides a versatile modulation strategy applicable to both systems: in EC reactions, it regulates the electronic structure of active sites to optimize intermediate adsorption energetics, whereas in PEC systems it additionally tunes surface defect states and trap-state distributions to enhance charge-separation efficiency. Noble-metal-based catalysts remain performance benchmarks for long-term operation under harsh acidic environments and high current densities, whereas emerging concepts such as dual-atomic-site catalysts and high-entropy materials continue to push beyond the limitations of conventional non-noble-metal systems.

Building upon these complementary modulation strategies, water-splitting technologies for clean energy conversion are progressing toward a multidimensional and synergistic design paradigm. Nevertheless, a substantial “valley of death” remains between laboratory-scale catalyst optimization and industrial deployment, as many high-performance nanostructured catalysts demonstrated under laboratory conditions experience significant activity decay and insufficient durability when subjected to industrially relevant current densities and prolonged operation. Overcoming this challenge requires not only continued innovation in catalyst materials but also integrated advances in scalable manufacturing, reactor engineering, and techno-economic optimization. Combined with advanced in situ characterization and theoretical simulations, the deep integration and innovation of the above strategies will drive the continuous development of clean hydrogen technologies toward high efficiency, stability, and low cost.

Deep Purification of Environmental Pollutants

With the continuous advancement of industrialization and globalization, the pollution of aquatic environments caused by industrial wastewater discharge has become increasingly prominent, evolving into a persistent environmental challenge. Against this backdrop, EC and PEC, as efficient electrochemical advanced oxidation processes, have been demonstrated to effectively degrade organic pollutants in wastewater, such as rhodamine B (RhB), ciprofloxacin (CIP), and tetracycline (TC). , The two technologies are suited to different application scenarios: EC is more suitable for the high-throughput treatment of high-strength wastewater, whereas PEC is better suited to solar-driven, low-energy purification of dilute pollutants. This technology offers advantages such as environmental friendliness, cost controllability, and operational simplicity, providing a promising green solution for industrial wastewater treatment.(a) Reaction Mechanisms and Degradation Performance Enhancement: Organic pollutant degradation in EC and PEC systems proceeds through multiple reaction pathways, which can be broadly categorized into radical-mediated processes (e.g., •OH and sulfate radicals) and nonradical pathways (e.g., direct electron transfer and singlet oxygen). To enhance degradation efficiency, extensive efforts have been devoted to the rational design of catalytic materials that promote the generation of reactive species and facilitate their effective utilization. Currently, electrocatalytic and photoelectrocatalytic materials generally face issues such as limited degradation efficiency, narrow pH adaptability, and insufficient visible light utilization. As a result, research efforts are primarily focused on developing new catalysts that are efficient with exhibiting a wide pH adaptability and broad-spectrum visible light responsiveness. Overview of Non-Piezoelectric/Ferroelectric Materials for Electrocatalytic and Photoelectrocatalytic Degradation of Organic Pollutants

In summary, the three core research dimensions discussed aboveperformance enhancement, visible-light-responsive system design, and broad-pH adaptation for complex water matricesserve complementary roles in EC and PEC pollutant degradation, collectively establishing a progressive research framework that spans fundamental catalyst optimization to practical implementation. Regulation of reaction pathways to enhance degradation performance represents the fundamental design principle for both EC and PEC systems. In EC systems, catalytic activity is primarily improved through oxidant activation to generate reactive species and the construction of nonradical electron-transfer pathways. In PEC systems, efficient photogenerated charge separation coupled with interfacial redox reactions further enables the degradation of highly recalcitrant emerging contaminants, such as PFAS.

The development of visible-light-responsive catalysts and integrated catalytic systems constitutes a key strategy for reducing energy consumption and expanding the practical applicability of EC and PEC technologies. In particular, visible-light-responsive material design enhances solar-energy utilization, whereas integrated platforms, such as PEC–electroenzymatic systems, overcome the limitations of individual catalytic processes for the degradation of refractory organic pollutants. Finally, the development of catalysts with broad pH tolerance addresses the complex chemical environments encountered in real water matrices, overcoming the widespread limitation that many high-performance catalysts operate effectively only within a narrow pH window. This capability is essential for advancing EC and PEC pollutant degradation technologies from laboratory-scale demonstrations toward practical engineering applications.

Building upon these three complementary research directions, EC and PEC technologies for the deep purification of environmental pollutants have achieved significant advances in degradation kinetics, visible-light utilization, and broad pH adaptability, while mechanistic understanding of the underlying reaction pathways has continued to deepen. − In particular, the electrocatalytic and photoelectrocatalytic degradation of persistent emerging contaminants, exemplified by PFAS, has become a major research frontier. A recent review systematically summarized the corresponding reaction mechanisms, degradation pathways, and material design strategies across different catalytic systems, highlighting the expanding application potential of EC and PEC technologies in complex water matrices.

Despite these advances, a substantial “valley of death” remains between laboratory-scale catalyst development and practical wastewater treatment. High degradation efficiencies demonstrated under controlled batch conditions often fail to translate into stable long-term performance in continuous-flow systems owing to the complexity of real water matrices, progressive catalyst deactivation, and limited techno-economic viability. Moreover, most current studies are still conducted using simulated pollutants under idealized laboratory conditions. Bridging this gap will require more systematic evaluations under realistic water-treatment conditions, together with improvements in process integration, scalability, and long-term operational stability, thereby accelerating the transition of EC and PEC technologies from laboratory research to practical engineering applications.

Carbon and Nitrogen Resource Recycling and Conversion

In the field of carbon and nitrogen resource utilization, purely electrocatalytic systems are capable of operating at high current densities with high productivities, making them more suitable for large-scale industrial implementation. By contrast, photoelectrocatalytic systems emphasize solar-driven conversion under mild reaction conditions, providing a complementary pathway for distributed chemical production. The following sections summarize recent advances in both technologies for CO2 reduction and NH3 synthesis, with particular emphasis on their material design strategies and underlying catalytic mechanisms.

CO2 Reduction

With the rapid depletion of nonrenewable energy sources, the global warming issue induced by CO2 emissions has become increasingly severe. − The CO2 reduction which converts CO2 into high-value-added chemicals and fuels, can be driven by renewable energy and has emerged as a crucial pathway for achieving carbon cycling. The products of CO2 reduction are diverse and primarily classified into two categories: C1 products (such as carbon monoxide, formic acid, methane, and methanol) and multicarbon C2+ products (such as ethylene, ethanol, acetic acid, and n-propanol). Among them, C2+ products not only possess higher energy density and economic value but also serve as key precursors for synthesizing longer-chain hydrocarbon fuels.

Copper-based catalysts have been extensively studied due to the excellent catalytic activity and product selectivity in CO2 reduction. Under various types of Cu catalysts (such as Cu foil, Cu NPs, and sputtered Cu), electrolyzer configurations (e.g., H-type cells, flow cells, and membrane electrode assemblies), and electrolyte conditions, Cu atoms undergo spontaneous reconstruction during the electrocatalytic process. Liu et al. systematically revealed the ubiquitous formation, kinetic behavior, and inert characteristics of bridge-site adsorbed CO (*CObridge) during Cu reconstruction for the first time, providing molecular-level insights into the instability mechanisms of Cu catalysts in CO2/CO reduction. Recent study has shown that the reconstruction is accompanied by the formation of *CObridge, in which Raman/infrared signals appeared in the 1700–1900 cm–1 range, clearly distinct from atop-adsorbed CO (*COatop) with the range of 2000–2100 cm–1 [Figure (a) and (b)]. This operando spectroscopic observation revealed the dynamic evolution of *CObridge during catalyst reconstruction, providing direct molecular-level insights into the structural transformation and deactivation mechanisms of Cu-based CO2RR catalysts and guiding the design of highly stable CO2RR electrodes.

Notably, the competitive HER significantly reduced the FE at high potentials during CO2 reduction. Therefore, the in-depth understanding of the potential-dependent competition between CO2 reduction and HER is of great significance for designing efficient CO2 reduction catalysts. Using Cu as a model catalyst, Fu et al. systematically investigated the reaction pathways of CO2 reduction and HER at different potentials through theoretical simulations and revealed that the applied potential governed the selectivity by regulating the interfacial K+ concentration. Specifically, under low potentials, the lack of interfacial K+ can give rise to a lower energy barrier for HER, which can dominate the reaction. In contrast, intermediate potentials promote an optimal interfacial K+ environment, which stabilizes key CO2 reduction intermediates, lowers activation barriers, and simultaneously suppresses the competing HER through electrostatic interactions, thereby favoring CO2 reduction as the dominant reaction pathway. However, excessive accumulation of K+ in the inner Helmholtz plane could cause steric obstruction of the CO2 migration channels at high potentials, thereby restoring the dominance of HER [Figure (c)–(e)]. This mechanism not only clearly elucidated the experimentally observed potential-dependent antagonism between HER and CO2 reduction, but also provided a theoretical basis and strategic guidance for suppressing HER on Cu-based catalysts.

In conventional bimetallic alloy catalytic systems, the complexity of catalyst composition and geometric configuration often obscures the identification of key active sites. Du et al. regulated the distribution of host and guest metals within a Cu–In single-atom alloy catalyst, achieving pronounced modulation of the reaction pathways in the electrochemical CO2 reduction. Introducing 1% indium into a copper matrix generated isolated In–Cu interfacial sites that efficiently convert CO2 to CO, with a FE exceeding 90%. In contrast, the doping of 1% copper into an indium matrix resulted in the formation of a CuIn alloy phase, shifting the product selectivity toward formate while maintaining a similarly high FE above 90%. In another study, indium doped on the In–Cu interface can modulate the adsorption energies of the host Cu atoms, promoting CO2 adsorption and activation while weakening the binding strength of linearly bound CO (*CO), which enhanced CO selectivity. In contrast, the rigid matrix of the CuIn alloy stabilized bridge-bonded CO, favoring the formate pathway [Figure (f) and (g)]. Similarly, Huang and co-workers employed a high-pressure membrane electrode assembly equipped with an indium/copper catalyst to directly convert CO2 captured from high-pressure gas streams into ethylene with higher economic value. At a reaction pressure of 20 bar, the FE for ethylene formation reached as high as 85%, and the local current density attained 750 mA cm–2 [Figure (h)]. The high-pressure environment induced the relocation of bicarbonate formation to the catalyst-membrane interface, effectively mitigating salt precipitation and enabling the system to operate stably for more than 1500 h at a current density of 600 mA cm–2 [Figure (i)].

Given that multicarbon C2+ products possess higher energy density and economic value, the development of highly selective catalysts has become a key direction for CO2 reduction research. In this context, framework materials have been widely employed owing to their advantages of structural tunability and well-defined active sites. For example, Wan et al. constructed a copper-coordinated covalent triazine framework (CuCTF) supported on a silicon nanowire array grown on a wafer substrate, where the catalytic activity originated from the nitrogen atoms of the triazine rings and the Cu–N bipyridine coordination sites. The Si@CuCTF6 heterojunction exhibited a high selectivity of 95.6% toward multicarbon products, with an apparent quantum efficiency of 0.89% for carbon-containing products [Figure (a) and (b)].

On the other hand, significant progress has also been achieved in catalytic systems targeting C1 products such as formic acid and methanol. Zhang et al. employed the interlayer confinement effect of a 2D covalent organic framework to first achieve the controllable exposure and stabilization of the metastable Bi(101) facet during electrocatalytic CO2 reduction. The yielded catalyst delivered a formate selectivity of 98.7 ± 0.1%, a partial current density exceeding 350 mA cm–2 at −1.0 V (vs RHE) in a flow cell, and an operational stability of more than 230 h [Figure (c)–(e)]. Dong et al. developed an artificial photosynthetic system composed of a metalloporphyrin-functionalized TiO2 photocathode and a BiVO4 photoanode, enabling the efficient conversion of CO2 and H2O into methanol accompanied by oxygen evolution. Li et al. prepared an amorphous Bi–O/metallic Bi0 composite nanofilm via an anodic oxidation-electroreduction strategy, and it was found that the adsorption-activation capability of the Bi–O structure synergized with the electronic conductivity of Bi0, significantly enhancing the formate production efficiency.

Although the C1 pathway has been relatively well established, achieving high selectivity toward C2+ products remains highly challenging. The flower-like structural photocathode Pd/R/ZrN/MoS2-2 constructed by Ullah et al. achieved a C2+ product selectivity of 80.99%, with the key intermediates such as COO–, CO, CHO, and COCHO [Figure (f)]. In another work, the CuO-MoO3/TiO2 nanotube composite photoelectrode developed by Li and co-workers enabled the conversion of CO2 to ethanol in a bicarbonate electrolyte under visible-light irradiation and 0.3 V vs SCE, and, the FE for ethanol reached 89% at 0.5 V vs SCE (SCE = saturated calomel electrode). In addition, Liu et al. developed the Cu2O/TiO2/Ti3C2 composite that was served as a photocathode, delivering a stable photocurrent density of 6 mA cm–2 under illumination with the ethanol and methanol production rates of 960.5 and 1276.2 μmol g–1 h–1, respectively, and achieving an ethanol selectivity as high as 60%.

For interfacial cation engineering in CO2RR, in addition to catalyst design, the electrolyte environment, particularly the arrangement of cations at the electrode/electrolyte interface, has emerged as a critical factor governing CO2 reduction kinetics. McGregor et al. systematically investigated phosphonium-based geminal dications [C n (P mmm )2]2+ to decouple the effects of cation-electrode distance and interfacial cation density on CO2RR over Ag in aprotic acetonitrile. They found that the smaller and more densely packed cations induced stronger interfacial electric fields, which reduced the activation barrier for CO2 adsorption and increased reaction rates. This work underscored that both the vertical and lateral positioning of cations within the electrical double layer independently affect reactivity, providing design principles for next-generation electrolytes through precise control of cation geometry and packing.

In summary, the four research dimensions discussed aboveregulation of catalyst structural evolution, bimetallic active-site engineering, product-oriented system design, and electrolyte cation engineeringestablish a multilevel modulation framework spanning intrinsic catalyst properties to interfacial reaction environments, collectively advancing EC and PEC CO2 reduction technologies. Studies on Cu-based catalyst reconstruction and competitive HER suppression provide the mechanistic foundation by elucidating the relationships among *CO adsorption configurations, Cu surface restructuring, and potential-dependent competition between CO2RR and HER, thereby guiding the rational suppression of side reactions.

Bimetallic catalyst design represents a key strategy for precise product-selectivity regulation, enabling selective control over C1 products (carbon monoxide, formate) through tailored metal-site configurations and interfacial electronic interactions. For product-oriented catalyst development, C1 systems (formate, methanol) based on Bi catalysts and framework materials have achieved relatively high maturity with near-unity selectivity, whereas C2+ product generation remains a major frontier. Although Cu-based catalysts and PEC photocathodes have demonstrated promising potential, challenges associated with selectivity control and long-term stability remain unresolved. Beyond catalyst engineering, electrolyte cation regulation provides an additional interfacial modulation strategy by tuning electric double-layer structures and intermediate stabilization, thereby expanding the design space for efficient CO2 conversion.

Supported by these multidimensional optimization strategies, CO2 reduction for carbon resource utilization has achieved significant advances in C1 product synthesis, with catalytic systems for single-carbon products such as formate and methanol approaching a relatively high level of maturity. However, the efficient generation of C2+ products remains highly challenging due to difficulties in selectivity control, complex multistep reaction pathways, and insufficient stabilization of key intermediates. From the perspective of industrial translation, a substantial “valley of death” persists between laboratory-scale catalyst optimization and practical CO2 reduction deployment. Although C1 production systems have progressed toward pilot-scale demonstration, C2+ synthesis remains largely confined to bench-scale studies, limited by performance losses during scale-up, poor tolerance toward impure CO2 feedstocks, and insufficient techno-economic competitiveness.

Future research efforts should focus on several key directions. First, in-depth investigations of C–C coupling mechanisms and the dynamic evolution of key intermediates are needed to provide mechanistic guidance for the rational design of advanced catalysts. Second, the development of emerging catalytic systems, including bimetallic atomic catalysts and heavy-metal-free quantum dots, − should be pursued to enhance C2+ product selectivity through precise regulation of active-site electronic structures and local microenvironments. Third, optimization of reactor architectures and electrolysis conditions is essential for suppressing side reactions and improving mass and charge transport, thereby enabling efficient and stable CO2 conversion toward multicarbon products. Ultimately, only through multiscale and integrated innovations spanning catalyst design, interfacial regulation, and reactor engineering can CO2 reduction technologies advance toward sustainable production of value-added chemicals with reduced carbon footprints.

NH3 Synthesis

As the cornerstone of the industrial NH3 synthesis, the Haber-Bosch process has not only facilitated the large-scale proliferation of fertilizers but also profoundly transformed traditional agricultural practices since its inception in the early 20th century, standing as a significant milestone in modern chemical industry. However, this process relies on high temperatures and high pressures as well as fossil fuels, accompanied by high energy consumption and significant carbon emissions [Figure (a) and (b)]. In terms of the backdrop of global sustainable development and energy transition, emerging pathways such as electrocatalytic and photoelectrocatalytic nitrogen reduction reaction (NRR), driven by electrical energy, light energy, or their couplings, have attracted widespread attention. − These technologies hold the promise of enabling green NH3 synthesis under ambient temperature and pressure, facilitating the synergistic application of renewable energy and novel catalytic materials, thus providing new pathways for sustainable NH3 production. Currently, researchers are dedicated to gaining in-depth insights into the underlying reaction mechanisms, optimizing material systems and enhancing conversion efficiency to overcome technical bottlenecks and advance green NH3 synthesis technologies toward practical applications.

Notably, recent advances have demonstrated an alternative pathway that bypasses the challenging NN bond activation by converting N2 to NO x – intermediates before reduction to NH3. This two-step approach effectively bridged the gap between N2 activation and nitrate reduction, offering a new paradigm for sustainable NH3 synthesis.

Although iron-based catalysts exhibit considerable potential in the field of electrocatalytic nitrate reduction for NH3 synthesis, their practical application is often hampered by limitations such as low NH3 selectivity and insufficient stability under high current densities. In addition, the specific mechanism of alumina which is a key promoter in iron-based NH3 synthesis catalysts, remains unclear. The electrocatalytic nitrate reduction reaction not only enables the resource treatment of nitrate-containing wastewater but also provides a crucial pathway for sustainable NH3 synthesis. Furthermore, the conversion of carbon-containing precursors (e.g., CO2, CH3OH) and nitrogen-containing species (e.g., N2, nitrate, and NH3) into high-value-added organic nitrogen compounds (e.g., urea, amides, and amino acids) via electrocatalytic or photocatalytic C–N coupling reactions also exhibits broad application prospects. For instance, Chen et al. recently demonstrated that Co3O4 nanoneedles with tip-induced local electric fields enable the efficient C–N coupling between CO2 and NO3 –, achieving a urea yield rate of 49.63 μmol h–1 cm–2 with a FE of 21.37%. Using an electrocatalytic oxidative coupling strategy, Yang et al. synthesized symmetrical and asymmetrical urea derivatives via CO and amines on Pt, yielding up to 77% FE for 1,3-di-tert-butylurea and enabling the synthesis of pharmaceutically relevant compounds, such as pimavanserin.

Such pathway, which is driven by renewable energy, aligns with the principles of green chemistry and the circular economy, and has attracted increasing attention in recent years. , Concurrently, NH3, which is recognized as an ideal hydrogen energy carrier due to the ease of liquefaction, storage, and transportation, has drawn great attention. On the other hand, the selective nitrate reduction to N2, rather than NH3, is also important for environmental remediation. Ding et al. developed sulfur-doped zerovalent iron aerogels that regulate *NO coupling and *H supply, achieving >90% N2 selectivity from nitrate-contaminated water and offering a sustainable solution for water treatment. In contrast, the reverse reaction of NH3 oxidation is required to release the stored energy for energy applications. Beyond NH3 synthesis, its electrocatalytic oxidation is also of great significance for energy release. For instance, Xiong et al. recently demonstrated a Ru-based molecular catalyst with a hybrid carboxylate-sulfonate ligand that enabled efficient NH3 oxidation to N2, offering new insights into catalyst design for NH3-based energy systems.

In summary, the research advances discussed above can be categorized into three core dimensionsreaction pathway innovation, intrinsic catalyst performance regulation, and function-oriented system expansioncollectively establishing a coherent development framework from fundamental mechanistic breakthroughs to diversified application scenarios. Reaction pathway engineering represents a key strategy for overcoming the intrinsic challenge of NN bond activation. Although direct N2 reduction operates under ambient conditions, it is still limited by sluggish kinetics and insufficient selectivity. In contrast, two-step tandem pathways (N2 → NO x – → NH3) bypass the direct cleavage of the highly stable NN bond and achieve substantially improved FE, providing a new paradigm for sustainable NH3 synthesis.

Optimization of catalyst performance, particularly in iron-based nitrate reduction systems, relies on synergistic strategies including defect engineering, electronic structure modulation, and high-entropy component design to simultaneously enhance NH3 selectivity, catalytic activity, and long-term stability under high current densities. Beyond catalyst-level optimization, function-oriented system expansion further extends the scope of nitrogen conversion technologies, encompassing value-added organic nitrogen synthesis through C–N coupling, nitrate remediation coupled with selective N2 production, and energy conversion via NH3 oxidation. These advances collectively enrich the technological framework of the artificial nitrogen cycle.

Taken together, these multidimensional research efforts have substantially expanded the technological landscape of EC and PEC nitrogen conversion. Despite significant advances in reaction pathway engineering and catalyst design, a pronounced translational gap remains between laboratory-scale proof-of-concept demonstrations and industrial implementation. This “valley of death” in catalytic technology translation arises from the difficulty of maintaining high performance achieved in small-scale laboratory reactors during system scale-up, as enlarged reactors often introduce intensified mass-transfer limitations, reduced tolerance toward impurities in realistic feedstocks, and diminished techno-economic viability compared with established industrial processes. Beyond these engineering and economic challenges, fundamental mechanistic uncertainties in nitrogen activation, intermediate transformation, and reaction pathway evolution remain major obstacles to further breakthroughs in nitrogen conversion technologies. In particular, the catalytic mechanisms governing NH3 decomposition toward hydrogen production require deeper investigation to enable the rational design of efficient and stable catalysts.

Green Chemical Synthesis

For green chemical synthesis, exemplified by H2O2 production, electrocatalytic and photoelectrocatalytic routes exhibit distinct technological advantages: the former emphasizes high productivity and scalability toward practical applications, whereas the latter focuses on solar-driven, energy-efficient operation. Electrocatalytic/photoelectrocatalytic H2O2 synthesis has emerged as a sustainable alternative to the conventional anthraquinone process, owing to its mild operating conditions, high reaction tunability, and potential integration with renewable energy sources. Both approaches enable H2O2 production through the two-electron oxygen reduction reaction (2e– ORR) and two-electron water oxidation reaction (2e– WOR) pathways, respectively, with substantial progress achieved in catalyst design, reactor engineering, and system integration.

In the field of EC, significant breakthroughs have been achieved in catalyst design and development. The Ni3N/MXene catalyst developed by Liu et al. underwent surface reconstruction during the electrocatalytic process with forming an active NiOOH layer. The yielded H2O2 production rate was 10.7 mol h–1 g–1 at a current density of 300 mA cm–2 with a FE of 92.0%, and it could maintain stable operation for over 100 h [Figure (a) and (b)]. The Ni2P-NiMoO x heterojunction catalyst constructed by Zhang et al. through interface engineering showed exceptional performance in an H-type electrolysis cell, yielding an H2O2 production rate of 4009 mmol g–1 h–1 and maintaining a selectivity exceeding 92% over a wide potential range [Figure (c) and (d)]. This exceptional performance can be attributed to interface-engineering-induced electron redistribution, which optimizes *OOH adsorption energetics and suppresses O–O bond cleavage, thereby contributing to the high 2e– ORR selectivity observed over a broad potential range. Notably, the performance evaluation was conducted in an H-type electrolysis cell, whose mass-transport characteristics and catalyst loading conditions differ from those of rotating disk electrode measurements. Such differences in reactor configuration and testing protocols may partially account for the relatively high mass-normalized H2O2 production rate. Separately, the Ni-PTC 2D conductive MOF nanoribbons developed by Feng et al. achieved an H2O2 production rate of 11.96 mol g–1 h–1 in a flow cell, producing an H2O2 solution concentration of 24.93 mmol L–1 at a current density of 50 mA cm–2 [Figure (e) and (f)].

The PEC system has significantly enhanced H2O2 synthesis efficiency through ingenious structural design. The ZnO-coated BiVO4 photoanode developed by Qu et al. significantly enhanced both the selectivity and production rate of H2O2 within the potential range of 1.0–2.0 V vs RHE. Photoelectrochemical tests revealed that the ZnO overlayer could flatten band bending and suppress the competitive oxygen evolution reaction. The S-scheme heterojunction of 1T-2H MoSe2/TiO2 nanorod arrays constructed by Zhang et al. enhanced the H2O2 production rate to 4.7 times that of the original TiO2 [Figure (c)]. Research has indicated that the 1T metallic phase MoSe2 serves as a charge transport bridge, playing a crucial role in promoting charge separation.

In particular, advanced Z-scheme heterojunctions have emerged as a rapidly developing frontier in H2O2 synthesis, as they enable efficient spatial charge separation while preserving the strong redox capabilities of individual components. Combined with precise nanoscale morphological engineering and modulation of local coordination environments, these rationally designed systems have achieved substantial improvements in catalytic activity and 2e– ORR selectivity. For example, Lu et al. constructed a Z-scheme I–/K+ codoped g-C3N4/NH2-UiO-66 heterojunction through electrostatic self-assembly. The synergistic effects of ion doping and heterojunction formation promoted Z-scheme charge-transfer pathways and suppressed carrier recombination, achieving an H2O2 production rate of 13.3 mM g–1 h–1 in pure water under visible-light irradiation, with an apparent quantum yield of 10.28% at 420 nm. More recently, Gao et al. developed a defect-engineered Z-scheme heterostructure by in situ growth of sulfur-deficient ZnIn2S4 nanosheets on UiO-66-NH2. This dual-modulation strategy integrated two complementary mechanisms: the Z-scheme pathway facilitated interfacial charge separation, while sulfur vacancies regulated the local coordination environment of active sites and promoted a Pauling-type O2 adsorption configuration, thereby enhancing 2e– ORR selectivity. The resulting catalyst achieved an H2O2 production rate of 3200 μmol g–1 h–1 with 94.3% selectivity in pure water under ambient air, demonstrating the potential of refined Z-scheme catalyst design for high-performance solar-driven H2O2 synthesis.

In the realm of photocathode materials, the pS-DBT photocathode reported by Chang et al. demonstrated over 90% 2e– ORR selectivity in alkaline solution. Under simulated visible light irradiation, the H2O2 concentration accumulated to 123 mmol L–1 (≈0.4 wt %) after 13 h of reaction, presenting a 20% enhancement compared to the previously state-of-the-art pTTh photocathode [Figure (d)]. The TaON/Ta3N5/CuI/Cu electrode developed by Wang et al. achieved an H2O2 production rate of 193.0 μmol L–1 at 1.23 V vs NHE, with solar-to-chemical conversion efficiency and apparent quantum efficiency values of 0.09% and 21.6%, respectively [Figure (e) and (f)].

In terms of system integration, several studies have exhibited innovative reactor designs. Morales et al. reported a paired (photo)­electrosynthesis system and achieved an overall energy efficiency of 7.05 mmol H2O2 Wh–1 with a FE as high as 132.4% by utilizing a SO4 2– electrolyte under blue light irradiation and at a cell voltage of 3.5 V [Figure (g)]. Li et al. applied Co/N codoped carbon nanofibers to a zinc-air battery, which maintained 93.5% of the initial activity after operating for 24 h at 20 mA cm–2, achieving a peak power density of 179 mW cm–2 [Figure (h)]. In addition, Kim et al. developed a bias-free PEC platform which successfully coupled the reduction of N2 to NH3 with water oxidation to H2O2/O2, enabling the simultaneous synthesis of valuable chemicals from nitrogen, water, and sunlight.

In the exploration of sustainable reaction media, the NiPS3 nanosheet electrocatalyst developed by Zhang et al. manifested approximately 98% selectivity for the 2e– ORR pathway, an H2O2 production rate of 6.0 mol gcat –1 h–1, and stable performance maintained for over 1000 h in simulated seawater [Figure (i)–(k)]. Mechanism analysis revealed that the S2– framework effectively repelled Cl– ions, while the Ni2+ sites conferred a moderate binding strength to the *OOH intermediate.

In mechanism investigations, the Fe–H4thb coordination polymer prepared by Liang et al. through iron site oxidation state regulation revealed that the planar Fe–O4 coordination structure significantly enhanced electron delocalization at the iron center, effectively promoting the chemical adsorption and activation processes of oxygen molecules. Li et al. reviewed the fundamental mechanisms of photocatalytic and electrocatalytic H2O2 synthesis. Starting from key strategies such as modulating oxygen adsorption configurations and suppressing competing side reactions, they provided an in-depth discussion on the advantages of single-atom catalysts for the selective H2O2 synthesis. These studies offer crucial theoretical foundations for understanding and optimizing the selectivity of H2O2 production, based on the underlying mechanisms of electronic structure and reaction pathways. Beyond the synthesis of small molecules like H2O2, PEC has also emerged as a powerful platform for constructing complex molecular architectures. Expanding the scope of green synthesis to asymmetric transformations, Peng et al. reported an electricity-enabled dual Co/pyridoxal catalysis inspired by vitamin B6-dependent enzymes. This system enabled highly enantioselective construction of quaternary amino esters via radical intermediates, demonstrating the power of electrochemistry in stereoselective organic synthesis. Expanding the scope of green synthesis to biomass valorization, Li et al. further demonstrated that high-spin cobalt oxyhydroxide (h-CoOOH) significantly enhanced the electrocatalytic oxidation of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural to 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid. The high-spin Co3+ configuration facilitated Co4+O formation, accelerating the nucleophilic oxidation kinetics and achieving >98% FE. In summary, recent advances in EC/PEC H2O2 synthesis can be categorized into three core dimensions: catalyst selectivity regulation, photoelectrocatalytic heterojunction engineering, and system integration for practical applications. Catalyst selectivity regulation constitutes the fundamental basis of both EC and PEC systems. In EC H2O2 synthesis, electronic structure modulation, interface engineering, and ligand design optimize the adsorption energetics of the *OOH intermediate, thereby promoting the selective 2e– ORR pathway while suppressing the competing four-electron reaction. Meanwhile, machine-learning-assisted catalyst screening is accelerating the discovery and optimization of high-performance catalytic materials.

PEC heterojunction engineering represents the central strategy for enhancing solar-to-chemical conversion efficiency. Rationally designed S-scheme and Z-scheme heterojunctions facilitate efficient spatial separation of photogenerated charge carriers, suppress charge recombination and competing oxygen evolution, and thereby markedly improve visible-light-driven H2O2 production. Beyond catalyst design, system integration further extends the practical applicability of EC and PEC H2O2 synthesis. Strategies including paired electrosynthesis, bias-free PEC systems, and seawater-compatible catalysts enhance energy efficiency and operational feasibility, while emerging application scenarios, such as biomass valorization, further broaden the scope of sustainable H2O2 production.

Taken together, these multilevel optimization strategies have collectively advanced the development of EC and PEC technologies for sustainable H2O2 synthesis. Recent progress demonstrates that rational catalyst engineering, reactor optimization, and system integration have substantially improved the efficiency, selectivity, and operational stability of H2O2 production, while emerging data-driven approaches such as machine learning are further accelerating catalyst discovery and optimization.

Despite these advances, a substantial “valley of death” remains between laboratory-scale proof-of-concept demonstrations and industrial commercialization. Most reported EC and PEC systems still produce relatively dilute H2O2 solutions in small-volume batch reactors, making downstream separation and concentration energy-intensive and thereby diminishing the economic advantages of green production routes. In addition, the long-term durability and productivity required for continuous industrial operation have yet to be convincingly demonstrated.

Future research should therefore place greater emphasis on the integration of advanced catalyst design, scalable reactor engineering, and process optimization under realistic operating conditions. Meanwhile, machine-learning-assisted materials discovery and performance prediction are expected to play an increasingly important role by accelerating the identification of high-performance catalysts, optimizing complex catalytic systems, and guiding the rational development of next-generation EC and PEC technologies for practical H2O2 production.

Biosensing and Environmental Monitoring

In biosensing and environmental monitoring, electrochemical sensors offer straightforward device architectures and broad applicability, whereas photoelectrochemical sensors provide superior sensitivity for trace-level detection owing to their intrinsically low background signals. The rational design of high-performance electrode materials has greatly expanded the capabilities of both electrochemical and photoelectrochemical sensing platforms, enabling applications ranging from wearable electronics and environmental monitoring to medical diagnostics. Despite these advances, constructing electrode interfaces that simultaneously achieve high sensitivity and high selectivity remains a major challenge because of the complexity of real sample matrices and the ultralow concentrations of target analytes. Consequently, the rational design of functional electrode materials and the establishment of quantitative structure–activity relationships have become critical prerequisites for the further development of high-performance electrochemical and photoelectrochemical sensing technologies.

To address core challenges such as low sensitivity, poor selectivity, and insufficient stability, researchers are committed to developing novel electrode materials with superior catalytic performance. − Nanomaterials have attracted extensive attention in the fields of sensing and environmental monitoring thanks to the unique physicochemical properties. Among them, carbon-based nanomaterials (such as carbon nanotubes, graphene derivatives, and carbon nanofibers) show significant application potential owing to their high specific surface area, excellent electrical conductivity, and good biocompatibility. Compared to conventional heterostructures, nanostructured materials often enable lower detection limits. For instance, the Au/P-CN heterojunction constructed by Rafique et al. exhibited a linear range of 0.1–52.1 μM and a detection limit of 0.02 μM for detecting the toxic pollutant 4-chlorophenol [Figure (a)]. The CsPb2Br5/CdSe p-n heterojunction developed by Zhao et al. achieved a linear range of 0.4–303.9 μM and a detection limit as low as 0.0124 μM for dopamine detection [Figure (b)]. In addition, Dhanasekaran et al. synthesized a Ag-embedded hollow polyaniline-enhanced nickel–bismuth oxychloride electrocatalyst, which achieved ultralow detection limits of 126 pM and 2.3 nM for the dual-component detection of 4-nitrophenol and uric acid, respectively, with sensitivities of 8.251 and 4.92 μA μM–1 cm–2, and linear ranges of 0.001–0.011 and 0.01–1.31 μM [Figure (c) and (d)]. The Ag-embedded hollow architecture provided abundant accessible active sites and promoted the preconcentration of target analytes, thereby contributing to picomolar-level detection limits that surpass those of many previously reported sensors. It is noteworthy that such catalyst has been successfully applied to the analysis of practical samples, including 4-nitrophenol in rainwater and tap water, as well as uric acid in urine and serum, exhibiting considerable practical utility.

In summary, recent advances in electrochemical and photoelectrochemical biosensing and environmental monitoring can be categorized into three major research dimensions: nanostructured electrode engineering, interfacial property regulation, and validation under practical sensing conditions. Nanostructured electrode engineering provides the foundation for performance enhancement. High-surface-area carbon materials, heterojunctions, and composite catalysts increase the density of accessible active sites, enhance target-analyte enrichment, and improve electrocatalytic activity, thereby lowering detection limits and broadening the linear detection range of both electrochemical and photoelectrochemical sensors. Interfacial engineering and atomic-level active-site regulation represent the central strategy for overcoming the trade-off between sensitivity and selectivity. Optimized electrode–analyte interactions accelerate interfacial charge transfer, while single-atom and cluster catalysts with well-defined active sites further enhance detection selectivity, sensitivity, and operational stability. Finally, validation under practical sensing conditions extends the applicability of electrochemical and photoelectrochemical sensing technologies to biomolecule detection, environmental contaminant monitoring, and real-sample analysis, thereby facilitating their practical deployment in healthcare, environmental surveillance, and agricultural safety.

Taken together, these multilevel optimization strategies have substantially advanced the performance and practical applicability of electrochemical and photoelectrochemical sensing platforms. Significant progress has been achieved in developing sensors with enhanced sensitivity, selectivity, and operational stability, enabling increasingly broad applications in precision agriculture, healthcare, environmental monitoring, and related fields. Despite these advances, a pronounced “valley of death” remains between laboratory-scale validation and practical deployment. The outstanding sensing performance demonstrated under standardized laboratory conditions is often difficult to reproduce in complex real-world sample matrices because of nonspecific adsorption, electrode fouling, and matrix interference. Moreover, the batch-to-batch reproducibility, long-term storage stability, and scalable manufacturing of nanostructured electrodes remain major obstacles to large-scale commercialization.

Future research should therefore place greater emphasis on the development of robust sensing interfaces capable of operating reliably in complex sample environments, together with scalable fabrication strategies and intelligent sensing systems. The integration of advanced functional materials, data-driven sensing methodologies, and miniaturized device technologies is expected to accelerate the practical deployment of electrochemical and photoelectrochemical sensors for rapid on-site analysis and sustainable environmental and biomedical monitoring.

Summary of EC and PEC Applications

Across the five application domains discussed aboveclean energy synthesis, environmental pollutant purification, carbon and nitrogen resource recycling and conversion, green chemical synthesis, and biosensing and environmental monitoringEC and PEC technologies exhibit a distinctly tiered landscape of technological maturity and industrial readiness. Pronounced disparities exist not only among different application fields but also between EC and PEC approaches within each field, reflecting their respective levels of scientific maturity, engineering development, and commercialization potential.

At the highest level of technological readiness, mature electrochemical biosensing platforms (e.g., glucose monitoring and heavy-metal detection) together with alkaline water electrolysis for hydrogen production have already achieved large-scale commercial deployment. These technologies benefit from well-established material synthesis protocols, standardized device manufacturing processes, and mature engineering infrastructures. Consequently, current research is increasingly focused on cost reduction, manufacturing optimization, and broader commercial adoption rather than on fundamental performance breakthroughs.

At the intermediate level of technological readiness, electrocatalytic organic pollutant degradation, electrocatalytic H2O2 synthesis, and electrocatalytic CO2 reduction toward representative C1 products (e.g., CO and formate) have progressed from laboratory-scale studies to pilot-scale demonstrations and early engineering deployment, with limited commercialization achieved in niche applications. Continued improvements in catalyst durability, reactor design, and reductions in system operating costs are steadily driving these technologies from proof-of-concept validation toward broader industrial implementation.

By contrast, most PEC-based systems across all application domains remain at the laboratory research stage in terms of overall technological readiness. Even within EC technologies, several high-value emerging directionsincluding electrocatalytic CO2 reduction to multicarbon C2+ products, direct electrocatalytic N2 fixation, and electrocatalytic C–N coupling for value-added chemical synthesisare still largely confined to proof-of-concept investigations. Across these emerging systems, irrespective of whether they rely on EC or PEC pathways, unresolved reaction mechanisms, insufficient long-term operational stability, and limited techno-economic viability remain the principal barriers to practical deployment. Addressing these challenges will be essential for translating fundamental scientific advances into scalable and commercially viable catalytic technologies.

Notably, the validated design principles and methodological advances established in mature application domains can be effectively transferred to emerging research areas, thereby overcoming the fragmented development landscape of individual application branches and accelerating the overall evolution of EC and PEC technologies. For example, defect engineering and interfacial electronic modulation strategies that have been systematically validated in water-splitting systems can be readily extended to the design of electrocatalysts for pesticide degradation and H2O2 synthesis, providing well-established material design principles for environmental remediation and green chemical synthesis. Likewise, precise active-site engineering approaches developed for EC sensing platformsincluding single-atom catalyst design and micro/nanostructure engineering for enhanced selectivityoffer valuable guidance for regulating product selectivity in CO2 reduction and NH3 synthesis.

Beyond catalyst design, advanced in situ and multimodal characterization methodologies established in mature systems, such as water electrolysis, can also be translated to emerging catalytic processes with more complex reaction networks, including nitrogen-cycle regulation and C–N coupling reactions. These approaches provide powerful tools for elucidating the dynamic evolution of active sites, reaction intermediates, and catalytic mechanisms, thereby enabling more rational catalyst design. Similarly, heterojunction band-engineering strategies developed for PEC water splitting, particularly S-scheme and Z-scheme heterostructures, are broadly applicable to PEC pollutant degradation and solar-driven H2O2 synthesis, where efficient photogenerated charge separation is equally critical. Such cross-domain transfer of design principles, characterization methodologies, and mechanistic understanding is expected to promote the coordinated development of the entire EC/PEC field and establish a more unified framework for catalyst design across diverse catalytic applications.

Conclusion and Outlook

EC and PEC technologies enable the direct conversion of renewable electricity and solar energy into high-value-added chemicals under mild conditions, offering a vital pathway toward sustainable energy cycling and environmental remediation. This review systematically outlines the latest research progress in this field, spanning from fundamental physicochemical principles to practical applications. It highlights core mechanisms such as the electronic structure and band engineering of catalytic materials, interfacial charge transfer kinetics, and the adsorption behavior of reaction intermediates. Furthermore, it introduces a range of advanced in situ characterization techniques, including in situ electron microscopy, XAS, and vibrational spectroscopy. These “observing eyes” provide critical experimental evidence for unraveling the dynamic evolution of active sites and their corresponding reaction pathways.

Building on this foundation, this review further highlights breakthrough advances in the rational design of various electrocatalytic and photoelectrocatalytic materials across multiple application domains. In the field of clean energy synthesis, researchers have significantly enhanced the efficiency and stability of water splitting for hydrogen production through various strategies such as morphology engineering, heterojunction construction, and defect modulation. In the field of deep environmental pollutant purification, some material systems have achieved efficient degradation of diverse organic pollutants, with notable progress in broadening the pH adaptability and visible-light response range. Regarding carbon and nitrogen resource recycling and conversion, the selectivity and FE of CO2 reduction and NH3 synthesis are constantly improving, demonstrating substantial potential particularly in the production of multicarbon products and the design of single-atom catalysts. Furthermore, in the realms of green chemical synthesis (e.g., H2O2 electrosynthesis) and biosensing/environmental monitoring, a series of high-performance catalytic materials and intelligent detection platforms have emerged, laying a solid foundation for practical applications.

Although EC and PEC technologies have achieved remarkable advances across a wide range of application domains, their further development toward practical implementation still faces several fundamental scientific and engineering challenges. As summarized in Figure , the remaining bottlenecks can be grouped into three closely interconnected aspects: (1) the incomplete mechanistic understanding of complex multielectron transfer reactions; (2) the insufficient elucidation of active-site evolution and catalyst reconstruction under operando conditions; and (3) the persistent gap between laboratory-scale optimization and real-world application environments. Addressing these challenges will be essential for bridging the transition from fundamental research to practical EC and PEC technologies.

Challenge, strategy, and future research of EC/PEC technology.

First, although extensive studies have focused on single-reaction pathways (e.g., HER, OER), the understanding of complex reactions involving multielectron transfer and multiple intermediates, such as CO2 reduction to C2+ products, NRR, and C–N coupling, remains limited. Their reaction networks are highly intertwined, and the rate-determining steps as well as the structural evolution of active centers are still unclear. Second, the vast majority of current studies are conducted in pure solutions, single-model pollutants, or high-purity gases, which deviate substantially from real-world environmental conditions. Actual water bodies or industrial exhaust gases are extremely complex, often containing abundant interfering ions, natural organic matter, or impurity gases. These components can readily induce catalyst poisoning, increase side reactions, and impede mass transport, making it difficult to replicate the excellent performance observed in laboratory settings under practical conditions. Therefore, future efforts must prioritize systematic evaluations in complex real water matrices, industrial waste gases, and biological samples. Furthermore, the scalable synthesis of catalysts, system integration, and engineering implementation remain critical bottlenecks hindering the translation of these technologies from laboratory research to industrial applications.

In response to the multifaceted challenges outlined above, EC and PEC research is evolving toward a new paradigm characterized by greater intelligence, precision, and system-level integration [Figure ]. This transformation is being driven by three complementary technological pillars: (1) artificial intelligence and high-throughput computational screening, (2) multimodal in situ and operando characterization techniques, and (3) automated experimentation platforms. Collectively, these advances are driving a paradigm shift from empirical trial-and-error approaches toward data-driven, rational catalyst design.

First, among these emerging strategies, the integration of artificial intelligence with high-throughput computational methods is poised to become a central driving force for next-generation catalyst discovery. By combining machine learning with DFT calculations, complex reaction networks involving multiple proton- and electron-transfer steps can be systematically analyzed. Representative examples include C–C coupling pathways in CO2 reduction to C2+ products, NN bond activation and protonation in NRR, and interfacial C–N coupling mechanisms for urea synthesis. Such data-driven approaches facilitate the identification of rate-determining steps, transient intermediates, and optimal active-site configurations that remain difficult to resolve experimentally.

Beyond mechanistic studies, artificial intelligence assisted models provide powerful tools for application-oriented catalyst design by enabling rapid prediction of reaction selectivity, catalyst durability, and environmental adaptability. For example, machine learning can predict the selectivity of the 2e– ORR pathway selectivity for H2O2 electrosynthesis, as well as catalyst resistance to poisoning, corrosion, and structural degradation under realistic operating environments, including complex water matrices, industrial flue gases, and biological sensing media. These capabilities provide quantitative guidance for the design of catalysts tailored to practical operating conditions. Realizing this vision will require the establishment of large, standardized databases integrating synthesis parameters, structural descriptors, electronic properties, and catalytic performance. Combined with advanced machine-learning algorithms, these databases will enable rapid exploration of the vast chemical space, prediction of previously inaccessible reaction pathways, and, more importantly, inverse catalyst design, in which target catalytic performance directly guides the identification of optimal material compositions, structures, and synthesis protocols. Such a paradigm has the potential not only to dramatically accelerate catalyst discovery but also to uncover catalytic systems that extend beyond conventional chemical intuition.

Concurrently, complementing these computational advances, automated experimentation platform has emerged as the second key technological pillar, providing the experimental throughput required to validate theoretical predictions, generate high-quality data sets, and establish a closed-loop catalyst discovery framework. Such platforms integrate robotic catalyst synthesis, parallel electrochemical screening, and automated data acquisition and analysis, thereby substantially accelerating catalyst development. For EC and PEC systems, high-throughput catalyst fabrication can be achieved through combinatorial electrodeposition, inkjet printing of multicomponent compositions, and programmable hydrothermal or solvothermal synthesis, enabling the rapid preparation of catalyst libraries with precisely controlled compositions, morphologies, and loadings. These catalyst libraries can subsequently be evaluated through parallel electrochemical measurements, allowing the simultaneous assessment of key performance metrics, including overpotential, Tafel slope, FE, product selectivity (e.g., the 2e– versus 4e– oxygen reduction pathways), and long-term operational stability under both model and application-relevant conditions. In the long term, the deep integration of artificial intelligence driven prediction with automated synthesis, high-throughput characterization, and autonomous experimentation is expected to establish a closed-loop research framework encompassing theoretical prediction → automated synthesis → experimental validation → mechanistic elucidation → performance optimization. This self-driving research paradigm will continuously accelerate catalyst discovery and optimization, ultimately enabling the seamless integration of rational design, rapid iteration, and practical deployment of next-generation EC and PEC technologies.

Additionally, next-generation characterization techniques must move beyond the limitations of single-dimensional analysis toward integrated multimodal platforms capable of real-time, in situ and operando monitoring of catalyst structures, chemical states, molecular intermediates, and interfacial charge-transfer processes. Such integrated approaches will enable simultaneous correlation of structural evolution with catalytic performance, thereby providing unprecedented insight into reaction mechanisms across diverse EC and PEC systems. Specifically, multimodal in situ characterization can capture the dynamic evolution of key reaction intermediates and active sites in complex catalytic processes, including transient CO dimerization during C–C coupling in CO2 reduction, N2 adsorption and activation during nitrogen reduction, and the formation of surface species governing the 2e– ORR pathway for H2O2 electrosynthesis. More importantly, under realistic operating conditionsincluding high current densities, elevated temperatures and pressures, or complex water matricesthese techniques can directly reveal catalyst reconstruction, corrosion, poisoning, and deactivation processes, thereby elucidating the intrinsic origins of performance degradation and providing experimental guidance for the development of highly durable catalytic systems. Ultimately, only structure–performance relationships established under application-relevant conditions can provide a reliable foundation for rational catalyst design and engineering optimization.

Beyond these advances in mechanistic understanding and characterization, translating laboratory discoveries into practical technologies will remain a central challenge for the EC and PEC fields. Despite these challenges, both technologies continue to demonstrate considerable potential across a broad range of emerging applications, including hydrogen production, pesticide degradation, drinking water purification, green chemical catalysis, antibacterial materials, and intelligent sensing. Their levels of technological readiness, however, differ substantially. EC technologies have already achieved varying degrees of commercialization in several application sectors and are steadily progressing toward cost-effective large-scale deployment. By contrast, most PEC systems remain at the transition stage between laboratory proof-of-concept studies and engineering-scale implementation, with further development constrained primarily by the fundamental scientific and engineering challenges discussed above.

Overall, the practical implementation of EC and PEC technologies will require coordinated optimization across multiple scales, encompassing catalyst design, electrode architecture, reactor engineering, energy management, and seamless integration with renewable energy systems. Achieving this goal demands not only advances in individual materials but also the synergistic development of devices, reactors, and system-level engineering. More fundamentally, EC and PEC research is undergoing a paradigm shift from empirical trial-and-error approaches toward data-driven, theory-guided, and intelligence-enabled catalyst discovery. The convergence of artificial intelligence, high-throughput computation, in situ and operando characterization, and autonomous experimentation is expected to establish a new research paradigm in which catalyst design, mechanistic understanding, and performance optimization are seamlessly integrated within closed-loop workflows.

Looking ahead, with the continued integration of chemistry, materials science, physics, artificial intelligence, and engineering, EC and PEC technologies are poised to play increasingly important roles in environmental remediation, clean energy conversion, carbon and nitrogen cycling, sustainable chemical synthesis, and intelligent sensing. These advances will provide a solid scientific and technological foundation for the development of sustainable energy systems and a cleaner ecological environment.
