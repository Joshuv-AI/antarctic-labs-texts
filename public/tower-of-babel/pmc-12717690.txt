IL-21 attenuates liver inflammation by enhancing myeloid-derived suppressor cells immunosuppressive function in chronic HBV infection
Authors: Xiaoyi Li, Zihan Jin, Meihan Pan, Zheyu Dong, Junlin Chen, Zhipeng Liu, Hongjie Chen, Guofu Ye, Shihong Zhong, Weiying He, Libo Tang, GuiRong Rao, Yongyin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection progression is closely associated with repeated inflammatory liver injury. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), known to suppress inflammatory responses during hepatitis, may play a protective role in mitigating liver damage; nevertheless, the precise functional specialization of MDSC subpopulations and the critical regulator in controlling their suppressive capacity remain incompletely understood. This study investigates the immunoregulatory role of IL-21, a pleiotropic immunomodulatory cytokine, in modulating MDSC function and its implications for HBV-associated liver inflammation prognosis.MethodsThe frequencies of peripheral and intrahepatic MDSCs, along with serum IL-21 levels, in patients with chronic HBV infection were measured with freshly isolated samples. C57BL/6 mice carrying pAAV-HBV1.2 plasmids was established to explore the effect of IL-21 on aminotransferase levels and MDSC function.ResultsPatients with chronic HBV infection demonstrated significantly elevated frequencies of circulating MDSCs compared to healthy controls. Of note, the granulocytic MDSCs (gMDSCs) were markedly more abundant in hepatic tissues than in peripheral blood. Strikingly, a significant inverse correlation was observed between circulating gMDSC frequencies and serum ALT levels specifically in patients with elevated serum IL-21 concentrations, whereas this correlation was absent in the low IL-21 subgroup. In vitro functional assays demonstrated that exogenous IL-21 potentiated the immunosuppressive capacity of MDSCs, significantly attenuating IFN-γ production in co-cultured T cells. Consistent with these findings, hydrodynamic injection of IL-21 plasmid in an HBV mouse model resulted in significantly reduced serum ALT levels concomitant with enhanced arginase I expression in hepatic MDSCs.ConclusionsThese results reveal that IL-21 enhances MDSC-mediated immunosuppression, implicating this regulatory axis in the attenuation of hepatic inflammation during chronic HBV infection. These findings provide a novel perspective on the involvement of IL-21 in the negative regulation of intrahepatic inflammation and provide a novel perspective on the role of IL-21 in developing immunotherapeutic strategies to optimize available anti-inflammatory treatments in chronic HBV infection.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07406-1.Keywords: Interleukin 21, Myeloid-derived suppressor cells, Hepatitis B virus, Intrahepatic inflammation, Immunosuppressive activity

Introduction

The hepatitis B virus (HBV) remains a critical global health challenge, chronically affecting approximately 254 million individuals and causing 1.1 million deaths in 2022, predominantly due to complications such as cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1]. HBV itself is non-cytopathogenic; instead, liver injury arises from the host immune response. During acute infection, innate immune cells release antiviral cytokines that recruit cytotoxic T cells and granulocytes, initiating a cascade of inflammatory events that promote viral clearance through necroinflammation [2–4]. In contrast, chronic HBV infection is marked by a dysfunctional immune response—insufficient to eliminate the virus but sufficient to sustain persistent inflammation, driving progressive liver damage, cirrhosis, and HCC [5, 6]. These observations underscore the dual role of immune cell interactions in balancing viral control and tissue injury, highlighting the need for therapeutic strategies that modulate inflammatory pathways [7].

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), a heterogeneous population of immunosuppressive cells, have emerged as key regulators of immune responses in HBV pathogenesis. MDSCs are broadly categorized into granulocytic (gMDSCs) and monocytic (mMDSCs) subsets, which phenotypically and morphologically resemble neutrophils and monocytes, respectively [8, 9]. In vitro studies demonstrate their plasticity, as MDSCs can differentiate into pro-inflammatory M1-like or immunosuppressive M2-like macrophages [10–12]. While MDSCs exhibit protective roles in acute liver inflammation [13], their function in chronic HBV infection is paradoxical: they suppress T cell activation via arginase I and indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), enzymes that deplete essential amino acids (L-arginine and tryptophan) [14, 15], and upregulate immune checkpoints such as galectin-9 and PD-L1 to further impair antiviral immunity [16]. Given the potent ability of MDSCs to inhibit T cell responses, it is reasonable to speculate that MDSCs play a protective role in preventing tissue damage in chronic HBV infection.

Interleukin (IL)-21, a pleiotropic cytokine secreted by activated CD4+ T cells and NKT cells, has dual roles in HBV infection. Our prior work revealed that IL-21 restores exhausted HBV-specific CD8+ T cell function and promotes HBeAg seroconversion, implicating its therapeutic potential for viral clearance [17, 18]. Conversely, dysregulated IL-21 responses exacerbate liver injury during chronic infection, underscoring its context-dependent effects [19, 20]. Despite these advances, the influence of IL-21 on MDSC expansion and immunosuppressive activity—particularly in the setting of chronic HBV-driven liver inflammation—remains unexplored.

This study aims to evaluate the status of MDSCs in patients with chronic HBV infection, investigate the role of MDSCs in liver inflammation, and elucidate the effects of IL-21 on MDSCs in liver inflammation by phenotypic and functional analyses.

Materials and methods

Patients and samples

Eighty treatment-naïve patients with chronic HBV infection were recruited and classified into the immune tolerant carrier (IT; n = 37), hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg)-positive chronic hepatitis B (CHB; n = 16), and inactive carrier (IC; n = 27) groups according to the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases guidelines [21]. Another 33 healthy control (HC) were also recruited in this study (Table S1). In addition, intrahepatic mononuclear cells (HMCs) and matched peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from 9 treatment-naïve patients with HBV-related HCC who underwent curative hepatectomy and 4 non-HBV-infected patients with hepatic hemangioma (Table S2). All individuals were recruited at Nanfang Hospital (Guangzhou, China). The exclusion criteria for these studies were coinfection with hepatitis A virus, hepatitis C virus, hepatitis D virus, hepatitis E virus, and human immunodeficiency virus. In addition, patients with primary biliary cirrhosis and autoimmune diseases were also excluded. All individuals provided written informed consent, and the Ethical Committee of Nanfang Hospital approved the studies.

Mice

Male C57BL/6 wild type (WT) mice were purchased from the laboratory animal center of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). Six- to eight-week-old age-matched male mice were used in the experiments. All animals were maintained under a 12 h light/dark cycle in a specific pathogen-free animal facility. The Ethical Committee for Experimental Animal Care of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China) approved all animal experiments. This study followed the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

Mice model

WT mice were intraperitoneal injected with 300 mg/kg acetaminophen (APAP) to induce an acute liver injury model, while the control group received isovolumetric normal saline (NS) injections. WT mice were used to establish the hydrodynamic injection (HDI) HBV mouse model, following a previously described protocol. [22]. In brief, 10 μg of pAAV-HBV1.2 plasmids in a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution equivalent to 8% of the body weight were injected through the tail vein within 5 to 8 seconds. Mice were sacrificed on day 28, and PBMCs, intrahepatic, and intrasplenic lymphocytes were collected. For investigate the role of IL-21 on MDSCs in vivo, WT mice were administrated with pAAV-HBV1.2 along with 20 μg of pIL-21 (Sino Biological Inc., Beijing, China) or pBlank plasmid via HDI one day after collecting blood on day −1 (baseline). Mice were then sacrificed on day 7, and PBMCs, intrahepatic and intrasplenic lymphocytes were isolated. Serum levels of IL-21 and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were detected at baseline and days 3 and 7. Ten WT mice received intraperitoneal injection of diethylnitrosamine (DEN) at a dose of 25 mg/kg (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) at 6 weeks of age to induce a chronic liver injury mice model. Starting at week 42, mice were given intraperitoneal injections of 20 μg of recombined mouse IL-21 (rmIL-21, Sino Biological Inc., Beijing, China) or PBS every 2 weeks for a total of 6 injections. Additionally, starting at week 48, mice received intraperitoneal injections of DEN at a dose of 25 mg/kg every 4 days, totaling 10 injections. At week 42, 46, and 54, we collected serum samples to measure ALT levels, and at week 52, liver tissues were obtained from sacrificed mice for the isolation of intrahepatic lymphocytes and histological analysis.

HBV serology and HBV DNA detection

The levels of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), HBeAg, and antibody to HBeAg (anti-HBe) in human serum were quantitatively determined using the Roche COBAS® 6000. For the patients with chronic HBV infection who participated in the cohort, levels of serum HBV DNA were quantified by the Roche LightCycler® 480 II (Roche Molecular Diagnostics, Pleasanton, CA) with Hepatitis B Viral Quantitative Fluorescence Diagnostic Kit (Sansure Biotech, Hunan, China). The lower limit of detection for HBsAg is < 0.05 IU/mL, while the detection limit for HBV DNA is at least 100 IU/mL. The normal ranges for ALT and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels in human serum samples were 9–50 U/L and 15–40 U/L, respectively.

Mononuclear cells isolation

PBMCs and HMCs were isolated by standard density centrifugation and were used freshly in this study. Preparation of HMCs was performed according to a previously described procedure [23]. Briefly, the liver tissues were flushed using 4 °C RPMI-1640 complete medium (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 2% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Hyclone; GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Logan, UT, USA), 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 2 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; Ambion; Applied Biosystems, San Mateo, CA, USA). Subsequently, HMCs were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation on Ficoll-Hypaque.

Flow cytometry

For cell-surface staining, cells were stained with the indicated antibodies (Table S3). For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were then fixed and permeabilized using a Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Biosciences Pharmingen) and stained with the corresponding antibodies (arginase I, galectin-9, and IDO, as well as CD68) at 4 °C for 30 min. Dead cells were excluded using Live/Dead (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All samples were analyzed on BD FACS CantoII flow cytometer or Aria III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). The data were analyzed with FlowJo V10.0.7 software (Tree Star).

MDSC and CD4+T cell isolation

Fresh isolated PBMCs were stained with anti-CD11b-FITC and anti-HLA-DR-PE-Cy7. The HLA-DR-/lowCD11b+ cells were sorted by BD Aria III flow cytometer. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, CD4+ T cells were isolated from autologous fresh PBMCs by immunomagnetic negative selection (EasySep Human CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit, STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). Cell purities were > 99%.

Immunofluorescence and quantitative analysis

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on paraffin-embedded liver biopsy sections using standard techniques. Antigen retrieval was performed in EDTA-Tris buffer (pH 9.0) using a pressure cooker. The slides were then blocked with 10% normal donkey serum for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the slides were incubated with mouse anti-CD15 (1:200, Affinity Biosciences, Cincinnati, OH, USA) and rabbit monoclonal to CD66b+ (1:500, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) at 4 °C for overnight. Afterward, the slides were washed three times with tris-buffered saline/Tween 20 and incubated for 45 min at 37 °C with Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-rabbit lgG [1: 400] and Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-mouse lgG (1:400, Life Technologies, Foster City, CA, USA). Finally, slides were stained with DAPI (Abcam). Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Fluoview FV10i; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Three samples per group were included. For each slide, three non-overlapping fields of view were randomly selected. DAPI-positive cells, indicating all cells, and Alexa Fluor 594 and Alexa Fluor 488 double-positive cells, indicating gMDSCs, were automatically counted using ImageJ software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The human and mice serum levels of IL-21 (Invitrogen; Carlsbad, CA, USA) and the mice serum levels of ALT (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) were assessed by commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

RNA-Sequencing (RNA-seq) and data analysis

PBMCs were obtained from 3 treatment-naïve patients with chronic HBV infection. MDSCs were sorted by Aria III flow cytometer and stimulated with IL-21 (50 ng/mL) or not for 20 hours. Total RNA was directly extracted using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies) and subjected to library construction and deep sequencing on Illumina HiSeq™ 2500 by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). Read counts were normalized based on the fragments per kilobase million reads (FPKM). Fold changes were calculated for all possible comparisons, and a two-fold cutoff was used to select genes with expression changes. All raw transcriptome data have been deposited in Genome Sequence Archive (GSA-Human accession number: HRA002369). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed by Guangzhou Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

MDSCs suppression assays

Sorted peripheral MDSCs from chronic HBV infected patients were cultured in the presence or absence of IL-21 (50 ng/mL). After culturing for 20 h, cells were washed three times and cocultured with the autologous purified CD4+ T cells at a ratio of 1:4 or 1:2 (MDSC:T-cell) in the presence of soluble anti-CD3 (5 μg/mL, Biolegend) and anti-CD28 (5 μg/mL, Biolegend). Following 3 days coculture, the proliferation of CFSE-labeled CD4+ T cells and the production of intracellular interferon (IFN)-γ in CD4+ T cells were measured by flow cytometry.

Activation of cell signaling

Purified circulating MDSCs were cultured in the presence of IL-21 (50 ng/mL) for 30 min. Then, cells were fixed with Fix Buffer I (BD Biosciences Phosflow) at 4 °C for 20 min followed by permeabilization with Perm Buffer III (BD Biosciences Phosflow) at 4 °C for 30 min. Finally, cells were stained with phospho-specific antibodies at room temperature for another 2 h. NF-κB, STAT1, STAT3, and STAT5 phosphorylation were examined by flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v.9.0.1 (La Jolla, CA, USA). Mann-Whitney’s U test or Wilcoxon sighed-rank test were used when two groups were compared. Correlations between variables were assessed with the Spearman rank-order correlation coefficient. All statistical analyses were based on two-tailed hypothesis tests with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Expanded circulating gMDSCs exhibit immunosuppressive activity in chronic HBV infection

Based on previous studies, we analyzed the frequency of MDSCs using a specific gating strategy and freshly isolated samples [15, 24]. The fragility of MDSCs makes them particularly susceptible to the processes of freezing and thawing, underscores the importance of using fresh samples to ensure the accuracy and reliability of our results (Fig. S1A-E). Our analysis revealed a significantly higher frequency of circulating MDSCs in patients with chronic HBV infection than in the HC group, particularly elevated in those at the IT and IC stages versus those with CHB (Fig. 1A). We also observed negative correlations between circulating MDSCs and serum levels of ALT and AST (Fig. 1B). To evaluate the immunosuppressive activity of MDSCs, we isolated them from chronic HBV infected patients and cocultured them with autologous purified CD4+ T cells. Our results showed that MDSCs inhibited the IFN-γ responses of CD4+ T cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1C). Additionally, we assessed the expression of M1-like (CD80+ and CD86+) and M2-like (CD163+ and CD206+) macrophage phenotypes in circulating MDSCs. Both HBV and HC groups exhibited a tendency to polarize toward immunosuppresive M2-like macrophages; however, chronic HBV patients showed significantly higher levels of the CD163+ CD206+ double-positive M2-like macrophage phenotypes than HC (Fig. 1D). These findings suggested that MDSCs were expanded in patients with chronic HBV infection and may play a role in ameliorating liver inflammation and influencing the natural history of HBV infection.

MDSCs were further classified into gMDSC and mMDSC subsets (Fig. S1A). We found that patients with chronic HBV infection had a significantly higher percentage of gMDSCs in MDSCs compared to mMDSCs, a difference not observed in the HC group (Fig. 1E). Additionally, the frequency of circulating gMDSCs was significantly higher in patients with chronic HBV infection than in the HC group (Fig. 1F), while there was no significant difference in the frequency of mMDSCs between the two groups (Fig. 1G). Our data revealed that only the frequency of circulating gMDSCs was negatively correlated with serum levels of ALT and AST, while mMDSCs showed a poor correlation with the biochemical and virological parameters of HBV infection (Fig. 1H). Furthermore, we detected significantly higher levels of galectin-9 in circulating MDSCs and an increasing trend (p = 0.09) in the expression of arginase I in circulating gMDSCs in chronic HBV infected patients than in HC. However, there was no significant difference in suppressor marker expression among patients with different natural histories (Fig. 1I). Taken together, these data suggested that MDSCs, particulaly gMDSCs, may have the capacity to suppress liver inflammation in chronic HBV infection by secreting suppressor molecules. Given that gMDSCs are particularly sensitive to the freezing and thawing process, this further emphasizes the necessity of using fresh samples for studying gMDSCs.

Liver-infiltrating gMDSCs correlate with hepatic inflammation in HBV infection

We established an acute liver injury model in mice induced by APAP to investigate the effects of intrahepatic injury on the frequency and function of MDSCs (Fig. S2A). Firstly, we confirmed elevated serum ALT and observed inflammatory cell infiltration in the liver after APAP injection (Fig. S2B and S2C). Using the gating strategy shown in Fig. S3A [8, 25], we found that compared to the NS group, the frequencies of MDSCs and gMDSCs in blood and liver were significantly higher in the APAP group. Furthermore, the frequencies of intrahepatic MDSCs and mMDSCs were significantly higher than their circulating counterparts in both groups, with an increasing trend (p = 0.08) in the frequency of intrahepatic MDSCs compared to circulating MDSCs in the APAP group at day 5 (Fig. S2D). We also detected significantly higher levels of arginase I in circulating gMDSCs and an increasing trend (p = 0.15) in the expression of arginase I in intrahepatic gMDSCs in the APAP group compared to the NS group (Fig. S2E). However, we did not observe any significant differences in the expression of galectin-9 in MDSCs between the two groups (Fig. S2F). Our data suggested that hepatitis may induce MDSC amplification and recruitment into the liver to further inhibit hepatitis activity through negative feedback regulation.

To investigate whether the expanded population of circulating MDSCs in subjects with chronic HBV infection could home to the liver to regulate inflammation, we compared the frequencies of and the expression of representative suppressor markers in MDSC subsets in paired blood and liver from 9 subjects with HBV-related HCC and 4 subjects with hepatic hemangioma who were non-HBV infected (Fig. S3B). Our data showed that MDSCs and gMDSCs tended to be more abundant in the liver than in the blood in both patients with HBV-related HCC and patients with non-HBV infected, while mMDSC remained comparable in the two organs. We observed higher frequencies of MDSCs and gMDSCs in blood and liver in patients with HBV-related HCC than in patients with non-HBV infected (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, we found that intrahepatic MDSCs exhibited higher arginase I and IDO expression but lower galectin-9 expression than their circulating counterparts in the two groups (Figs. 2B, 2D and 2E). Notably, arginase I expressing gMDSCs were significantly higher in the liver than in the blood in patients with HBV-related HCC (Fig. 2C). Then, we performed double epitope immunostaining of liver sections from patients with chronic HBV infection for CD15 and CD66b. Quantitative analysis revealed a significantly higher number of double-positively stained cells indicating gMDSCs in CHB patients than in IT and IC patients [15] (Fig. 2F). Our data suggested that gMDSCs accumulate in the liver with inflammation in HBV infection.

Next, an HBV mouse model established by pAAV-HBV1.2 HDI was used for further investigation (Fig. 2G). We found that the frequencies of gMDSCs and mMDSCs in the liver were higher than in the blood and spleen in the HBV mouse model (Fig. 2H). Additionally, gMDSCs expressed higher levels of arginase I in the liver than in the spleen. We also found higher expression of arginase I on circulating and intrahepatic gMDSCs in the HBV mouse model than in the PBS group. However, intrahepatic mMDSCs expressed lower levels of arginase I, galectin-9 and programmed death 1 ligand 1 (PD-L1) than circulating mMDSCs in the HBV mouse model (Figs. 2I, 2J and 2K). These data indicated that gMDSCs with immunouppressive activity may migrate to the liver in the HBV mouse model.

IL-21 reprograms the transcriptional profile of MDSCs in chronic HBV infection

Since B cells are known to express abundant IL-21 receptor (IL-21 R), we compared the expression levels of IL-21 R between MDSCs and B cells in patients with chronic HBV infection. We found that MDSCs expressed higher levels of IL-21 R than B cells (Fig. 3A). We then measured serum IL-21 concentration; however, no significant difference was found between HC and HBV-infected individuals (Fig. S4A). In addition, there was no correlation between ALT and IL-21 in patients with chronic HBV infection (Fig. S4B). We further subgrouped patients with chronic HBV based on the median serum IL-21 concentration. In the IL-21 high group, the frequencies of circulating MDSCs and gMDSCs were inversely correlated with serum ALT levels. In contrast, no correlation was observed between circulating mMDSC frequencies and serum ALT levels in either the IL-21 high or low groups. (Fig. 3B). Additionally, we noted a significant increase in IL-21 R expression on intrahepatic gMDSCs from patients with HBV-related HCC compared to those with non-HBV-related HCC. Moreover, intrahepatic gMDSCs expressed higher levels of IL-21 R than their circulating counterparts in HBV-related HCC patients (Fig. 3C). To explore the mechanisms underlying the association between IL-21 and MDSCs in chronic HBV infection, we performed RNA-seq on purified MDSCs stimulated with IL-21. This analysis identified 240 downregulated and 275 upregulated transcripts (Figs. 3D and 3E). Notably, MDSCs stimulated with IL-21 showed increased expression of transcripts encoding arginase (ARG2) and IDO (IDO2). GO analysis revealed a prominent abundance of gene signatures associated with humoral immune response and cell adhesion (Fig. 3F). Moreover, pathways related to immune disease and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction were significantly altered in response to IL-21 (Fig. 3G). Gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further indicated highly active immune-related pathways, including apoptosis, PD-L1 expression and programmed death 1 (PD1) checkpoint, JAK–STAT3 signaling, and NF-kappa B signaling, in the IL-21 treatment group compared to the control group (Fig. 3H). Overall, these findings suggest that IL-21 may enhance the functional capacity of gMDSCs, contributing to an immunosuppressive environment in chronic HBV infection and potentially influencing disease progression and liver inflammation.

IL-21 overexpression potentiates MDSC-mediated immunosuppression in vitro

We investigated the impact of IL-21 on the expansion and immunosuppressive function of MDSCs. First, PBMCs were culcured in vitro either with or without IL-21. Compared to untreated cells, IL-21 treatment significantly reduced the number of mMDSCs, but only slightly reduced the number of gMDSCs in the 100 ng/mL IL-21 treated group (Fig. 4A). Since PBMCs consist of multiple cell types, we further analyzed the effect of IL-21 on sorted MDSCs. Specifically, IL-21 stimulation derectly led to a decrease in the number of MDSCs (Fig. 4B). Moreover, we observed a significant increase in M2-like macrophage phenotypes in purified MDSCs following treatment with IL-21 (Fig. 4C). Taken together, these results suggest that IL-21 may reduce the number of mMDSCs by promoting their differentiation into M2-like macrophages. We also found that IL-21 enhanced the expression of arginase I, galectin-9, IDO, and PD-L1 in MDSC subsets (Figs. 4D, 4E and 4F). Furthermore, IL-21 increased the production of arginase I and PD-L1 in purified MDSCs (Fig. 4G). Most importantly, IL-21-treated MDSCs exhibited a more potent suppressive effect on IFN-γ production from CD4+ T cells than untreated MDSCs (Fig. 4H). However, IL-21 did not improve the suppressive effect of MDSCs on the proliferation of CD4+ T cells (Fig. 4I). Overall, our findings demonstrated that excess IL-21 enhanced the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs, which may reduce liver damage in chronic HBV infection.

Previous studies have shown that IL-21 signals via the Jak-STAT pathway [26], and that STAT proteins play critical roles in MDSC accumulation and function [27]. Therefore, we examined the transcription factors of sorted MDSCs after IL-21 treatment. Interestingly, IL-21 increased STAT3 phosphorylation and decreased STAT5 phosphorylation of purified MDSCs (Fig. 4J). Our data suggested that IL-21 may enhance MDSCs suppressive activity mainly through STAT3 activation.

IL-21 augments gMDSC-mediated immunosuppression and ameliorates liver inflammation in an HBV-expressing mouse model

We conducted an in vivo study in an HBV mice model to evaluate the effect of IL-21 on MDSCs and liver inflammation. The mice were injected with pAAV-HBV1.2 along with either 20 μg of pIL-21 or pBlank plasmids (Fig. 5A). We found that serum IL-21 levels were significantly elevated at day 3 after pIL-21 administration and then decreased at day 7, whereas they were undetectable in the pBlank group (Fig. 5B). Interestingly, the pIL-21-treated group exhibited a significant reduction in serum ALT levels compared to the pBlank group at day 3 (Fig. 5C). Additionally, treatment with pIL-21 increased the frequencies and expression of arginase I in MDSC subsets in blood, liver, and spleen at day 7 (Figs. 5D and 5E). Furthermore, pIL-21 upregulated the expression of galectin-9 in intrasplenic gMDSCs and downregulated the expansion of PD-L1 in intrahepatic gMDSCs (Fig. S5A). These data indicated that IL-21 could increase the numbers of MDSCs and enhance their activity in vivo.

To further investigate the role of IL-21 in acute liver inflammation, we established an HBV mice model carrying pAAV-HBV1.2 along with 20 μg of pIL-21 or pBlank plasmids in combination with APAP injection (Fig. 5F). We observed similar results to the above model for serum IL-21 levels in this model (Fig. 5G). Notably, the pIL-21-treated group exhibited a significant reduction in serum ALT levels compared to the pBlank group at day 3 after pAAV-HBV1.2 injection, although ALT levels increased 1 day after APAP injection (Fig. 5H). Moreover, treatment with pIL-21 increased the frequency and expression of arginase I in gMDSCs in the liver at day 5. We also observed a trend toward increased frequency of intrahepatic gMDSCs compared to circulating gMDSCs in both the pBlank and pIL-21 groups, although this trend did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.06) (Figs. 5I and 5J). These results indicated that IL-21 induces the aggregation and arginase I expansion of gMDSCs in the liver in the acute liver injury mouse model induced by HBV and APAP.

Addtionally, we investigated the impact of IL-21 on MDSC subsets on regulation of chronic liver inflammation in mice with injection of rmIL-21 combined with DEN-induced chronic liver injury (Fig. 5K). We observed rmIL-21 reduced the degree of liver damage (Fig. 5L) and intrahepatic inflammatory cells infiltration (Fig. 5M). rmIL-21 did not affect the numbers of MDSC subsets in the liver but increased arginase I expression in intrahepatic gMDSCs at week 54 (Figs. 5N and 5O). These data suggested that IL-21 signaling alleviated the chronic liver damage in mice by enhancing the arginase I expansion of gMDSCs.

Discussion

Chronic HBV infection exemplifies the dual role of the immune system, where insufficient T cell responses drive persistent inflammation, exacerbating liver damage and increasing the risk of cirrhosis, liver failure, and HCC [28]. Our study highlights the therapeutic potential of modulating inflammatory pathways, particularly through myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), to mitigate this immune-mediated injury. We demonstrated that gMDSCs—enriched in the liver compared to peripheral blood during chronic hepatitis B—are critical regulators of intrahepatic inflammation. Notably, IL-21 enhances gMDSC immunosuppressive activity and alleviates liver injury in murine HBV models, suggesting its role in tempering inflammation through MDSC-mediated mechanisms.

MDSCs are well-documented facilitators of immune evasion in cancer and chronic infections, promoting tumor progression and HBV persistence [9, 14]. However, their protective functions in liver disease remain underexplored. Using clinical samples, we observed intrahepatic gMDSC accumulation in HBV-related HCC patients and an inverse correlation between circulating gMDSC frequency and serum ALT/AST levels. This aligns with prior reports linking gMDSC abundance to reduced hepatic necroinflammation in chronic HBV [15] and the anti-fibrotic role of arginase I-expressing macrophages [29]. In murine models of APAP-induced acute injury and HBV infection, gMDSC expansion further supports their role in limiting inflammation. Collectively, these findings position gMDSCs as key players in balancing immune-mediated viral control and tissue protection during chronic HBV infection.

Recent work by Pal et al. has comprehensively described the diverse roles of MDSCs across different phases of chronic HBV infection, highlighting how their phenotypic and functional heterogeneity influences HBV-specific T-cell responses and intrahepatic immune regulation [30]. Consistent with these findings, our data reveal that IL-21 further modulates MDSC behavior, enhancing their immunosuppressive activity and contributing to the fine balance between antiviral immunity and liver inflammation.

While IL-21 is recognized for its pleiotropic effects in immunity, its influence on MDSCs has remained unclear. We identified high IL-21 R expression on both circulating and intrahepatic MDSCs, prompting functional investigations. IL-21 altered MDSC gene expression, promoted their differentiation into M2-like macrophages, and amplified their immunosuppressive capacity. Strikingly, in patients with elevated IL-21 levels, MDSC and gMDSC frequencies inversely correlated with ALT, a relationship absent in the IL-21 low group. This suggests that IL-21 may attenuate liver injury by enhancing MDSC-mediated immunosuppression—a hypothesis corroborated by reduced ALT levels in IL-21-treated HBV and APAP-HBV mouse models. Furthermore, IL-21 boosted arginase I expression in hepatic gMDSCs in DEN-induced chronic injury, underscoring its role in curbing excessive inflammation.

The dual nature of IL-21 in liver pathology remains contentious [31, 32]. While elevated IL-21 correlates with liver damage in chronic active hepatitis B [33], IL-21/IL-21 R signaling also protects against DSS-induced colitis by modulating Th1/Th2/Th17/Treg responses [34]. Our findings reconcile this paradox by demonstrating IL-21s context-dependent effects: in chronic HBV, IL-21 enhances MDSC activity, reducing ALT levels and hepatic inflammation without compromising viral clearance in mouse models. However, the potential for MDSC-mediated T cell suppression to perpetuate HBV replication warrants further study, particularly in balancing anti-inflammatory and antiviral responses.

While we observed increase arginase I production in intrahepatic gMDSCs in the pIL-21-treated HBV mouse model compared to the pBlank group, the data also indicate that rmIL-21 boosts arginase I expression in intrahepatic gMDSCs in DEN-induced chronic liver injury models. These results suggest that IL-21 not only accelerates HBV clearance but also inhibits excessive inflammatory responses, which could be crucial in treating chronic liver injury. However, while IL-21-induced enhancement of MDSC activity may alleviate liver inflammation, it could simultaneously suppress HBV-specific immune responses. Indeed, MDSCs in chronic HBV infection have been shown to inhibit HBV-specific T-cell proliferation and IFN-γ production, and their frequency is positively correlated with viral load. These cells exert suppression via mechanisms such as PD-L1/IL-10 production and arginase/IDO expression, which we observed to increase after IL-21 treatment in our study [35]. Given that MDSCs can create a tolerogenic microenvironment and limit antiviral T-cell function, the immunosuppressive amplification by IL-21 may, in on the one hand, reduce liver injury but on the other hand favour viral persistence. Thus, our findings highlight a potential trade-off between mitigating liver inflammation and preserving antiviral immunity. Future investigations should aim to delineate how IL-21–modulated MDSCs affect long-term HBV control, and whether targeting MDSC pathways may allow selective control of inflammation without compromising viral clearance. Moreover, since immunosuppression is critical for liver transplantation [36], the mechanisms presented in this study may have potential implications for the prevention and therapy of liver transplantation, warranting further exploration.

Mechanistically, IL-21 induced STAT3 phosphorylation while suppressing STAT5 activation in MDSCs. Given STAT3’s established role in MDSC expansion and function [27, 37], and STAT5’s inhibition of myeloid differentiation [38], we propose that IL-21 skews MDSC differentiation toward immunosuppressive M2-like macrophages via STAT3 activation and STAT5 downregulation. These pathways likely synergize to amplify MDSC suppressive activity, though detailed mechanisms require further elucidation. However, as this conclusion is based on phosphorylation and transcriptomic evidence rather than functional inhibition, the causal role of STAT3 activation in IL-21–mediated MDSC modulation warrants further confirmation.

Conclusion

In summary, our study underscores the potent immunosuppressive activity of MDSCs, which can be further enhanced by exogenous IL-21. This highlights their potential role in mitigating liver inflammation in chronic HBV infection. These findings provide a novel perspective on the role of IL-21 in developing immunotherapeutic strategies to optimize available anti-inflammatory treatments in chronic HBV infection.
