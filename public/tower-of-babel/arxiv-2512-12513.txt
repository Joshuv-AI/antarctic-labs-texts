AttenGW: A Lightweight Attention-Based Multi-Detector Gravitational-Wave Detection Pipeline Journal: Astronomy Computing
Victoria Tiki, E. A. Huerta

Abstract
We present AttenGW , an attention-based multi-detector gravitational-wave detection model and accompanying software stack designed for analysis of real LIGO data. AttenGW combines a per-detector hierarchical dilated convolutional network with an attention-based aggregation module that enforces cross-detector coherence, providing an alternative to graph-based aggregation schemes used in previous work. The pipeline adopts a LIGO-style preprocessing and data-loading workflow based on GWOSC time series, with standard whitening and filtering, and is released as a documented Python/PyTorch package. We benchmark AttenGW using simulated injections to estimate sensitive volume and on real O3 data, focusing on the February 2020 segment previously used to evaluate a spatiotemporal graph ensemble. On this month of data, a single AttenGW model reduces the false-positive rate relative to a single graph-based detector by a factor of a few, and an ensemble of three AttenGW models matches the performance of the corresponding six-model ensemble. Injection studies on real LIGO noise further indicate that attention-based aggregation yields stable performance on non-Gaussian backgrounds.



1
Introduction

Gravitational wave (GW) detection methods are foundational tools for studies of the universe, from understanding the formation and evolution of compact binaries
2
,
31
to testing general relativity in the strong-field regime
3
,
10
. They operate by identifying signals from black hole (BH) or neutron star (NS) mergers embedded in noisy data from GW observatories. Fast identification further enables prompt follow-up from telescopes looking for electromagnetic counterparts to these events.

Production pipelines such as PyCBC
24
and GstLAL
15
use matched filtering to correlate data against modeled waveform banks, which provides statistically robust detections. These pipelines have been successfully deployed across LIGO observation runs and constitute the current standard for GW detection. However, matched filtering is computationally intensive and sensitive to nonstationary noise as well as signal type
27
,
20
.

Machine learning offers a potential alternative, with models that can learn directly from real detector data and identify astrophysical signals without relying on explicit template banks. Early approaches integrating ML methods into the GW detection pipeline employed convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on simulated waveforms in Gaussian noise
7
, later extended to real LIGO/Virgo strain data. Wang et al. showed that CNNs trained on O1/O2 noise could recover all cataloged BBH events and identify additional sub-threshold candidates
28
. Qiu et al. trained a CNN on real LIGO noise with injected binary black hole (BBH), binary neutron star (BNS), and neutron star–black hole (NSBH) signals and demonstrated that the network can distinguish all three source classes from detector noise while recovering GW170817, GW190425, GW200105, and GW200115 in O2–O3 data
19
. More recently, Tian et al.
22
proposed a spatiotemporal graph neural network ensemble, consisting of a WaveNet-style Hierarchical Dilated Convolutional Network (HDCN) and graph neural network (GNN) that recovers six GW events in data from February 2020 with zero false positives, at the cost of training and maintaining several carefully preselected models rather than a single detector. Other approaches, such as the Aframe pipeline
14
, prioritize low-latency compact-binary coalescence (CBC) detection using a 1D ResNet-style CNN on time-series strain, achieving substantially lower latency than traditional matched-filter searches while retaining competitive sensitivity for higher-mass BBHs. Complementary work by Mobilia and Guidi
16
uses a Random Forest classifier to rescore existing matched-filter triggers, yielding a modest but consistent gain in detections at low false-positive rates without replacing the underlying pipeline.

Despite these advances, no ML method has yet demonstrated robust, production-level performance across a full observing run. Limitations include poor generalization to unseen noise artifacts, overfitting to narrow mass regimes, and the lack of reproducible software infrastructure for training and deployment.

In this work, we present
AttenGW
, a multi-detector GW detection model and accompanying software stack designed for real LIGO data. The model combines a per-detector HDCN with an attention-based aggregation module that performs explicit cross-detector communication. While attention-based architectures have become standard tools for sequence modeling in many domains
26
,
5
,
6
,
4
, ML-based GW searches have so far focused mainly on convolutional and graph-based models, with explicit attention-based aggregation across detectors receiving relatively little exploration. In
AttenGW
, the attention module replaces earlier graph-based aggregation schemes and is empirically found to reduce false positives in multi-detector data at comparable detection efficiency, while remaining lightweight enough for deployment close to the data. See section
2.3.2
for a discussion on the advantages of attention modules in this context.

AttenGW
is accompanied by a simple, GWOSC-based preprocessing and data-loading pipeline that follows standard whitening, band-pass filtering, and basic glitch-rejection procedures. This pipeline is deliberately lightweight and intended as a practical starting point for experimentation and reuse, rather than a replacement for the more complex production pipelines employed in current LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA analyses. The accompanying software release provides a full Python/PyTorch implementation, with dataloaders, training, and inference scripts that can be run on HPC clusters. The codebase is publicly available on GitHub
23
. For comparability with earlier work, our main benchmarks mirror the February 2020 segment of the O3b run considered by Tian et al., enabling a like-for-like comparison of detection efficiency and false-positive control on the same month of data.

An early version of
AttenGW
was integrated into the RADAR framework as the gravitational-wave component of a federated GW–radio follow-up system
17
. In that setting, the model was tuned specifically to perform well only on an hour-long demonstration segment of LIGO data, and the associated training configuration and preprocessing pipeline were not released as a standalone tool. In this work, we develop
AttenGW
into a standalone, general-purpose detector, with modest architectural changes and with a reworked preprocessing and data-loading pipeline. We further validate it on O3 LIGO data and synthetic datasets, using the reproducible preprocessing workflow and software release described above.

To summarize, this paper’s primary contributions to astronomical computing are: (i) a reproducible, GWOSC-based preprocessing and data-download pipeline; (ii) a lightweight attention-based multi-detector architecture suitable for HPC deployment; (iii) an open-source software stack with scripts for training and inference on real O3 data; and (iv) a benchmark comparison against existing graph-based methods on the February 2020 O3b segment.

2
Methods

The pipeline comprises four main components:

Downloader
: downloads strain data (signals and/or noise) from GWOSC and rejects or clamps glitchy segments.

Data generator
: constructs training segments from real or synthetic noise and synthetic signals.

Model and training script
: implements the
AttenGW
architecture and its optimization.

Inference notebook
: applies trained models to real detector data for inference.

We describe these components in more detail below.

2.1
Downloader

To construct a training dataset, we developed a configurable downloader that retrieves Hanford and Livingston strain from GWOSC and applies a sequence of quality-control steps. For a user-selected GPS window, we automatically require times when both detectors are collecting, excise padded regions around known events (which can, optionally, be saved separately), and apply optional raw-domain glitch clamping followed by optional amplitude and variance rejection criteria. A power spectral density is estimated from de-glitched full-band strain using Welch’s method. In addition, the strain can also be whitened using the estimated PSD. Each accepted window (noise or signal) is stored as an HDF5 file containing strain (raw or whitened), PSDs, and the frequency grid, forming a reproducible, analysis-ready dataset for training.

2.2
Data generator

To generate training and validation examples, we use a custom PyTorch dataloader that combines synthetic time-domain waveforms with real LIGO Hanford and Livingston noise. The noise samples are supplied by our downloader, whereas synthetic signals are supplied externally (e.g. through a surrogate waveform model or other waveform generator). In our default configuration, the dataloader receives raw strain time series from Hanford and Livingston. For each sample, in this raw-strain mode the dataloader either draws a pure-noise window or injects a waveform into a randomly selected stretch of strain from both detectors.

For injected samples, the signals can be optionally rescaled into a target SNR range and implement a simple SNR “curriculum” (higher SNRs are more likely in early epochs and gradually decrease over training). For the SNR evaluation, the PSDs are interpolated onto the FFT frequency grid and effectively band-limited to the LIGO sensitivity band (25–450 Hz), so that frequencies outside this range contribute negligibly. The strain is whitened using the same banded PSDs and cropped to a window of predefined length (’window length’), with a binary target label that is set to 1 in a short interval (’label width’) preceding the merger time and 0 elsewhere. To avoid teaching the network to fire on large glitches, we veto candidate positive windows in which the maximum absolute noise strain within the region that would be labeled as 1 exceeds a few local standard deviations, and resample those examples instead. For noise-only samples we keep typical non-Gaussian structure, rejecting only the most extreme transients, so that the model still sees realistic LIGO noise without being dominated by a small number of pathological glitches. for an injected-signal and noise-only example from the data generator output.

Alternatively, the same dataloader can operate on pre-whitened Hanford and Livingston strains. In this mode, we inject synthetic signals that are already whitened and scale them relative to the noise using an externally supplied amplitude schedule, without recomputing a matched-filter SNR inside the dataloader. This removes the need for repeated FFT and PSD operations, yielding a faster approximate curriculum.

This design provides flexibility in preprocessing choices, since the dataloader can operate on raw or pre-whitened strain and is compatible with a range of preprocessing, data-cleaning, and glitch-rejection criteria.

2.3
Model

To map fixed-length two-detector strain segments to per-timestep detection scores, we use a three-stage architecture: a per-detector Hierarchical Dilated Convolutional Network, a Cross-Attention Network (CAN), and an output head. With default parameters, used throughout the results section, the model encompasses 193,000 trainable parameters, allowing for lightweight implementation.

2.3.1
Per-detector encoder (HDCN)

The per-detector encoder follows a WaveNet-style stack of gated, dilated 1D convolutions with residual and skip connections
25
. Each of the two detector channels, LIGO Livingston (L) and LIGO Hanford (H), is first processed independently by the HDCN. Figure
2
presents a schematic of the HDCN as applied to the Hanford channel.

The HDCN maps the whitened strain time series
x_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
for detector
d\in\{\mathrm{H},\mathrm{L}\}
(“Channel Input”) to a sequence of 16-dimensional features of the same length (“Channel Output”). We first reshape
x_{d}
to a tensor of shape
(1,T)
and feed it into a stack of residual convolutional blocks. The first block begins with a
1\times 1
convolution that lifts the raw 1D strain to 16 channels, resulting in a tensor of shape
(16,T)
. Each subsequent block takes this 16-channel representation as input and

1.

applies a
1\times 1
input convolution that mixes information across the 16 channels at each timestep, followed by a ReLU activation;

2.

applies two parallel 1D dilated convolutions with kernel width 2 and a specified dilation factor
d_{\ell}
, one for a “filter” path and one for a “gate” path; a kernel of width 2 means that each output at time
t
combines two input samples, and the dilation factor controls how far apart these samples are in time (so larger
d_{\ell}
allows the block to relate points that are more widely separated). In our implementation each of these convolutions maps the 16-channel input to 32 channels;

3.

passes the filter path through a
\tanh
nonlinearity and the gate path through a sigmoid nonlinearity, and then combines them element-wise as
z=\tanh(f)\odot\sigma(g)

4.

projects the gated output
z
back to 16 channels with another
1\times 1
output convolution followed by a ReLU, ensuring that the block output has the same channel dimension as its input and can be added in a residual connection;

5.

adds this projected output to the result of the current input convolution to update the hidden state (residual connection), and, in parallel, records the same 16-channel output as a skip contribution that will be summed across all blocks at the end of the stack.

We use 33 such blocks in total, with dilation factors
d_{\ell}\in\{1,2,4,\dots,2^{10}\}
repeated three times across the stack; by repeating this pattern with increasing dilation factors in the parallel convolutions, the HDCN aggregates information over progressively longer time spans.

2.3.2
Cross-detector aggregator (CAN)

After per-detector feature extraction by the HDCN, we couple the Hanford and Livingston channels through a cross-attention module, mirroring the cross-attention processing by Vaswani et al.
26
, that operates along the time dimension. See Figure
3
for a schematic of the CAN and output module (together considered the “aggregator”). Let
H,L\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D}
denote the HDCN outputs for Hanford and Livingston, respectively, with time series length
T
and feature dimension
D=16
, in accordance with the HDCN processing steps. For Hanford attending to Livingston, we form queries from
H
and keys/values from
L
:

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=HW_{Q},

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=LW_{K},

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=LW_{V},

(1)

where
W_{Q},W_{K},W_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d_{k}}
are learned projections. We then apply scaled dot-product attention along the time axis,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\mathsf{T}}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V,

(2)

followed by a linear output projection that maps the cross-attention output back to
\mathbb{R}^{T\times D}
. This yields an updated Hanford feature sequence in which each timestep is a weighted combination of Livingston features across all times. A second, symmetric instance of the same module swaps the roles of
H
and
L
, so that Livingston attends to Hanford in an analogous way. This produces updated representations for each channel that are aware of context supplied by the other channel.

Our choice of a cross-attention–based aggregator, rather than a graph-based aggregation module, is motivated by results from sequence-processing and multi-sensor fusion tasks. In our setting, a graph-style block mixes Hanford and Livingston features only at the same step in the sequence, whereas a cross-attention layer can let each detector–time
feature attend to
all
timesteps in the other detector and adapts its weighting based on the signal and noise in the full window. The same
mechanism can, in principle, be extended to cases where some detectors are
missing or have reduced duty cycle, and in particular for use with
longer signals such as BNS mergers, where detector quality
and relative contribution can vary significantly across the inspiral. From a
theoretical perspective, transformer-style attention with positional
encodings can approximate very general sequence-to-sequence mappings

[
30
]
, and attention-based architectures have been
shown to match or outperform graph-based models on a variety of structured
and multimodal tasks
[
11
,
9
]
.
In practice, cross-attention has repeatedly improved performance in
multi-sensor and multimodal aggregation problems by learning context-dependent
weights between input streams, rather than relying on fixed positional rules

[
21
,
8
]
. Together, these results support
using cross-attention as a more flexible and adaptive mechanism for
detector aggregation in our gravitational-wave detection model.

2.3.3
Output module

In the final stage, the updated feature sequences from Hanford and Livingston are concatenated along a detector axis and reduced with a per-timestep max operation, yielding a single 16-channel sequence that emphasizes the most informative detector at each timestep. This sequence is passed through a

1\times 1
convolution and a sigmoid activation to produce a scalar output
p^{t}\in[0,1]
for each timestep
t
, interpreted as the model’s confidence that the corresponding sample belongs to the signal class.

2.4
Training

We train
AttenGW
in PyTorch Lightning using distributed data parallelism across available GPUs. The network is optimized with the Adam optimizer
12
, using a binary cross-entropy loss between predicted scores and the binary labels defined in the data generator. A
ReduceLROnPlateau
scheduler monitors the validation loss and reduces the learning rate when progress stalls. The SNRs seen by the models can optionally be rescaled into fixed target bands, with the injection mix gradually decaying from predominantly high-SNR samples to predominantly lower-SNR signals over a predetermined number of epochs. Training and validation use the same data pipeline and whitening procedure described above, differing only in the fixed SNR curriculum for the validation set.

2.5
Inference

For inference, we use a standalone Jupyter notebook that loads the trained
AttenGW
model together with the PSDs and strain segments produced by the downloader. Each HDF5 file is whitened using the same band-limited PSD interpolation as in training, and then decomposed into overlapping short windows for the Hanford and Livingston channels, which are passed through the network to obtain per-timestep detection scores. A simple peak-finding stage based on
scipy.signal.find_peaks
is then applied to the 1D score time series, with thresholds on peak height, average height, and minimum width, yielding a list of candidate event times for each processed file.

Training

Real O3b data and benchmark comparison

Training

Here, we do not process raw strain data with our own download module, but instead use pre-whitened Hanford and Livingston training and validation time series from three fixed O3a noise segments (GPS 1240989696, 1241128960, and 1242738688)

Inference

Our model processes six 68-minute-long segments of real data in under 2 minutes using one A100 GPU and 16 CPUs on the DeltaAI cluster. Using our inference notebook, we process all available data from February 2020 in parallel chunks.

To contextualize performance, we note that earlier implementations of spatiotemporal GW detectors reported false-positive counts on the order of several hundred and up to
10^{3}
per network over the February 2020 O3 dataset. Note that, while Ref.
22
considers a three-detector HLV network and notes the reduced sensitivity of Virgo during O3b, the
AttenGW
model in this implementation uses only the two LIGO detectors. Exclusion of the Virgo channel is therefore one plausible contributor to any improvement in model performance. We re-implemented the 3-channel architecture of Tian et al. (including whitened Virgo-strain) and trained it on the pre-whitened O3a Hanford and Livingston segments described above. In this configuration, a single network produces
\mathcal{O}(10^{2}
–
10^{3})
background triggers over the month, consistent with that regime. In the analysis of Ref.
22
, suppressing these background triggers to zero required coincidence across six independently trained models.

On the same February 2020 data (O3b run) and with the same detection efficiency, a single instance of our cross-attention model yields 124 background triggers for the month. Requiring coincidence across only two independently trained models reduces this to 5 false positives over the month, and a three-model coincidence eliminates all background triggers at the same detection efficiency. This corresponds to a reduction of the per-model background by a factor of a few and a reduction of the required ensemble size from six to three networks. Unlike Tian et al., we do not perform an additional fine-tuning stage on February 2020 (O3b) data; the same training configuration using O3a data only is applied throughout in order to challenge the model’s generalization abilities.

4
Discussion

This work introduces
AttenGW
, a lightweight, reproducible, and open-source gravitational-wave detection pipeline that integrates an attention-based multi-detector architecture with a complete preprocessing, data-generation, training, and inference framework. From an astronomical-computing perspective, the contribution is twofold: it demonstrates that attention mechanisms provide a viable next step in ML-based GW searches, and it provides a transparent, end-to-end software environment that enables others to evaluate, adapt, and extend the method on real LIGO data.

Ensemble deep-learning architectures are now common in gravitational-wave searches, where multiple independently trained models are combined to control false alarms
29
,
13
,
1
, and the ensemble baseline we compare against follows this pattern by relying on relatively large ensembles of independently trained models, each contributing many false positives before coincidence cuts were applied. In our case, single instances of the two-channel cross-attention aggregator already yield fewer false triggers per model, and we still require an ensemble, but of smaller size, to reach zero false positives on this month. Ensemble-coincidence schemes, including the one used here on February 2020 data, require more resources to train and maintain and remain sensitive to training stochasticity and to the parameter and model choices used to define coincidence, which complicates reproducibility. Within the software stack we release, the reduced per-model background and smaller ensemble size mean that attention-based aggregation can be adopted as a drop-in replacement for earlier graph-based modules while improving robustness. At the same time, the end-to-end pipeline remains computationally practical: pure inference on continuous O3 strain runs at high throughput with six 68-minute O3b segments processed in under two minutes on a single A100 GPU. This positions
AttenGW
as a realistic choice for exploratory ML-based searches and benchmarking on shared HPC resources. Further reducing any dependence on ensembles and achieving comparable background suppression with detectors that are stable at the single-model level is therefore a natural direction for future work, with the present results indicating that this may be achievable with changes in architecture and pre-processing choices.

This study does not disentangle how much of the observed improvement is due to the cross-attention aggregation itself and how much is based on our choices of training hyperparameters. While we did not tune hyperparameters across detection scenarios beyond selecting a single training configuration to keep the model lightweight, within that fixed setup, some choices, like weight regularization or exclusion of the Virgo channel, may contribute to stabilizing training relative to earlier models in addition to the model architecture. Empirically, this configuration yields smaller variation and lower false-positive rates per model than the baseline we compare against. Our current setup is limited by its sensitivity to the noise morphologies seen during training and by deliberately simple preprocessing.

Exploring more complex attention-based architectures, together with a more systematic treatment of SNR curricula and noise conditions and online fine-tuning, may help stabilize single-model detectors and eventually extend learning-based searches to more complex sources, such as spin-precessing binaries. To facilitate this, we release our full training and inference pipeline to encourage further experimentation with alternative architectures, curricula, and preprocessing strategies within a reproducible framework.
