Ni-interlayer-induced modulation of electrical and dielectric properties in ZnO/Ni/ZnO thin films fabricated using hybrid deposition techniques
Authors: S. S. Fouad, Hytham A. Abd El-Ghany, M. Nabil, E. Baradács, Neeraj Mehta, Z. Erdélyi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interface engineering in oxide multilayers provides a powerful pathway to regulate charge transport and dielectric response in low-dimensional material systems. Here, ZnO/Ni/ZnO (ZNZ) trilayer thin films with fixed ZnO thickness and systematically varied Ni interlayer thickness are fabricated using a hybrid combination of atomic layer deposition (ALD) and direct current magnetron sputtering (DCMS). Structural analyses confirm the formation of well-defined multilayer architectures with thickness-dependent interfacial modification. Broadband alternating current (AC) electrical measurements reveal a pronounced frequency-dependent increase in conductivity, strongly governed by the Ni interlayer thickness. Analysis of the frequency exponent indicates that correlated barrier hopping is the dominant transport mechanism, demonstrating that charge transport is mediated by Ni-modified interfacial potential barriers rather than bulk conduction. Increasing Ni thickness results in higher barrier heights and lower activation energies, indicating more efficient interfacial hopping pathways. The dielectric constant, dielectric loss, and loss tangent decrease with frequency while increasing with temperature, consistent with thermally activated dielectric relaxation dominated by interfacial polarization effects. These findings establish metallic interlayer thickness as an effective control parameter for tuning charge dynamics and dielectric behavior in ZnO-based multilayers, highlighting the potential of interface-driven design strategies for advanced electronic, capacitive, and energy-storage applications.

Introduction

Low-dimensional and layered material systems have emerged as key platforms for realizing tunable electronic and dielectric functionalities, where interfaces play a decisive role in governing charge transport and polarization dynamics1,2. In such systems, electrical behavior is often dominated not by the bulk properties of the constituent layers but by interfacial barriers, defect states, and charge redistribution across heterojunctions. Oxide-based multilayers are particularly attractive in this context due to their structural compatibility, chemical stability, and sensitivity to interlayer coupling3. By introducing metallic or semimetallic interlayers into oxide stacks, it becomes possible to engineer interfacial potential landscapes, modify carrier-hopping pathways, and control dielectric relaxation processes. Understanding how these interfaces regulate transport mechanisms is therefore essential for the design of next-generation capacitors, sensors, and energy-storage devices based on layered and quasi-two-dimensional architectures. Metal oxides have garnered considerable interest due to their pivotal role in advancing energy conversion and storage technologies across a broad range of technical applications4,5. Oxide materials form the foundation of various innovative functional materials. To enhance the electrical performance of oxides, various modification techniques and fabrication methods have been employed6,7. Previous studies suggest that preparing multicomponent oxides is a promising approach for such modifications. The incorporation of ZnO with transition metals such as Co, Ni, Fe, and Mn has generated significant attention in the literature due to their inherent applications in diverse industries, including optoelectronic devices and solar cells. Improving the device’s performance is a key objective in these applications8–10. Incorporating Ni as a dopant/interlayer offers a promising route to enhance the functional properties of ZnO-based nanostructures, owing to its superior electrical conductivity, strong metal–semiconductor coupling, and remarkable stability. Compared to conventional elements such as Cu, Fe, and Mn, Ni provides better band alignment and maintains crystal integrity, making it a more effective element for improving overall device performance and functionality11–13.

Previous research has investigated the AC electrical properties of multilayer oxide thin films such as TiO2/ZnO, ZnO/Cu/ZnO, and ZnO/Ag/ZnO, prepared by DCMS and ALD14–18. The ZnO layers deposited by ALD provide smooth, uniform, high-quality semiconducting films. The metallic interlayer introduced by (DC sputtering) introduces controlled defects, modifies electrical and optical properties, and influences the interface characterization. Together, they result in well-controlled interfaces, improved crystallinity, and enhanced optical/electronic performance. These improvements have demonstrated that external parameters, including frequency and temperature, can significantly influence dielectric properties. The dielectric constants and loss tangents are fundamental for understanding the behavior of these materials in various functional contexts. In some multilayer oxides, the correlated barrier hopping (CBH) model has been identified as the primary mechanism of conduction.

Understanding the AC conductivity and dielectric behavior of multilayer oxides is crucial for designing and optimizing devices such as sensors, capacitors, and energy storage systems. The incorporation of Ni into ZnO has been reported to enhance optical and magnetic properties for energy storage applications in nanogenerators and integrated photonic devices19–21. In this work, we demonstrate that a discrete metallic interlayer provides a practical, continuous means to regulate charge transport and dielectric relaxation in oxide multilayer systems. By systematically varying the thickness of a Ni interlayer sandwiched between ZnO layers, we isolate the role of interface-controlled barrier modulation in the frequency- and temperature-dependent electrical response. Unlike conventional bulk doping approaches, this strategy preserves the semiconducting framework while enabling tunable hopping transport governed by correlated barrier hopping and interfacial potential landscapes. Through combined structural analysis and broadband AC electrical measurements, we establish clear structure–transport–dielectric correlations that reveal how metallic interlayers reshape activation energy, barrier height, and relaxation dynamics. The results define an interface-engineering route that is broadly applicable to layered and quasi-two-dimensional oxide architectures for capacitive, sensing, and energy-storage applications.

While ZnO-based multilayers incorporating metallic components have been previously explored13,15,17–21, most reported studies rely on bulk doping or fixed metal layers, which often obscure the distinct role of interfaces in governing charge transport and dielectric relaxation. In particular, the continuous modulation of interfacial potential barriers through controlled metallic interlayer thickness remains insufficiently understood. In this work, we demonstrate an interface-engineering strategy in which a discrete Ni interlayer of systematically varied thickness is embedded between two ZnO layers, enabling direct regulation of hopping transport and dielectric response without altering the semiconducting framework of ZnO. By combining atomic layer deposition and DC magnetron sputtering, we achieve well-defined ZNZ trilayers that allow isolation of thickness-dependent interfacial effects. Through comprehensive AC electrical and dielectric investigations over wide temperature and frequency ranges, we establish clear correlations between Ni interlayer thickness, barrier height, activation energy, and dielectric relaxation dynamics. The results reveal how metallic interlayers reshape correlated barrier hopping processes and interfacial polarization, providing a tunable and scalable route for controlling charge dynamics in oxide multilayer systems relevant to capacitive, sensing, and energy-storage applications.

Materials and scientific methods

Four multilayered thin films with a standard ZNZ trilayer structure, differing in the thickness of the Ni interlayer, were fabricated using atomic layer deposition (ALD) for the ZnO layers and DC magnetron sputtering for the Ni interlayers. The thickness and microstructural parameters of the multilayer films were determined using a combination of X-ray reflectivity (XRR), spectroscopic ellipsometry, profilometry, and cross-sectional SEM analysis to ensure measurement reliability. The thickness values obtained from SEM cross-sectional images were calibrated and cross-verified using profilometer and ellipsometry measurements to minimize scale-bar-related uncertainties. Multiple measurements were performed at different positions across each sample and averaged to improve accuracy and reproducibility. The XRR-derived parameters, including density and interfacial roughness, were obtained by least-squares fitting of the reflectivity profiles, and the associated uncertainties were estimated from the fitting tolerance and instrumental resolution.

The films were successfully deposited on both soda-lime glass substrates. The multilayer preparation process was as follows: ZnO layers with a nominal thickness of 70 nm were deposited using a Beneq TFS-200-186 ALD system in thermal mode at 200 °C. The depositions were carried out over 391 cycles on glass (25 × 25 mm) substrates. The pulse and purge times for both diethylzinc (DEZ) and H2O precursors were 0.3 s and 3 s, respectively. The nitrogen gas flow rates for the NOP and NOV lines were set to 300 sccm. For the Ni interlayer deposition, a base pressure of Pbase = 4 × 10−7 mbar was achieved prior to sputtering. Ni layers with nominal thicknesses of 10, 30, 50, and 70 nm were deposited at an argon working pressure of PAr = 6 × 10−3 mbar. Deposition of thin-films was conducted in current-controlled mode at a current of 150 mA, a voltage of 300 V, and a power of 45 W. Prior to the deposition of each Ni layer, the high-purity nickel target was pre-sputtered for approximately 2 min to eliminate any surface contaminants and ensure stable deposition conditions. The sputtering times required to obtain Ni interlayers with nominal thicknesses of 10, 30, 50, and 70 nm were 68 s, 204 s, 340 s, and 476 s, respectively. Structural characterization was carried out using grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) employing a rotating copper anode with a wavelength of 0.154 nm. The thickness of the ZnO films was obtained from variable-angle spectroscopic ellipsometry measurements using a Semilab SE-2000 system, whereas the Ni layer thickness was determined with an AMBIOS XP-1 profilometer. Cross-sectional thickness values obtained from SEM micrographs were calibrated and cross-verified using profilometer and spectroscopic ellipsometry measurements to minimize scale-bar-related uncertainties. Multiple measurements were performed at different positions across each sample and averaged to improve the reliability and accuracy of the reported thickness values.

Dielectric measurements of the samples were performed using a Keysight E4990A impedance analyzer. Measurements were carried out over the frequency range 100 Hz to 200 kHz with an AC amplitude of 500 mV. The samples were placed between two parallel electrodes in a capacitor-like configuration, with an effective surface area of 1 cm × 0.5 cm and a separation distance of 1 mm, determined by the thickness of the glass substrate on which the ZNZ trilayers were deposited. Results and discussion

AC conductivity measurements provide valuable insights into charge-transport mechanisms in multilayer oxides and are crucial for assessing their suitability for device applications. The plots of AC conductivity (σac) versus Ln ω for the samples ZN10Z, ZN30Z, ZN50Z, and ZN70Z measured at different temperatures are presented in Fig. 6. The AC conductivity values presented in Fig. 6 correspond to the real part of the complex conductivity calculated from impedance measurements using the measured real component of impedance. This represents the effective conductive response of the trilayer structure. These measurements are primarily used to characterize the atomic-scale processes that influence the electrical behavior of the materials and to identify the nature of the underlying conduction mechanisms. Since the present system consists of ZNZ trilayers, the measured electrical response arises from the combined contribution of the two ZnO layers, the metallic Ni interlayer, and their interfaces. In principle, such a multilayer structure can be represented by an equivalent circuit comprising separate impedance elements for each layer, along with interfacial contributions. In the present work, the reported AC conductivity represents the effective total conductivity of the entire trilayer stack obtained from impedance measurements. The observed frequency-dependent behavior is therefore interpreted in terms of dominant hopping transport within the ZnO layers modulated by the Ni interlayer and interfacial potential barriers.Fig. 6Frequency dependence of AC conductivity for the four compositions of ZN10Z, ZN30Z, ZN50Z and ZN70Z, respectively, at different temperatures.

The exponent s is not fixed and varies with both temperature and frequency. The slope values (frequency exponent s) obtained from the linear fits lie in the range 0.8–0.95 for all samples, consistent with the correlated barrier hopping model.

As shown in Fig. 6, the AC conductivity (σac) exhibits a clear upward trend with increasing frequency across all four thin-film samples. Figure 7 illustrates the frequency-dependent behaviour of σac at 30 °C for the ZN10Z, ZN30Z, ZN50Z, and ZN70Z samples, revealing that σac increases not only with increasing frequency but also with higher Ni concentration. A slight deviation/dip observed in the ZN50Z sample around ln(ω) ≈ 13 may be attributed to localized interfacial polarization and trapping–detrapping processes at ZnO/Ni interfaces, which temporarily hinder charge hopping. A clear two-slope behavior is observed in the frequency-dependent conductivity curves. The low-frequency region is dominated by interfacial and long-range hopping processes, whereas the high-frequency region corresponds to localized hopping between defect states. The Ni interlayer is expected to modify the interfacial electronic structure and facilitate carrier transport across the ZnO layers by reducing interfacial resistance and introducing additional hopping pathways. This effect can reduce overall resistance and enhance the AC conductivity. Further, the Ni interlayer modifies the band alignment at the ZnO/Ni interfaces and provides additional conductive pathways for charge transport. This leads to enhanced carrier injection into the ZnO layers and reduced interfacial resistance. Although direct Hall-effect measurements were not performed, the observed increase in AC conductivity and reduction in activation energy with increasing Ni thickness provide indirect evidence for improved carrier transport across the multilayer interfaces. The observed increase in AC conductivity may be attributed to a hopping conduction mechanism between localized states within the ZnO layers, facilitated by the presence of the Ni interlayer, as shown in the schematic of Fig. 8. As seen, Wm increases with increasing Ni layer thickness. This increase may influence the conduction mechanism by altering electrical conductivity, a key factor in understanding a material’s ability to conduct an electric current. Although the calculated Wm increases, the AC conductivity also improves, as mentioned earlier. This behavior can be explained within the CBH framework, where the conductivity depends not only on Wm but also on the density of localized states near the Fermi level and the hopping distance. The increase in localized states due to Ni incorporation enhances the hopping probability, compensating for the increase in barrier height and leading to an overall increase in conductivity. Although an increase in barrier height typically reduces hopping probability, in the present multilayer system, this effect is offset by improved carrier transport and interfacial transport pathways introduced by the Ni interlayer. As a result, the overall conductivity increases despite the higher barrier height. In particular, the increase in Wm with Ni incorporation may result from modifications in band alignment and the enhancement of interfacial potential barriers induced by Ni-induced structural and electronic changes. This behavior supports the presence of a hopping conduction mechanism and highlights its relevance in device design and performance. The simultaneous increase in both Wm and Eg with increasing Ni content may be attributed to dopant-induced modifications of the electronic structure and to interface effects. The optical band gap values (Eg) were taken from previously experimental studies in25 and are included here for correlation with the electrical parameters.

Ni incorporation modifies the electronic structure of the ZNZ system by reducing deep defect states while simultaneously introducing localized potential fluctuations at the ZnO/Ni interfaces. These combined effects enhance charge carrier localization and favor hopping conduction. Thus, the observed changes in band gap and barrier height reflect interfacial electronic modifications rather than purely bulk ordering or disordering effects. This correlation indicates a systematic modification of the electronic structure of the ZNZ system with increasing Ni concentration26,27. As observed, ΔE is inversely proportional to both the frequency and the thickness of the Ni layer. This behavior may be attributed to several factors. An increase in Ni thickness can lead to a higher carrier concentration, thereby reducing the activation energy required for conduction. Additionally, a thicker Ni layer may modify the electronic structure of all samples of the ZNZ system, leading to a more pronounced decrease in ΔE as reported by other research groups29–31. Moreover, at higher applied frequencies, electronic transitions between localized states are more readily facilitated, leading to a reduction in the observed energy activation32,33. These findings collectively support the conclusion that a hopping conduction mechanism governs charge transport in the ZNZ system. A quantitative analysis of the frequency exponent, activation energy, and barrier height has been carried out to establish correlations between structural parameters and electrical transport behavior. The systematic variation of these parameters with Ni interlayer thickness provides strong support for hopping conduction governed by interfacial barrier modulation in the ZNZ trilayer system.

The dielectric constant provides valuable information about a material’s polarization and relaxation behavior. Dielectric properties significantly influence signal propagation in electronic devices and are critical for energy storage applications. The possible contribution of the glass substrate to the measured dielectric response was carefully considered. Since the glass substrate has a relatively low, nearly frequency-independent dielectric constant over the investigated frequency range and its thickness remains constant across all samples, its contribution acts mainly as a constant background. Therefore, the observed variations in dielectric properties are primarily attributed to the ZNZ trilayer structure. A decrease in both ε1 and ε2 is observed with increasing frequency, while an increase is observed with increasing temperature, indicating the presence of a dielectric relaxation process. At low frequencies, charge carriers can more effectively follow the externally applied electric field, resulting in higher dielectric constants. In contrast, at higher frequencies, the charge carriers cannot respond quickly enough to the rapidly oscillating field, leading to a reduction in both ε1 and ε2. The observed decrease in dielectric constant with increasing frequency also suggests the suppression of interfacial processes, as these mechanisms are unable to respond to the rapidly varying electric field at higher frequencies34. Relatively low values of ε1 and ε2 at higher frequencies indicate improved optical quality; however, direct conclusions about the optical range require measurements in the optical frequency region. Reduced defect density in the prepared samples makes them suitable for nonlinear optical applications. The loss tangent (tanδ) is a crucial parameter for characterizing dielectric behavior. It represents the ratio of energy lost to energy stored in the system and reflects the material’s ability to dissipate energy. The loss tangent is defined as: tan δ = ε2/ε1. Figures 13c, 14c, 15c and 16c illustrate the frequency-dependent behavior of tanδ at different temperatures for the thin-film samples ZN10Z, ZN30Z, ZN50Z, and ZN70Z under investigation.

As observed, the obtained tan δ values are directly proportional to temperature, showing a rapid decrease at low frequencies (ω), while becoming nearly frequency independent at higher frequencies. The behaviour of tan δ closely resembles that of ε1 and ε2. The influence of the Ni interlayer on the behaviour of tan δ in the ZNZ system can significantly impact the electronic and structural properties, especially at the interfaces, where the interaction between Ni and ZnO can lead to the formation of interfacial dipoles and charge redistribution, affecting the overall behaviour of the thin films under study. To provide a clear and more scientifically precise explanation of the observed behavior, consider the following: at low frequencies, ion migration within the material dominates the dielectric loss; at moderate frequencies, the observed values of tanδ result from both ion hopping and ion polarization loss; as the frequency increases, ion vibrations tend to be independent of the external field, which contributes minimally to energy dissipation35.

Conclusion

To summarize our findings, we have prepared and investigated the effect of Ni content on the structural, AC conductivity, and dielectric properties of ZN10Z, ZN30Z, ZN50Z, and ZN70Z thin films. GIXRD measurements confirmed the successful incorporation of Ni into the ZNZ system, while XRR analysis verified the expected thickness values of the four thin film samples. AC conductivity increased with frequency across the entire temperature range studied. The parameters extracted using the power-law model support the predominance of the Correlated Barrier Hopping (CBH) mechanism as the most appropriate description of the conduction process in the ZNZ system. Furthermore, the observed decrease in the dielectric constant, dielectric loss, and loss tangent (tan δ) with increasing frequency is a common and well-understood phenomenon. This behaviour provides valuable insights into dielectric relaxation and charge transport. At the same time, Ni interlayer thickness enables controlled tuning of conductivity, activation energy, and dielectric response, highlighting potential applications in nanoscale energy storage, capacitors, sensors, and optoelectronic devices.

Limitations and future work

While the present work demonstrates clear trends in the modulation of electrical and dielectric properties with Ni interlayer thickness in ZnO/Ni/ZnO trilayer thin films, some aspects still need further exploration. The analysis of the conduction mechanism is primarily based on AC conductivity data interpreted using the correlated barrier hopping (CBH) model, and additional techniques such as Hall-effect measurements could provide complementary quantitative insights into carrier transport. The role of the Ni interlayer in modifying interfacial potential barriers is supported by consistent structural and electrical correlations; however, a more detailed understanding could be achieved through advanced interface-sensitive characterization methods. Overall, these considerations will provide opportunities for future work to build upon the current findings and further strengthen the understanding of interface-driven transport in such multilayer systems.
