A glucose metabolism-targeted nanoplatform amplifies disulfidptosis-associated immunogenic cell death in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Jialin Xu, Yanting Sun, Bin Huang, Yifan Zhang, Hongyu Shen, Zhengxing Zhou, Min Tian, Tianyu Zeng, Shuai Wu, Yongmei Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Disulfidptosis is a metabolism-dependent form of cell death driven by disulfide stress that induces actin cytoskeletal collapse in SLC7A11-high cells under glucose deprivation. However, strategies to therapeutically exploit this vulnerability remain underdeveloped. Given the high dependence of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) on glucose metabolism, we engineered a tumor microenvironment-responsive MnOx nanoplatform for sustained delivery of the GLUT1 inhibitor BAY-876 and concurrent glutathione (GSH) depletion. Within this integrated system, BAY-876 restricts GLUT1-mediated glucose uptake, thereby reducing the availability of reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), impairing the reduction of cystine to cysteine, and promoting intracellular cystine accumulation. In parallel, MnOx undergoes GSH-triggered degradation in the GSH-rich intracellular environment of tumor cells, releasing Mn2+ and consuming intracellular GSH, thereby weakening cellular antioxidant defenses. Glucose restriction-induced NADPH depletion and MnOx-mediated GSH depletion synergistically intensify disulfide stress. Beyond direct cytotoxicity, the resulting metabolic and redox imbalance promotes the surface exposure and extracellular release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby enhancing immunogenic cell death (ICD). This process facilitates dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation, shifts the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment toward an immune-active phenotype, and ultimately enhances T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity. Collectively, this work establishes disulfidptosis as a therapeutically actionable metabolic vulnerability and presents a nanotherapeutic strategy that integrates metabolic intervention with immune activation for TNBC treatment.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive breast cancer subtype characterized by pronounced molecular heterogeneity, high metastatic propensity, and poor prognosis [1,2]. The absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression limits the available therapeutic options, with chemotherapy remaining a cornerstone of systemic treatment [3,4]. Although poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors, antibody-drug conjugates, and immune checkpoint blockade have shown clinical benefit in selected patients, their overall efficacy is frequently constrained by tumor heterogeneity, acquired resistance, and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. These challenges underscore the urgent need for therapeutic strategies that exploit TNBC-specific biological vulnerabilities while simultaneously overcoming barriers related to tumor selectivity and immune suppression.

Metabolic reprogramming represents a particularly compelling vulnerability in TNBC, as it drives both malignant progression and immune dysfunction [9,10]. Compared with other breast cancer subtypes, TNBC commonly displays elevated aerobic glycolysis, relying on excessive glucose uptake and lactate production to sustain rapid proliferation [11,12]. This glycolytic phenotype not only supports rapid tumor cell proliferation but also remodels the TME by depleting shared nutrients and increasing extracellular acidification [13]. Such metabolic stress compromises the effector functions of CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells and simultaneously promotes the accumulation of immunosuppressive populations, including regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [14]. Thus, glycolytic addiction represents a therapeutically actionable vulnerability in TNBC.

A particularly compelling consequence of glucose restriction is the induction of disulfidptosis, a recently identified form of regulated cell death driven by disulfide stress [15,16]. TNBC cells frequently upregulate glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) to sustain glycolytic flux [17], and inhibition of glucose uptake reduces nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) production, disrupts redox homeostasis, and impairs cystine reduction [18]. In SLC7A11-high tumor cells, this metabolic-redox imbalance promotes intracellular cystine accumulation and aberrant disulfide bonding in actin cytoskeletal proteins, leading to cytoskeletal collapse and ultimately disulfidptosis [19]. These findings identify SLC7A11-high TNBC as a metabolically vulnerable subtype and support GLUT1 inhibition as a rational strategy to therapeutically exploit disulfidptosis susceptibility. However, the translational potential of the selective GLUT1 inhibitor BAY-876 is limited by rapid systemic clearance, insufficient tumor selectivity, and potential off-target toxicity, highlighting the need for a tumor-responsive delivery strategy.

Nanomedicine can improve intratumoral drug accumulation and reduce systemic exposure, thereby enhancing therapeutic selectivity [[20], [21], [22]]. Moreover, the integration of functional materials with therapeutic agents enables tumor-responsive activation and improved spatiotemporal control, providing additional opportunities for precision cancer therapy [23]. In particular, microenvironment-responsive nanoplatforms can undergo GSH-triggered degradation in the GSH-rich intracellular environment of tumor cells, thereby enabling localized drug release while concomitantly depleting intracellular GSH [24,25]. Among them, MnOx-based nanoplatforms are particularly attractive because their GSH-responsive degradation weakens intracellular antioxidant defense and reduces cellular reducing capacity. In SLC7A11-high TNBC, such GSH depletion may cooperate with glucose restriction-induced NADPH depletion to exacerbate disulfide stress. The resulting disruption of metabolic and redox homeostasis can trigger disulfidptosis and promote immunogenic cell death (ICD), as characterized by calreticulin exposure, ATP release, and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) release, thereby providing an opportunity to couple metabolic intervention with immune activation [[26], [27], [28]]. In this study, we developed a GSH-responsive MnOx@BAY-876 nanoplatform for delivery of BAY-876 in SLC7A11-high TNBC. MnOx@BAY-876 was designed to restrict glucose metabolism, weaken antioxidant defenses, and promote immune remodeling. Collectively, our findings support the coordinated disruption of metabolic and intracellular antioxidant defense as a therapeutic strategy for exploiting disulfidptosis vulnerability and enhancing antitumor immunity in TNBC (Scheme 1).Scheme 1Schematic illustration of the in vivo therapeutic pathway of MnOx@BAY-876 for inducing disulfidptosis and immune remodeling in TNBC.

MnOx-BAY-876 was fabricated through a controllable synthesis strategy and exhibits GSH-responsive degradation and drug release. Following cellular internalization, the nanoplatform efficiently delivers the GLUT1 inhibitor BAY-876, thereby suppressing glucose uptake, inducing metabolic stress, and reducing NADPH availability. Meanwhile, GSH-triggered degradation of MnOx depletes intracellular GSH and weakens cellular reducing capacity. Reduced NADPH levels and GSH depletion indicate diminished cellular reducing capacity and antioxidant defense, accompanied by disrupted cellular redox homeostasis, exacerbates disulfide stress, and ultimately triggers disulfidptosis. Notably, the resulting metabolic-redox disruption enhances ICD-related responses, facilitating dendritic cell-mediated antigen presentation. This cascade ultimately elicits robust antitumor immune responses.

Materials and methods

Preparation, characterization, and drug release of MnOx@BAY-876

MnOx@BAY-876 was prepared via a solution-based synthesis followed by drug loading. 0.1 g of potassium permanganate (KMnO4) was dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water and magnetically stirred at 400 rpm for 30 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of oleic acid was added dropwise under continuous stirring, and the reaction was allowed to proceed at room temperature for 5 h. After completion, the reaction mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min to collect the precipitate, which was washed three times with deionized water and three times with absolute ethanol. The resulting product was vacuum-dried at 65 °C for 2 h. For drug loading, 10 mg of MnOx was dispersed in 5 mL of deionized water by sonication, while 5 mg of BAY-876 was dissolved in 5 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The two solutions were mixed and magnetically stirred overnight at room temperature to facilitate the adsorption of BAY-876 onto the MnOx nanocarrier. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the precipitate was washed three times with deionized water to remove unbound drug. The collected supernatants were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) to quantify free BAY-876, and the drug loading content (DLC) was calculated as follows:DLC (%) = [(Initial amount of BAY-876 − amount of free BAY-876 in the supernatant) /(Mass of MnOx + Loaded BAY-876)] × 100%

The morphology and elemental distribution of MnOx@BAY-876 were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping. The surface elemental composition and crystalline structure were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to characterize the functional groups and confirm the incorporation of BAY-876. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the nanomaterials were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS). After incubation in 10 mM GSH, structural changes of the nanomaterials were examined by TEM, and the concentration of manganese ions released into the supernatant was determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). In vitro release of BAY-876 and manganese ions from MnOx@BAY-876 was evaluated using a dialysis method. Briefly, 1 mL of MnOx@BAY-876 suspension (10 mg/mL) was transferred into a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff of 1000 Da and immersed in 20 mL of release medium. To evaluate GSH-triggered release, the dialysis bag was incubated in PBS containing 10 mM GSH at pH 7.4. To independently assess the effect of environmental acidity, samples were incubated in GSH-free release media adjusted to pH 7.4, 6.4, or 4.4. All samples were incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking at 100 rpm. At predetermined time points, 1 mL of the external release medium was collected and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium. An aliquot of each collected sample (100 μL) was analyzed by UV-vis spectrophotometry at 320 nm. The amount of released BAY-876 was calculated using a standard calibration curve (Fig. S18).

Cellular uptake

Cy5-labeled MnOx@BAY-876 was prepared by physical adsorption. Briefly, MnOx@BAY-876 nanoplatforms were dispersed in deionized water at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and mixed with Cy5 dye at a nanoparticle-to-dye mass ratio of 1:1. The mixture was gently stirred in the dark at room temperature for 12 h. Subsequently, the Cy5-labeled nanoplatform was collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min and washed three times with deionized water to remove unbound Cy5. The complete removal of free Cy5 was confirmed by measuring the fluorescence intensity of washing solutions until no detectable fluorescence remained. The resulting Cy5-labeled MnOx@BAY-876 nanoplatform was stored at 4 °C in the dark until use.

For confocal fluorescence imaging, 4T1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured overnight. When the cells reached approximately 70% confluence, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing Cy5-labeled MnOx@BAY-876 at a nanoparticle concentration of 10 μg/mL. After incubation for 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h, the cells were washed three times with cold PBS to remove extracellular nanoplatform and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. The cells were then washed three times with PBS, and the nuclei were stained with DAPI (Yeasen, 36308 ES) in the dark. After an additional PBS wash, fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Cy5 and DAPI fluorescence signals were detected using the corresponding excitation and emission settings. For flow cytometric analysis, 4T1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and treated with Cy5-labeled MnOx@BAY-876 under the same conditions. At the indicated time points, the cells were washed three times with cold PBS, detached using trypsin without EDTA, and collected by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 10 min. The cell pellets were resuspended in PBS, and Cy5 fluorescence intensity was measured using a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). At least 10,000 cells were recorded for each sample. Cellular uptake was quantified as the mean fluorescence intensity of Cy5 using FlowJo software.

Cell death and live/dead fluorescence assays

Cells were treated under different conditions, including glucose-containing medium, glucose-free medium, MnOx (10 μg/mL), or MnOx@BAY-876 (10 μg/mL). After treatment, cells were collected, washed twice with PBS, and stained using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGA1102-20). Annexin V-FITC/PI-defined cell death was analyzed by flow cytometry. To exclude the involvement of other cell death pathways and to further define the type of cell death induced by MnOx@BAY-876, cells in the logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized, resuspended as single-cell suspensions, and seeded into 6-well plates, followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2. When cell confluence reached approximately 70%, cells were treated with MnOx@BAY-876 in the presence or absence of various cell death inhibitors, including necrostatin-1 (5 μM), Tempol (10 μM), Z-VAD-FMK (5 μM), chloroquine (CQ, 5 μM), ferrostatin-1 (5 μM), and tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) (0.5 mM). After 12 h of treatment, cell death was assessed using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining. For fluorescence-based live/dead cell imaging, cells were seeded in confocal dishes and subjected to the indicated treatments, followed by staining with a Calcein-AM/PI Cell Staining Kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGA9501-100). Cells were incubated with the staining solution for 30 min at room temperature, washed with PBS, and imaged using a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal laser-scanning microscope.

Measurement of extracellular glucose levels

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates at an appropriate density and cultured overnight under standard conditions. Upon reaching approximately 70% confluence, cells were treated with PBS (control), 10 μg/mL MnOx, or 10 μg/mL MnOx@BAY-876 in complete medium. After 24 h, culture supernatants were collected, and extracellular glucose levels were quantified using a Glucose Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). A 40 μL aliquot of each glucose standard or conditioned-medium sample was added to a 96-well plate, followed by 160 μL of glucose working reagent. After gentle mixing, the plate was incubated at 40 °C for 10 min, and absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a microplate reader. Glucose concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve and normalized to the total protein content before statistical analysis.

Assessment of intracellular oxidant burden and malondialdehyde levels

MDA-MB-231, Hs-578T, and 4T1 cells were seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The culture medium was then replaced with glucose-containing medium, glucose-free medium, glucose-free medium supplemented with 0.5 mM TCEP, MnOx-containing medium (10 μg/mL), or MnOx@BAY-876-containing medium (10 μg/mL). After treatment for 24 h, the cells were incubated with serum-free medium containing 10 μM DCFH-DA (Beyotime, S0033) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Cells maintained in glucose-containing medium and incubated with DCFH-DA without any ROS-inducing treatment served as the negative control. DCFH-DA-loaded cells treated with Rosup (50 μg/mL) for 30 min served as the positive control. Unstained cells were used to determine cellular autofluorescence and establish the flow-cytometric gate. After incubation, all cells were washed three times with PBS, trypsinized, collected, and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry.

For malondialdehyde (MDA) determination, 4T1 cells (5 × 105) were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with PBS, MnOx (10 μg/mL), or MnOx@BAY-876 (10 μg/mL). After 24 h of treatment, the cells were collected and lysed, and MDA levels were quantified using an MDA assay kit (Beyotime, S0131). 300 μL of working solution was added to each sample, followed by incubation at 99 °C for 15 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm using a Tecan Spark microplate reader (Tecan, Austria).

Determination of cellular GSSG/GSH and NADP+/NADPH levels

MDA-MB-231, Hs-578T, and 4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h, followed by treatment with glucose-containing medium, glucose-free medium, glucose-free medium plus 0.5 mM TCEP, MnOx (10 μg/mL), or MnOx@BAY-876 (10 μg/mL) for 24 h. After treatment, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. The intracellular GSH and GSSG levels were determined using a GSH/GSSG assay kit (Beyotime, S0053), and the NADP+/NADPH levels were measured using an Enhanced NADP+/NADPH assay kit (Beyotime, S0180).

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Immunohistochemical staining was conducted on tissue microarrays comprising 150 TNBC specimens obtained from Shanghai Xingchao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. The sections were subsequently blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody against SLC7A11, followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at 37 °C for 30 min. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB chromogenic substrate, counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted for microscopic evaluation. The immunohistochemical (IHC) score was determined semi-quantitatively based on both the proportion of positively stained cells and staining intensity. The percentage of positive cells was classified as 1 (0-25%), 2 (26-50%), 3 (51-75%), and 4 (76-100%), while staining intensity was graded as 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (strong). The final IHC score was calculated as the product of these two parameters, yielding a total range of 1-12. Due to partial tissue loss or damage, 111 samples were ultimately included in the statistical analysis. All specimens were independently evaluated by two experienced pathologists who were blinded to the patients’ clinical data.

Tumor tissues obtained from animal experiments were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for histological analysis. Immunohistochemical staining was performed to assess the expression of Ki67, GLUT1, and SLC7A11 using the same protocol described above. In addition, TUNEL staining was performed to assess DNA fragmentation and tumor cell death. CD8 immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess CD8+ T-cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment. Immunofluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence or confocal microscope and analyzed accordingly.

Cytoskeleton staining

MDA-MB-231, Hs-578T, and 4T1 cells were seeded onto sterile glass coverslips placed in 24-well plates. After the cells adhered and reached an appropriate density, the medium was replaced, and the cells were treated for 24 h with one of the following media: glucose-containing medium, glucose-free medium, glucose-free medium supplemented with 0.5 mM TCEP, medium containing MnOx (10 μg/mL), or medium containing MnOx@BAY-876 (10 μg/mL). After treatment, the cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and washed three times with PBS. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with 1% BSA for 30 min to reduce nonspecific binding. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with Phalloidin-TRITC working solution (Nanjing Wobio Biotechnology) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After staining, the cells were washed three times with PBS and mounted with an antifade mounting medium containing DAPI. Cytoskeletal morphology was observed using a laser-scanning confocal microscope.

Detection of immunogenic cell death markers

4T1 cells were co-incubated with 10 μg/mL of MnOx or MnOx@BAY-876 for 24 h. ICD-related markers, including HMGB1 release, calreticulin (CRT) exposure, and extracellular ATP secretion, were subsequently evaluated. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against HMGB1 (1:100, Abcam, ab18256) and CRT (1:100, Abcam, ab2907) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594 conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and the samples were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope. To assess CRT expression on the cell surface, cells were digested and resuspended as single-cell suspensions, then incubated with an anti-CRT primary antibody on ice for 1 h, followed by incubation with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min. After washing with PBS, surface CRT expression was analyzed by flow cytometry. The release of HMGB1 and ATP into the culture supernatant was quantified using commercial ELISA kits (ABconal, China).

Maturation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells

Bone marrow was isolated from the femurs of 6-week-old BALB/c wild-type mice. A total of 1 × 106 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured in medium supplemented with interleukin-4 (IL-4, 10 ng/mL) and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF, 20 ng/mL) for 5 days to induce differentiation into bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). On day 5, 4T1 cells were seeded in the upper chambers of a co-culture system and treated with PBS, MnOx (10 μg/mL), or MnOx@BAY-876 (10 μg/mL), while BMDCs were cultured in the lower chambers. After 48 h of co-culture, the culture supernatants from the lower chambers were collected and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove suspended cells and cellular debris. The concentrations of IL-6, IL-12p70, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in the supernatants were determined using mouse-specific ELISA kits (Elabscience, China). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were calculated from the corresponding standard curves. Cytokine levels were normalized to the number of viable BMDCs in each group. In parallel, BMDCs from the lower chambers were collected and stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD11c, MHC-II, CD80, and CD86. DC maturation was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Animal model

Five-week-old female BALB/c mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 2412002). To establish a primary breast tumor model, 5 × 105 4T1 cells suspended in 100 μL of cold PBS were orthotopically injected into the second mammary fat pad. One week after tumor cell inoculation, when the tumor volume reached 50-100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into four groups and treated with PBS, BAY-876 (5 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection), MnOx (10 mg/kg, tail vein injection), or MnOx@BAY-876 (10 mg/kg, tail vein injection). Treatments were administered every 2 days for 2 weeks. The body weight and tumor volume of each mouse were recorded every two days. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = 0.5×length×width2

After the treatment, the mice were euthanized, and the major organs (heart, spleen, liver, lungs, and kidneys) as well as tumor tissues were collected for histological examination. The harvested tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to evaluate histopathological changes. Meanwhile, blood samples were collected from the mice to assess hepatic and renal function using standard biochemical assays.

Flow cytometric analysis

Tumor tissues were excised from mouse tumor models and finely minced. The tissue fragments were enzymatically digested at 37 °C for 20 min in a solution containing 1 mg/mL collagenase type IV (Sigma, C4-28) and 0.5 mg/mL DNase I (Roche) to obtain a single-cell suspension. After digestion, the cells were washed several times with ice-cold PBS and centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min. Red blood cells were lysed with RBC lysis buffer for 5 min, and the remaining cells were washed and resuspended in PBS. A total of 1×106 cells were used for flow cytometric staining. To evaluate the proportion and maturation status of DCs, cells were stained with Zombie Aqua BV510 viability dye, followed by anti-CD45-PerCP/Cy5, anti-MHC-II-FITC, anti-CD11c-PE, anti-CD80-PE/Cy7, and anti-CD86-APC antibodies. T-cell infiltration and subset composition were analyzed using Zombie Aqua BV510 viability dye together with anti-CD3-FITC, anti-CD4-APC, and anti-CD8-PE antibodies. All flow cytometry antibodies were purchased from BioLegend (USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.6. Data are presented as the mean ± SD. Two independent groups were compared using an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. Data involving two experimental factors were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Šídák's multiple comparisons test. Multiple independent groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Repeated time-course data were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Nonparametric comparisons were performed using the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Correlations were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were compared using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

SLC7A11 upregulation creates a glucose-dependent metabolic vulnerability in TNBC

TNBC is characterized by extensive metabolic reprogramming that supports its aggressive phenotype [29,30]. To identify TNBC-specific metabolic vulnerabilities, we performed single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) using transcriptomic data from TNBC, non-TNBC tumors, and normal breast tissues in the TCGA breast cancer cohort. Glycolysis and ROS-related pathways were significantly enriched in TNBC (Fig. 1A), suggesting coordinated alterations in glucose metabolism and redox homeostasis. This coordinated glycolytic-redox signature led us to examine SLC7A11, a key node linking cystine metabolism to NADPH-dependent redox homeostasis [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. Given the reported sensitivity of SLC7A11-high cells to glucose deprivation [35], we next assessed SLC7A11 expression in TNBC. SLC7A11 expression was significantly elevated in TNBC tissues relative to non-TNBC tumors and normal breast tissues and positively correlated with glycolysis enrichment scores (ρ = 0.26, P < 0.001; Fig. 1B). Immunohistochemical analysis of 111 clinical breast cancer specimens further confirmed elevated SLC7A11 protein expression in TNBC tumor tissues. Notably, SLC7A11 levels increased progressively with advancing tumor stage (P = 0.0051; Fig. 1C–D), supporting an association between SLC7A11 upregulation and TNBC progression. Multiple TNBC cell lines exhibited higher SLC7A11 expression than non-TNBC cell lines (Fig. 1E). In our cell panel, BT-549 and HCC-1937 cells, which expressed relatively low levels of SLC7A11, were largely resistant to glucose deprivation, whereas TNBC cell lines with moderate to high SLC7A11 expression, including MDA-MB-231, Hs-578T, SUM-1315MO2, and HCC-1806, were markedly more sensitive (Fig. 1F–G). Glucose restriction triggers disulfidptosis in SLC7A11-high TNBC cells

Because SLC7A11-high cells are susceptible to disulfidptosis under glucose deprivation [15], we asked whether glucose withdrawal could trigger this form of cell death in SLC7A11-high TNBC cells, including Hs-578T and MDA-MB-231. Glucose deprivation induced substantial cell death in both cell lines. Co-treatment with 0.5 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP), a thiol-reducing agent, almost completely rescued viability. Specifically, cell death decreased from 31.8% to 2.15% in Hs-578T cells and from 15.9% to 3.64% in MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 2A–B). These findings indicate that disulfide bond formation is required for glucose deprivation-induced cell death in SLC7A11-high TNBC cells. Consistent with this, glucose deprivation caused marked redox disruption, as indicated by increased GSSG/GSH and NADP+/NADPH ratios together with elevated intracellular DCF fluorescence (Fig. 2C–E). TCEP substantially attenuated these changes, linking impaired reducing capacity to disulfide stress accumulation. Phalloidin staining revealed marked F-actin condensation and loss of cytoskeletal architecture under glucose-free conditions, whereas TCEP preserved filament organization and cellular morphology (Fig. 2F). Moreover, non-reducing SDS-PAGE revealed high-molecular-weight aggregates of key cytoskeletal proteins, including FLNA, MYH9, Drebrin, and β-actin under glucose-free conditions (Fig. 2G). The pronounced mobility shift was consistent with intermolecular disulfide crosslinking. This aggregation signature was absent in SLC7A11-low or SLC7A11-moderate TNBC cell lines (Fig. S1), suggesting that SLC7A11 expression level may predict susceptibility to disulfidptosis. Design and physicochemical characterization of MnOx@BAY-876

MnOx nanoplatforms were synthesized according to previously reported protocols [36]. MnOx@BAY-876 was prepared by co-incubating MnOx with BAY-876 at room temperature (Fig. 4A). Previous studies have shown that nanoflower-like or nanosheet-assembled MnOx nanostructures provide accessible surfaces and abundant interstitial spaces. These features facilitate the adsorption and physical retention of small-molecule therapeutics [37,38]. During stirring, BAY-876 molecules can diffuse toward and repeatedly contact the accessible MnOx surface and interstitial spaces. BAY-876 is an aromatic small-molecule inhibitor containing several carbonyl and nitrogen-containing groups [39]. These groups may interact with surface hydroxyl groups and exposed Mn sites through hydrogen bonding and weak coordination. Dispersion interactions may also help stabilize drug adsorption. Quantification of BAY-876 in the collected supernatants by HPLC-MS indicated a drug loading content of approximately 30%. TEM revealed that both bare MnOx and MnOx@BAY-876 exhibited a characteristic nanoflower-like morphology with an average particle size of 100 nm (Fig. 4B). Elemental mapping further supported BAY-876 loading, as a fluorine signal was detected only in MnOx@BAY-876 but not in bare MnOx (Fig. 4C). Consistently, XPS spectra of MnOx@BAY-876 displayed an F 1s peak at 687.68 eV with an atomic percentage of 9.88%, further confirming BAY-876 incorporation (Fig. 4D). The Mn 2p spectrum indicated the presence of mixed-valence manganese species, including Mn2+ (641.34/652.49 eV, 23.45%), Mn3+ (642.11/653.73 eV, 51.71%), and Mn4+ (643.51/654.82 eV, 24.84%). These results indicate a mixed-valence structure predominantly enriched in Mn3+ (Fig. 4E). Given the elevated GSH levels and mildly acidic conditions characteristic of the tumor microenvironment [40], we next evaluated the responsiveness of MnOx@BAY-876 to GSH and pH. MnOx@BAY-876 was incubated in PBS containing different concentrations of GSH to mimic the reducing tumor microenvironment. The dispersion gradually turned transparent with increasing GSH concentration, indicating GSH-triggered degradation of the nanostructure (Fig. 4J). TEM observations further revealed that MnOx@BAY-876 progressively disintegrated and lost its original morphology over time in the presence of GSH, whereas negligible structural changes were observed in GSH-free PBS (Fig. 4K). Moreover, GSH-induced degradation enabled sustained release of both BAY-876 and Mn ions over time (Fig. 4L–M), suggesting a GSH-responsive corelease profile. We subsequently examined the effects of different pH conditions on the stability and drug-release behavior of MnOx@BAY-876. At pH 7.4 and 6.4, the nanoplatform exhibited similar release profiles of BAY-876 and Mn ions, indicating that tumor-relevant mild acidity alone was insufficient to induce appreciable material degradation or drug release. A modest degree of structural alteration and increased release was observed only under the strongly acidic condition of pH 4.4 (Fig. S15). Therefore, the degradation and drug release of MnOx@BAY-876 were predominantly driven by GSH rather than by the mildly acidic extracellular tumor microenvironment. Zeta-potential measurements showed no substantial difference between MnOx@BAY-876 and bare MnOx, suggesting that strong electrostatic attraction was unlikely to be the predominant driving force for BAY-876 loading (Fig. S16). DLS analysis indicated a larger hydrodynamic diameter for MnOx@BAY-876 than for bare MnOx. The nanoplatform remained colloidally stable in culture medium for at least one week without noticeable size variation (Fig. S17), confirming its favorable dispersion stability for subsequent biological applications.

Cy5-labeled MnOx@BAY-876 nanoplatform achieved maximal cellular uptake in 4T1 cells within 6 h and was predominantly localized in the cytoplasm after internalization (Fig. 5B). Across a range of concentrations, MnOx@BAY-876 exhibited significantly higher cytotoxicity than MnOx alone. The IC50 of MnOx was approximately 50 μg/mL, whereas MnOx@BAY-876 induced comparable cytotoxicity at a much lower concentration of 12.5 μg/mL (Fig. 5C). Consistently, treatment with 10 μg/mL MnOx@BAY-876 markedly increased the proportions of early and late Annexin V-FITC/PI-positive populations (18.1% and 22.4%, respectively), compared with 7.05% and 8.01% observed in the MnOx-treated group (Fig. 5D). Live/dead staining further demonstrated preserved morphology and viability in MnOx-treated cells, whereas MnOx@BAY-876-treated cells exhibited pronounced red fluorescence, indicating enhanced cytotoxicity (Fig. 5E). Notably, residual glucose levels in the culture supernatant of the MnOx@BAY-876 group were significantly higher than those in the MnOx and PBS groups, confirming the effective inhibition of glucose uptake by BAY-876 (Fig. 5F). Subsequent analysis revealed a pronounced reduction in intracellular NADPH levels following MnOx@BAY-876 treatment, consistent with reduced glucose-derived NADPH availability (Fig. 5G). In contrast, the reduction in NADPH observed in the MnOx group may reflect a compensatory redox response. In line with these metabolic alterations, MnOx@BAY-876 significantly increased the GSSG/GSH ratio, MDA levels, intracellular DCF fluorescence, and MitoSOX Red fluorescence, while markedly reducing SOD and GPx activities (Fig. 5H–J and S19). Collectively, these findings support pronounced disruption of cellular redox homeostasis, increased mitochondrial oxidant burden, impaired antioxidant defense, and lipid peroxidation-associated changes.

These results suggested that MnOx@BAY-876 induced a severe metabolic-redox imbalance. To define the dominant mode of cell death, we performed rescue experiments using a series of cell death inhibitors, including necrostatin-1 (5 μM), Tempol (10 μM), Z-VAD-FMK (5 μM), chloroquine (5 μM), ferrostatin-1 (5 μM), and the disulfide-reducing agent TCEP (0.5 mM). Among these agents, only TCEP substantially rescued MnOx@BAY-876-induced cell death (Fig. 5K), supporting disulfide stress-dependent cell death. Under non-reducing conditions, MnOx@BAY-876 also caused reduced electrophoretic mobility or the appearance of high-molecular-weight species of cytoskeletal proteins, particularly drebrin and β-actin (Fig. 5L). Although both MnOx and MnOx@BAY-876 induced compensatory upregulation of SLC7A11, sustained depletion of GSH and NADPH in the MnOx@BAY-876 group disrupted redox homeostasis and increased γ-H2AX expression (Fig. 5M). Fluorescence imaging further showed that PBS- and MnOx-treated cells maintained relatively intact cytoskeletal structures, whereas MnOx@BAY-876-treated cells displayed marked cytoskeletal contraction and collapse, consistent with extensive disulfide cross-linking (Fig. 5N).

The GSH-responsive reduction of MnOx also resulted in the release of Mn2+. MnOx@BAY-876 increased the phosphorylation levels of TBK1 and IRF3 (Fig. 5O–S20). Notably, a recent study using a manganese electrochemical battery showed that sustained Mn2+ release enhanced cGAS-STING signaling. The system also altered tumor glucose metabolism and reduced TREX2 expression. These effects jointly strengthened antitumor immunity [41]. This study provides a relevant example of combining manganese-ion delivery with metabolic intervention. In our system, BAY-876-mediated GLUT1 inhibition and GSH-responsive Mn2+ release may similarly act through complementary mechanisms. Glucose restriction may promote metabolic dysregulation and DNA damage, whereas the released Mn2+ may enhance the responsiveness of cGAS-STING signaling [[42], [43], [44]]. These findings indicate that MnOx@BAY-876 induces disulfide stress in 4T1 cells by coupling glucose restriction with GSH and NADPH depletion, thereby provoking redox imbalance, cytoskeletal collapse, and DNA damage, ultimately driving disulfidptosis-associated cell death.

MnOx@BAY-876 amplifies immunogenic cell death and promotes dendritic cell activation

Recent studies have shown that metal ions, including manganese, iron, and copper, can disrupt intracellular redox homeostasis and promote immunogenic cell death (ICD), thereby enhancing antitumor immune responses [41,45]. Manganese-based biomaterials may further deplete intracellular GSH and remodel the tumor immune microenvironment [23,46]. Similarly, fluorinated TiO2-xFx nanospikes generate reactive oxygen species under ultrasound stimulation and induce pyroptosis, leading to enhanced antitumor immunity and durable immune memory [47]. Although pyroptosis differs mechanistically from disulfide stress-mediated cell death, this example supports a broader link between material-induced redox disruption, immunogenic tumor cell death, and immune activation. However, whether metal-ion-mediated innate immune stimulation can cooperate with metabolism-driven cell death remains unclear. In this study, MnOx@BAY-876-induced disulfide stress may disrupt cytoskeletal organization and compromise cellular structural integrity, thereby facilitating the exposure and release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). Together with Mn2+-mediated redox imbalance and innate immune stimulation, these effects may enhance tumor immunogenicity and promote subsequent antitumor immune responses [48]. ELISA of BMDC culture supernatants further showed that MnOx@BAY-876 treatment significantly increased the secretion of IL-6, IL-12p70, and TNF-α (Fig. 6G). These findings indicate that MnOx@BAY-876 not only promotes the phenotypic maturation of BMDCs but also enhances their pro-inflammatory and immunostimulatory functions. In particular, the increased secretion of IL-12p70 supports the potential of activated BMDCs to promote Th1-type immune responses and enhance NK- and CD8+ T-cell activity, whereas elevated IL-6 and TNF-α levels further reflect enhanced pro-inflammatory activation [[49], [50], [51]]. These results demonstrate that the disulfide stress-mediated cell death induced by MnOx@BAY-876 enhances the exposure and release of ICD-associated DAMPs, thereby effectively promoting dendritic cell activation and increasing the immunogenicity of tumor cell death.

MnOx@BAY-876 suppresses tumor growth and reprograms the tumor immune microenvironment in vivo

Building on the robust immunogenicity and antitumor activity of MnOx@BAY-876 observed in vitro, we next evaluated its therapeutic efficacy in vivo. To accomplish this, we established a syngeneic SLC7A11-high 4T1 breast cancer model in BALB/c mice [52,53]. Once the tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomized into three groups: PBS (vehicle control), MnOx, and MnOx@BAY-876. Tumors and other specimens, including serum, spleens, and major organs, were collected on day 14 after initiating treatment (Fig. 7A). Before the therapeutic intervention, we assessed the biocompatibility of MnOx@BAY-876. Hemolysis assays showed that even at a high concentration of 400 μg/mL, MnOx@BAY-876 induced hemolysis rates below 1%, indicating good hemocompatibility (Fig. S23). In vivo safety was further evaluated by histopathological analysis of major organs. No discernible morphological abnormalities were observed in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney across all treatment groups (Fig. S24). There was no evidence of inflammatory infiltration, cellular necrosis, vacuolization, or structural disruption, indicating good tissue tolerance under the applied dosing regimen. Moreover, comprehensive blood biochemical and hematological profiling revealed no statistically significant differences among the groups in red blood cell count, white blood cell count, hemoglobin concentration, platelet count, or key markers of hepatic (ALT, AST, ALP) and renal (UREA, CREA) function (Fig. S25A–D). Compared with the PBS and MnOx control groups, MnOx@BAY-876 treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 7B). No noticeable body weight loss was observed throughout the study (Fig. 7C), indicating the absence of overt systemic toxicity. At the experimental endpoint, tumors were excised and weighed. Tumor weight was markedly lower in the MnOx@BAY-876 group, supporting its in vivo antitumor activity (Fig. 7D). In contrast, MnOx alone produced only moderate tumor growth inhibition, underscoring the critical role of BAY-876-mediated GLUT1 inhibition in enhancing therapeutic efficacy. To determine whether the antitumor effect was associated with remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), we performed flow cytometric profiling of tumor-infiltrating immune cells. MnOx@BAY-876 markedly increased the intratumoral DC population (from 10.7% to 46.7%) and significantly upregulated CD80 and CD86 expression, consistent with enhanced DC maturation and co-stimulatory signaling (Fig. 7E–F,7I-J and S26). Concurrently, MnOx@BAY-876 robustly promoted the infiltration of CD3+ T lymphocytes, increasing their proportion from 1.73% in the PBS group to 11.1%. Within the T-cell compartment, CD8+ cytotoxic T cells exhibited the most pronounced expansion, rising from 12.8% to 32.4% of total CD3+ T cells (Fig. 7G–H, 7K,7 M, and S27), indicating preferential recruitment and amplification of effector T cells. The proportion of CD4+ T cells decreased to 36.8% after treatment, although the specific CD4+ T-cell subsets responsible for this change were not determined (Fig. 7L). TUNEL staining revealed a marked increase in tumor cell death in MnOx@BAY-876-treated tumors, accompanied by a concomitant reduction in Ki67-positive proliferating cells. GLUT1 expression was significantly downregulated, whereas SLC7A11 expression was upregulated in the MnOx@BAY-876 group, consistent with restricted glucose transport capacity and activation of a disulfide stress response in vivo. Furthermore, CD8 immunofluorescence staining demonstrated dense infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes throughout the tumor parenchyma, rather than being restricted to the tumor periphery (Fig. 7N).

Genes associated with cytotoxic activity, including Gzma, Nkg7, and Xcl1 [54,55], were highly expressed in the NKT cells. Given their central roles in antitumor immunity, these populations were extracted and re-clustered for detailed downstream analyses (Fig. 8F). The analysis revealed functional heterogeneity among T cells, including CD8+ effector T cells, γδ T cells, CD4+ helper T cells, Tregs, as well as stress-associated and quiescent T-cell states (Fig. 8G–S30). The profiled MnOx@BAY-876-treated tumor showed high relative proportions of cytotoxic CD8+ effector T cells and γδ T cells and low relative proportions of Tregs (Fig. 8H). These compositional alterations indicate a shift of the TME from an immunosuppressive state toward an immune-activated phenotype.

Notably, CD8+ effector T cells enriched in the MnOx@BAY-876-profiled treated group demonstrated elevated expression of cytotoxicity- and activation-related genes, including Cd8a, Cd8b1, Nkg7, Ccl5, Ccl4, and Gzmk. This transcriptional profile suggests enhanced cytolytic capacity and an increased ability to recruit additional immune effector cells. Concurrently, γδ T cells showed relatively higher expression of lineage-defining markers (Trdc, Trgc1, and Trdv4) and functional mediators such as Il17a, implicating their involvement in Il17-driven inflammatory signaling and early tumor microenvironment remodeling, potentially acting synergistically with CD8+ T cells (Fig. 8I–J). In contrast, the Treg population was reduced following MnOx@BAY-876 treatment, while retaining high expression of key immunosuppressive regulators, including Foxp3, Ikzf2, and Lrrc32 (Fig. 8I and K). These descriptive observations suggest a potential shift toward a more immune-active tumor microenvironment.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a MnOx@BAY-876 nanotherapeutic strategy that targets the metabolic-redox vulnerability of SLC7A11-high TNBC. MnOx@BAY-876 depleted intracellular GSH and reduced glucose-derived NADPH availability. These effects disrupted redox homeostasis, increased disulfide stress, and induced disulfidptosis-associated cell death. The treatment also enhanced ICD-related signals and remodeled the tumor immune microenvironment. It reduced tumor cell viability in vitro and suppressed tumor growth in vivo. Collectively, our findings highlight disulfide stress as a potential mechanistic link between glucose metabolic restriction, redox imbalance, and antitumor immunity. They also support the potential of MnOx@BAY-876 as an integrated strategy for combining material-based redox modulation with metabolic intervention in TNBC.
