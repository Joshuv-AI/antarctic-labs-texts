Plant-based diets especially healthy ones are negatively associated with depression: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Bao Zhang, Meiling Li, Xiude Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiet was a potentially modifiable factor in the prevention of depression. We aimed to investigate associations of an overall plant-based diet index (PDI), a healthful PDI (hPDI), and an unhealthful PDI (uPDI) with the prevalence of depression. We hypothesized that plant-based diets especially the hPDI may be associated with lower depression, whereas uPDI would increase the depression.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional study using data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2005–2018. Diet was assessed by 24-hour recalls. Depression was measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Odds ratios (ORs) and percentage changes (%) were calculated by logistic regression and linear regression, respectively.ResultsAmong the 31,622 participants aged 20 years or older, the age-standardized prevalence of major depression (PHQ-9 score ≥ 10) was 8.8% (n = 2,744). The overall PDI was not associated with odds of depression (OR comparing extreme quintiles 0.84, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.66–1.07; ptrend=0.168), whereas it was associated with lower PHQ-9 scores in multiple linear regression (β=-0.07, 95%CI: -0.13, -0.02, ptrend=0.045). Participants with higher hPDI scores had lower odds of depression (OR = 0.69, 95%CI: 0.55, 0.87, ptrend=0.007) and lower PHQ-9 scores (β=-0.07, 95%CI: -0.14, 0.00, ptrend=0.013). Conversely, higher uPDI scores were positively associated with prevalence of depression (OR = 1.78, 95%CI: 1.44–2.20, ptrend<0.001) and PHQ-9 scores (β = 0.19, 95%CI: 0.14, 0.24, ptrend<0.001).ConclusionsPlant-based diets, especially when they are enriched with healthier plant foods, may be beneficial for primary prevention of depression. However, plant-based diets that emphasize less healthy plant foods are associated with higher depression levels among US adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25748-9.

Introduction

Depression is a common mental disorder and the primary cause of disability, impacting an estimated 350 million individuals worldwide [1]. In the United States (US), approximately 8.4% of adults experience depression, with less than one-third of them having received treatment [2]. Consequently, prevention plays a crucial role, and emerging evidence suggests that diet could be a modifiable factor in preventing depression [3]. A vegetarian diet has been linked to a reduced incidence and mortality rate of various chronic diseases [4]. However, epidemiological studies of the association between vegetarian diets and depression have produced inconsistent results, varying from a positive [5–15], to null [5, 16–21], to an inverse [22–28] association.

Previous studies have defined a vegetarian diet as one that excludes some or all animal foods. However, the number of vegetarians remains low in most countries [29] with only 3% of US Americans identifying as vegetarian [30]. This suggests that it is difficult for many individuals to fully relinquish animal products to embrace a vegetarian lifestyle [31]. Therefore, understanding the health implications of gradually enhancing the consumption of plant-based foods while reducing the intake of animal-derived foods is of utmost significance from a public health perspective. Additionally, it is important to note that not all plant foods are equally beneficial for our health. For example, our previous research found that starchy and non-starchy vegetables have opposite effects on hepatic steatosis and/or fibrosis [32]. In order to overcome these constraints, Satija et al. [33]. devised three plant-based dietary indices, including an overall plant-based diet index (PDI), a healthful PDI (hPDI), and an unhealthful PDI (uPDI). The main focus of the overall PDI is to progressively enhance the consumption of plant foods while decreasing the intake of animal foods, aligning with the initial pro-vegetarian eating plan [34]. The hPDI emphasizes the intake of healthy plant foods, while the uPDI focuses on less healthy plant food intake. We hypothesized that hPDI may be associated with lower depression, whereas uPDI was associated with higher depression.

However, only a limited number of studies have evaluated the relationships between these three indices and the prevalence of depression [35–40]. These studies were mainly conducted in the Asian population (four conducted in an Iranian and one conducted in a Chinese population) [35–39] and inconsistent results were obtained. Therefore, our study aims to investigate the associations between PDIs and depression among US adults, utilizing data from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Methods

Study population

In this study, participants were selected from the NHANES 2005–2018 cycles. NHANES is ongoing, nationwide cross-sectional survey that gathers information from the noninstitutionalized civilian population in the US, executing by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the National Centers for Health Statistics (NCHS). Details of the study design, protocol, and data collection methods of NHANES are available online: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx. The NHANES study protocols received approval from the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board (Protocol #2005-06; Protocol #2011-17; Protocol #2018-01). Every participant provided written informed consent.

Participants were excluded if they aged < 20 years (30,441), had missing dietary information (n = 4,440), had unreasonable energy intake (less than 600 or more than 3,500 kcal/day for women; less than 800 or more than 4,200 kcal/day for men, n = 1,401), or did not complete depression screening or had the incomplete data (n = 2,286). The final analysis comprised 31,622 individuals, consisting of 16,306 women and 15,316 men (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Plant-based diet indices

Diet was assessed using 24-hour recalls and the NHANES used validated Automated Multiple Pass Method to improve comprehensiveness and precision of dietary recalls while reducing the burden on respondents. Supplementary information regarding quality control for the dietary interview is available elsewhere [41]. From 1999 to 2002, only one 24-hour recall was conducted. Beginning in 2003, a second recall was collected via phone call, taking place 3–10 days subsequent to the initial recall. In the present study, most participants (n = 28,107, 88.9%) completed two 24-hour recalls.

Development of the PDIs has been described previously [33, 42, 43]. Initially, we established 18 food groups (Supplementary Table 1) by considering resemblances in nutritional composition and culinary characteristics. Then, we organized these food groups into three categories, including animal foods, healthy plant foods, and less healthy plant foods. The intake of each food group was divided into five quintiles, with scores assigned based on their ranking. For positive scoring, the lowest quintile received a score of 1, and the highest quintile a score of 5. Conversely, for reverse scoring, the lowest quintile was given a score of 5, while the highest received a score of 1. For the overall PDI, both healthy and less healthy plant foods were given positive scores, while animal foods received inverse scores. As for hPDI, positive scores were given to healthy plant foods while both less healthy plant and animal foods were given inverse scores. For uPDI, positive scores were given to less healthy plant foods, while animal and healthy plant foods received an inverse score. Finally, these three indices were calculated by summing scores of the above 18 food categories, where scores varied between 18 and 90.

Ascertainment of covariates

Demographic and lifestyle factors were collected via standardized questionnaires during household interviews. Data on alcohol intake, height, and weight were collected at the Mobile Examination Center. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight/height2 (kg/m2). Family income was measured as the ratio of family income to the poverty threshold. Physical activity was summed and expressed in metabolic equivalent (MET)-h/week. History of cancer, diabetes, and hypertension was defined as self-reported. In addition, we added criteria of fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL for diabetes and systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg for hypertension.

Ascertainment of depression

Since 2005, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) has been used to assess symptoms of depression over the previous 2 weeks. The PHQ-9 is well-validated (Cronbach’s α = 0.89) and consists of 9 questions. Response categories “not at all,” “several days,” “more than half the days” and “nearly every day” were assigned scores from 0 to 3. A total score was calculated by summing the scores of the 9 items, ranging from 0 to 27. We used a cut-off value of 10 points, with a sensitivity and specificity of 88%, to identify major depression [44].

Statistical analysis

In the present analysis, we used appropriate sampling weights, primary sampling units, and strata to generate nationally representative estimates of the US population. The prevalence of depression was standardized by the 2020 US population. Multivariable logistic regression was employed to calculate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We also assessed the β coefficients and 95%CIs of continuous PHQ-9 scores associated with each PDI using multivariate linear regression. Allowing for departures from the normal distribution, PHQ-9 scores were natural logarithm transformed in the regression. For ease in the interpretation of the results, we also provided the percentage changes (%) which were calculated by back-transforming the β coefficients from log scale to the original scale using the formula [exp(β) − 1]*100 [42]. Results were presented in the following two adjustment models: (1) adjustment for age, sex, and total energy intake (model 1); (2) adjustment for model 1 and race/ethnicity, education, marital status, family income, physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, and history of cancer (model 2). These covariates were chosen by considering the observed dissimilarity in participant characteristics and the risk factors for depression that have already been recognized in previous studies. Participants with missing covariate information were included in the models using the missing indicator method. We reported the ORs, β coefficients, and percentage changes (%) across quintile categories and for every 10-point increase in each PDI. To estimate the effect per 10-point increase, we divided the original PDIs scores by 10 and included this transformed variable in the regression model. Linear trend tests were performed by treating each PDI as a continuous variable. For the logistic regression analysis with depression status as the outcome, the trend test assessed whether the odds of depression changed linearly with increasing PDIs scores. For the linear regression analyses on the log-transformed PHQ-9 scores, the trend test evaluated whether the regression coefficients or corresponding percentage changes exhibited a linear association with PDIs scores. Additionally, restricted cubic splines were utilized to assess potential non-linear relationships between PDIs and the prevalence of depression.

Subgroup analyses were conducted by some possible confounders. The Wald test was employed to evaluate the cross-product terms between these variables and the exposures. The Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparisons, and p < 0.017 (0.05/3 exposures x 1 outcome) indicated statistical significance for the main analysis and p < 0.001 [0.05/(3 exposures x 1 outcome x 13 groups)] for subgroup analysis. We calculated the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF) for all predictors in the final model using the “vif” function from the “car” package in R to detect multicollinearity. To make the GVIF values comparable across variables with different degrees of freedom, we also computed GVIF^(1/(2*Df)), where Df is the degrees of freedom of the variable [45]. A GVIF^(1/(2*Df)) value greater than 2 is considered indicative of multicollinearity [45]. Since multicollinearity diagnostics are unavailable in the SAS SURVEYLOGISTIC/SURVEYREG procedures, and considering that collinearity assesses the relationships among independent variables rather than the dependent variable, an equivalent linear regression model can be used [46]. We conducted two sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our results, considering that the missing indicator method may generally introduce bias [47]. First, we applied multiple imputation to address missing data, generating 25 imputed datasets, and combined results using Rubin’s rules [48, 49]. In addition, we conducted a complete case analysis by excluding participants with missing covariate data. All statistical tests were two-sided and performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC).

Results

Characteristics of participants

The average age of study participants was 49.7 years [standard deviation (SD), 17.8 years], and the age-standardized prevalence of major depression was 8.8% (n = 2,744). Participants with higher scores on the overall PDI and hPDI were older, richer, leaner, more educated, more likely to be female and non-Hispanic white, less likely to be currently smoking; whereas we obrserved reversed trends for uPDI (Table 1).

Plant-based diets and depression

As shown in Table 2, higher hPDI scores were inversely (comparing extreme quintiles, OR = 0.69, 95%CI: 0.55–0.87; ptrend=0.007), whereas uPDI scores were positively (OR = 1.78, 95%CI: 1.44–2.20; ptrend<0.001) associated with odds of depression. Consistently, hPDI was linked to lower, whereas uPDI was linked to higher depression scores (Table 3, Supplementary Table 2). Compared with individuals in the lowest quintile of hPDI scores, those in the highest quintile had an average decrease of 0.07 in the log-transformed PHQ-9 score (β=-0.07, 95%CI: -0.14, 0.00, ptrend=0.013), corresponding to a 6.41% lower PHQ-9 score (percentage change=-6.41%, 95%CI: -12.75%, 0.40%, ptrend=0.013). Additionally, each 10-point increase in hPDI was associated with an average decrease of 0.04 in the log-PHQ-9 score (β=-0.04, 95%CI: -0.07, -0.01), equivalent to a 3.92% reduction in the PHQ-9 score (percentage change=-3.92%, 95%CI: -6.87%, -0.87%). Conversely, compared with individuals in the lowest quintile of uPDI scores, those in the highest quintile showed an average increase of 0.19 in the log-PHQ-9 score (β = 0.19, 95%CI: 0.14, 0.24, ptrend<0.001), corresponding to a 20.90% higher PHQ-9 score (percentage change = 20.90%, 95%CI: 14.80%, 27.33%, ptrend<0.001). Similarly, each 10-point increase in uPDI was associated with an average increase of 0.09 in the log-PHQ-9 score (β = 0.09, 95%CI: 0.07, 0.12), equivalent to a 9.87% increase in the PHQ-9 score (percentage change = 9.87%, 95%CI: 7.01%, 12.80%).

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated for the first time the relationships between plant-based diets and the prevalence of depression among US adults. Our results confirmed the hypothesis that higher adherence to plant-based diets, especially the hPDI, was associated with a lower prevalence of depression and/or PHQ-9 scores, whereas uPDI was positively associated with depression. If these findings are validated, it is crucial for public health recommendations and dietary guidelines to highlight the importance of consuming healthy plant-based foods for the prevention of depression.

Observational studies examining the relationship between these three plant-based diets and depression prevalence have been limited and have yielded inconsistent results [35–40]. Our findings were consistent with previous studies conducted among older Chinese adults [39] and Iranian women [35], which utilized the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10) and Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21) to define depression separately. In addition, two studies used DASS-21 [36] and hospital inpatient records [40] to assess depression in Iranian diabetic women and the UK middle-aged and older population, respectively, both of which found no significant association between overall PDI and depression prevalence and/or depression score. In a recent study involving 3,362 Iranian adults aged 18–55 years, which employed the Hospital-Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) for depression screening, the overall PDI and hPDI were found to have an inverse association with the prevalence of depression, while no association was observed with uPDI [37]. Conversely, a cross-sectional multicentric community trial was conducted in Iran, involving 2,033 adults aged 18 or older and using the same scale to assess depression. The results indicated that the uPDI was associated with higher depression, while the overall PDI and hPDI did not show any association with depression prevalence [38]. This inconsistency in findings could be attributed to variations in the study population, sample size, and methods used for outcomes ascertainment. Additionally, study design has a significant impact on the variability of results. Most of these studies [35–39], including the current study, were cross-sectional, while some were longitudinal cohort studies [40]. Overall, this longitudinal study supported our conclusion that a higher hPDI was protective against depression, whereas a higher uPDI promoted depression, although it showed no statistically significant association for the overall PDI [40]. One cross-sectional study also demonstrated similar results [36]. Two other cross-sectional studies were consistent with our findings [35, 39]. The remaining two studies partially supported our results, as mentioned above: one reported a negative correlation between overall PDI and hPDI with depression [37], while the other found a positive association between uPDI and depression [38]. Longitudinal cohort studies offer stronger evidence for causal inference than cross-sectional designs. In addition, prospective cohort designs can reduce recall bias. Therefore, further prospective cohort studies are warranted to validate our findings.

We observed that after full adjustment for covariates, the ORs for depression risk and the rate of change in PHQ-9 scores were smaller compared to Model 1, which only adjusted for sex, age, and energy intake. Although significant associations for hPDI and uPDI were maintained, the association between overall PDI and depression lost statistical significance. To investigate whether multicollinearity influenced these results, we conducted multicollinearity diagnostics for both logistic and linear regression models. The vast majority of variables had GVIF values less than 2, and all adjusted GVIF values [GVIF^(1/(2*Df))] were below 2. Therefore, there was no evidence of multicollinearity affecting the analyses. This suggests that the attenuation of effect estimates was unlikely due to multicollinearity, but rather reflected effective adjustment for confounding factors, which reduced confounding bias and thus revealed the independent association of the exposures with the outcomes. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that residual confounding and measurement error may also contribute to changes in effect sizes; further research is warranted to clarify these complex relationships.

Several mechanisms may explain our observations. First, it has been found that consuming some healthy plant foods such as whole grains, vegetables, and fruits is associated with lower odds of depression [50, 51]. Conversely, the consumption of less healthy foods like refined grains is linked to a higher prevalence of depression [52]. Second, certain micronutrients, such as folate and omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), play an important role in neurological function. Folate, for instance, can impact the synthesis of neurotransmitters like dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine [53]. Moreover, individuals who include more plant-based foods in their diets, particularly healthier options, tend to have a higher intake of folate. Similarly, PUFAs can regulate neurotransmission, cell survival, and neuroinflammation, all of which can have an impact on mental health [54]. Omega-3 PUFAs specifically play a role in neuroendocrine modulation, for example, influencing serotoninergic and dopaminergic transmission [55]. Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) are the two main omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) known for their highly unsaturated nature [56]. Although fish is the primary source of EPA and DHA [57], certain healthy plant foods like nuts and seed oils also provide omega-3 PUFAs. Additionally, vitamin B12, mainly from animal foods [58], has been linked to a reduced risk of depression [59, 60], supporting the idea that we should not completely exclude animal foods from our diets [61]. Third, inflammation and oxidative stress can adversely impact brain function and contribute to psychological disorders [35]. Research indicates that both EPA and DHA possess antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [62]. Furthermore, healthy plant-based foods like nuts, legumes, vegetables, fruits, and olive oil are rich in antioxidants and polyphenols, which can potentially enhance mental health through antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects [35]. Finally, there are still other potential mechanisms that should be considered, such as the presence of high levels of pesticide metabolites and phytoestrogens [63], as well as the influence of gut microbiota [35, 64]. However, further investigation is required to fully understand the mechanism.

The main strengths of this study are its large sample size and representativeness. However, this study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design restricted causal interpretations. Second, since diet and other lifestyle factors were self-reported, measurement error is inevitable. In addition, diet assessment using 2-day 24-hour recalls may not accurately reflect the usual intake of the population. Last, there is still a potential for residual or unmeasured confounding, despite adjusting for most known risk factors for depression.

In conclusion, our findings suggested that a plant-based diet, especially a healthy plant-based diet was inversely associated with the odds of depression. Conversely, an unhealthy plant-based diet was positively associated with depression. However, well-designed prospective or intervention studies with a more accurate depression diagnostic method are needed to validate these results.
