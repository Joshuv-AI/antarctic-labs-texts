Exploring social determinants of health and immunotherapy utilization in patients with stage III non-small-cell lung cancer after definitive chemoradiation
Authors: C. Hwang, N. Stambaugh, J. Levey, C. Stambaugh, D. Spiegel, K. Huber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSince the 2018 Food and Drug Administration approval of immunotherapy (IO) for consolidative therapy in stage III non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), real-world uptake across racial and socioeconomic groups remains poorly characterized. This study evaluated IO utilization patterns after definitive chemoradiation (CRT) in a diverse national cohort.Materials and methodsA retrospective analysis was carried out using the National Cancer Database for patients aged ≥18 years diagnosed with stage III NSCLC from 2018 to 2021. Inclusion criteria included receipt of ≥60 Gy of radiation in ≥30 fractions, and IO administered 50-150 days from CRT initiation. Surgical cases were excluded. Social determinants of health included race, ethnicity, insurance type, treatment facility, Charlson-Deyo comorbidity index, high school graduation rate (HSGR), income, sex, and age. Odds ratios (ORs) were calculated using univariable and multivariable logistic regression, and multicollinearity was tested via Cramér V.ResultsAmong 25 746 patients, 21 848 were white, 3236 black, 558 Asian, 104 American Indian or Alaska Native, and 620 Hispanic. Univariable analysis showed lower IO use among black (OR 0.86), Hispanic (OR 0.72), Mexican (OR 0.45), and South Asian (OR 0.48) patients, those with Medicare (OR 0.94), treated at community sites (OR 0.88), and from lower HSGR and income areas (ORs ≤ 0.90), all P < 0.05. Multivariable analysis confirmed reduced IO use in these groups, except for income. Income lost its independent significance in multivariable analysis, likely reflecting its collinearity with HSGR (Cramér V = 0.37).ConclusionDisparities in IO receipt persist after CRT for stage III NSCLC in the post-IO era, highlighting the need to address systemic barriers to equitable cancer care.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer death in both men and women in the United States.1 However, the advent of immunotherapy (IO) has improved overall survival in advanced non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) over the past decade.2, 3, 4 Lung cancer mortality dropped from 91 to 38 per 100 000 in men between 1990 and 2020 and from 42 to 27 per 100 000 in women between 2002 and 2020.1

For stage III NSCLC patients who are not surgical candidates, the standard of care is definitive chemoradiation (CRT).5 Since 2018, IO has been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as consolidative therapy after CRT, based on improved progression-free and overall survival shown in the PACIFIC trial and its 5-year update.6,7 A National Cancer Database (NCDB) study further confirmed survival benefits of IO across racial groups.8

Despite these benefits, disparities in IO use exist. A recent study found that non-Hispanic white patients with lower socioeconomic status and education were less likely to receive IO at both academic and nonacademic facilities. For non-Hispanic black patients, lower education status was associated with reduced IO use at nonacademic centers only.9 Another study found that non-Hispanic black patients had a lower risk of death than white patients after adjusting for clinical and sociodemographic factors,10 although other racial groups were not assessed. However, these studies looked more broadly at stage III and IV NSCLC patients, allowing for inclusion of patients who received systemic treatment only or a combination of CRT with systemic treatment. Additionally, these studies included patients diagnosed in years 2015-2016, which is before durvalumab was officially FDA-approved for consolidative therapy after definitive CRT for treatment of stage III NSCLC.

However, these findings highlight disparities in IO access, likely driven by multiple barriers including affordability, institutional availability, and systemic biases. This raises the importance of examining social determinants of health, defined by the WHO as the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age and their access to power and resources, which substantially influence health outcomes.11,12 Factors such as income, education, race, gender, transportation, and occupation are known to impact health.13, 14, 15

To date, no comprehensive study has evaluated disparities in IO use after CRT specifically for stage III NSCLC since 2018, across a broad range of social determinants, including race/ethnicity, income, education, insurance, and treatment setting. In this study, we aim to identify populations less likely to receive IO and hypothesize that historically marginalized groups, including black, Hispanic, and Asian patients, those with lower socioeconomic status, and those treated in community centers have lower IO use after CRT.

Materials and methods

A retrospective analysis was carried out using the NCDB. This study was conducted in compliance with our protocol approved by the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center institutional review board (IRB), and an IRB exemption was granted. We included patients ≥18 years of age with stage III NSCLC diagnosed between 2018 and 2021. American Joint Committee on Cancer Fifth to Eighth edition definition of stage III was used. The year 2018 was chosen in anticipation of increased IO use beginning that year due to FDA approval for the IO, durvalumab, for treatment of stage III NSCLC after definitive CRT. Patients receiving ≥60 Gy of radiation in ≥ 30 fractions and IO delivered within 50-150 days from starting CRT were included. Patients who did not receive chemotherapy with radiation or undergo surgery were excluded.

Categories of social determinants of health included standard categories provided by the NCDB, which were the following: race; ethnicity; insurance, subdivided into not insured, private insurance, Medicaid, Medicare, and other government; treatment site, categorized into academic/research program [which included National Cancer Institute (NCI)-designated comprehensive cancer centers], community cancer program, comprehensive community cancer program, and integrated network cancer program; Charlson-Deyo comorbidity index (CCI), with scores from 0 to 3, with 0 being no comorbidities and 3 being high comorbidity index; high school graduation rate (HSGR) based on patient’s zip code, categorized as quartiles (lowest, low, mid, high) per US Census data; income based on patient’s zip code, categorized similarly to HSGR, with lowest, low, mid, and high per US Census data; sex; and age.

Specifically for treatment sites, as defined by the Commission on Cancer Accreditation, the categorization used in NCDB, academic/research program, was defined as ‘facility that participates in postgraduate medical education in at least four program areas, including internal medicine and general surgery. The facility accessions >500 newly diagnosed cancer cases each year’. Community cancer program was defined as ‘The facility accessions >100 but <500 newly diagnosed cancer cases each year’. Comprehensive community cancer program was defined as a facility [that] accessions 500 or more newly diagnosed cancer cases each year’. Integrated network cancer program was defined as ‘facilities belonging to an organization that owns a group of facilities that offer integrated and comprehensive cancer care services and is overseen by a centralized governance structure/board and CEO’.16

Univariable logistic regression analyses were carried out in R (Version 4.4.1, R Core Development Team, Vienna, Austria) to evaluate the association between receipt of IO and each social determinant of health category. Race and Asian ethnicities were each compared with white patients, while Hispanic individuals and Hispanic ethnicities were compared with non-Hispanic patients. Insurance status used private insurance as the reference, and CCI scores were compared with a score of 0 as the reference. Treatment facility type was compared with academic/research facilities, and community-level HSGR and income were each compared using high levels as references.

Odds ratios (ORs) were calculated for IO use for each category of social determinants of health. The univariable analyses were followed by multivariable analysis with statistically significant factors (P < 0.05). Among individuals who received IO, the association between income level and area-level HSGR was assessed using a chi-square test of independence. Effect size was estimated using Cramér V analysis. R (Version 4.4.1, R Core Development Team) was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Median age was 68 years (interquartile range 62-74). Of the Asian patients, the majority were Other Asian (28.1%), followed by Chinese (16.5%), Filipino (12.0%), Vietnamese (11.5%), and South Asian, who were Indian and Pakistani patients (10.8%). Discussion

Our NCDB analysis showed that despite the advancement of IO, specifically with the FDA approval of durvalumab for consolidative therapy after definitive CRT for the treatment of stage III NSCLC, disparate patterns of consolidative IO exist across socioeconomic groups. Compared with white patients, black, Hispanic, South Asian, and Mexican patients were less likely to receive IO, highlighting persistent unequal access to and receipt of lifesaving treatment. Patients on Medicare, those treated at community centers, and those residing in areas with lowest and low HSGRs were less likely to receive IO, which was statistically significant in univariable and multivariable analyses.

Notably, patients with higher CCI scores (1 and 2), meaning a greater burden of coexisting medical conditions, were more likely to receive IO than those with a CCI score of 0, which persisted on multivariable analysis. One possible explanation is survivorship bias within this cohort: patients with comorbidities who successfully completed definitive CRT may represent a medically optimized and closely monitored subgroup, selected by their treating oncologists as suitable candidates for consolidative IO despite their comorbid conditions. In other words, the act of completing CRT itself may serve as a filter, enriching the surviving cohort with patients who—despite having comorbidities—were deemed fit enough by their providers to pursue aggressive multimodal therapy, and who subsequently were also offered IO. Patients with CCI of 0 in this cohort, by contrast, may include individuals whose other clinical characteristics, such as poor performance status not captured by CCI, marginal pulmonary function, or subclinical frailty, led clinicians to defer IO even in the absence of formal comorbidities. Although CCI serves as a proxy for performance status, without an assessment of fitness for receiving therapy, such as with the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) or Karnofsky Performance Status score, this analysis does not capture the effect of performance status on IO utilization.

This interpretation is consistent with a known limitation of CCI in the oncologic setting; it was originally developed and validated in a general medical inpatient population, not in patients with cancer undergoing active treatment. CCI captures specific organ-system comorbidities but does not incorporate performance status, pulmonary function, or functional reserve, all of which are critically relevant to the decision to initiate durvalumab after CRT. The PACIFIC trial, which established the standard of care for consolidative IO, enrolled patients with an ECOG performance status of 0 or 1, and real-world IO eligibility is heavily influenced by post-CRT pulmonary toxicity, including pneumonitis, which is not reflected in CCI. It is therefore plausible that patients with CCI of 0 who did not receive IO were excluded for reasons that CCI simply cannot measure. Future studies using datasets that capture ECOG performance status and pulmonary function testing would be better positioned to disentangle these relationships.

An additional consideration is that patients with higher CCI scores may have more frequent and intensive contact with the health care system, increasing the likelihood of follow-up appointments at which IO initiation could be discussed and facilitated. This health care engagement effect has been described in other treatment utilization studies and may partially account for the observed association.17, 18, 19, 20

Interestingly, in multivariable analyses, those who reside in areas with low and mid community income levels were more likely to receive IO after definitive CRT, which is a discordant finding from the univariable analysis, which showed that those who reside in areas with the lowest level of community income were less likely to receive IO. This finding was further investigated, as shown in Table 4: within each income group, the higher the HSGR in the area in which the patient resided, the greater was their likelihood of receiving IO as consolidative therapy. However, there was no clear pattern that emerged within each education level; income did not appear to dictate whether one received IO. In summary, among individuals who received IO, income and area-level HSGRs showed a strong association, indicating socioeconomic clustering within the treated population. Patients receiving IO tend to live in communities where income and education levels are aligned, indicating that socioeconomic factors group together among treated individuals and may influence patterns of treatment access and equity.

Additionally, patterns seen with community income could potentially be affected by the overall older population of patients who undergo treatment for NSCLC. One study showed that the median age of patients undergoing definitive CRT was 70 years, with an interquartile range of 63-77 years,21 which mirrored the median age of patients within this cohort. In this older patient group, most were likely retired or semiretired, meaning lower reported income levels, although they may have other assets that could offset income. This could potentially explain why, in the multivariable analysis, those who had low and mid community income levels were more likely to receive IO. However, as shown, income and educational attainment are more likely collinear and associated, such that one influences the other to some extent.

A strength of this study is the diverse representation of multiple categories of social determinants of health, particularly race and ethnicity. It is well known that there is underrepresentation of minority racial and ethnic groups in clinical trials and studies,22 in which Asians are often underrepresented.23 In a study that evaluated articles published in ‘New England Journal of Medicine’, ‘Journal of the American Medical Association’, ‘JAMA Internal Medicine’, ‘JAMA Pediatrics’, ‘Annals of Internal Medicine’, and ‘Pediatrics’ from 2015 to 2016, 263 articles (24.4%) identified Asians as a distinct race/ethnicity, and the median percentage of Asians per study was 3.8%. Of the 263 articles, 28 (10.6%) reported outcomes for Asians; nine (3.4%) included information about Asian subgroups.23 Asian Americans were the fastest-growing racial or ethnic group from 2000 to 2019 in the United States, with a percentage growth of 81%, followed by Hispanic individuals at 70%. By 2060, the population of Asian Americans in the US is projected to surpass 35 million.24 With this rapid increase in the Asian American population, as well as other ethnic and racial groups, it will be necessary to be more inclusive of their representation in studies examining treatment utilization, access to care, and health outcomes.

Although there are advantages to using a large national database, such as the inclusion of a more diverse group of patients, there are caveats to using such large databases. As this is a retrospective study, although we can examine associations between diverse social determinants of health categories and lower odds of receiving IO, causal relationships cannot be established. The NCDB does not collect information about smoking status, which can substantially affect the overall health of the patient in the context of lung cancer. The NCDB provides a category for CCI, which is a weighted score based on age and comorbidities, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, although it is still a different measure from looking at smoking separately. However, given that >85% of lung cancer deaths in the United States are attributable to cigarette smoking,25,26 it is highly likely that a substantial number of patients included in the NCDB for lung cancer would be former or current smokers.

Although NCDB includes diverse types of treatment facilities, as mentioned in this study, there may be other types of facilities that are not captured in the NCDB, such as nonaccredited treatment facilities or facilities serving rural areas. This may lead to the exclusion of vulnerable populations who likely face disparities in access to IO.

Additionally, at the time of data collection, NCDB did not have separate categories for individuals with known targetable mutations, nor did it have a category for other forms of systemic treatments such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors, and other inhibitors of targetable mutations. Therefore, in this analysis, all individuals who potentially harbored targetable mutations were included in a single cohort. The collection of driver mutation data is becoming more widespread in NCDB, and it will be interesting to note subsequent follow-up studies that consider driver mutation data as more detailed information on systemic treatments.

Clinicians may have been less likely to consider consolidative IO in patients with mutations in targetable oncogenic drivers such as EGFR and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), which could contribute to observed differences in IO receipt across racial and ethnic groups. Because the prevalence of these drivers varies by race and ethnicity,27, 28, 29, 30 this may partially explain disparities in IO utilization. However, the NCDB does not capture oncogenic driver mutation status, limiting the ability to directly assess this relationship.

With advancements in targeted cancer therapies, a potential area of future research is looking at disparate patterns of utilization of targetable cancer therapies in patients who have undergone definitive CRT for NSCLC. With the landmark LAURA trial,31 which showed overall survival benefit in stage III NSCLC patients with EGFR mutations undergoing consolidation therapy with osimertinib after definitive CRT, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines have been updated to reflect this finding.5 Additionally, this could allow for a push to expand the categorization of NCDB to include other emerging forms of systemic treatments that target mutations in EGFR, ALK, and rat sarcoma protein (RAS).

Conclusion

The current landscape for the treatment of NSCLC is rapidly evolving with new innovations such as IO, which is being used with other treatment modalities such as CRT. These advancements in treatment are improving recurrence-free and overall survival. However, with the rapid rise of advancements in treatment options and technology, there is also a danger of inequities in access to such therapies. This work demonstrated that compared with white patients, black, Hispanic, South Asian, and Mexican patients were less likely to use IO. Additionally, patients in the following groups were less likely to receive IO: those on Medicare, those with lower levels of comorbidities according to CCI, those treated at community centers, and those residing outside communities with the highest HSGRs. These findings underscore a critical need for targeted efforts to ensure equitable access to IO and other advanced therapies across all racial, socioeconomic, and health care delivery groups to fully realize the benefits of modern NSCLC treatment for every patient in the post-IO era.
