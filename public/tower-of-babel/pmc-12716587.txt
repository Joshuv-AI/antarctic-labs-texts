Silent losses: predictors of anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies and their associations with menstrual bleeding in Lebanon – findings from a national cross-sectional study
Authors: James P Wirth, Amirhossein Yarparvar, Valeria Galetti, Carla El-Mallah, Mira Boutros, Joelle Najjar, Mira El Mokdad, Diana Kobayter, Nicolai Petry, Mirella Abi Zeid Daou, Charles Wakim, Farah Asfahani, Firass Abiad, Omar Obeid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSignificant physiological changes occur in adolescence, including the onset of menarche in girls. However, the extent to which menstrual bleeding contributes to anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies remains unclear. This study assesses the prevalence of anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies and examines their association with menarcheal status, menstrual bleeding severity and other factors among adolescent girls in Lebanon.MethodsData were collected as part of a nationally representative cross-sectional study involving 2254 adolescent girls, 10–19 years of age. Household interviews were used to gather information on household demographics and wealth, and individual interviews assessed dietary diversity, menarcheal status and menstrual bleeding severity (assessed for post-menarche girls only). Blood samples were analysed to determine haemoglobin, ferritin, retinol-binding protein, folate and vitamin B12 levels. We used multivariable models to identify factors associated with anaemia and deficiencies in iron, folate, vitamin B12 and vitamin A.ResultsOverall, 20.3% of girls had anaemia, 29.5% iron deficiency, 13.9% iron deficiency anaemia, 14.4% folate deficiency, 17.7% vitamin B12 deficiency and 3.9% vitamin A deficiency. Among post-menarche adolescent girls, 22.4% reported severe menstrual bleeding. Bleeding severity was significantly associated with iron deficiency and iron deficiency anaemia in multivariable models. Iron deficiency was the primary predictor of anaemia, and a significant dose–response relationship was found between menstrual bleeding and anaemia in iron-deficient girls. Post-menarche status, but not bleeding severity, was associated with 46% and 54% higher prevalence of B12 and folate deficiencies, respectively. Neither menarcheal status nor bleeding severity was associated with vitamin A deficiency.ConclusionAmong adolescent girls in Lebanon, anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies were common, and menstrual bleeding severity was strongly associated with iron deficiency and anaemia in iron deficient girls. Menstrual health is a key predictor of nutritional status in adolescent girls and should be integrated into health programmes and nutrition assessments.Keywords: Nutrition, Public Health, Anaemia

Introduction

Adolescence is the transitional stage between childhood and adulthood, defined by the WHO as the period of life between 10 and 19 years of age.1 Nutrient requirements increase during adolescence to support rapid growth, and in females to compensate for nutrient losses from menstruation.2 As a result, adolescent girls face a higher risk of anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies than their male peers.3

Physical and hormonal transformations throughout this stage profoundly impact adolescent girls’ nutritional status. The combination of menstrual blood loss and iron status has been extensively studied. Heavy menstrual bleeding directly affects iron status and could lead to iron deficiency and iron deficiency anaemia. Moreover, a recent review article highlighted the increased mineral needs during the menstrual cycle.4

Adolescence has been described as a ‘nutrition-sensitive phase’.5 Indeed, malnutrition in adolescent girls directly affects their physical growth and brain development,6 predisposes them to morbidities and early ageing in adulthood,7 8 and contributes to the ‘intergenerational cycle of malnutrition’,9 especially in developing countries where early pregnancies pose a challenge. In their work on adolescent girls in low- and middle-income countries, Keats et al highlighted low consumption of nutritious foods and high consumption of energy-dense and nutrient-poor foods.10 Moreover, a representative study in Greater Beirut and Mount Lebanon on 437 girls 8–18 years of age reported a prevalence of 20.8% of iron deficiency, and the proportions were higher in menstruating girls (26.8%) compared with non-menstruating girls (11.6%).11

Our analysis aims to (a) assess the prevalence of anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies among adolescent girls in Lebanon prior to and post-menarche, with consideration of the intensity of menstrual bleeding and (b) examine how menarcheal status (pre-menarche and post-menarche) and bleeding severity (mild, moderate or severe bleeding) are associated with anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies, and identify other risk factors (inflammation, fever in the last 2 weeks, micronutrient deficiencies and self-reported consumption of multivitamins in the past 6 months) that may mediate these associations. By modelling these factors together and accounting for biological and behavioural covariates, the analysis offers insights into the interplay between menstrual health and nutritional status among adolescent girls in Lebanon.

Materials and methods

Stratification and sampling procedure

We used data from households and adolescent girls collected as part of the Lebanon Integrated Micronutrient, Anthropometry and Child Development Survey (LIMA). The LIMA survey was conducted between July and November 2023 on a representative household-based survey that recruited all adolescent girls from randomly selected households. The survey consisted of 10 strata: all 8 governorates of Lebanon, a Syrian informal settlement stratum and a Palestinian Camp stratum. The sampling universe included all households located in Lebanon during the data collection period. In the absence of census data, a sampling framework for the eight governorate strata was created using a hexagon grid, where each hexagon functioned as an enumeration area (EA) and satellite imagery was used to estimate the population within each EA.12 For the stratum of the Palestinian camps, the United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East provided lists and maps of EA from the 2017 Population and Housing Census in Palestinian camps and Gatherings in Lebanon.13 For the stratum of the Syrian informal settlement, the updated settlement list was supplied by the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees.

A two-stage sampling procedure was used in this survey. In the first stage, EAs were chosen based on probability proportional to their size. In the second stage, households were randomly selected with equal probability and adolescent girls between 10 and 19 years old from all participating households were recruited.12 A flow chart of data collection and participation is presented in online supplemental figure A.

Data collection

Questionnaires

13 teams of data collectors who received rigorous training were responsible for data collection. Household-level indicators included geographical location and other demographics, wealth index14 and household food security access.15 Individual indicators comprised education, chronic illnesses and morbidities, dietary supplementation and dietary diversity using the minimum dietary diversity for women (MDD-W) methodology.16 A special module investigating women’s physiology, which asked about pregnancy, lactation, menarcheal status and menstrual bleeding intensity, was recorded. Pre-menarche or post-menarche was determined by asking adolescent girls if they had ever menstruated. When yes, adolescent girls were asked to classify the intensity of their menstrual bleeding using the following categories and descriptions: minimal (ie, only a few drops of blood), mild (ie, soaking less than one pad or tampon every 3 hours or more), moderate (ie, soaking more than one pad or tampon every 3 hours or more) or severe (ie, soaking one or two pads or tampons every one or 2 hours). Information was recorded on Kobocollect using tablet computers.

Biological samples

Following the questionnaires, blood was withdrawn via venipuncture from the antecubital vein in two different tubes, the serum-separating tube and EDTA (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA), compatible for the measurements of different biomarkers. Blood was stored at +4°C to +8°C and transported to the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC), Nutrition Research Laboratory at the American University of Beirut (AUB) and the Laboratories for the Environment, Agriculture and Food of AUB for processing and analysis. Plasma aliquots were then shipped frozen at −70°C to the VitMin Lab (Willstätt, Germany) for the analysis of retinol-binding protein (RBP), serum ferritin (SF), soluble transferrin receptor, C reactive protein (CRP) and α1-acid glycoprotein (AGP). Folate and vitamin B12 concentrations were measured in serum aliquots at AUBMC.

Variable construction and case definitions

Adolescent girls with MDD-W scores ≥5 were classified as having a minimally diverse diet, whereas girls with MDD-W scores between 0 and 4 were classified as having a non-diverse diet.16 Adolescent girls were classified as anaemic when their haemoglobin concentration, adjusted for elevation and smoking status, was <120 g/L.17 Iron deficiency was defined as inflammation-adjusted SF levels <15 mg/L.18 Ferritin was adjusted for inflammation using the BRINDA equation19 and internal correction factors20 for CRP and AGP. Internal deciles were used instead of the BRINDA external deciles because the internal deciles for the log of CRP differed from the reference deciles by 26%.20 Internal deciles for the log of CRP and AGP were −2.30 and −0.53, respectively, and the internal coefficients were 0.1290442 for the log of CRP and 0.1365464 for the log of AGP.

Iron deficiency anaemia was defined as concurrent anaemia and iron deficiency. Vitamin A deficiency was defined as RBP values <0.62 µmol/L; the vitamin A deficiency cut-off using RBP was established using the correlation between RBP and plasma retinol.12 The BRINDA project does not recommend inflammation adjustment for RBP in women of reproductive age (ie, 15–49 years), and since adolescent girls 15–19 years of age are part of this population group, RBP was not adjusted for inflammation.19 Folate and vitamin B12 deficiencies were defined as serum folate concentrations <10 nmol/L and serum B12 vitamin B12 concentrations <150 pmol/L.21 Bleeding status was recoded as an ordinal variable to minimal/mild, moderate and severe, as very few adolescent girls reported ‘minimal’ bleeding.

Statistical analysis

A combined categorical variable capturing both menarcheal status and menstrual bleeding intensity (wMenarcheBleeding) was used and consisted of four categories: (a) pre-menarche/no bleeding, (b) post-menarche with minimum/mild bleeding, (c) post-menarche with moderate bleeding and (d) post-menarche with severe bleeding.

Missing or incomplete data for some biomarkers occurred as there was insufficient blood volume to measure all parameters. Because there were relatively few subjects with incomplete biomarker data, univariate and bivariate data analyses were conducted using all available data. Apart from the running of multivariable models, where listwise deletion occurs automatically, no data were excluded from the analysis, nor were statistical procedures used to impute missing values.

Data analysis was performed using Stata SE V.14.2. Univariate and bivariate analyses accounted for complex sampling design (ie, survey weights, stratification, clustering) to address unequal probability of selection, cluster-level response rates and the effects of stratification and clustering. When examining the bivariate associations between nutritional biomarkers and menarcheal status and bleeding severity (post-menarche girls only), statistical significance was set at a p-value of 0.05.

When constructing multivariable models, bivariate associations between potential risk factors of anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies were examined, and variables with p values <0.2 were flagged for potential inclusion in the initial regression models. Prior to model construction, the associations between independent variables were examined by calculating the subgroup prevalences and χ2 p values; a p value <0.05 was used to identify potential collinear relationships between independent variables. When potential collinearity was identified, independent variables were retained or excluded based on the authors’ judgement.

The use of menarcheal status or menarcheal/bleeding severity in multivariable models was based on the presence of significant bivariate associations and observed dose–response relationships in the bivariate analyses. Other factors examined included inflammation status, minimum dietary diversity,16 consumption of micronutrient supplements (ie, iron, folic acid, vitamin B12, vitamin A, vitamin D, multivitamins) in the past 6 months, usual consumption of coffee and/or tea after meals, illness in the past 2 weeks (ie, diarrhoea, fever or lower respiratory infection), household food insecurity status (ie, food secure, mild insecurity, moderate insecurity and severe insecurity) and household wealth status. Household wealth was assessed by calculating a wealth index using a principal component analysis model comprised of durable goods and household dwelling characteristics.22 At the household level, the wealth index was categorised into quintiles, accounting for household-level weights. Multivariable models were then constructed,23 and insignificant variables (ie, p value ≥0.05) were dropped from the model sequentially, starting with the variable with the largest p value.

Poisson regression models with robust (sandwich) SEs, which produce adjusted prevalence ratios, were preferred to regression models that produce odds ratios (eg, logistic regression) because the prevalence of all outcome variables, except vitamin A deficiency, exceeded 10% and thus violated the rare disease assumption.24 Poisson regressions were conducted without applying weights. We opted for unweighted analyses because our primary objective was to estimate associations between independent variables and outcomes, rather than to produce population-level parameter estimates.25 To partially account for the sampling design and individual characteristics, we included survey strata and age, respectively, as covariates in all multivariable models.

Results

The complete sample included 2247 adolescent girls, and descriptive statistics for all girls and by menarcheal status are provided in online supplemental table A. The median age of adolescent girls included in the survey was approximately 14.3 years (95% CI 12.1 to 16.9), with the median age of pre-menarche girls being 11.5 years (95% CI 10.7 to 12.3; n=686) compared with 15.7 years (95% CI 13.7 to 17.6; n=1561) in post-menarche girls. Approximately two-thirds of the adolescent girls were Lebanese, and about one-third were Syrian, and similar distributions were observed by menarcheal status. Few adolescent girls were of nationalities, with the majority of those classified as ‘other’ being Palestinian.

Nationally, approximately one-third of girls resided in households classified as food secure, with the remainder classified in households with some level of food insecurity. Food insecurity status was similar between pre-menarche and post-menarche girls. The highest proportion of girls resided in households in the lowest wealth quintile, and the smallest proportion resided in the highest wealth quintile. This same pattern was observed in pre-menarche girls, whereas there was a close to equal distribution of post-menarche girls by wealth quintile.

Educational attainment varied by menarcheal status, with a greater proportion of pre-menarche girls reporting that primary school was the highest schooling level completed (65.1%) compared with a lower proportion in post-menarche girls (26.3%). Similarly, 33.7% of post-menarche girls reported that secondary school or higher was the highest education level achieved, compared with only 1.2% of pre-menarche girls. The difference in educational attainment is undoubtedly influenced substantially by the age difference between pre-menarche and post-menarche girls.

Nationally, approximately 13%, 64% and 22% of post-menarche girls reported mild/minimal, moderate and severe menstrual bleeding, respectively (online supplemental table A). No statistically significant subgroup differences in bleeding severity were observed by minimum dietary diversity status (p=0.369), nationality (p=0.625), household food insecurity status (p=0.552) and household wealth quintile (p=0.832) online supplemental figure A). Among all subgroups examined, the prevalence of severe menstrual bleeding ranged from 19% to 26%.

Approximately 20% and 30% of girls were anaemic and iron-deficient, respectively (online supplemental figure B). The prevalence of iron deficiency anaemia, folate and B12 deficiencies was similar (14%–18%), and vitamin A deficiency was found in <5% of adolescent girls. The prevalence of anaemia, iron deficiency and iron deficiency anaemia was higher in post-menarche girls, and a positive dose–response relationship was found between bleeding severity and anaemia, iron deficiency and iron deficiency anaemia within this group.

The prevalence of folate and vitamin B12 deficiencies was higher in post-menarche girls. Although there were statistically significant differences in folate deficiency by bleeding severity, no dose–response relationship was observed. For vitamin A deficiency, no statistically significant differences were observed for either menarcheal status or bleeding severity (table 1).

Iron deficiency was associated with bleeding status, vitamin A status and vitamin B12 status. A dose–response relationship was observed between iron deficiency and menarcheal/bleeding status, with girls with mild, moderate and heavy menstrual bleeding having a 1.5, 1.7 and 2.0 times higher prevalence of iron deficiency, respectively. Girls with vitamin A deficiency and vitamin B12 deficiency had approximately 40% and 20% higher prevalence of iron deficiency compared with their replete counterparts.

Similarly to iron deficiency, iron deficiency anaemia was significantly associated with menarcheal/bleeding status. The risk of iron deficiency anaemia increased with bleeding severity, ranging from two times higher in girls with mild bleeding to four times higher in those with severe bleeding compared with girls with no bleeding (ie, pre-menarche). Additionally, vitamin A deficiency was associated with an 80% higher prevalence of anaemia. Use of multivitamin supplements in the past 6 months was associated with a 64% lower prevalence of iron deficiency anaemia.

Discussion

Our analysis finds that the anaemia and micronutrient status of adolescent girls in Lebanon is strongly influenced by menarcheal status and the severity of menstrual bleeding. In our study, bleeding severity was highly associated with iron deficiency, and girls with any level of menstrual bleeding had a substantially higher risk of anaemia if they were also iron deficient. The association between menstrual bleeding and iron deficiency has been previously documented in the blood donation literature26 27 and in cross-sectional studies.28 Menstrual bleeding leads to a loss of iron contained in haemoglobin, which eventually leads to iron depletion and iron deficiency.29 For example, in an adolescent girl with a haemoglobin concentration of 120 g/L (equivalent to 0.12 g haemoglobin per mL of blood), the loss of 100 mL of blood would correspond to a loss of about 41 mg of iron (0.12 g×100 mL×3.39 mg iron/g Hb30). Blood is not commonly considered an iron storage vessel; however, approximately 70% of an adult’s iron is found in haemoglobin.31 Moreover, erythropoiesis is largely driven by iron efflux from erythrophagocytosis, where approximately 80% of iron, initially in the form of heme, is recycled.32 The majority of iron required for red blood cell (RBC) production comes from the recycling of erythrocytes, and dietary iron absorption, which typically amounts to only 1–2 mg per day, is primarily used to compensate for iron losses and maintain overall iron balance.33

Our analysis also found that vitamin A deficiency was significantly associated with anaemia, iron deficiency and iron deficiency anaemia. These associations are likely due to the fact that sufficient quantities of vitamin A are needed to mobilise iron stores, and thus low levels of circulating retinol impair the mobilisation of iron from the liver which subsequently affects erythropoiesis.34 Vitamin A deficiency may also be associated with iron deficiency and iron deficiency anaemia because heme iron and preformed vitamin A are often found in the same animal source foods, such as meat, liver, dairy and eggs.

Our analysis found that anaemia was also associated with inflammation, which may suggest that chronic inflammation is responsible for a portion of the anaemia observed.35 In addition, we found that consumption of multivitamins in the past 6 months was significantly associated with a lower prevalence of iron deficiency anaemia, but not with iron deficiency and anaemia when examined separately. In the anaemia model, multivitamin use was excluded during variable selection, though its near-significant effect (p=0.089) suggests it may influence haemoglobin status without substantially improving iron stores. However, given the small number of adolescent girls consuming multivitamins (n=78; 3.8%, 95% CI 2.5 to 5.7), these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Similar to our study, a study of women from the UK and Australia found no significant association between anaemia prevalence and heavy menstrual bleeding36 and that a proxy for iron status mediated the effect of heavy menstrual bleeding on anaemia. This study did not measure or present the iron deficiency prevalence in women, but noted that a large proportion of Australian women (~25%) reported the use of intravenous iron, which was the only significant and protective factor against anaemia in the study.

The risk of vitamin B12 deficiency was significantly higher in post-menarche and iron-deficient girls. An increased risk of B12 deficiency in post-menarche is likely due to increased B12 requirements in adolescent girls aged 14–18 years.2 The association between vitamin B12 deficiency and iron deficiency may be due to the fact that heme iron, the form of iron that is better absorbed, and vitamin B12 are often found in the same animal source foods and commonly co-occur.37 Our study also found no association and no dose–response relationship between bleeding severity and vitamin B12 deficiency in post-menarche girls, which may be due to the fact that the majority of the body’s vitamin B12 is found in the liver, and only ≈10%–20% of vitamin B12 is found in the blood.38 As such, the amount of vitamin B12 lost during heavy menstrual bleeding is small, and even the vitamin B12 lost during repeated episodes of heavy menstrual bleeding can be replaced by the liver’s long-term B12 stores.

Folate deficiency was significantly associated with menarcheal status and vitamin A deficiency. In our study, the increased risk of folate deficiency in post-menarche girls is likely due to low intake of folate-rich foods39 and increased folate requirements in adolescent girls aged 14–18 years.2 Folate plays a critical role in DNA synthesis and cell differentiation, making its availability particularly important during a period of rapid growth. Moreover, folate is essential for post-menstrual endometrial regeneration and repair, which involves extensive cell proliferation.40 The association between vitamin A deficiency and folate deficiency is likely not causal, and the association may reflect the fact that folate and provitamin A are found in similar plant-based foods commonly consumed in Lebanon, such as legumes (eg, lentils, chickpeas, fava beans) and dark leafy greens (eg, parsley, molokhia, Swiss chard). Although our study found a significant association between folate deficiency and bleeding severity, no dose–response relationship was observed. Approximately 50% of the body’s folate is found in the blood, with the vast majority contained in RBCs.38 As such, increased blood loss should adversely impact folate status. One possible explanation for the lack of a dose–response relationship may be due to the physiological adaptive mechanism that maintains folate homeostasis during deficiency. In particular, folate absorption can be upregulated during deficiency through increased expression of intestinal transporters, including the proton-coupled folate transporter and the reduced folate carrier.41 This mechanism may affect the associations between blood loss and folate deficiency, which in our study is defined as low serum folate, rather than low RBC folate.

Iron deficiency and recent fever were the only factors significantly associated with vitamin A deficiency in our study. The relationship between fever and vitamin A deficiency could plausibly be mediated via subclinical inflammation, as acute and chronic inflammation can decrease circulating concentrations of retinol and RBP.19 However, ancillary analyses found no association between vitamin A deficiency and inflammation categories (ie, none, elevated CRP only, elevated CRP and AGP, elevated AGP only; p=0.530) or any inflammation (ie, no inflammation, elevated CRP and/or AGP; p=0.561) (data not shown). Additionally, the direction of the association could be reversed, with vitamin A deficiency increasing the risk of fever. This interpretation would suggest that vitamin A deficiency, which compromises immune function,42 increases adolescent girls’ susceptibility to infections with symptomatic fever.

The anaemia prevalence observed in Lebanon is similar to that found by recent national surveys from Jordan43 43 and Oman44 that measured the nutritional status in women of reproductive age, including adolescent girls 15–19 years of age. Among adolescent girls aged 15–19 years, anaemia prevalence in Jordan and Oman was 22.4% and 28.8%, respectively. Folate deficiency was also roughly comparable and was found in 7.3% and 13.7% of 15- to 19-year-old girls in Jordan and Oman. Lebanon’s iron deficiency prevalence was similar to that found in Oman (32.1%), but substantially lower than that found in Jordan (65.4%). In contrast, B12 deficiency prevalence in Lebanon was similar to that found in Jordan (21.6%) but was substantially higher than the prevalence found in Oman (9.5%). Vitamin A deficiency in Lebanon is also similar to Jordan and Oman, where the prevalence—although among women 15–49 years of age—is 3.0% and 0.2%, respectively.

The prevalence of severe bleeding in our study is slightly higher than that found in other studies in the Middle East region. A small-scale study of nursing students aged 18–26 years in Beirut (n=352) found that 11.7% of participants had heavy menstrual bleeding, defined as using ≥5 menstrual pads per day.45 A population-based study (n=1393) in Iran found that 15.2% of women 15–45 years of age had heavy menstrual bleeding,46 with heavy bleeding defined as excessive menstrual blood loss that interferes with a woman’s quality of life.47 Despite these differences, the severe menstrual bleeding prevalence in our study is comparable to the range of heavy menstrual bleeding (ie, >80 mL blood loss) in women of reproductive age reported by Mansour et al,48 18%–38%, based on seven studies/surveys related to menstrual bleeding.

A key limitation of our study is that we did not have information on gynaecological age (ie, number of years since menarche). This information could have been used to model the effect of cumulative blood loss on anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies. In addition, we did not use a validated indicator to assess the severity of menstrual bleeding. While using the gold standard alkaline-haematin method would not be feasible in a national survey, semi-quantitative tools, such as the pictorial blood assessment chart, have been validated and could have yielded more precise estimations of blood loss.49 While heavy menstrual bleeding is defined as blood loss exceeding 80 mL per cycle, and laboratory-based methods provide objective means for diagnosis, self-perceived assessments capture the subjective burden of menstruation and they are now recognised as a meaningful component in evaluating the severity of menstruation. In fact, Russo et al50 found that self-perception can reliably describe menstrual blood loss across various age groups, suggesting its utility in clinical evaluations. Another limitation to our study is that we did not ask respondents about certain conditions or actions that could cause heavy menstrual bleeding, such as inherited blood disorders51 52 and physical inactivity.53 Finally, our study assessed folate deficiency using serum folate rather than RBC folate, the latter being a long-term indicator of status as it reflects folate stored within RBCs.54 It is therefore plausible that the association between bleeding severity and folate status would be clearer and stronger had RBC folate been measured.

A key strength of this study is the comprehensive suite of nutritional biomarkers measured in adolescent girls. To our knowledge, this is the first population-level study to incorporate indicators of menstrual health, including bleeding severity, and examine their association with anaemia and multiple indicators of micronutrient deficiency among adolescent girls. This work contributes to a largely understudied area and highlights the intersection between menstrual health and nutritional status. Moreover, by identifying nutritional vulnerabilities associated with menstrual characteristics, the study offers valuable insights for designing targeted interventions addressing both micronutrient deficiencies and menstrual health in adolescent girls in Lebanon.

Conclusion

Overall, approximately 20% and 30% of adolescent girls were anaemic or had iron deficiency, respectively, and 14% of girls had iron deficiency anaemia. Folate and B12 deficiencies affected 14%–18% of adolescent girls, while vitamin A deficiency was found in <5% of girls. Beyond these descriptive statistics, our analysis presents a strong association between menarcheal status, bleeding severity and multiple micronutrient deficiencies. Severe menstrual bleeding, reported by approximately 22% of post-menarche girls, was significantly associated with increased prevalence of anaemia, iron deficiency and iron deficiency anaemia. However, our analysis indicates that menstrual bleeding severity does not directly affect anaemia but rather influences anaemia via iron deficiency. This is indicated by a dose–response relationship showing significant increases in the prevalence of anaemia by bleeding severity among iron-deficient girls but not among iron-replete girls.

These findings underscore the need to incorporate menstrual health indicators into adolescent nutrition assessments and anaemia-prevention strategies. Screening for heavy bleeding, improving access to iron and folate supplementation and strengthening health education on menstrual health should be prioritised in adolescent health programmes and policies to reduce the burden of anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies.
