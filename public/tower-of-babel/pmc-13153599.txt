Walking speed and sound limb loading are related to femoral cartilage thickness but not cartilage deformation in those with traumatic lower-limb amputation
Authors: Steven A Garcia, Nikou Nikoumanesh, Shraddha Sudhir, Kharma C Foucher, Lindsay S Hannigan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIndividuals with lower-limb amputation (LLA) are at high risk for sound knee osteoarthritis (OA) development, presumably due to altered gait mechanics [1]. Yet, few data have linked gait mechanics with direct imaging of sound knee health post-LLA. Here, we evaluated associations between preferred walk speed, sound limb loading and femoral cartilage thickness and strains in those with LLA.DesignTen persons with traumatic LLA (Transfemoral:8, Transtibial:2, Age:50.3 ± 15.8 yrs, BMI:27.1 ± 5.9, Time since Amputation: 17.5 ± 14.1 yrs) completed treadmill gait assessments and femoral cartilage ultrasound. Vertical GRFs (1st, 2nd peak), and knee flexion (KFM) and adduction moments (KAM) during treadmill walking at preferred speed were quantified and expressed as absolute (N, Nm) and normalized (%BW, %BW∗Ht). Femoral cartilage ultrasound were captured in the sound knee in max flexion to evaluate trochlear and medial femoral cartilage thickness (MFC) before and after a 15-min walk to evaluate cartilage mechanical function (i.e., strain). Pearsons r were used to evaluate correlations between walk speed, limb loads and cartilage outcomes (α = 0.05).ResultsWalking speed was correlated with resting MFC thickness (r = 0.71, p = 0.03). Larger absolute KFM was moderately correlated (r = 0.66, p = 0.05) with thicker resting trochlear cartilage thickness. No other loading metrics were associated with resting cartilage thickness or cartilage strains.ConclusionFaster habitual walking speeds and larger absolute KFM were associated with thicker sound limb MFC and trochlear cartilage which may suggest more functionally mobile individuals and higher knee loads exhibit healthier cartilage. Future work should evaluate how mobility and limb loading impacts OA trajectories post-LLA.Keywords: Lower limb amputation, Gait, Cartilage health

Introduction

As of 2019, approximately 2 million people in the United States live with a lower limb amputation (LLA); this number is expected to double by 2050 [1]. People living with LLA experience a host of challenges regaining ambulatory function and completing physical activities. Accompanying these mobility challenges, those with LLA are 17 times more likely to develop knee osteoarthritis (OA) compared to age-matched peers [2]. This is concerning as OA may contribute to more severe activity limitations [3], lead to earlier loss of functional independence, and further degrade health-related quality of life. Identifying modifiable risk factors that contribute to accelerated OA development in the sound knee after LLA could guide the development of targeted rehabilitation strategies to prevent or delay knee OA after LLA.

As those with LLA adapt to prosthesis use, they may place additional demands on their sound side. This has been measured during gait analysis studies as greater ground reaction forces (GRFs) on the sound limbs of people with LLA relative to both the prosthetic sides and the limbs of people without amputation [[4], [5], [6]]. Though, evidence on whether joint-specific loading metrics such as knee moments or knee contact force estimates are increased in the sound knee during gait remains largely inconclusive. Nonetheless, it remains widely theorized that increased sound limb loading demands is a primary facilitator of cartilage breakdown and OA development after LLA [7]. Recently, one prospective study of 17 male service members approximately 8 years after LLA examined associations between knee moments, joint contact force estimates via musculoskeletal modeling and radiographic OA progression over a 2-year follow-up. Authors observed that higher peak knee adduction moments and joint contact forces at baseline were associated with loss of tibiofemoral and patellofemoral joint space over the follow-up period [8]. Importantly, all service members at baseline reported no clinical signs of joint degeneration (i.e., 0 Kellgren-Lawrence Score). While this study underscores the importance of understanding the role of joint loading during gait on subsequent cartilage loss, it did not directly assess cartilage thickness (due to limitations of radiographs) – warranting additional work evaluating gait predictors of sound knee cartilage health.

Structural assessments of cartilage thickness can be routinely and easily accessed via ultrasound imaging offering substantial clinical value [9]. Further, functional assessments of cartilage strains following acute exercise (i.e., the amount of change in cartilage thickness) can provide complimentary evaluation of the tissue's mechanical integrity [10,11]. Alterations in cartilage matrix composition and presumably it's mechanical function are considered some of the earliest signatures of OA-related breakdown and occur prior to overt structural loss of cartilage [12]. Walking mechanics are consistently shown to be highly influential on cartilage structure and matrix composition across the life span and the OA time-course and thus, may be important factors underlying cartilage alterations in those with LLA. For instance, previous works in those with ACL injury have found that higher vertical GRFs and faster preferred walking speed are global gait makers linked with cartilage thickness, MRI-based markers of cartilage composition and biochemical markers of cartilage metabolism [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Further, knee mechanics such as knee flexion and adduction moments are related to ultrasound based-measures of cartilage thickness [[20], [21], [22], [23]] and strains [24] in both healthy and at-risk cohorts (i.e., ACLR, obesity). While joint moments (and GRFs) used in these previous studies do not fully reflect joint-specific forces, both the knee flexion moment and knee adduction moment uniquely contribute to joint contact force estimates [25]. Nonetheless, while the associations between gait and cartilage structure and function assessment using ultrasound have been shown in a variety of healthy and at-risk OA populations, we do not yet know whether similar associations between limb or joint loading metrics with cartilage outcomes can be expected in those with LLA. Given the utility of ultrasound and the limited work evaluating cartilage outcomes in those with LLA, these data would fill critical gaps in knowledge on amputee-specific gait features linked with knee health and OA-risk.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to probe the assumption that increased loading of the sound limb during walking contributes to OA development by evaluating the associations among sound limb vertical GRFs, knee joint moments (i.e., flexion and adduction moment), preferred walk speed, and femoral cartilage ultrasonography (i.e., cartilage thickness and strains) in those with traumatic LLA. We hypothesized that higher GRFs, knee moments and slower speeds would be associated with thinner femoral cartilage and greater acute strains in sound knees.

Methods

Ten participants with unilateral, traumatic LLA amputation (Table 1) volunteered to participate in this single-session study. All participants were 1) at least one year post-LLA, 2) comfortable ambulating with their prosthesis for a minimum of 6 months prior to participation, and 3) capable of walking continuously for at least 6 min without the use of external ambulatory aids. Exclusion criteria included LLA attributed to dysvascular etiologies, neurological disorders affecting gait and previous orthopedic injury within the previous 12 months. All study protocols were approved by the University Institutional Review Board and participants provided written, informed consent prior to study procedures.

Ultrasound imaging procedures

Ultrasound imaging was completed when participants arrived to the laboratory following the completion of informed consent procedures. Prior to imaging, participants rested supine for 30 min to allow for cartilage thickness normalization from preceding activity (i.e., walking into the lab). Following the standardized rest period, femoral cartilage ultrasound images were captured in the sound limb in maximum knee flexion at two sites using standardized parameters (Fig. 1: Butterfly IQ, 12 MHz, depth = 3 cm) by a single investigator with over 8 years of ultrasound imaging experience. We first evaluated the femoral trochlear cartilage (Fig. 1: Left panel) within the patellofemoral joint by aligning the probe transversely across the medial and lateral femoral condyles [20,26,27]. A second view of the distal medial femur (Fig. 1: Right Panel) was then acquired to evaluate the distal medial femoral cartilage by aligning the probe longitudinally [22,27]. Three images at each site were captured at this “resting” time-point and procedures were repeated immediately following a 15-min treadmill walk at participants preferred speeds to evaluate cartilage mechanical function (i.e., strain – percent and absolute (mm) change in cartilage thickness). For strain metrics, negative values indicated reductions in cartilage thickness following walking (i.e., thinning) whereas positive values indicate increased thickness following exercise.

Ultrasound imaging was performed at two sites with participants placed in maximum knee flexion. Left Panel: Transverse view of femoral trochlear cartilage and segmented regions of interest (ROIs). Right Panel: Longitudinal view of the distal medial femoral condylar cartilage and segmented ROIs. Cartilage thickness was evaluated as the average Euclidean distance between superficial and deep cartilage contours at each pixel across the ROI.

Femoral trochlear cartilage thickness from transverse and longitudinal ROIs were calculated via an open-source MATLAB application [28]. Images were imported and optimized using the SCOUT application via image filtering to improve contrast and clarity of cartilage boundaries. Then, the superior cartilage-synovium border and deep cartilage-bone interface were traced to define ROIs. The intercondylar notch was defined as the deepest point within the central trochlear region (ROI: Fig. 1, Left Panel). The superior medial femoral condyle was defined as the most superior point within the medial femoral condyle and (Fig. 1, Right Panel). Cartilage thickness was calculated as the minimum Euclidean distance (i.e., perpendicular distance) between cartilage-bone and cartilage-synovial space interfaces at every pixel across identified borders (Fig. 1). The average thickness across all identified pixels within the trochlear and medial femoral condyle image sites were used for further analysis. Images were de-identified prior to image processing and a separate researcher segmented images but was not blinded to imaging time points. All image segmentations were visually reviewed and verified for accuracy following data processing and prior to analysis. Reliability and precision for the investigator acquiring ultrasound images have been previously reported with ICC's for inter-rater reliability ranged from 0.971 to 0.991, and ICC's for test-retest reliability ranged from 0.874 to 0.976 [20]. Test-retest standard errors of the measure ranged from 0.06 to 0.08 mm and minimal detectable change range from 0.15 to 0.18 mm [20].

Treadmill walking assessment

Treadmill walking assessments included a 15-min walk at their preferred speed on an instrumented split-belt treadmill (Bertec, Columbus OH) synchronized with an 8-camera Vicon Motion Capture System (Vicon, Oxford, UK) while participants were outfitted with a set of retroreflective markers and 8 rigid clusters affixed to the lower limbs and upper body. Markers were placed on anatomical landmarks for model building bilaterally on the anterior superior iliac spine, medial and lateral femoral epicondyles, medial and lateral malleoli, second metatarsal head and posterior superior iliac spines, C7, T12, L5/S1 spinal processes. Dynamic markers only consisted of the rigid cluster markers, second metatarsal markers, and trunk markers. For the prosthetic limb, the centres of the prosthetic knee was designated as the knee joint while the medial and lateral covers of the prosthetic foot were designated as malleolar markers. Preferred walking speeds were determined while participants performed a 1-min familiarization where the treadmill belts were incrementally increased by 0.1 m/s until participants indicated they were comfortable. Participants were instructed that the goal was to reach a speed that they felt best resembled their normal walking speed if they were going for an outdoor walk. When reaching a comfortable speed, the belt speeds were increased by 0.1 m/s to confirm if the previous belt speed selection was preferred. The 15 min of self-selected (SS) walking was then completed following 1-min preferred speed determination.

Marker and analog force data were acquired during the treadmill walk at 250 Hz and 1000 Hz, respectively and digitally filtered via an 8 Hz (marker) 12 Hz (force) low-pass Butterworth filter. Filter cut-offs for marker data were determined via residual analysis while filter cut-offs for force data were confirmed following previous literature. Marker and force data were combined using Motion Monitor where standard inverse dynamics procedures were utilized to calculate joint moments (reported as external). Stance phases were defined as when the vertical GRF exceeded and subsequently fell below 20 N. Force metrics included the 1st and 2nd peak vertical GRF while knee moments included the external knee flexion (KFM) and adduction moments (KAM). For vertical GRF metrics, the peak values were extracted from the first and second half of stance phase, respectively. For knee joint moments, peak KFM and KAM were extracted from the first half of stance phase. Each gait metric was calculated for each stance phase identified during the initial 5 min of the walking exercise and the average was used in further statistical analyses.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were conducted in SPSS (IBM v26.0) and data were visually inspected using box plots for normality. Data were deemed normal and treated as such. For the primary objective, we used Pearson's R correlations to evaluate associations between preferred walking speeds, vertical GRF metrics (1st and 2nd peak GRF, and midstance minimum GRF) and femoral cartilage thickness and strains. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as weak (<0.4), moderate (≥0.4–0.59), strong (≥0.6–0.79) and very strong (≥0.8–1.0) [29]. Significance was evaluated at α ≤ 0.05.

Results

We omitted cartilage images for longitudinal views of the medial femoral cartilage in all analyses for one participant (N = 9) due to poor delineation of cartilage borders. For descriptive purposes, cartilage strains following the walking protocol ranged between -3 and 6% (approx. Δ = −0.10 mm) in trochlear cartilage and distal medial femoral cartilage ROIs (Table 2). Notably, the differences in cartilage strain following exercise (Δ thickness = 0.10 mm) exceeded our previously established SEM's (0.06–0.08 mm) but did not exceed MDC values (0.15–0.18 mm).

Discussion

We aimed to evaluate the associations between loading during gait and sound knee cartilage thickness and strains in those with traumatic LLA. Overall, our findings are partially aligned with our hypotheses. Individuals with LLA who walked faster exhibited thicker medial femoral cartilage and a larger absolute knee flexion moments were moderately associated with thicker medial femoral cartilage in sound knees. These data are promising as they may suggest that more functionally mobile individuals with LLA exhibit thicker sound knee cartilage – which is traditionally considered a feature of healthier tissue. Future work in a larger sample is needed to corroborate findings and to better understand how mechanical loads longitudinally influence OA trajectories in sound knees in individuals with unilateral LLA.

We observed moderate-strong associations between preferred walking speeds and medial femoral cartilage thickness in sound knees wherein those who walked faster exhibited thicker resting cartilage in the medial femoral condyle (Fig. 2). Preferred walking speeds is a global indicator of health and has been consistently associated with knee OA-related outcomes [[30], [31], [32]]. Slower preferred walking speed is predictive of OA worsening and severity in those with established OA [30,31]. In younger individuals following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, slower walking speeds are also linked with earlier worsening of cartilage composition and higher levels of serum biomarkers of cartilage breakdown [13,15,17]. Given that those with LLA often exhibit altered gait mechanics resulting from limb loss, our data may support that more functionally mobile individuals (i.e., those walking faster) exhibit more favorable cartilage measures. While intuitive, cartilage swelling due to matrix-related degradation is a common early-OA feature and is often considered an initial sign of disease progression [33,34]. Given our cross-sectional nature this investigation, our observations of thicker cartilage linked with faster walk speeds may also be interpretated as a “negative” or maladaptive association. Nonetheless, the direction of cartilage structural alterations in sound knees is not well characterized and it is plausible structural OA changes present uniquely in those with LLA. These data underscore the need for longitudinal observations of initial changes in cartilage health following limb loss in conjunction with gait assessment to better understand the time-course of OA development in those with LLA.

When evaluating limb-specific loading metrics, we found greater absolute knee flexion moments were moderately associated with thicker trochlear cartilage, but normalized knee moments were not. Further, moderate positive correlations (r = 0.50) were observed between first and second peak absolute vertical GRFs and trochlear cartilage thickness, though the relationship was not statistically significant (Table 4). No other knee moment or GRF metrics were associated with either resting thickness (Table 4). Articular cartilage is a highly-adapted tissue, though its thickness does not fully scale with height and weight and can be affected by factors such as OA, and obesity. Absolute measures of loading demands are more representative of the actual loads experienced by the tissue and thus, it is not surprising we observed positive associations between these metrics. Previous work have similarly observed that greater absolute joint contact forces are associated with thicker tibial and femoral cartilage in healthy [35,36] and ACLR knees [37]. While not fully reflective of internal joint contact forces, the external knee flexion moment independently contributes to tibiofemoral and patellofemoral joint contact forces, lending support for knee moments as a surrogate loading metric [25]. Further, while normalized loading variables are important to characterize relative loading demands across individuals, interpretation of absolute measures provide complementary insight particularly in the context of OA. For instance, previous work have found that absolute joint moments, such as the KAM, can better discriminate between OA severity [38] than normalized outcomes and are predictive of 3-year cartilage loss [39]. As such, it may be useful to interpret both absolute and normalized loading outcomes in those with LLA particularly in the context of any rehabilitative or biomechanical based intervention.

Contrary to our hypothesis we did not observe any associations between preferred walking speeds, gait outcomes and cartilage strains following our treadmill exercise. While we observed moderate correlations between 1st peak vertical GRF and KAM (normalized and absolute) with trochlear (r = 0.53–0.59) medial femoral cartilage strains (r = −0.50–0.62) correlations were not significant and opposite directions (Table 4). Evaluating acute cartilage strains following exercise provides a functional test of the mechanical integrity of the cartilage matrix [11]. These data are complimentary to structural cartilage measures like thickness as OA-related changes to matrix composition and mechanical function largely precede structural thinning or thickening. Slower walking speeds are associated with greater medial femoral cartilage strain after a 30-min treadmill walk in a young, healthy non-amputated populations [14]. Well slightly counterintuitive given that slow walking is associated with lower peak forces and knee loads, slower walking induces longer stance durations, greater load cycles and larger cumulative loads [14,40] which may lead to higher magnitude of cartilage loading (and strains). Nonetheless, It is important to highlight that while our walking protocol was only 15-min, the magnitude of cartilage strains observed in both trochlear (2–5%) and medial femoral cartilage regions (Table 2: 4–10%) were generally of similar magnitudes to those observed by other groups using longer and/or more intensive walking activities (i.e., incline walking) [[41], [42], [43]]. Interestingly, Okada et al., utilized a similar walk duration (15 min, fixed speed [0.9 m/s]) when comparing cartilage strains between those with early/mild OA and healthy matched controls and found similar magnitudes of cartilage strain as our study (≈0.095 mm avg) [44]. As such, we reason that observations of relatively equivalent strains between our data and with previous cohorts with OA, may highlight poorer cartilage mechanical function in sound knees as early OA-related breakdown results in impaired load-bearing properties and increased strains. Future work may benefit including a matched-control group to aid in contextualizing whether sound knee cartilage strains magnitudes following exercise are larger compared to those without LLA (i.e., representing poorer tissue properties).

Limb overloading has been consistently theorized as the central mechanism provoking cartilage breakdown in sound knees – though others have noted other factors (i.e., immobilization, “unusual” loading) may also contribute [8]. Thus, we expected knee moments and higher GRFs to be linked with thinner femoral cartilage and increased cartilage strains indicating poorer joint health. While knee flexion moments were associated with cartilage thickness, we also observed moderate positive correlation between 1st and 2nd peak GRF, KAM and resting cartilage thickness, which are also important to highlight. For example, peak GRFs between sound and the amputated limb were not different in our cohort and faster walking speeds were also linked with thicker femoral cartilage. Faster walking speeds naturally lead to higher forces placed about the lower limb joints and could be considered as an “overloading” stimulus. However, we are hesitant to conclude that faster preferred speeds (and higher GRFs/joint loading) would have a damaging impact on sound knee joint health, particularly given preferred walk speeds were relatively slow (0.79 m/s) compared to other OA [45,46] or LLA cohorts [8]. Faster walking is generally considered a positive functional-health indicator [47] while a wealth of data suggest that slow walking is a sign of underlying pathology [32], poor health-related function, and is a facilitator for disease progression [30]. Generally, articular cartilage exhibits a propensity to adapt positively to joint loading and exercise, particularly to more dynamic and cyclic types of joint load (i.e., faster walking) [48]. However, this positive relationship between loading and cartilage health is not invariable. Factors such as OA status/severity and inflammation (i.e., obesity) are both independent and potentially synergistic moderators between loading and cartilage adaptation [49,50]. While our data suggest sound knee cartilage may retain positive adaptability to joint loading in those with LLA, these findings may be specific to the status of our cohort. Given the paucity of data linking gait and cartilage outcomes in those with LLA, there is a strong need for studies focused on understand if and/or when the association between joint loading and sound knee health shifts from a positive, adaptive response to a negative, maladaptive response. These data are critical to illuminating windows of opportunity for timely interventions and disease management strategies.

Our study is not without limitations. We present a small sample size which makes it difficult to evaluate confounders such as sex, age, time since amputation, and BMI amongst other factors. Further, we did not directly evaluate or confirm the presence of OA in the sound knee which may limit generalizability of findings given the broad range of participants in our study. However, our data present a novel approach to understanding gait risk factors for OA progression after LLA by pairing low-cost, direct cartilage imaging (ultrasound) with gait biomechanical assessment. Additionally it is important to highlight that although our sample includes a mix of individuals with transtibial and transfemoral amputations, OA rates in sound knees in hips are relatively similar between those with TT and TF limb loss [2] and gait differences between these subgroups are somewhat dependent on the gait measure of interest [51,52]. As mentioned, to date only a single study to our knowledge has provided a similar analysis and utilized radiographs for clinical grading and structural progression [8]. Nonetheless, our data directly linking gait mechanics with cartilage-specific imaging is an initial step to informing the effective design of sound knee OA therapies. We did not observe associations between gait mechanics and measures of cartilage strain, and it is plausible our shorter exercise duration (15 min) may have limited the magnitudes of tissue strain across participants. As noted above, previous work has utilized 30–45 min of acute exercise across varying intensities (level walk, incline walk, running etc.) [10,41], however the strain magnitudes observed in our cohort were generally not different than what has been shown in young non-amputees, anterior cruciate ligament reconstructed individuals or obese populations [[41], [42], [43]] and are nearly identical to strains observed following 15-min walk in those diagnosed with early OA [44]. While the inclusion of a cartilage strain assessment was an exploratory objective of our parent investigation, future work may benefit from longer walk durations or intensities as this may provide a more robust mechanical stimulus to evaluate tissue load-bearing function. Lastly, we note that our data was cross-sectional and the relationship between gait and cartilage health is not linear, can change with OA-status, and may differ across tibiofemoral or patellofemoral joints. As such, these data serve as a first step into informing future observational investigations aimed at uncovering the natural history of OA following limb loss and the associated risk factors influencing these trajectories.

Conclusion

We observed that faster preferred walking speeds are associated with thicker sound knee femoral cartilage after LLA. Rehabilitation interventions or prosthesis design that optimizes mobility recovery after LLA may be beneficial for the maintenance of sound knee health. While these data represent a novel approach of incorporating imaging and gait analysis in those with LLA, future confirmatory work is needed to how mobility and limb loading impacts OA trajectories after LLA.
