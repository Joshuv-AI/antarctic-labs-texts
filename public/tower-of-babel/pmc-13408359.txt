Comparison of Parental Perceptions and Oxygen Saturation Response to Papoose Board and Comfy Wrap Restraints in Pediatric Dental Procedures: A Crossover Study
Authors: Ankita Tapkir, Nilesh Rathi, Vini Mehta, Tripti Lath, Raksha Shetty, Khushi Prasad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundPhysical restraint is necessary in pediatric dentistry to manage an uncooperative child. Although the Papoose Board is widely utilized, it has been implicated in causing psychological and physiological issues. The Comfy Wrap is a new option that aims to provide a more child-centered and comfortable environment. This study compared the two techniques based on parental perception and the physiological stress reaction assessed by oxygen saturation (SpO₂) levels.Materials and methodsA randomized crossover trial was carried out among 40 patients aged 3–7 years needing protective stabilization for dental treatment. They were equally allocated to two groups: group A was given the Papoose Board first and then the Comfy Wrap; group B was given the interventions in reverse order. Parental views were obtained on a structured questionnaire following each intervention. Children's SpO₂ levels were measured before, during, and after every restraint application. Statistical methods included descriptive statistics, repeated measures ANOVA, and Tukey's post hoc tests.ResultsA greater percentage of parents in each group preferred the Comfy Wrap to the Papoose Board (41.1% in group A and 56.25% in group B). Parents rated the Comfy Wrap as less scary and acceptable. SpO₂ values changed significantly with time in group A (p < 0.001), although no significant difference was found between restraint methods (p = 0.074). The Comfy Wrap showed greater physiological stability for group B with a significant intervention effect (p = 0.023).ConclusionAlthough both the Papoose Board and the Comfy Wrap enabled successful pediatric dental treatment, the Comfy Wrap was always more accepted by parents and was linked to improved physiological stability, as indicated by SpO₂.Clinical significanceIn pedodontics, the Comfy Wrap was found to be a more acceptable and physiologically superior restraint technique than the Papoose Board.

Introduction

Treatment of anxiety and uncooperative behavior among pediatric patients remains a significant challenge in clinical dental practice.1,2 Uncooperative children not only hinder the safe provision of care but also pose the risk of psychological trauma and failure of procedures if not effectively managed.3,4 Both pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic behavioral guidance methods promote cooperation, provide safety, and enhance treatment success in such situations.5,6

Protective stabilization is a standard mode among the nonpharmacologic approaches, particularly when urgent treatment is required and pharmacologic alternatives are unavailable or contraindicated.7 The Papoose Board, a passive restraint system, has been used in pediatric dentistry for many years to reduce the risk of injury and allow safe treatment delivery. It is economical and spares the risks of sedation or general anesthesia.8,9 Yet, besides its practicality, the Papoose Board has also drawn significant ethical attention and criticism from parents, some of whom have found it traumatic or oppressive.10

Newer alternatives to conventional physical restraints, including ergonomically shaped devices and swaddled-sheet wraps, have evolved to minimize perceived cruelty and emotional distress with pediatric immobilization. One of them, the Comfy Wrap, provides additional esthetics and functionality to help improve comfort and alleviate anxiety for children and parents alike. Unlike the traditional Papoose Board, which has come under fire for its stiffness and frightening appearance, these newer models emphasize ease of use and emotional comfort with comparable immobilization for procedures. These advances can enhance parents' acceptability and compliance without sacrificing procedure safety or success.11,12 Parental belief is vital to the acceptability and efficacy of behavior guidance methods.

There has been evidence to show variability in parental acceptance based on previous experience, sociocultural contexts, and restraint methods. Frankel reported that certain mothers had positive attitudes toward using the Papoose Board once appropriately explained.13 On the other hand, Malik et al. found that parents viewed physical restraint as an unprecedented action and asked themselves whether it was ethically correct.14 Despite the typical application of physical restraints in dentistry, there is little comparative data on newer methods such as the Comfy Wrap and their physiological and psychological implications. In addition, few trials have utilized a randomized crossover design that permits comparison within subjects and reduces interindividual variability. A randomized crossover trial also enables more accurate determination of parental preference and the child's physiological reactions under both restraint conditions.15 Therefore, the current study sought to compare and assess parents' perceptions and attitudes toward applying the Comfy Wrap and the Papoose Board as pediatric dental behavior guidance methods. The study also investigated oxygen saturation (SpO₂) as a physiological marker for the child's stress response to both restraint devices.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study used a randomized crossover in vivo design and was performed in the Department of Pedodontics and Preventive Dentistry for 12 months. It aimed to compare and assess parental attitudes and perceptions toward using the Papoose Board and the Comfy Wrap as physical restraint devices in pediatric dental treatment.

Ethical Considerations

Before the study began, the Institutional Ethics Committee approved it, Approval Number: DYPDCH/DPU/EC/582/134/2023. Written informed consent was obtained from all parent participants or legal guardians. The study complied with the guidelines in the Declaration of Helsinki for research among human subjects.

Participants and Sample Size

Forty healthy children between 4 and 7 years of age were chosen by random sampling (sealed envelope technique). All subjects had two or more dental treatment sessions involving the use of local anesthesia. Children in the study showed negative behavior (Frankl's behavior rating scale 1 or 2)16 and no history of systemic disease, allergy, asthma, or special medical needs.

This crossover allowed every participant to be exposed to both behavior guidance methods over two visits.

Intervention and Procedure

During the first visit, every child was physically restrained using either a Comfy Wrap or a Papoose Board based on their group allocation. A standardized local anesthetic procedure was administered using 2% lignocaine with adrenaline given through a 27-gauge needle. A pulse oximeter monitored the child's vital parameters, which included pulse rate and SpO₂.

After dental treatment, the parent accompanying the patient was requested to complete a pretested questionnaire measuring their attitude and perception of the physical restraint method. The questionnaire contained Likert-scale items and open questions rating factors such as perceived safety, comfort, acceptability, and future intentions to use the technique.

The second restraint device was applied using the same procedure at the follow-up dental visit (typically within 7–14 days). After finishing the second procedure, the parent once more completed the same survey assessing their impression of the second restraint method.

Data Collection Tool

A pretested, structured questionnaire was employed to measure parental attitudes and perceptions. It consisted of quantitative (5-point Likert scale) and qualitative (open-ended) parts and was prepared following the literature and expertise. The same questionnaire was completed during post-intervention visits for comparative assessment.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted using JAMOVI software (Version 2.6), an open-source statistical analysis environment. Descriptive statistics and frequency analysis were used to compare parental attitudes toward the two restraint techniques, and responses were given as percentages for categorical items. Physiological responses were assessed by measuring SpO₂ at three time points: before, during, and after each intervention. These multiple measures were compared using a repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) to investigate within-subject changes across time and between-subject differences for the two restraint methods (Papoose Board vs Comfy Wrap). In cases where statistically significant effects were found, Tukey's post hoc test was used to identify exact pairwise differences.

Results

Forty children participated in this randomized crossover trial to compare two physical restraint methods, Papoose Board and Comfy Wrap, applied during pediatric dental treatment. The participants were divided equally into two groups of 20 participants each.

Group A had 14 males and six females, and the Papoose Board was administered during the first intervention, followed by the Comfy Wrap during the second intervention. Among these, 17 participants successfully underwent both intervention phases. Group B had 11 males and nine females and was subjected to interventions in the opposite order, receiving the Comfy Wrap initially and the Papoose Board later. There were 16 participants in this group who successfully underwent both sessions.

Both restraint techniques were administered to all participants, facilitating within-subject comparisons of parental perception and physiological response. A few participants in both groups were prevented from completing the second intervention phase by attrition. This crossover design facilitated a strong assessment of the acceptability and physiological effects of the two restraint techniques in a variety of administration sequences.

Overall, parental preference after experiencing both forms of physical restraint was strongly biased toward the Comfy Wrap. In group A, 41.1% of parents preferred the Comfy Wrap, compared with only 17.6% who preferred the Papoose Board. In group B, a larger percentage (56.25%) preferred the Comfy Wrap, whereas the Papoose Board was acceptable to merely 12.5%. Interestingly, nearly a quarter of respondents from both groups (29.5% in group A and 25% in group B) were satisfied with both methods of restraint. Barely a minority were dissatisfied with both. These findings support the idea that although both devices are understood as functionally essential, the Comfy Wrap is better accepted by parents and preferred over the traditional Papoose Board in pediatric dentistry in the clinic.

The effect of the restraint techniques on the physiological level of stress in children was evaluated using SpO₂ as an objective and noninvasive biomarker. The repeated measures ANOVA demonstrated significant changes in relation to time for SpO₂ in group A (p < 0.001), which implies that the two restraint methods caused some physiological stress during the dental procedure. There was no statistically significant difference between the two interventions in group A (p = 0.074), which implies that despite variations in SpO₂, neither intervention had a better effect on stabilizing oxygenation in this group.

In contrast, group B showed no significant time-related fluctuation in SpO₂ values (p = 0.429), indicating overall physiological stability during the procedure. However, the type of restraint had a significant effect on SpO₂ (p = 0.023), with the Comfy Wrap associated with better physiological outcomes. This suggests that the Comfy Wrap may provide a more calming or less physiologically disruptive experience compared to the Papoose Board in specific patient contexts.

These results were also confirmed by post hoc comparisons, as shown in Table 5. Both interventions were found to cause statistically significant decreases in SpO₂ in group A, both at the Papoose Board (p = 0.001) and at the Comfy Wrap (p = 0.004), when compared to baseline, confirming that both techniques are stress-inducing to a certain degree. But there was also a pronounced recovery of SpO₂ following the Papoose intervention (p = 0.044), indicating that although the restraint causes temporary stress, the physiological effect might be transient.

In group B, there was another trend: the Comfy Wrap was linked to a statistically significant rise in SpO₂ during intervention vs baseline (p = 0.005), which suggests a stress-buffering effect of the device. This is consistent with the results of the parental perception, which indicated that the Comfy Wrap was more comfortable and less distressing for their child.

Discussion

This crossover randomized trial compared parental impressions and physiological responses to stress related to two physical restraint devices—the Papoose Board and Comfy Wrap—applied during pediatric dental treatment. The results show that although both methods were recognized as necessary to manage uncooperative children, parents rated the Comfy Wrap more positively, and it was linked with higher physiological stability, reflected in SpO₂ values.

The application of protective stabilization is still a viable choice in pediatric dentistry, especially where pharmacologic behavior management is not indicated or possible.7 Nevertheless, conventional equipment like the Papoose Board has been criticized for being too restrictive and having the capacity to cause distress to children and parents alike. Our results concur with previous reports demonstrating that while parents might approve of the utilitarian purpose of such restraints, they tend to feel uneasy about their use, particularly in situations lacking proper communication and consent.17

Conversely, the Comfy Wrap, being more ergonomically flexible and less visually threatening, was rated higher for comfort, perceived need, and acceptability in both study groups. The findings concur with earlier research indicating that restraint systems with sensory-acclimated or less threatening material are more acceptable to parents and less stressful to children.11,12 These designs could decrease procedural anxiety and enhance cooperation overall during dental treatment.

The crossover design is one of the main advantages of this study because it enabled within-subject comparisons and reduced inter-individual differences. Of interest, when the Papoose Board was first utilized (group A), parents rated the Comfy Wrap more favorably at the second session. However, when the Comfy Wrap was utilized first (group B), the second use of the Papoose Board was more likely to result in negative ratings. These trends imply that the primary introduction to a more acceptable instrument increases critical perception of the discomfort involved with more conventional techniques.

Physiological testing by SpO₂ showed additional contrast between restraint techniques. In group A, both restraint types were correlated with significant declines in SpO₂ levels when in use, reflecting stress-induced physiological response. Recovery of SpO₂ levels after the procedure implied these were temporary effects. Within group B, SpO₂ was fairly consistent throughout and was related to a notable rise in oxygenation during the procedure when the Comfy Wrap was used. These data are commensurate with the possible soothing effect of the Comfy Wrap, possibly attributed to its softer materials and less constrictive style, which could decrease anxiety-driven physiological responses.1,18,19

While SpO₂ provided valuable insights into the physiological stress responses during restraint, it is important to acknowledge that it is a limited marker of systemic arousal. Although SpO₂ may indirectly reflect autonomic nervous system responses under stress, it does not directly quantify anxiety levels. The decision to measure only SpO₂ was driven by the need for a noninvasive, real-time, and child-friendly physiological indicator that would not exacerbate anxiety during dental procedures. More comprehensive markers, such as heart rate, blood pressure, and salivary cortisol, were excluded to avoid additional stress to the child, given the age group and ethical considerations in a clinical dental setting. Future studies should incorporate these additional parameters for a more detailed evaluation of physiological stress.

Parent acceptance is crucial in the ethical justification of using physical restraints. While an overwhelming number of parents in this study recognized the need to apply restraint for safe treatment administration, the Papoose Board was more frequently linked to fear, negative post-treatment experience, and a sense of feeling too restrictive. This concurs with existing research emphasizing that, even when found to be necessary, standard restraint devices can cause emotional disturbance among parents.20,21

Notably, some parents suggested that sedation could have been a better option, particularly after using the Papoose Board. While sedation is still a strong contender for dealing with severe dental anxiety or behavioral complications, it poses extra risks, expenses, and logistical issues.19 Well-designed nonpharmacological options such as the Comfy Wrap provide a convenient, safe, and parent-accredited solution in this regard. This was reinforced by a Cochrane review conducted by Anthonappa et al., which indicated that nonpharmacological management was as effective as sedation for the control of mild to moderate dental anxiety in children.22

Effective communication between the dental team and caregivers significantly influences parental acceptance of behavior guidance techniques. Prior research has shown that providing clear explanations enhances understanding and improves acceptance of restraint methods.23,24 In our study, although all parents were exposed to both techniques, their final preferences were shaped not only by their child's behavior but also by their emotional responses, highlighting the importance of empathy and shared decision-making in pediatric dental care.

Ethically, the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) guidelines have highlighted that protective stabilization should be used only when less limiting options fail or are not appropriate and always with consent from informed parents.25 Our data indicate that the Comfy Wrap, being superior in emotional and physiological tolerance, might fare better against these standards and be a more ethically appropriate method in the right clinical conditions.

Few studies have contrasted various restraint techniques with objective physiological markers. Ismail et al., in a recent crossover trial, did not demonstrate any significant difference in SpO₂ between the Papoose Board and other methods of behavior guidance in children with special healthcare needs.15 Our study, however, demonstrates that the Comfy Wrap can positively affect physiological stability, particularly when employed as the first intervention. This might indicate how early procedural experience modulates stress.

Brill's observational study of 100 parents also reported high parental satisfaction with passive restraints, although it did not distinguish between types of restraint.26 These findings add further depth, illustrating that design and comfort play essential roles in determining parental acceptance and child reaction within the general class of protective stabilization.

These findings are reinforced by psychosocial literature that associates early traumatic dental experiences with long-term behavioral outcomes. It has been demonstrated that aversive early dental experiences can lead to heightened anxiety, avoidance of treatment, and poorer oral health in later life.27,28 Minimization of perceived trauma through the application of less distressing restraint techniques may thus have long-term benefits for the oral health of children.29,30

Although strong, this study has some limitations. It was performed at a single site with a modest sample size, and SpO₂ was administered as the only physiological marker. Future studies should include multicenter, large samples and objective measures like heart rate variability, salivary cortisol, and coded behavioral systems. Measuring clinician and parental opinions may also provide a more complete picture of restraint utility and acceptability.

A key limitation of this study is the exclusive use of SpO₂ as a physiological marker. Other established indicators of stress, such as heart rate, blood pressure, heart rate variability, or salivary biomarkers, were not measured due to practical constraints and to avoid increasing procedural anxiety among young participants. Consequently, while SpO₂ changes provided supportive evidence of physiological trends, they should not be interpreted as standalone measures of anxiety or systemic stress. Future studies should consider using multimodal physiological and psychological stress markers for more robust data.

Conclusion

This crossover study discovered that although both the Papoose Board and the Comfy Wrap enabled successful pediatric dental treatment, the Comfy Wrap was consistently more accepted by parents and was associated with improved physiological stability as reflected in SpO₂ values. However, as SpO₂ was the only physiological marker assessed, the findings must be interpreted with caution. Future studies incorporating additional physiological and behavioral measures are warranted to validate and expand upon these results.

Clinical Significance

The results of the study justify using more parent- and child-centered restraint techniques in clinical practice, with additional research needed to confirm these results in larger, more diverse groups using other physiological and behavioral stress markers.
