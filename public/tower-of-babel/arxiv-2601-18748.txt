Sampling Sphere Packings with Continuum Glauber Dynamics
Aiya Kuchukova, Santosh Vempala, Daniel Zhang

Abstract
We establish a spectral gap for Continuum Glauber dynamics on the hard sphere model assuming strong spatial mixing, thereby extending the range of parameters in which Continuum Glauber is provably rapidly mixing. To do this, we introduce continuous extensions of spectral independence and negative fields localization. Our techniques apply to general Gibbs point processes with finite-range repulsive pair potentials. As a corollary, we improve the threshold up to which packings of a fixed number of spheres can be sampled from a bounded domain.

Introduction

Sphere packing is a classical problem of great interest to mathematicians, physicists and computer scientists. Kepler’s conjecture posed in 1611 led to a body of work including upper and lower bounds on the optimal density of sphere packings in any dimension leading to breakthroughs by Hales
[
Hal05
]
on optimal packings in dimension 3, Viazovska
[
Via17
]
in dimension 8 and by Cohn, Kumar, Miller, Radchenko, and Viazovska in dimension 24
[
CKM+17
]
. Recent advances by
Campos, Jenssen, Michelen, Sahasrabudhe
[
CJMS23
]
and Klartag
[
Kla25
]
provide general lower bounds in high dimension.

A closely related problem, motivated in part by statistical physics, is efficiently
sampling
sphere packings of high density. Sampling is a rich direction of theoretical computer science on its own, and in the case of sphere packings, it also has connections to statistical physics phenomena such as phase transitions and correlation decay.
In fact, the classical Metropolis algorithm
[
MRR+53
]
was originally introduced to address the question of sampling from the hard sphere model.

Sampling Sphere Packings.

There are two closely related but distinct versions of the hard sphere model. In one, called the “canonical ensemble”,
the number of spheres is fixed and the probability density is uniform over all sphere packings with the prescribed number of spheres. In the other, called the “grand canonical ensemble”,
the weight of each sphere packing is proportional to the activity parameter
\lambda
raised to the power of the number of spheres in the packing. That is, the weight of configuration
S
is proportional to
\lambda^{|S|}
(for a formal definition, see
Definition

1.7
).

In the “canonical ensemble”, as the number of spheres increases, sampling generally becomes harder. In the “grand canonical ensemble”, as
\lambda
increases, configurations with more spheres become more likely and sampling generally becomes harder.
The problem of sampling sphere packings in both models has been addressed by various dynamics.

For the canonical ensemble, Kannan, Mahoney and Montenegro
[
KMM03
]
showed that a simple Markov chain that moves a sphere to an available place (Kawasaki-type dynamics)
converges rapidly to the uniform distribution over fixed-size sphere packings provided the density satisfies
\rho\leq(1-\delta)/2^{d+1}
for some
\delta>0
(here we assume that spheres have volume 1). The work of Boudou, Caputo, Pra, and Posta
[
DPP13
]
gives a more general result on spectral gap for repulsive particle interactions (which we will discuss later), but their result for Kawasaki-type dynamics requires slightly smaller density
\rho\leq\frac{1-\delta}{3\cdot 2^{d+1}}
.

In the grand canonical setting, Michelen and Perkins
[
MP21
,
MP22a
,
MP22b
]

showed that “block dynamics” on
\Lambda_{n}=[-n,n]^{d}
mixes in
O(N\log(N/\varepsilon))
steps where
N
is the volume of the box domain. This result gives the widest known range
of activity that allows efficient sampling. We discuss related work in more detail in
Section

1.4
.

The hard sphere model is a special case of a Gibbs point processes with finite-range repulsive pair potentials (a point does not forbid points nearby, but instead makes them less likely), see
Definition

1.8
. The main results of this paper apply to this general model with explicit corollaries stated for the hard spheres model.

Continuum Glauber Dynamics and Gibbs point processes.

In this paper, we analyze Continuum Glauber dynamics, a natural Markov process for sphere packings and repulsive point processes, defined by
[
BCC02
]
. It is an important special case of birth-and-death processes that were first defined by Preston
[
Pre75
]
.
These processes
are used in the context of biological, physical and chemical processes
[
FS02
]
.

The definition of the process (
Definition

\lambda
per unit volume and disappearing at a rate of 1 (i.e., each sphere lasts for
\Exp(1)
time).
Kondratiev and Lytvynov
[
KL03
]
showed that when
\lambda\leq(1-\delta)/C_{\varphi}
(see
Definition

1.11
for the “temperedness” constant), Continuum Glauber dynamics has a spectral gap of at least
\delta
, for an infinite domain. In this paper, we will focus on bounded domains, a more natural setting for sampling.
Starting from the empty configuration, a standard argument (
[
BGL14
]
Theorem 4.2.5) shows that a spectral gap implies convergence in
\chi^{2}
-distance.
The result of
[
KL03
]
implies
O_{d}(|\Lambda|)
mixing of Continuum Glauber for activity
\lambda<1/C_{\varphi}
from the empty configuration.

The usual definition of mixing time is a uniform time bound over all possible initial states to reach a certain distance of the stationary distribution. For general Gibbs point processes with finite-range repulsive pair potentials, the number of initial points could be arbitrarily large, and could take an arbitrarily large amount of time to die out, precluding a uniform mixing time bound. We address this problem by using a burn-in argument and obtain mixing results starting from any state that depend logarithmically on the number of initial points (see
Section

5
for details).

For implementation purposes, we give a discrete-time algorithm (

1.1
Main results

Our main result is a constant lower bound for the spectral gap of Continuum Glauber under Strong Spatial Mixing. The proof uses extensions of Spectral Independence and Negative Fields Localization to continuous state spaces, which we develop here. We will provide a detailed technical overview after stating the results.

Theorem 1.1

(Spectral Gap of CG)
.

For
\lambda<\frac{e}{\Delta_{\varphi}}
, the spectral gap of Continuum Glauber for any Gibbs point process with finite-range repulsive pair potentials is lower bounded by a positive constant independent of
|\Lambda|
.

Here
\Delta_{\varphi}
is the potential-weighted connective constant introduced in
[
MP21
]
, which satisfies
\Delta_{\varphi}\leq C_{\varphi}
. This implies improvement in the range of activity
\lambda
for which we can prove a spectral gap lower bound.
See Section
4.2
for more details. For estimates on
\Delta_{\varphi}
, we refer the reader to the discussion in
[
MP21
]
.

We provide explicit dependence on the dimension and other constants in our analysis for
\lambda<\frac{e}{C_{\varphi}}
.
Note that
C_{\varphi}/\Delta_{\varphi}\to 1
in the
d\to\infty
limit.
and
C_{\varphi}
also has a natural interpretation in hard sphere model:
C_{\varphi}=2^{d}\vol(B_{d}(0,r))
, and with

\Delta_{\varphi}\leq C_{\varphi}=2^{d}

Theorem 1.2

(Spectral Gap of CG for Hard Spheres)
.

For the hard sphere model with spheres of unit volume, when
\lambda<e^{-\delta}\dfrac{e}{2^{d}}
,
Continuum Glauber dynamics has spectral gap at least
e^{-\exp(O(d\log\frac{d}{\delta}))}

The spectral gap lower bound allows us to bound the mixing time of Continuum Glauber.

Theorem 1.3

(Mixing)
.

For any finite starting configuration

\displaystyle O_{d,\delta}\left(|\Lambda|+\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)+\log(|S|+1)\right).

•

Due to the generality of our
Theorem

, any improvement on the regime of Strong Spatial Mixing would lead to widening the activity range for which our mixing result holds. Strong Spatial Mixing of Gibbs point processes with finite-range repulsive pair potentials was established by Michelen and Perkins
[

]
for

We prove a lower bound for the spectral gap and show rapid mixing of Continuum Glauber for finite-range repulsive pair potentials for activity
\lambda<\frac{e}{\Delta_{\varphi}}
, and thus improve upon the range of activity known
for rapid mixing of dynamics analyzed by
[

[

Although this does not improve the known range of activity for efficient sampling, our results are for Continuum Glauber, a simpler and more natural process of independent interest.

To handle this, in

In summary, the main contributions of this paper are three-fold:

1.

We extend the notions of Spectral Independence and Negative Fields Localization to the continuous setting and develop their basic properties.

2.

We prove that Continuuum Glauber mixes rapidly and provides an efficient sampling algorithm for Gibbs point processes with finite-range repulsive pair potentials with activity all the way up to Strong Spatial Mixing.

3.

We improve the density up to which the Canonical Model can be efficiently sampled.

We discuss these and related aspects in more detail in the technical overview.

Preliminaries

Here we introduce basic definitions and notation needed for the technical overview in the next section.

Throughout the paper we will fix
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be a bounded measurable set and
\Omega\subseteq 2^{\Lambda}
to be the collection of all finite subsets of
\Lambda
. For hard spheres this is a collection of sets of sphere centers.
|\Lambda|
denotes the volume of
\Lambda
. We use the notational convention that
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
while
\bm{\lambda}:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

, we define

. For

Definition 1.6

(Poisson point process)
.

The Poisson point process of intensity
\bm{\lambda}:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}
on
\Omega

\eta\sim\rho_{\bm{\lambda}}

Definition 1.7

(Hard Sphere Model)
.

The hard sphere model on a bounded measurable set
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
at activity
\lambda\geq 0
is defined by conditioning a Poisson point process on
\Lambda
of intensity
\lambda

We can also think of it in terms of its partition function

\displaystyle Z_{\Lambda}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k\geq 0}\dfrac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\int_{\Lambda^{k}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}1_{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\geq 2r}dx_{1}\ldots dx_{k}

In this paper, we analyze more general Gibbs point processes with finite-range repulsive pair potentials.

(Gibbs point process)

\displaystyle H(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\varphi(x_{i},x_{j}).

Its

is defined by

\displaystyle\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}(A)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k\geq 0}\dfrac{1}{k!}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k}}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\in A}\dfrac{\bm{\lambda}(x_{1})\cdots\bm{\lambda}(x_{k})}{Z(\bm{\lambda})}e^{-H(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})}dx_{1}\cdots dx_{k}

.

A pair potential

Remark 1.10

The hard sphere model on

The last definition we need is of the spectral gap. The lower bound on the spectral gap for Continuum Glauber is one of the main contributions of this paper.

Definition 1.18

(Spectral Gap)
.

The spectral gap for continuum Glauber on
\mu
is the largest
\delta
for which

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f,f)\geq\delta\Var_{\mu}(f)

for all bounded measurable
f
with
\Var_{\mu}(f)<\infty
,
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f,g):=\int\sum_{x\in\eta}(f(\eta\setminus\{x\})-f(\eta))(g(\eta\setminus\{x\})-g(\eta))d\mu(\eta)

is the Dirichlet form for continuum Glauber and

\displaystyle\Var_{\mu}(f):=\int f^{2}d\mu-\left(\int fd\mu\right)^{2}

is the variance.

1.3
Technical overview

The goal of this paper is to prove a bound on the spectral gap of Continuum Glauber dynamics and thereby derive rapid mixing. To do this, we start with a known bound on the spectral gap for a lower value of activity
\lambda

[
KL03
]
and run a
localization scheme
, a general approach introduced by Chen and Eldan
[
CE22
]
. This transforms the distribution at high activity to a random distribution at low activity, and can be used to convert a spectral gap at low activity to one at high activity. Our goal is to show that this transformation approximately preserves
variance.
To do this, we use Spectral Independence (SI), a tool that has proven to be very effective in discrete settings. We show that Strong Spatial Mixing implies SI, and use SI to show approximate variance conservation. We extend the definitions of both SI and the localization scheme we use to continuous domains and establish basic properties. We explain the structure of the proof in more detail below. Below we provide necessary context for main concepts used in the paper
and give the overview of the proof.

The discrete setting.

The discrete analog of the hard sphere model is the
hard core
model, in which every independent set on a given graph has weight proportional to
\lambda
to the power of its size.
A key tool used to analyze hard core and other discrete models in recent work is
Spectral Independence
(SI)
[
ALG21
,
CLV23
,
CE22
,
AL20
,
FGYZ22
]
.
SI is a property of a probability distribution, which quantifies influence of one particle on the others “on average” (we are using this term very loosely).
We recall a definition of the influence matrix for the hard core model on a graph.

Definition 1.19

(Influence Matrix)
.

Let
\mu
be the distribution of hard core model over a graph with
n
vertices, and
S
be an independent set randomly sampled from
\mu
. Then, the influence matrix of
\mu
is

M(i,j)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}[j\in S~|~i\in S]-\mathbb{P}[j\in S~|~i\not\in S]\quad\mbox{ for }i\neq j\\
0\quad\mbox{ for }i=j.\end{cases}

Definition 1.20

(Spectral independence (discrete))
.

A distribution
\mu
is said to be
\kappa
-spectrally independent if its influence matrix
M
(under any pinning) satisfies
\rho(M)\leq 1+\kappa
.

SI has been used to prove a variety of results, many of which are optimal or best known in the parameter of the model (e.g. activity in hard core model).

[
ALG21
]
gave polynomial-time approximate sampling and counting algorithms for the hard core model on graphs with maximum degree
\Delta
with activity up to the tree-uniqueness threshold
\lambda_{c}(\Delta)
(which is best possible, since there is no sampling algorithm above the threshold unless
NP=RP
as established by a series of works
[
Sly10
,
SS12
,
GGŠ+11
]
).
The work of Chen, Liu, and Vigoda improves on the mixing time by simplifying the definition of spectral independence and generalizing its application to all 2-spin models
[
CLV23
]
.
Spectral independence is also one of the main ingredients in results for sampling colorings
[
CGŠV
]
, spin glasses
[
AJK+
]
, sampling hard core model at the uniqueness threshold
[
CCYZ25
]
, sampling antiferromagnetic two-spin systems with no maximum degree assumption
[
CCYZ25
]
and more. It is also used in “canonical” sampling, for example, sampling independent sets with fixed size
[
JMPV23
]
, and sampling from ferromagnetic Ising model with fixed magnetization
[
KPPY24
]
.

Spectral Independence in Continuous Space.

To the best of our knowledge, SI has so far only been defined and used in the setting of discrete space.
In this paper, we provide rigorous extensions of the definitions and analysis techniques of spectral independence to continuous space. We hope that these provide a useful framework for proving rapid mixing results in continuous settings.
To define spectral independence, we first need to introduce a generalization of the influence matrix.

Definition 1.21

(Influence Operator)
.

For a point process
\mu
, define its influence operator to be

\displaystyle\Psi f(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mathcal{R}_{+x}\mu}[\eta(f)]-\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu}[\eta(f)]

\displaystyle f:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R},x\in\Lambda

where
\eta(f):=\int fd\eta=\sum_{x\in\eta}f(x)
.

Definition 1.22

(Spectral independence (continuous))
.

A distribution
\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}
is said to be
C
-spectrally independent if its influence operator (under any decrease of
\bm{\lambda}
) satisfies
\rho(\Psi)\leq C
.

Strong Spatial Mixing.

SSM is a property of a probability distribution, which quantifies the correlation decay.
Intuitively, it says that the effect of the presence of a point on the distribution of distant points decays exponentially in the distance.

Mixing and Runtime.

The mixing time is bounded using the spectral gap by showing that the variance decays exponentially. This only works if the initial variance (of the relative density of the initial distribution with respect to the stationary distribution) is finite. This holds for the empty configuration, but not for any other fixed configuration, as the point mass on a nonempty configuration is not absolutely continuous with regard to the stationary distribution.
For the purposes of a sampling algorithm, we can just start with the empty configuration, but we believe bounding the mixing time for arbitrary starting configurations is of independent interest.

To deal with an arbitrary initial configuration
S
, we give a “burn-in” argument that shows that after
\Theta(\log|S|)
time, the distribution will be close to one whose relative density with respect to the stationary distribution is bounded.
The intuitive idea is: once the initial particles die out, no configuration will be too likely compared to the stationary distribution. Each initial particle will live for
\Exp(1)
time, so after waiting
\Theta(\log|S|)
time, the initial points will likely have all died.
However, during this time some particles may be born, and thus to show that the relative density is bounded we have to more carefully track how the distribution evolves over time. Note that we cannot hope for a mixing time bound that is independent of
S
, as we cannot be close to the stationary distribution (in TV distance) until it is likely that all the initial points have died, which takes roughly
\log|S|
time.

We can couple a run of the algorithm with a sample path of continuum Glauber to translate mixing of continuum Glauber after
T
time to a sampling guarantee of the algorithm for the same
T
.
To bound the runtime of the algorithm, we observe that the number of attempted births is distributed according to
\Pois(\lambda|\Lambda|T)
, and the number of deaths is bounded by the number of births.

Sampling from the canonical model.

To sample from the canonical model, i.e. the Gibbs point process conditioned on having exactly
k
points, we use rejection sampling, similar to the discrete setting
[
DP23
]
.
For
k
smaller than the expected number of points in a sample from the (grand canonical) Gibbs point process of activity
\bm{\lambda}
, we can show there exists a scaling
t_{k}\in[0,1]
for which the Gibbs point process with activity
t_{k}\bm{\lambda}
has probability
\Omega(\frac{1}{\bm{\lambda}(\Lambda)})
of getting exactly
k
points. (The proof of this uses the mixing of continuum Glauber.) We can then sample from this distribution repeatedly until we get a sample with exactly
k
points. Since we do not know a simple way to compute
t_{k}
, we instead sample from the Gibbs point process with activity
t\bm{\lambda}
for different values of
t
until we get a sample of exactly
k
points. This yields a polynomial-time (approximate) sampling algorithm for small enough
k
. The range of
k
for which this works depends on bounds on the expected size of a sample from the grand canonical model. In the case of the hard sphere model in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this approach provably works for

.
This improves on the previous best bound by nearly a factor of
2
(
k\leq|\Lambda|/2^{d+1}
from
[
KMM03
]
).

Technical challenges.

To conclude this overview, we remark that substantial parts of our analysis are included to ensure that our proofs are rigorous and complete. In many cases,
we try to provide general properties and lemmas in the hope that they will be useful in the future. A number of technical issues arise when going to the continuous setting. For example, the definition of spectral independence involves pinnings (conditioning that a set of points is included); however, including a specific point in space is a zero-probability event and this needs care.
The influence matrix is now an operator, and in order to bound its Rayleigh quotient by its
\ell_{\infty}
-operator norm, we need to show that the influence operator is self-adjoint (which is straightforward in the discrete case). We also have to extend the negative-fields localization scheme introduced by Chen and Eldan
[
CE22
]
to the continuous setting, which again needs extra care for events of probability zero.

Proof.

Let

. Fix

and let
\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}=\bm{\lambda}\cdot\exp(-\varphi(x,\cdot))
.
Then,
\supp(\bm{\lambda}-\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})\subseteq\overline{B(x,r)}
.

Let
A\subseteq\Lambda
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a partition of
A
into a finite number of pieces with diameter at most
\varepsilon
and
\lambda|B|<\varepsilon
for all
B\in\mathcal{P}
.

From the definition of Strong Spatial Mixing and supremum characterization of total variation distance, we have

\displaystyle|\Pr_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}(\eta(B)\geq 1)-\Pr_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}}(\eta(B)\geq 1)|\leq\|\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}-\mu_{\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}}\|_{B}\leq\alpha|B|e^{-\beta\cdot\dist(B,\supp(\bm{\lambda}-\bm{\lambda^{\prime}}))}

We next show that we can approximate
\Pr_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}(\eta(B)\geq 1)
with
\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\eta(B)]
.

Since
\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}
is stochastically dominated by a Poisson point process of intensity
\lambda
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\eta(B)-1_{\eta(B)\geq 1}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Pois(\lambda|B|)}[X-1_{X\geq 1}]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda|B|-(1-e^{-\lambda|B|})

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda|B|}-(1-\lambda|B|)\leq\dfrac{1}{2}(\lambda|B|)^{2}.

(1)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}}[\eta(B)-1_{\eta(B)\geq 1}]\leq\frac{1}{2}(\lambda|B|)^{2}

Now,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}}[\eta(A)]-\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\eta(A)]|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{B\in\mathcal{P}}|\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}}[\eta(B)]-\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\eta(B)]|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{B\in\mathcal{P}}\Big(|\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}}[1_{\eta(B)\geq 1}]-\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[1_{\eta(B)\geq 1}]|

\displaystyle~~+\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}}[\eta(B)-1_{\eta(B)\geq 1}]+\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\eta(B)-1_{\eta(B)\geq 1}]\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{B\in\mathcal{P}}\alpha|B|e^{-\beta\cdot\dist(B,B(x,r))}+\sum_{B\in\mathcal{P}}(\lambda|B|)^{2}

(2)

where the last inequality follows by (
1
).

Let us upper bound each of the two terms separately:

\displaystyle\sum_{B\in\mathcal{P}}\alpha|B|e^{-\beta\cdot\dist(B,B(x,r))}

\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{B\in\mathcal{P}}\int_{B}e^{-\beta\cdot\dist(B,B(x,r))}dy

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\sum_{B\in\mathcal{P}}\int_{B}e^{-\beta\cdot\max(0,\dist(x,y)-r-\varepsilon)}dy

\displaystyle=\alpha\int_{A}e^{-\beta\cdot\max(0,\dist(x,y)-r-\varepsilon)}dy

\displaystyle\leq\alpha e^{\beta\varepsilon}\int_{A}e^{-\beta\cdot\max(0,\dist(x,y)-r)}dy

(3)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{B\in\mathcal{P}}(\lambda|B|)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{B\in\mathcal{P}}\lambda|B|\varepsilon=\varepsilon\lambda|A|.

(4)

Combining
2

3

4
we get:

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}}[\eta(A)]-\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\eta(A)]|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha e^{\beta\varepsilon}\int_{A}e^{-\beta\cdot\max(0,\dist(x,y)-r)}dy+\varepsilon\lambda|A|.

Taking
\varepsilon\to 0
, this yields

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}}[\eta(A)]-\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\eta(A)]|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\int_{A}e^{-\beta\cdot\max(0,\dist(x,y)-r)}dy

By first proving for
f=\bm{1}_{A}
, and then using linearity and taking limits,
we get that for all bounded measurable
f:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}}[\eta(f)]-\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\eta(f)]|\leq\alpha\int_{\Lambda}|f(y)|e^{-\beta\cdot\max(0,\dist(x,y)-r)}dy.

Splitting into positive and negative parts then shows that this in fact holds for all bounded measurable
f:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}
.

Recall that

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}}f(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mathcal{R}_{+x}\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\eta(f)]-\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\eta(f)]

\displaystyle=f(x)+\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}}[\eta(f)]-\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\eta(f)]

Thus, for all bounded measurable

,

\displaystyle|(\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}}-\mathrm{Id})f(x)|\leq\alpha\int_{\Lambda}|f(y)|e^{-\beta\cdot\max(0,\dist(x,y)-r)}dy.

In particular,

\displaystyle\|(\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}}-\mathrm{Id})f\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha\int_{\Lambda}e^{-\beta\cdot\max(0,\dist(x,y)-r)}dy\|f\|_{\infty},

so

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\infty\to\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}:=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\beta\cdot\max(0,\dist(x,y)-r)}dy.

∎

3.1.1
Basic properties

To avoid technical difficulties, we define the following operation of “adding” a set, which is slightly different from a pinning.

Definition 3.1

(Addition)
.

Let
\nu
be a measure on
\Omega

\displaystyle f_{A}(\eta)=\eta\cup A

\displaystyle\nu^{+A}(\mathcal{B}):=\nu(f_{A}^{-1}(\mathcal{B})).

Observe that sampling from

is the same as sampling from

A

For a simple example, consider the discrete space
\Lambda=\{1,2,3\}

, and let

. Then, since only

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\left(\dfrac{1}{Z_{\Lambda}(\bm{\lambda}^{+x_{0}})}\sum_{k\geq 0}\dfrac{1}{k!}\int_{\Lambda^{k+1}}\bm{\lambda}^{+x_{k}}(x_{1})\cdots\bm{\lambda}^{+x_{k}}(x_{k})\varphi(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})e^{-H(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})}dx_{1}\cdots dx_{k}\right)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~\bm{\lambda}(x_{k+1})\dfrac{Z_{\Lambda}(\bm{\lambda}^{+x_{k+1}})}{Z_{\Lambda}(\bm{\lambda})}f(x_{k+1})dx_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}f(x)\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mathcal{R}_{+x}\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\varphi(\eta)]d\iota(x)

Now, for general
A
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda}f(x)\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mathcal{R}_{+x}\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}}[\varphi(\eta)]d\iota_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}f(x)\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mathcal{R}_{+x}\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}}[\varphi(\eta)]d\iota_{\bm{\lambda}}(x)+\sum_{x\in A}f(x)\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mathcal{R}_{+x}\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}}[\varphi(\eta)]

by
Lemma

3.6

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}f(x)\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mathcal{I}_{A}\mathcal{R}_{+x}\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\varphi(\eta)]d\iota_{\bm{\lambda}}(x)+\sum_{x\in A}f(x)\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}}[\varphi(\eta)]

by
Lemma

3.4

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}f(x)\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mathcal{R}_{+x}\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\varphi(\eta\cup A)]d\iota_{\bm{\lambda}}(x)+\sum_{x\in A}f(x)\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\varphi(\eta\cup A)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\eta(f)\varphi(\eta\cup A)]+\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[\sum_{x\in A}f(x)\varphi(\eta\cup A)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}[(\eta\cup A)(f)\varphi(\eta\cup A)]

3.1.2
Linear Approximation of Negative Tilt

In this section, we show a linear approximation for negative tilts (
Lemma

3.13
) and bound the effect of tilting on intensity (
Lemma

3.24
). The following is the main lemma we will prove in this section.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\varphi:\Omega\to[-1,1]
and
h\to 0
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}_{-h}\nu^{+A}}[\varphi]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{+A}}[\varphi]-h\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{+A}}[(|\eta|-\iota^{+A}(\Lambda))\varphi]+O(h^{2})

Note that we can apply this to any bounded
\varphi
by scaling.

First, we will recall the basic facts about stochastic domination and Poisson distribution.

Fact 3.14
.

If
\nu\preceq\rho_{\lambda}
, then for increasing
f:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f(|\nu|)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Pois(\lambda|\Lambda|)}[f(X)]

Fact 3.15
.

Let
X\sim\Pois(\lambda)
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]

\displaystyle=\lambda

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}+\lambda

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]

\displaystyle=\exp(\lambda(e^{t}-1))

We show some lemmas that will be useful for bounding error terms later. In this arguments we use the fact that
\lambda
and
|\Lambda|
are fixed, and thus we treat them as constants for
O(h)
.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
\nu\preceq\rho_{\lambda}
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|e^{-h|\xi|}-(1-h|\xi|)|d\nu(\xi)=O(h^{2})

In particular,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}e^{-h|\xi|}d\nu(\xi)=1-h\iota(\Lambda)+O(h^{2})

Proof.

By monotonicity of
e^{-x}-(1-x)
for
x\geq 0
and stochastic domination,

\displaystyle\int|e^{-h|\xi|}-(1-h|\xi|)|d\nu(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int e^{-h|\xi|}-(1-h|\xi|)d\nu(\xi)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Pois(\lambda|\Lambda|)}[e^{-hX}-(1-hX)]

\displaystyle=\exp(\lambda|\Lambda|(e^{-h}-1))-(1-\lambda|\Lambda|h)

\displaystyle=\exp(\lambda|\Lambda|(-h+O(h^{2})))-(1-\lambda|\Lambda|h)

\displaystyle=1-\lambda|\Lambda|h+O(h^{2})-(1-\lambda|\Lambda|h)

\displaystyle=O(h^{2})

∎

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
\nu\preceq\rho_{\lambda}
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(e^{-h|\xi|}-1)^{2}d\nu(\xi)=O(h^{2})

Proof.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(e^{-h|\xi|}-1)^{2}d\nu(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(1-e^{-h|\xi|})^{2}d\nu(\xi)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Pois(\lambda|\Lambda|)}[(1-e^{-hX})^{2}]

by monotonicity of
(1-e^{-x})^{2}
and
3.14

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\Pois(\lambda|\Lambda|)}[1-2e^{-hX}+e^{-2hX}]

\displaystyle=1-2\exp(\lambda|\Lambda|(e^{-h}-1))+\exp(\lambda|\Lambda|(e^{-2h}-1))

\displaystyle=1-2\exp(-h\lambda|\Lambda|+O(h^{2}))+\exp(-2h\lambda|\Lambda|+O(h^{2}))

\displaystyle=1-2(1-h\lambda|\Lambda|+O(h^{2}))+(1-2h\lambda|\Lambda|+O(h^{2}))

\displaystyle=O(h^{2})

∎

Recall that
\mathcal{T}_{-h}\nu:=g_{h}\nu
where
g_{h}(\eta)=\dfrac{e^{-h|\eta|}}{\int e^{-h|\xi|}d\nu(\xi)}
. We can now show the linear approximation of negative tilt (without addition).

Lemma 3.18
.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|g_{h}(\eta)-(1-h(|\eta|-\iota(\Lambda)))|d\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle=O(h^{2})

Proof.

Using
Lemma

3.16

\displaystyle g_{h}(\eta)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{e^{-h|\eta|}}{\int e^{-h|\xi|}d\nu(\xi)}=\dfrac{e^{-h|\eta|}}{1-h\iota(\Lambda)+O(h^{2})}=(1+h\iota(\Lambda)+O(h^{2}))e^{-h|\eta|}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|g_{h}(\eta)-(1-h(|\eta|-\iota(\Lambda)))|d\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|(1+h\iota(\Lambda)+O(h^{2}))e^{-h(|\eta|)}-(1-h(|\eta|-\iota(\Lambda)))|d\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle\leq O(h^{2})\cdot\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}e^{-h|\eta|}d\nu(\eta)}_{\leq 1}+\int\left|(1+h\iota(\Lambda))(e^{-h|\eta|}-1)+h|\eta|\right|d\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\int|(1+h\iota(\Lambda))(e^{-h|\eta|}-1+h|\eta|)|d\nu(\eta)+O(h^{2})+h^{2}\iota(\Lambda)^{2}

\displaystyle=(1+h\iota(\Lambda))\int|e^{-h|\eta|}-(1-h|\eta|)|d\nu(\eta)+O(h^{2})

\displaystyle=O(h^{2})

∎

We also need to show some useful bounds on second-order terms.

Lemma 3.19
.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(g_{h}(\eta)-1)^{2}d\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle=O(h^{2})

Proof.

As above

\displaystyle g_{h}(\eta)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{e^{-h|\eta|}}{\int e^{-h|\xi|d\nu(\xi)}}=(1+O(h))e^{-h|\eta|}.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(g_{h}(\eta)-1)^{2}d\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}((1+O(h))e^{-h|\eta|}-1)^{2}d\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(e^{-h|\eta|}-1)^{2}d\nu(\eta)+\int_{\Omega}(e^{-h|\eta|}-1)(O(h)e^{-h|\eta|})d\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle~~~~+\int_{\Omega}(O(h)e^{-h|\eta|})(O(h)e^{-h|\eta|})d\nu(\eta)

(since
e^{-h|\eta|}\leq 1
)

\displaystyle\leq O(h^{2})+\int_{\Omega}|e^{-h|\eta|}-1|d\nu(\eta)\cdot O(h)+O(h^{2})

\displaystyle=O(h^{2})

∎

Now we are ready to prove the linear approximation for tilts with addition.

Lemma 3.20
.

We have
\mathcal{T}_{-h}\nu^{+A}=g_{h}^{+A}\nu^{+A}
where

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|g_{h}^{+A}(\eta)-(1-h(|\eta|-\iota^{+A}(\Lambda)))|d\nu^{+A}(\eta)

\displaystyle=O(h^{2})

Proof.

Recall that

\displaystyle g_{h}^{+A}(\eta)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{e^{-h|\eta|}}{\int e^{-h|\xi|}d\nu^{+A}(\xi)}

For
\eta
disjoint from
A
(which holds for almost all
\eta
from
\nu
by
Lemma

3.4
),

\displaystyle g_{h}^{+A}(\eta\cup A)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{-h|\eta\cup A|}}{\int e^{-h|\xi\cup A|}d\nu(\xi)}=\dfrac{e^{-h|\eta|}}{\int e^{-h|\xi|}d\nu(\xi)}=g_{h}(\eta)

(8)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|g_{h}^{+A}(\eta)-(1-h(|\eta|-\iota^{+A}(\Lambda)))|d\nu^{+A}(\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|g_{h}^{+A}(\eta\cup A)-(1-h(|\eta\cup A|-|A|-\iota(\Lambda)))|d\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|g_{h}(\eta)-(1-h(|\eta|-\iota(\Lambda)))|d\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(h^{2})

∎

Lemma 3.21
.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(g_{h}^{+A}(\eta)-1)^{2}d\nu^{+A}(\eta)

\displaystyle=O(h^{2})

Proof.

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(g_{h}^{+A}(\eta)-1)^{2}d\nu^{+A}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(g_{h}^{+A}(\eta\cup A)-1)^{2}d\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(g_{h}(\eta)-1)^{2}d\nu(\eta)

\displaystyle=O(h^{2})

∎

Finally, we can prove the main lemma of the section.

Proof of
Lemma

3.13
.

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}_{-h}\nu^{+A}}[\varphi]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(1-h(|\eta|-\iota^{+A}(\Lambda)))\varphi]|

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\varphi(\eta)g_{h}^{+A}(\eta)d\nu^{+A}(\eta)-\int_{\Omega}(1-h(|\eta|-\iota^{+A}(\Lambda)))\varphi(\eta)d\nu^{+A}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(g_{h}^{+A}(\eta)-(1-h(|\eta|-\iota^{+A}(\Lambda))))\varphi(\eta)d\nu^{+A}(\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\cdot\int_{\Omega}|g_{h}^{+A}(\eta)-(1-h(|\eta|-\iota(\Lambda)))|d\nu^{+A}(\eta)

\displaystyle\leq O(h^{2})

∎

Remark 3.22
.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(|\eta|-\iota(\Lambda))\varphi]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[||\eta|-\iota(\Lambda)|]\cdot\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq 2\iota(\Lambda)=O(1)

Corollary 3.23
.

Let
\varphi:\Omega\to[-1,1]
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}_{-h}\nu}[\varphi]^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\varphi]^{2}=-2h\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\varphi]\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(|\eta|-\iota(\Lambda))\varphi]+O(h^{2})

Lemma 3.24
.

For bounded
f:\Lambda\to[-1,1]
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda}f(x)d\iota_{e^{-h}\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}=\int_{\Lambda}f(x)d\iota_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}+O(h)

Proof.

\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda}f(x)d\iota_{e^{-h}\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}-\int_{\Lambda}f(x)d\iota_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}f(x)d\iota_{e^{-h}\bm{\lambda}}-\int_{\Lambda}f(x)d\iota_{\bm{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mathcal{T}_{-h}\nu_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}}[\eta(f)]-\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\nu_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}}[\eta(f)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\nu_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}}[\eta(f)(g_{h}(\eta)-1)]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\nu_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}}[\eta(f)]^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\nu_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}}[(g_{h}(\eta)-1)^{2}]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\nu_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}}[|\eta|^{2}]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\nu_{\bm{\lambda}}^{+A}}[(g_{h}(\eta)-1)^{2}]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{(\lambda|\Lambda|)^{2}+\lambda|\Lambda|}\sqrt{O(h^{2})}

\displaystyle=O(h)

(Note that the

∎

3.2
Construction of the Localization Scheme

First we will construct the localization scheme and then prove necessary facts about it.

Let
\nu
be a point process (i.e. random set of points) on
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For us,
\nu
will be a hard sphere model on
\Lambda
at activity
\lambda
.

Remark 3.25
.

Our setup of the negative fields localization scheme is slightly different from the definition in
[
CE22
]
, as they use
\{-1,1\}
coordinates while we are using
\{0,1\}
coordinates.

Definition 3.26

(Negative-fields localization (continuous space))
.

The negative-fields localization process is the unique martingale of probability measures
(\nu_{t})_{t}
that can be written as

\displaystyle\nu_{t}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{T}_{-t}\mathcal{R}_{A(t)}\nu

where
A(t)
is an almost-surely increasing process of finite subsets of
\Lambda
.

Essentially, points will be randomly added to
A(t)
to balance out the negative tilt in expectation. We will give a more rigorous construction of
A(t)
below.

A(t)
is a time-inhomogeneous spatial birth process. Such processes were constructed in
[
BDP22
]
, but their construction is more complicated as they allow for deaths. We will explicitly construct the process here in a simpler way—we will be using details of the construction in the proof.

The following remark gives intuition for the choice of
A(t)
.

Remark 3.27
.

In the finite (i.e. hard core) case, both pinning and applying an infinitesimal external field can be viewed as linear tilts:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{+i}\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\nu(x)\dfrac{x(i)}{\iota(i)}=\nu(x)(1+\dfrac{x(i)-\iota(i)}{\iota(i)})

\displaystyle x\in\{0,1\}^{\Lambda}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{-h}\nu(x)

\displaystyle=\nu(x)(1-h(|x|_{1}-\iota(\Lambda)))+o(h)

where
\iota(i)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu}[x(i)]
, and
x(i)=1[i\in x]
.

Heuristically, to ensure that
\mathbb{E}[\nu_{t+h}|\nu_{t}]=\nu_{t}
, we want to balance out the negative tilts in expectation by pinning each coordinate
i
with probability
p_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\Lambda}p_{i}\left(\dfrac{x(i)-\iota(i)}{\iota(i)}\right)-h(|x|_{1}-\iota(\Lambda))

\displaystyle=0

This solves to
p_{i}=h\iota(i)
.

For us
A(t+h)\setminus A(t)
is approximately a Poisson point process with intensity equal to
h
times the intensity of
\nu_{t}
(excluding pinned points).
To avoid technical issues, we will construct
A(t)
as a dependent thinning of a Poisson point process.

We now formally construct
A(t)
.

See
3.7

Definition 3.28

(
A(t)
)
.

Let
X
be drawn from a Poisson point process on
\Lambda\times[0,\infty)\times[0,1]
with intensity
\lambda
(this will be our probability space). Intuitively, the first coordinate is location, the second coordinate is time, and the third coordinate is a random number in
[0,1]
used to perform a random choice without using external sources of randomness.

Almost surely,
X_{\leq t}:=X\cap\{\Lambda\times[0,t]\times[0,1]\}
is finite for all
t\in[0,\infty)
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
X_{\leq t}
,
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a filtration. Let

(x_{1},t_{1},l_{1}),(x_{2},t_{2},l_{2}),\ldots,(x_{k},t_{k},l_{k}),\ldots,
be the points of
X
in the increasing order of time
t_{i}
.
Let
t_{0}:=0
. We have
0=t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\ldots
, and

A(t)
will be constant on
[t_{i},t_{i+1})
for
i\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
.
We will set
A(t_{0})=\emptyset
, and for
i\in{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle A(t_{i})

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}A(t_{i-1})\cup\{x_{i}\}&\text{if $l_{i}\leq\dfrac{\tilde{\iota}_{t}(x)}{\lambda}$}\\
A(t_{i-1})&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
\tilde{\iota}_{t}
is the intensity measure of
\mathcal{T}_{-t_{i}}\mathcal{R}_{A(t_{i-1})}\nu
(excluding pinned points), or simply
\mathcal{T}_{-t_{i}}\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{A(t_{i-1})}\nu
.

(The purpose of the

with probability

Note that

is a (deterministic) function of

.

A(t)
is a measurable function of

The proof is deferred to

Remark 3.30
.

Note that in hard spheres model no overlapping spheres can be added since the intensity changes to prevent that after the addition of each sphere.

C(x)
and its neighbors (including diagonals). The number of such cells for a given dimension is

|\mathcal{N}(x)|=3^{d}

The maximum number of sphere centers that can fit inside the cell of size

is bounded by
k_{\max,d}=\frac{(4r)^{d}}{(2r/\sqrt{d})^{d}}=2^{d}{d^{d/2}}
, and hence the total cost for the non-overlap check for spheres is at most

|\mathcal{N}(x)|\cdot k_{\max,d}=3^{d}\cdot 2^{d}{d^{d/2}}=O_{d}(1)\,.

\displaystyle\int fd\mu_{s}\leq\int fdU_{s}

This theorem shows an upper bound on

We will show that if we condition

on the event that

conditioned on all points of
S

The intuition is the following.
First, imagine that points in
S
are not allowed to die. Then, the process behaves like continuum Glauber on the model with the points of
S
pinned. The relative density of the initial distribution with respect to this modified stationary distribution starts off bounded by
e^{\lambda|\Lambda|}
and cannot increase. In other words, the distribution is upper bounded by a constant multiple of the modified stationary distribution. Even if the initial distribution is not
\bm{1}_{S}

2^{|S|}

T\subseteq S

T
pinned. Like before, a linear combination of modified stationary distributions (one for each possible

pinned) will remain an upper bound.

On the other hand, suppose we ignore everything except the deaths of points in

S

In particular, if a linear combination of modified stationary distributions as described in the previous paragraph is an upper bound on the distribution, this will remain true over time with the coefficients of the linear combination evolving continuously. (Pinning points changes the distribution more than simply adding the points in, but because
H

The true process can be thought of as these two processes happening simultaneously. We can devise an upper bound on the measure that is preserved by the first process (continuum Glauber with points in
S

S

Proof.

6
Sampling Sphere Packings of Fixed Size

In this section, we show that if continuum Glauber mixes rapidly for
\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}
(and likewise for smaller
\bm{\lambda}
), then
Algorithm

3
can be used to efficiently (approximately) sample from the canonical ensemble for a size

less than the expected number of points of a sampler from
\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}
(
Corollary

1.5
, improving the density up to which sampling is provably efficient for hard spheres. Our techniques and result will apply to the more general setting of finite-range repulsive pair potentials.

Suppose that we want to sample sphere packings with size
|\eta|=k
. The idea is to first show a lower bound on
\Pr(|\eta|\leq k)
and
\Pr(|\eta|\geq k)
, and then use the mixing time of Continuum Glauber to show a lower bound for
\Pr(|\eta|=k)
. The sampling algorithm performs a search over
t\in[0,1]
to find
t
where
\Pr_{\eta\sim\mu_{t\bm{\lambda}}}(|\eta|=k)
is large, and performs rejection sampling from it. Note that
\mu_{t\bm{\lambda}}
conditioned on
|\eta|=k
is a uniform distribution over sphere packings, because the Gibbs weight depends only on
|\eta|
after conditioning, and thus it is the same distribution for all
t
. The next lemma holds for all repulsive Gibbs point processes, not just hard spheres.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}
be a repulsive Gibbs point process. For integer
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{\Pr_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}(|\eta|=k-1)}{\Pr_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}(|\eta|=k)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k}{\bm{\lambda}(\Lambda)}

To see where this ratio comes from intuitively, observe that in continuum Glauber, the jump rate from configurations of
k
points to configurations of
k-1
points is
k
, while the jump rate from configurations of
k-1
points to
k

\bm{\lambda}(\Lambda)
. This intuition can be formalized using the GNZ equations.

Proof.

Recall that the GNZ equations (see
Theorem

A.3
)
say that for any measurable

F:\Omega\times\Lambda\to[0,\infty)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sum_{x\in\eta}F(\eta,x)d\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\int e^{-\nabla_{x}^{+}H(\eta)}F(\eta\cup\{x\},x)\bm{\lambda}(x)\,dx\,d\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}(\eta).

Taking
F(\eta,x)=1[|\eta|=k]
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}k1[|\eta|=k]d\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\int e^{-\nabla_{x}^{+}H(\eta)}1[|\eta|+1=k]\bm{\lambda}(x)\,dx\,d\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}(\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\int\int 1[|\eta|=k-1]\bm{\lambda}(x)\,dx\,d\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}(\eta).

Hence,

\displaystyle k\Pr_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}(|\eta|=k)

\displaystyle\leq\bm{\lambda}(\Lambda)\Pr_{\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}}(|\eta|=k-1)

∎

Lemma 6.2
.

For
\eta\sim\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\Pr(|\eta|\geq\lfloor\mathbb{E}[|\eta|]\rfloor)\geq\frac{1}{1+\bm{\lambda}(\Lambda)}

Let

. Then,

\displaystyle\Pr(|\eta|\leq\lfloor k^{*}\rfloor-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\leq k^{*}-1}\Pr(|\eta|=k)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\leq k^{*}-1}\Pr(|\eta|=k)(k^{*}-k)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\leq k^{*}}\Pr(|\eta|=k)(k^{*}-k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k>k^{*}}\Pr(|\eta|=k)(k-k^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq\lfloor k^{*}\rfloor+1}\Pr(|\eta|=k)k

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq\lfloor k^{*}\rfloor+1}\Pr(|\eta|=k-1)\bm{\lambda}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\Pr(|\eta|\geq\lfloor k^{*}\rfloor)\bm{\lambda}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(|\eta|\geq\lfloor k^{*}\rfloor)(\bm{\lambda}(\Lambda)+1)

∎

Lemma 6.3

Let

Proof.

For integer

, let

\displaystyle w_{j}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{j!}\int_{\Lambda^{k}}e^{-H(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j})}\prod_{i=1}^{j}\bm{\lambda}(x_{i})dx

Then,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\eta\sim\mu_{t\bm{\lambda}}}(|\eta|\geq k)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}w_{j}t^{j}1[j\geq k]}{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}w_{j}t^{j}}

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Pr_{\eta\sim\mu_{t\bm{\lambda}}}(|\eta|\geq k)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}w_{j}t^{j}1[j\geq k]\cdot\frac{j}{t}}{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}w_{j}t^{j}}-\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}w_{j}t^{j}1[j\geq k]}{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}w_{j}t^{j}}\cdot\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}w_{j}t^{j}\frac{j}{t}}{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}w_{j}t^{j}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{t\bm{\lambda}}}\left[1[|\eta|\geq k]\cdot\frac{|\eta|}{t}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{t\bm{\lambda}}}[1[|\eta|\geq k]]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{t\bm{\lambda}}}\left[\frac{|\eta|}{t}\right]

For each
t
, this is the covariance of two non-decreasing functions of
|\eta|
. Hence, it is non-negative, and thus

\frac{d}{dt}\Pr_{\eta\sim\mu_{t\bm{\lambda}}}(|\eta|\geq k)\geq 0.

Also,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Pr_{\eta\sim\mu_{t\bm{\lambda}}}(|\eta|\geq k)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{t\bm{\lambda}}}\left[1[|\eta|\geq k]\cdot\frac{|\eta|}{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\eta\sim\mu_{t\bm{\lambda}}}\left[\frac{|\eta|}{t}\right]

(by stochastic domination by Poisson point process)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t\bm{\lambda}(\Lambda)}{t}=\bm{\lambda}(\Lambda).

∎

We assume
\bm{\lambda}(\Lambda)\geq 1
to simplify the arithmetic. This is no loss as our final theorem (

) only applies when

, and there is no such

.

Proof.

(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}

consists of a set
X
and a
\sigma
-algebra on
X
of measurable subsets of
X
. The complements, countable unions, and countable intersections of measurable sets are measurable.

We often abuse notation and simply write
X
when the
\sigma
-algebra is clear from context.

\mathbb{R}
and
[0,\infty]
will be equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra.

\Omega=\{\eta\subseteq\Lambda:|\eta|<\infty\}
will be equipped by the smallest
\sigma
-algebra for which
\eta\mapsto\eta(B)
is measurable for all measurable
B\subseteq\Lambda
.

Given two sets
X
and
Y
equipped with
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, we equip
X\times Y
with the product
\sigma
-algebra, generated by (i.e. smallest
\sigma
-algebra that contains)
\{A\times B:A\in\mathcal{X},B\in\mathcal{Y}\}
. In particular, the projection maps
(x,y)\mapsto x
and
(x,y)\mapsto y
are measurable. The product of
\sigma
-algebras is associative.

Fact C.2
.

A function
f:X\to Y
is measurable iff the preimage of a measurable subset of
Y
is a measurable subset of
X
. The composition of measurable functions is measurable.
An indicator function of a set is a measurable function iff the set is a measurable set. A simple function is a finite linear combination of indicator functions of measurable sets. Any
[0,\infty]
-valued function can be written as a point-wise limit of a monotonically increasing sequence of simple functions. The sum, product, and point-wise limit of (extended) real-valued measurable functions is measurable. The reciprocal of a non-zero measurable function is measurable.

Lemma C.3
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded measurable set.
Suppose
\varphi:\Lambda\times\Lambda\to[0,\infty]
is measurable.

Then,

\displaystyle(x,\eta)\mapsto\sum_{y\in\eta}\varphi(x,y)

is measurable.

Proof.

We know that the function
(x,\eta)\mapsto\eta(B)
is measurable for any measurable
B\subseteq\Lambda
.

For any measurable

,

\displaystyle(x,\eta)\mapsto 1_{A}(x)\eta(B)=\sum_{y\in\eta}1_{A}(x)1_{B}(y)

is measurable.
Consider

This is a
\lambda

∎
