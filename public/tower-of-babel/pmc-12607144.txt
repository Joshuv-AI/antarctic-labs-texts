Spreading depolarization is followed by seizure-like activation of the hippocampus: potential mechanism of migraine-aura triggered seizures
Authors: Maria P Smirnova, Irina V Pavlova, Lyudmila V Vinogradova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPropagation of spreading depolarization (SD), a wave of transient cellular depolarization, over the cerebral cortex drives migraine aura symptoms. Growing evidence suggests that hippocampus, brain region involved in regulation of mood and cognition, also contributes to pathogenesis of migraine. Hippocampus has highly inhomogeneous functional anatomy with strong dorsoventral difference in functional and network properties. We hypothesized that cerebral dysfunction induced by SD also varies within the hippocampus and studied characteristics and effects of SD in the dorsal and ventral hippocampus of awake animals. Given high susceptibility of the hippocampus to seizures, we focused on excitatory effects of SD.MethodsUnilateral SD was induced by a pinprick of the dorsal hippocampus in freely behaving rats. In electrophysiological experiments, local field potentials were recorded bilaterally in different sites along the dorsoventral axis of the hippocampus; propagation patterns, electrographic manifestations and excitatory effects of SD were evaluated. In separate experiments, effect of hippocampal SD on behavior was assessed.ResultsSD waveforms varied depending on local cytoarchitecture of hippocampal zones (CA, dentate gyrus and fiber-rich areas), irrespectively of their dorsoventral level. In the dentate gyrus, unusual double-wave SDs were recorded. Reaching ventral hippocampus, unilateral SD triggered a bilateral seizure-like discharge recruited both ipsilateral (affected by SD) and contralateral (unaffected by SD) sides. The post-SD activation of the ventral hippocampus had striking behavioral manifestation – a bout of wet dog shakes.ConclusionsThis is the first experimental evidence that SD can trigger seizure-like activity in particular regions of awake non-epileptic brain. We suggest that the phenomenon may be relevant to mechanisms of migraine-aura triggered seizures (migralepsy).Keywords: Spreading depolarization, Migraine, Aura, Hippocampus, Migralepsy, Migraine-aura triggered seizures

Background

Spreading depolarization (SD), a slowly propagating wave of transient neuroglial depolarization, is well-known pathophysiological mechanism of migraine with aura [1, 2]. Propagation of SD over the cerebral cortex drives reversible sensory (visual, somatosensory) symptoms of aura [3]. Cortical SD is likely to promote headache by enhancing expression of calcitonin-gene-related peptide [4, 5] and proinflammatory cytokines [6, 7] in the cortical parenchyma and meninges. There is increasing understanding that development of SD in subcortical brain regions, apart from the cerebral cortex, may be implicated in migraine pathophysiology and generation of aura symptoms [3].

Several lines of clinical and preclinical evidence indicate that the hippocampus, a temporal lobe structure implicated in affective and cognitive processing, may be a new target for studying migraine mechanisms. Imaging studies showed changes in the hippocampal volume in patients with migraine [8] and blood flow changes in the hippocampus during premonitory symptoms of migraine attacks [9]. Electrical stimulation of the hippocampus in humans produces visual disturbances [10]. The key role of the hippocampus in the control of memory and cognition suggests that dysfunction of the subcortical structure may contribute to memory deficit described in migraine patients [11, 12].

The hippocampus exhibits the highest vulnerability to SD among brain structures [13]. SD can be induced in the hippocampus of humans [14] and spontaneously occur in the hippocampus of mice with familial hemiplegic migraine type 2 (FHM2) mutation [15]. Development of SD in the hippocampus, which is involved in pain processing, produces nociceptive activation of the caudal trigeminal nucleus in experimental animals [16].

Charles and Hansen have suggested that aura symptoms depend on not only properties of SD itself but on functional anatomy of brain regions through which SD travels [17]. The hippocampus is the elongated structure with high anatomical inhomogeneity. Compelling evidence shows strong functional, network and molecular segregation of the hippocampus along its longitudinal axis [18, 19]. The dorsal (posterior in humans) hippocampus is critical for cognitive functions (memory, spatial navigation) while the ventral (anterior) hippocampus regulates emotional and affective processing [18]. Furthermore, the anterior hippocampus of epileptic patients, corresponding to the ventral hippocampus of rodents, shows higher excitability and propensity to seizures than the posterior (dorsal) hippocampus [20]. Contiguity of the grey matter along the longitudinal axis of the hippocampus suggests ability of SD to spread non-synaptically in the direction. But to the best of our knowledge, propagation of SD along the dorsoventral axis of the hippocampus, having complex curved shape, has not been investigated. Very little is known about properties of SD in the ventral hippocampus. Previous studies of hippocampal SD mostly targeted at the dorsal hippocampus and evaluated transverse propagation of SD between different zones (fields/layers) within the region [21–23]. Given the pronounced dorsoventral difference in functional and network properties of the hippocampus, we hypothesized that cerebral dysfunction induced by SD differs along the longitudinal axis of the hippocampus. To test the hypothesis, we studied dorsoventral propagation of hippocampal SD in awake animals, its behavioral manifestations, electrographic patterns and effects of SD at different dorsoventral levels of the hippocampus. The study used awake animals with undisturbed excitability and network activity. Despite well-known depressive and anti-seizure effects of SD [13, 24, 25], in tissue with enhanced excitability, SD can promote late epileptiform activation [26, 27]. Given high baseline excitability of the hippocampus [20], we expected hippocampal SD to favor hyperexcitation and focused on excitatory effects of SD that might underlie migraine-epilepsy comorbidity.

Methods

Subjects

Fifty-five adult male Wistar rats (350–450 g, Scientific center for Biomedical Technologies of the Federal Medical and Biological Agency, Russia) were used. Rats were housed in a temperature-controlled vivarium (22˚C ± 2˚C, a 12-h light/dark cycle, lights on at 08.00 h) with food and water ad libitum. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Higher Nervous Activity (protocol N1, 01.02.2022). Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering and to ensure reliability of the results.

Stereotaxic surgery

Under isoflurane anesthesia, stainless steel guide cannulas (23 gauge) for initiation of SD were implanted bilaterally in the dorsal hippocampus – either in its rostral (AP -2.6, ML ± 1.7, DV 2.7 mm) or caudal (AP -4.4, ML ± 3.9, DV 2.5 mm) parts. Electrodes (insulated silver wire) for recording local field potentials (LFP) were positioned either in the dorsal (AP -2.3, ML ± 1.6, DV 3.7 mm) and ventral (AP -6, ML ± 4.9, DV 6 mm) hippocampus (experiment I) or in the frontal cortex (AP + 1.2, ML ± 2.3 mm, DV 1.8) and ventral hippocampus (AP -4.8, ML ± 5, DV 7.7 mm) (experiment II). The guide cannulas and recording electrodes were positioned in homotopic sites of the left and right hemispheres. Reference electrode was placed over the cerebellum (common position providing stable LFP recording). The guide cannulas, recording electrodes and pin connector were fixed on the skull with acrylic dental plastic. During post-surgery period, rats were injected with antibiotic amoxicillin (0.3 ml, i.m.) and glucose solution (1 ml, s.c.). A 30-gauge stylus of the same length as guide cannula was inserted into it to prevent clogging. All experiments started two weeks after the surgery.

Experimental design

The study included electrophysiological (25 rats) and behavioral (30 rats) experiments.

Electrophysiological experiments

LFP were recorded bilaterally either in the dorsal and ventral hippocampus (experiment I, 14 rats, Fig. 1A) or in the frontal cortex and ventral hippocampus (experiment II, 11 rats, Fig. 1B). In each test, rats were individually placed in a shielded chamber and implanted connector was attached to the recording cable. After 15-min baseline LFP recording, a single unilateral SD was induced by a mechanical stimulation (pinprick) of the dorsal hippocampus, using the method described previously [24, 28]. Briefly, the needle was inserted into the guide cannula and extended 1.0 mm from its tip. As shown previously [28], the method produces a circumscribed damage (about 0.3 mm3) without obvious signs of inflammation. Fifteen minutes later, identical pinprick of the opposite dorsal hippocampus was produced. Therefore, in each test, two unilateral SDs were induced. Two tests with a week interval were performed in each rat.

Full-band electrical activity (0–100 Hz, 1 kHz sampling rate) of the hippocampus/cortex was recorded using a four-channel, high-input impedance (1 gΩ) dc amplifier and a/d converter (E14-440, L-Card, Russia) with simultaneous video-monitoring of behavior. Out of 25 rats with implanted electrodes, three animals were excluded from analysis because histological assessment showed that a guide cannula or a recording electrode was localized outside targeted regions. Data from the animals are not included in the paper. In remaining 22 rats with correct location of all cannulas and electrodes, only artefact-free LFP recordings were analyzed. Criteria for exclusion from analysis were loss of LFP recording or its contamination by artefacts in at least one channel. In total, 39 recordings of unilateral hippocampal SD of sufficient quality were obtained. LFP recordings were filtered with lowpass filter 0–50 Hz (direct current, dc) and bandpass filter 1–50 Hz (AC).

SD was identified by a characteristic high-amplitude negative DC potential shift. Amplitude of SD was measured from pre-SD baseline DC level to the maximal negative deflection. Latency of SD was determined by the time that elapsed between a pinprick triggering SD and the onset of negative DC shift at the recording point. Duration of SD was measured as a time period between the onset of DC deflection and return to 90% baseline level. Duration of bursting/seizure-like activity preceding/following hippocampal SD was measured using filtered AC recordings. The activity was defined as large-amplitude (exceeding two standard deviations) rhythmic spike-wave/spike discharge of at least 2-s duration.

Behavioral experiments

Behavioral effects of hippocampal SD were studied in a separate group of rats (n = 30) with bilateral guide cannula preliminary implanted in the two dorsal hippocampi. Rats were individually placed in experimental chamber (400 × 300 × 300 mm) and bilateral pinprick (15 rats) or sham treatment (15 age-matched control rats) of the hippocampus was performed. During sham treatment, a short needle was inserted into the guide cannula without penetration. Behavioral chamber was carefully cleaned with ethanol solution after each animal to remove odors. Rat behavior was videotaped during five minutes after stimulation and was analyzed offline using EthoVision software (Noldus, the Netherland). Total distance traveled, mean velocity, number of rearing and wet dog shakes were assessed in one-minute bins.

Histological analysis

After the end of experiment, animals were deeply anesthetized and perfused intracardially with 0.9% saline. The brains were removed, stored in 10% formalin for 48 h, sectioned in coronal 50-µm slices and stained with 0.1% сresyl violet. The slices were used for histological verification of localization of recording electrodes and SD initiation site.

Statistical analysis

Normality of the data was examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. To compare groups with normal distribution (behavioral effects, amplitude and duration of SD), one-way ANOVA or repeated measures ANOVA with Neuman-Keuls post hoc test was used. If data distribution did not pass normality test (latency of SD, duration and amplitude of SD/discharges in different hippocampal regions), we used Wilcoxon signed-rank test or Mann-Whytney test for pairwise comparisons and Kruskal-Wallis or Friedman ANOVA for multiple comparisons. To compare incidence of different events (SD and discharges) between groups, Fisher exact test was used. Data are shown as median ± interquartile range.

Results

Initiation and propagation of SD in the hippocampus of awake freely behaving rats

A pinprick of the dorsal hippocampus induced a single SD in all tests (39/39, Fig. 1). Unilaterally induced SD propagated through the entire ipsilateral hippocampus reaching its most anterior tip and the ventral hippocampus but never invaded the contralateral hippocampus (Fig. 1A, experiment I) and neocortex (Fig. 1B, experiment II). In experiment I (n = 23), SD induced in the dorsal hippocampus appeared in its rostral part and in the ventral hippocampus with similar delay − 24.7 ± 2.0 s and 24.0 ± 2.0 s, respectively (Fig. 1A, C). Given the distance of 3–4 mm from the SD initiation site to the recording electrodes in the dorsal hippocampus, the speed of non-synaptic propagation of SD along the longitudinal axis of the hippocampus was about 8–9 mm/min. In experiment II (n = 16), SD traveled from the rostral part of the dorsal hippocampus to the ventral hippocampus significantly longer - during 56.3 ± 2.2 s (Fig. 1C). Mean duration of SDs recorded in the dorsal and ventral portions of the hippocampus did not differ significantly (Fig. 1D). Thus, SD was always triggered by mechanical stimulation of the dorsal hippocampus and spread non-synaptically along the dorsoventral axis of the ipsilateral hippocampus.

Zone-specific patterns of SD within the dorsal and ventral hippocampus

Within both the dorsal and ventral parts of the hippocampus, we found great variability of the shape, amplitude and duration of SD. Figure 2A shows representative traces of three types of SD waveforms - a classic large SD, a low-amplitude single-peak SD and a double-wave SD. To assess regional specificity of the SD patterns, we analyzed LFP recordings from electrodes with histologically verified localization (n = 58, including 23 positions in the dorsal and 35 positions in the ventral hippocampus). The recordings were classified according to their position in the hippocampal zones with different cytoarchitecture: (1) Cornu Ammonis (CA) subfields (2), fiber-rich areas (f) with high density of myelinated fibers and (3) the dentate gyrus (DG). In both dorsal and ventral portions of the hippocampus, SDs of type 1 (n = 27), type 2 (n = 13) and type 3 (n = 18) were recorded mostly in CA subregions, fiber-rich areas and dentate gyrus zones, respectively (Fig. 2B). In CA subfields, we observed mainly high-amplitude negative DC shifts of saddle-like shape or large DC shifts either followed by a “tail” of residual negativity or preceded by a brief negative step. Similar SD patterns have been previously described in CA1 fields of the dorsal hippocampus in anesthetized rats [21, 22]. A low-amplitude SD with a single peak was detected mostly in areas with dense fiber bundles. Unusual double-wave SDs with two negative waves separated by pronounced positivity were reliably recorded in the dentate gyrus (Fig. 2A, B). Amplitude and duration of the SD types in the dorsal and ventral divisions of the hippocampus did not differ significantly (Fig. 2B, C, D). Therefore, we pooled the data from the dorsal and ventral hippocampus for each zone and estimated inter-regional variability of SD characteristics (Fig. 2E, F). We found significant difference in the amplitude (F = 17.85, p < 0.001) and duration (F = 28.09, p < 0.001) of SD between the hippocampal subregions. SDs had the highest amplitude in the CA areas and the longest duration in the dentate gyrus (Fig. 2E, F). Expectedly, SDs of minimal amplitude and duration were recorded in fiber-rich regions with low neuronal density. Thus, SDs had zone-specific patterns, irrespectively to their dorsoventral position within the hippocampus.

Excitation associated with SD in the dorsal and ventral hippocampus

In the hippocampus of awake animals, SD was accompanied by pronounced excitation preceding SD onset (a pre-SD prodromal burst) and following SD termination (a post-SD seizure-like discharge).

Prodromal burst ahead of SD onset

About a half of SDs recorded in the hippocampus (53%, 31/58) were preceded by a prodromal burst described previously in the dorsal hippocampus [21, 23]. Similar to the studies, the burst was detected on the side invaded by SD and was tightly coupled with SD onset in all regions of the hippocampus (Fig. 3A). The duration of the prodromal bursts was similar in the dorsal (9.2 ± 1.1 s) and ventral (9.9 ± 1.3 s) hippocampus but patterns of the bursts differed - high-frequency bursting in the dorsal hippocampus and slower irregular spiking in the ventral hippocampus (Fig. 3A). In CA subfields, pre-SD bursts developed more rarely in the dorsal hippocampus than in the ventral one (Fig. 3B) that is likely to result from very rostral position of recording electrodes in the dorsal hippocampus.

Seizure-like activity following hippocampal SD

Almost all hippocampal SDs (91%, 32/35) were followed by an epileptiform discharge detected in the ventral hippocampus (Fig. 4A). The discharge lasted 16.3 ± 1.2 s and represented a low-frequency spiking of progressively growing amplitude (up to 1.6 ± 0.2 mV). Development of the seizure-like activity did not depend on SD amplitude: small DC shifts might be followed by a large discharge (Fig. 4B) and vice versa high-amplitude SDs might develop without subsequent spiking (Fig. 4C). The post-SD seizure-like activity was recorded in 94.0 ± 2.4 s (n = 32) after initiation of SD in the dorsal hippocampus, i.e. when SD occupied the ventral part of the hippocampus. Typically, the discharge appeared immediately after termination of SD (Fig. 4A) although in some tests it was recorded during the recovery phase of SD (Fig. 4C) or with some delay after SD termination (Fig. 4E).

In the dorsal hippocampus, only a small portion of SDs (26%, 6/23) was followed by a discharge (Fig. 4A, F). The dorsal spiking was of lower amplitude (0.4 ± 0.1 mV, p = 0.0047) and duration (8.8 ± 1.2 s, p = 0.0076) than discharges recorded in the ventral hippocampus. The low-amplitude dorsal discharges might be produced by volume conduction or activation of intrahippocampal association pathways during the ventral seizure-like activity. All subregions of the ventral hippocampus (CA, fiber-rich areas and the dentate gyrus) showed similar incidence, amplitude and duration of the post-SD discharges (Fig. 4G, H).

Remarkably, most seizure-like discharges induced by unilateral SD (75%, 24/32) were detected not only in the ipsilateral ventral hippocampus affected by SD but also in the unaffected contralateral one (Fig. 5). Accordingly, post-SD discharges were superimposed on depressed activity of the ipsilateral hippocampus and on normal activity of the contralateral hippocampus. The bilateral seizure-like discharges induced by unilateral SD were either symmetrical with similar amplitudes in both sides (Fig. 5A) or asymmetrical with higher amplitude in the ventral hippocampus ipsilateral (Fig. 5B) or contralateral (Fig. 5C) to SD. The discharge patterns were detected with similar probability (Fig. 5D).

Most post-SD discharges (63%, 20/32) had striking behavioral manifestation – a bout of several (2.2 ± 0.6, range 0–17) violent shakes of the head and body (wet dog shakes). Shaking behavior started at 99.8 ± 4.6 s after SD initiation and lasted 4.7 ± 1.2 s. Wet dog shaking behavior was always time-locked the final phase of the post-SD discharge with tight temporal coupling between shakes and spikes (Fig. 5E).

Video-recordings, performed during electrophysiological experiments, showed that, in addition to wet dog shakes, hippocampal SD was accompanied by pronounced behavioral activation (rapid locomotion with rearing). By the end of 15-min baseline LFP recording, spontaneous locomotion ceased but after triggering hippocampal SD all animals started to move actively with frequent sniffing and rearing. To study the behavioral effects of hippocampal SD in more details, a separate group of rats was subjected to behavioral testing.

Behavioral hyperactivity induced by hippocampal SD

Spontaneous behavior of rats with guide cannulas preliminary implanted in the dorsal hippocampus was recorded during five minutes after a single bilateral pinprick of the hippocampus (15 rats, SD group) or sham treatment (15 rats, control group). Compared to sham-treated rats, animals of SD group increased traveled distance (Fig. 6A), velocity of movements (Fig. 6B) and rearing behavior (Fig. 6C) since the second minute after initiation of SD in the dorsal hippocampus. Enhanced locomotion and exploratory activity were detected up to 3–4 min after initiation of SD in the hippocampus, thus outlasting he period of SD propagation within the hippocampus (1–2 min). Similar to electrophysiological experiments, rats of SD group exhibited a bout of wet dog shakes at the second minute (mean latency of 100 ± 6 s) after triggering hippocampal SD (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

Hippocampal SD: initiation by mechanical stimulation, propagation pattern and behavioral manifestations

Our study shows very high vulnerability of the hippocampus to triggering SD by mechanical stimulation in freely behaving animals - a pinprick of the dorsal hippocampus always induces a single SD. Given existing evidence for easy initiation of SD by chemical and electrical stimulation of the hippocampus [13, 21, 29], the result indicates that the hippocampus readily generates SD in response to its focal activation of various nature.

Being induced in the dorsal hippocampus, SD non-synaptically propagates along the longitudinal axis of the ipsilateral hippocampus but invades neither the contralateral hippocampus nor neocortex. Similar propagation pattern without entering the neocortex was reported for chemically-induced hippocampal SD in awake rats [29]. However, in slices [30] and awake mice with FMH type 2 mutation [15], most hippocampal SDs invade the neocortex. This is likely to result from facilitated propagation of SD between the cortex and hippocampus in slices [31] and FHM mice model [32].

We have found very rapid velocity of non-synaptic propagation of SD along the longitudinal axis of the hippocampus in awake rats (8–9 mm/min). It is significantly higher than the SD propagation speed within the dorsal hippocampus of urethan-anesthetized rats (5–7.5 mm/min, 21) and in the neocortex of awake rats (5–6 mm/min, 28).

Consistent with other studies [15, 29], our experiments show that hippocampal SD promotes hyperlocomotion and exploratory behavior. Prolonged hyperactivity in the open field has been reported after focal unilateral ischemia in the hippocampus and hippocampal SD has aggravated the behavioral changes [33]. Here, we found that hippocampal SD has additional behavioral marker - a bout of wet dog shakes timed to post-SD seizure-like activation of the ventral hippocampus. The shaking behavior is characteristic for limbic seizures [34, 35] that supports seizure-related nature of the SD-induced activation. Remarkably, all the behavioral changes started when SD invaded the ventral hippocampus indicating its leading role in generation of the behavior.

Zone-specific electrographic patterns of hippocampal SD

Characteristics and effects of hippocampal SD in the uninjured waking brain with intact network activity remain mostly unknown. Layer-dependent variability of SD patterns in CA1 region of the dorsal hippocampus has been thoroughly studied in slices and anesthetized animals [21–23]. In our study, the diameter of recording electrodes was larger than the width of hippocampal layers, therefore we could not identify the layer-specific properties of SD but we caught striking difference in SD patterns between large hippocampal zones - CA1-3, dentate gyrus and fiber-rich areas. Consistent with the previous data [21, 36], high-amplitude saddle-like SDs with two (early fast and late slow) peaks separated by weak positivity were reliably recorded in CA1-3 regions of awake rats. The two components are usually attributed to SD waves generated and propagating independently in different cell layers of the hippocampus [21, 22]. Detection of low-amplitude SDs in regions with high density of white matter bands (fimbria, alveus etc.) is expectable because reduced amplitude of SD is typical for brain regions with enhanced myelinization and low neuronal density [37]. Our study describes for the first time unusual double-wave SDs in the dentate gyrus. We suggest that the waveform may result from complex pattern of SD propagation in the region that was described in horizontal hippocampal slices [38]. Unlike unidirectional spread of SD along CA fields, after entering the dentate gyrus, SD either dissipates in the hilus or propagates to the upper blade of granular cell layer [38]. Transient positivity between two negative waves may reflect extinction of SD in the hilus before invading granular layer of the dentate gyrus. We found that the zone-specific patterns of SDs were similar in the dorsal and ventral parts of the hippocampus. This result is in line with in vitro data showed no difference in SD characteristics between slices from the dorsal and ventral portions of the hippocampus [30, 38]. Thus, waveforms of hippocampal SD strongly depend on local cytoarchitectonic organization of hippocampus regardless of its dorsoventral level. However, we found that excitatory effects of SD differ in the dorsal and ventral parts of the hippocampus.

Excitatory effects of SD in the dorsal and ventral hippocampus

Prodromal burst preceding hippocampal SD

Fast field oscillations ahead of SD have been previously described in the dorsal hippocampus of anesthetized [21] and awake [15] rodents as well as in slices [23, 38]. In line with the studies, our experiments in awake rats have shown that the burst appeared 10 s before the depolarization phase of SD and subsided with its onset. The prodromal activity is referred as an integral component of SD evolution. It is thought that the pre-SD bursting reflects changes in functional properties of neurons and activation of local network mechanisms before SD onset [23, 39]. Detailed spatiotemporal analysis of the prodromal burst in anesthetized rodents has shown that it emerges in stratum radiatum of CA1 subfield (more rarely in granular cell layer and CA3) and does not depend on synaptic transmission [21–23, 39]. Our findings show that the bursts in the front of SD have different electrographic patterns in the dorsal and ventral hippocampus that may result from difference in their intrinsic neuronal properties [19].

Seizure-like activity following hippocampal SD

Our study shows for the first time that in awake non-epileptic animals, SD triggers transient seizure-like activation of the ventral hippocampus with no/minimal involvement of its dorsal segment. The result suggests that the ventral part of the hippocampus is especially vulnerable to excitatory effects of SD. It is well known that a basic network property of the ventral hippocampus is its high excitability even under physiological conditions. Anterior hippocampus of epileptic patients (the ventral hippocampus of rodents) shows higher seizure susceptibility than the posterior (dorsal) hippocampus [20]. In rodents, epileptic activity starts or it is more intense in the ventral hippocampus than in the dorsal one [19, 40]. Elevated potassium elicits higher interictal bursting in the ventral hippocampus than in the dorsal [41, 42]. Pyramidal cells of CA1 field are more excitable in the ventral than in the dorsal segment of the hippocampus [40, 43]. Under physiological conditions the hyperexcitability of ventral hippocampus is balanced by adaptive inhibitory mechanisms but transient disruption of the balance during SD may promote rebound overexcitation after SD offset.

Remarkably, the seizure-like discharge induced by unilateral SD reliably recruits both sides of the ventral hippocampus, affected and unaffected by SD. The bilateral pattern of the seizure induced by unilateral SD indicates that SD produces not only local but also distant effects at the network level by enhancing excitability drive to the contralateral ventral hippocampus via commissural pathways. Massive projections connect hilar mossy cells of one side with granular cells of the opposite dentate gyrus and the connections are strongly activated in hyperexcitable conditions [44]. Involvement of dentate granular cells and their commissural inputs in mechanisms of the SD-induced hyperexcitation is supported by close association between post-SD discharge and shaking behavior found in our study. The hippocampus is known as a crucial component of the network underlying wet dog shaking [45]. Epileptiform activation of the hippocampus by pharmacological or electrical stimulation is accompanied by wet dog shakes and propagation of epileptic activity to the ventral portion of the hippocampus is a pre-requisite of the behavior [45, 46]. Experimental evidence shows an importance of dentate granular cells for generation of wet dog shaking - it is induced by direct stimulation of the granule cell layer [35] and is eliminated by its destruction in the ventral dentate gyrus but not in the dorsal one [46, 47].

Previous studies have reported that SD produces epileptiform activation following a transient depressive period in neuronal tissue with pharmacologically enhanced excitability [26, 27, 48]. Seizure-like activity following SD has been described in the injured neocortex of humans [48, 49]. In patients with acute brain injury, the epileptiform activity slowly spreads with SD over the cortex (spreading convulsions) but sometimes remained restricted to one cortical area [48]. Our study in awake healthy animals shows that SD can be followed by seizure-like activity only in particular brain regions.

Migraine aura-triggered seizures and seizure-like activity induced by SD

Our findings may be relevant to understanding pathogenic mechanisms of migralepsy or migraine aura-triggered seizures (International Classification of Headache Disorders-3). This is a rare complication of migraine with aura in which a seizure occurs within one hour after typical migraine aura [50]. It is suggested that migraine aura acts as a trigger for epileptic seizure [51]. The concept of migralepsy is debated, its diagnosis and differentiation from occipital epilepsy with visual symptoms are extremely challenging, pathogenic mechanisms remain uncertain [51–53]. It has been hypothesized that hyperexcitability of the cortex makes it susceptible to both SD and seizures [54]. Our study provides the first experimental evidence for ability of SD to trigger seizure-like activity as a rebound event in non-epileptic uninjured brain of awake animals. The SD-induced activation is confined to a particular brain region and has no overt motor manifestations. It can be speculated that in some conditions the local activity triggered by SD can progress to tonic-clonic convulsions typical for migralepsy [52].

The present study has several limitations. First, we did not evaluate pain-related behavior specific for migraine and used SD as a model of migraine aura. Second, low spatial resolution of electrographic recordings did not allow detailed assessment of SD patterns/effects. The diameter of recording electrodes was much larger than the width of hippocampal layers. Third, electrophysiological experiments involved relatively small groups of animals. This resulted from difficulty of obtaining high-quality records of hippocampal SD in freely behaving animals and variability of implanted electrode positions within the hippocampus. Finally, the study was performed in male animals. Given sex-specific features of migraine in humans, the effects of SD need to be investigated in females.

Conclusion

The present study shows that propagation of SD within brain structures with high structural and network inhomogeneity produces variable region-specific effects. With spread of SD over the longitudinal axis of the hippocampus, pattern of SD remains unchanged but its effects on hippocampal activity significantly modify. This suggests dependence of SD-induced dysfunction (aura symptoms) on intrinsic and network properties of brain regions affected by SD. The study shows for the first time that development of unilateral SD in particular brain sites provokes strong bilateral excitation recruiting unaffected by SD contralateral areas. The effects contrasts to other SD-associated events (prodromal bursting and depressive effects) that are typically confined to the affected hemisphere. Further studies should clarify mechanisms underlying the region-specific and network effects of SD.
