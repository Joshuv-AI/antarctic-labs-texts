Adverse Reactions Identified after Approval of Novel Medicines: Analysis of 339 New Molecular Entities and Therapeutic Biologics
Authors: Ellen Pinnow, Brendan Day, Esther H. Zhou, Corinne Woods, Nana Dwumfour, Jenni Y. Lee, Ilynn Bulatao, Sanae Cherkaoui, Manish Kalaria, Sonja Brajovic, Nabila Sadiq, Farideh Sistani, Maggie Lee, Grace Chai, Gerald Dal Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postmarketing safety surveillance data identify safety issues not identified in preapproval clinical trials. We describe the frequency of new safety issues overall and by labeling section and the relationship of the most commonly added safety issues with the cumulative number of prescriptions dispensed via retail pharmacies. Using publicly available sources, we determined the frequency of the most common safety issues added after approval overall and by labeling section (Boxed Warning, Warnings and Precautions, and Adverse Reactions) for new molecular entities (NMEs) and new therapeutic biologics (NTBs) approved between 10/1/2002 and 12/31/2014. There were 339 products in the cohort (278 NMEs and 61 NTBs) with a median follow up of 13.1 years. There were 5,161 new safety issues overall. The most frequently added to the product labeling were anaphylactic reaction, angioedema, Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS), hypersensitivity, and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), which were added to the labeling of at least 12% of the 339 products. This was similar between NMEs and NTBs, except for cardiac failure, which was among the five most frequent adverse reactions (AR) added to NTBs, while TEN was less frequently added. Most other ARs were added to 10% or fewer products. Among drugs used primarily in the outpatient setting, the mean number of cumulative dispensed prescriptions exceeded 1 million at the time a safety issue was added to the labeling. Many of the most frequently added ARs are drug hypersensitivities. These findings are relevant to practitioners and patients, who should be alerted to the possibility of such reactions.

Serious hypersensitivity reactions are among the most commonly detected postmarketing adverse reactions and may be identified only many years after approval. Clinicians and patients need to be alert to the possibility of such reactions.

When a novel medicine is approved, common adverse reactions associated with it are generally well‐characterized and included in the product's labeling, also known as the Full Prescribing Information or the package insert. After approval, usage for extended durations, and in larger, more diverse patient populations leads to the identification of previously undetected adverse reactions, which are then added to the product labeling. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4

There have been many case reports and case series that describe specific adverse reactions with individual drugs. Case series derived from spontaneous reporting systems have known limitations but remain invaluable tools for early detection of rare but serious adverse events that may not be identified during pre‐approval clinical trials with limited sample sizes and follow‐up periods. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Prior research into safety‐related labeling changes for drugs and therapeutic biologics has explored their frequency and timing, 2 , 3 , 4 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 the content of Boxed Warning updates, 14 and the association between review characteristics and postmarketing safety outcomes. 2 , 3 , 4 , 14 , 15 These studies show that postmarketing safety labeling changes affect the majority of products after approval and continue throughout the product's lifecycle. 2 , 3 , 4 In our prior studies, we examined postmarket safety outcomes for drugs and biologics approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), providing comprehensive insights into safety surveillance patterns. The analysis of 278 new molecular entity drugs found that 70.1% of drugs had at least one safety‐related labeling update, with updates occurring throughout the product lifecycle. 4 Most safety issues were added to the Adverse Reactions section of the labeling (65.3%), followed by Warnings and Precautions (33.6%), while only 2% involved Boxed Warnings. 4 The analysis of 61 new therapeutic biologics found that 88.5% had safety‐related labeling updates, with biologics having significantly more safety updates than drugs overall. 3 Similarly, most biologic safety issues were added to Adverse Reactions (59.1%) and Warnings and Precautions (38.5%) sections of the labeling, with accelerated approval pathway associated with shorter time to safety events for both product types. 3 When investigating the relationship between premarket safety population size and postmarket safety outcomes for 339 products, the median size of the patient population was smaller for orphan products than for non‐orphan products (393 vs. 1,606 for non‐orphan), biologics compared to small‐molecule drugs, and products with expedited designations. 2 Importantly, smaller premarket safety population sizes were not associated with increased postmarket safety outcomes and did confirm prior assumptions that limited premarket exposure necessarily leads to more postmarket safety issues. 2 Other studies that have examined adverse reactions more broadly have generally been limited to those that are the subject of Boxed Warnings or certain communications from FDA. 12 , 14 , 16

Wu et al previously published of a list of MedDRA PT terms identified in the Boxed Warning, Warnings and Precautions, and Adverse Reactions sections of the labeling. 17 This was conducted as proof‐of‐concept study to determine whether the use of text search techniques to extract MedDRA terms could facilitate analysis of adverse events in drug labeling. While the study demonstrated that combining MedDRA standard terminologies with data mining techniques facilitated analysis of adverse events, it did not evaluate whether the search was able to discriminate if the identified term was equivalent to other adverse events in the labeling or represented an expansion on known adverse events; nor did it provide information on when the adverse event was added.

To our knowledge, no study has comprehensively described, from a clinical perspective, the actual adverse reactions that are incorporated after approval into the various safety‐related sections of the Full Prescribing Information or the extent of population utilization of the drug at the time the adverse reaction is added to the Full Prescribing Information.

We characterized the most common adverse reactions added to the labeling after approval for new molecular entity drugs (NMEs) and new therapeutic biologics (NTBs) approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) between October 2002 and December 2014. We also describe the timing, relative to initial approval, of the labeling change for the most common postmarketing safety issues and, for drugs used predominantly in the outpatient setting, the extent of population utilization at the time of the labeling update as well as 6 and 12 months before.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We previously identified all NMEs and NTBs approved by the FDA between October 1, 2002, and December 31, 2014 3 , 4 and retrospectively identified regulatory and review characteristics (Appendix S1), and safety‐related labeling changes from approval through June 30, 2018. 2 , 3 , 4 We excluded non‐NME drugs, biologics regulated by the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER), biosimilars, generic drugs, and over‐the‐counter products. For products in this cohort, we previously retrospectively identified regulatory and review characteristics (Appendix S1), and safety‐related label changes from approval date through June 30, 2018. 2 , 3 , 4 For this analysis, we expanded our data to include safety‐related labeling changes through December 31, 2022. To identify postmarketing safety issues, we determined when safety‐related label changes occurred for each product using publicly available FDA databases: Drugs@FDA, 18 the Drug Safety‐related Labeling Changes database, 19 and the MedWatch Medical Product Safety Information archive. 20 We compared each updated version of labeling to the preceding labeling to identify new safety issues in the following safety‐related sections: Boxed Warning, Contraindications, Warnings and Precautions, Adverse Reactions, and Drug Interactions. We considered as new safety issues any safety‐related change in one or more of these sections representing a new, not previously identified adverse reaction, medication error, or decrease in effectiveness. An issue added to more than one section during a labeling update was counted once. We excluded new terms that were synonymous with prior terms or added when a new indication was added based on new data, regardless of source. In contrast to our earlier studies, 2 , 3 , 4 we also excluded terms that expand on other safety issues in the labeling. See Appendix S2. Safety issues were abstracted from labeling by four independent reviewers (EP, EZ, IB, BD) and independently reviewed by a second team of reviewers with clinical training and expertise in MedDRA 21 coding (SB, MK). Discrepancies in labeling wording or interpretation of eligibility criteria were discussed and resolved. All safety issues were coded using MedDRA; Preferred terms (PTs) were used for analysis. We excluded from the main analysis safety issues appearing only in the Contraindications or Drug Interactions sections because MedDRA does not specify treatment settings, patient populations, or comorbid conditions typically included in these sections. We report these findings separately.

We describe the frequency of new safety issues for NMEs and NTBs overall and by labeling section (Boxed Warning, Warnings and Precautions, and Adverse Reactions). We rank the 20 most frequent terms for each labeling section and list ties in alphabetical order. When ties create rank lists with more than 20 terms, we indicate this with a final rank of “20+.” For these 20 adverse reactions not in the initially approved labeling, we evaluated the timing of the labeling changes, relative to the approval date, using boxplots with a Kruskal–Wallis test.

We identified a subset of drugs used primarily in the outpatient setting and dispensed via retail pharmacies by using IQVIA's proprietary National Sales Perspective™ database to determine products for which manufacturer sales to retail or mail‐order pharmacies comprised 50% or more of total sales. For each of these products, we used IQVIA's proprietary National Prescription Audit™ database to obtain the monthly estimated number of prescriptions dispensed from US outpatient retail and mail‐order pharmacies from October 2002 through December 2022. Both generic and branded products were included. We then determined the number of dispensed prescriptions at the time an adverse reaction was added to the labeling as well as six and 12 months before that date. Three timepoints are presented to account for the variable time from safety signal identification to their incorporation into the labeling.

Data analysis was performed using the SAS software, 22 version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and Microsoft Excel (Version 1808). Statistical significance was determined using a p value of <0.05.

RESULTS

Our study included 339 products: 278 NMEs and 61 NTBs. The regulatory characteristics of these products have been previously described. 3

The follow‐up time since approval ranged from 3.1 to 20.2 years (median 13.1 years, interquartile range, 10.0 to 16.9 years). Three hundred (88.5%) of products [241 (86.7%) of NMEs and 59 (96.7%) of NTBs] had a safety labeling change in the follow‐up period. Products had an average 4.2 ± 3.7 labeling updates and 15.2 ± 16.3 safety issues added. NMEs had an average 4.1 ± 3.7 labeling updates and 14.3 ± 15.6 safety issues added and NTBs had an average 4.6 ± 3.8 labeling updates and 19.4 ± 18.7 safety issues added.

Of 5,161 new safety issues added to any of the five safety‐related sections of the label, with 4,781 new safety issues added to the Boxed Warning, Warnings and Precautions, and Adverse Reactions sections. The most frequently updated section of the labeling was Adverse Reactions, with 3,420 (66%) safety issues appearing in this section (Appendix S3 , Figure S1 ); simultaneous the Boxed Warning and Warnings and Precautions sections were updated with the same issue, in 20 and 449 of the labeling updates, respectively. In 15 labeling updates the Boxed Warning. Warnings and Precautions, and Adverse Reactions sections had the same issue added. The proportion of new safety issues added to the Boxed Warning and Warnings and Precautions sections was higher for NTBs, while the proportion of issues added to the Adverse Reactions, Contraindications and Drug Interactions sections was higher for NMEs (Appendix S3 , Table S1 ). Safety issues found only in the Contraindications or Drug Interactions sections are summarized in Appendix S3 , Table S2 .

The 20 most frequently added adverse reactions are presented in Table 1 . Angioedema, Anaphylactic reaction, Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS), Hypersensitivity, and Toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) account for the five most frequently added adverse reactions of the combined group of NMEs and NTBs as well as for NMEs; for NTBs, Cardiac failure replaces TEN among the five most frequently added adverse reactions. These issues were added to the labeling of a substantial number of products, with Angioedema and Anaphylactic reaction added to the labeling of more than 25% of products and SJS and Hypersensitivity each added to the labeling of more than 15% of products. Cardiac failure was added to the labeling of more than 15% of NTBs. Most other safety issues were added to fewer than 10% of product labelings. The most frequently added adverse reaction by dosage form are summarized in Appendix S3 , Table S3 .

The number of adverse reactions added to the Boxed Warning section of the labeling was small (n = 150), accounting for only 3% of labeling additions, with any individual PT added to the Boxed Warning of no more than five products. Among adverse reactions added to the Boxed Warning section, hepatobiliary disorders (Hepatitis B reactivation, Hepatic failure, Hepatotoxicity, and Hepatitis fulminant) were more frequent among NMEs, while infections (e.g., Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, Fungal infection, Aspergillus infection, Blastomycosis, Candida infection, Legionella infection, Listeriosis, Opportunistic infection, and Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia) were more frequent among NTBs. The most common adverse reactions added to the Warnings and Precautions section (Appendix S3 , Table S4 ) were similar to those for all three labeling sections together (Table 1 ). In the Adverse Reactions section (Appendix S3 , Table S4 ), Rash was among the five most frequent adverse reactions added to the labeling of NMEs.

Across the 20 most frequent adverse reactions, there was a variability in time to labeling change, with the median time ranging from 3.5 (Angioedema) to 7.2 (Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS)) years after approval (Figure 1 ). For these adverse reactions, there was variability across products in the time to the labeling change, ranging from within the first year of approval to more than 18 years after approval. Among 85 products that had Anaphylactic reaction added after approval, 44 drugs (51.8%) had Hypersensitivity in the original labeling. Among 62 products that had SJS or TEN added after approval, 51 products (82.3%) had Rash in the original labeling (Appendix S3 , Table S5 ).

For each of the 20 most frequently added adverse reactions, there was substantial variation across 110 drugs used primarily in the outpatient setting in the cumulative number of dispensed prescriptions at the time the adverse reaction was added to the labeling, as well as six and 12 months earlier (Figure 2 ). The median number of dispensed prescriptions per product at the time the labeling was updated ranged from 878,616 (DRESS) to 17.9 million (Vomiting). One year before the update, the median number of dispensed prescriptions ranged from 285,676 (Dyspnoea) to 14.8 million (Vomiting). For all adverse reactions, the 25th percentile was above 75,000 prescriptions one year before the update and above 136,000 prescriptions at all subsequent timepoints (Figure 3 ).

DISCUSSION

Among 339 novel medicines we found the adverse reactions most frequently added to the product labeling were Anaphylactic reaction, Angioedema, SJS, Hypersensitivity, and TEN. This pattern was similar between NMEs and NTBs, except for Cardiac Failure, which was among the five most frequent adverse reactions added to the labeling of NTBs. Each of these adverse reactions was added to the labeling of at least 12% of the 339 products. Most other adverse reactions were added to 10% or fewer product labelings.

Many of the most frequently added adverse reactions are the so‐called Type B adverse drug reactions 23 —adverse reactions that are generally uncommon, not related to the pharmacological action of the drug, not dose‐related, unpredictable, and potentially fatal. Many of these reactions are immune‐mediated, as classified under the Gell and Coombs hypersensitivity framework, though not all represent true IgE‐mediated drug allergies. Serious cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) such as SJS and TEN have well‐characterized immunological mechanisms. In contrast, the pathogenesis of anaphylaxis extends beyond the classical IgE‐mediated pathway with evidence of non‐IgE‐mediated mechanisms that can clinically mimic IgE‐mediated reactions. 24

Less commonly, non‐IgE‐mediated anaphylaxis, including Mas‐related G protein‐coupled receptor X2 (MRGPRX2)‐related, complement‐mediated, cytokine storm‐like, and mixed reactions (IgE and cytokine storm‐like reactions), has been reported with various therapeutic drugs. 25 An IgG‐mediated mechanism of anaphylaxis, though demonstrated in murine models, remains controversial in humans. 26 A prospective human study suggested the IgG‐neutrophil pathway may be a marker of anaphylaxis severity or function as an alternative or concurrent mechanism for anaphylaxis in conjunction with the IgE‐mediated pathway. 27

Anaphylaxis is also classified as immunologic (includes IgE and non‐IgE reactions) and non‐immunologic (e.g., direct mast cell activation). Cases with an unknown mechanism are considered idiopathic and a diagnosis of exclusion. 28 Understanding the different mechanisms for anaphylaxis can inform management decisions and clinical outcomes. Skin testing to the therapeutic agent, if indicated, is a common strategy helpful in risk stratification. The approach to rechallenge, graded drug challenge, desensitization depends on the availability of alternative therapies and the severity of the underlying disease, particularly when no equally efficacious alternative therapies exist (e.g., chemotherapeutics). 29

Of the 339 novel medicines analyzed, 61 (18%) were approved as therapeutic biologics. These agents, primarily monoclonal antibodies or soluble fusion receptors, are derived from living cells, tissues, or organisms. 30 The number of approved monoclonal antibodies has steadily increased. Despite advances in engineering fully human or humanized monoclonal antibodies, immunogenicity remains a concern. 31 Like other therapeutic agents, biologics can cause adverse drug reactions and unexpected hypersensitivity reactions. However, unlike small molecule drugs, biologics resemble host proteins instead of synthetic chemicals, undergo proteolytic degradation instead of metabolic processing, and are administered parenterally, often via prolonged infusions. Notably, biologics are designed to exert immune‐mediated effects, which can lead to unintended adverse drug reactions. Unlike for more established therapeutic drugs, the utility of diagnostic testing (e.g., skin testing, in vitro testing) for hypersensitivity reactions to biologic agents remains undetermined. 32

Excipients, defined by FDA as any component of a drug product other than the active ingredient, are often overlooked but can cause serious immediate and delayed hypersensitivity reactions. 33 Vaccine‐associated anaphylaxis is most commonly caused by excipients; for drugs and therapeutic biologics, excipient allergy is uncommon and likely underrecognized. One exception, recently identified, 34 is that excipients, rather than corticosteroids themselves, have been implicated as the primary cause of corticosteroid‐associated anaphylaxis. 30 , 35 Excipients are included in drugs and therapeutic biologics for various reasons including, but not limited to, increased stability and preservation of the active ingredient. This is especially important for therapeutic biologics, which are less stable than small‐molecule drugs and are often developed in liquid form to be used for parental routes of administration. A clear mechanism for hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis, for therapeutic biologics has not been established apart from alpha‐gal allergy and cetuximab. However, excipients with allergenic potential are recognized as one of several drug‐related risk factors for hypersensitivity associated with therapeutic biologics. 36

Severe drug hypersensitivity reactions, such as drug‐induced anaphylaxis and SCARs, which include SJS and TEN, are rare occurrences. For example, in western countries, the estimated incidence of anaphylaxis is 8–50 cases per 100,000 person‐years, though the true incidence of drug‐induced anaphylaxis is not known. 37 The reported incidence of SJS and TEN is similarly low, with estimates ranging between 1.4 and 6 per million person‐years. 38 While severe immune‐mediated drug reactions are rare, immune‐mediated adverse drug reactions of any severity are a common reason for visits to emergency departments. In the United States in 2013–2014, the incidence of adverse drug reactions of any type was 4.0 visits per 1,000 population (95% CI: 3.1–5.0), of which 26.0% were immunologically mediated, with 9.2% resulting in hospitalization. 39 The incidence of emergency department visits for adverse drug reactions among children 18 years and younger was 2 per 1,000 persons (95% CI: 1.5, 2.6) with 35% attributed, by history, to drug allergy. 35 .

Adverse event reports are the most common source of data that give rise to post‐approval safety‐related labeling updates. 11 , 40 Each report describes an adverse event experienced by an individual person. When a report contains sufficient relevant details, a likely causal relationship between a drug and an adverse event can be inferred most reliably if the event has a low spontaneous background rate in the population, is generally caused by drug or toxin exposure, occurs in relatively close temporal proximity to drug exposure, and for which other reasonable explanations have been excluded. Many of the most frequently added adverse reactions have these characteristics. For example, many drug hypersensitivity reactions are generally the result of exposure to a drug or other inciting agent, occur within minutes to weeks of initiation drug, have low background rates, and can be attributed to a drug when other potential causes are excluded. In certain cases, similar reasoning can be used to identify a likely causal relationship between a drug and adverse events with longer onset latencies, such as liver injury, kidney injury, rhabdomyolysis, thrombocytopenia, and progressive multifocal encephalopathy. Although these reports can be sufficient to establish a likely causal relationship between a therapeutic agent and an adverse reaction, they generally do not allow for elucidation of the underlying pathogenic mechanism. Thus, for example, they generally cannot distinguish among the various mechanisms that give rise to hypersensitivity reactions.

The placement within the labeling of adverse drug reactions varied across adverse drug reactions and, for a given adverse drug reaction, across drug products. Anaphylactic reaction was added to 93 (27.4%) labelings but was added to the Boxed Warning section of 4 (1.2%) labelings; it was most frequently added to the Adverse Reactions section (63 labelings). By contrast, Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy was added to the labeling of 13 products; in four cases, it was added to the Boxed Warning section, and in seven cases, it was added to the Warnings and Precautions section. Because many factors, including the seriousness, severity, and frequency of the adverse reaction and the strength of the causal association, influence the placement of a particular adverse drug reaction within the labeling, it is not surprising that a given adverse reaction term is placed in one section of the labeling for one product and in a different section for another product. 38 , 41

The temporal and drug utilization data presented address the question of when previously unknown safety‐related issues are added to the drug labeling. The timing of addition of a particular adverse reaction to the product labeling varied across drugs, with median times ranging between three and seven years across adverse reactions. Across the 20 most frequently added adverse reactions, there is no single inflection point after which new safety issues are no longer added to the label. Similarly, the extent of drug utilization, measured by the cumulative number of dispensed outpatient prescriptions, varied across individual adverse reactions. The variability in cumulative prescription numbers at time of labeling update spans multiple orders of magnitude even within a single adverse reaction category, though the median utilization is above 1,000,000 prescriptions for 19 of the top 20 adverse reactions evaluated. Only DRESS, with a median of 878,618 dispensed prescriptions at the time of labeling update, was below 1,000,000 prescriptions. Looking at 6 and 12 months before the labeling change, 4 (20%) and 5 (25%) of the top 20 adverse events had a median utilization below 1,000,000 prescriptions. Although the time from safety issue identification to labeling change is variable, the dispensing data at 12 months before the labeling change serve as an estimate of the number of exposures when the safety signal was detected. We cannot conclude there is a utilization threshold beyond which new adverse events are detected.

Several factors could explain these findings. First, adverse drug reactions that are rare, such as Type B reactions, may not appear or be identified until a large number of people have used the drug. Second, the reliance of the pharmacovigilance system on case reports of adverse events requires that someone at the point of care, such as a physician, nurse, pharmacist, or patient, suspect that the event could be an adverse drug reaction and that this person report it to either the drug manufacturer or to FDA. While for events such as drug hypersensitivity reactions, there may be a high degree of suspicion, for other events, the level of suspicion may be lower, and thus less likely to generate a report of an adverse event. In addition, reporting an adverse event is a time‐consuming process, requiring an estimated 40 min to complete. 42 It is estimated that only 5 to 31% of adverse events are reported. 43 , 44 , 45 Even when reports are submitted, they often lack critical details necessary for causal inference. 46 Finally, while there is no pre‐specified number of well‐documented cases needed to conclude that the observed events are indeed adverse drug reactions and should be added to the product labeling, in general, a case series with several cases is usually necessary. 47 For rare events, it may take time for these cases to accumulate. Newer methods that do not rely on individual adverse event reports but rather use large healthcare databases to identify potential adverse drug reactions more quickly are being developed and piloted. 48 , 49 , 50

Drug development programs of novel medicines generally identify comprehensively the common adverse drug reactions associated with the drug. The median number of patients studied for safety in a drug development program for non‐orphan indications is 1,606, a number that allows identification of common adverse events. 2 It is important for clinicians to be aware of both the common adverse drug reactions as well as the less common ones. Because many of the adverse drug reactions added to a drug's labeling after approval—and after a large numbers of patients have taken the drug—are not related to the drug's pharmacological action and are not dose‐related, it is important for clinicians to check the most updated product labeling. 14 Similarly, it is also important for clinicians to report suspected adverse drug reactions, especially those that are not listed in the product's labeling, as drug safety surveillance depends in large part on careful observations made at the point of care.

This study has several strengths. First, it is the first study to our knowledge that comprehensively evaluates the actual adverse drug reactions added in the postmarket period to any safety‐related section of the labeling, rather than only to the Boxed Warning section. Second, we analyzed a large number of products, with up to 20 years of post‐approval follow‐up to detect safety issues. Third, we used MedDRA coding of safety issues to standardize the most frequent adverse reactions.

Several limitations are also worth considering. Although MedDRA coding of adverse drug reactions can add value through standardization, it does not adequately capture many of the safety issues represented in either the Contraindications or Drug Interactions section, limiting our ability to fully assess these sections. Because the time from safety issue identification to labeling change is variable, the labeling change does not characterize when a safety signal was first noted. 44 Because we excluded safety issues, regardless of source, added at the time a new indication was added, we may have missed some safety issues added based on postmarketing surveillance. In this study, we did determine the source of the labeling changes or when the signal of for an adverse reaction was initially identified. We have previously shown that safety reports from drug manufacturer safety databases and FAERS, and class‐labeling changes triggered safety‐related labeling changes more than any other sources. 11 , 40 Additionally, the data for some of our analyses became sparse when small subsets were evaluated (e.g., Boxed Warning section), which limits our ability to draw conclusions regarding safety issues that appear infrequently.

These findings provide valuable insight into the type of safety issues that arise in the course of marketed use of the product. Knowing that serious hypersensitivity reactions are among the most commonly detected postmarketing issues and may be identified only many years after approval, will alert clinicians to the possibility of such reactions. Practitioners should be encouraged to consult the most current version of a product's labeling throughout its use, and to maintain clinical vigilance for adverse drug reactions regardless of how long a drug has been on the market.
