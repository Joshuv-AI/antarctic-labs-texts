An exploratory study on spousal involvement in antenatal care and labor: antenatal mothers’ perspectives
Authors: Ilerioluwa Taiwo Adeniyi, Deborah Tolulope Esan, Adetunmise Oluseyi Olajide, Carlos Guillermo Ramos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpousal involvement in maternal healthcare is a critical strategy for reducing maternal and child mortality. However, husband involvement in antenatal care (ANC) and labor remains low in Nigeria. This study explored the perceptions of antenatal mothers regarding spousal involvement in ANC and labor.MethodsAn exploratory qualitative study design was used. Participants were recruited via purposive sampling, with 12 antenatal mothers participating in two focus group discussions (FGDs) conducted at a tertiary health institution in Nigeria. Data were collected using semi-structured interviews, audio-recorded for accuracy, and analyzed using a thematic approach.ResultsFour themes emerged regarding spousal involvement: limited partner involvement in ANC, active male participation in preparing delivery items and transportation, absent husbands during labor, and desire for husband involvement in the labor room. Three themes described the perceived effects of male involvement: alleviation of physical workload, emotional support, and positive emotional impact. Barriers to male involvement included negative maternal attitudes, partner fear or discomfort, and work-related constraints.ConclusionAntenatal mothers viewed male involvement as critical for improving maternal and newborn outcomes. Cultural and religious barriers, however, hindered such involvement. Addressing these barriers is essential to promote active male participation.

Introduction

Pregnancy and childbirth are transformative experiences, often accompanied by excitement, anticipation, and challenges. Traditionally, antenatal care (ANC) and labor support have been viewed as the sole responsibility of women However, in Southwest Nigeria, cultural and societal norms often position pregnancy and childbirth primarily as women’s responsibilities, with men’s participation in ANC and labor limited by traditional gender roles, societal expectations, and perceptions of masculinity. Research highlights the critical role of male involvement in improving maternal and child health outcomes, such as increased ANC utilization, better birth preparedness, and enhanced emotional support for pregnant women [1–3].

The World Health Organization emphasizes that antenatal care (ANC) is vital for promoting positive maternal health outcomes [4]. Active husband involvement in ANC has been associated with increased attendance at prenatal visits, higher uptake of recommended maternal health services, and improved birth outcomes, including higher infant birth weight and maternal psychological well-being [1, 3].

Despite its benefits, male involvement in ANC and labor remains inconsistent, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Barriers include deeply rooted cultural norms, limited awareness of the benefits of male participation, traditional gender roles, and time constraints [5–7]. Understanding maternal perspectives on facilitators and barriers to male involvement is essential for designing culturally appropriate and effective interventions.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the perceptions of antenatal mothers regarding spousal involvement in ANC and labor in a tertiary health facility in Ekiti State, Nigeria, providing insights to inform future interventions and policy development in the region.

Methods

Research design

This study employed a qualitative exploratory research design to gain an in-depth understanding of antenatal women’s perspectives on husbands’ involvement in antenatal care and labor in a tertiary health facility in Ekiti State.

Research setting

The study was conducted at a tertiary hospital, in a semi-urban city in Ekiti State, in Southwest Nigeria. The hospital is one of the two foremost government owned tertiary hospitals in the State. It serves as the primary referral center for healthcare institutions throughout Ekiti State. The hospital encompasses 24 operational departments, including 18 dedicated to clinical care and 6 providing support services. It boasts a 280-bed capacity across various wards, including male and female surgical and medical wards, pediatrics, accident and emergency, psychiatry, maternal and neonatal care, and dedicated surgical and medical outpatient departments. Additionally, it features functional units for renal care, cardiology, intensive care, ear, nose, and throat (ENT), and ophthalmology.

The maternity unit provides comprehensive maternal and neonatal care, including antenatal, labor, and postnatal services. Labor rooms are equipped with essential monitoring equipment and delivery facilities, ensuring privacy for patients. The unit operates continuously with a team of midwives and obstetricians who follow standard hospital protocols for admission, labor monitoring (including use of the partograph), and postpartum care. Emergency obstetric services, such as cesarean section and other life-saving interventions, are available on-site, allowing for prompt management of complications.

Study population

This study consisted of women attending the antenatal clinic at the hospital’s Obstetrics & Gynecology department. These women were receiving prenatal care for their pregnancies.

Sample size

This study aimed to achieve data saturation. Data saturation is a concept in qualitative research where collecting additional data yields no new significant insights [8].

The study did not predetermine a fixed sample size; instead, data collection followed the principle of saturation [8, 9]. Participants were grouped by shared characteristics, including preferred language (Yoruba or English) and similar ANC experiences, to enhance FGD homogeneity and encourage open discussion.

Two FGDs of six participants each were conducted. Data collection and preliminary analysis occurred concurrently, and after the second FGD, no new themes emerged, indicating saturation. This aligns with qualitative research showing that two to three homogeneous FGDs can achieve saturation for focused topics [9, 10]. The final sample of 12 antenatal women was adequate to address the study objectives.

Sampling technique

Purposive sampling techniques were employed to select participants most suited to address the research questions. Participants included 12 antenatal mothers attending the Obstetrics and Gynecology department of the hospital. Inclusion criteria included pregnant women fluent in Yoruba or English and receiving ANC at the facility. Women unwilling to consent or with language barriers were excluded.

Instrument for data collection

The study employed Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) as the primary method for gathering data. A semi-structured FGD interview guide was self-developed by the research team based on literature on male involvement in ANC and labor. The guide was reviewed by two maternal health experts to ensure content validity. A pilot FGD with four antenatal mothers at a nearby primary health center was conducted, and the guide was refined for clarity and appropriateness prior to the main study.

Method of data collection

Participants were provided with written informed consent forms, reviewed their rights (including the right to withdraw at any point), and signed the forms if they agreed to participate. The researcher acted as the moderator for each FGD, using the semi-structured guide to stimulate discussion while allowing for participant-driven exploration of relevant themes. Two FGDs, each comprising six participants, were conducted in a private room within the hospital’s Obstetrics & Gynecology department. Each session lasted approximately 60 min, was audio-recorded with participants’ consent, and supplemented by detailed field notes. This dual approach ensured comprehensive data collection and minimized the risk of losing valuable information during transcription.

Method of data analysis

Data were analyzed using framework analysis [11]. This involved familiarization with transcripts, identifying themes, indexing, charting data, and interpretation. The iterative process ensured a comprehensive understanding of the findings. This approach involves a systematic, five-stage process:

Familiarization: The researcher began by immersing themselves in the data, transcribing the FGD recordings verbatim and carefully reviewing the field notes. This process allowed for efficient data organization and retrieval.Charting and Mapping: The coded data was then compiled into charts that mapped the themes across all FGD transcripts. This visual representation facilitated the identification of patterns and relationships within the data.Interpretation: Finally, the researcher interpreted the identified themes considering the research questions and existing literature. Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics and Research Committee of the hospital with ethical number (ERC/2018/11/26/159B). Prior to participation, all individuals provided written informed consent. This process ensured participants fully understood the study’s objectives, their right to withdraw at any time, and the voluntary nature of their participation. Measures were taken to safeguard participants’ privacy. Audio recordings were only made during FGDs with explicit participant permission. These recordings solely served the purpose of aiding in accurate transcription and report writing. They were securely stored and accessed only by the researchers. Names or any identifying details were not collected. The data and findings presented in this manuscript have not been published elsewhere.

Findings/results

Participants were aged 30–32 years on average, with an average of 2.5 children. Most were married (92%) and identified as Christian (83%). Gestational ages ranged from 20 to over 35 weeks. Participants represented a mix of educational backgrounds and employment statuses, reflecting typical ANC attendees at the facility.

Themes on male involvement in pregnancy and labor

Limited partner involvement in ANC

Many mothers reported that their husbands were absent from ANC visits due to work commitments, traditional gender roles, or perceived irrelevance of their presence.

Discussion

This study significantly expanded on male involvement in ANC and labor, providing in-depth insights into cultural, emotional, and practical dimensions.

The study revealed a significant concern: limited partner involvement in antenatal care. Many men are not actively engaged in their partner’s pregnancy care, despite the established link between limited partner involvement in antenatal care and adverse maternal and child health outcomes. Similar findings have been reported in studies from Nigeria [12] and Kenya [13]. In contrast, research conducted in Sweden highlights high levels of male involvement [14]. Participants’ perception of ANC as solely a woman’s responsibility reinforces gender stereotypes and limits male participation, highlighting the need for community education and awareness campaigns.

These results emphasize the influence of cultural and societal factors on male participation in antenatal care, underscoring the urgency of interventions to address limited partner involvement in antenatal care and promote gender-inclusive antenatal care [13]. Work commitments and busy schedules were also identified as significant barriers to male involvement in ANC. Participants noted that their husbands were often unable to attend antenatal appointments due to work obligations. Studies from Tanzania and Ghana report similar challenges, highlighting the need for flexible work policies and gender-sensitive interventions to encourage male participation [15, 16]. Additionally, research from the United States revealed that men with flexible work arrangements were more likely to engage in antenatal care [17]. These findings highlight the critical need for workplace policies that support male involvement.

Another noteworthy finding was that many husbands only attended antenatal appointments during critical moments, such as ultrasound scans or complications. This behavior suggests that men often view antenatal care as primarily a woman’s responsibility, becoming involved only when absolutely necessary. Similar trends have been observed in studies from Malawi and Zambia [18, 19]. Research from the United Kingdom has demonstrated that consistent male involvement in antenatal care fosters stronger connections between partners and their baby [20]. Therefore, interventions that encourage male participation beyond critical moments are essential for improving maternal and child health outcomes.

Despite these challenges, the study found that men actively participated in tasks traditionally viewed as masculine, such as preparing delivery items and providing transportation to healthcare facilities. This aspect of male involvement aligns with findings from studies in Nigeria, Ghana, and the United States, which highlighted that men who engaged in these activities reported higher levels of satisfaction and engagement during the birth process [12, 16, 17]. These results suggest that encouraging male involvement in areas where they feel confident and capable is an effective strategy to foster continued engagement in maternal care.

However, the study revealed limited male presence in the labor room during delivery. Similar findings have been reported in Tanzania and Uganda, where cultural norms and fear of the labor process deter male participation [15, 21]. Conversely, studies in Australia and the United Kingdom indicate that men who were present in the labor room reported greater satisfaction and stronger emotional connections with their partners [22, 23]. Women in this study expressed a desire for male presence during labor, emphasizing that it fosters empathy and strengthens partner relationships [24–26].

Male involvement also significantly alleviated women’s physical workload, as demonstrated in this study. Similar findings were reported in studies from Kenya, South Africa, and India, where male participation reduced physical strain and improved mental health outcomes for women [25, 27, 28]. Additionally, male involvement enhanced emotional well-being by providing psychological support, love, and empathy, which reduced stress and anxiety during pregnancy and childbirth. Research from Tanzania, Uganda, and the United Kingdom corroborates these findings, linking male involvement to reduced postpartum depression and improved maternal emotional health [15, 21, 23].

Barriers to male involvement in antenatal care and labor identified in this study included negative maternal attitudes, partner fear or discomfort, and workload constraints. Some women believed that pregnancy and childbirth were exclusively their responsibilities and felt that men lacked the emotional and physical capacity to handle the process. Similar findings have been reported in Nigeria and Ghana, where cultural beliefs and societal norms heavily influence these attitudes [12, 16]. Addressing these perceptions through education, community engagement, and targeted interventions is critical.

Partner fear and discomfort were also significant barriers. For example, men’s fear of medical procedures and limited knowledge about childbirth hindered their participation. Studies from India and the Democratic Republic of Congo echo these findings, emphasizing the need for education and counseling to mitigate these fears [29, 30]. Additionally, women perceived that their partners’ work commitments and inflexible schedules took precedence over antenatal care. Research from Ghana supports this, highlighting the importance of addressing gender roles and promoting a balance between work and family responsibilities [16].

Overall, the findings underscore the importance of multi-level interventions, including policy changes, community education, and couple-focused strategies, to enhance male participation in ANC and labor, ultimately improving maternal and child health outcomes.

Limitation of the study

The study was conducted with a small sample of 12 antenatal mothers from a single health facility in Nigeria. While their perspectives provide valuable insights, the findings may not be representative of the broader population, particularly in diverse cultural or socioeconomic settings across the country. Future research should focus on multi-site studies and interventional programs.

Conclusion

Antenatal mothers perceive male involvement as essential for maternal and newborn health. Addressing cultural, religious, and practical barriers is crucial.
