Lower-dimensional posterior density and cluster summaries for overparameterized Bayesian models
Henrique Bolfarine, Hedibert F. Lopes, Carlos M. Carvalho

Abstract
The usefulness of Bayesian models for density and cluster estimation is well established across multiple literatures. However, there is still a known tension between the use of simpler, more interpretable models and more flexible, complex ones. In this paper, we propose a novel method that integrates these two approaches by projecting the fit of a flexible, over-parameterized model onto a lower-dimensional parametric summary, which serves as a surrogate. This process increases interpretability while preserving most of the fit of the original model. Our approach involves three main steps. First, we fit the data using nonparametric or over-parameterized models. Second, we project the posterior predictive distribution of the original model onto a sequence of parametric summary estimates using a decision-theoretic approach. Finally, given the lower parametric dimension of the summary estimate that best approximates the original model learned in the second step, we construct uncertainty quantification for the summary by projecting the original full posterior distribution. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our method in summarizing a variety of nonparametric and overparameterized models, providing uncertainty quantification for both density and cluster summaries on synthetic and real datasets.



Keywords:
model summarization, decision theory, density estimation, clustering, overparameterized, nonparametric.

1
Introduction

The usefulness of Bayesian models for density and cluster estimation is well established across various disciplines, including medicine, biology, engineering, and others
(see
Fruhwirth-Schnatter et al. 2019
;
McLachlan et al. 2019
;
Wade 2023
, and references therein)
. However, there is still a well-known tension between the flexibility and the interpretability of these models. For example, parametric low-dimensional models, such as finite mixture models
(
McLachlan and Peel 2000
;
Frühwirth-Schnatter 2006
, FMM;)
, excel in interpretability, as they represent complex distributions as finite weighted sums of known distributions. More formally, a
d
-dimensional observation
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, originates from a finite mixture model with
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
components if it is drawn from a density of the form

f(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{q=1}^{K}\omega_{q}f(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q}),

(1)

where
\omega=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{K})
are positive weights such that
\sum_{q=1}^{K}\omega_{q}=1
, and
f(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q})
depicts a component-specific kernel density, with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K}
, and where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{K};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{K})
. Gaussian kernels are a common choice for
f(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q})
, resulting in Gaussian mixture models (GMM).

As is well known, the FMM presented in Equation
1
can also be expressed using latent variables
z_{i}=q
, for
q\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}
, which indicate the component allocation for each observation. The model can then be written as
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}|z_{i}\sim f(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z_{i}})
, with probability of allocation given by
\mathbb{P}(z_{i}=q)=\omega_{q}
. Under this representation, the usefulness of mixture models for clustering becomes readily apparent.

Although straightforward to interpret due to its modular nature, FMMs may produce biased estimates due to restrictive modeling assumptions. Thus, potentially failing to fully capture the complexity of the underlying distribution.

In contrast, nonparametric models, such as the Dirichlet process mixture
(
Ferguson 1983
;
Escobar and West 1995
, DPM;)
and random Bernstein polynomials
(
Petrone 1999b
;
Petrone 1999a
, BP;)
, allow for a more flexible and accurate representation of the data. In Dirichlet process mixture models, one of the most popular approaches,
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
is an observation from
f(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
is drawn from a discrete distribution
G
, itself defined by a Dirichlet process,
DP(\alpha,G_{0})
, with concentration parameter
\alpha>0
and base distribution
G_{0}
. In this setting, the distribution
G
serves as the mixing measure,
G=\sum_{i\geq 1}\omega_{i}\delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2},\dots
are a sequence of random variables drawn from
G_{0}
,
\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\dots
are random weights such as
\sum_{i\geq 1}\omega_{i}=1
, and
\delta_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}}
is the Dirac measure
(
Müller et al. 2015
)
. This formulation highlights the flexibility of Dirichlet process mixture models in modeling the data, as there is no restriction on the number of parameters. Nonetheless, it is well known that Dirichlet process mixtures and similar approaches, can generate models with an excessive number of components, leading to challenges in interpretability and biased estimates
(
Miller and Harrison 2013
;
Miller and Harrison 2014
)
.

In this paper, we propose a method that addresses the aforementioned issues by connecting simple, interpretable models to complex, nonparametric approaches. In a nutshell, we take advantage of the flexibility and predictive power of the overparameterized and nonparametric models for density and cluster estimation and project the resulting fit onto a lower-dimensional parametric summary or surrogate. As a result, we obtain an interpretable, parsimonious summary that still preserves some of the fit and predictive accuracy of the original model. Furthermore, under our approach, we are able to measure the trade-off involved in performing such a projection and determine the minimal summary dimension necessary to capture the essential features of the underlying distribution without losing critical information.

The proposed method consists of three steps. In the first step, we estimate the model by adopting a flexible prior, which can be either nonparametric or overparameterized. In the second step, using a Bayesian decision-theoretic approach
(
Berger 2013
;
Hahn and Carvalho 2015
)
, we project the posterior predictive distribution of the reference model onto surrogates with different parametric dimensions. This process yields as sequence interpretable summary point estimates of varying dimensions that captures different levels of complexity relative to the full model. To select the lower-dimensional summary estimate that best approximates the fit of the original model, we propose a strategy that measures the divergence between the summary estimates and the reference or full model through its posterior predictive distribution. Finally, in the third step, given the summary dimension learned in the previous step, we construct uncertainty bounds by projecting the full posterior distribution onto the lower-dimensional posterior summary. This posterior summary is useful for inference and is well-suited to addressing various questions about the data and the underlying distribution. Moreover, it remains valid in the sense that the data is utilized only once, conditioned on the model’s posterior distribution.

(
Richardson and Green 1997
;

and sparse overparameterized finite mixture models
(
Malsiner-Walli et al. 2016
, SFM;)
.

1.1
Related Work

Our proposed method builds upon a rich literature based on decision analysis for posterior summarization.
MacEachern 2001
and
Gutiérrez-Peña and Walker 2005
used a similar approach to summarize nonparametric regression models.
Hahn and Carvalho 2015
used decision analysis to perform variable selection in regression models under sparse priors, extending the ideas from
Lindley 1968
. Similar techniques have also been adopted for seemingly unrelated regressions
(
Puelz et al. 2017
)
, graphical models
(
Bashir et al. 2019
)
, time-varying parameters
(
Huber et al. 2021
)
, and factor analysis
(
Bolfarine et al. 2024
)
, among others.
Woody et al. 2021
proposed projecting the posterior distribution of gaussian processes to a lower-dimensional posterior summary equipped with uncertainty quantification. This approach has since been successfully applied to create summaries for causal estimates for growth mindset intervention, as seen in
Yeager et al. 2019
, and more recently.
Fisher et al. 2024
, applied the procedure in estimate heterogeneous treatment effects in randomized trials with noncompliance, among others. Recently,
Buch et al. 2024
introduced a method for estimating level-set clustering using nonparametric surrogates, compared to this approach, our method provides a more flexible and modular alternative with the possibility of using different loss functions and summary classes for both density and cluster estimation.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we give an overview of our method for generating lower-dimensional posterior summaries for densities. In Section
3
, we discuss the choice of the loss function and the class of summaries. Given these conditions, we show how to obtain the sequence of optimal summary estimates and a method to select the lower-dimensional summary that best approximates the original model. In Section
4
, we show how to project the original posterior to generate uncertainty bounds for the summaries. In Section
5
we extend the procedure to include optimal and uncertainty quantification for cluster summaries. In Section
6
we apply our method to synthetic and real data under different models. In Section
7
, we end with a discussion.

2
Overview of Posterior Summarization for Densities

In Section
2.1
, we show how to obtain a sequence of summary estimates of varying dimensions that serve as a surrogates for the original model. In Section
2.2
, given the parametric dimension of the summary estimate that best approximates the original model in the previous step, we show how to project the full posterior distribution of the original model to generate a posterior summary equipped with uncertainty quantification for the surrogate.

2.1
Lower-Dimensional Summary Estimates for Densities

Consider
N
independent observations
\boldsymbol{y}=(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{N})^{T}
, where
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume that each
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
is generated from an unknown random variable
\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}
with probability distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, indexed by the parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
. Here, the goal is to learn
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot):=f(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\theta})
, the density associated with
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, based on the observed data.

In the first step of our method, we apply standard Bayesian procedures for making inferences, given a prior distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and a model for
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. We refer to this pair as the reference model. As a result, we obtain the posterior distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})\propto f(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, which serves as the basis for all inferences about
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. We refer to
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})
, as the full or original posterior distribution for
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. A natural choice for the prior
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is a nonparametric or overparameterized family of distributions to better capture the complexity of the data. However, as discussed in Section
1
, the posterior distribution under such priors can lead to overfitting and misleading results.

To address this challenge, in the second step for our method, we introduce a decision-theoretic approach
(
Berger 2013
;
Hahn and Carvalho 2015
)
to project the posterior predictive distribution yielded by the reference model, defined as
\tilde{f}(\cdot):=f(\boldsymbol{y}_{N+1}|\boldsymbol{y})
, with
f(\boldsymbol{y}_{N+1}|\boldsymbol{y})=\int f(\boldsymbol{y}_{N+1}|\boldsymbol{\theta})p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{\theta}
, onto a lower-dimensional summary
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in{\cal G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, where
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\cdot):=g(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\gamma})
, and where
{\cal G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}:=\{g(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\gamma});\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\}
is a class of density functions with the same support as
\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}
. The parameter of the summary,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, is the action in our decision-theoretic framework. This is a convenient choice, as parametric models, are typically preferred for their tractability and interpretability, especially in lower-dimensional settings

(
Gutiérrez-Peña and Walker 2005
;
Woody et al. 2021
)
.

Let
{\cal L}(\tilde{f},g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
denote the loss function that quantifies the discrepancy between the summary
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and
\tilde{f}
. In this context, the loss function is interpreted as the penalty incurred when selecting the summary
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, through
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, as an approximation for the model on the true density
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, under the lens of the posterior predictive distribution
\tilde{f}
. Different loss functions can be employed for this objective, including the Hellinger and squared distances
(
Marin et al. 2005
)
. In Section
3
, we define the loss function used in this paper.

Based on the preceding choices, selecting the optimal summary estimate for the reference model
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
resumes to selecting the action
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
that minimizes the expected posterior predictive loss

\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}:=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{y}}\left[\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f},g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\right]+\lambda P(\boldsymbol{\gamma}),

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}
represents the random variable generated from
\tilde{f}
,
P(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
denotes a regularization term that promotes model parsimony, and
\lambda>0
is a tuning parameter controlling the trade-off between model fit and complexity. From Equation (
2
), the optimal action is given by
\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}
, with the corresponding density summary given as
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\cdot):=g(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}})
.

In this paper, rather than using a penalty function in the minimization (
2
), we implement a greedy search across function spaces
\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}:=\{g(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{k});\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{k}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{k},k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\}
of varying parametric dimensions,
k\in\{1,2,\dots,K_{\max}\}
, where
K_{\max}
is chosen to be large enough to accommodate the complexity of the data. As a result, we generate a sequence of summary estimates,
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1},\dots,\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{K_{\max}}
, where
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}(\cdot):=g(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}^{k})
, with different levels of flexibility. We obtain the sequence of optimal actions as

\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}^{k}:=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{k}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{y}}\left[{\cal L}(\tilde{f},g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\right],\quad\text{for}\quad k=1,\dots,K_{\text{max}}.

(3)

It is important to note that in (
3
),
k
also serves as an action on our decision theoretic framework controlling the flexibility of the summary. A similar strategy was adopted by
Bolfarine et al. 2024
to generate summary estimates for factor loadings across different parametric dimensions. In Section
6
, we discuss conditions and a potential implementation where the use of regularization may benefit the summarization procedure.

The challenge now lies in selecting the summary estimate
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
given
k\in\{1,2,\dots,K_{\max}\}
that best approximates
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
through the posterior predictive density
\tilde{f}
. The chosen strategy depends on the type of model being used; for example, whether it is a regression model, functional data model, or graphical models, and whether a penalized loss function was used to obtain the estimates
(
Hahn and Carvalho 2015
;
Bashir et al. 2019
;
Woody et al. 2021
, see)
. The central idea behind the adopted strategy is to capture the decline in model fit through the summary estimates
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1},\dots,\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{K_{\max}}
and to establish a trade-off that balances the predictive power of
\tilde{f}
and the simplicity of
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
. In Section
3.4
, we present a procedure based on the Kullback-Leibler divergence
(
Kullback 1997
, KL;)
to enable this selection.

The method presented in this section is general and can be extended to different types of loss functions and different family of distributions for the summaries. More details on possible choices for the loss and class of summaries are given in Sections
3.1
, and
3.2
.

2.2
Posterior Summarization for densities

In previous summarization methods, such as those proposed by
Hahn and Carvalho 2015
;
Puelz et al. 2017
;
Bashir et al. 2019
;
Bolfarine et al. 2024
, the summary estimate was the final objective of the procedure. In this section, we extend our analysis by projecting the entire original posterior distribution onto a lower-dimensional summary, using the dimension of the summary estimate as a reference. This concept was first introduced by
Woody et al. 2021
to summarize the posterior distribution of Gaussian process regression in terms of linear and additive models. This approach facilitates interpretation, and uncertainty quantification through credible bounds, which is not feasible for the summary estimates obtained in the previous section.

Let
K^{*}\in\{1,2,\dots,K_{\text{max}}\}
be the dimension of the optimal summary estimate
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{K^{*}}
, where
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{K^{*}}(\cdot):=g(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{K^{*}})
, that provides the best trade-off between fit and simplicity, in relation to the reference model, and let
{\cal G}^{K^{*}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}:=\{g(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{K^{*}});\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{K^{*}}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{K^{*}}\}
, be the class of summary densities with this dimension. We construct the lower-dimensional posterior summary for densities, by projecting the posterior distribution of the reference model,
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})
, through the posterior predictive distribution
\tilde{f}
onto the summary. We obtain this projection by minimizing the loss between
\tilde{f}
and the summary density
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{K^{*}}\in{\cal G}^{K^{*}}
, using the same loss function and class of density summaries as in the process from Section
2.1
. The posterior projection is thus obtained by approximating
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{K^{*}}
in
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{K^{*}}
to match
\tilde{f}
over the entire posterior of the reference model
p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})
, by solving

\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}:=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{K^{*}}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{K^{*}}}\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f},g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{K^{*}}),

(4)

resulting in the lower-dimensional posterior summary, which we represent as
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}:=p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{K^{*}}|\boldsymbol{y})
, from which we obtain the posterior density summary
g(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime})
.

It is important to highlight the distinction between the summary estimate obtained from (
3
) and the lower-dimensional summary
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}
from (
4
), which is the lower-dimensional characterization of posterior distribution. These quantities are analogous to the Bayes estimator and the posterior distribution of a parameter, respectively. Moreover, as noted by
Woody et al. 2021
, the estimates obtained in (
3
) and lower posterior summary (
4
) are generally not equivalent

\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{y}}\left[\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f},g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\right]\neq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{y}}\left[\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\mathcal{L}(\tilde{f},g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\right].

The framework in this section is fundamental to our method. Basically, we establish inference over the distribution of interest through a lower-dimensional summary estimate. The novelty of our method lies in its generality, as it can be applied in situations where there are no covariates, and the target of inference is the latent structure of the underlying distribution.

In Sections
3
and
4
, we demonstrate how to obtain both the optimal summary estimate and the posterior summarization, defined in equations (
3
), and (
4
), respectively, using the negative log-likelihood loss and FMM as the class of summaries. In Section
5
, we extend the framework presented here for generating cluster summaries.

3
Finite Mixture Models Summary Estimates

In Sections
3.1
and
3.2
, we define the loss function and the class of summaries, respectively, used to approximate the reference model. In Section
3.3
, we introduce a numerical procedure to generate the summary estimates, and in Section
3.4
, we demonstrate how to select the summary estimate that best approximates the reference model.

3.1
Choice of the Loss function

Different loss functions can be used to measure the dissimilarity between the summary and the reference model. In this paper, we focus on the negative log-likelihood function defined as

{\cal L}(\tilde{f},g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=-\log g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}),

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}
is the random variable from the posterior predictive distribution
\tilde{f}
. The negative log-likelihood is an appropriate choice in this context, as it is known to possess optimal properties
(
Good 1992
)
.

Under the decision-theoretic formulation proposed in Section
2
, using the negative log-likelihood loss function and the class of functions
\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
, selecting the optimal summary estimate for the reference model is equivalent to selecting the action
\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}^{k}
that minimizes the
posterior predictive expected loss

\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}^{k}:=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{k}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{y}}\left[-\log g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})\right],\quad k=1,\dots,K_{\text{max}}.

(6)

Notably, minimizing equation (
6
) is equivalent, up to an additive constant, to minimizing the KL divergence between posterior predictive density
\tilde{f}
and the summary
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, where the KL divergence is defined as
\text{KL}(f\|g)=\int f\log\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)dy
. To demonstrate this, we express the expected posterior predictive loss in equation (
6
) as

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{y}}\left[-\log g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})\right]=-\int\log g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})d\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}=\text{KL}(\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})\|g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}))+\text{H}(\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})),

(7)

where
\text{H}(f)=-\int f\log f\,dy
is the entropy of
f
. In this case
\text{H}(f)
is a constant since it does not depend on
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

From the property in (
7
), we obtain the minimization of a forward KL divergence, which exhibits a mean-seeking behavior
(
Murphy 2012
)
. Under this scenario, we sample observations
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}
, for
n=1,\dots,\tilde{N}
, from
\tilde{f}
and aim to maximize the probability assigned by
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
to these points. More specifically, when
\tilde{f}
assigns a high probability to certain regions, the summary
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
must also assign a high density to these regions. This ensures that
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
adequately captures all modes and high-probability regions of
\tilde{f}
. This property is particularly important for our method, as these regions often correspond to modes or groups in the data, potentially indicating distinct clusters.

3.2
Finite Mixture Models Class of Summaries

In principle, there are no constraints on the form of the densities for
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, as long as they have the same support as
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
. It is up to the experimenter to determine the complexity of the summary density. We focus on the case where
\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
is chosen to be a class of FMM
(
McLachlan and Peel 2000
;
Frühwirth-Schnatter 2006
)
.

We thus update the functions in
\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
, which are still on the same support as
\boldsymbol{Y}_{i}
, but is now defined by the actions
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{k}=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{k};\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{k})
, and where
g(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{k})
is a mixture of densities with
k
components given as

g(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{k})=\sum_{q=1}^{k}\eta_{q}\,g_{q}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{q}),

(8)

where
0\leq\eta_{q}\leq 1
, for
q=1,\dots,k
, with the constraint
\sum_{q=1}^{k}\eta_{q}=1
, and where
g_{q}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{q})
represents the kernel densities defined by the summary parameters
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{q}
.

In this paper, we adopt Gaussian kernels for the summary throughout the numerical applications in Section
6
. However, under our approach, other kernel choices are possible depending on the type of data and the desired level of approximation.

3.3
Obtaining Finite Mixture Models Summary Estimates

Here we propose a procedure to solve the minimization problem in (
6
), as no simple closed-form solution exists under the FMM summary class introduced previously.

We approximate the expected loss function over the posterior predictive distribution in (
6
), by applying Monte Carlo integration
(
Gamerman and Lopes 2006
)
. Specifically, given a sample
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}=(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{\tilde{N}})^{T}
, from the posterior predictive distribution
\tilde{f}
we approximate the expected negative log-likelihood loss with respect to the FMM summary defined in (
8
), as

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{y}}\left[-\log g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})\right]=-\int\log g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})d\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}\approx-\frac{1}{\tilde{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{\tilde{N}}\log\sum_{q=1}^{k}\eta_{q}\,g_{q}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{q}).

Therefore, minimizing (
6
) under the above approximation is equivalent to maximizing the corresponding log-likelihood of the FMM summary. In particular, for a fixed
k
, we obtain the optimal set of parameters
\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}^{k}
as

\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}^{k}:=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{\tilde{N}}\log\sum_{q=1}^{k}\eta_{q}\,g_{q}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{q}),

(9)

resulting in of optimal summary estimate
\hat{g}^{k}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. By running this procedure for
k=1,\dots,K_{\text{max}}
provides different levels of approximation of the summary
\hat{g}^{k}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
to the reference model.

The action
k
plays an essential role in this framework, as it defines the complexity of the approximation relative to the reference model. As
k
increases, the approximation improves, capturing more details of
\tilde{f}
. As outlined in Section
2.1
, we apply a greedy search across summaries with
k=1,\dots,K_{\max}
components. We set
K_{\max}
to be large enough to capture the complexity of the model for
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. This search results in a sequence of summary estimates
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1},\dots,\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{K_{\max}}
, each offering a different degree of flexibility. For this paper, we set
K_{\max}
to be large enough to capture the complexity of the model.

Notably, the expression in (
9
) allows us to use several off-the-shelf procedures, such as the
mclust
R cran package from
Scrucca et al. 2023b
, among others, for obtaining
\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}^{k}
depending on the choice of kernel density. The
mclust
package applies the EM algorithm
(
Dempster et al. 1977
)
to solve problems as posted in (
9
). These procedures are discussed further in the numerical applications in Section
6
. Our approach also draws a direct connection with the approach from
Rodríguez et al. 2025
, which applies the EM algorithm to obtain posterior distribution for FMM under a Martingale Posterior approach
(
Fong et al. 2023
)
.

Next, we outline a framework for selecting the summary estimate
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
, through the optimal action
\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}^{k}
, and
k\in\{1,2,\dots,K_{\max}\}
, that best approximates
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
through the posterior predictive distribution.

3.4
Selecting the Lower-Dimensional Summary Estimate

The summary estimates
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
yielded by (
9
) will generally have a poorer fit, than the model associated with
\tilde{f}
. Therefore, the challenge lies in capturing the trade-off in predictive power between the reference model and the summary estimate. Under the FMM choice for the summary, our main concern will be the selection of
k
, since it controls the flexibility of the approximation over the reference model. To achieve this, we propose a discrepancy function that captures the changes in fit between the summary estimates
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1},\dots,\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{K_{\max}}
and the reference model, through
k
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}=(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{\tilde{N}})^{T}
be the same posterior predictive sample used for the optimization in (
9
), we define the discrepancy function between the posterior predictive distribution
\tilde{f}
and the summary estimates
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
as

d_{n}^{k}(\tilde{f},\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k})=\log\frac{\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})}{\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})},\quad k=1,\dots,K_{\max},\quad n=1,\dots,\tilde{N}.

(10)

Since the discrepancy function depends on the posterior predictive distribution through the random variable
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}
, we can obtain the expectation and uncertainty estimates of the approximation of
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
to
\tilde{f}
, for every
k=1,\dots,K_{\text{max}}
. Particularly, for a fixed
k
, the posterior predictive expectation of (
10
) approximates the negative KL divergence between
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
and
\tilde{f}
as

\mathbb{E}d_{n}^{k}:=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{y}}\left[d_{n}^{k}(\tilde{f},\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k})\right]=-\int\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})\log\frac{\tilde{f}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})}{\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n})}\,d\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}=-\text{KL}(\tilde{f}\parallel\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}).

(11)

From this relation, we establish a heuristic for selecting the summary estimate
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
, for
k=1,\dots,K_{\max}
, which yields the best fit. For example, if the expected posterior predictive value of the discrepancy function is close to zero,
\mathbb{E}d_{n}^{k}\approx 0
, this would suggest that the summary
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
provides a good approximation to the model associated with
\tilde{f}
. Otherwise, if
\mathbb{E}d_{n}^{k}<0
, this would indicate that the summary estimate does not provide an appropriate approximation to
\tilde{f}
. Thus, the more negative the discrepancy function is, the more it rejects the summary estimate in favor of the posterior predictive density.

It is important to note that the relation presented in (
11
) also relates to the expected log Bayes factor, or log likelihood ratios
(
Kass and Raftery 1995
)
. As a result, we have a scenario in which we are testing two distinctive “hypotheses.” If the expectation in (
11
) is less than zero, this favors the choice of
\tilde{f}
in relation to the summary estimate
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
for future observations
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}
. If it is close to zero, it indicates that the hypotheses are similar, and thus we have a favorable approximation of
\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}
to the reference model.

We also assess the quality of the approximation of the summary estimate to the reference model by evaluating the uncertainty quantification provided by the spread, or the standard deviation of (
10
),
\text{sd}(d_{n}^{k}(\tilde{f},\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}))
, associated with (
10
). If the spread is narrow and concentrated around the expected posterior
\mathbb{E}d_{n}^{k}
, this indicates lower uncertainty regarding the discrepancy between the estimate and the posterior predictive distribution. Conversely, if the spread is wide, it suggests a higher level of uncertainty in the approximation of the summary estimate to the posterior predictive distribution.

We present both the posterior expected value of the discrepancies and the standard deviation associated with discrepancy function by plotting (
10
), (
12
), and
\text{sd}(d_{n}^{k}(\tilde{f},\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}))
, for
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}
, with
n=1,\dots,\tilde{N}
, and
k=1,\dots,K_{\max}
. The resulting elbow plot visually represents the trade-off between model fit and the number of components, where each point,
\mathbb{E}d_{n}^{k}
, reflects the degree of uncertainty in the approximation.

We approximate the expectation using Monte Carlo integration and the posterior predictive sample
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}=(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{\tilde{N}})^{T}
as

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}\mid\boldsymbol{y}}\left[d_{n}^{k}(\tilde{f},\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k})\right]\approx\bar{d}^{k}(\tilde{f},\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k})=\frac{1}{\tilde{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{\tilde{N}}d_{n}^{k}(\tilde{f},\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k}).

(12)

To identify the optimal summary estimate, we examine the plot for points,
\bar{d}^{k}(\tilde{f},\hat{g}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{k})
, that are closest to zero while ensuring that the corresponding credible interval remains narrow, particularly for smaller values of
k
. Similar discrepancy plots, was applied in different model summarization methods, including those by
Hahn and Carvalho 2015
,
Woody et al. 2021
, and
Bolfarine et al. 2024

Let

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{K^{*}}
with dimension
K^{*}

\hat{g}^{K^{*}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is a finite mixture model density with
K^{*}

If the optimal estimate
\hat{g}^{K^{*}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})

\tilde{f}

\boldsymbol{\theta}

p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{y})

5
Posterior Cluster Summaries

An additional contribution of this paper is the extension of the results from Section
2
and Section
3
to provide uncertainty quantification for membership allocation summaries under different cluster loss functions. The proposed procedure is designed to preserve the predictive accuracy of the full model while yielding interpretable and parsimonious clustering summaries, even in cases where clustering was not the model’s original intent.

To achieve this, we use the optimal number of components
K^{*}
for the FMM summary, identified using the heuristic described in Section
3.4
, as a reference for the number of groups in the data. As discussed in Section
2.1
, the motivation for using
K^{*}
as the number of groups origins from the choice of the discrepancy function (
10
), and relation demonstrated in property (
7
), which approximates the summary to the reference model by identifying high-density regions or modes. These regions typically concentrate essential information about distinct groups within the data
(
Murphy 2012
)
.

A key advantage of our approach is its modularity, as different summary classes can be employed depending on the specific clustering procedure. In this paper, we explore two possible choices for the loss function to generate summary estimates for clustering. The first is the conditional probability loss, which serves as a natural extension of the finite mixture model framework for summaries presented in Section
2.2
, and the k-means loss, related to the k-means clustering method
(
Bishop 2006
)
.

5.1
Cluster Allocation Using Conditional Probability Summaries

Here, we first extend the posterior summarization approach to cluster summaries under the responsibility, or conditional probability, loss. As presented in Section
1
, finite mixture models can be written as
\cdot|z_{i}\sim f(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z_{i}})
, with
\omega_{i}(q):=\mathbb{P}(z_{i}=q)
, for
q\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}
, where the probability allocation can then be rewritten as

\omega_{i}(q)=\frac{\omega_{q}\,f(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{q})}{\sum_{l=1}^{K}\omega_{l}\,f(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{l})},\quad q\in\left\{1,2,\dots,K\right\}.

The above representation can also be extended to the finite mixture model summary defined in equation (
8
). Under our approach it represents the probability of a latent categorical variable
\boldsymbol{c}_{n}\in\{1,2,\dots,K^{*}\}
in
\cdot|\boldsymbol{c}_{n}\sim g_{\boldsymbol{c}_{n}}(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{n}})
, which indicates the cluster allocation for the summary.

Once an estimate for
\boldsymbol{c}_{n}
summary is obtained, it can be naturally extended to the original observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,N
, as a prediction. We begin by demonstrating how to obtain the optimal cluster allocation summary estimate given the conditional probability and subsequently extend the procedure to lower-dimensional posterior summaries.

5.1.1
Cluster Summary Estimate

Let
g_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{K^{*}}
denote the optimal finite summary estimate generated under the procedures of Section
2.2
, and Section
3.4
, defined by the set of parameters
\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}^{K^{*}}=(\hat{\eta}_{1},\dots,\hat{\eta}_{K^{*}};\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}_{K^{*}})
. The conditional probability estimate of the summary membership
\boldsymbol{\hat{\eta}}_{i}(c):=\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{c}_{i}=c)
in the group
c\in\{1,2,\dots,K^{*}\}
for the
i
-th observation
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
is defined as

\boldsymbol{\hat{\eta}}_{i}(c)=\frac{\hat{\eta}_{c}\,g(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}_{c})}{\sum_{l=1}^{K^{*}}\hat{\eta}_{l}\,g(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\hat{\gamma}}_{l})},\quad i=1,\dots,N,\quad c\in\left\{1,2,\dots,K^{*}\right\}.

The summary estimate for the cluster membership for each observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
is thus obtained from the highest conditional probability as

\boldsymbol{\hat{c}}_{i}=\argmax_{c\in\{1,2,\dots,K^{*}\}}\boldsymbol{\hat{\eta}}(c),\quad i=1,\dots N.

(16)

As mentioned in Section
3
, the cluster summary estimates
\boldsymbol{\hat{c}}_{i}
in (
16
) are akin to a Bayes point estimate; therefore, we cannot use them to directly quantify uncertainty allocation through the posterior.

5.1.2
Cluster Posterior Summary

In this paper, we propose obtaining an uncertainty estimate of the summary membership by projecting the whole posterior onto the conditional probability loss. We achieve this by computing the posterior conditional probability from the lower-dimensional posterior summary
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}
, defined in (
4
). We project the posterior draws
\boldsymbol{\gamma^{\prime}}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\gamma^{\prime}}^{(M)}
from (
14
), where
\boldsymbol{\gamma^{\prime}}^{(m)}=(\eta_{1}^{(m)},\dots,\eta_{K^{*}}^{(m)};\boldsymbol{\gamma^{\prime}}_{1}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\gamma^{\prime}}_{K^{*}}^{(m)})
, for
m=1,\dots,M
, to the conditional probability posterior summary for the
i
-th observation
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
as

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}^{(m)}(c)=\frac{\eta^{(m)}_{c}\,g(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\gamma^{\prime}}_{c}^{(m)})}{\sum_{l=1}^{K^{*}}\eta_{l}^{(m)}\,g(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\gamma^{\prime}}^{(m)}_{l})},\quad i=1,\dots,N,\quad c\in\left\{1,2,\dots,K^{*}\right\}.

(17)

From (
17
), we compute the posterior projected cluster allocation for each posterior draw and each observation as

\boldsymbol{c}^{(m)}_{i}=\argmax_{c\in\{1,\dots,K^{*}\}}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}^{(m)}(c),\quad i=1,\dots,N,\quad m=1,\dots,M,

(18)

resulting in a projected posterior distribution of cluster assignments for each observation, from which we can immediately derive uncertainty estimates by analyzing the variation in cluster assignments across different posterior draws as

\text{cond-Uncert}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{i}}=1-\max_{c\in\{1,2,\dots,K^{*}\}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{I}\{\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{(m)}=c\},

(19)

where
\mathbb{I}\{\cdot\}
is the indicator function.

5.2
k-means Summary and Uncertainty Quantification

Here, we present the k-means loss for cluster summaries. The k-means procedure partitions the data into a discrete clusters by introducing a set of cluster centers, or centroids, to which observations are assigned
(
Bishop 2006
)
. This is achieved by minimizing the squared distance between data points and their respective centroids. Cluster memberships and centroids can be efficiently determined using the k-means algorithm
(
MacQueen 1967
)
.

We apply the posterior summarization framework from Section
2
, and Section
3
to obtain cluster estimates and quantify the uncertainty associated with membership under the k-means loss for the predictive posterior from different models.

5.2.1
Cluster posterior summary

Again we use the optimal number of components
K^{*}
, identified using the criteria in Sections
3.4
, as the number of groups in the data. We use the same posterior predictive samples,
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}=(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{\tilde{N}})^{T}
from
\tilde{f}
, to obtain the cluster centroids for each cluster in the reduced dimensionality
K^{*}
. The optimal centroids are obtained by minimizing the k-means loss function

{\cal L}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\tilde{f},\boldsymbol{c}_{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{\tilde{N}}\sum_{q=1}^{K^{*}}\mathbb{I}\{\boldsymbol{c}_{n}=q\}\|\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}\|_{2}^{2},

(20)

where
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}=(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}})^{2}
, and we seek to minimize the sum of squared distances between the predictive samples
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}
, and centroids
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{K^{*}}\}
. We obtain the optimal summary estimates for the centroids by implementing the decision theoretic approach introduced in Section
2
, where minimize expected posterior predictive loss function (
20
) as

(\boldsymbol{\hat{\xi}},\boldsymbol{\hat{c}}_{n}):=\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}\\
\boldsymbol{c}_{n}\in\{1,2,\dots,K^{*}\}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}|\boldsymbol{y}}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\tilde{N}}\sum_{q=1}^{K^{*}}\mathbb{I}\{\boldsymbol{c}_{n}=q\}\|\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(21)

We approximate the minimization in (
21
), using Monte Carlo integration as

(\boldsymbol{\hat{\xi}},\boldsymbol{\hat{c}}_{n}):=\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}\\
\boldsymbol{c}_{n}\in\{1,2,\dots,K^{*}\}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\tilde{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{\tilde{N}}\sum_{q=1}^{K^{*}}\mathbb{I}\{\boldsymbol{c}_{n}=q\}\|\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{n}\sim\tilde{f},

from which we obtain the optimal centroids
\boldsymbol{\hat{\xi}}=\{\boldsymbol{\hat{\xi}}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\hat{\xi}}_{K^{*}}\}
using the standard k-means algorithm of
MacQueen 1967
. Again, as in Section
5.1
, we assign the original observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
as predictions, by finding the nearest centroid using the Euclidean distance as

\boldsymbol{\hat{c}}_{i}=\argmin_{q\in\{1,2,\dots,K^{*}\}}\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\hat{\xi}}_{q}\|_{2}^{2},\quad i=1,\dots N.

(22)

5.2.2
Cluster posterior summary

Typically, k-means clustering does not provide uncertainty estimates for the membership allocation, and as mentioned before in Section
5.1
, the summary (
22
) is akin to the Bayes point estimate. We approach this challenge by employing the posterior summarization approach as presented in Section
4
, to project the posterior distribution and obtain posterior summaries that capture uncertainty in cluster allocation under the k-means loss. Following the methodology presented in Section
3
, we project the posterior distribution through posterior predictive samples
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{1}^{(m)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{H}^{(m)}
from the conditional density
f(\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{h}^{(m)}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(m)})
in the k-means loss defined in (
20
) as

(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)},\boldsymbol{c}^{(m)}_{h}):=\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}\\
\boldsymbol{c}_{n}\in\{1,2,\dots,K^{*}\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{q=1}^{K^{*}}\mathbb{I}\{\boldsymbol{c}^{(m)}_{h}=q\}\|\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}_{h}^{(m)}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}\|_{2}^{2},

which results in the posterior predictive projection over the centroids
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)}=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}^{(m)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)}_{K^{*}}\}
, for
m=1,\dots,M
, allowing for uncertainty quantification in the assignments for the original observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
as prediction. Thus, for each posterior observation we have that the posterior summary estimate for cluster allocation is given by

\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{(m)}=\argmin_{q\in\{1,2,\dots,K^{*}\}}\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(m)}_{q}\|_{2}^{2},\quad i=1,\dots,N,\quad m=1,\dots,M.

(23)

Similar to the conditional probability allocation discussed in Section
5.1
, from the posterior summary allocation with k-means loss (
23
) we derive uncertainty quantification by analyzing the variation in cluster assignments across different posterior draws as

\text{k-Uncert}_{i}=1-\max_{q\in\{1,2,\dots,K^{*}\}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{I}\{\boldsymbol{c}_{i}^{(m)}=q\}.

(24)

23
) under the conditional allocation and k-means loss, respectively. Under this scenario, we propose to order the parameters associated with the location parameters as
\boldsymbol{\gamma^{\prime}}_{1}^{(m)}<\dots<\boldsymbol{\gamma^{\prime}}_{K^{*}}^{(m)}
and the centroids
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}^{(m)}<\dots<\boldsymbol{\xi}_{K^{*}}^{(m)}
. This process is similar to the methodology adopted by
Malsiner-Walli et al. 2016
, in cases where there’s label switching on the posterior samples in finite mixture models.

6
Numerical Applications

In this section, we apply our posterior summarization method to simulated and real data. Our goal is to obtain summary estimates and posterior summaries from the original posterior distribution of different nonparametric or overparameterized Bayesian models. We apply our method to both univariate and multivariate data in Sections
6.2
and
6.1
, respectively, with the goal of generating both density and cluster summaries equipped with uncertainty quantification.

6.1
Univariate Models

For univariate data, we apply our posterior summarization method to models generated under the random Bernstein polynomial
(
Petrone 1999b
)
, Dirichlet process mixture model
(
Ferguson 1983
;
Escobar and West 1995
)
, and mixture of finite mixtures
(
Richardson and Green 1997
;
Miller and Harrison 2018
)
. The goal is to demonstrate the flexibility of our method for generating summaries for densities and cluster allocation under the different approaches. Before we present the results of the numerical studies, we give a brief description of these methods.

The random Bernstein polynomial model is widely applied in density estimation for data with support on the unit interval and thus is useful when other methods that are susceptible to boundary effects fail. With a rather simple variable transformation, this model can be extended to unbounded data. In a nutshell, the BP prior is defined by the Bernstein polynomial, where the number of terms is random and where the partition of the data, where the polynomial is fit, is defined by a Dirichlet Process
(
Ferguson 1973
;
Ferguson 1983
)
. The Bernstein polynomial associated with a cumulative distribution function
G
on
[0,1]
is defined as
B(y|q,G)=\sum_{j=0}^{q}G(\frac{j}{q})\binom{q}{j}y^{j}(1-y)^{q-j}
, with
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, with corresponding density
b(y|q,G)=\sum_{j=1}^{q}G\left(\frac{j-1}{q},\frac{j}{q}\right]\text{Beta}(y|j,q-j+1)
.
If
G
has a continuous density
f
, then
b(y|q,G)
converges uniformly to
f
as
q
grows to infinity. By letting
G\sim\mathrm{DP}(\alpha,G_{0})
, a Dirichlet process, and by letting a prior on
q
on the naturals, results in a random Bernstein Dirichlet process, which can be viewed as a smoothed Dirichlet process
(
Petrone 1999b
)
. Key properties such as estimation, consistency, and convergence rates of the BP model have been explored by
Petrone and Wasserman 2002
. Throughout the numerical applications of this paper, we set the base measure
G_{0}
as a Beta distribution,
\text{Beta}(1,1)
, we let a
\text{Gamma}(2.01,0.1)
distribution prior on
\alpha
, and
q
to have a discrete uniform distribution prior
\text{Uniform}\{1,\dots,1000\}
. For inference we use the
Dppackage
library from
(
Jara et al. 2011
)
, where we generate a posterior of size of 5,000, with a burn-in of 1,000, in which we apply a thinning of size 10.

The Dirichlet process mixture model (DPM) provides a nonparametric framework for clustering and density estimation
(
Ferguson 1983
;
Escobar and West 1995
)
. As seen in Section
1
, the flexibility of the DPM stems from the model’s ability to adapt to an unknown number of clusters in the data and to its computational efficiency, as highlighted by
Maceachern and Müller 1998
. In this paper, the mixture kernel is taken to be Gaussian,
f(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\theta})=N(\cdot|\mu,\sigma^{2})
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, with a conjugate Normal-Gamma prior for the base measure
G_{0}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\kappa\right)=N(\mu|\mu_{0},\frac{\sigma^{2}}{k_{0}})\operatorname{Gamma}\!\left(\sigma^{-2}\,\middle|\,\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}\right)
, parameterized by

\kappa=(\mu_{0},k_{0},\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})
. We adopt the hyper‑parameters configuration

\mu_{0}=\texttt{median}(\boldsymbol{y})
,
k_{0}=1/5
,
\alpha_{0}=2
,
\beta_{0}=1
, and a
\text{Gamma}(2,4)
distribution prior for the concentration parameter
\alpha
. The posterior is generated using the R package
dirichletprocess

(
Ross et al. 2023
)
. A posterior sample of size 6,000 was generated, with a burn-in of 1,000.

As presented in Section
1
, FMM assume that the data are generated from a mixture of component distributions belonging to a well-defined parametric family. One of the issues of FMM is that the number of components has to be fixed in advance, before inference is made. A natural extension of FMM is to treat the number of components as an unknown parameter in which we can associate a distribution, and as consequently a prior
(
Richardson and Green 1997
;
Miller and Harrison 2018
)
. As a result we have the mixture of finite mixtures model. As with the DPM, in this paper, the kernels are Gaussian distributions,
f(\cdot|\boldsymbol{\theta})=N(\cdot|\mu,\sigma^{2})
, with independent priors defined as
\mu\sim N\bigl(\mu|\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2}\bigr),\sigma^{-2}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\sigma^{-2}|a,b)
.
A hyperprior is placed on
b
via
b\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(b|a_{0},b_{0})
. We set
K\sim\text{Geometric}(K|0.1)
for the number of components, and for the weights we have a Dirichilet distribution
(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{K})\sim\text{Dir}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{K}|1)
.

We use out of the box hyperparameters configuration, from the Julia implementation
1
1

1

https://github.com/jwmi/BayesianMixtures.jl
from
Miller and Harrison 2018
. For additional details on hyperparameters choice and the sampling procedure we refer to
Miller and Harrison 2018
. For MFM, we ran a total of 100,000 iterations per data set; we discarded the first 10,000 iterations as burn-in, in which we kept 5,000 by thinning.

For generating the GMM summary estimates and posterior summaries, we use the library

mclust
from R. We set the EM methods such that variances and covariances are as flexible
as possible.

7
Discussion

In this paper, we propose a novel posterior summarization method designed to approximate overparametric and non-parametric Bayesian models for denisty and cluster estimation. The primary objective of our approach is to preserve interpretability while maintaining model fit. The procedure involves three main steps. First, the data are fitted using a overparameterized or nonparametric model. In the second step, a decision-theoretic framework is employed to project the posterior distribution to a FMM summary estimate, guided by a discrepancy function that quantifies the trade-off between accuracy to the original model and the parsimony of the summary. Finally, in the third step, the full posterior distribution of the original model is projected onto the summary, resulting in a posterior summary. The effectiveness of the proposed approach in generating interpretable density and cluster summaries from different Bayesian overparameterized and nonparametric models is demonstrated through a series of numerical simulations and applications.

Despite the effectiveness of our approach, several challenges remain. In our experiments, we observed that the effectiveness of our method can be affected by outliers in posterior predictive samples. To address this issue, robust Bayesian sampling techniques may be employed to mitigate the influence of such posterior predictive samples. Another bottleneck of our method is label switching from the posterior cluster summaries given the different cluster loss functions. Different methods have been proposed to address this problem and can be adopted for our method
(
Malsiner-Walli et al. 2016
)
.

Several directions remain open for future research. One potential extension involves incorporating a penalty function on the locations of the summary loss function when using GMM summaries, as suggested by
Pan and Shen 2007
. Another important direction is the theoretical analysis of the proposed method, particularly concerning its convergence properties and robustness under varying prior specifications. Additionally, exploring alternative loss functions, as the recently intrduced expected misclassification rate loss
(
Adrian et al. 2025
)
, and optimization methods, such as variational Bayes procedures
(
Murphy 2012
)
, may improve computational efficiency and the quality of the approximations. Finally, further development could adapt the proposed procedure to methods that generate posterior summaries through predictive distributions as Martingale Posteriors
(
Fong et al. 2023
)
.
