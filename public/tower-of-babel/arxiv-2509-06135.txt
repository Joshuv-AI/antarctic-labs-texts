A Note on Persistence Theory with Applications
Narendra Pant *

Abstract
The persistence theory has been employed by several authors in order to study persistence properties of dynamical systems generated by ordinary differential equations or maps across diverse disciplines. In this note, the author discusses a roadmap to show the persitence of dynamical systems by discussing some problems from literature, which mainly involves either dealing with spectral radius or the Lyapunov exponents.

Introduction

The dynamical systems encompass a broad range of mathematical models studied across diverse disciplines, including mathematics, physics, biology, engineering, economics, and many more. A few references for different disciplines are: see
Guckenheimer and Holmes, 2013
;
Hirsch et al., 2013
;
Strogatz, 2018
for examples from General Dynamical Systems, see
Arnol’d, 2013
;
Ott, 2002
for Physical Systems,
Kot, 2001
;
Murray, 2007
for Biological and Ecological Systems,
Ogata, 2020
;
Pearson, 2003
for Engineering and Applied systems, and
Nowak, 2006
;
Weidlich, 2006
for Social and Economic Systems. Whichever is the discipline, the persistence results for the dynamical systems are extremely important.

The persistence results are central to understanding, predicting, and controlling the dynamics of complex systems, especially when extinction or collapse has profound implications. These results bridge the gap between theoretical insights and practical applications across a range of disciplines. The persistence of predator-prey models in Ecology guarantees coexistence
Hofbauer and Sigmund, 1998
;
Murray, 2007
, whereas in Epidemiology, the persistence of endemic disease models helps public health experts understand when disease will continue to exist and guide strategies for eradication
Anderson, 1991
;
Brauer et al., 2012
. Similarly, the persistence of oscillatory chemical reactions in Chemistry guarantees that oscillatory behavior is maintained, which is critical for understanding natural phenomena like circadian rhythms or enzyme reactions
Epstein and Pojman, 1998
. The competitive market models, for example, a Cournot competition model, in Economics, the persistence of which is crucial to maintaining diversity in the market and avoiding monopolistic collapse
Tirole, 1988
. Further, the persistence of the magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) equations in plasma physics ensures maintaining energy production in fusion reactors
Freidberg, 2008
. Also, the persistence of robotic systems in Engineering guarantees the system’s robustness to disturbances or environmental changes
Pearson, 2003
. These are only a few of the examples; the list is never-ending.

The focus of this paper is to make a roadmap by working out a few examples from different disciplines using difference equations, differential equations, and delay differential equations. That is, given any dynamical system, how can we show that the given system is persistent?

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we summarize the persistence theory for any given dynamical system generated by ordinary differential equations or by maps. The materials are directly inspired from
Salceanu and Smith, 2009
;
Smith and Thieme, 2011
. For proofs and other details, see
Salceanu and Smith, 2009
;
Smith and Thieme, 2011
. To this end, we consider following dynamical systems

a).

Discrete-time Systems (
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
)

Consider the discrete dynamical system

\displaystyle\begin{split}&z(t+1)=F(z(t))\\
&z_{0}=z(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{+}\end{split}

(1)

To study the persistence of
y
, we put the above system in the following form

\displaystyle\begin{split}&x(t+1)=\mathcal{F}(z(t))\\
&y(t+1)=\mathcal{A}(z(t))y(t)\end{split}

(2)

b).

Continuous-time Systems (
t\in\mathbb{R}
)

Consider the continuous dynamical system

\displaystyle\begin{split}&z^{\prime}(t)=F(z(t))\\
&z_{0}=z(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{+}\end{split}

(3)

To study the persistence of
y
, we put the above system in the following form

\displaystyle\begin{split}&x^{\prime}(t)=\mathcal{F}(z(t))\\
&y^{\prime}(t)=\mathcal{A}(z(t))y(t)\end{split}

(4)

Some of the common assumptions are: Let
z=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, such that
n=p+q
, and
z\mapsto(\mathcal{F}(z),\mathcal{A}(z))
is a continuous map. Here
F:\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{+}
is a continuous map,
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
,
g:\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}_{+}
, such that

F(z)=(\mathcal{F}(z),g(z))\quad\text{with}\quad g(z)=\mathcal{A}(z)y,\ \forall z\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}_{+}.

Further,
\mathcal{A}(z)
is a continuous matrix function with
\mathcal{A}(z)\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
satisfying
0\leq\mathcal{A}(x,0)
. The existence and uniqueness of the solutions for all times are assumed for both discrete and continuous cases. Let
\Phi(t,z)=z(t)=(x(t),y(t))
be the (state) continuous semiflow generated by the solutions of (
2
) or (
4
).

Now, we list the main steps to show the persistence of
y
in the case of discrete-time systems. Since the setting of the working problem is similar, the steps will also be valid for continuous-time systems (of course, the basics of ordinary differential equations will apply for the continuous case).

a).

Rewrite the system (
1
) as in (
2
).

b).

Define the persistent function
\rho(z)
as per requirement. For example, persistence of which species do we want to study? Of a species, of at least one of the species, or of all the species? A few examples of persistent functions are
\rho(z)=\sum_{i}|y_{i}|
,
\rho(z)=y
, etc.

c).

Once the persistent function is defined, we define the extinction set as

X_{0}^{y}:=\{z(t,z_{0})\mid\rho(\Phi(t,z_{0}))=0,\ \forall t\geq 0\}\equiv\{(x_{0},y_{0})\mid y_{0}=0\}.

d).

Study the dynamics on
X_{0}^{y}
, on which the subsystem is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{Discrete: }x(t+1)=\mathcal{F}(x(t),0),\\
&\text{Continuous: }x^{\prime}(t)=\mathcal{F}(x(t),0).\end{split}

(5)

Find the
\omega
-limit set of
X_{0}^{y}
, that is,
\Omega(X_{0}^{y}):=\{M_{1},M_{2},\cdots\}
.

e).

Write the linearized systems as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{Discrete: }u(t+1)=\mathcal{A}(\Phi(t,z))u(t),\hskip 28.45274ptu(0)=y(0),\\
&\text{Continuous: }v^{\prime}(t)=\mathcal{A}(\Phi(t,z))v(t),\hskip 28.45274ptv(0)=y(0).\end{split}

(6)

Let the
q\times q
fundamental solution of (
6
) be
P(t,z)
. Then, one can write

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{Discrete: }P(t+1,z)=\mathcal{A}(\Phi(t,z))P(t,z),\\
&\hskip 28.45274pt\equiv P(t,z)=\mathcal{A}(z(t-1))\mathcal{A}(z(t-2))\cdots\mathcal{A}(z(1))\mathcal{A}(z(0)),\\
&\text{Continuous: }\frac{d}{dt}P(t,z)=\mathcal{A}(\Phi(t,z))P(t,z),\end{split}

(7)

with
P(0,z)=I
in either case.

f).

To show that
\Omega(X_{0}^{y})
is a uniformly weak repeller set, we have the following two cases:

i).

When some of the
M_{i}\in\Omega(X_{0}^{y})
are equilibrium points and the remaining are intervals or sets.

In this case, use Proposition 1 from
Salceanu and Smith, 2009
. It uses the concept of Lyapunov exponents.

ii).

When all of the
M_{i}\in\Omega(X_{0}^{y})
are equilibrium points.

In this case, use Corollary 1 from
Salceanu and Smith, 2009
. It uses the concept of finding the spectral radius of periodic orbits from
\Omega(X_{0}^{y})
.

Either of these techniques can still be used even when the scenarios are the other way around.

g).

After showing that
\Omega(X_{0}^{y})
is a uniformly weak repeller set, use Theorem 2.3 from
Salceanu and Smith, 2009
. By this theorem, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}|y(t)|>\epsilon
for all positive initial conditions
x(0),y(0)
. Hence, by definition,
y
is uniformly strongly
\rho
-persistent.

Main Results: Problem Solving

3.1
A discrete-time predator-prey model from Ackleh et al.
Ackleh et al., 2023

Consider a discrete-time predator-prey periodic model in
Ackleh et al., 2023
by Ackleh et al. (2024). The model equations are listed below as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&n(t+1)=\phi(t,n(t))\hskip 2.84544pt[1-f(p(t))p(t)]\hskip 2.84544ptn(t)\\
&p(t+1)=s_{p}p(t)+\kappa\hskip 2.84544pt\phi(t,n(t))n(t)f(p(t))p(t).\end{split}

(8)

where
n(t)
and
p(t)
are the densities of the prey and predator population, respectively, at time
t
. In the absence of the predator, the prey is assumed to grow with the time-dependent nonlinear growth rate
\phi(n(t),t)
. We assume that
0<s_{n}<1
is the (density-independent) survival probability of prey and
b(n,t)
is the time and density-dependent prey fecundity. To represent seasonal breeding, we choose
b(n,t)
to be a periodic function of period two of the form
b(n,2t)=\hat{b}(n),\;b(n,2t+1)=0
,
t=0,1,2,\dots
. Thus, the seasonal growth rate is given by
\phi(n,2t)=s_{n}+\hat{b}(n)
and
\phi(n,2t+1)=s_{n}
,
t=0,1,2,\dots
. This form implies that individuals in the prey population reproduce during even time steps (breeding seasons) but not during odd time steps (non-breeding seasons).

The prey growth rate is modified by the factor
1-f(p)p
, where
0<f(p)<1
is defined to be the probability that an individual prey is consumed by an individual predator when
p
predators are present. Therefore,
0\leq f(p)p<1
gives the probability that an individual prey is consumed when
p
predators are present, and the term
0\leq 1-f(p)p<1
represents the fraction of prey that manage to escape predation. We assume that predator reproduction is proportional to the amount of prey consumed, where
\kappa>0
converts consumed prey into new predator individuals. In addition, we assume that the predator has a density-independent survival probability
0<s_{p}<1
. For more details, we refer the reader to
Ackleh et al., 2023
.

In order to discuss the periodic solutions of above model, we first time-tansform it using realtioin
\tau+i=2(t+i),i=0,1,2\cdots
. Then, the composite system takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}&n(\tau+1)=AB\left[1-f(x)x\right]n(\tau),\\
&p(\tau+1)=s_{p}x+\kappa n(\tau)ABf(x)x,\end{split}

(9)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}&A=A(n)=s_{n}^{2}+s_{n}\hat{b}(n),\\
&B=B(p)=1-f(p)p,\\
&x=x(n,p)=s_{p}p+\kappa\left(s_{n}+\hat{b}(n)\right)nf(p)p\equiv s_{p}p+\frac{\kappa}{s_{n}}A(n)nf(p)p.\\
\end{split}

(10)

In Theorem
3.1
, we show that system (
9
) is persistent when these two growth rates are greater than one. This persistence, combined with solution boundedness obtained in the proof of Lemma 2 in
Ackleh et al., 2023
, establishes the permanence of the system. To this end, the growth rates of the species related to dynamics of the above model are

a).

The inherent growth rate of prey is
r_{0}:=\phi(0)=s_{n}^{2}+s_{n}\hat{b}(0)
.

b).

The invasion growth rate of predator is
r_{i}:=s_{p}+\kappa\bar{n}f(0)
where
\bar{n}=\hat{b}^{-1}\left(\frac{1-s_{n}^{2}}{s_{n}}\right)
.

Theorem 3.1

Assume that
r_{0}>1
and
r_{i}>1
. Then system (
9
) is strongly uniformly persistent, i.e., there exists an
\epsilon>0
such that
\liminf_{\tau\to\infty}\min\{n(\tau),p(\tau)\}>\epsilon
for all positive initial conditions
(n(0),p(0))
.

Proof.

We first rewrite the system (
9
) in the form given in
Salceanu and Smith, 2009
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&n(\tau+1)=\mathcal{A}(n(\tau),p(\tau))n(\tau),\\
&p(\tau+1)=\mathcal{F}(n(\tau),p(\tau)),\end{split}

where
\mathcal{A}(n(\tau),p(\tau)):=AB[1-f(x)x]
and
\mathcal{F}(n(\tau),p(\tau)):=s_{p}x+\kappa nABf(x)x
. Consider the boundary dynamics where
n=0
, which is given by
p(\tau+1)=s_{p}^{2}p(\tau)
. Since
0<s_{p}<1
, it follows that
\lim_{{\tau\to\infty}}p(\tau)=0
. Note that
(0,0)
is unstable when
r_{0}>1
. Define

\displaystyle Z:=\{(n,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|n\geq 0,p\geq 0\},

\displaystyle X_{1}:=\{(n,p)\in Z|n=0\},

and
M\subseteq Z\cap X_{1}
. Take
M:=\{(n,p)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}|n=0,0\leq p\leq\widetilde{D}\}
where
\widetilde{D}
is the bound from Lemma 2 in
Ackleh et al., 2023
. Then
M
is non-empty, compact, and positively invariant. Since
\lim_{{\tau\to\infty}}p(\tau)=0
, the omega limit set of
M
is

\Omega(M)=\cup_{z\in M}\omega(z)=\{(0,0)\}.

Now let
\mathcal{P}:=\{E_{0}\}=\{(0,0)\}.
Then
\mathcal{A}(E_{0})=A(0)\neq 0
, and hence
\mathcal{A}(E_{0})
is primitive with
r(\mathcal{P})=r_{0}>1
. Take the unit vector
\eta=1
, and
(n,p)\in M,
then
\mathcal{A}(n,p)\eta\neq 0,\forall(n,p)\in M
. So, all the three conditions of Corollary 1 of
Salceanu and Smith, 2009
are satisfied. Hence, by Corollary 1 and Proposition 1 in
Salceanu and Smith, 2009
,
M
is a uniformly weak repeller set relative to the dynamics on
Z\backslash X_{1}
. Finally by Theorem 2.3 in
Salceanu and Smith, 2009
, there exists an
\widehat{\epsilon}>0
such that
\liminf_{\tau\to\infty}|n(\tau)|>\widehat{\epsilon}
for all
(n(0),p(0))\in Z\backslash X_{1}.
Thus, by definition,
n
is strongly uniformly persistent.

Now, we again rewrite the system (
9
) as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&n(\tau+1)=\mathcal{F}(n(\tau),p(\tau)),\\
&p(\tau+1)=\mathcal{A}(n(\tau),p(\tau))p(\tau),\end{split}

where
\mathcal{F}(n(\tau),p(\tau)):=AB[1-f(x)x]n(\tau)
and

\mathcal{A}(n(\tau),p(\tau)):=s_{p}\left(s_{p}+\frac{\kappa}{s_{n}}A(n(\tau))n(\tau)f(p(\tau))\right)+\kappa A(n(\tau))n(\tau)B(p(\tau))f(x)\left(s_{p}+\frac{\kappa}{s_{n}}A(n(\tau))n(\tau)f(p(\tau))\right).

Consider the boundary dynamics where
p=0
, which is given by
n(\tau+1)=\left(s_{n}^{2}+s_{n}\hat{b}(n(\tau))\right)n(\tau)
. Note that from Lemma 1 in
Ackleh et al., 2023
,
\lim_{{\tau\to\infty}}n(\tau)=\bar{n}
, and that
(\bar{n},0)
is unstable when
r_{i}>1
. Define

\displaystyle\begin{split}&Z:=\{(n,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|n\geq 0,p\geq 0\},\\
&X_{1}:=\{(n,p)\in Z|p=0\},\end{split}

and
M\subseteq(Z\cap X_{1})
. Take
M:=\{(n,p)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}|\frac{\epsilon}{2}\leq n\leq D,p=0\}
where
D
is the bound from Lemma 2 in
Ackleh et al., 2023
. Then
M
is non-empty, compact, and positively invariant. Since
\lim_{{\tau\to\infty}}n(\tau)=\bar{n}
, the omega limit set of
M
is

\Omega(M)=\cup_{z\in M}\omega(z)=\{(\bar{n},0)\}.

Now let
\mathcal{P}:=\{E_{1}\}=\{(\bar{n},0)\}
. Then
\mathcal{A}(E_{1})=r_{i}\neq 0
, and hence
\mathcal{A}(E_{1})
is primitive with
r(\mathcal{P})=r_{i}>1
. Take the unit vector
\eta=1,
and
(n,p)\in M,
then
\mathcal{A}(n,p)\eta\neq 0,\forall(n,p)\in M
. So, by using a similar argument as above, we can show that there exists
\widetilde{\epsilon}>0
such that
\liminf_{\tau\to\infty}|p(\tau)|>\widetilde{\epsilon}
for all
(n(0),p(0))\in Z\backslash X_{1}.
Thus, by definition,
p
is also strongly uniformly persistent.

Finally, let
\epsilon=\min\{\widehat{\epsilon},\widetilde{\epsilon}\}
, then the result follows and in particular,
\liminf_{\tau\to\infty}\min\{n(\tau),p(\tau)\}>\epsilon
for all positive initial conditions
(n(0),p(0))
.
∎

3.2
A discrete-time predator-prey model from Q. Din
Din, 2017

Consider a discrete-time predator-prey model in
Din, 2017
. The model equations are listed below as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&x(t+1)=x(t)e^{\left[r\left(1-\frac{x(t)}{K}\right)-\frac{\beta y(t)}{x(t)+\gamma}\right]}\\
&y(t+1)=y(t)e^{\left[1-d-\frac{ay(t)}{bx(t)+c}\right]}\end{split}

(11)

where
x(t)
and
y(t)
are prey and predator populations at time
t
respectively. Furthermore, the parameter
r
denotes the intrinsic growth rate of prey,
\beta
represents the maximum value of the per capita reduction rate of prey,
K
denotes environmental carrying capacity of the prey in a particular habitat,
\gamma
measures the extent to which the environment provides protection to prey,
c
measures the extent to which the environment provides protection to predator, and
a
represents the maximum value of the per capita reduction rate of predator. Assume that
b
measure the food quality that the prey provides for conversion into predator births, and
d
denotes the death rate of predator. For more details, we refer reader to
Din, 2017

We discuss the persistence of system (
11
) in the Theorem 2. To this end, first we have the following Lemma 1, where we discuss the persistence of the prey population. In Lemma 2, the persistence of the predator population will be discussed.

Lemma 1

Assume
r\gamma a>\beta c(1-d)
. Then, the prey population is uniformly strongly persistent, that is, there exists
\epsilon_{1}>0
such that
\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}|x(t)|>\epsilon_{1}
for all positive initial conditions
x(0),y(0)
.

Proof.

First, we rewrite the system (
11
) as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&x(t+1)=A(x(t),y(t))x(t),\\
&y(t+1)=f(x(t),y(t)),\hskip 14.22636pt\text{where }A(x,y)=e^{\left[r\left(1-\frac{x}{K}\right)-\frac{\beta y}{x+\gamma}\right]},\quad f(x,y)=ye^{\left[1-d-\frac{ay}{bx+c}\right]}.\\
\end{split}

The extinction set is

\displaystyle\begin{split}X_{0}&=\{z(t,z_{0})\in Z\mid x(t)=0,\ \forall t\geq 0\}\equiv\{(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\mid x_{0}=0\}.\end{split}

In what follows, we study the dynamics on
X_{0}
, which is given by

y(t+1)=y(t)e^{1-d-\frac{ay(t)}{c}}.

Clearly, the fixed points are
y=0
and
\bar{y}=(1-d)\frac{c}{a}
. Observe that if we let
f_{2}(y)=ye^{1-d-\frac{ay}{c}}
, then

f_{2}^{\prime}(y)=e^{1-d-\frac{ay}{c}}\left[1-\frac{ay}{c}\right].

Further, for
y>c/a
,
f_{2}^{\prime}(y)<0
, and for
0<y<c/a
,
f_{2}^{\prime}(y)>0
. Therefore, the function
f_{2}(y)
is increasing on
\left[0,\frac{c}{a}\right]
and decreasing on
\left[\frac{c}{a},\infty\right)
. Let
\bar{y}=(1-d)\frac{c}{a}
, and consider the following cases:

a).

When
y_{0}=0
, then
y(1)=0
.

b).

When
0<y_{0}<\bar{y}
, then
0<f_{2}(y_{0})<f_{2}(\bar{y})\implies 0<y(1)<\bar{y}
.

c).

When
\bar{y}<y_{0}<\frac{c}{a}
, then
f_{2}(\bar{y})<f_{2}(y_{0})<f_{2}\left(\frac{c}{a}\right)\implies\bar{y}<y(1)<f_{2}\left(\frac{c}{a}\right)
.

Note from a), b), c) that the maximum value of iterations of
f_{2}
is
f_{2}\left(\frac{c}{a}\right)
.

d).

For any
y_{0}\in\left(\frac{c}{a},\infty\right)
, the next iteration goes to the left of
y_{0}
since
f_{2}
is decreasing in this region. When
y_{0}>\frac{c}{a}
,
f_{2}(y_{0})<f_{2}\left(\frac{c}{a}\right)\implies y(1)<f_{2}\left(\frac{c}{a}\right)
. Note that here
y(1)
lies to the right of
\frac{c}{a}
. Again, since the function is decreasing in this region, the minimum it can decrease to is
f_{2}\left(f_{2}\left(\frac{c}{a}\right)\right)=f_{2}^{2}\left(\frac{c}{a}\right)
.

Therefore, one can write

f_{2}^{2}\left(\tfrac{c}{a}\right)<(1-d)\tfrac{c}{a}<\tfrac{c}{a}<f_{2}\left(\tfrac{c}{a}\right).

So, from the above discussion,
M_{1}=\{(0,0)\}
and
M_{2}=\{(0,M)\}
where
M=\left[f_{2}^{2}\left(\tfrac{c}{a}\right),f_{2}\left(\tfrac{c}{a}\right)\right]
. Therefore,

\Omega(X_{0})=\{M_{1},M_{2}\}.

Now, consider the linearized system

u(t+1)=A(x(t),y(t))u(t),\quad A(x,y)=e^{\left[r\left(1-\frac{x}{K}\right)-\frac{\beta y}{x+\gamma}\right]}.

The Lyapunov exponent is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lambda(z,\eta)=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln|P(t,z)\eta|,\\
&\lambda(z)=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln|P(t,z)|,\quad\text{for unit vector }\eta=1.\end{split}

(12)

We find
P(t,z)
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}P(t,z)&=A(x(t-1),y(t-1))\cdot A(x(t-2),y(t-2))\cdot\dots\cdot A(x(1),y(1))\cdot A(x_{0},y_{0})\\
&=\exp\left[r\left(1-\frac{x(t-1,z)}{K}\right)-\frac{\beta y(t-1,z)}{x(t-1,z)+\gamma}\right]\\
&\quad\cdot\exp\left[r\left(1-\frac{x(t-2,z)}{K}\right)-\frac{\beta y(t-2,z)}{x(t-2,z)+\gamma}\right]\dots\\
&\quad\cdot\exp\left[r\left(1-\frac{x(0,z)}{K}\right)-\frac{\beta y(0,z)}{x(0,z)+\gamma}\right]\\
&\\
&=\exp\left[rt-\frac{S_{1}(t,z)}{K}-\beta S_{2}(t,z)\right].\end{split}

Taking natural logs on both sides,

\displaystyle\ln P(t,z)=rt-\frac{S_{1}(t,z)}{K}-\beta S_{2}(t,z),

(13)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}&S_{1}(t,z)=x(t-1,z)+x(t-2,z)+\dots+x(0,z),\\
&S_{2}(t,z)=\frac{y(t-1,z)}{x(t-1,z)+\gamma}+\frac{y(t-2,z)}{x(t-2,z)+\gamma}+\dots+\frac{y(0,z)}{x(0,z)+\gamma}.\end{split}

Consider the following cases:

a).

Take
z_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})=(0,0)
. Clearly,
S_{1}(t,z_{0})=0
as
x_{0}=0
, and
S_{2}(t,z_{0})=0
as
y_{0}=0
. Therefore, equation (
13
) takes the form
\ln|P(t,z_{0})|=rt
. Finally, from equation (
12
),
\lambda(z_{0})=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\tfrac{1}{t}(rt)=r>0
.

b).

Take
z_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})
where
x_{0}=0
and
y_{0}\in M
. As above,
S_{1}(t,z_{0})=0
, and the expression for
S_{2}(t,z_{0})
takes the form

\gamma S_{2}(t,z_{0})=y(t-1,z_{0})+y(t-2,z_{0})+\dots+y(0,z_{0}).

To compute
S_{2}(t,z_{0})
, consider

\displaystyle\begin{split}y(t)&=y(0)\exp\left[t-dt-\tfrac{a}{c}\{y(t-1)+y(t-2)+\dots+y(0)\}\right].\end{split}

From this, one can get

\displaystyle\ln\left|\tfrac{y(t)}{y(0)}\right|

\displaystyle=t-dt-\tfrac{a}{c}\{y(t-1)+\dots+y(0)\}\implies y(t-1)+\dots+y(0)=\tfrac{c}{a}\left[t-dt-\ln\left|\tfrac{y(t)}{y(0)}\right|\right].

At
z=z_{0}
,

\displaystyle S_{2}(t,z_{0})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{c}{\gamma a}\left[t-dt-\ln\left|\tfrac{y(t)}{y(0)}\right|\right].

Using the values of
S_{1}(t,z_{0})
and
S_{2}(t,z_{0})
in equation (
13
), we get

\displaystyle\ln|P(t,z_{0})|

\displaystyle=rt-\tfrac{\beta c}{\gamma a}\left[t-dt-\ln\left|\tfrac{y(t)}{y(0)}\right|\right],

\displaystyle\lambda(z_{0})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\tfrac{1}{t}\left[rt-\tfrac{\beta c}{\gamma a}\left(t-dt-\ln\left|\tfrac{y(t)}{y(0)}\right|\right)\right]

\displaystyle=r-\tfrac{\beta c}{\gamma a}(1-d)-\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\tfrac{1}{t}\ln\left|\tfrac{y(t)}{y(0)}\right|

\displaystyle=r-\tfrac{\beta c}{\gamma a}(1-d)\quad\text{(since $y(t)$ is bounded)}

\displaystyle>0\quad\text{if }r>\tfrac{\beta c}{\gamma a}(1-d)\equiv r\gamma a>\beta c(1-d).

Since all the Lyapunov exponents are positive, by Proposition 1 of
Smith and Thieme, 2011
, all the
M_{i}
in
\Omega(X_{0})
are uniformly weak repellers. By Theorem 2.3 of the same paper
Smith and Thieme, 2011
, we may choose
\epsilon_{1}>0
such that
\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}|x(t)|>\epsilon_{1}
, for all
y_{0}\in Z\backslash X_{0}
. By definition,
y
is uniformly strongly persistent. This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Next, in the following lemma, we discuss the persistence of the predator population.

Lemma 2

Suppose
b<1
. Then, the predator is uniformly strongly persistent, that is, there exists
\epsilon_{2}>0
such that
\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}|y(t)|>\epsilon_{2}
for all positive initial conditions
x(0),y(0)
.

Proof.

The extinction set

\displaystyle\begin{split}X_{1}&=\{z(t,z_{0})\in Z\mid y(t)=0,\ \forall t\geq 0\},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{where }z(t)=(x(t),y(t))\\
&\equiv\{(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\mid y_{0}=0\}.\end{split}

Dynamics on the extinction set is given by

x(t+1)=x(t)e^{r\left(1-\frac{x(t)}{K}\right)}.

Clearly, the fixed points are
\bar{x}=0
and
\bar{x}=K
. So, with a similar argument as for the prey, we get
M_{1}=\{(0,0)\}
and
M_{2}=\{(M,0)\}
where

M=\left[f_{1}^{2}\!\left(\tfrac{K}{r}\right),f_{1}\!\left(\tfrac{K}{r}\right)\right].

Therefore,

\Omega(X_{1})=\{M_{1},M_{2}\}.

Now, consider the linearized system

u(t+1)=A(x(t),y(t))u(t),\hskip 14.22636pt\text{where }A(x,y)=e^{r\left(1-d-\frac{ay}{bx+c}\right)}.

The Lyapunov exponent is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda(z,\eta)&=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln|P(t,z)\eta|,\\
\lambda(z)&=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln|P(t,z)|,\quad\text{for unit vector }\eta=1.\end{split}

For
z_{0}\in M_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda(z_{0})&=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln|P(t,z)|\\
&=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\big|e^{t(1-d)}\big|\\
&=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\,t(1-d)\\
&=(1-d)>0\quad\implies d<1.\end{split}

For
z_{0}\in M_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda(z_{0})&=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln|P(t,z)|\\
&=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\big|e^{t(1-d)}\big|\\
&=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\,t(1-d)\\
&=(1-d)>0\quad\implies d<1.\end{split}

Since all the Lyapunov exponents are positive, by Proposition 1 of
Smith and Thieme, 2011
, all the
M_{i}
in
\Omega(X_{1})
are uniformly weak repellers. By Theorem 2.3 of
Smith and Thieme, 2011
, we may choose
\epsilon_{2}>0
such that

\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}|y(t)|>\epsilon_{2},\quad\forall y_{0}\in Z\backslash X_{1}.

By definition,
x
is uniformly strongly persistent.
∎

Combining the Lemmas
1
and
2
, we have the following theorem:

Theorem 3.2

Suppose
d<1
and
r\gamma a>\beta c(1-d)
. Then the system is uniformly persistent if there exist a
\epsilon=\min\{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\}
such that
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\min\{x(t),y(t)\}>\epsilon
,
\forall

x_{0},y_{0}>0
.

Proof.

The proof directly follows from the Lemmas
1
and
2
.
∎

3.3
A three species food chain model from
Alebraheem and Abu-Hasan, 2012

Consider the system in
Alebraheem and Abu-Hasan, 2012
where two predators are competing on a prey. Since our focus is to study persistence, we take nondimensional form of the original system. For more details, we refer the reader to
Alebraheem and Abu-Hasan, 2012
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{dx}{dt}=x(1-x)-\frac{\alpha xy}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-\frac{\beta xz}{1+h_{2}\beta x}\\
&\frac{dy}{dt}=-uy+\frac{e_{1}\alpha xy}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-\frac{e_{1}\alpha y^{2}}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-c_{1}yz\\
&\frac{dz}{dt}=-wz+\frac{e_{2}\alpha xz}{1+h_{2}\beta x}-\frac{e_{2}\beta z^{2}}{1+h_{2}\beta x}-c_{2}yz\\
\end{split}

(14)

The above equations in system (
14
) are of Kolgomorov type. Following are a few assumptions made originally in
Alebraheem and Abu-Hasan, 2012
:

a).

x
is a prey population and
y,z
are competing predators, living exclusively on the prey, i.e.

\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{i}}<0,\frac{\partial M_{i}}{\partial x}>0,M_{i}(0,y,x)<0,\frac{\partial M_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}\leq 0,i,j=1,2

where
L=(1-x)-\frac{\alpha y}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-\frac{\beta z}{1+h_{2}\beta x},

M_{1}=-u+\frac{e_{1}\alpha x}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-\frac{e_{1}\alpha y}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-c_{1}z
, and
M_{2}=-w+\frac{e_{2}\alpha x}{1+h_{2}\beta x}-\frac{e_{2}\beta z}{1+h_{2}\beta x}-c_{2}y
.

b).

In the absence of predators, the prey species x grows to carrying capacity, i.e.

L(0,0,0)>0,\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}(x,y,z)=-1<0,\exists k>0\ni J(k,0,0)=0.\text{ Here }k=1.

c).

There are no equilibrium points on the y or z coordinate axes and no equilibrium point in
yz-
plane.

d).

The predator
y
and the predator
z
can survive on the prey. This means there exist equilibrium points
\tilde{E}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},0)
and
\hat{E}(\hat{x},0,\hat{z})
such that
L,M_{i}
evaluated at
\tilde{E}
and
\hat{E}
are zero such that
\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\hat{x},\hat{z}>0
and
\hat{x},\tilde{x}<k.

We start by studying the persistence of prey. For this, define the state space to be
Z:=\{(x,y,z)|x,y,z\geq 0\}
and
Z_{+}:=\{(x,y,z)|x,y,z>0\}
. As similar to above problems, we rewrite model (
14
) in the form (
4
) as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{dY}{dt}=\mathcal{F}(x,Y)\\
&\frac{dx}{dt}=\mathcal{A}(x,y,z)x\\
\end{split}

where

\mathcal{A}=(1-x)-\frac{\alpha y}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-\frac{\beta z}{1+h_{2}\beta x},Y=\begin{bmatrix}y\\
z\end{bmatrix},\mathcal{F}=\begin{bmatrix}-uy+\frac{e_{1}\alpha xy}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-\frac{e_{1}\alpha y^{2}}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-c_{1}yz\\
-wz+\frac{e_{2}\alpha xz}{1+h_{2}\beta x}-\frac{e_{2}\beta z^{2}}{1+h_{2}\beta x}-c_{2}yz\end{bmatrix}.

Define the extinction set to be
X_{0}^{x}=\{(x,y,z)|x=0\}.
Then, the dynamics on
X_{0}^{x}
is given by the subsystem

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{dy}{dt}=-uy+-e_{1}\alpha y^{2}-c_{1}yz\equiv-y(u+e_{1}\alpha y+c_{1}z)\\
&\frac{dz}{dt}=-wz+-e_{2}\beta z^{2}-c_{2}yz\equiv-z(w+-e_{2}\beta z+c_{2}y)\\
\end{split}

It is not difficult to see, by assumption c), that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}(y,z)=(0,0)
which implies that
\Omega(X_{0}^{x})=\{(0,0,0)\}\equiv\{M_{1}\}
. The linearized system in
U
can be written as

\frac{dU}{dt}=U\left[(1-x)-\frac{\alpha y}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-\frac{\beta z}{1+h_{2}\beta x}\right].

Clearly, its fundamental solution is
P(t,x,y,z)=e^{t[(1-x)-\frac{\alpha y}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-\frac{\beta z}{1+h_{2}\beta x}]}
. The Lyapunov exponent is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda(x,y,z)&=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln|P(t,z)|=(1-x)-\frac{\alpha y}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-\frac{\beta z}{1+h_{2}\beta x}\\
\lambda(M_{1})=&1>0.\end{split}

Therefore, by a similar argument as in above problems we can choose
\epsilon_{x}>0
such that
\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}|x(t)|>\epsilon_{x},\forall x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}\in Z\backslash X_{0}^{x}
. By definition,
x
is uniformly strongly persistent.

To study the persistence of first predator, that is,
y
, rewrite model (
14
) in the form (
4
) as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{dY}{dt}=\mathcal{F}(y,Y)\\
&\frac{dy}{dt}=\mathcal{A}(x,y,z)y\\
\end{split}

\mathcal{A}=-u+\frac{e_{1}\alpha x}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-\frac{e_{1}\alpha y}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-c_{1}z,Y=\begin{bmatrix}x\\
z\end{bmatrix},\mathcal{F}=\begin{bmatrix}x(1-x)-\frac{\alpha y}{1+h_{1}\alpha xy}-\frac{\beta xz}{1+h_{2}\beta x}\\
-wz+\frac{e_{2}\alpha xz}{1+h_{2}\beta x}-\frac{e_{2}\beta z^{2}}{1+h_{2}\beta x}-c_{2}yz\end{bmatrix}.

Define the extinction set to be
X_{0}^{y}=\{(x,y,z)|\epsilon_{x}/2\leq x\leq B_{x},y=0\}.
Then, the dynamics on
X_{0}^{y}
is given by the subsystem

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{dx}{dt}=x(1-x)-\frac{\beta xz}{1+h_{2}\beta x}\\
&\frac{dz}{dt}=-wz+\frac{e_{2}\alpha xz}{1+h_{2}\beta x}-\frac{e_{2}\beta z^{2}}{1+h_{2}\beta x}\\
\end{split}

With the assumptions made above ( see
Alebraheem and Abu-Hasan, 2012
), there exist no limit cycles on
xy-
plane. Therefore,
\Omega(X_{0}^{y})=\{(\hat{x},0,\hat{z})\}\equiv\{M_{2}\}
. Again, the linearized system in
U
can be written as

\frac{dU}{dt}=U\left[-u+\frac{e_{1}\alpha x}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-c_{1}z\right].

and its fundamental solution is
P(t,x,y,z)=e^{t[-u+\frac{e_{1}\alpha x}{1+h_{1}\alpha x}-c_{1}z]}
. Therefore, the positivity of the Lyapunov exponent,
\lambda(M_{2})=-u+\frac{e_{1}\alpha\hat{x}}{1+h_{1}\alpha\hat{x}}-c_{1}\hat{z}
, implies that
e_{1}>\frac{(u+c_{1}\hat{z})(1+h_{1}\alpha\hat{x})}{\alpha\hat{x}}.
Therefore, by a similar argument as in above problems we can choose
\epsilon_{y}>0
such that
\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}|y(t)|>\epsilon_{x},\forall x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}\in Z\backslash X_{0}^{x}
. By definition,
y
is uniformly strongly persistent.

By a similar analysis, one can show the persistence of second predator, that is, that there exists
\epsilon_{z}>0
and hence that
z
is uniformly strongly persistent when
e_{2}>\frac{(w+c_{2}\tilde{y})(1+h_{2}\beta\tilde{x})}{\beta\tilde{x}}.

To summarize the all above for system (
14
), we have the following theorem:

Theorem 3.3

Assume
e_{1}>\frac{(u+c_{1}\hat{z})(1+h_{1}\alpha\hat{x})}{\alpha\hat{x}}
and
e_{2}>\frac{(w+c_{2}\tilde{y})(1+h_{2}\beta\tilde{x})}{\beta\tilde{x}}
. Then, the system (
14
) is uniformly persistent, that is, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}\{x(t),y(t),z(t)\}>\epsilon
for all
x_{0},y_{0},z_{0}>0
.

Proof.

In the above analysis, finally choose
\epsilon=\min.\{\epsilon_{x},\epsilon_{y},\epsilon_{z}\}
and the theorem holds.
∎

4
Discussion

We referred to many examples from the literature and discussed the persistence of each system. Some of them were solved using the spectral radius approach, while a few were solved using the Lyapunov exponent. Although this work is directly inspired by
Salceanu and Smith, 2009
and
Smith and Thieme, 2011
, the author believes that this note provides useful examples in order to understand the process by which a given dynamical system, generated by ordinary differential equations or by maps, is shown to persist.
