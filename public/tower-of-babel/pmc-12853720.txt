Comparative evaluation of the efficacy of the digital static guide versus the freehand approach in endodontic microsurgery: in vitro study
Authors: Abdulmalik Abdulrahman Alhelali, Amr Mohamed Abdallah, Muhammad Attia, Pervine Hassan Sharaf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndodontic microsurgery traditionally relies on the freehand approach. Recently, digital static guides have been introduced to improve surgical precision and safety. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of digital static guidance in preserving the mental foramen compared to the conventional freehand technique. Additionally, it assessed differences in the osteotomy size and the procedural time.MethodsForty-eight 3D-printed models with 3D-printed separate lower second premolars were subjected to optical scans and Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT). These models were divided into three groups according to the location of the mental foramen: Group 1 (located below the apex of the second premolar) Group 2 (located between first and second premolars) Group 3 (located between second premolar and first molar). Each group was further subdivided into two subgroups utilizing surgical static guides and the freehand technique. Post-operative CBCT scans were compared with preoperative scans to measure the distance between the osteotomy and the mental foramen. Additionally, the osteotomy surface area and the duration of the procedures were compared between the two techniques. The paired sample test was used at (P ≤ 0.05).ResultsThe guided technique demonstrated significantly greater distance from the osteotomy to the mental foramen in all three groups. The osteotomy surface area, the osteotomy length and width were significantly reduced in the guided groups compared to the freehand groups across all anatomical positions. Additionally, the procedural time was significantly shorter in the guided approach for all groups.ConclusionThe variable positions of the mental foramen in relation to the premolar root do not affect the overall benefits provided by the static surgical guide compared to the freehand technique for efficient endodontic microsurgical procedures; reducing the surgical time and the osteotomy size leaving a wider bone margin around the mental foramen.Keywords: 3D-printed models, CBCT, Digital static guide, Endodontic microsurgery, Freehand approach

Background

In the early 1990 s, a transformative era in endodontic microsurgery (EMS) began, marked by several significant advancements that have reshaped the field. The introduction of the surgical operating microscope, micro instruments, micro ultrasonic tips, enhanced root-end preparation techniques, the adoption of more biocompatible root-end filling materials, the use of Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT), computer-aided design/computer aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM), and three-dimensional printing technology catalyzed improvements in surgical precision and outcomes [1, 2]. These developments soared success rates from 44 to 53% to 90 and 91.1% [3, 4].

The adoption of digital workflow technologies, which have been proven accurate in dental implant placement, has recently been extended to root-end surgery [5]. Furthermore, the use of digital guides in endodontics presents a precise method to locate the calcified root canals system. The virtual planning of the access cavity was found to preserve the tooth structure, avoiding any procedural errors [6].

The dynamic navigation using optical tracking systems and the static navigation using three-dimensional printed templates have evolved to locate the target area and guide the instrument entry during endodontic surgery [7, 8].

The combination of CBCT images and guided endodontics using a printed surgical guide represents an innovative method for accessing the root tip through endodontic surgery [9, 10].This approach enables surgeons to perform more precise gingival incision, osteotomies, and root resections [9, 11, 12], while also promoting faster healing and improving overall efficiency compared to conventional methods [11, 13].

The three dimensions (3D) printing models has aided in education, research and inventions as it provides more exact tools to evaluate the different configurations of the root canal system and generate different scenarios for treatment [14–17].

Saving time during surgery is of great importance as it prevents the rebound phenomenon or reactive hyperemia which occurs due to localized tissue hypoxia and acidosis following prolonged vasoconstriction, leading to impossible hemostasis and post-operative hematoma [18].

Apex localization is tough in case of mandibular posterior teeth due to anatomical causes as the thick mandibular buccal cortical bone, the limited visibility, the limited suppleness of the buccal mucosa and due to the hemorrhage interference. Moreover, challenges remain in preserving the important inferior alveolar neurovascular bundles with variable position of the mental foramen between persons [19]. Any damage to the neurovascular bundle may lead to postoperative paresthesia. Meanwhile, aggressive bone removal delays healing and may reduce the success rate [20–24].

The EMS of mandibular molars and premolars necessitates an accurate estimation of the surgical access and the association of the roots with the mental foramen and mandibular canal. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of the digital static guide compared with the freehand approach in endodontic microsurgery on mandibular premolars.

The null hypothesis stated that no significant differences would exist in the outcomes of endodontic surgery performed with a digital static guide compared to surgery performed with the freehand approach, in terms of preservation of the mental foramen, osteotomy size and the procedure time.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is a randomized controlled in vitro study. The manuscript of this laboratory study was written according to Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory Studies in Endodontology (PRILE) 2021 guidelines (Fig. 1). Ethical approval was obtained from the research ethics committee, Faculty of Dentistry, Pharos University in Alexandria, Egypt (02202412293313).

Flowchart illustrating the study design and methodology based on the PRILE 2021 (Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory Studies in Endodontology)

Study setting

The study was conducted at the Faculty of Dentistry, Pharos University in Alexandria, Egypt.

Samples

This study used 3D-printed mandibular models, including separate lower premolars positioned on 3D-printed mandibles. The base stereolithography (STL) files were purchased from a commercial 3D model provider (CGTrader, model by Sakura-pm) and subsequently modified using Blender software (Blender Foundation, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) to fit the study requirements. The files were processed with CHITUBOX Pro2 slicer software (CHITUBOX, Shenzhen, China) and fabricated using an Elegoo Mars 3 3D printer (Elegoo Technology, Shenzhen, China). For printing, an epoxy-based photopolymer resin was used (Elegoo Water-Washable Resin, Yellow, 1KG V1.0, Product Number/SKU: DLQ05057; Elegoo Technology, Shenzhen, China). These models were designed to replicate specific clinical cases with high accuracy, ensuring reproducibility in mimicking clinical situations.

Study sample and sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using Power Analysis and Sample Size Software (PASS 2020, NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, Utah, USA). A minimum total of 48 paired observations (corresponding to 16 cases per experimental condition, each subdivided into two subgroups of n = 8) was required to compare the digital static guide with the freehand approach in terms of mental foramen preservation, osteotomy size, and procedural time. The calculation was based on a 95% confidence level, an anticipated effect size of ~ 0.60, and a statistical power of 80%, using a two-sided paired t-test for the primary comparison. In addition, a sensitivity analysis using smaller effect sizes (15% and 20%) confirmed that the planned sample size maintained sufficient statistical power (> 80%) across these scenarios [25–27].

Inclusion criteria involved 3D accurate, intact and standardized printed models based on specific dental cases [28–30]. Exclusion criteria included previously used models, damaged models and models that deviated from the established size, shape, or defect parameters set for the study [31]. The quality and dimensional accuracy of the 3D-printed models were assessed through both visual inspection and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) analysis.

Randomization

The models (n = 48) were first divided into three groups according to the location of the mental foramen: Group 1, below the apex of the second premolar (n = 16) [19]; Group 2, between the first and second premolars; and Group 3, between the second premolar and the first molar. Within each anatomical group, the models were randomly allocated into two subgroups (n = 8) using a computer-generated random list: one subgroup treated with a surgical static guide (1 A, 2 A, & 3 A) and the other treated freehand (1B, 2B, & 3B). This ensured unbiased allocation while maintaining consistency with the predetermined anatomical grouping [32].

Intervention

All surgical procedures were performed under a surgical operating microscope Carl-Zeiss (Meditec, Jena, Germany) and were carried out by a single postgraduate operator in endodontics under faculty supervision to ensure methodological consistency and minimize operator bias. Forty-eight 3D-printed lower premolars underwent standardized root canal therapy. CBCT images were taken using Veraview epocs 3D X800 (J Morita Corp, Koyoto, Japan) operating at 81 kV and 5 mA with an exposure time of 9.4 s, a field of view of 40 × 40, and voxel size of 0.125 mm., and optical scans (Medit, Seoul, South Korea) of the models were performed. The endodontic guide was produced after merging the CBCT scan and surface scan with a specific software DDP, Systems (DDP, Czestochowa, Poland) for image analysis. (Fig. 2)

A: Overview of the digital model during virtual planning. B: Design phase of the static surgical guide based on merged CBCT and surface scan data. C: Insertion of the fabricated static guide into the 3D-printed mandibular model to verify fit and accuracy

The models were covered with soft silicon simulating gingiva (CMEC Dentals, Suzhou, Jiangsu, China), which was stabilized on the models using cyanoacrylate adhesive (Loctite Instant Adhesive, Henkel–Loctite, Düsseldorf, Germany) to maintain its position during the procedures. The static surgical guide was designed and used exclusively for the osteotomy procedure, while apicoectomy and retrograde preparation were performed freehand. Root-end resections were standardized at 3 mm perpendicular to the long axis of the root. In the freehand approach, these steps were performed manually under the operating microscope using microsurgical instruments, without the aid of a static guide. Both approaches followed the same procedural steps: papilla base flap, osteotomy, apicoectomy, retrograde cavity preparation by using piezo endo tips altaqi piezo tips (Woodpecker Medical Instrument, Guilin, China) and endo microsurgical instruments kit (Hu-Friedy Mfg., Inc., USA), retrograde filling using Mineral Trioxide Aggregate MTA Cerkamed, Stalowa (Wola, Poland), and Micro-Apical Placement MAP system (Produits Dentaires SA, Vevey, Switzerland). Flap re-approximation and suturing GMS 6 − 0 (Ghatwary Medical, Alex., Egypt). All retrograde fillings were examined for adequate density and complete obturation of the root-end preparations, with no voids visible on the postoperative CBCT scans. (Fig. 3)

Outcome measurements

The study assessed three key outcome measures to compare the guided and freehand techniques in endodontic microsurgery. Firstly, the mental foramen preservation was evaluated by measuring the minimal distance from the osteotomy site to the mental foramen using CBCT imaging. Secondly, osteotomy size was quantified through CBCT surface area measurements; thirdly, the duration of the surgical procedures was recorded by stopwatch. (Fig. 4). All CBCT measurements were independently performed by two experienced examiners. To evaluate reproducibility, intra- and inter-examiner reliability were assessed. Intra-examiner reliability was determined by re-assessing 20% of the images after one week, while inter-examiner reliability was assessed by comparing the measurements between both examiners. Cohen’s Kappa test demonstrated excellent agreement (> 0.85) for both intra- and inter-examiner evaluations, consistent with previous CBCT reliability studies [33].

A: Measurement of osteotomy surface area on CBCT images using sagittal view analysis. B: Measurement of osteotomy length and width from the buccal perspective. C: Measurement of the minimal distance between the osteotomy site and the mental foramen on coronal CBCT sections

Statistical analysis

The mean and standard deviation values were calculated for each group in each test. Data were explored for normality using Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests, the data showed parametric (normal) distribution. The paired sample test was used to compare two groups in related samples. The significance level was set at P ≤ 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed with IBM® SPSS® Statistics Version 25 for Windows.

Discussion

The current study evaluated the benefits of the digital static guide being one of the technological advancements in endodontic microsurgery. This 3D printed guide relies on the use of CBCT and oral scanning to be designed. As none of the previous studies clearly focused on the use of the static guide during the endodontic microsurgery procedures near the mental foramen avoiding its traumatic insult, the present study assessed the minimum distance between the osteotomy and the mental foramen, the osteotomy size and the procedure time.

Moreover, the current work used 3D printed epoxy based photopolymer mandibular models for standardization and elimination of any confounding variable [34]. These models were covered with soft silicon to mimic the gingiva. The three different mental foramen positions simulating the most frequent clinical scenarios added strength to the present in vitro study [32].

CBCT was utilized in this study to provide a 3D evaluation of the parameters measured in this study, removing the effect of any superimposition found in periapical x-rays [35, 36]. The surface area of the osteotomy was examined from the sagittal view as the surgical guide and its point of entry were from the buccal surface. The distance between the osteotomy and the mental foramen was measured on the coronal view.

All the procedures carried in this present study were performed under surgical microscopic magnification to fulfill the requirements of endodontic microsurgery. Furthermore, piezosurgery endodontic tips were selected in the current work for precise osteotomy, root end resection, retrograde cavity preparation, and minimal damage to the neurovascular bundles. They are preferred by the endodontic surgeons, nowadays [37].

The choice of mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) in this study was based on its well-documented biocompatibility and superior sealing properties, making it the material of choice for root-end retrograde filling in microsurgical endodontics [38].

The 3D printed epoxy based photopolymer mandibular models proved their efficacy to conduct endodontic microsurgery, during the present study. These models were also supported by Oza et al. [34]. who demonstrated that CBCT-derived printed models can significantly enhance osteotomy planning accuracy and operator confidence in endodontic microsurgery.

Irrespective the position of the mental foramen, the current study demonstrated that the guided technique resulted in significantly safer distance to the mental foramen, smaller osteotomy dimensions and shorter operative times across all groups when compared to the freehand technique.

These significant findings align with Peng et al. 2021 [3] demonstrating the accuracy and the efficiency of the digital static guides in endodontic microsurgery performed in maxillary teeth. The results of this study are also consistent with Wei et al. 2023 [39] who reported shorter operative times and improved control with guided surgery compared to freehand techniques. Additionally, Zubizarreta-Macho et al. 2020 [40] confirmed that the static guide yield higher accuracy than freehand procedures during access cavity preparation. Similar advantages have been observed in other surgical fields, such as mandibular reconstruction and dental implant placement, where guided techniques have led to improved precision, reduced operative time, and more predictable outcomes compared to the freehand approaches [41–44].

The present findings showed that only the guided technique consistently produced smaller osteotomies less than 5 mm in length and width. These 5 mm limited dimensions were previously found sufficient for accessibility in endodontic microsurgery and associated with faster healing [45].

The clinical significance of the current study findings was to improve safety around the vital structures and to enhance the healing by preserving the mental foramen, reducing the osteotomy size and the procedure time [46–48]. These reductions carried another important clinical implication; decreasing the bleeding and the light scattering under the microscope that can obscure the surgical field, during endodontic microsurgery [4].

All procedures were performed by a postgraduate operator in endodontics with five years of experience, under faculty supervision, ensuring methodological consistency.

This study has certain limitations. As an in vitro investigation, it does not fully replicate the biological complexity of clinical conditions, including soft tissue dynamics, blood supply, patient variability, and natural healing responses. The experimental setting was conducted under standardized and controlled conditions, which may not reflect the heterogeneity encountered in real clinical scenarios. In particular, the 3D-printed jaws were stabilized directly on the working table without the dynamic support of surrounding musculature, soft tissues, or patient positioning, which further limits simulation of actual clinical conditions. Moreover, the static guide technique was evaluated under idealized model conditions, and its clinical performance may be influenced by patient-specific factors such as bone density, root morphology, patient age, and the presence of periapical pathology, which could impact osteotomy accuracy and healing response. In addition, the surgical guide was constructed according to the optical scan of the simulated oral cavity and the CBCT images, which provided only cross sections of teeth and bone. Although CBCT is considered the gold standard for surgical guide construction, the nerves cannot be directly visualized on CBCT. Therefore, the present study focused on the mental foramen as the reproducible anatomical landmark corresponding to the mental nerve’s location.

Further in vivo studies are therefore recommended to validate the clinical applicability, long-term outcomes, and potential advantages of static guided endodontic microsurgery compared to conventional freehand techniques.

Under the limitations of the current study, the null hypothesis was rejected.

Conclusion

The variable positions of the mental foramen in relation to the premolar root do not affect the overall benefits provided by the static surgical guide compared to the freehand technique for efficient endodontic microsurgical procedures; reducing the surgical time and the osteotomy size leaving a wider bone margin around the mental foramen.
