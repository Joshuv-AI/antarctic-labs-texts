From mechanisms to therapeutics: a narrative review on pyroptosis and autophagy in osteoporosis
Authors: Qiong Zheng, Qiu-cheng Zhu, Bao-kang Zhao, Ai-ling Chen, Yan Tu, Xiang-jie Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoporosis (OP) is a globally prevalent metabolic bone disorder, particularly affecting aging populations, and is characterized by an imbalance between bone resorption and formation. The pathogenesis of OP involves a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, hormonal changes, and chronic inflammatory pathways. Emerging evidence now indicates that pyroptosis, an inflammasome-mediated form of programmed cell death, and autophagy, a conserved intracellular catabolic process, are critical regulators of osteocyte viability and bone metabolism homeostasis.MethodsA systematic literature search was performed using PubMed and Web of Science for studies published from 2010 to 2024, with a focus on recent evidence (2020–2024). Seminal earlier publications were also considered. Inclusion criteria covered studies addressing both osteoporosis and pyroptosis/autophagy, their interaction, or related interventional strategies. Exclusions comprised articles not involving these mechanisms or lacking mechanistic insight into bone metabolism. Priority was given to experimental and clinical studies. Literature screening adhered to these criteria through title/abstract and full-text review.ResultsThe analysis confirmed that pyroptosis accelerated bone loss by activating inflammatory cascades, while autophagy exerted cytoprotective effects to maintain bone integrity. A dynamic interaction existed between these processes within osteocytes, forming a critical regulatory axis. Furthermore, specific natural compounds demonstrated the ability to modulate bone metabolism by targeting this axis, thereby offering novel mechanistic insights and promising therapeutic strategies for OP.ConclusionsPyroptosis–autophagy crosstalk represents a pivotal therapeutic target for osteoporosis. Future research should focus on developing dual-target therapies and conducting in vivo studies to elucidate the spatiotemporal dynamics of these interconnected processes.

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP), a major global health challenge, is characterized by reduced bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, leading to a profound increase in fracture risk. Affecting hundreds of millions worldwide, OP disproportionately impacts postmenopausal women [1]. This heightened susceptibility is primarily driven by the rapid decline in estrogen levels following menopause. Consequently, deepening our understanding of the underlying pathogenesis of osteoporosis is crucial to inform and advance the development of novel therapeutic approaches.

The core pathological imbalance in osteoporosis involves attenuated bone formation and enhanced bone resorption [2,3]. Emerging evidence highlights critical roles for pyroptosis and autophagy dysregulation in this pathogenesis. Pyroptosis, a programmed inflammatory cell death pathway, is primarily mediated by nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and Caspase-1 [4,5]. Growing research links pyroptosis to functional impairment of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), potentially inducing impaired osteogenic differentiation and contributing to progressive bone loss. This process often coincides with the establishment of a systemic chronic inflammatory microenvironment. Crucially, increased pyroptosis in osteoporosis patients is associated with osteocyte dysfunction and elevated bone resorption. Moreover, excessive NLRP3 inflammasome activation not only amplifies inflammation but also directly inhibits osteoblasts (OBs) formation, ultimately accelerating osteoporosis progression [6].

In contrast to the destructive nature of pyroptosis, autophagy serves as a fundamental cellular degradation process that maintains homeostasis through three distinct mechanisms: macroautophagy, microautophagy, and chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA). Among these, macroautophagy (commonly termed autophagy) serves as the central regulator of bone metabolism by efficiently eliminating compromised organelles and misfolded proteins for lysosomal degradation [7,8]. Therefore, the normal function of autophagy directly affects the survival and function of Osteocytes. If autophagy is inhibited, it may accelerate the development of osteoporosis. Activated autophagy not only accelerates osteoblast differentiation processes but also potently inhibits osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, thereby significantly ameliorating the pathological manifestations of osteoporosis [9,10].

The interplay between pyroptosis and autophagy presents promising therapeutic avenues for osteoporosis. Recent studies highlight that natural plant-derived compounds can modulate these pathways to exert protective effects [11,12]. For instance, trehalose enhances autophagy, thereby reducing osteoblast pyroptosis and attenuating bone loss in postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMOP) [13]. Similarly, flavonoids like quercetin demonstrate efficacy against mandibular osteoporosis in ovariectomized (OVX) rats by concurrently regulating autophagy and suppressing the NLRP3 pathway [14]. Despite progress, a key knowledge gap persists in understanding how pyroptosis and autophagy are coordinated in osteoporotic bone remodeling and how plant-derived compounds target this interaction. Although their roles in OP pathogenesis are established, the molecular mechanisms of phytochemicals need further exploration. This review aims to systematically elucidate the spatiotemporal dynamics of the pyroptosis–autophagy network and assess the therapeutic potential of natural compounds targeting this axis, providing a foundation for precision therapeutics.

Regulation mechanism of pyroptosis in bone metabolism

OP pathogenesis is critically driven by the NLRP3/GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis axis, which orchestrates inflammatory cell death and bone homeostasis disruption. This process is further modulated by genetic predispositions and epigenetic regulations that influence NLRP3 expression and activation. Importantly, gut microbiota dysbiosis exacerbates osteolytic pyroptosis via the ‘gut–bone axis’, whereby microbial metabolites prime and trigger NLRP3 inflammasome activation in bone cells. In light of these mechanisms, therapeutic strategies are increasingly focusing on targeting NLRP3/GSDMD signaling with specific inhibitors, natural compounds, and advanced delivery systems to mitigate pyroptosis and restore bone metabolic balance.

The NLRP3/GSDMD pyroptosis axis: mechanisms and therapeutic implications in osteoporosis

Pyroptosis, an inflammasome-induced programmed cell death pathway, is centrally regulated by caspase-dependent Gasdermin-D (GSDMD) cleavage[15]. Activation occurs via two primary pathways: (1) The classical route engages canonical inflammasomes (e.g. NLRP3) to activate caspase-1, which cleaves GSDMD at Asp275/Gln276, enabling N-terminal oligomerization and membrane pore formation; (2) The non-canonical pyroptosis pathway, mediated by caspase-4/5/11, directly senses intracellular lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [16]. Although it differs from the canonical pathway in initiation, both converge through GSDMD cleavage. The N-terminal fragment of GSDMD then forms membrane pores, triggering cell lysis and the release of inflammatory cytokines [16]. As the central executor of pyroptosis, GSDMD represents a promising therapeutic target for inflammatory bone diseases [17,18].

Crucially, the NLRP3 inflammasome—a key driver of osteolytic pyroptosis—requires dual activation signals: Priming via toll-like receptor (TLR)/NF-κB upregulates NLRP3 expression, while triggering by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) (e.g. ATP, crystalline aggregates) induces NLRP3-ASC-pro-caspase-1 complex assembly, leading to caspase-1 autocatalytic cleavage [19,20]. This assembly is mechanistically governed by K+ efflux, lysosomal rupture, and mitochondrial ROS generation [21–23]. Downstream, activated caspase-1 mediates: GSDMD-dependent cytolysis and inflammatory cytosolic release; maturation of pro-IL-1β/IL-18; collectively promoting osteocyte death and pathological bone loss [24–26].

In the bone microenvironment, NLRP3-driven pyroptosis orchestrates a tripartite disruption of bone homeostasis: it impairs osteoblast differentiation and function, promotes RANKL-dependent osteoclastogenesis, and skews mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) differentiation toward adipogenesis over osteogenesis. This collective suppression of bone formation, enhancement of resorption, and aberrant MSC commitment accelerates trabecular deterioration and cortical thinning, propelling osteoporotic progression. Therefore, targeting the NLRP3/GSDMD axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating inflammatory bone resorption. The impact of non-canonical pyroptosis on bone metabolism

The non-canonical pyroptosis pathway significantly disrupts bone metabolic homeostasis. In the bone microenvironment, various stimuli such as LPS/ATP and cigarette smoke (CS) extract can activate this pathway, inducing pyroptosis in osteoblasts and BMSCs [37,38]. This directly suppresses osteogenic differentiation and ultimately leads to bone loss. The released pro-inflammatory cytokines further disrupt the OPG/RANKL balance, promoting osteoclastogenesis. This pyroptotic cell death can be amplified through crosstalk with other stress pathways. For instance, endoplasmic reticulum stress—often triggered by inflammatory stimuli—activates the DNA damage-inducible transcript 3 (DDIT3)/CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein (C/EBP) homologous protein (DDIT3/CHOP) axis, which inhibits PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy. This exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production, forming a vicious cycle that intensifies pyroptosis [39].

Beyond this core pathway, alternative pathways like Caspase-3/GSDME activation (e.g. induced by sodium butyrate [40]) can also contribute to osteoblast dysfunction. Given its central role in bone loss, targeting the non-canonical pathway presents a promising therapeutic strategy. Inhibiting the core executor GSDMD, for example, with the GelMa/Mg/DMF MS hydrogel system, effectively suppresses pore formation and protects against bone loss [36]. Alternative approaches include blocking upstream inflammatory signals: the combination of Tofacitinib and Iguratimod ameliorates osteoporosis by suppressing the JAK/STAT pathway [41], while Yunnvjian Decoction acts via inhibition of the NF-κB/NLRP3/IL-1β axis [42]. In summary, targeting the caspase-4/5/11–GSDMD axis offers a clear strategic direction for developing innovative therapies against inflammatory bone disorders like osteoporosis.

NLRP3′s genetic and regulatory roles in osteoporosis pathogenesis

Genetic evidence solidifies the pivotal role of NLRP3 in osteoporosis pathogenesis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic intervention point. Studies using Nlrp3-knockout (KO) mice and humanized NLRP3 mouse models reveal that a disease-associated missense mutation (D305N) causes macrophages to secrete IL-1β without secondary signals, mimicking monocytes from cryopyrin-associated periodic syndrome (CAPS) patients. Furthermore, D305N-mutant mice exhibit heightened sensitivity to LPS, triggering systemic inflammation that promotes arthritis and osteoporosis [43]. Beyond genetics, emerging regulatory pathways further elucidate how NLRP3-driven pyroptosis is fine-tuned, offering novel mechanistic insights for targeting this axis. For instance, Immune-responsive gene 1 (IRG1)/Itaconate Pathway: IRG1 deficiency enhances NLRP3 activation, driving osteoclastogenesis. Exogenous itaconate supplementation reverses bone loss in IRG1-KO mice by suppressing NLRP3-dependent osteoclast formation [44]; methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14)-Homeobox protein A5 (HOXA5)-WNK lysine-deficient protein kinase 1 (WNK1 Axis): In OVX mice, reduced METTL14 and HOXA5 expression accompany elevated GSDMD-N and IL-1β levels. Overexpression of METTL14 or HOXA5 inhibits macrophage pyroptosis and osteoclast differentiation while upregulating WNK1. Mechanistically, METTL14-mediated m6A modification of HOXA5 inhibits NLRP3-driven pyroptosis via WNK1 transcriptional activation, attenuating osteoporosis [45].

Collectively, these genetic and regulatory findings converge to position the NLRP3 inflammasome not merely as a passive participant but as a central, druggable hub orchestrating osteolytic pyroptosis. Targeting these upstream modulators or NLRP3 itself presents a compelling strategy for mitigating inflammatory bone resorption in osteoporosis.

Gut microbiota, NLRP3 pyroptosis, and osteoporosis

Emerging evidence establishes gut microbiota as a critical regulator of osteoporosis pathogenesis through NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis. Epimedium decoction (EFD) attenuates bone loss by suppressing NLRP3/caspase-1/IL-1β signaling and enhancing intestinal barrier integrity [46]. Lactobacillus plantarum 45 (LP45) downregulates macrophage NLRP3/IL-1β expression, modulates exosomal secretory profiles, reduces oxidative stress proteins, and promotes osteoblast-osteoclast balance—significantly maintaining bone metabolic homeostasis [47]. Gut dysbiosis orchestrates osteoporosis pathogenesis through: (1) Metabolite-mediated mechanisms where microbiota-derived compounds (e.g. Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO)) activate NF-κB to inhibit BMSC osteogenesis while promoting adipogenesis, while concurrently suppressing sympathetic inhibition of bone formation via vagus nerve signaling [48]. (2) Immune-osteoclastogenic cascades wherein Th17 cell proliferation drives IL-17-dependent RANKL overexpression, accelerating osteoclastogenesis, whereas probiotics (e.g. Lactobacillus) inhibit inflammatory bone resorption by reducing tumor necrosis factor-α(TNF-α)/IL-6 [49].

Scarce direct evidence currently exists on microbiota regulation of osteocyte pyroptosis through NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD axis or metabolite modulation of osteocyte autophagy via lysosomal/mTOR pathways; future studies should determine whether gut flora directly regulates pyroptosis-autophagy crosstalk, notably elucidating how autophagy may negatively regulate pyroptosis in the bone microenvironment.

Therapeutic strategies targeting NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis in osteoporosis

Therapeutic strategies targeting NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis are emerging as promising approaches for osteoporosis intervention, leveraging both natural and synthetic pathways to restore bone homeostasis. Clinical evidence reveals elevated serum NLRP3 levels in PMOP, correlating inversely with estradiol [50], underscoring its clinical relevance. Natural compounds demonstrate multifaceted efficacy: Urolithin A [51] and Alloferon-1 [52] directly inhibit NLRP3/caspase-1 signaling, reducing inflammatory cytokine release; notably, Melatonin not only suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation but also promotes osteoblast differentiation, offering a dual therapeutic action in PMOP [53]. The synthetic inhibitor MCC950 suppresses osteoclastogenesis via NF-κB/c-Fos/NFATc1 axis inhibition [27]. Targeting upstream activators, mitochondrial dysfunction-induced ROS production triggers NLRP3 activation [12,54,55]. Tricalcium phosphate (TCP) particles exemplify a pathological stimulus that activates the ROS/NLRP3/Caspase-1 axis to exacerbate osteocyte pyroptosis [56], thereby highlighting the therapeutic potential of inhibiting this pathway. Conversely, compounds like selenomethionine (Se-Met) scavenge ROS to protect bone formation [47], and norcantharidin (NCTD) blocks ERK/ROS/NLRP3 signaling to inhibit osteoclastogenesis [57]. Endogenous regulators such as the deubiquitinase CYLD mitigate NLRP3 via WNK1 ubiquitination [58], while advanced delivery systems like CH6-LNPs-siNLRP3 [57] and multifunctional graphene oxide (GO) coatings [59] enable targeted therapy and enhance osteogenesis.

In summary, pyroptosis critically disrupts bone metabolic homeostasis by modulating osteocyte survival, function, and inflammatory microenvironment dynamics in osteoporosis. Future investigations should prioritize elucidating spatiotemporal interactions within pyroptosis-related signaling networks to accelerate the clinical translation of precision therapies targeting this pathway.

Regulatory roles of autophagy in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis

Autophagy serves as a central regulator in bone remodeling, where it promotes osteogenesis by maintaining mitochondrial integrity through mitophagy and supports skeletal homeostasis. It further exerts dual context-dependent regulation over osteoclastogenesis, either facilitating or inhibiting bone resorption. In BMSCs, autophagy critically balances osteogenic-adipogenic differentiation, thereby determining bone formation capacity. This autophagic activity is modulated by various signaling pathways, including HIF-1α-mediated adaptation to hypoxic conditions, which influences both osteoblast and osteoclast activity. Capitalizing on these mechanisms, emerging nanomaterials are now being designed to precisely modulate autophagic flux, offering innovative strategies for enhanced bone regeneration and the treatment of osteoporosis.

Autophagy and mitophagy in osteoporotic bone remodeling

Autophagy orchestrates a conserved self-degradative process essential for cellular homeostasis by eliminating damaged organelles and misfolded proteins through lysosomal degradation, generating metabolic precursors and energy substrates [60,61]. Mechanistically, this dynamic cycle initiates with ATG protein-mediated autophagosome formation, followed by autolysosome maturation, where acid hydrolases degrade cargo. Critically, autophagy regulates bone metabolism by augmenting osteoblast differentiation via mTOR/AMPK(AMP-activated protein kinase) activation while suppressing osteoclastogenesis through NF-κB/RANKL inhibition [62,63] (Figure 2).

Through mTOR/AMPK signaling, autophagy promotes osteogenesis while suppressing osteoclastogenesis via NF-κB-RANKL inhibition, thereby maintaining bone homeostasis.

By preserving the functional integrity and survival of bone cells, autophagy serves as a critical guardian of skeletal homeostasis. Its dysregulation accelerates mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, disrupting the balance between bone formation and resorption, thereby contributing to osteoporosis progression. Consequently, targeting autophagic pathways represents a promising therapeutic strategy to counteract pathological bone loss. As a key specialized branch of autophagy, mitophagy—the selective clearance of damaged mitochondria—plays a pivotal role in bone metabolism [64,65]. The core PINK1-Parkin axis operates whereby PINK1 senses mitochondrial damage and recruits Parkin, which ubiquitinates mitochondrial substrates to mark them for degradation via LC3 binding. This process reduces mitochondrial ROS generation, protects osteoblast differentiation capacity, and prevents the suppression of bone formation [66–69].

Autophagy promotes osteogenesis

Autophagy promotes osteogenesis by maintaining mitochondrial integrity through mitophagy, restoring cellular bioenergetics to enhance osteoblast differentiation and mineralization while suppressing cellular senescence [70]. Key regulatory mechanisms include: COL6A3 potentiates BMSC osteogenic/adipogenic differentiation by augmenting LPS-induced mitophagy and mitochondrial homeostasis [71]; the phytoestrogen Ferutinin activates Kruppel-like factor 2 (KLF2) to induce autophagy during dental pulp-derived stem cells (DPSC) osteoblastic differentiation [72]; L-arginine elevates PINK1/Parkin/BNIP3 (Bcl-2/adenovirus E1B 19 kDa interacting protein 3, key mitophagy effector) expression to enhance mitophagy and confer ROS protection in osteoblastic cells [73]; Pharmacologically, Nicotinamide Mononucleotide (NMN) upregulates LC3B-II expression while downregulating P62 to enhance mitophagy [74], whereas non-imprinted in Prader–Willi/Angelman syndrome region protein 2 (NIPA2) inhibits PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy via the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1-α(PGC-1α)/forkhead box O3a(FoxO3a) pathway, simultaneously increasing OCN/RUNX2 expression and Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) activity [75]. Resveratrol activates Sirtuin1 (SIRT1) to suppress PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling while elevating LC3/Beclin-1 expression [76]. Vitamin K2 activates autophagy/mitophagy, whereas dexamethasone suppresses autophagy-related protein expression [77].

Therapeutically, mitophagy antagonizes advanced oxidation protein product(AOPP)-induced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase (NOX)-reactive oxygen species(ROS) generation, inhibits mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) depolarization and apoptosis [78], reverses CoCrMo particle-induced BCL2-associated X protein (BAX) overexpression and apoptosis via ERN1 (endoplasmic reticulum to nucleus signaling 1)-MAPK8 signaling [79], and SIRT1 activators rescue fluoride-exposed osteoblasts by promoting autophagy while suppressing apoptosis [80].

In summary, autophagic regulation plays a central role in maintaining intracellular homeostasis, with dysfunction leading to impaired osteoblast activity. Concurrently, mitophagy modulates osteoclast differentiation processes. providing critical theoretical insights into the mechanisms underlying bone metabolic regulation.

Autophagy regulation in osteoclastogenesis

Autophagy dynamically modulates osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption through context-dependent mechanisms. Core autophagy proteins (Atg5/7/4B, LC3) guide lysosomal fusion with the plasma membrane, enabling directional secretion of lysosomal contents that facilitate osteoclast-mediated bone resorption [81]. RANKL signaling activates autophagy during osteoclastogenesis through Beclin1 upregulation and TNF receptor-associated factor 6(TRAF6)-dependent K117 ubiquitination [82], while autophagy reciprocally promotes RANKL-induced osteoclast formation by degrading TRAF3 to activate NF-κB signaling [83].while autophagy reciprocally promotes RANKL-induced osteoclast formation by degrading TRAF3 to activate NF-κB signaling [83]. p62 knockdown suppresses autophagy activation and osteoclastogenesis [84], and Nuclear Dbf2-related kinase(NDR2) inhibits both processes by phosphorylating ULK1 to trigger its degradation [85].

Additionally, microenvironmental modulators include: titanium particles that upregulate CD147 to activate autophagy and increase soluble RANKL [86]; hypoxia which reduces miR-20a expression via hypoxia-inducible factor-1α(HIF-1α) binding, thereby upregulating ATG16L1 to enhance autophagy and osteoclastogenesis [87]; and IL-17A that bidirectionally regulates osteoclast precursors’ apoptosis through Beclin1-TRAF3 signaling—high concentrations inhibit while low concentrations promote osteoclastogenesis [88]. Pharmacologically, kaempferol inhibits osteoclast formation by suppressing RANKL-induced ERK/c-Jun terminal kinase(JNK) pathways, downregulating NFATc1/TRAF6/c-Fos, and modulating autophagy markers (Beclin1↓, p62↓, LC3-I→II conversion↑) [89]. Starvation responses involve GPCR kinase 2-interacting protein 1 (GIT1)-mediated disruption of Bcl-2/Beclin1 binding to promote pro-osteoclast autophagy [90].

Autophagy regulation in BMSC differentiation and bone homeostasis

Autophagy critically balances osteogenic–adipogenic differentiation in BMSCs through context-dependent mechanisms. ROS-induced FOXO3 activation during osteoblast commitment upregulates autophagy-related genes, with inhibition impairing differentiation [91]. Age-associated LRRc17 overexpression accelerates senescence; its knockdown suppresses mTOR/PI3K signaling, activates mitophagy to resolve mitochondrial dysfunction, and promotes osteogenesis [92]. Sirt3-mediated mitophagy counteracts Advanced Glycation End Products(AGEs)-induced senescence and osteoporosis [93]. Pathologically, cigarette smoke extract (CSE) suppresses mitophagy, causing damaged mitochondria accumulation and ROS-driven senescence that impair osteogenesis—reversed by mitophagy agonist MitoQ [94]. Post-ovariectomy, BMSCs exhibit decreased Beclin1/LC3 and accumulated p62, diminishing osteogenesis while enhancing adipogenesis and immune dysfunction; autophagy activation rescues this phenotype [95].

Beyond these pathological mechanisms, recent studies have identified several key regulatory factors and therapeutic approaches that target autophagy to promote bone formation. For instance. WW domain-containing coiled-coil adaptor protein(WAC) promotes autophagy, stabilizes PINK1 against ubiquitination, and activates mitophagy to enhance osteogenesis [96]. Therapeutically, autophagy activation reverses aged BMSC dysfunction by regulating ROS/p53 pathways, restoring bone mass in aging models [97]. Translationally, ATG systems (e.g. Atg7-KO models) demonstrate autophagy’s role in organelle/protein turnover, with deficiency causing ubiquitinated protein aggregation [98]. Hence, systematic studies must define autophagy-related gene regulatory networks in osteocytes to clarify their centrality in bone metabolic homeostasis.

HIF-1α-mediated bone remodeling regulation

Emerging evidence establishes HIF-1α as a central regulator of autophagy activation under hypoxic conditions through the BNIP3/Beclin1 pathway, maintaining cellular bioenergetic homeostasis; crucially, HIF-1α overexpression rescues dexamethasone-impaired hypoxia-induced mitophagy by upregulating BNIP3, protecting osteocytes from apoptosis [99]. Furthermore, HIF-1α activation exerts dual regulatory functions: (1) promoting BMSC differentiation into osteoblasts while inhibiting osteocyte maturation, and (2) suppressing BMM differentiation into osteoclasts—collectively modulating bone microstructure and remodeling dynamics [100]. Future studies should prioritize hypoxia-adaptive HIF-1α signaling networks in osteocytes for therapeutic exploitation in bone disorders.

Nanomaterial-mediated autophagy regulation for bone regeneration

Nanomaterials demonstrate significant potential in modulating autophagy and promoting osteoblast differentiation. Bioactive silica nanoparticles activate ERK1/2 signaling to induce autophagosome formation, with LC3β-II and p62 directly interacting with nanoparticles; concurrently, they accelerate osteoblast differentiation while enhancing ALP activity and mineralization [101]. Honeycomb biomimetic graphene oxide quantum dot coatings activate mitophagy to promote osteoporotic bone regeneration, representing a promising design strategy for magnesium alloy implants treating bone defects [102]. Amine-hydroxyl-triazole-cerium/strontium metal–organic framework(AHT-Ce/SrMOF) implants potently activate the AMPK pathway in BMSCs, reducing ROS levels and restoring mitochondrial function to reverse cellular aging while stimulating new bone formation [103]. Future studies should prioritize elucidating nanomaterials’ transformative potential in regulating the autophagy–bone metabolism axis.

While these findings demonstrate significant therapeutic potential, critical evaluation of nanomaterial efficacy and safety profiles remains essential. Future comparative analyses of treatment strategies should address: (1) long-term biosafety and biodegradation kinetics of silica-based nanoparticles, (2) immunogenicity and potential off-target effects of graphene derivatives, and (3) metal ion release dynamics and potential cumulative toxicity of MOF implants. Such critical assessment and systematic comparison of nanotherapeutic approaches will be instrumental in determining priority research directions for clinical translation, particularly regarding their autophagy-modulating functionalities in bone metabolism regulation.

The pyroptosis–autophagy interplay: mechanism and therapy in osteoporosis

Based on the crosstalk between pyroptosis and autophagy in osteoporosis, natural compounds have emerged as promising therapeutic agents that dually target this interactive axis. These compounds suppress NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis to reduce inflammatory damage while concurrently activating protective autophagy to clear cellular debris and mitigate oxidative stress. By simultaneously modulating both processes, they rebalance osteoclast–osteoblast differentiation and restore bone homeostasis. This dual-target strategy represents a novel precision medicine approach for osteoporosis, offering synergistic anti-inflammatory and osteoprotective effects with significant clinical translation potential.

Pyroptosis–autophagy crosstalk in osteoporosis

Pyroptosis and autophagy interact dynamically in osteoporosis, wherein pyroptosis bidirectionally regulates osteogenic–osteoclastic differentiation via AMPK/mTOR-dependent autophagic flux remodeling. It can promote autophagy by upregulating LC3 and Beclin-1 to clear damaged components and mitigate inflammatory injury [104,105], yet excessive pyroptosis may suppress autophagic function, leading to autophagosome accumulation and cell death [106]. A critical feedback mechanism involves NLRP3-mediated mitochondrial damage, which activates Drp1-driven fission while impairing PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy. Caspase-1 also cleaves Parkin, further inhibiting mitophagy and amplifying pyroptosis. Additionally, autophagy directly degrades pro-IL-1β and inflammasomes, providing essential negative regulation [89,90].

This crosstalk modulates osteoclastogenesis through the RANKL/OPG axis: autophagy dominance inhibits NF-κB and reduces RANKL, whereas pyroptosis promotes osteoclast differentiation via IL-1β and IL-18, disrupting bone homeostasis (Figure 3). Targeting this interaction offers therapeutic potential; precision modulation of autophagy using small molecules or natural compounds can simultaneously suppress pyroptosis, providing anti-inflammatory and osteoprotective effects. For clinical translation, developing bone-targeted delivery systems (e.g. nanoparticles) and monitoring serum inflammatory markers and autophagic flux will be crucial, establishing a novel precision medicine paradigm for osteoporosis.

Crosstalk between pyroptosis and autophagy in osteocyte survival. Pyroptosis activation can upregulate autophagy-related molecules (e.g., Beclin-1, LC3) to enhance autophagic flux for clearance of damaged proteins and organelles. However, excessive pyroptosis overwhelms the system, leading to impaired autophagic flux, autophagosome accumulation, and accelerated cell death. At the same time, as a cytoprotective process, autophagy inhibits pyroptosis through two primary mechanisms: selective removal of pyroptotic triggers (e.g., overactivated inflammasomes, intracellular pathogens) via P62/SQSTM1-mediated autophagy; mitigation of pyroptosis-associated oxidative stress by regulating ROS homeostasis. This protective crosstalk is essential for maintaining osteocyte viability.

Targeting the pyroptosis–autophagy axis with natural compounds

Building upon this mechanistic crosstalk, natural compounds have emerged as promising therapeutic agents capable of dually modulating both pyroptosis and autophagy to restore bone homeostasis. These compounds target core nodes of the interaction network through two primary mechanisms: (1) suppression of the NLRP3–Caspase1–GSDMD axis to mitigate inflammatory cytokine release and pore-driven cell lysis; and (2) induction of cytoprotective autophagy (evidenced by upregulated LC3-II/Beclin-1) via mTOR inhibition, enhancing the clearance of damaged organelles and protein aggregates to alleviate oxidative stress. For instance, Scoparone blocks NLRP3 activation by specifically enhancing PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, thereby breaking the aforementioned damaging feedback loop [107]. The multifaceted actions of these compounds collectively establish novel, microenvironment-targeted strategies for osteoporosis intervention. Mechanisms of natural products regulating pyroptosis and/or autophagy in bone metabolism.

Discussion

Osteoporosis—a paradigm of age-related skeletal disorders—manifests as significant bone mineral density reduction and trabecular microarchitectural deterioration [125,126], with pyroptosis and autophagy crosstalk emerging as critical regulatory mechanisms. Pyroptosis contributes to osteoporosis through IL-1β/IL-18-mediated bone resorption and potential amplification of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP; IL-6/TNF-α/matrix-degrading enzymes), establishing an ‘inflammaging-fueled vicious cycle’ that accelerates bone loss [127–129]; senescent cell clearance alleviates osteoporotic progression in aging models, though pyroptosis-SASP mechanistic synergy requires further spatiotemporal mapping. Autophagy activation represents a therapeutic countermeasure: natural compounds (e.g. icariin) suppress pyroptosis/SASP and enhance bone quality [9,130], warranting prioritized development of autophagy–pyroptosis-SASP triad-targeting precision drugs. Natural products offer multidimensional advantages over conventional therapeutics via mechanistic polypharmacy (e.g. flavonoid-mediated Wnt/β-catenin activation coupled with RANKL/osteoprotegerin(OPG) suppression), favorable toxicity profiles, and cost-effectiveness [131]. Notwithstanding, clinical translation faces pharmacokinetic limitations (oral bioavailability), undefined pharmacodynamic bases, and insufficient Phase III RCT validation [132]. Future investigations must establish a three-tiered translational pipeline: (1) decrypting pyroptosis–SASP dynamics in bone microenvironments; (2) optimizing autophagy inducers’ therapeutic indices; (3) validating clinical efficacy through randomized trials.

Conclusions and perspectives

Osteoporosis involves a critical pyroptosis–autophagy interplay, where inflammatory cell death disrupts bone homeostasis while protective autophagy sustains cellular stability. Their imbalance accelerates bone loss, making this crosstalk a promising therapeutic target. Natural compounds can modulate this axis but face clinical translation challenges due to poor bioavailability, low bone specificity, and complex pharmacokinetics.

To overcome these limitations, integrated strategies are being developed. These include nano-delivery systems for improved targeting and controlled release, prodrug designs activated by bone-specific enzymes, structural optimization of active compounds, and combination therapies with conventional drugs. Such multidisciplinary approaches may enable viable precision treatments targeting pyroptosis/autophagy for osteoporosis.
