Unraveling the mechanisms and evolution of a two-domain module in IQGAP proteins for controlling eukaryotic cytokinesis
Authors: Kangji Wang, Hiroki Okada, Carsten Wloka, Erfei Bi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe IQGAP family of proteins plays a crucial role in cytokinesis across diverse organisms, but the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood. In this study, we demonstrate that IQGAPs in budding yeast, fission yeast, and human cells use a two-domain module to regulate their localization as well as the assembly and disassembly of the actomyosin ring during cytokinesis. Strikingly, the calponin homology domains (CHDs) in these IQGAPs bind to distinct cellular F-actin structures with varying specificity, whereas the non-conserved domains immediately downstream of the CHDs in these IQGAPs all target the division site, but differ in timing, localization strength, and binding partners. We also demonstrate that human IQGAP3 acts in parallel to septins and myosin-IIs to mediate the role of anillin in cytokinesis. Collectively, our findings highlight the two-domain mechanism by which IQGAPs regulate cytokinesis in distantly related organisms as well as their evolutionary conservation and divergence.

INTRODUCTION

Cytokinesis in fungal and animal cells entails spatiotemporally coordinated functions of a contractile actomyosin ring (AMR), targeted exocytosis, and localized extracellular matrix remodel-ng.1–6 However, the mechanistic roles of a multitude of proteins in ensuring the high efficiency and fidelity of cytokinesis remain largely unknown.

IQGAPs play critical roles in cytokinesis, cell migration, cell adhesion, and intracellular signaling by scaffolding signaling complex formation and controlling cytoskeletal assembly and organization.7–9 All three isoforms, IQGAP1–3, in mammalian cells contain a common set of domains including the N-terminal calponin homology domain (CHD) that binds actin filaments followed by the IQGAP-specific repeats (IRs), with poorly understood function.7–9 Only IQGAP3 localizes to the cleavage furrow, and its depletion in HeLa cells causes a mild defect in cytokinesis,10 but the underlying mechanism remains unclear.

Analyses of the sole IQGAP, Iqg1, in the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae and the IQGAP Rng2 in the fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe have provided the most compelling evidence for the roles of this family of proteins in cytokinesis. Both Iqg1 and Rng2 are essential for cell viability and cytokinesis in their respective organisms,11–14 despite their evolutionary divergence about 420 million years ago.15 Both proteins are required for actin ring assembly11–13 and myosin-II maintenance at the division site during cytokinesis.16–19 Both proteins contain all the typical domains of IQGAPs, including the CHD, but not the IR. In fact, the IR-corresponding region in Iqg1 or Rng2 has not been analyzed. More importantly, how Iqg1 or Rng2 drives AMR assembly at the molecular level has remained elusive.

In this study, we found that the experimentally defined CHD and the previously uncharacterized myosin-II tail-associated (MTA) domain in Iqg1 act as a two-domain module to regulate AMR assembly and function in budding yeast. We also found that a similar mechanism is employed by Rng2 in fission yeast and IQGAP3 in humans to control cytokinesis in their respective organisms.

RESULTS

Iqg1 associates with actin filaments and myosin-II via two distinct domains

To understand how Iqg1 controls cytokinesis in budding yeast, we first analyzed its N-terminal 411 amino acids (aa), as deletion of this region, which harbors a “predicted CHD” (Figure 1A), is known to affect cytokinesis.7,16,20 As expected, the full-length (FL) Iqg1, expressed from the constitutive ACT1 promoter, localized to actin cables before anaphase and then to the division site during cytokinesis (Figure 1B). Surprisingly, all three fragments containing the predicted CHD, constructed in the same way as for the FL, failed to decorate any filamentous actin (F-actin) structures (Figures 1A and S1A). In contrast, the fragment containing aa 1–291 localized to cable-like structures (Figures 1A and 1C; Video S1) (the spindle break is used as an indicator of mitotic exit and cytokinesis onset during the cell cycle throughout this study21,22). Notably, the cable signal was weak, and the cytoplasmic signal was strong, suggesting a low-affinity interaction with the cables. When two copies of this fragment were placed in tandem, the intensity of cables increased drastically and the cytoplasmic signal was almost depleted (Figure 1D), suggesting that two copies of this fragment can bind to the cables with high affinity. The association between CHD or 23CHD and actin cables was demonstrated by phalloidin staining (Figure S1B) and treatment with the F-actin-disrupting drug latrunculin A (LatA) (Figure 1E).23 These data indicate that Iqg1 aa 1–291 define the “minimal functional CHD” (referred to as CHD hereafter) that can decorate actin cables in vivo.

We also found that the region aa 292–411 and its two sub-fragments all localized to the bud neck but failed to decorate actin cables or patches (Figures 1A, 1F, and S1C and Video S1). Since Iqg1 arrives at the division site after the myosin-II heavy chain Myo1,11,12 and its immobility during cytokinesis depends on Myo1,24 we tested whether the neck localization of Iqg1 aa 361–411 depended on Myo1. Strikingly, this fragment completely failed to localize to the bud neck in a strain carrying myo1–1797 (Figure 1G), a truncation allele that encodes Myo1 lacking its C-terminal 131 residues (aa 1,798–1,928).24 Based on this observation, we designate Iqg1 aa 361–411 as the MTA domain. In contrast to CHD, LatA treatment did not affect the localization of MTA (Figure 1H). Thus, we have identified an unexpected domain in Iqg1 that localizes to the division site in a Myo1-tail-dependent but F-actin-independent manner.

The CHD and MTA act synergistically to enhance their localization efficiency and specificity at the division site

To determine the functional relationship between CHD and MTA, we monitored their localization efficiency and specificity when they acted alone or together. As described above, CHD alone localized to actin cables throughout the cell cycle (Figure 1C), but addition of the MTA biased CHD localization toward the division site (Figure 1I, bottom; Video S1). Reciprocally, MTA alone localized to the division site, albeit inefficiently (Figure 1I, top), but addition of the CHD enhanced its localization efficiency (Figure 1I, bottom; Video S1), resulting in the peak intensity of CHD-MTA being increased by 3.2-fold over that of MTA alone (Figure 1J, −14 min). When F-actin was abolished by LatA (Figure 1K), the peak intensity of preexisting CHD-MTA at the division site was reduced by 42.3% over the DMSO control (Figure 1L, −14 min), indicating that CHD contributes to neck localization by binding to F-actin. As expected, when the C terminus of Myo1 was deleted, CHD-MTA behaved like CHD alone, in terms of co-localization with and dependency on actin cables (Figure 1M). These data suggest that CHD and MTA act synergistically to increase their localization efficiency and specificity at the division site by associating with F-actin and myosin-II, respectively.

The CHD and MTA exert distinct impacts on the localization and function of Iqg1 during the cell cycle

To determine the impact of CHD and MTA on cytokinesis, we first assayed for cell morphology and septum formation in different iqg1 strains. Surprisingly, deletion of the CHD did not cause any obvious defects in cytokinesis, manifested in the formation of “cell chains” consisting of three or more cell bodies (Figure 2A). This was further corroborated by the observation that the accumulation kinetics of Chs2, which synthesizes the primary septum (PS) to stabilize the AMR and drive furrow ingression during cytokinesis,16,25–28 were similar in both wild-type (WT) and iqg1-CHDΔ cells (Figures 2B–2D and S1D–S1F). In contrast, deletion of MTA caused pronounced defects in cytokinesis, especially at 37°C (Figure 2A). The defect in cytokinesis was also demonstrated by an increased level of Chs2 at the division site as well as its slower removal during cytokinesis (Figures 2B–2D and S1D–S1F). We also directly monitored the PS formation by staining chitin with calcofluor white (CW) in different strains after the removal of Chs1 and Chs3 (leaving Chs2 as the sole chitin synthase for PS formation). Three-dimensional (3D) superresolution imaging revealed various defects in PS formation, especially in iqg1-MTAΔ cells (Figure 2E), as manifested by changes in the diameter and intensity of the chitin disk (Figures 2F and 2G) but not the diameter of the bud neck (Figure 2H). The increased chitin synthesis in the mutant cells is consistent with the increased localization and delayed removal of Chs2 at the division site (Figures 2B–2D).

We then analyzed the impact of CHD and MTA on the localization of Iqg1 during the cell cycle. As expected, the WT Iqg1, expressed from its native promoter, began to accumulate at the division site during G2/M, which increased progressively during anaphase, peaked during cytokinesis, and then constricted and disappeared from the division site (Figures 2I–2K).29,30 Iqg1-CHDΔ also localized to the division site (Figure 2I), but its maximal accumulation increased by 69.2% at 23°C (Figure 2J, −1 min) and 90% at 37°C (Figure S1H, −2 min). This was likely due to the increased protein level (Figure S1J). Iqg1-CHDΔ also showed a clear delay in its initial recruitment and subsequent removal (Figure 2I), which were best visualized using the normalized intensities (Figure 2K). The delayed removal was also observed at 37°C (Figures S1G–S1I). Thus, the CHD plays a role in controlling the timing of Iqg1 localization at the division site.

Iqg1-MTAΔ also localized to the division site but disappeared, without apparent constriction, during cytokinesis (Figure 2I). Its maximal intensity at the division site (Figure 2J, −8 min) was increased by 18.3% over the WT at 23°C (Figure 2J, −1 min), despite their similar expression levels (Figure S1J). At 37°C, the peak value of Iqg1-MTAΔ (Figure S1H, −4 min) decreased by 44.8% compared with the WT (Figure S1H, −2 min). In addition, the timing for its peak intensity was advanced by 7 min at 23°C and 2 min at 37°C. These data suggest that the MTA not only regulates the timing of Iqg1 localization but, more importantly, is required for Iqg1 constriction, which presumably accounts for its role in cytokinesis.

The CHD and MTA play distinct roles in the assembly, constriction, and disassembly of the actomyosin ring

The fact that the CHD and MTA associate with F-actin and myosin-II, respectively, raises the possibility that Iqg1 may scaffold AMR assembly via these domains. To test this possibility, we first examined and observed a deficiency in actin ring formation within both iqg1-CHDΔ and iqg1-MTAΔ cells (only the cells with their nuclei migrated to the opposite ends were scored) (Figure 3A). The CHD requirement was not surprising, as this region had been shown to bind F-actin in vitro.31 The MTA requirement was rather surprising, as there was no reported interaction between MTA and F-actin. The formins Bnr1 and Bni1 localize to the division site before and during cytokinesis, respectively,32,33 and are collectively required for actin ring assembly, with Bni1 playing a dominant role in the process.34,35 Because Iqg1 in Candida albicans associates with Bnr1 and Bni1, and the neck localization of Bni1 largely depends on Iqg1 in this organism,36 we examined the localizations of both formins in WT and iqg1-MTAΔ cells in S. cerevisiae and found them to be normal (Figures S1K and S1L). These data suggest that the CHD promotes actin ring formation via its ability to bind F-actin, whereas the MTA promotes actin ring formation by a mechanism that is independent of F-actin binding and formin localization at the division site.

We then examined the localization kinetics of Myo1 at the division site in different strains. As expected, Myo1 in WT cells reached its peak about 7 min before the spindle break and then constricted and disappeared progressively from the division site (Figures 3B–3D).30 However, Myo1 in iqg1-CHDΔ cells was reduced by 32.4% before cytokinesis (Figure 3C, −25 min), indicating a role for CHD in this process. Surprisingly, Myo1 was able to constrict despite the defect of iqg1-CHDΔ cells in actin ring formation (Figure 3B). The initial recruitment of Myo1 was clearly delayed in iqg1-CHDΔ cells, which was best visualized when the pre-anaphase localization of Myo1 was abolished by the deletion of BNI5 (Figures 3E–3G and Video S2), which encodes the sole linker between Myo1 and the septin hourglass at the division site before anaphase.16,37 Myo1 removal was also delayed in iqg1-CHDΔ cells (Figures 3B–3D), which became more apparent when BNI5 was deleted (Figures 3E–3G and Video S2). The tail domain (aa 856–1,928) of Myo1 is known to direct the formation of a “headless” AMR that constricts with a 20%–30% decreased rate.16,38 Strikingly, deletion of CHD caused 100% of the myo1-tail cells (n = 40) to retain one or two spots of Myo1-tail at the division site after AMR constriction (Figures S1M–S1O and Video S2). In contrast, none of the myo1-tail cells (n = 48) retained a spot of Myo1-tail. Collectively, these data indicate that the CHD is required not only for the timely recruitment and maximal accumulation of Myo1 at the division site before cytokinesis but also for its efficient removal during and after AMR constriction.

Deletion of MTA caused only slightly less Myo1 accumulation before cytokinesis and, more conspicuously, an early departure without constriction from the division site (starting at −11 min, instead of −7 min in WT) (Figures 3B–3D). Deletion of BNI5 further amplified these phenotypes, as both the accumulation and the disappearance of Myo1 at the division site were much slower in the iqg1-MTAΔ bni5Δ double-mutant cells (Figures 3E–3G and Video S2). The peak intensity of Myo1 in the double mutant (Figure 3F, −1 min) was reduced by 47.4% compared with bni5Δ cells (Figure 3F, −3 min). Thus, the MTA plays a clear role in the recruitment of Myo1 to the division site from the onset of anaphase to the onset of AMR constriction. When the iqg1-MTAΔ allele was combined with the myo1-tail allele, the Myo1-tail disappeared from the division site without constriction even more rapidly than the FL Myo1 in iqg1-MTAΔ cells (Figures S1M–S1O and Video S2), suggesting that both the MTA of Iqg1 and the head domain of Myo1 contribute to Myo1 stability during AMR constriction but in different ways. Together, these data indicate that the MTA plays an important role in the recruitment, maintenance, and constriction of Myo1 at the division site before and during cytokinesis.

Why was Myo1 removed from the division site earlier in iqg1-MTAΔ cells? Given that MTA failed to localize in myo1–1797 cells (Figure 1G), and this truncated Myo1 became mobile at the division site during cytokinesis, as revealed by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) analysis,24 we hypothesized that MTA might stabilize Myo1 by interacting with the C terminus of Myo1. To test this hypothesis, we performed FRAP analysis by photobleaching half of the Myo1-GFP ring during cytokinesis and monitoring the fluorescence intensities in the bleached and unbleached regions. As expected, Myo1 was immobile during cytokinesis in WT cells (Figure 3H).24,39 In contrast, Myo1 became mobile in iqg1-MTAΔ cells (Figure 3I). This acquired mobility likely reflects a state of less-organized Myo1 filaments, which would be predicted and indeed were observed to dissociate more rapidly from the division site during the septin hour-glass-to-double-ring transition (Figures S2A and S2B).40 These data indicate that the MTA plays a critical role in stabilizing Myo1 during cytokinesis.

We also examined the impact of MTA phosphorylation on Myo1 behavior. Iqg1 is known to contain 14 CDK1 sites upstream of the isoleucine and glutamine-rich repeat (IQ) motifs.29,41 We mutagenized the three sites (S365, S399, and S404) within the MTA to either alanines (iqg1–3A, the non-phosphorylatable form) or aspartic acids (iqg1–3D, the phosphomimetic form) and used the mutant alleles to replace the endogenous allele precisely. We found that Myo1 was able to localize and constrict in either iqg1–3A or iqg1–3D cells, with no apparent defect in cytokinesis (Figures S2C and S2D). Thus, the MTA phosphorylation does not appear to play a major role in regulating the localization and constriction of Myo1 during cytokinesis.

In addition to the AMR, we examined the localization kinetics of the cytokinesis proteins Inn1 (C2-domain protein), Cyk3 (transglutaminase-like protein), and Hof1 (F-BAR protein), which are known to form a complex involved in Chs2-catalyzed PS formation.42,43 We found that the peak intensity and duration of all three proteins at the division site were increased in iqg1-MTAΔ cells (Figures S3A–S3I), akin to the Chs2 behavior in this mutant (Figures 2B–2D). Similar but milder changes for Inn1 and Cyk3, but not Hof1, were observed in iqg1-CHDΔ cells (Figures S3A–S3I). These data suggest that when the AMR is compromised due to the removal of MTA or CHD, the proteins required for PS formation are increased at the division site, presumably to counter the cytokinesis defect caused by the AMR compromise. When the CHD and MTA were deleted together, Iqg1-CHDΔ-MTAΔ was still able to localize to the division site (Figures S3J–S3L), but its peak intensity dropped by 65.1% compared with WT (Figure S3K). This further corroborates our conclusion that both CHD and MTA contribute to the localization of Iqg1 at the division site. This result also indicates that Iqg1 must contain an additional bud neck-targeting signal(s) downstream of MTA as suggested by our previous analysis.16 Much like the iqg1-MTAΔ cells, Myo1 departed earlier from the division site without apparent constriction, whereas the peak intensity and duration of Chs2 were increased in iqg1-CHDΔ-MTAΔ cells (Figures S3M–S3R), suggesting that the MTA exerts a more prominent role in cytokinesis than the CHD does.

Collectively, these data indicate that the neighboring CHD and MTA of Iqg1 define a two-domain module that regulates AMR assembly, constriction, and disassembly during cytokinesis.

The salient features of the CHD-MTA module are conserved in the fission yeast IQGAP Rng2

To determine whether the CHD-MTA module is conserved in the evolutionarily distant fission yeast S. pombe, we performed a similar structure-function analysis on the sole IQGAP, Rng2, in fission yeast (Figure 4A).7,13,44–46 As expected, Rng2 localized to the nodes and ring upon entry into mitosis (Figure 4B), whereas the CHD (aa 1–190) localized to all F-actin structures including actin cables, actin patches, and the actin ring (Figure 4C and Video S3).44,45 Strikingly, the Rng2 fragment (aa 1–300), previously called Rng2Ns,44 began to localize to the division site at the onset of anaphase and constricted during cytokinesis (Figure 4D and Video S3). The distinct patterns of localization displayed by the two fragments suggest that the region (aa 191–300) downstream of the CHD in Rng2Ns likely possesses the ability to interact with a cytokinesis factor and is, thus, designated the “CykF” domain. Indeed, the fragment (aa 185–300) containing the CykF domain localized weakly, but consistently, to the division site during cytokinesis (Figures 4E and S4A and Video S3), suggesting that the binding affinity between CykF and the cytokinesis factor(s) is low.

Based on our analysis in budding yeast, we hypothesized that Rng2-CHD-CykF might localize to the division site by associating with F-actin and myosin-II via its CHD and CykF domain, respectively. To test this hypothesis, we first treated a mixture of cells expressing Rng2-CHD or Rng2-CHD-CykF with LatA and found that the localization of both fragments was reduced significantly upon LatA treatment (Figure 4F), demonstrating a critical role for F-actin in their maintenance at the division site. Because the Rng2-CykF signal was rather weak even in WT cells, to avoid misinterpretation, we decided to address the localization dependency issue by examining the accumulation of Rng2-CHD-CykF at the division site in different myosin mutants. We conditionally depleted the essential myosin-II, Myo2, in WT as well as in cells deleted for the non-essential myosin-II, Myp2,17,47 and found that the pattern and intensity of Rng2-CHD-CykF at the division site were similar in WT and myp2D cells (Figures 4G and 4H). When Myo2 was depleted by temperature-dependent, N-degron-mediated degradation,17,47 Rng2-CHD-CykF localized to short cable-like structures, and its intensity at the division site was slightly higher than that in WT cells (Figures 4G and 4H). However, when Myo2 was depleted in myp2D cells, the peak intensity of Rng2-CHD-CykF at the division site (Figure 4H, −6 min) dropped by 22.4% compared with WT (Figure 4H, −4 min). These data suggest that the localization of Rng2-CHD-CykF to the division site partially depends on the presence of both Myo2 and Myp2.

The fact that the CHD-CykF fragment still localized to the division site in myosin-II (Myo2 and Myp2)-depleted cells (Figure 4G), but did not decorate all the F-actin structures as the CHD did, raises the possibility that, in addition to myosin-IIs, the CykF domain might be able to interact with additional cytokinesis factors, at least when it is combined with CHD. The anillin-like protein Mid1 and the F-BAR protein Cdc15 are known to interact with Rng217,18; thus, we examined CHD-CykF localization in cells deleted for mid1+ or harboring a temperature-sensitive (Ts) mutation in cdc15+. In mid1Δ cells, the peak intensity of CHD-CykF at the division site dropped by 23.3% (Figures 4I and 4J). In the cdc15-Ts mutant grown and imaged at 36°C, the localization and maintenance of CHD-CykF at the division site were severely compromised (Figure 4K). In contrast, when the formin Cdc12, which is known to interact with Cdc15,48 was conditionally inactivated by a Ts mutation, CHD-CykF still localized to the division site but as scattered puncta (Figure 4L), suggesting that the Cdc12-nucleated actin filaments are required for the compaction of the CHD-CykF puncta into a ring structure. These results suggest that the localization of CHD-CykF to the division site partially depends on Mid1 and Cdc15.

Taken together, these data demonstrate that the CHD of Rng2 boosts the localization of the CykF domain to the division site, whereas CykF constrains the localization of CHD to the division site, preventing its association with actin cables and patches. This behavior resembles the mutual control between CHD and MTA of Iqg1 in budding yeast. Thus, the salient features of the CHD-MTA module in Iqg1 are conserved in the CHD-CykF module of Rng2 in fission yeast, with the exception that Iqg1-MTA associates with myosin-II, whereas Rng2-CykF associates with myosin-II, anillin (Mid1), and F-BAR protein (Cdc15).

The CHD-CykF module plays a critical role in cytokinesis by controlling actomyosin ring assembly in fission yeast

To determine the function of the CHD-CykF module in fission yeast, we deleted the DNA sequences encoding the CHD, CykF, or both at the endogenous locus precisely (Figure 5A) and examined their growth and cytokinesis phenotypes at both 25°C and 36°C. None of the deletions caused any obvious growth defect at either temperature (Figure S4B). Defects in cytokinesis were then examined by monitoring both cell-chain and PS formation (Figures 5B–5D and S4C). We found that the rng2-CHDΔ cells displayed similar cell and septum morphologies compared with the WT (rng2+) cells at both temperatures (Figures 5B–5D and S4C). The rng2-CykFΔ cells did not show any apparent defect in cell morphology, but ~10% of the cells formed a partial or complete septum doublet at both temperatures, suggesting a moderate defect in cytokinesis (Figures 5B–5D and S4C). However, when both domains were deleted together (rng2-CHDΔ-CykFΔ), most cells showed strong defects in cytokinesis, especially at 36°C, where >90% of the cells formed cell chains with various abnormal septa, including some “catastrophic” structures (Figures 5B–5D). These data indicate that CHD and CykF each play a specific and fine-tuning role in cytokinesis, but, together, they play a crucial role in this process, especially at the high temperature.

To determine the mechanisms of action for CHD and the CykF domain, we first examined their impact on Rng2 localization. We inserted GFP at the N terminus of the respective rng2 alleles at the endogenous locus such that the tagged alleles were under the rng2+ promoter control. As expected, GFP-Rng2 was initially recruited to the cytokinetic nodes at the division site (Figures 5E and 5F, −30 min). The accumulation was rapid from −30 to −25 min (designated the “rapid phase”), which was followed by a “slow phase” from −25 to +10 min, during which a ring structure emerged as the result of node coalescence at −9.6 ± 3.5 min (Figures 5E–5G) and then started constriction around the spindle break (Figure 5H, 0 min). The slow phase ended when Rng2 reached its peak during ring constriction (Figures 5F and 5H, +10 min). Rng2 then disappeared from the division site after ring constriction (Figures 5F and 5H, +25 min). GFP-Rng2-CHDΔ behaved like the WT in all aspects (Figures 5E–5H), except for the loss of accumulation during the slow phase (Figure 5F, from −15 to 0 min). The same phenomenon was observed with GFP-Rng2-CHDΔ-CykFΔ but not with GFP-Rng2-CykFΔ, suggesting that the CHD was responsible for this lack of accumulation during the slow phase. Strikingly, GFP-Rng2-CykFΔ showed a significant delay (about 8 min) in the node-to-ring transition compared with WT (Figures 5E and 5F, arrowheads), and this delay was accompanied by the formation of abnormal rings in ~50% of the cells (Figures 5G and S4D). The removal of GFP-Rng2-CykFΔ was also slowed down (Figures 5E and 5F), and its rate of constriction was reduced by ~30% compared with GFP-Rng2 (Figure 5H, p < 0.01). Surprisingly, GFP-Rng2-CHDΔ-CykFΔ showed a phenotype similar to that of GFP-Rng2-CHDΔ at 23°C (Figures 5E–5H), and its cytokinesis phenotype was not worsened at 36°C (Figure S4E). Given the pronounced cytokinesis defects displayed by the non-tagged Rng2-CHDΔ-CykFΔ (Figures 5B–5D and S4C), this observation suggests that the N-terminal GFP tagging somehow suppressed the mutant phenotype. While the precise mechanism for the suppression remains unknown, this observation could explain why the N-terminally GFP-tagged rng2-CHDΔ-CykFΔ (i.e., GFP-rng2-(aa1–300)D) suppressed the temperature sensitivity of rng2-D5 cells at 36°C robustly,46 whereas the non-tagged allele suppressed it poorly.44 Despite this caveat, these data suggest that CHD and CykF play distinct roles in controlling the localization, ring assembly, and constriction of Rng2 at the division site.

We then examined actin ring assembly in different rng2 mutants. Surprisingly, the rng2-CHDΔ cells formed WT-like actin rings, as revealed by phalloidin staining (Figure 6A), despite the ability of CHD to bind F-actin.31,44 Time-lapse analysis using the mNeonGreen (mNG)-tagged tropomyosin Cdc8 as a biosensor for the actin ring49 confirmed this result (Figures 6B–6D). The rng2-CykFΔ cells also assembled actin rings (Figures 6A and 6B), but the ring emergence and removal were significantly delayed (Figures 6B–6D). The rng2-CHDΔ-CykFΔ cells displayed a slight but significant delay in actin ring removal (Figures 6B and 6C, p < 0.05, after +26 min). Strikingly, nearly all the rng2-CHDΔ-CykFΔ cells failed to assemble a normal actin ring at 36°C (Figures 6A and 6E). These observations indicate that CHD and CykF play distinct and fine-tuning roles in actin ring assembly, but, together, they are required for actin ring assembly at 36°C.

Finally, we examined the behaviors of both myosin-II isoforms, Myo2 and Myp2, in the rng2 mutants. In the rng2-CHDΔ cells, Myp2 behaved as in WT cells (Figures S4F and S4G and Video S4). In contrast, the peak intensity of Myo2 was reduced by 11.3% (Figures 6F and 6G, p < 0.05) and this reduction was restricted to the period of node-to-ring transition (−28 to −10 min) (Figures 6F and 6G and Video S4). In the rng2-CykFΔ cells, the initial recruitment of Myo2 was delayed, and its peak intensity was also reduced by 9.9% (Figures 6F and 6G, p < 0.01, and Video S4), suggesting that CykF also contributes to Myo2 recruitment. Importantly, the node-to-ring transition for Myo2 was significantly delayed (Figure 6D and Video S4). In addition, the removal of both Myo2 and Myp2 was delayed in this mutant (Figures 6F, 6G, S4F, and S4G and Video S4). Inspired by the FRAP analysis of Myo1 in iqg1-MTAΔ cells in budding yeast, we performed a similar analysis to determine whether the CykF domain affects the turnover of Myo2 and Myp2 at the division site and found no significant change (Figures S4H–S4K). Thus, unlike the MTA in budding yeast, the CykF domain does not control the myosin-II turnover in fission yeast. In the rng2-CHD-D-CykFΔ cells grown at 23°C, the reduction in the maximal accumulation of Myo2 at the division site (by 20.5%) appeared to be the sum of corresponding Myo2 reductions in the rng2-CHDΔ (11.3%) and rng2-CykFΔ (9.9%) cells (Figures 6F and 6G and Video S4). This defect was further exacerbated (reduced by 31.1%) at 36°C (Figure 6I) and was accompanied by a pronounced defect in Myo2 ring formation (Figures 6H and 6J and Video S5). Strikingly, Myp2-GFP exhibited robust aggregation at the division site in the rng2-CHDΔ-CykFΔ cells, in contrast to its uniform distribution in WT cells, and Myp2-GFP moved rapidly within the ring during its constriction at 23°C (Figures S4F and S4L), suggesting that the assembly and stability of the Myp2 ring was compromised. At 36°C, Myp2-GFP aggregate was incorporated into a cable-like structure that moved around the central zone of the cell (Figure S4L and Video S5). The aberrant Myo2 and Myp2 ring structures observed in the rng2-CykFΔ and rng2-CHDΔ-CykFΔ cells likely explain the aberrant septal morphologies observed in these mutants (Figures 5B, 5D, and S4C), as myosin-II rings are thought to guide septum formation during cytokinesis.47 Taken together, these data indicate that CHD and CykF independently contribute to Myo2 recruitment, whereas CykF plays a unique role in the compaction of Myo2 nodes into a ring structure, and together, they control the assembly and stability of Myo2 and Myp2 rings, especially at 36°C.

Collectively, these data indicate that the CHD and CykF in the fission yeast Rng2 appear to define a module that controls AMR assembly and stability during cytokinesis.

The salient features of the CHD-MTA module are also conserved in the human IQGAP3

To determine whether the salient features of the CHD-MTA module of Iqg1 are also conserved in the human IQGAP3, we performed a domain-localization analysis (Figure 7A). To avoid any potential complication by the endogenous protein, we deleted IQGAP3 in HeLa-Kyoto cells using the CRISPR-Cas9 system (Figure 7B). Localization of all the IQGAP3 fragments was assessed in the IQGAP3−/− cells. As expected, the CHD (aa 1–151) of IQGAP3 co-localized with F-actin in interphase cells (Figure 7C), and this co-localization was largely disrupted by LatA treatment (Figure S5A). Surprisingly, the CHD completely failed to localize to the cleavage furrow during cytokinesis (Figure 7D and Video S6).

Equally surprising, the IR region (aa 152–669) of IQGAP3 localized primarily to the nucleus in interphase cells (Figure 7E). However, in dividing cells, the IR region localized to the cleavage furrow but only during the late stage of cytokinesis (Figure 7F and Video S6). Since non-muscle myosin-IIA (NM-IIA) delocalizes from the division site and NM-IIB remains there during the terminal stage of furrow ingression and abscission in HeLa cells,50 we tested the localization dependency of the IR region on NM-IIB and found no obvious impact (Figure 7F). A previous biochemical analysis suggests that the IR region likely mediates the interaction of IQGAP3 with an N-terminal fragment (aa 1–447) of anillin that contains NM-II- and actin-bindings sites.10 Consistent with this observation, we found that depletion of anillin by small interfering RNA (siRNA) completely abolished the localization of IR to the division site (Figure 7F). Thus, the IR localizes to the division site most likely by binding to anillin.

As expected, the CHD-IR fragment (aa 1–669) of IQGAP3 co-localized with F-actin in interphase cells and this localization was sensitive to LatA treatment (Figure S5B). Strikingly, the CHD-IR localized robustly to the division site from furrow initiation to the end of cytokinesis, and LatA treatment caused its fragmentation (Figure 7G and Video S6). These data suggest that the CHD promotes the localization of IRs to the division site even during the early stage of cytokinesis, whereas the IR region enables CHD to bind actin filaments at the division site. Furthermore, the CHD-IR co-localized with anillin throughout cytokinesis in IQGAP3−/− cells (Figure 7H). Similar to the IRs alone (Figure 7F) or the FL IQGAP3,10 the localization of CHD-IR to the cleavage furrow was abolished by siRNA-mediated depletion of anillin, but not of NM-IIB (Figure S5C). Taken together, these data indicate that the CHD-IR localizes to the cleavage furrow by binding to actin filaments and anillin simultaneously. These data also suggest that the CHD-IR of IQGAP3 likely defines the human counterpart of the CHD-MTA module of Iqg1 in budding yeast.

Unlike Iqg1 in budding yeast and Rng2 in fission yeast, IQGAP3 is dispensable for cytokinesis,10 suggesting the existence of additional mechanisms acting in parallel to IQGAP3 to control cytokinesis. In addition to IQGAP3, anillin binds to NM-II.51 Depletion of NM-IIB did not affect the localization of IQGAP3-IR or IQGAP3-CHD-IR (Figures 7F and S5C). Reciprocally, deletion of IQGAP3 did not affect the localization of NM-IIA and NM-IIB (un-published data). These observations raise the possibility that IQGAP3 and NM-II may act in parallel to mediate the role of anillin in cytokinesis. To test this hypothesis, we effectively knocked down NM-IIA and NM-IIB together in WT and IQGAP3−/− cells using the siRNA approach (Figure 7I) and then stained the cells with wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) and DAPI to visualize the cell outline and DNA/nuclei, respectively (Figure 7J). When treated with the control siRNA, only 2.3% ± 0.46% and 3.9% ± 0.69% of the WT and IQGAP3−/− cells showed the multinucleated phenotype (Figure 7J, top plot); however, the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.038). These data suggest that IQGAP3 plays a clear, but mild, role in cytokinesis, consistent with the previous report.10 Strikingly, when treated with the siRNAs against both NM-IIA and NM-IIB, 8.8% ± 2.4% and 20.8% ± 0.67% of the WT and IQGAP3−/− cells became multinucleated, respectively (Figure 7J, bottom plot), and the difference was even more significant (p = 0.009). These phenotypes were essentially copied by partially inhibiting NM-II activity in the WT and IQGAP3−/− cells with 3 μM blebbistatin,52 which resulted in 8.9% ± 1.8% and 21.2% ± 1.6% (p = 0.001) of multinucleated cells, respectively (Figure 7K). Together, these data indicate that IQGAP3 and NM-IIs act in parallel to regulate cytokinesis.

Anillin also binds to septins,53–55 a family of filament-forming proteins involved in cytokinesis and many other processes.56–58 Since septins bind to the C-terminal region,53,55 whereas IQGAP3 and NM-IIs bind to the N-terminal region of anillin,10,51 this raises the possibility that septins may act in parallel to IQGAP3 and NM-IIs to mediate the role of anillin in cytokinesis. To test this possibility, we first knocked down Sept9 in the WT and IQGAP3−/− cells (Figure S5D), which caused 6.7% ± 1.2% and 17.1% ± 1.3% (p = 0.001) multinucleated cells, respectively (Figures S5E and S5F). This suggests that IQGAP3 and Sept9 act in parallel to control cytokinesis. We then knocked down Sept9, NM-IIA, and NM-IIB together (Figure S5G), which caused the WT and IQGAP3−/− cells to become multinucleated at 9.4% ± 1.5% and 20.3% ± 2.2%, respectively (p = 0.003) (Figures S5H and S5I), suggesting that Sept9 and NM-IIs function in the same pathway to regulate cytokinesis. This conclusion is further supported by the observation that septins interact with NM-IIs and this interaction is required for the full activation of NM-IIs by the ROCK and citron kinases.59

Collectively, these data indicate that IQGAP3 act in parallel to Sept9 and NM-IIs to mediate the role of anillin in cytokinesis.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we have defined a two-domain mechanism (i.e., CHD-MTA/CykF/IR) employed by IQGAP family members to regulate their own localization at the division site as well as the assembly and function of the AMR during cytokinesis. In all three models examined (budding yeast, fission yeast, and humans), the CHDs bind to actin filaments in vitro and in vivo presumably via a common structure,20,31,44,46 but apparently differ in binding affinity and/or specificity, leading to organism-specific behaviors. In contrast, the MTA, CykF, and IR domains lack sequence and predicted structural similarity, but all target to the division site by associating with specific cytokinesis factors. The evolvement of the CHDs and the MTA/CykF/IR domains in IQGAP family members reflects their common origin and divergence during evolution.

Defining the CHD-MTA module and its role in AMR assembly, constriction, and disassembly in budding yeast

Using decoration of actin cables as the cellular assay, the boundary of the CHD in Iqg1 has been experimentally determined, and this domain is required not only for actin ring assembly but also for efficient recruitment of Myo1 to the division site before cytokinesis and its subsequent removal during and after cytokinesis. This regulation of Myo1 is likely related to its F-actin-binding function, as depletion of actin filaments by LatA resulted in similar Myo1 behaviors.30 We have also identified an unexpected domain, i.e., the MTA, which is required not only for actin ring assembly but also for the recruitment and stabilization of Myo1 during cytokinesis. Myo1 at the division site is mobile before cytokinesis and becomes immobile during cytokinesis.24 This change in mobility presumably reflects a cell-cycle-dependent change in its organizational state, as Myo1 filaments were observed only in cells synchronized at the mitotic exit or during cytokinesis, not at earlier stages of the cell cycle.40,60 Strikingly, Myo1 becomes more mobile during cytokinesis in iqg1-MTAΔ cells, resembling the behavior of Myo1 lacking its C-terminal region (aa 1,798–1,928).24 Importantly, this region of Myo1 is required for the localization of MTA to the division site. Thus, Iqg1 likely controls the immobility or a specific organizational state of Myo1 via a direct or an indirect interaction between the MTA and the C terminus of Myo1.

Taken together, our analyses indicate that Iqg1 associates with F-actin and Myo1 via its CHD and MTA, respectively. These domains help each other to achieve efficient localization of Iqg1 to control the assembly, constriction, and disassembly of the AMR during cytokinesis.

Conservation and divergence of the budding yeast CHD-MTA module in fission yeast and human cells

Motivated by our work in budding yeast, we decided to determine whether the budding yeast CHD-MTA module is conserved in the fission yeast and human IQGAPs. The CHD in the fission yeast Rng2 decorates all F-actin structures in vivo44,45 and binds to actin filaments in vitro.31,44,46 Our analysis indicates that this CHD regulates the accumulation of Myo2 at the division site specifically during the node-to-ring transition, akin to the CHD regulation of Myo1 accumulation before cytokinesis in budding yeast. However, in contrast to the CHD of Iqg1, the CHD of Rng2 is dispensable for actin ring formation, highlighting the divergence of the CHDs over evolution.

Rng2Ns (aa 1–300) was reported to localize and constrict at the division site,44 but the underlying mechanism has remained unknown. Our analysis indicates that Rng2Ns contains two separate domains, the CHD and the CykF. The CykF domain localizes weakly but clearly to the division site. Like the MTA in budding yeast, the CykF regulates the accumulation and constriction of both myosin-IIs, Myo2 and Myp2, at the division site. Different from the MTA, the CykF is not required for actin ring formation or myosin-II turnover, but it plays a clear role in the node-to-ring compaction.

Like the CHD-MTA module in budding yeast, the CHD of Rng2 boosts the localization of CykF to the division site, whereas the CykF constrains the CHD localization to the division site only. The CHD is known to stabilize the actin ring in fission yeast,45 whereas addition of the CykF presumably decreases the binding affinity of the CHD to F-actin, as indicated by the observation that GFP-CHD-CykF at the division site was clearly more sensitive than GFP-CHD to LatA treatment. Thus, the CykF appears to antagonize the CHD activity, allowing F-actin to be more dynamic at the division site. The binding of the CHD to F-actin also inhibits myosin-II movement along actin filaments.61 This antagonistic relationship between CHD and CykF can explain the observation that the node-to-ring transition was delayed in rng2-CykFΔ cells (presumably due to the dual role of the CHD in stabilizing F-actin and inhibiting myosin-II movement at the division site) but not in rng2-CHDΔ-CykFΔ cells. The functional analysis of the CHD-CykF module also provides experimental evidence for the hypothesis that F-actin dynamics and myosin-II movement along actin filaments are both required for efficient node-to-ring transition.62,63

Like the CHD in the budding yeast Iqg1 or the fission yeast Rng2,31,44 the CHD of human IQGAP3 binds actin filaments in vitro.64 Surprisingly, this CHD decorates stress fibers in interphase cells but completely fails to localize to the division site during cytokinesis. Equally surprisingly, the IR region of IQGAP3, which corresponds to the MTA of Iqg1 in budding yeast and the CykF of Rng2 in fission yeast, localizes to the division site only during late stages of cytokinesis. However, when CHD and IR are combined, the two-domain module localizes not only to the stress fibers in interphase cells but also robustly to the division site throughout the furrowing process. This division-site localization depends on F-actin and anillin. Thus, the CHD appears to boost the localization of the IR region, whereas the IR enables and constrains the binding of CHD to F-actin at the division site only, akin to the CHD-MTA in budding yeast and the CHD-CykF in fission yeast. Importantly, our analysis has also revealed that IQGAP3 acts in parallel to NM-IIs and Sept9 to mediate the role of anillin in cytokinesis.

Limitations of the study

While our genetic and cell biological analyses indicate that IQGAPs in budding yeast, fission yeast, and mammalian cells employ a two-domain strategy to regulate their localization as well as the assembly, constriction, and disassembly of the AMR during cytokinesis, several mechanistic questions remain unanswered. For example, the CHDs in Iqg1, Rng2, and IQGAP3 display varying specificity for distinct F-actin structures in their native cells, but the structural basis for this specificity remains unknown. Remarkably, the MTA, CykF, and IR differ in sequence, but all can target to the division site in their native cells by interacting, either directly or indirectly, with specific cytokinesis factors such as myosin-II, anillin, and F-BAR protein. The biochemical basis and spatiotemporal regulation of these interactions are also largely unknown.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Conditions for culturing budding yeast, fission yeast and HeLa cells

For budding yeast, standard culture media and genetic techniques were used65. Cells were grown routinely at 25°C in synthetic complete (SC) minimal medium lacking specific amino acid(s) and/or uracil or in rich medium YM-166 or yeast extract/peptone/dextrose (YPD). For fission yeast, standard culture media and methods were used67. Cells were grown in YE5S medium consisting of 0.5% yeast extract, 3% dextrose, and 0.0225% of each of the supplements: adenine, histidine, leucine, uracil, and lysine or EMM-AUH [Edinburgh Minimal Medium (EMM) with adenine, uracil, and histidine]. Cells were grown at 25°C or 30°C unless otherwise indicated. For Hela cells, they were routinely maintained in DMEM supplied by 10% FBS and 13 Plasmocin® Prophylactic and cultured in 37°C incubator supplied with 5% CO2. When required, stock solutions of 20 mM Latrunculin A (LatA) (in DMSO, Wako, Osaka, Japan) and 1% (w/v) Calcofluor White (CW) (in distilled water, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) were added to the medium to final concentrations as indicated.

Constructions of yeast and HeLa cells containing genetically modified genes

The budding yeast and fission yeast strains used in this study are listed in Tables S1 and S2, respectively. Strains were constructed by integrating a plasmid carrying a modified gene at a genomic locus, by transferring a deletion or tagged allele of a gene from a plasmid or one strain to another via PCR amplification, or by mating followed by random spore analysis; see footnotes in Tables S1 and S2. For Hela cells, gene deletion was made using the CRISPR-Cas9 system68, and GFP-tagged proteins were introduced into the cells by lentiviral infection.

Live cell imaging and data analysis

Quantitative time-lapse imaging analysis in budding yeast and fission yeast was performed as described previously, with slight modifications76. For budding yeast, cells were grown in SC medium at 25°C to exponential growth stage, and 1 ml of cell culture was centrifuged at 5000xg for 2 min. The pellet was resuspended in 100 μL fresh SC medium and plated to a concanavalin-A coated glass bottom chamber dish. 5 min after cells were attached to the bottom, excess cells were removed by removing supernatant and 500 μL fresh SC medium was added to the dish. Images were acquired using a Nikon microscope (model Eclipse Ti-U or Eclipse Ti2-U, Tokyo, Japan) with a Nikon 100x/1.49NA oil objective (model CFI Apo TIRF 100x) and a Yokogawa spinning-disk confocal scanner unit (model CSU-X1, Tokyo, Japan). A Photometrics EMCCD camera (Evolve 512 Delta, Tucson, AZ, USA) was used for image capture. Solid-state lasers for excitation (488 nm for GFP, 561 nm for RFP, and 405 nm for DAPI) were housed in a launch constructed by Spectral Applied Research (model ILE-400, Richmond Hill, ON, Canada). The imaging system was controlled by MetaMorph version 7.8.10.0 or version 7.10.4.431 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Images were taken with 11 z-stacks with a step size of 0.7 μm. For fission yeast, cells were grown in YE5S or EMM-AUH medium at 25°C to exponential growth, and 1 mL of the cell culture was centrifuged at 5000 x g for 2 min, and then the pellet was resuspended in 100 μL YE5S or EMM minus leucine liquid medium. For samples imaged at 36°C, cells were further cultured at 36°C for three hours prior to harvesting. The cells were plated to a lectin-coated 35mm glass-bottomed chamber dish. 5 min later, 1 mL YE5S or 500 μL EMM minus leucine medium was added to the top. The sample was imaged at room temperature (~23°C) or 36°C controlled by the OKO temperature control system (Okolab, Ottaviano, NA, Italy). Images were acquired with the same imaging system as for budding yeast. Images were taken with 11 z-stacks with a step size of 1 or 1.1 μm. For Hela cells, cells were grown in poly-D-lysine-coated glass bottom chambered dish with DMEM medium supplied with 10% FBS, medium was changed to fresh DMEM one day before they were imaged at 37°C in OKO chamber supplemented with 5% CO2. A sum or max projection was created with NIH ImageJ (1.53t). The quantification of fluorescence intensities was performed as described previously76. For calculating the constriction rate, we manually measured the contractile ring diameter during constriction from max projection images acquired from time-lapse imaging and then calculated the slope of the diameter curve from seven timepoints, including the midpoint of constriction. Data analyses were performed with Microsoft Excel, GraphPad Prism 9.4.1, and R (ver. 3.0.1).

Phalloidin staining of yeast cells and Hela cells

For budding yeast (Figure 3A), cells were grown in YM-1 at 25°C overnight to exponential growth. Cells were fixed by adding form-aldehyde directly to the culture to achieve a final concentration of 3.7% and shaken at 25°C for 1 h. Fixed cells were washed three times with PBS. The cells were then permeabilized in PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 at 25°C for 15 min. Cells were washed twice with PBS and then incubated at 25°C for 60 min in PBS with 1% BSA and 20 U/mL Alexa Fluor 568-phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Cells were then washed three times with PBS with 1% BSA. Cells were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in 10 μL mounting medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). 2 μL of the cell suspension was used for imaging. Images were acquired using a Nikon microscope described above. Lasers used for excitation were 405 nm for DAPI and 561 nm for Alexa Fluor 568-phalloidin. For fission yeast (Figure 6A), cells were grown in YE5S at 25°C overnight to log-phase, split into two and diluted 20-fold into fresh medium, and incubated at 25°C or 36°C for 5 h. Cell fixation and staining were performed as described for budding yeast.

For Hela cells (Figures 7C, 7E, S5A, and S5B), cells were grown on a 15mm poly-D-lysine-coated coverslips in DMEM medium with 10% BSA in a 12 well plate. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and then washed 3 times with PBS. Cells were then permeabilized in PBS with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min followed by washing with PBS for 3 times. They were then blocked in 1% BSA for 30 min followed by incubating with Alexa Fluor 568-phalloidin (20 U/mL) in 1% BSA for 1 h. Cells were washed three times with PBS and sealed in mounting medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories).

Septum staining of budding yeast and fission yeast cells

Budding yeast cells were grown to exponential phase in liquid SC medium. Cells were pelleted and resuspended in SC medium with 10 μg/ml CW (Sigma). Cells were attached to Concanavalin A coated glass bottom dish for VT-iSIM imaging.

To determine the cytokinetic phenotype by visualizing septum morphology (Figures 5B and S4C), fission yeast cells were grown to exponential phase in liquid YE5S at 25°C, diluted 20-fold into fresh medium, and incubated at 25°C or 36°C for 5 h. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation and washed once with YE5S containing 50 μg/ml CW (Sigma). Cells were then resuspended in the same medium and subjected to microscopic analysis.

VT-iSIM super-resolution imaging

CW-stained budding cells in a glass-bottom dish were imaged with the VT-iSIM super-resolution imaging system. Images were acquired using an Olympus microscope (model Olympus IX71 inverted microscope, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an Olympus UAPON 100x TIRF/NA1.49 oil immersion objective (Olympus), and a confocal scan head: VisiTech VT-iSIM (VisiTech International, Inc., Sunderland, UK). The Hamamatsu ORCA-Quest qCMOS (model C15550–20UP, Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan) was used for image capture. The imaging system was controlled by MetaMorph (Molecular Devices). Images were taken with 23 z-stacks each of 0.3μm. Microvolution deconvolution plugin of ImageJ was used to deconvolve the VT-iSIM images.

FRAP

Cell culture and sample preparation for FRAP analysis were the same as described above in “Live cell imaging and data analysis”. FRAP analyses in budding yeast (Figures 3H and 3I) and fission yeast (Figures S4H–S4K) were performed as described previously47. The imaging system used consists of a spinning-disk confocal scanner unit (model CSU-X1, Yokogawa) and a microscope (model IX81, Olympus) equipped with a 1003/1.40 NA oil objective (model UPlanSApo 100x/1.40 Oil, Olympus) and an EMCCD camera (model iXon X3 897, Andor Technology, Belfast, UK). MetaMorph ver. 7.8.10.0 (Molecular Devices) was used for hardware control and image acquisition. Diode lasers (488 nm for GFP and 561 nm for RFP) controlled via laser merge module (model LMM5, Spectral Applied Research) were used for excitation. Images were taken at room temperature with 11 z-stacks with a step size of 0.7 μm. For photobleaching, a diode-pumped 405nm laser (model DL405–050-O CrystaLaser, Reno, NV, USA) was applied to a defined subcellular region. Maximum projections were created and analyzed with NIH ImageJ. In ImageJ, a polygon was drawn encircling the bleached area to calculate the integrated density within the area over time. Data were analyzed with Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism 9, and half-life was determined using the one phase-association function in the GraphPad.

CRISPR-Cas9 knock out of IQGAP3 in Hela cells

Two guide RNAs targeting the exon2 and another two guide RNAs targeting the exon38 of IQGAP3 were cloned into the plasmid pX330A_D10A-1×4-Puro using BsaI-mediated Golden Gate cloning method75. The resultant plasmid was transfected into Hela cells using lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Puromycin at the concentration of 0.75 μg/mL was applied to these cells 48h after transfection for selection. 96 h after the puromycin selection, single clones were isolated by diluting the TrypLE (Thermo Fisher Scientific)-disassociated cells into 0.5 cell/100 μL and followed by culturing each 100 μL cells in a 96-well plate for 2 weeks. Single clones were amplified, and cell lysates were prepared for Western blotting to screen for the knockout clones.

Lentivirus packaging and transduction of Hela cells

Lentivirus was packaged by co-transfecting HEK-293T cells with the plasmid pLJM1-GFP-IQGAP3(aa1–151), pLJM1-GFP-IQGA-P3(aa152–669), or pLJM1-GFP-IQGAP3(aa1–669), together with the packaging plasmids pMDLg/pRRE and pRSV-Rev and the VSV-G envelope-expressing vector pMD2.G using the lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Medium was changed to DMEM with 10% FBS 8 h after transfection. The supernatant containing lentivirus was collected at 32 h and 56 h post transfection. The supernatants collected at these two timepoints were combined and filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter. The filtered lentivirus-containing medium was added to Hela cells in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS. Polybrene was added to a final concentration of 8 μg/mL. The expression of tagged proteins was checked 48 h after transduction.

siRNA-mediated knockdown of gene expression in Hela cells

siRNA-mediated knock down of gene expression was performed by following the Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent Manufacturer’s reverse transfection protocol, with some modifications. Briefly, RNAi duplex-Lipofectamine RNAiMAX complexes were prepared by combining the mixture of 50 μL opti-MEM medium plus 2 μL Lipofectamine RNAiMAX with the mixture of 50 μL opti-MEM medium plus 6 pmol siRNA. The combined solution was then incubated at 23°C for 20 min and then mixed with 1mL Hela cells at the concentration of 0.03×106 cells/mL. 72 h later, cells were either used for time-lapse imaging or fixed for staining.

The primary rabbit anti-NM-IIA (Cell Signaling, 3403) or rabbit anti-NM-IIB (Cell Signaling, 3404) antibody was diluted at 1:1000 and the mouse anti-α-tubulin antibody was diluted at 1:3000 with the Antibody Diluent followed by incubating with the membrane at 4°C for overnight. The membrane was then washed once in washing buffer and incubated with IRDye® 800CW goat anti-mouse secondary antibody that was diluted at 1:8000 with the Antibody Diluent at 23°C for 1 h. The Fast Western Mouse Optimized HRP Reagent was added to the solution followed by incubation at 23°C for another 20 min. The membrane was then washed with the washing buffer supplied in the kit for 3 times, incubated with the SuperSignal™ West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate for 5 min, and then scanned by the LI-COR ODYSSEY M (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA) controlled by the LI-COR Acquisition1.1 software.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Imaging data were quantified using NIH ImageJ and analyzed with Microsoft Excel, GraphPad Prism 9.4.1, and R (ver. 3.0.1). For statistical analysis, a two-sided unpaired t-test (assuming unequal variances) was performed.
