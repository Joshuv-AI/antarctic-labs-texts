Intraoperative measurement of breast-conserving surgery margins: surgical application
Authors: Wei-Ling Chen, Wan-Ling Liu, Cui-Ping Guo, Yuan-Yuan Zhu, Zi-He Ming, Yu-Tong Fang, Wan-Di Zhang, Rong Chen, Jun-Dong Wu, Jie Li, Christobel M Saunders, Yong-Qu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast-conserving surgery (BCS) is an effective treatment for early-stage cancer, provided that there is “no ink on tumor” reported in the excised breast specimen. However, approximately 15–35% of patients require additional surgery due to the presence of malignant cells at the surgical margin. In this review, we examine the available data on margin status in breast cancer and compare the advantages and disadvantages of different approaches in BCS margin evaluation. Of note, there are numerous concerns regarding traditional methods for assessing margins, and emerging technological approaches are gaining attention. The use of new imaging devices and molecular probes for surgical navigation represents a significant research direction for the future. Ultimately, collaboration between scientists from various fields is essential in reducing positive rates at breast-conserving margins and improving re-excision rates with associated poor outcomes, and ultimately decreasing recurrence.Keywords: Breast-conserving surgery, Margin assessment, Intraoperative measures

Introduction

Breast-conserving surgery (BCS) aims to completely remove tumors while preserving healthy breast tissue, with negative margins crucial for success. As illustrated in Fig. 1, histological margin assessment is the gold standard for confirming tumor clearance. If margins are positive or close, reoperation is necessary, which increases the risk of recurrence patient distress, therapy delays, costs, and complications [1]. Current methods of accurate intraoperative margin evaluation, like palpation and visual inspection, which lack precision. Though frozen section analysis and advanced imaging (e.g., micro-CT, MRI) are more reliable, they are limited by time, resources, and training requirements [2]. Conventional real-time imaging tools, such as mammography and ultrasound, although widely accessible, suffer from insufficient sensitivity and specificity for margin delineation [2]. It is important to distinguish these specimen-based assessment tools from intraoperative tumor localization techniques (e.g., wire-guided localization, radioguided occult lesion localization), which serve to guide the initial surgical excision but do not directly evaluate the final margin status of the resected specimen.

Recent advancements have introduced new technologies for real-time intraoperative margin assessment, such as radiofrequency spectroscopy (MarginProbe) [3, 4], bioimpedance spectroscopy (ClearEdge) [5], optical coherence tomography (OCT) [6], quantitative micro-elastography (QME) [7], and fluorescence-based probes. Despite promising initial results, these methods face challenges like high costs, limited access, and steep learning curves [3–7]. There remains a need for tumor-specific, real-time imaging that integrates smoothly into surgical workflows. Emerging fluorescent-guided navigation systems, especially those combining visible light with near-infrared imaging, could improve surgical precision by enhancing depth penetration and spatial resolution. However, standardized protocols and centralized training are crucial for widespread adoption [8].

This review evaluates current and emerging techniques for BCS margin assessment, analyzing their clinical applicability, limitations, and future directions, and aims to optimize margin assessment protocols to advance precision in breast cancer surgery.

Breast-conserving surgery (BCS)

In the 1970s, Fisher's hypothesis revolutionized breast cancer surgery by challenging the traditional view of tumor spread [9]. This led to large-scale trials showing that BCS with radiotherapy was as effective as modified radical mastectomy for early-stage breast cancer, in terms of recurrence and survival [10]. As a result, BCS became the standard treatment, focusing on preserving healthy tissue without losing effectiveness.

However, BCS faces the challenge of balancing cancer safety with cosmetic outcomes, as inadequate margins can increase recurrence risk, while overly wide excisions can harm appearance and function, affecting patient quality of life. Consequently, the definition and intraoperative assessment of “adequate” margins represent a pivotal yet unresolved challenge in surgical decision-making.

Margins in breast cancer

How much margin is enough?

The National Surgical Adjuvant Breast and Bowel Project B06 (NSABP B06) trial set the “no ink on tumor” standard for margins in BCS for invasive carcinoma [11]. However, current practice faces three challenges that call for a reevaluation of margin standards: lack of international consensus on optimal margin width leading to high reoperation rates, limitations in pathological margin assessment, and evolving understanding of the biological determinants of local recurrence [12]. This highlights the need for a standardized, evidence-based approach to margin definition.

Guidelines on how much is an adequate margin

This study identified 15 national guidelines on negative margins for breast cancer surgery, which are summarized in Table 1. For invasive breast cancer, the guidelines vary, with definitions of adequate margins ranging from less than 4 mm to at least 1 or 2 mm tumor-free areas [13–27]. According to the Society of Surgical Oncology (SSO)-American Society of Therapeutic Radiology and Oncology (ASTRO) guidelines, no tumor cells should be present on the resection surface in patients receiving whole-breast radiation [18]. For ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), SSO-ASTRO-ASCO (American Society of Clinical Oncology) recommended a 2 mm tumor-free margin which has been associated with low recurrence rates [23]. Although consensus has been reached on margin definitions, real-time guidance during surgery remains a challenge.

The traditional methods and limitations of marginal evaluation in BCS

Frozen section analysis allows real-time histological assessment and significantly reduces reoperation rates post-lumpectomy (13.2% vs. 3.6%, P < 0.001) [36]. However, its widespread use is limited by long processing times (> 30 min), the need for specialized expertise, and a shortage of pathologists [2]. Imprint cytology, while offering similar diagnostic accuracy, only provides qualitative assessments [30]. However, these methods remain susceptible to sampling error that can miss multifocal DCIS or the single-cell growth typical of invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) [29]. Also, these methods widely vary across the globe, and this can also dictate the rate of re-excision, increase pathologists' workload, and restrict their use to a few hospitals in some developed countries.

Imaging-based assessment techniques

Recent advancements in real-time image-guided localization techniques, such as radioactive seed localization, wire-guided localization (WGL) [37], and radioguided occult lesion localization (ROLL) [38], have transformed the management of non-palpable lesions but do not guarantee microscopic ink clearance. Traditional wire localization is associated with substantial re-excision when multifocality or extensive intraductal components are present [39]. Intraoperative specimen radiography helps verify margins in tumors (Fig. 2a–d) but is hindered by tissue displacement and longer surgery times, and it doesn't reduce re-excision rates [39, 40]. Intraoperative and conventional ultrasound offer real-time, cost-effective visualization but are limited by low sensitivity for certain conditions, such as DCIS, multifocal disease, microcalcifications, many ILCs and fibrotic tissue following neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) because it assesses gross anatomy rather than microscopic inked margins (Fig. 2e and f) [29, 35]. These limitations underscore the need for a precise, real-time method for intraoperative margin evaluation in BCS.

OCT

OCT (Fig. 3a–h) offers non-invasive, label-free imaging with micron-scale resolution [50]. While widely used in interventional cardiology, its potential in BCS margin assessment is promising but limited by factors like restricted scanning area, high cost, low accuracy of the perimeter tool, and its focus on excised specimens rather than the surgical cavity [6, 7].

Micro-CT

Micro-CT (Fig. 3i–k) is a three-dimensional imaging technique originally developed for industrial applications, but it has demonstrated promise in medicine for assessing surgical specimens, including partial mastectomy samples. In the context of BCS, micro-CT enables effective intraoperative margin assessment and demonstrates superior correlation to pathological tumor size compared to MRI, ultrasound, or mammography [7, 51]. Its compact, self-shielding design allows for placement near operating rooms, offering advantages such as rapid scanning and high-resolution images at near-cellular resolution (5–20 microns). However, several challenges must be addressed before widespread clinical adoption, including limited comparative studies, absence of biochemical data, constraints in resolution limits, and exposure to X-ray radiation. Further research is needed to evaluate its impact on margin assessment and re-excision rates in BCS. Importantly, micro-CT evaluates only the resected specimen and not the surgical cavity; therefore, it cannot confirm the presence of residual tumor in the patient.

Ex vivo MRI

MRI (Fig. 3l–q) has become a useful tool for the localization of breast cancer with high sensitivity [52, 53], particularly in situ disease [52, 54]. Although MRI-guided biopsy is considered useful in assessing tumor margins, it faces challenges like lesion localization and ensuring complete resection. Given the small size of the tumor, intraoperative manual detection can be challenging. Ex vivo MRI has emerged as a novel approach that facilitates the removal and verification of MRI-detected lesions during surgery [55]. However, similar to micro-CT, ex vivo MRI re-scans specimens rather than the surgical cavity. Therefore, it cannot confirm whether any residual tumor remain in the patient.

Radiofrequency spectroscopy: MarginProbe

A recently FDA-approved device (MarginProbe, Dune Medical Devices, Caesarea, Israel) (Fig. 3r and s), designed to assess incision margins by measuring breast tissue's electrical properties, distinguishes between normal and tumor tissues [3, 56]. It includes a console, cable, and handheld probe. In a study by Schnabel et al. with 596 lumpectomy patients, the device showed a false-negative rate of 24.8% versus 66.1% in the control group. Re-excision was needed in 19.8% of the device group versus 25.8% of the control group, indicating a 6% absolute reduction. No difference in resected tissue volume was noted [4]. LeeVan et al. [57] reported marginProbe performed equally well as surgeon’s standard operating procedure alone in terms of sensitivity and specificity, with a very high negative predictive value but very low positive predictive value.

Bioimpedance spectroscopy: ClearEdge Device

ClearEdge TM(CE) (Fig. 3t and u) is a portable imaging device using bioelectrical impedance spectroscopy to distinguish between benign and malignant tissues by analyzing their dielectric properties [5]. It helps surgeons identify abnormal tissue at the BCS margin and detect conditions like atypical hyperplasia and invasive carcinoma. A clinical trial demonstrated that CE reduced reoperation rates compared to tissue radiographs, with accuracy similar to permanent section pathology. The margin assessment accuracies observed in Phase I and Phase II were comparable to those of permanent section pathology, with sensitivity values of 84.3% and 87.3%, and specificity values of 81.9% and 75.6%. In Phase II, the use of CE, was associated with a reduction in the resection rate from 37 to 17%, suggesting a potential reduction in re-excision rates more than 50% [5]. The device is user-friendly, allowing imaging of all margins in under 5 min, with each image taking 3 s. However, during the study, its user interface software remained under development and had not yet undergone broad validation [28].

Raman spectroscopy with autofluorescence microscopy

Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 3vi-wiii) detects molecular vibrations using laser light and has demonstrated potential for breast cancer diagnosis. However, its clinical application is limited by prolonged analysis times and difficulty imaging small tumors within intact tissues. During BCS, the technique can be applied rapidly to precisely excised samples, although the use of small samples may compromise diagnostic accuracy. From a clinical standpoint, a cautious approach is recommended—a single positive measurement should suggest possible margin involvement. While device offers objective tumor margin assessment, the final decision regarding margin width ultimately rests with the surgeon [44].

Chemical probes

Optical molecular imaging is an advanced technology that enables visualization of molecules processes at multiple scales with high speed and resolution. It offers a safe, radiation-free approach for real-time tumor imaging during surgery. Recent studies have highlighted progress in the development of fluorescence-based probes for cancer diagnosis [58]. Traditional imaging agents such as FDA-approved indocyanine green (ICG) and methylene blue (MB), operate within the first NIR window (NIR-I, 650–900 nm) and demonstrate good performance,but are limited by relatively shallow tissue penetration (~ 1 mm) and low signal-to-noise ratios (SNR). In contrast, the second NIR window (NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm) offers deeper tissue penetration (~ 10 mm) and reduced photon scattering, making it ideal for surgical guidance [59]. Therefore, the exploration of clinically safe NIR-II probes holds significant potential to advance in vivo imaging and improve surgical outcomes in tumor resection.

Advances in instrumentation and contrast agents for fluorescence imaging in BCS margin assessment

Fluorescence imaging, particularly with wide-field macroscopic imaging, enable the detection of fluorescent signals several millimeters beneath the tissue surface using fluorescent-sensitive cameras and appropriate optical filters, especially within in the near-infrared spectrum [60]. This technique offers high sensitivity, specificity, without exposure to ionizing radiation. Its capacity for real-time visualization of lesion has shown considerable promise in BCS, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Fluorescence imaging relies on contrast agents to excite tissue fluorophores. Conventional NIR dyes such as MB and ICG are already FDA-approved for clinical use. Surgical instrumentation

Multispectral fluorescence imaging system

Due to the lower tissue attenuation of near-infrared light, NIR-based systems achieve deeper penetration than visible light modalities. A novel real-time fluorescence imaging system has been developed for intraoperative use, featuring a correction algorithm that compensates for uneven light absorption in tissues. This system uniquely captures fluorescence, light absorption, and color images simultaneously through a single lens, operating in both external and transparent modes. It employs a 750 nm laser diode for fluorescence excitation and a 250W halogen lamp for color imaging. (Fig. 4a–c) [61, 62]. Subsequently, Folic acid was conjugated to fluorescein thiocyanate to target the folic acid receptor alpha, enabling real-time multispectral imaging. This approach facilitated for the first real-time visualization of tumor-specific fluorescence during exploratory laparotomy in patients with suspected ovarian cancer [62].

Lumicell (LUM) imaging system

The LUM Imaging System consists of LUM015, a PEGylated protease-activated fluorescent agent, along with an optical head, a handheld probe, and image analysis software. A 630-nm light-emitting diode (LED) is used to excite LUM015, and the emitted fluorescent signals are captured by a charge-coupled device (PCO AG, Germany), with a reported signal value of 1010 counts/s/cm2. The handheld probe features a 2.6 cm field of view, and is covered with a sterile sleeve for intraoperative use [63]. In a Phase I study involving 15 patients, the system successfully differentiated malignant sarcoma and breast tumors from normal tissue in ex vivo samples [64]. A subsequent Phase II study demonstrated its ability to rapidly identify residual tumors in breast cancer lumpectomy cavities during BCS, suggesting its potential to reduce positive margin rates [65].

Fluorescence goggle system

A Fluorescence Goggle System was created to assist in tumor surgery. It used a standard night vision viewer with the iGEN NV20/20-IC lens and an 820 nm long-pass filter for tumor imaging. The light source was a modified headlamp with two 770 nm NIR LEDs replacing central white LEDs, while peripheral white LEDs remained for illumination [66]. Subsequently, the system was updated with four high-power LEDs and a 775 nm short-pass filter. This system successfully yielded promising results in breast cancer surgery. During procedures, 11 sentinel lymph nodes were detected in 4 patients (100% detection rate), and tumor margin status was accurately predicted in 3 additional patients, consistent with postoperative pathological findings [67].

Small portable interchangeable imager of fluorescence (SPIIF)

The SPIIF systerm consists of a 740 nm light source and two charge-coupled device (CCD) cameras fixed above the operating room table. Filters divide the emitted light into bright fields and near-infrared images, which are overlaid for the precise localization of fluorescent probes in tissue. Housed in a stainless-steel case above the table, the SPIIF system detected 16 of 18 primary pulmonary nodules and five additional subcentimeter nodules, including metastatic adenocarcinomas and sarcomas, in a previous study. This technology is capable of identifing nodules as small as 0.2 cm and as deep as 1.3 cm from the pleural surface. This study marked the first in-human use of NIR imaging to detect pulmonary nodules during thoracic surgery without prior knowledge of their location [68].

Portable real-time optical detection identification and guide for intervention(PRODIGI)

PRODIGI is a handheld device for real-time fluorescence imaging of breast specimens and surgical cavities (Fig. 4d–j) [69, 70]. It emits 405 nm light at 1.14 mW/cm2 from 10 cm away, using consumer-grade sensors, LEDs, and multiband optical filters to capture high-resolution images. The device can image porphyrins, like protoporphyrin IX, which fluoresce red (peak emission at 635 nm) when excited by violet-blue light (400–410 nm). A phase II trial evaluated PRODIGI's imaging performance with 5-ALA HCl versus no contrast in breast cancer patients undergoing surgery, confirming its safety, feasibility, and potential to enhance tumor visualization during BCS, achieved 100% detection of residual tumor within surgical margins [70].

DPM H2000 fluorescence imaging device

The DPM H2000 device (Fig. 4 k and l) is a four-channel imaging device invented by our collaborators at the Chinese Academy of Sciences. It features independent sensors for red, blue, yellow, and near-infrared light. These four modes can be be seamlessly switched with a single button at a frame rate of 60 fps. Because of the advanced design, the image is more realistic, and the details are richer. Therefore, surgeons can perform precise navigation during different surgeries. The greatest advantage of this device is that fusion imaging can show lesions without extinguishing the operating room light. Our team is currently using this device for fluorescence-guided BCS and axillary sentinel lymph node biopsies.

Molecular probes

ICG

Cyanine dyes, which consist of heterocycles linked by a methine chain, are ideal for NIR-I fluorescence imaging due to their high absorption and biocompatibility [49]. Several cyanine dyes are used in clinical NIR-I imaging, including ICG and IRDye800CW.

As the first FDA-approved cyanine dye used for NIR in vivo imaging in humans, ICG has been reported to be a promising, non-invasive, and low-cost technology for improving intraoperative tumor detection and guiding surgery in different clinical settings [71–73]. The mechanism of action is thought to be enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect in tumor tissues. Unlike in tumor tissue, ICG quickly exits normal tissue, with a blood half-life of 3–5 min [73]. Pop et al. examined the use of ICG fluorescence imaging (FI) for assessing surgical margins during BCS, finding it 71% accurate with a 100% negative predictive value [74]. Veys et al. assessed ICG-FI's ability to detect residual breast tumor tissue post-neoadjuvant chemotherapy, achieving a 94.2% sensitivity and 92.7% negative predictive value with a tumor-to-background ratio cutoff of 1.3. For patients with complete pathological response (pCR), the negative predictive value was 100% [73]. Qiu et al. [75] developed a lidocaine mucilage-ICG tracer to enhance tumor localization and margin assessment during BCS, using FI camera systems for detection. They evaluated its effectiveness as an intraoperative tumor delineation agent (NCT04438577). Current ICG-FI has a high false-positive rate due to non-specific targeting, limiting its BCS application. The development of more targeted fluorescent probes is therefore essential to enable effective fluorescence-guided navigation in breast-conserving surgery (Fig. 5a1–d2) [73].

Bevacizumab-IRDye800CW

IRDye800CW, with its high quantum yield and easy bioconjugation, is a leading NIR fluorescent dye, suitable for protein/antibody labeling and demonstrating renal excretion due to its stability and low molecular weight [76]. Its optical and pharmacokinetic properties have led to about 39 clinical trials focused on enhancing cancer visualization. A successful clinical trial of a bevacizumab-IRDye800CW conjugate showed clear cancer margin delineation in 20 breast cancer patients injected with 4.5 mg of the tracer, with no adverse events reported. Tracer levels were higher in primary tumors than in margins (P < 0.05) and healthy tissues (P < 0.0001). Their study revealed that systemic administration of the bevacizumab-IRDye800CW tracer is safe for breast cancer guidance and confirms tumor and tumor margin uptake, as evaluated using a systematic validation methodology [77]. Koller et al. further confirmed the safety and effectiveness of bevacizumab-IRDye800CW for breast cancer guidance, with an 88% increase in detecting tumor-invaded margins intraoperatively [78]. These findings highlight the clinical value of intraoperative molecular FI in BCS and suggest a future shift in treatment approaches. However, the long-term effects of molecular FI on clinical outcomes need further confirmation (Fig. 5e and f) [78].

Tozuleristide (BLZ-100)

BLZ-100 (Fig. 5g–i2) is a synthetic 36-amino-acid peptide from scorpion venom chlorotoxin, linked to an ICG fluorescent derivative [79]. It is well-tolerated in mice [80], and studies suggest it binds to proteins like matrix metalloproteinase 2, which is overexpressed in many cancers, including breast cancer [81, 82]. BLZ-100 is being developed for intraoperative tumor visualization [81]. A phase I trial (NCT02496065) showed that it binds to breast cancer cells, with bright signals in invasive carcinoma and diffuse signals in carcinoma in situ taken from fresh in vivo breast biopsies. Tumor detection was not affected by breast density, grade, or hormone receptor status. However, only one clinical trial has been conducted, providing limited evidence [79].

Activatable cell-penetrating peptides (ACPPs)

ACPPs are innovative agents comprising a polycationic cell-penetrating peptide linked to a neutralizing polyanion via a cleavable ligand, with two fluorophores enabling Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET). Proteolytic cleavage disrupts FRET, altering the fluorescence intensity, and is influenced by proteases in invasive and in situ carcinomas [83]. Jonathan et al. investigated the application of a novel proportional ACPPs molecule, AVB-620, in breast cancer surgery patients [84]. Imaging from the 8 mg cohort revealed differences between tumor-positive and tumor-negative tissues. The imaging system uses three cameras to capture color and fluorescence images, displaying four real-time images: a color image, Cy5 and Cy7 fluorescence images, and a Cy5/Cy7 ratio image. Phase I and II clinical trials have been completed, and a controlled study with a larger sample size is awaited (Fig. 5j and k) [84].

LUM015

LUM015 is a novel imaging probe combining a PEGylated protease-activated far-red fluorescent agent with a Cy5 fluorophore and a QSY21 quencher, linked by a GGRK peptide (Fig. 5l1–n2) [85]. In a Phase I study with 15 patients with either soft tissue sarcoma or breast cancer, LUM015 was well tolerated and showed higher tumor fluorescence compared to normal tissues. In a clinical trial, LUM015 was used for the first time in humans for intraoperative imaging of tumor beds [64]. Smith et al. evaluated its effectiveness in detecting residual tumor in 24 patients, noting no significant background fluorescence in normal breast tissue but elevated signals in invasive cancer and DCIS. The mean tumor-to-normal signal ratios were 4.70 ± 1.23 at 0.5 mg/kg and 4.22 ± 0.9 at 1.0 mg/kg, indicating LUM015’s potential to quickly identify residual tumor and potentially reduce positive margins in breast cancer surgeries [65]. LUM015 has been extensively studied, with phase II-III trials completed, supporting its efficacy with higher-level evidence (Table 4).

Rapid intraoperative visualization of breast tumors

Ex vivo rapid incubation imaging method (RIIM)

Despite significant progress in the development of imaging contrast agents, in vivo fluorescence-guided surgery faces challenges due to vascular dynamics and nonspecific absorption with intravenous administration. Extensive safety testing prolongs and increases the cost of clinical trials for new fluorescent agents. To address these limitations, our group have developed a NIR-II probe combining indocyanine green with sacituzumab govitecan, an FDA-approved antibody–drug conjugate targeting TROP2, and introduced the RIIM technology for timely intraoperative margin assessment. This was first tested on ex vivo human breast cancer specimens (NCT05708144), finding that RIIM allowed fluorescence imaging to effectively differentiate cancerous from noncancerous tissues, achieving an AUC of 0.9338. Histopathology confirmed fluorescence and TROP2 expression colocalization in tumor tissues (Fig. 5o–p2) [87].

In addition, the RIIM procedure was optimized for 15-min ex vivo processing, enabling quick margin identification. This technology surpasses current tumor margin detection methods by providing high-resolution, wide-field imaging of whole-tissue specimens within a clinically relevant timeframe and profiling the tumor biomarker TROP2. It is performed ex vivo, ensuring no interference with downstream pathology, and allows intraoperative observations to be confirmed by postoperative histology in clinical trials [87].

A “click-to-sense” probe for the detection of acrolein

A “click-to-sense” probe detects acrolein, a marker of oxidatively stressed cancer cells, using fluorescence microscopy. Tanei et al. found that at probe concentrations of 10 × 10–6 and 20 × 10–6 M, breast cancer cells showed significantly higher fluorescence than normal tissues, with a clear dose-dependent effect. At 10 × 10–6 M, the probe achieved 90% sensitivity and 97% specificity, while at 20 × 10–6 M, both sensitivity and specificity were 97% for distinguishing breast cancer from normal tissue [88]. Since acrolein is present in all cancer types, this method could quickly assess surgical margins in various cancers. Further studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Rapid intraoperative visualization of breast lesions with γ-glutamyl hydroxymethyl rhodamine green

Various techniques can monitor real-time biological activity in living cells, with fluorescence-based methods being fast, safe, and convenient. Organic small molecule-based cellular osmotic probes can quickly enter cells via the extracellular fluid [89]. Ueo et al. developed γ-glutamyl hydroxymethyl rhodamine green (gGlu-HMRG) to detect viable cancer cells, exploiting the overexpression of the enzyme γ-glutamyltranspeptidase on cancer cell membranes [90]. Subsequently, they explored its clinical use for intraoperative margin assessment during BCS by applying it to freshly excised breast tissue. Tumors exhibited increased green fluorescence during 5 min of co-incubation with gGlu-HMRG, differentiating them from healthy tissue. In tests with 35 patients, the method achieved 92% sensitivity and 94% specificity on excised tissue. However, the technique cannot distinguish between malignant and benign regions due to bright fluorescence in benign and hyperplastic areas, necessitating a pathologist's diagnosis of all fluorescence-positive areas during BCS (Fig. 5q–s) [90].

Rapid device probes and wide-field fluorescence scanners offer immediate feedback and have produced modest re-excision reductions in trials, but variable sensitivity/specificity, high false-positive rates, additional peripheral shavings, cost, and limited multicentre reproducibility constrain their real-world generalizability [5, 46, 57]. Across techniques, a recurrent problem is discordance between an imaging/device “positive” margin and the pathological gold standard—particularly in focal vs extensive DCIS, ILC patterns, and post-NAC specimens—highlighting the need for standardized intraoperative sampling, clear mapping of device/readout thresholds to ink-based criteria, and streamlined surgeon–pathologist workflows to avoid overtreatment or false reassurance [36].

Augmented reality (AR) and artificial intelligence (AI)-guided intraoperative imaging

In recent years, multiple studies have introduced AR/AI technologies for intraoperative tumor visualization. For instance, Khizar et al. noted in their review that AR can overlay three-dimensional tumor models onto the patient's body surface, enabling surgeons to visually assess the location and boundaries of tumors such as breast cancers, thereby improving the accuracy of margin clearance [91]. A clinical randomized trial by Lee et al. reported that AR-guided breast cancer localization showed comparable negative margin rates to conventional ultrasound localization, while significantly improving surgical convenience and safety in the AR group [92]. Additionally, a real-time AR/AI surgical system achieved over 95% tumor segmentation accuracy and approximately 0.64 mm navigation error in 106 skin tumor surgeries [93]. These preliminary findings suggest the potential of AR/AI for intraoperative tumor identification, though further clinical trials are needed for validation.

Conclusion and future perspectives

In conclusion, the accurate assessment of surgical margins remains a cornerstone of BCS, playing a critical role in reducing local recurrence for both DCIS and invasive carcinoma. This review provides a comprehensive overview for intraoperative margin evaluation in BCS. Among these, fluorescent molecular imaging has shown potential in supporting surgical procedures and improving clinical outcomes. Despite the initial enthusiasm surrounding these studies, intraoperative fluorescent molecular imaging is at a pivotal juncture, necessitating efficacy-oriented and results-driven research to advance beyond the current proof-of-concept stage and ultimately influence clinical practice. The future trajectory of fluorescent-guided breast surgery may involve the integration of visual light and near-infrared I/II fusion fluorescent-guided surgical navigation to enhance imaging depth and resolution. There is a pressing need for the establishment of specialized knowledge centers to train practitioners and promote broader recognition of this technology. Additionally, the application of AI in automatic deep learning represents a significant avenue for exploration [94]. Once effective intraoperative techniques are developed, AI could facilitate the rapid and automatic assessment of tumor margins.

Given that breast cancer is a highly heterogeneous disease, achieving accurate diagnosis of intraoperative incisal margins remains one of the most formidable challenges. In addition, the performance of these technologies can be influenced by the underlying tumor biology of ILC and DCIS, and we reference this as an area for future technology refinement. Therefore, interdisciplinary collaboration among scientists is essential to reduce the rate of positive margins and enhance patient outcomes.

Furthermore, it should not be overlooked that although some technologies have been approved by the FDA, there are still issues that need to be overcome from the regulatory approval stage to the clinical application stage to ensure the wide use of these technologies. These include: high equipment purchase and individual case costs, interference in the surgical process, and learning curve problems, reimbursement obstacles in different medical systems, doubts of surgeons about the clinical practicality of additional information, and logistical problems related to equipment maintenance and aseptic coverage. A great deal of effort and exploration still needs to be made.

Search strategy and selection criteria

References for this comprehensive narrative review with systematic search principles were identified through searches of PubMed, Web of Science databases, and various countries’ guidelines with the search terms “breast-conserving surgery”, “margins”, and “intraoperative measures” from 1997 to 2024. Articles were also identified through searches of the authors’ own files. The final reference list was generated on the basis of originality and relevance to the broad scope of this article.
