Pick Up Sports and Health (PUSH): a pilot evaluation of a student-led service-learning program for health literacy and professional development in underserved communities
Authors: Michelle Batlle, Kevin Crosby, Marissa Levey, Jude Okonkwo, Mansi Shah, Shawn Simmons, Tina Ting, Julie Glickstein, Joshua Dawson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedical education accreditation standards increasingly demand meaningful community engagement opportunities, yet many institutions struggle to establish sustainable student-led service-learning programs. Additionally, health literacy remains low in underserved communities, and medical students’ attitudes toward underserved populations often decline during training.AimTo conduct a pilot evaluation of PUSH (Pick Up Sports and Health), a student-led service-learning program that combines physical activities with health education for underserved youth, assessing feasibility and early educational outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a pilot study using a mixed methods approach (Fall 2024) involving 25 health profession student volunteers (“Coaches”) and 11 child participants (ages 8–12) from an underserved community in Upper Manhattan. This pilot study employed both quantitative and qualitative data collection including an 11-item non-validated Likert scale survey assessing Coaches’ perceived competency growth across four domains (community understanding, clinical knowledge application, communication skills, and leadership) and qualitative reflections. Child and parent surveys measured self-reported health knowledge retention (assessed four months after initial sessions), interest in healthcare careers, and program satisfaction.ResultsAmong Coaches, 92–96% reported perceived growth in competency across all domains, with 88% expressing willingness to volunteer again. Over 90% of child participants demonstrated improved self-reported health knowledge, and 45.5% expressed interest in pursuing healthcare careers. The program operated at a low cost of $20–50 per session, with minimal faculty time commitment (2–3 h per month). However, these findings are based on small-scale, self-reported data without a comparison group.ConclusionsPUSH appears feasible and demonstrates promising early outcomes as an affordable, student-led service-learning model with preliminary benefits for both health profession students and community members, although findings are based on small-scale, self-reported data. The findings support the need for larger-scale evaluation using validated tools and control groups before broader implementation.

Background

Medical education accreditation standards increasingly demand meaningful community engagement opportunities that build professional skills [1]. However, many institutions struggle to sustain these programs, especially when students are the primary leaders and designers [2]. This difficulty is compounded by two additional issues: medical students’ attitudes toward underserved populations often decline during their training [3], and health literacy remains significantly low in disadvantaged communities [4, 5].

Effective service-learning programs share key traits: they target real community needs, expand learning beyond classrooms, and include structured reflection [6]. Despite these evidence-based principles, few models are scalable in resource-limited settings. Most existing programs depend heavily on faculty involvement or institutional funding, which limits their adoption across institutions with different resource levels [2].

We introduce Pick Up Sports and Health (PUSH), a student-led service-learning program designed to address these challenges through organized curriculum development, genuine community partnerships, and affordable operations. This pilot evaluation assesses the program’s feasibility, early educational results for both student volunteers and community participants, and lessons learned from implementation.

Program description

Program development and context

Columbia University medical students founded PUSH in 2022 in collaboration with a local community activist in Upper Manhattan. The program grew from informal basketball sessions where students connected with neighborhood youth into a structured educational program. PUSH runs weekly at a public school near Columbia University Irving Medical Center, reaching over 300 children since its inception.

Health profession student volunteers, called “Coaches,” create age-appropriate curricula using a template adapted from Understanding by Design [7], a backward design framework starting with clear learning objectives. Topics focus on community-specific health priorities, such as respiratory health, cardiovascular disease prevention, diabetes awareness, and sleep hygiene. Lesson materials cost between $20 and $50, emphasizing resourcefulness rather than expensive equipment.

Organizational sustainability and governance

An Executive Board oversees program operations, with designated Directors managing Administration, Finance, Public Relations, Recruitment, and Education. The board functions under the oversight of a faculty advisor. A key innovation is the intergenerational leadership model, which pairs pre-clinical students handling day-to-day operations with senior students providing mentorship. This structure promotes knowledge transfer across cohorts and supports long-term sustainability. The organization’s faculty mentor dedicates 4–5 h per month to program oversight.

Methods

Study design

This pilot study employed a mixed-methods approach combining quantitative survey data with qualitative reflective analysis. Importantly, this study is positioned as an exploratory pilot evaluation rather than a formal experimental design. The quantitative and qualitative components were integrated sequentially, with survey data collected first, followed by qualitative analysis of open-ended reflections to provide contextual understanding of the quantitative findings. The Coach assessment component was categorized as a quality improvement project and was exempt from institutional review board (IRB) review. The community impact evaluation was carried out with IRB approval (Protocol #AAAV0034, Columbia University Irving Medical Center).

Note on recruitment and potential bias: Participation was voluntary and self-selected based on availability and interest. Parents and students who volunteered may have positive attitudes toward service-learning or community engagement that differ from the broader population.

Data collection

Quantitative data

An 11-item Likert scale survey (5-point scale: “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree”) evaluated Coaches’ perceived growth across four competency areas, the importance of professional development, and willingness to continue volunteering. Child and parent surveys assessed self-reported retention of health knowledge (measured four months after the initial sessions), interest in healthcare careers, program satisfaction, and engagement with health topics.

Important limitation: The survey instrument was developed specifically for this program and has not undergone psychometric validation. No information on validity, reliability, or prior use of this instrument exists. Therefore, findings reflect subjective self-reported perceptions rather than validated measures of competency or knowledge.

Qualitative data

Open-ended reflections from Coaches were analyzed to identify themes related to community connection, professional skill development, and program impact. Reflections were gathered both immediately after sessions and at the conclusion of the program.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Descriptive statistics (percentages and frequencies) were used to summarize Coach and participant responses on Likert scales. Given the small sample size and exploratory nature of this pilot study, no inferential statistical analysis was performed. Descriptive statistics are appropriate for pilot evaluation to characterize participant responses and generate preliminary insights.

Qualitative analysis

Open-ended reflections underwent thematic analysis to identify emerging themes. Two independent reviewers coded the qualitative data using an iterative approach: initial open coding to identify key concepts, followed by thematic grouping to organize codes into overarching themes. Discrepancies in coding were resolved through consensus discussion. The process was exploratory in nature, consistent with pilot study methods.

Coaches reported perceived growth across all competency domains (Table 2). Additionally, 88% of Coaches expressed willingness to volunteer again, and 84% felt that PUSH contributed significantly to their professional growth. Qualitative analysis

Qualitative analysis identified three major themes among Coach reflections:

Community connection and cultural humility

Coaches described building authentic relationships with the underserved community. One Coach noted: “I feel connected to the Washington Heights community in a way that allows for meaningful and mutually beneficial exchanges.” Many described how the experience challenged negative stereotypes and reinforced their commitment to serving diverse populations. Coaches emphasized gaining insight into social determinants of health and structural barriers affecting the community.

Enhanced communication and teaching skills

Coaches reported increased confidence in patient education and health communication. A Coach pursuing pediatrics stated: “I have gained confidence in working with children and explaining health topics engagingly, enhancing my future practice as a pediatrician.” Multiple Coaches noted that simplifying complex medical concepts for children strengthened their own clinical understanding while developing practical communication skills applicable to future clinical practice.

Professional identity and mentorship

Coaches acknowledged their role as role models in the community. One Coach reflected: “I have shared my experiences and passion for healthcare careers, providing meaningful exposure for the youth.” Several Coaches from underrepresented backgrounds emphasized the importance of culturally consistent mentorship in shaping their professional identity and commitment to serving underserved populations.

Community Impact

Child participants

Over 90% of child participants reported improved knowledge of human anatomy and health concepts. Notably, 45.5% (5/11) expressed interest in pursuing healthcare careers, compared to baseline reports indicating limited prior exposure to medical careers. Knowledge retention was evaluated four months after the initial lessons; all reported participants retained concepts from the baseline sessions, though this is based on child and parent recall rather than objective assessment.

Parent perspectives

80% of parents reported that children stayed consistently excited about health topics after participating in the program. 60% agreed that PUSH boosted their children’s awareness of and interest in healthcare careers. Parents also noted positive spillover effects, with children sharing health information learned during sessions with family members.

Program sustainability

PUSH has secured funding from three competitive grants: the Alpha Omega Alpha Medical Student Service Leadership Project, the Steve Miller Fellowship (Columbia University Department of Pediatrics), and the American Academy of Pediatrics Section on Pediatric Trainees Health Equity Grant. The program received recognition from the American Academy of Pediatrics and the Social Mission Alliance, with findings presented at five national and institutional conferences.

Discussion

This pilot evaluation provides preliminary evidence that PUSH, a low-cost student-led service-learning model, may offer educational benefits for both health profession students and community participants. However, the findings are based on a small sample size with self-reported outcomes and no comparison group, which limits the strength of conclusions that can be drawn. Three key findings warrant discussion with appropriate caveats:

Authentic community partnership as the foundation

PUSH’s organic growth from casual basketball sessions to a structured educational program demonstrates how genuine relationships—rather than formal rules—may encourage lasting community involvement. The partnership with a trusted community activist added vital credibility and access, allowing ongoing participation over two years. This aligns with research highlighting the importance of community-based, trust-focused partnerships in service-learning. For institutions aiming to develop similar programs, grassroots efforts driven by genuine relationships may warrant consideration as potentially more sustainable than top-down approaches, though this requires further investigation.

Structured curriculum design may enhance learning outcomes

Implementation of the Understanding by Design framework [7] transformed ad hoc volunteering into intentional education with clear learning objectives for both Coaches and children. This organized approach allowed Coaches to develop teaching skills systematically while addressing community health literacy needs [4, 5]. The backward design process—starting with desired outcomes and planning instruction—may help students see connections between professional skill development and community benefit, potentially supporting intrinsic motivation for ongoing service. However, without a comparison group or validated measurement tools, causality cannot be established.

Minimal resources support program operations

With session costs of $20–50 and faculty time of 2–3 h monthly, this program demonstrates that resource limitations may not necessarily prevent effective service-learning initiatives. The intergenerational leadership model, where pre-clinical students manage operations while senior students mentor them, addresses sustainability challenges common to student-led initiatives [2]. This distributed leadership approach spreads responsibilities and may help prevent burnout linked to over-reliance on faculty, though long-term data on program continuity across academic years is not yet available.

Implications for medical education

PUSH potentially contributes to three areas of medical education literature:

Service-learning sustainability

The program provides preliminary evidence that student-led initiatives can potentially remain sustainable with minimal institutional resources when carefully designed with strong leadership structures and genuine community partnerships. However, longer-term follow-up and multi-site replication are needed to confirm these findings.

Professional competency development

Preliminary findings suggest that service-learning in community settings may enhance multiple professional skills simultaneously—such as community understanding, clinical knowledge application, communication, and leadership—within a single, integrated program [1, 2]. Further research using validated instruments is needed to confirm these associations.

Health equity in medical education

By combining health education with physical activity and structured reflection, PUSH models an approach to health literacy that considers social determinants and community context. This aligns with calls for more community-embedded health literacy instruction [4, 5], though rigorous evaluation is needed before scaling broadly.

Study limitations

This pilot study has several important limitations that should be carefully considered when interpreting the findings:

Sample size and generalizability

The study enrolled only 25 Coaches and 11 child participants, which substantially limits statistical power and the ability to generalize findings to larger populations or different settings. The small sample size also prevented meaningful comparisons across subgroups, such as differences by professional discipline, which would require larger numbers for reliable analysis.

Measurement bias and lack of validation

All outcomes were based on self-reported survey data, reflecting participants’ subjective perceptions of competency growth and learning rather than objective measures. Self-reported data is prone to multiple forms of bias:

Social desirability bias: Respondents may overstate improvements or positive experiences.Recall bias: Participants may inaccurately remember baseline levels or prior knowledge.Selection bias: Voluntary participation introduces bias toward more motivated or engaged individuals.

Contextual and oversight limitations

The program was conducted at a single, well-resourced urban medical school within a specific community setting in Upper Manhattan, which may restrict generalizability to other institutional environments, communities with different resources, geographic regions, or demographic populations. Additionally, the Coach assessment component was classified as a quality improvement project rather than undergoing formal Institutional Review Board review, resulting in less rigorous ethical and methodological oversight than would be expected in formal research studies.

Recruitment and participation bias

Participation was entirely voluntary and self-selected. This introduces significant selection bias, as students and families with greater motivation, interest in health education, pre-existing positive attitudes toward service-learning, or schedule flexibility may be overrepresented. Students with negative attitudes toward community engagement or underserved populations may be underrepresented in this sample.

Conclusions

PUSH demonstrates preliminary feasibility and shows promise as a student-led, service-learning model that may align with medical education accreditation standards [1], while supporting professional skills development and community health literacy. Pilot data provide early evidence of potential benefits for both health profession students and community participants, achieved with minimal financial and faculty resources. The model appears accessible to resource-limited institutions and potentially adaptable across various settings.

However, larger-scale, more rigorous evaluation is necessary before broader implementation to confirm effectiveness and enable reliable replication. The current findings must be interpreted cautiously due to small sample size, self-reported outcomes, and lack of validated measures. Future research should include validated measurement instruments, appropriate comparison or control groups, multi-site designs, and long-term follow-up to assess whether competency development persists and impacts clinical practice patterns, specialty choice, or attitudes toward underserved populations.

Recommendations for future research and practice

This student-led service-learning program demonstrated feasibility and generated preliminary positive outcomes with minimal resources: 88% repeat participation and 92–96% perceived skill development across areas, operating at a cost of just $20–50 per session. Using backward curriculum design, the program delivered engagement opportunities for both community members and student learners simultaneously. Sustainability appeared to depend on intergenerational student leadership supported by only 2–3 hours of monthly faculty mentorship.
