Wearable ECG and PPG for anxiety detection: a translational digital medicine perspective
Authors: Mohamed Elgendi, Azza Elkhalifa, Noor Alhashmi, Alya Aldahmani, Noor Aljneibi, Dhabia Alamro, Herbert F Jelinek, Ahsan Khandoker, Rabab Ward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnxiety disorders affect hundreds of millions of people worldwide, yet objective and continuous assessment remains limited in clinical practice. To our knowledge, this is the first modality-specific, translational synthesis focusing on wearable ECG and PPG for anxiety detection. Wearable electrocardiography (ECG) and photoplethysmography (PPG), combined with data-driven analytics, have emerged as promising tools for anxiety monitoring, but translation into routine care has been slow. Here, we present a PRISMA-guided systematic review of 38 studies (2015–2025) investigating wearable ECG- and PPG-based anxiety detection. We analyze anxiety induction paradigms, sensor configurations, signal acquisition strategies, and analytical approaches, including statistical, machine learning, and hybrid methods. While autonomic markers derived from ECG and PPG consistently reflect anxiety-related physiological changes, substantial heterogeneity in study design, limited population diversity, and laboratory-centric validation constrain clinical generalizability. Critically, most studies lack evaluation in real-world settings and do not demonstrate clinical utility or impact on patient outcomes. We identify key translational barriers and propose a digital medicine roadmap emphasizing standardized protocols, robust validation across diverse populations, workflow integration, and outcome-driven evaluation to enable clinically actionable, real-world anxiety monitoring.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Engineering, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Anxiety disorders are among the most prevalent and disabling mental health conditions worldwide, significantly affecting quality of life and functional capacity1. Characterized by excessive worry, physiological hyperarousal, and persistent fear, these disorders affected an estimated 301 million people (≈4.05% of the global population) in 2019, with lifetime prevalence estimates ranging from 14.5% to 33.7%2,3. This already substantial burden has intensified in recent years; the COVID-19 pandemic precipitated an estimated 25–27% increase in global anxiety prevalence during its first year alone1, underscoring the urgent need for scalable detection and monitoring solutions. Anxiety disorders remain a leading contributor to years lived with disability2, and although this review used “anxiety” as the core search term, studies addressing related conditions such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), panic disorder, and phobias were also included.

Traditional methods for detecting anxiety have relied primarily on self-reported symptoms and periodic clinical evaluations. While widely used, these approaches are inherently subjective, prone to recall bias, and incapable of providing continuous or real-time insights into an individual’s mental state4. These limitations have driven the search for more objective, scalable, and continuous monitoring solutions.

While wearable biosensing hardware has matured considerably, recent innovations in signal processing and machine learning have unlocked new possibilities for anxiety detection. Modern analytical approaches, including automated feature extraction, real-time classification algorithms, and multimodal data integration, now enable the identification of subtle autonomic patterns from ambulatory recordings that were previously undetectable using conventional methods. Consumer-grade devices equipped with biosensors, particularly electrocardiogram (ECG) and photoplethysmogram (PPG), can continuously and non-invasively monitor physiological signals associated with autonomic responses to anxiety5. The selection of ECG and PPG as primary modalities for anxiety detection is grounded in the well-established relationship between the autonomic nervous system (ANS) and cardiovascular function6. Anxiety triggers activation of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) via the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in increased norepinephrine release at cardiac and vascular sites7. Concurrently, vagal (parasympathetic) withdrawal reduces inhibitory input to the sinoatrial node, contributing to elevated heart rate and reduced heart rate variability (HRV), a robust indicator of autonomic function that can be quantified from ECG signals8. Key HRV metrics relevant to anxiety include time-domain measures (SDNN, RMSSD), frequency-domain measures (LF/HF ratio), and nonlinear indices (sample entropy, Poincaré plot parameters), all of which show consistent alterations during anxiety states9–11.

PPG offers a complementary, low-cost optical method for assessing peripheral vascular dynamics. The PPG waveform reflects the pulsatile component of arterial blood flow modulated by cardiac output and autonomic regulation12. Anxiety-related sympathetic activation induces peripheral vasoconstriction, which manifests as reductions in PPG waveform amplitude and alterations in pulse morphology12–14. Experimental studies further demonstrate that stress-induced vasoconstriction is associated with measurable decreases in PPG signal amplitude and changes in microvascular blood flow, highlighting the sensitivity of PPG to autonomic responses relevant to anxiety12–16.

Recent work has linked PPG-derived markers, including pulse amplitude variability, pulse rate variability, and waveform morphology, with anxiety- and stress-related autonomic changes, supporting the role of PPG as a candidate digital biomarker in mental health monitoring13,14,17. Pulse rate variability (PRV) derived from PPG has been shown to approximate ECG-derived HRV under controlled and stationary conditions, although agreement may degrade in the presence of motion artifacts or altered hemodynamics18. Pulse transit time (PTT), measured as the interval between the ECG R-wave and the corresponding PPG pulse arrival, requires simultaneous acquisition of both signals and provides an additional marker reflecting arterial stiffness and sympathetic tone19. PPG waveform morphology20 offers further features sensitive to autonomic changes.

ECG and PPG offer complementary strengths: ECG provides high temporal resolution and remains the reference standard for HRV analysis, whereas PPG enables accessible wrist or finger-based sensing suitable for consumer wearables, albeit with greater susceptibility to motion artifacts. Multimodal approaches combining both modalities have shown potential for improved sensitivity and specificity compared to single-modality systems, although performance remains dependent on study design and validation protocols21.

Both modalities are well-suited for integration into wearable devices and digital health platforms, providing advantages in clinical translatability and deployment over other modalities such as galvanic skin response (GSR), electroencephalography (EEG), or respiration monitoring. ECG and PPG are among the most commonly used biosensors in mental health research, offering non-intrusive integration into daily life and straightforward incorporation into digital mental health ecosystems13,14.

A recent scoping review of wearable-based stress and anxiety monitoring found that over 60% of studies relied on HRV or heart rate metrics derived from ECG/PPG, compared with fewer than 35% using GSR and under 15% using EEG or respiration monitoring17. However, most previous reviews have either considered ECG and PPG as part of broader multimodal systems or focused on general stress detection, without providing a modality-specific synthesis of these two approaches in relation to anxiety or systematically identifying their methodological limitations. This review addresses this gap by offering a focused, clinically relevant evaluation of ECG and PPG methodologies, highlighting methodological trends, identifying limitations, and outlining opportunities for combining these modalities with complementary sensing technologies.

Standardized anxiety induction protocols, including cognitive stress tasks, virtual reality simulations, and audiovisual stimuli, have become central to experimental research in this domain. When combined with wearable ECG and PPG monitoring, these paradigms enable controlled interrogation of cardiovascular responses to anxiety-related stimuli.

Taken together, the literature reveals a clear translational opportunity, but also a critical conceptual gap. While anxiety remains a major clinical burden and wearable sensing technologies offer a pathway toward continuous and objective monitoring, the field is fragmented across heterogeneous populations, induction paradigms, sensing configurations, and analytical strategies. As a result, current evidence remains difficult to reconcile and insufficiently aligned with clinical translation.

Accordingly, this review provides a focused synthesis of wearable ECG- and PPG-based anxiety detection, structured around four core dimensions: anxiety induction paradigms, sensor configurations, signal processing and feature extraction, and wearable system design for continuous monitoring. By systematically evaluating these domains, we aim to clarify methodological inconsistencies, identify translational barriers, and outline a pathway toward clinically meaningful, real-time anxiety monitoring systems.

Results

Publications

A total of 38 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final synthesis (Fig. 1). The detailed PRISMA-based identification, screening, and eligibility workflow is described in the Methods section. The included studies span the period 2015-2025 and reflect the evolution of wearable ECG- and PPG-based approaches for anxiety detection.

Search workflow illustrating the identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and final inclusion of articles in the review.

This figure outlines the structured approach adopted in accordance with established systematic review guidelines (PRISMA)61, progressing through four stages: Identification, Screening, Eligibility, and Inclusion.

Of these, 76 articles were excluded during the eligibility phase for the following reasons: review articles (n = 35), conference abstracts (n = 2), symposium contributions (n = 2), studies not involving human subjects (n = 13), or lack of full-text access (n = 24). This left 542 full-text articles assessed for relevance. A total of 504 were excluded for not being related to the topic. Ultimately, 38 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. These studies employed various experimental paradigms for anxiety induction and measured physiological responses using ECG, PPG, or other biosignals. Many adopted multi-modal approaches, integrating diverse stimuli to simulate anxiety in controlled environments.

As shown in Fig. 2, the number of publications related to anxiety induction and detection has grown notably over the past decade. From 2015 to 2017, annual output remained low, with one or fewer publications each year, likely reflecting the early-stage availability of wearable technologies and a narrower research focus on physiological anxiety detection during that period. Research activity began increasing in 2018, coinciding with the rapid development of biosensor technologies, machine learning techniques, and digital health platforms that have opened new avenues for objectively measuring anxiety. This upward trajectory continued with a sharper rise in 2019 and consistent growth from 2020 onward; notably, 2024 marked the peak with 8 publications, followed closely by 2023 and 2021 with 6 each. The expanding variety of anxiety-inducing stimuli and experimental paradigms suggests a concerted effort to enhance ecological validity and methodological rigor. This trend also points to a shift toward practical applications, including real-time monitoring systems, mobile health interventions, and personalized treatment approaches. Overall, the rising publication volume underscores the interdisciplinary nature of contemporary anxiety research, drawing increasing attention from fields such as biomedical engineering, psychology, computer science, and clinical medicine.

Characteristics of participants

The health demographics of participants across the 38 reviewed studies reveal a varied representation spanning six main categories (Fig. 3). A total of 21 studies (55.3%) focused on healthy individuals. These typically included healthy control groups, university students, children, or healthcare professionals without psychiatric, neurological, or chronic medical conditions22–24. Such samples often serve as baseline data comparators to evaluate the effectiveness of anxiety induction methods and to explore typical physiological responses in the absence of clinical confounds.

Participant health status distribution.

Categorization of participants across the 38 reviewed studies by health status. The magenta bar highlights healthy participants (55.3%), the yellow bar denotes studies where health status was not specified (5.3%), and the grey bars represent clinically diagnosed or mixed populations (Anxiety Spectrum Disorders, Phobia/Specific Fear, Neurological, Mixed Psychiatric Conditions).

Conversely, five studies (13.2%) recruited participants with formally diagnosed anxiety-related disorders, such as generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), social anxiety disorder (SAD), or treatment-resistant anxiety25. These clinical samples offer insights into how chronic anxiety presents and is physiologically experienced, contributing to the development of targeted detection tools.

Another six studies (15.8%) focused on individuals with phobias or stimulus-specific fear, enabling detailed examination of intense anxiety responses and fear processing mechanisms26,27.

Three studies (7.9%) involved participants with chronic medical or neurological conditions, including Parkinson’s disease, coronary artery disease (CAD), or peripheral arterial disease. Anxiety in these groups was either a comorbid condition or an emotional factor influencing medical outcomes, underscoring the relevance of anxiety monitoring in clinical contexts28.

Two studies (5.3%) included general or unspecified populations. These cohorts often comprised mixed samples of healthy and undiagnosed individuals or did not clearly define participant health status, offering broader ecological validity but with some ambiguity in clinical interpretation29.

Lastly, one study (2.6%) involved individuals with mixed psychological or emotional profiles. These participants were not formally diagnosed with anxiety disorders but exhibited varying levels of anxiety symptoms, depressive traits, or emotional instability. Some studies categorized participants based on standardized psychological scales, allowing for comparative analysis between non-clinical and at-risk individuals15.

This distribution highlights a research trend favoring healthy participants, likely driven by practical advantages such as easier recruitment, fewer ethical constraints, and the avoidance of confounding effects associated with pre-existing mental health conditions. However, this reliance on non-clinical populations limits the translational potential of findings, as physiological patterns distinguishing clinical anxiety from typical stress responses remain underexplored. Ethical requirements and recruitment challenges may contribute to the lower prevalence of clinical samples in the literature, yet future studies would benefit from broader inclusion of diagnosed populations to increase the real-world applicability of physiological monitoring systems and support the development of clinically validated diagnostic tools.

Beyond participant health status, Supplementary Table 3 provides comprehensive methodological characteristics enabling quantitative synthesis across all 38 studies. Sample sizes varied substantially, ranging from 730 to 245 participants29 (mean = 63). A concerning proportion of studies employed small samples: 18 studies (47.4%) included fewer than 50 participants, 13 studies (34.2%) recruited 50–99 participants, and only 7 studies (18.4%) exceeded 100 participants. This distribution has important implications for statistical power and the reliability of reported effect sizes, particularly for machine learning approaches requiring adequate data for robust cross-validation.

Regarding detection modalities across all configurations (standalone and multimodal), ECG-based measurement was employed in 29 studies (76.3%), confirming its predominant role in physiological anxiety assessment. PPG appeared in 14 studies (36.8%), reflecting growing interest in unobtrusive wrist-worn monitoring suitable for consumer applications. Complementary autonomic measures, including electrodermal activity (EDA) or galvanic skin response (GSR) were incorporated in 9 studies (23.7%).

Anxiety induction paradigms demonstrated considerable heterogeneity. Video-based emotional stimuli were most frequently employed (6 studies)14,27,31–34, followed by the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST; 3 studies)30,35,36 and virtual reality scenarios (3 studies)37–39. Cognitive stressors, including Stroop tasks and mental arithmetic, were also common. While this diversity reflects the multidimensional nature of anxiety, it complicates direct cross-study comparisons and reinforces the need for standardized induction protocols in future research.

Stimulation method

As illustrated in Fig. 4, the 38 reviewed studies employed a range of stimuli to induce anxiety, broadly classified into active (n = 24; 63.2%) and passive (n = 14; 36.8%) methods, based on participant involvement.

Active stimuli required direct engagement and included tasks designed to provoke stress, such as cognitive challenges (e.g., mental arithmetic, n-back tasks), socially evaluative scenarios (e.g., public speaking, receiving feedback), and interactive simulations. Fear conditioning, where participants learned to associate neutral stimuli with aversive outcomes (e.g., mild electric shocks or loud noises), also fell under this category.

Passive stimuli, by contrast, involved minimal participant interaction. These included emotional triggers like anxiety-inducing images or videos, pharmacological interventions using anxiogenic agents, and physical stressors such as thermal pain or hyperventilation. Some studies also used multimodal approaches that integrated cognitive, emotional, or physical stimuli to simulate real-life scenarios more effectively. These were often classified as passive due to limited participant control, though some incorporated varying levels of interactivity.

The diversity of methods shown in Fig. 4 reflects the innovative strategies employed to elicit anxiety while balancing ecological validity and experimental control. Cognitive stimuli (n = 9) were the most common, followed by social stimuli (n = 6) that captured interpersonal stressors. Emotional stimuli (n = 6) included exposure to negative imagery or videos, while multimodal methods (n = 5) combined multiple stress dimensions. Physical stimuli (n = 5) included discomfort-inducing interventions, daily life stress (n = 3), and fear conditioning (n = 3) focused on learned aversive associations. Pharmacological methods (n = 1) involved chemical induction of anxiety states.

Passive stimuli, while less frequent overall, offered standardized, replicable conditions suitable for clinical or ethically sensitive studies. Active methods, on the other hand, allowed for richer, ecologically valid data by requiring direct participant involvement. This classification highlights the breadth of anxiety research methodologies, encompassing both controlled observational designs and dynamic, participant-driven tasks to capture the complex nature of anxiety responses.

Detection methods

The included studies employed a range of analytical approaches to detect or predict anxiety from physiological signals, broadly spanning feature-based machine learning and data-driven deep learning methods. Despite variability in implementation, most studies followed a common pipeline involving signal preprocessing, feature extraction, and classification or regression.

Feature-based approaches were the most prevalent. These methods typically relied on handcrafted features derived from ECG and PPG signals, particularly heart rate variability (HRV) metrics in the time, frequency, and nonlinear domains. Common features included mean heart rate, RMSSD, SDNN, LF/HF ratio, and entropy-based measures, which were used to quantify autonomic nervous system dynamics. These features were then input into conventional machine learning classifiers such as support vector machines (SVM), random forests (RF), k-nearest neighbors (k-NN), and logistic regression. These approaches were favored for their interpretability and relatively low computational requirements.

In parallel, several studies explored deep learning techniques to model physiological signals directly, reducing reliance on manual feature engineering. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), including long short-term memory (LSTM) and gated recurrent unit (GRU) architectures, were applied to capture temporal dependencies and nonlinear patterns in biosignals. These methods showed promise in handling complex, high-dimensional data, particularly in multimodal settings, although they often required larger datasets and careful regularization to avoid overfitting.

Although deep learning methods were identified in only a minority of studies, their presence is noteworthy as they reflect a shift from handcrafted physiological features toward end-to-end temporal representation learning. However, the current evidence does not yet justify strong claims of superiority. Most deep learning studies were conducted on relatively small datasets, often without external validation, making them vulnerable to overfitting and optimistic performance reporting.

In the context of anxiety detection, this limitation is particularly important, as model interpretability is not optional: clinical deployment requires that predictions are grounded in physiologically plausible autonomic signatures rather than spurious correlates. Accordingly, deep learning approaches should currently be viewed as promising but not yet clinically mature. Their future impact will depend on the availability of larger datasets, rigorous validation protocols, and the development of explainable and transparent deployment frameworks.

Hybrid approaches were also observed, combining feature engineering with deep learning models to leverage both domain knowledge and data-driven representations. For example, some studies used handcrafted features as inputs to neural networks, while others integrated multiple physiological streams before classification.

Model evaluation strategies varied across studies, with common metrics including accuracy, F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). However, there was substantial heterogeneity in validation protocols, including differences in subject-wise versus segment-wise data splitting, cross-validation schemes, and dataset sizes. Notably, only a limited number of studies employed subject-independent validation, raising concerns about potential overestimation of model performance due to data leakage.

Overall, the literature reveals a transition from traditional feature-based models toward more complex deep learning frameworks. While deep learning approaches offer increased modeling capacity, feature-based methods remain competitive, particularly in settings with limited data. From a translational perspective, achieving robust, generalizable anxiety detection will require standardized evaluation protocols, larger and more diverse datasets, and careful consideration of model interpretability and deployment constraints.

Reported physiological trends

Physiological responses in this context refer to measurable autonomic and cardiovascular changes triggered by anxiety, such as reductions in heart rate variability (HRV), elevated heart rate, changes in pulse wave amplitude, or increased electrodermal activity (EDA). These responses are typically captured using biosignals such as ECG and PPG, and serve as objective biomarkers of anxiety-related arousal. Several of the reviewed studies provided quantitative insights into how ECG and PPG signals change under anxiety-inducing conditions. In particular, decreases in heart rate variability (HRV) were the most consistently reported trend, observed across both ECG-based37,40,41 and PPG-based25,33 studies. These reductions in HRV typically reflected heightened sympathetic arousal. Additionally, increases in heart rate29,31, reduced pulse wave amplitude15, and elevated signal irregularities32 were noted in PPG recordings. However, not all studies reported statistically significant changes, which may be attributed to differences in study design, sample sizes, or stimulus intensity. These physiological trends further support the potential of both ECG and PPG as reliable modalities for anxiety detection and should inform future sensor calibration and algorithmic interpretation.

Signal acquisition length

As shown in Fig. 5, the signal acquisition lengths in the 38 reviewed studies varied widely, reflecting methodological diversity in anxiety detection research. Thirteen (13) studies adopted durations between 30 minutes and two hours15,32,34–36,38,40–46, balancing data richness with participant manageability. Six (6) studies25,28,30,47–49 extended monitoring beyond two hours, emphasizing long-term physiological tracking. Five (5) studies13,14,27,50,51 collected data for less than 30 minutes, targeting short-term responses to acute stimuli. Another nine (9) studies22–24,31,33,37,52–54 used very brief acquisition periods under five minutes, often for controlled experimental protocols. The remaining five (5) studies16,26,29,39,55 did not specify acquisition duration, possibly due to continuous monitoring systems or incomplete reporting.

Longer recordings typically facilitate the extraction of more robust physiological metrics, particularly frequency-domain HRV features such as LF/HF ratio or power spectral density measures38,40,45. In contrast, shorter acquisition durations often constrain analysis to time-domain metrics like RMSSD or SDNN15,33,34. These methodological constraints directly influence the type, reliability, and interpretability of derived features used to assess anxiety-related autonomic activity. Accordingly, the observed variability demonstrates the adaptability of signal acquisition strategies, tailored to experimental context, sensor capabilities, and specific anxiety paradigms. These temporal considerations have direct clinical relevance: brief recordings may suffice for detecting acute anxiety episodes in controlled settings, while sustained monitoring is essential for capturing the characteristic autonomic dysregulation patterns of clinical anxiety disorders. Clinically, shorter protocols align better with routine screening in primary care, while extended recordings are more suited for comprehensive psychiatric evaluations where detailed autonomic profiling is warranted.

Analytical paradigms and feature engineering

A variety of analytical techniques were employed across the 38 reviewed studies, each tailored to specific research goals, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

To provide clarity and facilitate comparison, we categorize these approaches into four distinct analytical paradigms: (1) traditional statistical methods, (2) machine learning approaches, (3) signal processing pipelines, and (4) hybrid or specialized techniques. Each paradigm offers unique strengths and limitations for anxiety detection, and understanding these distinctions is essential for selecting appropriate methods based on study objectives, data characteristics, and clinical requirements.

In terms of traditional statistical methods, statistical analysis was employed in 10 studies15,26,29,32,39–41,47,51,53, emphasizing its foundational role in hypothesis testing, group comparisons, and quantifying effect sizes. Parametric tests, including paired and independent t-tests, repeated-measures ANOVA, and MANOVA, were commonly used to compare HRV metrics such as RMSSD and LF/HF ratio between baseline and anxiety conditions or across experimental groups. When normality assumptions were violated, particularly for small sample sizes or skewed physiological distributions, non-parametric alternatives, including Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and Mann-Whitney U tests, were applied. Correlation analyses using Pearson and Spearman coefficients examined relationships between physiological markers and self-reported anxiety scores, establishing concurrent validity of biosignal-based measures. Statistical methods excel in confirmatory analyses with clear hypotheses but are limited in handling high-dimensional feature spaces or capturing complex nonlinear relationships characteristic of physiological anxiety responses.

Regarding machine learning approaches, machine learning was the most prevalent analytical approach, employed in 13 studies22,27,28,31,33,34,36–38,43–45,55. Classical machine learning classifiers predominated, with Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forest emerging as the most frequently applied algorithms. SVM was favored for binary anxiety classification due to its effectiveness in high-dimensional feature spaces, with kernel selection (linear, RBF, polynomial) significantly influencing performance outcomes. Random Forest and other ensemble methods were valued for their robustness to overfitting and capacity to handle imbalanced datasets; notably, feature importance rankings derived from Random Forest analyses helped identify the most discriminative HRV and PPG features across multiple studies. Additional classifiers, including k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Naive Bayes, and Decision Trees, were employed either for personalized detection using individual baselines or as baseline comparators in resource-constrained contexts.

Deep learning architectures represented a smaller but growing subset of approaches. Recurrent networks, particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and bidirectional LSTM variants, captured temporal dependencies in sequential physiological data and proved particularly effective for continuous anxiety monitoring. Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN), including dilated and multi-scale variants (MS-TCN), were applied for segmentation tasks to distinguish anxiety episodes within longer recordings. Transformer-based models such as ASFormer represent emerging approaches that leverage attention mechanisms for improved temporal modeling, though their application in anxiety detection remains limited.

A common methodological challenge across machine learning studies was class imbalance between anxious and non-anxious samples. Several studies addressed this using the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) to prevent model bias toward the majority class. Machine learning approaches offer superior pattern recognition and predictive capability compared to traditional statistical methods, but require larger datasets, careful validation protocols (including cross-validation and held-out test sets), and attention to model interpretability for clinical translation. For clinical implementation, algorithm interpretability remains paramount, as mental health clinicians require transparent decision pathways to contextualize physiological anxiety markers within the broader framework of patient history, symptom presentation, and therapeutic goals.

For signal processing and feature engineering, signal processing and feature extraction, employed in seven studies14,23,27,30,42,46,49, formed the critical preprocessing foundation for subsequent analyses. The preprocessing pipeline typically involved three key stages: filtering using band-pass filters (typically 0.5–40 Hz for ECG and 0.5–8 Hz for PPG) to remove baseline wander and high-frequency noise; artifact removal through adaptive filtering, wavelet denoising, and motion artifact correction algorithms to address signal contamination from movement; and beat detection using the Pan-Tompkins algorithm for ECG R-peak detection or derivative-based and template-matching methods for PPG pulse detection.

Feature extraction encompassed multiple domains. Time-domain features included SDNN, RMSSD, pNN50, mean heart rate, and inter-beat interval statistics. Frequency-domain analysis employed Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and Welch’s method for spectral analysis, yielding VLF, LF, HF power, and LF/HF ratio. Nonlinear features such as sample entropy, approximate entropy, Poincaré plot indices (SD1, SD2), and detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) provided complexity assessment. For PPG signals specifically, morphological features including pulse amplitude, pulse width, dicrotic notch timing, systolic/diastolic ratios, and second derivative characteristics offered additional discriminative information. Rigorous preprocessing is critical given the susceptibility of wearable-acquired signals to noise; however, seven studies did not explicitly report preprocessing methods, introducing uncertainty about data quality and reproducibility.

The remaining studies employed hybrid and specialized analytical frameworks. Regression analysis was used in three studies25,48,52, where linear and logistic regression models examined dose-response relationships between HRV metrics and anxiety severity, proving useful for understanding continuous anxiety gradients rather than discrete classification. One study50 employed mixed methods, combining quantitative physiological analysis with qualitative assessments, including interviews and open-ended questionnaires for a comprehensive understanding of anxiety experiences. Comparative and descriptive techniques were applied in three studies13,16,54 to conduct systematic comparisons across modalities (ECG vs. PPG), algorithms, or populations without predictive modeling aims. Finally, one study35 incorporated biochemical analysis using volatile organic compound (VOC) detection via gas chromatography-mass spectrometry, representing an emerging multimodal approach combining metabolomic with cardiovascular markers.

The selection of analytical methods is influenced by several factors: (1) Recording duration, where longer recordings enable frequency-domain and nonlinear HRV analysis, while short recordings constrain analysis to time-domain metrics; (2) Study objectives, where confirmatory studies favor statistical methods, while exploratory or predictive studies benefit from machine learning; (3) Sample size, as deep learning requires larger datasets than classical ML or statistical approaches; (4) Clinical translation requirements, where interpretable models (e.g., decision trees, logistic regression) may be preferred over black-box algorithms for healthcare adoption. This methodological diversity highlights the multifaceted nature of anxiety detection research and the need for transparent reporting of analytical choices to enable cross-study comparisons and reproducibility.

Feature performance synthesis

Across the reviewed studies, time-domain HRV features demonstrated the most consistent utility for anxiety detection. RMSSD (root mean square of successive differences) emerged as a robust biomarker, with significant anxiety-related reductions observed across multiple ECG-based37,40,41,47 and PPG-based25,33 studies. RMSSD reflects parasympathetic (vagal) modulation and is particularly suitable for short recording durations (≥1 minute). SDNN (standard deviation of NN intervals) was also widely employed but showed greater variability in discriminative power, likely due to its sensitivity to both sympathetic and parasympathetic influences.

Frequency-domain features yielded more heterogeneous findings. The LF/HF ratio, traditionally interpreted as sympathovagal balance, demonstrated inconsistent directional changes during anxiety; some studies reported increases23,30, while others showed decreases or no significant change48. This variability likely reflects both the contested physiological interpretation of LF/HF and its sensitivity to respiratory dynamics. In contrast, high-frequency (HF) power showed more consistent reductions in studies with longer recording durations38,40,45.

Nonlinear features, although less frequently employed, demonstrated promising discriminative capability in studies using advanced signal processing31,33. Metrics such as sample entropy and Poincaré plot indices (SD1, SD2) provide complementary insights into the complexity of cardiac dynamics beyond linear HRV measures.

For PPG-derived features, pulse rate variability (PRV) metrics broadly mirrored ECG-derived HRV findings under stationary conditions. Pulse amplitude consistently decreased during anxiety, reflecting peripheral vasoconstriction15,16, while morphological features (e.g., pulse width and dicrotic notch timing) contributed additional discriminative information.

Across studies, the most reproducible pattern was a reduction in vagally mediated variability, particularly RMSSD and, where longer recordings were available, HF power. In contrast, the LF/HF ratio showed inconsistent behavior and appears less reliable as a standalone marker of anxiety. Additionally, studies reporting very high classification performance were often based on small sample sizes, whereas studies with larger or more heterogeneous cohorts tended to report more moderate but more credible estimates.

Taken together, these findings suggest that feature robustness and validation design are more informative than nominal model complexity alone. Time-domain vagal markers such as RMSSD provide a strong foundation for anxiety detection, while additional features may enhance performance depending on recording duration, sensing modality, and application context.

Diagnostic performance synthesis

A critical synthesis of reported diagnostic performance metrics across the 38 included studies reveals substantial heterogeneity in both reporting practices and achieved performance levels (Supplementary Table 4). Only 10 studies (26.3%) reported quantitative classification metrics, while the remaining studies focused on physiological correlations, method comparisons, or treatment prediction without explicit classification outcomes.

Among studies reporting classification accuracy, values ranged from 56% to over 99%, with a concerning inverse relationship between sample size and reported performance. Studies with small sample sizes (N < 50) consistently reported exceptionally high accuracies: Szakonyi et al.30 achieved 96.31% accuracy with only 7 participants, Baygin et al.31 reported > 98.5% accuracy with 19 participants, and Dahal et al.36 claimed 99–100% accuracy with 40 participants. These findings raise significant concerns regarding overfitting and limited generalizability, as such small samples are insufficient for robust model validation and may not capture the physiological variability present in broader populations.

Sensitivity and specificity, metrics critical for evaluating clinical utility, were reported in only 4 studies (10.5%). Where reported, sensitivity ranged from 82.4% to 99% and specificity from 90% to 97.4%. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), a threshold-independent measure of discriminative ability, was reported in only 3 studies (7.9%), with values ranging from 0.88 to 0.98. This inconsistent reporting of diagnostic metrics significantly limits the ability to conduct meaningful cross-study comparisons and meta-analytic synthesis.

Regarding the comparison between unimodal and multimodal approaches, the evidence does not consistently support the superiority of multimodal systems. While Dahal et al.36 reported near-perfect accuracy using a six-signal multimodal approach, Ihmig et al.27 achieved only 74.4% accuracy for three-level classification using ECG combined with electrodermal activity. Conversely, unimodal PPG-based approaches achieved AUC values of 0.88–0.98 in properly designed studies with larger samples33,45. These findings suggest that methodological rigor and sample size may be more important determinants of apparent performance than modality selection.

The gap between laboratory performance and clinical readiness is substantial. None of the reviewed studies validated their models on independent external datasets, and only two studies30,36 employed leave-one-subject-out cross-validation. The absence of prospective validation in clinical populations, combined with the predominance of healthy young participants in most studies, limits the translational potential of current approaches. Future studies should prioritize transparent reporting of all diagnostic metrics (sensitivity, specificity, AUC, and positive/negative predictive values), validation on independent datasets, and evaluation in clinically relevant populations to bridge the gap between promising laboratory results and clinically deployable systems.

Anxiety type stratification

A critical distinction must be made between induced acute stress and clinical anxiety, as these represent fundamentally different constructs with distinct translational pathways (Supplementary Table 5). Stratification of the 38 included studies reveals that the majority (28 studies, 73.7%) assessed physiological responses to induced acute stress in healthy participants using laboratory stressors such as the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST), Stroop tasks, anxiety-inducing videos, virtual reality scenarios, or public speaking tasks. Only 4 studies (10.5%) included participants with formally diagnosed anxiety disorders: Costa et al.25 examined patients with generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), Ihmig et al.27 studied individuals with spider phobia, Reichenberger et al.34 compared patients with social anxiety disorder (SAD) to healthy controls, and Davies et al.49 recruited participants meeting DSM-IV criteria for anxiety disorders. An additional 3 studies (7.9%) employed mixed populations including both clinical and healthy participants or individuals with subclinical symptoms41,50,52, while 3 studies (7.9%) focused on medical conditions where anxiety was a secondary measure15,26,28.

This stratification has profound implications for clinical translation. The physiological signatures of momentary stress responses, characterized by transient sympathetic activation and rapid recovery, may differ fundamentally from the chronic dysregulation observed in clinical anxiety disorders, which often involves sustained autonomic imbalance, altered baseline HRV, and impaired stress recovery. Detection algorithms trained predominantly on induced stress data from healthy young participants may therefore fail to generalize to clinical populations with established anxiety disorders. Future research should prioritize recruitment of clinically diagnosed populations and validation across the spectrum from induced stress to chronic anxiety to establish the true translational potential of ECG/PPG-based anxiety detection systems.

Psychological assessment tools

The 38 studies reviewed employed a broad range of self-assessment tools to evaluate psychological states, as shown in Fig. 5. Anxiety- and stress-specific questionnaires were the most prevalent, reported in 14 studies13,22–24,27,31,33–35,37,44,45,47,50, highlighting the central role of anxiety in psychophysiological monitoring research. Standardized instruments such as the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) and the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A) were frequently used to assess the severity and frequency of anxiety symptoms. Mood and depression assessments were reported in four studies29,32,39,41, utilizing tools such as the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and the Profile of Mood States (POMS) to evaluate affective status alongside physiological measures. Four studies employed personality and psychological assessments26,40,46,53, incorporating metrics of neuroticism, fearfulness, or fear of negative evaluation to understand inter-individual differences in anxiety vulnerability. Emotional and cognitive assessments were present in four studies14,27,43,51, often using scales like NASA-TLX or self-reported arousal and intensity ratings to gauge task-related stress and cognitive load. Clinical and health-related assessments appeared in four studies38,49,52,55, applying tools such as the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) or diagnostic frameworks to categorize participants' mental health or risk factors, including markers such as MSIMI. Nine studies did not report using any standardized self-assessment tools15,16,25,28,30,36,42,48,54, instead relying on observational data or physiological responses alone. This variability in assessment approaches underscores the multidimensional nature of anxiety evaluation and the complementary value of combining subjective measures with physiological data.

Across the 38 included studies, the most frequently observed serious limitation was restricted participant selection (L1), reported as serious in 20 studies, moderate in 14, and low in only 4. Repetitive exposure effects (L2) were predominantly moderate (n = 18), with fewer rated serious (n = 7) or low (n = 12), while one study could not be assessed for this limitation. Sensory modality restriction (L3) was most often rated low (n = 23), moderate (n = 14), and rarely serious (n = 1). Mismatched stimulus duration (L4) was primarily moderate (n = 18) or low (n = 14), with 5 serious ratings and 1 undetermined. Detection modality constraints (L5) showed a balanced distribution between moderate (n = 18) and serious (n = 11), with fewer low ratings (n = 9). Inadequate signal collection duration (L6) was mostly rated low (n = 26), but 4 were serious, 2 moderate, and 6 could not be determined. Discussion

This review reframes wearable anxiety detection from a signal-processing problem into a clinical translation challenge. The emergence of wearable ECG and PPG technologies reflects a convergence of digital health innovation, psychophysiological theory, and clinical need. The accelerating publication trajectory observed in this review, from sporadic studies before 2018 to consistent annual output of five to eight papers thereafter, mirrors broader trends in digital mental health56 and highlights growing recognition that objective physiological monitoring may address fundamental limitations of traditional anxiety assessment.

Importantly, this evolution is not merely technological but conceptual. Rather than treating anxiety as a categorical and episodic condition, current approaches increasingly support its characterization as a continuous, dynamic physiological state. This perspective aligns with contemporary frameworks such as the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC), which emphasize transdiagnostic biological markers and dimensional representations of mental health. Within this context, wearable sensing enables a shift toward longitudinal, real-world tracking of anxiety-related physiology, offering a pathway to bridge the gap between experimental paradigms and clinically meaningful monitoring.

The present findings also resonate with adjacent fields beyond anxiety detection. In affective computing, multimodal fusion has long been used to improve the classification of emotional states, yet similar challenges persist, including limited ecological validity, subject dependence, and poor generalization. In digital phenotyping, continuous passive sensing is increasingly viewed as a means to capture temporal fluctuations in mental health, but the central challenge remains the clinical interpretation of high-dimensional data rather than its collection. Likewise, psychophysiological research has consistently shown that autonomic arousal is transdiagnostic rather than anxiety-specific, underscoring the need to contextualize ECG- and PPG-derived signals within broader behavioral and clinical frameworks.

Taken together, these cross-disciplinary parallels indicate that future progress will depend less on incremental improvements in classification accuracy and more on rigorous validation, model interpretability, and integration with clinically grounded assessment frameworks.

The theoretical foundation for peripheral physiological anxiety detection rests primarily on the autonomic nervous system (ANS) dysregulation model, which posits that anxiety disorders are characterized by sympathetic hyperactivation and parasympathetic withdrawal. This framework draws support from Polyvagal Theory, which emphasizes the role of vagal tone in emotional regulation and social engagement. The reviewed studies implicitly operationalize this model through HRV metrics that index vagal activity (RMSSD, HF power) and sympathovagal balance (LF/HF ratio). However, the relationship between peripheral autonomic markers and the subjective experience of anxiety is neither simple nor universal. Neuroimaging research has demonstrated that anxiety engages distributed brain networks, including the amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, and insular cortex, with peripheral autonomic changes representing downstream manifestations of central processing. This hierarchical relationship has important implications: peripheral signals may detect physiological arousal associated with anxiety but cannot distinguish anxiety from other states producing similar autonomic profiles, such as physical exertion, excitement, or anger.

Compared to neuroimaging approaches that directly assess central nervous system activity, wearable ECG and PPG systems offer practical advantages in accessibility, cost, and ecological validity, but sacrifice specificity. Functional MRI studies have identified anxiety-specific neural signatures with high precision, yet these methods are impractical for continuous monitoring outside laboratory settings. Conversely, behavioral research traditions in clinical psychology have developed sophisticated paradigms for anxiety induction, as illustrated in Fig. 6e–l, and validated subjective assessment instruments (STAI, HAM-A, DASS-21), yet these approaches rely on retrospective self-report, vulnerable to recall bias and demand characteristics. The wearable physiological approach reviewed here occupies an intermediate position: more ecologically valid than neuroimaging, more objective than self-report, but less specific than either. The methodological patterns observed, including the predominance of active induction paradigms (63.2%), preference for distal sensor placements (finger 34.2%, wrist 21.05% as shown in Fig. 6m), and reliance on established machine learning classifiers, reflect pragmatic compromises between scientific rigor and real-world applicability.

A critical gap emerging from this synthesis concerns the disconnect between algorithmic performance metrics and clinical utility. The reviewed studies predominantly report classification accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, yet these metrics, while necessary for technical validation, do not address whether detection translates to improved patient outcomes. This evaluation gap is not unique to anxiety detection; it pervades digital health more broadly and reflects the nascent state of the field. Clinical psychology has established that effective anxiety treatment requires not merely detection but appropriate intervention timing, therapeutic alliance, and sustained engagement. How wearable detection systems integrate with these established therapeutic elements remains largely unexplored. Furthermore, the variation in signal collection durations across studies, ranging from under five minutes to over two hours, suggests uncertainty about the temporal dynamics of anxiety manifestation in peripheral physiology. Research in affective science indicates that anxiety states unfold across multiple timescales, from acute stress responses resolving within minutes to chronic anxiety persisting over weeks, yet few reviewed studies explicitly addressed which temporal patterns their detection systems target.

To address this translational disconnect, we propose a conceptual three-layer framework that organizes wearable anxiety detection across physiological, computational, and clinical domains (Fig. 7).

The framework illustrates the progression from physiological signal acquisition to computational modeling and ultimately to clinical decision-making. Current research is concentrated in the first two layers, with limited integration into clinical workflows and outcome-based validation.

The methodological heterogeneity observed across studies, while reflecting the exploratory nature of this emerging field, complicates synthesis and limits cumulative progress. Figure 6n illustrates the processing pipeline variability, with substantial attrition from signal acquisition through feature extraction to model validation. The field would benefit from standardized reporting frameworks analogous to CONSORT for clinical trials or PRISMA for systematic reviews, ensuring that critical methodological details, including preprocessing steps, feature definitions, and validation procedures, are consistently documented. Such standardization would facilitate meta-analytic synthesis, enable direct comparison across studies, and accelerate translation toward clinical application. The integration of self-assessment tools such as STAI, HAM-A, and DASS-21 alongside physiological measures reflects an emerging recognition that comprehensive anxiety assessment requires multimodal validation, combining the ecological validity of wearable sensors with the clinical interpretability of established psychological instruments.

This review identifies key trends in anxiety research, including the rise in publication volume, the diversity of participant profiles, and the evolving methods of anxiety induction and detection. The increasing use of machine learning and integrated physiological monitoring reflects a shift toward real-time detection tools57. Additionally, there is a growing reliance on physiological indicators of anxiety. However, challenges remain, particularly the need to standardize signal collection durations and integrate objective and subjective assessment methods more effectively. Future research should refine anxiety detection by improving data collection techniques, applying personalized machine learning models, and designing user-friendly wearable devices for continuous monitoring. Importantly, successful translation of these findings into clinical practice will require seamless integration with existing healthcare infrastructures, including compatibility with electronic health records and adherence to regulatory standards for medical devices. Such integration would enhance the real-world impact of these technologies and support their adoption in routine mental health care. The findings of this review support ongoing advancements in anxiety research, bridging the gap between laboratory studies and real-world applications. This work is essential to developing accessible, effective solutions for managing anxiety in everyday settings.

This review highlights key limitations that affect how anxiety is measured and understood. These limitations are categorized into six core domains (L1-L6) as shown in Fig. 8, covering restricted participant selection, repetitive exposure effects, sensory modality restriction, mismatched stimulus duration, detection modality constraints, and inadequate signal collection duration, with full details provided in Fig. 8.

A major issue is the frequent use of small, homogenous samples, often college students, which limits the generalizability of findings. Repeated exposure to the same anxiety-inducing stimuli can lead to habituation, reducing their effectiveness over time. In addition, participants who are familiar with the study procedures may alter their responses, compromising the authenticity of anxiety reactions. Many studies also limit sensory input despite real-life anxiety involving multisensory experiences. Laboratory settings often fail to replicate real-world, emotional conditions, reducing ecological validity. The duration of stimulus exposure is another concern; if too brief or too prolonged, it may not reliably induce anxiety. Studies relying solely on a single physiological measure, such as ECG or PPG, face detection limitations that can reduce accuracy. Short signal collection periods may also fail to capture the full range of physiological responses, weakening the reliability of anxiety detection. Addressing these challenges is crucial for developing reliable, widely applicable, and real-time anxiety detection systems.

While wearable ECG and PPG technologies show considerable promise for anxiety detection, translating these research findings into clinical practice presents multifaceted challenges that must be systematically addressed. Medical devices intended for clinical anxiety assessment must navigate stringent regulatory pathways. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) classifies software-based diagnostic tools under Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) regulations, requiring demonstration of analytical validity (accurate physiological measurement), clinical validity (correlation with anxiety states), and clinical utility (improved patient outcomes). Similar frameworks exist in Europe (CE marking under the Medical Device Regulation) and other jurisdictions. The reviewed studies predominantly represent proof-of-concept work; none reported pursuing regulatory clearance, and most lack the large-scale, prospective clinical trials necessary for approval. Moving from research prototypes to regulated medical devices requires substantial investment in clinical validation studies, quality management systems, and post-market surveillance infrastructure.

A critical translational barrier is the limited generalizability of current findings. The predominance of healthy, young, university-educated participants (55.3% of studies) raises concerns about algorithm performance in diverse clinical populations. This demographic homogeneity is particularly problematic given the age-dependent nature of autonomic function: older adults exhibit reduced heart rate variability (HRV) at baseline due to decreased parasympathetic tone, elevated resting heart rate, and diminished cardiovascular reactivity to stressors6. Algorithms calibrated on young adults may therefore misinterpret the naturally lower HRV of older populations as chronic anxiety, leading to unacceptably high false positive rates. Furthermore, the anxiety-related HRV reduction effect size may be attenuated in older adults whose baseline vagal tone is already diminished, reducing diagnostic sensitivity.

Cardiovascular comorbidities represent an additional confounding factor that remains unaddressed in the current literature. Conditions prevalent in older and clinical populations (including hypertension, coronary artery disease, heart failure, and arrhythmias) fundamentally alter the ECG and PPG signals upon which anxiety detection algorithms depend. Beta-blocker medications, commonly prescribed for hypertension and cardiac conditions, directly suppress heart rate and modify HRV parameters, potentially masking anxiety-related tachycardia. Atrial fibrillation and other arrhythmias introduce irregular R-R intervals that violate the stationarity assumptions underlying many HRV analyses. PPG waveform morphology is altered by arterial stiffness, peripheral vascular disease, and reduced cardiac output. These pathophysiological changes create a fundamental domain shift between the training data (healthy young participants) and the target deployment population (older adults with comorbidities), threatening the validity of anxiety detection in precisely the populations that may benefit most from remote monitoring58.

Transitioning from controlled laboratory settings to real-world environments presents additional challenges. Laboratory-based anxiety induction provides controlled conditions for algorithm development but poorly approximates real-world anxiety experiences. Clinical anxiety often manifests as chronic, fluctuating states rather than discrete, stimulus-evoked episodes. Ecological factors absent in laboratory settings (physical activity, environmental temperature, caffeine consumption, sleep deprivation, and concurrent cognitive demands) introduce physiological confounds that can mimic or mask anxiety-related signals. The transition from controlled laboratory conditions to ambulatory monitoring in daily life requires robust motion artifact rejection, context-aware processing (e.g., distinguishing exercise-induced tachycardia from anxiety), and algorithms tolerant to missing data and variable signal quality.

Successful clinical adoption requires seamless integration into existing healthcare workflows. Key considerations include Electronic Health Record (EHR) interoperability, where wearable-derived anxiety metrics must be transmittable to clinical information systems using standardized formats (HL7 FHIR, SNOMED CT) to enable longitudinal tracking and clinical decision support. Healthcare providers require education on interpreting physiological anxiety markers and integrating wearable data into clinical reasoning alongside traditional assessment methods. Clear protocols must also define how wearable-detected anxiety episodes trigger clinical responses, ranging from automated therapeutic interventions (e.g., guided breathing prompts) to clinician notification and emergency escalation.

The patient perspective is essential for successful translation. Continuous physiological monitoring raises concerns about data privacy, surveillance anxiety (paradoxically increasing anxiety through awareness of monitoring), and the psychological burden of managing health technology. User adherence (consistently wearing devices and maintaining charged batteries) determines real-world effectiveness but was rarely assessed in reviewed studies. Device comfort, esthetics, and social acceptability influence long-term adoption, particularly for populations such as adolescents who may resist visible health monitoring devices.

Health equity implications must also be considered in translational efforts. Wearable devices and associated services incur costs that may exclude economically disadvantaged populations, groups often at elevated anxiety risk due to social determinants of health. Algorithms validated primarily in higher socioeconomic populations may perform poorly in underserved communities. Digital literacy requirements may create additional barriers for elderly users or those with limited technology access. Ensuring equitable access to wearable anxiety monitoring requires attention to device affordability, algorithmic fairness, and culturally appropriate implementation strategies.

The impact of false positives on clinical workflow represents a critical yet underexplored translational barrier. Alert fatigue, the desensitization of clinicians to frequent alarms leading to delayed or missed responses, poses a significant threat to the clinical viability of continuous anxiety monitoring systems. In wrist-based wearable devices, motion artifacts represent a primary source of false positive detections. As documented in limitation L5 (Detection Modality Constraints), 11 of 38 studies (28.9%) exhibited serious concerns regarding signal quality, and an additional 18 studies (47.4%) showed moderate concerns. Wrist-worn PPG sensors are particularly susceptible to motion-induced signal corruption during ambulatory monitoring, where activities such as walking, typing, or gesturing can produce artifactual pulse waveforms that mimic anxiety-related tachycardia or reduced HRV59. To prevent clinical abandonment, future systems must implement robust signal quality indices (SQI) that gate detection algorithms, rejecting unreliable segments before classification60. Multi-sensor fusion approaches, combining PPG with accelerometer data for motion compensation or integrating ECG for signal validation, may enhance specificity. Additionally, adaptive thresholding strategies that personalize alert criteria based on individual baselines and contextual factors (time of day, activity level, location) could substantially reduce false positive rates. Establishing acceptable performance benchmarks for ambulatory anxiety detection, balancing sensitivity for clinical utility against specificity for workflow feasibility, remains an essential prerequisite for regulatory approval and clinical adoption.

Perhaps most critically, the reviewed literature lacks evidence demonstrating that wearable-based anxiety detection improves clinical outcomes. While numerous studies report classification accuracy (sensitivity, specificity, AUC), none evaluated whether detection leads to earlier intervention, reduced symptom severity, improved quality of life, or healthcare cost savings. Establishing clinical utility requires randomized controlled trials comparing wearable-augmented care pathways against standard care, with patient-centered outcomes as primary endpoints.

To enhance the precision and applicability of anxiety detection research, several recommendations should be considered. Including participants from diverse demographics will improve the generalizability of findings. To prevent habituation and sustain participant engagement, studies should incorporate diverse and adaptive stimuli. Inputs combining multimodal stimuli, such as visual, auditory, and haptic inputs, can simulate real-life anxiety more accurately and elicit richer responses. Both passive stimuli (e.g., video clips or ambient sounds) and active stimuli (e.g., tasks or interactive challenges) should be explored, as they evoke different aspects of anxiety. Standardizing or dynamically adjusting stimulus durations with physiological response times will yield more consistent data.

Employing multiple detection modalities can offer a more comprehensive view of the physiological changes associated with anxiety, while extending signal collection durations can improve the assessment of sustained responses. Real-time feedback mechanisms that adapt stimuli based on physiological data can also enhance participant engagement. Immersive environments, such as virtual reality, may better simulate anxiety-provoking situations, improving ecological validity. Long-term studies are essential for evaluating the stability and reliability of detection models over time. Future studies would also benefit from incorporating formal risk of bias assessments using standardized tools, such as ROBINS-I or QUADAS-2, to improve interpretability, transparency, and methodological rigor. To further enhance transparency, a formal tabulation of methodological limitations was visualized in Fig. 8, providing a comparative view across six core dimensions of concern (L1 to L6) to support critical appraisal and guide future research.

Overall, the body of evidence reviewed highlights substantial progress in the use of physiological signals for anxiety detection, particularly when combined with controlled induction methods. ECG remains the most widely used modality due to its robust HRV measurements, while PPG is valued for its accessibility and adaptability. Literature supports chest placement for ECG and finger placement for optimal PPG signal-to-noise ratio, with the wrist offering the most practical option for everyday use. Recording durations of at least 5-10 minutes are recommended to ensure reliable physiological metric extraction. To bridge the gap between laboratory research and practical applications, future work should focus on validating these systems in real-world contexts and addressing persistent methodological and technical challenges. Based on the findings of this review, we identify several key directions to guide the development of next-generation wearable ECG and PPG systems for anxiety detection.

From a methodological perspective, future research should prioritize diversifying participant populations by conducting large-scale, multi-center studies that include varied age groups, genders, ethnicities, and clinical populations to improve generalizability. Equally important is the standardization of anxiety induction protocols through the development and adoption of consensus-based paradigms that ensure comparability of results across studies. Additionally, optimizing signal acquisition duration by establishing evidence-based guidelines, such as a minimum of 5–10 minutes for reliable HRV metrics, will help balance measurement accuracy with user comfort and compliance.

Technical advancements should focus on advancing multimodal integration by combining ECG and PPG with complementary signals such as respiration, EDA, and motion to enhance both sensitivity and specificity. Improving sensor placement and design through refined wearable form factors and optimized placement strategies (e.g., wrist, chest, ear) will maximize signal quality under daily-life conditions. Furthermore, addressing hardware and sampling limitations (including increased sampling rates, improved photodiode sensitivity in PPG sensors, and optimized electrode materials for better skin contact and reduced noise) remains essential for achieving clinical-grade signal quality.

Computational priorities include enhancing real-time processing capabilities by developing embedded algorithms for on-device feature extraction, artifact removal, and adaptive feedback that do not rely on cloud processing. Implementing personalized machine learning models that move from population-level approaches to individualized baselines will enable more accurate detection of both acute and chronic anxiety states.

Successful clinical translation will depend on integration within digital health ecosystems, including interoperability with electronic health records, mental health platforms, and telemedicine workflows. Equally critical is longitudinal validation through multi-week and multi-month field studies to establish robustness, user adherence, and real-world reliability.

Wearable ECG and PPG technologies offer a compelling pathway toward objective and continuous anxiety monitoring, but their impact will ultimately hinge on a shift from algorithm-centric performance to outcome-driven validation. The three-layer framework proposed in this work highlights that meaningful progress requires alignment across physiological insight, computational rigor, and clinical integration. Without such alignment, even highly accurate models are likely to remain confined to controlled experimental settings. Bridging this gap represents a defining challenge—and opportunity—for the next generation of digital mental health systems.

Methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study is a systematic review of previously published research and did not involve the collection or analysis of any new data from human participants or animals. In accordance with journal policy and institutional requirements, formal ethics approval was not required. All included primary studies reported obtaining ethics approval and informed consent from participants, as applicable, and procedures were described as conforming to relevant guidelines and regulations (including the Declaration of Helsinki).

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines61. The review process consisted of three main stages: literature search, study selection, and data extraction with methodological assessment.

Search strategy

A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, and ScienceDirect to identify relevant studies published between 1 January 2015 and 31 July 2025. These databases were selected for their complementary coverage of biomedical, engineering, and translational research domains relevant to wearable sensing technologies.

The 2015 start date was chosen to reflect the emergence of consumer-grade wearable devices integrating ECG and PPG sensors, alongside advances in machine learning for physiological signal analysis. Search queries were adapted to the syntax of each database. In PubMed, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms (“Anxiety”, “Electrocardiography”, “Photoplethysmography”) were combined with relevant keywords using Boolean operators. In Embase, Emtree terms (’anxiety’, ’electrocardiography’, ’photoplethysmography’) were used with equivalent keywords. In ScienceDirect, searches were applied to titles, abstracts, and author-supplied keywords. Across all databases, terms related to “detection”, “recognition”, or “prediction” were included.

Filters were applied to restrict results to English-language studies involving human participants. To ensure methodological relevance, only studies reporting analyzable physiological signals were retained.

Given the focused scope of this review, studies were required to involve wearable or portable ECG and/or PPG sensors and to address anxiety detection, recognition, or prediction. Consequently, broader studies on general stress, multimodal affect recognition without ECG/PPG, non-wearable clinical systems, or purely psychological assessments were excluded.

The complete database-specific search strings are provided in Supplementary Note 1 to ensure full transparency and reproducibility.

Study selection

All retrieved records were imported into a single database, and duplicates were removed. The study selection followed PRISMA guidelines and proceeded through four stages: identification, deduplication, screening, and eligibility assessment. Screening was conducted in two steps: (1) title and abstract screening and (2) full-text review. All screening and eligibility assessments were performed independently by multiple reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with the corresponding author.

The database search yielded 641 records (PubMed, n = 186; Embase, n = 347; ScienceDirect, n = 108). After the removal of 23 duplicates, 618 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 542 articles were assessed for full-text eligibility, and 504 were excluded for not meeting the predefined inclusion criteria. The most common reasons for exclusion included studies focusing on general stress rather than anxiety, use of non-wearable systems, absence of ECG or PPG modalities, or lack of analyzable physiological outcomes. Ultimately, 38 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the final synthesis.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies met all of the following criteria: (i) original research involving human participants, (ii) use of wearable or portable ECG and/or PPG sensors for anxiety detection, recognition, or prediction, and (iii) reporting of physiological signal analysis related to anxiety or stress with identifiable measurement parameters. Exclusion criteria comprised: review articles, meta-analyses, editorials, letters, conference abstracts, symposium papers, non-English publications, studies without human participants, and studies not meeting the outcome focus. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Data extraction and synthesis

For each included study, data were extracted on: study design, participant demographics, anxiety induction protocol, sensor type and placement, signal acquisition parameters, processing and feature extraction methods, analytical approach, and key findings. Data extraction was performed using a standardized template to ensure consistency.

Methodological limitation assessment

Six methodological limitation categories were defined a priori: participant selection (L1), repetitive exposure effects (L2), sensory modality restriction (L3), mismatched stimulus duration (L4), detection modality constraints (L5), and inadequate signal collection duration (L6). Each study was evaluated against these categories and assigned a rating of low, moderate, or serious concern based on predefined, operationalized criteria to minimize subjectivity.

For clarity, the rating levels were interpreted as follows: Low indicates methodological adequacy in the given category (e.g., diverse or justified participant selection for L1, appropriate randomization and washout for L2, sensory modality aligned with study aims for L3, stimulus duration matching physiological response windows for L4, suitable detection modality and sampling for L5, and sufficiently long signal collection with baseline and multiple trials for L6). Moderate reflects minor to moderate concerns that may affect generalizability or precision (e.g., over-representation of one group without full justification for L1, incomplete counterbalancing for L2, single-modality setup with broader conclusions implied for L3, stimulus duration mismatches of approximately 0.5–0.8 × or 1.2-1.5 × planned for L4, borderline sensor adequacy or limited calibration for L5, and shorter but usable recordings for L6). Serious denotes major methodological issues likely to bias results or limit interpretability (e.g., very narrow participant groups without justification for L1, fixed-order or high-repetition exposures without washout for L2, sensory modality misaligned with aims for L3, large mismatches in stimulus duration <0.5 × or >1.5 × planned for L4, use of unsuitable sensors or inadequate sampling for L5, and signal collection too short to derive stable features for L6).

These definitions were consistently applied in all included studies to ensure reproducibility and facilitate direct comparison of methodological rigor.

Comparison with QUADAS-2

To contextualize our methodological assessment within established evidence-based medicine frameworks, we mapped the L1–L6 categories to the QUADAS-2 (Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies) domains62. The mapping was performed as follows: (1) Patient Selection (QUADAS-2 Domain 1) corresponds directly to L1 (Restricted Participant Selection); (2) Index Test (Domain 2) was derived from the higher-risk rating between L3 (Sensory Modality Restriction) and L5 (Detection Modality Constraints), as both relate to how the detection test was conducted and interpreted; (3) Flow and Timing (Domain 4) was derived from the highest-risk rating among L2 (Repetitive Exposure Effects), L4 (Mismatched Stimulus Duration), and L6 (Inadequate Signal Collection Duration); and (4) Reference Standard (Domain 3) was rated as “Not Applicable” for all studies, as physiological anxiety detection research relies on self-report questionnaires, induced stress paradigms, or behavioral observation rather than established clinical gold standards such as structured diagnostic interviews or biomarker assays.

This mapping (Supplementary Table 6) reveals that 52.6% of studies exhibited serious concerns for Patient Selection, 28.9% for Index Test, and 39.5% for Flow and Timing. We acknowledge that our L1–L6 framework has not been formally validated as a standalone tool; however, its conceptual alignment with QUADAS-2 domains and the operationalized, transparent criteria (Supplementary Table 2) were designed to maximize reproducibility and facilitate evidence synthesis in this emerging field.
