When Prostheses Collide
Authors: Anas Noman, Swatam Jain, Aref Bin Abdulhak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Youssef et al1 report a compelling case in this issue of JACC: Case Reports: An 82-year-old woman with a prior surgical bioprosthetic mitral valve replacement who developed aorto-mitral curtain injury and a contained left ventricular pseudoaneurysm following transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with a balloon-expandable valve. The proposed mechanism—balloon interaction between the transcatheter heart valve and a protruding mitral bioprosthesis, in the setting of a markedly constrained mitro-aortic distance of only 2.3 mm—describes a pathway of structural injury that, to our knowledge, has not been previously characterized. As the reviewer for this report, I want to use this editorial to draw out 3 lessons I believe deserve emphasis beyond what the space available in a case report typically allows; Dr. Jain and Dr. Bin Abdulhak reviewed this editorial and contributed critical revisions before submission.

The first lesson is anatomic. TAVR in patients with a prior mitral prosthesis is increasingly common, and most attention in this population has rightly focused on left ventricular outflow tract obstruction from a protruding bioprosthesis.2, 3, 4 This case demonstrates that clearance, not just orifice geometry, matters just as much. A mitro-aortic distance of 2.3 mm is not simply a risk factor for embolization or malpositioning. It is also a risk factor for direct mechanical transmission of force from the delivery balloon, through the annular complex, into the aorto-mitral curtain itself.

It may help to think of structural injury in this population as falling into 3 failure modes rather than one undifferentiated category of “prosthesis interaction”: obstructive (a protruding bioprosthesis narrowing the left ventricular outflow tract, the mechanism that has dominated the literature to date), embolic (inadequate anchoring or seating distance leading to device migration), and transmissive—the mechanism this case brings into focus, in which mechanical force is conducted through rigid, closely apposed prosthetic material into structures never designed to absorb it. Naming this third category gives operators a framework to reach for the next time a completion angiogram looks slightly off in a patient with a prior mitral prosthesis.

This is worth grounding in numbers. Registry data suggest valve embolization occurs predominantly when the mitral prosthesis-to-aortic annulus distance falls below 7 mm, and proposed minimum working distances—3 mm for transfemoral Sapien S3, 4 mm for CoreValve, 8 mm for transapical SAPIEN—offer practical guidance.5 At 2.3 mm, this case sat well outside any reasonable margin. Preprocedural planning should extend beyond routine annular sizing to a dedicated assessment of the aortomitral relationship, ideally during systolic-phase interrogation, and a short aortomitral distance should prompt heightened caution in valve selection.6 Balloon-expandable valves offer greater deployment precision, whereas self-expanding platforms are repositionable but carry a longer frame that may increase the likelihood of interaction—a tradeoff this case shows is not purely theoretical.4 But CT-derived distance is, by definition, static, and the injury here occurred under dynamic balloon pressure that no preprocedural image can fully anticipate. This argues for extending the same vigilance intraprocedurally—real-time transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) or fluoroscopic assessment of the mitral prosthesis during balloon inflation, not only during positioning—and for device-specific deployment characteristics to be examined as a contributor to this mechanism across platforms.

The second lesson is about how we look at our own angiograms, and it is the point I most want to underscore. The completion aortogram in this case showed focal contrast extravasation that was not recognized at the time. This is not a criticism of the operators; retrospective clarity is a luxury the index procedure did not have, and the patient remained hemodynamically stable with a reassuring TEE. But it is precisely because the patient looked fine that the finding was easy to move past. Elsewhere in this literature, comparable aortographic findings have been described as “subtle.” I want to push back on that word. A finding is only subtle in the moment it is missed; framed by the clinical syndrome it preceded, it is the earliest available evidence of the exact injury that follows. Calling a missed finding subtle protects the reader from discomfort, not the next patient from harm. A more concrete fix: completion aortography in patients with a prior mitral prosthesis could include a dedicated, named step—specific attention to the aorto-mitral curtain interface—so this region is checked deliberately rather than incidentally.

The third lesson is temporal. This patient did not decompensate on the table. She did not tamponade. She went home the next day, as thousands of patients undergoing TAVR appropriately do, and returned 2 days later with dyspnea and back pain—an atypical presentation for a left ventricular pseudoaneurysm. Procedure-related pseudoaneurysms are typically broad-necked, and postinfarction left ventricular pseudoaneurysms carry an estimated 30% to 45% risk of rupture if untreated;7 procedure-related lesions may not share that same natural history, but the comparison still argues for vigilance rather than reassurance. That this patient presented subacutely rather than catastrophically should raise our index of suspicion for delayed structural injury after TAVR in patients with complex mitro-aortic anatomy, even when the index hospitalization and transthoracic echocardiogram were reassuring. Given how closely this timeline tracked a predictable window, a defined early postdischarge check—a structured phone assessment or scheduled transthoracic echocardiogram—may be reasonable for patients discharged with a documented mitro-aortic distance below the working thresholds noted above.

The management decision—conservative surveillance given prohibitive surgical risk and preserved hemodynamic stability—was reasonable and well justified by the Heart Team discussion described in the report. I would only add that this case argues for a lower threshold for extending intraprocedural TEE or angiographic surveillance in this anatomy, so that a finding like the one here is caught before the patient leaves the table rather than on retrospective review.

Prosthesis-to-prosthesis interaction is not yet a standard line item on preprocedural imaging worksheets. After this case, I think it should be. Youssef et al1 have done our field a service by naming a mechanism that likely explains injuries we may have been attributing to less specific causes or failing to recognize. The most important take-home message may be the simplest one: On completion angiography, there is no such thing as a subtle finding, only ones we have not yet learned to take seriously.
