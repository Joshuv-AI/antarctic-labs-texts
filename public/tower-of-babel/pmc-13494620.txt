Contraceptive use and unfulfilled preferences among women ages 15–49 years in the United States, 2022–2023
Authors: Mia R. Zolna, Doris W. Chiu, Megan L. Kavanaugh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo update estimates of, and examine the intersection between, method-specific contraceptive use and unfulfilled contraceptive preferences for US women ages 15–49 years since multiple events (e.g., 2019 Title X Final Rule, COVID-19 pandemic) disrupted reproductive health access in the United States. We situate findings within the changing health policy climate and broader evidence base.DesignThis secondary analysis uses the recently available 2022–2023 National Survey of Family Growth, a cross-sectional population-based household survey of US women ages 15–49. We conducted multivariable logistic regression analyses to identify associations between individual's characteristics, method-specific contraceptive use, and unfulfilled contraceptive preferences.SubjectsThe full sample of female respondents ages 15–49 (n = 5,586) and subsamples: all contraceptive users including permanent female method users (n = 3,163), contraceptive users and single-method users–both excluding those using only permanent female methods to address data constraints (n = 2,564 and n = 1,861 respectively), and contraceptive nonusers (n = 1,451).ExposureNone.Main Outcome MeasuresCurrent contraceptive use, method-specific use accounting for multiple methods, and unfulfilled contraceptive preferences because of cost.ResultsIn 2022–2023, 57% of women (42.8 million) reported currently using contraception for any reason. The most common methods were oral contraceptive pill (23%), condom (21%), female permanent methods (20%), withdrawal (19%), and intrauterine device (16%). One-quarter (22%) of users reported multiple methods. Thirteen percent of users preferred a different method, and 31% of nonusers preferred to start a method, if cost were not a worry. Higher levels of unfulfilled contraceptive preferences among users were associated with identifying as a sexual minority (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.63; confidence interval [CI]: 1.17–2.27) and among nonusers younger than age 40 (aOR = 1.58–2.26; CI: 1.02–4.85) or not married or cohabiting (aOR = 2.49; CI: 1.68–3.69). Among single-method users, 23% using coital-dependent methods, 11% using short-acting methods, and 7% using long-acting methods, reported unfulfilled contraceptive preferences.ConclusionMost US women used their preferred contraceptive method; however, barriers remain. Although trend analyses were not feasible because of changes in survey design, unfulfilled preferences were lower compared with other population-level studies; the extent to which this represents true shifts or shifts in data quality remains unclear. Healthcare systems grounded in sexual and reproductive equity and that reduce costs are needed to increase access to preferred methods.

Access to contraception, especially the full range of methods, is critical for individual sexual and reproductive well-being (SRWB), a state of fulfillment in all aspects related to sex and reproduction (1, 2). In the United States, contraception is widely used for various reasons, including to prevent pregnancy, manage health conditions, and avoid sexually transmitted infections (3, 4). One likely indicator of SRWB in a population is the alignment between individuals' contraceptive use—overall and method-specific—and their contraceptive preferences. Together, these measures highlight the extent to which individuals in the population are able to use their preferred contraception, and crucially, whether there are significant inequities in who is able to do so.

Setting public health goals that prioritize a population’s SRWB and monitoring progress toward those goals is a powerful way to both assess whether all people across a range of age, gender, race, and other intersectional identities have what they need to attain their highest level of SRWB, and to help identify how to best adapt and build public health systems to achieve sexual and reproductive health equity (SRHE). A key resource used to monitor indicators of SRWB in the United States is the National Survey of Family Growth (NSFG) (1, 5). The NSFG is a periodic nationally representative survey of individuals of ages 15–49, which collects information on pregnancies, contraceptive use, and other sexual and reproductive health-related information. Considered the gold standard for sexual and reproductive health data since 1973, the most recent round of data collection was conducted in 2022–2023 and underwent changes in study design since the prior round in 2017–2019 (5); most significantly, survey administration shifted to a new entity, data collection transitioned from single-mode face-to-face (FTF) interviews to multimode with a web component, and there was a marked decrease in response rates compared with earlier rounds.

Our last picture of the nation’s health, addressing both contraceptive use and preferences from the NSFG, covers the years 2017–2019. At that time, female contraceptive users ages 15–49 most commonly used female permanent methods (28%), followed by oral contraceptive pills (21%), male condoms, and intrauterine devices (13% each), and partner vasectomy (9%) (6). Almost one-quarter of female contraceptive users (23%) reported a preference to use a different method, and 39% of nonusers reported they would start using a method, if cost were not a worry (7). Of note, throughout this analysis, we use the terms “women” and “female” to reflect those used in the NSFG. However, the NSFG and other data collection processes do not always accurately, comprehensively, or appropriately account for respondents’ gender identity and sex assigned at birth; these processes may therefore miss capturing eligible contraceptive users.

Since then, multiple events in the United States have likely impacted access to contraception and individuals' ability to access their preferred method. Such events include the 2019 Title X Final Rule, which overhauled regulations governing the Title X national family planning program, the COVID-19 pandemic that impacted both contraceptive service delivery and how people accessed that care, and the US Supreme Court’s Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization decision that overturned federal protections for abortion access that has indirectly impacted contraceptive preferences (8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13). Importantly, data collection for the 2022–2023 NSFG occurred in the aftermath of, or overlapped with, these events, and survey responses were likely influenced by this broader context.

Several US population-level studies have highlighted contemporary trends in contraceptive use and preferences since the prior 2017–2019 NSFG, including KFF’s periodic Women’s Health Survey and the Sexual Health and Reproductive Equity (SHARE) Program’s Person-Centered Contraceptive Access Survey (3, 14, 15, 16). Although these studies help shed light on more recent trends, most employed an online-only survey administration protocol; thus, the samples may reflect a more advantaged population than a nationally representative one. A more recent data brief drawing on the multimode 2022–2023 NSFG data indicates a relatively similar method mix among all women ages 15–49 compared with 2017–2019, but the report does not account for simultaneous method use nor examine contraceptive preferences alongside method use (17).

Thus, we set two objectives for this analysis. First, for US women ages 15–49 years in 2022–2023, we estimate overall contraceptive use, method-specific use among users, and unfulfilled contraceptive preferences overall and by select sociodemographic characteristics. Second, given the recent shifts in the NSFG study design and resulting inability to directly compare our estimates to prior rounds of the survey, we situate our estimates within the broader evidence base. Our goal is to triangulate our findings along with other available evidence on contraceptive use and preferences at the US national level and to provide guidance on how to interpret this fuller body of work as it relates to SRWB in the US population.

Methods

Data

This cross-sectional study draws on the 2022–2023 NSFG, designed and administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (5). This publicly available, nationally representative household survey of US individuals ages 15–49 years was conducted via web-based surveys for some respondents and FTF interviews with a Computer-Assisted Self-Interview (CASI) component at the end for others. The sample was selected using a multistage probability-based complex design that oversampled Black individuals and adolescents aged 15–19. For this analysis, we drew from the female respondent file, which includes data from all respondents who self-identified as female at the time of interview (January 2022 to December 2023). Additional details regarding the survey and the data files can all currently be accessed through the NSFG website (5). Given the deidentified nature of the data used, our organization’s institutional review board (Department of Health and Human Services identifier IRB00002197) determined that this analysis was exempt from review.

Analytic Sample

This analysis examines outcomes among the full study sample of female respondents ages 15-49 and among several subsamples. Specifically, we examine current contraceptive use among the full sample (n = 5,586) and among a subsample of those who were sexually active (i.e., had penile-vaginal sex in the past three months) and not pregnant nor trying to become pregnant, excluding those who were surgically sterile for noncontraceptive reasons or by nonsurgical means (n = 2,700). We examine method-specific use, including use of multiple methods, among current contraceptive users (n = 3,163). Finally, among the full sample excluding those using only permanent female methods, we examined unfulfilled contraceptive preferences because of cost among current contraceptive users (n = 2,564) and single-method users2 (n = 1,861), as well as nonusers of contraception (n = 1,451). We excluded users of only female permanent methods for our analyses of unfulfilled contraceptive preferences because of inconsistent and erroneous skip patterns in the NSFG survey among these respondents. (Supplemental Figure 1, available online, includes detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria for our key analytic samples.) This analysis focuses on unfulfilled contraceptive preferences because of cost and reflects the language used in the single NSFG survey item related to contraceptive preferences. Analyses of unfulfilled contraceptive preferences according to method used were limited to users of a single method to directly link preference assessments to the specific method type used.

Variables

Our first key outcome is current contraceptive use. It is defined as whether a respondent reported using any contraceptive method regardless of reason in the month of the survey, and in this analysis, we include up to four methods reported to capture a fuller picture of people’s contraceptive behaviors. This approach is a notable change from many past analyses using NSFG data, which have highlighted current use of a single, most effective contraceptive method (17, 18, 19). Respondents are coded as a nonuser if they did not report using any contraceptive method during this time period. Among users, we also generated a series of individual method variables to identify respondents using any of the following: female permanent methods including female sterilizing operations, such as tubal ligation and hysterectomy for contraceptive reasons, male sterilization, intrauterine device (IUD), implant, oral contraceptive pill, either injectable, contraceptive patch, or contraceptive ring, male condom, withdrawal, fertility awareness-based methods (FABMs) including calendar-based, cervical mucus-based, temperature-based, and sympto-thermal-based methods and cycle apps, emergency contraceptive pill, and other coital dependent methods including the female condom, diaphragm, foam, sponge, suppository or insert, jelly or cream and “other” methods. We also grouped individual methods into four categories according to method-specific duration of protection: any permanent method (female or male), long-acting reversible contraception (LARC, including IUD or implant), short-acting reversible contraception (SARC, including oral contraceptive pill, injectable, contraceptive patch, or contraceptive ring), or any coital-dependent method (including male condom, withdrawal, FABMs, emergency contraceptive pill, or other). Finally, we generated a variable to identify multiple method users for respondents who reported use of more than one method during the time period. Respondents who reported they were surgically sterile for noncontraceptive reasons or by nonsurgical means were not considered to be method users.

Our second key outcome is unfulfilled contraceptive preferences because of cost, which reflects the only survey item related to preferences. It is defined by a “yes” response among contraceptive users to the item “If you did not have to worry about cost and could use any type of contraceptive method available, would you want to use a different method?” or to the modified item presented to nonusers “…would you want to use a method?”. For this set of analyses, we narrowed our samples of users and nonusers for distinct reasons. Among users, we identified erroneous skip patterns in the NSFG questionnaire that prevented some respondents who reported using only female permanent methods (n = 101) from being administered the subsequent contraceptive preference item; we therefore excluded all respondents who reported using only this method (n = 524) from all analyses examining unfulfilled contraceptive preferences among contraceptive users. An additional 3% of the remaining users were recoded as such by the NSFG team post-data collection, so preferences for this group were not elicited and are not reflected in this analysis. With regard to contraceptive nonusers, NSFG respondents who reported nonuse during the current and previous month were administered the contraceptive preference item specific to nonusers; however, we consider nonuse during current month only to align with the timeframe of contraceptive use examined in this analysis. Responses of “don’t know” and refused (1% of each sample) were coded as missing.

Our analysis examined several sociodemographic and other characteristics chosen a priori, given their well-documented associations with method use and preferences. These include self-reported age at interview, household income as a percentage of the federal poverty level (FPL), race and ethnicity, nativity, relationship status, sexual orientation, educational attainment, current insurance coverage, parity, and future births expected. Relationship status includes responses from those reporting both different and same sex partnerships. Individuals’ current insurance coverage prioritizes private sources of coverage, including Medi-Gap, then Medicaid and other public coverage, such as Children’s Health Insurance Program, state-sponsored health plans, Medicare, and military health care in cases where respondents indicated more than one type of coverage. Coverage through a single service plan or only Indian Health Service was grouped with no coverage, according to the NSFG recode documents. For sexual orientation, respondents who indicated “don’t know” were coded under the sexual minority category that includes “lesbian or gay,” “bisexual,” or “something else.” Missing demographic information is limited to nativity, relationship status, and sexual orientation (≤ 1–3% missing each); these respondents were omitted from analyses including these variables.

Statistical Analysis

We tabulated three sets of statistics to examine contraceptive use: the weighted percentage of all women who used and did not use any method, the weighted percentage of sexually active women not seeking pregnancy who used and did not use any method, and the weighted percentage of method users by method type and by sociodemographic and other characteristics. We present standard errors for each. We also ran multivariable logistic regression models to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of the associations between respondents’ characteristics and their use of the five most common contraceptive methods used and multiple methods, taking into account other co-occurring characteristics. We highlight findings from these multivariable models that were statistically significant at P<.05 levels, and we additionally note unadjusted associations between characteristics and method outcomes when the direction of these associations shifted between unadjusted and adjusted models, likely due to confounding by correlated demographic characteristics. Models examining the association between respondents’ characteristics and less commonly used methods are included in Supplemental Table 1, available online. All models include the full set of covariates, except for the regression model for female permanent methods, which excludes future births expected because of collinearity.

To examine unfulfilled contraceptive preferences, we tabulated the weighted percentage of current contraceptive users, excluding those using only female permanent methods, who would prefer to use another method if cost were not an issue, and nonusers who would prefer to start using a method if cost were not an issue. We then ran a multivariable logistic regression model to estimate associations between respondents’ characteristics and unfulfilled contraceptive preferences for each of these two samples. Given the high level of missingness of preference information among current nonusers (approximately 40% of whom were skipped out of the preference item; Supplemental Fig. 1, available online), we conducted a sensitivity analysis to assess whether there were meaningful differences in outcomes among this group compared with all nonusers for whom there is preference information available in the dataset, specifically contraceptive nonusers during both the current and previous month. Findings were similar (data not shown for the latter group), so we proceeded with the analysis focused on nonusers during the current month to align with the timeframe of our contraceptive use outcome. Finally, we calculated the percentage of contraceptive users who only used one method, excluding those using only female permanent methods, and reported unfulfilled contraceptive preferences by type of method used.

Analyses were conducted using Stata 18.0. We used the “svy” command to account for the NSFG’s complex sampling frame and design variables, to weight the data to represent the US civilian, noninstitutionalized female population ages 15–49 between January 2022 and December 2023, and to obtain accurate standard errors. We used the “subpop” command when limiting analyses to subsets of users and nonusers. Finally, we applied the National Center for Health Statistics Data Presentation Standards for Proportions for complex sample surveys and suppressed or flagged estimates that should be interpreted with caution according to the NCHS-contributed Stata program kg_nchs.

Results

In 2022–2023, more than half of all women, 57%, or 42.8 million, reported currently using contraception (Table 1). The majority of those who were sexually active and not seeking pregnancy (87%) used a method. The five most commonly used methods among contraceptive users were the oral contraceptive pill (23%), condom (21%), female permanent methods (20%), withdrawal (19%), and IUD (16%); followed by male sterilization (11%), FABMs (7%), implant and a combined group of injectable, contraceptive patch, contraceptive ring (5% each), and emergency contraceptive pills and other coital dependent methods (1% each). Approximately one-quarter (22%) of contraceptive users reported using more than one of these methods in the current month. Higher levels of oral contraceptive pill use were associated with being under age 30 (aOR = 1.79–3.19; CI: 1.08–5.51), having an income of ≥ 300% FPL (compared with those <200% FPL; aOR = 0.54–0.58; CI: 0.33–0.88), identifying as non-Hispanic white (compared with Hispanic; aOR = 0.70; CI: 0.53–0.92), not married or cohabiting (aOR = 1.55; CI; 1.12–2.14), not a sexual minority (compared with identifying as a sexual minority; aOR = 0.68; CI: 0.51–0.92), or never having given birth (compared with those who have; aOR = 0.30; CI: 0.23–0.41). In bivariate analyses, pill use was more common among respondents who identified as belonging to a sexual minority group (25%) compared with those who did not (23%) (data not shown).

Higher levels of IUD use were associated with having a college degree (compared with some high school or college; aOR = 0.57-0.65; CI: 0.36–0.96), identifying as non-Hispanic white (compared with non-Hispanic Black or other multiple races; aOR = 0.45–0.60; CI: 0.28–0.92), or a sexual minority (aOR = 1.82; CI: 1.34–2.46). In this analysis, age was not associated with IUD use among those aged 20–49 (estimates for 15–19 year olds did not meet reliability standards and are suppressed).

Higher levels of condom use were associated with being under age 40 (aOR = 2.20–4.13; CI: 1.56–5.94), identifying as Black or a race other than white or Hispanic (aOR = 1.82–1.94; CI: 1.20–2.77), being born outside of the United States (aOR = 1.60; CI: 1.24–2.08), or having a college degree (compared with attending some college; aOR = 0.56; CI: 0.37–0.84).

Higher levels of withdrawal use were also associated with being under age 40 (aOR = 1.51–2.17; CI: 1.01–4.53), identifying as Black (aOR = 1.64; CI = 1.07–2.51), or expecting one or more births in the future (aOR = 1.92; CI:1.42–2.61); lower levels of withdrawal use were associated with not being married or cohabiting (compared with being married; aOR = 0.56; CI: 0.40–0.78). In bivariate analyses, withdrawal was more common among those who were cohabiting (27%) compared with those who were married (17%) (data not shown).

Similarly, higher use of more than one method was associated with being under age 40 (aOR = 1.65–3.46; CI: 1.26–6.35), whereas lower levels of multiple method use was associated with not being married or cohabiting (compared with being married; aOR = 0.68; CI = 0.50-0.94). In bivariate analyses, use of multiple methods was more common among those who were not married or cohabiting (24%) compared with those who were married (20%) (data not shown). Discussion

Our findings, which are from the latest data available for the United States, indicate that the majority of women ages 15–49 (57%) report current use of contraception, continuing a pattern that has reflected US contraceptive behavior for decades (17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23). Additionally, 13% of contraceptive users with the exclusion of those who use female permanent methods, and 31% of nonusers, indicate that they would prefer to use a different method or to start a method if cost were not a worry. Taken together, these findings signal that in 2022–2023, whereas most US women ages 15–49 use their preferred contraceptive method, for a sizable proportion of these women—notably 10 million women—cost barriers to realizing one’s contraceptive preferences persist. Through the lens of SRHE, these findings suggest that those who are considering strategies for reducing the gap between actual and ideal use focus on the needs of and barriers faced by this group, who are disproportionately younger, not married or cohabiting, and sexual minority individuals.

This analysis considers contraceptive behavior more thoroughly than in past analyses (19), recognizing that people’s use of contraceptive methods is more complex than can be reflected by reporting on just the single, most effective method used. Specifically, it builds on Frohwirth et al.’s (24) scholarship that highlighted how people develop contraceptive strategies to align with their motivations regarding contraceptive use, Polis et al.’s (25) conceptual approach to accurately capturing FABMs as potentially one of multiple methods used within the NSFG, and Kavanaugh et al.’s (4) work to document multiple method use at the national-level. Inherent in this expanded conceptualization of contraceptive use is the recognition that multiple motivations, beyond just pregnancy prevention, may be driving contraceptive decisions (3).

Even with our expanded methodological approach, which accounts for the use of up to four methods and inherently reflects a fuller picture of an individual’s contraceptive use, we find that four of the five most popular contraceptive methods from 2017–2019 remained the most used in 2022–2023: oral contraceptive pills, male condoms, female permanent methods, and the IUD. Notably, condoms and withdrawal are used at higher levels than previously estimated, likely reflecting that these two methods, in particular, are part of some people’s contraceptive strategies that employ more than one method to achieve their contraceptive objectives. In addition to marked increases revealing these coital- and behavioral-methods are among the most popular methods used, we note that use of oral contraceptive pills and condoms both now surpass the use of female permanent methods, historically the most commonly used method among women ages 15–49. These findings are aligned with those from the most recent US population-based 2024 KFF survey (3).

Findings from several other national-level surveys conducted around the same time as the 2022–2023 NSFG, as well as past NSFG surveys, show that approximately one-quarter of contraceptive users report not using their preferred method, but several of these estimates are not restricted to cost-constrained preferences (3, 7, 26, 27). Our finding of cost-constrained unfulfilled contraceptive preferences among users, excluding users of only female permanent methods, at 13% is notably lower and is likely an underestimate of the true prevalence. Our estimate of cost-constrained unfulfilled contraceptive preferences among nonusers is also lower than the previous analysis of the 2015–2019 NSFG data (31% vs. 39%) (7). We hypothesize that the primary driver of these observed differences is due to shifts in the survey design, a point we take up in more detail below. Importantly, our findings regarding patterns of higher unfulfilled contraceptive preferences among users of coital-specific methods like condoms and FABMs and lower unfulfilled contraceptive preferences among users of longer-acting methods, particularly the IUD, largely mirror evidence from other state- and national-level studies (26, 28).

Triangulating findings regarding the prevalence of unfulfilled contraceptive preferences among individual method users with those on characteristics associated with method-specific contraceptive use reveals a key insight about inequities related to contraception. Individuals holding one or more identities typically associated with more socially dominant or well-resourced categories, like those reporting non-Hispanic white racial identities and college graduates, were more likely to report use of the IUD, and it is users of this method who reported the lowest level of unfulfilled contraceptive preferences. One notable exception to this pattern is the higher use of the IUD among those identifying as a sexual minority, which mirrors other evidence highlighting this method as a key contraceptive strategy among this group (29). On the other hand, use of withdrawal, a method whose users reported the highest levels of unfulfilled contraceptive preferences, was associated with characteristics typically associated with being in a less resourced group, including being younger or identifying as non-Hispanic Black. This method is inherently free, further underscoring that cost is a barrier that prevents people from accessing the care they feel would work best for them.

There has been a tremendous amount of upheaval in the reproductive health landscape over the last several years, including the 2019 Title X Final Rule, the COVID-19 pandemic, and the SCOTUS Dobbs decision. Indeed, several smaller studies drawing on administrative and claims data assessing changes in people’s contraceptive use from before to after the Dobbs decision indicate that some people who can afford the upfront costs are shifting toward using sterilization and longer-acting methods, especially, in response to perceived or actual barriers to reproductive freedom (30, 31, 32). Few studies have documented how contraceptive preferences may have shifted during this same time; however, attention to use and preferences in tandem as reported here, is critical to understanding whether the increased threats to people’s SRWB occurring from both state- and federal-policies may be changing both the prevalence of fulfilled contraceptive preferences as well as what people’s preferences are within a constrained environment. Importantly, the ongoing policy changes that threaten sexual and reproductive health access and freedom under the 2025–2029 Trump/Vance US presidential administration will likely continue to impact people’s contraceptive choices, and thus, we expect contraceptive use and preferences to have shifted even more since 2022–2023 (33, 34).

We report these 2022–2023 findings with caution regarding how best to interpret them alongside the broader evidence base on population-level trends on contraceptive use and preferences in the United States. Given reports highlighting how shifts in the NSFG study design and survey administration have yielded a noticeably different—especially more resourced—sample than achieved in years past (35), whether because of much lower response rates, changes in survey modes or, most likely, a combination of these factors, we focus our takeaways on broad, observable patterns in the data. Additionally, given NCHS guidance that cautions against making comparisons between these 2022–2023 data and previous years because of the shifts in study design, we do not make any direct assessments of how use and preference patterns may be shifting over time (5).

Our outcome representing unfulfilled contraceptive preferences has additional limitations. The single NSFG survey item available to measure this concept explicitly names cost as context for how preferences are assessed, and this approach is misaligned with scientific recommendations for how best to measure contraceptive preferences, which include using unconstrained language given multiple potential barriers to obtaining contraception and a two-item approach (14, 28, 36). We also exclude users of only female permanent methods from our preference analyses given data quality concerns, but this population of users of this commonly used method in the United States is an important group to continue to consider. We encourage administrators of future NSFG rounds to consider emerging evidence on best practices for contraceptive preference measurement.

Conclusions

The top-level takeaways from this analysis and the broader evidence around 2022–2023 highlight: IUDs continue to be an increasingly popular method; female permanent method use represents a smaller share of the method mix than in years prior; large proportions (almost one-quarter) of users use more than one method—often withdrawal and/or condoms alongside another method; and unfulfilled contraceptive preferences (because of cost) occur among nearly one in three nonusers and tend to be higher among single-method users relying on coital-dependent methods, whereas they are lower among those relying on longer-acting methods alone. These findings underscore the need for a healthcare system grounded in SRHE and person-centered care to ensure that all methods are available for people to choose the method or methods best for them, through evidence-based information, dialogue with providers, and free from coercion, discrimination, and financial barriers to help reduce the gap between actual and ideal use. In the current US climate, where access to sexual and reproductive health care is being disrupted by policies that cut resources for and limit people’s access to reproductive health care services—in addition to the ongoing federal efforts to dismantle and discontinue key datasets that include sexual and reproductive health data that stakeholders rely on to monitor the nation’s health (37)—we are concerned that we are moving farther from these goals of SRWB and person-centered care.
