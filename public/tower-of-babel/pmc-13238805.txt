Comparison of Clinical Outcomes of INBONE II Total Ankle Arthroplasty in Patients Under 50 and Over 65 Years: A 4‐Year Retrospective Study
Authors: Xiangyu Xu, Baozhou Zhang, Xuewen Wang, Yong Wu, Hui Du, Xiaofeng Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThere is no consensus on the age restriction for total ankle arthroplasty (TAA) patients. The aim of our study was to analyze the clinical function, radiographic outcomes, patient satisfaction, and complications between patients under 50 years old and those over 65 years old who underwent TAA with the INBONE II prosthesis for end‐stage ankle arthritis.MethodsPatients who received TAA with the INBONE II prosthesis between September 2016 and August 2021 were included in the study. A total of 68 patients were enrolled and divided into two groups based on age: the younger group (28 patients age ≤ 50 years) and the elderly group (40 patients age ≥ 65 years). Demographic data and patient‐reported outcomes, including 36‐Item Short‐Form Health Survey (SF‐36), American Orthopedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS), Foot Function Index (FFI), and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores, were collected preoperatively and at the latest follow‐up. Radiographic measurements, including tibial articular surface angle (TAS), talar tilt angle (TT), and tibial lateral surface angle (TLS), were evaluated on weight‐bearing images preoperatively and at the latest follow‐up. Range of motion (ROM) and complications were documented. Patient satisfaction was assessed using a five‐point Likert scale.ResultsWith a median follow‐up of 4 years, there were no statistically significant differences in AOFAS and SF‐36 scores between the two groups at the latest follow‐up. However, the elderly group showed slightly lower VAS and FFI scores compared to the younger group, while the younger group demonstrated slightly greater dorsiflexion angles. There were no statistically significant differences in radiographic evaluations, patient satisfaction, and complications between the two groups.ConclusionThe 4‐year postoperative outcomes of TAA using the INBONE II prosthesis were comparable between patients younger than 50 years and those older than 65 years, indicating that age alone may not serve as a definitive criterion for patient selection in TAA with this implant.Trial RegistrationRetrospectively registered.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

With the widespread utilization of total ankle arthroplasty (TAA) in the treatment of end‐stage ankle osteoarthritis in the elderly, the age threshold for surgical indications has attracted increasing attention [1, 2]. Unlike patients undergoing knee or hip arthroplasty, most patients with end‐stage ankle osteoarthritis have a history of trauma, leading to a younger patient demographic [3, 4, 5]. Compared to arthrodesis, TAA offers younger patients better mobility, a more natural gait, less adjacent joint disease of the subtalar and talonavicular, and an enhanced quality of life [6]. However, given the less‐than‐optimal long‐term survival rates of TAA compared to knee and hip arthroplasty, consensus is still lacking on whether TAA should be performed on younger patients [7, 8].

Currently, the number of studies evaluating the impact of age on outcomes of TAA is limited. Some studies have reported higher postoperative complication rates, higher implant failure rates, and worse patient‐reported outcomes in younger patients [9, 10, 11]. Conversely, other studies have shown that younger patients revealed similar results to older [12, 13, 14, 15]. These studies are often hindered by factors such as prosthesis type heterogeneity, short follow‐up periods, and mixed results. There is still a lack of reports on postoperative functional outcomes using a single prosthesis in the younger patient cohort.

Therefore, the present study was designed to address the following objectives in patients who underwent total ankle arthroplasty using the INBONE II prosthesis: (i) to compare postoperative clinical outcomes, including pain relief, functional recovery, health‐related quality of life, and patient satisfaction, between patients aged ≤ 50 years and those aged ≥ 65 years; (ii) to compare radiographic alignment correction, ankle range of motion, and complication profiles between the two age groups; and (iii) to evaluate whether younger age alone should remain a restrictive factor in patient selection for modern stemmed total ankle arthroplasty. We hypothesized that younger patients would demonstrate outcomes comparable to those of elderly patients, without increased rates of major complications or reoperation.

Materials and Methods

Patients

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients who underwent total ankle arthroplasty with the INBONE II implant at our institution between September 2016 and August 2021. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) patients who underwent primary TAA using the INBONE II prosthesis; (ii) age between 18 and 50 years, or 65 years and older at the time of surgery; (iii) diagnosis of end‐stage ankle arthritis requiring surgical treatment; and (iv) minimum follow‐up of 3 years. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) previous or concurrent lower‐limb surgery outside the affected foot and ankle that could influence postoperative function; (ii) neurogenic arthropathy; (iii) active infection; (iv) lower‐extremity vascular disease; and (v) loss to follow‐up.

A total of 68 patients were enrolled. Patients were divided into two groups based on age: 28 patients were classified into the younger group (≤ 50 years), and 40 patients were classified into the elderly group (≥ 65 years). All total ankle arthroplasty procedures were performed utilizing the INBONE II external system and were conducted by two experienced orthopedic surgeons specializing in foot and ankle surgery. Intraoperative additional procedures were required in three elderly patients, comprising one case of lateral displacement calcaneal osteotomy and two cases of percutaneous Achilles tendon lengthening. Demographic characteristics including age, gender, laterality, BMI, follow‐up duration, and etiology were recorded. All patients were classified according to the Takakura‐Tanaka Classification. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from **** HOSPITAL (K2024‐499‐00). This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. This was a retrospective study, informed consent was waived with agreement of the Ethical Committee.

Clinical Assessment

Functional outcomes were assessed using the American Orthopedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) hindfoot score, the Foot Function Index (FFI), and the 36‐Item Short‐Form Health Survey (SF‐36). Pain levels were evaluated using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). Patient satisfaction was measured using the Likert scale, which included five levels: very satisfied, satisfied, neutral, dissatisfied, and very dissatisfied [16]. The first two were grouped as “satisfied” and the last three as “dissatisfied”. All assessments were conducted preoperatively, at 6 months postoperatively, and at the latest follow‐up.

Radiographic Assessment

All radiographic measurements were performed on weight‐bearing plain X‐ray images (Figures 1 and 2). On the anteroposterior view of the ankle, the tibial articular surface angle (TAS) and talar tilt angle (TT) were measured. On the lateral view of the foot, the tibial lateral surface angle (TLS) was assessed. The range of motion (ROM) of the ankle joint was measured and recorded in extreme plantarflexion and dorsiflexion using standing clinical photographs. Measurements were conducted preoperatively, at 6 months postoperatively, and at the latest follow‐up. Two trained orthopedic residents independently performed all measurements, and the average values were used for analysis.

Postoperative Management and Complications

Postoperatively, the ankle joint was immobilized in a neutral position using a plaster cast, which was removed 3 to 4 weeks postoperatively. Suture removal was usually performed following cast removal, with judicious delay in cases of compromised wound healing based on clinical evaluation. A phased rehabilitation protocol was implemented consisting of immediate initiation of non‐weight‐bearing exercises after cast removal, transitioned to partial weight‐bearing at 6 weeks postoperatively, and then gradually transitioned to full weight‐bearing. Postoperative complications were recorded in follow‐up visits.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, values were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Non‐normally distributed data were presented as median and interquartile range. For normally distributed data, paired t‐tests or independent samples t‐tests were used, while non‐normally distributed data were analyzed with the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the chi‐square test. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

The median follow‐up time was 48.0 months (36.0, 57.8) in the younger group and 42.0 months (36.0, 55.0) in the elderly group (Table 1). The median age in the younger group was 47.5 years (43.3, 49.0), compared to 67.5 years (65.0, 70.8) in the elderly group. In the younger group, 12 patients (42.9%) were male and 16 (57.1%) were female. In the elderly group, 21 patients (52.5%) were male and 19 (47.5%) were female. The mean BMI was 24.9 ± 3.3 kg/m2 in the younger group and 23.6 ± 3.0 kg/m2 in the elderly group. In the younger group, 11 ankles (39.3%) were left and 17 (60.7%) were right. In the elderly group, 20 ankles (50.0%) were left and 20 (50.0%) were right. In the younger group, 10 patients (35.7%) were classified as type 3B and 18 (64.3%) as type 4. In the elderly group, 21 patients (52.5%) were classified as type 3B and 19 (47.5%) as type 4.

Patient satisfaction showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups. In the younger group, the satisfaction rate was 89.3% (25/28), with 18 patients reporting being very satisfied, seven satisfied, one neutral, two dissatisfied, and none very dissatisfied. In the elderly group, the satisfaction rate was 87.5% (35/40), with 32 patients being very satisfied, three satisfied, four neutral, one dissatisfied, and none very dissatisfied.

Radiographic Results

Both groups showed statistically significant differences in dorsiflexion angle, ROM, TAS, TLS, and TT compared to preoperative measurements (Table 2). There were no statistically significant differences in preoperative radiographic measurements between the two groups (Table 3). At the latest follow‐up, the dorsiflexion angle in the younger group was statistically significant higher than that in the elderly group.

Complications

By the latest follow‐up, six patients (21.4%) in the younger group and five patients (12.5%) in the elderly group experienced delayed wound healing postoperatively; all wounds had healed by the latest follow‐up. There were no statistical differences between the two groups in the ratio of delayed wound healing. One patient in the younger group underwent reoperation 6 years postoperatively due to ankle impingement and restricted motion. Following ankle debridement, Achilles tendon lengthening, and calcaneal lateralizing osteotomy, the patient's range of motion was restored, and symptoms were alleviated. No other severe postoperative complications, such as implant loosening, subsidence, or implant failure, were observed in either group. No superficial infection, deep infection, or skin necrosis were reported.

Discussion

Main Findings

The present study evaluated whether age influences outcomes after total ankle arthroplasty using the INBONE II prosthesis by comparing patients aged 50 years or younger with those aged 65 years or older. The most important finding was that both groups achieved substantial improvements in pain, function, and radiographic alignment at midterm follow‐up, with no significant differences in AOFAS score, SF‐36 score, patient satisfaction, major complications, or reoperation. Although younger patients demonstrated slightly greater postoperative dorsiflexion, the overall clinical outcomes were comparable between age groups. These findings suggest that younger age alone should not be considered an absolute limiting factor when selecting candidates for modern stemmed total ankle arthroplasty.

Comparison With Previous Literature

TAA has been shown to provide excellent long‐term outcomes in elderly patients [17, 18]. But the viability of this procedure in younger patients has been controversial. Our results are consistent with the postoperative follow‐up outcomes reported in other studies using the INBONE II prosthesis [19, 20]. Several studies have stratified patient populations by age to evaluate the impact of age on the outcomes of TAA, with varying age thresholds, implant types, and results. Anastasio et al. reported 1115 patients, found that patients under 55 years had higher revision and failure rates, as well as worse preoperative and postoperative patient‐reported outcomes compared to patients over 55 years [9]. Although the large sample size strengthened the study's power, heterogeneity in prosthesis types and baseline characteristics posed limitations. Similarly, Christian et al. focused on the Salto mobile‐bearing prosthesis, reporting higher rates of reoperation and revision in patients under 50 years of age [10]. In contrast, Demetracopoulos et al. observed comparable outcomes between patients under 55 and those over 55 at early follow‐up [13]. Cody, in a mid‐ to long‐term follow‐up study, reported that age is not associated with the failure rate of TAA [21]. Kipp et al. in a systematic review encompassing seven studies, found no differences in reoperation rates, complication rates, or prosthesis survival between younger and elderly patients [22].

In our study, no cases of revision surgery or prosthesis failure were observed. Only one patient in the younger group underwent reoperation 6 years postoperatively due to ankle impingement and restricted motion. Kevin et al. reported that younger age correlates with increased gutter impingement rates after TAA [23]. However, in our series, postoperative ankle impingement was uncommon, which was probably because our follow‐up time was relatively short, and we also took extreme care to debride the osteophytes of the medial and lateral gutter more thoroughly during the surgery. Additionally, both groups experienced delayed wound healing, which may be related to the early surgical incision design and the postoperative wound care. Considering the bowstring phenomenon of the tibialis anterior tendon, we moved the incision laterally to avoid opening the retinaculum of the compartment for the tibialis anterior tendon in the later surgeries.

Functional Differences Between Age Groups

The younger patients demonstrated slightly higher postoperative dorsiflexion angles, which may be attributed to their higher activity levels. In a gait comparison study between patients who underwent ankle arthrodesis and those who underwent TAA, the TAA group exhibited higher step frequencies, longer stride lengths, and faster walking speeds [24]. Moreover, the increased range of motion and normal gait afforded by TAA may help prevent the progression of adjacent joint degeneration. In a 22‐year follow‐up study of ankle fusion patients, nearly all patients showed signs of arthritis in the ipsilateral hindfoot joint, while the incidence of arthritis in the contralateral foot was statistically significantly lower [25]. In contrast, a 6.5‐year follow‐up study of 140 patients who underwent TAA showed that 73% of the subtalar joints and 69% of the talonavicular joints showed no signs of continued arthritis progression [26]. For younger patients, who are expected to have a longer life expectancy, the progression of disease in adjacent joints must also be considered.

Clinical Implications of Age in TAA Selection

The Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) was calculated using the 0.5 standard deviation (SD) methodology, which is widely utilized in the literature and recommended by experts as a standard approach for distribution‐based MCID determination [27, 28, 29]. In our study cohort, the distribution‐based MCID values for VAS, AOFAS, FFI, and SF‐36 scores were determined to be 1.3, 10.7, 20.9, and 10.6, respectively. Although a statistical difference was observed in the VAS (2.0 vs. 1.0) and FFI scores (20.0 vs. 6.5) at the latest follow‐up between the two groups, these differences may not represent clinically meaningful variations. The distribution‐based MCID for ankle dorsiflexion angle was calculated to be 2.4°. The observed difference in dorsiflexion angles between the two groups (13.5° vs. 10.9°) exceeded this MCID threshold, indicating a clinically relevant difference. Furthermore, neither a clinically nor statistically significant difference was noted in the AOFAS (82.0 vs. 82.5) and SF‐36 scores (122.5 vs. 119.1).

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. First, all patients were treated using a single contemporary prosthesis design (INBONE II), which reduced implant‐related heterogeneity seen in prior registry‐based studies. Currently, no studies have specifically investigated age‐related outcome differences in TAA using the INBONE II prosthesis system. Our study provides some data to fill this gap. Second, all surgeries were performed by experienced foot and ankle surgeons at a single institution using a consistent perioperative protocol, improving treatment uniformity. Third, both patient‐reported and radiographic outcomes were comprehensively evaluated with a minimum follow‐up of 3 years. Finally, the comparison of two clearly separated age cohorts may provide practical information for surgical decision‐making in younger candidates for TAA.

However, there are several limitations to our study. As a retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, the results may lack sufficient reliability. The average follow‐up time of 4 years is insufficient to assess long‐term complications following TAA, and thus no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding long‐term outcomes. Future studies with extended follow‐up periods are needed to provide more robust data and further support this area of research.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that patients under 50 years old with end‐stage ankle arthritis who underwent TAA with the INBONE II prosthesis achieved favorable outcomes over an average 4‐year follow‐up. These outcomes included good foot and ankle function and high satisfaction rates. Compared to patients aged 65 years and older, there were no statistically significant differences in clinical outcomes. This suggests that age alone may not be a strict criterion for TAA patient selection with INBONE II prosthesis.
