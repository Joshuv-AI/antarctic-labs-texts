Jet Functors and Weil Algebras in Automatic Differentiation: A Geometric Analysis
Amandip Sangha

Abstract
We present a geometric formulation of automatic differentiation (AD) using jet bundles and Weil algebras. Reverse-mode AD emerges as cotangent-pullback, while Taylor-mode corresponds to evaluation in a Weil algebra. From these principles, we derive concise statements on correctness, stability, and complexity: a functorial identity for reverse-mode, algebraic exactness of higher-order derivatives, and explicit bounds on truncation error. We further show that tensorized Weil algebras permit one-pass computation of all mixed derivatives with cost linear in the algebra dimension, avoiding the combinatorial blow-up of nested JVP/VJP schedules. This framework interprets AD theory through the lens of differential geometry and offers a foundation for developing structure-preserving differentiation methods in deep learning and scientific computing. Code and examples are available at https://git.nilu.no/geometric-ad/jet-weil-ad .

3
Background: Jets, Weil Algebras, and Functorial AD

We summarize the geometric constructions required for our results: jet spaces and bundles, pushforwards and pullbacks, and Weil algebras. These notions provide the formal setting for automatic differentiation.

3.1
Smooth manifolds and differentials

Let
M,N
be finite-dimensional
C^{\infty}
manifolds. For a smooth map
f:M\to N
and
x\in M
, the
differential
at
x
is

df_{x}:T_{x}M\longrightarrow T_{f(x)}N,

a linear map between tangent spaces. Its dual (cotangent) map is

(df_{x})^{*}:T^{*}_{f(x)}N\longrightarrow T^{*}_{x}M,

called the
cotangent pullback

[
10
]
. It satisfies contravariant functoriality:

(d(g\circ f)_{x})^{*}=df_{x}^{*}\circ dg_{f(x)}^{*}.

3.2
Pushforward and pullback

For a smooth map
f:M\to N
and
x\in M
:

•

The
pushforward
transports tangent vectors forward:

df_{x}:T_{x}M\longrightarrow T_{f(x)}N,\qquad v\mapsto df_{x}(v).

In local coordinates
(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
on
M
and
(y^{1},\dots,y^{m})
on
N
, this is represented by the Jacobian matrix
[
10
]

J_{f}(x)=\left[\frac{\partial f^{i}}{\partial x^{j}}(x)\right]_{i=1,\dots,m}^{j=1,\dots,n},

and the pushforward acts as

df_{x}(v)=J_{f}(x)\,v.

•

The
pullback
transports covectors backward:

(df_{x})^{*}:T^{*}_{f(x)}N\longrightarrow T^{*}_{x}M,\qquad\omega\mapsto\omega\circ df_{x}.

In coordinates, for
\omega=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{m})\in T^{*}_{f(x)}N
, the pullback acts by matrix transpose
[
4
]
:

(df_{x})^{*}(\omega)=J_{f}(x)^{\top}\omega.

3.3
Automatic differentiation: forward and reverse

Automatic differentiation (AD) computes derivatives of programs by applying the chain rule to a composition of elementary primitives at machine precision and without finite differencing or symbolic expansion
[
4
,
1
]
. A
program
in AD is a finite acyclic computational graph of differentiable primitives (basic operations or functions such as addition, multiplication, or smooth elementwise functions with known derivatives), mapping inputs
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to outputs
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Its semantics is the function obtained by evaluating nodes in topological order (an ordering of nodes so that every node appears after all its dependencies). A
straight-line program
(SLP) is a program with no branches or loops, represented by sequential assignments
u_{k}=\phi_{k}(u_{i_{1}},\ldots,u_{i_{r_{k}}})
with
i_{j}<k
; AD then applies local derivative propagation rules (forward mode for directional derivatives and reverse mode for gradients) along this sequence to compute derivatives efficiently.

Throughout, let

f:\,\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad y=f(x),\qquad J_{f}(x)=\Big[\tfrac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(x)\Big]_{i=1,\dots,m}^{\;\;j=1,\dots,n},

and recall from §
3.2
that the differential
df_{x}:T_{x}\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\to T_{f(x)}\mathbb{R}^{m}
(pushforward) and its dual
(df_{x})^{*}:T^{*}_{f(x)}\mathbb{R}^{m}\!\to T_{x}^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
(cotangent pullback) encode first-order variation geometrically
[
10
,
8
]
.

Forward mode (pushforward/JVP).

JVP stands for
Jacobian–Vector Product
, i.e., multiplying the Jacobian
J_{f}(x)
by a tangent vector
v
to propagate directional derivatives. Given a seed (tangent) direction
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the directional derivative of
f
at
x
in direction
v
is

\dot{y}\;=\;df_{x}(v)\;=\;J_{f}(x)\,v,

i.e. JVP
[
1
,
7
]
. Operationally, each intermediate scalar
u
is paired with its tangent
\dot{u}
, and elementary rules implement the chain rule:

\displaystyle u=a+b\;\Rightarrow\;\dot{u}=\dot{a}+\dot{b},

\displaystyle u=a\cdot b\;\Rightarrow\;\dot{u}=\dot{a}\,b+a\,\dot{b},

\displaystyle u=\varphi(a)\;\Rightarrow\;\dot{u}=\varphi^{\prime}(a)\,\dot{a}\quad(\varphi\in C^{1}).

In the manifold terms of §
3.1
-
3.2
, forward mode computes the pushforward
df_{x}

[
10
,
8
]
.

Reverse mode (pullback/VJP).

VJP stands for
Vector–Jacobian Product
, i.e., multiplying the transpose
J_{f}(x)^{\top}
by a
“seed”
covector
\omega=\nabla\ell(y)
to propagate gradients backward. Here
\nabla\ell(y)
denotes the gradient of a scalar function
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\nabla\ell(y)=\bigg(\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial y_{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial y_{m}}\bigg)^{\top}.

Given a seed covector
\omega=\nabla\ell(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, reverse mode computes the Vector-Jacobian Product (VJP)

\bar{x}\;=\;(df_{x})^{*}(\omega)\;=\;J_{f}(x)^{\top}\omega,

characterized by the dual pairing (evaluation map)
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{x}:T_{x}^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}\times T_{x}\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\langle(df_{x})^{*}\omega,\,v\rangle\;=\;\langle\omega,\,df_{x}(v)\rangle\quad\forall\,v\in T_{x}\mathbb{R}^{n},

i.e. the cotangent pullback along
f

[
10
,
8
]
. The corresponding adjoint rules apply along a reverse sweep:

\displaystyle u=a+b\;\Rightarrow\;\bar{a}\mathrel{+}=\bar{u},\;\;\bar{b}\mathrel{+}=\bar{u},

\displaystyle u=a\cdot b\;\Rightarrow\;\bar{a}\mathrel{+}=b\,\bar{u},\;\;\bar{b}\mathrel{+}=a\,\bar{u},

\displaystyle u=\varphi(a)\;\Rightarrow\;\bar{a}\mathrel{+}=\varphi^{\prime}(a)\,\bar{u}.

For
m=1
(scalar output) this yields the full gradient
\nabla_{x}f(x)
in time within a small constant factor of a primal evaluation, which underlies backpropagation in deep learning
[
1
,
7
]
. Categorically, reverse mode is the contravariant action (pullback) on covelocities—“backprop as functor”
[
6
,
4
]
.

Chain rule in both modes.

For a composition
g\circ f
,

d(g\!\circ\!f)_{x}\;=\;dg_{f(x)}\circ df_{x},\qquad(d(g\!\circ\!f)_{x})^{*}\;=\;(df_{x})^{*}\circ(dg_{f(x)})^{*},

which in coordinates gives

J_{g\circ f}(x)\;=\;J_{g}(f(x))\,J_{f}(x),\qquad\nabla_{x}(\ell\!\circ\!f)(x)\;=\;J_{f}(x)^{\top}\nabla\ell(f(x)).

These are precisely the functorial identities for pushforwards and cotangent pullbacks invoked in our geometric framework (§
3.4
–§
3.5
), providing a coordinate-free account of JVP/VJP and backpropagation
[
10
,
8
,
6
]
. In the manifold terms of §
3.1
-
3.2
, reverse mode computes the cotangent pullback
(df_{x})^{*}
.

3.4
Jets and jet bundles

We denote by
\mathbf{Man}
the category whose objects are smooth manifolds
and whose morphisms are smooth maps.
Thus, for manifolds
M,N\in\mathbf{Man}
, the hom-set

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Man}}(M,N)
consists of all smooth maps
f:M\to N
.

For
k\geq 1
, two smooth maps
f,g:M\to N
have the same
k
-jet at
x
if all partial derivatives up to order
k
agree in local charts.
The corresponding equivalence class is denoted
j^{k}_{x}f
.
The
k
-jet bundle

J^{k}(M,N)
is the bundle of all such jets, with projection
\pi^{k}:J^{k}(M,N)\to M
.
The
jet prolongation
of
f
is the section

j^{k}f:M\longrightarrow J^{k}(M,N),\qquad x\longmapsto j^{k}_{x}f.

Moreover, the assignment
(M,N,f)\mapsto J^{k}(M,N)
extends (for a fixed manifold
N
) to a
functor

J^{k}(-,N):\mathbf{Man}\longrightarrow\mathbf{Man},

(or, more precisely a bifunctor
J^{k}:\mathbf{Man}^{op}\times\mathbf{Man}\longrightarrow\mathbf{Man}
, but we will only consider
J^{k}=J^{k}(-,N)
here)
which acts on maps by

J^{k}(f)(j^{k}_{x}\phi)=j^{k}_{x}(f\circ\phi),

and satisfies the naturality property
[
10
,
8
]
:

J^{k}(g\circ f)=J^{k}(g)\circ J^{k}(f).

In this sense,
k
-jets encode the truncated Taylor expansion of smooth maps up to order
k
,
independently of any choice of coordinates.
Indeed, in local coordinates
(x^{i})
on
M
and
(y^{\alpha})
on
N
, the
k
-jet of
f
at
x
is determined by the
collection of partial derivatives

\left\{\frac{\partial^{|\beta|}f^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{\beta}}(x)\ \middle|\ 1\leq\alpha\leq\dim N,\ |\beta|\leq k\right\},

which represents the coefficients of the truncated Taylor polynomial of
f
at
x
.
The functoriality of
J^{k}
expresses the compatibility of these derivatives with composition:
if
f:M\to N
and
g:N\to P
, then in local coordinates

\frac{\partial^{|\gamma|}(g^{\lambda}\circ f)}{\partial x^{\gamma}}(x)=F_{\gamma}\!\left(\frac{\partial^{|\beta|}f^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{\beta}}(x),\frac{\partial^{|\delta|}g^{\lambda}}{\partial y^{\delta}}(f(x))\right),

where
F_{\gamma}
is the universal polynomial given by the multivariate chain rule
(Faà di Bruno formula,
[
3
]
).
Thus,
J^{k}
packages all partial derivatives of order
\leq k
into a coordinate-free object
whose transformation law under composition is precisely the functorial identity above.

3.5
Weil algebras and Weil functors

A
Weil algebra

W

[
8
,
9
]
is a finite-dimensional local
\mathbb{R}
-algebra with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
such that
\mathfrak{m}^{k+1}=0
for some
k
. Examples:

\mathbb{R}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{2}),\quad\mathbb{R}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{k+1}),\quad\bigotimes_{j=1}^{p}\mathbb{R}[\varepsilon_{j}]/(\varepsilon_{j}^{\rho_{j}+1}).

The
Weil functor

T_{W}
assigns to each manifold
M
the space
T_{W}M
of
W
-points of
M
, and to each smooth map
f:M\to N
the lifted map
T_{W}f:T_{W}M\to T_{W}N
. Evaluating
f
on
T_{W}
propagates truncated Taylor expansions coefficientwise, enabling higher-order AD in one pass.

3.6
AD primitives and their geometric meaning

For
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}^{m}
at
x
, the
pushforward

df_{x}
corresponds to the JVP
v\mapsto J_{f}(x)\,v
, and the
cotangent pullback

(df_{x})^{*}
corresponds to the VJP
\omega\mapsto J_{f}(x)^{\top}\omega
. We use the latter in Theorem
1
and in the respective experiments to realize reverse–mode as cotangent pullback. Functorially, these correspond to
df_{x}
and
(df_{x})^{*}
, respectively. Higher-order AD uses jet composition or Weil functor evaluation to propagate derivatives up to order
k
.

4
Main Results

Theorem 1

(Backpropagation as cotangent pullback)
.

Let
f:M\to N
and
\ell:N\to\mathbb{R}
be smooth maps between manifolds. For every
x\in M
,

\nabla_{x}(\ell\circ f)=(df_{x})^{*}(d\ell_{f(x)}).

Equivalently,
(j^{1}f)^{*}(j^{1}\ell)=j^{1}(\ell\circ f)
on covelocities.

Proof.

Coordinate-free formulation.

The identity above is intrinsic: it depends only on the cotangent pullback
(df_{x})^{*}
and not on any choice of coordinates. For smooth maps
f:M\to N
and
\ell:N\to\mathbb{R}
, the chain rule implies

d(\ell\circ f)_{x}=(df_{x})^{*}(d\ell_{f(x)}).

This expresses reverse-mode AD as the pullback of covectors along
f
. In Euclidean coordinates (or when covectors are identified with vectors via an inner product), this becomes the familiar backpropagation rule

\nabla_{x}(\ell\circ f)=J_{f}(x)^{\top}\nabla\ell(f(x)).

Coordinate-based formulation using local charts.

Let
(U,\varphi)
be a chart on
M
around
x
with coordinates
\varphi(x)=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
, and
(V,\psi)
a chart on
N
around
f(x)
with coordinates
\psi(f(x))=(y^{1},\dots,y^{m})
. In these coordinates, the differential

df_{x}:T_{x}M\to T_{f(x)}N

is represented by the Jacobian matrix

J_{f}(x)=\left[\frac{\partial(\psi^{i}\circ f\circ\varphi^{-1})}{\partial x^{j}}\right]_{i=1,\dots,m}^{j=1,\dots,n}.

Similarly,
d\ell_{f(x)}
corresponds to the gradient vector

\nabla\ell(f(x))=\left(\frac{\partial(\ell\circ\psi^{-1})}{\partial y^{i}}(f(x))\right)_{i=1,\dots,m}.

The cotangent pullback
(df_{x})^{*}
acts by matrix transpose in these coordinates, so

(df_{x})^{*}(d\ell_{f(x)})\quad\text{is represented by}\quad J_{f}(x)^{\top}\nabla\ell(f(x)).

This recovers the usual backpropagation formula in local coordinates
[
4
,
6
]
.
To make the jet-level equivalence precise, recall the canonical identification of first jets of
real-valued functions with values and covelocities:

\Theta_{M}:\;J^{1}_{x}(M,\mathbb{R})\;\xrightarrow{\;\cong\;}\;\mathbb{R}\oplus T_{x}^{*}M,\qquad\Theta_{M}\big(j^{1}_{x}u\big)\;=\;\big(u(x),\,du_{x}\big).

This is natural in
M
, and similarly for
N
. For
\ell:N\to\mathbb{R}
and
f:M\to N
we have the
jet functoriality (naturality)

J^{1}(\ell\circ f)\;=\;J^{1}\ell\circ J^{1}f.

Evaluating at
x\in M
and unpacking
\Theta
on source and target gives

\Theta_{M}\!\big(j^{1}_{x}(\ell\circ f)\big)\;=\;\big(\ell(f(x)),\,d(\ell\circ f)_{x}\big),\qquad\Theta_{N}\!\big(j^{1}_{f(x)}\ell\big)\;=\;\big(\ell(f(x)),\,d\ell_{f(x)}\big).

The map
J^{1}f
at the level of scalar jets preserves the value component and acts on the
covelocity component by cotangent pullback; concretely, under the above identifications,

\Theta_{M}\circ J^{1}f\circ\Theta_{N}^{-1}\;=\;\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{R}}\;\oplus\;(df_{x})^{*}:\ \ \mathbb{R}\oplus T^{*}_{f(x)}N\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\oplus T^{*}_{x}M.

Applying this to
j^{1}_{f(x)}\ell
yields

\Theta_{M}\!\Big((J^{1}f)\big(j^{1}_{f(x)}\ell\big)\Big)\;=\;\Big(\ell(f(x)),\;(df_{x})^{*}(d\ell_{f(x)})\Big).

Comparing with
\Theta_{M}\!\big(j^{1}_{x}(\ell\circ f)\big)=(\ell(f(x)),\,d(\ell\circ f)_{x})
, we obtain
equality of both the value and covelocity components. Pushing this equality back through

\Theta_{M}
gives

(J^{1}f)\big(j^{1}_{f(x)}\ell\big)\;=\;j^{1}_{x}(\ell\circ f),

and, in particular, on covelocities

(j^{1}f)^{*}(j^{1}\ell)\;=\;j^{1}(\ell\circ f).

Under local coordinates, this reduces exactly to the already-derived formula

d(\ell\circ f)_{x}=(df_{x})^{*}(d\ell_{f(x)})
, since
j^{1}_{f(x)}\ell
corresponds to the pair

\big(\ell(f(x)),\nabla\ell(f(x))\big)
and
(J^{1}f)
acts as

\operatorname{id}\oplus J_{f}(x)^{\top}
on
\mathbb{R}\oplus T^{*}_{f(x)}N
, recovering

\nabla_{x}(\ell\circ f)=J_{f}(x)^{\top}\nabla\ell(f(x))
.
∎

Lemma 1

(Backward stability for reverse sweeps)
.

Consider a straight–line program
f
with primitives
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
and reverse rules
\varphi_{i}^{*}
.
Assume each
\varphi_{i}^{*}
satisfies:

\|\varphi_{i}^{*}(v)\|\leq L_{i}\|v\|,\qquad\text{and computed }\widehat{\varphi_{i}^{*}}(v)\text{ obeys }\|\widehat{\varphi_{i}^{*}}(v)-\varphi_{i}^{*}(v)\|\leq\delta_{i}\|\varphi_{i}^{*}(v)\|,

where
L_{i}
is the adjoint–Lipschitz constant and
\delta_{i}=O(u\,\kappa(\varphi_{i}))
for machine precision
u
and condition number
\kappa(\varphi_{i})
.

Then for any seed covector
\bar{y}\in T^{*}_{f(x)}N
, the computed pullback
\widehat{f^{*}}(\bar{y})
satisfies:

\|\widehat{f^{*}}(\bar{y})\|\leq\Bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{L}(1+\delta_{i})L_{i}\Bigg)\|\bar{y}\|.

Proof.

First, the exact pullback is:

f^{*}(\bar{y})=\varphi_{1}^{*}\circ\varphi_{2}^{*}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{L}^{*}(\bar{y}),

and by Lipschitz bounds:

\|f^{*}(\bar{y})\|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{L}L_{i}\|\bar{y}\|.

Next, let
\widehat{\varphi_{i}^{*}}
denote the computed adjoint. Then for each step:

\|\widehat{\varphi_{i}^{*}}(v)\|\leq(1+\delta_{i})\|\varphi_{i}^{*}(v)\|\leq(1+\delta_{i})L_{i}\|v\|.

We apply the above multiplicatively:

\|\widehat{f^{*}}(\bar{y})\|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{L}(1+\delta_{i})L_{i}\|\bar{y}\|.

Now, since
\delta_{i}=O(u\,\kappa(\varphi_{i}))
and
u\ll 1
,

\prod_{i=1}^{L}(1+\delta_{i})\approx 1+\sum_{i=1}^{L}\delta_{i}+O(u^{2}),

so the relative error grows linearly with
\sum\kappa(\varphi_{i})
to first order.
∎

Theorem 2

(Exactness of Weil-mode evaluation)
.

Let
W
be a Weil algebra with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
satisfying
\mathfrak{m}^{k+1}=0
. For any smooth map
f\in C^{k+1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
x\in U
, the lifted map

T_{W}f:T_{W}U\longrightarrow T_{W}\mathbb{R}^{m}

computes all derivatives of
f
up to order
k
at
x
exactly as the coefficients of the truncated Taylor expansion.

Proof.

Since
W
is a local
\mathbb{R}
-algebra with
\mathfrak{m}^{k+1}=0
, every element
w\in W
can be written as

w=a_{0}+\sum_{|\alpha|\geq 1}^{|\alpha|\leq k}a_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha},\quad\varepsilon^{\alpha}=\varepsilon_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\varepsilon_{r}^{\alpha_{r}},

where
\varepsilon_{i}\in\mathfrak{m}
are nilpotent generators and
a_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}
. Nilpotency ensures
\varepsilon^{\alpha}=0
whenever
|\alpha|>k
. Each point of
T_{W}U
corresponds to a morphism
\text{Spec}(W)\to U
, which in local coordinates
(x^{1},\dots,x^{n})
is represented by

x_{W}=x+\sum_{|\alpha|\geq 1}^{|\alpha|\leq k}h_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha},\quad h_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This encodes a formal Taylor expansion truncated at order
k
. Applying
f
to
x_{W}
and expanding in a multivariate Taylor series gives:

f(x_{W})=f(x)+\sum_{|\alpha|=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha!}D^{\alpha}f(x)[h_{\alpha}]\varepsilon^{\alpha},

where
D^{\alpha}f(x)
denotes the
\alpha
-th derivative (multilinear map) of
f
at
x
. Because
\varepsilon^{\alpha}
vanish for
|\alpha|>k
, the series truncates exactly at order
k
. The coefficients of
\varepsilon^{\alpha}
in
f(x_{W})
are precisely the derivatives:

\text{Coefficient of }\varepsilon^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\alpha!}D^{\alpha}f(x)[h_{\alpha}].

Thus,
T_{W}f
computes
(f(x),Df(x),\dots,D^{k}f(x))
algebraically, without numerical approximation or step-size tuning.
Floating-point error propagates only through coefficient arithmetic, not through truncation, since truncation is enforced by
\mathfrak{m}^{k+1}=0
.
∎

Corollary 1

(Coefficient growth envelope)
.

Assume
f\in C^{k+1}(B_{r}(x),\mathbb{R}^{m})
and that its derivatives satisfy

\|D^{\ell}f(z)\|\leq M_{\ell}\quad\text{for all }z\in B_{r}(x),\;0\leq\ell\leq k+1.

Then the Taylor coefficients of
T_{W}f
at
x
obey

\|f_{\alpha}(x)\|\leq\frac{M_{|\alpha|}}{\alpha!},\qquad\text{for all multi-indices }\alpha\text{ with }|\alpha|\leq k,

and the truncated series is numerically stable for any step size
\rho<r
with explicit tail bounds.

Proof.

For
z\in B_{r}(x)
, write the Taylor expansion of
f
at
x
:

f(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\sum_{|\alpha|=\ell}\frac{1}{\alpha!}D^{\alpha}f(x)[(z-x)^{\alpha}]+R_{k+1}(z),

where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
,
\alpha!=\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{n}!
, and
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}
.
When evaluating
f
on
x_{W}=x+\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}h_{j}
in
T_{W}
, the coefficient of
\varepsilon^{\alpha}
in
f(x_{W})
is:

f_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha!}D^{\alpha}f(x)[h_{1}^{\alpha_{1}},\dots,h_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}],

where
h_{j}
are the seeded directions. For norm bounds, assume
\|h_{j}\|\leq 1
(unit directions).
By assumption,
\|D^{\alpha}f(x)\|\leq M_{|\alpha|}
for all
|\alpha|\leq k
. Thus:

\|f_{\alpha}(x)\|\leq\frac{M_{|\alpha|}}{\alpha!}\cdot\|h_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\|\cdots\|h_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\|\leq\frac{M_{|\alpha|}}{\alpha!}.

For any
\rho<r
, the remainder term satisfies the standard Cauchy estimate:

\|R_{k+1}(z)\|\leq\frac{M_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\|z-x\|^{k+1}\leq\frac{M_{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\rho^{k+1}.

Thus the truncated series is stable for
\rho<r
with explicit control on the tail.
Since truncation is enforced algebraically by
\mathfrak{m}^{k+1}=0
, floating-point error propagates only through coefficient arithmetic, not through step-size selection.
∎

Theorem 3

(Complexity of Tensorized Weil Algebras)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be
C^{k}
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Fix
p
input directions
v^{(1)},\dots,v^{(p)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and per–direction truncation orders
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{p}\in\mathbb{N}
with
\rho_{j}\leq k
. Consider the tensorized Weil algebra

W\;\cong\;\bigotimes_{j=1}^{p}\,\mathbb{R}[\varepsilon_{j}]/(\varepsilon_{j}^{\rho_{j}+1}),\qquad\dim W\;=\;\prod_{j=1}^{p}(\rho_{j}+1),

with commuting nilpotents
\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}=\varepsilon_{j}\varepsilon_{i}
and
\varepsilon_{j}^{\rho_{j}+1}=0
. A single evaluation of
f
on
T_{W}
at the
W
–point

x_{W}\;:=\;x\;+\;\sum_{j=1}^{p}\varepsilon_{j}\,v^{(j)}

produces
exactly
all mixed directional derivatives of order
\leq k
along the chosen directions, as the coefficients of the basis monomials
\varepsilon^{\alpha}:=\varepsilon_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\varepsilon_{p}^{\alpha_{p}}
for all multi–indices
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{p}
with
0\leq\alpha_{j}\leq\rho_{j}
. Moreover, if the primal straight–line program for
f
uses
Q
scalar primitives, then the lifted evaluation uses
O(\dim W)
coefficient arithmetic per primitive (hence time
O(\dim W)\cdot Q
and memory
O(\dim W)
per intermediate, with no adjoint tape).

Proof.

By construction

W\;\cong\;\mathbb{R}[\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{p}]\big/\bigl(\varepsilon_{1}^{\rho_{1}+1},\dots,\varepsilon_{p}^{\rho_{p}+1}\bigr),

with commuting variables
\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}=\varepsilon_{j}\varepsilon_{i}
. Every
w\in W
admits a unique expansion

w\;=\;\sum_{0\leq\alpha_{j}\leq\rho_{j}}\;c_{\alpha}\,\varepsilon^{\alpha},\qquad\varepsilon^{\alpha}:=\varepsilon_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\varepsilon_{p}^{\alpha_{p}},\quad c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R},

and the set
\{\varepsilon^{\alpha}:0\leq\alpha_{j}\leq\rho_{j}\}
is an
\mathbb{R}
–basis of
W
, hence
\dim W=\prod_{j=1}^{p}(\rho_{j}+1)
.
Define

x_{W}\;=\;x\;+\;\sum_{j=1}^{p}\varepsilon_{j}\,v^{(j)}\;\in\;(\mathbb{R}^{n})\otimes W\;\cong\;T_{W}\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In coordinates,
x_{W}
has coefficient array supported on degree
0
(the basepoint
x
) and degree
1
(the first–order seeds
v^{(j)}
), with no higher–degree seeds required; powers of the
\varepsilon_{j}
generated inside
W
will encode higher orders automatically.
Let
D^{\ell}f(x):(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
denote the symmetric
\ell
–linear differential. Using the multivariate Taylor formula up to total order
k
and nilpotency to truncate:

\displaystyle f(x_{W})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{1}{\ell!}\,D^{\ell}f(x)\!\big[\underbrace{(x_{W}-x),\dots,(x_{W}-x)}_{\ell\text{ times}}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{1}{\ell!}\,D^{\ell}f(x)\!\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{p}\varepsilon_{j}v^{(j)},\,\dots,\,\sum_{j=1}^{p}\varepsilon_{j}v^{(j)}\bigg].

Expanding the
\ell
–fold multilinear form and using commutativity of the
\varepsilon_{j}
yields

\displaystyle f(x_{W})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{1}{\ell!}\,\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell}=1}^{p}\Big(\varepsilon_{j_{1}}\cdots\varepsilon_{j_{\ell}}\Big)\,D^{\ell}f(x)\!\big[v^{(j_{1})},\dots,v^{(j_{\ell})}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{p}\\
|\alpha|=\ell\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\ell!}\cdot\frac{\ell!}{\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{p}!}\;\varepsilon^{\alpha}\,D^{\ell}f(x)\!\big[\underbrace{v^{(1)},\dots,v^{(1)}}_{\alpha_{1}},\dots,\underbrace{v^{(p)},\dots,v^{(p)}}_{\alpha_{p}}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq\alpha_{j}\leq\rho_{j}}\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{p}!}\;\varepsilon^{\alpha}\,D^{|\alpha|}f(x)\!\big[v^{(1)}{}^{\times\alpha_{1}},\dots,v^{(p)}{}^{\times\alpha_{p}}\big],

where
|\alpha|:=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{p}
, and we used that each
(\varepsilon_{j})^{\rho_{j}+1}=0
kills all terms with
\alpha_{j}>\rho_{j}
.
For each multi–index
\alpha
with
0\leq\alpha_{j}\leq\rho_{j}
and
|\alpha|\leq k
, the coefficient of the basis monomial
\varepsilon^{\alpha}
in
f(x_{W})
is exactly

\boxed{\quad\mathrm{Coeff}_{\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\big(f(x_{W})\big)\;=\;\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{p}!}\;D^{|\alpha|}f(x)\!\big[v^{(1)}{}^{\times\alpha_{1}},\dots,v^{(p)}{}^{\times\alpha_{p}}\big].\quad}

Thus the single evaluation
f(x_{W})
produces, in closed form,
all
mixed directional derivatives of orders
\leq k
along the chosen directions (restricted by the per–direction caps
\rho_{j}
). No step sizes, limits, or numerical differencing are involved; exactness is enforced
algebraically
by nilpotency.
Let the primal straight–line program for
f
use
Q
scalar primitives (additions, multiplies, smooth elementwise functions, linear algebra kernels, etc.). In the
W
–lift:
- Each scalar variable becomes a coefficient vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\dim W}
;
- Each primitive
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{a}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}^{b}
lifts to
\tilde{\phi}:W^{a}\!\to\!W^{b}
by coefficientwise truncated polynomial arithmetic. Hence the cost per primitive scales by a constant factor times
\dim W
, so total time is

T_{W}(f)\;\leq\;c_{1}\,\dim W\cdot Q,

for a constant
c_{1}
depending on primitive arities and the chosen dense representation of coefficient arrays. Memory per intermediate variable is
O(\dim W)
, and there is
no reverse tape
, as this is a single forward evaluation in
W
.
To obtain all mixed directional derivatives of order
\leq k
restricted to the
p
directions, one needs as many independent coefficients as the number of admissible monomials in
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{p}
of degree
\leq k
with per–direction caps
\rho_{j}
, namely

\#\{\alpha:0\leq\alpha_{j}\leq\rho_{j}\}\;=\;\prod_{j=1}^{p}(\rho_{j}+1)\;=\;\dim W.

Any schedule built from
first–order
JVP/VJP evaluations produces, per pass, at most a rank–one sample of the symmetric multilinear forms
D^{\ell}f(x)
restricted to
\mathrm{span}\{v^{(1)},\dots,v^{(p)}\}
; recovering all coefficients therefore requires, in the worst case, at least on the order of the number of distinct monomials (or solves in a Vandermonde/polarization system for each
\ell
), i.e.

\textstyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\binom{p+\ell-1}{\ell}\;=\;\binom{p+k}{k}\quad\text{(when all $\rho_{j}\equiv k$),}

and more generally at least
\prod_{j=1}^{p}(\rho_{j}+1)
samples subject to per–direction caps. Thus nested first–order schedules incur work growing with the
combinatorics
of mixed partials. In reverse–over–reverse constructions, storing activations for each level across program depth
L
induces an adjoint tape of size
\Omega(L)
, or else one must pay recomputation via checkpointing; in contrast, the
W
–mode forward evaluation stores only the current coefficient arrays (no tape)
[
1
]
.
Combining the above, the tensorized Weil algebra yields
one–pass
exact computation of all packed mixed derivatives with time and memory linear in
\dim W=\prod_{j=1}^{p}(\rho_{j}+1)
, while any nested first–order schedule must, in the worst case, pay at least the combinatorial count of monomials (and a reverse tape if using higher–order reverse nesting). This proves the claim.
∎

5
Discussion

This section interprets our results in the context of automatic differentiation (AD) theory and practice, situates them within the broader literature on category theory and computational geometry, and distills practical guidance for deep learning optimization and AD system design.

Why a geometric formulation matters.

Theorem 3.1 elevates reverse–mode AD from an algorithmic recipe to a canonical construction in differential geometry. In particular, gradients arise as
pullbacks of covelocities
along compositions, implying invariance under smooth reparametrizations. This invariance ensures that reparametrizing a model (e.g., switching charts on a manifold, rescaling coordinates, or composing with smooth bijections) preserves the semantics of reverse sweeps. In optimization, these properties underpin robust behavior under data/parameter preprocessing and are essential for intrinsic methods on manifolds and symmetry–aware architectures
[
4
,
6
]
.

Exactness and stability in higher order.

Theorem 3.3 identifies Taylor–mode as
evaluation in a Weil algebra
. Nilpotency enforces truncation
algebraically
, so the truncated Taylor jet is computed exactly (up to floating–point arithmetic on coefficients) without step–size tuning or differencing error. Corollary 3.4 (and its variant in Corollary 3.5) further clarifies how derivative bounds over a ball imply coefficient envelopes and explicit control of truncation tails. Together with Lemma 3.2 (backward stability), these results give a clean separation of concerns: (i) correctness is guaranteed by functoriality and nilpotency; (ii) numerical stability is governed by local Lipschitz/conditioning constants of primitives and by coefficient growth, both of which can be monitored or regularized
[
8
,
9
,
7
]
.

Connections to existing AD frameworks and categorical views.

The cotangent–pullback identity matches the compositional accounts of reverse–mode where backprop is a functor acting contravariantly on morphisms. This perspective recovers the multivariate Faà di Bruno structure inside
J^{k}
, explaining why higher–order rules compose via universal polynomial laws
[
3
]
. Practically, this situates modern AD systems
[
1
]
—-which expose
JVP
/
VJP
and higher–order operators—inside a geometric envelope that makes invariance, associativity, and rule lifting (for linear algebra and special functions) transparent.

Implications for deep learning optimization.

We emphasize three concrete consequences.

•

Chart invariance.
Gradients computed by reverse–mode agree across equivalent coordinate systems, enabling stable optimization on parameter manifolds, normalizing flows with smooth reparametrizations, and in vision models using intrinsic coordinates.

•

Large–scale stability accounting.
Lemma 3.2 yields a multiplicative bound for reverse sweeps via adjoint–Lipschitz constants
L_{i}
and relative errors
\delta_{i}
. This provides an actionable diagnostic: the product
\prod_{i}L_{i}
explains when gradient backpropagation amplifies perturbations and where to insert normalization or rescaling.

•

Higher–order efficiency.
For curvature–aware training (e.g., Newton/Krylov, meta–learning, implicit differentiation), Theorem
2
ensures exact coefficient propagation, while Theorem
3
shows that one pass over a tensorized Weil algebra computes all requested mixed partials with
linear
time and memory in
\dim W
and no adjoint tape. This replaces exponential blow–ups from nested first–order schedules by predictable algebraic scaling
[
2
]
.

Trade–offs and design choices.

The geometric picture suggests practical selection rules.

•

Reverse vs. forward (first order).
Use reverse–mode for scalar losses with many parameters (classic deep nets). Prefer forward–mode when the input dimension is small or for Jacobian–vector probes.

•

Weil/Taylor–mode vs. nesting (higher order).
For dense requests of mixed derivatives along
p
directions up to order
k
, tensorized Weil algebras win: a single evaluation costs
\mathcal{O}(\dim W)
with
\dim W=\prod_{j=1}^{p}(\rho_{j}+1)
and avoids tapes/recomputation. If only a few directional derivatives are needed, or if structure is extremely sparse, carefully scheduled JVP/VJP can still be competitive.

•

Memory vs. recomputation.
Higher–order reverse–over–reverse incurs tape or checkpointing; Weil/Taylor–mode stores only coefficient arrays. On accelerators, coefficientwise fusion can make the latter particularly attractive.

Future research directions.

•

Intrinsic AD on manifolds.
Extend stability bounds using connections and curvature; integrate parallel transport into reverse sweeps for geometric deep learning and robotics.

•

PDE–constrained and variational settings.
Exploit jet prolongations and the variational bicomplex to differentiate through PDE solvers and Lagrangian field models while preserving Euler–Lagrange structure.

•

Compression for high–order tensors.
Develop coefficient–array compression (CP/TT/low–rank formats), sparsity discovery, and block–structured Weil algebras to control
\dim W
in large programs.

•

Rule lifting for primitives.
Systematically lift linear algebra and special functions to Weil algebras with proven adjoint rules, yielding portable high–order kernels.
