Take a deep breath: managing the challenges of acute respiratory distress syndrome cases
Authors: Manuel Castillo-Angeles, Ali Salim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAcute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) remains a major challenge in trauma care, with high morbidity and mortality. Current management emphasizes lung-protective ventilation, careful fluid balance, and a multidisciplinary approach. New areas of discovery, such as ARDS phenotyping, precision medicine, ECMO innovations, and evolving adjunctive therapies, are reshaping practice and offering opportunities for improved outcomes. This comprehensive review highlights the current understanding of ARDS and management essentials and underscores the importance of emerging therapies that offer the clearest path to further reduce ARDS morbidity and mortality.Keywords: Respiratory Distress Syndrome, Adult; treatment outcome; airway management

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) was first recognized as a distinct clinical entity in 1967 and has since undergone successive, evidence-driven redefinitions, culminating in the 2012 Berlin criteria, which improved diagnostic reliability by specifying timing, imaging, origin of edema, and graded hypoxemia thresholds that better predict outcomes.

The syndrome now called ARDS was originally described by Ashbaugh and colleagues in 1967 as a constellation of acute onset respiratory failure with severe hypoxemia, decreased lung compliance, and bilateral radiographic infiltrates that were refractory to oxygen therapy; that seminal Lancet report framed ARDS as a recognizable clinical-pathologic pattern rather than a single disease, and it stimulated decades of observational and interventional research.1 Over the next quarter century, clinicians and investigators sought a reproducible working definition to enable epidemiologic study and clinical trials. The American-European Consensus Conference (AECC) provided the first widely adopted operational definition, specifying acute onset, PaO₂/FiO₂ thresholds (with or without positive end-expiratory pressure), bilateral infiltrates on chest radiograph, and the absence of left atrial hypertension as the primary cause of edema; this definition standardized case identification but later proved limited by variable interobserver reliability for chest radiographs, inconsistent application of the cardiac failure exclusion, and imperfect prognostic discrimination.2

Recognition of those limitations, together with accumulating multicenter data, led to a formal international effort to revise the definition. In 2012, the Berlin definition (table 1), developed by an expert task force and validated in large datasets, retained the core clinical features but introduced four key refinements1: a clear time window (within 1 week of a known clinical insult or new/worsening respiratory symptoms);2 objective imaging criteria (bilateral opacities not fully explained by effusions, lobar/lung collapse, or nodules);3 exclusion of cardiac failure or fluid overload as the primary cause;4 and three severity categories based on PaO₂/FiO₂ on a minimum level of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) (mild 200–300, moderate 100–200, severe ≤100 mm Hg). These changes improved predictive validity for mortality and ventilator-free days and enhanced consistency across centers; subsequent explanatory and validation analyses (including the Berlin rationale and supplementary reports) and large epidemiologic studies have confirmed better prognostic stratification compared with the AECC criteria while also highlighting remaining challenges, such as variability in imaging interpretation and the question of whether noninvasively supported patients (eg, highflow nasal oxygen) should be incorporated into future revisions.2,5

Epidemiology of ARDS and mortality

Worldwide, ARDS affects approximately 3 million patients annually, with over 200 000 in the USA. The LUNGSAFE study demonstrated that ARDS occurs in a substantial proportion of patients (10.4%) with respiratory failure in the intensive care unit (ICU), with an overall prevalence among patients receiving mechanical ventilation that is clinically meaningful across regions and income settings; importantly, the study documented that ARDS is often missed by clinicians at the time of onset, contributing to variability in care and outcomes.6 7 The LUNGSAFE investigators reported wide geographic and institutional variation in recognition, use of lung-protective ventilation, and adjunctive therapies, findings that help explain the heterogeneity in reported incidence across prior single-center and regional series.6

Mortality from ARDS remains substantial but is closely tied to severity and comorbid illness: hospital and 28 to 60-day mortality increase markedly from mild to severe ARDS (range, 16.7%–73.3%), and contemporary multicenter data show that while overall mortality has declined compared with historical cohorts, severe ARDS continues to carry high case fatality.7,9 The LUNGSAFE cohort quantified outcomes across the Berlin severity strata and found that patients meeting criteria for moderate and severe ARDS had progressively fewer ventilator-free days and higher in-hospital mortality than those with mild disease; the study also highlighted that underuse of evidence-based interventions (eg, low tidal-volume ventilation and prone positioning) was associated with worse outcomes in many centers, suggesting that implementation gaps, rather than immutable disease biology alone, account for a portion of contemporary mortality.6 Taken together, these epidemiologic findings argue for systematic ARDS screening, early application of lung-protective strategies, and regional systems to ensure access to advanced therapies for refractory cases.

Prevention of ARDS

Prevention of ARDS centers on avoiding iatrogenic lung injury, controlling the inciting insult, and applying targeted perioperative and critical-care measures that reduce progression from at-risk states to full-blown respiratory failure. Early application of lung-protective ventilation (limiting tidal volumes and pressures) in all ventilated patients at risk for lung injury, including during the intraoperative period, reduces the likelihood of ventilator-associated lung injury and subsequent ARDS; perioperative trials support low tidal-volume strategies to lower postoperative pulmonary complications.10 After initial resuscitation for shock or sepsis, a conservative fluid management approach decreases pulmonary edema and shortens ventilator duration, thereby lowering ARDS burden in critically ill cohorts.11 Prevention also requires aggressive source control and early antimicrobial therapy for pneumonia and intra-abdominal infections, strict aspiration-precaution protocols in at-risk surgical patients, and restrictive transfusion practices when feasible to limit transfusion-related lung injury. Unit-level measures, such as routine ARDS screening, standardized ventilator bundles, staff training in lung-protective strategies, and infection-control bundles, address the documented problem of under-recognition and variable practice that contributes to preventable cases.11,13

Several pharmacologic agents have been tested in an effort to prevent ARDS in at-risk patients, but none have demonstrated clinical benefit in large, well-designed trials. In 2016, a multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluated the efficacy of aspirin in preventing ARDS in high-risk patients but found no significant difference in ARDS incidence (adjusted OR (aOR), 1.24; 95% CI 0.67 to 2.31).14 Even though preclinical studies showed promising results of corticosteroids for prevention of ARDS, two early randomized trials evaluating intravenous methylprednisolone in critically ill patients at high risk for acute lung injury demonstrated no reduction in progression to ARDS.15 16 Similarly, other agents, including ketoconazole, dipyridamole, and aerosolized β2-agonists, have similarly failed to demonstrate efficacy in preventing ARDS in at-risk cohorts.17,19 Collectively, these findings underscore that no pharmacologic therapy has yet been shown to prevent ARDS, and current prevention strategies remain focused on evidence-based supportive measures.

When assessing prognostic factors for the development of ARDS following traumatic injury, Tran et al identified the amount of crystalloid resuscitation within the first 24 hours of injury as a modifiable risk factor (aOR, 1.19; 95% CI 1.15 to 1.24 for each additional liter of crystalloid administered within the first 6 hours after injury). This systematic review also identified nonmodifiable prognostic factors with a moderate or high certainty of association with posttraumatic ARDS: increasing age, non-Hispanic White race, blunt mechanism of injury, presence of head injury, pulmonary contusion or rib fracture, and increasing chest injury severity.20

Non-invasive modalities of oxygen support

Non-invasive modalities, including high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) and non-invasive positive-pressure ventilation (NIV) delivered by face mask or helmet, are physiologically attractive because they can improve oxygenation, reduce inspiratory effort, and avoid complications of intubation in some patients. Randomized and observational data in acute hypoxemic respiratory failure show that HFNC may reduce intubation and mortality compared with standard oxygen or NIV in selected cohorts, and it is generally better tolerated.21 Helmet NIV has shown promise in ARDS by improving tolerance and reducing intubation compared with facemask NIV in a randomized trial,22 suggesting that the interface choice materially affects success. Even though non-invasive modalities are frequently used in the management of ARDS, most studies have reported the outcomes with these are highly variable, and that patient selection and close monitoring are the main determinants of its success and whether noninvasive therapy prevents intubation or merely delays necessary invasive support.23 24 Clinicians should therefore reserve HFNC/NIV for hemodynamically stable patients with mild hypoxemia, low work of breathing, and reversible causes, and ensure continuous bedside observation, frequent reassessment of respiratory rate, oxygenation (PaO₂/FiO₂), and accessory muscle use, with predefined criteria for immediate intubation.

The principal risk of noninvasive support in ARDS is treatment failure with delayed intubation and patient self-inflicted lung injury from high inspiratory efforts, as experimental and clinical analyses highlight that vigorous spontaneous effort can worsen lung stress and progression of injury.25 For moderate and severe ARDS (Berlin moderate/severe), evidence and expert consensus favor early invasive mechanical ventilation with lung-protective settings rather than prolonged trials of NIV/HFNC, unless the patient is closely monitored in a setting able to perform rapid escalation.6 21 22 Practical safeguards when using non-invasive support include explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria, hourly bedside checks in the first 6–12 hours, objective failure triggers (worsening PaO₂/FiO₂, rising PaCO₂, increasing respiratory rate or accessory muscle use, hemodynamic instability), and a documented plan for prompt intubation if criteria are met. In selected subgroups (eg, some immunocompromised patients), carefully supervised HFNC or NIV can reduce intubation and improve outcomes, but in general, noninvasive strategies are a bridge for mild disease and a monitored trial—not a substitute for invasive, lung-protective ventilation in established ARDS.6 21 22 26

Mechanical ventilation

Mechanical ventilation for ARDS must prioritize lung protection: low tidal volumes based on predicted body weight, strict limits on plateau pressure, individualized PEEP to minimize cyclic atelectasis and overdistension, and continuous reassessment of driving pressure and recruitability to reduce ventilator-induced lung injury and improve survival.

ARDS management via mechanical ventilation begins with the lung-protective strategy established by the ARDS network: set tidal volume to ≈6 mL/kg predicted body weight (PBW), target plateau pressure ≤ 30 cm H₂O, and accept permissive hypercapnia when necessary to avoid injurious pressures; this approach reduced mortality and ventilator days in the well-known landmark randomized trial.27 At the bedside, this requires accurate PBW calculation, early adjustment of ventilator settings when oxygenation or mechanics change, and routine measurement of plateau pressure and static compliance to guide safe limits.28

PEEP and recruitment maneuvers are the next major domain of ventilator management. Trials comparing higher vs lower PEEP strategies in the context of low tidal-volume ventilation showed improved oxygenation but inconsistent mortality benefit, underscoring the need to individualize PEEP based on oxygenation response, respiratory mechanics, and hemodynamic tolerance rather than a one-size-fits-all table.7 27 29 30 Practical bedside methods include incremental PEEP trials with monitoring of compliance and oxygenation, use of driving pressure as a guide to overdistension, and cautious recruitment maneuvers in patients with high recruitability while avoiding sustained high pressures in poorly recruitable lungs.

The concept of driving pressure (Δp=plateau pressure − PEEP) has emerged as a key physiologic target that integrates tidal volume and respiratory system compliance; observational and pooled analyses suggest that lower driving pressures are more strongly associated with survival than tidal volume or PEEP alone, making ΔP a useful real-time metric to titrate tidal volume and PEEP to the patient’s functional lung size.1227 31,33 Clinicians should calculate driving pressure after each ventilator change and prioritize adjustments that reduce ΔP (eg, lowering tidal volume or increasing PEEP when compliance improves) while maintaining safe plateau pressures.

Additionally, two recent systematic review/meta-analyses of RCTs showed that airway pressure release ventilation was associated with improved oxygenation, mortality benefit, and increased number of ventilator-free days when compared with conventional ventilation, with no increased risk of barotrauma or worse hemodynamics.34 35 However, these data are limited as they included five RCTs with small sample size, consistent with low-quality evidence and moderate heterogeneity.

With respect to high-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV), two large multicenter RCTs confirmed no benefit, or even possible harm: The Oscillation in ARDS trial in the UK36 (higher mortality: RR, 1.41; 95% CI 1.21 to 1.79), and the Oscillation for Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome Treated Early trial in the USA.37 HFOV should not routinely be used in patients with moderate or severe ARDS. The potential use of HFOV as a rescue strategy for severe hypoxemia has been successful in some studies (eg, a meta-analysis suggested potential benefit in patients with very severe hypoxemia (PaO2/FIO2<64 mm Hg))38 and warrants further investigation.

Monitoring for ventilator-associated complications and tailoring weaning are integral to ventilator management. Regular assessment for barotrauma, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and ICU-acquired weakness is essential; sedation minimization, daily spontaneous breathing trials when oxygenation and mechanics permit, and early mobilization reduce ventilator days and long-term disability. In patients with persistent refractory hypoxemia despite optimized ventilator strategy and adjuncts, referral for extracorporeal support (ECMO) at experienced centers is considered, but such escalation should follow protocolized optimization of mechanical ventilation and adjunctive measures.

Finally, implementation matters: consistent application of lung-protective protocols, routine measurement of PBW and plateau pressure, use of driving pressure to guide adjustments, adjunctive therapies, and unit-level checklists markedly improve adherence to evidence-based ventilation and are associated with better outcomes. Embedding these practices into surgical and medical ICU workflows ensures that mechanical ventilation for ARDS is both protective and responsive to the patient’s evolving physiology.

Pharmacologic treatment

Pharmacologic therapy for ARDS remains largely supportive and adjunctive: no single drug is universally disease-modifying, but targeted pharmacologic interventions, notably corticosteroids in selected patients, short-course neuromuscular blockade for early severe ARDS, and selective use of inhaled pulmonary vasodilators or biologic/adjunctive agents in defined circumstances, can improve physiologic status and, in some trials, clinical outcomes when combined with lung-protective ventilation.

Because hypervolemia can cause pulmonary edema with resultant hypoxemia, conservative fluid management is needed. Randomized trials comparing liberal to conservative fluid management strategies in ARDS have confirmed better outcomes with conservative fluid management, including shortened duration of mechanical ventilation, and decreased ICU and hospital length of stay.39

Corticosteroids aim to blunt the dysregulated inflammatory response that drives alveolar injury. Multiple randomized trials and meta-analyses have evaluated different agents, doses, and timing; pooled analyses suggest early, prolonged, moderate-dose corticosteroids (eg, methylprednisolone or hydrocortisone regimens begun within days of ARDS onset) can reduce duration of mechanical ventilation and may reduce mortality in some cohorts, particularly when started early and tapered rather than given as a single short bolus.40 Steroid use requires careful patient selection (exclude uncontrolled infection, consider hyperglycemia risk) and protocolized dosing/tapering to limit ICU-acquired weakness and secondary infections.41

Paralysis with a short continuous infusion of a nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocker (commonly cisatracurium for 48 hours) was associated with improved oxygenation, more ventilator-free days, and lower adjusted mortality in an early randomized trial of severe ARDS, likely by improving ventilator synchrony and reducing patient-generated lung stress.27 A later large trial that compared routine early paralysis plus deep sedation with a lighter sedation usual-care strategy did not show a mortality benefit, highlighting that neuromuscular blockade should be reserved for selected patients with early severe ARDS, refractory asynchrony, or inability to achieve protective ventilation, and used with protocols to minimize sedation duration and monitor for weakness.42

Inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) and inhaled prostacyclin can transiently improve oxygenation by reducing intrapulmonary shunt and are useful as bridging therapies, while definitive measures (proning, optimization of PEEP, ECMO referral) are arranged. However, randomized data and meta-analyses show no consistent mortality benefit and potential renal or bleeding risks with routine use; therefore, inhaled vasodilators are best used selectively for severe, reversible hypoxemia or as a diagnostic tool to identify responders.43 44

Several pharmacologic strategies that showed promise in preclinical or early studies failed to improve outcomes in large, randomized trials. Beta2 agonists (intravenous salbutamol) increased harm in a multicenter trial and are not recommended (BALTI-2).45 Statins (simvastatin) did not improve primary outcomes in a large, randomized trial, though secondary analyses suggest possible benefit in biologic subphenotypes. High-dose vitamin C and other antioxidant or immunomodulatory regimens produced mixed results in randomized trials and remain investigational; routine use is not supported outside trials (CITRIS-ALI).46

Adjunctive therapies for ARDS

Adjunctive therapies to mechanical ventilation, prolonged prone positioning, extracorporeal carbon dioxide removal (ECCO₂R), and venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO), are used to improve oxygenation and permit lung-protective or ultraprotective ventilation in patients with moderate–severe ARDS. High-quality randomized trials support routine early proning for severe ARDS, while extracorporeal techniques are rescue or facilitation strategies whose benefits depend on patient selection and center experience.

Prone positioning improves oxygenation and lung mechanics by redistributing transpulmonary pressures, recruiting dorsal lung units, and reducing regional overdistension and cyclic atelectasis, thereby lowering ventilator-induced lung injury when used with low tidal-volume ventilation. The landmark randomized PROSEVA trial enrolled patients with severe ARDS (PaO₂/FiO₂ < 150 mm Hg on FiO₂ ≥ 0.6 and PEEP ≥ 5 cm H₂O) and applied early, prolonged prone sessions (median ≥ 16 hours/day), demonstrating a substantial reduction in 28- and 90 day mortality compared with supine care, establishing proning as a standard therapy for severe ARDS.47 Timing is critical: earlier initiation of prolonged proning, after confirming failure of optimized supine lung-protective ventilation, yields the greatest physiologic and survival benefit. PROSEVA’s protocol required a short period of optimization (including low tidal-volume ventilation and appropriate PEEP) followed by early randomization and proning, which contrasts with older trials that applied proning later or for shorter durations and failed to show benefit.47 Observational data and physiologic studies support that delayed or brief proning often produces only transient oxygenation gains without the sustained lung-protective effects seen with early, prolonged application.5 Clinically, teams should therefore screen ventilated patients with PaO₂/FiO₂ ≤150 for proning candidacy promptly, ensure contraindications (spinal instability, uncontrolled intracranial hypertension, recent major abdominal surgery) are considered, and proceed with a protocolized proning bundle that specifies session length, sedation, and neuromuscular blockade strategies when needed, and criteria for stopping or repeating sessions. When implemented in this way, proning is a high-value, low-cost adjunct that meaningfully reduces mortality in severe ARDS and should be integrated into ICU pathways with training, simulation, and audit to maintain safety and adherence.

Venovenous extracorporeal carbon dioxide removal (ECCO₂R) uses low-flow extracorporeal circuits to remove CO₂, enabling ultraprotective ventilation (very low tidal volumes and low driving pressures) in patients whose lung mechanics or hypercapnia otherwise limit safe ventilation. Physiologically, ECCO₂R reduces respiratory drive and the need for high minute ventilation, potentially lowering cyclic stretch and barotrauma; however, the evidence remains limited to physiologic studies, observational series, and small trials/reviews that show improved gas exchange and feasibility but inconclusive effects on mortality and a nontrivial complication profile (bleeding, circuit thrombosis, vascular injury).48 Current guidance frames ECCO₂R as a selective adjunct for patients with hypercapnic respiratory failure or to facilitate lung-protective strategies in centers with experience and protocols. Randomized outcome trials are ongoing, and careful risk-benefit assessment is required before use.

VV-ECMO provides full extracorporeal oxygenation and CO₂ removal and is indicated for refractory hypoxemia or severe respiratory acidosis despite optimized ventilation and adjuncts (including proning). Randomized and controlled data are nuanced: the CESAR trial suggested improved outcomes with referral to an ECMO center for severe respiratory failure, and the EOLIA trial did not show a statistically significant mortality benefit for early ECMO in its primary analysis but did demonstrate improved physiologic rescue and, in secondary or adjusted analyses and pooled meta-analyses, signals that ECMO can reduce mortality when used as rescue therapy in experienced centers.49 50 Key practical points are strict selection (reversible lung injury, limited comorbidity), early referral to high-volume ECMO centers, protocolized anticoagulation and complication surveillance, and a plan for lung-rest ventilation while on ECMO.

Emerging therapies, discoveries, and future research

Emerging therapies for ARDS are increasingly focused on biologic and cell-based interventions (notably mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC)), precision medicine approaches to match therapies to biologic subphenotypes and extracorporeal strategies that permit ultraprotective ventilation. Early-phase trials show safety and biologic signals for cell therapies, but larger, phenotype-guided randomized trials are needed to define clinical benefit and optimal patient selection.

MSC therapy is the most advanced biologic approach in clinical development for ARDS. MSC administration aims to modulate inflammation, enhance alveolar epithelial and endothelial repair, and reduce pulmonary edema through paracrine effects and extracellular vesicles, and early human studies have focused on safety, feasibility, and biologic activity rather than definitive mortality benefit.51 52 A randomized phase 2a safety trial (the START study) tested allogeneic MSCs in moderate/severe ARDS and demonstrated acceptable safety and signals of reduced biomarkers of lung injury, supporting further efficacy trials.53 Translational and preclinical work summarized in recent reviews have clarified mechanisms (immunomodulation, mitochondrial transfer, and secretion of reparative mediators); highlighted dose, timing, and route questions; and proposed strategies to enhance potency.51 52 54 These reports emphasize that MSC therapy is promising but remains investigational, with ongoing work needed to standardize cell products, dosing regimens, and endpoints for definitive trials.

Beyond MSCs, the near-term research agenda for ARDS emphasizes precision medicine, biomarker-driven trial design, and combination strategies. Meta-analyses and pooled data indicate heterogeneous treatment effects across biologic subphenotypes of ARDS, arguing that future trials should stratify or enrich for responders rather than testing “one size fits all” interventions.51 Parallel efforts are evaluating adjunctive biologics (targeted anti-inflammatory agents, cell-derived exosomes), repurposed drugs with immunomodulatory effects, and device-drug combinations that permit ultraprotective ventilation (eg, ECCO₂R paired with pharmacologic sedation strategies). Early translational and clinical reports show reductions in physiologic markers of lung injury with MSCs and related products, but confirmatory large randomized trials with clinically meaningful endpoints (mortality, ventilator-free days, long-term functional outcomes) are still required; investigators also call for standardized core outcome sets, harmonized biomarker panels, and multicenter platform trial designs to accelerate discovery and identify which patients truly benefit.51 55 56

Conclusion

ARDS remains a common, high-mortality ICU syndrome whose outcomes have improved mainly through better supportive care. The essentials are early recognition and prevention, lung-protective ventilation (low tidal volumes, limited plateau pressures, attention to driving pressure), timely adjuncts (early prolonged prone positioning for severe hypoxemia and, when refractory, extracorporeal support at experienced centers), plus conservative fluid management, targeted infection control, and selective pharmacologic measures (steroids, short neuromuscular blockade, inhaled vasodilators) as indicated; closing implementation gaps with protocolized screening, proning teams, and regional referral pathways, and pursuing precision medicine and biologic trials offers the clearest path to further reduce ARDS morbidity and mortality.
