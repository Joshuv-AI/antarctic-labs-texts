The risk perception of disease aggravation in older patients with non-communicable diseases: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Jizhe Wang, Xiaohong Lu, Yijun Xu, Na Na, Tong Qin, Yaqi Pan, Fan Li, Xiuli Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate risk perception promotes patients to identify and prevent disease aggravation. The ambiguity around risk perception of disease aggravation complicates efforts for clinical workers to improve the accuracy of patients’ risk perception. This study aimed to thoroughly describe the risk perception experience of disease aggravation in older patients with non-communicable diseases.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative descriptive analysis design. Utilising purposive sampling, we recruited 33 Chinese patients from comprehensive hospitals from October to December 2021. Face-to-face semi-structured interviews were conducted to collect qualitative data. Background

Disease aggravation of older patients with non-communicable diseases (NCDs) poses a major challenge to the global healthcare system [1]. A total of 41 million people die from NCDs each year, accounting for 71% of all deaths and posing great risks to the aging population [2]. Older patients who experience disease aggravation are vulnerable to psychological disorder, disability and mortality [3, 4]. Moreover, disease aggravation increases economic burden [5]. Improving the risk perception of disease aggravation in older patients can reduce the occurrence and negative impacts of disease aggravation [6].

Disease aggravation occurs frequently and has diverse clinical symptoms among older patients with NCDs. Patients with respiratory diseases experience acute and frequent disease aggravation. For example, patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease experience disease aggravation about 0.5–3.5 times per year because of pathogenic infections, overexertion, smoking, climate change and air pollution [1]. For patients with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, disease aggravation primarily arises from infection, high-salt diet, irritability and low treatment compliance (e.g. discontinuing antihypertensive drugs without medical consent) [7]. Disease aggravation of patients with cancer is characterised by local regional and distant metastatic recurrence [8]. Glycaemic control is the key indicator of effective health management behaviour in patients with diabetes [9]. The imbalance of glycaemic control leads to complications, such as heart failure, kidney dysfunction, cerebrovascular accident, proliferative retinopathy and diabetic foot ulcer [10, 11]. Disease aggravation in older patients with NCDs lacks a clear definition. This study synthesizes its characteristics as follows. (1) Disease aggravation of older patients with NCDs is manifested as severe symptoms or emerging complications. (2) Disease aggravation is caused by patients’ internal or external factors, including patients’ knowledge and behaviour of disease self-management, family’s caring ability and economic status, healthcare resource and living environment. (3) Disease aggravation can be diagnosed by new abnormal results of physical examination, laboratory tests and imaging, which require supplemental treatments to restore stable health. Improving the risk perception of older patients with NCDs and establishing risk prediction models are effective measures to maintain stable disease conditions.

Research on identifying and managing risk factors of disease aggravation emphasises the development of risk prediction models [12]. The model is primarily constructed based on objective evaluation and scientific measurement, including sociodemographic variables, laboratory tests and treatment programmes [12]. Evidence suggests that patients’ subjective perception reflects their feelings and cognition of risk identification [13]. Risk perception is a subjective evaluation of actual and potential threats to physical and mental well-being [14]. It is a cognitive process that requires to consider social, cultural and economic factors from the living environment [15]. Moreover, risk perception is the first step of risk management [16]. Proactive and accurate risk perception promotes risk identification and preventive behaviour among individuals.

Various studies have focused on the risk perception of disease aggravation in patients with NCDs. Recurrence risk perception among patients with ischemic stroke has been explored from the perspective of risk factors [17]. Recurrence risk perception of stroke patients is classified into four dimensions: probability, severity, behavioural risk factors and disease risk factors [18]. The dimensions of perceiving responsibility, prevention, control and severity have been summarised to understand how patients with rheumatoid arthritis perceive disease-related risks [19]. Studies regarded the entire older adult population or older patients with NCDs as subjects. A previous work recruited participants aged 50 years or older and concluded that their later-life risk perception includes terror, health problems, traffic accidents, violence and financial loss [20]. Fang (2015) concluded that older patients with NCDs perceive risks from economic, physical diagnosis and psychosocial perspectives [21]. To date, the risk perception of disease aggravation has not been described precisely and comprehensively among older patients with NCDs. Older patients with NCDs have common characteristics, such as senior status, functional impairments and comorbidities [22]. Shared features lead to similar cognition, affection and disease experiences. Therefore, the whole population of older patients with NCDs can be regarded as research subjects, similar to current qualitative studies [23].

Objective

This study aimed to describe the risk perception of disease aggravation in older patients with NCDs. The qualitative results may be utilised by clinical workers and health cadres to implement health management strategies aimed to improve the risk perception of disease aggravation in older patients with NCDs.

Methods

Design

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design, employing semi-structured interviews and conducting a conventional content analysis. The qualitative descriptions provided a comprehensive and straightforward summary of risk perception of disease aggravation for older patients with NCDs [24]. By focusing on a literal representation of participants’ words and events instead of deep interpretation, this approach keeps researchers closely aligned with original data [25]. Findings were reported by a Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [26].

Settings and participants

From October to December 2021, older patients with NCDs were recruited from two comprehensive hospitals in Qingdao, Shandong Province, China. Patients were included if they were (1) diagnosed with NCDs; (2) aged 60–80 years old; (3) at stable disease status; and (4) willing to participate. Patients were excluded if they (1) had mental symptoms or communication disabilities; (2) had not experienced disease aggravation or (3) were at the end of life. Purposive sampling was applied for participant selection. As a result, this study recruited 33 older patients with NCDs

Data collection

The researcher XZ was assigned as the interviewer for the face-to-face semi-structured interviews. She possessed a doctoral degree and had clinical working experience. She has been involved in teaching and researching the treatment of older patients with NCDs for more than 10 years. Therefore, XZ was a suitable candidate for the interviews.

Drawing from the literature review and clinical experience, researchers developed the first version of the semi-structured interview guide through group discussion. The guide was then pilot tested by an experienced researcher (XZ). XZ revised the guide twice according to participants’ feedback and her professional judgment. The semi-structured interview guide contained a set of questions that could be asked in a flexible manner (Supplementary file, Appendix B). It allowed interviewers to provide appropriate responses that prompted participants to elaborate further.

The participants received informed consent (Supplementary file, Appendix C) to ensure that no personal information would be divulged. Upon the participants’ consent to the informed agreement and audio recording, they completed the questionnaires on demographic and clinical characteristics. A one-on-one interview was conducted in the interview rooms. The interview room was a lounge or office designated for head nurses, offering a quiet and private space. Therefore, most participants stated that they felt at ease and expressed their feelings candidly. Interviews were usually conducted when patients had stable disease conditions and finished their treatment or examination. It ensured that the interviews would proceed without interruption.

Interviews were performed in Mandarin Chinese. Each interview lasted approximately 50–60 min and was audio-recorded. The researchers assured that there would be no leakage of audio recordings. The first author JW assigned numerical identifiers to the participants to protect their personal information. When the transcription was completed, participants read the text carefully and gave feedback on supplements and modifications. The interviewer reviewed and adjusted the text based on participants’ feedback to ensure the consistency of the text with their expressions. Furthermore, the interviewer documented field notes during and immediately after completing each interview to record details of participants’ facial expressions and body movements, reflections on the potential impacts of the interviewer’s behaviour on the interview and initial thoughts on the key points discussed. Audio recordings, multi-person transcripts and field notes were organised to reduce the probability of undiscovered data. Interviews were conducted until data saturation was reached, meaning that no new information was emerging from the data.

Data analysis

We employed a conventional content analysis approach [27]. The analysis was conducted collaboratively by JW (Master of Nursing, Clinical Nursing Researcher), XL (Master of Nursing, Clinical Nursing Manager) and XZ (Doctor of Medicine, Nursing Educator and Researcher).

All three authors independently read the transcripts several times to understand risk perception of disease aggravation for older patients with NCDs. Analytical memo, which is a core strategy in qualitative research, enabled us to capture researchers’ reflections and decision-making during coding, thereby fostering deep engagement and interaction among coders [28]. Memos were written freely and promptly whenever insights emerged, and an illustrative example is provided in Table 1. To ensure coding consistency, the first three transcripts were coded independently by the three authors. Discrepancies were resolved through iterative discussions, leading to a preliminary codebook. Subsequent transcripts were coded line-by-line and cross-checked. The codebook was systematically refined through ongoing consensus meetings. Finally, related codes were grouped into subcategories and categories, all of which were reviewed and confirmed by all authors for accurate data representation.

Research quality

Research quality was ensured by establishing credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability [29]. To achieve credibility, participants were given sufficient interviewing time to express their thoughts. And interviewer verified ambiguous contents again with the participants. The transferability and confirmability of this study were strengthened by comprehensive description of participant profiles, data collection and analytical procedures. The use of MAXQDA software facilitated a systematic and traceable analytic process, thereby enhancing the confirmability of the study. Specifically, all interview transcripts were imported into MAXQDA, where coders carefully read and annotated the texts. The software allowed the coders to generate initial codes by highlighting relevant segments and attaching descriptive labels. Each code was automatically linked to the corresponding original text, ensuring that the analytic decisions remained transparent and auditable. The software’s memo and code system functions were further employed to record reflections and emerging categories during the coding process. Finally, the coded data could be retrieved, compared, and exported within MAXQDA, which supported subsequent group discussions and consensus building among the research team. Similarly, iterative and in-deep discussions among the coders enhanced dependability through ongoing critical review.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Qingdao University (Approval Number: QDU-HEC-2021150). The participants provided informed consent, ensuring that their personal information would remain confidential.

Description of risk perception of disease aggravation for older patients with non-communicable diseases

Category 1: requirement of support from family

Patients relied on external support to maintain a stable healthy status due to the decline in self-care ability. They reported that economic and psychological support stemmed from families.

Subcategory 1: economic support

The patients emphasized that their family’s economic condition drove them to seek regular treatment and medication. Adequate economic assistance enabled patients to access enhanced medical resources and comprehensive health education. Therefore, patients’ ability to perceive the risk of disease aggravation was improved.

My children have enough money to pay for my medical expenses. They take me to the best hospital in my province. Doctors and nurses in this hospital often tell me how to prevent the rehospitalization. Gradually, I know more about my disease. And I believe I can manage it. (A33)

Meanwhile, adequate family support increased patients’ sense of responsibility. It encouraged them to monitor the disease and promptly recognise changes in its status.

I need to take this medication regularly to maintain a stable health condition. Since my family spend so much money on my disease treatment, I have to pay more attention to disease management. Otherwise, it will cost more money if my health condition gets worse. (A19)

Subcategory 2: psychological support

Unlike economic support, psychological support provides patients with internal motivation to combat diseases. The patients stated that psychological support primarily originated from family members, including partners and children. In general, partners spent the most time with patients and were familiar with their lifestyle and health status. Patients said that the psychological support provided by partners reflected their attentiveness and sensitivity to the perceived risk of disease aggravation. Patients accompanied by their partners had a positive mental status towards changes in their disease condition. They were eager to extend their lifespan to have additional time with their partners, so they prioritised their health status.

My partner is healthier than me. She takes care of me at most the time. To make her feel relaxed, I pay more attention to my disease condition. More precisely, we take care of each other. We often go to the hospital for health check-ups together, learn about healthy eating and proper exercise. I am worried that once I leave the world, she will be sad and lonely and lose hope in life. I need to know better of my disease condition to prevent unexpected changes. (A4)

Another source of psychological support originated from patients’ children. Throughout their lives, patients devoted the majority of their time and energy to their children’s development and education. If children provided patients with sufficient affection and support in their later life, they would feel happy and anticipate a longer lifespan to enjoy their current circumstances. Therefore, they focused on the disease aggravation risk.

Children do not allow me to do housework. And they buy nutritional supplements for me. They love me so much and usually encourage me to keep health lifestyle. I do not want to let them down. (A33)

Moreover, psychological support provided patients with a peaceful mind to confront the discomfort brought by disease. They had the confidence of overcoming disease aggravation and usually implemented proactive measures to prevent the risk of disease aggravation.

I keep a relaxing mind and regular lifestyle in my daily life. When I realize that the disease aggravation will happen, I take measures calmly to prevent it based on my disease management experience and advice given by nurses. (A12)

Category 2: emerging symptoms trigger risk perception

When patients were confronted with disease aggravation risk, they usually expressed risk perception based on personal experience. Patients were the first person who perceived the emerging symptoms of disease aggravation. When symptoms reappeared, they would associate them with previous experience and assess if disease aggravation had occurred.

Subcategory 1: accurately identifying symptoms

Some patients were familiar and sensitive to disease aggravation symptoms. They swiftly identified and obtained accurate risk perception. One patient reported,

I cough again and had medicine can’t relieve it. I feel it is similar to symptoms of the previous bronchitis attack. So I decide to come to the hospital for further examination. (A20)

These patients often experienced significant disease aggravation symptoms, such as angina pectoris in coronary heart disease and pain in gout.

I have coronary heart disease and I carried nitroglycerin with me. When I feel angina pectoris, I take one nitroglycerin immediately. Otherwise, my life will be in danger. (A9)

I experience gout. When severe swelling and pain appear in my knee joint, I realize that I should go to the clinic for a check-up. (A3)

Long-term disease experience and a serious approach to disease management promoted the development of accurate risk perception regarding disease aggravation.

I experience diabetes for 14 years. When I urinate more frequently and feel thirsty, I will measure blood sugar once to determine whether insulin injection is needed (A4)

Subcategory 2: neglecting symptoms

Several patients exhibited insensitivity to symptom changes and lacked disease-related knowledge. Thus, they often ignored symptoms that might be associated with disease aggravation, resulting in poor risk perception of disease aggravation.

Whenever I feel sick, I always choose to relieve it by myself rather than go to hospital. I rarely think about the possibility of disease aggravation, because I do not know what it will be like when it occurs. (A11)

Although patients perceived the disease aggravation symptom, the symptoms brought no physical pain to them. Thus, they did not associate it with disease aggravation.

My blood pressure is higher than before, although I am still taking antihypertensive medication. After a long period of time, I go to the hospital for a physical examination. The doctor tells me that I should have come earlier to adjust my medication. (A1)

Subcategory 3: exaggerating symptoms

Some of the participants overestimated the risk of symptoms unrelated to disease aggravation or that could be self-managed, such as physical exertion due to overwork.

I come to hospital as soon as I feel uncomfortable. But it is not as serious as I think. I may be too nervous. (A24)

Some patients might associate mild symptoms with serious complications, leading to an exaggerated view of the risk of disease aggravation.

I have pneumonia, and I have been diagnosed with pulmonary nodule for one year. Due to frequent coughing, I am afraid of getting lung cancer. I am scared and come to the hospital for lung cancer screening, but nothing happens. (A17)

Category 3: concerns of risk factors and adverse outcomes

Patients mainly perceived the risk of disease aggravation from the perspective of risk factors and adverse outcomes.

Subcategory 1: risk factors

Patients stated that disease aggravation was unavoidable. They accepted this with optimism and endeavoured to prevent disease aggravation. Risk factors were a significant aspect of their concerns in the disease management process.

I know clearly that my disease condition is constantly progressing, which is out of my ability to control. I have the great probability to suffer disease aggravation. So I need to take good care of myself. I am familiar with the risk factors that deteriorate health condition. (A11)

Patients know the risk factors through disease management experience, health education from healthcare workers and discussions with family members and patients suffering from similar conditions. They are aware of the risk factors including infection, low medication compliance and lack of exercise.

I have been diagnosed with this disease for many years, and I know the risk factors from my doctor.” (A28) “I often communicate with my friends who have NCDs. I know many risk factors of disease aggravation from them. (A17)

Subcategory 2: adverse outcomes

Patients would evaluate the adverse outcomes of disease aggravation, which was necessary in perceiving disease aggravation risk. The patients would suffer miserably if their physical health deteriorated. Therefore, individuals who were mostly affected by serious disease aggravation were themselves. Disease aggravation brought physical and mental suffering.

Increasing blood pressure results in my heart troubles. Once I feel chest tightness, I think my angina is about to attack.” (A28) “I am scared when I feel my head is dizzy. And I am very nervous during hospitalization caused by disease aggravation. (A2)

Apart from themselves, patients thought that disease aggravation would increase the financial strain on their families and intensify the burden on their caregivers.

Disease aggravation causes hospitalization. And family members must take care of me. It brings a lot of troubles to them. And the treatment during hospitalization costs a lot of savings of family. I always feel sorry to my children. So I try my best to perceive the disease aggravation risk as early as possible. (A19)

Category 4: self-reflection improves risk perception of disease aggravation

Through self-reflection, participants could identify deficiencies in their understanding and behaviour regarding disease management. Subsequently, they identified the aspects that could possibly exacerbate the risk of disease aggravation. The risk perception of disease aggravation would then be improved.

Subcategory 1: self-reflection of disease management cognition

Some participants exhibited erroneous or incomplete understanding of disease management. Self-reflection enhances cognition, thereby improving patients’ risk perception of disease aggravation.

Doctor has told me that I should take antihypertensive drugs twice a day. I measure the blood pressure before my second dose of the drugs. If the blood pressure is within the normal range, I will take it. Later on, I know that this behaviour will increase the difficulty of controlling hypertension. (A1)

Communicating with other patients highlighted participants’ cognitive shortcomings.

I have a WeChat group for patients with diabetes. Through the diet the shared in the group, I know that many of the foods I usually eat are not edible. (A27)

Subcategory 2: self-reflection of disease management behaviour

Some participants actively reflected on their behaviour to identify and correct actions that exacerbated their condition.

I follow doctors’ advice and go to examination every year. My doctors have told me what I should do to keep a stable health condition. Compared with the correct disease management behaviour, I clearly know what kind of behaviour will lead to disease aggravation. Sometimes, I find that my behaviour is harmful to prevent disease aggravation, and I will correct it in time. (A3)

Some patients knew what they should do to prevent disease aggravation, but they failed to implement them.

I know the side effects of smoking and drinking. I do not know if I can persist in quitting smoking and drinking after I turn back to home. If I cannot maintain healthy habits, I will inevitably experience disease aggravation. (A21)

Through self-reflection, patients identified deficiencies in their disease management cognition and behaviour. They would evaluate these deficiencies against the hazard level of risk factors to establish the risk perception of disease aggravation. As a result, they enhanced the risk perception of disease aggravation.

Discussion

This study described the risk perception of disease aggravation for older patients with NCDs. Clinical workers and health cadres could improve patients’ risk perception of disease aggravation by fostering family support; enhancing understanding of symptoms, risk factors and adverse outcomes; and encouraging self-reflection of disease management cognition and behaviour. The improvement of risk perception of disease aggravation could reduce the caring burdens of family members and clinical workers, create a healthy community environment and reduce social healthcare costs.

Requirement of support from family

Older patients with NCDs required economic and psychological support from family to cope with disease aggravation risk. It was rarely reported as an element of risk perception [18]. The difference could be explained by the characteristics of older population with NCDs. Most older patients with NCDs were exposed to advanced age, declining health conditions and loss of financial income, leading to dependence on others for daily life and disease treatment [30]. Therefore, they required additional support to maintain a health-related lifestyle and prevent disease aggravation.

Perceiving economic support was frequently cited by older patients with NCDs. Evidence supported that perceiving economic support influenced self-reported health conditions [31]. Meanwhile, economic support ensured that patients received adequate medical resources, which boosted their confidence in facing the risk of disease aggravation [32]. Individuals who reported sufficient assistance and care experienced low stress and uncertainty [33]. As a result, they exhibited more proactive and accurate risk perception. By contrast, some of the patients excessively focused on their disease condition due to their appreciation for support providers, resulting in a proactive but inaccurate risk perception of disease aggravation. China has a long-standing culture that emphasizes respect for the elderly and filial piety [34]. In this context, family members serve as the primary source of psychological support for older patients. Such psychological support provides motivation for patients to actively cope with the risk of disease aggravation. Adequate psychological support leads to increased satisfaction, cognitive improvement, and enhanced quality of life [35], which in turn fosters a more proactive and accurate perception of disease aggravation risk among older patients with NCDs [36]. In Western cultural settings, however, filial responsibility is generally not regarded as an absolute moral obligation, but rather as an internalized personal choice [37]. Consequently, older patients are more inclined to assume primary responsibility for their own well-being. The role of psychological support in shaping their risk perception of disease aggravation may therefore be less pronounced, suggesting an important direction for future research.

Emerging symptoms trigger risk perception

Older patients with NCDs perceived physical health changes firstly when disease aggravation occurred. Recognising emerging symptoms explained the tendency of patients to constantly seek out slight changes in their health condition. Symptom perception was usually reported by patients with obvious disease aggravation symptoms, such as chest tightness and shortness of breath in patients with heart failure, and abdominal pain in patients with gastrointestinal diseases [38]. They compared the emerging symptoms with previous disease experience to formulate the risk perception of disease aggravation. Some patients perceived these obvious symptoms accurately, whereas others exaggerated their severity and developed an inflated view of the risk of disease aggravation. However, if disease aggravation was chronic and insignificant, patients often disregarded it. For example, patients with diabetes often overlook the risk of early-stage renal function impairment [39]. The role of emerging symptoms was also emphasised in relation to patients’ self-care behaviours and healthcare outcomes [38]. Risk perception based on symptoms was similar to experiential thinking mode reported by dual-process theory, which operated quickly and directly [15]. Enhancing patients’ sensitivity to emerging symptoms might help them recognise the risk of disease aggravation promptly.

Concerns of risk factors and adverse outcomes

This study focused on two aspects of risk perception: the ‘risk factors’ of disease aggravation and ‘adverse outcomes’ brought by disease aggravation, which would be rationally evaluated by patients. As the disease progresses, disease aggravation is inevitable for older patients with NCDs. Whether patients perceived the risk factors of disease aggravation depended on their experience and accumulated knowledge of disease management [40]. The present study only explored patients’ perceptions of risk factors associated with disease aggravation. The risk factors for disease aggravation among individuals living with patients, such as partners, children or other caregivers, should also be explored. When patients saw the adverse outcomes of disease aggravation, they contemplated the physical and psychological distress they would endure. Simultaneously, family was the primary source of support for Chinese older adults [41]. Thus, patients often recognised the adverse outcomes of disease aggravation on their families, including the increasing caregiving burden and treatment costs. Unlike the collectivist orientation in Chinese society, Western countries are characterized by individualism and personal democratic values. As a result, older patients in Western societies are more likely to assess the adverse outcomes of disease aggravation in terms of personal impact rather than family burden. This contrast can be explained using cultural theory of risk, which posits that risk is socially constructed and that individual risk perception is shaped by cultural adherence and social learning [42].

Perceiving disease aggravation risk according to risk factors and adverse outcomes can be explained through the logical thinking framework described by dual-process theory [15]. Logical thinking stresses that the process of perceiving risk is rational and grounded in disease-related knowledge. Experiential and logical forms of thinking interacted with one another. On the one hand, patients applied logical reasoning to enrich their disease management experiences. On the other hand, experiential thinking prevented patients from being overly rational while disregarding experience and feelings. Moreover, experiential and logical thinking modes interacted with perceiving support from external resources to enhance patients’ perception of disease aggravation risks.

Self-reflection improves risk perception of disease aggravation

Self-reflection is a process of self-examination and development [43]. In the present study, self-reflection was described as a strategy of perfecting risk perception of disease aggravation by reevaluating disease management cognition and behaviour. The risk perception of disease aggravation in older patients with NCDs changed with various uncertainties in disease management. Self-reflection enabled patients to discern the appropriateness of their cognition and behaviour to avoid the risk of disease aggravation. Studies explored the role of self-reflection in the learning process, but rarely in risk perception [44]. Self-reflection of risk perception of disease aggravation was essential to make individuals aware of their disadvantages and improve their cognition and behaviour. Evidence supported that self-reflection requires support and guidance [43]. It was especially important for patients whose risk perception of disease aggravation was hardly changed due to lack of self-examination.

Researchers concluded that self-reflection improved individuals’ perceptions of feelings, attitudes, thoughts and actions [43]. Self-reflection could reconstruct risk perception of disease aggravation by modifying perceiving family support, emerging symptoms, risk factors and adverse outcomes, which in turn encouraged patients to recognize the beneficial role of self-reflection. Consequently, the perception of disease aggravation risk would persist in being enhanced.

Relevance to clinical practice

The results of this study offer clinical workers and health cadres insights into several aspects of improving the risk perception of disease aggravation in older patients with NCDs.

Firstly, a comprehensive assessment of family support perception is valuable. It is usually completed by clinical workers and assists them identify patients’ deficiencies in perceiving supports, especially for those with inaccurate risk perceptions of disease aggravation and limited medical resources. For patients lacking economic support, strategies to enhance the frequency of follow-up medical visits and impart knowledge and skills for preventing disease aggravation risks may be used. Clinical workers should encourage individuals with inadequate psychological support to engage in social activities.

Secondly, clinical workers, especially nurses, play an important role in providing patients with disease self-management knowledge and skills. Clinical workers can organize one-on-one or group health education sessions, distribute brochures on risk factor prevention or provide relevant videos to patients to enrich their perception of disease aggravation symptoms and improve the accuracy of perceiving risk factors and adverse outcomes of disease aggravation.

Thirdly, self-reflection plays a corrective role in improving risk perception of disease aggravation in older patients with NCDs. Clinical professionals can evaluate patients’ cognition of disease aggravation by developing questionnaires. Subsequently, targeted cognition supplementation will be provided to older patients. Clinical staff can encourage patients to articulate their coping strategies for disease aggravation prevention by documenting their experiences and talking with family members, other patients and health care providers. Through these methods, patients can engage in proactive and effective coping strategies to slow down disease development, thereby fostering a positive and accurate assessment of the risks associated with disease aggravation.

In rural China, many older patients are at risk of inadequate psychosocial support, have limited health literacy, and face difficulties in adopting telemedicine services [45]. After hospital discharge, health cadres—especially village doctors—serve as frontline providers who help patients recognize early warning signs of disease aggravation, thereby complementing scarce specialist resources. They not only provide routine medical follow-ups, but also offer psychosocial support and facilitate local peer interactions for socially isolated patients. Importantly, health cadres can strengthen patient engagement by introducing user-friendly educational materials (e.g., infographics, illustrative case studies) and guiding patients in the use of digital platforms such as WeChat to sustain family communication. Beyond China, similar community-based cadres exist in other Asian countries (e.g., kaders in Indonesia, community health workers in India), suggesting that these strategies may have broader regional applicability. Strengthening the frequency and quality of health cadre follow-ups, together with capacity-building through targeted training, may therefore represent a practical and cost-effective approach to improving risk perception and long-term disease management in resource-limited settings.

Strengths and limitations

There is a lack of literature associated with the risk perception of disease aggravation in older patients with NCDs. Therefore, the present study offers novel insights that enhance patients’ proactive and accurate awareness of disease aggravation risk and improve the efficiency of clinical workers’ health interventions. In addition, the enthusiasm of participants suggests that the topic of this study is interesting, motivating future research.

The study also had several limitations. Firstly, evidence suggests differences between patients with NCDs aged over 80 years old and those aged between 60 and 80 years old in health conditions, communication skills, daily activity abilities and social relationships [46]. The risk perception of disease aggravation in patients aged over 80 years old can be explored in future studies. Secondly, theoretical saturation is a relative concept. The characterisation of risk perception of disease aggravation in older patients with NCDs may be enriched by sampling from other geographical locations. Finally, there is a range of social respect, medical resource and economic support for the elderly across different countries and regions [47, 48], which may bring various risk perceptions of disease aggravation. Given the qualitative results derived from Chinese older patients, further investigation into generalisation across different social and cultural backgrounds is warranted.

Conclusion

This study initially described the risk perception of disease aggravation from the perspective of older patients with NCDs. Findings indicated that patients’ risk perception was incomplete without perceiving support from the external environment. The emerging symptoms of disease aggravation will trigger patients’ memory of disease management experiences and promote the development of risk perception. Patients rationally perceive the risk of disease aggravation by considering risk factors and adverse outcomes. However, disease condition and patients’ risk perception may change continuously throughout the prolonged experience of living with NCDs. Thus, self-reflection was necessary for enhancing the accuracy of risk perception of disease aggravation by improving patients’ understanding and management behaviour related to their condition.

Clinical workers and health cadres should prioritise enhancing the proactivity and accuracy of risk perception for disease aggravation in older patients with NCDs. The proactive and accurate perception of disease aggravation enables patients to implement effective disease management strategies, positively impacting families, medical institutions, communities and society. However, risk perception of disease aggravation may be influenced by patients’ educational attainment, availability of health services and perception on susceptibility and seriousness of disease condition [49–51]. Future quantitative and longitudinal studies can be conducted to explore the characteristics and influencing factors of risk perception of disease aggravation in older patients with NCDs.
