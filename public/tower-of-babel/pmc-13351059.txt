Carbon-incorporated polysilicon interconnection layer enables robust self-assembled monolayer anchoring for perovskite/TOPCon tandem solar cells
Authors: Haojiang Du, Huan Li, Zedong Lin, Zhenhai Yang, Xin Li, Haofan Ma, Meili Zhang, Weichuang Yang, Na Lin, Xuefeng Hu, Wei Liu, Liang Wen, Zhen Jia, Chenxu He, Zijia Li, Zhiqin Ying, Xi Yang, Yuheng Zeng, Jichun Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Due to the deficiency of anchoring sites (i.e., hydroxyl groups), self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) pose a significant challenge for achieving dense and uniform coverage on textured substrates, thereby hindering the development of perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells. Here, we develop an in-situ carbon incorporation strategy for polysilicon-based tunnel recombination interconnection layers to enhance SAM adsorption and anchoring. Carbon-incorporated polysilicon exhibits a higher surface hydroxyl group density and enhanced hydrophilicity, facilitating SAM adsorption and forming robust SAM anchoring. This leads to improved uniformity and continuity of SAM coverage, along with enhanced perovskite crystallization, resulting in pinhole‑free and denser perovskite deposition on textured substrates. Moreover, the strategy further adjusts the energy level alignment of the SAM surface, facilitating hole extraction. After integration into perovskite/tunnel oxide passivating contact (TOPCon) tandems (~1 cm2), we achieve a record efficiency of 33.84% (certified at 33.50%), with excellent operation stability, retaining 80% of its initial efficiency after 800 h.

Introduction

Perovskite/silicon tandem solar cells (PSTSCs) have rapidly emerged as a promising photovoltaic (PV) technology capable of surpassing the Shockley–Queisser (SQ) efficiency limit of single-junction silicon solar cells1–3. By leveraging the complementary spectral absorption between wide-bandgap perovskite top cells and narrow-bandgap crystalline silicon (c-Si) bottom cells, PSTSCs offer a viable and efficient route toward higher power conversion efficiency (PCE) together with a reduced levelized cost of electricity (LCOE)4,5. In recent years, the certified efficiency of PSTSCs has increased markedly, rising from 13.7% to 35.0% over the past decade, which underscores the significant commercial prospects of this technology6–9. In typical p-i-n PSTSCs, self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) are widely employed as hole-transporting layers deposited on the interconnection layers (ICLs)8,10–12. SAMs offer notable advantages, including high optical transparency, tunable energy levels, and solution processability13–16. Functionally, they facilitate perovskite crystallization, optimize interfacial energy level alignment, and suppress non-radiative recombination, thereby enhancing the overall performance of PSTSCs8,11,12,17.

Nevertheless, achieving uniform SAM anchoring on ICLs remains challenging, often resulting in incomplete coverage and weak adhesion. This issue is particularly pronounced on rough surfaces, such as the randomly pyramid-textured structures of industrial silicon bottom sub-cells18–20. Due to the ultrathin nature of SAMs (typically about 2–5 nm), their photoelectrical properties are strongly thickness-dependent; hence, inhomogeneous coverage can readily lead to electrical leakage and consequent degradation of device performance21,22. Successful SAM anchoring generally relies on the condensation reaction between the molecular terminals of SAMs and surface hydroxyl (‒OH) groups on the ICLs4,21. Although considerable progress has been made in screening suitable SAM molecules8,12,17,23,24, the low density of anchoring sites (i.e., –OH groups) on the ICL surface remains a major limitation to achieving dense SAM coverage. Current approaches for enhancing SAM anchoring often rely on additional processing steps, such as ultraviolet-ozone (UV-O3) treatment25,26, hydroxylation etching21,27, or the insertion of nickel oxide (NiOx) interlayers28–30, which aim to increase the surface density of ‒OH group. However, these methods not only add complexity to the fabrication process but are also largely restricted to commonly used transparent conductive oxide (TCO)-based ICLs. In contrast, polysilicon (poly-Si) tunnel recombination layers (TRLs) are regarded as a highly promising ICL technology, due to key advantages such as compatibility with tunnel oxide passivating contact (TOPCon) technology and the elimination of sputtering processes31–33. Currently, TOPCon SCs absolutely dominate the c-Si market, commanding a market share of over 80%34. Consequently, developing perovskite/TOPCon tandem solar cells based on poly-Si TRLs holds immense research and industrial significance, given their ability to be seamlessly integrated into existing large-scale manufacturing infrastructures. However, the abovementioned SAM anchoring enhancement approaches are difficult to apply to poly-Si TRLs, as they may lead to excessively thick interfacial oxide layers or degraded passivation quality. Therefore, it is imperative to develop a straightforward and universal surface modification strategy compatible with poly-Si TRL that enables uniform and dense SAM coverage and fully exploits the potential of poly-Si TRLs.

Herein, we introduce a straightforward in situ carbon (C) incorporation strategy for poly-Si TRLs, which significantly enhances SAM anchoring and improves perovskite precursor wettability. Since C incorporation effectively increases the surface energy and surface hydroxyl group density of the poly-Si, we achieve uniform and continuous SAM adsorption along with improved SAM anchoring. This improvement promotes perovskite crystallization, leading to pinhole-free and dense perovskite films on textured substrates, and adjusts the energy level alignment of the SAM surface, thereby facilitating hole extraction and suppressing non-radiative carrier recombination losses. Moreover, we fabricated perovskite/TOPCon TSCs with C-incorporated poly-Si TRL (~1 cm2) that achieve a certified efficiency of 33.50%, representing the highest reported value to date for perovskite/TOPCon TSCs. The device also exhibits excellent long‑term stability, retaining 80% of its initial efficiency after 800 h of MPP tracking.

Results

Self-assembled monolayer adsorption on carbon-incorporated polysilicon

The surface characteristics of substrates critically govern the formation of high-quality SAMs and perovskite films. To investigate the impact of different poly-Si substrates on SAM adsorption performance, we conduct density functional theory (DFT) calculations to analyze the adsorption behavior of Me-4PACz molecules on pristine poly-Si as well as on C-, nitrogen (N)-, and oxygen (O)-incorporated poly-Si substrates. As shown in Fig. 1a, the differential charge density distribution of [4-(3,6-dimethyl-9H-carbazol-9-yl)butyl] phosphonic acid (Me-4PACz) on the four types of substrates [considering the (111) crystal plane] reveals a pronounced interfacial charge redistribution. The cyan regions represent electron depletion, whereas the yellow regions indicate electron accumulation, providing direct visualization of charge transfer processes at the molecule-substrate interface. Quantitative Bader charge analysis demonstrates that the incorporation of C, N, or O significantly modulates the extent of charge transfer, with values of 2.16 e–, 1.51 e–, 0.44 e–, and 0.12 e– for C-, O-, N-incorporated, and pristine poly-Si, respectively. These results confirm that the incorporation of C, N, and O effectively enhances electron donation from the substrate to Me-4PACz molecules, thereby optimizing interfacial electronic coupling. Correspondingly, the molecular adsorption energy (Ead), which serves as a key indicator for evaluating the anchoring strength of SAMs, exhibits considerable variation across substrates. Relative to the pristine poly-Si substrate (–0.96 eV), the incorporation of C, N, or O substantially enhances SAM adsorption stability, with C incorporation yielding the most pronounced improvement (–3.69 eV). To evaluate potential crystallographic plane dependence and variations of SAM materials, we further investigate the adsorption behaviors of both Me-4PACz and the commonly used [2-(3,6-dimethoxy-9H-carbazol-9-yl)ethyl] phosphonic acid (MeO-2PACz) molecules on additional substrate planes, including the (100) and (110) orientations (Supplementary Figs. 1–5, Supplementary Table 1). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is further employed to investigate the adsorption behavior of Me-4PACz on modified poly-Si substrates. To eliminate interference from C, N, and O signals arising from different poly-Si substrates, phosphorus (P) is used as an elemental marker to evaluate the SAM coverage. The appearance of distinct P 2p peaks on all substrates after Me-4PACz deposition confirms the successful adsorption of SAMs (Supplementary Figs. 6–9)4,28. Notably, Me-4PACz/C-incorporated poly-Si exhibits the highest P/Si atomic ratio (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Note 1), indicating a substantially enhanced surface coverage of phosphonic acid (PA) anchoring groups on C-incorporated surfaces. Moreover, relative to pristine poly-Si substrates, the enhanced N 1s peak intensity associated with carbazole groups on O-incorporated poly-Si and C-incorporated poly-Si suggests stronger SAM adsorption (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11). To further investigate the mechanism behind the enhanced adsorption of SAMs, we perform an in-depth analysis of the O 1s spectra for the poly-Si samples prior to the spin coating of SAMs. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 12, light-element incorporation induces a marked increase in the O 1s signal intensity for surface Si–OH groups and physically adsorbed water, with the C-incorporated sample exhibiting the highest ‒OH/H2O ratio (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Note 1), which correlates well with SAM coverage. This increase in the ‒OH/H2O ratio may be attributed to the incorporation of C, N, and O atoms, which introduces lattice strain, thereby increasing the density of reactive sites (e.g., unsaturated dangling bonds and defects) available for hydrolytic dissociation and hydroxyl group formation35,36. The resulting higher density of Si–OH groups, in turn, provides more anchoring sites for the phosphonic acid groups of Me-4PACz via robust P–O–Si covalent bonds, significantly improving the overall adsorption behavior. It should be noted that due to the limited resolution of XPS, the signals from surface Si–OH groups and physically adsorbed water, whose binding energies are extremely close, cannot be fully separated in the O 1s spectrum. Nevertheless, the amount of adsorbed water is inherently regulated by the density of –OH groups, as a highly hydroxylated surface enhances hydrophilicity and promotes the adsorption of water molecules. Therefore, the signal intensity of this composite peak can be regarded as an effective indirect measure of surface hydroxyl density. Furthermore, the intensity trend of this composite peak aligns with the macroscopically observed contact angle variation trend, i.e., a higher peak intensity indicates greater hydrophilicity, further corroborating this conclusion.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy further corroborates Me-4PACz adsorption on all substrates through the appearance and shifts of characteristic peaks (Supplementary Fig. 13). Surface wettability characterization demonstrates the optimized anchoring of SAMs enabled by the incorporation of C, N, and O into poly-Si substrates. Contact angle (CA) measurements (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Figs. 14–17 and Supplementary Table 2) show that C-incorporated poly-Si exhibits an ultralow water contact angle (0°), indicating the highest surface energy among all substrates. This enhanced hydrophilicity promotes spontaneous two-dimensional growth of SAMs30,37,38, thereby improving the coverage uniformity of Me-4PACz. After Me-4PACz adsorption, Me-4PACz/C-incorporated poly-Si exhibits the maximal water contact angle (75.7°), confirming denser SAM adsorption and higher surface coverage30,39. Notably, the perovskite precursor dimethylformamide (DMF) shows the lowest contact angle (9.5°) on Me-4PACz/C-incorporated poly-Si, while displaying the highest angle (28.4°) on the bare C-incorporated substrate, indicating that enhanced DMF wettability originates from the improved SAM coverage rather than the bulk substrate properties. This optimized interface wettability facilitates uniform nucleation and crystal growth of the perovskite. It is worth mentioning that the inherent polarity difference between water and DMF leads to contrasting contact angle trends on various substrates.

We further perform scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) to directly observe the distribution of SAMs and the microstructure of the TRL structures. For the conventional TRL structure (Figs. 1d–f and Supplementary Fig. 18), a poly-Si layer with clear lattice fringes and a total thickness of ~45 nm is observed, consisting of a 20 nm-thick poly-Si(n+) and a 25 nm-thick poly-Si(p+). However, by combining bright field and dark field images to accurately determine the morphology of the SAMs, we observe that the Me-4PACz layer between the poly-Si(p+) and the MoOx protective layer exhibits pronounced inhomogeneity and discontinuity on the pyramid edge and tip. Specifically, this interfacial layer is extremely thin or entirely absent in localized areas, as observed at both the pyramid edge and tip. In contrast, the C-incorporated TRL structure achieves uniform and continuous Me-4PACz coverage at both features (Figs. 1g–i and Supplementary Fig. 19). The corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) spectra are provided in Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21. Notably, an ultrathin (~5 nm) C-incorporated poly-Si layer with low crystallinity is fabricated solely on the TRL surface. This layer is deposited atop the thicker conventional poly-Si layer, thereby enhancing the uniformity of SAMs without degrading the electrical performance. The detailed optimization process of this double-layer TRL structure will be discussed in the following section.

Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) is used to quantify the work function evolution across substrates, yielding values of 4.05 eV for poly-Si, 4.02 eV for C-incorporated poly-Si, 4.38 eV for Me-4PACz/poly-Si, and 4.64 eV for Me-4PACz/C-incorporated poly-Si (Fig. 2c). Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurements further support this trend, with contact potential difference (CPD) values of 162 mV (poly-Si), 197 mV (C-incorporated poly-Si), –80 mV (Me-4PACz/poly-Si), and –408 mV (Me-4PACz/C-incorporated poly-Si) (Figs. 2d, e). Although C incorporation slightly reduces the work function of poly-Si and consequently increases its CPD, the more substantial increase in work function and the negative shift in CPD (Supplementary Figs. 25, 2f) in C-incorporated samples collectively validate enhanced SAM adsorption. This interfacial modification optimizes the Fermi-level alignment at the perovskite/hole transport layer (HTL) interface and improves hole extraction efficiency.

To further investigate the effect of enhanced SAM coverage on the electric field distribution within the TSCs, cross-sectional KPFM is employed to map the vertical potential gradient (Supplementary Note 2). The electric field is determined from the first derivative of the net potential variation obtained under different bias voltages (Supplementary Fig. 26). Compared to the conventional poly-Si sample (Fig. 2g), the C-incorporated poly-Si sample exhibits significantly enhanced built-in electric fields at the SAM/perovskite interface (Fig. 2h), implying improved hole extraction efficiency.

Double-layer polysilicon tunneling junction design

In this section, we systematically investigate the photoelectric performance of the c-Si cells incorporating poly-Si tunneling junction (TJ) layers. Although the incorporation of C enhances SAM adsorption on poly-Si, it significantly suppresses dopant activation, thereby degrading electrical performance40. To achieve a balance between surface adsorption behavior and electrical properties, we propose an innovative double-layer poly-Si TJ structure, which consists of a ~5 nm-thick C-incorporated poly-Si surface on top of a thicker conventional poly-Si. The active dopant concentration profiles of different structures are first characterized by electrochemical capacitance-voltage (ECV) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 27, Figs. 3a–c), allowing the equilibrium band energy diagrams under dark and zero-bias conditions to be obtained through self-consistent solution of the fundamental semiconductor equations. The conventional TJ structure [poly-Si(p+/n+)/SiOx/n-Si] maintains high activated dopant concentrations with an abrupt doping profile at the p+/n+ interface. The corresponding band energy structure indicates a valence-conduction band overlap combined with a narrow depletion width (~6 nm), which facilitates efficient charge-carrier tunneling31. In contrast, the fully C-incorporated structure [C-incorporated poly-Si(p+)/poly-Si(n+)/SiOx/n-Si] exhibits severe performance degradation, as evidenced by a significantly expanded space-charge region of ~23 nm. This phenomenon is driven by the carbon-induced suppression of dopant activation, which, when coupled with rapid thermal annealing, facilitates dopant interdiffusion and mutual compensation. The resulting excessive compensation significantly broadens the space-charge region and severely hinders carrier tunneling. Notably, the double-layer structure [C-incorporated poly-Si(p+)/poly-Si(p+/n+)/SiOx/n-Si] demonstrates a doping distribution and band alignment nearly identical to the conventional poly-Si(p+)/poly-Si(n+) TJ structure, confirming minimal adverse impact on carrier transport.Fig. 3Electrical and passivation properties of c-Si cells with poly-Si tunneling junction layers.Activated dopant B and P concentration profiles of poly-Si(p+/n+)/SiOx/n-Si (a), C-incorporated poly-Si(p+)/poly-Si(p+/n+)/SiOx/n-Si (b), and C-incorporated poly-Si(p+)/poly-Si(n+)/SiOx/n-Si (c) structures measured by ECV with the corresponding calculated band diagram inset. d τeff spectra of the passivation samples. e ρc measured by the CS method with test structure inset in this figure and f the corresponding dark I–V curves. The passivation performance of the samples is further evaluated (Supplementary Note 3), with the structural schematics for passivation measurements presented in Supplementary Fig. 28. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 29, the implied open-circuit voltage (iVoc), saturation current density (J0), and effective minority carrier lifetime (τeff) for samples without and with different TJ structures exhibit comparable levels, suggesting that the introduction of an additional poly-Si(p+) layer and C incorporation has a negligible impact on passivation quality (detailed statistical data are provided in Supplementary Table 3, and τeff spectra are presented in Fig. 3d). This is attributed to the fact that the poly-Si(p+) layer is deposited onto the fully formed n-type passivating contact and subsequently undergoes rapid thermal annealing at a low 700 °C. Such a limited thermal budget hardly affects the already established excellent passivation quality. The vertical contact resistivity (ρc) values measured by the Cox-Strack (CS) method41 are presented in Fig. 3e. Although the conventional poly-Si(p+)/poly-Si(n+) TJ structure yields a moderately higher ρc than the TJ-free samples, the C-incorporated poly-Si TJ structure exhibits severely degraded contact properties with ρc of ~1000 mΩ·cm2. This degradation correlates with the reduced dopant activation and widened space-charge region, resulting in apparent non-linearity in the current–voltage (I–V) characteristics of Schottky contacts (Fig. 3f). In contrast, the double-layer TJ structure maintains Ohmic contact behavior, as evidenced by its linear I–V characteristic, achieving a low ρc of ~10 mΩ·cm2.

To validate the impact of TJ structures on device photovoltaic performance, complete single-junction TOPCon solar cells are fabricated, with the corresponding structural schematic illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 30. As shown in Fig. 3g, the influence of different tunnel junction structures on the pseudo J–V curves is found to be negligible when series resistance (Rs) is neglected. However, devices with C-incorporated tunnel junctions exhibit significantly poorer J–V performance under actual illumination conditions (Fig. 3h), indicating a substantial increase in their series resistance (calculated Rs values in Supplementary Fig. 31). Statistical analysis of photovoltaic parameters (Fig. 3i, Supplementary Fig. 32, and Supplementary Table 4) further reveals a pronounced degradation in fill factor (FF) and overall device efficiency, which is attributed to the poor contact property of the C-incorporated poly-Si TJ structure. In contrast, the TJ-free, conventional poly-Si TJ, and the double-layer TJ devices exhibit similar high performance. Specifically, high-quality TJ structures, including conventional and double-layer TJ structures, exhibit negligible influence on open-circuit voltage (Voc) and FF, while inducing only a slight reduction in short-circuit current density (Jsc), a minor photocurrent loss attributable to parasitic long-wavelength absorption in the additional poly-Si(p+) layer (Supplementary Fig. 33).

Carrier dynamics

In situ UV–visible spectroscopy is performed to track the crystallization behavior of perovskite in real time (Fig. 4b, c). In the initial stage (Stage I), a wet film is formed on the substrate after deposition of the perovskite precursor solution, as indicated by the absorption curve at 2 s showing nearly zero absorption in the long-wavelength region. Progressing to Stage II, as spin-coating proceeds, thickness oscillation signals resulting from thin-film interference emerge in the spectra of both samples, indicating a gradual thinning of the film. Notably, the C-incorporated sample displays a lower oscillation frequency (Supplementary Fig. 38), implying a slower variation in film thickness, likely attributable to improved wetting properties of the perovskite precursor and modified rheological behavior induced by the C-incorporated substrate. Following antisolvent stripping (Stage III), the perovskite film undergoes accelerated nucleation and gradual stabilization, with a significant increase in long-wavelength absorbance observed at 35 s. In contrast to the Me-4PACz/poly-Si substrate, the perovskite film formed on the Me-4PACz/C-incorporated poly-Si substrate exhibits accelerated development of absorption characteristics and a higher nucleation rate (Supplementary Fig. 39). This enhanced film-formation kinetics is directly correlated with the improved uniformity and compactness of the SAMs on the C-incorporated surface, which provides a uniform microscopic energy distribution and significantly improves macroscopic wettability. During the antisolvent dripping process, this highly homogeneous surface energy effectively lowers the nucleation barrier across the entire substrate, promoting a rapid and uniform nucleation process that ultimately leads to superior perovskite film quality.

The charge-carrier transfer and recombination dynamics of perovskite films are further investigated. Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectra (Fig. 4d) show a significantly enhanced PL intensity for the perovskite film deposited on Me-4PACz/C-incorporated poly-Si compared to that on Me-4PACz/poly-Si, suggesting that the C-incorporated poly-Si surface enables a denser and more uniform SAM coverage, thereby introducing more effective passivation sites for the perovskite layer and suppressing non-radiative recombination. Further carrier dynamics analysis via time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 5) shows that the Me-4PACz/poly-Si sample exhibits a fast decay lifetime (τ1) of 144.9 ns and a slow decay lifetime (τ2) of 3861.7 ns, whereas the Me-4PACz/C-incorporated poly-Si sample shows a shorter τ1 (73.3 ns) and a longer τ2 (5355.2 ns). These results indicate that the high-density Me-4PACz molecular layer on the C-incorporated poly-Si promotes efficient hole extraction and reduces bulk defect density. The enhanced electrical properties are further corroborated by PL quantum yield (PLQY) and quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) measurements under one-sun illumination (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 40, and Supplementary Table 6). The perovskite on Me-4PACz/C-incorporated poly-Si achieves a PLQY of 0.37% and a QFLS of 1.219 eV, significantly outperforming the control sample (0.19% and 1.200 eV). The carrier transfer dynamics are further characterized using ultrafast femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy (Fig. 4g, h, and Supplementary Note 4). The temporal evolution of the negative ground-state bleaching (GSB) signal at various pump-probe delay times (Supplementary Fig. 41) reveals that the perovskite film on the Me-4PACz/C-incorporated poly-Si exhibits a reduced negative GSB peak intensity and a faster recovery process, suggesting enhanced interfacial carrier extraction due to more uniform and denser SAM anchoring. This finding is further corroborated by the carrier decay kinetics monitored at 725 nm (Fig. 4i), which demonstrate a shortened fast decay lifetime of 81.2 ps for the C-incorporated poly-Si sample compared to 159.4 ps for the conventional poly-Si sample.

Photovoltaic performance of tandem devices

Building upon these advances, we fabricate perovskite/TOPCon tandem solar cells featuring a C-incorporated poly-Si TRL, with the device architecture illustrated in Fig. 5a. Cross-sectional SEM imaging (Fig. 5b) confirms that the perovskite layer fully covered the pyramidal texture of the TOPCon bottom cell without exposing any sharp tips or voids at the interface. We further compare the champion efficiencies of tandem devices employing three different TRLs, including the C-incorporated poly-Si, the conventional poly-Si without additional treatment, and the widely used IZO layer (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 42). The tandem device with a C-incorporated poly-Si TRL (1 cm2) achieves a remarkable efficiency of 33.84%, with a Voc of 1.986 V, a Jsc of 20.78 mA/cm2, and a high FF of 81.99% under reverse scanning, surpassing both the IZO-based and the conventional poly-Si TRL-based counterparts. We also achieve a certified efficiency of 33.50% for such a perovskite/TOPCon tandem solar cell (full certified report in Supplementary Fig. 43). Statistical analysis of photovoltaic parameters further corroborates the enhancement in device performance (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 9). The improvements in FF and Voc are attributed to more uniform SAM coverage and a consequently enhanced perovskite film quality with reduced defect density. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectrum is shown in Fig. 5e, yielding integrated current densities of 20.63 mA/cm2 and 20.65 mA/cm2 for the top and bottom subcells, respectively, which are consistent with the J–V characteristics. Operational stability is evaluated under continuous one-sun illumination provided by a xenon lamp (without UV filtering) at 40%–60% relative humidity and 25 °C–35 °C, with maximum power point (MPP) tracking. The simply-encapsulated tandem device with a C-incorporated TRL exhibits promising long-term stability, retaining 80% of its initial efficiency after 800 h, outperforming devices with conventional poly-Si TRL (initial J–V curves in Supplementary Fig. 46). Furthermore, continuous MPP tracking was performed on unencapsulated devices in an N2-flow chamber at an elevated temperature of 65 °C, where the device featuring the C-incorporated TRL retains nearly its initial efficiency after 300 h (Supplementary Fig. 47). This suggests that the improved SAM coverage effectively mitigates direct contact between the perovskite and silicon bottom cells, thereby enhancing device stability.

Discussion

In this study, we develop an in-situ carbon-incorporated poly‑Si TRL to improve the SAM anchoring and the overall performance of perovskite/TOPCon TSCs. Through a systematic screening of incorporated elements, we found that carbon incorporation significantly alters the surface properties of the poly‑Si, leading to an increased density of –OH groups and thus enhanced hydrophilicity. This increased–OH density facilitates more effective SAM adsorption, resulting in more robust SAM anchoring, improved uniformity and continuity of SAM coverage, and enhanced perovskite crystallization. These improvements collectively enable pinhole‑free and denser perovskite deposition on textured substrates. Moreover, by precisely controlling the carbon incorporation profile, we not only obtained a high‑quality tunneling layer, which simultaneously provides excellent passivation contact properties, but also engineered the SAM surface to have a higher work function, thereby improving hole transport and extraction. By incorporating the carbon‑incorporated poly‑Si TRL into perovskite/TOPCon TSCs, we achieved a certified efficiency of 33.50% (~1 cm2), representing the highest reported value to date. Additionally, the tandem device exhibits excellent long‑term stability, retaining 80% of its initial efficiency after 800 h of MPP tracking.

Methods

Materials

All chemical reagents were used as received without additional purification. Solvents, including N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF), ethanol, chlorobenzene, n-hexane, and cesium iodide, were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich. The organic hole-transport material [4-(3,6-dimethoxy-9H-carbazol-9-yl)butyl] phosphonic acid (Me-4PACz), lead (II) iodide (PbI2), and lead (II) bromide (PbBr2) were procured from TCI Chemicals. Formamidinium iodide (FAI) was obtained from GreatCell Solar Materials. Fullerene (C60, 99.5%) was supplied by Luminescence Technology Corp. Tetrakis(dimethylamino)tin(IV) (TDMASn, 99.99%-Sn), used as a precursor for SnOx deposition, was purchased from MNT Micro and Nanotech (source: Strem Chemicals). Acetone was acquired from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Fabrication of TOPCon bottom solar cells

N-type (100)-oriented Czochralski silicon wafers (resistivity: 1 Ω·cm, thickness: 200 µm) were used to fabricate the TOPCon bottom cells. The front surface was textured with conventional micrometer-scale pyramids using an alkaline solution containing KOH and organic additives. Following texturing, a boron emitter was formed via BCl3 diffusion in a quartz tube furnace, and the rear diffused layers were then removed through wet etching in an HF/HNO3 mixture. Mesa regions defining the tandem cell active areas were patterned by picosecond ultraviolet laser ablation. Subsequent processing involved the preparation of a submicron-textured rear surface using KOH with organic additives. A tunnel oxide layer was thermally grown on the submicron-textured rear surface, followed by the deposition of a 20 nm-thick intrinsic poly-Si layer via low-pressure chemical vapor deposition (LPCVD) at 600 °C. N-type doping was subsequently carried out by POCl3 diffusion at 850 °C. The poly-Si wrapping and laser-induced damage on the front side were selectively removed by employing a single-sided KOH etching process. The front-side boron silicate glass (BSG) and rear-side phosphosilicate glass (PSG) layers were then etched using HF. The front surface was passivated with an AlOx layer deposited by atomic layer deposition (ALD), and then capped with a SiNx layer prepared by plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD). Finally, an Ag/Al paste was screen-printed onto the micrometer-textured front surface and fired in a belt furnace to establish the front Ohmic contacts with a finger spacing of 1 mm.

Fabrication of poly-Si tunneling junction structures

For the fabrication of poly-Si tunneling junction structures, the oxide layer on the poly-Si(n+) surface of the TOPCon bottom cell was first removed using diluted HF. Subsequently, an ~25 nm-thick layer of either B-doped amorphous silicon (a-Si:H) or B-doped a-Si:H incorporated with C/N/O was deposited using a 13.56 MHz PECVD system with silane (SiH4), diborane (B2H6) and hydrogen (H2) as the primary precursors, supplemented as needed with methane (CH4), ammonia (NH3), or nitrous oxide (N2O). The content of C/N/O was precisely controlled by the gas flow ratio (R), defined as R = (CH4/NH3/N2O)/(CH4/NH3/N2O + SiH4) = 0.75. For the double-layer structure, an initial ~20 nm-thick a-Si:H(p+) layer was deposited, followed by a ~5 nm C-incorporated a-Si:H(p+) layer. Subsequently, a rapid thermal annealing (RTA) step was performed at 700 °C to crystallize the a-Si:H(p+). Finally, the samples were coated with a ~15 nm-thick AlOx passivation layer by ALD, followed by an annealing process at 450 °C for 30 min under a nitrogen (N2) atmosphere to recover the hydrogen effusion caused by the RTA process. It is worth noting that the thickness of the tunneling layer in this work was set to 25 nm to achieve a balance between minimizing optical parasitic absorption and maintaining excellent electrical contact.

Fabrication of perovskite/TOPCon tandem solar cells

TOPCon bottom cells integrated with tunneling junction structures were fabricated following the aforementioned procedures. Initially, the AlOx capping layer was removed using diluted HF to expose the conductive polysilicon layer. For the reference samples with the IZO interconnection layer, a 15-minute UV ozone pretreatment was applied to increase the surface hydroxyl groups and enhance the adsorption of the SAMs. In contrast, the poly-Si tunnel junctions did not undergo any additional surface pretreatment. After that, all samples were transferred to a nitrogen-filled glove box (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm) for subsequent processing. A Me-4PACz solution (1 mg/mL in anhydrous ethanol) was spin-coated onto the TRL at 4000 rpm for 30 s and then thermally cured at 100 °C for 10 min. Following the thermal curing, the film was washed twice using 200 μL of ethanol. Subsequently, Cs0.05(FA0.77MA0.23)0.95Pb(I0.77Br0.23)3 perovskite layers were deposited via a one-step anti-solvent method, wherein the precursor solution was spin-coated at 3500 rpm for 40 s with 300 μL chlorobenzene dispensed during the final 15 s of rotation, followed by thermal annealing at 100 °C for 10 min. Following the perovskite annealing, a 1,4-diazabicyclo[2.2.2]octane chloride (DCl) solution (0.4 mg/mL in Isopropyl Alcohol (IPA)) was spin-coated at 6000 rpm for 35 s and then annealed at 100 °C for 10 min to passivate the perovskite surface and optimize the interface with C60. After that, a 20 nm-thick C60 electron transport layer (ETL) was first deposited via thermal evaporation. Subsequently, a SnOx buffer layer was deposited by ALD at 80 °C. The ALD cycle consisted of 0.2 s pulses of TDMASn (vaporized at 70 °C) and 0.1 s pulses of H2O (at 25 °C), each followed by a 15 s Ar purge. High-purity Ar served as both the carrier and purge gas. This was followed by the deposition of a 100 nm-thick indium zinc oxide (IZO) transparent electrode using magnetron sputtering. Silver electrodes with a thickness of 250 nm were then thermally evaporated through a shadow mask to define device active areas of 1 cm2. Finally, a 120 nm-thick MgFx antireflection layer was evaporated at a rate of 1 Å/s to complete the device structure.

Characterizations

The dopant distribution profiles within the tunneling junction structures were characterized by electrochemical capacitance-voltage measurements (ECV; Buchanan CVP21). The ρc was determined by the Cox-Strack method using a semiconductor parameter analyzer (Keithley 4200-SCS). Passivation parameters, including τeff, J0,s, and iVoc, were quantified through quasi-steady-state photoconductance decay (QSSPC, Sinton WCT-120). The chemical compositions of different poly-Si layers before and after Me-4PACz deposition were characterized by XPS (AXIS SUPRA) and FTIR (Vertex 70 v). Surface wettability was assessed by contact angle measurements (Dataphysics OCA25) using deionized water and DMF probes. Cross-sectional samples for high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) were prepared using focused ion beam milling (Thermo Scientific Helios G4 CX) with a carbon protection layer, followed by ex-situ lift-out. TEM imaging was performed using a double Cs-corrected instrument (Thermo Fisher Spectra 300). Nanoscale chemical mapping was performed using atomic force microscopy-infrared spectroscopy (Bruker nanoIR3). Work function variations were measured by UPS (Axis Ultra DLD). KPFM and PFQNM measurements were conducted in an Ar-filled glove box with H2O and O2 concentrations kept below 0.1 ppm using a scanning probe microscope (Bruker Dimension Icon). Cross-sectional surfaces were exposed by cleaving from the film side. During measurements, bias voltages were applied while scanning identical regions. For data processing, potential profiles were averaged and zero-bias references subtracted to isolate field-induced variations. Electric field distributions were derived from the first derivative of bias-modulated potential gradients, with spatial registration ensured by topographic alignment. The surface morphology and cross-sectional images of the perovskite films were examined by SEM (Regulus SU8230, Hitachi). Crystal structure analysis utilized X-ray diffraction (Bruker D8 Discover; Cu Kα, λ = 1.5418 Å). In situ UV–vis absorption spectra of the perovskite films during processing were acquired using a dynamic spin-coating spectrometer (Spec-vision, DU-300/TU-300). Comprehensive optical characterization was subsequently performed using an Ultraviolet–Vis spectrophotometer (Lambda 950, PerkinElmer), measuring absorption, transmittance, and reflectance spectra across the 300–1200 nm wavelength range. Photoluminescence properties were assessed via steady-state and time-resolved spectroscopy (Horiba FL3-111), with PLQY determined under 520 nm excitation (LQ-100X). Urbach energies were extracted from Fourier-transform photocurrent spectroscopy. The fs-TA measurements employed a pump-probe system (Ultrafast Systems Helios) coupled to an optical parametric amplifier (TOPAS-800-fs). Samples were illuminated through quartz substrates using 7 mJ per pulse, 520 nm pump pulses (35 fs, 1 kHz) generated by a Ti:sapphire amplifier (Coherent Legend Elite-1K-HE) seeded by a mode-locked oscillator (Coherent Micra 5) and an Nd:YLF-pumped laser (Coherent Evolution 30). White-light continuum (WLC) probes (430–770 nm) were generated by focusing a tiny portion of the 800 nm beams (~0.01 mW, split from the regenerative amplifier) onto a sapphire plate. Probe fluctuations were corrected by reference channel normalization using a beam split from the WLC. Time delays (0–8 ns) were controlled by an optical motorized delay stage, with the instrument response function (~100 fs) determined by routine cross-correlation. Pump modulation at 500 Hz enabled differential transmission (ΔA) measurements captured by a chirp-corrected fiber-coupled spectrograph (equipped with a CMOS sensor) and processed using Surface X-plorer software.

The pseudo J–V curves were measured on the Sinton instrument (Suns-Voc MX). Light J–V characteristics were measured under AM 1.5 G illumination (100 mW cm–2) using a Class AAA solar simulator (EnliTech) calibrated with National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL)-certified reference cells. The tandem device measurements were performed without preconditioning at 200 mV s–1 scan rate in both forward and reverse directions. Maximum power point (MPP) tracking stability assessments employed a simplified encapsulation method. Devices were sandwiched between ordinary glass slides with epoxy resin edge sealing, featuring external electrical contacts via copper wires. Testing occurred under ambient conditions with fluctuating relative humidity (40–60%) and temperature (25 °C–35 °C). Additionally, high-temperature operational stability was evaluated by tracking the MPP of unencapsulated devices in an N2-flow chamber at an elevated temperature of 65 °C. EQE spectra were measured using an EnliTech QE-R system calibrated with certified single-crystal Si (300–1100 nm) and Ge (800–1200 nm) reference solar cells. EQE spectra for the perovskite top subcell and silicon bottom subcell were acquired independently under spectral biasing conditions. The silicon bottom subcell was electrically saturated by applying a 0.5 V bias while illuminating it with continuous > 800 nm long-pass filtered light. Conversely, the perovskite top subcell was measured under 1 V bias with concurrent <800 nm short-pass filtered illumination to saturate its response.

DFT simulation

DFT simulations were performed by running the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) code42. The core-valence electron interaction was described by the pseudopotential of projector augmented wave (PAW)43. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA-PBE) was used to treat the exchange-functional44. An energy cutoff of 450 eV was set for all calculations. The convergence of force for structural relaxation was set to less than 0.02 eV/Å. The Brillouin zone was sampled using a Γ-centered 2 × 2 × 1 k-point mesh. The QVASP software was used to write the input file45. Supplementary Table 10 shows the lattice parameters for the bulk Si. The bulk Si was cut along the (100), (110), and (111) planes, respectively. A 15 Å vacuum layer was added to the supercells at the same time to eliminate the interactions between periodic images. Then, we replaced some Si atoms in Si (001) with B atoms to construct 3% B-doped Si (100), 3% B-doped Si (110), and 3% B-doped Si (111). Approximately 1/6 of Si atoms were replaced in above surface structures with C, N, O atoms, respectively, to construct these surface structures (C-incorporated poly-Si(p+) (100), N-incorporated poly-Si(p+) (100), O-incorporated poly-Si(p+) (100), C-incorporated poly-Si(p+) (110), N-incorporated poly-Si(p+) (110), O-incorporated poly-Si(p+) (110), C-incorporated poly-Si(p+) (111), N-incorporated poly-Si(p+) (111), O-incorporated poly-Si(p+) (111)). To purely evaluate the intrinsic electronic interactions, the initial adsorption of intact phosphonic acid molecules on unhydroxylated silicon lattices was simulated directly based on the standard chemical formulas of Me-4PACz and MeO-2PACz (Supplementary Fig. 48). Binding energies of these surface structures with molecules (Me-4PACz or MeO-2PACz) were calculated according to the formula Eb = E(slab-molecule) – E(slab) – E(molecule). Here, the slab represents above-surface structures. E(slab-molecule) is the total energy of the slab-molecule. E(slab) is the total energy of the slab. E(molecule) is the total energy of the molecule. Charge difference and Bader charge analysis were calculated to investigate the charge transfer between these surface structures and molecules46.
