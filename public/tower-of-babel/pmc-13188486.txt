Comparative plastome analyses reveal life-form-associated selection signatures in Orchidaceae
Authors: Xiaoxia Deng, Junchao Ni, Tianyi Lin, Xinyu Li, Ling Yang, Haitao Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrchidaceae, one of the largest and most ecologically diverse families of angiosperms, exhibits remarkable variation in life form and includes numerous species with significant medicinal and economic value. However, comparative analyses of plastomes across orchid genera—particularly those explicitly linking selection signatures to life-form divergence and evaluating their evolutionary implications—remain scarce. Therefore, systematic characterization of plastome structural variation and life-form-associated selective pressures has the potential to advance orchid conservation strategies, sustainable cultivation practices, and fundamental evolutionary understanding.ResultsWe conducted comparative plastome and phylogenetic analyses of 67 orchid species representing 15 genera to characterize plastome structure, selective pressures, and evolutionary relationships. The plastomes exhibited GC contents ranging from 24.75% to 39.16% and a distinct codon usage bias toward A/U-ending codons. Our analyses revealed lineage-specific variation in genome size, ndh gene loss, repeat content, and codon usage, with fully mycoheterotrophic orchids generally exhibiting more extensive genome reduction. Positive selection analyses uncovered life-form-associated patterns: positively selected genes in terrestrial orchids included atpI and rpl22, whereas those in epiphytic orchids included rpl22, rpoA, and rps2; no positively selected genes were detected in mycoheterotrophic orchids. Site‑level analyses further indicated that amino acid substitutions at residues 122 and 124 of Rpl22 may be associated with life-form divergence. Structural analyses revealed inversions, IR boundary shifts, and lineage-specific disruptions in genomic synteny. Phylogenetic reconstructions largely recovered the expected generic relationships, whereas anomalous placements—such as Dendrobium chrysotoxum Lindl. nested within Pholidota—may reflect historical hybridization or introgression.ConclusionThis study provides insights into the potential effects of life form on plastid gene evolution in orchids, highlights molecular selection patterns associated with ecological strategies, and establishes a comprehensive genomic framework for research on orchid systematics, conservation, and sustainable cultivation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08728-y.

Introduction

Orchidaceae, one of the largest families of angiosperms, comprises approximately 736 genera and 28,000 species worldwide [1, 2]. This family is widely distributed across tropical and temperate regions, although it is absent from polar regions and hyper-arid deserts [1, 3, 4]. Orchids are renowned for their exceptional ornamental value and include numerous species with medicinal and horticultural importance [5, 6]. In addition, Orchidaceae exhibits remarkable morphological diversity, encompassing epiphytic, terrestrial, and mycoheterotrophic life forms [7]. This diversity reflects a broad adaptive capacity to heterogeneous environments and is closely linked to the evolutionary trajectories of the family [8]. Furthermore, seed germination and early developmental stages are highly dependent on specific mycorrhizal symbioses, a distinctive trait that underscores their evolutionary plasticity [8, 9]. In recent years, however, overexploitation and habitat destruction have led to declines in many wild orchid populations, rendering numerous species rare or endangered [8]. Consequently, the conservation and sustainable utilization of Orchidaceae resources require urgent attention.

Plastids are semi-autonomous organelles unique to green plants. They possess their own extranuclear genomes and play essential roles in key biological processes such as photosynthesis [10]. The plastome is characterized by a relatively simple structure, high sequence conservation, and predominantly maternal inheritance, which distinguishes it from nuclear and mitochondrial genomes [11]. These properties make it a valuable resource for phylogenetic reconstruction, population genetics, phylogeography, and species identification [11, 12]. Studies have shown that as plants adapt to diverse environments, plastid genes involved in photosynthesis often undergo positive selection, and specific sites may play critical roles in responses to environmental stress [13]. Given their diverse life forms and ecological adaptability, orchids represent an ideal system for investigating the relationship between plastome evolution and ecological function. Systematic identification of positively selected sites in chloroplast genes may help clarify the phylogenetic relationships within Orchidaceae and support germplasm identification and conservation efforts.

Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing have enabled the assembly of complete plastomes for an increasing number of orchid species, thereby providing a solid foundation for comparative genomic studies. Although previous research on orchids has largely focused on artificial cultivation, chemical composition, and medicinal applications [14, 15], an increasing number of studies have begun to examine plastome structure and phylogenetic relationships [16]. Nevertheless, most of these studies have been limited to single genera, and cross‑generic comparative analyses—particularly those investigating selection signals associated with different life forms and their evolutionary implications—remain scarce. To address this gap, we selected 67 representative species from 15 major genera within Orchidaceae. By integrating comparative genomics with phylogenetic analyses, we aimed to characterize conserved plastome features and interspecific variation, elucidate evolutionary mechanisms associated with life-form adaptations, and support molecular identification, artificial cultivation, evolutionary history reconstruction, and the conservation of endangered orchids.

Methods

Sequence collection and processing

Plastome sequences for 67 orchid species were retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Sample selection was based on the following criteria: (1) representation of the 15 genera included in this study; (2) genome assembly completeness ≥ 95%; and (3) availability of complete annotation information. The retrieved sequences were verified using BLAST to confirm accuracy. Life-form information for each species (terrestrial, epiphytic, or mycoheterotrophic) was primarily obtained from POWO (Plants of the World Online; https://powo.science.kew.org/). For species where POWO lacked relevant information or the reported life form was inconsistent with other sources, data were cross-checked and supplemented using relevant published studies [17–24]. Notably, Pleione exhibits both terrestrial and epiphytic habits and was therefore classified as having a variable life form. Here, “variable” indicates that the life form of this genus could not be assigned unambiguously based on the available evidence. Basic information for the 67 selected species is provided in Table S1.

Codon usage bias analysis

Coding sequence extraction

Coding sequences (CDSs) were extracted from 30 representative orchid plastomes (two species from each of the 15 genera examined) using a Python script and filtered according to the following criteria: sequence length ≥ 300 bp, with the start codon ATG and one of the stop codons TAA, TAG, or TGA.

Analysis of codon composition characteristics

Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU), effective number of codons (ENC), nucleotide composition at the third synonymous codon position (A3s, T3s, G3s, and C3s), total GC content (GCall), and GC content at the first (GC1), second (GC2), and third (GC3) codon positions were calculated using CodonW 1.4.2 [25] and the EMBOSS online suite [26]. Following previous studies, an ENC value of 45 was adopted as an empirical threshold to distinguish relatively strong codon usage bias from relatively weak codon usage bias [27, 28]. These parameters were analyzed collectively to evaluate codon usage patterns and signatures of evolutionary selection in the plastome.

Optimal codon analysis

Protein-coding genes were ranked in descending order according to their ENC values. The top 10% and bottom 10% of genes were selected to construct high-expression and low-expression gene datasets, respectively. CodonW 1.4.2 was used to calculate the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) of each codon in both datasets. The ΔRSCU value (ΔRSCU = RSCU in the high-expression dataset − RSCU in the low-expression dataset) was then calculated to quantify differences in codon usage between the two datasets. Based on the established criteria, codons with both high frequency (RSCU > 1) and strong expression bias (ΔRSCU ≥ 0.08) were defined as optimal codons [29].

Genome sequence variation analysis

Anoectochilus roxburghii (Wall.) Lindl. (MN880626), which possesses a structurally complete and well-annotated plastome, was selected as the reference. The plastomes of 30 orchid species (two species from each of the 15 genera examined) were aligned using the online tool mVISTA (http://genome.lbl.gov/vista) [30] with a window size of 100 bp, and a circular genome map was generated using BRIG [31]. Nucleotide diversity (Pi) was calculated for 22 orchid species using DnaSP v6 [32], with a sliding window size of 600 bp and a step size of 200 bp. Mycoheterotrophic species were excluded from this analysis because their plastomes typically exhibit extensive sequence divergence.

IR region feature analysis

Based on the four plastome regions—large single-copy (LSC), small single-copy (SSC), and two inverted repeat (IR) regions—and their junctions (JLB, JSB, JSA, and JLA), CPJSdraw was used to analyze the contraction and expansion of the LSC, SSC, and IR boundaries in 27 species exhibiting a typical quadripartite plastome structure (LSC, SSC, IRa, IRb) [33]. Three species among the 30 representative orchids, Gastrodia elata Bl., Gastrodia pubilabiata Sawa, and Epipogium aphyllum (F. W. Schmidt) Sw., were excluded because their plastomes do not retain a sufficiently intact quadripartite structure for meaningful comparison of IR boundaries. The extent of IR contraction and expansion was quantified, and the gene types and overlap patterns at the boundary were identified.

Synteny analysis

Synteny analysis was conducted to detect genomic rearrangements and inversions through the identification of collinear blocks among plastomes. Multiple-genome alignment was performed on 26 representative orchid plastomes with relatively intact structures using the Mauve plugin [34] in Geneious v2025. Four species among the 30 representative orchids, Gastrodia elata, Gastrodia pubilabiata, Epipogium aphyllum, and Epipogium roseum (D. Don) Lindl., were excluded because their plastomes are extremely reduced, and the extensive gene loss and structural degradation therefore hinder reliable identification of locally collinear blocks. Conserved syntenic blocks were identified to systematically characterize core conserved regions, local syntenic breakpoints, genomic rearrangement events (including recombination and inversion events), and the boundary-gene features of inverted regions.

Repeat sequence analysis

For the 30 representative orchid plastomes, simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were identified using CPStools [35] with the minimum repeat thresholds set to 10, 5, 4, 3, 3, and 3 for mono- to hexanucleotides, respectively. Long dispersed repeats (length ≥ 30 bp) were identified using REPuter [36] (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer/), including forward (F), reverse (R), palindromic (P), and complementary (C) repeats. The Hamming distance, maximum number of repeats, and minimum repeat length were set to 3, 90, and 30, respectively.

Phylogenetic analysis

Based on the complete plastome sequences of 67 orchid species and the outgroup Lilium bakerianum, CDS sequences were extracted using PhyloSuite v1.2.3 [37]. Continuous plastid regions, including genes and intergenic spacers were extracted from the aligned sequences using SeqKit v2.11.0 [38]. Sequence alignment was initially performed using MAFFT [39], followed by codon-aware refinement of CDS alignments with MACSE [40]. Poorly aligned or ambiguous regions were removed with Gblocks [41]. Optimal nucleotide substitution models for phylogenetic analyses of single genes and continuous plastid genomic regions were selected using ModelFinder [42]. For concatenated multi-gene datasets, PartitionFinder [43] was used to determine optimal partitioning schemes and corresponding substitution models. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using IQ-TREE [44] under the maximum-likelihood (ML) framework with 20,000 bootstrap replicates, and using MrBayes [45] under the Bayesian inference framework with two million generations and a sampling frequency of one tree per 1,000 generations. Mycoheterotrophic species were excluded from some single-marker analyses because extensive gene loss or the absence of homologous regions precluded reliable sequence extraction and alignment.

Ancestral state reconstruction of life-form traits

To investigate the evolutionary history of life-form transitions in Orchidaceae, ancestral state reconstruction (ASR) was performed based on discrete life-form traits. Each of the 67 orchid species was classified into one of four categories: terrestrial, epiphytic, mycoheterotrophic, or variable. To avoid artificially rigid categorization, variable species, which represent intermediate ecological strategies between terrestrial and epiphytic life forms, were coded as ambiguous states. ASR was conducted in Mesquite v4.02 [46] under the maximum-likelihood (ML) framework using the Mk1 model, which assumes equal transition rates among states.

Selection pressure analysis

To assess selective pressures among species with different life forms, EasyCodeML [47] was applied under the branch-site model (BSM). Foreground branches were defined by grouping all species sharing the same life form into a single foreground class, whereas species with variable life forms were assigned to the background branches to minimize ecological ambiguity. The ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution rates (Ka/Ks, ω) was calculated for each gene. Sequence alignments were trimmed using Gblocks [41] to remove poorly aligned or ambiguous regions, thereby improving the reliability of downstream analyses. Significance was evaluated using the likelihood ratio test (LRT), and the resulting p-values were corrected for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. Genes with Ka/Ks > 1 and FDR-corrected p < 0.05 were considered candidates for positive selection, whereas genes with extreme Ka/Ks estimates (e.g., 9999) were excluded from downstream analyses. Positively selected sites were identified using Bayes Empirical Bayes (BEB) analysis, with posterior probabilities ≥ 0.95 considered strong support. All reported positively selected site positions were standardized to reference residue positions based on Anoectochilus roxburghii (MN880626).

Amino acid states at positively selected sites were classified according to the GS schemes of Chen et al. [48]; GS2 was adopted in this study because of its relevance to critical physicochemical properties governing protein structure and function [49]. Life-form-specific enrichment or depletion was assessed using Fisher’s exact tests, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The resulting p-values were further corrected using the FDR method, and FDR-corrected p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.5.2. Protein structures corresponding to positively selected sites were predicted using AlphaFold (https://alphafoldserver.com/) with default parameters and visualized in PyMOL [50].

Results

General features of the plastomes in 67 orchid species

The complete plastomes of 67 orchid species representing 15 genera were characterized in this study (Table S1). Genome size varied substantially, ranging from 19,047 bp in Epipogium roseum to 165,812 bp in Bulbophyllum crassipes Hook. f. With the exception of five Gastrodia species (Gastrodia angusta S. Chow & S. C. Chen, Gastrodia elata, Gastrodia javanica (Bl.) Lindl., Gastrodia menghaiensis Z. H. Tsi & S. C. Chen, and Gastrodia pubilabiata) and Epipogium aphyllum, all species exhibited the typical quadripartite plastome structure. The lengths of the LSC, SSC, and IR regions ranged from 9,618 to 87,767 bp, 8,907 to 18,877 bp, and 261 to 30,927 bp, respectively.

Codon usage bias analysis

Codon usage patterns were analyzed in two representative species from each of the 15 genera (Table S2). The overall GC content of the plastomes ranged from 24.75% to 39.16%. Across all genera, the GC content at the third codon position (GC3) was significantly lower than that at the first (GC1) and second (GC2) positions. Conversely, adenine and thymine frequencies at the third position (A3s and T3s) were markedly higher than guanine and cytosine (G3s and C3s), indicating a pronounced bias toward A/U-ending codons. The effective number of codons (ENC) exceeded 45 in all species except Gastrodia and Epipogium, suggesting relatively weak codon usage bias in most orchids. Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values were similar across the 30 species (Fig. S1), with 28–31 high-frequency codons identified. Of these, 14 ended in A, 16 with U, one with G, and one with C, further confirming the strong preference for A/U-ending codons. The number of optimal codons ranged from 14 (Pholidota yunnanensis Rolfe, Corallorhiza sinensis G. W. Hu & Q. F. Wang) to 21 (Bulbophyllum kwangtungense Schltr., Cymbidium sinense (Jackson ex Andr.) Willd.). Notably, UUA was the most widely used optimal codon (occurring in 29 species, with the exception of Neottia pinetorum (Lindl.) Szlach.). Except for UUG, all optimal codons ended with A or U, underscoring the preference for A/U-ending codons as a defining feature of plastid codon usage in these orchids.

Synteny analysis

Synteny analysis revealed overall structural conservation together with localized variation among 26 representative orchid plastomes with relatively intact structures (Fig. 1). For instance, several inversion events were consistently detected around the 30 kb region of the plastomes. These included an inversion of petN and psbM in Bletilla and Cymbidium, as well as an inversion encompassing trnT-GGU in Corallorhiza sinensis. Another inversion of rpl32 within the 110–120 kb region was observed in six species from the genera Anoectochilus, Bulbophyllum, and Goodyera. In addition, a distinct inversion was detected in the SSC region of Neottia.

Genomic sequence divergence

Whole-genome alignment against the Anoectochilus roxburghii reference sequence revealed substantial sequence divergence among the 30 species representing 15 genera (Fig. 2, Fig. S2). The results showed that the patterns of variation were generally genus-specific, with Gastrodia and Epipogium exhibiting the most pronounced differences, including large-scale sequence deletions consistent with their mycoheterotrophic condition.

Nucleotide diversity analysis across 22 species (excluding mycoheterotrophic species) yielded an overall Pi value of 0.02905, with 15,668 polymorphic sites identified, underscoring the substantial genetic diversity within orchids. Highly variable sites were predominantly located in the LSC and SSC regions, whereas the IR regions were comparatively more conserved (Fig. S3). Using Pi > 0.08 as the criterion, three hypervariable regions were identified: trnC–trnD in the LSC region, and ndhF–ccsA and ycf1 in the SSC region. The highest Pi value (0.14804) was observed in the LSC region between 34,899 bp and 38,573 bp within the psbM region. These regions may serve as potential molecular markers for phylogenetic analyses of the 15 orchid genera included in this study, as well as for the identification of morphologically similar species across genera. To further evaluate the discriminatory potential, maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees were independently reconstructed for each region (Fig. S8–S10). The resulting topologies showed that, although resolution among closely related species was occasionally limited, these markers generally recovered well-supported relationships at the generic level.

IR region boundary analysis

Comparative analysis of the junctions among the LSC, SSC, and IR regions among 27 orchid species with a typical quadripartite plastome structure revealed generally conserved boundary patterns, together with several lineage-specific variations (Fig. S4). The JLB (LSC–IRb) junction was relatively conserved, with rps19 and rpl22 located in this region in 25 species. In 21 species, the JLB boundary was located within rpl22, which extended 220–342 bp into the LSC region. The JLA (IRa–LSC) junction was largely symmetrical with JLB and typically located between rps19 and psbA in most species. In Bletilla, Bulbophyllum, Neottia, Goodyera repens (L.) R. Br., and Corallorhiza sinensis, the junction lay between rpl22 and psbA. The JSA (SSC–IRa) junction was relatively conserved across species, except in Goodyera schlechtendaliana Rchb. f., in which the IRa region appeared to expand into the SSC. The greatest variation was observed at the JSB (IRb–SSC) junction: in most epiphytic species (Bulbophyllum, Dendrobium, and Phalaenopsis) and some terrestrial species (Bletilla, Cymbidium), the ycf1 gene on the left side was replaced by trnN; in Bulbophyllum, Phalaenopsis, Corallorhiza, and Cymbidium sinense, ndhF on the right side was further replaced by rpl32. In Neottia, IR region boundary analysis additionally detected an inversion in the SSC region. Notably, Epipogium roseum exhibited the most extreme junction divergence: its plastome possessed ultra-short IR regions (261 bp, far shorter than those of other species). At JLB, the conserved rps19/rpl22 were replaced by rps7/trnF; at JLA, rpl16 extended 604 bp into the LSC; JSB lacked typical ycf1/ndhF and contained only rps4 with minimal 2 bp overlap; and at JSA, rpl16 extended 390 bp into the SSC.

Repeat sequence analysis

Long dispersed repeats were detected across the plastomes of 30 representative orchids. As shown in Fig. S5, most species exhibited a predominance of forward and palindromic repeats. The number of forward repeats ranged from 1 to 57 (average proportion: 30.87%), reverse repeats from 0 to 32 (10.78%), palindromic repeats from 10 to 42 (54.97%), and complementary repeats from 0 to 16 (3.38%). Considerable interspecific variation was observed in repeat characteristics. Bletilla ochracea Schltr., Bletilla striata (Thunb. ex A. Murray) Rchb.f., Cremastra appendiculata (D. Don) Makino, Cymbidium ensifolium (L.) Sw., Dendrobium huoshanense Z. Z. Tang & S. J. Cheng, and Gastrodia pubilabiata contained the highest number of long repeats (90 each), far exceeding the average of 42 observed in other species. Specifically, Bletilla ochracea showed relatively high proportions of reverse (32.22%) and complementary (16.67%) repeats. In contrast, the mycoheterotrophic Epipogium aphyllum and Epipogium roseum possessed only nine repeat sequences, the fewest among the species studied, reflecting reductive plastome evolution associated with host dependence.

SSR analysis (Fig. 3) showed that mononucleotide repeats predominated, accounting for an average of 60.7%, followed by dinucleotide (18.22%), trinucleotide (5.49%), tetranucleotide (12.34%), pentanucleotide (2.73%), and hexanucleotide (0.51%) repeats. These results indicate that short‑period SSRs are a defining feature of orchid plastomes, with A/T mononucleotide repeats being the most common. In terms of genomic distribution, SSRs were most abundant in the LSC region and least frequent in the IR regions. In most species, SSRs were primarily located in intergenic spacer (IGS) regions, followed by introns and coding regions. However, in Gastrodia elata and Goodyera repens, SSRs were mainly located within coding regions, and showed strong clustering in the ycf gene family.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analyses based on a partitioned concatenated dataset of 10 shared plastid protein-coding genes produced largely congruent topologies under both maximum-likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) frameworks (Fig. 4). In both analyses, most sampled genera were recovered as monophyletic with moderate to strong statistical support, and the overall backbone relationships were broadly consistent between the two methods. Species of Bletilla, Pleione, Bulbophyllum, Cymbidium, Phalaenopsis, Goodyera, Cremastra, and Corallorhiza each formed well-supported monophyletic clades in both the ML and BI trees, with their relative positions along the backbone remaining consistent across inference frameworks. Notably, Dendrobium chrysotoxum Lindl. was consistently recovered within the clade comprising Pholidota species rather than clustering with other Dendrobium taxa. This placement was strongly supported in both analyses and remained stable across inference methods.

Ancestral state reconstruction of life-form traits

Ancestral state reconstruction (Fig. 5, Table S3) indicated that the terrestrial life form represents the ancestral condition of the sampled orchid lineages (Node 1, proportional likelihood (PL) for terrestrial = 0.850). In contrast, epiphytic and mycoheterotrophic life forms appeared to have evolved independently multiple times at different internal nodes across the phylogeny. Transitions to epiphytism occurred in several phylogenetically distant clades, including Phalaenopsis, Dendrobium, Pholidota, and Bulbophyllum; these shifts were reflected in the strong support for epiphytic states at nodes 9 (PL = 0.960), 12 (PL = 0.966), 21 (PL = 0.539), 24 (PL = 0.595), and 25 (PL = 0.975). Similarly, mycoheterotrophy arose repeatedly in separate lineages, such as Gastrodia, Epipogium, and Corallorhiza, with unambiguous reconstructions at nodes 3 (PL = 0.958), 18 (PL = 0.960), and 26 (PL = 0.960). Most key nodes received robust support. Only node 22 exhibited ambiguity, with the proportional likelihoods for epiphytic and terrestrial states being nearly equivalent (ΔPL < 0.05), highlighting uncertainty in the evolutionary history of life forms within the Cymbidium lineage. These evolutionary transitions are reflected in the scattered distribution of life forms across the phylogeny.

Selection pressure analysis

Selection pressure analysis was performed on 44 protein-coding genes across the 67 orchid species (Fig. 6). Signatures of positive selection (FDR-corrected p < 0.05, ω > 1) were detected in a life-form-specific manner. Terrestrial orchids exhibited significant positive selection in atpI and rpl22; epiphytic orchids showed positive selection in rpl22, rpoA, and rps2; no positively selected genes were identified in mycoheterotrophic orchids. Using Bayes empirical Bayes (BEB) analysis with a posterior probability threshold of ≥ 0.95, 12 positively selected amino acid sites were identified (Table S4). These sites were located at AtpI residues 226, 227, 228, 232, 233, 235, 236, and 237; Rpl22 residues 120, 122, and 124; and Rps2 residue 130.

Discussion

Sequence variation

Across Orchidaceae lineages spanning autotrophic to fully mycoheterotrophic life forms, plastome divergence exhibits a coordinated, multilayered pattern that includes genome-wide reduction, reconfiguration of LSC/SSC/IR junctions, shifts in coding-sequence composition and codon usage, and structural rearrangements in certain lineages. Fully mycoheterotrophic orchids, such as Gastrodia and Epipogium, occupy the extreme end of this continuum, displaying pronounced plastome contraction and atypical junction architectures, consistent with previous reports [51, 52].

A well-documented feature of orchid plastome evolution is the loss or pseudogenization of the plastid ndh gene family, which has been proposed as an early and recurrent event [53]. The ndh genes encode subunits of the NADH dehydrogenase complex [54]; however, their evolutionary trajectories differ among life forms. In fully mycoheterotrophic orchids, extensive ndh loss is consistently associated with functional redundancy following the loss of photosynthesis [55]. In contrast, partial ndh degradation in autotrophic orchids has been attributed to relaxed selective constraints under low-light conditions or functional replacement following transfer to the nuclear genome, rather than abrupt gene loss [54].

Repeat-associated variation further reflects the lineage-specific consequences of plastome reduction. As documented in plant organelles, dispersed repeats can facilitate recombination and influence plastome stability [56]. Consistent with this, orchid plastomes contain predominantly forward and palindromic long repeats, although repeat abundance varies widely among lineages: some species harbor up to 90 repeats, whereas the highly reduced plastomes of Epipogium aphyllum and Epipogium roseum retain only nine, consistent with genome streamlining. Our results indicate that SSRs are typically dominated by A/T mononucleotide tracts and preferentially accumulate in the LSC and intergenic regions, where selective constraints are generally weaker, in agreement with previous plastome-wide surveys [57]. Such SSR-rich regions are also prone to replication slippage, which can generate length polymorphisms and localized sequence variation, thereby potentially contributing to fine-scale plastome diversity within and among lineages [58, 59]. Interestingly, Gastrodia elata and Goodyera repens exhibit relative enrichment of SSRs in coding regions, particularly within the ycf gene family. In Gastrodia elata, this enrichment is likely linked to plastome reduction itself [51]: as intergenic regions contract and gene content becomes streamlined, SSRs may become proportionally concentrated in the remaining coding sequences, especially in long loci such as members of the ycf gene family. By contrast, Goodyera repens does not exhibit the typical features of severe plastome reduction; therefore, its apparent enrichment of SSRs in coding regions is more plausibly explained by lineage-specific compositional redistribution.

Codon-usage patterns were also consistent with previous plastome studies. Most autotrophic orchids exhibit weak codon bias and a preference for A/U-ending codons [60, 61], with high ENC values (48.700–49.692) indicating largely neutral evolution. By contrast, Gastrodia and Epipogium show reduced GC content (24.75%–26.98% vs. 36.99%–39.16%) and lower ENC values (35.283–40.197), consistent with genome-wide AT enrichment accompanying genome reduction [51, 52]. Given the extensive loss of photosynthesis-related genes, the apparent increase in codon bias in these lineages is more plausibly attributable to compositional effects than to adaptive optimization, although codon preferences may still influence the expression of retained essential genes through codon–tRNA interactions [62, 63].

Phylogenetic analyses

Plastome-based phylogenetic analyses revealed an unexpected clustering of Dendrobium chrysotoxum with species of Pholidota. Because plastid phylogenies can be influenced by marker choice, taxon sampling, and inference methods [64], additional analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of this pattern. Phylogenetic reconstructions based on a partitioned concatenated dataset of 10 shared plastid CDSs, two widely used DNA barcodes—matK (Fig. S6) and rbcL (Fig. S7) [65]—and three highly variable plastid regions (trnC–trnD, ndhF–ccsA, and ycf1) (Figs. S8–S10) consistently recovered Dendrobium chrysotoxum as nested within the Pholidota clade.

Several evolutionary processes may account for the observed plastid affinity between Dendrobium chrysotoxum and Pholidota. Given that plastomes are generally maternally inherited [11], plastid phylogenies primarily reflect maternal lineage history and may depart from the species tree when the evolutionary trajectories of the plastid and nuclear genomes become decoupled. Three mechanisms merit consideration. One plausible explanation is plastid capture: intergeneric hybridization is well documented in Orchidaceae [66], and asymmetric hybridization followed by introgression and repeated backcrossing can replace the resident plastome, yielding a plastid genome that reflects maternal ancestry and is discordant with the nuclear background [67]. A second scenario involves plastid introgression, particularly under incomplete reproductive isolation, whereby plastid haplotypes may cross species boundaries and become fixed even when nuclear gene flow is limited [68]. Alternatively, incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) could generate discordance between the plastid tree and the species tree during rapid diversification, when ancestral plastid lineages persist and are randomly sorted among descendant taxa. However, ILS appears less likely in the present case, as it typically results in weakly supported or topologically unstable relationships rather than a clade that is consistently recovered across multiple plastid markers [69].

Phylogenetic reconstructions based on the three highly variable plastid regions occasionally showed limited resolution among closely related taxa (e.g., the placement of Bletilla ochracea within Cymbidium in the ndhF–ccsA tree and reduced discrimination among Bletilla, Pleione, and Pholidota in the ycf1 tree), but generally recovered well-supported relationships at the generic level. A comparable pattern was observed for the standard barcodes matK and rbcL, which also exhibited limited resolving power among closely related species while remaining broadly congruent with established generic boundaries [70]. Future studies integrating expanded taxon sampling, nuclear markers, and multilocus phylogenetic frameworks combining concatenation and coalescent-based approaches—together with quantitative assessments such as K2P distance distributions, barcode gap analyses, and distance-based identification success rates—will facilitate a more comprehensive evaluation of DNA barcode performance in Orchidaceae [71].

Life-form-associated selection signatures

Interpreting life-form-associated selection signatures in Orchidaceae requires consideration of a phylogenetic background shaped by repeated ecological transitions and extensive plastome reduction. Ancestral state reconstruction supports a terrestrial habit as the ancestral condition, with epiphytic and mycoheterotrophic life forms arising independently multiple times across the phylogeny [3, 72]. The repeated emergence of similar ecological strategies in distantly related lineages provides a framework for testing whether plastid genes exhibit convergent responses to comparable selective pressures. However, such inference is complicated by uneven plastid gene retention, particularly in fully mycoheterotrophic orchids [23]. Given that lineage-specific plastome reduction may bias the detection of selection signals, selection patterns were interpreted using stratified gene sets, including a conservative set shared by all three life forms and a broader set shared between terrestrial and epiphytic orchids, the latter of which retain more complete plastomes. This strategy helps distinguish robust selection signals from those potentially influenced by plastome reduction in mycoheterotrophic lineages. Although species sampling among life forms is relatively balanced, residual differences in lineage representation and branch lengths may still affect statistical power. Furthermore, because the plastid genome represents only a small fraction of the plant genome and follows a distinct mode of inheritance, signals of adaptive evolution inferred from plastid genes are expected to capture only partial and organelle-specific aspects of ecological adaptation. Accordingly, all selection and evolutionary inferences in this study are restricted to plastid-encoded protein-coding genes at the plastome scale. In addition, although FDR correction was applied to control for multiple testing, Fisher’s exact tests assume independence among observations and do not account for phylogenetic non-independence among species. Because life forms may exhibit phylogenetic clustering within Orchidaceae, some observed patterns of enrichment or depletion may partly reflect shared ancestry rather than independent ecological associations. Therefore, life-form-associated signals reported here should be interpreted with caution, as they may partially reflect phylogenetic structure rather than independent ecological adaptation.

Within this framework, signatures of positive selection were unevenly distributed among orchid life forms, although the total number of selected genes was limited. Previous comparative plastome studies have reported elevated Ka/Ks ratios or positively selected sites associated with biological processes such as photosynthesis, biosynthesis, and self-replication, which may be important factors enabling species to adapt to their habitats over evolutionary history [73, 74]. It is reasonable to expect that genes involved in these processes are more likely to undergo detectable positive selection, as they are involved in processes in which minor changes may fine‑tune performance under variable environmental conditions. However, explicit life-form-specific analyses in orchids remain scarce. In the present study, significant positive selection was detected only in a small subset of genes and was largely confined to terrestrial and epiphytic orchids. Terrestrial orchids exhibited positive selection in atpI and rpl22, whereas epiphytic orchids showed signals in rpl22, rpoA, and rps2. These genes are primarily involved in ATP synthase assembly (AtpI) [75], plastid ribosome biogenesis (Rpl22, Rps2) [76], and plastid-encoded RNA polymerase assembly (RpoA) [77], indicating that adaptive changes were concentrated in energy-related and plastid gene-expression processes. No positively selected genes were detected in mycoheterotrophic orchids, consistent with the extensive reduction of photosynthesis-related genes and the functional simplification of plastid genomes in these lineages.

Structural mapping showed that the positively selected residues were distributed across helices, strands, and coil regions, suggesting potential roles in modulating local structural stability or interaction surfaces [78]. The cluster of selected sites within helix 10 of AtpI is particularly notable, as transmembrane helices in ATP synthase subunits are typically involved in subunit packing and stabilization of the F₀ complex [75]. Amino acid substitutions in such regions may subtly alter helix–helix interactions or membrane-embedded stability, potentially affecting the assembly efficiency or proton-coupling performance of the ATP synthase complex under different environmental conditions. The positively selected site at Rps2 residue 130 is located within helix 6, a structural element likely involved in maintaining ribosomal protein architecture and mediating interactions with rRNA or adjacent ribosomal proteins [79]. Substitutions at this position may influence the stability or interaction dynamics of the plastid small ribosomal subunit. In contrast, rpl22 was the only gene in which site-level amino-acid enrichment was significantly associated with life form, highlighting its potential importance in life-form-related plastid adaptation. Positive selection on rpl22 has also been reported in other plant lineages [80], supporting its potential role in the adaptive evolution of plastid ribosomal proteins. The positively selected sites in Rpl22 are located in structurally distinct elements, including strand and helix regions that are commonly associated with ribosomal protein–rRNA or protein–protein interfaces [76]. Changes at these positions could influence local folding stability or alter interaction surfaces within the plastid ribosome. Notably, the significant enrichment of polar residues at Rpl22 residue 122 and the strong preference for Tyr at Rpl22 residue 124 in terrestrial orchids indicate a shift toward residues capable of forming hydrogen bonds or aromatic interactions [78]. Such substitutions may enhance local interaction flexibility or stabilize contacts with neighboring ribosomal components, potentially improving translational efficiency or stability under terrestrial environmental conditions.

Conclusions

In this study, we analyzed the plastomes of 67 orchid species representing 15 genera to characterize plastome variation, life-form-associated selection signatures, and phylogenetic relationships. Our findings revealed lineage-specific differences in plastome architecture, including variation in genome size, recurrent ndh gene loss, repeat landscapes, and codon usage patterns, with fully mycoheterotrophic orchids generally exhibiting more pronounced plastome reduction. Selection analyses identified heterogeneous selective pressures among terrestrial, epiphytic, and mycoheterotrophic lineages and detected putatively positively selected sites in genes involved in photosynthesis, ATP synthesis, and plastid transcription. Phylogenetic reconstructions largely recovered the expected generic relationships, whereas ancestral state reconstruction suggested that the terrestrial habit was ancestral. A small number of anomalous placements (e.g., Dendrobium chrysotoxum nested within Pholidota) may reflect processes such as hybridization, introgression, or plastid capture. Future studies should expand taxon sampling across additional orchid lineages to further evaluate the generality of life-form-associated evolutionary patterns and reduce potential lineage-specific effects. Integrating plastome data with nuclear phylogenomic datasets and linking candidate sites to biological function through experimental validation will provide deeper insight into the mechanisms underlying plastome diversification and life-form transitions in Orchidaceae.
