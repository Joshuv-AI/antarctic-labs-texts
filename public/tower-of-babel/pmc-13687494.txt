Lactylation modification: a new bridge connecting macrophage metabolism and pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Wende Ma, Yumei Ma, Cen Guo, Xiaoling Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractPulmonary hypertension (PH) is a multifactorial, progressive disease characterized by pulmonary vascular remodeling and right heart failure, representing a substantial global health burden. Dysregulated interactions among multiple cell types within the pulmonary vascular microenvironment, particularly macrophage polarization imbalance with a predominant transition toward the M2 phenotype, play a critical role in driving pro-fibrotic and pro-remodeling processes in PH. Lactylation, a novel post-translational modification that covalently links lactate to lysine residues, translates glycolytic metabolic signals into persistent epigenetic changes. Recent advances have identified lactylation on histones (e.g., H3K18la), non-histone proteins, and mitochondrial proteins, revealing new layers of regulatory complexity. Elucidating the role of lactylation, particularly its impact on macrophage polarization and vascular cell function, may offer novel insights into the pathogenesis of PH and identify potential therapeutic targets.Key points• Macrophage polarization imbalance (predominant M2 phenotype) is a critical driver of pulmonary vascular remodeling in pulmonary hypertension (PH), and lactylation serves as a novel metabolic–epigenetic bridge linking macrophage metabolic reprogramming and phenotypic switching.• Lactylation modifications occur extensively on histones, non-histone proteins, and mitochondrial proteins in PH, regulating fibrosis-related pathways, arginine metabolism signaling, and the dysfunction of pulmonary vascular cells.• Targeting lactylation and lactate metabolism represents a promising therapeutic strategy for PH, supported by our previous studies on M2 macrophage polarization and ongoing lactylomic research.Keywords: Pulmonary hypertension, Macrophages, Lactylation, M2 phenotype, Metabolic reprogramming

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a multifactorial progressive disease. Its pathophysiological core begins with the homeostatic imbalance of various cell types within the pulmonary vascular microenvironment, including pulmonary artery endothelial cells (PA-ECs), pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs), pulmonary artery fibroblasts (PA-Fibs), and immune cells. This imbalance triggers irreversible pulmonary vascular remodeling. As the disease progresses, sustained increases in afterload induce right ventricular remodeling and dysfunction, with right heart failure becoming the predominant clinical feature in the terminal stage. Although the age-standardized disease burden of PH has declined globally, the absolute number of PH-related deaths has significantly increased by 48.5% over the past three decades, particularly in countries with a high sociodemographic index. The disease distribution exhibits a notable age shift, with the burden transitioning from younger populations (especially males) to older adults. Moreover, by 2050, women and elderly populations are expected to be disproportionately affected (Yang et al. 2025). Given this increasingly severe disease burden, elucidating the mechanisms underlying pulmonary vascular remodeling—the core pathological process—has become a central research focus. Pulmonary vascular remodeling manifests as PA-EC dysfunction, excessive proliferation and migration of PASMCs, PA-Fib activation, and immune imbalance. A thorough elucidation of its molecular and cellular mechanisms is critical for overcoming major obstacles in the prevention and treatment of PH.

Pulmonary artery endothelial cells

Under normal physiological conditions, PA-ECs maintain the low-pressure, high-flow state of the pulmonary circulation by synthesizing and releasing nitric oxide (NO) and prostacyclin (PGI2) to counteract endothelin-1 (ET-1)-mediated vasoconstriction, thereby finely regulating basal pulmonary artery tone (Félétou 2011; Seybold and Suttorp 2001). Meanwhile, PA-ECs maintain vascular wall integrity and exert anti-inflammatory barrier functions through the secretion of anticoagulant substances, inhibition of aberrant PASMC proliferation, and regulation of adhesion molecule expression (Seybold and Suttorp 2001; Sturtzel 2017).

In the pathological context of PH, PA-EC dysfunction plays a critical role. PA-ECs undergo profound phenotypic switching, characterized by reduced vasodilatory factors (NO, PGI2) coupled with a surge in vasoconstrictors (ET-1), endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), disruption of the anticoagulant surface, and a shift from anti-proliferative to pro-proliferative signaling, collectively driving pulmonary vascular remodeling (Lind 2000; Rowan et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2025a). Drugs targeting the NO-soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC)-cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) pathway, such as sildenafil, can improve hemodynamics and exercise tolerance in the short term but show limited effects on pulmonary function (Alqarni et al. 2025). Novel vaccines or monoclonal antibodies targeting the ET-1 type A receptor (ETAR) have been shown to significantly reduce right ventricular pressure and ameliorate small pulmonary artery remodeling in PH models (Dai et al. 2019). Additionally, endothelium-derived hyperpolarizing factor (EDHF) may serve as a compensatory vasodilatory mechanism when NO function is impaired (Jin et al. 2011; Ozkor et al. 2011; Ozkor and Quyyumi 2011).

Pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells

During the pathological progression of PH, PASMCs are aberrantly activated under the influence of various vasoactive mediators, epigenetic regulation, and metabolic reprogramming. Their normal contractile regulatory capacity is impaired, rendering them unable to effectively maintain pulmonary vascular diameter and blood flow resistance, thereby becoming a core executor of pulmonary vascular remodeling. PASMCs transition from a quiescent contractile phenotype to a proliferative synthetic phenotype, characterized by excessive proliferation, enhanced migration, and resistance to apoptosis, directly leading to medial thickening and luminal narrowing of the pulmonary arteries. Studies have shown that multiple vasoactive mediators regulate the pathological behavior of PASMCs through specific receptors. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), via its EP4 receptor, activates the cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)/protein kinase A (PKA) signaling pathway, promoting phosphorylation of annexin A2 (ANXA2) at threonine 208, which in turn drives β-catenin nuclear translocation and ultimately results in aberrant PASMC proliferation and migration (Xu et al. 2026). Similarly, serotonin exerts pro-contractile and pro-proliferative effects through its 2 A receptor (Htr2a); Htr2a knockout significantly reduces right ventricular systolic pressure, ameliorates right ventricular hypertrophy, and inhibits medial thickening of distal pulmonary arterioles via regulation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) pathway (Lu et al. 2025).

Epigenetic regulation plays a critical role in PASMC phenotypic switching. LncRNA LINC00599, through an N6-methyladenosine (m6A)-dependent liquid-liquid phase separation mechanism, cooperates with MYH9 to drive PASMC proliferation, and in vivo intervention targeting this lncRNA demonstrates therapeutic potential (Wang et al. 2025c). Additionally, the phosphorylation modification network is also involved in the regulatory process. Under hypoxic conditions, the expression of phosphohistidine phosphatase PHPT1 is downregulated, attenuating its negative regulation of the transient receptor potential vanilloid 5 (TRPV5) channel and the AKT/SMAD/TGF-β signaling pathway, thereby promoting PASMC proliferation and migration (Guo et al. 2025a). Reprogramming of cellular metabolism also underlies the aberrant activation of PASMCs. The activity of PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)/Parkin-mediated mitophagy is reduced in the pulmonary vasculature of PH patients, driving the transition of PASMCs toward a proliferative-apoptosis-resistant phenotype (Rehman et al. 2025). Regarding growth factor signaling, platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB) promotes PASMC proliferation and migration through upregulation of the neural cell adhesion molecule NCAM1, whereas NCAM1 knockdown effectively alleviates pulmonary vascular remodeling (Chen et al. 2024c). Therefore, targeting pathways involved in vasoactive mediators, epigenetic regulation, and cellular reprogramming provides new perspectives for the prevention and treatment of pulmonary vascular remodeling.

Alterations in pulmonary artery fibroblasts

PA-Fibs are key cells residing in the adventitia of the vessel wall. Under normal conditions, they maintain extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis by synthesizing collagen, elastin, and other components, providing structural support and elasticity to the vascular wall, and remain in a quiescent, low-proliferative state. In the pathological progression of PH, PA-Fibs become important participants in driving vascular remodeling through phenotypic switching, cytokine secretion, metabolic abnormalities, and complex intercellular interactions. Similar to proliferative diseases such as cancer, PH-Fibs exhibit pronounced proliferative, anti-apoptotic, and pro-inflammatory characteristics, constituting an early, marked, and sustained pathological response to vascular stress (Zhang et al. 2024a). PH-Fibs undergo phenotypic conversion to myofibroblasts, a process primarily regulated by the transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) signaling pathway. Once PH-Fibs transform into myofibroblasts, they evolve from “maintainers” of pulmonary vascular structure into “destroyers”: on one hand, they acquire robust contractile and migratory capabilities (expressing α-smooth muscle actin, α-SMA), exacerbating vascular wall stiffness, while their synthetic function becomes dysregulated, leading to excessive secretion of type I and type III collagen and fibrosis; on the other hand, they release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TGF-β and interleukin-6 (IL-6), stimulating PASMC proliferation and PA-EC dysfunction. Coupled with matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)/tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase (TIMP) imbalance, these changes ultimately result in aberrant rather than reparative tissue remodeling. The synergistic exacerbation of these functions collectively drives progressive thickening, stiffening, and occlusion of the pulmonary vessel wall.

Metabolic abnormalities in PH-Fibs share similarities with the Warburg effect in tumors. PH-Fibs exhibit downregulation of the mitochondrial complex I subunit NDUFS4, leading to metabolic reprogramming toward a glycolytic-dominant phenotype, accompanied by mitochondrial hyperpolarization, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and suppressed bioenergetic synthesis (Plecitá-Hlavatá et al. 2016). Consistently, PH-Fibs maintain aerobic glycolysis even under normoxic conditions, resulting in an elevated NADH/NAD+ ratio, which activates the NADH-sensing protein C-terminal binding protein 1 (CtBP1), promoting cell proliferation and suppressing anti-inflammatory gene expression (Li et al. 2016).

PH-Fibs interact with other immune cells through a complex inflammatory network. They activate macrophages via paracrine IL-6 and the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1), and C/EBPβ signaling pathways, driving chronic inflammation and PH development (El Kasmi et al. 2014). Leukotriene B4 (LTB4), through its G protein-coupled receptor BLT1, activates pulmonary artery adventitial fibroblasts, promoting their proliferation, migration, and differentiation, thus exacerbating PH progression (Qian et al. 2015). The cholinesterase inhibitor donepezil suppresses T helper 17 (Th17) cell differentiation via the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR)-Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)-STAT3 pathway, thereby modulating immune-fibroblast crosstalk (Guo et al. 2023). In IL-6-overexpressing transgenic mice, inhibition of pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) significantly blocks IL-6-induced transglutaminase 2 (TG2) activation and fibrotic progression, indicating that PKM2 serves as a critical node in the IL-6/TG2 axis regulating fibrosis (Penumatsa et al. 2024).

Immune cells and pulmonary hypertension: from the inflammatory microenvironment to the core role of macrophages

Beyond the functional abnormalities of vascular wall-resident cells, including PA-ECs, PASMCs, and PA-Fibs, which form the basis of pulmonary vascular remodeling, the involvement of immune cells further amplifies this pathological process. In PH, the immune system is aberrantly activated and persists in an inflammatory state. Large numbers of immune cells infiltrate the pulmonary vessel wall, releasing inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and proteases, driving smooth muscle cell proliferation, fibroblast activation, and endothelial dysfunction, collectively contributing to pulmonary vascular remodeling. Neutrophil-secreted elastase promotes PA-EC and PASMC proliferation, and the inhibitor sivelestat effectively ameliorates vascular remodeling (Deng et al. 2025). Under hypoxic stimulation, neutrophils activate the endothelial receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE)/phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT pathway by releasing S100A9, leading to endothelial dysfunction (Guo et al. 2025b). In chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH), platelets promote the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) by upregulating NOX2 and PAD4, and NET-related protein levels are significantly correlated with disease severity (Sun et al. 2025b). Crocin directly binds to the neutrophil protein HCK, inhibiting its activation and NET formation, thereby blocking the pro-proliferative effect on smooth muscle cells (Sheng et al. 2025). Beyond neutrophils, other immune cells also play important regulatory roles. Single-cell sequencing has revealed abnormal distribution of monocyte subsets in idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (IPAH) patients, with downregulation of the non-classical monocyte-specific gene HLA-DPA1 driving immune dysregulation (Chen et al. 2025). Dendritic cells exhibit subset-specific roles in schistosomiasis-induced PH, with conventional dendritic cell type 1 (cDC1) being protective and cDC2 promoting disease progression (Mickael et al. 2025). The proportion of T cells is elevated in the peripheral blood of CTEPH patients, and the level of platelet-monocyte aggregates correlates with disease severity (Sun et al. 2025a). Other studies have shown that systemic inflammation status is closely associated with PH prognosis, with patients in the high C-reactive protein (CRP) cluster exhibiting worse hemodynamics and survival rates (De Bie et al. 2026).

In summary, PH involves multidimensional pathological changes including PA-EC dysfunction, PASMC phenotypic switching, PA-Fib activation, and immune cell infiltration. Among these, macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype is particularly prominent, driving vascular remodeling through the secretion of pro-fibrotic factors such as TGF-β and IL-6. However, how these cells sense and respond to sustained metabolic stress in the PH microenvironment, especially the metabolic drivers of M2 polarization, remains incompletely understood. In recent years, lactylation, as a novel post-translational modification that directly links lactate to epigenetic regulation, has been shown to precisely regulate gene transcription and protein function. Whether M2 macrophage polarization and functional maintenance are precisely regulated by lactylation in the lactate-rich PH microenvironment and how endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and fibroblasts coordinate with M2 macrophages through lactylation to drive vascular remodeling remain to be systematically elucidated. In-depth dissection of this “metabolic-epigenetic-immune” regulatory network may offer new breakthroughs for understanding PH pathogenesis and developing therapeutic strategies targeting M2 polarization.

Literature search strategy

This review aims to systematically summarize the role of lactylation in PH, with a particular focus on its regulation of macrophage polarization and pulmonary vascular remodeling. We conducted a systematic search in the PubMed and Web of Science databases, with the primary search period spanning from January 2019 to December 2025 (2019 marks the year of the initial discovery of lactylation; earlier seminal literature was incorporated through citation tracking). The search strategy combined the following keywords: (“pulmonary hypertension” OR “pulmonary arterial hypertension”) AND (“lactylation” OR “lactate” OR “macrophage polarization” OR “M2 macrophage”). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) original studies investigating the association between PH and lactylation/lactate metabolism, (2) studies exploring the molecular mechanisms of M2 macrophage polarization, and (3) key findings from high-quality review articles. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-English articles and (2) conference abstracts or meeting reports without full-text availability. Ultimately, 85 representative articles were included. Priority was given to direct evidence from PH studies; when PH-specific evidence was limited, homologous mechanisms from tumor, inflammatory, and fibrotic diseases were cautiously cited as rational inferences, with the sources of evidence explicitly distinguished throughout the main text.

Lactylation: a new frontier in PH research

Discovery and development of lactylation

For a long time, the classic depiction of lactate in biology textbooks has been as an end product of glycolysis under hypoxic conditions, with its accumulation regarded as a marker of tissue hypoperfusion or energy metabolic stress. However, this traditional understanding has been reshaped by a series of groundbreaking discoveries in recent years. Studies have shown that in various pathological conditions such as PH and the tumor microenvironment, even when oxygen supply is sufficient, multiple cell types including immune cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, and endothelial cells actively undergo metabolic reprogramming and preferentially utilize the “aerobic glycolysis” pathway to produce large amounts of lactate. This metabolic shift is not a passive compensatory or decompensatory response but rather an active regulatory mechanism driving pathological processes such as cell proliferation, migration, inflammatory activation, and tissue remodeling, suggesting that lactate itself may play complex biological roles beyond being a mere metabolic waste product.

A key breakthrough in the understanding of lactate function was achieved in 2019. The study by Zhao Yingming’s team first revealed that lactate can directly serve as a precursor to catalyze the formation of a novel histone post-translational modification—lysine lactylation—thereby directly linking cellular metabolic status to epigenetic regulation (Zhang et al. 2019). Subsequent studies rapidly expanded this discovery: lactylation is widely present on both histones and various non-histone proteins, and its modification level is dynamically regulated by intracellular lactate concentrations, constituting a sensitive and reversible metabolic sensing mechanism (Zhang et al. 2024b). At the molecular level, class I histone deacetylases have been identified as key enzymes catalyzing the formation of lactylation (Gonzatti et al. 2025), while the histone acetyltransferases p300 and HBO1 also participate in this process as lactyltransferases (Niu et al. 2024). The specific reader protein TRIM33 achieves high-affinity and specific recognition of histone lactylation through its bromodomain, thereby translating the modification signal into downstream gene transcriptional responses (Nuñez et al. 2024). Collectively, this body of work has established lactate as a novel endogenous epigenetic modifier, transcending the realm of energy metabolism to become a central messenger molecule linking cellular metabolic flux to gene expression programs, thereby providing a new integrated perspective on metabolism-epigenetics for understanding numerous physiological and pathological processes.

Histone lactylation

Histones are the fundamental structural proteins of chromatin, and their N-terminal tails can undergo various post-translational modifications (such as methylation, acetylation, and lactylation), constituting a dynamic “histone code” that precisely regulates gene transcription. Recent studies have further revealed that histones can act as direct sensors of cellular metabolic status. A series of novel modifications driven by metabolic intermediates have been discovered, among which lactylation is the most representative. This modification covalently links the glycolytic end-product lactate to histone lysine residues and is enriched in transcriptionally active regions, thereby directly writing metabolic states such as hypoxia or the Warburg effect into the epigenome and reshaping gene expression programs. Lactylation plays a critical role in pulmonary vascular remodeling, immunity, neuroplasticity, and tumorigenesis, and its “writer” and “eraser” mechanisms constitute a key bridge connecting metabolic reprogramming with epigenetic remodeling.

As a representative paradigm of this metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk, the presence and function of lactylation modifications on H3 and H4 histones have been fully validated, forming a multilayered, multi-site regulatory network (Liu et al. 2025). Current research is highly focused on histone H3, with H3K18la being the most abundant and best-characterized modification site, broadly involved in the epigenetic regulation of normal physiological processes including immune homeostasis maintenance and neural development. During human embryonic development, lactate drives the specific deposition of H3K18la at the promoter regions of key zygotic genome activation (ZGA) genes, ensuring orderly ZGA activation (Li et al. 2024b), and its deficiency leads to 2-cell developmental arrest (Zhao et al. 2024b). In terms of immune regulation, H3K18la plays a key role by stabilizing and enhancing the expression of key genes and promoting chromatin accessibility. H3K18la and H3K9la are significantly enriched in CD8+ T cells, regulating T cell functional differentiation (Gonzatti et al. 2025). Additionally, lactate promotes alveolar epithelial cell ferroptosis through p300-mediated H3K18la modification (Wu et al. 2024). Dysregulation of the physiological regulatory function of lactylation directly leads to pathological phenotypes: endothelial cell TRPM7 deficiency reduces H3K18la levels by decreasing lactate production, upregulates the senescence gene p21, and accelerates vascular premature aging (Wang et al. 2025b).

It should be noted that histone lactylation also plays critical roles in various pathological processes including tumor immunity, neurodegenerative diseases, metabolic syndrome, and aberrant proliferation-repair. Studies in these non-PH diseases provide important clues for understanding the potential functions of lactylation in PH. In fibrosis-related pathologies, this modification often drives pathological repair characterized by excessive collagen deposition through epigenetic activation of pro-fibrotic signaling pathways, maintenance of myofibroblast activation, and formation of pro-fibrotic positive feedback loops: H3K18la levels are significantly elevated in fibroblasts in scar tissue, continuously promoting collagen expression and cell proliferation and migration (Gu et al. 2026); H3K18la activation in fibroblasts promotes atrial fibrosis in a spontaneous atrial fibrillation mouse model (Fang et al. 2025); and in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), H3K18la promotes TGF-β1 secretion through the YTHDF1/m6A/NREP axis, driving fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition (Wang et al. 2024) (Fig. 1).

In the tumor microenvironment, histone lactylation promotes tumor progression by stabilizing the open chromatin state of oncogenes and driving the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. In lung adenocarcinoma, lactate accumulation induces M2 macrophage polarization and impairs CD8+ T cell function, and H3K18la in macrophages further exacerbates this immunosuppression (Wu et al. 2025). In gastric cancer, lactate-induced H3K18la activates VCAM1 transcription, promoting M2 macrophage infiltration and tumor progression (Zhao et al. 2024a); in ovarian cancer, H3K18la in M2 macrophages enhances CCL18 expression, promoting immune evasion (Sun et al. 2024a). The above tumor characteristics—enhanced aerobic glycolysis, lactate accumulation, and M2 macrophage enrichment—are highly similar to the PH pulmonary vascular microenvironment.

The above evidence from fibrotic diseases and the tumor microenvironment indicates that histone lactylation plays a key role in promoting fibrosis, regulating M2 macrophage polarization, and remodeling the immune microenvironment. Notably, the PH pulmonary vascular microenvironment is similarly characterized by sustained TGF-β signaling activation, enhanced aerobic glycolysis, lactate accumulation, and M2 macrophage enrichment, which are highly similar to the above pathological states. This suggests that histone lactylation may participate in pulmonary vascular remodeling in PH through analogous mechanisms. The following sections will systematically elaborate on the direct research evidence for lactate metabolic reprogramming and lactylation modifications in PH.

Non-histone lactylation

With advances in high-resolution mass spectrometry, non-histone lactylation—a post-translational modification directly driven by a metabolite—has increasingly demonstrated its prevalence and importance. It is now established that this modification is extensively involved in key biological pathways including metabolism, transcriptional regulation, signal transduction, and the cytoskeleton, serving as a core mechanism regulating various pathophysiological processes.

In the regulation of metabolic pathways and energy homeostasis, lactylation directly targets core metabolic enzymes. Under hypoxic conditions, AARS2 catalyzes the lactylation of pyruvate dehydrogenase E1α subunit (PDHA1) at lysine 336 and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 2 (CPT2) at lysines 457/458, leading to their inactivation and thereby limiting acetyl-CoA production and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle flux at the source (Mao et al. 2024). Furthermore, lactylation of PKM2 at lysine 62 stabilizes its highly active tetrameric conformation, driving the transition of pro-inflammatory macrophages toward a reparative phenotype (Wang et al. 2022a). These studies demonstrate that lactylation acts as a precise “valve” at metabolic nodes, directly determining cellular metabolic flux and fate.

At the level of transcriptional regulation and RNA metabolism, lactylation profoundly influences gene expression programs by acting on transcription factors and RNA-binding proteins. Lactylation of HIF-1α enhances its stability by hindering VHL recognition, thereby amplifying hypoxic responses (Li et al. 2025a). Lactylation of TFEB at lysine 91 enhances autophagy-related gene transcription by preventing E3 ubiquitin ligase WWP2 binding (Huang et al. 2024). Lactylation of insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 3 (IGF2BP3) at lysine 76 enhances its binding to m6A-modified mRNA, mediating chemoresistance in hepatocellular carcinoma (Zhu et al. 2025). Lactylation of nucleolin (NCL) at lysine 477 regulates the pre-mRNA splicing of MAP kinase-activating death domain protein (MADD), driving cholangiocarcinoma progression (Yang et al. 2024).

In signal transduction and cell communication, lactylation of the repair protein MRE11 at lysine 673 enhances its DNA-binding capacity, promoting homologous recombination repair efficiency (Chen et al. 2024d). Lactylation of METTL16 at lysine 229 amplifies cuproptosis signaling, synergistically triggering cancer cell death (Sun et al. 2023). In the cytoskeleton and neural function, histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) catalyzes the lactylation of α-tubulin, enhancing microtubule dynamics in neurons (Sun et al. 2024b). The above evidence for non-histone lactylation is mainly derived from the fields of oncology, neuropsychiatry, and inflammatory diseases; the specific substrates, modification sites, and functions of non-histone lactylation in PH remain to be systematically identified.

Non-histone lactylation has formed a global, networked regulatory system that precisely translates cellular metabolic status (lactate levels) into the regulation of protein function in real time. However, the above evidence is mainly derived from non-PH disease fields, and the specific substrates, modification sites, and functions of non-histone lactylation in PH remain to be systematically identified. Given that the PH microenvironment is similarly characterized by metabolic reprogramming, this direction warrants particular attention.

Mitochondrial protein lactylation

As the carbon metabolic center and oxygen metabolic hub of the cell, the functional state of mitochondria directly determines cellular energy production, biosynthetic capacity, redox balance, and fate decisions. In recent years, lactylation of mitochondrial proteins, as an emerging metabolism-function coupling mechanism, has become a core research direction for elucidating mitochondrial dysfunction under pathological conditions.

Under metabolic stress, glycolytic burst leads to massive lactate production, which in turn induces lactylation of isocitrate dehydrogenase 3 subunit gamma (IDH3G), a key enzyme in the mitochondrial TCA cycle, directly reshaping carbon metabolic flux and energy production by altering the activity of the IDH3 enzyme complex (Wang et al. 2023). Furthermore, lactylation of acyl-CoA synthetase family member 2 (ACSF2) at lysine 182 impairs its ability to activate fatty acids, leading to mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation defects and lipotoxic accumulation (Chen et al. 2024a). In inflammatory stress, LDHA catalyzes the lactylation of the inflammasome protein NLRP3 at lysine 245, stabilizing NLRP3 protein and enhancing pyroptosome assembly, thereby converting metabolic stress into a potent inflammatory injury signal (Fang et al. 2024). These studies demonstrate that lactylation precisely targets key nodes of mitochondrial metabolism (IDH3G, ACSF2) and mitochondria-associated stress signals (NLRP3), playing a central driving role in metabolic reprogramming, tissue injury, and inflammatory responses (Table 1).

Research progress of lactylation in PH

Lactate metabolic reprogramming and elevated lactate levels in PH

With deepening research into the pathogenesis of PH, the interplay between metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation has garnered increasing attention. Lactylation, as a newly discovered post-translational modification involving the covalent addition of a lactyl group to lysine residues, serves as an important bridge linking cellular metabolic status to gene expression regulation (Zhang). In the pathological environment of PH, enhanced glycolysis leads to lactate accumulation, which provides abundant substrates for lactylation, thereby participating in disease progression through the regulation of key protein functions.

In the PH tissue microenvironment, multiple metabolic abnormalities provide favorable conditions for lactylation. Thiamine (vitamin B1) induces metabolic remodeling and lactic acidosis by inhibiting key enzymes of the TCA cycle, thereby promoting PH progression (Zhao et al. 2025). Upregulation of the mitochondrial protease LONP1 not only blocks pyruvate entry into mitochondria by degrading MPC1, leading to enhanced glycolysis and increased lactate production, but also directly contributes to lactate metabolic dysregulation, manifested by elevated lactate dehydrogenase activity and intracellular lactate accumulation (Li et al. 2025b). This lactate metabolic abnormality forms a vicious cycle with glycolytic reprogramming, collectively promoting PASMC proliferation and migration.

The lactate accumulation driven by the above metabolic reprogramming provides an abundant substrate basis for lactylation. In PH, lactylation participates in pulmonary vascular remodeling primarily through two layers—histone and non-histone lactylation—as elaborated below.

At the histone lactylation level, preliminary validation has been achieved in PH. Under hypoxic conditions, mitochondrial ROS (mROS) inhibit HIF-1α hydroxylation, activating the HIF-1α/PDK1&PDK2/p-PDH-E1α axis to trigger glycolytic switching in PASMCs, promoting lactate accumulation and histone lactylation enrichment at the promoter regions of HIF-1α target genes (Bmp5, Trpc5, Kit), thereby driving PASMC proliferation and vascular remodeling (Chen et al. 2023). Pharmacological inhibition of lactate dehydrogenase reduces histone lactylation and improves the PH phenotype (Chen et al. 2023). Furthermore, in obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)-associated PH, hypoxia-induced lactate accumulation activates PDE4B transcription through histone lactylation at its promoter, which via FUS nuclear translocation enhances angiotensinogen (AGT) expression, driving PASMC proliferation and vascular remodeling (Yang et al. 2026). Microplastic exposure promotes histone lactylation and EndMT through HK2-mediated glycolysis (Long et al. 2026). Histone lactylation also drives PASMC senescence by upregulating prelamin A, and senescent cells promote vascular remodeling through IL-6 secretion (Zhang et al. 2025).

At the non-histone lactylation level, research in PH has also made preliminary progress. Integrated lactylomic profiling in hypoxic human PASMCs and validation in rodent PH models revealed that hypoxia-induced ALDOB-K87 lactylation recruits dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) to mitochondria via sentrin/SUMO-specific peptidase 3 (SENP3)-mediated deSUMOylation of DRP1, promoting mitochondrial fission and exacerbating PASMC proliferation, migration, and phenotypic switching (Yi et al. 2026). Additionally, in hypoxic human pulmonary artery endothelial cells, lactate induces Twist1 lactylation by enhancing its binding to p300/CBP, promoting its nuclear translocation, upregulating TGFB1 transcription, and activating the Smad2 pathway, thereby driving EndMT (Li et al. 2026). Although these findings reveal the important roles of non-histone lactylation in PH, the specific regulatory networks in different cell types (especially macrophages) and the synergistic mechanisms with other cell types require further exploration.

Histone lactylation, built upon the above metabolic basis, constitutes a key epigenetic layer regulating the pathological progression of PH. As described above, in the hypoxic PH model, upregulated histone lactylation promotes prelamin A expression, induces PASMC senescence, and further promotes cell proliferation and vascular remodeling through the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (e.g., secretion of IL-6)(Zhang et al. 2025). More refined regulation is exemplified by monocarboxylate transporter 4 (MCT4)-mediated lactate transport: MCT4 deficiency promotes histone H3K18la, specifically activating the transcription of anti-inflammatory genes and TCA cycle genes, driving macrophage transition from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to a reparative M2 phenotype; this process is specifically regulated by lactylation rather than by methylation or acetylation (Zhang et al. 2024c). In hypoxia-induced PASMC proliferation, lactate accumulation-driven histone lactylation, particularly at the promoter regions of HIF-1α downstream target genes (such as Bmp5, Trpc5, and Kit), directly promotes transcriptional activation of pro-proliferative genes, thereby driving vascular remodeling (Chen et al. 2023).

Thus, the regulation of lactylation in PH forms a complete logical closed loop from “metabolic drive” to “epigenetic execution”: lactate accumulation resulting from metabolic abnormalities (thiamine inhibition, LONP1/MCT4 imbalance, mROS-HIF-1α axis activation) provides the substrate basis for histone lactylation. In turn, histone lactylation converts metabolic signals into sustained cellular phenotypic changes (cellular senescence, macrophage polarization, smooth muscle cell proliferation) by precisely regulating the transcription of key genes (such as prelamin A, M2 polarization-associated genes, and HIF-1α target genes), ultimately driving vascular remodeling and disease progression. The elucidation of this “metabolic-epigenetic” axis not only reveals a new dimension in the pathological mechanisms of PH but also provides a solid theoretical foundation and potential therapeutic targets for the development of innovative therapies targeting lactate metabolism or lactylation (such as MCT4 modulators, LDH inhibitors, and interventions targeting lactylation “writers/readers/erasers”).

Focus: core regulation of the immune microenvironment in PH by lactylation—with macrophage polarization as a hub

Macrophages are a focal point in PH research. CSF1R-positive macrophages abnormally accumulate around remodeled vessels in the lung tissues of PH patients, accompanied by elevated circulating levels of their ligands, CSF1 and IL-34. Interfering with CSF1R signaling reduces CCL2 production, thereby attenuating the pro-proliferative effect on smooth muscle cells (Gredic and Weissmann 2026). Notably, the MHCII(hi)LYVE1(lo)CCR2(hi) macrophage subset significantly infiltrates the tunica media in PH and, by secreting WNT11, activates the planar cell polarity pathway, inducing smooth muscle cells to transition toward a fibroblast-like phenotype, leading to medial collagen deposition and reduced vascular compliance (Qiu et al. 2025).

In addition to the specific subsets described above, the broader academic community widely adopts the M1/M2 dichotomy to describe macrophage functional polarization. M1 (classically activated) macrophages primarily secrete pro-inflammatory factors, exacerbating the initial inflammatory response, whereas M2 (alternatively activated) macrophages promote fibrosis and tissue repair through the production of factors such as TGF-β. This classification system, due to its simplicity and intuitiveness, serves as an important conceptual framework. However, its limitations are increasingly evident: in vivo, macrophages actually exist along a functional continuum, with pure M1 or M2 phenotypes being rare.

In PH, M2 macrophages aggregate around small pulmonary arteries and drive M2 polarization through the IL-6/IL-21 signaling axis (Hashimoto-Kataoka et al. 2015), CX3CL1/CX3CR1 axis (Amsellem et al. 2017), TGF-β/Smad2/3 axis (Ma et al. 2025), and IL-4/JAK/STAT6 axis, participating in pulmonary vascular remodeling (Yoshida et al. 2022). Intercellular regulation also plays a crucial role: in hypoxic environments, resident interstitial macrophages in lung tissue promote the recruitment of CCR2+ monocytes by expressing CCL2; these recruited monocytes then secrete thrombospondin 1 to activate the TGF-β pathway, driving vascular lesions (Kumar et al. 2025). Follicular helper T cells drive M2 macrophage polarization by secreting IL-21 and induce smooth muscle cell proliferation and pyroptosis (Li et al. 2024a).

Notably, PH macrophages exhibit a temporal polarization pattern: the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype appears in the early stages of the disease, whereas progression to the late stage is characterized by a predominance of pro-fibrotic, pro-remodeling M2 macrophages (Kumar et al. 2024). In CTEPH patients, non-polarized macrophages predominate in fresh thrombi, whereas the proportion of M2 macrophages characterized by a CD206+INOS− phenotype is significantly elevated in organized thrombi that have undergone fibrosis and remodeling (Koudstaal et al. 2024). These findings indicate that macrophages in PH display a complex, dynamic, and highly context-dependent polarization landscape, with their phenotype shifting precisely in a temporal and spatial manner according to disease stage and microenvironment.

Macrophage phenotypic remodeling is precisely regulated by lactylation in a highly context-dependent manner. In 2019, when first reporting histone lactylation, the Zhang Yingming team (Zhang) discovered that in the late phase of bacterially exposed M1 macrophage polarization, elevated histone lactylation induces homeostatic genes involved in wound healing, including arginase 1 (Arg1)—an important marker of M2 macrophages—suggesting that lactylation can directly drive macrophage transition from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to a reparative M2 phenotype. Studies in the tumor microenvironment further demonstrate that lactate-induced H3K18la in macrophages upregulates CCL18 expression, promotes disease progression (Sun et al. 2024a), and reinforces the immunosuppressive function of myeloid immune cells through the METTL3/m6A-YTHDF1 axis (Xiong et al. 2022). Given that the PH pulmonary vascular microenvironment is similarly characterized by enhanced aerobic glycolysis, lactate accumulation, and M2 macrophage enrichment—features highly similar to those of the tumor microenvironment—it is reasonable to speculate that M2 macrophage polarization in PH may likewise be regulated by histone lactylation. Furthermore, the functional maintenance and energy metabolic reprogramming of M2 macrophages after polarization may also involve non-histone lactylation—for example, lactylation of key metabolic enzymes or signaling proteins may further consolidate metabolic features associated with the M2 phenotype. This speculation requires further validation through PH-specific lactylomic studies and macrophage-specific conditional knockout models.

In contrast, in periodontitis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and other pathological states dominated by M1 pro-inflammatory responses, lactylation is associated with different functional outputs. For example, H3K18la levels are significantly elevated in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from ARDS patients and are positively correlated with inflammatory markers and disease severity (Li et al. 2025c). Targeting lactate metabolism and downstream lactylation effectively alters inflammatory outcomes: dexamethasone alleviates asthmatic airway inflammation by inhibiting the HIF-1α-glycolysis-lactate axis (Chen et al. 2024b), and the LDHA inhibitor GNE-140 attenuates PM2.5-induced pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis (Li et al. 2024c). These findings suggest that the function of lactylation is not fixed but rather acts as a “metabolic-epigenetic switch,” with its downstream effects determined by specific cell types, modification sites, and microenvironmental signals: driving pro-inflammatory programs in M1-type inflammation, and promoting repair and fibrosis in M2-type remodeling.

Notably, the regulation of macrophage phenotype by lactylation can occur early in their differentiation or even remotely. In a myocardial infarction model, before reaching the heart, monocytes already undergo H3K18la via MCT1-mediated lactate uptake, thereby pre-activating repair genes such as Lrg1 and Vegf-a (Wang et al. 2022b). This “remote preconditioning” mechanism may also exist in monocytes during early PH. Furthermore, lactylation of PKM2 at lysine 62 stabilizes its tetrameric conformation, suppresses the Warburg effect, and promotes the transition of macrophages toward a reparative phenotype (Wang et al. 2022a), illustrating a non-canonical pathway through which metabolic status directly influences immune function via altered enzyme activity (Fig. 2).

In summary, in chronic remodeling diseases such as PH, lactylation may primarily drive M2-like immunosuppressive and pro-fibrotic programs through histone H3K18 and non-histone METTL3 in the lactate-rich microenvironment; in acute inflammation, it may participate in pro-inflammatory regulation through different modification sites such as H3K9la and H3K14la. This context dependency highlights the importance of considering specific pathological backgrounds and cellular states when targeting lactylation therapeutically.

Summary and perspectives

The discovery of lactylation has broken the traditional view of metabolites merely as energy substrates or biosynthetic precursors, directly linking the glycolytic product lactate to epigenetic regulation and providing a completely new perspective for understanding the pathogenesis of PH. From histone H3K18la to non-histone ALDOB and Twist1, from PASMC proliferation and EndMT to M2 macrophage polarization, lactylation has demonstrated multi-layered, multi-cell-type regulatory potential in PH pulmonary vascular remodeling. Although this field is still in its infancy, existing evidence has outlined a complete logical chain from metabolic stress to epigenetic remodeling to cellular functional transformation, opening new directions for mechanistic research in PH.

However, it must be candidly acknowledged that current evidence for lactylation in PH remains largely concentrated on histone H3K18la and a few non-histone targets (such as ALDOB and Twist1), and most mechanistic inferences are derived from studies on tumors, fibrosis, and inflammatory diseases. Although existing evidence is suggestive, it remains relatively weak in establishing causal relationships, and there is an urgent need for PH-specific lactylomic analysis and cell-type-specific intervention experiments for validation. Looking forward, the following directions merit particular attention. First, construct a panoramic map of PH-specific lactylation modifications. Using high-resolution mass spectrometry and lactylation-specific antibodies, systematically identify the lactylation profiles of different cell types (endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts, macrophages) in PH lung tissue; clarify modification sites, substrates, and their dynamic changes; and provide foundational data for subsequent functional studies. Second, elucidate the causal role of lactylation in M2 macrophage polarization. Although evidence from the tumor microenvironment suggests a close association between lactylation and M2 polarization, this causal relationship has not yet been established in PH. Using macrophage-specific conditional knockout models (targeting lactylation “writers/erasers” such as p300 and HDACs), combined with single-cell transcriptomics and lactylomics, it is possible to directly verify whether and how lactylation drives M2 macrophage polarization and functional maintenance in PH. Third, explore the crosstalk mechanisms between lactylation and other epigenetic modifications. Different modifications such as lactylation, acetylation, and methylation may exhibit cross-regulation (e.g., sharing p300 as a “writer”). How these modifications synergistically or antagonistically regulate the expression of the same target genes in the PH microenvironment is a scientific question that urgently needs to be answered.

In conclusion, lactylation, as a key bridge connecting metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation, provides a novel paradigm for understanding the complex pathological mechanisms of PH. From “metabolite” to “signaling molecule” to “epigenetic modifier,” the functional understanding of lactate is undergoing a profound transformation. In PH—a disease characterized by metabolic dysregulation and immune imbalance—research on lactylation not only promises to reveal new pathogenic mechanisms but also has the potential to generate novel therapeutic strategies targeting the metabolic-epigenetic interaction network.
