A vertically integrated model with phase change for aquifers in cold firn
Mohammad Afzal Shadab, Howard A. Stone, Reed M. Maxwell

Abstract
Surface meltwater from glaciers and ice sheets contributes significantly to sea level rise, yet the dynamics governing its transport and retention in cold firn remain poorly constrained. We present a vertically integrated model that includes phase change during the migration of aquifers in cold firn and a constant residual trapping of liquid water. The model provides a unified framework connecting gravity-driven flows in temperate soils with those in cold firn, highlighting the analogous physics governing both systems. Analytical solutions are derived for constant volume aquifers and validated against numerical solutions that can be used to elucidate key features of meltwater dynamics and serve as benchmarks for firn hydrologic models. Finally, we demonstrate a three-dimensional expansion of an aquifer in cold, heterogeneous firn. The analytical and numerical solutions demonstrate that the dynamics of the laterally propagating aquifer slow at lower initial firn temperatures because of the enhanced reduction in porosity and the associated loss of liquid water. In summary, our framework offers insights into the formation and evolution of firn aquifers in percolation zones, which will help elucidate the role of modulating meltwater fluxes that affect surface mass loss and contribute to changes in global sea levels.

Introduction

Understanding the subsurface migration and retention of meltwater in sintered and compacted snow, called firn, on ice sheets is a central challenge in cryospheric hydrology
(
Team 2024
)
. Surface meltwater generated on glaciers and ice sheets may percolate into the porous firn layer where it can get stored as a liquid, refreeze, or propagate laterally as subsurface flow, thereby modulating the amount of water that eventually contributes to runoff and sea level rise
(
Harper et al. 2012
;
Meyer and Hewitt 2017
;
Team 2024
)
. In cold conditions, the interplay between percolation and refreezing alters pore-space structure, controlling meltwater pathways and storage
(
Colbeck 1972
;
Marsh and Woo 1984
;
Pfeffer and Humphrey 1996
)
. Classical experiments describe both the advance of a uniform wetting front and preferential flow through discrete channels or pipes, highlighting the sensitivity of infiltration to meltwater flux, initial temperature, density, and grain size
(
Williams et al. 2010
;
Humphrey et al. 2012
;
Avanzi et al. 2016
)
. Recent dye-tracing experiments have confirmed the occurrence of lateral and preferential flow within cold firn and snow, indicating complex three-dimensional meltwater redistribution
(
Humphrey et al. 2012
;
Clerx et al. 2022
)
. Thermal processes further modulate these flows as refreezing releases latent heat, warms the firn, and reduces porosity, often creating ice layers that impede subsequent infiltration
(
Humphrey et al. 2021
;
Shadab et al. 2024
)
. Modern multilayer firn models simulate meltwater percolation and refreezing using coupled thermodynamic and hydrologic formulations
(
Meyer and Hewitt 2017
;
Vandecrux et al. 2020
;
Ashmore et al. 2020
;
Moure et al. 2023
;
Shadab et al. 2024
;
Shadab et al. 2025b
)
, but they typically neglect lateral transport. Thus, a theoretical framework to describe lateral gravity-driven flow embedded within a thermally evolving, partially refreezing porous medium remains underdeveloped, particularly for expanding firn aquifers where heat transport, phase change, and pore closure strongly modulate flow dynamics.

Meltwater percolation in cold firn occurs through a combination of matrix flow, where the wetting front advances under gravity, and preferential flow, where focused pathways or channels transmit water rapidly through partially saturated firn
(
Marsh and Woo 1984
;
Williams et al. 2010
;
Humphrey et al. 2012
;
Avanzi et al. 2016
)
. The dominance of one regime over the other depends on firn temperature, saturation, density, stratification, heterogeneity, and grain structure, which together govern permeability and capillary retention
(
Pfeffer and Humphrey 1996
;
Vandecrux et al. 2020
)
. Preferential flow can promote deep infiltration even under subfreezing conditions, although refreezing along channel walls and pore constrictions can locally seal flow paths and generate ice layers that redirect subsequent percolation
(
Humphrey et al. 2012
;
Moure et al. 2023
;
Jones et al. 2024
)
. Observational studies using dye tracers and radar imaging have revealed that such heterogeneous infiltration patterns are widespread across temperate and cold firn zones
(
Pfeffer and Humphrey 1998
;
Marsh and Woo 1984
;
Harper et al. 2012
;
Humphrey et al. 2012
;
Wever et al. 2016
)
. While high fidelity firn models
(
Meyer and Hewitt 2017
;
Vandecrux et al. 2020
;
Ashmore et al. 2020
;
Shadab et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
can reproduce vertical percolation and refreezing, their one-dimensional (1D) structure limits the representation of lateral connectivity and flow redistribution. Consequently, the spatial extent and evolution of subsurface water storage, as well as the conditions under which firn aquifers laterally expand or dissipate, remain poorly constrained by existing models.

A critical limit to infiltration arises from freezing: in cold firn, a portion of infiltrating liquid water may refreeze within the pore space, reducing permeability and capacity for further flow
(
Colbeck 1976
;
Clark et al. 2017
;
Meyer and Hewitt 2017
;
Jones et al. 2024
;
Shadab et al. 2024
;
Shadab et al. 2025b
)
. These studies highlight the strong coupling between heat transfer, phase change, and fluid flow that governs the evolution of permeability and saturation in cold porous media. When lateral pressure gradients are present, water may spread laterally within a saturated layer, behaving as a gravity current within the porous medium. In the firn hydrology research community, firn aquifers have drawn increasing attention as they act to buffer meltwater input and modulate runoff timing and magnitude. Some observations of firn aquifers in Antarctica, Greenland, and Svalbard, along with their typical geometries, are summarized in Table
1
. Typically, firn aquifers are located tens of meters below the surface and are tens of meters thick. Their thickness changes over the seasons, and a higher melt supply increases their thickness. Figure
1
a
shows a firn aquifer in Antarctica, whereas Figure
1
b
illustrates the large extent of the perennial firn aquifer in Greenland. Warming will enhance melt production and accelerate the transition from accumulation to ablation, likely increasing their spatial extent over time
(
Miller et al. 2023
)
.

Although many models exist for firn aquifer dynamics in one (vertical) dimension (see numerical models in
Vandecrux et al. 2020
; theoretical model in
Shadab et al. 2025b
), there are few studies on modeling firn aquifers in two and three dimensions, as summarized in Table
2
. These studies include high-fidelity, vertically-integrated modeling or a point-wise approach between both. High fidelity models are computationally expensive and in some cases are intractable; therefore, vertically integrated models are utilized, for example, in the groundwater science community
(
Bear 1972
;
Pinder and Celia 2006
;
Shadab and Hesse 2024
)
. Extensive literature on gravity currents in porous media offers useful foundations
(
Benjamin 1968
;
Kochina et al. 1983
;
Huppert and Woods 1995
;
Simpson 1999
;
Ungarish 2009
;
Zheng and Stone 2022
)
. The proposed firn aquifer models directly implement the vertically integrated models for groundwater. However, to the best of our knowledge, existing firn hydrology models have not addressed lateral, groundwater-style flow in cold porous media with freezing, which produces changes in porosity and permeability. More specifically, thermodynamics has been coupled in vertically integrated models via 1D (vertical) firn hydrology; however, it has never been explicitly incorporated into the vertically integrated flow equations themselves. This represents a conceptual gap between gravity current theory in porous media and the cryospheric context, where heat transport and phase change, causing water loss and pore closure, strongly influence aquifer flow paths.

Here, we present a new vertically integrated modeling framework for aquifers in cold firn that accounts for phase change due to refreezing. The framework applies to regimes where capillary forces are negligible relative to gravitational and hydraulic forces, and where heat advection dominates heat conduction. The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we derive the governing equations for the lateral spreading of a saturated layer from the enthalpy and total water balance, incorporating residual trapping of liquid water (i.e., not all pore water is mobile) and porosity reduction due to refreezing. In Section
3
, we present analytical (self-similar) solutions for a constant-volume gravity current in cold firn, which are used for analysis and numerical validation. In Section
4
, we simulate aquifer propagation in cold firn with heterogeneous porosity and temperature fields. Section
5
discusses the model limitations, its connections to high-fidelity 1D firn hydrology models, and potential extensions to incorporate vertical resolution and evolving microstructure. Finally, Section
6
concludes the paper.

Our model contributes to firn hydrology by providing a low dimensional but physically grounded tool for predicting the lateral redistribution of meltwater in cold regions. Because lateral propagation can significantly alter the spatial extent and residence time of subsurface meltwater, our results have implications for meltwater retention, refreezing, and runoff partitioning in cold firn zones. This framework enables the development of fast and accurate firn hydrologic models and provides benchmarks to verify and validate existing firn models. It encapsulates previous firn aquifer problems and may be particularly useful in regions undergoing the formation of new firn aquifers.

We also assume that there is no loss of heat from the base of the aquifer. Assuming local thermodynamic equilibrium and neglecting the effect of heat conduction compared to heat advection, a simple model for a representative elemental volume (REV) involves refreezing liquid water when it invades a new pore space to warm the solid ice grains to the melting temperature, as shown in

However, liquid water is lost during pore invasion due to refreezing and during drainage due to the trapping of residual water in the pore space.

Solutions for expansion of a constant volume aquifer in cold firn

3.1
Analytical solutions in cartesian and cylindrical coordinates

We consider the late stage expansion of a constant volume aquifer at 0
∘
C in an otherwise cold porous medium at a uniform initial temperature
T_{0}\leq T_{m}
and porosity
\phi_{0}
. As a result, the reduction in porosity due to refreezing
\Delta\phi(\phi_{0},T_{0})
is constant. For the case where the height either only decreases or only increases with time, the governing equations can be rewritten in the following form in 1D cartesian (
x
) or cylindrical (
r
) coordinates, respectively, as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}=\begin{cases}{\kappa_{1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial h^{2}}{\partial x}\right)&\forall\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}\leq 0,\\
{\kappa}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial h^{2}}{\partial x}\right)&\forall\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}>0\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}=\begin{cases}\frac{\kappa_{1}}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial h^{2}}{\partial r}\right)&\forall\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}\leq 0\\
\frac{\kappa}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial h^{2}}{\partial r}\right)&\forall\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}>0\\
\end{cases}

(4)

where
\kappa_{1}=\frac{K(\phi^{\prime})}{2\phi^{\prime}(s_{s}-s_{r})}
and
\kappa=\frac{K(\phi^{\prime})}{2\left[\phi^{\prime}s_{s}+\left(\frac{\rho_{i}}{\rho_{w}}\Delta\phi\right)\right]}
.

We consider an evolving volume of liquid water in the aquifer
Q(t)
defined in cartesian (in m
2
) and cylindrical coordinates (in m
3
) as

\displaystyle Q(t)=\phi^{\prime}s_{s}\int_{0}^{x_{max}(t)}h(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{and}\quad Q(t)=\phi^{\prime}s_{s}2\pi\int_{0}^{r_{max}(t)}rh(r,t)\mathrm{d}r,

(5)

respectively, with

and

being the maximum horizontal extents of the aquifer (m). As

The self-similar solution for this problem (
4
-
5

\displaystyle h=\frac{B^{2}}{\kappa_{1}t^{1-2\beta}}\Phi\left(\zeta,\frac{\kappa}{\kappa_{1}}\right),\quad x\,\text{ or }\,r=\zeta Bt^{\beta}

(6)

in cartesian or cylindrical coordinates, respectively. Here
\Phi

5
Discussion and limitations

The theoretical framework developed here provides a foundation for understanding the interplay between aquifer dynamics, heat transport, and phase change in firn aquifers. In particular, for problems where the firn is initially warmed to the melting temperature when initially invaded, the resulting change in porosity
(\Delta\phi)
and reduction in water volume can be leveraged as a condition to solve a range of gravity current problems, such as late-stage drainage or a constant flux gravity current. Furthermore, for a steady state the same equations could be used with a reduced porosity such as a steady gravity current in porous media. A compiled set of such problems in soil is presented in
Shadab and Hesse 2024
, offering a suite of simplified yet physically consistent formulations that can be employed as benchmark solutions. These limiting-case scenarios are especially useful for validating and diagnosing high-fidelity firn hydrology simulators such as
Miller et al. 2022
, where coupled thermodynamics, multiphase flow, and evolving microstructure interact in complex ways. Further, in addition to being implemented in prior models for temperate firn, the present model extends the framework to problems where firn aquifers invade cold regions, a process which has not been studied previously and will be increasingly important due to warming causing expansion of firn aquifers as suggested by studies such as
Horlings et al. 2022
;
Brils et al. 2024
.

From a numerical standpoint, realistic simulations must account for spatial heterogeneity in temperature, thermal conductivity, and permeability. Allowing each computational cell to possess distinct thermophysical properties captures the natural variability present in firn columns, where layering and firn metamorphism modulate energy and water mass transfer. Extending this framework enables the exploration of evolving porous media, where porosity evolves dynamically due to deposition, dissolution, or refreezing reactions.

The framework in Section
2
elucidates how ice layer morphology influences the dynamics of aquifers in firn. Thin, discontinuous ice layers may not substantially impede lateral spreading, allowing the aquifer to remain largely unconfined, as observed in
Killingbeck et al. 2020
. In contrast, thicker and more continuous ice slabs can compartmentalize the pore space, leading to the formation of confined aquifers and localized pressurization. Such structural transitions are controlled in part by heat conduction, which governs the refreezing of percolating meltwater and the subsequent stratification of ice layers. Enhanced conduction can promote ice layer growth, reducing aquifer propagation through refreezing-induced water loss and lowering hydraulic head.

The sharp-interface approximation is crucial, provided that the Peclet numbers associated with liquid water diffusion via capillary forces and heat loss via heat conduction are considered large. The Peclet number is defined as the ratio of advective transport to diffusive transport of a quantity. In unsaturated region above the water table, capillary forces can lead to suction of water out of the water table, forming unsaturated regions with with saturation
s_{s}<s_{w}<s_{r}
. For large scales and regions where the Peclet number for hydrology
Pe_{w}
is high, i.e., gravitational forces dominate over capillary forces, the sharp interface approximation is justified, leading to no unsaturated regions. As a result, the saturation can only take two values, i.e.,
s_{w}\in\{s_{r},s_{s}\}
. This is a good approximation for large spatial scales and firn having a larger grain size. A more detailed discussion on variably saturated flows with sharp interfaces can be found in
Shadab and Hesse 2022
;
Shadab and Hesse 2024
for soil and
Shadab et al. 2025b
for glacial firn.

Next, we discuss the sharp-interface assumption and its limitations for heat transport. The thermal diffusivity of ice
\alpha\approx 10^{-6}
m
2
/s at 0
∘
C
(
James 1968
)
. For porous firn, a prefactor of 0.25-1 could be applied to the thermal diffusivity to account for the thermal diffusivity of cold firn for a porosity between 0 and 0.7 (e.g., the factor being
(1-\phi)^{l-1}
; see
Shadab et al. 2024
with exponent
l=1.885
taken from
Yin-Chao Yen 1981
). In the vertical direction, the heat conduction length scales from the scaling
\sqrt{\alpha t}
are on the order of one meter at yearly timescales. If the length scales for heat advection along with the aquifer motion are on the order of ten meters or more in a year, conduction could be considered small. But if the vertical motion of the aquifer is on the order of one meter per year or less, then heat conduction would be prominent. In the case where heat conduction is dominant, the loss of water due to conduction could be accounted for as a sink term in the governing equations (
1
-
2
). This is an excellent direction for future work.

Latent heat provides dominant thermal buffering for aquifer thermodynamics. Because water’s latent heat of fusion far exceeds firn’s sensible heat capacity, small amounts of refreezing warm the surrounding firn and maintain near-isothermal conditions at the melting point. This self-limiting mechanism for conductive heat loss keeps deep firn aquifers thermally stable despite seasonal surface forcing. Field observations confirm this behavior: deep firn aquifers show minimal seasonal temperature variation and remain near the melting point year-round
(
Koenig et al. 2014
;
Miller et al. 2018
;
Miller et al. 2020
;
Van Den Akker et al. 2025
;
Miller et al. 2023
)
. For the firn where the aquifer reaches close to the surface, climate effects on the firn aquifer dynamics are much more prominent and need to be accounted for, as pointed out in
Van Den Akker et al. 2025
. This new vertically integrated model can easily be coupled with a 1D firn model for localized heat conduction, as was done in
Van Den Akker et al. 2025
. A source term in the model equation (
2
) can account for aquifer recharge but will lead to more intricate thermodynamics impacting the maximum aquifer thickness
h_{max}
, defined in Equation (
3
).

Importantly, these insights demonstrate the efficiency and utility of vertically integrated models in capturing the bulk behavior of firn aquifers while maintaining computational tractability. By integrating the governing equations over depth, these models naturally incorporate effective source terms derived from surface boundary conditions in the full Richards equation, linking recharge, drainage, and refreezing processes in a reduced-dimensional framework. This approach preserves the dominant physics, particularly mass and energy conservation, while substantially reducing the degrees of freedom relative to fully three-dimensional multiphase flow models. As a result, vertically integrated formulations offer a powerful balance between physical fidelity and computational efficiency, making them ideally suited for long-term simulations, sensitivity analysis, and benchmarking exercises. They can also serve as foundational components for hybrid models that couple surface energy balance, hydrologic routing, and thermodynamic evolution at larger spatial and temporal scales.

Overall, the results highlight the importance of combining analytical limits, vertically integrated formulations, and numerical experiments to unravel the coupled processes of heat transport, phase change, and evolving porosity in firn hydrology. The simplified problems outlined here, when used as benchmark tests, can help evaluate the fidelity of multiphase flow models under well-defined physical conditions, guiding the development of more robust and predictive firn hydrology simulators.

6
Conclusions

We developed a vertically integrated model coupling meltwater dynamics, heat transport, and phase change for firn aquifers. The framework unifies temperate and cold aquifer systems by explicitly accounting for meltwater refreezing caused by heat advection and residual water trapping. Analytical and numerical solutions demonstrate that colder initial temperatures and residual trapping both slow lateral aquifer expansion through enhanced refreezing and water loss. This is the first model to represent lateral groundwater-style flow with freezing in firn, linking porosity evolution, permeability, and heat transfer. By integrating over depth, it captures meltwater drainage and refreezing in a reduced-dimensional form that preserves mass and energy conservation while remaining computationally efficient. These results establish a foundation for benchmarking firn hydrology simulators and investigating how aquifer formation, ice-layer structure, and phase change govern meltwater storage. The framework offers new insights into the evolution of firn aquifers in and beyond percolation zones and their role in modulating surface mass loss and sea-level rise.
