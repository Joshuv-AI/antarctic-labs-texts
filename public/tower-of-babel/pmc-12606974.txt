The effects of progesterone supplementation in pregnancies assessed by doppler ultrasound: a systematic review of maternal and perinatal outcomes
Authors: Somayeh Khanjani, Sheida Bashiri Goodarzi, Mohammad Mehdi Khaleghi, Seyyede Sepide Ashraf Moosavi, Reza Eshraghi, MohammadSaeed Soleimani Meigoli, Mobina Fathi, Maryam Nasr Azadani, Nazanin Sadraei, Ashkan Bahrami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProgesterone is essential for pregnancy maintenance, but its effects on complications like intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), preeclampsia, prelabor rupture of membranes (PROM), preterm birth, and placental abruption are still debated. This review investigates the effects of progesterone supplementation on Doppler ultrasound indices and pregnancy outcomes.Material and methodsTo assess the efficacy of progesterone supplements in reducing pregnancy complications during the first and second trimesters using Doppler ultrasound indices. Databases such as PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar were searched from inception to March 2024. Cohort, case–control, and clinical trials on progesterone supplementation in early to mid-pregnancy were included in the review. The primary outcomes were Doppler ultrasound indices, with secondary outcomes including preterm birth, IUGR, preeclampsia, PROM, and placental abruption. Nine studies with 2282 women were examined. Independent reviewers extracted data and assessed its quality, resolving discrepancies through consensus.ResultsProgesterone supplementation had varying effects on Doppler indices. There was no significant difference in uterine or umbilical artery Doppler indices, but some studies reported changes in fetal middle cerebral artery flow. Furthermore, there were no significant improvements in the rates of IUGR, preeclampsia, or PROM.ConclusionsProgesterone supplementation in early and mid-pregnancy may benefit certain subgroups, particularly those at risk of preterm birth. However, its overall effectiveness in improving clinical outcomes is unclear. More research using standardized progesterone regimens is needed to better understand its role in pregnancy management. Progesterone supplementation may help prevent preterm birth in high-risk populations, such as women with a short cervix. However, Doppler ultrasound findings should not be the sole factor in guiding its use. However, its role in improving other clinical outcomes, such as IUGR and preeclampsia, remains uncertain. Obstetricians should consider progesterone use as part of individualized treatment plans, particularly for women at high risk for preterm birth.Keywords: Doppler ultrasound, Progesterone, Preterm birth, Preeclampsia, Intrauterine growth restriction, Premature rupture of membranes

Introduction

During pregnancy, various life-threatening conditions may occur, such as intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), preeclampsia, prelabor rupture of membranes (PROM), preterm birth, and placenta abruption [1, 2].

IUGR or fetal growth restriction (FGR), restricted fetal growth in utero relative to gestational age and or genetic potential is typically defined using an estimated fetal weight or abdominal circumference below the 10th centile [3]. By definition, ~ 10% of fetuses meet this threshold. Its consequences can be evident at birth and in the neonatal period, but the condition itself occurs during pregnancy [3, 4]. Preeclampsia, a hypertensive disorder complicating 2–8% of pregnancies, is another major cause of morbidity [5]. Prelabor rupture of membranes (PROM), including preterm PROM (< 37 weeks), complicates 5–10% of pregnancies and increases the risk of infection, maternal morbidity, and preterm birth [6–8].

In 2020, an estimated 13.4 million infants were delivered preterm, accounting for ~ 10% of births globally [9].

Another less common yet more fatal condition is Placental abruption, the premature detachment of the placenta, which complicates 0.4–1% of pregnancies and remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity and perinatal death [10, 11].

Understanding these conditions and finding effective interventions are critical for improving pregnancy outcomes.

Given the burden of these complications, Doppler ultrasound has become a critical tool in evaluating fetal and maternal health. The described pregnancy complications can be assessed and evaluated via ultrasound and radiological techniques and criteria, such as an examination of the middle cerebral artery (MCA), uterine artery Doppler, and umbilical artery Doppler indices (UADIs) in arterial and venous flow or flow resistance. Doppler sonography is a noninvasive imaging technique that measures blood flow in a variety of fetal and maternal arteries.

MCA assessment demonstrates the brain-sparing effect, with compensatory vasodilation in response to hypoxia. Cerebroplacental ratio improves sensitivity and specificity for detecting this manifestation. About 80% of brain blood flow comes from the MCAs, making them a key indicator of prenatal brain health and development [12]. Uterine artery Doppler can predict preeclampsia and IUGR by detecting high resistance or abnormal waveforms indicating insufficient placentation. The uterine artery Doppler index (UADI) helps identify placental insufficiency, early fetal abnormalities, and potential adverse outcomes [13, 14]. Umbilical artery Doppler examination of umbilical artery blood flow can be used to identify fetal distress and the time of birth. Doppler indices detect fetal and maternal issues early, enabling faster intervention and improved results.

Progesterone is an endogenous hormone secreted by the corpus luteum and is necessary for the establishment and maintenance of a healthy pregnancy [4]. Progesterone prepares the uterus’s tissue lining (the endometrium) for implantation while also stimulating endometrial glands to create nutrition for the growing fetus. The corpus luteum releases progesterone during the first 8 weeks of pregnancy; however, between 8 and 12 weeks, the placenta takes control, and the pregnancy continues [4]. Exogenous progesterone can be administered in certain cases during pregnancy for specific therapeutic causes that impact both the mother and the fetus, and research into these effects is ongoing [5, 6].

Because of the importance of progesterone throughout pregnancy, many physicians believe that a lack of progesterone causes recurrent miscarriage (RM) and that first-trimester progesterone medication can reduce risk [6, 7]. In 2003, the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (RCOG) guideline1 and Cochrane review2 recommended a trial to evaluate whether first-trimester progesterone treatment could reduce the risk of miscarriage in women with unexplained RM [8].

According to previous studies, progesterone has no significant effect on the uterine artery pulsatility index (PI). However, further prospective trials with all potential complicating factors, including serum progesterone levels, are needed to address this issue [9]. One of the reasons for conducting this study in the form of a systematic review is to address the existing gap due to the lack of specific guidelines regarding the administration of progesterone supplements during pregnancy and its benefits, which results in the form of a summary and a comprehensive view. This systematic review aims to establish how supplementation with progesterone affects criteria for Doppler ultrasound, including the uterine artery, umbilical artery, and indices of the MCA, with related pregnancy complications. The findings emphasize the need to reduce progesterone overuse or misuse in the first and second trimesters, as well as the importance of proper application on the basis of evidence-based indications. Correct progesterone use can enhance outcomes determined by Doppler ultrasound, leading to better clinical practice guidelines and reducing injuries to both mothers and fetuses.

Materials and methods

Purpose

The purpose of this systematic review was to assess the effects of progesterone supplementation on a range of Doppler indices and pregnancy complications in the first and second trimesters. The methods used for this review were those suggested by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [10] and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Intervention [11] and also with PROSPERO registration: CRD420251147097 (available in: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251147097).

Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was employed across multiple databases, including Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and PubMed. The search covered a 30-year period from inception to March 2024. Keywords and MeSH terms such as “progesterone,” “uterine artery Doppler index,” “umbilical artery index,” “MCA index,” “IUGR,” “preeclampsia,” “PROM,” “preterm birth,” and “placenta abruption” were used to identify relevant studies. Additionally, reference lists of identified papers and previously published systematic reviews were manually searched. The search strategy incorporated a combination of main keywords, MeSH terms, and Emtree terms to ensure thorough identification of relevant studies. The keywords used included “progesterone,” “uterine artery Doppler index,” “umbilical artery index,” “MCA index,” “IUGR,” “preeclampsia,” “PROM,” “preterm birth,” and “placenta abruption.” Additionally, manual searches of reference lists from identified papers, previously published systematic reviews, and obstetrics and maternal–fetal medicine conference proceedings were conducted to capture all pertinent literature. This query was applied to databases (“progesterone” AND (“uterine artery Doppler index” OR “umbilical artery index” OR “MCA index” OR “middle cerebral artery Doppler indices” OR “middle cerebral artery Doppler flow” OR “umbilical artery Doppler flow” OR “uterine artery Doppler flow” OR “Doppler blood flow velocity” OR “prenatal ultrasonic diagnosis” OR “fetal ultrasonography”)) AND (“IUGR” OR “intrauterine growth restriction” OR “preeclampsia” OR “PROM” OR “prelabor rupture of membranes” OR “preterm birth” OR “placenta abruption”).

Eligibility criteria

The review for inclusion, we considered original research articles in the form of randomized controlled trials, clinical trials, cohort studies, and case–control studies, provided they were published in English and available in full text. Eligible participants were pregnant women in the first or second trimester, without a recent history of miscarriage and without prior or concurrent progesterone supplementation. Only studies that explicitly reported relevant primary and secondary outcomes were included. The primary outcomes were defined as ultrasound and Doppler indices that could reflect placental or fetal circulation. These included prenatal ultrasonographic parameters, fetal biometric assessments, uterine artery Doppler indices (e.g., pulsatility index and resistance index), middle cerebral artery Doppler indices and flow patterns, umbilical artery Doppler flow, uterine artery blood flow, cerebroplacental ratio, and Doppler blood flow velocities. Studies were also eligible if they reported combined indices such as the uterine artery notch or cerebroplacental Doppler ratio, which are increasingly used in perinatal research. The secondary outcomes encompassed clinically significant maternal and neonatal endpoints. These included intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, preterm prelabor rupture of membranes (PROM/PPROM), preterm birth (< 34 or < 37 weeks), placental abruption, neonatal morbidity (e.g., respiratory distress syndrome, intraventricular hemorrhage, necrotizing enterocolitis), neonatal mortality, and admission to neonatal intensive care.

Exclusion criteria were defined to reduce heterogeneity and ensure clinical relevance. We excluded conference abstracts, systematic reviews, case reports, case series, meta-analyses, animal studies, and studies without accessible full text. Furthermore, studies were excluded if they were restricted to pregnancies in the third trimester, if participants had a history of recent miscarriage, or if prior or concurrent progesterone supplementation was administered before the intervention under study. By restricting inclusion to studies that directly assessed these Doppler and clinical outcomes, we aimed to capture evidence most relevant to the effects of progesterone supplementation initiated in the first or second trimester.

Main outcome measures

This review primarily assessed ultrasound and Doppler indices in pregnant women who had no recent history of miscarriage or prior/prolonged progesterone supplementation. The key parameters examined included prenatal ultrasonography, uterine artery Doppler indices (such as pulsatility index (PI) and resistance index (RI)), middle cerebral artery Doppler indices and flow, umbilical artery Doppler flow, and Doppler blood flow velocities. Secondary outcomes included intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), preeclampsia, preterm birth (PTB), prelabor rupture of membranes (PROM), and placental abruption. Due to the variability in how outcomes were defined across studies, we recorded these variables as described by the original authors. For instance, PTB was defined variably as delivery before 34 or 37 weeks of gestation. Significant changes in Doppler indices were considered when studies reported uterine artery PI or other Doppler indices above the 95th percentile for gestational age, the presence of an early diastolic notch, or relative changes as specified by the authors. Regarding uterine artery PI, we incorporated the updated reference ranges based on fractional polynomials as proposed by Cavoretto et al., which provide gestational age-specific norms derived from serial Doppler measurements throughout the first to third trimesters [12]. To further clarify, we grouped studies based on whether they utilized percentile-based thresholds or notching criteria.

Screening process and data extraction

The screening and data extraction were carried out independently and in parallel by two co-authors (A.B. and R.E. for study screening, M.K. and S.S. for data extraction), with all reviewers blinded to each other’s decisions to minimize bias. To ensure consistency and accuracy, discrepancies were resolved through discussions between the two initial reviewers, and where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (M.F. for screening, A.B. for data extraction) was consulted for arbitration. To address potential issues such as errors or subjective bias in the data extraction process, a dual-reviewer approach was used. This approach involved two independent reviewers conducting the data extraction simultaneously, with discrepancies resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (for final adjudication) when necessary. The data extracted included study characteristics, demographic variables (such as publication date, country of origin, maternal age, gestational age at the time of intervention, fetal age, and other relevant baseline characteristics), study type, and the form and dosage of progesterone supplementation. Additionally, we extracted primary outcomes, including Doppler indices (e.g., uterine artery, umbilical artery, middle cerebral artery) and secondary outcomes such as IUGR, preeclampsia, PROM, preterm birth, placental abruption, and neonatal mortality.

Quality assessment and risk of bias

The quality of cohort and case–control studies was assessed using the ROBINS-I (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions) tool. This tool evaluates studies across seven domains: bias due to confounding, bias in participant selection, bias in classification of interventions, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing data, bias in outcome measurement, and bias in the selection of the reported result. Each domain is rated as “low,” “moderate,” or “high” risk of bias, and studies with a low risk of bias in all domains were deemed to be of high quality [13] (Fig. 1). Clinical trials were evaluated via the Revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (RoB 2) [14], with assessments performed independently by M.K. and discrepancies resolved through consensus (Table 1).

Data synthesis

Data synthesis involved both qualitative and quantitative methods to determine the impact of progesterone supplementation on Doppler indices and pregnancy outcomes. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study features and findings, ensuring a comprehensive evaluation of the potential benefits and risks of progesterone in early to mid-pregnancy. This systematic approach ensures a thorough understanding of existing data on progesterone supplementation, contributing to a better understanding of its effects on pregnancy Doppler findings, outcomes and related complications.

Results

Study selection

A database search yielded a total of 215 records. After eliminating duplicate articles, the authors screened titles and abstracts, and approximately 137 of the screened articles remained. Finally, nine articles were included in our systematic review after their full texts were assessed (Fig. 2).

Prenatal ultrasonography

In the study of Barda et al. [23], 35 women were found to have a short cervix, with a cervical length ≤ 15 mm in 13 (37.1%) women and 16–25 mm in 22 (62.9%) women. Vafai et al. [15] found that 13 participants (37.1%) had a short cervix, i.e., a cervical length ≤ 25 mm. Norman et al. [21] reported that the proportion of babies with neonatal brain injuries on cerebral ultrasound scanning was lower in the progesterone group (18 [3%] of 584 vs. 34 [6%] of 574; OR 0·50, 95% CI 0·31–0·84), and a reduction in brain injury was also observed in a sensitivity analysis restricted to participants who underwent neonatal brain scans (n = 776; OR 0·54, 95% CI 0·32–0·88).

Resistance index (RI)

The effects of progesterone on uterine artery RI varied across the studies included in the review. Some studies observed no significant changes in uterine artery RI, while others reported significant differences. For example, Maged et al. found no significant changes in the uterine artery RI, with values remaining virtually the same before (0.66 ± 0.12) and after progesterone administration (0.66 ± 0.107; P = 0.770). This suggests that progesterone supplementation did not have a measurable effect on the uterine artery RI in their cohort [17]. In contrast, Barinov et al. demonstrated a significant difference in uterine artery RI following progesterone treatment. This finding indicates that progesterone may influence vascular resistance in some pregnancies, although the exact mechanism remains unclear [19]. Similarly, Jamal et al. [14] reported significant reductions in uterine artery RI on both sides: the right uterine artery RI decreased from 0.76 ± 0.09 before treatment to 0.60 ± 0.11 after treatment (P < 0.001), and the left uterine artery RI showed a similar decrease, from 0.76 ± 0.10 to 0.60 ± 0.11 (P < 0.001) [16]. These significant changes suggest a potential vasodilatory effect of progesterone on uterine blood flow.

Uterine artery PI

The effects of progesterone on uterine artery pulsatility index (PI) were inconsistent across studies. Some studies (about 2) found no significant changes [17, 22], while others (2 studies) reported significant reductions in PI following progesterone treatment [16, 23]. For instance, Maged et al. observed no significant difference in uterine artery PI after progesterone supplementation. The PI remained nearly identical before (1.00 ± 0.26) and after treatment (1.016 ± 0.24; P = 0.531), suggesting that progesterone had little to no effect on uterine artery blood flow in their cohort [17]. Similarly, Çintesun et al. reported that progesterone had no significant impact on uterine artery PI, with a mean PI of 1.71 ± 0.40 in the progesterone group (P = 0.184) [22]. In contrast, Badr found a significant decrease in uterine artery PI following progesterone administration. The PI decreased from 0.969 ± 0.27 to 0.817 ± 0.16 (P = 0.000), indicating a potential effect of progesterone in improving uterine blood flow. This effect was observed across different gestational periods: between weeks 18–23 (from 0.914 ± 0.14 to 0.782 ± 0.16, t = 6.72, P = 0.001), weeks 24–29 (from 0.962 ± 0.18 to 0.824 ± 0.17, t = 3.16, P = 0.027), and weeks 30–35 (from 0.968 ± 0.14 to 0.816 ± 0.12, t = 3.85, P = 0.014). Notably, uterine artery PI was significantly correlated with gestational age both before and after progesterone treatment (P < 0.05) [18]. Jamal et al. also reported significant changes in uterine artery PI, with the right uterine artery PI decreasing from 1.82 ± 0.51 before treatment to 1.12 ± 0.39 after treatment (P < 0.001) and the left uterine artery PI decreasing from 1.87 ± 0.56 to 1.06 ± 0.31 (P < 0.001), indicating a notable effect of progesterone on uterine vascular resistance [16]. Also Agra et al. found no significant differences in the mean uterine artery PI between the progesterone and placebo groups from 18 to 34 weeks and 6 days of gestation (P > 0.05). Additionally, there was no significant difference in uterine artery PI variation between the two groups at any point throughout gestation or post-randomization (P > 0.05) [20].

Umbilical artery

Doppler flow

Across three studies evaluating umbilical artery Doppler, no significant change was detected following progesterone [15, 21, 23]. Barda et al. reported no significant changes [23]. Vafaei et al. likewise found no pre–post difference (before progesterone: 1.02, after progesterone: 0.99; P = 0.41) [15]. In the randomized trial by Norman et al., the proportion with umbilical artery Doppler > 95th percentile was 6% in the placebo group versus 5% in the progesterone group, a non-significant difference [21]. Taken together, 3 of 3 studies showed no significant effect of progesterone on umbilical artery Doppler flow.

Umbilical artery RI

Maged et al. [17] did not observe any significant changes in the umbilical artery RI (before progesterone: 0.69 ± 0.049; after progesterone: 0.68 ± 0.041; P = 0.077). Vafaei et al. [15] reported that progesterone treatment promoted a higher umbilical artery RI at various gestational ages. Overall, one-half of the studies reported no significant change in umbilical artery RI, while the other half observed an increase in RI following progesterone treatment.

Umbilical arteries PI

Several studies evaluated the effects of progesterone on umbilical artery PI, with most reporting no significant changes. In a study by Maged et al. [17], following the use of progesterone, no significant difference was detected in the umbilical artery PIs (before progesterone: 1.14 ± 0.118; after progesterone: 1.11 ± 0.116; P = 0.053). The studied women showed no measurable difference (P > 0.05) in umbilical artery PI (pretreatment: 0.870 ± 0.18; posttreatment: 0.863 ± 0.18). During the three gestation periods studied, there was no considerable change in the umbilical artery PI (P > 0.05) among the women. The specific PI values for weeks 18–23, 24–29, and 30–35 are 0.881 ± 0.19 to 0.853 ± 0.16, t = 1.26 and P = 0.473, 0.864 ± 0.18 to 0.852 ± 0.20, t = 0.87 and P = 0.621, and 0.870 ± 0.16 to 0.863 ± 0.16, t = 0.63 and P = 0.890, respectively, and only for the weeks 18 to 23, progesterone can decrease the PI statistically significant [18].

Fetal middle cerebral artery (MCA)

Doppler flow

Studies on the effect of progesterone on MCA PI yielded mixed results. There was a significant decrease in the pulsatility index (PI) of the fetal MCA by 18.2% (mean change in the PI: 0.44, 95% CI 0.25–0.63, P < 0.001), indicating a vasodilatory effect on fetal circulation [23]. There were no significant changes in this index following progesterone administration in a study by Vafaei et al. (before progesterone: 2.39, after progesterone: 2.39; P = 0.97) [15]. In total, half of the studies observed a significant decrease in MCA PI [23], while the other half reported no significant changes following progesterone treatment [15].

MCA RI

Barinov et al. [19] demonstrated that patients receiving progesterone tend to have greater resistance in the middle cerebral artery (MCA) index.

MCA-PI

In a study by Maged et al. [17], following the use of progesterone, no significant difference was detected in the MCA PI (before progesterone: 1.27 ± 0.18, after progesterone: 1.26 ± 0.23; P = 0.553). The studied women showed no measurable difference (P > 0.05) in MCA Doppler PI (pretreatment: 1.882 ± 0.286; posttreatment: 1.880 ± 0.286). No statistically significant shifts (P > 0.05) in MCA Doppler PI occurred during different gestation periods (weeks) for the studied women: weeks 18–23 (1.771 ± 0.33–1.766 ± 0.32, t = 0.11, P = 0.473), weeks 24–29 (1.905 ± 0.07–1.903 ± 0.07, t = 0.02, P = 0.973), and weeks 30–35 (1.880 ± 0.06–1.878 ± 0.07, t = 0.03, P = 0.641), also the progesterone has been shown to significantly reduce the PI only during weeks 18 to 23 of pregnancy. The relationship between the umbilical and MCA Doppler PIs and gestational age was not significant (P > 0.05) [18]. Both studies observed no significant change in MCA PI after progesterone treatment.

MCA-Peak systolic velocity (PSV)

In 44 pregnancies with threatened preterm birth, as reported by Barda et al., there was no significant change in the fetal MCA-PSV before or after progesterone administration (before progesterone: 36.0 ± 11.1; after progesterone: 36.79 ± 10.8; P = 0.598), and there were no significant changes in the Doppler flow parameters of the maternal uterine arteries. In women who subsequently delivered at term or preterm (< 37 weeks), there was no significant change in the fetal MCA-PSV from before to after progesterone administration (preterm delivery (n = 17): 0.03 ± 0.37; term delivery (n = 27): 0.02 ± 0.21; P = 0.531), and in any of the blood vessels studied, there was no significant difference in the change in Doppler flow parameters from before to after progesterone treatment between women who gave birth at term and those who gave birth preterm [23]. Overall there was no significant change found in fetal MCA-PSV following progesterone treatment.

Preterm delivery

Barda et al. reported that 17 women (38.6%) gave birth before 37 weeks of gestational age, including nine (20.4%) who gave birth before 34 weeks. Preterm delivery was documented in seven of the 15 (46.6%) patients with a previous history of preterm delivery and 11 of the 28 (31.4%) patients with an incidental finding of a short cervix [23]. Barinov et al. reported that 36.7% (50 women) gave birth before 37 weeks, 23.5% (32 women) before 34 weeks, and 13.2% (18 women) between 34 and 36.9 weeks [19]. Norman et al. [21] reported that progesterone did not significantly reduce the overall risk of preterm birth. Specific subgroups, such as women with short cervixes, showed some benefit, but the overall effect was weak and inconsistent across different studies. The Maged et al. [17] study involved women at risk for preterm birth, but their results indicated no significant changes in Doppler indices; therefore, direct effects on preterm birth prevention cannot be concluded.

Comparison by delivery term

No significant differences in Doppler flow parameters were noted between women who delivered at term and those who delivered preterm, both before and after progesterone treatment [23].

Intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR)

Norman et al. [21] measured fetal abdominal circumference as a marker for IUGR and found that 22% of individuals in the placebo group and 32% of those in the progesterone group had an abdominal circumference below the 5th percentile, indicating no significant improvement with progesterone. Maged et al. [17] did not directly examine the patients in this study, but their results revealed no significant impact on blood flow in the major fetal and uterine arteries, suggesting that IUGR has no effect. Although Çintesun et al. [22] did not directly mention the effect of progesterone on IUGR, since the PI is a predictive tool for IUGR and progesterone does not affect the PI, it seems that progesterone may not affect IUGR.

Preeclampsia

The incidence of preeclampsia in the study by Norman et al. [21] was similar between the groups, with 2% in both the placebo and progesterone groups, suggesting that progesterone had no significant effect on reducing preeclampsia risk. Maged et al. [17] did not directly examine preeclampsia, but they observed no significant changes in the Doppler indices of major arteries, suggesting that progesterone may not affect preeclampsia prevention or treatment. Although Çintesun et al. [22] did not directly mention the effect of progesterone on preeclampsia, since the PI is a predictive tool for preeclampsia and progesterone does not affect the PI, it seems that progesterone may not affect preeclampsia.

Premature rupture of membranes (PROM)

Norman et al. [21] reported that the rates of preterm premature rupture of membranes (PROM) were 12% in the placebo group and 11% in the placebo and progesterone groups, respectively, with no significant difference.

Other variables

In the progesterone group, the risk of pregnancy loss was 55.1%, nonspecific vaginitis was 16.9%, moderate and severe preeclampsia was 13.2%, bleeding during delivery was 33.1%, and intrauterine infection was 23.5% [19].

Discussion

In this systematic review, nine studies with a total of two thousand two hundred and eighty-two pregnant women were surveyed to determine whether progesterone supplementation affects Doppler indices and pregnancy complications. The findings contradict each other, demonstrating the complexities of progesterone’s effects on maternal and fetal outcomes. The studies reviewed revealed conflicting results regarding the effects of progesterone on uterine, umbilical, and fetal middle cerebral artery (MCA) Doppler indices. On the one hand, some studies, such as those by Vafaei et al. and Barinov et al., reported significant reductions in uterine resistance and pulsatility indices following progesterone treatment; however, Barda et al. and Maged et al. found no significant changes. Nonetheless, one study found a decrease in the MCA pulsatility index, which could indicate a potential vasodilation effect on fetal circulation but does not appear to be consistent across all studies. Also, the review found that progesterone supplementation had limited effects on major pregnancy complications. However, there was no significant decrease in the incidence of IUGR, preeclampsia, or preterm PROM. However, some subpopulations, particularly those with a short cervix, appeared to benefit from progesterone supplementation by having a lower risk of preterm birth. However, this effect was not observed in every study. The review concludes that, while progesterone may benefit certain high-risk groups, there is no definitive conclusion on its overall effect on Doppler indices and pregnancy outcomes. According to these findings, whether or not progesterone should be used in pregnancy management must be determined for each individual case, as more research is needed to establish standardized protocols and identify patients who will benefit the most.

Uterine artery doppler indices

The studies that were investigated identified inconsistent effects on uterine artery Doppler indices. These indices demonstrated no significant changes after progesterone administration, as reported by Barda et al. in 2010 and Maged et al. in 2020 [17, 23]. However, a study by Vafaei et al. in 2016 among the Iranian population revealed a significant decrease in the maternal uterine artery PI after the administration of progesterone, implying a vasodilatory effect [15]. An animal study among pregnant rats in 2014 conducted by Amaral et al. revealed that treatment with progesterone reduced the uterine artery resistance index (UARI) when a hypertensive rat model for preeclampsia was used as an illustration of probable improvements in placental perfusion and fetal growth [24]. Furthermore, a systematic review by Perkovic-Kepeci et al. in 2023 indicated that uterine artery pulsatility index (UtA-PI) levels are significantly higher during the third trimester in diabetic pregnancies than in nondiabetic pregnancies, suggesting that the stage of pregnancy may influence the effectiveness of progesterone on Doppler indices [25].

Consistent with our findings, several reports show no meaningful change in uterine artery Doppler measures after vaginal progesterone. Barda et al. observed no discernible difference in uterine artery resistance or pulsatility following treatment [23]. In a later cohort, Çintesun et al. likewise found no apparent difference in uterine artery pulsatility between those who used progesterone and those who did not, suggesting that early-pregnancy progesterone may not materially alter uterine artery flow [22]. Similarly, a randomized, placebo-controlled study in twin pregnancies by Agra et al. reported no clear group differences in uterine artery pulsatility across gestation [20].

By contrast, signals of potential benefit appear in selected high-risk populations. A double-blind trial by Jamal et al. reported a reduction in uterine artery pulsatility after progesterone among women with elevated baseline uterine artery resistance [26], and El-maghraby et al. described a notable decline in a composite uterine artery index among women at risk of preterm birth [27]. These clinical patterns align with the computational and observational work of Clark et al., which indicates that increased uterine artery resistance and notching reflect abnormal uterine vascular remodeling [28]. Overall, progesterone shows no consistent effect on uterine artery impedance in unselected populations, with possible improvements confined to women who begin pregnancy with abnormal uterine artery profiles or other high-risk features. While most changes occurred gradually, Mulic-Lutvica et al. revealed notable variations from the earliest postpartum measurements in a longitudinal study of 45 women’s arterial resistance following normal vaginal births [29]. Following participants for 56 days, they reported that the uterine artery pulsatility index, a measure of vascular impedance, remained low at 1.23 on day one but had risen considerably to 2.25 by the end of the study [29]. These findings emphasized the extended time required for circulatory characteristics to fully recover from the demands of pregnancy [29]. Vaginal progesterone, which is used to prevent preterm birth and manage pregnancy complications, has varying effects on uterine artery Doppler indices, necessitating further research for its benefits in different clinical scenarios.

Umbilical artery doppler indices

The effect of progesterone on the flow of Doppler in umbilical arteries also revealed some inconsistencies between studies. Studies by Barda et al. in 2010 and Vafaei et al. in 2016 [15] revealed no significant changes in umbilical artery flow after the administration of progesterone [15, 23]. However, a randomized clinical study in 2007 by Fonseca et al. discovered that progesterone reduced the prevalence of abnormalities in the umbilical arteries of women at risk of preterm birth, indicating a potential protective effect [30]. Furthermore, in 2007, Gupta et al., among prospective randomized controlled clinical trials involving Indian patients, [31] reported that the use of progesterone to treat placental insufficiency resulted in significant reductions in the PI and RI values for the umbilical artery [31]. Additionally, Sanad et al., in a prospective randomized placebo‑controlled trial in 2019 [32], reported that sildenafil citrate and nitroglycerine could improve Doppler indices in the umbilical artery, implying that these effects are similar to those caused by progesterone [32].

MCA doppler indices

Across studies, Doppler responses to progesterone are heterogeneous. In a 2010 cohort, Barda et al. observed a reduction in fetal MCA PI after progesterone—consistent with vasodilation, while MCA-PSV was unchanged [23]. By contrast, Vafaei et al. found no significant change in MCA PI overall yet reported a decrease in maternal uterine artery PI after progestogen suggesting uterine vasodilation, alongside an increase in umbilical artery resistance indices at various gestational ages, implying higher fetoplacental impedance [15]. In an Egyptian cohort, Maged et al. reported no significant changes in uterine or umbilical artery RI/PI or in MCA PI with progesterone [17]. Barinov et al. noted a trend toward higher MCA indices and a significant difference in uterine artery RI in the progesterone group [19], whereas a Turkish case–control study by Çintesun et al. (2021) found no progesterone effect on uterine artery PI [22].

Findings from non-progesterone comparators were likewise mixed. Sanad et al. reported no significant changes in MCA Doppler indices after sildenafil citrate or nitroglycerin [32], although elsewhere the same trial is described as showing an increase in MCA PI with sildenafil, underscoring inconsistency and the need to verify the original report [32]. As a historical comparator, Zimmermann et al. (1997) found low-dose aspirin did not influence uterine, umbilical, or renal resistance indices [33].

Overall, progesterone does not produce a consistent change in uterine or umbilical artery impedance, and fetal cerebral effects vary, ranging from lower MCA PI [23] to higher MCA indices [19] or no change [15, 17], likely reflecting population, timing, dosing, and methodological differences.

Preterm birth

The role of progesterone in preventing preterm birth in patients who undergo Doppler ultrasound is unclear. Nonetheless, some authors [19, 21], have reported a lack of significance in terms of a general reduction in the risk of preterm births due to progesterone administration. However, some groups, such as those with a short cervix, experience positive outcomes [19, 21]. Similarly, in a meta-analysis in 2018 performed by Quist-Nelson et al., progesterone did not prolong pregnancy in singleton gestations with extremely low PPROM levels after delivery prematurely [34]. Similarly, the study performed by Fonseca et al. [30] found that women with inadequately short cervices had lower rates of preterm labor than those who received a placebo [30]. Moreover, recent consensus work by Villar and colleagues in 2024 has introduced an etiologically based functional taxonomy of the preterm birth syndrome. This framework classifies PTB into distinct phenotypes according to underlying mechanisms, which in turn predict differential maternal and neonatal outcomes. Integrating this taxonomy into future research may improve the ability to tailor progesterone therapy to specific PTB subtypes, enhance risk stratification, and ultimately inform more personalized approaches to prevention [35].

IUGR

There is insufficient evidence to support the hypothesis that progesterone affects IUGR, which was evaluated by Doppler ultrasonography during pregnancy. There were no significant improvements in fetal abdominal circumference, a marker for IUGR, between the progesterone and placebo groups in a study by Norman et al. in 2016 [21]. Furthermore, there were no significant changes in blood flow in major fetal or uterine arteries, as reported by Maged et al. in 2020 [17], indicating that progesterone may not be used to prevent IUGR. Nonetheless, in 2018, Amaral et al. proposed that PIBF could enhance fetal growth restriction by reducing inflammation and placental cytolytic NK cells [36].

Preeclampsia

There are also conflicting findings from different studies on the benefits of progesterone in women with preeclampsia, especially those assessed by Doppler ultrasonography during pregnancy. According to the study by Norman et al. in 2016 [21], the incidence of preeclampsia did not differ between groups receiving progesterone or placebo [21]. Another study conducted by Amaral et al. in 2014 [24] revealed, however, that progesterone supplementation during pregnancy could reduce OH pressure while also improving the inflammatory response and nitric oxide bioavailability due to placental ischemia, indicating its potential role in preeclampsia management [24].

PROM

The potential effect of progesterone on PROM among patients who are evaluated by Doppler ultrasonography is negligible. Norman et al. reported no significant difference in PROM rates between the progesterone and placebo groups [21]. On the other hand, a randomized clinical trial by Abdali et al. in 2017 among the Iranian population [37] discovered that progesterone suppositories could lengthen the latent phase and increase birth weight in patients with PPROM at 28–30 weeks gestation, indicating potential situational benefits [37].

Clinical implications

From a clinical standpoint, Doppler changes should be interpreted cautiously. While altered uterine or umbilical artery indices may reflect impaired placental perfusion, available evidence does not justify progesterone supplementation based solely on Doppler findings. For example, a study by Çintesun et al. showed no significant difference in uterine artery PI at 11–14 weeks among women using progesterone versus controls [22]. Clinical trials like OPPTIMUM likewise relied instead on validated risk factors such as a short mid-trimester cervical length and prior spontaneous preterm birth to guide inclusion and treatment [21]. In contrast, women with a cervical length ≤ 25 mm have been shown consistently to benefit from vaginal progesterone in terms of reduced preterm birth and improved neonatal outcomes [38]. Doppler assessments may still offer supplemental risk stratification when used in conjunction with cervical measurements, but their independent predictive or decision-making value remains unproven. Future studies should test whether combining Doppler with cervical length or other markers yields clinically actionable improvements in patient management.

While the present review focused primarily on Doppler indices and short-term perinatal endpoints, it is essential to situate these findings within a broader clinical context. Maternal complications such as postpartum hemorrhage and peripartum infection, although not consistently reported in the included trials, remain highly relevant determinants of maternal health. Likewise, longer-term neonatal outcomes including neurodevelopmental trajectories, respiratory morbidity, and cardiometabolic risk represent critical measures of efficacy that extend beyond delivery and are insufficiently addressed in current evidence. Future studies should incorporate these dimensions to provide a more holistic assessment of progesterone’s impact.

Strength and limitations

This systematic review follows the PRISMA guidelines and employs a robust search strategy across multiple databases to ensure that all relevant studies are included. Focusing on specific Doppler ultrasound indices such as uterine and umbilical artery Doppler, as well as their relationship with progesterone supplementation, provides valuable insights into the clinical outcomes of pregnancy-related complications. This review concludes by focusing on the effects of progesterone supplements during early and mid-pregnancy, which could be used to inform clinical practices regarding the use of progesterone in preventing conditions such as preterm birth, IUGR, and preeclampsia. The inclusion of studies from different parts of the world, such as Iran, Egypt, and Europe, helps increase generalizability across diverse populations. The results are challenging to summarize due to the various study designs, Doppler measurements, and dosages of progesterone used in these studies. The heterogeneity makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about the efficacy of progesterone therapy. Furthermore, this review is limited to papers published in English, which may exclude valuable research in other languages, resulting in potential language and publication biases. By excluding studies involving pregnancies in the third trimester or those with previous or ongoing progesterone supplementation, the findings may be limited in their applicability to broader clinical settings. The preceding ignores the importance of progesterone in advanced stages of gestation in terms of complete clinical guidelines. Another important limitation is the lack of standardized definitions for key outcomes, such as preterm birth and clinically significant Doppler changes, which varied across studies. This inconsistency complicates direct comparisons and highlights the need for consensus definitions in future research. Comparing results across studies becomes more difficult due to variations in the type/amounts of progesterone used, as well as ultrasound scan and Doppler indices care protocol heterogeneity that exists currently, necessitating more standardized research protocols for future investigations. Also, the heterogeneity observed in this review underscores the need for more well-designed and standardized studies in the future. The small number of studies and the variability in progesterone regimens and participant populations are significant limitations. The absence of sufficient studies with consistent findings for key outcomes prevented us from conducting a meta-analysis. According to Cochrane’s guidelines, a meta-analysis can only be conducted when at least three studies with homogeneous numerical data for a particular finding are available. This limitation has hindered our ability to quantitatively assess heterogeneity and perform formal statistical analyses such as evaluating publication bias.

Conclusion

This systematic review aimed to assess the efficacy of using progesterone to improve various Doppler indices as well as women’s pregnancy outcomes from the first to second trimesters. The study included six studies involving prospective cohorts and randomized controlled trials, with a total of 1892 participants from various geographic locations.

In conclusion, progesterone supplementation had a significant effect on the fetal middle cerebral artery (MCA) pulsatility index (PI), with significant decreases implying improved fetal blood flow; however, other Doppler flows, specifically those in the uterine and umbilical arteries, did not significantly change after supplementation. This mix of results indicates that the effectiveness of progesterone on Doppler indices varies. Clinical outcomes such as preterm birth, intrauterine growth retardation (IUGR), preeclampsia, and premature rupture of membranes were also considered. In some subgroups, progesterone reduced the risk of preterm birth, particularly among women with a short cervix. Nevertheless, the overall reduction in preterm birth rates varied across studies. Furthermore, supplementation had no significant effect on reducing the incidence of IUGR, preeclampsia, or PROM, demonstrating that these conditions are most likely unrelated. Variations in the types and doses of progesterone interventions, as well as differences in study designs and measurement techniques, all contribute to heterogeneity, making synthesizing and interpreting results difficult. Nonetheless, this study provides significant findings concerning the role of progesterone in early pregnancy and mid-pregnancy. For future research, it will be necessary to standardize progesterone regimens and agree on uniform outcomes to better understand their potential and challenges. The current study contributes to the existing evidence base, informing clinical practice and guiding future research into the use of progesterone during pregnancy. However, the high degree of heterogeneity across studies highlights the need for more large-scale, standardized trials with consistent methodologies, aiming to reduce this heterogeneity by adopting standardized treatment regimens and outcome measures.
