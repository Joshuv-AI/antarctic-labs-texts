Effect of synbiotic supplementation and psychobiotic diet on prenatal and postnatal depression and anxiety in women with gestational diabetes: a three-arm randomised controlled trial protocol
Authors: Shokoufeh Sadat Mousavi, Laleh Payahoo, Roghaiyeh Nourizadeh, Zeinab Moshfeghy, Zahra Mohammadi Bina, Fatemeh Abbasalizadeh, Solmaz Ghanbari-Homaie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is one of the most prevalent metabolic disorders during pregnancy and may lead to various short-term and long-term complications for both the mother and the fetus. It is also associated with psychological disorders, such as anxiety and depression, the prevalence of which is reported to be two to four times higher than that among healthy pregnant women. Psychobiotics may improve cognitive and emotional function through modulation of the gut–brain axis. Therefore, this study was designed to evaluate the prophylactic effects of synbiotic supplementation and a psychobiotic diet during pregnancy in women with GDM who do not have clinically significant depressive symptoms or psychiatric disorders at baseline. The primary outcomes are antenatal depression and pregnancy-related anxiety at 38 weeks of gestation; secondary outcomes include postpartum depression and anxiety at 4–6 weeks after birth, maternal metabolic indices at 38 weeks of gestation and selected maternal and neonatal outcomes assessed immediately after birth.Methods and analysisThis is a randomised, controlled, three-arm parallel trial. A total of 102 pregnant women diagnosed with GDM between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation will be randomly assigned (1:1:1) to one of three groups: a synbiotic capsule (Group 1), a psychobiotic diet (Group 2) and a placebo capsule (control group). Allocation will be performed using block randomisation, stratified by parity. Regardless of group assignment, all participants will receive a standardised healthy diet education session in accordance with the Iranian Ministry of Health protocols. The intervention will be administered from the time of GDM diagnosis (28 weeks of gestation) until 4 weeks postpartum. Outcomes will be assessed through maternal and neonatal medical records, laboratory analyses, the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, Revised Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Questionnaire, Postpartum Specific Anxiety Scale-Research Short Form and a 3 day dietary food record. Data analysis will be performed according to the modified intention-to-treat approach. Additionally, a per-protocol analysis will be conducted for participants with an adherence rate of ≥90%.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (IR.TBZMED.RCE.1404.565). Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals and presentations at relevant scientific conferences.Trial registrationIRCT20220926056046N7

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), one of the most common metabolic disorders during pregnancy, is defined as glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition at 24–28 weeks of gestation.1 The global prevalence of GDM is estimated to be approximately 14%, with a prevalence of 27.6% reported in the Middle East region and 6.7% in Iran.2 The most important risk factors for GDM include elevated body mass index (BMI), advanced maternal age, a family history of diabetes and a previous history of GDM in prior pregnancies.3

GDM can lead to a wide range of adverse short-term maternal and fetal outcomes through mechanisms such as fetal hyperinsulinaemia and placental dysfunction.4 These complications include fetal macrosomia, preterm delivery and neonatal conditions such as hypoglycaemia, polycythaemia, hypokalaemia, respiratory distress and hyperbilirubinaemia. In addition, GDM is associated with long-term consequences, including an increased risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases and dyslipidaemia in both mothers and their offspring.5

Beyond physical complications, the prevalence of depressive symptoms among women with GDM has been reported to range from approximately 26% to 57%, while anxiety symptoms range from 5% to 58%,6 which is about two to four times higher than that observed in non-diabetic pregnant women.7 Perinatal mental health disorders are associated with an increased risk of several short-term adverse outcomes, including preeclampsia, low birth weight, preterm birth, impaired mother–infant bonding and infant undernutrition during the first year of life.8 Furthermore, these disorders are linked to long-term adverse outcomes, such as chronic mental health conditions in mothers and cognitive and neurodevelopmental impairments in children, including anxiety, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, emotional and learning disorders, as well as an increased risk of metabolic diseases in adulthood.9

Several potential mechanisms have been proposed to explain the bidirectional association between GDM and psychological disorders, including the following: First, the diagnosis of GDM itself acts as a psychosocial stressor, which may lead to emotional distress, anxiety and depressive symptoms.10 Second, accumulating evidence supports a bidirectional relationship, whereby psychological disorders such as anxiety and depression may contribute to the development of GDM through chronic activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and elevated cortisol levels, ultimately leading to insulin resistance and an increased risk of GDM.11 Conversely, GDM may increase the risk of psychological disorders by two- to fourfold through metabolic and inflammatory alterations.11 12 A third emerging mechanism involves the role of the gut microbiota and the gut–brain axis.13 According to this hypothesis, alterations in the composition and diversity of the gut microbiota (dysbiosis) may contribute to the development of depressive and anxiety symptoms by influencing neurotransmitters such as serotonin, regulating stress responses and promoting systemic inflammation.14 These microbial changes may further contribute to the pathophysiology of both metabolic and psychological disorders by inducing inflammation through increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and C-reactive protein.15 Fourth, inflammatory conditions during pregnancy can affect the abundance and balance of bacterial taxa such as Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides. Imbalances in microbial composition or diversity, referred to as microbial dysbiosis, in pregnant women may therefore play a role in the development and progression of mental health disorders.16 Based on the proposed gut–brain axis mechanisms, several studies have reported that certain dietary patterns may help prevent psychological disorders by contributing to brain biosynthesis and function, regulating neurotransmitter release and activity, maintaining neural–synaptic membrane fluidity and modulating the gut microbiota.17 18 In this context, dietary and lifestyle modifications appear to modulate the effects of pregnancy and metabolic disturbances on maternal gut dysbiosis.18 In this context, psychobiotic diets have emerged as a potentially promising approach. These diets comprise a combination of probiotics and prebiotics (ie, synbiotics) and may confer mental health and cognitive benefits by favourably modulating the gut microbial composition through the gut–brain axis.19

Studies suggest that psychobiotics may enhance cognitive function and may help attenuate neurodegenerative damage through multiple mechanisms, including regulation of the gut–brain axis, attenuation of neuroinflammation, modulation of neurotransmitter systems and enhancement of synaptic plasticity.20 Probiotics, as the live components of this formulation, may exert beneficial effects on depression and anxiety by influencing neuro-immunological processes and facilitating the synthesis of neurotransmitters.21 Prebiotics, as non-digestible dietary substrates, undergo fermentation in the gut, leading to the production of beneficial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids, which play a critical role in microbiota–gut–brain signalling pathways.22 Accordingly, psychobiotic diets may represent a potential non-pharmacological approach in preventive care strategies and in promoting mental health among both healthy and vulnerable populations.19

As previously noted, when probiotics are added synergistically to prebiotics, synbiotics are formed, which simultaneously exhibit the characteristics of both components.23 Synbiotic supplementation during pregnancy and lactation has been recognised as safe, and several studies have reported beneficial effects on improving insulin sensitivity, reducing systemic inflammation and regulating lipid metabolism.24 Notably, the positive effects of these supplements may persist in the body for up to 4 weeks after discontinuation.25

Numerous studies have evaluated the effects of prebiotic, probiotic and synbiotic supplementation on the improvement of metabolic indices, lipid profiles and inflammatory markers among individuals with overt diabetes or GDM; however, overall findings across studies remain inconsistent.26 Most investigations examining prebiotics, probiotics and synbiotics have primarily focused on their gastrointestinal and metabolic effects. Nevertheless, emerging evidence also supports an association between prebiotic supplementation and improvements in mental health outcomes.27

A review of studies assessing the effects of these supplements on anxiety and depression in non-pregnant populations suggests that they may alleviate neuroinflammation through interactions with the central nervous system and promote the growth of neuroprotective Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species. These effects are associated with increased production of short-chain fatty acids, enhanced neurotransmitter synthesis and immune modulation. However, evidence regarding their effectiveness in the treatment or prevention of anxiety and depression remains limited and highly heterogeneous.28

Although several studies have investigated the effects of synbiotics on mental health outcomes in other populations, no study has yet examined the impact of synbiotic supplementation on mental health among women with GDM. Given that the prevalence of anxiety and depression in women with GDM is two to four times higher than in healthy pregnant women, and considering that insulin resistance in this population—as previously noted—leads to increased systemic inflammation and oxidative stress compared with the general population, there is a rationale for investigating targeted preventive interventions. Moreover, fluctuations in blood glucose levels, which adversely affect both brain function and psychological well-being, further underscore the need for effective strategies in this high-risk group.

In light of the potential anti-inflammatory properties of synbiotics and their proposed mechanisms of action on mental health, as well as emerging evidence supporting the role of psychobiotic dietary interventions, the present study was designed to evaluate the prophylactic effects of synbiotic supplementation and a psychobiotic diet (the nutritional equivalent of synbiotic supplementation), initiated immediately after the diagnosis of GDM. Specifically, this study aims to determine whether these interventions can prevent the development of antenatal depression and anxiety in women with GDM who do not have clinically significant depressive symptoms or psychiatric disorders at baseline. The primary outcomes are antenatal depression and pregnancy-related anxiety, whereas the secondary outcomes include postpartum depression and anxiety, mode of birth (caesarean section rate), stillbirth, neonatal mortality, Apgar score, birth weight, and selected maternal metabolic indices, including fasting blood sugar (FBS) and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c).

Methods and statistical analysis

Study objectives

The objective of this clinical trial is to determine the preventive effects of synbiotic supplementation and a psychobiotic diet during pregnancy on antenatal depression and pregnancy-related anxiety at 38 weeks of gestation, the primary outcomes, among women with GDM who do not have clinically significant depressive symptoms or psychiatric disorders at baseline. Secondary outcomes include postpartum depression and anxiety assessed at 4–6 weeks after birth, maternal metabolic indices, including FBS and HbA1c, assessed at 38 weeks of gestation, and selected maternal and neonatal outcomes assessed immediately after birth.

Study design and setting

This study is designed as a randomised, placebo-controlled, three-arm, parallel-group superiority trial. The trial will be conducted at the health centres and outpatient clinics of hospitals, as well as affiliated gynaecology clinics, in Shiraz, Iran. Participant recruitment is scheduled to begin on 1 July 2026, and the anticipated study completion date is 1 July 2027.

Study procedures

The researcher (first author) will explain the study objectives and procedures to eligible pregnant women, assess eligibility criteria and obtain written informed consent from those who agree to participate. Participants will be informed that participation is voluntary and that they may withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason and without any consequences.

Following the acquisition of informed consent and the collection of baseline data, participants will be randomly assigned to one of the following three study groups: synbiotic supplementation, psychobiotic diet or control group.

Randomisation and blinding

The allocation sequence will be generated using stratified block randomisation, stratified by parity (nulliparous vs multiparous), with variable block sizes of 4 and 6 and an allocation ratio of 1:1:1 within each stratum. Randomisation will be performed using the Random Allocation Software (RAS) software. To ensure allocation concealment, identical, opaque and sequentially numbered sealed envelopes containing the type of intervention (synbiotic supplementation plus healthy diet education; psychobiotic diet; or placebo plus healthy diet education) will be used. The envelopes will be opened sequentially according to the order of participants’ enrolment in the study.

Intervention

Participants will be allocated to one of three study groups: (1) the Synbiotic Supplementation Group (receiving Femilact), (2) the Psychobiotic Diet Group (receiving an individually prescribed diet) or (3) the Control Group (receiving placebo capsules). All participants, regardless of group assignment, will receive standardised healthy diet education in accordance with the protocols of the Iranian Ministry of Health.

Supplementation and placebo (Groups 1 and 3)

To ensure double blinding, the Femilact synbiotic and placebo capsules will be identical in shape, size and packaging and will be supplied by Zist Takhmir Pharmaceutical Company. Femilact Synbiotic: contains 1×10⁹ CFU/g of nine probiotic strains (Lactobacillus plantarum, L. rhamnosus, L. acidophilus, L. casei, Bifidobacterium breve, B. longum, B. lactis, B. bifidum and Streptococcus thermophilus) and 74 mg of fructooligosaccharides (FOS). Placebo: contains pharmacologically inert excipients (lactose monohydrate, talc, magnesium stearate, maltodextrin, Avicel, sodium starch glycolate and Aerosil), excluding all probiotics and prebiotics.

Administration and adherence

Adherence to the assigned interventions will be assessed separately for each study group. For participants receiving synbiotic supplementation or placebo capsules, supplementation will begin at 28 weeks of gestation and continue until 4 weeks postpartum (a total of 16 weeks). Participants will be instructed to consume two 500 mg capsules daily, one every 12 hours. At each 28-day follow-up visit, participants will receive a new 28-day supply (56 capsules). Adherence will be assessed using two methods: (1) objective verification through counting returned capsules and empty or partially used containers at each follow-up visit and (2) subjective verification using a daily medication consumption checklist completed by participants.

For participants assigned to the psychobiotic diet group, dietary adherence will be assessed using dietary record booklets completed by participants. These records will be reviewed during weekly telephone follow-up calls by the dietitian to evaluate compliance and provide necessary dietary adjustments. Adherence will be considered acceptable when participants complete ≥90% of the prescribed intervention. If adherence falls below 90%, the event will be documented in the study records and additional counselling will be provided to reinforce adherence. Participants will remain in the study and continue receiving the assigned intervention according to the study protocol.

Psychobiotic diet (Group 2)

Participants will receive a personalised diet prescribed by a registered dietitian and tailored to their demographic, anthropometric and gestational profiles. The diet emphasises natural prebiotics and probiotics (eg, whole grains, legumes and fermented foods) to influence the gut–brain axis. Dietary adherence will be monitored using a dietary record booklet. The dietitian will review these records during weekly telephone follow-up calls to assess compliance and suggest modifications based on the participant’s socioeconomic status or food preferences.

Follow-up and safety monitoring

Follow-up schedule

Weekly telephone follow-ups will be conducted from 28 weeks of gestation to 4 weeks postpartum. These calls will serve to: (a) reinforce adherence, (b) ensure completion of supplement intake and dietary records, (c) collect self-reported adverse events and (d) identify the need for dietary adjustments.

Adverse events and safety

All adverse events (AEs) will be systematically recorded at each follow-up visit using a standardised checklist.

Safety oversight

As this is considered a low-risk intervention, a formal Data and Safety Monitoring Board will not be established. Instead, safety monitoring will be conducted by an independent clinical monitor (a clinician not involved in participant recruitment or data analysis, sixth author), who will periodically review all reported AEs to ensure participant safety.

Serious adverse events

In the event of a serious adverse event (SAE), the intervention will be immediately suspended for the affected participant and the participant will be referred to an appropriate specialist (eg, a perinatologist or clinical nutritionist) for urgent medical management.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study are antenatal depression and pregnancy-related anxiety at 38 weeks of gestation among the three study groups. Pregnancy-related anxiety will be assessed using the Revised Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Questionnaire (R-PRAQ) and depression during pregnancy will be assessed using the EPDS. Baseline anxiety and depression scores will be assessed before the intervention to characterise participants and will be included as covariates in the statistical analyses.

The secondary outcomes include postpartum anxiety and depression, assessed at 4–6 weeks after birth using the PSAS-RSF and the EPDS, respectively. Maternal metabolic indices, including FBS and HbA1c, will be assessed at 38 weeks of gestation. Selected maternal and neonatal outcomes, including caesarean section rate, stillbirth, neonatal mortality, Apgar score, intrauterine fetal death, preterm labour, neonatal admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) and birth weight will be assessed immediately after birth based on maternal and neonatal medical records and compared among the three study groups.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (V.3.1.9.7). Based on the study by Bozdoğan et al,29 we assumed a mean (SD) of 20.71 (4.90) for pregnancy-related anxiety and 12.01 (3.15) for pregnancy-related depression. Assuming a 20% reduction in mean scores between groups, a 95% confidence level (α=0.05), and 90% statistical power (using an independent samples t-test), the required sample size was estimated to be 24 participants per group.

To account for an anticipated 10% attrition rate, the required sample size was adjusted to 27 and 31 participants for the anxiety and depression outcomes, respectively. To ensure adequate power for both primary outcomes, we adopted the larger estimate of 31 participants per group. Finally, to further enhance the study’s robustness and account for potential non-compliance or dropouts beyond the initial estimate, the sample size was set at 34 participants per group, resulting in a total sample size of 102 women with GDM.

Interim analysis

No interim analysis will be conducted in this study.

Data collection

Data collection will be conducted by trained members of the research team according to standardised study procedures. The first author, a PhD candidate in Midwifery, will coordinate the data collection process and oversee data recording and study procedures under the supervision of the research team, including a midwifery specialist, a nutrition specialist, and a perinatology fellow. The psychobiotic diet will be designed by a certified nutritionist, who will oversee dietary data collection and adherence assessment. Mental health outcomes will be assessed separately by a trained and blinded outcome assessor, as described in the Blinding section. The following instruments will be used for data collection:

Sociodemographic questionnaire

The sociodemographic characteristics questionnaire includes items related to maternal age, spouse’s age, age at marriage, marital status, household income level, maternal educational attainment, spouse’s educational level, maternal employment status, spouse’s employment status, and pre-pregnancy anthropometric data, including weight, height and BMI. Additional items assess satisfaction with marital life, experience of violence during pregnancy, level of social and familial support during pregnancy, and history of cigarette smoking, substance use, and alcohol consumption prior to pregnancy.

Obstetric questionnaire

The obstetric characteristics questionnaire comprises items related to medications used for glycaemic control, gestational age at delivery, gravidity, history and number of miscarriages, history and number of stillbirths, whether the current pregnancy was planned or unplanned, attendance at antenatal education classes, sex of the current neonate, history of difficult labour in previous deliveries, experience of difficult labour in the current delivery, history of preterm birth, and pregnancy-related complications in the current pregnancy.

Further items include mode of previous delivery, presence or absence of a doula or birth companion during labour, place of delivery, mode of delivery, postpartum skin-to-skin contact, history of maternal admission to the intensive care unit during the current pregnancy, and presence of neonatal illness and neonatal admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU).

Maternal and neonatal outcomes checklist

The maternal and neonatal outcomes checklist includes items assessing the occurrence of intrauterine fetal death, stillbirth, preterm birth, mode of the current delivery, neonatal birth weight, neonatal death, Apgar score and neonatal admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). In addition, maternal metabolic indices, including FBS and HbA1c, will be assessed at baseline and at 38 weeks of gestation. The 38-week measurements will be considered secondary outcomes, with baseline values included as covariates in the statistical analyses. All data related to maternal and neonatal outcomes will be extracted from official maternal and neonatal medical records.

Revised Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Questionnaire

Pregnancy-related anxiety will be assessed using the Revised Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Questionnaire (R-PRAQ). This instrument consists of 10 items encompassing three domains: fear of childbirth (three items), fear of giving birth to a child with physical or mental disabilities (four items) and concerns related to changes in physical appearance (three items). Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5. The total score is obtained by summing the scores of all items, with higher scores indicating greater pregnancy-related anxiety. No cut-off point has been defined for this questionnaire.30 The psychometric properties of the Persian version of the R-PRAQ have been confirmed. The reliability of the instrument has been demonstrated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC=0.73) and internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha=0.74).31 The questionnaire will be administered using a structured interview method at baseline (prior to the intervention) and again at 38 weeks of gestation.

Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale

Depressive symptoms during pregnancy and the postpartum period will be assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). This instrument consists of 10 items rated on a four-point Likert scale, with a total score ranging from 0 to 30. Each item is scored from 0 to 3 based on the severity of depressive symptoms, with lower scores indicating a better mental health status. A score of 13 or higher is indicative of a probable depressive disorder. The validity and reliability of the Persian version of the EPDS have been confirmed by Montazeri et al. The internal consistency of the questionnaire has been reported with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.77 and test–retest reliability of 0.80, indicating acceptable reliability.32 The EPDS will be administered using a structured interview approach at baseline (prior to the intervention), at 38 weeks of gestation and during the postpartum period (4–6 weeks after birth).

Postpartum Specific Anxiety Scale-Research Short Form (PSAS-RSF

Postpartum-specific anxiety will be assessed using the Postpartum Specific Anxiety Scale-Research Short Form (PSAS-RSF). This instrument has been specifically designed and validated to measure anxiety unique to the postpartum period in mothers with infants aged 0–12 months.

The PSAS-RSF consists of 16 items across four domains, including: psychosocial adjustment to motherhood; practical infant care anxieties; maternal competence and bonding anxieties; infant safety and well-being concerns.

Responses are rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from ‘never’ to ‘almost always’, scored from 1 to 4, respectively. The total score ranges from 16 to 64, with higher scores indicating greater levels of postpartum-specific anxiety.33 The Persian version of the PSAS-RSF has been psychometrically validated by Hassanzadeh et al, demonstrating excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.93.34 In the present study, the PSAS-RSF will be administered using a structured interview approach at 4–6 weeks after birth.

Three-day food record

Dietary intake will be assessed using a 3-day food record, including two typical weekdays and 1 weekend day, to evaluate participants’ nutritional status. After receiving standardised training, participants will be instructed to record all foods and beverages consumed throughout the day, excluding water. On completion, the recorded food items will be converted into gram weights based on predefined food codes in the Nutritionist IV software. The processed data will then be entered into the software to estimate daily intakes of macronutrients and micronutrients. The mean intake across the three recorded days will be considered as the participant’s actual dietary intake.35 The food record form will be self-administered, and participants will be asked to complete it for two usual days and one non-working (holiday) day and return it to the researcher. This dietary assessment will be conducted at baseline (prior to the intervention), at 38 weeks of gestation and at 4–6 weeks after birth. The 3-day food record will also be used to assess dietary adherence. Dietary data will be analysed using Nutritionist IV, and an adherence index will be calculated as the percentage of conformity between actual dietary intake and the prescribed psychobiotic diet or healthy diet recommendations.

Food Frequency Questionnaire

Dietary habits over the preceding year, rather than recent intake, will be assessed using a Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ). This instrument evaluates participants’ usual dietary patterns and nutritional status and consists of a comprehensive list of commonly consumed foods, each accompanied by a standard serving size. Participants will be asked to report the frequency of consumption for each listed food item, including foods relevant to the psychobiotic diet, according to predefined categories of daily, weekly, monthly or yearly intake. The reported consumption frequencies will be converted into average daily intakes (grams per day) based on the specified standard serving sizes for each food item. The FFQ will be administered once at baseline and completed retrospectively to capture habitual dietary intake over the previous year.36

Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS)

Perceived social support will be assessed using the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS), a widely used instrument developed by Zimet et al.37 The MSPSS consists of 12 items across three subscales: family support (four items), friend support (four items) and significant other support (partner/spouse) (four items). The total score ranges from 12 to 84, with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support.37 The MSPSS has demonstrated good validity and reliability, and the Persian version has been psychometrically validated in an Iranian population, showing acceptable internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.85.38 In the present study, the MSPSS will be completed prior to study enrolment for participants in all three groups and will be administered through a structured interview. All questionnaires and checklists are included in online supplemental file 2).

Blinding

To minimise assessment bias, mental health outcomes will be assessed by a trained outcome assessor with expertise in reproductive health. The outcome assessor (the fourth author) will be blinded to participants’ group allocation and will not be involved in participant recruitment, intervention delivery or data analysis. Depression and anxiety outcomes will be collected through structured interviews using validated questionnaires. The collected data will be coded using participant identification numbers and entered into the study database for statistical analysis.

To evaluate the reliability of mental health assessments, a psychiatrist (the fifth author), who will remain blinded to participants’ group allocation and will not be involved in recruitment or data analysis, will conduct telephone-based interviews with a subset of participants. The agreement between the assessments performed by the primary outcome assessor and the psychiatrist will be evaluated and reported.

To maintain the integrity of blinding, the synbiotic and placebo capsules will be identical in appearance, smell, taste and packaging. Therefore, participants and researchers involved in outcome assessment will not be able to distinguish between the study groups receiving capsules.

Confidentiality and data management

To ensure strict confidentiality, each participant will be assigned a unique study identification code on enrolment. All personally identifiable information, including names and contact details, will be stored separately from the research data and will be accessible only to authorised members of the research team. The data used for statistical analysis will be coded and will not contain direct personal identifiers.

Data collected from questionnaires, clinical assessments and laboratory measurements will be recorded in paper-based data collection forms. Data entry will be performed manually by the first author, who is a PhD student in midwifery. The accuracy and completeness of data entry will be independently reviewed and verified by the corresponding author and the second author (supervisors of the study). Any identified discrepancies will be checked against the original source documents and corrected accordingly.

All physical records, including signed informed consent forms, questionnaires and clinical assessment documents, will be stored in locked cabinets with restricted access limited to the principal investigator and authorised research personnel. Data management procedures will be conducted in accordance with institutional ethical requirements to ensure the confidentiality, integrity and security of participant information.

Access to study data will be restricted to authorised members of the research team who are directly involved in the study. Any request for access to the dataset from external researchers will be evaluated by the principal investigator and will require a scientifically justified purpose and compliance with ethical requirements. If data sharing is approved, only de-identified data will be provided to protect participant confidentiality.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis plan was developed based on consultation with experts in clinical research methodology. Data will be analysed based on the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) principle, defined as all randomised participants who provide at least one post-randomisation outcome measure.

Baseline and pre-intervention data

Baseline characteristics and pre-intervention scores for depression and anxiety will be summarised descriptively. According to Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) recommendations, no significance testing will be performed for baseline differences between groups. Instead, pre-intervention scores will be included as covariates in the Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) models for continuous outcomes to account for baseline variability and improve the precision of treatment effect estimates.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary continuous outcomes, including antenatal depression and pregnancy-related anxiety scores at 38 weeks of gestation, will be analysed using ANCOVA models adjusted for baseline scores and prespecified prognostic covariates. Secondary continuous outcomes will be analysed using appropriate ANCOVA models adjusted for baseline values and relevant prognostic covariates, where applicable. Binary and categorical secondary outcomes will be analysed using appropriate regression models. Missing outcome data will be handled using multiple imputation under the assumption that data are missing at random. All available baseline variables and outcome measures associated with missingness will be included in the imputation model. In addition to the primary mITT analysis, a per-protocol analysis including participants with≥90% adherence to the assigned intervention will be conducted as a sensitivity analysis.

Adverse events and patient-reported outcomes

Adverse events will be monitored throughout the study, recorded and summarised using descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages). Patient-reported outcomes (depression and anxiety scales) will be analysed as continuous variables using the primary analytical models described above. All analyses will be two-sided, with a significance level of p<0.05, using SPSS V.24.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design of the study or in the recruitment of participants.

Ethics approval and dissemination

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (approval code: IR.TBZMED.RCE.1404.565) and was also registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (registration number: IRCT20220926056046N7). Prior to enrolment, all participants will provide written informed consent (online supplemental file 1). Any protocol amendments will be formally reported to the ethics committee. The study findings will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed scientific journal and presented at relevant national and international conferences.

Discussion

This study is designed to evaluate the potential effects of synbiotic supplementation and a psychobiotic diet on depressive and anxiety symptoms among women with GDM. Given the increased risk of psychological distress in women with GDM, identifying feasible and non-pharmacological approaches to support maternal mental health is of clinical importance. This trial uses a three-arm randomised controlled design to compare the effects of these interventions on mental health outcomes during the prenatal and early postpartum periods. By focusing on a well-defined population and applying validated psychological assessment tools, this study aims to provide a rigorous evaluation of these interventions. The findings may provide preliminary evidence to inform future larger-scale clinical trials investigating nutritional strategies targeting the gut–brain axis in obstetric care.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several methodological strengths. The three-arm randomised controlled design allows the comparison of synbiotic supplementation, a psychobiotic diet and placebo control while accounting for potential differences between intervention approaches. The use of validated psychological assessment tools and a clearly defined study population is expected to improve the consistency and reliability of outcome assessment. In addition, the use of an mITT analytical approach and predefined adherence monitoring procedures will help address potential challenges related to participant withdrawal and intervention compliance.

Several anticipated limitations should also be considered. First, although the interventions are designed based on proposed gut–brain axis mechanisms, microbiome-related biological assessments (such as faecal microbiota profiling) will not be performed due to resource limitations; therefore, mechanistic pathways cannot be directly evaluated. Second, follow-up is limited to 4 weeks postpartum, which may restrict assessment of the long-term persistence of intervention effects. Finally, as the study focuses on the prevention of psychological symptoms among women with GDM without clinically significant psychiatric disorders at baseline, the findings may not be generalisable to women with established psychiatric conditions requiring pharmacological treatment.
