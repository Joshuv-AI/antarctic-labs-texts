Evaluation of cross-functional antibody responses elicited by a four-component GMMA Shigella vaccine
Authors: Valentina Caradonna, Eleanna Sarakinou, Roberta Di Benedetto, Agnes Hunyady, Usman Nakakana, Renzo Alfini, Marika Pinto, Federica Boretto, Salvatore Gemmellaro, Pietro Ferruzzi, Miren Iturriza, Francesco Berlanda Scorza, Simona Rondini, Valentino Conti, Carlo Giannelli, Ashwani Kumar Arora, Omar Rossi, Francesca Micoli, Francesca Mancini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Shigella is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in children in low- and middle-income countries. The O-antigen (OAg) component of the lipopolysaccharide is considered a protective antigen, however, their diversity challenges vaccine development, since more than 50 OAg serotypes have been identified. No licensed vaccine against shigellosis is available. A 4-component GMMA-based vaccine, altSonflex1-2-3, delivering S. sonnei and S. flexneri 1b, 2a and 3a OAg has been developed. Coverage is expected against non-vaccine serotypes, due to cross-reactivity, mediated by structural similarities among S. flexneri OAg. The vaccine is currently being tested in phase I and II clinical trials. In this work, sera from mice, rats, and rabbits injected with altSonflex1-2-3 were analyzed for their ability to bind to and kill S. flexneri serotypes not included in the vaccine. Results obtained were compared to corresponding results from vaccinated European adults. While no cross-reactive antibodies were measured in mouse sera, the antibodies elicited by altSonflex1-2-3 in rats, rabbits and humans were able to bind and kill the tested S. flexneri X, Y, 6, 4a and 5b strains. A study in African children and infants will confirm how data from animal models may predict the immune response in different age groups.

Introduction

Shigella remains a significant public health threat, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where inadequate sanitation and limited access to clean water facilitate its transmission1–3. As a leading cause of bacillary dysentery (shigellosis), it disproportionately affects young children, immunocompromised individuals, and malnourished populations2. The increasing prevalence of antimicrobial-resistant strains further exacerbates the disease burden, limiting treatment options. In recognition of this threat, fluoroquinolone-resistant Shigella was recently reclassified from a “medium” to a “high” priority pathogen in the 2024 World Health Organization (WHO) Bacterial Priority Pathogens List4, emphasizing the urgency of developing alternative preventive measures, including vaccines.

While Shigella primarily affects LMICs, shigellosis also occurs in high-income countries (HICs), particularly among men who have sex with men in urban settings or young children living in crowded communities5 with a notable rise in multidrug-resistant outbreaks6–10. These trends highlight the evolving epidemiology of Shigella and reinforce the need for a broadly protective vaccine capable of addressing diverse populations and transmission contexts.

Among the four species of the genus Shigella—S. dysenteriae, S. flexneri, S. boydii, and S. sonnei—the distribution of S. flexneri and S. sonnei is of particular epidemiological relevance5. Specifically, S. sonnei is reported to predominate in HICs, whereas S. flexneri is identified as the leading causative agent in LMICs11. In both endemic and non-endemic countries, the predominant serotype of S. flexneri reported is consistently S. flexneri 2a7,12–15. Other serotypes, including S. flexneri 1b, 3a, and 6, were observed mainly in endemic countries12,15–17.

O-antigen (OAg), a key component of the Shigella lipopolysaccharide (LPS), is central to serotype classification and immune recognition18. S. flexneri comprises at least 23 serotypes sharing a conserved OAg backbone composed of repeating tetrasaccharide units containing three rhamnose residues (rhamnose I, II, and III) and N-acetylglucosamine19. This is true for all S. flexneri serotypes except for S. flexneri 6, presenting a different OAg backbone composed of two rhamnose residues, one galacturonic acid and one N-acetylgalactosamine20. The diversity in serotypes arises from the addition of glucosyl and/or O-acetyl groups at various positions within the tetrasaccharide18. No cross-functionality of antibodies specific for S. sonnei OAg is expected against S. flexneri due to the different polysaccharide structure. Therefore, preclinical studies evaluating the cross-reactivity and cross-functionality among 14 S. flexneri serotypes guided the design of a four-component Shigella vaccine that included S. flexneri 1b and 3a OAg for the broad cross-reactivity of induced immune responses, and S. sonnei and S. flexneri 2a OAg due to the high prevalence of the two bacteria in endemic regions21. This combination should maximize cross-protection while covering the most relevant disease-causing serotypes.

To address the need for a Shigella vaccine, the Generalized Modules for Membrane Antigens (GMMA) platform was selected based on specific characteristics which make it suitable for LMIC vaccine development. GMMA are outer membrane vesicles naturally shed by Gram-negative bacteria, genetically modified to enhance vesicles release and to modulate endotoxin-related potential reactogenicity22. Their strong immunogenic properties, combined with simple and cost-effective production, make them well-suited for development of vaccines for LMICs.

The quadrivalent GMMA-based vaccine, named altSonflex1-2-3, showed good immunogenicity in animal models23. A phase 1 trial (NCT05073003 stage 1) has already assessed the safety and immunogenicity of the vaccine in European adults24, allowing the progression into the target population of 9-month-old infants in Kenya, where shigellosis is endemic (NCT05073003 stage 2 and NCT06663436).

Given that altSonflex1-2-3 is designed to provide broad coverage, it was important to assess the cross-reactivity of the immune response elicited, thereby extending the protective coverage beyond the vaccine’s direct components. Different studies have already demonstrated that certain S. flexneri serotypes can induce antibodies that are broadly reactive, covering a range of serotypes21,25–28.

In this study, we aimed to build a comprehensive profile of the altSonflex1-2-3 vaccine’s immunogenic potential by evaluating the contribution of each vaccine component to cross-functional immune responses against S. flexneri 4a, 5b, 6, X and Y serotypes that are not included in the vaccine but of epidemiological importance12,15. The cross-functionality of antibodies was assessed in mice, rats and rabbits. In addition, sera from vaccinated European adults were evaluated for cross-functionality and results were compared to pre-clinical data.

Results

Assessing the serological response to altSonflex1-2-3 vaccine components

In this study, we aimed to dissect the serological response induced by each of the four vaccine GMMA components of altSonflex1-2-323 to understand the relative contribution to the OAg-specific IgG response (measured by ELISA) and antibody functionality (measured by SBA). To do this, we compared the immune response elicited by altSonflex1-2-3 GMMA vaccine in mice with that elicited by corresponding mono-, bi- or tri-component formulations. The bi-component formulation was composed of S. sonnei GMMA and S. flexneri 2a GMMA, which represent the most medically and epidemiologically relevant serotypes7,12,15. The tri-component formulations were composed of S. sonnei GMMA, S. flexneri 2a GMMA, and either S. flexneri 1b GMMA or S. flexneri 3a GMMA. The comparison was also conducted between all these multi-component formulations and the four mono-component ones, each consisting of a single GMMA from one of the four strains. Anti-OAg specific IgG levels (Fig. 1) against, S. sonnei, S. flexneri 2a, S. flexneri 3a, and S. flexneri 1b and related serum bactericidal activity (Fig. 2) were observed when the corresponding GMMA was present in the formulation. Regarding functionality against the four vaccine strains, bactericidal activity was significantly higher in mice immunized with altSonflex1-2-3 compared to the mono-component formulations for S. sonnei (P = 0.0011) and S. flexneri 2a (P = 0.0007). When comparing the functional response of altSonflex1-2-3 to that elicited by the bi- and tri-component formulations containing the respective antigen, no significant differences were observed, except for a notably lower IC50 against the S. sonnei strain in mice immunized with the bi-component formulations.

Although some limited cross-reactivity between S. flexneri 3a and 1b was observed in both ELISA and SBA assays, no significant cross-reactivity was detected between the other vaccine components. The results further demonstrated that the 4-component vaccine elicited higher IgG titers and functional antibody responses against homologous antigens and strains, indicating the necessity of including all four components to achieve an optimal immune response against all the vaccine serotypes.

All tested formulations induced higher anti-OAg IgG specific response on day 42 (14 days after the second immunization) compared to day 27 (27 days after the first immunization), indicating a boost effect following the second immunization, which occurred one month after the first immunization for all the GMMA formulations tested.

Cross-functionality of vaccine-induced antibodies in different animal models

After having dissected the response induced against the vaccine components of altSonflex1-2-3, we then assessed the ability of antibodies elicited by altSonflex1-2-3 and its corresponding mono-component formulations (S. sonnei GMMA, S. flexneri 2a GMMA, S. flexneri 1b GMMA, or S. flexneri 3a GMMA) to bind to (by FACS) and kill (by SBA) a panel of epidemiologically relevant heterologous S. flexneri serotypes (S. flexneri 4a, 5b, 6, X and Y). This evaluation was conducted in three different animal models: mice, rats, and rabbits.

In rats, a broader cross-reactivity pattern was observed. Antibodies generated by the altSonflex1-2-3 four-component formulation showed high binding and bactericidal activity across most tested heterologous strains, except for S. flexneri 6 (Fig. 3c, d). Interestingly, the mono-component vaccines demonstrated that individual antigens could elicit cross-reactive responses against specific heterologous strains. In particular, strong responses were elicited against S. flexneri 4a, 5b, X, and Y when rats were immunized with S. flexneri 1b GMMA; against 4a, X, and Y when immunized with S. flexneri 2a GMMA; and against 4a, 5b, X, and Y when immunized with S. flexneri 3a GMMA, both in terms of antibody binding (FACS) and bactericidal activity (SBA).

In rabbits, the results aligned with those observed in rats, with cross-reactivity patterns largely maintained. Differently from rats, antibody binding and bactericidal activity were also detected against S. flexneri 6.

In many cases, across the three tested species, stronger responses were observed in sera of animals immunized with altSonflex1-2-3 compared to the corresponding mono-component formulations.

Overall, these findings highlight species-specific differences in vaccine-induced immune responses and underscore the role of individual antigens in mediating cross-reactivity against heterologous strains.

Impact of antigen depletion on functionality of antibodies

To further investigate the contribution of each antigen to the wide cross-functionality observed in rabbits and rats, sera from rabbits immunized with altSonflex1-2-3 were depleted of S. sonnei, S. flexneri 1b, 2a and 3a OAg-specific antibodies. The concentration of each OAg was selected to have >70% inhibition against the homologous strain, and same amounts were then tested to look at residual bactericidal activity against heterologous strains. Depletion using the four different competitors led to an IC50 inhibition >90% in many cases, such as when the altSonflex1-2-3 serum was depleted with S. flexneri 3a OAg and tested against S. flexneri 5b; when it was depleted with S. flexneri 1b OAg or S. flexneri 2a OAg and tested against S. flexneri 6; when it was depleted with S. flexneri 1b or S. flexneri 3a OAg and tested against S. flexneri X (Fig. 4a). Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the capability of the 4-component altSonflex1-2-3 vaccine to generate functional antibodies against a range of epidemiologically significant heterologous S. flexneri serotypes. Additionally, it sought to analyze the contribution of each antigen in the vaccine to the cross-functionality against these heterologous serotypes. This research utilized three different animal models and compared the preclinical findings with clinical outcomes.

Importantly, in all animal species it was observed that the combination of the four GMMA resulted in no negative immune-interference and increased coverage against heterologous Shigella serotypes21,23. Indeed, by testing the ability of altSonflex1-2-3-elicited antibodies to bind and kill a panel of epidemiologically relevant Shigella strains we confirmed that the altSonflex1-2-3 four-component formulation enhanced the breadth of the immune response, suggesting potential cross-protection against additional S. flexneri serotypes.

Data from Rossi et al.23 prefigured some key findings of the current work. In mice, altSonflex1-2-3 elicited a dose-dependent anti-OAg IgG response at very low doses (from 0.58 to 37 ng of each OAg), with a booster effect observed 14 days post-second vaccination. In rabbits, the full human dose (60 μg of total OAg) induced significant responses and mediated bactericidal activity against the homologous strains and heterologous S. flexneri serotypes (4a, 5b, 6, X, and Y)23.

In this study, we observed cross-reactivity against all heterologous S. flexneri strains tested in both rat and rabbit models, with the exception of S. flexneri 6 in the rat model.

This finding aligns with the results of a study by Noriega et al., in which guinea pigs were immunized intranasally with attenuated strains of S. flexneri 2a and 3a. Following immunization, the guinea pigs were challenged with wild-type S. flexneri strains from various serotypes, and cross-protection was observed against all strains tested, except for S. flexneri 627. In particular, efficacy percentages of 92% against S. flexneri 2b, 80% against S. flexneri Y, 64% against S. flexneri 5b, and 56% against S. flexneri 1b were reported, with lower percentages of 42% against S. flexneri 1a, 20% against S. flexneri 4b, and 4% against S. flexneri 627. Similarly, in our study, consistent cross-reactivity with S. flexneri Y and 5b was observed when animals were immunized with either the altSonflex1-2-3 four-component formulation or mono-component formulations of S. flexneri 2a and 3a. Furthermore, the cross-reactivity we observed between S. flexneri 3a and 1b aligns with the findings of Noriega et al.

In our study, the rabbit model demonstrated the most comprehensive response, with antibodies binding to and effectively killing all five heterologous strains tested. This behavior does not fully correlate with magnitude of the response against vaccine antigens (see Supplementary Table 3), and indicates an enhanced cross-reactivity in the rabbit model, in line with results previously reported23.

Another study, that explored cross-reactivity in humans and monkeys challenged with S. flexneri 2a and in humans and guinea pigs immunized with a recombinant vaccine expressing the S. flexneri 2a OAg, found that human sera exhibited cross-reactive IgA, particularly against S. flexneri 2b and other serotypes (1a, 5a, Y). Guinea pig sera showed cross-reactive IgA and IgG antibodies against S. flexneri 1a, 5a, and Y, but not 2b, while monkey sera displayed broad cross-reactivity with multiple S. flexneri serotypes, with 1a, 2b, and Y being most recognized, and with IgG responses being more prominent than IgA28. In the present work, IgA responses were not assessed as the study focused on IgG-mediated bactericidal activity measured by SBA. However, given the demonstrated importance of IgA in protection, future studies will evaluate OAg-specific IgA responses in sera from vaccinees in the target population.

Notably, the results indicate that stronger cross-functionality can be elicited when all four antigens are present together, rather than in mono-component formulations. This could suggest a synergistic effect on cross-reactivity elicited by the four GMMA components, or higher adjuvanticity due to the increased amount of GMMA components in the multi–component formulation22. Additionally, the altSonflex1-2-3 formulation contains a 4-fold higher content of the common S. flexneri OAg tetrasaccharide chain domain than the correspondent mono-component S. flexneri formulations, which may contribute to increased OAg-specific IgG titers and enhanced functionality against both the homologous and heterologous S. flexneri serotypes tested. A similar pattern was observed in a previous study conducted in rabbits, in which a quadrivalent bioconjugate Shigella vaccine (containing S. sonnei and S. flexneri 2a, 3a, and 6 OAg) and the corresponding monovalent vaccines were compared for their ability to elicit bactericidal activity against non-targeted serotypes. Specifically, cross-reactivity with S. flexneri 4a and 4b was observed only when the quadrivalent formulation was tested25.

Discrepancies between FACS and SBA results were observed in the rabbit model, with strong FACS binding not reflected in high SBA titers. This suggests that while vaccine-induced antibodies were capable of binding to bacterial targets, they were not always functional in mediating killing. This behavior could be attributed to higher anti-GMMA protein and/or anti-LPS core antibody response in this animal model, which we previously demonstrated to be non-functional under the experimental conditions used29,30.

In the study by Citiulo et al.21, the functional outcomes measured by SBA using sera from animals immunized with S. flexneri GMMA against the same panel of S. flexneri strains did not align with between serotypes cross-reactivity predictions based solely on group-specificities (serogroups 3(4), 6, 7(8), 9 and 10). This suggests that factors beyond classical serogrouping may contribute to mediate binding and bactericidal activity across different S. flexneri serotypes and underscores the complexity of immune response against Shigella. Indeed, several other mechanisms might explain the observed pattern of cross-reactivity. For instance, the functional response against S. flexneri 6 seen in rabbits immunized with S. flexneri 2a GMMA could be linked to an O-acetylated disaccharide shared by both S. flexneri 6 and 2a. Specifically, the shared O-acetylated disaccharide Rhap-(1 → 2)-Rhap has been identified as a common epitope. However, in the study from Farzam et al., this cross-reactivity mechanism between S. flexneri 2a and S. flexneri 6 was investigated using a competitive inhibition assay with antibodies induced by a S. flexneri 2a conjugate vaccine in humans. Inhibition was observed with both O-acetylated and non-O-acetylated S. flexneri 6 OAg, with the inhibition curve slightly higher when the non-acetylated S. flexneri 6 OAg was used as inhibitor26. This finding evidences the need for further studies to clarify the impact of O-acetylation on cross-reactivity26.

In rabbits, we were able to observe also the contribution of S. flexneri 1b OAg in the bactericidal activity against S. flexneri 6 and the two OAg share the same O-acetylated disaccharide. The cross-reactivity between S. flexneri 3a and 5b is likely caused by their shared O-factor 7,8 (glucosylation on O-3 of rhamnose III), which influences OAg conformation and antibody recognition31. Although O-acetylation may be the basis for the observed cross-reactivity, other studies conducted on GMMA indicate that OAg O-acetylation is not critical for generating functional antibodies in mice against either homologous or heterologous strains32.

To clarify the immune response against the heterologous panel of S. flexneri and the role of each antigen in the altSonflex1-2-3 vaccine, we depleted sera of rabbits and humans of LPS-specific antibodies. In both cases, this revealed that most of the response to each heterologous strain is mediated by LPS/OAg rather than protein contained in GMMA. If, in theory, we could speculate that the latter may still contribute to some of the remaining response observed, recent studies have shown that the presence of OAg chains on the bacterial surface can mask protein antigens and limit anti-protein antibody binding22,29,33. Consequently, anti-OAg antibodies were confirmed as the primary mediators of bactericidal activity against OAg-positive bacteria.

Sera from human participants immunized with altSonflex1-2-3 exhibited stronger bactericidal activity against S. flexneri X, and Y compared to S. flexneri 5b, S. flexneri 4a and 6. The fold rise in bactericidal titers observed after vaccination against heterologous Shigella strains was lower compared to the rat and rabbit model. However, this trend can be linked to the numerically lower response specific for S. flexneri 1b and 3a OAg in comparison to S. sonnei and S. flexneri 2a, which we showed to be important components of the cross-reactive response24. Also, it is important to note that the adults from this clinical cohort had modest pre-existing Shigella OAg-specific IgG titers, especially against S. flexneri 1b and 3a, a feature absent in the animal models. This difference in baseline immunity may influence the observed vaccine responses. As the clinical study is currently still blinded, we are not able to correlate, at the subject level, bactericidal titers observed against Shigella strains included in the vaccine with bactericidal titers against heterologous serotypes.

A limitation of the study is that the number of human serum samples tested is low. Moreover, a threshold for protection has not yet been established for any of the serotypes, and this threshold might vary among the different serotypes. Finally, using strain-specific complement concentrations in SBA testing may restrict direct head-to-head comparisons among serum bactericidal results against different strains. However, this tailoring is critical to ensure that the bactericidal effect observed is primarily attributable to the antibody response rather than differences in intrinsic complement susceptibility.

The study shows that the altSonflex1-2-3 vaccine can generate cross-functional antibodies against various Shigella serotypes in different animal models and in humans. It confirmed the necessity of the four selected GMMA components to produce a strong IgG response with bactericidal activity against the most common Shigella serotypes and highlighted the importance of the OAg in facilitating cross-serotype immunity. However, in mice, the immune response against the heterologous strain showed very low titers. Cross-reactivity was observed in the rat and rabbit models, but each animal model had distinct patterns and specificities, especially when compared to clinical data. This could indicate that the same epitopes might be recognized differently by the immune system of different species. Therefore, it is essential to explore how these findings apply to the target population, e.g. infants in LMICs. Testing three doses of altSonflex1-2-3 in African infants with different immunization schedules (NCT05073003 stage 2 and NCT06663436) will determine if the results observed in European adults are consistent across different age groups and will help understand which animal model is mostly predictive for this kind of studies.

Methods

Strains and reagents

S. sonnei 53 G was obtained from Walter Reed Army Institute of Research, Washington, D.C., USA. The S. sonnei ΔvirG::cat strain with stabilized OAg used in flow cytometry binding experiments (FACS) and for serum bactericidal assay (SBA) was generated by Caboni et al.34. S.S. flexneri strains were purchased from Public Health England, London, UK, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Frozen 20% glycerol stocks were prepared from lyophilized cultures and stored at −80 °C.

The LPS from S. sonnei and S. flexneri strains used in SBA were extracted and analyzed for sugar structural features, protein, and nucleic acid impurities, as previously reported35.

GMMA formulation and characterization

altSonflex1-2-3 was produced and characterized according to the methods previously described by Rossi et al.23. altSonflex1-2-3 was formulated by adsorbing at 1:1:1:1 ratio GMMA (OAg content based) from S. sonnei and S. flexneri serotypes 1b, 2a, and 3a in a solution containing 154 mM NaCl and 10 mM NaH2PO4 at pH 6.5 and Alhydrogel at a concentration of 0.7 mg/ml (Al3+). The corresponding mono-, bi-, and tri-component formulations were prepared using the same procedure, maintaining the same GMMA content for each component and Alhydrogel amount.

Immunogenicity studies in animals

altSonflex1-2-3 vaccine, and bi-, tri- and mono-component GMMA formulations were used to immunize mice, rats and rabbits.

Eight CD1 mice per group (female, 4 to 6 weeks old), eight Sprague Dawley rats (female, 200–300 g) per group or eight New Zealand white rabbits (female, 2.5 kg) per group were immunized at days 0 and 28. All animals were immunized intramuscularly with either 50 µl (mice), 200 µl (rats) or 500 µl (rabbits) of vaccine formulation. Mice were immunized with 0.15 µg of OAg from a single serotype (S. sonnei, S. flexneri 1b, 2a, or 3a) when administered with each of the mono-component formulations, or 0.6 µg total OAg (0.15 µg of each serotype, combined in a 1:1:1:1 ratio) when formulated as the altSonflex1-2-3 four-component formulation. Similarly, rats were immunized with 0.6 µg of OAg from a single serotype when administered with mono-component formulations, or 2.4 µg total OAg when formulated as altSonflex1-2-3. Rabbits were immunized with either 1.5 µg of OAg from a single serotype (6 µg total OAg when formulated as altSonflex1-2-3) or, for depletion experiments, 60 µg total OAg formulated as altSonflex1-2-3. Sera were collected on days −1, 27 and 42.

All the animals had unrestricted access to food and water. To minimize stress, anesthesia is administered only during the terminal withdrawal phase. Euthanasia methods vary by species. Carbon dioxide is used for euthanasia of mice and rats, with no wake procedures before bleeding. Once the final bleed is completed, cervical dislocation is performed for mice while for rats rigor mortis is awaited before confirming death.

For rabbits, anesthesia is induced with a pentobarbital overdose, followed by exsanguination via intracardiac puncture for final serum collection. The absence of a heartbeat indicates death, which is confirmed by the onset of rigor mortis or cervical dislocation.

Sera were pooled and used for FACS binding (day −1 and day 42), while individual sera were used to conduct Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA; day −1, day 27 and day 42) and SBA (day −1 and day 42). Pre-immune sera were used as negative controls.

Human sera

Individual sera from a subset of 25% of healthy European adults enrolled in the NCT05073003 stage 1 clinical trial were tested by SBA24. These participants were vaccinated with altSonflex1-2-3 at a dose of 15 μg of each OAg. Vaccine injections were given with either a 3-month or a 6-month interval. Analyzed sera were collected pre-dose 1 (day 1), post-dose 1 (day 29), and post-dose 2 (day 113/197).

The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Ghent University Hospital and conducted as per Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Stage 1 study population were healthy, nonpregnant participants aged 18–50 years, without known prior exposure to Shigella or to experimental Shigella vaccines and who provided written informed consent.

For antigen depletion experiments, a human standard serum was created by pooling sera from the same clinical trial selected based on their anti-S. sonnei and S. flexneri 2a O-Ag-specific IgG EU/mL elevated levels.

Frozen 50 µL working aliquots of the serum were stored at −80 °C until use.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To assess OAg-specific IgG concentration by ELISA, 384-well microplates (Greiner Bio-One, Kremsmünster, Austria) were coated with 40 µL/well of coating antigen and incubated overnight (16 h) at 4 °C. The coating concentrations and buffers used were as follows: S. sonnei LPS at 0.5 μg/ml in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), S. flexneri 1b OAg at 2 μg/ml in carbonate buffer, S. flexneri 2a OAg at 5 μg/ml in carbonate buffer, and S. flexneri 3a OAg at 1 μg/ml in PBS. Coating buffers were selected based on results from preliminary experiments to maximize the ELISA signal given by a standard serum generated by immunizing mice with the respective Shigella GMMA. Absence of impact of coating buffer on antigen acetylation status was confirmed by NMR (data not shown). Following coating, plates were aspirated and blocked with 100 µL/well of 5% PBS milk powder (pH 7.4) (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) for 1 hour at 25 °C. Plates were then washed 3 times with PBS-Tween 0.05% (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) and incubated for 2 h at 25 °C with 40 µL/well of sera diluted at 1:100, 1:4000, and 1:160000 in PBS-Tween 0.05% with 0.1% BSA (pH 6.5) (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) for mice and rat sera and in 5% PBS milk (pH 7.4) for rabbit sera. Following this, plates were washed 3 times with PBS-Tween 0.05% and IgG detection was carried out using 40 µL/well of an enzyme-labeled secondary antibody (anti-species-specific IgG-alkaline phosphatase) (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) in PBS-Tween 0.05% with 0.1% BSA (pH 6.5) at different concentrations depending on the antigen used and for mice, rabbits and rats, and incubated for 1 h at 25 °C. After that, plates were washed 3 times with PBS-Tween 0.05%. The presence of anti-S. sonnei LPS/S. flexneri 1b, 2a, 3a OAg antibodies was identified by adding 40 µL/well of a p-Nitrophenyl phosphate substrate solution (Sigma-fast, Sigma-Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), resulting in a yellow color, which was measured by absorbance at 405 nm minus the absorbance at 490 nm detected via a Synergy HTX reader (Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA). The samples were compared to calibrated species-specific reference standard sera, with results expressed in ELISA units/mL (EU/mL) relative to the reference serum. One ELISA unit is defined as the reciprocal of the dilution of the reference serum that produces an Optical Density (OD) of one in the assay.

Luminescence-based SBA

SBA was performed against a panel of homologous (serotypes included in the vaccine) and heterologous (serotypes not included in the vaccine) Shigella serotypes by adapting previously published conditions. All tested samples were heat inactivated (HI) prior to testing in SBA at 56 °C for 30 min to remove endogenous complement activity. Heat-inactivated sera were diluted in PBS when tested against S. sonnei and S. flexneri 2a, 3a, 6, X and Y strains and in Luria Bertani broth (LB) when tested against S. flexneri 1b, 4a and 5b strains, starting from 1:100 with 6 subsequent 3-fold serial dilutions. Serial dilutions of sera from mice, rats, rabbits or humans were added to 96-well plates, followed by the addition of baby rabbit complement (BRC, Cederlane, Ontario, Canada). For each tested strain we initially determined the optimal lot and percentage of BRC to be used, which lacked intrinsic bactericidal activity. To achieve this, we conducted BRC titration experiments where target bacterial cells were incubated in assay diluents containing various percentages of BRC, ranging from 0% to 50%. At the conclusion of the incubation period (T180), we measured the growth of the bacteria by luminescence and compared these values to the initial time point (T0). This approach allowed us to identify the maximum BRC concentration not causing non-specific bacterial killing (data not shown36,). The concentration of BRC utilized in each assay was therefore different depending on the strain utilized: 30% for S. flexneri 1b, 20% for S. sonnei and S. flexneri 3a, 7% for S. flexneri 4a and 5b, 7.5% for S. flexneri 2a, X and Y, and 25% for S. flexneri 6.

Working aliquots of all strains stored frozen at −80 °C in 20% glycerol stocks were grown overnight (16–18 h) at 37 °C in a LB medium, stirring at 180 rpm. Bacterial strains were cultured to log-phase (OD600 = 0.22 + /−0.03) as previously described37 and then diluted to approximately 1 × 106 CFU/mL in PBS. An adequate volume of reaction mixture containing the target bacterial cells (10 µL/well) and BRC in assay buffer was prepared; 75 µL/well of the reaction mixture were added to each well of the SBA plate containing HI serum dilutions (final reaction volume 100 µL), mixed and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. The viability of the surviving bacteria was assessed using BacTiter-Glo reagent (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) to measure ATP production, with luminescence detected via a Synergy HT luminometer (Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA). The bactericidal activity of each serum was quantified by determining the IC50 value, defined as the reciprocal serum dilution that leads to a 50% reduction in luminescence (indicating 50% inhibition of bacterial growth) with respect to the maximum growth of bacteria (no serum control). For titers below the minimum measurable signal, a value equal to half of the lowest dilution tested was assigned for animal sera (therefore 50), or equal to half of the limit of quantification in case of human sera37–39. Curve fitting and IC50 calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, LLC, San Diego, CA, USA).) A 4-parameter non-linear regression was applied to the raw luminescence (no normalization of data was applied) obtained for all the sera dilutions tested for each serum; an arbitrary serum dilution of 1015 was assigned to the well containing no sera. Fitting was performed by weighing the data for the inverse of luminescence2.

To assess the impact of antigen-specific antibody depletion on SBA, HI rabbit or human standard sera were subjected to a pre-incubation process with LPS. HI standard sera were mixed with an equal volume (1:1; v) of the competitor (or PBS as control) and incubated overnight (16–18 hours) at 4 °C in an orbital shaker set at 200 rpm. Following the pre-incubation, each spiked sample was assayed on the same day by SBA against each Shigella serotype. Binding of cross-antibodies measured by flow cytometry

Bacteria were cultured in LB to the log-phase (OD600 = 0.22 + /−0.03) as described in previous paragraph. Approximately 5 ×106 bacterial cells were then washed with PBS and fixed with BD Cytofix™ (BD Biosciences, CA, USA) by incubating them for 20 minutes at room temperature (RT). After fixation, the bacteria were washed and resuspended in PBS. Pooled serum samples from mice, rats, and rabbits, pre-diluted in PBS with 1% BSA (1:200 for mice and rabbits, 1:500 for rats), were added to the bacterial suspension, which had been previously distributed in 96-well plates, and incubated for 20 minutes at RT. Sera dilutions were established through preliminary titration experiments. The plates were washed, and AlexaFluor488-conjugated IgG anti-mouse, anti-rabbit or anti-rat antibodies (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA)(diluted 1:200 for mice and rabbits, and 1:1000 for rats) were added and incubated for an additional 20 minutes at RT. The plates were washed and read in a BD Accuri™ C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, CA, USA) and signal was measured in the fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) channel. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was used to generate heatmaps depicting the binding of antisera to Shigella bacterial strains.

Statistical analyses

For clinical results, the following metrics were calculated for SBA titers against each serotype: geometric mean titers (GMTs) with their associated 2-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs), within-participant geometric mean ratios (GMRs) with their associated 2-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and the number and percentage of participants showing a ≥4-fold increase in titers from baseline (seroresponse rate) with their exact (Clopper-Pearson) 96% Cis.

For animal studies, GMTs and within-animal GMRs were calculated.

For the comparison of anti-OAg IgG responses from sera at day 27 or day 42 of mice immunized using altSonflex1-2-3 and tri-, bi- or mono-component formulations, the Mann-Whitney test was used.

Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was calculated to assess the relationship between SBA responses induced after immunization with altSonflex1-2-3 and tri-, bi- or mono-component formulations against homologous versus heterologous strains. The Pearson correlation data are available in Supplementary Table 3.

When animals are required, the application of robust study design principles and peer review minimizes animal use, reduces harm, and improves benefit in studies. The animal husbandry and experimental procedures were ethically reviewed and carried out in the GSK Animal Research center (mice and rabbits) and in Charles River Laboratories (France, rats) in compliance with the relevant guidelines (European directive 2010/63/UE) and the institutional policies of GSK.
