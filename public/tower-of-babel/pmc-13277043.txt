Biomechanical efficacy of a medial buttress plate in the management of concomitant ipsilateral subcapital and intertrochanteric femoral fractures: a finite element analysis

ABSTRACT

Background

Ipsilateral femoral neck and intertrochanteric fractures (IFNIF) are relatively rare, with a higher incidence observed in infrahead femoral fractures, which often occur as secondary complications following internal fixation surgery for combined intertrochanteric fractures [1–4]. The simultaneous occurrence of IFNIF is exceedingly uncommon, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature [5–8]. The dynamic hip screw (DHS) fixation has been widely utilized for managing this type of fracture [8, 9]. However, DHS is associated with a high failure rate, ranging from 25% to 60% [10, 11]. Although there are literatures report that the treatment of unstable intertrochanteric fractures with proximal femoral plates can achieve good healing compared to DHS [12, 13], there is still a high incidence of complications [12, 14].

Consequently, there is an urgent need for improved fixation techniques to address these fractures in clinical practice. The management of femoral neck fractures may provide valuable insights for orthopedic surgeons when treating this complex fracture pattern. Studies indicate that 20% to 50% of patients undergoing surgery for displaced femoral neck fractures experience post-operative complications, including avascular necrosis (AVN), malunion, and nonunion [15, 16]. The treatment of displaced femoral neck fractures remains a significant challenge in orthopedic surgery. A common clinical approach involves the placement of three parallel cannulated lag screws along the femoral neck axis. However, this method carries a substantial risk of failure, particularly due to shear forces in type III fractures [17]. Previous studies have reported failure rates of 10% to 30% with parallel screw fixation, with complications such as screw dislodgement, nonunion, and femoral head osteonecrosis [18, 19]. To address these challenges, Mir and Collinge proposed the use of a medial buttress plate for managing displaced femoral neck fractures [20]. This approach was inspired by the application of buttress (or “anti-glide”) plate fixation in tibial and distal radius fractures, where stability is achieved by resisting shear forces under compression or axial loading [21, 22]. The proposed technique involves placing a buttress plate over the apex of the displaced femoral neck fracture to convert shear forces into compressive forces, thereby achieving Medial Buttress Stability (MBS). The surgical procedure combines cannulated screws with a medial buttress 1/3 tubular plate. Previous studies have demonstrated that this technique resulted in a higher fracture union rate compared to historical controls using cannulated screws alone [23–25].

Building on this concept, we recently employed a medial buttress plate combined with a proximal femoral plate to manage IFNIF, achieving favorable clinical outcomes (Fig. 1). However, the lack of biomechanical evidence currently limits the validation of this approach. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the biomechanical effects of a medial buttress plate on IFNIF using finite element analysis.

Methods

Finite element biomechanical study

Collection of imaging data

Radiographic images of an unremarkable femur from a 25-year-old male were obtained using a 64-slice computed tomography (CT) scanner with 0.5-mm thick slices to examine bone tissue. The scanning parameters were set to 155 mA and 120 kV. The resulting CT data were stored in DICOM format with a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels.

Finite element model of proximal femur construction

The DICOM images were imported into Mimics 17 software (Materialise, Belgium) to construct a three-dimensional (3D) model of the femur. The process involved threshold segmentation, region growth, and 3D model calculation within Mimics 17. Subsequent physicochemical processing was performed using Imageware 13.0 software (Siemens, Plano, TX) and Geomagic 2012 software (Cary, NC) to refine the proximal femur model.

IFNIF was delineated using ProE 5.0 software (PTC Inc., Boston, MA). Two distinct surgical procedures were simulated for fracture fixation: In the individual proximal femoral plate (PP) group, the fracture was stabilized using only a proximal femoral plate (Fig. 2a); In the medial buttress plate combined with a proximal femoral plate (MPP) group, the fracture was stabilized using both a medial buttress plate and a proximal femoral plate (Fig. 2b).

Construction of the PP (a) and MPP (b) model

Volume mesh generation

The 3D model, along with the internal fixation system, was exported in STEP format and imported into ANSYS Workbench 2020R2 software (ANSYS, Inc., Pittsburgh, PA, USA). The screws were modeled with a thickness of 3 mm, and the plates were modeled with a thickness of 2 mm. After meshing, the PP model consisted of 127,808 elements and 285,000 nodes, while the MPP model comprised 135,642 elements and 302,470 nodes.

Model validation

To ensure the predictive validity of the finite element framework, a two-step validation process was employed. First, the mesh convergence analysis guaranteed that the results were not dependent on discretization parameters. Second, the mechanical response of the intact femur model under a simplified stance-phase load was validated against published experimental data. The structural stiffness of our intact femur model was calculated to be approximately 1840 N/mm under axial loading, which falls within the range of 1750–1950 N/mm reported for composite femur models in the literature [26, 27]. This quantitative comparison confirms that the model’s output is within established physiological ranges, providing confidence in its use as a baseline for further comparisons.

Study design, ethics, and statistical analysis

This study is a finite element analysis (FEA) comparing the biomechanical performance of two fixation constructs (MPP vs. PP) for the management of ipsilateral femoral neck and intertrochanteric fractures (IFNIF). The study was approved by the Ethics Committees of both Ningbo Hangzhou Bay Hospital and Shanghai Jiao Tong University Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital South Campus. As this was a deterministic computational study without repeated sampling, traditional inferential statistical analysis was not applicable. All comparisons are based on the outputs of the finite element model and reflect relative differences in simulated biomechanical responses.

Assignment of material properties

Based on previous studies [28], material parameters were assigned as shown in Table 1. The Poisson’s ratio for cortical and cancellous bone was set to 0.3, with elastic moduli of 18,160 MPa and 840 MPa, respectively. The plates were simulated using titanium alloy Ti6Al4V, with an elastic modulus of 210,000 MPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3.

Material parameters for the bone and fixation systems

The percentages (2%, 10%, 20%, 50%) correspond to the bone reduction factor

For the fracture bone callus, material properties were assigned based on the degree of bone reduction. Bone tissue exhibits remarkable self-repair and regeneration capabilities, with healing time influenced by intrinsic genetic factors. Following Manjubala et al. [29], who evaluated the nanoindentation modulus of callus during bone healing, and considering the variability in bone reduction, elastic modulus values were assigned proportionally to the healthy cortical bone (100% bone reduction factor). Given that the fracture occurred three months post-implantation, the elastic modulus of the callus was set to 50% of the healthy bone value. The Poisson’s ratio was maintained at 0.3 for all scenarios.

Contact definitions

The “Tolerance Type” in the software’s connections option was set to “Value” with a “Tolerance Value” of 0.05, allowing the software to automatically generate connections. Based on prior literature [30–32], contact definitions were established as follows: (1) Cortical and cancellous bone interfaces were defined as Bonded; (2) Bone fragments at the fracture ends were designated as Rough; (3) All other interfaces were set to No Separation.

Loading and boundary conditions

A mesh was generated with element sizes ranging from 4 mm to 0.3 mm (Supplementary Material 1), and a convergence analysis with a 5% tolerance [31, 32] was performed.

To evaluate the structural behavior of the plate, three load conditions were simulated: walking, stumbling, and lateral falling. These conditions were selected to assess stress and strain distributions under daily and incidental loads, identifying configurations that may exceed the plate’s breaking limit. The force magnitudes and directions were derived from established in-vivo measurements. For the walking condition, the peak contact force during the gait cycle was applied [33]. The stumbling condition, representing a high-impact event, was modeled using forces measured during a stumble [34]. For the lateral falling condition, a simplified model was applied based on prior FEA studies [28, 35]. Muscle forces were not directly simulated in this analysis, as the primary focus was on comparing the internal fixation construct’s response to the dominant joint contact forces. Walking Configuration: Based on Bergmann et al. [33], the peak contact force during the walking cycle was applied. Force components were calculated for a 95 kg patient. Stumbling Configuration: Following Bergmann et al. [34], force components were derived for the same patient mass. Lateral Falling Configuration: Based on Aldieri et al. [35] and Terzini et al. [28], the prosthetic head was connected to the ground via a spring element (stiffness: 10,000 N/mm) along the x-axis to simulate soft tissue damping. Patient mass and height were overestimated (95 kg, 1.70 m) to test plate resistance under extreme conditions.

Mechanical load application

The distal femur point B was identified using ANSYS, and loading was simulated as follows: Walking: A force of 2218 N was applied (Fig. 3a); Stumbling: A force of 6710 N was applied (Fig. 3a); Lateral Falling: A force of 7688.6 N was applied (Fig. 3b). The load magnitudes and directions for the three conditions were built based on previous studies [28, 33–35].

Loading and boundary conditions.

Walking (a) [31]: Load 2218 N at point A, set as fixed constraint at point B; Stumbling (a) [32]: load 6710 N at point A, set as fixed constraint at point B, with loading and fixing positions consistent with walking. Lateral Falling (b) [33]: Apply a spring element connected to the ground at the femoral head with a stiffness coefficient of 10,000 N/mm, load 7688.6 N at point A, and set as a fixed constraint at point B

Evaluation of results

After applying mechanical loads, deformation, von Mises stress and equivalent elastic strain distribution changes in the femur were analyzed.

Results

Finite element analysis: deformation distribution

This study examines the relationship between elastic modulus and deformation under three loading conditions: Walking, Stumbling, and Lateral Falling (Tables 2, 3 and 4). Walking Condition (Figs. 4 and 5, Supplementary Material 1): As the elastic modulus increased, deformation generally decreased in both the MPP and PP groups. However, the PP group consistently exhibited higher deformation values compared to the MPP group across all elastic modulus values. Specifically, at an elastic modulus of 363.2 MPa, the PP group demonstrated a total model deformation of 68.249 mm, significantly higher than the 51.875 mm observed in the MPP group. It is important to clarify that this value represents the global displacement of the entire model under the applied load, not the relative motion at the fracture site (interfragmentary displacement). Stumbling Condition (Figs. 6 and 7, Supplementary Material 2): Both groups showed a significant increase in deformation compared to the Walking condition. The PP group continued to exhibit higher deformations, reaching a maximum of 206.520 mm at 363.2 MPa. This suggests that the MPP group is less susceptible to deformation under sudden impact loads, highlighting its superior ability to distribute stress effectively. Lateral Falling Condition (Figs. 8 and 9, Supplementary Material 3): Deformations in both groups were notably lower compared to the Walking and Stumbling conditions, indicating minimal impact strain under such loads. However, the PP group again demonstrated slightly higher deformation values.

Finite element analysis: von Mises stress distribution

Walking Condition (Figs. 4, 5 and 10): The von Mises stress in the PP group consistently exceeded that in the MPP group. For example, at an elastic modulus of 363.2 MPa, the stress in the large plate reached 721.65 MPa in the PP group, compared to 621.29 MPa in the MPP group. Based on the finite element model outputs, this suggests that the PP construct may be more prone to fixation failure under normal walking conditions. Stumbling Condition (Figs. 6, 7 and 10): Stress levels increased dramatically in both groups. The PP group recorded a maximum stress of 2183.70 MPa at 363.2 MPa, significantly higher than the MPP group. Lateral Falling Condition (Figs. 8, 9 and 10): Stress values decreased compared to the Stumbling condition, suggesting reduced impact. The maximum stress in the medial buttress plate of the MPP group reached 1699.3 MPa, while the femur experienced a stress of 253.20 MPa (Table 4). The PP group again exhibited higher stress on the large plate.

Overall, the PP group incurred higher stress levels, which may increase the risk of material failure in areas subjected to high stress concentrations, particularly during sudden impacts.

Finite element analysis: equivalent elastic strain distribution

Walking Condition (Figs. 4 and 5): The equivalent elastic strain values in the PP group consistently surpassed those in the MPP group. The maximum strain observed in the PP group was 0.18771, compared to 0.1422 in the MPP group, indicating higher deformation in the PP group under low-load scenarios. Stumbling Condition (Figs. 6 and 7): Strain values increased markedly in both groups. The PP group exhibited a maximum strain of 0.56801, approximately 34% higher than the MPP group’s maximum of 0.42382. This suggests that the PP group is more susceptible to significant deformation under dynamic loading, raising concerns about its structural integrity. Lateral Falling Condition (Figs. 8 and 9): Strain values were comparatively lower than those observed during Stumbling. The PP group’s maximum strain was 0.1418, slightly exceeding the MPP group’s maximum of 0.13446. Both groups exhibited similar performance, indicating that lateral falling imposes less severe strain compared to stumbling. In both Walking and Stumbling conditions, the PP group displayed significantly higher strain values than the MPP group, suggesting that the MPP group offers greater stability, particularly under dynamic loading. The Stumbling condition resulted in the highest strain values across all groups, emphasizing the need for robust design solutions to mitigate deformation during sudden impacts.

Discussion

This finite element analysis demonstrates that MPP technique provides superior biomechanical stability compared to PP fixation in managing IFNIF. The MPP group exhibited reduced deformation (51.875 mm vs. 68.249 mm at 363.2 MPa elastic modulus), lower Von Mises stress (621.29 MPa vs. 721.65 MPa), and diminished equivalent elastic strain (0.1422 vs. 0.18771) under walking conditions. These advantages persisted during high-stress scenarios, with the MPP group showing 34% lower strain than the PP group during stumbling (0.42382 vs. 0.56801) and reduced stress concentration during lateral falls (1699.3 MPa in MPP vs. 2183.70 MPa in PP). These findings align with our clinical observations of improved fracture union and reduced complications using MPP, suggesting its potential to address the historically high failure rates associated with DHS or proximal femoral plate fixation [12, 14].

IFNIF remains a rare but surgically challenging entity, predominantly affecting elderly patients after low-energy falls. Current evidence, primarily derived from small cohort studies [5–8], indicates that treatment algorithms remain age-dependent: younger patients (< 60 years) typically undergo joint-preserving DHS with cannulated screw fixation, while elderly patients (≥ 65 years) more frequently receive hemiarthroplasty to minimize surgical risks. Although DHS demonstrates satisfactory outcomes in stable fracture configurations, its biomechanical limitations become apparent in unstable or displaced patterns, with a high failure rate in comminuted fractures [36]. The MPP technique builds on the principles of MBS proposed by Mir and Collinge [20], which converts shear forces into compressive loads-a concept validated in tibial and distal radius fractures [21, 22]. By integrating a 1/3 tubular buttress plate with proximal femoral fixation, our method extends this biomechanical advantage to IFNIF, potentially mitigating common complications such as screw cut-out (10%-30% risk in traditional methods) and fixation migration [18, 19].

Clinically, the MPP’s enhanced stability may facilitate early postoperative mobilization, critical for reducing immobilization-related complications (e.g., deep vein thrombosis, pneumonia) prevalent in 20%-60% of femoral neck fracture patients [15, 37]. Ye Y et al. [23] previously reported a noticeable increase in union rates with medial plating for isolated femoral neck fractures; our data suggest similar benefits could extend to IFNIF through optimized load distribution.

A critical observation from our analysis pertains to the magnitude of the recorded stresses. The peak von Mises stresses observed, particularly under high-impact conditions (e.g., 2180 MPa in the PP construct during stumbling and 1700 MPa in the medial buttress plate during a lateral fall), exceed the typical yield strength of Ti6Al4V titanium alloy (approximately 800–900 MPa) [31]. It is critical to interpret these values within the context of our model’s assumptions. The material behavior was modeled as linear-elastic; thus, these stresses indicate locations of high numerical stress concentration that, in a clinical scenario, would lead to plastic deformation or mechanical failure. This finding directly addresses a core clinical concern: the potential for implant failure under accidental loading. The biomechanical advantage of the MPP construct lies in its ability to distribute these critical loads. By offloading stress from the primary proximal femoral plate to the medial buttress plate-which is specifically designed to resist shear-the MPP system likely mitigates concentrated stress that could lead to catastrophic failure in a single-plate construct. It suggests that during an extreme event such as a stumble, both fixation methods may be pushed beyond their elastic limit, risking plastic deformation or fatigue failure. However, the biomechanical advantage of the MPP construct lies in its ability to distribute these critical loads. By offloading stress from the primary proximal femoral plate to the medial buttress plate-which is specifically designed to resist shear-the MPP system likely mitigates concentrated stress that could lead to rapid, catastrophic failure in a single-plate construct. Therefore, while neither construct may be immune to failure under ultimate loads, the MPP technique appears to offer a superior safety margin by optimizing load sharing, potentially reducing the risk of sudden fixation collapse in the postoperative period.

While our FEA results robustly demonstrate the biomechanical superiority of the MPP construct, a direct extrapolation to clinical outcomes requires careful consideration. Our model necessarily operates under several idealized assumptions, including perfect fracture reduction, optimal bone-implant contact, and homogeneous, linear-elastic material properties. In clinical reality, failure is often governed by biological and patient-specific factors not captured here, such as screw cut-out in osteoporotic bone, variability in bone healing (non-union), or suboptimal surgical technique.

Consequently, the statement that enhanced biomechanical stability will directly facilitate “early postoperative mobilization” is a compelling hypothesis derived from this biomechanical study but remains speculative based on computational evidence alone. Future research, ideally in the form of controlled clinical trials comparing the two techniques, is essential to validate whether the mechanical advantages of the MPP construct translate into tangible benefits such as faster rehabilitation, lower complication rates, and improved functional outcomes. Limitations and future directions

While this study provides mechanistic insights, its finite element model does not account for biological variables such as osteoporotic bone remodeling or soft tissue contributions. Furthermore, the model is based on the femur geometry of a 25-year-old male, which does not fully represent the osteoporotic bone quality of the elderly population most susceptible to these fractures. The bone-implant interface was modeled with a “No Separation” contact, which is a simplification that does not fully capture the potential for micromotion or screw loosening. Additionally, the analysis focused on a single fracture geometry under standardized loading, which may not fully capture clinical variability. The progression of callus stiffness was modeled based on literature values, but individual healing rates can vary significantly. Future research should incorporate: Multiscale modeling integrating bone healing dynamics; Comparative clinical trials assessing long-term functional outcomes; Cost-benefit analyses of MPP versus arthroplasty in elderly populations. In addition, experimental validation using cadaveric specimens with adequate sample size is recommended to account for biological variability and to further corroborate the biomechanical findings of this finite element analysis.

Conclusion

In this finite element analysis, the MPP technique demonstrated enhanced biomechanical stability compared to PP fixation for the management of IFNIF. These findings suggest a potential to reduce the risk of internal fixation migration and failure, providing a biomechanical rationale for its clinical use. Further clinical studies are warranted to confirm these biomechanical advantages in patient outcomes.
