On constant mean curvature surfaces satisfying integrable boundary conditions Thanks: Mathematics Subject Classification. 53A10, 53C42, 37K10. August 24, 2026
Martin Kilian

Abstract
. We consider the local theory of constant mean curvature surfaces that satisfy one or two integrable boundary conditions and determine the corresponding potentials for the generalized Weierstrass representation.



Introduction

Imposing additional properties, like symmetries, embeddedness, simply-connectedness or compactness on a constant mean curvature surface often severely limits the classes of examples. Historically, the first reduction by Delaunay
[
3
]
resulted in classifying the rotationally symmetric examples. A few decades later, Enneper
[
7
,
8
]
and Dobriner
[
4
,
5
]
considered surfaces foliated by planar or spherical curvature lines. Modern accounts and further developments can be found in the works of Abresch
[
1
]
and Wente
[
12
,
13
]
amongst many others, where the structure equations are reduced to elliptic ordinary differential equations.

Here we consider the local theory of constant mean curvature surfaces that satisfy one or two integrable boundary conditions. The integrable boundary condition reflects the geometric property that the surface meets a sphere at a constant angle along the curve of intersection. From our integrable systems point of view this means studying the corresponding ’potentials’ in the generalized Weierstrass representation of Dorfmeister, Pedit and Wu
[
6
]
.

By a result of
[
10
]
all such surfaces are of finite type, so their potentials are polynomial, and the purpose here is to determine all such potentials. We first study the additional so called ’K-symmetry’ on the space of potentials that comes from the integrable boundary condition. In Theorem
2.5
we show that K-symmetry halves the dimension of the space of potentials of arbitrary fixed degree. We show in Corollary
3.4
that all off-diagonal K-symmetric potentials of arbitrary high degree yield degree one surfaces, that is, associated family members of Delaunay surfaces. In the second part we consider potentials that give solutions of the elliptic
\sinh
-Gordon equation satisfying two integrable boundary conditions, that is free boundary constant mean curvature surfaces with two boundary components. We do not consider period problems here, but focus on the algebraic conditions for the local theory of such surfaces.

1.
Integrable boundary conditions

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an open, connected and simply-connected set containing the origin.
An associated family
\mathbf{f}_{\lambda}:U\to\mathfrak{su}_{\mbox{\tiny{2}}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3},\,\lambda\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
of conformaly immersed surfaces with constant mean curvature
H\neq 0
can be framed by an
\mathrm{SU}_{2}
–valued
extended frame

\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}
. It satisfies a linear partial differential system
\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}^{-1}d\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{\Omega}_{\lambda}
. Here
\mathbf{\Omega}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{U}_{\lambda}dx+\mathbf{V}_{\lambda}dy
is a
2\times 2
matrix-valued 1-form which encodes the surface invariants. For the induced metric
\exp[2\omega]\,(dx^{2}+dy^{2})
the
\sinh
-Gordon equation

\Delta\,\omega+\sinh\omega=0

is the integrability condition

2d\mathbf{\Omega}_{\lambda}+[\mathbf{\Omega}_{\lambda}\wedge\mathbf{\Omega}_{\lambda}]=0

for
\mathbf{\Omega}_{\lambda}
. There is a loop group method to obtain integrable connections via a generalized Weierstrass representation
[
6
]
. For us it suffices, due to a result in
[
10
]
, to restrict to the finite gap setting, for which we invoke the Symes method
[
2
]
: extended frames can be obtained from polynomial Killing fields, and these in turn are determined by their intial conditions, the finite gap potentials. A polynomial Killing field is a solution of a Lax equation

d\eta=[\eta,\,\Omega(\eta)]\qquad\eta(0)=\xi_{\lambda}\,.

The initial condition (or potential)
\xi_{\lambda}
is a
\mathrm{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
–valued Laurent-polynomial
\lambda\mapsto\xi_{\lambda}
and
\eta\mapsto\Omega(\eta)
is a projection, and
\Omega=\Omega(\eta)
integrates to an extended frame
\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}^{-1}d\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}=\Omega
of a constant mean curvature surface. The relationship between extended frame and polynomial Killing field is
\eta_{\lambda}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}^{-1}\xi_{\lambda}\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}
. Writing
\partial_{\lambda}
for differentiation with respect to
\lambda
, the corresponding associated family is given by

(1.1)

\mathbf{f}_{\lambda}=-2\,\mathbbm{i}\lambda H^{-1}(\partial_{\lambda}\mathbf{F}_{\lambda})\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}^{-1}\,.

After a choice of normalizations as in
[
9
]
for the surface invariants,
\mathbf{U}_{\lambda}
takes the form

\mathbf{U}_{\lambda}=\frac{\mathbbm{i}}{8}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-2\omega_{y}&e^{\omega}\lambda^{-1}+e^{-\omega}\\
e^{-\omega}+e^{\omega}\lambda&2\omega_{y}\\
\end{array}\right)\,.

Note that

\overline{\mathbf{U}_{\bar{\lambda}}}^{t}=-\mathbf{U}_{\lambda^{-1}}\qquad\mbox{ and}\qquad\overline{\mathbf{F}_{1/\bar{\lambda}}}^{t}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}^{-1}\,.

We study solutions
\omega:U\to\mathbb{R}
of the sinh-Gordon equation that satisfy an additional
integrable boundary condition

(1.2)

\omega_{y}=e^{\omega}A+e^{-\omega}B\qquad\mbox{ along \quad$y=0$}\,.

Here
A
and
B
are arbitrary real numbers. Also, when we write ’along
y=0
’ we mean its restriction to the domain, so
\{y=0\}\cap U
.

1.1.
K-matrix

Define for
A,\,B\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
the K-matrix

(1.3)

K(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}4A-4B\lambda&\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\\
\lambda-\lambda^{-1}&4A-4B\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

Note that
K(\pm 1)=4(A\mp B)\,\mathbbm{1}
, as well as

(1.4)

K(\lambda)=K(\lambda)^{\,t}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\overline{K(\bar{\lambda})}=K(\lambda)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad K(\lambda^{-1})=\mathrm{adj}\,K(\lambda)\,.

Lemma 1.1
.

For
\lambda\neq\pm 1
we have
\omega_{y}=e^{\omega}A+e^{-\omega}B
along
y=0
if and only if

(1.5)

K\,\mathbf{U}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{U}_{\lambda^{-1}}\,K\,.

Proof.

Compute
K\,\mathbf{U}_{\lambda}-\mathbf{U}_{\lambda^{-1}}\,K=\tfrac{\mathbbm{i}}{2}(\lambda^{-1}-\lambda)\left(\omega_{y}-\left(e^{\omega}A+e^{-\omega}B\right)\right)\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\\
\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
.
∎

Lemma 1.2
.

Suppose
\tfrac{d}{dx}\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}\mathbf{U}_{\lambda},\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}(0)=\mathbbm{1}
. Then along
y=0
the condition (
1.5
) holds if and only if

(1.6)

K\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}\,K\qquad\mbox{ along \hskip 10.22217pt$y=0$}\,.

Proof.

Suppose along
y=0
the condition (
1.5
) holds. Consider
\mathbf{G}_{\lambda}=K\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}\,K^{-1}
. Then
\tfrac{d}{dx}\mathbf{G}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{G}_{\lambda}\mathbf{U}_{\lambda^{-1}},\,\mathbf{G}_{\lambda}(0)=\mathbbm{1}
. By uniqueness of solutions
\mathbf{G}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}
, and thus
K\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}\,K^{-1}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}
.

Conversely, suppose (
1.6
) holds. Differentiating with respect to
x
gives
K\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}^{\prime}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}^{\prime}\,K
and hence
K\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}\mathbf{U}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}\mathbf{U}_{\lambda^{-1}}\,K
. Using (
1.6
) again gives the claim.
∎

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}
be an extended frame satisfying (
1.6
) along
y=0
, and
\zeta_{\lambda}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}^{-1}\xi_{\lambda}\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}
a polynomial Killing field. Then along

y=0
we have

(1.7)

K\zeta_{\lambda}+\overline{\zeta_{\bar{\lambda}}}^{t}K=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}^{-1}\bigl(\,K\,\xi_{\lambda}+\overline{\xi_{\bar{\lambda}}}^{t}K\,\bigr)\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}\,.

A polynomial Killing field
\zeta_{\lambda}
is K-symmetric (along
y=0
) if

(1.8)

K\zeta_{\lambda}+\overline{\zeta_{\bar{\lambda}}}^{t}K=0\,.

By (
1.7
) this holds if and only if its potential
\xi_{\lambda}
is K-symmetric

(1.9)

K\,\xi_{\lambda}+\overline{\xi_{\bar{\lambda}}}^{t}K=0\,.

1.2.
K-symmetry and the Symes map

The Symes map
\xi_{\lambda}\mapsto\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}
utilizes the Iwasawa factorization
[
11
]
at each
z\in U
, and we write

(1.10)

\mathbf{\Phi}_{\lambda}(z)=\exp[z\,\xi_{\lambda}]=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}(z,\bar{z})\,\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}(z,\bar{z})\,.

With
\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}^{-1}d\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{\Omega}_{\lambda}
we have the gauge relation

(1.11)

\xi_{\lambda}=\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}_{\lambda}\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}+\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}^{-1}d\,\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}\,.

Next, we determine how K-symmetry (
1.9
) on a potential descends to the maps
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\lambda}
and
\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}
. Lemma
1.2
already dealt with the extended frame
\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}
, as its K-symmetry (
1.6
) came directly from its differential equation.

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
\xi_{\lambda}
be a K-symmetric potential. Then

(1.12)

K\,\mathbf{\Phi}_{\lambda}(z)=\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}_{\bar{\lambda}}(\bar{z})}^{t^{-1}}K\qquad\mbox{for all $z\in U$}\,.

Proof.

We compute
\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}_{\bar{\lambda}}(\bar{z})}^{\,t}=\exp[z\,\overline{\xi_{\bar{\lambda}}}^{\,t}]=\exp[-z\,K\,\xi_{\lambda}K^{-1}]=K\,\mathbf{\Phi}_{\lambda}^{-1}(z)\,K^{-1}
.
∎

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
\xi_{\lambda}
be a
K
-symmetric potential, and
\exp[z\,\xi_{\lambda}]=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}\,\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}
the pointwise Iwasawa factorization for
z\in U
. Assume that

K\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}\,K
along
y=0
. Then

(1.13)

K\,\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}=\overline{\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\lambda}}}^{t^{-1}}K\qquad\mbox{ along \hskip 10.22217pt$y=0$}\,.

Proof.

By Lemma
1.4
we have
K\,\mathbf{\Phi}_{\lambda}=\overline{\mathbf{\Phi}_{\bar{\lambda}}}^{t^{-1}}K
and thus

K\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}\,\mathbf{B}_{\lambda}=\overline{\mathbf{F}_{\bar{\lambda}}}^{t^{-1}}\overline{\mathbf{B}_{\bar{\lambda}}}^{t^{-1}}K\,.

Using the assumption on
\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}
along
y=0
, and that
\overline{\mathbf{F}_{\bar{\lambda}}}^{t}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}^{-1}
we obtain (
1.13
).
∎

1.3.
K-symmetry and the associated family

Finally, we look at K-symmetry of the associated family. Let
\partial_{\lambda}
denote differentiation with respect to
\lambda
.

Lemma 1.6
.

Assume that
K\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}\,K
along
y=0
. Then for the associated family (
1.1
) we have along
y=0
that

(1.14)

K\,\mathbf{f}_{\lambda}+\overline{\mathbf{f}_{\lambda}}^{t}K=-2\mathbbm{i}\lambda H^{-1}\left(K(\partial_{\lambda}\mathbf{F}_{\lambda})-(\partial_{\lambda}\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}})K\right)\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}^{-1}\,.

Proof.

Differentiating
K\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}\,K
with respect to
\lambda
gives

(1.15)

\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}\,\partial_{\lambda}K-\partial_{\lambda}K\,\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}=K\partial_{\lambda}\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}-\partial_{\lambda}\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}\,K\,.

Inserting
\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}=K^{-1}\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}K
into (
1.1
) yields

\mathbf{f}_{\lambda}=K^{-1}\mathbf{f}_{\lambda^{-1}}K-2\mathbbm{i}\lambda H^{-1}\left(K^{-1}\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}\partial_{\lambda}K\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}^{-1}-K^{-1}\partial_{\lambda}K\right)

and thus

K\mathbf{f}_{\lambda}-\mathbf{f}_{\lambda^{-1}}K=-2\mathbbm{i}\lambda H^{-1}\left(\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}}\partial_{\lambda}K-\partial_{\lambda}K\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}\right)\mathbf{F}_{\lambda}^{-1}\,.

Using (
1.15
) and
\mathbf{f}_{\lambda^{-1}}=-\overline{\mathbf{f}_{\lambda}}^{t}
proves the claim.
∎

We say that an associated family is K-symmetric along
y=0
if

K\,\mathbf{f}_{\lambda}+\overline{\mathbf{f}_{\lambda}}^{t}K=0\,.

By equation (
1.14
) this holds if and only if
K(\partial_{\lambda}\mathbf{F}_{\lambda})=(\partial_{\lambda}\mathbf{F}_{\lambda^{-1}})K
.

2.
K-symmetric potentials

Expanding the matrix of a finite-gap potential as

\xi_{\lambda}=\sum_{k=-1}^{d}\hat{\xi}_{k}\lambda^{k}\,,

then its coefficient matrices
\hat{\xi}_{k}\in\mathrm{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
satisfy the
reality condition

(2.1)

\hat{\xi}_{k}=-\overline{\hat{\xi\,}}_{d-k-1}^{t}\quad-1\leq k\leq d\,.

To match up with the structure of
\mathbf{U}_{\lambda}
we have

(2.2)

\hat{\xi}_{-1}\in\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbbm{i}\mathbb{R}_{+}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

which ensures that also
\hat{\xi}_{d}\neq 0
. We say that
\xi_{\lambda}
has
degree

d
. Let us write

\xi_{\lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{\lambda}&\beta_{\lambda}\\
\gamma_{\lambda}&-\alpha_{\lambda}\end{pmatrix}

with entries

\alpha_{\lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\alpha_{k}\lambda^{k}\,,\qquad\beta_{\lambda}=\sum_{k=-1}^{d-1}\beta_{k}\lambda^{k}\,\,,\qquad\gamma_{\lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\gamma_{k}\lambda^{k}\qquad\mathrm{for}\quad\alpha_{k},\,\beta_{k},\,\gamma_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\,.

The reality condition (
2.1
) on the coefficients read

(2.3)

\gamma_{k}=-\overline{\beta}_{d-k-1}\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\alpha_{k}=-\overline{\alpha}_{d-k-1}

The latter splits into real and imaginary parts

\RE[\alpha_{k}+\alpha_{d-k-1}]=0=\IM[\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{d-k-1}]\,.

When degree
d
is odd, then for
k=(d-1)/2
also
d-k-1=(d-1)/2
and hence

\RE\alpha_{(d-1)/2}=0\,.

Note that there is no freedom in the choice of
\gamma_{\lambda}
, as all its coefficients are determined by the coefficients of
\beta_{\lambda}
. There are
2d+1
real degrees of freedom for
\beta_{\lambda}
, and
d
real degrees of freedom for
\alpha_{\lambda}
when
d
is even, and
d-1
when
d
id odd. Hence the space of potentials of degree
d
is an open subset of a
3d+1
dimensional real vectorspace when
d
is even, and an open subset of a
3d
dimensional real vectorspace when
d
is odd.

In terms of the four matrix entries [ij] in the order [11], [12], [21] respectively [22], equation (
1.9
) reads

(2.4)

\begin{split}(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})\left(\gamma_{\lambda}+\overline{\gamma_{\bar{\lambda}}}\right)+4(A-B\lambda)\left(\alpha_{\lambda}+\overline{\alpha_{\bar{\lambda}}}\right)&\equiv 0\,,\\
-(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})\left(\alpha_{\lambda}-\overline{\alpha_{\bar{\lambda}}}\right)+4(A-B\lambda)\beta_{\lambda}+4(A-B\lambda^{-1})\overline{\gamma_{\bar{\lambda}}}&\equiv 0\,,\\
(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})\left(\alpha_{\lambda}-\overline{\alpha_{\bar{\lambda}}}\right)+4(A-B\lambda)\overline{\beta_{\bar{\lambda}}}+4(A-B\lambda^{-1})\gamma_{\lambda}&\equiv 0\,,\\
(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})\left(\beta_{\lambda}+\overline{\beta_{\bar{\lambda}}}\right)-4(A-B\lambda^{-1})\left(\alpha_{\lambda}+\overline{\alpha_{\bar{\lambda}}}\right)&\equiv 0\,.\end{split}

Inspection of the two diagonal conditions in (
2.4
) proves

Proposition 2.1
.

For the entries of a
K-symmetric potential we have

(i)

\displaystyle{\alpha_{\lambda}+\overline{\alpha_{\bar{\lambda}}}\equiv 0\Longleftrightarrow\beta_{\lambda}+\overline{\beta_{\bar{\lambda}}}\equiv 0}\Longleftrightarrow\gamma_{\lambda}+\overline{\gamma_{\bar{\lambda}}}\equiv 0
.

(ii)

\displaystyle{\alpha_{\lambda}\equiv 0\Longrightarrow\beta_{\lambda}+\overline{\beta_{\bar{\lambda}}}\equiv 0}
.

In each of the four equations (
2.4
) we have powers of
\lambda
ranging from
\lambda^{-1}
to
\lambda^{d+1}
. Comparing coefficients gives for
k=-1,\,\ldots,\,d+1
the system

(2.5)

\begin{split}-\left(\overline{\gamma}_{k+1}+\gamma_{k+1}\right)+4A\left(\overline{\alpha}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\right)+\overline{\gamma}_{k-1}+\gamma_{k-1}-4B\left(\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}+\alpha_{k-1}\right)&=0\\
\alpha_{k+1}-\overline{\alpha}_{k+1}-4B\overline{\gamma}_{k+1}+4A(\overline{\gamma}_{k}+\beta_{k})+\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}-\alpha_{k-1}-4B\beta_{k-1}&=0\\
\overline{\alpha}_{k+1}-\alpha_{k+1}-4B\gamma_{k+1}+4A(\overline{\beta}_{k}+\gamma_{k})+\alpha_{k-1}-\overline{\alpha}_{k-1}-4B\overline{\beta}_{k-1}&=0\\
4B(\overline{\alpha}_{k+1}+\alpha_{k+1})-\overline{\beta}_{k+1}-\beta_{k+1}-4A(\overline{\alpha}_{k}+\alpha_{k})+\beta_{k-1}+\overline{\beta}_{k-1}&=0\,.\end{split}

Splitting into real part
\RE
and imaginary part
\IM
gives

(2.6)

\begin{split}-\RE\gamma_{k+1}+4A\RE\alpha_{k}+\RE\gamma_{k-1}-4B\RE\alpha_{k-1}&=0\\
\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{k+1}-2B\overline{\gamma}_{k+1}+2A(\overline{\gamma}_{k}+\beta_{k})-\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{k-1}-2B\beta_{k-1}&=0\\
-\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{k+1}-2B\gamma_{k+1}+2A(\overline{\beta}_{k}+\gamma_{k})+\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{k-1}-2B\overline{\beta}_{k-1}&=0\\
4B\RE\alpha_{k+1}-\RE\beta_{k+1}-4A\RE\alpha_{k}+\RE\beta_{k-1}&=0\,.\end{split}

2.1.
Real Parts.

Adding the first and last equations in (
2.6
) gives the recursion

(2.7)

4B\RE[\alpha_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}]-\RE[\beta_{d-k}-\beta_{d-k-2}]+\RE[\beta_{k-1}-\beta_{k+1}]=0\,.

Plugging in
k=-1
gives

4B\RE\alpha_{0}=\RE\beta_{0}

Plugging in
k=0
gives

4B\RE\alpha_{1}+\RE\beta_{d-2}+\RE[\beta_{-1}-\beta_{1}]=0

Hence
\RE\alpha_{0},\,\RE\alpha_{1}
are uniquely determined by
\RE\beta_{0},\,\RE\beta_{1}
and
\RE\beta_{d-2}
. Now the recursion (
2.7
) uniquely determines the remaining
\RE\alpha_{k}
. We summarize in the following

Proposition 2.2
.

The real parts of the coefficients of
\beta_{\lambda}
uniquely determine the real parts of the coefficients of
\alpha_{\lambda}
. If the coefficients of
\beta_{\lambda}
are all imaginary, then also all the coefficients of
\alpha_{\lambda}
are imaginary.

2.2.
Imaginary Parts.

Adding and subtracting the two middle equations in (
2.6
) and using (
2.3
) that
\gamma_{k}=-\overline{\beta}_{d-k-1}
we get recursions

(2.8)

\begin{split}A\,\RE[\beta_{k}-\beta_{d-k-1}]+B\,\RE[\beta_{d-k-2}-\beta_{k-1}]&=0\,,\\
\IM[\alpha_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}]+2A\IM[\beta_{k}-\beta_{d-k-1}]+2B\IM[\beta_{d-k-2}-\beta_{k-1}]&=0\,.\end{split}

and combining gives

(2.9)

\mathbbm{i}\IM[\alpha_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}]+2A(\beta_{k}-\beta_{d-k-1})+2B(\beta_{d-k-2}-\beta_{k-1})=0\,.

For
k=-1
we obtain

(2.10)

\begin{split}A\,\RE\beta_{-1}+B\,\RE\beta_{d-1}&=0\,,\\
\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{0}+2A\beta_{-1}+2B\beta_{d-1}&=0\,,\end{split}

and since
\RE\beta_{-1}=0
by (
2.2
) it follows that

(2.11)

\RE\beta_{d-1}=0\,,

and the two imaginary numbers
\beta_{-1},\,\beta_{d-1}
determine the imaginary part of
\alpha_{0}
.

For
k=0
we obtain

\begin{split}A\RE\beta_{0}+B\RE\beta_{d-2}&=0\,,\\
\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{1}+2A(\beta_{0}-\beta_{d-1})+2B(\beta_{d-2}-\beta_{-1})&=0\,,\end{split}

Hence
\RE\beta_{0}
determines
\RE\beta_{d-2}
, and the imaginary number
A\beta_{0}+B\beta_{d-2}
determines
\IM\alpha_{1}
.

Next for
k=1
we get

\begin{split}A\RE[\beta_{1}-\beta_{d-2}]+B\RE[\beta_{d-3}-\beta_{0}]&=0\,,\\
\mathbbm{i}\IM[\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{0}]+2A(\beta_{1}-\beta_{d-2})+2B(\beta_{d-3}-\beta_{0})&=0\,,\end{split}

and therefore
\RE\beta_{1}
determines
\RE\beta_{d-3}
. Likewise,
\RE\beta_{2}
determines
\RE\beta_{d-4}
. Thus
\RE\beta_{k}
determines
\RE\beta_{d-k-2}
. When the degree is even
d=2N
, then the last degree of freedom is given by
\RE\beta_{N-2}
which determines
\RE\beta_{N}
.
Hence choices for
\RE\beta_{0},\RE\beta_{1},\,\ldots,\,\RE\beta_{N-2}
determine the remaining
\RE\beta_{N-1},\,\ldots,\,\RE_{d-2}
. Thus for even degree there are
(d-2)/2
choices. Likewise, when the degree is odd, there are
(d-1)/2
choices.

When
d
is odd, then for
k=(d+1)/2
we have
d-k-1=(d-3)/2
and hence

\begin{split}\RE[\alpha_{(d+1)/2}+\alpha_{(d-3)/2}]&=0\,,\\
\IM[\alpha_{(d+1)/2}-\alpha_{(d-3)/2}]&=0\,.\end{split}

Now
(d+1)/2-(d-3)/2=2
, so these two indices are two apart, but the recursion (
2.8
) gives no additional condition, as it reads

\IM[\alpha_{(d+1)/2}-\alpha_{(d-3)/2}]+2A(\beta_{(d-1)/2}-\beta_{(d-1)/2})+2B(\beta_{(d-3)/2}-\beta_{(d-3)/2})=0\,.

Therefore there is no additional constraint on the imaginary parts of the coefficients of
\beta_{\lambda}
for the case
d
odd.

If
d
is even then the indices
k
and
d-k-1
are never two apart, so the recursion (
2.8
) in conjunction with the reality condition gives no additional constraint in this case. There is a further one-dimensional reduction on the coefficients of
\beta_{\lambda}
when the degree is even due to the following

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\xi_{\lambda}
be a
K-symmetric potential with non-zero diagonal. If the degree
d
is even, then

\sum_{k=-1}^{d-1}(-1)^{k+1}\beta_{k}=0\,.

Proof.

When
d
is even, the recursion (
2.9
) gives

\begin{split}\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{0}&=-2A\,\beta_{-1}-2B\,\beta_{d-1}\\
\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{1}&=-2A(\beta_{0}-\beta_{d-1})-2B(-\beta_{-1}+\beta_{d-2})\\
\vdots&\\
\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{d-2}&=-2A(\beta_{-1}+\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}+\ldots-\beta_{d-2})-2B(-\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}-\ldots+\beta_{d-3}+\beta_{d-1})\\
\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{d-1}&=-2A(\beta_{0}-\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}-\ldots-\beta_{d-1})-2B(-\beta_{-1}+\beta_{0}-\beta_{1}+\ldots+\beta_{d-2})\,.\end{split}

The symmetry
\IM\alpha_{k}=\IM\alpha_{d-k-1}
for all
k=0,\,\ldots,\,d-1
gives the claim.
∎

In conclusion, when
d
is even, there are
d
choices, and when
d
is odd there are
d+1
real choices for
\IM\beta_{-1},\,\ldots,\,\IM\beta_{d-1}
that uniquely determine
\IM\alpha_{0},\,\ldots,\,\IM\alpha_{d-1}
. This proves

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\xi_{\lambda}
be a
K-symmetric potential of degree
d
.

(i) If
d
is even, there are
(d-2)/2
real degrees of freedom for the real parts of the coefficients of
\beta_{\lambda}
. If
d
is odd, there are
(d-1)/2
real degrees of freedom for the real parts of the coefficients of
\beta_{\lambda}
.

(ii) The imaginary parts of the coefficients of
\beta_{\lambda}
uniquely determine the imaginary parts of the coefficients of
\alpha_{\lambda}
, and there are
d+1
many real choices when
d
is odd, and
d
many when
d
is even.

Adding up parameter counts from Proposition
2.4
shows that K-symmetry halves the dimension of the parameter space. More precisely, we have proven

Theorem 2.5
.

The space of
K-symmetric potentials of degree
d
is real
(3d-2)/2
–dimensional for even
d
, and
(3d+1)/2
–dimensional for odd
d
.

Example 2.6
.

(i) For
d=1
we have
\RE\beta_{0}=0,\,\RE\alpha_{0}=0
and
\IM\alpha_{0}=-2(A\beta_{-1}+B\beta_{0})
. Hence degree one potentials all have purely imaginary entries and have the form

\xi_{\lambda}=\mathbbm{i}\begin{pmatrix}-2(A\,\beta_{-1}+B\,\beta_{0})&\beta_{-1}\lambda^{-1}+\beta_{0}\\
\beta_{0}+\beta_{-1}\lambda&2(A\,\beta_{-1}+B\,\beta_{0})\end{pmatrix}

with
\beta_{-1},\,\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
free,
\beta_{-1}>0
.

(ii) For
d=2
we have
\RE\beta_{1}=0
and
(A+B)\RE\beta_{0}=0
so either
\RE\beta_{0}=0
or
B=-A
. Further,
\RE\alpha_{0}=-\RE\alpha_{1}=\RE\beta_{0}/(4B)
and
\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{0}=-2(A\beta_{-1}+B\beta_{1})
as well as
\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{1}=-2(A(\beta_{0}-\beta_{1})+B(\beta_{0}-\beta_{-1}))
. Thus reality
\overline{\alpha_{0}}=-\alpha_{1}
holds if and only if

\beta_{-1}-\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}=0\,,

and again all entries are purely imaginary and given by

\begin{split}\alpha_{\lambda}&=-2(A\beta_{-1}+B\beta_{1})(1+\lambda)\,,\\
\beta_{\lambda}&=\beta_{-1}\lambda^{-1}+(\beta_{-1}+\beta_{1})+\beta_{1}\lambda\,.\end{split}

The general form of degree two
K-symmetric potentials is

\xi_{\lambda}=\mathbbm{i}\begin{pmatrix}-2(A\beta_{-1}+B\beta_{1})(1+\lambda)&\beta_{-1}(\lambda^{-1}+1)+\beta_{1}(1+\lambda)\\
\beta_{1}(1+\lambda)+\beta_{-1}(1+\lambda^{2})&2(A\beta_{-1}+B\beta_{1})(1+\lambda)\end{pmatrix}\,.

(iii) For
d=3
we have
\RE\beta_{2}=0
and
A\RE\beta_{0}+B\RE\beta_{1}=0
. Hence
\RE\beta_{1}=-(A/B)\RE\beta_{0}
. Further,
\RE\alpha_{0}=-\RE\alpha_{2}=(1/4B)\RE\beta_{0}
and
\RE\alpha_{1}=0
. Hence
\RE\beta_{0}\neq 0
guarantees that not all coefficients are purely imaginary. Continuing, we get
\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{0}=\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{2}=-2(A\beta_{-1}+B\beta_{2}),\,\mathbbm{i}\IM\alpha_{1}=2(A\beta_{2}+B\beta_{-1})
. Thus in the degree 3 case we see that it is possible to get K-symmetric potentials which do not have purely imaginary entries, and

\begin{split}\alpha_{0}&=1/(4B)\RE\beta_{0}-2(A\beta_{-1}+B\beta_{2})\,,\\
\alpha_{1}&=2(A\beta_{2}+B\beta_{-1})\,,\\
\alpha_{2}&=-1/(4B)\RE\beta_{0}-2(A\beta_{-1}+B\beta_{2})\,.\end{split}

Here
\beta_{-1},\beta_{2}\in\mathbbm{i}\mathbb{R}
, so also
\alpha_{1}\in\mathbbm{i}\mathbb{R}
. But
\beta_{0},\,\beta_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
in general, subject only to
A\RE\beta_{0}+B\RE\beta_{1}=0
, and thus also
\alpha_{0},\,\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
in general.

3.
Imaginary potentials

By Proposition
2.1
(i), a K-symmetric potential satisfying
\alpha_{\lambda}+\overline{\alpha_{\bar{\lambda}}}\equiv 0
has purely imaginary coefficients so that

(3.1)

\alpha_{\lambda}+\overline{\alpha_{\bar{\lambda}}}\equiv\beta_{\lambda}+\overline{\beta_{\bar{\lambda}}}\equiv\gamma_{\lambda}+\overline{\gamma_{\bar{\lambda}}}\equiv 0\,.

Hence we can write such an
imaginary potential
as

\xi_{\lambda}=\mathbbm{i}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{\lambda}&\beta_{\lambda}\\
\gamma_{\lambda}&-\alpha_{\lambda}\end{pmatrix}

where
\alpha_{\lambda},\,\beta_{\lambda},\,\gamma_{\lambda}
all have real coefficients. Now the recursion (
2.9
) reads

(3.2)

\alpha_{k+1}-\alpha_{k-1}+2A(\beta_{k}-\beta_{d-k-1})+2B(\beta_{d-k-2}-\beta_{k-1})=0\,.

For
k=-1
we obtain

(3.3)

\alpha_{0}=-2A\,\beta_{-1}-2B\,\beta_{d-1}\,.

Example 3.1
.

Since
K-symmetric potentials of degree one and two are imaginary and computed in example
2.6
, the first notable difference occurs for
d=3
:

(i) For
d=3
the recursion gives

\begin{split}\alpha_{0}&=-2A\beta_{-1}-2B\beta_{2}\\
\alpha_{1}&=-2A(\beta_{0}-\beta_{2})-2B(-\beta_{-1}+\beta_{1})\\
\alpha_{2}&=-2A\beta_{-1}-2B\beta_{2}\,.\end{split}

Hence reality
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{2}
is satisfied without a further condition.

(ii) For
d=4
the recursion gives

\begin{split}\alpha_{0}&=-2A\beta_{-1}-2B\beta_{3}\\
\alpha_{1}&=-2A(\beta_{0}-\beta_{3})-2B(-\beta_{-1}+\beta_{2})\\
\alpha_{2}&=-2A(\beta_{-1}+\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})-2B(-\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}+\beta_{3})\\
\alpha_{3}&=-2A(\beta_{0}-\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}-\beta_{3})-2B(-\beta_{-1}+\beta_{0}-\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})\end{split}

The reality conditions
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{3},\,\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
hold for all
A,\,B
if and only if

(3.4)

\beta_{-1}-\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}=0\,.

3.1.
Off-diagonal K-symmetric potentials.

By Proposition
2.1
(ii), an off-diagonal K-symmetric potential is imaginary, so of the form

(3.5)

\xi_{\lambda}=\mathbbm{i}\,\begin{pmatrix}0&\beta_{\lambda}\\
\gamma_{\lambda}&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

where
\beta_{\lambda},\,\gamma_{\lambda}
have only real coefficients. Now the recursion (
2.9
) reads

(3.6)

A(\beta_{k}-\beta_{d-k-1})+B(\beta_{d-k-2}-\beta_{k-1})=0

for
k=-1,\,\ldots,\,d+1
. Amongst the
d+1
free paramters for off-diagonal, and hence imaginary
\beta_{\lambda}
, this recursion reduces the freedom by a further
d/2
when
d
is even, and
(d-1)/2
for
d
odd. The first equations for
k=-1,\,\ldots,\,2
read

\begin{split}A\beta_{-1}+B\beta_{d-1}&=0\\
A(\beta_{0}-\beta_{d-1})+B(\beta_{d-2}-\beta_{-1})&=0\\
A(\beta_{1}-\beta_{d-2})+B(\beta_{d-3}-\beta_{0})&=0\\
A(\beta_{2}-\beta_{d-3})+B(\beta_{d-4}-\beta_{1})&=0\end{split}

Example 3.2
.

We compute some low degree examples:

1. For
d=1
we get
\beta_{0}=-\beta_{-1}A/B
, so degree one off-diagonal
K-symmetric potentials look like

(3.7)

\xi_{\lambda}=\mathbbm{i}\beta_{-1}\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda^{-1}-A/B\\
-A/B+\lambda&0\end{pmatrix}

which drops rank at
\lambda=A/B,\,B/A
.

2. For
d=2
we get
A\beta_{-1}+B\beta_{1}=0
and
A(\beta_{0}-\beta_{1})+B(\beta_{0}-\beta_{-1})=0
, so degree two potentials are of the form

(3.8)

\begin{split}\xi_{\lambda}&=\mathbbm{i}\beta_{-1}\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda^{-1}+(1-A/B)-A/B\lambda\\
-A/B+(1-A/B)\lambda+\lambda^{2}&0\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\mathbbm{i}\beta_{-1}(\lambda+1)\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda^{-1}-A/B\\
\ -A/B+\lambda&0\end{pmatrix}\end{split}

Again
\det\xi_{\lambda}
has simple roots at
\lambda=A/B,\,B/A
. In addition
\xi_{\lambda}\equiv 0
at
\lambda=-1
.

3. For
d=3
we can write
\beta_{1},\,\beta_{2}
in terms of
\beta_{-1},\,\beta_{0}
, in fact

\beta_{1}=(1-\tfrac{A^{2}}{B^{2}})\beta_{-1}-\tfrac{A}{B}\beta_{0}\,\,,\qquad\beta_{2}=-\tfrac{A}{B}\beta_{-1}\,.

Inserting these expressions into
\beta_{\lambda},\,\gamma_{\lambda}
, we observe that they have a common factor of degree 2, more specifically

\beta_{\lambda}/(\lambda^{-1}-A/B)=\gamma_{\lambda}/(\lambda-A/B)=\beta_{-1}\left(\lambda^{2}+\tfrac{A}{B}\lambda+1\right)+\lambda\beta_{0}\,.

Hence degree 3 off-diagonal K-symmetric potentials are all of the form

(3.9)

\xi_{\lambda}=\mathbbm{i}(\beta_{-1}\left(\lambda^{2}+\tfrac{A}{B}\lambda+1\right)+\lambda\beta_{0})\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda^{-1}-A/B\\
\ -A/B+\lambda&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

This is the general behaviour of off-diagonal finite gap K-symmetric potentials, and the content of the next theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\xi_{\lambda}
be an off-diagonal
K-symmetric potential of degree
d
. Then there exists a unique real polynomial
\lambda\mapsto p(\lambda)
of degree
d-1
such that

\xi_{\lambda}=\mathbbm{i}\,p(\lambda)\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda^{-1}-A/B\\
\ -A/B+\lambda&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

Proof.

Write
\xi_{\lambda}
as in (
3.5
) with entries
\beta_{\lambda},\,\gamma_{\lambda}
with
\deg\gamma_{\lambda}=d
. Long division shows that the unique polynomial
\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}p_{k}\lambda^{k}
of degree
d-1
with coefficients recursivley defined by

p_{0}=\beta_{-1}\,,\qquad p_{k}=\beta_{k-1}+\frac{A}{B}\,p_{k-1}\quad\mathrm{for}\quad k=1,\,\ldots,\,d-1

divides both
\beta_{\lambda},\,\gamma_{\lambda}
with quotients
\lambda^{-1}-A/B
respectively
\lambda-A/B
. Since all coefficients
p_{k}
are real, this concludes the proof.
∎

The spectral curve
\Sigma
is the 2-point compactification of

\Sigma^{*}=\left\{(\nu,\,\lambda)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\mid\nu^{2}=-\det\xi_{\lambda}\right\}\,.

When
\xi=p\,\tilde{\xi}
for a polynomial
p
, then after renormalization
\xi
and
\tilde{\xi}
give the same spectral curve, since the roots of
p^{2}
all have even order. Any spectral curve comes from an isospectral family of potentials that all share the same determinant. In each isospectral family there are off-diagonal potentials, and by Proposition
2.1
(ii) they are automatically imaginary.

Corollary 3.4
.

All spectral curves of off-diagonal
K
-symmetric potentials have genus one. Higher spectral genus examples must come from potentials with non-zero diagonal.

4.
Spectral Theory of K-matrices

We compute the values of
\lambda
for which a K-matrix fails to be invertible, and determine the corresponding kernels. We show that the eigenvectors of
K
are
\lambda
-independent, and we compute the residues at the simple poles of
K^{-1}
.

4.1.
Kernels.

Computing

-\lambda^{2}\det K(\lambda)=\lambda^{4}+16AB\lambda^{3}-2\lambda^{2}\left(8A^{2}+8B^{2}+1\right)+16AB\lambda+1

and
\lambda\mapsto\det K(\lambda)
has four simple roots

(4.1)

\begin{split}\varrho&=\frac{2A-\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}}{2B+\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad r=\frac{2A+\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}}{2B+\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}}\,,\\
\varrho^{-1}&=\frac{2A+\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}}{2B-\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad r^{-1}=\frac{2A-\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}}{2B-\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}}\,.\end{split}

The null-set of
\det K
, that is

(4.2)

\mathcal{N}=\{\varrho,\,r,\,\varrho^{-1},\,r^{-1}\}\,,

is invariant under inversion as well as under the map
(A,\,B)\mapsto(-A,\,-B)
.
The matrices
K(\lambda),\,\lambda\in\mathcal{N}
drop rank, and kernels compute to

(4.3)

\begin{split}&\ker K(\varrho)=\ker K(r)=\ker\begin{pmatrix}1&2B+\sqrt{1+4B^{2}}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\\
&\ker K(\varrho^{-1})=\ker K(r^{-1})=\ker\begin{pmatrix}1&2B-\sqrt{1+4B^{2}}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\,.\end{split}

Note that

\ker K(\varrho)\perp\ker K(\varrho^{-1})

and that both kernels are independent of the value of
A
in the
K
-matrix.

4.2.
Spectrum.

K
has eigenvalues
\mu_{\pm}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{tr}K\pm\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}^{2}K-4\det K})
and compute to

(4.4)

\begin{split}\mu_{-}(\lambda)&=4A-2B\left(\lambda+\lambda^{-1}\right)-\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}\left(\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\right)\\
\mu_{+}(\lambda)&=4A-2B\left(\lambda+\lambda^{-1}\right)+\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}\left(\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\right)\end{split}

Note that for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
we have

(4.5)

\mu_{-}(\lambda^{-1})=\mu_{+}(\lambda)\,.

Now
\mu_{-}
has simple roots at
\varrho,\,r
, while
\mu_{+}
has simple roots at
\varrho^{-1},\,r^{-1}
, with
\varrho,\,r,\,\varrho^{-1},\,r^{-1}
as in (
4.1
). While the eigenvalues of a K-matrix depend on
\lambda
as well as both constants
A
and
B
, the eigenvectors do not depend on
\lambda
, nor the constant
A
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The eigenvectors of
K
are given by

(4.6)

\mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B\\
1\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathbf{v}^{\perp}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B\\
1\end{pmatrix}\,.

so for all
\lambda\in\mathbf{C}^{\ast}
we have

K(\lambda)\,\mathbf{v}=\mu_{-}(\lambda)\,\mathbf{v}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad K(\lambda)\,\mathbf{v}^{\perp}=\mu_{+}(\lambda)\,\mathbf{v}^{\perp}\,.

For the orthogonal matrix
V
whose columns are
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}^{\perp}
, that is

V=\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B&\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}

we have the diagonalization of
K
by

(4.7)

K=V\,\mathrm{diag}[\,\mu_{-},\,\mu_{+}\,]\,V^{-1}\,.

The span
\langle\,\,\rangle
of the eigenvectors
\mathbf{v},\,\mathbf{v}^{\perp}
of
K
coincide with the kernels in (
4.3
), and we have

\begin{split}\langle\mathbf{v}\rangle&=\ker K(\varrho)=\ker K(r)\\
\langle\mathbf{v}^{\perp}\rangle&=\ker K(\varrho^{-1})=\ker K(r^{-1})\,.\end{split}

Suppose we have a K-symmetric potential
\xi_{\lambda}
. Evaluating the symmetry (
1.9
) at
\lambda=\varrho
and applying to the vector
\mathbf{v}\in\ker K(\varrho)
gives

K(\varrho)\,\xi_{\varrho}\,\mathbf{v}+\overline{\xi_{\varrho}}^{t}K(\varrho)\,\mathbf{v}=K(\varrho)\,\xi_{\varrho}\,\mathbf{v}=0\,,

and hence
\xi_{\varrho}\,\mathbf{v}\in\ker K(\varrho)=\langle\mathbf{v}\rangle
. Similarly,
\xi_{r}\,\mathbf{v}\in\ker K(r)=\langle\mathbf{v}\rangle
,
\xi_{\varrho^{-1}}\,\mathbf{v}^{\perp}\in\ker K(\varrho^{-1})=\langle\mathbf{v}^{\perp}\rangle
and
\xi_{r^{-1}}\,\mathbf{v}^{\perp}\in\ker K(r^{-1})=\langle\mathbf{v}^{\perp}\rangle
. We summarize this in the following

Proposition 4.2
.

At each of the four points
\lambda\in\{\varrho,\,r,\,\varrho^{-1},\,r^{-1}\}
where
K(\lambda)
drops rank, the kernel of
K(\lambda)
is an eigenspace of
\xi_{\lambda}
. We may assume without loss of generality that

\xi_{\varrho}\mathbf{v}=\xi_{r}\mathbf{v}=-\nu\,\mathbf{v}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\xi_{\varrho^{-1}}\mathbf{v}^{\perp}=\xi_{r^{-1}}\mathbf{v}^{\perp}=\nu\,\mathbf{v}\,.

4.3.
The Inverse and Residues.

Now
K^{-1}=V\,\mathrm{diag}[\,\mu_{-}^{-1},\,\mu_{+}^{-1}\,]\,V^{-1}
has simple poles at the four points
\lambda\in\{\varrho,\,r,\,\varrho^{-1},\,r^{-1}\}
since
\mu_{\pm}
have simple roots there. Since the matrix
V
does not depend on
\lambda
, the residues of
K^{-1}
at the four points
\lambda\in\mathcal{N}
as in (
4.2
) are given by

\mathrm{res}[K^{-1},\lambda]=V\,\mathrm{diag}\bigl[\,\mathrm{res}[\mu_{-}^{-1},\,\lambda],\,\mathrm{res}[\mu_{+}^{-1},\,\lambda\,\bigr]\,V^{-1}\,,\qquad\lambda\in\mathcal{N}.

Lemma 4.3
.

The kernels of the residues of
K^{-1}
coincide with the kernels of
K
as follows

\begin{split}\ker\mathrm{res}[K^{-1},\varrho]&=\ker K(\varrho^{-1})=\ker\mathrm{res}[K^{-1},r]=\ker K(r^{-1})\,,\\
\ker\mathrm{res}[K^{-1},\varrho^{-1}]&=\ker K(\varrho)=\ker\mathrm{res}[K^{-1},r^{-1}]=\ker K(r)\,.\end{split}

Proof.

To compute the residues, first multiply away the poles

\begin{split}(\lambda-\varrho)\,\mu_{-}^{-1}&=((\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}+2A)\lambda^{-1}-\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B)^{-1}\\
(\lambda-r)\,\mu_{-}^{-1}&=-((\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}-2A)\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}+2B)^{-1}\\
(\lambda-\varrho^{-1})\,\mu_{+}^{-1}&=((-\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}+2A)\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B)^{-1}\\
(\lambda-r^{-1})\,\mu_{+}^{-1}&=((\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}+2A)\lambda^{-1}+\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B)^{-1}\end{split}

then evaluate to obtain the four residues

\begin{split}\mathrm{res}[\mu_{-}^{-1},\,\varrho]=\lim_{\lambda\to\varrho}(\lambda-\varrho)\,\mu_{-}^{-1}&=(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{A}{\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}})(2B-\sqrt{4B^{2}+1})\\
\mathrm{res}[\mu_{-}^{-1},\,r]=\lim_{\lambda\to r}(\lambda-r)\,\mu_{-}^{-1}&=(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{A}{\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}})(2B-\sqrt{4B^{2}+1})\\
\mathrm{res}[\mu_{+}^{-1},\,\varrho^{-1}]=\lim_{\lambda\to\varrho^{-1}}(\lambda-\varrho^{-1})\,\mu_{+}^{-1}&=(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{A}{\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}})(2B+\sqrt{4B^{2}+1})\\
\mathrm{res}[\mu_{+}^{-1},\,r^{-1}]=\lim_{\lambda\to r^{-1}}(\lambda-r^{-1})\,\mu_{+}^{-1}&=(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{A}{\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}})(2B+\sqrt{4B^{2}+1})\end{split}

The residues of
K^{-1}
at the four simple poles compute to

\begin{split}\mathrm{res}[K^{-1},\varrho]&=V\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathrm{res}[\mu_{-}^{-1},\varrho]&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)V^{-1}\\
&=\tfrac{\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}-2A}{4\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-1&\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B\\
\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B&-(\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B)^{2}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\sim\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2B-\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\\
\mathrm{res}[K^{-1},r]&=V\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathrm{res}[\mu_{-}^{-1},r]&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)V^{-1}\\
&=\tfrac{\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}+2A}{4\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-1&\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B\\
\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B&-(\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}-2B)^{2}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\sim\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2B-\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\\
\mathrm{res}[K^{-1},\varrho^{-1}]&=V\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&\mathrm{res}[\mu_{+}^{-1},\varrho^{-1}]\end{smallmatrix}\right)V^{-1}\\
&=\tfrac{\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}+2A}{4\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-1&\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}+2B\\
\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}+2B&-(\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}+2B)^{2}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\sim\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2B+\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\\
\mathrm{res}[K^{-1},r^{-1}]&=V\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&\mathrm{res}[\mu_{+}^{-1},r^{-1}]\end{smallmatrix}\right)V^{-1}\\
&=\tfrac{\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}-2A}{4\sqrt{4A^{2}+1}\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-1&\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}+2B\\
\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}+2B&-(\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}+2B)^{2}\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)\sim\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2B+\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\end{split}

Comparing row-reduced matrices in (
4.3
) and the above row-reduced residues proves the claim.
∎

5.
Products of K-matrices

We next look at products and ratios of K-matrices. Suppose we have two K-matrices

(5.1)

K_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}4A_{0}-4B_{0}\lambda&\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\\
\lambda-\lambda^{-1}&4A_{0}-4B_{0}\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\,K_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}4A_{1}-4B_{1}\lambda&\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\\
\lambda-\lambda^{-1}&4A_{1}-4B_{1}\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

[K_{0},\,K_{1}]=4\left(\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\right)^{2}(B_{0}-B_{1})\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

and thus

(5.2)

[K_{0},\,K_{1}]=0\quad\mbox{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\Longleftrightarrow B_{0}=B_{1}\,.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose
K_{0},\,K_{1}
are two K-matrices with

(5.3)

A_{0}B_{1}=-A_{1}B_{0}\,.

Then there exist unique rational functions
p,\,q
, and a degree one potential
\eta_{\lambda}
with

(5.4)

K_{1}^{-1}K_{0}=p\,\mathbbm{1}+q\,\eta_{\lambda}

Proof.

We compute

\begin{split}\mathrm{adj}K_{1}K_{0}&=\begin{pmatrix}4A_{1}-4B_{1}\lambda^{-1}&-(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})\\
-(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})&4A_{1}-4B_{1}\lambda\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}4A_{0}-4B_{0}\lambda&\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\\
\lambda-\lambda^{-1}&4A_{0}-4B_{0}\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\\
=&\bigl(16(A_{0}A_{1}+B_{0}B_{1})-(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})^{2}\bigr)\,\mathbbm{1}\,\,+\\
\lambda^{-1}&\begin{pmatrix}-16(A_{0}B_{1}+A_{1}B_{0}\lambda^{2})&4(A_{1}-A_{0}-(B_{1}-B_{0})\lambda^{-1})(\lambda^{2}-1)\\
4(A_{1}-A_{0}-(B_{1}-B_{0})\lambda)(\lambda^{2}-1)&-16(A_{1}B_{0}+A_{0}B_{1}\lambda^{2})\end{pmatrix}\end{split}

The last matrix is trace-free if and only if (
5.3
) holds, and then computes to

\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}-16(A_{0}B_{1}+A_{1}B_{0}\lambda^{2})&4(A_{1}-A_{0}-(B_{1}-B_{0})\lambda^{-1})(\lambda^{2}-1)\\
4(A_{1}-A_{0}-(B_{1}-B_{0})\lambda)(\lambda^{2}-1)&-16(A_{1}B_{0}+A_{0}B_{1}\lambda^{2})\end{pmatrix}&\\
=4(\lambda^{2}-1)\begin{pmatrix}4A_{0}B_{1}&A_{1}-A_{0}-(B_{1}-B_{0})\lambda^{-1}\\
A_{1}-A_{0}-(B_{1}-B_{0})\lambda&-4A_{0}B_{1}\end{pmatrix}&\,.\end{split}

Replacing
\mathrm{adj}K_{1}=\det K_{1}K_{1}^{-1}
and putting the above computation together we set

(5.5)

\begin{split}p(\lambda)&=\bigl(16(A_{0}A_{1}+B_{0}B_{1})-(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})^{2}\bigr)/\det K_{1}(\lambda)\,,\\
q(\lambda)&=-4\mathbbm{i}(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})/\det K_{1}(\lambda)\,,\\
\eta_{\lambda}&=\mathbbm{i}\begin{pmatrix}4A_{0}B_{1}&A_{1}-A_{0}-(B_{1}-B_{0})\lambda^{-1}\\
A_{1}-A_{0}-(B_{1}-B_{0})\lambda&-4A_{0}B_{1}\end{pmatrix}\,.\end{split}

∎

We next show that potentials that are K-symmetric with respect to two distinct K-matrices only give rise to Delaunay surfaces.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
K_{0},\,K_{1}
be two K-matrices satisfying (
5.3
), and
\xi_{\lambda}
a potential that is K-symmetric with respect to both
K_{0}
and
K_{1}
. Then
\xi_{\lambda}
is a polynomial multiple of a degree 1 potential.

Proof.

Suppose we have two different K-matrices
K_{0}
and
K_{1}
that satisfy the condition (
5.3
). Suppose we have a potential
\xi_{\lambda}
that is K-symmetric with respect to both
K_{0}
and
K_{1}

Lemma 6.1
.

\mathrm{(1)}

Proposition 7.1
.

The map

Proof.

The four simple poles are the four simple roots of
\det K_{1}
. The last assertion follows from
\det C_{\lambda}=1
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
.
∎

Since
[M_{\lambda},\,\xi_{\lambda}]=0
, there exist
\lambda
-dependent functions
f,\,g
such that

(7.1)

M_{\lambda}=f\,\mathbbm{1}+g\,\xi_{\lambda}\,.

Taking determinants gives

\frac{\det K_{0}}{\det K_{1}}=f^{2}+g^{2}\det\xi_{\lambda}\,.

In terms of the eigenvalues,
\det K_{0}=\mu^{0}_{-}\mu^{0}_{+},\,\det K_{1}=\mu^{1}_{-}\mu^{1}_{+}
, where
\mu_{\pm}^{0},\,\mu_{\pm}^{1}
are as in (
4.4
) with

replaced by
A_{0},\,B_{0}
respectively

, and writing

we obtain

f^{2}-g^{2}\nu^{2}=(f-g\,\nu)(f+g\,\nu)=\mu^{0}_{-}\mu^{0}_{+}/(\mu^{1}_{-}\mu^{1}_{+})

Setting
f-g\,\nu=\mu^{0}_{-}/\mu^{1}_{-}
and
f+g\,\nu=\mu^{0}_{+}/\mu^{1}_{+}
gives

f=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mu^{0}_{-}}{\mu^{1}_{-}}+\frac{\mu^{0}_{+}}{\mu^{1}_{+}}\right)\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad g=\frac{1}{2\,\nu}\left(\frac{\mu^{0}_{+}}{\mu^{1}_{+}}-\frac{\mu^{0}_{-}}{\mu^{1}_{-}}\right)\,.

By (
4.5
), and since
\nu(\lambda^{-1})=\lambda^{1-d}\nu(\lambda)
we have

f(\lambda^{-1})=f(\lambda)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad g(\lambda^{-1})=-\lambda^{d-1}g(\lambda)\,.

7.1.
Kernels.

We briefly verify that
f\mathbbm{1}+g\,\xi_{\lambda}
has the given kernels at the four roots of
\det K_{0}
. Suppose the four roots of
\det K_{0}
are
\{\varrho_{0},\,r_{0},\,\varrho_{0}^{-1},\,r_{0}^{-1}\}
as in (
4.3
) with
A,\,B
replaced by
A_{0},\,B_{0}
. Suppose further that
\mu^{0}_{-}(\varrho_{0})=\mu^{0}_{-}(r_{0})=0
, and that
\langle\mathbf{v}_{0}\rangle=\ker K_{0}(\varrho_{0})=\ker K_{0}(r_{0})
. Then
M_{\varrho_{0}}\mathbf{v}_{0}=M_{r_{0}}\mathbf{v}_{0}=0
and as we may assume that
\xi_{\varrho_{0}}\mathbf{v}_{0}=\xi_{r_{0}}\mathbf{v}_{0}=-\nu\,\mathbf{v}_{0}
we obtain

\begin{split}\left(f(\varrho_{0})\,\mathbbm{1}+g(\varrho_{0})\,\xi_{\varrho_{0}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{0}&=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu^{0}_{+}(\varrho_{0})}{\mu^{1}_{+}(\varrho_{0})}\left(\mathbbm{1}+\frac{1}{\nu}\xi_{\varrho_{0}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{0}=0\,,\\
\left(f(r_{0})\,\mathbbm{1}+g(r_{0})\,\xi_{r_{0}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{0}&=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu^{0}_{+}(r_{0})}{\mu^{1}_{+}(r_{0})}\left(\mathbbm{1}+\frac{1}{\nu}\xi_{r_{0}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{0}=0\,.\end{split}

Similarly, if
\mu^{0}_{+}(\varrho_{0}^{-1})=\mu^{0}_{+}(r_{0}^{-1})=0
, and that
\langle\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\perp}\rangle=\ker K_{0}(\varrho_{0}^{-1})=\ker K_{0}(r_{0}^{-1})

and as we may assume that
\xi_{\varrho_{0}^{-1}}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\perp}=\xi_{r_{0}^{-1}}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\perp}=\nu\,\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\perp}
, we obtain

\begin{split}\left(f(\varrho_{0}^{-1})\,\mathbbm{1}+g(\varrho_{0}^{-1})\,\xi_{\varrho_{0}^{-1}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\perp}&=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu^{0}_{-}(\varrho_{0}^{-1})}{\mu^{1}_{-}(\varrho_{0}^{-1})}\left(\mathbbm{1}-\frac{1}{\nu}\xi_{\varrho_{0}^{-1}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\perp}=0\,,\\
\left(f(r_{0}^{-1})\,\mathbbm{1}+g(r_{0}^{-1})\,\xi_{r_{0}^{-1}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\perp}&=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu^{0}_{-}(r_{0}^{-1})}{\mu^{1}_{-}(r_{0}^{-1})}\left(\mathbbm{1}-\frac{1}{\nu}\xi_{r_{0}^{-1}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\perp}=0\,.\end{split}

7.2.
Spectrum.

The eigenvalues of
f\mathbbm{1}+g\,\xi_{\lambda}
are
f\pm g\nu
. From above we have
f-g\,\nu=\mu^{0}_{-}/\mu^{1}_{-}
and
f+g\,\nu=\mu^{0}_{+}/\mu^{1}_{+}
so both eigenvalues of
M_{\lambda}
are rational with two simple roots, and two simple poles, and principal divisors

(f-g\,\nu)=\varrho_{0}+r_{0}-\varrho_{1}-r_{1}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad(f+g\,\nu)=\varrho_{0}^{-1}+r_{0}^{-1}-\varrho_{1}^{-1}-r_{1}^{-1}\,.

Since
[M_{\lambda},\,\xi_{\lambda}]=0
, these two matrices have the same eigenvectors, and thus

\xi_{\lambda}\mathbf{v}=\pm\nu\,\mathbf{v}\Longleftrightarrow M_{\lambda}\mathbf{v}=(f\pm g\,\nu)\,\mathbf{v}\,.

7.3.
Complementary boundary conditions

Of particular interest is the case when the two integrable boundary conditions are
complimentary
, that is when the constants in the two K-matrices
K_{0},\,K_{1}
have opposite signs so that

A_{1}=-A_{0}\quad\mbox{ and }B_{1}=-B_{0}.

Omitting subscripts, and reverting back to constants
A,\,B
we thus consider

K_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}4A-4B\lambda&\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\\
\lambda-\lambda^{-1}&4A-4B\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\,\mbox{ and }\,K_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}-4A+4B\lambda&\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\\
\lambda-\lambda^{-1}&-4A+4B\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

From (
4.4
) we know that
K_{0}
has eigenvalues

\begin{split}\mu_{-}^{0}(\lambda)&=4A-2B\left(\lambda+\lambda^{-1}\right)-\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}\left(\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\right)\\
\mu_{+}^{0}(\lambda)&=4A-2B\left(\lambda+\lambda^{-1}\right)+\sqrt{4B^{2}+1}\left(\lambda-\lambda^{-1}\right)\end{split}

so under
(A,\,B)\mapsto(-A,\,-B)
we see that
K_{1}
has eigenvalues

(7.2)

\begin{split}\mu_{-}^{1}&=-\mu_{+}^{0}\\
\mu_{+}^{1}&=-\mu_{-}^{0}\end{split}

Thus
\det K_{0}=\det K_{1}
, and hence
\det M_{\lambda}=f^{2}-g^{2}\nu^{2}=(f-g\,\nu)(f+g\,\nu)=1
. As in the previous section, and using (
7.2
) we have

f-g\,\nu=\frac{\mu^{0}_{-}}{\mu^{1}_{-}}=-\frac{\mu^{0}_{-}}{\mu^{0}_{+}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad f+g\,\nu=\frac{\mu^{0}_{+}}{\mu^{1}_{+}}=-\frac{\mu^{0}_{+}}{\mu^{0}_{-}}

and so indeed
(f-g\,\nu)(f+g\,\nu)=1
. Further, solving for
f,\,g
gives

f=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mu^{0}_{-}}{\mu^{0}_{+}}+\frac{\mu^{0}_{+}}{\mu^{0}_{-}}\right)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad g=\frac{1}{2\,\nu}\left(\frac{\mu^{0}_{-}}{\mu^{0}_{+}}-\frac{\mu^{0}_{+}}{\mu^{0}_{-}}\right)\,.
