Covariant decomposable maps on C*-algebras and quantum dynamics
Krzysztof Szczygielski

Abstract
. We characterize covariant positive decomposable maps between unital C*-algebras in terms of a dilation theorem, which generalizes a seminal result by H. Scutaru from Rep. Math. Phys. 16 (1):79–87, 1979. As a case study, we provide a certain characterization of the operator sum representation of maps on 𝕄 n ​ ( ℂ ) \mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}) , covariant with respect to the maximal commutative subgroup of U ⁡ ( n ) \mathrm{U}(n) . A connection to quantum dynamics is established by specifying sufficient and necessary conditions for covariance of D-divisible (decomposably divisible) quantum evolution families, recently introduced in J. Phys. A: Math. Theor. 56 (2023) 485202.



1.
Introduction

Covariant positive linear maps gained a lot of attention due to their significance in quantum physics, where they serve as a theoretical framework for modeling quantum channels, or quantum evolutions, of systems with high level of internal symmetry. For instance, covariance appears in quite a natural manner in the widely used Davies approach to Markovian semigroups
[
1
,
2
]
and to their generalizations in the weak coupling limit regime (see e.g.
[
3
,
4
]
). Covariant quantum channels found numerous applications in open quantum systems theory, quantum metrology, quantum optics and notably in quantum information. For example, they were employed for characterization of entanglement in spin systems, description of spin chains, modeling the evolution in presence of processes of pure dephasing, absorption and emission, description of spin-boson model and many others; see e.g.
[
5
,
6
]
and references therein for a more comprehensive summary. The algebraic framework of covariant maps was established by H. Scutaru
[
7
]
who characterized completely positive covariant maps from a C*-algebra into
B(H)
,
H
being a Hilbert space, by providing an explicit construction in the spirit of Stinespring
[
8
]
. An important result by A. Holevo related covariance of completely positive unital semigroups to properties of their generators
[
9
,
10
]
.

The article is structured as follows. In Section
2
we provide some necessary mathematical background, which includes Frobenius Hermitian basis in
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, transposition map on general
B(H)
and elementary notions of various classes of positive maps on algebras. This is then followed by the core part of the article, namely Sections
3
and
4
, which contain all main results. In particular, in Section
3
we extend results of Scutaru
[
7
]
onto the more general class of
decomposable
positive linear maps, in the spirit of Stinespring and Størmer; this is formulated in a form of Theorems
3
and
5
. As a kind of a case study, in Section
3.3
we focus on a particular case of maps covariant with respect to the so-called maximal commutative subgroup of
\mathrm{U}(n)
and provide characterization for their operator sum representation (Proposition
4
, Theorems
7
and
8
). The second core part, Section
4
, is then devoted to quantum dynamics and it mainly focuses on recently introduced
D-divisible
(decomposably divisible) quantum evolution families
[
11
,
12
]
. In particular, a characterization of time-local regular generators of covariant, D-divisible evolution families is provided (Proposition
10
, Theorems
9
and
10
) which can be seen as a generalization of result by Holevo
[
9
]
applied for C*-algebras of square matrices.

2.
Preliminaries

Here, we briefly outline most basic notions necessary for formulating further results, including elementary facts and constructs of a theory of positive maps on algebras.

2.1.
Notation

Throughout the article we will use a common notation.
B(X,Y)
(resp.
B(X)
) will be the Banach space of bounded linear maps between normed spaces
X
and
Y
(resp. on
X
).
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
will be the algebra of complex square matrices of size
n
. For
A\in B(H)
,
H
a Hilbert space, we denote with
A^{*}
the Hermitian conjugate of
A
and with
\left\|A\right\|_{1}=\operatorname{tr}{\sqrt{A^{*}A}}
and
\left\|A\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}{A^{*}A}}
the
trace norm
and
Frobenius (Hilbert-Schmidt) norm
, respectively, with the latter induced by Frobenius (Hilbert-Schmidt) inner product
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle_{2}=\operatorname{tr}{A^{*}B}
. Space
B(H)
with its standard operator (spectral) norm is a C*-algebra (and so is
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
), and becomes a Banach *-algebra when either
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1}
or
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}
is taken instead. For any linear operator
A
, symbol
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)}
will denote the spectrum of
A
. For a set
S
in an algebra
\mathscr{A}
we denote with
S^{\prime}
the commutant of
S
.

2.2.
Algebra
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and Frobenius basis

In Sections
3.3
and
4.2
, devoted to finite-dimensional C*-algebras, we will make an extensive use of Frobenius-orthonormal bases spanning
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. In particular, two of them will be of importance: the
canonical
one,
\{E_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
for
E_{ij}
containing
1
at place
(i,j)
and zeros elsewhere, and
Hermitian
one,
\{F_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n^{2}}
, consisting of matrices
F_{i}
subject to

F_{i}=F_{i}^{*},\quad\operatorname{tr}{F_{i}F_{j}}=\delta_{ij},\quad F_{n^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}I,\quad\operatorname{tr}{F_{i}}=\delta_{in^{2}}.

(2.1)

We choose such basis in a following, common way: matrices
F_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant\binom{n}{2}
are chosen
symmetric
, for
1+\binom{n}{2}\leqslant i\leqslant n(n-1)

antisymmetric
, and for
1+n(n-1)\leqslant i\leqslant n^{2}

diagonal
; we will sometimes denote them with
F_{i}^{\mathrm{s}}
,
F_{i}^{\mathrm{a}}
and
F_{i}^{\mathrm{d}}
, respectively. They span mutually orthogonal subspaces in
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
of all symmetric, antisymmetric and diagonal matrices which we will respectively denote
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
\mathrm{Asym}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
\mathrm{Diag}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. General structure of
F_{i}
is the following
[
13
,
14
]
:

•

when
F_{i}
is either
symmetric
or
antisymmetric
:

F_{i}^{\mathrm{s}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(E_{\mu\nu}+E_{\nu\mu})\quad\text{or}\quad F_{i}^{\mathrm{a}}=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(E_{\mu\nu}-E_{\nu\mu})

(2.2)

for some pair
(\mu,\nu)\in\{1,...,n\}^{2}
uniquely determined by
i
, in symmetric or antisymmetric case, respectively;

•

when
F_{i}
is chosen
diagonal
: first, define matrix
K_{k}
as

K_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(k+1)}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}E_{jj}-kE_{k+1,k+1}\right)

(2.3)

for
k\in\{1,...,n-1\}
and set
F_{i}^{\mathrm{d}}=K_{i-n(n-1)}
,
F_{n^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}I
.

Such constructed basis naturally satisfies properties (
2.1
) and will be referred to simply as the
Frobenius basis
. All
F_{i}^{\mathrm{s}}
,
F_{i}^{\mathrm{a}}
are off-diagonal and have only two non-zero matrix elements, while
F_{i}^{\mathrm{d}}
are of zero trace, except for
F_{n^{2}}
. Frobenius bases so constructed may be then regarded as natural generalizations of (normalized) Pauli and Gell-Mann matrices, which respectively span
\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and
\mathbb{M}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
.

2.3.
Transposition on
B(H)

Let a complex Hilbert space
H
be spanned by basis
{\{e_{i}:i\in\mathcal{I}\}}
for some set
\mathcal{I}
of indices (possibly uncountable). Let
J_{H}:H\to H
be an antilinear isometric involutive isomorphism given via

J_{H}x=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\overline{\left\langle e_{i},x\right\rangle}e_{i},

(2.4)

i.e. a
complex conjugation
of
x
in basis
\{e_{i}\}
; naturally, one has
J_{H}^{2}x=x
. For any
A\in B(H)
, the linear operator

\tau(A)=A^{\tau}:H\to H,\quad\tau(A)=J_{H}A^{*}J_{H}

(2.5)

will be called the
transpose of
A

[
15
]
. Every Hilbert space
H
is isometrically anti-isomorphic to its dual
H^{*}
, i.e. there exist antilinear isometry
\eta:H\to H^{*}
given by
\eta(y)=\left\langle y,\cdot\right\rangle
. Then,
\eta
gives rise to the
Hermitian adjoint

A^{*}\in B(H)
defined by
\eta(y)\circ A=\eta(A^{*}y)
, or simply
\left\langle x,Ay\right\rangle=\left\langle A^{*}x,y\right\rangle
, for all
x,y\in H
. Naturally, map
\eta(y)\mapsto\eta(A^{*}y)
is a transformation of a functional
\eta(y)
in
H^{*}
. Then, a mapping
\zeta=\eta\circ J_{H}
,
\zeta(y)=\left\langle J_{H}y,\cdot\right\rangle
can be checked to be a
linear
isometry between
H
and
H^{*}
, which in turn gives rise to the
transpose
of
A
: indeed, taking
A:H\to H
, any
x,y\in H
and utilizing involutiveness of
J_{H}
,

\displaystyle\zeta(y)(Ax)

\displaystyle=\left\langle J_{H}y,Ax\right\rangle=\left\langle J_{H}J_{H}A^{*}J_{H}y,x\right\rangle

(2.6)

\displaystyle=\zeta(J_{H}A^{*}J_{H}y)(x)=\zeta(A^{\tau}y)(x).

More generally, let
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
be Hilbert spaces endowed respectively with complex conjugations
J_{1}
,
J_{2}
(in some chosen bases), antilinear isometries
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
, and linear ones
\zeta_{1}
,
\zeta_{2}
, all defined as above. Let
A\in B(H_{1},H_{2})
. Then,
A^{*}:H_{2}\to H_{1}
satisfies
\eta_{2}(y)\circ A=\eta_{1}(A^{*}y)
,
y\in H_{2}
, while the
transpose
must obey
\zeta_{2}(y)\circ A=\zeta_{1}(A^{\tau}y)
. Then, a simple computation shows that

\zeta_{2}(y)(Ax)=\zeta_{1}(J_{1}A^{*}J_{2}y)(x),

(2.7)

and so the transpose of
A:H_{1}\to H_{2}
is

A^{\tau}:H_{2}\to H_{1},\quad A^{\tau}=J_{1}A^{*}J_{2}.

(2.8)

Both
A^{*}
and
A^{\tau}
can be then interpreted as (representations of) liftings of operator
A
to maps between dual spaces. For finite-dimensional spaces,
\tau
will simply mean the matrix transposition.

By analogy to matrix algebras, let us define the
conjugate

\overline{A}
of operator
A:H_{1}\to H_{2}
by

\overline{A}:H_{1}\to H_{2},\quad\overline{A}=(A^{*})^{\tau}.

(2.9)

A simple exercise is to show validity of the above lemma:

Lemma 1
.

Let
A\in B(H_{1},H_{2})
,
B\in B(H_{2},H_{3})
for
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
,
H_{3}
Hilbert spaces. The following statements hold:

(1)

\overline{A}=J_{2}AJ_{1}=(A^{*})^{\tau}=(A^{\tau})^{*}
,

(2)

(AB)^{\tau}=B^{\tau}A^{\tau}
,
\overline{AB}=\overline{A}\,\overline{B}
,

(3)

(A^{\tau})^{\tau}=A
,
\overline{\overline{A}}=A
,

(4)

(J_{2}AJ_{1})^{*}=J_{1}A^{*}J_{2}
, and
(J_{2}AJ_{1})^{-1}=J_{1}A^{-1}J_{2}
for
A
invertible.

2.4.
Decomposable maps

Let
\mathscr{A}
,
\mathscr{B}
be partially ordered, unital C*-algebras and denote
\mathscr{A}^{+}
,
\mathscr{B}^{+}
the convex cones of
positive elements
in
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
, respectively. A bounded linear map
\phi:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
is called
[
16
]
:

•

positive
if
\phi(\mathscr{A}^{+})\subseteq\mathscr{B}^{+}
, i.e. when it is order preserving,

•

k
-positive
if its extension
\phi_{k}:\operatorname{\mathbb{M}}_{k}(\mathscr{A})\to\mathbb{M}_{k}(\mathscr{B})
defined by

\phi_{k}\left([a_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{k}\right)=\left[\phi(a_{ij})\right]_{i,j=1}^{k},\quad a_{ij}\in\mathscr{A}

(2.10)

is positive for given
k\in\mathbb{N}
(
1
-positive map is simply positive), and

•

completely positive
(CP) if it is
k
-positive for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let us now take
\mathscr{B}=B(H)
and let
\tau
be the transposition,
\tau(A)=A^{\tau}=J_{H}A^{*}J_{H}
as in Section
2.3
. Then, a linear map
\phi:\mathscr{A}\to B(H)
is additionally called:

•

k
-copositive
if
\tau\circ\phi
is
k
-positive, and

•

completely copositive
(coCP) if
\tau\circ\phi
is CP.

We will denote sets of all CP and coCP maps from
\mathscr{A}
to
B(H)
by
\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{coCP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
, respectively. Both these are pointed convex cones in
B(\mathscr{A},B(H))
, closed with respect to the BW-topology (for a comprehensive coverage of various subclasses of positive maps on C*-algebras see e.g.
[
16
]
). Finally, a positive linear map
\varphi:\mathscr{A}\to B(H)
is called
decomposable
if there exist maps
\phi_{1}\in\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
and
\phi_{2}\in\operatorname{\mathsf{coCP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
such that

\varphi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}.

(2.11)

The set
\operatorname{\mathsf{D}}(\mathscr{A},H)
of all decomposable maps is then the
Minkowski sum
of cones of CP and coCP maps,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathsf{D}}(\mathscr{A},H)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathscr{A},H)+\operatorname{\mathsf{coCP}}(\mathscr{A},H)

(2.12)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Conv}{\left(\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathscr{A},H)\cup\operatorname{\mathsf{coCP}}(\mathscr{A},H)\right)},

where
\operatorname{Conv}{\left(A\right)}
is the
convex hull of set
A
, i.e. the smallest convex set containing
A
. Hence,
\operatorname{\mathsf{D}}(\mathscr{A},H)
is also a BW-closed pointed convex cone, and moreover it is an example of a
mapping cone
, i.e. is algebraically closed with respect to compositions with CP maps, from left and right. For maps on
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
we will employ a simplified, self-explanatory notation
\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{coCP}}(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{D}}(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
.

Decomposable maps are characterized in terms of a dilation theorem by Størmer
[
16
,
17
]
: for every
\varphi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{D}}(\mathscr{A},H)
there exists a Hilbert space
K
, a bounded linear map
V:H\to K
and a Jordan morphism
\pi:\mathscr{A}\to B(K)
such that

\varphi(a)=V^{*}\pi(a)V

(2.13)

for
a\in\mathscr{A}
. Størmer’s result is a generalization of a celebrated Stinespring’s dilation theorem
[
8
]
which characterizes, in a similar manner, all CP maps: any
\phi
which is CP enjoys the factorized form (
2.13
), however with a *-homomorphism replacing Jordan morphism. Similarly, any coCP map is also in a form (
2.13
), but with
\pi
being a *-antihomomorphism. Despite an enormous effort of many mathematicians the exact structure of cones of not only positive, but also decomposable maps is still far from being well-understood, even in finite-dimensional case, with one notable exception: when
\mathscr{A}=\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
\mathscr{B}=\mathbb{M}_{m}(\mathbb{C})
and
mn\leqslant 6
, then every positive map
\phi:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
is automatically decomposable, as was shown by Woronowicz
[
18
]
. In higher-dimensional algebras this is however not the case since already for
n=m=3
there exist positive, indecomposable maps (i.e. not in a form (
2.11
)), as first demonstrated by Choi
[
19
]
and then explored in literature.

3.
Covariant decomposable maps

In this section, we will focus on some aspects of covariant linear maps, which are further assumed to be decomposable in the sense of Section
2.4
. Main results of this part are presented in a form of Theorems
3
and
5
below. We divide our results into three major subsections, where the first one concerns maps on general C*-algebras and the second one is devoted to finite-dimensional case and focused on operator sum representation with respect to Frobenius Hermitian basis.

3.1.
Maps on general C*-algebras

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a compact Lie group and let
\phi:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
be a linear map between unital C*-algebras. For any
g\in\mathcal{G}
, we denote with
g(\cdot)
the
action
of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathscr{A}
, i.e. we treat
g
as a *-automorphism on
\mathscr{A}
. For
a
,
b
in any C*-algebra we denote
\mathrm{Ad}_{a}(b)=aba^{*}
.

Definition 1
.

Let
U:\mathcal{G}\to\mathscr{B}
be a unitary representation of
\mathcal{G}
. If it happens that

\phi\circ g=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\phi,

(3.1)

or, explicitly,

\phi(g(a))=U(g)\phi(a)U(g)^{-1}

(3.2)

for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
a\in\mathscr{A}
, then
\phi
will be called
U
-covariant
.

Let us now take
\mathscr{B}=B(H)
. For a chosen representation
U:\mathcal{G}\to B(H)
denote by
\mathcal{C}_{U}
a norm-closed linear subspace in
B(\mathscr{A},B(H))
of all
U
-covariant maps (see Theorem
11
in the Appendix
A
).

Theorem 1
.

A linear map
\varphi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{D}}(\mathscr{A},H)
is
U
-covariant if and only if there exist
U
-covariant maps
\phi_{1}\in\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
and
\phi_{2}\in\operatorname{\mathsf{coCP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
such that
\varphi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}
.

Proof.

Only the “if” part needs to be addressed. Since
\mathcal{G}
is compact, it is amenable i.e. it admits a unique translation invariant mean with respect to Haar measure
\mu
. Define a linear operator
\mathsf{P}_{U}
on
B(\mathscr{A},B(H))
as a Bochner integral

\mathsf{P}_{U}(\phi)=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\phi\circ g^{-1}\,d\mu(g).

(3.3)

It is not hard to see that in fact
\mathsf{P}_{U}
is a projection onto the subspace of
U
-covariant maps: indeed, for any
\phi
and any
g\in\mathcal{G}
we have, by translation invariance,

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{U}(\phi)\circ g

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{Ad}_{U(h)}\circ\phi\circ h^{-1}g\,d\mu(h)=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{Ad}_{U(gh)}\circ\phi\circ(gh)^{-1}g\,d\mu(h)

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{U(h)}\circ\phi\circ h^{-1}g^{-1}g\,d\mu(h)=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\mathsf{P}_{U}(\phi),

since
\mu(\mathcal{G})=1
so
\mathsf{P}_{U}(\phi)
is
U
-covariant and
\operatorname{im}{\mathsf{P}_{U}}=\mathcal{C}_{U}
; also, by similar arguments we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{U}^{2}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{Ad}_{U(gh)}\circ\phi\circ(gh)^{-1}\,d\mu(h)d\mu(g)

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}}d\mu(g)\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{Ad}_{U(gg^{-1}h)}\circ\phi\circ(gg^{-1}h)^{-1}\,d\mu(h)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}}d\mu(g)\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{Ad}_{U(h)}\circ\phi\circ h^{-1}d\mu(h)=\mathsf{P}_{U}(\phi),

so it is a projection with range
\mathcal{C}_{U}
. Let
\varphi
be decomposable and
U
-covariant, so that
\varphi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}
with
\phi_{1}
being CP and
\phi_{2}
coCP. Then,

\varphi=\mathsf{P}_{U}(\varphi)=\mathsf{P}_{U}(\phi_{1})+\mathsf{P}_{U}(\phi_{2}).

(3.6)

However, by virtue of Theorem
12
(see Appendix
A
) we have
\mathsf{P}_{U}(\phi_{1})\in\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
and
\mathsf{P}_{U}(\phi_{1})\in\operatorname{\mathsf{coCP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
so
\varphi
indeed admits the claimed decomposition into a sum of
U
-covariant maps.
∎

It follows immediately that it suffices to find appropriate characterization of
U
-covariant coCP maps in order to determine all
U
-covariant decomposable maps. For a given unitary representation
U:\mathscr{A}\to B(H)
we define its
conjugate representation

\overline{U}
by setting

\overline{U}(g)=\overline{U(g)},

(3.7)

also unitary, with conjugation
A\mapsto\overline{A}
in
B(H)
given by
\overline{A}=(A^{*})^{\tau}=J_{H}AJ_{H}
as in Section
2.3
. A direct observation about covariance of coCP maps can be easily checked:

Theorem 2
.

The following statements hold:

(1)

A map
\phi
is
U
-covariant if and only if
\tau\circ\phi
is
\overline{U}
-covariant;

(2)

Conversely,
\phi
is
\overline{U}
-covariant if and only if
\tau\circ\phi
is
U
-covariant;

(3)

A map
\phi
is coCP and
U
-covariant if and only if there exists a
\overline{U}
-covariant CP map
\psi
such that
\phi=\tau\circ\psi
.

As the above theorem suggests, it is enough to find a characterization of
\overline{U}
-covariant CP maps. The following result, formulated in terms of a dilation theorem, allows to achieve this:

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a unital C*-algebra and
H
a Hilbert space. A linear map
\phi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
is
\overline{U}
-covariant if and only if there exists a Hilbert space
K
, a *-homomorphism
\pi:\mathscr{A}\to B(K)
and a bounded linear operator
V:H\to K
such that, for all
a\in\mathscr{A}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
,

\phi(a)=V^{*}\pi(a)V

(3.8)

and

(\pi\circ g)(a)=W(g)\pi(a)W(g)^{-1}

(3.9)

for a representation
W:\mathcal{G}\to B(K)
satisfying

V\overline{U}(g)=W(g)V.

(3.10)

Proof.

The theorem is precisely the Scutaru’s result
[
7
, Theorem 1]
, however formulated for a representation
\overline{U}
instead of
U
. For Reader’s convenience we show the construction explicitly.

Let
\mathscr{A}\odot H
be the algebraic tensor product. For any
\xi,\eta\in\mathscr{A}\odot H
in form
\xi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\otimes w_{i}
,
\eta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\otimes v_{i}
where
a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathscr{A}
,
w_{i},v_{i}\in H
, we define a positive semidefinite sesquilinear form
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\odot}
by setting

\left\langle\xi,\eta\right\rangle_{\odot}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left\langle w_{i},\phi(a_{i}^{*}b_{j})v_{j}\right\rangle

(3.11)

where
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
is an inner product in
H
. Since this form is degenerate in general, it induces a
seminorm

\xi\mapsto\sqrt{\left\langle\xi,\xi\right\rangle_{\odot}}
on
\mathscr{A}\odot H
. Therefore, the quotient space
(\mathscr{A}\odot H)/\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathcal{N}=\{\xi:\left\langle\xi,\xi\right\rangle_{\odot}=0\}
, is a pre-Hilbert space endowed with the inner product
\left\langle[\xi],[\eta]\right\rangle=\left\langle\xi,\eta\right\rangle_{\odot}
; here
[\xi]=\xi+\mathcal{N}
denotes the equivalence class of
\xi
in the quotient. Then one can define
K
as
K=\overline{(\mathscr{A}\odot H)/\mathcal{N}}^{\|\cdot\|}
, the Hilbert space completion of the quotient with respect to the norm induced by this inner product. Operator
V:H\to K
is then

Vx=[1_{\mathscr{A}}\otimes x],\quad x\in H

(3.12)

where
1_{\mathscr{A}}
is a unit in
\mathscr{A}
, while
\pi:\mathscr{A}\to B(H)
given by

\pi(a)[\xi]=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}aa_{i}\otimes w_{i}\right],\quad a\in\mathscr{A}

(3.13)

serves as a desired *-homomorphism. Construction of
K
,
V
and
\pi
is then standard as in the proof of the Stinespring’s theorem
[
8
]
. Next we define a representation
W_{0}:\mathcal{G}\to B(\mathscr{A}\odot H)
as well as its lifting
W:\mathcal{G}\to B(K)
as

W_{0}(g)\xi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(a_{i})\otimes\overline{U}(g)w_{i},\quad W(g)[\xi]=[W_{0}(g)\xi].

(3.14)

With direct computation one confirms that

\left\langle W_{0}(g)\xi,W_{0}(g)\eta\right\rangle_{\odot}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left\langle\overline{U}(g)w_{i},\phi(g(a_{i}^{*}b_{j}))\overline{U}(g)v_{j}\right\rangle_{\odot}=\left\langle\xi,\eta\right\rangle_{\odot}

(3.15)

which comes from unitarity of
\overline{U}
, group homomorphism properties of
g
and
\overline{U}
-covariance of
\phi
. In result, subspace
\mathcal{N}
is
W_{0}(g)
-invariant,
W_{0}(g)(\mathcal{N})=\mathcal{N}
. This allows to check

W(g)\pi(a)W(g^{-1})[\xi]=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(a)a_{i}\otimes w_{i}\right]=(\pi\circ g)(a)[\xi]

(3.16)

for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
\xi\in\mathscr{A}\odot H
. Since this equality carries over to the completion, we obtain

(\pi\circ g)(a)=W(g)\pi(a)W(g)^{-1},\quad g\in\mathcal{G},a\in\mathscr{A}

(3.17)

i.e. equation (
3.9
). Executing similar calculation for
\pi(a)V\overline{U}(g)h
,
h\in H
, leads to

\pi(a)V\overline{U}(g)h=[a\otimes\overline{U}(g)h].

(3.18)

Notice that “covariance” property (
3.9
) holds for
any
element
g\in\mathcal{G}
, so in particular, also for
g^{-1}
; this implies

(\pi\circ g^{-1})(a)=W(g^{-1})\pi(a)W(g),

(3.19)

\pi(g^{-1}(a))Vh=W(g^{-1})\pi(a)W(g)Vh,

(3.20)

giving

\displaystyle\pi(a)W(g)Vh

\displaystyle=W(g)\pi(g^{-1}(a))Vh=W(g)[g^{-1}(a)\otimes h]

(3.21)

\displaystyle=\left[g(g^{-1}(a))\otimes\overline{U}(g)h\right]=[a\otimes\overline{U}(g)h].

This however is the same as (
3.18
), i.e. we obtain

\pi(a)V\overline{U}(g)h=\pi(a)W(g)Vh,

(3.22)

satisfied for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
h\in H
so
V\overline{U}(g)=W(g)V
and (
3.10
) is also proved.
∎

Theorem 4
.

A linear map
\phi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{coCP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
is
U
-covariant if and only if there exists a Hilbert space
K
, a *-antihomomorphism
\pi_{-}:\mathscr{A}\to B(K)
and a bounded linear operator
V:H\to K
such that, for all
a\in\mathscr{A}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
,

\phi(a)=V^{*}\pi_{-}(a)V

(3.23)

and
(\pi_{-}\circ g)(a)=\tilde{U}(g)\pi_{-}(a)\tilde{U}(g)^{-1}
for a representation
\tilde{U}:\mathcal{G}\to B(K)
satisfying
VU(g)=\tilde{U}(g)V
.

Proof.

From Theorem
2
it is clear that it suffices to combine Theorem
3
with appropriate transposition map. Let
\psi:\mathscr{A}\to B(H)
be CP and
\overline{U}
-covariant, so
\psi(a)=X^{*}\pi_{+}(a)X
where
X:H\to K
is linear and bounded, and
\pi_{+}:\mathscr{A}\to B(K)
is a *-homomorphism for Hilbert space
K
properly constructed, as Theorem
3
dictates.
K
comes with its own antilinear isometric isomorphism
J_{K}:K\to K
of complex conjugation of vectors,
J_{K}x=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{I}}\overline{\left\langle\kappa_{n},x\right\rangle}\kappa_{n}
for
x\in K
and
\{\kappa_{n}\}_{n\in\mathcal{I}}
an orthonormal basis spanning
K
; then,
A\mapsto\tau(A)=J_{K}A^{*}J_{K}
is a transposition on
B(K)
(we use the same symbol
\tau
regardless of the underlying space). Lemma
1
and idempotence of
J_{K}
yield

\displaystyle(\tau\circ\psi)(a)

\displaystyle=J_{H}(X^{*}\pi_{+}(a)X)^{*}J_{H}=J_{H}X^{*}J_{K}\,J_{K}\pi_{+}(a)^{*}J_{K}\,J_{K}XJ_{H}

(3.24)

\displaystyle=V^{*}(\tau\circ\pi_{+})(a)V=\phi(a)

for
V=J_{K}XJ_{H}:H\to K
, and
\tau\circ\pi_{+}=\pi_{-}:\mathscr{A}\to B(K)
a *-antihomomorphism, as claimed. Employing equations (
3.11
) and (
3.14
), homomorphism properties of
g
and
\overline{U}
-covariance of
\psi
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle\xi,W_{0}(g)\eta\right\rangle_{\odot}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left\langle w_{i},\psi(g(g^{-1}(a_{i})^{*})b_{j})\overline{U}(g)v_{j}\right\rangle

(3.25)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left\langle g^{-1}(a_{i})\otimes\overline{U}(g)^{*}w_{i},b_{j}\otimes v_{j}\right\rangle,

after some algebra, which yields the Hermitian conjugation of
W(g)
to be

W(g)^{*}[\xi]=W(g)^{-1}[\xi]=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{-1}(a_{i})\otimes\overline{U}(g)^{*}w_{i}\right],

(3.26)

i.e.
W(g)
is clearly unitary. Then, transposing both sides of equality (
3.9
) gives

\displaystyle(\pi_{-}\circ g)(a)

\displaystyle=J_{K}(W(g)^{-1})^{*}J_{K}\,J_{K}\pi(a)^{*}J_{K}\,J_{K}W(g)^{*}J_{K}

(3.27)

\displaystyle=\overline{W}(g)\pi_{-}(a)\overline{W}(g)^{-1},

from which we decipher
\tilde{U}=\overline{W}
. Finally, by transposing left hand side of (
3.10
) with
V
replaced by
X
we have

\displaystyle\tau(X\overline{U}(g))

\displaystyle=J_{H}\overline{U}(g)^{*}J_{H}\,J_{H}X^{*}J_{K}=\left((J_{K}XJ_{H})\overline{\overline{U}(g)}\right)^{*}

(3.28)

\displaystyle=(VU(g))^{*},

while transpose of the right hand side is

\tau(W(g)X)=J_{H}X^{*}J_{K}\,J_{K}W(g)^{*}J_{K}=V^{*}\overline{W}(g)^{*}.

(3.29)

Equating (
3.28
) with (
3.29
) yields
VU(g)=\tilde{U}(g)V
, i.e.
\tilde{U}=\overline{W}
satisfies the required equation and the proof is finished.
∎

As a kind of a corollary, we present the following dilation theorem characterizing
U
-covariant decomposable maps:

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a unital C*-algebra and
H
a Hilbert space. A linear map
\varphi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{D}}(\mathscr{A},H)
is
U
-covariant if and only if there exists a Hilbert space
K
, a Jordan morphism
\pi:\mathscr{A}\to B(K)
and a bounded linear operator
V:H\to K
such that, for all
a\in\mathscr{A}
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
,

\varphi(a)=V^{*}\pi(a)V

(3.30)

and

(\pi\circ g)(a)=Z(g)\pi(a)Z(g)^{-1}

(3.31)

for a representation
Z:\mathcal{G}\to B(K)
satisfying

VU(g)=Z(g)V.

(3.32)

Proof.

Let
\varphi:\mathscr{A}\to B(H)
be decomposable and
U
-covariant. Theorem
1
guarantees that there exist a CP map
\phi_{1}
and a coCP map
\phi_{2}
, both
U
-covariant, such that
\varphi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}
. Scutaru’s result
[
7
, Theorem 9]
and Theorem
3
infer existence of Hilbert spaces
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
, bounded linear operators
V_{1}:H\to K_{1}
,
V_{2}:H\to K_{2}
, a *-homomorphism
\pi_{1}:\mathscr{A}\to B(K_{1})
and a *-antihomomorphism
\pi_{2}:\mathscr{A}\to B(K_{2})
, such that

\phi_{i}(a)=V_{i}^{*}\pi_{i}(a)V_{i},

(3.33a)

(\pi_{i}\circ g)(a)=\tilde{U}(g)\pi_{i}(a)\tilde{U}(g)^{-1},

(3.33b)

V_{i}U(g)=\tilde{U}(g)V_{i},

(3.33c)

for
i=1,2
and for all
a\in\mathscr{A}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Following Størmer
[
16
,
17
]
, let us then take
K=K_{1}\oplus K_{2}
and define
V
and
\pi
by

V:H\to K_{1}\oplus K_{2},\quad V=V_{1}+V_{2},

(3.34a)

\pi:\mathscr{A}\to B(K_{1}\oplus K_{2}),\quad\pi(a)=\mathsf{P}_{1}\pi_{1}(a)\mathsf{P}_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{2}\pi_{2}(a)\mathsf{P}_{2},

(3.34b)

where
\mathsf{P}_{1}
,
\mathsf{P}_{2}
are orthogonal projections onto
K_{1}
,
K_{2}
. By direct check,
a\mapsto\mathsf{P}_{1}\pi_{1}(a)\mathsf{P}_{1}
and
a\mapsto\mathsf{P}_{2}\pi_{2}(a)\mathsf{P}_{2}
are a *-homomorphism and *-antihomomorphism, respectively, and so
\pi
is a Jordan morphism. Therefore, since
\operatorname{im}{\mathsf{P}_{i}}=\operatorname{im}{V_{i}}=K_{i}
, we can put
V_{i}=\mathsf{P}_{i}V_{i}
and

\displaystyle\varphi(a)

\displaystyle=V^{*}\pi(a)V

(3.35)

\displaystyle=(V_{1}^{*}\mathsf{P}_{1}+V_{2}^{*}\mathsf{P}_{2})(\mathsf{P}_{1}\pi_{1}(a)\mathsf{P}_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{2}\pi_{2}(a)\mathsf{P}_{2})(\mathsf{P}_{1}V_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{2}V_{2})

\displaystyle=V_{1}^{*}\pi_{1}(a)V_{1}+V_{2}^{*}\pi_{2}(a)V_{2}

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}(a)+\phi_{2}(a),

so
\varphi
has the claimed form (
3.30
). Let
Z:\mathcal{G}\to B(K_{1}\oplus K_{2})
be given by

Z(g)=\mathsf{P}_{1}\tilde{U}(g)\mathsf{P}_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{2}\tilde{U}(g)\mathsf{P}_{2}.

(3.36)

One easily confirms
Z(g)^{-1}=\mathsf{P}_{1}\tilde{U}(g)^{-1}\mathsf{P}_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{2}\tilde{U}(g)^{-1}\mathsf{P}_{2}
. Then, a simple algebra involving orthogonality of projections
\mathsf{P}_{i}
shows

Z(g)\pi(a)Z(g)^{-1}=(\pi\circ g)(a),

(3.37)

i.e. (
3.31
) is satisfied. Finally,

\displaystyle Z(g)V

\displaystyle=(\mathsf{P}_{1}\tilde{U}(g)\mathsf{P}_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{2}\tilde{U}(g)\mathsf{P}_{2})(\mathsf{P}_{1}V_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{2}V_{2})

(3.38)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{1}\tilde{U}(g)V_{1}+\mathsf{P}_{2}\tilde{U}(g)V_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}_{1}V_{1}U(g)+\mathsf{P}_{2}V_{2}U(g)

\displaystyle=VU(g),

since
\tilde{U}(g)V_{i}=V_{i}U(g)
, so (
3.32
) also holds.
∎

3.2.
Maps on
B(H)

As a particular example, consider a von Neumann algebra
\mathscr{A}=B(H)
. Let
\varphi:B(H)\to B(H)
be decomposable and
U
-covariant; then, Theorems
1
and
2
yield existence of maps
\phi,\psi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(B(H))
such that
\varphi=\phi+\tau\circ\psi
with
\phi
being
U
-covariant and
\psi
being
\overline{U}
-covariant. Employing Kraus lemma
[
20
]
, map
\varphi
may be further written as

\varphi(A)=\sum_{n}X_{n}AX_{n}^{*}+\sum_{n}W_{n}A^{\tau}W_{n}^{*},\quad A\in B(H),

(3.39)

for some countable families of bounded operators
(X_{n})
,
(W_{n})
on
H
such that
\sum_{n}X_{n}X_{n}^{*}
and
\sum_{n}W_{n}W_{n}^{*}
converge in weak-* topology in
B(H)
. For
\mathcal{G}
a compact Lie group, let
g
be definad via

g(A)=V(g)AV(g)^{-1}

(3.40)

where
V(g)\in B(H)
is unitary, so
g
is also a unitary representation of
\mathcal{G}
. The covariance condition then reads

\varphi\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{V(g)}=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\varphi,\quad g\in\mathcal{G}

(3.41)

which translates to conditions for the CP and coCP parts

\displaystyle\phi(A)

\displaystyle=U(g)\phi\left(V(g)^{-1}AV(g)\right)U(g)^{-1},

(3.42)

\displaystyle\psi(A)

\displaystyle=\overline{U}(g)\psi\left(V(g)^{-1}AV(g)\right)\overline{U}(g)^{-1}

(3.43)

being satisfied for all
A\in B(H)
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
.

Theorem 6
.

A decomposable map
\varphi
on
B(H)
is
U
-covariant if and only if there exist families
(X_{n})
,
(W_{n})
in
B(H)
such that
\sum_{n}X_{n}X_{n}^{*}
,
\sum_{n}W_{n}W_{n}^{*}
are weakly-* convergent in
B(H)
and

U(g)X_{n}V(g)^{-1}=\sum_{m}N_{nm}X_{m},\quad\overline{U}(g)W_{n}V(g)^{-1}=\sum_{m}M_{nm}W_{m},

(3.44)

for some unitary matrices
[N_{nm}]
,
[M_{nm}]
.

Proof.

It is enough to notice that covariance conditions (
3.42
) and (
3.43
) yield, with assumption that Stinespring representations involved are minimal,

\displaystyle\phi(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}U(g)X_{n}V(g)^{-1}AV(g)X_{n}^{*}U(g)^{-1},

(3.45)

\displaystyle\psi(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\overline{U}(g)W_{n}V(g)^{-1}AV(g)W_{n}^{*}\overline{U}(g)^{-1}

(3.46)

where both series converge in weak-* topology in
B(H)
for every
A
, i.e. they represent CP maps via new Kraus operators
\tilde{X}_{n}=U(g)X_{n}V(g)^{-1}
,
\tilde{W}_{n}=\overline{U}(g)W_{n}V(g)^{-1}
. Since minimal Stinespring representations of the same map are unique up to a unitary transformation, the claim follows.
∎

3.3.
Maps covariant w.r.t. the maximal commutative subgroup of
\mathrm{U}(n)

In this section we focus our attention on maps on
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
subject to a very particular covariance condition. Namely, let
\mathscr{A}=\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and let
\mathcal{G}=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times...\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
for
\mathbb{S}^{1}
a circle group, so that any element
g\in\mathcal{G}
is of a form
g=(g_{j})_{j=1}^{n}=(e^{ix_{1}},...,e^{ix_{n}})
for some real vector
(x_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Group structure of
\mathcal{G}
is defined element-wise, i.e. for
g,h\in\mathcal{G}
we have
gh=(g_{j}h_{j})
,
g^{-1}=(g_{j}^{-1})=(e^{-ix_{j}})
and
(1,...,1)
is a neutral element. We define
U:\mathcal{G}\to\mathrm{U}(n)
as

U(g)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{ix_{j}}E_{jj},\quad g=(e^{ix_{j}}),

(3.47)

i.e.
U(\mathcal{G})
is the
maximal commutative subgroup
of all unitary diagonal matrices of size
n
. For the automorphism
g:\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we take it in a form
g(A)=U(g)AU(g)^{-1}
. In other words, we will be interested in all
U
-covariant maps
\varphi:\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
subject to equality

\varphi\left(U(g)AU(g)^{-1}\right)=U(g)\varphi(A)U(g)^{-1}

(3.48)

for all
g=(e^{ix_{j}})
,
(x_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and all
A\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
; we will simply refer to them as
covariant
throughout this section.

3.3.1.
Conditions for covariance

One shows
\phi
is covariant if and only if
[
21
, Proposition 28]

\phi(A)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(1-\delta_{ij})E_{ij}AE_{ji}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}b_{ij}E_{ii}AE_{jj}

(3.49)

for some
a_{ij},b_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}
. Further,
\phi
is
Hermiticity preserving
,
\phi(A)^{*}=\phi(A^{*})
, if and only if
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
and
[b_{ij}]
is Hermitian. Also,
\phi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
if and only if
a_{ij}\geqslant 0
and
[b_{ij}]\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})^{+}
. Equation (
3.49
) is a complete characterization of the so-called
operator sum representation
of covariant maps, expressed in terms of a canonical basis in
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. In this section however we find such characterization also with respect to the
Frobenius basis

\{F_{i}\}
. That said, we focus on the operator sum representation

\phi(A)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n^{2}}c^{\phi}_{ij}F_{i}AF_{j}^{*}

(3.50)

and internal structure of the matrix
\hat{c}_{\phi}=[c^{\phi}_{ij}]\in\mathbb{M}_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{C})
which guarantees the covariance of
\phi
; note that
[c^{\phi}_{ij}]
uniquely determines the map
\phi
, up to the choice of a basis in
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})

[
11
]
. For this we first formulate the following sufficient and necessary condition for covariance in a slightly more general manner than we need.

Let
U,V:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be (any) two unitary representations. We say a linear map
\phi:\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is
(U,V)
-covariant
if

\phi\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}=\mathrm{Ad}_{V(g)}\circ\phi

(3.51)

for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Also, let us define a matrix-valued function
\hat{\alpha}_{U,V}:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{M}_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{C})
by

\hat{\alpha}_{U,V}(g)=[\alpha^{U,V}_{ij}(g)],\quad\alpha^{U,V}_{ij}(g)=\left\langle F_{i},F_{j}^{\prime}(g)\right\rangle_{2},

(3.52)

for

F_{i}^{\prime}(g)=V(g)^{-1}F_{i}U(g)

(3.53)

constituting for a new orthonormal basis in
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. We have the following result:

Proposition 1
.

A linear map
\phi:\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is
(U,V)
-covariant if and only if
\hat{c}_{\phi}
commutes with
\hat{\alpha}_{U,V}(g)
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
. In result, a linear subspace
\mathcal{C}_{U,V}
of all
(U,V)
-covariant maps on
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is isomorphic to
\hat{\alpha}_{U,V}(\mathcal{G})^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The covariance condition (
3.51
) yields

\phi=\mathrm{Ad}^{-1}_{V(g)}\circ\phi\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}

(3.54)

for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Putting (
3.50
) into (
3.54
) and then expanding new matrices
F_{i}^{\prime}(g)
in the Frobenius basis,
F_{i}^{\prime}(g)=\sum_{j}\left\langle F_{j},F_{i}^{\prime}(g)\right\rangle_{2}F_{j}
, we deduce

c^{\phi}_{ij}=\sum_{kl}\alpha^{U,V}_{ik}(g)c^{\phi}_{kl}\overline{\alpha^{U,V}_{jl}(g)},\quad\text{or}\quad\hat{c}_{\phi}=\hat{\alpha}_{U,V}(g)\hat{c}_{\phi}{\hat{\alpha}(g)}_{U,V}^{*}

(3.55)

after easy algebra. Since
\hat{\alpha}_{U,V}(g)
is clearly unitary,
\hat{c}_{\phi}
and
\hat{\alpha}_{U,V}(g)
commute. The claim then follows after noting that
\hat{c}_{\phi}
identifies
\phi
uniquely.
∎

For covariance w.r.t. the diagonal unitary matrices as discussed here,
U=V
is given by (
3.47
). The subspace
\mathcal{C}_{U}
of all covariant maps is then determined by structure of
\hat{\alpha}_{U,U}(g)\equiv\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)
and appropriate commutant can be found with a bit of effort: it is shown in the Proposition
11
(Appendix
A
) that each
\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)
has the following block structure

\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\cos{\Delta}&-\sin{\Delta}&0\\
\sin{\Delta}&\cos{\Delta}&0\\
0&0&I_{n}\end{array}\right),

(3.56)

where
I_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is an identity matrix, and

\Delta=\operatorname{diag}{\{x_{\mu(k)}-x_{\nu(k)}\}},\quad 1\leqslant k\leqslant\binom{n}{2}

(3.57)

is determined by
g
; here notation
\mu(k)
,
\nu(k)
denotes a bijection
k\mapsto(\mu,\nu)
which defines an ordering of basis
F_{i}
as mentioned in Section
2.2
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. We say that the set
S\subset\mathbb{C}
is
z
-congruence free
when no two different
z_{1}
,
z_{2}
exist in
S
such that
z_{1}-z_{2}=kz
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, or when
S+(\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\})z
has no intersection with
S
. We make the following observation:

Proposition 2
.

For every
g\in\mathcal{G}
there exists an anti-Hermitian matrix
A(g)
such that
\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)=e^{A(g)}
and
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A(g)\right)}
is
2\pi i
-congruence free.

Proof.

Restricting the mapping
\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto g=(e^{ix_{j}})_{j=1}^{n}
to the hypercube
[0,2\pi)^{n}
makes the mapping bijective, so it is always possible to represent matrices
U(g)
by vectors
(x_{j})
such that
0\leqslant x_{j}<2\pi
. Matrix
\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)
given in (
3.56
) almost resembles the rotation matrix so we can deduce the form of
A(g)
,

A(g)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-\Delta&0\\
\Delta&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right).

(3.58)

Naturally,
(x_{j})\in[0,2\pi)^{n}
implies
r(\Delta)<2\pi
, with
r(T)=\max_{\lambda\in\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(T\right)}}{|\lambda|}
being the spectral radius of
T
. One then confirms with power series expansion that indeed
e^{A(g)}=\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)
so
A(g)
is the matrix logarithm of
\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)
, clearly anti-Hermitian. The non-zero eigenvalues
\lambda
of
A(g)
satisfy

0=\det{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\lambda I&-\Delta\\
\Delta&-\lambda I\end{array}\right)}=\det{(\lambda^{2}I+\Delta^{2})},

(3.59)

so
\lambda\in\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A(g)\right)}
if and only if
\lambda^{2}\in\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(-\Delta^{2}\right)}
. This means all
\lambda
must be of a form

\lambda=\pm i|x_{j}-x_{k}|

(3.60)

for some
j<k
. However

r(A(g))=\max_{j,k}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|}=r(|\Delta|)<2\pi,

(3.61)

so there exists no pair
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
of eigenvalues in
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A(g)\right)}
such that
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}=2k\pi i
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
i.e.
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A(g)\right)}
is
2\pi i
-congruence free.
∎

The
2\pi i
-congruence freedom of
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A(g)\right)}
allows to simplify calculations significantly:

Proposition 3
.

Let
A(g)
be as in Proposition
2
. Then
[\hat{c}_{\phi},\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)]=0
if and only if
[\hat{c}_{\phi},A(g)]=0
.

Proof.

It is proved in Theorem
14
(Appendix
A
), that for any two bounded linear operators
A,B
on a Banach space such that
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)}
is
2\pi i
-congruence free, we have
[e^{A},B]=0
if and only if
[A,B]=0
. The claim then follows by Proposition
2
.
∎

With the
2\pi i
-congruence freedom of the spectrum in our disposal, finding the commutant of
\hat{\alpha}_{U}(\mathcal{G})
is fairly easy:

Lemma 2
.

M\in\mathbb{M}_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{C})
satisfies
[M,\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)]=0
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
if and only if

M=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}A_{1}&-A_{2}&0\\
A_{2}&A_{1}&0\\
0&0&A_{3}\end{array}\right)

(3.62)

for arbitrary matrices
A_{1},A_{2}\in\operatorname{Diag}_{n(n-1)/2}(\mathbb{C})
and
A_{3}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

Let
M=[M_{ij}]
where sizes of blocks
M_{ij}
correspond with those of
\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)
. Equation
[M,\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)]=0
yields a system of matrix equations

M_{11}\Delta=\Delta M_{22},\quad\Delta M_{11}=M_{22}\Delta,

(3.63a)

M_{12}\Delta=-\Delta M_{21},\quad\Delta M_{12}=-M_{21}\Delta,

(3.63b)

\Delta M_{23}=\Delta M_{13}=M_{32}\Delta=M_{31}\Delta=0,

(3.63c)

which must hold for all possible
\Delta
, specified again by (
3.57
). In particular, when
x_{j}
are chosen all distinct,
\Delta
is invertible and so
M_{23}
,
M_{32}
,
M_{13}
and
M_{31}
must all vanish. Moreover, we must have, for
\Delta
invertible,

M_{11}=\Delta M_{22}\Delta^{-1}=\Delta^{-1}M_{22}\Delta,

(3.64a)

M_{22}=\Delta M_{11}\Delta^{-1}=\Delta^{-1}M_{11}\Delta,

(3.64b)

M_{12}=-\Delta M_{21}\Delta^{-1}=-\Delta^{-1}M_{21}\Delta,

(3.64c)

M_{21}=-\Delta M_{12}\Delta^{-1}=-\Delta^{-1}M_{12}\Delta,

(3.64d)

which yield
[\Delta^{2},M_{ij}]=0
, or
M_{ij}=\Delta^{2}M_{ij}\Delta^{-2}
, for all
i,j\in\{1,2\}
. Let us denote
M_{ij}=[m^{(i,j)}_{kl}]_{kl}
. Since
\Delta^{n}
is diagonal for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, equality
M_{ij}=\Delta^{2}M_{ij}\Delta^{-2}
yields

m^{(i,j)}_{kl}=\left(\frac{x_{\mu(k)}-x_{\nu(k)}}{x_{\mu(l)}-x_{\nu(l)}}\right)^{2}m^{(i,j)}_{kl},

(3.65)

for all
k\neq l
. For (
3.65
) to hold for all possible choices of vectors
(x_{j})
we need
m^{(i,j)}_{kl}=0
, i.e. all blocks
M_{ij}
must be diagonal. This in turn yields
M_{11}=M_{22}
,
M_{12}=-M_{21}
and
M_{33}
can be arbitrary. This shows
M
indeed has the claimed form.
∎

Clearly, decomposable maps require conditions for both covariance and conjugate covariance by Theorems
1
and
2
, so let us now focus on a conjugate covariant case. We will call a map
\phi:\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})

conjugate covariant
if and only if it is
(U,\overline{U})
-covariant, i.e. when it satisfies

\phi\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}=\mathrm{Ad}_{\overline{U}(g)}\circ\phi

(3.66)

for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
, where
\overline{U}(g)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{-ix_{j}}E_{jj}
. The matrix
\hat{\beta}_{U}(g)\equiv\hat{\alpha}_{U,\overline{U}}(g)
in this case admits a structure (see again Proposition
11
in Appendix
A
)

\hat{\beta}_{U}(g)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}e^{i\Theta}&0&0\\
0&e^{i\Theta}&0\\
0&0&R(g)\end{array}\right),

(3.67)

where

\displaystyle\Theta

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}{\{x_{\mu(j)}+x_{\nu(j)}\}},\quad 1\leqslant j\leqslant\binom{n}{2}

(3.68)

\displaystyle R(g)

\displaystyle=[\langle F^{\mathrm{d}}_{i},{F^{\mathrm{d}}_{j}}^{\prime}\rangle_{2}]_{ij},\quad 1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n,

(3.69)

with
R(g)
symmetric and unitary. And again, determining a general structure of
\hat{c}_{\phi}
can be achieved with a bit of algebraic work, in a course of solving a number of linear matrix equations:

Lemma 3
.

M\in\mathbb{M}_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{C})
satisfies
[M,\hat{\beta}_{U}(g)]=0
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
if and only if

M=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}A_{11}&A_{12}&0\\
A_{21}&A_{22}&0\\
0&0&A_{33}\end{array}\right)

(3.70)

for
A_{ij}\in\operatorname{Diag}_{n(n-1)/2}(\mathbb{C})
arbitrary and
A_{33}=[\kappa_{rs}]\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
symmetric and specified by conditions

\displaystyle\kappa_{rs}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{rs(r+1)(s+1)}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}-ra_{r+1}\right)\quad\text{for }r<s<n,

(3.71a)

\displaystyle\kappa_{rn}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{nr(r+1)}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}-ra_{r+1}\right)\quad\text{for }r<n,

(3.71b)

\displaystyle\kappa_{rr}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r(r+1)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}+r^{2}a_{r+1}\right)\quad\text{for }r<n,

(3.71c)

\displaystyle\kappa_{nn}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i},

(3.71d)

for arbitrary sequence
(a_{i})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Proof.

Denote again
M=[M_{ij}]
with blocks
M_{ij}
corresponding to those in
\hat{\beta}_{U}(g)
. Equation
M=\hat{\beta}_{U}(g)M\hat{\beta}_{U}(g)^{*}
yields a system of matrix equations

e^{i\Theta}M_{ij}e^{-i\Theta}=M_{ij},\quad 1\leqslant i,j\leqslant 2,

(3.72a)

e^{i\Theta}M_{i3}R(g)^{*}=M_{i3},\quad R(g)M_{3i}e^{-i\Theta}=M_{3i},\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant 2,

(3.72b)

R(g)M_{33}R(g)^{*}=M_{33}.

(3.72c)

For case
i,j\leqslant 2
let
M_{ij}=[m^{(i,j)}_{kl}]_{kl}
and notice (
3.72a
) yields

\left(1-e^{i(x_{\mu(k)}+x_{\nu(k)}-x_{\mu(l)}-x_{\nu(l)})}\right)m^{(i,j)}_{kl}=0

(3.73)

which must hold for all possible choices of
(x_{j})
. Since this is clearly true for
k=l
, we infer all off-diagonal terms
m^{(i,j)}_{kl}
,
k\neq l
, vanish, i.e. all blocks
M_{ij}
for
i,j\leqslant 2
are diagonal. For (
3.72b
), notice that both those matrix equations are in fact eigenequations for linear mappings

T_{g}(X)=e^{i\Theta}XR(g)^{*},\quad S_{g}(X)=R(g)Xe^{-i\Theta},

(3.74)

for eigenvalue
1
. Recall that every such mapping
X\mapsto AXB
, where
X,A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, can be isomorphically represented as a linear operator
A\otimes B^{\tau}:\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}\to\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
under the transformation of
vectorization
(or
flattening
) of matrices
[
22
]
. Hence, (
3.72b
) is equivalent to the pair of mutually adjoint eigenequations

\left(e^{i\Theta}\otimes R(g)^{*}\right)x=x,\quad\left(R(g)\otimes e^{-i\Theta}\right)x=x.

(3.75)

However,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(T_{g}-\mathrm{id}\right)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(\left(e^{i\Theta}\otimes R(g)^{*}-I_{n^{2}}\right)\right)}

(3.76)

\displaystyle=-1+\{e^{-i(x_{\mu(k)}+x_{\nu(k)}-2x_{j})}\}

and one can always find such vector
(x_{j})
, and such
g_{0}\in\mathcal{G}
, that
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(T_{g_{0}}-\mathrm{id}\right)}
does not contain
0
, i.e. the resolvent operator
(T_{g_{0}}-\mathrm{id})^{-1}
exists and
T_{g_{0}}
has no eigenvalue
1
. The equation
T_{g_{0}}(M_{i3})=M_{i3}
can be therefore satisfied only when
M_{i3}=0
. Since matrix
M
is expected to satisfy (
3.72b
) for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
, we infer
M_{i3}=0
is the only case. The same argument then applies to
S_{g}
as its spectrum is simply a complex conjugate of
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(T_{g}\right)}
and so
M_{3i}=0
as well.

For the remaining equation (
3.72c
), note that (
3.69
) implies
R(g)
is the matrix of a linear map
\mathcal{U}_{g}(A)=U(g)AU(g)
restricted to a subspace
\mathrm{Diag}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, with matrix elements computed in basis
\{F_{i}^{\mathrm{d}}\}
. It is straightforward to check that the spectrum of this map is

\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(\left.\mathcal{U}_{g}\right|_{\mathrm{Diag}_{n}(\mathbb{C})}\right)}=\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(R(g)\right)}=\{e^{2ix_{j}},1\leqslant j\leqslant n\},

(3.77)

its eigenvectors are
E_{ii}
and in result, eigenvectors
r_{i}
of
R(g)
are

r_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\langle F_{k}^{\mathrm{d}},E_{ii}\right\rangle_{2}e_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(F_{k}^{\mathrm{d}})_{ii}e_{k},

(3.78)

for
e_{k}
the standard basis spanning
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
(F_{k}^{\mathrm{d}})_{ii}
the
i
-th diagonal element of
F_{k}^{\mathrm{d}}
. The equation (
3.72c
) is an eigenequation for a map
\mathrm{Ad}_{R(g)}
for eigenvalue
1
. Again, with vectorization one then confirms that

\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(\mathrm{Ad}_{R(g)}\right)}=\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(R(g)\otimes R(g)^{*}\right)}=\{1,e^{\pm 2i(x_{j}-x_{k})}:1\leqslant j<k\leqslant n\}

(3.79)

and so
1\in\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(\mathrm{Ad}_{R(g)}\right)}
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
and eigenequation
\mathrm{Ad}_{R(g)}(X)=R(g)XR(g)^{*}
always has a nontrivial solution. Since
\{r_{j}\}
is an orthonormal basis spanning
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we can always postulate
X
in a general form

X=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}x_{ij}\left\langle r_{j},\cdot\right\rangle r_{i},\quad x_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}.

(3.80)

Substituting
X
into the eigenequation with
R(g)
,
R(g)^{*}
expressed by their spectral decompositions we obtain

X=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}e^{2i(x_{j}-x_{k})}x_{jk}\left\langle r_{k},\cdot\right\rangle r_{j},

(3.81)

which must be satisfied for all possible choices of
(x_{j})
. This however is again guaranteed only if off-diagonal elements
x_{ij}=0
, i.e.
X
has a diagonal form

X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\left\langle r_{i},\cdot\right\rangle r_{i},

(3.82)

for arbitrary coefficients
a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. This can be further rewritten with (
3.78
) as

X=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{n}a_{i}(F^{\mathrm{d}}_{j})_{ii}(F^{\mathrm{d}}_{k})_{ii}E_{jk},

(3.83)

where from (

) we have

(F^{\mathrm{d}}_{k})_{ii}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(k+1)}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\delta_{ij}-k\delta_{k+1,k+1}\right).

(3.84)

Now, with a bit of algebra, one confirms all matrix elements of
X
are indeed of a form (
3.71
), so one identifies
A_{33}=X
and the proof is concluded.
∎

As a corollary of this section we formulate the following characterization of operator sum representation of a map with respect to the Frobenius basis, for both covariance and conjugate covariance:

Theorem 7
.

A linear map
\phi:\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is:

(1)

covariant w.r.t. the diagonal unitaries
if and only if
\hat{c}_{\phi}
admits a form (
3.62
), i.e.

\hat{c}_{\phi}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}C_{1}&-C_{2}&0\\
C_{2}&C_{1}&0\\
0&0&C_{3}\end{array}\right),

(3.85)

for arbitrary matrices
C_{1},C_{2}\in\operatorname{Diag}_{n(n-1)/2}{(\mathbb{C})}
and
C_{3}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
;

(2)

conjugate covariant w.r.t. the diagonal unitaries
if and only if
\hat{c}_{\phi}
admits the form elaborated in Lemma
3
, i.e.

\hat{c}_{\phi}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}C_{11}&C_{12}&0\\
C_{21}&C_{22}&0\\
0&0&C_{33}\end{array}\right)

(3.86)

for arbitrary matrices
C_{ij}\in\operatorname{Diag}_{n(n-1)/2}{(\mathbb{C})}
,
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant 2
, and
C_{33}=[\kappa_{ij}]\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
given by equations (
3.71
) for arbitrary sequence
(a_{i})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

3.3.2.
Decomposable maps

Recall, that a linear map
\phi:\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is
Hermiticity preserving
if and only if
\hat{c}_{\phi}
is Hermitian and
completely positive
if and only if
\hat{c}_{\phi}
is positive semidefinite
[

]
.

Proposition 4
.

Let a linear map
\phi:\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be covariant w.r.t. the diagonal unitaries. Then:

(1)

\phi
is
Hermiticity preserving
if and only if
\hat{c}_{\phi}
admits the form (
3.85
) with
C_{1}
real,
C_{2}
imaginary and
C_{3}
Hermitian;

(2)

\phi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
if and only if
\hat{c}_{\phi}
admits the form (
3.85
) with
C_{1}\geqslant|C_{2}|
and
C_{3}\geqslant 0
;

(3)

\phi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{coCP}}(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
if and only if
\hat{c}_{\tau\circ\phi}
admits the form (
3.86
) with
(a_{i})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
,
C_{11},C_{22}\geqslant 0
,
C_{12}=C_{21}^{*}
and
|C_{12}|\geqslant\sqrt{C_{11}C_{22}}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
7
,
\hat{c}_{\phi}
is of a form (
3.85
) with diagonal
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
. Hence,
\hat{c}_{\phi}
is Hermitian if and only if
C_{1}
,
C_{3}
are Hermitian and
C_{2}
is anti-Hermitian, i.e.
C_{1}
is real and
C_{2}
is purely imaginary. For positive semidefiniteness, consider a characteristic polynomial

\displaystyle w(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\det{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C_{1}-\lambda I&-C_{2}\\
C_{2}&C_{1}-\lambda I\end{array}\right)}=\det{\left[(C_{1}-\lambda I)^{2}+C_{2}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=\det{\left(C_{1}^{2}-2\lambda C_{1}+\lambda^{2}I+C_{2}^{2}\right)}=\det{\operatorname{diag}{\{s_{k}^{2}+a_{k}^{2}-2\lambda s_{k}+\lambda^{2}\}}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{n(n-1)/2}(s_{k}^{2}+a_{k}^{2}-2\lambda s_{k}+\lambda^{2}),

where we denoted
s_{k}\in\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(C_{1}\right)}
,
r_{k}\in\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(C_{2}\right)}
. Solving
w(\lambda)=0
we see

\lambda_{k}=s_{k}\pm ir_{k}.

(3.89)

where
s_{k}\geqslant 0
and hence

\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(\hat{c}_{\phi}\right)}=\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(C_{3}\right)}\cup\{s_{k}\pm ir_{k}:s_{k}\geqslant 0,r_{k}\in\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(C_{2}\right)}\}.

(3.90)

Then we see
\lambda_{k}\geqslant 0
is satisfied if and only if
r_{k}
is imaginary,
r_{k}=it_{k}
for
t_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
s_{k}\pm ir_{k}=s_{k}\pm t_{k}\geqslant 0
, or if
s_{k}\geqslant|r_{k}|
; hence

must be positive semidefinite, as stated. Finally,
\phi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{coCP}}(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
is covariant if and only if
\phi=\tau\circ\psi
for
\psi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
conjugate covariant by Theorem
2
. Theorem
7
then shows

must be positive semidefinite and of a form (
3.86
); this immediately yields
C_{11},C_{22}\geqslant 0
,
C_{12}=C_{21}^{*}
and
C_{33}\geqslant 0
; via (
3.82
) then

. For the remaining blocks, compute

\displaystyle w(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\det{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}C_{11}-\lambda I&C_{21}^{*}\\
C_{21}&C_{22}-\lambda I\end{array}\right)}

\displaystyle=\det{\left[(C_{11}-\lambda I)(C_{22}-\lambda I)-C_{21}^{*}C_{21}\right]}

\displaystyle=\det{\operatorname{diag}{\{(s_{k}-\lambda)(r_{k}-\lambda)-|w_{k}|^{2}\}}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{n(n-1)/2}\left(s_{k}r_{k}-|w_{k}|^{2}-(s_{k}+r_{k})\lambda+\lambda^{2}\right)

for

,
r_{k}\in\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(C_{22}\right)}
and

. Solving

we obtain

\lambda_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\left(s_{k}+r_{k}\pm\sqrt{(s_{k}-r_{k})^{2}+4|w_{k}|^{2}}\right).

(3.93)

Since

we have
\lambda_{k}\geqslant 0
if and only if
|w_{k}|\geqslant\sqrt{s_{k}r_{k}}
, or
|C_{12}|^{2}\geqslant C_{11}C_{22}
.
∎

4.
Decomposable quantum dynamics

In this section we highlight some applications of general theory of decomposable covariant maps, as elaborated earlier, in the description of quantum dynamics.

Let
B_{1}(H)
be the Banach space of trace class operators on Hilbert space
H
. A positive semidefinite operator
\rho\in B_{1}(H)
of trace
1
will be called a
density operator
(or
density matrix
when
H\simeq\mathbb{C}^{n}
). Density operators are traditionally used to express (mixed) states of physical systems in quantum mechanics. Their time dependence is then characterized in terms of a
quantum evolution family
, or
quantum dynamical map
, i.e. a time-parametrized family
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
of positive, trace preserving linear maps on
B_{1}(H)
such that
\rho_{t}=\Lambda_{t}(\rho_{0})
for some initial
\rho_{0}
. The most common approach further requires
\Lambda_{t}
to be not only positive, but rather CP for all
t\geqslant 0

[
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

An evolution is called
divisible
if and only if there exists a map
V_{t,s}
on
B_{1}(H)
such that

\Lambda_{t}=V_{t,s}\circ\Lambda_{s}\quad\text{for all }t\geqslant 0,\,s\in[0,t];

(4.1)

V_{t,s}
is then called a
propagator
. Further, evolution is
P-divisible
when
V_{t,s}
is positive and
CP-divisible
, or
Markovian
, when
V_{t,s}
is CP, for all
t
,
s
. Recently, a new class of the so-called
D-divisible
evolution families on
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, generalizing Markovian ones, was proposed in
[
11
]
. The defining property is that
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is D-divisible (or decomposably divisible) if it is divisible and its propagator
V_{t,s}
is
decomposable
for all
t,s
. In particular it was shown that D-visibility of trace preserving families satisfying a time-local Master Equation is totally characterized in terms of an associated
generator
. In subsection
4.2
below we elaborate more on a D-divisible case and provide necessary and sufficient conditions for covariance of such families.

4.1.
Some general remarks

Recall that
B(H)
is (isometrically isomorphic to) the
dual
of
B_{1}(H)
, with the duality pairing given by the trace, i.e. for any bounded linear functional
f\in B_{1}(H)^{*}
there exists a unique
A\in B(H)
such that
f(T)=\operatorname{tr}{AT}
for each
T\in B_{1}(H)
. Since
\mathscr{A}\simeq B(H_{0})
for some Hilbert space
H_{0}
, the predual
\mathscr{A}_{*}
is isomorphic to
B_{1}(H_{0})
. Therefore, for any linear map
\Phi:B_{1}(H)\to\mathscr{A}_{*}\simeq B_{1}(H_{0})
, its
dual
map
\Phi^{\prime}:\mathscr{A}\simeq B(H_{0})\to B(H)
can be, with a slight abuse of terminology, defined by property
\operatorname{tr}{A\Phi(T)}=\operatorname{tr}{\Phi^{\prime}(A)T}
for
T\in B_{1}(H)
and
A\in B(H_{0})\simeq\mathscr{A}
being (a representation of) a linear functional on
\mathscr{A}_{*}
.

Now, let
\phi:\mathscr{A}\to B(H)
be
U
-covariant, so that

\phi\circ g=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\phi

(4.2)

for
U:\mathcal{G}\to B(H)
and all
g\in\mathcal{G}
, as in previous sections. Assume that a map
\phi_{*}:B_{1}(H)\to\mathscr{A}_{*}
is the predual of
\phi
. One easily shows

Proposition 5
.

A linear map
\phi:\mathscr{A}\to B(H)
is
U
-covariant if and only if its predual
\phi_{*}
is covariant in the sense that

\phi_{*}\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}^{-1}=g_{*}\circ\phi_{*}

(4.3)

for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
, where
g_{*}:\mathscr{A}_{*}\to\mathscr{A}_{*}
is the predual of
g
.

Let
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be norm-continuous quantum evolution family as introduced at the beginning of this section and assume it is subject to the Cauchy problem

\frac{d}{dt}\Lambda_{t}=L_{t}\circ\Lambda_{t},\quad\Lambda_{0}=\mathrm{id},

(4.4)

i.e. the
Master Equation
, for a bounded generator
L_{t}
such that
\operatorname{tr}{L_{t}(\rho)}=0
for all
\rho\in B_{1}(H)
; the derivative is to be understood in norm topology sense. Its dual
\Phi_{t}:B(H)\to B(H)
can be then quickly checked to satisfy a dual Cauchy problem

\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{t}=\Phi_{t}\circ\mathcal{L}_{t},\quad\Phi_{0}=\mathrm{id},

(4.5)

for
\mathcal{L}_{t}
a map dual to
L_{t}
. Family
(\Phi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is sometimes referred to as the
Heisenberg picture
of the evolution, while
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is the
Schrödinger picture

[
23
]
. Since the general theory earlier was established for maps on C*-algebras, we will stay with the dual picture and consider rather map
\Phi_{t}
than
\Lambda_{t}
.

Let us take
\mathscr{A}=B(H)
. The requirement for
\Phi_{t}
to satisfy (
4.5
) has an immediate consequence on a particular form of covariance:

Proposition 6
.

Let
(\Phi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
,
\Phi_{t}:B(H)\to B(H)
, be subject to the ODE (
4.5
). If
\Phi_{t}
is
U
-covariant then necessarily
g(A)=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}(A)
for all
A\in B(H)
.

Proof.

Note that, by
U
-covariance property and boundedness of
\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}\circ\mathcal{L}_{t}\circ g

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\Phi_{t+\epsilon}\circ g-\Phi_{t}\circ g\right)

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\Phi_{t+\epsilon}-\Phi_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\Phi_{t}\circ\mathcal{L}_{t},

so
\Phi_{t}\circ\mathcal{L}_{t}
is also
U
-covariant. Integrating both sides of ODE (
4.5
) we obtain a linear Volterra equation

\Phi_{t}=\mathrm{id}+\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{s}\circ L_{s}^{\prime}\,ds.

(4.7)

After composing it with
g
and applying (
4.6
)

\Phi_{t}\circ g=g+\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{s}\circ L_{s}^{\prime}\,ds.

(4.8)

On the other hand, by covariance we have

\Phi_{t}\circ g=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\Phi_{t}=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\left(\mathrm{id}+\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{s}\circ L_{s}^{\prime}\,ds\right).

(4.9)

Equating (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) we see it must hold
g=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}
.
∎

In consequence the only well-posed
U
-covariance condition for family
(\Phi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
must be of a form

\Phi_{t}\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\Phi_{t},

(4.10)

satisfied for all
t\geqslant 0
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
; we will be only considering such covariance from now on until the end of this section. Since
g_{*}=\mathrm{Ad}^{-1}_{U(g)}
by direct check, Proposition
5
also shows that
\Phi_{t}
is
U
-covariant if and only if its predual
\Lambda_{t}
is
U^{-1}
-covariant, so all of the results can be easily translated to the dynamical maps itself by switching the representation
U
to its inverse.

Recall that
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is divisible if and only if
\Lambda_{t}=V_{t,s}\circ\Lambda_{s}
for trace preserving propagator
V_{t,s}:B_{1}(H)\to B_{1}(H)
. The dual family
(\Phi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is then also
divisible
, i.e.

\Phi_{t}=\Phi_{s}\circ\mathcal{V}_{t,s},\quad 0\leqslant s\leqslant t

(4.11)

for
\mathcal{V}_{t,s}
dual to
V_{t,s}
. Likewise, all forms of X-divisibility mentioned so far (for X = P, CP or D) apply to
(\Phi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
as well, i.e. the dual family is, say, D-divisible if and only if
\mathcal{V}_{t,s}
is decomposable, which happens if and only if
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is itself D-divisible and so on.

Proposition 7
.

Let
(\Phi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be divisible. Then the following statements hold:

(1)

(\Phi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is
U
-covariant if and only if
\operatorname{im}{[\mathcal{V}_{t,s},\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}]}\subseteq\ker{\Phi_{s}}
for all
t\geqslant 0
,
s\leqslant t
;

(2)

If
(\Phi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is invertible then it is
U
-covariant if and only if
(\mathcal{V}_{t,s})_{s\leqslant t}
is
U
-covariant.

Proof.

Divisibility condition (
4.11
) combined with (
4.10
) implies

\Phi_{s}\circ\mathcal{V}_{t,s}\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\Phi_{s}\circ\mathcal{V}_{t,s}=\Phi_{s}\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\mathcal{V}_{t,s},

(4.12)

so for any
A\in B(H)
we have

\Phi_{s}\circ\left(\mathcal{V}_{t,s}\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}-\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\mathcal{V}_{t,s}\right)(A)=0,

(4.13)

i.e.
[\mathcal{V}_{t,s},\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}](A)\in\ker{\Phi_{s}}
for all
s\leqslant t
, proving the first statement. For the second one, when
\Phi_{t}
is invertible it has a trivial kernel so (
4.13
) is satisfied if and only if
\ker{[\mathcal{V}_{t,s},\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}]}
is the whole
B(H)
, i.e. when
\mathcal{V}_{t,s}
and
\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}
commute; this however is the
U
-covariance condition for
\mathcal{V}_{t,s}
.
∎

An analogous result regarding the generator itself can also be formulated (we omit the proof as it goes along the same lines):

Proposition 8
.

Let
(\Phi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
satisfy ODE (
4.5
). The following statements hold:

(1)

If
\Phi_{t}
is
U
-covariant then
\operatorname{im}{[\mathcal{L}_{t},\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}]}\subseteq\ker{\Phi_{t}}
;

(2)

If
\Phi_{t}
is invertible then it is
U
-covariant if and only if
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is
U
-covariant.

Naively speaking, covariance of the generator seems as the most natural and intuitive measure of covariance of the dynamics, despite being not a necessary condition but only a sufficient one. However, a very frequently appearing scenario in applications is that the generator which defines equation of motion for density operators (or equivalently observables in Heisenberg picture) is
regular
, i.e.
t\mapsto\|L_{t}\|
is uniformly bounded over (sufficiently large interval in)
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. In such case the dynamical maps are always invertible and so the
U
-covariance of the generator and dynamics itself become equivalent.

Proposition 9
.

Let the quantum evolution family
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
satisfy ODE (
4.4
) with
L_{t}
regular. Then,
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is U-covariant if and only if
L_{t}
is U-covariant for all
t\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5
the
U
-covariance of
\Lambda_{t}
is equivalent to
U^{-1}
-covariance of
\Phi_{t}
. Regularity of
L_{t}
then implies regularity of
\mathcal{L}_{t}
and invertibility of
\Phi_{t}
in consequence, so Proposition
8
invoked for representation
U^{-1}
shows
\mathcal{L}_{t}
must be
U^{-1}
-covariant, i.e.
L_{t}
is
U
-covariant.
∎

4.2.
D-divisible families on
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})

In
[
11
]
it was shown that the quantum evolution family
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
on
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
which satisfies an ODE (
4.4
) with
L_{t}
regular is D-divisible, i.e. its propagator
V_{t,s}
is a
decomposable
map, if and only if the generator admits a structure

L_{t}=-i[H_{t},\cdot]+\varphi_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\{\varphi_{t}^{\prime}(I),\cdot\}

(4.14)

for Hermitian
H_{t}
and decomposable map
\varphi_{t}
. In time-independent case equation (
4.14
) defines the most general form of the generator of trace preserving (or unital, in dual version) decomposable semigroup on
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, which extends seminal result by V. Gorini, A. Kossakowski, E. C. G. Sudarshan
[
26
]
and G. Lindblad
[
27
]
for completely positive semigroups. We remark here that characterization (
4.14
) applies only to finite-dimensional C*-algebras and it is currently unknown if it admits a direct generalization to the case of any
B(H)
. Such characterization, if true, would provide a substantial extension of
[
27
]
and would contribute to more general theory of dynamical semigroups beyond complete positivity.

For start, let us take the generator constant,
L_{t}=L
, so the evolution family is simply a semigroup,
(e^{tL})_{t\geqslant 0}
. The following theorem can be considered a generalization of result by Holevo
[
9
]
towards decomposable case, applied for finite-dimensional C*-algebras:

Proposition 10
.

Generator
L
of decomposable and trace preserving semigroup on
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is
U
-covariant if and only if it admits a form (
4.14
) (with time dependence suppressed) for Hermitian matrix
H\in U(\mathcal{G})^{\prime}
and a
U
-covariant map
\varphi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{D}}(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
.

Proof.

Only the “
\Rightarrow
” direction needs to be shown. Let
L
be of a form (
4.14
) for Hermitian
H
and decomposable
\phi
and assume it is
U
-covariant. Define yet again the projection
\mathsf{P}_{U}:B(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))\to\mathcal{C}_{U}
onto subspace
\mathcal{C}_{U}
of
U
-covariant maps,

\mathsf{P}_{U}(\phi)=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\phi\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}^{-1}\,d\mu(g),

(4.15)

i.e. as in the proof of Theorem
1
. Naturally, we have
\mathsf{P}_{U}(L)=L
and, after some easy algebra,

\mathsf{P}_{U}(L)(a)=-i[\hat{H},a]+\psi(a)-\frac{1}{2}\{\psi^{\prime}(I),a\}

(4.16)

for

\hat{H}=\int_{\mathcal{G}}U(g)HU(g)^{*}\,d\mu(g),\quad\psi=\mathsf{P}_{U}(\phi).

(4.17)

Map
\psi
is then
U
-covariant and still decomposable by Theorem
1
. Moreover, for any
g\in\mathcal{G}
we have

\displaystyle U(g)\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}}U(g)U(h)HU(h)^{*}\,d\mu(h)=\int_{\mathcal{G}}U(g)U(g^{-1}h)HU(g^{-1}h)^{*}\,d\mu(h)

(4.18)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}}U(h)HU(h)U(g)\,d\mu(h)=\hat{H}U(g),

by translation invariance of the integral, i.e.
U(g)
commutes with
\hat{H}
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
and the proof is finished.
∎

We conclude this section with the following

Theorem 9
.

Let
U:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a unitary representation. A semigroup
(e^{tL})_{t\geqslant 0}
of decomposable trace preserving maps on
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is
U
-covariant if and only if there exists a Hermitian matrix
H\in U(\mathcal{G})^{\prime}
and a
U
-covariant map
\varphi\in\operatorname{\mathsf{D}}(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
such that
L
is of a form (
4.14
) (with time suppressed).

Proof.

Since all maps of a form
e^{tL}
are invertible, trivial application of Propositions
9
and
10
yields the claim.
∎

As a final result of the article, we present a generalization of Theorem
9
which covers the case of regular time-dependent generators.

Theorem 10
.

Let
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be a D-divisible family of trace preserving maps on
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
satisfying ODE (
4.4
) with
L_{t}
regular and let
U:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a unitary representation. Then,
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is
U
-covariant if and only if there exist a Hermitian matrix
H_{t}\in U(\mathcal{G})^{\prime}
and a
U
-covariant map
\varphi_{t}\in\operatorname{\mathsf{D}}(\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
such that
L_{t}
admits a form (
4.14
).

Proof.

When the family is D-divisible,
[
11
]
implies
L_{t}
must be of a form (
4.14
) for Hermitian
H_{t}
and decomposable
\varphi_{t}
. Regularity of
L_{t}
implies, via Proposition
9
, that
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is
U
-covariant if and only if
L_{t}
is
U
-covariant for all
t\geqslant 0
. From Proposition
10
it then follows that
H_{t}\in U(\mathcal{G})^{\prime}
and
\varphi_{t}
is
U
-covariant. Conversely, when
L_{t}
admits the claimed form then it is automatically
U
-covariant, so Proposition
9
yields the family
(\Lambda_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
must be
U
-covariant, and D-divisible by
[
11
]
.
∎

5.
Summary

We presented some extensions of a well-established theory of covariant completely positive maps on C*-algebras onto the broader class of decomposable maps. Analogously to the initial seminal work of
[
7
]
, a characterization of such was given in a form of a dilation theorem. A possible quantum physics application was highlighted by providing necessary and sufficient conditions for covariance of decomposable quantum dynamical maps admitting regular, time-dependent generators. It would be possibly of interest to verify applicability of Theorems
9
and
10
to maps on a general C*-algebra
B(H)
. This however is presently unclear due to the lack of characterization of decomposable, norm-continuous contraction semigroups in infinite dimension in the spirit of Lindblad
[
27
]
, as we remarked earlier.

Appendix A
Mathematical supplement

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{U}
denote the set of all
U
-covariant bounded linear maps from
\mathscr{A}
to
B(H)
. Then,
\mathcal{C}_{U}
is a closed linear subspace in
B(\mathscr{A},B(H))
.

Proof.

\mathcal{C}_{U}
is clearly a subspace since for any two
U
-covariant maps
\phi_{1}
,
\phi_{2}
their linear combination is also
U
-covariant. Let then
\phi\in B(\mathscr{A},B(H))
be a limit point of
\mathcal{C}_{U}
, i.e. let there exist a sequence
(\phi_{n})
,
\phi_{n}\neq\phi
, of
U
-covariant maps such that
\phi_{n}\to\phi
in norm. Mapping
\phi\mapsto\mathrm{Ad}_{U}\circ\phi
is an isometry in
B(\mathscr{A},B(H))
for any unitary
U
, so we have

\|\phi_{n}-\phi\|=\|\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ(\phi_{n}-\phi)\|=\|\phi_{n}\circ g-\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\phi\|

(A.1)

for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
, and
(\phi_{n}\circ g)
converges to
\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\phi
in norm. On the other hand,

\|\phi_{n}\circ g-\phi\circ g\|\leqslant\|\phi_{n}-\phi\|\cdot\|g(\cdot)\|\to 0

(A.2)

hence
(\phi_{n}\circ g)
converges to
\phi\circ g
. This implies
\phi\circ g=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\phi
by uniqueness of a limit, i.e.
\phi\in\mathcal{C}_{U}
and subspace
\mathcal{C}_{U}
is closed in norm topology.
∎

Theorem 12
.

Let
C
denote either
\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
or
\operatorname{\mathsf{coCP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
. Then we have
\mathsf{P}_{U}(C)\subset C
.

Proof.

Let
\{\psi_{g}\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\subset\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
be a family of CP maps s.t.
g\mapsto\psi_{g}
is measurable and uniformly bounded, i.e.
\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\|\psi_{g}\|<\infty
. Then, by Stinespring’s dilation theorem
[
8
]
there exists a family
\{K_{g}\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
of Hilbert spaces, family
\{V_{g}\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
of bounded linear operators
V_{g}:H\to K_{g}
and a family
\{\pi_{g}\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
of *-homomorphisms
\pi_{g}:\mathscr{A}\to B(K_{g})
such that
\psi_{g}(a)=V_{g}^{*}\pi_{g}(a)V_{g}
for every
a\in\mathscr{A}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}
. Define a Hilbert space

K=\int_{\mathcal{G}}^{\oplus}K_{g}\,d\mu(g),

(A.3)

i.e. a direct integral of family
\{K_{g}\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
, as well as linear operator
V:H\to K
by

Vx=\int_{\mathcal{G}}^{\oplus}V_{g}x\,d\mu(g),\quad x\in H,

(A.4)

and a *-automorphism
\pi:\mathscr{A}\to B(K)
by

\pi(a)=\int_{\mathcal{G}}^{\oplus}\pi_{g}(a)\,d\mu(g),\quad a\in\mathscr{A}.

(A.5)

Let us denote inner products in spaces
H
,
K_{g}
and
K
by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{H}
,
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{g}
and
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{K}
, respectively. Then, for any
x,y\in H
,
a\in\mathscr{A}
we have

\displaystyle\left\langle x,V^{*}\pi(a)Vy\right\rangle_{H}

\displaystyle=\left\langle Vx,\pi(a)Vy\right\rangle_{K}=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\left\langle V_{g}x,\pi_{g}(a)V_{g}y\right\rangle_{g}\,d\mu(g)

(A.6)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\left\langle x,V_{g}^{*}\pi_{g}(a)V_{g}y\right\rangle_{H}\,d\mu(g)=\left\langle x,\int_{\mathcal{G}}V_{g}^{*}\pi_{g}(a)V_{g}\,d\mu(g)\,y\right\rangle_{H}

\displaystyle=\left\langle x,\int_{\mathcal{G}}\psi_{g}(a)\,d\mu(g)\,y\right\rangle_{H}

which yields equality of operators

V^{*}\pi(a)V=\int_{\mathcal{G}}\psi_{g}(a)\,d\mu(g)

(A.7)

satisfied for all
a\in\mathscr{A}
. This means the integral
\int_{\mathcal{G}}\psi_{g}\,d\mu(g)
of a completely positive family
\{\psi_{g}\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
admits a Stinespring form, i.e. is also CP. Now, putting

\psi_{g}(a)=\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}\circ\phi\circ g^{-1}

(A.8)

where
\phi
is CP yields
\mathsf{P}_{U}(\phi)\in\operatorname{\mathsf{CP}}(\mathscr{A},H)
. The remaining coCP case then follows along the same lines after replacing a *-homomorphism with a *-antihomomorphism in Stinespring’s form.
∎

Theorem 13
.

Let
A,B\in B(X)
for a Banach space
X
and let
f,g:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
. If there exist open sets
U_{A}
,
U_{B}
containing spectra of
A
and
B
, respectively, such that
f:U_{A}\to\mathbb{C}
and
g:U_{B}\to\mathbb{C}
are holomorphic and injective, then
[f(A),g(B)]=0
if and only if
[A,B]=0
.

Proof.

The proof method is inspired by the one employed by Wermuth in
[
28
]
where a special case of
f
,
g
being operator exponentials was considered. We note that a virtually identical result, albeit achieved with different methods, was quite recently shown by Paliogiannis
[
29
]
.

Note that since
f
and
g
are assumed holomorphic, power series expansions of
f(A)
and
g(B)
commute term by term so only “
\Rightarrow
” direction needs to be addressed. Let then
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)}\subset U_{A}
,
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(B\right)}\subset U_{B}
with
U_{A}
,
U_{B}
open and bounded (we may assume so since both
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)}
,
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(B\right)}
are compact). By open mapping theorem, both images
f(U_{A})
,
g(U_{B})
are open subsets of
\mathbb{C}
, while continuity implies that
f(\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)})
and
f(\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(B\right)})
are also compact. By injectivity assumption, both
f
and
g
are then invertible, i.e. there exist functions

f^{-1}:f(U_{A})\to U_{A},\quad g^{-1}:g(U_{B})\to U_{B},

(A.9)

which are also necessarily holomorphic on
f(U_{A})
and
f(U_{B})

[
30
, Theorem 10.33]
. Since
f(\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)})
is closed and embedded in an open set
f(U_{A})
, it may be covered by another open set
D_{A,\epsilon}
defined as a union of all open balls, each one of radius
\epsilon
, covering all possible points of
f(\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)})
,

D_{A,\epsilon}=\bigcup_{z\in f(\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)})}K_{z,\epsilon}

(A.10)

where such
\epsilon>0
is taken that
f(\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)})\subset D_{A,\epsilon}\subset f(U_{A})
. Then, compactness of
f(\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)})
infers existence of a finite subcover
E_{A,\epsilon}
of
f(\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)})
,

E_{A,\epsilon}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}K_{z_{j},\epsilon}

(A.11)

where
\{z_{j}:1\leqslant j\leqslant n\}\subset f(\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)})
, such that
f(\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)})\subset E_{A,\epsilon}\subset D_{A.\epsilon}
. Then,
\overline{E_{A,\epsilon}}
is compact and its boundary
\gamma_{A}
is a Jordan curve, i.e. closed, continuous and with no self-intersections. By holomorphic functional calculus, operator
A=(f^{-1}\circ f)(A)
can be expressed as an integral

A=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma_{A}}f^{-1}(z)\left(z-f(A)\right)^{-1}dz.

(A.12)

By Runge’s theorem
[
30
, Theorem 13.9]
, a holomorphic function
f^{-1}
may be approximated by a sequence
(r_{n})
of rational functions,
r_{n}(z)=p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
for
p_{n}
,
q_{n}
polynomials, converging to
f^{-1}
uniformly on
\overline{E_{A,\epsilon}}
and it is always possible to choose polynomials
q_{n}
that have no zeros in
\overline{E_{A,\epsilon}}
. Functions
r_{n}
are therefore holomorphic in this set and in particular, this implies that the operator

q_{n}(f(A))^{-1}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\gamma_{A}}\frac{1}{q_{n}(z)}\left(z-f(A)\right)^{-1}dz

(A.13)

exists and is bounded, i.e.
r_{n}(f(A))=p_{n}(f(A))q_{n}(f(A))^{-1}
is well-defined and
\|r_{n}(f(A))-A\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. The same reasoning may be then carried out for
B
and function
g
, resulting in existence of a sequence
(s_{n})
of rational functions, with poles outside a compact set containing
g(\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(B\right)})
, and converging to
g^{-1}
uniformly on this set; in result, a sequence
(s_{n}(g(B)))
exists in
B(X)
and converges to
B
in norm. From this it is easy to see that

\Delta_{n}=r_{n}(f(A))s_{n}(g(B))-s_{n}(g(B))r_{n}(f(A))\to[A,B]

(A.14)

in norm when

Theorem 14
.

Let

for a Banach space

. If spectrum of

.

Proof.

When

–congruence free, the function
f(z)=e^{z}
is injective on an open cover

(A.15)

of
\operatorname{\sigma}{\left(A\right)}
, for

[

]
. Hence, the claim follows from Theorem

Below we supply the Reader with an alternative proof which employs Wermuth’s result in a more direct manner by invoking a simple observation of “shrinking”, or “rescaling” property of bounded subsets of complex plane, stated as a Lemma
4
:

Lemma 4
.

For every bounded nonempty set
U\subset\mathbb{C}
and every
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
there exists such
\tau>0
small enough, that set
tU=\{tw:w\in U\}
is
z
–congruence free for every
t\in(0,\tau)
.

Proof.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{C}
be nonempty and bounded and let

\Delta=\sup_{z_{1},z_{2}\in U}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|}

(A.16)

be its
diameter
. If
\Delta>0
, define
\tau=\frac{|z|}{\Delta}
. Then, for every
t\in(0,\tau)
and every pair
z_{1},z_{2}\in U
we have
|z_{1}-z_{2}|\leqslant\Delta
which yields

t|z_{1}-z_{2}|<\tau\Delta=|z|.

(A.17)

Let
tU=\{tz:z\in U\}
and take any
w_{1},w_{2}\in tU
. As
w_{1}=tz_{1}
and
w_{2}=tz_{2}
for some
z_{1},z_{2}\in U
, equation (
A.17
) implies

|w_{1}-w_{2}|=t|z_{1}-z_{2}|<|z|,

(A.18)

which automatically results in

w_{1}-w_{2}\neq kz,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}

(A.19)

for every
w_{1},w_{2}\in tU
, i.e.
w_{1}\neq w_{2}\,(\mathrm{mod}\,z)
and
tU
is
z
–congruence free for any
t\in(0,\tau)
. On the other hand, if
\Delta=0
, i.e.
U=\{z_{0}\}
is a singleton, then set
tU
is automatically
z
–congruence free by definition; then, one can take any
\tau\in(0,\infty)
.
∎

(alternative).

Proposition 11
.

For each
g\in\mathcal{G}
,

(1)

matrix
\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)
is unitary and admits a block structure

\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}K_{1}(g)&-K_{2}(g)&0\\
K_{2}(g)&K_{1}(g)&0\\
0&0&I_{n}\end{array}\right),

(A.20)

where
I_{n}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is an identity matrix and
K_{1}(g),K_{2}(g)\in\mathrm{Diag}_{n(n-1)/2}(\mathbb{C})
;

(2)

matrix
\hat{\beta}_{U}(g)
is unitary and admits a block diagonal structure

\hat{\beta}_{U}(g)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}R_{1}(g)&0&0\\
0&R_{1}(g)&0\\
0&0&R_{2}(g)\end{array}\right),

(A.21)

where
R_{1}(g)\in\mathrm{Diag}_{n(n-1)}(\mathbb{C})
and
R_{2}(g)\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is unitary and symmetric.

Proof.

Due to mutual orthogonality of subspaces
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
\mathrm{Asym}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
\mathrm{Diag}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, matrix
\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)
will admit a natural block structure,
\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)=[M_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{3}
, where every block
M_{ij}
corresponds to a certain pair of subspaces: for example,
M_{11}=[\left\langle F_{i}^{\mathrm{s}},{F_{j}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{\prime}\right\rangle_{2}]
,
M_{12}=[\left\langle F_{i}^{\mathrm{s}},{F_{i}^{\mathrm{a}}}^{\prime}\right\rangle_{2}]
and so on. Matrices
F_{i}^{\mathrm{s}}
and
F_{i}^{\mathrm{a}}
come in pairs labeled by the same index
i
running from
1
up to
\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)
, which is uniquely determined by the same pair
(\mu,\nu)
of indices appearing in (
2.2
). One easily checks that map
\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}^{-1}
leaves
F_{i}^{\mathrm{d}}
unchanged, and so blocks
M_{31}
,
M_{32}
,
M_{13}
and
M_{23}
vanish by orthogonality of subspaces and
M_{33}=I_{n}
. The remaining non-zero blocks
M_{ij}
are determined by simply calculating appropriate inner products. With simple algebra involving property
E_{ij}E_{kl}=\delta_{jk}E_{il}
one verifies

\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}^{-1}(F_{i}^{\mathrm{s}})=\cos{(x_{\mu}-x_{\nu})}F_{i}^{\mathrm{s}}+\sin{(x_{\mu}-x_{\nu})}F_{i}^{\mathrm{a}},

(A.22a)

\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}^{-1}(F_{i}^{\mathrm{a}})=-\sin{(x_{\mu}-x_{\nu})}F_{i}^{\mathrm{s}}+\cos{(x_{\mu}-x_{\nu})}F_{i}^{\mathrm{a}},

(A.22b)

i.e.
\mathrm{Ad}_{U(g)}^{-1}
is effectively the rotation by angle
x_{\mu}-x_{\nu}
. This naturally leads to

\left\langle F_{i}^{\mathrm{s}},{F_{j}^{\mathrm{s}}}^{\prime}\right\rangle_{2}=\left\langle F_{i}^{\mathrm{a}},{F_{j}^{\mathrm{a}}}^{\prime}\right\rangle_{2}=\cos{(x_{\mu}-x_{\nu})}\delta_{ij},

(A.23b)

here with
j

M_{11}=M_{22}=K_{1}(g)=\cos{\Delta},\quad M_{21}=-M_{12}=K_{2}(g)=\sin{\Delta}

(A.24)

where

,
1\leqslant k\leqslant\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)

\mu(k)

\nu(k)

\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)

A.20

For

F_{j}^{\prime}=U(g)F_{j}U(g)

\hat{\beta}_{U}(g)=[M_{ij}]

M_{ij}
of the same size as in
\hat{\alpha}_{U}(g)
. Note that a mapping
