Self-assembled polymeric nanosheet-anchored nanofiber membrane for emulsion separation
Authors: He Li, Keyi Che, Fei Yin, Jingfang Liu, Pan Jiang, Liu Liu, Zhaoling Li, Bin Ding, Jianyong Yu, Shichao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-performance water-oil separation materials are essential to reduce environmental pollution and improve resource utilization towards achieving carbon neutrality; however, most present separation materials are limited by the monotonous pore structure and uncontrollable interface, resulting in low separation efficiency and flux. Here, we develop a facile electrohydrodynamic strategy, based on the electrospray-sheeting technique, to create self-assembled polymeric nanosheet-anchored nanofiber membranes, for high-performance emulsion separation. By manipulating relative humidity to fabricate stem-like nanofibers and regulating their topological structure to control the resulting microelectric field, polymer-charged droplets are induced to deform and then innovatively assemble into leaf-like nanosheets anchored on the nanofibers, thereby constructing the membrane with hierarchical stem-leaf-like nanoarchitecture. The nanoarchitecture with superwettable pore channels enables the obtained membrane to simultaneously achieve striking separation efficiency (>99.32%) and flux (up to 4179 L m−2 h−1) for water-in-oil emulsions, all attributed to its supercapillary and demulsification-interception effects. This work may provide a distinctive platform for the development of advanced nanomaterials for filtration and purification.Subject terms: Polymers, Polymer synthesis, Pollution remediation

Introduction

As an unintended product that is common and ineluctable in productive activities such as oil extraction and industrial processing, oil-water mixtures have posed severe threats to the ecological environment and human health1–3. The main types of oil-water mixtures include free, dispersed, and emulsified states, which are distinguished by the size of the dispersed phase4. Particularly problematic are the separation of emulsions containing dispersed droplets in the submicron range, which exhibit extraordinary stability due to surfactant-mediated interfacial effects, making the removal of water from emulsified mixtures to obtain high-purity oil a pressing issue5,6. At present, the methods for separating oil-water mixtures involve centrifugation7, gas flotation8, adsorption9,10, flocculation11, membrane separation12,13, etc. Among them, the membrane separation method based on selective permeation has become a high-quality solution for oil-water emulsion separation, which is attributed to high separation efficiency, simple operation, low carbon emission, no secondary pollution, and so on14–16. In recent years, microfiltration and ultrafiltration membranes, characterized by small pore sizes, can effectively intercept emulsified droplets based on the size-sieving effect, making them successfully applied in oil-water emulsion separation17–20. However, most existing separation membranes still face serious structural deficiencies, such as low porosity and poor pore connectivity, resulting in limited separation flux of the membranes21,22. Therefore, the development of facile and efficient oil-water emulsion separation membranes is imperative.

Ascribed to small pore size and high porosity, nanofiber membranes are widely regarded as a transformative platform for oil-water emulsion separation23–26. Electrospinning technology, due to its powerful process scalability and adjustable material structure, has become the mainstream nanofiber manufacturing method27–29. Recently, the construction of selective separation surfaces could be achieved by creating rough structures or altering interfacial chemical properties of the membranes, and their effectiveness has been demonstrated in emulsion separation30–32. However, the acquisition of stable selective separation surfaces in electrospun nanofiber membranes mainly relies on subsequent modifications, such as coating33,34, chemical crosslinking26, hydrothermal synthesis35,36, etc., which suffer from the drawbacks of complex processes and potential damage to the pore structure. Additionally, the pore size and porosity of the separation membranes are important factors affecting their permeation fluxes37–39. Although increasing the pore size of the separation membrane can improve permeation flux for the continuous phase, it may reduce interception efficiency for emulsion droplets, leading to a decrease in separation performance. The improvement in the application performance of separation membranes is actually the result of the collaborative actions of the surface and pore structures40. Nevertheless, achieving synchronous progress in enhancing both the efficiency and flux of separation membranes has been a major challenge that urgently needs to address41. Hence, the rational design and optimization of the surface and pore structures of oil-water emulsion separation membranes are crucial.

Herein, we demonstrate a powerful electrohydrodynamic methodology, using electrospray-sheeting, to fabricate self-assembled polymeric nanosheet-anchored nanofiber membranes with hierarchical stem-leaf-like architecture, which realizes synchronous regulation of surface and pore structures. The fundamental principle of the design is to regulate the phase separation-deposition behavior of spinning jets, forming stem-like polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) nanofibers with low surface potential, which induces the controllable deformation and assembly of charged polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) droplets into leaf-like nanosheets anchored on the nanofibers. Our research indicates that the anchoring of nanosheets on nanofibers enables the formation of a rough structure with sharp edges, and constructs capillary pore channels to facilitate the rapid transport of oil, which endows the resultant membrane with superoleophilicity and underoil superhydrophobicity, thereby achieving high separation efficiency and flux for water-in-oil emulsions. More importantly, the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes also exhibit remarkable mechanical properties and long-term durability. The striking performance of the membranes all originated from the synergistic engineering of surface and pore structures, which presents an effective solution for emulsion separation and opens up broader prospects for the development of efficient separation materials.

Results

Assembly and architectures of nanosheet/nanofiber membranes

We fabricated the high-performance membranes by precisely designing the hierarchical nanoarchitecture formed by nanosheets anchoring nanofibers, which could effectively enhance the oil transport performance and improve their demulsification capabilities, thereby achieving high separation efficiency and flux. The hierarchical, architectured membranes were created involving the preparation of stem-like PVDF rough nanofibers and the assembly of leaf-like PMMA nanosheets, using the humidity-induced electrospinning and electrospray-sheeting techniques, as shown in Fig. 1a. Variations in relative humidity (RH) altered the diffusion kinetics between the solvent in the jet and nonsolvent (H2O) from the spinning environment. Under elevated RH, water molecules accumulated quickly, triggering the jets to rapidly enter the unstable region where phase separation occurred. As the solvent evaporated, the polymer-rich phase underwent accelerated solidification, forming stem-like rough nanofibers. The rough structure could provide more anchor points, making them suitable for employing as scaffolds. By adjusting the topological structure of nanofibers, precise control of surface potential was achieved. This led to an alteration in the microelectric field, thereby influencing the electric field force exerted on the charged PMMA droplets and ultimately driving the droplets to evolve into nanosheets. The resultant hierarchical organization achieved the coupling of stem-like rough nanofibers and leaf-like nanosheets. When PMMA nanosheets were deposited on nanofibers, the residual solvent inside caused a slight softening of the nanofibers, which promoted the migration and entanglement of polymer chains at the interface. Subsequently, as the solvent evaporated, the entanglement was locked42, thus forming physical bonding points (Supplementary Figs. 1–3). Moreover, the spectroscopic data confirmed the successful anchoring of PMMA nanosheets onto PVDF nanofibers (Supplementary Fig. 4), and the hydrogen bonding between the carbonyl groups of PMMA and the hydrogen atoms of PVDF provided an additional interaction that further enhanced the bonding between nanosheets and nanofibers43,44.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images confirm that the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes consist of stem-like rough nanofibers and leaf-like polymer nanosheets, forming a structure similar to the stems and numerous leaves of plants (Fig. 1b–e and Supplementary Fig. 5). The assembly of nanosheets on the scaffold nanofibers constructed capillary pore channels that were conducive to oil transport. More importantly, the pronounced structural scale disparity between nanosheets and micron-sized emulsion droplets led to the manifestation of sharp-edged interfacial characteristics in the nanosheets, which promoted demulsification during separation (Fig. 1f). The leaf-like nanosheet also exhibited a nanoscale roughness, constructing a hierarchical architecture together with the rough nanofiber and the mastoid structure formed between them, which contributed to improving the wetting performance of the resultant membrane in accordance with the Cassie’s law45. Taking advantage of the hydrophobic groups (-CF2-, -CH2-), together with the hierarchical rough structure, the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes exhibited rapid wetting of oil in air and anti-water adhesion under oil (Fig. 1g). Such special surface and pore structures of nanosheet/nanofiber membranes endowed them with high separation efficiency and flux for water-in-oil emulsion, which were significantly higher than those of conventional nanofiber membranes (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 6). The separation process of nanosheet/nanofiber membranes could be carried out by a filtration system (Fig. 1i), the emulsions could be separated by a membrane module connected with peristaltic pump and pressure pump, which could meet the facile and efficient requirements of emulsion treatment and offer robust application potential in environmental protection and resource recycling.

Design and nanostructures of stem-like nanofibers and leaf-like nanosheets

The hierarchical architectured membranes were composed of stem-like rough nanofibers and leaf-like nanosheets, and this collaborative engineering provided the resultant materials with striking selective separation capabilities. The formation of stem-like PVDF rough fibers was highly dependent on the RH during the electrospinning process, where elevated RH governed accelerated charge dissipation in the jet and amplified axial instabilities of the jet. At the same time, RH-dependent solvent-nonsolvent exchange dynamics dictated a difference in solidification rates between the inside and outside of the jet, resulting in the formation of stem-like rough fibers under high RHs (Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Fig. 7). However, excessive RH caused accelerated diffusion of solvent and nonsolvent, enabling faster phase separation and solidification rate of the jets, which led to insufficient stretching of the jet and larger fiber diameters (Supplementary Fig. 8). To further illustrate the formation mechanism of rough fibers, the ternary phase diagram of the PVDF/N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF)/H2O system was drawn via cloud point titration method to clarify the phase separation behavior of PVDF solutions. The cloud point showed a linear relationship (Supplementary Fig. 9), indicating that the bimodal curve could be substituted with the cloud point curve. The bimodal curve could divide the ternary phase diagram into two regions, involving the homogeneous region and the unstable region (Fig. 2d). During the stretching process of the jets, the evaporation of DMF absorbed a large amount of heat, and water molecules condensed into droplets on the surface of the jets in high RH levels. With the displacement of solvent and nonsolvent, the jets rapidly underwent phase separation through the bimodal curve, forming a nonsolvent-rich phase and a polymer-rich phase. Eventually, as the jets solidified, the polymer-rich phase evolved into a rough surface, which could provide more anchoring points for nanosheets (Fig. 2e).

Regulating the surface potential of the stem-like nanofibers could facilitate the creation of leaf-like polymeric nanosheets that were distinct from inorganic nanosheets with layered structures. The surface potential varied with the pore size and thickness of the scaffold layer46, which could be achieved by controlling the deposition behavior of the jet. We analyzed the pore sizes of nanofiber membranes fabricated from different spinning durations, as presented in Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 10. With the extension of duration, the pore size of the obtained membrane decreased, mainly due to the increase in packing density filling the gaps between the nanofibers. Meanwhile, longer deposition time may lead to a more uniform distribution of nanofibers, reducing the presence of large pores. The surface potential of aluminum foil and the nanofiber membranes prepared at different spinning durations were characterized using an electrostatic fieldmeter, as depicted in Fig. 2g. As the duration got longer, the thickness of the fiber membrane increased, while the surface potential increased from 0.3 to 1.42 kV. The dense accumulation of the nanofibers resulted in small contact areas with the conductive drum, making it difficult for the charges on the nanofiber surface to dissipate, thereby displaying high surface potential. To customize the structure of nanomaterials, aluminum foil and the nanofiber membranes prepared at different spinning durations were used as collectors, and electrospray-sheeting was carried out. The SEM images of the obtained nanomaterials are shown in Fig. 2h, i and Supplementary Figs. 11–13, the collectors with lower surface potential, such as aluminum foil and thin nanofiber membranes, were more effective in inducing charged PMMA droplets to form leaf-like nanosheets. We had deeply considered the forming principle of nanosheets (Fig. 2j, k). The interlocking nanofibers formed a closed loop, which could be equivalent to a resistor-capacitor circuit47,48. The larger the pore, the faster the discharge rate of the capacitor, resulting in a smaller surface potential of the collector. The lower surface potential of the collector increased electric field strength between the needle and collector, which enhanced the electric field force on PMMA solution droplets. Simultaneously, as the solvent inside the PMMA droplets evaporated, the polymer formed a thin shell at the air-droplet interface, and the pressure inside the shell was lower than the outside, causing the droplet to tend to collapse inward. Under the stretching force of a larger electric field, the solvent and polymer phases migrated, and the droplets tended to form flat nanosheets after stretching and solidification.

Properties of nanosheet/nanofiber membranes

To guarantee the application stability of oil-water separation materials, they should have sufficient mechanical strength to withstand the pressure during liquid filtration without being damaged. As demonstrated in Fig. 3a, the nanosheet/nanofiber membrane exhibited a tensile strength of 12.9 MPa with a strain of ~31%. The stretching process of the resultant membrane mainly contained the deformation and alignment of the stem-like nanofibers. The membrane first passed through the elastic deformation zone, where the nanofibers gradually aligned along the stretching direction. As the stretching continued, the nanofibers aligned further, and stress concentration points appeared, ultimately leading to fiber fracture. The nanosheet/nanofiber membrane could maintain >85% of the maximum tensile stress at 1000 tensile cycles, and the plastic deformation was ~15% (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, the thermodynamic curves of the nanosheet/nanofiber membrane indicated its good thermal stability, which also served as indirect evidence for the anchoring of nanosheets on the nanofibers (Supplementary Fig. 14). In addition, the emulsion separation performance of the nanosheet/nanofiber membrane was primarily affected by their pore and surface structures. Therefore, we characterized the mean pore sizes and porosities of the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes and the PVDF nanofiber membranes without assembled nanosheets. As shown in Fig. 3c, after the assembly of the leaf-like nanosheets, the gaps between the scaffold nanofibers were effectively filled, which reduced the mean pore size of the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes by 20.9% compared to the control group, while the porosity remained >80%. The surface profilometry scan of the resultant membrane revealed that the PMMA nanosheets were uniformly adhered to the stem-like nanofibers, endowing the membrane with surface roughness of ~4.9 μm, which was crucial for improving its wettability (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Figs. 15, 16).

To investigate the selective separation performance of the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes, we analyzed their wetting behavior in different environments. As illustrated in Fig. 3e, the oil droplets were transported due to the Laplace pressure difference effect caused by the distribution of nanosheets, and then permeated through the pore channels between the nanosheets and nanofibers. Meanwhile, when an oleophilic surface came into contact with water droplets underoil, the stable oil film, formed by the oil on the membrane surface, could block the penetration of water droplets, endowing the material with underoil hydrophobic properties. By assembling leaf-like nanosheets on stem-like nanofibers to construct a mastoid rough structure, cavities were formed that stored a large amount of oil, preventing water from infiltrating. The nanosheet/nanofiber membranes exhibited superoleophilicity and superhydrophobicity under various oils (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Figs. 17, 18), which could be attributed to the combined effect of low surface energy and hierarchical rough structure. In practical applications, the anti-fouling performance was crucial, mainly correlated to the underoil water adhesion force. As shown in Fig. 3g, when the water droplet was compressed by an external force and separated from the surface of the resultant membrane, the shape of the water droplet remained almost unchanged, indicating low water adhesion. The calculated water adhesion forces under various oils were <1 μN, primarily attributed to their ability to absorb oil and form a stable oil layer, preventing direct contact between water and the membranes. Thanks to the superwetting surface, combined with small pores and connected pore channels, the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes had shown great potential in the field of emulsion separation. Emulsified droplets could be intercepted by small pores and underwent emulsification-coalescence under the action of the mastoid structure, thereby achieving efficient separation (Fig. 3h). In addition, the interconnected pore channels could rapidly transport liquids, as confirmed by its 3D model (Fig. 3i).

Functionality of nanosheet/nanofiber membranes

In considering the small pore size, high porosity, interconnected pore channels, and superwetting surface, the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes were well-suited for applications in filtration and separation. Most existing separation materials for oil-water emulsions encountered a trade-off between efficiency and flux, while our membranes with hierarchical stem-leaf-like architecture provided a facile and efficient solution. We had prepared surfactant-free emulsions (SFEs) and surfactant-stabilized emulsions (SSEs) based on different types of oil. A dead-end filtration device was used to separate water-in-oil emulsions, and the whole process was driven by a pressure of 10 kPa. The nanosheet/nanofiber membrane was placed between two glass tubes, and the milky emulsion became clear and transparent after passing through the membrane. Optical microscopy images of the emulsion revealed that it contained a large number of sub-micrometer-scale water droplets, while the filtrate was essentially free of emulsified droplets, which was consistent with the results from Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) testing (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 19). As expected, the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes could achieve demulsification, coalescence, and interception of emulsified droplets, demonstrating remarkable separation performance for water-in-oil emulsion. This powerful ability of removing emulsified water droplets was attributed to the rough surface and pore structure of the resultant membranes.

SFEs and SSEs were prepared using different oils of n-hexadecane, petroleum ether, soybean oil, and dichloroethane. Fluxes of 1699, 4179, 934, and 3191 L m−2 h−1 were obtained for H2O/n-hexadecane, H2O/petroleum ether, H2O/soybean oil, and H2O/dichloroethane, respectively, with the efficiencies >99% (Fig. 4b). Compared with SFEs, SSEs were more difficult to separate due to their higher stability. As shown in Fig. 4c, the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes presented fluxes were 1143, 3353, 368, and 1444 L m−2 h−1 for Span 80/H2O/n-hexadecane, Span 80/H2O/petroleum ether, Span 80/H2O/soybean oil, and Span 80/H2O/dichloroethane, respectively, while the separation efficiencies did not decrease. For different types of oil, the separation fluxes were different, mainly attributed to the viscosities of oils and the interactions between oil with the membrane surface and pores. High-viscosity oils spread more slowly on the surface of the nanosheet/nanofiber membrane than low-viscosity oils, and they were predisposed to form thickened boundary layers within membrane pore channels, leading to lower flux49,50. Additionally, high-viscosity oils could hinder the coalescence of emulsified water droplets, thereby increasing the possibility of droplet intrusion into pore channels and causing pore blockage51. As demonstrated in Fig. 4d and Supplementary Table 1, our material exhibited high separation efficiency and high flux compared with other separation materials reported in the literature52–55. To evaluate the reusability of the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes, we performed cyclic separation to the water-in-n-hexadecane SFE. As the separation time increased, the flux decreased, possibly due to the formation of a filter cake on the surface of the membranes. Interestingly, the flux recovered immediately after simple cleaning with ethanol and drying, and there was no significant change in separation efficiency (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 20), indicating striking antifouling performance of the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes, which was ideal for long-term use. Based on the developed high-performance separation membranes, we have built a continuous separation device, in which the milky emulsion became transparent after being filtered through the membrane (Fig. 4f). By further expanding the scale of the relevant equipment, it is expected to achieve large-scale processing of emulsions, which holds significant importance for environmental protection and resource recycling.

Discussion

In summary, we reported a robust strategy, based on electrospray-sheeting technique, to assemble polymeric nanosheets on nanofibers, enabling the fabrication of high-performance emulsion separation membranes with hierarchical stem-leaf-like nanoarchitecture. By governing the phase separation and solidification rate of jets through RH modulation, the stem-like nanofibers were fabricated. Building on this, topological structure manipulation of nanofibers controlled their microelectric field to achieve the precise assembly of leaf-like nanosheets. This nanostructured engineering strategy has made progress in optimizing liquid-solid interface interactions and liquid transport pathways, making it an ideal option for efficient selective separation. Our work demonstrated that the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes could effectively intercept, demulsify, and coalesce emulsified droplets while allowing oil phase to pass through rapidly, showing superwettability, high separation efficiency, and high flux. Additionally, the nanosheet/nanofiber membranes exhibited prominent mechanical properties and recyclability, which were crucial for long-term use. We believe that our work will provide valuable insights into the design of advanced fibrous nanomaterials and present new opportunities for their application in the field of filtration and purification.

Methods

Materials and chemicals

PVDF powder (Mw = 1000000) and petroleum ether (AR) were supplied by Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd. PMMA powder (Mw = 500000) and Span 80 (CP) were bought from Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. DMF (≥99.5%) and n-hexadecane (AR) were obtained from Aladdin Industrial Co., Ltd. 1,2-Dichloroethane (AR) was acquired from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Soybean oil was provided by Bide Pharmatech Co., Ltd. Deionized water was provided by Millipore system. Aluminum foil was bought from Shanghai Hugong Electromechanical Equipment Co., Ltd. Nonwoven fabric was purchased from Hunan Xinlong Nonwoven Co., Ltd.

Fabrication of stem-like PVDF nanofibers

The PVDF precursor solution with a concentration of 18 wt% was prepared by mixing PVDF powders with DMF and continuously stirring for 8 h at 60 °C. The fabrication of nanofiber materials was conducted utilizing an electrospinning machine (DXES, SOF Nanotechnology Co., Ltd., China). The electrospinning parameters were configured with voltage of 18 kV, a distance of 20 cm between the nozzle and the conductive drum, and the polymer solution was fed at a rate of 1 mL h−1. The whole spinning process was carried out at a temperature of 23 ± 2 °C, while the RH values were 20 ± 2%, 35 ± 2%, 50 ± 2%, and 65 ± 2%, respectively.

Construction of leaf-like PMMA nanosheets

PMMA powders were dissolved in DMF and stirred at room temperature for 6 h to prepare a dilute solution with a concentration of 2 wt% for electrospray-sheeting. The supplied voltage was set at 25 kV, the receiving distance was kept at 20 cm, and the polymer solution was fed at a rate of 1 mL h−1. The creation of the nanosheet/nanofiber membrane was performed in an environment maintained at a temperature of 23 ± 2 °C and a humidity of 50 ± 2%. Aluminum foil and nanofiber membranes obtained at various spinning durations were served as the collectors to facilitate the formation of nanosheets.

Preparation of water-in-oil emulsions

The oil was mixed with deionized water in a volume ratio of 9:1, and the mixture was subsequently subjected to ultrasonic treatment at room temperature for an hour to obtain SFE. For the preparation of SSE, 0.2 mg L−1 of Span 80 was individually dissolved in various oils, such as n-hexane, soybean oil, petroleum ether, and 1,2-dichloroethane. A suitable amount of deionized water was then added, keeping the oil/water ratio at 99:1. After an hour of ultrasonication at room temperature, the solution was transformed into an emulsion. The resultant emulsions were stable and showed no stratification or precipitation within 24 h.

Characterizations

The surface potentials of Aluminum foil and nanofiber membranes obtained at various spinning durations were measured by the electrostatic fieldmeter (FMX-004, Simco-Ion, An ITW Company). The microscopic structures were observed by SEM (SU5000, Hitachi Co., Ltd., Japan). The roughnesses of the nanofibers and materials were characterized by an Atomic Force Microscope (Dimension Icon, Bruker Inc., Germany), and an optical profilometer (UP2000, RTEC-Instruments Inc., USA), respectively. A capillary flow porometer was used to study the pore structures (CFP-1100AX, Porous Materials Inc., USA). A tensile tester was employed to analyze the mechanical properties (ST200C, Suzhou Rising Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd., China). The tensile samples were measured with a sample width of 3 mm, a tensile speed of 10 mm/min, and an initial clamping length of 10 mm. The contact angle of the fibrous materials was characterized using a goniometer (SL 200B, Kino, USA). The water content in oil was detected by a Karl Fischer titrator (831KF Coulometer, Metrohm, Switzerland).
