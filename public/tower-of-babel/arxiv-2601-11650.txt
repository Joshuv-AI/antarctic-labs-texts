Large Language Model Agent for User-friendly Chemical Process Simulations
Jingkang Liang, Niklas Groll, Gürkan Sin

Abstract
Modern process simulators enable detailed process design, simulation, and optimization; however, constructing and interpreting simulations is time-consuming and requires expert knowledge. This limits early exploration by inexperienced users. To address this, a large language model (LLM) agent is integrated with AVEVA Process Simulation (APS) via Model Context Protocol (MCP), allowing natural language interaction with rigorous process simulations. An MCP server toolset enables the LLM to communicate programmatically with APS using Python, allowing it to execute complex simulation tasks from plain-language instructions. Two water-methanol separation case studies assess the framework across different task complexities and interaction modes. The first shows the agent autonomously analyzing flowsheets, finding improvement opportunities, and iteratively optimizing, extracting data, and presenting results clearly. The framework benefits both educational purposes, by translating technical concepts and demonstrating workflows, and experienced practitioners by automating data extraction, speeding routine tasks, and supporting brainstorming. The second case study assesses autonomous flowsheet synthesis through both a step-by-step dialogue and a single prompt, demonstrating its potential for novices and experts alike. The step-by-step mode gives reliable, guided construction suitable for educational contexts; the single-prompt mode constructs fast baseline flowsheets for later refinement. While current limitations such as oversimplification, calculation errors, and technical hiccups mean expert oversight is still needed, the framework’s capabilities in analysis, optimization, and guided construction suggest LLM-based agents can become valuable collaborators.

Introduction

Chemical process simulators serve as an indispensable cornerstone of modern
chemical engineering practice, supporting engineers to design, analyze, and optimize
complex industrial systems. From petrochemical refineries to pharmaceutical
manufacturing plants

However, despite decades of technological advancement, traditional simulation

The current state of process simulation is characterized by steep learning

domain expertise that can take years to develop

junior engineers, interdisciplinary teams, and organizations with limited simulation
expertise, effectively restricting the accessibility of these powerful
mechanistic tools

Simultaneously, the artificial intelligence (AI) landscape has witnessed unprecedented
breakthroughs with the advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) and sophisticated AI
agent systems
[

. These technologies have already
demonstrated remarkable success across diverse domains, from autonomous software
development and scientific research to complex problem-solving tasks that require
intricate tool integration and coordination
[

The emergence of agentic AI systems, capable of planning, reasoning, and executing
multi-step workflows, presents a compelling opportunity to address the
accessibility challenges of conventional chemical process simulators
[
41
]
.

This combination of mature simulation technologies with cutting-edge AI
capabilities raises fundamental questions about the future of chemical
engineering practice: Can the sophisticated reasoning abilities of LLM-based agents
be effectively harnessed to assist (unexperienced) users in navigating complex simulation
workflows? How can we maintain the deterministic accuracy and safety standards
required in chemical engineering while leveraging the accessibility benefits of
conversational AI interfaces?

Addressing these questions, this work presents a novel framework that strategically
couple an LLM-based agent with a commercial chemical process simulation software
via a custom toolset. Rather than replacing traditional simulation methodologies
and workflows, our approach enhances them by providing an intelligent,
conversational interface that maintains the underlying rigor and reliability
that safety-critical applications demand. The proposed framework is designed to serve
as an assistant to experienced users in their daily tasks using the conventional
process simulator while simultaneously guiding new users through complex tasks of
analysing and synthesizing process simulation flowsheets, thereby broadening
access to advanced simulation capabilities without compromising engineering
standards.

Despite recent advancements, key challenges remain in developing trustworthy LLM-based
assistants that can truly support experienced chemical engineers in complex,
real-world tasks. Specifically, existing LLM-based technologies have not yet
addressed tasks requiring deep system understanding and decomposition into
interconnected subproblems, such as the analysis and synthesis of industrial production

domain expertise. Thus, a fundamental gap exists in transforming traditional,
human-centric simulation software into flexible, conversational tools powered by
LLMs, while preserving the rigor and reliability necessary for safety-critical
engineering applications.

To address this gap, our work aims to develop and evaluate an LLM agent framework,
built on MCP, that enables natural-language interactions with the commercial simulator
APS. This framework is designed to ensure safe, reliable, and reproducible
execution by integrating a curated toolset that bridges conversational AI with
deterministic process simulation. We demonstrate the framework’s practical utility
through two comprehensive case studies, showcasing its application in process
understanding, analysis, optimization, and flowsheet synthesis. Finally, we critically
assess the strengths and limitations of LLM-driven agents in supporting chemical
engineering workflows, highlighting how such systems can make commercial process
simulators more accessible and efficient without sacrificing the rigor required
for industrial use.

3
Methodology

Our proposed framework, as illustrated in Figure

1
, intends to replace the direct, manual
interaction between a user and a commercial process simulator with an
intelligent, conversational interface powered by an LLM. Instead of navigating
the simulator’s graphical user interface or writing custom scripts, the user communicates
with an MCP-enabled LLM agent, also called the MCP client, in natural language.
The LLM agent interprets the request, plans the necessary actions, and invokes a
curated toolset exposed by a dedicated MCP server. The server, in turn, interfaces
with the process simulator and executes all deterministic engineering operations.
This section details the framework’s architecture, covering the MCP client, the
MCP server, and the integration with the process simulator, including the
curated toolset.

Figure 1:
Schematic overview of the proposed framework including MCP.

3.1
LLM Agent and MCP Client

The LLM agent, running within a host application, serves as the primary
interface to the simulation environment. It interprets natural-language requests,
decomposes complex tasks, orchestrates tool calls, and maintains conversational
context. This contextual reasoning is essential for ensuring that the system correctly
interprets user intent and provides accurate, relevant responses. Additionally,
the LLM agent should detect and handle user errors and present results in clear,
user-friendly natural-language outputs.

Selecting a suitable host application, LLM agent, and underlying model
involves careful consideration of several criteria, including strong reasoning
capabilities for multi-step tool planning, reliable tool-use behavior, and a
sufficiently large context window to preserve conversation history. For this work,
we chose Claude Desktop, powered by Claude Sonnet 4.0 (Anthropic), as our LLM
agent
[
2
]
. This decision was motivated by its state-of-the-art performance
on reasoning tasks and tool-use benchmarks in technical and scientific domains
[
32
]
.

Within this architecture, Claude Desktop acts as the host application, instantiating
an MCP client responsible for protocol-level communication. The LLM interacts
with this client, instructing it to discover, connect to, and invoke tools exposed
by MCP servers. Because Claude Desktop includes native MCP client functionality,
this setup enables seamless integration with custom MCP servers without requiring
additional middleware or custom communication layers.

3.2
MCP Server

The MCP server acts as the intermediary layer between the LLM agent and the
process simulation software. By abstracting the simulator’s Python API into a clean,
high-level toolset with clearly defined inputs, outputs, and usage descriptions,
the MCP server enables the LLM to interact with the simulator without requiring model-specific
customization. Because the LLM only accesses these abstract tool definitions, rather
than the simulator’s internal API, the same toolset can be reused across
different LLMs or host applications, enabling seamless migration and future extension
of the framework.

Our implementation uses FastMCP, a modern Python framework for building
lightweight MCP servers
[
33
]
. FastMCP provides a
streamlined interface for defining tools, specifying request and response
schemas, and handling communication via standardized JSON-RPC messaging. Each
tool is implemented as a typed Python function annotated with metadata
describing its parameters, return types, and purpose. This design pattern simplifies
server development while providing automatic schema generation and robust tool discovery
for the host application.

The MCP server runs as a standalone process, and the host application (Claude Desktop)
connects to it through its built-in MCP client. Once connected, the LLM agent
can query available tools, invoke them with appropriate arguments, and receive
structured responses containing simulation data or execution results. This architecture
ensures a reliable, transparent communication pipeline, enabling the LLM agent to
coordinate simulation steps while deterministic computations are handled by the server
and the underlying simulator.

3.3
Process Simulator Integration

The connection between the MCP server and the employed process simulator, APS (version
2025), is established via the APS scripting interface, a comprehensive Python-based
API that provides programmatic access to all core simulation functionalities,
including flowsheet construction, parameter manipulation, and result extraction.
Our custom MCP server implementation wraps selected APS scripting interface functions
into a curated set of high-level tools. Each MCP tool encapsulates one or more
APS API calls, which are the building blocks of the tools, into a single
meaningful operation, aligned with common simulation steps a process engineer would
take.

The full toolset exposed by the MCP server is summarized in Table
1
.
It enables the MCP client to perform a wide range of simulation tasks, from simple
data queries and flowsheet inspections to the construction of new flowsheets by adding
models and connectors. The modular design of the MCP server architecture ensures
the toolset is easily extensible when additional functionality is needed, enabling
future expansion with capabilities such as automated sensitivity analysis,
optimization routines, or advanced convergence troubleshooting without requiring
changes to the underlying LLM agent configuration. Critically, the MCP server serves
as a semantic bridge, orchestrating high-level simulation logic through natural
language reasoning and tool selection. It translates conversational intent into rigorous
engineering actions, maintaining complete control over the deterministic
execution logic. At the same time, all actual computations remain firmly
grounded in the deterministic, physics-based solver algorithms provided by APS.

4
Case Study Applications

To demonstrate the practical application of our proposed agentic framework, we select
an existing simulation model from the APS examples library as a baseline and comparison
flowsheet. The simulation example "C1 - Water Methanol Separation" models the
binary separation of methanol and water via distillation, and is detailed in the
next section. Based on this example, we design two case studies to test our proposed
workflow. The case studies target common tasks of process simulation engineers, the
(1) analysis of simulation flowsheets and (2) synthesis of simulation flowsheets
detailed in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
.

4.1
Water Methanol Separation Process

Methanol serves as a key feedstock for the production of various chemicals and
fuels making its production and purification critical processes in the chemical
industry
[
9
]
. In particular, the mixture of water and
methanol is commonly encountered in industrial processes, for example in biodiesel
production, where methanol is used as a reactant and must be first recovered
from the aqueous phase to facilitate the downstream processing steps

[
10
]
.

For analyzing the process configuration, an understanding of the fundamental chemical
and physical principles is necessary. Although the boiling points of methanol (64.7
°C) and water (100 °C) at ambient pressure differ by approximately 35 °C, suggesting
a relatively straightforward separation, this binary system features some
interesting challenges. The water-methanol mixture exhibits non-ideal vapour-liquid
behaviour, characterized by positive deviations from Raoult’s law, requiring accurate
thermodynamic models, such as NRTL, to capture phase equilibrium correctly
[
27
]
.
While the system does not form an azeotrope, the relative volatility varies significantly
with composition, affecting separation efficiency across the column height. Due to
these non-ideal behaviors, careful consideration of design (e.g., number of stages)
and operational (e.g., reflux ratio) parameters is required to achieve desired
product purities while maintaining energy efficiency. These characteristics position
the binary water-methanol separation problem as an ideal benchmark system for process
analysis and optimization. The system is simple enough for rapid analysis by
domain experts, yet sophisticated enough to test the LLM agent’s understanding of
thermodynamic principles, equipment configuration, energy-efficiency considerations,
and process-optimization strategies in a realistic industrial context.

Figure 2:
Simulation flowsheet of APS example "C1 - Water Methanol Separation".

The process simulation flowsheet for the water-methanol separation, shown in
Figure
2
, is rather simple, consisting of a source stream, a distillation
column equipped with both a reboiler and a condenser, and sink modules for
collecting the bottom and distillate products. We define the feed stream as an equimolar
mixture of water and methanol, with a flow rate of 10 kg/s, at the specified operating
conditions (e.g., 80°C and 1 atm). Critical parameters that can be adjusted to
optimize the separation include reflux and reboiler ratios, the number of stages
in the distillation column, the feed stage location, and overall operating pressure
and temperature profiles. While the synthesis of the process requires careful
selection of appropriate modules, considering the underlying chemical and physical
principles to obtain a converged simulation flowsheet, the number of steps
required is limited, implying a fast flowsheet construction for experienced users.
Therefore, the binary water-methanol system provides a realistic yet manageable example
that captures the key challenges of synthesizing industrial separation processes.

4.2
Case Study 1: Analysis of the Simulation Flowsheet

In the first case study, we aim to evaluate the LLM agent’s ability to analyze,
interpret, and improve the existing water-methanol separation simulation modeled
in APS. The task is structured into two distinct steps. First, we ask for a
detailed summary of the modeled process, and second, we request a list of
potential process improvements. This case study should assess the system’s capacity
to comprehend complex process simulation models, communicate findings
effectively to the user, and offer expert-aligned recommendations for system improvements.

The initial user prompt asks for a detailed summary of the specified simulation file.
To fulfill this request, we expect the agent to connect to APS, open the
relevant simulation file, and gather data on all present modules and their connections.
Further, it should independently identify and extract the values of relevant key
process variables and parameters. After screening all extracted information, we expect
to obtain a structured, detailed natural-language description of the simulated
process. The summary should include the flowsheet structure and key process specifications,
such as feed composition, operating conditions, and energy demands.

In the second step, the user asks the agent how to improve the simulated process.
Based on the previously extracted information, the agent can decide to extract more
information from the simulation model if needed. After that, the agent should provide
a summary of multiple points that could improve the separation task based on different
metrics, such as separation efficiency, purity of the product streams, or energy
demand. Dependencies between different variables and operating conditions should
be clearly stated. Finally, a comprehensive list of suggested improvements should
be provided to the user.

4.3
Case Study 2: Synthesis of the Simulation Flowsheet

The second case study assesses the agent’s process synthesis capability with the
primary goal of constructing an entire simulation flowsheet in APS from
high
-
level user specifications. We evaluate and compare two construction modes based
on different user interactions and prompting: first, we test the assembly with a
stepwise dialogue between the user and the LLM agent; second, we provide the agent
with a single user prompt.

In the first approach, the user has almost full control, guiding the agent to assemble
the simulation flowsheet by incrementally requesting the addition of unit operations,
connection of units, and setting of parameter values. This approach mirrors
manual construction, but instead of manually clicking through the APS graphical user
interface, the user now provides the LLM agent with the required next step. This
way, the user can control that each step is conducted correctly and provide help,
or even take over a step, when the agent encounters problems. The user reduces the
complexity of each step by breaking the task into steps so that the agent requires
only a single tool call. Concurrently, the complexity can also be enhanced by combining
subtasks into the next user prompt. In this mode, we test the agent’s ability to
follow stepwise user instructions to construct the simulation flowsheet in APS using
the provided toolset.

In contrast, with the second mode, we evaluate the agent’s capacity to autonomously
construct a simulation flowsheet from a single user prompt. This mode requires
the agent to possess a sophisticated, abstract understanding of the problem statement
and to independently decompose the task into executable subtasks using the available
toolset. The process requires orchestrating multiple sequential tool calls with
progressively increasing information availability to fulfill this complex
objective. With this mode, we test the agent’s ability to independently plan and
execute a multi-step workflow to synthesize a complete process simulation
flowsheet based on concise high-level user specifications.

The suggestions labeled "Process Configuration Improvements" target the physical
design of the distillation column. Increasing the number of stages (S1) or
determining the optimal feed stage location (S2) are common design

However, it does not mention the higher investment cost for adding additional stages
to the column (=B). Lastly, it proposes introducing (S3) intermediate product

In the next list, the agent describes possible improvements to the separation by
adjusting process operating conditions. First, it clearly displays the influence
of the reflux ratio (S4) on energy consumption and separation efficiency.
However, we miss a mention about the influence of the reboiler boilup ratio,
which is set to its default value of 1.0 (=B). Adjusting the boilup ratio
alongside the recycle ratio can lead to substantial reductions in energy demand
while maintaining acceptable separation quality. Next, the agent explains how
the operating pressure influences the condenser temperature (S5). However, we
miss a comment that increasing the pressure can also affect separation
efficiency by reducing the relative volatilities of the components, and that the
stated heat recovery option is only valid if a suitable heat sink is provided (=B).
Conversely, the option to preheat the feed to achieve partial vaporization (S6)

The next group of suggestions concerns advanced process configurations that require
significant adjustments or the replacement of complete process equipment. The
first option describes the possibility of integrating the hot bottom stream to
preheat the feed stream (S7). This heat integration is a promising option, since
the separation would benefit from a preheated feed stream (=A) and using the hot
bottom stream would omit the need for additional external heating. The stated
amount of energy savings, however, has not been verified by simulation of the
considered system and must stem from the foundation model’s underlying training
data. Still, we believe that the predicted reduction is too high; reductions of about
2-5% in reboiler heating demand are more reasonable. Next, a double-effect
configuration with two columns at different pressures is suggested (S8). In general,
integrating heat between the condenser of the low-pressure column and the
reboiler of the high-pressure column can lead to substantial energy requirements
and is also theoretically possible for the water-methanol separation problem. However,
the additional investment cost for two distillation columns instead of one needs
to be considered and is not mentioned at all (=B). In addition, the exact energy
reductions can only be assessed with a rigorous simulation model of the system,
and we believe the stated 40% reduction is likely too high for practical applications.
Lastly, the agent suggests using reactive distillation rather than a simple distillation
column (=S9). Reactive distillation is a common example of process intensification,
combining two unit operations, a reactor and a distillation column, into one. However,
the problem statement includes only the separation of the two substances, not any
reaction step (=C). The agent also does not include any examples of when reactive
distillation with methanol and water could make sense, and does not specify any
possible reaction steps or additional third substances needed. Overall, the
agent presents multiple advanced ideas in this group that require additional
equipment. While only preheating the feed stream is easily implementable for inexperienced
users, the other options can help experienced users enhance their problem-solving
skills and develop creative solutions to practical problems.

In the last section, the agent provides multiple suggestions for modifying the distillation
column’s equipment. First, it reiterates that tray efficiency of 100% is
defined and suggests installing better trays to increase separation efficiency (S10).
In general, better trays improve efficiency, so fewer trays are needed overall.
However, since no trays can have an efficiency exceeding 100%, we can not improve
the separation with this idea, and the agent should better comment on the
reasonability of the efficiency assumption (=D). Further, the agent suggests implementing
an advanced control system using feedforward controllers to reject feed disturbances

column. However, the control design is not within the scope of the current preliminary
basic design phase (=C). For later, the feedforward variant is a promising
alternative for controlling distillation column operation, but its impact on product
quality and energy efficiency needs to be examined in detail. Overall, the suggested
equipment modifications focus more on design decisions made in subsequent detailed
engineering, but they remind the process designer to consider already points
that become relevant later.

The evaluation reveals that most suggestions fall into the upper-quality
categories, with three classified as "Very Good" and four as "Good but missing
details." Notably, three suggestions were deemed "Good but not relevant,"
indicating that, while these ideas represent valid process engineering concepts,
they do not directly address the specific requirements and constraints of the
water-methanol separation problem as currently defined in the simulation model. These
suggestions, though technically sound in a broader process engineering context, would
require significant modifications to the problem scope or additional process objectives
to become practically applicable. Critically, only one suggestion was classified
as "Potentially misleading," and importantly, none were categorized as completely
wrong. This highlights the agent’s baseline level of technical competence even
when its suggestions are imperfect. This distribution suggests that the agent’s knowledge
base encompasses sound engineering principles, but its ability to discriminate
between universally applicable concepts and context-specific solutions remains
an area requiring further development. The encouraging absence of fundamentally incorrect
suggestions underlines the robustness of the agent’s technical competence.

In its conclusion, the agent summarizes all suggestions and highlights the most
promising options. Two of the three suggestions categorized as "Very Good" (S6
and S7) are listed here, with only the recommendation to optimize the feed stage
location (S2) missing. Additionally, two suggestions from the "Good but missing details"
category are mentioned (S1 and S4). Indeed, we concur that these four
suggestions are among the most quickly testable in the simulation, demonstrating
that the agent possesses a reasonable ability to self-assess and prioritize its own
recommendations. This self-ranking capability is particularly valuable, as it helps
guide users, especially those with limited experience, toward the most actionable
improvements while filtering out more complex or context-dependent alternatives that
may require deeper expertise to evaluate properly. Further, the agent explains the
trade-off between energy and separation efficiency again and provides targets for
reducing energy demand and improving separation efficiency. Here, we expect more
detail, including the relation to possible increases in investment costs and
marking unverified numerical values as such. While we are sure that methanol purity
can exceed 95%, we do not believe that energy savings of 20-40% with heat
integration are possible. Stating these numbers as is requires a critical-thinking
user who carefully reviews the values before making conclusions.

In our prompt, we did not specify the user’s experience level, but the practical
function of the agent’s suggestions depends significantly on the user’s level of
expertise and the intended application context. For experienced process
engineers, even a potentially misleading suggestion can serve a valuable purpose
in the creative brainstorming process, offering alternative perspectives and
unconventional approaches that may inspire novel solutions or help identify
overlooked opportunities. We expect experienced engineers to possess the domain knowledge
necessary to critically evaluate each suggestion, separate valid concepts from
flawed assumptions, and adapt promising ideas to their specific process context.
The opposite applies to inexperienced users: the combination of high-quality and
potentially misleading suggestions can be daunting. However, knowing that the agent
generally maintains a baseline of technical competence can also encourage less experienced
users to approach different problems and explore novel solutions.

A notable limitation observed in the agent’s response is the common tendency of LLMs
to "overgenerate" information that may not be fully correct or entirely relevant
to the specific case at hand. This phenomenon occurs when no explicit
constraints are imposed on the agent’s output, for example, specifying a maximum
number of suggestions or defining a priority criterion. In the absence of such guidance,
the agent tends to produce long lists that prioritize breadth over depth, including
marginal or speculative ideas alongside high-confidence recommendations. While
this exhaustive approach may be beneficial for experienced users conducting
exploratory studies, it can overwhelm and confuse inexperienced users who lack the
judgment to prioritize among competing alternatives. The incorporation of
explicit output constraints into the prompt or a confidence score could yield
better, more focused suggestions, enhancing the practical applications of the
agent’s suggestions for users at all experience levels.

In response to Prompt 1.2, the agent demonstrates its capability to perform
iterative process optimization through systematic parameter adjustment and
performance evaluation. The user provides a precise target, achieving methanol
purity above 95 mol% in the distillate stream by adjusting the reflux ratio while
maintaining the current column configuration. The agent starts by querying the
current reflux ratio (1.0) and baseline methanol purity (84.2 mol%) to establish
a reference point for later comparison. It then proceeds with a methodical
approach by iteratively adjusting the reflux ratio and checking the targeted
methanol purity. First, the reflux ratio is increased to 1.5, yielding a methanol
purity of 96.3 mol%, exceeding the target. The agent recognizes that a lower
reflux ratio would reduce energy consumption while still meeting the
specification; thus, it continues to fine-tune the parameter by testing
intermediate values (1.2, 1.4, and finally 1.45). This iterative method,
involving 12 tool calls in total across multiple simulation runs, demonstrates the
agent’s ability to conduct systematic parametric studies without explicit user guidance
at each step. The final optimized reflux ratio of 1.45 achieves 95.1 mol% methanol
purity, closely meeting the target while minimizing energy consumption compared
to the initial 1.5 setting.

Following successful optimization, the agent autonomously generates a detailed performance
comparison table contrasting the original configuration (reflux ratio = 1.0) with
the optimized configuration (reflux ratio = 1.45), presenting the values for six
key process metrics. All numerical values are accurately extracted from the APS simulation,
demonstrating the agent’s reliability in data retrieval and presentation. The agent
also calculates the percentage changes between the baseline and optimized cases.

is reported as +10.9% instead of the correct +12.9%, and the distillate flow rate

if no specialized tool is available. This highlights how important tools are
when expecting an answer based on rigorous mathematical equations. In the absence
of such tools, the user must still verify any derived quantities to ensure
accuracy.

Beyond quantitative results, the agent provides a qualitative summary under "Key
Observations," highlighting the achievement of the target methanol purity, maintained
water quality in the bottoms stream, the energy trade-off associated with the
higher reflux ratio ( 6% increase in energy duties), and the impact on the total
distillate yield. This summary effectively communicates the main implications of

methanol typically commands a 10-15% price premium, which may offset the 6% increase

figures, particularly the price premium estimate, are not validated against
external sources or simulation data; they appear to originate from the LLM’s training

industrial contexts, they should be treated as approximations requiring verification
against actual market conditions and site-specific cost structures. We miss a
note stating this disclaimer. The final concluding statement that the optimisation
represents "an excellent operational improvement option" might be qualitatively correct.
However, it is generic and lacks the technical depth that a domain expert would probably
include.

Overall, the agent’s performance in this improvement task demonstrates several
notable strengths. It successfully executes a multi-step, iterative optimization
workflow, systematically adjusting parameters, monitoring objective values, and evaluating
the result without requiring user intervention at each step. The final summary
is well-structured and comprehensive, effectively communicating both technical results
and their practical implications. The agent’s ability to autonomously organize
findings into tables and categorize observations enhances usability for non-expert
audiences. However, the agent’s performance also reveals important limitations. Minor
calculation errors and unsupported economic claims highlight that while the agent
reliably extracts raw simulation data, derived quantities require independent
verification by domain experts. This improvement study demonstrates that the agent
can effectively serve as a collaborative optimisation assistant, accelerating
parametric studies and generating preliminary analyses, but it cannot yet replace
the critical judgment and validation rigour that experienced process engineers bring
to optimization tasks.

With Case Study 1, we illustrate the agent’s ability to autonomously analyze a
simulation flowsheet in APS, identify relevant process improvement options, and
implement a selected improvement suggestion to enhance methanol purity. The agent
effectively leverages its toolset to extract detailed simulation data and adjust
simulation variables in APS independently. Further, it interprets results and communicates
findings in an accessible manner for the user. While the agent demonstrates
strong capabilities in systematic analysis, broad and creative suggestions, and
systematic adjustments in APS, some limitations, such as oversimplification of
complex interactions and minor calculation errors, highlight the importance of expert
oversight. Nonetheless, the case study showcases the potential of LLM-based assistants
for early-stage analysis of simulation flowsheets, creative brainstorming of
possible process improvements, and automated, iterative adjustment of simulation
flowsheets.

In summary, Case Study 1 reveals multiple practical applications for LLM-based agents

access to advanced process simulation capabilities. For experienced engineers, the

preliminary optimization. Most valuably, the framework provides an effective
brainstorming partner that generates diverse improvement suggestions, explores unconventional
solution pathways, and identifies optimization opportunities that might
otherwise be overlooked. These capabilities position LLM-based agents as collaborative
tools that enhance rather than replace engineering expertise. Critically, this framework

6
Conclusion and Outlook

This study introduces a novel framework that integrates large language model (LLM)-based
agentic systems with the commercial AVEVA Process Simulation (APS) platform
through the open-standard Model Context Protocol (MCP), highlighting the potential
for autonomous flowsheet analysis and synthesis in chemical process simulations.
A comprehensive toolset deployed on an MCP server facilitates programmatic
communication with APS via its Python interface, allowing the LLM agent to execute
complex simulation tasks using natural language instructions. Two case studies, both
utilizing a water-methanol separation process as the test system, were conducted
to assess the agent’s performance across varying task complexities and
interaction modes. The results demonstrate the framework’s potential to enhance the
usability and efficiency of chemical process simulations.

Case study 1 illustrates the agent’s ability to autonomously analyze simulation flowsheets,
identify opportunities for process improvement, and implement optimization strategies
through iterative parameter adjustments. The agent effectively extracts relevant
data from thousands of variables, interprets complex thermodynamic relationships,
and presents findings in accessible formats. Although limitations such as oversimplification
of complex interactions and minor calculation errors highlight the ongoing necessity
for expert oversight, the framework demonstrates substantial practical value. In
educational settings, the agent reduces barriers to entry by translating
technical concepts into clear explanations and demonstrating systematic
workflows. For experienced practitioners, the framework automates data
extraction, accelerates daily routine tasks, and serves as a productive
brainstorming partner by generating diverse improvement suggestions and
identifying optimization opportunities that may otherwise be overlooked. These capabilities
position the LLM-based framework as a collaborative tool that augments, rather
than replaces, engineering expertise.

The second case study focused on the more complex task of autonomous flowsheet synthesis,
which requires creative problem-solving, multiple sequential decisions, and
complex troubleshooting capabilities that may exceed the current toolset’s
capabilities. Two construction approaches were evaluated: a step-by-step
dialogue mode and a single-prompt autonomous mode. In the step-by-step variant,
the agent exhibited reliable performance when provided with detailed, incremental
guidance, resulting in correct outcomes with only minor issues related to
presentation clarity. However, this approach revealed a key limitation: the necessity
for highly detailed user prompts, indicating that substantial domain knowledge
remains essential for effective model construction. The single-prompt variant,
which involved minimal user guidance, produced less consistent results. While
the agent was able to construct a functional flowsheet and demonstrated autonomy
in variable selection and configuration, several technical issues arose,
including attempts to set unspecified variables, premature parameter adjustments,
and minor redundancies in tool usage. Despite these challenges, the case study
highlights practical value for users with varying levels of experience. For novices,
the agent lowers initial barriers by autonomously managing routine setup tasks, enabling
the creation of functional flowsheets from simple problem descriptions. We
believe the step-by-step approach can be particularly advantageous in
educational contexts. In contrast, for experienced engineers, the framework can deliver
efficiency gains through automated repetitive tasks and rapid prototyping, with
the single-prompt mode enabling quick baseline flowsheet generation for further
refinement. These results indicate that the investigated framework is a promising
first step towards an intelligent assistant for process synthesis tasks; however,
critical user review of the agent’s implementation remains necessary.

Flowsheet synthesis is inherently complex, requiring careful management of
simulation context, model relationships, variable dependencies, and thermodynamic
considerations. The relatively simple water-methanol separation case flowsheet
was intentionally selected to clearly demonstrate the framework’s core
capabilities in process synthesis. Future work will scale this to more complex industrial
flowsheets, including multiple units, recycle streams, and heat integration, enabling
quantitative benchmarking against traditional methods (e.g., superstructure
optimization). As the size and complexity of the flowsheet increase, the above-mentioned
challenges are likely to intensify before becoming intractable for current single-agent
architectures. To address these issues, several architectural enhancements are proposed
and visualized in Figure
4
. First, transitioning to a multi-agent
system (MAS) in which specialized agents manage distinct tasks, such as
thermodynamic model selection, equipment configuration, or troubleshooting, could
focus reasoning and reduce context overload. Implementing dynamic context
filtering could enable agents to prioritize relevant data, mitigating information
overload. Structuring agents into organized workflows could facilitate
persistent storage of process information and design decisions, while incorporating
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) would ground agent reasoning in specific process-simulation
knowledge, addressing challenges in variable selection and operational modes. Expanding
the toolset to include automated sensitivity analysis, multi-objective
optimization, convergence diagnostics, and validation checks could enhance
reliability and minimize errors. Additionally, dedicated troubleshooting
capabilities would directly address common issues such as convergence failures
and specification errors. Last, coupling the agentic framework with advanced
algorithms, such as simulation-based optimization, superstructure optimization, and
process design under uncertainty, could further extend the framework’s
applicability to advanced design methodologies and establish the LLM assistant
as an effective copilot for process simulation engineers. Ultimately, rigorous
evaluation in more complex case studies, including those involving reactors,
recycle streams, heat integration, and systematic troubleshooting, is essential to
assess the framework’s scalability and robustness in industrial environments.

Figure 4:
Schematic overview of an extended framework including MAS and RAG.

In summary, the single LLM agent demonstrates significant potential to improve human-simulator interaction, particularly for tasks such as data extraction, analysis, stepwise implementation, and creative brainstorming. Their effectiveness, however, depends on well-structured workflows, clearly defined toolsets, and appropriate task decomposition strategies. The findings indicate that MASs with specialized roles, targeted information access, and domain-specific reasoning could be the most promising approach for reliable and scalable integration of LLM agents with commercial chemical process simulators. Although current limitations restrict fully autonomous operation in complex flowsheet synthesis, the demonstrated capabilities in analysis, brainstorming, iterative optimization, and guided construction suggest that these systems can serve as valuable assistants to process engineers in the near term. This framework fundamentally positions LLM agents as collaborative tools for engineers rather than autonomous replacements, highlighting the need for human oversight due to LLM limitations, such as hallucinations and unpredictable reasoning failures. The framework’s architecture inherently provides verification through the deterministic APS simulator, which enforces physical laws and thermodynamic constraints, offering automatic validation for novice users while enabling experienced engineers to apply their domain knowledge as an additional verification layer.
