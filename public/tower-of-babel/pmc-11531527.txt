TGF-β superfamily-induced transcriptional activation pathways establish the RAD52-dependent ALT machinery during malignant transformation of MPNSTs
Authors: Eunji Choi, Jungwoo Lee, HyoJu Kim, Young-Joon Kim, Seung Hyun Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
To study telomere maintenance mechanism (TMM) activation during malignant transformation, we compared neurofibroma (NF) and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST) in the same patient with type-1 neurofibromatosis (NF1), a total of 20 NF-MPNST pairs in 20 NF1 patients. These comparisons minimized genetic bias and contrasted only changes associated with malignant transformation, while subtracting changes that developed upon the transformation of normal cells to the benign tumor. TGF-β superfamily genes were found to activate the PAX and SOX transcription factors, leading to TMM activation. BMPER activates PAX6 through BMP2 and PAX7 through BMP4; BMP15 activates SOX14; and INHBC activates PAX9 and SOX14. The activated PAX and SOX genes sequentially establish the core architecture of the RAD52-dependent alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT). Specifically, PAX7 activates the recombinase (RAD52) and a negative regulator (SLX4IP). PAX6 and SOX14 activate positive regulators (BLM and BRCA2, respectively). PAX9 and SOX14 activate RAD9B and FEN1, which are responsible for the stability of homologous recombination intermediates and increase, together with RAD52, the telomere length. Telomere elongation achieved by the activation of PAX7 and PAX9 is associated with a poor prognosis. We demonstrated that TGF-β superfamily-induced transcriptional activation pathways activated the RAD52-dependent ALT during malignant transformation of MPNSTs.

Introduction

Eukaryotes with double-stranded linear DNA genomes experience the end-replication problems that accumulate over time, resulting in replicative and cellular senescence1. Telomeres are located at both ends of the double-stranded linear DNA genomes and protect against terminal DNA loss due to end-replication problems. Accumulated telomere attrition beyond a critical point renders cells unable to divide, an event called the Hayflick limit2. Type-1 neurofibromatosis (NF1) is an autosomal dominant disorder of neurofibromin 1 with a low incidence of approximately one in 3000 people3. The most fatal clinical manifestation of NF1 is malignant transformation from a preexisting neurofibroma (NF) to a malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST)4,5. The cumulative lifetime risk for malignant transformation in NF1 patients is 8–13%6. MPNSTs are rare sarcomas that account for approximately 5–10% of all soft tissue sarcomas. Among MPNSTs, 40–50% are associated with NF1, and 40–47% develop sporadically7. Notably, both telomerase activity and alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT) are observed in MPNSTs8,9, which suggests that MPNSTs may be an ideal research target to gain an insight into TMMs.

Two major TMMs have been studied, namely telomerase and ALT. While more than 85% of human cancers have increased telomerase activity, the remaining cancers lack telomerase activity but exhibit the ALT10. Telomerase has been reported to be a major TMM in carcinomas of epithelial origin, whereas ALT, which is based on homologous recombination (HR), has been reported to be a major TMM in sarcomas of mesenchymal origin. There are two main ALT mechanisms, RAD51- and RAD52-dependent ALT, which have distinct mechanisms and activation times. RAD51-dependent ALT promotes semiconservative HR (crossover HR) with high fidelity11, whereas RAD52-dependent ALT promotes conservative HR (noncrossover HR) as well as break-induced replication-related DNA synthesis12,13. This conservative DNA synthesis is observed only in the late phase of the cell cycle, G2/M14,15. These findings suggest that RAD51- dependent ALT and RAD52-dependent ALT are activated by separate signals. Many studies have shown that RAD51-dependent HR is involved in the DNA damage repair (DDR) response and that double strand breaks (DSBs) initiate RAD51-dependent ALT16,17. In contrast, RAD52-dependent ALT activation signals have yet to be elucidated.

To study the activation of TMMs during malignant transformation, we compared NF (benign) and MPNST (malignancy) within the same NF1 patient. Given that NF1-associated MPNST arises from a preexisting NF, each NF-MPNST pair in this model shared the same genetic and differentiation background. Comparison of NF-MPNST pairs allowed us to subtract or minimize genetic bias and to consider only changes associated with malignant transformation (Fig. 1B). A total of 20 NF-MPNST pairs from 20 NF1 patients were analyzed in this study. Whole transcriptome sequencing (WTS) was used to profile the transcriptional relationships and whole genome sequencing (WGS) was to measure telomere lengths18.

Dysregulated transcription factors may mediate aberrant gene expression associated with differentiation and apoptosis, leading to oncogenesis19. Therefore, we focused on genes encoding transcription factors, namely, the paired box (PAX) and SRY-related HMG-box (SOX) genes. Although they are known to be major transcription factors essential for neural differentiation during development (orthodox function)20–23, unexpected “neogenic” transcriptional events of the PAX24,25 and SOX genes26,27 have recently been reported to be responsible for the expression of oncogenes in various cancers (unorthodox function) (Fig. 1C).

TGF-β was originally identified as a component of sarcoma growth factor secreted from Moloney murine sarcoma virus-infected 3T3 cells28, and genes in the TGF-β superfamily have been reported to be involved in neural development29 and development of sarcomas30–33. Therefore, we hypothesized that genes of the TGF-β superfamily could indue transcriptional activation of the TMM through the PAX and SOX genes during malignant transformation and evaluated this hypothesis. As a result, we demonstrated that the TGF-β superfamily genes established the core architecture of RAD52-dependent ALT by inducing transcriptional activation through PAX and SOX transcription factors during the malignant transformation of NF1-associated MPNSTs.

Results

Patient demographics and tumor characteristics

Twenty NF-MPNST pairs from 20 NF1 patients were analyzed in this study. The mean latency to malignant transformation (mean age at diagnosis of an MPNST) was 36.55 years. Thirty percent of the patients were diagnosed with MPNSTs in the metastatic stage (IIIB and IV), the metastasis rate was 71.4%, and 50% of the patients died from MPNSTs, with a 5-year survival rate of 35%. In terms of tumor characteristics, 50% of the NF cases were cutaneous NFs, and 80% of MPNSTs were high grade. There were no significant differences in the tumor location (P = 0.625) or tumor size (P = 0.430) between NFs and MPNSTs, but there was a significant difference in the telomere length, indicating cumulative telomere attrition (P = 0.043) (Supplementary Information 2).

PAX and SOX genes are upregulated during malignant transformation

Both the PAX and SOX genes encode transcription factors, that play important roles in neural development20,21. In addition to their orthodox functions in normal neural development, several studies have described their unorthodox oncogenic functions in various cancers24,26,27 (Fig. 1C). Because NFs and MPNSTs are tumors of neuronal origin, we focused on the unorthodox oncogenic functions of PAX and SOX during malignant transformation of NFs to MPNSTs.

PAX6, PAX7, PAX9, and SOX14 transcriptionally activate 6 TMM genes

Through in-depth literature research, we selected 100 genes involved in the TMMs. The gene symbols and details are provided in Supplementary Information 1. We assessed the transcriptional activation of these 100 TMM-related genes in relation to the above mentioned 13 upregulated or downregulated PAX and SOX genes. A total of 1,300 gene pairs were evaluated. Transcriptional activation of GENE A by GENE B was defined as ΔGENE B having a significant positive correlation with both the ΔGENE A transcript level and the MPNST-GENE A transcript level according to LR. In addition, to distinguish only TMM-related transcriptional activation, the interaction effects of ΔGENE A and ΔGENE B on the MPNST-telomere length were analyzed by a generalized linear model (GLM). Overall, 3,900 P values for the two LRs and the GLM from 1,300 pairs were verified by controlling the false discovery rate (FDR) by the Benjamini-Hochberg (B-H) procedure (FDR = 0.1) (Supplementary Information 3).

The six TMM genes establish the core architecture of the RAD52-dependent ALT

We classified the six genes activated by the PAX and SOX genes according to their effects on the telomere length (associations between MPNST-transcript levels with the MPNST-telomere lengths) via factor analysis with varimax rotation and the number of characteristic groups via a scree plot. The six genes were classified into four groups: BRCA2 and BLM; FEN1 and RAD9B; SLX4IP; and RAD52. First, BRCA2 promotes RAD52-dependent ALT while antagonizing nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ)34–37. BLM, TOP3A, and RMI1 constitute the BTR complex, which acts as a positive regulator of the RAD52-dependent ALT by dissolution of HR intermediates, which results in noncrossover events with telomere elongation16,38. Thus, BRCA2 and BLM were classified as positive regulators. Second, FEN1 contributes to telomere stability in ALT-positive cells independently of the MRE11-RAD50-NBS (MRN) complex10. RAD9B, RAD1, and HUS1 constitute the 911 complex, which stabilizes HR intermediates in ALT39. FEN1 and RAD9B were classified to be involved in the stability of HR intermediates. Third, SLX4IP is responsible for resolution of telomeric HR intermediates and acts as a negative regulator of ALT by generating crossover events with aborted telomere elongation, thus antagonizing the BTR complex40–42. SLX4IP was classified as a negative regulator. Fourth, RAD52 is the recombinase in the RAD52-dependent ALT. The TGF-β superfamily activates the PAX6, PAX7, PAX9, and SOX14 genes through highly redundant pathways

The TGF-β superfamily comprises the TGF-β family, bone morphogenetic factors (BMPs), growth differentiation factors (GDFs), the activin/inhibin subfamily, and left–right determination factors. Thirty members of the TGF-β superfamily were analyzed for the activation of PAX6, PAX7, PAX9, and SOX14 in the same manner as mentioned above. The gene symbols and details of these 30 members of the TGF-β superfamily are provided in Supplementary Information 1. All 360 P values for the two LRs and the GLM from 120 pairs were verified to control for false-positives by the B-H procedure (FDR = 0.1). The activation of the PAX and SOX genes by genes of the TGF-β superfamily was found to be highly redundant. Twenty-four pairs between TGF-β superfamily genes and PAX or SOX were identified to be significant without controlling false-positives; these included three pairs for PAX6, six pairs for PAX7, nine pairs for PAX9, and six pairs for SOX14. Using a false-positive control with an FDR of 0.1, we identified five pairs between TGF-β superfamily genes and PAX or SOX. BMPER activated PAX6 and PAX7; BMP15 activated SOX14; and INHBC activated PAX9 and SOX14 (Supplementary Information 4, Fig. 2). The ΔBMP15 (P = 0.004) and ΔINHBC (P = 0.017) transcript levels were positively correlated with the MPNST-telomere length based on simple LRs, but none of them were independent according to multiple LR analyses (Table 2).

Although BMPER was cloned as a secreted glycoprotein and was shown to mediate endothelial cell differentiation by interacting with BMP2 and BMP443, BMP signaling pathways44,45 and BMPER46,47 have been associated with several cancers. BMPER has a completely different structure than BMPs. BMPs have TGF-β superfamily and TGF-β propeptide superfamily domains48, whereas BMPER has five tandem cysteine-rich von Willebrand C-like domains, a single von Willebrand D domain, and a trypsin inhibitor-like domain43. Tandem von Willebrand C-like domains are typical of BMP binding proteins. These findings suggest that BMPER transduces signals by interacting with BMPs rather than through direct interactions with receptors. Therefore, we evaluated the associations between BMPER and BMPs. BMP2 and BMP4 were associated with BMPER (Table 4, Fig. 2). Without controlling for false-positives, BMP2 activated PAX7 and BMP4 activated PAX6 and PAX7 (Supplementary Information 4). Discussion

Limitations of the research model

This study included only 20 patients. However, we designed a research model with a solution to overcome the small number of cases. We designed a genetic model that was conceptually similar to a subtractive cDNA library. A total of 20 NF (benign)-MPNST (malignant) pairs, arising from the same NF1 patients were compared. Since an NF1-associated MPNST arises from a preexisting NF, each NF-MPNST pair in this model shared the same genetic background and differentiation lineage. Therefore, comparisons of the NF-MPNST pairs allowed the subtraction of genetic and differentiation bias, and only changes associated with malignant transformation were considered (Fig. 1B). To distinguish between TMM-related and TMM-unrelated transcriptional activation, we analyzed the interaction effects on the telomere length and transcriptional activation for each pair. TMM-related transcriptional activation was considered when both transcriptional activation and interaction effects on the telomere length were significant after controlling for false-positives using the B-H procedure. These strategies helped ameliorate the limitations of the small sample size.

Additionally, quantitative validation of transcripts level through qPCR or protein expression level through immunoblotting was not performed for the genes evaluated in this study. Since our results were derived at the transcript level, they need to be further validated at the protein level. In particular, the effects of epigenetic modifications such as phosphorylation or SUMOylation (small ubiquitin-related modifier) on TMM activation should be evaluated.

PAX and SOX act as mediators of the TGF-β superfamily-induced transcriptional activation, which establishes RAD52-dependent ALT

The PAX and SOX genes are essential for neural development and organogenesis (orthodox functions), but they also have unorthodox functions in oncogenesis. There are common aspects between their orthodox and unorthodox functions at the molecular level. One of the key functions of the PAX and SOX genes during normal development is to maintain cell proliferation with self-renewal, which is similar to overcoming the Hayflick limit during malignant transformation. Additionally, resistance to apoptosis and cell migration are required for both normal development and oncogenesis. Therefore, the unorthodox functions of the PAX and SOX genes may be considered a reinvocation of their orthodox functions during oncogenesis, albeit with different biological consequences (Fig. 1C).

We elucidated the transcriptional activation pathways in the TMM that involved the PAX and SOX genes during malignant transformation. These pathways establish the core architecture of the RAD52-dependent ALT during malignant transformation. In more detail, PAX7 activates the recombinase (RAD52) and the negative regulator of the RAD52-dependent ALT (SLX4IP); SLX4IP is responsible for the resolution of HR intermediates, which results in aborted telomere elongation. PAX9 and SOX14 activate FEN1 and RAD9B, which are involved in the stability of HR intermediates, while PAX6 and SOX14 activate the positive regulators (BLM and BRCA2) of the RAD52-dependent ALT. BLM promotes the dissolution of HR intermediates, which results in telomere elongation, and BRCA2 promotes RAD52-dependent ALT by antagonizing NHEJ34–37 (Fig. 2). Other than the inhibition of NHEJ by BRCA2, there was no transcriptional regulation of NHEJ-related genes by the PAX and SOX genes. Among the 221 gene pairs between 13 PAX and SOX genes and 17 NHEJ-related genes, three pairs (PAX6 and LIG4, PAX9 and LIG4, and PAX9 and SHLD1) showed transcriptional activation and, one pair (SOX10 and SHLD2) showed transcriptional repression; however, all these pairs failed to exceed the FDR as determined by the B-H procedure (Supplementary Information 3).

Although telomere length maintenance is prerequisite for malignant transformation to overcome the Hayflick limit, it is unclear whether telomere length maintenance is involved in other key processes of oncogenesis. Although cancer cells are often characterized by their capacity for immortal cell division, the medical definition of cancer is a neoplasm with the capacity to metastasize distantly. For this reason, we first analyzed the association between telomere length maintenance and metastasis. There was essentially no correlation between the telomere length and metastasis. This study demonstrated that the stability of HR intermediates played a dominant role in maintaining the telomere length in the TMMs whose transcriptional activation was induced by genes of the TGF-β superfamily. However, neither FEN1 nor RAD9B, which are involved in the stability of HR intermediates, were associated with metastasis. Furthermore, RAD52, the recombinase of the RAD52-dependent ALT, was found to be associated with the telomere length but not with metastasis. Considering upstream factors of the TGF-β superfamily-induced transcriptional activation, transcriptional cascades from BMP3, GDF3, BMP15, INHBC, and LEFTY2 to SOX14 were found to play a critical role in TMM activation by activating FEN1 and RAD9B. None of them appeared to be associated with metastasis. However, the transcriptional cascades BMP2 to PAX7 and INHBC to PAX9 were shown to be associated with metastasis in addition to being responsible for telomere lengthening by activating RAD52 and RAD9B, respectively. In summary, the telomere length may not affect metastasis, while the TGF-β superfamily-induced transcriptional activation of the PAX and SOX genes independently promotes telomere elongation and metastasis.

High redundancy of the TGF-β superfamily-induced transcriptional activation pathways

Cancer does not result from simple aggregation of transformed cells but involves a high level of interaction between transformed cells and the TME. We hypothesized that the TME was responsible for reinvocation of the orthodox functions of the PAX and SOX genes during malignant transformation of MPNSTs. Because TGF-β was originally identified as a component of sarcoma growth factor secreted from Moloney murine sarcoma virus-infected 3T3 cells28, we focused on the TGF-β superfamily as a representative component of the TME.

TGF-β has been reported to have contradictory functions in oncogenesis. TGF-β acts as a tumor suppressor by inducing cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis in the early stage of oncogenesis but promotes cell migration, metastasis, and chemoresistance in later stages50. This paradoxical activity of TGF-β can be attributed to the different actions of canonical TGF-β/SMAD pathways and noncanonical TGF-β/non-SMAD pathways. TGF-β signaling pathways have long been considered targets for cancer therapy. Many targeting agents, including monoclonal antibodies that can neutralize/sequester TGF-βs and TGF-β receptors, small molecules targeting the kinase activity of TGF-β receptors and antisense RNA- or siRNA-based oligonucleotides against TGF-β have been evaluated. Unfortunately, oncological outcomes have been reported to be unsatisfactory, even after excluding adverse effects associated with TGF-β-induced fibrosis (hepatic, renal, and pulmonary fibrosis) and cardiotoxicity30,31,50. The poor outcomes of various agents that target multiple steps of TGF-β signaling cannot be fully explained by complex downstream signaling pathways alone.

We propose a new perspective on the poor outcomes associated with the inhibition of TGF-β signaling as a cancer therapy. We found highly redundant TGF-β superfamily-induced transcriptional activation pathways that activate the TMM. Without controlling false-positives, 24 pairs between TGF-β superfamily genes and PAX and SOX genes were identified to be significant (Supplementary Information 4). PAX9 was found to be regulated by nine ligands (BMP2, BMP3, BMP8B, BMP15, BMPER, GDF3, GDF9, INHBC, and LEFTY2) from the TGF-β superfamily; SOX14 was found to be activated by six ligands (BMP3, BMP4, BMP15, GDF3, INHBC, and LEFTY2) from the TGF-β superfamily; PAX7 was found to be activated by six ligands (BMP2, BMP4, BMP15, BMPER, GDF1, and INHBC) from the TGF-β superfamily; and PAX6 was found to be regulated by three ligands (BMP4, BMP8B, and BMPER) from the TGF-β superfamily. Although only four independent transcriptional activations (PAX6 and PAX7 by BMPER, SOX14 by BMP15, and PAX9 by INHBC) were identified after controlling false-positives (FDR = 0.1) and using multiple LR analyses, these findings strongly suggested that the TGF-β superfamily-induced TMM transcriptional activation pathways were highly redundant (Supplementary Information 4, Fig. 2). We believe that the high redundancy of TGF-β superfamily signaling explains the poor results of TGF-β signaling inhibition in cancer therapy and highlights obstacles that need to be overcome for better results. Indeed, combinations of pan-TGF-β neutralizing antibodies31 and combinations of TGF-β signaling inhibitors with cytotoxic agents or immune checkpoint inhibitors50 have been tested for the treatment of various cancers, and improved results have been reported.

Methods

Patients and samples

We analyzed 20 patients with NF1 who underwent surgery for both NFs and MPNSTs from November 2000 to July 2017. The demographics of the patients and the characteristics of the tumors (NF and MPNST) are described in Table 1. The incidence of NF1 is approximately one in 3000 people and the cumulative lifetime risk for malignant transformation in NF1 patients is 8–13%. MPNST associated with NF1 is very rare in nature. Additionally, since this study required both NF and MPNST samples, only patients who underwent surgery for both NF and MPNST in our institute could be included in this study. Therefore, all the patients who underwent surgery for both NF and MPNST in our institute with adequate FFPE tissues preserved were enrolled in the study. As a result, 16 FFPE tissues of NF-MPNST pairs were enrolled over 17 years (from November 2000 to July 2017) and four pairs of fresh frozen tissues were prospectively enrolled. FFPE samples were prepared as slides to enrich tumor tissue and filter out normal tissue.

Whole genome sequencing

The quality of genomic DNA was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis and DNA was quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq DNA Nano library prep kits (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Genomic DNA (100 ng) was fragmented using adaptive focused acoustic technology (Covaris) and was modified to create 5-phosphorylated blunt-ended dsDNA molecules. DNA fragments were selected by size using a bead-based method and further processed by the addition of a single A base and ligation of TruSeq indexing adapters. Purified libraries were quantified using qPCR (KAPA library quantification kit for Illumina sequencing platforms), and their quality was assessed using an Agilent Technologies 2200 TapeStation. Finally, paired-end (2 × 150 bp) sequencing was performed by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea) using the NovaSeq platform (Illumina).

Whole transcriptome sequencing

The total RNA concentration was measured using the Quant-iT RiboGreen kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). To determine the DV200 (% of RNA fragments > 200 bp) value, samples were analyzed on a TapeStation RNA screentape (Agilent). Total RNA (100 ng) was subjected to sequencing library construction using a TruSeq RNA Access library prep kit (Illumina). Total RNA was fragmented using divalent cations at an elevated temperature. The fragmented RNA was reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA using the SuperScript II reverse transcriptase Kit (Invitrogen, #18064014), followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis with random primers. cDNA fragments were then subjected to an end repair process, the addition of a single ‘A’ base, and the ligation of adapters. The resulting products were purified and enriched via PCR to create a cDNA library. All libraries were normalized and pooled into a single hybridization/capture reaction. Pooled libraries were incubated with a cocktail of biotinylated oligonucleotides corresponding to coding regions. The targeted library molecules were captured via two rounds of hybridization with biotinylated oligonucletide probes using streptavidin-conjugated beads. The enriched library molecules were subjected to a second round of PCR amplification. Captured libraries were quantified using the KAPA library quantification kit for Illumina sequencing platforms (Kapa Biosystems, #KK4854) and their quality was assessed using a TapeStation D1000 screentape (Agilent Technologies, # 5067-5582). Indexed libraries were paired-end (2 × 100 bp) sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea). The raw data of gene transcripts were quantified and normalized as Transcripts per million (TPM). All TPM values were log2 transformed (log2(TPM + 1)), where the entire expression profile was proportionally transformed, and the 0 conversions were corrected by adding 1 to the substitution.

Measuring telomere lengths

Telomeric reads were analyzed using TelomereHunter18. This program extracts telomeric reads with nonconsecutive repeats and categorizes them according to alignment coordinates and mapping quality. Reads are classified into different telomeric regions. The telomere content is provided as a fraction of intratelomeric reads per million reads. The program also accounts for GC bias, and the GC content is used to correct the telomeric content by dividing intratelomeric reads by the number of GC reads with a GC content between 48 and 52%. In this study, GC-corrected telomeric reads were used.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed for each gene pair using SPSS26 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). 1,300 gene pairs comprising all possible combinations of 13 PAX and SOX genes and 100 TMM-related genes (Supplementary Information 2) and 120 gene pairs comprising all possible combinations of 30 TGF-β superfamily genes and PAX6, PAX7, PAX9, and SOX14 (Supplementary Information 3). Details and symbols of all genes used in this study are provided in Supplementary Information 1.

Transcriptional activation of GENE A by GENE B was defined as a significant positive correlation of ΔGENE B with both ΔGENE A transcript level and MPNST-GENE A transcript level according to linear regression (LR). In addition, to achieve only TMM-related transcriptional activation, the interaction effects of ΔGENE A and ΔGENE B on the MPNST-telomere length were analyzed using a generalized linear model (GLM). All the LR and GLM results were corrected to control the false discovery rate (FDR) by the Benjamini-Hochberg (B-H) procedure at FDR = 0.25 (Supplementary Information 2) or FDR = 0.01 (Supplementary Information 3).

We performed paired t-tests to evaluate changes in the transcript levels of each gene between NFs-MPNSTs during malignant transformation and performed LR to evaluate the effect of each gene on the telomere length. We performed factor analysis using varimax rotation, determined the number of groups via scree plot, and used factor scores from the factor analysis for LR to assess the effects of each characteristic group on the telomere length. We performed Cox regression to assess the association of each gene with distant metastasis.
