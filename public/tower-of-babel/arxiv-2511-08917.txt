Kapil Garg, Xinru Tang, Jimin Heo, Dwayne R. Morgan, Darren Gergle, Erik B. Sudderth, Anne Marie Piper

Abstract
. Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are increasingly used by blind and low-vision (BLV) people to identify and understand products in their everyday lives, such as food, personal products, and household goods. Despite their prevalence, we lack an empirical understanding of how common image quality issues, like blur and misframing of items, affect the accuracy of VLM-generated captions and whether resulting captions meet BLV people’s information needs. Grounded in a survey with 86 BLV people, we systematically evaluate how image quality issues affect captions generated by VLMs. We show that the best model recognizes products in images with no quality issues with 98% accuracy, but drops to 75% accuracy overall when quality issues are present, worsening considerably as issues compound. We discuss the need for model evaluations that center on disabled people’s experiences throughout the process and offer concrete recommendations for HCI and ML researchers to make VLMs more reliable for BLV people.

. While Vision-Language Model (VLM)-based

Despite the enthusiasm towards VLM-based captioning tools for identifying and understanding products, three challenges complicate their real-world use and evaluation. First, extensive prior work has studied and introduced VLM-based captioning tools to help BLV people understand objects and products in their environment

Xie et al. 2025

Tang et al. 2025

Alharbi et al. 2024b

We know less about what factors (e.g., privacy, accuracy, safety) shape their decision to turn to automated systems versus humans and their experience with captioning errors using existing tools (e.g., Be My AI, Seeing AI). Second, VLM-based image captioning tools work best when BLV users take and upload high-quality photos, a known challenge for BLV people

Gurari et al. 2018a

;
Lee et al. 2019
)
. However, limited prior work examines how BLV people assess image quality issues with existing VLM tools and perceive their impact on captions
(
Hong and Kacorri 2024
)
. Third, interview studies with BLV people indicate that pervasive image quality issues affect whether images are captioned accurately
(
Zhao et al. 2018b
;
Alharbi et al. 2024b
)
, but the relationship between image quality factors and the accuracy of resulting product captions has yet to be analyzed systematically. Prior datasets examine the prevalence of image quality issues, but evaluation of these issues remains coarse (e.g., determining whether an image is captionable or not)
(
Gurari et al. 2018a
;
Chiu et al. 2020
;
Davis et al. 2020
)
. Moreover, existing evaluation approaches for image captions focus on how well a generated caption aligns with a reference text. This can result in false positives where a caption seems reasonable even when it contains serious errors or is missing critical information. Understanding how pervasive image quality issues affect generated captions among state-of-the-art VLMs is critical, given that these models are used in a wide range of assistive technologies and research prototypes
(
Chang et al. 2024a

Chang et al. 2024b

.

To help bridge these gaps in the literature, this paper examines challenges in using VLM tools to identify and understand products through two complementary efforts. First, we report results from a survey with 86 BLV people, which details their experiences and perspectives on captioning product images with existing VLM-based tools. More than half of survey respondents emphasized using only AI tools (over human assistance) when personal privacy matters most, and roughly two-thirds said they would most often just use AI when reading a label on a food item, identifying personal care products or toiletries, and identifying an unknown item in their home. Taking a good photo remains the hardest part of the process for many participants (echoing
(
Lee et al. 2019
;
Hong and Kacorri 2024
)
). Even with current tools that provide photo-taking guidance (e.g., SeeingAI, Be My AI), detecting and resolving image quality issues remains challenging. Moreover, the most frequently experienced error in captions of product images involves missing critical information, such as product brand names and ingredients, which may be obscured when images have quality issues.

Building on our survey findings, we then develop a structured annotated dataset of naturalistic product images (based on the VizWiz dataset
(
Gurari et al. 2020b
;
Chiu et al. 2020
)
) and use it to evaluate the extent to which four VLMs (GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Llama 3.2 90B, and Molmo 72B) are robust to common image quality issues. All VLMs were proficient at product identification for high-quality images (i.e., without blur, rotation, framing, or other issues) taken by BLV people, with accuracy rates of 95% or better for GPT and Gemini. Performance for all VLMs drops substantially for low-quality images, with the best model, GPT, only achieving an accuracy of 75%. Accuracy is even lower when images have multiple image quality issues, with GPT dropping down to 69% for these images; see Figure
1
. Our regression analysis confirms that all models are sensitive to image quality issues and specific content (e.g., cans with rounded labels, nutritional facts text panel) that reduces performance, but also informs us about which image quality issues specific models are more susceptible to and should be a focus to improve their performance.

This paper makes three primary contributions to the accessibility and HCI literature. First, we provide further empirical evidence of BLV people’s preferences and experiences with VLM-based tools for captioning images of products, underscoring the continued need for improvements in real-world product captioning applications. Second, we discuss the complexities of disability-centered approaches to model evaluation, including task and data selection, annotation procedures, and determining which models and metrics to use. Our work not only benchmarks the performance of four widely-used VLMs, which underlie many modern-day accessibility tools, but it also provides an example of how to approach the evaluation of VLMs that center on BLV people’s information needs. Third, we provide concrete recommendations for making VLMs more reliable for BLV people at all stages of the development pipeline, including data curation, improving model performance, and addressing captioning errors.

2.
Background

Describing images for BLV people has been a long-standing research area in HCI. Historically, on-demand human assistance was the primary way for BLV people to access visual information about their environment. These include remote interpretation services, like Aira
(
aira 2025
)
, that connect the caller with a trained visual interpreter, and crowdsourcing-based systems, including VizWiz
(
Bigham et al. 2010
)
or Be My Eyes
(
Be My Eyes 2025a
)
, that ask a paid worker or volunteer to describe an image or video. More recently, advances in computer vision have enabled machines to provide such descriptions (e.g.,
(
Vinyals et al. 2015
)
). For example, early versions of Seeing AI from Microsoft combined various deep learning techniques for computer vision and natural language processing to describe images
(
Linn 2016
)
.
2
2

2

Architecture details for Seeing AI are sparse, but the original system’s release date suggests it lacked attention-based mechanisms found in modern VLMs.
More recently, vision-language models (VLMs) have exploded in prevalence and capability, with many tools that support image description, like ChatGPT, Gemini, and Be My AI, all using variants of these models. Given their ubiquity, our work focuses on understanding these technologies in the context of BLV people’s need for product identification, and their limitations when describing degraded images.

2.1.
How Blind and Low-Vision People Use VLMs for Image Understanding

Image captioning is a well-studied task in computer vision that aims to generate descriptive text for images and has led to extensive work within accessible computing
(
Gurari et al. 2020b
;
Mohanbabu and Pavel 2024
;
Stangl et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2022
;
MacLeod et al. 2017
)
. It is often studied alongside other visual tasks such as visual question answering
(
Bigham et al. 2010
;
Cao et al. 2022
)
, object recognition
(
Kacorri et al. 2017
;
Morrison et al. 2023
;
Theodorou et al. 2021
)
, and image obfuscation
(
Alharbi et al. 2022a
)
. With the introduction of VLMs, researchers are exploring many new applications of image captioning for BLV people, such as context-aware captions for web images
(
Mohanbabu and Pavel 2024
)
, assisting with image editing
(
Chang et al. 2024b
)
, and real-time scene interpretation of live environments
(
Gonzalez Penuela et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
;
Chang et al. 2024a
;
Chang et al. 2025
)
. Of these applications, object recognition is a core aspect of visual access tasks
(
Zeng et al. 2020
)
, and it represents a critical need among BLV people
(
Brady et al. 2013
)
. Significant efforts have been dedicated to helping BLV people identify objects
(
Gamage et al. 2023
)
, including personal belongings
(
Theodorou et al. 2021
;
Morrison et al. 2023
)
and specific products
(
Hong et al. 2022
)
.

More broadly, a large body of work has studied how VLMs perform at object recognition. Modern VLMs are largely performant on zero-shot image classification benchmarks like ImageNet
(
Deng et al. 2009
)
and MSCOCO
(
Lin et al. 2015
;
Chen et al. 2015
)
that include a broad set of objects to identify
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
. When VLMs fail, recent work suggests that failures are not due to inference-time (e.g., prompts; decoding strategies) or training-time issues (e.g., learning objective) but rather to limited data frequency for the objects the model is trying to identify
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. Despite this interest, little work has studied what happens when VLMs fail because the image is distorted, rather than if the model lacks knowledge about its contents.

2.2.
Understanding and Addressing Image Quality Issues

A key issue in using VLMs for blind people’s visual needs lies in the photos they take.
Gurari et al. 2018a
revealed two challenges presented in VizWiz related to image quality: (1) taking high-quality photographs is challenging for blind users; and (2) many visual questions posed by blind users remain unanswered because the images fail to capture the relevant objects
(
Gurari et al. 2018a
)
. While these “low-quality” images are often treated as edge cases (labeled as “other”
(
Brady et al. 2013
)
, excluded in analysis
(
Gurari et al. 2020b
)
, or treated as a direction for future work
(
Chang et al. 2024a
)
), they make up a significant portion of the photos taken by blind individuals
(
Davis et al. 2020
)
. Image quality has been identified as a major challenge in both model development
(
Gurari et al. 2020b
)
and user interactions
(
Zhao et al. 2018b
)
, leading to issues with annotation
(
Simons et al. 2020
;
Gurari and Grauman 2017
;
Yang et al. 2018
;
Bhattacharya et al. 2019
)
and poor model performance
(
Zhao et al. 2018b
)
. For example,
Davis et al. 2020
analyzed 265 medication package images from the VizWiz dataset, showing only 46% were legible. The prevalence of low-quality images has made image quality assessment a stand-alone task in developing image captioning tools for BLV individuals
(
Chiu et al. 2020
)
.

Recognizing the importance of image quality, considerable efforts have been made to support BLV people in taking photos that both VLMs and humans can caption, with training and instruction playing a crucial role in data collection for model development
(
Sharma et al. 2023
;
Kacorri et al. 2017
;
Morrison et al. 2023
;
Theodorou et al. 2021
)
. Various techniques have been explored to improve data collection, such as using video feeds to capture objects
(
Morrison et al. 2023
;
Theodorou et al. 2021
)
, taking sequential photos of objects
(
Kacorri et al. 2017
)
, and sending notifications when objects are out of frame
(
Morrison et al. 2023
)
. While training may help, BLV users may still find it hard to properly orient objects or unintentionally capture private content in the background
(
Sharma et al. 2023
;
Theodorou et al. 2021
)
. They may also be uncertain about what to do even when they know objects are poorly framed
(
Hong et al. 2022
)
. Across this literature, the emphasis is on having BLV people produce “high-quality” images for recognition. Despite the importance of image captioning, prior work has yet to systematically understand how image quality issues affect BLV people’s experiences using VLMs and their associated accuracy.

2.3.
BLV People’s Perspectives on AI Errors

There is growing awareness among BLV people regarding errors that result from using AI tools, leading to many creative and adaptive strategies to identify them
(
Alharbi et al. 2024b
;
Adnin and Das 2024
;
Penuela et al. 2024
;
Tang et al. 2025
)
. Yet, identifying errors in some cases is still hard for BLV people. For example, when using a prototype object recognizer to identify common food items (e.g., soda, bags of chips, canned foods), BLV participants were only able to identify half of the object recognition errors, even with successive attempts, potentially due to objects’ similarity in shape and size
(
Hong and Kacorri 2024
)
. Moreover, most platforms provide little support for helping BLV users understand errors, such as confidence rates or multiple likely image descriptions
(
Adnin and Das 2024
;
Alharbi et al. 2024b
)
. In addition, perceived inaccuracy in image captioning is often amplified by external factors such as low-quality images or unreliable internet connectivity
(
Zhao et al. 2018b
)
. When users encounter delays or fail to receive meaningful responses, they may view the system as inaccurate or untrustworthy, even if the underlying AI model is functioning properly
(
Zhao et al. 2018b
)
. As more products integrate VLMs into accessibility applications for BLV people (e.g.,
(
Chang et al. 2024a
;
Huh et al. 2023
)
), it is critical to understand how robust they are to issues of accuracy in everyday tasks, such as identifying household products or goods where details matter and captioning errors can affect one’s health and safety.

3.
Study 1: Understanding Preferences and Experiences with AI-based Captioning of Product Images

To understand how image quality issues relate to errors during captioning, we first aim to understand BLV people’s experiences using VLM-based tools to identify and understand products, such as household goods and foods. This study extends prior work on how BLV people use AI tools for object recognition
(
Hong et al. 2022
;
Hong and Kacorri 2024
)
as well as tradeoffs between using AI and human assistance based on privacy risks
(
Stangl et al. 2022
;
Stangl et al. 2023
)
, social norms
(
Tang et al. 2025
;
Lee et al. 2021
)
, and other factors.

3.1.
Method

We conducted an online survey with 86 BLV people who use AI tools for image captioning tasks. To clarify the distinction between varying kinds of captioning support, the survey first asked about the general use of (1)
human-assistance
through remote sighted interpreting services that provide crowdsourced support (e.g., Be My Eyes) or a trained visual interpreter (e.g., Aira), (2)
accessibility-specific AI tools
(e.g., Microsoft Seeing AI, Be My AI, TapTapSee, Access AI), and (3)
general-purpose AI tools
(e.g., OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Google’s Gemini, Anthropic’s Claude AI). It then focused on their use of AI tools to identify and understand products, which we defined as “packaged items and objects, such as foods, toiletries, cleaning supplies, and other household goods.” The survey then covered their preferences for using AI tools compared to human alternatives and experiences using AI to understand products (e.g., taking photos, image quality issues, captioning errors). We pilot tested the survey for accessibility, question wording, and length, and revised it accordingly. Complete survey questions are provided as supplementary material.

The survey was hosted via Google Forms due to its accessibility for screen reader users and was open in March 2025. Participants were recruited through email lists maintained by the research team as well as the National Federation of the Blind (NFB)
(
nfb 2025
)
and the American Foundation for the Blind (AFB)
(
afb 2025
)
. In addition to using AI tools for image captioning tasks, eligible participants must identify as blind or low-vision, be age 18 or older, use a screen reader to access digital content, speak English, and reside in the United States. Upon confirming eligibility, participants were invited to take the survey. Participants provided consent before beginning. Each participant received a $20 Amazon card after completing the survey. The survey study was approved by our university IRB.

We received 97 survey responses, which were reviewed by the research team for duplicates and quality issues (e.g., answers that were spam-like or lacked variation). Eleven responses were removed, resulting in a total sample of 86. Our sample had more respondents who identified as women (n=58, 67.4%) than men (n=24, 27.9%) or non-binary (n=4, 4.7%). Most participants were aged 39-49 (n=44, 51.2%) or 50-64 (n=25, 29.1%), with smaller groups reporting age 18-29 (n=9, 10.5%) and 65 or older (n=8, 9.3%). Roughly 67.4% (n=58) of participants identified as white, with some identifying as Asian (n=16, 18.6%), Black or African American (n=9, 10.5%), and/or Native American or Alaska Native or Native Hawaiian (n=3, 3.5%). Seven percent (n=6) identified as Hispanic, Latino, or Spanish. More than 80% (n=72) of our sample had earned their bachelor’s degree or higher.

3.2.
Analysis

We present descriptive statistics of survey questions in the findings below. Where appropriate, we compare the experience of BLV users and different tools or image quality issues that affect image captioning. We use a Mann-Whitney U Test because the data being compared is defined on an ordinal (Likert) scale
(
Mann and Whitney 1947
)
. We also present excerpts of quotes from open-ended responses that provide additional context for our interpretations.

3.3.1.
AI captioning is preferred for identifying food, personal products, and items in the home

Building on previous research showing that BLV people move across human and AI assistance for access
(

When considering common scenarios for product identification, roughly two-thirds of the participants said they would almost always or most often only use AI when reading a label on a food item (68.6%, n=59), identifying personal care products or toiletries (67.4%, n=58), and identifying an unknown item in their home (64%, n=55). Surprisingly, more than 45% of participants (n=39) said they would almost always or most often just rely on AI for reading a medication label, despite multiple AI tools having warnings about such use. Fewer participants said they would mainly rely on AI when checking allergen information on products (37.2%, n=32), comparing the details of two products side by side (31.4%, n=27), or checking expiration dates on products (27.6%, n=23). In contrast, more participants indicated they would most often rely on human assistance when data privacy mattered most (46.5%, n=40). Several people noted the dilemma between personal and data privacy, saying, “it’s a catch-22: go with AI-generated [services] where they store a photo, or a person who could be sitting there copying down my information,” which led to varying priorities regarding the associated risks. People were seen as a greater direct risk due to the potential for bad human actors (e.g., “If I ask a human, someone will know”), while AI tools presented a broader indirect risk (e.g., “A human has a limited number of people they could potentially share the information with, but AI means more companies can access your data”). Like data privacy, most participants leaned towards just using human-sighted assistance when safety (53.5%, n=46) and accuracy (44.2%, n=38) mattered most, as humans were generally seen as more reliable in these areas, especially when there was a clear and specific need, such as counting or reading text.

3.3.2.
Photo taking is the longest and most difficult part of captioning

To understand participants’ experience using AI tools for product captioning tasks, we asked them about their captioning process. Considering the tool they used most, nearly half of the participants (47.7%, n=41) said it took 2-4 minutes to get the information they wanted, with others saying it took 0-1 minutes (27.9%, n=24) or 5-9 minutes (19.8%, n=17). Two-thirds of participants (67.1%, n=49) said taking a good photo was the hardest part of the process, and roughly half (45.9%, n=34) said it took the longest. Multiple photos were often needed, with most participants (62.8%, n=54) saying 2-3 photos; fewer needed just one photo (23.3%, n=20) or more than four photos (10.5%, n=9). For some participants, taking photos was difficult due to physical disabilities, which made it hard to hold the camera steady. Participants described learning to take photos over time, including learning from how Aira interpreters guide them to angle their camera and adjust the environment for visual captioning.

3.4.
Final Reflections

Near the end of the survey, we asked BLV people what they would like to communicate to researchers and developers building these tools. Respondents across the board emphasized accuracy and precision, saying “I need accuracy and precise captions,” “Be more specific!” and “Please please be sure your tools are accurate. Especially if people are using it for life-reliant things like medicine.” Others emphasized frustration (e.g., “It’s really frustrating that I have to go through so many hoops just to be able to find out what’s in a box or can”) and that there is more work to be done, saying developers need to “Take time to understand the specific use cases and needs that are unique to users who are blind or low vision.” Another suggested that many of the issues BLV people are facing with such tools are because underlying models are typically “trained by non-disabled people, [and] show implicit bias toward disabled people.” Their final reflections underscore the importance of evaluating how the image quality issues BLV people contend with daily —which are often set aside in research—affect whether VLMs can accurately identify products with the level of detail that BLV people need for safe and effective use.

4.
Study 2: Evaluating VLM Captions for Product Understanding

Given the pervasive challenges with using VLMs to identify products, we systematically examine how image quality issues affect a VLM’s ability to identify them correctly and in detail.

4.1.
Challenges in Evaluating VLMs’ Product Captioning Performance

We initially conducted experiments using
Gurari et al. 2020b
’s VizWiz Image Captioning dataset
(
Gurari et al. 2020b
)
, but encountered three challenges. First, captions from crowd workers varied in whether they correctly identified products and the level of detail provided, making it difficult to assess whether a VLM was performing poorly or we lacked accurate product identification data against which to benchmark the caption. Second, existing metrics for measuring caption quality—like BLEU
(
Papineni et al. 2001
)
, METEOR
(
Banerjee and Lavie 2005
)
, ROUGE
(
Lin 2004
)
, CIDEr
(
Vedantam et al. 2015
)
, SPICE
(
Anderson et al. 2016
)
, and BERTScore
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
—primarily measure
text alignment
and thus are unreliable for evaluating
correctness
of captions in providing information that supports accurate product identification. Adding images to such measures (e.g., Vilbertscore
(
Lee et al. 2020b
)
, TIGEr
(
Jiang et al. 2019
)
, SCAN
(
Lee et al. 2018
)
) or using reference-free measures (e.g., CLIPScore
(
Hessel et al. 2022
)
) can help, but degraded images can be a confound in the score and obscure how well the caption identifies products. These challenges motivated us to develop a dataset with verified annotations to determine whether products were correctly identified in captions.

4.2.
Method

4.2.1.
Data Selection

To create a dataset focused on products, we start with
Gurari et al. 2020b
’s VizWiz Image Captioning dataset
(
Gurari et al. 2020b
)
, which includes five crowd worker-provided image captions on photos taken by blind people, and
Chiu et al. 2020
’s VizWiz Image Quality Assessment dataset
(
Chiu et al. 2020
)
, which includes annotations on image quality issues by five crowd workers. Using their training dataset (23,431 images), we first filter based on the captionability of images, where humans can confidently provide a caption, indicating that image quality issues are not severe enough to prevent image description. We select these data by including images where unrecognizable
\leq
2 (i.e., two or fewer people could not provide a caption; conversely, three to five raters provided a caption). Upon inspection of the dataset, we noticed that most images of products included text; thus, we only included images where captioners identified text in the image as a heuristic for products. This resulted in a filtered dataset of 14,398 images (61.5% of the original dataset).

We then created two subsets of data. First, we focused on
high-quality
images of products without quality issues. This dataset is a benchmark for how well VLMs perform at product identification on natural images. We selected images where 4 or 5 raters flagged the image as having no issues, and at most 1 person flagged each image quality issue, resulting in 2,599 images (11.1% of the original). Second, we created a dataset of
low-quality images
, where 4 or 5 raters flagged the image having an
image quality
issue (blur, rotation, framing, obstruction, or being too bright or too dark), resulting in 5,432 images (23.2% of the original). This dataset corresponds to images where human captioners felt confident providing a caption despite identifying image quality issues that could hinder captioning.

Given our aim to understand how accurately VLMs identify products, we then identified images that appeared to contain products by manually reviewing all images. Four researchers reviewed all images in each subset. They excluded images that did not include products, such as nondescript boxes or pictures of rooms in the home. We excluded images of computer screenshots, currency, printed papers, books, CDs, DVDs, clothing, and unpackaged electronic devices. These were, on the whole, difficult for annotators to objectively verify, such as identifying an article of clothing or the name of a book based on a page of its text.
3
3

3

While these are real-world cases where objects are ambiguous and valuable to identify, we require images with clear, correct, and assessable annotations to understand how VLMs fail (our focus), where more ambiguous or hard to verify examples could create a confound in our analysis.
We also excluded any images where more than one product is pictured. For the high-quality images specifically, we also eliminated any product images with even mild distortions (e.g., camera blur; lens flares) to ensure the subset was completely free from image quality issues. This resulted in a high-quality subset of 729 images and a low-quality subset of 1,696 images.

4.2.2.
Data Annotation

Our survey results showed that BLV people want specific product information when selecting foods, medicines, and personal products. We developed a three-part annotation scheme consisting of:

•

Product: the generic term for the product (e.g., cereal, soup, meal, medication).

•

Brand: any detectable brand information (e.g., Betty Crocker, Kraft, Great Value, Kellogg’s).

•

Variety: details about the type, flavor, or variety (e.g., peanut, low sodium)

A team of four researchers manually annotated each image using this structure. When annotating a product, researchers reviewed the image and human captions; if unsure, researchers also searched online for product images or noted they were unsure about the image, so another researcher could look. The image was excluded from the dataset if researchers were uncertain about the pictured product. For example, we excluded images that only showed product barcodes or did not have visible details required for product verification. To ensure the validity of product annotations, a second researcher then reviewed and confirmed agreement with each image and annotation. Any discrepancies were flagged for discussion, and if no agreement was reached, the image was removed from the dataset. To enable consistency in product naming, the research team aimed for the most specific name within the product (e.g., granola instead of cereal, Sprite instead of soda) and included both brand and sub-brand names when available. When possible, we include known flavor or ingredient details (e.g., vanilla soymilk; chicken with potatoes and green beans) and details related to dietary needs or potential allergies (e.g., zero-sugar Gatorade, peanut butter granola bars). This detailed and validated labeling is distinct from coarse data labels of objects and products in prior work
(
Gurari et al. 2018a
;
Kacorri et al. 2017
)
. This process resulted in a final dataset of 1,859 images annotated with product details, including 729 high-quality images and 1,130 low-quality images. See Appendix
B
, Table
7
for the number of images where 0-5 crowd workers identified an image quality issue.

During data annotation, researchers also noted
product properties
that may affect caption quality, including when labels were rounded (e.g., on cans, bottles) or contained large panes of text (e.g., nutrition label, back of box recipes) by double-coding if a product had one or both properties. We identified 671 (36.1% of our dataset) products with rounded labels, 175 (9.4%) with large text panels, and 49 (2.6%) with both characteristics (e.g., back of a can).

Finally, two researchers observed that while the agreement of two crowd workers on the presence of blur or framing issues effectively captured issue quality, it did not for rotation. Therefore, the researchers recoded rotation as an orientation beyond 45 degrees from the product’s natural axis—depending on the product’s top and bottom (such as a can) and text orientation—marking the image as rotated if both agreed. 4.2.4.
Dataset Coding

As the final step in our dataset creation process, we manually coded each VLM-generated caption for correctness. We performed human coding due to the issues with existing captioning metrics (see Section
4.1
) and because LLM-as-judges—while correlating well with human judgment for simple question-answer tasks
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
—may be falsely lenient on more open-ended tasks, like slightly incorrect product descriptions (e.g., Coke Zero versus Diet Coke)
(
Thakur et al. 2025
)
. All VLM captions were anonymized to reduce potential bias during coding (i.e., Model A, B, C, D), and any image metadata (e.g., whether it was high-quality versus low-quality; quality issues) was hidden, aside from the product annotations. The order of images was also randomized to reduce any ordering effects.

Four researchers were involved in coding the accuracy of the four VLM captions for each of the 1,859 images in our dataset (7,436 captions in total). Before coding, the research team met and developed a coding scheme that allowed for minor spelling mistakes and term variation (e.g., soda vs. soft drink; chips vs. crisps) but was strict on key details (e.g., brand and sub-brand; ingredients when describing food variety). Captions were marked as incorrect if there were major hallucinations (e.g., 12 ounces reported as 12-pack, for a soda can) or contained errors that changed their meaning (e.g., grilled chicken instead of fried chicken). The researchers each coded a sample of 50 random images with all VLM captions (200 captions total). IRR was computed using Krippendorff alpha, with an agreement of 0.859. Following the training period, the four researchers independently coded the remaining images, marking ones they were unsure about as "maybe". The team reviewed and discussed these and other challenging cases.

4.2.5.
Analytical and Statistical Approach

The first step in our analysis was to assess the overall accuracy of VLMs for identifying products across the range of image attributes and quality issues previously identified. Here, we computed descriptive statistics to reveal how often each VLM correctly identified products across each image quality type, image quality issue, and product properties.

The next stage of our analysis applied inferential statistics to determine how different types of image degradations and product properties influence each VLM’s ability to accurately identify products. We modeled this relationship over a series of logistic regressions. We began with a single model that included all images and captions for each VLM. This allowed us to assess overall patterns in how the VLMs performed with degraded images, and to make direct statistical comparisons of performance across VLMs. The model predictors included binary variables capturing image quality dimensions (blur, framing, and rotation)
7
7

7

, product image properties (rounded label, text panel), and a categorical factor representing the VLMs (GPT, Gemini, Llama, Molmo). We binned each image quality variable as either true (if 2–5 crowd workers reported the issue) or false (if 0–1 workers did). Because image quality issues co-occur
(
Chiu et al. 2020

, we included all two- and three-way interactions among image quality issues, two-way interactions between image quality issues and product properties, and two-way interactions between image quality issues and the VLM factor.

Our final analyses fit a set of individual logistic regression models for each VLM, independently. This allows us to more clearly delineate and assess how a given VLM’s performance degrades across different image quality issues. The predictors in these models include image quality dimensions (blur, framing, and rotation) and all 2- and 3-way interactions among them; product properties were excluded, as they resulted in poorer model fit.

We used the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) metric during model development to compare candidate models and determine final model parameterizations. The AIC metric assesses the balance between model fit and complexity, penalizing models with excessive numbers of parameters to avoid overfitting. We also checked for and observed no outliers in the dataset nor evidence of multicollinearity in the final models (all variance inflation factor (VIF) scores were less than five). 4.3.
Findings

4.3.1.
VLMs Struggle to Correctly Identify Products When Images Are Degraded

All VLMs struggled to provide accurate captions for degraded images (see Table
2
). For high-quality images taken by BLV people, GPT and Gemini performed well, recognizing 98.4% and 95.9% of products, respectively. Accuracy for open-source models drops slightly for high-quality images, with Molmo correctly recognizing 87.1% and Llama recognizing 86.3%. Performance for all VLMs drops substantially for low-quality images, with the best model, GPT, only achieving an accuracy of 74.9%. While Gemini only performed slightly worse than GPT (recognizing 71.3%), Llama and Molmo faired much worse, with Llama accurately identifying 44.1% of products in images and Molmo 36.4%.

What’s more, accuracy is even worse when images have multiple distortions, with GPT dropping down to 69.3% for these images, Gemini to 66.4%, Llama to 35.7%, and Molmo to 27.9%. While identifying over two-thirds of products in images with image quality issues may not seem problematic, the stakes for misidentifying products are higher for BLV people, especially for products with health or safety-related issues. For instance,
Davis et al. 2020
showed how medical packaging presents a challenging task for VLMs and is a case where knowing the correct medicine and dosage is critical
(
Davis et al. 2020
)
. Moreover, images with multiple degradations are common in our dataset, at nearly 60% of all low-quality images, and 36% of the entire dataset.

Recognizing products with rounded labels is a challenge more generally
(
Davis et al. 2020
)
, as is identifying products based on a large panel of text – which may be further distorted by image quality issues. However, we found that these product properties do not always affect the studied VLMs (see Table
3
). For high-quality images with only rounded labels or only text panels, GPT, Gemini, and Llama have little to no performance loss compared to high-quality images with neither (maximum drop of 1.6%, for GPT on text panels only). For low-quality images, performance loss for these models was similar (maximum drop of 0.3%, for GPT on text panels only). Molmo has a larger performance drop for text panels in high-quality images (87.4% to 76.7%) and low-quality images (35.1% to 30.2%). The studied VLMs seem more susceptible to product images with rounded labels and text panels. While GPT was unaffected, Gemini, Llama, and Molmo all dropped to 77.8% for high-quality images; similar drops occurred for low-quality images (67.5%, 42.5%, and 30.0%, respectively). We suspect these drops related to text panels are due to the VLMs overfocusing on visible text details. Recent work has suggested that VLMs can become conflicted between text and visual details, incorrectly overfocusing on text
(
Deng et al. 2025
)
. Molmo did this frequently, where in one instance it labeled a carton of “O Organics almond milk” as “Horizon Organic” due to reading “organics”, despite completely different package designs.

Our regression results reveal that image quality issues impact all VLMs and their ability to identify products; see Table
4
. All image quality variables (blur, framing, rotation) were statistically significant and negative, indicating that their presence would make the studied VLM more likely to incorrectly identify a product. Blurred images were the most likely to be incorrect, reducing the odds of correct product identification by 87.8%. We hypothesize that all four VLMs are trained on high-quality (i.e., not blurry) images, never learning to handle blurred images during inference. In examples of blurred images, we observe discrepancies in identifying the product generally versus providing necessary details for BLV (see Table
5
, row 1-2)

8

We focus our examples here on brands from English-speaking countries, primarily the U.S., given that these models should perform the best on such products. While our dataset includes brands from English-speaking countries outside the U.S. (e.g., crisps in the U.K.), these examples are sparse and less likely to be in training data for models built by U.S. companies.
. For example, GPT and Gemini correctly identify a box of Chewy Lemonhead & Friends candy, while Llama only identifies “Lemon Heads” (missing “& Friends” sub-brand) and that it is candy (missing “chewy” variety). Molmo is similar, missing sub-brand and variety details. This suggests that VLMs can capture large, easily readable text, such as brand labels, that is more resistant to distortion than fine-text details (e.g., food flavor). In another example, only GPT can correctly identify a can of Great Value light red Kidney Beans; Llama can identify the brand, but it, Gemini, and Molmo all fail to identify kidney beans.

Framing was the second most problematic image quality issue across models, reducing the odds of correct product identification by 83.7%. Specific examples from show that framing issues even affect the identification of common U.S. brands (e.g., Tide detergent, Sprite Zero), which almost certainly occur frequently in the internet-scale training data for these models; see Table
5
, row 3-4. What makes framing interesting is how well VLMs fill in or infer what the rest of the content should be. Each VLM was varied in this regard. For example, GPT and Gemini could fill in “Tid” and “DS” for Tide Pods, while Llama could fill in “Tide” and Molmo filled in neither (despite recognizing it was laundry detergent). However, no models could fill in “Sprite”.

Finally, rotation was the least problematic image quality issue, reducing the odds of correct product identification by 80%. Qualitatively, we found that rotation makes it harder for VLMs to understand fine text details—which often includes key details about the product—compared to larger attributes, like brand text and logos, or well-known varieties (e.g., Diet for Coke); see Table
5
, row 5-6. For product and variety details, we saw that GPT, Llama, and Molmo failed to identify the product (herb tea) and brand (Bigelow) of a K-Cups pod, while Gemini was correct. In the second example of Select Choice Chewy granola bars, all models identified the product (granola bars) and variety (chewy, with chocolate chips), but only GPT correctly recognizes the brand.

As shown earlier, co-occurring quality issues can drop performance, and are common in BLV people’s photos
(
Chiu et al. 2020
)
, complicating the challenge of using VLMs to identify products. The regression results reveal a significant interaction effect for blur by framing (p < 0.001), blur by rotation (p < 0.05), and framing by rotation (p < 0.001). Inspecting the interaction plots revealed that when both independent variables are true (i.e., blurred and misframed), the drop in performance is less steep than when only one is true. This suggests that once product images are sufficiently degraded, all models struggle to identify them, regardless of further image degradation. Our qualitative observations of captioning errors echo these findings; see Appendix
C
, Table
11
. For example, all three models fail to correctly identify a box of Mucinex Expectorant medication when the image is blurry, rotated 90 degrees, and half of the “M” of Mucinex is out of frame (the remainder of the label is visible). Yet, in a second image that is moved ever so slightly, such that the “M” in Mucinex is fully in view but still containing blur and 90-degree rotation, all three models caption the product correctly. Further disentangling how co-occurring image quality issues affect product identification and resulting captions is an important area for subsequent work.

As we saw earlier, rounded labels and text panels had varying effects on model performance; our regression results show this more clearly. Only text panels caused a significant drop in performance, reducing the odds of correct product identification by 40.8%. An interaction effect for framing by text panels was also significant (p < 0.001), with the interaction plot showing that framing drops performance with and without a text panel, but no text panel often results in
poorer
performance when misframed. This suggests that text panels can provide the VLM clues about the product (such as from a longer description of a frozen meal), even if other identifying features are not in clear view (the brand logo or meal title). While having a rounded label had an insignificant effect on product identification odds, the interaction effect for rotation by rounded labels was significant (p < 0.01), with the drop in performance being less steep than when only one variable is true (similar to image quality interactions). Appendix
C
, Table
12
shows examples of these effects. For instance, no model correctly identified the ground beef as 90% lean, 10% fat, despite it being clearly visible in the upper left, and all models but Llama noticed the text. The rounded Manwich sloppy Joe can partially shows the “M” from the logo and an image of prepared sloppy joes, but all models focused on the more visible tomatoes instead, inferring it was just tomato sauce.

Finally, our regression analysis shows model-wise differences in product recognition performance. Compared to GPT, the best-performing model, all VLMs had significantly reduced performance (Gemini: 42.5% reduced odds; Llama: 82.0%; Molmo: 83.1%). We found a significant interaction effect for blur by Gemini (p < 0.05) and rotation by Gemini (p < 0.05). The interaction plots showed that Gemini’s performance relative to GPT drops less quickly when the image is blurred or rotated, suggesting it may be more resistant to these distortions than GPT. We also found a significant, negative interaction for rotation by Molmo (p < 0.05). The interaction plot shows that the drop in performance is steeper when images are rotated, suggesting that Molmo is worse at handling rotations than GPT is.

4.3.3.
Differences in what each VLM struggles with

We now analyze each VLM separately to understand what image quality issues each model is most susceptible to; see Table
6
. Our VLM-level regression shows that GPT and Llama are relatively less affected by rotated images compared to blurred or misframed images (GPT: 85.4% versus 93.3% and 90.6% lower odds; Llama: 59.8% versus 84.1% and 79.9% lower odds). This suggests that efforts to improve GPT and Llama’s performance may want to prioritize blurred images, which are also the most prevalent in our dataset. On the other hand, Molmo is more susceptible to rotated images (91.0% lower odds) than to blurred (83.6%) or misframed (84%) images, suggesting that additional training with rotated images is likely to bring the most benefit. Gemini was the only model that had relatively worse performance for misframing (85.1% lower odds), versus blur (82.5%) or rotation (68.0%).

All models had an interaction effect for blur by framing with significant, positive coefficients (all p < 0.001). GPT also had a significant, positive interaction effect for framing by rotation (p < 0.05), while Molmo had significant, positive interaction effects for blur by rotation (p < 0.05) and framing by rotation (p < 0.001). Inspecting the interaction plots for all of these interactions revealed that when both independent variables are true (i.e., blur and misframing), the drop in performance is less steep than when only one is true, similar to image quality interactions in our prior regression.

5.
Discussion

Despite their impressive capabilities for object recognition, our analysis shows that VLMs struggle to provide detailed, accurate product captions that BLV people need when images have common quality issues (e.g., blur, rotation, framing). To our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically examine how image quality affects VLMs’ ability to recognize products. While numerous studies have examined how VLMs can support BLV people’s visual access needs, they largely sidestep image quality issues by asking for better photos (e.g., Seeing AI, Be My AI,
(
Mandal et al. 2023
)
) or leaving users to triangulate facts across multiple models
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
. While such adaptive practices are creative and skillful, the normalization of errors signals a dire need to improve how VLMs (and large AI models, broadly) are adapted to applications for BLV people. We argue that image quality issues must be addressed across the entire pipeline for building VLMs, which our study provides the foundation for. We discuss our approach that moves towards disability-centered VLM evaluation and development, proposing research directions for improving VLM performance and reliability through data curation, targeted model post-training, and lower-effort techniques at inference time when models may fail.

5.1.
Towards Disability-Centered Model Evaluations

Developing methods to evaluate model performance is an active area of research across HCI and AI/ML communities. As such, accessibility researchers within these areas have begun to develop various approaches to disability-centered model evaluation that involve prompting
(
Gadiraju et al. 2023
;
Park et al. 2025
)
, metric assessment
(
Kapur and Kreiss 2024
)
, interviews
(
Alharbi et al. 2024b
;
Tang et al. 2025
)
, and more. A disability-centered approach not only depends on disability-first dataset creation (e.g.,
(
Sharma et al. 2023
;
Theodorou et al. 2021
)
) but also on the evaluation, which must center on disability throughout. This includes questions of which data are focal to the study, how data are annotated to establish ‘ground truth’, which tasks and models are selected for evaluation, and which criteria or metrics are used to assess model performance. Below, we describe these issues and the challenges of disability-centered model evaluation.

We began by understanding BLV people’s information needs within a common but often challenging everyday task: using AI tools to identify household products and goods. Our approach of using a survey complemented related interview studies
(
Tang et al. 2025
;
Alharbi et al. 2024b
;
Adnin and Das 2024
;
Xie et al. 2025
)
and allowed a relatively large sample of BLV people to share their experiences and issues with a diversity of AI tools for captioning images of products, surfacing unmet needs around details in images, and the difficulty of understanding and resolving common image quality issues. Our research team is all sighted, making it even more critical to understand and prioritize BLV people’s perspectives from the start.

While related disability-centered approaches aim to support disabled people in generating ‘good’ data for training systems
(
Hong et al. 2022
;
Goodman et al. 2021
)
, our study looked at the opposite side of this issue. We intentionally curated a disability dataset such that it targets important but understudied cases (i.e., product images with quality issues), thus aiming to interrogate cases that are central to BLV people’s lived experiences but often set aside in research (i.e., labeled as others
(
Brady et al. 2013
)
, excluded in analysis
(
Gurari et al. 2020b
)
, or treated as a direction for future work
(
Chang et al. 2024a
)
). Rather than placing the burden on BLV users to consistently capture ‘high-quality’ photos required for successful object recognition or training, we argue that future datasets should treat image quality variability as a central design consideration, in contrast to existing datasets that overwhelmingly focus on high-quality images (e.g., ImageNet
(
Deng et al. 2009
)
and MSCOCO
(
Lin et al. 2015
;
Chen et al. 2015
)
) that VLMs are optimized on. Including representative quality variations that reflect the real-world conditions under which BLV people capture images can help us develop VLMs that are more resistant to real-world image quality variations from the start, rather than needing to fix them in post-training.

Although academic scholars and industry corporations have emphasized the pressing need for more disability-centered datasets
(
Sharma et al. 2023
;
Morrison et al. 2023
;
Theodorou et al. 2021
;
Li and Wu 2024
;
Bragg et al. 2021
;
Gurari et al. 2018a
;
Desai et al. 2023
)
, annotating these datasets with meaningful ‘ground truth’ labels so that they can be used in benchmark studies and model evaluations such as the present paper remains a challenge, particularly when the phenomena of interest are inaccessible to the people who matter most
(
Hong et al. 2022
;
Goodman et al. 2021
)
. Relying on crowd workers is a common approach to annotation, but they may lack insight into disabled people’s information needs and apply varying standards of visual interpretation in the case of BLV-focused datasets
(
Simons et al. 2020
)
. They are also often limited by time per annotation and move on quickly when difficult cases are encountered. Using other VLMs to synthetically generate annotations is a popular approach
(
Tan et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
, but likely to perpetuate inaccuracies or biases the model already has (see distribution shift
(
Schroeder et al. 2025
)
), rather than capturing important nuances. In other words, use cases that are the most challenging for machines require extensive human labor. In our case, four researchers spent more than three months reviewing, discussing, validating, and annotating low-quality images (we plan to make our dataset available after publication). While we developed a structured annotation framework based on BLV users’ information needs, we were still limited by the information available in images, and could not reliably code expiration dates or product ingredient lists (other details that BLV people wanted captured and should be examined in future work).

Another challenge is in selecting models to evaluate that align with disabled people’s experiences and needs, as well as allow for further research. Thus, we selected a complementary set of VLMs to evaluate. We selected two closed-source models that power the AI image captioning tools BLV people use daily (e.g., Seeing AI, Be My AI), enabling industry relevance and application of our findings. We also evaluated two open-source models, as data privacy was an important concern to BLV people, and these models can be run locally, allowing for greater control over privacy-sensitive data, which we discuss below. Open-source models also allow for understanding training procedures that can aid in interpreting evaluation results.

Finally, disability-centered approaches must contend with which metrics or measures of “success” matter and best represent disabled people’s concerns. For example,
Kapur and Kreiss 2024

(
Kapur and Kreiss 2024
)
demonstrate bias in reference-based metrics against BLV people, calling for evaluation methods based on user groups’ specific needs. Towards this end, the research team manually reviewed and coded 7,436 model captions for accuracy and complete information rather than relying on metrics that assess similarity
(
Papineni et al. 2001
;
Banerjee and Lavie 2005
;
Lin 2004
;
Vedantam et al. 2015
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
, which could result in false positives where a caption seems reasonable even when it contains serious errors or is missing critical information. That is, we aimed to emphasize BLV people’s information needs by requiring models to generate both necessary and accurate product details rather than settling for general category identification (e.g., “can of food”) or brand recognition (e.g., “Campbell’s”). Given the difficulty BLV people reported in assessing errors, let alone the risk of mis-identification, more nuanced and consistent frameworks for data annotation and error analysis are crucial for reliable VLMs, especially for high-stakes uses, such as identifying food products, medications, and household cleaners. Our annotation structure provides a pathway for annotating products, with similar structures being an important direction of future work on disability datasets.

5.2.
Recommendations for Improving Performance on Low-Quality Images

Despite the higher performance of the closed-source models we studied (GPT-4.1, Gemini) on low-quality images, we argue that open-source models (e.g., Llama, Molmo) will be more fruitful to develop reliable VLMs for BLV people’s needs, like product identification. Closed-source models are limited to prompt engineering—which is insufficient for handling distorted images—and fine-tuning for improving performance. While such black-box APIs for VLMs allow for limited fine-tuning on specialized data, closed-source models offer far less flexibility since details regarding the model architecture, training data, and how the tuning is done (i.e., what weights are frozen; loss function used) are not disclosed. Moreover, closed-source models may leak private data
(
Loizos 8 25
;
Emily Forlini 2025
)
, compromising data privacy that our survey respondents strongly desired. In contrast, open-source models make the model’s architecture and training details available to researchers
9
9

9

Molmo goes further and makes training data available; Llama does not.
, along with preserving privacy if run locally. However, our results show that the open-source models have a large performance gap to narrow; we propose two promising directions to reduce this gap.

5.2.1.
Post-Training Strategies for Improving Performance

VLM performance is heavily shaped by post-training activities, including fine-tuning on specific tasks (e.g., PixMoCap for captioning
(
Deitke et al. 2025
)
) and diverse datasets
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, or training to provide answers in specific formats (e.g., instruction tuning
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
). One way to improve models at this stage is to give examples when the model lacks knowledge about a task
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. For recognizing products and their attributes, recent research suggests that VLMs require fine-tuning for good performance
(
Prabhakaran et al. 2025
;
Trabelsi et al. 2025
)
. However, our analysis shows that off-the-shelf VLMs perform well for U.S.-based products when product images are high-quality, suggesting the issue is not because of knowledge gaps in the model. That being said, such training could help adapt models for different user populations, such as BLV people in non-English speaking countries, which we did not study. Products in those countries are infrequently found in the U.S. or on English-written webpages, which we hypothesize are the primary sources of training data for the VLMs studied.

Post-training activities could also include helping models learn more robust representations of how products look when the image is degraded. While performing well on high-quality images, all models had substantially lower performance on low-quality images, suggesting that they could not find enough distinguishing characteristics in the images that led to successful identification (like humans can).
To remedy this, future research could develop synthetic datasets where high-quality images are systematically degraded with different image quality issues, such as a can of soda with progressively greater blur or different framing issues, and fine-tune a VLM with them. Our findings help focus these efforts when coupled with knowledge about model training. For example, Molmo struggled the most with rotated images, suggesting that providing pairs of correctly aligned and rotated images with high-quality annotations could help the model recognize object similarities despite different orientations. Moreover, open-source training procedures allow us to focus on fine-tuning specific parts of the model for this task, such as the vision encoder, while freezing parts that work well, like the language encoder. Synthetic datasets, however, should still be tied to and evaluated alongside user-generated datasets to help preserve the nuanced qualities of authentic data. Our existing dataset serves as a good starting point for such initiatives, as it includes high-quality images that can be altered and low-quality images for naturalistic comparison.

Alongside the data used for training, effective post-training may require re-evaluating commonly used loss functions if they do not capture correctness for the domain-specific task well, such as product identification. Our study showed that VLMs often generate believable product descriptions that are subtly wrong, resulting in a caption with a different meaning (e.g., Coke Zero versus Diet Coke). While VLM loss functions differ, many use cross-entropy loss between the logits of the model and true labels of tokens from training data. To more directly capture whether different attributes of product annotations are being preserved during fine-tuning, future work may consider developing evaluation metrics based on semantic relationships in the annotations. Inspiration could be taken from evaluation metrics like SPICE
(
Anderson et al. 2016
)
that evaluates overlaps between scene graphs (e.g., can
\rightarrow
on
\rightarrow
countertop) or Cap F1
(
Deitke et al. 2025
)
, which evaluates overlap between atomic concepts (e.g., “A can of soda”; “Soda is on the kitchen countertop”). Such loss functions could steer models towards learning what good product annotations are, more so than measures like cross-entropy that look at differences in distributions of token probability.

5.2.2.
Inference-Time Techniques for Ensuring Caption Quality

Finally, inference-time techniques may also help improve VLM output without additionally burdening the BLV user to take another photo. One way is to leverage image reconstruction techniques that repair images before captioning. For instance, with misframed images, researchers could explore inpainting techniques that provide multiple possible versions of a repaired image for captioning
(
Chung et al. 2023
;
Agarwal et al. 2024
)
, without needing additional photos to be taken. Another is to ensure key product details are included or excluded, for which we can look to related work on reducing toxicity or enforcing lexical constraints in LLM outputs, in which constraint-based optimization can have advantages over conventional fine-tuning
(
Lu et al. 2023
;
Qin et al. 2022
)
. These techniques can help get better outputs from the model from the original photo they take, rather than necessarily needing to take additional photos when captions are poor.

Lastly, VLMs may still make errors on low-quality images that cannot be fixed by post-training or inference-time techniques, which is when they should abstain. Simple techniques involving prompt engineering to abstain are of limited efficacy, with no guarantees that the instruction to abstain will be followed (e.g., best abstention prompting yields only 0.78 accuracy on question-answer tasks with similarly low-quality images
(
Huh et al. 2024
)
). In contrast, recent work in LLM abstention explores techniques based on self-consistency, where the model evaluates its own outputs and checks how uncertain it is before returning a response, showing good performance on question-answer settings
(
Yadkori et al. 2024
;
Kuhn et al. 2023
;
Manakul et al. 2023
;
Cole et al. 2023
)
. However, abstaining when generating image captions is trickier. In our study, we observed numerous cases where parts of our product annotations were correct, even when the caption as a whole was wrong. In future work, we argue that systems for partial abstention, where the system only abstains on inconsistent caption parts, should be explored. Such a system could help the user understand what the model knows and is confident about, allowing them to decide if they want to re-take a photo to try and get more information about the image, or confirm the information with someone else. Together, these techniques help make VLMs more reliable by providing high-quality responses when possible and only sharing what it is confident in when not.

5.3.
Supporting Better User Understanding of Image Quality Issues

While we emphasize multiple ways to improve VLM performance on low-quality images, BLV people may still need to re-take photos, which participants in our study wanted better guidance on.
Thus, we must still make efforts to design applications that provide richer feedback on the photo-taking experience by helping users understand their environment and potential image quality issues and guiding them in resolving issues. For example, as our participants suggested, a multi-faceted approach could provide feedback
before
taking the photo, pointing out lighting conditions and other environmental details that may affect the process;
during
photo taking, offering continuous feedback about the camera angle and object positioning, such as which part of a product the user is capturing (e.g., product logo, back of the box, nutrition label)
(
Lee et al. 2019
;
Hong et al. 2022
)
; and
after
taking the photo, with information on any image quality issues to help users learn what might affect captioning and how to make adjustments. However, survey participants also raised concerns that people with multiple disabilities may find such interventions more difficult. For example, participants mentioned difficulty holding the camera steady enough and carefully controlling their breathing to prevent blur. Others mentioned their dexterity makes it difficult to orient the camera in particular ways. While improving the photo-taking experience is important, the complexities of photo-taking for disabled users underscore the need for technical improvements first and foremost, rather than transferring the labor of taking good photos onto the users themselves.

5.4.
Limitations and Future Work

Our study has a few limitations that future work should address. First, we focus on evaluating product identification rather than the caption quality of VLMs generally. We focus on products since BLV people in our survey strongly wanted to know what products they took photos of. However, VLMs provide numerous details in image captions, including key product information (e.g., a can of Coca-Cola), plus visual details of the product and nearby objects (e.g., the can is red; the can is on the counter). Moreover, BLV people want these extra details, as our survey and prior work
(
Morrison et al. 2023
;
Kapur and Kreiss 2024
)
have shown. Future studies should study VLM captions holistically to understand different kinds of errors.

A second limitation is reducing image quality issues to a binary variable. Our dataset included a count of crowd workers who identified an image quality issue, but treating this count as continuous or ordinal overinterprets it (i.e., 5 does not necessarily mean more blurry than a 2), so we converted it to binary. In reality, image degradation occurs on a spectrum, likely affecting VLMs differently as it worsens. For instance, low blur may cause no issues with captioning, while higher blur is problematic. Future work can draw from computer vision research to quantify image degradations (e.g., blur kernel estimation
(
Fergus et al. 2006
;
Sun et al. 2015
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
; occlusion-robust object detection and segmentation
(
Zhan et al. 2022
;
Qi et al. 2022
)
; rotation-robust object and text detection
(
Saxena et al. 2009
;
Yao et al. 2012
;
Ma et al. 2018
)
) and, for instance, use these values in regression analysis similar to ours.

Finally, our experiment focused on VLMs and data with a U.S. and English-speaking bias. Their performance would likely be lower if we studied the same VLMs with BLV people’s product photos in a non-English-speaking country. Previous research has identified cross-cultural bias as a significant limitation of VLMs perceived by BLV users
(
Alharbi et al. 2024b
)
. Future work should consider how well the VLMs we studied perform in cross-cultural contexts and may also explore other open-source models that explicitly train on other languages (e.g., Qwen
10
10

10

https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen
or Deepseek
11
11

11

https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3
with Chinese products).

6.
Conclusion

As blind and low-vision (BLV) people increasingly rely on Vision-Language Model (VLM)-based tools to generate image captions about products, we need a more nuanced understanding of how these systems handle image quality issues common in BLV people’s photographs. Our survey of 86 BLV people reveals their perspectives on understanding image quality issues and errors when using VLM-based tools for product captioning, and the difficulties BLV people have in recovering from those errors. We then construct a dataset of 1,859 images taken by BLV people (729 high-quality, 1,130 low-quality images that are blurred, misframed, or rotated) with detailed product annotations—including product type (e.g., soup), brand (e.g., Campbell’s), and variety (e.g., tomato, low-sodium)—and evaluate four different VLMs on it. We find that all models drop in product identification when image quality issues are present, with performance dropping further with multiple issues. Moreover, we show how each VLM can be more or less susceptible to the studied image quality issues, suggesting ways to prioritize improving their performance. Making these tools reliable will require collaboration among HCI researchers, tool designers, and ML researchers. Together, we will need to revisit the datasets used to evaluate these models; improve model performance through fine-tuning or inference-time techniques, especially for privacy-preserving open-source models; and design systems to provide richer feedback on VLM errors.

Appendix A
Image Captioning Prompt for all VLMs

You are a helpful assistant who identifies products in images for blind and low-vision individuals. Identify the product in the image while following these guidelines:

1: Identify crucial features about the product, including:

— Object type (can, bag, plastic container, etc.)

— Product type (prepared or frozen meal, seasoning mix, soda, coffee)

— Brand (Heinz, Coca-Cola, Starbucks, etc.)

— Variety (specific flavors, sizes, count of items, etc.)

— Visual features (color, shape, size, etc.)

2: Use clear, direct, and objective language. Do not use vague adjectives like ‘large’ or ‘small’, or vague adverbs like ‘prominently’ or ‘clearly’.

3: DO NOT mention camera artifacts (e.g., blur) or if an object is partially visible.

4: DO NOT use introductory phrases (e.g., ‘The image shows’, ‘The object is’, ‘The primary object is’).
