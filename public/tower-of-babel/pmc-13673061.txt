Dysregulated Cholesterol Metabolism, Chronic Inflammation, and Disability in Severe Progressive Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Taylor R Wicks, Mujeeb Shittu, Anna Wolska, Diala Ghazal, Bianca Weinstock‐Guttman, Alexander Burnham, Alan T Remaley, Robert Zivadinov, Murali Ramanathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDysregulated lipid metabolism, which can affect remyelination and neural repair, has not been extensively investigated in progressive multiple sclerosis (PMS). The goal was to examine whether dysregulated cholesterol metabolism was associated with disability in PMS.MethodsThe study sample comprised 53 Severe PMS patients (Expanded Disability Status Scale, EDSS score: 7.0–9.5) requiring skilled nursing and 53 age, sex‐ and disease duration‐matched community‐dwelling Control PMS patients (EDSS score: 3.0–6.5). Patients underwent neurological examinations and provided blood samples. Cholesterol and apolipoprotein (Apo) profiles, nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy metabolomic profiles, inflammatory cytokines, neuroaxonal, and glial injury biomarkers were obtained. Disability was measured on the EDSS and the Combinatorial Weight‐Adjusted Disability (CombiWISE) scales.ResultsThe Severe PMS group had lower high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), ApoA‐I, and ApoA‐II levels (all p < 0.001) and higher ApoB and ApoE levels. The nutritional status biomarker, albumin, was lower, and the chronic inflammation marker, GlycA, was higher in Severe PMS versus Control PMS (all p < 0.001). Total cholesterol, ApoA‐II, and ApoB were associated with disability on the EDSS and CombiWISE scale. Difference‐correlogram analyses revealed substantial remodeling of biomarker interdependencies in Severe PMS. ApoE showed stronger associations with lipid and nutritional biomarkers in Severe PMS, but with neuroaxonal markers (serum neurofilament light chain, glial fibrillary acidic protein) in Control PMS. Inflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor, interleukin‐6) showed stronger associations with apolipoproteins in Severe PMS, whereas several lipid‐inflammation and lipid nutritional correlations were attenuated.ConclusionsDisrupted cholesterol metabolism and chronic inflammation are strongly linked to greater disability in Severe PMS. Our results also indicate marked alterations in metabolic, inflammatory, nutritional, and neuroglial biomarker networks in Severe PMS.Keywords: biomarkers, cholesterol, metabolic dysfunction, multiple sclerosis, progressive

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a disease of the central nervous system (CNS) defined by loss of blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity, demyelination, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration that causes physical, cognitive, and neurological disability. Approximately 10%–15% of people with MS have a progressive subtype (PMS) of the disease, comprising secondary‐progressive (SPMS) and primary‐progressive (PPMS) forms, where disability worsens without relapses or new MRI activity.

PMS can be further categorized by disability trajectory as early‐aggressive or late‐severe subtypes. Early‐aggressive PMS is marked by rapid disability progression from onset, with patients requiring walking assistance (Expanded Disability Status Scale, EDSS ≥ 6.0) within 10 years of onset. Late‐severe PMS is defined as disability accrual > 10 years from onset [1]. Early‐aggressive and late‐severe MS can be differentiated based on spinal cord involvement, while sharing global gray matter atrophy [1]. Research on patients with PMS is limited compared to relapsing forms of MS, with fewer effective treatment options for slowing disability progression. Therefore, it is important to consider other contributing factors, such as dysmetabolism.

Cholesterol is a central structural component of myelin that is crucial to the functioning and maintenance of neurons and astrocytes [2, 3]. Defects in cholesterol signaling and metabolism have been linked to impaired neuronal activity, leading to neurodegeneration of dendritic spines and synapses [4, 5]. Loss of BBB integrity during MS neuroinflammation can allow lipoproteins to enter the CNS, and the entry of low‐density lipoprotein‐cholesterol (LDL‐C) and its oxidation in the inflammatory milieu promotes the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), IL‐8, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) [6]. High‐density lipoprotein‐cholesterol (HDL‐C) has been shown to exert anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant effects by reducing BBB permeability through its promotion of cholesterol efflux. Apolipoprotein A‐I (ApoA‐I) exerts an inhibitory effect on cytokine‐induced endothelial cell activation [7], while the role of ApoA‐II remains unknown.

Dysregulation of lipid and cholesterol metabolism is associated with worsened outcomes in PMS. Low levels of HDL‐C and ApoA‐I were associated with greater gray matter and cortical volume loss, and conversion from RRMS to SPMS [8]. Higher serum very low‐density lipoprotein (VLDL) levels were associated with increased EDSS scores [9]. Increases in serum LDL‐C were positively correlated with contrast‐enhancing lesions, new or enlarging T2 lesions, and EDSS progression [9].

The objective of this study was to investigate the roles of lipids and cholesterol, their associations with disability, and to identify differences in crosstalk between metabolic, inflammatory, nutritional, and neuroglial biomarker networks in a cohort of early‐aggressive and late‐severe PMS.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

Study Setting

The patient groups and data in this study were collected from a prospective, cross‐sectional multi‐center study: the Comprehensive Assessment of Severely Affected Multiple Sclerosis (CASA‐MS). Recruitment for Control PMS occurred at the Jacobs Center for Multiple Sclerosis Treatment and Research at the University at Buffalo, Buffalo, NY, while patients with late‐severe PMS who were enrolled at The Boston Home, a skilled nursing facility in Dorchester, MA. We refer to the late‐severe PMS group as “Severe PMS” and the control group as “Control PMS”.

Patients were excluded if they could not provide informed consent, and/or if they had a relapse or received corticosteroids within 30 days of study enrollment.

The inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of MS based upon the 2017 McDonald criteria [10], 30–80 years of age, and an EDSS score of 3.0–6.5 for the Control PMS group, and 7.0–9.5 for the Severe PMS group to differentiate Severe MS. Both PMS groups were age‐, sex‐, and disease duration‐matched within ±2 years.

The University at Buffalo Human Subjects Institutional Review Board approved the study, and data were collected in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients provided written informed consent; an authorized legal healthcare proxy provided consent on the patient's behalf if the patient was unable to provide consent.

Study Participants

A Neurostatus‐certified researcher conducted the patient examination under the supervision of a board‐certified MS neurologist. Demographic, neurological, and clinical assessment information was collected. MS was classified according to the 2013 Lublin criteria [11].

The EDSS was utilized as a measure of disability. The Combinatorial Weight‐Adjusted Disability Score (CombiWISE), a combination of the EDSS, timed 25‐ft walk, Scripps Neurological Rating Scale (SNRS), and 9‐hole peg test, was also obtained [12].

Serum Sample Analysis

Patient blood samples were collected, separated into serum and plasma within 24 h of collection, and frozen at −80°C. Samples were de‐identified for analysts.

Cholesterol and Lipids

Total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides, HDL‐C, LDL‐C, apolipoproteins (Apo) ApoA‐I, ApoA‐II, ApoB, and ApoE levels were measured as previously published [13] on a Pentra 400 chemical analyzer utilizing immunoturbidometric diagnostic reagent kits, quality control materials, and calibrators (Kamiya Biomedical, Thousand Oaks, CA). All concentrations are reported in mg/dL.

Albumin and Prealbumin

Serum albumin and prealbumin levels were measured using immunoturbidimetry (Kamiya Biomedical, Thousand Oaks, CA). Albumin concentrations were expressed in g/dL, and prealbumin concentrations in mg/dL.

Neurofilament Light Chain (NfL), Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP), and Growth Differentiation Factor‐15 (GDF15)

Serum NfL (sNfL) and GFAP concentrations were obtained by utilizing a single‐molecule array (SIMOA) by collaborators at the University of Basel. GDF15 levels were measured using an immunoassay (R & D Systems Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA). All concentrations are expressed in pg/mL.

Integrative Metabolomics Measures

GlycA (a systemic inflammation marker corresponding to the number of N‐acetyl methyl groups on circulating plasma glycoproteins), lipoprotein particle size distributions, branched chain amino acids (valine, leucine, and isoleucine), and ketone body concentrations were measured via nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy on a Vantera analyzer using the LP4 algorithm (LabCorp, Madison, WI).

Small HDL particles (SHDLP) were defined as the sum of H3P (8.7 nm) + H2P (7.8 nm) + H1P (7.4 nm). Metabolite concentrations were expressed in μmol/L.

The Inflammatory Vulnerability Index (IVX), Metabolic‐Malnutrition Index (MMX), Diabetic Risk Index (DRI), and Metabolic Vulnerability Index (MVX), which are clinically validated integrative markers for interactions among lipid, carbohydrate, and branched‐chain amino acid metabolic pathways [14, 15, 16], were computed using the equations in Methods S1.

Cytokines

The proinflammatory cytokines TNF IL‐6, and the anabolic peptide hormone, insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF‐1), were measured using immunoassays (R & D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

Data Analysis

The R statistical computing platform (4.1.2) was used for statistical analyses and visualization.

Severe PMS status was an indicator variable taking the value of 1 for individuals in the Severe PMS group and 0 for the Control PMS group. A binary statin‐use variable was created based on patients' use of statin medications. Cholesterol, apolipoproteins, albumin, prealbumin, sNfL, GFAP, and GDF15 levels were logarithmically transformed (base 10).

All regression analyses included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers as predictors. The Severe PMS status variable was analyzed as the dependent variable in logistic regression. EDSS and CombiWISE disability measures were analyzed as dependent variables in a linear regression model that included an interaction term between the Severe PMS status variable and the biomarker of interest. Regression predictor coefficients, standard errors, and p‐values were extracted.

The ggplot2 package was used for plotting. The Spearman correlation matrices for the interdependencies among metabolic, inflammatory, nutritional, neuroaxonal, and glial biomarker variables were computed for the Control and Severe PMS groups. Differences in Spearman correlation values between groups were calculated. The difference correlogram was plotted using ggcorplot. The variables were organized using hierarchical clustering (with the default complete‐linkage agglomerative method) to facilitate the identification of patterns.

Bayesian network (BN) modeling was employed to assess directed associations and conditional dependencies among the biomarkers. Sex, age, BMI, Control versus Severe PMS status, and metabolic, inflammatory, nutritional, neuroaxonal, and glial biomarkers were included as predictors. EDSS was the target node. The derived variables, DRI, IVX, MMX, and MVX, were excluded.

The BN structure was learned using the hill‐climbing (HC) algorithm in the bnlearn R package, with the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) used to penalize model complexity [17]. A blacklist was applied to preclude edges into age and sex predictor variables. Arc strength was defined by the frequency of appearance, and the final consensus network structure was constructed with edges that exceeded an optimal inclusion threshold of 0.5.

Since this was an exploratory study with a small sample size, p‐values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The associations of ApoE with the neuroaxonal markers sNfL and GFAP were greater (difference in Spearman correlation (∆R = 0.73 and 0.45, respectively)) in the Control group. In contrast, the associations of ApoE with HDL cholesterol biomarkers, including TC (∆R = −0.51), HDL (∆R = −0.40), and ApoAII (∆R = −0.37), as well as albumin and prealbumin (∆R = −0.40 and −0.51, respectively), were greater in the Severe PMS group. The associations of ApoE with inflammatory biomarkers, including TNF, IL‐6, and GlycA, were not substantially changed.

The associations of GFAP with IVX and MVX were greater in the Control PMS compared to the Severe PMS. The associations of MVX with GlycA were also greater (∆R = −0.38) in the Severe PMS group.

The association of TNF with ApoA‐II was greater in the Severe PMS group (∆R = −0.33). IL‐6 associations with HDL (∆R = −0.32), ApoA‐I (∆R = −0.32), and ApoA‐II (∆R = −0.41) were greater in Severe PMS. The ApoA‐II‐GlycA association was greater (∆R = 0.42) in the Control PMS group, but the ApoA‐II associations with IL‐6, TNF, and ApoE were greater in Severe PMS. The associations of ApoA‐I with GlycA (∆R = 0.37) and SHDLP (∆R = 0.34), and of SHDLP with GlycA (∆R = 0.44), were greater in Control PMS.

These results indicate that there are consequential alterations in the interdependencies among key metabolic, inflammatory, nutritional, neuroaxonal, and glial biomarkers. Among the metabolic biomarkers, the associations of ApoE and ApoA‐II with the inflammatory biomarkers, TNF, IL‐6, and GlycA were notable. In the difference correlogram comparison between Control PMS and Severe MS, GFAP showed more altered associations with metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers than sNfL.

We used BN modeling to identify the patterns and candidate interactions among the biomarker associations with Severe MS status.

Severe PMS status was the major intermediary hub in the BN (Figure 4B) and the only parent node for EDSS, indicating that EDSS variance is explained by differences between the Severe and Control PMS groups.

Age was the primary driver of sNfL and GFAP. The cascade downstream of age included GDF15, which was linked to Severe PMS via albumin. This supports a key role for GDF15 in mediating the nexus between inflammation, nutritional, and metabolic status. The lipid/apolipoprotein networks contain separate LDL‐biomarker and HDL‐biomarker modules, with ApoB in the LDL module influencing ApoE. In the inflammatory cytokine module, TNF influenced both IL‐6 and IGF‐1. The covariation of TNF with IL‐6 is expected in systemic inflammation, and the effects on IGF‐1 could reflect modulation of anabolic metabolism. The Severe PMS status node was the parent node connected to chronic inflammation (GlycA), HDL, and LDL pathway biomarkers, and the inflammatory cytokine pathway via IGF‐1.

Effects of Statins on Biomarkers

Statin use was reported in 17 (32%) of Control PMS and 18 (34%) of Severe PMS. The patients were on one of the following medications: Atorvastatin, Pravastatin, Simvastatin, or Rosuvastatin. Figure 5 shows the associations of the metabolic, inflammatory, and neuroglial biomarkers with the statin use variable.

Figures A‐L show the effects of statin use in the Control PMS (blue region) and Progressive PMS (gray region) groups on total cholesterol (mg/dl), apolipoprotein B (ApoB, mg/dl), apolipoprotein E (ApoE, mg/dl), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C, mg/dl), apolipoprotein A‐I (ApoAI, mg/dl), apolipoprotein A‐II (ApoA‐II, mg/dl), albumin (g/dl), tumor necrosis factor (TNF, ng/ml), metabolic vulnerability index (MVX, no units), serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL, pg/ml), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, pg/ml), and Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS). The green bars correspond to those not on statins, and the magenta bars to the group on statins.

As expected, the statin‐use group had lower levels of LDL‐pathway biomarkers, including TC, LDL‐C, and ApoB, in the Severe PMS and Control PMS groups. We did not obtain evidence for statin effects on HDL‐C, ApoA‐I, and ApoA‐II (Figure 5D–F) or ApoE in either group.

We did not obtain evidence for inter‐group or intra‐group differences between statin use and albumin, TNF, MVX, sNfL, and GFAP (Figure 5G–K).

Statin use was not a significant modifier of EDSS in Control (p = 0.93) or Severe (p = 0.45) PMS (Figure 5L). We did not find any differences between statin use and CombiWISE in either group (results not shown).

Discussion

This research examined the role of dysregulated cholesterol metabolism in disability and the crosstalk among metabolic, inflammatory, nutritional, and neuroglial biomarker networks in PMS. The Severe PMS group had lower HDL‐C, ApoA‐I, ApoA‐II, and SHDLP levels, and higher ApoB and ApoE levels. Nutritional and inflammatory biomarkers differed, with lower albumin and higher GlycA and TNF in the Severe PMS cohort (p < 0.001). Integrative metabolomic indices (IVX, DRI, MVX) were greater in Severe PMS (p < 0.001). TNF levels were modestly higher (p = 0.016), whereas IL‐6 and IGF‐1 were unchanged. However, sNfL and GFAP were similar between groups. Intriguingly, GDF‐15, an emerging biomarker at the intersections of metabolism, inflammation, stress, and aging pathways, was higher in Severe PMS.

Regression analyses adjusted for age, sex, and BMI showed that cholesterol and apolipoprotein biomarkers (TC, ApoAII, ApoB, and LDL), as well as for SHDLP, IVX, MVX, TNF, and IL‐6, were associated with disability (EDSS and/or CombiWISE). Difference‐correlogram analyses revealed substantial remodeling of biomarker interdependencies in Severe PMS. Notably, ApoE showed stronger associations with lipid and nutritional biomarkers in Severe PMS but stronger associations with neuroaxonal markers (sNfL, GFAP) in Control PMS. Inflammatory cytokines (TNF, IL‐6) exhibited stronger associations with apolipoproteins in Severe PMS. Overall, these findings indicate marked alterations in metabolic, inflammatory, nutritional, and neuroglial biomarker networks in Severe PMS.

It is informative to consider any metabolomic findings in PMS in the context of the Phase 2 MS‐STAT (NCT00647348) and Phase 3 MS‐STAT2 (NCT03387670) trials [18, 19], which provided the initial clinical trial evidence that pharmacological targeting of cholesterol biosynthetic pathways could modify neurodegeneration in SPMS [20]. Both trials were randomized, placebo‐controlled studies of 80 mg (high dose) simvastatin, a hydroxymethylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG‐CoA) reductase inhibitor that blocks cholesterol synthesis. Simvastatin has high lipophilicity and is hydrophobic, allowing it to cross the BBB and exhibit more direct CNS effects than other statins [21]. MS‐STAT (n = 140, 70 per arm) reported a 43% reduction in brain atrophy in the simvastatin‐treated group vs. placebo, a potent atrophy‐slowing effect for PMS at the time [18]. The atrophy effects suggested that the lipid‐lowering, vascular, and other effects of simvastatin may be neuroprotective. MS‐STAT2 (n = 964), designed to confirm if simvastatin had an impact on disability progression, did not find differences in disability progression in the simvastatin‐treated group [19]. The clinico‐radiological disconnect between marked atrophy effects and disability progression raises fundamental issues regarding the causality of atrophy and the sensitivity of disability endpoints in SPMS.

In addition to the clinico‐radiological disconnect, the MS‐STAT results indicated a possible biomarker‐atrophy disconnect, as NfL in the simvastatin group was similar to placebo; higher sNfL was linked to greater atrophy progression [22]. This disconnect may be underpinned by the limitations of relying on a single, specific, and selective biomarker (sNfL) in PMS, where clinical outcomes, such as disability progression and cognition, are influenced by multiple pathways. Our multimodal approach of interrogating validated sentinel biomarkers, spanning diverse physiological pathways, in the context of Severe PMS represents a promising strategy for identifying new treatment approaches.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate marked alterations in metabolic, inflammatory, nutritional, and neuroglial biomarker networks in Severe PMS, and that disrupted cholesterol metabolism and chronic inflammation are associated with greater disability in Severe PMS.
