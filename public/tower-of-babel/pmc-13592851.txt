Soybean fermentation broth alleviates indomethacin-induced gastric mucosal injury
Authors: Hsiao-Chi Wang, Yue-Jhu Chen, Hui-Yun Tsai, Jue-Liang Hsu, Yu-Kuo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDietary strategies and plant-based fermented foods have attracted increasing interest for supporting gastrointestinal health, particularly under conditions of drug-induced mucosal stress. Soybean fermentation broth (SFB), a fermented soybean-derived product, may possess enhanced nutritional and functional properties through fermentation-associated generation of bioactive peptides and antioxidant metabolites. This study evaluated the gastroprotective effects of SFB in an indomethacin-induced gastric injury model in rats and investigated the underlying mechanisms. Indomethacin markedly induced gastric ulcer formation (16.61 ± 1.38 mm2), whereas SFB pretreatment significantly attenuated ulcer development in a dose-dependent manner, reducing ulcer area to 6.66 ± 0.98, 3.89 ± 0.63, and 1.46 ± 0.32 mm2 in low-, medium-, and high-dose groups, respectively (p < 0.05 vs. Vehicle). Histopathological examination revealed that SFB preserved epithelial integrity and reduced submucosal edema. SFB pretreatment significantly decreased lipid peroxidation, restored gastric glutathione content, and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, including glutathione peroxidase (GPx), glutathione reductase (GRd), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT). Furthermore, SFB suppressed pro-inflammatory mediator expression and favorably modulated cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) and COX-2 expression, accompanied by restoration of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) production. These findings indicate that SFB protects gastric mucosa against indomethacin-induced injury through coordinated regulation of oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and prostaglandin-mediated mucosal defense, supporting its potential as a functional fermented soybean product.Keywords: Antioxidant defense, Gastric mucosal injury, Indomethacin, Inflammatory signaling, Soybean fermentation broth

1. Introduction

Peptic ulcer disease (PUD), encompassing gastric and duodenal ulcers, remains a prevalent gastrointestinal disorder with a substantial tendency to recur and impose persistent clinical and socioeconomic burdens worldwide [1,2]. Beyond recurrent epigastric symptoms, PUD can lead to clinically significant outcomes such as upper gastrointestinal bleeding, perforation, hospitalization, and impaired quality of life. In many settings, early symptoms may be mild or non-specific, which can delay recognition and increase the risk of severe complications. The pathogenesis of gastric ulcer is primarily attributed to an imbalance between aggressive factors and mucosal defensive mechanisms, in which gastric acid, pepsin, oxidative stress, and inflammatory mediators overwhelm endogenous protective barriers. Major contributors include Helicobacter pylori infection, alcohol consumption, psychological stress, and medication exposure, with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) being among the most important factors [3]. Importantly, the global burden of PUD continues to evolve with changes in demographics, comorbidities, and medication use patterns, underscoring the need for preventive strategies alongside standard clinical management [4].

NSAIDs are among the most frequently used medications for pain, fever, and inflammatory conditions, yet gastrointestinal toxicity remains a major limitation of long-term use [5]. Indomethacin, a representative NSAID, is widely used in experimental and clinical contexts and is well recognized to induce gastric mucosal injury. Mechanistically, indomethacin compromises mucosal protection through cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibition and subsequent suppression of protective prostaglandin synthesis, which in turn weakens mucus/bicarbonate secretion, mucosal blood flow, and epithelial restitution. Moreover, indomethacin can aggravate mucosal injury by promoting leukocyte–endothelial interactions, neutrophil infiltration, oxidative stress, and inflammatory cascades that collectively amplify tissue damage [6]. These processes increase epithelial permeability, impair repair capacity, and accelerate ulcer development, making indomethacin-induced injury a clinically relevant model for investigating NSAID-associated gastric toxicity. Given the multi-factorial nature of NSAID-related mucosal damage, interventions that simultaneously target oxidative stress and inflammation are of particular interest as complementary preventive approaches [7].

Gastric mucosal integrity is maintained by coordinated defensive systems, including the mucus-bicarbonate-phospholipid barrier, epithelial restitution and renewal, mucosal microcirculation, and endogenous mediators such as prostaglandins and nitric oxide [8]. Among these mediators, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) plays a pivotal role in sustaining mucosal blood flow, stimulating mucus and bicarbonate secretion, and supporting barrier function, thereby limiting acid back-diffusion and promoting mucosal homeostasis [9]. Disruption of these protective networks, particularly under NSAID exposure, shifts the balance toward injury by facilitating oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling, which further compromise epithelial integrity. Although proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are commonly used to prevent or treat NSAID-associated ulceration, concerns have been raised regarding safety considerations and potential risks associated with prolonged acid-suppressive therapy [10]. Consequently, there is increasing interest in dietary and functional-food strategies that may support mucosal defenses and reduce NSAID-related gastric injury risk, either as adjunctive measures or as preventive options in individuals with recurrent exposure [11].

Soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merrill] is a widely consumed food crop and an important plant-based protein source, and soybean-derived products have been associated with multiple health-related bioactivities [12]. Importantly, fermentation can enhance the functional value of soybean products by modifying the food matrix and generating fermentation-derived components, potentially improving overall bioactivity and bioaccessibility [13,14]. While soybeans are mainly processed into vegetable oil and animal feed in Western countries, they are widely consumed as fermented foods in many Asian regions, such as soy milk, soy sauce, miso, and fermented soybean pastes. Fermentation promotes protein hydrolysis and the generation of low-molecular-weight peptides and aglycone forms of isoflavones, which may improve bioavailability and contribute to antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities [15,16]. Soybean fermentation broth (SFB) is a fermented soybean-derived preparation produced through mixed microbial fermentation and subsequent processing, and may contain fermentation-generated metabolites relevant to redox regulation and inflammatory control, suggesting promise for gastrointestinal protection [17]. In addition to gastrointestinal applications, soybean-based fermented products containing mixed probiotic cultures have also demonstrated biological activities related to tissue protection and repair. A recent clinical study reported that topical application of probiotics in a soybean-based concentrate significantly promoted healing in patients with diabetic foot ulcers, supporting the functional relevance of fermented soybean matrices in modulating oxidative stress, inflammation, and tissue integrity [18]. However, evidence remains limited regarding the protective efficacy of SFB against NSAID-induced gastric mucosal injury and its relationship to oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and mucosal defense mediators, particularly within a well-characterized indomethacin injury model. Therefore, this study investigated the gastroprotective effects of SFB in rats with indomethacin-induced gastric mucosal injury, focusing on ulcer formation and histopathological changes, oxidative stress and antioxidant defense (e.g., lipid peroxidation and glutathione-related systems), inflammatory responses, and COX/PGE2-associated mucosal defense pathways.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Materials, chemicals, and reagents

Soybean fermentation broth (SFB) was kindly provided by Bao Geng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (New Taipei, Taiwan). Basically, SFB was prepared by fermenting a soybean-based liquid medium with a consortium of probiotic microorganisms. Non-defatted, non-genetically modified soybeans were ground and mixed with water at a ratio of 1:16 (w/v). Brown sugar and mineral salts were added, and the mixture was heated to obtain a soybean liquid medium without filtration, which was used for microbial cultivation. The probiotic consortium included species from the Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Bacillus, Lactococcus, and Streptococcus genera. Each strain was first cultured individually in liquid medium, followed by scale-up cultivation using the soybean liquid medium. Subsequently, all strains were combined and subjected to final mixed fermentation in the soybean medium. Fermentation was conducted at 38 °C for more than 168 h. After fermentation, the broth was heat-sterilized, concentrated under reduced pressure, and freeze-dried to obtain SFB powder. The lyophilized powder was stored under appropriate conditions until use and freshly reconstituted in distilled water prior to oral administration.

Indomethacin, phosphotungstic acid, pyrogallol, sodium acetate, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), thiobarbituric acid, and other general analytical reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Reduced and oxidized glutathione (GSH and GSSG) and 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene were obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Methanol and inorganic salts used for buffer preparation, including sodium chloride (NaCl), potassium chloride (KCl), sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3), and phosphate salts, were purchased from J.T. Baker Chemical Co. (Center Valley, PA, USA). Anchor skim milk powder (Fonterra Co., Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand) was used for membrane blocking in Western blot analysis. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) test strips (REF77170) were purchased from Arkray Co., Ltd. (Kyoto, Japan). The standard laboratory rodent diet (5001) was obtained from Young Li Co., Ltd. (New Taipei, Taiwan). All other chemicals and solvents used in this study were of analytical grade and obtained from standard commercial suppliers.

2.2. Animals and indomethacin-induced gastric injury model

Male Wistar rats (6 weeks old, body weight 176–200 g) were obtained from BioLASCO Taiwan Co., Ltd. (Taipei, Taiwan). Animals were housed under controlled environmental conditions (temperature 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity 50–70%, and a 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to standard laboratory chow and water. After a one-week acclimatization period, rats were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 6 per group) using body weight-based stratified randomization to ensure comparable mean body weights among groups: (1) Control group; (2) Vehicle group; (3) low-dose SFB group (SFBL, 0.2 g/kg); (4) medium-dose SFB group (SFBM, 0.4 g/kg); (5) high-dose SFB group (SFBH, 1.0 g/kg); and (6) omeprazole-treated group (OME, 30 mg/kg). The selected SFB doses were based on preliminary tolerability observations and practical intake considerations for functional food applications. Rats in the Control and Vehicle groups received distilled water, whereas rats in the treatment groups received SFB or omeprazole, once daily by oral gavage for seven consecutive days. On day 6, animals were fasted for 24 h in metabolic cages with free access to water and were closely monitored for general health conditions. On day 7, 2 h after the final administration of vehicle, SFB, or omeprazole, gastric mucosal injury was induced by oral administration of indomethacin (100 mg/kg), except for the Control group, according to doses used in previous studies, including our prior work [19,20]. Six hours after indomethacin challenge, rats were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation until cessation of cardiac activity. Blood samples and stomach tissues were collected for subsequent biochemical, histological, and molecular analyses. The liver, kidney, and spleen were excised and weighed to assess potential effects on organ weights. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of National Pingtung University of Science and Technology (IACUC approval no. NPUST-109-119).

2.3. Serum biochemical analysis

To evaluate potential systemic effects and safety of SFB administration, serum biochemical parameters were assessed. At the end of the experimental period, blood samples were collected from rats and allowed to clot at room temperature, followed by centrifugation to obtain serum. Serum samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis. Prior to measurement, frozen serum samples and ALT test strips were equilibrated to room temperature. An appropriate volume of serum was then applied to the reaction well of the test strip, and serum ALT activity was analyzed using a dry chemistry automatic analyzer (SPOTCHEM EZ SP-4430, Arkray, Kyoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ALT levels were recorded as an indicator of hepatic function and systemic safety.

2.4. Assessment of gastric mucosal injury and ulcer area

After sacrifice, the stomach was immediately excised by cutting at the junctions of the cardia and pylorus. The stomach was opened along the greater curvature, gently rinsed with ice-cold 0.9% saline to remove gastric contents, and spread flat on a grid-lined board. Gross images of the gastric mucosa were captured using a digital camera for macroscopic evaluation of mucosal injury. Gastric ulcer areas were quantified from the digital images using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The total ulcerated area (mm2) was measured for each stomach. Portions designated for biochemical and molecular analyses were snapfrozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C, whereas the remaining tissues were processed for histopathological analysis.

2.5. Histopathological examination

For histopathological evaluation, gastric tissues collected at the 6 h time point after indomethacin administration were processed according to a modified protocol based on our previous study [20]. Briefly, representative portions of the stomach were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, thoroughly rinsed with tap water, dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 4–5 μm, mounted on glass slides, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using standard procedures. The stained sections were examined under a light microscope equipped with a digital imaging system, and representative histological images were captured to assess gastric mucosal architecture, epithelial integrity, and submucosal alterations.

2.6. Measurement of lipid peroxidation

Lipid peroxidation in gastric tissues was assessed by measuring malondialdehyde (MDA) levels using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) method. Briefly, gastric tissues were homogenized in ice-cold phosphate buffer containing potassium phosphate (K2HPO4 and KH2PO4) and KCl, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant. Protein concentrations were determined using a Coomassie protein assay with bovine serum albumin (BSA, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as the standard. For TBARS analysis, tissue supernatants were mixed with sodium dodecyl sulfate and phosphotungstic acid to precipitate proteins, followed by acidification and reaction with thiobarbituric acid under high-temperature acidic conditions. After heating, the reaction mixtures were cooled, extracted with n-butanol, and centrifuged. The fluorescence intensity of the organic phase was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer with excitation at 515 nm and emission at 555 nm. MDA concentrations were calculated from a standard curve generated using 1,1,3,3-tetramethoxypropane and normalized to protein content. Results were expressed as nmol MDA per mg protein.

2.7. Determination of glutathione status and antioxidant enzyme activities

The gastric antioxidant defense system was evaluated by measuring total glutathione (GSH + GSSG), oxidized glutathione (GSSG), and the activities of glutathione peroxidase (GPx), glutathione reductase (GRd), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT). Gastric tissues were homogenized in ice-cold buffer and centrifuged to obtain clear supernatants. Total glutathione and GSSG levels were determined using a colorimetric assay based on the reaction between GSH and 5,5′-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB), with absorbance measured at 412 nm. For GSSG determination, samples were pretreated with a glutathione scavenger prior to analysis. Glutathione concentrations were calculated using GSH standards supplied with the assay kit (Cat. No. E-BC-K097-M, Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA).

Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity was determined using a commercial assay kit (Cat. No. E-BCK096-M, Elabscience), based on monitoring the consumption of GSH during the enzymatic reduction of peroxide substrates. Residual GSH was quantified via its reaction with 5,5′-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB), and absorbance was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Glutathione reductase (GRd) activity was assessed using a colorimetric assay kit (Cat. No. E-BC-K099-S, Elabscience), which measures the NADPH-dependent reduction of GSSG to GSH. GRd activity was calculated by monitoring the decrease in NADPH absorbance at 340 nm. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured using the pyrogallol autoxidation method, as previously described, based on the ability of SOD to inhibit superoxide-mediated pyrogallol oxidation under alkaline conditions. The rate of inhibition was monitored spectrophotometrically at 420 nm. Catalase (CAT) activity was determined using a commercial assay kit (Cat. No. 707002, Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA), which quantifies the formation of formaldehyde generated from the reaction between hydrogen peroxide and methanol. The resulting chromogenic product was measured colorimetrically at 540 nm following the manufacturer’s protocol. For all enzyme assays, protein concentrations were determined using a Coomassie protein assay with BSA as the standard. Enzyme activities were normalized to protein content and expressed as units per milligram of protein.

2.8. Prostaglandin E2 assay

Gastric mucosal prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) levels were quantified using a competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Cat. No. 500141, Cayman Chemical), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, gastric tissues were homogenized in ice-cold phosphate buffer containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and indomethacin to prevent ex vivo prostaglandin synthesis. Tissue homogenates were centrifuged at 8000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected for analysis. Protein concentrations were determined using a Coomassie protein assay with BSA as the standard. PGE2 concentrations were measured based on competitive binding between endogenous PGE2 in the samples and a PGE2-acetylcholinesterase (AChE) tracer for a limited amount of PGE2-specific monoclonal antibody. After incubation, Ellman’s reagent was added to generate a yellow chromogenic product, and absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a microplate reader. PGE2 levels were calculated from a standard curve generated with PGE2 standards provided in the kit and normalized to protein content.

2.9. Western blot analysis

Gastric tissues were homogenized in ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Homogenization was performed under cold conditions to minimize protein degradation. The homogenates were centrifuged at 16,400×g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected, re-centrifuged under the same conditions, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Protein concentrations were determined using a Coomassie protein assay with BSA as the standard. Equal amounts of protein were mixed with loading buffer, heat-denatured, and separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Proteins were then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Buckinghamshire, UK). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk prepared in phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; Cat. No. 4841), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2; Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 12282), and nuclear factor-κB p65 (NF-κB p65; Cell Signaling Technology; Cat. No. 8242). Primary antibodies against inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA; Cat. No. 18985-1-AP), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α; Proteintech; Cat. No. 17590-1-AP), interleukin-6 (IL-6; Proteintech; Cat. No. 26404-1-AP), and β-actin (Proteintech; Cat. No. 81115-1-RR) were also used. β-Actin served as the internal loading control. After washing with PBST, membranes were incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies, including anti-rabbit IgG (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany; Cat. No. AP187P) and anti-mouse IgG (Merck Millipore; Cat. No. 12–349), for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagent (Amersham™ ECL Select™, GE Healthcare Life Sciences) and captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to β-actin expression.

2.10. Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) or mean ± standard deviation (SD), as indicated in the figure legends. Data obtained from animal experiments were expressed as mean ± SEM with a sample size of six animals per group (n = 6). Quantitative data derived from Western blot analyses were expressed as mean ± SD from three independent experiments (n = 3). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM SPSS Statistics, Armonk, NY, USA). Differences among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for post hoc comparisons. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the figures, different lowercase letters indicate statistically significant differences between groups.

3. Results

3.1. Effects of SFB on gastric ulcer formation in indomethacin-treated rats

Prior to ulcer induction, the general physiological condition of the animals was monitored throughout the experimental period. Body weight increased steadily in all groups, with no significant differences observed among treatments (Fig. S1 (https://doi.org/10.38212/2224-6614.3601)). Similarly, absolute and relative organ weights (liver, kidney, and spleen) showed no significant intergroup differences (Table S1 (https://doi.org/10.38212/2224-6614.3601)), and no gross abnormalities were detected at necropsy. To further evaluate the systemic safety of SFB administration, serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were measured at the end of the experiment. As shown in Fig. 1A, ALT levels did not differ significantly among groups (p > 0.05), indicating that SFB pretreatment did not induce detectable hepatic toxicity under the experimental conditions. Following indomethacin challenge (100 mg/kg), the Vehicle group exhibited extensive gastric mucosal injury characterized by prominent hemorrhagic lesions and linear ulcerative streaks (Fig. 1B). These macroscopic features are consistent with the indomethacin-induced gastric injury pattern observed in our previous study [19], confirming the reliability of the ulcer induction model used in the present experiment. In contrast, SFB-pretreated groups displayed visibly attenuated mucosal damage in a dose-dependent manner. Omeprazole (OME) treatment also markedly reduced ulcer formation. Quantitative analysis of ulcer area (Table 1) revealed a substantial increase in ulceration in the Vehicle group (16.61 ± 1.38 mm2) compared with the Control group (p < 0.05). Pretreatment with SFB significantly reduced ulcer area to 6.66 ± 0.98, 3.89 ± 0.63, and 1.46 ± 0.32 mm2 at doses of 0.2, 0.4, and 1.0 g/kg, respectively (p < 0.05 vs. Vehicle). The highest SFB dose produced a protective effect comparable to that of the OME group. Consistently, the preventive index increased dose-dependently in SFB-treated groups (59.87 ± 5.92%, 76.56 ± 3.82%, and 91.21 ± 1.94%, respectively), indicating substantial gastroprotective efficacy against indomethacin-induced gastric mucosal injury. To preliminarily characterize the composition of SFB, HPLC profiling followed by LC-MS/MS analysis was performed, and the results are presented in Supplementary Table S2 (https://doi.org/10.38212/2224-6614.3601). The analysis identified soybean storage proteins, including glycinin and peptides derived from the 11S globulin G5 subunit, suggesting the presence of fermentation-modified soybean protein components.

3.2. Effects of SFB on gastric histopathological changes

Histopathological alterations in gastric mucosa were evaluated using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 2). As shown in Fig. 2A (low magnification), the Control group exhibited intact mucosal architecture with well-organized gastric glands and a continuous epithelial lining, consistent with normal gastric histological features reported in healthy rodents. In contrast, the Vehicle group demonstrated extensive mucosal damage, including large areas of epithelial disruption and submucosal swelling, which are characteristic morphological features of NSAID-induced gastric injury [21]. Higher-magnification images (Fig. 2B) further revealed marked epithelial cell loss, inflammatory cell infiltration, and prominent submucosal edema in the Vehicle group, indicating severe mucosal barrier disruption and inflammatory activation following indomethacin administration. Pretreatment with SFB attenuated these pathological changes, with greater preservation of mucosal structure observed at higher doses. The SFBL group showed partial preservation of epithelial structure with reduced inflammatory infiltration, whereas the SFBM group exhibited moderate restoration of mucosal integrity. Notably, the SFBH group displayed near-normal mucosal architecture, with minimal epithelial erosion and inflammatory infiltration, comparable to that observed in the OME group. These morphological improvements suggest that SFB may preserve mucosal integrity by limiting inflammatory cell recruitment and maintaining epithelial barrier stability under oxidative and inflammatory stress conditions [22]. Overall, SFB pretreatment markedly alleviated indomethacin-induced histopathological damage, as evidenced by improved epithelial integrity and reduced inflammatory changes.

3.3. Effects of SFB on oxidative stress and antioxidant defense in gastric mucosa

To further elucidate whether the gastroprotective effect of SFB is associated with modulation of oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation and key components of the gastric antioxidant defense system were evaluated in indomethacin-treated rats. As shown in Fig. 3A, indomethacin administration markedly increased gastric malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in the Vehicle group compared with the Control group (p < 0.05), indicating enhanced lipid peroxidation and oxidative injury in the gastric mucosa. This finding is consistent with previous reports demonstrating that NSAID-induced gastric injury is closely associated with excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and subsequent lipid peroxidation in gastric tissue [23,24]. Pretreatment with SFB significantly attenuated this elevation in a dose-dependent manner. Rats receiving 0.2, 0.4, and 1.0 g/kg SFB exhibited progressively lower MDA concentrations compared with the Vehicle group (p < 0.05). Notably, the highest SFB dose reduced MDA levels to values comparable to those observed in the Control and OME groups, suggesting a substantial suppression of lipid peroxidation. The glutathione redox system was subsequently assessed. As presented in Table 2, total glutathione (T-GSH) content was significantly decreased in the Vehicle group following indomethacin challenge (p < 0.05), reflecting impaired redox homeostasis. Depletion of gastric glutathione has been recognized as a hallmark of NSAID-induced oxidative damage and is closely associated with increased mucosal susceptibility to injury [5]. SFB pretreatment restored T-GSH levels in a dose-dependent manner. In particular, the SFBM and SFBH groups showed significant increases compared with the Vehicle group (p < 0.05), approaching levels observed in the Control group. Although oxidized glutathione (GSSG) levels exhibited a decreasing trend in the SFB-treated groups without reaching statistical significance, reduced glutathione (GSH) levels were significantly elevated in the SFBM and SFBH groups compared with the Vehicle group (p < 0.05). Consistently, the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 3B), which serves as an indicator of intracellular redox balance [25], was markedly reduced in the Vehicle group (p < 0.05). SFB administration significantly elevated the GSH/GSSG ratio relative to the Vehicle group (p < 0.05), with the highest dose demonstrating the most pronounced effect. These findings indicate that SFB effectively preserved glutathione redox status in gastric tissue subjected to indomethacin-induced oxidative stress.

In addition to non-enzymatic antioxidant components, enzymatic antioxidant activities were also significantly affected by indomethacin treatment. As shown in Fig. 3C–F, the activities of glutathione peroxidase (GPx), glutathione reductase (GRd), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) were significantly decreased in the Vehicle group compared with the Control group (p < 0.05). Pretreatment with SFB significantly restored GPx activity in a dose-dependent manner (p < 0.05), with the SFBM and SFBH groups exhibiting activities comparable to those of the Control and OME groups. Similarly, GRd activity, which was markedly suppressed in the Vehicle group, was significantly elevated by SFB administration, particularly at 0.4 and 1.0 g/kg (p < 0.05). Furthermore, SOD activity was significantly reduced in the Vehicle group but progressively increased with SFB pretreatment (p < 0.05). The highest SFB dose restored SOD activity to a level not significantly different from that of the Control group. A comparable pattern was observed for CAT activity, which was significantly decreased by indomethacin (p < 0.05) and dose-dependently enhanced by SFB administration, with the high-dose group showing activity similar to that of the OME group. Restoration of endogenous antioxidant enzyme activities, including GPx, GRd, SOD, and CAT, has been reported to play a critical role in protecting gastric mucosa against oxidative injury induced by NSAIDs and other stressors [26]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that indomethacin-induced gastric injury was accompanied by enhanced lipid peroxidation and impaired antioxidant defense. SFB pretreatment effectively reduced oxidative damage and restored both enzymatic and nonenzymatic antioxidant systems in a dose-dependent manner, indicating that reinforcement of antioxidant capacity may contribute to its protective effect against gastric mucosal injury.

3.4. Effects of SFB on inflammatory responses in gastric mucosa

Indomethacin administration markedly activated inflammatory signaling in gastric tissue. As shown in Fig. 4A, NF-κB protein expression was significantly elevated in the Vehicle group compared with the Control group (p < 0.05), indicating activation of pro-inflammatory transcriptional regulation following NSAID challenge. NF-κB is a central transcription factor that regulates the expression of multiple inflammatory mediators, and its activation is commonly associated with amplification of mucosal inflammatory injury [27]. Pretreatment with SFB reduced NF-κB expression in a dose-dependent manner. The SFBM and SFBH groups exhibited significantly lower NF-κB levels compared with the Vehicle group (p < 0.05), suggesting effective suppression of indomethacin-induced inflammatory activation. Consistent with NF-κB upregulation, indomethacin markedly increased the protein expression of downstream inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (Fig. 4B). These proinflammatory proteins were significantly elevated in the Vehicle group relative to the Control group (p < 0.05), reflecting an enhanced inflammatory state in the gastric mucosa. SFB pretreatment attenuated TNF-α expression in a dose-dependent manner, with significant reductions observed in the SFBM and SFBH groups (p < 0.05 vs. Vehicle). Similarly, IL-6 expression was reduced following SFB administration, particularly at medium and high doses. Indomethacin also significantly elevated iNOS protein levels, which are associated with excessive nitric oxide production under inflammatory conditions. Although constitutive nitric oxide plays a protective role in maintaining gastric mucosal integrity, overproduction of NO via iNOS may contribute to oxidative and inflammatory damage [28]. SFB pretreatment significantly downregulated iNOS expression compared with the Vehicle group (p < 0.05), indicating attenuation of inflammation-associated nitric oxide overproduction. Overall, these results demonstrate that indomethacin-induced gastric injury was accompanied by activation of NF-κB signaling and upregulation of multiple pro-inflammatory mediators. SFB pretreatment effectively suppressed NF-κB activation and reduced the expression of TNF-α, IL-6, and iNOS in a dose-dependent manner. These findings indicate that modulation of inflammatory signaling pathways contributes to the gastroprotective effect of SFB against indomethacin-induced gastric mucosal injury.

3.5. Effects of SFB on COX expression and PGE2 production in gastric mucosa

Indomethacin-induced gastric injury was accompanied by marked alterations in cyclooxygenase expression and disruption of prostaglandin synthesis in gastric tissue. As shown in Fig. 5A, COX-1 protein expression was significantly decreased in the Vehicle group compared with the Control group (p < 0.05). Given that COX-1 is constitutively expressed in gastric mucosa and plays a pivotal role in maintaining basal prostaglandin production, its suppression indicates impairment of physiological mucosal defense mechanisms following NSAID exposure [29]. Pretreatment with SFB dose-dependently restored COX-1 expression, with the SFBM and SFBH groups showing significant recovery relative to the Vehicle group (p < 0.05). The restoration of COX-1 suggests preservation of the enzymatic machinery responsible for protective prostaglandin synthesis. In contrast, COX-2 expression (Fig. 5B) was markedly upregulated in the Vehicle group compared with the Control group (p < 0.05), reflecting activation of inflammation-associated signaling pathways in response to mucosal injury. COX-2 is typically expressed at low levels under physiological conditions but is induced during inflammatory responses [30]. SFB administration significantly attenuated COX-2 overexpression in a dose-dependent manner. The medium- and high-dose SFB groups exhibited significantly lower COX-2 levels compared with the Vehicle group (p < 0.05), indicating suppression of inflammation-associated cyclooxygenase induction. Consistent with these alterations in cyclooxygenase isoforms, gastric PGE2 levels (Fig. 5C) were significantly reduced in the Vehicle group compared with the Control group (p < 0.05), demonstrating impairment of mucosal prostaglandin synthesis following indomethacin challenge. Because PGE2 is a key mediator that enhances mucus and bicarbonate secretion, maintains mucosal blood flow, and supports epithelial restitution, its reduction reflects compromised gastric barrier function [31]. SFB pretreatment significantly restored PGE2 production in a dose-dependent manner (p < 0.05). Notably, the SFBH group exhibited a significant recovery of PGE2 levels compared with the Vehicle group (p < 0.05), indicating effective restoration of prostaglandin-mediated mucosal defense. In contrast, the OME group showed only a modest, non-significant increasing trend in PGE2 levels compared with the Vehicle group. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that indomethacin-induced gastric mucosal injury was characterized by suppression of constitutive COX-1, induction of inflammatory COX-2, and consequent reduction of protective PGE2 production. SFB pretreatment rebalanced cyclooxygenase expression by restoring COX-1 while suppressing COX-2, accompanied by recovery of gastric PGE2 levels. These coordinated regulatory effects on the COX/PGE2 axis likely contribute to preservation of mucosal integrity and represent an important mechanism underlying the gastroprotective action of SFB.

4. Discussion

To explore the potential bioactive basis underlying the gastroprotective activity of soybean fermentation broth (SFB), we first conducted a preliminary compositional characterization using HPLC profiling, followed by LC-MS/MS identification of major components. The LC-MS/MS results indicated that SFB predominantly contained soybean storage proteins, notably glycinin (11S globulin), including peptides mapped to the G5 subunit. This compositional feature is meaningful because accumulating evidence suggests that gastrointestinal digestion, enzymatic hydrolysis, food processing, and fermentation can markedly reduce anti-nutritional factors in soybean protein matrices while promoting the generation of bioactive peptides [32]. In parallel, soybean-derived peptides have been discussed as physiologically relevant molecules that may become bioavailable after digestion and subsequently modulate tissue responses [33]. Therefore, the enrichment of glycinin-related sequences/peptides in SFB supports the plausibility that fermentation-derived soybean protein components may represent potential contributors, at least in part, to the biological effects observed in vivo. The generation of these peptide-enriched components may be mechanistically related to the design of the multi-genus probiotic consortium used in the fermentation process. The multi-genus probiotic consortium used in this study was designed to integrate complementary fermentation functions. Lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus, Lactococcus, and Streptococcus) can rapidly acidify the soybean medium and promote proteolysis-related biochemical conversion [34], whereas Bacillus species are well known for strong extracellular protease activity that facilitates the breakdown of soybean storage proteins into low-molecular weight peptides [35]. Such mixed-culture fermentation has been reported to broaden metabolite diversity and enhance the generation of bioactive peptides and antioxidant-related components in fermented soybean products [36,37]. Consistent with this rationale, our LC-MS/MS profiling indicated enrichment of soybean storage protein-derived peptides (e.g., glycinin-related sequences), which may represent potential contributors to the observed restoration of the gastric antioxidant defense system and attenuation of inflammatory signaling in indomethacin-challenged rats. Before focusing on gastric protection, it is important to clarify the systemic tolerance of SFB under our experimental conditions. During the pretreatment period, animals exhibited steady weight gain without intergroup differences, and organ weights (absolute and relative) remained comparable among groups, with no gross abnormalities observed at necropsy. In addition, serum ALT, commonly used as a sensitive indicator of hepatocellular injury, did not differ significantly among groups. Because ALT increases are often associated with hepatocyte damage and correlate with histopathological alterations in toxicity settings [38], the absence of ALT elevation suggests that SFB pretreatment did not induce detectable hepatotoxicity in this short-term model. Collectively, these findings provide supportive safety context for interpreting the subsequent gastroprotective outcomes.

Indomethacin is a widely used NSAID with well-recognized gastrointestinal adverse effects, and acute high-dose indomethacin models reliably reproduce gastric mucosal injury characterized by hemorrhagic lesions and linear ulcerative streaks. Mechanistically, NSAID-induced injury is typically linked to suppression of prostaglandin synthesis, impairment of mucus/bicarbonate secretion, microcirculatory disturbance, and recruitment/activation of inflammatory cells, ultimately amplifying mucosal damage [39]. In our study, indomethacin challenge produced extensive macroscopic injury and a marked increase in ulcer area in the Vehicle group. In contrast, SFB pretreatment reduced lesion severity and ulcer area in a clear dose-related pattern, with the high-dose SFB group exhibiting protection comparable to omeprazole (OME). When combined with the preventive index results, these macroscopic outcomes support that SFB possesses substantial gastroprotective efficacy against indomethacin-induced gastric mucosal injury, consistent with patterns reported for other candidate gastroprotective agents in similar NSAID ulcer paradigms [40]. The histopathological observations further reinforced the macroscopic findings. While the Control group showed intact glandular organization, a continuous epithelial lining, and no obvious pathological abnormalities, indomethacin caused severe gastric mucosal damage characterized by prominent epithelial cell loss, mucosal and submucosal edema, inflammatory cell infiltration, hemorrhagic lesions, and focal necrotic changes. These pathological features are consistent with typical NSAID-induced mucosal injury [41]. Importantly, SFB pretreatment attenuated these abnormalities in a dose-dependent manner, as evidenced by improved epithelial continuity, reduced edema, decreased inflammatory cell infiltration, and less severe hemorrhagic and necrotic damage. The high-dose SFB group showed relatively well-preserved mucosal architecture, comparable to that observed in the OME group. These findings further support the protective effect of SFB against indomethacin-induced gastric histopathological injury. Future studies incorporating standardized histopathological scoring systems would further strengthen the objective evaluation of tissue injury severity and therapeutic protection. These tissue-level improvements further support the concept that limiting inflammatory cell accumulation and maintaining epithelial barrier integrity are pivotal to restraining lesion expansion during NSAID exposure. In this regard, recent evidence showed that dietary wheat peptides alleviated NSAID-induced intestinal epithelial injury by suppressing NF-κB/MAPK activation and improving tight junction-related barrier integrity, highlighting a peptide-related mechanism framework that aligns well with our observations in gastric tissue [27]. Although our model focuses on gastric mucosa rather than small intestine, both contexts underscore the importance of epithelial barrier preservation under NSAID-associated oxidative/inflammatory stress.

Oxidative stress is a central contributor to NSAID-associated mucosal injury. Excess reactive oxygen species promote lipid peroxidation and damage membrane integrity, with malondialdehyde (MDA) commonly used as a terminal lipid peroxidation marker [42]. In our study, indomethacin significantly elevated gastric MDA levels, indicating pronounced oxidative injury, whereas SFB pretreatment lowered MDA in a dose-dependent manner and normalized MDA at the highest dose to levels comparable to Control/OME. These findings indicate that SFB can effectively attenuate indomethacin-induced lipid peroxidation, which is relevant because oxidative membrane damage can exacerbate epithelial disruption and delay mucosal repair. Beyond lipid peroxidation, the glutathione system represents a core non-enzymatic defense that maintains redox homeostasis. Indomethacin challenge markedly reduced total glutathione (TGSH) and shifted the glutathione pool toward a more oxidized state, consistent with impaired redox balance. SFB pretreatment increased T-GSH and significantly elevated reduced GSH at medium/high doses, while oxidized GSH (GSSG) displayed a downward trend. Concordantly, the GSH/GSSG ratio, an integrative indicator of intracellular redox status, was restored by SFB in a dose-responsive manner. Such preservation of glutathione redox status is biologically meaningful because GSH availability supports detoxification of peroxides and limits redox-driven amplification of inflammatory signaling (e.g., redox-sensitive transcriptional programs) [43,44]. Notably, soybean-derived peptides have been reported to protect cells against oxidative insult by improving GSH status and reinforcing antioxidant enzyme activities [45], providing a plausible mechanistic bridge between the peptide-enriched nature of SFB and the redox outcomes observed in gastric tissue.

Enzymatic antioxidant defenses were also suppressed by indomethacin, as evidenced by reduced GPx, GRd, SOD, and CAT activities. SFB pretreatment restored these enzymes, with medium/high doses generally producing recovery comparable to Control and/or OME. Because coordinated enzymatic defenses are required to sequentially detoxify superoxide and hydrogen peroxide and to regenerate reduced GSH, restoration of these activities suggests that SFB reinforced both upstream (SOD/CAT) and GSH-dependent (GPx/GRd) antioxidant axes. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence that diminished antioxidant enzyme activities contribute to NSAID-associated oxidative injury, while restoration of these enzymes mitigates mucosal damage and improves tissue resilience [46]. Together with the reduced MDA and improved glutathione status, our data support that enhancement of endogenous antioxidant capacity is a major component of SFB-mediated gastroprotection. Our findings are also consistent with previous studies reporting that fermented soybean products and other food-derived bioactive compounds can attenuate NSAID-induced gastric injury through antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and mucosal protective mechanisms [17,26,40]. These observations support the potential of food-based interventions as complementary strategies for prevention of NSAID-associated gastric damage. Inflammatory signaling is another hallmark of indomethacin-induced injury, and NF-κB is widely recognized as a key transcriptional regulator controlling downstream pro-inflammatory mediators. Under oxidative and cytokine stimuli, NF-κB activation can amplify mucosal damage by inducing cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 and by upregulating iNOS, thereby promoting immune cell recruitment and sustaining inflammatory cascades [47,48]. In our study, indomethacin increased NF-κB expression in gastric mucosa, accompanied by upregulation of TNF-α, IL-6, and iNOS. SFB pretreatment suppressed NF-κB and reduced these downstream mediators, with clearer inhibition at medium/high doses. These findings suggest that SFB attenuates the inflammatory amplification loop that contributes to lesion progression. The observed suppression is consistent with prior in vitro evidence that the soybean peptide lunasin can downregulate NF-κB signaling and reduce iNOS/IL-6 expression in LPS-stimulated macrophages [49], supporting the hypothesis that fermentation-derived soybean peptides may contribute to anti-inflammatory effects. Moreover, a recent report on wheat peptides by Feng et al. [27] further supports that peptide-based interventions can blunt NSAID-related inflammatory signaling by inhibiting NF-κB (and MAPK) activation and preserving epithelial barrier function.

Finally, disruption of the COX/prostaglandin axis represents a core initiating event in NSAID gastropathy. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) is a critical mucosal protective factor that supports mucus/bicarbonate secretion, modulates acid output, and improves mucosal blood flow, collectively maintaining barrier integrity and promoting repair [9,50]. In our study, indomethacin significantly reduced gastric PGE2 content, whereas SFB pretreatment restored PGE2 in a dose-dependent fashion, with the high-dose group reaching levels comparable to OME. To better interpret this recovery, we examined COX isoforms. Indomethacin suppressed COX-1 expression, consistent with impaired basal prostaglandin synthesis in gastric mucosa, while COX-2 was induced, reflecting an injury/inflammation-associated response. SFB increased COX-1 expression while suppressing COX-2 overexpression, suggesting that SFB helped re-balance COX signaling toward a more protective mucosal state. In contrast, omeprazole primarily protects gastric mucosa through suppression of gastric acid secretion. Therefore, the reduced COX-2 expression observed in the OME group may reflect attenuation of mucosal injury and inflammation following ulcer prevention, rather than direct regulation of cyclooxygenase signaling. Although PGE2 levels in the OME group showed a slight increasing trend compared with the Vehicle group, this difference was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that SFB and OME may exert gastroprotective effects through partially distinct mechanisms. This coordinated pattern, restoration of COX-1 with concurrent suppression of excessive COX-2 induction, provides a mechanistic explanation for the recovered PGE2 levels and aligns with the concept that maintaining prostaglandin-mediated defenses is essential to limiting NSAID-induced lesions [30,51]. Oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and impairment of prostaglandin-mediated mucosal defense are closely interconnected during NSAID-induced gastric injury [5,30]. Excess oxidative stress can amplify NF-κB activation and cytokine production, whereas depletion of prostaglandins weakens epithelial barrier protection and mucosal repair [30,31]. Therefore, coordinated modulation of these pathways likely contributes to the protective effects of SFB. In addition, functional mucosal defense involves not only prostaglandins but also barrier components such as mucins. Previous evidence indicates that reinforcement of the mucus barrier attenuates NSAID-induced gastrointestinal injury, as demonstrated by processed Aloe vera gel, which enhanced mucin-related protection in an NSAID enteropathy model [52]. In the present study, SFB restored gastric PGE2 levels, a key mediator that supports mucus/bicarbonate secretion and mucosal blood flow, and this was accompanied by marked histological improvement. Although we did not directly measure mucin-related endpoints, these findings raise the possibility that SFB may potentially support mucosal defense partly through mucus barrier-related mechanisms, which warrant direct investigation in future studies. From a translational perspective, based on body surface area normalization, the highest rat dose (1.0 g/kg) corresponds to approximately 162 mg/kg in humans, equivalent to about 9.7 g/day for a 60-kg adult, suggesting potential feasibility as a concentrated functional food preparation. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Although protein profiling indicated enrichment of glycinin-related peptides, the specific bioactive sequences responsible for the observed effects remain to be identified. In addition, while modulation of NF-κB and COX/PGE2 signaling was demonstrated, the upstream regulatory mechanisms warrant further investigation. Moreover, NSAID-induced gastric injury involves complex alterations in mucosal barrier integrity, and additional evaluation of barrier-related parameters would provide a more comprehensive mechanistic understanding. Additional studies using chronic injury models, purified active fractions, microbiota-related approaches, and clinical investigations are warranted to further validate the gastroprotective potential of SFB.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, soybean fermentation broth (SFB) effectively protected against indomethacin-induced gastric mucosal injury. The protective effect was associated with attenuation of lipid peroxidation, restoration of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems, suppression of NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses, and rebalancing of the COX/PGE2 axis toward a mucosal-protective state. These coordinated effects collectively contributed to preservation of gastric structural integrity. The present findings highlight the potential of SFB as a functional fermented soybean product with gastroprotective properties and provide a mechanistic basis for further development in NSAID-associated gastric injury management.
