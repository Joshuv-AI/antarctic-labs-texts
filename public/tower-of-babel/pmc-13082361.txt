Novel Technique to Remove Intramedullary Nails with Difficult End Caps Using Minimal Instrumentation
Authors: Mark Ayoub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case:A 17-year-old adolescent boy undergoing removal of a piriformis-entry femoral nail had an associated end cap that was unable to be engaged. Accordingly, an appropriately sized threaded guidewire was cannulated inside the implant cap, allowing the construct to be removed as one unit.Conclusion:When an intramedullary nail has a difficult-to-remove end cap, a threaded guidewire can obtain an interference fit inside of it. The wire can then pull the nail and cap together as one structure. We believe this is a reproducible technique that minimizes soft tissue injury, requires minimal instrumentation, and remains cost effective.

Lower extremity long bone fractures are typically treated with intramedullary fixation. Occasionally, intramedullary implants must be removed due to infection, nonunion, malunion, and need for exchange nailing1. While there are multiple described techniques for removing broken nails, specifically the distal end of a broken nail2-5, there is significantly less focus on removal of the proximal nail end.

The proximal nail end can be challenging to remove when the nail is countersunk and when there is significant bone overgrowth6,7. This can be made even more challenging when a nail has an end cap in place that does not come out easily7-9. Removing a nail cap that is either from an unknown manufacturer, is stripped, or has significant bone overgrowth, as was the case for our patient, can require alternative techniques. To the author's knowledge, this is the first description of a nail extraction by threading a guidewire into its end cap and removing the construct in its entirety.

The patient and his parents were informed that data concerning the case would be submitted for publication, and they provided consent.

Case Report

A healthy 17-year-old adolescent boy was involved in a motorcycle collision with a distal third femoral shaft fracture. He underwent subsequent surgery at an outside facility in another country with an antegrade piriformis entry femoral nail. He was eventually referred to our clinic for concerns of a valgus nonunion 8 months postoperatively (Figs. 1-A and 1-B). The patient was noted to have significant pain with weight bearing and functional limitations.

He was counseled on these findings along with his parental guardian, and surgical treatment was recommended. He was brought to the operating room for revision surgery, with the plan for hardware removal, deformity correction, and revision nailing. Inflammatory markers and intraoperative findings were consistent with an aseptic nonunion.

The patient was placed under general anesthesia and positioned supine on a radiolucent table with a large bump placed under his hip. The prior incision was reopened. Multiple options were prepared for his case, including but not limited to multiple nail extractor sets, ball tip guidewires, flexi-nails, and hook extractors (Table I). Intraoperatively, after failed removal of the nail end cap with a screwdriver, there was presumed to be bone overgrowth, and an attempt was made to cannulate the nail and proximal end cap with a 2.8 mm threaded tip guidewire (Depuy Synthes). The tip of the wire was localized to the proximal portion of the hardware on anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopic views. The wire was then driven further into the cap against some resistance using a power driver. At this point, it was recognized there was a tight interference fit with the wire inside the cap (Fig. 1-C). A cannulated reamer was then placed over the wire to clear a path for the nail. A T-handle was then attached and used to backslap the entire implant out after removing the nail interlocking screws (Figs. 1-C and 1-D).

The remainder of the surgery was uneventful; alignment was corrected using blocking screws and revision nailing. He presented for his 2-year follow-up radiographically healed (Fig. 1-E). He denied any hip or knee pain and reported return to full activities including running.

Discussion

Intramedullary nail removal can be technically demanding. Several commercially available extraction systems exist (Table I), including both manufacturer-specific and universal extractor sets. While there has been emphasis on surgical techniques to removal the distal end of broken nails2-5, there are situations where proximal nail removal can be equally or even more challenging.

Nail end caps historically are placed to adjust nail length in situ and to prevent bone overgrowth to facilitate proximal nail retrieval8. Ironically, this same component can sometimes make nail removal significantly more arduous if the end cap is stripped or if there is significant bone overgrowth7-9, with some authors even recommending avoiding them for this reason6,8.

There are a series of techniques described to remove an end cap. A nail that is protruding from bone may be directly grabbed with forceps8. However, this is not always an option depending on how the nail was initially placed. A damaged screw extraction device can be threaded into the cap but only if it is able to accommodate the diameter of the device, and even so this is not guaranteed to remove the nail in its entirety9. Another option is to core out surrounding bone and use a bone hook through a proximal locking screw hole to bypass the cap entirely9. This can be problematic, potentially creating iatrogenic injury to the surrounding structures and sacrificing bone stock. Finally, there are multiple retrograde techniques described; however, these can involve making an additional incision through the knee joint, or potentially making bone windows distally2 which some suggest may require protected weight bearing9.

In this case, the proximal nail end cap was unable to be removed using a traditional screwdriver. Engagement of the cap with a threaded wire has the potential to avoid additional soft tissue trauma and prolonged surgical times that may be associated with alternative extraction methods7,10,11. This approach may be applied to nail end caps of varying sizes and manufacturers using threaded Steinman pins or, for larger diameter caps, threaded Schanz pins. The threaded pin should be small enough to fit through the cap's inner diameter but large enough to engage and cross thread inside of the component, creating a tight interference fit (Fig. 1-F). Surgeons should confirm compatibility between the pin and the implant cap's internal geometry either preoperatively (if manufacturer's specifications are available) or intraoperatively on the back table. Examples of common end cap interfaces are provided in Table II. Although a single-threaded pin size was sufficient in this case, multiple adjacent-sized small diameter threaded rods should be available to achieve optimal fit, particularly given the lack of published manufacturer specifications for end cap driver dimensions12-23. A cannulated reamer is then advanced over the threaded pin to clear any bony overgrowth obstructing nail removal. Initial forward threading of the pin into the cap is preferred over reverse threading to avoid the pin disconnecting during the overreaming process. Finally, a T-handle can be applied to the pin and backslapped to remove the entire construct as a unit.

This technique is not without limitations. The risk of pin breakage should be considered. This can be mitigated by using the largest diameter pin that fits in the end cap, as well as ensuring the pin maintains axial alignment with the nail on insertion and extraction. In addition, metallic debris may be generated with this procedure. Metallic particles have been associated with metallosis, soft tissue inflammation, and osteolysis24. Although no debris was observed in the wound or under fluoroscopic imaging in this case, thorough irrigation and debridement should be performed to minimize retained metallic particles and potential adverse tissue reactions.

When an intramedullary nail has an attached end cap that is difficult to remove, a threaded guidewire can be used to obtain an interference fit inside of it. The wire can then be backslapped out, allowing the nail and its cap to be removed together as one unit. We believe this is a reproducible technique that may offer less soft tissue injury than the currently described methods for dealing with troublesome nail caps. It also requires minimal instrumentation and is cost effective, allowing application in multiple settings. Although the described technique provides a reliable salvage solution, caution should be exercised in implanting end caps; the benefits of implantation must be weighed against the challenges of removal.
