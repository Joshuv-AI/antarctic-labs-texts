The Relationship Between Neonatal and Maternal Factors and the Timing of Primary Tooth Eruption in Preterm Infants: An Observational Study in Iran
Authors: Ali Karimi Farani, Mohammadamin Parsaei, Ava Taghizadeh, Mohammad Javad Kharazifard, Soheila Manifar, Maryam Koopaie, Mohammad Reza Zarkesh, Sedigheh Hantoushzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsPreterm birth is known to be associated with delayed primary tooth eruption, yet data on the factors influencing this delay in premature infants remain sparse. This study aims to investigate neonatal and maternal factors affecting the timing of the first primary tooth eruption.MethodsIn this retrospective‐prospective cohort study, medical records of 145 preterm neonates (85 males) admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit at a tertiary center in Iran (January–June 2024) were reviewed. Comprehensive data were collected on maternal comorbidities, birth details, and neonatal status. Weekly follow‐up interviews assessed tooth eruption and feeding status. Linear regression analysis examined variables against eruption timing for both chronological and corrected age, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.ResultsMedian chronological eruption age was 8.0 [7.0, 10.0] months; mean corrected age was 6.5 ± 2.1 months. For chronological age, male sex (p = 0.015), higher gestational age (p = 0.001), and larger head circumference (p = 0.025) predicted earlier eruption, while higher magnesium delayed it (p = 0.045). For corrected age, lower gestational age at birth (p < 0.001), birthweight (p = 0.001), and head circumference (p = 0.002) were predictors of earlier eruption. Multivariate analysis showed that higher breastfeeding predicted earlier chronological eruption (p = 0.043), while lower gestational age at birth (p < 0.001), birthweight (p = 0.012), and head circumference at birth (p = 0.010) predicted earlier corrected eruption.ConclusionFeeding status and anthropometric indices at birth emerged as independent predictors of tooth eruption. Exclusive breastfeeding associated with earlier chronological eruption, while earlier corrected‐age eruption in more premature infants may reflect compensatory accelerated growth.Keywords: infant, neonate, odontogenesis, tooth bud, tooth eruption

Introduction

Premature birth remains a critical global health issue, affecting > 10% of newborns worldwide [1, 2, 3]. As the leading cause of infant mortality, prematurity carries significant acute risks (respiratory distress, hemorrhage) and long‐term consequences, including neurodevelopmental disorders [4, 5, 6]. Oral and dental structures may also be affected by prematurity and early invasive treatments like intubation, resulting in crown dilacerations, altered palatal morphology, delayed dental eruption, increased caries risk, enamel hypoplasia, and skeletal anomalies [7, 8]. A key hypothesis attributes these defects to calcium homeostasis disruption caused by systemic conditions from conception through the postnatal period [7].

Tooth development begins at 5 weeks of gestation, with primary teeth typically erupting at 4–36 months postpartum [9, 10, 11, 12]. This complex biological process reflects systemic development and is influenced by genetic, nutritional, and environmental factors [13, 14]. The timing and sequence of primary tooth eruption are essential indicators of systemic health, reflecting the maturation of endocrine, skeletal, and nutritional systems [15]. Disruptions in the process may lead to complications such as dental caries, malocclusion, and periodontal disease, which can increase the need for dental and orthodontic care [16, 17].

Premature infants, particularly those admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), are at higher risk for delayed tooth eruption [13, 18]. However, studies on this topic have yielded inconsistent results, and the specific neonatal and maternal factors influencing this delay remain unclear. Elucidating these factors is critical for identifying at‐risk infants and implementing targeted preventive oral healthcare. This study aims to investigate the neonatal and maternal factors associated with the timing of first primary tooth eruption in a cohort of premature infants (gestational age [GA] at birth < 37 weeks) [2] to enhance oral health outcomes in this vulnerable population. We hypothesized that a lower GA at birth and a higher severity of respiratory morbidity at birth (e.g., requiring resuscitation or endotracheal intubation) would be associated with a later chronological age of first tooth eruption.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective‐prospective cohort study was conducted in Vali‐e‐Asr Hospital, Imam Khomeini Hospital Complex, Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran, between January 1, 2024, and June 1, 2024.

Ethical Considerations

Following the birth of the neonates, informed consent was obtained from the parents for the anonymous use and publication of their infants' data. Additionally, during the study process, informed consent for participation was acquired after parents were thoroughly briefed on the study's objectives. Parents were assured of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any effect on the medical care provided to their infant. The research was conducted in strict compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [19].

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and ethics committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (approval number IR.TUMS.DENTISTRY.REC.1403.022). We adhered to the strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [20].

Eligibility Criteria

We aimed to include all eligible infants born preterm (before 37 weeks of gestation) who were admitted to the NICU at our center between January 1, 2024, and June 1, 2024. Patient selection was conducted through a detailed review of the medical records of neonates born at our center during this period. Infants born at term (GA ≥ 37 weeks) and those whose parents declined to provide informed consent were excluded. Participants who were initially enrolled but were subsequently found to be ineligible or who withdrew consent were also excluded. During follow‐up, participants with incomplete follow‐up data, those lost to follow‐up, and those who died before tooth eruption were excluded from the final analysis.

Data Collection

The medical records of the study participants were reviewed, and demographic and clinical information were collected using a predesigned checklist developed by an epidemiologist. Maternal factors included diabetes mellitus, hypertensive disorders, hypothyroidism, cardiac diseases during pregnancy, smoking during pregnancy, and the birth order of the child among the mother's offspring. Comprehensive neonatal data were also gathered, including sex, GA at birth, birthweight, head circumference (HC) at birth, resuscitation at birth, endotracheal intubation at birth, electrolyte profiles (serum sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium levels), septicemia (positive blood culture), corticosteroid administration, and neonatal jaundice.

Furthermore, we conducted a weekly follow‐up investigation by contacting the parents of the study participants via telephone. Data regarding the timing of primary tooth eruption (i.e., chronological age from birth) and feeding status (exclusive breastfeeding, breastfeeding with formula, and exclusive formula feeding) were collected. Follow‐ups began around 5 months of age and were conducted weekly until the eruption of primary teeth was reported.

Based on the chronological age at first primary tooth eruption and normative data (6–12 months postnatal) [21], we categorized the cohort into three groups: (1) early (< 6 months), (2) normal (6–12 months), and (3) delayed (> 12 months).

As our cohort consisted of preterm infants, we also calculated a corrected age of eruption. This was done by subtracting the number of weeks the infant was born before 40 weeks' gestation from the chronological age at eruption [22].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM® SPSS® Statistics version 25.0. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages) and compared between groups using the Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test. The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated for normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Based on this assessment, normally distributed variables (p > 0.05) are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent t‐tests and one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with the homogeneity of variances confirmed with Levene's test. Non‐normally distributed variables (p < 0.05) are presented as median [interquartile range] and compared using the Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests. A two‐tailed p < 0.05 was set as the statistical significance level in all of our analyses.

Linear regression analyses were conducted to assess the predictive ability of each variable on the timing of primary tooth eruption. Key assumptions of the regression model were evaluated: the Durbin–Watson statistic was used to assess the independence of errors, scatterplots were examined for homoscedasticity, histograms of residuals were checked to verify the normality of residuals, and the variance inflation factor (VIF) was assessed to detect multicollinearity. Univariate (unadjusted) linear regression analyses were performed for each predictor variable, calculating the β coefficients, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), standard errors, Ps, and t‐values. Subsequently, multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to adjust for potential confounders. Predictor variables were selected based on the results of correlation analyses to minimize multicollinearity. When pairs of variables exhibited strong relationships in the Pearson's correlation analysis (r > 0.7), only one variable from each pair was included in the model. The final model included 13 predictors: maternal diabetes mellitus, birth order, sex, GA at birth, birthweight, HC at birth, resuscitation at birth, endotracheal intubation at birth, feeding status, sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium.

Power Calculation

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.2 [23] under the “Linear multiple regression: Fixed model, R 2 deviation from zero” framework (F‐test). Assuming an α = 0.05, 13 predictors, and an anticipated effect size (R 2) of 0.15, a minimum sample size of 131 participants was required to achieve 80% power.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

The medical records of 401 neonates admitted to the NICU at Vali‐e‐Asr Hospital were reviewed for eligibility. Of these, 210 preterm infants met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study. During follow‐up, 59 infants died before tooth eruption, and 6 were lost to follow‐up. Therefore, a total of 145 cases were included in the final analyses (Figure 1). Of these, 85 (58.6%) were male, and 60 (41.4%) were female. The median GA at birth was 34.0 [32.0, 36.0] weeks. The median chronological age of primary tooth eruption for the cohort was 8.0 [7.0, 10.0] months, with the majority of infants (91.7%) experiencing eruption between 6 and 12 months after birth. Also, the mean corrected age of eruption was 6.5 ± 2.1 months (Table 1).

Chronological Age of Tooth Eruption

Univariate analyses based on chronological age revealed that male sex (β = −0.831 [95% CI: −1.502, −0.161]; p = 0.015; R 2 = 0.040), higher GA (β = −0.215 [95% CI: −0.340, −0.089]; p = 0.001; R 2 = 0.074), and a larger HC at birth (β = −0.118 [95% CI: −0.221, −0.015]; p = 0.025; R 2 = 0.035) were associated with earlier tooth eruption. Conversely, higher serum magnesium levels at birth were associated with a delayed chronological age of tooth eruption (β = 1.370 [95% CI: 0.030, 2.709]; p = 0.045; R 2 = 0.038). No other variables demonstrated significant predictive value in the univariate analysis of chronological age. In the multivariate model for chronological age, breastfeeding status was a significant predictor of earlier eruption (β = −0.693 [95% CI: −1.365, −0.021]; p = 0.043), while no other variables remained independent predictors (Table 3).

Corrected Age of Tooth Eruption

Univariate analyses based on corrected age identified that lower GA at birth (β = 0.256 [95% CI: 0.131, 0.381]; p < 0.001; R 2 = 0.103), lower birthweight (β = 0.001 [95% CI: 0.000, 0.001]; p = 0.001; R 2 = 0.078), and smaller HC (β = 0.165 [95% CI: 0.063, 0.267]; p = 0.002; R 2 = 0.067) predicted earlier tooth eruption, which all of them survived as independent predictors of earlier tooth corrected age of in the multivariate analyses. Discussion

Main Findings

We investigated the relationship between various neonatal and maternal factors and the timing of primary tooth eruption in preterm infants, analyzing both chronological age and age corrected for GA. After adjusting for multiple confounders, infant feeding status was associated with the chronological timing of eruption, with breastfeeding associated with earlier eruption compared to formula feeding. Conversely, analysis using corrected age yielded different results, indicating that infants born with lower GA, lower birth weight, and smaller HC exhibited earlier tooth eruption. This suggests a potential pattern of compensatory accelerated growth following birth in this population.

Anthropometric Indices and Tooth Eruption

Our analysis revealed a critical dichotomy in the predictors of tooth eruption based on the age metric applied. When using chronological age, univariate analysis showed that greater GA and a larger HC at birth were associated with earlier eruption, consistent with previous research suggesting preterm infants experience delayed dentition [24]. However, neither of these factors survived multivariate analysis, indicating their effects may be mediated by other confounders.

In contrast, when age was corrected for prematurity, the relationship inverted. Univariate analysis demonstrated that lower GA, lower birthweight, and smaller HC at birth all predicted earlier tooth eruption. Crucially, all three factors remained significant independent predictors in the multivariate model. This inversion is highly significant; it suggests that while more mature infants erupt teeth sooner in absolute time, more premature infants exhibit a faster relative rate of dental development once their early birth is accounted for. This finding aligns with the concept of a compensatory accelerated growth mechanism [14, 18] and supports the conclusion that the apparent delay in preterm infants is a function of their shorter gestation rather than a pathological delay in the biological process of odontogenesis itself [10, 25, 26, 27].

Infant's Feeding Status and Tooth Eruption

In our study, infant feeding status was the sole variable that significantly predicted the timing of tooth eruption based on chronological age. Specifically, breastfeeding was associated with earlier tooth eruption, while formula feeding corresponded to delayed eruption. This finding aligns with previous studies showing that preterm infants exclusively breastfed tend to experience earlier primary tooth eruption compared to those fed a combination of breast milk and formula or formula alone [10, 13]. A systematic review of 12 studies also found that nonbreastfeeding diets are linked to delayed primary tooth eruption in preterm infants [27]. Furthermore, a study examining feeding habits during the first year of life found that exclusively breastfed infants had a higher number of age‐appropriate teeth compared to those fed both breast milk and formula, with bottle‐fed infants having the lowest count [28].

The link between breastfeeding and earlier tooth eruption may be attributed to the mechanical and developmental benefits of sucking. Breastfeeding promotes mandibular growth and activates facial muscles, which support craniofacial development and enhance functions such as breathing, swallowing, and chewing [29]. It has been linked to improved hard palate contouring, reduced malocclusion risk, and proper alignment of primary dentition. In contrast, bottle feeding may alter jaw growth, affecting dental arch dimensions and delaying tooth eruption [10]. Unlike the rigid structure of a bottle nipple, the natural flexibility of the breast facilitates correct lip closure, helps correct mandibular retrognathism, and promotes proper tongue positioning through enhanced tongue muscle activity [29]. These factors collectively contribute to improved craniofacial development in infants, which is directly linked to better dental development [29].

These findings underscore the impact of infant feeding practices on early dental development, emphasizing that earlier initiation and sustained breastfeeding among preterm infants may contribute to improved oral health and enhanced dental outcomes in this population. Moreover, exclusive breastfeeding provides broad benefits beyond oral health—including enhanced protection against infections, reduced risk of metabolic and immunologic disorders, and overall healthier development [30, 31, 32]—which may further support, directly or indirectly, the timely eruption and development of teeth. Its multifactorial protective and promotive effects underscore the importance of encouraging breastfeeding as a key component of infant care and health policy.

However, it is important to note that feeding status did not emerge as a predictor when eruption timing was analyzed using corrected age. This discrepancy raises questions about the strength of its direct biological relationship with tooth development itself, as opposed to its association with overall maturity in preterm infants. Therefore, while our findings underscore the potential oral health benefits of breastfeeding, further research is needed to elucidate the precise nature of this relationship in preterm infants.

Sex Differences in Tooth Eruption

We observed that male infants experienced earlier tooth eruption compared to females in analyses based on both chronological and corrected age. This is consistent with previous literature, which frequently reports earlier tooth eruption in male infants, although the underlying cause remains unclear [33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. Wu et al. suggested that earlier primary dentition onset could be linked to differences in sexual maturity, influenced by both environmental and genetic factors [35]. However, a large‐scale study in Singapore found no significant sex differences in tooth eruption timing [38]. Similarly, after adjusting for confounding factors in our multivariate regression analysis, sex was no longer a significant predictor of either chronological or corrected age. Nevertheless, there is a need for future large‐scale longitudinal studies to clarify the underlying causes and refine our understanding of sex differences in tooth eruption patterns.

Serum Electrolytes and Tooth Eruption

Calcium and magnesium are vital minerals in the human body, and their deficiencies are commonly linked to delayed tooth eruption and conditions like enamel or dentin hypoplasia [39]. Preterm infants are particularly vulnerable to bone diseases due to reduced bone mineral content, as most calcium and phosphate accumulation occurs in the third trimester, which preterm infants miss [40]. This results in a higher risk of calcium deficiency after birth, potentially leading to delayed dental development. However, our study did not find a significant association between serum calcium levels at birth and tooth eruption timing in preterm infants, suggesting that while the link between calcium levels and tooth development is well‐established, it may not be as pronounced in high‐risk preterm infants who face additional factors affecting dental development.

Magnesium, which influences extracellular calcium levels and activates over 600 enzymes, may also impact tooth eruption timing [39, 41]. Fetal magnesium levels increase progressively during the third trimester of pregnancy, a critical accumulation period missed by preterm infants [42]. Approximately 60% of magnesium is stored in bones and teeth, while only 1% is found in extracellular fluids [43]. Chronic magnesium deficiency in preterm infants can lead to alveolar bone fragility, gingival hypertrophy, delayed tooth eruption, and enamel or dentin hypoplasia [39, 44]. Surprisingly, our univariate regression analysis revealed a significant positive relationship between neonatal serum magnesium levels and delayed tooth eruption (chronological age), contrary to studies linking low magnesium with disrupted dental development. In vitro studies suggest that low to moderate magnesium supplementation enhances dental cell activity and mineralization, while high levels suppress cell proliferation, potentially delaying tooth development [45]. However, this relationship did not hold in our multivariate analysis, underscoring the need for further research to clarify these complex findings.

Neonatal Intubation and Tooth Eruption

Endotracheal intubation in neonates may adversely affect the development of primary teeth, oral soft tissues, and even permanent teeth. Reported complications include delayed or ectopic tooth eruption, dental malformations, and conditions like cleft palate and alveolar groove, often due to localized trauma to the dental bud [46]. Trauma from laryngoscope blades or pressure from endotracheal tubes can disrupt normal dental development [47]. Our findings indicate that neonatal intubation, along with resuscitation at birth, had no significant effect on the timing of tooth eruption. Although intubation is associated with delayed tooth development in the general neonate population, evidence in preterm infants is mixed. Some studies found no significant effect of intubation on tooth eruption timing in preterm infants [48, 49], while others reported significant delays in tooth eruption in preterm and term neonates undergoing major medical interventions, including intubation [13]. Also, [50] found that a longer intubation duration was linked to delayed eruption [50].

Given the controversial evidence and our findings, we hypothesize that the effects of intubation or resuscitation on dental development in preterm neonates may be confounded by other risk factors, making the impact less pronounced than in full‐term neonates. This suggests that delayed tooth eruption may be more closely associated with these severe illnesses than with intubation itself. Future studies should further investigate these factors to better understand the role of intubation, along with the severity of underlying conditions, in determining the timing of primary tooth eruption.

Limitations and Further Directions

There are several limitations to our study, which necessitate cautious interpretation of the findings. First, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of our results. Second, while we collected comprehensive neonatal data, the maternal factors, such as maternal age, as well as postnatal variables like growth indices (e.g., weight gain), were limited. Lastly, while regression analysis identified statistically significant associations between certain predictors and primary tooth eruption timing, the observed effect sizes were modest and fell below the threshold used in our a priori power calculation (R 2 < 0.15). Therefore, while this work offers valuable insights into the timing of primary tooth eruption in preterm infants, highlighting the role of infant feeding status in both oral and overall child health, further research may help to confirm and expand these observations. In addition to assessing the timing of tooth eruption, future studies should also consider evaluating the sequence of eruption, as it may have a greater impact on orthodontic outcomes and overall dental development.

Conclusions

This study revealed distinct patterns of correlates for tooth eruption based on chronological versus GA‐corrected age. Chronological eruption was associated with infant feeding status; breastfeeding was linked to earlier eruption, suggesting it may confer specific benefits for dental development. Conversely, analysis using GA‐corrected age identified earlier eruption in infants with lower GA, birthweight, and HC at birth, indicating a potential pattern of compensatory accelerated growth postnatally in the preterm population.
