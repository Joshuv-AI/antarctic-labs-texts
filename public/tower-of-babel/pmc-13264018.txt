Datasets for top-down estimates of sectoral CO2 and CH4 emissions, terrestrial and ocean carbon exchanges, and atmospheric concentrations over the Korean Peninsula
Authors: Jinkyu Hong, Changsub Shim, Bonhoon Koo, Doyoon Kwon, Eri Saikawa, Alex Avramov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article describes a gridded dataset of quarterly mean posterior emissions and atmospheric mole fractions of CO2 and CH4 over the Korean Peninsula for 2019 and 2020. The data were generated with the WRF-Chem (v4.4)/DART (v9.8.0) regional inversion framework at 9 km × 9 km horizontal resolution. This inversion framework assimilates high-precision in situ CO2 and CH4 observations from WMO/GAW stations using an ensemble adjustment Kalman filter. The released archive contains gas-specific NetCDF files. The CO2 emissions files include sector-resolved posterior anthropogenic CO2 emissions, an aggregated posterior CO2 emission field with its uncertainty, terrestrial ecosystem carbon exchange variables (ecosystem respiration, gross ecosystem exchange, net ecosystem exchange), and ocean CO2 exchange. The CH4 emissions files include sector-resolved posterior anthropogenic CH4 emissions and an aggregated posterior CH4 emission field with its uncertainties. Separate concentration files provide quarterly mean three-dimensional atmospheric CO2 and CH4 mole fraction fields on 51 vertical levels over the same domain. The dataset is distributed through Zenodo and is organized to support reproducible access to posterior flux and concentration products. These data can be reused for comparison with bottom-up greenhouse gas inventories, development of higher-resolution inversion systems, assessment of COVID-19 pandemic period changes on regional emission patterns, examination of CH4-to-CO2 emission relationships, and regional-scale evaluation of atmospheric concentration fields over East Asia.Keywords: Atmospheric inversion, WRF-Chem, Methane, Carbon dioxide, Greenhouse gas emissions, Ensemble Kalman filter, Korean Peninsula, NetCDF dataset

Background

Carbon dioxide (CO₂) and methane (CH₄) are the most significant anthropogenic greenhouse gases, yet national GHG inventories compiled through bottom-up approaches carry substantial uncertainties, particularly for CH₄, where emission factors exhibit high spatial variability that such methods cannot fully resolve [1,2]. Top-down atmospheric inversion provides an observation-constrained alternative, optimizing surface fluxes by assimilating near-surface GHG mole fraction data into an atmospheric transport model, independent of emission factor assumptions. Global inversion systems such as NOAA CarbonTracker and ECMWF (European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts)’s CAMS (Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service) operate at coarse horizontal resolutions of typically 1 - 3°, limiting their utility over regions of complex terrain and heterogeneous land use such as the Korean Peninsula [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. This dataset was compiled to address the absence of a publicly available, high-resolution dual-species top-down product simultaneously constraining both CO₂ and CH₄ fluxes over Korea. The data were produced within the INVERSE-Korea project, officially endorsed by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) as part of the Integrated Global Greenhouse Gas Information System (IG3IS), using the WRF-Chem (v4.4)/DART (v9.8.0) regional inversion framework (https://ig3is.wmo.int/) [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. The dataset covers 2019 and 2020, years covered by the Republic of Korea's first Biennial Transparency Report (BTR) submitted to the UNFCCC in 2024 and provides independent verification data for GHG assessment over the Korean Peninsula.

Data Description

The dataset is publicly available at zenodo (DOI:10.5281/zenodo.19720880) and consists of three compressed archive files: concentration.tar.gz (21.2Mb), emissions.tar.gz (8.3 Mb), and uncertainty.tar.gz (21.2 Mb). All three archives contain quarterly gridded fields of CO₂ and CH₄ emissions and concentrations derived from the WRF-Chem (v4.4)/DART (v9.8.0) regional atmospheric inversion system over the Korean Peninsula for 2019 and 2020. Emission and concentration variables are stored in separate files within each archive. The overall repository structure is summarized in Table 1 and the dimension structure and key variables for each file type are summarized in Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5, Table 6. emissions.tar.gz file contains quarterly mean total CO₂ or CH₄ surface emission flux fields on the WRF-Chem horizontal grid, with array dimensions of (quarter, south_north, west_east) = (4, 135, 96) (Table 2, Table 3). Emissions are expressed in units of ton substance per 9 km × 9 km grid cell per year (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The emissions archive is gas-specific in structure. The CO2 emissions files contain sector-resolved posterior anthropogenic CO2 emissions, an aggregated posterior CO2 emission field, terrestrial ecosystem carbon exchange variables (GEE, RES, and NEE), and ocean CO2 exchange (oce_emis). In contrast, the CH4 emissions files contain sector-resolved posterior anthropogenic CH4 emissions and an aggregated posterior CH4 emission field. These two-dimensional fields are directly comparable to gridded bottom-up emission inventories such as EDGAR on a sector-aggregated basis [9]. Sector abbreviations follow the EDGAR sector naming convention (Table 4). In the released files, some codes are shortened relative to the original EDGAR labels; for example, PRO corresponds to fuel exploitation (PRO_FFF in EDGAR), REF to oil refineries and transformation industry (REF_TRF), PRU to solvents and product use (PRU_SOL), LDF to solid waste landfills (SWD_LDF), and SWD/INC to solid waste incineration (SWD_INC).

Concentration.tar.gz file contains quarterly mean atmospheric CO₂ or CH₄ mole fraction fields produced by the WRF-Chem simulation driven by the inversion-optimized posterior emissions (Fig. 3). The concentration fields are three-dimensional, spanning 51 atmospheric vertical levels (bottom_top dimension) in addition to the horizontal grid, yielding arrays of shape (4, 51, 135, 96) (Table 5). CO₂ mixing ratios are provided in units of parts per million by volume (ppmv) and CH₄ mixing ratios in parts per billion by volume (ppbv). The substantially larger file size of concentration.tar.gz (21.2Mb) relative to emissions.tar.gz (8.3 Mb) reflects the inclusion of the vertical dimension. uncertainty.tar.gz file contains quarterly mean uncertainties of posterior emissions and concentrations of CO₂ or CH₄ produced by the inversion system (Table 6).

Annual mean CO₂ (left) and CH₄ (right) concentrations and wind vector in 2020.

All files share a common horizontal grid of 135 (from south to north) × 96 (from west to east) cells at a horizontal resolution of 9 km × 9 km, covering the Korean Peninsula and surrounding regions. The temporal dimension is organized by quarter, where each file contains four time steps indexed as quarter = 1 (January–March), 2 (April–June), 3 (July–September), and 4 (October–December). The emissions files are two-dimensional in space, whereas the concentration files additionally include 51 vertical atmospheric levels (bottom_top dimension).

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

This dataset was generated within INVERSE-Korea, a regional greenhouse-gas inversion activity endorsed by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) under the Integrated Global Greenhouse Gas Information System (IG3IS) framework [5]. IG3IS aims to combine atmospheric observations and modelling tools to provide policy-relevant information on greenhouse-gas emissions and removals at national, sectoral, and urban scales (https://ig3is.wmo.int). The workflow used here is shown in Fig. 4 and the released archive was organized as quarterly gridded products for 2019 and 2020.

Data flow for generation of sectoral CO2 and CH4 emission, terrestrial and ocean fluxes, and their concentrations based on the WRF-Chem combined with the DART.

This assimilation framework generates observation-constrained three-dimensional field of greenhouse gas concentrations and meteorological variables, together with optimized regional posterior emissions over the Korean Peninsula. These optimized estimates provide an improved characterization of regional and national emission sources, thereby enhancing the consistency of modeled emissions with observed atmospheric variability. This approach aligns with recent studies on top-down estimation methodologies, which emphasize the integration of atmospheric observations and transport modeling for robust emission quantification.

The released products include top-down information relevant to sectoral anthropogenic emissions, terrestrial ecosystem carbon exchange, ocean carbon exchange, and atmospheric concentrations over the Korean Peninsula. This design is consistent with the IG3IS objective of producing observation-based information that can complement bottom-up inventories and support national greenhouse-gas monitoring, reporting, and verification activities at policy-relevant spatial scale [5,8].

Regional inversion framework

The dataset was produced with a coupled regional inversion system based on the Weather Research and Forecasting model with Chemistry (WRF-Chem v4.4) and the Data Assimilation Research Testbed (DART v9.8.0) [10,11]. The modelling system was designed for dual-species inversion of CO2 and CH4 over the Korean Peninsula at 9 km horizontal resolution with 51 terrain-following vertical levels extending to 50 hPa. To improve boundary-layer transport over complex terrain, the system incorporated surface heterogeneity treatments including revised canopy-height representation and roughness-sublayer parameterization [5,8]. The physics configuration followed those used by [5,8] and the references therein. Meteorological initial and lateral boundary conditions were taken from the ERA5 global reanalysis at 0.25° × 0.25° resolution and preprocessed through the WRF Preprocessing System. Initial and lateral boundary conditions for CO2 and CH4 were supplied from the ECMWF CAMS global greenhouse-gas reanalysis (EGG4).

DART was used as the ensemble-based data assimilation system to update greenhouse-gas tracer fields and meteorological fields in a dynamically consistent manner. The inversion framework used the Ensemble Adjustment Kalman Filter (EAKF) and generated analyses every 6 h (00:00, 06:00, 12:00, and 18:00 UTC) using observations within a ±3 h assimilation window. Observations that differed from the background ensemble by more than three standard deviations were rejected. Prior uncertainty was represented by Gaussian perturbations of ±5 % (1σ) for chemical initial and lateral boundary conditions and ±30 % for anthropogenic fluxes. Covariance localization followed a Gaspari–Cohn polynomial with a horizontal half-width of approximately 150 km and a vertical half-width of 10 km [1]

The updated meteorological and greenhouse gas concentration fields obtained from the assimilation step were reintroduced into the WRF-Chem model to initiate the next cycle of data assimilation. This iterative framework ensured that the modeled greenhouse gas distribution progressively converged toward observed atmospheric conditions, refining emission estimates with each assimilation cycle.

Atmospheric GHGs and meteorological observations used in data assimilation

The inversion assimilated continuous in situ CO2 and CH4 mole fraction measurements from three WMO/GAW monitoring sites operated by the Korea Meteorological Administration: Anmyeondo (AMY), Gosan on Jeju Island (GSN), and Ulleungdo (ULD). All three stations used harmonized measurement systems based on cavity ring-down spectroscopy (CRDS; Picarro Inc., USA) combined with a cryogenic drying system developed jointly by KMA and KRISS. Measurements were collected at 1 min intervals and processed into hourly Level-2 quality-assured products. Reported intake heights were 40 m at AMY, 12 m at GSN, and 10 m at ULD [5 and references therein].

In addition to the greenhouse-gas observations, the inversion system simultaneously assimilated standard meteorological observations from the NCEP PREPBUFR dataset, including surface pressure, air temperature, wind speed and direction, specific humidity, sea surface temperature, and satellite-derived upper-air profiles.

Prior surface fluxes

The prior flux configuration combined multiple surface source and sink components. Anthropogenic CO2 and CH4 emissions were prepared from EDGAR v8.0 and regridded to the regional model domain [9]. Monthly and diel temporal scaling factors were applied to represent the temporal variability of anthropogenic activity. Biomass-burning emissions were taken from FINN v2.5. Terrestrial ecosystem carbon exchange was simulated online with the Vegetation Photosynthesis and Respiration Model (VPRM) coupled to WRF-Chem, using meteorological drivers together with remotely sensed vegetation indices (EVI and LSWI) from MODIS MOD09A1 and land-cover information from SYNMAP [12,13]. Oceanic CO2 exchange was prescribed from the SeaFlux ocean carbon-flux product [14].

Production of posterior flux and concentration fields

During each assimilation cycle, the coupled WRF-Chem/DART system updated the ensemble state vector and then advanced the model forecast to generate the background for the next cycle. The resulting posterior fields were used to produce gridded estimates of atmospheric composition and surface fluxes over the Korean Peninsula. For the public dataset described in this article, model outputs were aggregated to quarterly means for 2019 and 2020. The released flux products were archived as quarterly gridded NetCDF files on the WRF-Chem horizontal grid, and the atmospheric concentration products were archived as quarterly mean three-dimensional mole-fraction fields on the same horizontal grid with 51 vertical levels [15]. The current archive description indicates that emissions and concentrations were packaged into separate compressed files and distributed through zenodo.

Limitations

This dataset has several limitations. First, the horizontal resolution of 9 km × 9 km is appropriate for national- to regional-scale applications, but it may not fully resolve fine-scale spatial heterogeneity in urban areas, industrial complexes, and coastal transition zones. Second, the released emission fields represent posterior total surface fluxes, so caution is required when directly comparing them with sector-specific bottom-up anthropogenic inventories unless component definitions are harmonized. Third, the posterior fluxes and concentration fields are subject to uncertainties inherent to the atmospheric inversion framework, including transport model errors, observation representativeness due to the limited stations, ensemble covariance structure, localization, inflation, and other assumptions adopted in the data assimilation system. Fourth, although the inversion constrains the fluxes with atmospheric observations, the posterior estimates remain partly dependent on the specification of the prior emissions and flux components, including their spatial allocation, temporal variation, and prescribed uncertainties.
