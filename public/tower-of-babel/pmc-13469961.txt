Protocol for high-resolution multiplexed sequencing of single-cell full-length transcriptome with CBTi-seq
Authors: Liyong He, Wenyi Zhang, Peidong Qi, Zhe Jiao, Dongmei Zhang, Xiangwei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBalancing throughput and full-length coverage remains challenging in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). Here, we present combinational barcoded Tn5 transposon insertion sequencing (CBTi-seq), a highly scalable protocol for constructing multiplexed, full-length transcriptome libraries from single cells and micro-region tissues. The workflow details sample acquisition, one-step reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) cDNA amplification, orthogonal combinational barcoded Tn5 tagmentation, multiplexed pooling, dual-option purification, and final library enrichment.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to He et al.1

Innovation

CBTi-seq introduces a high-resolution and cost-effective framework for multiplexed single-cell full-length transcriptome. The primary innovation is the implementation of orthogonal combinational barcoded Tn5 transposomes, which significantly reduces the diversity of DNA barcodes required for high-throughput experiments while enhancing multiplexing flexibility. Unlike traditional methods, this protocol integrates Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMIs) directly into the Tn5-mediated tagmentation process. This allows for precise quantification of transcript abundance and eliminates library enrichment PCR amplification bias, a capability often lacking in standard full-length sequencing protocols. Additionally, the protocol streamlines the library preparation into a rapid 5 h workflow by utilizing a one-step RT-PCR strategy for cDNA amplification, followed by direct tagmentation of unpurified cDNA. This advancement significantly reduces hands-on time and costs compared to gold-standard methods like Smart-seq2/3.2,3 Furthermore, the versatility of CBTi-seq is compatible with both single cells and micro-region tissue samples, bridging the gap between single-cell resolution and spatial transcriptomics.

Expected outcomes

Capturing single-cell/micro-region tissue using a microneedle platform

If the single-cell/micro-region tissue have been successfully captured on the capillary microneedles, they can be identified by visual inspection in the bright-field view (Figure 2). The success rate of subsequent injections being transferred into the PCR tubes was nearly 100%.

Amplified full-length cDNA

Following the one-step RT-PCR and cDNA amplification (Steps 6-8), the concentration of the amplified full-length cDNA typically ranges from10 to 20 ng/μL when starting from a single high-quality cell. For micro-region tissue samples, the yield may be proportionally higher depending on the cell number.

When evaluated on an Agilent Bioanalyzer using a High Sensitivity DNA Kit, a high-quality cDNA should have an average size of 1.5–2 kb and a small amount of short fragments (Figure 5A). In the case of suboptimal preamplification, the peak of primer dimers will be higher than the cDNA library (Figure 5B). The presence of significant peaks below 500 bp usually indicates RNA degradation or premature termination during RT. Mitochondrial fraction: Unless analyzing tissues with high metabolic activity or severe apoptotic stress, the mitochondrial RNA read fraction should remain below 20%, which confirms excellent cell viability during the initial capturing process.

Final CBTi-seq sequencing library

The Bioanalyzer trace of a high-quality library should exhibit a broad, bell-shaped distribution ranging from 300 bp to 700 bp (Figure 5D). Crucially, the profile should be free of sharp peaks at ∼130 bp, which correspond to primer dimers resulting from non-specific hybridization during enrichment PCR (Figure 5C). Libraries with severe primer dimer contamination will waste sequencing data and require an additional 0.75× DNA Clean Beads purification before sequencing.

Expected sequencing quality and key performance metrics

Upon completing the data preprocessing and bioinformatic workflow described in Steps 24–28, end-users should evaluate the library performance using a set of standard benchmarking criteria. While minor variations may occur depending on the specific cell lines or tissue types processed, a high-quality CBTi-seq dataset typically aligns with the following expected thresholds:

Base calling quality: A successful sequencing run must exhibit reliable base calling. The proportion of Q20 and Q30 bases is expected to exceed 90% and 85%, respectively.

GC content: The GC content distribution should exhibit a stable bell curve, typically falling within the 40%–60% range, indicating the absence of severe AT/GC bias during amplification.

Barcode integrity: Due to the orthogonal combinatorial indexing strategy of CBTi-seq, the fraction of valid, correctly retrieved cell barcodes should ideally remain above 95% and strictly no less than 85% (valid combinatorial barcoded reads/total sequenced reads) to guarantee efficient sample de-multiplexing.

Alignment efficiency: After mapping the reads to the reference genome via STAR, the overall genomic mapping rate should optimally exceed 50%.

Limitations

Scalability limits compared to droplet-based ultra-high-throughput systems

Although CBTi-seq leverages multiplexed tagmentation to substantially enhance the throughput of full-length transcriptomics, its reliance on plate-based front-ends (e.g., 96well formats) imposes an inherent ceiling on cell capacity. While this architecture effectively balances multiplexing scale with comprehensive transcript coverage, its absolute yield (hundreds to thousands of cells per experiment) is fundamentally eclipsed by droplet-based microfluidic systems (such as 10x Genomics). However, these massive-throughput platforms routinely sacrifice full-length information, achieving their scale at the expense of severe 3′ or 5′ positional bias. Consequently, rather than serving as a tool for constructing ultra-large-scale pan-tissue atlases, CBTi-seq protocol is uniquely positioned for the deep, high-resolution interrogation of complex isoforms and splicing architectures within rare, highly targeted cell populations.

High dependency on RNA integrity

Similar to other full-length transcriptome sequencing technologies based on oligo-dT primer and template switching mechanisms, CBTi-seq strictly demands high RNA integrity. In highly degraded samples such as formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues or apoptotic cells, the impaired processivity of reverse transcriptase frequently triggers premature termination. This molecular dropout manifests as either severe 3′ coverage bias or a complete failure in full-length cDNA amplification. Consequently, the successful application of this protocol currently necessitates high-viability fresh cells or well-preserved fresh-frozen (FF) specimens.

Imbalanced read distribution and inflexible resequencing within pooled libraries

Although the CBTi-seq protocol normalizes cDNA input mass prior to tagmentation, intrinsic single-cell variations in initial RNA integrity and transcript length drive differential barcoded Tn5 tagmentation kinetics. This heterogeneity inevitably manifests as an imbalanced distribution of valid fragments within the final multiplexed library, causing suboptimal samples to yield disproportionately low sequencing read depths. Furthermore, because individual samples are irreversibly pooled prior to final library enrichment, the targeted re-sequencing of these underrepresented cells is precluded. Consequently, achieving deeper coverage for specific low-yield cells necessitates re-sequencing the entire pool, which unavoidably generates redundant data for well-represented samples and substantially inflates overall sequencing costs.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low or undetectable cDNA yield after pre-amplification (Step 9). This is typically caused by failure in initial single-cell capture, suboptimal lysis conditions, or omission of critical enzymes during the one-step RT-PCR assembly. Alternatively, sample evaporation may occur if the thermal cycler lid is not properly heated to 105°C.

Potential solution

For micromanipulation, visually confirm the successful deposition of the cell into the lysis buffer by observing dynamic changes in the microscopic field of view and the inner wall of the microneedle. For FACS-based sorting, ensure rigorous cytometer calibration by performing a preliminary test sort onto a plate covered with a sealing film. This step is essential to adjust the stream and verify that the sample droplet is accurately deposited directly into the center of the target well before proceeding with actual sample sorting. To address potential issues related to suboptimal lysis and compromised RNA integrity, ensure that RNase inhibitors are freshly added to the lysis buffer to prevent RNA degradation. Furthermore, strictly avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles of the single-cell samples and lysis reagents, as this can lead to severe RNA fragmentation and subsequent failure of the pre-amplification step. Check the expiration dates of all RT-PCR master mix components prior to assembly and confirm the thermal cycler lid is properly heated to 105°C to prevent evaporation.

Problem 2

Shortened cDNA size distribution lacking large-fragment peaks (Step 8). This molecular dropout is often driven by severe RNA degradation resulting from RNase contamination during sample preparation, or low initial cell viability that leads to premature reverse transcriptase termination.

Potential solution

Strictly maintain an RNase-free environment (e.g., utilizing RNaseZap and certified nuclease-free consumables). For FACS-based sorting, ensure starting cell viability exceeds 90% (determined by fluorescent double-staining, ensuring that only the strictly gated viable cell population is sorted using the analysis software). Process samples immediately post-lysis and maintain all reagents on ice.

Problem 3

The peak of the final library fragment size distribution occurs at the place smaller than 350 bp (Step 23). This over-tagmentation artifact typically occurs when the too less cDNA is used in the tagmentation reaction, or when the 55°C tagmentation incubation exceeds the strict 10 min limit.

Potential solution

Accurately quantify the cDNA concentration using a Qubit fluorometer strictly prior to tagmentation. Use a calibrated timer to limit the 55°C incubation to exactly 10 min.

Problem 4

A prominent sharp primer dimers peak in the final sequencing library.

Potential solution

Strictly adhere to the recommended PCR cycle thresholds based on cDNA input (9–12 cycles for 5 ng; 11–14 cycles for 1 ng). Use a lower ratio of DNA Clean Beads: DNA (never exceed a 1:1 ratio, preferably use a 0.75:1 ratio).

Problem 5

Insufficient concentration of the final pooled library for sequencing (Step 21). This substantial DNA loss generally occurs during magnetic bead or spin-column purification, resulting either from over-drying the DNA Clean Beads (causing cracking and poor elution) or from residual ethanol carryover that inhibits downstream enrichment PCR.

Potential solution

Carefully monitor the air-drying of magnetic beads; they should appear matte but not cracked. Ensure complete aspiration of 80% ethanol prior to elution. When using a purification spin-column for collection, make sure to maintain the centrifugation speed strictly in accordance with the specified requirements.
