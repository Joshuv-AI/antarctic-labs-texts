Intramedullary and extramedullary tibial alignment guides applied to each knee of the same patient resulted in no significant differences in radiological or clinical outcomes
Authors: Seung Joon Rhee, Kyeong Baek Kim, Won Chul Shin, Seung Hun Woo, Min Uk Do, Eun Sung Lee, Sang-Min Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe superiority of intramedullary versus extramedullary alignment guides in total knee arthroplasty remains controversial, with previous studies reporting inconsistent results. Unlike studies comparing different patient groups, this study controlled for inter-patient variables by applying each method to the left and right knees of the same patients.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 344 patients (688 knees) who underwent bilateral TKA between March 2012 and December 2023. After excluding cases with the same guide used for both knees, revision procedures, PROSTALAC insertion, and loss to follow-up, 94 knees (47 patients) were analyzed. Each patient received IM guidance in one knee and EM guidance in the other, performed by a single experienced surgeon using a standardized technique. Radiological parameters (hip–knee–ankle axis, medial proximal tibial angle, joint line convergence angle, posterior tibial slope) and clinical measures (flexion contracture, further flexion, range of motion) were compared at a minimum of 12 months postoperatively.ResultsNo significant differences were found between IM and EM groups in postoperative radiological alignment (HKA axis, p = 0.308; MPTA, p = 0.523; JLCA, p = 0.984; posterior slope, p = 0.269) or clinical outcomes (flexion contracture, p = 0.339; further flexion, p = 0.724). Both methods achieved similar improvements from preoperative values.ConclusionsIM and EM guides provided comparable radiographic and clinical outcomes, allowing flexible selection based on patient anatomy and surgical conditions.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Tibial alignment guides, Intramedullary alignment, Extramedullary alignment, Osteoarthritis, Radiological outcomes, Clinical outcomes

Background

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a major surgical procedure indicated for patients with pain and functional limitations due to osteoarthritis or other joint disorders [1, 2]. In TKA, two main types of alignment guides are commonly used—intramedullary (IM) and extramedullary (EM) guides. The IM method involves inserting a guide rod into the tibial medullary canal to assist with alignment, and because it relies on internal bony landmarks, it is less susceptible to variations in the overlying soft tissue envelope, though its accuracy can be compromised by internal anatomical variations such as significant diaphyseal bowing or a narrow medullary canal. This approach facilitates accurate intraoperative alignment, which may help reduce long-term implant wear and functional deterioration. However, the use of an IM guide can increase the risk of complications such as fat embolism or infection and may be unsuitable for patients with narrow or deformed medullary canals. In contrast, the EM method relies on external anatomical landmarks and uses surface-based measurements, thereby avoiding complications associated with entering the medullary canal. Additionally, as it does not involve violation of the medullary canal, it may promote faster postoperative recovery than the IM method. Nevertheless, its accuracy may be compromised by individual anatomical factors such as subcutaneous fat thickness and muscle mass [3–5].

Each of the methods has distinct impact on intraoperative alignment, postoperative functional recovery, and complication rates, thereby playing a critical role in determining the overall success of the procedure. Moreover, differences in postoperative outcomes between the two methods have led to varied conclusions in the literature. Mahaluxmivala et al. [6] reported that while the IM method provides superior lower limb alignment, the EM method offers shorter operative times and fewer complications. Thus, the choice between IM and EM guides should be individualized based on the patient’s anatomical features and surgical goals. In addition, Razzaghof et al. [7] observed a higher proportion of medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) outliers in the EM group, indicating potentially greater alignment accuracy with the IM method. Similarly, Cashman et al. [8] found that the IM guide is more accurate for coronal alignment, whereas the EM guide may offer advantages in sagittal alignment; however, they noted that the overall differences between the two methods were minimal.

Previous studies have primarily compared surgical outcomes between patient groups treated with a single guide method. However, studies directly comparing the IM and EM alignment guides in the same patient are rare. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate and compare the outcomes of IM and EM methods under identical physiological and anatomical conditions by applying each method to the left and right knees of the same patients. By directly comparing the clinical and radiographic outcomes of the two methods in the same patient, this study seeks to provide more robust, evidence-based insights to guide surgical decision-making regarding the choice of alignment method in TKA.

Methods

This study was approved by the hospital ethics committee and the institutional review board (IRB No.55-2025-090). It includes data from all patients who underwent primary TKA between March 2012 and December 2023. According to a preformatted electronic database, 688 cases of bilateral TKA were performed during the study period. The minimum follow-up duration was 12 months. Patients were excluded if they were lost to follow-up, underwent revision surgery or PROSTALAC insertion, or received the same type of alignment guide on both knees.

Surgical indications for TKA included Kellgren–Lawrence grade ≥ 3 osteoarthritis in patient’s aged 65 years or older, Kellgren–Lawrence grade 4 osteoarthritis in patients younger than 65 years, rheumatoid arthritis, or avascular necrosis unresponsive to conservative treatment. Patients with a history of local or systemic infection, active joint infection, or significant joint instability were excluded. Preoperative demographic and anthropometric data—including age, sex, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI)—were extracted from electronic medical records.

Surgical technique and postoperative care

All procedures were performed by a senior surgeon at a single institution who had received formal training and had prior experience with the Zimmer Biomet Persona knee implant system (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA) before the initiation of this study. A single implant design was used in all cases, and the surgeries were performed using a standardized technique. All procedures were planned to achieve a neutral mechanical axis [9–11], based on preoperative weight-bearing whole-leg radiographs. An anterior midline incision was made, followed by a medial parapatellar arthrotomy and patellar eversion. Thereafter, osteophytes were removed. The valgus angle for femoral resection was determined using an IM guide. This angle was individualized for each patient, typically set at 4° or 8°, based on the deviation of the femoral anatomical axis from the mechanical axis identified on the preoperative whole-leg radiograph, with the goal of achieving a neutral postoperative mechanical axis. Femoral component sizing was based on the anterior–posterior (AP) dimension of the distal femur, using an anterior referencing system. After selecting the appropriate femoral cutting guide, femoral component rotation was adjusted according to the gap between the preoperatively measured transepicondylar axis and posterior condylar axis [12, 13]. The femoral cutting guide was positioned with 3° to 5° of external rotation relative to the posterior condyles, and its alignment was verified using the AP trochlear sulcus.

Minimal tibial bone resection was performed to achieve a cut surface perpendicular to the tibial mechanical axis in the coronal plane. In the sagittal plane, the target posterior tibial slope was set at 5°, consistent with the Persona implant design specifications, relative to the tibial mechanical axis. The allocation of IM or EM guide to each knee was randomized using a computer-generated sequence list prior to surgery. For the IM method, the Vanguard IM guide (Biomet, Warsaw, IN) was used, taking into account the posterior tibial slope. The entry point was determined based on the preoperative mechanical axis planning. The tibial canal was accessed using a T-hand fluted rod inserted through the main body of the tibial resection guide, and the IM rod was gently advanced into the canal after confirming proper positioning of the resection block [10].

For the EM method, the Persona EM guide (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN) was used and aligned with the preoperatively planned mechanical axis. The goal of this alignment was to achieve a cut surface perpendicular to the tibial mechanical axis, consistent with the goal for the IM method. Proximally, the guide was positioned just medial to the midpoint of the tibial tubercle. Distally it was aligned to a consistent point approximately 5 mm medial to the midpoint between the palpable medial and lateral malleoli, according to the preoperative plan. This specific reference point, which represents a common conventional technique [14], was consistently applied to all patients in the EM group [15]. The “preoperative plan” for this landmark involved clinically correlating the center of the talar dome (identified on whole-leg radiographs) with the palpable landmark. While we acknowledge the limitation of using a 2D radiographic reference due to tibial torsion [16], this plan was used to clinically confirm the consistent application of the guide at this distal point. Tibial rotational alignment was established based on a reference line passing through the medial third of the tibial tubercle and the second metatarsal or the center of the talus [17]. All surrounding osteophytes were meticulously removed. Following the femoral and tibial cuts, trial components were inserted. Soft tissue releases were performed as needed to achieve proper gap balancing. A laminar spreader applying 150 N of force was used to control the gap. Patellar tracking was assessed using the no-thumb test. A posterior-stabilized prosthesis was used in all surgeries.

Postoperatively, pain was managed with patient-controlled analgesia. Compression dressings and closed suction drains were removed 24 hours after surgery. All patients followed a standardized rehabilitation protocol. They were instructed to begin quadriceps strengthening exercises on postoperative day 1 and passive range-of-motion exercises and physical therapy on postoperative day 2. Thromboprophylaxis with enoxaparin was initiated on postoperative day 2, and patients were discharged on postoperative day 14.

Radiological evaluation

All radiographic images were acquired using a standardized imaging protocol at the same hospital and department and subsequently reviewed for quality by two orthopedic surgeons to ensure adequate rotation and component visualization. In our study cohort, all included patients had radiographs of sufficient quality for measurement, thus precluding the need for patient exclusion based on poor image quality. Preoperative and postoperative knee radiographs, including standing AP views, lateral views, 45-degree flexed weight-bearing AP views, and full-length standing AP views of the entire lower extremity, were obtained. The hip–knee–ankle (HKA) axis was measured on full-length standing AP radiographs based on three anatomical landmarks—the center of the femoral head, the center of the proximal tibial articular surface, and the center of the talus. Two lines were drawn—one connecting the femoral head to the proximal tibial center and another connecting the proximal tibial center to the talar center—and the angle formed by these lines was defined as the HKA axis [18]. The MPTA was measured as the angle between the tibial mechanical axis and the articular surface of the proximal tibia on full-length standing AP radiographs [19]. The joint line convergence angle (JLCA) was measured as the angle between the femoral and tibial joint lines on the AP radiographs [20]. The tibial posterior slope was measured on lateral knee radiographs as the angle between a line parallel to the baseplate of the tibial component postoperatively and a line representing the anatomical axis of the tibia (Fig. 1) [20]. Postoperative MPTA, JLCA, and tibial posterior slope were measured based on the orientation of the implanted prosthetic components. For radiological analysis, all parameters (HKA axis, MPTA, JLCA, and tibial posterior slope) were measured and compared between preoperative images and those obtained at a minimum of 1 year postoperatively.

Clinical evaluation

Flexion contracture (FC) was defined as the angle between full extension and the resting position of the knee when active extension was maximally achieved. Further flexion (FF) was defined as the maximum flexion angle measured from the point of full extension. The range of motion (ROM) was calculated as the difference between FF and FC. All measurements were performed using a standard goniometer at each follow-up interval (6 weeks, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years postoperatively). All measurements were performed by the same orthopedic surgeon using a standardized and reliable physical examination protocol. Each measurement was taken three times with the patient in a standardized supine position, and the average of the three values was recorded to enhance accuracy. In addition, clinical outcomes were assessed using the Knee Society Score (KSS) system to evaluate patients’ return to daily activities and functional recovery. The KSS is a comprehensive tool that evaluates patient satisfaction, functional ability, and joint stability, and it served as a key instrument in comparing the advantages and disadvantages of the IM and EM methods [21]. These clinical assessments provided essential data for analyzing the short-term recovery process and the long-term clinical differences between the two alignment methods.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 28.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) to compare outcomes between the EM and IM groups. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. When data met the assumption of normality, group comparisons were conducted using the independent samples Student’s t-test. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test; if the assumption was not met, the Mann–Whitney U test was used instead. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To evaluate temporal changes and postoperative recovery trends, repeated measures analysis of variance was used. Bonferroni correction was applied to control for type I error in multiple comparisons. Additionally, paired t-tests were conducted to assess the differences between preoperative to postoperative outcomes within each group. Effect sizes were calculated to determine the clinical relevance of group differences, and 95% confidence intervals were reported for key outcomes. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical procedures were verified by an independent biostatistician to ensure the reliability of the findings.

Results

Between March 2012 and December 2023, 344 patients (688 knees) underwent bilateral TKA at our institution. A total of 594 knees were excluded for the following reasons: loss to follow-up (24 knees), use of the same alignment guide for both knees (444 knees); and revisional TKA (108 knees) or PROSTALAC insertion (18 knees), as these procedures were unilaterally performed and did not meet the study criteria. After applying the exclusion criteria, 94 knees (47 patients) were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2).

The mean age of the patients was 71.36 ± 7.42 years (range: 58–82 years). Among the 47 patients, 4 were male (8.49%) and 43 were female (91.51%). All patients underwent bilateral TKA, with an equal distribution of surgical laterality—47 right knees (50.0%) and 47 left knees (50.0%). No significant differences in HKA axis, MPTA, JLCA, or posterior tibial slope were observed preoperatively or postoperatively between the IM and EM groups (all p > 0.05).

Clinical outcomes

The mean preoperative FC was 8.47° ± 5.44° in the EM group and 9.32° ± 5.99° in the IM group. Postoperatively, FC improved to 2.78° ± 1.47° in the EM group and 3.07° ± 1.34° in the IM group. The differences between the two groups were not statistically significant preoperatively and postoperatively (p = 0.437 and 0.339, respectively). The mean preoperative FF was 134.26° ± 22.58° in the EM group and 135.43° ± 17.44° in the IM group. Postoperatively, FF increased to 144.89° ± 9.52° in the EM group and 145.54° ± 8.11° in the IM group, with no significant difference observed between the groups preoperatively and postoperatively (p = 0.779 and 0.724, respectively) (Table 3).

Discussion

The present study evaluated and compared the outcomes of IM and EM alignment methods under identical physiological and anatomical conditions by applying each method to the left and right knees of the same patients. Unlike previous studies that compared different patient groups, this intra-patient comparative design minimized confounding anatomical and external factors, thereby strengthening the accuracy and reliability of the radiographic alignment outcome assessment. Our findings demonstrated no statistically significant differences between the IM and EM methods with respect to MPTA, JLCA, HKA axis, and posterior tibial slope. Similarly, no differences were observed in FC, FF, or overall ROM postoperatively. These findings suggest that under controlled intra-patient conditions—where external factors such as weight distribution are standardized—both IM and EM methods can provide comparable alignment accuracy.

TKA remains a key surgical intervention for managing pain and functional limitations associated with osteoarthritis and other degenerative joint diseases [22, 23]. To achieve accurate lower extremity alignment, two principal techniques—the IM and EM tibial alignment methods—are frequently employed [11, 12]. Among the two tibial alignment methods commonly used for TKA, the IM method involves inserting a rod into the medullary canal, providing alignment based on the internal anatomy of the diaphysis. While this approach can be highly accurate in the tibias without significant deformity, its alignment can be compromised by anatomical variations such as significant tibial bowing, as the IM rod will tend to follow the deformed medullary canal. While this method may enhance surgical precision and reduce long-term implant wear, it carries risks of fat embolism and infection and may be unsuitable for patients with deformed or narrow canals [24, 25]. In contrast, the EM method relies on external bony landmarks, allowing surgeons to “bypass” diaphyseal deformities and avoid opening the medullary canal, thereby reducing the risk of fat embolism and other intramedullary complications. However, its accuracy can be affected by anatomical variability such as subcutaneous fat or muscle mass [7, 26].

We acknowledge that the conventional EM method employed (e.g., 5 mm medial to the intermalleolar midpoint) is susceptible to varus malalignment due to tibial torsion [16]. This occurs because coronal accuracy is determined primarily by the guide’s rotational alignment relative to the proximal tibial axis rather than by its fixed distal translational position [27]. Alternative methods, such as proximal-only referencing and explicit rotational control, have been shown to substantially mitigate these errors [14, 27]. Therefore, while our study demonstrated comparable outcomes, our conventional protocol is limited by not explicitly incorporating the crucial rotational adjustment.

The debate over the optimal tibial guide in TKA has persisted since both IM and EM methods were introduced [3, 4, 6, 7]. In a meta-analysis, Zeng et al. [5] reported that 52.6% of studies found comparable alignment accuracy between the two methods, whereas 36.8% favored the IM method and 10.5% favored the EM method. Razzaghof et al. [7] found significantly fewer MPTA outliers with the IM method, but no major difference was observed in the overall alignment or functional recovery. Similarly, Mahaluxmivala et al. [6] emphasized that surgical experience significantly affected alignment outcomes, with the IM method showing superior overall accuracy and the EM method resulting in more outliers, particularly among less experienced surgeons. Consistent with these findings, our study showed no significant difference in radiographic alignment outcomes between the IM and EM methods. This finding may be attributed to the inclusion of a single experienced surgeon and the use of standardized preoperative planning based on full-length radiographs and a consistent surgical protocol. While IM guides are aligned with internal mechanical axes and EM guides rely on external landmarks [10, 20, 28, 29], the use of templated alignment planning appears to have compensated for these differences. From a clinical standpoint, functional recovery outcomes such as ROM, FC, and FF also showed no significant intergroup differences. This is likely due to the uniform application of postoperative rehabilitation protocols and pain management strategies. Furthermore, accurate restoration of the joint line and soft tissue balancing—key determinants of clinical success in TKA [1, 2, 22]—were achieved using objective tools such as laminar spreaders and the no-thumb test.

Additionally, our patient cohort was characterized by relatively homogeneous varus knee alignment, without excessive deformities or tibial bowing. This observation may be attributed to patient selection; patients with severe deformities at our institution typically undergo unilateral TKA, and those undergoing bilateral TKA did not meet the specific criteria for this study. The mean preoperative alignment was 7.41° ± 4.94° with the EM method and 8.43° ± 4.70° with the IM method, and only 19.1% of patients had a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. The reduced anatomical variability may have minimized the potential differential impact of guide selection. Previous studies have suggested that the accuracy of EM guides decreases in patients with high BMI or severe deformity [3]; however, our sample had few extreme outliers.

Overall, the choice between the IM and EM methods has long been influenced by factors such as alignment precision, ease of instrumentation, and complication risk. By employing a within-subject design, this study offered a neutral and reliable analysis of alignment accuracy—arguably the most debated aspect of guide selection.

This study has some limitations. First, it was a retrospective study conducted at a single institution with a relatively small sample size (47 patients, 94 knees) [30], which may restrict generalizability. Although the minimum follow-up duration was 12 months, longer-term data are necessary to evaluate implant survival and sustainably of alignment outcomes. Additionally, intraoperative factors such as operative time, blood loss, and specific guide-related complications (e.g., fat embolism for IM, postoperative infection rates, or deep vein thrombosis) were not analyzed. This is a notable limitation, as these factors are critical for clinical decision-making when choosing between the two methods. Finally, the study was performed by a single experienced surgeon following a standardized protocol, which may have limited the generalizability of the findings to broader clinical practice settings.

Conclusions

Under standardized surgical techniques and preoperative planning by a single experienced surgeon, the use of IM or EM tibial alignment guides in TKA resulted in comparable radiographic alignment and clinical outcomes. These findings suggest that surgical expertise and meticulous planning may play a more critical role in achieving optimal results than the choice of guide type itself. Consequently, the choice of method can be flexibly tailored based on the patient’s anatomy and surgical context.
