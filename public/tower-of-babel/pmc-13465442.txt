Quantitative assessment of the tendon–pulley interface in trigger finger: a pilot feasibility study of a wearable electrical contact resistance sensor
Authors: Jhih-Fong Huang, Rajat Subhra Karmakar, Yu-Chun Lin, Jui-I Chao, Yen-Wen Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrigger finger (stenosing tenosynovitis) reflects abnormal tendon–pulley mechanics, primarily at the A1 pulley; however, objective assessment during functional motion remains limited in routine clinical practice. We evaluated electrical contact resistance (ECR) from a wearable tactile sensor during standardized digit motion and examined its association with symptoms.MethodsNine participants with Green grade II–III trigger finger were prospectively enrolled. An origami-inspired pentagon-knot graphene–paper tactile sensor measured ECR over the A1 and A2 pulleys during repeated flexion–extension cycles. A 4-week intervention program (electrical stimulation plus infrared irradiation; three sessions per week) enabled repeated measurements. ECR and patient-reported outcomes were collected at weekly Tuesday assessments, with ECR recorded immediately before and after the session (ECRpre and ECRpost). Outcomes included pain (visual analog scale [VAS]) and triggering severity (ST), frequency (FT), and functional impact (FIT). Within-session change was defined as ΔECRsession = ECRpre − ECRpost. Analyses used linear mixed-effects models, repeated-measures correlation (rmcorr; rrm coefficient) and week-adjusted fixed-effects regression.ResultsSensor placement and recording were feasible, with no adverse events. Across 27 paired weekly sessions (Weeks 1–4), ECR decreased from pre- to post-session at both pulleys (model-estimated mean ΔECRsession: A1 0.0795; A2 0.0990; both P < 0.001). A1 ECRpre declined over Weeks 1–4 (rrm = − 0.672; P = 0.0016). At matched weekly timepoints (N = 6; n = 24), A1 ECRpre correlated with VAS (rrm = 0.781; P < 0.001), FT (rrm = 0.578; P = 0.0095), and ST (rrm = 0.543; P = 0.0164), but not FIT (rrm = 0.165; P = 0.50). Larger A1 ΔECRsession values were associated with greater subsequent week-to-week pain improvement (rrm = 0.625; P = 0.0042). In a fixed-effects model adjusting for week, A1 ECRpre independently predicted VAS (β = 29.19; P = 0.018).ConclusionsWearable ECR sensing was safe and feasible in this pilot study and demonstrated consistent within-session responsiveness. Clinically, motion-acquired ECR may serve as an objective adjunct for tracking session-level changes and week-to-week status with symptom scales.Trial registrationRetrospectively registered at ChiCTR (ChiCTR2600120811 approved).Keywords: Electrical contact resistance, Wearable tactile sensor, Biomechanical monitoring, Tendon–pulley interface, Trigger finger

Introduction

Trigger finger, or stenosing flexor tenosynovitis, is a frequently encountered condition in orthopedic clinics [1, 2]. It is one of the most common causes of hand disability, affecting around 2% of the general population and up to 10%–20% of individuals with diabetes [3, 4]. The annual incidence is approximately 28 per 100,000 [5]. Trigger finger most commonly presents in a bimodal distribution, with peak occurrence in adults in their fifth and sixth decades, and women are at a 2- to 6-fold higher risk than men [6]. It is characterized by pain, stiffness, and a locking or catching sensation during finger movement, which can significantly impair grasping and object manipulation and reduce quality of life [7]. Although diagnosis is typically straightforward, treatment monitoring still relies largely on subjective and semi-quantitative measures [7].

The treatment options for trigger finger include hand therapy, splinting (orthosis), corticosteroid injection, and surgical intervention [2, 5, 8]. Currently, evaluations of clinical outcome rely heavily on subjective self-report measures such as the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain and disability questionnaires such as the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (QuickDASH), while more objective metrics are often limited to semi-quantitative clinical staging (e.g., Stages of Stenosing Tenosynovitis) and grip or pinch strength testing, which may be influenced by inter-observer variability and patient effort [9–12]. There remains a need for a quantitative outcome metric with clear clinical interpretability that can capture biomechanical change beyond patient-reported symptoms and semi-quantitative clinical staging.

With the advancement of wearable sensor technology, there is an opportunity to bridge this gap by providing real-time and quantitative monitoring of biomechanical changes during functional movement [13]. For example, 3-axis gyroscopes and inertial measurement units have already been applied to detect functional impairment and monitor disease progression in orthopedics [14–16]. Wearable pressure/tactile sensors have also been utilized not only in scientific research but also in clinically relevant contexts such as diagnosis, rehabilitation, and the evaluation of patient outcomes [13, 17, 18]. However, as highlighted in a recent scoping review of non-invasive wearable tendon monitoring, current literature has largely concentrated on large lower-limb tendons, particularly the Achilles and patellar tendons [13]. In contrast, quantitative dynamic assessment for hand pathologies such as stenosing tenosynovitis (trigger finger) remains scarce, and routine clinical metrics are still largely based on static or semi-quantitative assessments [19]. Moreover, conventional modeling and experimental approaches (e.g., finite element modeling and cadaveric experiments) have limited ability to capture active, in vivo functional motion and neuromuscular complexity [20, 21], constraining their applicability to real-world functional variability.

In this pilot study, we introduce a wearable tactile sensing system designed to quantify contact mechanics at the A1 pulley in participants with trigger finger. In this system, contact mechanics are quantified via electrical contact resistance (ECR), recorded during functional motion. Given that trigger finger results from impaired flexor tendon gliding through a stenotic pulley–sheath interface, pressure-related signals captured during functional motion may serve as a biomechanically relevant proxy for tendon–pulley interactions and may be sensitive to changes in mechanical obstruction following intervention. We hypothesize that dynamic A1 pulley ECR-derived profiles may reflect biomechanical improvements post-intervention. By capturing objective quantitative measures before and after treatment, we aim to evaluate the clinical feasibility of this approach and explore its associations with established clinical outcome measures.

Materials and methods

This was a prospective, longitudinal pilot study using a single-group repeated-measures design. The study was conducted from October 2025 to January 2026. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University (Approval Number: 202503HM002). Participants

Nine participants (6 females and 3 males; mean age 60.7 ± 11.9 years; range, 38–75 years), each with a single affected finger, were prospectively enrolled. All participants were diagnosed and classified as Green grade II or III [22] by a single orthopedic specialist and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Participants were excluded if they had received a local corticosteroid injection or physiotherapeutic intervention to the affected finger within 6 months prior to enrollment; had radiographic evidence of arthritis in the metacarpophalangeal, proximal interphalangeal, or distal interphalangeal joints of the affected finger; had a history of severe trauma to the target hand or digits or had undergone previous surgery for trigger digit; had a history of inflammatory joint disease, infection, stroke, or malignancy; were pregnant; had cardiac arrhythmia or an implanted cardiac pacemaker; or exhibited clinical signs suggestive of advanced cervical spine pathology.

Wearable tactile sensor and measuring setup

This study used wearable tactile sensors based on a pentagon-knot origami architecture to quantify tendon–pulley interactions in participants. The pentagon-knot configuration forms a multilayered, overlapping structure that enhances interfacial contact sensitivity. As demonstrated in our previous studies [24], this design provides high sensitivity in the low-pressure range (0–1 kPa), which is important for detecting low-magnitude mechanical signals from the tendon–pulley interface. Sensors were fabricated on 220-µm air-laid paper substrates by screen printing a graphene-based, water-insoluble conductive ink (Euflex Corp., Taiwan) and subsequently cured at 100 °C for 30 min. Conductive paper sheets were cut into 1 cm-wide strips and aligned in a face-to-face stacked configuration. The stacked layers were then folded into origami-inspired pentagon-knot structures following a folding pattern described in previous work [25, 26] which increases interfacial contact area and improves sensitivity. Electrical contact resistance (ECR) was measured using a voltage-divider circuit as the primary output. The sensor was integrated with a Bluetooth module for real-time wireless data acquisition and visualization on a smartphone.

For the pilot study, two identical pentagon-knot sensors with the same sensing area were placed on the A1 and A2 pulleys of the symptomatic digit. The A1 pulley sensor (S1) utilized a flat geometry for direct palmar attachment, while the A2 pulley sensor (S2) featured a wearable ring design to ensure conformal contact and stability during finger flexion and extension. The anatomical landmarks for the A1 and A2 pulleys were identified by physical examination and confirmed by ultrasonography. Skin landmarks were marked to ensure reproducible placement. All sensors were secured using medical-grade adhesive tape (3 M #1530-1) to minimize motion artifacts during dynamic finger motion. To reduce variability in fixation tension, the same fixation material and attachment procedure were used for all assessments. ECR signals were recorded after confirming a stable baseline from both S1 and S2 in the same clinical setting to reduce variability related to testing environment and timing. ECR was acquired using a voltage-divider circuit (schematic shown in Fig. 2). Signals were streamed via a Bluetooth module and visualized in the Phyphox mobile app for real-time monitoring (Fig. 2). The Phyphox application (Physical Phone Experiments; RWTH Aachen University, Aachen, Germany; https://phyphox.org) was used for real-time visualization and data recording. During each measurement session, the acquired signal data were stored locally on the smartphone. After completion of each recording, the data were exported as comma-separated value (CSV) files and transferred to a desktop computer for signal processing and statistical analysis.

Study procedures

Interventions were delivered in a routine clinical-care context to enable repeated ECR measurements and symptom tracking; the protocol was not designed as a comparative trial to estimate intervention efficacy. The study comprised a 4-week primary phase using intervention A (Phase 1) and an optional 4-week exploratory extension in which intervention A was continued with the addition of intervention B (Phase 2; described briefly for completeness but not analyzed in this study).

Intervention A consisted of low-frequency electrical stimulation (ES; model MH-306) and infrared irradiation (IR; model MH-250), both manufactured by Min-Hong Enterprise Co., Ltd. (Taichung, Taiwan). In Phase 1, intervention A was administered three times weekly (Tuesdays, Thursdays, and Saturdays). All weekly ECR assessments were performed at the Tuesday visit in the same clinical setting to reduce variability related to testing environment and timing. To ensure consistent joint mobility before ECR acquisition, participants performed a standardized 10-cycle warm-up of full finger flexion–extension using the affected hand. Participants were then seated with the affected hand supported. ES electrode pads were placed adjacent to the A1 pulley, and the infrared lamp was positioned 40–50 cm above the target area. ES was delivered in “Mode 1,” in which the frequency ramped linearly from 0 to 30 Hz over 30 s and reset cyclically, for 15 min at intensity levels 2–3 (device-specific scale; adjusted to a comfortable sensory level without visible muscle contraction). IR was applied concurrently for 15 min using the same target area and positioning.

ECR acquisition and patient-reported outcomes were collected only at the Tuesday visit to standardize the weekly time point, using the following sequence: (1) collection of clinical outcomes and pre-session ECR (ECRpre) during a separate 10–cycle flexion–extension recording segment after the warm-up; (2) administration of intervention A; and (3) recording of post-session ECR (ECRpost) during a second 10–cycle segment immediately after intervention. To facilitate delivery of the intervention, the sensors were removed during the 15-minute intervention period and subsequently re-applied at the same pre-marked skin landmarks prior to the post-intervention recording, using a standardized fixation method to maintain consistent sensor placement and contact conditions between pre- and post-intervention measurements. To minimize potential bias related to repositioning, segment-specific normalization was applied to ECRpre and ECRpost. The additional Thursday and Saturday sessions followed the same intervention A protocol but did not include ECR recording or clinical outcome collection. All interventions were delivered by the same therapist throughout the study. ECR acquisition was performed by trained study personnel using the same landmarking, fixation, and recording protocol across sessions. Patient-reported outcomes included the visual analog scale (VAS) for current pain intensity assessed at the Tuesday visit, while the severity of triggering (ST), frequency of triggering (FT), and functional impact of triggering (FIT) were completed as retrospective weekly ratings reflecting symptoms since the previous Tuesday visit. For clarity, ECRpre and ECRpost refer to measurements obtained immediately before and after the Tuesday session, respectively. ECR was recorded continuously during each flexion–extension recording segment; each segment was summarized as a single pre-specified value (ECRpre and ECRpost) using the same rule across all sessions to support consistent pre–post and longitudinal comparisons. Phase 2 consisted of continuing intervention A with the addition of intervention B (extracorporeal focused shockwave therapy) once weekly; detailed Phase 2 procedures are not presented because Phase 2 outcomes were not analyzed.

Data recording and analysis

Measurement Processing and Derived ECR Metrics.

All raw ECR signals were normalized to reduce between-visit offsets related to sensor fixation and coupling, enabling within-subject pre–post and longitudinal comparisons. The raw ECR signal was expressed in ohms (Ω), whereas all analyzed ECR-derived metrics were based on normalized ECR values. Normalized ECR was computed as ECRnorm = R / R0, where R denotes the instantaneous resistance recorded during motion, and R0 is the baseline resistance measured at the start of each recording segment (defined separately for pre- and post-intervention measurements as R0pre and R0post). Because ECRnorm is a ratio of resistance values, normalized ECR values and all ECR-derived difference metrics are dimensionless. ECRpre and ECRpost were normalized using their respective segment-specific baselines, reducing the influence of resistance shifts associated with sensor repositioning. ΔECRsession was defined as ECRpre − ECRpost, such that positive values indicate a reduction in ECR from pre- to post-session measurement.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean (standard deviation [SD]) unless otherwise stated. Because multiple weekly assessment visits were collected per participant, session-level pre–post differences were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept for participant to account for within-subject clustering. These models accommodate unbalanced data and include all available observations. All P values are two-sided. As a distribution-free sensitivity analysis, participant-level summaries of ΔECRsession (mean across available weekly visits) were additionally evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and normality of participant-level differences was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Associations between ECR-derived metrics and clinical outcomes (VAS, ST, FT, and FIT) were quantified using repeated measures correlation (rmcorr), which estimates a common within-participant association while accounting for repeated observations. Week-to-week pain change was defined as ΔVASt = VASt – VASt+1. Baseline-referenced cumulative changes were defined as ΔECRpre, WK1→t = ECRpre, WK1 − ECRpre, t and ΔVASWK1→t = VASWK1 − VASt, where positive values indicate improvement; cumulative analyses were restricted to follow-up timepoints (Weeks 2–5). To assess robustness against spurious associations driven by shared time trends, we performed additional analyses after removing (1) subject-specific baseline offsets and (2) the linear effect of week from each variable prior to association testing. This was implemented by regressing each variable on subject indicators and week (modeled as a continuous covariate), and then re-estimating associations using the resulting residuals. In addition, fixed-effects regression models with subject fixed effects and week as a covariate were fitted to estimate the independent association between pre-intervention ECR and pain while adjusting for between-subject differences and overall time trends. All analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

No formal intent-to-treat analysis was performed. Analyses were conducted using all available data, with methods applied according to their respective data requirements.

Normality of participant-level ΔECRsession summaries was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated no significant deviation from normality for either the A1 pulley (W = 0.846, P = 0.067) or the A2 pulley (W = 0.965, P = 0.846).

Results

Objective ECR and subjective clinical outcomes (VAS, ST, FT, and FIT) were collected and analyzed. Six participants completed weekly Tuesday assessments through Weeks 1–4, generating 24 paired ECR sessions with ECRpre and ECRpost measurements; three additional participants completed only the Week 1 assessment, generating 3 additional paired ECR sessions, and then withdrew from further follow-up. No additional ECR or clinical outcome data were collected from these three participants after Week 1. For acute within-session analyses (ΔECRsession), all available paired sessions were included (N = 9 participants, n = 27 sessions). However, longitudinal trajectories and repeated-measures correlation (rmcorr) analyses were restricted to participants with ≥ 2 weekly observations (N = 6, n = 24) due to methodological requirements for within-subject estimation. No additional exclusions were applied. After landmark confirmation, data acquisition required less than five minutes without device-related adverse events, supporting integration into routine practice. Four participants entered the Phase 2 exploratory extension; Phase 2 outcomes are not analyzed in the present manuscript.

Weekly pre-intervention ECR

Pre-intervention ECR (ECRpre) showed an overall decrease over Weeks 1–4 with modest week-to-week variability. The mean (SD) ECRpre decreased from 0.9334 (0.0049) to 0.9206 (0.0130) at A1. Among participants with ≥ 2 weekly ECR observations (N = 6; n = 24 weekly observations), repeated-measures correlation demonstrated a significant within-subject negative association between week and ECRpre at A1 (rrm = − 0.672, P = 0.0016). A2 trajectories are shown for anatomical comparison and were directionally similar but weaker (rrm = − 0.506, P = 0.0272) (Fig. 3; Table 3, Panel A).

Acute within-session ECR dynamics

Across the primary analysis window (Weeks 1–4; N = 9 participants; n = 27 paired sessions), ECR decreased from pre- to post-intervention at both pulleys, yielding positive within-session changes (ΔECRsession = ECRpre − ECRpost). To account for repeated sessions within participants, we used a linear mixed-effects model with a participant-specific random intercept to estimate the mean within-session reduction. The estimated mean ΔECRsession was 0.0795 at A1 (SE = 0.0033, 95% CI 0.0731–0.0860; Wald z = 24.24, P < 0.001) and 0.0990 at A2 (SE = 0.0037, 95% CI 0.0918–0.1062; Wald z = 26.89, P < 0.001) (Table 3, Panel B).

Concordance of ECR with clinical symptoms

To assess convergent associations, we tested whether pre-intervention ECR (ECRpre) aligned with patient-reported outcomes at matched timepoints among participants with ≥ 2 weekly ECR observations (Weeks 1–4; N = 6 participants; n = 24 weekly observations). A1 was pre-specified as the primary analytic site and A2 as an exploratory comparator. At A1, a higher ECRpre was associated with higher VAS (rrm = 0.781, P = 7.81 × 10− 5), FT (rrm = 0.578, P = 0.0095), and ST (rrm = 0.543, P = 0.0164), but not FIT (rrm = 0.165, P = 0.5000). At A2, associations were directionally similar but attenuated (VAS: rrm = 0.525, P = 0.0211; FT: rrm = 0.524, P = 0.0213; ST: rrm = 0.492, P = 0.0324; FIT: rrm = 0.158, P = 0.5187) (Fig. 4; Table 3, Panel C).

Within-session ECR change and subsequent week-to-week pain improvement

Across consecutive weekly assessment pairs (N = 6; n = 24 consecutive weekly pairs), larger within-session A1 ECR reductions (ΔECRsession = ECRpre − ECRpost) were associated with larger subsequent week-to-week improvements in pain, quantified as ΔVASt = VASt − VASt+1 (rrm = 0.625, P = 0.0042), with a common within-subject regression slope of β = 17.35. At the A2 pulley, the corresponding association was weaker but remained statistically significant (rrm = 0.507, P = 0.0266), with a smaller slope (β = 12.17) (Fig. 5; Table 4, Panel A).

Lagged ECRpre and VAS association in week-adjusted fixed-effects models

Lagged symptom–sensor alignment analyses were performed to examine the association between pre-intervention ECR and VAS. VAS collected at week t was paired with the subsequent pre-intervention ECR measurement (ECRpre) at week t + 1 and analyzed using fixed-effects regression models adjusting for subject fixed effects and week.

Using this fixed-effects framework, A1 ECRpre was a significant independent predictor of VAS (β = 29.19, 95% CI 5.71–52.66; P = 0.018; N = 6 participants, n = 24 weekly observations), explaining approximately 30% of the residual variance in VAS as quantified by partial R2. Here, this model coefficient is equivalent to approximately 0.034 normalized ECR units per 1-point lower VAS score under the model. At the A2 pulley, the association with VAS was weaker and not statistically significant (β = 11.77, 95% CI − 22.02–45.56; P = 0.471), with a negligible partial R2 (Fig. 7; Table 4, Panel B). Discussion

This pilot study was designed to evaluate feasibility and to characterize the relationship between patient-reported outcomes and objective changes in ECR in trigger finger, rather than to estimate comparative treatment efficacy of the Phase 1 intervention [27]. To our knowledge, this is the first report of applying an ECR-based wearable sensor to trigger finger. We emphasize standardized acquisition and within-subject contrasts (ECRpre vs. ECRpost; and longitudinal ECRpre trends) rather than absolute between-subject comparisons. Clinically, A1 ECR measures tracked pain and triggering frequency/severity more closely than A2 measures in this study [28]. Importantly, ECR should be interpreted as a motion-acquired interface signal that reflects local tendon–pulley contact conditions, rather than as a direct mechanical parameter of the tendon–pulley system. Consistent within-session reductions (ΔECRsession = ECRpre − ECRpost) support responsiveness of this ECR signal to short-timescale interface changes during standardized motion [29]. This acute ECR response should not be interpreted as an immediate improvement in patient-reported symptoms. ST, FT, and FIT summarize symptom experience over a preceding interval and are not intended to change meaningfully within minutes after a single session; even VAS, although measurable immediately, may have limited interpretability at this timescale. Instead, within-session ECR change provides an objective session-level measure that complements weekly symptom tracking by capturing motion-evoked variation at the tendon–pulley interface [30].

This interpretation is consistent with prior work showing that contact-resistance sensing is highly sensitive to interface contact conditions—including contact pressure, effective contact area, and loading-dependent geometry—supporting its use for tracking motion-related changes at the tendon–pulley interface [29]. In the tendon–pulley system, this signal provides a practical output of contact changes during motion, which is the aspect most relevant to triggering mechanics. Consistent with our assumption, the present data show intra-individual concordance between ECR metrics and symptom measures at matched weekly timepoints: at A1, higher ECRpre aligned with higher pain and more severe triggering/entrapment as quantified by ST and FT, supporting the convergent validity of this motion-acquired signal. On the other hand, the absence of a significant association between A1 ECR and FIT (rrm = 0.165, P = 0.50) suggests that ECR and FIT capture different levels of clinical information. ECR reflects localized, motion-evoked tendon–pulley interface conditions, whereas FIT reflects broader limitations in daily activities that may depend on grip posture, force requirements, compensatory strategies, hand dominance, occupational demands, and individual adaptation. The coarse ordinal structure of the FIT scale may also limit sensitivity to subtle within-subject biomechanical changes, particularly in a small pilot sample. Thus, the lack of an ECR–FIT association is more consistent with construct-level differences than with insufficient sensor sensitivity. In contrast, A2 associations were consistently weaker, consistent with the pathophysiologic primacy of the A1 pulley and supporting anatomical specificity [31, 32]. This systematic attenuation at A2 suggests that the signal is localized to the site where pathology is typically most pronounced (A1) rather than reflecting a non-specific global effect. These repeated-measures relationships were quantified using repeated-measures correlation, which estimates a common within-person association while accounting for the non-independence of repeated observations [33]. From a longitudinal perspective, ECRpre showed an overall decline over Weeks 1–4 with minor week-to-week variability, suggesting that the tendon–pulley interface environment progressively shifted toward lower ECRpre levels rather than fluctuating transiently. Accordingly, integrating longitudinal ECRpre trends with symptom tracking may offer an objective profile of interface conditions [29]. Nevertheless, to make ECR-based changes clinically interpretable at the individual level, measurement properties and actionable thresholds remain to be established, including responsiveness as well as minimal detectable change (MDC) and minimal clinically important difference (MCID); these will require validation in larger cohorts using anchors appropriate to the study context [34–36]. Because this pilot study did not include healthy controls, normative ECR values and diagnostic thresholds should be understood as future validation targets rather than quantities derivable from the present dataset.

To evaluate whether the observed ECR–symptom relationships merely reflected parallel improvement over time, we performed a fixed-effects robustness analysis adjusting for both subject-specific differences and time effects. Under this specification, the estimates reflect individual deviations from each subject’s own trajectory, rather than between-person differences or shared time trends. After adjustment, the association between A1 ECRpre and VAS remained directionally consistent, suggesting that the A1 ECR–pain relationship was not solely explained by shared time trends. In this model, the coefficient β = 29.19 provides a quantitative scale for interpreting the magnitude of the ECR–VAS association. Taking the reciprocal of this coefficient gives 0.034, indicating that an approximate 0.034-unit decrease in normalized A1 ECRpre would correspond to a 1-point lower VAS score under this model. This estimate should be interpreted as a model-based indication of effect magnitude within this pilot cohort, rather than as a definitive minimal clinically important difference (MCID), diagnostic cut point, or treatment threshold. Meanwhile, the corresponding A2 relationship was comparatively weaker, reinforcing the anatomical primacy of A1 in trigger finger and further supporting site specificity. Together, these robustness results suggest that A1 ECRpre captures pain-related within-participant variation beyond shared time trends alone.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be addressed. First, this was an exploratory pilot study with a small sample (N = 9), which limits generalizability and precision of effect estimates. Although repeated measurements strengthened within-subject inference for the primary ECR analyses, associations involving multiple clinical outcomes (VAS, ST, FT, and FIT) should be interpreted as preliminary; analyses of ST, FT, and FIT may have been underpowered and susceptible to type II error. Second, a healthy control group was not included. As a result, normative ECR values could not be established, and the present study does not allow the definition of thresholds distinguishing “normal” from “pathological” biomechanical signals. Instead, the analytical framework was designed to evaluate within-subject changes and associations with clinical outcomes over time. Accordingly, ECR should be interpreted as a relative, within-subject marker of tendon–pulley interface conditions rather than an absolute diagnostic metric.

Third, while ECR is a biomechanically plausible proxy for tendon–pulley contact mechanics, measurements may be influenced by extrinsic factors such as skin hydration, ambient temperature, and subtle variations in sensor placement or fixation tension. Although weekly Tuesday measurements were standardized by using the same clinical setting, pre-marked skin landmarks, a consistent fixation method, stable-baseline confirmation, and trained study personnel following the same acquisition protocol, skin hydration and local skin temperature were not directly measured. Future studies should implement more tightly standardized acquisition procedures and formally quantify reliability (e.g., test–retest repeatability and placement reproducibility). Fourth, the follow-up window was limited, which restricts evaluation of longer-term stability of ECR–symptom relationships and their reproducibility across broader clinical trajectories. Finally, multiple exploratory associations were examined across pulleys and outcomes; therefore, findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger, more diverse cohorts. Such studies are needed to establish clinically actionable measurement thresholds (e.g., MDC/MCID) and to validate the clinical interpretability of ECR across patient subgroups and assessment contexts.

Conclusion

This pilot study provides preliminary evidence that wearable electrical contact resistance (ECR) measured during standardized digit motion can function as an objective metric that aligns with clinical outcomes in trigger finger. Across repeated weekly assessments, A1-pulley ECR demonstrated consistent within-session responsiveness and anatomically specific associations with patient-reported symptoms, supporting convergent validity with pain and triggering measures (FT and ST). Additional analyses suggested that the association between A1 ECR and pain was not simply due to both measures changing over time, but reflected a consistent within-subject relationship.

Moving forward, motion-acquired ECR appears feasible and complementary to interval-based symptom scales in trigger finger. Larger prospective studies are warranted to establish reliability and clinical interpretability, validate ECR against external biomechanical or imaging references, and derive actionable thresholds across broader settings and patient subgroups.
