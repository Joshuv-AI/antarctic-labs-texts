Surface tension of Bose-Einstein condensate at finite temperature
Nguyen Van Thu

Abstract
We examine the influence of nonzero temperature on a bounded surface of a dilute Bose gas confined by a hard wall, utilizing the Gross-Pitaevskii theory and the Bogoliubov-de Gennes equation to describe surface excitations. The theoretical calculations are compared with experimental data for liquid helium II, demonstrating excellent agreement. An empirical relation is found for the temperature-dependence of the surface tension. Furthermore, our findings indicate that the contribution of surface excitations remains below 0.7% in the most of recent experiments on Bose-Einstein condensate.



I
Introduction

The surface tension of classical as well as quantum liquids represents the free energy per unit area of their surface. It is a crucial parameter that plays a key role in determining the properties of any liquid
[
1
]
. In the wetting phenomenon, surface tension governs an ability of a liquid to spread upon contact with a substrate
[
1
,
2
]
.

In the study of wetting phenomena in Bose-Einstein condensates (BECs), the possibility of wetting induced by a BEC adsorbed on a hard wall was first investigated in 2004
[
3
]
. Subsequently, the wetting phase diagram of a BEC adsorbed on both hard and soft walls was also examined in Refs.
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, experimental verification of the wetting phenomenon in BECs has not yet been conducted and remains an open challenge. Several factors have been identified as potential obstacles, the foremost being the complexities associated with the production of a hard wall
[
8
]
, despite significant advancements in cooling technology
[
9
]
. A recently proposed alternative solution involves the use of a ternary BEC, wherein the hard wall is replaced by the interface between two condensed components, while the third component may wet this interface
[
8
,
10
]
. Another theoretical prediction suggests that the static properties of the prewetting state should be considered
[
7
]
. A common characteristic of existing studies on wetting in BECs is the reliance on the Gross-Pitaevskii theory, which inherently assumes that the system is at zero temperature. This approximation neglects the role of temperature, despite the fact that absolute zero has never been achieved, even with state-of-the-art technologies. Nevertheless, temperature may play a crucial role in wetting phenomena, yet, to the best of our knowledge, its effects have never been explicitly addressed.

The main aim of this study is to investigate the influence of nonzero temperature on the surface tension of a single BEC confined by a hard wall and to compare the findings with available experimental data for helium isotopes. In particular, the study focuses on assessing the contribution of surface excitations to surface tension. This effect may have profound implications for the feasibility of the wetting phenomenon in BECs.

II
Wave function and surface tension at zero temperature

We start by considering a BEC described the Lagrangian density
[
11
]

{\cal L}=\psi^{*}(\vec{r},t)\left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}\right)\psi(\vec{r},t)-\mu\left|\psi(\vec{r},t)\right|^{2}+\frac{g}{2}\left|\psi(\vec{r},t)\right|^{4},

(1)

wherein
\mu
is the chemical potential, which has the form
\mu=g\rho
in the mean field theory with
\rho
the particle density. The field operator
\psi(\vec{r},t)
depends on both the coordinate
\vec{r}
and time
t
. The interatomic interaction potential between the atoms can be chosen as the hard-sphere model. In the Born approximation, the strength of the interaction between pairwise atoms is determined via the
s
-wave scattering length
a_{s}
as
g=4\pi\hbar^{2}a_{s}/m
. Now, thermodynamic stability requires that
g>0
, i.e., the boson interactions are repulsive
[
11
,

Minimizing this Lagrangian with respect to field operator one arrives at the time-dependent Gross-Pitaevskii equation

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi^{*}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}\psi^{*}(\vec{r},t)+g\left|\psi^{*}(\vec{r},t)\right|^{2}\psi^{*}(\vec{r},t).

Our system exhibits homogeneity in the

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(z)=\psi(\vec{r},t)e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\mu t}.

Plugging (

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{d^{2}\psi_{0}(z)}{dz^{2}}-\mu\psi_{0}(z)+g\left|\psi_{0}(z)\right|^{2}\psi_{0}(z)=0.

(4)

The boundary condition is imposed that the wave function vanishes at the hard wall

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(0)=0.

(5)

Using the healing length
\xi=\hbar/\sqrt{2mg\rho}
one can introduce the dimensionless coordinate
\tilde{z}=z/\xi
. Therefore the time-independent GP equation (
4
) and the boundary condition (
28
) can be written in dimensionless form

\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}\tilde{\psi}_{0}(\tilde{z})}{d\tilde{z}^{2}}-\tilde{\psi}_{0}(\tilde{z})+\tilde{\psi}_{0}^{3}(\tilde{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}_{0}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(6)

where the normalized wave function is defined as
\tilde{\psi}_{0}(z)=\psi_{0}(z)/\sqrt{\rho}
. The solution for (
6
) has the well-known form

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}_{0}(\tilde{z})=\tanh\left(\frac{\tilde{z}}{\sqrt{2}}\right),

(7)

or in dimensional form

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(z)=\sqrt{\rho}\tanh\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}\xi}\right).

(8)

We now calculate the surface tension or wall tension. At zero temperature, the grand potential is read

\displaystyle\Omega=\int d^{3}\vec{r}\left(-\psi_{0}^{*}\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}\psi_{0}-\mu\psi_{0}^{2}+\frac{g}{2}\psi_{0}^{4}\right),

(9)

or in dimensionless form

\displaystyle\Omega={\cal A}P\xi\int d\tilde{z}\left(\tilde{\psi}_{0}^{*}\frac{d^{2}\tilde{\psi}_{0}}{d\tilde{z}^{2}}-\tilde{\psi}_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\psi}_{0}^{4}\right),

(10)

in which
{\cal A}=\int dxdy
is the area of the surface and

is the bulk pressure.

By multiplying both sides of the above Eq. (
6

and integrating by parts with respect to
\tilde{z}
, under the constraint imposed by the boundary condition, one obtains the ”constant of motion”

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d\tilde{\psi}_{0}}{d\tilde{z}}\right)^{2}+\tilde{\psi}_{0}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\psi}_{0}^{4}=\frac{1}{2}.

(11)

Combining Eqs. (
11
) and (
10
) one arrives at

\displaystyle\gamma(0)=4P\xi\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{d\tilde{\psi}_{0}}{d\tilde{z}}\right)^{2}d\tilde{z}.

(12)

Inserting (
7
) into (

) one obtains the surface tension at zero temperature

\displaystyle\gamma(0)=\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}P\xi.

(13)

Eq. (

[

]

It is worthy of note that the quantum fluctuations arising from zero-point energy are neglected in Eq. (

III
Effect from surface excitations to surface tension

As already mentioned, the surface tension in Eq. (
13
) has been calculated within assumption that all bosonic atoms stay in the ground state at zero temperature. In fact, a number of the atoms is in the excited states. These atoms perform the surface excitation
[
11
]
.

To analyze the surface excitations, the wave function is expressed as the sum of the condensate wave function,
\psi_{0}(z)
, and the non-condensed component,

\delta\psi(\vec{r}_{\perp},t)

[
11
]

\displaystyle\psi^{*}(\vec{r},t)=\left[\psi_{0}(z)+\delta\psi(\vec{r}_{\perp},t)\right]e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\mu t},

(14)

in which
\vec{r}_{\perp}=(x,y)
lies in the plane orthogonal to
z
-axis. Substituting (
14
) into (
2
) one arrives at the equation for the non-condensed part up to first order

\displaystyle i\hbar\frac{\partial\delta\psi(\vec{r}_{\perp},t)}{\partial t}=\left(-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}-\mu+2g\psi_{0}^{2}\right)\delta\psi(\vec{r}_{\perp},t)+g\psi_{0}^{2}\delta\psi^{*}(\vec{r}_{\perp},t).

(15)

The wave function of the non-condensed part is now disturbed as a sum of plane waves with the wave vector
\vec{k}
traveling within the surface in
\vec{r}_{\perp}=(x,y)
-plane
[
15
]

\displaystyle\delta\psi(\vec{r}_{\perp},t)=u_{\vec{k}}(z)\exp(i\vec{k}\vec{r}_{\perp}-i\omega t)+v^{*}_{\vec{k}}(z)\exp(-i\vec{k}\vec{r}_{\perp}+i\omega t),

(16)

in which
u_{\vec{k}}
and
v_{\vec{k}}
are magnitude of the plane wave. The angular frequency
\omega=\omega(k)
allows us to determine the energy of the surface excitation
\varepsilon_{k}=\hbar\omega

Substituting Eq. (
16
) into (
14
) and then (
2

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-k^{2}\right)-\mu+2g\psi_{0}^{2}\right]u_{\vec{k}}+g\psi_{0}^{2}v_{\vec{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}u_{\vec{k}},

(17a)

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-k^{2}\right)-\mu+2g\psi_{0}^{2}\right]v_{\vec{k}}+g\psi_{0}^{2}u_{\vec{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon_{k}v_{\vec{k}}.

(17b)

For the hard wall boundary, the surface excitations have to be satisfied the normalization conditions

\displaystyle\int\left(\left|u_{\vec{k}}\right|^{2}-\left|v_{\vec{k}}\right|^{2}\right)dz=1.

(18)

Introducing the dimensionless wave vector
\kappa=k\xi
and two functions

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\vec{k}}=u_{\vec{k}}+v_{\vec{k}},

\displaystyle\Delta_{\vec{k}}=u_{\vec{k}}-v_{\vec{k}},

(19)

the BdG equations (
17

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&{\cal D}_{k}-{\cal F}_{k}\\
{\cal D}_{k}+{\cal F}_{k}&0\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Sigma_{\vec{k}}\\
\Delta_{\vec{k}}\\
\end{array}\right)=\tilde{\varepsilon}_{k}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Sigma_{\vec{k}}\\
\Delta_{\vec{k}}\\
\end{array}\right),

in which

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{d^{2}}{d\tilde{z}^{2}}-\kappa\right)-1+2\tanh^{2}\left(\frac{\tilde{z}}{\sqrt{2}}\right),

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tanh^{2}\left(\frac{\tilde{z}}{\sqrt{2}}\right),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon_{k}}{g\rho_{0}}.

The boundary condition for

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\vec{k}}(0)=\Delta_{\vec{k}}(0)=0,

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime}_{\vec{k}}(0)=0.

The BdG equations (
III
) together with boundary condition (
28
) are proven yielding two kinds of excitations, which are ripplon or capillary wave and surface phonon
[
17
]
. The ripplon mode with the dispersion relation

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}_{\kappa}=\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}\kappa^{3},

(29)

or in dimensional form

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}=\hbar\sqrt{\frac{\gamma(0)}{m\rho_{0}}}k^{3/2}.

(30)

The second kind is the surface phonon with the dispersion relation

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}=\hbar\sqrt{\frac{g\rho_{0}}{m}}k.

(31)

We now proceed to examine the contribution of surface excitations to surface tension. As previously stated, these excitations propagate within the surface near the hard wall in the plane
\vec{r}_{\perp}=(x,y)
. This characteristic implies that the system exhibits a two-dimensional topological singularity. At a given nonzero temperature
T
, the surface tension is expressed as follows

18
,
19

\displaystyle\gamma\equiv\gamma(0)+\gamma(T)=\gamma(0)+k_{B}T\sum_{i}\int\frac{d^{2}\vec{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\ln\left(1-e^{-\varepsilon_{k}^{(i)}/k_{B}T}\right),

(32)

where
k_{B}
represents the Boltzmann constant, and

denotes ripplons and phonons, respectively. The summation accounts for contributions from capillary waves and surface phonons. Due to the two-dimensional topological singularity, the second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (

\displaystyle\gamma^{(i)}(T)=\frac{k_{B}T}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}k\ln\left(1-e^{-\varepsilon_{k}^{(i)}/k_{B}T}\right).

(33)

For ripplons, substituting Eq. (
30

\displaystyle\gamma^{\rm(r)}(T)=-\frac{\Gamma(7/3)\zeta(7/3)(m\rho)^{2/3}}{4\pi\hbar^{4/3}\gamma(0)^{2/3}}(k_{B}T)^{7/3}.

Eq. (

\displaystyle\gamma^{\rm(p)}(T)=-\frac{\zeta(3)m}{2\pi\hbar^{2}g\rho}(k_{B}T)^{3}.

Due to the extremely small magnitude of the gas parameter, the contribution of temperature to the surface tension is negligible. Moreover, in the most BEC experiments,

This result provides unequivocal evidence that the influence of nonzero temperature on the surface tension of a BEC is very small.

IV
Conclusion

In the preceding sections, the influence of surface excitations on the surface tension of a single BEC confined by a hard wall has been investigated. These surface excitations comprise ripplons and phonons propagating along the bounded surface. When applied to liquid helium II, an empirical relation for surface tension as a function of temperature is derived, exhibiting close agreement with experimental data. A comparison with previous results has been conducted, demonstrating the superiority of our empirical relation.

For a single BEC, our analysis indicates that the contribution of nonzero temperature to surface tension is less than 0.7% in recent experiments. Notably, in our calculations, the contribution of the zero-point energy of surface excitations has been neglected. Taking into account the zero-point energy, which will cause the quantum fluctuations. In that case, the pressure in the surface tension at zero temperature becomes
[
14
]

\displaystyle P=\frac{1}{2}g\rho^{2}\left(1+\frac{128}{15\sqrt{\pi}}\alpha_{s}^{1/2}\right).

(47)

Nonetheless, this contribution is sufficiently small.

Regarding the wetting phenomenon in BECs, the interface tension between components and/or wall tension must be considered
[
27
]
