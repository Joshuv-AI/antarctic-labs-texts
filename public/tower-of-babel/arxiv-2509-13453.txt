From virtual Z Z gates to virtual Z Z pulses
Christopher K. Long, Crispin H. W. Barnes

Abstract
Virtual Z Z gates have become integral for implementing fast, high-fidelity single-qubit operations. However, virtual Z Z gates require that the system’s two-qubit gates are microwave-activated or normalise the single-qubit Z Z rotations—the group generated by X X , SWAP \operatorname{SWAP} , and arbitrary phase gates. Herein, we extend the theory of virtual Z Z gates to the pulse-level, which underlies both gate design and the recent advancements of pulse-level quantum algorithms. These algorithms attempt to utilise the full potential of present-day noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices by removing overheads associated with the compilation and transpilation of gates. To extend the theory of virtual Z Z gates, we derive a platform-agnostic theoretical framework for virtual Z Z pulses by employing time dilations of the pulse sequences that control the quantum processor. Additionally, we provide worked examples of the implementation of virtual Z Z pulses on both semiconductor spin qubit and superconducting quantum processor architectures. Moreover, we present a general overview of the hardware support for virtual Z Z pulses. We find virtual Z Z pulses (and thus, virtual Z Z gates) can be used on hardware that, with previous methods, did not support the virtual Z Z gate. Finally, we present two additional applications of virtual Z Z pulses to pulse-level algorithms. First, broadening the class of Hamiltonians that can be natively simulated in an analogue manner. Second, increasing the expressibility of pulse-based variational quantum algorithms.

II A 1
The virtual frame

Suppose we have a time-dependent system Hamiltonian
H\left(t\right)
, but desire to implement

H_{\textrm{eff}}\left(t\right)\coloneqq H\left(t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\left(t\right)Z_{i}

(1)

over the time interval
t\in\left[T\right]\coloneqq\left[0,T\right]
, where
Z_{i}
is the Pauli-
Z
operator on the
i
th qubit. We will achieve this by virtually introducing the drive terms
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\left(t\right)Z_{i}
. The unitary that implements the desired time evolution is the solution to the Schrödinger equation:

\derivative{t}U\left(t\right)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H\left(t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\left(t\right)Z_{i}\right]U\left(t\right),\quad\forall t\in\left[T\right].

(2)

Making the substitution

U\left(t\right)=R\left(t\right)\tilde{U}\left(t\right),\quad\forall t\in\left[T\right],

(3)

with

\displaystyle R\left(t\right)\coloneqq\exp\left(-\frac{i}{2\hbar}\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}\left(t\right)Z_{i}\right)

(4)

and

\displaystyle V_{i}\left(t\right)\coloneqq V_{i0}+\int_{0}^{t}\differential{x}v_{i}\left(x\right)\quad\forall i\in\left\llbracket n\right\rrbracket,

(5)

where
\left\llbracket n\right\rrbracket
is the set of positive integers 1 through
n
inclusive, we move into
the virtual frame
. In which we find

\derivative{t}\tilde{U}\left(t\right)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}R^{\dagger}\left(t\right)H\left(t\right)R\left(t\right)\tilde{U}\left(t\right),\quad\forall t\in\left[T\right].

(6)

The effective Hamiltonian in the virtual frame is

\tilde{H}(t)\coloneqq R^{\dagger}\left(t\right)H\left(t\right)R\left(t\right),\quad\forall t\in\left[T\right].

(7)

In other words, the virtual frame is the unique rotating frame, up to a constant rotation, within which the effective Hamiltonian is instantaneously unitarily equivalent to our system Hamiltonian
H\left(t\right)
at all times. The constant rotation,

\exp\left(-\frac{i}{2\hbar}\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i0}Z_{i}\right),

(8)

allows prior virtual
Z
gates to be pulled through the evolution.

Thus, providing that we physically implement
\tilde{H}(t)
in the laboratory frame, then we can implement the evolution from a time
t=0
to
t=T
under the Hamiltonian
H_{\textrm{eff}}\left(t\right)
. This can be achieved as follows: Evolve the system with
\tilde{H}\left(t\right)
from a time
t=0
to
t=T
to induce the unitary evolution
\tilde{U}\left(T\right)
. Then, Apply the
Z
rotations
R(T)
. In many cases, these
Z
rotations can be absorbed into the measurement operators or can be implemented as virtual
Z
gates
McKay et al. 2017
;
Meunier et al. 2011
;
Knill et al. 2008
;
Vandersypen and Chuang 2005
.

II A 3
Rotating frames

Importantly, the Hamiltonian that implements the virtual
Z

\displaystyle=\hat{O}^{\dagger}(t)\tilde{H}(t)\hat{O}(t)-i\hbar\hat{O}^{\dagger}(t)\derivative{\hat O}{t},

(15)

However, using the commutation relations in

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\textrm{eff}}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{H}\left(t\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\left(t\right)Z_{i}

\displaystyle\textrm{and}\quad\hat{\tilde{H}}(t)

\displaystyle=R^{\dagger}\left(t\right)\hat{H}\left(t\right)R\left(t\right),

(17)

for all
t\in\left[T\right]
. Thus,

1

7

Finally, consider transforming

into the

\hat{H}^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)=\hat{O}^{\dagger}\circ f\left(\tau\right)H^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)\hat{O}\circ f\left(\tau\right)\\
-i\hbar\hat{O}^{\dagger}\circ f\left(\tau\right)\derivative{f}{\tau}\left.\derivative{\hat O}{t}\right|_{t=f\left(\tau\right)},\quad\forall\tau\in\mathcal{T}.

Now, substituting in

\mathcal{E}_{ij}\left(0\right)

\displaystyle J_{ij}^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)\!

\displaystyle=\!J_{ij}\left(\tau\right),

(63)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}I_{k}^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)\\
Q_{k}^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)\end{bmatrix}\!

\displaystyle=\!\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\!\frac{V_k\left(\tau\right)}{\hbar}\!)&-\sin(\!\frac{V_k\left(\tau\right)}{\hbar}\!)\\
\sin(\!\frac{V_k\left(\tau\right)}{\hbar}\!)&\phantom{-}\cos(\!\frac{V_k\left(\tau\right)}{\hbar}\!)\end{bmatrix}\!\!\!\begin{bmatrix}I_{k}\left(\tau\right)\\
Q_{k}\left(\tau\right)\end{bmatrix},

(64)

where we did not need a time dilation, so
t=\tau
.

However, for transversal couplings, we must use

\displaystyle J_{ij}^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)\!

\displaystyle=\!\frac{J_{ij}\circ f\left(\tau\right)}{\displaystyle 1+\frac{\Delta^{-}v_{ij}\circ f\left(\tau\right)}{\hbar\left(\omega_{j}-\omega_{i}\right)}},

(65)

Q_{k}^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)\end{bmatrix}\!

\displaystyle=\!\frac{\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\!\frac{V_k\circ f\left(\tau\right)}{\hbar}\!)&-\sin(\!\frac{V_k\circ f\left(\tau\right)}{\hbar}\!)\\

(66)

where
f\left(\tau\right)
is the solution to

f\left(\tau\right)+\frac{\Delta^{-}V_{ij}\circ f\left(\tau\right)}{\hbar\left(\omega_{j}-\omega_{i}\right)}=\tau.

(67)

For flux-tunable superconducting qubits, the qubit frequencies depend on the flux applied to the qubit

III B 3
Cross-resonance superconducting qubits

For cross-resonance superconducting qubits
Krantz et al. 2019
, neither the fluxes nor the couplings are tunable. However, by utilising the Schrieffer-Wolff transformation
Malekakhlagh et al. 2020
to decouple the states in the coupling resonators from the computational space to leading order, we obtain the following Hamiltonian
Krantz et al. 2019
;
Malekakhlagh et al. 2020
:

H_{\textrm{cr}}\left(t\right)=-\frac{\hbar}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}Z_{i}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Omega_{i}\left(t\right)\left[\nu_{ij}X_{j}+\mu_{ij}Z_{i}X_{j}\right],

(75)

where
\nu_{ii}=1
and
\mu_{ii}=0
.
Moving to the rotating frame of the qubits,

\hat{K}_{\textrm{cr}}\coloneqq\exp(i\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n\omega_iZ_it),

(76)

the Hamiltonian becomes

\hat{H}_{\textrm{cr}}\left(t\right)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Omega_{i}\left(t\right)[\nu_{ij}\cos(\omega_jt)X_{j}-\nu_{ij}\sin(\omega_jt)Y_{j}\\
+\mu_{ij}\cos(\omega_jt)Z_{i}X_{j}-\mu_{ij}\sin(\omega_jt)Z_{i}Y_{j}].

(77)

To yield sufficient degrees of freedom to implement virtual
Z
pulses, we assume single-qubit drives take the following local form

\Omega_{i}\left(t\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}I_{ij}\left(t\right)\cos(\omega_jt)+Q_{ij}\left(t\right)\sin(\omega_jt).

(78)

Substituting for
\Omega_{i}\left(t\right)
and dropping oscillating terms in the rotating wave approximation, we find the approximate Hamiltonian
Malekakhlagh et al. 2020

\hat{H}_{\textrm{cr}}\left(t\right)\approx\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\nu_{ij}I_{ij}\left(t\right)X_{j}-\nu_{ij}Q_{ij}\left(t\right)Y_{j}\\
+\mu_{ij}I_{ij}\left(t\right)Z_{i}X_{j}-\mu_{ij}Q_{ij}\left(t\right)Z_{i}Y_{j}].

(79)

Thus, to implement virtual
Z
pulses approximately, we use

\begin{bmatrix}I_{ij}^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)\\
Q_{ij}^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)\end{bmatrix}\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}\cos(\!\frac{V_j\left(\tau\right)}{\hbar}\!)&\sin(\!\frac{V_j\left(\tau\right)}{\hbar}\!)\\
-\sin(\!\frac{V_j\left(\tau\right)}{\hbar}\!)&\cos(\!\frac{V_j\left(\tau\right)}{\hbar}\!)\end{bmatrix}\!\!\!\begin{bmatrix}I_{ij}\left(\tau\right)\\
Q_{ij}\left(\tau\right)\end{bmatrix},

(80)

where we did not need a time dilation, so
t=\tau
.

IV
Potential Applications

We have considered how to implement virtual
Z
pulses on several platforms. Now we turn to the potential applications of virtual
Z
pulses. We identify three applications: gate design (
Section

IV A
), variational quantum algorithms (
Section

IV B
), and Hamiltonian simulation (
Section

IV C
).

IV A
Wider hardware support for virtual
Z
gates

An existing method for implementing virtual
Z
gates is using frame tracking
Wei et al. 2024
;
Singh et al. 2024
. Frame tracking allows virtual
Z
gates to be pulled through any multi-qubit gate that normalises the Lie group generated by weight-one Pauli-
Z
strings,
L\coloneqq\exp(i\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\left\{Z_i\right\}_{i=1}^n)
. These multi-qubit gates form the normaliser group

\mathcal{N}\coloneqq\left\{u\in\operatorname{U}(2^{n}):uL=Lu\right\},

(81)

which is generated by
X_{1}
,
\left\{\text{SWAP}_{i,i+1}\right\}_{i=1}^{n-1}
, and arbitrary phase gates,
\exp(i\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}\left\{I,Z\right\}^{\otimes n})
—see
Appendix

E
for a proof. This group includes some notable two-qubit gates which are compatible with virtual
Z
gates: SWAP,
i
SWAP,
b
SWAP, and CPHASE. However, CNOT and general powers of SWAP,
i
SWAP, and
b
SWAP are not in
\mathcal{N}
. Thus, many parameterised two-qubit gates do not support the use of virtual
Z
gates. The two existing solutions are utilising microwave-activated multi-qubit gates
Wei et al. 2022
;
Lin et al. 2025
;
Dogan et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2022
;
Roy et al. 2020
;
Roy et al. 2018
;
Ganzhorn et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2024
, or implementing physical
Z
gates either via idling or a combination of
X
and
Y
rotations. This work presents a new alternative: By employing virtual
Z
pulses, we can pull virtual
Z
gates through pulses that generate CNOT and arbitrary powers of SWAP,
i
SWAP, and
b
SWAP. In fact, we can pull virtual
Z
pulses through any two-qubit gate generated by one of the Hamiltonian algebras
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{Z}\cup\mathcal{C}_{\pm}
or
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{Z}\cup\mathcal{Q}_{k}
supplemented with simultaneous driving of
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
via
I_{i}(t)
and
Q_{i}(t)
—
i.e.
, any
\operatorname{U}(2)
gate, as all four Hamiltonian algebras yield the full
\mathfrak{u}(2)
dynamic Lie algebra. As the virtual
Z
gate is not applied simultaneously with the two-qubit gate the pulse distortion is similar to applying physical
Z
gates. The distortion corresponds to an ideling delay before applying the two-qubit gate. However, the delay is not chosen to implement the whole
Z
rotation, but to accumulate the phase that will not commute through the two-qubit gate.

IV B
Variational quantum algorithms

Variational quantum algorithms (VQAs)
Cerezo et al. 2021
;
Tilly et al. 2022
;
Blekos et al. 2024
are a promising contender for demonstrating quantum advantage on noisy intermediate-scale quantum devices. VQAs evolve an initial state on a quantum computer under an ansatz specified by a parameterised unitary. Next, VQAs measure a cost function of the output state. A classical optimiser then minimises the cost function.

In general, virtual
Z
pulses could be employed to increase the expressiveness of pulse-based ansätze
Ebadi et al. 2022
;
Magann et al. 2021
;
Sherbert et al. 2025
;
Meitei et al. 2021
;
Egger et al. 2023
;
Ibrahim et al. 2022
;
Meirom and Frankel 2023
;
Araz et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2017
;
Asthana et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Lu et al. 2017
;
Long et al. 2025a
and potentially decrease the evolution time
Asthana et al. 2023
;
Long et al. 2025a
. As the optimiser already optimises the pulse shapes, we can absorb the pulse distortions into the optimiser: We only need to prepend and append parameterised
Z
rotations to the ansatz.

Due to the constraints outlined above, not all virtual
Z
pulses can be pulled through the remaining pulse sequence. However, we can try the reverse process to pull the virtual
Z
pulses through to the beginning of the pulse. This, of course, also may not work. Nonetheless, this justifies both appending and prepending parameterised
Z
rotations to the ansatz.

Unfortunately, two common cases yield either the appended or prepended parameterised
Z
rotations redundant: (i) If the measurements are in the computational basis (
e.g.
, QAOA
Farhi et al. 2014
;
Blekos et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2017
for problems such as MaxCut
Farhi et al. 2014
;
Zhu et al. 2022
;
Yanakiev et al. 2024
or financial modeling
Hodson et al. 2019
;
Brandhofer et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Yuan et al. 2024
) the virtual
Z
pulses will not increase the expressiveness as they will not affect the measurement outcome. (ii) Similarly, if the input state is a computational basis state (
e.g.
, the Hartree-Fock state within the Jordan-Wigner encoding for molecular simulations
McArdle et al. 2020
), then virtual
Z
pulses will not increase the expressiveness.

In many cases, the prepended and appended parameterised
Z
rotations can be virtualised. The appended
Z
rotations can be absorbed into the measurements by updating the measurement basis. The prepended
Z
rotations can be absorbed into the initial state preparation. For example, for QAOA, it is common to take the initial state as
\ket{+}^{\otimes n}
. By absorbing the parameterised
Z
rotation, we take the initial state to be
\left[\cos(\theta)\ket{+}+i\sin(\theta)\ket{-}\right]^{\otimes n}
. The original initial state can be prepared with single-qubit Hadamard gates; the new initial state can be prepared with the same pulse sequence but a shifted microwave phase.

IV C
Hamiltonian simulation

Hamiltonian simulation
Altman et al. 2021
is both the seminal
Feynman 1982
and one of the most promising use cases for quantum computers
Altman et al. 2021
. Virtual
Z
pulses increase the number of Hamiltonians we can perform analogue simulations of. As an example, we will consider semiconductor spin qubits
Burkard et al. 2023
. In
Section

III A
, we found that in the rotating frame of the qubits,

\hat{K}_{{}\mathrm{Si}}(t)\coloneqq\exp(i\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n\omega_iZ_it),

(82)

we could implement the effective Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{{}\mathrm{Si}\textrm{ eff}}\left(t\right)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[I_{i}\left(t\right)X_{i}+Q_{i}\left(t\right)Y_{i}+v_{i}(t)Z_{i}\right]\\
+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{i,j=1}\\
{:i<j}\end{subarray}}^{n}J_{ij}\left(t\right)\mathcal{E}_{ij}\left(t\right),

(83)

providing that, at each
t\in\left[T\right]
and for each
i
, there was at most one value of
j
for which
J_{ij}(t)\neq 0
. Moving to the rotating frame with the average Zeeman splitting,

\hat{K}_{{}\mathrm{Si}}^{\textrm{avg}}(t)\coloneqq\exp(i\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i,j=1}^n\omega_iZ_jt),

(84)

the Hamiltonian becomes

\hat{\hat{H}}_{{}\mathrm{Si}\textrm{ eff}}\left(t\right)=\\
-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\left(I_{i}\left(t\right)\cos(\Delta\omega_i t)+Q_{i}\left(t\right)\sin(\Delta\omega_i t)\right)X_{i}\\
+\left(Q_{i}\left(t\right)\cos(\Delta\omega_i t)-I_{i}\left(t\right)\sin(\Delta\omega_i t)\right)Y_{i}+v_{i}(t)Z_{i}]\\
+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{i,j=1}\\
{:i<j}\end{subarray}}^{n}J_{ij}\left(t\right)\left[X_{i}X_{j}+Y_{i}Y_{j}+Z_{i}Z_{j}\right].

(85)

Next, by choosing

\begin{bmatrix}I_{i}\left(t\right)\\
Q_{i}\left(t\right)\\
v_{i}\left(t\right)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\Delta\omega_it)&-\sin(\Delta\omega_it)&0\\
\sin(\Delta\omega_it)&\phantom{-}\cos(\Delta\omega_it)&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\vec{B}_{i}(t),

(86)

we can simulate a Heisenberg Hamiltonian with arbitrary magnetic fields:

\hat{\hat{H}}_{{}\mathrm{Si}\textrm{ eff}}\left(t\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\vec{B}_{i}(t)\cdot\vec{\sigma}_{i}\\
+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{i,j=1}\\
{:i<j}\end{subarray}}^{n}J_{ij}\left(t\right)\left[X_{i}X_{j}+Y_{i}Y_{j}+Z_{i}Z_{j}\right].

(87)

Note that without the virtual
Z
pulses, we would only be able to natively simulate a Heisenberg Hamiltonian

with the same Zeeman splittings as the qubits up to a

global field:
B_{i}^{(z)}(t)=\hbar\omega_{i}+B_{\textrm{global}}^{(z)}
. One use of this is preparing the ground states of Heisenberg Hamiltonians with adiabatic transitions
Farhi et al. 2000
.

V
Discussion and conclusion

Within this article, we have presented a theoretical framework for implementing virtual
Z
pulses. This framework is architecture-independent, and we demonstrate example pulse shapes for semiconductor spin qubits and superconducting qubits. In contrast to virtual
Z
gates, we find that virtual
Z
pulses can only be implemented approximately on most platforms. Thus, much like the theory of Rabi-oscillations, which also employs the rotating wave approximation, this framework should act as the first step in designing a pulse sequence before applying pulse-shaping methods
Glaser et al. 2015
;
Mahesh et al. 2023
;
Müller et al. 2022
to correct the errors introduced by the approximations. Alternatively, if virtual
Z
pulses are utilised as degrees of freedom within a variational quantum algorithm, then the classical optimiser can compensate for the approximation errors.

We find that virtual
Z
pulses can be applied to more hardware platforms than virtual
Z
gates. Virtual
Z
gates require the two-qubit gates to either be microwave-activated or to normalise the single-qubit
Z
rotations. In contrast, virtual
Z
pulses have a weaker constraint on the Hamiltonian algebra. This allows more freedom in architecture and gate design. We demonstrate that this increased freedom allows for more Hamiltonians to be natively simulated in an analogue manner.

Further, we found that on many platforms, virtual
Z
pulses can only be implemented if each qubit is coupled to at most one other qubit simultaneously. The trivial exception is qubits coupled by a
ZZ
interaction. However, the more interesting exception is cross-resonance superconducting qubits.

Much like their gate-based counterparts, we anticipate that virtual
Z
pulses will allow for faster and more robust quantum information processing. Additionally, virtual
Z
pulses open a new zoo of native evolutions with increased flexibility and tunability.
