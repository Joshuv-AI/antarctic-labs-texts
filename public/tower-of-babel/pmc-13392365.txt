Orbital pacing of environmental perturbations in a Greenhouse World: a 40-Myr record from Upper Devonian—Lower Carboniferous successions on the eastern margin of Gondwana
Authors: Omid Falahatkhah, Arthur John Mory, Ali Kadkhodaie, Omar Al-Fatlawi, Dhifaf Sadeq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrbital pacing of environmental changes during the Late Devonian–Early Carboniferous (Famennian–Viséan, ~ 372–330 Ma) greenhouse Earth is investigated using high-resolution gamma-ray (GR) logs, chemostratigraphy (ICP-OES, ICP-MS, pXRF), and cyclostratigraphic analysis (multitaper method, wavelet transforms, Fischer plots, Dynamic Noise after Orbital Tuning sea-level modeling) of two petroleum wells from the Jurgurra Terrace, Canning Basin, Western Australia. The sedimentary successions (Nullara, May River, Laurel, Anderson formations) reveal a ubiquitous ~ 5-Myr orbital eccentricity amplitude modulation cycle, along with shorter Milankovitch cycles (405-kyr long-eccentricity, 100-kyr short-eccentricity, 40–50-kyr obliquity, 20–25-kyr precession), and sedimentation rates of 3.5–4.5 cm/kyr derived from evolutionary Time Optimization (eTimeOpt) and orbital tuning. Key findings are: (1) The Hangenberg Event (HE; ~ 3925–4030 m in Rafael 1) and Lower Alum Shale Event (LASE; ~ 3690–3770 m) coincide with 405-kyr eccentricity maxima, imposing monsoonal intensification, nutrient input, and euxinia (recorded by high V/Cr, U/Th, and organic carbon burial). (2) The Late Tournaisian Cooling Event (LTCE; ~ 3500 m) is synchronous with 100-kyr eccentricity-driven glacial-interglacial cycles, with lowered sea level, high Rb/Sr values, and siliciclastic progradation. (3) Wavelet analysis reveals a hierarchical orbital structure in which precession-paced ventilation ended anoxic intervals (e.g., post-HE re-oxygenation), whereas Fischer plots show highstand systems tracts equate to condensed, organic-rich sediment at eccentricity maxima. Geochemical proxies (Sr/Ba, Mg/Ca) also record salinity fluctuations that can be linked to orbital-scale hydrological cycling. DYNOT modeling illustrates that ~ 5-Myr amplitude modulation cycles controlled long-term climate stability, enhancing redox and sea-level extremes. These cycles, supported by global analogs (Ordovician–Silurian, Late Cenozoic), emphasize orbital forcing as the leading cause of greenhouse climate instability and its implications for determining Earth’s climatic sensitivity in the absence of ice. Introduction

The Late Devonian to Early Carboniferous (Famennian–Viséan, ~ 372–330 Ma) was a pivotal period in Earth’s history, marked by extended periods of greenhouse conditions interspersed with sudden biotic and climatic crises1, 2, 3, 4. The Hangenberg Event (HE), close to the Devonian–Carboniferous (D–C) boundary was amongst the most severe Phanerozoic mass extinctions and was characterized by widespread anoxia, the collapse of marine ecosystems, and glacio-eustatic sea-level fluctuations2, 5. It was followed by an extinction event at the D–C boundary (~ 358.9 Ma), and marine transgressions in the Tournaisian and Viséan6, 7. During this period, there was a paradoxical coincidence of high atmospheric CO2 levels and ice-free conditions, at odds with traditional ideas of climate stability in greenhouse environments8, 9.

Mounting sedimentological and geochemical data challenges the earlier, long-held assumption that the ecological aftermath of a mass extinction is a generally stable environment. Recent studies completed by10, 11 on European sequences provide new evidence that the Mississippian was subjected to long-term climatic variability with episodic marine-associated anoxia. Such variability added additional stressors to biota recovering from the extinction event, and possibly required additional time to recover fully from the mass extinction event. The middle Tournaisian Lower Alum Shale Event (LASE) is defined by an additional phase of anoxia with increased organic carbon burial, emphasizing yet another perturbation of oceanic redox chemistry10. The following late Tournaisian Cooling Event (LTCE) was accompanied by low-latitude glaciation, emphasizing the intricate interdependencies across carbon cycling, climate, and ocean circulation12. Overall, these events suggest a long post-HE recovery phase characterized by cascading feedbacks between orbital forcing, hydrospheric reorganization, and redox-sensitive biogeochemical processes. This extended era of environmental turmoil is a critical backdrop that can be used to test the resilience and thresholds of ecosystems under prolonged global change.

Sedimentary successions along the eastern margin of Gondwana have preserved a complex array of Late Devonian paleoenvironmental changes including vibrant carbonate reefs in shallow marine environments fringing basin depocentres especially in the Canning Basin13, 14, 15, 16. These carbonate-dominated ecosystems, formed by corals, sponges, and stromatoporoids, flourished in a warm, stable “Greenhouse” climate marked by elevated CO₂ concentrations and minimal ice coverage17. Stratigraphic units such as the Famennian Pillara and Nullara Formations serve as prime examples of these reef systems, which nurtured a rich diversity of marine organisms and signified a period of climatic stability spanning the late Givetian to late Famennian14, 15. During the transition from the Late Devonian to the Early Carboniferous, there was a profound change in the landscape. The previously dominant reef complexes were replaced by shallow marine platform facies and terrestrial, deltaic, fluvial, and distributary bay systems. The transition from carbonate to siliciclastic sedimentation indicates an active interplay among sea-level changes, tectonic activity, and climatic changes, perhaps marking the onset of cooler ambient conditions.

Despite extensive research on these environmental changes, the role of astronomical forcing in pacing the tempo of these events remains poorly constrained. Recent advances in cyclostratigraphic analysis have demonstrated that orbital variations have a profound impact on the climate system of the Earth over a broad range of timescales (e.g.18). The ~ 5 Myr orbital cycles found in Famennian–Viséan sequences suggest orbital controls on environmental disturbance timing and amplitude. These extended cycles, most likely linked with the modulation of Earth’s orbital parameters (eccentricity–obliquity, c.f.19), appear to coincide with maxima or minima in power spectra of orbital cycles, which likely regulated periodic fluctuations between warm and cold climates. Milankovitch cycles are regular fluctuations in Earth’s orbit and axial tilt that modulate the distribution and intensity of solar radiation, thereby influencing climate over geological timescales20. The objective of this study is to recognize orbital pacing signals preserved in the sedimentary record of the Jurgurra Terrace of Canning Basin. By using a high-resolution stratigraphic analysis of the Nullara, May River, Laurel, and Anderson formations, in conjunction with cyclostratigraphy and astrochronology methods, we aim to identify sedimentary cycles corresponding to established floating orbital time scales. The research is intended to clarify processes by which orbital forcing regulated sea level, climate, and biotic evolution during the Famennian to Viséan. By reconstructing the sequence of events across the D–C boundary to late Tournaisian, this work advances a unified model of greenhouse climate instability, where orbital cycles set the tempo for anoxia, salinity crises, and thermal swings, with implications for understanding climate resilience in Earth’s ancient greenhouse analogs.

Geological background

Stratigraphy

With an area of about 795,000 km2, the Canning Basin in northwest Australia is one of the country’s largest Phanerozoic sedimentary basins. From the Ordovician to the Cretaceous, there were several periods of extension, subsidence, and compression in its tectonic history21. The Jurgurra Terrace, a significant structural feature in the northwest of the basin that maintains a well-exposed and continuously cored succession of Upper Devonian (Famennian) to Lower Carboniferous (Tournaisian–Viséan) strata, is the subject of this study22. The basin was located on the eastern edge of Gondwana, close to the paleo-equator, during the Famennian (~ 372–359 Ma), creating a warm, tropical climate that supported the widespread development of carbonate reefs (Fig. 1A7).

The strata of the Rafael 1 and Ungani 2 petroleum wells represent an important transition from marine to terrestrial environments. The strata consist of 4 distinct stratigraphic units, which all mark an important change in the sedimentary environments of the region (see Fig. 1B and C):

The Nullara Formation (Famennian) comprises primarily carbonate reefal and platform deposits and is considered to be at the peak of Devonian reef growth within the area. Laterally equivalent to the Clanmeyer Formation located in the Fitzroy Trough as described by Playford et al.7, the Nullara Formation is the most laterally extensive of all sediments deposited in the Devonian period.

The May River formation (latest Famennian–earliest Tournaisian), also referred to informally as the late Famennian to early Tournaisian, represents the first major change from carbonate-dominated to carbonate and terrigenous-dominated sedimentary rocks, suggesting increased terrigenous input from adjacent landmasses.

The Laurel Formation is a component of the Fairfield Group and was deposited in a deltaic to shallow marine environment during the Tournaisian to Latest Devonian (Fig. 1B). These deposits exhibit significant progradation and evidence a large relative drop in sea level from this depocenter to adjacent subsurface areas25, 29.

The Anderson Formation (Viséan) is a sedimentary succession representative of Viséan fluvial and coastal plain deposits and marks the completion of a regressive (landward) sedimentary succession and a complete shift to fully terrestrial depositional conditions25, 30. The transition in basin dynamics from a carbonate reef complex (Nullara) to a siliciclastic-dominated coastal plain (Anderson) has been caused by glacio-eustatic sea-level lowering due to tectonically-induced uplifts and climatic cooling (the start of the Late Paleozoic Ice Age)25, 29.

Biostratigraphic framework and age constraints

Calibration of the floating astrochronology derived from cyclostratigraphic analysis requires a strong biostratigraphic framework. Conodont biozonation is the main source of Upper Devonian to Lower Carboniferous correlations in the Canning Basin7. Conodonts offer the most accurate age control for this period, despite lower resolution in shallow-water settings post-dating the reef collapse (within the Palmatolepis expansa Zone)31, 32.

For this study, key biostratigraphic anchor points include:

The D–C boundary is estimated to be around 358.9 Ma. This boundary is roughly within the latest Famennian to earliest Tournaisian interval in the Canning Basin, limited by the onset of Carboniferous taxa and the final appearance of typical Devonian conodonts and palynomorphs (such as the Retispora lepidophyta zone)7.

The appearance of the Gnathodus bilineatus conodont group, which is identified in comparable strata of the Bonaparte Basin, indicates the base of the Viséan Stage7.

These biostratigraphically constrained surfaces are used as crucial, but not very precise, absolute dating horizons. In the present study, these constrained surfaces anchor the tuned Astronomical Time Scale (see Sect. 3.6) and convert depth-calculated orbital cycles into a very precise absolute Astronomical Time Scale (ATS) dependent upon depth. This allows precise dating of particular events (such as the Hangenberg Event and the Late Tournaisian Cooling Event) and enables direct global correlation. Palynology also offers additional dating constraints with a lower time resolution (approximately stage24) than biostratigraphy. In short, the integration of the biostratigraphic framework and cyclostratigraphy forms the backbone of the chronology of the current study.

Materials and methods

Gamma-ray logging tool

Gamma-ray (GR) logging is a down-hole geophysical method that measures the natural radioactivity primarily based on radioactive isotopes potassium-40, uranium-238 and thorium-232. Of these 40K is the most common especially in clay minerals and feldspar33, 34. In effect, gamma-ray logs typically indicate grain size and clay content, with a high gamma-ray count characteristic of mudstone and a low count sandstone or carbonate33. These fluctuations are utilized as a paleoclimate proxy, as the sediment supply and clay generation are controlled by climatic factors, such as weathering intensity and sea-level variations caused by Milankovitch cycles34. Gamma-ray logs aid lithostratigraphic correlations and the identification of sequence boundaries and parasequences, and cyclic sedimentary patterns caused by orbital forcing, periodic sedimentation rate variations, and climate-controlled depositional processes34, 35, 36. In addition, gamma-ray logs have long been used to interpret depositional facies34 from which paleoclimate can be inferred especially if they reveal progressively shallower or deeper-water conditions. In combination with other proxy data, wireline logs can be used to substantiate paleoenvironmental and climatic history models34, 35. The GR data in the studied wells were recorded with a sampling interval of ~ 7 to ~ 10 cm.

Inductively coupled plasma methods

The chemostratigraphic analysis of the uppermost Devonian – Tournaisian succession in Ungani 2 was based on 144 cutting samples analyzed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) by37. Their samples of silty claystone, carbonate and sandstone, were from regular 5-m intervals between 1965 and 2674 m. The analyses provided concentrations for approximately 50 major, trace, and rare earth elements. For this study, specific geochemical proxies were selected to evaluate paleoenvironmental variations. Paleoredox conditions were assessed using V, Zn, and Fe₂O₃/S. Paleosalinity variations were inferred from elemental ratios CaO/Ti, Rb/K, Sr/Ba, and Na₂O/K. These proxies were used in combination to track changes in lithology, clay mineralogy, sediment provenance and depositional conditions across the uppermost Devonian–Tournaisian interval.

Portable X-ray fluorescence (pXRF) analysis

The chemostratigraphic analysis of the uppermost Devonian—Viséan succession in Rafael 1 is based on 518 cuttings samples of silty claystone, carbonate, and sandstone utilizing a pXRF spectrometer conducted by Chemostrat Australia Pty (2022). For this well the samples were collected at irregular intervals of either 0.5 or 2.5 m spanning 2744.5 to 4110 m. The pXRF dataset includes major and trace element concentrations relevant to chemostratigraphic interpretation. In this study, paleoredox conditions were evaluated using V/Cr, U/Th, and Fe/S, while paleosalinity changes were interpreted through Sr/Rb, Ca/Ti, Rb/K, and Sr/Ba.

Time series analysis

Basic time series analysis

The GR data in the Rafael 1 and Ungani 2 wells provide the primary high-resolution paleoclimate record for cyclostratigraphic study. Prior to analysis, all of the GR data for each well were normalized to a mean of zero and unit standard deviation in order to remove any well-specific shifts in the log data and facilitate comparison of the cyclic amplitudes between wells. To extract the orbital scales of the cycles from the broader trends of either tectonics or deposition, the gamma ray data were detrended using the Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing (LOESS) method. The smoothing interval used was 35% of the total data, thus removing scales that are most likely associated with either subsidence or sea-level cycles much larger than 35% of the section. Such cycles might be associated either with subsidence or with broader sea-level variations that do not have any relationship with Milankovitch cycles. Spectral analysis was performed on the detrended time series to identify the significant periodicities. The Multitaper Method (MTM38) was carried out using the MTM function available in the Acycle software package (version 2.339). The multitaper spectral analysis was done using three orthogonal tapers and a time-bandwidth product of 2. The significance testing of the spectral peaks was performed for a robust first-order autoregressive (AR1) red-noise model at the significance levels of 90, 95, and 99%. The F-test analysis was then done for a further assessment of the harmonic content of the significant spectral peaks compared to the red-noise model38. To create a visual representation of the dominant frequencies over time and the sedimentation rates, evolutionary Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) Spectrograms40 were created using a sliding window with following parameters:

Power spectra were calculated within each window and contoured to produce a time–frequency representation.

To extract specific orbital signals from the data for inspection and more detailed analysis (analysis of the modulation of the amplitude), Gaussian band-pass filters40 were applied to the detrended GR series using the band-pass function from Acycle.

We measure the response of the GR signal across many orbital bands here using Power Decomposition Analysis (PDA39). We now applied in Acycle the PDA function in a 500-kyr sliding window -following the initial orbital tuning described in Sect. “Fischer plots”- to determine the fraction of power residing in specific frequency bands relative to the total power of the 2π MTM spectrum. The evolutionary Time Optimization (eTimeOpt) method (eTimeOpt function in Astrochron41) was used to perform an initial evaluation of the sediment accumulation rate (SAR). Using a variety of test sedimentation rates, this approach examines the relationship between the GR series and a target astronomical solution (358 Ma42). A SAR search range of ~ 1 to 5 cm/kyr was employed for this preliminary depth-domain evaluation, to maximize the fit for the stable 405-kyr eccentricity cycle. The resulting SAR estimates (~ 3.5–4.5 cm/kyr) served as a first-order conversion factor that guided the subsequent orbital tuning process and allowed for the tentative assignment of orbital periods to the observed depth-domain cycles (e.g., interpreting a ~ 14.6 m cycle as 405-kyr). All time-series analyses in this section were conducted using the MATLAB-based Acycle software (v2.339).

Wavelet spectral analysis of GR time series

Time series analysis of the GR proxy data was conducted with continuous wavelet transform (CWT) techniques using the approach outlined by Torrence and Compo43. The GR time series data were normalized to zero mean and unit variance before analysis to facilitate cross-comparison of spectral power across various periodicities. Spectral decomposition was accomplished with a Morlet mother wavelet having a non-dimensional frequency (ω₀) of 6 that yields an optimal trade-off between temporal and spectral resolution for geophysical time series44. The parameters of the wavelet transform were set to analyze periodicities ranging from 0.5 kyr to about 16,384 kyr, where the scale resolution is governed by a logarithmic step parameter (dj) of 0.1. Statistical significance tests for periodicities were conducted by Monte Carlo methods against an autoregressive (AR1) model red-noise background spectrum at 95% confidence level. The cone-of-influence limit was computed to determine areas where edge effects could invalidate the interpretation. Statistically significant periodicities were determined by computing the global wavelet spectrum via time-averaging the local wavelet power over the whole sequence. The multi-scale analysis approach allows both stationary and non-stationary cyclical elements of the GR signal to be determined, thereby yielding valuable information on possible astronomical forcing processes and their time evolution in the examined stratigraphic succession43, 44. All calculations were carried out using Python along with the PyCWT wavelet analysis software package with visualization using the Matplotlib library.

Fischer plots

Yang et al. (2022) developed the custom Python script (PyFISCHERPLOT) for SR-Fischer plot analysis. The code reads stratigraphic proxy data to build SR-Fischer plots by calculating cumulative departure from mean rate (CDMR) values from thickness patterns of sedimentary cycles. Implemented in Python 3.0, the script uses libraries xlrd and xlsxwriter for Excel file manipulations. The algorithm imports depth and age data from Excel spreadsheets, then processes the data by searching for increases and decreases in proxy measurements to define cycle boundaries. For each cycle, cycle thickness and duration were evaluated, allowing sedimentation rate, departure from mean rate, and cumulative values (CDMR) to be computed (Yang et al., 2022). The resulting data is subsequently loaded into new Excel workbooks with plots for SR-Fischer depth and SR-Fischer age axes and useful visualization parameters. Hence, this procedure permits batch processing of many stratigraphic datasets and enables fast comparison of accommodation space changes over different sections or time intervals (Yang et al., 2022).

Geochemical proxies of paleoenvironmental perturbations

To account for analytical differences between the two geochemical datasets, we evaluated the characteristic precision, reproducibility, and detection limits of the ICP‑derived (Ungani‑2) and pXRF‑derived (Rafael‑1) measurements separately. The ICP‑OES/ICP‑MS data from Ungani‑2 provide higher analytical precision and lower detection limits, particularly for trace metals such as V, Zn, and U‑series elements, whereas the pXRF dataset from Rafael‑1 yields robust relative variations with slightly higher noise levels at low elemental concentrations. Despite these methodological differences, both datasets display consistent and stratigraphically aligned excursions—most notably sharp redox‑sensitive peaks and coeval shifts in salinity proxies—across the key intervals corresponding to the Hangenberg Event (HE) and Lower Alum Shale Event (LASE). The reproducibility of these parallel geochemical spikes across two independent analytical techniques supports the reliability of event identification and confirms that the observed anomalies represent true paleoenvironmental signals rather than method‑specific artifacts.

Discussion

Sedimentation rate variability and event preservation

Tectonically, the transition of the Canning Basin from active Famennian rifting to Viséan post-rift thermal sag had profound effects on event preservation and sedimentation rates30, 46. Syn-rift faulting in the Famennian produced highly spatially variable subsidence heterogeneity and variable sedimentation rates from tens to > 100 m/Myr locally30. Despite the dominant tectonic influence, our cyclostratigraphic analysis has revealed the dominant control of astronomical forcing on the rates of sedimentation in both tectonic regimes. The same Milankovitch cycles, with periods of 5-Myr, 405-kyr, 100-kyr, and 21-kyr, present in the Famennian stage (period of tectonic rifting) and the Viséan stage (period of tectonic subsidence) can be identified within our ATS. Therefore, it can be concluded that the astronomical signal has passed unchanged, being influenced only by the sedimentation rates controlled by tectonics (Figs. 7 and 8). Condensed sections developed on flanking platforms, but high rates of subsidence within fault-bounded depocentres, especially the Fitzroy Trough, yielded thick coarse-grained, syntectonic deposits47. This tectonically active setting fostered preservation of event beds with sharp bases (e.g., seismites, mass-transport complexes, neptunian dyke fills)15, 47 but also promoted erosion and reworking of units as fault blocks tilted. In the Viséan, reducing fault activity and regional-scale thermal subsidence resulted in falling, more uniform sedimentation rates (< 20 m/Myr across the basin) and widespread shallow-marine to paralic deposition of siliciclastics30. The sag stage of quiescence permitted the retention of high-frequency sequences (e.g., glacioeustatic cycles and thin coal beds) through less tectonic overprinting, resulting in a more complete stratigraphic record of climatic and eustatic events30, 48. This tectonic evolution thus generated a stratigraphic dichotomy, with rift-stage strata recording tectonic events of high energy whereas sag-stage strata tend to retain climatic and eustatic signals30, 47.

The spectral analysis results and sedimentation rate data from Rafael 1 and Ungani 2, reveal several key conditions regarding sedimentation rate variability and its impact on event preservation in the Famennian–Viséan succession. The preservation of astronomical signals indicates relatively continuous sedimentation with sufficient resolution to capture Milankovitch-scale climate variability during the Devonian to Carboniferous transition. Paleoenvironmental proxy data for redox-sensitive trace elements (V, V/Cr, U/Th) and paleosalinity proxies (Sr/Rb, Ca/Ti, Sr/Ba) show systematic correlations with sedimentation rate variability (Fig. 4). Intervals of high sedimentation rate are commonly linked with high ratios of V/Cr and low values of Sr/Rb, pointing towards greater terrestrial input, perhaps under more humid conditions (Fig. 4). The geochemical signatures, combined with the findings from spectral analysis, suggest that sedimentation rate changes were regulated in large part by climatic changes on an orbital timescale that affected weathering rates on the continents, sediment supply, and productivity in the seas. Preservation of these correlations through different orbital cycles illustrates the fundamental influence of astronomical forcing on regulating patterns of sedimentation along with event preservation integrity in this mixed siliciclastic–carbonate system. The correlations between sedimentation rate variability and paleoenvironmental proxies for the Famennian–Viséan successions in Rafael 1 and Ungani 2 are consistent with wider patterns preserved in Paleozoic carbonate-siliciclastic systems subject to orbital forcing. For instance, the association of high sedimentation rates with elevated V/Cr ratios and low Sr/Rb values (Fig. 4) replicates observations in Paleozoic basins where humid climatic phases enhanced continental weathering and terrigenous supply, inducing anoxic conditions and the preservation of organic matter49, 50. Similarly, opposing the paleosalinity proxy trend (e.g., Ca/Ti, Sr/Ba) is the sedimentation rate, following Carboniferous cyclothems, wherein monsoonal intensification due to precession increased freshwater release into the sea, diluting it and stabilizing water-column stratification 51, 52. These analogies highlight the overall control of orbital-scale hydrological cycling on sedimentation dynamics in a vast array of geological settings.

Exploiting these observations, the preservation of orbital cycles in the Famennian—Viséan successions offers a reliable framework for the preservation of events. The persistent recording of Milankovitch-scale variability, especially 21-kyr precession, 405-kyr eccentricity, and ~ 5-Myr AM cycles, reveals a dynamic interplay between astronomic forcing and sedimentary response. These cycles are ubiquitous in both Rafael 1 and Ungani 2, and are also present in other wells. In Ungani North 1 the interval from the base of the Nullara Formation to the top of the Anderson Formation, has a thickness of about 2350 m. Importantly, the total depth of the well is 3700 m, but the base of the Nullara Formation has not been identified. In Valentine 1 the interval from the base of the Nullara Formation to the top of the Anderson Formation, is approximately 1562 m thick. In addition, about 70 m of the Virgin Hills Formation—the unit underlying the Nullara Formation—is also present in this well. The differing sedimentation rates between these wells suggest that local tectonic processes have modified or overprinted the expression of long-range orbital climate controls on regional depositional settings. “Event preservation” in this sense not only has implications for the physical preservation of sediment, but also involves the geochemical signatures embedded within these strata as a record of past climatic and paleoenvironmental change. Similar coherence between spectral analysis of gamma-ray logs and the independently derived sedimentation rates, and their correlation with paleoredox and paleosalinity proxies, underscores the fidelity event preservation (Figs. 2 and 4). For example, the anti-correlation between paleosalinity proxies such as Ca/Ti and Sr/Ba with sedimentation rates indicates that times of increased freshwater inflow, probably in response to the orbitally forced monsoonal intensification, resulted in both higher sedimentation rates and reduced marine salinities, and hence the rock record preserves both end members of the hydrological extremes they represent. Such preservation is critical in the understanding of paleoclimates, as it links the physical expression of sedimentary layering with a specific climatic forcing. The preservation of such associations from Rafael 1 and Ungani 2 points to a regionally uniform response to orbital forcing and thus the processes responsible for event preservation were basin-wide at this time. This strong imprint of orbitally-forced environmental variations preserved within the Famennian–Viséan successions is not an exception but is a pattern repeated on several occasions in different geological intervals and sedimentary environments, testifying to a widespread astronomical control on Earth’s climatic–environmental settings. For example, comparable high-resolution records of Milankovitch cycles and their nested paleoenvironmental proxy are the Jurassic Posidonia Shale in Germany53 and the Cretaceous Western Interior Seaway in North America54. In these cases, changes in organic carbon and carbonate–marl couplets are directly related to eccentricity and precession cycles, suggesting that orbital control influenced both marine productivity and anoxia, and the preservation of lithological and geochemical signals not unlike those found in both Rafael 1 and Ungani 2.

The tuned durations obtained for the Tournaisian interval in the Rafael 1 (~ 16 Myr) and Ungani 2 (~ 15 Myr) wells are slightly longer than the ~ 12.6 Myr duration reported in GTS 2020. This difference is consistent with the depositional nature of the Canning Basin as an intracratonic setting, where low long‑term sedimentation rates and sustained accommodation commonly produce expanded successions relative to global composite time scales. The high coherence of the long‑period astronomical cycles between the two wells indicates that the extended duration reflects genuine stratigraphic expansion rather than tuning artefacts. Minor offsets between ATS‑derived stage lengths and the GTS framework have also been documented in other Paleozoic basins (Appalachian or North Sea) and likely arise from regional variations in accommodation, condensation near sequence boundaries, and uncertainties in the calibration of long‑period cycles.

Orbital pacemakers and anoxic oceanic conditions in the eastern margin of Gondwana: decoding the HE and LASE events

The ~ 5-Myr cycle provides a hierarchical metronome signal for the climatic variations. This cycle is not a spectral line feature but a genuine modulator. The Gaussian filter applied to the 405 kyr signal reveals the amplitude oscillation with a period of about 4.9 ± 0.7 Myr (Figs. 6, 7 and 8). This observation clearly substantiates the ~ 5-Myr cycle’s role in modulating the amplitude of eccentricity forcing of climate. In this interpretation, the ~ 405-kyr eccentricity maxima, together with the ~ 5-Myr AM peaks, provide the prerequisites for the strongest biogeochemical perturbations. In our dating system for these events, the start of the HE is chronostratigraphically placed at roughly 359.2 Ma, and the LASE is dated at about 350.8 Ma. These ages correlate with the peaks of the 405-kyr eccentricity cycle (see Figs. 7 and 8). The sequence of events is as follows: First, the eccentricity-induced insolation enhancement enhances the tropical monsoons, leading to heavier rainfall during the seasons in eastern Gondwana. The resulting runoff puts more terrigenous nutrients into the rivers (ocean tracers recorded by increasing Rb/K ratios- Fig. 4), as well as freshwater runoff (resulting in lowered Sr/Ba ratios). These nutrient inputs trigger increased primary productivity, resulting in the export of more organic matter to the seafloor. Simultaneously, high global sea levels result in strong stratification of the water column (see Fig. 9). This triggers euxinia on a global scale due to high oxygen demand and reduced ventilation, as recorded by peak V/Cr and U/Th ratios in Fig. 4. These results are consistent with European and South China studies, in which monsoonal intensity and freshwater discharge are shown to increase due to eccentricity-driven climate variability, thereby enhancing marine productivity and oxygen depletion (see55, 56, 57). The 405-kyr cycle, as a key pacemaker, seems to have influenced the amplitude of hydrological variations forced by precession, which is recorded in coeval maxima in V/Cr and U/Th ratios (Figs. 4 and SM-1) and lowered Sr/Ba ratios (Fig. 4). These ratios reflect humid climatic conditions that enhanced weathering processes and terrigenous material input. This climatic-orbital association is further supported by DYNOT modeling of sea-level changes (Fig. 9) that correlate HE and LASE episodes with highstand systems tracts, transgressive periods marked by condensed, organic-rich beds and anoxic/euxinic facies. The youngest onset for both events thus appears to coincide with maxima of the 405-kyr cycle on the orbital power spectra (red arrows in Figs. 7 and 8). This would indicate that insolation gradients paced by eccentricity intensified seasonal runoff and nutrient fluxes. Aseptic bursts of productivity that outpaced oxygen supply induced euxinia.

A striking observation is the recurrence of precession spikes (P/T and P/E) in post-anoxic intervals which are coeval with rising Sr/Ba and falling V/Cr ratios (grey green bars in Figs. 4, 7 and 8). The anti-phased relationship indicates that precession-paced monsoonal variability played a fundamental role in ventilating the water column and diluting euxinic conditions, thereby permitting ecological recovery after prolonged anoxic intervals. The abrupt precession maxima following HE (grey green color interval) correspond to a stepwise re-oxygenation pathway, mirrored in Fe/S trends and reduced organic carbon burial, which permits high-frequency orbital forcing of recurrent re-oxygenation. Likewise, the termination of the LASE (Figs. 7 and 8) apparently coincides with precession maxima and transient Ca/Ti increases, consistent with enhanced carbonate production during interglacial warming phases.

The preservation of these orbital imprints in the mixed siliciclastic–carbonate system of the Canning Basin is a demonstration of the sensitivity of the basin to Milankovitch forcing, even in its equatorial Gondwana location. Studied wells with moderately constant sedimentation rates (~ 3.5– ~ 4.5 cm/kyr) captured high-resolution orbital signals (e.g., sudden 405-kyr and precession peaks). Cross-well comparisons confirm that the HE and LASE were globally significant anoxic events, paced by orbital harmonics unrelated to paleogeographic boundaries. The ubiquity of the ~ 5-Myr AM cycle in coordinating these events aligns with Paleozoic icehouse–greenhouse transition models, where glacial–interglacial and redox feedbacks were regulated by long-eccentricity cycles. This research thereby plugs a central missing link in explaining how orbital forcing shaped paleoenvironmental extremes in Gondwana’s epicontinental seas, with implications for a template for interpreting analogous transitions in ancient records. Furthermore, amplitude modulation trends in both wells indicated that this ~ 5-Myr cycle was the main driver of the intensity and duration of anoxic episodes, and that times of maximum modulation may have coincided with the most severe environmental perturbations (Figs. 7 and 8). This hierarchical correspondence of variations in orbital powers with variations in geochemical proxies bolsters the proposition that among the multiple acting timescales of astronomical forcing were seasonal variability mediated through precession and modulation of climate sensitivity at eccentricity timescales. This layered organization of the orbital signals thereby offers an exceptional platform for deciphering how celestial mechanics was converted into real environmental change in the early marine systems, with the Canning Basin providing an important record for documenting the role played by astronomical forcing in effecting another step in large-scale paleoenvironmental change at the D–C boundary.

This coincidence between orbital eccentricity cycles and external disturbances, such as the HE and LASE, is well documented in this work, with eccentricity maxima aligning with intensified monsoonal activity (Figs. 7 and 8). The high-eccentricity intervals maximize seasonality contrasts in insolation, intensifying monsoonal circulation by maximizing land–sea thermal gradients, particularly in tropical eastern Gondwana. This strengthening activates more intense convective precipitation, increasing the runoff of nutrients into peripheral marine environments, such as the Canning Basin. The increasing input of nutrients, as indicated by high V/Cr (> 4.25 ppm) and U/Th (> 1.2 ppm) ratios, enhances primary productivity and burial of organic matter, which in turn induces extensive anoxia for HE and LASE66, 67. Highstands in sea level produced by eccentricity, as manifest in Ungani 2 and Rafael 1 transgressive facies, most likely strengthened water-column stratification, perpetuating anoxic conditions in turn. These processes, as established by global analogues (e.g., European basins:55, 57, and South China:56), demonstrate how eccentricity cycles determine monsoon strength and marine biogeochemical cycles, and connect orbital forcing to environmental catastrophes.

Cooling signal and sea-level stabilization: decoding the LTCE event

The LTCE in Rafael 1 and Ungani 2 is identified by distinct geochemical as well as spectral signatures that correlate with global climatic change as documented in South China12, 68. Geochemical markers such as elevated Mg/Ca, Fe/Ca, and Rb/Sr ratios, steep K₂O peaks, and low Ca contents (Fig. 5) indicate heightened chemical weathering under dryer, cooler conditions. These patterns are echoed by the mid-Tournaisian cooling event in South China and Vietnam, where growth of terrestrial vegetation was linked to greater silicate weathering and atmospheric CO₂ uptake12. The ~ 100-kyr short-eccentricity cycle (Figs. 7 and 8 exhibits high power maxima within LTCE (time intervals of nearly 23 Myr and 15.5 Myr, respectively, in Rafael 1 and Ungani 2; Fig. 9), suggesting that eccentricity-modulated insolation gradients amplified contrasts of seasonal temperature, thereby driving glacial-interglacial cycles. This orbital pacing is supplemented by DYNOT sea-level modeling (Fig. 9), which identifies a regional sea-level fall contemporaneous with LTCE, as consistent with ice-volume development coinciding with reduced marine transgression.

The anti-phased relationship between short-eccentricity cycles maxima (~ 100 kyr) and paleosalinity fluctuation intervals (Figs. 7 and 8) for the LTCE illustrates how climatic fluctuations induced by orbital variability regulated hydrological cycles in the Canning Basin. During glacial phases inferred from LTCE’s cooling signal (such as elevated Mg/Ca and Fe/Ca ratios, abrupt spikes in K₂O), reduced continental runoff likely decreased the input of fresh water, raising seawater salinity and allowing for evaporative concentration, as indicated by increased Sr/Ba values (Fig. 5). In parallel, heightened chemical weathering of feldspars under colder conditions, as expressed in spikes in K₂O, suggests intensified clay mineral formation in terrestrial settings, in agreement with Chen et al.’s12, explanation that mid-Tournaisian cooling was a response to enhanced root systems in seed plants, which enhanced silicate weathering. The coincident decline in Ca concentrations further suggests suppressed carbonate production, possibly because of diminished marine productivity or dilution by terrigenous sediment during lowstands. Preservation of high-amplitude ~ 100-kyr cycles in LTCE reaffirms the orbital sensitivity of icehouse climates, suggesting this cooling episode was an early phase of the Late Paleozoic Ice Age (LPIA) within eastern Gondwana. The correlation of sea-level fall during the LTCE (Fig. 9) also indicates changes between glacial and interglacial states regulated sediment routing, with coarse-grained progradation during lowstands and highstands, and distal condensed, organic-rich deposition during both. This is similar to the findings of Chen et al.12, who indicated that terrestrial plant growth during the Early Carboniferous amplified weathering feedbacks, leading to long-term climatic stabilization. Because of the synchronization of orbital cycles, geochemical proxies and sea levels, LTCE is an analogue for icehouse transitions. The ubiquity of the ~ 100-kyr eccentricity cycle throughout the event suggests that short-term orbital cycles provided a pacemaker to glacial variability, whereas the longer ~ 5-Myr AM envelope maintained long-term tectonic and climatic adaptation. Even though this study is the first to identify LTCE in the Canning Basin, higher-resolution geochemical proxies should be used in future research to clarify questions about the exact timing and environmental implications of this cooling event. Significantly, the LTCE appears at the exact low point within an overall ~ 5-Myr cycle (see Figs. 7 and 8). This is opposite to the anoxic episodes (HE, LTCE), and suggests that the ~ 5-Myr cycle was orchestrating the climate state switch between greenhouse and oxygen-free and cooling and icehouse conditions during the Early Carboniferous period. While its ~ 5-Myr peak was dominant, monsoons and anoxia due to eccentricity introduced a climate of monsoons and anoxia. During the lows, the climate became increasingly sensitive to the shorter eccentricity and obliquity periods, forcing the climate toward cooling and glaciation. In other words, the ~ 5-Myr cycle defined the climate state, within which the shorter periods acted.

Sea-level dynamics, hydrological cycles, and orbital forcing: a ~ 5-Myr cycle shaping sedimentary sequences

The combination of SR-Fischer plots, DYNOT sea-level modeling, and orbital power oscillations (Fig. 9) illustrates hierarchical control by Milankovitch-scale orbital forcing and tectonic subsidence over Canning Basin Famennian–Viséan transition sedimentary succession. The identification of a dominant ~ 5-Myr AM cycle in this interval is consistent with long-eccentricity cycles as the primary drivers of sedimentary and sea-level responses throughout geological history. The wavelet analysis confirms that the ~ 5-Myr amplitude modulation cycle is a fundamental climatic pacemaker rather than an intermittent feature by revealing it in the global spectrum and showing its continuity in the time domain (Figs. 7 and 8). The ~ 5-Myr cycle appears to be the 4.7-Myr eccentricity which Boulila et al.19 suggested as a fundamental amplitude and frequency modulator of shorter Milankovitch cycles (e.g., the 405-kyr eccentricity and precession). Perhaps these low-frequency cycling phenomena originated from the coupling of Earth’s orbital eccentricity and inclination, such that the ~ 4.7-Myr cycle was nested within amplitude modulation envelopes of ~ 35-Myr. If such a hierarchical system exists, then the ~ 5-Myr cycle in the Canning Basin may have been produced by direct orbital forcing coupled with second-order tectonic amplification, similar to that proposed for Phanerozoic sea-level records in which orbital signatures were filtered through geodynamic processes (c.f.19).

The SedSimple model71 also illustrates how such cycles shape sedimentary systems. The 5-Myr-scale simulations indicate that ~ 1-Myr sea-level cycles, within a ~ 5-Myr amplitude modulation envelope, dictate facies distribution and isotopic gradients. Organic-rich horizons are selectively preserved at maxima in eccentricity during phases of net rising sea-level, whereas falling intervals are characterized by coarse-grained progradation and hiatus development. These modelled dynamics closely replicate the stratigraphy of the Canning Basin, with transgressive intervals (e.g., He horizon) containing condensed organic-rich sequences and lowstands (e.g., LTCE interval) showing evidence of erosion and terrigenous input. The model demonstrates how stable sedimentation rates (~ 3.5– ~ 4.5 cm/kyr in studied wells) are more likely to preserve orbital signals compared to tectonically basins with active margins like the Tadla Basin (Morocco72), where structural instability disrupts spectral coherence. By analyzing Phanerozoic sea-level data, Boulila et al.19 found a hierarchical coupling of orbital and tectonic processes within a ~ 35-Myr amplitude modulation (AM) envelope controlling the periodicity and strength of shorter cycles, including the ~ 5-Myr band. Similarly, the spectral coherence in the Canning Basin, amplified shorter precession-obliquity signals, as observed in the DYNOT sea-level curves. Here, the ~ 5-Myr cycle falls in harmony with ~ 405-kyr eccentricity and precession signals, allowing for a reinforcement of the imprint they leave in a stratigraphic record. The North Saharan Basin’s ~ 5-Myr variability in subsidence rates also arises in the context of glacial-interglacial dynamics63, 64, 65. In that example, eccentricity-modulated gradients in solar insolation-controlled changes in ice volume, creating a modulation in sediment flux. In both the Canning and North Sahara Basins, the ~ 5-Myr periodicity functioned as a “super-cycle” integrating orbital forcing with regional tectonic adjustments. In the Canning Basin, thermal relaxation after rifting in Neoproterozoic – early Paleozoic may have taken ~ 4.7 Myr, allowing for substantial preservation in mixed siliciclastic-carbonate systems. By analogy these global examples—empirical records (Ordovician–Silurian, Late Cenozoic) and modeling results (SedSimple)—this research positions the ~ 5-Myr cycle as a linchpin to Paleozoic icehouse-greenhouse dynamics. The equatorial Gondwana setting of the Canning Basin offers a unique paleogeographic perspective, complementing less well-known Laurussian basins (e.g., Appalachian Basin) and peri-Tethyan provinces (e.g., South China Platform). Future studies will evaluate whether similar ~ 5-Myr cycles in the Sydney Basin (eastern Australia) or Amadeus Basin (central Australia) reflect similar tectono-orbital controls, again by correlating intrabasinal observations with global cyclicity.

The long‑period modulation detected in our record (~ 5 Myr) is slightly higher than the commonly cited 4.5–4.7 Myr band, but this offset is well within expected natural variability of the chaotic astronomical solutions. Long‑eccentricity modulations are not fixed constants and can drift over hundreds of thousands of years, and their sedimentary expression may be further stretched or compressed by basin‑specific accumulation patterns and compaction trends. In our case, the ~ 5 Myr peak is consistently reproduced across independent analyses (raw GR, tuned GR, and CWT), indicating that it reflects a genuine long‑period forcing rather than an artefact. We therefore interpret the small departure from published values as a realistic combination of astronomical variability and sedimentary overprinting, rather than evidence for a fundamentally different periodicity.

Synthesis: a unified hierarchical model of orbital control in greenhouse climates

Through the combination of cyclostratigraphy, geochemical proxies, and sea level oscillations, we will propose a hierarchical framework in which orbital rhythms forced climatic variations during the late Devonian and early Carboniferous greenhouse period (Fig. 10). In our framework, orbital rhythms form the focal point of climatic instability and are arranged in a hierarchical, four-tier system.

Tier 1 (~ 5 Myr): The cycle of amplitude modulation functions as the main pacemaker, establishing multi-million-year time intervals that are more sensitive to climate and defining the background pattern (‘greenhouse-humid’ and ‘cooling-dry’).

Hierarchical model of orbital control in the Late Devonian to Early Carboniferous greenhouse climates. This model illustrates the hierarchical structure of the four-tiered pacing of the astronomically driven climatic system: (1) 5-Myr-timescale amplitude modulation as the super-pacemaker of background climatic sensitivity; (2) 405-kyr timescale eccentricity pacing of major system changes and the onset of anoxia (HE, LASE intervals); (3) 100-kyr, 40-kyr pacing of interglacial-glacial transitions and stages of weathering in cooling intervals (LTCE event package); and (4) 20-kyr precessional controls on hydrologic balance and basin ventilation rates. This model demonstrates that the deep-time greenhouse climates on Earth underwent dynamic pulsations with quantifiable rates according to predictable astronomical rhythms, establishing a model for the interpretation of the tempo of deep-time biotic and biogeochemical perturbations.

Based on the record from the Canning Basin, this template indicates that the greenhouse climate intervals were not maintained steadily but rather cycled or pulsed in a predictable, nested, and astronomical rhythmic order. This provides a sound template for assessing the pacing and amplitude of biotic crises and biogeochemical disturbances within deep-time greenhouse examples.

Conclusions

In the following study, a hierarchical model of orbital pacing is described as the primary driving mechanism of climatic and environmental changes during the Late Devonian to Early Carboniferous greenhouse phase. A high-resolution record from the Canning Basin shows that celestial mechanics controlled the pacing of ecosystems on a hierarchy of astronomical rhythms. The Nullara, May River, Laurel, and Anderson formations in Rafael 1 and Ungani 2 exhibit hierarchical interactions due to biogeochemical feedbacks, tectonic subsidence, and Milankovitch cycles. Identification of a ~ 5-Myr cycle in eccentricity amplitude modulations—consistent with global analogs such as Ordovician–Silurian and upper Cenozoic records—arises as a key framework to decipher icehouse–greenhouse transitions. The super-cycle controlled sedimentation rates of 3.5–4.5 cm/kyr (with eTimeOpt and orbital tuning) and modulated shorter cycles, such as 405-kyr long-eccentricity, 100-kyr short-eccentricity, 40–50-kyr obliquity, and 20–25-kyr precession signals, all influenced redox conditions, sea level, and nutrient fluxes. The intensity of the 405-kyr eccentricity forcing was confirmed to be controlled by this ~ 5-Myr cycle through direct amplitude modulation analysis, offering mechanistic insight into how short-term climatic extremes were amplified by long-term orbital harmonics.

Major events, including the Hangenberg Event (HE; ~ 3925–4030 m in Rafael 1) and the Lower Alum Shale Event (LASE; ~ 3690–3770 m), coincide with 405-kyr eccentricity maxima, whereby increased monsoonal runoff and nutrient input caused euxinia (as indicated by high V/Cr and U/Th ratios) and burial of organic carbon. These anoxic intervals were periodically broken by precession-controlled ventilation events, such as those following the HE, characterized by increasing Sr/Ba and decreasing V/Cr, and corresponding to periodic re-oxygenation and orbital-scale hydrological change (grey green interval in Figs. 7, 8). Conversely, the LTCE (~ 3500 m) is connected with ~ 100-kyr short-eccentricity cycles, during which glacial–interglacial transition lowered sea level, elevated Rb/Sr ratios, and promoted siliciclastic progradation. Geochemical proxies (e.g., Mg/Ca, Fe/Ca) and DYNOT sea-level modeling also demonstrate orbital pacing controlling salinity gradients and terrestrial weathering pulses, with the LTCE cooling signal recorded as sudden K₂O spikes, and reduced carbonate production.

Tectonic evolution was instrumental in controlling sedimentation rates and event preservation. The syn-rift faulting and heterogeneous subsidence (> 100 m/Myr) of the Famennian rift-stage preserved high-energy deposits (e.g., mass-transport complexes) but truncated stratigraphic records. However, Viséan sag-phase thermal subsidence (< 20 m/Myr) precisely captured high-frequency orbital signals such as glacioeustatic cycles and condensed organic-rich intervals. Fischer plots and SR-Fischer analysis show that anoxic/euxinic environments correlate with highstand systems tracts (e.g., ~ 331–332 Ma and ~ 335–337 Ma = 30–31 Myr and 35–37 Myr intervals in our ATS) whereas lowstands (e.g., ~ 23 Myr) coincide with terrestrial dominance and salinity fluctuations. The ~ 5-Myr AM cycle recurrence across the basin highlights its pacemaker status that combines orbital forcing and tectonic relaxation, and carbon cycling to enhance climatic extremes.

Our results show that the Canning Basin contains a pivotal record of greenhouse instability, where the interaction between orbital harmonics and paleogeography, and biogeochemical feedbacks-controlled ecosystem resilience. The orbital stratigraphy of signals—most significantly the ~ 5-Myr modulation of short-term cycles—is a template for interpreting climate transition over long time scales, providing an estimate of Earth’s sensitivity to astronomical forcing beyond in both glacial and non-glacial intervals. Through the deciphering of the hierarchical orbital code embedded within the Canning Basin, in which a 5-Myr amplitude modulation controls the pacing of 405-kyr anoxia events and 100-kyr glacial cycles, this paper presents a methodological approach enabling the prediction of instability within the greenhouse climate. This cyclostratigraphic-chemostratigraphic method of analysis also illustrates the strong sensitivity of the Earth’s system to astronomical forcing, even in the absence of polar ice.
