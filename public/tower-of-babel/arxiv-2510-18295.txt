Geometric Manifold Statistics of Turbulence-Impacted Beam Propagation and Compensation in Optical Communication
Shouvik Sadhukhan, C. S. Narayanamurthy

Abstract
The present study extends the analysis of turbulence-affected beam statistics through a manifold-based statistical framework that unifies probabilistic modeling with geometric interpretation. The spatial intensity distributions, distorted by dynamic turbulence, are represented using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs), whose probability landscapes are refined via Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) applied to pixel-level intensity data across temporal frames. The temporal evolution of turbulence is quantified by monitoring the variation of the unnormalized volumetric integrals under the GMM surfaces, providing a continuous measure of power redistribution within the optical field. Experimental investigations were carried out under four propagation conditions: turbulence-free reference, turbulence only, turbulence with a single PMMA compensator, and turbulence with dual PMMA compensators. To assess statistical dissimilarity, Affine-Invariant Riemannian Metric (AIRM) distances were computed between the covariance representations of successive frames, capturing the geometric evolution of beam topology in the space of Symmetric Positive Definite (SPD) matrices. Complementary topological distance analyses, performed with respect to the initial frame of each set, revealed distinct signatures of turbulence-induced aberrations and their progressive mitigation through dielectric compensation. The observed reduction in inter-frame distance metrics confirms the ability of coupled dipole interactions within PMMA slabs to partially restore statistical coherence in turbulence-distorted optical beams. Keywords: Lorentz Dipole Oscillation, Nonlinear Restoring Forces, Kolmogorov Statistics, Manifold statistics, Scintillation Index, Pseudo Random Phase Plate (PRPP)

The Fisher–Rao distance formalism establishes a differential–geometric framework for quantifying dissimilarity between continuous probability distributions. Unlike classical divergence measures that depend on coordinate representations, the Fisher–Rao metric defines an intrinsic Riemannian structure on the manifold of probability densities. Within this framework, each distribution is represented as a point on a curved manifold, whose local geometry is determined by the Fisher information matrix—the expected curvature of the log-likelihood function with respect to its parameters. The resulting line element measures infinitesimal statistical fluctuations, and the geodesic distance between two distributions provides a natural measure of their distinguishability. This formalism forms the foundation of information geometry, bridging probability theory and differential geometry, and offering coordinate-invariant insights into the structure of statistical models. In the context of optical field analysis, turbulence characterization, and stochastic light propagation, the Fisher–Rao metric enables a rigorous description of how intensity or phase distributions evolve on curved statistical manifolds. Consequently, it serves as a fundamental tool for quantifying turbulence-induced randomness and for developing compensation strategies based on the intrinsic geometry of distributional dynamics.

Optical beam propagation through random media, such as atmospheric turbulence or disordered dielectric materials, introduces significant challenges for both theory and applications by causing scintillation, beam wander, wavefront distortion, and topological changes that degrade free-space optical communication, imaging, and adaptive optics performance. Conventional statistical tools—like scintillation index, correlation coefficients, and kernel density estimation—capture limited aspects of these distortions but lack a unified geometric or topological perspective. To address this, we develop a rigorous framework that combines statistical modeling with information geometry for analyzing turbulence-affected beams. In this approach, experimentally obtained intensity distributions are treated as n-dimensional data set and modeled using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs), which are then embedded into the manifold of Symmetric Positive Definite (SPD) matrices. This embedding enables the use of affine-invariant Riemannian metrics and geodesic distances, providing a principled method to quantify similarities and differences in beam topology across turbulence conditions. Experimentally, turbulence is studied using polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) slabs, with Gaussian laser beams recorded under turbulence-free, raw turbulence, and turbulence-compensated scenarios using one or two PMMA rods. The SPD-based analysis reveals topological distance trends and correlation measures, showing how compensation strategies mitigate turbulence effects. Beyond immediate validation, this framework has potential for machine learning, secure optical encryption, data compression, and robust turbulence imaging.

When an electromagnetic wave passes through a transparent or semi-transparent medium, its electric field perturbs the electron clouds of the constituent molecules, breaking their stationary symmetry and inducing oscillating dipoles. These dipoles do not behave independently; instead, they interact and exchange energy, giving rise to coupled oscillations. The resulting coupling introduces non-diagonal terms into the governing equations, so the dynamics cannot be expressed in simple Cartesian coordinates. To address this, diagonalization is applied, redefining the system in terms of new orthonormal modes. This transformation uncovers collective oscillations, where dipoles synchronize and exhibit coherent dynamics, in contrast to the initial random, uncorrelated motion. The diagonalized modes thus capture ordered, emergent behavior arising from microscopic interactions. By highlighting the transition from chaotic individual oscillations to stabilized collective modes, this framework provides a clear understanding of how local dipole coupling governs the macroscopic electromagnetic response of the medium.

The fundamental theoretical work begings with the dynamical equation of the Lorentz Oscillation model under external field as follows.

m\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_{i}+m\gamma\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{i}+m\omega_{0}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{i}=-e\mathbf{E}_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}_{i},t)

(1)

Here,
\vec{\mathbf{r}}

\frac{1}{m}=\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}E(\kappa)}{\partial\kappa^{2}}

2.1
Lorentz Anharmonic Dipole Oscillator

In general, not all materials and their molecular structures generate purely linear restoring forces on their bound electron clouds when an electromagnetic wave propagates through them. Complex molecular configurations can give rise to higher-order restoring effects as well. Therefore, in the present context, we incorporate second- and third-order nonlinearities in the restoring forces associated with the molecules of the PMMA (Poly(methyl methacrylate)) rod. Accordingly, the generalized form of the Forced Anharmonic Oscillator differential equation can be expressed as follows:

When an electromagnetic (EM) field propagates into a transparent or semi-transparent medium, its electric field component interacts with the constituent molecules of the material. This interaction induces perturbations in the electron clouds associated with the molecular structure. Such perturbations break the stationary symmetries of the molecules and their corresponding electron distributions, thereby generating induced dipole moments within the medium. The temporal oscillations of the external electric field cause these dipoles to oscillate in response. Since the molecules within the material are mutually coupled, they can exchange energy with one another. Consequently, this leads to the emergence of a coupled dipole oscillation model to describe the collective behavior of the system.

2.3
Gradient Force Impact on Dipole Moment

The gradient of an electric field with a non-uniform amplitude introduces an additional effect on the coupling between neighboring dipoles. The presence of non-zero spatial gradients and higher-order derivatives of the electric field induces non-uniform dipole moments across the spatially distributed dipoles. Such non-uniformities modify the conventional dipole–dipole interaction by introducing gradient-dependent repulsive forces. Therefore, the contribution of these gradient forces must be incorporated into the generalized Lorentz force dynamics. Within the present framework of the Generalized Anharmonic Dipole Oscillator with Dipole–Dipole Coupling, two dominant forces are identified as the primary modulators of the localized dipole moments. 2.4
Diagonalization of Coupled Dynamical System

The presence of coupling within a dipole dynamical system imposes constraints that alter its effective degrees of freedom. As a result, the coupling coefficient defines a non-diagonal transformation metric, rendering the conventional Cartesian coordinate system unsuitable as an orthonormal frame for such dynamics. Consequently, the system cannot yield independent dynamical equations without performing a diagonalization of the coupled dipole dynamics. This diagonalization process establishes an orthonormal reference frame and redefines the modified degrees of freedom. The resulting diagonalized dynamics correspond to coupled-mode oscillatory dipole motion, which mitigates the chaotic behavior arising from the initially random dipole moments. In the present framework, we extend the diagonalization and modal-expansion formalism developed in the previous section, and demonstrate how the inclusion of gradient and higher-order terms modifies both the modal dynamics and the resulting output field. The collective displacement vector is defined as follows:

R(t)=\big[r_{1}(t),r_{2}(t),\dots,r_{N}(t)\big]^{T},

(16)

0,&i=j.\end{cases}

(30)

Using the scalar Green’s function

G(r,r^{\prime})=\frac{e^{ik_{0}|r-r^{\prime}|}}{4\pi|r-r^{\prime}|},

(31)

the radiated output field is expressed as

E_{\rm out}(r)=k_{0}^{2}\varepsilon_{0}\int\!\!\int G(r,r^{\prime})\,\chi(r^{\prime},r^{\prime\prime};\omega,|E_{\omega}|)\,E_{\omega}(r^{\prime\prime})\,d^{3}r^{\prime\prime}\,d^{3}r^{\prime},

(32)

where the effective susceptibility kernel contains the nonlinear and modal corrections. In modal form, inserting Eq. (
29
), we obtain

E_{\rm out}(r)=k_{0}^{2}\varepsilon_{0}\sum_{n}\phi_{n}(r)\,\frac{Ne^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}m}\,\frac{\langle\phi_{n}|E_{\omega}\rangle-\tfrac{1}{e}\langle\phi_{n}|F_{{\rm grad},\omega}\rangle}{\Omega_{n}^{2}-\omega^{2}-i\gamma\omega}\;\otimes\;\Big(\int\!\!\int\phi_{n}(r^{\prime})\otimes\phi_{n}^{*}(r^{\prime\prime})\,G(r,r^{\prime})\,E_{\omega}(r^{\prime\prime})\,d^{3}r^{\prime\prime}\,d^{3}r^{\prime}\Big).

(33)

Equation (
33
) shows that the gradient terms in Eq.
2.3
act as additional driving sources for the collective dipole modes. They do not primarily shift modal resonances (denominator), but rather enhance or suppress excitation of specific modes via the modal projection
\langle\phi_{n}|F_{{\rm grad},\omega}\rangle
. This modifies the polarization spectrum and, consequently, the scattered output field. Nonlinear corrections from
B^{(1)}_{2}
and
B^{(1)}_{3}
Further modify the response in higher-order perturbation theory.

2.5
Lorentz Force Impact on Dipole Moment

After synchronisation, if the intensity distribution changes suddenly, the Lorentz Force also comes into play due to the pre-existence of oscillation dipoles. The magnetic force part of the Lorentz force can again perturb the coupling forces. The general mathematical form of Lorentz forces is as follows.

\mathbf{F}_{Lorentz}=(\mathbf{p}_{i}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{E}^{\prime}_{Ext}(\mathbf{r}_{i},t)+\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}\times\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}_{i},t)+HOMP

(34)

Here, the influence of the magnetic field has been taken into account. However, this effect can generally be neglected since
\mathbf{B}
may be expressed as
\mathbf{B}=\frac{\mathbf{E}}{\mathbf{c}}
, making its numerical contribution insignificant. The term
\mathbf{E}^{\prime}_{\text{Ext}}
represents the modified electric field distribution, which is redistributed from the original external field
\mathbf{E}_{\text{Ext}}
due to the presence of dynamic turbulence. The term
HOMP
denotes the Higher-Order Multipole components contributing to the Lorentz force. In the present analysis, only dipolar contributions are considered, and thus higher-order terms are neglected. The force arising from the newly redistributed field introduces perturbations into the previously synchronized dipole system, thereby reintroducing randomness into both the spatial distribution and the oscillation modes of the dipoles. Accordingly, the total perturbation force can be expressed as follows when the field distribution changes after synchronization:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\delta F}_{Pert}=\mathbf{F}_{Tot}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{Tot}=\mathbf{F}_{Ext}+\mathbf{F}_{Coupling}+\mathbf{F}_{HO}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{Ext}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{Coupling}-\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{HO}-\mathbf{F}_{Lorentz}

\displaystyle=-e\mathbf{E}_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}_{i},t)+e^{2}\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j})\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}(t)+\sum_{j\neq i}\left((e(\vec{\mathbf{r}}_{j}-\vec{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{E}_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}_{j},t)+HO\right)

\displaystyle+e\mathbf{E^{\prime}}_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}_{i},t)-e^{2}\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j})\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}(t)-\sum_{j\neq i}\left((e(\vec{\mathbf{r}}_{j}-\vec{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{E^{\prime}}_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}_{j},t)+HO\right)

\displaystyle-(\mathbf{p}_{i}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{E}^{\prime}_{Ext}(\mathbf{r}_{i},t)-\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}\times\mathbf{B}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}_{i},t)-HOMP^{\prime}

(35)

The influence of the gradient force acts to counter the effect of abrupt changes in the external field on the synchronized dipole moments. Simultaneously, this gradient force mitigates variations in the output field by promoting synchronization of the dipole moments within the medium. As a result, the standard deviation of the output field distribution becomes larger than that of the input field. Furthermore, as the length of the medium increases, synchronization is enhanced, leading to an increase in the standard deviation. The fluctuations in the standard deviation, caused by continuous changes in the input field distribution, are consequently reduced due to the action of these gradient forces.

2.6
d’Alembert’s Principle & Effective Force of Inertia

In the present context of electric field forces acting on a dipole system, the presence of dynamic turbulence alters both the magnitude and spatial distribution of the optical forces. These variations in the electric field forces induce corresponding changes in the inertial forces experienced by the dipoles. Such phenomena can be rigorously analyzed using d’Alembert’s principle applied to the Lorentz dipole dynamics. For the current system, the following relations can be derived from d’Alembert’s principle under the influence of two distinct electric field distributions:

\int{\delta W}=\int{\left(\mathbf{F}_{0th}+\mathbf{F}_{HO}+\mathbf{F}_{Coupling}+\mathbf{F}_{Inertia}\right)\cdot dx}=0

(36)

\int{\delta W^{\prime}}=\int{\left(\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{0th}+\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{HO}+\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{Coupling}+\mathbf{F}_{Lorentz}+\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{Inertia}\right)\cdot dx}=0

(37)

Here, the
(^{\prime})-
notation denotes the parameters corresponding to the modified forced condition. For each variation in the field distribution and its propagation through the medium, the dipole system experiences varying inertial forces. Each inertial force establishes a corresponding inertia level within the system, governing the oscillatory behavior of the dipoles. Following the propagation of the initial field, the dipoles begin to oscillate in a coupled mode. Upon synchronization, if the field distribution changes, the inertial forces generated by the first field act in opposition to those of the second field. Consequently, the perturbing force arises from the difference between the inertial forces associated with the two distinct electric field distributions. Therefore, the perturbation force induced by dynamic turbulence acting on the varying electric fields can be expressed as follows:

\delta\mathbf{F}_{Pert}=\mathbf{F}^{\prime}_{Inertia}-\mathbf{F}_{Inertia}

(38)

In the present context, the presence of dynamic turbulence impact on the electric field spatial distribution of the propagating optical field includes the time dependency of this perturbation force. Hence, we must have
\delta\mathbf{F}_{Pert}\rightarrow\delta\mathbf{F}_{Pert}(t)
. After such perturbation, the dynamical equation must look like the following equation.

\displaystyle m\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_{i}+m\gamma\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{i}+m\omega_{0}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{i}+\beta_{i}|\mathbf{r}_{i}|\mathbf{r}_{i}+\alpha_{i}|\mathbf{r}_{i}|^{2}\mathbf{r}_{i}

\displaystyle=-e\mathbf{E}_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}_{i},t)+e^{2}\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\mathbf{r}_{j})\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}(t)+\sum_{j\neq i}\left((e(\vec{\mathbf{r}}_{j}-\vec{\mathbf{r}}_{i})\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{E}_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}_{j},t)+HO\right)+\delta\mathbf{F}_{Pert}(t)

(39)

We can find the following conditions on the perturbed force depending upon corresponding magnitudes.

•

\delta\mathbf{F}_{Pert}\rightarrow 0
: The output field distribution can be caused by the saturated synchronized dipole moment distribution. Hence, the turbulence impact can be compensated fully with presence of medium dipole-dipole coupling energy transitions.

•

\delta\mathbf{F}_{Pert}\rightarrow
Small but
\neq 0
: The dynamic nature of turbulence changes the synchronization with induction of perturbed inertia force. The perturbation is small, hence, output field turbulence impact can be found compensated.

•

\delta\mathbf{F}_{Pert}>0
: In such case, the output field distribution will be dependent upon the frequency of change of perturbation. If turbulence is strong, i.e., change of perturbation is rapid, the medium dipole coupled system can’t find time to be synchronized. Thus, for strong turbulence, we can find un-compensated turbulence impacted output field. If the turbulence is weak, output field can be found compensated from turbulence.

3
Statistical Background

The statistical analysis presented in this study investigates the behaviour of turbulence-affected optical beams using both probabilistic modeling and information-geometric frameworks. Intensity fluctuations recorded under different turbulence and compensation conditions are modeled through Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) and Kernel Density Estimation (KDE), providing continuous representations of the evolving beam statistics. Several statistical divergence measures—such as Kullback–Leibler, Jensen–Shannon, Bhattacharyya, and Wasserstein distances—are employed to quantify dissimilarities between fitted intensity distributions. To explore the intrinsic geometry of the data, the GMM manifolds are embedded into the space of Symmetric Positive Definite (SPD) matrices, where Affine-Invariant Riemannian Metric (AIRM) distances are used to characterize the evolution of beam topology. This unified framework combines statistical inference, geometric distance computation, and experimental optical data, enabling a comprehensive understanding of turbulence compensation mechanisms through PMMA-based optical elements.

3.1
Fisher-Rao Distance Formalism

To quantify statistical differences between continuous intensity or probability distributions, we employ the Fisher-Rao formalism, which provides a differential-geometric framework for measuring distances on the manifold of probability densities. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{G},P)
denote a probability space, and
X:\Omega\to\mathcal{X}
a random variable taking values in a measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},\mu)
. Assuming absolute continuity of the pushforward measure
X_{*}P
with respect to
\mu
, the corresponding probability density is expressed as
p_{\xi}(x)=\frac{dX_{*}P}{d\mu}
, parameterized by a vector
\xi=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})\in\Xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The family,

\mathcal{S}=\left\{p_{\xi}(x)\,\big|\,\xi\in\Xi,\;\int_{\mathcal{X}}p_{\xi}(x)\,d\mu(x)=1,\;p_{\xi}(x)>0\right\}

(40)

constitutes an
n
-dimensional statistical manifold, where each point corresponds to a distinct probability distribution. The tangent space
T_{p_{\xi}}\mathcal{S}
is spanned by the score functions
\partial_{i}\log p_{\xi}(x)
, and the Fisher information metric provides a natural Riemannian structure on
\mathcal{S}
:

g_{ij}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\xi}}\!\left[\frac{\partial\log p_{\xi}}{\partial\xi_{i}}\frac{\partial\log p_{\xi}}{\partial\xi_{j}}\right]=\int_{\mathcal{X}}p_{\xi}(x)\frac{\partial\log p_{\xi}(x)}{\partial\xi_{i}}\frac{\partial\log p_{\xi}(x)}{\partial\xi_{j}}\,d\mu(x),

(41)

where the expectation is taken over
p_{\xi}(x)
. The Fisher information matrix
G(\xi)=[g_{ij}(\xi)]
is symmetric and positive-definite, thus defining a Riemannian metric on the parameter space. An alternative equivalent expression follows from the curvature of the log-likelihood
\ell(x;\xi)=\log p_{\xi}(x)
:

g_{ij}(\xi)=-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\xi}}\!\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\ell(x;\xi)}{\partial\xi_{i}\partial\xi_{j}}\right].

(42)

A differentiable curve
\xi(t)
,
t\in[0,1]
, in
\Xi
induces a path
\gamma(t)=p_{\xi(t)}
in
\mathcal{S}
, with an infinitesimal line element,

ds^{2}=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}(\xi)\,d\xi_{i}\,d\xi_{j}.

(43)

The total length of the curve is,

\ell(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\dot{\xi}(t)^{\!\top}G(\xi(t))\dot{\xi}(t)}\,dt,

(44)

and the Fisher–Rao distance between two distributions
p_{\xi_{1}}
and
p_{\xi_{2}}
is defined as the minimum geodesic length:

d_{\mathrm{FR}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\inf_{\gamma}\left\{\ell(\gamma)\;\middle|\;\gamma(0)=p_{\xi_{1}},\,\gamma(1)=p_{\xi_{2}}\right\}.

(45)

The geodesic
\gamma(t)
satisfies the Euler–Lagrange equations with Christoffel symbols,

\ddot{\xi}^{k}+\Gamma^{k}_{ij}(\xi)\dot{\xi}^{i}\dot{\xi}^{j}=0,\quad\Gamma^{k}_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{2}g^{k\ell}\left(\frac{\partial g_{\ell i}}{\partial\xi_{j}}+\frac{\partial g_{\ell j}}{\partial\xi_{i}}-\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial\xi_{\ell}}\right).

(46)

For a single-parameter distribution
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, Eq. (
45
) reduces to,

d_{\mathrm{FR}}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\left|\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi_{2}}\sqrt{g_{11}(\xi)}\,d\xi\right|.

(47)

This one-dimensional case represents an exact geodesic length in the statistical manifold. For the exponential distribution
p_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}
,
x\geq 0
, the Fisher information is
g_{11}(\lambda)=1/\lambda^{2}
, yielding
d_{\mathrm{FR}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=|\log(\lambda_2 / \lambda_1)|
. For the univariate Gaussian distribution
p(x|\mu,\sigma)=(1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}))e^{-(x-\mu)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}
, the Fisher information matrix is
G(\mu,\sigma)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}&0\\
0&\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}\end{pmatrix},

which corresponds to a hyperbolic metric
ds^{2}=\frac{d\mu^{2}+2\,d\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}
. The resulting Fisher–Rao distance is,

d_{\mathrm{FR}}((\mu_{1},\sigma_{1}),(\mu_{2},\sigma_{2}))=2\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{arctanh}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}+2(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})^{2}}{(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}+2(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2})^{2}}}\right)

(48)

The Fisher–Rao metric provides a quantitative measure of distinguishability between infinitesimally close distributions through the local metric tensor
ds^{2}=g_{ij}(\xi)\,d\xi_{i}d\xi_{j}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\xi}}\!\left[\partial_{i}\log p_{\xi}\,\partial_{j}\log p_{\xi}\right]d\xi_{i}d\xi_{j}
. The corresponding geodesic distance
d_{\mathrm{FR}}
satisfies all metric properties and encodes the intrinsic curvature of the statistical manifold. It arises as the second-order term in the expansion of the Kullback–Leibler divergence,
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\xi_{1}}\,\|\,p_{\xi_{2}})=\tfrac{1}{2}\,d_{\mathrm{FR}}^{2}(p_{\xi_{1}},p_{\xi_{2}})+o(d_{\mathrm{FR}}^{2})
thus linking information geometry with classical divergence measures.In the context of optical field propagation and turbulence-induced distortions, it provides a natural quantitative framework for describing the manifold geometry underlying statistical fluctuations and compensation processes.

3.2
AIRM Distance Formalism

In the information geometric framework, n-dimensional data sets are modelled as samples from a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), allowing their representation on a statistical manifold. For a n-dimensional data set
X=\{x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the underlying probability density function is given by

p(x;\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k}),\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}=1

(49)

where
\theta=\{(\pi_{k},\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
parameterizes the manifold
\mathcal{M}
. To measure similarities between such models, an embedding
f:\mathcal{M}\to SPD_{K(n+1)}(\mathbb{R})
is defined, where
SPD_{m}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the manifold of
m\times m
symmetric positive definite matrices. The embedding is

f(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}A&X\\
X^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}

(50)

with

A=diag(\Sigma_{1}+\pi_{1}\mu_{1}\mu_{1}^{T},\dots,\Sigma_{K}+\pi_{K}\mu_{K}\mu_{K}^{T})\in\mathbb{R}^{Kn\times Kn}

(51)

X=diag(\pi_{1}\mu_{1},\dots,\pi_{K}\mu_{K})\in\mathbb{R}^{Kn\times K}

(52)

B=diag(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{K})\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}

(53)

This block matrix is symmetric positive definite, embedding the GMM into the SPD manifold. The geometry of
SPD_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is equipped with a Riemannian metric

ds^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left((S^{-1}dS)^{2}\right)

(54)

for
S\in SPD_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
dS
an infinitesimal perturbation in the tangent space. The affine-invariant metric on
SPD_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is defined for
A\in SPD_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
X,Y\in T_{A}SPD_{n}(\mathbb{R})
as

\rho_{A}(X,Y)=\Tr(A^{-1} X A^{-1} Y)

(55)

This metric is invariant under transformations
A\mapsto G^{T}AG
for
G\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. The geodesic path connecting
A,B\in SPD_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is

\gamma(t)=A^{1/2}(A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2})^{t}A^{1/2},\quad t\in[0,1]

(56)

The corresponding geodesic distance, known as the Affine-Invariant Riemannian Metric (AIRM) distance, is

d(A,B)=\left\|\log(A^{-1/2} B A^{-1/2})\right\|_{F}

(57)

where
\log
denotes the matrix logarithm and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. This distance quantifies the dissimilarity between embedded n-dimensional data set on the SPD manifold, facilitating analysis of geometric and statistical properties in turbulence-impacted optical beams. By Theorem 3 from reference
[
1
]
, the embedding
f:\mathcal{M}\to\mathrm{SPD}_{K(n+1)}(\mathbb{R})
makes

(\mathcal{M},f^{*}\rho)
isometric to its image
(f(\mathcal{M}),\rho|_{{f(\mathcal{M})}})
, so the pullback of the affine invariant Riemannian Metric endows the GMM manifold with a Riemannian metric. By Theorem 4 from reference
[
1
]
, the affine invariant Riemannian distance on an ambient space is a
lower bound
for the the pullback distance on the manifold of GMMs. This enables one to use the affine invariant Riemannian distance on the ambient space for the similarity measure for GMMs. In analyzing the statistical properties of turbulence-impacted optical beams, Pearson correlation provides a measure of similarity between probability density functions derived from beam intensity distributions. For two probability density functions
f(x)
and
g(x)
sampled over bins
x_{i}
(
i=1,\dots,N
), the Pearson correlation coefficient is defined as

\rho_{f,g}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(f(x_{i})-\bar{f}\right)\left(g(x_{i})-\bar{g}\right)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(f(x_{i})-\bar{f}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(g(x_{i})-\bar{g}\right)^{2}}},

\rho_{f,g}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(f(x_{i})-\bar{f}\right)\left(g(x_{i})-\bar{g}\right)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(f(x_{i})-\bar{f}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(g(x_{i})-\bar{g}\right)^{2}}}

(58)

where
\bar{f}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(x_{i})
and
\bar{g}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g(x_{i})
are the means of
f
and
g
, respectively. The coefficient
\rho_{f,g}
ranges from
-1
to
1
. The value of
\rho_{f,g}
offers insights into the relationship between
f
and
g
:
- A positive correlation (
\rho>0
) indicates that
f
and
g
vary in the same direction, with peaks and troughs aligning, suggesting greater similarity as
\rho
increases.
- A negative correlation (
\rho<0
) implies variation in opposite directions, where peaks of one coincide with troughs of the other, indicating structural dissimilarity.
- A correlation near zero (
\rho\approx 0
) suggests no clear linear relation, with fluctuations appearing independent.

k

3.4
Kernel Density Estimation

In the analysis of optical beams affected by turbulence, the spatial intensity pattern often exhibits non-Gaussian fluctuations that cannot be captured adequately by parametric models. To obtain a continuous and smooth representation of such data, a nonparametric approach based on Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) is employed. KDE constructs a continuous approximation of the underlying probability density function (PDF) directly from discrete pixel intensity samples, enabling the treatment of optical images as realizations of stochastic irradiance fields. Let
\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\}
denote
n
samples drawn from an unknown distribution with
density
f(x)
. The kernel density estimator of
f(x)
is defined as,

\hat{f}_{h}(x)=\frac{1}{nh}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\!\left(\frac{x-x_{i}}{h}\right),

(60)

where
K(\cdot)
is a kernel function satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(u)\,du=1
, and
h>0
is the bandwidth parameter controlling the degree of smoothing. Small bandwidths lead to overfitting (high variance), while large bandwidths cause oversmoothing (high bias), thereby defining a bias–variance trade-off. For image data, the pixel intensities act as samples of the optical irradiance field, and Eq. (
60
) provides a smooth, data-driven estimate of the underlying distribution. For two-dimensional spatial data
\mathbf{x}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})^{\top}
, the multivariate kernel density estimator is expressed as,

\hat{f}_{H}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|H|^{-1/2}\,K\!\left(H^{-1/2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i})\right),

(61)

where
H
is a symmetric, positive-definite bandwidth matrix. The determinant
|H|
ensures proper normalization, and the matrix form allows anisotropic smoothing, which is critical in turbulence-affected data where intensity distortions may exhibit directional dependencies. A Gaussian kernel is typically adopted for its smoothness and analytical tractability:

K_{H}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2\pi|H|^{1/2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}^{\top}H^{-1}\mathbf{u}\right),

(62)

leading to the Gaussian KDE formulation,

\hat{f}_{H}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{n(2\pi)|H|^{1/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i})^{\top}H^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{i})\right).

(63)

The bandwidth matrix
H
defines both the smoothing scale and the orientation of local averaging and is often parameterized as,

H=h^{2}\Sigma,

(64)

Where
\Sigma
is the sample covariance matrix and
h
is a scalar scaling factor. The covariance structure allows the estimator to adapt to anisotropic turbulence distortions, where beam elongation or directional spread occurs due to phase-front perturbations. The computational cost of KDE can be reduced by exploiting the convolution theorem. Since KDE corresponds to the convolution of an intensity image
I(\mathbf{x})
with a Gaussian kernel
K_{H}(\mathbf{x})
, it can be expressed as,

I*K_{H}\;\longleftrightarrow\;\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left(\mathcal{F}[I](\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\mathcal{F}[K_{H}](\boldsymbol{\omega})\right),

(65)

where
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
denote the Fourier and inverse Fourier transforms, and
\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_{x},\omega_{y})
is the spatial frequency vector. For a Gaussian kernel, the Fourier transform is,

\mathcal{F}[K_{H}](\boldsymbol{\omega})=\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\top}H\boldsymbol{\omega}\right),

(66)

which yields the efficient FFT-based KDE formulation:

\hat{f}_{H}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{S}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left[\mathcal{F}[I](\boldsymbol{\omega})\cdot\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\top}H\boldsymbol{\omega}\right)\right],

(67)

where
S
is a normalization constant ensuring
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\hat{f}_{H}(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}=1
. This formulation enables high-resolution probability field estimation with minimal computational overhead. In turbulence-impacted optical propagation, pixel intensities correspond to local realizations of the fluctuating irradiance field. Applying KDE yields, 1) A smooth, continuous probability map that suppresses high-frequency noise; 2) A differentiable field suitable for information-geometric and statistical analysis and 3) An adaptable representation that preserves the topological and energetic structure of the optical beam. Consequently, KDE transforms discrete intensity data into a continuous statistical manifold, forming a foundation for subsequent analyses using Fisher–Rao geometry or Affine-Invariant Riemannian metrics on the space of distributions.

3.5
Statistical Divergences and Distances

Statistical divergence and distance measures form the mathematical basis for quantifying the dissimilarity between probability distributions. In the analysis of optical beams propagating through turbulence, such measures enable a rigorous comparison of intensity distributions, which fluctuate randomly due to refractive index perturbations. Unlike direct pixel-based or deterministic comparisons, divergence-based metrics capture differences in the underlying statistical structure while preserving geometric and probabilistic characteristics of the data. These formulations are widely used in optical physics, image analysis, machine learning, and turbulence characterization to describe and compare complex intensity distributions. The following discussion summarizes several fundamental statistical measures used in this work, including the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence,
L_{2}
Euclidean distance, Bhattacharyya distance, and Wasserstein (Earth Mover’s) distance. Each of these measures originates from distinct mathematical principles—information theory, probability geometry, or optimal transport—but all serve to describe how one distribution differs from another over a common domain
\mathcal{X}
. The Kullback–Leibler divergence, introduced by Kullback and Leibler (1951), quantifies the expected information loss when a reference distribution
q(x)
is used to approximate a true distribution
p(x)
. For discrete and continuous probability spaces, it is defined as:

D_{KL}(P\|Q)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)},\qquad D_{KL}(p\|q)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx.

(68)

It satisfies
D_{KL}(P\|Q)\geq 0
with equality only when
P=Q
, and is asymmetric, hence not a true distance. In turbulence-affected optical fields,
D_{KL}
quantifies the entropy-based deviation of distorted beam intensities from their reference profiles, reflecting information loss due to scattering or phase noise. The Jensen–Shannon divergence provides a symmetric, bounded variant of the KL divergence, defined using the mixture distribution
M=\tfrac{1}{2}(P+Q)
:

D_{JS}(P\|Q)=\tfrac{1}{2}D_{KL}(P\|M)+\tfrac{1}{2}D_{KL}(Q\|M).

(69)

In continuous form,

D_{JS}(p\|q)=\tfrac{1}{2}\int p(x)\log\frac{2p(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}\,dx+\tfrac{1}{2}\int q(x)\log\frac{2q(x)}{p(x)+q(x)}\,dx.

(70)

This divergence is symmetric and bounded (
0\leq D_{JS}\leq\log 2
), and
\sqrt{D_{JS}}
defines a true metric. In optical beam analysis,
D_{JS}
serves as a stable similarity measure for comparing turbulence-affected intensity distributions, being less sensitive to rare events than
D_{KL}
. The Euclidean or
L_{2}
distance measures geometric deviation between two probability densities:

d_{L_{2}}(p,q)=\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}}(p(x)-q(x))^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}.

(71)

It is symmetric, satisfies the triangle inequality, and forms a true metric. In optical turbulence studies,
d_{L_{2}}
represents the pointwise difference between two smoothed intensity fields (e.g., via KDE), serving as a direct geometric measure of deviation. The Bhattacharyya distance measures the degree of overlap between two probability distributions through the Bhattacharyya coefficient:

BC(p,q)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{p(x)q(x)}\,dx,\qquad D_{B}(p,q)=-\ln\big( BC(p, q) \big).

(72)

A smaller
D_{B}
indicates greater similarity between distributions. This measure provides an upper bound on the Bayes classification error and is related to the Hellinger distance by
H^{2}(p,q)=1-BC(p,q)
. In optical systems, the Bhattacharyya distance quantifies the probabilistic overlap between turbulence-distorted and compensated beams, indicating the degree of statistical coherence retained after propagation. The Wasserstein or Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD) originates from optimal transport theory. It measures the minimum “cost” of transporting probability mass to transform one distribution into another. The first-order Wasserstein distance is defined as,

W(P,Q)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(P,Q)}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}|x-y|\,d\gamma(x,y),

(73)

where
\Pi(P,Q)
is the set of all joint distributions with marginals
P
and
Q
. The general
p^{\text{th}}
-order form is,

W_{p}(P,Q)=\left(\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(P,Q)}\int|x-y|^{p}\,d\gamma(x,y)\right)^{1/p}.

(74)

Unlike information divergences, Wasserstein distance incorporates the geometry of the underlying space, capturing both spatial displacement and distributional shape. In optical imaging, it quantifies the spatial transport cost required to morph a turbulence-distorted beam into its reference state. Each of the above divergence measures emphasizes a distinct aspect of statistical dissimilarity—entropy, geometry, overlap, or transport cost. Together, they provide a comprehensive framework for evaluating beam similarity and turbulence compensation efficiency. In the present analysis, these metrics are employed to compare probability density functions of beam intensity distributions obtained from experimental and computational data, thereby linking optical field statistics with information-geometric structure.

Experimental Varifications

The experimental setup is schematically depicted in Fig.
1
. A continuous-wave laser beam was first passed through a spatial filter assembly (SFA) to generate a clean Gaussian profile with minimized higher-order distortions. The spatially filtered Gaussian beam was then directed using two mirrors (M1 and M2) to control the propagation path and ensure proper alignment. A programmable rotating phase plate (PRPP) was placed in the optical path to introduce controlled turbulence effects by applying dynamic random phase modulations. Subsequently, the turbulence-affected beam was transmitted through polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) rods, which served as dielectric media. One or two rods were employed to examine cumulative light–matter interaction effects under turbulence conditions. Finally, the transmitted beam was recorded using a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera, providing both turbulence-impacted and turbulence-compensated intensity distributions of the emerging field.

The data acquisition procedure, summarized in Fig.

, was designed to capture the statistical fluctuations of the transmitted beam under four distinct experimental conditions. For each condition, 200 frames were recorded to ensure statistical convergence. The four experimental sets are: (i) Set 1: reference condition without turbulence (baseline), (ii) Set 2: turbulence introduced via the PRPP in the absence of PMMA rods, (iii) Set 3: turbulence in combination with a single PMMA rod, and (iv) Set 4: turbulence combined with two PMMA rods. Each recorded frame was analyzed by fitting a two-dimensional bivariate Gaussian function to the intensity distribution, with higher-order deviations quantified via skewness and kurtosis excess using a Gram–Charlier expansion. This systematic acquisition and analysis approach enabled direct comparisons between the turbulence-only and turbulence–PMMA coupled conditions, providing a robust statistical characterization of light propagation dynamics through dielectric media under turbulence.

Classical turbulence originates from irregular velocity fluctuations in viscous fluids, such as the atmosphere, where the flow may exist in either laminar or turbulent states. Laminar flow is characterized by smooth and orderly motion, whereas turbulent flow is dominated by random subflows or eddies that enhance mixing. The transition between these regimes is determined by the Reynolds number,
Re=\frac{Vl}{\nu}
where
V
is the flow velocity,
l
is the characteristic flow scale, and
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity. When
Re
exceeds a critical threshold (typically
\sim 10^{5}
near the ground), turbulence develops. According to Kolmogorov’s theory, turbulence is statistically homogeneous and isotropic at small scales, with energy generated at large scales through shear or convection cascading down to smaller eddies. Energy transfer occurs across an inertial range, bounded by an outer scale
L_{0}
and an inner scale
l_{0}
, until the remaining energy is dissipated as heat. The associated turbulence power spectrum is then expressed as:

\Phi(k)=0.023r_{0}^{-5/3}k^{-11/3}.

(75)

The Pseudo-Random Phase Plate (PRPP) employed in our experiment is a five-layered optical device designed to emulate atmospheric turbulence. It comprises two BK7 glass windows that enclose a central acrylic layer, which is patterned with a Kolmogorov-type turbulence profile. Near-index-matching polymer layers on either side of the acrylic provide mechanical stability, while the glass sealing enhances durability and reduces sensitivity to environmental variations. With an overall thickness of approximately 10 mm, the plate is robust and can be easily mounted on a rotary stage. It produces aberrated wavefronts with adjustable Fried coherence lengths (
r_{0}=16
–
32
samples) distributed over 4096 phase points, enabling precise and controllable turbulence simulations.

6
Conclusion

In this work, a unified theoretical and experimental framework has been developed to investigate the statistical and geometric properties of turbulence-impacted optical beam propagation and its compensation through dielectric media. By integrating the Lorentz anharmonic coupled dipole oscillator model with manifold-based statistical analysis, the study establishes a bridge between microscopic dipole dynamics and macroscopic beam statistics. Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) and Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) were employed to model the fluctuating intensity distributions, while Affine-Invariant Riemannian Metric (AIRM) distances quantified the evolution of beam topology within the manifold of Symmetric Positive Definite (SPD) matrices. Experimental validation using PMMA rods under controlled turbulence conditions revealed a consistent reduction in statistical distance measures and power fluctuations with increasing dielectric compensation, confirming partial restoration of statistical coherence through dipole–dipole interactions. The incorporation of gradient and inertial coupling forces in the Lorentz oscillator formalism elucidated how synchronization among dipoles leads to turbulence mitigation and stabilization of optical field dynamics. Furthermore, the observed geometric convergence on the SPD manifold demonstrated that the dielectric-induced mode synchronization effectively suppresses random fluctuations induced by dynamic turbulence. Overall, this research provides a comprehensive statistical–geometric interpretation of optical turbulence and establishes a new methodology for quantifying and compensating turbulence effects using coupled dipole interactions in transparent media. The developed framework offers potential extensions toward adaptive optics, optical encryption, and intelligent beam control systems, where turbulence resilience is critical for next-generation free-space optical communication.
