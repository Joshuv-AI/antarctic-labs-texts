Influence of hydrodynamics and depth on the performance of 3D-printed microbial fuel cells
Authors: Yuvraj Maphrio Mao, Ramya K, Khairunnisa Amreen, Sanket Goel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSubmersible devices, such as underwater monitoring systems and aquatic environmental sensors, represent a rapidly evolving research frontier driven by hydrodynamic challenges and the need for autonomous operations. Sustainable powering of these systems using Microbial Fuel Cells (MFCs) offers a viable and eco-friendly alternative. This study presents a comprehensive evaluation of 3D-printed MFCs fabricated via Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM), employing fiber-based electrodes as both anode and cathode materials. The devices were tested at varying depths within an acrylic tank under various hydrodynamic conditions (stagnant and aerated) in both lake and artificial seawater. Results revealed a moderate decline in power density with increasing submersion depth. However, aeration markedly enhanced performance in lake water, improving power retention from 62.88% to 68.06%, owing to increased oxygen availability that facilitated the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and microbial activity. In contrast, aerated artificial seawater exhibited a minor decline in performance (from 85.22% to 75.76%), likely due to ionic turbulence and bubble-induced disturbances within the electrolyte. Repeatability and operational stability tests confirmed consistent electrochemical performance, underscoring the reliability of these systems for long-term operation. Overall, this work advances the development of depth-adaptive, self-powered platforms for real-time underwater monitoring and environmental sensing applications.Subject terms: Fuel cells, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

The increasing interest in aquatic exploration for various purposes, such as marine ecosystem monitoring, water quality, pollution detection, and undersea surveillance, has led to significant attention on autonomous technologies. This, in turn, has driven advancements in underwater sensor networks and energy harvesting systems, particularly underwater microbial fuel cells (MFCs)1,2. Although conventional batteries have been widely used as power sources, they are not well-suited for the long-term operation of microdevices in underwater sensor networks. In contrast, energy harvesters, such as underwater MFCs, utilize naturally available resources in water, ranging from ions to microorganisms, to generate power, offering a more robust and sustainable solution for continuous underwater operations3,4. This configuration or setup not only eliminates the need for maintenance but also is aligned to promote environmentally friendly and self-sustaining energy technologies.

Building on these advancements, the integration of 3D-printing technology has gained momentum in advancing energy harvesting research, enabling the fabrication of miniaturized, customizable, and structurally efficient MFC structures designed for underwater energy harvesting systems and sensor networks5,6 Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM), a widely used additive manufacturing technique and a type of 3D printing, is frequently preferred for fabricating electrode materials and lightweight MFC casings. Compared to subtractive manufacturing, additive manufacturing offers greater design flexibility, allowing for the creation of complex geometries and architectures. This makes it suitable for developing high-performance, industrial-grade prototypes and devices7. Moreover, FDM-based printing supports both scaling down and scaling up of MFCs. For miniaturized applications, it enables the use of biocompatible materials to fabricate environmentally friendly, self-sustaining prototypes, while also reducing manufacturing costs8. Overall, the accuracy of FDM-based 3D printing offers great potential for advancing the design and materials of next-generation MFC configurations for sustainable energy harvesting applications.

Materials and electrodes play equally vital roles in enhancing the performance of MFCs by facilitating efficient electron transfer and supporting biofilm growth9. Among various types of materials and substrates, carbon-based electrodes have gained significant interest due to their excellent electrical conductivity and high surface area10–12 In addition, carbon-based materials are cost-effective and corrosion-resistant compared to traditional metal-based electrodes, making them particularly suitable for long-term and stable MFC operations13. A study by A. Janicek14 demonstrated the superior performance of carbon cloth compared to stainless steel, delivering greater stability and approximately 34 % higher power output over a month of operational cycles14. Moreover, carbon-based materials are inherently bio-compatible and available in various forms, such as carbon felt, carbon paper, carbon cloth (CC), and carbon nanotubes (CNTs), all of which have shown promising results not only in energy harvesting but also in energy storage applications15. While both CC and carbon paper offer beneficial properties such as flexibility, CC holds a distinct advantage, as carbon paper is prone to saturation over time, which reduces its durability and limits its suitability for repeated or long-term use16. Therefore, the combination of favorable electrochemical properties, structural versatility, and long-term stability makes carbon-based electrodes ideal candidates for effective MFC configuration.

This work comprehensively focuses on fabricating 3D-printed, fiber-based MFCs for energy-harvesting applications, evaluated at varying water depths (artificial seawater and lake water) under various hydrodynamic (aerated and stagnant) environmental conditions. The fuel cell casing is fabricated using polylactic acid (PLA) filament via 3D printing, incorporating a dual-chamber configuration. The anodic chamber is inoculated with Escherichia Coli (E. coli) cultured in LB broth nutrient medium. Both the anode and cathode materials consisted of commercially available carbon cloth, separated by a Nafion 212 membrane. The performance of the MFCs is assessed in different water types (artificial seawater and lake water) inside an acrylic tank (10 × 10 × 30 cm³) at varying depths under both aerated (influenced by bubbles) and stagnant (absence of bubbles) hydrodynamic conditions. In stagnant conditions, the artificial seawater showed the power density of 10.22 µW/cm2 at the surface, reducing to 8.71 µW/cm2 at 25 cm, while lake water showed a steeper decline starting at 8.73 µW/cm2 and dropping to 5.49 µW/cm2. Under aerated conditions, artificial seawater demonstrated slightly lower power output at the surface (7.80 µW/cm2) but yielded more stable and repeatable performance, obtaining 5.91 µW/cm2 at 25 cm depth. In contrast, aerated lake water showed a slight decline in maximum power output compared to stagnant conditions with increasing depth (starting at 8.83 µW/cm² and dropping to 6.01 µW/cm²), likely due to microbial respiration. Despite a marginal reduction in maximum power output, aeration improved stability and repeatability. These findings suggest that aerated conditions help maintain consistent performance of the MFCs at varying depths, possibly due to the improvement of oxygen transfer. A key distinguishing feature of this work is its depth-resolved electrochemical evaluation of 3D-printed underwater MFCs across two distinct aquatic environments, integrating both static and bubble-driven dynamic conditions—thereby enabling the study of how hydrostatic pressure, oxygen transport pathways, and electrolyte turbulence collectively influence energy-harvesting behaviour in submersible environments. Overall, this work highlights the potential of 3D-printed MFCs for underwater energy harvesting, particularly in applications requiring long-term deployment and stable performance at variable depths.

Methods

Materials and instrumentation

The fuel cell casing is fabricated using 3D printing (BCN Sigma D25, Barcelona, Spain) with non-conductive polylactic acid (PLA) filament, 2.85 mm in diameter, obtained from the SLS Engineer Store. Commercially available carbon cloth is used as both the anode and cathode materials, and Nafion 212 (thickness of 50 microns with ± 2 variation in the thickness), which serves as the membrane, is purchased from a fuel cell store. Luria Bertani (LB) broth (a widely referenced nutrient-rich microbial growth medium, pH range 6.8-7.2) is purchased from the online store of Merck. Artificial seawater is prepared by dissolving synthetic sea salt (Sampige crystal and unfiltered Sea Salt purchased from Amazon), with salinity carefully maintained between 33 and 37 g/L, measured and verified using a refractometer. An aeration simulator (Despacito air pump with two outlets and a filter motor) is also purchased from the e-commerce store of Amazon. Electrochemical analyses are carried out using a potentiostat (Biologic SP-150, France), while morphological characterization is performed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). X-Ray Diffraction (XRD, Rigaku, Japan) is utilized to identify the crystalline phase and structural composition of the electrode 17.

Bacteria inoculum

A preserved strain of Escherichia coli (E. coli) is revived from a previously cultured stock using LB broth as the nutrient medium. To initiate the culture, 200 µL of the preserved bacterial stock is added to 20 mL of freshly prepared LB broth. The nutrient broth is prepared by dissolving 5 g of LB Broth (Miller) in 250 mL of distilled water, followed by sterilization through autoclaving at 15 psi for 25 min to ensure a contamination-free environment. After inoculation, the culture is incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow sufficient bacterial growth. This prepared bacterial culture serves as the anodic fuel for the operation of the MFC, facilitating electron transfer in the anodic chamber.

Fabrication of the MFC and experimental setups

Carbon cloth electrodes measuring 3.5 cm × 3.5 cm are used as both the anode and cathode materials. Nafion 212 serves as the proton exchange membrane. The fuel cell casing is designed using computer-aided design (CAD) software and fabricated using an FDM 3D printer using standard black PLA filament (2.85 mm diameter). During printing, the nozzle temperature is maintained at ~220 °C, while the bed temperature is kept at around 60 °C to ensure optimal adhesion and print quality. The overall design is illustrated in Fig. 1 (a), where the fuel cell features an open cathode chamber configured as an air cathode, while the anodic chamber is fully enclosed and houses the bacterial inoculum. When the fuel cell is submerged to increasing depths in the acrylic tank (10 cm × 10 cm × 30 cm), the open cathode chamber interacts with ions or microorganisms present in the synthesized water (artificial seawater and lake water), enabling the device to function effectively under various environmental conditions. Figure 1(b) shows the fully assembled device and the experimental setup used for electrochemical testing under stagnant and aerated conditions.

The electrochemical analysis of the MFC is carried out using a two-electrode system with connections at various depths under both aerated and stagnant hydrodynamic aquatic environments, with carbon cloth serving as both the anode and cathode. In contrast, the electrochemical characterization of the individual electrodes is performed using a three-electrode setup, utilizing Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode and a platinum rod as the counter electrode, to gain insight into the reaction kinetics.

Nafion 212 activation

The Nafion 212 membrane (3.5 cm × 3.5 cm) is activated by immersing it in a beaker over a hot plate in a 3 M H2SO4 solution for 1 h at 80˚ °C, followed by a treatment in DI water under the same operating conditions. This process activates Sulfonic acid (-SO3H) groups, which enhances proton conductivity and removes surface contaminants, enhancing the ion exchange capacity18. The choice of 3 M H2SO4 and 80˚ °C is based on previous studies by S. Pujiastuti19, who have reported optimal and peak performance under these conditions, a claim supported by electrochemical and physicochemical characterizations19. These activation steps are crucial to ensure the dependable operation of the MFC.

Results and discussions

Morphological analysis

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is conducted to analyze the morphological structure of the working electrode, as illustrated in Fig. 2 (a-b), under SE mode with the accelerating voltage of around 20 kV and a magnification of 1200 ×. The images reveal a well-aligned, cylindrical structure with smooth surfaces and well-distributed fibers, as illustrated in Fig. 2 (a). The highly interconnected fiber network facilitates consistent film formation, providing a large active surface area that enhances microbial adhesion at the electrodes and thereby improves the reaction mechanism with the microorganisms. This is crucial for efficient energy and power generation for the operating MFC20. This morphological analysis confirms the formation of a porous fibrous network with a uniform structure and demonstrates its suitability for uniform film formation, which is crucial for enhanced microbial interaction and attachment in MFC. In contrast, Fig. 2(b) shows the anodic surface after MFC inoculation, where rod-shaped microbial cells and surface deposits are clearly visible, confirming successful biofilm formation on the carbon fibers. Similar SEM micrograph demonstrating rod-based bacterial attachment and biofilm development has also been reported by Lee et al21. The intimate contact between microorganisms and conductive fibers enhances electron transfer pathways, thereby improving the bio-electrochemical reaction kinetics and power generation efficiency of the MFC. These observations demonstrate the transition from a clean fibrous electrode to an active bio-electrode, validating the functional role of the electrode morphology in supporting microbial colonization and sustained MFC performance

a pristine CC showing baseline fiber morphology, b CC after MFC inoculation, displaying rod-like structures indicative of E. coli colonization and early-stage biofilm formation. Images were captured at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, and a magnification of 1200 ×.

Elemental composition of the working electrode surface as determined by Energy Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The analysis reveals a high carbon content, with an apparent concentration of 69.02% and a corresponding weight percentage of 90.68%, indicating the carbonaceous nature of the electrode substrate. Oxygen was also detected at 2.52% (apparent concentration) and 9.07% (weight%), likely due to the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups. All the detected elements conform to the expected factory/industry standard, confirming the materials purity. The elemental profile of high carbon content and the presence of oxygen functionalities support the electrode suitability for enhanced electron transfer (EET), beneficial for the application of MFC.

Electrochemical characterization

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) analysis is conducted to evaluate and characterize the electrodes using a standard three-electrode setup, with Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode, a platinum rod as the counter electrode, and carbon cloth as the working electrode. This is done within a specified potential window of 1 V to −1 V at a scan rate of 50 mV/s. The CV graph in Fig. 3 (a) shows a well-defined redox peak, indicating electrochemical activity at the electrode-electrolyte interface and confirming the electrode is active for further electrochemical applications.

Electrochemical and Structural characterization of the electrodes.

a cyclic Voltammetry (CV) of the electrode recorded in 5 mM K3[Fe(CN)6] in 1 mM KCl as the supporting electrolyte at a scan rate of 50 mV/s. b XRD pattern of CC recorded in the 2ϴ range of 0˚ to 90˚.

Figure 3(b) illustrates X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the CC electrodes to determine their crystallinity and phase structure, which directly influence EET and biofilm formation. The diffraction pattern exhibits two broad peaks centered at 2ϴ values of 23.41˚ and 43.71˚, corresponding to graphitic carbon planes of (002) and (100), respectively, as reported in the literature22. These graphitic carbon sites contribute to electron conduction and influence electrochemical reactions 23.

The average crystalline size, calculated using the Scherrer equations, is ~4.60 nm, confirming the nanocrystalline size of the CC electrodes. Such a phase structure and nanoscale crystalline size are advantageous for MFC application, as they contribute to increasing surface area, enhancing electrochemical activity, and promoting microbial adhesion. This, in turn, provides abundant active sites that facilitate EET between the electrode and electroactive biofilms, ultimately supporting superior bio-electrochemical performance in MFC systems.

Polarization studies

Artificial sea water

Polarization studies of the fabricated MFC are conducted in artificial seawater within a controlled acrylic tank environment. The experiments are first performed under stagnant conditions to enable a comprehensive analysis of power output at varying depths. The power density decreases progressively with increasing depth, ranging from 10.22 µW/cm² at the surface level to 8.71 µW/cm² at a depth of 25 cm, as illustrated in Fig. 4. This decline in power output is attributed to the reduced availability of dissolved oxygen as the electrode is submerged deeper into the water column. Oxygen plays a vital role in the cathodic reaction of MFCs, and its limited diffusion at greater depths likely results in decreased electrochemical performance. This observation underscores the significance of oxygen accessibility in optimizing MFC output in marine or submerged conditions.

Polarization Studies of the fabricated MFC in a controlled environment of an acrylic tank containing artificial seawater in stagnant conditions with varying depths (surface level-25 cm).

Lake water

Similarly, in the lake environment, polarization studies are conducted under stagnant conditions in a controlled acrylic tank to evaluate the performance of MFC at varying immersion depths. This experimental setup provides insights into how depth-related oxygen availability influences electrochemical behavior. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the power density is 8.73 μW/cm² at the surface, gradually decreasing with depth to 5.49 μW/cm² at 25 cm. A similar trend is observed in the case of artificial seawater, as previously reported, attributing to the importance of the dissolved oxygen in the deeper region of the water column, where its limited availability may restrict the cathodic kinetics. Building on these observations, subsequent studies simulate aerated conditions using an aquarium pump to enhance the dissolved oxygen levels and assess their direct influence on improving MFC performance.

Polarization Studies of the fabricated MFC in a controlled environment of an acrylic tank containing lake water in stagnant conditions with varying depths (surface level-25 cm).

Artificial sea water (aerated condition)

Aerated Condition is simulated in the artificial seawater environment using an aquarium pump to further examine its impact on MFC performance across different depths. As illustrated in Fig. 6, a decline in power densities is still observed with increasing depth, similar to the trend under stagnant conditions. However, the drop in performance is slightly more pronounced in this case. For example, the power density decreases from 7.80 μW/cm² at the surface to 5.91 μW/cm² at 25 cm, compared to 10.22 μW/cm² to 8.71 μW/cm² in the stagnant setup. This greater reduction may be attributed to the high ionic strength of artificial seawater, which may enhance bubble-induced turbulence and create additional mass transport resistance. Such conditions hinder the effective diffusion of dissolved oxygen to the cathode, thereby reducing the efficiency of the cathodic reaction. These observations suggest that while aeration can improve oxygen availability, it must be carefully optimized to avoid negative effects associated with bubble-induced flow behavior, especially in high-salinity environments.

Polarization Studies of the fabricated MFC in a controlled environment of an acrylic tank containing artificial seawater simulated in the aerated conditions with varying depths (surface level-25 cm).

Lake water (aerated conditions)

Lake water is also examined under aerated conditions, and a similar depth-dependent decline in performance is observed. However, the reduction in power density with depth is less pronounced compared to the stagnant condition. For example, power density decreases from 8.83 μW/cm² at the surface to 6.01 μW/cm² at 25 cm, indicating improved performance relative to the stagnant setup, where values declined from 8.73 μW/cm² at the surface to 5.49 μW/cm² at 25 cm. This enhancement is likely due to improved oxygen availability facilitated by bubbles, which promotes greater electrochemical activity and microbial respiration as illustrated in Fig. 7.

Polarization Studies of the fabricated MFC in a controlled environment of an acrylic tank containing lake water simulated in aerated conditions with varying depths (surface level-25 cm).

These observations highlight that in both saline and stagnant (microorganism-rich) water, factors such as dissolved oxygen levels and the properties of the aquatic medium critically influence MFC performance. Therefore, tailoring oxygen delivery strategies such as controlled aeration becomes essential to mitigate depth-related performance losses in submerged energy harvesting systems.

Lake water consistently demonstrates higher power retention across cycles compared to artificial seawater, with minimal degradation even at a depth of 25 cm, highlighting the potential of lake environments to support the long-term operation of submersible electronics powered by bio-electrochemical systems.

Interestingly, the aerated conditions exhibited distinct effects depending on the medium. In artificial seawater, bubbling reduced retention (from 85.22% to 75.76%), likely due to ionic turbulence interfering with stable ion transport and electron flow, as well as the observed decrease in ion current with increasing turbulence24. In contrast, in lake water, bubbling enhanced retention (from 62.88% to 68.06%), attributed to improved microbial respiration via increased oxygen availability25. These observations reveal the dual nature of aeration. While potentially destabilizing in high ionic strength environments like seawater, it can be advantageous in nutrient-rich freshwater settings. The repeatable power generation demonstrated here validates the feasibility of integrating MFCs into submerged autonomous devices for continuous environmental monitoring and localized energy harvesting.

Stability

Stability (%) data over a 2 h operational window reveals significant insight into the long-term consistency of MFCs positioned at varying depths of varying aquatic conditions (artificial seawater) with environments (under both aerated and stagnant conditions). As observed from Table 2 and Fig. S5, lake water (both aerated and stagnant conditions) consistently demonstrates better stability across all depths. In contrast, artificial seawater in stagnant conditions exhibited the poorest stability, with values dropping below 40 % across all depths after 2 h of operational time and continuing to further decline over time. The inclusion of aeration in both environments moderately improved stability, likely due to enhanced oxygen diffusion and localized mixing.

Interestingly, deeper placements (15–25 cm) generally show more stable output profiles across all the aquatic media, possibly due to the reduced surface disturbance, which helps maintain a more controlled electrochemical environment. Stability in power output is a critical parameter for submersible or underwater energy harvesting applications, where continuous and predictable performance is essential for powering low-energy devices such as sensors or environmental monitoring systems. Fluctuating stability may disrupt power output and compromise data integrity in remote deployments. Therefore, even if absolute power output is reduced at depth due to lower oxygen availability or ionic turbulence, the observed increase in voltage stability suggests a promising operational trade-off. These findings highlight the potential of depth-optimized, various aquatic-environment-based MFC systems for sustained underwater energy harvesting, where durability, minimal maintenance, and consistent output are prioritized

When viewed on the broader context of underwater energy harvesting technologies—including piezoelectric, triboelectric, magnetic, and fluid-driven systems that exploit waves, flow-induced oscillations, bubbles, or biological motion—the present MFC-based approach occupies a distinct and complementary niche. Unlike motion- or turbulence-dependent harvesters, MFCs operate through bio-electrochemical processes, enabling energy generation under low-frequency, low-dynamic, or quiescent aquatic conditions. Consequently, the depth- and hydrodynamics-resolved analysis presented in this work provides critical insight into how MFC performance evolves under realistic underwater environments, strengthening their potential role as a stable, environmentally adaptive energy source for long-term submerged applications.

S.J.Nordfjord26 systematically examined and reviewed recent advancements in powering underwater robotic and sensor networks, focused on ocean energy harvesting (OEH) and underwater wireless power transfer (WPT). A structured literature review methodology based on the PRISMA framework is adopted to analyze peer-reviewed studies from major academic databases26. In comparison to previous literature based on various underwater energy harvesters, O. Alamu27 provided a comprehensive review of energy harvesting technologies, identifying MFC as a promising alternative for powering wireless sensors or underwater autonomous vehicles by harvesting energy naturally from the aquatic environments27. Additionally, C.L. Faria28 demonstrated an energy harvester system capable of delivering milliwatt-level power under ocean-wave-induced operation, indicating its ability to power multiple low-power sensors through appropriate energy management and system optimization28. Beyond MFC-based systems, bubble-driven pipe flow energy harvesters have also been explored by Z. Guan29 where rising bubble gas has been shown to significantly enhance power output by 5.48 × 103 times, generating sufficient energy to operate practical electronic devices, including timers, and powering a laser pointer by charging a 33 µF supercapacitor29. Furthermore, X. Wang30 developed a triboelectric-based energy harvester inspired by fish-tail movement, generating approximately 0.74 mW through oscillatory swinging in water. The assimilation of an anti-microbial coating enhanced biocompatibility and operational stability, thereby demonstrating the successful advancement of the sustainable underwater energy system30. Extending these concepts towards a mechanically robust harvesting system, Zou31. reported a magnetically coupled underwater energy harvester that delivered power of 450.5 µW while maintaining stable operation for an extended duration up to 144 h. Their results demonstrate that magnetic coupling combined with flextensional mechanisms offers strong potential for reliable energy harvesting under harsh underwater conditions31. Similarly, M.C.Chiu32 developed a single mass subsea electromagnetic energy harvester that converts wave-induced magnetic motion into electrical power, demonstrating that increased wave amplitude, reduced wavelength and higher harvester elevation significantly enhance the generated voltage and power output32. In addition, S. Kim33 fabricated a propeller-based underwater piezoelectric energy harvester to address low output and waterproofing challenges, utilizing rotating hitting sticks to excite a cantilever beam. The system achieved an output power of 17 mW under real flow conditions and successfully powered 972 LEDs, demonstrating practical feasibility for underwater sensing applications33. A. Khan34 categorized and evaluated energy harvesting techniques for UAWSNs, including thermal, piezeoelectric, solar, turbine, AUV motion, ultrasonic, and hybrid methods, in terms of operational mechanism, harvested power, and associated limitations, with reported power output ranging from 10 µW to 86.11 kW, providing practical insight for selecting suitable harvesting strategies for specific underwater applications34. In a complementary direction, N. Saeed35, developed a hybrid acoustic–optical underwater wireless sensor network (AO-UWSN) localization technique, employing an energy harvesting framework and a weighted multiple-observation strategy to enhance localization accuracy, mitigate noisy RSS measurements, and extend the operational lifetime of battery-limited sensor nodes 35.

By positioning the present work within this broader context, its contribution lies not in achieving the highest power density but in advancing the understanding of MFC behavior under varying submersion depths and hydrodynamic (stagnant and aerated) conditions, providing critical insights for the design of low-cost, depth-adaptive systems suitable for underwater energy harvesting and aquatic electronics.

Figure 8 illustrates that the power density decreases with increasing depth across all aquatic media, primarily due to the reduced availability of dissolved oxygen at lower levels, which limits cathodic reactions. The error bar observed here represents the standard deviation calculated from three independent experimental measurements. In the case of artificial seawater, the aerated condition exhibited a slightly greater decline in power density compared to the stagnant medium. This degradation can be attributed to the high ionic concentration and turbulence in artificial seawater, which may disrupt stable biofilm formation and hinder effective ionic current pathways. Conversely, the lake water medium exhibited improved stability and a smaller reduction in power density, indicating enhanced microbial activity and sustained electron transfer facilitated by native microbial communities. The organic matter and nutrients present in lake water likely supported efficient microbial respiration and biofilm growth, contributing to superior anodic performance even at increased depths. As further confirmed by the stability analysis, the lake water medium maintained consistent output under both stagnant and aerated conditions. This stability may be ascribed to the adaptability of its microbial community, capable of switching between aerobic and facultative anaerobic respiration pathways depending on oxygen availability. Additionally, natural buffering ions and organic substrates in the lake water likely contributed to a steady electrochemical environment, minimizing output fluctuations. These observations collectively highlight the synergistic influence of microbial respiration, dissolved oxygen concentration, and ionic equilibrium on the depth-dependent performance of submersible MFC.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the successful design, fabrication, and evaluation of 3D-printed, fiber-based MFCs for underwater energy harvesting across varying depths and aquatic environments. The air-cathode MFCs, powered by E. coli and constructed with PLA casings and fiber-based electrodes, showed measurable performance shifts as a function of depth, aquatic type (artificial seawater and lake water), and aquatic conditions (stagnant and aerated). In stagnant lake water, introducing an aerated environment improved overall power from 62.88% to 68.06%, whereas in artificial seawater, aeration reduced performance from 85.22% to 75.76% due to ionic turbulence. These variations underscore the importance of oxygen availability in enhancing microbial activity and supporting the ORR. The scientific insight of this work lies in elucidating how submersion depth, oxygen transfer dynamics, and electrolyte composition collectively modulate the performance of underwater MFCs, providing an autonomous understanding of depth-dependent energy harvesting studies. The devices further demonstrated strong repeatability and operational stability, validating their robustness for long-term submerged deployment. Looking ahead, optimizing electrode surface chemistry and integrating with wireless sensor systems could enable next-generation depth-adaptive, self-powered platforms for real-time underwater monitoring and environmental sensing.
