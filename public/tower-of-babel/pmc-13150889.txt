Comparing the Asia-Pacific Colorectal Screening and adjusted Asia-Pacific Colorectal Screening scores for risk stratification of advanced colorectal neoplasia in asymptomatic Vietnamese: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Duc Trong Quach, Nhu Thi Hanh Vu, Huong Thi Thuy To, Hong Nguyen Diem Tong, Dao Thi Anh Nguyen, Thu Dang Anh Phan, Dat Quoc Ngo, Toru Hiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThe Asia-Pacific Colorectal Screening (APCS) score is a validated tool for predicting advanced colorectal neoplasia (ACN) in the Asia-Pacific region. An adjusted APCS (A-APCS) score was recently developed by incorporating body mass index into the original score and was reported to outperform the original score but lacked external validation. This study aimed to compare the A-APCS score with the original APCS score in asymptomatic Vietnamese individuals.DesignA cross-sectional study.SettingDai Phuoc Polyclinic, Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam.ParticipantsAsymptomatic individuals underwent colonoscopy screening.Outcome measuresParticipants were categorised into three risk groups based on the sum of the APCS and A-APCS scores: average risk (AR), moderate risk (MR) and high risk (HR). The performance of the two scores was compared via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and McNemar tests.ResultsA total of 714 participants (median age 51 years, range: 18–79; female-to-male ratio: 1:1.46) were included, with an ACN prevalence of 9.0%. Both scores indicated effective predictive ability for ACN in the HR group compared with the AR group, with OR=3.878 (95% CI 1.777 to 8.068) and 3.266 (95% CI 1.617 to 6.595), respectively. The A-APCS score was more effective than the APCS score in predicting ACN in the MR group than in the AR group. However, no significant differences in area under the ROC curve were observed between the two scores for ACN prediction.ConclusionsCompared with the APCS score, the A-APCS score may not provide improved ACN risk stratification in asymptomatic Vietnamese individuals. Both scores are suboptimal and should be used to prioritise, rather than exclude, candidates for colonoscopy.Keywords: Risk Assessment, Early Detection of Cancer, Gastrointestinal tumours

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a substantial global health issue, being the third most frequently diagnosed cancer and the second most common cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide.1 The majority of CRC cases arise from colorectal neoplasia, especially advanced colorectal neoplasia (ACN), which encompasses advanced adenomas and CRC, and may rapidly develop into CRC.2 Therefore, early detection and removal of these premalignant lesions are indispensable for decreasing the incidence of CRC.3 4 Moreover, faecal immunochemical test (FIT)-based screening programmes are currently not available in some settings, including Vietnam. Colonoscopy is the only screening method that enables both the detection and endoscopic removal of premalignant colorectal lesions and selected early-stage CRC.5 In many Asian countries, however, single-payer healthcare systems and insufficient medical resources pose significant barriers to the implementation of CRC colonoscopy screening programmes.6 Therefore, non-invasive tools to identify high-risk individuals who should be prioritised for screening colonoscopy are crucial. In this context, the Asia-Pacific Colorectal Screening (APCS) score, which is based on four clinical factors, namely age, sex, family history of CRC and smoking, and demonstrated to be useful for stratifying the risk of ACN.6 7 Moreover, recent studies have suggested that incorporating other risk factors of ACN into the APCS score could enhance its performance.8,10 Obesity has been widely recognised as a risk factor for CRC,11 and meta-analyses have demonstrated that higher body mass index (BMI) is associated with an increased risk of colorectal neoplasia.12 13 The adjusted APCS (A-APCS) score, which includes BMI as a new predictor, has been recently developed and shown to outperform the original APCS score in the Chinese population.9 10 Nonetheless, the number of external validation studies remains limited, and discrepancies in BMI cut-off points persist across studies.

In Vietnam, Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) 2022 data estimated that CRC ranked fourth among all cancers, with 16 835 new cases, accounting for approximately 9.3% of all newly diagnosed malignancies. The estimated cumulative lifetime risk of developing CRC in the Vietnamese population was 1.7%, equivalent to a low-to-intermediate risk.1 The value of the APCS score has only been investigated in Vietnamese patients with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS),14 and there is currently no evidence regarding the efficacy of the APCS score or the A-APCS score in asymptomatic individuals. This study aimed to compare the performance of the A-APCS score against the original APCS score in stratifying the risk of ACN in Vietnamese asymptomatic adults.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This cross-sectional validation study was conducted between April 2022 and March 2023 on consecutive asymptomatic individuals undergoing screening colonoscopy at Dai Phuoc Polyclinic, Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. This study represents a post hoc analysis of a previously collected dataset, undertaken to address a research question distinct from the primary analysis. As there is currently no national CRC screening programme in Vietnam, participants underwent health check-ups and screening colonoscopies on a self-pay basis. The inclusion criteria were aged 18 years or older; absence of lower intestinal symptoms such as abdominal pain, recent bowel habit changes or lower gastrointestinal bleeding and absence of alarm symptoms, including anaemia and unexplained weight loss of >5% body weight over 6–12 months. The exclusion criteria included a history of CRC or colorectal surgery, inherited cancer syndromes, inflammatory bowel disease, coagulation disorders, inadequate bowel preparation as determined by the Boston Bowel Preparation Scale with a total score of <6 and a region score of <2,15 incomplete colonoscopies and loss of polyp tissue samples.

The sample size was estimated using DeLong’s method for comparing two correlated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Assuming a baseline area under the curve (AUC) of 0.60 for the APCS score, a clinically meaningful AUC improvement of 0.10 for the A-APCS score, a correlation coefficient of 0.70 between the two scores, an ACN prevalence of 9.1%, a two-sided alpha of 0.05, and 80% statistical power, the minimum required sample size was 561 participants, which was increased to 624 to account for potential non-evaluable cases.9 16

Overweight was defined as having a BMI equal to or greater than 23.0 kg/m2 according to the Asian population.17 The APCS scores were calculated for all participants based on age, sex, family history of CRC and smoking status (table 1).6 A family history of CRC was determined as having at least one first-degree relative affected at any age. The participants were stratified into three risk groups based on the sum of scores: average risk (AR), score 0–1; moderate risk (MR), score 2–3 and high risk (HR), score 4–7.

Colonoscopy procedure

Bowel preparation was carried out with 3 litres of polyethylene glycol-based material (Fortrans, Ipsen Pharma, Paris, France). Colonoscopy procedures were performed by experienced endoscopists via the Olympus Evis Exera III High Definition CV-190 (Olympus Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) either without sedation or under propofol sedation (PropofolLipuro 1%, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany). The sedation approach was selected by the patient after consultation with both the endoscopist and the anaesthesiologist. All participating endoscopists had adenoma detection rates ≥30% and had completed at least 3000 colonoscopies over the last 5 years. All participants signed a written informed consent form. This study was approved by the Board of Ethics in Biomedical Research of Pham Ngoc Thach University of Medicine (ID number: 663/TDHYKPNT-HDDD, signed on 4 April 2022). The reporting of this study is in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy (STARD) and Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (onlinesupplemental files 1 2).

Cecal intubation was confirmed by visualising the appendiceal orifice or cecal valve or by intubating the ileum. The withdrawal observation time was ≥6 min, which is in accordance with the current quality standards for colonoscopy.18 The morphology, location and size of all the detected polyps were documented. In individuals with multiple lesions, the most advanced lesion was used for data analysis. The polyps were classified macroscopically according to the Paris classification into three main categories: type 0-I (polypoid), which includes sessile (0-Is) and pedunculated (0-Ip) polyps; type 0-II (non-polypoid), which consists of slightly elevated (0-IIa), flat (0-IIb), and slightly depressed (0-IIc) lesions and type 0-III (excavated).19 The proximal colon included the cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure and transverse colon. The distal colon included the splenic flexure, descending colon, sigmoid colon and rectum. The size of the lesions was measured by comparing them to biopsy forceps, either open (7 mm width) or closed (3 mm width), or polypectomy snares of known diameter. The Narrow Band Imaging International Colorectal Endoscopic classification system was employed to categorise the colorectal lesions.20 All the detected polyps were removed and obtained for histopathological examination.

Histopathological analysis

All resected lesions were collected in separate containers and preserved in 10% formalin. Histological examinations were conducted by experienced gastrointestinal histopathologists at the Department of Histology-Embryology and Pathology, University Medical Center in Ho Chi Minh City. The polyps were classified pathologically as either colorectal neoplasia or non-neoplasia. Colorectal neoplasia was defined as adenoma (tubular adenoma, villous adenoma or tubulovillous adenoma), sessile serrated lesions or CRC.21 22 ACN was defined as having one of the following criteria: tubular adenoma ≥1 cm, any adenoma with villous features or high-grade dysplasia regardless of size, sessile serrated lesion ≥1 cm or with cytological dysplasia, traditional serrated adenoma of any size or CRC.23

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS (V.23; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and MedCalc Statistical Software version 19.6.1 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium). The normality of continuous variables was assessed via the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test. The data are reported as the means and SDs for variables that followed a normal distribution and were analysed via t tests. For variables that did not follow a normal distribution, the data were presented as medians along with upper and lower quartiles, and the Mann‒Whitney U test was applied for analysis. Pearson’s χ2 test was employed to examine the proportions of categorical data. All p values (two-sided) <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The AUC and concordance (c)-statistics were employed to assess the effectiveness of the APCS and A-APCS in predicting ACN, and the DeLong test was performed to compare the two scores.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 735 asymptomatic individuals who underwent screening colonoscopy participated in this study. Among these patients, 21 were excluded because of incomplete colonoscopy, unqualified bowel preparation, withdrawal time of less than 6 min or loss of polyp tissue samples. A total of 714 participants were subsequently included in the analysis (figure 1).

Flow chart of the study participants.

The median age of the participants was 51 years, with a range of 18–79 years. The female-to-male ratio was 1:1.46. The mean BMI was 23.5±3.0 kg/m², with 405 participants (56.7%) classified as overweight. The proportions of participants who had a history of smoking and a family history of CRC in their first-degree relatives were 14.7% and 8.4%, respectively. Colorectal neoplasia was identified in 187 (26.2%) participants. ACNs were found in 64 (9.0%) participants, including 2 (0.3%) patients with adenocarcinoma.

According to the APCS score, the prevalence of ACN in the three tiers (AR, MR and HR) was 20.3%, 51.6% and 28.1%, respectively. Meanwhile, using the A-APCS score, the prevalence of ACN in the AR, MR and HR groups was 23.4%, 45.4% and 31.2%, respectively. For predicting overall colorectal neoplasia, both the APCS and A-APCS scores indicated significant differences between the MR and HR tiers compared with the AR tier (p<0.001). For predicting ACN, the A-APCS score was more effective than the APCS score in the MR group compared with the AR group, with the individuals in the MR tier having 2.3-fold (95% CI 1.205 to 4.388) increased rates of ACN compared with those in the AR tier (table 5).

Diagnostic performance of the APCS and A-APCS scores for predicting overall colorectal neoplasia and advanced colorectal neoplasia

ROC analysis demonstrated that in predicting overall colorectal neoplasia, the c-statistic for the A-APCS score (0.665; 95% CI 0.619 to 0.710) was greater than that for the APCS score (0.650; 95% CI 0.605 to 0.695); however, this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.645). For the prediction of ACN, the c-statistics of the APCS and A-APCS scores were comparable, at 0.636 (95% CI 0.564 to 0.708) and 0.630 (95% CI 0.560 to 0.700), respectively, with no statistically significant difference (p=0.908) (figure 2).

There were two patients with invasive CRC in our study. The lesions were located in the rectum and the sigmoid colon. Both patients had APCS and A-APCS scores in the HR tier (online supplemental table 1).

Discussion

Our results revealed that the prevalence of ACN was 9.0%. Both the APCS and A-APCS scores indicated effective predictive ability for ACN in the HR group compared with the AR group. Nevertheless, the A-APCS score was more effective than the APCS score in predicting ACN in the MR group than in the AR group. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to validate the value of the APCS and A-APCS in stratifying the risk of ACN among asymptomatic Vietnamese individuals. Although the FIT is the most widely used method for identifying individuals at HR for ACN in the Asia-Pacific region, it has yet to be readily available in many developing countries, including Vietnam. Therefore, the third Asia-Pacific consensus recommendations on CRC screening suggest that in countries with increasing CRC incidence but without organised screening programmes, risk stratification systems using simple demographic risk factors can serve as effective alternatives to FIT for identifying high-risk individuals who could benefit from colonoscopy.24 Consequently, the APCS score was developed and has proven beneficial in stratifying ACN risk in the Asian population.6

In Vietnam, the utility of the APCS has been evaluated only in patients with IBS, and the results revealed that the APCS score was a useful non-invasive tool for stratifying ACN risk among Vietnamese IBS patients.14 In asymptomatic subjects, we also found that the APCS score exhibited relatively good discriminatory power in predicting the overall risk of colorectal neoplasia and ACN, with c-statistics of 0.650 (95% CI 0.605 to 0.695) and 0.636 (95% CI 0.564 to 0.708), respectively. Our findings were consistent with those of the initial study by Yeoh et al, who reported a c-statistic of 0.64±0.04 for predicting ACN.6

Overweight and obesity have been identified as environmental variables for ACN and CRC in addition to established CRC risk factors.13 25 Furthermore, BMI, as an additional parameter in the APCS score, has been demonstrated to increase the discriminatory ability of the scoring system.8,1026 27 In Asia, the mean BMI tends to be lower than that in Western countries because of the lower prevalence of obesity.28 Hence, BMI cut-off points also vary across studies, even in Asian countries. In our study, the A-APCS score was defined on the basis of the risk factors for the original APCS score incorporating BMI, with a BMI threshold of 23.5 kg/m2 and corresponding points according to a study by Zhang et al10 We chose this study for its comprehensive survey of a large cohort of asymptomatic participants in China, which closely resembles the population characteristics of our study.

Our results revealed that both the APCS and A-APCS scores effectively predicted ACN in the HR group compared with the AR group. However, the A-APCS score demonstrated superior predictive efficacy for ACN in the MR group than in the AR group. Nevertheless, concerning diagnostic performance, there was no significant difference in the area under the ROC curve between the APCS and A-APCS scores for predicting overall colorectal neoplasia or the ACN. Although we used the A-APCS score as described by Zhang et al, our results differed from their findings. Zhang’s study revealed that the A-APCS score had superior discriminatory power for ACN compared with the APCS score, with c-statistics of 0.68 for the derivation cohort and 0.80 for the validation cohort.10 Sung et al reported that adding BMI to the APCS score enhanced its discriminative ability over the original APCS score (c-statistics=0.65 vs 0.60).9 However, our findings showed that the A-APCS score may not provide improved ACN risk stratification compared with the APCS score in asymptomatic Vietnamese individuals. Consequently, the original APCS score should be favoured in clinical practice because it has fewer parameters, making it easier to apply and remember.

Previous studies concluded that combining the APCS score and FIT improved the identification of high-risk ACN individuals who should be prioritised for colonoscopy screening.7 29 FIT and these risk scores serve specific and complementary roles within screening pathways. FIT reflects occult bleeding at a single time point, whereas APCS and A-APCS scores provide a non-invasive assessment of baseline risk that may help inform screening decisions, especially in low- to intermediate-risk settings without organised national screening programmes. Despite the high sensitivity and specificity of the FIT, the results are influenced by the grade of CRC as well as the location and size of the lesions.30 Studies have demonstrated that the FIT is less effective in identifying colon tumours in the proximal colon. Furthermore, FIT is less sensitive for detecting adenomas less than 10 mm in diameter because of its lower likelihood of bleeding than larger adenomas or carcinomas.31 32 Our study revealed that the majority of ACNs were located in the distal colon (68.1%) and had a size of 10 mm or greater (82.4%). This suggests that the FIT test has the potential to be of great help in detecting these lesions. Consequently, an approach that combines APCSs and FITs is promising for future research in stratifying ACNs among the Vietnamese population.

Our study had some limitations. First, the study was conducted at a single medical centre, which may restrict the generalisability of the findings. Second, participants were more likely to be health-conscious and economically affluent, thereby increasing the probability of volunteer screening and potential selection bias. Third, the limited number of CRC cases may restrict CRC-specific analyses. However, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate ACN, which includes not only CRC but also advanced adenomas. Therefore, this limitation is unlikely to substantially affect the study’s main outcome. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to further validate these findings.

In conclusion, our study indicated that the A-APCS score may not provide improved ACN risk stratification compared with the APCS score in asymptomatic Vietnamese individuals seeking screening colonoscopies. Importantly, the diagnostic performance of both scoring systems was suboptimal. Therefore, in clinical practice, these scores should be considered primarily as tools for risk stratification to prioritise individuals for CRC screening, particularly in resource-limited settings, rather than as standalone screening methods.
