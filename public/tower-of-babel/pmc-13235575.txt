Clinical profile, intensive care needs, outcome, and predictors of mortality in children with severe sepsis: Secondary analysis of FerriPedS study
Authors: Kirti Pai, Karthi Nallasamy, Arun Bansal, Jayashree Muralidharan, Prateek Bhatia, Amit Rawat, Suresh Kumar Angurana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesSepsis remains a leading cause of mortality among children worldwide. This study aimed to describe clinical profile, intensive care needs, outcome, and predictors of mortality among children with severe sepsis.MethodsThis secondary analysis of FerriPedS study was conducted in a tertiary pediatric intensive care units (PICU) in North India involving 115 children (3 months-12 yr) with severe sepsis. Data were collected regarding clinical details, laboratory investigations, organ dysfunction, intensive care needs, and outcome. Univariate and multinomial logistic regression analyses were used to determine predictors of mortality among severity score, organ dysfunction, serum ferritin, and PICU needs.ResultsThe median (IQR) age was 3 (1-7) years, and common diagnoses were community acquired pneumonia (CAP) (n=45 39.1%), scrub typhus (n=16, 13.9%), and CNS infections (n=12,10.4%). Mortality was 27.8% (n=32). Non-survivors had significantly higher Pediatric Risk of Mortality-III (PRISM III) score, serum ferritin, and daily Pediatric logistic organ dysfunction-2 (PeLOD-2) score. Higher proportion of non-survivors had positive blood culture at admission, coagulopathy, hepatic dysfunction, shock, acute respiratory distress (ARDS), acute kidney injury (AKI), and multiple organ dysfunction score (MODS); and required invasive mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drugs, blood products, and RRT. On multinomial logistic regression analysis, PRISM-III, ARDS, and AKI were independent predictors of mortality.Interpretation and conclusionsIn children with severe sepsis, the common etiologies included CAP, scrub typhus, and CNS infections, and characterised by high mortality (27.8%). PRISM-III score, ARDS, and AKI were independent predictors of mortality.

Sepsis remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in children worldwide.1-5 The clinical presentation of severe sepsis in children is notably heterogeneous, influenced by underlying health status, pathogen profile, and host immune responses. Despite notable advances in paediatric critical care, predicting outcomes for children with severe sepsis remains challenging, primarily due to the complex and varied presentations of the disease.4 Conventional prognostic tools in the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) including clinical indices like the presence of shock, multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and laboratory measures such as platelet counts, renal function tests, biomarkers like ferritin and cytokines and composite scores [e.g., Paediatric risk of mortality (PRISM) or paediatric logistic organ dysfunction (PELOD)] scores offer valuable, but sometimes limited, prognostic information.6-9 These markers generally reflect the extent of systemic compromise but may lack specificity in distinguishing high-risk children at an early or pre-clinical stage of deterioration. Identifying children with risk factors for mortality will enhance early identification of children at the greatest risk for poor outcomes, institution of tailored interventions, resource allocation, and, ultimately, improved clinical outcome among children with severe sepsis.

There is paucity on data of predictors of mortality in children admitted to PICU with severe sepsis.10-13 Using data from FerriPedS study,8 we describe the demographic and clinical profile, laboratory features, intensive care needs, outcome, and predictors of mortality among children with severe sepsis.

Methods

This study was a secondary analysis of FerriPedS study which was conducted in PICU at department of Paediatrics, Post Graduate Institute of Medical Education and Research, Chandigarh, India. The original study was approved by the Institute Ethics Committee.8 One hundred fifteen critically ill children aged 3 months to 12 years admitted with severe sepsis as per International Paediatric Sepsis Consensus Criteria3 were included. Children with pre-existing chronic systemic illnesses like chronic liver/kidney disease, malignancies, autoimmune disorders, familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), or those on chronic immunosuppressive treatment were excluded.8

The primary outcome of the FerriPedS study was to determine the association between serum ferritin levels with mortality among critically ill children with severe sepsis; and secondary outcomes were estimation of serum ferritin levels, prevalence of hyperferritinemia, best serum ferritin cut-off to predict mortality, and correlation between serum ferritin and severity scores (PRISM 3, PeLOD 2 II and VIS).8 Whereas, the primary outcome of this secondary analysis of the FerriPedS study was to determine predictors of mortality and secondary outcomes were to describe clinical profile, intensive care needs, and mortality in critically ill children with severe sepsis.

The data were analysed for demographic details, clinical presentation, diagnoses, severity scores [PRISM III score at admission, daily PeLOD-2 score, and daily vasoactive inotropic score (VIS)], laboratory and microbiological investigations, organ dysfunction [coagulopathy, shock, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), hepatic dysfunction, and acute kidney injury (AKI)] (any time during admission), and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) (at admission, and new or progressive).14,15 Details about intensive care treatment including mechanical ventilation (invasive/non-invasive), vasoactive drugs, renal replacement therapy (RRT), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and corticosteroids), healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs), length of PICU stay, and final outcomes (survival or death) were noted.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2013 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA) and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test was used to check the distribution of the data. Categorical variables were expressed as number and percentages and continuous variables as median with interquartile range (IQR). Survivors and non-survivors were compared for the clinical profile, diagnosis, severity score, laboratory investigations, organ dysfunction, intensive care needs, and duration of PICU stay. Categorical variables between two groups were compared using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test; and continuous variables using Mann–Whitney U test. A P-value <0.05 was taken as significant. Multinomial logistic regression analysis was done to determine the independent predictors of mortality.

The sample size for the FerriPedS study was calculated based on 33-53% prevalence of hyperferritinemia (>500 ng/mL) involving children with severe sepsis.16,17 With the estimated prevalence of hyperferritinemia as 43%, precision of 10% and 95% level of confidence, the sample size calculated was 100. However, a total of 115 children were enrolled in the FerriPedS study.8

Results

Among 115 children with severe sepsis, median (IQR) age was 3 (1-7) years, 45.2% (n=52) were males, and median duration of illness before admission was 6 (3-10) days. Common diagnoses were community acquired pneumonia (CAP) (39.1%, n=45), scrub typhus (13.9%, n=16), CNS infections and multisystem viral illness (10.4%, n=12 each), dengue (9.6%, n=11), and gastrointestinal (GI) sepsis (6.1%, n=7). The median (IQR) PRISM-3 score was 7 (3-12) and the median PeLOD 2 score on day 1 of admission was 5 (3-8). Organ dysfunction noted were coagulopathy (54.8%, n=63), hepatic dysfunction (53.9%, n=62), shock (50.4%, n=58), encephalopathy (47.8%, n=55), acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) (43.5%, n=50), acute kidney injury (AKI) (33%, n=33). Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) at admission was noted in 76.5% (n=88) children. The rate of healthcare associated infections (HCAIs) was 11.3% (13/115). The lower rates could be due to the practices we follow strictly in our PICU to prevent HCAIs (Supplementary Table). Intensive care treatment included invasive mechanical ventilation in majority (76.5%, n=88), non-invasive ventilation (45%, n=52), vasoactive drugs (51.3%, n=59), blood product transfusion (27%), renal replacement therapy (RRT) (11.3%), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (12.3%), steroids (7%), and plasma exchange (1.7%). The median duration of PICU stay was 8 (5-12) days.

Due to small sample size (n=115), fewer outcome of interest (mortality, n=32), and several significant variables on univariate analysis, the full multinomial logistic regression analysis including all the significant variables on univariate analysis was not performed to avoid over-fitting. When putting the significant variables on univariate analysis into the multinomial logistic regression model, we found that PRISM-III scores, ARDS, and AKI were significant predictors of mortality. Therefore, considering 1 predictor variable for every 10 outcomes of interest (mortality), we put only PRISM-III scores, ARDS, and AKI into the multinomial logistic regression model. We noted that PRISM-III score [odds ratio (OR) (95% confidence interval, CI): 2.7 (1.2-4.8), P=0.042]; ARDS [OR (95% CI): 3.7 (1.2-11.4), P=0.004], and AKI [OR (95% CI): 9.4 (2.7-25.9), P=0.001, respectively) were independent predictors of mortality.

Discussion

In this secondary analysis involving 115 children with severe sepsis, the mortality rate was 27.8%, aligning with reported pediatric sepsis mortality rates in similar settings.13,18 We also noted that higher PRISM-III and PeLOD scores, organ dysfunction, and hyperferritinemia were associated with morality. In line with higher number of organ dysfunction, non-survivors had higher needs of intensive care interventions (mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drugs, blood/blood product transfusion, and RRT). PRISM-III scores, ARDS, and AKI were independent predictors of mortality.

In the literature, few studies evaluated the predictors of mortality in pediatric sepsis. Usluer et al18 in a retrospective study involving 219 children with sepsis and septic shock demonstrated that the mortality rate was 32.9% and VIS, PIM-II and PeLOD scores were predictors of mortality. Miura et al19 in an epidemiological study using sepsis surveillance method analysed 875 children with sepsis. The mortality rate was 7.2% and hospital mortality incrementally increased with number of organ dysfunction. In a study from India, Kaur et al13 enrolled 50 children with severe sepsis and septic shock and concluded that mortality among children with severe sepsis could not be predicted by any individual factor.

In index study, CAP was the leading diagnosis, consistent with global data identifying pneumonia as the primary sepsis source in children.20 A trend towards longer illness duration among non-survivors highlights the impact of delayed presentation on the severity of illness and ultimately outcome. This finding was confirmed by another study by Smith et al21 who enrolled 1,803 children with severe sepsis and found that delayed presentation to the hospital was an independent risk factor for mortality. Positive blood culture at admission was more frequently observed among non-survivors. Chen et al22 demonstrated that there is critical role of confirmed bacteremia in predicting adverse outcomes. We also noted that hyperferritinemia at admission to PICU was associated with mortality. These findings were consistent with the studies done by Garcia et al16 who first described the independent association between serum ferritin levels and mortality in 36 children with sepsis admitted to PICU. Similarly, other authors also demonstrated that higher serum ferritin levels predicted mortality in children with sepsis.23,24

We also noted that non-survivors had more organ dysfunction including coagulopathy, shock, ARDS, AKI, and hepatic dysfunction. These findings corroborate with findings from several other studies which studied association between sepsis and individual organ dysfunction. Tsantes et al25 described association between coagulopathy and mortality in sepsis. Yehya et al26 demonstrated that in children with sepsis and PARDS, increasing number of non-pulmonary organ failures is associated with a linear increase in mortality risk. Yan et al27 demonstrated that hepatic dysfunction after sepsis to be an independent risk factor for multiple organ dysfunction and sepsis-induced death.

We also demonstrated that non-survivors had higher intensive care needs (invasive mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drugs, RRT, and blood/blood products) going hand in hand with more organ dysfunction among them. A study from Ethiopia, involving 124 children admitted to PICU demonstrated that Glasgow coma scale <8 and mechanical ventilation requirement within first 24 hours were independently associated with mortality.28 Rusmawatiningtyas et al29 among 665 children with sepsis noted that fluid overload, mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drugs, and congenital anomalies were significant predictors of mortality predictors on multivariate analysis. A scoping review by Yuniar et al11 analysed 187 children with sepsis and demonstrated that shock, inotropic drugs, mechanical ventilation, PRISM-III and MODS were significantly associated with mortality out of which MODS, PRISM III score >15, and inotropic use emerged as independent predictors of mortality. In another study, Yuniar et al12 demonstrated that among 225 children with sepsis, PeLOD-2 score correlated with high mortality. Fitzgerald et al30 enrolled 493 children with severe sepsis admitted in PICU and demonstrated that severe AKI was independently associated with mortality and morbidity.

The strengths of this secondary analysis included well‐characterised, prospectively assembled cohort of children with severe sepsis spectrum of regional heterogenous diagnoses which enhanced the internal validity of identified mortality predictors. The limitations included single-centre study with limited external generalisability. Also, reliance on variables recorded only during the first seven days of PICU admission precluded assessment of late-emerging predictors or complications arising after that period. We have not considered MODS phenotyping in this secondary analysis, which may be undertaken in future research to have more information on this aspect. Also, there was lack of long-term follow up which prevented evaluation of longer-term mortality or morbidity outcomes (re-admission or chronic organ dysfunction).

To conclude, in Indian settings, severe sepsis is commonly caused by CAP, scrub typhus, and CNS infections and associated with high mortality highlighting the importance of early diagnosis and prompt treatment with antibiotics. Severity score (PRISM-III), ARDS, AKI were independent predictors of mortality highlighting the need of organ supportive care in PICU to improve outcome.

शोध-संदेश

यह अध्ययन गंभीर सेप्सिस से ग्रस्त बच्चों में उनके क्लिनिकल प्रोफाइल, गहन चिकित्सा (ICU) की आवश्यकता, परिणाम और मृत्यु के पूर्वानुमान कारकों का आकलन करने के लिए किया गया। अध्ययन से पता चला कि इन बच्चों में निमोनिया, स्क्रब टायफस, और दिमागी बुखार बीमारी के मुख्य कारण थे तथा मृत्यु दर लगभग 27.8% रही। जिन बच्चों की मृत्यु हुई, उनमें फेरिटिन स्तर अधिक पाया गया। यह बच्चे गंभीर रूप से बीमार थे एवं उनके शरीर के विभिन्न अंगो ने सुचारु रूप से कार्य करना बंद या कम कर दिया था। खासतौर पर फेफड़े या गुर्दे प्रभावित हुए। बीमारी की गंभीरता एवं फेफड़े या गुर्दे का नाकाम होने से मृत्यु का पूर्वानुमान किया जा सकता है। इनकी पहचान से समय पर उपचार और बेहतर प्रबंधन में मदद मिल सकती है।
