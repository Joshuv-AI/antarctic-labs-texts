Biosensor technologies in cancer: tools for early detection and prognostic monitoring
Authors: Mei Kei Fam, Nurul Izza Ismail, Afzal Izzaz Zahari, Nor Azlin Ghazali, Norfarazieda Hassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, necessitating advances in diagnostic technologies to improve early detection and patient outcomes. Traditional methods such as imaging and biopsy are effective and widely used clinically but are often limited by invasiveness, high cost, and lack of sensitivity for early-stage cancer. In response, biosensors have emerged as promising tools capable of detecting cancer-associated biomarkers including circulating tumor DNA, microRNAs, exosomes, and tumor-associated proteins. These devices are noninvasive, rapid, highly sensitive, and offer real-time monitoring capabilities. This review bridges engineering principles with clinical readiness, covering basic principles, material advances, and evidence supporting clinical use across electrochemical, optical, piezoelectric, and field-effect transistor (FET)-based biosensors. We highlight recent breakthroughs in nanomaterials, amplification strategies, device miniaturization, and wearable biosensor platforms. Despite their advances, current biosensors face challenges in terms of sensitivity, nonspecific binding and signal drift, standardization, manufacturing scalability, production costs, regulatory validation and real-world reproducibility, which restrict their widespread clinical application. This review also discusses the integration between biosensors, artificial intelligence, and liquid biopsy workflows that could pave the way for point-of-care testing and personalized cancer management.Keywords: Cancer, Biosensors, Electrochemical, Optical, Piezoelectric, FET

Background

Cancer has remained a major global health challenge for many years, ranking among the leading causes of death worldwide, with approximately 10 million mortalities reported in 2022 [1]. As a highly complex and heterogeneous disease, cancer development is largely influenced by diverse genetic mutations that vary between individuals. This biological variability prompted the emergence of personalized and precision medicine as central strategies in cancer. Early and accurate cancer detection plays a crucial role in improving treatment outcomes and prognosis, ultimately contributing to increased survival rates among patients [2].

Conventional diagnostic methods such as ultrasound, X-ray, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography (PET) are widely used in clinical settings [3]. However, these methods often present limitations such as low sensitivity for early-stage cancer detection, high background signal interference, lengthy procedures, high costs, and radiation exposure. Tissue biopsy remains the clinical gold standard for cancer diagnosis, although it is invasive and can cause patient discomfort and risks, such as infection and bleeding. Additionally, sampling from a single tumor site may fail to represent the full heterogeneity of the cancer, and the overall process is time-consuming because of complex sample preparation and analysis [4, 5]. Consequently, these challenges have driven the development of biosensors, which address many of these limitations by offering miniaturized, noninvasive or minimally invasive alternatives with high sensitivity that can provide real-time molecular information for early-stage cancer.

Biomarkers such as circulating tumor cells (CTCs), circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), exosomes and microRNAs (miRNAs) are increasingly recognized as potential candidates for cancer detection, prognosis, and therapeutic monitoring [6]. Distinct miRNA signatures including miR-17, miR-21 and miR-221 have been established as reliable diagnostic panels for early cancer detection across various cancer types such as breast, prostate, and cervical cancers [7]. These biomarkers can be identified in biological fluids such as blood, urine and saliva, which are easier to collect, enable repeated sampling, and minimize patient discomfort compared with tissue biopsies [8].

Biosensors consist of biorecognition elements such as antibody, aptamer and nucleic acid, coupled to transducer that convert biological interactions into measurable signals. This technology has gained increasing attention as an innovative diagnostic approach, enabling rapid and often label-free detection of these cancer-associated biomarkers [9]. Among biosensors, electrochemical, optical, piezoelectric, and field-effect transistor (FET)-based biosensors are the most explored due to high analytical sensitivity, specificity, scalability, and portability. Electrochemical biosensors offer favorable miniaturization with low detection limit; optical biosensors provide high selectivity and multiplexing capability; piezoelectric biosensors detect mass changes directly with low-cost detection; and FET-based biosensors offer label-free, real-time electrical signals with high charge sensitivity, making them highly attractive for detecting exosomes in liquid biopsy [10–13].

Understanding the operational principles and their differences among these biosensors is essential for their roles in cancer diagnostics. However, a comprehensive review connects biosensor engineering perspective with clinical readiness, regulatory considerations, and future use in oncology remains limited. This review aims to provide an overview of potential biosensor platforms used in cancer detection, emphasizing their basic principles, advantages, emerging applications in early diagnosis and disease monitoring, as well as translational challenges and future trends in advancing biosensor technologies toward routine parts of personalized cancer care. This review also evaluates the translational gap between engineering innovations and clinical oncology requirements. We bridge the discussion on nanomaterial advancements with practical considerations for point-of-care workflows, including regulatory validation, AI-assisted analysis, and therapeutic monitoring.

Classification of biosensors

The main components of a biosensor include biorecognition elements, which selectively interact with target analytes such as nucleic acids, CTCs, exosomes, antibodies, and aptamers. The transducer converts this biological interaction between the bioreceptor and target analytes into a measurable signal, which is typically electrical, optical, or mechanical in nature. The signal processor then amplifies and processes the signal for display or data analysis. Biosensors can be classified into electrochemical, optical, piezoelectric, and field-effect transistor (FET)-based biosensors, as shown in Fig. 1. Each type has its own unique advantages in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and suitability for different types of cancer. Recent advances in materials science, nanotechnology, and microfabrication have significantly enhanced the performance and feasibility of biosensors, enabling rapid, real-time diagnostics and point-of-care monitoring of cancer patient progression.

Electrochemical biosensors

Electrochemical biosensors are widely used as cancer diagnostic instruments because of their high sensitivity, rapid response, and ease of miniaturization. The principle of this biosensor relies on electrochemical interactions between the electrode and the target analyte in biological samples, generating measurable signals such as current, voltage, or impedance [14, 15]. An electrochemical biosensor is typically composed of 3 electrodes: a working electrode (WE) where biorecognition occurs, reference electrode (RE) to provide a stable potential, and counter electrode (CE) to complete the circuit [16].

Electrodes for electrochemical biosensors are commonly made from materials such as carbon, graphite, glassy carbon, gold, and platinum. The choice of electrode material is important in determining biomolecule immobilization efficiency, signal stability, and sensitivity in cancer biosensing applications. For example, glassy carbon is the most commonly used carbon material because of its high thermal stability, high conductivity and low electrical resistance [17]. There are several types of electrochemical detection methods for these biosensors, including amperometry, voltammetry, impedance spectroscopy, potentiometry, and conductometry, as shown in Table 1 and Fig. 2 [15, 18–20].

Application of electrochemical biosensors in cancer treatment

Electrochemical biosensors have rapidly evolved from early concept devices to clinically relevant diagnostic tools due to their high sensitivity, small sample volume requirements, and compatibility with portable point-of-care testing. To achieve the ultrasensitivity required for detecting low concentration of cancer biomarkers at early stage, recent advances focus on improving performance of electrode interfaces with advanced nanomaterials such as metal nanoparticles, graphene derivatives, carbon nanotubes, and metal-organic frameworks (MOFs). These materials are employed to enhance electron transfer efficiency and increase surface binding affinity. For instance, a label-free electrochemical immunosensor using gold nanoparticle/polyethyleneimine/reduced graphene oxide nanocomposites was developed for ultrasensitive detection of cancer biomarkers, matrix metalloproteinase-1 (MMP-1) [21]. Similarly, an electrochemical biosensor was developed by Dezhakam et al. (2024) by integrating metal-organic frameworks/covalent organic frameworks (MOF/COF) nanocomposites with gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) for detection of CA15-3 in human serum [22]. These nanomaterials are mainly used due to their excellent electrical conductivity, thermal and catalytic properties, large surface area as well as good stability.

However, a critical challenge in using these sensors in real biological samples is biofouling, therefore recent material innovations also focus heavily on improving sensors’ performance in complex biological samples. Addressing this, an antifouling peptide hydrogel based electrochemical biosensor was developed for HER2, breast cancer biomarker in human serum. The use of biocompatible peptide hydrogels and conducting polymers poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) (PEDOT) enables effective alleviation of nonspecific adsorption, while maintaining high sensitivity and selectivity for HER2, and achieving clinically acceptable accuracy in complex biological samples [23].

Complementary to these protein applications, advanced biosensing platforms have been extensively developed particularly for nucleic acid biomarkers like miRNAs. Sabahi et al. (2020) fabricated a biosensor using a fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) electrode modified with single-wall carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) and dendritic gold nanostructures to detect miR-21 in human serum. This biosensor showed high sensitivity and a low detection limit, highlighting its potential for prostate cancer diagnosis [24]. Similarly, locked nucleic acid (LNA)-assisted strand displacement systems combined with ratiometric electrochemical impedance spectroscopy/differential pulse voltammetry (EIS/DPV) for detection of exosomal miR-21. This dual-signal method improves reliability and reduces false positives [25].

These innovations in materials, design and antifouling strategies collectively underscore the advantages of electrochemical biosensors offer in a clinical context. Their advantages include high sensitivity and specificity to detect cancer biomarkers at very low concentrations, the ability to provide rapid results for timely clinical decisions and interventions, compatibility with noninvasive samples, and broad use across cancer types for personalized treatment approaches. Crucially, their small and portable designs make them ideal for point-of-care testing [26, 27].

Overall, electrochemical biosensors show strong promises for early cancer detection and monitoring. They are now transitioning toward miniaturized designs with microfluidics and wireless readouts, bringing diagnostics closer to decentralized and point-of-care use. However, challenges like standardized electrode fabrication, antifouling in complex blood samples, and multiplex detection to cover diverse cancer biomarkers need to be addressed for clinical adoption.

Optical biosensors

Optical biosensors are light-based analytical devices that are used to detect specific biological analytes. It involves the conversion of biological interactions between the biorecognition element and the target analyte into a measurable optical signal. The signal is often observed through changes in properties such as absorbance, fluorescence, luminescence, scattering, or the refractive index. The intensity or wavelength shift of the optical signal provides quantitative or qualitative information about the target analyte [28]. Optical biosensors can be broadly classified into several types, as shown in Fig. 3.

Schematic of optical biosensors. Biorecognition elements immobilized on the sensor interface capture target analytes, generating measurable signals through the transducer and signal processor. Different detection strategies include colorimetric, fluorescence, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), luminescence and surface-enhanced Raman scattering (sers) methods. Some optical biosensors such as SERS-based biosensors operate without biorecognition elements, relying on nanoparticle aggregation upon analyte interaction

Colorimetric biosensors

A device that detects the presence of the target analyte by observing a visible color change owing to the biorecognition interaction. They are simple, visually detectable and portable for quantitative measurement, which is ideal for clinical diagnostics [29–31]. Gold nanoparticles are often used for colorimetric sensing because of their strong optical properties and aggregation-induced color changes. Hasan et al. (2022) developed a rapid and colorimetric nanobiosensor by employing gold nanoparticles to detect platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), a circulating biomarker that is upregulated in plasma in prevalent ovarian cancer [32].

Fluorescence-based biosensors

An analytical device that detects and quantifies target analytes by measuring changes in fluorescence intensity. It combines a biorecognition element with a fluorescent indicator such as dyes, fluorescent proteins, or quantum dots [33]. The binding of the analyte to the recognition element induces a conformational change that alters the fluorophore’s optical properties, resulting in the emission of fluorescent light [34]. The emitted light is concentrated through a condensing lens, enters a waveguide at an angle enabling total internal reflection, and is ultimately detected by a photodetector, which can further determine the presence and concentration of the target analyte. The main approaches include fluorescence turn-on/off, enhancement, and Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) [30, 35]. These methods are widely used because of their high sensitivity and specificity, particularly in detecting low-abundance cancer biomarkers such as miRNAs or ctDNA.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) biosensors

This analytical device can measure changes in the refractive index at a metal‒dielectric interface, typically gold, when target analytes bind to ligands that are immobilized on the sensor surface [28]. The key components of an SPR biosensor include a gold-coated sensor chip that enables ligand (the biorecognition elements) immobilization, a microfluidic system to deliver analytes over the sensor chip surface, and an optical detector to measure intensity shifts. The resonance angle refers to the angle at which the light source passes through a prism and reflects off the sensor chip at a specific incident angle [36]. At this angle, light excites surfaces and makes them sensitive to any changes in their surrounding environment. As analytes bind to ligands, the local refractive index changes, altering the resonance angle and resulting in a shift in the reflected light intensity. This change is recorded by a sensorgram, providing valuable information on analyte concentration, binding kinetics, affinity, and specificity. The ability of SPR to analyze turbid, colored, or opaque samples without labeling makes it widely applicable in cancer biomarker detection [31].

Luminescence-based biosensors

An analytical device that detects and quantifies target analyte by measuring light emitted from chemical or biological reactions. Unlike fluorescence-based biosensors, which require external light sources to excite a fluorophore, luminescence-based sensors generate their own light, making them suitable for ultrasensitive detection in low-background environments. Luminescence occurs when a substance emits light after absorbing energy, and this can arise through various mechanisms, such as chemiluminescence (light from chemical reactions), bioluminescence (light produced by living organisms), electrogenerated chemiluminescence, and thermochemiluminescence [37, 38]. In these biosensors, the binding of biorecognition elements with the target analyte initiates a luminescent reaction and produces light. The intensity of the emitted light is then measured by a luminometer, and the signal is directly proportional to the analyte concentration. Owing to their high sensitivity, specificity, and lack of background interference, luminescence-based biosensors are widely used in clinical diagnostics for cancer, as they can identify biomarkers at very low concentrations.

Surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) biosensors

There are two types of light scattering, elastic or Rayleigh scattering, which refers to scattered light that retains the same frequency as the incident light, and inelastic or Raman scattering, which is a small fraction that undergoes a frequency shift due to interactions with molecular vibrations. This Raman effect provides valuable insights into the molecular composition and structural dynamics of a substance [39]. Despite its specificity, Raman scattering is typically weak and difficult to detect [35]. To overcome this, SERS has been developed, which significantly amplifies the Raman signal. SERS involves placing the analyte on a nanostructured metallic surface (gold or silver), where roughness or nanoscale features enhance local electromagnetic fields through the excitation of localized surface plasmons (LSPs) [40]. These “hot spots” enhanced the electric field near the surface, significantly increasing the Raman signal. The electromagnetic mechanism (EM) affects molecules close to the surface by increasing their vibrational response, resulting in a signal enhancement of up to a million-fold. The chemical mechanism (CM) involves charge transfer between the metal and molecules directly attached to the surface, resulting in a signal enhancement of up to a hundred-fold. Since the intensity of the electric field decreases rapidly with distance, SERS is extremely sensitive, enabling the detection of single molecules at ultralow concentrations [40, 41]. This makes SERS a powerful tool for detecting cancer biomarkers at early stages.

Application of optical biosensors in cancer

Recent advances in optical biosensors have significantly improved cancer detection and prognosis through improved sensitivity, specificity, and rapid analysis. Significant attention has been focused on advanced nanomaterials. Alongside well-known materials like gold nanoparticles, layered double hydroxides (LDHs) have also emerged as a versatile platform for constructing optical biosensors, due to their unique layered structure and large surface area [42, 43].

Colorimetric biosensors, especially gold-nanoparticle-based systems, enable visible biomarker detection without complex instruments. For example, aptamer-functionalized gold nanostars can detect mucin 1 (MUC1)-positive breast and lung cancer cells at single-cell sensitivity, showing promise for point-of-care screening. However, these approaches may face challenges with sample interference and require standardized recognition chemistries to ensure consistency [44]. Similarly, Li et al. (2023) developed a dye-enhanced colorimetric biosensors using rhodamine derivatives and gold nanobipyramids to visually distinguish alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels for prostate cancer bone metastasis diagnosis [45].

Fluorescence and FRET-based biosensors have demonstrated outstanding analytical performance for detecting nucleic acids and ovarian cancer markers like human epididymis protein 4 (HE4). Smartphone-assisted fluorescence readouts bridge the gap between high-performance laboratory instruments and portable devices for real-time diagnostics. Severi et al. (2020) developed a smartphone-compatible FRET-based nanoprobe for ultrasensitive detection of cancer-related nucleic acids, surviving through a visible color change that can be quantified via a smartphone camera [46]. Another ultrasensitive all-graphene quantum dot (GQD) FRET-based biosensor was developed by Bharathi et al. (2021) for the detection of HE4 with high sensitivity and specificity [47].

SPR and LSPR biosensors are currently among the most clinically promising optical platforms. They enable label-free detection of biomarkers like exosomal epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) in lung cancer with accuracy comparable to ELISA, demonstrating readiness for clinical workflows. Liu et al. (2018) developed a compact, intensity-modulated SPR biosensor capable of real-time exosome protein detection without complex nanostructures [48]. Mahani et al. (2021) reported an ultrasensitive, label-free LSPR biosensor using antibody-conjugated gold nanoparticles to detect PSA at low serum concentrations [49]. Furthermore, Abdelghaffar et al. (2023) presented a novel photonic crystal fiber (PCF) SPR biosensor with zirconium nitride (ZrN) coating, providing sensitive, noninvasive detection across various cancers [50].

A highly specific electrochemiluminescence resonance energy transfer (ECL-RET) biosensor was developed by Yang et al. (2021) using graphitic carbon nitride quantum dots. This biosensor detects DNA molecules that code for EGFR L858R in blood plasma, enabling noninvasive, cost-effective liquid biopsy for non-small cell lung cancer [51]. Recently, Ye et al. (2024) developed a dual-function ECL-based biosensor that uses enzyme-free DNA amplification circuits to detect miR-21 for breast cancer diagnosis and miR-105 for identifying metastatic breast cancer. It enables sensitive, specific, and clinically validated detection in both cellular and serum samples [52].

SERS-based biosensors combined with microfluidics offer ultrasensitive detection of cancer exosomes and mRNA biomarkers. Jia et al. (2024) developed a SERS-based microfluidic chip integrated with a cactus-like array substrate (CAS) for ultrasensitive detection of prostate cancer exosomes, with detection limit as low as 1 particle/μL [53]. Similarly, Li et al. (2024) developed a plasmonics-enhanced spikey nanorattle (SpNR)-based SERS biosensor for the detection of Keratin 14 (KRT14), a mRNA biomarker for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [54].

Optical biosensors offer distinct advantages including label-free and naked eye detection, as in colorimetric biosensors, integration with smartphones for image capture and compatibility with minimally processed samples. Overall, optical biosensors are advancing toward multiplexed detection and miniaturized readout devices. However, challenges remain in bulk manufacturing, maintaining optical setup stability, and standardizing calibration for clinical use. Their ability to deliver real-time results at the point of care holds great promise for improving cancer survival rates and optimizing clinical decision-making.

Piezoelectric/Acoustic biosensors

Piezoelectric biosensors, also known as acoustic biosensors, are a type of mass-based biosensor that operates based on the principle of piezoelectricity. This biosensor commonly uses piezoelectric materials such as quartz to generate electrical signals when subjected to mechanical stress or pressure. Quartz is preferred because of its abundance, low cost, mechanical strength, and chemical stability. This quartz serves as an oscillator, vibrating with a specific fundamental frequency and creating acoustic waves. The behavior of acoustic waves depends on how the quartz crystal is cut, typically as AT or ST cuts, which determines the direction of wave propagation. On this basis, acoustic sensors are classified into two types: surface acoustic wave (SAW) sensors, where waves travel along the surface, and bulk acoustic wave (BAW) sensors, where waves propagate into the interior of a crystal [55].

The working principle is based on changes in the mass of the quartz crystal. When an analyte interacts with biorecognition elements immobilized on a piezoelectric crystal surface, it leads to an increase in the mass of the crystal. These mass changes cause a shift in the resonance frequency of the crystal’s vibrations. The difference in frequency between the coated sensor crystal and a reference crystal is measured, and this shift is directly proportional to the concentration of the target analyte [56].

The most common type of piezoelectric biosensor is the quartz crystal microbalance (QCM), which is a subtype of BAW sensor. QCMs consist of quartz plates with metallic electrodes and operate in thickness shear mode (TSM) or bulk acoustic mode. They are widely used because of their sensitivity in detecting minor changes in mass [57]. QCMs are particularly effective for quantifying cancer biomarkers in blood and serum samples, and their ability to monitor antibody‒antigen interactions in real time makes them widely used in cancer diagnostics [Fig. 4].

Application of piezoelectric/acoustic biosensors in cancer

Recent advances in piezoelectric biosensors for cancer detection have demonstrated significant progress in both fabrication techniques and analytical performance, particularly using QCM and SAW. These sensors provide real-time, label-free detection with high sensitivity to surface mass changes, making them valuable for detecting cancer biomarkers. For example, Kim et al. (2025) developed a QCM immunosensor for α-fetoprotein (AFP) detection using a WO3-mediated photocatalytic silver deposition to amplify the mass response. This enables ultralow detection limits suitable for early-stage liver cancer diagnosis [58]. Furthermore, specialized systems using lead zirconate titanate (PZT) ceramic resonators feature a dual-resonator design that helps to reduce temperature and environmental drift, improving measurement stability compared to conventional QCM sensors [59, 60].

The functionality and performance of piezoelectric biosensors are continually enhanced through advanced material integration. Nanomaterials such as gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) or Titanium dioxide (TiO2) are frequently incorporated to amplify the signal via mass enhancement or catalytic silver deposition [61, 62]. Recent developments also emphasize microfluidic integration, allowing smaller sample volumes, faster response times and multiplexed analysis within compact lab-on-chip platforms [63]. For instance, integration of microfluidic exosome enrichment with SAW sensors further enhances sensitivity, enabling detection of exosomal miRNAs like miR-106b with performance comparable to RT-qPCR [64, 65].

These examples collectively demonstrate that piezoelectric biosensors provide label-free, rapid and highly sensitive detection of low-concentration biomarkers. Their potential for miniaturization makes them strong candidates for point of care monitoring. However, significant challenges remain including fragility of crystal, temperature-dependent signal drift, and multiplexing [66]. Future advances in polymer-coated resonators, surface antifouling strategies, and hybrid acoustic-microfluidic chips are expected to address issues and promote clinical adoption [65, 67].

Field-effect transistor (FET)-based biosensors

Field-effect transistor (FET)-based biosensors are semiconductor devices with 3 terminals (drain, source, and gate) that control the flow of current between two terminals (source and drain) on the basis of the voltage applied to the gate terminal. The applied gate voltage creates an electric field, which modulates the conductivity of the channel [68]. When target analytes interact with biorecognition elements, changes in the surface potential are induced. This shift affects the electrical conductance of the semiconductor channel, which can be detected as a variation in the current or threshold voltage [Fig. 5]. The changes in current or voltage are directly proportional to the concentration of the target analytes. This mechanism enables real-time and label-free detection based on their interaction with selective biological elements [69, 70].

Schematic of FET-based biosensors. The binding of target analytes with biorecognition elements immobilized on the semiconductor layer induces changes in the surface potential. This shift affects electrical conductance, which can be detected as a variation in current or threshold voltage, providing quantitative information on target analytes

To optimize and improve the performance, the fabrication of FET-based biosensors involves the selection and integration of substrate materials, terminal electrode fabrication, and dielectric layer and gate configurations. Silicon/silicon dioxide (Si/SiO₂) wafers are commonly used as substrates for FET-based biosensors because of their compatibility with most operating conditions [71]. Substrates may be coated with dielectric materials such as aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) or hafnium oxide (HfO₂) to tune the operating voltage and improve environmental stability [72]. Metals such as gold (Au) are widely used as conductive electrode pads for source and drain terminals because of their high conductivity. Chromium (Cr) and titanium (Ti) are also often used to enhance adhesion [73].

The sensing performance of an FET-based biosensor is largely determined by its configuration, which can be categorized as back-gate, top-gate, or solution-gate. In the back-gate configuration, a silicon (Si) substrate serves as the gate with a silicon dioxide (SiO₂) dielectric layer, offering a large sensing area. The top-gate configuration involves placing the sensing element on top of the device, enabling relatively easy fabrication and strong sensing performance. The solution-gated configuration mimics an electrochemical cell and uses reference electrodes such as silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) or platinum immersed in an electrolyte solution. This setup enables sensitive detection of physiological analytes at low operating voltages, leveraging materials such as graphene or reduced graphene oxide (rGO) as the active channel. This configuration is particularly suitable for biosensing applications in liquid environments [70].

Application of FET-based biosensors in cancer

FET-based biosensors are revolutionizing cancer diagnostics due to their capability for label-free, real-time electronic detection of very low biomarker concentrations, often down to femtomolar levels. Unlike electrochemical and optical sensors, FETs directly convert biomolecular interactions into electrical signals, offering exceptional sensitivity. Their high sensitivity and selectivity are achieved through precise functionalization tailored to target specific biomarkers.

The choice of semiconductor material is critical, Graphene-based FETs (GFETs) are excellent in detecting targets like cytokines and miRNAs due to their high carrier mobility and large surface area. For example, Hao et al. (2019) developed a portable, smartphone-sized GFET sensor for interleukin-6 (IL-6) in saliva, supporting noninvasive point-of-care cancer diagnostics [74]. Similarly, Dong et al. (2020) developed an electronic biosensor based on a carbon dot-modified liquid-exfoliated graphene FET (CDs-LEG-FET) to detect hypermethylation of the SEPT9 gene, a biomarker for colorectal cancer, demonstrating broad methylation profile and diagnostic potential [75].

Beyond graphene, emerging 2D semiconductor materials such as indium selenide (InSe), which offer enhanced stability in aqueous environments and reduced electrical noise. This has been demonstrated in the ultrasensitive detection of CA125 within clinically relevant timeframes, especially when integrated with microfluidic channels [76]. Organic FET (OFET) chips have been devised for multiplexed detection, successfully and simultaneously measuring multiple cancer biomarkers, including AFP and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), showing promise for early liver cancer diagnosis [77]. Carbon nanotube FETs (CNT-FETs) have shown high sensitivity in measuring exosomal miR-21 in plasma, validating their role in liquid biopsy applications. For instance, Li et al. (2021) designed a label-free CNT FET biosensor with a floating gate and ultrathin yttrium oxide (Y₂O₃) layer to sensitively detect exosomal miR-21, distinguishing patients from healthy controls [78].

These studies highlight the key advantages of FET-based biosensors including exceptional sensitivity, label-free and real-time response with high potential for miniaturization. Despite strong analytical performance, most FET biosensors remain in prototype stage. Key translational challenges include Debye-length effects in physiological fluids, surface fouling, scalable semiconductor fabrication, and regulatory validation requirements. Integration with complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) technology and on-chip electronics is expected to accelerate clinical adoption [79–81].

Table 2 highlights the distinct performance profiles of these technologies. Electrochemical and FET-based biosensors generally offer the superior LOD required for early-stage liquid biopsy in the fM-aM range, due to their high sensitivity to surface charge changes. In contrast, optical biosensors, particularly colorimetric and fluorescence platforms, stand out for ease of use and clear visualization, making them ideal for rapid qualitative screening. While piezoelectric sensors provide a robust label-free option for larger proteins, they often face challenges with miniaturization compared to the scalability of FET and electrochemical arrays. Ultimately, the choice of transducer depends on the clinical goal, whether to prioritize extreme sensitivity for early detection or simplicity for point-of-care testing.

Current challenges and limitations

Despite significant advances in biosensor technologies for cancer detection and prognosis, several challenges and limitations restrict their implementation in clinical settings. Among these, sensitivity and specificity remain the most important parameters. Cancer biomarkers often present at low concentrations, especially in the early stage, making it difficult for biosensors to consistently achieve both high sensitivity and specificity. In biological samples such as blood and serum, the presence of other biomolecules can interfere with biosensor performance. Nonspecific binding or cross-reactivity can result in false positives or negatives, reducing the accuracy and reliability of the test results [82, 83]. To address this, ongoing efforts focus on antifouling strategies on biosensing surface are needed to minimize nonspecific adsorption of biomolecules to improve biosensor performance [84]. Advanced biorecognition probes such as aptamer-nanomaterial hybrids, together with microfluidic filtration and selective exosome enrichment techniques significantly improve signal accuracy and reliability in complex biological samples [85, 86]. Addressing the issue of low biomarker levels in non-invasive samples often needs new enrichment methods. Using paper-based preconcentration or multiplexed lateral flow assays (LFAs) shows promise for point-of-care use. However, these approaches still need development to reach the reliability of laboratory techniques.

The lack of standardization and reproducibility among biosensors has become another major limitation. For instance, the wide variety of fabrication strategies, from gold nanoparticle modified electrodes in electrochemical setups to quantum dot-based FRET probes in optical systems, creates inconsistency in performance reporting. Variations in surface chemistry, probe density, and transducer architecture make it challenging to establish standardized benchmarks across laboratories. Moreover, many studies have shown promising results in controlled laboratory settings but show variability across batches or when tested using clinical samples. It is difficult to assess the true diagnostic value without extensive testing in real-world clinical settings and across diverse populations [82, 87]. To overcome this, standardized fabrication workflows, scalable manufacturing techniques, and rigorous inter-laboratory validation are needed to ensure consistent and reliable performance at industrial scale [88].

Additionally, regulatory and clinical adoption require biosensors to meet strict standards set by Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA), including accuracy, stability, biosafety, lot-to-lot consistency and clinical validation in diverse patient cohorts [89, 90]. Although biosensors are often described as cost-effective tools, high production and manufacturing costs are required for advanced materials such as nanoparticles, nanowires, or microfluidic components and for microfabrication of biosensors [82, 91]. However, it is important to distinguish high initial development costs and the potential for future cost reductions through scalable manufacturing techniques such as CMOS-compatible semiconductor processing, printed electrode fabrication and roll-to-roll nanomaterial integration [92–94]. These approaches promise economies of scale that can lower production costs significantly over time.

Overall, while biosensors are promising candidates in cancer, especially in terms of early detection and prognosis, progress toward clinical translation will require coordinated advances in surface engineering, manufacturing scalability, clinical trial validation and regulatory alignment alongside deeper understanding of cancer biomarker biology (Fig. 6).

Future perspectives

Although biosensors largely remain in the research and development phase and are still somewhat distant from widespread clinical adoption for cancer detection and prognosis, the future direction of biosensor technology in cancer treatment offers exciting opportunities. As advancements continue, several promising directions, which could significantly enhance the role of biosensors in cancer, are emerging.

For sensitivity and specificity, amplification techniques can be considered to increase the detection limits and minimize false results. For example, nanostructured sensing interfaces, hybrid recognition systems combining aptamers, antibodies, and nucleic acid probes are likely to detect even small amounts of target biomarkers with high accuracy, improving their diagnostic value.

Unlike conventional biosensors that detect single biomarkers, multiplexed biosensors can analyze multiple biomarkers simultaneously in a single assay. This approach is particularly valuable for tackling cancer heterogeneity, where different subtypes may present with distinct biomarker profiles. By capturing broader molecular information, multiplexed biosensors and biomarker panels enable accurate classification of cancer types and support personalized treatment decisions. Although modern biosensors are fully capable of multiplexing, clinical translation remains challenging because each biomarker in a multiplex panel requires independent regulatory validation, and most multianalyte platforms currently remain at low-to-mid technology readiness levels (TRL) [95]. Bridging the gap between this technical capability and regulatory approval is a critical step for integrating multiplexed biosensors into routine oncology.

AI integration is transitioning from theory to practice. Deep learning algorithms are currently being employed to automate the classification of test lines in lateral flow assays, while machine learning models like support vector machines (SVMs), achieve over 90% accuracy in classifying disease states from complex electrochemical signals [96, 97]. These examples show AI becoming an active component of biosensor design, essential for reducing false positives in complex clinical samples. These capabilities will support development of smart, clinically assisted tools and personalized monitoring platforms.

The rise of wearable, portable, and point-of-care biosensors will promote real-time biomarker monitoring and remote patient management, important for immunotherapy and post-treatment surveillance. This move toward point of care testing is tightly linked to the growing field of liquid biopsy, where biosensors are being designed to detect biomarkers directly from blood samples, offering a noninvasive alternative to tissue biopsies. Manufacturing methods such as 3D-printed electrodes and microfluidic chips, along with smartphone-based readers are transforming portability and accessibility of biosensors.

To enable widespread adoption, scalable manufacturing methods like CMOS-compatible processes, printed electrode, microfluidic integration, and modular sensor cartridges are expected to reduce costs and enhance reproducibility, alongside standardized validation frameworks and early regulatory engagement. Large-scale studies involving diverse patient populations are essential to ensure that biosensors can accommodate patient variability. The field is poised for transformative breakthroughs that will enhance early detection, monitor therapeutic outcomes, and ultimately improve cancer patient survival and quality of life.

Beyond initial diagnosis, biosensors are also being applied to monitor therapeutic response. Liquid biopsy biosensors are being adapted to track ctDNA mutations and protein markers to gauge therapy efficacy in real-time. For example, recent studies have utilized SPR to quantify exosomal PD-L1 and EGFR, offering insights into immune checkpoint blockade response [98]. Similarly, electrochemical aptasensors are being explored to track drug serum levels and metabolic markers, allowing clinicians to assess tumor burden and potential drug resistance earlier than traditional imaging methods.

Conclusion

Biosensor technologies have emerged as promising alternatives for early cancer detection and cancer monitoring. Their unique ability to deliver noninvasive, rapid, and highly sensitive detection of cancer biomarkers positions them as key components in precision oncology. Advancements in nanomaterials, microfluidics, biorecognition engineering, and signal transduction strategies have enabled biosensors to detect ultralow concentrations of biomarkers from small sample volumes. Despite this progress, several challenges must be overcome before these biosensor technologies can be used as routine tools in clinical settings, including enhancing sensitivity and specificity, minimizing interference from complex biological samples, establishing standardized fabrication and compliance with regulatory and clinical validation requirements.

Looking ahead, the integration of biosensors with multiplexed liquid biopsy systems, wearable and portable health technologies, and AI-driven data analytics is expected to accelerate their clinical adoption. Key milestones for the coming decade include demonstration of reproducible analytical performance in multicenter clinical studies, establishment of regulatory-aligned validation frameworks, and embedding biosensors into routine oncology workflows. As ongoing efforts to overcome current technical and clinical translation challenges, biosensors are well positioned to become a cornerstone of future cancer diagnostics and prognosis, enabling earlier and more personalized treatment decisions.
