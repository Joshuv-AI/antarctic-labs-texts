Long-term cardiopulmonary function in children treated for empyema: comparison of chest drain with fibrinolytics and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery
Authors: Yoshua Selvadurai, Brendan Kennedy, Jamie Mcbride, Penelope Field, Bahaven Jeyaratnam, Jonathan Karpelowsky, Paul Robinson, Dominic A Fitzgerald, Melinda Solomon, Andrew Bush, Adam Jaffe, Hiran Selvadurai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplicated pneumonia in children can result in an empyema. The management options include chest drain insertion with fibrinolytic therapy (CDF) and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) with decortication. No studies have compared the long-term cardiopulmonary impact of treatment interventions using sensitive static and dynamic assessments of lung function.AimTo compare long-term cardiopulmonary function outcomes in children with empyema treated with CDF with those who underwent VATS using highly sensitive static and dynamic lung function measures.MethodsChildren aged >8 years at the time of testing who were previously hospitalised with empyema and treated with either CDF or VATS were prospectively recruited. At one hospital, children were only treated by VATS, whereas CDF was used exclusively at the other site. As both sites belonged to the same network, all other aspects of treatment protocols were identical, and clinicians worked across both sites. Healthy controls were assessed as a comparison. Lung function testing included spirometry, lung volumes, multiple-breath washout (lung clearance index), oscillometry and cardiopulmonary exercise testing, all performed according to international standards.Results51 children treated with CDF, 28 treated with VATS and 24 healthy controls participated in this study. Spirometry and lung clearance index were normal across all groups. However, oscillometry revealed significantly lower reactance in both treatment groups compared with controls (respiratory system reactance at 5 Hz: CDF −2.54 (0.98), VATS −4.08 (0.94), controls −0.24 (0.85)), with VATS showing greater impairment. The ratio of minute ventilation to oxygen uptake was highest in the VATS group compared with CDF and controls (39.7 (2.8), 36.8 (3.2) and 32.2 (2.7), respectively).ConclusionLong-term pulmonary outcomes following both CDF and VATS were generally reassuring. However, oscillometry and exercise testing revealed subtle differences in ventilatory strategies, with children post-VATS demonstrating mild residual impairment.Keywords: Empyema, Paediatric Lung Disease, Lung Physiology

Introduction

Pneumonia is a leading cause of childhood mortality globally1. The incidence of empyema as a complication of pneumonia ranges from 2% to 8%.2 3 Despite early diagnosis and improved treatment strategies, empyema prevalence is increasing.4–6

The goals of empyema management in children are to sterilise the pleural space and prevent or release lung entrapment.7 Common management options include using antibiotics alone, chest drain insertion with fibrinolytic therapy (CDF) and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) with decortication.8–10 The British Thoracic Society currently recommends the use of CDF as the first line management strategy, with VATS considered an alternative management option.8 These recommendations derive from guidelines published in 2005; since then, randomised studies in adults, including the MIST-3 feasibility trial of early VATS versus intrapleural enzyme therapy and the ongoing MIST-4 trial, have re-examined the timing and choice of pleural intervention, although long-term functional data specific to children remain scarce.11 Trials comparing CDF and VATS have failed to demonstrate clinically significant differences between the management approaches using outcome variables such as length of hospital stay and days febrile after treatment.12–14 These studies have been limited by non-randomised treatment approaches that were determined by local policy and available expertise. A recent meta-analysis of six randomised controlled studies concluded that children undergoing primary VATS had better short-term outcomes such as shortened hospital stay, shorter postoperative fever and earlier chest drain removal.15 In contrast, a systematic review of randomised controlled trials concluded that VATS and CDF had comparable short-term clinical outcomes.16

No study has formally related long-term outcomes to the initial mode of treatment. In one study children had normal spirometry and chest radiographs 2 years after admission to hospital with empyema.17 Children received various treatments, ranging from simple supportive therapy with intravenous antibiotics to open thoracotomy and decortication. Lung volumes or dynamic airway function were not assessed. Kontouli et al compared patients with empyema and a history of asthma with patients with empyema and without a history of asthma using plethysmography and cardiopulmonary exercise testing.18

Therefore, the present study was conducted to compare highly sensitive, long-term cardiopulmonary function outcomes of children with empyema treated with CDF with those of children treated with VATS and healthy controls. Two opposing mechanisms could be anticipated: VATS with decortication may achieve more complete evacuation of infected pleural material and earlier lung re-expansion and might therefore be expected to preserve or even improve long-term ventilatory function, whereas decortication entails greater pleural and chest-wall disruption that could adversely affect dynamic respiratory mechanics and chest-wall compliance. Given these competing possibilities, we hypothesised that overall lung function would be normal in a healthy control group, but children who underwent VATS would demonstrate subtle deficits in dynamic respiratory measures compared with those who received CDF.

Materials and methods

We conducted a dual-site cohort study with controls to assess static and dynamic pulmonary function in children at least 4 years after admission for empyema as defined by the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, diagnostic code J86.9. Empyema was defined as a parapneumonic effusion accompanied by clinical and biochemical evidence of infection, together with sonographic evidence of a complex or septated pleural collection and, or frank pus obtained at drainage, corresponding to this code. Eligible survivors of an index admission were identified from hospital records and were prospectively recruited for cross-sectional physiological assessment. Both study sites [The Children’s Hospital, Westmead (CHW) & Sydney Children Hospital (SCH)] belong to the same hospital network, and all other aspects of policy and protocol management such as the determination of antibiotic choices, indication for supplemental oxygen and discharge criteria were the same. Clinicians worked across both sites within the hospital network, and no substantial differences in outcomes for other respiratory diseases were observed between the two institutions. Due to differences in interventional radiological expertise, CHW routinely used CDF as the primary standard operating procedure, whereas SCH routinely used VATS. Thus, the primary procedure at each site was determined by the site-specific access to the intervention rather than by the patient’s clinical status. Because allocation to CDF or VATS was determined solely by the site where a child initially presented, rather than by disease severity, more severe cases were not selectively indicated for either modality. No child was transferred between sites for the index intervention, and children who required a second procedure, such as rescue VATS after an incomplete response to CDF, were excluded by design. Both sites operate within a single publicly funded network serving overlapping catchment populations, so differences in local health economy were unlikely to have influenced management or outcomes.

Children with empyema who were managed with a single primary intervention and were >8 years old at the time of testing were included. The age threshold applied was based on the age at physiological testing, not age at the index empyema, when the children were considerably younger (table 1). Any patient who needed a second intervention or, with chronic co-morbid medical conditions that precluded reliable pulmonary function testing, was excluded from the study. Healthy siblings and children of staff were recruited to comprise the healthy control group. The control group was required to be free of chronic cardiorespiratory disease, a history of prematurity requiring neonatal respiratory support, previous empyema or recurrent lower respiratory tract infections and active respiratory symptoms at the time of assessment. Healthy siblings were included to help account for shared genetic and environmental exposures, whereas children of staff were recruited to achieve the required sample size. As the two subgroups did not differ in anthropometry or lung function, they were combined into a single control group. Patients who had an active respiratory illness 4 weeks preceding testing had their assessment deferred until they recovered. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents and, where appropriate, assent obtained from the patients.

Patients underwent physical examinations, with attention to chest-wall and spinal abnormalities. Spirometry and lung volume measurements were completed as per American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines in the respective Thoracic SOciety of Australian and New Zealand (TSANZ) accredited laboratory using identical equipment.19 20 Physiologists performing the tests work cross-site between the two hospitals. Ventilatory inhomogeneity was assessed using the multiple-breath nitrogen washout technique, performed according to standard protocols using the Exhalyzer D system (Eco Medics AG, Switzerland) to derive the lung clearance index (LCI).21 This was followed by forced oscillometry testing using the Tremoflo device (Thorasys Medical Systems Ltd, Montreal, Canada), performed according to established protocols22 23 and interpreted using validated reference values.24 Oscillometry provided sensitive measures of airway resistance (respiratory system resistance at 5 Hz (Rrs5)) and pulmonary reactance (respiratory system reactance at 5 Hz (Xrs5) and area of reactance (Ax)). Cardiopulmonary exercise test was performed on a separate day according ATS standards using the progressive Bruce protocol on a treadmill (VmaxSeries, SensorMedics, Yorba Linda, CA).25 26 Patients discontinued any short-acting beta-mimetic agents at least 12 hours before testing. The test termination criteria were defined as achieving at least 2 of the following: a heart rate ≥80% predicted maximum (using the formula (peak HR=220-age)) and Borg’s rating of perceived exertion >827 or respiratory exchange ratio (RER) ≥1.10. For additional quality control of data, all aspects of testing and interpretation of physiological measures were interpreted centrally by investigators (JM, BK, PF and HS) blinded to the primary intervention.

Sample size calculation was determined using LCI as the primary outcome variable, as it is a sensitive marker of lung disease. Based on the study by Subbarao et al, a sample size of 22 patients would detect a clinically significant treatment difference of 2.0 (SD 2) with 90% power at 5% alpha.28

Analysis of data

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.19 and R V.4.2. Continuous variables are reported as mean (SD) when approximately normally distributed or median (IQR) when skewed; categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. Normality was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk tests and by inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. The primary outcome was LCI. Secondary outcomes included spirometry z-scores, lung volumes, oscillometry indices (Rrs5, Xrs5, resonant frequency (Fres) and Ax) and cardiopulmonary exercise testing variables (peak aerobic capacity (VO2 peak), ventilatory equivalent for oxygen (VE/VO2), inspiratory duty cycle (Ti/Ttot), tidal volume (VT) recruitment and oxygen uptake efficiency plateau (OUEP)). Between-group comparisons across the three groups (CDF, VATS and healthy controls) were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for approximately normally distributed variables and Kruskal–Wallis tests for non-normally distributed variables. If ANOVA indicated an overall significant effect, pairwise comparisons were performed with Tukey’s honestly significant difference; if the Kruskal–Wallis test was significant, post-hoc Dunn’s tests with Bonferroni correction were used. Two-group comparisons (CDF vs VATS) were performed using independent-samples t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, as appropriate. To account for potential confounding by age at testing, sex, time since intervention and body mass index, multivariable linear regression models were fitted for key continuous outcomes (Xrs5, VE/VO2 and LCI). Model residuals were inspected for normality and homoscedasticity, and skewed outcomes were transformed or analysed using robust regression when required. Adjusted mean differences with 95% CIs and p-values are reported. Because multiple physiological outcomes were assessed, a hierarchical testing strategy with LCI designated as the primary outcome was used. For secondary outcomes, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure (5%) was applied to control for multiple comparisons. Reported p-values are two-sided, and significance was defined as p<0.05 after correction, when appropriate. Technically unacceptable tests and missing data were documented, and complete-case analyses were conducted for each outcome. Sensitivity analyses included (1) repeating primary models after exclusion of participants with a prior diagnosis of asthma and (2) propensity-score adjustment using available baseline clinical variables to explore robustness to non-random treatment allocation. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d or appropriate non-parametric measures) with 95% CIs were calculated to aid clinical interpretation.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and their families were involved in the design and conduct of this research. They were consulted during the feasibility stage, choice of outcome measures and methods of recruitment. The study received no external funding support and all authors have no competing interests to declare.

Results

Of the eligible children, 76% of those treated with VATS and 81% of those treated with CDF consented to participate in the study. Therefore, 51 children treated with CDF at CHW and 28 children with VATS at SCH participated. The demographic details of the VATS and CDF cohorts were comparable and are presented in table 1. No differences in demographic details were found between the children who participated in the study and those who declined. The principal reasons for non-participation were relocation outside the referral area, an inability to establish contact despite repeated telephone and mailed invitations and declined consent; attempts to re-contact non-responders were recorded.

The age of initial presentation with empyema was similar in both the VATS and CDF groups. The length of stay in hospital and complications post primary intervention were also similar in the two cohorts (table 1).

The age of the children at the time of testing and time since intervention were likewise similar. Lung function data and cardiopulmonary test results are presented in tables 2 and 3, respectively. Three children in the CDF group and two in the VATS group had been diagnosed with asthma by their physician, but none had any significant asthma exacerbations in the previous 2 years. In the CDF group, two children used medications, as needed, to relieve symptoms, and one child used regular inhaled corticosteroids as prevention. In the VATS group, one child used medications, as needed, to relieve symptoms, and one child used inhaled corticosteroids. The CDF, VATS and healthy control groups had normal spirometry values and LCI. However, based on the Xrs, Ax and Fres, the CDF and VATS groups had significantly lower reactance than healthy controls. Interestingly, the VATS group had a significantly lower reactance than the CDF group (table 2).

Physical examination results of all patients were normal, with no chest-wall deformities and scoliosis noted.

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing demonstrated that the CDF and VATS groups had similar peak aerobic capacity, peak heart rate and breathing reserve compared with the healthy control group. However, all children who had empyema had a significantly increased peak VE/VO2 and concurrent reduced OUEP compared with healthy children. The VATS group had a significantly increased VE/VO2 and reduced OUEP compared with the CDF group. As evidenced by the Ti/Ttot ratio, the VATS group spent a greater proportion of their respiratory duty cycle in inspiration compared with the CDF or healthy control group. The VT recruitment was similar in the healthy control and CDF groups but was significantly higher than that achieved by the VATS group. The oxygen uptake efficiency was reduced in children who received empyema treatment than in healthy children. No evidence of exercise-induced bronchospasm was noted in any of the children.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare long-term static and dynamic assessments of cardiopulmonary function in children treated with CDF and VATS for empyema. Although overall lung function was normal, oscillometry and cardiopulmonary exercise tests revealed subtle but consistent differences in ventilatory strategy among children treated with VATS.

The mean duration of hospitalisation and complication rates after primary intervention in both CDF and VATS groups were similar to previous reports.6 7 Normal static lung function and ventilatory homogeneity were noted in children with empyema treated with CDF or VATS. This finding was in contrast to the result of another study that reported mild restrictive and obstructive disease as well as ventilatory inhomogeneity in children who had a history of empyema.16 29 However, their cohort was heterogeneous, with 72% of children with empyema being treated with a chest drain, of whom a third received fibrinolytics and only four underwent VATS as a primary procedure.17 Additionally, 25% of their cohort had preceding variably controlled asthma. Only two children in the present study were on regular inhaled corticosteroids, and none had asthma exacerbations for at least 2 years prior to testing.

The peak aerobic capacity in all groups was similar and within normal limits, with no evidence of respiratory limitation regardless of the previous intervention. In contrast, Kontouli et al17 demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in breathing reserve between empyema treatment and healthy controls, but the study was not powered to compare children who underwent VATS and those who received CDF. The present study demonstrated increased ventilatory equivalents in the VATS group compared with the CDF and healthy control groups. Additionally, OUEP was reduced in the VATS group, which is further evidence of altered ventilatory strategies during exercise. The increased ventilatory equivalent and reduced OUEP indicated reduced reactance in the VATS group compared with the CDF and healthy control group. Further, the reduced VT recruitment and prolonged inspiratory time in the VATS group further raises the potential long-term effect of decortication. It would have been interesting to compare patients who underwent VATS without decortication, for example, lung biopsy, but we were not able to find a comparable group to include in the study. It remains an open question whether VATS alone, or the additional effect of decortication, was responsible for our findings. Interestingly, the similar Borg score at peak exercise in the VATS and CDF groups suggests that these altered ventilatory strategies during exercise are subclinical. Although the VATS and the CDF groups demonstrated similar demographics and complication rates, the former underwent the primary procedure on average a day later. Whether the subtle abnormalities seen were related to treatment delay or the VATS procedure itself cannot be determined.

The aim of decortication is to remove fibrinous peel and prevent lung entrapment. None of the children had any postoperative complications such as haemorrhage, persistent air leak, bronchopleural fistula and chest wall deformity. However, the basis for the reduced reactance is unclear and warrants further investigation. Reduced reactance (Xrs5), together with increased Ax and Fres, indicates a stiffer, less compliant respiratory system; in this context, it more plausibly reflects residual pleural thickening and reduced chest-wall or lung-periphery compliance following decortication than large-airway obstruction, consistent with the preserved spirometry values and LCI. The accompanying rise in VE/VO₂ and a decrease in OUEP indicate a less efficient ventilatory strategy, characterised by greater ventilation for a given oxygen uptake, rather than a true gas-exchange or cardiovascular limitation. This interpretation is supported by the normal peak VO₂, oxygen pulse and breathing reserve. Because peak aerobic capacity and perceived exertion were normal, these oscillometric and exercise findings are best interpreted as statistically significant but subclinical markers of altered small-airway and chest-wall mechanics, and their long-term clinical importance remains uncertain and merits longitudinal follow-up.

A strength of this study was its design; identical treatment protocols were implemented by the same clinicians working across both sites, and physiologists, who were blinded to the intervention, interpreted the data, which helped mitigate the risk of bias. Ideally, all measurements would have been performed in the same physiology laboratory; however, as this was not logistically possible, we took all possible precautions to minimise potential confounding. Physiological measurements were performed at the primary TSANZ-accredited site, and the equipment used, physiologists who performed the tests and all aspects of quality control were identical across sites. Furthermore, physiological measurements were performed by personnel who were blinded to the intervention. Another limitation is that only approximately three-quarters of the eligible children returned for follow-up. Although the demographic details of the tested children were similar across the entire cohort, a risk of selection bias remains. Nonetheless, because treatment was assigned by the site of presentation rather than by clinician judgement of severity and thoracoscopic management was offered uniformly at the surgical site irrespective of disease stage, confounding by indication is likely to have been limited, although residual unmeasured differences cannot be excluded. Moreover, although detailed static and dynamic physiological measurements were performed, high-resolution imaging could not be justified by Human Research Ethics Committee. Consequently, systematic longitudinal imaging was not performed to confirm the completeness of lung re-expansion, and thoracic ultrasonography was not performed at follow-up. Likewise, the prevalence of acute parenchymal complications such as necrotising pneumonia, cavitation or lung abscess was not systematically quantified, so an imbalance in these features between the groups cannot be excluded as a contributor to the observed differences. Pain and musculoskeletal symptoms at the time of exercise testing were not formally assessed, and their potential influence on ventilatory pattern cannot be discounted.

Interestingly, lower respiratory tract infections occurring in early childhood were found to be significantly associated with premature adult death.30 However, the sample size of the present study was insufficient to detect an effect of age at infection on any subsequent physiological outcomes. Nevertheless, this study warrants consideration of any interaction between age at infection and the long-term impact of the treatment approach for childhood empyema.

In contrast to current British Thoracic Society recommendations, recent meta-analyses have demonstrated short-term benefits of VATS over CDF. This study demonstrates reassuring long-term outcomes for children who required intervention for empyema, regardless of whether they were treated with CDF or VATS. However, subtle, subclinical differences in ventilatory strategies, identified during cardiopulmonary exercise testing and oscillometry, were observed in children who underwent VATS. The long-term significance of these findings remains to be determined.
