GLP-1 Receptor Agonist Combination Therapy Before and After Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery: A Review of Outcomes
Authors: Chang Seok Ko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe paradigm of obesity treatment is rapidly evolving with the introduction and widespread adoption of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs). Semaglutide, the representative agent, has demonstrated substantial weight reduction and cardiometabolic benefits across multiple pivotal clinical trials, reshaping societal perceptions of anti-obesity pharmacotherapy. In parallel, sleeve gastrectomy (SG) has become the most commonly performed metabolic and bariatric surgical procedure worldwide due to its technical simplicity, strong safety profile, and durable outcomes. The use of anti-obesity medication in the preoperative setting of metabolic and bariatric surgery has been previously explored; however, its clinical benefit has long remained controversial and inconsistent. Recent evidence suggests that GLP-1 RAs can safely achieve greater preoperative weight loss than earlier pharmacologic agents. Furthermore, GLP-1 RAs have emerged as an effective treatment option for postoperative weight regain, demonstrating significant weight-loss benefits in both SG and gastric bypass patients compared with conventional therapies. As GLP-1–based therapies continue to advance, future strategies should shift toward a multimodal treatment model analogous to oncology, integrating pharmacologic and surgical interventions throughout all phases of obesity care. Further research is needed to establish the ideal timing, duration, safety, and long-term weight loss and metabolic effects of GLP-1 RA administration in both pre- and postoperative phases.Keywords: Obesity, Bariatric surgery, Metabolic surgery, GLP-1 RA, Anti obesity medication

PARADIGM SHIFT IN OBESITY TREATMENT AND THE ROLE OF GLUCAGON-LIKE PEPTIDE-1 RECEPTOR AGONIST (GLP-1 RA)

In recent years, the landscape of obesity management has undergone a paradigm shift with the emergence of GLP-1 RAs. From the perspective of a metabolic and bariatric surgeon, the integration of these agents into perioperative care represents transformative changes in modern obesity treatment.

GLP-1 is a peptide hormone composed of 31 amino acids and belongs to the incretin family. It is secreted predominantly from the intestinal L-cells but also from the nucleus tractus solitarius in the brain. As an endogenous incretin, GLP-1 is released in response to food intake, leading to a cascade of physiological effects: stimulation of insulin secretion, suppression of hepatic glucose production and glucagon release, delayed gastric emptying, and enhancement of satiety [1,2].

Liraglutide was the first GLP-1 RA approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of obesity. It is a glucagon-like peptide-1 analogue with 97% homology to native human GLP-1. In the SCALE clinical trials, which enrolled patients with obesity, prediabetes, type 2 diabetes, and sleep apnea, liraglutide demonstrated a mean weight reduction of approximately 5.7–8% [3,4,5]. Furthermore, in the LEADER trial evaluating cardiovascular safety in patients with type 2 diabetes, liraglutide reduced the risk of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and nonfatal stroke—by 13% compared with placebo [6].

Semaglutide, which has shown even greater weight-reducing efficacy and has marked another paradigm shift in obesity management, is a representative human GLP-1 analogue sharing 94% homology with native GLP-1 and characterized by a prolonged half-life of approximately one week. Several pivotal STEP trials have demonstrated that semaglutide can induce up to 18% weight loss, along with substantial cardiometabolic benefits. These include reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events as well as improved renal outcomes [7,8,9,10].

With such remarkable effects, GLP-1 RAs have become widely recognized—not only through clinical evidence but also through their increasing visibility in public discourse. It is no exaggeration to say that we are now living in the era of GLP-1 RAs.

METABOLIC AND BARIATRIC SURGERY: CURRENT TRENDS

Among surgical options, sleeve gastrectomy (SG) has become the most dominant procedure worldwide [11]. It involves removing the greater curvature of the stomach from the antrum to the fundus, creating a tubular gastric remnant. Compared with bypass procedures, SG does not require intestinal anastomosis, which makes it technically simpler and safer, with lower rates of short- and long-term complications [12,13]. The American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery (ASMBS) has even stated that SG is as safe as cholecystectomy in terms of perioperative risk.

These advantages have significantly influenced both surgeon and patient preferences. According to the International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity and Metabolic Disorders (IFSO) Global Registry Report (2021), SG has rapidly increased since the 2010s, becoming the most dominant metabolic and bariatric surgery procedure worldwide [14]. The 8th IFSO Global Registry Report also reported SG as accounting for 62.5% of all primary bariatric surgeries [11]. In the Asia-Pacific region, data from the IFSO-Asia Pacific Chapter Registry show that SG represents approximately 76% of all bariatric operations [15]. In Korea, SG now accounts for nearly 80% of all bariatric procedures [16].

Taken together, these global and regional trends highlight 2 converging pillars of modern metabolic therapy: GLP-1 RAs and SG.

HISTORY OF PREOPERATIVE WEIGHT MANAGEMENT

The concept of preoperative medical weight management (MWM) was first introduced at the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Consensus Conference in 1991 [17]. At that time, the NIH recommended bariatric surgery only for patients who were unable to achieve meaningful weight reduction through nonsurgical approaches. In the 2004 ASMBS Consensus Conference, experts acknowledged that patients should attempt nonsurgical weight loss before surgery but emphasized that completion of formal medical therapy should not be a prerequisite for surgical eligibility [17,18].

Despite this, many insurance providers began to require documentation of MWM completion as a condition for surgical approval. In 2011, the ASMBS Clinical Issues Committee once again opposed the mandatory enforcement of preoperative MWM, explicitly stating that “any policy that delays or denies access to bariatric surgery without strong supporting evidence cannot be justified.” Although multiple statements since then have reiterated opposition to mandatory MWM, many insurers continue to treat it as a standard preoperative step [17,19]. To date, there remains no strong consensus or high-level evidence supporting that MWM improves surgical outcomes.

POTENTIAL BENEFITS AND DRAWBACKS OF PREOPERATIVE WEIGHT LOSS

A large retrospective analysis of Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery Accreditation and Quality Improvement Program (MBSAQIP) data (2015–2018) including over 548,000 patients showed that more than 80% of patients lost some weight before surgery, and over 40% achieved a preoperative weight reduction exceeding 5% of total body weight. Multivariable analyses demonstrated that preoperative weight loss was associated with lower rates of 30-day mortality and anastomotic leak, although there were no differences in postoperative bleeding or other major complications [20]. Another single-center retrospective study (427 patients, 2003–2017) published in 2022 evaluated the relationship between preoperative weight loss and perioperative outcomes. Greater preoperative weight reduction was associated with a slightly shorter hospital stay, but there was no significant difference in operative time, complication rates, intensive care unit admissions, or intraoperative events [21]. A meta-analysis of nine studies also demonstrated that preoperative weight loss reduced liver volume by 5–20%, potentially offering technical advantages such as improved visualization and easier access during laparoscopy. However, the review concluded that the clinical significance of this reduction for actual surgical risk remains uncertain [22]. Similarly, a 2021 MBSAQIP study including 171,000 patients who underwent SG or Roux-en-Y gastric bypass found no consistent association between preoperative weight loss and improved surgical outcomes or shorter operative times. Notably, some procedure-specific benefits were observed among patients with body mass index (BMI) ≥50 kg/m2, suggesting that preoperative optimization may be selectively valuable for extreme obesity rather than universally beneficial [23]. Potential advantages of preoperative weight loss include the opportunity to achieve partial weight reduction through less-invasive means, technically easier surgery, reduced operative time, and shorter hospital stay [17].

However, there are also important disadvantages. Preoperative dieting requirements may delay or discourage patients from proceeding to surgery, leading to higher dropout rates. Several studies have shown a negative correlation between time to surgery and the percentage of weight loss, suggesting that longer preoperative waiting periods do not translate into improved outcomes [24]. Moreover, insurance-mandated programs often increase administrative burden and limit access to surgery, occasionally resulting in preoperative weight regain [25].

The most recent ASMBS position statement concludes that while preoperative weight loss may provide certain benefits, evidence is insufficient to justify making it mandatory for all bariatric surgery candidates [26].

EVIDENCE FOR PHARMACOLOGIC PREOPERATIVE OPTIMIZATION

Despite the increasing popularity of anti-obesity medications (AOMs), only a few studies have investigated their use as preoperative adjuncts to bariatric surgery.

One retrospective cohort study examined the impact of early phentermine and/or topiramate therapy before and after surgery. The primary endpoints were the percentage of total body weight loss and the deviation from the expected postoperative weight loss curve. The authors reported that early initiation of AOMs improved total weight loss (TWL), with the greatest benefit when treatment began before surgery, though the number of patients receiving preoperative AOMs was small [27].

A double-blind, prospective, randomized controlled trial assessed the efficacy of low-dose phentermine (Lomaira; KVK-Tech, Inc., Newtown, PA, USA) in patients undergoing laparoscopic SG or Roux-en-Y gastric bypass. Preoperative phentermine significantly increased short-term weight loss compared to placebo, without safety concerns, suggesting that low-dose phentermine is an effective and well-tolerated preoperative option [28].

Another study evaluated liraglutide as a short-term preoperative intervention. Patients received an initial daily dose of 0.6 mg, titrated weekly to 3.0 mg and maintained for 12 weeks. Mean body weight decreased from 147.4 kg to 139.3 kg, representing an average 5.5% weight loss over three months. Notably, 94.6% of patients (35/37) achieved weight reduction, indicating liraglutide’s strong efficacy in this setting [29].

A retrospective single-center study examined the use of GLP-1 RAs—including semaglutide—prior to bariatric surgery. Patients treated with GLP-1 RAs achieved a greater BMI reduction (−5.5±3.2 vs. −2.9±2.4 kg/m2) compared to controls, without any GLP-1-related complications [30]. Another recent study, which included a larger cohort with 182 patients in the semaglutide group, used propensity score matching to compare outcomes with patients who did not receive preoperative semaglutide. The group that was administered semaglutide before surgery demonstrated a median TWL of 4%. Early major postoperative complications—including bleeding, leak, infection, reoperation, and readmission—were not significantly different between the 2 groups [31]. These findings suggest that GLP-1-based pharmacotherapy may be a safe and effective adjunct to preoperative optimization, although larger prospective trials are needed to confirm its benefits. INADEQUATE WEIGHT LOSS AND WEIGHT REGAIN: TOWARD A UNIFIED NOMENCLATURE AND DEFINITION

Historically, the field of metabolic and bariatric surgery has lacked a consistent nomenclature to describe patients who fail to achieve adequate weight loss after surgery. Terms such as insufficient weight loss, unsuccessful weight loss, and weight-loss failure have often been used interchangeably, usually without precise definitions. This inconsistency has had practical consequences, particularly in insurance coverage policies, where many insurers rely on arbitrary or outdated criteria [35]. For example, revisional surgery is often reimbursed only if performed at least two years after the primary operation and only when the patient has lost less than 50% of excess body weight or remains at least 30% above ideal body weight. In some cases, recurrence is defined as returning to less than 20–30% excess body weight loss, thresholds that are not grounded in modern clinical or physiological understanding.

To resolve this inconsistency, the International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity and Metabolic Disorders (IFSO) convened a task force to develop standardized definitions for inadequate weight loss and weight regain. The task force recommended replacing the vague term “success” with the more precise concept of “adequate weight loss,” which refers to an optimal clinical response defined as achieving at least 20% TWL and/or demonstrating improvement in obesity-related comorbidities. In doing so, IFSO also emphasized that TWL relative to preoperative weight should be used as the standard metric for reporting outcomes after metabolic and bariatric surgery. TWL is less influenced by variations in baseline body weight and tends to remain more consistent across obesity classes for a given procedure, making it a superior and more equitable metric compared with excess weight loss [36].

Patients who do not meet these criteria are classified as having a suboptimal clinical response (SoCR). This term replaces expressions such as “insufficient weight loss” or “weight-loss failure” and refers specifically to individuals whose weight loss after surgery is inadequate from the beginning—defined as less than 20% TWL or a failure to improve, or even a worsening of, the obesity-related conditions for which the surgery was performed. In contrast, recurrent weight gain (RWG) denotes the reaccumulation of weight after an initially adequate clinical response [36]. RWG is common and to some extent physiologic across all bariatric procedures, with reported incidence varying widely from 3.9% to as high as 75%, depending on duration of follow-up, population characteristics, and the definition employed. The literature contains a wide range of definitions, including regaining more than 10 kg, gaining more than 5 BMI units, or regaining more than 10–25% of the maximum weight loss at postoperative nadir. Such heterogeneity highlights the need for unified criteria that reflect both weight trajectories and clinical improvement [37].

POSTOPERATIVE COMBINATION THERAPY: BENEFITS AND LIMITATIONS

With the advent of modern incretin-based therapies, GLP-1 RAs have emerged as promising pharmacologic treatments for RWG after bariatric surgery. Recent investigations have evaluated their efficacy in patients who experienced RWG. One important study by Jensen and colleagues examined patients who developed weight regain approximately six years after their primary bariatric procedure, which was predominantly Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (82%). At the initiation of GLP-1 RA therapy, the mean weight was 90.5 kg and the mean BMI was 34 kg/m2. After 6 months of treatment, patients receiving liraglutide achieved an average TWL of 7.3%, whereas those receiving semaglutide achieved 9.8%. Moreover, the proportion of individuals losing at least 10% of their body weight was substantially higher in the semaglutide group, at 48% compared with 31% in the liraglutide group, demonstrating a more robust effect with semaglutide [32].

In another study, Lautenbach and colleagues [33] evaluated semaglutide 0.5 mg in patients with RWG approximately 64 months after bariatric surgery, most of whom had undergone SG (65.9%). Among 44 participants—15 who had undergone Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and 29 who had undergone SG—there was no significant difference in semaglutide effectiveness based on the type of primary surgery. The investigators reported that mean TWL was approximately 6% at three months and 10% at 6 months, with 16% of patients achieving at least 10% weight reduction at 3 months and 45% achieving this threshold by 6 months. These findings suggest that semaglutide can be effective across different surgical anatomies and time intervals following surgery [33].

A more recent comparison of tirzepatide and semaglutide, published in Obesity Surgery in 2024, demonstrated even greater efficacy with tirzepatide. In this study, individuals with a mean BMI of 35.1 kg/m2 who had undergone SG approximately 6 years earlier experienced weight regain and were subsequently treated for six months with either semaglutide or tirzepatide. The semaglutide group achieved roughly 10.3% TWL, whereas the tirzepatide group reached 15.5%, highlighting the potential benefit of dual gastric inhibitory polypeptide/GLP-1 receptor agonism in this population [34].

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses published recently have confirmed these findings, showing consistently that GLP-1 RAs—and more recently, dual incretin therapies—produce meaningful weight loss in patients with RWG after bariatric surgery [38,39,40]. Despite these promising results, authors of these studies emphasize common limitations including small sample sizes, retrospective designs, heterogeneity of surgical procedures, short follow-up durations, and the lack of randomized controlled trials. They also highlight the need for structured treatment algorithms, a deeper understanding of post-surgical gut hormone physiology, and long-term studies to establish durable outcomes and guide evidence-based clinical decision-making.

FUTURE STRATEGY AND SUMMARY

In this new era shaped by the widespread use of GLP-1 RAs and SG, we must redefine the way we conceptualize obesity treatment. Much like the evolution of cancer therapy, obesity care now requires a multimodal strategy that integrates pharmacologic, metabolic, and surgical approaches in a coordinated continuum (Fig. 1). GLP-1 RAs can serve as a form of neoadjuvant therapy before primary surgical intervention such as bariatric surgery, enhancing preoperative optimization and improving surgical readiness. They may also function as an adjuvant therapy after surgery or as a secondary intervention in patients who experience RWG after an initially adequate response.

Multimodal approach to obesity treatment.

In summary, preoperative weight management has traditionally been recommended with the aim of improving bariatric surgery outcomes. However, the evidence supporting its benefits remains inconsistent, and its mandatory use is not justified by current data. GLP-1 RAs appear to safely enhance perioperative weight loss, yet existing studies are limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous protocols, and insufficient long-term follow-up.

As SG continues to dominate global bariatric practice and GLP-1 RA therapy rapidly expands, the role of pharmacologic obesity treatment—both before and after surgery—is expected to become increasingly important. The authors anticipate that the use of GLP-1 RAs will expand in both the neoadjuvant and adjuvant settings, where evidence remains relatively limited. In particular, short-term neoadjuvant use (within 6 months) in super-obese patients is likely to become more widely adopted as part of preoperative optimization strategies. In the adjuvant setting, early initiation of GLP-1 RAs in patients demonstrating a SoCR is expected to be increasingly discussed and actively considered in clinical practice. Future research should focus on optimizing SG outcomes, determining the ideal timing and duration of GLP-1 RA administration, and establishing standardized, evidence-based multimodal treatment protocols.
