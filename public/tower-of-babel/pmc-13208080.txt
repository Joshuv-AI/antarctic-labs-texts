NRF2 as a guardian of health: from an ancient survival pathway to a modern therapeutic target
Authors: Sandra Tenreiro, Ana Sofia Falcão, Nicole Bender, Iveta Bernatova, Alessandra Berry, Antonio Cuadrado, Patrick Eppenberger, Letizia Giona, Anna Grochot-Przeczek, Elke Heiss, Monika Jakubowska, Paul F Long, Asya Makhro, Mojgan Masoodi, Christina Morgenstern, Luciano Saso, Miguel C Seabra, Alicja Sznarkowska, Gerasimos Sykiotis, Ioannis P Trougakos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNRF2 (Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2-Related Factor 2) has traditionally been viewed as a master regulator of the antioxidant response. However, emerging evidence redefines NRF2 as a pleiotropic and evolutionarily conserved systems-level integrator of redox, metabolic, proteostatic, and inflammatory homeostasis, i.e., an evolutionarily conserved guardian of health. From its origins as an adaptive response to rising atmospheric oxygen in early metazoans to its role in orchestrating complex cytoprotective networks in humans, NRF2 exemplifies the biological transition from stress resistance to adaptive resilience. Understanding its regulation across species, tissues, life stages and gender offers novel perspectives for combating age-related cellular dysfunction and reframing the dynamic process of redox regulation as a component of adaptive homeostasis in a precision medicine perspective. This review summarizes and integrates essential concepts currently being developed regarding the evolutionary roots of NRF2, its molecular and regulatory complexity, its dualistic role in health and disease, and its potential as a biomarker of resilience and therapeutic target. We highlight NRF2's function as a rheostat rather than a binary stress switch, including the role of hormetic activation through lifestyle and environmental stimuli. By maintaining redox homeostasis and dynamic adaptive stress responses, NRF2 bridges molecular defense mechanisms with strategies for healthspan extension and preventive medicine from very early life stages until senescence.Keywords: NRF2, Redox signaling, Aging, Chronic inflammation, Therapeutic target, Evolutionary biology, Cancer risk, Personalized medicine

Introduction

Over the past two decades, NRF2 has shifted from being considered a redox-specific transcription factor to a central player in systems-level health maintenance. This progression mirrors a broader understanding of physiology: from static homeostasis to dynamic allostasis, i.e. the process of achieving stability through adaptive change. This review aims to reframe NRF2 as a biological nexus, evolved to orchestrate allostatic responses, balance stress adaptation, modulate aging, and fine-tune resilience. We trace the evolutionary emergence of NRF2 within the context of fundamental energetic and survival trade-offs, explore its molecular complexity as a substrate for evolutionary tinkering, and examine its paradoxical roles through the lens of antagonistic pleiotropy. We argue that NRF2's pleiotropy and hormetic activation positions it as a strategic focal point for future preventive and personalized medicine.

From oxygen toxicity to redox adaptation: the evolutionary roots of NRF2

The emergence of aerobic metabolism represented not only an energetic revolution but also a toxic challenge. As molecular oxygen became abundant during the Great Oxygenation Event (2.45–1.85 billion years ago), aerobic organisms faced the task of neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated as by-products of mitochondria respiration that would otherwise damage macromolecules [[1], [2], [3]]. Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2–Related Factor 2 (NRF2) evolved as a molecular solution to this trade-off: an adaptive transcription factor capable of sensing redox fluctuations beyond the hemodynamics zone and activating cytoprotective genes that sustain cell homeostasis. Molecular evolutionary analyses indicate that the axis linking NRF2 to its repressor Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) arose in primordial animals in tandem with rising atmospheric oxygen levels and the increasing metabolic complexity of motile multicellular life [4] (Fig. 1).

This evolutionary path highlights a critical physiological principle: the shift from constitutive to inducible defense as an energy-saving strategy. Whereas plants often rely on constitutive production of antioxidant compounds, motile animals evolved an inducible defense mechanism, such as NRF2, that can be rapidly activated to counteract acute oxidative bursts from high metabolic activity, allowing greater energetic investment in growth, reproduction, and locomotion under non-stress conditions [8].

Its cap'n’collar (CNC) basic-region leucine zipper (bZIP) architecture and its control by the cysteine-rich sensor KEAP1 appear to have emerged before the divergence of cnidarians and bilaterians [4,9]. In basal metazoans such as sponges and cnidarians, organisms exposed to fluctuating oxygen and symbiont-driven redox stress, NRF2-like proteins coordinate detoxification and antioxidant defenses [10,11].

The regulatory components of this system show evolutionary flexibility, underscoring its core importance. In cnidarians, the KEAP1 homologs lack some reactive cysteine residues essential for sensing oxidative cues in vertebrates, suggesting that alternative regulatory circuits may have evolved early to preserve redox adaptability and balance [9]. For instance, nematodes such as Caenorhabditis elegans possess a single NRF2 ortholog (called SKN-1) but have no KEAP1, implying that these worms use alternative regulatory mechanisms (e.g., phosphorylation), demonstrating that the function of redox-sensitive transcription is more deeply conserved than any single regulatory mechanism [11]. This represents a classic case of convergent evolution to solve a universal biological problem: managing the double-edged sword of redox chemistry. Bacteria utilize the OxyR transcription factor and fungi employ Yap1, each of which activates antioxidant genes in response to ROS, exemplifying a convergent evolution of NRF2-like stress response systems across life [8,11].

The evolutionary persistence of the NRF2/KEAP1 system underscores its fundamental role as master integrator of cellular stress and resource allocation, linking environmental stressors to gene expression long before the emergence of complex immune and metabolic systems.

Alternative splicing determines the diversity and stability of NRF2 isoforms

According to the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), the human NRF2 gene (NFE2L2) generates eight curated mRNA transcripts, which can be grouped into transcripts originating from two alternative promoters (Fig. 2). The canonical 605-amino-acid NRF2 protein is produced from transcript variant 1 (NM_006164.5), which comprises five exons and is expressed from the primary promoter P1. Additional P1-driven transcripts, such as NM_001313903.2 and NM_001313902.2, arise through alternative splicing and encode NRF2 isoforms lacking exon 2 or exons 2 and 3, respectively [12]. Since the KEAP1-binding motifs DLG and ETGE are encoded within exon 2 (Table 1), NRF2 isoforms missing this exon are unable to bind KEAP1 and are therefore exceptionally stable [12]. These alternatively spliced NFE2L2 transcripts were shown to be expressed in cells of squamous non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and head and neck squamous carcinoma (HNSC) that do not harbour mutations in the NRF2 pathway [12]. Although their frequency is relatively low - 3.3% (16/481) in NSCLC and 1.5% (6/403) in HNSC - they demonstrate that alternative splicing can provide an additional mechanism contributing to constitutive NRF2 pathway activation.

The functional relevance of the alternative NRF2 promoter remains poorly understood. Using long-read RNA sequencing Otsuki et al. identified the alternative promoter-derived transcript ENST00000397063 (corresponding to NM_001145412.3) as the second most abundant NFE2L2 transcript in primary human lymphoblastoid cells under both basal and stress-induced conditions [20]. The biological rationale for alternative promoter usage and the presence of the corresponding NRF2 isoforms remains to be elucidated. However, by analogy to other transcription factors, such as isoforms of the p53 family [[21], [22], [23]], these variants are likely to further expand the NRF2 regulatory network.

Beyond antioxidant defense: NRF2 as a systems-level integrator

NRF2 regulates genes that contain the Antioxidant Response Element (ARE), or the Electrophile Response Element (EpRE), in their promoter sequences. This enhancer likely originated as a regulatory sequence controlling genes involved in phase II detoxification, enabling cells to counteract electrophilic and oxidative stress. Over evolutionary time, the ARE–NRF2 system expanded its role, reflecting the co-option of a stress-response pathway for daily physiological coordination. In mammals, NRF2 coordinates the expression of over 250 genes, involved not only in antioxidant defense but also in xenobiotic detoxification, mitochondrial maintenance, protein quality control, metabolic reprogramming [24,25]. The systems-level physiology of NRF2 can be understood through two principal integrative nodes.

1.The Metabolism-ROS Nexus: NRF2 transcriptionally upregulates genes for NADPH production (e.g., glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase) and glutathione synthesis. This directly couples the redox defense capacity to the cellular energy and nutrient status, ensuring detoxification is fueled by available resources. A molecular checkpoint linking nutrient signaling to NRF2 activity is Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3 beta (GSK3β) which phosphorylates NRF2 within its Neh6 domain, thereby promoting KEAP1-independent proteasomal degradation of NRF2 [15,17,26]. During nutrient abundance and insulin signalling, activation of the PI3K–AKT pathway inhibits GSK3β, allowing nuclear accumulation and transcriptional activity of NRF2. Notably, the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), activated also by energy depletion, also leads to GSK3 inhibition, or directly activates the NRF2 phosphorylation, adapting NRF2 activity in response to any change in energy or nutrient supply [[27], [28], [29]] (Fig. 3A).

As said above, the human NFE2L2 gene, exhibits additional complexity, producing multiple isoforms via alternative promoters and splicing events [12]. Isoforms lacking exon 2, encoding KEAP1-binding motifs, escape degradation, resulting in constitutive NRF2 activation, as observed in certain carcinomas [12]. This molecular versatility allows cells to fine-tune NRF2 activity according to developmental stage, metabolic state, and stress exposure.

Such intricate regulation suggests that NRF2 functions as a physiological rheostat rather than an emergency responder, constantly modulating redox tone and cellular content to preserve fitness. Its activation represents an investment in cellular maintenance, a trade-off against immediate energy expenditure for biosynthesis or growth. This ability to adapt (also to meet sex/gender differences) is well reflected under physiological conditions (e.g.: sensitive time-windows during development or in the transition from adulthood to middle-age and senescence) or may be observed in the context of overt pathologies as the attempt to counteract an insult [31,32] (Fig. 3B).

From oxidative stress to redox regulation: a paradigm shift

The classical definition of oxidative stress as a harmful imbalance has guided decades of research. Contemporary redox biology reframes ROS as essential signalling intermediates. This aligns with an evolutionary perspective: molecules initially perceived as threats often become co-opted for regulation. Redox proteomics reveal that reversible oxidative modifications in proteins like KEAP1 and Phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) dynamically modulate transcriptional and metabolic networks [[33], [34], [35], [36]]. Both oxidative and reductive imbalances disrupt this fine-tuned equilibrium. The principle of hormesis is central here: low-level stressors can activate cytoprotective pathways, enhancing resilience [37].

From an evolutionary standpoint, hormetic triggers (exercise, fasting, phytochemicals) mimic ancestral "signals of vitality" in the form of the environmental cues that predicted the need for enhanced defense and repair [38]. Examples include moderate physical exercise [39,40], intermittent fasting [41,42], and phytochemicals such as sulforaphane [43,44], curcumin [45,46], and resveratrol [47,48]. These low-level stressors engage NRF2 through electrophilic or mild oxidative triggers, inducing a state of "adaptive homeostasis" where the capacity to maintain homeostasis is transiently expanded. As a result, cells induce a temporary stress response that boosts detoxification, mitochondrial repair, and anti-inflammatory pathways, laying the groundwork for longer-term resilience.

This updated model advocates for targeted modulation of redox-sensitive pathways, such as NRF2, rather than indiscriminate antioxidant use. However, the absence of standardized clinical biomarkers for “redox resilience” remains a critical bottleneck to translation [24]. In this context, the identification of “redox resilience biosignatures” (i.e. specific clusters of the most relevant and sensitive redox biomarkers) on an individual-basis has the potential not only to monitor redox health, but also to advance precision medicine providing a useful tool to tailor lifestyle intervention and also NRF2-based therapies ultimately leading to meaningful changes for the person and the whole health system.

From cellular resilience to healthspan: NRF2 in aging and disease prevention

Aging can be viewed as a progressive decline in adaptive homeostasis, resulting in decreased ability to efficiently respond to stress. NRF2 lies at the core of this adaptive machinery. Its activity declines with age, weakening detoxification capacity, mitochondrial quality control, and immune regulation [37,49]. Accordingly, exogenous mild NRF2 activation extends healthspan in model organisms by maintaining redox equilibrium, dampening inflammation, and enhancing stress tolerance [[50], [51], [52]]. This supports the "redox stress" theory of aging, where a progressive failure of redox signaling and control contributes to the aging phenotype. In practice, transient NRF2 activation by a mild stressor triggers endogenous defense programs that ultimately strengthen the organism's resilience and promote longevity [50]. Indeed, lifestyle factors such as regular exercise or phytochemical-rich diets induce subtle oxidative or electrophilic stress, which upregulates NRF2 regulated cytoprotective pathways, thereby conferring long-term health benefits via repeated hormetic adaptation [37].

These findings support the concept that NRF2 activation represents an evolutionarily conserved mechanism of cytoprotection and longevity. Yet, chronic overactivation is deleterious, with pathological phenotypes depending on the context and tissue: oncogenic NFE2L2 or KEAP1 somatic mutations can promote tumor survival under metabolic stress [53], and KEAP1 germline loss-of-function can cause esophageal epithelial hyperkeratosis [54] or goiter [55], depending on the degree of NRF2 activation. Thus, NRF2 represents a context-dependent regulator of redox homeodynamics, as both severe deficiency and sustained hyperactivation of NRF2 may disrupt redox signaling networks, leading to pathological outcomes [[53], [54], [55]].

The key to solving this paradox lies in timed activation and/or context-dependent NRF2 modulation using lifestyle, pharmacological, and/or nutraceutical interventions that sustain resilience without favoring reductive or oncogenic states. Remaining challenges in realizing the full clinical potential of NRF2 as a therapeutic target have already been reviewed [56,57].

Toward an evolution-informed preventive medicine framework: from “sickcare” to “healthcare”

The growing burden of non-communicable diseases in aging societies calls for a transition from reactive “sickcare” to proactive, resilience-centered healthcare [58]. From an evolutionary perspective, NRF2 can be understood not merely as a regulator of antioxidant genes, but as a conserved environmental sensor shaped by fluctuating nutrient availability, intermittent physical exertion, exposure to plant-derived electrophiles, and variable ecological and social contexts. Modern lifestyles, characterized by sedentarism, caloric excess, environmental monotony, and chronic psychosocial strain, may therefore represent a mismatch with ancestral cues that historically tuned NRF2-dependent adaptive homeodynamics. Within this framework, monitoring NRF2 pathway functionality and elasticity may provide a dynamic readout of adaptive capacity and redox resilience [24,59]. Preventive strategies should aim not at suppressing reactive species indiscriminately, but at restoring physiologically coherent patterns of NRF2 activation.

Regular physical activity represents one such evolutionarily conserved signal. Exercise-induced metabolic and redox fluctuations transiently activate NRF2-dependent programs that support mitochondrial maintenance, proteostasis, and metabolic flexibility [60,61]. Repeated moderate activation may expand adaptive homeostasis over time, strengthening organismal robustness.

Nutrition constitutes a second key axis. Dietary phytochemicals, including polyphenols, flavonoids, and marine-derived metabolites such as mycosporine-like amino acids (MAAs), interact with the KEAP1-NRF2 system and may function as xenobiotic signals historically associated with environmental variability [[62], [63], [64], [65]]. Rather than merely counteracting molecular damage, these compounds may engage conserved signaling circuits that enhance preparedness and stress adaptability.

Beyond metabolic inputs, psychosocial and cognitive dimensions likely intersect with NRF2-regulated networks through neuroendocrine-immune crosstalk. Social engagement, cognitive stimulation, and stress-buffering behaviors modulate inflammatory and metabolic tone, indirectly influencing redox-sensitive transcriptional programs [[66], [67], [68]]. Although mechanistic resolution remains incomplete, integrating these domains aligns with a systems-level view of NRF2 as a nodal regulator connecting behavior, environment, and cellular physiology.

In this context, the elasticity of the NRF2 pathway could serve as a functional biomarker of health resilience. Stratifying individuals according to their adaptive response capacity would enable personalized, primarily lifestyle-based interventions. Importantly, many NRF2-modulating strategies are scalable and accessible, offering particular relevance for low-resource settings. Therefore, the lack of standardized, clinically applicable biomarkers capturing redox network dynamics remains a critical translational bottleneck [24,59].

Concluding perspective

NRF2 stands as a molecular embodiment of evolutionary innovation and biological trade-offs. Emerging at the intersection of rising atmospheric oxygen and increasing metabolic complexity, it enabled the transition from constitutive defense to inducible adaptive regulation. In modern humans, it operates at the crossroads of metabolism, proteostasis, inflammation, and aging.

Redefining NRF2 as a guardian of adaptive homeodynamics shifts redox biology from a damage-centered to resilience-centered paradigms with evolutionary coherence. With this view, NRF2 is not only a therapeutic target in disease but also a modulator of lifelong adaptive capacity.

Future research should focus on elucidating the temporal dynamics of NRF2 activity across life stages, its tissue-and sex-specific regulation, and its integration with metabolic and inflammatory networks. It will be also crucial to develop interventions that sustain adaptability throughout life. In promoting prolonged physiological resilience during aging, NRF2 may function as the nexus facilitating the convergence of healthspan with lifespan, so that the years of healthy living progressively approach the total years lived.
