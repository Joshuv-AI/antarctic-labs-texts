Pre-sowing proline seed priming enhances germination, emergence, and antioxidant capacity of pepper under chilling stress
Authors: Aygül Karaca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Low temperature stress is a major limiting factor for seed germination and uniform seedling establishment in warm-season crops, particularly during early sowing periods. Exogenous application of osmo-protective compounds is a promising strategy to alleviate the adverse effects of abiotic stress on seed germination and early seedling establishment. Proline, a multifunctional amino acid, plays a key role in osmotic adjustment, membrane protection, and antioxidant defense. This study investigated the effects of pre-sowing proline seed priming on germination and emergence performance of pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) seeds under chilling stress conditions. Pepper seeds were primed with proline solutions at 0 (control), 5, 10, and 15 mM for 24 h, air-dried for 24 h, and subjected to germination and emergence tests under optimum (25 °C) and chilling stress (15 °C) conditions. Germination and emergence traits were assessed together with biochemical parameters, including thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content, antioxidant enzyme activities, total chlorophyll content, and endogenous proline levels. Chilling stress significantly reduced germination and emergence in untreated seeds. Proline priming markedly mitigated these negative effects, with 15 mM proline resulting in the highest germination and emergence percentages under chilling stress. Proline-treated seedlings exhibited lower TBARS and H₂O₂ levels, indicating reduced oxidative damage, along with enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, total chlorophyll content, and endogenous proline accumulation. Pre-sowing proline priming effectively improves pepper seed germination and emergence under chilling stress by strengthening antioxidant defense and reducing lipid peroxidation.

Introduction

The main abiotic stress factors encountered throughout plant life include salinity, drought, heat stress, heavy metal toxicity, UV-B radiation, and ozone, and these stress factors have detrimental impacts on the growth of plants, productivity, and product quality [1, 2]. Abiotic stress conditions can cause losses of 50–70% in plant yield [3]. Chilling stress, in particular, is a significant factor that has a direct inhibitory effect on seed germination, plant growth, and yield and is responsible for a significant portion of product losses. In pepper, chilling stress can cause significant reductions in germination, growth, and yield, particularly under field conditions in temperate regions, leading to notable yield losses [4]. In response to chilling stress, flowers activate defense mechanisms by adapting through common and biochemical regulation. It also triggers numerous defense mechanisms such as cell division, cell configuration modification, a list of anti-oxidants and osmo-protectants, gene expression regulation, and protein production [5, 6].

Various strategies are being developed to eliminate the adverse consequences of abiotic stress on plant yield and to improve performance zones under stressful conditions. Recent studies, in particular, highlight that external plant protection treatments are a promising approach in reducing stress-induced and biochemical damage. These protective agents are highlighted in three main categories: osmolytes, sugars and sugar alcohols, ammonium pyrethrum, and amino acids such as proline [7]. Studies using physiological, biochemical, and procedural methods clearly show that osmolytes play an important role in the defense systems against applied stress [8]. Furthermore, under stressful conditions, the accumulation of osmolytes in the plant helps maintain turgor by enabling water uptake through osmotic regulation [9].

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are produced in cells by abiotic stressors; as a result, these ROS oxidize proteins, lipids, and DNA. However, proline acts as a powerful osmo-protectant and signaling molecule to mitigate this damage. It accumulates in the cytosol under stress to maintain osmotic balance and protect protein and membrane structures. That is also functions as an effective antioxidant, metal chelator, and ROS scavenger, reducing oxidative damage [10–13]. Proline accumulation and improved stress tolerance are positively correlated, which can be explained by these complex characteristics [14, 15]. Therefore, exogenous proline application stands out as an effective strategy to increase tolerance by activating plants’ stress defense mechanisms [16, 17]. Various methods exist for exogenous proline application, the most common and effective of which are foliar spraying and seed priming [18].

Seed priming is known as a pretreatment technique that enables plants to develop a more effective defense response against abiotic stresses. This technique triggers pre-germination metabolic activation through controlled dehydration, allowing the plant to adapt more quickly and strongly under stress. It also enables the plant to detect environmental stress signals in advance, thus activating its defense mechanisms [19]. Use of osmo-protectant compounds such as proline as priming agents is a promising strategy for enhancing plant stress tolerance [20, 21]. Proline is defined as an amino acid with multifunctional roles under stress conditions. One of the key mechanisms of proline is the regulation of the balance of ROS. Proline exerts its effects both by directly scavenging free radicals and by enhancing the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, and peroxidase. Additionally, it has been reported that proline acts as a signaling molecule that interacts with stress response pathways [4]. Protecting cellular activities by detoxifying ROS and preserving the integrity of membranes, proteins, and intracellular structures are the key roles of this significant bio-molecule [20]. Proline, which accumulates at high levels in response to a variety of abiotic stressors, serves as a radical scavenger and osmotic agent to prevent cell damage [22]. Numerous studies in the literature, particularly in seed priming applications, have emphasized that proline not only protects plants from various stresses but also accelerates the recovery process after stress [18]. For example, exogenous proline applications have been shown to reduce salt stress in a wide range of plant species [23–27], low temperature [28], heavy metals [15, 29], and drought [30]. Overall, seed priming represents a critical agronomic strategy that integrates physiological and biochemical preconditioning, thereby improving crop performance under environmental stress conditions and enhancing field establishment efficiency.

Pepper is a warm-season vegetable and requires an optimum temperature of 25–30 °C for rapid germination. In direct sowing into cold soil in early spring, germination may be irregular and delayed. This leads to an uneven plant population, yield loss, and delayed harvest. Under early spring sowing conditions, chilling stress further exacerbates these effects by reducing seedling establishment and stand uniformity in temperate growing regions [31]. Therefore, rapid and uniform seedling emergence is critical for ideal plant development [32]. It is hypothesized that proline seed priming improves germination and seedling emergence of pepper under chilling stress by enhancing antioxidant defense mechanisms. The aim of this study is to investigate the effects of different proline treatments applied to pepper seeds before sowing on the germination and emergence performance of pepper seeds under chilling stress conditions and to evaluate the potential of proline to prevent stress-induced damage during this early development stage.

Materials and methods

Proline application

In this study, seeds of the ‘Sera Demre’ pepper variety, obtained through open pollination from a seed company in Istanbul, were used. Pepper seeds were soaked in water containing 1% sodium hypo-chlorite for ten minute to destroy the microorganisms that could be carried on the seeds, washed in pure water, and dried superficially on a paper towel for 2 h under room conditions. The initial seed moisture content determination was carried out according to ISTA [33] rules and found to be 8.45%. The seeds were put in 20 × 30 × 5 cm aluminum containers with a double layer of blotting paper soaked in pure water containing 0, 5, 10, and 15 mM proline solutions, and 20 mL of solution was applied per container to ensure uniform imbibition [32]. Proline treatments were done 25 °C for priming and the seeds were then left in dark for 24 h. After priming, the seeds were rinsed for 1 min under running water and left to dry on paper towels for 24 h under room conditions (20–22 °C and 50–60% relative humidity). Subsequently, seeds were stored in airtight bags at 4 °C in darkness until use for the germination and emergence tests. Germination test

Seeds were soaked in ten ml proline solutions prepared at four different concentrations [0 (control), 5, 10, and 15 mM] for 24 h, thoroughly dried, and each application was repeated four times, using fifty seeds in each repetition. Seeds taken for germination were placed in petri dishes and placed in an incubator in a dark environment where two germination temperatures (15℃ and 25℃) were maintained. For germination, root emergence (2 mm) was considered enough [33]. Until the number of germinated seeds stabilized, the number of germinated seeds was counted every day. The germination test under stress conditions lasted 21 days. The final germination percentage (FGP, %), mean germination time (MGT), germination uniformity (G10-90), root length (cm), and vigor index were done as described previously [34] at the conclusion of the germination test. All parameters were calculated using Germination Analysis software (Version 1.0).

Seedling emergence test

To assess the impact of proline applications on the germination performance of pepper seeds, four different concentrations (0 (control), 5, 10, and 15 mM) were used. Seeds were soaked in 10 ml proline solutions prepared at 4 different concentrations for 24 h, thoroughly dried, and each application was set up with four replicates, each replicate containing 40 seeds. For the stress application, the pots were placed in climate chambers where 25 °C and 15 °C conditions were maintained after adding sufficient water. White LED lights with a light intensity of 225 µmol m⁻²s⁻¹ were used to illuminate the climatic chambers for 16 h every day. During the emergence test, additional watering was performed with the application solutions if the surface of the peat dried out. Seedling emergence was accepted as the appearance of the hypocotyl spread on the soil surface. Emerging seedlings on the soil surface were counted daily, and the counts were continued regularly until the emergence number stabilized. The emergence test lasted 33 days under chilling stress application. The final emergence percentage (FEP, %), mean emergence time (MET), and emergence uniformity (E10−90) were measured at conclusion of seedling emergence test. The fresh weights of the above-ground parts of the seedlings (g plant− 1) were also determined. The leaves of these seedlings were stored at -80 °C and later used in the analyses described below.

Chlorophyll and carotenoid analysis

To minimize light exposure, fresh leaf tissue (0.25 g) was placed in aluminum foil-covered Falcon tubes. Eight milliliters of 80% acetone were added, and samples were homogenized thoroughly. A clean extract was obtained by filtering the homogenate using Whatman No. 42 filter paper. Absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically at 480, 645, and 663 nm. Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoid contents were calculated according to the equations of Lichtenthaler and Wellburn [35] and expressed as mg/g fresh weight (FW):

Thiobarbituric Acid Reactive Substances (TBARS) content

0.25 g of plant material was homogenized with 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged for five minutes at 6000 rpm to determine the TBARS level. Four milliliters of a 20% TCA solution with 0.5% Thio barbituric acid (TBA) were added to one milliliter of the supernatant. After 30 min of incubation at 95 °C in a water bath, the mixture was moved to an ice bath. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content

H2O2 was determined using the procedure outlined by Özden et al. [37]. In a porcelain mortar that was chilled on ice, fresh leaf sample (0.25 g) was extracted using 3 mL of 0.1% TCA and then put into two mL Eppendorf tubes. The next a ten-minute centrifugation at 4 °C and 10,000 g in a chilled centrifuge, 5 mL tubes were progressively filled with 100 µL of supernatant, 100 µL of K-P buffer solution, and 1.5 mL of potassium iodide (KI). The samples were briefly mixed on a vortex, and then a spectrophotometer was used to measure their absorbance at 390 nm. When preparing the blank sample, pure water was used instead of supernatant, and the other steps were performed identically. The standard curve was created using solutions containing H₂O₂ at different concentrations, and the H₂O₂ content of the samples was calculated as nm/g FW.

Proline content

The proline content of the samples was calculated using the Bates et al. [38] method. As a result, roughly 0.25 g of fresh leaf material was homogenized using 3% 5-sulfosalicylic acid. The filtered homogenate was then mixed with glacial acetic acid and acid ninhydrin, and it was then stored for an hour at 100 °C in a water bath. After the samples were extracted with toluene and placed in an ice bath, the absorbance of the toluene fraction aspirated from the liquid phase was measured at 520 nm using a spectrophotometer. Using a calibration curve, the proline concentration was computed and found to be nmol/g fresh weight.

Antioxidant enzymes activity

The method outlined in Özden et al. [37] was used to prepare the enzyme extracts. The Bradford [39] method was utilized to calculate the total protein content. Superoxide dismutase (SOD; EC 1.15.1.1) was measured using the method Gong et al. [40] by evaluating the suppression of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photoreduction and expressed as U/mg protein, ascorbate peroxidase (APX; EC 1.11.1.11) was measured using the Nakano and Asada method [41] by monitoring the decrease in absorbance due to ascorbate oxidation at 290 nm and expressed as U/mg protein, and catalase (CAT; EC 1.11.1.6) enzyme activity was measured using the method modified according to Çakmak et al. [42] by following the decomposition of H₂O₂ at 240 nm and expressed as U/mg protein. Peroxidase (POD; E.C. 1.11.1.7) was determined by using the Dolatabadian et al. [43]. The guaiacol oxidation was monitored at 470 nm and expressed in U/mg protein.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was conducted as a factorial arrangement in a completely randomized design with four replications, where the factors included stress and proline applications. Experimental data were analyzed using JMP software (Version 13; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess the effects of treatments and their interactions at a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Tukey’s HSD test was used to compare means.

Results

Effects of proline applications on germination parameters under optimum and chilling stress conditions

The effect of different proline applications on the FGP, MGT, G10-90, root length, and vigor index under optimum (25 °C) and stress conditions (15 °C) is presented in Table 1. Both proline application and temperature had statistically significant effects on all evaluated germination parameters. FGP was significantly impacted by both proline applications and chilling stress (p < 0.001). FGP decreased from 92.9% at 25 °C to 87.4% under chilling stress. Seeds treated with all proline concentrations showed higher FGP values than untreated seeds (0 mM). The interaction between chilling stress and proline applications was found to be significant, with proline applications increasing germination percentage at both temperatures. The lowest germination percentage was observed at 15 °C with 0 mM proline application (80.0%), while the highest values were determined at 25 °C with 10 and 15 mM proline applications (94.0%). Chilling stress negatively affected the MGT of seeds, increasing the MGT from an average of 5.90 days in seeds germinated at 25 °C to 11.90 days under stress. As a result of the proline applications, the 1 mM, 5 mM, and 10 mM applications positively affected the germination rates of the seeds and reduced the MGT value of the seeds treated with 0 mM proline, which was 9.56 days, to 8.79, 8.70, and 8.60 days, respectively. When examining the interaction effect of chilling stress and proline applications, no difference was observed between proline applications in terms of MGT below 25 °C. However, under chilling stress conditions, the germination time was lowest in seeds treated with 0 mM proline (MGT = 12.30 days) while the germination time was highest (MGT = 11.71 days) in seeds treated with 15 mM proline. Additionally, chilling stress negatively affected seed germination uniformity, increasing it from an average of 2.69 days under optimum conditions to 4.44 days under stress. As a result of proline applications, seed germination uniformity was positively affected, and the G10-90 value of seeds treated with 0 mM proline decreased from 3.86 days to 3.27 days. In pepper seeds, proline applications and chilling stress were found to be the main significant effects on root length and vigor index, while the interaction between the factors was not statistically significant. Root length, which was 7.09 cm at 25 °C, decreased to 1.06 cm under the effect of chilling stress. Among the different concentrations of proline applications, the shortest root length was obtained from the 0 mM application (3.45 cm), while the root lengths of seeds treated with 5 mM, 10 mM, and 15 mM proline were significantly higher (4.11 cm, 4.35 cm, and 4.40 cm, respectively). Under optimum conditions, the vigor index, which was 659.06, decreased to 94.34 due to the effect of chilling stress. Among proline applications, the lowest vigor index was obtained from the 0 mM application, while seeds treated with 5 mM, 10 mM, and 15 mM proline had higher vigor index.

Effects of proline applications on seedling emergence parameters

The results showed that under both optimum and chilling stress circumstances, proline treatments improved total emergence parameters (Table 2). It has been determined that the stress factor has a notable impact on the FEP of the seeds, and that the average rate of 83.9 under optimum conditions decreased to 79.1 due to the effect of stress. Furthermore, proline applications were found to have a notable impact on the germination rate, and seeds treated with all proline concentrations were found to have a higher germination rate compared to the 0 mM application. On the other hand, 15 °C negatively affected the emergence rates of seedlings, and the mean emergence time of 15.17 days at 25 °C increased to 23.30 days due to the effect of stress. The proline applications positively affected the emergence times of seedlings, and the MET value, which was 20.22 days at 0 mM application, decreased to a range of 18–64 days − 19.04 days. When the effects of proline applications below 15 °C and 25 °C on E10-90 were analyzed, it was determined that only chilling stress had a notable impact on seedling emergence uniformity, while the stress and proline applications’ interaction impact was not significant. At 25 °C, E10-90, which was 7.28 days, increased due to the effect of stress and reached 12.29 days. On the other hand, it was determined that stress had a notable impact on seedling fresh weight, and the average seedling fresh weight of 2.91 g at 25 °C decreased to 1.90 g due to the effect of stress. Furthermore, it was determined that the weight of seedlings obtained from seeds treated with proline ranged between 2.41 g and 2.56 g and was higher compared to the 0 mM application (2.20 g).

Effects of proline applications on chlorophyll, carotenoid, TBARS, H₂O₂, and Proline Content

The effects of proline applications on chlorophyll, carotenoid, TBARS, H₂O₂, and endogenous proline contents are presented in Table 3. The highest chlorophyll content was recorded in seedlings obtained from seeds treated with 15 mM proline. Chlorophyll content increased from 39.56 mg/g FW under optimum conditions to 41.08 mg/g FW under chilling stress. Carotenoid content was not significantly affected by stress, proline application, or their interaction. TBARS content was highest in untreated seedlings and significantly decreased with all proline treatments. Chilling stress increased TBARS and H₂O₂ contents, while proline application significantly reduced both parameters. TBARS content, determined to be 0.49 nmol/g FW on average under optimal conditions, increased to 0.60 nmol/g FW under the effect of chilling stress. H2O2 content, which is 0.27 nmol/g FW on average under optimum conditions, increased to 0.33 nmol/g FW due to the effect of stress. Endogenous proline content increased under chilling stress and with increasing proline concentrations, with the highest accumulation observed in proline-treated seedlings under stress conditions. Under optimum conditions, the proline content of 93.7 nmol/g FW increased to 127.7 nmol/g FW due to the effect of stress. When the effect of proline applications at increasing concentrations on proline content was examined, it was observed that seeds treated with all proline concentrations had higher proline content compared to the 0 mM (89.8 nmol/g FW) application. When examining the interaction effect of stress and proline concentrations, it was observed that the highest proline content was obtained under chilling stress conditions from all proline applications, while the lowest proline content was obtained under optimal conditions from the 0 mM (84.3 nmol/g FW) proline application.

Discussion

One of the most dynamic and intricate stages of physiological metabolism in the life cycle of a plant is seed germination. The transformation of the seed embryo from a dormant to an active development state is the fundamental process of germination. In other words, the germination process starts when the radicle emerges from the seed coat, the embryo cells start to lengthen, and the seed absorbs water. At this stage, the embryo cells begin to elongate and cell division is activated; as a result, the rootlet (radicle) and young shoot grow rapidly, and seedling development occurs. Low temperatures, droughts, and oxygen shortages are just a few of the variables that might affect the entire seed germination process, which is extremely susceptible to external environmental circumstances [44, 45]. The vigor of plant seedlings is determined by the quality of seed germination, and the ability of the seedlings to enter the ideal growing medium for their development is determined by the germination period. This has a significant impact on yield [46]. In addition to slowing down seed cell membrane repair, low temperatures also degrade the restored membrane structure and produce phase changes in membrane lipids. This leads to leakage of solutes out of the cell, disruption of respiratory metabolism, weakening of enzyme activities, inhibition of substance turnover, and accumulation of toxic substances. As a result, germination slows down or may not occur at all. In this study, under chilling stress, the germination and emergence percentage, radicle length, vigor index, and seedling weight of pepper seeds were significantly reduced. In addition, stress negatively affected the average germination and emergence times of the seeds. This shows that under chilling stress, the metabolic activities of the seeds are negatively affected and germination is suppressed [32, 47, 48]. When plants are under stress, they create high amounts of proline, which is one of the most efficient compatible osmotic regulators. Studies investigating the regulatory effect of proline on plants under different stress conditions show that exogenous proline applications can effectively mitigate the negative effects of stress-induced plant growth and the suppressive effect of chilling stress on seed germination [49]. The inhibitory effect of low-temperature stress on pepper seed germination can be effectively mitigated by soaking seeds in various concentrations of proline solutions. In our study results, seeds treated with proline had a higher germination percentage compared to the 0 mM (control) application, increasing from 85.5% to 93.0% (an increase of approximately 8.8%). This finding indicates that proline applications can have germination-promoting effects [50, 51]. Furthermore, this supports the hypothesis that proline applications can activate the antioxidant defense systems of seeds and thus increase stress tolerance [28, 52]. Similar studies in the literature also support our research results. For example, Ashraf and Foolad [16] investigated the effect of proline applications on seed germination, seedling development, and ion balance; while Meena et al. [53] emphasized the importance of proline protective mechanisms against a variety of environmental stresses. Rao et al. [54–56] reported that seed priming applications and silicic/humic acid-based priming methods significantly improved germination characteristics, seedling viability, nutrient uptake, and stress tolerance under abiotic stress conditions. Similarly, Rao et al. [57, 58] highlighted that priming applications improved germination and reduced the negative effects of salinity stress in lentils. Furthermore, Chauhan et al. [59] and Bhardwaj et al. [60] observed that biochar, humic acid, and green priming strategies increased seed quality, early growth, and antioxidant activity.

The results of this study reveal that chilling stress significantly suppresses the germination physiology and seedling formation of pepper seeds. The delay and reduction in emergence rate can be attributed to the slowing of metabolic processes and disruption of membrane stability by low temperatures. In contrast, the improvement of emergence parameters and seedling weight by exogenously applied proline under both optimum and stressful conditions has been assessed as a result of the osmolytic and antioxidant functions of this amino acid. Proline regulation of cellular water balance, thus maintaining embryo viability and minimizing oxidative stress, stands out as possible mechanisms behind the increase in emergence performance [5, 51, 61, 62]. These data confirm the potential of pre-planting proline applications to reduce the adverse impacts of chilling stress on emergence parameters.

Proline is considered one of the most common and important osmotic regulators [27]. Studies in the literature report a significant increase in the accumulation of osmotic regulators such as proline, soluble sugars, and soluble proteins in plants following low-temperature stress. This increase contributes to the balancing of intracellular and extracellular osmotic pressure, preserving the stability of the membrane, and the upkeep of ion balance. In this study, after proline application to pre-planting seeds, seedlings subjected to seedling emergence tests under optimum and stress circumstances showed significant decreases in H2O2 and TBARS content, while chlorophyll, carotenoid, and proline content increased. Total chlorophyll content, which was 39.56 mg/g FW at 25 °C, increased to 41.08 mg/g FW under the effect of the stress (an increase of approximately 3.8%). This increase is noteworthy, contrary to the commonly held view in the literature. Although chilling stress generally reduces chlorophyll content, the increase observed in plants treated with proline can be interpreted as an indication of this amino acid protective and even stimulating role in photosynthetic capacity [63, 64]. This effect is thought to be related to proline’s ability to reduce ROS accumulation and stabilize chloroplast membranes under stress conditions. As an indicator of lipid peroxidation, the TBARS content, determined as an average of 0.49 nmol/g FW under optimum conditions, increased to 0.60 nmol/g FW under the effect of stress (an increase of approximately 22.4%). This is consistent with reports by Hasanuzzaman et al. [65] that chemical and natural applications reduce oxidative damage by strengthening the antioxidant systems of plants. Karaca and Yakupoğlu [48], stated that chilling stress causes an increase in TBARS levels in plants, reflecting the severity of the stress. Based on these results, it can be said that proline applications play a protective role in reducing oxidative damage due to chilling stress. This protective effect may be related to the ability of proline to act as an osmo-protectant and to stabilize cellular structures under stress conditions, thereby limiting stress-induced metabolic disturbances. Furthermore, the H2O2 content of the tissues decreased significantly as a result of the proline applications. This finding reveals that proline application alleviates oxidative stress by reducing the accumulation of H₂O₂, one of the primary components of ROS [44]. This decrease could be linked to proline-treated plants producing fewer ROS and/or scavenging more reactive oxygen species. The increase in proline content under stress in this study reinforces the view that proline accumulation is one of the fundamental physiological adaptation strategies developed by plants against abiotic stress conditions [11, 12, 18]. This accumulation is generally accepted to contribute to osmotic regulation and the protection of cellular macromolecules, especially under stress conditions.

Increased antioxidant enzyme activity in plants exposed to stress conditions has been reported to be associated with reduced oxidative stress-induced damage [50]. Research shows that when plants are under stress, changes and increases in antioxidant enzyme activity occur, depending on the genetic makeup of the species and variety [6, 28, 66, 67]. Shafiq et al. [26] emphasized that proline is a crucial osmo-regulator, stating that proline plays important roles in eliminating the negative consequences of free radicals and stimulating antioxidant systems of enzymes. Furthermore, exogenous proline application increases antioxidant enzyme activity and strengthens the plant defense system against oxidative stress [68, 69]. In this study, chilling stress generally increased antioxidant enzyme activities, and proline applications, especially depending on the applied concentrations, enhanced these activities. The idea that proline positively affects antioxidant enzyme activities is supported by the existence of variations in antioxidant enzyme activities. Overall, the findings demonstrate that proline seed priming strengthens antioxidant defense systems, reduces oxidative damage, and improves germination and seedling establishment of pepper under chilling stress conditions. This study provides additional insight into the combined effects of chilling stress and proline seed priming on the antioxidant defense response of pepper seedlings.

Conclusions

The results of this study demonstrate that proline application is a physiologically effective complementary strategy for mitigating the adverse effects of chilling stress in pepper. Pre-sowing seed treatment with 15 mM proline significantly improved seed germination and seedling emergence under chilling stress conditions. Proline application shortened emergence time, improved seedling establishment, and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, thereby reducing oxidative damage and stabilizing cellular membranes. These findings suggest that proline seed priming can be an effective agronomic practice for improving pepper establishment during early spring sowing under suboptimal temperature conditions.
