The P4HA1/HIF1α feedback loop modulates endothelial dysfunction in pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Yunwei Chen, Yifei Lv, Meng Yang, Zhiyuan Qian, Xiaomin Jiang, Zihao Jiang, Chaohua Kong, Yue Gu, Yunfei Deng, Shao-Liang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary hypertension is a severe vascular disease characterised by pulmonary arterial remodeling and right heart failure. However, the role of the glycolytic protein P4HA1 in its pathogenesis remains elusive.MethodsRNA-seq analysis was performed on hypoxic human pulmonary artery endothelial cells, and P4HA1 expression was validated in PH animal models. In vitro, P4HA1 knockdown was used to assess its effects on glycolysis, proliferation, and migration. Mechanistic studies included HIF1α transcriptional regulation and P4HA1-mediated HIF1α stabilization via reduced ubiquitination. AAV9-shP4HA1 was employed to assess P4HA1’s function in pulmonary vascular remodeling in vivo.ResultsP4HA1 was significantly upregulated in human pulmonary arterial endothelial cells (HPAECs) exposed to hypoxia. Mechanistic investigations revealed that HIF1α transcriptionally activates P4HA1 expression, while P4HA1 reciprocally stabilizes HIF1α by attenuating its ubiquitin-mediated degradation, thereby establishing a self-amplifying signaling loop. Functional studies demonstrated that P4HA1 knockdown effectively suppressed hypoxia-induced glycolytic reprogramming, inhibited cellular proliferation, and impaired migratory capacity in HPAECs. Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a progressive, life-threatening and currently incurable pulmonary vascular disease [1, 2]. As the disease advances, sustained narrowing of the pulmonary arteries leads to elevated pulmonary arterial pressure and increased pulmonary vascular resistance, ultimately resulting to right heart failure—the primary cause of mortality in PH patients [3, 4]. Pulmonary vascular remodeling represents a pathognomonic feature of pulmonary hypertension (PH), characterized by concomitant endothelial dysfunction and enhanced aerobic glycolysis in pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (PASMCs), which exhibit hallmark pathological behaviors including aberrant proliferation, increased migration, and profound apoptosis resistance [4–7]. Emerging evidence highlights the crucial role of pulmonary arterial endothelial cells (PAECs) dysfunction in the vascular remodeling process [8]. During early PH stages, endothelial injury and apoptosis predominate; however, as the disease progresses, PAECs undergo phenotypic switching marked by excessive proliferation and enhanced migratory capacity [9, 10]. These cellular events demand a rapid and sustained energy supply, which is facilitated by metabolic reprogramming: notably, an upregulation of glycolytic flux [11] coupled with suppressed mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation—a metabolic shift first described in cancer cells as the “Warburg effect” [12]. In PH, increased glycolysis enhances PAEC proliferative capacity [13, 14].

P4HA1, the catalytic subunit of prolyl 4-hydroxylase, is integral to collagen biosynthesis. As an α-ketoglutarate (α-KG)-dependent dioxygenase, P4HA1 hydroxylates proline residues in collagen, utilizing α-KG and oxygen, producing succinate and carbon dioxide as byproducts [15, 16]. Its expression is significantly upregulated in human umbilical vein endothelial cells under hypoxic conditions, where it modulates glycolytic metabolism and angiogenesis via the TET2/FBP1 signaling axis [17]. Beyond endothelial function, P4HA1 has been implicated in various malignancies—such as prostate cancer and oral squamous cell carcinoma-where it can be transcriptionally activated by factors like STAT1 or contribute to chemoresistance and tumor progression through stabilization of HIF1α and activation of related pathways [18]. In breast cancer, P4HA1 contributes to chemoresistance in triple-negative subtypes and enhances the stability of HIF1α [19]. Furthermore, P4HA1 has been reported to facilitate colorectal cancer growth and progression by stabilizing HIF1α and activating the Wnt signaling pathway [20]. Evidence also suggest reciprocal regulation of P4HA1 by HIF1α [21], as seen in glioblastoma, where HIF1α/ATF3 signaling influences P4HA1 and succinate pathways [22]. Despite these findings, the specific function of P4HA1 in PAECs remains largely unexplored and warrants further investigation.

Recent studies emphasize the pivotal role of hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF1α) in PH pathogenesis [23–25]. Under normoxic conditions, HIF1α undergoes hydroxylation at proline residues 402 and 564 by prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD) enzymes, enabling recognition by the von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, leading to ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation [26–30]. In contrast, under hypoxia, PHD activity diminishes, resulting in HIF1α accumulation [22, 31]. During PH progression, PAEC dysfunction characterized by abnormal proliferation and migration involves HIF1α as a central regulatory molecular [32]. For instance, HIF1α promotes glycolytic reprogramming and vascular remodeling via a positive feedback loop with the miR-130/301 family during pulmonary hypertension development [33]. The role of HIF1α in regulating glycolysis in PAECs has been well-documented across multiple studies [34]. Additionally, HIF1α influences other pathological pathways; for example, silencing HIF1α can attenuate hypoxia-induced endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition in HPAECs [35], while it also mediates IL-33–induced proliferation and adhesion in PAECs [36]. Moreover, the ERBB3–HIFs–POSTN signaling axis promotes PAEC proliferation and migration, contributing to pulmonary vascular remodeling [37].

In this study, we identified a significant upregulation of P4HA1 in hypoxia-exposed HPAECs via RNA sequencing. This observation was validated through bioinformatics analyses and complemented in vivo and in vitro experiments. Endothelial-specific knockdown of P4HA1 alleviated PH in vivo, while silencing P4HA1 partially reversed hypoxia-induced endothelial dysfunction in HPAECs in vitro. Mechanistic investigations revealed that the P4HA1–HIF1α axis contributes to endothelial injury under hypoxic conditions. Collectively, our results suggest that P4HA1 represents a potential therapeutic target for PH, offering new insights into its underlying pathogenesis and paving the way for novel treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing First Hospital (DWSY-25084716) and conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines. After one week of acclimatization, twelve eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (purchased from Hangzhou Ziyuan Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.) were randomly assigned to two groups: a normoxia group and a Su5416 plus hypoxia (Su5416 + Hypoxia) group, with six mice in each group. The Su5416 + Hypoxia group was housed in a hypoxia chamber with an oxygen concentration of 10% and received weekly subcutaneous injections of Su5416 (20 mg/kg; HY-10374, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China). Throughout the 4-week experimental period, all mice had free access to food and water.

To achieve in vivo knockdown of P4HA1 in PAECs, an adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9) vector carrying an endothelial cell-specific promoter and shRNA targeting P4HA1 was constructed (Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd.). A total of twenty-four C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 each): Normoxia + AAV9-ENT-NC, Normoxia + AAV9-ENT-shP4HA1, Su5416 + Hypoxia + AAV9-ENT-NC, and Su5416 + Hypoxia + AAV9-ENT-shP4HA1. Mice in the P4HA1 knockdown groups received a tail vein injection of 1 × 10¹² viral genomes (V.g) of the AAV9-shP4HA1 vector, while control groups received an equivalent dose of control AAV9. One week post-injection, mice in the Su5416 + Hypoxia groups were subjected to the 4-week hypoxia and Su5416 treatment regimen described above.

Echocardiography

Following induction of isoflurane anesthesia, mice were placed in a supine position on the procedure table and the anterior chest hair was removed. Cardiac function was assessed using a high-frequency small-animal ultrasound system (Vevo 2100). Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) was measured in M-mode, while pulmonary artery hemodynamics were evaluated using pulsed-wave Doppler to determine pulmonary acceleration time (PAT) and pulmonary ejection time (PET).

Right heart catheterization (RHC)

Right heart catheterization was carried out as previously described [38]. After induction of anesthesia, mice were positioned and secured on the operating table, and fur over the anterior chest was shaved. Following endotracheal intubation, a small incision was made in the left precordial region to fully expose the heart. A pre-calibrated right heart catheter connected to a physiological recording system was inserted into the right ventricle to measure and record right ventricular pressure waveforms.

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining

Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, then dehydrated, cleared, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5-µm slices. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on the lung sections. Images of distal pulmonary arterioles were captured using a light microscope. Morphometric analysis was conducted with Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software to measure the wall thickness (WT) and external diameter (ED) of pulmonary arterioles. Pulmonary arterial wall thickness was expressed as a percentage (WT%) and calculated using the formula: WT% = (2 × WT/ED) × 100%.

Cell culture

Human pulmonary artery endothelial cells (HPAECs) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ScienCell, 1001, CA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells within 8 passages were used for experiments. For normoxic conditions, cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Hypoxic conditions were achieved by culturing cells in an atmosphere containing 1% O₂, 5% CO₂, and 94% N₂.

Cell transfection

When cells reached approximately 60–70% confluency, they were transfected with either Si-NC or specific siRNA using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer’s instructions. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with complete medium. Small interfering RNA sequences are listed in Table S1.

RNA sequencing

When HPAECs reached approximately 90% confluence after 24 h under normoxic or hypoxic conditions, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. Three biological replicates per condition were collected and sent for RNA-seq analysis by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China). Additionally, HPAECs treated with Si-NC or Si-P4HA1 under hypoxia for 24 h had their RNA extracted and analyzed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Raw reads were aligned to the human reference genome using HISAT2 (V2.2.4 or V2.2.1), and transcript quantification was performed with RSEM (V1.2.19) or featureCounts (V2.0.6). Differential gene expression was analyzed using DESeq2 (V1.20.0 or V1.42.0) in R (V4.4.2). Additionally, raw data from the GSE163827 dataset retrieved from GEO were analyzed. Genes with p-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| >1 were considered differentially expressed. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to interpret biological functions of the DEGs.

Bioinformatic analysis of microarray datasets

For the GSE160255 dataset, raw data from 6 samples were downloaded from the GEO database, comprising 3 normoxic and 3 hypoxic human pulmonary arterial endothelial cells (HPAECs). After converting probe IDs to gene symbols, we constructed the gene expression matrix. The expression levels of P4HA1 between the two groups were compared using Student’s t-test. For the GSE113439 dataset, which includes 26 human lung tissue samples (11 normal controls and 15 pulmonary hypertension patients), the gene expression matrix was similarly obtained from GEO. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package, with genes considered differentially expressed at p < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| >0.5. Subsequently, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) [39] was conducted using the WGCNA package to identify gene modules significantly associated with pulmonary hypertension.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis

The raw single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (CRA006636) was obtained from the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA; https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa/), comprising lung tissue scRNA-seq data from three normoxic mice and three mice treated with Su5416 under hypoxic conditions. The data were processed and quantified using Cell Ranger software (V8.0) with the GRCm39-2024-A mouse reference genome. Additionally, the GSE169471 dataset was retrieved from the GEO database for integrative analysis. The processed data were imported into R (V4.4.2) for downstream analysis using the Seurat package (V4.4.0). Quality control involved retaining cells with more than 200 detected genes and mitochondrial gene content below 5%. Genes expressed in fewer than three cells were filtered out for further analysis. Data normalization was performed using the SCTransform package (V0.4.1) [40], followed by principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction. Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony package. A shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph was constructed based on selected principal components, and cells were clustered using the Louvain algorithm with a resolution of 0.3. UMAP was then employed for further dimensionality reduction, and cell clusters were manually annotated according to canonical marker genes.

To evaluate P4HA1 pathway activity, the irGSEA package (V2.1.5) [41] was utilized. Additionally, the Scissor package (V2.0.0) [42] was applied to identify subpopulations of human pulmonary artery endothelial cells (HPAECs) within the scRNA-seq data that were strongly associated with the Si-P4HA1 treatment group, providing insights into the transcriptional features related to P4HA1 silencing.

Data independent acquisition quantitative proteomics

HPAECs were treated with either Si-NC or Si-P4HA1. Cell pellets were collected and sent to Benagen Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) for Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA)-based quantitative proteomics analysis. The resulting protein quantification data were imported into R (V4.4.2) for differential expression analysis using the limma package. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the clusterProfiler package (V4.14.6) [43], and visualizations were generated with the enrichplot package (V1.26.6).

Immunofluorescent staining

Cells or frozen lung tissue sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 60 min. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1.5 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min at room temperature. Between steps, samples were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. Images were captured using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal laser scanning microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded mouse lung tissue sections were deparaffinized, and antigen retrieval was performed. Sections were then incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 30 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. After washing with PBS three times, sections were blocked with 5% BSA for 60 min at room temperature. Following additional PBS washes, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody against α-SMA. After washing, sections were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 60 min at room temperature. Following further washes, sections were developed with DAB chromogenic substrate, counterstained with hematoxylin for 3 min, dehydrated, mounted, and images were acquired under a microscope.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and quantified spectrophotometrically. Reverse transcription was performed using the ABScript Neo RT Master Mix with gDNA Remove kit (ABclonal, Wuhan, China). RT-qPCR was conducted using the 2×Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (ABclonal). The relative expression levels were calculated via the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Western blotting

Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1.5 h at room temperature, washed with TBST three times, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (details provided in Table S3). After washing, membranes were incubated with species-appropriate secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL reagents and imaged with the GeneGnome XRQ system (Syngene).

Glycolysis assays

The Seahorse XF96 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies) was used to measure extracellular acidification rate (ECAR). Cells were seeded in XF Pro M microplates and, following siRNA transfection and hypoxic treatment, ECAR was measured via sequential injections of glucose (20 mM), oligomycin (2 µM), and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (50 mM).

Cell cycle analysis

Cells were harvested with 0.25% trypsin, washed with PBS, fixed in 70% ethanol, and stained with propidium iodide (PI). Cell cycle distribution was analyzed by flow cytometry (BECKMAN COULTER CytoFLEX), with excitation at 488 nm.

EdU staining

After treatment, HPAECs were incubated with culture medium containing 10 µmol/L EdU for 2 h. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min, followed by three washes with PBS containing 3% BSA, each for 5 min. Subsequently, the cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min and washed again with PBS containing 3% BSA twice for 5 min each. EdU detection was performed using Click Additive Solution at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. Afterward, cells were washed three times with PBS containing 3% BSA, each for 5 min. Nuclear staining was performed using Hoechst 33,342 at room temperature for 10 min, followed by three washes with PBS (3–5 min each). Finally, images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope and stored for analysis.

Transwell migration assay

Cell migration was assessed using a Transwell chamber with an 8-µm pore size. HPAECs were seeded in the upper chamber of the Transwell, and after the cells adhered to the surface, they were subjected to the respective treatments. The upper chamber was filled with serum-free medium, and the lower chamber contained ECM supplemented with 10% FBS. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, followed by staining with crystal violet at room temperature for 1 h. The cells were then washed with PBS and observed under a microscope. Images were captured and stored for further analysis.

ChIP-PCR

The chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay was performed using a commercial kit (Beyotime, P2078) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by quenching with 125 mM glycine for 5 min at room temperature. After washing with cold PBS containing 1 mM PMSF, cells were lysed in SDS Lysis Buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and subjected to ultrasonication. The lysates were then centrifuged, and the supernatant was diluted with ChIP Dilution Buffer. A small aliquot (20 µL) was saved as input control. The remaining lysate was pre-cleared with Protein A/G Agarose/Salmon Sperm DNA, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with HIF1α antibody (1:100). Immune complexes were collected with Protein A/G Agarose, followed by sequential washing with low-salt, high-salt, LiCl, and TE buffers. Bound chromatin was eluted in Elution Buffer and reverse cross-linked by incubation with 5 M NaCl at 65 °C for 4 h. The resulting DNA was purified and subsequently analyzed by PCR using primers listed in the Supplementary Materials.

Dual-Luciferase reporter assay

After treatment, 293 T cells were subjected to the dual-luciferase reporter assay using the Dual-Lumi™ Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Beyotime, RG088S), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The culture medium was removed, and cells were washed once with PBS. Then, 150 µL of reporter lysis buffer was added to each well, and cells were lysed for approximately 2 min with gentle pipetting to ensure complete lysis. The lysates were transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatants were collected into new tubes. For each sample, 20 µL of cell lysate was mixed with 100 µL of firefly luciferase assay reagent, incubated for 10 min, and the luminescence intensity was measured. Subsequently, Renilla luciferase assay reagent was added, mixed well, incubated for another 10 min, and luminescence was measured again.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Protein A/G magnetic beads were washed three times with TBST and then incubated with the corresponding primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. After incubation, the beads were washed three more times with TBST and subsequently incubated with 1000 µg of protein sample at 4 °C overnight. Following another three washes with TBST, the beads were resuspended in 1× protein loading buffer, thoroughly mixed, and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. The resulting protein samples were then subjected to Western blot analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using unpaired, two-tailed t-tests. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

P4HA1 is upregulated in hypoxic HPAECs

We performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on HPAECs cultured under hypoxia (1% O₂) and normoxia for 24 h. Using criteria of p < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| >1, we identified a total of 3,074 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 1,700 upregulated and 1,374 downregulated genes in the hypoxia group (Fig. 1A and B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these DEGs revealed that the largest group (324 genes) was involved in metabolic pathways, with P4HA1 notably significantly upregulated in hypoxic cells (Fig. 1C and D). We further analyzed the average counts of these 324 genes (Fig. 1E), observing that, besides well-known glycolytic enzymes such as ALDOA and LDHA, P4HA1 exhibited the highest average expression level. To validate these findings, we analyzed the GSE163827 and GSE160255 datasets from the GEO database, which confirmed that P4HA1 expression was significantly increased in hypoxic HPAECs (Fig. 1F and G). We also validated these results through quantitative RT-qPCR (Fig. 1H). Furthermore, immunofluorescence and Western blot analyses demonstrated that hypoxia treatment led to elevated protein levels of P4HA1 (Fig. 1I and J).

P4HA1 is upregulated in PH lung tissues

The GSE113439 dataset from the GEO database contains gene expression profiles of lung tissues from 15 pulmonary hypertension (PH) patients and 11 healthy controls, generated using Affymetrix microarray technology. Differential expression analysis showed that P4HA1 expression was significantly higher in lung tissues from PH patients (Figure S1A, S1B). Additionally, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified a PH-associated turquoise module, within which P4HA1 was included (Figure S1C, S1D). To further investigate, we downloaded the single-cell RNA sequencing dataset CRA006636 from the GSA database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa/). Analysis indicated that P4HA1 expression was also elevated in PAECs derived from lung tissues of mice in the Su5416 plus hypoxia group (Figure S1E, Fig. 1K). To experimentally validate this finding, we established a mouse model of pulmonary hypertension using SU5416 combined with chronic hypoxia. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant increase in P4HA1 protein level in the pulmonary arterial endothelium (Fig. 1L).

P4HA1 in PAECs is involved in the regulation of PH

To investigate the role of P4HA1 in PH, we performed in vivo knockdown of P4HA1 specifically in PAECs by delivering an endothelial cell–targeted AAV9-shP4HA1 vector (Fig. 2A). The efficiency of knockdown was confirmed in PAECs via immunofluorescence (Figure S2). We found that P4HA1 knockdown partially alleviated the decreases in TAPSE and the PAT/PET ratio induced by Su5416 combined with hypoxia (Fig. 2B and C). Additionally, it partially reversed the elevations in right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) and the right ventricular hypertrophy index [RV/(LV + S)] caused by the Su5416 plus hypoxia treatment (Fig. 2D and E). Histopathological analysis further demonstrated that P4HA1 knockdown partially attenuated pulmonary vascular remodeling induced by Su5416 and hypoxia (Fig. 2F and H).

P4HA1 as a modulator of HPAECs dysfunction

To investigate the role of P4HA1 in pulmonary artery endothelial cells (PAECs), we designed three small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting P4HA1 and validated their efficiency by RT-qPCR and Western blotting. Among these, P4HA1-SiRNA#2 was selected for subsequent experiments (Figure S3A, S3B). HPAECs transfected with either Si-NC or Si-P4HA1 were exposed to hypoxia. Following this, RNA-seq analysis was performed, and the GEO dataset GSE169471 was downloaded for single-cell annotation (Fig. 3A). Next, we integrated the RNA-seq data with GSE169471 using the Scissor algorithm (Fig. 3B). The Scissor + cells corresponded to HPAECs transfected with Si-P4HA1. Further analysis with Ro/e revealed that Scissor + HPAECs were predominantly enriched in the control group region (Fig. 3C). Additionally, using the irGSEA package, we calculated the “P4HA1 pathway” score based on differentially expressed genes related to Si-P4HA1 and applied it to the GSE169471 dataset. The results showed a significant difference in the pathway score between the PH group and the control group (Fig. 3D).

Subsequently, we conducted Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA) quantitative proteomics on HPAECs transfected with Si-NC or Si-P4HA1 under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 3E and F). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) indicated that P4HA1 was significantly associated with multiple biological processes, including oxidative phosphorylation, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, glycolysis, cell cycle regulation, DNA replication, and wound healing (Fig. 3H).

P4HA1 knockdown alters hypoxia-induced Glycolysis and proliferative signatures in HPAECs

We successfully silenced P4HA1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels under normoxic and hypoxic conditions using small interfering RNA (siRNA) (Figure S4A, S4B), confirming the effectiveness and reliability of our knockdown approach. Notably, P4HA1 knockdown significantly attenuated the hypoxia-induced increase in glycolytic activity, indicating that P4HA1 may play a key role in the metabolic reprogramming of endothelial cells in response to hypoxic stress (Fig. 4A). Additionally, flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that silencing P4HA1 markedly impeded the G1-to-S phase transition of the cell cycle in hypoxic HPAECs (Fig. 4B), suggesting a regulatory role of P4HA1 in cell cycle progression. Consistent with this, EdU incorporation assays showed a significant reduction in hypoxia-induced endothelial cell proliferation following P4HA1 knockdown (Fig. 4C). These findings collectively indicate that P4HA1 is essential for hypoxia-driven proliferative signaling in HPAECs. Moreover, transwell migration assays revealed that the enhanced migratory capacity of HPAECs under hypoxia was significantly diminished by P4HA1 silencing (Fig. 4D). Together, these data provide strong evidence that P4HA1 facilitates multiple aspects of hypoxia-induced endothelial dysfunction.

HIF1α dependent transcriptional activation of P4HA1 in HPAECs

To explore the mechanism responsible for the significant upregulation of P4HA1 under hypoxic conditions, we employed an online bioinformatics tool [44] to predict potential upstream transcriptional regulators of P4HA1. By intersecting datasets, three candidate transcription factors were identified, with HIF1α emerging as the top candidate (Fig. 5A). To validate this prediction, we conducted transcription factor activity inference analysis using the dorothea R package based on RNA-seq data from normoxic and hypoxic HPAECs. The results indicated a significant activation of HIF1α in hypoxia-treated cells (Fig. 5B). Consistently, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrated robust enrichment of the HIF1 signaling pathway under hypoxia (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis of lung tissues from the GTEx database revealed a positive correlation between P4HA1 and HIF1α expression levels (Fig. 5D), supporting a potential regulatory relationship.

To confirm this at the protein level, Western blot analysis showed concurrent upregulation of HIF1α and P4HA1 in hypoxic PAECs (Fig. 5E). Additionally, silencing HIF1α with siRNA significantly reduced P4HA1 expression at both mRNA (Fig. 5F) and protein levels (Fig. 5G), indicating that HIF1α acts as an upstream regulator of P4HA1 under hypoxic conditions. Using the JASPAR database, we predicted the HIF1α binding motif within the promoter region of P4HA1 (Fig. 5H) and confirmed this interaction through chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-PCR, demonstrating direct binding of HIF1α to the P4HA1 promoter (Fig. 5I and J). To assess the functional significance of this binding, dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed in 293 T cells. Overexpression of HIF1α significantly increased the luciferase activity (Luc/Rluc ratio), indicating transcriptional activation of the P4HA1 promoter. Mutation of the predicted binding site markedly diminished this activity, confirming that the activation depends on this specific motif (Fig. 5K and L). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HIF1α directly binds to the P4HA1 promoter and enhances its transcription in HPAECs.

P4HA1 stabilizes HIF1α protein levels by attenuating its ubiquitination

To explore the potential mechanism by which P4HA1 regulates dysfunction in HPAECs, we conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on proteomic data from hypoxia-treated HPAECs transfected with either Si-NC or Si-P4HA1. The results indicated that P4HA1 knockdown significantly suppressed the HIF1 signaling pathway (Fig. 6A). Additionally, transcription factor activity analysis using the Dorothea R package revealed a notable inhibition of HIF1α activity following Si-P4HA1 treatment (Fig. 6B). We then examined HIF1α protein levels in HPAECs after Si-P4HA1 transfection via Western blot analysis, which showed a significant reduction in HIF1α protein expression upon P4HA1 knockdown (Fig. 6C). Interestingly, RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that HIF1α mRNA levels remained unchanged after Si-P4HA1 treatment (Fig. 6D).

Given that P4HA1 knockdown affected HIF1α protein expression without altering its mRNA, we hypothesized that P4HA1 may influence the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of HIF1α. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays confirmed that the interaction between HIF1α and ubiquitin was enhanced in P4HA1-deficient cells (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, co-treatment with Si-P4HA1 and the proteasome inhibitor MG132 further supported the involvement of P4HA1 in modulating HIF1α ubiquitination and degradation (Fig. 6F).

P4HA1 regulates HIF1α hydroxylation via modulation of the α-KG/Succinate axis

GSEA analysis of the proteomic data indicated that P4HA1 knockdown significantly affected pathways involved in 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism and protein hydroxylation in HPAECs (Fig. 7A and B). As previously reported, hydroxylation of proline residues at positions 402 and 564 in HIF1α is essential for its recognition by the von Hippel-Lindau (pVHL) E3 ubiquitin ligase, which marks HIF1α for subsequent ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [45]. This hydroxylation process is catalyzed by prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs), utilizing oxygen and α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) as cofactors, and producing succinate and CO₂ as byproducts. Consistent with this mechanism, we observed that P4HA1 knockdown resulted in reduced HIF1α protein levels. Notably, supplementation with α-KG further decreased HIF1α protein expression (Fig. 7C), while treatment with succinate increased HIF1α levels (Fig. 7D). We also assessed hydroxylation status of HIF1α by measuring Hydroxy-HIF1α (Pro564), a key marker of its hydroxylation. Si-P4HA1 treatment increased Hydroxy-HIF1α (Pro564) levels, which were further elevated upon α-KG supplementation (Fig. 7E). Conversely, succinate treatment decreased Hydroxy-HIF1α (Pro564) levels (Fig. 7F), supporting the regulatory role of the α-KG/succinate axis in HIF1α hydroxylation and stability.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that in vivo knockdown of P4HA1 in endothelial cells effectively attenuated pulmonary vascular remodeling and alleviated PH. In vitro, silencing P4HA1 in HPAECs significantly inhibited hypoxia-induced increases in glycolysis, proliferation, and migration. Notably, hypoxia-induced upregulation of P4HA1 was mediated by HIF1α, which transcriptionally activates P4HA1 expression. Moreover, increased P4HA1 levels in hypoxic HPAECs regulated HIF1α hydroxylation via the α-ketoglutarate (α-KG)/succinate pathway, reducing HIF1α ubiquitination and stabilizing its protein levels. Collectively, these findings suggest that P4HA1 may serve as a promising therapeutic target for pulmonary hypertension.

P4HA1 has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various cardiovascular diseases. Cao et al. reported that P4HA1 overexpression promotes atherosclerotic plaque expansion [46]. Conversely, Zhao et al. found that silencing P4HA1 significantly inhibited liraglutide-induced hypertrophic proliferation of cardiac fibroblasts (CFs) under high-glucose conditions, highlighting its role in myocardial fibrotic remodeling associated with diabetic cardiomyopathy [47]. Additionally, Xu et al. revealed that P4HA1 enhances post-ischemic angiogenesis by promoting endothelial glycolysis through downregulation of FBP1. In our findings, hypoxia markedly increased P4HA1 mRNA and protein expression in HPAECs. During PH progression, PAECs exhibit increased proliferation and migration—processes that demand heightened energy production. This demand is met through metabolic reprogramming characterized by enhanced glycolysis and suppressed oxidative phosphorylation, akin to the “Warburg effect” originally described in cancer cells [48]. Although previous research mainly focused on this shift in PASMCs, emerging evidence suggests that a similar glycolytic reprogramming occurs in PAECs [9, 49, 50]. P4HA1 has been identified as a glycolysis-associated gene [51], and our RNA-seq data showed that hypoxia significantly upregulates P4HA1 in HPAECs. Functional experiments indicated that P4HA1 knockdown inhibits glycolysis, promotes oxidative phosphorylation, and reduces proliferation and migration in these cells. Additionally, endothelial-specific P4HA1 silencing in vivo reduced pulmonary vascular remodeling and significantly lowered right ventricular systolic pressure in a PH mouse model, supporting its key role in endothelial metabolic regulation during disease development. Our findings establish P4HA1 as a central regulator of glycolytic reprogramming in PAECs. This mechanism appears distinct from well-characterized glycolytic drivers in PASMCs, such as PFKFB3, which has been reported to be upregulated in PASMCs under pulmonary hypertension (PH), enhancing glycolysis, promoting proliferation, and contributing to pulmonary vascular remodeling [52]. In contrast, our study reveals that P4HA1 promotes glycolytic activity and leads to functional dysregulation in PAECs—a process critically involved in PH pathogenesis. Unlike PFKFB3, which acts as a canonical glycolytic regulator in PASMCs, P4HA1 is not a classical enzyme within the core glycolytic pathway. Nevertheless, it exerts a significant regulatory influence on endothelial glycolysis. This discovery deepens our understanding of the mechanisms underlying PAEC dysfunction in pulmonary hypertension.

To understand the mechanism behind P4HA1 upregulation under hypoxia, we used bioinformatic tools to identify upstream transcription factors, with HIF1α emerging as a primary candidate. HIF1α is a central mediator of hypoxic responses and has been extensively studied in PH pathogenesis. For example, FUNDC1 promotes pulmonary vascular remodeling by activating ROS-HIF1α signaling and enhancing PASMC proliferation [53]. Although most studies focus on PASMCs, recent evidence also implicates HIF1α in PAEC dysfunction. Saddouk et al. showed that endothelial HIF1α induces PDGF-B expression, which activates PASMCs and contributes to vascular remodeling [54]. Bian et al. further demonstrated that HIF1α and HIF2α coordinate ERBB3/YB1 signaling to promote endothelial dysfunction in PAECs during PH. [37] Consistent with this, we observed elevated HIF1α protein levels under hypoxia, and validated that HIF1α functions as a transcriptional activator of P4HA1—confirmed by bioinformatics prediction and experimental validation—indicating that P4HA1 is a hypoxia-responsive gene regulated by HIF1α. To elucidate the mechanistic basis of P4HA1-mediated glycolytic regulation, we sought to identify its downstream signaling pathways through an unbiased proteomic screen. Notably, this analysis revealed that the HIF1 signaling pathway was among the most significantly suppressed upon P4HA1 knockdown. Given our prior findings that P4HA1 modulates glycolysis in pulmonary arterial endothelial cells, together with the established central role of HIF1 in glycolytic control, these results provide a compelling rationale for further investigating this reciprocal regulatory axis. This approach offers a plausible mechanistic link between P4HA1 and metabolic reprogramming in hypoxic endothelial cells. Under hypoxia, HIF1α escapes degradation due to reduced activity of HIF prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs), which normally hydroxylate key proline residues (402 and 564) to trigger degradation [55]. PHD activity depends on cofactors like α-KG, producing succinate and CO₂, and P4HA1 exhibits a higher affinity for α-KG than PHDs [56]. Our GSEA confirmed P4HA1’s involvement in 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism, with both α-KG and succinate being critical intermediates in HIF1α hydroxylation and collagen biosynthesis. Experimentally, P4HA1 modulates HIF1α hydroxylation by regulating intracellular α-KG and succinate levels, affecting its ubiquitination and stability. Furthermore, our data show that hypoxia induces HIF1α to promote P4HA1 expression in HPAECs. Together, these findings support the existence of a positive feedback loop between P4HA1 and HIF1α, which may play a crucial role in promoting endothelial dysfunction in HPAECs under hypoxic stress. Our study unveils a crucial pathogenic mechanism in hypoxic pulmonary endothelium: a self-amplifying HIF1α-P4HA1 feedback loop. While HIF1α initiates P4HA1 transcription, P4HA1 is, in turn, required for full HIF1 pathway activation. This reciprocal regulation represents a significant shift from the conventional linear understanding of hypoxic signaling. This loop likely serves as a core driver of PAEC dysfunction, locking the cells into a persistent glycolytic and proliferative state that fuels vascular remodeling. Consequently, P4HA1 emerges not merely as a downstream effector, but as a novel metabolic checkpoint whose inhibition could break this vicious cycle, offering a targeted strategy against the metabolic basis of pulmonary hypertension.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identifies a P4HA1/HIF1α feedback loop that contributes to pulmonary artery endothelial dysfunction and promotes pulmonary vascular remodeling in the context of PH. This regulatory axis may represent a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of this disease.
