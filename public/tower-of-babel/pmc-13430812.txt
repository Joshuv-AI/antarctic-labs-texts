Effect of controlled hypotension combined with low-pressure tourniquet on hemoglobin reduction after total knee arthroplasty
Authors: Jia Ji, Ming Jiang, Lixin Huang, Huilin Yang, Wu Xu, Yijian Zhang, Dinghua Jiang, Linyan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlood loss is common in total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Tourniquet use and controlled hypotension are standard interventions to minimize intraoperative bleeding and avoid transfusions. This study compared the effects of conventional tourniquet use versus controlled hypotension combined with a low-pressure tourniquet on postoperative hemoglobin reduction in TKA.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort study, 90 patients undergoing unilateral TKA under general anesthesia were assigned to either the conventional tourniquet group (n = 49) or the controlled hypotension–low-pressure tourniquet group (n = 41). Primary outcomes included postoperative red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin levels, reductions in RBC and hemoglobin, drainage volume, estimated total blood loss, and transfusion rate. Secondary outcomes were intraoperative duration, duration of hospitalization, and incidence of lower extremity deep venous thrombosis (DVT).ResultsThe controlled hypotension–low-pressure tourniquet group exhibited higher postoperative RBC count and hemoglobin levels. Reductions in RBC, hemoglobin, and cumulative drainage volume were lower, while estimated total blood loss, transfusion requirements, intraoperative duration, and duration of hospitalization were significantly reduced compared with the conventional tourniquet group. DVT incidence was similar between the two groups.ConclusionControlled hypotension with a low-pressure tourniquet effectively reduced postoperative blood loss without increasing the risk of lower limb DVT.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Hemoglobin drop, Tourniquet, Controlled hypotension, Clinical outcomes

Introduction

Osteoarthritis is becoming more common mainly because of aging and obesity, leading to a significant global health burden. Early clinical treatments for OA involve the oral administration of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), the injection of glucocorticoids and sodium hyaluronate, or stem cell injection [1]. For patients with late-stage OA, total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is considered a highly effective therapeutic intervention [2, 3]. Clinical evidence supports that TKA significantly alleviates pain and enhances physical function [4, 5]. However, this procedure carries inherent risks, including infection, blood loss or anemia, venous thromboembolism, pulmonary embolism, and pneumonia [6]. Postoperative anemia, affecting up to 80% of patients, is particularly concerning, as it elevates the risk of complications [7], such as infection, mortality, diminished quality of life, and extended hospitalization [8]. As such, perioperative blood management is crucial, and strategies to minimize blood loss remain a focal point of ongoing research.

Tourniquet use is a widely adopted technique known to effectively reduce blood loss during TKA [9]. Its application reduces calculated blood loss, improves surgical field visibility, and contributes to enhanced knee extension in the early postoperative period [10]. When used in conjunction with dexamethasone or tranexamic acid (TXA), tourniquets further decrease perioperative bleeding and mitigate associated adverse events [11]. In patients undergoing revision TKA, tourniquet use has been shown to reduce blood loss, improve postoperative hemoglobin levels, and lower reoperation rates [12]. However, some studies report no significant differences in clinical outcomes between patients who do and do not receive a tourniquet [13, 14]. Additionally, a meta-analysis revealed that TKA without a tourniquet may facilitate earlier recovery and reduce complications, raising questions about its clinical benefit [15].

Controlled hypotension, introduced in 1917 [16], is an anesthetic technique that utilizes short-acting hypotensive agents to lower blood pressure and decrease intraoperative bleeding. In TKA, controlled hypotension has been shown to reduce intraoperative blood loss and enhance surgical visibility without adversely affecting cerebral oxygen saturation or postoperative cognitive function [17]. It has not been associated with an increased risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, or acute kidney injury in arthroplasty patients [18]. Compared to tourniquet use, controlled hypotension provides better blood preservation and reduces the inflammatory response [19]. In older patients, it may alleviate chronic pain and support long-term recovery [20]. These findings highlight the need for optimal blood management strategies during TKA.

Given these considerations, this study aimed to compare red blood cell (RBC) count and hemoglobin levels between conventional tourniquet use and a combination of controlled hypotension with a low-pressure tourniquet in patients undergoing TKA.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (No. 20251040). Between July 1, 2024, and June 30, 2025, patients diagnosed with primary knee OA (KOA) who underwent unilateral TKA were enrolled. Inclusion criteria were: age > 50 years, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III, and scheduled for unilateral TKA under general anesthesia. Exclusion criteria included severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular conditions (e.g., acute myocardial infarction or unstable angina), psychiatric disorders, cognitive impairment, significant hepatic or renal dysfunction, coagulation abnormalities, contraindications to tourniquet use, or refusal to participate.

Surgical procedures

All surgeries were performed by the same surgical team using a standard anterior midline incision and medial parapatellar approach. Femoral and tibial cuts were guided intramedullarily, and anchoring holes were drilled into the tibial plateau to enhance bone-cement contact. High-pressure pulsatile lavage was applied to remove blood remnants and improve cement interdigitation. For general anesthesia induction, midazolam (0.05 mg/kg), propofol (1.5 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.5 µg/kg), and rocuronium (0.8 mg/kg) were administered intravenously. Anesthesia maintenance included intermittent rocuronium injections (10 mg every 45 min), continuous intravenous remifentanil (0.2 µg/kg/min) and propofol (3 mg/kg/h), and continuous sevoflurane inhalation. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was directly monitored via radial artery cannulation.

Controlled hypotension was initiated at the discretion of the anesthesiologist, based on the patient’s comorbidities and clinical condition. In the conventional group, the tourniquet was inflated to 330 mmHg at the proximal thigh [21], and MAP was maintained at approximately 80% of baseline. In the controlled hypotension–low-pressure tourniquet group, the tourniquet pressure was set at 250 mmHg, with MAP continuously monitored and maintained at 70 to 80% of baseline (55 to 60 mmHg), while systolic blood pressure (SBP) was kept ≥ 80 mmHg to ensure vital organ perfusion [20]. Controlled hypotension was achieved by titrating sevoflurane and remifentanil, with sodium nitroprusside or esmolol added as needed. Blood pressure was gradually reduced over 30 min to the target range. Heart rate (HR) was maintained between 50 and 80 bpm; atropine (0.5 mg) was administered if HR fell below 50 bpm. After key surgical steps, hypotension was gradually reversed, and furosemide (5 to 10 mg) was given based on volume status. Anesthesia was terminated at the conclusion of the procedure, with fresh gas flow increased to 8 L/min to wash out sevoflurane, and residual neuromuscular blockade reversed with neostigmine and atropine.

Both groups underwent tourniquet treatment throughout the entire TKA procedure. The tourniquet was inflated before incision and deflated after wound closure [22]. After component implantation, a solution of 1 g TXA dissolved in 60 mL normal saline was used to irrigate the wound [23]. Postoperatively, a uniform negative pressure drainage system was placed at the outer thigh region for all patients. The drainage tube was removed 2–3 days after surgery when drainage volume was less than 50 mL/24 h.

Outcome measures

Baseline characteristics, including sex, age, body mass index (BMI), and comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes, were documented. Blood samples were collected at 1, 3, and 6 days post-surgery. Every patient had all four blood tests (one preoperative and three postoperative) performed during the inpatient period. Primary outcomes included nadir postoperative RBC count, nadir hemoglobin levels, maximum postoperative decline in RBC or hemoglobin (calculated as postoperative minus nadir preoperative value), cumulative drainage volume, estimated total blood loss, and transfusion rate. Estimated blood loss was calculated using the following formula: Estimated blood loss = Estimated blood volume × (Final Hct reduction / Mean Hct) [24]. Transfusion criteria were set as hemoglobin levels below 70 g/L or hemoglobin levels between 70 and 100 g/L with severe cardiovascular decompensation symptoms [25]. Secondary outcomes included intraoperative duration, duration of hospitalization, and incidence of lower extremity deep venous thrombosis (DVT). Diagnosis of DVT was confirmed through ultrasound examination of the venous circulation in the lower limbs three days postoperatively. All patients received prophylactic-dose low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) on the first postoperative day and were prescribed oral rivaroxaban after discharge.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 20.0; IBM, New York, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. The normality of data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated with Levene’s test to determine whether parametric or non-parametric tests should be used. Normally distributed data were compared using the Student’s t-test. Non-normal distributions were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Binary outcomes were compared between groups using χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Multivariable regression models were used to assess the independent association between controlled hypotension and perioperative outcomes. All models adjusted for a prespecified set of baseline covariates, including sex, age, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and preoperative Hb. Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) was employed using stabilized weights (truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles) that were derived from a logistic propensity score model encompassing all baseline covariates. The balance was verified by the Standardized Mean Difference (SMDs) being less than 0.1 for all covariates, and subsequently, weighted regression with robust standard errors was applied. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 90 patients were enrolled: 49 (39 women, 10 men) in the conventional tourniquet group and 41 (26 women, 15 men) in the controlled hypotension–low-pressure tourniquet group. Baseline characteristics, including sex, age, BMI, hypertension, and diabetes, ASA class, anticoagulant/antiplatelet use, coronary artery disease, liver dysfunction, kidney dysfunction, or K-L grade were comparable between groups (Table 1).

Primary outcomes

Preoperative RBC counts were similar between groups (4.4 ± 0.4 vs. 4.5 ± 0.4 × 1012/L, P = 0.14; 95% CI [-0.05, 0.32]). Postoperatively, the controlled hypotension–low-pressure tourniquet group demonstrated higher RBC counts (4.2 ± 0.4 vs. 3.8 ± 0.5 × 1012/L, P < 0.001; 95% CI [0.27, 0.64]) and a smaller decline (-0.3 ± 0.4 vs. -0.6 ± 0.3 × 1012/L, P < 0.001; 95% CI [0.19, 0.46]) (Fig. 1). Similarly, postoperative hemoglobin levels were higher in the controlled hypotension–low-pressure tourniquet group (127.0 ± 11.5 vs. 113.8 ± 14.6 g/L, P < 0.001; 95% CI [7.62, 18.8]), with a lesser reduction (-7.3 ± 7.5 vs. -17.7 ± 10.1 g/L, P < 0.001; 95% CI [6.58, 14.2]) compared to the conventional group (Fig. 2). Furthermore, cumulative drainage volumes were significantly lower in the controlled hypotension–low-pressure tourniquet group (132.3 ± 85.2 vs. 223.6 ± 103.7 mL, P < 0.001; 95% CI [-131.5, -51.0]) (Fig. 3). Notably, estimated total blood loss (363.1 ± 254.1 vs. 672.1 ± 381.9 mL, P < 0.001; 95% CI [168.6, 449.3]) and transfusion rates were reduced (0% vs. 14.3%, P = 0.015; RR = 1.98, 95% CI [1.24, 7.91]) (Table 2).

Secondary outcomes

Intraoperative duration was shorter in the controlled hypotension–low-pressure tourniquet group (91.0 ± 14.2 vs. 128.9 ± 21.4 min, P < 0.001; 95% CI [-45.7, -30.2]), as was duration of hospitalization (9.4 ± 1.4 vs. 13.4 ± 2.9 days, P < 0.001; 95% CI [-5.02, -3.02]) (Fig. 4). The incidence of lower limb DVT did not differ between groups (9.8% vs. 8.2%, P > 0.99; RR = 0.91, 95% CI [0.39, 1.53]) (Table 3). Deep venous ultrasound of the lower limbs was performed 1 month after surgery, showing that in three out of eight DVT patients, the thrombosis had resolved. No patients presented symptomatic DVT, or pulmonary embolism. No cases of acute kidney injury, myocardial injury, neurologic events, wound problems, or delayed mobilization occurred in the two groups. Multivariable linear regression analysis revealed that, after adjusting for confounders such as age, sex, BMI, and comorbidity, the controlled hypotension low-pressure group exhibited less blood loss and shorter hospital stays (Table 4). According to the IPTW based on propensity scores, the majority of baseline covariates were well-balanced (Table 5). After applying propensity score IPTW and further adjusting for covariates, the direction and magnitude of the effect estimates were substantially consistent with those from the primary regression analysis (Table 6).

Discussion

Optimized blood management in TKA, which focuses on minimizing perioperative blood loss and reducing transfusion requirements, is essential for enhancing recovery after surgery (ERAS). Our findings highlight the advantages of a combined approach using local pressure (tourniquet) and systemic arterial pressure control (controlled hypotension) to minimize blood loss. The pneumatic tourniquet occludes arterial flow distal to its application, creating a blood-free surgical field. Controlled hypotension reduces bleeding from collateral vessels bypassing the tourniquet. Together, these methods offer synergistic hemostasis: the tourniquet blocks major arterial inflow, while controlled hypotension minimizes back-bleeding from periosteal, muscular, and medullary sources. This dual strategy ensures consistent surgical field clarity, reduces the need for intraoperative hemostatic interventions (e.g., electrocautery or bone wax), and shortens intraoperative duration. Additionally, controlled hypotension helps mitigate rapid blood loss upon tourniquet release and prevents complications such as reactive hyperemia, hypotension, or shock. Reduced intraoperative bleeding and less physiological disruption may contribute to more rapid postoperative recovery. The observed substantial reduction in operative time may not be solely attributable to the blood‑conservation strategy. Unmeasured workflow factors or subtle differences in surgical efficiency cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies should utilize time‑motion analysis or factorial designs to analyze the specific contributions of each intervention component to the operative duration.

Perioperative blood loss and blood transfusion following TKA are closely linked to the intensity of postoperative pain [26]. Enhanced blood management has been associated with improved perioperative hemodynamic stability and a reduction in complications, including lower infection rates, more effective pain relief, and earlier joint mobility. These benefits contribute to a shorter duration of hospitalization by promoting faster functional recovery, allowing patients to achieve independent ambulation and meet discharge criteria more quickly. Therefore, optimized blood management correlates with reduced postoperative duration of hospitalization. In comparison to previous studies, the drainage volume of 200 to 300 mL in the conventional tourniquet group aligns with prior findings [27, 28]. Similarly, the change in hemoglobin in our single-tourniquet group was consistent with other studies (change values: 15 to 30 g/L) [29, 30]. Notably, these parameters were significantly improved in the controlled hypotension–low-pressure tourniquet group, suggesting the potential advantage of this combined approach. However, due to the small sample size and the lack of randomized controlled trials, these results should be interpreted with caution. The safety of intraoperative controlled hypotension must also be carefully considered. Hypotension can lead to ischemic stroke, myocardial injury, or renal damage due to organ hypoperfusion [31]. As such, controlled hypotension should be used cautiously in patients with high cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk.

Pharmacologic hemostatic agents, particularly TXA, are essential in minimizing blood loss [32]. Both intravenous and oral TXA have been shown to significantly reduce hemoglobin drop and transfusion rates in patients undergoing TKA [33]. Intravenous administration produces similar hemoglobin outcomes but with lower drainage output compared to intra-articular use [34]; however, no significant differences in hemoglobin reduction or transfusion requirements have been found between oral and intravenous TXA [35]. Topical application of TXA has been significantly more effective than systemic administration in reducing blood loss during TKA [36]. The combination of TXA with other procedures further reduces perioperative bleeding and accelerates early recovery [37]. Erythropoietin (EPO), a renal hormone, also reduces transfusion requirements in elective total hip and knee replacements [38]. In two-stage revision surgery for infected TKA, perioperative EPO increased hemoglobin levels and reduced transfusion needs [39]. Moreover, intraoperative administration of iron isomaltoside helps prevent postoperative anemia [40], while preoperative intravenous iron supplementation increases RBC units and lowers transfusion rates [41]. High-dose iron results in faster recovery of hemoglobin levels compared to low-dose iron [42]. Conversely, postoperative iron administration immediately after acute blood loss from TKA is ineffective in reducing transfusion requirements [43], emphasizing the importance of preoperative optimization.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center, retrospective study with a limited sample size. Second, unmeasured confounders may still affect the results. Third, the timing of preoperative and postoperative laboratory testing lacked standardization. Fourth, the statistical power was insufficient to detect changes in rare patient outcomes such as DVT. Although we employed multivariable adjustment and propensity score matching to alleviate confounding by indication, this study fundamentally remains observational. Unmeasured confounding factors may still lead to bias in the results. Hence, causal inferences should be drawn with caution, and randomized controlled trials are required to validate our findings. Future research should involve multi-center, prospective studies with more rigorous protocols and larger sample sizes.

Conclusions

This study presents two key findings: first, the combination of controlled hypotension with a low-pressure tourniquet regimen led to reduced estimated total blood loss and postoperative drainage, preserved postoperative RBC count and hemoglobin levels, and lowered transfusion requirements. Second, this combined approach was associated with shorter intraoperative duration and reduced duration of hospitalization. Therefore, for patients undergoing TKA, controlled hypotension with a low-pressure tourniquet may offer an effective strategy for optimizing blood management and promoting enhanced recovery.
