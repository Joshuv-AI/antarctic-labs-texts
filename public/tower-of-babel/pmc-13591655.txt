Asymmetric Lamellar Templating of the Perovskite/C60 Interface for Scalable Inverted Perovskite Photovoltaics
Authors: Taeyoon Ki, Jong‐Guk Ahn, Sangcho Kim, Sangjin Kim, Min‐Ho Lee, In‐Wook Hwang, Jinju Jeong, Soobin Shim, Jaeboong Ahn, Jun Hong Park, Seungjin Lee, Jung‐Yong Lee, Yousoo Kim, Ju‐Hyeon Kim, Hongkyu Kang, Sukwon Hong, Kwanghee Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTScalable inverted perovskite solar cells require top interfaces that combine defect passivation with spatially uniform electron extraction as the device area increases. Low‐dimensional capping layers based on bulky spacer cations can suppress surface recombination, but poorly controlled molecular packing often introduces transport barriers and local interfacial heterogeneity. Here, we introduce indol‐3‐ylethylammonium iodide (Ind) as a π‐electron‐rich spacer cation for asymmetric lamellar templating at the perovskite/C60 junction. The heteroatom‐polarized indole framework creates lateral electrostatic anisotropy within the aromatic plane, promoting face‐to‐face spacer association and aligning the lamellar interphase. The resulting π‐rich lamellar interphase suppresses interfacial recombination, preserves electron extraction, and improves spatial optoelectronic uniformity in large‐area devices. Ind‐based devices achieve a certified efficiency of 26.94% in small‐area cells and a certified module efficiency of 23.21% for a 25 cm2 monolithic module. They further retain > 93% efficiency after 1000 h at 85°C and maintain 85% of their initial efficiency after 1800 h under continuous 1‐sun MPP tracking. These results identify lateral electrostatic anisotropy as a molecular design principle for scalable perovskite/C60 top interfaces.Keywords: asymmetric spacer cations, electron extraction, inverted perovskite solar cells, lamellar interphases, large‐area modules, lateral electrostatic anisotropy, molecular templating

Introduction

As perovskite photovoltaics move from laboratory‐scale cells toward large‐area modules, achieving high‐efficiency devices that retain their performance upon area scaling has become a central requirement for practical deployment [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Inverted PSCs are particularly attractive because their dopant‐free charge‐selective contacts offer advantages in operational stability, yet their performance remains highly sensitive to interfacial losses [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Recent advances have relied heavily on molecular post‐treatments to suppress non‐radiative recombination at the perovskite surface [7, 12, 13]. However, as device areas increase, the role of the top interface extends beyond defect passivation [14, 15, 16]. The perovskite/C60 junction must also preserve efficient vertical electron extraction and contact uniformity across large active areas.

Low‐dimensional capping layers based on bulky organic spacer cations are widely used to passivate the perovskite surface and improve interfacial stability [17, 18, 19, 20]. These capping layers have been improved by tuning anchoring strength, steric bulk, aromatic extension, or low‐dimensional phase formation to reduce surface defects and protect the perovskite film from chemical degradation. Nevertheless, stronger surface coverage and passivation can still be accompanied by poorly controlled spacer packing and hindered vertical charge extraction [21, 22]. This trade‐off becomes more consequential in large‐area devices, where local variations in molecular packing, surface potential, and perovskite/C60 contact quality can translate into spatially nonuniform optoelectronic response, fill‐factor loss, and reduced cell‐to‐module efficiency retention [23, 24]. An ideal top interphase should therefore combine defect passivation with controlled molecular ordering that supports efficient and homogeneous electron extraction.

Aromatic spacer cations provide a promising route to control this molecular ordering because their π‐systems can mediate intermolecular stacking, surface interactions, and contact formation with C60 [25, 26, 27, 28]. However, conventional aromatic spacers often exhibit multiple competing packing motifs, including tilted or herringbone‐like arrangements, arising from quadrupolar π–π interactions and steric constraints between adjacent aromatic cores [29, 30]. Such packing can improve defect passivation while still limiting through‐plane electronic coupling across the top interphase. We reasoned that the key design parameter is not simply aromatic size or total dipole moment, but lateral electrostatic polarization within the π‐system, which can provide directional control over spacer–spacer assembly after surface anchoring.

Here, we introduce indol‐3‐ylethylammonium iodide (Ind) as a π‐electron‐rich spacer cation for asymmetric lamellar templating at the perovskite/C60 junction. Compared with phenethylammonium iodide (Bz) and naphthyl‐1‐ethylammonium iodide (Naph), Ind combines π‐extension with a heteroatom‐polarized indole framework, generating a pronounced lateral dipole within the aromatic plane. This lateral electrostatic asymmetry promotes surface‐anchored Ind spacer cations toward face‐to‐face π–π stacking rather than tilted packing, thereby initiating an asymmetric lamellar templating sequence that couples surface anchoring with spacer–spacer assembly. The resulting π‐rich lamellar interphase creates a more ordered molecular environment at the perovskite/C60 junction, reducing local contact heterogeneity and maintaining uniform electron extraction as the device area increases.

By combining defect passivation with more ordered molecular packing, the Ind interphase mitigates the passivation–transport trade‐off that commonly limits low‐dimensional capping layers. It suppresses interfacial recombination while maintaining efficient electron extraction, yielding brighter and more uniform electroluminescence (EL) across large‐area devices with reduced area‐dependent losses. This interfacial design enables certified efficiencies of 26.94% in small‐area cells and a certified module efficiency of 23.21% for a 25 cm2 monolithic module. The devices further show operational and thermal robustness, retaining > 93% efficiency after 1000 h at 85°C and maintaining 85% of their initial efficiency after 1800 h under continuous 1‐sun MPP tracking. These results identify lateral electrostatic anisotropy as a molecular design principle for constructing π‐rich lamellar interphases in scalable inverted perovskite photovoltaics.

Results and Discussion

Lateral Electrostatic Anisotropy Governs Lamellar Assembly

To establish a molecular design rule for the perovskite/C60 top contact, we compared Bz, Naph, and Ind spacer cations with different π‐extension and lateral electrostatic anisotropy. Bz serves as a compact carbocyclic reference [25], Naph extends the aromatic π‐surface while retaining a largely symmetric hydrocarbon framework [31], and Ind combines π‐extension with a heteroatom‐polarized indole framework. This series separates the effects of aromatic extension from lateral electrostatic anisotropy in determining spacer packing, surface binding, and interfacial electronic structure.

Electrostatic potential mapping and dipole decomposition reveal the key distinction among the spacer cations (Figure 1a; Table S1). All ammonium‐terminated spacers possess an axial dipole component associated with the ammonium anchoring group. However, Ind exhibits a pronounced lateral dipole component within the aromatic plane, whereas Bz has a negligible lateral component and Naph shows only weak lateral polarization. This lateral electrostatic anisotropy originates from the indole nitrogen, which polarizes the aromatic π‐system and creates an electron‐rich/electron‐deficient distribution across the fused ring. Thus, Ind is distinguished by directional polarization within the π‐system, which biases spacer–spacer assembly at the perovskite surface.

To examine how this lateral anisotropy affects supramolecular organization, we calculated Gibbs free‐energy landscapes for representative dimer conformations, including T‐shaped, transitional, and face‐to‐face configurations (Figure 1b; Note S1 and Figure S1 and Table S2). These configurations represent competing π–π stacking motifs that determine whether aromatic spacer cations assemble into tilted arrangements or lamellar packing [30]. Bz exhibits a finite barrier between T‐shaped and face‐to‐face configurations, consistent with competing quadrupolar interactions. Naph, despite its extended π‐surface, is constrained by its laterally expanded rigid framework and tends to retain tilted packing motifs. In contrast, Ind follows a downhill energy profile from a slipped configuration toward face‐to‐face stacking, indicating that lamellar assembly is both energetically favored and kinetically accessible. These results show that the laterally polarized indole framework biases surface‐anchored Ind spacer cations toward face‐to‐face π–π stacking, providing the molecular basis for asymmetric lamellar templating.

We next evaluated the interaction between the spacer cations and perovskite surfaces to clarify the origin of surface passivation (Figure 1c–e; Note S2 and Figures S2–S6). On pristine and iodide‐vacant perovskite surfaces, all three spacer cations exhibited comparably strong binding energies (Figures S2 and S3), indicating that simple surface affinity alone does not explain the distinct behavior of Ind. A larger difference emerged at formamidinium (FA)‐vacant surface sites, where spacer incorporation more directly affects the local interfacial environment. Naph, with its laterally extended rigid aromatic framework, experiences a larger steric penalty and provides only weak stabilization (−0.06 eV). By contrast, the compact indole framework of Ind provides sterically accessible conformational flexibility, allowing the spacer cation to accommodate surface binding while preserving favorable π–π stacking. This leads to stronger stabilization at the FA‐vacant site (−0.26 eV), as summarized in Figure 1d.

Projected density‐of‐states analysis further supports this interpretation by revealing electronic coupling between indole C/N p‐orbitals and surface iodine p‐orbitals near the perovskite valence‐band maximum (Figures S4–S6). This coupling indicates stronger electronic engagement between the Ind spacer and the surface lattice, consistent with the formation of a more integrated perovskite/2D interfacial structure [32]. Comparable coupling is not evident for Bz and Naph, highlighting the distinct role of the heteroatom‐polarized indole framework in linking surface binding with interfacial electronic structure.

The calculated trends were experimentally supported by x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) (Figure 1f,g). XPS Pb 4f spectra show that Ind strongly suppresses the metallic Pb0 component compared with the control and other spacer‐treated films, consistent with improved passivation of undercoordinated Pb‐related surface defects. Shifts in the I 3d and Pb 4f core levels further indicate modification of the local chemical and electrostatic environment after spacer treatment (Figure S7 and Table S3). UPS measurements reveal corresponding changes in surface energetics, including a lower work function for the Ind‐treated surface (Figures S8 and S9 and Table S4). These shifts indicate spacer‐dependent interfacial dipole formation and surface electrostatic modification at the treated perovskite surface, which set the energetic boundary for the subsequent C60 contact.

These computational and spectroscopic results define the molecular basis for the first two steps of asymmetric lamellar templating: anchoring of Ind spacer cations at the perovskite surface and lateral‐dipole‐driven Ind–Ind assembly into a π‐rich lamellar interphase. The structural manifestation of this templating sequence and its influence on the perovskite/C60 contact are examined in the following section.

Structural Manifestation of Asymmetric Lamellar Templating

To examine how the molecular design principles identified above translate into interfacial structure, we analyzed the perovskite/spacer/C60 junction using cross‐sectional high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR‐TEM) [9, 33]. The local interfacial motifs varied strongly with the spacer cation (Figure 2a). The Bz‐treated interface showed randomly tilted lamellar segments with repeated angular derivations, whereas the Naph‐treated interface exhibited a mixed arrangement containing herringbone‐like and tilted face‐to‐face motifs within the RP‐type 2D interfacial region [17], consistent with the competing stacking pathways predicted by the dimer calculations. In contrast, the Ind‐treated interface showed a more continuously aligned face‐to‐face lamellar assembly with a periodicity of ∼13.7 Å, indicating that the heteroatom‐polarized Ind spacer promotes ordered lamellar organization at the perovskite surface. Low‐angle GIWAXS (grazing‐incidence wide‐angle x‐ray scattering) features further supported the assignment of the Ind interphase to an RP‐type n = 1 interfacial phase (Figure S10).

The Ind interphase also exhibited a shorter projected modulation of ∼2.3 Å within the enlarged HR‐TEM image, arising from projected contrast in the closely stacked lamellar region at the capped interface. Together with the annotated vertically oriented spacer arrangement and the calculated 3.52 Å distance between ring centroids (Table S2), this lamellar ordering supports the tendency of Ind spacer cations to form face‐to‐face aromatic associations. The lateral dipole analysis accounts for this spacer‐dependent structural motif, supporting a hierarchical templating process in which surface‐anchored Ind spacer cations organize neighboring Ind cations into a π‐rich 2D lamellar interphase.

GIWAXS comparison was performed using the optimized mixed spacer treatments, where each aromatic spacer was combined with a co‐passivating propane‐1,3‐diammonium iodide (PDAI2) additive. To clarify the structural contribution of Ind, we further evaluated an Ind‐only film as an attribution control. The Ind‐only film retained the characteristic lamellar signatures observed for the mixed Ind/PDAI2 treatment, indicating that the crystallographic lamellar motif is primarily associated with the asymmetric Ind spacer, while PDAI2 improves the optimized passivation treatment without changing the Ind‐driven structural assignment (Figures S11–S13).

Lower‐magnification TEM further showed that the lamellar organization in the Ind‐treated film persists beyond isolated nanoscale regions (Figure 2b). The Ind‐treated interface formed a laterally continuous and well‐defined 2D interfacial region along the perovskite surface, whereas the Bz‐ and Naph‐treated interfaces showed less persistent ordering and more disrupted local motifs. This distinction suggests that the compact, laterally polarized indole framework can accommodate surface anchoring while sustaining lamellar spacer–spacer assembly, consistent with the sterically accessible conformational flexibility inferred from the surface‐binding calculations. This lateral persistence is important for area scaling, because the top contact must maintain a consistent interfacial structure over extended device areas rather than only within isolated ordered domains.

Cross‐sectional SEM images confirmed that the C60/BCP overlayer remained continuous across all spacer‐treated devices (Figure S14). This continuity places the spacer‐dependent structural variation primarily at the 2D interfacial region beneath C60. Consistent with this interfacial assignment, the Ind‐treated perovskite/2D/C60 stack showed smoother C60‐deposited surface morphology (Figure S15), while the Ind‐treated perovskite/2D surface exhibited a more homogeneous surface potential (Figure S16). These observations indicate that the ordered Ind interphase provides a more uniform morphological and electrostatic landscape for the subsequent C60 growth morphology and contact formation.

Guided by these observations, we examined the perovskite/C60 contact region using fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis (Figure 2c). The Bz‐treated interface showed diffuse features with weak lamellar signatures, whereas the Naph‐treated interface exhibited multiple rotated domains, consistent with less uniform orientational ordering. In contrast, the Ind‐treated interface displayed clearer lamellar reflections together with a C60‐related diffuse feature near the interfacial region. This C60‐related diffuse contrast was observed within the FFT region containing the ordered Ind‐rich lamellar interphase, indicating spatially correlated structural signatures from the π‐rich lamellar interphase and the perovskite/C60 contact region [34]. Combined with the C60‐coated SEM/KPFM analysis, this FFT response indicates that the π‐rich Ind surface provides a more uniform morphological and electrostatic environment for subsequent C60 growth morphology and contact formation.

These microscopic observations define the structural sequence of asymmetric lamellar templating (Figure 2d). The perovskite surface first anchors Ind spacer cations, and the lateral electrostatic anisotropy of the indole framework then biases neighboring Ind cations toward face‐to‐face π–π stacking. Through this coupled anchoring‐and‐assembly process, Ind acts as a structure‐directing spacer cation that generates a π‐rich 2D lamellar interphase. This interphase provides a π‐rich and laterally uniform surface environment for subsequent C60 deposition, leading to more uniform C60 growth morphology and contact formation at the perovskite/C60 junction. The resulting sequence connects surface‐directed spacer assembly, Ind–Ind lamellar organization, and interphase‐guided C60 contact formation. This contact architecture reduces local heterogeneity, and its functional consequences are examined next through recombination, extraction, and spatial optoelectronic analyses.

Resolving the Passivation–Transport Trade‐Off Through a π‐Rich Lamellar Interphase

Having established the formation of a π‐rich lamellar Ind interphase, we next examined how this interfacial structure affects recombination, electron extraction, and spatial optoelectronic uniformity. Low‐dimensional capping layers often improve surface passivation, but poorly controlled molecular packing can introduce an additional barrier to vertical charge extraction at the perovskite/C60 junction. The central question is therefore whether the ordered Ind interphase can suppress interfacial recombination while preserving efficient electron extraction.

Drive‐level capacitance profiling (DLCP) was first used to probe defect responses across the active junction (Figure 3a; S17) [35]. Compared with the control and Bz‐treated devices, the Naph‐ and Ind‐treated devices exhibited markedly reduced trap densities near the perovskite/C60‐side interfacial region, indicating that aromatic spacer treatment mitigates surface‐related defects. The Ind‐treated device showed the lowest interfacial trap density among the series, consistent with stronger stabilization at defective surface sites predicted by the surface‐binding calculations. These results show that the Ind interphase provides the most effective suppression of trap‐assisted recombination at the perovskite/C60 interface.

Time‐resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements were used to evaluate surface recombination before and after C60 contact formation (Figure 3b; Table S7). For glass/perovskite/2D films without C60, all spacer‐treated films showed long‐lived decay components associated with reduced surface recombination. The Ind‐treated film exhibited the longest average lifetime of 867 ns, compared with 806 ns for Bz, 843 ns for Naph, and 747 ns for the control. The absence of a resolvable initial fast decay component in the Ind‐treated film further suggests suppression of fast trap‐assisted surface decay. Steady‐state photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) measurements showed the same overall trend, with Ind and Naph giving the highest radiative efficiencies among the treated samples (Figure S18). These results indicate that spacers capable of reorganizing the top interphase also provide more effective suppression of non‐radiative surface recombination before C60 deposition.

We then compared TRPL decays after C60 deposition to assess carrier quenching at the perovskite/2D/C60 contact. In glass/perovskite/2D/C60 stacks, PL quenching was fastest for the Ind‐treated film, with an average decay time of 49 ns, compared with 65 ns for Bz, 68 ns for the control, and 108 ns for Naph. The Naph‐treated stack retained the slowest decay after C60 deposition despite its long lifetime before C60 deposition, showing that strong surface passivation does not necessarily ensure fast C60‐side carrier quenching. In contrast, the rapid PL quenching observed for the Ind‐treated stack indicates the most favorable interfacial carrier transfer at the Ind‐modified perovskite/C60 contact.

Transient photovoltage (TPV) and transient photocurrent (TPC) measurements on complete devices further resolved the recombination and charge‐collection dynamics implied by the TRPL results (Figure 3c,d; Tables S7 and S8). In TPV measurements, the Naph‐ and Ind‐treated devices showed prolonged average voltage‐decay lifetimes of 3.66 and 3.71 ms, respectively, compared with 3.47 ms for Bz and 3.17 ms for the control (Figure 3c). This indicates that both Naph and Ind suppress recombination under open‐circuit perturbation, consistent with their reduced interfacial trap densities and enhanced PL lifetimes.

In contrast, TPC measurements in Figure 3d revealed a clear difference in charge‐collection dynamics. The Ind‐treated device showed the fastest photocurrent response, with the shortest rise and decay times of 20.5 and 304 ns, respectively, whereas the Naph‐treated device showed slower rise and decay times of 25.1 and 368 ns. This contrast distinguishes Ind from the passivation‐dominant Naph case and shows that the Ind interphase combines suppressed recombination with rapid photocurrent collection at the perovskite/C60 contact.

Steady‐state transport and light‐intensity‐dependent measurements further supported this interpretation. Electron‐only space charge limited current (SCLC) measurements showed that the Ind‐treated stack exhibited the highest effective electron mobility among the series, whereas the Naph‐treated stack showed a weaker transport response despite its strong passivation effect (Figure S19). Light‐intensity‐dependent open‐circuit voltage (V OC) measurements also showed the lowest ideality factor for the Ind‐treated device, with n = 1.40 compared with 1.52 for Naph, 1.55 for Bz, and 1.89 for the control (Figure S20). This indicates that trap‐assisted recombination is most effectively suppressed in the Ind‐treated device under operating conditions. The short‐circuit current density (J SC) scaling exponent remained close to unity for all devices, with Ind showing α = 0.973 compared with 0.961 for Naph, 0.971 for Bz, and 0.968 for the control. These steady‐state measurements confirm that the Ind interphase reduces trap‐assisted recombination without compromising photocurrent collection.

Finally, we used spatially resolved EL mapping to visualize optoelectronic uniformity in 1.56 cm2 devices (Figure 3e). The EL response under forward‐bias injection reflects local variations in radiative recombination, non‐radiative loss, current spreading, and charge‐selective contact quality. The control device showed weak and spatially discontinuous emission, indicating poor radiative efficiency across the active aperture. Bz‐ and Naph‐treated devices exhibited enhanced EL emission but retained pronounced spatial variations. In contrast, the Ind‐treated device showed the strongest and most spatially continuous emission across the active aperture, consistent with reduced local contact heterogeneity.

To quantify this spatial response, we analyzed the EL intensity distribution over the active aperture (Figure S21; Table 1). The relatively narrow distribution of the control device originated from uniformly weak emission rather than improved spatial operation. Bz‐ and Naph‐treated devices showed higher EL intensities but broader intensity distributions, indicating that passivation alone did not fully remove lateral nonuniformity. The Ind‐treated device combined the highest mean EL intensity with a comparatively narrow distribution among the passivated devices, showing that the Ind interphase improves both average junction quality and spatial optoelectronic uniformity [36].

Overall, these results show that the π‐rich lamellar Ind interphase resolves the passivation–transport trade‐off more effectively than the reference spacer treatments. The Ind interphase suppresses interfacial trap‐assisted recombination, maintains efficient electron extraction through the perovskite/C60 contact, and enables spatially uniform device operation. This combination of reduced recombination loss, efficient C60‐side extraction, and uniform EL response provides the interfacial basis for the high FF, reduced scaling losses, and module‐level performance discussed in the following section.

Device Performance, Scalability, and Stability

The functional balance established by the Ind interphase was reflected in photovoltaic performance. The champion small‐area device incorporating Ind achieved a PCE of 27.05% with a VOC of 1.206 V, a JSC of 26.81 mA cm−2, and an FF of 83.79% with an anti‐reflection (AR) coating. Independent certification confirmed a PCE of 26.94% for the 0.0896 cm2 device (Figures 4a; S22), in good agreement with the in‐house measurement. Representative in‐house J–V curves for small‐area devices showed minimal hysteresis for the Ind‐based device (Figure S23). The stabilized power output and EQE‐integrated current density further supported the reliability of the measured performance (Figures S24 and S25 and Table S9). These results show that the Ind interphase translates the passivation–transport balance identified above into high photovoltaic efficiency.

We next examined whether the interfacial advantage persisted across device statistics and area scaling (Figures 4b and S26; Tables 2 and S10). For 0.0896 cm2 devices, Ind‐based cells yielded an average PCE of 26.59% ± 0.16% over 20 devices, exceeding Bz‐ and Naph‐based devices while also showing a lower relative standard deviation. More importantly, this advantage was retained when the aperture area increased to 1.0 cm2. Ind‐based large‐area cells averaged 25.92% ± 0.21% over eight devices, whereas Naph‐ and Bz‐based devices showed lower average efficiencies and broader distributions. Representative 1.0 cm2 J–V curves further showed minimal hysteresis for the Ind‐based large‐area device (Figure S27). The improved FF retention in the Ind devices is consistent with the EL mapping results, suggesting that asymmetric lamellar templating helps suppress area‐dependent extraction losses during cell‐area scaling.

The same interfacial design translated to monolithic modules. An Ind‐based 10‐cell monolithic module with an aperture area of 25.0 cm2 delivered an in‐house aperture‐area efficiency of 24.23% with an AR coating (Figure 4c; Table S11). Independent certification confirmed a module efficiency of 23.21% (Figure S28), placing the device among the highest‐performing inverted perovskite modules in the 10–50 cm2 aperture‐area class (Figure 4d and Table S12). Considering the geometrical fill factor of 93.5%, the active‐area equivalent efficiency approaches the performance level of the corresponding large‐area cells, indicating limited cell‐to‐module loss. This scaling behavior further supports the role of the Ind lamellar interphase in preserving contact quality over large active areas.

We also evaluated whether the improved interfacial organization contributes to mechanical robustness. Double‐cantilever beam measurements showed that the Ind‐treated interface exhibited a higher interfacial fracture energy than the control, Bz‐, and Naph‐treated interfaces (Figure S29 and Note S3) [37]. Flexible devices incorporating Ind achieved a champion PCE of 24.70% and retained 95.2% of their initial efficiency after 1000 bending cycles under a 5 mm radius (Figures S29 and S30 and Table S13). These results indicate that the lamellar Ind interphase contributes to interfacial cohesion as well as electronic contact quality.

Finally, we assessed operational and thermal stability (Figure 4e,f). Under continuous 1‐sun‐equivalent illumination in N2 following the ISOS‐L‐1I protocol [38], unencapsulated Ind‐based devices maintained 85% of their initial efficiency after 1800 h. Encapsulated devices further retained more than 93% of their initial efficiency after 1000 h at 85°C under dark ambient‐air conditions. These stability results are consistent with reduced interfacial recombination and improved interfacial cohesion in the Ind‐treated devices. The faster degradation of the control and reference spacer‐treated devices further highlights the importance of interfacial molecular organization for stable device operation.

These performance, scalability, and stability results demonstrate that the Ind lamellar interphase enables high‐efficiency inverted perovskite photovoltaics with reduced area‐dependent losses and improved device robustness.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates that lateral electrostatic anisotropy in aromatic spacer cations can be used to control molecular organization at the perovskite/C60 top interface. By incorporating a heteroatom‐polarized indole framework, Ind promotes face‐to‐face π–π stacking and forms a π‐rich lamellar interphase at the perovskite surface. This interphase reduces local contact heterogeneity at the perovskite/C60 junction, suppresses interfacial recombination, and preserves efficient electron extraction, thereby mitigating the passivation–transport trade‐off commonly associated with low‐dimensional capping layers. As a result, Ind‐based inverted PSCs achieve a certified small‐area efficiency of 26.94% and a certified module efficiency of 23.21% in 25 cm2 monolithic modules, together with improved spatial optoelectronic uniformity and enhanced operational, thermal, and mechanical robustness.

More broadly, these results identify lateral electrostatic anisotropy as a practical molecular design principle for constructing π‐rich lamellar interphases in scalable inverted perovskite photovoltaics. The findings suggest that spacer cations should be designed not only for defect passivation, but also for controlling molecular packing, local contact uniformity, and charge‐extraction homogeneity across device‐relevant areas. This molecular‐design framework can provide a useful basis for developing top‐contact interphases that maintain contact uniformity while retaining high efficiency and stability during area scaling.

Methods

Materials

Fullerene (C60, sublimed), bathocuproine (BCP, sublimed), aluminum oxide nanoparticles (AlOx, 20 wt.% in isopropanol), tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAOH, 25 wt.% in H2O), methylammonium chloride (MACl, 98%), and sodium 4‐chlorobenzenesulfonate (4Cl‐BZS) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich, along with all anhydrous solvents including isopropyl alcohol (IPA), 2‐methoxyethanol, ethanol, anisole, N,N‐dimethylformamide (DMF), and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Cesium iodide (CsI, 99.999%) and nickel(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, 98%) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Methylammonium bromide (MABr, 99.99%), formamidinium iodide (FAI, 99.99%), propane‐1,3‐diammonium diiodide (PDAI2), and phenethylammonium iodide (Bz) were sourced from GreatCell Solar. [4‐(3,6‐dimethyl‐9H‐carbazol‐9‐yl)butyl]phosphonic acid (Me‐4PACz, 99%), lead(II) iodide (PbI2, 99.99%), lead(II) bromide (PbBr2, 98%), and hydroiodic acid (57 wt.% in H2O) were purchased from TCI. 2‐(1H‐Indol‐3‐yl)ethanamine and 2‐(1‐Naphthyl)ethanamine were purchased from Combi Blocks Inc. Naph and Ind were synthesized in this work as described below. Unless otherwise specified, all chemicals and materials were purchased from commercial suppliers and used as received without further purification.

Synthesis

Naphthyl‐1‐Ethylammonium Iodide (Naph)

Naph was synthesized by reacting 2‐(1‐Naphthyl)ethanamine with hydriodic acid (57 wt.% in H2O) in a 1:1.1 molar ratio. The acid was added dropwise to the amine solution under ice‐bath conditions (0°C). The mixture was stirred for 30 min at 0°C, during which the product precipitated. The solid was collected by vacuum filtration, washed thoroughly with diethyl ether, and dried in a vacuum oven at 80°C for 24 h, affording the product a 67.1% yield. 1H NMR (400 MHz, DMSO‐d6, ppm): δ 8.15–7.40 (m, 7H), 8.04–7.81 (s, 3H), 3.42–3.31(t, 2H), 3.19–3.06(t, 2H). 13C NMR (100 MHz, DMSO‐d6, ppm): 133.42, 133.18, 131.20, 128.69, 127.43, 126.97, 126.38, 125.82, 125.66, 123.39, 39.59, 30.12. (Figures S32 and S33)

Indol‐3‐Ylethylammonium Iodide (Ind)

Ind was synthesized by dissolving 2‐(1H‐Indol‐3‐yl)ethanamine in deionized water (0.1 M), followed by the dropwise addition of aqueous hydriodic acid (57 wt.% in H2O) at a 1:1 molar ratio under stirring. The solution was evaporated at elevated temperature until crystalline solids precipitated. The product was isolated by filtration, rinsed with diethyl ether, and dried in a vacuum oven at 80°C for 24 h, affording the product in a 41.2% yield. 1H NMR (400 MHz, DMSO‐d6, ppm): δ11.14(s, 1H), 7.77 (s, 3H), 7.47–6.40(m, 5H), 3.10(s, 4H). 13C NMR (100 MHz, DMSO‐d6, ppm): δ 135.88, 128.34, 127.10, 125.30, 121.39, 118.96, 110.56, 99.43, 39.71, 31.10. (Figures S34 and S35)

Nickel Oxide (NiOx) Nanoparticles

The NiOx nanoparticles were synthesized following a previously reported method [39]. Briefly, Ni(NO3)2·6H2O was dissolved in deionized water to form a 1 M solution. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 10 by the dropwise addition of a 2 M TMAOH solution. The resulting precipitates were washed with deionized water five times and subsequently dried in an oven at 80°C overnight. The dried powder was calcined in air at 270°C for 2 h to yield the final NiOx nanoparticles.

Formamidinium Lead Triiodide (FAPbI3)

The FAPbI3 powder was synthesized by dissolving FAI and PbI2 in a 1:1 molar ratio in 2‐ME to a final concentration of 0.8 M, following a previously reported procedure [40]. The mixture was stirred on a hotplate at 120°C. Upon precipitation, the resulting FAPbI3 powder was collected by filtration using a 0.45 µm PVDF filter and subsequently baked at 150°C for 30 min.

Device Fabrication

Small‐Sized Solar Cell Fabrication

Fluorine‐doped tin oxide (FTO, Asahi VU8) coated glass substrates were sequentially cleaned by ultrasonication in detergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol for 15 min each. The substrates were treated with ultraviolet–ozone for 30 min immediately prior to use.

The hole transport layer (HTL) was NiOx/Me‐4PACz/AlOx. A 1 wt.% aqueous NiOx ink was spin‐coated onto the substrates at 3000 r.p.m. for 30 s, followed by drying at 120°C for 10 min. Then, substrates were transferred to the N2‐filled glovebox (O2 and H2O < 10 ppm, 23°C–25°C). A 3 mM Me‐4PACz in ethanol was spin‐coated on substrate at 3000 r.p.m. for 30 s and subsequently annealed at 100°C for 10 min. Finally, a 0.33 wt.% AlOx in isopropanol was dynamically dripped onto the spinning substrate at 3000 r.p.m. for 30s and dried at 100°C for 5 min.

The 1.56 eV perovskite precursor ink with the composition Cs0.05MA0.04FA0.91PbI2.88Br0.12 was prepared by dissolving 17.5 mg of CsI, 777.6 mg of FAPbI3, 6.1 mg of MABr, 56.0 mg of PbI2, 25.9 mg of PbBr2, with PbI2 (4 mol% excess), MACl (15 mol%), and 4Cl‐BZS (0.3 mol%) [15] additives (relative to Pb) in a mixed solvent of 600 µL of DMF and 150 µL of DMSO to yield a total concentration of 1.73 M. The ink was filtered through a 0.22 µm PVDF filter before use. The perovskite ink was spin‐coated onto the HTL at 4000 r.p.m. for 30 s. At the 23‐s mark, 200 µL anisole was quickly dripped as an anti‐solvent. The film was immediately transferred to a hotplate and annealed at 100°C for 60 min, yielding a film thickness of ≈700 nm.

The 2D capping layer ink was prepared by dissolving the primary organic cation (Bz, Naph, or Ind) at 4 mM and PDAI2 at 2 mM in isopropanol (total cation concentration = 6 mM; molar ratio = 2:1). After filtration, 80 µL of each respective ink was dynamically spin‐coated onto the perovskite films at 4000 r.p.m. for 30 s, followed by annealing at 100°C for 5 min.

Finally, devices were completed by sequential thermal evaporation under high vacuum (< 2 × 10−6 Torr). A 25 nm layer of C60 was deposited as the ETL, followed by a 7 nm layer of BCP as a hole‐blocking layer. A 100 nm Ag or Cu electrode (99.999%, iTASCO) was then deposited through a shadow mask to define the active area of the device.

Module Fabrication

The fabrication of perovskite modules followed the same procedures as those for the small‐area devices, with each ink volume scaled to cover the larger substrate area. Modules consisting of ten monolithically series‐connected subcells were fabricated on 7.2 × 7.2 cm2 FTO‐coated glass substrates. The series interconnection was achieved via sequential P1, P2, and P3 laser scribing steps, performed using a 532 nm picosecond laser system operating at 300 kHz. The respective laser powers were 3.6 W (P1), 1.0 W (P2), and 1.0 W (P3).

The P1 scribe for FTO patterning (5.0 × 5.0 cm2) was conducted prior to substrate cleaning. The P2 scribe, isolating the perovskite and charge‐transport layers, was performed before the top‐electrode evaporation, whereas the P3 scribe for electrode isolation was conducted after the final metal deposition. An additional P4 scribe was implemented under identical conditions as P1 to precisely define the module aperture area.

The respective line widths for P1, P2, and P3 were 22, 54, and 40 µm (Figure S36), resulting in a geometrical fill factor of 93.5%. The corresponding aperture and active areas were 25.0 and 23.375 cm2, respectively.

Characterizations

Photovoltaic Performance and Stability Measurements

Current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics were measured under ambient air conditions, with the device temperature maintained at room temperature using a cooling fan. A Keithley 2450 sourcemeter was used for the measurement, with temperature monitored by a Keithley DAQ6510 multimeter. A class AAA solar simulator equipped with a Xe arc lamp (SAN‐EI XES‐50S2, spectrum shown in Figure S21) served as the AM1.5G 1‐sun light source. The light intensity (100 mW cm−2) was calibrated using a certified Newport 91150‐KG5 silicon reference cell (certified by Newport in January, 2024).

The J–V scan was performed from −0.1 to 1.26 V for forward scan and 1.26 to −0.1 V for reverse scan with a voltage step of 20 mV and a scan rate of 70 mV/s. No preconditioning was applied to the devices. The active area of the solar cells was defined by an opaque metal mask (cell 0.0896 or 1.00 cm2; module 25.0 cm2). The electrode‐to‐mask ratios were 0.12 → 0.0896 cm2, 1.56 → 1.00 cm2, and 25 → 25 cm2, respectively. Incident photon‐to‐current efficiency (IPCE) spectra were measured using a Newport QuantX‐300 system; the calculated spectral mismatch factor was typically 0.97 (Table S6).

Operational stability was evaluated following the ISOS‐L‐1I protocol. Unencapsulated devices were placed inside an inert chamber and illuminated by a white LED (spectrum in Figure S21), with the intensity adjusted to match the J SC under 1‐sun conditions. A Keithley 2450 sourcemeter was used to track the maximum power point (MPP) using a perturb‐and‐observe algorithm, with the MPP being updated at 5‐min intervals.

Thermal stability was evaluated according to the ISOS‐D‐2 protocol. Encapsulated devices were heated on a hotplate at 85°C in a dark, ambient air environment. J–V characteristics were measured with the standard protocol after cooling the devices to room temperature. Encapsulation was performed by sealing the devices with a capping glass slide using a UV‐curable adhesive (NOA‐60). Mechanical stability measurements procedures are described in Note S3.

Structural and Microscopic Characterization

XPS and UPS were performed using a NEXSA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to investigate the surface chemistry and electronic properties. GIWAXS were measured using a SmartLab II (Rigaku) with a HyPix‐3000 2‐D detector. The incident angle and sample‐to‐detector distance were 0.2° and 65.8 mm, respectively. Cu Kα radiation was used for the measurements.

The analysis of surface potential was conducted using the KPFM (MFP‐3D Origin, Oxford Instruments). The sample surfaces were probed using the tapping‐mode technique with a scan speed of 0.7 Hz. To extract the work function value of the Ti/Ir‐coated cantilever tip (ASYELEC‐01‐R2), pre‐scanning was performed on a highly ordered pyrolytic graphite (HOPG), which had been cleaved by mechanical exfoliation. Afterward, the contact potential difference (V CPD) value of the sample was extracted with the following equation: eV CPD = Φ sample−Φ tip.

Module scribe lines were examined by 3D laser scanning microscopy (VK‐X3000, Keyence). Surface morphology was characterized using UHR FE‐SEM (Verios 5 XHR, Thermo Scientific). TEM lamellae were prepared by FIB (NX5000, Hitachi) at 30 kV (Ga+ source), and TEM imaging was performed using a double Cs‐corrected TEM (JEM‐ARM300F2, JEOL) at 300 kV.

Optoelectronic and Electrical Characterization

UV–vis absorption spectra of the perovskite films were measured using an Agilent Cary 5000. TRPL decay was recorded via TCSPC, with excitation from a 470 nm picosecond diode laser (LDH‐P‐C‐470, PicoQuant), a monochromator (SP‐2150, Princeton Instruments), a PMT detector (R3809U‐51, Hamamatsu), and a photon counting module (SPC‐130‐EM, Becker & Hickl). PLQY was obtained using a HORIBA FluoroMax Plus spectrofluorometer with an integrating sphere, excited at 470 nm (3 nm bandpass, both excitation/emission). EL mapping was conducted using a MANTA G145B NIR camera (Edmund Optics). A Toyotech TL503N DC power supply applied a forward bias adjusted to match JSC under 1‐sun illumination. Light‐intensity dependent V OC and J SC were recorded under neutral‐density filters (1.0–0.03 sun).

SCLC measurements were carried out in the dark using electron‐only devices (EODs) with the structure FTO/SnO2/perovskite/2D/C60/BCP/Ag. The electron mobility was extracted from the trap‐free space‐charge‐limited region using the Mott–Gurney law, J = 9εε0µV2/8L3, where J is the current density, ε is the relative dielectric constant, ε 0 is the vacuum permittivity, µ is the effective electron mobility, V is the applied voltage, and L is the perovskite thickness. The voltage was corrected as V = V appl−V bi, where V bi is the built‐in potential estimated from the J–V characteristics of the EODs. The extracted values were used as effective transport parameters for comparing spacer‐treated stacks.

DLCP was performed using an Agilent E4980A LCR meter, following established methods [35]. The DC bias was scanned from 0 V to V OC; at each bias, an AC voltage (20–200 mV) was applied. A compensating offset DC voltage maintained constant forward bias. Data was collected via a Python interface and fitted to extract C 0 and C 1, from which trap density was calculated. Representative data at 10 kHz are presented for comparison.
