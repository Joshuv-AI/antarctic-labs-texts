Elephant random walks with multiple extractions and general reinforcement functions
Moumanti Podder and Archi Roy

Abstract
. We consider a generalized model of elephant random walks wherein the walker, during the ( n + 1 ) (n+1) -st time-stamp, draws from the past (i.e. the set { 1 , 2 , … , n } \{1,2,\ldots,n\} ) a sample of k k time-stamps, either with replacement or without, where k k may either remain fixed as n n grows, or k = k ⁡ ( n ) k=k(n) may grow with n n . Letting U n , 1 , U n , 2 , … , U n , k U_{n,1},U_{n,2},\ldots,U_{n,k} denote the time-stamps sampled, the step taken by the walker during the ( n + 1 ) (n+1) -st time-stamp, denoted X n + 1 X_{n+1} , is a ± 1 \pm 1 -valued random variable whose distribution depends on the proportion of ( + 1 ) (+1) -valued steps out of X U n , 1 , X U n , 2 , … , X U n , k X_{U_{n,1}},X_{U_{n,2}},\ldots,X_{U_{n,k}} via a reinforcement function f f . In this paper, we investigate the asymptotic behaviour – i.e. strong and weak convergence – of this random walk model under suitable assumptions made on the function f f (as well as on the sequence { k ⁡ ( n ) } \{k(n)\} when the sample size varies with n n ).



1.
Introduction

Motivated by the theory of increasing returns in economics (see, for instance,
[
1
]
), we consider a situation where two companies release similar products into the market around the same time, and we propose and analyze a model that tracks the sale, over time, of one of these products relative to the other. Our model involves a setting in which consumers possess imperfect information (for instance, see
[
2
]
) about the underlying quality of the competing products, and therefore, rely on informal signals such as peer choices, reviews, or aggregated opinions, to guide their decisions. These feedback mechanisms introduce path dependence into the market dynamics, often ultimately leading to the dominance of one product.

We begin with a brief and informal overview of this model, which is formally introduced in §
2
. In this work, time is considered discrete, and hence indexed by the set
\mathbb{N}_{0}
of non-negative integers. Each of the two competing products is assumed to have been released into the market at time
t=0
, and
t=n
indicates the moment at which the
n
-th sale happens (i.e. combining the sales of both types of products, a total of
n
items have been sold in the time-interval
[0,n]
). A simplifying assumption we make here is that each customer purchases precisely one product at a time. The
(n+1)
-st customer samples
k
customers, with or without replacement, from the past (i.e. from among the customers who made their purchases at time-stamps
t=1
,
t=2
,
\ldots
,
t=n
), and collects their responses (regarding the choices these customers made). The decision made by the
(n+1)
-st customer is a random variable whose distribution is
some
function of the responses obtained from the sample drawn. The sample size,
k
, can either remain constant as
n
varies, or be a function of
n
(in which case we write
k=k(n)
) that grows with
n
. Following the terminology of
[
27
]
, we refer to this framework as an elephant random walk model with
multiple extractions
.

Although our work is largely motivated by the market share example, the underlying structure has broad applicability across a range of fields
[
15
,
40
,
55
]
. Here, we highlight that a similar model has been considered in
[
28
]
and
[
27
]
, but with the sample size being an odd positive integer that remains fixed as
n
varies, and the reinforcement function
f
(see §
2
, and in particular, (
2.1
), for understanding the role of the reinforcement function in our model) being the majority function (in other words,
f(x)=1
for
x>1/2
, and
f(x)=0
for
x<1/2
). In this context, we also mention
[
26
]
that considers a two-colour generalized urn model wherein the urn configuration is updated at each time-stamp via a stochastic update rule that depends on the current configuration of the urn via a continuous reinforcement function. In
[
28
]
and
[
26
]
, the primary focus is on strong convergence and large deviation principles.

Denoting the product manufactured by the first company by
P_{1}
, and the second by
P_{2}
, we maintain a chart that tracks the sale of each of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
over time. Every time a unit of
P_{1}
is purchased, we mark this sale by adding a
(+1)
to our chart, while every time a unit of
P_{2}
is purchased, we mark it using a
(-1)
, so that the sum of all the markings up to and including time-stamp
n
equals the
relative performance
of
P_{1}
with respect to
P_{2}
up to and including time-stamp
n
(i.e. to what extent the sale of
P_{1}
exceeds, or trails behind, that of
P_{2}
, provided a total of
n
units, of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
combined, have been sold so far). Such a chart is evidently reminiscent of a random walk in which the position of the walker at time
n
is given by the relative performance of
P_{1}
with respect to
P_{2}
up to and including time-stamp
n
. This observation, in turn, opens up another perspective from which our model may be studied and may find applicability: that of the relatively novel notion of
elephant random walks
.

A third perspective from which our model can be viewed is that of a
generalized urn model
comprising balls of two colours, namely,
(+1)
and
(-1)
. At time
t=0
, we begin with an empty urn, and at time
t=1
, we add to it a ball which is of colour
(+1)
with a certain prespecified probability
q
, and of colour
(-1)
with the remaining probability
(1-q)
. At time-stamp
t=(n+1)
,
k
balls are chosen, uniformly at random, either with replacement or without, from the existing balls in the urn, and their colours noted. The sampled balls are then returned to the urn, along with a new ball whose colour is a
\pm 1
-valued random variable with probability distribution depending on the colours of the sampled balls.

The primary objective of this work is to understand the limiting behaviour of the stochastic process outlined above. In particular, when the model is studied as a version of the celebrated elephant random walk, with the position of the walker (or elephant) at time
n
denoted by
S_{n}
, we analyze the almost sure convergence of the sequence
\{S_{n}/n\}
, as well as the distributional convergence of a sequence obtained via a suitable scaling of
S_{n}
, as
n
approaches
\infty
. Such results on strong and weak convergence are achieved via the representation of our stochastic process as a suitable
stochastic approximation process
. In fact, results from the existing literature on stochastic approximation are instrumental in proving the results in this paper.

2.
Formal description of the model

2.1.
Model involving sampling with replacement

We begin by describing the model in which the
(n+1)
-st customer (equivalently, as the elephant is about to decide the step it would take at the
(n+1)
-st time-stamp, in our version of the elephant random walk) samples,
with
replacement,
k
customers from the past (equivalently, the elephant samples,
with
replacement,
k
of the steps that it has taken so far). At the very outset, we introduce the notation
[n]
to indicate the set
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
of the first
n
positive integers, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We set
X_{0}=S_{0}=0
, and
X_{1}
to be a Rademacher
(q)
random variable for a prespecified
q\in[0,1]
, i.e.

X_{1}=\begin{cases}1&\text{with probability }q,\\
-1&\text{with probability }(1-q).\end{cases}

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
U_{n,i}
, for
i\in[k]
, to be independent and identically distributed random variables, with each
U_{n,i}
distributed uniformly over the support
[n]
. Evidently, the set
\{U_{n,1},U_{n,2},\ldots,U_{n,k}\}
can be interpreted as a sample, drawn with replacement, from among the first
n
customers (equivalently, it is a set of time-stamps, drawn with replacement, from
[n]
, which is the past up to and including time-stamp
n
). We let
\mathcal{F}_{n}
indicate the
\sigma
-field consisting of all information pertaining to our stochastic process up to and including time-stamp
n
. Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and the value of
U_{n,i}
for each
i\in[k]
, we define

(2.1)

X_{n+1}=\begin{cases}1&\text{with probability }pf\left(C_{n}^{+}/k\right)+(1-p)\left\{1-f\left(C_{n}^{+}/k\right)\right\},\\
-1&\text{with probability }(1-p)f\left(C_{n}^{+}/k\right)+p\left\{1-f\left(C_{n}^{+}/k\right)\right\},\end{cases}

where
p\in(0,1)\setminus\{1/2\}
is a prespecified parameter that is often referred to as the
memory parameter
or
self-excitation parameter
in the literature,
f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is a predefined function that may be referred to as a
reinforcement function
or a
replacement function
(see
[
26
,
28
,
61
]
among others to find different examples of function classes to which
f
could belong) when the stochastic process is viewed as a generalized urn model, and (henceforth, for any event
A
, letting
\chi\{A\}
denote the indicator random variable for
A
)

(2.2)

C_{n}^{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\chi\left\{X_{U_{n,i}}=1\right\}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(X_{U_{n,i}}+1\right)

is the number of occurrences of
+1
in the sample drawn (in the context of customers making decisions based on responses sampled from past customers,
C_{n}^{+}
equals the number of customers sampled, up to and including time-stamp
n
, who chose product
P_{1}
, whereas when the model is studied as an elephant random walk,
C_{n}^{+}
equals the number of
(+1)
-valued steps that the elephant samples from the past, up to and including time-stampe
n
). We set

(2.3)

S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},

so that
S_{n}
denotes the position of the elephant (i.e. its displacement from the origin, which is where the walk begins from) at time-stamp
n
, or, equivalently, the relative performance of product
P_{1}
, in comparison to product
P_{2}
, up to and including time-stamp
n
. Note that it makes sense to exclude the case of
p=1/2
, since this value of
p
boils our stochastic process down to the ordinary simple symmetric random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
, and this has already been thoroughly studied in the literature.

The description of this stochastic process remains the same even when we replace
k
by
k(n)
, a function that maps the set
\mathbb{N}
of all positive integers to itself, with the restriction that
k(n)\leqslant n
. As mentioned in §
1
, we are primarily interested in
k(n)
that grows with
n
as
n
approaches
\infty
.

2.2.
Model involving sampling without replacement

The description of the model remains nearly the same as we consider a sampling scheme that is without replacement. Here, we begin with
X_{0}=0
, and random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{k}
that are independent and identically distributed as Rademacher
(q)
. For each
n\geqslant k
, at time-stamp
(n+1)
, a sample
(U_{n,1},U_{n,2},\ldots,U_{n,k})
, is drawn, without replacement, from the set
[n]
of past indices (or time-stamps). Evidently,
\left(U_{n,1},U_{n,2},\ldots,U_{n,k}\right)
denotes a
k
-dimensional random ordered tuple whose support is given by the set of all ordered
k
-tuples in which all the coordinates are distinct and each coordinate belongs to
[n]
, such that

(2.4)

\Prob\left[\left(U_{n,1},U_{n,2},\ldots,U_{n,k}\right)=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})\right]=\left\{{n\choose k}k!\right\}^{-1}\text{ for all distinct indices }i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n].

We define
C_{n}^{+}
, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, to be as in (
2.2
), and define the distribution of
X_{n+1}
, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and the realized value of
(U_{n,1},U_{n,2},\ldots,U_{n,k})
, as in (
2.1
). Finally, we define
S_{n}
to be as in (
2.3
).

Once again, we consider the version of this model where
k=k(n)
is a function of
n
(and that grows with
n
as
n
approaches
\infty
, with the restriction that
k(n)\leqslant n
for each
n
). We assume that
k(n)
is a monotonically increasing function of
n
, and we write
k(n)\uparrow\infty
to indicate that the sequence
\{k(n)\}
approaches
\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. The assumption that
k(n)\leqslant n
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, allows us to let
X_{1}
follow Rademacher
(q)
, and the distribution of
X_{n+1}
, for each
n\geqslant 1
, to be exactly as in (
2.1
), where
C_{n}^{+}
, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, is as defined in (
2.2
).

3.
Review of literature pertinent to the work in this paper

We now turn to a brief review of the key developments in the literature related to the elephant random walk (ERW) model. The classical ERW model was proposed by
[
62
]
with the motivation of introducing long memory into random walk models and studying how, in such cases, the asymptotic behavior differs from the more traditional simple random walk models where the walker takes each step independent of the past. In
[
62
]
, a one-dimensional random walk
\{S_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
on the
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice was considered, wherein the walker starts from a position
S_{0}
(the general convention is to take
S_{0}=0
) at time-stamp
t=0
, and during the first time-stamp, the walker takes a forward (i.e.
+1
) step with probability
q
and a backward (i.e.
-1
) step with probability
(1-q)
, where
q\in(0,1)
is prespecified. At all subsequent time-stamps, the walker keeps a complete memory of all of the steps taken so far. At time-stamp
(n+1)
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the walker takes a random step of unit size, either forward or backward, so that
S_{n+1}=S_{n}+X_{n+1}
, where
\{X_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N},n\geqslant 2\}
is a sequence of
(\pm 1)
-valued random variables defined recursively as follows:

X_{n+1}=\begin{cases}X_{U_{n}}\text{ with probability }p,\\
-X_{U_{n}}\text{ with probability }(1-p),\end{cases}

where
U_{n}
is a randomly chosen time-stamp from the past, i.e.
[n]
, and
p\in(0,1)
is referred to, in various contexts, as the
trust parameter
, the
memory parameter
or the
self-excitation parameter
, and its value may differ from that of
q
. Several extensions and variations of the classical ERW have since been proposed. For instance,
[
35
]
introduced a framework where the walker remembers only a few of the most recent steps, certain distant steps from the past, or a combination of both. In a related development,
[
36
]
proposed a model where the memory of the walker grows over time. A “lazy” version of the walk, wherein the walker may, during any time-stamp, choose to stay in the same position as they were in during the previous time-stamp, instead of moving forward or backward, was proposed by
[
34
]
. Random step sizes were incorporated into the model by
[
19
]
and
[
25
]
, while
[
20
]
recently introduced a version in which the memory itself is random. Reinforced variations of the walk have been explored in works such as
[
47
]
and
[
46
]
. Multidimensional extensions of the elephant random walk have also received considerable attention
[
8
,
10
,
50
,
52
, see, e.g.,]
. Other significant variants of the walk can be found in works such as
[
7
,
37
,
38
,
41
,
43
,
59
,
53
,
54
,
63
]
. More recently,
[
49
]
investigated limit theorems for a broad class of step-reinforced random walks that includes the classical ERW as a special case. Motivated by earlier studies such as
[
43
]
and
[
41
]
, they analyzed a model in which the walker either repeats a previously taken step or takes a new step with complementary probability.

The asymptotic behavior of the ERW is well investigated in the literature
[
12
,
14
,
16
,
42
]
. In this context,
[
17
]
proposed strong numerical evidence that the displacement distribution of ERWs in some regimes of the trust parameter
p
is non-Gaussian as well as non-Lévy. In an important contribution,
[
6
]
and
[
47
]
proposed a martingale approach in deriving the asymptotics, where results on weak convergence and large deviation are derived using the martingale central limit theorem and the law of iterated logarithms respectively. The martingale approach was also used by papers such as
[
9
]
,
[
14
]
and
[
60
]
. A powerful set of analytical tools was introduced in
[
4
]
, which studied the asymptotic behavior of the process across various regimes by reformulating the walk as a Pólya urn scheme
[see
39
, for a detailed discussion]
. In this paper, we take inspiration from existing works such as
[
18
]
,
[
52
]
and
[
65
]
, to derive the asymptotic results using a stochastic approximation
[
21
]
technique. Stochastic approximation (SA), originally introduced by
[
58
]
to find the root of a regression function via successive approximations, has since found wide application across various scientific domains (see
[
44
]
and
[
45
]
for reviews). While the classical SA theory focused on almost sure convergence to a unique limit, it was later extended to address weak convergence results as well, particularly in control and optimization theory
[
3
,
22
,
23
,
66
]
. To the best of our knowledge,
[
29
]
was the first to apply SA methods to analyze urn models with random replacement matrices, laying the groundwork for using SA in processes interpretable as generalized urn models, including the random walk model considered in this paper, as previously discussed. This line of work was further developed in
[
30
]
, where the authors studied a variant of the ERW on the nonnegative integer lattice in multiple dimensions, with memory-driven reinforcement and potential delay at each step. Although their results focused on almost sure convergence, their approach motivated us to adopt SA as the central tool to analyze the convergence behavior of our proposed random walk model. A very important contribution to this literature has been recently accomplished by
[
52
]
, who developed the SA theory for multidimensional ERW.

5.1.
Proofs of the results from §
4.1

We focus, in §
5
, on the model described in §
2.1
(i.e. where the sampling scheme employed is one with replacement).

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Recalling, from §
2.1
, the definitions of the i.i.d. random variables,
U_{n,i}
for all
i\in[k]
, we have

\Prob\left[U_{n,1}=i_{1},\ldots,U_{n,k}=i_{k}\right]=n^{-k}\quad\text{for all }i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n].

As described in §
1
, a parallel may be drawn between our model and a generalized urn scheme as follows: we consider an urn that is empty to begin with, and that, at time-stamp
n
, contains a single ball which is of colour
(+1)
with probability
q
and of colour
(-1)
with probability
(1-q)
. For
n\geqslant 2
, a sample of size
k
is drawn, with replacement, from the existing balls in the urn, and we let
C_{n}^{+}
(as defined in (
2.2
)) denote the number of
(+1)
-coloured balls appearing in this sample. The sampled balls are then returned to the urn, and a new ball, whose colour is random, and follows the same distribution as described in (
2.1
), is added to the urn. Evidently, the urn contains a total of
n
balls at time-stamp
n
, and evidently,
S_{n}
equals the difference between the number of
(+1)
-coloured balls and the number of
(-1)
-coloured balls in the urn at time-stamp
n
, where
S_{n}
is as defined in (
2.3
). The number of
(+1)
-coloured balls in the urn, at time-stamp
n
, is then given by
(S_{n}+n)/2
, and the number of
(-1)
-coloured balls is given by
(n-S_{n})/2
. Thus, we may write:

(5.1)

\Prob\left[C_{n}^{+}=i\big|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=\frac{1}{n^{k}}{k\choose i}\left(\frac{S_{n}+n}{2}\right)^{i}\left(\frac{n-S_{n}}{2}\right)^{k-i},\text{ for each }i\in[k]\cup\{0\},

where
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is (as defined earlier) the
\sigma
-field comprising all information pertaining to the generalized urn process up to and including time-stamp
n
. From (
5.1
) and (
2.1
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\Prob\left[S_{n+1}=S_{n}+1\big|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]={}

\displaystyle\Prob\left[X_{n+1}=1\big|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Prob\left[X_{n+1}=1\big|C_{n}^{+}=i,\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\Prob\left[C_{n}^{+}=i\big|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left[pf\left(\frac{i}{k}\right)+(1-p)\left\{1-f\left(\frac{i}{k}\right)\right\}\right]{k\choose i}\left(1+\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)^{i}\left(1-\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)^{k-i}

(5.2)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}g\left(\frac{i}{k}\right){k\choose i}\left(1+\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)^{i}\left(1-\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)^{k-i},

where the function
g
is as defined in (
4.1
). From (
5.2
) and the fact that
X_{n+1}\in\{\pm 1\}
, we deduce that

(5.3)

\E\left[X_{n+1}\big|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=2^{1-k}\sum_{i=0}^{k}g\left(\frac{i}{k}\right){k\choose i}\left(1+\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)^{i}\left(1-\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)^{k-i}-1.

At this point, we come to what may be considered the principal technique implemented in the proofs of all of the results stated in §
4
: that of representing our stochastic process as a stochastic approximation process. The specific representation to be considered depends on the set-up (i.e. whether the sampling scheme is with or without replacement, whether the sample size remains constant or grows with
n
, and whether we are interested in strong or weak convergence). For the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we write, for
n\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{S_{n+1}}{n+1}={}

\displaystyle\frac{S_{n}+X_{n+1}}{n+1}=\frac{S_{n}}{n}+\frac{1}{n+1}\left\{X_{n+1}-\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right\}

(5.4)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{S_{n}}{n}+\frac{1}{n+1}\left\{H\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)-\frac{S_{n}}{n}+X_{n+1}-H\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)\right\}=\frac{S_{n}}{n}+\gamma_{n}\left\{h\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)+\epsilon_{n+1}\right\},

where
H
is as defined in (
4.2
), and we set

(5.5)

h(x)=H(x)-x\text{ for each }x\in[-1,1],\quad\gamma_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\quad\text{and}\quad\epsilon_{n+1}=X_{n+1}-H\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right).

In the literature on stochastic approximations (see, for instance,
[
21
]
,
[
5
]
and
[
11
]
), the function
h
is referred to as the
drift function
, the sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\}
is referred to as the sequence of
step sizes
, and the sequence
\{\epsilon_{n+1}\}
is referred to as the sequence of
errors
. The representation of the sequence
\left\{S_{n}/n\right\}
in the form given by (
5.4
) is referred to as a stochastic approximation process. From (
4.2
) and (
5.3
), we see that

(5.6)

\E\left[X_{n+1}\big|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=H\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)\quad\implies\quad\E\left[\epsilon_{n+1}\big|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=0

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, thus establishing that the sequence of errors,
\{\epsilon_{n+1}\}
, as defined in (
5.5
), forms a martingale difference sequence.

We now come to a well-known result from the literature on stochastic approximation processes, as this is instrumental in proving all our results pertaining to the almost sure convergence of the sequence
\{S_{n}/n\}
:

Theorem 5.1

(Proposition 1.2.3 of
[
21
]
)
.

Let
F
be a continuous real-valued function such that, for a prespecified
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
F(x^{*})=\alpha
, and the following criteria are satisfied:

(5.7)

\displaystyle(F(x)-\alpha)(x-x^{*})<0\text{ for all }x\neq x^{*},

(5.8)

\displaystyle|F(x)|\leqslant K(1+|x|)\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R},\text{ for some constant }K>0.

Let
\{\gamma_{n}\}
be a sequence of positive reals decreasing towards
0
such that
\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}
diverges. Let
\{\epsilon_{n}\}
be another sequence of reals such that
\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}\epsilon_{n+1}
converges. Then the sequence
\{x_{n}\}
, defined iteratively by

(5.9)

x_{n+1}=x_{n}+\gamma_{n}\{F(x_{n})-\alpha+\epsilon_{n+1}\}\text{ for each }n\in\mathbb{N},

converges to
x^{*}
, for all initial values
x_{0}
.

Right away, we notice that the representation of the stochastic process
\{S_{n}/n\}
in (
5.4
) is of the same form as the iterative definition of
\{x_{n}\}
in (
5.9
), once we set
\alpha=0
, and replace
x_{n}
by
S_{n}/n
and the function
F
by the function
h
. Given that
H
, as defined in (
4.2
), is a polynomial defined on
[-1,1]
, and
h
is as defined in (
5.5
), it is evident that
h
is a continuous function. We now verify that (
5.8
) holds for the function
h
, and postpone the verification of (
5.7
) for
h
. Note, first, that we may expand the polynomial that
h
is, as is evident from its definition via (
5.5
) and (
4.2
), to write

(5.10)

\displaystyle h(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}x^{j},\text{ where }a_{j}=\begin{cases}2^{1-k}\sum_{i=0}^{k}g\left(\frac{i}{k}\right){k\choose i}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}(-1)^{j-\ell}{i\choose\ell}{k-i\choose j-\ell}&\text{for }j\in[k]\setminus\{1\},\\
2^{1-k}\sum_{i=0}^{k}g\left(\frac{i}{k}\right){k\choose i}(2i-k)-1&\text{for }j=1,\\
2^{1-k}\sum_{i=0}^{k}g\left(\frac{i}{k}\right){k\choose i}-1&\text{for }j=0.\end{cases}

This then allows us to write

\left|h(x)\right|\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left|a_{j}\right||x|^{j}\leqslant K(1+|x|)\text{ for all }x\in[-1,1],\quad\text{ where }K=\max\left\{\left|a_{0}\right|,\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left|a_{j}\right|\right\},

thereby proving that (
5.8
) holds for the function
h
, as desired.

Next, we note, from (
5.5
), that
\gamma_{n}=(n+1)^{-1}
, so that
\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}
, indeed, diverges. We now come to the analysis of the series
\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}\epsilon_{n+1}
. Since (
5.6
) is true, we have
\E\left[\gamma_{n}\epsilon_{n+1}\big|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=0
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so that the sequence
\{\gamma_{n}\epsilon_{n+1}\}
forms a martingale difference sequence as well. We now prove the following lemma, which allows us to conclude that yet one more criterion stated in Theorem
5.1
is satisfied:

Lemma 5.2
.

The sum
\sum_{n}\gamma_{n}\epsilon_{n+1}
converges almost surely.

Proof.

Let us define
M_{0}=0
and
M_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\epsilon_{i+1}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. As
\{\gamma_{n}\epsilon_{n+1}\}
, as justified above, forms a martingale difference sequence with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{n+1}\}
, the sequence
\{M_{n}\}
forms a martingale with respect to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{n+1}\}
(note that
M_{n}
, by definition, is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
). The
increasing process

\{I_{n}\}
, corresponding to
\{M_{n}\}
, is given by

\displaystyle I_{n}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E\left[\left(M_{i}-M_{i-1}\right)^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{i}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E\left[\gamma_{i}^{2}\epsilon_{i+1}^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{i}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}^{2}\E\left[\left\{X_{i+1}-H\left(\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)\right\}^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{i}\right]

(5.11)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(i+1)^{2}}\left[1+H^{2}\left(\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)-2H\left(\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)\E\left[X_{i+1}\big|\mathcal{F}_{i}\right]\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(i+1)^{2}}\left[1-H^{2}\left(\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)\right],

where the last line follows from the first identity stated in (
5.6
). Here, we have made use of the fact that
X_{i+1}\in\{\pm 1\}
, so that
X_{i+1}^{2}=1
, and we have also used the notation
H^{2}(x)
to mean
(H(x))^{2}
. By definition of the function
H
in (
4.2
), and the observation that
0\leqslant g(x)\leqslant 1
for all
x\in[0,1]
(for
g
as defined in (
4.1
), using the fact that
f
takes values in
[0,1]
), we have:

(5.12)

\displaystyle-1\leqslant H(x)\leqslant 2^{1-k}\sum_{i=0}^{k}{k\choose i}(1+x)^{i}(1-x)^{k-i}-1=1\text{ for all }x\in[-1,1].

Consequently, the final expression in (
5.11
) can be bounded above by
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(i+1)^{-2}
, which tells us that
I_{\infty}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{n}
is finite almost surely. By Theorem 4.5.2. of
[
24
]
, we conclude that the martingale

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\E\left[\left\{X_{k}-H_{k-1}\left(\frac{S_{k-1}}{k-1}\right)\right\}^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]=\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\left\{1-H_{k-1}^{2}\left(\frac{S_{k-1}}{k-1}\right)\right\}.

From (
5.42
), we know, given any
\epsilon>0
, that there exists some
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(c-\epsilon)(n+1)^{-\tau}\leqslant\beta_{n}\leqslant(c+\epsilon)(n+1)^{-\tau}
for all
n\geqslant N_{1}
. Using this and (

), we can write, for
n\geqslant(N_{1}+1)
:

\displaystyle I_{n}\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{N_{1}}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\left\{1-H_{k-1}^{2}\left(\frac{S_{k-1}}{k-1}\right)\right\}+(c-\epsilon)^{-2}\sum_{k=N_{1}+1}^{n}k^{-2+2\tau}.

Evidently, when
\tau<1/2
, the second series in the expression above converges as
n\rightarrow\infty
, so that
I_{\infty}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{n}
is finite almost surely. We now conclude, by Theorem 4.5.2. of
[
24
]
, that
\{M_{n}\}
converges almost surely to a finite random variable. This, along with (
5.42
), tells us that
\{(n+1)^{\tau}\beta_{n}M_{n+1}\}
converges almost surely to a finite random variable when
\tau<1/2
.

We now come to the cases of
\tau=1/2
and
\tau>1/2
. Since all criteria stated in Theorem
4.6
remain true in the hypothesis of Theorem
4.7
, we conclude that
S_{n}/n\rightarrow(2x^{*}-1)
almost surely. Let
\Omega
indicate the subset comprising all those sample points
\omega
such that
S_{n}(\omega)/n\rightarrow(2x^{*}-1)
. Evidently,
\Prob[\Omega]=1
. Since
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[0,1]
, the function
\hat{g}
, defined in the proof of Theorem
4.6
as
\hat{g}(x)=2g(x)-1
for all
x\in[0,1]
, is continuous, so that
\hat{g}\left(\left(S_{n}(\omega)/n+1\right)/2\right)\rightarrow\hat{g}(x^{*})
for each
\omega\in\Omega
. In what follows, let us fix some
\omega\in\Omega
. Given
\epsilon>0
, we can find
N_{2}=N_{2}(\omega)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\left|\hat{g}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{S_{n}(\omega)}{n}\right)\right)-\hat{g}^{2}(x^{*})\right|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}\quad\text{for all }n\geqslant N_{2}.

From (
2.1
) and (
4.1
), we have
0\leqslant g(x)\leqslant 1
for all
x\in[0,1]
, so that
\hat{g}(x)=2g(x)-1
takes values in
[-1,1]
for all
x\in[0,1]
. From (
5.27
), we have
H_{n}(x)\in[-1,1]
for all
x\in[-1,1]
. Using the fact that all these functions are uniformly bounded, and the uniform convergence deduced in (
5.23
), we conclude that

H_{n}^{2}(x)\rightarrow\hat{g}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}(1+x)\right)\text{ uniformly for all }x\in[-1,1]\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Given
\epsilon>0
, we can thus find
N_{3}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\left|H_{n}^{2}(x)-\hat{g}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}(1+x)\right)\right|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}\quad\text{for all }x\in[-1,1]\text{ and }n\geqslant N_{3}.

Consequently, for
n\geqslant\max\{N_{2},N_{3}\}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\left|H_{n}^{2}\left(\frac{S_{n}(\omega)}{n}\right)-\hat{g}^{2}(x^{*})\right|\leqslant\left|H_{n}^{2}\left(\frac{S_{n}(\omega)}{n}\right)-\hat{g}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{S_{n}(\omega)}{n}\right)\right)\right|+\left|\hat{g}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{S_{n}(\omega)}{n}\right)\right)-\hat{g}^{2}(x^{*})\right|<\epsilon.

This, in turn, yields:

(5.44)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}H_{n}^{2}\left(\frac{S_{n}(\omega)}{n}\right)\rightarrow\hat{g}^{2}(x^{*})\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,\text{ for each }\omega\in\Omega.

Recalling
N_{1}
defined right after (
5.43
), we conclude, for all
n\geqslant N_{1}+1
, that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{N_{1}}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}+(c+\epsilon)^{-2}\sum_{k=N_{1}+1}^{n}k^{2\tau-2}\leqslant\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\leqslant\sum_{k=2}^{N_{1}}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}+(c-\epsilon)^{-2}\sum_{k=N_{1}+1}^{n}k^{2\tau-2},

which tells us that (since
\epsilon>0
was chosen arbitrarily)

(5.45)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\ln n)^{-1}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}=c^{-2}\quad\text{when }\tau=1/2,

(5.46)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{1-2\tau}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}=c^{-2}(2\tau-1)^{-1}\quad\text{when }\tau>1/2.

It is evident that the series
\sum_{k=2}^{n}\gamma_{k-1}^{2}/\beta_{k-1}^{2}
diverges as
n\rightarrow\infty
, when
\tau\geqslant 1/2
. This, along with (
5.43
), (
5.44
) and Lemma 2.2.13. of
[

and for each

(5.47)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-1}I_{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\left\{1-H_{k-1}^{2}\left(\frac{S_{k-1}(\omega)}{k-1}\right)\right\}=1-\hat{g}^{2}(x^{*}).

This ensures that one of the criteria stated in the hypothesis of the martingale central limit theorem (see, for instance, Corollary 2.1.10. of
[
21
]
, or Theorem 2 of
[
13
]
) holds.

Next, we verify that the Lyapunov condition (see §2.1.4. of
[
21
]
) for
\tau=1/2
. Making use of a computation very similar to (
5.20
), and letting
N_{1}
be as defined right after (
5.43
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\E\left[\left|M_{k}-M_{k-1}\right|^{4}\big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]=\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\left(\frac{\gamma_{k-1}}{\beta_{k-1}}\right)^{4}\E\left[Z_{k}^{4}\big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\left(\frac{\gamma_{k-1}}{\beta_{k-1}}\right)^{4}\left[1+2H_{k-1}^{2}\left(\frac{S_{k-1}}{k-1}\right)-3H_{k-1}^{4}\left(\frac{S_{k-1}}{k-1}\right)\right]

(5.48)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\frac{4}{3}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\left(\frac{\gamma_{k-1}}{\beta_{k-1}}\right)^{4}\leqslant\frac{4}{3}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left[\sum_{k=2}^{N_{1}}\left(\frac{\gamma_{k-1}}{\beta_{k-1}}\right)^{4}+(c-\epsilon)^{-4}\sum_{k=N_{1}+1}^{n}k^{4\tau-4}\right].

For
\tau=1/2
, the second series within the square brackets in the expression above will converge, and this, along with (
5.45
), tells us that the entire expression of (
5.48
) converges to
0
as

.

Next, we verify that the Lindeberg condition (see assumption A2. of Corollary 2.1.10. of
[

]
, or Equation 2 of
[

) is true in our set-up for
\tau>1/2
. Fixing any

5.46

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\E\left[\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}Z_{k}^{2}\chi\left\{\left|\frac{\gamma_{k-1}Z_{k}}{\beta_{k-1}}\right|\geqslant\epsilon\sqrt{\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}}\right\}\Bigg|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\epsilon^{-2}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-2}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\E\left[\left\{\epsilon\sqrt{\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)}\right\}^{2}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}Z_{k}^{2}\chi\left\{\left|\frac{\gamma_{k-1}Z_{k}}{\beta_{k-1}}\right|\geqslant\epsilon\sqrt{\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}}\right\}\Bigg|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\epsilon^{-2}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-2}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\E\left[\left|\frac{\gamma_{k-1}Z_{k}}{\beta_{k-1}}\right|^{4}\right]\leqslant\epsilon^{-2}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-2}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\left(\frac{\gamma_{k-1}}{\beta_{k-1}}\right)^{4}\E\left[|Z_{k}|^{4}\big|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\epsilon^{-2}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-2}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\left(\frac{\gamma_{k-1}}{\beta_{k-1}}\right)^{4}\E\left[1+2H_{k-1}^{2}\left(\frac{S_{k-1}}{k-1}\right)-3H_{k-1}^{4}\left(\frac{S_{k-1}}{k-1}\right)\right]

(5.49)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\frac{4}{3}\epsilon^{-2}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{\beta_{k-1}^{2}}\right)^{-2}\left[\sum_{k=2}^{N_{1}}\left(\frac{\gamma_{k-1}}{\beta_{k-1}}\right)^{4}+(c-\epsilon)^{-4}\sum_{k=N_{1}+1}^{n}k^{4\tau-4}\right]=\Theta\left(n^{2-4\tau}\right)\left[\Theta(1)+\Theta\left(n^{4\tau-3}\right)\right],

which is
o(1)
since
\tau>1/2
. Combining (
5.47
), (
5.48
) and (
5.49
), as well as (
5.45
) and (
5.46
) we deduce, via Corollary 2.1.10. of
[

]
:

(5.50)

\displaystyle(\ln n)^{-1/2}M_{n}\xlongrightarrow{D}N\left(0,c^{-2}\left\{1-\hat{g}^{2}(x^{*})\right\}\right)\quad\text{when }\tau=1/2,

(5.51)

\displaystyle n^{1/2-\tau}M_{n}\xlongrightarrow{D}N\left(0,c^{-2}(2\tau-1)^{-1}\left\{1-\hat{g}^{2}(x^{*})\right\}\right)\quad\text{when }\tau>1/2.

The final stage of the proof concerns itself with the convergence of
\{R_{n}\}
, where
\{R_{n}\}
is as defined in (
5.41
). It suffices for us to prove that

(i)

\{R_{n}\}
converges almost surely when
\tau<1/2
,

(ii)

(\ln n)^{-1/2}R_{n}
converges in probability to
0
when
\tau=1/2
,

(iii)

and
n^{1/2-\tau}R_{n}
converges in probability to
0
when
\tau>1/2
.

From (
5.25
), (
5.23
), (
5.38
) and the definition of
\hat{g}
in the proof of Theorem
4.6
, we know that
h_{n}\rightarrow\hat{h}
uniformly on
[-1,1]
. From (
5.41
), (
5.39
), (
5.38
) and (
5.25
), we have

(5.52)

\displaystyle|R_{n}|\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\beta_{i}}\left|H_{i}\left(\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)-\hat{g}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)\right)\right|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\beta_{i}}\left|\hat{h}\left(\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)+\tau\left\{\frac{S_{i}}{i}-(2x^{*}-1)\right\}\right|.

We focus on the first series of (
5.52
). Recall that
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[0,1]
, so that
\hat{g}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[0,1]
as well. By (
5.34
) (which, recall, follows from Exercise 6.8 of
[
57
]
and (
5.42
), we obtain

\displaystyle(n+1)^{\tau}\beta_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\beta_{i}}\left|H_{i}\left(\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)-\hat{g}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)\right)\right|\leqslant O\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\beta_{i}k(i)}\right)=\Theta\left((n+1)^{\tau}\beta_{n}\right)\Theta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(i+1)^{\tau-1}k(i)^{-1}\right),

and for
\tau<1/2
, this converges because of the first of the two criteria in (
4.7
). Likewise, we have

\sqrt{\frac{(n+1)}{\ln(n+1)}}\beta_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\beta_{i}}\left|H_{i}\left(\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)-\hat{g}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)\right)\right|=\Theta\left(\sqrt{n+1}\beta_{n}\right)\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ln(n+1)}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(i+1)^{-1/2}k(i)^{-1}\right),

and this converges to
0
by the first criterion of (
4.8
) when
\tau=1/2
. Finally, we have

\sqrt{n+1}\beta_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\beta_{i}}\left|H_{i}\left(\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)-\hat{g}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)\right)\right|\leqslant\Theta\left((n+1)^{\tau}\beta_{n}\right)\Theta\left((n+1)^{1/2-\tau}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(i+1)^{\tau-1}k(i)^{-1}\right),

and this converges to
0
by the first criterion of (
4.9
) when
\tau>1/2
.

We now come to the second series of (
5.52
). Since
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[0,1]
, we have
\hat{h}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[-1,1]
from (
5.25
). Since
x^{*}
is a fixed point of
g
, we have
\hat{h}(2x^{*}-1)=0
. Recalling
\tau
from the statement of Theorem
4.7
, we see that
\hat{h}^{\prime}(2x^{*}-1)=-\tau
. For all
x\in[-1,1]
, there exists some
\xi
between
(2x^{*}-1)
and
x
such that:

\displaystyle\hat{h}(x)=\hat{h}(2x^{*}-1)+\hat{h}^{\prime}(2x^{*}-1)\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}+\frac{\hat{h}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\implies{}

\displaystyle\left|\hat{h}(x)+\tau\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\zeta\in[-1,1]}\hat{h}^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}^{2}=M\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}^{2},

where
M
is finite since
\sup_{\zeta\in[-1,1]}\hat{h}^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)
is finite, since
\hat{h}^{\prime\prime}
is continuous on the compact interval
[-1,1]
. This allows us to write

(5.53)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\beta_{i}}\left|\hat{h}\left(\frac{S_{i}}{i}\right)+\tau\left\{\frac{S_{i}}{i}-(2x^{*}-1)\right\}\right|\leqslant M\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\beta_{i}}\left\{\frac{S_{i}}{i}-(2x^{*}-1)\right\}^{2},

so that it suffices for us, when analyzing the second series of (
5.52
), to consider the series on the right side of (
5.53
).

Using (
5.37
), (
5.38
), (
5.27
) (the latter two guarantee that
-2\leqslant h_{n}(x)\leqslant 2
for all
x\in[-1,1]
), and the fact that
\E[\{X_{n+1}-H_{n}(S_{n}/n)\}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{n}]=1-H_{n}^{2}(S_{n}/n)\leqslant 1
, we have

\displaystyle\E\left[\left\{\frac{S_{n+1}}{n+1}-(2x^{*}-1)\right\}^{2}\Bigg|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=\E\left[\left\{\frac{S_{n}}{n}-(2x^{*}-1)+\frac{1}{n+1}\left\{Y_{n+1}-h_{n}\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)\right\}+\frac{1}{n+1}h_{n}\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)\right\}^{2}\Bigg|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{S_{n}}{n}-(2x^{*}-1)\right\}^{2}+\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\E\left[\left\{X_{n+1}-H_{n}\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)\right\}^{2}\Bigg|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]+\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}h_{n}^{2}\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)+\frac{2}{n+1}\left\{\frac{S_{n}}{n}-(2x^{*}-1)\right\}

(5.54)

\displaystyle h_{n}\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right)\leqslant\left\{\frac{S_{n}}{n}-(2x^{*}-1)\right\}^{2}+\frac{5}{(n+1)^{2}}+\frac{2}{n+1}\left\{\frac{S_{n}}{n}-(2x^{*}-1)\right\}h_{n}\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}\right).

We now suitably bound the third term of (
5.54
). Since
\hat{h}^{\prime}(2x^{*}-1)=-\tau
and
\hat{h}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[-1,1]
(since
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[0,1]
), given any
0<\eta<\tau
, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\left|\hat{h}^{\prime}(x)+\tau\right|\leqslant\eta
whenever
|x-(2x^{*}-1)|\leqslant\epsilon
. For any
x\in[2x^{*}-1,2x^{*}-1+\epsilon]
, we have (keeping in mind that
\hat{h}(2x^{*}-1)=0
):

\displaystyle\int_{2x^{*}-1}^{x}(-\eta)dt\leqslant\int_{2x^{*}-1}^{x}\left(\hat{h}^{\prime}(t)+\tau\right)dt=\hat{h}(x)+\tau\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}\leqslant\int_{2x^{*}-1}^{x}\eta dt

\displaystyle\implies{}

\displaystyle(-\tau-\eta)\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}^{2}\leqslant\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}\hat{h}(x)\leqslant(-\tau+\eta)\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}^{2},

and a similar bound can be shown to hold when
x\in[2x^{*}-1-\epsilon,2x^{*}-1]
. These bounds, along with (
5.34
) (which is applicable since
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[0,1]
), allows us to write, for all
x\in[2x^{*}-1-\epsilon,2x^{*}-1+\epsilon]
:

\displaystyle\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}h_{n}(x)\leqslant|x-(2x^{*}-1)|\left|h_{n}(x)-\hat{h}(x)\right|+\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}\hat{h}(x)

(5.55)

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\frac{(2p-1)||f^{\prime\prime}||_{\infty}|x-(2x^{*}-1)|}{4k(n)}+(-\tau+\eta)\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}^{2}\leqslant\frac{M^{\prime}}{k(n)}+(-\tau+\eta)\{x-(2x^{*}-1)\}^{2},

where we set
M^{\prime}=(2p-1)||f^{\prime\prime}||_{\infty}/4
. For
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
\epsilon>0
, we set
T=\inf\left\{n\geqslant j:\left|S_{n}/n\right|>\epsilon\right\}
. Setting
u_{n}=\E\left[\left\{S_{n}/n-(2x^{*}-1)\right\}^{2}\chi\{T>n\}\right]
for each
n>j
, taking the unconditional expectation of both sides of (
5.54
), and incorporating the bound in (
5.55
), we obtain:

(5.56)

\displaystyle u_{n+1}\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\left\{1-2(n+1)^{-1}(\tau-\eta)\right\}u_{n}+5(n+1)^{-2}+2M^{\prime}(n+1)^{-1}k(n)^{-1}.

Setting
\tau^{1}=(\tau-\eta)
, and defining
v_{n}=\{1-2\tau^{1}(n+1)^{-1}\}
and
w_{n}=v_{j}v_{j+1}\ldots v_{n}
for each
n\geqslant j
, we have, iterating the recurrence in (
5.56
) (similar to the derivation of (
5.40
)):

(5.57)

\displaystyle u_{n+1}\leqslant w_{n}u_{j}+5w_{n}\sum_{i=j}^{n}w_{i}^{-1}(i+1)^{-2}+2M^{\prime}w_{n}\sum_{i=j}^{n}w_{i}^{-1}k(i)^{-1}(i+1)^{-1},

and similar to (
5.42
), we have
w_{n}(n+1)^{2\tau^{1}}
converging to some constant
c^{\prime\prime}\in(0,\infty)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

The first two terms on the right side of (
5.57
) can be taken care of in the same way as the corresponding terms in the proof of Theorem 2.2.12. of
[
21
]
, and we need only care about the third term on the right side of (
5.57
). Recall
N_{1}
defined right after (
5.43
) (with
(c-\epsilon)(n+1)^{-\tau}\leqslant\beta_{n}\leqslant(c+\epsilon)(n+1)^{-\tau}
for all
n\geqslant N_{1}
), and given
\epsilon>0
, let
N_{4}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
(c^{\prime\prime}-\epsilon)(i+1)^{-2\tau^{1}}\leqslant w_{i}\leqslant(c^{\prime\prime}+\epsilon)(i+1)^{-2\tau^{1}}
for all
i\geqslant N_{4}
. For
n\geqslant N=\max\{N_{1},N_{4}\}
, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\beta_{i}}w_{i}\sum_{t=j}^{i-1}w_{t}^{-1}k(t)^{-1}(t+1)^{-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\gamma_{i}w_{i}}{\beta_{i}}\sum_{t=j}^{i-1}\frac{1}{w_{t}k(t)(t+1)}+O\left[\sum_{i=N+1}^{n}(i+1)^{\tau-2\tau^{1}-1}\sum_{t=j}^{i-1}w_{t}^{-1}k(t)^{-1}(t+1)^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle O(1)+O\left[\sum_{i=N+1}^{n}(i+1)^{\tau-2\tau^{1}-1}\left\{\sum_{t=j}^{N-1}w_{t}^{-1}k(t)^{-1}(t+1)^{-1}+\sum_{t=N}^{i-1}w_{t}^{-1}k(t)^{-1}(t+1)^{-1}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle O(1)+O\left[\sum_{i=N+1}^{n}(i+1)^{\tau-2\tau^{1}-1}\sum_{t=j}^{N-1}w_{t}^{-1}k(t)^{-1}(t+1)^{-1}\right]+O\left[\sum_{i=N+1}^{n}(i+1)^{\tau-2\tau^{1}-1}\sum_{t=N}^{i-1}(t+1)^{2\tau^{1}-1}k(t)^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle O(1)+O\left[n^{\tau-2\tau^{1}}\right]+O\left[\sum_{i=N+1}^{n}(i+1)^{\tau-2\tau^{1}-1}\sum_{t=N}^{i-1}(i+1)^{2\tau^{1}}(t+1)^{-1}k(t)^{-1}\right]

(5.58)

\displaystyle={}

Proof of Corollary

All we have to do is make sure that (
