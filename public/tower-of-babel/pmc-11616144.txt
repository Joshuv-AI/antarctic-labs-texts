Delayed percutaneous intradiscal hydrogel herniation causing neurological injury after minor trauma: illustrative case
Authors: Sowmya Gopalakrishnan, Rena Far, Catherine Veilleux, Ganesh Swamy, Michael M. H. Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDPercutaneous intradiscal hydrogel injection has been used to treat low-back pain (LBP) due to degenerative disc disease with or without mild radicular pain. Complications from these procedures are underreported. In this case lesson, the authors present a rare case of a patient with herniated intradiscal hydrogel following a minor trauma leading to neurological injury.OBSERVATIONSA 36-year-old female who had been previously treated with L4–5 and L5–S1 intradiscal hydrogel injections for LBP presented with painful, progressive right-sided lower-extremity weakness after experiencing a twisting trauma. Cross-sectional imaging results revealed a herniated hydrogel-based disc implant in the spinal canal. She underwent an urgent L4–5 laminectomy for decompression and removal of the herniated implant. Low-back pain (LBP) is the leading cause of disability and pain worldwide.1 In 2020, 619 million people were affected by LBP globally, and 12 million Americans were diagnosed with LBP each year.1 Degenerative disc disease (DDD) is thought to be a contributor to the pathogenesis of LBP.2 The degenerative cascade begins with disc desiccation, leading to a loss of disc height and stress redistribution within the annulus fibrosus, adjacent endplates, vertebral bodies, and facet joints.2 This is thought to incite local production of inflammatory mediators, causing pathological neoinnervation and sensitization of nociceptors, leading to LBP.3 The first-line management for LBP due to DDD is nonoperative. This can include exercise, physical therapy, nonopioid oral analgesic therapies, and spinal injections.4 While surgical management for isolated LBP is controversial, options include lumbar fusion or lumbar arthroplasty.5 A recent Canadian multicenter observational study showed that lumbar fusion achieved clinically significant improvement in LBP and disability scores at the 1-year follow-up.6, 7 However, surgical management is often invasive and associated with adverse events such as poor postoperative pain control, surgical site infection, pseudoarthrosis, and adjacent segment disease.6–9

Percutaneous intradiscal hydrogel injection is a technique advocated by interventional pain physicians for the treatment of chronic LBP with or without mild radicular pain due to DDD.10 These procedures have been positioned as an intermediate treatment option between conservative and surgical modalities for addressing LBP due to DDD.10, 11 Thermosensitive injectable hydrogels can be used to augment the nucleus pulposus and can promote regeneration of the intervertebral disc by mimicking the extracellular environment of the intervertebral disc, increasing intradiscal height, and preventing further degeneration of the intervertebral disc.12 Early in vitro and animal studies suggest that hydrogel can facilitate the regeneration of nucleus pulposus and can increase the mechanical function of the degenerative motion segment, particularly in patients with early or moderate DDD.13–16

In a 23-patient, open-label feasibility study conducted by Mazza et al., patients with moderate to severe LBP experienced significant improvement within 24 weeks of injecting a single dose of HYALODISC hydrogel.17 In a 22-patient feasibility study evaluating Hydrafil hydrogel technology (ReGelTec Inc.), LBP, as measured by the numeric rating scale (NRS) for pain, decreased from a score of 7 preinjection to 1 at the 6-month follow-up (p < 0.001).18 Similarly, patient disability scores as measured using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) improved from 54 to 2 at the 6-month follow-up (p < 0.001).18

While early evidence suggests that percutaneous intradiscal hydrogel injection can improve LBP, complications that can arise from these procedures are rarely reported.11 In this paper, we present a percutaneous intradiscal injection case causing neurological injury after a minor trauma.

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

A 36-year-old female presented to the emergency department at the Foothills Medical Center in Calgary, Alberta, Canada, with a sudden onset of right-sided L5 radiculopathy and weakness after a fall in which she twisted her right leg and lower back on an escalator at the airport. On examination, she had 1/5 motor power in right dorsiflexion, big toe extension, and plantarflexion. All other lower-extremity myotomes had 5/5 motor power. She had a positive right-sided straight leg raise at 10°. Sensation to pinprick in the right L5 dermatome was reduced. She had an intact voluntary anal contraction and perianal sensation to light touch. Her postvoid residual was 100 ml.

Her past medical history was significant for chronic LBP and right leg pain secondary to DDD. She had undergone L4–5 and L5–S1 percutaneous intradiscal hydrogel injection (Hydrafil) by interventional radiology 1 year prior to her presentation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed before the hydrogel injection showed L4–5 DDD with evidence of a central annular tear and a right-sided L5–S1 paracentral disc herniation causing impingement of the S1 nerve root (Fig. 1). Evidence of intraprocedural hydrogel extrusion was observed. A computed tomography (CT) scan showed small amounts of the extruded hydrogel at L4–5 and L5–S1 without causing a mass effect on the thecal sac (Fig. 2). Investigations

Lumbar spine CT after her fall showed a right-sided L4–5 paracentral hyperdense hydrogel material in the spinal canal, causing severe spinal stenosis (Fig. 3). MRI examination re-demonstrated significant spinal stenosis and right lateral recess stenosis from the extruded hydrogel material (Fig. 4). FIG. 3.Posttrauma sagittal (A) and axial (B) lumbar CT. Treatment

The patient was taken urgently to the operating room for an open L4–5 laminectomy, decompression, and removal of the herniated hydrogel. Intraoperatively, a large extruded hydrogel implant and loose disc fragments were found in the epidural space, causing significant compression of the traversing right L5 nerve root and thecal sac. The hydrogel was removed with ease in a piecemeal fashion using standard pituitary instruments and curettes (Fig. 5). The hydrogel had a rubbery consistency and did not appear to elicit an inflammatory response to the surrounding neural elements. FIG. 5.Resected hydrogel implant.

Postoperative Course

Postoperatively, the patient had complete resolution of her right lower-extremity pain. Her right-sided motor power improved to 3/5 in dorsiflexion, 1/5 for toe extension, and 2/5 for plantar flexion. She continued to have patchy numbness to the right lateral leg and foot. There were no complications from the surgical procedure.

At the 1-year follow-up, she had complete resolution of her right lower-extremity weakness, except for 2/5 power in her big toe extension. She was able to walk long distances independently but required continued use of an ankle-foot orthosis. She has persistent numbness to pinprick in the right L5 dermatome. Her NRS LBP intensity score was 2/10.

Discussion

Observations

Our patient was treated with a percutaneous intradiscal hydrogel implant, Hydrafil, at L4–5 and L5–S1 for chronic LBP due to DDD. Twisting trauma and a fall led to herniation of the hydrogel implant at L4–5 into the spinal canal, which caused painful weakness of the right leg. This version of the intradiscal hydrogel implant was granted a US Food and Drug Administration investigational device exemption in September 2023.19 Its safety and effectiveness are currently being evaluated in a 225-patient multicenter randomized controlled trial.20 To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of this particular implant causing neurological injury requiring urgent spinal decompression and removal of the implant.

Hydrafil is made using a combination of poly-N-isopropylacrylamide, polyvinyl alcohol, polyvinyl pyrrolidone, polyethylene glycol, and barium sulfate.18, 19 At room temperature, this material exists in a liquid state. Upon injection, this material seeps into the cracks and deformities within the annulus fibrosus and ultimately solidifies as a cohesive implant within the disc space.18 The disc space to be treated is localized using a posterolateral approach with a 17-gauge needle under fluoroscopic visualization.19 The needle cannula and stylet are introduced and advanced to the central disc region to prepare for the hydrogel injection.19 The stylet is removed, and the primed preassembled ReGelTec system is connected.19 The hydrogel is introduced under the controlled delivery device. Once complete filling is observed, any back pressure is released, and the delivery system is disconnected from the needle. The stylet is reintroduced to push any remaining hydrogel into the disc space. Once the hydrogel has solidified, the needle and stylet are removed. The entire procedure takes approximately 30 minutes to complete.19

An unpublished report by ReGelTec Inc. claimed that Hydrafil hydrogel was effective in reducing the NRS score for back pain from 7.2 (pretreatment) to 2.2 at the 1-year follow-up (n = 58). At 2 years, the NRS score for pain further decreased to 1.8 (n = 17).21 A reduction in the ODI from 58.6 to 11 at 1 and 2 years of follow-up, respectively, was also reported.21

There is a paucity of data on complications associated with intradiscal hydrogel injection in the literature.11, 17, 18, 22, 23 There have been three previous reports of other intradiscal hydrogel products causing neurological injury at variable time periods after implantation.23–25 In all three cases, the hydrogel implant was found to migrate into the spinal canal, causing varying degrees of spinal stenosis. Akgun et al.24 reported hydrogel extrusion 2 days after implantation, while Durdag et al.25 and Tufan et al.26 reported hydrogel expulsion at 4 months and 6 months, respectively. Our patient experienced hydrogel expulsion 1 year after implantation. Given the small number of reported cases, it remains unclear whether the risk of implant expulsion decreases over time. Furthermore, long-term follow-up of these patients is lacking, and the selection criteria for patients undergoing percutaneous hydrogel disc implantantation are variable in the literature.11

Risk factors for hydrogel extrusion causing neurological injury are yet to be elucidated. In our case, and in all previously reported cases, there was evidence of a preexisting annular injury at the level of hydrogel implantation. Injection of hydrogel material into a collapsed disc space would invariably further increase the intradiscal pressure at rest and during normal weight-bearing activities. A preexisting annular tear and/or posterior longitudinal ligament injury coupled with increased intradiscal pressures can provide a natural pathway of minimal resistance for the hydrogel to extrude through. Moreover, the needle used to deliver the hydrogel can further create a defect within the annulus fibrosus. There is also a lack of consensus on the ideal volume of hydrogel injection that is sufficient to provide relief from LBP while minimizing the risk of extrusion. Larger injected volumes could be associated with an increased likelihood of hydrogel extrusion causing neurological injury. Volume based on patient-specific characteristics such as extent of DDD, disc volume, disc height, and level of interest may increase the therapeutic index of these procedures. Furthermore, increased hydrogel fixation time before needle removal may also reduce the risk of implant expulsion.

In our patient, evidence of intraprocedural leakage of the hydrogel material through a preexisting annular defect was observed. This likely increased the chance of implant herniation in the context of a minor trauma. It remains unclear whether these partially herniated hydrogel implants should be removed preemptively when asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic to reduce the risk of overt herniation causing neurological deficit. In our experience, the hydrogel could be removed easily through standard discectomy approaches and using standard surgical instruments.

One year after hydrogel removal, the patient experienced sustained improvement in her LBP. There are several possible explanations. It has been postulated that the discectomy procedure itself, via coagulation of the disc space, can disrupt the sinuvertebral nerve that innervates the intervertebral disc and can lead to improvements in LBP.27–29 Hydrogel injection might have aided in the regeneration of the intervertebral disc by mimicking the extracellular environment and disrupting the inflammatory pathways causing pain.30 Several in vitro and animal studies have shown improvements in the histological grade of intervertebral discs following hydrogel insertion.2, 3, 31 Finally, the natural history of LBP is generally favorable.32 It is possible that the patient’s LBP would have improved without intervention.

In conclusion, intradiscal hydrogel herniation leading to neurological injury is a rare complication. Complications such as the one described in our case lesson are suspected to be underreported. Future research is needed on appropriate patient selection, risk factors for implant herniation, and strategies to mitigate this risk.

Lessons

Main Lessons

Intradiscal hydrogels can herniate into the spinal canal and can cause significant morbidity. Risk factors for delayed herniation are not yet elucidated but could be related to preexisting disruptions to the annulus and/or posterior longitudinal ligament, excess volume of hydrogel injection, and insufficient fixation time. Both patients and practitioners must be aware of the risks before proceeding with such treatments.

Limitations of Findings

It is unknown whether the clinical findings and lessons from this case are specific to Hydrafil or whether they are generalizable to all hydrogels inserted in the intradiscal space.
