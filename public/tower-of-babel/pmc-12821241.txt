Longitudinal trajectories of depressive symptoms in adolescent students
Authors: Mian Li, Zejun Huang, Jinqi Ding, Huaineng Wu, Sugai Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLongitudinal studies examining depressive symptom trajectories in adolescents remain limited, particularly in school-based populations. This study aimed to identify psychosocial risk and protective factors associated with depressive symptom trajectories in a cohort of Chinese adolescents.MethodsA four-semester longitudinal survey was conducted among 1,145 primary and secondary school students using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and the Mental Health Diagnostic Test Scale (MHT). Quasi-Poisson generalized linear models adjusted for overdispersion in PHQ-9 scores. Latent class mixed models delineated distinct depressive symptom trajectories. Binary logistic regression evaluated predictors of high-risk group membership, and generalized linear mixed-effects models assessed time-by-group interactions, adjusting for age, sex, and grade.ResultsAcross four time points, 27.9%–32.2% of adolescents scored ≥ 5 on the PHQ-9, indicating elevated risk for depression. Learning anxiety was the most prevalent anxiety subtype (41.9%-49.1%). Significant risk factors associated with higher depressive symptom scores included learning anxiety, hopelessness, interpersonal anxiety, sensitiveness, impulsive tendency, and temper loss, whereas family warmth and help-seeking behavior served as protective. Depressive symptoms in the past year were strong predictors of current depressive symptom severity. Two distinct depressive symptom trajectories were identified: a stable low-risk group and a high-risk group with worsening symptoms. The high-risk group, particularly among sixth-grade students, exhibited a sharper increase in loneliness over time, with hopelessness and somatic anxiety as key predictors.ConclusionsThis study delineates the developmental trajectories of depressive symptoms in adolescents and identifies modifiable psychosocial factors associated with high-risk patterns. The findings underscore the importance of early identification and targeted, school-based interventions to improve adolescent mental health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03874-8.

Introduction

Depression during adolescence represents a critical public health concern with far-reaching consequences for cognitive development, psychosocial functioning, and long-term mental health outcomes [1]. It is a leading risk factor for functional impairment and suicide among youth globally [2]. Longitudinal and meta-analytic studies have consistently shown that depressive symptoms in adolescence are associated with an elevated risk of adult depression, anxiety disorders, substance misuse, and poor general health [3–5]. These adverse trajectories frequently emerge in early adolescence, emphasizing the critical need for early identification and timely intervention.

Emerging evidence from population-based cohort studies has revealed a complex interplay between anxiety and depressive symptoms from early childhood through mid-adolescence [6–8]. Notably, early anxiety symptoms often precede and predict persistent depressive courses [3], suggesting that comorbid presentations may serve as early indicators of chronic emotional distress. In addition, psychosocial adversities—such as hopelessness, persistent irritability, familial conflict, and low levels of help-seeking—further heighten vulnerability and complicate the course of recovery [9–11]. Adolescents with enduring depressive symptoms into early adulthood are more likely to experience poorer mental health outcomes, reduced academic performance, and impaired occupational functioning [4, 12], reinforcing the need for preventive strategies and sustained care.

In China, recent data from the Global Burden of Disease study have identified depression as one of the leading contributors to disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) among adolescents aged 10–14 years [13]. Despite this substantial burden, depressive symptoms in this age group often go underdetected and undertreated, partly due to limited routine screening and insufficient longitudinal monitoring. School-based mental health initiatives incorporating validated screening instruments—such as the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and the Mental Health Diagnostic Test Scale (MHT)—offer promising avenues for systematic identification and early intervention [14–19]. When integrated into structured, longitudinal follow-up frameworks, these tools may provide valuable insights into the developmental course and contextual determinants of adolescent depression [3]. However, most existing studies in China have focused on cross-sectional prevalence or short-term symptom assessment, offering only a limited understanding of how depressive symptoms evolve over time and how psychosocial risk factors interact with these trajectories. Consequently, there remains a lack of longitudinal evidence elucidating the dynamic course and predictors of adolescent depressive symptoms within the Chinese sociocultural context.

To address this gap, the present longitudinal cohort study investigated the prevalence and developmental trajectories of depressive symptoms among adolescents over a two-year period. Specifically, we examined the impact of psychological and social factors on depressive symptom dynamics. By adopting a prospective design with repeated assessments in a large school-based sample, this study offers novel insights into the temporal patterns and contextual determinants of adolescent depression. We hypothesized that baseline mental health status and contextual social variables would predict divergent depressive symptom trajectories, and that early symptom manifestations would be associated with greater persistence and severity of symptoms over time. These findings are expected to facilitate the early identification of at-risk youth and to inform tailored, school-based prevention strategies in similar sociocultural settings.

Methods

Participants and procedures

This prospective longitudinal cohort study enrolled complete grade-level cohorts of students in grades 4 through 7 from primary and secondary schools. Data were collected over four academic semesters between January 2020 and December 2021. A total of 1,480 students were recruited at baseline. Among them, 1,145 participants (aged 10–13 years; 51.1% female) completed all four waves of assessment: baseline (T1), and three follow-ups at 6-month intervals (T2-T4). No significant differences were observed in age, sex, or grade level between the included participants (n = 1,145) and those excluded from the analysis (n = 335) (Table S1). Informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians prior to enrolment, and all procedures adhered to ethical guidelines. Participant data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Measures

Assessments were administered during school hours in designated computer rooms, supervised by trained research personnel and classroom teachers. Baseline demographic data—including age, sex, and grade—were collected.

Depressive symptoms were measured using the PHQ-9 [14], a widely validated tool for evaluating the presence and severity of depressive symptoms. A threshold of ≥ 5 was used to indicate the presence of depressive symptoms, with higher total scores reflecting greater symptom severity.

Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the MHT [20], comprising 100 dichotomous (agree/disagree) items covering general anxiety and eight subtypes: learning anxiety, interpersonal anxiety, loneliness, self-blame, sensitiveness, somatic anxiety, phobia, and impulsive tendency. Subscale scores ≥ 8 were considered clinically significant. Total scores were categorized as normal (≤ 55), moderate risk (56–64), or high risk (≥ 65). The MHT demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.94).

In addition to standardized instruments, five self-report items were included to assess psychosocial factors. These items, rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), covered loss of temper, hopelessness, perceived family environment, quality of friendships, and help-seeking behavior.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the severity of depressive symptom as measured by PHQ-9 scores. Only participants who completed all four assessment waves (N = 1,145) were included in the longitudinal analyses. Data preprocessing and participant flow are detailed in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.2). PHQ-9 scores were treated as overdispersed count data. An overdispersion test using the qcc package [21] confirmed significant overdispersion at all timepoints (P < 0.05), supporting the use of quasi-Poisson generalized linear models (GLMs), fitted using the glm function [22].

Multicollinearity was assessed by calculating variance inflation factors (VIFs) using the performance package [23]. All VIFs were below the conventional threshold of 5, indicating no problematic collinearity among variables [24] (Table S2). In GLMs, PHQ-9 scores served as the dependent variable, with independent variables including demographic characteristics (age, sex, and grade), MHT subscale scores, and five psychosocial indicators. To account for within-subject temporal dependencies, PHQ-9 scores from the preceding wave (T-1) were included as covariates. Model comparisons were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with chi-squared statistics, and adjusted R-squared values were computed via the rsq package [25, 26] to assess model fit.

To identify subgroups with distinct depressive symptom trajectories, latent class mixed models (LCMMs) were estimated using the lcmm package [27]. Fixed effects included time and grade, and a random intercept was specified for each participant. Model solutions with one to five latent classes were compared based on Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and entropy values. The optimal solution identified two distinct trajectory classes—low-risk and high-risk depressive symptom profiles. Class membership was further analyzed using binary logistic regression, with the low-risk group as the reference category. Predictors included the eight MHT subscales and five psychosocial variables, adjusted for age, sex, and grade.

To examine longitudinal changes in psychological variables and group-by-time interaction effects, generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) were estimated using the glmer function from the lme4 package [28]. Each model included fixed effects for time, group (low-risk vs. high-risk), and their interaction, along with random intercepts for individual participants. All models were adjusted for baseline demographic covariates. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P value < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 1,480 eligible students, 1,145 participants (51.1% female, N = 585) provided valid data across all four assessment waves. The final sample included 277 students in Grade 4 (24.2%), 242 in Grade 5 (21.1%), 258 in Grade 6 (22.5%), and 368 in Grade 7 (32.2%) (Table 1). The prevalence of depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 score ≥ 5) was 27.9% at T1, 27.9% at T2, 32.2% at T3, and 31.2% at T4. The proportion of students with elevated MHT total scores (≥ 56) was 7.4% at T1, 8.4% at T2, 10.5% at T3, and 8.7% at T4. Notably, the prevalence of learning anxiety increased from 41.9% at baseline to 49.1% at T3 (Table S3).

Depressive symptom risk factors

At baseline (T1), quasi-Poisson regression (Model II) identified several psychological risk factors significantly associated with elevated PHQ-9 scores. These included learning anxiety (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00-1.04), hopelessness (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.20–1.33), interpersonal anxiety (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.07), sensitiveness (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.06), impulsive tendency (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.05–1.10), and loss of temper (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.04–1.11). In contrast, a warm family environment (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.86–0.94) and proactive help-seeking behavior (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.87–0.95) were protective (Table 2). Model fitting and selection criteria are provided in Table S4.

Longitudinal predictors of depressive symptom severity

Depressive symptoms demonstrated persistence over time. As shown in Table 2, PHQ-9 scores at T1 significantly predicted scores at T2 (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.04). At T3, prior scores at both T1 (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03) and T2 (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03) were significant predictors. At T4, symptom severity was predicted by PHQ-9 scores at T2 (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03) and T3 (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.04), while T1 scores were no longer significant (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00-1.02; P > 0.05), indicating that more recent symptom history exerted stronger predictive influence. Model fit statistics are provided in Tables S5-S7.

Depressive symptom trajectories and group profiles

LCMMs identified a two-class solution as optimal based on BIC and entropy indices (Table S8). These classes represented a stable low-risk group and a high-risk group with worsening symptoms (Table 3). Compared with the low-risk group, high-risk participants exhibited persistently higher PHQ-9 scores, elevated MHT subscale scores (e.g., learning anxiety, interpersonal anxiety, loneliness), greater hopelessness and temper loss, and lower scores for family warmth, friendship quality, and help-seeking (Figs. 1 and 2).

Time and group interactions between high- and low-risk groups

Distinct risk factors in sixth graders

Grade-level comparisons revealed that sixth-grade students exhibited the most pronounced deviation from expected class distribution patterns (Table 3; residual diagnostics in Table S10). A targeted subgroup analysis was therefore conducted. VIFs for all variables remained below 5, indicating no multicollinearity (Table S11). Within the sixth-grade subsample, hopelessness (OR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.17–3.24) and somatic anxiety (OR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.10–1.72) emerged as significant predictors of high-risk trajectory classification (Table S12). GLMM analyses revealed a significant time-by-group interaction for loneliness (β = 0.13, P = 0.002), indicating a sharper rise in loneliness over time among high-risk sixth-grade students (Table S13).

Discussion

In this two-year, school-based longitudinal study of 1,145 primary and secondary students, prior-year depressive symptoms robustly predicted subsequent symptom severity. Latent class mixed modeling identified two distinct trajectories of depressive symptoms: a stable low-risk group and a high-risk group characterized by escalating severity. Compared with the low-risk group, the high-risk group consistently exhibited higher scores across all MHT subscales, greater emotional dysregulation, and fewer protective social assets, including lower perceived family warmth, poorer friendship quality, and reduced help-seeking behaviors. Notably, the high-risk group showed a steeper longitudinal increase in loneliness. A subgroup analysis of sixth-grade students replicated these findings, underscoring heightened vulnerability during the transition to secondary education.

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous epidemiological research but reveal slightly higher prevalence rates. The prevalence of depressive symptoms in the present study (27.9%-32.2%) exceeded both the pooled estimate from a meta-analysis of Chinese adolescents (24.3%) [29] and the global average (25.2%) [30]. In line with prior studies, learning anxiety was the most prevalent anxiety subtype in this population [17, 31, 32]. The prevalence observed in the current study (41.9%–49.1% across waves) was moderately lower than the 56.4% reported in a survey during the pandemic period in China [32].

In the current findings, learning anxiety, hopelessness, interpersonal anxiety, sensitiveness, impulsive tendency, and loss of temper emerged as significant risk factors for elevated depressive symptoms, whereas a warm family environment and help-seeking were protective. Within China’s educational context, intense academic demands have been linked to learning anxiety and heightened vulnerability to hopelessness [13, 33–35]. Hopelessness, recognized as a key cognitive diathesis for depression, showed robust associations with depressive symptom severity, consistent with cognitive diathesis-stress models [11, 36–38]. Moreover, interpersonal anxiety, emotional sensitivity, and affective reactivity (e.g., irritability, loss of temper) were also associated with symptom persistence, corroborating prior findings that implicate the role of interpersonal dysfunction in the maintenance of adolescent depression [39, 40]. Poor family functioning, including conflict and low cohesion, has been consistently implicated in depressive symptomatology among youth [41]. In contrast, proactive help-seeking emerged as a protective factor; however, adolescents with more severe symptoms may be less likely to seek help [9], highlighting the importance of stigma reduction and improved access to school-based mental health services.

Consistent with longitudinal research [42, 43], our results underscore the persistence of depressive risk over time, with prior-year PHQ-9 scores significantly predicting subsequent severity. Trajectory analyses revealed a pronounced increase in loneliness among high-risk students, particularly sixth graders. Loneliness is a common internalizing issue in adolescence and is closely linked to somatic complaints, emotional dysregulation, and poor mental health outcomes [44–47]. Although warm family environments and supportive peer relationships are known buffers against loneliness [44], these were notably deficient in the high-risk group. Longitudinal studies have further identified childhood loneliness as a prospective risk factor for adult psychopathology [48], warranting close attention to this domain.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, depressive symptoms were assessed using self-reported PHQ-9 questionnaires, which may be subject to reporting and social desirability biases. Second, although the sample size was adequate for longitudinal modeling, broader geographic representation and larger-scale sampling would enhance the generalizability of the findings. Third, unmeasured confounding factors—such as early-life adversity, family socioeconomic status, parenting style, screen time exposure, genetic predisposition, and pandemic-related stress—were not accounted for in this analysis. Future studies incorporating these variables will provide a more comprehensive understanding of adolescent depressive symptomatology.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study offers novel insights into the prevalence, predictors, and trajectories of depressive symptoms in Chinese adolescents. Key risk factors—including hopelessness, somatic anxiety, and loneliness—were significantly associated with membership in high-risk depressive trajectories, while protective factors such as family warmth and help-seeking behavior mitigated symptom escalation. Prior-year depressive severity emerged as a strong predictor of current outcomes, emphasizing the importance of early detection and sustained follow-up. The findings highlight the urgent need for proactive, school-based mental health programs and public health strategies targeting modifiable psychosocial risk factors. Future research should prioritize the development and evaluation of scalable interventions that reduce depressive symptoms and promote psychological resilience in youth populations.
