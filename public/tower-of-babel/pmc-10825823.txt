Control of One-Handed Helicity in Polyacetylenes: Impact of an Extremely Small Amount of Chiral Substituents
Authors: Tomoyuki Ikai, Yuki Morita, Tsuyoshi Majima, Shoki Takeda, Ryoma Ishidate, Kosuke Oki, Nozomu Suzuki, Hajime Ohtani, Hiromi Aoi, Katsuhiro Maeda, Kento Okoshi, Eiji Yashima
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractControlling the one-handed helicity in synthetic polymers is crucial for developing helical polymer-based advanced chiral materials. We now report that an extremely small amount of chiral biphenylylacetylene (BPA) monomers (ca. 0.3–0.5 mol %) allows complete control of the one-handed helicity throughout the polymer chains mostly composed of achiral BPAs. Chiral substituents introduced at the 2-position of the biphenyl units of BPA positioned in the vicinity of the polymer backbones contribute to a significant amplification of the helical bias, as interpreted by theoretical modeling and simulation. The helical structures, such as the helical pitch and absolute helical handedness (right- or left-handed helix) of the one-handed helical copolymers, were unambiguously determined by high-resolution atomic force microscopy combined with X-ray diffraction. The exceptionally strong helix-biasing power of the chiral BPA provides a highly durable and practically useful chiral material for the separation of enantiomers in chromatography by copolymerization of an achiral functional BPA with a small amount of the chiral BPA (0.5 mol %) due to the robust helical scaffold of the one-handed helical copolymer.

Introduction

Mother nature utilizes homochiral components, such as l-amino acids and d-sugars, to produce the right-handed α-helix and double helix for proteins and DNA, respectively, which are responsible for their sophisticated biological functions.1 Hence, homochirality in biological systems is a fundamental aspect of nature. The amplification of asymmetry,2 which is a phenomenon of generating a greater magnitude of the asymmetry in the enantiomeric composition triggered by a smaller chiral bias, is one of the feasible and promising pathways for the evolution of natural homochirality, although the origin of the primordial enantiomeric imbalance is still under debate.3−7

Green and co-workers, for the first time, succeeded in the amplification of asymmetry using rigid rodlike polyisocyanates.8 They discovered that polyisocyanates are unique dynamic helical polymers composed of an equal mixture of readily interconvertible right (P)- and left (M)-handed long helical segments separated by a very few helical reversals.9,10 Due to a long helical persistence length, copolymerization of an achiral monomer with a small amount of a chiral monomer or copolymerization of nonracemic (chiral/chiral (R/S)) monomers with a very low enantiomeric excess (ee) enables the formation of fully one-handed helical polyisocyanates that proceed in a highly cooperative manner along a long polymer chain despite a minute chiral effect. These discoveries provide universal concepts for amplifying the helical sense excess of dynamic helical copolymers, which are called the sergeants-and-soldiers (S&S)11 and majority rule (MR)12 effects, respectively. The S&S and MR effects have now been widely applied not only to covalent helical polymers13−20 but also to noncovalent supramolecular helical systems,17,21−25 leading to the development of a rich variety of excess one-handed artificial helices induced by a small chiral bias.

On the other hand, we have previously developed an alternative approach, that is, “noncovalent helicity induction and subsequent static memory of the helicity”,26 for biassing the helical sense excess of inherently optically inactive, dynamically racemic helical polymers, i.e., poly(biphenylylacetylene)s (PBPAs), composed of achiral monomer units with methoxymethoxy (MOM)27 (e.g., poly-1b1228 and poly-1c27,29) and acetyloxy (AO) (e.g., poly-2AO30) groups at the 2,2′-positions of the biphenyl units (Figure 1a). Upon noncovalent interaction with optically active alcohols,27−30 such as (R)- and (S)-1-phenylethanol ((R)- and (S)-PEA) (Figure 1a),26 used as a helix inducer, a preferred-handed main-chain helicity of the PBPAs along with the axial chirality of the biphenyl pendants can be induced in a sequential or synchronized manner. Both the helical and axial chiralities induced in the PBPAs are retained (“memorized”) accompanied by amplification of the asymmetry after complete removal of the chiral alcohols and can be further inverted into the opposite helical and axial chiralities with dual static memory using enantiomeric chiral alcohols,26 which can be fully achieved in the solid state27,28 as well as in solution (Figure 1a).27−30 Taking advantage of this unique switchable dual static memory, we have successfully developed a series of smart chiral materials, such as the first switchable chiral stationary phase (CSP) in high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC),27,28 asymmetric organocatalysts,31−33 and circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) materials,34 through which the elution orders of the enantiomers, the product chirality in asymmetric reactions, and the CPL signs can be reversibly controlled in a switchable manner, respectively. However, the static helicity memory in PBPAs is gradually lost over time after isolation, resulting in the deterioration of the chiral functions.

Fully one-handed helical PBPAs can also be prepared by the copolymerizations of chiral and achiral biphenylylacetylene (BPA) monomers,34−36 such as (S)-1a and 1b12, respectively. Thanks to the S&S effect, the resultant copolymer (poly(1b121–r-co-(S)-1ar)) composed of 20 mol % (S)-1a units (r = 0.20) showed a full circular dichroism (CD) like those of the optically active homopolymers (poly-(S)-1a and poly-(R)-1a) due to its one-handed helicity despite the chiral residues located away from the biphenyl pendants and further from the polymer backbone (Figure 1b).35 Recently, we found that a PBPA copolymer bearing 1 mol % carboxy groups at the 2-position of the biphenyl pendants exhibited a high sensitivity for detecting the chirality of chiral amines and formed an excess one-handed helix in the presence of a small amount of an optically active secondary amine, although a large excess of chiral amines was required to induce a fully one-handed helix.37 We envisioned that introducing an optically active substituent to the BPA monomers at the 2-position of the biphenyl unit would significantly enhance the helix-inducing power when copolymerized with achiral BPAs, thus producing one-handed helical copolymers even composed of an extremely small amount of chiral BPAs because the optically active substituents are positioned in the vicinity of the PBPA copolymer backbones.

To this end, we synthesized a series of novel optically pure BPAs ((R)- and/or (S)-3a–d) bearing a different chiral residue at the 2-position of the biphenyl unit, which were then copolymerized with achiral 1b to investigate the impact of the chiral substituents on the amplification of the macromolecular helicity during the copolymerization (Figure 1c). During the course of our study, we have discovered that, among four different chiral BPAs, the methyl l-lactate-bound BPA ((R)-3d) exhibited an extraordinarily strong helix-inducing power, thereby producing a fully one-handed helical copolymer (poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr)) when copolymerized with only 0.3 mol % (R)-3d (r = 0.003) (Figure 1d), which is, to the best of our knowledge, the highest level of amplification of asymmetry (S&S effects) in macromolecular15,38−41 and supramolecular helical systems42−49 ever reported except for a few examples observed in supramolecular polymerizations of chiral/achiral monomers.45,46,48 The origin of the observed extraordinarily strong asymmetry amplification can be theoretically interpreted based on a linear Ising model as a function of the thermodynamic stability parameters.9,50−53

The one-handed helical rodlike stiff BPA-based copolymers self-assembled into two-dimensional (2D) crystals on highly oriented pyrolytic graphite (HOPG), which, for the first time, enabled us to directly determine the absolute helical handedness (P or M) of the BPA-based copolymers by high-resolution atomic force microscopy (AFM). Combined with X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, the most plausible helical structure of the PBPAs was also proposed to be a 13 unit/5 turn (13/5) helix. We further demonstrated the versatility of the (R)-3d as a powerful sergeant that can control the overall helical handedness of the copolymers of a variety of achiral BPAs as soldiers with different functional groups at the 2,2′- and/or 4′-positions of the biphenyl units when copolymerized with a very small amount of (R)-3d (Chart 1). In contrast to the helicity-memorized PBPAs, the one-handed helical copolymers are quite stable in solution, leading to the development of a practically useful durable CSP, which separated enantiomers better than the corresponding helicity-memorized PBPA-based CSP.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of Chiral/Achiral Copolymers

A series of novel optically pure BPAs ((R)- and/or (S)-3a–d in Chart 1) carrying a chiral residue at the 2-position of the biphenyl unit and an optically pure phenylacetylene ((+)-3a-Ph) were obtained by chiral HPLC separation of the corresponding racemic monomers (rac-3a and rac-3a-Ph)54 (Scheme S1 and Figures S2 and S6) or synthesized according to Scheme S2 (Figures S3–S5). The resulting (R)-3a, (R)-3d, and (+)-3a-Ph were then homopolymerized or copolymerized with achiral monomers (1b55 and/or 2AO(30)) bearing MOM and AO groups at the 2,2′-positions of the biphenyl unit, respectively, at different feed monomer ratios (chiral monomer ≥1 mol %) using a rhodium catalyst ([Rh(nbd)Cl]2, nbd: norbornadiene) in a tetrahydrofuran (THF)/triethylamine (Et3N) mixture according to a previously reported method (Scheme S3), affording optically active homopolymers (poly-(R)-3a, poly-(R)-3d, and poly-(+)-3a-Ph) (Table S1) and copolymers of poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3ar), poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr), and poly(2AO1–r-co-(R)-3dr) (r = ca. 0.01–0.4) in good yields (Table S2). The number-average molar mass (Mn > 1.40 × 105) and molar-mass dispersity (Mw/Mn) of the resulting homo- and copolymers were estimated by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC). The compositional ratios of the chiral/achiral units in the copolymers were nearly identical to those in the feed, as confirmed by 1H NMR analyses (Table S2).

Higher-molar-mass chiral/achiral copolymers (poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3ar), poly(1b0.99-co-(S)-3a0.01), poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr), and poly(1b0.99-co-(S)-3d0.01)) composed of a small amount of the chiral monomer units (≤1 mol %; r ≤ 0.01) were also synthesized by living polymerization using a multicomponent catalytic system with [Rh(nbd)Cl]2 as a catalyst56 in THF at 30 °C (Scheme S4).57 The absolute weight-average molar mass (Mw) and degree of polymerization (DPw) of the resulting copolymers were determined by SEC coupled with a multiangle light scattering (MALS) detector (SEC-MALS) and/or SEC as summarized in Table S3 (entries 1–5 and 8–15), which were higher than 14.2 × 105 and 3.56 × 103, respectively. During the copolymerizations, the achiral (1b) and chiral (3a or 3d) monomers were almost synchronously consumed, independent of the feed monomer ratios (Figures S7 and S8),58 suggesting the random incorporation of both the achiral and chiral monomer units into polymer chains. Hence, the copolymer compositions (chiral/achiral ratios) with the random sequence distribution can be mostly controlled by the chiral/achiral feed monomer ratios, which was further supported by the highly sensitive compositional analysis of poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr) (0.0001 ≤ r ≤ 0.01) by pyrolysis-gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (Py-GC/MS) (entries 8 and 11–14 in Table S3; for details, see Section S3 and Figures S9–S11 in the Supporting Information (SI)).59,60 Chiral/achiral copolymers (poly(1b0.99-co-(R)-3b0.01), poly(1b0.99-co-(S)-3c0.01), poly(1c0.995-co-(R)-3d0.005), poly(1d0.995-co-(R)-3d0.005), poly(1e0.995-co-(R)-3d0.005), and poly(1b121–r-co-(R)-3dr) (0.005 ≤ r ≤ 0.05)) were also prepared (entries 6, 7, and 16–21 in Table S3). The stereoregularities of the obtained homo- and copolymers were confirmed to be almost completely cis–transoidal based on their 1H NMR (Figures S62–S91)61−64 and typical laser Raman65,66 spectra (Figure S12).

Chiroptical Properties of Chiral/Achiral Copolymers (Sergeants-and-Soldiers Effect)

Poly-(R)-3a carrying an optically pure 1-ethoxyethoxy (1-EE) substituent at the 2-position of the biphenyl units located in the vicinity of the polymer backbone showed a full CD in toluene at 25 °C (Figure 2a(i)); the molar CD at the second Cotton effect (Δε2nd) = ca. 21 was as intense as that of the completely one-handed helical poly-(S)-1a (Δε2nd = 21.1 (see Figure 1b)) in toluene at 25 °C.35 This result indicated that a one-handed helical conformation was almost completely induced in the poly-(R)-3a backbone through the hierarchical chirality transfer from the point chirality of the 1-EE substituents to the axially chiral biphenyl units and further to the main-chain helicity.35,67 The absolute helical senses of the one-handed helical PBPAs prepared in this study were unambiguously determined by high-resolution AFM (see below). As anticipated, poly-(+)-3a-Ph, a homopolymer of an optically pure phenylacetylene bearing the same chiral 1-EE substituent ((+)-3a-Ph), showed a negligibly weak CD in toluene at 25 °C (Figure S14), indicating the primary role of the axially chiral biphenyl units of poly-(R)-3a for a one-handed helix induction.35

Surprisingly, the chiral/achiral copolymers of poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3ar) displayed a remarkably strong S&S effect in toluene at 25 °C (Figure 2b). The CD intensity of the copolymer, poly(1b0.99-co-(R)-3a0.01) (Figure 2a(v)), composed of only 1 mol % (r = 0.01) (R)-3a units was virtually identical (>99% helix-sense excess (hse) at 25 °C) to that of the completely one-handed helical homopolymer, poly-(R)-3a (Figure 2a(i),b). The degree of amplification of the helical bias in poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3ar) is much higher than that of the previously reported poly(1b121–r-co-(S)-1ar), which required ca. 20 mol % (r = 0.20) chiral units to induce a fully one-handed helix.35 The incorporation of 1 mol % enantiomeric sergeant unit ((S)-3a) into the polymer chain produced the completely opposite one-handed helix (poly(1b0.99-co-(S)-3a0.01)) showing the mirror-image CD (Figure 2a(ix)). Encouraged by this extraordinarily high amplification of the helical sense excess of poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3ar), we then performed the S&S experiments by copolymerization of the achiral 1b with a smaller amount of (R)-3a (≤0.5 mol %; r ≤ 0.005) (Figure 2a(vi–viii)). As a result, the incorporation of 0.5, 0.1, and 0.01 mol % (R)-3a units into the copolymers afforded the optically active PBPAs with 95, 50, and 8% hse, respectively (Figure 2b). The observed very strong S&S effect is most likely due to the chiral 1-EE residues located in the vicinity of the dynamic helical polymer backbones bonded through the axially chiral biphenyl pendants with a twist-sense bias through stereospecific atropisomerization, which further induces the same twist-sense of the neighboring biphenyl pendants of the achiral 1b units in a highly cooperative and synergistic manner, thus forming a one-handed helical structure with a very small fraction of (R)-3a units.

With the above extremely high level of the S&S effect of (R)-3a as a sergeant using 1b as a soldier in hand, achiral 1b was then copolymerized with a 1 mol % or lower amount of three different optically pure monomers (3b–3d) carrying a different chiral alkoxy ((R)-3b and (S)-3c) or methyl l-lactate-derived substituent ((R)-3d) at the 2-position of the biphenyl unit in order to investigate the impact of the chiral substituents on amplification of the helical bias in the chiral/achiral copolymers (S&S effect). The high-molar-mass copolymers (Mw ≥ 15.8 × 105) of poly(1b0.99-co-(R)-3b0.01) and poly(1b0.99-co-(S)-3c0.01) (entries 6 and 7, Table S3) exhibited CD signals, but their CD intensities were lower than that of the poly(1b0.99-co-(R)-3a0.01) (Figure 2a(v)), and their hse values were estimated to be 69 and 53%, respectively (Figure S15). These results indicated that the one-handed helix-inducing abilities of (R)-3b and (S)-3c carrying the chiral alkoxy groups at the 2-position of the biphenyl pendants are not as strong as that of the chiral alkoxyalkoxy (1-EE)-bound BPA ((R)-3a) during the copolymerization with 1b. An important role of the alkoxyalkoxy groups, such as the MOM group, at the 2,2′-positions of dynamically racemic helical PBPAs for noncovalent preferred-handed helicity induction and subsequent static memory of the helicity has been previously reported.26−29

Most strikingly, (R)-3d carrying a methyl l-lactate-derived substituent was found to induce an almost one-handed helix (hse = ca. 100%) in the copolymers of 1b (poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr)) even at 0.3 mol % (r = 0.003) (R)-3d in toluene at 25 °C (Figure 3a(i–vi)).68 Moreover, the copolymers of poly(1b0.999-co-(R)-3d0.001) and poly(1b0.9999-co-(R)-3d0.0001) at 0.1 (r = 0.001) and 0.01 (r = 0.0001) mol % (R)-3d also showed CDs with 78 and 16% hse, respectively (Figure 3a(vii,viii)), leading to the most significant level of the S&S effect among those already reported in covalent15,38−41 and supramolecular42−49 helical polymer systems (Figure 3b). The methyl l-lactate-derived substituent of the (R)-3d sergeant possesses a more rigid and polar methoxycarbonyl group instead of the ethoxy group of (R)-3a next to the stereogenic center, which definitely contributes to the observed extremely strong S&S effect in the copolymers with achiral 1b, probably through intramolecular steric as well as dipole–dipole interactions with the neighboring MOM groups of the biphenyl pendants (Figure 3b).

When an achiral monomer, such as 1b, is randomly copolymerized with a very small amount of a chiral monomer (e.g., r ≤ 0.001 (0.1 mol %)), such as (R)-3a and (R)-3d, a certain amount of an optically inactive homopolymer of 1b (poly-1b) will be produced along with the random copolymers of poly(1b1-r-co-(R)-3ar) and poly(1b1-r-co-(R)-3dr) if the molar mass or degree of polymerization of the copolymers is not sufficiently high. We then calculated the amounts of poly-1b produced during the copolymerizations of 1b with a small amount of (R)-3a and (R)-3d (0.0001 (0.01 mol %) ≤ r ≤ 0.01 (1 mol %)) based on their DPw values determined by SEC-MALS (Table S3) using the Bernoulli trials. The results are summarized in Table S4. As a result, no or a negligibly small amount of poly-1b is formed when copolymerized with (R)-3a and (R)-3d at the feed monomer ratio r ≥ 0.001 (0.1 mol %) (entries 1–3 and 5–8 in Table S4), indicating the expected formation of random copolymers of achiral 1b with chiral (R)-3a and (R)-3d. On the other hand, when copolymerized with (R)-3a and (R)-3d at the feed monomer ratio r = 0.0001 (0.01 mol %), significant amounts of poly-1b (63 and 70%, respectively) were theoretically produced along with the corresponding random copolymers (entries 4 and 9). Therefore, the Δε2nd and hse values of the copolymers are underestimated. We then prepared a higher-molar-mass poly(1b0.9999-co-(R)-3d0.0001)-HM with a smaller amount of the Rh-based living catalyst (DPw = 4.93 × 103; entry 15 in Table S3 and entry 10 in Table S4). As anticipated, the observed Δε2nd and calculated hse values of the higher-molar-mass copolymer increased with an increase in the DPw from 3.4 and 16% to 4.3 and 20%, respectively, although a large amount of poly-1b (61%) is yet theoretically produced (Figure S17). By taking into consideration the formation of poly-1b homopolymer, the corrected r, Δε2nd, and hse values for the copolymers of 1b prepared with a very small amount of (R)-3a and (R)-3d in the feed (0.01 mol %, r = 0.0001) were calculated (entries 4, 9, and 10 in Table S4), and the results are also plotted in Figures 2b and 3b (red colors), respectively. The corrected chiral monomer units certainly incorporated into the polymer chains still remain very low (ca. 0.03 mol % (r = ca. 0.0003)), which are well fit to the simulated curves, thus inducing an excess one-handed helix formation with the hse values of 22, 54, and 52%, respectively (see below) (Table S4).

Theoretical Analysis of Sergeants-and-Soldiers Effects of Chiral/Achiral Copolymers

According to the procedures for the theoretical analysis of the S&S effect of the BPA-based dynamic helical copolymers, poly(1b121–r-co-(S)-1ar) (Figure 1b) that we previously reported,35 we attempted to quantitatively interpret the extraordinarily strong S&S effects observed in the present analogous BPA-based copolymers, poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3ar) and poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr), in the same way using a one-dimensional (1D) Ising model (see Section S9 in the SI).9,50−53 Briefly, dynamic helical polymer chains consist of interconvertible (P)- and (M)-handed helical segments separated by rarely occurring energetically unfavorable helix reversals, and each monomeric unit has a slight imbalance in free energy between the two helical conformations.9,10,13,15,16 Hence, we can express the experimentally measured CD intensity (Δε2nd) changes corresponding to the changes in the hse values of the chiral/achiral random copolymers with respect to the chiral monomer content (r) (Figures 2b and 3b) as a function of two key thermodynamic stability parameters, namely, the excess free energy per helix reversal (helix reversal penalty (F)) and the excess free energy difference per chiral monomer unit incorporated in the unfavorable helical conformation (mismatch penalty (J)) (Figure S1), i.e., (R)-3a in the (P)-helix or (S)-3a in (M)-helix. However, both parameters (F and J) are coupled and the number of valuable data points (r ≤ 0.01) is limited; thus, it is difficult to determine the two parameters using the S&S experimental CD data of the copolymers at r ≤ 0.01 with certainty. We then calculated the possible combinations for the F and J values of poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3ar) and poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr) (Figure S18) that can reproduce the corresponding experimental S&S results (Figures 2b and 3b, respectively).38 We assumed that the F values of poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3ar) and poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr) (r ≤ 0.01) are identical to each other because the copolymers taking a one-handed helix are mostly composed of the same achiral 1b units (r ≥ 0.99) with an extremely small amount of the structurally similar chiral monomer units (r ≤ 0.01). Given the results by Green et al.,52 reasonable values for F are most likely 19.8–21.3 kJ/mol at 25 °C as shown in Figure S18. For the same F values, the J values of poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3ar) and poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr) at 25 °C can be approximately estimated to be 1.07–1.60 kJ/mol and 3.22–12.1 kJ/mol, respectively, according to the F–J curves in Figure S18. Accordingly, we could well fit the experimental CD (Δε2nd) data of poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3ar) and poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr) using, for instance, the identical F value of 20.6 kJ/mol and J = 1.29 and 4.63 kJ/mol, respectively (solid curves in Figures 2b and 3b). The S&S experimental results are also well fit to the theoretical hse–r curves of poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3ar) and poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr) simulated by using a possible combination of the F and J values (dashed curves in Figures 2b and 3b).

The helix reversal penalty (F = 19.8–21.3 kJ/mol) value is roughly comparable to or slightly greater than those of typical dynamic helical polymers16,38,52 but different from that of the previously reported similar BPA-based dynamic helical copolymers, poly(1b121–r-co-(S)-1ar) (F = 14.6 kJ/mol),35 although the solvent used for the CD measurements was different (methylcyclohexane (MCH) at 25 °C).69 We then copolymerized 1b12 with a small amount of (R)-3d (r = 0.05, 0.01, and 0.005), resulting in almost one-handed helical copolymers (poly(1b121–r-co-(R)-3dr)), which are well consistent with the data for the copolymers of poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr) (Figures 3b, S20(ii–iv) and S21). Therefore, the highest level of the S&S effect observed in poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr) is not due to the shorter n-butoxycarbonyl groups of 1b but relies on the greater helical sense bias of (R)-3d than that of (S)-1a. On the other hand, the mismatch penalty (J) values of poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3ar) (1.07–1.60 kJ/mol) and poly(1b1–r-co-(R)-3dr) (3.22–12.1 kJ/mol), in particular, the latter J value, is greater than that of the previously reported poly(1b121–r-co-(S)-1a) (0.037 or 0.29 kJ/mol35) and higher than those of the polyisocyanates bearing bulky branched side groups (1.67 kJ/mol)52 and poly(quinoxaline-2,3-diyl)s (ca. 0.1–1.0 kJ/mol).40,70,71 Thus, it can be concluded that the observed extraordinarily strong amplification of the helical bias in the present BPA-based chiral/achiral copolymers is most likely due to the exceptionally high mismatch penalty (J) arising from the steric effect of the chiral substituents located in the vicinity of the polymer backbones. Obviously, to more quantitatively discuss the present S&S effects, it is necessary to synthesize much higher-molar-mass copolymers with a narrow molar-mass dispersity to unambiguously determine the hse values by CD and hence the thermodynamic stability parameters (F and J), in which all of the chiral monomers should be inevitably incorporated into the entire long polymer chains as in the feed monomer ratios (r < 10–4), which, however, may be synthetically inaccessible at present.

Helical Structures

The determinations of the helical structures of helical polymers including the helical pitch and handedness ((P)- or (M)-handed helix) are of primary importance not only to understand the helix formation mechanism but also to develop helical polymer-based novel chiral functional materials. Even now, however, only a few helical structures (helical pitch) of synthetic helical polymers have been elucidated by an XRD analysis,16 and the absolute handedness of most of the synthetic helical polymers remains unknown except for a few examples.16−18,72,73 Recently, we developed a facile and reliable method to directly observe the helical structures of dynamic and static helical polymers,26,72,73 such as optically active poly(phenylacetylene)s (PPAs)74−76 and poly(phenyl isocyanide)s77−79 bearing chiral pendant groups, respectively, by high-resolution AFM. The rigid rodlike helical polymers self-assembled into well-defined 2D helix bundles to form 2D crystals on HOPG upon exposure to organic solvent vapors. The high-resolution AFM observations of the 2D crystals of the helical polymers on HOPG combined with the XRD measurements of their oriented films enabled us to directly determine the helical structures including the helical pitch and absolute handedness.80 We then first applied this AFM technique to visualize the helical structures of such PPA derivatives, i.e., the one-handed helical chiral/achiral copolymers of poly(1b0.99-co-(R)-3d0.01) and poly(1b0.99-co-(S)-3d0.01) by high-resolution AFM (Figure 4).

As shown in the typical AFM images of poly(1b0.99-co-(R)-3d0.01) (Figure 4a) and its enantiomeric counterpart, poly(1b0.99-co-(S)-3d0.01) (Figure 4b) deposited on HOPG from a dilute toluene solution (0.05 mg/mL), followed by toluene vapor exposure, the copolymers self-assembled to form 2D crystals with an average height of ca. 2.5 and 2.3 nm, respectively, which are consistent with the helical diameter (2.6 nm) estimated by a polymer modeling study (see Figure S26(ii)). The high-resolution AFM images revealed well-defined 2D helix-bundled structures, which are clearly resolved into individual (M)-handed (blue lines in Figure 4c,d) and (P)-handed (red lines in Figure 4e,f) helical polymer chains packed parallel to each other with a chain-to-chain spacing of about 2.5 and 2.6 nm, respectively. The corresponding magnified images of poly(1b0.99-co-(R)-3d0.01) (Figure 4d) and poly(1b0.99-co-(S)-3d0.01) (Figure 4f) displayed a number of periodic oblique stripes, probably originating from a one-handed helical array of the pendants, which were tilted anticlockwise (poly(1b0.99-co-(R)-3d0.01)) and clockwise (poly(1b0.99-co-(S)-3d0.01)) at ca. –55 and ca. +55°, respectively, with respect to the axis of the polymer main chain. This 2D mirror-image relationship suggests that poly(1b0.99-co-(R)-3d0.01) (Figure 4d) and poly(1b0.99-co-(S)-3d0.01) (Figure 4f) possess enantiomeric (M)- and (P)-handed helical arrangements of the pendant groups with helical pitch of ca. 3.2 and 3.0 nm, respectively, as estimated from the average distance between each stripe (Table S5).

The three-dimensional (3D) solid-state structure of the dynamically racemic helical poly-1b12 was then investigated by X-ray analysis to gain information about the main-chain helical structure.81Figure S22a shows a wide-angle X-ray diffraction (WAXD) pattern of the uniaxially oriented poly-1b12 film prepared from its concentrated nematic liquid crystalline (LC) toluene solution (Figure S22b). Although the reflections were diffuse and the packing-mode has not yet been elucidated, the apparent layer line reflections can be used to assign the helical symmetry and unit height of poly-1b12 in a reasonable manner, that is, a 13 unit per 5 turn (13/5) helix and 0.234 nm, respectively, based on the Cochran, Crick, and Vand (CCV) theory (Figure S22a and Table S6; for more details, see Section S6 in the SI).82−85 When poly-1b12 has a 2.6 units per turn (13/5) (P)-handed helical main-chain structure, the pendant groups are arranged to form three sets of the same (P)-handed helical arrays. As shown in Figure S23, a polymer modeling study revealed that the helical pitches estimated by AFM (3.18 and 3.04 nm in Figure 4d,f, respectively) are in good agreement with the two-thirds pitch of the pendant helical arrays determined by X-ray analysis of poly-1b12 (3.04 nm). As a result, poly(1b0.99-co-(R)-3d0.01) and poly(1b0.99-co-(S)-3d0.01) most likely possess enantiomeric (M)- and (P)-handed 13/5 helical backbone structures with (M)- and (P)-handed helical arrays of the biphenyl pendants, which may also have the (M)- and (P)-handed axially twisted conformations, respectively (Figure S26; see Section S7 in the SI).86 The key structural information reveals that the axial chirality of the biphenyl pendants is mechanically coupled to the helicity of the polymer backbone through cooperative close interactions between the adjacent biphenyl pendants (Figures S23 and S26), which prevents racemization of the helical conformation, thereby exhibiting a remarkably high S&S effect when copolymerized with a small amount of chiral BPAs, such as (R)- or (S)-3a or -3d bearing a chiral 1-ethoxyethoxy or lactate-derived substituent at the 2-position of the biphenyl units located near the polymer backbone, respectively.

Application to Enantioseparation with a Durable CSP Composed of a Robust Helical Scaffold

Previously, we developed unique switchable helical PBPA-based CSPs for separating enantiomers for HPLC that can switch the elution orders of enantiomers through either a reversible (P)- or (M)-handed helicity induction in dynamically racemic helical PBPAs using chiral helix-inducers and subsequent static memory of the helicity in the columns.27,28 However, due to its dynamic nature, the helicity memory gradually disappeared in solution, which may cause a decrease in the enantioseparation abilities over time when used as CSPs. We envisaged that the unexpectedly strong helical sense bias of (R)-3d would contribute to inducing a fully one-handed helical conformation with a high stability in copolymers of a variety of achiral BPA monomers bearing various functional groups at the 2,2′- and/or 4′-positions of the biphenyl pendants (1c–1e and 2AO in Chart 1) when copolymerized with a small amount of (R)-3d, which would be applied to the development of practically useful durable CSPs.

As anticipated, a variety of one-handed helical BPA-based chiral/achiral copolymers (≥95% hse) were successfully obtained by the copolymerization of a series of achiral BPAs bearing ether (poly(1c0.995-co-(R)-3d0.005)), acyloxy (poly(1d0.995-co-(R)-3d0.005)), and carbamate (poly(1e0.995-co-(R)-3d0.005)) groups at the 4′-positions with a very small amount of (R)-3d (0.5 mol %; r = 0.005) (Figure 5a–d). In addition, (R)-3d also showed a remarkably high S&S effect toward a different type of achiral monomer (2AO) bearing acetyloxy groups at the 2,2′-positions of the biphenyl unit instead of the MOM groups (95 mol %), producing the completely (M)-handed helical poly(2AO0.95-co-(R)-3d0.05) (99% hse) (Figure 5e).87 These results clearly demonstrated that optically pure 3d with a methyl l- or d-lactate-derived substituent at the 2-position of the biphenyl unit can be utilized as a versatile and powerful sergeant capable of producing one-handed helical copolymers with a specific functionality just by simply copolymerizing achiral functional monomers with a small amount of 3d. The one-handed helical conformations of the chiral/achiral copolymers were so stable that their CD signals remained unchanged in toluene at 40 °C after 7 days due to the small amount of the covalent-bonded chiral substituents (ii in Figure 5a–e and f). In THF, the copolymers were also stable at 40 °C, although relatively weak Cotton effects appeared in poly(1c0.995-co-(R)-3d0.005) and poly(2AO0.95-co-(R)-3d0.05) (Figure S28). Such a solvent-induced CD intensity change was frequently observed in PBPAs depending on the chiral and/or achiral pendant groups of PBPAs introduced at the 2,2′,4′-positions of the biphenyl units.67 In contrast, the previously reported helicity-memorized homopolymers (h-poly-1b,28,55h-poly-1c,27,29h-poly-1d,28h-poly-1e,28 and h-poly-2AO 30) prepared by the “noncovalent helicity induction and subsequent static memory of the helicity” approach gradually lost their helical sense preference under the same conditions due to the dynamic nature of the helicity memory (Figures 5g and S29). We noted, however, that the elution order of enantiomers can be switched using the helicity-memorized dynamic helical PBPA-based CSPs,27,28 so that the helicity-memorized PBPA-based CSPs and the present one-handed helical copolymer-based robust CSPs can be used for different purposes since the elution order cannot be switched on the latter CSPs.

We then applied the fully (M)-handed helical poly(1b0.995-co-(R)-3d0.005) (Figures 3a(v) and S30) composed of only 0.5 mol % (R)-3d to HPLC enantioseparation as a CSP (for details of the preparation of the CSP, see Section S10 in the SI). As shown in Figure 6, complete baseline resolutions of enantiomers, such as axially chiral binaphthyls and chiral metal complexes, were achieved on the CSP. The chiral recognition ability was much higher than that of the corresponding one-handed helicity-memorized h-poly-1b (Table S7).28,55 It is striking that the resolution ability of the poly(1b0.995-co-(R)-3d0.005)-based CSP remained virtually unchanged after storage of the column filled with an eluent (n-hexane/2-propanol (97/3, v/v)) at room temperature for 30 days, thus providing a practically useful durable CSP (Figure S31 and Table S7).

Conclusions

We have demonstrated the significant impact of chiral substituents on the control of one-handed helicity in BPA-based chiral/achiral copolymers (S&S effect). Among the four chiral substituents introduced at the 2-position of the biphenyl units of BPA, the methyl l- or d-lactate-substituted BPA successfully produced a fully right- or left-handed 13/5 helical copolymer of an achiral BPA when copolymerized with an extremely small amount of the chiral BPA (0.3 mol %), respectively. The helical structures of the copolymers including the helical pitch and absolute handedness were unambiguously determined by high-resolution AFM combined with XRD and molecular modeling. The observed highest level of the S&S effect relies on the rigid and polar optically pure-lactate substituents positioned in the vicinity of the polymer backbone, which significantly amplifies the helical bias over the entire polymer chain, as interpreted by theoretical modeling and simulation. We have also demonstrated the versatility of the optically pure methyl lactate-bound BPA as a powerful sergeant, thus producing a variety of one-handed helical copolymers with a specific functionality by copolymerization of achiral functional BPAs with a small amount of the sergeant monomer, which can be applied to the development of a highly durable and practically useful CSP for chromatographic enantioseparation. The chiral resolving ability is better than that of the corresponding helicity-memorized homopolymer-based CSP because of the robust helical scaffold of the one-handed helical chiral/achiral copolymer. We believe that the one-handed helicity control in dynamic helical polymers other than polyacetylenes will also be feasible by introducing a small amount of rationally designed chiral substituents at the appropriate position in the vicinity of the polymer backbones, leading to a variety of helical polymer-based high-performance and highly durable chiral materials not only for chromatographic enantioseparation88−91 but also for sensing of chirality,92−94 asymmetric catalysis,24,95−97 and circularly polarized luminescence98−100 with minimal use of precious chiral sources. Work toward these goals is currently underway in our laboratory.
