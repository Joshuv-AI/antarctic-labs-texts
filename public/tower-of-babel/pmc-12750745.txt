The moderating role of academic achievement attribution in shaping the impact of negative life events on adolescent problem behaviors and school dropout tendency
Authors: Da Yi, Xing Xu, Qingqi Zhang, Ai Ma, Xiaoqian Liu, Cheng Lian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study explores how negative life events influence dropout tendencies through internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors, with a particular focus on the moderating effect of academic achievement attribution tendencies on the relationship between negative life events and internal/external problem behaviors.MethodsThe study used structural equation modeling to analyze a sample of 1,012 students from middle and vocational high schools.ResultsThe results showed that: (1) negative life events had no direct impact on dropout tendency; (2) internalizing/externalizing problem behaviors fully mediated the relationship between negative life events and dropout tendency; (3) high external attribution for academic achievement strengthened the link between negative life events and internalizing behaviors, while low external attribution for academic achievement exacerbated the impact on externalizing behaviors. These findings emphasize the importance of considering academic achievement attribution tendencies in educational interventions to reduce problem behaviors and the risk of school dropout.Keywords: Negative life events, Internalizing problem behavior, Externalizing problem behavior, Cademic Achievement Attribution, Dropout tendency

Introduction

The phenomenon of school dropout is a global educational issue. According to a report by UNESCO [1], school dropout and inadequate education result in approximately $10 trillion in economic losses worldwide each year. In addition to economic losses, dropout has far-reaching negative impacts on both individuals and society. Dropping out not only limits an individual’s career prospects and economic opportunities, but also exacerbates social inequality and crime rates [2]. Numerous studies have identified negative life events as a significant predictor of adolescent dropout [2, 3]. As a response, governments worldwide have implemented various measures to reduce school dropout, primarily focusing on lowering the likelihood of negative life events, such as reducing academic pressure and school bullying [4, 5]. However, adolescents continue to encounter many unavoidable negative life events in their daily lives, such as complex interpersonal relationships, difficulties adapting to academics, and family disruptions (e.g., parental divorce or the death of loved ones). Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the psychological mechanisms linking negative life events and dropout in order to develop more effective intervention strategies and explore how to mitigate the impact of negative life events on dropout.

Moreover, dropout is closely linked to increased risk of juvenile delinquency [6, 7], making dropout prevention crucial for both individual outcomes and social stability [8, 9].

Due to their lack of social experience and the developmental characteristics of their brains, adolescents often exhibit heightened sensitivity to stress and tend to adopt inappropriate coping strategies when faced with stressful events, such as internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors [10, 11]. However, what factors contribute to the different patterns of problem behaviors exhibited by adolescents when confronted with similar negative life events?

According to ABC theory, an individual’s beliefs (Belief, B) about the same stimulus (Activating Event, A) determine the resulting behaviors and emotional outcomes (Consequence, C) [12]. In other words, even when facing similar negative life events, different individuals may display drastically different behavioral responses due to variations in their internal beliefs and cognitive styles.

In this process, attribution style, as a component of cognitive style, plays a crucial role in interpreting events, which in turn leads to different emotional and behavioral outcomes. Therefore, the central focus of this paper is to investigate whether attribution style can explain why adolescents exhibit different behavioral and emotional responses to negative life events.

Negative life events and dropout

Negative life events refer to adverse and stressful experiences in an individual’s life, such as family problems, financial difficulties, or interpersonal conflicts [13]. Research indicates that these events significantly impact an individual’s mental health and behavior, increasing the likelihood of school dropout [14–16].

For instance, changes in family structure, such as parental divorce or the death of a family member, are key factors influencing adolescent dropout [3]. Similarly, Alexander et al. [17] found that the deterioration of family economic conditions, such as parental unemployment or a significant reduction in household income, also greatly increases the risk of adolescent dropout.

Furthermore, interpersonal issues like peer bullying and conflicts with teachers increase adolescents’ tendencies to avoid school and academic activities, which are strong predictors of dropout [18, 19]. Additionally, many qualitative studies have shown that dropout often occurs under various stressful conditions, such as long-term hospitalization, car accidents, pregnancy, conflicts with teachers, peer rejection and bullying, family instability, or parental incarceration [16].

In summary, numerous studies have highlighted that negative life events can be a significant factor influencing dropout rates. Therefore, we propose Hypothesis 1: Negative life events positively predict the tendency to drop out of school.

The mediating role of internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors

Internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors describe two different ways individuals cope with negative emotions, internal conflicts, or life stressors. Internalizing problem behaviors refer to the internalization of negative emotions or internal conflicts, manifesting as emotional distress and self-harm, which are often more hidden [20]. These behaviors include symptoms such as depression, anxiety, and social withdrawal [21]. In contrast, externalizing problem behaviors involve the externalization of negative emotions or conflicts, commonly seen in aggression, oppositional behaviors, or rule-breaking behaviors [21].

Negative life events and internalizing/externalizing problem behaviors

Adolescents who experience negative life events often display a range of both internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors [22]. Zhu et al. [23] found that negative life events significantly affected both behavior types in Tibetan middle school students, with impacts ranking in order: academic pressure, self-identity and expectations, opposite-sex relationships, and peer relationships.

Research has established the directional relationship between negative events and behavioral outcomes. Kim et al. [24] observed that social problems, aggression, and externalizing behaviors are outcomes of bullying experiences rather than their causes, highlighting the directional nature of the relationship between negative life events and internalizing/externalizing behaviors. Research by Ladd [25] and Jiang et al. [26] further confirmed that peer relationships and academic pressure directly influence the development of internalizing and externalizing behaviors in adolescents.

Additionally, changes in family structure and financial instability have been shown to significantly increase the risk of internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors [27–29]. Moreover, a longitudinal study by South Korean researchers Sun and Lee [30] demonstrated that stress induced by the COVID-19 pandemic led to an increase in internalizing and externalizing behaviors in preschool children.

Collectively, these studies indicate that negative life events are strong predictors of both internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors in adolescents.

Internalizing/externalizing problem behaviors and dropout tendency

Jessor’s Theory of Problem Behavior offers a crucial perspective: adolescents’ problematic behaviors and mental health issues often lead to academic failure and an increased risk of dropping out of school [31, 32].

On one hand, internalizing problem behaviors are significantly and positively associated with adolescents’ tendency to drop out. Research shows that adolescents with mental health issues tend to perform academically poor and achieve lower levels of educational attainment compared to their psychologically healthy peers [33]. Needham [34] highlighted that emotional distress is linked to an increased risk of academic failure among high school students. Furthermore, longitudinal studies by Quiroga et al. [35] and Van Dijk et al. [36] have confirmed that internalizing problem behaviors not only affect academic achievement but also significantly increase the likelihood of dropout.

On the other hand, externalizing problem behaviors are also key predictors of dropout risk. The failure model suggests that adolescents who display high levels of aggression and antisocial behavior are more likely to experience lower academic success compared to their peers and face greater social challenges and failures [37]. Moffitt’s [38] research further supports this idea, noting that externalizing problem behaviors during adolescence are strong predictors of academic failure and dropout. A longitudinal study by Alexander et al. [39] found that students exhibiting externalizing behaviors are significantly more likely to drop out than those with better behavioral records.

Similarly, longitudinal studies by Bradshaw et al. [40] and Alatupa et al. [41] found that externalizing problem behaviors are strong predictors of poor academic achievement and dropout. Additionally, externalizing behaviors not only damage adolescents’ relationships with teachers and peers, but also lead to more disciplinary issues and adaptation difficulties [42], further increasing the risk of school sanctions and dropout [43].

In conclusion, both internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors may increase adolescents’ dropout risk through different psychological and behavioral mechanisms. Therefore, we propose Hypothesis 2a: Negative life events positively predict adolescents’ dropout tendency through internalizing problem behaviors; and Hypothesis 2b: Negative life events positively predict dropout tendency through externalizing problem behaviors.

The moderating role of academic achievement attribution

Academic Achievement Attribution refers to the process by which individuals analyze, explain, and speculate about the causes of their academic achievement, interpreting both academic success and failure [44]. According to Ellis’s ABC theory [12], when individuals face similar negative life events, differences in their cognitive patterns and beliefs can lead to vastly different behavioral responses.

As a key component of the cognitive process, attribution style plays a critical role in explaining why identical events can lead to divergent emotional and behavioral outcomes. We argue that an over-reliance on uncontrollable external attributions may impede personal growth and adaptation, as it diminishes individuals’ perceived ability to influence outcomes [45].

In the following sections, we will examine the moderating role of uncontrollable external attributions in different contexts (i.e., high academic achievement vs. low academic achievement). (Note: For brevity, “uncontrollable external attribution” will be referred to as “external attribution” in the following text.)

The Moderating Role of High Academic Achievement External Attribution Tendency

According to Bandura’s self-efficacy theory, individuals with high self-efficacy are typically better equipped to maintain motivation and resilience when facing challenges. In contrast, individuals with low self-efficacy tend to feel helpless in the face of adversity, which can lead to negative emotions such as depression and anxiety [46, 47].

When individuals attribute their success to uncontrollable external factors—such as high academic achievement coupled with an external attribution tendency—they struggle to connect their success to their own abilities and efforts. This disconnection hampers positive reinforcement, which in turn undermines their self-efficacy [48].

As a result, their sense of control over their environment diminishes, making them more likely to interpret negative life events as reflections of their own inadequacies or incompetence. This kind of interpretation fosters negative expectations and internalized problem behaviors, including depression and anxiety [45, 49].

These findings suggest that a tendency to externalize high academic achievement may influence how individuals cope with negative life events, particularly when confronted with stress or frustration, thereby heightening the risk of internalized problem behaviors.

Therefore, we propose Hypothesis 3a: High academic achievement with external attribution tendency positively moderates the relationship between negative life events and internalized problem behaviors.

The Moderating Role of Low Academic Achievement External Attribution Tendency

When individuals attribute their failures or frustrations to uncontrollable external factors, such as other people or their environment, this attribution style plays a key moderating role in the development of anger, hostility, and externalizing problem behaviors [45]. Research indicates that this cognitive style, known as hostile attribution bias, involves a tendency to perceive malicious intent in ambiguous situations [50, 51]. This tendency becomes especially pronounced in the context of negative life events [52].

When such events occur, individuals with a predisposition for low academic achievement with external attribution tendency are more likely to interpret them as unjust actions by others or the environment, leading to feelings of anger and hostility. These emotions may then manifest as aggressive or antisocial behaviors [53, 54]. Furthermore, a tendency to ruminate on perceived injustices (i.e., anger rumination) can intensify these emotions, increasing the likelihood of aggressive responses to future provocations [55].

This suggests that a low academic achievement external attribution style fosters a hostile worldview, particularly when faced with stress or frustration, which in turn heightens the risk of externalizing problem behaviors.

In conclusion, we propose Hypothesis 3b: Low academic achievement with external attribution tendency positively moderates the relationship between negative life events and externalizing problem behaviors. A conceptual model is shown in Fig. 1.

Theoretical framework

To understand the complex relationships among negative life events, problem behaviors, and dropout tendencies in adolescents, this study proposes three complementary theoretical perspectives: Ellis’s ABC theory [12], Jessor’s problem behavior theory [31, 32], and Weiner’s attribution theory [45].

Ellis’s ABC theory [12] provides the foundational logic for understanding how individuals cope with stressful events, explaining why adolescents may exhibit dramatically different behavioral responses when facing similar negative life events, and emphasizing the crucial role of cognitive beliefs and attribution styles in determining outcomes. Jessor’s problem behavior theory provides the theoretical foundation for understanding how negative life events lead to dropout through pathways of internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors [31, 32]. Weiner’s attribution theory [45] and Bandura’s self-efficacy theory [46, 47] jointly explain the moderating role of academic achievement attribution tendencies.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

Data for this study were collected using a questionnaire survey. The questionnaires were distributed among students from Grade 7 to Grade 10 by teachers from a middle school in Xinyi City, Guangdong Province, and a vocational school in Guangzhou. A total of 1,097 questionnaires were collected. After excluding those that failed the lie detection questions and those with abnormal responses, 1,012 valid questionnaires were obtained, resulting in an effective response rate of 92.25%. The participants’ ages ranged from 11 to 18 years (M = 14.37, SD = 0.039), with 7 participants not reporting their age. The sample consisted of 43.57% males (N = 441) and 56.42% females (N = 571). All respondents participated voluntarily and provided informed consent before answering the questionnaires.

Research instruments

Negative life events

This study employed the Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC), which was originally developed by Liu et al. [56] and later revised by Xin and Yao [57], designed to assess the frequency and intensity of stressful life events experienced by adolescents. The scale contains 26 items across five dimensions: adaptation, loss, punishment, interpersonal relationships, and academic pressure. Each item describes a specific life event, requiring participants to report whether they or their families had experienced similar events in the past 12 months (e.g., “being misunderstood or blamed”). If the event had not occurred, it was scored as 0; if it had occurred, participants selected a rating from 1 to 5 based on the degree of impact: 1 = no impact, 2 = mild impact, 3 = moderate impact, 4 = severe impact, 5 = extremely severe impact. Therefore, in this study, each item was scored on a range from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating a greater degree of impact from negative life events. Academic achievement attribution

Academic achievement attribution was assessed using the Academic Achievement Attribution Scale from the Multidimensional and Multicausal Attributional Scale [44]. The scale consists of 24 items, divided into four dimensions: ability, effort, background, and luck, with six items in each dimension. The first three items in each dimension measure attributions related to high academic achievement, while the last three assess attributions related to low academic achievement. The total attribution score is calculated as the difference between external attributions (background + luck) and internal attributions (ability + effort).

In this study, we further subdivided external attributions into high and low academic achievement with external attribution tendency, based on success and failure scenarios. For example, “The most important reason I achieved good grades is that my learning ability is very strong” represents an external attribution tendency in high academic achievement, while “In my opinion, sometimes I received lower evaluations, which only means that some teachers are too strict in their assessments” represents an external attribution tendency in low academic achievement. A five-point Likert scale was used, where “0” represented “strongly disagree” and “4” represented “strongly agree.” Higher scores indicate a stronger tendency toward each attribution style. “The scale exhibited excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.951).

Problem behaviors

The Adolescent Risk Behavior Scale, developed by Bai [58], was used to assess problem behaviors. This scale consists of four subscales; however, for the purposes of this study, only the internalizing and externalizing problem behavior subscales were utilized. Internalizing problem behaviors include depression, anxiety, withdrawal, and neuroticism, while externalizing problem behaviors encompass truancy, running away from home, theft, defiance, and violent behavior. Sample items for internalizing problem behaviors include: “I worry that some bad things will happen”. Sample items for externalizing problem behaviors include: “I often quarrel with my parents and oppose them”. The two subscales together include 34 items in total. Responses were rated on a five-point scale, where “never” was scored as 1 and “always” was scored as 5. Students answered each item based on their behavior, with higher scores indicating more frequent problem behaviors. The internalizing problem behavior subscale demonstrated strong structural validity, with fit indices presented in Table 2 and a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.970. Similarly, the externalizing problem behavior subscale also showed good structural validity, with fit indices in Table 2 and a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.913.

Dropout tendency

The Dropout Tendency Scale for middle school students, developed by Ou [59], was used to assess dropout tendency. This scale consists of two dimensions: dropout identification and dropout intention, with a total of 10 items. Sample items for dropout intention include: “I often have thoughts of dropping out in my head.” Sample items for dropout identification (reverse-scored) include: “Dropping out halfway is a shortsighted decision.” The original scale employed a five-point Likert scale, but this study adopted a six-point scale to minimize central tendency bias, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” The fit indices for the scale were χ²/df = 51.802, RMSEA = 0.224, SRMR = 0.251, CFI = 0.442, TLI = 0.282, and the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.752. Further analysis revealed a negative correlation between the two dimensions—dropout identification and dropout intention. Despite correctly handling the reverse-scored items, the two dimensions remained negatively correlated. Therefore, to maintain structural validity, we decided to use only the dropout intention dimension as the dependent variable. This dimension demonstrated strong structural validity, with fit indices presented in Table 2, and a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.917.

Data analysis

The data analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0 and Mplus 8.3 and consisted of two main steps. First, descriptive statistics (N = 1012) and correlation analysis were conducted for all variables. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was then carried out using Mplus 8.3. With the exception of the moderating variables related to high academic achievement with external attribution tendency (W1) and low academic achievement (W2), which were treated as observed variables, the negative life events (X), internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors (M1 and M2), and dropout tendencies (Y) were treated as latent variables. Following recommendations from methodological literature [60, 61], we report effect sizes alongside all statistical tests. For explained variance (R²), we adopt the benchmarks from Wei and Hu, which are highly relevant to survey-based studies such as the present one: R² = 0.005 (small), 0.01 (typical), 0.02 (large), and 0.09 (very large) [62].

To assess potential common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was applied. The results revealed the presence of 12 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, with the largest factor accounting for 32% of the total variance. Since this value is below the 40% threshold recommended by Malhotra et al. [63], it suggests that common method bias is not a significant concern in this study.

The study also included several demographic variables (G1-G10) mainly to control for potential confounding factors that might influence the study variables, thus enhancing the accuracy of the research. Gender (G1) is an important factor influencing individual behavior and psychological states [64] and is often used as a control variable in research. Age (G2) can affect psychological development and behavior, making it a key demographic variable in many studies [65]. Grade level (G3) may reflect students’ academic and social maturity, which could influence the study variables [66]. School type (G4) might impact educational resources and learning environments, subsequently affecting students’ behavior and psychological states [67]. Family location (G5) may be related to economic, cultural, and social environments, all of which could influence individuals [68]. Whether an individual is an only child (G6) could influence their role and experiences within the family, impacting behavior [69]. Birth order (G7) may affect an individual’s status and experiences within the family, making it an important family dynamic factor [70]. Whether a child is a “left-behind” child (G8) may affect their social support and daily experiences, warranting an examination of adolescents’ living arrangements [71]. Parents’ highest level of education (G9 for fathers, G10 for mothers) reflects the family’s educational background, a key factor influencing individual development [72].

The analysis revealed that, except for school type (G4), family location (G5), and birth order (G7), which showed no significant correlations with the study variables, all other demographic variables were significantly correlated with the study variables. Therefore, G4, G5, and G7 will be excluded from subsequent analyses, and other significantly correlated demographic variables will be controlled for. Since the main goal of the study is to explore the internal psychological mechanisms by which negative life events contribute to student dropout, the differences in demographic variables are not directly relevant to understanding these psychological mechanisms. Therefore, the study will not examine differences between demographic variables (e.g., gender, only-child status) and the study variables (e.g., internalizing problem behaviors, externalizing problem behaviors).

Structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis

If the main purpose of SEM is to explore complex relationships between latent variables, such as path relationships between factors or potential interaction effects, it may not be necessary to first conduct hierarchical regression analysis to identify predictors of the dependent variables [73]. This is because SEM can directly assess these complex relationships and provide path coefficients, goodness-of-fit indices, and other relevant statistics that may not be easily obtainable through hierarchical regression models.

In this study, apart from the moderating variables of high academic achievement with external attribution tendency (W1) and low academic achievement with external attribution tendency (W2), which are treated as observed variables, all other variables are modeled as latent variables. Since latent variables must satisfy the unidimensionality assumption [74], each latent variable was subjected to a single-factor confirmatory factor analysis. For negative life events (X), which did not meet the unidimensionality assumption, second-order latent variable modeling was used. The number of factors was divided according to the dimensions of the scale, as shown in Table 2. The results indicated that the second-order latent variable model for X had a good fit.

Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices with consideration for model complexity and sample size. While traditional cutoff criteria (CFI/TLI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08) provide general guidelines, recent research suggests that fit index evaluation should consider model complexity, theoretical support, and sample characteristics [75, 76]. In large samples, chi-square tests may be overly sensitive to minor deviations, making slightly relaxed criteria acceptable when models have strong theoretical foundations [77]. For the problem behavior variables (M1, M2), while CFI/TLI values were slightly below the traditional 0.90 threshold, the RMSEA values were within acceptable ranges, and considering the complexity of measuring psychological constructs and the strong theoretical foundation, these fit indices were deemed acceptable for the current study.

The study employed the Latent Moderated Structural Equation (LMS) method to construct a moderated parallel mediation model. Since the LMS method does not provide traditional model fit indices, the model fit of the LMS model needs to be evaluated by comparing it with a null model (Model 0) that does not include latent moderation factors [78, 79]. First, the null model without latent moderation factors was evaluated, and the fit indices of the null model are shown in Table 3. Except for the χ²/df and RMSEA indices, which were acceptable, other fit indices did not meet the criteria. In the second step, the model with latent moderation factors (Model 1) was evaluated. The results showed: (1) The AIC and BIC indices of the moderated parallel mediation model were lower than those of the baseline model, indicating an improvement in model fit. Additionally, external attribution tendencies significantly moderated these relationships with meaningful effect sizes. High academic achievement external attribution positively moderated the effect of negative life events on internalizing problem behaviors (β = 0.101, SE = 0.030, 95% CI [0.041, 0.160], p < 0.01), with R² change = 0.010 representing a typical effect size. Low academic achievement external attribution positively moderated the effect on externalizing problem behaviors (β = 0.242, SE = 0.033, 95% CI [0.178, 0.307], p < 0.001), with R² change = 0.024 representing a large effect size according to the benchmarks from Wei and Hu [62]. Specifically, a high academic achievement combined with an external attribution tendency positively moderates the effect of negative life events on internalizing problem behaviors, while low academic achievement combined with an external attribution tendency positively moderates the effect of negative life events on externalizing problem behaviors (supporting Hypotheses 3a and 3b).

To further investigate the role of external attribution tendencies in moderating the pathways through which negative life events influence internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors, a simple slope analysis was performed. Using the pick-a-point method, the moderating variable was divided into high and low categories based on the mean ± 1 standard deviation. The moderating effect of high academic achievement with external attribution tendency is shown in Fig. 4: when an individual’s high academic achievement combined with an external attribution tendency is lower, negative life events result in fewer internalizing problem behaviors (b = 0.683, p < 0.001); conversely, when an individual’s high academic achievement with an external attribution tendency is higher, negative life events lead to more internalizing problem behaviors (b = 0.710, p < 0.001). Discussion

Practical significance of findings

The findings in this study demonstrate practical importance beyond statistical significance. Both mediation pathways showed meaningful effects (β = 0.545 for internalizing behaviors, β = 0.459 for externalizing behaviors). These effects suggest that problem behaviors represent primary mechanisms through which negative life events influence dropout tendencies, indicating that interventions targeting these pathways could yield meaningful improvements in dropout prevention. The moderation effects show meaningful influences. The R² change values (0.010 and 0.024) fall within the “typical” to “large” range according to the benchmarks from Wei and Hu [62], suggesting that attribution styles are important moderators worthy of consideration in intervention design.

The parallel mediating role of internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors

This study explores the impact of negative life events on dropout tendency. The results showed that the direct effect of negative life events on dropout tendency was not significant (β = −0.055, hypothesis H1 not supported), while both the total effect (β = 0.477***) and indirect effects (β = 0.570***) were significant.

From a statistical perspective, this pattern aligns with the suppression effect characteristics described by MacKinnon et al. [80] and Fairchild & McQuillin [81], which occurs when direct and indirect effects operate in opposite directions. Suppression effect theory suggests that two competing mechanisms may exist: first, negative life events may activate protective responses or support systems (such as increased teacher attention, family support mobilization, or activation of school counseling services) that directly reduce dropout tendency; second, negative life events may indirectly increase dropout risk by triggering psychological and behavioral problems.

However, based on China’s specific national conditions and educational development reality, we believe the current findings more likely reflect a historical transformation in influence mechanisms during social development processes rather than a typical suppression effect. This finding is inconsistent with previous research and may be related to China’s rapid socio-economic development and the deeper implementation of nine-year compulsory education policy. With the growth of the Chinese economy and the gradual improvement of educational resources, family economic conditions and social support networks have significantly improved. Dropout tendencies caused by objective factors in the past (such as changes in family structure, economic difficulties, or the death of relatives) have been effectively mitigated.

Simultaneously, with the increasing academic pressure and the widespread use of the internet, the impact of negative life events on adolescents’ dropout tendencies has become more complex. Academic pressure makes adolescents more sensitive to negative life events, making them more prone to anxiety, depression, and other mental health issues, which increases the risk of dropping out [82]. Moreover, negative factors brought about by the internet, such as cyberbullying, information overload, and social pressure, may lead adolescents to adopt poor coping mechanisms, such as violence and rebellious behavior. These externalizing behaviors further affect academic performance and increase the tendency to drop out [83].

Therefore, in the current social context, negative life events are more likely to indirectly affect adolescents’ dropout tendency through subjective factors, such as internalizing and externalizing problems (hypotheses 2a and 2b are supported). This is consistent with previous research, which suggests that negative life events, through increasing internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors, may significantly affect adolescents’ academic performance [84–86].

The large effect sizes observed for the mediating pathways (β > 0.45 for both internalizing and externalizing behaviors) indicate that these psychological mechanisms represent not only statistically significant but also practically meaningful pathways through which negative life events influence dropout risk. This suggests that interventions targeting these problem behaviors could yield practical benefits for dropout prevention.

The mediating role of internalizing problem behaviors

On one hand, negative life events significantly contribute to the development of internalizing problem behaviors such as depression, anxiety, and withdrawal. These behaviors may not only impact academic performance but also lead to a loss of academic interest, decreased classroom engagement, and a decline in overall academic achievement, thus increasing the likelihood of dropping out [87–89]. For instance, depressive moods may reduce classroom participation, while anxiety may create discomfort in the school environment, further diminishing academic motivation and sense of belonging [86, 90–92]. Additionally, internalizing problem behaviors can weaken adolescents’ social support networks, such as reducing interactions with peers and teachers, leading to feelings of social isolation, which in turn increases dropout risk [41, 86, 92–94].

The mediating role of externalizing problem behaviors

On the other hand, the study also revealed that negative life events significantly increase externalizing problem behaviors, such as aggression and disruptive actions [87, 95]. These behaviors may exacerbate adolescents’ tendencies to drop out of school [38, 86]. Research indicates that externalizing behaviors may be closely linked to issues in adolescents’ social adaptation, such as developing rebellious attitudes toward school rules and discipline [96], which may heighten friction with schools, teachers, and peers, ultimately leading to more school conflicts, strained peer relationships, and academic decline [97]. This deteriorating school experience further undermines students’ sense of school belonging and academic motivation, creating a vicious cycle that increases the likelihood of disciplinary actions and dropout.

In summary, negative life events have a direct impact on students’ mental health and academic performance by triggering internalizing behaviors, while also indirectly increasing the likelihood of dropout through complex psychological and social mechanisms. On the other hand, such events worsen students’ difficulties in social adaptation and increase school conflicts by triggering externalizing behaviors. This weakens their sense of school belonging and academic motivation, ultimately heightening the risk of dropout.

The moderating role of external attribution tendency

It is important to note that this study further suggested the potential moderating effect of specific external academic achievement attribution tendencies on the relationship between negative life events and problem behaviors (supporting Hypotheses H3a and H3b). Specifically, a high tendency for external attribution of academic achievement significantly amplified the impact of negative life events on internalizing problem behaviors. In contrast, a low tendency for external attribution of academic achievement notably strengthened the effect of negative life events on externalizing problem behaviors.

The moderating role of high academic achievement with external attribution tendency

In terms of the impact of high academic achievement coupled with a tendency toward external attribution, the results suggest that individuals who exhibit both high academic performance and a strong external attribution tendency may be more prone to internalizing problem behaviors when faced with negative life events. This finding supports the theoretical premise from previous research that attribution styles influence emotional regulation, and aligns with the self-efficacy theory, which posits that individuals who do not attribute their successes to their own abilities or efforts are likely to experience a decrease in self-efficacy [46, 47]. This suggests that the combination of high academic achievement and an external attribution tendency may exacerbate internalizing behaviors, such as depression and anxiety, by diminishing one’s sense of accomplishment and control [45, 48, 49]. This finding may support the theoretical view that an over-reliance on uncontrollable external attributions can impede individual adaptation [45], but also provides a fresh perspective on interventions for internalizing problem behaviors, highlighting the potential significance of attribution training [98, 99].

The moderating role of low academic achievement with external attribution tendency

In contrast, the impact of low academic achievement coupled with a tendency for external attribution may be strongly linked to the exacerbation of externalizing problem behaviors. Research has shown that individuals with low academic achievement and a tendency for external attribution are more likely to express anger and hostility in response to negative life events, which can, in turn, manifest as aggressive or antisocial behaviors [54, 90]. This finding builds upon earlier research on hostile attribution bias and anger rumination, suggesting that low academic achievement, combined with external attribution, not only increases the likelihood of interpreting others’ behaviors with hostility, but may also lead to emotional regulation failures, making individuals more prone to venting their emotions through aggressive actions [50, 55]. This underscores the importance of considering attribution patterns when addressing externalizing problem behaviors. Interventions aimed at reducing hostile attribution bias and fostering more positive emotional regulation strategies could be key in managing these behaviors [100].

Practical implications

In conclusion, specific external attribution tendencies play a moderating role in the relationship between negative life events and problem behaviors by influencing how individuals regulate their emotions and behavior, thereby aggravating either internalizing or externalizing issues. From a practical standpoint, these findings offer valuable insights for educational and psychological interventions, particularly in addressing the risk of school dropout. we propose specific, actionable recommendations for different stakeholders to address the complex relationship between negative life events, attribution styles, and dropout tendencies.

Implications for Students

The findings suggest that students may benefit from attribution style training programs that incorporate cognitive restructuring exercises to help them recognize and modify external attribution patterns. Such programs should include self-reflection journals where students can practice identifying internal factors such as effort and strategy that contribute to both academic successes and failures. Additionally, peer mentoring programs where successful students with healthy attribution styles can model adaptive thinking patterns would provide valuable social learning opportunities. Students also need emotional regulation skills training, particularly mindfulness-based stress reduction techniques specifically targeting internalizing behaviors such as anxiety and depression. For those prone to externalizing behaviors, anger management workshops and peer support groups for students experiencing similar negative life events would provide essential coping mechanisms and social support networks. The large effect sizes observed for both mediation pathways (β > 0.45) suggest that these interventions could potentially improve dropout prevention outcomes, as the mediating mechanisms account for significant proportions of variance in dropout tendency.

Implications for teachers

eachers play a crucial role in early identification and intervention by recognizing attribution patterns through classroom observations, particularly identifying students who consistently blame external factors for poor performance. The development of screening tools to identify students at risk for internalizing and externalizing behaviors following negative life events would enable timely interventions. Teachers should implement brief classroom-based interventions that promote internal, controllable attributions for academic outcomes through modified instructional strategies. These strategies should include attribution retraining in feedback practices, such as emphasizing “Your improvement shows your hard work is paying off” rather than “You’re naturally good at this.” Creating classroom environments that emphasize effort and strategy over ability, while providing multiple opportunities for students to experience success through their own efforts, would foster healthier attribution patterns and reduce dropout risk.

Implications for parents

Family-based attribution training represents a critical intervention point, as parents need education about the importance of attribution styles in their children’s responses to setbacks. Parents require guidance on how to respond to children’s academic difficulties using internal, controllable attributions, along with workshops on recognizing early signs of internalizing and externalizing behaviors. Effective communication strategies are essential, including training parents in active listening techniques to support children experiencing negative life events. Providing parents with scripts and examples of how to discuss academic performance in ways that promote healthy attributions, combined with establishing family goal-setting practices that emphasize effort and process over outcomes, would create supportive home environments that complement school-based interventions.

Implications for Policy Makers

Educational policy frameworks should integrate attribution style training into standard curriculum requirements for social-emotional learning, while mandating professional development on trauma-informed practices and attribution theory for educators. Establishing funding mechanisms for school-based mental health services targeting attribution-related interventions is essential for widespread implementation. Resource allocation should provide dedicated funding for early intervention programs targeting students at risk for dropout, support research initiatives examining the effectiveness of attribution-based interventions in diverse populations, and invest in training programs for school counselors and psychologists in evidence-based attribution retraining techniques. Systemic interventions require developing inter-agency collaboration protocols between schools, mental health services, and social services, creating standardized assessment tools for monitoring the effectiveness of attribution-based interventions, and establishing quality indicators for school-based prevention programs targeting dropout risk.

Limitations and future directions

This study sheds light on the intricate relationship between negative life events, attribution tendencies, and dropout tendencies, yet it has certain limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional design employed in the study restricts our ability to draw causal inferences between negative life events, problem behaviors, and dropout tendencies. Future research would benefit from longitudinal designs to investigate these relationships over time, offering a more comprehensive understanding of the causal mechanisms at play.

Secondly, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to social desirability bias and common method bias. Although we implemented safeguards such as Harman’s single-factor test to assess these biases, future studies should incorporate multiple data sources (e.g., teacher reports or objective behavioral assessments) to improve the reliability and validity of the findings.

Thirdly, the sample was limited to one region in China, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other cultural contexts. Future research could expand to include cross-cultural comparisons, exploring whether these relationships hold true across different cultural settings or among diverse populations.

Lastly, while some of our model fit indices were slightly below traditional stringent criteria, this should be interpreted in the context of model complexity and the challenges inherent in measuring complex psychological constructs. Future research might benefit from developing more refined measurement instruments or employing alternative modeling approaches to achieve optimal fit indices.

Additionally, our choice to focus solely on R² for effect size interpretation, while methodologically sound for cross-study comparisons, may limit the comprehensiveness of our effect size reporting. The R² values provide information about explained variance but do not capture all aspects of effect magnitude. Furthermore, the attribution style measures used in this study were developed and validated primarily in Western contexts. While we adapted them for use in a Chinese sample, the cultural appropriateness and validity of these attribution constructs in Chinese educational contexts warrant further investigation. Cultural differences in attribution patterns and their relationship to academic outcomes may influence the generalizability of our findings.

Despite these limitations, the study makes significant contributions to understanding the complex interplay between negative life events, attribution tendencies, and dropout tendencies. By examining the mediating role of internalizing and externalizing problem behaviors in the effect of negative life events on dropout tendencies, this research provides deeper insights into the psychological and behavioral adaptation mechanisms of adolescents.

Furthermore, it is the first to highlight the moderating role of specific external attribution tendencies—such as external attributions related to both high and low academic achievement—in this mechanism, thereby enhancing the application of attribution theory in educational psychology and juvenile delinquency prevention. Based on these findings, educational and psychological interventions could be developed to help at-risk students adjust their attribution styles and stay on track academically, reducing the likelihood of dropout and associated delinquency, and promoting greater social safety and stability.
