Analysis of Chemical Kinetics of Multistep Reactions by Mean Reaction Time
Authors: Chiwook Park
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Although methods to derive the rate equation from a kinetic model have been known for over a century, it remains mathematically challenging to derive the rate equation for complex reactions involving multiple steps, as the derivation requires a solution for simultaneous differential equations. Furthermore, the derived kinetic equations are often difficult to intuitively understand. Here, we report a radically different approach to analyze chemical kinetics using the mean reaction time, the average of the time required for the completion of a chemical reaction. This mathematical description of chemical kinetics provides an intuitive understanding of how individual steps in a multistep reaction contributes to the overall kinetics. We demonstrate here the step-by-step approach to derive the formula for the mean reaction time from kinetic models. Surprisingly, one can derive the formula for the mean reaction time without solving simultaneous differential equations or using a steady-state approximation. Being an ensemble-averaged value, the mean reaction time cannot describe the distribution of the reaction time of the individual chemical entities. However, the mean reaction time reveals invaluable insights on how the energy levels of the ground states and the transition states affect the kinetics of the multistep reaction. As proof of principle, we apply the mean reaction time to enzyme kinetics and demonstrate that one can readily derive the expressions of the kinetic parameters (kcat/KM and kcat) even without deriving the Michaelis–Menten equation.

Introduction

Chemical kinetics is a fundamental topic in chemistry. By analyzing how experimental conditions and parameters influence the reaction rate, chemists have deciphered the molecular mechanism and the energy requirement for chemical reactions.1−3 The quantitative analysis of the reaction rate requires the formulation of the rate equations, which describe the dependence of the reaction rate on the concentration of the reactants. When a chemical reaction occurs in multiple steps, the rate equation is expressed as a function of the elementary rate constants for the individual steps. Understanding how the elementary rate constants affect the overall reaction rate is critical in analyzing the kinetic data. However, being a solution of simultaneous differential equations in principle, the rate equations are mathematically complex even for a chemical reaction with only a few steps.4,5Thus, it is not so straightforward to assess the relationship between the overall reaction rate and the elementary rate constants. For example, Scheme 1 shows a kinetic model for a simple two-step reaction that occurs between one substrate (A) and one product (C) with an intermediate (B). Predicting how the change in any of the rate constants affects the overall reaction rate is not so intuitive, even in the case of this simple kinetic model. As more steps are added to the kinetic model, the solution of the simultaneous differential equations becomes exponentially more complex. Due to this technical difficulty, chemists commonly rely on numerical methods, which are readily available to any desirable level of accuracy, without deriving the analytical solution of the rate equation. In case the analytical solution is necessary, chemists employ various approximations, such as the steady-state approximation or the rapid equilibria approximation, especially for enzyme-catalyzed reactions.1,3,6 Still, extracting the physical and chemical meaning of the terms in the equations is challenging.7,8

Here we report a radically different approach to describe chemical kinetics using the mean reaction time (τrxn), which is the average time required for the completion of a reaction, for multistep reactions composed of first-order (and pseudo-first-order) elementary reactions. Surprisingly, the mean reaction time can be determined without solving simultaneous differential equations or assuming any approximation. Moreover, the mean reaction time has the unit of time, which allows a more intuitive appreciation of the contribution of the individual terms to the overall speed of the reaction. In reactions with only irreversible steps, the mean reaction time is simply the sum of the time constant (1/k) of the individual steps. For the reaction with reversible steps, extra reaction time needs to be added due to the repeated forward and reverse reactions at the reversible steps. We report here a step-by-step approach to calculate the extra reaction time required for the reversibility. Based on a few simple rules, one can easily derive the expression for the mean reaction time even for multistep reactions with reversible steps. We demonstrate the feasibility and the utility of the mean reaction time by deriving the expression of the mean reaction time for an enzyme-catalyzed reaction and the expressions for kcat and kcat/KM, the two key parameters describing the enzyme kinetics.

Results and Discussion

Definition of the Mean Reaction Time

When a chemical reaction ends with the formation of a product, the time when each product is formed has a distribution. We define the mean reaction time (τrxn) as the mean of the time required for product formation. We calculate the mean reaction time by averaging the time (t) weighted by the amount of the product that has formed between time t and t + dt, which is, by definition, the rate of the reaction (d[P]/dt) at time t. Therefore,5For the first-order elementary reaction shown in Scheme 2, the kinetic equation for the production of B is a single-exponential function as6where [A]0 is the concentration of A at t = 0. When calculated with eq 5,7For first-order elementary reactions, the mean reaction time (τrxn) is identical to the time constant (τ), which is also known as the residence time or the mean lifetime of A. The half-life of the first-order reaction (t1/2 = ln 2/k ∼ 0.693τ), which is the statistical “median” of the reaction time, is smaller than the “mean” of the reaction time. As it is the same as the time constant, the mean reaction time of a first-order reaction is independent of the concentration of the reactant. The conversion of each reactant molecule to a product molecule is an independent event. Each molecule has the same probability of conversion regardless of the quantity of its kind. Therefore, the mean reaction time is a characteristic constant of the reaction under the given experimental conditions regardless of the concentration of the reactant.

For higher-order reactions, the concept of the mean reaction time is not applicable because the reaction time of one reactant is dependent on the concentrations of other reactants and the concentrations of other reactants vary during the reactions. However, when the concentration of the reactant of interest is significantly lower than the concentrations of other reactants, the concentrations of other reactants can be treated as constants, and the higher-order rate constant is converted to a pseudo-first-order rate constant. We use this approach later to deal with the substrate-association step in an enzyme-catalyzed reaction (vide infra).

Multistep Reactions with Only Irreversible Steps

To calculate the mean reaction time for a multistep reaction, we sum up the mean lifetimes of the reactant and the intermediate in the reaction trajectory until the reaction is complete. When a reaction occurs in two sequential irreversible steps, as shown in Scheme 3, the mean reaction time (τrxn) is the sum of the reciprocals of the two kinetic constants (Rule 1).8Obviously, 1/k1 and 1/k2 are the mean lifetimes of A and B, respectively, and the overall mean reaction time is the sum of the lifetimes. One can verify eq 8 easily by deriving the rate equation for the production of C and applying the definition of the mean reaction time in eq 5 (see Supporting Information). As long as the two steps are independent of each other, which means that B has no memory of when it has formed from A, the mean reaction time is the sum of the mean lifetime of A and B. The half-lives of A and B do not have this additive property because the half-life is not the mean but the median. This rule is valid for reactions with more than two sequential irreversible steps. The mean reaction time is the sum of the reciprocals of all the first-order rate constants of the steps in the reaction.

When a reaction occurs in two parallel irreversible steps, as shown in Scheme 4, the mean reaction time (τrxn) is the reciprocal of the sum of the two kinetic constants (Rule 2).9The reactant (A) disappears through the two parallel steps; still, the decay of A follows first-order kinetics with a rate constant of k1 + k2. Therefore, the mean lifetime of A is 1/(k1 + k2). The fraction of A that is converted to B is proportional to the relative magnitude of the rate constant k1 to the sum of k1 and k2, which we define as p:10Then, the fraction of A converted to C is proportional to 1 – p. Therefore, the final concentrations of B and C are p[A]0 and (1 – p)[A]0, respectively. This rule is valid for reactions with more than two parallel steps. The mean reaction time is the reciprocal of the sum of all of the first-order rate constants, and the partition of A to each product is proportional to the rate constant for each step.

The expression for the mean reaction time in eq 20 has a total of six terms. Three of them, 1/k1, 1/k3, and 1/k5, corresponds to the energy barriers for A to B, B to C, and C to D, respectively (Figure 2). If the steps of the reaction are all irreversible, then the mean reaction time will be determined by only these three energy barriers. The rest three terms, k2/(k1k3), k2k4/(k1k3k5), and k4/(k3k5), are the kinetic cost of the reversibility of the reaction. The energy barriers corresponding to the three terms can be visualized easily on the reaction energy diagram.8k2/(k1k3) corresponds to the height of the second transition state relative to the energy of A, k2k4/(k1k3k5) corresponds to the height of the third transition state relative to the energy of A, and k4/(k3k5) corresponds to the height of the third transition state relative to the energy of B (Figure 2). Therefore, the total residence time of A (eq 21) is determined by the heights of the three subsequent barriers relative to the energy of A. Likewise, the total residence time of B (eq 22) is determined by the heights of the two subsequent barriers relative to the energy of B. Therefore, all the energy barriers until the first irreversible step (C → D) contribute to the total residence time of A, and the contribution of each subsequent barrier to the total residence time of A is determined by the height of each barrier relative to the energy of A. In the nominal energy diagram in Figure 2, the second barrier is the major contributor to the total residence time of A.

The six terms in eq 20 are arranged in Table 1 according to their corresponding ground states (A, B, or C) and transition states (A → B, B → C, and C → D). Each term in the table is the contribution of the corresponding barrier to the total residence time of the corresponding ground state. The pattern of the terms is quite obvious; each term is the ratio of the product of all the reverse rate constants to the product of all the forward rate constants between the ground state and the transition state. Note that the reverse rate constant corresponding to the transition state is not included. The diagonal of the table contains the mean lifetime of each ground state, which is also the residence time of each ground state in case all the steps are irreversible. Above the diagonal are the kinetic costs of the reversibility, i.e. the increase in the residence time of each ground state due to the barriers connected through reversible steps. The sum of the terms in each row corresponds to the total residence time of each ground state. The ground state with the greatest total residence time is the kinetically significant intermediate(13) or the least conducive step.14 The sum of the terms in each column is the contribution of each transition state to the mean reaction time. The transition state with the greatest contribution to the mean reaction time is the kinetically significant barrier(13) or the most sensitive step.14 (We do not use the more common term “rate-limiting step” intentionally here because the meaning of rate-limiting step is not as clear as the terms we use above.)

Application to Enzyme Kinetics

Now we apply the mean reaction time to the enzyme kinetics. Scheme 10 shows a simple enzyme-catalyzed reaction containing only one substrate (S) and one product (P). To determine the formula of the mean reaction time for the enzyme-catalyzed reaction in Scheme 10, we need to address two issues. First, the rate constant for the association of E and S (k1) is a second-order rate constant. We convert the second-order rate constant to a pseudo-first-order rate constant (k1[S]), which allows us to determine the mean reaction time at the given substrate concentration. Second, the enzyme-catalyzed reaction is a cyclic reaction, which starts with E and ends with E. Under this condition, 1/τrxn is the rate constant of the reaction. One enzyme converts one substrate molecule to the product during the single turnover, which takes τrxn; then, one enzyme generates 1/τrxn product molecules per unit time. The reciprocal form of the Michaelis–Menten equation has been used not only to fit the experimental data (e.g., Lineweaver–Burk plot) but also for analyzing enzyme kinetics in the time domain.7,15 Also, it has been shown that the reciprocal of the net rate constant corresponds to the transit time of each enzyme species.12 In previous studies, however, the reciprocal form of the Michaelis–Menten equation was obtained only after the Michaelis–Menten equation was derived by using the state–state approximation. Here, we derive the expression of the mean reaction time directly from the kinetic model without deriving the Michaelis–Menten equation.

Conclusions

Here we demonstrate the use of the mean reaction time in analyzing kinetics of multistep chemical reactions composed of first-order and pseudo-first-order elementary steps. The derivation of the formula of the mean reaction time is surprisingly simpler and easier than the derivation of the rate equation, which requires a solution for simultaneous differential equations.

The mean reaction time is an ensemble average, which does not reveal the distribution of the reaction time for individual reaction trajectories as in typical integrated rate equations. Therefore, the mean reaction time alone may not provide us with sufficient information to accept or reject a proposed kinetic model, let alone to demonstrate the practicality of measuring the mean reaction time experimentally. However, because the derivation and the analysis of the formulas of the mean reaction time is simple, intuitive, and insightful even for multistep reactions, this method is extremely useful in analyzing and assessing complex kinetic models and visualizing the contribution of individual steps to the overall kinetics. Moreover, the formula of the mean reaction time provides us with an intuitive understanding of the contribution of the individual steps and their elementary rate constants to the overall reaction rate. The arrangement of the terms comprising the mean reaction time as shown in Table 1, offers a straightforward way to visualize and dissect the contribution of each ground state and transition state to the mean reaction time.

We focus our analysis here on linear kinetic models. Deriving the formula of the mean reaction time for the kinetic models with branched pathways, loops, or autocatalysis would be the next step to broaden the potential applications of this method. Also, the mean reaction time can be a great teaching tool to introduce chemical kinetics to chemistry students and nonexperts. This method is applicable not only to chemical kinetics but also to the quantitative analysis of any processes comprising multistep exponential decay in physics and engineering.
