Empowering data-driven load forecasting by leveraging long short-term memory recurrent neural networks
Authors: Waqar Waheed, Qingshan Xu, Muhammad Aurangzeb, Sheeraz Iqbal, Saadat Hanif Dar, Z.M.S. Elbarbary
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The integration of renewable energy sources has resulted in an increasing intricacy in the functioning and organization of power systems. Accurate load forecasting, particularly taking into account dynamic factors like as climatic and socioeconomic impacts, is essential for effective management. Conventional statistical analysis and machine learning methods struggle with accurately capturing the intricate temporal relationships present in load data. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), specifically long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, demonstrate potential in representing and interpreting sequences of data. This study presents an approach that employs an LSTM-RNN model for load forecast prediction. This study highlights the importance of this technology in combining effective demand response with distributed renewable energy sources, which are crucial for the stability of smart grids and accurate power demand estimation. The LSTM-RNN model has outstanding accuracy, with a Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of 1.5% and a Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of 26.5 for hourly forecasts. Additionally, it achieves a MAPE of 1.77% and an RMSE of 30 for yearly load estimations. The hourly LSTM-RNN load forecast model outperforms the yearly LSTM-RNN load forecast model, as evidenced by lower error rates. Importantly, this model demonstrates robustness even when the inputs are inadequate or noisy. To summarize, this research suggests that LSTM-RNN is a practical and efficient approach for precisely forecasting load in power systems. This underscores its capacity to enhance operational efficiency and resilience in power systems.

Introduction

Accurate load forecasting is crucial to the success of any system for managing electricity operations. A precise projection of future power loads is beneficial for designing the power system's operation plan. It contributes to the reliability of the electrical grid and the equilibrium between supply and demand. Predicting future energy consumption is a crucial component of any energy management system, as well as essential information for any market that bases pricing on energy consumption [1]. Load forecasting, which has been the focus of extensive research over the past decade, is essential for reliable power delivery, efficient power system operation, strategic planning of power infrastructure, and preventative maintenance of power system. As an analytical tool, load forecasting can help with power system scheduling, demand response, contingency analysis, and load flow analysis [2], [3].

After utilizing cutting-edge communication, computation, and control methods, smart power systems have completely transformed the conventional power grid. Smart grids can function more efficiently and securely than traditional electricity grids because of their increased interactivity, automation, and intelligence. To lessen their negative effects on the environment, smart grids are increasingly incorporating renewable energy sources, energy storage devices, and electric vehicles into their operations. Smart grids enable utilities to more effectively manage electricity supply and demand, reduce the number of outages, and maximize their resources by monitoring and controlling energy consumption in real time. In order to implement efficient strategies in the fields of capacity planning, management, distribution, and corrective maintenance, it is essential to forecast future energy consumption [6], [7]. In view of the expanding population and rising demand for resources such as electrical power and other resources, accurate forecasting provides effective capacity planning and minimizes overestimation or underestimating of utility growth plans. Long-term forecasting is the most important time range because of the consequences of strategic and expensive decisions, including the construction of utility facilities. An overestimation of electricity demand, especially in long-term forecasts, will lead to a significant rise in the construction of unnecessary power-generating plants. On the other hand, if the predicted electricity demand is underestimated, there would be a shortage of electricity generation and dissatisfied customers. Therefore, the focus of this research will be on long-term electricity demand forecasts and the application of the models that result in the most accurate projections. Load forecasting is a technique that anticipates the future demand for electricity within a given time frame. This method enables electric utility companies to effectively regulate the power grid and assure sufficient capacity to meet future energy demands [8]. It is possible to conduct load forecasting for individual customers, extensive regions, and even an entire nation. Long-term, medium-term, short-term, and very short-term forecasting are the four primary classifications of load prediction. A crucial aspect of power grid management is short-term forecasting, which is typically conducted fora few hours to a few days. It entails analyzing historical data, upcoming weather forecasts, and anticipated changes in electricity consumption patterns due to holidays, sporting events, and other special events. On the other hand, Long-term forecasting is typically conducted over a period of several months to several years and is useful for investment planning. Long-term forecasting takes into account various factors such as population growth, economic trends, changes in energy regulations, and the implementation of new technologies. Forecasting the load for electricity consumption is challenging due to a number of factors, such as the unpredictability of electricity consumption, the impact of external factors such as weather conditions, and the rapid development of the energy sector. Professionals in the field of load forecasting use complex statistical models and machine learning techniques to analyze historical data and forecast the future condition of the power grid.

Maintaining power system reliability, minimizing power outages, and controlling energy generation and transmission costs requires accurate load forecasting. Data inputs, intended time frame, spatial and temporal parameters, and scale can affect load forecasting model structure, which can range from a single building to a nation's power usage. The paper suggests using LSTM-RNN to predict load, overcoming the limitations of statistical and machine learning methods in capturing load data's complex temporal correlations. Pre-processing of experimented data

Power system operators' ability to accurately predict future electricity demand is essential to efficient energy management. Blackouts may be avoided, resources can be used effectively, and new insights into customer behavior can be gained with reliable load forecasting. However, best outcomes in predicting can only be achieved by a complicated and multi-stage pre-processing of data. Data cleaning is the first step in the preparation process for load forecasting. In this step, incorrect or unnecessary details are eliminated once again; this includes data that is either missing or inconsistent as well as any extreme outliers. Predictive models rely heavily on clean data, so cleansing the data is essential. Initially, detection and rectification of missing values by imputation techniques was done, specifically by substituting gaps with the mean or median values of the corresponding datasets, contingent upon the data distribution. Furthermore, conducted outlier detection and removal to eradicate extreme data that could distort the analysis.

Transforming the data is the second step. Electricity consumption is a trending and seasonal time series. The data needs to be translated into a format that highlights these patterns to enable efficient modeling. Numerous methods exist, such as data normalization, logarithmic implementations, and data differentiation, for removing patterns. The third step, feature engineering, is developing additional characteristics that add context to the data in order to improve the accuracy of the forecasting algorithm. At this point, new features are built from existing data in order to enhance the model's accuracy. Features that collect weather-related data, including temperature, humidity, or wind speed, which may effect electrical usage, can be built using weather data as an application. Splitting the dataset into training and testing data is the fourth step. To accurately predict how well a model will perform on untested data, it must first be trained on one set of data and then tested on another. Overfilling in forecasting models can be found by splitting data into training and testing sets. Last but not least, normalizing data is commonly utilized to boost the efficiency of machine learning models. In this step, we make sure all features are on the same scale by either shifting the data such that the minimum and maximum values are 0 and 1, or by shifting the data so that the mean is 0 and standard deviation is 1. For optimal model performance and less influence from larger-scale features, normalizing the data is a crucial step. The min-max method was used to standardize the load demand data in this study to the range 0-1 for easier analysis. Min-max normalization is expressed as [37] in Eq. (1):(1)v⁎t=vt−minmax⁡−min

When a value is normalized, it is represented by v*t, where vt is the unnormalized value, min and max are the minimum and maximum values of the vector.

In conclusion, establishing reliable and efficient load forecasting models requires a multi-step pre-processing of data. Data normalization, data splitting, feature engineering, and data cleaning are all steps in this process. Blackouts may be avoided, resources can be used effectively, and customer behavior can be better understood if certain steps are implemented to improve energy management.

Sliding-window approach

The sliding window method is a prevalent technique for partitioning time series data into training and testing sets in load forecasting. This method employs a fixed-size window that systematically examines the data, with each window functioning as a training set for the model, while the following window serves as a testing set for assessment as depicted in Fig. 7. The crux of this strategy is the selection of an acceptable window size (the training length) and step size (the interval for window movement); for instance, a 24-hour window may use a step size of one hour, resulting in overlapping data segments. Each window is utilized to train the model, which forecasts the load for the subsequent time step, after which the window progresses by the step size, reiterating the process. The model's efficacy is evaluated by juxtaposing anticipated loads with actual measurements. This iterative method enables the model to discern temporal dynamics and patterns within the data. The sliding window method gives a more accurate picture of how well the model works on new data because it takes into account the time series' temporal dependencies. This is different from regular train-test splits, which don't take these correlations into account. Due to the vanishing or expanding gradient problem, time dependencies can be difficult to train with traditional RNN. This issue has recently been solved in deep RNNs using methods such as LSTM units. LSTM was developed as a distinctive, efficient gradient-based solution to the vanishing or expanding gradient problem. The LSTM-RNN is often used in deep learning for sequential data processing tasks. In typical RNNs, the network is taught to accumulate data over numerous time steps and use it to inform predictions for the current time step. However, due to the vanishing gradient problem, in which the gradients necessary to update the network's parameters become very small, neural networks may have difficulties learning long-term dependencies.

To address this issue, the memory cell in LSTM networks is a set of gates that controls the intake and outflow of data within the network. The gates are controlled by activation functions, which can be programmed to keep or discard data selectively. Three fundamental gates play critical roles in the information processing of LSTM network. The forget gate determines which bits of data in a memory cell should be discarded, whereas the input gate controls the incoming data flow. Finally, the output gate determines how much data is transmitted to the next time step. Speech recognition, natural language processing, and time series prediction are just a few of the applications of these gates. As a result, LSTM networks have gained popularity as a go-to method for addressing difficult difficulties in various domains. All three gates input, forget, and output in an LSTM network include weights and biases that serve as the network's governing parameters. During training, the parameters are adjusted based on the back-propagated error using a method called gradient descent. The weights define the effectiveness of the gate's input, and the biases determine the minimum input required to activate the gate. At the outset of training, the weights and biases are set arbitrarily, and they can be adjusted at each iteration of the optimization method.

To learn the optimal weights and biases, a loss function is used to quantify the deviation between the network's expected and actual output. Here, the network processes only a small amount of training data at a time and adjusts its parameters based on the gradient of the loss function with respect to those settings. This is a form of stochastic gradient descent. The weights of the input and output layers, as well as any extra hidden layers in the network, are examples of further parameters that LSTM networks may include in addition to the gating parameters. The output of an LSTM network is determined by its weights and biases, which in turn are determined by the information flow through the gates. Backpropagation and gradient descent are used to learn these parameters, which the network then uses to adjust to the particular patterns present in the input data.

It is essential to note that the efficacy of LSTM networks for long-short term load forecasting can vary based on the load data attributes and prediction horizon. STLF involves estimating the load for the next few hours to days. LSTM networks can capture short-term dependencies and patterns in the load data, such as daily and weekly cycles, weather effects, and holiday effects, making them ideally adapted for this task. In general, LSTM networks can be useful for STLF when the load data has a high level of temporal variability and complexity. In contrast, LTLF involves predicting the load several days to years in advance. LSTM networks may be less effective for this task due to their inability to capture long-term trends and seasonality in the data, which can be crucial for accurate long-term forecasts. However, there are ways to improve the performance of LSTM networks for LTLF, such as incorporating external factors (e.g., economic indicators, demographic data) into the model or utilizing hybrid models that combine LSTM networks with other methods. Conclusions

This study presents a complex Long Short-Term Memory Recurrent Neural Network (LSTM-RNN) model that is specifically developed for load forecasting and effectively captures the complex nonlinear interactions in energy demand forecast by utilizing substantial historical data that includes load profiles, temperature fluctuations, humidity levels, and seasonal patterns. By conducting a thorough analysis utilizing actual demand data obtained from a well-known German utility, this approach clearly shows a significant enhancement in forecast precision. Our LSTM-RNN model consistently beats baseline approaches in both short-term (1-24 hour) and long-term (annual peak load) forecasting horizons, obtaining noteworthy performance metrics. LSTM-RNN model demonstrates exceptional accuracy with a remarkably low Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of 1.5% and a Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of 26.5 for hourly forecasts. Additionally, it obtains excellent results of 1.77% MAPE and 30 RMSE for annual estimates. In addition, this investigation highlights the LSTM-RNN's exceptional capacity to detect intricate patterns, store information over long periods of time, and adjust to different data circumstances. The intrinsic resilience of LSTM-RNN approach makes it especially suitable for practical implementation in real-world scenarios, where the availability and quality of data may differ. The findings outlined consequences that go beyond the realm of load forecasting by providing utilities with a valuable tool to improve their operational efficiency, optimize resource allocation, and enhance the overall reliability of the grid. The LSTM-RNN model enhances the ability of utility firms to make informed decisions regarding generation, transmission, and distribution infrastructure planning.

In the future, the anticipation of sophisticated neural network models, specifically LSTM-RNNs, will have a wider range of uses in solving various problems encountered by the power industry. These advanced technologies, such as demand response optimization, grid management, and energy trading, have the potential to greatly transform the operations of utilities in a rapidly changing and linked energy environment. This research adds to the development of AI-based solutions in energy grids, emphasizing the significant impact of LSTM-RNNs in improving forecasting accuracy and guiding strategic decision-making. By continuing to refine and expand upon these methodologies will pave the way for a more resilient, efficient, and sustainable energy future.
