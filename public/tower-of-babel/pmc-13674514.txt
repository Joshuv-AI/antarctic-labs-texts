Latent effector capacity governs reversible T cell exhaustion: A theoretical mathematical model of PD-1 blockade response
Authors: Aubrey Y. Liew, Ying Li, Haidong Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
T cell exhaustion is often viewed as a terminal state marked by irreversible loss of function during chronic infection and cancer. However, the rapid recovery of antitumor activity in clinical responders after PD-1 blockade suggests that some exhausted T cells retain hidden functional potential. To explain this paradox, we present a theoretical framework that separates latent effector capacity from active effector function. Latent effector capacity represents a history-dependent state reflecting preserved epigenetic accessibility and regulatory readiness, distinct from ongoing gene expression. In this model, PD-1 signaling acts as a reversible mask that suppresses effector function without eliminating the underlying effector capacity. The framework explains preserved chromatin accessibility, rapid reinvigoration after checkpoint blockade, and variable therapeutic responses. By incorporating nonlinear maintenance and erosion of latent effector capacity, the model predicts a bistable system with a point of no return beyond which exhaustion becomes irreversible. These principles may guide development of mechanistically informed biomarkers and artificial intelligence models predicting response to PD-1 therapy.

INTRODUCTION

A defining feature of chronic infection and cancer is the emergence of so-called exhausted T cells, a population characterized by diminished cytokine production, impaired cytotoxicity, sustained expression of inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, and altered transcriptional profiles (1–3). For many years, these cells were viewed as terminally dysfunctional, i.e., locked into an irreversible state of differentiation that precluded meaningful contribution to immunity against pathogens or cancer cells (4–6). This view was grounded in observations of reduced effector gene expression, defective proliferative capacity, and stable transcriptional signatures associated with long-term antigen exposure (1, 7, 8). However, the clinical success of immune checkpoint blockade has fundamentally challenged this interpretation. T cells that appear functionally inert immediately before therapy can rapidly regain effector activity following PD-1 blockade, exhibiting restored cytotoxic killing and clonal expansion on timescales shorter than those required for de novo differentiation or extensive epigenetic reprogramming in both preclinical and clinical studies (9–12). The speed and robustness of this response are incompatible with models in which exhaustion reflects wholesale erasure of effector programs (9, 13, 14). Instead, these observations imply that, at least in a substantial fraction of exhausted T cells, core components of effector identity remain preserved in a latent form (13, 15–17).

This paradox, apparent terminal dysfunction followed by rapid functional recovery, suggests that exhaustion cannot be fully explained by irreversible loss of differentiation state. Rather, it points to the existence of an active, reversible suppressive mechanism that constrains effector function without eliminating the underlying molecular mechanism required to execute it (18, 19). Chromatin accessibility studies have shown that exhausted T cells often retain open regulatory regions at canonical effector loci (17, 20), including genes encoding cytotoxic molecules and inflammatory cytokines (21, 22). Similarly, lineage-defining transcription factors associated with effector identity can remain bound at key regulatory elements even when downstream gene expression is attenuated (8, 23, 24). These findings indicate that T cell exhaustion involves dissociation between potential and output (25, 26).

To resolve this conceptual tension, we propose that T cell state is governed by two partially decoupled variables. The first variable is latent effector capacity, reflecting the epigenetic, chromatin, and regulatory architecture accumulated through prior antigenic stimulation. This latent effector capacity encodes T cell’s readiness to mount an effector response via its accessible enhancers, poised promoters, and stabilized transcription factor networks, without necessarily requiring ongoing transcription or functional output. This component evolves on relatively slow timescales, shaped by the history of T cell receptor (TCR) engagement, inflammatory cues, and epigenetic reinforcement mechanisms, and is therefore resistant to rapid collapse. The second variable is active effector output, representing realized gene expression and functional execution at a given time. This output is highly dynamic, sensitive to proximal signaling inputs, and subject to rapid suppression by inhibitory pathways. In this framework, immune checkpoint signaling, i.e., most prominently through PD-1, acts primarily on this active layer, attenuating transcriptional realization of effector programs despite preserved epigenetic competence. Accordingly, a recent study reveals that some thymic CD8+ single positive T cells have a latent effector differentiation program, but their effector phenotype and function are masked by PD-1 signal (27). To that end, PD-1 engagement masks effector activity without directly dismantling the underlying chromatin landscape that supports it (28). This decouple effect provides a unifying explanation for several hallmark features of T cell exhaustion. It accounts for the coexistence of preserved epigenetic accessibility with diminished cytokine production (1, 24), explains why exhaustion can be rapidly reversed in some contexts yet refractory in others (26, 29), and clarifies why phenotype-based measurements alone can be misleading indicators of true functional potential of T cells (22).

Within this view, exhausted T cells are not uniformly inert (8, 22, 23) but, instead, occupy a spectrum of states defined by how much latent effector capacity remains and how strongly that capacity is suppressed at the level of realization. By explicitly separating capacity from output, we shift the interpretation of exhaustion from a static terminal fate to a dynamic, history-dependent process. From this perspective, immune checkpoint blockade does not primarily reprograms T cells de novo; rather, it unmasks preexisting effector potential to the extent that this potential remains intact. Establishing this conceptual distinction is essential for understanding heterogeneity in therapeutic response, defining the point at which exhaustion becomes irreversible, and developing quantitative frameworks that integrate transcriptional, epigenetic, and functional data into a coherent model of T cell exhaustion.

Current experimental evidence has established that exhausted T cells are heterogeneous and include progenitor-like or stem-like populations that retain responsiveness to PD-1 blockade, as well as more terminally exhausted states with limited reversibility (6). Similarly, reinvigoration and resilience models emphasize that therapeutic responses often reflect preserved functional potential rather than complete reversal of exhaustion. The present work builds upon these concepts but differs in its level of abstraction and mechanistic objective. Rather than defining an additional exhausted T cell subset, we introduce latent effector capacity as a quantitative dynamical state variable that is independent of instantaneous effector gene expression and shared across multiple T cell phenotypes. Within this framework, progenitor-like, stem-like, resilient, and terminally exhausted states are interpreted as different regions of a common state space governed by three interacting processes: preservation of latent effector capacity, checkpoint-mediated masking of functional output, and cumulative history-dependent loss of capacity. The novelty of the present work lies in formulating these qualitative concepts as quantitative dynamical variables, which is intended as a theoretical and hypothesis-generating tool rather than a definitive description of T cell biology.

Latent effector capacity as a dynamical state variable

Rather than reflecting instantaneous transcriptional activity, E(t) captures the structural and regulatory state of the chromatin landscape that permits rapid effector gene expression when inhibitory constraints are relieved. This distinction is critical, as numerous studies demonstrate preserved chromatin accessibility and transcription factor occupancy at effector regions in exhausted T cells (8, 22), indicating that effector identity can persist in a latent, masked form. Biologically, latent effector capacity is accumulated during periods of effective TCR engagement through chromatin remodeling, enhancer activation, and stabilization of transcription factor networks, producing durable epigenetic programs that persist beyond acute signaling events (30, 31). Conversely, chronic antigen exposure and sustained inhibitory signaling progressively erode this epigenetic infrastructure through chromatin closing, enhancer decommissioning, and destabilization of effector-associated regulatory circuits (6, 32). Notably, this decay occurs more slowly than the suppression of active transcription, consistent with the observation that exhausted T cells can rapidly reacquire effector function following checkpoint blockade without requiring extensive reprogramming (9, 10, 14, 33).

The equations below define a theoretical dynamical framework rather than a calibrated quantitative simulator. Parameters are introduced to formalize biological mechanisms and generate experimentally testable hypotheses, but their numerical values are not estimated in the present work. Consequently, the model should be interpreted as structurally predictive through identifying relationships that should hold if the assumptions are correct, rather than empirically predictive of individual patient responses at this stage.

We capture these biological processes using a minimal first-order dynamical equation for latent effector capacitydE(t)dt=αSext(t)−δE(t)(1)where Sext(t) represents time-dependent external T cell activation signals. However, Sext(t) should not be interpreted as antigen abundance alone. Instead, Sext(t) represents productive activation input, integrating antigen recognition with costimulatory signals, cytokine support, and metabolic sufficiency. Conceptually, Sext(t) can be viewed as the product of antigen recognition and a permissive context. Under these conditions, stimulation promotes chromatin accessibility and reinforcement of effector-associated regulatory circuits, thereby increasing latent effector capacity. The parameter α quantifies the efficiency with which these external inputs are converted into lasting epigenetic priming, integrating chromatin remodeler recruitment, histone modification, and transcription factor binding (30, 34). The decay rate coefficient δ represents slow exhaustion-associated silencing of effector loci driven by sustained inhibitory signaling, metabolic stress, and repressive chromatin modifications (35). Notably, persistent antigen exposure is treated differently within this framework. Although chronic antigen stimulation may initially contribute to capacity loading, prolonged exposure progressively induces inhibitory receptor engagement, metabolic stress, and checkpoint-dependent erosion of latent capacity. Thus, chronic antigen exposure does not simply increase E(t); instead, it shifts the balance from productive capacity loading toward inhibitory masking and progressive loss of latent-capacity maintenance, which will be further discussed in the section of “the point of no return”.

This formulation embodies several key T cell biological principles (4, 36, 37). First, epigenetic priming is integrative: Transient activation signals incrementally load latent capacity over time, rather than triggering binary state transitions. Second, epigenetic decay is slow and proportional to existing capacity, reflecting the progressive nature of exhaustion rather than abrupt loss of effector identity. Last, the linear structure of Eq. 1 emphasizes that latent effector capacity evolves independently of instantaneous gene expression, allowing it to remain elevated even when functional output is suppressed by checkpoint signaling. Therefore, Eq. 1 explains how exhausted T cells can occupy a state characterized by high latent potential but low functional output. When Sext(t) has been historically strong, E(t) may remain elevated for prolonged periods, even if active effector gene expression is masked by inhibitory pathways such as PD-1 signaling. This decoupling establishes the foundation for rapid functional rebound upon checkpoint blockade: Effector activity can reemerge on transcriptional timescales because the underlying epigenetic readiness has already been established. Thus, modeling latent effector capacity as a dynamical state variable provides a mechanistic bridge between stimulation history, epigenetic memory, and reversible T cell dysfunction.

PD-1 signaling as a checkpoint-dependent realization factor

Building on our previous conceptual model of T cell responses (38) and supported by studies demonstrating that PD-1 engagement does not eliminate effector programs but instead suppresses their transcriptional realization through cooperative, multistep inhibition of TCR and costimulatory signaling pathways (39–42), we model PD-1–mediated inhibition as a Hill-type checkpoint-dependent realization factor. The Hill formulation captures the threshold-dependent nature of immune checkpoint regulation by introducing tunable parameters that govern both inhibitory sensitivity and cooperativity. As PD-1 signaling increases, effector output transitions smoothly from a largely uninhibited state to a strongly suppressed state.

Within this framework, PD-1 blockade is interpreted as the removal of a functional masking constraint rather than the generation of new effector potential. Consequently, therapeutic blockade can rapidly restore functional activity by unmasking preexisting latent effector capacity without requiring de novo transcriptional or epigenetic reprogramming.

Checkpoint inhibition is represented by the Hill-type masking functionM(PD1(t))=11+(PD1(t)·L(t)K)n(2)

Here, PD1(t) denotes receptor occupancy or signaling activity, and L(t) represents ligand availability, such as PD-L1 or PD-L2 expression. The product PD1(t)·L(t) therefore represents the effective receptor-ligand engagement–mediated signals for immune suppression. The parameter K is the half-maximal realization constant and defines the effective inhibitory threshold of PD-1 signaling. Notably, K should not be interpreted as the biochemical dissociation constant for PD-1–PD-L1 binding. Rather, K is a composite phenomenological parameter that summarizes the cumulative effects of multiple processes linking receptor engagement to transcriptional suppression, including signal transduction efficiency, chromatin accessibility, epigenetic stabilization, and downstream regulatory feedback. The Hill coefficient n determines the degree of cooperativity and controls the steepness of the transition between low and high levels of functional masking.

The output of the Hill function, M(PD1(t)), is a dimensionless realization coefficient ranging from 0 to 1 (but ≠ 0), where larger values indicate greater realization of latent effector capacity. Because M(PD1(t)) decreases as effective PD-1 checkpoint engagement increases, we refer to it biologically as PD-1–dependent masking term rather than as the inhibitory input itself. The inhibitory input is the upstream PD-1/PD-L1 engagement signal, whereas M(PD1(t)) denotes the fraction of preserved latent effector capacity that remains available for conversion into active output. Thus, strong checkpoint signaling drives M(PD1(t)) toward 0, whereas PD-1 blockade drives M(PD1(t)) toward 1. This masking term thereby preserves the conceptual distinction between retained effector potential and active effector output.

Next, we performed sensitivity analyses of the checkpoint threshold parameter K and the Hill coefficient n in Eq. 2. Varying K while holding n constant demonstrated that K primarily controls the threshold for checkpoint-mediated inhibition (Fig. 1A). Increasing K shifted the masking curve toward higher levels of checkpoint signaling, indicating that stronger PD-1 pathway engagement was required before effector realization became substantially suppressed. In contrast, decreasing K shifted the transition toward lower signaling levels, resulting in increased sensitivity to checkpoint inhibition. Biologically, larger K values are consistent with T cell states that maintain functional competence despite persistent inhibitory signaling, whereas smaller K values correspond to states that are more readily driven into dysfunction. On the other hand, varying the Hill coefficient n while holding K constant had little effect on the location of the inhibitory threshold but markedly altered the shape of the response curve (Fig. 1B). Low values of n produced a gradual decline in effector function across a broad range of checkpoint signaling, whereas higher values generated an increasingly steep and ultrasensitive transition between permissive and inhibited states. Thus, n primarily controls the sharpness of the checkpoint response and determines whether loss of function occurs progressively or in a switch-like manner. Biologically, n does not imply direct molecular cooperativity at the receptor-ligand level; rather, it serves as a phenomenological measure of how decisively checkpoint signaling suppresses transcriptional realization of otherwise intact effector programs.

The combined effects of K and n define distinct regions of checkpoint response behavior (Fig. 1C). Parameter combinations characterized by large K and low n produced a region that is conceptually consistent with progenitor-like exhausted T cells, which retain stem-like properties, proliferative capacity, and responsiveness to PD-1 blockade. Conversely, low K and high n generated a region that is conceptually consistent with the behavior of terminally exhausted T cells, which is refractory or less responsive to PD-1 blockade. Intermediate parameter combinations occupied a continuum between these extremes, suggesting that exhaustion phenotypes may emerge from gradual changes in both checkpoint sensitivity and response cooperativity rather than from discrete state transitions alone. Collectively, these analyses demonstrate that K and n regulate distinct dimensions of checkpoint biology. Whereas K determines the level of inhibitory signaling required to impair T cell function, n governs the abruptness of the transition into exhaustion. Their combined effects provide a mechanistic explanation for why T cells expressing comparable levels of PD-1 can display markedly different responses to immune checkpoint blockade (43, 44).

Although PD-1 is used throughout this work as the primary illustrative example, the realization function formalism can be generalized to other inhibitory checkpoint pathways, including LAG-3, TIM-3, TIGIT, and CTLA-4. In principle, any inhibitory pathway that suppresses effector realization without immediately eliminating latent effector capacity can be represented as a realization coefficient acting on the conversion of latent effector capacity into active effector output. Thus, in a generalized checkpoint network model, the PD-1–dependent masking term M(PD1(t)) can be replaced by a composite function.M [I1(t),I2(t),…,Ij(t)]where Ij(t) denotes the signaling strength of the jth inhibitory receptor or suppressive pathway. Different checkpoint systems may exhibit distinct masking thresholds, cooperativity, reversibility, and capacities to accelerate the decay of latent effector capacity. This extension naturally allows combination checkpoint blockade to be modeled as the simultaneous perturbation of multiple masking inputs rather than as a PD-1–specific phenomenon.

Functional history: Distinguishing active gene expression from cumulative functional output

Instantaneous active effector gene expression reflects the current transcriptional state of a T cell, but measurable immune function is a downstream biological outcome rather than a direct molecular state variable. Processes, such as cytokine secretion and tumor cell killing, depend not only on effector gene expression but also on protein translation, cytotoxic granule release, target cell encounter, and local tissue conditions. Consequently, active gene expression and functional execution are related but distinct quantities.

To avoid conflating these levels of organization, we introduce a distinct cumulative functional output variable, Q(t), separate from the active gene expression vector G(t). Whereas G(t) describes the instantaneous expression state of the effector program, Q(t) quantifies the cumulative biological activity generated by that expression state over time. We therefore define cumulative functional output in Eq. 4 asQ(t)=Q(0)+∫0tΦ [G(t),Z(t)] dt(4)

Here, Q(t) denotes cumulative measurable immune activity, such as cumulative cytokine secretion or integrated cytotoxic function. The quantity Q(0) represents the baseline functional state at the initial time point.

The functionΦ:[G(t),Z(t)]⟶functional output raterepresents an effector realization map that converts the internal active expression state into an instantaneous rate of biological function. The variable Z(t) captures context-dependent factors that influence the conversion of molecular activity into measurable function, including immune-synapse formation and other extrinsic constraints. Different biological processes may exhibit distinct functional relationships between gene expression and execution. For example, cytokine production may scale approximately linearly with specific effector programs over time, whereas cytotoxicity may display threshold behavior, saturation effects, or strong dependence on target cell encounters. The function Φ therefore serves as a flexible phenomenological bridge between intracellular molecular states and experimentally observable immune outputs.

Equation 4 should not be interpreted as the time integral of the differential equation governing G(t). Rather, it defines a downstream functional readout generated by the active gene expression state. The model distinguishes three hierarchically organized but mechanistically linked levels of T cell biologyE(t)→G(t)→Q(t)

This separation reflects the hierarchical organization of T cell responses. A cell may retain substantial latent effector capacity, E(t), yet display little immediate activity when checkpoint signaling suppresses realization of effector gene expression, resulting in low G(t). Conversely, elevated effector gene expression may not translate into effective function if execution remains constrained by environmental factors represented by Z(t). Thus, molecular reinvigoration and functional recovery are not necessarily equivalent. Consistent with this view, clinical studies have shown that down-regulation of BIM, a biomarker of PD-1 blockade, does not always correlate with durable functional benefit (47). Chronic PD-1 signaling can therefore maintain functional suppression despite the persistence of latent rebound potential (40, 48). Within this framework, the rapid recovery observed after PD-1 blockade is explained by the release of a preexisting effector program rather than by de novo differentiation (14, 16, 49). Following checkpoint blockade, effector gene expression may increase rapidly, yet therapeutic benefit can remain limited if antigen stimulation is weak or if suppressive tissue factors impede effector function. Conversely, T cells with similar transcriptional profiles may exhibit markedly different functional outputs because they reside in distinct microenvironments. By introducing Q(t) as a separate state variable, the model explicitly links molecular states to clinically relevant outcomes. In this sense, Q(t) represents the final functional endpoint through which latent capacity and active gene expression influence measurable therapeutic efficacy.

Mapping latent capacity to realized function

As a reduced approximation of the full active expression dynamics described by Eq. 3, we define the relationship between latent effector capacity and active effector expression asG(t)=E(t)·M(PD1(t))(5)where E(t) denotes latent effector capacity and M(PD1(t))∈[0,1] is a checkpoint-dependent realization factor. In this reduced form, gene-gene regulatory interactions and molecular turnover are absorbed into an effective coefficient, allowing the influence of checkpoint signaling on effector expression to be represented explicitly.

Equation 5 formalizes the distinction between effector potential and effector activity. Latent capacity provides the ability to express effector programs, whereas checkpoint signaling determines the extent to which that potential is realized. Consequently, effector expression depends on both factors and can be limited either by loss of latent capacity or by persistent inhibitory signaling.

This framework naturally explains a characteristic feature of exhausted T cells. Under chronic antigen stimulation, latent effector capacity may remain partially preserved while sustained PD-1 signaling suppresses its realizationE(t)>0, G(t)≈0

As a result, cells can retain epigenetic and transcriptional potential despite exhibiting minimal effector activity. Rather than representing irreversible loss of function, low effector gene expression may therefore reflect an actively maintained inhibited state.

Equation 5 also clarifies the response to checkpoint blockade. Because latent effector capacity evolves relatively slowly, its value at the time of treatment largely reflects prior cellular history. In contrast, blockade reduces effective PD-1 signaling and increases the realization factor M(PD1(t)), thereby allowing preexisting capacity to be expressed. Recovery of effector function thus emerges as an unmasking process rather than as de novo differentiation. Notably, Eq. 5 cautions against interpreting low effector gene expression as direct evidence of lost cellular potential. Similar expression profiles may arise from fundamentally different underlying states, with distinct implications for therapeutic responsiveness. By explicitly separating latent capacity from its realization, the model provides a mechanistic framework for understanding reversible dysfunction, heterogeneous responses to checkpoint blockade, and the persistence of latent functional potential in exhausted T cells.

PD-1 blockade responses and the rebound effect

A defining feature of at least a subset of exhausted T cells is their ability to regain effector function rapidly following PD-1 blockade despite exhibiting little activity immediately before treatment (10, 11, 25). Within the present framework, this rebound arises from the release of checkpoint-mediated inhibition acting on preexisting latent effector capacity rather than from the generation of new effector programs.

We model PD-1 blockade as a reduction in effective downstream PD-1 signaling. Let PD1(t) denote the aggregate inhibitory signal experienced by a T cell, integrating receptor occupancy, receptor-ligand interactions, and intracellular signaling competence. Checkpoint blockade reduces this effective signal even when PD-L1 remains present within the tissue microenvironment.

Equation 5 therefore reduces tolimPD1(t)→0E(t)M(PD1(t))=E(t)(6)indicating that active effector gene expression directly reflects the latent effector capacity already present in the cell.

Equation 6 provides a simple mathematical description of functional rebound. Before treatment, strong inhibitory signaling can maintain low effector expression despite preserved latent capacity. Following blockade, removal of the inhibitory constraint allows this stored capacity to be rapidly expressed. Because the underlying regulatory and epigenetic programs are already established, recovery is governed primarily by transcriptional and translational processes rather than by slow chromatin remodeling or differentiation. Notably, the framework describes a cell-intrinsic mechanism of response and is not intended to capture all effects of checkpoint therapy. In vivo outcomes may additionally depend on factors such as expansion of progenitor-like exhausted T cells, clonal replacement, alterations in antigen burden, changes in antigen-presenting cells, and remodeling of the tissue microenvironment. These processes are beyond the scope of the current model.

A key implication of Eq. 6 is that the magnitude of rebound depends on the latent effector capacity present at the time of treatment, which provides a theoretical mathematical definition of functional rebound or resilience (13, 15, 18, 19). Cells retaining substantial capacity can exhibit strong functional recovery following blockade, whereas cells that have undergone extensive capacity loss show limited responsiveness even when inhibitory signaling is removed. Thus, PD-1 blockade can reveal existing effector potential but cannot restore capacity that has already been lost (13, 19). In this framework, functional rebound reflects the interaction between checkpoint blockade and the preexisting state of the T cell. The effectiveness of therapy therefore depends not only on the degree of inhibitory signaling but also on the amount of latent effector capacity available for reactivation. This interpretation provides a mechanistic explanation for the heterogeneous responses to PD-1 blockade observed among exhausted T cell populations.

The point of no return: Loss of epigenetic plasticity

While earlier sections describe exhaustion as a reversible state arising from inhibitory masking of preserved latent effector capacity, prolonged chronic antigen stimulation and checkpoint signals ultimately lead to a qualitatively different regime in which the latent epigenetic capacity itself deteriorates (6, 23, 28). Experimentally, this terminally exhausted state is characterized by stable loss of chromatin accessibility at effector loci (epigenetic scar) (6), reduced transcription factor binding, and failure to respond to checkpoint blockade (28). To capture this transition within our framework, we extend the latent capacity dynamics to explicitly model self-maintenance of epigenetic programs and their checkpoint-dependent destabilization.

1) Stable low-capacity equilibrium (EL∗): This state represents terminal T cell exhaustion, where latent functional capacity is minimal and self-renewal is insufficient to overcome checkpoint-mediated decline. Small perturbations away from this state decay back toward EL∗, making it a robust exhausted attractor. Biologically, T cells in this regime are predicted to have limited functional potential and reduced responsiveness to therapeutic (anti–PD-1) rescue.

2) Unstable intermediate equilibrium (EM∗): This equilibrium serves as a threshold or separatrix between recovery and exhaustion trajectories. Small increases in latent capacity above EM∗ drive the system toward the high-capacity state, whereas small decreases below EM∗ lead toward terminal exhaustion. Biologically, this threshold defines the critical transition point between reversible dysfunction and progressive commitment to exhaustion.

3) Stable high-capacity equilibrium (EH∗): This state corresponds to a preexhaustion-like or stem-like T cells that retain substantial latent capacity, self-renewal potential, and responsiveness to stimulation. Because perturbations return to EH∗, this equilibrium acts as a resilient functional attractor that can support sustained immune responses and remains amenable to therapeutic reinvigoration.

Thus, bistability implies that CD8+ T cells can occupy either a high-capacity functional state or a low-capacity exhausted state under similar antigenic conditions. The unstable intermediate equilibrium acts as a fate boundary separating these attractors. Consequently, cell fate depends not only on current antigen stimulation but also on the prior history of stimulation and checkpoint signaling, providing a mechanistic basis for the persistence of T cell exhaustion and the existence of long-lived stem-like T cell populations.

Before this threshold is crossed, PD-1 blockade primarily acts by reducing the decay of effector capacity through lowering δ(PD1), thereby enabling the system to recover toward the high-capacity attractor. This framework formalizes irreversible T cell exhaustion as a qualitative transition in the underlying dynamical state of the system rather than a simple quantitative decline in effector function. An important implication of this analysis is that PD-1 blockade can reveal, but cannot recreate, latent effector capacity. Consequently, therapeutic failure may not necessarily reflect inadequate drug activity; instead, it may indicate that the exhausted T cell population no longer retains sufficient latent capacity to support functional reinvigoration. Notably, since the present study is intended as a theoretical framework, we do not perform parameter estimation or numerical bifurcation analyses. Rather, the model provides a conceptual mechanistic basis for understanding therapeutic timing, emphasizing that checkpoint blockade is most effective when administered before epigenetic plasticity has crossed the point of no return.

Integral criterion for irreversibility

While the preceding sections define exhaustion as a dynamical competition between preserved latent effector capacity and checkpoint-mediated masking, an important unresolved question is when reversibility is lost altogether. Empirically, exhausted T cells exhibit a continuum of responses to PD-1 blockade, ranging from rapid functional rebound to complete refractoriness. These observations suggest that irreversibility is not determined by instantaneous signaling strength alone but, instead, reflects the history of inhibitory exposure experienced by the cell.

To formalize this concept at a coarse-grained level, we introduce Eq. 10 as a phenomenological history criterion rather than an independent governing equation. This formulation reflects a central biological principle: Epigenetic deterioration arises primarily from sustained signaling history rather than transient signaling intensity, which has been experimentally observed in T cell exhaustion (5, 24). Short-lived or intermittent PD-1 engagement may transiently suppress effector gene expression while leaving the underlying chromatin architecture largely intact. In contrast, prolonged checkpoint signaling can progressively drive repressive chromatin remodeling, loss of enhancer accessibility, and erosion of transcription factor occupancy (6, 7). Because these processes accumulate over time, their effects are inherently history dependent and are not adequately represented by instantaneous threshold models. The bifurcation framework defines irreversibility through the disappearance of the high-capacity steady state, whereas Eq. 10 proposes that the time-integrated inhibitory signal may serve as an experimentally measurable surrogate for progression toward that transition. Thus, the two descriptions are mechanistically consistent but not mathematically equivalent within the present model. Future extensions incorporating the full time–dependent dynamics of latent capacity may permit a direct derivation of Θepi from the underlying equations.

DISCUSSION

The latent effector capacity framework suggests that response to PD-1 blockade depends not only on observed transcriptional states but also on unobserved, history-dependent variables, including latent effector capacity, checkpoint-mediated masking, and cumulative inhibitory exposure. Although these quantities are not directly measurable, they may be inferred from multimodal molecular data. This perspective motivates the development of mechanistically constrained artificial intelligence (AI) models that explicitly represent biological state variables rather than relying solely on black-box prediction of clinical outcomes. One possible future implementation is a neural-state space or neural ordinary differential equation framework that infers latent biological states from molecular measurements and evolves them according to mechanistically informed dynamics. These models would ideally be trained and validated using longitudinal multiomic datasets and ex vivo perturbation experiments involving PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, with functional readouts including cytokine production, proliferation, and cytotoxicity.

Mechanistic design principles

The model developed here suggests several principles that future predictive frameworks could test: (i) Latent effector capacity and active effector output should be represented as distinct state variables. Latent capacity evolves slowly through epigenetic processes, whereas active expression responds rapidly to inhibitory signaling. (ii) PD-1 signaling should be modeled as a reversible regulator of effector realization rather than an immediate driver of epigenetic erasure. (iii) Irreversibility should emerge from cumulative inhibitory exposure, reflecting the gradual loss of epigenetic self-maintenance rather than instantaneous checkpoint activity. These principles imply that therapeutic response is fundamentally a dynamical process with execution of preexisting effector capacity. Consequently, an appropriate AI architecture may be a latent state-space model that (i) infers hidden biological states, (ii) evolves them according to mechanistic constraints, and (iii) predicts T cell responses to therapeutic perturbations.

Inferring latent effector capacity from multiomic data

Within this framework, latent effector capacity constitutes a central state variable describing the preserved regulatory and epigenetic infrastructure of effector programs. Future models could estimate this quantity from chromatin accessibility and transcriptional datasets, including bulk or single-cell RNA sequencing and ATAC-seq, by learning representations of enhancer accessibility, transcription factor activity, and regulatory network organization. Notably, inferred latent effector capacity would represent the potential for effector realization rather than current transcriptional activity. Mapping high-dimensional molecular measurements onto a small number of biologically meaningful state variables would also enable direct testing of theoretical predictions arising from the model, including bistability, self-maintaining effector states, and eventual capacity collapse.

Modeling checkpoint masking and immune history

Transcriptional measurements can be used to estimate realized effector activity, while checkpoint-mediated masking can be represented as a graded inhibitory process linking latent capacity to observed gene expression. Explicitly modeling this relationship may help distinguish low effector output caused by active suppression from low output caused by true loss of effector potential—two biologically distinct states with different therapeutic implications. A particular feature of the present framework is the incorporation of immune history. Because irreversibility depends on cumulative inhibitory exposure, future AI models should maintain an internal state representing the long-term burden of checkpoint signaling. These history-aware models could estimate clinically relevant quantities including rebound potential, durability of response, and probability of irreversible exhaustion, extending beyond simple responder versus nonresponder classifications.

Toward interpretable digital immune models

Together, these considerations motivate a new class of interpretable AI models for immunotherapy. Rather than predicting outcomes directly from static molecular snapshots, these models would infer latent biological states, evolve those states through mechanistically informed dynamics, and simulate responses to interventions such as PD-1 blockade. Compared with purely data-driven approaches, this strategy offers greater biological interpretability, causal consistency, and the ability to perform counterfactual analyses, including predictions of alternative treatment timing or combination therapies aimed at preserving effector capacity. In the long term, validated state space models of this type may enable quantitative representations of T cell trajectories that integrate molecular state, signaling history, and epigenetic memory. Within this framework, latent effector capacity serves not only as a conceptual construct but also as a practical organizing variable for predictive and quantitative immunology.

Limitations and scope

The present framework provides a theoretical foundation for understanding reversible and irreversible T cell exhaustion, but several limitations should be acknowledged. These limitations primarily reflect deliberate modeling choices made to preserve interpretability, analytical tractability, and conceptual clarity.

Model abstraction and molecular resolution

The model adopts a coarse-grained representation of complex biological processes by summarizing chromatin remodeling, transcription factor networks, and signaling pathways into a small number of effective state variables. In particular, latent effector capacity is represented as a scalar or low-dimensional vector, whereas epigenetic accessibility and regulatory potential are distributed across thousands of genomic loci and multiple interacting regulatory modules (5, 6, 23, 30). This abstraction sacrifices molecular detail in favor of a unified dynamical framework capable of explaining reversible dysfunction, preserved epigenetic potential, and heterogeneous responses to PD-1 blockade. Experimental studies have shown that cytotoxicity, cytokine production, and proliferative capacity can become partially uncoupled during exhaustion (8, 22, 37). Consequently, latent effector capacity should be viewed as an aggregate variable representing the overall readiness of effector programs rather than any specific molecular pathway.

Reduced representation of checkpoint networks

Our model focuses on PD-1–mediated inhibition as the principal example of reversible masking of functional competence (28, 39, 45). Although PD-1 provides a well-characterized biological and clinical context, exhausted T cells are subject to regulation by multiple inhibitory receptors, including TIM-3, LAG-3, TIGIT, and suppressive microenvironmental signals (1, 26, 32, 50). These pathways may differ not only in their ability to suppress effector function but also in their capacity to influence the preservation or erosion of latent effector capacity. The current model does not explicitly represent these pathway-specific effects and therefore provides a reduced description of checkpoint regulation, conceptually as a composite masking effect rather than discrete binary switches based on experimental studies (36, 44). Future work could extend the framework to incorporate interacting inhibitory pathways and evaluate how distinct checkpoint combinations shape reversibility, therapeutic response, and the transition to irreversible exhaustion.

Cell-intrinsic focus and population-level effects

The framework is explicitly cell intrinsic and does not incorporate population-level processes such as clonal selection, cell-cell interaction/regulation, step-wise differentiation, or competitive expansion. In vivo responses to PD-1 blockade frequently involve selective expansion of progenitor-like exhausted T cell subsets with preserved epigenetic capacity, rather than direct reinvigoration of terminally exhausted cells (9, 23, 33). By focusing on single-cell dynamical states, the present model does not attempt to disentangle reinvigoration from clonal replacement (9, 51, 52). This limitation is intentional: The goal is to define the intrinsic cellular conditions required for reversibility, independent of downstream population dynamics. Coupling the present framework to population-level (spatial biology) or evolutionary models (cell development) represents an important future direction and will be necessary for linking single-cell trajectories to bulk clinical outcomes (9, 14, 16).

Parameter identifiability and empirical calibration

While the theoretical mathematical formulation is mechanistically motivated and consistent with extensive transcriptional and epigenetic datasets, several parameters, particularly those governing epigenetic self-maintenance, checkpoint-dependent decay, and the chromatin locking threshold, are not directly measurable with current experimental tools. Experimental studies demonstrate durable epigenetic “scars” following chronic antigen exposure (6, 28), but quantitative thresholds governing the loss of reversibility remain poorly defined. Thus, our model should not be interpreted as an already calibrated simulator or clinically validated predictor of PD-1 blockade response. Its current contribution is structural and hypothesis generating: It identifies candidate latent variables, dynamical regimes, and testable relationships that may guide future data collection and model calibration. Quantitative prediction will require longitudinal perturbation-resolved datasets combining chromatin accessibility, transcriptional output, checkpoint signaling, antigen exposure, and functional response measurements. These data would enable estimation of parameters governing epigenetic self-maintenance, checkpoint-dependent masking, capacity decay, and the chromatin-locking threshold, followed by numerical simulation, sensitivity analysis, and independent validation. Until these steps are completed, the AI-oriented claims should be viewed as proposed future applications rather than demonstrated predictive performance.

Scope of generalization and perspective

Although this framework was developed to describe CD8+ T cell exhaustion during chronic antigen exposure and PD-1 blockade, the underlying distinction between latent competence and realized function may extend to other immune states, including anergy, tolerance, memory differentiation, and immune resilience. Whether the same dynamical principles apply in these contexts remains an open question and will require experimental validation, potentially involving additional state variables or alternative regulatory mechanisms. These limitations define the intended scope of the model rather than diminish its utility. The framework identifies latent effector capacity as a central organizing variable and formulates exhaustion as a history-dependent dynamical process governed by a mathematically defined point of no return, consistent with current transcriptional and epigenetic observations (5, 6, 28). From this perspective, the AI-based approaches outlined above represent a natural extension of the framework. By combining mechanistic dynamical models with multimodal molecular data, these approaches could enable scalable inference of latent biological states, empirical testing of model predictions, and ultimately more accurate prediction of therapeutic responses while preserving biological interpretability.
