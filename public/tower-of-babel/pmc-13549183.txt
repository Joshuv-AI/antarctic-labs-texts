Investigating the Relationship Between Moral Resilience and Turnover Intention in Nurses
Authors: Vahid Barari, Fatemeh Ebrahimi Belil, Mehdi Ajri-khameslou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to investigate the relationship between moral resilience and turnover intention among nurses.Patients and MethodsThis cross-sectional descriptive-analytical study was conducted from December 2024 to September 2025 among nurses working in educational and treatment hospitals affiliated with Ardabil University of Medical Sciences. A total of 330 nurses were selected using simple random sampling. Data were collected using a demographic characteristics questionnaire, the 16-item Rushton Moral Resilience Scale, and the 15-item Kim et al. Turnover Intention Questionnaire. Pearson’s correlation coefficient and multiple linear regression analysis were used to examine the relationships between variables and identify predictors of turnover intention. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 14.ResultsThe mean age of the nurses was 32.01 ± 4.81 years, and their mean work experience was 6.45 ± 4.46 years. Most participants were married (60.6%) and held a bachelor’s degree (90.0%). The mean moral resilience score was 40.78 ± 1.29, indicating a moderate level, while the mean turnover intention score was 16.65 ± 2.77, indicating a high level. A significant negative correlation was found between moral resilience and turnover intention (r = −0.72, P < 0.001). Regression analysis showed that age (B = −0.238, P = 0.047), workplace department (B = 0.145, P = 0.042), job satisfaction (B = −1.139, P < 0.001), average number of patients per shift (B = −0.422, P = 0.042), errors leading to patient death in the past year (B = 1.672, P = 0.010), and moral resilience (B = −1.015, P < 0.001) were significant predictors of reduced turnover intention.ConclusionEnhancing moral resilience can effectively reduce turnover intention. These findings suggest that strengthening moral resilience may be relevant to reducing nurses’ turnover intention. Further longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to determine whether interventions aimed at enhancing moral resilience can reduce turnover intention.Keywords: moral resilience, turnover intention, nurses, ethical challenges

Introduction

Nurses, as a cornerstone of healthcare teams, play a pivotal role in delivering health and care services. Beyond providing direct patient care, their continuous interaction with patients and their families exposes them to complex ethical challenges and conflicts. These conflicts, often arising from tensions between ethical values, professional duties, and patients’ interests, can profoundly impact the quality of care, nurses’ mental health, and job satisfaction.1,2

Ethical conflicts in nursing commonly occur in situations such as decision-making regarding the continuation or cessation of treatment for critically ill patients, balancing patient confidentiality with the need for disclosure to protect society, and respecting patient autonomy when their decisions may harm their health.3,4 Nurses may also face ethical dilemmas when dealing with end-of-life patients, torn between alleviating suffering and prolonging life through medical interventions.5 A study by Larsman et al (2024) revealed that 99% of nurses have encountered ethical conflicts at least once, with 37% experiencing these situations repeatedly.6 If not managed appropriately, these challenges can lead to burnout, diminished public trust, and a decline in the quality of healthcare services.7,8 Several factors, including high workload, resource shortages, and lack of organizational support, exacerbate the intensity of these conflicts. Heavy workloads, driven by a high patient-to-nurse ratio and staffing shortages, reduce the time available for thorough ethical deliberation, thereby increasing nurses’ stress levels.9 Resource scarcity, such as outdated equipment or insufficient medical facilities, forces nurses to prioritize certain patients’ needs over others, which can generate ethical conflicts and feelings of guilt.10 Furthermore, the absence of organizational support and clear guidelines leaves nurses isolated in navigating complex ethical decisions, contributing to moral distress, reduced self-confidence, and an increased intention to leave the profession.11 Studies have shown that 38% of nurses express an intention to leave their current job, while 28% plan to exit the nursing profession entirely.12 In Iran, this figure reaches 49.6%, and in Ardabil, it stands at 32.7%.13,14 Such turnover intentions not only contribute to staffing shortages and increased pressure on remaining nurses but also disrupt the quality of care and team morale.15,16 The shortage of nursing staff, resulting from turnover, adds to the workload of remaining nurses, escalating their stress and burnout levels. This situation can trigger further issues and higher turnover rates, creating a vicious cycle.15 Moreover, disruptions in the healthcare system led to reduced quality of care, longer patient wait times, and an increased likelihood of medical errors. Additionally, nurse turnover can negatively impact team morale. The reduced workforce and heightened workload on remaining staff can diminish team cohesion and morale, ultimately leading to decreased motivation, reduced team efficiency, and lower quality of patient care.15,16

In this context, moral resilience, defined as nurses’ ability to cope with ethical conflicts and maintain psychological and professional balance, plays a critical role. Moral resilience encompasses a deep understanding of ethical principles, effective management of psychological stress, and the ability to navigate conflicts, enabling nurses to make principled decisions in challenging situations and uphold care quality.17,18 This capacity is influenced by factors such as individual characteristics, social support from colleagues and managers, and environmental conditions like a positive organizational culture and access to resources.19,20 Nurses with high moral resilience are better equipped to handle job-related challenges and stress, experience less burnout, and demonstrate greater job satisfaction and improved quality of care.19,20 Conversely, a lack of moral resilience can lead to moral distress, depression, and job dissatisfaction.20,21

Nurses, as the backbone of the healthcare system, face increasingly complex ethical challenges that can significantly impact their motivation and the quality of their services. While many existing studies have focused on the general aspects of occupational stress and burnout among nurses, the specific and in-depth relationship between moral resilience and turnover intention has received less attention. Therefore, this study was conducted to investigate the relationship between moral resilience and turnover intention among nurses.

Materials and Methods

This study is a cross-sectional, descriptive-analytical research conducted from December 2024 to September 2025 to investigate the relationship between moral resilience and turnover intention among nurses working in educational and therapeutic hospitals affiliated with Ardabil University of Medical Sciences. The study population consisted of all nurses employed in educational and therapeutic hospitals affiliated with Ardabil University of Medical Sciences who were actively working at the time of the study.

The sample size was calculated using the Cochran formula, considering a prevalence of turnover intention among nurses ranging from 38% to 61% based on previous studies.12,22 With the values P = 0.5, q = 0.5, Z = 1.96, and d = 0.05, a sample size of approximately 300 nurses was determined. Accounting for a 10% potential dropout rate, the final sample size was estimated at 330 nurses. A simple random sampling method was used to select the participants. The inclusion criteria for the study included having a nursing degree, having no self-reported history of mental illness, having no use of psychotropic medications, having no experience of bereavement during the previous six months, having no history of divorce, a minimum of six months of work experience, active involvement in direct patient care, and willingness to participate voluntarily in the study. The exclusion criterion was incomplete completion of the questionnaires by participants.

Data were collected using three questionnaires: a demographic characteristics questionnaire, the 16-item Rushton Moral Resilience Scale (RMRS-16), and a turnover intention questionnaire. The demographic characteristics questionnaire included information such as gender, age, workplace department, work experience, marital status, education level, income, shift type, level of patient interaction, job satisfaction, employment type, and overtime hours, which was completed by the participants.

The RMRS-16 consists of 16 items and four subscales: “Response to Moral Adversity,” “Personal Integrity,” “Relational Integrity,” and “Moral Efficacy.” Responses are scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly Disagree” (1) to “Strongly Agree” (4). Items 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 14, and 16 are reverse-scored. Subscale and total scores are calculated as averages, ranging from 16 to 64, with higher scores indicating greater moral resilience. This tool was psychometrically validated in a study by Rushton et al (2024), with a Cronbach’s alpha of ≥0.70 reported for all subscales and the total scale.23 In the present study, after cross-cultural adaptation using the translation and back-translation method, content validity was confirmed by 10 experts (CVR ranging from 0.62 to 1, CVI = 0.92), and reliability was established with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.74 to 0.81 for subscales and 0.85 for the total scale.

In this study, Turnover intention was assessed using the 15-item Turnover Intention Questionnaire developed by Kim et al (2013). The questionnaire consists of 15 items across three dimensions: individual, social, and environmental. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly Disagree” (1) to “Strongly Agree” (5). The total score, calculated by summing the scores of all items, ranges from 15 to 75, with higher scores indicating a greater intention to leave the job. The validity of this tool was confirmed in the study by Kim et al, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89.24 For use in the Iranian context, the Persian version of the questionnaire validated by Haji et al was used. In their study, the questionnaire underwent cultural adaptation and validation by Iranian experts and academics, and its reliability was established with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.76. Therefore, the validated Persian version was used in the present study.25

After obtaining approval and an introductory letter from the Research Deputy of the Nursing Faculty, the researcher visited the nursing units of educational and therapeutic centers in Ardabil with permission from the relevant authorities. During these visits, the researcher introduced themselves, explained the study’s objectives, and assured participants that providing their full name on the questionnaires was not required, participation was entirely voluntary, and it would not impact their professional evaluations. Sampling was conducted randomly, and questionnaires were distributed among nurses across morning, evening, and night shifts on working days to ensure accessibility for nurses with varying schedules.

The collected data were entered into SPSS version 14. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation (SD), were used to summarize the demographic characteristics and the scores of moral resilience and turnover intention. The normality of the continuous variables was assessed before inferential analyses. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to examine the relationships between moral resilience, its dimensions, and turnover intention, as well as between the main continuous variables and demographic/occupational characteristics where appropriate. Independent-samples t tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare mean moral resilience and turnover intention scores across categorical demographic and occupational groups, as appropriate. Multiple linear regression analysis using the enter method was performed to identify independent predictors of turnover intention. Turnover intention was entered as the dependent variable, while age, work experience, gender, hospital department, marital status, education level, shift type, employment status, job satisfaction, average number of patients per shift, occurrence of a fatal error in the previous year, and moral resilience were entered as independent variables. The regression model was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2), adjusted R2, F statistic, regression coefficients (B), standardized coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals, and P values. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

The mean age of the nurses was 32.01 ± 4.81 years, and their mean work experience was 6.45 ± 4.46 years (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the relationship between moral resilience and turnover intention among nurses. The findings revealed a significant inverse relationship between moral resilience and turnover intention among employed nurses. This result is consistent with previous studies, such as Yi et al (2024), who reported that nurses with higher moral resilience exhibit lower turnover intention due to their enhanced ability to manage ethical challenges.26 Furthermore, Kovanci et al (2024) explored the mediating role of moral distress in the relationship between moral resilience and turnover intention, demonstrating that moral resilience is associated with reduced turnover intention.27 Moral resilience enables nurses to maintain personal and professional integrity when confronting ethical dilemmas, such as complex patient care decisions or pressures arising from resource shortages.15,16

However, the findings of Damshenas et al (2020), which examined the relationship between moral distress and turnover intention in nurses, reported no significant association between these variables, even when moderate levels of moral distress were observed.28 This discrepancy with the current study’s findings may be attributed to differences in the variables examined, measurement tools, or the socioeconomic and cultural contexts of the study settings. Additionally, unfavorable economic conditions and limited job opportunities may compel nurses to remain in their roles despite experiencing moral distress, thereby reducing turnover intention. In the present study, the subscales of moral efficacy and personal integrity exhibited the strongest inverse relationships with turnover intention, highlighting the pivotal role of these dimensions in mitigating psychological stress and sustaining professional commitment.

The mean score for moral resilience was assessed at a moderate level, with the highest score observed in the subscale of relational integrity. Consistent with these findings, Kovanci et al (2024) investigated the mediating effect of moral distress on the relationship between moral resilience and turnover intention, reporting that the mean total score for nurses’ moral resilience was also at a moderate level.27 Furthermore, this finding aligns with Rushton et al (2024), who demonstrated that relational integrity, defined as the ability to establish professional relationships grounded in trust and respect, plays a critical role in enhancing moral resilience.29 However, the lowest score was observed in the subscale of personal integrity, which may be associated with environmental pressures such as extended rotating shifts (96.1%) and low job satisfaction (67.3%). These challenging work conditions may undermine nurses’ ability to uphold personal values, potentially increasing turnover intention. In contrast to the present study’s findings, Solaimani Moghaddam et al (2024) reported that nurses exhibited a higher level of moral resilience.30 This discrepancy may stem from differences in demographic variables, types of healthcare facilities, or prevailing organizational cultures. Also Given the significant inverse association between moral resilience and turnover intention, strengthening moral resilience may be a promising strategy. Continuing education, reflective discussions of ethical challenges, peer support, and organizational support could be considered, although their effectiveness should be evaluated in future intervention studies.

Job satisfaction was identified as a strong predictor of turnover intention and a variable associated with moral resilience. This finding aligns with O’Callaghan and Sadath (2025), who reported that low job satisfaction, particularly related to work schedules and colleague relationships, is associated with increased turnover intention among nurses.31 In the present study, only 32.7% of nurses reported moderate to high job satisfaction, highlighting an urgent need to improve working conditions. Enhancing job satisfaction through policies such as reducing workload, improving reward systems, and fostering supportive work environments could indirectly strengthen moral resilience and reduce turnover intention. In contrast, a study conducted in Indonesia found no direct relationship between job satisfaction and turnover intention. However, burnout served as a mediating factor, whereby decreased job satisfaction was associated with increased burnout, which in turn heightened turnover intention.32 These divergent findings may be attributed to differences in organizational culture, management systems, or the lack of examination of mediating variables in various studies.

Regression analysis revealed that factors such as age, workplace department, number of patients per shift, and experience of errors leading to patient mortality significantly influenced turnover intention. Notably, older nurses exhibited lower turnover intention, consistent with findings from a study indicating that more experienced individuals are less likely to consider leaving their jobs due to professional commitment or financial dependencies.33 Additionally, employment in high-stress departments, such as emergency units, was associated with increased turnover intention, likely due to the intense workload and psychological burden in these settings. This finding aligns with research identifying workload as a key contributor to burnout and turnover.34 Furthermore, the results indicated that nurses who experienced errors resulting in patient mortality demonstrated a higher turnover intention. This may stem from feelings of guilt, psychological stress, or inadequate organizational support following such incidents, as noted in Pappa et al (2023).35 These findings highlight the critical need for improved working conditions and enhanced organizational support to reduce turnover intention among nurses.

Limitations

This study may be subject to response bias due to the use of self-reported questionnaires. Additionally, its focus on teaching hospitals in Ardabil limits the generalizability of the findings to other regions or non-teaching hospitals. Other factors, such as management style or organizational support, which may influence turnover intention, were not controlled for in this study.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate a significant inverse relationship between moral resilience and turnover intention among nurses, whereby higher moral resilience is associated with reduced turnover intention. Additionally, variables such as job satisfaction, experiences of clinical errors, workplace environmental conditions, and demographic factors contribute to shaping turnover intention. Overall, these results suggest that enhancing moral resilience and improving job satisfaction can effectively reduce nurses’ turnover intention, thereby contributing to the retention of skilled healthcare professionals within the healthcare system. However, the effectiveness of such strategies should be evaluated in future intervention studies.
