Examining vulnerability of planar networks from a large-scale region segmentation perspective
Authors: Gang Liu, Chengfu Huang, Ye Tian, Jing He, Zhiyong Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEvaluating the vulnerability of planar networks, such as road systems, is crucial for optimizing network topology and enhancing network defensive capabilities. In this study, we introduce the concept of “large-scale region segmentation” and propose a novel approach for assessing the vulnerability of planar networks. To validate our method, we employ the network efficiency metric to quantify network performance under various attack scenarios, with taking random failures and betweenness-based targeted attacks for comparison. Experiments are conducted on both real-world urban road networks (including Atlanta, Chengdu, Florence, and Paris) and randomly generated planar networks. Results across all tested datasets indicate that the proposed method outperforms both random and betweenness-based attack strategies in rapidly degrading network efficiency. The main contribution of this work lies in considering the overall connectivity of planar networks, rather than focusing solely on individual nodes or edges, thereby introducing a vulnerability analysis approach from a large-scale region segmentation perspective. This approach significantly advances the assessment of planar network vulnerability. Our findings offer practical implications for the planning and emergency management of planar networks, such as transportation systems, and provide a foundational basis for improving network security and efficiency.Keywords: Planar networks, Vulnerability, Large-scale region segmentation, Network efficiencySubject terms: Computer science; Statistical physics, thermodynamics and nonlinear dynamics

Introduction

The structural and functional characteristics of infrastructure systems have been extensively investigated1,2. Vulnerability analysis of planar infrastructure systems, such as urban transportation networks, is a significant research in multiple disciplines, including geographic information science (GIS), complex networks, and transportation infrastructure, etc3–9. Urban street networks serve not only as critical backbones for socioeconomic activities but also as reflections of urban spatial structure. Given the essential role of transportation performance in sustaining urban ecosystems, the vulnerability of street networks is closely linked to urban safety and sustainable development10. Both random attacks (such as natural disasters, traffic accidents, and congestion) and targeted attacks (e.g. attacking key edges or nodes) can affect network connectivity and performance to varying degrees. Thus, systematic vulnerability assessment of real-world planar networks is a challenging research topic, particularly in GIS, transportation science, and complex network research11–14.

In recent years, scholars have conducted extensive discussions on the vulnerability of various complex systems15,16. Most urban street networks, when defined based on road-road relationships, exhibit scale-free properties. It has been proved that such scale-free networks display a “robust yet fragile” nature (e.g. highly resilient under random failures yet extremely vulnerable to targeted attacks)17. Research on network robustness and resilience often explores phase transitions influenced by factors such as degree distribution, spatial embedding, interdependencies, and coupled dynamics18. Indeed, a scale-free network may preserve global connectivity even after random removal of numerous nodes, yet targeted disruption of a small set of critical nodes can trigger systemic collapse19,20. Jenelius and Mattsson11 emphasized that vulnerability analysis of street networks should account for the societal impacts of disruptions, which manifest through both operational and structural dimensions. This allows the overall network vulnerability to be assessed from integrated topological and functional perspectives21. Shang22,23 developed theoretical frameworks for analyzing robustness in networks with general or uncorrelated degree distributions, providing important supports for the design of resilient networked systems. Huang et al.24 advanced the evaluation of network invulnerability by incorporating cascading failures and demand-side factors into attack simulations, offering critical methodological support for urban network vulnerability analysis. From a community structure perspective, module-based attack strategies have shown considerable promise in network vulnerability studies25–30. However, such approaches may be not suitable for planar networks like road network structured based on junction-junction relationships, which often lack pronounced community organization.

Previous studies have examined the vulnerability of road networks from connectivity perspectives, such as the relative size of the largest connected component and network efficiency under attacks based on node degree or betweenness. Among these metrics, connectivity and betweenness centrality (BC) have proven effective in characterizing the importance of nodes or edges. Besides, studies have indicated that betweenness-based attacks, whether targeting nodes or edges, lead to faster degradation of network performance and yield more disruptive effects compared to other strategies. Consequently, BC has been widely adopted in road network vulnerability analysis13. Based on BC, Casali and Heinimann10 further incorporated Seepage Theory to model the progressive damage process in urban road networks after attacks, thereby elucidating how major disruptions influence overall network performance.

Scholars have also developed attack strategies based on both initial and dynamically updated degree or betweenness centralities12,20,21. These studies revealed that recalculating centrality measures during the attack process results in more effective disruption compared to static initial values. In terms of vulnerability quantification, average path length and network efficiency are commonly used to assess performance degradation under attacks. Among them, network efficiency offers a distinct advantage in road network vulnerability analysis and has thus been extensively applied20,31.

Compared to random failures, targeted attacks can more clearly reveal the impact of critical road disruptions on global network performance. The novelty of existing approaches mainly lies in developing deliberate attack strategies by quantifying the importance of nodes or edges. These methods typically remove individual network elements sequentially. Since network components vary in importance, they induce differing vulnerability effects. From a systemic and holistic perspective, it is therefore valuable to investigate how to rapidly disrupt network connectivity and significantly degrade network efficiency through large-scale attacks, rather than via single-node or single-edge removals. In planar networks, high-betweenness nodes and edges tend to cluster near the geometric center. Consequently, even if certain critical edges or nodes are disrupted, the network may still maintain global connectivity. This prompts the need for novel attack strategies that can rapidly disrupt the overall connectivity of planar networks-a challenging task that demands the simultaneous consideration of both topological and spatial constraints.

As a planar network structure, urban road networks differ from highly abstract dual networks without geographic information. Attacks on a road network cause geographically-embedded damage and break its spatial connectivity. Such networks possess clear regional spatial structural significance, and attacks may change the two-dimensional spatial coverage. Severe disruptions can fragment the road network into isolated regions, thereby breaking overall connectivity. However, dividing the road network into multiple regions does not necessarily imply significant functional damage. If most nodes remain connected, network efficiency loss may be minimal even. Therefore, segmentation alone does not guarantee effective disruption. With this in mind, we introduce the concept of “large-scale region segmentation” and argue that an effective deliberate attack strategy should induce significant network efficiency loss through large-scale segmentation (e.g. macroscopic spatial fragmentation).

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. “Related works” section introduces topological representations of planar networks, the network efficiency metric, and traditional attack strategies. “Vulnerability analysis approach based on large-scale region segmentation” section presents the principle of large-scale region segmentation and the corresponding vulnerability analysis approach. “Results” section describes experiments on real urban street networks. “Discussions” section draws discussions, and “Conclusions” section concludes the paper.

Related works

Topological representation of planar networks

Every planar spatial network is defined by fundamental topological relationships. Taking a fictional road network as an example, Fig. 1 shows the principles of two topological models. In the dual graph model based on road-road relationships (Fig. 1b), each road is abstracted as a node, and an edge is related to two nodes if their corresponding roads intersect32,33. In the junction-junction model (Fig. 1d), nodes correspond to street intersections, and edges represent road segments with weighted by their physical length.

Topological representations of a planar spatial network (taking an urban road network as an example). (a) Axial map (8 roads), where R1, R2,…, R8 are the names or codes for each road; (b) road-road relationship (8 nodes and 14 edges); (c) geometric road network; and (d) junction–junction relationship.

The dual graph model facilitates the analysis of connectivity among roads and helps assess the overall connectivity of the network. In contrast, the junction-junction model enables accurate computation of centrality metrics for individual intersections and segments. In a road-road representation, key roads often exhibit high degree or betweenness centrality. However, this representation is not intrinsic to the network: road names or IDs are assigned artificial attributes, and their interpretation may vary. By contrast, the junction-junction structure provides a natural and unambiguous topological representation of a street network34. It is geometrically determined and independent of any anthropogenic labeling. Therefore, the junction-junction relationship offers a more robust basis for evaluating the vulnerability of urban road systems.

Network efficiency

Network vulnerability refers to the ability of a network to maintain performance under various attack strategies, typically quantified as the degradation in overall network performance following the removal of certain nodes or edges. Theoretically, as an increasing number of nodes or edges are removed from a road network, its global performance is expected to decline continuously. Therefore, to assess the vulnerability of a road network, it is essential to measure the function or performance of the network reasonably. In conventional approaches, the average path length (which is defined as , where denotes the shortest path distance between nodes and , i.e. the sum of length of edges along the shortest path between two nodes measured by Euclidean distances; and is the total number of nodes) has been widely used as a performance metric for road networks. When certain nodes or edges are attacked, tends to increase, and this increase has been used to gauge network vulnerability. However, empirical studies have revealed a major drawback of this metric: as damage intensifies, exhibits an initial rise followed by a decline, primarily due to the sharp reduction in network scale during later attack stages. Consequently, the average path length is unsuitable for vulnerability analysis from a global perspective.

In contrast, network efficiency has been demonstrated to offer significant advantages in characterizing the vulnerability of road networks. This metric decreases steadily and continuously as attacks proceed. Thus, in this study, we employ network efficiency to evaluate road network vulnerability. Attack strategies

Random failures

The random failure strategy involves attacking nodes or edges with equal probability, without considering any specific properties of the network components. Many scale-free networks exhibit considerable robustness under random failures. By calculating the network efficiency under different attack ratios R, the vulnerability of a road network to random failures can be effectively evaluated.

In this context, the attack ratio is generally defined as the proportion of removed edges (or nodes) relative to the total number of edges (or nodes) in the original network . Since this study focuses on edge-based attacks in vulnerability analysis, is specifically formulated as the ratio of the number of removed edges to the total number of edges in .

Betweenness-based targeted attacks

Targeted attacks prioritize nodes or edges based on important topological properties, such as degree or betweenness centrality. However, for the road network based on junction-junction relationships, node degrees show little variation, typically ranging between 1 and 5, making degree-based targeted attacks ineffective. Therefore, betweenness-based targeted attacks are more commonly employed in road network vulnerability studies. By attacking edges or nodes with high betweenness, the connectivity of the road network can be rapidly compromised. Indeed, many real-world networks demonstrate high fragility under betweenness-based targeted attacks.

Vulnerability analysis approach based on large-scale region segmentation

Basic Idea

The purpose of regional segmentation in road networks is to disrupt connectivity as rapidly as possible, thereby significantly reducing network efficiency and achieving a stronger attack impact. It also facilitates the identification of key roads across different spatial scales. A given urban road network can be partitioned in numerous ways, though the resulting subnets often differ in size. Figure 2 illustrates two segmentation outcomes: in (a), the two subnets differ substantially in size, whereas in (b), they are nearly equal. It is evident that using network efficiency as an evaluation metric, case (b) leads to a more severe efficiency loss. Therefore, this study introduces the concept of “large-scale region segmentation” to describe segmentation that divides the network into two disconnected subnets of roughly comparable spatial coverage, thereby maximizing efficiency reduction. Figure 2b exemplifies this concept, while (a) represents a non-large-scale region segmentation.

Basic idea of large-scale region segmentation. (a) Is not a large-scale region segmentation; (b) reflects a large-scale region segmentation.

Notably, large-scale region segmentation emphasizes spatial coverage division. In urban road networks, where nodes are generally distributed with limited spatial disparity, an appropriate segmentation strategy can yield two subnets that are balanced in both spatial extent (that is, both are maximized in coverage). Importantly, large-scale segmentation does not merely split the network arbitrarily into two equally sized regions. It accounts for the influence of distance and route planning on spatial accessibility. Nodes and edges within each subnet are confined to a compact spatial zone (i.e. all nodes are situated near a central location within the region, which aligns more closely with human spatial cognition. Thus, the concept of large-scale region segmentation better addresses inter-regional connectivity and routing issues in road networks, carrying significant geographical implications. Attack strategies designed based on this idea differ from conventional approaches grounded in connectivity or betweenness centrality of roads, segments, or nodes, and are likely to achieve more effective targeted disruption. The subsequent sections of this paper will present specific region segmentation attack strategies and corresponding vulnerability analysis algorithms for road networks.

Large-scale region segmentation strategy

To implement the aforementioned region segmentation idea, we introduce a segmentation strategy for road networks based on the maximum coverage principle. The maximum coverage location problem is a significant research topic in Geographic Information Systems (GIS). Its underlying principle involves determining optimal facility locations by specifying impedance thresholds and the number of facilities to be selected within a given road network model. This ensures that the chosen facilities cover all or the maximum number of demand nodes while allocating as many demand nodes as possible to each selected facility. The maximum coverage location method is widely used in GIS network analysis, and is frequently applied to solve facility siting challenges such as optimal fire station placement. Figure 2 illustrates a schematic diagram of maximum coverage within a sample road network, where the number of selected facilities is set to two. All nodes in the road network are treated as both demand points and potential facility sites, enabling the identification of optimal facility locations within the network. Both in terms of spatial domain partitioning and demand quantity, the two facilities achieve nearly equivalent coverage scope, realizing an optimal spatial coverage configuration.

These street networks were sourced from the OpenStreetMap (OSM) database and extracted using the osmnx Python package. Each street network comprises two datasets, i.e. nodes and edges, and requires no further preprocessing as the topological relationships based on junction-junction connections are inherently accurate. For comparison, we introduced random failures and betweenness-based attacks. The results for random failures represent the average of 10 independent experiments, illustrating the trend of efficiency loss against the attack ratio R. Discussions

A critical challenge in network vulnerability research lies in how to rapidly disrupt the topological structure of a road network with minimal cost, thereby significantly reducing its efficiency. Conventional vulnerability analyses often rely on targeted attacks based on node degree or betweenness. In the context of junction-junction relationships, betweenness-based attacks have been shown to impair street network connectivity more effectively than random failures. However, most existing methods focus predominantly on the importance of individual nodes or edges. In contrast, the approach proposed in this study introduces a novel vulnerability analysis strategy, i.e. large-scale region segmentation, which aims to partition the network into two subnetworks of comparable size. Experimental results from real-world urban networks (Atlanta, Chengdu, Florence, and Paris) demonstrate that the proposed method leads to a sharp decline in network efficiency, as connectivity between many node pairs is lost after segmentation. This study investiagets the vulnerability of urban street networks, as a kind of planar networks. To further validate the reliability of our method, experiments were also conducted on random planar networks generated via Delaunay Triangulation (DT)36 using ArcGIS software. The results (Fig. 9) confirm that our method outperforms the two baseline approaches. Besides, findings from both urban and synthetic planar networks support the conclusion that targeted attacks based on large-scale region segmentation are effective in analyzing the vulnerability of planar networks.

It is important to evaluate the vulnerability of planar networks from the perspective of overall spatial structural connectivity. Modularity maximization, a well-established method, identifies community structures based on topological tightness. However, it is most suitable for networks with pronounced community characteristics, such as social networks. While many planar networks, including urban road networks structured around junction-junction relationships, often lack such features. Consequently, modularity-based approaches are limited in interpreting the spatial performance of planar networks. In contrast, the method proposed in this paper addresses the problem from the viewpoints of spatial coverage and accessibility which reflects the concept of spatial service by segmenting the original network into two mutually exclusive subnetworks through spatial coverage maximization. Thus, our approach fundamentally differs from modularity-based techniques. In future work, we plan to extend this approach to study non-planar networks, including but not limited to airline and communication networks. Moreover, the methodology shows potential for application to general complex networks even in the absence of explicit spatial information.

As summarized in Table 2, when the attack ratio reaches 1–2%, the efficiency of the street networks decreases by more than 50%. With continued attacks, the efficiency loss rapidly exceeds 80%, corresponding to values of 4.26% for Atlanta, 10.19% for Chengdu, 8.92% for Florence, and 5.52% for Paris. Based on the experimental results from the four urban street networks and the random planar network, it can be concluded that the proposed method leads to a more severe decline in network efficiency compared to conventional approaches.

Conclusions

The vulnerability analysis of planar networks is essential for infrastructure safety and localized Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Compared to random failures, targeted attacks can disrupt a network’s structure more effectively by identifying and attacking key nodes or edges. This paper introduces the concept of large-scale region segmentation to propose a novel vulnerability analysis approach that accounts for global connectivity, thereby extending beyond conventional targeted attacks that focus solely on the importance of individual nodes or edges. Using the proposed method, a planar spatial network is partitioned into two subnetworks of approximately equal size, extracted via a maximum coverage analysis algorithm. This segmentation disconnects numerous node pairs, leading to a sharp decline in network efficiency. Experiments conducted on four urban street networks (including Atlanta, Chengdu, Florence, and Paris) and a random planar network) demonstrate that the proposed method attacks the global structure of the network more effectively than random failures and betweenness-based attacks.

The main contributions of this study are twofold: (1) it considers the overall connectivity of planar spatial networks rather than focusing on individual nodes or edges, and (2) it incorporates a large-scale region segmentation strategy that drastically reduces network efficiency. In summary, this work offers foundational theoretical and practical insights into the vulnerability and accessibility analysis of planar spatial networks. It should be noted that our study primarily examines the vulnerability of planar networks from a structural perspective, without incorporating additional attributes or characteristics of nodes or edges, such as capacity. In real-world planar networks, however, nodes and edges often possess critical attributes, such as capacity, flow, or functional constraints, which may introduce additional complexity and challenges in vulnerability assessment. In future work, we plan to integrate such attributes into the vulnerability analysis of planar networks, with the aim of developing a more generalized framework for assessing network vulnerability.
