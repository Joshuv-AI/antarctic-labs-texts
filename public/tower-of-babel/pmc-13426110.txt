Dermatological lesions in diabetes
Authors: Sofía Victoria Casado-Hoces, Delia González-Tejedor, Rebeca Martín-Velasco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes is a chronic metabolic disease characterised by high glucose levels and altered carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, affecting an increasing number of people worldwide. Spain ranks fifth among European countries with the highest incidence of diabetes. Hyperglycaemia causes damage to numerous cell types, including keratinocytes and fibroblasts in the skin. Dermatological manifestations appear in more than 30% of cases of diabetes at the onset of the disease and in up to 100% during the course of the disease. This makes skin examination of patients particularly important, as early treatment of diabetes can slow the progression of the disease and improve its prognosis. In addition, some dermatological changes may alert us to suboptimal metabolic control of previously known diabetes.Some lesions are associated with insulin resistance, others are due to fungal or bacterial infections, there are also alterations due to diabetes itself or to chronic complications of diabetes; finally, there are lesions due to pharmacological treatment of the disease and to devices such as insulin infusion pumps and sensors for interstitial glucose measurement. Knowing and recognising dermatological changes is therefore essential in our daily work, because they guide us towards a better management of the disease and its causes.

Introduction

According to data by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), 65.6 million people were affected by diabetes in Europe in 2024 and it is estimated that there will be a 10% increase in this figure over the next 25 years1. Spain occupies fifth place amongst European countries with the highest incidence of diabetes, with a percentage of 9.7%1.

According to Vera-Remartínez, the prevalence of diabetes in prisons stood at 5.3% in 20142, and this percentage may well have increased given the upward trend in diabetes incidence.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a heterogenous group of disorders characterised by high levels of blood glucose and a metabolism affected by carbohydrates and lipids3. It is classified into type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, monogenic diabetes, diabetes induced by medication or diseases of the exocrine pancreas and gestational diabetes4. Type 2 diabetes is responsible for more than 80% of cases3,5. Insulin resistance is defined as a diminished response by tissues to normal concentrations of insulin and is one of the key factors in the physiopathology of type 2 diabetes. This particular problem may be present for several years before it is diagnosed6.

The hyperglycaemia produced in diabetes damages many cell types, including the endothelial, neuronal and renal cells, although it also affects the keratinocytes and fibroblasts present in the epidermis and dermis5.

The skin is the body’s largest organ and functions as a metabolically active biological barrier that separates internal homeostasis from the external surroundings. It is exposed to constant variations caused by solar radiation, mechanical energy, humidity and chemical and/or biological changes7. Physical examination of the skin makes it possible to identify changes linked to dysfunctions in other endocrinal organs such as the pituitary gland, thyroids, parathyroids, thymus, adrenal glands, pancreas and gonads8.

More than 30% of people who suffer from diabetes present symptoms of skin disorders at the onset of diabetes, and up to 100% present similar symptoms over the course of the disease9. Recognising such symptoms is vitally important, as early treatment can slow down the progression of diabetes and improve prognosis10. Furthermore, some skin conditions can alert physicians to the likelihood of suboptimal metabolic control of an already diagnosed diabetes10.

The aim of this article is to describe the clinical characteristics and physiopathology of skin lesions associated with diabetes, following a classification according to their aetiology (Table 1).

Physiopathology

Diabetes affects the skin through a variety of mechanisms, two of which, hyperglycaemia and advanced glycation end products (AGE), have been described in considerable detail11. High glycaemia levels have a major impact on skin homeostasis, inhibiting both the proliferation and migration of keratinocytes and protein biosynthesis, leading to endothelial cell apoptosis, reducing nitric oxide synthesis and altering phagocytosis and chemotaxis of different cells. Besides this direct damage, hyperglycaemia also leads to the formation of AGE in proteins, lipids and nucleic acids11.

These products cause the formation of reactive oxygen species and stop them from being eliminated, which in turn causes oxidative stress. They also lead to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines11. AGEs change the properties of collagen, reducing its flexibility and solubility. They participate in skin ageing, the development of fibrosis and in the immunosuppression related to diabetes. These immunological changes affect wound cicatrisation, mainly due to dysfunctions of leukocytes and growth factors11. The effects of diabetes on skin hydration include reduced activity of the sebaceous glands and an imbalance in the composition of the lipids in the corneal layer, caused by reduced levels of free fatty acids8.

Skin diseases associated with insulin resistance

Acanthosis nigricans

Acanthosis nigricans (Figure 1) presents as velvety hyperpigmented and hyperkeratotic plaques in the folds of the neck, armpits and groin9,12. It appears in a range of endocrinal disorders such as diabetes, obesity, acromegalia, Cushing’s syndrome, thyroid dysfunction and polycystic ovary syndrome3. It may also appear in malignant processes such as gastric cancer13,14. Treatment in cases where diabetes is present includes general measures for weight loss, increased physical activity and an adequate diet13,14.

Acrochordons or skin tags

Acrochordons (Figure 1) are asymptomatic, benign, peduncular skin lesions of a natural or pigmented colour, which are usually located in the eyelids, neck, armpits and other skin folds12. A slightly higher percentage of women present with this condition than men, and prevalence increases with age10. Keratolytic treatments are not effective and even when they are eliminated, they reappear over time. If the lesion is a large one or causes discomfort due to irritation, they can be extirpated15.

Androgenetic alopecia

Androgenetic alopecia affects both men and women. In the case of men, the hair loss commences with a receding front hairline and diffuse loss on the vertex, to form a horseshoe or Hippocratic hairline6,15. Hair loss in women takes the form of diffuse thinning and does not affect the hairline. Although women’s hair loss may be considerable, it rarely reaches a point of complete baldness15.

Lesions caused by diabetic microangiopathy

Diabetic dermopathy

Diabetic dermopathy (Figure 2) is characterised by the appearance of blemishes that are usually atrophic, hyperpigmented and oval or round. They are bilaterally but not symmetrically distributed, without scaling or scabbing on the surface. They normally appear in the pretibial regions, and may occasionally extend to the thighs, forearms, trunk and hypogastrium8,9. They are regarded as a clinical sign of chronic complications in diabetes.

Rubeosis faciei

A persistent erythema that affects the face and neck as a result of loss of vascular tone. The recommendation when evaluating these patients is to seek other microangiopathies such as retinopathy and nephropathy. Treatment includes strict glucose control, and not consuming alcohol, caffeine or other vasodilators8.

Bullosis diabeticorum

Diabetic bulla affects patients with long-term diabetes, microvascular complications and poor metabolic control8. The clinical signs of this condition are taut, painless blisters with no surrounding erythema or itching. They appear suddenly and spontaneously in the acral regions, especially the legs and feet8; and are often bilateral, asymmetrical and disappear rapidly without leaving any scars8.

Pigmented purpuric dermatosis

This condition appears in about 50% of cases of diabetic dermopathy9.

It consists of a heterogenous group of skin disorders that cause orange or brown patches speckled with cayenne pepper spots, distributed mainly in the pretibial region of the lower extremities. They may occasionally affect the ankles and back of the feet10. They are caused by extravasation of the erythrocytes to the superficial venous plexus16. They frequently appear in elderly persons suffering from diabetes who present congestive heart failure with oedemas in the lower extremities16. This condition is not regarded as a marker for diabetes if there are no signs of diabetic dermopathy10.

Lesions caused by macroangiopathy

Diabetes increases the risk of peripheral arterial disease by two to four times17. This condition is characterised by reduced posterior arterial blood flow to the aortic arch as a secondary feature of an intrinsic or extrinsic obstructive mechanism, mainly caused by atherosclerosis, due to an abnormal accumulation of lipoprotein particles and fibrous tissue between intimal and muscle layers of the arterial wall18.

Atherosclerotic changes present in the lower extremities with symptoms such as skin atrophy, hair loss in the legs and feet, cold feet, onychodystrophy and pallid skin when the legs are raised. Ischemic ulcers may also appear, generally on the toes, when the reduced blood flow reaches a critical stage9.

Skin infections

The susceptibility of diabetes patients to severe, recurrent and atypical infections is a well-known fact19. The incidence of skin infections is greater if metabolic control is deficient and when there are diabetes complications20. Altered microcirculation, peripheral neuropathy, vasculopathy, altered functions of the skin barrier, acid-base disorders and an affected immune response are all factors that make diabetics vulnerable to infections9.

Recurrent mycoses such as tinea corporis, onychomycosis and vulvovaginal or balanopreputial candidiasis are signs that should alert health professionals to the possibility of diabetes14,21. Bacterial infections such as impetigo, erythrasma, abscesses, folliculitis and cellulitis, generally caused by Staphylococcus aureus or beta-haemolytic group A streptococcus may also be a first sign of the disease14,21.

Lesions caused by neuropathy

Motor neuropathy in the lower nerves includes an imbalance between the flexor and extensor muscles, displacement of the adipose tissue pads and subluxation of the bones of the feet. Typical symptoms of sensory neuropathy in patients with diabetes include paraesthesia with reduced sensitivity to pain and temperature, along with burning sensations in the legs, especially at night9. Motor and sensory neuropathy combined with mechanical factors and microangiopathy play an important role in the onset and development of diabetic foot9.

A neuropathic ulcer originates at the points of greatest pressure, starting with hyperkeratosis that subsequently becomes a painless ulcer that is almost always circular. It is very difficult to cicatrise and often becomes chronic with frequent infections, and in serious cases may form deep abscesses and osteomielitis9,20. Prevention of diabetic foot includes regular exploration of the feet, assessing the presence of callouses and ulcers, sensitivity to pressure and vibration and the presence of peripheral arterial disease22.

Dermatopathy associated with diabetes mellitus

Xerosis and thickening of the skin

Xerosis or dryness of the skin is a very common complaint amongst people with diabetes, and is one of the earliest signs of the disease. Another notable feature is desquamation, reduced elasticity and thickening of the skin19. The latter condition is caused by the activation of fibroblasts and increased collagen polymerization23.

The hands and feet are the locations where benign skin thickening associated with limited joint mobility syndrome are likely to occur. This condition can become extremely disabling19. Methods to control xerosis include emollient creams8. Strict glucose control also appears to improve the skin thickening associated with diabetes23.

Pruritus

Generalised pruritus is often present in patients with diabetes, and in some cases is associated with dry skin. Another factor that plays a part in the presence of pruritus is neuropathy, since dysfunctional sympathetic nerves can affect thermoregulation and cause hipohydrosis8,20.

Pruritus of the trunk is regarded as a symptom of neuropathy in diabetic patients, while pruritus of the scalp may be one of the first symptoms of the disease in geriatric patients8. Pruritus may sometimes be associated with concomitant autoimmune diseases, drug reactions and opportunistic infections8,21. Severe itching may cause cycles of itching-scratching that should be avoided, as the trauma caused in the affected areas may act as a point of entry for infectious agents8,9. The use of emollient creams improves xerosis and therefore helps to relieve the associated pruritus8.

Eruptive xanthomas

Eruptive xanthomatosis associated with diabetes is especially common when there are high triglyceride levels resulting from poor metabolic control. They may appear as yellow dome-shaped papules of 1-2 mm with an erythematic base and are mainly located in the extensor surfaces of the extremities and the hips8 (Figure 3). Eruptive xanthomas tend to be resolved when carbohydrate and lipid metabolism is controlled8.

Psoriasis

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory disease with a variable impact on the skin that can range from a few plaques to extensive areas with many lesions. When over 10% of the body’s surface area is affected, it may be associated with psoriatic arthritis and metabolic syndrome8. A generally held belief is that patients with severe psoriasis are twice as likely to develop diabetes in comparison to the general population8,10.

Vitiligo

Vitiligo is a chronic autoimmune disease that causes areas of the skin to lose colour. It may be associated with type 1 diabetes, as it is more common amongst type 1 diabetics than in persons with type 2 diabetes or the general population. It appears in the form of clearly defined patches without pigment or colour. There may be some degree of symmetry in the location of the patches, which generally predominate around the natural orifices, on the back of the hands, wrists, forearms, flexion creases, extensor regions, thorax and abdomen8,10.

Scleredema diabeticorum

This condition is characterised by a thickening of the reticular dermis that affects the neck, shoulders and back and occasionally extends to the face, chest, arms and abdomen. The affected skin becomes hard, thick, red in colour, with the texture of orange peel9. It is not easy to identify in many cases during examination, and the hardened area needs to be palpated to establish that it exists8. Treatment includes immunosuppressive agents, systemic and intralesional corticoids, colchicine, pentoxifylline, radiotherapy and phototherapy with ultraviolet light (UVA)8.

Granuloma annulare

Granuloma annulare (Figure 4) is characterised by a large number of red or pink papules and patches that are generally distributed symmetrically on the lower extremities, backs of the hands and feet and areas of the trunk that are exposed to the sun. The lesions gradually expand to form rings with raised edges and an involuted centre. They are generally asymptomatic and leave no scarring9. The eruption itself is usually asymptomatic but may itch10.

Powerful intralesional or topical corticoids and calcineurin inhibitors are effective in treating the localised variety. Treatment of the generalised version includes UV light therapy with psoralenes (PUVA), systemic treatment with antimalarials and biologic therapy with anti-tumour necrosis factor (FNT)(l8).

Necrobiosis lipoidica

Necrobiosis lipoidica (Figure 5) is a chronic granulomatous disease characterised by collagen degeneration, thickening of the endothelial wall and the appearance of fat deposits. It commences with the appearance of erythematous papules that evolve to brownish-yellow patches with an atrophic centre and telangiectasias9,10. The yellowish zone in the centre of the patch is caused by the loss of dermis, which makes the subcutaneous fat more visible. 35% of lesions produce painful ulcerations. The lesions appear in pretibial areas, the thighs, behind the knees and feet, with a bilateral and symmetric distribution8. Treatment is similar to the one used for granuloma annulare with powerful intralesional and topical corticoids, calcineurin inhibitors and PUVA light therapy8.

Yellow skin and nails

The skin and nails take on a yellow colour. This condition is asymptomatic and benign and is probably due to non-enzymatic glycation of the dermal collagen resulting from poor glycaemic control10. The yellow colouration of the nails may partly be attributed to an increase in the prevalence of onychomycosis amongst persons who suffer from diabetes10.

Lichen planus

This condition clinically presents as itchy erythematous papules, typically with Wickham’s striae. It mainly affects the ankles and wrists, and may spread and join to form plaques8,9.

Reactive perforating collagenosis

A disease characterised by the transepidermal elimination of the connective tissue in the dermis8. The clinical presentation of the condition consists of umbilicated nodules with a hyperkeratotic cap or pruriginous erythematous patches of 2-10 mm with adherent scabs on the extremities, trunk and the backs of the hands8. The lesions may resolve spontaneously after some months if trauma and scratching are avoided8.

Reactions to treatment

The most common skin reactions caused by insulin include localised inflammatory reactions at the injection site and urticaria12. The incidence of allergic and local reactions has dropped considerably since the appearance of highly purified human insulin, new systems of administration and the use of subcutaneous insulin infusion pumps21. One particular condition is lipohypertrophy, which is characterised by non-inflammatory subcutaneous nodules, similar to lipomas that appear in the most frequently used injection sites12. Injections in such areas are not recommended because insulin absorption may be delayed21. Lipoatrophy can also appear at injection sites in the form of subcutaneous atrophy that creates a dented cutaneous patch.

Cases or irritant or allergic contact dermatitis caused by some of the adhesives or components of insulin pump systems or continuous glucose monitoring sensors have been described in recent years12,24.

Oral antidiabetics can cause a host of skin disorders. Common ones include maculopapular rashes, lichenoid eruptions, photosensitivity and pruritus12.

Bullous pemphigoid is an autoimmune disease that presents as tense blisters or initially as pruritus or urticarial plaques. Although it is a rare complication, a link has been observed in recent years between the appearance of this condition and the commencement months or years before of administrations of dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors12,21,25.

Patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors are more likely to suffer from a greater number of urinary tract infections such as candidal balanitis, vulvovaginitis and inflammatory vulvitis, which may be caused by the irritative effect of high concentrations of glucose in urine12,21.

Patients treated with glucagon-like peptide 1 analogies have been described with adverse reactions at the injection site, including hypersensitivity, eosinophilic panniculitis and bullous pemphigoid26.

Conclusions

Dermatological lesions are common amongst persons with diabetes. Examinations of the skin can enable professionals to recognise many cutaneous symptoms of the disease. Some, like acanthosis nigricans, can act as early markers of diabetes, which permits prompt diagnosis and treatment. Skin conditions can also guide health professionals in cases of poor glycaemic control of diabetes and the presence of any related complications. Treatment of cutaneous symptoms of the disease depends on the adequate metabolic control and self-care of each patient. Professionals should make every effort in the fight against diabetes and its complications to educate and guide the population towards healthier lifestyles.
