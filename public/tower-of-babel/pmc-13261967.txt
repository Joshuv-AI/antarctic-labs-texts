Cyclic alternating patterns of encephalopathy: implications for triphasic waves in noncomatose patients with metabolic-toxic encephalopathies
Authors: Philippe Gélisse, Arielle Crespel
Published: 2026

Cycling Alternating Pattern of Encephalopathy (CAPE) is a new concept that refers to an EEG pattern observed in encephalopathic patients. It is characterized by spontaneous alternation between two distinct background patterns, each lasting for at least 10 s and recurring in a regular sequence for at least six cycles [1, 2]. Unfortunately, the term “encephalopathy” is broad and ambiguous, encompassing a wide range of etiologies with distinct pathophysiological and EEG correlates. It does not specify whether it refers to patients with metabolic-toxic or multifactorial encephalopathy with generalized periodic discharges (GPDs) with triphasic morphology, patients with spongiform encephalopathy, or comatose cases (particularly those receiving mechanical ventilation) with a severe brain condition such as severe head trauma or stroke. This lack of specificity limits CAPE’s clinical utility, as different types of encephalopathy carry unique prognoses and management implications.

Three non-comatose female patients presenting with triphasic waves (TWs) on their EEGs are reported. Two of them had metabolic/multifactorial encephalopathy, and the third developed contrast-induced encephalopathy following radiological imaging. A CAP was observed during spontaneous sleep in two cases (Fig. 1) and during benzodiazepine-induced sleep in one case. This pattern showed two distinct phases: Phase A (characterized by TWs) and Phase B (during which the patients returned to sleep), reflected by lower amplitude background EEG activity. Auditory stimulation delivered during Phase B reliably triggered the reappearance of TWs.

The isolated impairment of vigilance, without subtle clinical ictal phenomena, in addition to the typical morphology of TWs lacking spatiotemporal evolution, supported a nonictal origin. In the third case, EEG improvement without a parallel improvement in consciousness further argued against nonconvulsive status epilepticus. All these findings favored a vigilance-related cyclic pattern that was consistent with a CAP rather than an ictal process. The details of these observations, along with their corresponding EEG data, are provided in the supplementary material.

Patients admitted with acute respiratory failure, cardiovascular emergencies, and neurologic impairments (brain stem lesions, high cervical cord injuries, and neuromuscular disorders)—as well as patients with renal failure—often have sleep disruptions like insomnia, hypersomnia, or related breathing disorders, with symptoms worsening in critically ill patients. This vulnerability is particularly relevant in older patients. As well as GPDs observed in Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD)—which may occasionally present as TWs but more commonly as periodic sharp wave complexes (PSWC)—TWs in metabolic/toxic encephalopathies are influenced by the level of vigilance and vary throughout the sleep-wake cycle. In patients with CJD who exhibit GPDs on their EEG, sleep may be characterized by a CAP with two observable phases [3]. Phase A is associated with increased arousal and the emergence of the GPDs, while phase B is associated with a reduction in the arousal level and hypotonia with low amplitude EEG activity. The CAP in CJD was first characterized by Terzano and colleagues [4], the authors who introduced the concept of CAP in NREM sleep as a natural phenomenon. Yet, until recently, the CAP in CJD had received insufficient attention.

In metabolic-toxic or multifactorial encephalopathies, TWs occur during an intermediate level of consciousness where patients are somnolent and lethargic, with EEGs showing fewer muscle and eye movement artifacts. An increased state of alertness, marked by muscle artifacts and open eyes, may temporarily suppress TWs—this reactivity can help distinguish between nonictal and ictal patterns [5]. TWs disappear when patients spontaneously fall asleep and after administration of IV hypnotics, only to reappear in cases of sleep disruption due to sleep apnea. An inadequate comprehension of sleep architecture in patients with GPDs may result in the misinterpretation of recurring GPDs as electrographic seizures [3], rather than recognizing them as a typical transitional pattern from sleep to wakefulness.

CAP is characterized by periodic shifts between two distinct background patterns, reflecting fluctuations in arousal states. The presence of CAP in comatose patients may carry prognostic significance—it suggests preserved functionality within certain components of the arousal system [2]. However, the term “encephalopathy” in the CAPE acronym may be misleading. In comatose patients, CAP represents a distinct entity compared to noncomatose individuals with a spongiform encephalopathy or in patients with toxic-metabolic encephalopathies with TWs, where impaired vigilance and GPDs are prominent.

Further research is needed to clarify the prognostic value and clinical significance of CAP across different populations, particularly in comatose versus noncomatose patients, as the clinical correlations of CAPE remain unclear and may lead to uncertainty in interpretation. We propose that CAP in comatose patients, especially those on mechanical ventilation, as a separate entity. Accordingly, CAPE in comatose patients should be differentiated from recurrent GPDs observed during sleep in noncomatose patients (e.g., metabolic-toxic, multifactorial, or CJD-related), where they more often reflect arousal mechanisms such as sleep apnea. Because their implications and prognoses likely differ, reports should specify either “Cycling Alternating Pattern of Encephalopathy in Coma (CAPEC) or Cycling Alternating Pattern of Encephalopathy during Sleep (CAPES).
