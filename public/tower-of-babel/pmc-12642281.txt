PCR-based detection of viral and parasitic etiologies in anterior uveitis: a cross-sectional study from Iran

Background

Anterior uveitis (AU) contributes to 5–10% of global visual impairment, with viral etiologies posing diagnostic challenges due to overlapping clinical features [1, 2]. Herpes simplex virus (HSV), varicella-zoster virus (VZV), and cytomegalovirus (CMV) are key pathogens, necessitating accurate identification to initiate antiviral therapy and prevent complications like glaucoma [3]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis of aqueous humor is a cornerstone for diagnosing viral AU, offering rapid and specific detection to complement clinical diagnosis [4]. Data from the Middle East are scarce despite regional variations in pathogen prevalence [5, 6]. This study examines viral and parasitic etiologies of AU in an Iranian cohort using PCR, aiming to identify clinical predictors and enhance therapeutic precision in an understudied region.

Methods study design and population

This cross-sectional study was conducted at Feiz Eye Hospital, Isfahan, Iran, approved by the Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.MED.REC.1399.697) and adherent to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained. We reviewed records of 80 patients with 81 eyes affected by unilateral AU undergoing PCR analysis from January 2015 to February 2020. Inclusion criteria included clinical suspicion based on unilateral presentation, sectoral iris atrophy, keratic precipitates (KPs), ocular hypertension (IOP > 21 mmHg), and poor response to steroids. Exclusion criteria were incomplete records or loss to follow-up. The study followed STROBE guidelines.

Data collection

Clinical data included demographics, visual acuity (logMAR), intraocular pressure (IOP), slit-lamp findings (KPs, iris atrophy, posterior synechiae), and treatment history (Table 1). Uveitis was classified per Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature (SUN) criteria [7]. Prior antiviral therapy in 46.9% of patients may have reduced PCR sensitivity.

Anterior chamber paracentesis and PCR analysis

Paracentesis used a 27- or 30-gauge needle to collect 0.2 mL of aqueous humor, stored at − 70 °C, and analyzed within 24 h. Real-time PCR on the Rotor-Gene Q platform (Qiagen, Germany) targeted HSV, VZV, CMV, EBV, and Toxoplasma gondii. Primer/probe sequences are in Supplementary Table S1 or upon request. DNA extraction used the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen; sensitivity, 10 copies/µL; specificity, > 95%). Validated primers/probes (Metabion, Germany), a bromo mosaic virus internal control, and positive/negative controls ensured reliability. Samples were collected before or 48 h after antiviral therapy. Viral load was not quantified, limiting differentiation of active vs. latent infection.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25. Shapiro-Wilk tests confirmed non-normality for IOP; Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for non-normal variables. Categorical variables used chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. PCR performance was assessed against clinical diagnoses based on SUN criteria, evaluated independently of PCR to minimize bias. Multivariate logistic regression, adjusted for age and gender, assessed predictors of PCR positivity. Assumptions were verified: independence (no clustering), no multicollinearity (VIF < 5), no outliers (Cook’s distance < 1), linearity (Box-Tidwell P > .05), and adequate sample size (EPV = 10.8 for 54 positives, 5 variables). Firth’s penalized regression mitigated overfitting. Model calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow P = .42) and discrimination (AUC = 0.78) were acceptable. Significance was set at P < .05.

Results patient characteristics

The cohort included 80 patients (38 males, 43 females; mean age, 46.3 ± 16.4 years) with 81 affected eyes (98.7% unilateral) (Table 1). Most (96.3%) were immunocompetent. Chronic AU was predominant (46.9%), followed by acute (27.1%) and recurrent (25.9%). Mean visual acuity was 0.46 ± 0.34 logMAR; mean IOP was 16.39 ± 7.75 mmHg. KPs were observed in 71.6%, iris atrophy in 48.8%, and posterior synechiae in 17.3%.

PCR outcomes

Of 81 samples, 54 (66.7%) were PCR-positive: VZV (27/54, 50.0%), HSV (25/54, 46.3%), CMV (2/54, 3.7%) (Table 2). All samples were negative for EBV and Toxoplasma gondii. PCR confirmed the diagnosis in 62.9% and prompted treatment changes in 24.7%. Stratified PCR positivity was 75% (24/32) in chronic, 60% (13/22) in acute, and 65% (14/22) in recurrent AU. No complications occurred.

Diagnostic performance

PCR sensitivity was 72.2% (95% CI, 58.4–83.5), specificity 77.8% (95% CI, 57.7–91.4), PPV 86.7%, and NPV 58.3% (Table 3). The low NPV indicates PCR is less reliable for ruling out viral AU, necessitating clinical correlation.

Predictors of PCR positivity

Multivariate logistic regression identified iris atrophy (aOR, 2.12; 95% CI, 1.1–4.0; P = .01) and a borderline association with ocular hypertension (aOR, 2.88; 95% CI, 0.9–8.7; P = .06) as predictors (Table 4). KPs and corneal edema were not significant.

Discussion

This study provides novel insights into infectious etiologies of anterior uveitis (AU) in Iran, identifying VZV and HSV as predominant pathogens, with low CMV prevalence. These trends align with Middle Eastern data but differ from East Asian reports, potentially due to genetic, environmental, or vaccination differences [6, 8, 9]. The absence of EBV and T. gondii supports focusing on herpetic viruses in this region. Our VZV and HSV findings exceed European and Japanese reports but are lower than Indian data, while CMV rates are consistent with Iranian studies but lower than East Asian ones [5, 8, 10–13] Table 5. These regional variations highlight the need for tailored screening in Iran, where resource limitations (e.g., limited access to multiplex PCR or viral load testing) and high herpetic burden due to incomplete vaccination coverage pose diagnostic challenges. PCR sensitivity and specificity were moderate, likely reduced by prior antiviral therapy (46.9%), which decreases viral DNA load [14]. The high PPV supports PCR for confirming infection, but low NPV and moderate sensitivity underscore its unreliability for exclusion, necessitating clinical correlation [15]. PCR altered treatment in a quarter of cases, demonstrating utility as an adjunct in resource-limited settings like Iran, where delayed diagnosis risks glaucoma [16, 17]. Iris atrophy as a predictor aligns with herpetic AU hallmarks, while the lack of KPs association may reflect cohort chronicity. Stratification showed consistent etiologies across AU forms, aiding broad application. Clinical implications include prioritizing PCR in Iranian patients with iris atrophy or hypertension, but selection bias from suspicion-based inclusion and lack of viral load (distinguishing active vs. latent) limit interpretation. The cross-sectional, single-center design and small sample restrict generalizability; overfitting risk was low (EPV >10) but larger, multicenter studies with quantified loads are needed.
